The harmonic maps compactification of Teichmüller spaces for punctured Riemann surfaces
Kento Sakai

Abstract
. Wolf gave a homeomorphism from the Teichmüller space to the space of quadratic differentials on a closed Riemann surface by using harmonic maps. Moreover, using harmonic maps rays, he gave a compactification of the Teichmüller space and show that it coincides with the Thurston compactification. In this paper, we extend the harmonic maps compactification to the Teichmüller spaces of punctured Riemann surfaces, and show that it still coincides with the Thurston compactification.



1.
Introduction

Let
S
be a finite type surface of genus
g
with
n
punctures such that
\chi(S)=2-2g-n<0
. The
Teichmüller space
of
S
is defined as the set of complete finite-area hyperbolic metrics on
S
modulo isotopy. Let
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
denote the Teichmüller space of
S
.

For
\sigma,\rho\in\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
, there exists a unique harmonic diffeomorphism
h(\sigma,\rho)
from
(S,\sigma)
to
(S,\rho)
isotopic to the identity with finite energy
[
Loh91
]
.
Then the
(2,0)
-part of the pull-buck metric
h(\sigma,\rho)^{\ast}\rho
is a holomorphic quadratic differential on
(S,\sigma)
, which is called the
Hopf differential
of
h(\sigma,\rho)
.
Therefore fixing the domain metric
\sigma
, we obtain a map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{T}_{g,n}\to\QD(\sigma)
given by
\Phi(\rho)=(h(\sigma,\rho)^{\ast}\rho)^{2,0}
, where
\QD(\sigma)
denotes the vector space of holomorphic quadratic differentials with finite
L^{1}
-norm on
(S,\sigma)
. It is shown that
\Phi
is a homeomorphism by Wolf for
n=0
, by Lohkamp for
n>0

[
Wol89
,
Loh91
]
.

Using the map
\Phi
, we can obtain the compactification of
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
since the vector space
\QD(\sigma)
has the natural compactification which is obtained by adding an infinity point to each ray from the origin.
This compactification is called the
harmonic maps compactification
and denote by
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\textrm{harm}}}
. The boundary of the harmonic maps compactification is identified with the unit sphere
\SQD(\sigma)
in
\QD(\sigma)
. On the other hand, Thurston introduced the Thurston compactification
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
, which is given by embedding
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
to the projective space of functionals on the set
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S)
of isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves on
S
.
The boundary
\partial\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
of the Thurston compactification is the projective space
\PMF
of measured foliations on
S
.
The action of the mapping class group on
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
extends contiunously to
\partial\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
.

Wolf showed that, if
S
is closed, the homeomorphism
\Phi
continuously extends to the homeomorphism
\overline{\Phi}
from
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,0}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
to
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,0}^{\mathrm{harm}}}
.
This implies that the restriction of
\overline{\Phi}
to the Thurston boundary coincides with the canonical identification
\overline{F_{v}}
of
\SQD(\sigma)
and
\PMF
, where
\overline{F_{v}}
is induced by the map
F_{v}\colon\QD(\sigma)\to\MF
given by the vertical measured foliation
F_{v}(\Psi)
for
\Psi\in\QD(\sigma)
.
Therefore, the harmonic maps compactification is independent on the choice of the domain metric
\sigma
. In addition, the extension
\overline{\Phi}
provides the Thurston compactification with a global parametrization by the closed unit ball in
\QD(\sigma)
.

In this paper, we extend the compatibility of the harmonic maps compactification and the Thurston compactification for the Teichmüller spaces of punctured surfaces.

Main Theorem
.

The homeomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathcal{T}_{g,n}\to\QD(\sigma)
continuously extends to a homeomorphism from
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
to
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{harm}}}
. Moreover, this extension on the boundary coincides with the inverse of the canonical identification
\overline{F_{v}}
.

Our strategy is based on Wolf’s proof for a closed Riemann surface. However, the compactness of the surface is essentially used in his proof. In our setting, a holomorphic quadratic differential may have a simple pole at a puncture, so we have to substaintially change his proof.

We outline our proof below, comparing with Wolf’s proof. Fixing
\Phi_{0}\in\SQD(\sigma)
, we set
\rho_{t}\coloneqq\Phi^{-1}(t\Phi_{0})
for
t>0
. The one-parameter family
\{\rho_{t}\}
is called the
harmonic maps ray
in the direction of
\Phi_{0}
.
We first show that, for the harmonic diffeomorphsim
h(t)\colon(S,\sigma)\to(S,\rho_{t})
, the norm of the Beltrami differential of
h(t)
converges monotonically to
1
as
t\to\infty

(
3.7
).
For the proof of this convergence,
[
Wol89
]
uses the compactness of the closed surface.
Therefore, using some results on holomorphic energy functions for punctured surfaces in
[
Loh91
]
, we prove
3.7
without the compactness.
From
3.7
, we obtain the asymptotic length of arcs along the leaves of the horizontal or vertical measured foliation of
\Phi_{0}
(
3.8
).

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S)
be the set of isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves on
S
and
\beta
be the identification of
\QD(\sigma)
and
\MF
given by
\beta\Phi=F_{v}(4\Phi)
.
We next recall the fundamental lemma, which is an inequality of the intersection number function
i(\beta\Phi(\rho),\cdot)
and the hyperbolic length function
\ell_{\rho}
on
\mathcal{C}
for a metric
\rho\in\mathcal{T}_{g,0}
.

Theorem

(
[
Wol89
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

Fix an isotopy class
[\gamma]\in\mathcal{C}
. Then, for every
\rho\in\mathcal{T}_{g,0}
, there exist positive constants
k_{0}=k_{0}(\|\Phi(\rho)\|)
and
\eta(\|\Phi(\rho)\|,[\gamma])
such that

i(\beta\Phi(\rho),[\gamma])\leq\ell_{\rho}([\gamma])\leq k_{0}i(\beta\Phi(\rho),[\gamma])+\eta

where
k_{0}\searrow 1
and
\eta\|\Phi(\rho)\|^{-1/2}\to 0
as
\|\Phi(\rho)\|\to\infty
.

The key to show the lemma is constructing a “staircase” representative
\gamma_{\Phi}
for
\Phi\in\SQD(\sigma)
. The representative
\gamma_{\Phi}
satisfies some conditions. For example, it consists of curves along leaves of horizontal or vertical foliation of
\Phi
and the intersection number
i(\beta\Phi(\rho),\gamma_{\Phi})
is equal to
i(\beta\Phi(\rho),[\gamma])
. However, for punctured surfaces, we cannot construct
\gamma_{\Phi}
in the same manner as
[
Wol89
]
, since a quadratic differential may have a simple pole at each puncture. Hence, we show the following instead for punctured surfaces.

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix an isotopy class
[\gamma]\in\mathcal{C}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, there exists a nonnegative number
c_{0}=c_{0}([\gamma],\varepsilon)<\varepsilon
satisfying the following: for every
\rho\in\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
, there exist positive constants
k_{0}=k_{0}(\|\Phi(\rho)\|,\varepsilon)
and
\eta=\eta(\|\Phi(\rho)\|,[\gamma],\varepsilon)
such that

i(\beta\Phi(\rho),[\gamma])\leq\ell_{\rho}([\gamma])\leq k_{0}i(\beta\Phi(\rho),[\gamma])+\eta

where
k_{0}\searrow 1
and
\eta\|\Phi(\rho)\|^{-1/2}\to c_{0}
as
\|\Phi(\rho)\|\to\infty
.

This propositon is different from Wolf’s lemma in that the limit of
\eta\|\Phi(\rho)\|^{-1/2}
as
\|\Phi(\rho)\|\to\infty
may not be
0
. Setting the allowance for arbitraly small
\varepsilon>0
, we can construct such a “staircase” representative
\gamma_{\Phi}
. However, unlike the above lemma, we need to increase the horizontal measure of the representative
\gamma_{\Phi}
from
i(\beta\Phi(\rho),[\gamma])
by a little. Therefore, in order to estimate the length of the additional horizontal arcs, we use some results on quadratic differential metrics in
[
Min92
,
Wol89
]
.

Theorem

4.1
is a week version of
[
Wol89
, Lemma 4.1]
, but still leads to the main theorem.

2.
Background

2.1.
Harmonic maps

Let
\overline{S}
be a closed, oriented, connected surface of genus
g
, and
S\coloneqq\overline{S}-\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
.
If
\chi(S)=2-2g-n<0
, the surface admits (complete, finite area) hyperbolic metrics.
Throughout this paper, we fix
S
such that
\chi(S)<0
and
S
is not a thrice punctured surface.
Let
\sigma
be a hyperbolic metric on
S
.
The hyperbolic surface
(S,\sigma)
can be regarded as a (punctured) Riemann surface by taking the isothermal coordinates system for
\sigma
.

Let
\sigma|dz|^{2},\rho|dw|^{2}
be hyperbolic metrics on
S
, where
z=x+iy
and
w=u+iv
denote the conformal structures of
(S,\sigma)
and
(S,\rho)
, respectively. For a
C^{2}
map
f\colon(S,\sigma|dz|^{2})\to(S,\rho|dw|^{2})
, we define the
energy density
of
f
by

e(f)\coloneqq\frac{\rho(f(z))}{\sigma(z)}(|f_{z}|^{2}+|f_{\bar{z}}|^{2}),

the
total energy
of
f
by

\mathcal{E}(f)\coloneqq\int_{S}\rho(f(z))(|f_{z}|^{2}+|f_{\bar{z}}|^{2})\,\frac{i}{2}dzd\bar{z}

and, the
holomorphic energy

H(f)
and
anti-holomorphic energy

L(f)
by

\displaystyle H(f)\coloneqq\frac{\rho(f(z))}{\sigma(z)}|f_{z}|^{2},\ L(f)\coloneqq\frac{\rho(f(z))}{\sigma(z)}|f_{\bar{z}}|^{2}.

Let
J(f)
be the Jacobian of
f
, then
J(f)=H(f)-L(f)
. The Beltrami differential of
f
is defined by

\nu(f)\coloneqq\frac{f_{\bar{z}}{d\bar{z}}}{f_{z}dz}.

The norm of the Beltrami differential of
f
is the well-defined function on
(S,\sigma)
. Clearly, we have
|\nu(f)|^{2}=L(f)/H(f)
.

A
C^{2}
map
f\colon(S,\sigma)\to(S,\rho)
is said to be
harmonic
if
f
is a critical point of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
. By considering the variation problem for energy,
f
is a harmonic map if and only if
f
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

f_{z\bar{z}}+\frac{\rho_{w}}{\rho}f_{z}f_{\bar{z}}=0.

It is a well-known fact that if
S
is closed, there exists a unique harmonic diffeomorphism to the identity map from
(S,\sigma)
to
(S,\rho)
. (Eells and Sampson proved the existence of a harmonic map in each homotopy class
[
ES64
]
. Hartman proved its uniquness
[
Har67
]
. Shoen-Yau and Sampson indepndently proved that the harmonic map homotopic to the identity is a diffeomorphism
[
SY78
,
Sam78
]
.)

In the case of punctured surfaces, Lohkamp showed the following.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
Loh91
]
)
.

Let
S
be a surface of finite type, and
\sigma,\rho
be complete hyperbolic metrics of finite area on
S
.
Then, there exists a unique harmonic diffeomorphism
h(\sigma,\rho)\colon(S,\sigma)\to(S,\rho)
such that
h(\sigma,\rho)
is homotopic to the identity and
\mathcal{E}(h(\sigma,\rho))<+\infty
.

2.2.
Compactifications of the Teichmüller space

The
Teichmüller space
of the surface
S
is the quotient space of hyperbolic metrics on
S
by the pull-buck action of diffeomorphisms isotopic to the identity. Let
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
denote the Teichmüller space of
S
. We often write
\rho
simply for
[\rho]\in\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
.

A holomorphic quadratic differential
\Phi
on
(S,\sigma)
is said to be
integrable
, if the
|\Phi|
-area of
(S,\sigma)
is finite, namely

\|\Phi\|=\int_{S}|\Phi|dxdy<+\infty.

Let
\QD(\sigma)
denote the space of integrable holomorphic quadratic differentials on
(S,\sigma)
. For a holomorphic quadratic differential
\Phi
on
(S,\sigma)
,
\Phi
is integrable if and only if
\Phi
has poles of at most one degree at punctures.

Fixing the metric
\sigma
of the domain surface, we set
h(\rho)\coloneqq h(\sigma,\rho)
for
\rho
. Since
(h(\rho)^{\ast}\rho)^{2,0}
is a holomorphic quadratic differenttial on
(S,\sigma)
with finite norm (
[
Loh91
]
Lemma 7), we can define the map
\Phi
as

\Phi:\mathcal{T}_{g,n}\to\QD(\sigma);[\rho]\mapsto(h(\rho)^{\ast}\rho)^{2,0}.

The uniqueness of the harmonic diffeomorphism shows that the map
\Phi
is well-defined. Here, we list very useful well-known formulae.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
J(\rho)=J(h(\rho)),H(\rho)=H(h(\rho)),L(\rho)=L(h(\rho))
, and
\nu(\rho)=\nu(h(\rho))
. Then the following hold:

(I)

J(\rho)=H(\rho)-L(\rho)

(II)

|\Phi(\rho)|/\sigma^{2}=H(\rho)L(\rho)

(III)

|\nu(\rho)|H(\rho)\sigma=|\Phi(\rho)|

(IV)

\nu(\rho)=L(\rho)/H(\rho)

(V)

\Delta_{\sigma}H(\rho)=2H(\rho)-2L(\rho)-2

(VI)

\Delta_{\sigma}L(\rho)=2L(\rho)-2H(\rho)-2
on
S-\{\Phi(\rho)=0\}

Here,

\Delta_{\sigma}\coloneqq\frac{4}{\sigma}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\partial\bar{z}}

is the
Laplace-Beltrami operator
on
(S,\sigma)
.

For the proof of (V) and (VI), see
[
Jos13
, Lemma 3.10.1]
.

The following theorem is shown by Wolf for
n=0
and by Lohkamp for
n>0
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
Wol89
]
,
[
Loh91
]
)
.

\Phi
is a homeomorphism.

Using the Riemann-Roch theorem, we see that
\QD(\sigma)
is a vector space of real dimension
6g-6+2n
. Since a vector space can be compactified by adding an infinite point to the endpoint of every ray from the origin, we can obtain the compactification of
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
through the homeomorphism
\Phi
. The compactification of
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
is called the
harmonic maps compactification
and denoted by
\overline{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{harm}}_{g,n}}
. In other words,

\overline{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{harm}}_{g,n}}=\BQD(\sigma)\cup\SQD(\sigma),

where
\BQD(\sigma)\coloneqq\{\Phi\in\QD(\sigma)\mid\|\Phi\|<1\}
and
\SQD(\sigma)\coloneqq\{\Phi\in\QD(\sigma)\mid\|\Phi\|=1\}
.

On the other hand, Thurston introduced a compactification of
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
, which is called the
Thruston compactification
and denoted by
\overline{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{Th}}_{g,n}}
.
Here, we put a brief description of
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
with reference to
[
FLP12
]
.

A simple closed curve
\gamma
on
S
is
peripheral
if
\gamma
bounds a punctured disk.
If a simple closed curve
\gamma
is neither null-homotopic nor peripheral,
\gamma
is said to be
essential
.
Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(S)
be the set of homotopy classes of essential simple closed curves in
S
.
Given functionals
f,g\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}}
,
f
and
g
are said to be equivalent, if there exists a positive real number
\lambda>0
such that
f=\lambda g
.
The quotient space of the functionals by the equivalence relation is denoted by
P(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}})
, and let
\pi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}}-\{0\}\to P(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}})
be the projection.
Let
\MF^{\ast}
be the set of nontrivial measured foliations on
S
which may have possibly one pronged singularities at the punctures. Then,
\MF^{\ast}
can be embedded into
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}}-\{0\}
by the map
I_{\ast}
which is defined by

I_{\ast}(F)\coloneqq(i(F,\cdot):\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\qquad(F\in\MF^{\ast}),

where
i(F,[\gamma])
is the infimum of the transeverse measure of representatives of
[\gamma]\in\mathcal{C}
. Thus we identify
\MF^{\ast}
with its image in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}}
. Thurston showed that
\pi\circ I_{\ast}(\MF^{\ast})
is homeomorphic to a sphere of dimension
6g-7+2n
.

For
\rho\in\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
, the length functional
\ell_{\ast}(\rho)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}}
is given by

\ell_{\ast}(\rho)([\gamma])\coloneqq\ell_{\rho}([\gamma])=\inf_{\gamma\in[\gamma]}\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)\qquad([\gamma]\in\mathcal{C}).

It is known that
\ell_{\ast}
is an embedding of
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
into
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}}-\{0\}
and
\pi\circ\ell_{\ast}
is still an embedding of
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
into
P(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}})
.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
be the subset
\pi\circ\ell_{\ast}(\mathcal{T}_{g,n})\cup\pi\circ I_{\ast}(\MF^{\ast})
in
P(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{C}})
. We call
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}^{\mathrm{Th}}}
the
Thuston compactification
of
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
.
In fact, it is a manifold with boundary and homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension
6g-6+2n
. The boundary
\pi\circ I_{\ast}(\MF^{\ast})
of the Thurston compactification is denoted by
\PMF
and called the
Thrston boundary
. By the construction, the mapping class group action on
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
extends continuously to the Thurston compactification.

3.
Deformation along harmonic maps rays

3.1.
Norm functions of Beltrami differentials

We denote the inverse homeomorphism
\Phi^{-1}:\QD(\sigma)\to\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
by
\rho
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Phi_{0}\in\SQD(\sigma)
. The
harmonic maps ray
in the direction of
\Phi_{0}
is the ray defined by
\{\rho_{t}\coloneqq\rho(t\Phi_{0})\}_{t>0}
in
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
.

Let
h(t)
denote the unique harmonic diffeomorphism
h(\rho_{t})
homotopic to the identity with
\mathcal{E}(h(\rho_{t}))<+\infty
. We denote the holomorphic energy by
H(t)
, anti-holomorphic energy by
L(t)
, and the Beltrami differentials of
h(t)
by
\nu(t)
. Clearly
\Phi(\rho_{t})=t\Phi_{0}
by the definition. The main purpose of this subsection is to prove the following propositon.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
\Phi_{0}\in\SQD(\sigma)
, let
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t>0}
be the harmonic maps ray in the direction of
\Phi_{0}
. Then, for every nonzero point
p
of
\Phi_{0}
, we have

|\nu(t)(p)|^{2}\nearrow 1\qquad(\text{as}\ t\to\infty).

Remark 3.3
.

Let
M
be the domain surface
(S,\sigma)
. In the case of closed surfaces, Wolf
[
Wol89
]
proved this propositon in the following three steps.

Step 1:

Show that
|\nu(t)(p)|^{2}
converges to
1
at alomost everywhere on
M
.

Step 2:

Show that
(|\nu(t)(p)|^{2})^{\prime}>0
on
M-\{\Phi_{0}(p)=0\}
.

Step 3:

Exclude the possibility that
|\nu(t)(p)|^{2}\to\delta\neq 1
as
t\to\infty
for a nonzero point
p
of
\Phi_{0}
.

If the surface
S
has punctures, we can proceed Step 1 and Step 3 in essentially the same way as the paper
[
Wol89
]
. Therefore we give the proof of Step 2 here. Since the compactness of the surface is critical for Step 2 in
[
Wol89
]
, We prove this inequality for punctured surfaces.

Proposition 3.4
.

For every nonzero point
p\in M-\{\Phi_{0}(q)=0\}
, the derivative
(|\nu(t)(p)|^{2})^{\prime}
with respect to
t
is positive.

Proof.

By the formula (II) in
2.2
, we have

(H(t)L(t))^{\prime}=H^{\prime}(t)L(t)+L^{\prime}(t)H(t)=\frac{2t|\Phi_{0}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{2}{t}H(t)L(t).

Therefore, for every
p\in M-\{\Phi_{0}(p)=0\}
,

(3.1)

\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)}(p)+\frac{L^{\prime}(t)}{L(t)}(p)=\frac{2}{t}.

Next, we introduce some results on the holomorphic energy functions
H(t)
. The following is shown by Lohkamp.

Lemma 3.5

(
[
Loh91
]
Lemma 14)
.

For
t_{1},t_{2}>0
,

\min\left\{1,\frac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}\right\}\cdot H(t_{1})\leq H(t_{2})\leq\max\left\{1,\frac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}\right\}\cdot H(t_{1})\ \ \text{on}\ M.

Using
lemma

3.5
, we show the following.

Lemma 3.6
.

For every
t>0
and
p\in M
, we have

0\leq\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)}(p)<\frac{1}{t},

where
H^{\prime}
is a derivative with respect to
t
.

Proof.

Since the holomorphic energy
H(t)
is positive, by
Lemma

3.5
,

\min\left\{0,\log\frac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}\right\}\leq\log\frac{H(t_{2})}{H(t_{1})}\leq\max\left\{0,\log\frac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}\right\}.

Therefore, we obtain

0\leq\frac{\log H(t_{2})-\log H(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\leq\frac{\log t_{2}-\log t_{1}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}.

Setting
t_{1}=t,t_{2}=t+h
and tending
h
to
0
, we find

(3.2)

0\leq\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)}\leq\frac{1}{t}.

Next, we show that the the second inequality of
eq.

3.2
is strict. If there exists a point
p_{0}\in M
such that
H^{\prime}(t)/H(t)(p_{0})=1/t
, since
p_{0}
maximizes
H^{\prime}(t)/H(t)
, we have

0\geq\Delta_{\sigma}\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)}(p_{0})=\Delta_{\sigma}(\log H(t))^{\prime}=2(H^{\prime}(t)-L^{\prime}(t)).

Therefore, we find
H^{\prime}(t)(p_{0})\leq L^{\prime}(t)(p_{0})
.

This implies that
p_{0}
is not a zero of
\Phi_{0}
. If
p_{0}
is a zero, by
H(t)>0
and
H(t)L(t)=t^{2}|\Phi_{0}(p_{0})|^{2}/\sigma^{2}=0
,

L(t)(p_{0})=0

holds for any
t>0
. Hence we have
H^{\prime}(t)(p_{0})\leq 0
, however this is impossible since
H(t)(p_{0})>0
and
H^{\prime}(t)/H(t)(p_{0})=1/t
. Thus, we find that
\Phi_{0}(p_{0})\neq 0
.

By
H(t)-L(t)>0
, we have

\frac{L^{\prime}(t)}{L(t)}(p_{0})\geq\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{L(t)}(p_{0})>\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)}(p_{0}).

However, this contradicts
H^{\prime}(t)/H(t)(p_{0})=1/t
and
Equation

3.1
.
∎

By
Lemma

3.6
and
Equation

3.1
, we have

\frac{L^{\prime}(t)}{L(t)}>\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)}\ \ \text{on}\ M-\{\Phi_{0}(p)=0\}.

Thus, we obtain

(|\nu(t)|^{2})^{\prime}=\left(\frac{L(t)}{H(t)}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{L^{\prime}(t)H(t)-L(t)H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)^{2}}>0\ \ \text{on}\ M-\{\Phi_{0}(p)=0\}.

This complets the proof.
∎

3.2.
Asymptotic length of horizontal and vertical arcs

Given hyperbolic structures
\sigma,\rho
on
S
, we can isotope
\rho
so that the identity map
\id\colon(S,\sigma)\to(S,\rho)

A holomorphic quadratic differential
\Phi
defines two measured foliations on

which are orhthogonal to each other away from the zeros. By changing a conformal coordinate

on

(resp.

(resp.

If

(resp.

Let
\Phi_{0}\in\SQD(\sigma)

\Phi_{0}

Therefore, the

metric length of a tangent vector

\displaystyle|\nu(t)|^{-1/2}-1

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{|\nu(1)|^{1/2}}(1-|\nu(t)|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{\gamma}|\nu(1)|^{1/2}}\log|\nu(t)|^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\min_{\gamma}|\nu(1)|^{1/2}}C_{0}e^{-\sqrt{t}d(1)/2}.

\displaystyle\ell_{\rho_{t}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma}\left\|\partialderivative{x}\right\|_{\rho_{t}}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma}\{H(t)^{1/2}+L(t)^{1/2}\}\,ds_{\sigma}

The extended homeomorphism

The following lemma is an extension of

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\{\rho_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
be a sequence diverging to
\infty
(i.e. it leaves every compact in
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
). Then
\{\pi\circ\ell(\rho_{i})\}
converges in
\PMF
if and only if
\{\pi\circ\beta\Phi(\rho_{i})\}
converges in
\PMF
. Moreover, their limits in
\PMF
coincide when they converges.

Proof.

We can take finitely many essential simple closed curves
[\gamma_{1}],\ldots,[\gamma_{k}]\in\mathcal{C}
and a positive number
\delta>0
so that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}i(\beta\Phi_{0},[\gamma_{j}])>\delta>0

holds for every
\Phi_{0}\in\SQD(\sigma)
.

Suppose that
\pi\circ\ell(\rho_{n})
converges. Then, there exists a sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\lambda_{n}\ell(\rho_{n})
converges in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\mathcal{C}}
. Therefore, there exists
B>0
such that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle>\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{n}\ell_{\rho_{n}}([\gamma_{j}])

(4.5)

\displaystyle>\lambda_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}i(\beta\Phi(\rho_{n}),[\gamma_{j}])\ \ \ (\text{By \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{prop:FundamentalInequality}})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|^{1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}i(\beta(\Phi(\rho_{n})/\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|),[\gamma_{j}])

\displaystyle>(\lambda_{n}\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|^{1/2})\delta.

Hence, we see that

\lambda_{n}\cdot\eta([\gamma],\varepsilon,\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|)<(B/\delta)\cdot\eta\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|^{-1/2}.

By
Theorem

4.1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\eta\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|^{-1/2}=c_{0}([\gamma],\varepsilon)<\varepsilon.

Morever, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
[\gamma]\in\mathcal{C}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}i(\beta\Phi(\rho_{n}),[\gamma])

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|^{1/2}i(\beta(\Phi(\rho_{n})/\|\Phi(\rho_{n})\|),[\gamma])

\displaystyle<(B/\delta)\max_{\Phi_{0}\in\SQD(\sigma)}i(\beta\Phi_{0},[\gamma]).

Thus by
Theorem

4.1
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
[\gamma]\in\mathcal{C}
,

|\lambda_{n}\ell_{\rho_{n}}([\gamma])-\lambda_{n}i(\beta\Phi(\rho_{n}),[\gamma])|<(k_{0}-1)\lambda_{n}i(\beta\Phi(\rho_{n}),[\gamma])+\lambda_{n}\eta

and

as

\lim_{n\to\infty}(\lambda_{n}\ell_{\rho_{n}}([\gamma])-\lambda_{n}i(\beta\Phi(\rho_{n}),[\gamma]))<\varepsilon.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\beta\Phi(\rho_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\ell(\rho_{n}).

Theorem 4.6
.

The map
\psi
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

We first show that
\psi
is well-defined on
\PMF
. For sequences
\{x_{n}\}
,
\{x_{n}^{\prime}\}
with
x_{n},x_{n}^{\prime}\to x\in\PMF
, there exist

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\beta\Phi(x_{n})\ \text{and}\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\beta\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime}),

and they coincide by
4.5
. Therefore there exist sequences
\{\lambda_{n}\}
,
\{\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}\beta\Phi(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\beta\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime}).

Since
\beta
is a homeomorphism,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\lambda^{\prime}_{n}}^{2}\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime}).

Hence, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(x_{n})}{\|\Phi(x_{n})\|}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi(x_{n})}{{\lambda_{n}}^{2}\|\Phi(x_{n})\|}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\lambda_{n}^{\prime}}^{2}\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime})}{{\lambda_{n}^{\prime}}^{2}\|\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime})\|}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime})}{\|\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime})\|}.

Thus,
\psi
is well-defined.

We secondly show that
\psi
is continuous. In particular, we need to show
\psi
is continuous at a point
x\in\PMF
. Let
\{x_{n}\}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
with
x_{n}\to x
. Then, since
x_{n}
diverges to
\infty

\frac{4\|\Phi(x_{n})\|}{1+4\|\Phi(x_{n})\|}\to 1\ \ \text{as}\ \ n\to\infty.

Thus, we find that the second componet of
\psi
is continuous. The first componet is continuous by the definition of
\psi
.

We thirdly show that
\psi
is injective. The injectivity on
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
follows from the injectivity of
\Phi
. Therefore, we need to show
\psi
is injective on
\PMF
. Suppose that
\psi(x)=\psi(x^{\prime})
for

. Let
x_{n}
and
x_{n}^{\prime}
be sequences in
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
such that
x_{n}\to x\in\PMF
and
x_{n}^{\prime}\to x^{\prime}\in\PMF
as
n\to\infty
.
Since
\psi(x)=\psi(x^{\prime})
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(x_{n})}{\|\Phi(x_{n})\|}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime})}{\|\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime})\|}.

Therefore, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\beta\Phi(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\beta\Phi(x_{n}^{\prime}).

By
4.5
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\ell(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\ell(x_{n}^{\prime}).

This imples

.
Thus we find that
\psi
is injective on
\PMF
.

We forthly show that
\psi
is surjective. The restriction
\psi|_{\mathcal{T}_{g,n}}
is surjective, since
\Phi
is a homeomorphism, so we need to show the surjectivity for
\SQD(\sigma)
.
Taking each
\theta\in\SQD(\sigma)
, we set

. Then

\pi\circ\beta\Phi(x_{n})=\pi\circ\beta(n\theta)=[F_{v}(\theta)]\in\PMF
for every

. Therefore, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\ell(x_{n})=[F_{v}(\theta)]

\psi([F_{v}(\theta)])=\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(x_{n})}{\|\Phi(x_{n})\|},1\right)=(\theta,1).

Thus,
\psi
is surjective.

We finally show that
\psi^{-1}
is continuous. Let
(\theta_{n},r_{n})\to(\theta,1)
as
n\to\infty
for each
\theta\in\SQD(\sigma)
, where
\theta_{n}\in\SQD(\sigma)
and

\psi^{-1}(\theta_{n},r_{n})=\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{r_{n}\theta_{n}}{4(1-r_{n})}\right)\text{ and }\psi^{-1}(\theta,1)=[F_{v}(\theta)].

Therefore, setting

\pi\circ\beta\Phi(x_{n})=[F_{v}(\theta_{n})].

Thus,

\psi^{-1}(\theta,1)=[F_{v}(\theta)]=\lim_{n\to\infty}[F_{v}(\theta_{n})]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi\circ\beta\Phi(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi^{-1}(\theta_{n},r_{n}),
