Modular symmetry of massive free fermions
Max Downinglabel=e1]max.downing@kcl.ac.uk, Sameer Murthylabel=e2]sameer.murthy@kcl.ac.uk, Gerard Wattslabel=e3]gerard.watts@kcl.ac.uk

Abstract
We construct an infinite set of conserved tensor currents of rank 2 ​ n 2n , n = 1 , 2 , … n=1,2,\dots , in the two-dimensional theory of free massive fermions, which are bilinear in the fermionic fields. The one-point functions of these currents on the torus depend on the modular parameter τ \tau and spin structure ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) . We show that, upon scaling the mass m m so as to keep the combination m 2 ​ Im ⁡ ( τ ) m^{2}\operatorname{Im}(\tau) invariant, the one-point functions are non-holomorphic Jacobi forms of weights ( 2 ​ n , 0 ) (2n,0) or ( 0 , 2 ​ n ) (0,2n) and index 0, with respect to the modular parameter τ \tau and elliptic parameter z = α ​ τ + β z=\alpha\tau+\beta . In particular, we express the one-point functions as Kronecker-Eisenstein-type sums over the lattice ℤ ​ τ + ℤ \mathbb{Z}\tau+\mathbb{Z} , which makes the modular symmetry manifest. We show that there is an action of three differential operators on these Jacobi forms which form an 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ​ ( ℝ ) \mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) Lie algebra. Further we show that these Jacobi forms obey three differential equations arising from the representation theory of the Jacobi group.

Introduction

The relation between two-dimensional
conformal field theory (CFT) and modular forms is well-known, for example characters of a CFT are vector-valued modular forms
and the partition function on a torus is invariant under modular transformations
[
7
]
.
The rigorous mathematical development of this relation involves Vertex Operator Algebras (VOAs), as in
[
19
]
, but it is firmly based in the expected properties of physical theories arising in statistical mechanics or string theory.
For conformal fields, the modular properties of expectation values of fields (observables) also follow from the general mathematical theory
[
19
]
.
The known modular properties of massless free fermions fall into this class.

One physical realisation of CFT is in terms of statistical models defined on a torus and their calculation in
terms of traces through a transfer matrix approach. In the continuum limit,
this becomes a trace over a suitable space of states in a quantum field theory. The modular property arises from the
equality of these two different ways to calculate the same physical quantity.
There is an alternative point of view in physics given by the functional integral, which—although
not rigorous—leads to a much more intuitive understanding of the modular properties of the free
fermion or, more generally, of any CFT.
Modular transformations are
simply a combination of large diffeomorphisms of the torus and
scale transformations. The former is a classical symmetry of any diffeomorphism-invariant theory
and the latter is a symmetry of any conformal field theory.
The combination, therefore, is a symmetry transformation of the underlying functional integral of the CFT,
and any observable of such a theory should therefore transform in a covariant manner under it. For example,
the partition function of massless free fermions
on a torus leads to theta functions, one-point functions lead to Eisenstein series, and so on.

It has long been recognised that partition functions of massive theories should also have modular properties.
Indeed, this was shown for the partition function of the off-critical Ising model—which is equivalent to the
theory of massive free fermions—by Itzykson and Saleur in 1987
[
17
]
, see also
[
11
]
and more recently
[
12
]
.
The question arose again in connection with string theory when it was realised that strings in a PP-wave background in the light-cone Green-Schwarz formalism were equivalent to
free massive fields
[
14
]
and modular covariance of open strings
[
5
]
and
closed strings
[
18
]
was shown. This in turn led to massive Maass forms, defined in
[
3
]
and further explored in
[
2
]
.

There is, however, no similar general mathematical theory of modular properties of massive field theories (the Ising results come from direct calculation), but we can still use the physical intuition from the path-integral approach.
The main difference is that scale transformations are explicitly broken. In general this will correspond to a renormalisation group flow in the space of massive field theories, but in the case of free field theories (such as massive fermions) this simply corresponds to a change in the mass parameter, and the above intuition can very easily be extended.

In this paper we show that there is an infinite class of observables in the massive free fermion theory
which lead to modular forms. These modular forms take the form of Kronecker-Eisenstein series, which
are non-holomorphic deformations of the Eisenstein
series occurring in free fermion CFTs, and reduce to them in the massless limit.
In the rest of this introduction, we briefly summarise the arguments of modular symmetry for massive
fermion theories, focusing on one-point functions. We review the functional integral explanation of
modular symmetry in Appendix
A
.

In physics, a one-point function is an expression

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{\widehat{\cal O}}\bigr\rangle_{\cal M}^{\cal T}\;,

(1)

which is determined by a quantum field theory
{\cal T}
, an operator
{\cal O}
in that theory and the manifold
{\cal M}
on which the theory is defined. If one considers a coordinate transformation, each of
{\cal T}
,
{\cal O}
and
{\cal M}
can change leading to identities of the form

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{\widehat{\cal O}}\bigr\rangle_{\cal M}^{\cal T}=\bigl\langle{\widehat{\cal O}^{\prime}}\bigr\rangle_{\cal M^{\prime}}^{\cal T^{\prime}}\;.

(2)

By suitable choices of
\{{\cal T,O,M}\}
this will lead to functions with well-defined modular properties.

In this paper we always take
{\cal M}
to be a two-dimensional torus defined as the quotient of the complex plane

\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}R\tau+\mathbb{Z}R)
.
Here the complex number
\tau
is the complex modulus while the positive real number
R
is the Kähler modulus.
We will denote a one-point function on such a torus by
\bigl\langle{\cdots}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,R}
.
This is long understood to lead to modular forms in the case that
{\cal T}
is a conformal field theory.
If
\cal T
is a conformal field theory and the coordinate transformation is a combined scale, rotation and translation, by definition one has
{\cal T}={\cal T^{\prime}}
. Taking
\cal O
to be a “scaling field”
\phi
with conformal dimensions
(h,\overline{h})
leads, through combining suitable translations, rotations and scalings, to the result

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{\phi}\bigr\rangle_{\widehat{\tau},1}^{\cal T}\;=\;(c\tau+d)^{h}(c\overline{\tau}+d)^{\overline{h}}\bigl\langle{\phi}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,1}^{\cal T}

(3)

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}\;\equiv\;\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\,,\qquad{\Big({\small\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}}\Big)\in\text{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\,,

i.e. the function

\displaystyle\tau\mapsto\bigl\langle{\phi}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,1}^{\cal T}\;,

(4)

is a non-holomorphic modular form of weights
(h,\overline{h})
.

In this paper we are interested in generalisations of this construction when
{\cal T}
is no longer a conformal field theory but depends on a mass scale
m
.
In particular, we
consider the theory of two-dimensional, two component
fermion
\psi
of mass
m
(with components
\Psi
and
\overline{\Psi}
). As explained in Appendix
B
,
this can either be formulated in Lorentzian signature as a Majorana fermion, or in Euclidean signature as a fermion obeying a Majorana-like relation and which is
formally described by the Lagrangian from
[
10
]

\mathcal{L}\;=\;\Psi\overline{\partial}\Psi-\overline{\Psi}\partial\overline{\Psi}+m\Psi\overline{\Psi}\,.

(5)

Under a scaling transformation, the kinetic terms in (
5
) are still invariant, but the mass term is not.
However, the scaling can be easily absorbed into the mass term as

m\to|c\tau+d|\,m\,.

(6)

This has the result that one has to pay close attention to the dependence of the theory
{\cal T}
on the size
R
of the torus and instead of (
3
) we instead find relations of the form

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{\phi}\bigr\rangle^{{\cal T}(\widehat{m})}_{\widehat{\tau},1}\;=\;F\bigl\langle{\phi}\bigr\rangle^{{\cal T}(m)}_{\tau,1}\,.

(7)

where
F
contains factors arising from the rotational and scaling properties of the field
\phi
.

We now summarise the main results presented in this paper. In Section
2
we describe suitable fields in the free Majorana fermion model. These fields, defined in (
21
), are the components of a conserved tensor field which is bilinear in the fermions. They are denoted by
T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}
for
n=0,1,\dots
.

In Section
2.1
we calculate the one point functions on the torus of these fields in the different spin structures (where we have set
R=1
). The one point functions (
46
) are Kronecker–Eisenstein lattice sums that we record here for convenience

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,m;\alpha,\beta}{=}\left(\frac{m}{2}\right)^{2n}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{(r,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left(\frac{\overline{\tau}r-\ell}{\tau r-\ell}\right)^{\pm n}K_{2n}(m|r\tau-\ell|)\,{\rm e}^{2\pi i(\alpha\ell+\beta r)}\;,

(8)

where
\alpha,\beta\in\{0,\frac{1}{2}\}
and the prime indicates we are excluding the origin from the lattice sum. These are the non-holomorphic modular forms we study in the rest of the paper.

In Section
2.2
we extend this calculation to related fields in the free massive Dirac fermion for twisted sectors. The components of the conserved tensor are now denoted
T^{D}_{(2n,\pm 2n)}
and are defined in (
52
). The one point functions (
55
) of these fields take the same form as (
8
) except now
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Finally, in Section
2.3
we derive the modular transformation properties of the Dirac fermion expectation values (equations (
57
) and (
58
))

\begin{split}\bigl\langle{T^{D}_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau+1,m;\alpha,\beta-\alpha}&\;=\;\bigl\langle{T^{D}_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,m;\alpha,\beta}\;,\\
\bigl\langle{T^{D}_{(2n,2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{-1/\tau,|\tau|m;-\beta,\alpha}&\;=\;\tau^{2n}\bigl\langle{T^{D}_{(2n,2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,m;\alpha,\beta}\;,\\
\bigl\langle{T^{D}_{(2n,-2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{-1/\tau,|\tau|m;-\beta,\alpha}&\;=\;\overline{\tau}^{2n}\bigl\langle{T^{D}_{(2n,-2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,m;\alpha,\beta}\;.\end{split}

(9)

The mass
m
transforms as a weight
(1,1)
modular form so we introduce an invariant mass
\mu\;=\;m^{2}\operatorname{Im}(\tau)\;=\;m^{2}\tau_{2}
and write our one point functions in terms of this new parameter.
The massless limit of our formulae is discussed in Section
2.4
.

In Section
3
we turn to the differential equations satisfied by the functions we found in Section
2
. We show that the one point functions are massive Maass and massive Jacobi forms. (The relevant definitions were first given in
[
3
]
and are repeated in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
respectively.) In Section
3.1
we discuss an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
action on the functions. The differential operators (
74
)

X_{+}\;=\;X_{+}^{(k)}\;=\;(\tau-\overline{\tau})\partial_{\tau}+k\,,\qquad X_{-}\;=\;X_{-}^{(\overline{k})}\;=\;-(\tau-\overline{\tau})\partial_{\overline{\tau}}+\overline{k}\,,

(10)

act on modular forms of weight
(k,\overline{k})
, and together with
Z=(k-\overline{k})
form a representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
(see (
76
)). They can then be used to move between the different one point functions (
79
)

\begin{split}&X_{\pm}\bigl\langle{T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}{=}-4\tau_{2}\,\partial_{\mu}\bigl\langle{T_{(2n+2,\pm(2n+2))}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}\;,\\
&X_{\mp}\bigl\langle{T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}{=}\frac{1}{4\tau_{2}}(-\mu^{2}\partial_{\mu}{+}2\mu(n-1))\bigl\langle{T_{(2n-2,\pm(2n-2))}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}\;.\end{split}

(11)

In Section
3.2
we give the relation to massive Maass forms. We define the Laplace operator (
83
)

\Delta_{\tau}\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}(X_{+}X_{-}+X_{-}X_{+})\;,

(12)

and show

\Delta_{\tau}\bigl\langle{T_{2n,\pm 2n}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu,\alpha,\beta}=\left(-\mu^{2}\partial_{\mu}^{2}+2\mu(n-1)\partial_{\mu}+n\right)\bigl\langle{T_{2n,\pm 2n}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu,\alpha,\beta}\;.

(13)

Hence the one point functions are massive Maass forms.

In Section
3.3
we discuss the differential equations arising from the Jacobi group. We combine the spin structure parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
into a complex Jacobi variable
z=\alpha\tau+\beta
. Introducing the differential operators (
89
)

Y_{+}\;=\;i\sqrt{\frac{\tau-\overline{\tau}}{2{\rm i}}}\,\partial_{z}\;,\quad Y_{-}\;=\;-i\sqrt{\frac{\tau-\overline{\tau}}{2{\rm i}}}\,\partial_{\overline{z}}\,,

(14)

and modifying the
X_{\pm}
operators (
88
)

X_{+}=(\tau-\overline{\tau})\partial_{\tau}+(z-\overline{z})\partial_{z}+k\,,\quad X_{-}=-(\tau-\overline{\tau})\partial_{\overline{\tau}}-(z-\overline{z})\partial_{\overline{z}}+\overline{k}\,,

(15)

gives us a representation of the Jacobi group. The Laplacian in the Jacobi variable (
93
) is

\Delta_{z}=Y_{+}Y_{-}+Y_{-}Y_{+}\;,

(16)

and the Casimir of the Jacobi group is

C_{\tau,z}=\!\frac{1}{2}(Y_{+}^{2}X_{-}+\!X_{-}Y_{+}^{2}-\!Y_{-}^{2}X_{+}-\!X_{+}Y_{-}^{2}+\!(k{-}\overline{k})\!(Y_{+}Y_{-}+\!Y_{-}Y_{+}\!)\!).

(17)

The one point functions are shown to satisfy

\displaystyle C_{\tau,z}\bigl\langle{T_{2n,\pm 2n}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu,z}=0\;,

(18)

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}\bigl\langle{T_{2n,\pm 2n}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu,z}=-8\pi^{2}\left(\mu\partial_{\mu}^{2}-(2n-1)\partial_{\mu}\right)\bigl\langle{T_{2n,\pm 2n}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu,z}\;,

(19)

and therefore are massive Jacobi forms.

4
contains our conclusions. We include a series of appendices containing technical details.

2.1
One-point functions of fermion bilinears

Now we calculate the one-point functions of
T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}
and
T_{(0,0)}
in the Hamiltonian formalism.
We begin by quantising the free fermion.
Consider the 2 component fermionic field
(\overline{\Psi}(x),\Psi(x))

obeying the general twisted boundary condition

\displaystyle\Psi(z+R,\overline{z}+R)={\rm e}^{2\pi i\alpha}\,\Psi(z,\overline{z})\;,\;\;\overline{\Psi}(z+R,\overline{z}+R)={\rm e}^{2\pi i\alpha}\,\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})\;,

(26)

for
\alpha=0
or
\tfrac{1}{2}
. Such a field has the following Fourier expansion,

\Psi(z,\overline{z})\;=\;\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}{\cal A}_{k}(z-\overline{z})\,{\rm e}^{\frac{\pi ik}{R}(z+\overline{z})}\;,

(27)

where
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is a 2 component spinor that only depends on
z-\overline{z}
(similarly for
\overline{\Psi}
).
{\cal A}_{k}
is then fixed by the equations of motion. The solution to the equations of motion following from the Lagrangian (
20
)
is, upon imposing a Majorana-like condition explained in appendix
B
,

\begin{split}&\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z}){=}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\!\!\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\!\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{-}}{2\omega_{k}}}\!\left(\!\Psi_{k}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{\frac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{+}z-\omega_{k}^{-}\overline{z})}{+}\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-\frac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{+}z-\omega_{k}^{-}\overline{z})}\!\right)\,,\\
&\Psi(z,\overline{z}){=}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\!\!\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\!\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{+}}{2\omega_{k}}}\!\left(\!\Psi_{k}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{\frac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{+}z-\omega_{k}^{-}\overline{z})}{+}\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-\frac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{+}z-\omega_{k}^{-}\overline{z})}\!\right)\,,\end{split}

(28)

where

\omega_{k}\;=\;\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\frac{2\pi k}{R}\right)^{2}}\;,\qquad\omega_{k}^{\pm}\;=\;\omega_{k}\pm\frac{2\pi k}{R}\;.

(29)

In the quantum theory one imposes the anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\{\Psi_{k},\Psi_{\ell}^{\dagger}\}\;=\;\delta_{k,\ell}\,,

(30)

with all other anti commutators vanishing. In terms of the operators
\Psi_{k}
,
the Hamiltonian and momentum of the free fermion theory are given by

\begin{split}H&\;=\;\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\omega_{k}\left(\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\Psi_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\;=\;\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\omega_{k}\,\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\Psi_{k}+E_{0}\;,\\
P&\;=\;\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\frac{2\pi k}{R}\,\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\Psi_{k}\;.\end{split}

(31)

The zero-point energy is formally given by the series
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\omega_{k}
, which is, a priori, divergent.
One can regularise this sum, and this leads to

E_{0}\;=\;\frac{m}{\pi}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}K_{1}(mR\ell)\cos(2\pi\ell\alpha)\,,

(32)

where
K_{1}(x)
is a modified Bessel function of the second kind.
We present the calculation in Appendix
D
.
As it turns out, this value of
E_{0}
is precisely the one consistent with modular invariance.

We define the
trace in the twisted sector
\alpha,\beta
for any operator
\mathcal{O}
in the theory as

\text{Tr}_{\alpha,\beta}\;\mathcal{O}\;=\;\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}\,{\rm e}^{-2\pi i\bigl(\beta+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)F}\;\mathcal{O}\,,

where the Hilbert space

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}{\rm e}^{-2\pi i\bigl(\beta+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)F}=-{\rm e}^{2\pi i\beta}{\rm e}^{-2\pi i\bigl(\beta+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)F}\Psi_{k}\;.

We define the partition function of the theory in the twisted sector

Z_{\alpha,\beta}(a,L,R)\;=\;\text{Tr}_{\alpha,\beta}\,\exp\bigl(-LH+iaP\bigr)\,.

Our goal is to calculate the normalised one-point functions

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{a,L,R,m;\alpha,\beta}\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha,\beta}}\;\text{Tr}_{\alpha,\beta}\,\Bigl(T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}\,\exp\bigl(-LH+iaP\bigr)\Bigr)\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{T_{(0,0)}}\bigr\rangle_{a,L,R,m;\alpha,\beta}\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha,\beta}}\;\text{Tr}_{\alpha,\beta}\,\Bigl(T_{(0,0)}\,\exp\bigl(-LH+iaP\bigr)\Bigr)\,.

(37)

The

We first gather some basic building blocks.
A short calculation, using the anticommutation relations (
30
), shows that
for any
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha
we have

\begin{split}&\text{Tr}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\Psi_{\ell}\,{\rm e}^{-LH+iaP)})\;=\;\delta_{k,\ell}\,\frac{-{\rm e}^{-L\omega_{k}+2\pi i\frac{a}{R}k+2\pi i\beta}}{1-{\rm e}^{-L\omega_{k}+2\pi i\frac{a}{R}k+2\pi i\beta}}\,Z_{\alpha,\beta}(a,L,R)\,,\\
&\text{Tr}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Psi_{k}\Psi_{\ell}\,{\rm e}^{-LH+iaP})\;=\;0\,,\qquad\text{Tr}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\Psi_{\ell}^{\dagger}\,{\rm e}^{-LH+iaP})\;=\;0\,.\end{split}

From (

2.3
Modular transformation property

We now discuss the modular properties of the one-point functions

In this section we make comments on certain structural properties of the massive fermion one-point

The first of these equations involves the hyperbolic Laplacian and the other two involve a Laplacian in the elliptic variable

We show below how the functions arising from the massive theory obey generalisations of these equations.
Furthermore, we show that there are raising and lowering operators that move us through the members of the family.

algebra
[
6
]
,

[X_{+},X_{-}]\;=\;Z\,,\quad[Z,X_{\pm}]\;=\;\pm 2X_{\pm}\,.

(76)

These operators factor through powers of
\tau_{2}
as follows,

X_{\pm}(\tau_{2}^{k}f(\tau,\overline{\tau}))\;=\;\tau_{2}^{k}X_{\pm}f(\tau,\overline{\tau})\,,\qquad Z(\tau_{2}^{k}f(\tau,\overline{\tau}))\;=\;\tau_{2}^{k}Zf(\tau,\overline{\tau})\,.

(77)

The operators
X_{\pm}
relate the various one-point functions to each other.
To see this, note that they act on the functions
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)
as follows,

\begin{split}&X_{+}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)\;=\;(-\mu\partial_{\mu}+n)\mathcal{F}^{n+1,m-1}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)\,,\\
&X_{-}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)\;=\;(-\mu\partial_{\mu}+m)\mathcal{F}^{n-1,m+1}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)\,.\end{split}

(78)

Using the relation (
73
), we obtain, for
n=0,1,2,\dots
,

\begin{split}&X_{\pm}\bigl\langle{T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}{=}-4\tau_{2}\,\partial_{\mu}\bigl\langle{T_{(2n+2,\pm(2n+2))}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}\;,\\
&X_{\mp}\bigl\langle{T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}{=}\frac{1}{4\tau_{2}}(-\mu^{2}\partial_{\mu}{+}2\mu(n-1))\bigl\langle{T_{(2n-2,\pm(2n-2))}}\bigr\rangle_{\!\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}\;.\end{split}

(79)

We can therefore move along the two edges of the cone
described in Figure
1
using the
X_{\pm}
operators, thus relating all the one-point functions
with purely holomorphic weight to each other. (Similarly, the one-point functions with purely
anti-holomorphic weight are all related to each other).
Notice that the first and second lines of (
79
) also hold
for
n=0
and
n=1
, respectively, and this
allows us to go around the apex of the cone in Figure
1
, thus relating
all one-point functions with holomorphic or anti-holomorphic weight.

Furthermore, we can relate all these one point functions to the partition function. In
[
3
]
the following expression for the partition function is derived,

\log Z_{\alpha,\beta}(\tau,\overline{\tau})=-\frac{\sqrt{\mu\tau_{2}}}{\pi}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{(\ell,r)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{1}{|\tau r-\ell|}K_{1}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\tau_{2}}}|\tau r-\ell|\!\right)\!e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\alpha\ell+\beta r)}\;,

(80)

where the prime indicates as before that we exclude the origin from the sum. One can explicitly see the action of the differential operators:

X_{\pm}\log Z_{\alpha,\beta}\;=\;-\frac{2\tau_{2}}{\pi}\bigl\langle{T_{(2,\pm 2)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}\;,

(81)

\partial_{\mu}\log Z_{\alpha,\beta}\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\bigl\langle{T_{(0,0)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}\,,

(82)

as mentioned at the beginning of Section
2
.

In the massless limit, the one-point functions are purely holomorphic (anti-holomorphic) Kronecker-Eisenstein series, and therefore are annihilated by
X_{-}
(
X_{+}
).

3.2
Massive generalisation of (harmonic) Maass forms

The paper
[
3
]
introduces a notion of
massive Maass forms
as follows.
Introduce the hyperbolic Laplacian of weight
(k,\overline{k})
which acts on a modular form of weight
(k,\overline{k})

\begin{split}\Delta_{\tau,k,\overline{k}}&\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}(X_{+}X_{-}+X_{-}X_{+})\\
&\;=\;(\tau-\overline{\tau})^{2}\partial_{\tau}\partial_{\overline{\tau}}+(\tau-\overline{\tau})(k\partial_{\overline{\tau}}-\overline{k}\partial_{\tau})+\tfrac{1}{2}(k+\overline{k}-2k\overline{k})\,.\end{split}

(83)

This Laplacian factors through powers of
\tau_{2}
as follows,

\Delta_{\tau,k-n,\overline{k}-n}(\tau_{2}^{n}f(\tau,\overline{\tau}))=\tau_{2}^{n}\Delta_{\tau,k,\overline{k}}f(\tau,\overline{\tau})\;.

(84)

According to Definition 1 in
[
3
]
, a function
f(\mu,\tau,\overline{\tau})
is
called a massive Maass form of weight
(k,\overline{k})
if for fixed
\mu
it transforms as a modular
form of weight
(k,\overline{k})
and satisfies the partial differential equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{\tau,k,\overline{k}}f=(g_{2}(\mu)\partial_{\mu}^{2}+g_{1}(\mu)\partial_{\mu}+g_{0}(\mu))f\;,

(85)

for some smooth functions
g_{i}(\mu)
.

Using the expression (
83
) for the Laplacian in terms of the raising and lowering operators,
and their action (
78
),
it is easy to check that the functions
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)
satisfy (
85
)
with

g_{2}(\mu)\;=\;-\mu^{2}\,,\quad g_{1}(\mu)\;=\;\mu(n+m-2)\quad g_{0}(\mu)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}(n+m-2nm)\,,

(86)

i.e., they are weight
(n,m)
massive Maass forms. Note that for the special cases
m=-n
, which are related to the one point functions the equation simplifies to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\tau,n,-n}\,\mathcal{F}^{n,-n}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)\;=\;(-\mu^{2}\partial_{\mu}^{2}-2\mu\partial_{\mu}+n^{2})\mathcal{F}^{n,-n}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)\,,

(87)

By Lemma 3.1 in
[
3
]
the function
\mu^{k}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(\alpha\tau+\beta,\tau)

is also a massive Maass form of weight
(n,m)
for any
k
, hence the one point
functions
\bigl\langle{T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau,\mu;\alpha,\beta}
are all massive Maass forms.

In the massless limit the one-point functions become either holomorphic or anti-holomorphic in
\tau
.
In both these cases, the second line of (
83
) makes it clear that the hyperbolic Laplacian acts
trivially on the one point functions as multiplication by half the modular weight.

3.3
Differential equations arising from the Jacobi group

As was shown in
[
3
]
, the partition function of the massive fermion theory
fits into the structure of Jacobi Maass forms as presented in
[
16
]
. Here we show that the
one-point functions also fit into the same structure.

In this subsection we return to using
z
as a variable.
The operators
X_{\pm}
defined in (
74
) are written as

X_{+}=(\tau-\overline{\tau})\partial_{\tau}+(z-\overline{z})\partial_{z}+k\,,\quad X_{-}=-(\tau-\overline{\tau})\partial_{\overline{\tau}}-(z-\overline{z})\partial_{\overline{z}}+\overline{k}\,.

(88)

We also introduce the derivatives

Y_{+}\;=\;i\sqrt{\frac{\tau-\overline{\tau}}{2{\rm i}}}\,\partial_{z}\;,\quad Y_{-}\;=\;-i\sqrt{\frac{\tau-\overline{\tau}}{2{\rm i}}}\,\partial_{\overline{z}}\,,

(89)

which map a modular form of weight
(k,\overline{k})
to one of weight
(k\pm\tfrac{1}{2},\overline{k}\mp\tfrac{1}{2})
.
Acting on
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)
with the
Y_{\pm}
operators gives

\begin{split}&Y_{+}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)\;=\;-\frac{i\pi}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\mathcal{F}^{n,m-1}(z,\tau)\;,\\
&Y_{-}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)\;=\;-\frac{i\pi}{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\mathcal{F}^{n-1,m}(z,\tau)\;.\end{split}

(90)

We now define the Laplacian in the
z
space

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}=-i(\tau-\overline{\tau})\partial_{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}\,,

(91)

and the Casimir operator

\displaystyle C_{\tau,z,k,\overline{k}}=\frac{\tau{-}\overline{\tau}}{2i}\!\left(\!(\tau{-}\overline{\tau})(\partial_{\overline{\tau}}\partial_{z}^{2}{+}\partial_{\tau}\partial_{\overline{z}}^{2}){+}(z{-}\overline{z})(\partial_{\overline{z}}\partial_{z}^{2}{+}\partial_{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}^{2}){+}(\partial_{z}{+}\partial_{\overline{z}})(k\partial_{\overline{z}}{-}\overline{k}\partial_{z})\!\right)\!\,.

(92)

As in
[
6
]
these operators can be written in terms of the
X_{\pm}
and
Y_{\pm}
above as

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}=Y_{+}Y_{-}+Y_{-}Y_{+}\,,

(93)

\displaystyle C_{\tau,z,k,\overline{k}}=\!\frac{1}{2}(Y_{+}^{2}X_{-}+\!X_{-}Y_{+}^{2}-\!Y_{-}^{2}X_{+}-\!X_{+}Y_{-}^{2}+\!(k{-}\overline{k})\!(Y_{+}Y_{-}+\!Y_{-}Y_{+}\!)\!).

(94)

These operators factor through powers of
\tau_{2}
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}(\tau_{2}^{k}f(z,\tau))\;=\;\tau_{2}^{k}\Delta_{z}f(z,\tau)\;,

(95)

\displaystyle C_{\tau,z,k-n,\overline{k}-n}(\tau_{2}^{n}f(z,\tau))\;=\;\tau_{2}^{n}C_{\tau,z,k,\overline{k}}f(z,\tau)\;.

(96)

We now consider the definition of a
massive Maass-Jacobi form
as given in
[
3
]

and apply it to our case with index 0.
We say that a function
f(\mu,\tau,\overline{\tau},z,\overline{z})
is a
massive
Maass-Jacobi form
of index
0
and weight
(k,\overline{k})
if for fixed
\mu
it transforms as a Jacobi form
of index 0 and weight
(k,\overline{k})
and satisfies the partial differential equations

\displaystyle C_{\tau,z,k,\overline{k}}f\;=\;0\;,

(97)

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}f\;=\;(G_{2}(\mu)\partial_{\mu}^{2}+G_{1}(\mu)\partial_{\mu}+G_{0}(\mu))f\;,

(98)

for some smooth
functions
G_{n}(\mu)
.
5
5

5

The paper
[
3
]
also make a remark about higher order
differential operators on the right-hand side of (
98
), but we do not discuss this here.

This definition is given in
[
3
]
for massive Jacobi-Maass
forms of general index
(m,\overline{m})
using a suitable modification of the differential
operators
C_{\tau,z,k,\overline{k}}
and
\Delta_{z}
above, but we will not need this in our discussion.

Now we discuss the family
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)
in the above context.
Using the expression (
94
), it is easy to check that the
functions
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)
are annihilated by the Casimir operator

\displaystyle C_{\tau,z,n,m}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)\;=\;0\,.

(99)

The condition that
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)
satisfies (
98
) implies that
there are functions
G_{n}(\mu)
with

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)\;=\;(G_{2}(\mu)\partial_{\mu}^{2}+G_{1}(\mu)\partial_{\mu}+G_{0}(\mu))\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)\;.

(100)

This is equivalent to the function
h(x)
satisfying the following differential equation

2\pi^{2}xh(x)+\mu G_{0}(\mu)h(x)+xG_{1}(\mu)h^{\prime}(x)+\frac{x^{2}}{\mu}G_{2}(\mu)h^{\prime\prime}(x)\;=\;0\;.

(101)

Since the first term is independent of
\mu
for every
x
, the same should be true for all the terms which forces the
G_{n}
to all take the form

G_{n}(\mu)\;=\;(2\pi)^{2}\lambda_{n}\,\mu^{n-1}\;,

(102)

where
\lambda_{n}
are constants. This gives us the differential equation

(x+\lambda_{0})h(x)+\lambda_{1}xh^{\prime}(x)+\lambda_{2}x^{2}h^{\prime\prime}(x)\;=\;0\;.

(103)

If the function
h(x)
satisfies the above differential equation, the function
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(h;z,\tau)
satisfies

\Delta_{z}\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)\;=\;(2\pi)^{2}\left(\lambda_{2}\,\mu\,\partial_{\mu}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\,\partial_{\mu}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\mu}\right)\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)\;.

(104)

The above differential equation can be rewritten, in the variable
y=2{\rm i}\sqrt{{x}/{\lambda_{2}}}
and upon setting
f(y)=\left(\tfrac{i}{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}\,y\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{2}}}h\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\lambda_{2}y^{2}\right)
, as

\displaystyle y^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(y)+yf^{\prime}(y)-(y^{2}+\nu^{2})f(y)\;=\;0\;,\quad\nu^{2}\;=\;\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}-1\right)^{2}-\frac{4\lambda_{0}}{\lambda_{2}}\,.

(105)

The above equation is the modified Bessel equation whose two linearly independent solutions
are
I_{\nu}(y)
and
K_{\nu}(y)
, the modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind respectively.
Since the function
I_{\nu}(y)
grows exponentially and
K_{\nu}(y)
decays exponentially as
y\to\infty
(for
y\in\mathbb{R}
), the solution to (
103
)
can only contain
K_{\nu}
for convergence of the lattice sum (
71
).
Note that for
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}=-4
and
\lambda_{0}=(2n)^{2}
,
the exponentially decaying solution to (
103
) is
h(x)=K_{2n}(\sqrt{x})
,
which is precisely the value (see (
73
)) for which
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu}(z,\tau)
is related to the one point functions of the massive fermions.

It is also interesting to note the relation of the mass term
m^{2}\tau_{2}=\mu
to this Casimir operator.
If we replace the parameter
\mu
with
\mu f(\tau,\overline{\tau})
where the function
f(\tau,\overline{\tau})

is a weight
(0,0)
modular form then the function
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu f}(z,\tau)
is again a
Jacobi form of index 0 and weight
(n,m)
.
Now, applying the Casimir to the series definition of
\mathcal{F}^{n,m}_{\mu f}
shows that it is only annihilated
if
f(\tau,\overline{\tau})
satisfies the partial differential equation

\displaystyle(\overline{\tau}r-\ell)^{2}\partial_{\overline{\tau}}f+(\tau r-\ell)^{2}\partial_{\tau}f\;=\;0\,,

(106)

for every
r,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Clearly, the only solution is
f=
constant.
This means that if we want the one point function to be annihilated by
the Casimir the only option for the mass term is
m^{2}\tau_{2}=\mu
.

4
Conclusions

On physical grounds, the torus one-point expectation values of fields in massive quantum field theories should have well-defined modular properties. We have calculated these for a range of tensor fields in the massive free fermion model and shown that, upon a rescaling of the mass, these are indeed modular forms. In addition, these one point functions obey three differential equations that arise from the representation theory of the Jacobi group.

In the massless limit,
the fields
T_{(2n,\pm 2n)}
we have considered become chiral fields (for
n>0
),
but it is well known that one point functions of non-chiral fields also have well-defined modular properties

[
15
,
9
]
, as do multi-point functions of chiral fields
[
19
]
.
Obvious further ideas are to try to extend our results to similar cases in the massive free fermion/Ising model.

All these ideas could be extended, in principle,
to other massive field theories. However, since there are few examples where the partition functions
and correlation functions are known explicitly, this may prove challenging.
More generally one would like to obtain a mathematical proof that such modularity properties must exist in massive theories (in the way that
[
19
]
gives a general proof for rational conformal field theories).
[
19
]
uses the underlying algebraic structure when proving modular invariance and hence if one wished to proceed in a similar fashion then the underlying algebra of the massive theories would have to be well understood.

Most immediately, this project was, in part, motivated by the desire to analyse generalised Gibbs ensembles in the massive free fermion model, extending the work in
[
8
]
. Since the one-point functions do indeed have the same modular properties as those in the massless CFT it is very likely that the GGE in the massive case will have similar modular properties to those discussed in
[
8
]
. We hope to return to this in the future.

Appendix A
One-point functions and modular forms

In this appendix we explain the functional integral argument for why the one-point function of any tensor field in a
translation-invariant theory on the torus is a modular form.

By a torus of modular parameter
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
, we mean the elliptic
curve
E_{\tau}=\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}\tau+\mathbb{Z})
. We use the complex coordinates
x=(x^{1},x^{2})=(z,\overline{z})
on the torus.
The modular group has an action on the modular parameter as

\gamma:\tau\;\mapsto\;\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\;\equiv\;\widehat{\tau}\,,\qquad\gamma\;=\;\Big(\,{\small\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}}\,\Big)\in\text{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\,.

(107)

The action (
107
) relates equivalent tori. This equivalence is implemented by the map

(z,\overline{z})\;\to\;\bigl(\,\widehat{z}\,,\,\widehat{}\overline{z}\,\bigr)\;=\;\Bigl(\,\frac{z}{c\tau+d}\,,\frac{\overline{z}}{c\overline{\tau}+d}\,\Bigr)\,,

(108)

which maps the torus
E_{\tau}
to
E_{\widehat{\tau}}
. If

f_{\widehat{\tau}}

is a well-defined function on
E_{\widehat{\tau}}
,
then
f_{\tau}(z,\overline{z})=f_{\widehat{\tau}}(\widehat{z},\widehat{\overline{z}})
is well-defined on
E_{\tau}
.
We can say that
f_{\tau}(z,\overline{z})
is invariant under the simultaneous transformations (
107
), (
108
).

The measure

d\mu\;=\;\frac{{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}\overline{z}}{\operatorname{Im}(\tau)}\;,

(109)

is also invariant under such a simultaneous transformation.

We want to study the properties of the function
\tau\mapsto\bigl\langle{T}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}
where
T
is a tensor field.
A tensor field
T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}(x)
transforms, under a transformation
x\mapsto\widehat{x}
as

T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}(x)\;\to\;\frac{\partial x^{\nu_{1}}}{\partial\widehat{x}^{\mu_{1}}}\dots\frac{\partial x^{\nu_{n}}}{\partial\widehat{x}^{\mu_{n}}}\,T_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}(x)\,.

(110)

Note that the coordinate transformation (
108
) acts on
z,\overline{z}
separately, so that the
components
T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}
of the tensor diagonalise the action of this transformation on the tensor field.
(In other words, under the transformation (
108
), the tensor components on the two sides
of (
110
) have the same indices.)
In what follows, we focus on transformations that consist of a combination of rotations and scaling transformations, i.e.,

(z,\overline{z})\;\to\;\bigl(z\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta},\overline{z}\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}\bigr)\,,\qquad(z,\overline{z})\;\to\;(rz,r\overline{z})\,,\qquad r,\theta\in\mathbb{R}\,,

(111)

or, more compactly,

(z,\overline{z})\;\to\;\bigl(\lambda z,\overline{\lambda}\overline{z}\bigr)\,,\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\,.

(112)

A component of the tensor field
T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}
with
m
of its legs
\mu_{i}=z

and
n-m
of them with
\mu_{i}=\overline{z}

transforms under (
112
) as

T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}\bigl(z,\overline{z}\bigr)\;\to\;\lambda^{-m}\,{\overline{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}^{m-n}\,T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}\bigl(z,\overline{z}\bigr)\,.

(113)

Now we consider a quantum theory of fields (generically denoted by
\phi
below)
defined on the torus
E(\tau)
with an action functional

S_{\tau}[\phi]\;=\;\int_{E_{\tau}}\,d\mu\,\operatorname{Im}(\tau)\,\mathcal{L}\bigl(\phi(z,\overline{z})\bigr)\,,

(114)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the Lagrangian density.
The quantum expectation value of any operator
\mathcal{O}(\phi)
in the QFT is defined by the path integral

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{\mathcal{O}(\phi)}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{\tau}}\int\,[D\phi]\,\mathcal{O}(\phi)\,{\rm e}^{-S_{\tau}[\phi]}\,,

(115)

where

Z_{\tau}\;=\;\int\,[D\phi]\,{\rm e}^{-S_{\tau}[\phi]}\,,

(116)

is the partition function of the theory.

A theory which is invariant under the symmetry transformation discussed above obeys

S_{\tau}[\phi]\;=\;S_{\widehat{\tau}}[\widehat{\phi}]\,.

(117)

The meaning of the right-hand side of the above equation is that we transform the field

\phi(z,\overline{z})\to\widehat{\phi}(\widehat{z},\widehat{}\overline{z})
according to the tensorial properties of the field
\phi

under (
107
), (
108
),
and integrate the resulting Lagrangian over
(\widehat{z},\widehat{}\overline{z})
coordinates.
When this symmetry is not anomalous in the quantum theory, the measure
[D\phi]
of the
path integral (
116
) is also invariant under the transformation. As a consequence, the partition function
is invariant under the symmetry transformation, i.e.,

Z_{\tau}\;=\;Z_{\widehat{\tau}}\,.

(118)

In such a theory, consider a local tensor operator
T_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}(x)
transforming as (
110
).
Its quantum expectation value
\bigl\langle{T_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}(x)}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}
, as defined by (
115
),
transforms under the symmetry transformation (
107
), (
108
) as

\begin{split}\bigl\langle{T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}(x)}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}&\;\to\;\frac{1}{Z_{\tau}}\int\,[D\phi]\,\frac{\partial x^{\nu_{1}}}{\partial\widehat{x}^{\mu_{1}}}\dots\frac{\partial x^{\nu_{n}}}{\partial\widehat{x}^{\mu_{n}}}\,T_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}(x)\,{\rm e}^{-S_{\tau}[\phi]}\\
&\;=\;\frac{\partial x^{\nu_{1}}}{\partial\widehat{x}^{\mu_{1}}}\dots\frac{\partial x^{\nu_{n}}}{\partial\widehat{x}^{\mu_{n}}}\,\bigl\langle{T_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}(x)}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}\,.\end{split}

(119)

Now we consider a theory of the above sort that is also translation-invariant.
When the theory is translation-invariant, the one-point function
\bigl\langle{T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}
is independent of
x
.
In such a theory, consider a component of the tensor field
T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}
with
m
of its legs
\mu_{i}
equal to
z

and
n-m
of them equal to
\overline{z}
.
The one-point function of this tensor field
\bigl\langle{T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}
is a function only of
\tau,\overline{\tau}
.
Since the coordinate transformation (
108
)
is a combination of rotation and scaling with
\lambda=(c\tau+d)^{-1}
,
the transformation (
113
) implies that under
\tau\to\widehat{\tau}
,

\bigl\langle{T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}\;\to\;(c\tau+d)^{m}\,(c\overline{\tau}+d)^{n-m}\,\bigl\langle{T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}\,,

(120)

i.e., the function
\tau\mapsto\bigl\langle{T_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}}\bigr\rangle_{\tau}

is a non-holomorphic modular form of weights
(m,n-m)
.
Any CFT automatically satisfies the symmetry requirements that we assumed above, and therefore gives rise to modular forms.

Now we apply this general discussion to the theory of massive fermions.
Consider the Lagrangian density for Euclidean Majorana free fermions

\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{0}+m\Psi\overline{\Psi}\,,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{0}\;=\;\Psi\overline{\partial}\Psi-\overline{\Psi}\,\partial\overline{\Psi}\;.

(121)

Under the symmetry transformation (
112
) the fermion fields
\Psi
and
\overline{\Psi}

have the transformation property

\Psi(z,\overline{z})\;\to\;\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Psi(z,\overline{z})\;,\qquad\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})\;\to\;\overline{\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})\;,

(122)

and the derivatives transform as

\partial\;\to\;\lambda^{-1}\partial\;,\qquad\overline{\partial}\;\to\;\overline{\lambda}^{-1}\overline{\partial}\;.

(123)

This implies that the kinetic terms scale with weight 2 under the transformation (
112
), i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{0}\bigl(\phi(z,\overline{z})\bigr)\;=\;|\lambda|^{-2}\,\mathcal{L}_{0}\bigl(\phi(\lambda z,\overline{\lambda}\overline{z})\bigr)\,.

(124)

Indeed, the above scaling with a weight of 2 is obeyed by any two-dimensional conformal field theory with a Lagrangian
description.

Now, the mass term in the Lagrangian (
121
) for fixed mass scales as
1/|\lambda|
, but
it scales as
1/|\lambda|^{2}
if the transformation on the fields is accompanied by the following transformation
of the mass parameter

m\;\to\;m/|\lambda|\;.

(125)

It is therefore natural to define
\mu=m^{2}\operatorname{Im}(\tau)
, which is kept fixed under the modular transformation.
Under this condition, the one-point functions in the massive fermion theory transforms exactly as (
120
).

Appendix B
Quantising the free fermion

In this appendix we outline the conventions used for the fermion fields and define what we mean by an Euclidean Majorana fermion.

B.1
Dirac fermions

We start with a two component Lorentzian, Dirac fermion,
(\Psi,\overline{\Psi})
, satisfying the equations of motion

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}-\partial_{t})\Psi=-m\overline{\Psi}\;,

(126)

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}+\partial_{t})\overline{\Psi}=-m\Psi\;,

(127)

and impose the periodicity conditions

\displaystyle\Psi(x+R,t)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\Psi(x,t)\;,\;\overline{\Psi}(x+R,t)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\overline{\Psi}(x,t)\;,

(128)

for any
\alpha
, on the fields. The mode expansion for the fields is

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}(x,t)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{-}}{2\omega_{k}}}\Psi_{k}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{i(\omega_{k}t+\frac{2\pi k}{R}x)}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{+}}{2\omega_{k}}}\chi_{k}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{k}t-\frac{2\pi k}{R}x)}\!\right),

(129)

\displaystyle\Psi(x,t)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{+}}{2\omega_{k}}}\Psi_{k}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{i(\omega_{k}t+\frac{2\pi k}{R}x)}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{-}}{2\omega_{k}}}\chi_{k}^{\dagger}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{k}t-\frac{2\pi k}{R}x)}\!\right),

(130)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{k}=\sqrt{m^{2}+\tfrac{4\pi^{2}k^{2}}{R^{2}}}\;,\;\omega_{k}^{\pm}=\omega_{k}\pm\tfrac{2\pi k}{R}\;.

(131)

These fields are not hermitian and hence in addition we need the equations of motion for
(\Psi^{\dagger},\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger})
, give by

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}-\partial_{t})\Psi^{\dagger}=-m\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}\;,

(132)

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}+\partial_{t})\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}=-m\Psi^{\dagger}\;.

(133)

Canonical quantisation gives the mode anti commutators

\displaystyle\{\Psi_{n}^{\dagger},\Psi_{m}\}=\delta_{n,m}\;,\quad\{\chi_{n}^{\dagger},\chi_{m}\}=\delta_{n,m}\;.

(134)

If we Wick rotate the
t
coordinate to be
t=iy
and define the complex coordinates
z=x+iy
and
\overline{z}=x-iy
the mode expansion of the fields is

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{-}}{2\omega_{k}}}\Psi_{k}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{+}z-\omega_{k}^{-}\overline{z})}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{+}}{2\omega_{k}}}\chi_{k}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{-}z-\omega_{k}^{+}\overline{z})}\!\right),

(135)

\displaystyle\Psi(z,\overline{z})=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\left(\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{+}}{2\omega_{k}}}\Psi_{k}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{+}z-\omega_{k}^{-}\overline{z})}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{k}^{-}}{2\omega_{k}}}\chi_{k}^{\dagger}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{k}^{-}z-\omega_{k}^{+}\overline{z})}\!\right),

(136)

The periodicity condition is now expressed as

\displaystyle\Psi(z+R,\overline{z}+R)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\Psi(z,\overline{z})\;,\;\overline{\Psi}(z+R,\overline{z}+R)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})\;.

(137)

The equations of motion are

\displaystyle\partial\overline{\Psi}=-\tfrac{1}{2}m\Psi\;,\quad\partial\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}=-\tfrac{1}{2}m\Psi^{\dagger}\;,

(138)

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}\Psi=-\tfrac{1}{2}m\overline{\Psi}\;,\quad\overline{\partial}\Psi^{\dagger}=-\tfrac{1}{2}m\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}\;.

(139)

These formally come from the Lagrangian density

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\Psi^{\dagger}\overline{\partial}\Psi-\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}\partial\overline{\Psi}+\frac{1}{2}m(\Psi^{\dagger}\overline{\Psi}-\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}\Psi).

(141)

The Lagrangian density is invariant under a coordinate rotation given by

(z,\overline{z})\rightarrow(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})\;,

(142)

if the fields transform as

\displaystyle\Psi(z,\overline{z})=e^{\frac{i\theta}{2}}\Psi(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})\;,\;\Psi(z,\overline{z})^{\dagger}=e^{\frac{i\theta}{2}}\Psi(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})^{\dagger}\;,

(143)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})=e^{-\frac{i\theta}{2}}\overline{\Psi}(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})\;,\;\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})^{\dagger}=e^{-\frac{i\theta}{2}}\overline{\Psi}(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})^{\dagger}\;.

(144)

Hence bilinear fields of the form
\Psi^{\dagger}\partial^{2n-1}\Psi
and
\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi}
transform under coordinate rotations as

\displaystyle\Psi^{\dagger}\partial^{2n-1}\Psi\rightarrow e^{-2n{\rm i}\theta}\Psi^{\dagger}\partial^{2n-1}\Psi\;,

(145)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi}\rightarrow e^{2n{\rm i}\theta}\overline{\Psi}^{\dagger}\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi}\;.

(146)

B.2
Majorana fermions

We again solve the equations of motion (
126
) but now impose a Majorana condition. The mode expansion is

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}(x,t){=}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\!\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{n}^{-}}{2\omega_{n}}}\!\left(\!\Psi_{n}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{i(\omega_{n}t+\frac{2\pi n}{R}x)}{+}\Psi_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{n}t+\frac{2\pi n}{R}x)}\!\right),

(147)

\displaystyle\Psi(x,t){=}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\!\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{n}^{+}}{2\omega_{n}}}\!\left(\!\Psi_{n}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{i(\omega_{n}t+\frac{2\pi n}{R}x)}{+}\Psi_{n}^{\dagger}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{n}t+\frac{2\pi n}{R}x)}\!\right),

(148)

where now
\alpha\in\{0,\tfrac{1}{2}\}
and we define
\omega_{n}
and
\omega_{n}^{\pm}
as in (
131
). These fields again have the periodicity property

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}(x+R,t)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\overline{\Psi}(x,t)\;,\;\Psi(x+R,t)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\Psi(x,t)\;,

(149)

and we must impose
\alpha\in\{0,\tfrac{1}{2}\}
in order to respect the reality condition on the fermions. After canonical quantisation the fermion modes have the anti commutation relations

\displaystyle\{\Psi_{k},\Psi_{l}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{k,l}\;.

(150)

If we Wick rotate the
t
coordinate to be
t=iy
and define the complex coordinates
z=x+iy
and
\overline{z}=x-iy
we get the fields

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z}){=}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\!\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{n}^{-}}{2\omega_{n}}}\!\!\left(\!\!\Psi_{n}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{n}^{+}z-\omega_{n}^{-}\overline{z})}{+}\Psi_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{n}^{+}z-\omega_{n}^{-}\overline{z})}\!\!\right),

(151)

\displaystyle\Psi(z,\overline{z}){=}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{R}}\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha}\!\!\!\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega_{n}^{+}}{2\omega_{n}}}\!\!\left(\!\!\Psi_{n}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{n}^{+}z-\omega_{n}^{-}\overline{z})}{+}\Psi_{n}^{\dagger}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}e^{-\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{n}^{+}z-\omega_{n}^{-}\overline{z})}\!\!\right).

(152)

Again the periodicity condition is now expressed as

\displaystyle\Psi(z+R,\overline{z}+R)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\Psi(z,\overline{z})\;,\;\overline{\Psi}(z+R,\overline{z}+R)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})\;.

(153)

The equations of motion are

\displaystyle\partial\overline{\Psi}=-\tfrac{1}{2}m\Psi\;,

(154)

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}\Psi=-\tfrac{1}{2}m\overline{\Psi}\;,

(155)

and these formally come from the Lagrangian density

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\Psi\overline{\partial}\Psi-\overline{\Psi}\partial\overline{\Psi}+m\Psi\overline{\Psi}\;.

(156)

This is the same Lagrangian density as appears in
[
10
]
but differs by a factor of 2 and the sign of
m
from that in
[
17
]
so that here
m>0
corresponds to the low-temperature phase. As for the Dirac case the Lagrangian density is invariant under a coordinate rotation given by

(z,\overline{z})\rightarrow(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})\;,

(157)

if the fields transform as

\Psi(z,\overline{z})=e^{\frac{i\theta}{2}}\Psi(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})\;,\;\overline{\Psi}(z,\overline{z})=e^{-\frac{i\theta}{2}}\overline{\Psi}(e^{i\theta}z,e^{-i\theta}\overline{z})\;.

(158)

Hence bilinear fields of the form
\Psi\partial^{2n-1}\Psi
and
\overline{\Psi}\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi}
transform under coordinate rotations as

\displaystyle\Psi\partial^{2n-1}\Psi\rightarrow e^{-2n{\rm i}\theta}\Psi\partial^{2n-1}\Psi\;,

(159)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi}\rightarrow e^{2n{\rm i}\theta}\overline{\Psi}\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi}\;.

(160)

Appendix C
Uniqueness of tensor currents

Here we show that, up to total derivatives and powers of the mass
m
, all bilinear fermion currents take the form of (
21
). We also show these bilinear currents can be arranged into a conserved, symmetric tensor.

If we only want to construct bilinear currents from the Majorana fields
\overline{\Psi}
,
\Psi
and derivatives of these two fields then the most general currents take the form

\displaystyle\partial^{a}\overline{\partial}^{b}\Psi\partial^{c}\overline{\partial}^{d}\Psi\;,\;\partial^{a}\overline{\partial}^{b}\overline{\Psi}\partial^{c}\overline{\partial}^{d}\Psi\;,\;\partial^{a}\overline{\partial}^{b}\overline{\Psi}\partial^{c}\overline{\partial}^{d}\overline{\Psi}\;,

(161)

for non-negative integers
a,b,c,d
. Up to total derivatives these currents are equivalent to

\displaystyle(-1)^{a+b}\Psi\partial^{a+c}\overline{\partial}^{b+d}\Psi\;,\;(-1)^{a+b}\overline{\Psi}\partial^{a+c}\overline{\partial}^{b+d}\Psi\;,\;(-1)^{a+b}\overline{\Psi}\partial^{a+c}\overline{\partial}^{b+d}\overline{\Psi}\;.

(162)

We drop the factor
(-1)^{a+b}
and set
n=a+c
and
k=b+d
(so
n
and
k
are non-negative integers). The currents are

\displaystyle\Psi\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi\;,\;\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi\;,\;\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\overline{\Psi}\;.

(163)

Now we use the equations of motion

\partial\overline{\Psi}=-\lambda\Psi\;,\quad\overline{\partial}\Psi=-\lambda\overline{\Psi}\;,

(164)

where we have set
m=2\lambda
.
First let us assume that
n>k
. Then using the equations of motion gives

\displaystyle\Psi\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi=\lambda^{2k}\Psi\partial^{n-k}\Psi\;,

(165)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi=\lambda^{2k}\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n-k}\Psi\;,

(166)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\overline{\Psi}=-\lambda^{2k+1}\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n-k-1}\Psi\;.

(167)

Again using the equations of motion, up to total derivatives the currents are

\displaystyle\Psi\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi=\lambda^{2k}\Psi\partial^{n-k}\Psi\;,

(168)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi=\lambda^{2k+1}\Psi\partial^{n-k-1}\Psi+\partial(\dots)\;,

(169)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\overline{\Psi}=-\lambda^{2k+2}\Psi\partial^{n-k-2}\Psi+\partial(\dots)\;.

(170)

Note if
n-k-1=0
then the current (
167
) is

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2k+1}\overline{\Psi}\Psi\;.

(171)

Alternatively if
k>n
in (
163
), using the equations of motion we find that up to total derivatives we have the three currents

\displaystyle\Psi\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi=-\lambda^{2n+2}\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{k-n-2}\overline{\Psi}+\overline{\partial}(\dots)\;,

(172)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\Psi=-\lambda^{2n+1}\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{k-n-1}\overline{\Psi}\;,

(173)

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}\partial^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k}\overline{\Psi}=\lambda^{2n}\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{k-n}\overline{\Psi}\;.

(174)

Note if
k-n-1=0
then the current (
172
) is

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2n+1}\Psi\overline{\Psi}\;.

(175)

Finally for
n=k
in (
163
), using the equations of motion, we see that the only non-vanishing current is

\displaystyle\lambda^{2k}\overline{\Psi}\Psi\;.

(176)

Hence up to powers of the mass and total derivatives all currents take the form

\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{n}\overline{\Psi}\;,\quad\Psi\partial^{n}\Psi\;,\quad\overline{\Psi}\Psi\;.

(177)

Additionally, we have

\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{2n}\overline{\Psi}=\overline{\partial}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{\partial}^{k-1}\overline{\Psi}\overline{\partial}^{2n-k}\overline{\Psi}\!\!\right),\;\Psi\partial^{2n}\Psi=\partial\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial^{k-1}\Psi\partial^{2n-k}\Psi\!\!\right),

(178)

so that for
n
even the currents are total derivatives.
Hence the non-trivial currents take the form

\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi}\;,\quad\Psi\partial^{2n-1}\Psi\;,\quad\overline{\Psi}\Psi\;.

(179)

Now these currents can be arranged in a conserved symmetric tensor. We have the conservation equation

\displaystyle\partial(\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi})+\overline{\partial}(\lambda\Psi\overline{\partial}^{2n-2}\overline{\Psi})\;=\;0\;,

(180)

and up to total derivatives we have

\displaystyle\lambda\Psi\overline{\partial}^{2n-2}\overline{\Psi}\;=\;\lambda^{2}\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{2n-3}\overline{\Psi}+\overline{\partial}(\dots)\;.

(181)

Hence up to total derivatives we have the conserved current

\displaystyle(\overline{J}^{z},\overline{J}^{\overline{z}})=(\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{2n-1}\overline{\Psi},\lambda^{2}\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}^{2n-3}\overline{\Psi})\;.

(182)

Note for
n=1
then the conserved current becomes

\displaystyle(\overline{J}^{z},\overline{J}^{\overline{z}})=(\overline{\Psi}\,\overline{\partial}\,\overline{\Psi},\lambda\Psi\overline{\Psi})\;.

(183)

Similarly we have

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}(\Psi\partial^{2n-1}\Psi)+\partial(\lambda\overline{\Psi}\partial^{2n-2}\Psi)=0\;,

(184)

\displaystyle\lambda\overline{\Psi}\partial^{2n-2}\Psi=\lambda^{2}\Psi\partial^{2n-3}\Psi+\partial(\dots)\;,

(185)

so up to total derivatives we have the conserved current

\displaystyle(J^{z},J^{\overline{z}})=(\lambda^{2}\Psi\partial^{2n-3}\Psi,\Psi\partial^{2n-1}\Psi)\;.

(186)

and again if
n=1
the current is

\displaystyle(J^{z},J^{\overline{z}})=(\lambda\overline{\Psi}\Psi,\Psi\partial\Psi)\;.

(187)

These calculations show that the following symmetric rank-
2n
tensor

T_{(2n,k)}\;=\;\begin{cases}\frac{i}{2}\lambda^{2n+k}\overline{\Psi}\,{\overline{\partial}}^{-k-1}\overline{\Psi}\;,\qquad&k\;=\;-2n,-2n+2,\dots,-2\;,\\
\frac{i}{2}\lambda^{2n-1}\Psi\overline{\Psi}\;,\qquad&k\;=\;0\;,\\
-\frac{i}{2}\lambda^{2n-k}\Psi\partial^{k-1}\Psi&k\;=\;2,4,\dots,2n\,,\end{cases}

(188)

is conserved, i.e.

\partial T_{(2n,k)}+\overline{\partial}T_{(2n,k+2)}\;=\;0\;.

(189)

Appendix D
Regularisation of constants

In this appendix we calculate the constant terms
E_{0}
and
C
that appear in the Hamiltonian (
31
) and one point functions (
53
) respectively. This is done via a zeta-function style regularisation process and the values we obtain respect the modular properties of the partition and one point functions.

D.1
Regularising
E_{0}

The Hamiltonian (
31
) has a constant term formally given by the divergent series

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}}\;.

(190)

In order to make sense of this expression, we consider
the following sum which converges for Re
(\nu)>0

E_{0}(\nu)\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}\right)^{-\nu-\tfrac{1}{2}}\;.

(191)

This series is periodic in
\alpha
with period 1, and so it has a Fourier expansion

E_{0}(\nu)\;=\;\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{\ell}\,e^{2\pi{\rm i}\ell\alpha}\,.

(192)

The coefficients
a_{\ell}
are given by

\begin{split}a_{\ell}&\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\,e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\ell\alpha}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}\right)^{-\nu-\tfrac{1}{2}}\\
&\;=\;-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,\left(m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi\alpha}{R}\right)^{2}\right)^{-\nu-\tfrac{1}{2}}\cos(2\pi\ell\alpha)\;,\end{split}

(193)

where, in going to the second line, we have used

\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k+\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\alpha\,f(\alpha)

(194)

for any integrable function
f
,
combined with the fact the integrand is even in
\alpha
.
The integral in (
193
) can be evaluated,
for Re
(m)>0
and Re
(\nu)>-\tfrac{1}{2}
, to be ((7) on page 11 of
[
1
]
)

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{R}{2\pi}\right)^{2\nu+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,\left(\alpha^{2}+\left(\tfrac{mR}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\right)^{-\nu-\frac{1}{2}}\cos(2\pi\ell\alpha)=\\
&\frac{R}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\nu+\frac{1}{2})}\left(\frac{R|\ell|}{2m}\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}(mR|\ell|)\;,\end{split}

(195)

where
K_{\nu}(x)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. We thus obtain, for
\ell\neq 0
,

a_{\ell}\;=\;-\frac{R}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\nu+\frac{1}{2})}\left(\frac{R|\ell|}{2m}\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}(mR|\ell|)\;.

(196)

The coefficient
a_{0}
is defined as a limit
\ell\to 0
of the above expression considering
\ell
as a continuous real variable:

a_{0}\;=\;-\frac{R\,\Gamma(\nu)}{4\sqrt{\pi}\,m^{2\nu}\,\Gamma(\nu+\frac{1}{2})}\;.

(197)

Thus we obtain the following expression,

E_{0}(\nu)=-\frac{R\,\Gamma(\nu)}{4\sqrt{\pi}\,m^{2\nu}\,\Gamma(\nu{+}\frac{1}{2})}-\frac{R^{\nu+1}}{(2m)^{\nu}\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\nu{+}\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\!\ell^{\nu}K_{\nu}(2\pi m\ell)\cos(2\pi\ell\alpha).

(198)

The series in the second term is convergent for all
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
.
The function
E_{0}(\nu)
as given in (
198
) is therefore a meromorphic function in
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
with simple poles at
\nu=0,-1,-2,\dots
.
If we subtract the
a_{0}
term and analytically continue to
\nu=-1
we find

E_{0}=\lim_{\nu\to-1}\left(E_{0}(\nu)+\frac{R\,\Gamma(\nu)}{4\sqrt{\pi}\,m^{2\nu}\,\Gamma(\nu{+}\frac{1}{2})}\right)=\frac{m}{\pi}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}K_{1}(mR\ell)\cos(2\pi\ell\alpha)\;.

(199)

where we have used
K_{-1}(x)=K_{1}(x)
and
\Gamma(-\tfrac{1}{2})=-2\sqrt{\pi}
.
This is the regularised value of the zero-point energy
E_{0}
that we use, which as mentioned below (
32
), ensures that the partition function is modular invariant.

D.2
Regularising
C

Similarly, when calculating the one-point functions (
53
) for the fermions we have constant terms formally given by the divergent series

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\left(\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}}{+}\frac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2n}{+}\left(\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}}{-}\frac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2n}}{2\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}}}\;,

(200)

for Dirac fermions. In the Majorana case we have
\alpha=0,\tfrac{1}{2}
and the sum can be written as

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\left(\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}}\pm\frac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2n}}{\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}}}\;.

(201)

As above, we regulate this series by the parameters
\nu
and
\mu
,

\begin{split}&C(\nu,\mu)=\\
&\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\!\frac{\!\left(\!\!\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\!\left(\!\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\!\right)^{2}}{+}\frac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\!\!\right)^{\nu}\!\!{+}\!\left(\!\!\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\!\left(\!\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\!\right)^{2}}{-}\frac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\!\!\right)^{\nu}}{2\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\!\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\!\right)^{2}}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}\mu(k+\alpha)},\end{split}

(202)

which converges for
|
Re
(\nu)|<1
and
\mu\in(0,1)
.
Note that if
\mu=0,1
then
C(\nu,\mu)
does not converge for any
\nu
.
This function is periodic in
\alpha
with period 1 and so has a Fourier expansion,

C(\nu,\mu)\;=\;\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{\ell}e^{2\pi{\rm i}\ell\alpha}\;.

(203)

The coefficients
a_{\ell}
are given by

\begin{split}&a_{\ell}=\int_{0}^{1}\!d\alpha\sum_{\pm}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\left(\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\!\right)^{2}}\pm\frac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{\nu}}{2\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi(k+\alpha)}{R}\right)^{2}}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\mu-\ell)(k+\alpha)}\;,\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!d\alpha\frac{\left(\!\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\left(\tfrac{2\pi\alpha}{R}\right)^{2}}{+}\frac{2\pi\alpha}{R}\!\right)^{\nu}{+}\left(\!\sqrt{m^{2}{+}\left(\tfrac{2\pi\alpha}{R}\right)^{2}}{-}\frac{2\pi\alpha}{R}\!\right)^{\nu}}{\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\tfrac{2\pi\alpha}{R}\right)^{2}}}\cos(2\pi(\ell{-}\mu)\alpha)\;,\end{split}

(204)

where we have unfolded the sum as in (
194
),
and used the fact the integrand is even in
\alpha
. We use the integral identity ((22) on page 13 of
[
1
]
)

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{2\pi}{R}\right)^{\nu-1}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\alpha\frac{\left(\!\!\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{mR}{2\pi}\right)^{2}{+}\alpha^{2}}+\!\alpha\!\!\right)^{\nu}\!{+}\left(\!\!\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{mR}{2\pi}\right)^{2}{+}\alpha^{2}}-\!\alpha\!\!\right)^{\nu}}{\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{mR}{2\pi}\right)^{2}+\alpha^{2}}}\cos(2\pi(\ell{-}\mu)\alpha)\;,\\
&\;=\;\frac{m^{\nu}R}{\pi}\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)K_{\nu}(mR|\ell-\mu|)\end{split}

(205)

which is valid for Re
(m)>0
and
|
Re
(\nu)|<1
(since
\mu\in(0,1)
we never have
\ell=\mu
).
We find

C(\nu,\mu)\;=\;\frac{m^{\nu}R}{\pi}\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{\nu}(mR|\ell-\mu|)e^{2\pi{\rm i}\ell\alpha}\;.

(206)

which is a convergent sum for all
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
when
\mu\in(0,1)
.

Now we want to take the limit
\mu\to 0
.
In this limit, the
a_{\ell}
are all well-defined except for
a_{0}
which diverges.
As before, we define
C(\nu)
by subtracting the
a_{0}
term,

\begin{split}C(\nu)&\;=\;\lim_{\mu\to 0}\left(C(\nu,\mu)-\frac{m^{\nu}R}{\pi}\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)K_{\nu}(2\pi m|\mu|)\right)\\
&\;=\;\frac{2m^{\nu}R}{\pi}\cos\left(\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}K_{\nu}(mR\ell)\cos(2\pi\ell\alpha)\;.\end{split}

(207)

Finally, we analytically continue to
\nu=2n
,

C\;=\;C(2n)\;=\;\frac{2(-1)^{n}m^{2n}R}{\pi}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}K_{2n}(mR\ell)\cos(2\pi\ell\alpha)\;.

(208)

As before, this value of the regularised constant term ensures that the one-point functions are modular forms.

Appendix E
Sum over Bessel functions

In this appendix we derive an expression for the one point functions
as a lattice sum of Bessel functions, which makes the modular properties obvious. We follow the same procedure as was used in
[
3
]
.

We start with the expression given in (
45
),

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}+2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}}\text{Li}_{0}\!\left(\!e^{-\tau_{2}\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}+2\pi i\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)+2\pi i\beta}\!\right),

(209)

where
\text{Li}_{0}(x)=\tfrac{x}{1-x}
.
This function is clearly periodic in
\alpha
and
\beta
with period 1 so we can write it as a Fourier expansion of the form

\sum_{(r,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}a_{r,\ell}\,{\rm e}^{2\pi i(\alpha\ell+\beta r)}\;.

(210)

To find the coefficients
a_{r,\ell}
we need to compute the integral

\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha d\beta e^{-2\pi i(\ell\alpha+r\beta)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(\omega_{k+\alpha}^{+})^{2n}}{\omega_{k+\alpha}}\text{Li}_{0}\!\left(\!e^{-\tau_{2}\omega_{k+\alpha}+2\pi i\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)+2\pi i\beta}\right)\,,

(211)

where

\omega_{k}=\sqrt{(2\pi k)^{2}+m^{2}}\;,\quad\omega_{k}^{+}=\omega+2\pi k\;.

(212)

We expand the Li
0
as a power series and rearrange to get

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!d\beta e^{2\pi i(j-r)\beta}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!d\alpha\frac{(\omega_{k+\alpha}^{+})^{2n}}{\omega_{k+\alpha}}e^{2\pi i(\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)j-\ell\alpha)-\tau_{2}j\omega_{k+\alpha}}\;.

(213)

The
\beta
integral gives us a Kronecker delta

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\delta_{j,r}\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\frac{(\omega_{k+\alpha}^{+})^{2n}}{\omega_{k+\alpha}}e^{2\pi i(\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)j-\ell\alpha)-\tau_{2}j\omega_{k+\alpha}}\;.

(214)

Note that since
j>0
,
a_{r,l}
vanishes for
r\leq 0
. For the rest of the calculation we assume
r>0
. The Kronecker delta kills the
j
sum

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!d\alpha\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}+\!2\pi(k{+}\alpha)\!)^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}}e^{2\pi i(\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)r-\ell\alpha)-\tau_{2}r\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}}\;.

(215)

We can replace the sum over integers with an integral over the reals
using the identity

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha f(k+\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\alpha f(\alpha)\;.

(216)

Using this we find

\begin{split}&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\alpha\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi\alpha)^{2}+m^{2}}+2\pi\alpha)^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi\alpha)^{2}+m^{2}}}e^{2\pi i(\tau_{1}r-\ell)\alpha-\tau_{2}r\sqrt{(2\pi\alpha)^{2}+m^{2}}}\;,\\
&(2\pi)^{2n-1}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\alpha\frac{(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}{+}(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}}{+}\alpha)^{2n}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}{+}(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}}}e^{-2\pi i\ell\alpha+\pi i\tau r(\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}})+\pi i\overline{\tau}r(\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}})}\;,\end{split}

(217)

where
\tau=\tau_{1}+{\rm i}\tau_{2}
. We can exchange the
(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}}+\alpha)^{2n}
term for
\tau
derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{n}2^{2n-1}}{\pi\,r^{2n}}\frac{\partial^{2n}}{\partial\tau^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\alpha\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}}}e^{2\pi i(\tau_{1}r-\ell)\alpha-2\pi\tau_{2}r\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}}}\;.

(218)

We have the following integral identity for modified Bessel functions of the second kind ((27) on Page 17 of
[
1
]
),

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\alpha\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}{+}(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}}}\cos(2\pi\alpha(\tau_{1}r{-}\ell)\!)e^{-2\pi\tau_{2}r\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+(\frac{m}{2\pi})^{2}}}{=}K_{0}(\!m|\tau r{-}\ell|)\;,

(219)

for Re
(m)>0
and Re
(\tau_{2}r)>0
. This gives us

\displaystyle a_{r,\ell}\;=\;\frac{(-1)^{n}2^{2n}}{\pi\,r^{2n}}\frac{\partial^{2n}}{\partial\tau^{2n}}K_{0}(2\pi m|r\tau-\ell|)\;.

(220)

Using the following integral representation ((29) on page 146 of
[
1
]
)

\displaystyle K_{2n}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\frac{x^{2n}}{(2s)^{2n+1}}e^{-s-\frac{x^{2}}{4s}}\;,

(221)

for Re
(x^{2})>0
, one can show

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2n}}{\partial\tau^{2n}}K_{0}(m|r\tau-\ell|)=\left(\frac{mr}{2}\right)^{2n}\left(\frac{\overline{\tau}r-\ell}{\tau r-\ell}\right)^{n}K_{2n}(m|r\tau-\ell|)\;,

(222)

which gives

\displaystyle a_{r,\ell}\;=\;\begin{cases}0\;,&r\leq 0\;,\\
\frac{(-1)^{n}m^{2n}}{\pi}\left(\frac{\overline{\tau}r-\ell}{\tau r-\ell}\right)^{n}K_{2n}(m|r\tau-\ell|)\;,&r>0\;.\end{cases}

(223)

Thus we obtain the Fourier expansion

\begin{split}&\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\!\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}+\!2\pi(k{+}\alpha)\!)^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}}\text{Li}_{0}\!\left(\!e^{-\tau_{2}\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}+2\pi i\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)+2\pi i\beta}\!\right)\\
&\;=\;\frac{(-1)^{n}m^{2n}}{\pi}\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\\
r>0\end{smallmatrix}}\left(\frac{\overline{\tau}r-\ell}{\tau r-\ell}\right)^{n}K_{2n}(m|r\tau-\ell|)e^{2\pi{\rm i}(\alpha\ell+\beta r)}\,.\end{split}

(224)

Restricting to
\alpha,\beta\in\{0,\tfrac{1}{2}\}
allows us to write the Fourier transform as a lattice sum plus a constant term

\begin{split}&\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}+\!2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}}\text{Li}_{0}\!\left(\!e^{-\tau_{2}\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}+2\pi i\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)+2\pi i\beta}\!\right)\\
&=\frac{(-1)^{n}m^{2n}}{2\pi}\!\!\left(\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{(r,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\!\!\!\left(\!\frac{\overline{\tau}r{-}\ell}{\tau r{-}\ell}\!\right)^{n}\!\!K_{2n}(m|r\tau{-}\ell|)e^{2\pi i(\alpha\ell+\beta r)}{-}2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\!K_{2n}(m\ell)\cos(2\pi\alpha\ell)\!\!\right),\end{split}

(225)

where we exclude the origin from the lattice sum. If, instead, we start with

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}{-}2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}}\text{Li}_{0}\!\left(\!e^{-\tau_{2}\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}+2\pi i\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)+2\pi i\beta}\!\right),

(226)

then all the above calculations go through, except that in (
218
) we replace the
\tau
-derivatives
with
\overline{\tau}
-derivatives, so that the final result is

\begin{split}&\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}-\!2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}}\text{Li}_{0}\!\left(\!e^{-\tau_{2}\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}+2\pi i\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)+2\pi i\beta}\!\right)\\
&=\frac{(-1)^{n}m^{2n}}{2\pi}\!\!\left(\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{(r,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\!\!\!\left(\!\frac{\tau r{-}\ell}{\overline{\tau}r{-}\ell}\!\right)^{n}\!\!K_{2n}(m|r\tau{-}\ell|)e^{2\pi i(\alpha\ell+\beta r)}{-}2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\!K_{2n}(m\ell)\cos(2\pi\alpha\ell)\!\!\right),\end{split}

(227)

Note that if we symmetrise (
224
) in
\alpha
and
\beta
then for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
we have the lattice sum

\begin{split}&\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}+\!2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2\pi(k{+}\alpha))^{2}{+}m^{2}}}\text{Li}_{0}\!\left(\!e^{-\tau_{2}\sqrt{(2\pi(k+\alpha))^{2}+m^{2}}+2\pi i\tau_{1}(k+\alpha)+2\pi i\beta}\!\right)\\
&=\frac{(-1)^{n}m^{2n}}{\pi}\!\!\left(\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{(r,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\!\!\!\left(\!\frac{\overline{\tau}r{-}\ell}{\tau r{-}\ell}\!\right)^{n}\!\!K_{2n}(m|r\tau{-}\ell|)e^{2\pi i(\alpha\ell+\beta r)}{-}2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\!K_{2n}(m\ell)\cos(2\pi\alpha\ell)\!\!\right),\end{split}

(228)

We can similarly symmetrise (
226
) in
\alpha
and
\beta
to obtain a lattice sum for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
These symmetrised expressions are precisely the one-point functions of
the Dirac fermions as discussed in Section
2.2
.
