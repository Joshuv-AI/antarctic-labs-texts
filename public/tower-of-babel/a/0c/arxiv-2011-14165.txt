Verifying liquidity of recursive Bitcoin contracts
Massimo Bartoletti \rsuper a, Stefano Lande \rsuper a, Maurizio Murgia \rsuper b, Roberto Zunino \rsuper b

Abstract
. Smart contracts — computer protocols that regulate the exchange of crypto-assets in trustless environments — have become popular with the spread of blockchain technologies. A landmark security property of smart contracts is liquidity : in a non-liquid contract, it may happen that some assets remain frozen, i.e. not redeemable by anyone. The relevance of this issue is witnessed by recent liquidity attacks to Ethereum, which have frozen hundreds of USD millions. We address the problem of verifying liquidity on BitML, a DSL for smart contracts with a secure compiler to Bitcoin, featuring primitives for currency transfers, contract renegotiation and consensual recursion. Our main result is a verification technique for liquidity. We first transform the infinite-state semantics of BitML into a finite-state one, which focusses on the behaviour of a chosen set of contracts, abstracting from the moves of the context. With respect to the chosen contracts, this abstraction is sound, i.e. if the abstracted contract is liquid, then also the concrete one is such. We then verify liquidity by model-checking the finite-state abstraction. We implement a toolchain that automatically verifies liquidity of BitML contracts and compiles them to Bitcoin, and we assess it through a benchmark of representative contracts.

1.
Introduction

Smart contracts —
computer protocols that regulate the exchange of assets
in trustless environments —
have become popular with the growth of interest in blockchain technologies.
Mainstream blockchain platforms like Ethereum, Tezos and Cardano,
feature expressive high-level languages for programming smart contracts.
This flexibility has a drawback in that
it may open the door to attacks that steal or tamper with the
assets controlled by vulnerable contracts
[
ABC17
,
LCO+16
]
.

An alternative approach is to sacrifice the expressiveness of smart contracts
to reduce the attack surface.
This approach was pursued first by Bitcoin, where transactions
can specify simple conditions on how to redeem them,
using a limited set of logic, arithmetic, and cryptographic operators.
Despite the limited expressiveness of these conditions,
it is possible to encode a variety of smart contracts,
e.g. gambling games, escrow services, crowdfunding systems,
by suitably chaining transactions

[
ADMM14a
,
ADMM14c
,
ADMM16
,
ABC+18
,
BDM16
,
BZ17
,
BK14
,
KB14
,
KB16
,
KMB15
,
KVV16
,
MB17
]
.
The common trait of these works is that they render contracts
as cryptographic protocols, where participants
can exchange/sign messages, read the blockchain, and append transactions.
Verifying the correctness of these protocols is hard,
since it requires to reason in a computational model,
where participants can manipulate arbitrary bitstrings,
only being constrained to use PPTIME algorithms.

Departing from this approach, BitML
[
BZ18a
]

allows to write Bitcoin contracts in a high-level, process-algebraic language.
BitML features a compiler that translates contracts
into sets of standard Bitcoin transactions.
The compiler enjoys a computational soundness property, which guarantees that
the execution of the compiled contract is coherent with
the semantics of the source BitML specification,
even in the presence of adversaries.

In this paper we address the problem of verifying BitML contracts,
in an extension of BitML with renegotiation and recursion
[
BMZ20
]
.
In particular, we focus on a landmark property of smart contracts,
called
liquidity
, which ensures that funds cannot remain frozen
within a contract.
Before discussing our main contributions, we overview below
BitML and our analysis technique.

1.2.
Liquidity

The
liquidity
property requires that the contract balance
can always be transferred eventually to some participant:
in a non-liquid contract, funds can be frozen forever,
unavailable to anyone, hence effectively destroyed
2
2

2

To the best of our knowledge, the use of the term “liquidity”
to refer to a contract property was first introduced in
[

]
,
in the setting of Ethereum contracts.
.
A simple form of liquidity could just require that
participants can always cooperate to unfreeze funds.
However, this notion would contrast with the setting of smart contracts,
where participants are mutually untrusted, and may refuse to cooperate.

where
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

1.3.
Verifying liquidity

One of the main contributions of this paper is
a verification technique for the liquidity of BitML contracts.
Our technique is based on a more general result,
i.e. a correspondence between the concrete semantics of BitML
and a new abstract semantics, which is finite-state and
correctly approximates the concrete semantics.
To obtain a finite-state abstraction,
we need to cope with several sources of infiniteness
of the concrete semantics:
the unbounded passing of time,
the stipulation and renegotiation of contracts,
and the operations on bitcoin deposits.
When studying the liquidity of a set of contracts
X
from the point of
view of a participant
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
,
we abstract away all this, by just recording the actions which can be
performed on the descendants of
X
, distinguishing between
the actions doable by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
alone from those which require
the cooperation of other participants.
This abstraction produces a finite-state transition system,
which we model-check for liquidity.

1.4.
Contributions

We summarise our main contributions as follows:

•

We introduce an extension of BitML featuring
the renegotiation primitive
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}}}
.
Compared to the version in
[
BZ18a
]
, the current language
is more expressive:
besides allowing participants to provide new deposits and secrets
at run-time, it also allows for
unbounded
recursion,
still admitting compilation to Bitcoin.

•

We formalize a notion of liquidity (Definition
3
).
With respect to a participant
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
, a contract is liquid
when
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
alone can ensure that funds do not remain frozen within
the contract, even in the presence of adversaries.

•

We introduce an abstraction of the semantics of BitML
which is finite-state, and sound with respect to the concrete
(infinite-state) semantics.
Building upon this abstraction,
we devise a sound verification technique for liquidity in BitML
(Theorem
4
).

•

We develop a toolchain for writing and verifying the liquidity of
BitML contracts, and for deploying them on Bitcoin.
The toolchain is based on a BitML embedding in Racket
[
Fla12
]
,
which allows for programming BitML contracts within the DrRacket IDE.
The toolchain also implements a compiler from BitML contracts to standard Bitcoin transactions.

•

We implement a collection of BitML contracts,
which we use as a benchmark to evaluate our toolchain.
This collection contains a variety of complex contracts, including
financial services, auctions, lotteries, and other gambling games.

•

We discuss alternative renegotiation primitives,
which allow participants to choose some parameters
(e.g. the amounts to be deposited) at renegotiation time,
to change the set of participants involved in the renegotiated contract,
and to renegotiate contracts without the consent of all participants.

•

We discuss alternative notions of liquidity,
e.g. taking into account participants’ strategies.

1.5.
Comparison with previous work

This paper borrows and extends the contributions of some past papers of ours.
BitML was originally introduced in
[
BZ18a
]
, in a version without
renegotiation and recursion.
A main limitation of this version was that the participants could not renegotiate the terms
of a stipulated contract: this prevented from expressing common financial contracts, where
funds have to be added by participants at run-time.
Renegotiation and recursion were added in
[
BMZ20
]
,
where we showed that, despite the increased expressiveness,
it was still possible to execute BitML on standard Bitcoin,
preserving the security guarantees of BitML.
These papers did not deal with verification of contracts, and with liquidity.
This notion was introduced in
[
BZ19
]
, in the original version of BitML,
i.e. without renegotiation and recursion.
The BitML toolchain was first presented in
[
ABL+19
]
, supporting the
compilation and verification of contracts in the original version of BitML.

The current paper is the first one which studies liquidity
in the full BitML.
Recursion adds significant complexity to verification, as it makes the calculus Turing-complete.
Because of this, the abstraction in
[
BZ19
]
is no longer usable,
so the current paper devises an alternative verification technique.
The current paper also improves the BitML toolchain, extending the compiler and the liquidity verifier to contracts with renegotiation and recursion.

[BitML semantics]

The semantics of BitML is a Labelled Transition System (LTS)
between timed configurations.
In the rest of this
section
we describe
the reduction rules of the LTS,
which implicitly define the labels
\ell
.
A
concrete run

\mathcal{R}
is a sequence

\Gamma_{0}\xrightarrow{\ell_{0}}_{\flat}\Gamma_{1}\xrightarrow{\ell_{1}}_{\flat}\cdots
,
where
\Gamma_{0}
is timed.
If
\mathcal{R}
is finite, we write

\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}
for the untimed part of its last configuration.

Below we gently introduce the BitML semantics,
first illustrating each construct through examples,
and then giving the general rule.
Labels represent the actions performed by participants.
A decoration
{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}:{\cdots}

in the label means that the action can be performed only by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
.
The absence of such a decoration means that the action can be performed by anyone.
Note that labels are not instrumental to define the BitML semantics: yet, they are essential to the definition of liquidity, since there we need to associate actions to the participants who can perform them.
In the examples, we will omit the labels.

Deposits

A deposit
\langle{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}},{v}\rangle_{x}

it can be split into smaller deposits, joined with another deposit,
transferred to another participant, or destroyed.
In all cases, its owner

must first authorise the action.
The reduction rules for deposits are detailed in

Rule

The label of the form

The rules

Revealing secrets

Any participant can reveal one of her secrets, using the rule:

\small\dfrac{N\neq\bot}{\{{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}:{\mathord{{a}}}\#{N}\}\mid\Gamma\xrightarrow{{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}:{a}}_{\flat}{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}:{\mathord{{a}}}\#{N}\mid\Gamma}{\tiny\textsc{[C-AuthRev]}}

The premise
N\neq\bot
is needed to avoid the case where
a participant does not know the secret she has committed to.
Indeed, at the level of Bitcoin, commitments are represented as
cryptographic hashes of bitstrings,
and revealing a secret amounts to broadcasting a preimage,
i.e. a value whose hash is equal to the committed value.
If a participant commits to a random value,
then with overwhelming probability she will not be able to
provide a preimage.
The label
{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}:{a}
represents the fact that only

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
, the participant who performed the commitment,
can fire the transition.

Liquidity

In this

a notion of contract liquidity.
Aiming at generality, we parameterise this notion over:

•

•

Once these parameters are fixed, we define when,

Roughly, this happens when the honest participant

can always make the funds stored by the contracts

In the meanwhile, the other participants may play against her,
e.g. by not revealing some secrets,
or by not granting their authorizations for some branch.
Note that by suitably instantiating the parameters

and

For instance, for the concrete semantics we choose

and as

Before formalising liquidity, we give some further intuition.
Assume that
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
is an honest participant,
who cares about the liquidity of a set of contracts
X
in
\Gamma
.
After an arbitrary sequence of transitions

\Gamma\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}
,
where any participant may perform actions,

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
wants to liquidate all the contracts in
\Gamma^{\prime}

originating from
X
, transferring their funds to
participants’ deposits.
We want
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
to be able to liquidate contracts
without

the help of the other participants.

{defi}

[Liquidity]

Let
\rightarrow
be an LTS, let
\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}
be a subset of
its labels, let
\Gamma
be a configuration of the LTS,
and let
x
be a contract name.
We say that:

•

x
is
liquidable
in
\Gamma
if there exists a run

\mathcal{R}\;=\;\Gamma\xrightarrow{\ell_{1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\ell_{n}}\Gamma^{\prime}

such that:

(1)

for all
i\in 1..n
,
\ell_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}
;

(2)

there exists no
z\in\operatorname{cn}(\Gamma^{\prime})
such that

\mathit{orig}_{\Gamma}(\mathcal{R},z)=x
.

•

x
is
liquid
in
\Gamma

if, for all runs
\mathcal{R}=\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xrightarrow{}\Gamma^{\prime}
,
all the contract names
y

with
\mathit{orig}_{\Gamma}(\mathcal{R},y)=x

are liquidable in
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

We extend this to sets
X
of contracts names:

X
is liquid in
\Gamma
iff all
x\in X
are liquid in
\Gamma
.

Intuitively, a contract
x
is liquidable by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

when
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
can perform a sequence of transitions which
eventually lead to a configuration containing no contract names
originated from
x
.
Consequently, all the funds in the contract
x
have been
transferred to participants’ deposits.
When liquidating
x
,

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
’s moves can not reveal secrets of other participants,
or generate authorizations for them:

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
must be able to unfreeze the funds on her own,
performing actions
\ell_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}
.
Note that if
x\not\in\operatorname{cn}(\Gamma)
, then

x
is trivially liquidable in
\Gamma
.

The notion of liquidity is based upon that of liquidability.
A contract
x
is liquid in a configuration
\Gamma

when, after an arbitrary sequence of moves
performed by
any
participant,
the contract names originated by
x
are liquidable by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
.

We remark that, although Definition
3

will be instantiated with the semantics of BitML,
the basic concepts it relies upon
(runs, origin of contracts, moves of a participant)
are quite general.
Hence, our notion of liquidity,
as well as the variants proposed in Section
7
,
can be applied to other languages for smart contracts,
using their transition semantics.

Note that liquidability in Definition
3

requires that the moves
\ell_{1}\ldots\ell_{n}
are performed atomically,
effectively forbidding the adversary to nterfere.
Atomicity might be realistic in some blockchains, but not in others.
For instance,
Algorand features atomic sequences of transactions natively
[
BBL+21
]
,
while in Ethereum it is possible to perform atomically
a sequence
\ell_{1}\ldots\ell_{n}
of calls
by deploying a new contract with a suitable function which
calls
\ell_{1}\ldots\ell_{n}
in sequence.
Bitcoin, instead, does not support atomic sequences of transactions:
an honest participant could start to perform the sequence,
but at some point in the middle the adversary can interfere.
Repeated interference could lead to an infinite run,
where each attempt by the honest participant is hindered by the adversary.
To illustrate the issue, consider a (not-BitML) LTS
with states 0, 1, 2 and transitions

0\xrightarrow{s}1
,

1\xrightarrow{s}2
,
and
1\xrightarrow{p}0
,
where 0 is the initial state and 2 is the final state, where the funds have been liquidated.
If atomic sequences of moves were allowed,
an honest participant could always reach the final state by firing the atomic sequence
s\ s
.
Otherwise, an adversary could always prevent the participant from reaching the final state,
by firing
p
after each
s
.
Hence, this LTS would be considered liquid only by assuming atomic sequences of moves.

However, infinite adversary interference as those shown above
are not reproducible in BitML, for the following reason.
Our notion of liquidity requires that any descendent
of the contract must be liquidable by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

alone
.
This means that
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
can do that by performing a sequence of moves
which do not include any renegotiation, since renegotiations
can be finalized only with the cooperation of all the other participants.
Without renegotiation, the contract eventually terminates,
so an adversary can interfere at most a finite number of times.
After the last interference,
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
has still a way of
terminating the contract, and she can do this alone,
satisfying item (
2
) of Definition
3
.

2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
stored therein are frozen.
We can recover liquidity by replacing

with the following contract,
where
t^{\prime}>t
:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Win}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{2}}}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Win}}+(\textup{{after}}\,{t^{\prime}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{reveal}}\,{\mathord{{a}}}.\;\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}})+(\textup{{after}}\,{t^{\prime}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{reveal}}\,{\mathord{{b}}}.\;\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}})

Now, even if one of the two players does not reveal,
the honest player fire her
reveal
at time

,
liquidating the
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
stored in
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Win}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{2}}}
.

Zero-coupon bonds

A zero-coupon bond
[
JES00
]
is a financial contract
where an investor
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
pays
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
upfront to a bank
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
,
and receives back
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
after a maturity date (say, year 2030).
We can express this contract in BitML as follows.
The contract precondition requires
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
to provide a deposit
x

of
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
to provide a deposit of
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
.
The contract is as follows:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\textup{{split}}\;\big({1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}\;\mid\;{2}\rightarrow{\textup{{after}}\,{\text{2030}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}}\big)

Upon stipulation, all the deposits required in the preconditions
pass under the control of
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB}}
,
and can no longer be spent by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
.
The contract splits these funds in two parts:

1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
, that can be withdrawn by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
at any moment,
and
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
, that can be withdrawn by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
after the maturity date.

Although
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB}}
correctly implements the functionality of zero-coupon bounds,
it is quite impractical:
for the whole period from the stipulation to the maturity date,

2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
are frozen within the contract, and cannot be used by the bank
in any way.
Although this is a desirable feature for the investor,
since it guarantees that he will receive
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
even if the bank bankrupts,
it is quite undesirable for the bank.
In the real world, the bank would be free to use its own funds,
together with those of investors,
to make further financial transactions through which to repay the investments.
The risk that the bank bankrupts is mitigated
by external mechanisms, like insurances or government intervention.

To overcome this issue, we can exploit renegotiation.
We first revise the precondition, which now requires only
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
’s deposit.
The revised contract is:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB2}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\textup{{split}}\;\big({1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}\;\mid\;{0}\rightarrow{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}}}}\big)

X

\displaystyle\;=\;\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}\textup{:}\,{2}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{\;\textup{{after}}\,{\text{2030}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}}

As before, the bank can withdraw
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
at any moment after stipulation.
In the second part of the
split
,
the participants renegotiate the contract:
if they both agree,
0\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
pass under the control of the contract
X
.
The precondition of
X
requires the bank
to provide
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
in a fresh deposit;

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

after the maturity date.
The crucial difference with
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB}}
is that the deposit variable
d

is instantiated at
renegotiation

which must be fixed at

The revised contract
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB2}}
solves the problem of
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB}}
,
in that it no longer freezes
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
for the whole duration of the bond:
the bank could choose to renegotiate the contract,
paying
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}

This flexibility comes at a cost, since
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
loses
the guarantee to eventually receive
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
.

More specifically, we assume an insurance company

that,
for an annual premium of

covers a face amount of

\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\textup{:}\,{1}\,\textup{{@}}\,{x_{1}}$}\mid\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}\textup{:}\,{p}\,\textup{{@}}\,{x_{2}}$}\mid\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf I}}}\textup{:}\,{f}\,\textup{{@}}\,{x_{3}}$}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it ZCB3}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\textup{{split}}\;\big({1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}\textup{:}\,{p}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.0pt\textup{{split}}\;\big({p}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf I}}}}}}

\displaystyle{\texttt{{X}}}\langle{10}\rangle

\displaystyle\;=\;\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}\textup{:}\,{2}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.0pt\textup{{split}}\;\big({f}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf I}}}}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 48.0pt\mid{2}\rightarrow{\textup{{after}}\,{\text{2030}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}}\big)

The contract starts by transferring
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}

.

or

can redeem

also,

could e.g. increase the premium for future interactions
with
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
.

which sanction dishonest participants

and in messages broadcast by participants.
For instance, if
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
and

Despite the apparent complexity, the contract is liquid for any participant:
indeed, any potentially blocking renegotiation has an alternative
timeout branch, which allows to liquidate the contract.

A fair recursive coin flipping game

Consider a simple game where two players repeatedly flip coins,
and the one who wins two consecutive flips takes the pot.
The precondition requires each player to deposit
3\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}

A safe abstraction of the BitML semantics

The concrete BitML semantics is infinite-state,
because participants can always create new deposits,
stipulate new contracts and renegotiate them,
and can advance the current time.
In this

we introduce an abstract semantics of BitML,
which reduces the state space to a finite one,
safely approximating liquidity.
We construct our abstraction in three steps:

X

A finite-state abstraction of BitML

The abstract semantics
\rightarrow_{\sharp}
is infinite-state,
even up-to renaming of contract names.
Indeed, each application of rule
[A-Rngt]
unfolds a contract
X
,
which can spawn other parallel contracts before recursing.
Therefore, the number of parallel contracts in reachable abstract configurations
can grow unboundedly.
This hinders verification based on model-checking the whole state space.

We now introduce another abstract semantics, called
\rightarrow_{\sharp\textit{fin}}
,
which has a
finite
number of reachable configurations
(up-to renaming of contract names),
and which preserves the liquidity w.r.t.
\rightarrow_{\sharp}
:
more specifically, a contract is liquid w.r.t.
\rightarrow_{\sharp}

if and only if it is liquid w.r.t.
\rightarrow_{\sharp\textit{fin}}
.
We define the transition relation
\rightarrow_{\sharp\textit{fin}}
by the following rule:

\dfrac{\Gamma\xrightarrow{\ell}_{\sharp}\Gamma^{\prime}\quad\nexists\Delta,{\texttt{{X}}}:\Gamma^{\prime}=\Delta\mid{\texttt{{X}}}\mid{\texttt{{X}}}}{\Gamma\xrightarrow{\ell}_{\sharp\textit{fin}}\Gamma^{\prime}}

Intuitively,
\rightarrow_{\sharp\textit{fin}}
can mimic any move of
\rightarrow_{\sharp}
,
except for the moves
[A-Rngt]
which renegotiate a contract
which has already been renegotiated.
Technically, this is ensured by constraining the configuration to contain at most one
occurrence of each recursion variable
X
.

Theorem 5
.

Starting from any abstract configuration,
the set of states reachable through
\rightarrow_{\sharp\textit{fin}}
is finite,
up-to renaming of contract names.

.

Using this workaround we have managed to compile a 4-players lottery
into standard transactions,
at the price of increasing the number of transactions
(587 for the standard version
vs.
138 for the nonstandard one).
Similar techniques (e.g. simplification of predicates
7
7

7

https://github.com/bitml-lang/bitml-compiler/blob/master/bitml/exp.rkt

) allowed us to compile
all the contracts in
Table

1

into standard Bitcoin transactions.

In general, the 520-byte constraint intrinsically
limits the expressiveness of Bitcoin contracts:
for instance, since public keys are 33 bytes long,
a contract which needs to simultaneously verify 15 signatures
can not be implemented using standard transactions.

7.
Variants of liquidity

We now discuss some variants of the notion of liquidity of Section
3
.

Liquidity under a strategy

The runs
\mathcal{R}=\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xrightarrow{}\Gamma^{\prime}

in Definition
3
allow any participant to perform

any
enabled move.
For instance, consider the contract:

\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\textup{:}\,{1}\,\textup{{@}}\,{x}$}\mid{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\!:\!\textup{{secret}}\,{\mathord{{\mathord{{a}}}}}\mid{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\!:\!\textup{{secret}}\,{\mathord{{\mathord{{a^{\prime}}}}}}\}{\big(\textup{{reveal}}\,{\mathord{{a}}}.\,\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}\,+\,\textup{{reveal}}\,{\mathord{{a^{\prime}}}}.\,{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}\big)}

This contract is
not
liquid for
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
:
indeed, if
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
performs
\textup{{reveal}}\,{\mathord{{a^{\prime}}}}
, the reduct

{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}
is not liquidable by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
alone,
since
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
may refuse to give his authorization.
To overcome this issue,
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
can follow the strategy of
always performing the
\textup{{reveal}}\,{\mathord{{a}}}
.
In this way, she is sure to be able liquidate the contract
from any reachable state.
A possible extension of the notion of liquidity
in Definition
3
is to make it parametric on

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
’s strategy, and consider only the runs

\mathcal{R}=\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xrightarrow{}\Gamma^{\prime}

which are coherent with it.

Multiparty liquidity

Definition
3
requires that
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

alone

can liquidate each descendent of the contract.
We can relax it by considering a set of collaborative participants.
For instance, consider an escrow contract between
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
,
involving also a mediator
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Escrow}}={{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}\;+\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}\;+\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Resolve}}}\;+\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Resolve}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Resolve}}=\textup{{split}}({0.1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}}}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 72.0pt\mid\;{0.9}\rightarrow{{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}+{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}})

After the contract has been stipulated,

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
can choose to pay
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
, by authorizing the first branch.
Similarly,
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
can allow
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
to take her money back,
by authorizing the second branch.
If they do not agree, any of them can invoke a mediator
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
to resolve the dispute,
invoking a
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Resolve}}
branch.
There, the initial deposit (say, of
v\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
) is split in two parts:

0.1v
goes to the mediator,
while
0.9v
is assigned either to
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
,
depending on
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
’s choice.
This contract is
not
liquid for
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
,
because
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
can invoke the mediator, who can refuse to act,
freezing the funds within the contract (similarly for
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
).
Instead, assuming that
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
is collaborative,
the contract is liquid for both
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
.
Indeed, a collaborative
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
will always authorize either the

\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}
or the
\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}

to unlock
0.9v
.
Multiparty liquidity where all participants are collaborative
was used e.g. in
[
TDD+18
]
in the context of Ethereum contracts.

Quantitative liquidity

Definition
3
requires that no funds remain frozen within the contract.
However, in some cases
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
could accept the fact that
a portion of the funds remain frozen, especially when these funds
would be assigned to other participants.
We could define a contract
v
-liquid
for
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

if at least
v
bitcoins are guaranteed to be redeemable
by anyone.
For instance,
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Lottery}}({\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Win}})
of Section
4

is non-liquid for
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
, but it is
4\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
-liquid.
Instead,
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Lottery}}({\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Win}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{2}}})
is
6\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
-liquid, and then also liquid,
under this strategy.
A refinement of this notion could require that at least
v\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}

are transferred to
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
, rather than to any participant.
Under this notion, both
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Lottery}}({\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Win}})
and
{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Lottery}}({\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{\it Win}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{2}}})

would be
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
-liquid for
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
.

Other variants of liquidity may take into account the time when funds become liquid,
the payoff of strategies (e.g., ruling out irrational adversaries),
or fairness issues.

8.
Variants of contract primitives

The renegotiation primitive we have proposed for BitML
is motivated by its simplicity, and by the possibility of compiling
into standard Bitcoin transactions.
By adding some degree of complexity, we can devise more general primitives,
which could be useful in certain scenarios.
We discuss below some alternatives.

Renegotiation-time parameters.

The primitive
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}}\langle{\vec{\mathcal{E}}}\rangle}

allows participants to choose at run-time only the deposit variables
used in the renegotiated contracts,
and to commit to new secrets.
A possible extension is to allow participants
to choose at run-time
arbitrary
values for the renegotiation
parameters
\vec{\mathcal{E}}
.

For instance, consider a mortgage payment,
where a buyer
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
must pay
10\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
to a bank
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
in 10 installments.
After
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
has paid the first five installments (of
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
each),
the bank might propose to renegotiate the contract,
varying the amount of the installment.
Using the BitML renegotiation primitive presented in Section
2
,
we could not model this contract, since the new amount and the number
of installments are unknown at the time of the original stipulation.
Technically, the issue is that the primitive
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}}\langle{\vec{\mathcal{E}}}\rangle}

only involves static expressions
\mathcal{E}
,
the value of which is determined at stipulation time.

To cope with non-statically known values,
we could extend guarded contracts with terms of the form

{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}}\langle{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}:v}\rangle}
,
declaring that the value
v
is to be chosen by
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}

at renegotiation time.
For instance, this would allow to model our installments payment plan
as
\texttt{{IPP}}{\langle{1}\rangle}
, with the following defining equations:

\displaystyle\texttt{{IPP}}{\langle{\beta<5}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\textup{:}\,{1}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{\big(\textup{{split}}\;{1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}\mid{0}\rightarrow{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{\texttt{{IPP}}{\langle{\beta+1}\rangle}}}\big)}

\displaystyle\texttt{{IPP}}{\langle{5}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\textup{:}\,{1}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{\big(\textup{{split}}\;{1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}\mid{0}\rightarrow{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{{\texttt{{Y}}}\langle{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}:k,{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}:v}\rangle}}\big)}

\displaystyle{\texttt{{Y}}}\langle{\beta\neq 1,\beta^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\textup{:}\,{\beta^{\prime}}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{\big(\textup{{split}}\;{\beta^{\prime}}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}\mid{0}\rightarrow{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}\;{{\texttt{{Y}}}\langle{\beta-1,\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}}\big)}

\displaystyle{\texttt{{Y}}}\langle{1,\beta^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\textup{:}\,{\beta^{\prime}}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{\;\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}

where in
\texttt{{IPP}}{\langle{5}\rangle}
, the bank chooses the number of installments
k
,
as well as the amount
v
of each installment.
Note that if
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
does not agree with these values,
the renegotiation fails.
A more refined version of the contract should take this possibility into
account, by adding suitable compensation branches.
Although adding the new primitive would moderately increase the complexity
of the semantics and of the compiler,
this extension can still be implemented on top of standard Bitcoin.

Renegotiation with a given set of participants.

As we have remarked in Section
2
,
a renegotiation can be performed only if
all

the participants of the contract agree.
We could relax this, by just requiring the agreement of
a
given
set of participants
(possibly, not among those who originally stipulated the contract).

For instance, consider an escrow service between a buyer
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

and a seller
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
for the purchase of an item worth
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
.
The normal case is when the
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
authorizes the transfer of
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}

after receiving the item, but it may happen that a dishonest
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}

never ships the item, or that a dishonest buyer never authorizes the payment.
To cope with these cases, the participants could renegotiate the contract,
including an escrow service
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
which mediates the dispute:

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}}\,+\,{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}}+\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}:{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}}:\textup{{rngt}}\;{{\texttt{{Refd}}{}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}\,+\,{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}:{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}}:\textup{{rngt}}\;{{\texttt{{Refd}}{}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}

\displaystyle\texttt{{Refd}}{}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf P}}}=\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf P}}}\textup{:}\,{0.1}\,\textup{{@}}\,{d}$}\}{\;\textup{{split}}\;\big({0.1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}}}}}\mid{1}\rightarrow{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf P}}}}}}}\big)

where
{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}:{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}}:\textup{{rngt}}\;{{\texttt{{Refd}}{}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}

means that only
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
need to agree in order
for the contract
\texttt{{Refd}}{}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}
to be executed, resolving the dispute.
In this case it is crucial that the renegotiation is possible
even without the agreement between
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
.
Indeed, if
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf M}}
decides to refund
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}

(by authorizing
\texttt{{Refd}}{}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}
),
it is not to be expected that also
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
agrees.
Similarly to the one discussed before,
also this extension can be implemented on-top of Bitcoin.

Non-consensual renegotiation.

In the variants of
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}{}
discussed before,
renegotiation requires one or more participants to agree.
Hence, each use of
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}{}
must include
suitable alternative branches,
to be fired in case the renegotiation fails.
In certain scenarios, we may want to renegotiate the contract
without the participants having to agree.
To this purpose, we can introduce a new primitive

call

X
, which continues as
X
without
requiring anyone to agree.
We assume that the defining equations of this primitive have the form

{\texttt{{X}}}({\vec{\beta}})=\{v\}{\mathord{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{C}}}}
,
where
v
represents the amount of

B

added to the contract,
by anyone.

We exemplify the new primitive in a two-players game which starts with
a bet of
1\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
from
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
, and a bet of
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
from
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}
.
Then, starting from
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}
, players take turns adding
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
each to the pot.
The first one who is not able to provide the additional
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
within a given time
loses the game, allowing the other player to take the whole pot.
The contract is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathord{{\color[rgb]{0.88,0.07,0.37}{C}}}

\displaystyle=\{\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}\textup{:}\,{1}\,\textup{{@}}\,{x}$}\mid\mbox{${{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}\textup{:}\,{2}\,\textup{{@}}\,{y}$}\}(\textup{{call}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}\langle{2}\rangle}+\textup{{after}}\,{1}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}})

\displaystyle{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}\langle{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\{2\}(\textup{{call}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}\langle{n+1}\rangle}+\textup{{after}}\,{n}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}}})

\displaystyle{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}\langle{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\{2\}(\textup{{call}}\;{{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}\langle{n+1}\rangle}+\textup{{after}}\,{n}\,\textup{:}\,{\textup{{withdraw}}\;{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}}})

Unlike
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}*}:{\textup{{rngt}}}{}
, the action
call

can be fired without the authorizations of all the players:
it just requires that the authorization to gather
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}

is provided, by anyone.
Even though the sender of these
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
is not specified in the contract,
it is implicit in the game mechanism:
for instance, when
{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}\langle{n}\rangle
calls

{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}\langle{n+1}\rangle
, only participant
{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}

is incentivized to add
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
,
since not doing so will make

win.

Implementing the
call
primitive on top of Bitcoin seems unfeasible:
even if it were possible to use complex off-chain multiparty computation
protocols
[
GMR+19
]
,
doing so might be impractical.
Rather, we would like to extend Bitcoin as much as needed
for the new primitive.
In our implementation of BitML,
we compile contracts to sets of transactions and make participants sign them.
In standard BitML this is doable since, at stipulation time,
we can finitely over-approximate the reducts of the original contract.
Recursion can make this set infinite,
e.g.
{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}\langle{2}\rangle,{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}\langle{3}\rangle,\ldots
,
hence impossible to compile and sign statically.
A way to cope with this is to extend Bitcoin with
malleable

signatures which only cover the part of the transaction not affected
by the parameter
n
in
{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}\langle{n}\rangle
.
Further, signatures must not cover the
in
fields of transactions,
since they change as recursion unfolds.
In this way, the same signature can be reused for each call.

Adding malleability provides flexibility, but poses some risks.
For instance, instead of redeeming the transaction corresponding to

{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf A}}}}\langle{n}\rangle
with the transaction of

{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}\langle{n+1}\rangle

one could instead use the transaction of

{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}\langle{n+100}\rangle
,
since the two transactions have the same signature.
To overcome this problem, we could add a new opcode
to allow the output script of
{\texttt{{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.13,0.55,0.13}{\sf B}}}}\langle{n}\rangle

to access the parameter in the redeeming transaction,
so to verify that it is indeed
n+1
as intended.
Similarly, to check that we have
2\textup{\vtop{\vbox{\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}\hbox{B}}\hbox to7.08336pt{\hfil\hskip-0.29999pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hskip 0.80002pt\vrule height=1.29167pt,width=0.6458pt\hfil}}}
more in the new transaction,
an opcode could provide the value of the new output.
The same goal could be achieved by exploiting

covenants

[
MES16
,
OP17
,
BLZ20
]
.

9.
Conclusions

We have investigated linguistic primitives to renegotiate BitML contracts, and
their implementation on standard Bitcoin.
More expressive primitives could be devised
by relaxing this constraint,
e.g. assuming the extended UTXO model
[
CCM+20
]
.

Our verification technique is based on a sound abstraction
of the state space of contracts.
Since this abstraction is finite-state,
it can be model-checked to verify the required properties.
If we assume that integers are unbounded,
and that participants always accept renegotiations,
the extension of BitML presented in Section
2

can simulate a counter machine,
so making BitML Turing-complete.
Hence, any verification technique for BitML cannot be sound and complete.
Alternative techniques to model checking
(e.g., type-based approaches
[
DBHP19
]
)
could be used to analyse relevant contract properties.
