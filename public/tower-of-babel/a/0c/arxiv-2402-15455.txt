Rings with u − 1 u-1 quasinilpotent for each unit u u
Peter Danchev, Arash Javan, Omid Hasanzadeh, Ahmad Moussavi

Abstract
. We define and explore in-depth the notion of UQ rings by showing their important properties and by comparing their behavior with that of the well-known classes of UU rings and JU rings, respectively. Specifically, among the other established results, we prove that UQ rings are always Dedekind finite (often named directly finite) as well as that, for semipotent rings R R , the following equivalence hold: R / J ⁡ ( R ) R/J(R) is UQ ⇔ \iff R R is UQ having the property that the set Q ​ N ​ ( R ) QN(R) of quasinilpotent elements of R R coincides with the Jacobson radical J ⁡ ( R ) J(R) of R R .

1.
Introduction and Motivation

Throughout the present paper, all rings considered are associative and unital. For such a ring
R
, the notations of Jacobson radical, the group of units, the set of nilpotent elements, the set of quasinilpotent elements and the set of all idempotent elements of
R
are denoted by
J(R)
,
U(R)
,
N(R)
,
QN(R)
and
Id(R)
, respectively. For any subring
S
of
R
, we shall almost always assume that
1_{R}=1_{S}
, which means that the subring is unital. If, however, it is
not
the case, we hereafter will explicitly indicate this in the text. As usual, the used terminology is standard being in agreement with the classical book
[
19
]
.

An element
a
of a ring
R
is said to be
quasinilpotent
if
1-ax
is invertible for every
x\in R
with
xa=ax
. It is thereby clear that both
N(R)
and
J(R)
are contained in
QN(R)
. It is also worth noting that quasinilpotents play an important role in the investigation of the complicated structure of a Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}
. By means of quasinilpotents, some interesting concepts were introduced like these: (strongly) J-clean rings
[
4
]
, nil-clean rings
[
11
]
, generalized Drazin inverses
[
14
]
, quasipolar rings
[
26
]
, etc.

A ring
R
is called a
UU ring
if
U(R)=1+N(R)
. This notion was introduced by Cǎlugǎreanu
[
1
]
, and furthermore studied in more details by Danchev and Lam in
[
10
]
. They showed that
R
is strongly nil-clean if, and only i,f
R
is an exchange UU ring. In
[
13
]
, Zhou et al. demonstrated that
R
is a UU ring if, and only if, every invertible element (i.e., a unit) in
R
is uniquely nil-clean.

It is clear that
1+J(R)\subseteq U(R)
. Motivated by this inclusion and the above idea of UU rings, in
[
9
]
were introduced the concept of a
JU ring
, that is, a ring
R
for which
U(R)=1+J(R)
. Further, this notion was also studied in
[
17
]
, defined as a
UJ ring
. In fact, it was shown there that
R
is a UJ ring if, and only if, every clean element of
R
is J-clean. The structural characterization of UJ rings and their potential applications in various aspects of non-commutative ring theory have been examined in several recent studies
[
9
,
17
,
15
,
20
]
.

Inspired by what we have stated so far, we call a ring
R
a
UQ ring
if
U(R)=1+QN(R)
. It is evident that UU and UJ rings are both UQ rings, but the converse is manifestly
not
generally true. In the current article, we shall provide below a concrete counterexample to substantiate this statement.

Next, we say that an element
a\in R
is
(strongly) quasi nil-clean
if there exists
e\in Id(R)
such that
a-e\in QN(R)
(with the additional commuting condition
ea=ae
in the strongly case). Let us recollect that an element
a\in R
is said to be
generalized strongly Drazin invertible
(or just
gs-Drazin invertible
for short) (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
) if there is an element
x\in R
satisfying the requirements

xax=x,ax=xa,\textrm{and}a-ax\in QN(R).

Similarly, a ring
R
is called
gs-Drazin invertible
if each its element is gs-Drazin invertible. In
[
12
]
, Gürgün showed that an element
a\in R
is strongly quasi nil-clean if, and only if,
a
is gs-Drazin invertible. Later on, in
[
3
]
it was illustrated that a Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}
is gs-Drazin invertible if, and only if, for all
a\in A
,
a-a^{2}\in QN(A)
.

Our motivating tool is to give a satisfactory description of UQ rings by comparing their crucial properties with these of UU and JU rings, respectively, as well as to find some new exotic properties of UQ rings that are not too characteristically frequently seen in the existing literature.

Our organization to achieve the desired establishments is subsumed in the following manner: In the next second section, we list our examples and individual properties of UQ rings. In the subsequent third section, among the other obtained results, we establish two important theorems, namely Theorems
3.1
and
3.13
, as well as the interesting Lemma
3.7
. In the next fourth section, we study when groups rings are UQ and thus we prove the enclosed Theorem
4.4
. Moreover, we somewhat reverse it by proving Proposition
4.6
and, in this direction, we reconfirm once the recognizability of Theorem
4.7
which was originally obtained in
[
18
]
. In the next final fifth section, we show the abundance of some more unexpected classes of UQ rings which depend on the complicated structure of certain matrices.

2.
Examples and Basic Properties of UQ Rings

We begin our work with the following three constructions.

Example 2.1
.

For any ring R, we have:

(1)
J(R),N(R)\subseteq QN(R)
, but the reverse relation does not necessarily hold. Let
S=M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
and
K=\mathbb{F}_{2}[[x]]
. Take

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\in S
, then
A\notin J(S)
but
A\in QN(S)
(see
[
24
, Example 4.3]
). On the other hand,
x\in QN(K)
but
x\notin N(K)
.

(2) We always have
QN(R)\cap U(R)=\emptyset
and
QN(R)\cap Id(R)=\emptyset
(see
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
).

(3) Every UJ ring and every UU ring are UQ rings, but the converse is not necessarily true. Let
S=\mathbb{F}_{2}\left\langle x,y\mid x^{2}=0\right\rangle
,
K=\mathbb{F}_{2}[[x]]
and
L=S\times K
. Then
S
is a UQ ring but not a UJ ring (see
[
17
, Example 2.2]
and
[
10
, Example 2.5]
). Also, by Corollary
2.9
,
K
is a UQ ring but not a UU ring, since
1+x\in U(K)
but
x\notin N(K)
. And
L
is a UQ ring but neither a UJ nor a UU ring.

We call a subring
S
of
R
a
good subring
if
U(R)\cap S=U(S)
. Clearly,
R
is a good subring of
R[x]
and
R[[x]]
. However,
R[x]
is obviously
not
necessarily a good subring of
R[[x]]
, because
1+x\in U(\mathbb{Z}[[x]])\cap\mathbb{Z}[x]
, but
1+x\notin U(\mathbb{Z}[x])
.

We continue our considerations with a series of technicalities needed for our successful presentation in the sequel.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
S
be a good subring of
R
. Then
QN(R)\cap S\subseteq QN(S)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
S
be a good subring of
R
, so if
R
is a UQ ring. Then
S
is also a UQ ring.

Proof.

Let
u\in U(S)\subseteq U(R)=1+QN(R)
, thus
u-1\in QN(R)\cap S\subseteq QN(S)
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(R_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of rings for some index set
I
. Then
QN(\prod_{i\in I}R_{i})

=\prod_{i\in I}QN(R_{i})
.

Proof.

Let
(a_{i})\in QN(\prod_{i\in I}R_{i})
and assume that a
{}_{i}b_{i}=b_{i}a_{i}
. Therefore,
(a_{i})(b_{i})=(b_{i})(a_{i})
, implying that
1-(a_{i})(b_{i})\in U(\prod_{i\in I}R_{i})
. This implies that
1-a_{i}b_{i}\in U(R_{i})
. Hence, for every
i\in I
, we have
a_{i}\in QN(R_{i})
.

Conversely, let
(a_{i})\in\prod_{i\in I}QN(R_{i})
and assume that
(a_{i})(b_{i})=(b_{i})(a_{i})
. Therefore,
a_{i}b_{i}=b_{i}a_{i}
, which implies that
1-a_{i}b_{i}\in U(R_{i})
. This in turn implies that
1-(a_{i})(b_{i})\in U(\prod_{i\in I}R_{i})
. Hence,
(a_{i})\in QN(\prod_{i\in I}R_{i})
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

A direct product
\prod_{i\in I}R_{i}
of rings is UQ if and only if each
R_{i}
is UQ.

Proof.

We know that
(a_{i})\in U(\prod R_{i})
exactly when
a_{i}\in U(R_{i})
, and with the aid of Lemma
2.4
we derive
QN(\prod R_{i})=\prod QN(R_{i})
, as required.
∎

For a subring
S
of a ring
R
, the set

\mathcal{R}[R,S]:=\{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n},s,s,\ldots):r_{i}\in R,s\in S,n\geq 1\},

with addition and multiplication defined componentwise, is a ring.

As an immediate consequence, we yield:

Corollary 2.6
.

The ring
\mathcal{R}[R,S]
is UQ ring if, and only if,
R
and
S
are UQ rings.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
R
be a UQ ring and
e\in Id(R)
. Then, the corner subring
eRe
is a UQ ring.

Proof.

Let
u\in U(eRe)
with inverse
v
. Therefore,
u+(1-e)\in U(R)
with inverse
v+(1-e)
, so
u+(1-e)\in 1+QN(R)
and
u-e\in QN(R)\cap eRe=QN(eRe)
by
[
26
, Lemma 3.5]
. Hence,
u\in e+QN(eRe)=1_{eRe}+QN(eRe)
, and thus
eRe
is a UQ ring, as claimed.
∎

Let
R
be a ring and
M
a bimodule over
R
. The trivial extension of
R
and
M
is defined as

T(R,M)=\{(r,m):r\in R\text{ and }m\in M\},

with addition defined componentwise and multiplication defined by the equality

(r,m)(s,n)=(rs,rn+ms).

Observe that the trivial extension
T(R,M)
is isomorphic to the subring

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}r&m\\
0&r\end{pmatrix}:r\in R\text{ and }m\in M\right\}

consisting of the formal
2\times 2
matrix ring
\begin{pmatrix}R&M\\
0&R\end{pmatrix}
, and also
T(R,R)\cong R[x]/\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle
.

A Morita context is defined as a 4-tuple
\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
, where
A
and
B
are rings,
{}_{A}M_{B}
and
{}_{B}N_{A}
are bimodules, and there exist context products
M\times N\to A
and
N\times M\to B
written multiplicatively as
(w,z)=wz
and
(z,w)=zw
. The Morita context
\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
forms an associative ring with the usual matrix operations.

A Morita context is referred to as trivial if the context products are trivial, meaning that
MN=0
and
NM=0
(see
[
22
, p. 1993]
). In this case, we have the isomorphism

\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}\cong T(A\times B,M\oplus N),

where
\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
represents a trivial Morita context as stated in
[
16
]
.

The following statement represents some critical set-theoretical inclusions.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
R
,
S
be rings,
N
be an
(R,S)
-bimodule, and
M
a bimodule over
R
. Then we have:

(1)
\{(r,m)\in T(R,M):r\in QN(R)\text{ and }m\in M\}\subseteq QN(T(R,M))
.

(2)
\{\begin{pmatrix}r&m\\
0&s\end{pmatrix}:r\in QN(R),s\in QN(S)\text{ and }m\in N\}\subseteq QN(\begin{pmatrix}R&N\\
0&S\end{pmatrix})
.

(3)
\{(a_{ij})\in T_{n}(R):a_{ii}\in QN(R)\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\}\subseteq QN(T_{n}(R))
.

(4)
\{a_{0}+\cdots a_{n-1}x^{n-1}\in R[x]/\left\langle x^{n}\right\rangle:a_{0}\in QN(R)\}\subseteq QN(R[x]/\left\langle x^{n}\right\rangle)
.

(5)
\{a_{0}+a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+\cdots\in R[[x]]:a_{0}\in QN(R)\}\subseteq QN(R[[x]]
.

In the above relations, equality holds when
R
and
S
are UQ rings.

Proof.

(1) Assuming
(r,m)\in T(QN(R),M)
and
(s,n)(r,m)=(r,m)(s,n)
, since
r\in QN(R)
and
rs=sr
, so we must have
1-rs\in U(R)
, it follows that
(1,0)-(r,m)(s,n)=(1-rs,\ast)\in T(U(R),M)=U(T(R,M))
.
Now, if
R
is a UQ ring, then for every
(r,m)\in QN(T(R,M))
, we have
1+r\in U(R)=1+QN(R)
, so
r\in QN(R)
.

(2) We know that
\begin{pmatrix}R&N\\
0&S\end{pmatrix}\cong T(R\times S,N)
, so point (1) along with Lemma
2.4
allow us to see that nothing remains to be proven.

(3) It is sufficient to substitute
S=T_{n-1}(R)
and
N=R^{n-1}
in (2).

(4) We know that

R[x]/\left\langle x^{n}\right\rangle\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&\cdots&a_{n}\\
0&a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n-1}\\
0&0&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{n-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a_{1}\end{pmatrix}\in T_{n}(R):a_{i}\in R\right\}.

So, it is clear that
R[x]/\left\langle x^{n}\right\rangle
is a good subring of
T_{n}(R)
. Therefore, according to point (3) and Lemma
2.2
, the proof is self-evident.

Reciprocally, if
R
is a UQ ring and

f=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots a_{n}\in QN(R[x]/\left\langle x^{n}\right\rangle),

then
1-a_{0}\in U(R)=1+QN(R)
, so
a_{0}\in QN(R)
.

(5) Assuming

f=\sum_{i=0}a_{i}x^{i}\in QN(R)+R[[x]]x

and

g=\sum_{i=0}b_{i}x^{i}\in R[[x]]

such that
fg=gf
, we find that
a_{0}b_{0}=b_{0}a_{0}
whence
1-a_{0}b_{0}\in U(R)
. Consequently,

1-fg\in U(R)+R[[x]]x=U(R[[x]]).

Thus,
f\in QN(R[[x]])
. Now, if
R
is a UQ ring and
f\in QN(R[[x]])
, we have

(1+a_{0})+\sum_{i=1}a_{i}x^{i}=1+f\in U(R[[x]])=

U(R)+R[[x]]x=1+QN(R)+R[[x]]x.

This, in turn, forces that
a_{0}\in QN(R)
, as required.
∎

As a direct consequence, we extract:

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
R
,
S
be rings,
N
be an
(R,S)
-bimodule, and
M
a bimodule over
R
. Then we have:

(1) The trivial extension
T(R,M)
is a UQ ring if and only if
R
is a UQ ring

(2) The formal triangular matrix ring
\begin{pmatrix}R&N\\
0&S\end{pmatrix}
is a UQ ring if and only if
R
and
S
are a UQ ring.

(3) For
n\geq 1
,
T_{n}(R)
is a UQ ring if and only if
R
is a UQ ring.

(4) For
n\geq 1
,
R[x]/\left\langle x^{n}\right\rangle
is a UQ ring if and only if
R
is a UQ ring.

(5)
R[[x]]
is a UQ ring if, and only if,
R
is a UQ ring.

Proof.

We only need to establish (1), as the other cases can be proved similarly. Suppose
T(R,M)
is a UQ ring, and we can consider
R
as a good subring of
T(R,M)
(because,
R\cong T(R,0)
). Therefore, by Lemma
2.3
,
R
is a UQ ring. Now, conversely, if
R
is a UQ ring and
(r,m)\in U(T(R,M))
, then
r\in U(R)
. Since
R
is a UQ ring, we have
1-r\in QN(R)
. Thus, by Lemma
2.8
,
(1,0)-(r,m)=(1-r,m)\in T(QN(R),M)\subseteq QN(T(R,M))
.
∎

3.
Principal Results

We now come to our chief assertion which gives necessary and sufficient conditions in the following two situations. Standardly, the symbol
N_{\ast}(R)
stands for the lower nil-radical of a ring
R
. Recall that a ring
R
is termed
2
-primal
, provided that
N(R)=N_{\ast}(R)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, the following two items are true:

(1)
R
is a
2
-primal ring if, and only if,
QN(R[x])=N_{\ast}(R)[x]
.

(2)
R
is a reduced ring if, and only if,
QN(R[x])=\{0\}
.

Proof.

(1) Let us assume
R
is a
2
-primal ring. If
f\in N_{\ast}(R)[x]
, then according to the idealness of
N(R)
, for every
g\in R[x]
such that
fg=gf
, we have
1-fg(0)\in U(R)
and
fg(n)\in N(R)
for every
n
. Therefore, by
[
5
, Theorem 2.5]
, we conclude that
1-fg\in U(R[x])
. Thus,
f\in QN(R[x])
.

Now, if
f\in QN(R[x])
, then
1-xf\in U(R[x])
since
xf=fx
. In virtue of
[
5
, Theorem 2.5]
, one derives that
a_{i}\in N(R)
for every
0\leq i\leq n
. Therefore,
f\in N(R)[x]=N_{\ast}(R)[x]
.

Conversely, let us assume that
QN(R[x])=N_{\ast}(R)[x]
. If
a\in N(R)
and
a^{n}=0
, then
a^{n}\in QN(R[x])
. By virtue of
[
8
, Proposition 2.7(1)]
, one infers that
a\in QN(R[x])=N_{*}(R)[x]
. Thus,
a\in N_{*}(R)
.

(2) Based on what we have proved in the first part, nothing remains to be showed.
∎

As two consequent corollaries, we deduce:

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
R
be a 2-primal ring. Then, we have:

J(R[x])=QN(R[x])=\text{N}(R)[x]=\text{N}_{*}(R)[x]=\text{N}_{*}(R[x])

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Theorem
3.1
, it can be shown that
J(R[x])=N(R)[x]
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
R
be a
2
-primal ring. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)
R
is a UU-ring.

(2)
R[x]
is a UQ-ring.

(3)
R[x]
is a UJ-ring.

(4)
R[x]
is a UU-ring.

Proof.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(4). This is immediate directing to
[
13
, Theorem 2.11]
.

(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3). It follows at once from Corollary
3.2
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). Let us assume
f=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}\in U(R[x])
. Since
R
is a
2
-primal ring, exploiting
[
5
, Theorem 2.5]
, we may write
a_{0}\in U(R)
and
a_{i}\in N(R)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. However, since
R
is a UU ring, we find that

1-f=(1-a_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}\in N(R)[x]=QN(R[x]).

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1). Let us assume
u\in U(R)
. Then, we can write that

u\in U(R[x])=1+QN(R[x])=1+N(R)[x].

Therefore,
u-1\in\text{Nil}(R)
.
∎

Our next series of technical claims is as follows:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
b\in Z(R)
. Then, we have:

(1) If
a\in QN(R)
, then
ab\in QN(R)
.

(2) If
a
and
b\in QN(R)
, then
a+b\in QN(R)
.

Proof.

(1) Assume
x\in R
such that
x(ab)=(ab)x
. So,
(bx)a=a(bx)
. Since
a\in QN(R)
, we have
1-a(bx)\in U(R)
, which implies that
ab\in QN(R)
.

(2) Assume
x\in R
such that
x(a+b)=(a+b)x
. Since
b\in Z(R)
, we have
ax=xa
and
1+xb\in U(R)
. Therefore, we can conclude that
((1+xb)^{-1}x)a=a((1+xb)^{-1}x)
. This implies that
1+x(a+b)=(1+xb)(1+(1+xb)^{-1}xa)\in U(R)
. Hence,
a+b\in QN(R)
.
∎

In the above lemma, the condition
b\in Z(R)
is surely
not
redundant and cannot be removed. To substantiate this, let us consider for instance the setting:
R=M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
a=e_{12}
, and
b=e_{21}
. According to Example
2.1
(1), we have
a,b\in QN(R)
. However, based on Example
2.1
(2), we see that
ab
and
a+b
are not in
QN(R)
. Therefore, it is
not
necessarily true that
QN(R)
is a subring of
R
.

According to
[
17
, Lemma 1.1]
, a ring
R
is UJ if and only if
U(R)+U(R)=J(R)
. Similarly, we can state the following lemma:

Lemma 3.5
.

R
is a UQ ring if, and only if,
U(R)+(U(R)\cap Z(R))=QN(R)

Proof.

Evidently,
QN(R)
is always a subset of
U(R)+(U(R)\cap Z(R))
. Now, if
R
is a UQ ring and
u\in U(R)
and
v\in U(R)\cap Z(R)
, then
1+u
,
1-v\in QN(R)
. Therefore, by Lemma
3.4
, we have
u+v=(1+u)-(1-v)\in QN(R)
. The reverse is also evident since for every
u\in U(R)
, we have
u-1\in QN(R)
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

For any ring
S\neq 0
and any integer
n\geq 2
, the matrix ring
M_{n}(S)
of size
n
is not a UQ ring.

Proof.

Since
M_{2}(S)
is isomorphic to a corner ring of
M_{n}(S)
(for
n\geq 2
), it suffices to show that
M_{2}(S)
is not a UQ ring. Consider the matrix

U=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},
where is a unit. Since

I-U=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}

is a unit too, it cannot be quasinilpotent, so
M_{2}(S)
is not a UQ ring, as asserteds.
∎

A set
\{e_{ij}:1\leq i,j\leq n\}
of nonzero elements of
R
is said to be a system of
n^{2}
matrix units if
e_{ij}e_{st}=\delta_{js}e_{it}
, where
\delta_{jj}=1
and
\delta_{js}=0
for
j\neq s
. In this case,
e:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{ii}
is an idempotent of
R
and
eRe\cong M_{n}(S)
, where

S=\{r\in eRe:re_{ij}=e_{ij}r,~~\textrm{for all}~~i,j=1,2,...,n\}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Every UQ ring is Dedekind finite.

Proof.

If
R
is not a Dedekind finite ring, then there exist elements
a,b\in R
such that
ab=1
but
ba\neq 1
. Assuming
e_{ij}=a^{i}(1-ba)b^{j}
and
e=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{ii}
, there exists a nonzero ring
S
such that
eRe\cong M_{n}(S)
. However, owing to Proposition
2.7
,
eRe
is a UQ ring, so
M_{n}(S)
must also be a UQ ring, which contradicts proposition
3.6
.
∎

We, thus, infer the following consequence.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
R
be a UQ ring and let
a,b\in R.
Then,
1-ab\in QN(R)
if, and only if,
1-ba\in QN(R)
.

Proof.

Assuming that
1-ab\in QN(R)
, we have
ab\in U(R)
. Therefore, invoking Lemma
3.7
,
a\in U(R)
. Since
1-ab=(1-ab)aa^{-1}\in QN(R)
, it follows from [2, Proposition 3] that
a^{-1}(1-ab)a\in QN(R)
. Hence,
1-ba=a^{-1}(1-ab)a\in QN(R)
, as needed.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
R
be a UQ ring and let
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
. The following hold:

(1) For any
u_{1},u_{2}\in U(R)
,
u_{1}+u_{2}\neq 1
.

(2) For any
\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{2}\in U(\overline{R})
,
\bar{u}_{1}+\bar{u}_{2}\neq\bar{1}
.

Proof.

(1) According to Lemma
3.5
and Example
2.1
, the proof is straightforward.

(2) Let us assume
\bar{u}_{1}+\bar{u}_{2}\neq\bar{1}
. Then,
u_{1},u_{2}\in U(R)
and
u_{1}+u_{2}-1\in J(R)
. Therefore,
u_{1}-1\in U(R)+J(R)
. However, since
U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R)
, there exists
u^{\prime}_{2}\in U(R)
such that
u_{1}+u^{\prime}_{2}=1
, which contradicts (1).
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
R
be a UQ ring. Then, the following conditions hold:

(1)
2\in QN(R)
and, in particular,
2\in J(R)
.

(2)
x\in QN(R)
precisely when
x^{2}\in QN(R)
.

(3)
QN(R)
is closed under addition uniquely when
QN(R)
is a subring of
R
.

Proof.

(1) It is elementary consulting with Lemma
3.5
.

(2) If
x\in QN(R)
, then
1\pm x\in U(R)
. Therefore,

1-x^{2}=(1-x)(1+x)\in U(R)=1+QN(R),

so
x^{2}\in QN(R)
. Now, if
x^{2}\in QN(R)
, then

(1-x)^{2}=(1+x^{2})-2x\in U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R),

which implies
1-x\in U(R)=1+QN(R)
, thus
x\in QN(R)
.

(3) Assuming that QN(R)is closed under addition, it is sufficient to show that QN(R) is closed under multiplication. Let’s assume
a,b\in QN(R)
. Then, we have

(1+x)(1+y)\in U(R)=1+QN(R)
, which gives
x+y+xy\in QN(R)
. Moreover, from the assumption that
x+y\in QN(R)
, it follows that
xy\in QN(R)
. Therefore, QN(R) is closed under multiplication.
∎

Concretely, we obtain:

Corollary 3.11
.

The ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is UQ if, and only if,
n
is a power of 2.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is UQ ring, then
2\in J(R)
, so it must be the case that
n=2^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Conversely, if
n=2^{k}
, then it is clear that
QN(R)=\{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{k}}:a\textit{ is even}\}
and
U(R)=\{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{k}}:a\text{ is odd}\}
.

∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
R
be a UQ ring. Then, the following conditions hold:

(1) If
R
is a division ring, then
R\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

(2) If

is a local ring, then

. (In particular, a local ring

is UQ if, and only if,

is uniquely clean ring).

is a semisimple ring, then

.

Proof.

(1) If
R
is a division ring, then we know that
QN(R)\setminus\{0\}\subseteq U(R)
, and since
QN(R)\cap U(R)=0
it must be
QN(R)=0
. Therefore,
U(R)={1}
.

(2) If

is a local ring, then
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
is a division ring. For every
\bar{u}\in U(\overline{R})\setminus\{\bar{1}\}
, we have

(3) If

. Consequently, based on Lemma

, we conclude

∎

Our next main assertion is the following criterion which gives some connections between UU, UJ and UQ semipotent rings, respectively.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
R
be a semipotent ring. The following are equivalent:

(1) The ring
R/J(R)
is a UQ ring.

(2) The ring
R/J(R)
is a Boolean ring.

(3) The ring
R
is a UJ ring.

(4) The ring
R/J(R)
is a UU ring.

(5) The ring
R
is a UQ ring such that
QN(R)=J(R)
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). Since
R
is a semipotent ring, we have
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
, which is also a semipotent ring. We will prove that
\overline{R}
is a reduced ring.

Assume
x^{2}=0
but
0\neq x\in\overline{R}
. Then, by
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
, there exists
e\in\overline{R}
such that
e\overline{R}e\cong M_{2}(S)
, where
S
is a nonzero ring. However, since
\overline{R}
is a UQ ring (by assumption), by Lemma
2.7
,
e\overline{R}e
is a UQ ring. But on the other hand, by Lemma
3.6
,
M_{2}(S)
is not a UQ ring, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\overline{R}
is a reduced ring.

Now, assume there exists
x\in\overline{R}
such that
x-x^{2}\neq 0
in
\overline{R}
. Since
\overline{R}
is a semipotent ring, there exists
e=e^{2}\in\overline{R}
such that
e\in(x-x^{2})\overline{R}
. So,
e=(x-x^{2})y
for some
y\in\overline{R}
. Since
e
is central (indeed, as
[er(1-e)]^{2}=0=[(1-e)re]^{2}
, so we have
er(1-e)=0=(1-e)re
), we can write
e=ex\cdot e(1-x)\cdot ey
, so that both
ex,e(1-x)\in U(e\overline{R}e)
. But, by Lemma
2.7
, we know that
e\overline{R}e
is a UQ ring. However,
ex+e(1-x)=e
, which contradicts Lemma
3.9
. Therefore,
\overline{R}
is a Boolean ring.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3). Let us assume
u\in U(R)
. Then,
\bar{u}\in U(\overline{R})
. Since
\overline{R}
is a Boolean ring, we have
\bar{u}=\bar{1}
, which implies
u-1\in J(R)
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(4). Let us assume
\bar{u}\in U(\overline{R})
. Then,
u\in U(R)
. Since
R
is a UJ ring, we have
u=1+j
, where
j\in J(R)
. Therefore,
\bar{u}=\bar{1}+\bar{0}
.

(4)
\Rightarrow
(1). It is apparent, because as already noted above
N(R)\subseteq QN(R)
.
∎

Surprisingly, we arrive at the following consequence.

Corollary 3.14
.

A (von Neumann) regular ring

is UQ if, and only if,
R
is UJ if, and only if,
R
is UU if, and only if,

is Boolean.

Our further assertions of this aspect are the following.

Proposition 3.15
.

For any UQ ring
R
, the following statements are true:

(1) An element

in R is clean if, and only if, it is quasi nil-clean.

(2) An element
a
in R is strongly clean if, and only if, it is strongly quasi nil-clean.

Proof.

We will only prove part (1), as the other case is similar.

Let us assume that
a\in R
is clean, and

is a clean decomposition. Since

is a UQ ring, we have
u=1+q

Conversely, if
a\in R
is
q

is a

-nil-clean decomposition, we have

∎

Incidentally, we receive:

Corollary 3.16
.

Suppose that
R
is an arbitrary ring. Then, the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

is a UQ ring.

(2) Every clean element is a strongly quasi nil-clean.

(3) For every

, there exists

and

such that

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). it follows at once from Proposition

.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1). Given

is strongly clean, by (2) we have

, where

,

, and

. Thus,

(1)
\Rightarrow
(3). It suffices to assume

, which manifestly yields our claim.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1). Assume

. So,

and, therefore,

.

We, thereby, conclude:

Corollary 3.17
.

Each strongly quasi nil-clean ring is UQ.

Proof.

Indeed, the result can be obtained directly from Corollary
3.16
(2).

Lemma 3.18
.

Any strongly quasi nil-clean ring is strongly clean.

Let

A ring

(1)

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
R
be a potent UQ ring, and
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
. Then, we have:

(1) For any
\bar{e}=\bar{e}^{2}\in\overline{R}
and any

\bar{u}_{1}+\bar{u}_{2}\neq\bar{e}

(2) There does not exist
\bar{e}=\bar{e}^{2}\in\overline{R}

\bar{e}\overline{R}\bar{e}\cong M_{2}(S)
for some ring

(1) Given
\bar{e}

\bar{u}_{1}

is UQ by Lemma

(2) In a

Let

(2)

(4)

\Rightarrow

(1)
\Rightarrow
(3). Since
R
is a semipotent ring, we have
\overline{R}=R/J(R)

\overline{R}

4.
Group Rings

We begin here with the following preliminaries.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
R
be a ring,
I
an ideal of
R
such that
I\subseteq J(R)
, and
\overline{R}=R/I
. Then, the following are valid:

(1) For every
\bar{q}\in QN(\overline{R})
, we have
q\in QN(R)
.

(2) For every
\bar{q}\in QN(\overline{R})
and
p\in I
, we have
q+p\in QN(R)
.

Proof.

(1) Let us assume
\bar{q}\in QN(\overline{R})
. If
pq=qp
, then one follows that
\bar{q}\bar{p}=\bar{p}\bar{q}
. Therefore,
\bar{1}-\bar{p}\bar{q}\in U(\overline{R})
implying that
1-pq\in U(R)
. Hence,
q\in QN(R)
.

(2) For each
p\in I
, we can write
q+I=(q+p)+I\in QN(\overline{R})
. Thus, using property (1), we can conclude that
p+q\in QN(R)
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
R
be a ring,
I\subseteq J(R)
, and
\overline{R}=R/I
be a UQ ring. Then,
R
is a UQ ring.

Proof.

For any
u\in U(R)
, we have
\bar{u}\in U(\overline{R})
. Since
\overline{R}
is a UQ ring, we can write
\bar{u}=\bar{1}+\bar{q}
, where
\bar{q}\in QN(\overline{R})
. Now, Lemma
4.1
(1) is applicable to get that
q\in QN(R)
. Consequently,
u-(1+q)\in I
. Thus, there exists
p\in I
such that
u=1+(p+q)
. But, consulting with Lemma
4.1
(2), we deduce
p+q\in QN(R)
. Therefore,
R
is a UQ-ring, as stated.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
R
be a UQ-ring,
a\in U(R)
, and
f_{n}=1+a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{n}
. Then, the following two points occur:

(1) If
n
is even, then
f_{n}\in U(R)
.

(2) If
n
is odd, then
f_{n}\in QN(R)
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose
n
is even. We can rewrite
f_{n}
as

f_{n}=1+(1+a)(a+a^{3}+\cdots+a^{n-1}).

Since
a\in U(R)
, we know
a+1\in QN(R)
, so that
[
8
, Proposition 2.10]
can be applied to infer that

(1+a)(a+a^{3}+\cdots+a^{n-1})\in QN(R).

Hence,
f_{n}\in U(R)
.

(2) Suppose
n
is odd. We can rewrite
f_{n}
as
f_{n}=1+af_{n-1}
. So, the usage of (1) guarantees that
f_{n-1}\in U(R)
, and therefore

f_{n}=1+af_{n-1}\in 1+U(R)=QN(R),

as asked for.
∎

We now have all the ingredients necessary for proving the following major statement.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
G
be a group. If
RG
is a UQ-ring, then
R
is a UQ-ring and
G
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

Since
R
is a good subring of
RG
, it follows directly from Lemma
2.3
that
R
is a UQ-ring.

Let us now assume
g\in G
. Then,
R\left\langle g\right\rangle
is also a good subring of
RG
. Thus,
R\left\langle g\right\rangle
is a UQ-ring. If, however, we assume in a way of contradiction that
\left\langle g\right\rangle
is an infinite cyclic group, then the application of Lemma
4.3
(1) insures that
1+g+g^{2}\in U(RG)
. Hence, there exist integers, say
a\leq b
, and
c_{i}\in R
, with the property

c_{a}\neq 0\neq c_{b}
such that

1=\sum_{i=a}^{b}c_{i}g^{i}(1+g+g^{2})=c_{a}g^{a}+\sum_{a+1}^{b+1}d_{i}g^{i}+c_{b}g^{b+2}

for suitable
d_{i}\in R
,

. It is not too hard to verify that this gives the expected contradiction.

So, if there exists an element of
G
such that its order is divisible by an odd prime, say

, then there will exist an element
g
of
G
with
{\rm order}(g)=p
. But since
\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}g^{i}\in U(RG)
in view of Lemma

(1), and obviously
(1-g)\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}g^{i}=0
, we detect that

, i.e.,

, again a contradiction, as needed.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

be a locally finite

-group and let

\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\overline{R}
equals to

. Then, employing Lemma
3.10
,
\bar{2}\in N(\overline{R})
. Therefore, with
[
7
, Proposition 16]
in hand, we derive
\Delta(\overline{R}G)\subseteq J(\overline{R}G)
.

On the other hand, with
[
6
, Lemma 4]
in mind, we deduce
J(R)G\subseteq J(RG)
, so one checks that

J(\overline{R}G)=J(R/J(R)G)\cong J(RG/J(R)G)=J(RG)/J(R)G.

However, for each
f=\sum a_{g}(1-g)\in\Delta(RG)
, it is readily seen that

\sum\bar{a_{g}}(1-g)\in\Delta(\overline{R}G)\subseteq J(RG)/J(R)G.

Hence, there exists
j\in J(RG)
such that
f-j\in J(R)G\subseteq J(RG)
, and because
J(RG)
is an ideal, we freely may conclude that
f
belongs to
J(RG)
, completing the arguments after all.
∎

We are now in a position to prove validity of the following sufficient condition.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
R
is a UQ ring and
G
is a locally finite
2
-group, then
RG
is a UQ ring.

Proof.

With Lemma
4.5
into account, we have
\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
and, on the other side, we have
RG/\Delta(RG)\cong R
. Therefore,
RG/\Delta(RG)
is a UQ ring. Consequently, Lemma
4.2
forces that the ring
RG
is a UQ ring.
∎

It has been proven in
[
18
]
that if
R
is a UJ ring and
G
a locally finite
2
-group, then
RG
is a UJ ring. We now attempting to reprove the same result approaching a different method which is definitely more concise and transparent.

Theorem 4.7
.

If
R
is a UJ ring and
G
is a locally finite
2
-group, then
RG
is a UJ ring.

Proof.

Suppose
R
is a UJ ring and
G
is a locally finite
2
-group. Now, Lemma
3.10
allows us to have
2\in J(R)
. Thus, assuming
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
, it must be that
\bar{2}\in N(\overline{R})
. Therefore, in virtue of
[
7
, Proposition 16]
,
\Delta(\overline{R}G)
is a nil-ideal guaranteeing the inclusion
\Delta(\overline{R}G)\subseteq J(\overline{R}G)
. But since
\overline{R}G/\Delta(\overline{R}G)\cong\overline{R}
, the utilization of
[
17
, Proposition 1.3(5)]
enables us that
\overline{R}G
is a UJ ring.

Furthermore, because of the isomorphism
\overline{R}G\cong RG/J(R)G
, and with the aid of
[
6
, Lemma 4]
, we obtain the inclusion
J(R)G\subseteq J(RG)
. Hence, thanks to Proposition
[
17
, Proposition 1.3(5)]
,
RG
is a UJ ring, as pursued.
∎

5.
Some Specific Classes of UQ Rings

In this section, we first introduce the rings
A_{n,m}(R)
,
B_{n,m}(R)
, and
C_{n}(R)
for each
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and using these rings, we present certain new classes of UQ rings.

In fact, letting
R
be an arbitrary ring, we define the aforementioned rings as follows:

\displaystyle A_{n,m}(R)=R[x,y|x^{n}=xy=y^{m}=0],

\displaystyle B_{n,m}(R)=R\left\langle x,y|x^{n}=xy=y^{m}=0\right\rangle,

\displaystyle C_{n}(R)=R\langle x,y|x^{2}=\underbrace{xyxyx...}_{\text{$n-1$ words}}=y^{2}=0\rangle.

In another vein, Wang introduced in
[
25
]
the following matrix ring
S_{n,m}(R)
. Supposing
R
is a ring, the matrix ring
S_{n,m}(R)
can be represented as

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n-1}&c_{1n}&\cdots&c_{1n+m-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&a&b_{1}&c_{n-1,n}&\cdots&c_{n-1,n+m-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&a&d_{1}&\cdots&d_{m-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&a&d_{1}\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0&a\end{pmatrix}\in T_{n+m-1}(R):a,b_{i},d_{j},c_{i,j}\in R\right\}

Also, let

T_{n,m}(R)=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}\begin{matrix}a&b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{n-1}\\
0&a&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n-2}\\
0&0&a&\cdots&b_{n-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a\end{matrix}&\mbox{\Large 0}\\
\hline\cr\mbox{\Large 0}&\begin{matrix}a&c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{m-1}\\
0&a&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{m-2}\\
0&0&a&\cdots&c_{m-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a\end{matrix}\end{array}\right)\in T_{n+m}(R):a,b_{i},c_{j}\in R\right\}

and

U_{n}(R)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}&b_{4}&\cdots&b_{n-1}\\
0&a&c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&\cdots&c_{n-2}\\
0&0&a&b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{n-3}\\
0&0&0&a&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-4}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&\cdots&a\end{pmatrix}\in T_{n}(R):a,b_{i},c_{j}\in R\right\}

We are now ready to demonstrate in the following assertion the relationships between these rings. Specifically, we are able to establish the following technical claim.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
R
be a ring and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the next three isomorphisms of rings are fulfilled:

(1)
A_{n,m}(R)\cong T_{n,m}(R)
.

(2)
B_{n,m}(R)\cong S_{n,m}(R)
.

(3)
C_{n}(R)\cong U_{n}(R)
.

Proof.

(1) We write

f=a+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i}x^{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}c_{j}y^{j}\in A_{n,m}(R)

and define
\varphi:A_{n,m}(R)\to T_{n,m}(R)
as

\varphi(f)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}\begin{matrix}a&b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{n-1}\\
0&a&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n-2}\\
0&0&a&\cdots&b_{n-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a\end{matrix}&\mbox{\Large 0}\\
\hline\cr\mbox{\Large 0}&\begin{matrix}a&c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{m-1}\\
0&a&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{m-2}\\
0&0&a&\cdots&c_{m-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a\end{matrix}\end{array}\right).

It plainly can be shown via some routine technical tricks that
\varphi
is a ring isomorphism, which fact we leave for a direct inspection by the interested reader.

(2) We write
f\in B_{n,m}(R)
such that

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=a_{00}y^{0}x^{0}+a_{01}y^{0}x^{1}+\cdots+a_{0,n-1}y^{0}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle+a_{10}y^{1}x^{0}+a_{11}y^{1}x^{1}+\cdots+a_{1,n-1}y^{1}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots\qquad\qquad\vdots\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle+a_{m-1,0}y^{m-1}x^{0}+a_{m-1,1}y^{m-1}x^{1}+\cdots+a_{m-1,n-1}y^{m-1}x^{n-1}

and define
\psi:B_{n,m}(R)\to S_{n,m}(R)
as

\psi(f)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{00}&a_{10}&\cdots&a_{m-1,0}&a_{m-1,1}&\cdots&a_{m-1,n-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&a_{00}&a_{10}&a_{11}&\cdots&a_{1,n-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&a_{00}&a_{01}&\cdots&a_{0,n-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&a_{00}&a_{0,1}\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0&a_{00}\end{pmatrix}.

It routinely follows that the so defined map is a ring isomorphism, which verification we omit to be done by the interested reader.

(3) We write the coefficients as follows

f=\sum_{0\leq i_{j}\leq 1\atop 1\leq j\leq n-1}d_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{n-1})}\underbrace{y^{i_{1}}x^{i_{2}}y^{i_{3}}x^{i_{4}}...}_{\text{$n-1$ words}}\in C_{n}(R)

and define
\phi:C_{n}(R)\to S_{n,m}(R)
as

\phi(f)=\begin{pmatrix}d_{(0,0,0,\dots,0)}&d_{(1,0,0,\dots,0)}&d_{(1,1,0,\dots,0)}&d_{(1,1,1,\dots,0)}&\cdots&d_{(1,1,1,\dots,1)}\\
0&d_{(0,0,0,\dots,0)}&d_{(0,1,0,\dots,0)}&d_{(0,1,1,\dots,0)}&\cdots&d_{(0,1,1,\dots,1)}\\
0&0&d_{(0,0,0,\dots,0)}&d_{(1,0,0,\dots,0)}&\cdots&d_{(1,\dots,1,0,0)}\\
0&0&0&d_{(0,0,0,\dots,0)}&\cdots&d_{(0,1,\dots,1,0,0)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&d_{(0,0,0,\dots,0)}\end{pmatrix}.

A simple game with matrices is only needed in order to check that the so defined map is an isomorphism of rings, which technical manipulation we leave to the interested reader.
∎

Example 5.2
.

Let
