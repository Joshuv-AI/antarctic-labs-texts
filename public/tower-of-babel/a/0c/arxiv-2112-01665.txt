On the stability of isothermal shocks in black hole accretion disks
Eric W. Hester, Geoffrey M. Vasil, Martin Wechselberger

Abstract
Most black holes possess accretion disks. Models of such disks inform observations and constrain the properties of the black holes and their surrounding medium. Here, we study isothermal shocks in a thin black hole accretion flow. Modelling infinitesimal molecular viscosity allows the use of multiple-scales matched asymptotic methods. We thus derive the first explicit calculations of isothermal shock stability. We find that the inner shock is always unstable, and the outer shock is always stable. The growth/decay rates of perturbations depend only on an effective potential and the incoming–outgoing flow difference at the shock location. We give a prescription of accretion regimes in terms of angular momentum and black hole radius. Accounting for angular momentum dissipation implies unstable outer shocks in much of parameter space, even for realistic viscous Reynolds numbers of the order ≈ 10 20 \approx 10^{20} .

Theory

2.1
Equations of motion

We begin with conservation of mass and momentum

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho u)=0,

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\cdot((p-\mu_{1}\nabla\cdot u)I-\mu_{2}(\nabla u+\nabla u^{\top}))+\nabla\phi=0.

(2)

The viscous thin-disk equations are derived using the following assumptions

1.

Cylindrical coordinates
(r,\theta,z)
.

2.

Axisymmetric flow
\partial_{\theta}\to 0
.

3.

Isothermal equation of state
p=K\rho
.

4.

Constant kinematic diffusivities
\nu_{1}=\mu_{1}/\rho,\nu_{2}=\mu_{2}/\rho
.

5.

Monatomic gas, with bulk viscosity
\nu_{1}=0
.

6.

No self-gravity.

7.

Pseudo-Newtonian black hole potential
\phi=\frac{GM}{r-r_{Sc}}
.

8.

Thin disk
z/r\ll 1
.

9.

Hydrostatic balance
\partial_{z}u=0
.

The horizon radius in the Pseudo-Newtonian potential
[
41
]
is given by the Schwarzschild radius
r_{Sc}=\frac{2GM}{c^{2}}
, where
G
is the gravitational constant,
M
is the black hole mass, and
c
is the speed of light.

2.1.1
Non-dimensional equations

We nondimensionalise according to length and time scales given by the sound speed
\sqrt{K}
, defining the dimensional velocity scales
U_{r},U_{\theta}
, length scale
R
, and time scale
T

\displaystyle U_{r}

\displaystyle=U_{\theta}=\sqrt{K},

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\frac{GM}{2K},

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{U_{r}},

(3)

which reduces the equations to

\partial_{t}\delta+u\partial_{r}\delta+\partial_{r}u+\frac{u}{r}=0,

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{r}u+\partial_{r}\delta+\left(\frac{2}{(r-r_{h})^{2}}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\left(\partial_{r}^{2}u+\frac{\partial_{r}u}{r}\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{u}{r^{2}}+\partial_{r}\delta\partial_{r}u\right),

(5)

\partial_{t}\ell+u\partial_{r}\ell=\varepsilon\left(\partial_{r}^{2}\ell-\frac{\partial_{r}\ell}{r}+\left(\partial_{r}\ell-\frac{2\ell}{r}\right)\partial_{r}\delta\right).

(6)

where we have defined the dimensionless horizon scale
r_{h}
and inverse Reynolds number
\varepsilon

\displaystyle r_{h}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{4K}{c^{2}},

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{2\nu_{2}\sqrt{K}}{GM},

(7)

and rewritten in terms of the logarithmic density
\delta
and specific angular momentum
\ell

\displaystyle\delta(t,r)

\displaystyle=\log\rho(t,r),

\displaystyle\ell(t,r)

\displaystyle=ru_{\theta}(t,r).

(8)

2.2
Asymptotic analysis

Equations

4
,
2.1.1
and
6
are singularly perturbed in
\varepsilon
.
Neglecting viscous terms fails within narrow shock boundary layers of thickness
O(\varepsilon)
.
In these regions higher-order derivatives must be included.

2.2.1
Multiple-scales matched asymptotic expansions

We analyse these shocks using multiple scales matched asymptotic expansions in
\varepsilon
.
We divide the analysis into two subproblems.
The
reduced/outer problem
determines the ‘outer’ fluid variables
f^{-}(r)
and
f^{+}(r)
for radii less than (
r<r_{s}
) and greater than (
r>r_{s}
) the shock radius
r_{s}
respectively.
(Here
f
represents an arbitrary fluid variable, encompassing the choices
\delta,u,\ell
.)
The
layer/inner
problem solves the ‘inner’ fluid variables
f(x)
within the shock itself by defining rescaled inner coordinates
x=\frac{r-r_{s}}{\varepsilon}
.
Applying this coordinate transformation to
eqs.

4
,
2.1.1
and
6
rescales the radial derivatives

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r_{s}+\varepsilon x,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}.

(9)

For each problem, we then expand the fluid variables
f
in a formal asymptotic series in
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle f^{\pm}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}\varepsilon^{k}f^{\pm}_{k}(r),

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}\varepsilon^{k}f_{k}(x),

(10)

and solve the problems order-by-order in
\varepsilon
.

2.2.2
Asymptotic matching

The final stage connects inner and outer subproblems through boundary conditions.
We specify asymptotic agreement in the region
x\sim\varepsilon^{-1/2}
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f(\pm\varepsilon^{-1/2}x)

\displaystyle\sim\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f^{\pm}(r_{s}\pm\varepsilon^{+1/2}\,x).

(11)

This limit simultaneously allows the layer coordinate
x
to approach infinity and the reduced coordinate
r
to approach
r_{s}
.
We derive order-by-order boundary conditions by expanding each variable in an asymptotic series in
\varepsilon
,
expanding each term of the outer variable
f^{\pm}(r)
as a Taylor series around
r_{s}
,
and equating each order in
\varepsilon

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\sum_{k=0}\varepsilon^{k}f_{k}(x)\sim\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\sum_{k=0}\varepsilon^{k}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\partial_{r}^{\ell}f^{\pm}_{k-\ell}(r_{s})\right).

(12)

The first two conditions simplify to

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f_{0}(x)\sim f^{\pm}_{0}(r_{s}),\quad\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f_{1}(x)\sim f^{\pm}_{1}(r_{s})+\partial_{r}f^{\pm}_{0}(r_{s})\,x.

(13)

This procedure allows us to determine analytic formulae for the asymptotic behaviour of the steady problem and growth/decay rates of the shock stability problem.

3.1
Steady transonic solutions

We find stationary solutions by neglecting time derivatives in

4

The steady mass conservation law

We then solve the steady momentum equations order-by-order in

3.1.1
Conserved quantities for inviscid flow

Substituting the steady log-density (

section

\displaystyle\partial_{r}e

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\ell_{0}^{\pm}

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\equiv{\frac{1}{2}\left({u^{\pm}_{r,0}}^{2}+\frac{{\ell_{0}^{\pm}}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)-\frac{2}{r-r_{h}}-\log(r|u^{\pm}_{r,0}|)}.

(16)

3.1.2
Shock boundary conditions

The inviscid solutions (
eq.

15
) are not valid across a shock.
To derive the shock jump conditions we rescale to the shock length scale, and solve the leading order asymptotic problem in
u_{0}(x),\ell_{0}(x)
, detailed in
appendix

A
.
The solutions are given by

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\cosh a-\sinh a\tanh\left(\frac{\sinh(a)}{2}\,(x-x_{0})\right),

(17)

\displaystyle\ell_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\ell^{\pm}_{0}(r_{s})\equiv\ell.

(18)

where we have defined
a\equiv\log|u^{+}_{0}(r_{s})|>0
.
The leading order specific angular momentum is therefore conserved across a shock, and so is constant throughout the domain.
We define this constant as
\ell\equiv\ell_{0}(x)=\ell_{0}^{\pm}(r)
.

Sonic points
—
The outer radial velocity
u^{\pm}_{0}
is given by level sets of the specific energy
e
.
The global behaviour of
u^{\pm}_{0}
therefore depends on the critical points of the specific energy
e
.
Partial derivatives of
e
with respect to the velocity
u^{\pm}_{0}
and radius
r
give the critical point conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{u^{\pm}_{0}}e

\displaystyle=u^{\pm}_{0}-\frac{1}{u^{\pm}_{0}}=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}e

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}-\frac{2}{(r-r_{h})^{2}}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{3}}=0.

(19)

The critical points
(r_{*},u^{\pm}_{0,*})
are therefore given by
u^{\pm}_{0,*}=-1
, and

\displaystyle r_{*}^{4}-2(1+r_{h})r_{*}^{3}+(\ell^{2}+r_{h}^{2})r_{*}^{2}-2\ell^{2}r_{h}r_{*}+\ell^{2}r_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

(20)

There are at most four critical points of

Proceeding inwards, the first three are a saddle type critical point
r_{*,2}
, a centre type critical point
r_{*,0}
, and another saddle type critical point
r_{*,1}
.
The final unphysical critical point occurs within the black hole for
r<r_{h}
.
The only locations where the flow can become transonic are the inner
r_{*,1}
and outer
r_{*,2}
sonic points. Geometric Singular Perturbation theory proves that the transonic flow through the sonic points (canard points in the GSPT literature) persists even with added viscosity
[

,
10

32
]
.

and outer

We thus determine the shock location by finding intersections of the projection of the outer transonic velocity

This completely describes the structure of the solution as a function of

We simplify by using
{}{\delta}_{0}^{+}=-\log(-r{}{u}^{+}_{0})
, and
{}{\ell}_{0}^{+}=\ell
,
and abbreviate by dropping
\pm
superscripts and
0
subscripts,

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+{}u\partial_{r})\tilde{\delta}+\left(\partial_{r}-\frac{{}u^{\prime}}{{}u}\right)\tilde{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(29)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\tilde{\delta}+\left(\partial_{t}+{}u^{\prime}+{}u\partial_{r}\right)\tilde{u}-\frac{2\ell}{r^{3}}\tilde{\ell}

\displaystyle=0,

(30)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+{}u\partial_{r})\tilde{\ell}

\displaystyle=0.

(31)

The specific angular momentum
\tilde{\ell}
is advected by the background flow.
If initially
\tilde{\ell}(0,r)\to 0
as
r\to\infty
, then all
\tilde{\ell}
will eventually be advected through the sonic point.
After this point, disturbances evolve without any perturbation in specific angular momentum.
For this reason, we can consider stability in the absence of angular momentum perturbation
\tilde{\ell}
for long times.
Hence, we deal with sound waves alone.
Rearranging, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\tilde{u}+\partial_{r}\left(2{}u\partial_{t}\tilde{u}+{}u\partial_{r}\left(\left({}u-\frac{1}{{}u}\right)\tilde{u}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(32)

To prove stability, we derive a decreasing energy functional for the system.
We define

\displaystyle E=\int_{r_{*}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left(h(r)(\partial_{t}\tilde{u})^{2}-{}u\partial_{r}\left(h(r)\tilde{u}\right)^{2}\right)dr,

(33)

\displaystyle\text{ where }h(r)={}u(r)-\frac{1}{{}u(r)}>0,\quad-1<{}u<0,

and observe that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E

\displaystyle=\left[-{}uh\left(\partial_{r}(h\tilde{u})+\partial_{t}\tilde{u}\right)\partial_{t}\tilde{u}\right]_{r_{*}}^{\infty}-\int_{r_{*}}^{\infty}\frac{{}u^{\prime}}{|{}u|}(\partial_{t}\tilde{u})^{2}dr.

(34)

The boundary terms cancel, because
\tilde{u}
tends to zero as
r\to\infty
, and
h(r_{*})=-1-1/(-1)=0
at the sonic point
r_{*}
.
The integrand on the right hand side is manifestly positive.
Thus, total energy of linear perturbations can only decay over time beyond the outermost sonic point (though not necessarily to zero).

Supersonic regime
—
Between the centre type critical point and the inner sonic point the background flow slows down, and the system energy increases (simply apply
eq.

34
over
[r_{h},r_{*}]
).
We cannot rely on an energy argument for stability.
Instead, we examine the normal modes
\tilde{u}(t,x)=e^{\lambda t}\tilde{u}(x)

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\tilde{u}+\partial_{r}\left(2{}u\lambda\tilde{u}+{}u\partial_{r}\left(\left({}u-\frac{1}{{}u}\right)\tilde{u}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(35)

We transform this into a standard Sturm-Liouville form, following similar procedure to
[
37
]
.
We first examine the integral
\psi\equiv\int\tilde{u}\,dr
, to solve the simpler equation

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\left(\lambda^{2}\psi+{}{u}\partial_{r}\left(2\lambda\psi+h(r)\partial_{r}\psi\right)\right)=0.

(36)

Then, using a Liouville transformation
\psi(r(x))=w(x)e^{\lambda x}
, we recover the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}w

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{{}{u}(r(x))^{2}}w,\quad\text{where}\quad x(r)=\int_{r_{h}}^{r}\frac{-{}{u}}{{}{u}^{2}-1}dr^{\prime}.

(37)

The coordinates transform
r\in[r_{h},r_{*}]
to
x\in[0,\infty)
.

To prove stability, we show that normal modes with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions must have negative growth rates.
While the outer subsonic flow can temporarily perturb the supersonic region, eventually the flow decays to zero at the sonic point.
After this time, the supersonic flow must satisfy zero Dirichlet boundary conditions at
r_{*}
.
We show all such solutions decay over time using WKB asymptotic analysis near the sonic point (see
appendix

B
).
In

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{0}(t,x)

4
Discussion

The Rankine-Hugoniot shock conditions neglect microscopic dissipation.
This omission has impeded previous linear stability analyses.
Earlier works determined bounds on growth/decay rates of shock instabilites
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
,
corresponding to the range

\displaystyle\frac{1}{u^{-}_{0}(r_{s})+u^{+}_{0}(r_{s})}\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{2}{(r-r_{h})^{2}}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\leq\lambda_{0}\leq\frac{1}{u^{-}_{0}(r_{s})-1}\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{2}{(r-r_{h})^{2}}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{3}}\right).

(42)

Specific growth rates
\lambda_{0}
can only be resolved by accounting for microscopic dissipation.

The drawback of dissipation is that it increases the mathematical order of the problem.
But multiple scales matched asymptotics provide a straightforward procedure to solve singular perturbation problems,
giving an explicit formula for the growth/decay rates,

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=\frac{1}{u^{+}_{0}(r_{s})+u^{-}_{0}(r_{s})}\left(\frac{1}{r_{s}}-\frac{2}{\left(r_{s}-r_{h}\right){}^{2}}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r_{s}^{3}}\right).

(43)

This allows a description of shock stability throughout parameter space (
figs.

1
and
6
).

Isothermal shock regimes are narrow
—
Realistic isothermal black hole shocks are restricted to a narrow sliver of parameter space (
figs.

1
and
8
).
Consider Cygnus X-1.
Assuming an ideal monatomic gas with sound speed and viscosity proportional to
T^{1/2}

$100\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$\approx$1\text{\times}{10}^{9}\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$

The range shrinks to

5
Conclusions

Previous models of isothermal shocks in black hole accretion disks predicted two possible steady shocks
[
12
]
, and found bounds on their stability
[
36
]
.
We provide precise values for shock wave stability properties by considering microscopic dissipation.
Using multiple scales matched asymptotic expansions, we calculate the first explicit formula for growth/decay rates of isothermal shocks (
eq.

43
).
We support our asymptotic calculations using Mathematica, and provide numerical validation of these properties using Dedalus
[
8
]
.
We show that the inner shock is unstable, the outer shock is stable, and that growth/decay rates tend to zero as shocks approach the sonic points.
Using a simple energy argument, we find that the smooth transonic flow is also stable, suggesting two possible physical solutions.
However, our asymptotic analysis suggests that realistic black holes only support steady shock waves in extremely narrow parameter regimes, arguing against the existence of steady isothermal shocks for most black holes.
Better models of shocks in accretion disks require additional physics, such as non-isothermal gases, radiation, and magnetohydrodynamic dissipation.
Fortunately, the techniques developed within this paper are general, and can in future help understand shocks in more complex astrophysical models.

Appendix A
Steady viscous shock waves

A.1
Zeroth-order inner problem

The leading order reduced problem cannot predict shocks.
To understand them we must rescale our problem around these shocks.
The leading order behaviour for the layer problem becomes

\displaystyle\left(u_{0}-\frac{1}{u_{0}}\right)\partial_{x}u_{0}-2\left(\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}-\frac{\partial_{x}u_{0}^{2}}{u_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=u_{0}\partial_{x}\left(u_{0}+\frac{1}{u_{0}}-2\frac{\partial_{x}u_{0}}{u_{0}}\right)=0,

(44)

which can be integrated once to find

\displaystyle u_{0}^{2}-cu_{0}+1-2\partial_{x}u_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(45)

where
c=u_{0}(\infty)+1/u_{0}(\infty)=u^{+}_{0}(r_{s})+u^{-}_{0}(r_{s})
by the asymptotic matching conditions, giving

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{0}^{+}+u_{0}^{-}+(u_{0}^{+}-u_{0}^{-})\tanh\left(-\frac{1}{4}(u_{0}^{+}-u_{0}^{-})(x-x_{0})\right)\right).

(46)

The angular momentum problem becomes

\displaystyle\left(u_{0}+\frac{\partial_{x}u_{0}}{u_{0}}\right)\partial_{x}\ell_{0}-\partial_{x}^{2}\ell_{0}=0,

(47)

with the general solution

\displaystyle\ell_{0}(x)=c_{1}+c_{2}\exp\left(\int^{x}u_{0}\,dy\right).

(48)

The asymptotic matching conditions for
\ell_{0}
require constant limiting behaviour as
x\to\pm\infty
, but the function behaves like
\exp({|u_{0}^{+}||x|})
as
x\to-\infty
.
The only way to satisfy the asymptotic matching boundary conditions is for
c_{2}=0
and
c_{1}=\ell^{+}_{0}(r_{s})=\ell
.
The specific angular momentum is therefore conserved across a shock.
We abbreviate the solutions as

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\cosh a-\sinh a\tanh\left(\frac{\sinh(a)}{2}\,x\right),

\displaystyle\ell_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\ell,

(49)

where we have defined the constant
a\equiv\log(-u^{+}_{0}(r_{s}))>0
.

A.2
First-order outer problem

The first order reduced problem is linear, and simplifies upon substitution of
\ell_{0}=\ell

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\left(\left(u_{0}^{\pm}-\frac{1}{u_{0}^{\pm}}\right)u_{r,1}^{\pm}\right)-\frac{2\ell}{r^{3}}\ell_{1}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=2{u_{0}^{\pm}}^{\prime\prime}-\frac{2\left({u_{0}^{\pm}}^{\prime}\right){}^{2}}{u_{0}^{\pm}}-\frac{2u_{0}^{\pm}}{r^{2}},

(50)

\displaystyle{\ell_{1}^{\pm}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\ell}{ru_{0}^{\pm}}\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{u_{0}^{\pm^{\prime}}}{u_{0}^{\pm}}\right)=2\ell{\rho_{0}^{\pm}}^{\prime}.

(51)

The angular momentum equation can be integrated to find

\displaystyle\ell_{1}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=2\ell\rho_{0}^{\pm}+c_{1}^{\pm}.

We then apply boundary conditions to complete the system.
The limiting boundary condition
\lim_{r\to\infty}\ell_{1}^{+}\to 0
implies
c_{1}^{+}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\rho_{0}^{+}=\rho_{\infty}=\exp(e^{*})
, the exponential of the specific energy at the outer sonic point.
At a sonic point
r_{*}
the problem simplifies, and we can substitute the values of
u_{0}(r_{*})=-1
and its derivatives to determine a value for
u_{1}(r_{*})

\displaystyle u_{1}(r_{*})

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{1}{\partial_{r}u_{0}}\left(\partial_{r}^{2}u_{0}+\partial_{r}u_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}+\frac{\ell}{r^{3}}\ell_{1}(r)\right)\right|_{r=r_{*}}.

(52)

A.3
First-order inner problem

The inner system at first order can be written in terms of
u_{1}
alone

\displaystyle L[u_{1}]\equiv u_{1}^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{0}-\frac{1}{u_{0}}+\frac{4u_{0}^{\prime}}{u_{0}}\right)u_{1}^{\prime}+\cosh a\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}}{u_{0}}u_{1}=g(r_{s}),

(53)

where

\displaystyle g(r_{s})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r_{s}}-\frac{2}{\left(r_{s}-r_{h}\right){}^{2}}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r_{s}^{3}}\right).

(54)

To proceed we analyse the linear operator
L
.

First kernel component

u_{1,1}
—
The zeroth order layer problem was translation symmetric.
It follows that the derivative of the zeroth order solution is in the kernel of this operator,

\displaystyle L[u_{1,1}]

\displaystyle=0,\qquad u_{1,1}=u_{0}^{\prime}=-\frac{\sinh^{2}a}{2}\operatorname{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{\sinh a}{2}x\right).

(55)

Wronskian

W
—
To find the second kernel component
u_{1,2}
we calculate the Wronskian
{W=u_{1,1}u_{1,2}^{\prime}-u_{1,1}^{\prime}u_{1,2}}
.
If
L[y]=y^{\prime\prime}-p(x)y^{\prime}-q(x)y
, then
W^{\prime}=p(x)W
.
Hence

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\propto\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{\sinh ax}{2}\right)\left(-2\cosh a-2\sinh a\tanh\left(\frac{\sinh a\,x}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\propto 2\operatorname{csch}^{2}a\,u_{0}^{\prime}u_{0}.

(56)

Second kernel component

u_{1,2}
—
It is then straightforward to solve the Wronskian for
u_{1,2}

\displaystyle u_{1,2}

\displaystyle\propto u_{0}+xu_{0}^{\prime}+\operatorname{sech}(a)

\displaystyle\propto-\sinh a\tanh\left(\frac{1}{2}x\sinh a\right)-\frac{1}{2}x\sinh^{2}a\operatorname{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}x\sinh a\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\cosh a+\operatorname{sech}a.

(57)

If we pick the preferred elements

\displaystyle u_{1,1}

\displaystyle=u_{0}^{\prime},

\displaystyle u_{1,2}

\displaystyle=u_{0}+xu_{0}^{\prime}+\operatorname{sech}a,

(58)

then the Wronskian becomes

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\sinh a\tanh a\,u_{0}^{\prime}\,u_{0}.

(59)

Inhomogeneous solution

u_{1,i}
—
We then solve the particular solution
L[u_{1,i}]=1
using variation of parameters,

\displaystyle u_{1,i}

\displaystyle=c_{1}(x)u_{1,1}+c_{2}(x)u_{1,2},

(60)

where

\displaystyle L[c_{1}u_{1,1}+c_{2}u_{1,2}]

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle c_{1}^{\prime}u_{1,1}+c_{2}^{\prime}u_{1,2}

\displaystyle=0.

(61)

Simplifying the constraints shows

\displaystyle c_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{u_{1,2}}{W},

\displaystyle c_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{1,1}}{W},

(62)

which can be integrated to find

\displaystyle c_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sinh a\tanh a}\left(2x+\frac{1}{2}e^{-a}x^{2}\right.-2x\log\left(e^{-x\sinh a-2a}+1\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 65.44142pt-4\operatorname{sech}a\log\left(\cosh\left(\frac{1}{2}x\sinh a+a\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 65.44142pt+\operatorname{csch}^{2}a\operatorname{sech}a\cosh(x\sinh a)

\displaystyle\hskip 65.44142pt+2\operatorname{csch}a\operatorname{Li}_{2}\left(-e^{-2a-x\sinh a}\right)\bigg).

\displaystyle c_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sinh a\tanh a}\left(2\log\left(\cosh\left(\frac{1}{2}x\sinh a+a\right)\right)-x\cosh a\right).

where
\operatorname{Li}_{2}
is the polylogarithm of order 2.
The full solution
u_{1}
combines the inhomogeneous solution
u_{1,i}
and the two kernel components

\displaystyle u_{1}=g(r_{s})u_{1,i}+b_{1}u_{1,1}+b_{2}u_{1,2}.

(63)

To determine the coefficients

of each kernel component requires further work.
The coefficient of

is constrained by the limiting boundary conditions of the first order problem, which requires knowledge of both

and

As

decays exponentially fast toward the boundaries, it cannot be determined by boundary conditions at any order.
Instead it is determined through a solvability condition at subsequent order.
Such solvability conditions are most easily determined for self-adjoint operators.

Self-adjoint

L^{\dagger}
—
We recast in self-adjoint form by reweighting

\displaystyle L^{\dagger}[v]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{v^{\prime}}{u_{0}^{\prime}u_{0}}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{\cosh a}{u_{0}^{2}}v=\frac{1}{u_{0}^{\prime}u_{0}}L[v],

(64)

where

is a Sturm-Liouville operator.
Hence the kernel and cokernel of

are the same, simplifying solvability conditions.

Zeroth order shift
x_{0}
—
Given the limiting behaviour of the solution we write

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=g(r_{s})u_{1,i}(x_{0})+b_{1}u_{1,2}(x_{0}),

\displaystyle u_{1,i}

\displaystyle=c_{1}u_{1,1}+c_{2}u_{1,2},

\displaystyle u_{1,2}

\displaystyle=u_{1,2}=u_{0}+xu_{0}^{\prime}+\operatorname{sech}a.

The boundary conditions require

\displaystyle u_{1}(-X,x_{0})

\displaystyle\sim u_{1}^{-}(r_{s})-X\partial_{r}u_{0}^{-}(r_{s}),

\displaystyle u_{1}(X,x_{0})

\displaystyle\sim u_{1}^{+}(r_{s})+X\partial_{r}u_{0}^{+}(r_{s}).

The limiting linear behaviour of
u_{1,i}
, and the limiting constant behaviour of
u_{1,2}
imply

\displaystyle u_{1}(-X,x_{0})

\displaystyle=g(r_{s})u_{1,i}(-X,x_{0})+b_{2}u_{1,2}(-X,x_{0}),

\displaystyle=g(r_{s})(u_{1,i}(-X,0)-x_{0}u_{1,i}^{\prime}(-X))+b_{2}u_{1,2}(-X,0),

\displaystyle u_{1}(X,x_{0})

\displaystyle=g(r_{s})u_{1,i}(X,x_{0})+b_{2}u_{1,2}(X,x_{0}),

\displaystyle=g(r_{s})(u_{1,i}(X,0)-x_{0}u_{1,i}^{\prime}(-X))+b_{2}u_{1,2}(X,0).

This reduces to a linear system for
x_{0}
and
b_{2}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-g(r_{s})u_{1,i}^{\prime}(-X)&u_{1,2}(-X)\\
-g(r_{s})u_{1,i}^{\prime}(X)&u_{1,2}(X)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}\\
b_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{-}(r_{s})-X\partial_{r}u_{0}^{-}(r_{s})-g(r_{s})u_{1,i}(-X,0)\\
u_{1}^{+}(r_{s})+X\partial_{r}u_{0}^{+}(r_{s})-g(r_{s})u_{1,i}(X,0)\end{bmatrix}.

Angular momentum equation
—
The
\ell_{1}
equation, after substituting for
\ell_{0}
, becomes

\displaystyle\ell_{1}^{\prime\prime}-\left(u_{0}+\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}}{u_{0}}\right)\ell_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\ell}{r_{h}}\left(\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}}{u_{0}}\right).

(65)

We then find

\displaystyle\ell_{1}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\ell}{r_{h}}\left(e^{\int^{r}u_{0}ds}\int^{r}e^{-\int^{s}u_{0}dt}ds\right)+c_{1},

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{r_{h}}\left(\operatorname{sech}a+\cosh(a-x\sinh a)\right)\operatorname{sech}\left(\frac{\sinh a}{2}x\right)^{2}+c_{1},

(66)

which behaves like a tanh profile plus a constant determined by asymptotic matching conditions.
This jump is precisely that predicted by the jump in density for the first order outer equation, meaning that
\ell^{\pm}_{1}=2\ell(\rho^{\pm}-\rho_{\infty})
for both inner and outer solutions.

Appendix B
Smooth supersonic stability analysis

We show that smooth inviscid supersonic flow within the sonic point is stable by showing that all normal modes with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions at the sonic point have negative growth rates.
Sufficiently small viscous perturbations will not change this spectral property, i.e. these growth rates stay negative.

We have the following asymptotic behaviour for
r,x
, and
{}{u}
as
r\to r_{*}
from below,

\displaystyle{}{u}

\displaystyle\approx-1+(r-r_{*})\partial_{r}{}{u},

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dr}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{}{u}}{{}{u}^{2}-1}\approx\frac{1}{2\partial_{r}{}{u}(r_{*}-r)},

\displaystyle x(r)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{-\log(r_{*}-r)}{2\partial_{r}{}{u}},

\displaystyle r_{*}-r

\displaystyle\approx e^{-2\partial_{r}{}{u}x},

\displaystyle\tilde{u}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e^{2\partial_{r}{}{u}x}}{2\partial_{r}{}{u}}\partial_{x}\psi.

(67)

This leads to the following asymptotic behaviour for the
w
equation

\displaystyle w^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda^{2}(1-2\partial_{r}{}{u}e^{-2\partial_{r}{}{u}x})w.

(68)

We can determine limiting behaviour for the solution
w
(and the physical perturbation velocity
\tilde{u}
) using WKB analysis.
We assume the following asymptotic behaviour for
w
,

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=Ae^{-|\lambda|x-f},\quad f>0,\quad f\to 0\text{ as }x\to\infty,

(69)

which when substituted into the
w
equation implies

\displaystyle w^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=A(-f^{\prime\prime}+f^{\prime 2}+2|\lambda|f^{\prime}+|\lambda|^{2})w,

\displaystyle\approx A(-f^{\prime\prime}+2|\lambda|f^{\prime}+|\lambda|^{2})w.

(70)

We can then solve for the decaying term
f
to find

\displaystyle-f^{\prime\prime}+2|\lambda|f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-2\partial_{r}{}{u}|\lambda|^{2}e^{-2\partial_{r}{}{u}x},

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2(|\lambda|+\partial_{r}{}{u})}e^{-2\partial_{r}{}{u}x},

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\approx e^{-|\lambda|x}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(|\lambda|+\partial_{r}{}{u})}e^{-2\partial_{r}{}{u}x}\right).

This then implies that the physical perturbation velocity
\tilde{u}
has the following limiting behaviour as
r\to r_{*}

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left((\lambda-|\lambda|)x-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(|\lambda|+\partial_{r}{}{u})}e^{-2\partial_{r}{}{u}x}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{u}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+\partial_{r}{}{u}}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(|\lambda|+\partial_{r}{}{u})}e^{-2\partial_{r}{}{u}x}\right),

\displaystyle\to\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+\partial_{r}{}{u}}>0\quad\text{ as }\quad r\to r_{*}\quad\text{ if }\quad\lambda>0.

Modes with non-negative growth rate therefore have non-zero boundary conditions at the sonic point, and so all normal modes with zero boundary conditions at the sonic point must have negative growth rates.
Smooth transonic accretion flows are therefore linearly stable.

Appendix C
Shock stability analysis

We determine which possible solutions (inner shock, outer shock, or smooth transonic flow) are physical by determining their linear stability.

C.1
Shock stability analysis

To anlayse the shock stability problem we must zoom in to the shock length scale, following the same asymptotic procedure as for the steady problem.

Zeroth order equations
—
The leading order problem in
\varepsilon
reduces to

\displaystyle{}{u}_{0}\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{0}-\frac{\tilde{u}_{0}{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}+\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle{}{u}_{0}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{0}+{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}\tilde{u}_{0}+\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{0}+\frac{2{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{0}}{{}{u}_{0}}-2{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{0}-2\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{u}_{0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\left({}{u}_{0}+\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)\partial_{x}\tilde{\ell}_{0}-\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{0}

\displaystyle=0

(71)

First order equations
—
At the next order the equations satisfy

\displaystyle{}{u}_{0}\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{1}-\frac{\tilde{u}_{1}{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}+\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{1}=-\left(\partial_{t}\tilde{\delta}_{0}+{}{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{0}-\left(\frac{{}{u}_{1}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)^{\prime}\tilde{u}_{0}\right),

\displaystyle{}{u}_{0}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{1}+\tilde{u}_{1}{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}+\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{1}+\frac{2{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{1}}{{}{u}_{0}}-2{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{1}-2\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}=

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\bigg(\partial_{t}\tilde{u}_{0}+{}{u}_{1}^{\prime}\tilde{u}_{0}+{}{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{0}-2{}{u}_{1}^{\prime}\partial_{x}\tilde{\delta}_{0}+2\left(\frac{{}{u}_{1}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)^{\prime}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{0}\bigg),

\displaystyle\left({}{u}_{0}+\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)\partial_{x}\tilde{\ell}_{1}-\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{\ell}_{1}=-\left(\frac{2\ell}{r_{h}}\left(\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)^{\prime}+\left({}{u}_{0}-\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)^{\prime}{}{\ell}_{1}^{\prime}\right),

(72)

The left-hand side is a consistent linear operator applied to the highest order terms.
The right-hand side represents source terms from higher order problems.
These source terms are constrained by solvability conditions on the left-hand linear operators.

C.2
Zeroth order shock instability problem

If we put in the ansatz

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{0}(t,x)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda_{0}t}{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}(x),

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}_{0}(t,x)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda_{0}t}{}{\delta}_{0}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{0}(t,x)

\displaystyle=0,

(73)

then the zeroth order equations are satisfied.
The spatial profile thus corresponds to translation of the shock location at rate
\lambda_{0}
.

C.3
First order shock instability problem

Substituting these solutions into the next order gives the equations

\displaystyle{}{u}_{0}\tilde{\delta}_{1}^{\prime}-\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\tilde{u}_{1}+\tilde{u}_{1}^{\prime}=\lambda_{0}\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}+{}{u}_{0}\left(\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\frac{u_{1}}{u_{0}}\right)^{\prime},

\displaystyle{}{u}_{0}\tilde{u}_{1}^{\prime}+\tilde{u}_{1}{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}+\tilde{\delta}_{1}^{\prime}+2\left(\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}\tilde{u}_{1}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}-{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}\tilde{\delta}_{1}^{\prime}-\tilde{u}_{1}^{\prime\prime}\right)=

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\lambda_{0}{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}-\left({}{u}_{0}^{\prime}{}{u}_{1}\right)^{\prime}-2\left(\left(\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)^{\prime}{}{u}_{1}^{\prime}+\left(\frac{{}{u}_{1}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)^{\prime}{}{u}_{0}^{\prime\prime}\right).

(74)

The zeroth order angular momentum perturbation drops out of the analysis, and so does not contribute further to the stability calculation.

Rearranging in terms of
u
—
We can rearrange this problem to derive the same linear operator as the steady problem.
Substituting for
\tilde{\delta}_{1}
and simplifying derivatives of
{}{u}_{0}
, we find

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left({}{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{{}{u}_{0}}+\frac{4{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)\partial_{x}+\cosh a\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}}\right)\left[\tilde{u}_{1}+\lambda_{0}\right]=

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{2{}{u}_{0}^{2}}\left(\cosh a\left(1-{}{u}_{0}^{2}\right){}{u}_{1}+\left(3{}{u}_{0}^{2}-1\right){}{u}_{1}^{\prime}\right).

(75)

Self-adjoint form
—
To clarify solvability conditions (where we must integrate against the cokernel), we rewrite the problem in self-adjoint form

\displaystyle L^{\dagger}[\tilde{u}_{1}+\lambda_{0}]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(\tilde{u}_{1}+\lambda_{0})^{\prime}}{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}{}{u}_{0}}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{\cosh a}{{}{u}_{0}^{2}}(\tilde{u}_{1}+\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{}{u}_{0}^{3}}\left(\cosh a\left(1-{}{u}_{0}^{2}\right){}{u}_{1}+\left(3{}{u}_{0}^{2}-1\right){}{u}_{1}^{\prime}\right).

(76)

Solving for
\lambda_{0}
—
We can expand the problem as

\displaystyle L^{\dagger}[\tilde{u}_{1}]=-\cosh a\frac{\lambda_{0}}{{}{u}_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2{}{u}_{0}^{3}}\left(\cosh a\left(1-{}{u}_{0}^{2}\right){}{u}_{1}+\left(3{}{u}_{0}^{2}-1\right){}{u}_{1}^{\prime}\right).

Solvability requires that the right hand side is orthogonal to the cokernel (equal to the kernel for a self-adjoint operator)
which gives an explicit formula for
\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{}{u}_{0}^{\prime}}{2{}{u}_{0}^{3}}\left(\cosh a\left(1-{}{u}_{0}^{2}\right){}{u}_{1}+\left(3{}{u}_{0}^{2}-1\right){}{u}_{1}^{\prime}\right)\,dx}{\cosh a\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}}{u_{0}^{2}}\,dx},

which simplifies to the concise form

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=-g(r_{s})\operatorname{sech}a=\frac{\frac{1}{r_{s}}-\frac{2}{\left(r_{s}-r_{h}\right){}^{2}}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r_{s}^{3}}}{u^{+}_{0}(r_{s})+u^{-}_{0}(r_{s})}.

(77)
