Shear viscosity expression for a graphene system in relaxation time approximation
Cho Win Aung, Thandar Zaw Win, Gaurav Khandal, Sabyasachi Ghosh

Abstract
We have gone through the detailed microscopic calculation of the shear viscosity of a 2-dimensional graphene system in the relaxation time approximation-based kinetic theory framework. After getting its final expressions, we compared it with the shear viscosity expressions of other possible 2-dimensional as well as 3-dimensional nonrelativistic and ultra-relativistic fluid systems. The aim of the comparison is to reveal - how their different one-body dispersion relations affect their many-body fluid properties like shear viscosity and the viscosity to entropy density ratio. It is also aimed to reveal the 3-dimension to the 2-dimension transformation of their mathematical structures. We have numerically explored the differences in their order of magnitude and dependence on thermodynamical parameters – temperature and chemical potential. Marking two thermodynamical domains – Dirac fluid and Fermi liquid – for a 2-dimensional graphene system, we have noticed that shear viscosity, entropy density as well as their ratios decrease toward saturated values when one goes from Fermi liquid to Dirac fluid domain. When one shifts from mili-electron volt scales of temperature and chemical potential in condensed matter physics location to their Mega-electron volt scales in high energy physics location, then the same results may be expected for hot quark matter case, where the transition from the neutron star to early universe domains may be considered as Fermi liquid to Dirac fluid transition.

II
Formalism

Let us start our formalism from energy-momentum tensor (
T^{\mu\nu}
), as practised for RF like quark and hadronic matter. Here, we will go for GF calculation, so reader should have to be careful on some particular steps, where it is different from the RF case. Showing these differences is one of the core agenda of the present article. Although, reader can find similarities between most of steps of GHD of GF and RHD of RF. The
T^{\mu\nu}
has two parts - the ideal part

, related to the knowledge of thermodynamics and the dissipative part
T_{D}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{0}^{\mu\nu}+T_{D}^{\mu\nu}.

(1)

In this dynamic picture of fluid, ideal energy-momentum tensor and electron number flow can be expressed in macroscopic form as,

\displaystyle T_{0}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\frac{u^{\mu}u^{\nu}}{v_{g}^{2}}-P\left(g^{\mu\nu}-\frac{u^{\mu}u^{\nu}}{v_{g}^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle N_{0}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\frac{u^{\mu}}{v_{g}},

(2)

in terms of the building blocks - energy density
\epsilon
, pressure
P
, number density
n
, fluid (element) velocity
u^{\mu}
and metric tensor
g^{\mu\nu}
. Here, four-velocity
u^{\mu}=\gamma_{g}(v_{g},\vec{u})
for GHD is designed by following the four velocity structure
u^{\mu}=\gamma(c,\vec{u})
for RHD as done in Ref.
Narozhny 2019
. One can notice that speed of light
c
in RHD is replaced by graphene Fermi velocity
v_{g}
in GHD. So, Lorentz factor
\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-u^{2}/c^{2}}
in RHD is also converted into
\gamma_{g}=1/\sqrt{1-u^{2}/v_{g}^{2}}
in GHD. In static limit (
{\vec{u}}\rightarrow 0
), four velocity,
u^{\mu}=\gamma_{g}(v_{g},\vec{u})\rightarrow u^{\mu}=\gamma_{g}(v_{g},0)
and
\gamma_{g}=1/\sqrt{1-u^{2}/v_{g}^{2}}\rightarrow 1
. So, Eq. (
2
) provides a static electron number flow
N^{\mu}_{0}\equiv n
, and static energy-momentum tensor,

T_{0}^{\mu\nu}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&0&0&0\\
0&P&0&0\\
0&0&P&0\\
0&0&0&P\\
\end{pmatrix},

(3)

which reflects the standard static fluid aspect like Pascal’s law. The macroscopic quantities

and

can be expressed in terms of the microscopic quantities; four-momentum (
p^{\mu}
) and four-velocity (
v^{\mu}
) of electrons as,

T_{0}^{\mu\nu}=N_{s}\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}p^{\mu}v^{\nu}f_{0},

(4)

and

N_{0}^{\mu}=N_{s}\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}v^{\mu}f_{0},

(5)

where
N_{s}=2
is spin degeneracy factor of electron and

is its Fermi-Dirac (FD) distribution function
f_{0}=1/\{\exp{(\beta(E-\mu))}+1\}
. Here,
\beta=1/(k_{B}T)
and

4
) and (

II.1
Entropy density in two-dimensional Graphene

For Graphene, the dispersion relation is given by

E=pv_{g}~.

(6)

The total number of fermions at any value of temperature is given by,

N=\int_{0}^{\infty}D\left(E\right)dEf_{0},

(7)

where
D\left(E\right)dE
is number of energy states in energy range
E
to
E+dE

After plugging the value of

A

7
) and
f_{0}

\displaystyle N=N_{s}\frac{2\pi a}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}v_{g}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{E}{A^{-1}e^{\beta E}+1}dE,

where
A=\exp{(\beta\mu)}
is the fugacity and

B

n_{g}^{2D}=\frac{N}{a}=\frac{N_{s}}{2\pi v_{g}^{2}}f_{2}\left(A\right)T^{2}.

(8)

Now from the Eq. (
4

\epsilon_{g}^{2D}=T_{0}^{00}=N_{s}\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left(E\right)f_{0}.

(9)

After using the graphene dispersion relation and plugging the value of
f_{0}
, we get

\epsilon_{g}^{2D}=\frac{1}{\pi v_{g}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{E^{2}}{A^{-1}e^{\beta E}+1}dE

(10)

and after replacing this integral in terms of Fermi integral function, the final expression of the energy density is given by

\epsilon_{g}^{2D}=\frac{N_{s}}{\pi v_{g}^{2}}f_{3}\left(A\right)T^{3}.

(11)

Now again from the Eq. (
4

P_{g}^{2D}=T_{0}^{11}=N_{s}\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left(\frac{E}{2}\right)f_{0},

(12)

since
\vec{p}_{x}\vec{v}_{x}\approx\frac{|\vec{p}|}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{|\vec{v}_{g}|}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{E}{2}

After solving this expression as similar to the energy density, we get

P_{g}^{2D}=\frac{N_{s}}{2\pi v_{g}^{2}}f_{3}\left(A\right)T^{3}.

(13)

In terms of number density, energy density, and pressure, we can write the entropy density from the Euler thermodynamic relation in 2D graphene system:

(14)

After substituting the value of energy density (

(15)

II.2
Shear viscosity in two-dimensional graphene

Next, let us come to dissipative part of
T_{D}^{\mu\nu}
, where only shear stress tensor
\pi^{\mu\nu}
will be considered for calculating shear viscosity coefficient (
\eta
). The detailed description of relativistic hydrodynamics for calculating transport coefficients can be found in Refs.
Gavin 1985
;
Chakraborty and Kapusta 2011
;
Fernandez-Fraile and Gomez Nicola 2009
,
whose graphene or unconventional version (neither relativistic nor nonrelativistic) is considered here.
The dissipative term of energy-momentum tensor includes shear stress
\pi^{\mu\nu}
and bulk pressure
\Pi
as

T^{\mu\nu}_{D}=\pi^{\mu\nu}+\Pi\Delta^{\mu\nu}.

(16)

Here, we have assumed Landau-Lifshitz definition of flow, where
T^{\mu\nu}_{D}
will be orthogonal to fluid velocity
u^{\mu}
, i.e.
u_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}_{D}=0
.
These shear stress
\pi^{\mu\nu}
and bulk pressure
\Pi
have proportional relations with fluid velocity gradient as

\displaystyle\pi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\mathcal{U}^{\mu\nu},

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta{\cal U}_{\zeta},

(17)

where proportional constants are shear viscosity
\eta
and bulk viscosity
\zeta
. Their respective fluid velocity gradients are

\mathcal{U}^{\mu\nu}_{\eta}=D^{\mu}u^{\nu}+D^{\nu}u^{\mu}-\frac{2}{3}\Delta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\rho}u^{\rho}.

(18)

and

{\cal U}_{\zeta}\equiv\partial_{\rho}u^{\rho},

(19)

where
\Delta^{\mu\nu}=-g^{\mu\nu}+u^{\mu}u^{\nu}
is projection tensor normal to
u^{\mu}

and
D^{\mu}=\partial^{\mu}-u^{\mu}u^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}
is derivative normal to
u^{\mu}
. They are designed
such a way that we can get
\Delta^{\mu\nu}\equiv\delta^{ij}
and
D^{\mu}\equiv\partial^{i}
in fluid rest frame
u^{\mu}\equiv(1,{\vec{0}})
.
Usually, greek index like
\mu\equiv(0,i)
takes values 0 for the temporal component and
i=1,2,3
for the spatial component for 3D system but here for 2D system, we will consider
\mu\equiv(0,i=1,2)
because z-component
i=3
will not be considered.
During the transition from
\mu
-index to spatial component
i
, one can get disspative part of energy momentum tensor
as

\displaystyle T^{ij}_{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi^{ij}+\Pi\delta^{ij}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(\partial^{i}u^{j}+\partial^{j}u^{i}-\frac{2}{3}\delta^{ij}\nabla\cdot u)+\zeta\delta^{ij}\nabla\cdot u,

which ensure that diagonal part (
i=j
) is linked with bulk viscosity
\zeta
and off-diagonal part
(i\neq j)

is linked with shear viscosity
\eta
. Present work will focus only on shear viscosity coefficients, so
we will not proceed with discussion of bulk viscosity further.

The microscopic theory describes shear stress tensor in terms of particle velocity
v
and momentum
p
as,

\pi_{\mu\nu}=N_{s}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}p_{\mu}v_{\nu}\delta f_{\eta},

(21)

where we are assuming that equilibrium distribution function
f_{0}
gets a small deviation
\delta f
, which can be considered as first-order Taylor series expansion equilibrium distribution function:

\displaystyle\delta f

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial E}=\phi_{\eta}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial E}

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{U}_{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial E}.

Considering the relation
v_{\nu}=(E/p^{2})p_{\nu}
, macroscopic
\pi_{\mu\nu}=\eta\mathcal{U}_{\mu\nu}
and microscopic Eq. (
21
) can be connected as,

\displaystyle\pi_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta~\mathcal{U}_{\mu\nu}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{s}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)p_{\mu}p_{\nu}\mathcal{A}^{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{U}_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial E}.

The four-momentum of an electron can be defined as
p^{\mu}=(E/v_{g},\vec{p})
in unconventional notation.
Considering energy as a static limit of
p^{\nu}u_{\nu}
, we can write FD as,

f_{0}=\frac{1}{exp{\left(\frac{p^{\nu}u_{\nu}-\mu(x)}{T(x)}\right)+1}}.

(24)

Here, we have to consider the local thermalization concept, where thermodynamical quantities
T(x)
,
\mu(x)
as well as fluid velocity
u^{\mu}(x)
are assumed to be functions of
x\equiv x^{\mu}=(x^{0},x^{i})
.
To find the unknown coefficient
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha\beta}
, we will use Boltzmann transport equation (BTE)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+v^{\mu}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\mu}}+F^{\mu}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\mu}}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)_{Col},

(25)

where
\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Big)_{Col}
is the collision term that leads the system out of equilibrium.
F^{\mu}
is represented as all external forces, and
v^{\mu}
is the velocity of the fluid particles.
Using velocity expression in terms of
E
and
p
for graphene,
v^{\mu}=(\frac{E}{p^{2}})p^{\mu}
, we get:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)_{Col},

(26)

where we ignore
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
and
F^{\mu}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\mu}}
as they will not contribute in shear dissipation. Using the Relaxation Time Approximation (RTA) method, the collision term can be considered as,

\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)_{Col}=-\frac{\delta f}{\tau_{c}},

(27)

where
\tau_{c}
is the relaxation time. Putting
f\approx f_{0}
in the left-hand side (lhs) of BTE,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f_{0}=-\frac{\delta f}{\tau_{c}}.

(28)

Using the Eq. (
24
), the lhs of the above Eq. (
28
) can be expanded as,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f_{0}

\displaystyle=-f_{0}(1-f_{0})\Big[\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)\frac{p^{\mu}p^{\nu}}{T}\partial_{\mu}u_{\nu}(x)\Big]

\displaystyle=-f_{0}(1-f_{0})\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)\frac{p^{\mu}p^{\nu}}{2T}(\partial_{\mu}u_{\nu}+\partial_{\nu}u_{\mu})

\displaystyle=-f_{0}(1-f_{0})\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)\frac{p^{\mu}p^{\nu}}{T}\mathcal{U}_{\mu\nu},

(29)

and right hand side (rhs) of Eq. (
28
) can be written as

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta f}{\tau_{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{0}(1-f_{0})}{T}\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\mathcal{A}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{U}_{\mu\nu}.

(30)

So, equating lhs and rhs of Eq. (
28
), we get
\mathcal{A}^{\mu\nu}=(-\frac{E}{p^{2}}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\tau_{c})
. Transforming temporal+spatial to only spatial index, we can write the shear stress tensor as

\displaystyle\pi_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\mathcal{U}_{ij}~,

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{s}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left(\frac{E}{p^{2}}\right)^{2}\tau_{c}(p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}p_{l})\mathcal{U}^{kl}\beta f_{0}(1-f_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{s}}{8}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}E^{2}\tau_{c}\beta f_{0}(1-f_{0})\mathcal{U}_{ij},

where, we used
<p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}p_{l}>=\frac{\vec{p}^{4}}{8}\big(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\big)
(see Appendix
C

\beta f_{0}\left(1-f_{0}\right)=-\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial E}

(32)

and

-\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial E}=\beta\frac{e^{\beta\left(E-\mu\right)}}{\left(e^{\beta\left(E-\mu\right)}+1\right)^{2}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left(\frac{1}{e^{\beta\left(E-\mu\right)}+1}\right).

(33)

Finally, the expression for shear viscosity is,

\eta=\frac{N_{s}}{8}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}E^{2}\tau_{c}\beta f_{0}(1-f_{0}).

(34)

After using the Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) and converting the momentum terms into energy using dispersion relation (
6
), the Eq. (

\eta=\frac{N_{s}}{16\pi v_{g}^{2}}\tau_{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{E^{3}}{A^{-1}e^{\beta E}+1}dE.

(35)

After solving this integration by using the identity of the Fermi integral function, the expression of shear viscosity for 2D graphene (using subscript and superscript notation to distinguish the expressions of different systems) is

\eta_{g}^{2D}=\frac{3N_{s}}{8\pi v_{g}^{2}}\tau_{c}f_{3}\left(A\right)T^{3}.

Now, on taking the ratio of the shear viscosity (
36

\frac{\eta_{g}^{2D}}{s_{g}^{2D}}=\frac{3}{4}\tau_{c}\Bigg[3f_{3}\left(A\right)-\frac{\mu}{T}f_{2}\left(A\right)\Bigg]^{-1}f_{3}\left(A\right)T.

\displaystyle s_{g}^{3D}=\frac{N_{s}T^{3}}{\pi^{2}v_{g}^{3}}\Bigg[4f_{4}\left(A\right)-\frac{\mu}{T}f_{3}\left(A\right)\Bigg],

\displaystyle\eta_{g}^{3D}=\frac{4N_{s}}{5\pi^{2}v_{g}^{3}}\tau_{c}f_{4}\left(A\right)T^{4},

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{g}^{3D}}{s_{g}^{3D}}=\frac{4}{5}\tau_{c}\Bigg[4f_{4}\left(A\right)-\frac{\mu}{T}f_{3}\left(A\right)\Bigg]^{-1}f_{4}\left(A\right)T.

(43)

Now we have a 3-dimensional nonrelativistic (3D-NR) system of fermions. After applying the same methodology to this system, we get all the expressions of entropy density, shear viscosity, and the ratio of shear viscosity to entropy density,

\displaystyle s_{NR}^{3D}=N_{s}\left(\frac{m}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}T^{\frac{3}{2}}\Bigg[\frac{5}{2}f_{\frac{5}{2}}\left(A\right)-\frac{\mu}{T}f_{\frac{3}{2}}\left(A\right)\Bigg],

(44)

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{NR}^{3D}}{s_{NR}^{3D}}=\frac{1}{4}\tau_{c}\Bigg[\frac{5}{2}f_{\frac{5}{2}}\left(A\right)-\frac{\mu}{T}f_{\frac{3}{2}}\left(A\right)\Bigg]^{-1}f_{\frac{5}{2}}\left(A\right)T.

(46)

This 3D-NR system, showing fluid behavior, can be applicable for most of the fluids or liquids (e.g. water) used in our daily life.

IV
Summary

We can summarize our investigation in the following steps. First, we introduce a brief macroscopic description of electron fluid in graphene, then we focus on its microscopic description. Our dealing quantity is considered as energy-momentum tensor, whose ideal part represents energy density and pressure in the static limit picture of fluid dynamics. Using those thermodynamical quantities, our destination from the ideal part of the energy-momentum tensor becomes entropy density, which will be used to be normalized with shear viscosity. From the dissipative part of the energy-momentum tensor, shear viscosity coefficients of electron fluid are calculated based on the kinetic theory approach with relaxation time approximation. Temperature and chemical potential-dependent general expressions of shear viscosity, entropy density, and the shear viscosity to entropy density ratio has been calculated and plotted for different cases of electron fluid like nonrelativistic, graphene and ultra-relativistic cases. For completeness of comparison, we considered both 3D and 2D systems. Analyzing the results of different cases, we get a comparative understanding and conclusions, which are addressed briefly in bullet points:

1.

The
\mu/T
dependence of shear viscosity
\eta
as well as entropy density
s
for URF and GF are exactly similar but a little different from the NRF.

2.

We notice a huge difference among URF, GF, and NRF in terms of the order of magnitude of
\eta
and
s
with ranking URF
\ll
GF
\ll
NRF.

3.

During transiting from 3D to 2D, order of magnitude of
\eta
and
s
shift towards lower values.

4.

When we go from Fermi Liquid (
\mu/T\gg 1
) to Dirac Liquid (
\mu/T\ll 1
) domain, values of
\eta
,

s
and
\eta/s
ratio decrease towards a saturated values.

5.

Interesting ranking for
\eta/s
becomes URF
=
GF
\geq
NRF.

The present comparative study on the microscopic calculation of shear viscosity may be considered as a good documentation of master formulas of different cases from 3D-URF, GF, NRF to 2D-URF, GF and NRF. In the future, it may be useful for actual graphene system estimation, where one should go with some first principle or model-dependent calculation of relaxation time. Also, one should deal with electron-hole plasma with appropriate degeneracy factor in the Dirac Fluid domain for the actual graphene system but the present work sticks with electron description only due to observing the estimations for its different dispersion relations. Our immediate future plan is to concentrate on the actual graphene phenomenology on the viscous aspects.

Appendix C
Average Angular Integral in 2D

We have the integral form as

\displaystyle\int p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}p_{l}d^{2}p=pdp\int p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}p_{l}d\theta.

(68)

Since

\vec{p}=p\hat{n},

where

\hat{n}=\cos{\theta}\,\hat{i}+\sin{\theta}\hat{j},

and

p_{i}=\vec{p}.\hat{e_{i}}=p\left(\hat{n}.\hat{e_{i}}\right)=pn_{i},

the integral becomes

\displaystyle\int p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}p_{l}d^{2}p=pdp,p^{4}\int n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}\,d\theta.

(69)

Now, we have to calculate

\int n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}\,d\theta=?

Case:1.
The above integral becomes

\displaystyle\int n_{1}^{2}n_{2}^{2}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2}{\theta}\sin^{2}{\theta}\,d\theta

(70)

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos^{2}{\theta}\sin^{2}{\theta}\,d\theta.

(71)

Now, using the Beta function, we know that

\displaystyle B\left(u,v\right)=2\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\cos{\theta}\right)^{2u-1}\left(\sin{\theta}\right)^{2v-1}\,d\theta

(72)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle B\left(u,v\right)=\frac{\Gamma u\,\Gamma v}{\Gamma\left(u+v\right)}.

(73)

Applying this, we get

\int n_{1}^{2}n_{2}^{2}\,d\theta=\frac{2\pi}{8}.

(74)

Case:2.

\displaystyle\int n_{1}^{3}n_{2}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int n_{1}n_{2}^{3}\,d\theta

(75)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{3}{\theta}\sin{\theta}\,d\theta=0.

(76)

Case:3.

\displaystyle\int n_{1}^{4}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int n_{2}^{4}\,d\theta

(77)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{4}{\theta}\,d\theta

(78)

\displaystyle=2\,B\left(\frac{5}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{3\pi}{4}.

(79)

Now, the above integral can be written as

\displaystyle\int n_{1}^{4}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int n_{2}^{4}\,d\theta=\frac{2\pi}{8}\times 3,

(80)

Now, the integral can be expressed as

\displaystyle\int n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}\,d\theta=\frac{2\pi}{8}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right)

(81)

and

\displaystyle\int p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}p_{l}\,d^{2}p=2\pi\,p\,dp\frac{p^{4}}{8}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right).

(82)

Now, the average final expression will be

<p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}p_{l}>=\frac{p^{4}}{8}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right).

(83)
