Energy-variational solutions for viscoelastic fluid models
Abramo Agosti, Robert Lasarzik, Elisabetta Rocca

Abstract
In this article, we introduce the concept of energy-variational solutions for a large class of systems of nonlinear evolutionary partial differential equations. Under certain convexity assumptions, the existence of such solutions can be shown constructively by an adapted minimizing movement scheme. Weak-strong uniqueness follows by a suitable relative energy inequality. Our main motivation is to apply the general framework to viscoelastic fluid models. Therefore, we give a short overview on different versions of such models and their derivation. The abstract result is applied to two of these viscoelastic fluid models in full detail. In the conclusion, we comment on further applications of the general theory and its possible impact.

Introduction

Viscoelastic fluids can be seen as an in-between-state of Newtonian fluids and elastic solids. In elastic materials, the resistance to deformation is described in terms of the deformation and in viscous fluids in terms of displacement rates. A combination of both leads to characteristics like a nonlinear stress strain relation or even hysteresis in the stress-strain curve, creep, or memory effects in the material (see for instance
[
7
,
20
,
28
]
).

A standard model for viscoelastic fluids, is the so-called Oldroyd-B model, which couples the Navier-Stokes equations for the fluid velocity
{\boldsymbol{v}}:\overline{{\Omega}}\times[0,\infty)\ra\R^{d}
with an additional evolution equation for the left Cauchy–Green tensor
\mathbb{B}:\overline{{\Omega}}\times[0,\infty)\ra\R^{d\times d}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla p-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}+\beta\nabla\cdot\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}-2((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{B})_{\text{sym}}-\alpha((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{B})_{\text{sym}}+\mathbb{B}-\MI

\displaystyle=0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}^{T}\,,

(1b)

endowed with appropriate initial and boundary conditions, where
p
denotes the pressure of the system,
\mu\in(0,\infty)
and
\alpha\,,\beta\in\R
having the same sign. Finally,
{\boldsymbol{f}}
represents a given source.

If
\alpha\neq 0
, this model formally satisfies the associated dissipation mechanism

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{O}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B})\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\Psi_{O}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B})\text{d}\tau=\int_{s}^{t}\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle\text{d}\tau\,,

(1c)

where the corresponding energy is given by

\mathcal{E}_{O}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B})=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\tr(\mathbb{B}-\MI-\ln\mathbb{B})\text{d}x

and the dissipation functional by

\Psi_{O}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B}):=\int_{\Omega}2\mu\lvert(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\rvert^{2}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\tr(\mathbb{B}(\MI-\mathbb{B}^{-1})^{2})\text{d}x.

From a mathematical point of view, analysis for these kinds of models is rather challenging. First and foremost the quadratic terms in (
1b
) are not known to be integrable from the
a priori
bounds of (
1c
), since these only assure an
L^{1}
-bound for the stress tensor
\mathbb{B}
. Secondly, the highly nonlinear terms are not treatable by compactness methods, since no compactness due to embeddings is known to hold for
\mathbb{B}
.
For
\alpha=0
, Lions and Masmoudi
[
31
]
showed existence of global weak solutions to (
1a
)–(
1b
) by a technique called propagation of compactness (see also
[
34
]
).
Many other works in the field introduce so-called stress diffusion
-\Delta\mathbb{B}
on the left hand-side of (
1b
) in order to improve
compactness
[
12
,
32
]
.
This stress diffusion can be rigorously introduced by thermodynamical modeling
[
37
]
, and can serve to model the dispersion and attenuation of elastic waves which may be experimentally observed in some particular situations
[
42
]
, but often it is added in order to infer strong convergence properties for a suitable approximating sequence of
\mathbb{B}
.
Moreover, there are numerical studies showing that numerical simulations for viscoelastic models are close to empirical data in the absence of stress diffusion effects
[
36
]
or the simulated effects are closer to the expected phenomena for vanishing stress diffusion
[
1
]
.

In the current article, we therefore propose a novel analytical framework to treat especially viscoelastic models without stress diffusion. We introduce the concept of
energy-variational solutions
for a general class of evolutionary PDEs of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{U}}+A({\boldsymbol{U}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\Y^{*}\text{ with }\hskip 10.00002pt{\boldsymbol{U}}(0)={\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\V\,

(2)

for two Banach spaces
\V
and
\Y
such that
\Y\subset\V^{*}
,
A\,:\,[0,\infty)\times\V\to\Y^{*}
,
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\in\V
.
A smooth solution to (
2
) for any initial value formally fulfills the energy dissipation mechanism

\displaystyle\E({\boldsymbol{U}})\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\Psi({\boldsymbol{U}})\text{d}\tau\leq 0

(3)

for all
s<t\in[0,\infty)
, where
\E
and
\Psi
are suitable energy and dissipation functionals defined on
\V
.
A standard weak solution to (
2
) fulfills

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{U}},\Phi\rangle\Big|_{s}^{t}-\int_{{s}}^{t}\langle{\boldsymbol{U}},\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle+\langle A({\boldsymbol{U}}),\Phi\rangle\text{d}\tau=0\,

(4)

for all
s<t\in[0,\infty)
and
\Phi\in\C^{1}([0,\infty);\Y)
. Introducing an upper bound for the energy
E\geq\E({\boldsymbol{U}})
, subtracting (
4
) from (
3
), and, adding a term for the associated energy defect with weight
\mathcal{K}\,:\,\Y\to[0,+\infty)
, gives the formulation

\left[E-\langle{\boldsymbol{U}},\Phi\rangle\right]\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\langle{\boldsymbol{U}},\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle+\Psi({\boldsymbol{U}})-\langle A({\boldsymbol{U}}),\Phi\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)\left[\E({\boldsymbol{U}})-E\right]\text{d}\tau\leq 0

(5)

for a.e.
{s}<t\in[0,\infty)
and all
\Phi\in\C^{1}([0,\infty);\Y)
.
(
5
) is a convex function in
{\boldsymbol{U}}
. Besides this existence of solutions, which is proven constructively via a time-discretization based on sequential minimization, we also provide the weak-strong uniqueness of solutions. This means that all
energy-variational solutions
coincide with a local strong solution emanating from the same initial datum, as long as the latter exists. Moreover, the solution is a semi-flow. Up to our knowledge, this result is the first one entailing global existence and weak-strong uniqueness result for such a general class of models.

In comparison to more established solutions concepts,
energy-variational solutions
can be seen as an in-between state of dissipative solutions and measure-valued solutions. In dissipative solutions, the formulation is generalized to an inequality
[
30
, Sec. 4.4]
while in measure-valued solutions an auxiliary measure is introduced such that the equations is fulfilled in the limit
[
10
]
.
Energy-variational solutions
combine both approaches by adding an auxiliary variable relaxing the formulation to an inequality. Nevertheless, the concept has many desirable properties, for instance: the existence and weak-strong uniqueness of
energy-variational solutions
for a large class of models via a constructive existence proof. For certain systems in fluid dynamics, the equivalence of measure-valued solutions and
energy-variational solutions
is known
[
13
]
, even though the degrees of freedom are heavily reduced in the second one, since the auxiliary matrix-valued measure is replaced by a real number in every point in time. Moreover, in
[
27
]
it was observed that
energy-variational solutions
are very flexible, in the sense that they allow to identify the limit of numerical schemes, while this result seems to be out of reach with measure-valued solutions.
Moreover, the set of
energy-variational solutions
is weakly-star closed and in some cases set-valued continuous, which enables to define minimization schemes on the solution set as proposed in
[
26
]
.

Our main result, similar to the one of
[
13
]
related to hyperbolic conservation laws, introduces a stronger solution concept with respect to the one of dissipative solutions
[
30
,
23
]
and thus also stronger than the solution concept proposed in
[
11
]
. Moreover, the proposed result has potentially multiple applications outside of viscoelastic fluid dynamics, like, for instance, the ones considered in
[
13
]
about the compressible and incpompressible Euler equations as well as the incompressible magnetohydrodynamics.

After a brief introduction to different variants of viscoelastic models, the general result is applied to two viscoelastic fluid models in full detail.
The first model we consider is taken from
[
6
]
, but allowing for vanishing stress diffusion. As in
[
6
]
, in case where the energy in (
1c
) is regularized by a quadratic term, leading to the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla p-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-\alpha\nabla\cdot\left((1-\beta)(\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})+\beta(\mathbb{B}^{2}-\mathbb{B})\right)={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}-2[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{B}]_{\text{sym}}-\alpha[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{B}]_{\text{sym}}+(\mathbb{I}+\delta\mathbb{B})(\mathbb{B}-\mathbb{I})={}

\displaystyle 0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt(\mathbb{B})_{\text{skw}}=0\,,

with
\mu
,
\delta\in(0,\infty)
,
\alpha\in\R
,
\beta\in(0,1)
, and the associated energy

\displaystyle\E_{Q}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B}):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+(1-\beta)\tr(\mathbb{B}-\MI-\ln(\mathbb{B}))+\frac{\beta}{2}|\mathbb{B}-\MI|^{2}\text{d}x\,

and dissipation

\Psi_{Q}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B}):=\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+(1-\beta)\mathbb{B}:(\MI-\mathbb{B}^{-1})^{2}+(\beta+\delta(1-\beta))|\mathbb{B}-\MI|^{2}+\delta\beta\mathbb{B}:(\mathbb{B}-\MI)^{2}\text{d}x\,,

such that an associated energy-dissipation relation like in (
1c
) is formally fulfilled for solutions to (
6
) as well.
We prove existence and weak-strong uniqueness of
energy-variational solutions
to this system by applying the aforementioned general result.

Secondly, we consider a model inspired by
[
39
]
for a symmetrized Neo–Hookean approach, which leads to the equations for the deformation gradient
\mathbb{F}
, the velocity field
{\boldsymbol{v}}
, and pressure
p
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla p-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-\alpha\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbb{F}^{2}-\mathbb{I}\right)={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\,,

(7a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{F}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{F}-[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{F}]_{\text{sym}}-\alpha[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{F}]_{\text{sym}}+(\mathbb{F}-\mathbb{F}^{-1})={}

\displaystyle 0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt(\mathbb{F})_{\text{skw}}=0\,.

(7b)

with
\mu\in(0,\infty)
,
\alpha\in\R
, and the associated energy

\displaystyle\E_{S}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\lvert\mathbb{F}\rvert^{2}-|\mathbb{I}|^{2}-\ln\det(\mathbb{F}^{2})\text{d}x\,

and dissipation

\Psi_{S}({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F}):=\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+|\mathbb{F}-\mathbb{F}^{-1}|^{2}\text{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\tr(\mathbb{F}^{2}-2\MI+\mathbb{F}^{-2})\text{d}x,

such that an associated energy-dissipation relation like in (
1c
) is formally fulfilled for solutions to (
6
) as well.
Also for this model we prove existence and weak-strong uniqueness of
energy-variational solutions
, based on our general result in Banach spaces (cf. Theorem
3.3
).

In the conclusions, we mention different other models that fit into the proposed abstract framework and give an outlook on possible future research directions.

We note that we prove the abstract existence result by a constructive time-discretization approach. Similar to the minimizing movements scheme for Gradient flows, we define a time incremental minimization algorithm for an approximation of the solution. We think that this is not the only similarity to the gradient flow setting, where a lot of results were achieved in recent years, like general existence and uniqueness results
[
3
]
and singular limit results bridging between different scales
[
35
]
. We think that the general approach presented in this article has the potential to generalize such results toa more general framework.

3
General existence result

In this section, we consider a general nonlinear evolution equation of the form

\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)+A(t,{\boldsymbol{U}}(t))=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\Y^{*}\text{ with }\hskip 10.00002pt{\boldsymbol{U}}(0)={\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\in\V\,.

(13)

Hypothesis 3.1
.

Let
\Y\subset\V
be two Banach spaces such that
\Y\subset^{d}\V^{*}
.
Let
\V
be reflexive Banach space and
\E:\V\ra[0,\infty]

(Hypothesis

the inequality with the opposite sign is inferred such that we showed the weak formulation (
4
).

From the formulation (
16
), we can even read off a higher regularity of the solution such that the relations are even fulfilled everywhere instead of almost everywhere in time.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{U}},E)
be an
energy-variational solution
in the sense of Definition
3.2
. Then there exists
(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\tilde{E})\in\C_{w}([0,\infty);\V)\times\BVl
such that
(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\tilde{E})=({\boldsymbol{U}},E)
a.e. in
(0,\infty)
and the inequality
\tilde{E}(t)\geq\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}(t))
holds true for all
t\in[0,\infty)
and

\displaystyle\left[\tilde{E}-\langle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\Phi\rangle\right]\Big|_{s-}^{t+}+\int_{s}^{t}\left[\langle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle+\Psi(\tau,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\langle A(\tau,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),\Phi\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)\left[\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\tilde{E}\right]\right]\text{d}\tau\leq 0\,

holds for all
\Phi\in\C^{1}([0,\infty);\Y)
and
for all
s<t\in(0,\infty)
.

If, additionally, it holds that
E(s+)=\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(s))
and
\E
is
\rho
-uniformly convex,
i.e.,
there exists a strictly monotone increasing function
\rho:[0,\infty)\ra[0,\infty)
with
\rho(0)=0
such that

2\E\left(\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{U}}+{\boldsymbol{V}})\right)\leq\E({\boldsymbol{U}})+\E({\boldsymbol{V}})-\rho(\|{\boldsymbol{U}}-{\boldsymbol{V}}\|_{\V})

for all
{\boldsymbol{U}}
,
{\boldsymbol{V}}\in\dom\E
, then
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is even right-hand continuous at
s
,
i.e.,

\lim_{t\searrow s}{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)={\boldsymbol{U}}(s)
in
\V
.

Proof.

In the same way as in
[
13
, Prop. 3.1]
, we infer the existence of functions
(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\tilde{E})\in\C_{w}([0,\infty);\Y^{*})\times\BVl
fulfilling the asserted inequalities. The higher regularity
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\in\C_{w}([0,\infty);\V)
follows from Lemma
2.2
.
Indeed, consider a sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n\in\N}\subset[0,\infty)
such that
t_{n}\ra t\in[0,\infty)
. Form the boundedness,
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);\V)
, we infer that the sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n})\}_{n\in\N}
admits a weakly converging subsequence in
\V
. The fact that
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\C_{w}([0,\infty);\Y^{*})
allows to identify this limit in
\Y
such that Lemma
2.2
and the uniqueness of weak limits also guarantee that
{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n})\rightharpoonup{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)
in
\V
for the whole sequence.

Now let
\{t_{n}\}_{n\in\N}\subset(s,\infty)
such that
t_{n}\searrow s
, we infer from
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\C_{w}([0,\infty);\V)
that
{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n})\rightharpoonup{\boldsymbol{U}}(s)
. The monotonicity of
E
and the weakly lower semi-continuity of
\E
imply

\displaystyle E(s)\geq\lim_{t_{n}\searrow s}E(t_{n})\geq\liminf_{n\ra\infty}\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n}))\geq\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(s))=E(s)\,

(17)

such that
\lim_{n\ra\infty}\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n}))=\E({\boldsymbol{U}})
. From the weak convergence and the strict convexity of
\E
, we infer the strong convergence by

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\ra\infty}\rho\left(\left\|{{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n})-{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)}\right\|_{\V}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\ra\infty}\left[\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n}))+\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t))-2\E\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n})+{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\ra\infty}\left[\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n}))-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t))-\langle\partial\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t)),{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{n})-{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)\rangle\right]=0\,,

where the first inequality is due to the
\rho
-uniform convexity of
\E
, the second one follows from the definition of the subdifferential, and the equality follows from the convergence of the energy and the weak convergence. This proves the strong convergence.
∎

Remark 3.3

(Properties of solutions)
.

Energy-variational solutions
enjoy the semi flow property. That means that for a solution on
[0,\infty)
every restriction on an interval
[s,t]
for all
s<t\in[0,\infty)
to the initial value
({\boldsymbol{U}}(s),E(s-))
is again a solution. Moreover, if
({\boldsymbol{U}}^{1},E^{1})
is an
energy-variational solution
on
[r,s]
to the initial value
({\boldsymbol{U}}^{1}(r),E^{1}(r-))
and
({\boldsymbol{U}}^{2},E^{2})
is an
energy-variational solution
on
[s,t]
with initial value
({\boldsymbol{U}}^{2}(s),E^{2}(s-))
with
0\leq r<s<t<\infty
and
({\boldsymbol{U}}^{1}(s),E^{1}(s+))=({\boldsymbol{U}}^{2}(s),E^{2}(s-))
then the concatenation

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{U}},E):=\begin{cases}({\boldsymbol{U}}^{1},E^{1})&\text{ on }[r,s]\\
({\boldsymbol{U}}^{2},E^{2})&\text{ on }[s,t]\end{cases}

(18)

is a solution on
[r,t]
with initial value
({\boldsymbol{U}}(r),E(r-))=({\boldsymbol{U}}^{1}(r),E^{1}(r-))
.

Energy-variational solutions
are not unique, they are even far from being unique. If
({\boldsymbol{U}},E)
is an
energy-variational solution
in the sense of Definition
3.2
, also
({\boldsymbol{U}},E+h)
is an
energy-variational solution
for any
h\geq 0
. The set is therefore admittedly too large. But this peculiar non-uniqueness is ruled out by the condition
E(0)=\E({\boldsymbol{U}}_{0})
in Theorem
3.3
. Nevertheless,
energy-variational solution
cannot be expected to be unique. However, the set of
energy-variational solutions
to a given initial value is convex and weakly-star closed, which follows in the same way as in
[
13
, Prop. 3.4]
. Moreover, for the example of incompressible Newtonian fluid dynamics the solution set is continuous in the Kuratowski sense. These can be seen as indicators that the solution set is amenable for additional selection criteria in order to single out a reasonable solution via minimization.
Already the proposed algorithm in (
22
) follows from this idea by selecting the time-discrete solution that minimizes the energy at the current time point.

In order to state the second main result of the paper concerning weak-strong uniqueness of solutions, we need to reinforce Hypothesis
3.1
by the following.

Hypothesis 3.5
.

In addition to Hypothesis
3.1
, we
assume that
\E\in\C^{2}(\inter\dom\E)
and that there exist a Banach space
\W
with
\Y\subseteq\W\subseteq\V
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, and a constant
C>0
such that

\int_{0}^{T}\|{\boldsymbol{U}}\|_{\W}^{p}\text{d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left(\Psi(t,{\boldsymbol{U}})+C_{\Psi}+(\E({\boldsymbol{U}}))^{p}+1\right)\text{d}t\,

for all
T>0
and for all
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\dom\Psi
, which implies that
\dom\Psi\subset\W\subset\V
.
Additionally, we assume that
\langle A,\Phi\rangle
and
\Psi
, are Gateaux differentiable for all
\Phi\in\Y
and a.e.
t\in(0,T)

with respect to the second variable.

Using the space
\W
, we define
\mathbb{Z}(0,T):=L^{\infty}((0,T);\V)\cap L^{p}((0,T);\W)
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{Z}}(0,T):=L^{1}((0,T);\V^{*})\oplus L^{p^{\prime}}((0,T);\W^{*})
, where
p^{\prime}
denotes the conjugated exponent to
p
,
i.e.
,
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
.

Remark 3.4
.

This space
\mathbb{Z}(0,T)
is only one possibility to characterize additional regularity of the
energy-variational solution
. Indeed, it is possible to choose a larger space
\mathbb{Z}(0,T)
in certain examples.
One possibility is to assume that the space
\W
admits a decomposition
\W=\W_{1}\times\ldots\times\W_{n}
such that
{\boldsymbol{U}}=({\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{U}}^{T}_{n})^{T}
. Then, we assume that there exist
\{p_{i}\}_{i=\{1,\ldots,n\}}
such that
pi\in[1,\infty)
and we may replace the left hand-side of the condition in Hypothesis
3.5
by

\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}\|_{\W_{i}}^{p_{i}}\text{d}t
.
This will already imply higher regularity in the first example below.

Proposition 3.6

(Relative energy inequality)
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{U}},E)\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);\V)\times\BVl
be an
energy-variational solution
in the sense of Definition
3.2
and let Hypothesis
3.5
be fulfilled. Then it holds

\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{U}},E|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{W}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{U}}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\langle\partial_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}+A(\tau,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),D^{2}\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})({\boldsymbol{U}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\rangle\text{d}\tau\\
\leq\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}))\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{U}},E|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\text{d}\tau

(19)

for all
s<t\in[0,T]
for all
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\in\C^{0}([0,T];\V)\cap L^{p}(0,T;\W)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})&\in L^{\infty}((0,T);\Y)\,,\\
D\Psi(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})&\in\widetilde{\mathbb{Z}}(0,T)\,,\\
DA(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})&\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Z}(0,T);L^{1}((0,T);\Y^{*}))\,,\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\,A(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})&\in\widetilde{\mathbb{Z}}(0,T)\,,\\
D^{2}\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})&\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Z}(0,T),\mathbb{Z}(0,T))\,,\end{split}

(20)

where
T\in(0,\infty]
.
Here we denote the relative energy by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{U}},E|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})

\displaystyle:=E-\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\langle D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),{\boldsymbol{U}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\rangle

and the relative dissipation via

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(t,{\boldsymbol{U}}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})

\displaystyle:=\Psi(t,{\boldsymbol{U}})-\Psi(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\langle D\Psi(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),{\boldsymbol{U}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\langle A(t,{\boldsymbol{U}})-A(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-DA(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})({\boldsymbol{U}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\mathcal{K}(D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}))\left[\E({\boldsymbol{U}})-\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\langle D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),{\boldsymbol{U}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\rangle\right]\,.

Remark 3.5
.

We note that, due to the convexity of the energy
\E
and the condition
E\geq\E({\boldsymbol{U}})
, the relative energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{U}},E|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})

\displaystyle:=[E-\E({\boldsymbol{U}})]+[\E({\boldsymbol{U}})-\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\langle D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),{\boldsymbol{U}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\rangle]

is non-negative and vanishes iff

and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}={\boldsymbol{U}}
.
Moreover, due to assumption (
15
), the relative dissipation
\mathcal{W}
is nonegative, since it is the subdifferential of the convex function in (
15
).

Furthermore, the condition
D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\in\Y
implies that
\mathcal{K}(D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}))
is well defined.
Finally, we observe from Hypothesis
3.1
that
D\E^{*}(D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}))\in\dom\Psi
.

Note that the Gateaux-derivatives of
\Psi
and
A
are also well defined due to Hypothesis
3.5
.

Corollary 3.7

(Weak-strong uniqueness)
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{U}},E)
be an
energy-variational solution
in the sense of Definition
3.2
with
E(s)=\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(s))
for one
s\in[0,\infty)
and let Hypothesis
3.5
be fulfilled. Let
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\in\C^{0}([0,T];\V)\cap L^{p}(0,T;\W)

be a strong solution,
i.e.
, a function fulfilling the equation (
13
) in
\widetilde{\mathbb{Z}}(0,T)
with
{\boldsymbol{U}}(s)=\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}(s)
and
s<T
, enjoying the additional regularity of (
20
), then it holds that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)=\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}(t)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }t\in[s,T]\,.

(21)

Remark 3.6
.

We note that the above corollary is stronger than the usual weak-strong uniqueness results, since the generalized solution coincides with the strong solution as soon as both coincide at one point in time. Usually these results are only formulated for
s=0
such that the solutions only coincide, if they coincide in the initial value. Moreover, inequality (
19
) gives a continuous dependence results for solutions on the initial value as long as the strong solution exists.

Remark 3.7

(Comparison to dissipative solutions)
.

Under Hypothesis
3.5
every
energy
-
variational

solution
is a dissipative solution in the sense of Lions
[
30
, Sec. 4.4]
. Indeed, from inequality (
19
), we infer by a version of Gronwall’s inequality and estimating
E\geq\E({\boldsymbol{U}})
and inserting
E_{0}=\E({\boldsymbol{U}}_{0})
as in the result of Theorem
3.3
that

\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{U}}(t),\E({\boldsymbol{U}}(t))|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}(t))\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\mathcal{W}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{U}}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\langle\partial_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}+A(\tau,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),D^{2}\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})({\boldsymbol{U}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\rangle\right]\text{{e}}^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}))\text{d}\tau}\text{d}s\\
\leq\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{U}}_{0},\E({\boldsymbol{U}}_{0})|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}(0))\text{{e}}^{\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}(D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}))\text{d}s}\,,

for all
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\in\C^{0}([0,T];\V)\cap L^{p}(0,T;\W)
fulfilling (
20
),
which is the usual dissipative formulation according to Lions.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We divide the proof into several steps.

Step 1: equidistant time discretization.

Let
N\in\N
such that
\mathcal{K}(0)<N
, we define
\tau:=1/N
, and we set
t^{n}:=\tau n
for
n\in\N

to obtain
an equidistant partition of
[0,\infty)
.
For convenience, we define
\Psi^{n}:\V\ra[0,\infty)
via
\Psi^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}):=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n}}\Psi(t,{\boldsymbol{U}})\text{d}t
,
A^{n}:\dom\Psi^{n}\ra\Y^{*}
via
A^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}):=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n}}A(t,{\boldsymbol{U}})\text{d}t
, and
C_{\Psi}^{n}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n}}C_{\Psi}(t)\text{d}t
.
We set
{\boldsymbol{U}}^{0}:={\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\in\dom\E
and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi):={}

\displaystyle\E({\boldsymbol{U}})-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})+\left(\E({\boldsymbol{U}})-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})-\tau C_{\Psi}^{n}\right)\tau\mathcal{K}(\Phi)

\displaystyle-\left\langle{\boldsymbol{U}}-{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1},\Phi\right\rangle+\tau\Psi^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}})-\tau\langle A^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}),\Phi\rangle\,.

The set
\mathbb{D}^{n}:=\{{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\V\mid\E({\boldsymbol{U}})+\tau\Psi^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}})\leq\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})\}
is convex and weakly compact in
\V
. Indeed, the convexity follows from condition (
15
) with
\Phi=0
and all
t\in[t^{n-1},t^{n})
and the fact that
\tau\mathcal{K}(0)<1
such that
\E({\boldsymbol{U}})+\tau\Psi^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}})=\tau(\Psi^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}})+\mathcal{K}(0)\E({\boldsymbol{U}}))+(1-\tau\mathcal{K}(0))\E({\boldsymbol{U}})
. Since
(1-\tau\mathcal{K}(0))>0
and
\E
is coercive, the set
\mathbb{D}^{n}
is even weakly-compact.
We define the iterate for
n\geq 1
for a given
{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1}
via

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n}=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}}

\displaystyle\sup_{\Phi\in\Y}\!\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi)\,.

(22)

In the next steps, we show that this definition actually makes sense.

Step 2: Min-max theorem.

In order to show that

\inf_{{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}}\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbb{Y}}\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi)=\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbb{Y}}\inf_{{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}}\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi)\,

(23)

we apply a min-max theorem.
Recall that
the set
\mathbb{D}^{n}
is convex and weakly compact in
\V
.
The function

{\boldsymbol{U}}\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi)
is convex and weakly lower semi-continuous for every
\Phi\in\Y
due to Hypothesis
3.1
.
Moreover, the function
\Phi\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi)
is concave for all
{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\V
with
\E({\boldsymbol{U}})\leq\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})+\tau C_{\Psi}^{n}

since
\mathcal{K}
is convex.
Therefore, (
23
) follows from Fan’s
min-max theorem
[
14
, Theorem 2]
.

Step 3: Discrete energy variational inequality.

We want to prove the inequality

\inf_{{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}}\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbb{Y}}\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi)\leq 0\,.

(24)

From
Step 2
, we infer the equality (
23
). In order to show (
24
), we consider
\Phi\in\Y
arbitrary and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}:=D\E^{*}(\Phi)\in\dom\E
. We define
\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}:=D\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi)
with
\alpha=1
if
\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\tau\Psi^{n}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})<\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})
and with
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that
\E(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\tau\Psi^{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})=\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})
, if
\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\tau\Psi(t^{n},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\geq\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})
. We can always find such a value
\alpha\in(0,1]
since the function

f:[0,1]\ra[0,\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\tau\Psi^{n}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})];\hskip 10.00002ptf(\alpha)=\E(D\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi))+\tau\Psi^{n}(D\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi))

is continuous with
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\tau\Psi^{n}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})\geq\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})
due to the intermediate value theorem. The continuity of
\Psi\circ D\E^{*}
follows from Hypothesis
3.1
and the continuity of
\alpha\mapsto\E\circ D\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi)
from convex analysis.
Indeed, via Fenchel’s equivalences, we may write

\E\circ D\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi)=\langle D\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi),\alpha\Phi\rangle-\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi)
and from the assumptions on
\E
, we infer the continuity
of
\E^{*}
with
\dom\E^{\ast}=\V^{*}

[
5
, Prop. 2.25 and Thm. 2.14]
.
The mapping
\partial\E^{*}
is single-valued and demi-continuous
[
41
, Thm. 5.20]
, which implies the continuity of
f
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\min}
is the strict minimizer of
\E
, it holds
{\boldsymbol{0}}\in\partial\E({\boldsymbol{U}}_{\min})
and thus by Fenchel’s equivalences
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\min}\in\partial\E^{*}({\boldsymbol{0}})
. Since the subdifferential is indeed single-valued, due to the superlinearity and strict convexity of
\E

[
5
, Prop. 2.47]
and
[
41
, Thm. 5.20]
, we infer
\{{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\min}\}=D\E^{*}({\boldsymbol{0}})
, which implies due to Hypothesis
3.1
that
f(0)=0
.

With this choice of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
, we observe that
\inf_{{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}}\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}},\Phi)\leq{}\mathcal{F}^{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\Phi)
for which, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\Phi)={}

\displaystyle[\E(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})]+\tau\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[\E(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})-{\tau}C_{\Psi}^{n}]+\tau\Psi^{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\langle\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}-{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1},\alpha\Phi\rangle+\tau\langle A^{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}),\alpha\Phi\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\tau\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[\E(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})-\tau C_{\Psi}^{n}]+\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left[\E(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})+\tau\Psi^{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\tau\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[\E(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})-\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1})-\tau C_{\Psi}^{n}]\leq-\tau^{2}\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[C_{\Psi}^{n}+\Psi^{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}})]\leq 0\,,

where we used (
14
) and the convexity of
\E
to infer the inequality and the choice of
\alpha
to infer the second equation.
The choice of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
and the lower bound on
\Psi
allow to deduce the two last inequalities.

Step 4: Well defined optimization problem.

Let us define
\mathcal{H}({\boldsymbol{U}})=\sup_{\Phi\in\Y}\mathcal{F}^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}|\Phi)
. As the supremum of convex lower semi-continuous functions,
\mathcal{H}
is a convex lower semi-continuous function on the convex and weakly compact set
\mathbb{D}^{n}
,
\mathcal{H}
is even strictly convex due to the strict convexity of
\E
.
From the previous two steps, we infer that
\mathcal{H}
is proper,
i.e.,
not equal to
+\infty
everywhere on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
.
Thus, the minimization problem
\min_{{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}}\mathcal{H}({\boldsymbol{U}})
has a unique minimizer and so definition (
22
) makes sense.

Step 5: Prolongations.

For functions
\phi\in\C^{\infty}_{c}([0,\infty))
and
\Phi\in\C^{1}([0,\infty);\mathbb{Y})
,
we define
\phi^{n}:=\phi(t^{n})
,
\Phi^{n}:=\Phi(t^{n})
, and
E^{n}:=\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n})
for
n\in\N
.
Inserting
\Phi=\Phi^{n-1}
in (
24
),
multiplying the resulting inequality by
\phi^{n-1}
and summing this up for
n\in\N
provides

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left[\phi^{n-1}(E^{n}-E^{n-1})-\phi^{n-1}\langle{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{U}}^{n-1},\Phi^{n-1}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle+\tau\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi^{n-1}\left[\Psi^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n})-\langle A^{n}({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n}),\Phi^{n-1}\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\Phi^{n-1})(\E({\boldsymbol{U}}^{n})-E^{n-1}-\tau C_{\Psi}^{n})\right]\leq 0\,.

Since there exists a
n_{0}\in\N
such that
\phi^{n}=0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
we may use a discrete integration-by-parts formula
and divide by
\tau>0
,
to obtain

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}

4
Overview of viscoelastic models

In this section we introduce a brief presentation of different incompressible viscoelastic models available in the literature. Our aim is to apply the abstract theoretical framework introduced in Section
3
to prove the existence of
energy-variational solutions
of viscoelastic models, without the need to introduce, as typically done in the literature, diffusive regularizations in the transport equations for the kinematic variables to obtain the existence of weak solutions. Throughout the discussion, we will indicate the model variants which constitutively satisfy the assumptions introduced in Section
3
, and thus which are fitted by the general theoretical framework, while we will highlight the limits which forbid its application to some other model variants.

The starting point for the derivation of viscoelastic models is the definition of a kinematic variable
\mathbb{G}:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, which is a mapping for infinitesimal deformations between the initial and the current configurations of a viscoelastic body. In Eulerian coordinates, given the velocity field
{\boldsymbol{v}}:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the kinematic variable
\mathbb{G}
satisfies an hyperbolic equation of type

D_{t}\mathbb{G}=h(\mathbb{G},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}),

(31)

where
D_{t}\mathbb{G}:=\partial_{t}\mathbb{G}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla\mathbb{G}
is the material derivative in the Eulerian coordinates and
h:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a function representing the time variation of the mapping associated to infinitesimal deformations, depending on both
\mathbb{G}
and
\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

The model equations can be derived from the principle of virtual powers, which gives the conservation equations for the linear and angular momenta expressed in terms of the kinematic
variable
\mathbb{G}
and of a stress tensor
\mathbb{T}
, which is the power-conjugated variable to
\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}
. The form of the stress tensor is constitutively assigned, in terms of the kinematic variable and of the velocity field, in order for the system to satisfy the Clausius–Duhem inequality. Specifically, considering an arbitrary part of the material
R(t)\subseteq\Omega
, moving with the material, the principle of virtual powers states that the sum of the virtual power of acceleration forces plus the virtual power of internal forces expended within
R(t)
is equal to the virtual power expended in
R(t)
by material external to
R(t)
or by external forces (see e.g.
[
17
,
19
]
). Moreover, as a consequence of frame-indifference, the internal power expended within
R(t)
for rigid virtual velocities must be equal to zero. Defining the set of virtual velocities
\mathcal{V}:=\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}={{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{d}\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{d}\times[0,\infty)\}
, where
\partial\Omega_{d}\subseteq\partial\Omega
, we may define the virtual power of acceleration and internal forces as

p_{\text{acc}}(R(t),\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}):=\int_{R(t)}D_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\text{d}x},\hskip 10.00002ptp_{\text{int}}(R(t),\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}):=\int_{R(t)}\mathbb{T}:\nabla\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\text{d}x},

(32)

and the virtual power of external forces as

p_{\text{ext}}(R(t),\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}):=\int_{R(t)}{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\text{d}x}+\int_{\partial R(t)}{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n}\cdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\text{d}S},

where
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is an external field, while
{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n}
is a traction on the boundary of
R(t)
. Then, the principle of virtual powers becomes

p_{\text{acc}}(R(t),\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}})+p_{\text{int}}(R(t),\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}})=p_{\text{ext}}(R(t),\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in\mathcal{V},\forall R(t)\subseteq\Omega,

which implies, together with the kinematical constraint (
31
), that the following system of coupled PDEs is satisfied

\displaystyle D_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}-\operatorname{div}\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{f}},\hskip 10.00002pt\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{v}}=0,

(33)

\displaystyle D_{t}\mathbb{G}

\displaystyle=h(\mathbb{G},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}),

(34)

endowed with the boundary conditions

{{\boldsymbol{v}}}={{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{d}\;\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{d}\times[0,\infty),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{T}{\boldsymbol{n}}={\boldsymbol{t}}_{n}\;\;\text{on}\;\;\partial\Omega_{n}\times[0,\infty),

(35)

with
\partial\Omega_{d}\cup\partial\Omega_{n}=\partial\Omega
,
|\partial\Omega_{d}\cap\partial\Omega_{n}|=0
. We observe that we are considering only Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions for ease of exposition, but also other boundary conditions, like Robin, could be allowed in the present theoretical framework.
Moreover, defining the set of rigid virtual velocities as
\mathcal{V}_{\text{rigid}}:=\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}:\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}={\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}+\mathbb{A}{\boldsymbol{x}}\}
, for any constant
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and constant
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{skw}^{d\times d}
, the fact that
p_{\text{int}}(R(t),\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}})=0
for any
\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{rigid}}
,
\forall R(t)\subseteq\Omega
, implies that
\mathbb{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
. Assuming the energy density of the system to be of the form

e(\mathbb{G},{\boldsymbol{v}})=\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\tilde{e}(\mathbb{G}),

(36)

i.e. given by the sum of the kinetic energy density and of the elastic free energy density
\tilde{e}
, the Clausius–Duhem inequality takes the form
[
19
]

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{R(t)}e(\mathbb{G},{\boldsymbol{v}})\leq p_{\text{acc}}(R(t),{{\boldsymbol{v}}})+p_{\text{int}}(R(t),{{\boldsymbol{v}}})+\int_{R(t)}p\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{v}},

(37)

where
p
is the Lagrange multiplier for the incompressibility constraint
\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{v}}=0
.
Applying the Reynolds transport theorem and using (
36
) and (
32
), the Clausius–Duhem inequality becomes

\displaystyle\int_{R(t)}{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot D_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\int_{R(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{e}}{\partial\mathbb{G}}:D_{t}\mathbb{G}+\int_{R(t)}e\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{v}}=

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R(t)}{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot D_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\int_{R(t)}\mathbb{T}:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}+\int_{R(t)}p\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{v}},

(38)

Specific forms for the stress tensor
\mathbb{T}
are then consitutively assigned in order for (
38
) to be satisfied.

Different choices for the kinematic variable
\mathbb{G}
, together with different constitutive assumptions for the form of the free energy density
\tilde{e}(\mathbb{G})
, lead to different viscoelastic models. In the following, we list different variants, associated to different choices of the kinematic variable, which have been considered in the literature.

•

Kelvin–Voigt viscoelasticity.
A standard approach is to consider as kinematic variable
\mathbb{G}
the deformation gradient
\mathbb{F}
(i.e. the Jacobian of the deformation map between the initial and the current configuration) (see e.g.
[
20
]
), which satisfies the transport equation

D_{t}\mathbb{F}=\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{F}.

(39)

Considering (
39
) in (
38

\int_{R(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{e}}{\partial\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{F}^{T}:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\leq\int_{R(t)}\mathbb{T}:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}+\int_{R(t)}(p-e)\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{v}},

hence a general constitutive assumption for the stress tensor which verifies inequality (
37

\mathbb{T}=2\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{sym}-p\mathbb{I}+\frac{\partial\tilde{e}}{\partial\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{F}^{T}=:\mathbb{T}_{visc}+\mathbb{T}_{el},

(40)

where we renamed
p\leftarrow p-e
, and
\mu>0
is the fluid viscosity of the material. Here,
\mathbb{T}_{visc}:=\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{sym}-p\MI
is the viscous contribution to the stress tensor and
\mathbb{T}_{el}=\frac{\partial\tilde{e}}{\partial\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{F}^{T}
is the elastic contribution.

Another common possibility is to consider as kinematic variable the left Cauchy–Green tensor
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{F}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
, which satisfies, as a consequence of (
39
), the transport equation

D_{t}\mathbb{B}=\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})^{T},

(41)

which is a form of the Oldroyd-B equation
[
38
]
.
Considering (
41

38
), analogously to (
40
), a general constitutive assumption for the stress tensor in terms of the variable
\mathbb{B}

which verifies inequality (
37
) is

\mathbb{T}_{el}=2\frac{\partial\tilde{e}}{\partial\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}.

(42)

We also cite the possibility to describe viscoelasticity through the kinematic variable given by the elastic stress tensor (
42
) (see e.g.
[
33
]
).

Remark 4.1
.

As reported in

(43)

for any

In

the following general form of the transport equation for

with

does not necessarily respond as the Jacobian of the deformation, but it may take into account incompatible components coming from the molecular theory of viscoelasticity. Frame indifference implies that there exists

(45)

As a consequence of (

Generalized viscoelasticity with stress relaxation.
We now introduce viscoelastic models which consider the phenomenon of stress relaxation, which is a process resulting from the collective microscopic dissipative phenomena in a viscoelastic material modeling living tissues. We refer the interested reader to

A first variant of (

\mu_{p}>0

\tilde{\Psi}:=\frac{1}{\mu_{p}}\int_{R(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{e}}{\partial\mathbb{B}}:\frac{\partial\tilde{e}}{\partial\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}.

We conclude this section by observing that a possible way to overcome the impossibility of inscribing the generalized Oldroyd-B model (

, i.e. considering the Neo–Hookean elastic free energy density

which is superlinear. The drawback of this approach is that (

is not symmetric, and hence it does not satisfy assumption

[

Unlike the kinematic variable

,
48
or

do not imply that their solutions have any symmetry. A possible way to proceed is to consider a transport equation for

of the type (

choosing the skew tensor

implying that the solution of the transport equation is symmetric if it is symmetric at the initial time. This approach has been employed in
[

]
. In the Appendix we will derive the general form for such a

is the square root of
\mathbb{B}
and contains information about the stretching in the system.
We observe that, since

depends linearly on

but non linearly on
\mathbb{F}
, it’s difficult to fit the theoretical framework to the model obtained with this approach, checking that assumption

An alternative and more practicable way to proceed in this direction is to consider the symmetrized

with

and in this case the viscoelastic model becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla p-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-\alpha\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbb{F}^{2}\right)={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\,,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\,,

(65a)

The subdifferential is single-valued on its domain and is given by

\displaystyle\partial\E:\V\ra 2^{\V^{*}}\hskip 20.00003pt\partial\E({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F})=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\mathbb{F}-\mathbb{F}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(86)

Its inverse gives the subdifferential of the convex conjugate according to Fenchels equivalences, which is single-valued on
\V^{*}=\V
and given by

\displaystyle\partial\E^{*}:\V^{*}\ra 2^{\V}\hskip 20.00003pt\partial\E^{*}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\\
{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\end{pmatrix}\,\hbox{when ${\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in L^{2}_{\text{sym},+}$ and $({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{0}})^{T}$ else.}

(87)

Here, we used the definition
{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2}}{4}+\MI}
,
which comes from the fact that
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

solves the matrix equation
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\mathbb{F}-\mathbb{F}^{-1}
and as a consequence of the positive definitesness of
\mathbb{F}
, it holds
0=\mathbb{F}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\mathbb{F}-\MI
.
The plus sign in front of the square root in the definition of
{\boldsymbol{F}}
reflects the fact that
\partial\E^{*}
should map into the domain of
\E

We note that the derivative

for all
({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\in\Y

{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\in\Hsig
and
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in L^{2}_{\text{sym},+}
implying that the second component of
\partial\E^{*}
belongs to
L^{2}_{\text{sym},+}(\Omega)
.

The operator
A:\dom\Psi\ra\Y^{*}
is given by all terms in the equations (
82
) despite the time derivative, such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle A({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F}),({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})^{T}\rangle=&\mu\langle\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle-\left({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}-\alpha\mathbb{F}^{2};\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right)-\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle\\
&-\left(\mathbb{F}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}\text{\,\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){,}}\,\,\,\,\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)-2\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{F};{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)-\alpha\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{F};{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)+\frac{1}{\mu_{p}}\left(\mathbb{F}-\mathbb{F}^{-1},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(88)

We need to verify that (

) is fulfilled for

and
\Psi
as given above,
i.e.,

\left\langle A({t,}D\E^{*}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}));\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\\
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle=\Psi(t,D\E^{*}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))

In order to verify this assumption, we insert
{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
and
\mathbb{F}={\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
in (

).
This calculation resembles the usual energy estimate.
Since,
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
is a solenoidal vector field, the convection term vanishes,
({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varphi}};\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})=0
. From the identity
{\boldsymbol{F}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=I

\alpha({\boldsymbol{F}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}});\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})-\alpha((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{sym}}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}});{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\alpha({\boldsymbol{F}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}});(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{sym}})=\alpha(\MI;\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})=0\,.

Furthermore, we observe by an integration by parts and expressing
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
in terms of
{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
that

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\text{\,\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){,}}\,\,\,\,\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{skw}}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}});{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})

\displaystyle=(({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}});\mathbb{F}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))^{-1})-((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{skw}}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}});\mathbb{F}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))^{-1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\cdot\nabla)\tr\left(\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-\ln({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))\right)-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{skw}}:({\boldsymbol{F}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-\MI)\text{d}x=0\,.

The first term vanishes since
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
is a solenoidal vector field and the second one since
{\boldsymbol{F}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})-\MI
is symmetric.
Using all these cancellations, we find for the remaing dissipative terms the following equality

\displaystyle\langle A({t,}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})),({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})^{T}\rangle={}

\displaystyle\mu\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\langle{\boldsymbol{f}}{(t)},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle+\frac{1}{\mu_{p}}\|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\Psi({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))\,

holding true for all
({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\in\Y
.
Due to (
87
), this calculation implies that assumption (
14
) is fulfilled and
also that the mapping
\Psi\circ D\E^{*}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\Psi({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},\mathbb{F}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))
given above is continuous on
\Y
.
We also observe from (

\E({\boldsymbol{v}}_{\min},\mathbb{F}_{\min})=\Psi({\boldsymbol{v}}_{\min},\mathbb{F}_{\min})=0
with
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\min}={\boldsymbol{0}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{\min}=\MI
.

The final condition, we need to verify is (
15

Therefore, we observe the estimates

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}-\alpha\mathbb{F}^{2};\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right)={}

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}-\alpha\mathbb{F}^{2};(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{sym}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\left(\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\alpha\|\mathbb{F}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\|(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{sym}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega;\R^{d\times d})}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\|(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{sym}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega;\R^{d\times d})}2\max\{1,\alpha\}\E({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F})

and

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{F}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}\text{\,\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){,}}\,\,\,\,\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}\lVert{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\|\mathbb{F}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\mathbb{F}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{C^{2}}{\mu}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}\E({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F})\,.

Furthermore, we obtain, from the fact that
2\|(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=2\|(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\Hsig
, that

\displaystyle-2\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{F};{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)

\displaystyle-\alpha\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{F};{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2\|(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\alpha\|(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\|\mathbb{F}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(2+\alpha)\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\mathbb{F}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{(2+\alpha)^{2}}{4\mu}\|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\E({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F})\,.

These three inequalities imply that the mapping

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F})\mapsto

\displaystyle\mu\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}-\alpha\mathbb{F}^{2};\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right)+\left(\mathbb{F}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}\text{\,\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){,}}\,\,\,\,\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)+\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{F};{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)+\alpha\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{F};{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(2\max\{1,\alpha\}\|(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})_{\text{sym}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega;\R^{d\times d})}+\frac{C^{2}}{\mu}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{(2+\alpha)^{2}}{4\mu}\|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\E({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F})

is nonegative. Since it is quadratic, it is also a convex mapping. The linearity of the mapping,

({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{F})\mapsto-\mu\langle\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle-\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}}-{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle-\frac{1}{\mu_{p}}(\mathbb{F},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})

assures its convexity and therewith, weakly-lower semi-continuity
[
21
]
.

For the dissipative term depending on the stress tensor
\mathbb{F}
, we find

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{F}-\mathbb{F}^{-1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\lVert\mathbb{F}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\mathbb{F}^{-1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-2d.

(89)

The first term is obviously quadratic and convex and the constant term, does not change this.
Finally, we consider the mapping
\mathcal{S}:\R^{d\times d}_{\text{sym},+}\ra\R
given via

\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{F}\mapsto\tr((\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2})+\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\Phi)-\tr((\Phi)_{-})^{2}\ln\det(\mathbb{F})\,.

The first derivative in direction
\mathbb{G}\in\R^{d\times d}_{\text{sym},+}
is given by

\langle D\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{F}),\mathbb{G}\rangle=-\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})-\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1})-\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\Phi)-\tr((\Phi)_{-})^{2}\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G})\,.

Calculating the second derivative, in the directions
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}\in\R^{d\times d}_{\text{sym},+}
, we find

\displaystyle\langle D^{2}\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{F})),(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H})\rangle

\displaystyle=\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\tr(\Phi(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}))

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\tr((\Phi)_{-})^{2}\tr(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}+\mathbb{H}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})

such that

\displaystyle\langle D^{2}\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{F})),(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})\rangle

\displaystyle=2\tr((\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}\mathbb{F}^{-1})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2\tr(\Phi(\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}))+2\tr((\Phi)_{-})^{2}\tr((\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2})

\displaystyle\geq 2\tr((\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}\mathbb{F}^{-1})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-2\tr((\Phi)_{-})\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2})+2\tr((\Phi)_{-})^{2}\tr((\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2})

\displaystyle\geq 2\tr((\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}+\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2}\mathbb{F}^{-1})>0\,

as long as
\mathbb{G}\neq\MO
.
The first inequality follows from

\displaystyle\tr((\Phi)_{+}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})=\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1/2}(\Phi)_{+}\mathbb{F}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{F}^{-1/2}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1/2})^{2})\geq 0\,.

(90)

since the positive semi definite matrix
\mathbb{F}^{-1/2}(\Phi)_{+}\mathbb{F}^{-1/2}
induces a positive quadratic form.
Note that
\mathbb{F}
is a positive definite matrix such that also their inverse and products remain positive definite.
In order to deduce the second inequality, we used the trace property

\tr(\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B})\leq\tr(\mathbb{A}^{2})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{2})
, which implies

\tr(\mathbb{A}\lambda\mathbb{B}^{2})=\tr(\mathbb{A}{\mathbb{B}}\lambda{\mathbb{B}})\leq\tr((\mathbb{A}{\mathbb{B}})^{2})+\tr((\lambda{\mathbb{B}})^{2})\leq\tr(\mathbb{A}^{2}\mathbb{B}^{2})+\lambda^{2}\tr(\mathbb{B}^{2})

for positive definite symmetric matrices
\mathbb{A}
, and
\mathbb{B}
, as well as
\lambda>0
.
The inequality

\displaystyle\tr((\Phi)_{-})\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2})\leq\tr(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2})+\tr((\Phi)_{-})^{2}\tr((\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1})^{2})

follows from by choosing
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{F}^{-1}
,
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{G}\mathbb{F}^{-1}
and
\lambda=\tr((\Phi)_{-})
.

Thus, we have shown that the mapping
\mathcal{S}
is convex, since its second derivative is positive definite.
Hence (
15
) is fulfilled and thus, Hypothesis
3.1
is fulfilled, which implies the first assertion concerning the existence.

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}(0,T)

\displaystyle:=L^{2}((0,T);(\Hsig\times L^{2}_{\text{sym}})(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}((0,T);(\Ha\times L^{2}_{\text{sym}})(\Omega))

\displaystyle:=L^{1}((0,T);(L^{2}\times L^{2}_{\text{sym}})(\Omega))\cap L^{2}((0,T);((\Hsig)^{*}\times L^{2}_{\text{sym}})(\Omega))\,.

7
Conclusion and Outlook

In the previous sections, we introduced and exemplified a new formulation and solvability concept for nonlinear evolution equations especially suited to treat viscoelastic fluid models. This formulation allows to pass to the limit in possible approximations only be means of compactness in weak topologies and thus, does not require to add stress diffusion in viscoelastic fluid models.
We are able to provide existence and weak-strong uniqueness under proper assumptions, which we show to be reasonable at the hand of two different examples in the Sections
5
and
6
.

In order to further support our claim that this new approach has the potential to provide an adaptable and transferable concept, we want to comment on the applicability to other viscoelastic fluid models and possible directions of future research in this last section.

Peterlin model.

Another viscoelastic fluid model that received a lot of attention in recent years with a quadratic (hence superlinear) elastic free energy density

\tilde{e}(\mathbb{B})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tr(\mathbb{B})^{2}-\tr(\MI)^{2}-2\tr(\ln(\mathbb{B}))\right)

(91)

is the so-called viscoelastic Peterlin model for polymeric fluids introduced in
[
40
]
(
cf.

[
9
]
). Collecting (
33
), (
52
), (
51
) and (
91
), and assuming, as in
[
9
]
, that the parameter
\mu_{p}^{-1}
is a linear function of
\tr\mathbb{B}
representing a generalized relaxation term as a material viscometric function, we obtain the following model:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla p-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-{\alpha}\nabla\cdot\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\mathbb{B}\right)={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\,,

(92a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}-2[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{B}]_{\text{sym}}-\alpha[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{B}]_{\text{sym}}+\tr\mathbb{B}\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\mathbb{B}-\MI\right)={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{0}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt(\mathbb{B})_{\text{skw}}=0\,.

(92b)

The dissipation term (
53
) coming from the relaxation processes is in this case

\displaystyle\Psi({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B}):={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+(\tr\mathbb{B})\mathbb{B}\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\MI-\mathbb{B}^{-1}\right):\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\MI-\mathbb{B}^{-1}\right)\text{d}x

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\tr(\mathbb{B})^{4}-2\tr(\mathbb{B})^{2}+\tr(\mathbb{B})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1})\text{d}x\,.

The last term in this dissipation potential is not convex as a mapping of
\mathbb{B}
.
Defining
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{B})=\tr(\mathbb{B})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1})
, we may calculate the second derivative as

\displaystyle\langle\partial^{2}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{B}),(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\tr(\mathbb{G})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{B}^{-1})-\tr(\mathbb{H})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{B}^{-1})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2\tr(\mathbb{B})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{B}^{-1})\,.

This form is not positive for
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{H}
and can not be made positive by adding a multiple of the energy. Indeed, we may estimate the two terms with the negative sign via

\displaystyle\langle\partial^{2}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{B}),(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\tr(\mathbb{G})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{B}^{-1})+2\tr(\mathbb{B})\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{G}\mathbb{B}^{-1})

\displaystyle\geq-2\tr(\mathbb{G})^{2}\frac{\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1})}{\tr(\mathbb{B})}\geq-2d\tr(\mathbb{G})^{2}\tr((\mathbb{B}^{-1})^{2})\,.

However, from the second derivative of the energy, we get the term
-\langle\partial^{2}_{\mathbb{B}}\ln\det(\mathbb{B}),(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})\rangle=\tr((\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{G})^{2})
.
In order to have any hope to find a multiple of the energy that when added to
\mathcal{S}
makes the sum convex, we would need to find a
\eta>0

\tr(\mathbb{G})^{2}\tr(\mathbb{B}^{-1})^{2}\leq\eta\tr((\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{G})^{2})
. But this does not seem to be possible. Mainly, the problem lies in the non-convexity of the dissipation potential.
Thus, the hypothesis of convexity of the mapping (
15
) cannot be shown to be satisfied, and hence we cannot apply Theorem
3.3
to infer the existence of
energy-variational solutions
to system (
92
).

Collecting (
33
), (
52
), (
54
) and (
91
), we obtain the following variant of the generalized Peterlin model:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla p-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-{\alpha}\nabla\cdot\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\mathbb{B}\right)={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\,,

(93a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{B}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{B}-2[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{skw}}\mathbb{B}]_{\text{sym}}-\alpha[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\text{sym}}\mathbb{B}]_{\text{sym}}+\frac{1}{\mu_{p}}\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\mathbb{B}^{2}-\mathbb{B}\right)={}

\displaystyle\MO\,,\hskip 10.00002pt(\mathbb{B})_{\text{skw}}=\MO\,.

(93b)

The dissipation term (
55
) coming from the relaxation processes is in this case

\Psi({\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbb{B}):=\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu_{p}}\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\mathbb{B}-\MI\right):\left((\tr\mathbb{B})\mathbb{B}-\MI\right)\text{d}x.

With the latter term in the system dissipation the hypothesis of convexity of the mapping (
15
) is satisfied.
We can also allow more general dissipation functionals as long as the convexity assumption (
15
) for the associated mapping is fulfilled.

Lin–Liu–Zhang model.

There are also other viscoelastic fluid models that fit into the proposed model framework. A very simple one arises, if we combine (
40
) with the energy
\tilde{e}(\mathbb{F})=\frac{1}{2}|\mathbb{F}|^{2}
, this leads to the system considered in
[
29
]
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla p-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbb{F}\mathbb{F}^{T}\right)={}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{F}+({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{F}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{F}={}

\displaystyle 0\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\cdot\mathbb{F}=0\,.

For this system all assumptions of abstract result in Theorem
3.3

are fulfilled with regularity weight
\mathcal{K}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=2\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+C(\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}^{2})
, where the test functions are chosen as in the two examples above.
In this context, the normalization condition
\nabla\cdot\mathbb{F}={\boldsymbol{0}}
could also be disregarded.
But, by the div-curl lemma it can be observed that the weakly-lower semi-continuity of the associated functional in the assumption (
15
) can already deduced for

\mathcal{K}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\varphi}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=2\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. Thus a future direction to further sharpen our result is to weaken the convexity assumptions of the associated form in (
15
) in order to also sharpen the associated result for this basic example.
Note that for the improved regularity weight
\mathcal{K}_{2}
, it can be shown that the existence of
energy-variational solutions
implies the existence of measure-valued solutions as proposed in
[
29
]
in the same way as it is proven for the Ericksen–Leslie system in
[
27
]
.

Moreover, it would be desirable to also include energies into our framework, that do not necessarily have superlinear growth, which could allow to further understand at least the Giesekus model (
58
) or variants of it. We note that the superlinearity of the energy is essential to infer that
\dom\E^{*}=\V^{*}
. In case that
\E
is only of linear growth, its convex conjugate
\E^{*}
may only be finite on a subset of
\V^{*}
. This especially hinders an argument as in
Step 3.
of the proof of Theorem
3.3
, where the continuity of the function
f(\alpha)=\E(D\E^{*}(\alpha\Phi))
is shown for all
\Phi\in\Y\subset\V^{*}
.

But still it may be possible to show the continuity of the function on a smaller (non-linear) set,
\Y\subset\dom\E^{*}
. This would lead to a set of test functions
\Y
that is not linear anymore. But this can still be a reasonable class of solutions fulfilling existence and weak-strong uniqueness. Indeed, in the proof of the weak-strong uniqueness result in Proposition
3.6
, we have to chose
\Phi=D\E(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}})
for the strong solution
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
. By the Fenchel equivalences (
8
)
it is clear that
D\E({\boldsymbol{y}})\subset\dom D\E^{*}\subset\dom\E^{*}
such that it would make sense to restrict the test space to
\Y\subset\dom\E^{*}
. We plan to investigate such models in the future.

Thus, we think that this new approach may provides some fruitfull new insight into the analysis of viscoelastic fluid models.
The proposed formulation includes a large class of models and incorporates desirable properties. Beside the existence and weak-strong uniqueness, we observed that the solutions form a semi flow (see Remark
3.3
). It can also be inferred that the set of solutions is weakly-star closed. Therefore, it could be desirable to select a special solution of the set of solutions as the physically relevant one as proposed in
[
25
]
for incompressible fluid dynamics. We note that this is already the idea of the time-incremental minimization algorithm proposed in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, where the energy is minimized in (
22
).

This appendix has two parts. Firstly, we give an explicit formula for the matrix
\mathbb{W}
in (

A: explicit formula for the matrix
\mathbb{W}

Let us consider the polar decomposition of the deformation gradient, i.e.

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{S}\mathbb{Q},

valid if
\det\mathbb{F}>0
, where
\mathbb{Q}
is a rotation tensor and
\mathbb{S}
is a symmetric and positive definite matrix. We start from the relation

D_{t}{\mathbb{F}}=a\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{F}+b(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})^{T}\mathbb{F},

(94)

which becomes

D_{t}{\mathbb{F}}=D_{t}({\mathbb{S}})\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{S}D_{t}{\mathbb{Q}}=a\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{Q}+b(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})^{T}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{Q}.

(95)

The latter relation involves
d^{2}
equations, the tensor
\mathbb{Q}
has
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
degrees of freedom and the tensor
\mathbb{S}
has
\frac{d(d+1)}{2}
degrees of freedom, so both
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{S}
can be determined by the previous relation. Multiplying (
95
) by
\mathbb{Q}^{T}
from the right, and introducing the angular velocity tensor
\mathbb{W}:=D_{t}({\mathbb{Q}})\mathbb{Q}^{T}
, we get that

D_{t}({\mathbb{F}})\mathbb{Q}^{T}=D_{t}{\mathbb{S}}+\mathbb{S}\mathbb{W}.

(96)

Also,

\mathbb{Q}D_{t}\left({\mathbb{F}}^{T}\right)\mathbb{Q}=D_{t}{\mathbb{S}}-\mathbb{W}\mathbb{S}.

(97)

Subtracting the latter two equations, we obtain that

\mathbb{S}\mathbb{W}+\mathbb{W}\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{H}:=a[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})\mathbb{S}-\mathbb{S}(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})^{T}]+b[(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})^{T}\mathbb{S}-\mathbb{S}(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})].

The latter relation is a well known tensorial equation of the kind

\mathbb{A}\mathbb{S}+\mathbb{S}\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{H},

with
\mathbb{S}\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{3\times 3}
,
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{H}\in\mathbb{R}_{skw}^{3\times 3}
(in three space dimension). A solution of the latter equation is of the form (see e.g.
[
43
]
)

\mathbb{A}=f(\mathbb{S})\mathbb{H}-g(\mathbb{S})(\mathbb{S}^{2}\mathbb{H}+\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}^{2}),

(98)

where
f(\mathbb{S}),g(\mathbb{S})
are defined in terms of the linear invariants of
\mathbb{S}
as

f(\mathbb{S}):=\frac{I_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}-II_{\mathbb{S}}}{I_{\mathbb{S}}II_{\mathbb{S}}-III_{\mathbb{S}}},\hskip 10.00002ptg(\mathbb{S}):=\frac{1}{I_{\mathbb{S}}II_{\mathbb{S}}-III_{\mathbb{S}}}.

Note that (
98
) is well defined if
\mathbb{S}
is positive definite. Hence, we have that

D_{t}{\mathbb{S}}+\mathbb{S}\mathbb{W}-a\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{S}-b(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})^{T}\mathbb{S}=\boldsymbol{0},

with

\mathbb{W}(\mathbb{S},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})=f(\mathbb{S})\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{S},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})-g(\mathbb{S})(\mathbb{S}^{2}\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{S},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})+\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{S},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})\mathbb{S}^{2}).

We note that the latter model is equivalent to the model for
\mathbb{S}
derived in
[
4
]
, where
\mathbb{S}
is interpreted as the symmetric square root of the conformation tensor
\mathbb{B}
. In the present case we have derived more coincise formulas for the expression of
\mathbb{W}
, with a clearer mechanical and geometrical interpretation, then the ones reported in
[
4
, Appendix A]
.

By the definition of the relative energy in Proposition
