Epstein Semantics: Characterization, Interpolation, Undefinability, and (In)Completeness
Krzysztof A. Krawczyk

Abstract




Abstract

This paper is a mathematical investigation on Epstein semantics.
One of the main tools of the present paper is the model-theoretic
\mathsf{S}
-set construction introduced in
[
15
]
. We use it to prove several results: 1) that each Epstein model has uncountably many equivalent Epstein models, 2) that the logic of generalised Epstein models is the
\mathsf{S}
-set invariant fragment of
\mathsf{CPL}
(analogon of the celebrated van Benthem characterization theorem for modal logic), 3) that several sets of Epstein models are undefinable, 4) that logics of undefinable sets of relations can be finitely axiomatised. We also use other techniques to prove 5) that there is uncountably many Epstein-incomplete logics and that 6) the logic of generalised Epstein models has the interpolation property.

Introduction

In a series of publications
[

6

, Richard Epstein has introduced a conceptually simple semantics for non-classical logics. His models are two-element tuples containing standard boolean valuation of propositional variables and a binary relation defined on the set of formulas. Epstein’s motivations were mainly philosophical: he wanted to formalise the content-relationship between propositions. However, he extended his research of the so-called relatedness and dependence logics to purely mathematical form. Also, he argued that his semantics can serve as adequate interpretations for other non-classical logics, and can be used as a tool for analysing other, different from the content-relationship, philosophical problems. This perspective has been known as the Epstein programme, see

. Epstein’s ideas inspired other researchers in the late seventies

[

These simple generalisations open a new perspective on the Epstein programme: it can be extended and carried out with greater impetus.

The current work, being a continuation of
[

What is interesting, undefinable sets of structures turn out to be finitely axiomatisable (we do not need to add special rules – unlike in the case of modal logics). \mathsf{S}
–set basic definitions

In this section, we recall the notion of an
\mathsf{S}
–set introduced in
[
15
]
and prove that each Epstein model has uncountably many equivalent Epstein models.

Given a model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
, we define the theory of
\mathfrak{M}
to be the set:
\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})=\{\varphi\in\mathsf{FOR}:\mathfrak{M}\vDash\varphi\}
. Now let us define the relation of a theoretical equivalence.

Definition 3.1

(Theoretical equivalence)
.

Given the models
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
,
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle
we say that
\mathfrak{M}
and
\mathfrak{N}
are theoretically equivalent, symbolically:
\mathfrak{M}\approx\mathfrak{N}
iff
\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})=\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{N})
. Obviously
\approx
is an equivalence relation, hence for arbitrary model by
|\mathfrak{M}|_{\approx}
we shall denote the equivalence class:
\{\mathfrak{N}\in\mathsf{M}:\mathfrak{M}\approx\mathfrak{N}\}
.

In
[
15
]
, we have already introduced the
\mathsf{S}
-set construction which enables us to generate the whole equivalence class of a given model with respect to relation
\approx
. Let us remind the construction. Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
. The Omega set of
\mathfrak{M}
is
\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}=\{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle:\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\varphi\to\psi\}
. Having specified the Omega set of
\mathfrak{M}
we define the
\mathsf{S}
-set of
\mathfrak{M}
to be
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}=\{\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle:v^{\prime}=v\,\,and\,\,\mathfrak{R}\setminus\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}\cup\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}\}
. Let us recall that the following holds:

Lemma 3.2

(Krawczyk, 2021)
.

For any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
and
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle
, we have:
\mathfrak{N}\in\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}
iff
\mathfrak{N}\approx\mathfrak{M}
. Hence
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}=|\mathfrak{M}|_{\approx}
.

Now, we shall prove that for any model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
, its
\mathsf{S}
-set
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}
is always uncountable:

Theorem 3.3
.

For any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
the cardinality of
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}
is
2^{\aleph_{0}}
.

Proof.

First note that for any
\mathfrak{M}\in\mathsf{M}
,
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}
is equinumerous with omega’s powerset:
\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}})
. The mapping
X\mapsto\langle v,(\mathfrak{R}\setminus\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}})\cup X\rangle
is a bijection from
\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}})
onto
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}
. It is easy to observe that
\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}
is countably infinite. Define
\neg^{0}\top:=\top
and
\neg^{n+1}\top:=\neg\neg^{n}\top
. For any natural number
n
,
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\neg^{2n}\top\to\bot
, so
\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}
is infinite. Our language is countable hence
\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}
is of cardinality
\aleph_{0}
and thus the cardinality of
\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}})
is
2^{\aleph_{0}}
.
∎

Theorem
3.3
yields an immediate corollary: that each model has uncountably many theoretically equivalent models.

The van Benthem characterization analogon

In the context of expressive power over Kripke frames, the van Benthem characterization theorem
[
20
,
1
]
– put in a popular manner – states that modal languages are the bisimulation invariant fragment of first-order logic. The notion of a standard translation plays a crucial role in proving this theorem. Standard translation maps modal formulas into first order formulas of the corresponding first order language in a natural way: it reflects the truth conditions of a given modal formula. Modal operators naturally correspond to quantifiers, so first order logic is the obvious target of the standard translation mapping. Note though, that in the case of generalised Epstein logic, such a mapping does not seem that obvious. There are no connectives which necessarily impose using quantifiers to express their truth conditions. Nonetheless, the use of atomic first order sentences comes quite naturally i.e.
St(\varphi\looparrowright\psi)=St(\varphi\to\psi)\wedge\mathcal{R}(\varphi,\psi)
, where
\mathcal{R}
is the binary relational symbol corresponding to
\mathfrak{R}
. However, an atomic first order sentence
\mathcal{R}(\varphi,\psi)
can be treated as an atomic expression of classical propositional logic. If we were to decode such a sentence by indexing it with a pair of related elements, it would have the following form:
p_{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle}
. Now we can define the corresponding classical propositional language. Let
\mathsf{At}
be the set
\Phi\cup\Phi^{\mathcal{R}}
, where
\Phi^{\mathcal{R}}=\{p_{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle}:\varphi,\psi\in\mathsf{FOR}\}
. One can easily see that
\Phi^{\mathcal{R}}
is countable, so our extended language remains countable. The set of
\mathsf{CPL}
formulas
\mathsf{FOR}^{\ast}
is the set generated from
\mathsf{At}
and connectives:
\neg,\wedge,\lor,\to,\leftrightarrow
.
Each Epstein model
\mathfrak{M}
can be seen as a model of
\mathsf{CPL}
defined on the set of formulas
\mathsf{FOR}^{\ast}
. We say that
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}p_{n}
iff
v(p_{n})=1
and
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}p_{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle}
iff
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
. Definition can be extended to arbitrary formulas in a standard way. For
A\in\mathsf{FOR}^{\ast}
, we will write
\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A
to indicate the fact that for any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
we have
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A
. Now we are ready to define the standard translation for the language of Epstein logic.
St:\mathsf{FOR}\longrightarrow\mathsf{FOR}^{\ast}
.

•

St(p_{n})=p_{n}
,

•

St(\neg\varphi)=\neg St(\varphi)
,

•

St(\varphi\ast\psi)=St(\varphi)\ast St(\psi)
, for
\ast\in\{\lor,\wedge,\to,\leftrightarrow\}
.

•

St(\varphi\looparrowright\psi)=St(\varphi\to\psi)\wedge p_{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle}

•

St(\varphi\vartriangle\psi)=St(\varphi\wedge\psi)\wedge p_{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle}
.

Obviously, the following holds for any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
:
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\varphi
iff
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}St(\varphi)
. Now we can ask the following question: when is a formula of
\mathsf{CPL}
equivalent to a standard translation of some Epstein formula? The answer is given by the characterization theorem. Let us first define the
\mathsf{S}
-set invariance for
\mathsf{CPL}
formulas.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a formula
A\in\mathsf{FOR}^{\ast}
is
\mathsf{S}
-set invariant iff for any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
,
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle
,
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A
and
\mathfrak{N}\in\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}
implies
\mathfrak{N}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A
.

Now we can move on to the characterization theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
A\in\mathsf{FOR}^{\ast}
. There is
\varphi\in\mathsf{FOR}
such that
\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A\leftrightarrow St(\varphi)
iff
A
is
\mathsf{S}
-set invariant.

Proof.

The left to right direction follows from the
\mathsf{S}
-set Lemma
6.6
and from the previously mentioned fact that for any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
:
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\varphi
iff
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}St(\varphi)
. For the right to left, assume that
A\in\mathsf{FOR}^{\ast}
is
\mathsf{S}
-set invariant. Let
RC(A)=\{B:B=St(\psi)\,\,for\,\,some\,\,\psi\in\mathsf{FOR}\,\,and\,\,A\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}B\}
. If
A
is inconsistent, then
A
is equivalent to
St(p_{0}\wedge\neg p_{0})
. Assume then, that
A
is consistent. This means that
RC(A)
is consistent. Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
be such that
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}RC(A)
. Let
T(\mathfrak{M})=\{B:B=St(\psi)\,\,for\,\,some\,\,\psi\in\mathsf{FOR}\,\,and\,\,\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}B\}
.
T(\mathfrak{M})\cup\{A\}
is consistent. If it was not, then
A\not\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}X
for some
X=\{C_{1},...,C_{n}\}\subseteq T(\mathfrak{M})
. This would mean that
\neg C_{1}\lor...\lor\neg C_{n}\in RC(A)
and also
\neg C_{1}\lor...\lor\neg C_{n}\in T(\mathfrak{M})
which leads to a contradiction. Let
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle
be such that
\mathfrak{N}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}T(\mathfrak{M})\cup\{A\}
. Since
\mathfrak{N}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}T(\mathfrak{M})
, we know that
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
, so
\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})\subseteq\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{N})
. Each theory of a model is maximally consistent, so we immediately can infer the opposite inclusion which gives
\mathfrak{N}\approx\mathfrak{M}
. This means that
\mathfrak{M}\in\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{N}}
. We assumed that
A
is
\mathsf{S}
-set invariant so
\mathfrak{M}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A
. This means that
RC(A)\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A
. By compactness of
\mathsf{CPL}
, we obtain
\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}B_{1}\wedge...\wedge B_{n}\leftrightarrow A
, where
\{B_{1},...,B_{n}\}\subseteq RC(A)
. Each
B_{i}=St(\psi_{i})
, so
\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}A\leftrightarrow St(\psi_{1}\wedge...\wedge\psi_{n})
.
∎

5
Epstein incompleteness

It was a significant breakthrough in the theory of modal logic when examples of Kripke-incomplete logics (the ones with no adequate class of Kripke frames) were found
[
19
,
8
,
21
]
. This discoveries culminated in a shocking Blok’s result
[
2
]
where uncountably many normal modal logics with no adequate classes of Kripke frames were proven to exist. We will prove an analogous theorem for Epstein relations. However, to make the reasoning clear, we shall proceed step by step. Since the phenomenon of Epstein-incompletess is terra incognita, we shall start with an example of continuum many logics which are Epstein complete. Then, we will proceed to give a single example of Epstein-incomplete logic (logic with no adequate set of Epstein relations). Then, we will combine the techniques used in previous two results to prove the main theorem of this section sating that there are uncountably many Epstein incomplete axiomatic extensions of
\mathcal{F}
.

Following the preliminary section, by a logic we understand a set of formulas containing
\mathcal{F}
, closed under uniform substitution and Modus Ponens. A logic
\lambda
is Epstein complete iff there is a set
X\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{FOR}^{2})
of Epstein relations such that
\lambda=\{\varphi:X\vDash\varphi\}
. Otherwise, a logic is said to be Epstein-incomplete.

Let us start with an Epstein-completeness result.

Theorem 5.1
.

There are
2^{\aleph_{0}}
Epstein complete extensions of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Let
p^{0}:=p
. Define
p^{n+1}:=p\to p^{n}
. Now let
K_{\omega}=\{p\looparrowright p^{n}:n\in\omega\}
. Hence elements of
K_{\omega}
are of the form
p\looparrowright(p\to p)(=p\looparrowright p^{1})
,
p\looparrowright(p\to(p\to p))(=p\looparrowright p^{2})
, and so on. Let
T\subseteq\omega
. Let
K_{T}=\{p\looparrowright p^{k}:k\in T\}
. Hence
K_{T}\subseteq K_{\omega}
. To prove the theorem, we will show that for each two non-empty subsets of positive natural numbers
T,V\subseteq\omega\setminus\{0\}
such that
T\neq V
we have
\mathcal{F}K_{T}\neq\mathcal{F}K_{V}
. Let
T,V
be such subsets. Without the loss of generality assume that
n\in T\setminus V
. Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
be such that
\mathfrak{R}=(\mathsf{FOR}\times\mathsf{FOR})\setminus\{\langle p,p^{n}\rangle\}
. Let
0<k\neq n
. It is obvious that
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(p^{k})
for any
\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}
, since
p^{k}
is a tautology. Also
\langle\sigma(p),\sigma(p^{k})\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
since otherwise we would get
n=k
, contradiction. Thus
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(p\looparrowright p^{k})
. Thus we have shown that
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\{\sigma(p\looparrowright p^{k}):k\neq n,\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
and thus obviously
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\{\sigma(\psi):\psi\in K_{V}\}
, since
\{\sigma(\psi):\psi\in K_{V}\}\subseteq\{\sigma(p\looparrowright p^{k}):k\neq n,\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
. But also
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash p\looparrowright p^{n}
, so
\{\sigma(\psi):\psi\in K_{V}\}\nvDash p\looparrowright p^{n}
which means
\mathcal{F}K_{V}\neq\mathcal{F}K_{T}
.

Now we need to show that each
\mathcal{F}K_{T}
is Epstein-complete. Let
\mathfrak{R}_{0}^{T}=\{\langle\sigma(p),\sigma(p^{k})\rangle:\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst},k\in T\}
. Define
\mathsf{R}^{T}:=\{\mathfrak{R}\subseteq\mathsf{FOR}^{2}:\mathfrak{R}_{0}^{T}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}\}
. We claim that
\mathcal{F}K_{T}=\{\varphi\in\mathsf{FOR}:\mathsf{R}^{T}\vDash\varphi\}
. The (
\subseteq
) inclusion is a matter of a routine check. For the opposite inclusion let
\Sigma^{+}
be maximally consistent set which includes
\mathcal{F}K_{T}
as a subset. It is easy to observe that a model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
of
\Sigma^{+}
defined in the same way as it is done at the end of section 2, is such that
\mathfrak{R}_{0}^{T}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}
, i.e.
\mathfrak{R}\in\mathsf{R}^{T}
. This proves the opposite inclusion. ∎

Thus, we already know that there is an abundance of logics which have adequate sets of Epstein relations.
Now we begin our exploration of incompleteness phenomenon. Let us start with a single example of Epstein-incomplete logic. Let
\alpha:=p\to(q\looparrowright p)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any Epstein relation
\mathfrak{R}
such that
\mathfrak{R}\vDash\{\sigma(\alpha):\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
we also have
\mathfrak{R}\vDash p\looparrowright p
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathfrak{R}\nvDash p\looparrowright p
. Hence it must be the case that
\langle p,p\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}
. Take substitution
\sigma
s.t.
\sigma(q)=p
and for the remaining propositional letters
\varphi\neq q
,
\sigma(\varphi)=\varphi
. Then
\sigma(\alpha)=p\to(p\looparrowright p)
. Take valuation
v
s.t. for any propositional letter
\varphi
,
v(\varphi)=1
. Then
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash p\to(p\looparrowright p)
, so
\mathfrak{R}\nvDash p\to(p\looparrowright p)
. Hence
\mathfrak{R}\nvDash\{\sigma(\alpha):\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

p\looparrowright p\notin\mathcal{F}\alpha
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that
\{\sigma(\alpha):\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}\nvDash p\looparrowright p
. Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
where
\mathfrak{R}=\mathsf{FOR}^{2}\setminus\{\langle p,p\rangle\}
and
v(\varphi)=0
for each propositional letter
\varphi
. Let
\psi\in\mathsf{FOR}
be any formula s.t.
\psi\neq p
. Assume
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\psi
. Hence also
\chi\to\psi
for any
\chi\in\mathsf{FOR}
. Also
\langle\chi,\psi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
, so
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\psi\to(\chi\looparrowright\psi)
. If, on the other hand,
\psi=p
, then
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash p
, so also
\mathfrak{M}\vDash p\to(p\looparrowright p)
. This gives us
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\{\sigma(\alpha):\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
. But obviously
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash p\looparrowright p
, so
\{\sigma(\alpha):\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}\nvDash p\looparrowright p
which gives the result.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

\mathcal{F}\alpha
is Epstein incomplete.

Proof.

Assume that there is a set of relations
\mathsf{R}
such that
\mathcal{F}\alpha=\{\varphi:\forall\mathfrak{R}\in\mathsf{R}\,\,\,\mathfrak{R}\vDash\varphi\}
. Then by Lemma
5.2

p\looparrowright p\in\mathcal{F}\alpha
. But by Lemma
5.3

p\looparrowright p\notin\mathcal{F}\alpha
. Contradiction, so
\mathcal{F}\alpha
is Epstein incomplete.
∎

Now we are ready to extend our previous techniques to show that there are exactly uncountably many extensions of
\mathcal{F}
which are Epstein incomplete.
First we construct a sequence of formulas.

\displaystyle p^{0}

\displaystyle:=q\looparrowright p

\displaystyle p^{n+1}

\displaystyle:=p\to p^{n}

We have
p^{1}=p\to(q\looparrowright p)
and
p_{2}
is
p\to(p\to(q\looparrowright p))
etc. Now we define a set of formulas
\Lambda_{\omega}:=\{q\looparrowright p^{n}:n\in\omega\}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
n\in\omega\setminus\{0\}
, any Epstein relation
\mathfrak{R}
, if
\mathfrak{R}\vDash\{\sigma(q\looparrowright p^{n}):\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
, then
\mathfrak{R}\vDash p\looparrowright p
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a substitution such that
\sigma(q)=p
and for the rest of propositional letters
\sigma(\varphi)=\varphi
. Hence
\sigma(q\looparrowright p^{n})
is of the form
p\looparrowright\underbrace{(p\to(p\to...}_{\text{n times}}(p\looparrowright p)...)
. Let
\mathfrak{R}\vDash\sigma(q\looparrowright p^{n})
. Obviously, it must be the case that
\langle p,\sigma(p^{n})\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
. Assume that
\langle p,p\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}
. Let
v(p)=1
. Obviously
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash p\looparrowright p
, hence
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash p\to(p\looparrowright p)
, and
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash p\to(p\to(p\looparrowright p))
, and so on. Thus
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash\sigma(p^{n})
for any
n\in\omega
. Hence also
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash p\looparrowright\sigma(p^{n})
for any
n\in\omega
which gives us contradiction. Hence
\langle p,p\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
and for this reason
\mathfrak{R}\vDash p\looparrowright p
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
S
be a non-empty subset of
\omega
. Then
\{\sigma(q\looparrowright p^{n}):n\in S,\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}\nvDash p\looparrowright p
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
be such that
\mathfrak{R}=\mathsf{FOR}\times\mathsf{FOR}\setminus\{\langle p,p\rangle\}
and
v(p)=0
. Let
\psi\in\mathsf{FOR}
and
\psi\neq p
. For any
\varphi\in\mathsf{FOR}
, we have
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\vDash\varphi\to(\psi\looparrowright\varphi)
. To see that assume
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\vDash\varphi
. Hence also
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\vDash\psi\to\varphi
and since
\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
, we get
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\vDash\psi\looparrowright\varphi
. Straightforward inductive step shows that we get for any
n\in\omega
,
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\vDash\varphi\to\varphi^{n}
and since
\langle\psi,\varphi^{n}\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
it is also the case that
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\vDash\psi\looparrowright\varphi^{n}
. Now let
\psi=p
. If
\varphi\neq p
, the reasoning from the previous case applies since then
\langle p,\varphi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
. Now let
\varphi=p
. Then for each formula of the form
p\to(p\to...(p\looparrowright p)...)
we have
\mathfrak{M}\vDash p\to(p\to...(p\looparrowright p)...)
since
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash p
. Observe that
p
is not a formula of the above form – see definition of
\varphi^{n}
. Thus we also have
\langle p,p\to(p\to...(p\looparrowright p)...)\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
which means
\mathfrak{M}\vDash p\looparrowright(p\to(p\to...(p\looparrowright p)...)
. Thus we get
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\{\sigma(p\looparrowright p^{n}):n\in\omega,\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
and since
S\subseteq\omega
also
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\{\sigma(p\looparrowright p^{n}):n\in S,\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
. But also
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash p\looparrowright p
since
\langle p,p\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}
which proves the lemma.
∎

For our final lemma, define the set
\Lambda_{T}:=\{q\looparrowright p^{m}:m\in T\}
, for each
T\subseteq\omega\setminus\{0\}
. Obviously, we have
\Lambda_{T}\subseteq\Lambda_{\omega}
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
P
and
T
be a non-empty subsets of
\omega\setminus\{0\}
such that
P\neq T
. Then
\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{P}\neq\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{T}
.

Proof.

Without the loss of generality, assume that there is
k\in P\setminus T
. Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
be such that
\mathfrak{R}=\mathsf{FOR}\times\mathsf{FOR}\setminus\{\langle q,p^{k}\rangle\}
and for any propositional letter
\varphi
,
v(\varphi)=0
. Observe that for any
n\neq k
, any
\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}
,
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(q\looparrowright p^{n})
. To see that, let
0\neq n\neq k
and
\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}
. We will consider two cases.

If
\sigma(q)=q
, then
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\sigma(q)
and so
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(q\to p^{n})
. It is obvious that
\sigma(p^{n})\neq(p^{k})
so
\langle\sigma(q),\sigma(p^{n})\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
and thus
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(q\looparrowright p^{n})
.

Let
\sigma(q)\neq q
. Then
\langle\sigma(q),\sigma(p)\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
and by a straightforward induction we get
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(p^{n})
. Also
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(q)\to\sigma(p^{n})
and
\langle\sigma(q),\sigma(p^{n})\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
, so
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(q)\looparrowright\sigma(p^{n})
.

We have shown that
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\sigma(q\looparrowright p^{n})
for each
1\leq n\neq k
and thus also
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\{\sigma(\varphi):\varphi\in\Lambda_{T},\sigma\in\mathsf{Subst}\}
. Obviously
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash q\looparrowright p^{k}
, which means that
q\looparrowright p^{k}\notin\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{T}
. We conclude that
\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{S}\neq\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{T}
.
∎

Now we can prove the theorem.

Theorem 5.8
.

There are
2^{\aleph_{0}}
Epstein incomplete extensions of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

For any non-empty
S\subseteq\omega\setminus\{0\}
, we have
p\looparrowright p\notin\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{S}
by Lemma
5.6
. Also for each Epstein relation
\mathfrak{R}
s.t.
\mathfrak{R}\vDash\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{S}
we have
\mathfrak{R}\vDash p\looparrowright p
by Lemma
5.5
, so
\mathcal{F}\Lambda_{S}
is Epstein incomplete for each
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq\omega\setminus\{0\}
. By Lemma
5.7
we have uncountably many such logics.
∎

6
The two conditions: undefinability and strong completeness

In order to show the power of our methods, we choose to analyze two exemplary conditions: symmetry and the one that may be called predecessor – negation elimination. Both of them, among some others, are imposed by Epstein on the models of his relatedness logics
[
6
]
.

(s)

: Symmetry: for any
\varphi,\psi
: if
\varphi\mathfrak{R}\psi
, then
\psi\mathfrak{R}\varphi
,

(n)

: for any
\varphi,\psi
: if
\neg\varphi\mathfrak{R}\psi
then
\varphi\mathfrak{R}\psi
.

Observe that both of these conditions are undefinable. We will extend the technique already signalised in
[
15
]
to prove their undefinability. In each case we will show that neither the set of Epstein relations fulfilling the respective condition is definable nor is the set of Epstein models meeting the condition.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be some set of Epstein relations,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathsf{FOR}
. We say that
\Gamma
defines
\mathsf{K}
when the following holds: for any Epstein relation
\mathfrak{R}
,
\mathfrak{R}\vDash\Gamma
iff
\mathfrak{R}\in\mathsf{K}
. We say that the set of relations
\mathsf{K}
is definable iff there is
\Gamma\subseteq\mathsf{FOR}
such that
\Gamma
defines
\mathsf{K}
. Analogously, we define the definability of a given set of Epstein models.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\mathsf{R}^{s}
be the set of all symmetric relations,
\mathsf{R}^{n}
the set of all relations fulfilling
n
condition. Both
\mathsf{R}^{s}
and
\mathsf{R}^{n}
are undefinable.

Proof.

For symmetry assume that
\mathsf{R}^{s}
is definable. Let
\Gamma
define
\mathsf{R}^{s}
. Let
\mathfrak{R}=\{\langle p,q\rangle,\langle q,p\rangle\}
.
\mathfrak{R}
is symmetric, so
\mathfrak{R}\vDash\Gamma
. This means that for any
v
, any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
we have
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\Gamma
. Let
v^{\prime}
be arbitrary and
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}\rangle
. Obviously
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\nvDash\top\to\bot
. Let
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{R}\cup\{\langle\top,\bot\rangle\}
. It is easy to see that
\langle\top,\bot\rangle\in\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}
(actually,
\langle\top,\bot\rangle
is in
\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}
for any
\mathfrak{M}
), so
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}\cup\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}
. Also
\mathfrak{R}\setminus\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
. Let
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime\prime}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle
. Valuation is the same, so
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}
, hence
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime\prime}\vDash\Gamma
. Note that
v^{\prime}
was arbitrary hence
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\vDash\Gamma
. But
\langle\bot,\top\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
, so
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
is not symmetric! Contradiction.

Assume that
\mathsf{R}^{n}
is definable. Let
\Delta
define
\mathsf{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathfrak{R}=\{\langle\neg p,q\rangle,\langle p,q\rangle\}
. Obviously
\mathfrak{R}\in\mathsf{R}^{n}
. This means that
\mathfrak{R}\vDash\Delta
. Let
v
be arbitrary valuation. We know that
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\vDash\Delta
and also that
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\neg\bot\to\bot
. This means that
\langle\neg\bot,\bot\rangle\in\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}
. Let
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle
where
v^{\prime}=v
and
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{R}\cup\{\langle\neg\bot,\bot\rangle\}
. It is easy to see that
\mathfrak{R}\setminus\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}\cup\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}
, so
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\Delta
. But
v=v^{\prime}
was arbitrary hence
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\vDash\Delta
. But
\langle\bot,\bot\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
so
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\notin\mathfrak{R}^{n}
. Contradiction. ∎

Observe that we can use similar argument to prove:

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\mathsf{M}^{s}
be the set of symmetric models,
\mathsf{M}^{n}
be the set of models with (n)-relations i.e.
\mathsf{M}^{s}=\{\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle:\mathfrak{R}\,\,\text{is symmetric}\}
,
\mathsf{M}^{n}=\{\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle:\neg\varphi\mathfrak{R}\psi\Longrightarrow\varphi\mathfrak{R}\psi\}
. Both
\mathsf{M}^{s}
and
\mathsf{M}^{n}
are not definable.

Proof.

Adding valuations will not affect the argument in any way, since the reasoning is based on
\top
and
\bot
.
∎

Further we have:

Proposition 6.4
.

\mathsf{M}^{s}\cap\mathsf{M}^{n}
and
\mathsf{R}^{s}\cap\mathsf{R}^{n}
are undefinable.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathsf{M}^{s}\cap\mathsf{M}^{n}
is definable. Let
\Gamma
define
\mathsf{M}^{s}\cap\mathsf{M}^{n}
. Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
, where
\mathfrak{R}=\emptyset
. Empty relation is both symmetric and meets the (n) condition, so
\mathfrak{M}\in\mathsf{M}^{s}\cap\mathsf{M}^{n}
. This means
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\Gamma
. Let
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle
where
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\langle\top,\bot\rangle
. Obviously
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}\cup\Omega^{\mathfrak{M}}
, so
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\Gamma
. But
\mathfrak{N}\notin\mathsf{M}^{s}\cap\mathsf{M}^{n}
, since
\mathfrak{N}
is not symmetric.

To show that
\mathsf{R}^{s}\cap\mathsf{R}^{n}
is undefinable we reason in the same manner, assuming that
\mathsf{R}^{s}\cap\mathsf{R}^{n}
is definable by some set of formulas
\Sigma
. We notice that
\mathfrak{R}=\emptyset\in\mathsf{R}^{s}\cap\mathsf{R}^{n}
. We use (
3.2
) to show that
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\langle\top,\bot\rangle
satisfies the defining set of formulas
\Sigma
which immediately leads us to contradiction.
∎

6.1
The
\mathsf{S}
set – syntactical version

In order to obtain
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{M}}
we have to know the structure of a model
\mathfrak{M}
. It can be the case, though, that we do not know the structure of a given model
\mathfrak{M}
, but we want to construct the set
|\mathfrak{M}|_{\approx}
knowing only its theory. This is precisely the case in standard completeness proofs, where a model is constructed from maximally consistent set of formulas. Now we shall introduce the way of obtaining
|\mathfrak{M}|_{\approx}
from the theory of
\mathfrak{M}
, which will prepare us for the later completeness proofs. Assume that we know the theory
\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
of some model
\mathfrak{M}
.

Definition 6.5
.

We define two relations in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{min}}

\displaystyle=\{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle:\varphi\looparrowright\psi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{max}}

\displaystyle=\{\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle:\varphi\looparrowright\psi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})\,\,or\,\,\varphi\to\psi\notin\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})\}.

We also define the valuation
v^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}=\Phi\longrightarrow\{0,1\}
win an obvious manner:
v^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}(\varphi)=1
iff
\varphi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
, for any
\varphi\in\Phi
. Here there is no surprise – valuations are uniquely determined by the theories of respective models (unlike relations).
Now we define the
\mathsf{S}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}
set, the set of models similar to
\mathfrak{M}
:

\mathsf{S}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}=\{\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle:v^{\prime}=v^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}\,\,\&\,\,\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{min}}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{max}}\}

Lemma 6.6
.

For any
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
the following equality holds:

\mathsf{S}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}=\{\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle:\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})=\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{N})\}

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
be arbitrary. To prove the left to right inclusion, let
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathsf{S}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}
. First, we will show that for any
\varphi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
we have
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\varphi
. Assume that
\varphi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. Let
\varphi\in\Phi
. By (
6.5
) we know that
v^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}=v^{\prime}
, hence
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\varphi
. Let
\varphi=\neg\psi
.
\neg\psi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
iff
\psi\notin\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. By inductive hypothesis
\mathfrak{N}\nvDash\psi
, which means
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\neg\psi
. Let
\varphi=\psi\lor\chi
.
\psi\lor\chi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
iff
\psi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
or
\chi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. By inductive hypothesis
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\psi
or
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\chi
, so
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\psi\lor\chi
. The proof for the rest of boolean cases goes in a similar manner. Assume that
\varphi=\psi\looparrowright\chi
.
\psi\looparrowright\chi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
, so
\psi\notin\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
or
\chi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
, which by inductive hypothesis gives us
\mathfrak{N}\nvDash\psi
or
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\chi
. By (
6.5
) we know that
\langle\psi,\chi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{min}}
and
\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{min}}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
, which means that
\langle\psi,\chi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
. So
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\psi\looparrowright\chi
. Let
\varphi=\psi\vartriangle\chi
.
\psi\vartriangle\chi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
means
\psi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
and
\chi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. By inductive hypothesis we obtain
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\psi
and
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\chi
. We also know that
\psi\looparrowright\chi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
, since
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\psi\vartriangle\chi
implies
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\psi\looparrowright\chi
. This means that
\langle\psi,\chi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{min}}
. We know that
\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{min}}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
, so
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\psi\vartriangle\chi
. This way, we have shown that
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
.

To show the opposite inclusion, assume that
\mathfrak{N}=\langle v^{\prime},\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\rangle\notin\mathsf{S}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}
. Hence at least one of the following must hold 1)
v^{\prime}\neq v^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}
or 2)
\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{min}}\nsubseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
or 3)
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\nsubseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})}_{\mathsf{max}}
. Let 1) hold. This means that for some
\varphi\in\Phi
we have
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\varphi
and
\varphi\notin\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
or
\mathfrak{N}\nvDash\varphi
and
\varphi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. In both cases we get
\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{N})\neq\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. Suppose 2) is the case. This means that there is
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{FOR}\times\mathsf{FOR}
such that
\varphi\looparrowright\psi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
and
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
. This means that
\mathfrak{N}\nvDash\varphi\looparrowright\psi
, so
\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{N})\neq\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. Finally assume that 3) holds. This means that there is
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
such that
\varphi\looparrowright\psi\notin\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
and
\varphi\to\psi\in\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})
. Now we have two possibilities: either
\mathfrak{N}\nvDash\varphi\to\psi
or
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\varphi\to\psi
. If the first disjunct is true, then
\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{M})\neq\mathsf{Th}(\mathfrak{N})
follows immediately. Thus, assume the second disjunct holds. This way, we obtain
\mathfrak{N}\vDash\varphi\looparrowright\psi
. But

Before we move on, we need to recall some results from

. He studies the language with basic boolean connectives

[

Thus, we claim that weaker Epstein-type languages are more interesting: precisely the interesting ones are those which are not capable of expressing the relation – just like the one we are currently investigating.

To make things precise, we show that

We close our study of Epstein semantics with the proof of the interpolation theorem for
\mathcal{F}

Theorem 7.1

.

If
\varphi\to\psi\in\mathcal{F}
then there is
\chi
such that
\mathsf{Var}(\chi)\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\varphi)\cap\mathsf{Var}(\psi)
and
\varphi\to\chi\in\mathcal{F}
,
\chi\to\psi\in\mathcal{F}
.

We shall refer to formulas like
\chi
in the above formulated interpolation theorem as interpolants (of
\varphi
and
\psi
). Before moving directly to the proof, we shall first introduce some useful definitions.

Definition 7.2

(Realisable pairs)
.

Let
\Gamma,\Sigma\subseteq\mathsf{FOR}
. The pair
\langle\Gamma,\Sigma\rangle
will be called realisable iff there is
\mathfrak{M}\in\mathsf{M}
such that
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\varphi
for any
\varphi\in\Gamma
and
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\psi
for any
\psi\in\Sigma
.

It is easy to observe that by
7.2
,
\varphi\to\psi\notin\mathcal{F}
is equivalent to
\langle\{\varphi\},\{\psi\}\rangle
being realisable. We shall also introduce the notion of a separable pair.

Definition 7.3

(Separable pairs)
.

We say that a pair
\langle\Gamma,\Sigma\rangle
is separable iff there is
\chi
such that
\mathsf{Var}(\chi)\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma)\cap\mathsf{Var}(\Sigma)
and both
\langle\Gamma,\{\chi\}\rangle
and
\langle\{\chi\},\Sigma\rangle
are not realisable. In such cases, we say that
\{\chi\}
separates
\langle\Gamma,\Sigma\rangle
.

Now we can start the proof of theorem
7.1
.

Proof.

Assume that
\varphi\to\psi
does not have an interpolant. We will prove that the pair

t_{0}
is not separable. We shall construct a finite sequence of pairs starting from
t_{0}
. First let
\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{j}
and
\psi_{1},...,\psi_{k}
be an enumeration of all
\varphi
’s proper subformulas and all
\psi
’s proper subformulas respectively. Let

. We shall construct the first part of our sequence in the following way:

\displaystyle t_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\varphi_{i+1}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle,&\text{if }\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\varphi_{i+1}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle\text{ is not separable};\\
\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\neg\varphi_{i+1}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle,&\text{if }\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\varphi_{i+1}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle\text{ is separable}\end{cases}

First observe that each
t_{i}
,
i\leq j
is not separable. We already mentioned that
t_{0}
is not separable (otherwise it would have an interpolant). Assume that
t_{i+1}
is separable. Then by the construction, both
\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\varphi_{i+1}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle
and
\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\neg\varphi_{i+1}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle
are separable. This means that there are
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
, such that
\mathsf{Var}(\chi_{1})\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma\cup\{\varphi_{i+1}\})\cap\mathsf{Var}(\Sigma_{0})
,
\mathsf{Var}(\chi_{2})\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma\cup\{\neg\varphi_{i+1}\})\cap\mathsf{Var}(\Sigma_{0})
and all the pairs:
\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\varphi_{i+1}\},\{\chi_{1}\}\rangle
,
\langle\{\chi_{1}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle
,
\langle\Gamma_{i}\cup\{\neg\varphi_{i+1}\},\{\chi_{2}\}\rangle
and
\langle\{\chi_{2}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle
are not realisable. Note that for any
i\leq j
,
\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma_{i})=\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma_{0})
, since at each step of the construction we add only
\varphi
’s subformulas. For this reason,
\mathsf{Var}(\chi_{1}\lor\chi_{2})\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma_{i})\cap\mathsf{Var}(\Sigma_{0})
. Both
\langle\Gamma_{i},\{\chi_{1}\lor\chi_{2}\}\rangle
and
\langle\{\chi_{1}\lor\chi_{2}\},\Sigma_{0}\rangle
are not realisable, because otherwise we obtain contradiction with the assumption that
t_{i+1}
is separable. Hence
t_{i}
is separable, which proves the first observation by contraposition.

Now we move on to the second part of our sequence where
m<k
:

\displaystyle t_{j+m+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\psi_{m}\}\rangle,&\text{if }\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\psi_{m}\}\rangle\text{ is not separable};\\
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\neg\psi_{m}\}\rangle,&\text{if }\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\psi_{m}\}\rangle\text{ is separable}\end{cases}

Analogously, we will prove that for any
m\leq k
,
t_{j+m}
is not separable. Let
t_{j+m+1}
be separable. Hence there are
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
, such that
\mathsf{Var}(\chi_{1})\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma_{j})\cap\mathsf{Var}(\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\psi_{m+1}\})
,
\mathsf{Var}(\chi_{2})\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\Gamma_{j}\})\cap\mathsf{Var}(\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\neg\psi_{m+1}\})
and all the pairs:
\langle\Gamma_{j},\{\chi_{1}\}\rangle
,
\langle\{\chi_{1}\},\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\psi_{m+1}\}\rangle
,
\langle\Gamma_{j},\{\chi_{2}\}\rangle
and
\langle\{\chi_{2}\},\Sigma_{m}\cup\{\neg\psi_{m+1}\}\rangle
are not realisable. In this case
\{\chi_{1}\wedge\chi_{2}\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{m}\rangle
which gives us the desired result.

Now, we are going to prove that
t_{j+k}=\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
is realisable. We will define the model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle
in the following way:

\displaystyle v(p)=1\text{ iff }

\displaystyle p\in\Gamma_{j}\text{ or }\neg p\in\Sigma_{k}

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}\text{ iff }

at least one of the followin is true:

\displaystyle\text{either }\varphi\looparrowright\psi\in\Gamma_{j},

(a)

\displaystyle\text{or }\varphi\vartriangle\psi\in\Gamma_{j},

(b)

\displaystyle\text{or }\neg(\varphi\looparrowright\psi)\in\Sigma_{k},

(c)

\displaystyle\text{or }\neg(\varphi\vartriangle\psi)\in\Sigma_{k},

(d)

We will prove that:

1.

for any
\chi\in\Gamma_{j}
,
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\chi
iff
\chi\in\Gamma_{j}
,

2.

for any
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
,
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\sigma
iff
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}

We shall start with 1.
Assume that
\chi=p\in\mathsf{Var}
. If
p\in\Gamma_{j}
, then by definition
\mathfrak{M}\vDash p
. Let
\mathfrak{M}\vDash p
.
p\in\Gamma_{j}
gives us the result immediately, so assume
\neg p\in\Sigma_{k}
. By our construction either
p\in\Gamma_{j}
or
\neg p\in\Gamma_{j}
. If
\neg p\in\Gamma_{j}
, then
\{\neg p\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
which gives us contradiction, hence
p\in\Gamma_{j}
. Let
\chi=\gamma\wedge\delta
. Assume that
\gamma\wedge\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. Hence
\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
or
\neg\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
and similarly
\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
or
\neg\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. If
\neg\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
, then it is easy to see that
\{\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, which leads to contradiction. The case when
\neg\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
is analogous. Hence both
\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
and
\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
, so by inductive hypothesis
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma
and
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\delta
, which means
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma\wedge\delta
.
Assume now that
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma\wedge\delta
. Obviously
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma
and
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\delta
, so by inductive hypothesis
\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
,
\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. If
\neg(\gamma\wedge\delta)\in\Gamma_{j}
, then it is easy to see that
\{\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, so
\gamma\wedge\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
.
The rest of the boolean cases are treated similarly, so we move on to Epstein connectives. Let
\chi=\gamma\looparrowright\delta
. If
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma\looparrowright\delta
, then
\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
which means that one of the four cases from the definition is true.
The first case
a
immediately leads to the desired result. Assume
b
. Assume for reductio that
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\notin\Gamma_{j}
, which means
\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)\in\Gamma_{j}
. Then
\{\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, because there is no
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{M}
, such that
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)
. Contradiction, so it must be the case that
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. Similarly, assume
c
and
\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)\in\Gamma_{j}
. then it can easily be seen that
\{\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, so
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. Assume
d
holds and
\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)\in\Gamma_{j}
. Then it can be easily observed that
\{\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)\}
, as well as
\{\neg(\gamma\vartriangle\delta)\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, so
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
.

For the other direction assume
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. Hence we already know that
\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle\in\Gamma_{j}
. If both
\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
and
\delta\notin\Gamma_{j}
, then it is easy to see that
\{\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
. For this reason it is the case that
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{j}
or
\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. By inductive hypothesis, we get
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma
and
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\delta
. This immediately leads to
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma\looparrowright\delta
.

Assume now that
\chi=\gamma\vartriangle\delta
. If
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
, then
\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
, so again we have four cases to consider. We can say already that
b
gives us the result immediately. Also we have
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma
and
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\delta
. By hypothesis:
\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
and
\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. Assume
a
. If
\gamma\vartriangle\delta\notin\Gamma_{j}
, then we obtain the contradiction, since
\{\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
. Now assume
c
or
d
. If
\gamma\vartriangle\delta\notin\Gamma_{j}
, then in both cases
\{\neg(\gamma\vartriangle\delta)\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, so we obtain
\gamma\vartriangle\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. For the other direction assume
\gamma\vartriangle\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
. Hence
\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
. It is also easy to observe that it must be the case that
\gamma\in\Gamma_{j}
and
\delta\in\Gamma_{j}
(otherwise we get the separation by
\{\bot\}
), so by inductive hypothesis we get
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
.

For 2. assume that
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash p
. Hence by definition of
\mathfrak{M}
, we have
\neg p\notin\Sigma_{k}
, which means
p\in\Sigma_{k}
. Assume
p\in\Sigma_{k}
. If
p\in\Gamma_{j}
, then
\{p\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, so
p\notin\Gamma_{j}
. Also
\neg p\notin\Sigma_{k}
, so
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash p
. We shall skip the boolean cases and move to Epstein connectives right away. Let
\chi=\gamma\looparrowright\delta
. Assume that
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma\looparrowright\delta
. Hence we have two possibilities: either i)
\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}
or ii)
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma
and
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\delta
. By i) we get that neither of the conditions
a
–
d
holds, so
c
gives us
\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)\notin\Sigma_{k}
which means
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
. Assume ii) holds. Hence by inductive hypothesis
\neg\gamma\in\Sigma_{k}
and
\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
. If
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\notin\Sigma_{k}
, then
\neg(\gamma\looparrowright\delta)\in\Sigma_{k}
. But in such case it is easy to see that
\{\neg\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, because there is no
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
such that
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\gamma\looparrowright\delta
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\gamma
,
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\nvDash\delta
. For this reason we obtain
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
.

For the other direction assume
\gamma\looparrowright\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
. Assume now that
a
holds. Then obviously
\{\gamma\looparrowright\delta\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
. So
a
does not hold. For similar reasons, it must be the case that
b
does not hold. It is obvious that
c
cannot be true. If
d
holds, then it also must be the case that
\{\neg\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, since there is no
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{M}
such that
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\nvDash\gamma\looparrowright\delta
. Hence
d
is not true, so
\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}
which means
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma\looparrowright\delta
.

Let
\chi=\gamma\vartriangle\delta
. Assume
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
. Hence either i)
\langle\gamma,\delta\rangle\notin\mathfrak{R}
, or ii)
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma
or
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\delta
. Again, i) gives us the result immediately, so assume ii). By inductive hypothesis we get
\gamma\in\Sigma_{k}
or
\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
. Assume for reductio that
\neg(\gamma\vartriangle\delta)\in\Sigma_{k}
. There is no model
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
such that
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
and (
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma
or
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\delta
), so
\{\neg\bot\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
– contradiction. Hence
\gamma\vartriangle\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
.
For the other direction assume
\gamma\vartriangle\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
. Obviously
d
does not hold. If
c
holds, then it can be easily checked that it must be the case that
\gamma\in\Sigma_{k}
or
\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
(otherwise we get separation by
\{\neg\bot\}
). Hence
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma
or
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\delta
which by inductive hypothesis leads to
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
. When
b
holds, then
\{\gamma\vartriangle\delta\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
, so
b
cannot be true. Assume
a
. Assume further that
\neg\gamma\in\Sigma_{k}
and
\neg\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
. But then
\{\gamma\looparrowright\delta\}
separates
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle
(to see that observe that for no
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
:
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\gamma\looparrowright\delta
,
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\gamma,\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\vDash\delta
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\nvDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
). It must be the case then that
\gamma\in\Sigma_{k}
or
\delta\in\Sigma_{k}
which by inductive hypothesis means either
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma
or
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\delta
, so
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\gamma\vartriangle\delta
.

This way, we have proven that
t_{j+m+1}
is realisable which means that
\mathfrak{M}\vDash\varphi
and
\mathfrak{M}\nvDash\psi
. This ends the proof.
∎

We finish with two additional remarks.

Remark 7.4
.

Observe that the relation in model
\mathfrak{M}
from the proof of theorem
7.1
could be defined in a following way:
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\in\mathfrak{R}
iff
\neg(\varphi\looparrowright\psi)\notin\Gamma_{j}
or
\neg(\varphi\vartriangle\psi)\notin\Sigma_{k}
or
\varphi\looparrowright\psi\notin\Gamma_{j}
or
\varphi\vartriangle\psi\notin\Sigma_{k}
. Note that, although the change seem to be purely cosmetic, thus defined relation can be radically different from the one defined in the previous part! To see that, let
\langle\Gamma_{j},\Sigma_{k}\rangle=\langle\{p\},\{q\}\rangle
. According to the first definition
\mathfrak{R}=\emptyset
, whilst the current one gives us
\mathfrak{R}=\mathsf{FOR}\times\mathsf{FOR}
. Actually, the same could be done with the definition of
v
.

One may also wonder if the analogous result of interpolation theorem could be obtained for Epstein implication.

Remark 7.5
.

The interpolation theorem holds trivially for Epstein implication, that is
\varphi\looparrowright\psi\in\mathcal{F}
implies existence of
\chi
, such that
\mathsf{Var}(\chi)\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\varphi)\cap\mathsf{Var}(\psi)
,
\varphi\looparrowright\chi\in\mathcal{F}
and
\chi\looparrowright\psi\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{R}=\emptyset
and
v
be arbitrary. Observe that for any
\varphi,\psi\in\mathsf{FOR}
, we have
\langle v,\mathfrak{R}\rangle\nvDash\varphi\looparrowright\psi
. For this reason
\varphi\looparrowright\psi\notin\mathcal{F}
.
∎

8
Conclusion

In the paper, we have used model-theoretic (mainly the
\mathsf{S}
–set) techniques to obtain some results on generalised Epstein semantics. We hope that our work is a step towards building a serious theory of Epstein semantics. We showed that each Epstein model has uncountably many equivalent models. We have also proven that the logic of Epstein structures is the
\mathsf{S}
-set invariant fragment of
\mathsf{CPL}
. Additionally, we isolated continuum many extensions of
\mathcal{F}
which are Epstein-incomplete.
We have presented a strategy for proving completeness with respect to undefinable sets of Epstein relations. What is important, is that we did not have to incorporate any extra rules to the system
\mathcal{F}
to achieve the result – we have simply enriched
\mathcal{F}
with extra axioms. What also seems worth mentioning, is that providing the axiom system for the intersection of
\mathsf{R}^{s}
and
\mathsf{R}^{n}
, was not just a matter of combining the system
\mathcal{F}S
and
\mathcal{F}N
. To obtain completeness, we had to include additional axioms. Both undefinability and completeness were proven by means of the
\mathsf{S}
-set construction.
