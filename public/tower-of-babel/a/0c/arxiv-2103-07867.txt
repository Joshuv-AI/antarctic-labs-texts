The top-order energy of quasilinear wave equations in two space dimensions is uniformly bounded
Shijie Dong, Philippe G. LeFloch, Zhen Lei

Abstract
Alinhac solved a long-standing open problem in 2001 and established that quasilinear wave equations in two space dimensions with quadratic null nonlinearities admit global-in-time solutions, provided that the initial data are compactly supported and sufficiently small in Sobolev norm. In this work, Alinhac obtained an upper bound with polynomial growth in time for the top-order energy of the solutions. A natural question then arises whether the time-growth is a true phenomena, despite the possible conservation of basic energy. Analogous problems are also of central importance for Schrödinger equations and the incompressible Euler equations in two space dimensions, as studied by Bourgain, Colliander-Keel-Staffilani-Takaoka-Tao, Kiselev-Sverak, and others. In the present paper, we establish that the top-order energy of the solutions in Alinhac theorem remains globally bounded in time, which is opposite to Alinhac’s blowup-at-infinity conjecture.

Introduction

Background.

We consider quasilinear wave equations in two space dimensions with quadratic null nonlinearities.
In 2001 Alinhac established a global-in-time existence theory
[
2
]
when the initial data are
compactly supported and sufficiently small in Sobolev norm, which was a long-standing open problem then.
In this work, Alinhac established an upper bound with polynomial growth (in time) for the top-order energy of the solutions.
A natural question is that whether the time-growth of the top-order energy is a true phenomena or not. In fact, Alinhac conjectured
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
that this should be a true phenomena and called it “blowup-at-infinity”, in the context of three dimensional, quasilinear wave equations enjoying a weak null structure.
Interestingly, Alinhac conjecture was recently established by Deng and Pusateri
[
14
]
for a class of three-dimensional nonlinear wave equations satisfying the weak null condition, and the authors did prove
the high-order energy of the solutions cannot be uniformly bounded in time.
On the other hand, the same question for two-dimensional quasilinear wave equations with null nonlinearities remained
open until now. Yet, there has been substantial progress concerning this question in recent years.

In
[
11
]
, Cai, Lei, and Masmoudi showed the uniform boundedness of the top-order energy for a class
of two-dimensional quasilinear wave equations with (hidden) strong null structure in the sense of Lei
[
31
]
.
More recently, Cai
[
10
]
considered the system of two-dimensional incompressible elastodynamics, and again proved
the uniform boundedness of the top-order energy.
There are also some related results for three-dimensional wave equations.
For instance, Wang
[
37
]
proved that quasilinear wave equations with quadratic null nonlinearities in
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
admit compactly supported small global-in-time solutions with uniformly bounded energy.
Lei and Wang in
[
33
]
showed the uniform boundedness of the top-order energy for the three dimensional incompressible isotropic elastodynamics. Introducing the “hyperboloidal foliation method”, LeFloch and Ma
[
29
]
treated nonlinear systems coupling wave equations and Klein-Gordon equations and prove a uniform energy bound,
while LeFloch and Wei
[
30
]
treated the problem of evolving relativistic membranes.

Other models.

In addition to wave equations, the study of the time-growth behavior of the (high-order) energy is also of central importance in the theory of the Schrödinger equations, two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations, as well as Hamiltonian systems. The time-growth of the top-order energy reflects the
cascade of energy
to its high frequency part.
In
[
7
]
(and
[
8
]
, respectively), Bourgain constructed a wave equation (Schrödinger equation, resp.) whose solutions have unbounded high-order energy, and later in
[
9
]
raised a conjecture concerning
the time-growth of high-order Sobolev norms for Schrödinger equations.
Later on, Colliander, Keel, Staffilani, Takaoka, and Tao
[
13
]
proved that the
H^{s}
–energy (with
s>1
) of
the cubic defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation posed on the two dimensional torus
has the property of generating high-frequency modes within a certain bounded time interval. This result
can be regarded as a partial answer to Bourgain conjecture in
[
9
]
.
Recently, in the study of the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations posed on a disk, Kislev and Sverak
[
24
]
constructed initial data such that the vorticity gradient grows as a double exponential in time.
(See also Lei and Shi
[
32
]
for a result on the torus and an exponential growth.)
Various other interesting results related to this topic can be found in the aforementioned references as well as the references cited therein.

Main result.

In the present paper, we establish that the top-order energy of the solutions to (
1.1
)
remains
globally bounded in time.
This is achieved by applying the hyperboloidal foliation method
and taking advantage of the null structure in order to uncover extra decay in the hyperbolic time
s=\sqrt{t^{2}-|x|^{2}}
. We emphasize that such a phenomena substantially differs from that of Alinhac’s blowup-at-infinity conjecture
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
14
]
.

We are interested in the quasilinear wave equation

\displaystyle-\Box w+P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{\gamma}w{\partial}_{\alpha}{\partial}_{\beta}w=0,

(1.1)

when compactly supported initial data are prescribed on a constant time slice
t=t_{0}=2
:

(w,{\partial}_{t}w)(t_{0},\cdot)=(w_{0},w_{1}),

(1.2)

the data
w_{0},w_{1}
having Sobolev regularity (see below).
The wave operator is defined as
\Box=m^{\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{\alpha}{\partial}_{\beta}=-{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}+{\partial}_{a}{\partial}^{a}
with
m=\text{diag}(-1,1,1)
, while for simplicity in the presentation
P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}
are constants satisfying the standard null condition, i.e.

P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}\xi_{\gamma}\xi_{\alpha}\xi_{\beta}=0
for all

\xi_{0}^{2}=\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi^{2}_{2}
and, in addition, the symmetry condition

P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}=P^{\gamma\beta\alpha}
.
While we focus here on a model problem, our method should be applicable well beyond (
1.1
).

Latin letters
a,b,\ldots\in\{1,2\}
are used for spatial indices and Greek letters
\alpha,\beta,\ldots\in\{0,1,2\}
represent spacetime indices, while Einstein summation convention is adopted unless otherwise specified.
Without loss of generality, the initial data
(w_{0},w_{1})
are assumed to be supported in a ball, say

\{(t,x):t=2,|x|\leq 1\}
, so that the solution is supported within the region
\{(t,x):t\geq|x|+1,t\geq 2\}
.

Our main result is as follows, and provides us with a uniform control of the energy associated with any vector field in the following list, referred to as admissible vector fields,

\Gamma\in\{{\partial}_{\alpha},L_{a}=x_{a}{\partial}_{t}+t{\partial}_{a},\Omega_{ab}=x_{a}{\partial}_{b}-x_{b}{\partial}_{a},L_{0}=t{\partial}_{t}+x^{a}{\partial}_{a}\}.

(1.3)

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N\geq 4
be an integer.
Consider the wave equation (
1.1
) together with initial data
(w_{0},w_{1})
on the time slice
t=2
supported in the ball
\{x:|x|\leq 1\}
.
For any
\delta>0
there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
and all initial data satisfying

\|w_{0}\|_{H^{N+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|w_{1}\|_{H^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\epsilon,

(1.4)

the Cauchy problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) admits a global-in-time solution
w
, which
decays according to

|w(t,x)|\lesssim t^{-1/2+\delta/2},\qquad|{\partial}w(t,x)|\lesssim t^{-1/2}.

(1.5)

In addition, the total energy of this solution is
uniformly bounded
, i.e. for any admissible field
\Gamma

\|{\partial}\Gamma^{I}w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim 1,\qquad|I|\leq N.

(1.6)

The global existence part in this theorem has been established by Alinhac using the ghost weight energy method in
[
2
]
. Here the improvement concerns the uniform boundedness of the top-order energy as stated in (
1.6
) (together with the decay rate for the solution itself in (
1.5
)).
Our strategy is first to show the energy for the restriction of the solution on a hyperboloidal foliation is uniformly bounded, and then to deduce that the energy on constant time
t
slices is also uniformly bounded.
The analysis is based on the Hyperboloidal Foliation Method developed in LeFloch and Ma
[
29
]
for coupled systems of wave and Klein-Gordon equations. As far as either (uncoupled) wave equations or Klein-Gordon equations are concerned this strategy was investigated first
in pioneering work by Klainerman
[
26
]
and Hörmander
[
20
]
.

Brief history on related topics.

In seminal work by Klainerman
[
25
]
and Christodoulou
[
12
]
, the wave equation with null nonlinearities and sufficiently small initial data was shown to admit global-in-time solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
. However due to the slow decay of linear waves in
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
, nonlinear wave equations in
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
are somewhat more difficult to handle. In the framework of Klainerman’s vector field method, Alinhac found a class of “good derivatives” and proved a new kind of energy estimate, which is called the “ghost weight” energy estimate. Based on this idea, Alinhac
[
2
]

succeeded to prove that quasilinear wave equations with null nonlinearities admit small global-in-time solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
.
In
[
2
]
, the top-order energy to the solutions grows polynomially in time.
Whether or not the time-growth on this two-dimensional problem is a true phenomena as stated
in Alinhac’s blowup-at-infinity conjecture
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
14
]
remains an open question until now.

Following Alinhac’s pioneering work
[
2
]
on two-dimensional quasilinear null wave equations, several interesting advances were also made in recent years. In
[
17
]
concerning the equation (
1.1
), the authors removed the compactness assumption on the initial data and, to this end, relied on a class of weighted
L^{\infty}
–
L^{\infty}
estimates.
A similar result for two-dimensional, fully nonlinear, wave equations satisfying the null condition was obtained in
[
11
]
, and this result was achieved by relying on an inherent strong null structure
[
31
]
enjoyed by nonlinearities. Another interesting work is
[
19
]
, in which a detailed description of the scattering properties of solutions was derived.

Next we turn to the description of various progress related to Alinhac conjecture on obtaining time-growth properties or
deriving uniform bounds for the top-order energy for the wave-type equations.
On one hand, in
[
14
]
, the authors studied the large-time behavior of the solutions to a class of three-dimensional nonlinear wave equations satisfying the weak null condition, and proved that the decay of solutions at high-order
is strictly slower than
t^{-1}
; this means that the high-order energy of the solutions cannot be uniformly bounded, and this establishes Alinhac conjecture concerning the blowup-at-infinity for this problem.
On the other hand, there exists many progress on establishing uniform bounds for the top-order energy of solutions for various classes of wave-type equations.
In the recent work
[
11
]
on a class of two-dimensional quasilinear wave equations with hidden strong null structure, as well as in
[
10
]
on two-dimensional incompressible elastodynamics, the authors obtained a uniform bound for
the top-order energy. In their proof, the main ingredients include the use of the ghost weight method
[
2
]
and
the inherent strong null structure
[
31
]
.
In the work
[
37
]
and
[
33
]
, the authors derived a uniform bound for
the top-order energy for three-dimensional quasilinear null wave equations and
for the system of three-dimensional incompressible isotropic elastodynamics, respectively; their
proof was based on a novel use of the null condition at the top-order energy level.
Furthermore, as mentioned earlier when introducing the hyperboloid foliation method, similar results
were discovered for three-dimensional coupled wave-Klein-Gordon equations
[
29
]
and for evolving relativistic membranes
[
30
]
.

Main challenge.

Let us describe here the main challenge in deriving a uniform bound for the top-order energy of the solutions to (
1.1
), which are mainly due to the nature of the (slow!) decay of two-dimensional waves. Recall that the ghost energy estimate for the equation
-\Box u=f

reads

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|{\partial}u(t)|^{2}\,dx+\sum_{a}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{|G_{a}u|^{2}\over\langle t-|x|\rangle^{1+\delta}}\,dxdt\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|{\partial}u(t_{0})|^{2}\,dx+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big|f{\partial}_{t}u\big|\,dxdt,

(1.7)

in which
\delta>0
and
G_{a}=(x_{a}/|x|){\partial}_{t}+{\partial}_{a}
are the so-called “good derivatives” associated with
the ghost weight energy estimate.
Returning to our model (
1.1
), we see that, when estimating the top-order energy of the solution
w
, we need to control the time-integral

\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{\gamma}w{\partial}_{\alpha}\Gamma^{I}w{\partial}_{\beta}\Gamma^{I}w\|_{L^{1}}\,dt,

(1.8)

with
|I|=N
and
\Gamma\in\{{\partial}_{\alpha},L_{a},\Omega_{ab},L_{0}\}
.
(See (
3.13
) below for further details.)
Then we find

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{\gamma}w{\partial}_{\alpha}\Gamma^{I}w{\partial}_{\beta}\Gamma^{I}w\|_{L^{1}}\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{a}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|{\partial}{\partial}wG_{a}\Gamma^{I}w{\partial}\Gamma^{I}w\|_{L^{1}}\,dt+\textsl{similar}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{a}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|{\partial}{\partial}wG_{a}\Gamma^{I}w\|_{L^{2}}\|{\partial}\Gamma^{I}w\|_{L^{2}}\,dt+\textsl{similar}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|{\partial}{\partial}w\langle t-|x|\rangle^{1/2+\delta/2}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\,dt\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Big\|{G_{a}\Gamma^{I}w\over\langle t-|x|\rangle^{1/2+\delta/2}}\Big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\,dt\Big)^{1/2}\sup_{\tau\in[t_{0},t]}\|{\partial}\Gamma^{I}w(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}+\textsl{similar},

where “similar” stands for integral terms that will be similarly controled.
It is easy to see that
even if we assume
that
w
enjoys the properties enjoyed by linear waves, i.e.

\|{\partial}{\partial}w\langle t-|x|\rangle^{1/2+\delta/2}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{-1},\qquad\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Big\|{G_{a}\Gamma^{I}w\over\langle t-|x|\rangle^{1/2+\delta/2}}\Big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\,dt+\|{\partial}\Gamma^{I}w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim 1,

(1.9)

then
we are still left with a
bad growth
in time associated with
(\log\langle t\rangle)^{1/2}
and the latter
blows up at infinity.

Our strategy of the proof.

We now present our new key technique which helps to overcome the aforementioned challenge.
In our approach we use hyperboloids
\mathcal{H}_{s}=\{(t,x):t^{2}=s^{2}+|x|^{2}\}
in order to foliate the spacetime, and we
estimate the energy of the wave equation along these hyperboloids, as in
[
26
,
20
,
29
]
;
see Lemma
2.1
below.
We find that in the energy estimate we integrate with respect to the hyperbolic time
s=\sqrt{t^{2}-|x|^{2}}
instead of
t
, and this allows us to take advantage of the
t-|x|
decay
,
which is precisely provided by the ghost weight energy estimate
[
2
]
.
A second key ingredient for our proof is the conformal–type energy estimate and its quasilinear version

[
38
,
35
,
34
,
27
]
, leading to almost sharp bounds on the norms

\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}
(defined in (
2.5
), below) of the following terms (
|I|\leq N
):

(s/t)\Gamma^{I}w,\qquad(s/t)L_{a}\Gamma^{I}w,\qquad(s/t)\Omega_{ab}\Gamma^{I}w,\qquad(s/t)L_{0}\Gamma^{I}w.

In addition, we also rely on a version of the estimate for null forms, which is well-adapted to the hyperboloidal foliation setting. This reads as follows
[
29
]
:

\displaystyle|P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{\gamma}w{\partial}_{\alpha}{\partial}_{\beta}w|

\displaystyle\lesssim(s/t)^{2}|{\partial}_{t}w{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}w|+\sum_{a}\big|{L_{a}w\over t}{\partial}{\partial}w\big|+\sum_{a}\big|{\partial}w{L_{a}{\partial}w\over t}\big|+t^{-1}|{\partial}w{\partial}w|.

(1.10)

We refer to Lemma
3.2
for further details. Based on this null form estimate, we roughly expect

\big\|P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{\gamma}w{\partial}_{\alpha}{\partial}_{\beta}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}\lesssim s^{-2},

(1.11)

which, now, is an integrable function. For a better understanding that null condition can compensate the slow decay of two-dimensional waves, we provide here a comparison with
three-dimensional wave equations with general quadratic nonlinearity.
Namely, if
u
denotes a (local) solution to

-\Box u={\partial}_{t}u{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}u,\qquad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{1+3},

then we find that the best decay we can get is

\big\|{\partial}_{t}u{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}u\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}\lesssim\big\|(t/s){\partial}_{t}u\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}\big\|(s/t){\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}u\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}\lesssim s^{-1},

which non-integrable. Clearly, the null condition forces the nonlinearities to decay fast, even if in lower dimension.

2
Fundamental energy estimate

2.1
Notation

We work in the spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
with metric
m=\text{diag}(-1,1,1)
, and the indices are raised or lowered by the metric
m
. As usual, we use

{\partial}_{\alpha}={\partial}_{x^{\alpha}}

to denote partial derivatives, with
x^{0}=t
(i.e.
x_{0}=-t
). The rotating vector fields are denoted by

\Omega_{ab}=x_{a}{\partial}_{b}-x_{b}{\partial}_{a},\qquad a,b\in\{1,2\}.

We represent the Lorentz boosts by

L_{a}=x_{a}{\partial}_{t}+t{\partial}_{a},\qquad a\in\{1,2\},

and the scaling vector field is denoted by

L_{0}=x^{\alpha}{\partial}_{\alpha}.

We will use
\Gamma
to denote a vector field in the set
V:=\{{\partial}_{0},{\partial}_{1},{\partial}_{2},L_{1},L_{2},\Omega_{12},L_{0}\}
.

On the other hand, we use
s\geq s_{0}=2
to denote the hyperbolic time, and the hyperboloids are denoted by

\mathcal{H}_{s}=\{(t,x):t^{2}=|x|^{2}+s^{2}\}.

In order to fit well with the hyperboloidal foliation of the interior of the cone

\mathcal{K}=\{(t,x):t\geq|x|+1\},

we first introduce (see
[
29
]
) the hyperboloidal frame

\overline{{\partial}}_{0}={\partial}_{s}=(s/t){\partial}_{t},\qquad\overline{{\partial}}_{a}={L_{a}\over t}={x_{a}\over t}{\partial}_{t}+{\partial}_{a},\quad a=1,2.

(2.1)

The original partial derivatives can be expressed by the hyperboloidal frame, which reads

{\partial}_{\alpha}=\Psi^{\beta}_{\alpha}\overline{{\partial}}_{\beta},

(2.2)

with the transition matrix

(\Psi_{\alpha}^{\beta})=\begin{pmatrix}{t/s}&0&0\\
-{x_{1}/s}&1&0\\
-{x_{2}/s}&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(2.3)

Next, we introduce (see
[
29
]
) the so-called semi-hyperboloidal frame

\underline{\partial}_{0}={\partial}_{t},\qquad\underline{\partial}_{a}=\overline{{\partial}}_{a},\quad a=1,2.

(2.4)

The usual partial derivatives can also be expressed by the semi-hyperboloidal frame

{\partial}_{t}=\underline{\partial}_{0},\qquad{\partial}_{a}=-{x_{a}\over t}\underline{\partial}_{0}+\underline{\partial}_{a},

and this will be used when estimating the null forms.

Worth to mention, for all points
(t,x)\in\mathcal{H}_{s}\cap\mathcal{K}
with
s\geq 2
we have

|x|\leq t,\qquad s\leq t\leq s^{2},\qquad t\leq t+|x|\leq 2t.

Given a sufficiently nice function
u
with support
\mathcal{K}
, its weighted norms on hyperboloids
\mathcal{H}_{s}
(
s\geq 2
) are defined by

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}^{p}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{s}^{*}}\big|u(t,x)\big|^{p}\,dx:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big|\widetilde{u}(s,x)\big|^{p}\,dx,\qquad\widetilde{u}(s,x):=u\big(\sqrt{s^{2}+|x|^{2}},x\big),

(2.5)

for all
1\leq p<+\infty
, in which
\mathcal{H}_{s}^{*}
represents the projection of
\mathcal{H}_{s}
onto the slice
\{(t,x):t=2\}
, and the
L^{\infty}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}
is defined in the natural way.

2.2
Energy estimate

Given a sufficiently nice function
u
on
\mathcal{H}_{s}
, we define its (natural) energy and conformal energy respectivly by (following
[
29
,
35
,
34
]
)

\displaystyle E(u,s)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{s}^{*}}\Big(\big({\partial}_{t}u\big)^{2}+\sum_{a}\big({\partial}_{a}u\big)^{2}+2(x^{a}/t){\partial}_{t}u{\partial}_{a}u\Big)\,dx

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{s}^{*}}\Big(\big((s/t){\partial}_{t}u\big)^{2}+\sum_{a}\big(\underline{\partial}_{a}u\big)^{2}\Big)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{s}^{*}}\Big(\big(\underline{\partial}_{\perp}u\big)^{2}+\sum_{a}\big((s/t){\partial}_{a}u\big)^{2}+\sum_{a<b}\big(t^{-1}\Omega_{ab}u\big)^{2}\Big)\,dx,

\displaystyle E_{con}(u,s)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{s}^{*}}\Big(\sum_{a}\big(s\underline{\partial}_{a}u\big)^{2}+\big(Ku+u\big)^{2}\Big)\,dx,

with
a,b\in\{1,2\}
, the orthogonal vector field
\underline{\partial}_{\perp}:=L_{0}/t={\partial}_{t}+(x^{a}/t){\partial}_{a}
and
Ku:=\big(s{\partial}_{s}+2x^{a}\underline{\partial}_{a}\big)u
. Easily, we see that

\int_{\mathcal{H}_{s}^{*}}\sum_{\alpha}\big((s/t){\partial}_{\alpha}u\big)^{2}+(\Gamma u/t)^{2}\,dx\lesssim E(u,s),

with
\Gamma\in\{{\partial}_{\alpha},L_{a},L_{0},\Omega_{ab}\}
.

Lemma 2.1

(Energy estimates on hyperboloids)
.

Consider the wave equation

-\Box u=h,\qquad(u,{\partial}_{t}u)(s_{0},\cdot)=(u_{0},u_{1}).

We have the following three kinds of energy estimates on hyperboloids.

•

The standard energy estimates (see
[
29
]
):

E(u,s)^{1/2}\leq E(u,s_{0})^{1/2}+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\|h\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\,d\tau.

(2.7)

•

The conformal energy estimates (see
[
38
,
35
,
34
]
):

E_{con}(u,s)^{1/2}\leq E_{con}(u,s_{0})^{1/2}+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\tau\|h\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\,d\tau.

(2.8)

•

The
L^{2}
norm estimates (see
[
34
]
):

\|(s/t)u\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{s})}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{s_{0}})}+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{E_{con}(u,\tau)^{1/2}\over\tau}\,d\tau.

(2.9)

Proof.

We revisit the proof for the conformal energy estimates only.

First recall

{\partial}_{t}={t\over s}{\partial}_{s},\qquad{\partial}_{a}=-{x_{a}\over s}{\partial}_{s}+\overline{{\partial}}_{a},

and the wave operator
-\Box
can thus be expressed in terms of
\overline{{\partial}}_{\alpha}
, which reads

\displaystyle-\Box u

\displaystyle={\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}u-{\partial}_{a}{\partial}^{a}u

\displaystyle={t\over s}{\partial}_{s}\big({t\over s}{\partial}_{s}u\big)-\big(-{x_{a}\over s}{\partial}_{s}+\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\big)\big(-{x^{a}\over s}{\partial}_{s}u+\overline{{\partial}}^{a}u\big)

\displaystyle={t^{2}\over s^{2}}{\partial}_{s}{\partial}_{s}u+{t\over s}{1\over s}{s\over t}{\partial}_{s}u-{t\over s}t{1\over s^{2}}{\partial}_{s}u-{x_{a}x^{a}\over s^{2}}{\partial}_{s}{\partial}_{s}u+{x_{a}x^{a}\over s^{2}}{1\over s^{2}}{\partial}_{s}u

\displaystyle+{x_{a}\over s}{\partial}_{s}\overline{{\partial}}^{a}u+{x_{a}\over s}\overline{{\partial}}^{a}{\partial}_{s}u+{2\over s}{\partial}_{s}u-\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\overline{{\partial}}^{a}u

\displaystyle={\partial}_{s}{\partial}_{s}u+2{x^{a}\over s}\overline{{\partial}}_{a}{\partial}_{s}u+{2\over s}{\partial}_{s}u-\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\overline{{\partial}}^{a}u

\displaystyle=s^{-1}{\partial}_{s}\big(s{\partial}_{s}u+2x^{a}\overline{{\partial}}_{a}u+u\big)-\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\overline{{\partial}}^{a}u.

In succession, we have

\displaystyle+\sum_{a}\big\|\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w{\partial}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}+\big\|t^{-1}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\Big).

Now we estimate each of the terms. First, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq|I|}\big\|(\tau/t)^{2}{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{|I_{1}|\leq N-3,|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|(\tau/t)(t-|x|)^{-1}{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t)(t-|x|){\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{|I_{1}|\leq N-1,|I_{2}|\leq N-3}\big\|(\tau/t){\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t){\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{|I_{1}|\leq N-3,|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|(\tau/t)(t-|x|)^{-1}{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t)\Gamma{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{|I_{1}|\leq N-1,|I_{2}|\leq N-3}\big\|(\tau/t){\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t)(t-|x|)^{-1}\Gamma{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\lesssim(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}\tau^{-2},

in which we used the relation
|{\partial}w|\lesssim(t-|x|)^{-1}|\Gamma w|
.
Next, we proceed by estimating

\displaystyle\sum_{a,|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\overline{{\partial}}_{a}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{a,|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{1}}wt^{-1}L_{a}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{a,|I_{1}|\leq N-1,|I_{2}|\leq N-3}\big\|(\tau/t){\partial}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(t/\tau)t^{-1}L_{a}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{a,|I_{1}|\leq N-3,|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{1}}wt^{-1}(t/\tau)\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t)L_{a}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\lesssim(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}\tau^{-2}.

Then, we note

\displaystyle\sum_{a,|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w{\partial}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{a,|I_{1}|\leq N-3,|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w(t/\tau)\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t){\partial}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{a,|I_{1}|\leq N-1,|I_{2}|\leq N-3}\big\|\tau\overline{{\partial}}_{a}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|\tau^{-1}{\partial}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\lesssim(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}\tau^{-2+\delta}.

Finally, we easily get (without details)

\displaystyle\sum_{|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq N-1}\big\|t^{-1}{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{1}}w{\partial}\Gamma^{I_{2}}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\lesssim(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}\tau^{-2}.

To sum things up, we arrive at

\displaystyle\big\|\Gamma^{I}N(w,w)\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\lesssim(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}\tau^{-2+\delta},\qquad|I|\leq N-1.

(4.6)

Then the energy estimates give us

\displaystyle E(\Gamma^{I}w,s)^{1/2}\lesssim

\displaystyle E(\Gamma^{I}w,s_{0})^{1/2}+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\big\|\Gamma^{I}N(w,w)\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\epsilon+(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\tau^{-2+\delta}\,d\tau\lesssim\epsilon+(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2},\qquad|I|\leq N-1.

Similarly, the conformal energy estimates lead us to

\displaystyle E_{con}(\Gamma^{I}w,s)^{1/2}\lesssim

\displaystyle E_{con}(\Gamma^{I}w,s_{0})^{1/2}+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\tau\big\|\Gamma^{I}N(w,w)\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\epsilon+(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\tau^{-1+\delta}\,d\tau\lesssim\epsilon+(C_{1}\epsilon)^{2}s^{\delta},\qquad|I|\leq N-1,

in which we used (
4.6
).
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Under the assumptions in (
4.1
), we have the following improved estimates for all
s\in[s_{0},s_{1})

\displaystyle E(\Gamma^{I}w,s)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon+(C_{1}\epsilon)^{3/2},\qquad|I|\leq N.

(4.7)

Proof.

Applying the vector field
\Gamma^{I}
(with
|I|=N
) to the wave equation (
1.1
), we have

-\Box\Gamma^{I}w=\Gamma^{I}N(w,w)=\sum_{|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|+d=|I|}N_{d}(\Gamma^{I_{1}}w,\Gamma^{I_{2}}w),

|I_{2}|\leq N-1\end{subarray}}N(\Gamma^{I_{1}}w,\Gamma^{I_{2}}w).

(4.8)

Recall the energy estimates for quasilinear wave, and we have

\displaystyle E(\Gamma^{I}w,s)

\displaystyle\lesssim E(\Gamma^{I}w,s_{0})+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{*}}\Big|(\tau/t)P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{\gamma}{\partial}_{\alpha}w{\partial}_{\beta}\Gamma^{I}w{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I}w\Big|\,dxd\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{*}}\Big|(\tau/t)P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{\gamma}w{\partial}_{\alpha}\Gamma^{I}w{\partial}_{\beta}\Gamma^{I}w\Big|\,dxd\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{*}}\Big|(\tau/t)\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|+d=|I|\\
d\geq 1\end{subarray}}N_{d}(\Gamma^{I_{1}}w,\Gamma^{I_{2}}w)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|=|I|\\
|I_{2}|\leq N-1\end{subarray}}N(\Gamma^{I_{1}}w,\Gamma^{I_{2}}w)\Big){\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I}w\Big|\,dxd\tau.

\displaystyle\big\|P^{\gamma\alpha\beta}{\partial}_{\gamma}{\partial}_{\alpha}w{\partial}_{\beta}\Gamma^{I}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big\|(\tau/t)^{2}{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}w{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}+\sum_{a}\big\|{\partial}_{t}\underline{\partial}_{a}w{\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{a}\big\|\tau^{-1}{\partial}_{t}{\partial}_{t}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|\tau\underline{\partial}_{a}\Gamma^{I}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}+\sum_{a,b}\big\|\tau^{-1}{\partial}_{t}\underline{\partial}_{a}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|\tau\underline{\partial}_{b}\Gamma^{I}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle+\big\|(t/\tau)\underline{\partial}_{a}{\partial}_{t}w\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t){\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}\big\|(\tau/t){\partial}_{t}\Gamma^{I}w\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|+d=|I|\\
d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\big\|N_{d}(\Gamma^{I_{1}}w,\Gamma^{I_{2}}w)\big\|_{L^{2}_{f}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|=|I|\\
