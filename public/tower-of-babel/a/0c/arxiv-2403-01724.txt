Constructing monads from cubical diagrams and homotopy colimits
Kristine Bauer, Robyn Brooks, Kathryn Hess, Brenda Johnson, Julie Rasmusen, Bridget Schreiner

Abstract
This paper is the first step in a general program for defining cocalculus towers of functors via sequences of compatible monads. Goodwillie’s calculus of homotopy functors inspired many new functor calculi in a wide range of contexts in algebra, homotopy theory and geometric topology. Recently, the third and fourth authors have developed a general program for constructing generalized calculi from sequences of compatible comonads. In this paper, we dualize the first step of the Hess-Johnson program, focusing on monads rather than comonads. We consider categories equipped with an action of the poset category 𝒫 ⁡ ( n ¯ ) \mathcal{P}(\hskip 1.00006pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 1.00006pt) , called 𝒫 ⁡ ( n ¯ ) \mathcal{P}(\hskip 1.00006pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 1.00006pt) -modules. We exhibit a functor from 𝒫 ⁡ ( n ¯ ) \mathcal{P}(\hskip 1.00006pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 1.00006pt) -modules to the category of monads. The resulting monads act on categories of functors whose codomain is equipped with a suitable notion of homotopy colimits. In the final section of the paper, we demonstrate the monads used to construct McCarthy’s dual calculus as an example of a monad arising from a 𝒫 ⁡ ( n ¯ ) \mathcal{P}(\hskip 1.00006pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 1.00006pt) -module. This confirms that our dualization of the Hess-Johnson program generalizes McCarthy’s dual calculus, and serves as a proof of concept for further development of this program.

monads, cubical diagrams, homotopy colimits, module categories, functor calculus

Goodwillie’s calculus of homotopy functors

The abelian functor calculus of Johnson and McCarthy

Under certain hypotheses, the discrete calculus agrees with Goodwillie’s calculus for homotopy functors, thereby serving as a bridge between the algebraic abelian functor calculus and the topological calculus of homotopy functors.

The discrete and abelian functor calculi are further distinguished by the means by which they are constructed. Underlying each stage of the Taylor tower for both calculi is a comonad that is simultaneously used to define what it means for a functor to be of “degree
n

n
” approximation to an arbitrary functor. The nature of these constructions suggests that these methods can be generalized, to develop new calculi from sequences of compatible comonads, and dualized, to create a framework for building cocalculi from sequences of compatible monads. A general program for constructing calculi using this approach, is being carried out by the third and fourth authors of this work

On the dual side, McCarthy’s dual calculus provides an example of a cocalculus defined via monads that is dual to the discrete calculus of

This paper is the first step in the process of developing a general program for defining cocalculi via sequences of compatible monads, dual to the program of Hess and Johnson, which will include McCarthy’s dual calculus as an example.

The comonads underlying the abelian and discrete calculi arise as the iterated homotopy fibers of certain
n
-cubical diagrams
, that is, functors out of poset categories
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
whose objects are subsets of sets
\underline{n}:=\{1,\dots,n\}
where
n
can be any natural number. That one can construct the comonads used in the discrete and abelian functor calculi in this way is a consequence of the fact that each cubical diagram arises from a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module

\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)

\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
as a symmetric monoidal category with union as monoidal product. In the case of dual calculus, there are monads underlying the constructions which arise as iterated cofibers of cubical diagrams. The cubical diagrams involved in dual calculus have different features, which indicate that an interpretation of these diagrams as
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules require a different monoidal structure.

The goal of this paper is to show that this process of producing comonads from
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules can be dualized to produce monads from
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules, when considering
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
equipped with the symmetric monoidal structure given by intersection instead of union. Our main result, which appears as Theorem
5.5
, establishes the existence of a functor from the category of
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules to the category of monads and can be summarized as follows.

Theorem
5.5
.

For a category
\mathscr{M}
with homotopy colimits, there exists a contravariant functor from the category of
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules to the category of monads. For a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module on a category

The monads in the target of this functor are obtained by taking the iterated homotopy cofiber of certain cubical diagrams. In Proposition

1.1
Organization

The paper is organized as follows. In
section 2
, we review the definitions of monads, comonads and morphisms between monads, allowing us to define the category of monads. We further discuss ways in which monads and comonads can arise from adjunctions. We use
section 3
to introduce
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules and establish the equivalence between
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules, strict idempotent comonads, and strict coreflective subcategories. In
section 4
, we identify the properties for a choice of model for homotopy colimits required for our main construction, which we construct in
section 5
. This construction transforms a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module into a monad and yields the functor between the category of
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules and the category of monads, which is the subject of Theorem
5.5
. Finally, in
section 6
, we review the monads of McCarthy’s dual calculus and show that they arise from our framework for constructing monads from cubical diagrams and homotopy colimits.

3
(\mathcal{P}(n),\cap,\underline{n})
-modules

For
n
a non-negative integer, let
\underline{n}:=\{1,...,n\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
the category with subsets of
\underline{n}
as objects and morphisms given by inclusions, and by convention we set
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{0}}\hskip 0.92505pt):=\{\emptyset\}
, the category with one object and no non-identity morphisms. Henceforth, we consider
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
equipped with the symmetric monoidal structure given by intersection and with unit
\underline{n}
, i.e.,
(\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt),\cap,\underline{n}\,)
. In this section we study
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules with respect to this monoidal structure. We start by recalling the definition of a module over a symmetric monoidal category, specialized to
(\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt),\cap,\underline{n}\,)
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module
consists of a category
\mathscr{A}
together with a functor
\theta:\mathscr{A}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\mathscr{A}
, which we call the
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure
on
\mathscr{A}
, that is both unital and associative, in the following sense:

1.

(Unital)
\theta(-,\underline{n}\,)=\text{Id}_{\mathscr{A}}
, and

2.

(Associative)
\theta\left(\theta(a,\mathscr{U}),\mathscr{V}\right)=\theta(a,\mathscr{U}\cap\mathscr{V})
for all
\mathscr{U},\mathscr{V}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
and
a\in\text{Ob}\,\mathscr{A}
.

A
morphism of
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules
between two
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules
(\mathscr{A},\theta_{\mathscr{A}})
and
(\mathscr{B},\theta_{\mathscr{B}})
consists of a functor
F:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
compatible with the
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structures, in the sense that the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{B}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{B}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F\times\text{Id}_{\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.45003pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.45003pt)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta_{\mathscr{A}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta_{\mathscr{B}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}

We let
\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.80504pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.80504pt)}
denote the category of
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules and morphisms between them.

The following example will be used in Section 6, which concerns dual functor calculus.

Example 3.2
.

For
\mathscr{A}
a category with an initial object
-\infty_{\mathscr{A}}
, we can define a functor

\displaystyle\theta^{n}:\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathscr{A}^{\times n}

\displaystyle\left(\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right),\mathscr{U}\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\right),

where

b_{i}=\begin{cases}a_{i}&\text{if }i\in\mathscr{U}\\
-\infty_{\mathscr{A}}&\text{if }i\not\in\mathscr{U}.\end{cases}

For any morphism
\big((f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}),\iota\big):\big((a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),\mathscr{U}\big)\to\big((a_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n}^{\prime}),\mathscr{V}\big)
in
\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
, there is a morphism
\theta^{n}\big((f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}),\iota\big)
in
\mathscr{A}^{\times n}
which is given by
f_{i}
in the
i
th coordinate if
i\in\mathscr{U}
, and the unique map from the initial object otherwise.

It is not difficult to check that
\theta^{n}
is a functor.

Since
\theta^{n}\left((a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),\underline{n}\,\right)=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
for any
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\text{Ob}\,\mathscr{A}^{\times n}
, and

\theta^{n}\left(\theta^{n}\left(\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right),\mathscr{U}\right),\mathscr{V}\right)=\theta^{n}\left(\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right),\mathscr{U}\cap\mathscr{V}\right),

this functor defines a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure on
\mathscr{A}^{\times n}
.

To be able to create and understand large classes of
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules, it is helpful to characterize them in terms of
coreflective subcategories
and
comonads
. Since we have chosen to consider
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules with respect to the symmetric monoidal structure given by intersection instead of union, the following results are dual to those in
[
11
]
.
We start by recalling the remaining notion necessary to formulate these characterizations.

Definition 3.3
.

A
coreflective subcategory
of a category
\mathscr{A}
is a full subcategory
\mathscr{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathscr{A}
whose inclusion into
\mathscr{A}
admits a right adjoint
R
, which we call the
colocalisation functor
. The unit of such an adjunction is a natural isomorphism
a\cong Ra
for any
a\in\text{Ob}\,\mathscr{A}
, and if this is in fact the identity, we say that
\mathscr{A}_{0}
is a
strict coreflective subcategory
of
\mathscr{A}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

The following structures on a small category
\mathscr{A}
are equivalent:

1.

a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure on
\mathscr{A}
,

2.

a set of
n
strict idempotent comonads
(\mathbb{K}_{1},...,\mathbb{K}_{n})
on
\mathscr{A}
, with
\mathbb{K}_{\imath}=(K_{\imath},\Delta_{\imath},\varepsilon_{\imath})
, such that their underlying endofunctors commute under composition, i.e.,
K_{\imath}K_{k}=K_{k}K_{\imath}
for all
0\leq\imath,k\leq n
, and

3.

a set of
n
strict coreflective subcategories of
\mathscr{A}
,
\{R_{\imath}:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}_{\imath}\}_{\imath=1}^{n}
, such that
R_{\imath}|_{\mathscr{A}_{k}}R_{k}=R_{k}|_{\mathscr{A}_{\imath}}R_{\imath}
for every
1\leq\imath,k\leq n
.

Proof.

Properties
2
and
3
are equivalent by
[
6
, Proposition 1.3]
, hence we prove only that properties
1
and
2
are equivalent.
We first note that by taking the transpose, the existence of a functor
\theta:\mathscr{A}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\mathscr{A}
is equivalent to the existence of an
n
-cube of endofunctors
{\theta}^{\#}:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\operatorname{End}(\mathscr{A})
. Furthermore, the unit and associativity properties required for
\theta
to be a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure on
\mathscr{A}
, are equivalent to assuming that

{\theta}^{\#}(\underline{n})=\text{Id}_{\mathscr{A}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})\circ{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{V})={\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U}\cap\mathscr{V})={\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{V})\circ{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})

for all
\mathscr{U},\mathscr{V}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
. In particular, note that this will imply that
{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U}){\theta}^{\#}\circ(\mathscr{U})={\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})
for all
\mathscr{U}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
.

Before proceeding with the argument, we fix the following notation:
\widehat{\imath}:=\underline{n}\smallsetminus\{\imath\}
for
1\leq\imath\leq n
and
\mathscr{U}^{\text{c}}:=\underline{n}\smallsetminus\mathscr{U}
for any
\mathscr{U}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
. We see that the conditions on the transpose
{\theta}^{\#}
above are furthermore equivalent to the following requirements.

1.

Each
{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})
is idempotent, i.e.,
{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})\circ{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})={\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})
, and these functors are equipped with a natural transformation

\varepsilon_{\mathscr{U}}={\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U}\hookrightarrow\underline{n}\,):{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})\Rightarrow\text{Id}_{\mathscr{A}}.

2.

For any
\mathscr{U}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
with
\mathscr{U}^{\text{c}}=\{\jmath_{1},...,\jmath_{k}\}
,

{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})={\theta}^{\#}\left(\,\widehat{\jmath_{1}}\,\right)\circ\cdots\circ{\theta}^{\#}\left(\,\widehat{\jmath_{k}}\,\right).

3.

For all
1\leq\imath,\jmath\leq n
,

{\theta}^{\#}\left(\,\widehat{\imath}\,\right)\circ{\theta}^{\#}\left(\,\widehat{\jmath}\,\right)={\theta}^{\#}\left(\,\widehat{\jmath}\,\right)\circ{\theta}^{\#}\left(\,\widehat{\imath}\,\right).

We claim that these conditions are furthermore equivalent to the existence of a collection of
n
strict idempotent comonads with commuting underlying endofunctors. It is clear that each
{\theta}^{\#}(\mathscr{U})
is such a comonad, hence it is sufficient to show that any such collection
(\mathbb{K}_{1},...,\mathbb{K}_{n})
of
n
comonads on
\mathscr{A}
, with
\mathbb{K}_{\jmath}=(K_{\jmath},\Delta_{\jmath},\varepsilon_{\jmath})
gives rise to an
n
-cube of endofunctors
\varphi:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\operatorname{End}(\mathscr{A})
satisfying the three desired properties.

We define this
\varphi
by

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\operatorname{End}(\mathscr{A})

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}=\underline{n}\smallsetminus\left\{\jmath_{1},...,\jmath_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\mapsto K_{\jmath_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ K_{\jmath_{k}}.

Properties
1
and
3
follow by the assumptions that each comonad
\mathbb{K}_{\jmath}
is strict idempotent with commuting underlying endofunctors and noting that the composition

\varepsilon_{\mathscr{U}}:=\varepsilon_{\jmath_{1}}\cdots\varepsilon_{\jmath_{k}}=\varphi\left(\mathscr{U}\hookrightarrow\underline{n}\,\right):\varphi(\mathscr{U})\Rightarrow\text{Id}_{\mathscr{A}}

gives the desired natural transformation.

Finally, property
2
follows by observing that since
\varphi\left(\,\widehat{\jmath}\,\right)=K_{\jmath}
,

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathscr{U})

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(\,\underline{n}\smallsetminus\left\{\jmath_{1},...,\jmath_{k}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=K_{\jmath_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ K_{\jmath_{k}}

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(\,\widehat{\jmath_{1}}\,\right)\circ\cdots\circ\varphi\left(\,\widehat{\jmath_{k}}\,\right).

∎

Our method for constructing monads from
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules uses right Kan extensions and homotopy colimits. In fact, we need models for homotopy colimits that satisfy certain standard properties up to natural isomorphism rather than weak equivalence. We call such models
systems of homotopy colimits
, and use this section to define them and provide examples. We also review right Kan extensions, and establish two important ways in which right Kan extensions interact with systems of homotopy colimits.

Throughout this section,
(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{W})
denotes a category
\mathscr{M}
equipped with a distinguished wide subcategory
\mathscr{W}
, the morphisms of which we refer to as
weak equivalences
, and we assume that
\mathscr{M}
has a terminal object, which we denote by
+\infty_{\mathscr{M}}
. In the following we work in the category
\Cat
, which consists of small categories and functors between them.

Definition 4.1
.

A
system of homotopy colimits
on
(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{W})
is a collection of functors

\mathbb{H}\text{C}:=\{\hocolim_{\mathbb{I}}:\operatorname{Fun}(\mathbb{I}\text{,}\mathscr{M})\rightarrow\mathscr{M}\,|\,\mathbb{I}\in\text{Ob}\,\Cat\}

such that the following properties hold.

1.

(Fubini) For all
\mathbb{I},\mathbb{J}\in\text{Ob}\,\Cat
and functors
F:\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{J}\rightarrow\mathscr{M}
, there exist natural isomorphisms

\hocolim_{\mathbb{J}}\ \hocolim_{\mathbb{I}}\ F\cong\hocolim_{\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{J}}\ F\cong\hocolim_{\mathbb{I}}\ \hocolim_{\mathbb{J}}\ F.

2.

For any functor
\alpha:\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{J}
, there exists a natural transformation
\tilde{\alpha}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathbb{J}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{M},}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathbb{I}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hocolim_{\mathbb{J}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hocolim_{\mathbb{I}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\alpha}\;}

where
\alpha^{*}
denotes the functor given by precomposition with
\alpha
.

3.

If
\mbox{Const}_{+\infty_{\mathscr{M}}}:\mathbb{I}\rightarrow\mathscr{M}
is the constant functor at
+\infty_{\mathscr{M}}
for any
\mathbb{I}\in\text{Ob}\,\Cat
, then there exists a natural isomorphism

{\hocolim}_{\mathbb{I}}\ (\mbox{Const}_{+\infty_{\mathscr{M}}})\cong+\infty_{\mathscr{M}}.

4.

If
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{0}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
denotes the category with one object, denoted
\emptyset
, and no non-identity morphisms, then there exists a natural isomorphism

{\hocolim}_{\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.80504pt\underline{\text{0}}\hskip 0.80504pt)}\ F\cong F(\emptyset)

for all functors
F:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{0}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\ \rightarrow\mathscr{M}
.

5.

Let
F,G\in\text{Ob}\,\operatorname{Fun}(\mathbb{I}\text{,}\mathscr{M})
. For any natural transformation
F\Rightarrow G
that is an objectwise weak equivalence,
the induced map
\hocolim_{\mathbb{I}}F\rightarrow\hocolim_{\mathbb{I}}G
is a weak equivalence as well.

Notation 4.2
.

A category
\mathscr{M}
that has a terminal object and is equipped with a distinguished wide subcategory
\mathscr{W}
and a system of homotopy colimits
\mathbb{H}\text{C}
is denoted by a triple
(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{W},\mathbb{H}\text{C})
.

Any cocomplete category that has a terminal object also has an associated system of homotopy colimits.

Example 4.3
.

Suppose
\mathscr{M}
is cocomplete and has a terminal object. Then standard properties of colimits ensure that

\{{\operatorname{colim}}_{\mathbb{I}}:\operatorname{Fun}(\mathbb{I}\text{,}\mathscr{M})\rightarrow\mathscr{M}\,|\,\mathbb{I}\in\text{Ob}\,\Cat\}

is a system of homotopy colimits for
\mathscr{M}
, when the weak equivalences are the isomorphisms in
\mathscr{M}
.

As the next two examples show, standard models for homotopy colimits can be used to form systems of homotopy colimits.

Example 4.4
.

Suppose
\mathscr{M}
is a simplicial model category in which every object is cofibrant. For a small category
\mathbb{I}
and an
\mathbb{I}
-diagram
X:\mathbb{I}\to\mathscr{M}
, one can use
[
13
, Definition 18.1.2]
to define
{\hocolim}_{\mathbb{I}}X
. It follows by arguments dual to those used in Appendix A of
[
3
]
, that properties 1 through 4 are satisfied by this model for homotopy colimits. Property 5 is
[
13
, Theorem 18.5.4(1)]
. If
\mathscr{M}
is a simplicial model category with functorial cofibrant replacement, the condition that every object is cofibrant can be removed, provided that each diagram
X
is postcomposed with the cofibrant replacement functor before applying
[
13
, Definition 18.1.2]
.

Example 4.5
.

Another standard model for homotopy colimits of diagrams in simplicial model categories is the geometric realization of the simplicial bar construction of the diagram (see
[
16
, Chapter 5]
). Arakawa showed in
[
1
]
that this model can be adapted for use in
Ch(\mathscr{A})
, equipped with the injective model structure. Here
\mathscr{A}
is a Grothendieck abelian category, and
Ch(\mathscr{A})
is the category of chain complexes in
\mathscr{A}
. The injective model structure on
Ch(\mathscr{A})
has as its weak equivalences the quasi-isomorphisms of chain complexes. For an
\mathbb{I}
-diagram in
Ch(\mathscr{A})
, its bar construction is a simplicial object in
Ch(\mathscr{A})
and by normalizing this in the simplicial direction one obtains a bicomplex in
\mathscr{A}
whose total complex serves as a model for the homotopy colimit of the diagram
[
1
, Corollary 3.14]
. One can check that this model satisfies the properties of Definition
4.1
and provides
Ch(\mathscr{A})
with a system of homotopy colimits. If one uses the Moore complex instead of the normalized chain complex construction, one also gets a model for homotopy colimits in
Ch(\mathscr{A})
, which however does not satisfy axioms 3 and 4 in Definition
4.1
.

In the next sections of the paper, systems of homotopy colimits will be used in conjunction with right Kan extensions. The remainder of this section will be used to review right Kan extensions and prove Lemma
4.9
, in which we establish useful properties of the relationship between homotopy colimits and right Kan extensions.

Theorem 4.7
.

[
17
, Theorem 6.2.1]

Given functors
\gamma:\mathbb{I}\to\mathscr{A}
and
F:\mathbb{I}\to\mathscr{B}
such that for every

the limit

\text{Ran}_{\gamma}{F}(a)=\lim\left(a\downarrow\gamma\xrightarrow{\text{pr}_{a}}\mathbb{I}\xrightarrow{F}\mathscr{B}\right)

exists in
\mathscr{A}
, these limits define the right Kan extension
\text{Ran}_{\gamma}{F}:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
, and the natural transformation
\varepsilon:\text{Ran}_{\gamma}{F}\gamma\Rightarrow F
can be extracted from the limit cone.

In fact, the right Kan extension is functorial in the sense that there exists a functor

\text{Ran}_{\gamma}{}:\operatorname{Fun}(\mathbb{I}\text{,}\mathscr{B})\rightarrow\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}\text{,}\mathscr{B}).

See
[
17
, Proposition 6.5]
for further details.

Remark 4.8
.

It follows directly by Theorem
4.7
that if
\mathscr{A}
is a small category, and
\mathscr{B}
is complete, then the right Kan extension of

along
\gamma:\mathbb{I}\to\mathscr{A}
always exists. To ease the assumptions we will therefore throughout usually assume
\mathscr{A}
is small and
\mathscr{B}
is complete to ensure existence of right Kan extension, but these assumptions can often be relaxed to existence of the limits in Theorem
4.7
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{W},\mathbb{H}\text{C})
be a complete category with terminal object

and a system of homotopy colimits, and let

and

be two functors satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem

.

,

, there exists a natural isomorphism

and

5
Main theorem

In this section, we complete the process of deriving monads from cubical diagrams. Starting with a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module on a category
\mathscr{A}
, we use the iterated homotopy cofibers of cubical diagrams arising from this
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module to construct a monad on
\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}\text{,}\mathscr{M})
for any complete category with a system of homotopy colimits
(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{W},\mathbb{H}\text{C})
. We achieve this goal by using a model for the iterated homotopy cofiber that allows us to realize it as a single homotopy colimit in a functorial fashion. We develop this model in Section
5.1
. In Section
5.2
, we construct the monads and prove our main result. Throughout this section, we assume that that
(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{W},\mathbb{H}\text{C})
is a complete category with a system of homotopy colimits.

5.1
A model for iterated homotopy cofibers

In this section, we describe our model for the iterated homotopy cofiber of a cubical diagram. To construct this model, we generate an associated expanded cubical diagram, and then take the homotopy colimit of this expanded diagram. We begin by providing an alternate description of
n
-cubical diagrams, which we use to obtain the desired expanded diagram for an
n
-cubical diagram
\chi:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\mathscr{M}
.

Let
\mathscr{I}
be the category with two objects, denoted by
0
and
1
, and a single non-identity morphism
t:0\to 1
. If we equip this with the symmetric monoidal product
\dicei
, for which
1
is the unit, and
0\dicei 0=0
, then the morphism
\omega_{1}:(\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{1}}\hskip 0.92505pt),\cap)\to(\mathscr{I},\dicei)
, which sends
\emptyset
to
0
and
\{1\}
to 1 is a monoidal isomorphism. By equipping
\mathscr{I}^{\times n}
with the component-wise symmetric monoidal product extending
\dicei
, this isomorphism can be extended to a monoidal isomorphism
\omega_{n}:(\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt),\cap)\to(\mathscr{I}^{\times n},\dicei)
as follows.

For any
\mathscr{U}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
, let
\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}}
be the characteristic function of
\mathscr{U}
, i.e., for
1\leq i\leq n
,
\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}}:\underline{n}\to\{0,1\}
is given by

\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}}(i)=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }i\notin\mathscr{U}\\
1&\textrm{ if }i\in\mathscr{U}.\end{cases}

We define
\omega_{n}:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\mathscr{I}^{\times n}
on objects
\mathscr{U}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
by

\omega_{n}(\mathscr{U})=(\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}}(1),\dots,\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}}(n)),

and extend to morphisms in the obvious way.
It is clear that
\omega_{n}
is an isomorphism. To see that
\omega_{n}
is monoidal, consider two subsets
\mathscr{U},\mathscr{V}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
. Using our description of
\dicei
, we see that

\begin{split}\omega_{n}(\mathscr{U}\cap\mathscr{V})&=(\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}\cap\mathscr{V}}(1),\dots,\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}\cap\mathscr{V}}(n))\\
&=(\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}}(1)\dicei\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{V}}(1),\dots,\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{U}}(n)\dicei\textrm{char}_{\mathscr{V}}(n)))\\
&=\omega_{n}(\mathscr{U})\dicei\omega_{n}(\mathscr{V}).\end{split}

The category
\mathscr{I}
embeds into another symmetric monoidal category, denoted by
(\Lambda,\dicei,1)
, which is the category with objects
\{0,1,\mathscr{l}\}
, and non-identity morphisms given by

\mathscr{l}\xleftarrow{s}0\xrightarrow{t}1.

The symmetric monoidal product on
\mathscr{I}
extends to
\Lambda
by setting
\mathscr{l}\dicei d=\mathscr{l}
for all
d\in\text{Ob}\,\Lambda
, and we further extend this to
\Lambda^{\times n}
component-wise.
For example, the category
\Lambda\times\Lambda
is given by the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(1,1)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(0,1)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathscr{l},1)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(1,0)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(0,0)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathscr{l},0)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(1,\mathscr{l})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(0,\mathscr{l})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathscr{l},\mathscr{l}).}

Let

denote the inclusion functor, and set

\varphi_{n}=\iota^{\times n}\omega_{n}:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\Lambda^{\times n}.

It is along this composite functor that we will extend

-cubical diagrams.

.

Let

, which lets us derive, for any object

. If

, then

, where

contains

if

, and does not contain
i
if

. So,

\begin{split}\text{Ran}_{\varphi_{n}}{\chi}(l_{1},\dots,l_{n})&=\lim\left(\mathscr{U}\xrightarrow{\text{pr}_{\underline{l}}}\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\xrightarrow{\chi}\mathscr{M}\right)\\

If

for some

, then

is the empty category, and

(5.1)

In other words,

such that

, which justifies considering this right Kan extension as an extended

-cubical diagram of
\chi
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{W},\mathbb{H}\text{C})
be a complete category equipped with a system of homotopy colimits. The
iterated homotopy cofiber
of an
n
-cubical diagram
\chi:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\mathscr{M}
, denoted by

, is given by

\icofib\chi=\hocolim_{\Lambda^{\times n}}\text{Ran}_{\varphi_{n}}{\chi}.

.

6
Recovering dual calculus

The goal of this section is to show that the cocross effects in the dual calculus tower of
[
15
]
are obtained naturally from a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module. Dual calculus is a dualization of the constructions that appear in
[
14
]
for functors of abelian categories (McCarthy’s dual calculus was originally constructed for functors of spectra). The functor calculus of
[
14
]
provides both an obstruction, the
(n+1)^{\textrm{st}}
cross effect
\text{cr}_{n+1}F
, which measures the failure of a functor
F
to take
(n+1)
-fold coproducts to
(n+1)
-fold products, and the best “degree
n
” approximation of
F
to a functor
P_{n}F
satisfying this property. Dually, the
(n+1)^{\textrm{st}}
cocross effect of the dual calculus tower measures the failure of a functor to take
(n+1)
-fold products to coproducts, and can be used to provide the best “codegree
n
” approximation to a functor.

The construction of the dual functor calculus tower in
[
15
]
proceeds in stages. First, the cocross effects are defined using homotopy cofibers of certain cubical diagrams. The cocross effects give rise to a monad, which is used to define the stages of the dual calculus tower,
P^{n}F
. Finally, the functors
P^{n}F
are shown to assemble into a tower of functors satisfying a universal property. In this section, we will show that the construction of the cocross effects of a functor
F:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
can be obtained from a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure on the category
\mathscr{A}
. The cocross effects and the associated monad are then shown to be direct consequences of the constructions in Section 5.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a category with finite products and a zero object denoted by
\infty_{\mathscr{A}}
. Given a function
g:\underline{n}\to\text{Ob}\,\mathscr{A}
, a key element of the construction of the cocross effects in
[
15
, Definition 1.3]
is a functor

\displaystyle U^{g}:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}

\displaystyle\mapsto\prod_{v\in\mathscr{V}}g(v).

Functoriality is defined using the universal properties of
\infty_{\mathscr{A}}
and of the product.

Recall from Example
3.2
that for each
n\geq 1
there is a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure

\theta^{n}:\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\mathscr{A}^{\times n}

taking
((a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),\mathscr{V})
to
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
where
b_{i}=a_{i}
for
i\in\mathscr{V}
and
b_{i}=\infty_{\mathscr{A}}
otherwise.
The functor
\sc{U}^{g}
can now be expressed in terms of this
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure by composing
\theta^{n}
with the product functor
\sqcap
from Example
2.6
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\xrightarrow{\theta^{n}}\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\xrightarrow{\sqcap}\mathscr{A}.

The fact that
\sqcap\theta^{n}((g(1),\ldots,g(n),\mathscr{V})\cong U^{g}(\mathscr{V})
follows immediately
from the definitions of
\theta^{n}
and
\sqcap
and the fact that
\infty_{\mathscr{A}}\times a\cong a

.
The functoriality of

in

U^{g}(\iota):\ =\sqcap\left(\theta^{n}\left(\text{Id}_{(g(1),\ldots,g(n))},\iota\right)\right).

.

of functors, as in Equation (

.

[

, Def. 1.3]

Proposition 6.2
.

The diagonal
n
-th cocross effect
\Delta^{*}\operatorname{cr}^{n}
construction underlies a monad
(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}})
on

Proof.

Let
\left(\text{T}^{\theta^{n}}_{\mathscr{M}},\mu_{n},\eta_{n}\right)

from Proposition

The first main theorem of
[
15
]
used in the construction of the dual calculus tower relates the
n
-th cocross effect to the
m
-th cocross effect of a functor to spectra whenever there is a surjection
s:\underline{n}\to\underline{m}
. We now extend this to our setting.

Let
Surj
denote the category which has as objects the sets
\underline{n}
and surjective functions as morphisms. It will be useful to have notation for several variants of the diagonal and product functors needed to compare
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{k}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules for different
k
’s. To keep track of these, let
\Delta^{k}:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}^{\times k}
and
\sqcap^{k}:\mathscr{A}^{\times k}\to\mathscr{A}
denote the relevant functors to and from the category
\mathscr{A}^{\times k}
. Given a surjection
s:\underline{n}\to\underline{m}
, let
\Delta^{(s)}:\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\to\mathscr{A}^{\times n}
denote the diagonal functor defined by

\Delta^{(s)}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})=\left(a_{s(1)},...,a_{s(n)}\right)

and let
\sqcap^{(s)}:\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\to\mathscr{A}^{\times m}
denote the product functor defined by

\sqcap^{(s)}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\left(\prod_{i\in s^{-1}(1)}a_{i}\text{ },\ldots,\prod_{i\in s^{-1}(m)}a_{i}\right).

These two variants of the usual diagonal and product functors are in fact obtained from the usual ones as follows. Let
|s^{-1}(i)|
denote the cardinality of the subset
s^{-1}(i)
in
\underline{n}
. Then the adjoint pairs of functors for
i=1,\ldots,m

\Delta^{|s^{-1}(i)|}\dashv\sqcap^{|s^{-1}(i)|}:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}^{\times|s^{-1}(i)|}

can be combined to produce an adjoint pair of functors

\Delta^{|s^{-1}(1)|}\times\cdots\times\Delta^{|s^{-1}(m)|}\dashv\sqcap^{|s^{-1}(1)|}\times\cdots\times\sqcap^{|s^{-1}(m)|}

from
\mathscr{A}^{\times m}
to
\mathscr{A}^{s^{-1}(1)\times\cdots\times s^{-1}(m)}=\mathscr{A}^{\times n}
.
One can verify from the definitions that

\displaystyle\Delta^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{|s^{-1}(1)|}\times\cdots\times\Delta^{|s^{-1}(m)|}

\displaystyle\sqcap^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\sqcap^{|s^{-1}(1)|}\times\cdots\times\sqcap^{|s^{-1}(m)|}.

In particular, there is an adjunction
\Delta^{(s)}\dashv\sqcap^{(s)}:\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\to\mathscr{A}^{\times n}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

[
15
, Theorem 1]
There exists a well-defined functor

Surj^{op}\to\text{Monad}_{\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}

given by sending the set
\underline{n}
to the monad
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}}\right)
.

The proof of this theorem is technical, involving many constructions that are somewhat hidden in our notation for monads arising from
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules. The core of the proof involves producing a morphism of monads taking
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}}\right)
to
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}}\right)
. It may be helpful for the reader to know that there are really only three key monad morphisms used in the proof.

1.

Since
\operatorname{cr}^{n}
and
\operatorname{cr}^{m}
are obtained from the
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module
(\theta^{n},\mathscr{A}^{\times n})
and the
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module
(\theta^{m},\mathscr{A}^{\times m})
, respectively, there is no direct comparison of
\operatorname{cr}^{n}
and
\operatorname{cr}^{m}
via modules. Given a surjection
s:\underline{n}\to\underline{m}
, we first produce a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module,
(\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{s}}\hskip 0.92505pt)^{*}(\theta^{n})\,,\mathscr{A}^{\times n})
, from the
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module
(\theta^{n},\mathscr{A}^{\times n})
. There is a module morphism from
(\theta^{m},\mathscr{A}^{\times m})
to
(\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{s}}\hskip 0.92505pt)^{*}(\theta^{n})\,,\mathscr{A}^{\times n})
, and we apply Theorem
5.5
to obtain a map of the associated monads.

2.

The
m
-cubical diagram giving rise to the monad
\mathbb{T}^{m}_{\mathscr{M}}\big(\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}(\theta^{n})\big)
can be identified as a sub-cube of the
n
-cubical diagram used to compute the
n
-th cocross effect. There is an induced map between the iterated cofibers of these cubes.

3.

The final ingredient in the proof is a relationship between the different diagonal functors needed to evaluate the
m
-th and
n
-th cocross effects on their diagonals. The adjunction between
\Delta^{(s)}
and
\sqcap^{(s)}
produces the desired map of monads, using Lemma
2.9
.

Proof.

We proved in Proposition
6.2
that
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}}\right)
is indeed a monad on
\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}\text{,}\mathscr{M})
, so it remains to prove that for every surjection
s:\underline{n}\to\underline{m}
, there is a morphism of monads taking
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}}\right)
to
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}}\right)
.

The surjection
s
induces a functor
\mathcal{P}{(s)}:\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)\to\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
given by sending
\mathscr{U}\in\text{Ob}\,\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
to
s^{-1}(\mathscr{U})
. This in turn induces a functor
\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}:\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.80504pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.80504pt)}\to\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.80504pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.80504pt)}
given by sending a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure
\theta
on
\mathscr{A}
to its precomposition with
\text{Id}_{\mathscr{A}}\times\mathcal{P}(s)
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\text{Id}_{\mathscr{A}}\times\mathcal{P}(s)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta}

The resulting composite is a
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-module structure precisely because
s^{-1}
preserves intersections and the empty set, by Proposition
5.4
.

To recover McCarthy’s result, first note that there is a commuting diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{n}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times n}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta^{(s)}\times\mathcal{P}(s)\,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta^{(s)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta^{n}}

Rephrasing slightly in terms of the functor
\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}
, we see that the functor
\Delta^{(s)}
is a morphism of
\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)
-modules from
(\mathscr{A}^{\times m},\theta^{m})
to
(\mathscr{A}^{\times n},\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}(\theta^{n}))
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\times\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.92505pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.92505pt)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{A}^{\times n}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta^{(s)}\times\text{Id}_{\mathcal{P}(\hskip 0.45003pt\underline{\text{m}}\hskip 0.45003pt)}\,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta^{(s)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}(\theta^{n})}

Applying Theorem
5.5
now produces a map of monads on
\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\text{,}\mathscr{M})
,

\mathbb{T}^{m}_{\mathscr{M}}\left(\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}\theta^{n}\right)\to\mathbb{T}^{m}_{\mathscr{M}}\left(\theta^{m}\right).

Recall from the proof of Theorem
5.5
that the monad morphism is induced by the functor

\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}:\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\text{,}\mathscr{M})\to\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\text{,}\mathscr{M})

given by precomposition with
\Delta^{(s)}
, and the natural transformation

\text{T}^{\theta^{m}}_{\mathscr{M}}\left(\Delta^{{(s)}}\right)^{*}\Rightarrow\left(\Delta^{{(s)}}\right)^{*}\text{T}^{\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}\theta^{n}}_{\mathscr{M}},

which is the identity natural transformation.

Now consider the monads given by pre- and post-composing with the diagonal and product functors,
\Delta^{(s)}
and
\sqcap^{(s)}
, respectively. Since
\Delta^{(s)}\dashv\sqcap^{(s)}
is an adjoint pair,
\left(\sqcap^{(s)}\right)^{*}\dashv\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}
is also an adjoint pair, with
\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}
now being the right adjoint. Consider the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}^{\times n}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}\text{,}\mathscr{M}).}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fun}(\mathscr{A}^{\times m}\text{,}\mathscr{M})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\text{T}^{\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}\theta^{n}}_{\mathscr{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\text{T}^{\theta^{n}}_{\mathscr{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\Delta^{n}\right)^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\sqcap^{n})^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\sqcap^{m}\right)^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\text{T}^{\theta^{m}}_{\mathscr{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\Delta^{m}\right)^{\ast}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta\;}

The existence of a morphism of monads

\eta:\left(\text{T}^{\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}\theta^{n}}_{\mathscr{M}},\mu_{s},\eta_{s}\right)\to\left(\text{T}^{\theta^{n}}_{\mathscr{M}},\mu,\eta\right)

is a consequence of Proposition
5.4
. The existence of the natural transformation
\alpha
follows by noting that

\sqcap^{n}=\sqcap^{m}\sqcap^{(s)},

so that
\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}\left(\sqcap^{n}\right)^{*}=\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}\left(\sqcap^{(s)}\right)^{*}\left(\sqcap^{m}\right)^{*}
.
Whiskering the unit

of the adjunction
\left(\sqcap^{(s)}\right)^{*}\dashv\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}
on the right with
\left(\sqcap^{m}\right)^{*}
gives rise to a natural transformation

\alpha=u^{(s)}*\left(\sqcap^{m}\right)^{*}:\left(\sqcap^{m}\right)^{*}\Rightarrow\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}\left(\sqcap^{n}\right)^{*}.

We are now in the context of Lemma
2.9
, with

L=(\sqcap^{n})^{*},\hskip 9.24994ptR=(\Delta^{n})^{*},\hskip 9.24994ptT=\text{T}^{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}\theta^{n}},

L^{\prime}=\left(\sqcap^{m}\right)^{*},\hskip 9.24994ptR^{\prime}=(\Delta^{m})^{*},\hskip 9.24994ptT^{\prime}=\text{T}^{\mathscr{M}}_{\theta^{m}},

and

F=\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*},\hskip 9.24994pt\tau_{L}=\alpha,\hskip 9.24994pt\tau_{R}=\text{Id}_{(\Delta^{n})^{*}}.

To ensure that we can apply Lemma
2.9
, we need to show that

\left(\left(\Delta^{(s)}\right)^{*}\,{*}\,\varepsilon^{n}\right)\left(\alpha\,{*}\,\left(\Delta^{n}\right)^{*}\right)=\varepsilon^{m}\,{*}\,(\Delta^{(s)})^{*}

and

\left((\Delta^{m})^{*}\,{*}\,\alpha\right)u^{m}=u^{n},

where
\varepsilon^{k}
and
u^{k}
denotes the counit and unit of the adjunction
(\sqcap^{k})^{*}\dashv(\Delta^{k})^{*}
for all
k
. The second of these equations is an immediate consequence of the fact that the adjunction
(\sqcap^{n})^{*}\dashv(\Delta^{n})^{*}
is the composite
(\sqcap^{m})^{*}\dashv(\Delta^{m})^{*}
and
\big(\sqcap^{(s)}\big)^{*}\dashv\big(\Delta^{(s)}\big)^{*}
, while the proof of the second requires further that we apply the triangle identity for the counit of the adjunction
\big(\sqcap^{(s)}\big)^{*}\dashv\big(\Delta^{(s)}\big)^{*}
.

Thus there is a morphism of the monads whose underlying functors are the composites along the bottom and top of the diagram above:

(\Delta^{m})^{*}\text{T}^{\mathscr{M}}_{\theta^{m}}(\sqcap^{m})^{*}\Rightarrow(\Delta^{n})^{*}\text{T}^{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathcal{P}(s)^{*}\theta^{n}}(\sqcap^{n})^{*}.

The end result, composing this monad morphism with
\eta
, is a natural transformation

\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}}=(\Delta^{m})^{*}\text{T}^{\theta^{m}}_{\mathscr{M}}(\sqcap^{m})^{*}\Rightarrow(\Delta^{n})^{*}\text{T}^{\theta^{n}}_{\mathscr{M}}(\sqcap^{n})^{*}=\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}}

underlying a monad morphism from
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{m}}\right)
to
\left(\text{T}_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\mu_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}},\eta_{\operatorname{cr}^{n}}\right)

as desired.

∎
