Sojourn times of Markov symmetric processes in continuous time.
Pechersky E.A, Presman E.L, Yambartsev A.A

Abstract
The symmetric birth and death process in the integers { 1 , … , N } \{1,\ldots,N\} with linear rates is studied. The process moves slowly and spends more time in the neighborhood of the state 1. It represents our attempt at explaining the asymmetry between amounts of matter and antimatter by the inhomogeneity of the sojourn time. The state of the process reflects a relative frequency of an antimatter amount. The observed matter-antimatter imbalance in the Universe we consider a result of stochastic competition between them. The amount of matter significantly exceeds the amount of antimatter, which corresponds to the lower states of the process, and the path toward matter-antimatter equilibrium can be very long.

Introduction

1.1
Markov process

Markov Process.

We study a continuous time Markov process
X^{N}(t)
with the state space
\mathcal{N}=\{1,...,N\}
and piecewise constant paths. We consider two kinds of the initial state, it is either a point from
\mathcal{N}
or the stationary distribution (
2.3
) on
\mathcal{N}
.
The jumps of the process with non-zero transition rates are
k\to k+1
and
k\to k-1
with the rates
\lambda_{k,k+1},\lambda_{k,k-1}
if
1<k<N
, and
1\to 2,N\to N-1
with the rates
\lambda_{1,2}=1,\lambda_{N,N-1}=N
. The dynamics of the process is described by the
site-symmetry
generator with rates
\lambda_{k,k+1}=\lambda_{k,k-1}=k
for
k\neq 1,N
, thus, in this case we have the
edge-asymmetric
,
\lambda_{k,k+1}\neq\lambda_{k+1,k}
, see (
2.2
). It means that the considered processes
X^{N}
are space non-homogeneous.

Invariant measures.

Note that the corresponding
embedded
discrete-time Markov chain
\widehat{X}^{N}
is a symmetric simple random walk over
\mathcal{N}
with reflections at the boundaries
1
and
N
(see Section
2.2
). However, the continuous-time process
X^{N}
is far from any symmetry. As will be shown, despite the property of symmetry
\lambda_{k,k+1}=\lambda_{k,k-1}
the process
X^{N}
spends most of its time in the vicinity of the point
0
. That is, the distribution of
X^{N}(t)
is concentrated in the neighborhood of
0
, while the embedded process
\widehat{X}^{N}
has a uniform distribution over the set
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{1,N\}
. Both
X^{N}
and
\widehat{X}^{N}
satisfy detailed balance with respect to their stationary distribution.

Law of Large Numbers.

We study a sequence
\xi^{N}
of the Markov processes obtained by the scaling by
N
of the original processes
X^{N}
, that is
\xi^{N}:=\frac{X^{N}}{N}\in{\mathfrak{N}}^{N}=\{\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},...,1\}
.
We show that if the initial state is one-pointed then the mean value of
\xi^{N}
tends to the constant path equal to the limiting initial value
\xi^{N}(0)
with growing
N
.
And if the initial state is sampled according to the stationary distribution then the limit of the stationary distribution of
\xi^{N}
shows the effect of the concentration near the path identically equal to 0.

Large Deviations.

Finally, the rate function of the large deviations is obtained by the method described in
[
9
]
. For any given
x_{0},x_{T}\in[0,1]
we find the optimal path
\gamma(t),t\in[0,T]
starting at
\gamma(0)=x_{0}
and taking value
x_{T}
at the end of time interval
\gamma(T)=x_{T}
. We show that the optimal path is a parabola.

1.2
Motivations

The vast majority of laws in physics are related to a certain symmetry. Often, symmetries imply the existence of conservation laws, such as the well-known conservation laws in mechanics and the conservation of the electric charge.

However, the most impressive discoveries are associated with asymmetric manifestations in nature. Many asymmetries have been found in the microcosm that obeys quantum laws.

One well-known phenomenon is the asymmetry between matter and antimatter or the matter-antimatter imbalance. It is an impressive problem that has not yet been solved. The attempt to find an answer to this problem is based on the search for a violation of some symmetry in physical law, such as the violation of the law of conservation of baryon charges (see, for example,
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
). According to
[
3
]
this asymmetry is a reason for the matter-antimatter imbalance.

There is another reason for the asymmetry: randomness inherent in the system. Randomness can cause fluctuations, often quite large.

Very often, the asymmetry of physical processes caused by the random behavior of the system expresses itself through a phenomenon of phase transition. The phase transition is associated with very different behaviors of the same physical system on opposite sides of a “critical point” of some system parameter. Usually, a phase transition is related to the randomness created by fluctuations in the system or the system environment.

Here we propose another reason for the asymmetry. It is based in fluctuations modeled as a spatially inhomogeneous continuous-time Markov process. The asymmetry arises due to distinctions in the occupation time spent by the Markov process in different states.

The state-space of the process under consideration is a finite set
\mathcal{N}=\{1,...,N\}
, and the process dynamics creates piecewise constant trajectories with jumps
+1
or
-1
. The transition probabilities to the left and right neighbors from any state
2<i<N-1
are equal, but the transition rate linearly increases with the growth of
i
. The process exhibits the asymmetry related to the sojourn time. In the area close to
1\in\mathcal{N}
the process spends much more time than in the states near the
N
. Assuming the process describes the particle dynamics, it means that if a particle enters a region close to
1\in\mathcal{N}
due to the fluctuations, it takes a long time before leaving this region.

This work is the first step toward explaining the asymmetry between matter and antimatter due to the fluctuations created by a stochastic symmetric competition between matter and antimatter. We assume that violations of baryon-antibaryon symmetry arise in the course of Markovian dynamics. The breaking of symmetry occurs when the process path is constant during some time. The longer the constant period, the higher the probability of violation.

This effect can be sharper if we consider Markov dynamics out of the average in the corresponding direction. Such an event may have a small probability. However, it may strengthen the studied effect. This study belongs to the area of theory probability called
large deviations theory
.

Another motivation comes from our study of large deviations for birth-death processes with polynomial transition rates. In the series of papers,
[
15
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
12
]
, we consider the birth-death processes where the transition rates
\lambda_{i,i+1}
and
\lambda_{i,i-1}
depends on the state
i
polynomially:
\lambda_{i,i+1}=Pi^{\alpha}
and
\lambda_{i,i+1}=Qi^{\beta}
. The papers
[
14
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
12
]
explore the method of exchange of measures suggested in
[
15
]
in the cases when
\alpha\neq\beta
or
\alpha=\beta,P\neq Q
. But all our attempts have failed to extend the method for the case
\alpha=\beta,P=Q
.

The model considered here corresponds to the birth-death processes (on the interval
0,1,\ldots,N
) with
\alpha=\beta=1,P=Q
, where the method of
[
9
]
was employed.

2
Model. Generator

The main subject of our research is the sequence (in
N
, as
N\to\infty
) of Markov processes
(X^{N}(t),t\in[0,T])
for some fixed
T>0
. The upper index means that the states of the process belong to the set
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
. The stochastic dynamics of
X^{N}(t)
is controlled by the generators
G^{N}
, which is an operator on the set of probe functions
\mathcal{F}^{N}=\{f:\>\mathcal{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
:

G^{N}f(m)=\lambda m\bigl(f(m+1)-f(m)\bigr){\mathbbm{1}}_{m<N}(m)+\lambda m\bigl(f(m-1)-f(m)\bigr){\mathbbm{1}}_{m>1}(m),

(2.1)

where
{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{B}}(\cdot)
is an indicator for subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{N}

{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{B}}(m)=\begin{cases}1,\mbox{if }m\in\mathcal{B},\\
0,\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

and the rates
\lambda_{m,m+1}
and
\lambda_{m,m-1}
are

\displaystyle\lambda_{m,m+1}=\lambda_{m,m-1}=\lambda m,\ m=2,\ldots,N-1,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,2}=\lambda,\ \lambda_{N,N-1}={\lambda}{N}.

Note that the endpoints of
\mathcal{N}
are reflective. The initial state
X^{N}(0)
has some distribution on
\mathcal{N}
. In particular, it can be a measure concentrated at the one point state
m\in\mathcal{N}
.

Remark 2.1
.

It can be seen from the form of
G^{N}
that the Markov process has jump-wise paths. The jumps are equal to
\pm 1
. The intensities of jumps from the state
m
depend on the value of
m
, and the intensities to the left and right are equal if
m\in\{2,...,N-1\}
.
Thus, we have symmetrical jumps except for the endpoints of
\mathcal{N}
.

2.1
Stationary distribution

Detailed balance allows finding stationary distributions of the processes. The stationary distribution concentrates near 0 as
N
increases.

Denote by
{\pi^{N}(m),m\in\mathcal{N}}
, the stationary measure of
X^{N}(t)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

X^{N}(t)
satisfies the detailed balance with respect to the stationary distribution.
\pi^{N}
is given by

\pi^{N}(m)=\frac{\frac{1}{m}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k}},\ \ m\in\mathcal{N}.

(2.3)

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=\{1,...,M\}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
. Let
h(N)
be a sequence of integers such that

\frac{\ln(h(N))}{\ln(N)}\to 1,\mbox{ and }\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{N}{h(N)}>1.

Assume that
M=h(N)
. Then

\pi^{N}(\mathcal{M})\to 1,\mbox{ as }N\to\infty,

Proof.

The proof uses the detailed balance. The balance equations have the following shape (see (
2.1
))

\pi^{N}(m)m=\pi^{N}(m+1)(m+1),\mbox{ for all }1\leq m\leq N-1.

For
m>1
we obtained

\pi^{N}(m)=\pi^{N}(1)\frac{1}{m}.

Since
\sum_{m=1}^{N}\pi^{N}(m)=1
we have

\pi^{N}(1)=\frac{1}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{1}{m}},

and

\pi^{N}(m)=\frac{\frac{1}{m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{1}{m}},\ \ m\in\mathcal{N}.

(2.4)

The probability of the set
\mathcal{M}=\{1,...,M\}
is

\pi^{N}(\mathcal{M})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\pi^{N}(m)=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{1}{m}}.

(2.5)

Here we use the Euler representation of the partial sum of the Harmonic series
H_{K}=\sum_{m=1}^{K}\frac{1}{m}=\ln K+\gamma+\varepsilon_{K}
, where
\gamma
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant and
K\varepsilon_{K}\to\frac{1}{2}
as
K\to\infty
. Then

\pi^{N}(\mathcal{M})=\frac{\ln M+\gamma+\varepsilon_{M}}{\ln N+\gamma+\varepsilon_{N}}=\frac{\ln h(N)+\gamma+\varepsilon_{M}}{\ln N+\gamma+\varepsilon_{N}}\to 1

(2.6)

as
N\to\infty

\blacksquare

2.2
Embedded chain

The stationary distribution of the embedded Markov chain
\widehat{X}
is
fundamentally different from the stationary distribution of the process
X^{N}
.

Consider the embedded chain
\widehat{X}^{N}(k)
corresponding to
X^{N}(t)
with initial value
\widehat{X}^{N}(0)=m
. The chain
\widehat{X}^{N}(k)
instantly changes its state immediately upon the occurence of jumps
X^{N}(t)
, regardless of the time the process spends in the state before the jump. The discrete-time dynamics is determined by the following probability transition matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&\displaystyle{\frac{\lambda_{1,2}}{R_{1}}}&0&0&0&...&0&0\\
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{2,1}}{R_{2}}&0&\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{2,3}}{R_{2}}&0&0&...&0&0\\
0&\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{3,2}}{R_{3}}&0&\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{3,4}}{R_{3}}&0&...&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&...&0&\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{N-1,N}}{R_{N-1}}\\
0&0&0&0&0&...&\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{N,N-1}}{R_{N}}&0\end{pmatrix},

where

R_{m}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{m,m+1}+\lambda_{m,m-1}=2\lambda m,\mbox{ if }m\in\{2,...,N-1\},\\
\lambda_{1,2}=\lambda,\mbox{ if }m=1,\\
\lambda_{N,N-1}=\lambda N,\mbox{ if }m=N.\end{cases}

The value of
R_{m}
is the rate at which
X^{N}(t)
leaves the state
m
. For the case
m\in\{2,...,N-1\}
the transition probability is
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{m,m+1}}{R_{m}}=\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{m,m-1}}{R_{m}}=\frac{1}{2}
, and for
m=1
and
m=N
the transition probabilities are
\displaystyle{\frac{\lambda}{R_{1}}=\frac{\lambda N}{R_{N}}}=1
.
The two transition probabilities equal to 1 represent the reflection from states 1 and
N
.

Using the detailed balance equations, we obtain the stationary distribution
\widehat{\pi}
of
\widehat{X}(k)

\widehat{\pi}(1)=\widehat{\pi}(N)=\frac{1}{2(N-1)},\ \widehat{\pi}(m)=\frac{1}{N-1},\ m=2,...,N-1.

Remark 2.3
.

The chain
\widehat{X}(k)
, with initial state
\widehat{X}(0)
is a simple random walk in
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
with reflections on the boundaries. If the current state
\widehat{X}(k-1)\in\{2,...,N-1\}
, then the next state
\widehat{X}(k)=\widehat{X}(k-1)+Y_{k}
, where the independent increments
Y_{k}
take values
\pm 1
uniformly
{\mathbf{P}}(Y_{k}=1)={\mathbf{P}}(Y_{k}=-1)=1/2
, and
{\mathbf{P}}(Y_{k}=1)=1\ \left({\mathbf{P}}(Y_{k}=-1)=1\right)
on the boundary state, when
\widehat{X}(k-1)=1\ \left(\widehat{X}(k-1)=N\right)
.

The dynamics of the process
(X^{N}(t))
on continuous-time changes dramatically compared to the embedded chain
\widehat{X}(k)
. The occupation (or sojourn) time plays a crucial role in the nature of the dynamics. Our aim is to understand the occupation time behavior on different subsets of
\mathcal{N}
.

For any
N
the process
X^{N}(t)
is homogeneous in time, but non-homogeneous in space. Thus, the sojourn time in any subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{N}
may vary depending on
N
. We have shown that for any
M<N
which is not very close to
N
the time of the sojourn probability in
\mathcal{M}=\{1,\ldots,M\}
tends to 1 as
N,M\to\infty
(see Theorem
2.2
).

3
Scaling

In this section we consider the scaled version of the processes
X^{N}
:
\xi^{N}(t)=\frac{X^{N}(t)}{N}
. The state space of
\xi^{N}
is
{\mathfrak{N}}^{N}=\left\{\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},...,1\right\}
. The generator of
\xi^{N}(t)
is the following operator (cf. (
2.1
)) acting on the function space
{\mathfrak{F}}^{N}=\{{f}:\>{\mathfrak{N}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
for all
\gamma\in{\mathfrak{N}}^{N}\subset[0,1]

g^{N}f(\mathscr{\gamma})=\lambda N\gamma\Bigl(f\Bigl(\gamma+\frac{1}{N}\Bigr)-f(\gamma)\Bigr){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\gamma<1\}}(\gamma)+\lambda N\gamma\Bigl(f\Bigl(\gamma-\frac{1}{N}\Bigr)-f(\gamma)\Bigr){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\gamma>\frac{1}{N}\}}(\gamma).

(3.1)

Denote
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}
the process
\xi^{N}
with the initial point
\gamma^{N}\in{\mathfrak{N}}^{N}
, that is
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(0)=\gamma^{N}
. Let
\mathbf{D}^{N}[0,T]
be the set of all trajectories of
\xi^{N}
during the time interval
[0,T]
and let

\mathbf{D}[0,T]=\bigcup_{N}\mathbf{D}^{N}[0,T].

Since
\mathbf{D}^{N}[0,T]\subset\mathbf{D}[0,T]
then all measures
\mu^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}
of the prcesses
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}
are concentrated in
\mathbf{D}[0,T]
. However, the set
\mathbf{D}[0,T]
does not contain all continuous functions. Let
\bar{\mathbf{D}}[0,T]
be completion of
\mathbf{D}[0,T]
with respect to the uniform metric. Then
\mathbf{C}[0,T]\subseteq\bar{\mathbf{D}}[0,T]
.

Let
(\gamma^{N})
be the sequence of the initial points such that
\gamma^{N}\to\gamma\in(0,1]
, for example
\gamma^{N}=\frac{\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor}{N}
.
In what follows we will show that the measures
\mu^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}
converge to the measure
\mu_{\gamma}
concentrated on the path set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}:\>\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(0)=\gamma\}
. Further the bold letters we reserve for the functions. For example,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)=\gamma
means that the function
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}
at the point
t
takes the value
\gamma
. We hope this notation will not confuse a reader.

4
The Law of Large Numbers

In this section, we study the dynamics of a sequence of the processes
\xi^{N}
as
N
grows.
One of the purposes of this study is to determine the evolutionary behavior of the scaled processes
\xi^{N}
with the initial state from
{\mathfrak{N}}=\bigcup_{N}{\mathfrak{N}^{N}}
.
Recall that the process
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(t)
denotes the process
\xi^{N}(t)
with the initial
\xi^{N}(0)=\gamma^{N}\in{\mathfrak{N}}
. The measure
\mu_{\gamma^{N}}
which is the distribution of
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(t)
is concentrated in the path set
\mathbf{D}^{N}[0,T]
.

We consider two cases for the law of large numbers: the single-point initial distribution (Section
4.1
) and the stationary initial distribution (Section
4.2
).

4.1
The single-point initial distribution

We introduce a set of constant functions. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)\equiv\gamma\in[0,1]
, where
\gamma
is a fixed number. Let
\gamma^{N}
be a sequence of the initial states,
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(0)=\gamma^{N}
, such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\gamma^{N}=\gamma\in[0,1]
(for example, we can choose
\gamma^{N}=\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor/N
). Here we prove the law of large numbers for
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(t)
when
N
goes to infinity.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
\gamma\in[0,1]

the relation

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)=0.

(4.1)

holds true.

Proof.

Let
J^{N}(t)
be the number of the jumps of
\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(t)
on
(0,t]
. Consider an auxiliary process
Y(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{J^{N}(t)}Y_{i},
on the time interval
[0,T]
, where
Y_{i}
are i.i.d. sequence of random variables with
P(Y_{i}=1)=1-P(Y_{i}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
. Let
Y_{N}(t):=\frac{1}{N}Y(t)
.
Observe that the following inequality holds:

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\gamma_{N}+Y_{N}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)\right|<\varepsilon\right)\leq{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)\right|<\varepsilon\right).

Note that there exists
N_{\varepsilon}
such that
|\gamma^{N}-\gamma|<\varepsilon
for all
N>N_{\varepsilon}
. For these
N
we have

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|Y_{N}(t)\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)<{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\gamma^{N}+Y_{N}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)\right|<\varepsilon\right).

To finish the proof of (
4.1
) we use Kolmogorov inequality for the maximum of sum of i.i.d random variables (see, for example,
[
5
]
, Theorem 22.4). We have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|Y_{N}(t)\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left.{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{J^{N}(t)}Y_{i}\Bigr|\geq\varepsilon N\ \right|\ J^{N}(T)=n\right){\mathbf{P}}\left(J^{N}(T)=n\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left.{\mathbf{P}}\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{i}\Bigr|\geq\varepsilon N\ \right|\ J^{N}(T)=n\right){\mathbf{P}}\left(J^{N}(T)=n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{n}{4\varepsilon^{2}N^{2}}{\mathbf{P}}(J^{N}(T)=n)={\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}N^{2}}\mathbb{E}(J^{N}(T))\leq\frac{NT}{4\varepsilon^{2}N^{2}}}\to 0\mbox{ as }N\to\infty.

Finally,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\xi^{N}_{\gamma^{N}}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)\right|<\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-{\mathbf{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|Y_{N}(t)\right|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)

(4.2)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{T}{\varepsilon^{2}N}\to 1,\mbox{ as }N\to\infty.

This finishes the proof of (
4.1
).
\Box

Remark 4.2
.

We apply the inequality
\mathbb{E}(J^{N}(T))\leq NT
because the intensity of the jumps is maximal near the boundary on 1.

4.2
Stationary initial distribution

We now turn to the case when the initial state is distributed according to the stationary measure. To this end, we introduce the constant process
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)
as before. That is
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)\equiv\gamma\in[0,1]
for
t\in[0,T]
if
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(0)=\gamma
. The distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)
satisfies the following conditions: for any
u\in(0,1]
the probabilities
{\mathbf{P}}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)<u)=1
and
{\mathbf{P}}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)>u)=0
. The described property means that the process
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t)
is concentrated on the line
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)\equiv 0
.

Let
\xi_{\mathbf{st}}^{N}(t)
be the piecewise constant process on
\mathbf{D}^{N}[0,T]
with the initial distribution
\pi^{N}
. Note that the distribution
\pi^{N}
is defined on the set
\mathcal{N}
, but we will maintain the same notation for the stationary measure on
{\mathfrak{N}}^{N}
. Then the distribution
\xi_{\mathbf{st}}^{N}(t)
at any
t\in[0,T]
is also
\pi^{N}
since
\pi^{N}
is stationary. Recall that the generator of
\xi_{\mathbf{st}}^{N}(t)
is (
3.1
).

Theorem 4.3
.

\xi^{N}_{\mathbf{st}}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}\mbox{ \ weakly}

(4.3)

as
N\to\infty

The proof of the theorem is based on the result of the paper
[
6
]
on the convergence of integrals of finite-dimensional distributions of random processes. The paper
[
6
]
states conditions for the weak convergence of the functionals

\Phi(\xi_{st}^{N})=\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(\xi_{\mathbb{st}}^{N}(u)\mathrm{d}u
to
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}})=\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(u))\mathrm{d}u
, where
\varphi
is the continuous function from
\mathbf{C}[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

The mentioned theorem in the considered case is

Theorem 4.4

(Borovkov, Pechersky,
[
6
]
)
.

Let

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\dots\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}(\xi^{N}_{\mathbb{st}}(t_{i})<u_{i})\right)\mathrm{d}t_{1},...,\mathrm{d}t_{\ell}=\int_{0}^{T}\dots\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\zeta}}(t_{i})<u_{i})\right)\mathrm{d}t_{1},...,\mathrm{d}t_{\ell}

for any finite number
t_{1},...,t_{\ell}\in[0,T]
then the distribution of

5
Large Deviations

This section is devoted to studying the process deviations far from average values. This study belongs to the well-developed area of probability theory known as the theory of
Large Deviations
. Here we use the techniques developed in
[
9
]
. We apply and adapt these results with additional calculations corresponding to the peculiarities of our model. We also refer a reader to
[
11
]
and
[
4
]
, where the method was applied to some similar Markov processes. Thus, here we only pass through some points related to our process, and we would like to avoid cumbersome constructions used in the proof of LDP.

Remark 5.1
.

The one-dimensional case of the generalized Ehrenfest model studied in
[
11
]
is very similar to the model studied here with a slight difference. Despite the differences in the generators of Fleming semigroups the corresponding Hamiltonians are similar. We will refer to this paper passing through the proof of LDP.

Let
\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,T]
be a set of continuously differentiable function on
[0,T]
with values from
[0,1]
.
The following two terms are our main interest.

Rate function (functional)

I:\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\to\mathbb{R}
for
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\in\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,T]
, see (
5.14
).

Hamiltonian

H(\gamma,\varkappa)
,
with
\gamma\in[0,1]
and
\varkappa\in\mathbb{R}
, see (
5.5
).

For any
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\in\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,T]
the rate function
I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})
measures deviations of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}
from the process average.
I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})
is approximately the logarithm of the following probability of the deviation
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)-\mathbf{E}\xi^{N}(t)
:

I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})=\lim_{\varepsilon}\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\ln{\mathbf{P}}\left(\xi^{N}(\cdot)\in U_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})\right),

where
U_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})
is
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}
.
If
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)=\lim_{N}\mathbf{E}\xi^{N}(t)
then
I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})=0
.
The existence of this limit is known as a
local large deviation principle (LLDP)

Definition 5.2
.

Let the sequence of random processes
\xi^{N}(\,\cdot\,)
be
defined as in (
4.1
).
We say that this sequence satisfies a
local large deviation principle

with an
LD
functional

I\,:\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,T]\to[0,\infty]
if for all
f\in\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,T]

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\ln\mathbf{P}(\xi^{N}(\,\cdot\,)\in U_{\varepsilon}(f))=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\liminf\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\ln\mathbf{P}(\xi^{N}(\,\cdot\,)\in U_{\varepsilon}(f))=-I(f),

where

U_{\varepsilon}(f)=\{g\in\mathbf{D}[0,T]:\ \rho(f,g)<\varepsilon\},\ \mbox{ and }\ \rho(f,g):=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|f(t)-g(t)\right|.

Frequently, the existence of this limit together with exponential tightness verify the
large deviation principle (LDP)
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
\xi^{N}(\,\cdot\,)
be sequence of random processes defined as in (
4.1
). We say that it satisfies a large deviation principle with an
LD
functional
I\,:\;\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,T]\to(0,\infty]
if, whenever measurable set
\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,T]
, we have that

\begin{array}[]{c}\limsup\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\ln\mathbf{P}(\,\xi^{N}(\,\cdot\,)\in\mathbb{B}\,)\leq-I({\rm{Cl}}(\mathbb{B})),\\
\liminf\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\ln\mathbf{P}(\,\xi^{N}(\,\cdot\,)\in\mathbb{B}\,)\geq-I({\rm{Int}}(\mathbb{B})).\end{array}

This way worked well in papers
[
12
]
–
[
15
]
, where the method to obtain the LLDP was proposed and applied for birth-and-death processes. But we couldn’t extend the method for the process considered here, so we opted for the operator method proposed by Fend & Kurtz
[
9
]
.

It seems the shortest way to find the rate function is Bryc’s theorem. It claims the following.

I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})=\sup_{\mathscr{L}}\left\{\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})-\Lambda(\mathscr{L})\right\},

(5.1)

where
\{\mathscr{L}\}
is a set of functionals on the process path set
\mathbf{D}[0,T]
, and

\Lambda(\mathscr{L})=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\ln\int\exp\{\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})\}{\mathbf{P}}(\xi^{N}\in\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})

for any functional
\mathscr{L}
. We have omitted the conditions and other details required for Bryc’s theorem (see Proposition 3.8 in
[
9
]
).

Unfortunately, this is not a very practical way. The main difficulty is related to the fact that we consider Markov-dependent random variables. In this case,
\Lambda(\mathscr{L})
is too general, and therefore, it is very rarely computable. Do the functionals
\Lambda(\mathscr{L})
form a semigroup acting on a set of functions? A positive answer to this question would construct a generator of this semigroup, which makes it possible to create a research method of
\Lambda(\mathscr{L})
.

5.1
Fleming semigroup

The above question is solved with the help of a (Fleming) semigroup, proposed by Fleming (
[
10
]
):

V_{t}^{N}{f}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{N}\ln\int\exp\{N{f}(\gamma)\}{\mathbf{P}}(\xi^{N}(t)\in\mathrm{d}{\gamma})\mid\xi^{N}(0)=\gamma_{0}),

(5.2)

where the probe functions belongs to the set of
C_{1}[0,1]
continuously differentiable function on
[0,1]
with values from
\mathbb{R}
,
f\in\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,1]
.
The problem now is to investigate the limiting semigroup

\mathbf{V}_{t}{f}(\gamma)=\lim_{N}V_{t}^{N}{f}(\gamma).

(5.3)

Since
V_{t}^{N}
is a semigroup it is reasonable to find its generator. The generator corresponding to
V_{t}^{N}
describes the Markov dynamics which is called
non-linear
and its generator is called
non-linear Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{N}
: if
\gamma\in{\mathfrak{N}}^{N}\setminus\{\frac{1}{N},1\}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{N}{f}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\exp\big\{-N{f}(\gamma)\big\}\left(\lambda N\gamma\left(\exp\left\{N{f}\left(\gamma+\tfrac{1}{N}\right)\right\}-\exp\big\{N{f}(\gamma)\big\}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\lambda N\gamma\left(\exp\left\{N{f}\left(\gamma-\tfrac{1}{N}\right)\right\}-\exp\big\{N{f}(\gamma)\big\}\right)\Big),

if
\gamma=\frac{1}{N}
, then

\mathcal{H}^{N}{f}(\tfrac{1}{N})=\frac{\lambda}{N}\exp\left\{-N{f}(\tfrac{1}{N})\right\}\left(\exp\left\{N{f}\left(\tfrac{2}{N}\right)\right\}-\exp\left\{N{f}(\tfrac{1}{N})\right\}\right),

and if
\gamma=1
, then

\mathcal{H}^{N}{f}(1)=\lambda\exp\left\{-N{f}(1)\right\}\left(\exp\left\{N{f}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{N}\right)\right\}-\exp\left\{N{f}(1)\right\}\right).

It is easy to see that the limiting operator
\mathcal{H}
should be

\mathcal{H}f(\gamma)=\lim_{N}\mathcal{H}^{N}{f}(\gamma)=\lambda\gamma\left(\exp\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(\gamma)\right\}-1\right)+\lambda\gamma\left(\exp\left\{-\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(\gamma)\right\}-1\right)=:H\left(\gamma,\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(\gamma)\right),

(5.4)

where the set of probe functions is the set
\mathbf{C}_{1}^{0}[0,1]
of continuously differentiable functions with zero derivative at 1,
\mathbf{C}_{1}^{0}[0,1]\subset\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,1]
. Using the notation
\varkappa=\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(\gamma)
we write the Hamiltonian

H(\gamma,\varkappa)=\lambda\gamma(e^{\varkappa}-1)+\lambda\gamma(e^{-\varkappa}-1).

(5.5)

The proof of the operator convergence

\mathcal{H}=\lim\mathcal{H}^{N}

in the
\mathbf{C}_{1}^{0}[0,1]
is not diffcult. First, we consider the convergence on
{\mathfrak{N}}
, where the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\gamma\in{\mathfrak{N}}}\left|\mathcal{H}^{N}{f}(\gamma)-\mathcal{H}f(\gamma)\right|=0,

(5.6)

is obvious.
Second, consider a sequence
(\gamma_{N})
where
\gamma_{N}\in{\mathfrak{N}}
such that
\gamma_{N}\to\gamma
for an arbitrary
\gamma\in\mathbf{[}0,1]
. Then

\lim_{N}\frac{f(\gamma_{N}+1/N)-f(\gamma_{N})}{1/N}=\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(\gamma)

and the derivative of
f
is continuous on
[0,1]
. Therefore (
5.6
) can be extended to

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\gamma\in[0,1]}\left|\mathcal{H}^{N}{f}(\gamma)-\mathcal{H}f(\gamma)\right|=0.

As can be seen the resulting function
\mathcal{H}f(\gamma)=H\left(\gamma,\frac{\mathrm{d}{f}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\right)
depends on the derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}{f}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}
, the first variable
\gamma
is the reason for the non-linearity of the dynamics.

Next considerations prove that
\mathcal{H}
is the generator of the semigroup
\mathbf{V}_{t}
.

5.2
Exponential compact containment

The
exponential compact containment
can be obtained directly from the definition for our case. The sequence
\xi^{N}
satisfy exponential compact containment condition in
\bar{\mathbf{D}}(0,T]
iff for each
a>0
there exists a compact
K_{a}\subset[0,1]
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln P(X_{n}(t)\notin K_{a}\mbox{ for some }t\leq T)\leq-a.

In our case this condition is easily verified taking
K_{a}=[0,1]
. Observe that it is not exponential tightness. But together with generator convergence and the comparison principle (see further for the definition) exponential containment will imply exponential tightness according to Theorem 6.14,
[
9
]
.

5.3
Fleming semigroup generator

We now consider when
\mathcal{H}
, (
5.4
), is the generator of the Fleming semigroup
\mathbf{V}_{t}
, (
5.3
). The proof is a rather cumbersome sequence of arguments. One of the important conditions for
\mathcal{H}
to be the generator is the existence of solutions to the equation
(I-\beta\mathcal{H})f=h
for each
h
and small positive
\beta
(Chapter 6,
[
9
]
).
It is so called the
viscosity equation
. This condition is difficult to verify. The
comparison principle
overcomes this difficulty.

Let us rewrite the equation using Hamiltoniam:

f(\gamma)-\beta H\left(\gamma,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(\gamma)\right)-h(\gamma)=0.

(5.7)

Let
u
and
v
be subsolution and supersolution of equation (
5.7
) (see Definition
5.4
in Appendix 1). We say that the equation verifies the

comparison principle
if for any subsolution
u
and supersolution
v
, it is verified that
u\leq v
.

The proof of the comparison principle for the case under study is given in Appendix 1, see Proposition
5.5
. When the comparison principle holds, if there exists a viscosity solution, it is unique.

5.4
Rate function. Variational representation.

We have established that Hamiltonian
H
is Fleming group generator. The next our goal is to represent the rate function
I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})
as Legendre transform of the Hamiltonian that is to obtain the
variational form
of the rate function

I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})=\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{\varkappa}\left\{\varkappa\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}(t)-H(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t),\varkappa)\right\}\mathrm{d}t.

(5.8)

This expression is difficult to obtain directly from Fleming semigroup terms (see (
5.2
), (
5.3
)) and require some results from control theory. The Fleming semigroup in the large deviation is interpreted as Nisio semigroup in control theory.
The proof of (
5.8
) uses a method developed in a number of works.
The detail presentation of the method is represented in Capter 8 from
[
9
]
, see also
[
11
]
,
[
4
]
, for the application of the method.

The method is a series of transformations of the generator
g^{N}
(
4.1
). The first non-linear transformation provided the Fleming semigroup
\mathbf{V}_{t}
and its generator is
H=\lambda\gamma\left(e^{\varkappa}+e^{-\varkappa}-2\right)
, see Section
5.1
and Section
5.3
.
The Fleming semigroup has the exponential form not easy to investigate. The last transformation is the Nisio semigroup construction.

5.4.1
Nisio Semigroup

In this stage the method shows that the Fleming semigroup coincides with the so-called
Nisio semigroup

\mathbf{W}(t)
from the control theory (see
[
9
]
, Chapter 8). The control theory provides the variational representation (
5.8
) for the rate function.

Before starting let us observe that
H(\gamma,\varkappa)
is a convex function on the second coordinate
\varkappa
, and

H(\gamma,\varkappa)=\sup_{\gamma}\left(\varkappa\gamma-L(\gamma,\varkappa)\right),

(5.9)

where

L(\gamma,\varkappa):=\sup_{\varkappa}\left(\varkappa\gamma-H(\gamma,\varkappa)\right).

(5.10)

The Nisio semigroup has the following form

\mathbf{W}(t)f(x)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{AC}[0,T],\\
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(0)=\gamma}f(\gamma(t))-\int_{0}^{t}L(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))ds,

(5.11)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{AC}[0,T]
, and
\mathcal{AC}[0,T]
is the set of all absolutely continuous paths on
[0,T]
.

A more easy approach is to use a
relaxed presentation
of Nisio semigroup instead of (
5.11
). Let
U=\{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}(\cdot)\}
and
\mathcal{M}_{m}(U)
be the space of Borel measures
\lambdabar
on
U\times[0,T]
satisfying
\lambdabar(U\times[0,t])=t
for all
t\in[0,T]
. The measure
\lambdabar
is known as a relaxed control (see detailed definition in Appendix 2).

The relaxed controls determine the Nisio semigroup as

\mathbf{\bar{W}}(t)f(x)=\sup_{(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}},\lambdabar),\ \boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(0)=x}f(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t))-\iint_{U\times[0,1]}L(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(s),u)\lambdabar(du\times ds),

(5.12)

5.4.2
Variational representation of rate function

Finally, the relation (
5.8
) follows from
[
4
]
, Theorem 3.1, which states that

1.

\mathbf{V}_{t}(f)=\bar{\mathbf{W}}(t)f

2.

I({\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}})=\inf_{\lambdabar:\ (\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}},\lambdabar)}\iint_{U\times[0,T]}L(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(s),u)\lambdabar(du\times ds)
;

3.

the rate function can be written as an action integral:

(5.13)

if

is absolutely continuous function.

We have omitted the required conditions.

5.5
Hamiltonian system

At that point, we have made four steps to obtain LDP and the rate function.

Step 1. Section
5.1
established the convergence of the sequence of operators
\mathcal{H}^{N}
, where we obtained the limiting operator
\mathcal{H}
.

Step 2. Section
5.2

Step 3. Section

with Appendix 1 established the comparison principle. It proves that the limiting operator
\mathcal{H}
generates the semigroup

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{V}(f)=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{V}_{t}(f))
.

According to Theorem 6.14 from
[

]
these three steps assure that the sequence of our processes is exponentially tight and satisfies an LDP with rate function expressed via the Fleming semigroup (see formulas for rate functions (5.30) and (5.31) in
[

]
). Practically the exponetial tightness implies that the rate function is a good rate function (see, for example, Theorem 3.7
[

This result does not provide a useful presentation for the rate function. The next fourth step of Feng & Kurtz theory provides the practical version of the rate function based on control theory.

Step 4. Section
5.4
with Appendix 2 state and checked conditions when the rate function
I
can be written as an action integral (

). (Condition 8.9 and 8.11 from
[

]
are stated in Appendix 2. They are easily verified. See, for example, Propositions 3.6 and 3.8 from

]

Here, based on a variational representation of the rate function, fortunately, we can obtain the rate functional (
5.14
) explicitly. Moreover, we can find the optimal path for the following rare event: starting at the point
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(0)
the process will stay at time
T
at the point
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(T)
far from their mean,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(0)\neq\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(T)
(see example in Appendix 3.)

Let us now find paths between fixed boundary conditions. To this end, we should investigate the following Hamiltonian system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\left(e^{\varkappa}-e^{-\varkappa}\right)\\
&\dot{\varkappa}=-\lambda\left(e^{\varkappa}+e^{-\varkappa}-2\right)\end{aligned}\right.

First equation of Hamiltonian system is the derivative on
\varkappa
,
\dot{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\varkappa}
, to find the supremum. The second one is Euler–Lagrange equation.

Rate function
: The first equation provides the explicit formula for the rate function. Let
z=e^{\kappa}
. For given functions
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}(t)
we obtain the quadratic equation on
z

z^{2}-\frac{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}}{\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}z-1=0,

with admissible solution

z(t)=e^{\kappa(t)}=\frac{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}}{2\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}}{2\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}\right)^{2}+1}.

Finally,

I(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}\ln\left(\frac{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}}{2\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}}{2\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}\right)^{2}+1}\right)-\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}+2\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}-\frac{(2\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})^{2}}{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}+\sqrt{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}^{2}+(2\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})^{2}}}\right]dt

(5.14)

Solution of the system
:
Let
z=e^{\varkappa}
then
\dot{\varkappa}=\frac{\dot{z}}{z}
. The second equation of the above system is

\frac{\dot{z}}{z}=-\lambda\left(z+\frac{1}{z}\right)+2\lambda,

or

\dot{z}=-\lambda(z-1)^{2}.

The solution is

z(t)=\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\lambda t-c_{1}}+1,&\mbox{ when }\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)\nequiv const;\\
1,&\mbox{ when }\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)\equiv const.\end{aligned}\right.

where
c_{1}
is some constant.

Now the first equation of the system (when
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\nequiv const
) is

\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\left(z(t)-\frac{1}{z(t)}\right)=\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda t-c_{1}}+\frac{1}{\lambda t-c_{1}+1}\right),

or

\frac{\mathrm{d}\ \ln(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}})}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda\left(\frac{1}{\lambda t-c_{1}}+\frac{1}{\lambda t-c_{1}+1}\right)

Then

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}(\lambda t-c_{1})(\lambda t-c_{1}+1)c_{2},&\mbox{ if }\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)\nequiv const\\
const,&\mbox{ if }\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)\equiv const\in[0,1].\end{array}\right.

where
c_{2}
is some positive constant. The parameters
c_{1},c_{2}
are uniquely determined by the boundary conditions
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(0),\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(T)
and by the fact that the solution
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{\gamma}}(t)\in[0,1]
when
t\in[0,T]
.

The examples of the solutions are in Appendix 3.

for every
\varphi\in\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,1]
and
x_{0}:=\mbox{argmin}(v(x)-\varphi(x))
we have
v(x_{0})-\beta H(x_{0},\varphi^{\prime}(x_{0}))-h(x_{0})\geq 0.

We say that
u
is a (viscosity) solution if it is both a sub and super solution.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\beta>0
and
h\in\mathbf{C}[0,1]
. Then the comparison principle holds true for

f(x)-\beta H(x,f^{\prime}(x))-h(x)=0.

(5.15)

Proof.
With small adaptation the proof follows
[
11
]
, Section 4. See especially
[
11
]
, Section 4.1, where comparison principle verified for a one-dimensional Erenfest model similar to our case.

For given
\beta>0
,
h\in\mathbf{C}[0,1]
let
u,v
be some sub- and super-solutions of (
5.15
). Choose the following penalized function
\Psi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(x-y)^{2}
. For a positive increasing parameter
\alpha
(for example, we can suppose
\alpha
as natural numbers) we define the following points
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}\in[0,1]

u(x_{\alpha})-v(y_{\alpha})-\frac{\alpha}{2}(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha})^{2}=\sup_{x,y\in E}\left\{u(x)-v(y)-\frac{\alpha}{2}(x-y)^{2}\right\}.

Since the functions
u,v
are bounded the limit
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha\Psi(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})
should be zero. All limit points of
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})
should take the form
(z,z)
such that

u(z)-v(z)=\sup_{x\in E}u(x)-v(x).

It is the general result, and we refer to Lemma 4 in
[
11
]
.

For such chosen sequence of points
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}
according to Proposition 2 in
[
11
]
we need to prove that

\liminf_{\alpha\to\infty}H(x_{\alpha},\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}))-H(y_{\alpha},\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}))\leq 0.

In our case

H(x_{\alpha},\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}))-H(y_{\alpha},\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}))=\lambda(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha})\left(e^{\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha})}+e^{-\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha})}-2\right).

(5.16)

Lemma 5 in
[
11
]
provides more control: accoding this lemma we have

\sup_{\alpha}H(y_{\alpha},\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}))=\lambda y_{\alpha}\left(e^{\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha})}+e^{-\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha})}-2\right)<\infty.

(5.17)

Thus, suppose that
z\in(0,1]
then, according to (
5.17
) the sequence
\alpha(x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha})
is bounded, and it provides
5.16
(see some details in Proposition 3,
[
11
]
). The case
z=0
does not follow the proof in
[
11
]
.

If
z=0
, we prove directly that
v(0)>u(0)
. Let us suppose for simplicity that
u
and
v
are continuous functions. Then for any small
\varepsilon>0
we can find the probe functions

\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{(1)},\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}\in\mathbf{C}_{1}[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle x_{0}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\mbox{argmax}(u(x)-\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(x))<\varepsilon

\displaystyle x_{0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\mbox{argmin}(v(x)-\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}(x))<\varepsilon

and

\displaystyle u(x_{0}^{(1)})-\beta H\left(x_{0}^{(1)},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(x_{0}^{(1)})\right)-h(x_{0}^{(1)})\leq 0,

\displaystyle v(x_{0}^{(2)})-\beta H\left(x_{0}^{(2)},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}(x_{0}^{(2)})\right)-h(x_{0}^{(2)})\geq 0.

Getting
\varepsilon\to 0
we obtain that
v(0)>u(0)
. The case of semicontinuous
u
and
v
is proved in a similar way adding some conditions for probe functions. It finishes the proof of the proposition.

\lambdabar

Definition 5.6
.

(
[

]
, Definition 8.1, or
[
4
]
, Definition 3.5) Let

\lambdabar(U\times[0,t])=t

any
