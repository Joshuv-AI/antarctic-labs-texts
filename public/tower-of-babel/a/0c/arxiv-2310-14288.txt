Popular and Dominant Matchings with Uncertain, Multilayer and Aggregated Preferences
Gergely Csáji

Abstract
Multi-agent systems and dealing with uncertainty are two major topics in AI. In this paper, we study the Popular Matching problem in multiple models of multi-agent systems where the agents’ preferences in the instance may change or may be unknown/uncertain. Intuitively, the popularity of a matching means that no other matching can beat it in a head-to-head comparison, which leads to a strengthening of Pareto-optimality, one of the most desirable properties in matching and allocation. In particular, we study an Uncertainty model, where each agent has a possible set of preferences, a Multilayer model, where there are layers of preference profiles, a Robust model, where any agent may move some other agents up or down some places in his preference list and an Aggregated Preference model, where votes are summed over multiple instances with different preferences. We study both one-sided and two-sided preferences in bipartite markets. In the one-sided model, we show that all studied problems can be solved in polynomial time by utilizing the structure of popular matchings. We also obtain compact characterization results. With two-sided preferences, we show that all four of the above models lead to NP-hard questions for popular matchings. However, by utilizing the connection between dominant matchings - which are the set of popular matchings that strictly beat any larger matching - and stable matchings, we show that in the robust and uncertainty model, a certainly dominant matching in all possible preference profiles can be found in polynomial time, whereas in the multilayer and aggregated models, the problem remains NP-hard for dominant matchings too. We also answer an open question about d d -robust stable matchings from Chen et al [ 7 ] .



1
Introduction

Matching markets are an important interdisciplinary area of research in computer science, economics, mathematics and AI due to their profound theoretical structure and diverse practical applications. These markets, which pair (or group) agents based on preferences, underpin systems like school choice, medical residency allocations, and resource distribution. The seminal work by Gale and Shapley in 1962
[
14
]
laid the foundation of this field with the stable marriage problem and its celebrated algorithm, guaranteeing a stable matching. Stability, defined as the absence of pairs of agents who would prefer each other over their current matches, remains a fundamental property in real-world settings.
However, the size of stable matchings often falls short of practical requirements. For example, in scenarios like school choice, a stable solution might leave numerous students unassigned, highlighting a key limitation in maximizing utility. As an effort to address this, Gardenfors
[
15
]
introduced the concept of
popularity
in matchings. Popular matchings, which win or tie in a head-to-head comparison against all other matchings, offer an appealing relaxation of stability, often yielding significantly larger solutions. Notably, stable matchings are a subset of popular matchings, ensuring their existence while enabling potentially larger outcomes.
The concept of popularity has since been extended and enriched, particularly in one-sided preference models where one side (e.g., objects) lacks preferences
[
1
]
. While these matchings might not always exist, efficient algorithms can determine their feasibility and compute them when possible. In recent literature, popularity became a central area, partly because of its beautiful connection with stable matchings through dominant matchings (popular matchings that beat any larger one)
[
19
]
.
Popularity also has several nice properties, such as

1.

it can be defined for both one-sided and two-sided preferences, while stability only makes sense for two-sided preferences,

2.

it can naturally be made compatible with requiring maximum size or maximum weight by restricting the set of candidate matchings,

3.

it is stronger than Pareto-optimality, hence more desirable if it can be achieved,

4.

while it is weaker than stability, it allows larger solutions, while still preserving a global form of stability.

For a survey about popular matchings, see Cseh
[
8
]
.

In some cases, the participating agents in a matching market may have unknown or partially known preferences, or even the agents themselves might not be sure about their own preferences, i.e. they are uncertain. These kind of problems have come into attention in the related literature only recently. In such problems, we want to compute stable or popular matchings, that are certainly stable or popular based on the information that we know about the preferences of the agents. Such problems have been studied for stable matchings (Aziz et al.
[
3
]
, Chen et al.
[
6
]
) and Pareto-optimal matchings (Aziz et al.
[
2
]
). However, so far there is no work for similar questions on popular matchings. Addressing this gap is essential to extend the utility of popular matchings to dynamic, real-world environments. In our paper, we consider the corresponding questions for popular and dominant matchings that has been studied for stability and Pareto-optimality in the literature and provide a comprehensive overview of their complexity.

Our work addresses foundational problems in computational social choice and multi-agent systems, key subfields of AI. The algorithms developed in the paper can be applied to optimize AI-driven decision-making processes in cases such as automated negotiation, resource allocation, and matching algorithms. The study explores popular and dominant matchings under complex conditions, such as uncertainty and multilayer preferences, which are increasingly relevant in dynamic and real-world environments where AI systems must operate.

1.1
Related Work

Popularity was introduced in a paper of Gardenfors back in 1975
[
15
]
for the two-sided matching markets. He showed that every stable matching is popular (in fact they are minimum size popular matchings), hence such matchings always exist and can be found efficiently.

For quite some time, popularity remained mostly unknown and has come to central attention only in the past 15 years.
Abraham et al.
[
1
]
introduced popularity for the one-sided preference model, also referred to as the House Allocation (
ha
) model. They provided an algorithm for finding a maximum size popular matching in the model and also a characterization of popular matchings.

Since popular matchings are easy to find, interest shifted to finding maximum-size popular matchings. Huang and Kavitha
[
17
]
have shown that even maximum-size popular matchings can be found in polynomial time. Furthermore, Cseh and Kavitha
[
10
]
have shown that a nonempty subset of maximum size popular matchings, called dominant matchings of an instance
I
are in correspondence with the stable matchings of an other instance
I^{\prime}
.

In the roommates model, where there is only a single class of agents and the underlying graph is not necessarily bipartite, finding a popular matching is NP-hard (Faenza et al.
[
12
]
, Gupta et al.
[
16
]
). The problem also becomes NP-hard for another generalization, where the market is still two-sided, but ties are allowed in the preference lists of the agents (Cseh
[
9
]
).

Models, where the preferences of agents can vary or may be uncertain have been studied in many papers in recent literature.

Uncertain preferences have been studied by Aziz et al. (
[
3
]
,
[
2
]
). In
[
3
]
they studied both a Lottery model, where there was a probability distribution for each different agent over some possible preferences and a Joint Probability model, where the probability distribution was over only certain preference profiles. In both frameworks, they studied when it is possible to find a matching that is stable with probability one (that is, stable in all possible preferences). In
[
2
]
, they studied similar questions for one-sided preferences and Pareto optimality as a condition instead of stability. Our work is very similar to this, as we consider many of the same problems for one-sided and two-sided popularity as they considered for Pareto-optimality and stability.

Miyazaki and Okamoto
[
22
]
studied the jointly stable matching problem, where the goal is to find stable matchings across multiple instances with the same agents and contracts, but different preferences, which is equivalent to finding a matching that is stable with probability 1, if each possible preference profile has some positive probability. They strengthened the result of Aziz et al.
[
3
]
and showed that it is NP-hard even for two preference profiles.

Multilayer preferences in bipartite matching markets have been studied in Chen et al.
[
6
]
. They defined
\alpha
-global and
\alpha
-pair stability. The former means we aim to find a matching that is stable in at least
\alpha
layers and the latter means that each edge can block in at most
\alpha
layers. The bipartite graph of the instances in each layer is the same, hence if
\alpha
is the number of layers, then
\alpha
-global stability becomes equivalent to deciding whether there is a stable matching across multiple preference profiles.
They proved that deciding whether an
\alpha
-global or
\alpha
-pair stable matching exists is NP-hard even with strict preferences. They also showed that for
\alpha
-global stability, the problem becomes polynomial solvable, if one side of the agents have the same preferences in all layers. Bentert et al.
[
4
]
studied some similar and new notions in Multilayer preferences, where in each layer the preferences of the agents are approval based, i.e. their preference list is a single tie over some acceptable partners.

Chen, Skowron, and Sorge
[
7
]
studied a notion of
d
-globally-robust-stability. This means that the agents may change the position of another agent in their preference list, by moving them up or down such that the number of such swaps in the preference profile is at most
d
. They have shown that finding
d
-globally-robust stable matching can be done in polynomial time. They posed as an open question, whether finding a
d
-robust stable matching, where each agent is allowed to make
d
swaps in their preference lists independently is solvable in polynomial time. We answer this question and give a polynomial algorithm for it.

Steindl and Zehavi
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
studied multilayer preferences in the one-sided model. They also considered similar problems, e.g. problems where we aim to find matchings that satisfy many properties like Pareto-optimality in multiple layers.

More generally, multilayer preferences have started to gain increasing popularity in the area of computational social choice, e.g., in multiwinner voting (Jain and Talmon, 2020) and fair division (Kyropoulou et al., 2020; Segal-Halevi and Suksompong, 2019; Suksompong, 2018).

Finally, independently of our work, Bullinger et al.
[
5
]
also studied similar problems for popular matchings with two-sided preferences. They considered the problem of finding a matching that is popular in two different instances, which only differ in the preferences of the agents. Their model coincides with our multilayer model with two layers. They provided an algorithm for the case, where the two preference profiles only differ at a single agent and showed NP-hardness in general, even if only four agents have different preferences. Compared to our NP-hardness result of the same problem, the advantage of ours is that it only uses one-sided ties, while the advantage of their approach is that only a constant number of agents have different preferences in the layers.

1.2
Our Results

This paper provides the first comprehensive study of popular matchings under uncertain and multilayered preferences. We propose and analyze four models to address these challenges: Uncertainty, Multilayer, Robust, and Aggregation. For each model, we examine both the computational complexity and algorithmic approaches for finding popular and dominant matchings.

Uncertainty Model

Agents possess a set of possible preference lists, and a matching is considered
certainly popular
if it is popular for every possible preference lists.
This models situations, where either the central authority is unsure about some information regarding the agent’s preferences, or the agents themselves have uncertainty in their preferences, for example because of the lack of information about certain options.

1.

Two-sided preferences:
Determining the existence of a certainly popular matching is NP-hard, even with fixed preferences on one side.

2.

One-sided preferences:
The problem becomes solvable, and we provide a polynomial-time algorithm and characterization of certainly popular matchings.

Multilayer Model

Preferences are organized into layers, and a matching must be popular across all layers. This models situations, where there can be multiple different criteria (for example in job markets, they can be salary, work-life balance, location, etc) and agents can have different preference lists based on these different criteria.

1.

Two-sided preferences:
Determining the existence of a certainly popular matching is NP-hard.

2.

One-sided preferences:
We present an efficient algorithm for computing certainly popular matchings.

Robust Model

Agents have known preferences, but these may deviate with up to
k
swaps (adjacent preferences exchanged).
This model corresponds to situations, where there can be small errors in the input, and the central authority wants a solution that has safeguards with respect to possible small differences in the preferences.

1.

Two-sided preferences:
Determining the existence of a certainly popular matching is NP-hard.

2.

One-sided preferences:
We provide a polynomial-time algorithm for finding certainly popular matchings.

Aggregation Model

Agents’ preferences are aggregated across multiple instances to compute a popular matching.
Similarly to the Multilayer model, this case can correspond to situations, where agents have different preferences based on different criteria, and when making a decision or comparing two outcomes, they take into account all of these criteria when casting their votes.

1.

Two-sided preferences:
Determining the existence of a certainly popular matching is NP-hard.

2.

One-sided preferences:
With limited preferences (e.g., each agent has two options), we provide a polynomial-time solution.

In the two-sided preference model, we also study dominant matchings, which are the subset of popular matchings that strictly beat any larger matching than themselves.

Dominant Matchings

For dominant matchings, we show that:

1.

In the
Uncertainty
and
Robust
models, certainly (or
k
-robust) dominant matchings can be computed in polynomial time by leveraging connections to stable matchings.

2.

In the
Multilayer
and
Aggregation
models, determining the existence of a certainly dominant matching is NP-hard, even under restrictive assumptions.

Preliminaries

In this section we describe the basic notions of two-sided and one-sided matching markets and our studied problems.

2.1
Two-sided markets

In
two-sided markets
, we are given a bipartite graph
G=(A,B;E)
, where the vertices represent the agents and the edges represent the possible contracts or acceptability relations. Furthermore, for each agent
u\in A\cup B
, we are given a strict linear order (called a
preference list)

\succ_{u}
over
V(u)
, where
V(u)
denotes the neighbors of
u
in
G
, i.e. the acceptable partners of
u
. We call the set of these preferences the
preference profile
of the instance and denote it by
L
, so
L=\{\succ_{u}\mid u\in A\cup B\}
.

We say that
M\subset E
is a
matching
, if
|M(u)|\leq 1
for all
u\in A\cup B
, where
M(u)\subseteq V(u)
denotes the partner(s) of
u
in
M
. If
u
does not have any partners in
M
, then
M(u)=\emptyset
, which is considered strictly worse than any acceptable partner
v\in V(u)
. Depending on the context, we may also refer by
M(u)
to the edge adjacent to
u
in
M
.

Stability.

In two-sided matching markets, the most well-known optimality concept is stability. Let
M
be a matching. We say that an edge
(a,b)

blocks

M
, if
b\succ_{a}M(a)
and
a\succ_{b}M(b)
both hold. We say that
M
is
stable
, if no edge blocks
M
. A well-known result is that a stable matching always exist and can be found in linear time
[
14
]
. Furthermore, a so called
A
-optimal stable matching
also exists. In this
A
-optimal stable matching, each agent
a\in A
obtains his best possible partner in any stable matching simultaneously.

Popularity.

A relaxation of stability is popularity. To define the popularity of a matching, take two arbitrary matchings
M
and
N
. Then, we let each agent
u\in A\cup B
cast a vote
\textit{vote}^{L}_{u}(M,N)
as follows. If
M(u)\succ_{u}N(u)
, then
\textit{vote}^{L}_{u}(M,N)=+1
, if
M(u)=N(u)
, then
\textit{vote}^{L}_{u}(M,N)=0
and if
N(u)\succ_{u}M(u)
, then
\textit{vote}^{L}_{u}(M,N)=-1
. We sum up these votes to obtain
\Delta^{L}(M,N)=\sum_{u\in A\cup B}\textit{vote}^{L}_{u}(M,N)
.

We say that a matching
M
is
popular
(with the preference profile
L
), if for any other matching
N
it holds that
\Delta^{L}(M,N)\geq 0
. In other words, a matching
M
is popular if it does not lose any head-to-head comparison with any other matching. Otherwise, if
\Delta^{L}(M,N)<0
, we say that
N

dominates

M
.

Dominant matchings.

We say that a matching
M
is
dominant
, if (i)
M
is popular and (ii) for any other matching
N
with
|N|>|M|
it holds that
\Delta^{L}(M,N)>0
, or equivalently
\Delta^{L}(N,M)<0
. Dominant matchings are a nonempty subset of maximum size popular matchings, and can be found in polynomial time. The motivation behind this definition, is that even a matching where the same number of people are getting better and worse, but more people get matched may be a more desirable solution and hence it would "dominate" the matching in a weaker, but still practical sense.

We will denote the problem of deciding whether an instance
I
of a two-sided matching market admits the popular matching by
pm
.

Even though

can be solved in polynomial time, the problem becomes NP-hard if we want to decide whether there is a popular matching that contains a given edge

and covers a given agent

. For our main hardness result, we will use the following restricted, but still NP-hard variant of this problem.

.

popular-robust

,

Finally, we define a new, Aggregation Model, where instead of aiming to find matchings that are popular in multiple instances, the goal is to find a matching that is popular in the aggregation of them. We define this for both the uncertainty and the multilayer model as follows. For the multilayer model, we define
\Delta^{ML}(M,N)=\sum_{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}}\Delta^{L_{i}}(M,N)
to be the sum of the results of the votes in the preference profiles

. We say that a matching

is

. We say that

One-sided markets

One-sided markets

3
Two-sided preferences

In this section, we investigate the problems for the two-sided markets. First, in Subsection
3.1
we show that verifying if a given matching is certainly popular or certainly dominant can be done in polynomial time. Then, we study the existence questions, first for dominant matching in Subsection
3.2
, and then for popular matchings for Subsection
3.3
.
We give a thorough complexity picture for these problems. Our algorithms provide efficient methods of computing desirable, certainly popular solutions in such uncertain or dynamic conditions. On the other hand, our hardness results highlight the boundaries of tractability and show the cases where we need heuristics, integer programming or other machinery to tackle these problems.

3.1
Verification is solvable

In this subsection, we show that deciding if a matching
M
is certainly popular; sum-popular; or robust popular can be done in polynomial time.

Theorem 2
.

Verifying if a matching
M
is certainly popular (resp. dominant), sum-popular (resp. dominant) or robust-popular (resp. dominant) can be done in polynomial-time in all of
popular-multilayer
,
dominant-multilayer
,
popular-uncertain
,
dominant-uncertain
,
popular-uncertain-aggregated
,
dominant-uncertain-aggregated
,
popular-robust
and
dominant-robust
.

Proof.

The case of
popular-multilayer
and
dominant-multilayer
is trivial, because we only need to verify that
M
is popular (resp. dominant) in polynomially many preference profiles in the input size, and verifying if a matching is popular can be done in polynomial-time.

We may always assume without loss of generality that
|A|=|B|
. Otherwise, we can add isolated agents to the instance, whose vote will always be 0 when comparing any two matchings and the other agents’ votes are unaffected.

Let
I
be an instance of
popular-uncertain
or
popular-robust
with
G=(A,B,E)
and
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of possible preference profiles. Let
M
be a matching. First, we extend
G
to be a complete bipartite graph by adding all missing edges. Call this new graph
H
. Then, we create a weight function
w:E(H)\to\mathbb{Z}
as follows. For each
e=(a,b)\in E
, we let
w(e)=\textit{vote}^{*}_{a}(M(a),e)+\textit{vote}^{*}_{b}(M(b),e)
. Here,
M(a)
is the edge incident to
a
, if there is any, otherwise it is
\emptyset
. Furthermore,
\textit{vote}_{a}^{*}(g,f)
is defined to be
-1
, if there is a possible preference list, where
a
prefers his partner in
f
,
0
if
g=f
and
+1
, if
a
always prefers his partner in
g
(so in the
k
-robust case, this corresponds to that partner being at least
(k+1)
-better). For an edge
e=(a,b)\notin E
, we let
w(e)=\textit{vote}_{a}^{*}(M(a),\emptyset)+\textit{vote}_{b}^{*}(M(b),\emptyset)
(here
\textit{vote}_{a}^{*}(M(a),\emptyset)=+1
, except the case when
M(a)=\emptyset
, when it is
0
).

We claim that
M
is popular, if and only the minimum weight perfect matching in
H
has weight at least
0
. Indeed, for any perfect matching
N^{\prime}
of
H
, if we let
N=N^{\prime}\cap E
, then
w(N^{\prime})
is exactly
\min_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\Delta^{L}(M,N)
by the definition of the weights and the fact that since
N^{\prime}
is perfect, we count the smallest possible vote for every agent in
A\cup B
exactly once. Hence,
M
is popular, if and only if there is no perfect matching
N^{\prime}\subset E
such that
w(N^{\prime})<0
. This can be verified in polynomial-time with various methods. For the case of
dominant-uncertain
and
dominant-robust
, we can decide similarly if
M
is certainly popular, and if not, then conclude that it is not certainly dominant. To decide whether there is a larger matching
N
, such that
\Delta(M,N)=0
in some possible preference profile, we subtract
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{|M|+1}
from each original edge’s weight and check whether there is a perfect matching with weight at most
-1
. If there is a perfect matching
N^{\prime}
of weight at most
-1
, then for
N=N^{\prime}\cap E
, we have that
|N|\geq|M|+1
, as
|M|
is popular. Also,
|N|\leq 2|M|
, because any popular matching is (inclusionwise) maximal. Hence, the weight of
N
cannot be
1
or more without subtracting the
|N|
many
\varepsilon
values, so it is exactly
0
. Hence,
N
is larger than
M
, but
\Delta(M,N)=0
in some possible preference profile, so
M
is not certainly dominant. In the other direction, if
M
is not certainly dominant, then there is a matching
N
with
|N|\geq|M|+1
and
\Delta(M,N)=0
in some possible preference profile, so
N
can be extended to a perfect matching
N^{\prime}
with weight at most
-1
.

Finally, let
I
be an instance of
popular-uncertain-aggregated
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}
be the set of preference profiles. Again, we create
H
by making the graph
G
a complete bipartite graph. Then, we create a weight function
w:E(H)\to\mathbb{Z}
as follows. If
e=(a,b)\in E
, then
w(e)=\sum_{L\in\mathcal{L}}(\textit{vote}_{a}^{L}(M(a),e)+\textit{vote}_{b}^{L}(M(b),e))
. If
e=(a,b)\notin E
, then
w(e)=\sum_{L\in\mathcal{L}}(\textit{vote}_{a}^{L}(M(a),\emptyset)+\textit{vote}_{b}^{L}(M(b),\emptyset))
.

Again, the problem of deciding whether
M
is sum-popular, becomes equivalent to deciding if there is a perfect matching
N^{\prime}
in
H
such that
w(N^{\prime})<0
. For
dominant-uncertain-aggregated
, we can check dominance too with the same trick of subtracting
\frac{1}{|M|+1}
from the weight of the original edges.
∎

3.2
Dominant Matchings

We start by discussing our results for the two-sided markets in the case of dominant matchings. Our first result is the hardness of

.

Theorem 3
.

dominant-multilayer
is NP-hard, even for a fixed constant

.

Proof.

We reduce from the NP-complete problem
2-stable-multilayer
, which consists of finding a certainly stable matching in a bipartite graph

in two preference profiles

. This is shown to be NP-complete by
[
22
]
. We can also suppose that the graph is a complete bipartite graph, by adding the remaining edges and extending the preferences in

and
L_{2}
by putting every new neighbor of an agent to the end of his preference list in the same arbitrary order in both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}

Let

be an instance of

. We create an instance

of

. Then, we add

a_{i}^{\prime}
only considers
a_{i}
acceptable and

is the worst in
a_{i}

and
L_{2}

and

We claim that there is a matching

that is certainly stable in

if and only if there is a certainly dominant matching in

.

Let

be a certainly stable matching in

. Then,

is also certainly stable in

is certainly popular, as stable matchings are always popular.
[
15
]
Moreover, as one side of the bipartite graph in

has size

is a maximum size matching, therefore

is certainly dominant too.

For the other direction, suppose that there is a certainly dominant matching

. Observe that no edge
(a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime})
can be included in

is a matching in

too. Suppose that there is a blocking edge
(a_{i},b_{j})
to

in

,

. Then,

∎

We proceed by describing a well known reduction that connects dominant and stable matchings. Let
I=(G,L)
,
G=(A,B,E)
be an instance of
pm
. We create an instance

, by adding two parallel edges

for each edge

(over the edges now, as we have parallel edges in the new instance) from

, we first rank all the edges of type
x
incident to it, in the same order as

’s preference over his neighbors, then we rank all incident edges of type
y
, again in the order of
a

. For the agent

and

Theorem 4

(
[
19
,
18
]
)
.

Every stable matching

in

gives a dominant matching
M=f(M^{\prime})
in
I
, where
f(M^{\prime})=\{e\mid|M^{\prime}\cap\{x(e),y(e)\}|\geq 1\}
. Furthermore, for every dominant matching
M
in
I

M^{\prime}
in

such that

.

The only problem with Theorem

is that for a dominant matching

with

also depends on the preference profile in

may be different for each preference profile, so there may not be a certainly stable matching in

that gives

We use this connection to the stable matching problem to solve

and

reduces in polynomial-time to

Let
I
be an instance of

. We create an instance

of

with the same underlying bipartite graph. Then, we create the set of preference lists for each agent. Let
P_{u}=\{\succ_{u}^{v}\mid v\in V(u)\}

by swapping

Clearly, for a fixed matching

\min_{\succ_{u}^{\prime}\mid d(\succ_{u},\succ_{u}^{\prime})\leq k}\{\textit{vote}_{\succ_{u}^{\prime}}(M,N)\}=\min_{v\in V(u)}\{\textit{vote}_{\succ_{u}^{v}}(M,N)\}
holds for each

and matchings

, therefore a matching

is certainly dominant in

if and only if it is certainly dominant in

.

Remark 1
.

We note that with the same idea, we get that we can reduce the problem of finding a
k
-robust stable matching, that is, a matching which remains stable even if each agent can apply

swaps in his preference list, to finding a certainly stable matching in polynomial time. As the latter problem can be solved in polynomial time (see Aziz et al.
[
3
]

[
7
]

Theorem 5
.

and

Proof.

We have already seen that it is enough to give an algorithm for

Let
I
be an instance of

. We create an instance
I^{\prime}
of

, where for each edge

and

as in Theorem

, such that if

and then the

By Theorem

, we get that if there is a certainly stable matching in

in

in

in

for each

\min_{i\in k}\{\textit{vote}_{\succ_{u}^{i}}(M,N)\}=\textit{vote}_{\succ_{u}^{\prime}}(M,N)

By Theorem

with respect to the extensions of the

3.3

We continue the section with our results for popular matchings.
We state a simple observation that we are going to use many times in our proofs without further reference.

Lemma 8
.

If there is a popular matching

in
I
that covers
z
and contains
(x,y)
, then there is a matching

that is popular with respect to any preference profile in
I^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
M
be a matching that is popular in
I
, covers
z
and contains
(x,y)
. Extend it to a matching
M^{\prime}
, by adding the edges
\{(d_{a},d_{b}),(z^{\prime},d_{z})\}
.

We claim that
M^{\prime}
is popular with respect to any possible preference profile
L
in the instance
I^{\prime\prime}
.

Suppose for the contrary that there is a possible preference profile
L
, such that there exists a matching
N^{\prime}
that dominates
M^{\prime}
and let
N
be the restriction of
N^{\prime}
to
G
.

Let
\eta=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\textit{vote}_{v}^{I}(M,N)\geq 0
, as
M
is popular.
Note that
d_{a}
and
d_{b}
have their only possible best partner matched to them in
M^{\prime}
, so they vote with
+1
, whenever they obtain a different partner, otherwise, they vote with
0
. For agent
d_{z}
, he can only vote with
-1
if
(d_{z},z)\in N^{\prime}
, but in this case,
z^{\prime}
casts a
+1
vote that cancels it. For agent
a_{1}^{\prime}
, if he casts a vote of
-1
, then he must get a
d_{a}
as a partner in
N^{\prime}
, who then votes with
+1
that cancels it.

Therefore, if
\textit{vote}_{v}^{L}(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})\geq\textit{vote}_{v}^{I}(M,N)
for each agent
v\in V(G)
, then
\Delta^{L}(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})\geq 0
, contradicting that
N^{\prime}
dominates
M^{\prime}
.

Suppose there is an agent
v\in V(G)
that casts a smaller vote, so
\textit{vote}_{v}^{L}(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})<\textit{vote}_{v}^{I}(M,N)
. As each such agent has the same situation in both
M
and
M^{\prime}
, this is only possible if
v
obtains a better partner in
N^{\prime}
than in
N
.

Suppose this agent is
z
. Then,
z
must be unmatched in
N
and be matched to
d_{z}
in
N^{\prime}
, otherwise either he would have the same partners in both matchings or would not prefer
N^{\prime}
to
N
. However, we know that
z
was matched in
M
, so he was matched to a better partner in
M^{\prime}
than
d_{z}
, so he casts the same
+1
vote in both scenarios.

Suppose this agent is
a_{1}
. This means that
a_{1}
was unmatched in
N
, but is matched to some
d_{a}
in
N^{\prime}
. Then,
vote_{a_{1}}^{I}(M,N)=0
, as
a_{1}
must be unmatched in
M
too, since
(x,y)\in M
. Furthermore, if
\textit{vote}_{a_{1}}^{L_{1}}(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})=-1
in the vote
M^{\prime}
versus
N^{\prime}
, then this decrease of
1
in
a_{1}
’s vote can be canceled with the
+1
vote of his partner
d_{a}
’s in
N^{\prime}
(using that in this case
\textit{vote}_{a_{1}^{\prime}}^{I}(M,N)=\textit{vote}_{a_{1}^{\prime}}^{L_{1}}(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})=0
).

Suppose lastly that this agent is
b_{i}
, for some
i\in[l]
. Again, this means that
b_{i}
is unmatched in
N
but is matched in
N^{\prime}
, otherwise
b_{i}
has the same vote in both situations. However, this implies that he must be matched to
d_{b}
in
N^{\prime}
, and therefore his possible
-1
vote can be canceled with the
+1
vote of his partner

.

In summary, we concluded that the sum of votes must remain nonnegative in the vote
M^{\prime}
versus
N^{\prime}
, contradicting the fact that

dominated

.
∎

Finally, we note that it is trivial that if there is a matching that is certainly popular in

Next we proceed to the hardness of

.

Theorem 9
.

popular-robust
is NP-complete, even for a fixed constant
k=1
.

Proof.

It is in NP by Theorem
2
.
Let
k=1
.
We reduce from the NP-complete problem
super-pm
. Let
I
be an instance of
super-pm
, where each agent either has a strict preference list, or has only two neighbors that he puts in a single tie. By the reduction of
[
11
]
, this is also NP-complete.

Let
G=(A,B,E)
be the graph of
super-pm
, with
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
and
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}
. Suppose without loss of generality that
n\geq m
. Let
A^{\prime}\subset A
and
B^{\prime}\subset B
be the sets of agents of
A
and
B
with strict preference lists.

We add four sets of agents
U=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\}
,
U^{\prime}=\{u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime}\}
,
W=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\}
and
W^{\prime}=\{w_{1}^{\prime},\dots,w_{m}^{\prime}\}
.

Now, we extend the original preferences of each agent
a_{i}\in A
with a strict preference list by inserting
u_{1}
between his first and second choice,
u_{2}
between his second and third choice, etc, and put the remaining
u_{i}
agents to the bottom of the preference list in the order of their indices. For example, if the preference list of
a_{i}
was
b_{3}\succ b_{2}\succ b_{5}
, then now it becomes
b_{3}\succ u_{1}\succ b_{2}\succ u_{2}\succ b_{5}\succ u_{3}\succ\dots\succ u_{n}
. We extend the preferences of the agents in
B
similarly, by inserting the ones from
W
. For the agents in
I
with a tie, we just break the tie and not add any more agents to their preference lists.

Finally, we create the preferences of the added new agents. For
u_{i}
, we let his preference list be
u_{i}^{\prime}\succ u_{i+1}^{\prime}\succ u_{i+2}^{\prime}\succ\dots\succ u_{i-1}^{\prime}\succ[A^{\prime}]
, where
i+1
is taken as
i\;(mod\;n)\;+1
, and
[A^{\prime}]
denotes an arbitrary strict ordering of the agents in
A^{\prime}
. For
w_{j}
, we create it as
w_{j}^{\prime}\succ w_{j+1}^{\prime}\succ\dots\succ w_{j-1}^{\prime}\succ[B^{\prime}]
, where
j+1
is taken as
j\;(mod\;m)\;+1
. Finally, for
u_{i}^{\prime}
, we have
u_{i}\succ u_{i+1}\succ u_{i+2}\succ\dots\succ u_{i-1}
and for
w_{j}^{\prime}
we have
w_{j}\succ w_{j+1}\succ\dots\succ w_{j-1}
.

Claim 4
.

Suppose that there is a
1
-robust popular matching in
I^{\prime}
. Then, there is a super popular matching in
I
.

Proof:

Let
M^{\prime}
be a 1-robust matching. We claim that
\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}\subset M^{\prime}
. Suppose that
(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime})\notin M^{\prime}
. Then, as they are mutual best,
x=M^{\prime}(u_{i})
and
y=M^{\prime}(u_{i}^{\prime})
exist, and hence none of them is with their best partner in
M^{\prime}
. Then,
u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime},x
and
y
can all get matched to their favorite, so strictly better partners, and only the original partners of
x
and
y
get left alone in doing so, so
M^{\prime}
is not even popular, contradiction. Similar reasoning works for
(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\notin M^{\prime}
.

Hence, each edge of
M=M^{\prime}\setminus\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}
must be an edge of
G
, so
M
is a matching in
G
and every
u\in A\cup B
has the same partner in
M
and
M^{\prime}
. We claim that
M
is super-popular. Suppose that some matching
N
dominates it. Let
N^{\prime}=N\cup\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}
.

We claim that each agent has the same vote when comparing
N
to
M
; and
N^{\prime}
to
M^{\prime}
.
For the agents in
U\cup U^{\prime}\cup W\cup W^{\prime}
, this is trivial, as their vote is always 0. For the agents in
A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
, it is also clear, because between any two of their neighbors in
G
, there is an agent
u_{i}\in U
or
w_{i}\in W
, so if they get a better or worse partner in
N
than in
M
in
I
, then in
I^{\prime}
this partner is at least
2
-better or
2
-worse, so definitely better or definitely worse respectively. For the agents who had a single tie, they had only two neighbors in
I
and also only two neighbors in
I^{\prime}
. If such an agent is matched in
M
but is not matched in
N
or is unmatched in
M
but is matched in
N
or he has the same partner (possibly no one) in both, then his votes in
I
and
I^{\prime}
are the same. Otherwise, if he switches between his two partners, then he also votes with
-1
in both
I
by the definition of super-popularity and in
I^{\prime}
by the definition of 1-robust popularity.

Hence, this implies that
N^{\prime}
dominates
M^{\prime}
in
I^{\prime}
, contradiction.

\blacksquare

Claim 5
.

If there is a super-popular matching in
I
, then there is a 1-robust matching in
I^{\prime}
.

Proof:

Let
M
be a super-popular matching in
I^{\prime}
. Create
M^{\prime}=M\cup\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}
.

Suppose that
M^{\prime}
is not 1-robust popular and let
N^{\prime}
be a matching that dominates it. We claim that we may assume that
\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}\subset N^{\prime}
. Suppose that each edge of
N^{\prime}
is either an edge of
G
, or an edge of type
(u_{i},u_{j}^{\prime})
or
(w_{i},w_{j}^{\prime})
. In this case, the sum of votes on each edge
(u_{i},u_{j}^{\prime})
and
(w_{i},w_{j}^{\prime})
is at most 0, because if
i\neq j
, then at least one of them has a partner that is more than
1
-worse. So in this case, if we take the restriction of
N^{\prime}
to
G
and add
\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}
, then it still dominates
M^{\prime}
, but now this matching satisfies our assumption.

So let us suppose that there is an edge
(u_{i},a_{j})\in N^{\prime}
. Let the number of such edges be
\eta
. This means that there must be at least
\eta
unmatched agents from
U^{\prime}
in
N^{\prime}
. Also, in an
(u_{i},a_{j})
edge,
u_{i}
votes with
+1
in
I^{\prime}
. Hence, if we drop each edge adjacent to any

or

from

and add
\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n]\}
, then the sum of the votes of the
\eta

a_{i}
agents may increase by at most
2\eta
, but the sum of the votes in

decreases by at least
2\eta
too. Hence, this matching also dominates
M^{\prime}
. Similar arguments with assuming
(w_{i},b_{j})\in N^{\prime}
show that indeed we may assume that
\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}\subset N^{\prime}
.

Therefore, let us take
N=N^{\prime}\setminus\{(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime}),(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}
. Then, each agent in
A\cup B
has the same partners in
M
and
M^{\prime}
and also in
N
and
N^{\prime}
. However, with the same reasoning as in the previous claim, we get that now each agent in
A\cup B
has the same vote in
M
versus
N
in
I
and in
M^{\prime}
versus
N^{\prime}
in
I^{\prime}
. As each agent in
U\cup U^{\prime}\cup W\cup W^{\prime}
votes with
0
, this implies that
N
dominates
M
in

, contradiction.

\blacksquare

The theorem follows from the two claims. For

, a similar reduction works with more dummy agents, where we insert at least
k
of them between any two entries in a preference list of an agent from

.

Proof.

It is in NP by Theorem

We reduce from the NP-hard

The reduction itself is quite simple. Let

Create an instance

of

as follows.
For each agent

we keep his preference list in both

in

We claim that for any matchings

For an agent

To show the statement of the theorem for

Theorem 14
.

denote the set of possible preference profiles, which in the case of

Theorem 19
.

ha-popular-robust

-robust popular.

Proof.

Verification is trivial from Lemma
17

.

For the existence problem, we first compute

for each agent

17

\hat{G}
, such that for any agent
a
with at least two acceptable houses, we delete
(a,b)
for any

. This can be done in polynomial-time. Then, each
a\in A
has degree 0 or

in
\hat{G}
. Then, for each

5
Conclusions and Open Questions

In this paper we considered models where agents’ preferences may vary or may be uncertain for popular matching problems both in one-sided and two-sided preferences. We gave a comprehensive overview of the complexity of our studied problems.

For future work, it may be interesting to consider similar questions for other well known notions. We also left open the complexity of
ha-pop-multilayer-aggregated
for
k\geq 3
and the complexity of the problems
dominant-multilayer
and
popular-robust
if only one side has uncertain preferences.
