Landau level collapse in graphene in the presence of in-plane radial electric and perpendicular magnetic fields
I.O. Nimyi, V. Könye, S.G. Sharapov, V.P. Gusynin

Abstract
It is known that in two-dimensional relativistic Dirac systems placed in orthogonal uniform magnetic and electric fields, the Landau levels collapse as the applied in-plane electric field reaches a critical value ± E c \pm E_{c} . We study this phenomenon for a distinct field configuration with in-plane constant radial electric field. The Dirac equation for this configuration does not allow analytical solutions in terms of known special functions. The results are obtained by using both the WKB approximation and the exact diagonalization and shooting methods. It is shown that the collapse occurs for positive values of the total angular momentum quantum number, the hole (electron)-like Landau levels collapse as the electric field reaches the value + ( − ) E c / 2 +(-)E_{c}/2 . The investigation of the Landau level collapse in the case of gapped graphene shows a number of distinctive features in comparison with the gapless case.

Introduction

It was Rabi

who solved the just-discovered Dirac equation
in a homogeneous magnetic field in the symmetric gauge and showed
that the energies of the free electrons are quantized. This occurred
two years before the corresponding quantized levels were found in the nonrelativistic
quantum theory by Frenkel and Bronstein

and Landau

.
However the experimental exploration of the relativistic

became possible almost 80 years later
after the discovery of graphene

;
Kim2005

Naturally, the most exciting are the properties
of the relativistic Landau levels that do not have their counterparts
for standard electron systems and among them is the
Landau level collapse phenomenon predicted in Ref.
Lukose2007PRL
(see also Ref.
Peres2007JPCM
)
and observed experimentally in Refs.

;

This phenomenon occurs when, in addition to a magnetic field

to the sheet of graphene, an in-plane electric field

It consists of the merging of the Landau-level staircase when the applied
electric field reaches a critical value
\pm E_{c}

CGS units, where

is the Fermi velocity.

There are several ways to understand the origin of the collapse.
The first one is based on the consideration of the motion
in crossed electric and magnetic fields

;
Lifshitz1973book
, where
the motion of a quasiparticle having a dispersion
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{p})

in crossed fields, can be viewed as a motion of a particle in the magnetic field
only with the modified dispersion law,

\mathcal{E}^{\ast}(\mathbf{p})=\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{p})-\mathbf{v}_{0}\mathbf{p},

(1)

with
\mathbf{v}_{0}=c\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}/H^{2}

being the drift velocity for the motion in the crossed fields.
Then the quasiclassical spectrum follows from the Lifshitz-Onsager quantization condition,

S(\mathcal{E}^{\ast})=2\pi\hbar\frac{eH}{c}(n+\gamma_{B}),\quad n=0,1,\ldots,

(2)

where
S(\mathcal{E}^{\ast})
is the electron orbit area in the
momentum space,
\gamma_{B}
is the
topological part of the Berry phase.
For the quadratic dispersion law
\mathcal{E}=p^{2}/(2m)
with
m

being the effective mass and

the absolute value of the momentum,
the area is

S(\mathcal{E}^{\ast})=2\pi m\mathcal{E}^{\ast}
. Thus, one can see that
in this case the electric field does not change the distance between Landau levels.

The massive Dirac fermions with the dispersion,

\mathcal{E}=\pm\sqrt{v_{F}^{2}p^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
, are characterized by the area

S(\mathcal{E})=\pi(\mathcal{E}^{2}-\Delta^{2})/v_{F}^{2}
, where
\Delta
is the gap
in the quasiparticle spectrum.
The Lifshitz-Onsager quantization
condition Eqs. (
2

Alisultanov2014PB

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{n}&=\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{n}-\hbar k\frac{E}{H},\\
\mathcal{E}^{\ast}_{n}&=\pm(1-\beta^{2})^{3/4}\sqrt{\frac{2n\hbar v_{F}^{2}eH}{c}+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{(1-\beta^{2})^{1/2}}},\end{split}

(3)

where

is the in-plane wave vector along the direction perpendicular to the electric field,

\beta=v_{0}/v_{F}=cE/(v_{F}H)
,
the phase
\gamma_{B}=0

\Delta=0

the spectrum Eqs. (
3

an exact solution of the problem
Lukose2007PRL
;
Peres2007JPCM

The Landau level collapse occurring at

a transition from the closed elliptic quasiparticle orbits for

) to open hyperbolic orbits for
|\beta|>1
(

)

Another elegant way to understand the origin of the collapse and even
to derive the spectrum Eqs. (
3

Lukose2007PRL

(see also Refs.
Shytov2009SSC
;
Arjona20017PRB

One can employ the effective Lorentz covariance of the equation of

8

We assume that the magnetic and electric fields are directed in the

\displaystyle E_{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(E_{y}-\frac{v}{c}H_{z}\right),

(4a)

\displaystyle H_{z}^{\prime}

The purpose of this paper is to study the Landau

In practice, an approximately constant radial field can be created

V(r)=V_{0}\ln\frac{r}{a}\approx V_{0}\left(\frac{r}{a}-1\right),\qquad b\leq r\leq a,

(7)

It turns out

Following the above-mentioned arguments with the Lorentz transformation, one
may develop a qualitative understanding of the present problem.
However, one should keep in mind that because the electric field is radial

Thus, the Thomas precession must be properly taken into

One can see from the transformation Eq. (

We emphasize that due to Thomas precession

4b

\beta

the fields in Fig.

\beta=-1/2

II
Model and main equations

We consider the stationary Dirac equation

\left[-\hbar v_{F}\left(\alpha_{1}iD_{x}+\alpha_{2}iD_{y}\right)+\Delta\alpha_{3}+V(\mathbf{r})-\mathcal{E}\right]\Psi(\mathbf{r})=0,

(8)

which describes low-energy excitations in graphene (see, e.g., Ref.
Gusynin2007review

for a review and notations)
and eigenenergy
\mathcal{E}
.
The
4\times 4

\alpha
-matrices
\alpha_{i}=\tau_{3}\otimes\sigma_{i}

and the Pauli matrices
\tau_{i}
,
\sigma_{i}
(as well as the
2\times 2
unit
matrices
\tau_{0}
,
\sigma_{0}
) act on the valley (
\mathbf{K}_{\eta}
with

\eta=\pm
)
and sublattice (
A,B
) indices, respectively, of the
four component spinors
\Psi^{T}=\left(\Psi_{+}^{T},\Psi_{-}^{T}\right)=\left(\psi_{AK_{+}},\psi_{BK_{+}},\psi_{BK_{-}},\psi_{AK_{-}}\right)
.

We consider both the massless Dirac-Weyl fermions in the pristine graphene
and the massive Dirac fermions with the mass
\Delta
.
We recall that a global
A/B
sublattice asymmetry gap

2\Delta\sim{\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{350}$}}\,\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{K}$}
can be introduced in graphene

Hunt2013Science
;
Gorbachev2014Science
;
Woods2014NatPhys
;
Chen2014NatCom

when it is placed on top of hexagonal boron nitride (G/hBN) and
the crystallographic axes of graphene and hBN are aligned.

The orbital effect of a perpendicular magnetic field
\mathbf{H}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}

is included via the covariant spatial
derivative
D_{j}=\partial_{j}+(ie/\hbar c)A_{j}
with
j=x,y
and
-e<0
,
while the potential
V(\mathbf{r})
corresponds to the
static electric field
e\mathbf{E}=\nabla V(\mathbf{r})
.
The Zeeman interaction is neglected in this paper
(see, e.g., Ref.
Gusynin2007review
) and
the spin index is omitted in what follows.

We consider the configuration of crossed magnetic and electric fields,
with the magnetic field applied perpendicular
to the infinite plane of graphene along the positive
z
axis
foot2
and the corresponding
vector potential is taken in the symmetric gauge

(A_{x},A_{y})=(H/2)(-y,x)
and
radial in-plane electric field
E
with the potential
V(r)=eEr
(see Fig.
1
).

It is clear that the solution at the
\mathbf{K}_{-}
point is obtained from the solution
at the
\mathbf{K}_{+}
point by changing

\Delta\to-\Delta
and exchanging the spinor components

\psi_{A}\leftrightarrow\psi_{B}
, so in what follows we only consider
the
\mathbf{K}_{+}
point.

Since the system has rotational symmetry,
it is natural to consider the problem in polar coordinates, where

iD_{x}\pm D_{y}=e^{\mp i\phi}\left(i\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\pm\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\pm\frac{ieHr}{2\hbar c}\right).

(9)

Accordingly, the total angular momentum

J_{z}
is conserved and we can represent
\Psi(\mathbf{r})
in terms of the eigenfunctions of

J_{z}=L_{z}+\sigma_{z}/2=-i\partial/\partial\phi+\sigma_{z}/2
as follows:

\Psi_{+}(\mathbf{r})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}e^{i(j-1/2)\phi}f(r)\\
ie^{i(j+1/2)\phi}g(r)\end{array}\right],

(10)

where
j=\pm 1/2,\pm 3/2,\ldots
is the total angular momentum quantum number.
Then for the spinor

\chi^{T}(r)=\left(f(r),g(r)\right)
,
we obtain the following system of equations written in a matrix form:

\chi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\hbar}N(r)\chi(r),

(11)

where the prime denotes the derivative over
r
and the matrix

N=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\hbar(j-1/2)}{r}+\frac{eHr}{2c}&-\frac{{\cal E}+\Delta-V(r)}{v_{F}}\\
\frac{{\cal E}-\Delta-V(r)}{v_{F}}&-\frac{\hbar(j+1/2)}{r}-\frac{eHr}{2c}\end{array}\right].

(12)

For a constant electric field with
V(r)=eEr
,
one can rewrite the last equation in the form

\chi^{\prime}(\rho)=N(\rho)\chi(\rho)=\left(\frac{A}{\rho}+B+C\rho\right)\chi(\rho),

(13)

where
we introduced the dimensionless variable
\rho=r/l
with

l=\sqrt{\hbar c/eH}
being the magnetic length; the prime now
denotes the derivative over
\rho
,
and the
2\times 2

\rho
-independent matrices

are, respectively,

\begin{split}A&=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}j-1/2&0\\
0&-j-1/2\end{array}\right],\\
B&=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\frac{l(\mathcal{E}+\Delta)}{\hbar v_{F}}\\
\frac{l(\mathcal{E}-\Delta)}{\hbar v_{F}}&0\end{array}\right],\\
C&=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1/2&\beta\\
-\beta&-1/2\end{array}\right].\end{split}

(14)

The matrix
C
contains the
important dimensionless parameter
\beta=cE/(v_{F}H)

that describes the strength of the electric field relative to the
magnetic field. In this paper, we restrict ourselves to the
|\beta|\leq 1/2

Comparing the system of equations (
13

the corresponding system describing the Dirac fermions
in the uniform magnetic
field and constant electric field in the

direction

;
Peres2007JPCM
(see also Ref.
MacDonald1983PRB
),
one can see that the latter contains only two matrices
\tilde{B}+\tilde{C}x
.
The problem in the crossed uniform fields in the Cartesian coordinates
is exactly solvable by diagonalizing the matrix
\tilde{C}
, while the present
problem with the radial electric field cannot be solved analytically.
The situation is similar to the case of 2D Dirac fermions in a
constant magnetic field and Coulomb potential (see, e.g., Refs.
Khalilov2000PRA
;
Gamayun2011PRB
;
Zhang2012PRB
;
Sun2014PRB
;
Li2021
) and to the parabolic potential
V(r)\sim r^{2}

,
which do not have analytical solutions in terms of known special
functions.

Making in Eq. (
13
) the transformation of dependent variable

\chi(\rho)=U(\rho)\xi(\rho)
, where
U(\rho)
is a
2\times 2
matrix with
{\rm det}\,U(\rho)\neq 0
,
we obtain a different system of equations,

\xi^{\prime}(\rho)=M(\rho)\xi(\rho),

(15)

with matrix
N(\rho)
related to matrix
M(\rho)
by the transformation

M(\rho)=U^{-1}(\rho)N(\rho)U(\rho)-U^{-1}(\rho)U^{\prime}(\rho).

(16)

Choosing the matrix

proportional to the unit matrix
U=\sigma_{0}e^{-a\rho^{2}/2}\rho^{s}
, one can see that matrix
B

does not change, while matrices
A
and
C
become

\begin{split}A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}j-1/2-s&0\\
0&-j-1/2-s\end{array}\right],\\
C=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1/2+a&\beta\\
-\beta&-1/2+a\end{array}\right].\end{split}

(17)

Different sets of parameters
a
and
s
are appropriate for the consideration of the system (
15
),
for example, choosing
a=1/2
and
s=-j-1/2
one obtains the following
second-order differential equation for the lower component
\xi_{2}(\rho)
of the spinor
\xi(\rho)
:

\xi_{2}^{\prime\prime}-\left(\frac{2j}{\rho}+\rho+\frac{\beta}{\beta\rho-\epsilon+\delta}\right)\xi^{\prime}_{2}+[(\beta\rho-\epsilon)^{2}-\delta^{2}]\xi_{2}=0.

(18)

Here we introduced the dimensionless energy
\epsilon=l\mathcal{E}/(\hbar v_{F})
, and mass (gap)
\delta=l\Delta/(\hbar v_{F})
.
This equation has three singular points, two regular at
\rho=0,\rho=(\epsilon-\delta)/\beta
and an irregular one at infinity.
The singularity at
\rho=(\epsilon-\delta)/\beta
is apparent [it is absent in the system (
15

The equation is similar to the confluent Heun equation but the singularity at infinity is more strong (with rank three according to the definition
of the rank of singular points of differential equations in the Ref.
Slavyanov-book
).
The rank of the irregular singular point at infinity of the confluent Heun
equation is equal to two. Analytical results for such type of equations
are absent in the literature, thus it is
necessary to use another method to investigate the problem.

III
WKB method

We employ the WKB method to study the spectrum
of bound states for the problem
described by Eqs. (
15
) and (
17
).
It is convenient to choose the exponent
s=-1/2
and
a=0
in Eqs. (
17
), so
the spinor
\xi(r)=\sqrt{r}\chi(r)
.
Restoring the dimensional variables,
we rewrite Eq. (
15
) as follows:

\xi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\hbar}M\xi(r),

(19)

where the matrix

\begin{split}M&=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{J}{r}+\frac{eHr}{2c}&-\frac{\mathcal{E}+\Delta-V(r)}{v_{F}}\\
\frac{\mathcal{E}-\Delta-V(r)}{v_{F}}&-\frac{J}{r}-\frac{eHr}{2c}\end{array}\right]\\
&=\frac{\hbar}{l}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{j}{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{2}&\beta\rho-\epsilon-\delta\\
-(\beta\rho-\epsilon+\delta)&-\frac{j}{\rho}-\frac{\rho}{2}\end{array}\right].\end{split}

(20)

Here we denoted
J=\hbar j
in the first line.
Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) are similar
to the corresponding system written in Refs.
Khalilov2000PRA
;
Zhang2012PRB
;
Sun2014PRB
;
Rodriguez-Nieva2016PRB
;
Li2021

(see Ref.
foot2
).

As one can see from the first line of Eq. (
20
), the system Eq. (
19
) contains a small parameter
\hbar
and it is possible to use
the standard scheme for solving asymptotically systems of linear differential equations
Fedoryuk-book
, which represents the base of the WKB
method. Notice that in matrix
M
, the energy
\mathcal{E}
and total angular momentum
J
are the conserved quantities.

Following Ref.
Fedoryuk-book
, one writes

\xi(r)=\varphi(r)\exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}S(r)\right],

(21)

which gives

\varphi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(M-iS^{\prime}\cdot\sigma_{0}\right)\varphi(r),\quad S(r)=\int^{r}p(r)dr.

(22)

Then one seeks the solution of system Eq. (
19
)
as an asymptotic series in powers of
\hbar
(see, e.g., Ref.
Lazur2005TMP
):

p(r)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{n}p_{n}(r),\quad\varphi(r)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{n}\varphi^{(n)}(r).

(23)

Substituting these expansions in Eqs. (
22
) and equating the coefficients of equal powers of
\hbar
to zero, we
obtain an infinite system of recursive equations for the unknown scalar

p_{n}(r)
and vector
\phi^{(n)}(r)
functions,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\left(M-ip_{0}I\right)\varphi^{(0)}(r)=0,

(24a)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\left(M-ip_{0}I\right)\varphi^{(n+1)}(r)={\varphi^{(n)}}^{\prime}(r)+i\sum\limits_{l=0}^{n}p_{n+1-l}\varphi^{(l)}(r),

(24b)

where
l=0,1,\ldots n
.
It follows from Eq. (
24a
) that
ip_{0}
and
\varphi^{(0)}(r)

must be the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix
M
.
In particular, we find that
p_{0}(r)=\pm p(r)
with

p(r)=\frac{\hbar}{l}\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}-\delta^{2}-j-\left(\frac{1}{4}-\beta^{2}\right)\rho^{2}-\frac{j^{2}}{\rho^{2}}-2\beta\epsilon\rho}.

(25)

Recall that
\rho=r/l
.

The WKB approximation for the 2D massless Dirac fermions was discussed
in detail in Ref.
Zhang2012PRB
, so we directly proceed to the analysis of our problem. In particular,
the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition for eigenenergies
for the Dirac fermions was obtained in Ref.
Zhang2012PRB
both by using
Zwaan’s method and by considering the condition of single
valuedness of the WKB wave function (see also
Refs.
Kormanyos2008PRB
;
Brack.book
). It reads

I(\epsilon,j)\equiv\int_{b}^{a}drp(r)=\pi\hbar\left(n_{\mathrm{BS}}+\frac{1-\theta(-j)}{2}\right),

(26)

where
a\geq b
are positive turning points [roots of the equation
p^{2}(r)=0
],

n_{\mathrm{BS}}=0,1,2,\ldots
is the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantum number,

\theta(-j)
is the step function
foot2

whose presence takes into account the spinor nature of the
Dirac quasiparticles and the existence
of the lowest
n=0
Landau level when the classically allowed region shrinks to a point.

III.1
WKB approximation in the absence of electric field

Let us recapitulate how the WKB method can be applied in the absence
of an electric field,
E=0
. In this case, the integral
I(\epsilon,j)
on the
left hand side (LHS) of the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition Eq. (
26
)
can be calculated:

I(\epsilon,j)=\frac{\hbar\pi}{2}(\epsilon^{2}-\delta^{2}-|j|-j).

(27)

Solving Eq. (
26
) for the energy
\epsilon
, one recovers the
well-known Landau-level spectrum,

\mathcal{E}=\pm\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(2n_{\mathrm{BS}}+j+|j|+1-\theta(-j))eH\hbar v_{F}^{2}/c}

(28)

with
n_{\mathrm{BS}}=0,1,2,\ldots
.
Except for the asymmetric lowest Landau level,
this result agrees perfectly with the exact solution
of the Dirac equation in the symmetric gauge (see Appendix D of
Ref.
Gusynin2006PRB
, where the final result is written in
the form identical to the spectrum obtained
in the Landau gauge). For
j<0
, the spectrum Eq. (
28
)
is consistent with the result in the Landau gauge if one identifies
the quantum number
n_{\mathrm{BS}}
and the Landau level index
n=n_{\mathrm{BS}}
.
To reproduce the spectrum for the
j>0
case, one should relabel
the quantum numbers

2n=2n_{\mathrm{BS}}+2j+1
, so
n
corresponds to
the Landau-level index
and
\mathcal{E}=\pm\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+2neH\hbar v_{F}^{2}/c}

with
n=1,2,\ldots
and
1/2\leq j\leq n-1/2
.
Thus, one observes that in the absence of an electric field,
the WKB method reproduces well-known exact results (see, for example, Ref.
Zhang2012PRB
).

III.2
WKB approximation in the radial electric field

Here we restrict ourselves to the gapless case,

\Delta=0
. Then the expression Eq. (
25
)
acquires the form

p(r)=\frac{\hbar\sqrt{1/4-\beta^{2}}}{l\rho}\sqrt{-(\rho-\rho_{1})(\rho-\rho_{2})(\rho-\rho_{3})(\rho-\rho_{4})},

where
\rho_{i}
are the roots of the quartic equation

\begin{split}\rho_{1,2}&=\frac{-\epsilon\mp\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}-2j(1-2\beta)}}{1-2\beta},\\
\rho_{3,4}&=\frac{\epsilon\mp\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}-2j(1+2\beta)}}{1+2\beta}.\end{split}

(30)

As one can see, there are always two positive and two negative roots.
In particular, for the zero electric case considered in Secs.
III.1
and
C

\rho_{1}=-\rho_{4}
and
\rho_{2}=-\rho_{3}
. This

Depending on the signs of
\epsilon

IV.1.2
Diagonalization and shooting method

To verify the accuracy of the WKB approximation, we use two numerical methods: discretization with exact diagonalization and shooting method.

For the diagonalization method, we use the Hamiltonian which one can obtain from Eq. (
20
). To do this, one should rewrite Eq. (
20
)
in terms of an eigenvalue problem multiplying it by
i\sigma_{2}
matrix.
The first derivative (momentum) is discretized using finite differences.
In this way, the eigenvalue problem can be solved on a 1D lattice with
two orbitals, where the momentum is turned into hopping terms and
the other parts of the Hamiltonian into on-site terms.

It is worth noting that the choice of grid spacing of the lattice
should correspond to the behavior of the onsite term to reach all
important values. To increase the efficiency, we use a non-constant grid
with slowly increasing spacing, thus obtaining more dots closer to zero, where our effective potential is singular.

To implement the discretization method, the
K
WANT
kwant
Python package was used. With this package, we build the Hamiltonian of a 2000-site-long chain and then numerically diagonalize it.

It also should be noted that with such a method one can obtain
a fermionic-doubling effect
NielsenNin
, as we
observe in our numerical work.
Although this effect is not clearly seen in the gapless case,
it immediately appears if one introduces a non-zero gap,
thus resulting in the spectra doubling.

The shooting method is an integration process performed on the system Eq. (
20
) with a guessed energy-parameter (for more details, see
Appendix
D
). Such a method does not
result in the fermionic doubling.
Thus we can numerically solve the corresponding equations for each

\mathbf{K}_{\pm}
separately and prove that the diagonalization method
produces the results for the two valleys together.

The exact diagonalization method is more efficient in a sense of speed
and precision in comparison with shooting. Thus, we use the diagonalization as a main computation method and the shooting method as an auxiliary one to distinguish different
\mathbf{K}_{\pm}
valleys.

The results of the numerical methods described above for the gappless case
are shown in Fig.

\beta

.
The gapped case was investigated using numerical methods
only and is considered in what follows.

V
Conclusion

As already mentioned in the Introduction, the Landau-level collapse was
already observed experimentally
Singh2009PRB
;
Gu2011PRL
.
For example, in Ref.
Gu2011PRL

the Shubnikov-de Haas-type resonances arising
from quantized states associated with closed orbits are
used to directly observe the competition between magnetic
confinement and deconfinement due to electric field.
While these observations were made in the rectangular geometry,
there are no limitations for repeating them in the
circular Corbino geometry considered in this work.

High-mobility Corbino devices in a dual-gated geometry were recently studied
in Ref.
Dean2019PRL
. Bulk conductance measurement outperforms previously reported Hall bar measurements and allowed one to observe both the integer and fractional QHE states.
It should be technically possible to modify the existing devices
introducing the radial electric field by gating. Another way to apply
the radial electric field might be achieved by injecting a high current
density in the device
Singh2009PRB
.
Since for nonzero electric field the degeneracy of
the Landau levels with different total angular momenta

j
is lifted, this should be manifested in the
transport measurements. Another possibility to observe
the predicted features is to employ scanning tunneling spectroscopy
that allowed one to observe Dirac Landau levels in graphene
Li2007NP
.

Further increase of the
electric field would allow one to realize
the Landau-level collapse in the geometry studied in
this paper. Moreover,
the critical electric field required for the collapse
is
\pm E_{c}/2
is twice smaller than
the corresponding field
E_{c}=(v_{F}/c)H

for the rectangular geometry that should make its observation easier.
In contrast to the rectangular geometry, the
collapse in the circular geometry is sensitive to the sign of the electric
filed, so the hole (electron)-like levels collapse at
+(-)E_{c}/2
.
One can estimate that for the magnetic field

H={\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{1}$}}\,\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{T}$}
and the Fermi velocity
v_{F}={\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{1{}\times{}10^{6}}$}}\,\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{m/s}$}
,
the critical electric field in the circular geometry
is
E_{c}={\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{0{.}5{}\times{}10^{4}}$}}\,\text{\unboldmath$\mathrm{V/cm}$}
which should be possible to create
by gating.

It should also be possible to investigate the collapse
in the gapped case by using graphene
placed on top of hexagonal boron nitride (G/hBN).

Another experimental setup that would allow one to realize the Landau-level collapse
by generating strain induced either pseudomagnetic or electric fields was
suggested in Refs.
Castro2017PRB
;
Grassano2020PRB
, respectively.
Although the corresponding experiments were not done, it should
be possible to make them both in rectangular and Corbino geometries.
Finally, it might also be necessary to generalize the
presented results with a radial electric field for a
finite-size Corbino disk
Yerin2021PRB
.

S=\int\limits_{b}^{a}\frac{a_{0}x^{3}+4a_{1}x^{2}+6a_{2}x+4a_{3}+a_{4}/x}{y(x)},

(44)

where
y^{2}(x)=a_{0}x^{4}+4a_{1}x^{3}+6a_{2}x^{2}+4a_{3}x+a_{4}
with

\begin{split}a_{0}&=-1,\qquad 4a_{1}=a+b+c+d,\\
6a_{2}&=-a(b+c)-b(c+d)-d(a+c),\\
4a_{3}&=acd+bcd+abc+abd,\qquad a_{4}=-abcd.\end{split}

(45)

Accordingly, for the relabeled roots
\rho_{4}\to a,\rho_{3}\to b,\rho_{2}\to c,\rho_{1}\to d

(recall that we assumed that
a>x>b>c>d
), one obtains that

\begin{split}a_{0}&=-1,\quad a_{1}=-\frac{2\beta\epsilon}{1-4\beta^{2}},\quad a_{2}=\frac{2(\epsilon^{2}-j)}{3(1-4\beta^{2})},\\
a_{3}&=0,\qquad a_{4}=-\frac{4j^{2}}{1-4\beta^{2}}.\end{split}

(46)

Defining the integrals

J_{n}=\int\limits_{b}^{a}\frac{dx\,x^{n}}{y(x)},\quad n=-1,0,1,2,3,

(47)

one can rewrite Eq. (
44
) in the following form:

S=a_{0}J_{3}+4a_{1}J_{2}+6a_{2}J_{1}+4a_{3}J_{0}+a_{4}J_{-1}.

(48)

To get rid of the integral
J_{3}
from Eq. (
48
), we consider
the derivative

\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2a_{0}x^{3}+6a_{1}x^{2}+6a_{2}x+2a_{3}}{y},

(49)

and integrating it
b
to
a
we obtain the identity

a_{0}J_{3}+3a_{1}J_{2}+3a_{2}J_{1}+a_{3}J_{0}=0.

(50)

Hence

S=a_{1}J_{2}+3a_{2}J_{1}+3a_{3}J_{0}+a_{4}J_{-1},

(51)

and using the coefficients Eqs. (
46
) we arrive at the expression

S=-\frac{2}{1-4\beta^{2}}\left[\beta\epsilon J_{2}-(\epsilon^{2}-j)J_{1}+2j^{2}J_{-1}\right].

(52)

The integrals
J_{1}
and
J_{-1}
given by Eqs. (
34a
) and (
34b
), respectively,
can be found in Ref.
Gradshtein.book
[see Eqs. (3.148.6) and (3.150.4)].
The integral
J_{2}
is considered, for example, in Ref.
Lazur2008TMP
,
where it is rewritten in the form

\begin{split}J_{2}&=\frac{2}{\sqrt{(a-c)(b-d)}}\bigg[c^{2}K(k)+2c(b-c)\\
&\times\Pi\left(\frac{a-b}{a-c},k\right)+(b-c)^{2}T_{2}\left(\frac{a-b}{a-c},k\right)\bigg],\end{split}

(53)

where

T_{2}(\nu,k)=\int\limits_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\varphi}{(1-\nu\sin^{2}\varphi)^{2}\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}.

(54)

Evaluating the last integral (see Eq. (2.592.6) in Ref,
Gradshtein.book
),
one obtains

\begin{split}T_{2}(\nu,k)&=-\frac{1}{2(1-\nu)}K(k)-\frac{\nu}{2(k^{2}-\nu)(1-\nu)}E(k)\\
&+\frac{k^{2}(3-2\nu)+\nu(\nu-2)}{2(k^{2}-\nu)(1-\nu)}\Pi(\nu,k).\end{split}

(55)

Finally, substituting (
55
) in Eq. (
53
),
we obtain Eq. (
34
).

Appendix B
Calculation of the integral
I(\epsilon,j,\beta=1/2)
and energies
\epsilon(n,j,\beta=1/2)

We provide below the results of the analytical consideration
of the
\beta=1/2
case. Formally, an equation for the energies

\epsilon(n,j,\beta=1/2)
is still given by the
Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition
Eq. (
26
), but its LHS is simpler than
the generic expression in Eq. (
33
).
Indeed, the expression for the momentum Eq. (
25
)
acquires a more simple form than Eq. (
29
),

p(r)=\frac{\hbar}{l\rho}\sqrt{-\epsilon(\rho-\rho_{1})(\rho-\rho_{2})(\rho-\rho_{3})},

(56)

where the roots of the cubic equation
p^{2}(r)=0
are

\rho_{1,2}=\frac{\epsilon\pm\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}-4j}}{2},\quad\rho_{3}=-\frac{j}{\epsilon}.

(57)

Again depending on the signs of
\epsilon
and
j
, these roots
are ordered differently.

The calculation of
I(\epsilon,j,\beta=1/2)
is rather
similar to the general case considered in Appendix
A

and we restrict ourselves by showing the final result.
We obtain

I(\epsilon,j,\beta=1/2)=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}-j}{3}L_{1}-j^{2}L_{-1}\right),

(58)

where

L_{n}=\int_{b}^{a}\frac{dxx^{n}}{\sqrt{(a-x)(x-b)(x-c)}},\quad a>b>c,

(59)

with
n=-1,1.

Using Eqs. (3.132.5) and (3.137.6) from Ref.
Gradshtein.book
, we have

\displaystyle L_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{2c}{\sqrt{a-c}}K(k)+2\sqrt{a-c}E(k),

(60a)

\displaystyle L_{-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{a\sqrt{a-c}}\Pi\left(\frac{a-b}{a},k\right),

(60b)

with
k^{2}=(a-b)/(a-c)
.

In the considered
\epsilon>0
and
j<0
case, we have

a=\rho_{3}=|j|/\epsilon
,
b=\rho_{1}
and
c=\rho_{2}
.
Substituting Eq. (
58
) in the quantization condition Eq. (
26
)
we arrive at the following transcendental equation for the eigenenergy

\epsilon(j,n,\beta=1/2)
:

\begin{split}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3\sqrt{ts}}&\left[(t+1)sE(k)+(t+1)(t-\sqrt{t^{2}+4t})K(k)\right.\\
&\left.-6t\Pi(\nu,k)\right]=\frac{\pi n}{|j|},\end{split}

(61)

where we introduced
t=\epsilon^{2}/|j|
and

\begin{split}s=2-t+\sqrt{t^{2}+4t},&\qquad r=2-t-\sqrt{t^{2}+4t},\\
\nu=1-\frac{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+4t}}{2},&\qquad k^{2}=\frac{r}{s}.\end{split}

(62)

In the limit
|j|\to\infty
, Eq. (
61
)
has an exact solution
t=1/2
, so asymptotically

\epsilon\sim\sqrt{|j|/2}
. Expanding the LHS of Eq. (
61
)
around the point
t=1/2
up to
(t-1/2)^{2}
term one obtains
the following equation:

\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\left(t-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{\pi n}{|j|},

(63)

which leads to the expression Eq. (
41
) in the main text.

Here we show that the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition Eq. (

Appendix D
Shooting method

Equation (
20
) for the spinor components

f(\rho)
and
g(\rho)
defined by Eq. (
10
)
has the form:

\begin{split}\frac{df(\rho)}{d\rho}=\left(\frac{j}{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{2}\right)f(\rho)+\left(\beta\rho-\varepsilon-\delta\right)g(\rho),\\
-\frac{dg(\rho)}{d\rho}=\left(\frac{j}{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{2}\right)g(\rho)+\left(\beta\rho-\varepsilon+\delta\right)f(\rho).\end{split}

(69)

One can obtain from Eqs. (
69
) the derivative

df/dg
. It can be integrated up to a constant which is taken to
be equal to zero, because the subsequent equation should have a trivial solution
f=g=0
as
\rho\xrightarrow{}\infty
:

f^{2}\left(\beta\rho-\varepsilon+\delta\right)+2gf\left(\frac{j}{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{2}\right)+g^{2}\left(\beta\rho-\varepsilon-\delta\right)=0.

(70)

Thus, the ratio
f(\rho)/g(\rho)
can be obtained. Using it,
we can guess the initial value for one function and then calculate
the initial value for the other one. The boundary values at
\rho\xrightarrow{}\infty
are expected to be zero, since functions

f(\rho),g(\rho)
have to be square integrable.

Now, when we know the initial and boundary conditions, we perform an integration process on the system Eq. (
69
) setting the numeric value of
\varepsilon
from an interval of interest. Such a method is called a shooting method, for more details see Ref.
PresShooting
. To do the integration we use the Runge-Kutta sixth-order method. The boundaries for integration are
\rho\in[0.001,100]
,
where the beginning of the interval replaces zero (which is a point of singularity
in the system) and the end replaces the infinity
(in this case,
r=l\rho
is comparable to the length of the chain
used in the diagonalization method).

Shooting with energies from an interval of interest and performing a numerical integration from some initial values one can obtain a dependence of
f(\rho\xrightarrow{}\infty)
[or
g(\rho\xrightarrow{}\infty)
] on
the energy
\varepsilon
. Such a function goes through zero every time the energy value is guessed correctly. This allows us to use a bisection method
on the mentioned dependencies to calculate the energies.
