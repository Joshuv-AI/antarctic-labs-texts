Certifying optimality for convex quantum channel optimization problems
Bryan Coutts, Mark Girard, John Watrous

Abstract
We identify necessary and sufficient conditions for a quantum channel to be optimal for any convex optimization problem in which the optimization is taken over the set of all quantum channels of a fixed size. Optimality conditions for convex optimization problems over the set of all quantum measurements of a given system having a fixed number of measurement outcomes are obtained as a special case. In the case of linear objective functions for measurement optimization problems, our conditions reduce to the well-known Holevo–Yuen–Kennedy–Lax measurement optimality conditions. We illustrate how our conditions can be applied to various state transformation problems having non-linear objective functions based on the fidelity, trace distance, and quantum relative entropy.



1
Introduction

Several problems and settings that arise in quantum information theory can
be expressed as
optimization problems
in which a real-valued function,
defined for a class of quantum channels or measurements, is either minimized or
maximized.
The problem of
minimum error quantum state discrimination

[
3
]
, in which a quantum state randomly selected from a known
ensemble of states is to be identified with the smallest possible probability
of error by means of a measurement, provides a well-known example.
This problem is naturally expressed as the optimization of a real-valued linear
function defined on the set of all measurements with a fixed number of
outcomes.
Other examples arise in the study of
quantum cloning

[
11
,
28
]
and the closely related notion of

quantum money

[
1
]
,
where one is generally interested in knowing how well an optimally selected
quantum channel can transform a single copy of a given state into multiple
copies of the same state, with respect to a number of different figures of
merit.
Another example can be found in quantum complexity theory, in which

two-message quantum interactive proof systems

[
21
]
are
naturally analyzed as optimization problems in which the objective function
describes the probability that a given verifier accepts, and where the
optimization is over all quantum channels of a fixed size, which describe the
possible actions of a prover.

Concerning the optimization of
linear functions
defined on the set of
all measurements with a fixed number of outcomes, necessary and sufficient
conditions for optimality were identified by Holevo

[
18
,
19
]
and Yuen, Kennedy,
and Lax
[
33
,
34
]
.
These conditions, which are described explicitly later in this paper, are
relatively easy to check;
the problem of actually finding or approximating an optimal measurement, while
efficiently solvable through the use of semidefinite programming

[
22
,
20
,
13
]
, is in general a more computationally involved
task.
These optimality conditions can be easily extended to obtain optimality
conditions for real-valued linear functions defined on the set of all quantum
channels transforming one quantum system to another.

We prove a generalization of these results to convex optimization problems
whose objective functions are not necessarily linear.
To be more precise, we consider optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle f(\Phi)

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}),

where

f:\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

(2)

is a convex function.
Here,
\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
denotes the set of all channels (i.e., completely
positive and trace-preserving linear maps) from an input system to an output
system having associated complex Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
,
respectively.
A channel
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is said to be
optimal
for the
problem (
1
) if it is the case that
f(\Phi)\leq f(\Psi)

for all
\Psi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.
In this paper we do not consider the difficulty of finding or approximating
an optimal channel
\Phi
for a given function
f
, but instead we focus only
on the task of
verifying
that a given channel
\Phi
is indeed optimal.
The optimality conditions we obtain can be easily checked for differentiable
functions
f
, and can also be used to verify optimality of channels for
some non-differentiable functions.
We stress that our optimality conditions are not generic optimality conditions
that hold for all convex optimization problems, but rather rely on a specific
structure that arises when the optimization is over all quantum channels of a
fixed size.

There are, of course, situations in which one would prefer a method to find an
optimal channel
\Phi
for a chosen function
f
, as opposed to simply verifying
the optimality of a given
\Phi
, but the task of verifying optimality
nevertheless has value for multiple reasons.
For instance, one might hypothesize that a particular channel
\Phi
is optimal
based upon an intuition concerning the function
f
, or upon a heuristic
method, making the task of verifying optimality essentially important.
The computational task of finding an optimal solution for a chosen function

f
might also be expensive, time-consuming, or delegated to an untrusted
computer, but once this task has been performed the optimality of the solution
can be verified, allowing anyone who performs the verification to trust in
the optimality of the solution.
Finally, there are situations in which the function
f
could be indeterminate
in some respect, eliminating the possibility that a numerical computation
could reveal an optimal solution, but potentially allowing for an optimal
solution to be expressed and checked analytically.
(The results of Bacon, Childs, and van Dam
[
2
]
on hidden subgroup
algorithms for certain groups provide a striking example of this potential.)

Our optimality conditions are applicable to convex optimization problems in
which the optimization is over all measurements having a fixed number of
outcomes, as opposed to being over all channels of a fixed size.
This is done through a standard correspondence between measurements and

quantum-to-classical channels
, described in the section following this
one.
We observe that Holevo
[
18
,
19
]
also derived
optimality conditions for optimizations over measurements having differentiable
(but not necessarily convex) objective functions.
Holevo proved that these conditions are necessary for optimality, but did not
prove they are sufficient (as they are not sufficient in general).
When one restricts their attention to convex objective functions, our
optimality conditions are equivalent to a set of intermediate conditions
identified by Holevo, but not to the final set of conditions he identified.

We provide a few examples of how our optimality conditions can be applied to
interesting categories of optimization problems.
As a simple warm-up, we first explain how our conditions imply the
Holevo–Yuen–Kennedy–Lax conditions for the optimality of measurements for
minimum error state discrimination, which are easily extended to channel
optimization problems having linear objective functions.
We then discuss optimization problems relating to state transformations having
objective functions based on fidelity, trace distance, and quantum relative
entropy.
We note that optimization problems of the form we consider have recently been
studied in the context of
recovery measures
(i.e., the

fidelity of recovery
and generalizations of this measure)

[
16
,
29
,
10
,
12
,
4
,
5
]
.
Expositions of this topic can be found in
[
30
]
and Chapter 12 of

[
32
]
.

Background and notation

This section summarizes some concepts from convex analysis and optimization
theory, narrowly focused on their applications to this paper.
Further information on these topics can be found in
[
27
]
,

[
8
]
,
[
9
]
, and
[
25
]
, for
instance.
We assume the reader is familiar with quantum information theory, which is
covered in the books
[
26
]
,
[
32
]
, and
[
31
]
,
among others.
We will, however, summarize the notion of the Choi operator of a channel,
clarify its basic connection to the sorts of optimization problems we consider,
and discuss the correspondence between measurements and quantum-to-classical

It will also be helpful to begin the section by establishing some basic

Linear algebra notation and terminology

When we refer to a
complex Euclidean space
, we mean
\mathbb{C}^{n}
for some
positive integer
n
, or more generally the complex vector space consisting of
vectors indexed by an arbitrary finite set in place of the index set

\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
The elementary unit vectors of the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are denoted

e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
.
Complex Euclidean spaces will be denoted by capital calligraphic letters such
as
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
.

For a complex Euclidean space
\mathcal{X}
, the space of linear operators on
\mathcal{X}

is denoted
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{X})
, and the identity operator on
\mathcal{X}
is denoted

\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}
.
The adjoint (or conjugate-transpose) of an operator
X
is denoted
X^{\ast}
.
For indices
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the operator
E_{j,k}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is
defined as
E_{j,k}=e_{j}e_{k}^{\ast}
.
Equivalently, with respect to the basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, the matrix
representation of
E_{j,k}
has a 1 in the
(j,k)
entry, with all other
entries 0.

The real vector space of Hermitian operators acting on a complex Euclidean
space
\mathcal{X}
is denoted
\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
; the cone of positive semidefinite
operators acting on
\mathcal{X}
is denoted
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
; the set of positive
definite operators acting on
\mathcal{X}
is denoted
\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{X})
; and the set of
density operators (i.e., positive semidefinite operators having unit trace) is
denoted
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X})
.
The Hilbert–Schmidt inner product of two Hermitian operators

X,Y\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
is given by

\langle X,Y\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(XY).

(3)

For a subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of a complex Euclidean space
\mathcal{X}
, we
write
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
to denote the (orthogonal) projection
operator that projects onto the subspace
\mathcal{V}
.
Finally, whenever we refer to the
inverse
of a positive semidefinite
operator
X\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
, it should be understood that we are referring to
the
Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse
of
X
(i.e., the operator that acts as
the inverse of
X
on the image of
X
and zero on the kernel of
X
).

Convex functions taking real or infinite values

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a complex Euclidean space and let

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

(4)

be a function mapping each Hermitian operator to either a real number or to
positive infinity.
The
domain
of
f
is defined as

\operatorname{dom}(f)=\{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X}):f(X)\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(5)

For any function of the form
f:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined only on a
subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, one may naturally extend
f
to a
function of the form (
4
) by defining

f(X)=\infty
whenever
X\not\in\mathcal{C}
.

A function
f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is
convex

if
\operatorname{dom}(f)
is a convex set and
f
is convex on
\operatorname{dom}(f)
.
More explicitly,
f
is convex if for all
X,Y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
and

\lambda\in[0,1]
, it is the case that

\lambda X+(1-\lambda)Y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
and

f(\lambda X+(1-\lambda)Y)\leq\lambda f(X)+(1-\lambda)f(Y).

(6)

A function
f
of the form (
4
) is

proper
if
\operatorname{dom}(f)\neq\varnothing
.

The
indicator function
of a set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
is the
function

I_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

(7)

defined as

I_{\mathcal{C}}(X)=\begin{cases}0&X\in\mathcal{C}\\
\infty&X\notin\mathcal{C}\end{cases}

(8)

for all
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.
It is evident that
\operatorname{dom}(I_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{C}
for every set

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, and if
\mathcal{C}
is a convex set then
I_{\mathcal{C}}

is a convex function.

Subdifferentials

Let
f
be a proper function of the form (
4
)
and let
X\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
.
The
subdifferential
of
f
at
X
is the set defined as

\partial f(X)=\{Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\,:\,f(Y)-f(X)\geq\langle Z,Y-X\rangle\;\text{for all}\;Y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\}.

(9)

A key property of the subdifferential of a proper function, which follows
trivially from the definition of the subdifferential, is its relation to global
minima:

X\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
is a global minimizer of
f
if and only if
0\in\partial f(X)
.
Two additional properties of subdifferentials that are relevant to this paper
are the following:

1.

If
f
is convex and differentiable at
X\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
then

\partial f(X)=\{\nabla f(X)\}
.

2.

If
\lVert\mspace{1mu}\cdot\mspace{1mu}\rVert
is any norm on
\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
and
f(X)=\lVert\mspace{1mu}X\mspace{1mu}\rVert

for all
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, then

\partial f(X)=\{Y:\langle Y,X\rangle=\lVert X\rVert,\,\lVert Y\rVert_{*}\leq 1\},

(10)

where
\lVert\mspace{1mu}Y\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\ast}=\sup\{\langle Y,X\rangle:\lVert\mspace{1mu}X\mspace{1mu}\rVert\leq 1\}
is
the dual norm to
\lVert\mspace{1mu}\cdot\mspace{1mu}\rVert
. (See Example 14.4.2 in
[
23
]
.)

We will make use of the following proposition, which presents a variant of
the
chain rule for subdifferentials
.
In the statement of this proposition,
\operatorname{relint}
denotes the

relative interior
of a set, and for a linear map

\Lambda:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, the map

\Lambda^{\ast}:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})

(11)

denotes the adjoint map to
\Lambda
, which is the uniquely determined linear
map that satisfies

\langle X,\Lambda(Y)\rangle=\langle\Lambda^{\ast}(X),Y\rangle

(12)

for all
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
and
Y\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
.
While the following result can be easily derived from basic rules of convex
analysis, we note that this particular result is stated and proved as
Theorem 7.2 in
[
24
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be complex Euclidean spaces, let

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a convex function,
and let
g:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
be an affine linear map,
meaning that

g(Y)=\Lambda(Y)+A

(13)

for all
Y\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
, for some choice of a linear map

\Lambda:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
and an operator

A\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.

1.

For every
Y\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
, it is the case that

\partial(f\circ g)(Y)\supseteq\Lambda^{\ast}(\partial f(g(Y))).

(14)

2.

If
\operatorname{im}(\Lambda)\cap\operatorname{relint}(\operatorname{dom}(f))\not=\varnothing
, then

\partial(f\circ g)(Y)=\Lambda^{\ast}(\partial f(g(Y)))

(15)

for every
Y\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
.

Lastly, we present the subdifferentials of some indicator functions.
The subdifferential of the indicator function of a set

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
at an operator
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
may be
expressed as

\partial I_{\mathcal{C}}(X)=\begin{cases}\{Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\,:\,\langle Z,X\rangle\geq\langle Z,Y\rangle\;\text{for all}\;Y\in\mathcal{C}\}&\text{if }X\in\mathcal{C}\\
\varnothing&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(16)

The set described in (
16
) is sometimes referred to as the

normal cone
of
\mathcal{C}
at
X
.
(For further discussion of normal cones and subdifferentials of indicator
functions, see Section 2.2 in
[
25
]
and Chapter 23 in

[
27
]
.)
For a positive semidefinite operator
X\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
it is straightforward to
verify that

\partial I_{\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})}(X)=\{Z\,:\,-Z\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X}),\,\langle Z,X\rangle=0\}=\{Z\,:\,-Z\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X}),\,ZX=0\}.

(17)

Let
\Lambda:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
be a linear map, let

A\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
be an operator, and define the set

\mathcal{K}=\{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\,:\,\Lambda(X)=A\}.

(18)

(This is the pre-image of
A
under
\Lambda
.)
For an operator
X\in\mathcal{K}
, it holds that

\partial I_{\mathcal{K}}(X)=\{Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\,:\,\langle Z,Y\rangle=0\;\text{for all}\;Y\in\ker(\Lambda)\}=\operatorname{im}(\Lambda^{*}).

(19)

(See for example Proposition 2.12 in
[
25
]
.)

Convex optimization problems

Convex optimization problems in quantum information theory often have the
following general form, for some choice of a convex function

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and a convex set

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
:

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle f(X)

(20)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle X\in\mathcal{C}.

An operator
X\in\mathcal{C}\cap\operatorname{dom}(f)
is said to be
optimal
for the
optimization problem (
20
) if
f(X)\leq f(Y)
for all

Y\in\mathcal{C}
.
As we will see below, convenient conditions for optimality can be given if it
is the case that

\operatorname{relint}\bigl(\operatorname{dom}(f)\bigr)\cap\operatorname{relint}(\mathcal{C})\neq\varnothing.

(21)

A (constrained) convex optimization problem of the form (
20
)
can be considered as an unconstrained convex optimization problem by
minimizing
f(X)+I_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
over all Hermitian operators

X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.
The domain of the function
f+I_{\mathcal{C}}
is given by

\operatorname{dom}(f+I_{\mathcal{C}})=\operatorname{dom}(f)\cap\mathcal{C}.

(22)

As was mentioned previously, an operator
X\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\cap\mathcal{C}
is a global
minimizer of
f(X)+I_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
if and only if

0\in\partial(f+I_{\mathcal{C}})(X)
.
If the condition in (
21
) holds and both
f
and
\mathcal{C}

are convex, a subdifferential sum-rule (see, e.g. Theorem 23.8 of

[
27
]
) implies that

\partial(f+I_{\mathcal{C}})(X)=\partial f(X)+\partial I_{\mathcal{C}}(X)

(23)

for all
X\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\cap\mathcal{C}
.
For this reason, a characterization of the subdifferential set

\partial I_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
for a convex set
\mathcal{C}
can be useful in
identifying necessary and sufficient conditions for optimality.

Optimizing over Choi operators of channels

For complex Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, the set of completely
positive, trace-preserving linear maps (i.e., channels) from
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{X})
to

\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y})
is denoted
\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.
Given a linear map
\Phi:\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y})
, its Choi
representation
J(\Phi)\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
is defined as

J(\Phi)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\Phi(E_{j,k})\otimes E_{j,k},

(24)

assuming
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
(An analogous definition is used for index sets other than

\{1,\ldots,n\}
.)
Through this representation, the set of channels is isomorphic to the set

J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))=\{X\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\,:\,\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(X)=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\}\subseteq\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X}).

(25)

This set is convex, and it is helpful to observe that its relative interior is

\operatorname{relint}\bigl(J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))\bigr)=\{X\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\,:\,\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(X)=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\}.

(26)

The action of a map
\Phi
can be recovered from its Choi representation
by the relation

\Phi(X)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{X}}((\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}})J(\Phi))

(27)

for all operators
X\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{X})
, where
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
denotes the transpose of
X
.

An optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle g(\Phi)

(28)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}),

where

g:\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

(29)

is a given function, can equivalently be expressed as

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle f(X)

(30)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})),

where

f:J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

(31)

is defined as
f(J(\Phi))=g(\Phi)
for all
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.
Although the formulations (
28
) and
(
30
) are equivalent, it will be convenient for us to
focus primarily on the formulation (
30
).
The results we obtain can, however, easily be adapted to the formulation
(
28
).

Subdifferentials of the indicator function of the set of
Choi operators of channels

The following proposition, which follows from a straightforward application of
the rules of subdifferentials, provides a useful characterization of the
subdifferential of the indicator function of
J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))
at every
point
X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))
.
While we suspect the result is not new, we are not aware of a reference for it,
and have included its proof for completeness.

Proposition 2
.

For complex Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, and for
X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))
, it holds that

\partial I_{J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))}(X)=\{-Y-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z\,:\,Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X}),\;Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X}),\;YX=0\}.

(32)

Proof.

Define

\mathcal{K}=\{K\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\,:\,\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(K)=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\}
.
It is the case that

J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))=\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\cap\mathcal{K},

(33)

and therefore

I_{J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))}=I_{\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})}+I_{\mathcal{K}}.

(34)

As
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
and
\mathcal{K}
are both convex and

\operatorname{relint}(\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X}))\cap\operatorname{relint}(\mathcal{K})=\{P\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\,:\,\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(P)=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\}\not=\varnothing,

(35)

one has that

\partial I_{J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))}(X)=\partial I_{\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})}(X)+\partial I_{\mathcal{K}}(X).

(36)

Finally, from (
17
) it holds that

\partial I_{\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})}(X)=\{-Y\,:\,Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X}),\;XY=0\}

(37)

and from (
19
) it holds that

\partial I_{\mathcal{K}}(X)=\{\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z\,:\,Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\}.

(38)

This implies the proposition.
∎

The Lagrange dual problem for channel optimization

Consider a channel optimization problem expressed in the following form
that is equivalent to (
30
):

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle f(X)

(39)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(X)=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}},

\displaystyle X\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X}).

One may then formulate the associated Lagrange dual problem:

\displaystyle\text{maximize}

\displaystyle g(Y,Z)

(40)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X}),

\displaystyle Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X}),

where

g(Y,Z)=\operatorname{Tr}(Z)+\inf_{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})}\bigl(f(X)-\bigl\langle X,Y+\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z\bigr\rangle\bigr)

(41)

for all
Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
and
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.

The optimal value of the Lagrange dual problem (
40
)
is a lower-bound for the optimal value of the original problem
(
39
) (even if the function
f
is not convex), which is a
property known as weak duality.

Slater’s theorem
implies that if
f
is convex, and there exists a
positive definite operator
X\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
such that

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(X)=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
X\in\operatorname{relint}(\operatorname{dom}(f))
, then the problems
(
39
) and (
40
) have the same
optimal value—a property known as
strong duality
.
(A proof of Slater’s theorem can be found in Section 5.3.2 of
[
9
]
.)

Quantum measurements as channels

Optimizations over the set of all measurements having a fixed number of
outcomes can be expressed as optimizations over channels, as we now explain.
Consider first a measurement on a complex Euclidean space
\mathcal{X}
that has
m

possible measurement outcomes, and is described by measurement operators

\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\}\subseteq\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
.
Letting
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{C}^{m}
, this measurement can be represented by the channel

\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
defined as

\Phi(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle P_{k},X\rangle\,E_{k,k}

(42)

for all
X\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{X})
.
Any channel expressible in this form is called a

quantum-to-classical channel
.
An equivalent condition to a channel taking the form
(
42
) is that its Choi operator takes the
form

J(\Phi)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}E_{k,k}\otimes P_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}.

(43)

Next, for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, define a linear map

\Xi_{k}:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
as

\Xi_{k}(X)=\bigl((e_{k}^{\ast}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}})X(e_{k}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}})\bigr)^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}},

(44)

and observe that one has

P_{k}=\Xi_{k}(J(\Phi))

(45)

for the quantum-to-classical channel
\Phi
given by
(
42
).
In words, for a measurement represented by operators
\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\}
, the
linear map
\Xi_{k}
allows for the recovery of the operator
P_{k}
from the Choi
operator
J(\Phi)
of the quantum-to-classical channel associated with that
measurement.
A function
g(P_{1},\ldots,P_{m})
of these measurement operators can therefore
be expressed as a function

f(J(\Phi))=g\bigl(\Xi_{1}(J(\Phi)),\ldots,\Xi_{m}(J(\Phi))\bigr)

(46)

of the Choi operator
J(\Phi)
.
Note that if
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is an arbitrary (i.e., not necessarily
quantum-to-classical) channel, then the operators
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
defined by
(
45
) will still necessarily satisfy
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
and
P_{1}+\cdots+P_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}
, and therefore represent a
valid measurement.
(In general, the same measurement is given by a continuum of channels
\Phi
,
including the quantum-to-classical channel described earlier.)
An optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle g(P_{1},\ldots,P_{m})

(47)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle P_{1}+\cdots+P_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}

can therefore be expressed equivalently as follows:

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle f(X)

(48)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))

for
f
defined from
g
as in (
46
).

3
Optimality conditions for convex channel optimization

In this section, we present our main general result regarding optimality
conditions for convex optimization problems over quantum channels.
As suggested in the previous section, it is convenient to associate quantum
channels with their Choi representations, and to consider optimization problems
of the form

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle f(X)

(49)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))

for various choices of convex functions

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}.

(50)

Optimality conditions for such problems can be translated to optimality
conditions for problems of the form (
1
), as will be
illustrated in the section following this one.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a convex
function such that

\operatorname{relint}\bigl(\operatorname{dom}(f)\bigr)\cap\operatorname{relint}\bigl(J\bigl(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\bigr)\bigr)\not=\varnothing,

(51)

and let
X\in J\bigl(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\bigr)
be the Choi representation of a
channel such that
X\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
.
The following statements are equivalent:

1.

The operator
X
is optimal for the optimization problem
(
49
).

2.

There exists an operator
H\in\partial f(X)
satisfying

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX)\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{and}\quad H\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX).

(52)

Proof.

An operator
X
is optimal for the problem (
49
) if and
only if

0\in\partial(f+I_{J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))})(X)=\partial f(X)+\partial I_{J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))}(X).

(53)

By the characterization of the subdifferential of the indicator function

I_{J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))}
given by
Proposition
2
, it follows that
X
is optimal
for the optimization problem (
49
) if and only if there
exist operators
Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
,
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, and

H\in\partial f(X)
satisfying

YX=0\quad\text{and}\quad H-Y-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z=0.

(54)

Assume first that statement 2 holds, and choose

Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
and
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
as

Y=H-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z\quad\text{and}\quad Z=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX).

(55)

As
X
and
Y
are both positive operators and

\langle Y,X\rangle=\langle H-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX),X\rangle=\langle H,X\rangle-\langle\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX),\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\rangle=0,

(56)

it follows that
YX=0
.
The conditions in (
54
) are therefore satisfied, implying that
statement 1 holds.

Now assume that statement 1 holds, so that there exist operators

Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
,
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, and
H\in\partial f(X)

such that
YX=0
and
H-Y-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z=0
.
It follows that
HX=(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z)X
and therefore

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}\bigl((\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z)X\bigr)=Z\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(X)=Z,

(57)

which is a Hermitian operator.
Moreover, one has
H-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX)=Y
, which is positive
semidefinite by assumption.
The conditions in (
52
) are therefore satisfied, which implies
that statement 2 holds, completing the proof.
∎

To make use of Theorem
3
, one requires that the
relative interior of the domain of the objective function contains at least one
point in the relative interior of the set of channels.
This requirement is precisely Slater’s condition for this optimization problem,
which guarantees strong duality with the corresponding dual problem
(
40
).
This regularity condition is automatically satisfied if
f
is continuous at at
least one point in the set of Choi representations of channels.
In particular, if
f
is differentiable at
X
then one may take the operator

H
in Theorem
3
to be
H=\nabla f(X)
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a convex
function, let
X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))
be the Choi representation of a
channel, and assume that
f
is differentiable at
X
.
The operator
X
is an optimal solution to the optimization problem
(
49
) if and only if

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\nabla f(X)X)\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla f(X)\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\nabla f(X)X).

(58)

Proof.

As
f
is differentiable at
X
, one has that

\partial f(X)=\{\nabla f(X)\}
.
Furthermore,
f
must be finite in some neighborhood around
X
and thus

\operatorname{relint}(\operatorname{dom}(f))\cap\operatorname{relint}(J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})))\neq\varnothing
must hold.
The result now follows from Theorem
3
.
∎

We remark that the optimality conditions represented by this corollary
appear to be a special feature of problems involving an optimization over
channels.
In essence, for differentiable convex quantum channel optimization problems,
every optimal primal solution
X
determines an optimal dual solution
consisting of operators

Z=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\nabla f(X)X)
and

Y=\nabla f(X)-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\nabla f(X)X)
.

It is natural to ask if there is an approximate version of the implication that
statement 2 implies statement 1 in Theorem
3
.
That is, if the requirements (
52
) hold approximately for some

H\in\partial f(X)
, then is
X
necessarily close to being optimal?
The following theorem demonstrates that this is indeed the case, which allows
for bounds to be placed on the optimal value of such optimization problems in
the case the conditions in (
52
) cannot be verified exactly.

Theorem 5
.

Let
f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a function,
let
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
be a channel such that
J(\Phi)\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
,
and let
H\in\partial f(X)
.
It is the case that

f(J(\Phi))\leq\inf_{\Psi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}f(J(\Psi))+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{X}),

(59)

where

\varepsilon=\inf_{P\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})}\bigl\lVert\mspace{1mu}H-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))-P\mspace{1mu}\bigr\rVert_{\infty}.

(60)

Proof.

Because the spectral norm is a continuous function, a compactness argument
implies that there must exist a positive semidefinite operator

P\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
for which

\varepsilon=\bigl\lVert\mspace{1mu}H-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))-P\mspace{1mu}\bigr\rVert_{\infty}.

(61)

We will let such a
P
be fixed for the remainder of the proof.

Define a Hermitian operator

Z=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))^{\ast}-\varepsilon\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}},

(62)

and define

Y=H-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z.

(63)

Also define

A=H-\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))-P,

(64)

so that
\lVert\mspace{1mu}A\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}=\varepsilon
, and therefore

\biggl\lVert\mspace{1mu}\frac{1}{2}A+\frac{1}{2}A^{\ast}\mspace{1mu}\biggr\rVert_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon.

(65)

It is the case that

Y=P+\frac{A+A^{\ast}}{2}+\varepsilon\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}\geq P,

(66)

and therefore
Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
.

Now consider the Lagrange dual problem (
40
)
associated with the minimization of
f
over all channels in

\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.
As
Z
is Hermitian and
Y
is positive semidefinite,
(Y,Z)
is a dual
feasible solution to this dual problem.
As
H\in\partial f(J(\Phi))
, one has that

f(X)-\langle H,X\rangle\geq f(J(\Phi))-\langle H,J(\Phi)\rangle

(67)

for every
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
.
Therefore, when the dual objective function
g
is evaluated at
(Y,Z)
,
we find that

\displaystyle g(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(Z)+\inf_{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})}\bigl(f(X)-\bigl\langle X,Y+\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes Z\bigr\rangle\bigr)

(68)

\displaystyle=\langle H,J(\Phi)\rangle-\varepsilon\,\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{X})+\inf_{X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})}\bigl(f(X)-\langle H,X\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq f(J(\Phi))-\varepsilon\,\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{X}).

The theorem follows by weak duality.
∎

We note that the previous theorem does not require the function
f
to be
convex, or for the associated optimization problem to satisfy the conditions
of Slater’s theorem.
However, having knowledge of the subdifferential
\partial f(X)
at an operator

X
requires knowledge of the global behavior of the function
f
when
f
is
not convex, so the usefulness of Theorem
5
may be limited
when
f
is not convex.

Remark 6
.

Theorem
5
implies that every feasible primal solution

X
to the optimization problem in (
30
) for which

\partial f(X)
is non-empty provides both an upper bound
and
a lower
bound to the optimal value. Indeed, from (
59
) one has that

f(X)-\varepsilon\leq\inf_{Y\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))}f(Y)\leq f(X)

(69)

provided that one can compute the value of
\varepsilon
as defined in
(
60
) for some
H\in\partial f(X)
.

4
Applications

In this section we apply the optimality conditions given by
Theorem
3
to a few categories of examples,
including the simple case of channel optimization problems having linear
objective functions and three variants of problems involving quantum state
transformations.
In these examples we will make use of various facts concerning differentiation
for functions mapping between spaces of Hermitian operators; a short discussion
of this topic, along with a lemma that is needed for one of the examples, can
be found in Appendix
A
.
Additionally, we remark that Theorem
7
can be
used to prove that the generalized fidelity of recovery is multiplicative.

4.1
Linear objective functions

We will begin by considering the simple case in which the objective function

f
in Theorem
3
is linear.
In this situation, the optimization problem (
49
)
may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle\langle H,X\rangle

(70)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}))

for some choice of a Hermitian operator
H\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
.
The subdifferential of the function
f(X)=\langle H,X\rangle
is given by

\partial f(X)=\{H\}
for all
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
.
By Theorem
3
it follows that the Choi operator

X=J(\Phi)
of a channel
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is optimal for the
problem (
70
) if and only if

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX)\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{and}\quad H\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX).

(71)

We observe that this optimality criterion can alternatively be obtained through
semidefinite programming duality and complementary slackness.
(See, for instance, Exercise 3.5 of
[
31
]
, observing that the
inequality is reversed in that exercise because the optimization problem is
expressed as a maximization rather than a minimization.)

The problem of
minimum error state discrimination
, which was mentioned
in the introduction, is a special case in which the function
f
in the
optimization problem (
49
) is linear.
Consider an ensemble of states, which represents the random selection of one of
a finite number of quantum states according to a given probability
distribution.
Formally speaking, an ensemble is described by a collection

\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}\subseteq\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X})
of density operators together with
a probability vector
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
.
The problem of minimum-error state discrimination seeks a measurement on
the system represented by the space
\mathcal{X}
that identifies, with the minimum
possible probability of error, a state chosen randomly according to this
ensemble.

For a given choice of a measurement, represented by operators

\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\}\subseteq\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
, the error probability incurred by
this measurement can be expressed as

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl\langle\rho-p_{k}\rho_{k},P_{k}\bigr\rangle,

(72)

where

\rho=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\rho_{k}

(73)

is the average state of the ensemble.
A minimization of the error probability
(
72
) over all measurements

\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\}
can be represented as an optimization of the form
(
70
) by letting
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
setting

H=\sum_{k=1}^{n}E_{k,k}\otimes(\rho-p_{k}\rho_{k})^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}.

(74)

The measurement described by
\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\}
, which may alternatively be
represented by a quantum-to-classical channel whose Choi operator is defined as

X=\sum_{k=1}^{n}E_{k,k}\otimes P_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}},

(75)

is optimal for the minimization of the error probability
(
72
) if and only of the conditions
(
71
) hold.
These conditions can be simplified by first calculating that

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\rho-p_{k}\rho_{k})^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}P_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}=\rho^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\rho_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}P_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}},

(76)

then observing that this operator is Hermitian if and only if the operator

\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}P_{k}\rho_{k}

(77)

is Hermitian, and finally noting that the inequality

H\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HX)
is equivalent to

\sum_{j=1}^{n}E_{j,j}\otimes p_{j}\rho_{j}\leq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}P_{k}\rho_{k},

(78)

which may alternatively be expressed as

\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}P_{k}\rho_{k}\geq p_{j}\rho_{j}\quad\text{(for all $j=1,\ldots,n$)}.

(79)

The conditions that the operator (
77
) is Hermitian and satisfies
the inequalities (
79
) comprise the Holevo–Yuen–Kennedy–Lax
conditions for measurement optimality

[
18
,
19
,
33
,
34
]
.

4.2
State transformation

The second category of channel optimization problems we consider involves
channels that transform one state into another in such a way that the distance
to a target state is minimized.
One may consider any number of specific measures of distance in such a problem;
we will analyze measures based on the fidelity, trace distance, and quantum
relative entropy.
For each of these measures, we will consider the situation in which two
bipartite states,
\rho\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
and

\sigma\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
, for complex Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{X}
,

\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
, are given.
The optimization problem to be considered is to minimize the distance (or
maximize the similarity) between the states

\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})}\bigr)(\rho)
and
\sigma
, with respect to the
measure under consideration, over all possible channels

\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.

Optimization problems of this sort were suggested in

[
29
]
(see Remark 6) in the special case that

\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{W}
and
\rho=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{W}}(\sigma)
, so that the
aim of the channel
\Phi
is to “recover” the original state
\sigma
from
the portion of it represented by
\rho
.
The resulting quantities are known as the
fidelity of recovery
,

relative entropy of recovery
, and so on.
These and related measures have been studied in a number of recent papers
(such as
[
16
,
10
,
12
,
4
,
5
]
), and
the more general situation in which
\rho
and
\sigma
are arbitrary states
arises naturally in this study.
(For example,
[
4
]
uses the term

generalized fidelity of recovery
in the situation mentioned above in the
case of the fidelity.)

As a further application of the methods introduced in this paper,
it is straightforward to show (by applying the result of Theorem

7
) that the generalized fidelity of recovery is
multiplicative. This fact has already been shown in
[
4
]
, where it
was shown by examining the fidelity of recovery as a semidefinite program.
A detailed proof using Theorem
7
is presented in
Appendix
B
.

When analyzing optimality conditions for these problems, it will be helpful to
refer to the
evaluation map
corresponding to the operator

\rho\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
.
This is the uniquely determined completely positive map

\Psi_{\rho}\in\mathrm{CP}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z})
that satisfies

\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho}\bigr)(J(\Phi))=\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})}\bigr)(\rho)

(80)

for every complex Euclidean space
\mathcal{Y}
and every channel

\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.
The relationship between the map
\Psi_{\rho}
and the state
\rho
is closely
related to the Choi representation of maps: assuming for the moment that

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, one has that

\rho=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}E_{j,k}\otimes\Psi_{\rho}(E_{j,k}).

(81)

That is, up to swapping the tensor factors corresponding to
\mathcal{X}
and

\mathcal{Z}
, the state
\rho
is the Choi operator of the map
\Psi_{\rho}
.

Objective functions based on the fidelity

For positive semidefinite operators
P,Q\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
, one defines the
fidelity between
P
and
Q
as

\operatorname{F}(P,Q)=\Bigl\lVert\mspace{1mu}\sqrt{P}\sqrt{Q}\mspace{1mu}\Bigr\rVert_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}\sqrt{\sqrt{P}Q\sqrt{P}}.

(82)

The first variant of the optimal state transformation problem we will consider
is as follows:

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle-\operatorname{F}\bigl(\sigma,\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})}\bigr)(\rho)\bigr)

(83)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

The fidelity function is jointly concave (see for example Corollary 3.26 in

[
31
]
), and therefore is concave in each of its arguments, from
which it follows that this problem is a convex optimization problem.
It is possible to express this optimization problem as a semidefinite program,
as is demonstrated in
[
5
]
.

The following theorem establishes optimality conditions for a channel

\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
in the optimization problem
(
83
) under the assumption that the
operator
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{X}}(\rho)
is positive definite.
We note that the theorem statement does not actually require
\rho
and

\sigma
to have unit trace—they can be arbitrary positive semidefinite
operators, but we nevertheless use the letters
\rho
and
\sigma
to make the
connection to the optimization problem
(
83
) clear.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{Z})

be positive semidefinite operators, for complex Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{X}
,

\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
, and assume that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{X}}(\rho)
is a positive
definite operator.
A channel
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is optimal for the optimization problem
(
83
) if and only if the following two
conditions are met:

1.

\operatorname{im}(\sigma)\subseteq\operatorname{im}\bigl(\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})}\bigr)(\rho)\bigr)
.

2.

The operator

H=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho}^{\ast})\Bigl(\sqrt{\sigma}\mspace{1mu}\bigl(\sqrt{\sigma}\,(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)\sqrt{\sigma}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\sigma}\Bigr)

(84)

satisfies

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{and}\quad H\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi)).

(85)

(As per the convention mentioned in Section
2
,
the inverse in (
84
) refers to the Moore–Penrose
pseudo-inverse in case
\sigma
does not have full rank.)

Remark 8
.

The theorem assumes that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{X}}(\rho)
has full rank, as this
assumption allows for a cleaner theorem statement.
It is, however, straightforward to apply the theorem to a situation in which

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{X}}(\rho)
does not have full rank.
Specifically, for an arbitrary choice of
\rho
and
\sigma
, one may take

B\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{Z})
to be an isometry for which
BB^{\ast}
is the

An interesting special case of the optimization problem

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

Objective functions based on trace distance

Next we consider an optimization problem that is analogous to
(
83
), but based on the trace distance
rather than the fidelity:

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle\bigl\lVert\mspace{1mu}\sigma-(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)\mspace{1mu}\bigr\rVert_{1}

(107)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

The objective function of this optimization problem is convex (with respect to

\Phi

to obtain
optimality conditions for this problem.
We note that, similar to its variant based on the fidelity described above,
this optimization problem can be represented as a semidefinite program.

For this optimization problem, the optimality conditions we obtain

We do, however, obtain an efficiently checkable condition that is sufficient
for optimality, and we conjecture that this condition is also a necessary for

Corollary 10
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{Z})

\mathcal{X}
,

\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
, and let
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
be a channel.
The channel
\Phi
is optimal for the state transformation problem in
(
107

Y\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
with
\lVert\mspace{1mu}Y\mspace{1mu}\rVert_{\infty}=1

following conditions are satisfied:

1.

It is the case that

\bigl\lVert\mspace{1mu}\sigma-(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)\mspace{1mu}\bigr\rVert_{1}=\bigl\langle Y,\sigma-(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)\bigr\rangle

The operator

H=\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho}^{\ast}\bigr)(Y)

(108)

satisfies

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{and}\quad H\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}\bigl(HJ(\Phi)\bigr).

(109)

Corollary
10
follows directly from
Theorem
3
and applying the rules of
subdifferentiation presented in Section
2
.
Indeed, operators of the form in (

elements of the subdifferential of the objective function in
(
107
) at
J(\Phi)
.
As a generalization, one can replace the trace norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}
in
the statement of Corollary
10
with any other norm
on operators, and replace
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
with the corresponding
dual norm.

In the event that the operator
\sigma-(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)

arising in Corollary

has no zero eigenvalues,
there is a unique choice of
Y
for which the first condition of the theorem

Specifically, if

\sigma-(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\Pi_{k}

However, if it is the case that

, and from

we conclude only that the optimality

is equivalent to the existence of at least one such choice of

for

It is reasonable, though, to view the operator

defined by
(

), where now it is to be understood that

\operatorname{sign}(0)=0

optimality may be verified.
We conjecture, based on numerical evidence, that this choice yields an
efficiently checkable necessary and sufficient optimality condition.

Objective functions based on relative entropy

Finally, we consider a variant of the optimal state transformation problem
based on the quantum relative entropy.
For positive semidefinite operators

entropy of

with respect to

\mathrm{D}(P\lVert Q)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}(P\log(P))-\operatorname{Tr}(P\log(Q))&\text{if}\;\operatorname{im}(P)\subseteq\operatorname{im}(Q)\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The specific variant of the problem to be considered is

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}\bigl(\sigma\mspace{1mu}\big\|\mspace{1mu}(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)\bigr)

(116)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

Because the relative entropy can be approximated through the use of

[

15
]

efficiently approximate the optimization problem
(

Theorem 12
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{Z})

be positive semidefinite operators, for complex Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{X}
,

\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
, and assume that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{X}}(\rho)
is a positive
definite operator.
A channel
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is optimal for the optimization problem
(
116
) if and only if the following
two conditions are met:

1.

\operatorname{im}(\sigma)\subseteq\operatorname{im}\bigl((\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)\bigr)
.

2.

The operator

H=-(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho}^{\ast})\bigl(D\log\bigl(\Pi\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})}\bigr)(\rho)\Pi\bigr)(\sigma)\bigr)

satisfies

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi))\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{and}\quad H\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}}(HJ(\Phi)).

(118)

Here,
\Pi
denotes the projection onto the image of
\sigma
and

D\log(P)
denotes the differential operator of the
logarithm function at the operator
P
(as described in (
167
)
at the end of Appendix
A
).

Proof.

If the first condition does not hold for a given channel
\Phi
, then the
objective function in (
116
) takes
an infinite value.
However, by the assumption that
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{X}}(\rho)
is positive definite,
one has that the channel

\Omega(X)=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(X)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}}{\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{Y})}

(119)

yields a finite value for the same objective function, implying that

\Phi
is not optimal.
If
\Phi
is optimal, the first condition must therefore hold.
It remains to prove that if
\Phi
satisfies the first condition, then

\Phi
is optimal if and only if the second condition holds.

Let
r
be the rank of
\sigma
, let
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{C}^{r}
, and let

A\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{Z})
be an isometry that satisfies

AA^{\ast}=\Pi_{\operatorname{im}(\sigma)}
.
Define a linear map
\Xi:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{W})

as

\Xi(X)=A^{\ast}\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho}\bigr)(X)A

(120)

for all
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})
.
For every channel
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
it is the case that

\mathrm{D}\bigl(\sigma\mspace{1mu}\big\|\mspace{1mu}(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z})})(\rho)\bigr)=\mathrm{D}\bigl(A^{\ast}\sigma A\mspace{1mu}\big\|\mspace{1mu}\Xi(J(\Phi))\bigr).

(121)

With this observation in mind, define a function

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}\otimes\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
as

f(X)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{D}\bigl(A^{\ast}\sigma A\mspace{1mu}\big\|\mspace{1mu}\Xi(X)\bigr)&\text{if}\;\Xi(X)\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{W})\\
\infty&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

so that a given channel
\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle f(X)

\displaystyle X\in J(\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})).

is differentiable at every operator

Appendix A
Gradients and subdifferentials of functions on matrices

Results in the main body of this have required the computation of gradients
and subdifferentials for various functions mapping Hermitian operators to the
real numbers.
In this appendix we provide details on these computations.

Definitions and basic results

It is appropriate to begin with some basic definitions.
Throughout this discussion,
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
are arbitrary complex
Euclidean spaces.

Suppose that

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})

(128)

is a partial function, meaning that it may only be defined on some subset of
inputs
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.
The function
f
is
(Fréchet) differentiable
at
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})

if there exists a linear map

\Phi:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})

(129)

for which the equation

\lim_{Z\rightarrow 0}\frac{\bigl\lVert\mspace{1mu}f(X+Z)-f(X)-\Phi(Z)\mspace{1mu}\bigr\rVert}{\lVert\mspace{1mu}Z\mspace{1mu}\rVert}=0

(130)

is satisfied.
If there does exist such a map, it must be unique, and we denote it by

Df(X)
.
Whenever
f
is differentiable at
X
, it must be the case that

Df(X)(Z)=\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}f(X+tZ)\>\Big|_{t=0}

(131)

for all choices of
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.
In the special case that
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{C}
, which is equivalent to
f
taking the
form

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

(132)

one has that

Df(X)(Z)=\big\langle\nabla f(X),Z\big\rangle

(133)

for all
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, assuming
f
is differentiable at
X
.

The
chain rule
for differentiation states that if

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\quad\text{and}\quad g:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Z}),

(134)

f
is differentiable at
X
, and
g
is differentiable at
Y=f(X)
, then

D(g\circ f)(X)(Z)=Dg(f(X))(Df(X)(Z))

(135)

for all
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.

Affine linear functions

A function

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})

(136)

is
affine linear
if there exists a linear map

\Phi:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})

(137)

and an operator
Y\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
such that

f(X)=\Phi(X)+Y

(138)

for all
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.
Every such function is differentiable at every
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, with its
derivative given by

Df(X)=\Phi.

(139)

For an arbitrary function

g:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Z}),

(140)

one therefore finds that

D(g\circ f)(X)(Z)=Dg(f(X))(\Phi(Z)),

(141)

provided that
g
is differentiable at
f(X)
, and if
g
takes the form

g:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

(142)

then it is the case that

\nabla(g\circ f)(X)=\Phi^{\ast}(\nabla g(f(X))).

(143)

Real-valued functions extended to Hermitian operators

If
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function, then it may be extended to a function
of the form

g:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})

(144)

in a standard way:
for any choice of
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
, one considers the spectral
decomposition

X=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\Pi_{k}

(145)

of
X
, then defines

g(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}f(\lambda_{k})\Pi_{k}.

(146)

(It is typical that this extended function is given the same name as the
original function on the real numbers, but for the sake of clarity we have
introduced a distinct name for the extended function.)
Naturally, if
f
is defined only on a subset of
\mathbb{R}
, then
g
is defined for
all
X
whose eigenvalues are contained in the domain of
f
.

The function
g
is differentiable at every Hermitian operator whose
eigenvalues correspond to differentiable points of the function
f
.
The derivative of
g
can be described explicitly by first defining a function

h(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{cases}\frac{f(\alpha)-f(\beta)}{\alpha-\beta}&\text{if}\;\alpha\not=\beta\\
f^{\prime}(\alpha)&\text{if}\;\alpha=\beta\end{cases}

(147)

for every pair of points
(\alpha,\beta)
for which
f
is differentiable at
both
\alpha
and
\beta
.
(The function
h
is sometimes called the
first divided difference
of

f
, although this terminology is sometimes limited to the case that

\alpha\not=\beta
.)
In terms of this function, the derivative of
g
at an operator
X
having a spectral decomposition (
145
) is

Dg(X)(Z)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}h(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k})\Pi_{j}Z\Pi_{k}

(148)

for every
Z\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
(see for example Theorem V.3.3 in
[
6
]
and Theorem 3.25 in
[
17
]
).

Gradients of functions involving the fidelity

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a complex Euclidean space, and consider the function

f:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
defined as

f(Y)=\begin{cases}-\operatorname{Tr}\sqrt{Y}&\text{if}\;Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y})\\
\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(149)

This function is differentiable at every positive definite operator

Y\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{Y})
, with its gradient being

\nabla f(Y)=-\frac{1}{2}Y^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(150)

One way to verify this expression is to first consider the function

g(Y)=\sqrt{Y}
, defined for every positive semidefinite operator

Y\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{Y})
, and to use the formula
(
148
) to conclude that

Dg(Y)(Z)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}\frac{\Pi_{j}Z\Pi_{k}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}},

(151)

provided that
Y
is positive definite and has spectral decomposition

Y=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\Pi_{k}

(152)

(see also Example 3.26 in
[
17
]
).
The equation (
150
) follows from the chain rule.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a complex Euclidean space, let
P\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{X})
be a positive
definite operator, and define a function

g:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
as

g(X)=\begin{cases}-\operatorname{F}(P,X)&\text{if}\;X\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(153)

For every
X\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{X})
, the function
g
is differentiable at
X
, and

\nabla g(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(P\,\#\,X^{-1}\bigr),

(154)

where
P\,\#\,X^{-1}
denotes the operator geometric mean of
P
and

X^{-1}
, as discussed in Remark
9
.
For every operator
X\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
that is not positive definite, it is the
case that
\partial g(X)=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Define a linear map
\Lambda:\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y})
as

\Lambda(X)=\sqrt{P}X\sqrt{P}

(155)

for all
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X})
.
It is the case that
g=f\circ\Lambda
, where
f
is as defined in
(
149
) above.
By the chain rule for differentiation, one has

\nabla g(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{\ast}(\nabla f(\Lambda(X)))=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{P}\Bigl(\sqrt{P}X\sqrt{P}\Bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{P},

(156)

provided that

\sqrt{P}X\sqrt{P}\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{Y}),

(157)

which is equivalent to
X\in\mathrm{Pd}(\mathcal{X})
.
In the case when
X
is positive definite, one therefore has that

\nabla g(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{P}\Bigl(\sqrt{P^{-1}}X^{-1}\sqrt{P^{-1}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{P}=-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(P\,\#\,X^{-1}\bigr),

(158)

as desired.

Now suppose that
X\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{X})
is not positive definite, and let

\Delta
be the projection onto the kernel of
\sqrt{P}X\sqrt{P}
, which is
nonzero by the assumption that
X
is not positive definite.
Consider the operator

Y=X+\lambda P^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta P^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(159)

for an arbitrary choice of
\lambda>0
.
It is the case that

g(Y)-g(X)=\operatorname{Tr}\sqrt{\sqrt{P}X\sqrt{P}}-\operatorname{Tr}\sqrt{\sqrt{P}X\sqrt{P}+\lambda\Delta}=-\sqrt{\lambda}\operatorname{Tr}(\Delta).

(160)

Thus, if there were to exist an element
Z\in\partial g(X)
, one would have

g(Y)-g(X)\geq\langle Z,Y-X\rangle

(161)

for all
Y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
, including the operator (
159
) for every

\lambda>0
.
It would then follow that

\lambda\Bigl\langle Z,P^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta P^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr\rangle=\langle Z,Y-X\rangle\leq g(Y)-g(X)=-\sqrt{\lambda}\operatorname{Tr}(\Delta),

(162)

or equivalently

\frac{\Bigl\langle Z,P^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta P^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr\rangle}{\operatorname{Tr}(\Delta)}\leq-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}},

(163)

for every
\lambda>0
, which is impossible given that the left-hand side is
a finite value independent of
\lambda
and the right-hand side approaches

-\infty
as
\lambda
approaches 0.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of multiplicativity of fidelity of recovery

In this appendix, we illustrate how Theorem
7
may
be used to prove that the generalized fidelity of recovery is multiplicative.
This fact was first proved in
[
4
]
, through the expression of the
fidelity of recovery as a semidefinite program.

Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0})
,
\rho_{1}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})
,
\sigma_{0}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0})
, and
\sigma_{1}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{Y}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})
be density operators for some complex Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{0},\mathcal{Z}_{1}
, and consider the following pair of optimization problems:

\displaystyle\text{maximize}

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}\bigl(\sigma_{0},\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{0})}\bigr)(\rho_{0})\bigr)

\displaystyle\text{maximize}

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}\bigl(\sigma_{1},\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})}\bigr)(\rho_{1})\bigr)

(168)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{0})

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{1}).

Define density operators
\rho\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})
as

\rho=W(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})W^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma=V(\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{1})V^{*}

(169)

where

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad V

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})

are the isometries defined as

\displaystyle W(x_{0}\otimes z_{0}\otimes x_{1}\otimes z_{1})

\displaystyle=x_{0}\otimes x_{1}\otimes z_{0}\otimes z_{1}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad V(y_{0}\otimes z_{0}\otimes y_{1}\otimes z_{1})

\displaystyle=y_{0}\otimes y_{1}\otimes z_{0}\otimes z_{1}

for all choices of vectors
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
,
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}
,
y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
,
z_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
, and
z_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}_{1}
. Consider now the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\text{maximize}

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}\bigl(\sigma,\bigl(\Phi\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})}\bigr)(\rho)\bigr)

(170)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}).

The fact that the generalized fidelity of recovery is multiplicative can be stated as follows.
Let
\Phi_{0}\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{0})
and
\Phi_{1}\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{1})
be channels and suppose that this pair of channels is optimal for the pair of optimization problems in (
168
). Then the channel
\Phi_{0}\otimes\Phi_{1}\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1})
is optimal for the optimization problem in (
170
). To prove this fact, we may define operators
H_{0}\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{0})
and
H_{1}\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{Y}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1})
as

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho_{0}}^{\ast})\Bigl(\sqrt{\sigma_{0}}\mspace{1mu}\bigl(\sqrt{\sigma_{0}}\,(\Phi_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{0})})(\rho_{0})\sqrt{\sigma_{0}}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\sigma_{0}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad H_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y}_{1})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho_{1}}^{\ast})\Bigl(\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\mspace{1mu}\bigl(\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\,(\Phi_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})})(\rho_{1})\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\Bigr).

From the assumption that both
\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi_{1}
are optimal, it follows from Theorem
7
that the following conditions hold:

1.

\operatorname{im}(\sigma_{0})\subseteq\operatorname{im}\bigl(\bigl(\Phi_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{0})}\bigr)(\rho_{0})\bigr)
and
\operatorname{im}(\sigma_{1})\subseteq\operatorname{im}\bigl(\bigl(\Phi_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})}\bigr)(\rho_{1})\bigr)

2.

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}}(H_{0}J(\Phi_{0}))\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
and
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}(H_{1}J(\Phi_{1}))\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X}_{1})

3.

H_{0}\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}}(H_{0}J(\Phi_{0}))
and
H_{1}\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}(H_{1}J(\Phi_{1}))
.

It is evident that
\operatorname{im}(\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{1})\subseteq\operatorname{im}\bigl(\bigl(\Phi_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{0})}\otimes\Phi_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})}\bigr)(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})\bigr)
by taking tensor products, and it follows that

\operatorname{im}(\sigma)\subseteq\operatorname{im}\bigl(\bigl(\Phi_{0}\otimes\Phi_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})}\bigr)(\rho)\bigr))

(171)

by rearranging the spaces. Define the operator

H=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1})}\otimes\Psi_{\rho}^{\ast})\Bigl(\sqrt{\sigma}\mspace{1mu}\bigl(\sqrt{\sigma}\,(\Phi_{0}\otimes\Phi_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Z}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{1})})(\rho)\sqrt{\sigma}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\sigma}\Bigr),

(172)

and define the isometry
U\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1})
as the operator satisfying

U(y_{0}\otimes x_{0}\otimes y_{1}\otimes x_{1})=y_{0}\otimes y_{1}\otimes x_{0}\otimes x_{1}

(173)

for every choice of vectors
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
,
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}
, and
y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
. With these choices of operators, it is evident that
H=U(H_{0}\otimes H_{1})U^{*}
and it follows that

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}}(HJ(\Phi_{0}\otimes\Phi_{1}))=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}}(H_{0}J(\Phi_{0}))\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}(H_{1}J(\Phi_{1}))\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathcal{X}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{1}),

(174)

as the tensor product of Hermitian operators is Hermitian. Moreover it holds that

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=U(H_{0}\otimes H_{1})U^{*}

\displaystyle\geq U\Bigl(\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}}(H_{0}J(\Phi_{0}))\bigr)\otimes\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}(H_{1}J(\Phi_{1}))\bigr)\Bigr)U^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{Y}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{Y}_{1}}(HJ(\Phi_{0}\otimes\Phi_{1})),

which follows from the fact that
P_{0}\otimes P_{1}\geq Q_{0}\otimes Q_{1}
holds for every choice of positive semidefinite operators
P_{0},P_{1},Q_{0},Q_{1}
satisfying
P_{0}\geq Q_{0}
and
P_{1}\geq Q_{1}
. As the conditions of Theorem
7
are satisfied for the optimization problem in (
170
), one has that
\Phi_{0}\otimes\Phi_{1}
is optimal for this problem.
