Factor Modelling for High-dimensional Functional Time Series
Shaojun Guo, Xinghao Qiao, Qingsong Wang, Zihan Wang

Abstract
Many economic and scientific problems involve the analysis of high-dimensional functional time series, where the number of functional variables p p diverges as the number of serially dependent observations n n increases. In this paper, we present a novel functional factor model for high-dimensional functional time series that maintains and makes use of the functional and dynamic structure to achieve great dimension reduction and find the latent factor structure. To estimate the number of functional factors and the factor loadings, we propose a fully functional estimation procedure based on an eigenanalysis for a nonnegative definite and symmetric matrix. Our proposal involves a weight matrix to improve the estimation efficiency and tackle the issue of heterogeneity, the rationale of which is illustrated by formulating the estimation from a novel regression perspective. Asymptotic properties of the proposed method are studied when p p diverges at some polynomial rate as n n increases. To provide a parsimonious model and enhance interpretability for near-zero factor loadings, we impose sparsity assumptions on the factor loading space and then develop a regularized estimation procedure with theoretical guarantees when p p grows exponentially fast relative to n . n. Finally, we demonstrate that our proposed estimators significantly outperform the competing methods through both simulations and applications to a U.K. temperature data set and a Japanese mortality data set.



Keywords:
Dimension reduction; Functional time series; Functional thresholding; High-dimensional data; Sparse principal component analysis; Weight matrix.

1
Introduction

Functional time series, which refers to a sequential collection of curves observed over time exhibiting serial dependence, has recently received a great deal of attention. Despite progress being made in this field, existing literature has focused on the estimation based on a single or fixed number of functional time series, see, e.g.,

Bosq 2000
;
Hörmann and
Kokoszka 2010
;
Bathia
et al. 2010
;
Panaretos and
Tavakoli 2013
;
Hörmann et al. 2015
;
Aue
et al. 2015
;
Li
et al. 2020
;
Chen
et al. 2022
and among many others.

With the rapid development in technology, data sets containing a large collection of functional time series are becoming increasingly available in various applications. Examples include cumulative intraday return trajectories
Horváth
et al. 2014
and functional volatility processes
Müller et al. 2011

for hundreds of stocks,
annual temperature curves collected at a number of stations,
daily energy consumption curves for thousands of households, age-specific mortality rates in different prefectures
Gao
et al. 2019
, to list a few. Such data, which are referred to as high-dimensional functional time series, take the form of
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)=\{Y_{t1}(\cdot),\dots,Y_{tp}(\cdot)\}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
(
t=1,\dots,n
) defined on a compact interval
{\cal U},
where the number of random functions
p
is comparable to, or even larger than the number of serially dependent observations
n
.
Under such scenario, not only
p
is large, but each
Y_{tj}(\cdot)
is a functional object with serial dependence across observations, posing extra challenges. When jointly modelling the entire curve dynamics, it is of great interest and importance to explore the latent common component from a dimension-reduction viewpoint, while preserving the functional and time series structure. This motivates us to develop a functional factor model based on
{\bf Y}_{1}(\cdot),\dots,{\bf Y}_{n}(\cdot).

In this paper, we deal with the factor modelling for high-dimensional functional time series, in which each functional datum
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
arises as the sum of two unobservable components, one common
{\bf Z}_{t}(\cdot)
and one idiosyncratic
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot).
The co-movement of
p
functional time series is assumed to be driven by a small number of functional factors and their inherited dynamic structures in
{\bf Z}_{t}(\cdot)
. Specifically, our proposed factor model admits the representation:

{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)={\bf Z}_{t}(\cdot)+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)={\bf A}{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot),~~t=1,\dots,n,

(1)

where
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)=\{X_{t1}(\cdot),\dots,X_{tr}(\cdot)\}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
is a set of latent functional factor time series with (unknown) number of functional factors
r<p,

{\bf A}=(A_{ij})_{p\times r}
is the factor loading matrix
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)
is
p
-vector of functional idiosyncratic errors.

For multivariate scalar time series
\{{\bf Y}_{t}\},
the factor model (
1
) degenerates to the classical dynamic factor model
{\bf Y}_{t}={\bf A}{\bf X}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},
where two different types of model assumptions are considered in econometrics and statistics literature.
One type of models assumes that each common factor has non-trivial contribution to
\{{\bf Y}_{t}\}
while the idiosyncratic noise
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\}
is allowed to have weak cross-correlations and serial correlations. An incomplete list of the relevant references
includes
Chamberlain and
Rothschild 1983
;
Forni
et al. 2000
;
Bai and
Ng 2002
;
Stock and
Watson 2012
;
Fan
et al. 2013
;
Fan
et al. 2019
.
However, the rigorous definition of the common and idiosyncratic components in those factor models can only be established asymptotically when the dimension of
{\bf Y}_{t}
tends to infinity.
Another type of models assumes that the common factors accommodate all dynamics of
\{{\bf Y}_{t}\},
thus making the idiosyncratic component white noise with no serial correlations but allowing substantial contemporary cross-correlation among it; see
Pena and
Box 1987
;
Lam
et al. 2011
;
Lam and Yao 2012
and extensions to matrix-valued time series
Wang
et al. 2019
;
Chen
et al. 2020
and tensor-valued time series
Chen
et al. 2022
.
For functional time series, the factor modelling remains less touched in the literature.
Hays
et al. 2012
considered functional dynamic factor models for univariate functional time series, where the functional factors do not depend on
t
and the dynamics are modelled by scalar coefficients that follow Gaussian autoregressive processes.
Kokoszka
et al. 2015
extended this model by allowing for dynamic functional factors, but both models are still resctried to the univariate case.

Gao
et al. 2019
adopted factor modelling techniques to predict high-dimensional functional time series by fitting a factor model to estimated scores obtained via eigenanalysis of the long-run covariance function. Compared to our model setup (
1
),
Hallin
et al. 2023
and
Tavakoli
et al. 2023
provide an alternative factor model formulation to tackle a rather different situation with scalar factors and functional factor loadings. Their methodology follows the framework in
Bai and
Ng 2002
and substantially differs from our estimation procedure. See detailed discussion in Section
7
.

Our paper is along the line of
Lam and Yao 2012
in the model setup. To facilitate methodological development, we assume that the idiosyncratic component
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)
is a white noise sequence with
\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(u)\}=0
and
\text{Cov}\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t+k}(u),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(v)\}=0
for any
u,v\in{\cal U}
and
k\neq 0.

Although the white noise assumption is not the most ideal for extracting those asymptotically identifiable factors, it enables the unique decomposition in (
1
), thus largely simplifying the tasks of model identification and inference. In our established theory, we allow for weak serial correlations in
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)\}
, which relaxes the white noise assumption.
Additionally, unlike
Bai and
Ng 2002
, we do not place any assumption on the covariance structure of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot).

We propose to estimate both the number of functional factors and the factor loadings in terms of an eigenanalysis for a nonnegative definite and symmetric matrix defined as the double integral and sum of weighted quadratic forms in autocovariance functions of
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
from different time lags.
In practical applications, it is common that many estimated factor loadings are close to zero especially when
p
is large, This inspires us to add certain sparsity constraints on the factor loading space defined in terms of the factor loading matrix
{\bf A}.
Since functional sparsity patterns in autocovariance functions of
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
inherits from the imposed sparsity structures in
{\bf A},
we first propose Hilbert-Schmidt-norm based functional thresholding of entries of the sample auotocovariance functions, which ensures the consistencies under
\log p/n\rightarrow 0.
Furthermore, to recover sparse factor loading space and produce a parsimonious model with interpretable factors, we perform sparse principal component analysis
Vu and Lei 2013
rather than standard eigenanalysis.

This paper makes useful contributions at multiple fronts.
On the method side, our proposal has four main advantages.
First, it involves a weight matrix to improve the estimation efficiency and tackle the issue of heterogeneity. We illustrate the rationale of its presence by formulating the estimation procedure based on a novel regression viewpoint. Our empirical results also demonstrate the consistent superiority of our method compared to the unweighted competitors across all simulation scenarios. Furthermore, our strategy of incorporating the weight matrix is general and can be applied broadly to a range of dimension reduction problems, e.g.,
Lam
et al. 2011
and
Wang
et al. 2019
.
Second, our estimation relies on the sample autocovariance functions of
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot).
This not only gets rid of the impact from the noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot),
but also makes the good use of the serial correlation information, which is the most relevant in the time series modelling and prediction.
Third, the double integral over
(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}
takes advantage of the functional nature of the data by accumulating the autocovariance information as much as possible, leading to more efficient estimation. Such fully functional procedure avoids the information loss incurred by the dimension reduction step in
Gao
et al. 2019
. Fourth, aided by the enforced sparsity, we enhance the model interpretability and enlarge the dimension of the feature space that our model can handle. Our regularized estimation procedure adopts techniques of novel functional thresholding and sparse principal component analysis, each of which leads to the estimation consistency and sparsity recovery at its own step.

On the theory side, we investigate convergence properties of relevant estimated terms under the assumption that the idiosyncratic errors are either white noise or weakly serially correlated, in the context of
high-dimensional scaling, where
p
grows polynomially fast relative to
n.
With additional sparsity constraints and tail assumptions, we further provide convergence analysis of the regularized estimation in an ultra-high-dimensional regime, where
p
diverges at an exponential rate as
n
increases. Empirically, we demonstrate through an extensive set of simulations that our estimators significantly outperform the competitors.
Moreover, we integrate our factor modelling framework into the challenging task of predicting high-dimensional functional time series and use a real data example to illustrate the superiority of our strategy over existing prediction methods.

The paper is set out as follows. In Section
2
, we develop a fully functional procedure with weight matrix to estimate the proposed factor model and explain it from a novel regression perspective. The theoretical properties of the proposed estimators are investigated in Section
3
. Under further sparsity assumptions, we develop the regularized estimation procedure and present the associated convergence analysis in Section
4
. The finite-sample performance of our methods is illustrated through extensive simulation studies and two real data examples in Sections
5
and
6
, respectively. Section
7
concludes the paper by discussing the relationship to relevant work as well as three extensions.
We relegate all technical proofs and additional empirical results to the Supplementary Material.

2
Methodology

2.1
Model Setup and Estimation Procedure

We assume that the
p
-vector of functional time series
{\bf Y}_{1}(\cdot),\dots,{\bf Y}_{n}(\cdot)
satisfies the functional factor model in (
1
), where each
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
is decomposed as the sum of two parts: a common dynamic part driven by a
r
-vector of latent factor process
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)
and an idiosyncratic part of white noise process
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)=\{\varepsilon_{t1}(\cdot),\dots,\varepsilon_{tp}(\cdot)\}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}.
Our formulation ensures that both functional and linear dynamic structure of
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
are inherited from common factors
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot),
while all white noise elements are absorbed into
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot).

Note that
\{{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)\}_{t=1}^{n}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)\}_{t=1}^{n}
are unobservable. The white noise assumption is adopted here to facilitate the methodology but is relaxed in Section
3
when establishing the supporting theory.
We further assume that the rank of the factor loading matrix
{\bf A}
is
r
. If this full rank condition is violated, model (
1
) can be expressed in terms of a lower-dimensional functional factor. When
r
is much smaller than
p,
we achieve an effective dimension reduction.

There is an identifiable issue among
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)
and
{\bf A},
since model (
1
) remains unchanged if
\{{\bf A},{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)\}
is replaced by
\{{\bf A}\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)\}
for any invertible matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}.
Even if we assume that the columns of
{\bf A}
are orthonormal, i.e.
{\bf A}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\bf A}={\bf I}_{r}
(the
r\times r
identity matrix),
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)
and
{\bf A}
still can not be determined uniquely. However, the linear space spanned by the columns of
{\bf A}
(denoted by
\mathcal{C}({\bf A})
) is unique regardless of the column-orthogonality of
{\bf A}
. Hence we will focus on the estimation of the factor loading space
{\mathcal{C}}({\bf A}).

We next develop a fully functional procedure to estimate
\mathcal{C}({\bf A})
and the number of factors
r.
Under the assumption that
\{{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)\}
is weakly stationary, we define the lag-
k
(
k\geq 0
) autocovariance functions:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\{{\bf Y}_{t+k}(u),{\bf Y}_{t}(v)\},~~\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)=\text{Cov}\{{\bf X}_{t+k}(u),{\bf X}_{t}(v)\},

(2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{x\varepsilon}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\{{\bf X}_{t+k}(u),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(v)\}.

We further assume that the future white noise components are uncorrelated with the common factors up to the present. It then follows from (
1
) and (
2
) that

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)={\bf A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v){\bf A}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}+{\bf A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{x\varepsilon}(u,v),~~u,v\in{\cal U},~k\geq 1.

(3)

Given a prescribed and fixed integer
k_{0}\geq 1,
we define a nonnegative definite and symmetric matrix

{\bf M}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\int_{{\cal U}}\int_{{\cal U}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v){\bf W}(v)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

where the
p\times p
weight matrix
{\bf W}(v)
is nonnegative definite and symmetric
for any
v\in{\cal U}.
Here
{\bf W}(v)
is introduced to improve the estimation efficiency. In Section
2.2
, we will suggest specific forms of
{\bf W}(v)
and illustrate the rationale from a regression perspective.
Replacing
{\bf W}(v)
by its sample version
\widehat{\bf W}(v),
we define

\widecheck{\bf M}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\int_{{\cal U}}\int_{{\cal U}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\widehat{\bf W}(v)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

(4)

Let
\nu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\nu_{p}\geq 0
be eigenvalues of
\widecheck{\bf M}

and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}
be a unit eigenvector of
\widecheck{\bf M}
corresponding to
\nu_{j}
for each
j.
We claim that, under mild Conditions
2
and
4
imposed in Section
3
,
\text{rank}(\widecheck{\bf M})=r
(i.e.,
\nu_{r}>0=\nu_{r+1}=\dots=\nu_{p}
) holds with overwhelming probability as justified in our proof in Section
S.2.1
of the Supplementary Material. As a result, the factor loading space
{\cal C}({\bf A})
can be recovered by

{\cal C}({\bf K})=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}\}
with

{\bf K}=(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}.

To see this, let
\widecheck{\bf A}
be a
p\times(p-r)
orthogonal complement of matrix
{\bf A}
such that
\widecheck{\bf A}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\bf A}={\bf 0}
and
\widecheck{\bf A}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\widecheck{\bf A}={\bf I}_{p-r}.
It then follows from (
3
) and (
4
) that
\widecheck{\bf M}\widecheck{\bf A}={\bf 0},
which implies that the columns of
\widecheck{\bf A}
are eigenvectors of
\widecheck{\bf M}
corresponding to
(p-r)
zero eigenvalues and hence
{\cal C}({\bf A})
is spanned by the eigenvectors of
\widecheck{\bf M}
corresponding to
r
nonzero eigenvalues.

Remark 1
.

(i) We take the double integral and the sum in the definition of
\widecheck{\bf M}
in (
4
) to accumulate the information of autocovariance functions as much as possible from each
(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}
and from different time lags leading to more efficient estimation, whereas fixing at certain
(u,v)
or time lag may lead to spurious estimation results.

(ii) The definition in (
4
) ensures that
\widecheck{\bf M}
is nonnegative definite and symmetric and there is no cancellation of information accumulated from lags 1 to
k_{0}.
Hence the estimation is insensitive to the choice of
k_{0}.
In practice we tend to select a small value,
1\leq k_{0}\leq 5,
as the strongest autocorrelations usually appear at small lags.

To estimate
{\cal C}({\bf A}),
we need an eigenanalysis of the sample estimator for
\widecheck{\bf M},

\widehat{\bf M}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\int_{{\cal U}}\int_{{\cal U}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\widehat{\bf W}(v)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

(5)

where

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)=\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}\big\{{\bf Y}_{t+k}(u)-\bar{\bf Y}(u)\big\}\big\{{\bf Y}_{t}(v)-\bar{\bf Y}(v)\big\}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}},~~u,v\in{\cal U},

(6)

is the estimator for
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)
and
\widebar{\bf Y}(\cdot)=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot).
Performing eigen-decomposition of
\widehat{\bf M}
leads to estimated eigenvalues
\hat{\nu}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\hat{\nu}_{p}
and the associated eigenvectors
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{p}.
Let
\widehat{\bf A}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{r}).
Then
{\cal C}(\widehat{\bf A})=\text{span}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{r}\}
forms the estimate of
{\cal C}({\bf A}).

We have developed the estimation of model (
1
) assuming that the number of factors
r
is known or can be identified correctly. In practice,
r
is unknown and there is vast literature on the topic of determining it, see
Bai and
Ng 2002
;
Onatski 2010
;
Lam and Yao 2012
;
Ahn and
Horenstein 2013
;
Fan
et al. 2022
and
Han
et al. 2022
, to quote a few. Here we take the commonly adopted ratio-based estimator for
r
as:

\hat{r}=\arg\min_{1\leq j\leq c_{r}p}\frac{\hat{\nu}_{j+1}}{\hat{\nu}_{j}},

(7)

where
c_{r}\in(0,1)
is a prespecified constant.
In empirical studies, we take
c_{r}=0.75
to avoid the fluctuations due to the ratios of extreme small values.

2.2
Weight Matrix

Under the nonnegative-definiteness and symmetry constraint, we suggest the following weight matrix:

{\bf W}(v)={\bf Q}\big\{{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(0)}(v,v){\bf Q}\big\}^{-1}{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},~~v\in{\cal U},

(8)

where
{\bf Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times q}
is a full-rank matrix and
q
is larger than
r
but much smaller than
p.
The entries of
{\bf Q}
are independently sampled from some random distribution with zero mean and unit variance, e.g.,
\text{Uniform}[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}]
and
N(0,1),
which is used here to facilitate technical analysis. Our experiments indicate that the results are not sensitive to the choices of
q
and the sampling distribution. Then we can obtain the sample version of
{\bf W}(v)
by

\widehat{\bf W}(v)={\bf Q}\big\{{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}^{(0)}(v,v){\bf Q}\big\}^{-1}{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}.

(9)

From a regression perspective, we next provide an intuitive explanation why the weight matrix takes the suggested form in (
8
). Despite the unobservable common factors
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot),
we can simply view (
1
) fixing at
u\in{\cal U}
as a multiple linear regression with multivariate responses
{\bf Y}_{t}(u)
and the coefficient matrix
{\bf A}
to be estimated. To construct the covariate vector from observed data in a regression setup, we assume that there exists some column-orthogonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q\times r}
with
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}={\bf I}_{r}
such that
\widecheck{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)
can be represented by the linear combination of past observed curves with the addition of some random noise:

\widecheck{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)={\bf Q}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(\cdot)+\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{tk}(\cdot),~~k=1,\dots,k_{0},

where
\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{tk}(\cdot)
is the white noise process, independent of
{\bf Y}_{t-1}(\cdot),\dots,{\bf Y}_{t-k_{0}}(\cdot).
Then model (
1
) can be rewritten as

{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)={\bf B}\widecheck{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)={\bf B}{\bf Q}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(\cdot)+{\mathbf{e}}_{tk}(\cdot),~~t=k+1,\dots,n,

(10)

where
{\mathbf{e}}_{tk}(\cdot)=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)+{\bf B}\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{tk}(\cdot)
and

{\bf B}={\bf A}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
is a
p\times q
matrix satisfying
\text{rank}({\bf B})=r
and
{\cal C}({\bf B})={\cal C}({\bf A}).

Now we can treat (
10
) fixing at
u\in{\cal U}
as a linear regression model with observed covariate vectors
\{{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{{\bf Y}_{t-k}}(u)\}_{t=k+1}^{n}
and unknown low-rank coefficient matrix
{\bf B}.
Based on (
10
) and the population moment equation
\text{Cov}\{{\mathbf{e}}_{tk}(u),{\bf Q}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(v)\}=0
for
u,v\in{\cal U},
we can solve
{\bf B}
by

{\bf B}=\text{Cov}\Big\{{\bf Y}_{t}(u),{\bf Q}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(v)\Big\}\text{Cov}\Big\{{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(u),{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(v)\Big\}^{-1}.

(11)

Replacing the covariance terms in (
11
) by their sample versions, we obtain the estimator

\widehat{\bf B}_{u,v}=\Big\{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}{\bf Y}_{t}(u){\bf Y}_{t-k}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(v){\bf Q}\Big\}\Big\{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(u){\bf Y}_{t-k}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(v){\bf Q}\Big\}^{-1}.

(12)

Denote the
j
-th row vector of
\widehat{\bf B}_{u,v}
by
{\widehat{\mathbf{b}}}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
. Since
{\widehat{\mathbf{b}}}_{j}
represents a statistic whose variation plays a pivotal role in subsequent analyses, it is advantageous to utilize the normalized statistic
\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{j}\{\text{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{j})\}^{-1/2}
rather than
\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{j}
itself, where
\text{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q\times q}
denotes the invertible covariance matrix of
\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{j}
. To simplify our subsequent derivations, we suppose
\mathbb{E}\{{\bf X}_{t}(u)\}={\bf 0}
and
\text{Cov}\{{\mathbf{e}}_{tk}(u)\}={\bf I}_{p}
uniformly across all
t,

k
, and
u\in{\cal U}
. By (
12
), it follows that:

\begin{split}\text{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{j})&=\Big\{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(v){\bf Y}_{t-k}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(u){\bf Q}\Big\}^{-1}\Big\{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(v){\bf Y}_{t-k}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(v){\bf Q}\Big\}\\
&\quad\cdot\Big\{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}{\bf Q}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf Y}_{t-k}(u){\bf Y}_{t-k}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(v){\bf Q}\Big\}^{-1}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{u,v},\end{split}

(13)

indicating that
\text{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{j})
remains the same across
j
. Our primary objective is to identify the rank of
\widehat{\bf B}_{u,v}

Motivated by the normalized idea, this can be achieved through an eigenanalysis of the scaled nonnegative-definite and symmetric matrix

Combining (

An alternative choice of the weight matrix is

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf M}_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\int_{{\cal U}}\int_{{\cal U}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf M}_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\int_{{\cal U}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,u)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,u)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathrm{d}u.

(16)

Remark 2
.

It is noteworthy that, without the double or single integral, the unweighted estimators

3
Theoretical Properties

In this section, we study asymptotic properties of the proposed method under a high-dimensional regime, where
p
and
n
tend to infinity together and

is fixed. Before presenting the theoretical results, we impose some regularity conditions.

Condition 1
.

The idiosyncratic component

is a white noise sequence with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}^{(k)}(u,v)=0
for any
(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}
and

Condition
1
′
.

The idiosyncratic component

\sup_{(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}^{(k)}(u,v)\|=O(1)
as
p\to\infty
for
k=1,\dots,k_{0}.

The white noise assumption in Condition
1
is imposed to facilitate the methodological development in Section
2
. However, our theoretical results can be established under a weaker Condition
′
‣
3
, which allows for weak serial correlations in
\{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)\}.

Condition 2
.

(i) The latent functional factor process
\{{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)\}
is second-order weakly stationary with
\mathbb{E}(\|X_{tj}\|_{2}^{2})=O(1)
for
j=1,\dots,r;

(ii) There exists at least one
k\in\{1,\dots,k_{0}\}
such that the rank of
\iint\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v
is
r.

Condition 3
.

(i)
\max_{j}{\mathbb{E}}(\|\varepsilon_{tj}\|_{2}^{2})=O(1);

(ii)
\inf_{v\in{\cal U}}\lambda_{\min}\big\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}(v,v)\big\}

Conditions
2
and
3
contain some standard finite moment assumptions in functional data analysis literature.
Condition
2
(ii) can be viewed as functional generalization of Condition 2 in
Wang
et al. 2019
for matrix-valued time series, which ensures that the latent factor process
{\bf X}_{t}
has exactly
r
components.
Condition
3
(ii) is imposed for technical convenience.
It precludes the case when
{\bf Y}_{t}(v)
is non-random at some point
v\in{\cal U}.

However, replacing
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
with a contaminated process
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t},
where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}
’s are independent with zero mean and
diagonal covariance matrix with small diagonal components and are independent of
{\bf Y}_{t+k}(\cdot)
’s for all
k,
Condition
3
(ii) is then satisfied while the autocovariance structure in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)}
remains the same
in the sense of
\text{Cov}\{{\bf Y}_{t+k}(u)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+k},{\bf Y}_{t}(v)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}\}=\text{Cov}\{{\bf Y}_{t+k}(u),{\bf Y}_{t}(v)\}
for
k\geq 1
and
u,v\in{\cal U}.

Condition 4
.

There exists some constant
\delta\in[0,1]
such that
\|{\bf A}\|^{2}\asymp p^{1-\delta}\asymp\|{\bf A}\|^{2}_{\min}
.

Condition 5
.

(i) For
k=1,\dots,k_{0},

\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{x\varepsilon}\|_{{{\cal S}},\infty}=o(p^{(1-\delta)/2});
(ii)
\text{Cov}\{{\bf X}_{t}(u),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t+k}(v)\}={\bf 0}
for any
k\geq 0
and
(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}.

The parameter
\delta
in Condition
4
can be viewed as the strength of factors with smaller values yielding stronger factors.
It measures the relative growth rate of the amount of information contained in
{\bf Z}_{t}(\cdot)
as the dimension
p
increases, compared to that in
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot),
see (
1
).
When
\delta=0
, Condition
4
corresponds to the pervasiveness assumption in
Fan
et al. 2013
, which means that all factors are strong. When
\delta>0,
the factors are termed as weak factors.
Condition
5
(i) requires that the correlation between
{\bf X}_{t+k}(\cdot)
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot)
is not too strong, while Condition
5
(ii) assumes that the future idiosyncratic components are uncorrelated with the common factors up to the present.

Condition 6
.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)=\big\{\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy,ij}(u,v)\big\}_{p\times p}
and

{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)=\big\{\Sigma^{(k)}_{yy,ij}(u,v)\big\}_{p\times p}
for
(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}.
Then (i) For
k=1,\dots,k_{0},

\max_{i,j}{\mathbb{E}}\big\{\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{yy,ij}^{(k)}-\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|_{{\cal S}}\big\}=O(n^{-1/2});

(ii)
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{yy}(u,v)
is Lipschitz-continuous over
(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U};

(iii)
\max_{i,j}{\mathbb{E}}\big\{\sup_{(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}}\big|\widehat{\Sigma}_{yy,ij}^{(0)}(u,v)-\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(0)}(u,v)\big|\big\}=O\big\{n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2}\big\}.

There are several sufficient conditions that have been commonly imposed in functional time series literature when
p
is fixed and can lead to the convergence result under expectation in Condition
6
(i), which implies
\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{yy,ij}^{(k)}-\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|_{{\cal S}}=O_{\mathrm{p}}(n^{-1/2}).

The key requirement in establishing the convergence is to control the temporal dependence in
\{Y_{tj}(\cdot)\}
for each

Examples include strong mixing conditions
Bosq 2000
;
Chen
et al. 2022
, cumulant mixing conditions
Panaretos and
Tavakoli 2013
and
L^{q}
-
m
-approximability
Hörmann and
Kokoszka 2010
;
Hörmann et al. 2015
. When
p
diverges, we assume the rate under deterministic expectation uniformly in Condition
6
(i).
With sub-Gaussian-type tail assumption and Lipschitz-continuity in Condition
6

{\cal U}\times{\cal U}
to the maximum over a grid of pairs, then the uniform concentration inequalities and hence the uniform rate of

in Condition

Qiao
et al. 2020
.

Now we are ready to present theorems about the rates of convergence for estimators of the factor loading space
{\cal C}({\bf A})
and the eigenvalues

To measure the accuracy in estimating
{\cal C}({\bf A}),
we use the metric of the distance between

and

For two orthogonal matrices

\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{C}({\bf K}_{1}),\mathcal{C}({\bf K}_{2})\big)=\Big\{1-\frac{1}{\max(r_{1},r_{2})}\mbox{tr}({\bf K}_{1}{\bf K}_{1}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\bf K}_{2}{\bf K}_{2}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}})\Big\}^{1/2}.

This distance ranges between
0
and

It equals
0
if and only if
\mathcal{C}({\bf K}_{1})=\mathcal{C}({\bf K}_{2}),
and

if and only if
{\bf K}_{1}
and

Theorem 1
.

Let Conditions
′
‣
3
,
2

6

Suppose that

which reduces to

instead of Condition

‣

Remark 3
.

(i) Theorem
1
implies that, as the factors become stronger with smaller

the estimated factor loading space achieves a faster rate of convergence. The presence of

in the established rate, under Condition

and

(ii) If Condition

Let Conditions
′

.

Part (ii) of Theorem

(i) Theorem

4
Sparse Factor Model

Despite the phenomenon of “curse of dimensionality” being avoided when all factors are strong, our method does not guarantee a parsimonious and interpretable model in the presence of weak factors. In real applications, it is quite common that many estimated factor loadings are close to zero especially when
p
is large, see the gene expression study in
Carvalho et al. 2008
and the sea surface air pressure records example in
Lam and Yao 2012
.
Such phenomenon motivates us to propose a sparse factor model by imposing sparsity assumptions on the factor loading space
{\cal C}({\bf A})
of (
1
). In this section, we target to develop the regularized estimation under sparsity constraints, which not only leads to the enhanced intepretability in practice, but also theoretically enlarges the dimension of the feature space that our proposed sparse factor model can handle compared with the nonsparse factor model with weak factors.

We consider two complementary notions of subspace sparsity defined in terms of the factor loading matrix
{\bf A}
: row sparsity and column sparsity, which are consistent with the definitions in
Vu and Lei 2013
. Intuitively, the row sparsity entails that only a small subset of components in
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
are driven by the common factors
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot),
thus making the row sparse factor loading space generated by a small number of variables, independent of the choice of the basis. The column sparsity, on the other hand, corresponds to the case that each common factor
X_{tj}(\cdot)
has impact on only a small fraction of components of
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)
and hence the column sparse factor loading space has orthonormal basis consisting of sparse vectors. We begin by introducing parameter spaces of “approximately column sparse” and “approximately row sparse” factor loading matrices respectively defined in Conditions
7
and
′
‣
4
below. Let
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}
be the
i
-th row vector of
{\bf A}=(A_{ij})_{p\times r}.

Condition 7

(Column sparsity)
.

The matrix
{\bf A}
is from the class

{\cal V}(\tau,c_{1}(p),L)=\Big\{{\bf A}:\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq r}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|A_{ij}|^{\tau}\leq c_{1}(p),\ \max\limits_{1\leq i\leq p,1\leq j\leq r}|A_{ij}|\leq L\Big\},\quad\tau\in[0,1).

Condition
7
′

(Row sparsity)
.

The matrix
{\bf A}
is from the class

{\cal V}^{*}(\tau,c_{1}(p),L)=\Big\{{\bf A}:\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\|^{\tau}\leq c_{1}(p),\ \max\limits_{1\leq i\leq p,1\leq j\leq r}|A_{ij}|\leq L\Big\},\quad\tau\in[0,1).

The parameters
c_{1}(p)
and
\tau
together control the column or row sparsity in the factor loading matrix
{\bf A}.
In the special case of
\tau=0,

{\cal V}(0,c_{1}(p),L)
and
{\cal V}^{*}(0,c_{1}(p),L)
correspond to the truly sparse situations, in which
{\bf A}
has at most
c_{1}(p)
nonzero entries on each column under Condition
7
or
c_{1}(p)
nonzero row vectors under Condition
′
‣
4
. By Hölder’s inequality, it is easy to check that
{\cal V}^{*}(\tau,c_{1}(p),L)\subseteq{\cal V}(\tau,c_{r,\tau}c_{1}(p),L)
, where
c_{r,\tau}=1
for
\tau=0
and
r^{1-\tau/2}
for
\tau\in(0,1).
Provided that
r
is fixed, our subsequent analysis will focus on the class of column sparse factor loading matrices,
{\cal V}(\tau,c_{1}(p),L).

Our estimation procedure in Section
2
is developed based on the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)}
’s. When
p
grows faster than
n^{1/2},
it is known that the sample autocovariance functions
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}^{(k)}
’s are no longer consistent estimators. Nevertheless, under the sparse factor model setup, the decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)}
in (
3
) suggests that our enforced column sparsity in
{\bf A}
is inherited by the functional sparsity structure in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)},
thus enabling us to possibly construct the threhsolding-based estimator for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)}
to ensure the consistency. Before introducing the estimator, we slightly modify Condition
5
to accommodate the sparse setting and present a lemma that reveals the functional sparsity pattern in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)}.

Condition
5
′
.

(i) Let
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{x\varepsilon}(u,v)=\big\{\Sigma^{(k)}_{x\varepsilon,ij}(u,v)\big\}_{r\times p}
and
\max\limits_{1\leq l\leq r}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\Sigma^{(k)}_{x\varepsilon,lj}\|^{\tau}_{{\cal S}}\leq c_{2}(p)
for
k=1,\dots,k_{0}
; (ii)
\text{Cov}\{{\bf X}_{t}(u),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t+k}(v)\}={\bf 0}
for any
k\geq 0
and
(u,v)\in{\cal U}\times{\cal U}
.

Condition
′
‣
4
(i) requires relatively weak correlations between
{\bf X}_{t+k}(\cdot)
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot).
As long as
c_{2}(p)=o(p^{(1-\delta)/2}),
Condition
5
(i) follows directly from Condition
′
‣
4
(i). Moreover, Condition
′
‣
4
(i)
relies on the Hilbert-Schmidt norm to encourage the functional sparsity in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x\varepsilon}^{(k)}
, i.e., each common factor
X_{(t+k)l}(\cdot)
is only correlated with a few components of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot).
With imposed sparsity constraints in Conditions
′
‣
4
and
7
, it can be inferred from (
3
)
that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}
is functional sparse.
Lemma
1
in Section
S.3
of Supplementary Material shows that the functional sparsity patterns in columns/rows of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)}
are determined by parameters
c_{1}(p)
and
c_{2}(p)
with smaller values yielding functional sparser
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)}.

To obtain a functional sparse estimator for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(k)},
we apply the hard functional thresholding rule, which combines functional versions of hard thresholding and shrinkage based on the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of functions, on entries of the sample autocovariance function
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}^{(k)}.
Then the functional thresholding estimator is constructed as

\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy})(u,v)=\ \Big[\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy,ij}(u,v)I\big\{\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy,ij}\big\|_{{\cal S}}\geq\eta_{k}\big\}\Big]_{p\times p},\quad u,v\in{\cal U},

(18)

where
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function and
\eta_{k}\geq 0
is the thresholding parameter. Under mild regularity conditions, Lemma
4
in Section
S.3
of the Supplementary Material yields

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq p}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy,ij}-\Sigma^{(k)}_{yy,ij}\|_{{\cal S}}=O_{\mathrm{p}}\left({\cal M}_{y}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right),

(19)

where
{\cal M}_{y}
is the functional stability measure
Guo and
Qiao 2023
defined in (
S.1
) in Section
S.1
of the Supplementary Material. The rate under the functional version of
\ell_{\infty}
norm in (
19
) plays a crucial role in our theoretical analysis under an ultra-high-dimensional regime and, in particular, suggests us to set the thresholding level as
\eta_{k}\asymp{\cal M}_{y}(n^{-1}\log p)^{1/2}.

Replacing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}
in (
5
) with
{\cal T}_{\eta_{k}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}),
we obtain the corresponding estimator for
{\bf M}:

\widetilde{\bf M}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\int\int\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy})(u,v)\widehat{\bf W}(v)\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy})(u,v)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

(20)

To recover the column sparsity structure in
{\cal C}({\bf A}),
we perform sparse principal component analysis (PCA)
Vu and Lei 2013
on
\widetilde{\bf M}
rather than the standard eigenanalysis for
\widehat{\bf M}
in Section
2.1
. For matrices
{\bf A}_{1}
and
{\bf A}_{2}
with the same dimension, let
\langle{\bf A}_{1},{\bf A}_{2}\rangle:=\text{trace}({\bf A}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf A}_{2}).
We define
\widetilde{\bf K}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
as a solution to the following optimization problem:

\widetilde{\bf K}=\underset{{\bf K}=(K_{jl})_{p\times r}}{\arg\max}~~\langle\widetilde{{\bf M}},{\bf K}{\bf K}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\rangle~~\text{subject to}~~{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bf K}={\bf I}_{r},~\max\limits_{1\leq l\leq r}\sum_{j=1}^{p}|K_{jl}|^{\tau}\leq C_{\tau},

(21)

where
C_{\tau}>0
is a regularization parameter. Alternatively, to estimate the row sparse factor loading space, we can substitute the second constraint in (
21

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|{\mathbf{k}}_{i}\|^{\tau}\leq\widetilde{C}_{\tau},
where
{\mathbf{k}}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row vector of
{\bf K}
and
\widetilde{C}_{\tau}>0
is a regularization parameter. It is worth noting that, without the sparsity constraint in
(
21
), the optimization problem degenerates to the ordinary PCA. Despite being challenging to solve (
21
) due to the non-convex constraint, some efficient and computationally tractable algorithms have been developed, see, e.g., under the truly sparse case (
\tau=0
), the combinatorial approaches
Moghaddam
et al. 2006
;
d’Aspremont
et al. 2008
;
Mackey 2009
, the semi-definite relaxation
d’Aspremont et al. 2007
and its variants, and the random-projection-based method
Gataric
et al. 2020
. We refer to
Zou and
Xue 2018

Sparse case

In this section, we conduct some simulations to evaluate the performance of the functional-thresholding-and-sparse-PCA-based approach (TSPCA) developed in Section
4
to estimate the sparse factor model. Two kinds of subspace sparsity constraints are imposed on
{\bf A}.
Specifically, for the row sparsity, we randomly select
80\%
rows of
{\bf A}
to be zero vectors, while for the column sparsity, we randomly set
80\%
elements within each column of
{\bf A}
as zeros. We focus on Scenario 1 with
\kappa_{0}=1,

n=100,

and generate nonzero entries of
{\bf A}
from
\text{Uniform}[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}].

Implementing TSPCA requires choosing the thresholding parameter
\eta_{k}
in (
18
) and the columnwise number of nonzero elements
C_{0}
in (
21
). To select the optimal
\hat{\eta}_{k}
for each
k=1,\dots,k_{0},
we implement a
G
-fold cross-validation approach
Cai and
Liu 2011
. To be specific, we first sequentially divide the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
into
G
blockwise groups
{\cal D}_{1},\dots,{\cal D}_{G}
of approximately equal size. We then treat
the
g
-th group as a validation set, compute the sample lag-
k
autocovariance functions
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}^{(k),(g)}(u,v)
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}^{(k),(-g)}(u,v)
for
u,v\in{\cal U}
based on the validation set and the remaining
G-1
groups, respectively, and repeat the above procedure
G
times. We finally select
\widehat{\eta}_{k}
by minimizing

R_{k}(\eta_{k})={G}^{-1}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\big\|{\mathcal{T}}_{\eta_{k}}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k),(-g)}_{yy}\big)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k),(g)}_{yy}\big\|_{{\cal S},\text{F}}^{2}.

We adopt a similar cross-validation method to select the optimal
\widehat{C}_{0}.

Given the
g
-th group as a validation set, we obtain the solution
\widetilde{\bf K}^{(-g)}
to the constrained optimization problem in (
21
) with
\tau=0
based on the remaining
G-1

et al. 2006
;
Mackey 2009
to

which is formed by (
20

{\mathcal{T}}_{\widehat{\eta}_{k}}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k),(-g)}_{yy}\big)
for
k=1,\dots,k_{0}.

We also obtain
\widehat{\bf K}^{(g)}
by carrying out an eigenanalysis for
\widehat{\bf M}^{(g)}
based on the validation set. The above procedure is repeated
G
times and
\widehat{C}_{0}
is selected by minimizing

D(C_{0})={G}^{-1}\sum_{g=1}^{G}{\cal D}\big({\cal C}(\widetilde{\bf K}^{(-g)}),{\cal C}(\widehat{\bf K}^{(g)})\big).

1.

1

\{{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)\}_{t=1}^{T},

The

-step-ahead prediction based on the best fitted functional ARMA model

et al. 2017

\widehat{\bf A}.

Discussion

It is noteworthy that
Hallin
et al. 2023
develops an alternative functional factor model with scalar factors and functional loadings. Their methodology allows different components of multivariate functional time series to reside in different Hilbert spaces or a mix of functional and scalar time series, which is not possible with our approach. Nevertheless, for all applications mentioned in Section
1
and explored in Section
6
, the corresponding components of multivariate functional time series all lie in the same Hilbert space. In such a common practical setting, their model reduces to

{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot)=\widetilde{\bf A}(\cdot)\widetilde{\bf X}_{t}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{t}(\cdot),~~t=1,\dots,n,

(24)

where
\widetilde{\bf X}_{t}=(\widetilde{X}_{t1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{t\tilde{r}})^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
is a set of latent factor time series and
\widetilde{\bf A}(\cdot)=\{\widetilde{A}_{jl}(\cdot)\}_{p\times\tilde{r}}
is the unknown functional factor loading matrix.
Both (
1
) and (
24
) generate useful factor models for high-dimensional functional time series
{\bf Y}_{t}(\cdot),
but are designed to tackle rather different situations.
Note one crucial question in functional time series modelling is how to characterize the functional and time series structures. Our factor model (
1
) with static factor loadings assumes that both structures are inherited from
r
common functional time series factors
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)
with reduced dimension from
p\times\infty
to
r\times\infty
before subsequent analysis. By comparison, factor model (
24
) treats
\tilde{r}
common factors in
\widetilde{\bf X}_{t}
as
\tilde{r}
-dimensional vector time series, which reduces the dimension from
p\times\infty
to a much lower value
\tilde{r},
while the infinite-dimensional structure is maintained in the functional factor loading matrix
\widetilde{\bf A}(\cdot).

We next discuss a possible way to apply our method to estimate model (
24
). Given an orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{\infty},

we expand the functional objects in (
24
) by
Y_{tj}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\xi_{tji}\phi_{i}(\cdot),

\widetilde{A}_{jl}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{jli}\phi_{i}(\cdot),

\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{tj}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\epsilon_{tji}\phi_{i}(\cdot)

and hence rewrite (
24
) as

\xi_{tji}=\sum_{l=1}^{\tilde{r}}a_{jli}\widetilde{X}_{tl}+\epsilon_{tji},~~i=1,\dots,\infty.

(25)

To simplify notation, we assume the same truncated dimension
M
across
j
and stack the basis coefficients
\{\xi_{tjl}\}_{1\leq j\leq p,1\leq l\leq L},

\{a_{jkl}\}_{1\leq j\leq p,1\leq l\leq L}
and
\{\epsilon_{tjl}\}_{1\leq j\leq p,1\leq l\leq L}

\xi_{tji},

a_{jli}
and
\epsilon_{tji}
for
j=1,\dots,p
and
i=1,\dots,M
to
pM
-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},{\mathbf{a}}_{l}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t},
respectively. As a result, (
25
) can be equivalently represented as the following factor model for
pM
-dimensional vector time series
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}
:

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\widetilde{\bf X}_{t}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t},

(26)

where the factor loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=({\mathbf{a}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{a}}_{\tilde{r}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{pM\times\tilde{r}}

and the noise vector
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{pM}
is decomposed as the sum of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime}_{t}
formed by truncation errors. Then we can integrate our suggested weight matrix into the estimation of
\tilde{r}
and
{\cal C}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
for the factor model (
26
). Hence the space spanned by columns of
\widetilde{\bf A}(\cdot)
can be recovered accordingly.

We finally identify three important directions for future research.
The first direction considers the extension of (
1
) to other forms of functional factor models. One possible extension admits the representation

{\bf Y}_{t}(u)={\bf A}(u){\bf X}_{t}(u)+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(u),u\in{\cal U},

which treats both the latent factor time series
\{{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)\}_{t=1}^{n}
and the factor loading matrix
{\bf A}(\cdot)=\{A_{jl}(\cdot)\}_{p\times r}
as functional objects in the sense of the concurrent functional model
Ramsay and
Silverman 2005
. On the other hand, inspired from the functional linear regression with functional response, we can consider another more generalized functional factor model in the form of

{\bf Y}_{t}(u)=\int_{{\cal U}}{\bf A}(u,v){\bf X}_{t}(v){\rm d}v+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(u),u\in{\cal U},
where
{\bf A}(\cdot,\cdot)=\{A_{jl}(\cdot,\cdot)\}_{p\times r}
is the operator-valued factor loading matrix with
p\times r
entries of bivariate functions. It is interesting to develop estimation procedures to fit the above two models. However, compared to the fittings for models (
1
) and (
24
), this would pose more complicated challenges that require further investigation.

Second, our estimation procedure is developed by assuming that the idiosyncratic component is a white noise sequence. It is also interesting to estimate the factor model (
1
) following the framework in
Bai and
Ng 2002
and
Fan
et al. 2013
, where the idiosyncratic noise is allowed to exhibit serial correlations.
Note the covariance function
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{(0)}(u,v)
or its integral over
{\cal U}\times{\cal U}
can not be used directly in the decomposition since they are not nonnegative definite and symmetric.
Under the column-orthogonality of
{\bf A}
and the uncorrelatedness between
{\bf X}_{t}(\cdot)
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}(\cdot),
we can slightly modify our scheme and consider decomposing the nonnegative definite and symmetric matrix
\int_{{\cal U}}\int_{{\cal U}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{yy}(u,v)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{yy}(u,v)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v
as the sum of the leading term
{\bf A}\big\{\int_{{\cal U}}\int_{{\cal U}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{xx}(u,v)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{xx}(u,v)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v\big\}{\bf A}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
and the remaining three terms of smaller orders. By imposing suitable eigenvalue conditions in a similar spirit to those in
Fan
et al. 2013
, the common factors are asymptotically identifiable and hence the factor loading space can be recovered by carrying out an eigenanalysis for
\int_{{\cal U}}\int_{{\cal U}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(0)}_{yy}(u,v)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(0)}_{yy}(u,v)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

Third, our estimation procedure is naturally adaptable to fit the factor model for high-dimensional scalar time series
Lam and Yao 2012
. We also believe that such procedure can be extended to deal with the factor modelling for high-dimensional matrix-valued time series
Wang
et al. 2019
or even tensor-valued time series
Chen
et al. 2022
. Despite the integration step being no longer needed, we can still incorporate the suggested weight matrix to account for the heterogeneous effect and improve the estimation efficiency.
These topics are beyond the scope of the current paper and will be pursued elsewhere.

\big\|\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M}\big\|=O_{\mathrm{p}}\left[c_{1}(p)\big\{c_{1}(p)+c_{2}(p)\big\}\mathcal{M}_{y}^{1-\tau}\Big(\frac{\log p}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1-\tau}{2}}\right].

(S.18)

Note that

\displaystyle\big\|\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M}\big\|=

\displaystyle O_{\mathrm{p}}\Bigg(\Big\|\iint\big[\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}(u,v)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\Big]\widehat{\bf W}(v)\big[\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}(u,v)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\Big]^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\Big\|

\displaystyle+\Big\|\iint\big[\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}(u,v)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\big]\widehat{\bf W}(v)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\Big\|\Bigg).

By Lemma
5
and (
S.8
), (
S.15
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\Big\|\iint\big[\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}(u,v)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\big]\widehat{\bf W}(v)\big[\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}(u,v)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\big]^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\Big\|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\big\|_{{\cal S},1}\big\|\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\big\|_{{\cal S},\infty}\big\{1+o_{\mathrm{p}}(1)\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{\mathrm{p}}\left[c_{1}(p)\big\{c_{1}(p)+c_{2}(p)\big\}\mathcal{M}_{y}^{2(1-\tau)}\Big(\frac{\log p}{n}\Big)^{1-\tau}\right],

\displaystyle\Big\|\iint\big[\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{k}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}(u,v)-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)\big]\widehat{\bf W}(v)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}(u,v)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\Big\|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\Big[\|\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{n}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\|_{{\cal S},1}\|\mathcal{T}_{\eta_{n}}\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(k)}_{yy}\}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\|_{{\cal S},\infty}\Big]^{1/2}\Big[\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\|_{{\cal S},1}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\|_{{\cal S},\infty}\Big]^{1/2}\big\{1+o_{\mathrm{p}}(1)\big\}.

For the second term above, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\|_{{\cal S},1}=\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|_{{\cal S}}\leq\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|^{\tau}_{{\cal S}}\|\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|^{1-\tau}_{{\cal S}}\lesssim c_{1}(p),

where
\tau\in(0,1)
and
\|\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|_{{\cal S}}
is uniformly bounded under Condition
8
. Following the similar procedure, we can show that
\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{yy}\|_{{\cal S},\infty}\lesssim c_{1}(p)+c_{2}(p)
. For the truly sparse case with
\tau=0
, it follows from the derivation in Section
S.3
under Conditions
2
,
3
,
7
with fixed
r
and
L
that

\displaystyle\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|_{{\cal S}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq p}\sum_{1\leq m,m^{\prime}\leq r}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|A_{im}A_{jm^{\prime}}|\|\Sigma_{xx,mm^{\prime}}^{(k)}\|_{{\cal S}}+\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq p}\sum_{m=1}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|A_{im}|\|\Sigma^{(k)}_{x\epsilon,mj}\|_{{\cal S}}

\displaystyle\lesssim r^{2}Lc_{1}(p)+rc_{1}(p)\asymp c_{1}(p).

Similarly, under Condition
′
‣
4
, we can show that

\displaystyle\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\Sigma_{yy,ij}^{(k)}\|_{{\cal S}}\lesssim r^{2}Lc_{1}(p)+rLc_{2}(p)\asymp c_{1}(p)+c_{2}(p).

The above results together with (
S.15
) from Step 1 implies (
S.18
).

Step 3
.
Denote
\hat{\epsilon}=\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{C}({\bf K}),\mathcal{C}(\widetilde{\bf K})\big).

By the fact that
\hat{\epsilon}^{2}=(2r)^{-1}\|{\bf K}{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}-\widetilde{\bf K}\widetilde{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
and Corollary 4.1 in
Vu and Lei (2013)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle 2r\nu_{r}\hat{\epsilon}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M},\widetilde{\bf K}\widetilde{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}-{\bf K}{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Big\langle\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M},\frac{\widetilde{\bf K}\widetilde{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}-{\bf K}{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\|\widetilde{\bf K}\widetilde{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}-{\bf K}{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\|_{\text{F}}}\Big\rangle\right|\cdot\sqrt{2r}\hat{\epsilon},

which further implies that

\nu_{r}\hat{\epsilon}\leq(2r)^{-1/2}\Big|\big\langle\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M},\boldsymbol{\widehat{\Delta}}\big\rangle\Big|,

where
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\Delta}}=\frac{\widetilde{\bf K}\widetilde{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}-{\bf K}{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}{\|\widetilde{\bf K}\widetilde{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}-{\bf K}{\bf K}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\|_{\text{F}}}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\Delta}}\|_{\text{F}}=1.

Note that
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\Delta}}
is a matrix with rank at most
2r
and the associated singular values
\hat{\sigma}_{1}\geq\hat{\sigma}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\hat{\sigma}_{2r}\geq 0.
Then by the von-Neumann’s trace inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\nu_{r}\hat{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq(2r)^{-1/2}\big\|\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M}\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{2r}\hat{\sigma}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq(2r)^{-1/2}\big\|\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M}\big\|\sqrt{2r\sum_{i=1}^{2r}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}=\big\|\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M}\big\|\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{\Delta}}\|_{\text{F}}=\big\|\widetilde{\bf M}-\widecheck{\bf M}\big\|.

By (
S.18
) from Step 2, we complete the proof of Theorem
4
.
\square

Lemma 1

Proof of Lemma

{\bf A}=(A_{ij})_{p\times r}

(x+y)^{\tau}\leq x^{\tau}+y^{\tau}

\displaystyle\big\|\Sigma^{(k)}_{yy,ij}\big\|^{\tau}_{{\cal S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq m,m^{\prime}\leq r}\big|A_{im}A_{jm^{\prime}}\big|^{\tau}\big\|\Sigma^{(k)}_{xx,mm^{\prime}}\big\|_{{\cal S}}^{\tau}+\sum_{m=1}^{r}\big|A_{im}\big|^{\tau}\big\|\Sigma^{(k)}_{x\epsilon,mj}\big\|_{{\cal S}}^{\tau},

where, under Conditions

\begin{split}\big\|\Sigma^{(k)}_{xx,mm^{\prime}}\big\|_{{\cal S}}^{2}=&\iint[E\{X_{(t+k)m}(u)X_{t,m^{\prime}}(v)\}]^{2}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\\

(S.19)

Lemma 2
.

For a measurable function
f:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a compact set and
\mathcal{B}
is a separable Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|
, if the norm of
f
has finite integral, then
\|\textstyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}f(x)\mathrm{d}x\|\leq\textstyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}\|f(x)\|\mathrm{d}x
.

Proof.

Let
\nu=\int_{{\cal D}}f(x){\rm d}x\in{\cal B},{\cal B}^{*}
is the dual space of
{\cal B}
and
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is the dual norm. By the Hahn-Banach theorem, there exists
\phi\in{\cal B}^{*}
such that
\|\phi\|_{*}=1
and
\|\phi(\nu)\|_{*}=\|\nu\|=\big\|\int_{{\cal D}}f(x){\rm d}x\big\|.
Thus, we have

\left\|\int_{{\cal D}}f(x){\rm d}x\right\|=\left\|\phi\Big(\int_{{\cal D}}f(x){\rm d}x\Big)\right\|_{*}=\left\|\int_{{\cal D}}\phi\{f(x)\}{\rm d}x\right\|_{*}\leq\int_{{\cal D}}\|\phi\{f(x)\}\|_{*}{\rm d}x\leq\int_{{\cal D}}\|f(x)\|{\rm d}x,

where the second equality follows from that the dual space
{\cal B}^{*}
of
{\cal B}
is a Banach space (also a linear space) and the last inequality follows from
\|\phi(f)\|_{*}\leq\|\phi\|_{*}\cdot\|f\|=\|f\|
.
∎

The third lemma specifies the order of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that Condition
2
holds, then for
k=1,\dots,k_{0},

\iint\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)\|^{2}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v=O(1)
and
\|\iint\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\|=O(1).

Proof of Lemma
3
.

By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, Condition
2
and Fubini Theorem,

\displaystyle\iint\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)\|^{2}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\leq\iint\|\mathbb{E}\{{\bf X}_{t+k}(u){\bf X}_{t}(v)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\iint\big[{\mathbb{E}}\{X_{(t+k)i}(u)X_{tj}(v)\}\big]^{2}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\leq\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\|X_{(t+k)i}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\Big\}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{r}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\|X_{tj}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\Big\}=O(1).

On the other hand, by Lemma
2
, we have that

\left\|\iint\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\right\|^{2}\leq\Big(\iint\big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)\big\|\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\Big)^{2}\leq\text{Vol}({\cal U}\times{\cal U})\iint\big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{xx}(u,v)\big\|^{2}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v=O(1),

where
\text{Vol}(\cdot)
represents the volume.
∎

Lemma 5
.

For
{\bf S}(u,v)=\{S_{ij}(u,v)\}_{p\times p}

{\bf T}(u,v)=\{T_{ij}(u,v)\}_{p\times p}

\displaystyle\Big\|\iint{\bf S}(u,v){\bf T}(u,v)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\,{\rm d}u{\rm d}v\Big\|_{1}
