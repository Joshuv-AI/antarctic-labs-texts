Operational Methods in the Study of Sobolev-Jacobi Polynomials
Nicolas Behr *, Giuseppe Dattoli, Gérard H. E. Duchamp, Silvia Licciardi, and Karol A. Penson

Abstract
Inspired by ideas from umbral calculus and based on the two types of integrals occurring in the defining equations for the gamma and the reciprocal gamma functions, respectively, we develop a multi-variate version of umbral calculus and of the so-called umbral image technique. Besides providing a class of new formulae for generalized hypergeometric functions and an implementation of series manipulations for computing lacunary generating functions, our main application of these techniques is the study of Sobolev-Jacobi polynomials. Motivated by applications to theoretical chemistry, we moreover present a deep link between generalized normal-ordering techniques introduced by Gurappa and Panigrahi, two-variable Hermite polynomials and our integral-based series transforms. Notably, we thus calculate all K K -tuple L L -shifted lacunary exponential generating functions for a certain family of Sobolev-Jacobi (SJ) polynomials explicitly.

Introduction

The operational methods established in the second half of the 19th century have paved the way to the formalism of quantum mechanics and of umbral calculus
Roman and Rota 1978
. The formulation and the technicalities of the quasi-monomiality
Dattoli 2000
deepened their roots in the operational formalism and offer a powerful tool to simplify most of the computations associated with the handling of special functions relevant e.g., to the evaluation of generating functions, integrals, and other associated issues. The methods which we will exploit in this paper trace back to the original formulation
Roman and Rota 1978
;
Roman 1984
, merged with the complementary aspects developed within the context of the solution of evolution problems
Dattoli et al. 1997
and the theory of generalized special functions
Dattoli et al. 1999
. Some of the aforementioned complementary aspects were already developed in a wide range of fields, including astrophysics
Dattoli and Zhukovsky 2007
, quantum mechanics
Zhukovsky and Dattoli 2011
, free-electron lasers
Artioli et al. 2017
and heat conduction
Dattoli et al. 2017
, with some of the most recent developments reported in
Zhukovsky 2017
;
Zhukovsky 2016a
;
Zhukovsky 2016b
;
Zhukovsky 2017
;
Zhukovsky 2016
(which are based on
Dattoli 2000
;
Roman 1984
;
Dattoli et al. 1997
;
Dattoli et al. 1999
;
Dattoli et al. 2007
).

Among the well-established results of such operational methods are formulae such as the operational definition of the two-variable Hermite polynomials
H^{(m)}_{n}(x,y)
:

H_{n}^{(m)}(x,y):=e^{y\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}}x^{n}=n!\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor}\frac{x^{n-km}y^{k}}{(n-km)!k!}\,

(1)

In this paper, based on a remarkable operational technique developed by Gurappa and Panigrahi
Gurappa et al. 1996
;
Gurappa et al. 2001
;
Gurappa et al. 2002
;
Gurappa et al. 2003
, we will present an extension of the concept of such exponential formulae by virtue of including a rational function of the Euler operator
D=x\frac{d}{dx}
in the exponential. This will provide a definition of the Sobolev-Jacobi (SJ) polynomials, which are the central objects of our study here.

The second main technical tool for our study is the so-called
umbral image
method, which has been introduced in
Babusci et al. 2013
;
Babusci et al. 2014
;
Dattoli et al. 2017
(see also
Dattoli et al. 2007
;
Licciardi 2018
). In favorable cases, this method simplifies computations associated with special functions or orthogonal polynomials considerably. Some concrete examples of such techniques are presented in Section
2
. However, in the applications related to SJ polynomials studied in this paper, we will need a certain multi-variate extension of the umbral image method, which we realize in the form of integral transforms related to gamma function-type integrals. This allows us to clarify en passant several technical details that remain somewhat implicit in the traditional umbral calculus approach. Our technique is entirely elementary and, while motivated by and largely analogous to the ideas of umbral calculus, is mathematically self-contained.

Referring the interested readers to
Behr et al. 2017
for a more detailed exposition and further technical details, we would like to briefly mention our original motivation in studying SJ polynomials, which stems from the theory of chemical reaction systems. It is customary in this field to encode the dynamics of such systems in terms of certain probability generating functions and evolution equations thereof, thus in certain special cases, (generalized) orthogonal polynomials may take the role of eigenfunctions of the evolution operators and may thus permit to solve the dynamics problem in closed form. The simplest possible example in which SJ polynomials arise is the case of a chemical reaction system of one species of molecules and with a single reaction of the form
2X\rightharpoonup\emptyset
(whence in each transition two particles of type
X
decay, for simplicity at unit base rate). Mathematically, we may describe the system via defining a probability generating function (with real-valued time parameter
t
and formal variable
x
)

P(t;x):=\sum_{n\geq 0}p_{n}(t)x^{n}\,

(2)

where
x^{n}
encodes the system state of having
n
particles present, and
p_{n}(t;x)
the probability of encountering such a state at time
t
. The evolution equation of this reaction system reads

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial}P(t;x)=(1-\hat{x}^{2})\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\,P(t;x)\qquad\qquad(t\geq 0)\,

(3)

with
\hat{x}
the formal multiplication operator on formal power series (i.e.,
\hat{x}(x^{n})=x^{n+1}
). As will be presented in full technical detail in Section
3
, the Sobolev-Jacobi polynomials of a particular type are precisely the eigenfunctions of the differential operator
(1-\hat{x}^{2})\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}
. Given an initial system state, which could for example consist in a state with precisely
N_{0}
particles (whence
P(0;x)=x^{N_{0}}
), one may provide a closed-form solution to (
3
) if it is in addition possible to express the initial configuration in terms of the SJ polynomials, a computation which requires knowledge of the so-called connection coefficients and which is performed in Section
5.6
. We mention in passing that the Sobolev-Jacobi polynomials for the other set of parameters as discussed in Section
4
are another particularly interesting case of relevance to chemical reaction systems, since as explained in
Behr et al. 2017
they permit to solve exactly problems involving reaction systems with all three elementary decay-type reactions of one particle species present.

Structure of the paper:
In Section
2
, we introduce an integral transform technique that permits to implement the analogue of multi-variate umbral images in the spirit of umbral calculus. Sobolev-Jacobi polynomials are introduced in Section
3
, and some first results for a particular subclass of such polynomials are presented in Section
4
. The remaining class of Sobolev-Jacobi (SJ) polynomials is studied in detail starting from Section
5
, including a type of generalized normal-ordering technique involving Euler operators in Section
5.1
that is of relevance to these considerations. We then proceed to compute both exponential and lacunary exponential generating functions for the SJ polynomials in Sections
5.2
and
5.5
. This study is made possible due to a discovery of a deep relationship between two-variable Hermite polynomials and SJ polynomials, which is explained in Section
5.3
, and which relies on our series transform techniques (which coincidentally also permit a concise definition of lacunary exponential generating functions (EGF) themselves, see Section
5.4
). Finally, we devote Section
5.6
to the study of the connection coefficients of the SJ polynomials, and provide some further details and proofs in the technical appendices.

2
Multi-Variate Umbral Calculus via Integral Transforms

The key result of this section will be a reformulation (and suitable extension of) the so-called
umbral image method

Dattoli et al. 2007
;
Babusci et al. 2013
;
Babusci et al. 2014
;
Dattoli et al. 2017
;
Górska et al. 2012
, which permits to perform certain widely applicable types of series transforms. Rather than working with the umbral calculus notions of operators acting on “umbral vacua” as typical in the more traditional formulations of umbral calculus (see e.g.,
Licciardi 2018
for an extensive overview of modern umbral calculus), we opt here for an alternative approach: series transforms will be implemented based on certain elementary integrals. Our motivations are two-fold: firstly, we wish to make our techniques accessible to a wider audience, and thus found it necessary to develop a formulation that is both mathematically sound and firmly rooted in well-established elementary concepts. Secondly, the calculations in this paper will often require the analogues of
multi-variate
umbral calculus techniques, which are immediately accessible in our present formalism, but in tendency would require certain somewhat ad hoc constructions in the traditional approach. For the interested readers’ convenience, we will present several illustrative examples, in which we will also comment on the relationship to the more traditional umbral calculus approach.

The two types of integrals upon which our calculus will be based are those of the defining equations for the gamma function (Equation (5.2.1) in
DLMF
),

\Gamma(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;e^{-t}t^{z-1}\,

(4)

and of the reciprocal gamma function (Equation (5.9.2) in
DLMF
)

\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}dt\;e^{t}t^{-z}\,

(5)

here, according to loc. cit.,
t^{-z}
is understood as having its principal value where
t
crosses the positive real axis and is continuous, and the integration is taken along the
Hankel contour

\gamma
(see
DLMF
, Image 5.9.F1). It is well-known that
\Gamma(z)
as defined in (
4
) (and for
Re(z)<0
by analytic continuation) is a meromorphic function with no zeros, and simple poles at
z=-n
(
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
) of residue
(-1)^{n}/n!
. The reciprocal gamma function
1/\Gamma(z)
is an entire function with simple zeros at
z=-n
(
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
). The gamma function (resp. reciprocal gamma function) used here will be its full analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
(resp.
\mathbb{C}
).

As a second key ingredient, we will need the following notion of
generalized monomials
(and of formal power series thereover):

{Definition}

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an alphabet (i.e., a set of symbols or of formal variables), and let
\mathbb{C}^{(\mathcal{A})}
denote the set of all functions
\alpha:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
finite support
,

\mathbb{C}^{(\mathcal{A})}:=\{\alpha:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mid|\mathop{supp}(\alpha)|<\infty\}\,

(6)

Here, we define the support
\mathop{supp}(\alpha)
of a function
\alpha:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
as the set of elements
a\in\mathcal{A}
on which
\alpha
takes non-zero values, i.e.,
\mathop{supp}(\alpha):=\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid\alpha(a)\neq 0\}
.

Then we denote by
G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{X})\equiv(\mathbb{C}^{(\mathcal{A})},\cdot)
the group of
generalized monomials
, whose multiplication
\cdot
is defined as

\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\cdot\mathcal{A}^{\beta}=\mathcal{A}^{\alpha+\beta}\,

(7)

here, we have employed the
multi-index notation

\{\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{(\mathcal{A})}}\,

(8)

such that for a given function
\alpha:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with support

\mathop{supp}(\alpha)=\{a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{A}

the corresponding element
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\in G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A})
reads more explicitly

\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}=a_{1}^{\alpha(a_{1})}\dotsc a_{n}^{\alpha(a_{n})}\,

(9)

Moreover, we denote by
\mathbb{C}[G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A})]
the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra
of the group
G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A})
.

It is instructive to note that for an element
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\in G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A})
with
\mathop{supp}(\alpha)\subset\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}
is nothing but a Laurent polynomial, while for
\mathop{supp}(\alpha)\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
it is an ordinary polynomial, which motivated the moniker “generalized monomials” for generic elements of
G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A})
. In other words, a generic element
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\in G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A})
might be thought of as a “monomial with complex exponents”.

Combining the notion of generalized polynomials (extended to certain formal power series) with the two types of integrals as introduced above, we will now introduce a formal integration operator that will play the central role in our implementation of multi-variate umbral calculus.

{Definition}

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{\lambda\}\uplus\mathcal{U}\uplus\mathcal{V}\uplus\mathcal{X}
be an alphabet of formal variables, where
\uplus
denotes the operation of disjoint union, and where
\{\lambda\}
,
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{X}
are four (disjoint) alphabets of auxiliary formal variables. We will typically employ notations such as
\mathcal{X}=\{x,y,x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc\}
, where we make use of the indexed variable notations in case of many variables for convenience. Let furthermore
\mathcal{A}_{\bullet}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\lambda\}
.

We define a
formal integration operator

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
via specifying first its domain
\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}})
as

\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}):=\{S\in\mathbb{C}[G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A}_{\bullet})][[\lambda]]\mid\mbox{for all }\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\in supp(S):\mathop{range}(\alpha|_{\mathcal{U}})\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}\}\,

(10)

whence elements of
\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}})
are formal power series in
\lambda
with coefficients that are generalized polynomials over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{\bullet}
(where the extension to formal power series requires a suitable notion of summability, see below). Here, the notation
\mathop{range}(\alpha|_{\mathcal{U}})\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
entails that functions
\alpha:\mathcal{A}_{\bullet}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
are required to not take zero or negative integer values when evaluated on elements of
\mathcal{U}
. Then for some monomial
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\in\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}})
, which reads more explicitly (recall that by definition of
\mathbb{C}^{(\mathcal{A})}
, we have that
\alpha(u_{i})\neq 0
and
\alpha(v_{j})\neq 0
for only finitely many indices
i
and
j
)

\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}=u_{1}^{\alpha(u_{1})}u_{2}^{\alpha(u_{2})}\dotsc v_{1}^{\alpha(v_{1})}v_{2}^{\alpha(v_{2})}\dotsc\,

the action of
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
on
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle:=\left(\prod_{u_{i}\in U(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}du_{i}\;\frac{e^{-u_{i}}}{u_{i}}\right)\left[\left(\prod_{v_{j}\in V(\alpha)}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}dv_{j}\;e^{v_{j}}\right)\left[\mathcal{A}^{\tilde{\alpha}}\right]\right]

(11)

\displaystyle U(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{supp}(\alpha)\cap\mathcal{U}\,,\;V(\alpha):=\mathop{supp}(\alpha)\cap\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(a)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\alpha(a)&\text{if }a\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{V}\\
-\alpha(a)\quad&\text{if }a\in\mathcal{V}\end{cases}

We extend
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
by linearity to finite sums. For infinite sums, this requires an appropriate notion of convergence. A series
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\mathcal{A}^{\alpha_{i}}
will be in
\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}})
(the domain of
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
) if the family
\Big(c_{i}\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(\mathcal{A}^{\alpha_{i}})\Big)_{i\in I}
is
summable
in the target (in the sense of discrete summability or compact convergence for entire functions). For example, in Equation (
19
), the target is the space of entire functions endowed with the topology of compact convergence. In the sequel, the target will always be a topological ring to ensure continuity of products and sums.

Note that our definition of the integration over the formal variables
\mathcal{V}
(i.e., integrating the monomial
\mathcal{A}^{\bar{\alpha}}
given an input
\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}
) was designed purely for notational convenience, i.e., such as to avoid having to carry negative exponents in our standard application of e.g., expressing
1/\Gamma(\beta)
as
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(v^{\beta})
(see also the examples provided in Section
2
for further illustrations).

The readers may verify that our choice of domain
\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}})
as given in (
10
) precisely ensures that we avoid the poles in the definition of
\Gamma(z)
in our series transformation calculations, which renders the technique mathematically well-posed.
In all of our practical applications, we will further restrict the formal power series in
\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}})
to
\mathop{range}(\alpha)\cap\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, which entails that the
group
of monomials
G_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A}_{\bullet})
restricts to a
group
for
\mathcal{V}
, a
semigroup
for
\mathcal{X}
and not even a semigroup for
\mathcal{U}
: for example, consider the admissible monomial
u^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, whose square is not in the domain.

The operator
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
is
multiplicative
on monomials whose underlying alphabets do not overlap, and it is checked easily that it is not so, in general, for monomials and sums of monomials which have variables in common. To ensure mathematical consistency, we provide the reader below with a precise statement on multiplicativity.

{Lemma}

We suppose that

1.

the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is partitioned into two disjoint subalphabets

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\uplus\mathcal{A}_{2}

2.

we are given two series

S_{1}=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}c_{\alpha_{i}}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\alpha_{i}}\;\text{and}\;S_{2}=\sum_{j\in I_{2}}c_{\alpha_{j}}\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}}

such that
S_{1},S_{2}\in dom(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}{})
.

Then
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(S_{1}S_{2})
exists and

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(S_{1}S_{2})=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(S_{1})\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(S_{2})

{proof}

In the sequence of steps below,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(S_{1})\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(S_{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}c_{\alpha_{i}}\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\alpha_{i}})\sum_{j\in I_{2}}c_{\alpha_{j}}\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(2)}}{{=}}\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}}c_{\alpha_{i}}c_{\alpha_{j}}\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\alpha_{i}})\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(3)}}{{=}}\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}}c_{\alpha_{i}}c_{\alpha_{j}}\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\alpha_{i}}\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(4)}}{{=}}\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\Big(\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}}c_{\alpha_{i}}c_{\alpha_{j}}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\alpha_{i}}\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\alpha_{j}}\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(5)}}{{=}}\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(S_{1}S_{2})\,

step
(1)
follows because
S_{k},\ k=1,2
are in
\mathop{dom}(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}{})
, step
(2)
because the target is a topological ring, step
(3)
because the alphabets are disjoint, step
(4)
because the series is summable, and finally step
(5)
via rewriting of the argument (which is summable for the discrete topology).

Examples and Relation to Traditional Umbral Calculus

Consider first as an elementary example the formulae (for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
)

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u^{\alpha}\right)=\Gamma(\alpha)\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(v^{\beta}\right)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,

(12)

Compared to the umbral calculus literature, we find the analogous equations (see e.g.,
Babusci et al. 2013
;
Babusci et al. 2014
;
Dattoli et al. 2017
)

\hat{d}^{\gamma}\psi_{0}:=\Gamma(\gamma+1)\,,\quad\hat{c}^{\delta}\varphi_{0}:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\delta+1)}\,

(13)

here,
\psi_{0}
and
\varphi_{0}
are the “umbral vacua”, and we have the parameters
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-1}
and
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
. The “umbrae”
\hat{c}
and
\hat{d}
are usually further characterized by formulae such as

\hat{c}^{\alpha}\hat{c}^{\beta}\varphi_{0}=\hat{c}^{\alpha+\beta}\varphi_{0}\,,\quad\hat{d}^{\gamma}\hat{d}^{\delta}\psi_{0}=\hat{d}^{\gamma+\delta}\psi_{0}\,

(14)

However, it is important to note that
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\alpha+\beta
must all be elements of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-1}
, which renders claims that
\hat{c}
satisfies some group-like properties as typical in the umbral calculus parlance somewhat questionable. Moreover, typically the precise typing of the “vacua” is left implicit, thereby adding to the difficulties in validating umbral techniques beyond individual application examples. The nature of the “vacua” becomes even more implicit to characterize upon considering multi-variate extensions of the umbral methods, the analogues of which in our own formulation will find frequent application in this paper. Here, we thus prefer to work instead solely based on the definition of the operation
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
, taking care in each individual example that
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
is applied to a well-formed expression in its domain
D(\lambda;\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V};\mathcal{X})
.

To provide a few first application examples for our integral operator approach seen to manipulate formal power series, note first that by definition (for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
)

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u^{\alpha+n}v^{\alpha}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=(\alpha)_{n}\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u^{\beta}v^{\beta+n}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta+n)}=\frac{1}{(\beta)_{n}}\,

(15)

here, we employ the notation
(x)_{y}:=\Gamma(x+y)/\Gamma(x)
for the
Pochhammer symbol
, as customary e.g., in the literature on hypergeometric functions. The relations provided in (
15
) induce an interesting alternative formula for the generalized hypergeometric functions, with parameters
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, and where we consider the variable
z
as
formal
in order to avoid issues of convergence (see e.g.,
DLMF
, §16.2, according to which in particular all generalized hypergeometric series with
p>q+1
are divergent):

{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\vec{\alpha}}{\vec{\beta}};z\right]}\equiv{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\seq{\alpha_i}{1\leq i\leq p}}{\seq{\beta_j}{1\leq j\leq q}};z\right]}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\frac{(\alpha_{1})_{n}\cdots(\alpha_{p})_{n}}{(\beta_{1})_{n}\cdots(\beta_{q})_{n}}\

(16)

namely

{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\vec{\alpha}}{\vec{\beta}};z\right]}=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}(u_{i}v_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{q}(u_{j+p}v_{j+p})^{\beta_{j}}\right)\;e^{zu_{1}\cdots u_{p}v_{p+1}\cdots v_{p+q}}\right)\,

(17)

To demonstrate the utility of the integral transform approach as a method to study special functions, we next present an adaptation of a result obtained with umbral methods in
Babusci et al. 2014
. Consider first the following computation:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}(G_{v}(x))

\displaystyle:=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(ve^{-v\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}}\right)

(18a)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2r}v^{r+1}\right)

(18b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(r!)^{2}}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2r}=J_{0}(x)\,.

(18c)

here,
J_{0}(x)
denotes the
0
-th order cylindrical Bessel function. We have thus established that the so-called
umbral image
of the Gaussian function
G_{\alpha}(x):=\alpha\exp(-\alpha(x/2)^{2})
(at
\alpha=v
and integrated using the operation
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
) is the function
J_{0}(x)
. This finding may be further generalized to the following relation involving the
n
-th order cylindrical Bessel functions
J_{n}(x)
:

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(v\left(\frac{vx}{2}\right)^{n}e^{-v\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}}\right)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(n+r)!r!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{n+2r}=J_{n}(x)\,

(19)

As explained in
Babusci et al. 2014
, such a restyling has allowed noticeable simplifications, concerning the handling of integrals of Bessel functions and of many other problems associated with the use of the Ramanujan master theorem. For the purposes of the present paper, a multi-variate analogue of the “umbral image” concept will permit us to uncover some hidden structures in the study of SJ polynomials.

Our methods inherit several equivalences from the properties of the gamma and reciprocal gamma functions, which depending on the application may pose an obstacle or a virtue but are in a certain sense unavoidable. Based on the functional relations

\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)\qquad(z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus{\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}})\,

we find the umbral image analogues (for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(e^{\frac{\partial}{\partial a}}u^{a}\right)\big|_{a\to\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u^{\alpha+1}\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\alpha u^{\alpha}\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u}\,u^{\alpha}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(e^{\frac{\partial}{\partial b}}v^{b}\right)\big|_{b\to\beta}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(v^{\beta+1}\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{v^{\beta}}{\beta}\right)\,

For convenience, we will from hereon take a notational convention for formal derivatives that permits to rewrite the first line of the above equation in the more concise form

e^{\partial_{\beta}}v^{\beta}\equiv\left(e^{\frac{\partial}{\partial b}}v^{b}\right)\big|_{b\to\beta}\,

(21)

As an example, for interesting consequences of such identities, consider Euler’s beta function (
DLMF
, §5.12)
B(\alpha,\beta)

B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta}\right)\,

(22)

here and throughout the remainder of this section, we assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
are in the domain of definition of
B(\alpha,\beta)
, whence that
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
with
(\alpha+\beta)\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}

(\alpha+\beta)

B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{(\alpha+\beta)\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{(\alpha+\beta)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}=\frac{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)}+\frac{\beta\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)}=B(\alpha+1,\beta)+B(\alpha,\beta+1)\,,

(23)

we may identify
B(\alpha,\beta)
as eigenfunctions of eigenvalue
1
of the differential operator
e^{\partial_{\alpha}}+e^{\partial_{\beta}}
,

\displaystyle B(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\left(e^{\partial_{\alpha}}+e^{\partial_{\beta}}\right)B(\alpha,\beta)

(24a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\left(e^{\partial_{\alpha}}+e^{\partial_{\beta}}\right)u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta}\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta+1}(u_{1}+u_{2})\right)\,

(24b)

We find in fact an infinite tower of equivalences by noting that for any non-negative integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle B(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\left(e^{\partial_{\alpha}}+e^{\partial_{\beta}}\right)^{n}B(\alpha,\beta)

(25a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\left(e^{\partial_{\alpha}}+e^{\partial_{\beta}}\right)^{n}u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta}\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta+n}(u_{1}+u_{2})^{n}\right)\,

(25b)

Moreover, by rephrasing (

B(\alpha,\beta+1)=B(\alpha,\beta)-B(\alpha+1,\beta)=\left(1-e^{\partial_{\alpha}}\right)B(\alpha,\beta)

(26)

iterating yields the family of identities (for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
)

\displaystyle B(\alpha,\beta+n)

\displaystyle=\left(1-e^{\partial_{\alpha}}\right)^{n}B(\alpha,\beta)

(27a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta+n}v^{\alpha+\beta+n}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\left(1-e^{\partial_{\alpha}}\right)^{n}u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta}\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u_{1}^{\alpha}u_{2}^{\beta}v^{\alpha+\beta}\left(1-u_{1}v\right)^{n}\right)\,

(27b)

The last Equation (

) will prove to be of particular importance for the forthcoming discussion. It will be exploited to settle out the proof of important identities, whose technicalities are detailed e.g., in Appendix
D
.

We conclude this section with a particularly interesting use of the auxiliary relations (
20
), regarding explicit expressions for the derivative of generalized hypergeometric functions
{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta};z)
(which we will need in our calculations of shifts of lacunary generating functions in Section

). Acting with a derivative by

on (

) and using the auxiliary relations (
20
) repeatedly leads to:

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial z}{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\vec{\alpha}}{\vec{\beta}};z\right]}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}(u_{i}v_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{q}(u_{j+p}v_{j+p})^{\beta_{j}}\right)\;u_{1}\cdots u_{p}v_{p+1}\cdots v_{p+q}e^{zu_{1}\cdots u_{p}v_{p+1}\cdots v_{p+q}}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}}\right)\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}(u_{i}v_{i})^{\alpha_{i}+1}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{q}(u_{j+p}v_{j+p})^{\beta_{j}+1}\right)\;e^{zu_{1}\cdots u_{p}v_{p+1}\cdots v_{p+q}}\right)\,

We thus find the expression

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial z}{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\vec{\alpha}}{\vec{\beta}};z\right]}=\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}}\right){}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\vec{\alpha+1}}{\vec{\beta+1}};z\right]}\,.

(29)

Proceeding analogously for higher
z
-derivatives then reproduces the following closed-form expression (see Chapter 1.30.1.1 in
Brychkov 2008
), for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
:

\tfrac{\partial^{n}}{\partial z^{n}}{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\vec{\alpha}}{\vec{\beta}};z\right]}=\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}(\alpha_{i})_{n}}{\prod_{j=1}^{q}(\beta_{j})_{n}}\right){}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\vec{\alpha+n}}{\vec{\beta+n}};z\right]}\,

(30)

3
The Sobolev-Jacobi Polynomials

We introduce (following Kwon and Littlejohn
Kwon et al. 1994
;
Kwon et al. 1996
;
Kwon and Littlejohn 1998
) the
SJ polynomials

\tilde{P}^{(-1,-\beta)}_{n}(x)
as the polynomial eigenfunctions of the Jacobi-type differential operators
\hat{D}^{(-1,\beta)}_{Jac}
(with
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq-1}
):

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{(-1,\beta)}_{Jac}\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=-n(n+\beta)\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)

(31)

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{(-1,\beta)}_{Jac}

\displaystyle=(1-\hat{x}^{2})\partial_{x}^{2}+(\beta+1)\big(1-\hat{x}\big)\partial_{x}\,

here, we have made use of the notations
\hat{x}
and
\partial_{x}
for the following linear operators:

\hat{x}f(x):=xf(x)\,,\quad\partial_{x}f(x)=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)\,

(32)

It will prove useful in our later considerations that
\hat{x}
and
\partial_{x}
form a representation of the
Heisenberg-Weyl algebra
(i.e., the
Bargmann-Fock representation
):

[\partial_{x},\hat{x}]=\mathbbm{1}\,

(33)

For clarity, let us briefly comment on the relationship of the SJ polynomials to the ordinary Jacobi polynomials
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
. The Jacobi-type differential operator(cf. the standard reference book
Olver et al. 2010
, chapter 18, p. 445, Table 18.8.1)

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{Jac}

\displaystyle:=(1-x^{2})\partial_{x}^{2}+q^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\partial_{x}

(34)

\displaystyle q^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)

\displaystyle=(\beta-\alpha-(\alpha+\beta+2)x)

with
real parameters

\alpha,\beta>-1

(35)

is known to possess a
system of orthogonal polynomials

\{P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(z)\}_{n\geq 0}
, the so-called
classical Jacobi polynomials
, as its complete basis of eigenfunctions (see (
38
) for the associated inner product). More explicitly (cf. e.g., Equation (18.5.8) in
Olver et al. 2010
),

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n+\alpha}{\ell}\binom{n+\beta}{n-\ell}\left(\frac{x-1}{2}\right)^{n-\ell}\left(\frac{x+1}{2}\right)^{\ell}\,

(36)

satisfying the eigenequation

\hat{D}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{Jac}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=-n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,

(37)

The orthogonality property is found by defining for each admissible choice of parameters
\alpha,\beta>-1
an
inner product

\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
on the space of polynomials
\mathbb{R}[x]
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(p,q)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-1}^{+1}dx\;w_{\alpha,\beta}(x)p(x)q(x)

(38a)

\displaystyle w_{\alpha,\beta}(x)

\displaystyle:=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}\,,\;p,q\in\mathbb{R}[x]\,

(38b)

It is one of the classical results of the theory of orthogonal polynomials that for all
n\geq 0

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\in L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}([-1,1])\,

(39)

here,
L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}([-1,1])
denotes the space of functions on the interval
[-1,1]
which are
square-integrable
with respect to the integral against the weight function
w_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
. Moreover, orthogonality manifests itself as

\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}\left(P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)},P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\right)=\delta_{m,n}\phi_{\alpha,\beta}(n)\,

(40)

where
\phi_{\alpha,\beta}(n)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are some (non-zero) real numbers.

However, if one wishes to study a Jacobi-type differential operator with
\alpha=-1
and either
\beta>-1
or
\beta=-1
, the family of polynomials
\{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
ceases to constitute a complete system of orthogonal polynomials: for both cases, the polynomial
P_{0}^{(-1,\beta)}(x)=1
is not integrable, and moreover in the case
\alpha=-1
and
\beta=-1
one finds that
P_{1}^{(-1,-1)}(x)=0
. Referring to
Behr et al. 2017
for a detailed discussion, the resolution of this problem proposed by Kwon and Littlejohn
Kwon et al. 1994
;
Kwon et al. 1996
;
Kwon and Littlejohn 1998
;
Bruder and Littlejohn 2012
consists in redefining the notion of orthogonality: one no longer requires orthogonality of the polynomials with respect to the inner product
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
, but instead defines a Sobolev-type inner product. We quote from
Kwon and Littlejohn 1998
the relevant material (cf. Propositions 4.2 and 4.3 of loc cit):

{Proposition}

Let the
Sobolev inner products

\Phi^{(-1,-1)}_{A,B}
and
\Phi_{C}^{(-1,\beta)}
(for
\beta>-1
) be defined as follows (for
p,q\in\mathbb{R}[x]
some polynomials with real coefficients):

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-1,-1)}_{A,B}(p,q)

\displaystyle:=Ap(1)q(1)+Bp(-1)q(-1)+\int_{-1}^{+1}dx\;p^{\prime}(x)q^{\prime}(x)

(41)

\displaystyle\Phi^{(-1,\beta)}_{C}(p,q)

\displaystyle:=Cp(1)q(1)+\int_{-1}^{+1}dx\;(x+1)^{\beta+1}p^{\prime}(x)q^{\prime}(x)\qquad(\beta>-1)\,

here,
p^{\prime}(x)
and
q^{\prime}(x)
denote the first derivatives of the polynomials, and
A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}
are parameters. For the resulting inner products to be
positive definite
, the parameters
A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}
must satisfy the following conditions: for
\Phi^{(-1,-1)}_{A,B}
,
A
, and
B
must verify

A+B>0\,,\;A(\gamma+1)^{2}+B(\gamma-1)^{2}+2\neq 0\,,\;A(\gamma+1)+B(\gamma-1)=0\,,\;\gamma:=(B-A)/(A+B)\,

(42)

while for
\Phi_{C}^{(-1,\beta)}
the parameter
C
must verify

C>0\,

(43)

Then the
SJ orthogonal polynomials

\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
are defined for
\alpha=-1,~\beta=-1
as

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{0}(x):=1

(44)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{1}(x):=x+\gamma

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{n\geq 2}(x):=\binom{2n-2}{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{n-1}{n-k}(x-1)^{n-k}(x+1)^{k}\,

where
\gamma=(B-A)/(A+B)
, while for the parameters
\alpha=-1,~\beta>-1
one defines

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{0}^{(-1,\beta)}(x):=1

(45)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{1}^{(-1,\beta)}(x):=x-1

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{n\geq 2}^{(-1,\beta)}(x):=\binom{2n+\beta-1}{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{n+\beta}{n-k}(x-1)^{n-k}(x+1)^{k}\,

The polynomials
\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
form a complete orthogonal system of polynomial eigenfunctions of the Jacobi differential operator at parameters in the aforementioned parameter ranges.

The readers may have noticed that indeed the SJ polynomials
\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
as defined above coincide for
n\geq 2
with the
monic versions of the classical Jacobi polynomials
(i.e., a different normalization choice where in each polynomial of degree
n
the coefficient of
x^{n}
is normalized to be
1
).

There exists an alternative approach to the definition of the SJ polynomials due to Gurappa and Panigrahi
Gurappa et al. 1996
;
Gurappa and Panigrahi 1999
;
Gurappa et al. 2001
;
Gurappa et al. 2002
;
Shreecharan et al. 2004
;
Gurappa and Panigrahi 2004
, which will prove quintessential to our present paper. Quite remarkably, their technique of solving rather general differential equations by providing a constructive algorithm to determine polynomial eigenfunctions of the respective differential operators leads to a
unified construction
of classical
and
SJ monic orthogonal polynomials (albeit the authors do not appear to highlight this feature explicitly in their articles). Since there unfortunately appears to occur a systematic typographic error in loc. cit. in the equations pertaining to Jacobi polynomials, we will now present a careful re-derivation of the relevant results.

Specializing the general technique of Gurappa and Panigrahi to the Jacobi-type differential Equations (
31
)and (
34
), consider an eigenequation of the form (
37
), where we however from here on permit the parameter ranges
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq-1}
(i.e., explicitly including the special cases
\alpha=-1
and
\beta=-1
or
\beta>-1
discussed above). We will temporarily employ the notation
\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
for this extended parameter range (with
\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x):=P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
if
\alpha>-1
and
\beta>-1
). The first step in their technique consists in a certain splitting of the linear operators in the eigenequation (
37
) into a “diagonal” part
F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})
and an “non-diagonal” part
N(\hat{x},\partial_{x})
(where we chose to use the notation
N(\dotsc)
for the “non-diagonal” parts rather than the notation
P(\dotsc)
used in loc cit. in order to avoid any potential confusion of the operator
N(\dotsc)
with polynomials):

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{D}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{Jac}+n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\right)\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)

(46)

\displaystyle=(F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})+N(\hat{x},\partial_{x}))\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)\,

Here,
\hat{D}_{x}:=\hat{x}\partial_{x}
denotes the
Euler operator
, whence the
diagonal operator
with action on monomials

\hat{D}_{x}x^{n}=nx^{n}\,

(47)

Using the canonical commutation relation (
33
) to compute the auxiliary formula

\hat{D}_{x}^{2}=\hat{x}^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+\hat{D}_{x}\,

(48)

the concrete expressions for the operators
F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})
and
N(\hat{x},\partial_{x})
in (
46
) read (see Appendix
A
for the details of the derivation; compare
Gurappa and Panigrahi 2004
, which however contains a slight error in the respective formula for
F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})
):

\displaystyle F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})

\displaystyle:=-(\hat{D}_{x}-n)(\hat{D}_{x}+n+\alpha+\beta+1)

(49)

\displaystyle N(\hat{x},\partial_{x})

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{2}+(\beta-\alpha)\partial_{x}\,

The key idea of the technique of Gurappa and Panigrahi consists then in recognizing the following relationship:

F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})x^{n}=0\,

(50)

Moreover,
F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})
acts as a
diagonal
operator on any monomial, which allows to perform the following computations (with shorthand notations
\hat{F}_{n}\equiv F^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\hat{D}_{x})
, considered temporarily
as a symbol, and
\hat{N}\equiv N(\hat{x},\partial_{x})
):

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\hat{F}_{n}+\hat{N})\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)=\hat{F}_{n}(1+\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}\hat{N})\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)\,

(51)

where it is important to note that
\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}
and
\hat{N}

do not commute
, whence the ordering in the last equation is important. The final step of the technique then consists in recognizing the following expression for the eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)=c_{n}\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}\hat{N}}x^{n}=c_{n}\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}\left[\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}\hat{N}\right]^{m}x^{n}

(52a)

\displaystyle\quad=c_{n}\sum_{m\geq 0}\left[\tfrac{1}{(\hat{D}_{x}-n)(\hat{D}_{x}+n+\alpha+\beta+1)}\left(\partial_{x}^{2}+(\beta-\alpha)\partial_{x}\right)\right]^{m}x^{n}\,.

(52b)

here, the non-zero real parameter
c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is a normalization constant. The proof that the functions
\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
as defined in (
52
) indeed solve the eigenequation is straightforward (cf. e.g.,
Gurappa et al. 2002
):

\displaystyle(\hat{F}_{n}+\hat{N})\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\hat{F}_{n}(1+\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}\hat{N})c_{n}\frac{1}{\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}\hat{N}\right)}x^{n}

\displaystyle=c_{n}\hat{F}_{n}(1+\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}\hat{N})\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}\left[\tfrac{1}{\hat{F}_{n}}\hat{N}\right]^{m}x^{n}

\displaystyle=c_{n}\hat{F}_{n}x^{n}\overset{\eqref{eq:auxRel}}{=}0\,

Since in applications we will be interested in studying the
monic
SJ polynomials (i.e., with leading coefficient equal to
1
), we will set
c_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and summarize the findings of this section with the following proposition:

{Proposition}

The
(monic) SJ polynomials

\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)
(with
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq-1}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
) and the
classical monic Jacobi polynomials

\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
(for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>-1}
), defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}\left[\tfrac{1}{(\hat{D}_{x}-n)(\hat{D}_{x}+n+\alpha+\beta+1)}\left(\partial_{x}^{2}+(\beta-\alpha)\partial_{x}\right)\right]^{m}x^{n}\,

(53)

are
polynomial eigenfunctions
of the Jacobi-type differential operator
\hat{D}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}
with eigenvalues

\lambda_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}=-n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,

(54)

The degeneracies of the denominator in (
53
) are counteracted by the derivatives, so the statement can be made rigorous by completing this operator with identity on its kernel: for
\alpha+\beta+1\neq 0
we have that

ker((\hat{D}_{x}-n)(\hat{D}_{x}+n+\alpha+\beta+1))=\mathbb{C}\,x^{n}\,

and for
\alpha+\beta+1=0

ker((\hat{D}_{x}-n)(\hat{D}_{x}+n+\alpha+\beta+1))=\mathbb{C}\,x^{n}\uplus\mathbb{C}\,1\,

Completing the operator
by the identity map on its kernel renders
(\hat{D}_{x}-n)(\hat{D}_{x}+n+\alpha+\beta+1)

invertible. The readers may verify easily that
Id
can be replaced by any invertible endomorphism
N\to N
without modifying the final result of Equation (
53
).

The detailed analysis of the two cases (i)
\alpha=\beta=-1
, and (ii)
\alpha=-1
and
\beta>-1
will reveal that the corresponding families of SJ polynomials have in fact a rather distinct structure. Accordingly, they will be treated separately in the following.

4
SJ Polynomials for
\mathbold{\alpha=-1}
and
\mathbold{\beta>-1}

Compared to the SJ polynomials with parameters
\alpha=\beta=-1
as discussed in the next section, the case
\alpha=-1
and
\beta>-1
is somewhat more intricate from the viewpoint of the technique of Gurappa Panigrahi. The origin of this difficulty (compare also the results of Gurappa,
Panigrahi et al.
presented in
Gurappa 2007
) resides in the fact that the SJ polynomials for the case
\alpha=-1
and
\beta>-1
may not be expressed in the form of the exponential of a differential operator acting on a monomial (in contrast to the case
\alpha=\beta=-1
, see Proposition
5.2
). On the other hand, as evident from the explicit formula as given in (
45
) (see also
Behr et al. 2017
and references given therein for further details) for these polynomials, they coincide precisely with the
ordinary
(monic) Jacobi polynomials (while of course their orthogonality property is a different one). Therefore, while we postpone a more detailed analysis of the connection coefficients for these polynomials to future work, we briefly present a derivation of the EGF for this case, which is based on
Dattoli 2000
;
Dattoli and Sabia 2010
.

Note first that the results presented in (
45
) entail that the formula for
\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{1}(x)
may alternatively be obtained by setting
n=1
in the formulae for
\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n\geq 2}(x)
. Making use of the integral transform techniques introduced in Section
2
, we may rewrite the formula for
\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n\geq 1}(x)
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n\geq 1}(x)

\displaystyle=\binom{2n+\beta-1}{n}^{-1}\Gamma(n+\beta+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}\frac{(x-1)^{n-k}(x+1)^{k}}{\Gamma(n-k+1)\Gamma(\beta+k+1)}

(55)

\displaystyle=\binom{2n+\beta-1}{n}^{-1}\Gamma(n+\beta+1)(x-1)\;\cdot

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}(x-1)^{n-1-k}(x+1)^{k}v_{1}^{n-k+1}v_{2}^{\beta+k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{2n+\beta-1}{n}^{-1}\Gamma(n+\beta+1)(x-1)\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{\beta+1}\left(v_{1}(x-1)+v_{2}(x+1)\right)^{n-1}\right)\,

To formulate generating functions for these polynomials, it will prove convenient to work with a rescaled variant
P^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)
of the SJ polynomials
\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n}(x)
, defined as follows:

P^{(-1,\beta)}_{0}(x):=\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{0}(x)=1\,,\quad P^{(-1,\beta)}_{n\geq 1}(x):=\binom{2n+\beta-1}{n}\tfrac{1}{\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}\tilde{P}^{(-1,\beta)}_{n\geq 1}(x)\,

(56)

with these preparations, it is possible to compute the
1
-shifted EGF

\mathcal{G}_{P_{1,1}}^{(-1,\beta)}(\lambda;x)
of the rescaled SJ polynomials as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{P_{1,1}}^{(-1,\beta)}(\lambda;x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}P^{(-1,\beta)}_{n+1}(x)

(57a)

\displaystyle=(x-1)\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{\beta+1}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\left(v_{1}(x-1)+v_{2}(x+1)\right)^{n}\right)

(57c)

\displaystyle\overset{*}{=}(x-1)\left(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(v_{1}^{2}e^{\lambda v_{1}(x-1)}\right)\right)\left(\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(v_{2}^{\beta+1}e^{\lambda v_{2}(x+1)}\right)\right)

here, in the step marked

5.1
Euler Operators and Normal-Ordering Type Techniques

Denoting by
\hat{D}_{x}:=\hat{x}\partial_{x}
the Euler operator as before, it is straightforward to derive the following auxiliary normal-ordering type identities:

{Lemma}

For any entire function
f\equiv f(\hat{D}_{x})
in the Euler operator
\hat{D}_{x}
, and for any non-negative integers
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, it holds that

\displaystyle f(\hat{D}_{x})\hat{x}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}f(\hat{D}_{x}+p-q)

(60a)

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}f(\hat{D}_{x})

\displaystyle=f(\hat{D}_{x}-p+q)\hat{x}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}\,

(60b)

{proof}

Via straightforward computations, applying the above operator formulae to monomials
x^{n}
.

As a first application, these auxiliary relations allow us to refine the formula for the monic SJ polynomials
\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{n}(x)
as follows:

{Proposition}

The monic Sobolev-Jacobi polynomials
\tilde{P}_{n}^{(-1,-1)}(x)
are given in terms of the equivalent
exponential formula

\tilde{P}_{n}^{(-1,-1)}(x)=e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{1}{\hat{D}_{x}+n-1}\partial_{x}^{2}}x^{n}\,

(61)

Here, the operator in the denominator of (
61
) is understood as rendered invertible via a suitable completion of its kernel without modifying the final result (compare Proposition
3
).

{proof}

Starting from the original definition of
\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{n}(x)
as given in Proposition
3
, the claim follows from the following application of Lemma
5.1
:

\displaystyle\left[\tfrac{1}{(\hat{D}_{x}-n)(\hat{D}_{x}+n-1)}\partial_{x}^{2}\right]^{m}x^{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:ENO1}}{=}\left[\tfrac{1}{(\hat{D}_{x}+n-1)}\partial_{x}^{2}\right]^{m}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\tfrac{1}{\hat{D}_{x}-n-2j}\right)x^{n}=\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{m}\left[\tfrac{1}{(\hat{D}_{x}+n-1)}\partial_{x}^{2}\right]^{m}x^{n}\,

5.2
Exponential Generating Function

It will prove convenient to introduce the following bi-variate polynomials:

P_{n}(x,y):=e^{\mathbb{B}}(xy)^{n}\,

(62)

where the differential operator
\mathbb{B}
is defined as

\mathbb{B}:=\mathbf{b}_{0}\,\partial^{2}_{x}\,,\quad\mathbf{b}_{p}:=-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{1}{\hat{D}_{x}+\hat{D}_{y}+p-1}\quad(p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\,

(63)

As in previous formulae involving operators in denominators, we tacitly employ the same strategy of completing the kernels of these operators by any invertible function such as the identity function on their kernel, thereby rendering them invertible. This entails that in concrete applications, one must check that the resulting formulae are
independent
of these choices of completions.

Returning to the definition of the polynomials
P_{n}(x,y)

\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{n}(x)=\left(P_{n}(x,y)\right)\bigg|_{y\to 1}\,

To compute the EGF of the SJ polynomials

—in practice, the fact that the differential operator

does not explicitly depend on the degree

{Proposition}

The EGF
\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)
of the polynomials

\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}P_{n}(x,y)=e^{\mathbb{B}}e^{\lambda xy}

(64)

has the explicit form

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\tfrac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)^{m}\tfrac{\Gamma(m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(2m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}

(65a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\;{}_{1}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{n-\tfrac{1}{2}}{\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{n}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}};-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{16}\right]}\,

(65b)

which is one of the key results of this paper.

{proof}

See Appendix
B
.

5.3
Series Transform Techniques and Sobolev-Jacobi Polynomials

Upon closer inspection, the formula for the EGF of the SJ polynomials as presented in (
65

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
reveals a hidden structure in
\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\tfrac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)^{m}\tfrac{\Gamma(m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(2m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}

(66a)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}\tfrac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)^{m}u^{m+n-\tfrac{1}{2}}v^{2m+n-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)

(66b)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{uv}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\lambda uvxy)^{n}}{n!}\right)\left(\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{1}{m!}\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}uv^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)^{n}\right)\right)\,

(66c)

whence

\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{uv}}e^{\lambda(uvxy)+\lambda^{2}\left(-\tfrac{uv^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)}\right)\,

(67)

To proceed, let us recall (see e.g.,
Dattoli et al. 2000
) the definition of the bi-variate Hermite polynomials
H_{r}(x,z)
,

H_{r}(x,z)=r!\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\tfrac{r}{2}\rfloor}\tfrac{x^{r-2m}z^{m}}{m!(r-2m)!}\,

(68)

as well as their well-known
EGF

\mathcal{H}(\lambda;x,z):=\sum_{r\geq 0}\tfrac{\nu^{r}}{r!}H_{r}(x,z)=e^{\nu x+\nu^{2}z}\,

(69)

which may be derived from the important operational identity
Dattoli et al. 1997
;
Dattoli et al. 1999

H_{n}(x,z)=e^{z\partial^{2}_{x}}x^{n}\,

(70)

Here, to avoid any potential notational confusion, we have denoted the second variable of the Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(x,z)
by
z
(rather than
y
), since in some of our formulae the notation
y
is used in a different context (i.e., for defining the SJ polynomials).

Another useful set of identities is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}H_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}H_{n}(x,y)=(n)_{2}H_{n-2}(x,y)\,

(71)

with these preparations, we find the following result with far-reaching consequences for the rest of this paper, and which hints at the potential of our novel series transformation methods:

{Theorem}

The EGF
\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x)
is an integral-based series transform of the generating function
\mathcal{H}(\lambda;x,z)

\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{uv}}e^{(\lambda uv)x+(\lambda uv)^{2}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4u}\right)}\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{uv}}\mathcal{H}\left(\lambda uv;x,-\tfrac{1}{4u}\right)\right)\,

(72)

This entails the following explicit formula for the monic SJ polynomials

\displaystyle P_{n}(x)\equiv\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left((uv)^{n-\tfrac{1}{2}}H_{n}(x,-\tfrac{1}{4u})\right)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{uv}}e^{-\tfrac{1}{4u}\partial^{2}_{x}}(xuv)^{n}\right)\,

(73)

5.4
Aside: General Operational Definitions for Calculating Lacunary Generating Functions

Suppose we were given the EGF
G(\lambda;x)
of some set of polynomials
p_{n}(x)
(where
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and where we assume
degree(p_{n}(x))=n
). We will be particularly interested in the relationship between the following two forms of presenting
G(\lambda;x)
as a formal power series:

\displaystyle G(\lambda;x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}p_{n}(x)

(74a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}x^{n}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{m+n}}{n!}g_{n,m}\,

(74b)

In certain situations, one might be interested in so-called
lacunary generating functions
:

{Definition}

Given an EGF
G(\lambda;x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}p_{n}(x)
, the
K
-tuple
L
-shifted lacunary generating function

G_{K,L}(\lambda;x)
of the polynomials
p_{n}(x)
is defined as (for
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
)

G_{K,L}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}p_{K\cdot n+L}(x)\,

(75)

Thus, in particular
G_{1,0}(\lambda;x)=G(\lambda;x)
.

The difficulty in explicit calculations of lacunary generating functions resides in finding the explicit coefficient form as indicated in (
74b
). The series transform methods presented thus far allow to cast these computations into a concise operational form:

{Lemma}

Let
G(\lambda;x)
be an EGF,
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
integer parameters and

\eta_{K}:=e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbf{i}}{K}}

(76)

a
K
-th root of unity (
(\eta_{K})^{K}=1
). Then the lacunary generating function
G_{K,L}(\lambda;x)
is given by

\displaystyle G_{K,L}(\lambda;x)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}\circ\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{L}G(\lambda;x)

(77)

here, the
lacunary dilatation operator

\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}
is defined as acting on a generic formal power series
F(\lambda;x)
as

\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}F(\lambda;x):=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{uv}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}F\left(\eta_{K}^{j}u(v\lambda)^{\frac{1}{K}};x\right)\right)\,

(78)

Moreover, the following relations hold:

\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{L}\circ\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}=\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}\circ\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{K\cdot L}\,

(79)

{proof}

The first (well-known) part of the claim follows from

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{L}G(\lambda;x)=\sum_{n\geq L}\frac{\lambda^{n-L}}{(n-L)!}p_{n}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}p_{n+L}(x)=G_{1,L}(\lambda;x)

(80)

To prove the formula for the action of
\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}
on a generic formal power series
F(\lambda;x)
, note first that for
k>1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}(\eta_{K}^{n})^{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}1\quad&\text{if }n\ \mathrm{mod}\ K=0\\
\frac{1}{K}\frac{\eta_{K}^{K}-1}{\eta_{K}-1}&\text{else}\end{cases}=\delta_{(n\ \mathrm{mod}\ K),0}\,

(81)

and that by definition for all
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(uv(uv^{\frac{1}{K}})^{n}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{K}+1)}\,

(82)

Whence for an EGF
G(\lambda;x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}p_{n}(x)
, the application of
\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}
results in

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}G(\lambda;x)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}p_{n}(x)u^{n+1}v^{\frac{1}{K}+1}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\left(\eta_{K}^{n}\right)^{j}\right)

(83)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}p_{n}(x)\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{K}+1)}\delta_{(n\ \mathrm{mod}\ K),0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{r}}{r!}p_{r\cdot K}(x)=\mathcal{G}_{K,0}(\lambda;x)\,

Finally, the relations presented (
79
) follow directly from the definitions.

The readers will notice the similarity of the definition given in (
78
) with the technique of so-called
multisection of series

Wilf 2005
. However, the presence of the operator
\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}
enriches this technique in a substantial way. For comparison, we refer the interested readers to our recent work
Behr et al. 2018
for an alternative, but equivalent definition of the lacunary dilatation operator
\mathop{\mathbb{L}_{(K)}}
.

5.5

While it may not be immediately evident, the true merit of the definition of the lacunary dilatation and shift operators

and

is revealed upon applying them to computations of the EGF forms described in (

(with

and

for the interested readers’ convenience.

For the present paper, we would like to focus on the interaction of series transforms with generating function formulae. More concretely, we will present an immediate consequence of this general theorem for the lacunary generating functions of the Hermite polynomials in terms of the corresponding generating functions of the SJ polynomials. As a tool that we will frequently invoke in the following considerations, let us recall the

(

\Gamma(nz)=n^{nz-\frac{1}{2}}(2\pi)^{\frac{(1-n)}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\Gamma\left(z+\frac{j}{n}\right)\,

More precisely, we will make use of the following variant of this formula: for
n(s+x)

and

\Gamma(n(s+x))=\left(n^{s\cdot n}\right)\Gamma(nx)\;\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(x+\frac{j}{n}\right)_{s}\,

(85)

with the Pochhammer symbol

(a)_{b}:=\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)}\,

To compute the lacunary generating functions

5.3
to the results presented in Theorem
C

\mathcal{H}_{K,L}(\lambda;x,z)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K,L}(\lambda;x,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}x^{r}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{r+m}}{(r+m)!}\;h^{(K,L)}_{r,m}(z)

(86)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{K,L}(\lambda;x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}x^{r}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{r+m}}{(r+m)!}\;g^{(K,L)}_{r,m}\,

we find the following precise relationship between the generating functions
\mathcal{H}_{K,L}(\lambda;x)
and

{Corollary}

For all

and

of the SJ polynomials

\mathcal{G}_{K,L}(\lambda;x)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left((uv)^{L-\frac{1}{2}}\;\mathcal{H}_{K,L}\left(\lambda(uv)^{K};x,-\tfrac{1}{4u}\right)\right)\,

(87)

g^{(K,L)}_{r,m}=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left((uv)^{K\cdot(r+m)+L-\frac{1}{2}}\;h^{(K,L)}_{r,m}\left(-\tfrac{1}{4u}\right)\right)\,

(88)

{proof}

The proof follows from inserting the expression given for
P_{n}(X)

73

Finally, we will need the following auxiliary statement (throughout which we will consider

{Lemma}
[“Pochhammer proliferation”]

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\seq{a_j}{1\leq j\leq p}}{\seq{b_{\ell}}{1\leq\ell\leq q}};zu^{r}v^{s}\right]}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\;{}_{p+r}F_{q+s}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\left(\seq{a_j}{1\leq j\leq p},

{proof}

The proof follows via a straightforward application of (

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\seq{a_j}{1\leq j\leq p}}{\seq{b_{\ell}}{1\leq\ell\leq q}};zu^{r}v^{s}\right]}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:GMFC}}{=}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{z^{m}}{m!}\;\frac{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}(a_{j})_{m}\right)}{\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}(b_{q})_{m}\right)}\;\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\;\frac{(r^{r})^{m}\;\prod_{k=0}^{r-1}\left(\tfrac{\alpha+k}{r}\right)_{m}}{(s^{s})^{m}\;\prod_{t=0}^{s-1}\left(\tfrac{\beta+t}{s}\right)_{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\;{}_{p+r}F_{q+s}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\left(\seq{a_j}{1\leq j\leq p},

Connection Coefficients for SJ Polynomials for the Case
\alpha=\beta=-1

Consider the so-called
connection coefficients

A_{M,n}
of the SJ polynomials
\tilde{P}^{(-1,-1)}_{n}(x)
(see

for an explicit solution in terms of integral computations), which are defined for non-negative integers

and

as

(98)

In this section, we will highlight some aspects of computations related to these coefficients in terms of operational techniques. To this end, we first derive the connection coefficients for the two-variable Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(x,y)

Dattoli 2000
:

\displaystyle H_{M}(x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{y\partial^{2}_{x}}x^{M}

(99)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad x^{M}

\displaystyle=e^{-y\partial^{2}_{x}}H_{M}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor\tfrac{M}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-y)^{n}}{n!}\partial_{x}^{2n}H_{M}(x,y)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:Heqs}}{=}\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor\tfrac{M}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-y)^{n}M!}{n!(M-2n)!}H_{M-2n}(x,y)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{M,n}(y)H_{n}(x,y)\,

In consequence, the generating function for the connection coefficients
a_{M,n}(y)
of the polynomials
H_{n}(x,y)
reads

\displaystyle A_{H}(\lambda,\mu;y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{M,n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{M}}{M!}\mu^{n}a_{M,n}(y)

(100)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{M}}{M!}\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor\tfrac{M}{2}\rfloor}\frac{M!\mu^{M-2n}(-y)^{n}}{n!(M-2n)!}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:BH}}{=}\sum_{M\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{M}}{M!}H_{M}(\mu,-y)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:EGFhp}}{=}e^{\lambda\mu-\lambda^{2}y}\,

For later convenience, let us also consider a consistency check for this result involving the EGF

\displaystyle B_{H}(\lambda,\mu;y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}H_{n}(\mu,y)\equiv\sum_{n,M\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\mu^{M}b_{n,M}(y)=e^{\lambda\mu+\lambda^{2}y}\,

\tfrac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}w\;e^{-|w|^{2}}w^{r}\bar{w}^{s}=r!\delta_{r,s}\,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\pi}\int d^{w}\;e^{-|W|^{2}}A_{H}(\alpha,w;y)B_{H}(\bar{w},\beta;y)=e^{\alpha\beta}

and

\displaystyle x^{M}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{M,n}(y)b_{n,L}(y)=\delta_{M,L}\,

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have applied operational methods
to the study of SJ polynomials. We have implemented, starting from first principles, a variant of multi-variate umbral calculus in the form of a certain integral transform technique based on the notions of generalized monomials and on the elementary integrals defining the special functions
\Gamma(z)
and
1/\Gamma(z)
(cf. Section
2
). Our approach provides a bridge between the plethora of results and specialized techniques known from the umbral calculus literature and more conventional mathematical techniques of elementary algebra and calculus, which we hope will allow a wider audience to profit from this fruitful approach. To apply our technique to the study of SJ polynomials, we adapted a remarkable method invented by Gurappa and Panigrahi
Gurappa et al. 1996
;
Gurappa and Panigrahi 1999
;
Gurappa et al. 2001
;
Gurappa et al. 2002
;
Shreecharan et al. 2004
;
Gurappa and Panigrahi 2004
(cf. Section
3
) as well as a certain generalized normal-ordering technique (for expressions involving Euler operators and fractions thereof, cf. Section
5.1
). We have achieved not only a deeper understanding of the Sobolev-Jacobi polynomials for parameters
\alpha=\beta=-1
as
umbral images
of the two-variable Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(x,y)
(see Theorem
5.3
), but were in consequence in particular able to derive explicit formulae for
all

K
-tuple
L
-shifted lacunary exponential generating functions (see Theorem
5.5
) for these SJ polynomials (based on a previous work
Behr et al. 2018
on lacunary EGFs for the bi-variate Hermite polynomials). A crucial step in this derivation consisted of a technical Lemma called “Pochhammer Proliferation Lemma” (See Lemma
5.5
), which highlights the strengths of our integral transform techniques and fully explains the explicit structure of the lacunary EGFs as formal power series over certain generalized hypergeometric functions. To the best of our knowledge, these results have not been known before in the literature. Additional results include an explicit formula for the connection coefficients of the aforementioned family of SJ polynomials (cf. Section
5.6
), a derivation of the EGFs for the remaining family of SJ polynomials (cf. Section
4
), and finally several auxiliary results and examples (cf. Section
2
) that illustrate our novel umbral calculus-type integral transform techniques, and which highlight a certain resemblance with earlier ideas as expressed in
Dattoli et al. 2017
;
Licciardi 2018
(referred to therein as the “principle” of permanence of formal properties). The results we have obtained appear to be promising and seem to offer new and interesting developments within the framework of the theory of special functions and of the relevant applications. A forthcoming investigation, along the lines suggested here, will be devoted to further progress towards a unified theory of Legendre-like polynomials.

Appendix B
Proof of the SJ Generating Function Formulae

Recalling the definitions

P_{n}(x,y):=e^{\mathbb{B}}(xy)^{n}\,,\quad\mathbb{B}:=\mathbf{b}_{0}\partial^{2}_{x}\,,\quad\mathbf{b}_{p}:=-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{1}{\hat{D}_{x}+\hat{D}_{y}+p-1}\,

(110)

we first prove the following auxiliary set of formulae:

{Lemma}

The differential operators
\mathbf{b}_{m}
(
m\in\mathbb{Z}
) fulfill the following relations (for
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{m}\hat{y}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}

\displaystyle=\hat{y}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}\mathbf{b}_{m+p-q}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\hat{y}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}\mathbf{b}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{m-p+q}\hat{y}^{p}\partial_{x}^{q}\,

(111)

and therefore, in particular for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\mathbf{b}_{m}\hat{y}^{p}\partial_{x}^{p}=\hat{y}^{p}\partial_{x}^{p}\mathbf{b}_{m}\,

(112)

{proof}

Via a direct computation, employing the multi-variate analogues of the relations (
60
) presented in Lemma
5.1
.

Next, we prove by induction the following formula:

\mathbb{B}^{m}e^{\lambda xy}=(\lambda\hat{y})^{2m}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_{2(m+j)}\right)e^{\lambda xy}\,

(113)

Starting the proof with verifying the case
m=1
(making use of the auxiliary relations (
111
)),

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}e^{\lambda xy}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{0}\partial_{x}^{2}e^{\lambda xy}=\lambda^{2}\mathbf{b}_{0}\hat{y}^{2}e^{\lambda xy}\overset{\eqref{eq:auxBB}}{=}(\lambda\hat{y})^{2}\mathbf{b}_{2}e^{\lambda xy}\,

the induction step
m\to m+1
may be verified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\left(\mathbb{B}^{m}e^{\lambda xy}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{0}\partial_{x}^{2}(\lambda\hat{y})^{2m}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_{2(m+j)}\right)e^{\lambda xy}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:BBcomm}}{=}\lambda^{2m}\mathbf{b}_{0}\hat{y}^{2(m-1)}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_{2(m+j)}\right)\hat{y}^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}e^{\lambda xy}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2(m+1)}\hat{y}^{2(m-1)}\mathbf{b}_{2(m-1)}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_{2(m+j)}\right)\hat{y}^{4}e^{\lambda xy}

\displaystyle=(\lambda\hat{y})^{2(m+1)}\mathbf{b}_{2(m+1)}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_{2((m+1)+j+1)}\right)e^{\lambda xy}

\displaystyle=(\lambda\hat{y})^{2(m+1)}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{(m+1)-1}\mathbf{b}_{2((m+1)+j)}\right)e^{\lambda xy}\,

which proves the induction hypothesis.

Assembling all the auxiliary formulae, it is then straightforward to prove the explicit formula (
65
) for the EGF
\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)
of the polynomials
P_{n}(x,y)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{\mathbb{B}}e^{\lambda xy}=e^{\lambda xy}+\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{(\lambda^{2}\hat{y}^{2})^{m}}{m!}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_{2(m+j)}\right)e^{\lambda xy}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\left[1+\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{(\lambda^{2}\hat{y}^{2})^{m}}{m!}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_{2(m+j)}\right)\right](xy)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\left[1+\sum_{m\geq 1}\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{2}\right)^{m}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\tfrac{1}{2(n+m+j)-1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\left[1+\sum_{m\geq 1}\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)^{m}\tfrac{\Gamma(m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(2m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)^{m}\tfrac{\Gamma(m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(2m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})}\,

Finally, invoking the auxiliary formula (
85
) to compute

\Gamma(2m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})=4^{m}\Gamma(n-\tfrac{1}{2})\left(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)_{m}\left(\tfrac{n}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)_{m}

(114)

and since

\Gamma(m+n-\tfrac{1}{2})=\left(n-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{m}\;\Gamma(n-\tfrac{1}{2})\,

(115)

we arrive at the final form for
\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\lambda;x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\lambda xy)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{m!}\left(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}y^{2}}{4}\right)^{m}\frac{\left(n-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{m}\;\Gamma(n-\tfrac{1}{2})}{4^{m}\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2})\left(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)_{m}\left(n+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)_{m}}

(116)

Appendix D

The generating functions
A(\lambda,\mu)
and
B(\lambda,\mu)
verify

\frac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}w\;e^{-|w|^{2}}A(\alpha,w)B(\bar{w},\beta)=e^{\alpha\beta}\,

{proof}

Writing out the definitions of
A(\alpha,w)
and
B(\bar{w},\beta)
explicitly as

\displaystyle A(\alpha,w)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\sqrt{u_{1}v_{1}}\;e^{\alpha wu_{1}v_{1}+\alpha^{2}\tfrac{v_{1}}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle B(\bar{w},\beta)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{u_{2}v_{2}}}e^{\beta\bar{w}u_{2}v_{2}+\bar{w}^{2}\left(-\tfrac{u_{2}v_{2}^{2}}{4}\right)}\right)\,

taking care in particular that the respective integration variables
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
are distinct. Inserting these formulae into the integral in question, we obtain:

\frac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}w\;e^{-|w|^{2}}A(\alpha,w)B(\bar{w},\beta)=\hat{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\left(e^{\alpha^{2}\tfrac{v_{1}}{4}}\;\sqrt{\frac{u_{1}v_{1}}{u_{2}v_{2}}}\;\frac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}w\;e^{-|w|^{2}}\;e^{w\alpha u_{1}v_{1}+\bar{w}\beta u_{2}v_{2}+\bar{w}^{2}\left(-\tfrac{u_{2}v_{2}^{2}}{4}\right)}\right)\,

(122)

Making use of the auxiliary integral formula (which itself is a straightforward consequence of the formula for the exponential generating function (
101
) of the Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(x,z)
),

\tfrac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}\;e^{-|w|^{2}}e^{w\mu+\bar{w}\nu+\bar{w}^{2}\rho}=e^{\mu\nu+\mu^{2}\rho}\,

(123)

the proof of the claim thus reduces to verifying the following equation:
