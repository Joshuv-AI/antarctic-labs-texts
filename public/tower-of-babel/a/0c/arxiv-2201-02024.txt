Fast Toeplitz eigenvalue computations, joining interpolation-extrapolation matrix-less algorithms and simple-loop theory
M. Bogoya, S.E. Ekström, S. Serra–Capizzano

Abstract
Under appropriate technical assumptions, the simple-loop theory allows to deduce various types of asymptotic expansions for the eigenvalues of Toeplitz matrices generated by a function f f . Independently and under the milder hypothesis that f f is even and monotonic over [ 0 , π ] [0,\pi] , matrix-less algorithms have been developed for the fast eigenvalue computation of large Toeplitz matrices, within a linear complexity in the matrix order: behind the high efficiency of such algorithms there are the expansions predicted by the simple-loop theory, combined with the extrapolation idea. Here we focus our attention on a change of variable, followed by the asymptotic expansion of the new variable, and we adapt the matrix-less algorithm to the considered new setting. Numerical experiments show a higher precision (till machine precision) and the same linear computation cost, when compared with the matrix-less procedures already presented in the relevant literature. Among the advantages, we concisely mention the following: a) when the coefficients of the simple-loop function are analytically known, the algorithm computes them perfectly; b) while the proposed algorithm is better or at worst comparable to the previous ones for computing the inner eigenvalues, it is extremely better for the computation of the extreme eigenvalues. Keywords : Eigenvalue computation, Toeplitz matrix, Matrix-less method, Asymptotic expansion. MSC Classes : Primary 15B05, 65F15, 65D05, 47B35. Secondary 15A18, 47A38.

•

2
Preliminaries and Tools

For a real or complex valued function
f
in
L^{1}[-\pi,\pi]
, let
\mathfrak{a}_{j}(f)
be its
j
th Fourier coefficient, i.e.

\mathfrak{a}_{j}(f)\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}j\theta}\dif\theta,\quad j\in\mathbb{Z},

and consider the sequence
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of the
n\times n
Toeplitz matrices defined by
T_{n}(f)\equiv(\mathfrak{a}_{j-k}(f))_{j,k=0}^{n-1}
. The function
f
is customarily referred to as the generating function of this sequence.

As a second step, we introduce some notations and definitions concerning general sequences of matrices. For any function
F
defined on the complex field and
for any matrix
A_{n}
of size
d_{n}
, by the symbol
\Sigma_{\lambda}(F,A_{n})
, we denote the mean

\Sigma_{\lambda}(F,A_{n})=\frac{1}{d_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}F[\lambda_{j}(A_{n})],

while by the symbol
\Sigma_{\sigma}(F,A_{n})
, we denote the mean

\Sigma_{\sigma}(F,A_{n})=\frac{1}{d_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}F[\sigma_{j}(A_{n})].

Definition 2.1
.

Given a sequence
\{A_{n}\}
of matrices of size
d_{n}
with
d_{n}<d_{n+1}
and given a Lebesgue-measurable function
\psi
defined over a measurable set
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\nu}
,
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, of finite and positive Lebesgue measure
\mu(K)
, we say that
\{A_{n}\}
is distributed as
(\psi,K)
in the sense of the eigenvalues if for any continuous function
F
with bounded support, the following limit relation holds

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Sigma_{\lambda}(F,A_{n})=\frac{1}{\mu(K)}\int_{K}F(\psi)\,\dif\mu.

In this case, we write in short
\{A_{n}\}\sim_{\lambda}(\psi,K)
. Furthermore we say that
\{A_{n}\}
is distributed as
(\psi,K)
in the sense of the singular values if for any continuous function
F
with bounded support, the following limit relation holds

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Sigma_{\sigma}(F,A_{n})=\frac{1}{\mu(K)}\int_{K}F(|\psi|)\,\dif\mu.

In this case, we write in short
\{A_{n}\}\sim_{\sigma}(\psi,K)
, which is equivalent to
\{A_{n}^{*}A_{n}\}\sim_{\lambda}(|\psi|^{2},K)
.

In Remark
2.1
we provide an informal meaning of the notion of eigenvalue distribution. For the singular value distribution similar statements can be written.

Remark 2.1
.

The informal meaning behind the above definition is the following. If
\psi
is continuous,
n
is large enough, and

\Big\{{\bf x}_{j}^{(d_{n})},\ j=1,\ldots,d_{n}\Big\}

is an equispaced grid on
K
, then a suitable ordering
\lambda_{j}(A_{n})
,
j=1,\ldots,d_{n}
, of the eigenvalues of
A_{n}
is such that the pairs
\big\{\big({\bf x}_{j}^{(d_{n})},\lambda_{j}(A_{n})\big),\ j=1,\ldots,d_{n}\big\}
reconstruct approximately the hypersurface

\big\{({\bf x},\psi({\bf x})),\ {\bf x}\in K\big\}.

In other words, the spectrum of
A_{n}
‘behaves’ like a uniform sampling of
\psi
over
K
.
For instance, if

\nu=1
,
d_{n}=n
, and
K=[a,b]
, then the eigenvalues of
A_{n}
are approximately equal to
\psi\big(a+\frac{j}{n}(b-a)\big)
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, for
n
large enough and up to at most
o(n)
outliers.
Analogously, if we have
\nu=2
,
d_{n}=n^{2}
, and
K=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times[a_{2},b_{2}]
, then the eigenvalues of the matrix
A_{n}
are approximately equal to
\psi\big(a_{1}+\frac{j}{n}(b_{1}-a_{1}),a_{2}+\frac{k}{n}(b_{2}-a_{2})\big)
,
j,k=1,\ldots,n
, for
n
large enough and up to at most
o(n^{2})
outliers.

The asymptotic distribution of eigenvalues and singular values of Toeplitz matrix sequences has been studied deeply and continuously in the last century (for example see
[
2
,
3
,
10
,
16
,
17
]
and references therein).
The starting point of this theory, which contains many extensions and other results, is a famous theorem of Szegő
[
18
]
, which we report in
the version due to Tyrtyshnikov and Zamarashkin
[
25
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

If
f
is integrable over
Q\equiv(-\pi,\pi)
, and if
\{T_{n}(f)\}
is the sequence of
Toeplitz matrices generated by
f
, then

\{T_{n}(f)\}\sim_{\sigma}(f,Q).

Moreover, if
f
is also real-valued, then each matrix
T_{n}(f)

is Hermitian and

\{T_{n}(f)\}\sim_{\lambda}(f,Q).

Furthermore, strong localization results are known in the case where the generating function is real-valued, as stated in
[
20
, Th.2.2]
which we partly report below.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
f
is integrable and real-valued almost everywhere over
Q\equiv(-\pi,\pi)
,
m
is the essential infimum of
f
,
M
is the essential supremum of
f
, and if
\{T_{n}(f)\}
is the sequence of Toeplitz matrices generated by
f
, then

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))\in(m,M)

for every
j=1,\ldots,n
, for every positive integer
n
, under the assumption that
m<M
.
Moreover, if
m=M
then the generating function
f
is constant almost everywhere and trivially we conclude that
T_{n}(f)
coincides with
m
times the identity matrix.

2.1
The Simple-Loop case

For
\alpha>0
, the well-known weighted Wiener algebra
W^{\alpha}
is the collection of all functions
f\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
whose Fourier coefficients satisfy

\|f\|_{\alpha}\equiv\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}|\mathfrak{a}_{j}(f)|(|j|+1)^{\alpha}<\infty.

It is easy to see that if
f\in W^{\alpha}
then
f\in C^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}[-\pi,\pi]
, hence the constant
\alpha
is measuring the smoothness of the symbol
f
. In what follows we extend every symbol
f
to the whole real line in the natural way turning it into a
2\pi
-periodic function, and we denote this extension by
f
as well.

The simple-loop class, denoted by
\SL^{\alpha}
, consists in the collection of all the real-valued symbols in
W^{\alpha}
tracing out a simple-loop over the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
with the following properties:

1.

the range of
f
is a segment
[0,\mu]
with
\mu>0
;

2.

f(0)=f(2\pi)=0
,
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=f^{\prime\prime}(2\pi)>0
;

3.

there is a unique
\theta_{0}\in[0,2\pi]
such that
f(\theta_{0})=\mu
,
f^{\prime}(\theta)>0
for
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
and
f^{\prime}(\theta)<0
for
\theta\in(\theta_{0},2\pi)
.

Note that if
f\in\SL^{\alpha}
then
f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(2\pi)=0
, and that
\theta_{0}=\pi
for every even symbol, i.e. a symbol satisfying
f(\theta)=f(-\theta)
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Consider an even symbol
f\in\SL^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>2
. For a
n\times n
matrix
A
let
\lambda_{j}(A)

(j=1,\ldots,n)
be its eigenvalues. In the works
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, for example, the authors state that

1.

the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(f)
are all distinct, i.e.

\lambda_{1}(T_{n}(f))<\lambda_{2}(T_{n}(f))<\cdots<\lambda_{n}(T_{n}(f));

2.

the numbers
s_{j,n}\equiv\big[f\big|_{[0,\pi]}\big]^{-1}(\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f)))

(j=1,\ldots,n)
satisfy

(n+1)s_{j,n}+\eta(s_{j,n})=\pi j+E_{j,n,\alpha},

(2.1)

where
E_{j,n,\alpha}
is an error term satisfying certain bounding condition, and
\eta
is a function depending only on
f
with certain smoothness depending on
\alpha
.

3.

the previous equation (
2.1
) has exactly one solution
s_{j,n}\in[0,\pi]
for each
j=1,\ldots,n
.

In the bulk of the so-called
simple-loop method
, the authors use the Banach fixed-point theorem to iterate over (
2.1
) and solve it for
s_{j,n}
, obtaining an expansion of the kind (see
[
5
, Th.2.2]
for example)

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))=f(s_{j,n}),\qquad s_{j,n}=\theta_{j,n}+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}r_{k}(\theta_{j,n})h^{k}+E_{j,n,\alpha},

(2.2)

where

•

the numbers
s_{j,n}
are arranged in nondecreasing order;

•

h\equiv\frac{1}{n+1}
and
\theta_{j,n}\equiv\pi jh
;

•

the coefficients
r_{k}
depend only on
f
and can be found explicitly, for example

r_{1}=-\eta
,

r_{3}=-\eta(\eta^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\eta^{\prime\prime}
,

r_{2}=\eta\eta^{\prime}
,

r_{4}=\eta(\eta^{\prime})^{3}+\frac{3}{2}\eta^{2}\eta^{\prime}\eta^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{6}\eta^{3}\eta^{\prime\prime\prime}
.

•

E_{j,n,\alpha}=O(h^{\alpha})
is the remainder (error) term, which satisfies the bounding
|E_{j,n,\alpha}|\leqslant\kappa_{\alpha}h^{\alpha}
for some constant
\kappa_{\alpha}
depending only on
\alpha
and
f
.

Using the expansion (
2.2
) and the smoothness of the symbol
f
, the authors apply
f
to both sides obtaining an expansion of the kind

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))=f(\theta_{j,n})+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}c_{k}(\theta_{j,n})h^{k}+E_{j,n,\alpha},

(2.3)

with similar characteristics but where the coefficients
c_{k}
involve the symbol and its derivatives, for instance
c_{1}=-f^{\prime}\eta
and
c_{2}=f^{\prime}\eta\eta^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}\eta^{2}
. The previous works
[
8
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
used (
2.3
) as the basic expansion. We noticed that (
2.3
) is absorbing all the almost non-increasining consequences of
f
at
\{0,\pi\}
and all the related troubles that the derivatives of
f
can produce. Hence, we decided to work with (
2.2
) instead.

Indeed, in the light of Theorem
2.2
, since
f
is even and real-valued, we find
\{T_{n}(f)\}\sim_{\lambda}(f,[0,\pi])
. Consequently we deduce

\{{\diag}_{j=1,\ldots,n}(s_{j,n})\}\sim_{\lambda}({\rm id},[0,\pi]),\ \ \ \ {\rm id}(\theta)\equiv\theta,

with
s_{j,n}\in(0,\pi)
, by virtue of Theorem
2.3
.
Notice that the function id
(\cdot)
is very basic, and, as already claimed, when compared with the study and the proposals in
[
5
,
6
,
9
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, the troubles that the derivatives of
f
produce are completely removed: these nice features are clearly evident by looking at the high precision of the numerical computations, reported in Section
4
, containing the numerical experiments.

3
The Algorithm

Our algorithm is based on the expansion (
2.2
) and is an evolution of the algorithms proposed in
[
8
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. As in the mentioned works it is suited for parallel implementation and can be called matrix-less since it does not require to calculate or even, to store the matrix entries. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
h\equiv\frac{1}{n+1}
and
\theta_{j,n}\equiv\pi jh
, thus the collection
\{\theta_{j,n}\}_{j=0}^{n+1}
is a regular grid for the interval
[0,\pi]
with step size
\pi h
. We will use similar notations for
n
and
h
, for example,
h_{k}
means
\frac{1}{n_{k}+1}
, and so on. We assume that

•

the symbol
f
is even and real-valued, strictly increasing in the interval
[0,\pi]
, and
f(0)=0
;

•

n_{1}
and
\alpha
are fixed natural numbers and
n\gg n_{1}
;

•

for
k=1,\ldots,\alpha
let
n_{k}\equiv 2^{k-1}(n_{1}+1)-1
;

•

for
j_{1}=1,\ldots,n
and
k=1,\ldots,\alpha
, let
j_{k}\equiv 2^{k-1}j_{1}
.

Note that
j_{k}
depends on
j_{1}
, and similarly,
n_{k}
depends on
n_{1}
, but for notation simplicity, we suppressed those dependencies.
The indexes
j_{k}
and the matrix sizes
n_{k}
were calculated in such a way that

\theta_{j_{1},n_{1}}=\theta_{j_{2},n_{2}}=\cdots=\theta_{j_{\alpha},n_{\alpha}},

which is the key idea of the following extrapolation phase, see Figure
1
.
We also want to emphasize that
f
is not necessarily simple-loop.

\theta_{2,n_{1}}
\theta_{n_{1}-1,n_{1}}
\theta_{n_{1},n_{1}}

n_{2}

. In the precomputing phase we will need to calculate the eigenvalues of

for

As in
[

, our algorithm is designed to calculate eigenvalues for “big” matrix sizes

with respect to
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\alpha}
, meaning that, from a computational viewpoint, the calculation of the eigenvalues of

is hard while for

can be easily done with any standard eigensolver (i.e.
Eigenvalues

). But our proposal is able to reach machine precision accuracy easily. The algorithm has two phases, the first one involves an extrapolation procedure, and the second one consists in a local interpolation technique.
As a precomputing phase we need to calculate the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(f)
for
n=n_{1},\ldots,n_{\alpha}
.

Extrapolation
For each fixed
j_{1}=1,\ldots,n_{1}
let
\sigma_{j_{1}}\equiv\theta_{j_{1},n_{1}}=\cdots=\theta_{j_{\alpha},n_{\alpha}}
(see the orange dots in Figure
1
), and apply
\alpha
times the expansion (
2.2
) obtaining

\displaystyle\lambda_{j_{1}}(T_{n_{1}}(f))-f(\sigma_{j_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{1}+r_{2}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{1}^{2}+\cdots+r_{\alpha}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{1}^{\alpha}+E_{j_{1},n_{1},\alpha},

\displaystyle\lambda_{j_{2}}(T_{n_{2}}(f))-f(\sigma_{j_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{2}+r_{2}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{2}^{2}+\cdots+r_{\alpha}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{2}^{\alpha}+E_{j_{2},n_{2},\alpha},

\displaystyle\lambda_{j_{\alpha}}(T_{n_{\alpha}}(f))-f(\sigma_{j_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{\alpha}+r_{2}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{\alpha}^{2}+\cdots+r_{\alpha}(\sigma_{j_{1}})h_{\alpha}^{\alpha}+E_{j_{\alpha},n_{\alpha},\alpha}.

Let
\hat{r}_{k}(\sigma_{j_{1}})
be the approximation of

obtained by removing all the error terms

\lambda_{j_{2}}(T_{n_{2}}(f))\\
\vdots\\

(3.1)

As mentioned in previous works, a variant of this extrapolation strategy was first suggested by Albrecht Böttcher in

Interpolation
For any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we will estimate

. If
\theta_{j,n}
coincides with one of the points in the grid
\{\theta_{0,n_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{n_{1}+1,n_{1}}\}
, then we have the approximations
\hat{r}(\theta_{j,n})
from the extrapolation phase for free. In any other case, we will do it by interpolating the data

(\theta_{0,n_{k}},\hat{r}_{k}(\theta_{0,n_{k}})),(\theta_{1,n_{k}},\hat{r}_{k}(\theta_{1,n_{k}})),\ldots,(\theta_{n_{k}+1,n_{k}},\hat{r}_{k}(\theta_{n_{k}+1,n_{k}})),

for

, and then evaluating the resulting polynomial at
\theta_{j,n}
. This interpolation can be done in many ways, but to avoid spurious oscillations explained by the Runge phenomenon
[

, p.78]
, and following the strategy of the previous works, we decided to do it considering only the
\alpha-k+5
points in the grid
\{\theta_{0,n_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{n_{1}+1,n_{1}}\}
which are closest to
\theta_{j,n}
. Those points can be determined uniquely unless
\theta_{j,n}
is the mid point of two consecutive points in the grid, in which case we can take any of the possible two choices.

Finally, our eigenvalue approximation with

\hat{\lambda}_{j,k}^{\textrm{NAS}}(T_{n}(f))\equiv f\Big(\theta_{j,n}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\hat{r}_{\ell}(\theta_{j,n})h^{\ell}\Big),

(3.2)

where
k=1,\ldots,\alpha
, and NAS stands for “Numerical Algorithm in the variable

”.

Remark 3.1
.

To get the best results possible, in the precomputing phase, we advise to use the proposed algorithm (
3.2
), calculating the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(f)
for

with a significant number of precision digits, let’s say

. For
\alpha=5
and
n_{1}=100
, for instance, this can be done in a standard computer in a few minutes, and it only needs to be done once. While for the extrapolation phase (
3.1
), we advice to do ti with the largest

Numerical Experiments

Let
\lambda_{j,k}^{\SL}(T_{n}(f))
be the
k

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))
obtained with the simple-loop method in the variable
s_{j,n}
, that is

\lambda_{j,k}^{\SL}(T_{n}(f))\equiv f\Big(\theta_{j,n}+\sum_{k=1}^{k-1}r_{k}(\theta_{j,n})h^{k}\Big),

where
\theta_{j,n}=\pi jh
. Let also
\lambda_{j,k}^{\textrm{NA}}(T_{n}(f))
be the
k
th term approximation of
\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))
given by the Numerical Algorithm in
[
14
]
and finally, let
\lambda_{j,k}^{\textrm{MNA}}(T_{n}(f))
be the respective approximation given by the Modified Numerical Algorithm
[
8
, §4]
. In order to compare the results of the different methods we use the following notation for the absolute individual errors

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\SL}_{j,n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))-\lambda_{j,k}^{\SL}(T_{n}(f))|,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\textrm{NA}}_{j,n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))-\lambda_{j,k}^{\textrm{NA}}(T_{n}(f))|,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\textrm{MNA}}_{j,n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))-\lambda_{j,k}^{\textrm{MNA}}(T_{n}(f))|,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\textrm{NAS}}_{j,n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))-\lambda_{j,k}^{\textrm{NAS}}(T_{n}(f))|,

and the respective maximum absolute errors

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\SL}_{n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv\max\{\varepsilon^{\SL}_{j,n,k}\colon j=1,\ldots,n\},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\textrm{NA}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv\max\{\varepsilon^{\textrm{NA}}_{j,n,k}\colon j=1,\ldots,n\}],

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\textrm{MNA}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv\max\{\varepsilon^{\textrm{MNA}}_{j,n,k}\colon j=1,\ldots,n\},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\textrm{NAS}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle\equiv\max\{\varepsilon^{\textrm{NAS}}_{j,n,k}\colon j=1,\ldots,n\}.

We start with an example involving a well-known simple-loop symbol for which we can exactly calculate the coefficients
r_{k}
in (
2.2
) easily, thus we will be able to compare the accuracy of the different eigenvalue approximations.

(A simple-loop symbol)

(4.1)

\mathfrak{a}_{k}(f)=\frac{1}{4}(\rho^{2}-1)\rho^{|k|-1}

Example 4.2

(A non-simple-loop symbol)
.

We now test our algorithm with a Real Cosine Trigonometric Polynomial (RCTP), see
[
15
, §1]
. For
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, consider the symbol

f_{\ell}(\theta)\equiv(2-2\cos(\theta))^{\ell},\quad\theta\in Q.

(4.2)

The respective Fourier coefficients can be exactly calculated as
\mathfrak{a}_{k}(f_{\ell})=(-1)^{k}{{2\ell}\choose{\ell+k}}
for
|k|\leqslant\ell
and
\mathfrak{a}_{k}(f_{\ell})=0
in any other case, then the respective Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(f_{\ell})
are banded with a band of size
2\ell+1
. The case
\ell=2
was carefully studied by Barrera and Grudsky in
[
4
]
, where they formally deduced that

s_{j,n}=\frac{\pi(j+1)}{n+2}+\frac{u_{1,j}}{n+2}+\frac{u_{2,j}}{(n+2)^{2}}+O(h^{3}),

with some bounded and continuous coefficients
u_{1,j},u_{2,j}
.
See Theorem 2.5 there. The previous expansion is slightly different from (
2.2
) but we was able to show that our algorithm is producing fine results in this case.

It is clear that
f_{\ell}\in W^{\alpha}
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and any
\alpha>0
, but
f_{\ell}
is simple-loop only when
\ell=1
because
f^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(0)=0
for
\ell\neq 1
. The Figures
5
,
6
, and the Tables
2
,
3
, show the data for the cases
\ell=2,3
. Since our method is based on the simple-loop expansion (
2.2
), we expected difficulties for the very first eigenvalues, corresponding to the point
\theta=0
, nevertheless, the numerical approximations are good enough for machine precision purposes.

4
shows a comparison between the individual errors

[
15
]
, and

, which corresponds to its boundary modification given by
[
8
, §4]
. Then it is clear that the modified version works better.

5
Conclusions

Under appropriate technical assumptions, the simple-loop theory allows to deduce various types of asymptotic expansions for the eigenvalues of Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(f)
generated by a function
f
. Independently and under the milder hypothesis that
f
is even and monotonic over
[0,\pi]
, matrix-less algorithms have been developed for the fast eigenvalue computation of large Toeplitz matrices. These procedures work with a linear complexity in the matrix order
n
and behind the high efficiency of such algorithms there are the expansions predicted by the simple-loop theory, combined with the extrapolation idea.

In this note we have focused our attention on a change of variable, followed by the asymptotic expansion of the new variable that is

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(f))\equiv f(s_{j,n}),\ \ \ \ \ s_{j,n}=\theta_{j,n}+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}r_{k}(\theta_{j,n})h^{k}+E_{j,n,\alpha},

and then we adapted the matrix-less to the considered new setting.

Numerical experiments have shown in a clear way a higher precision (till machine precision) and the same linear computation cost, when compared with the matrix-less procedures already presented in the relevant literature. More specifically, among the advantages, we concisely mention the following:

1.

when the coefficients of the simple-loop function are analytically known, the algorithm computes them perfectly;

2.

while the proposed algorithm is better or at worst comparable to the previous ones for the computation of the inner eigenvalues, it is extremely better for the computation of the extreme eigenvalues, which are essential for determining important quantities, like the conditioning in the positive definite case.

•

a fine error analysis for having a theoretical explanation of the reason why the new expansion leads to a much smaller errors, when compared with the numerical results in
[
12
,
13
,
15
,
14
]
;

•

applications to the block cases (see
[
2
,
3
]
for the theory in the block case) and related applications
[
12
,
13
]
to differential problems;

•

taking inspiration from
[
1
]
, extension of the technique to preconditioned case
X_{n}=T_{n}^{-1}(g)T_{n}(l)
in which
g
is positive over
(0,\pi)
and is not identically constant, making use of the ergodic theorems given in
[
21
]
, where Theorem
2.2
is extended to the preconditioned case in a very general setting.
