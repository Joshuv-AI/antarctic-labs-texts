1.
Introduction

Let
p_{0},\ldots,p_{k-1},g_{0},\ldots,g_{k-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and

(1.1)

g_{n+k}=p_{k-1}g_{n+k-1}+\dots+p_{0}g_{n}

for all
n\geq 0
then
(g_{n})
is called
linear recursive sequence
with
characteristic polynomial

G(X)=X^{k}-p_{k-1}X^{k-1}-\dots-p_{0}
. To avoid improper examples we assume
p_{0}
and at least one of
g_{0},\dots,g_{k-1}
non-zero. Denote
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{l}
the distinct zeroes of
G(X)
with multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{l}
respectively. Set
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{l})
. It is well known, see e.g
[
12
]
Chapter C, that there exist polynomials
G_{j}(X)\in{\mathbb{K}}[X]
of degree at most
m_{j}-1,j=1,\dots,l
such that

g_{n}=G_{1}(n)\gamma_{1}^{n}+\dots+G_{l}(n)\gamma_{l}^{n}

holds for
n\geq 0
.

The breakthrough works of A. Baker
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
on lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers made it possible, among others, the systematic study of diophantine properties of linear recursive sequences. Moreover the results obtained with the help of Baker’s theory are effective, they serve computable upper bounds for the size of the solutions. You find an excellent overview of earlier results in the book of Shorey and Tijdeman
[
12
]
.

The subspace theorem of W.M. Schmidt
[
11
]
became an other strong tool in the theory of linear recurrences, see e.g. J.-H. Evertse
[
5
]
. Usually the results obtained with the derivations of the subspace theorem are more general, but not effective.

One of the basic questions is the description of the common terms of recurrences. This includes values appearing several times in a recurrence. As recurrences are linear combinations of exponential functions, one expects that they have usually only finitely many common terms, and, in the opposite case, they have to be strongly related. This was proved ineffectively by J.-H. Evertse
[
5
]
and M. Laurent
[
7
]
. Effective version is known only for the equations
g_{n}=g_{m}
provided the characteristic polynomial of
(g_{n})
has at most three roots with the same absolute value and for
g_{n}=h_{m}
when both recurrences have dominating simple and real roots. The book of T.N. Shorey and R. Tijdeman
[
12
]
gives detailed overview on the related results.

Recently E.F. Bravo et al.
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
established all solutions
n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
of the diophantine equation
T_{n}=T_{m},
where
(T_{n})
denotes the tribonacci sequence, which is defined by the initial terms
T_{-1}=T_{0}=0,T_{1}=1
and by the recursion
T_{n+3}=T_{n+2}+T_{n+1}+T_{n},n\geq-1
. The relation (
1.1
) allows to extend
(g_{n})
to negative indices, but that terms are usually not integers. If, however,
p_{0}=\pm 1
the terms with negative indices are integers too. This holds to the tribonacci sequence, which negative branch is defined as
T_{-n-3}=-T_{-n-2}-T_{-n-1}+T_{-n}
. The characteristic polynomial of
(T_{n})
is
T(X)=X^{3}-X^{2}-X-1
, which has a dominating real root. On the other hand the characteristic polynomial of
(T_{-n})
is
X^{3}+X^{2}+X-1=-X^{3}T(1/X)
, whose conjugate complex roots are dominating, i.e. the absolute values of them are larger than the absolute value of the third root. In this circumstances the older methods are not applicable, the authors needed a novel idea. As I mentioned Bravo et al. were interested to find all common terms for a given recurrence, but after a closer look became clear that the method is capable to prove effective finiteness result for a wide class of recurrences. My original goal was to generalize the result of E.F. Bravo et al.
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
to common values of
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers, see e.g.
[
10
]
. Although I was unable to fulfill the original expectation, the results are general enough to summarize in these notes.

To formulate our theorems we have to introduce some notation. Let
(a_{n}),(b_{n})
be linear recursive sequences of integers with characteristic polynomials
A(X),B(X)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
respectively. Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l}
be the distinct roots of

as well as of

with multiplicities
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
and

respectively. We assume that
|\alpha_{1}|>|\alpha_{2}|\geq|\alpha_{3}|\geq\ldots\geq|\alpha_{k}|
; further
\beta_{2}=\bar{\beta}_{1}
and
|\beta_{1}|=|\beta_{2}|>|\beta_{3}|\geq\ldots\geq|\beta_{l}|
.

In the sequel
c_{0},c_{1},\ldots
denote effectively computable constants depending only on the degrees and on the roots of the characteristic polynomials of
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
. The algebraic numbers
\alpha,\beta
are called multiplicatively dependent if there are non-zero integers
a,b

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(a_{n})
and
(b_{m})
be as above. Assume that
m_{1}=n_{1}=1,\alpha_{1}/\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}/\beta_{1}
are not roots of unity and
\delta=\log|\alpha_{1}|/\log|\beta_{1}|\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, i.e.
\alpha_{1}
and
|\beta_{1}|
are multiplicatively dependent. Then the diophantine inequality

(1.2)

|a_{n}-b_{m}|>|a_{n}|^{1-(c_{0}\log^{2}n)/n}

holds for all
n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}_{\geq 0}
with
\max\{n,m\}>c_{1}
, where

c_{0}>\frac{9c_{15}}{\log|\alpha_{1}|}

and
c_{1},c_{15}
denote effectively computable positive constants defined in the proof.

An immediate consequence of our theorem is

Corollary 1.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
the equation

a_{n}=b_{m}

has only finitely many, effectively computatble solutions in
n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}_{\geq 0}
.

Using the theory of weighted

-unit equations, see e.g.
[
6
]
it is easy to prove that if
\alpha_{1}/\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}/\beta_{1}
are not roots of unity then the equation

has for any given integer
K
only finitely many solutions in
(n.m)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
. We do not give here the proof, but refer to the proof of Theorem 3.4 of
[

]
.

The assumption
\alpha_{1}/\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}/\beta_{1}
are not roots of unity is natural because, otherwise,
a_{n}=b_{m}
may have infinitely many solutions. Indeed, set
\alpha_{1}=2,\beta_{1}=2i,\beta_{2}=-2i
and
a_{n}=\alpha_{1}^{n},b_{m}=\beta_{1}^{m}+\beta_{2}^{m}
, then
b_{4k}=2^{4k+1}=a_{4k+1}
hold for all
k\geq 0
. Let now
(b_{m})
be the same and
(a_{n})
any sequence satisfying our assumptions and with
a_{0}=0
. As
b_{4k+1}=0
for all
k\geq 0
the equation
a_{n}=b_{m}
has infinitely many solutions.

On the other hand the assumption
\alpha_{1}
and
|\beta_{1}|
are multiplicatively dependent is not at al natural, moreover it is quite restrictive, but without it we can prove only finiteness, but usually not effective result. To highlight the reason consider the three appearances of Baker type inequalities in our proof, which are

(1)

the proof of a lower bound on
|b_{m}|
,

(2)

the proof of a lower bound on

,

(3)

the proof of an upper bound on

The first two steps can be performed without any restrictions on

, but it seems to be essential for Step 3.

Notice that Theorem
1.1
requires properties only on the characteristic polynomials but not on the initial values of the sequences. Our second theorem gives a sufficient condition for pairs of linear recurrences for which Theorem
1.1
is applicable. We show that the restriction
\log|\alpha_{1}|/\log|\beta_{1}|\in{\mathbb{Q}}
narrow considerably the possible irreducible polynomials, but there are still infinitely many examples. Multiplying such polynomials with suitable ones we get the characteristic polynomials of linear recursive sequences for which the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
hold.
To avoid repetition of the phrase
maximal size of the roots of the polynomial

.

With

2.
A Baker’s type inequality

An essential part of the proof of the main theorem depends on A. Baker’s theory on linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers. Among the many variants we use here the one due to Matveev, which is one of the sharpest nowadays.

For an algebraic number
\eta
with minimal polynomial

f(X)=a_{0}(X-\eta^{(1)})\cdots(X-\eta^{(N)})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]

with positive
a_{0}
, write
h(\eta)
for its absolute logarithmic or Weil height given by

h(\eta)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\log a_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\max\{0,\log|\eta^{(j)}|\}\right).

We list important and well known properties of the function
h
in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\gamma,\eta
be algebraic numbers and
u\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then we have

(1)

h(\gamma\pm\eta)\leq h(\gamma)+h(\eta)+\log 2

(2)

h(\gamma\eta^{\pm 1})\leq h(\gamma)+h(\eta)

(3)

h(\gamma^{u})=|u|h(\gamma)
.

For the proof see e.g. Waldschmidt
[
13
]
, Ch. 3.2.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraic number field of degree
d_{\mathbb{K}}
and let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{t}\in\mathbb{K}
not
0

or
1
, and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
be nonzero integers. Put

B=\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|,3\}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\Gamma=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\eta_{i}^{b_{i}}-1.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{t}
be positive integers such that

A_{j}\geq\max\{d_{\mathbb{K}}h(\eta_{j}),|\log\eta_{j}|,0.16\},\quad{\text{\rm for}}\quad j=1,\ldots t.

Under the circumstances above, Matveev
[
8
]
proved

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\Gamma\neq 0
, then

\log|\Gamma|>-3\cdot 30^{t+4}(t+1)^{5.5}d_{\mathbb{K}}^{2}(1+\log d_{\mathbb{K}})(1+\log tB)A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{t}.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Under the assumptions there are polynomials

hold for all

. As
m_{1}=n_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{1}
and
\beta_{1}
is dominating among the roots of

respectively we have
|\alpha_{1}|,|\beta_{1}|>1
,
\alpha_{1}
is real
n_{2}=n_{1}=1
,
\beta_{1}=\bar{\beta_{1}}
and
A_{1},B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are constants. Putting

\delta_{a}=\frac{\log|\alpha_{2}|}{2\log|\alpha_{1}|}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\delta_{b}=\frac{\log|\beta_{3}|}{2\log|\beta_{1}|}

then
\delta_{a},\delta_{b}<1
and it is an easy exercise to prove

(3.1)

\displaystyle|a_{n}-A_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n}|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle c_{2}|\alpha_{1}|^{\delta_{a}n},

(3.2)

\displaystyle c_{3}|\alpha_{1}|^{n}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle|a_{n}|<c_{4}|\alpha_{1}|^{n}

and

(3.3)

|b_{m}-(B_{1}\beta_{1}^{m}+B_{2}\beta_{2}^{m})|<c_{5}|\beta_{1}|^{\delta_{b}m}

with suitable positive effective constants
c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5}
, provided
n
and
m
are large enough.

In contrast, it is a consequence of the deep theory of A. Baker that if
\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}
is not a root of unity then

(3.4)

|\beta_{1}|^{m-c_{6}\log m}<|B_{1}\beta_{1}^{m}+B_{2}\beta_{2}^{m}|<c_{7}|\beta_{1}|^{m},

see Corollary 3.7 of
[
12
]
. The last two inequalities imply

(3.5)

|\beta_{1}|^{m-2c_{6}\log m}<|b_{m}|<2c_{7}|\beta_{1}|^{m}.

provided
m
is large enough. We derive only the lower bound, because the proof of the upper bound is straight forward

|b_{m}|>|B_{1}\beta_{1}^{m}+B_{2}\beta_{2}^{m}|-c_{8}|\beta_{3}|^{m}>|\beta_{1}|^{m-c_{6}\log m}-c_{8}|\beta_{1}|^{\delta_{b}m}>|\beta_{1}|^{m-2c_{6}\log m}.

Contrary to the statement of Theorem
1.1
we assume that there exist infinitely many
(n,m)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
for which (
1.2
) does not holds. We show that the choice of any such solutions with
n
large enough leads to a contradiction. In the sequel let
(n,m)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
be a solution of

||a_{n}|-|b_{m}||\leq|a_{n}|^{1-(c_{0}\log^{2}n)/n}

and
n
is large enough. As
||a_{n}|-|b_{m}||=|a_{n}-b_{m}|,
if
a_{n}
and
b_{m}
have the same sign and by
|-a_{n}-b_{m}|
, we have to deal with the two cases separately. In the sequel we are dealing with the first case because replacing
a_{n}
by
-a_{n}
in the argument below one gets easily the proof of the second case. Thus we are to do with the inequality

(3.6)

|a_{n}-b_{m}|\leq|a_{n}|^{1-(c_{0}\log^{2}n)/n}.

We also assume
a_{n}\geq b_{m}
. The case
a_{n}<b_{m}
can be handled similarly.

Instead of
a_{n}-b_{m}
, which may have many terms we will study

, which has only three summands, thus much more easy to treat. Moreover

Using (
3.1
) and (

\displaystyle|A_{n,m}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|a_{n}-b_{m}|+|a_{n}-A_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n}|+|b_{m}-(B_{1}\beta_{1}^{m}+B_{2}\beta_{2}^{m})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|a_{n}|^{1-c_{0}(\log^{2}n)/n}+c_{4}|\alpha_{1}|^{\delta_{a}n}+c_{5}|\beta_{1}|^{\delta_{b}m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(c_{4}+c_{5})\max\{|\beta_{1}|^{\delta_{b}m},|\alpha_{1}|^{\delta_{a}n}\}+c_{3}^{1/2}|\alpha_{1}|^{n-c_{0}\log^{2}n}.

Case I.
|\alpha_{1}|^{\delta_{a}n}\geq|\beta_{1}|^{\delta_{b}m}

The first summand on the right hand side of (

(c_{4}+c_{5})|\alpha_{1}|^{\delta_{a}n}

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Assume that
A(X),B(X)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
. Let
P_{1}(X),P_{2}(X)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
be such that
\overline{|P_{1}|}<\overline{|A|}
and
\overline{|P_{2}|}<\overline{|B|}
then
A_{1}X),B_{1}(X)
with
A_{1}(X)=A(X)P_{1}(X),B_{1}(X)=B(X)P_{2}(X)
satisfy obviously the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
too. Thus it is enough to characterize the irreducible polynomials among them.

Let
A(X),B(X)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
be irreducible, and denote
\alpha
the real root of
A(X)
with maximal size, while
\beta
the non-real root of
B(X)
with maximal size. In Theorem
1.1
a crucial (and unnatural) assumption is that
|\alpha|
and
|\beta|
are multiplicatively dependent. This means that there are coprime, non-zero integers
p,q
such that
\pm\alpha^{p}=(\beta\bar{\beta})^{q/2}
or, equivalently

(4.1)

\alpha^{2p}=(\beta\bar{\beta})^{q}.

As
\alpha
is the dominant real root of
A(X)
and
\beta
one of the dominant complex roots of
B(X)
both have to lie outside the unit circle and the last equation is only possible if
p
and
q
have same sign, but then we may assume that both are positive. Denote
{\mathbb{K}}
the normal closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha,\beta,\bar{\beta})
and
G
its Galois group. If
\sigma\in G
then
\sigma(\alpha)
is a root of
A(X)
, thus
|\sigma(\alpha)|<|\alpha|
. Because of the same reason
|\sigma(\beta)|<|\beta|
for all
\sigma\in G
except when
\sigma(\beta)=\bar{\beta}
.

Let
\sigma\in G
not the identity be such that
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
. If
\sigma(\beta)\not=\bar{\beta}
then

(\beta\bar{\beta})^{q}=\alpha^{2p}=\sigma(\alpha^{2p})=\left(\sigma(\beta)\sigma(\bar{\beta})\right)^{q},

but this is impossible because
|\sigma(\beta)|<|\beta|
and
|\sigma(\bar{\beta})|\leq|\bar{\beta}|
. Thus either
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\deg G=2
or
B(X)
has apart
\bar{\beta}
a third root, say
\gamma
.

If
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}
then the only algebraic conjugate of
\beta
is
\bar{\beta}
, thus
\beta\bar{\beta}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, which has to be a
p
-th power, say
b^{p}
. Hence
\alpha^{2}=b^{q}
and
\alpha=\pm b^{q/2}
. If
b
is a square then
q
is arbitrary, otherwise it must be even. Hence the defining polynomial of
\beta
has the shape
X^{2}+aX+b^{p}
such that
a^{2}-4b^{p}<0
, further the defining polynomial of
\alpha
is
X\pm b^{q/2}
. This proves that if
A
has an integer root then (i) is not only sufficient, but also necessary that the pair
A(X),B(X)
fulfill the requirements of Theorem
1.1
.

We now turn to the second case, but now we can prove only sufficiency. Denote
\alpha
the real root and
\gamma,\overline{\gamma}
the pair of conjugate complex roots of
Q_{2}(X)
. We have
\alpha\gamma\overline{\gamma}=1
, thus
|\gamma|<1
because by assumption
|\alpha|>1
. Further the last equation means
|\alpha||\gamma|^{2}=1
too, i.e
|\alpha|
and
|\gamma|
are multiplicatively dependent. The roots of
X^{3}Q_{2}(1/X)
are
1/\alpha,1/\gamma,1/\overline{\gamma}
. With
\beta=1/\gamma
we have that
\alpha
and
\beta
are multiplicatively dependent.

It remains to prove that neither
\alpha/\beta
nor
\beta/\overline{\beta}
is a root of unity. In contrary assume that
\alpha/\beta=\omega
is a root of unity. Taking conjugate and multiplying the resulting equation with the first one we get

\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{\alpha}{\overline{\beta}}=\omega\overline{\omega}=1.

We also have
\alpha/(\beta\overline{\beta})=1
, which together with the last equation implies
\alpha^{3}=1
, i.e.
\alpha=1
, which is absurd.

Assume finally that
\beta/\overline{\beta}=\omega
is a root of unity. Then, especially
\omega\in{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\beta,\overline{\beta})
. We also have
\alpha\in{\mathbb{K}}
because
\alpha+\beta+\overline{\beta}=a
, thus the field
{\mathbb{K}}
is normal. There is an automorphism
\sigma
of
{\mathbb{K}}
, with
\sigma(\beta)=\alpha
. It maps
\overline{\beta})
to itself or to
\beta
and
\omega
to one of its conjugates, which is also root of unity. In both cases
\alpha/\beta
is a root of unity, which is by the last paragraph absurd. Hence the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
fulfill.
\Box
