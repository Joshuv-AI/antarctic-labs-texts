Enforced symmetry breaking by invertible topological order
Shang-Qiang Ning, Yang Qi, Zheng-Cheng Gu, Chenjie Wang

Abstract
It is well known that two-dimensional fermionic systems with a nonzero Chern number must break the time reversal symmetry, manifested by the appearance of chiral edge modes on an open boundary. Such an incompatibility between topology and symmetry can occur more generally. We will refer to this phenomenon as enforced symmetry breaking (ESB) by topological orders. In this work, we systematically study ESB of a finite symmetry group G f G_{f} by fermionic invertible topological orders. Mathematically, the group G f G_{f} is a central extension over a bosonic symmetry group G G by the fermion parity group ℤ 2 f \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f} , characterized by a 2-cocycle λ ∈ ℋ 2 ​ ( G , ℤ 2 ) \lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2}) . For given G G and λ \lambda , we are able to obtain a series of criteria on the existence or non-existence of ESB by the corresponding fermionic invertible topological orders. Using these criteria, we discover many ESB examples that are not known previously. For 2D systems, we define a set of physical quantities to describe symmetry-enriched invertible topological orders and derive obstruction functions using both fermionic and bosonic languages. In the latter case which is done via gauging the fermion parity, we find that some obstruction functions are consequences of conditional anomalies of the bosonic symmetry-enriched topological states, with the conditions inherited from the original fermionic system. We also study ESB of the continuous group S ​ U f ​ ( N ) SU_{f}(N) by 2D invertible topological orders through a different argument.

While the mutual appreciation between topology and symmetry has resulted in many interesting physics, it is also known that they are not always compatible. For example, 2D Chern insulators with a nonzero Chern number must break the time reversal symmetry
Haldane 1988
. It is manifested by the existence of chiral edge modes that reverse the direction under time reversal action and thereby cannot appear in time-reversal symmetric systems. In fact, time-reversal is broken in any chiral topological order. Such incompatibility can occur more generally in other systems and for other symmetries. We will refer to this phenomenon as
enforced symmetry breaking
(ESB) by topological orders. Of course, depending on one’s viewpoint, it can also be called symmetry constraints on topological order.

To be possibly enforced to break by certain topological orders, there is a precondition on the symmetry: it must have a nontrivial action on the Hilbert space by its very definition. A few examples of these symmetries are: (1) the time-reversal symmetry
\mathcal{T}
, under which the wave function must be complex-conjugated, (2) the mirror reflection
\mathcal{R}

P_{f}
, under which the states containing an odd number of fermions must obtain a minus sign. More generally, a symmetry group
\mathcal{G}
that contains
P_{f}
,
\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{R}

U_{f}(1)

\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}=\{1,P_{f}\}

In this work, we study the ESB physics for a special class of topological orders, namely

(iTOs)

They are topological phases somewhat in between SPT phases and the topological orders that host anyons: they cannot be smoothly connected to the trivial product state even in the absence of symmetry, but they do not host anyon excitations. II
ESB by 0D and 1D iTOs

To warm up, we discuss ESB by 0D and 1D fermionic iTOs in this section. We begin with the definition of symmetry group in fermionic systems. Then, we discuss ESB criteria, examples, and derivations of the criteria for 0D and 1D fermionic iTOs.

II.1
Symmetry

II.2
ESB criterion for 0D iTO

In the usual sense, 0D systems neither host topological order nor support spontaneous symmetry breaking. So, it is not very interesting to study the phenomenon of ESB. However, there is indeed a sense of ESB as we explain below, exploring which helps to establish a few basic concepts.

One way to define 0D fermionic iTO is as follows. Consider 0D fermionic systems with a gapped unique ground state, so that they are invertible. Two systems are said to have the same topological order if the ground states can be smoothly deformed to each other by a fermionic unitary transformation(or bosonic unitary transformation with a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
symmetry). Under this definition, there are two inequivalent fermionic iTOs: those with the ground state being even under
P_{f}
, and those with the ground state being odd under
P_{f}
. Simple examples are the states
|0\rangle
and
c^{\dagger}|0\rangle
, respectively, where
|0\rangle
is the vacuum in the Fock space and
c^{\dagger}
is a fermion creation operator. The latter is considered to be topologically non-trivial. We will refer to it as the “complex-fermion iTO”.

Now we ask if the complex-fermion iTO enforces certain symmetry group
G_{f}
to break. It should be an explicit symmetry breaking, as there is no spontaneous symmetry breaking in 0D. Let
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
be the ground state of any system that hosts the complex-fermion iTO. It follows from the definition that

P_{f}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=-|\Psi_{0}\rangle.

(8)

In the presence of a symmetry group
G_{f}
, the ground state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
should form a one-dimensional representation of
G_{f}
, with the condition (
8
) satisfied. Therefore, if
P_{f}={1}
in all its one-dimensional representations,
G_{f}
is incompatible with the complex-fermion iTO. That is,
G_{f}
is enforced to break by the complex-fermion iTO.

This understanding can be used to derive a quantitative criterion on whether ESB occurs for a given symmetry group. Let
G_{f}
be determined by the pair
(G,\lambda)
, where
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is a 2-cocycle. We show that

.

(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}
is a non-trivial cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U_{T}(1))

are even. Then, the cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})=(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{k(k+1)/2}
. A class of representative 2-cocycles in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is

\displaystyle\lambda(a,b)=\sum_{i}p_{i}w_{i}(a,b)+\sum_{i<j}p_{ij}w_{ij}(a,b),

(11)

where the indices
i,j
run in
1,2,\dots,k
, and the parameters
p_{i},p_{ij}=0
or 1. To define
w_{i}(a,b)
and
w_{ij}(a,b)
, let us denote the group elements as
a=(a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{k})
where
a_{i}=0,1,...,N_{i}-1
. Then,
w_{i}(a,b)
and
w_{ij}(a,b)
are given by

\displaystyle w_{i}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{i}}(a_{i}+b_{i}-[a_{i}+b_{i}]_{N_{i}}),

(12)

\displaystyle w_{ij}(a,b)

\displaystyle=a_{i}b_{j}{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)},

(13)

where
[n_{1}+n_{2}]_{N}=n_{1}+n_{2}{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ N)}
. Both
w_{i}
and
w_{ij}
only take a value
0
or
1
. One can check
\lambda(a,b)
in the form (
11
) is indeed a 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\prod_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}},\mathbb{Z}_{2})
for any choice of
p_{i}
and
p_{ij}
. It is useful to define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{i}-1}\lambda(ne_{i},e_{i}),

\displaystyle\Omega_{ij}

\displaystyle=\lambda(e_{i},e_{j})-\lambda(e_{j},e_{i}),

(14)

where “modulo 2” is assumed,
e_{i}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)
with only the
i
th entry being
1
and others being
0
. These quantities have a nice property that they are invariant under coboundary transformations, so we will call them
topological invariants
for
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
(see Appendix
C.1
for more details). Inserting the explicit cocycle in (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}={p_{i}},\quad\Omega_{ij}={p_{ij}},

(15)

where
i<j
. By varying
p_{i}
and
p_{ij}
, the set
\{\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}\}
can have
2^{k(k+1)/2}
distinct values, saturating the number
|\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})|
of cohomology classes. Therefore, it is a complete set of topological invariants and the representative cocycle in (
11
) exhausts all inequivalent cocycles in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

Now consider the cocycle
(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}
in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U(1))
(we drop the subscript
T
as we only consider unitary symmetries here). Under coboundary transformations of
U(1)
-valued functions, one can check that
(-1)^{\Omega_{i}}
is not invariant, but
(-1)^{\Omega_{ij}}
remains a well-defined topological invariant. Moreover,
(-1)^{\Omega_{ij}}
is complete in the sense that it is enough to tell if
(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}
is trivial or non-trivial in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U(1))
Wang and Levin 2015
. Therefore,
(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}
is non-trivial in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U(1))
if and only if any
\Omega_{ij}=1
. For convenience, here and after, we call a 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
as Type-I if all invariants
\Omega_{ij}=0
, and otherwise we call it Type-II. Accordingly, the corresponding
G_{f}
is enforced to break by the complex-fermion iTO if and only if
\lambda
is Type-II.

Take the simplest case
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and let
\Omega_{12}=1
. Then, ESB occurs for the corresponding
G_{f}
. Depending on the values of
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, we have two cases: (i) when
\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{2}=1
,
G_{f}
is the quaternion group
Q_{8}
; (ii) otherwise,
G_{f}
is the dihedral group
D_{8}
.

II.4
Derivation of 0D criterion

We prove an alternative statement that is equivalent to Criterion
1
:
G_{f}
is compatible with the complex-fermion iTO, if and only if
(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}
is trivial in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U_{T}(1))
. The equivalence is obvious. Derivation of this statement mainly involves the representation theory of groups.

Let us start with the “only if” direction. To show that, we assume that
G_{f}
is compatible with the complex-fermion iTO. Then, the ground state forms a one-dimensional representation of
G_{f}
. Let the representation be
U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}
for
\mathbf{g}_{\sigma}\in G_{f}
, where
U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})
is a unitary operator and
K
is the operator of complex conjugation. In the special case that
s(\mathbf{g})=0
for all
\mathbf{g}\in G
, it reduces to the usual unitary representation of a group. The operators satisfy

U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}U(\mathbf{h}_{\tau})K^{s(\mathbf{h})}=U[(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})_{\sigma+\tau+\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}]K^{s(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})},

(16)

which follows from the group multiplication law (
5
). To fulfill Eq. (
8
) and the requirement
U(\mathbf{1}_{0})=1
, we have
U(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma})=(-1)^{\sigma}
. The convention (
4
) implies
\mathbf{g}_{0}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}=\mathbf{g}_{\sigma}
. So, we have

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}

\displaystyle=U(\mathbf{g}_{0})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}U(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{1})}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sigma}U(\mathbf{g}_{0})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}.

(17)

Combining this equation with Eq. (
16
) and taking the shorthand notation
U(\mathbf{g}_{0})=U(\mathbf{g})
, we immediately have

U(\mathbf{g})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}U(\mathbf{h})K^{s(\mathbf{h})}=(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}U(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})K^{s(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})}.

(18)

It is a projective representation of
G
, where
(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}\equiv\alpha(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is called a factor set. In general, the factor set of projective representations can be any 2-cocycle
\alpha(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U_{T}(1))
. A well known result is that if
\alpha
is a non-trivial 2-cocycle, the representation cannot be 1D. The fact that we have a 1D representation implies that
\lambda
must be a trivial 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U_{T}(1))
. Therefore, we have proven that if
G_{f}
is compatible with the complex-fermion iTO,
(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}
must be trivial in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U_{T}(1))
.

To show the “if” direction, we explicitly construct a 1D representation of
G_{f}
with Eq. (
8
) satisfied. If
(-1)^{\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}
is trivial in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U_{T}(1))
, it can be written as
\epsilon(\mathbf{g})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}[\epsilon(\mathbf{h})]/\epsilon(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})
, where
\epsilon(\mathbf{g})
is some
U(1)
-valued function. Then, we take the representation to be

U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})=(-1)^{\sigma}\epsilon(\mathbf{g})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}.

(19)

One can check that it is a 1D representation of
G_{f}
that satisfies both (
16
) and (
8
). This completes our proof.

II.5
ESB by 1D iTO

Invertible topological orders in 1D fermionic systems are classified by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. A representative of the nontrivial iTO is the famous Majorana chain, first discovered by Kitaev
Kitaev 2001
. So, we will refer to the nontrivial iTO as the “Majorana-chain iTO”. The salient feature of the Majorana chain is that, when it is open, there exist robust zero modes at both ends, known as the Majorana zero modes (MZM). More specifically, it means the existence of Majorana operators
\gamma_{l}
and
\gamma_{r}
at the left and right ends, respectively, such that
[\gamma_{l},H]=[\gamma_{r},H]=0
, where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the chain. Majorana operators are fermionic, self-adjoint, squared to 1, and guarantee a two-fold ground-state degeneracy. Intuitively, the latter means the MZM at each end carries a “fractional” Hilbert space of dimension
\sqrt{2}
. The degeneracy is topologically protected and cannot be lifted by any local perturbations that respect the fermion parity.

Now consider a symmetry group
G_{f}
, determined by the pair
(G,\lambda)
with
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. We ask if
G_{f}
is compatible with the Majorana-chain iTO. This question has already been answered in the seminal paper Ref.
Fidkowski and Kitaev 2011
. In our language, the result of Ref.
Fidkowski and Kitaev 2011
can be stated as the following criterion:

Criterion 2
.

G_{f}
is enforced to break by the Majorana-chain iTO, if and only if
\lambda
is a non-trivial 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

In other words, the Majorana-chain iTO is only compatible with
G_{f}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}\times G
. This criterion holds regardless if
G
contains antiunitary symmetries.

To be self-contained, we briefly revisit the proof given in Ref.
Fidkowski and Kitaev 2011
. Without loss of generality, systems hosting the Majorana-chain iTO can be viewed as a stack of
2n+1
Majorana chains with interaction between the chains allowed. For open boundaries, there are
2n+1
Majorana operators at each end. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\dots,\gamma_{2n+1}
be those at the left end. Then, any local operator at the left end (left-local operator) can be written as a sum of products of the Majorana operators. An important feature of the algebra of left-local operators is that there exist and only exist two operators that commute with all left-local operators and that square to 1. The two operators are
Z
and
-Z
, with
Z=i^{n}\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{2n+1}
and
Z^{2}=1
. The operator
Z
contains an odd number of Majorana operators so that
P_{f}Z=-ZP_{f}
. Note that the fermion parity
P_{f}
is not left-local.

Now consider symmetries in
G_{f}
. For
\mathbf{g}_{\sigma}\in G_{f}
, let
U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}
be the corresponding operator that acts on the whole low-energy Hilbert space that includes both left and right ends. Under the action of
U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}
, left-local operators remain left-local, with their algebraic structure preserved. In particular,
Z
can only be transformed to either
Z
or
-Z
. More specifically,

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}Z[U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}]^{-1}=(-1)^{\mu(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})}Z.

(20)

where
\mu(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})=0,1
specifies how
Z
transforms under the action of
\mathbf{g}_{\sigma}
. The specific
\mu(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})
depends on details, except
\mu(\mathbf{1}_{1})=1
due to
P_{f}Z=-ZP_{f}
. The operators
U(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})K^{s(\mathbf{g})}
shall form a representation of
G_{f}
, so that
\mu(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})
is a group homomorphism from
G_{f}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{g}_{\sigma})=\sigma+\mu(\mathbf{g}_{0}),{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}.

(21)

Then, between
\mu(\mathbf{g}_{0})
and
\mu(\mathbf{g}_{1})
, one of them must be
0
. Let us pick out all the group elements with the
\mu
value being 0. They are closed under multiplication and form a subgroup
G^{\prime}\subset G_{f}
. Since
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
is central in
G_{f}
, we must have
G_{f}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}\times G^{\prime}
and accordingly
G^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
G
. Hence, we have shown that the Majorana-chain iTO is compatible with
G_{f}
only if it is a trivial extension of
G
by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
, i.e.,
\lambda
is a trivial 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. On the other hand, if
\lambda
is trivial,
G_{f}
is always compatible with the Majorana-chain iTO — one simply represents all elements in
G
by the identity operator. This concludes the proof.

III.1
Symmetry-enriched fermionic iTOs

Different from usual 2D topological orders, iTOs do not support anyon excitations. Characterization of symmetry-enriched iTOs is more like that of SPT phases rather than SET phases. We will discuss two equivalent descriptions to characterize 2D fermionic symmetry-enriched iTOs: (i) by studying properties of symmetry defects (i.e., static version of gauge fluxes) in the fermionic theory and (ii) by gauging the fermion parity
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
and studying the resulting
bosonic
SETs of symmetry group
G
. In this section, we will use the former to define a set of data to describe 2D symmetry-enriched iTOs, as it is physically more intuitive. This description allows us to obtain two obstructions,
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}
, which are important quantities in the ESB criteria. However, it is theoretically less mature than the latter, so we will use the second description to provide more detailed derivations of the ESB criteria in Sec.
IV
.

We define a triplet
(\nu,n_{1},n_{2})
to describe 2D symmetry-enriched fermionic iTOs. The first quantity
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
is defined above. It is a quantity associated with the intrinsic topology, which indexes the iTO and determines the chiral central charge
c_{-}=\nu/2
. Depending on whether
\nu
is even or odd, the fermion-parity defect behaves differently: for
\nu
being odd, it carries an odd number of Majorana zero modes (MZMs)
Ivanov 2001
, which cannot be completely annihilated by local perturbations; for
\nu
being even, it carries an even number of MZMs, whose stability relies on the protection of other symmetries. A refined characterization is given by the dynamical fermion-parity gauge flux, which is an anyon, denoted as
v
. According to Kitaev’s 16-fold way
Kitaev 2006
, braiding statistics of the fermion-parity fluxes exhibit a 16-fold periodicity. In particular, the topological spin
\theta_{v}=e^{i\pi\nu/8}
. In fact, the
\nu=16
fermionic iTO is topologically equivalent to the bosonic
E_{8}
state stacked with a trivial fermionic insulator. The group
G_{f}
acts the same as
G
on the the bosonic
E_{8}
state, so they are always compatible — we simply set all elements in
G_{f}
by the identity operator for the bosonic
E_{8}
state. Since
G_{f}
is compatible with a trivial fermionic insulator, we arrive at a conclusion: the
\nu=16
iTO is compatible with any
G_{f}
, i.e., no ESB will occur. Therefore, the phenomenon of ESB exhibits a 16-fold periodicity in
\nu
for 2D iTOs.

III.2
ESB criteria

We have defined the triplet
(\nu,n_{1},n_{2})
for the description of 2D symmetry-enriched fermionic iTOs. In principle, there is another piece of data, corresponding to stacking bosonic SPT states. However, that data is irrelevant to our study of ESB physics, so we will not discuss it. We have also derived the
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction functions, by checking the consistency between the MZM composition rule or fermion parity conservation and the defect fusion rules. In fact, there is another level of obstruction, denoted as
\mathcal{O}_{4}
, which follows from the consistency between symmetry action on local Hilbert spaces of defects and defect fusion rules. We will not derive
\mathcal{O}_{4}
in the fermionic language in this work, but instead turn to the bosonic SET language and discuss it in Sec.
IV
. A symmetry group
G_{f}
is enforced to break by a fermionic iTO of index
\nu
, if there are no valid
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
that make
\mathcal{O}_{2},\mathcal{O}_{3}
and
\mathcal{O}_{4}
all trivial, i.e., if there are no valid 2D symmetry-enriched fermionic iTOs. In this section, we will state and elaborate the ESB criteria, and illustrate the criteria via various examples. Derivations of the criteria, including re-derivation of
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}
and the derivation of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
in bosonic SET language, will be given in Sec.
IV
.

III.2.1

\nu

We first consider 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu
being odd. Consider symmetry ground
G_{f}
, determined by the pair
(G,\lambda)
. We show that

is enforced to break by the 2D fermionic iTOs with odd

\lambda

\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

In other words, the odd-

. This criterion can be easily seen from the
\mathcal{O}_{2}
obstruction alone. For odd
\nu
, we have
\mathcal{O}_{2}=\lambda
. If
\lambda
is nontrivial in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,Z_{2})
, so is
\mathcal{O}_{2}
. Then,
G_{f}
is enforced to break. On the other hand, if
\lambda
is trivial,
G_{f}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}\times G
and there is always a valid symmetry-enriched iTO — the one with all symmetries in
G
represented by the identity operator.

One may notice that this criterion is the same as Criterion
2
for the Majorana-chain iTO. It is not a coincidence. One can understand this connection by dimensional reduction. Imagine we insert a pair of fermion parity defects in a cylindrical geometry (Fig.
4
). There are two MZMs located at the two ends when
\nu
is odd. If we reduce the cylinder to a 1D system, it becomes a 1D Majorana-chain iTO. It is important to note that

is in the center of

so that

remains the symmetry group of the effective 1D system. Then, if we apply Criterion

defect is allowed to carry MZMs. One subtlety is that inserting a

is a nontrivial extension by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
, then
\mathbf{g}
cannot carry MZM as it contradicts with Criterion
2
. However, if
C_{\mathbf{g}}
is a trivial extension of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}

is always a (trivial or nontrivial) 3-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
as long as
n_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
and
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Second, we do not have the most general expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
yet. However, we know that it is a functional of
\lambda,n_{1}
,
n_{2}
, and
\nu
. It can be defined only if
\mathcal{O}_{3}
is a coboundary. We also know that it should be a
U(1)
-valued 4-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,U(1))
from our knowledge of bosonic SET physics. In the special case that
n_{1}=0
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\lambda\cup_{1}\lambda
is trivial,
\mathcal{O}_{4}
has the following expression:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{4}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\nu\frac{\pi}{8}[\lambda\cup\lambda+\hat{\mathrm{d}}\lambda\cup_{1}\lambda](\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})}\times e^{i\pi(n_{2}+\lambda)\cup n_{2}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})}

(44)

where “
\hat{\mathrm{d}}
” is used to denote the usual coboundary operation but without taking modulo 2, and additions in the exponents do not assume modulo 2 either. We will argue in Sec.
IV
that a more general (but still incomplete) expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
can be obtained by stacking fermionic iTOs with fermionic SPT states.

While we do not have the general expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
, the
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction alone is enough to give rise to many ESB examples. In particular, we give a complete answer to ESB phenomena that come from the
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction for general finite Abelian groups
G=\prod_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}}
. This is discussed in Appendix
C.2
. A few simple examples will be discussed in Sec.
III.3.1
.

III.2.3
\nu=8k+4

Now we consider the case
\nu=8k+4
. As discussed in Sec.
III.1.3
, when
\nu=4k
, the
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction reduces to
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\lambda\cup n_{1}
. Then,
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction alone cannot give rise to enforced symmetry breaking, because there always exists the case
n_{1}=0
such that
\mathcal{O}_{3}=0
. Accordingly, ESB may occur only if both
\mathcal{O}_{3}
and
\mathcal{O}_{4}
are considered. We claim that

Criterion 5
.

G_{f}
is enforced to break by the 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu=8k+4
, if a quantity
\mathcal{O}_{4}=\mathcal{O}_{4}[\lambda,\nu,n_{1},n_{2}]

is a nontrivial cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,U(1))
, for all
n_{1}
that make
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\lambda\cup n_{1}
a coboundary and for all
n_{2}
that satisfy
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\mathrm{d}n_{2}
. We conjecture that there is no other situation such that
G_{f}
is enforced to break for
\nu=8k+4
.

Regarding the expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
, we will argue in Sec.
IV.5
that a general and complete expression can be obtained by a stacking trick. Here, we give a simpler version of the formula for the case that
n_{1}=0
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{4}=e^{i\pi\mathcal{Q}_{4}/2}

(45)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{4}=\frac{\nu}{4}(\lambda\cup\lambda+\hat{\mathrm{d}}\lambda\cup_{1}\lambda)+2n_{2}\cup(n_{2}+\lambda),

(46)

where both
\lambda
and
n_{2}
are valued in
\{0,1\}
, “modulo 2” is not taken for
\hat{\mathrm{d}}
, and additions elsewhere are taken to be “modulo 4”. That
\mathcal{O}_{4}
is 4-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,U(1))
follows from the fact that
\mathcal{Q}_{4}
is actually a 4-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{4})
. To see that, first, it is straightforward to check that
2n_{2}\cup(n_{2}+\lambda)
is
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-valued 4-cocycle, following the general properties of cup products. Second, the piece
\mathcal{P}(\lambda)\equiv\lambda\cup\lambda+\hat{\mathrm{d}}\lambda\cup_{1}\lambda
is mathematically known as the Pontryagin square of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued cocycle
\lambda
. It is known that
\mathcal{P}(\lambda)
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-valued cocycle (see Appendix
A
). Accordingly,
\mathcal{Q}_{4}
is a 4-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{4})
. This special expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
allows us to discover a few ESB examples for
\nu=8k+4
fermionic iTOs with a finite Abelian group
G
, which we will discuss in Sec.
III.3.2
. We note that (
45
) and (
44
) are the same but
\nu
takes different values.

III.2.4
\nu=16k+8

In the case
\nu=16k+8
, again both
\mathcal{O}_{3}
and
\mathcal{O}_{4}
need to be taken into account to support ESB. The statement of ESB criterion is the same as in Criterion
5
:

Criterion 6
.

G_{f}
is enforced to break by the 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu=16k+8
, if a quantity
\mathcal{O}_{4}=\mathcal{O}_{4}[\lambda,\nu,n_{1},n_{2}]

is a nontrivial cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,U(1))
, for all
n_{1}
that make
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\lambda\cup n_{1}
a coboundary and for all
n_{2}
that satisfy
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\mathrm{d}n_{2}
. We conjecture that there is no other situation such that
G_{f}
is enforced to break for
\nu=16k+8
.

Compared to the
\nu=8k+4
case, the difference lies in the expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
. We will argue in Sec.
IV.5
that a general and complete expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
can be obtained by stacking fermionic SPTs and using the SPT formulas. Here, we give a simpler version of the formula for the case that
n_{1}=0
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}_{4}=e^{i\pi(\lambda\cup\lambda+n_{2}\cup n_{2}+n_{2}\cup\lambda)}.

(47)

Note that if one sets
\nu=16k+8
in (
46
) and considers that
\hat{\mathrm{d}}\lambda=0{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}
, then Eq. (
45
) reduces to Eq. (
47
).

The above special expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
allows us to obtain a general result for finite Abelian
G
. We show that for finite Abelian group
G
, ESB will never occur at
\nu=8k
, regardless of
\lambda
. That is, if
G_{f}
is determined by a finite Abelian group
G
and an arbitrary
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, it will never be enforced to break by the
\nu=8k
fermionic iTOs. (The case of
\nu=16k
has been generally discussed at the beginning of Sec.
III.1
.) To see that, we first notice that the piece
(-1)^{\lambda\cup\lambda}
or
(-1)^{n_{2}\cup n_{2}}
has appeared in the study of 2D fermionic SPT states
Cheng et al. 2018
. It was known that this piece is always a trivial 4-cocycle for finite Abelian
G
Wang et al. 2017b
. One may use the topological invariants in (
55
) to directly check this fact. Therefore,
\mathcal{O}_{4}
reduces to
(-1)^{n_{2}\cup\lambda}
. Then, we can choose
n_{2}=0
such that
\mathcal{O}_{4}
is trivial. That is, if
n_{1}=n_{2}=0
, the obstruction functions
\mathcal{O}_{3}
and
\mathcal{O}_{4}
are both trivial, which gives rise to a valid symmetry-enriched iTO.

Therefore, to look for ESB by
\nu=16k+8
fermionic iTOs, we must go to non-Abelian
G
. It is usually not easy to check if
\mathcal{O}_{4}
is a trivial or non-trivial cocycle for non-Abelian group
G
, even for the expression in (
47
). Fortunately, we will argue in Sec.
III.4
that
SU_{f}(8n)
(

being any positive integer) will be enforced to break by 2D fermionic iTOs with index
\nu=16k+8
, through a different argument. That argument actually applies to iTOs with any even
\nu
.

Finally, we remark that although ESB will never occur for
\nu=16k
, there are still nontrivial obstruction functions. These obstruction functions were obtained previously in the study of SPT states.
Kapustin and Thorngren 2017
;
Wang and Gu 2018
;
Wang and Gu 2020

III.3
Examples

In this subsection, we give a few examples of ESB by 2D fermionic iTOs. For odd-
\nu
iTOs, any
G_{f}
with
\lambda
being a non-trivial 2-cocycle is enforced to break. For
\nu=16k+8
, we do not have an example of finite groups. However, in Sec.
III.4
, we give an example of Lie group. So, we discuss the cases of
\nu=4k+2
and
\nu=8k+4
below.

III.3.1
\nu=4k+2

We will consider examples with
G
being Abelian and focus on those ESB solely due to
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstructions for
\nu=4k+2
. We will discuss some general setup regarding group cohomology of finite Abelian groups and topological invariants (quantities that are invariant under coboundary transformations). Then, we will specialize to
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{4}
. Discussions on general Abelian
G
as well as some discussions on
\mathcal{O}_{4}
are given in Appendix
C
.

Recall that
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
can be generally parametrized in Eq. (
11
) and the topological invariants
\{\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}\}
in (
14
) are complete for finite Abelian group
G=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}}
(we assume
N_{i}
being even without loss of generality). We note that
\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}
are valued in
\{0,1\}
, and
\Omega_{ij}=\Omega_{ji}
,
\Omega_{ii}=0
. Also, a general 1-cocycle
n_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
can be parametrized as

\displaystyle n_{1}(a)=\sum_{i}q_{i}v_{i}(a)

(48)

where
q_{i}=0
or
1
,
v_{i}(a)=a_{i}{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}
, and
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
is an integer vector to denote group elements of
G
. The cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})=2^{k}
and different choices of
\{q_{i}\}
exhaust all cohomology classes of 1-cocycles.

We show in Appendix
C
that one can define a complete set of topological invariants for 3-cocycles in
\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Given
u\in\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, the topological invariants can be defined as follows. Let
\chi_{a}(b,c)=u(a,b,c)-u(b,a,c)+u(b,c,a)
, then

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}-1}\chi_{e_{i}}(e_{j},ne_{j}),

\displaystyle\Xi_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\chi_{e_{i}}(e_{j},e_{k})-\chi_{e_{i}}(e_{k},e_{j}),

(49)

where
e_{i}
is the
i
th generator of
G
and “modulo 2” is again assumed. We remark that
\Xi_{ij}
and
\Xi_{ji}
are independent, while
\Xi_{ijk}
is a fully antisymmetric tensor. For the special
3
-cocycle
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\lambda\cup n_{1}+\lambda\cup_{1}\lambda
, we show in Appendix
C.2
that

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\Omega_{j}+\frac{N_{j}}{2}(q_{j}-1)\Omega_{ij},

\displaystyle\Xi_{ijk}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\Omega_{jk}+q_{j}\Omega_{ki}+q_{k}\Omega_{ij}.

(50)

Accordingly, for given
\{\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}\}
(i.e., given
\lambda
), the corresponding
G_{f}
is enforced to break if there is no solution of
\{q_{i}\}
to the equations
\Xi_{ij}=\Xi_{ijk}=0
. This result applies to an arbitrary finite Abelian group. If there are solutions, then one needs to further check the
\mathcal{O}_{4}
obstruction.

Now we specialize to
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. In this case, Eq. (
50
) becomes

\displaystyle\Xi=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\Omega_{1}\\
q_{2}\Omega_{2}\\
q_{1}\Omega_{2}+(q_{2}-1)\Omega_{12}\\
q_{2}\Omega_{1}+(q_{1}-1)\Omega_{12}\end{pmatrix}

(51)

where
\Xi=(\Xi_{11},\Xi_{22},\Xi_{12},\Xi_{21})^{T}
. We ask if there is any choice of
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
and
\Omega_{12}
such that there is no
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
making
\Xi=0
. It turns out that when
\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{2}=\Omega_{12}=1
, there is indeed no solution. In this case, the fermionic symmetry group
G_{f}=Q_{8}
, the quaternion group with its center identified as
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
. Therefore,
G_{f}=Q_{8}
is enforced to break by
\nu=4k+2
fermionic iTOs.

Similarly, for
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{4}
, Eq. (
50
) becomes

\displaystyle\Xi=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\Omega_{1}\\
q_{2}\Omega_{2}\\
q_{1}\Omega_{2}\\
q_{2}\Omega_{1}+(q_{1}-1)\Omega_{12}\end{pmatrix}

(52)

It is not hard to check that there is ESB by
\nu=4k+2
iTOs for two cases: (1)
\Omega_{1}=0
and
\Omega_{2}=\Omega_{12}=1
, (2)
\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{2}=\Omega_{12}=1
.

III.3.2
\nu=8k+4

For
\nu=8k+4
, we consider examples with
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{n}
. Again, we use Eq. (
11
) to parameterize
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
and use the topological invariants
\{\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}\}
in (
14
) to characterize it. We use (
48
) to parameterize
n_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. In the current case, evaluating the topological invariants in (
49
) for
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\lambda\cup n_{1}
gives

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\Omega_{j}+q_{j}\Omega_{ij},

\displaystyle\Xi_{ijk}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\Omega_{jk}+q_{j}\Omega_{ki}+q_{k}\Omega_{ij}.

(53)

To limit the possible solutions
\{q_{i}\}
, we will constrain ourselves to the case that
\Omega_{i}=1
for all
i
. Then, we have
\Xi_{ii}=q_{i}
. The only solution to
\Xi_{ii}=0
is
q_{i}=0
for all
i
, i.e,
n_{1}=0
. This makes the special expression of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
in (
45
) applicable. In this case
\mathrm{d}n_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{3}=0
is a cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

Therefore, ESB will occur if
\mathcal{O}_{4}
in (
45
) is nontrivial for all possible
n_{2}
. Like
\lambda
in (
11
), we will parameterize
n_{2}
as follows

\displaystyle n_{2}(a,b)=\sum_{i}x_{i}w_{i}(a,b)+\sum_{i>j}x_{ij}w_{ij}(a,b)

(54)

where
x_{i}
and
x_{ij}
can also take 0 or 1. Using these parametrization,
\mathcal{O}_{4}
in (
45
) can be parametrized by the integers
p_{i},p_{ij}
and
x_{i}
,
x_{ij}
. It can be proven that for Abelian
G
,
(-1)^{n_{2}\cup n_{2}}
is a trivial U(1)-valued 4-cocycle. Therefore, we only need to consider the rest part of
\mathcal{O}_{4}
, which fortunately is linear in
x_{i},x_{ij}
.

For Abelian group
G=\prod_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}}
, one efficient way to check whether a 4-cocycle
\chi(a,b,c,d)\in\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,U(1))
is nontrivial or not is to evaluate the following set of topological invariants
Wang and Levin 2015
:

\displaystyle e^{i\Theta_{i,l}}

\displaystyle=\text{$\prod_{n=1}^{N_{i}}$}\chi_{e_{l},e_{i}}(e_{i},ne_{i}),

\displaystyle e^{i\Theta_{ij,l}}

\displaystyle=\text{$\prod_{n=1}^{N^{ij}}$}\chi_{e_{l},e_{i}}(e_{j},ne_{j})\chi_{e_{l},e_{j}}(e_{i},ne_{i}),

\displaystyle e^{i\Theta_{ijk,l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{e_{l},e_{i}}(e_{k},e_{j})}{\chi_{e_{l},e_{i}}(e_{j},e_{k})}

(55)

where
\chi_{a,b}(c,d)
is two-steps slant product of
\chi(a,b,c,d)
over
a
and
b
recursively and we denote the group element of
G
as
a=\sum_{i}n_{i}e_{i}
with
e_{i}
as the generator of subgroup
\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}}
. The sufficient and necessary condition that
\chi(a,b,c,d)
is a trivial 4-cocycle is that all these topological invariants are equal to one.

We consider two cases:
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
with
n=3
and
n=4
. We will show that the former case does not lead to ESB but the latter might do. First, we consider
n=3
. For
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{3}
, there are many different
\lambda
. Assisted by computer, we can show that for none of these
\lambda
’s,
\mathcal{O}_{4}
is nontrivial for all
n_{2}
. Here, we specifically focus on the case
\lambda=\sum_{i}w_{i}+\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}
, the calculation of which helps for the
n=4
case. We need to consider
eight independent topological invariants — six
e^{i\Theta_{i,l}}
with
i\neq l
and two
e^{i\Theta_{ij,l}}
with
i\neq j\neq l
. To look for ESB, it is equivalent to ask whether there exists at least one set of
x_{i}
and
x_{ij}
suc that

\displaystyle e^{i\Theta_{i,l}}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle e^{i\Theta_{ij,l}}

\displaystyle=1,

(56)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\Theta_{i,l}

\displaystyle=0,\quad{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)}

\displaystyle\Theta_{ij,l}

\displaystyle=0,\quad{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)}.

(57)

Recall that we can ignore
n_{2}\cup n_{2}
in
\mathcal{O}_{4}
. So, these equations are a set of (modular) linear equations respect to
x_{i}
and
x_{ij}
and can be solved straightforwardly. It turns out that there exsit only two solutions, that is

\displaystyle(1)\,x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}=x_{12}=x_{13}=x_{23}=0,

\displaystyle(2)\,x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}=x_{12}=x_{13}=x_{23}=1.

Therefore, there exists
n_{2}
such that all the invariants are trivial. In other words, there is no ESB for
G=(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{3}
with
\lambda=\sum_{i}w_{i}+\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}
.

Now we consider the case
n=4
and
\lambda=\sum_{i}w_{i}+\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}
. Now there are in total 21 independent topological invariants — twelve
e^{i\Theta_{i,l}}
, eight
e^{i\Theta_{ij,l}}
and one
e^{i\Theta_{ijk,l}}
. First, we find out the solution space of
x_{i}
and
x_{ij}
such that all the 20 topological invariants
e^{i\Theta_{i,l}}
and
e^{i\Theta_{ij,l}}
are equal to one. Similar to the case
n=3
, we find that there are only two such solutions of
x_{i},x_{ij}
:

\displaystyle(1)\ x_{i}=x_{ij}=0

\displaystyle(2)\ x_{i}=x_{ij}=1

for all
i
and
j
. However, neither of the two solutions make the last topological invariant
e^{i\Theta_{ijk,l}}
to be 1. In other words, there is no such
n_{2}
that all the 21 topological invariants are equal to 1. Therefore, we find an example of ESB by 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu=8k+4
:
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4}
with
\lambda=\sum_{i}w_{i}+\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}
. The fermionic group
G_{f}
is of order 32.

III.4
G_{f}=SU_{f}(N)

In this subsection, we consider 2D fermionic iTOs with a continuous symmetry group
SU_{f}(N)
(
N
being even). The main motivation is to look for examples of ESB by fermionic iTOs with
\nu=16k+8
. It turns out that this example is quite neat and gives rise to ESB for all even
\nu
’s, when
N
varies. Our argument in this example does not follow other parts of this work. It is a generalization of an argument given in Ref.
Ning et al. 2021b
for
SU_{f}(2)
.

Let us first explain some basic structures of
SU_{f}(N)
. It is the usual
SU(N)
group, and the subscript “
f
” denotes that the fermion parity group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
is embedded. To be concrete, let us use a representation of
N\times N
matrices. The group
SU_{f}(N)
has a center
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{f}
, represented by the diagonal matrices
e^{in2\pi/N}\mathbb{I}
, with
n=0,1,\dots,(N-1)
and
\mathbb{I}
being the
N\times N
identity matrix. We consider even
N
, and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
is identified to the group
\{\mathbb{I},-\mathbb{I}\}
. An important fact that we will use is that there exists a
U_{f}(1)
subgroup and the center
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{f}
is its subgroup. Such a group is not unique. One of the choices is
U_{f}(1)=\{U_{\theta}|0\leq\theta<2\pi\}
and

\displaystyle U_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&e^{i\theta}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&e^{i(1-N)\theta}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(58)

where the last diagonal term is
e^{i(1-N)\theta}
and other diagonal terms are
e^{i\theta}
. It is easy to check that
U_{2\pi/N}
generates the center and the fermion parity is
U_{\pi}
. In short, we have
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{f}\subset U_{f}(1)\subset SU_{f}(N)
.

Now we consider an
SU_{f}(N)
-enriched 2D fermionic iTO with even
\nu
. First, with respect to
U_{f}(1)
, we can define a Hall conductance
\sigma_{H}
. It is known that, for fermionic iTOs with even
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}=\frac{\nu}{2}{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)}.

(59)

The “modulo
8
” is due to the
E_{8}
state which does not contribute to
\sigma_{H}
but changes the chiral central charge
c_{-}=\nu/2
by 8. Next, we compute
\sigma_{H}
in two ways. On one hand, by Laughlin’s argument,
\sigma_{H}
is equal to the charge
Q_{2\pi}
accumulated on an adiabatically inserted
2\pi
flux. On the other hand, we may also adiabatically insert
N
copies of
2\pi/N
fluxes. Let us denote the defect corresponding to a
2\pi/N
flux as
v
, and
Q_{v}
is the
U_{f}(1)
charge carried by
v
. The total charge carried by the
v
defects should be equal to
Q_{2\pi}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}=NQ_{v}.

(60)

In general,
Q_{v}
is fractional, because
v
is a defect instead of a local excitation.

We argue that in the presence of
SU_{f}(N)
, the charge
Q_{v}
must be an integer. Let us first give a simple argument and then justify it more rigorously. Recall that
U_{2\pi/N}
lies in the center of
SU_{f}(N)
. Accordingly, inserting a
2\pi/N
flux (the
v
defect) does not break
SU_{f}(N)
. So,
v
must carry a projective representation of
SU_{f}(N)
. However,
SU_{f}(N)
is a connected and simply connected compact Lie group, which does not support nontrivial projective representations. Accordingly,
v
carries a linear representation of
SU_{f}(N)
, which is also a linear representation of the subgroup
U_{f}(1)
. That means,
v
must carry an integer charge of
U_{f}(1)
. The linear representation carried by
v
is generally irreducible due to energy consideration.

Two clarifications are needed to make the above argument justified. First, from our understanding of SETs, symmetry fractionalization goes beyond projective representations. Technically speaking, we need to replace
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U(1))
which classifies projective representations with
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathcal{A})
, where
\mathcal{A}
is an Abelian group formed by Abelian anyons under fusion. However, for
G=SU_{f}(N)
, it can be shown mathematically that both
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,U(1))
and
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathcal{A})
are trivial. Since
v
is a defect, not an anyon, readers may still not be convinced. There is another way to see this: one can gauge the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{f}
center and turn the fermionic iTO into a bosonic topological order enriched by the quotient group
PSU(N)=SU_{f}(N)/\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{f}
. In this way,
v
becomes a true anyon. Symmetry fractionalization on
v
is classified by
\mathcal{H}^{2}(PSU(N),\mathcal{A})
, whre
\mathcal{A}
is the fusion group associated with the bosonic topological order whose structure depends on
\sigma_{H}
. Using the universal coefficient theorem, one can show that
\mathcal{H}^{2}(PSU(N),\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{Tor}\{\mathcal{H}^{2}(PSU(N),U(1)),\mathcal{A}\}
, where
\mathrm{Tor}
is a cohomological operation and
\mathcal{H}^{2}(PSU(N),U(1))=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. Regardless of what group
\mathrm{Tor}\{\mathcal{H}^{2}(PSU(N),U(1)),\mathcal{A}\}
is, what is important to us is the physical meaning:
\mathrm{Tor}
picks out those projective representations of
PSU(N)
that are compatible with the fusion group
\mathcal{A}
. This interpretation has been widely used for
SO(3)
, which is equal to
SU_{f}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
(see, e.g., Ref.
Wang and Senthil 2014b
). We believe it is applicable generally. Accordingly, symmetry fractionalization on
v
can all be characterized by projective representations of
PSU(N)
, before considering the compatibility to fusion rules. Finally, it is well known that projective representations of
PSU(N)
are simply linear representations of
SU_{f}(N)
. So, it goes back to our argument above.

Second, the irreducible linear representation of
SU_{f}(N)
carried by
v
in the fermionic picture is usually multi-dimensional. Then, when we insert multiple
2\pi/N
fluxes, they may stay in different states inside the irreducible linear representation, as these states are energetically degenerate. However,
U_{f}(1)
charges carried by different states in an irreducible representation of
SU_{f}(N)
can only differ by a multiple of
N
. This is again because
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{f}\subset U_{f}(1)
is the center of
SU_{f}(N)
, so that all states in an irreducible representation of
SU_{f}(N)
must carry the same
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{f}
charge. This is equivalent to say that
U_{f}(1)
charges can only differ by a multiple of
N
. Accordingly, even if this subtlety is taken into account, Eq. (
60
) is relaxed to

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}=0{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ N)}

(61)

which is enough for our purpose.

With these clarifications, we now combine (
59
) and (
61
). We immediately have

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{2}=n\gcd(N,8),

(62)

where
n
is any integer and “
\gcd
” stands for greatest common divisor. Equation (
62
) must be satisfied by all symmetry-enriched fermionic iTOs. If it cannot be satisfied, that means the symmetry group
SU_{f}(N)
is enforced to break by the iTO. More explicitly, we have

1.

SU_{f}(2l)
is enforced to break by 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu=4k+2
;

2.

SU_{f}(4l)
is enforced to break by 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu=8k+4
;

3.

SU_{f}(8l)
is enforced to break by 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu=16k+8
;

where
k,l
are any positive integers. For
\nu=16k
, (
62
) is always satisfied, which is consistent to the 16-fold periodicity of ESB physics argued above.

IV
Derivation of 2D ESB criteria

In this section, we give alternative derivations of
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstructions, and derive the
\mathcal{O}_{4}
obstruction and 2D ESB criteria. Compared to Sec.
III.1
where we define
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
using defects in the fermionic language, the main strategy here is to gauge the fermion parity group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
and turn the fermionic iTO into a bosonic topological order.

Let us denote the resulting bosonic topological order as
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
, for the fermionic iTO of index
\nu
. Detailed properties of
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
can be found in Ref.
Kitaev 2006
. When
\nu
is odd,
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
contains three anyons:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\nu}=\{1,f,\sigma\},

where
f
corresponds to the original fermion and
\sigma
is a non-Abelian fermion-parity flux of quantum dimension
d_{\sigma}=\sqrt{2}
. When
\nu
is even,
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
contains four anyons:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\nu}=\{1,f,v,vf\},

where
v
and
vf
are two Abelian fermion-parity fluxes satisfying the fusion rule
v\times f=vf
. The even-
\nu
cases can be further distinguished by the fusion rule of fermion-parity fluxes,

\displaystyle v\times v=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f,&\ \text{if }\nu=4k+2\\
1,&\ \text{if }\nu=4k\end{array}\right.

where
k
is an integer. In all cases, the topological spin of a fermion parity flux is
\theta_{\sigma}=e^{i\nu\pi/8}
or
\theta_{v}=\theta_{vf}=e^{i\nu\pi/8}
, and the mutual statistics between
f
and fluxes is
M_{f,\sigma}=-1
or
M_{f,v}=-1
. Hence, there is a 16-fold periodicity in
\nu
.

With
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
gauged, there remains a global symmetry group
G
. Hence, we obtain an SET state of topological order
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
and symmetry group
G
. We will see that it is not an arbitrary SET, but one with certain symmetry properties fixed by the 2-cocycle
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Studying such a conditional SET leads to new consistency relations that do not exist in unconditional SETs. These conditional consistency relations, which we call
conditional anomalies
, give rise to the
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstructions which otherwise do not exist. (There is also an
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}
obstruction in general bosonic SETs, but
\mathcal{O}_{3}
is different from, although related to,
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}
.) Together with an
\mathcal{O}_{4}
obstruction of bosonic SETs, they can be used to derive the ESB criteria associated with the original fermionic iTOs.

IV.1
Basics of SETs

Before studying our specific SETs, we review the basics of the general theory of bosonic SET states. For more details, one can refer to Ref.
Barkeshli et al. 2019
. The description of SETs involves several layers of data which we briefly explain below one by one.

First, a bosonic topological order
\mathcal{C}
contains anyons
1,a,b,\dots
, where
1
is the trivial anyon. Mathematically,
\mathcal{C}
is described by a unitary modular tensor category (UMTC).
Kitaev 2006
Physically, anyons are characterized by their fusion and braiding properties. They follow a set of fusion rules
a\times b=\sum_{c}N_{ab}^{c}c
, where the fusion coefficient
N_{ab}^{c}
is a non-negative integer. There exists a unique anyon
\bar{a}
, namely the anti-particle of
a
, such that
N_{a\bar{a}}^{1}=1
. Two important quantities of each anyon
a
are the quantum dimension
d_{a}
and topological spin
\theta_{a}
. For Abelian anyons,
d_{a}=1
and
\theta_{a}
is its self-statistics. Associated with each
N_{ab}^{c}\neq 0
there is a vector space
{V}_{c}^{ab}
, called the fusion or splitting space, whose dimension is
N_{ab}^{c}
. A key quantity that relates different fusion spaces is the so-called
F
symbol, which is an isomorphism between fusion spaces of three anyons
\left(F^{abc}_{d}\right)_{ef}:\bigoplus_{e}V^{ab}_{e}\otimes V^{ec}_{d}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{f}V^{af}_{d}\otimes V^{bc}_{f}
. If one exchanges two anyons, it corresponds to another isomorphism, the
R
symbol,
R^{ab}_{c}:V^{ab}_{c}\rightarrow V_{c}^{ba}
.

Every
\mathcal{C}
processes a set of topological symmetries, which form a group denoted as
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C})
. We only consider those that are unitary and orientation-preserving. A topological symmetry is an invertible map from
\mathcal{C}
to itself. It contains two parts: (i) a permutation of anyons

\displaystyle a\rightarrow a^{\prime}\equiv\varphi(a),

(65)

and (ii) an action on the fusion space
V_{c}^{ab}

\displaystyle\varphi(|a,b;c\rangle)=u_{c^{\prime}}^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}|a^{\prime},b^{\prime};c^{\prime}\rangle,

(66)

where
u_{c^{\prime}}^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}
is a phase factor and
|a,b;c\rangle\in V_{c}^{ab}
. For simplicity, we have assumed
N_{ab}^{c}=0
or
1
, as all our cases satisfy this assumption. Topological symmetries keep all the data of
\mathcal{C}
invariant, e.g.,

\displaystyle N_{ab}^{c}

\displaystyle=N_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}^{c^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\theta_{a}

\displaystyle=\theta_{a^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\left[F^{abc}_{d}\right]_{ef}

\displaystyle=u^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}_{e^{\prime}}u^{e^{\prime}c^{\prime}}_{d^{\prime}}\left[F_{d^{\prime}}^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\right]_{e^{\prime}f^{\prime}}\left[u^{a^{\prime}f^{\prime}}_{d^{\prime}}\right]^{*}\left[u^{b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}_{f^{\prime}}\right]^{*},

\displaystyle R^{ab}_{c}

\displaystyle=u^{b^{\prime}a^{\prime}}_{c^{\prime}}R_{c^{\prime}}^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\left[u^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}_{c^{\prime}}\right]^{*}.

(67)

One can see that if
u_{c}^{ab}
is multiplied by
{\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}}/{\gamma_{c}}
, equations (
67
) remain unchanged. Such a transformation is called a natural isomorphism. It is regarded as an equivalence relation between different sets of
\{u_{c}^{ab}\}
. Group multiplication of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C})
in the aspect of
u_{c}^{ab}
is upto natural isomorphisms. It is believed that
\{u_{c}^{ab}\}
is fixed up to natural isomorphisms once anyon permutation in (
65
) is given, which is indeed the case in our examples.

Second, consider a system with the microscopic symmetries forming a group
G
. If it is unbroken at low energy,
G
must be mapped into
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C})
. This is characterized by a group homomorphism
\rho:G\rightarrow\text{Aut}(\mathcal{C})
.
That is, every
\mathbf{g}\in G
is associated with a topological symmetry, denoted as
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
. We denote the anyon permutation
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(a)={}^{\mathbf{g}}a
for short. For anyon permutations,
\rho
is an exact group homomorphism. On the other hand, the action on states in fusion space is upto natural isomorphisms:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathbf{gh}}=\kappa_{\mathbf{g,h}}\circ\rho_{\mathbf{g}}\circ\rho_{\mathbf{h}},

(68)

where
\kappa_{\mathbf{g,h}}
is a natural isomorphism. Let
u_{\mathbf{g}}(a,b,c)
be the phase factor associated with
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
’s action on fusion spaces. Then, the explicit expression of
\kappa_{\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}}
is

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathbf{g,h}}(a,b,c)=\frac{u_{\mathbf{gh}}(a,b,c)}{u_{\mathbf{g}}(a,b,c)u_{\mathbf{h}}({}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}a,{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}b,{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}c)},

(69)

where
\bar{\mathbf{g}}
is a short-hand notation for
\mathbf{g}^{-1}
. Since
\kappa_{\mathbf{g,h}}
is a natural isomorphism, we can decompose it as

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathbf{g,h}}(a,b,c)=\frac{\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})\beta_{b}(\mathbf{g,h})}{\beta_{c}(\mathbf{g,h})}.

(70)

The quantity
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is subject to two kinds of ambiguities: (i) the decomposition (
70
) is not unique and a shift
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})\rightarrow\nu_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})
is also valid as long as
\nu_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})\nu_{b}(\mathbf{g,h})=\nu_{c}(\mathbf{g,h})
if
N_{ab}^{c}\neq 0
; (ii) a natural isomorphism in
u_{\mathbf{g}}(a,b,c)
induces a shift
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\rightarrow\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\gamma_{a}(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})/\gamma_{a}(\mathbf{g})\gamma_{{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}a}(\mathbf{h})
.

With
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
, we define an important quantity

\displaystyle\Omega_{a}(\mathbf{g,h,k})=\frac{\beta_{{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}a}(\mathbf{h,k})\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g,hk})}{\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})\beta_{a}(\mathbf{gh,k})}.

(71)

By definition,
\Omega_{a}
is a coboundary in
\mathcal{H}^{3}_{\rho}(G,U(1))
for every
a
. Also, associativity of
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
can be used to show that
\Omega_{a}(\mathbf{g,h,k})\Omega_{b}(\mathbf{g,h,k})=\Omega_{c}(\mathbf{g,h,k})
if
N_{ab}^{c}\neq 0
. The latter implies that

\displaystyle\Omega_{a}(\mathbf{g,h,k})=M_{a,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g,h,k})}^{*}

(72)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k})
is an Abelian anyon in
\mathcal{C}
and
M_{a,b}
is the mutual statistical phase between
a
and an Abelian anyon
b
. Moreover,
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g,h,k})
is a 3-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{3}(G,\mathcal{A})
, where
\mathcal{A}
denotes the group of Abelian anyons in
\mathcal{C}
. However, it is important to note that
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k})
might be a nontrivial cocycle. The ambiguities in
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
induce coboundary transformations in
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k})
. Accordingly, only the cohomology class
[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}]
in
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{3}(G,\mathcal{A})
matters.

Third, other than topological actions, symmetries in
G
also act on local degrees of freedom around each anyon. Consider a state that contains a set of anyons
\{a_{i}\}
, which are spatially well-separated. The overall action of
\mathbf{g}\in G
is a combination of local and topological actions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{g}}=\prod_{i}U_{\mathbf{g}}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathbf{g}},

(73)

where
U_{\mathbf{g}}^{(i)}
is a local unitary operator, supported in the neighborhood of anyon
a_{i}
, and
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
is the topological action in the fusion space of
\{a_{i}\}
which can be decomposed into those in (
66
). The local operators form a projective representation

\displaystyle U_{\mathbf{g}}^{(i)}{}^{\mathbf{g}}U_{\mathbf{h}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\eta_{a_{i}}(\mathbf{g,h})U_{\mathbf{gh}}^{(i)},

(74)

where
{}^{\mathbf{g}}U^{(i)}_{\mathbf{h}}=\rho_{\mathbf{g}}U_{\mathbf{h}}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathbf{g}}^{-1}
, and
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is a 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{\rho}(G,U(1))
for every
a
. The local unitary operator
U_{\mathbf{g}}^{(i)}
has a phase ambiguity. At the same time,
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
also has a phase ambiguity due to natural isomorphisms. The two ambiguities shall be correlated such that
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{g}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{h}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h}}
. It was shown in Ref.
Barkeshli et al. 2019
that a nice quantity to look at is the ratio

\displaystyle\omega_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=\frac{\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})}{\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})},

(75)

which satisfies
\omega_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\omega_{b}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=\omega_{c}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
if
N_{ab}^{c}\neq 0
. That means, we have

\displaystyle\omega_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=M_{a,w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}^{*},

(76)

where
w(\mathbf{g,h})
is an Abelian anyon in
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{C}
. It is equivalent to say that

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\eta_{b}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}{\eta_{c}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}=\frac{\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\beta_{b}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}{\beta_{c}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})},

(77)

whenever
N_{ab}^{c}\neq 0
. Accordingly, gauge transformations of
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
and
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
shall be correlated such that (
77
) always holds. Combining (
71
), (
72
), (
75
), (
76
) and that
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is 2-cocycle, one can show that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{g,h,k})=\text{d}w(\mathbf{g,h,k})

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathbf{g}}[w(\mathbf{h,k})]\overline{w(\mathbf{gh,k})}w(\mathbf{g,hk})\overline{w(\mathbf{g,h})},

(78)

where
\bar{x}
stands for the anti-particle of anyon
x
. This implies that
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g,h,k})
should be a 3-coboundary in
\mathcal{H}^{3}_{\rho}(G,\mathcal{A})
to give rise to a valid
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
. If
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g,h,k})
, obtained from its definitions (
71
) and (
72
), is a nontrivial 3-cocycle, Eq. (
78
) can never have a solution for
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
, implying that the form of symmetry action in (
73
) does not hold. Then, it is said that the topological action
\rho
has a
symmetry localization
obstruction.

When the obstruction
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k})
is trivial, one then look for
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
that satisfies (
78
). It is not hard to see that given
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
a solution,
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})t(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is also a valid solution if
t(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{2}(G,\mathcal{A})
. At the same time,
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is subject to an ambiguity
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\rightarrow w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\rho_{\mathbf{g}}[\zeta(\mathbf{h})]\zeta(\mathbf{g})\overline{\zeta(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})}
, where
\zeta(\mathbf{g})
is an arbitrary Abelian anyon in
\mathcal{A}
. This ambiguity is due to an independent phase shift that we can perform on
U_{\mathbf{g}}^{(i)}
or equivalently on
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
for a fixed
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
. Then, different solution classes
[w]
to (
78
) are said to describe different
symmetry fractionalization
classes. They are related to each other by a cohomology class
[t]\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{\rho}(G,\mathcal{A})
. Once
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
and
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
are given, the projective phase factor
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is determined by (
75
) and (
76
).

Fourth, for a given symmetry action
\rho\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C})
and a symmetry fractionalization class described by
w(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
, there are additional consistency conditions to satisfy. For an SET to be valid, one shall be able to insert symmetry defects (i.e., couple to a background gauge field) and these defects shall form a fusion category. It was shown in Ref.
Chen et al. 2015
that given
\rho
and
\omega
, one can construct a quantity
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{4}
out of them. It is a 4-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,U(1))
and subject to coboundary transformations. For the defects to form a consistent fusion theory, it is required the cohomology class
[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{4}]
is trivial. If it is nontrivial, we say there is an
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{4}
obstruction. The general expression
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{4}
is complicated. A simple one with a trivial
\rho
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{4}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},

\displaystyle\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=R_{w\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\right)}\frac{F_{w\left(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\mathbf{k}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h}\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\right)}}{F_{w\left(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\right),w\left(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{l}\right)}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{F_{w\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\right),w\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\mathbf{l}\right)}}{F_{w\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h}\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\right)}}\frac{F_{w\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\right),w\left(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\mathbf{l}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{l}\right)}}{F_{w\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\right),w\left(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\mathbf{l}\right)}},

(79)

where the
R
and
F
symbols are associated with Abelian anyons in
\mathcal{C}
. If
[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{4}]
is trivial, one can study the fusion theory of defects, which involves additional
F
symbols among the defects. It was shown that different defect theories can be obtained by twisting the defect
F
symbols with a 3-cocycle
\alpha_{3}\in\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,U(1))
. Physically, it corresponds to stack an SET with a 2D SPT state which is indeed classified by
\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,U(1))
. In this work, we will not explore the data
\alpha_{3}
as it is irrelevant to the phenomenon of enforced symmetry breaking.

To summarize, there are two obstruction classes: (i) existence of a valid symmetry fractionalization class
[w]
requires
[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}]
to be trivial; (ii) a consistent defect theory further requires
[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{4}]
to be trivial. A valid bosonic SET state requires both obstruction classes to be trivial.

IV.2
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and symmetry fractionalization condition

We now apply the above general theory to our case, the topological order
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
enriched by symmetry group
G
. In this subsection, we consider the case of odd
\nu
. In this case, there is no nontrivial topological symmetry, i.e.,
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{C}_{\nu})=\mathbb{Z}_{1}
. Then, no symmetries in
G
permute anyons. It is known that the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}
obstruction is always trivial without anyon permutations. We can take the gauge
u_{c}^{ab}=1
,
\beta_{a}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=1
, such that
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{3}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k})=1
.

We make some important comments here before proceeding. The fermion
f
is a local excitation in the original iTO, so the fusion space
{V}^{ff}_{1}
is not topological but instead local. Then, one should impose the condition that
u^{ff}_{1}=1
after
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}
is gauged. This condition was previously discussed in Ref.
Tata et al. 2021
. It is a necessary condition for establishing an inverse mapping from the bosonic SET back to the fermionic iTO. With this condition imposed, natural isomorphisms on
f
can only be
\gamma_{f}=\pm 1
. Then, the quantity
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
in the decomposition (
70
) shall also take a value
+1
or
-1
. Under an appropriate gauge choice, we find that we can set
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=1

for all
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
’s (the cases of even
\nu
’s are discussed in Appendix
B
). In fact, we postulate that the most general case is
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})/[\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{g})\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{h})]
, and a shift
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\rightarrow\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\nu_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is not allowed in general (see a discussion below). The
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstructions in Sec.
III.1
are correctly recovered only under this assumption. We believe that it is due to the locality of
f
in the original fermionic systems, such that (
80
) below describes the absolute symmetry fractionalization on
f
and disallows a shift
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\rightarrow\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\nu_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
. We will take
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})/[\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{g})\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{h})]
as an assumption in this work, and we refer the readers to recent works
Aasen et al. 2021
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2021a

\displaystyle(-1)^{\{\lambda\cup n_{2}+n_{2}\cup n_{2}+n_{2}\cup_{1}\hat{\text{d}}n_{2}\}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})+\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\mathbf{k})\hat{\text{d}}n_{2}(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})}

\displaystyle(-1)^{\hat{\text{d}}n_{2}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\mathbf{l})\hat{\text{d}}n_{2}(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})}(-i)^{\hat{\text{d}}n_{2}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k})[1-\hat{\text{d}}n_{2}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{k}\mathbf{l})](\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}.

We will also denote

Stacking the two anomalous states together, their

V
Discussions

To summarize, we have studied the phenomenon of enforced symmetry breaking (ESB) by 0D, 1D and 2D fermionic invertible topological orders (iTOs). We have obtained a set of criteria for asserting the existence or non-existence of ESB for finite groups, and illustrated the ESB physics with examples in all cases. We give both fermionic and bosonic descriptions (via gauging fermion parity) of symmetry-enriched fermionic iTOs.

It is very interesting to generalize the current study to ESB by general 2D fermionic topological orders, i.e., those with anyon excitations. For example, our dimensional reduction argument in Sec.
III.2.1
can be easily used to show that
G_{f}
from a nontrivial cocycle
\lambda(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is incompatible with those fermionic topological orders that support both Majorana and non-Majorana vortices after gauging the fermion parity (e.g., the
SO(3)_{3}
fermionic topological order). Also, it is interesting to study enforced breaking of anti-unitary symmetries, such as time reversal. In this work, we only study 2D fermionic iTOs which are chiral, so that enforced breaking of time-reversal is obvious. However, for non-chiral fermionic topological orders, it is a challenging problem. Moreover, ESB might also exist for 3D fermionic topological order, and it would be very interesting to explore such examples.

For ESB phenomenon to occur, it is required that, by definition, the symmetries should have some nontrivial action in the Hilbert space, such as non-trivial extension by fermion parity or time reversal symmetry. In other words, there are conditions imposed at the very beginning. It has been clearly seen in our exploration of bosonic SETs that the obstruction functions
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and
\mathcal{O}_{3}
are due to the imposed conditions. We have named them
conditional anomalies
. The conditions on the bosonic SETs in our study follow from the conditions in the original fermionic iTOs. However, generally speaking, conditions on properties (such as symmetry fractionalization) of topological orders may be imposed by other reasons. For example, in the study of spin liquids, the spinon should always carry a half-integer spin in systems with an odd number of spin-
\frac{1}{2}
s per unit cell
Cheng et al. 2016
, and this indeed causes ESB phenomenon for certain topological orders
Zaletel and Vishwanath 2015
. Therefore, a general study on conditional anomalies or conditional obstructions are very important and helpful. Finally, we conjecture that the novel concept of ESB can also be defined for gapless systems and might have important implications in fundamental physics, such as CP violation problem in Standard Model. We will leave these potential directions for future study.

Appendix A
Group cohomology

In this appendix, we review some basic knowledge of group cohomology, including cup products and Pontryagin square.

A.1
Definition

Consider a finite group
G
and a
G
-module
M
. A
G
-module
M
is an Abelian group equipped with a
G
action
a\rightarrow g\cdot a
, where
a,g\cdot a\in M
and
g\in G
. The action is compatible with the multiplication in
M
,

\displaystyle g\cdot(a+b)=g\cdot a+g\cdot b,

(104)

where we have used additive notation for the multiplication of Abelian group. Note that
g\cdot 0=0
, where
0
is the group identity of
M
. Simple examples of
M
include
U(1)=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}=\{\alpha|\alpha\in[0,2\pi)\}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\{0,1,2,\dots,n-1\}
with a trivial action
g\cdot a=a
. In the presence of anti-unitary symmetries in
G
and for
M=U(1)
, the group action is given by
g\cdot\alpha={s(g)}\alpha{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)}
, where
s(g)=1
for unitary
g
and
s(g)=-1
for anti-unitary
g
.

A function
w_{n}:G^{n}\rightarrow M
, i.e.,
w_{n}(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n})
with
g_{i}\in G
, is called an
n
-cochain. All
n
-cochains form an Abelian group
\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,M)
under the function multiplication

w_{n}(g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n})=w_{n}^{\prime}(g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n})+w_{n}^{\prime\prime}(g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n})
, which inherits from the multiplication of
M
. The differential map
\mathrm{d}:\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,M)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{n+1}(G,M)
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\text{d}w_{n}(g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{1}\cdot w_{n}(g_{2},g_{3},...,g_{n+1})+(-1)^{n+1}w_{n}(g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{n})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}w_{n}(g_{1},...,g_{i-1},g_{i}g_{i+1},g_{i+2},...,g_{n+1}).

(105)

One can check that the differential operator satisfies the nice property

\text{d}^{2}=1
.

With the differential map, one can define two special types of cochains: an
n
-coboundary is defined as
w_{n}
that satisfies
w_{n}=\mathrm{d}w_{n-1}
for some
w_{n-1}
, and an
n
-cocycle is defined as
w_{n}
that satisfies
\mathrm{d}w_{n}=0
. The
n
-coboundaries form the group
\mathcal{B}^{n}(G,M)
and the
n
-cocycles form the group
\mathcal{Z}^{n}(G,M)
. The property
\text{d}^{2}=1
implies that any coboundary is also a cocycle. Therefore,
\mathcal{B}^{n}(G,M)\subset\mathcal{Z}^{n}(G,M)\subset\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,M)
. Two cocycles
w_{n}
and
w_{n}^{\prime}
are said to be equivalent, or belonging to the same cohomology class, if
w_{n}=w_{n}^{\prime}+\mathrm{d}w_{n-1}
. Then, inequivalent cocycles are classified by the quotient group

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,M)=\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{n}(G,M)}{\mathcal{B}^{n}(G,M)}

(106)

which is called the
n
-th cohomology group of
G
over
M
.

A.2
Cup product

It is useful to define maps between different cochain groups
\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,M)
, beyond the differential map
\mathrm{d}
. In the main text, we have used the cup products and Pontryagin square to simplify some expressions. Here, we give a brief review on the two maps and refer to Ref.
Steenrod 1947
for more general discussions.

The cup product is a map

\displaystyle\cup:\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,M_{1})\times\mathcal{C}^{m}(G,M_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{n+m}(G,M_{3}).

(107)

To define it, we need a bilinear map
B:M_{1}\times M_{2}\rightarrow M_{3}
such that
B(a+a^{\prime},b)=B(a,b)+B(a^{\prime},b)
and
B(a,b+b^{\prime})=B(a,b)+B(a,b^{\prime})
. We will give a specific example of
B
below. Given
B
and two cochains
w_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,M_{1})
and
w_{m}\in\mathcal{C}^{m}(G,M_{2})
, the cup product is defined as

\displaystyle w_{n}\cup w_{m}(g_{1},...,g_{n+m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B[w_{n}(g_{1},...,g_{n}),w_{m}(g_{n+1},...,g_{n+m})].

(108)

Under the differential map, it satisfies

\displaystyle\text{d}(w_{n}\cup w_{m})=\text{d}w_{n}\cup w_{m}+(-1)^{n}w_{n}\cup\text{d}w_{m}.

(109)

This is a very nice property, which implies that if
w_{n}
and
w_{m}
are both cocycles, so is
w_{n}\cup w_{m}
. Moreover, one can show that if either
w_{n}
or
w_{m}
is a coboundary and the other is a cocycle, then
w_{n}\cup w_{m}
is a coboundary. Therefore, the cup product can actually be understood as a cohomological map
\cup:\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,M_{1})\times\mathcal{H}^{m}(G,M_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{n+m}(G,M_{3})
.

One can generalize it to higher cup products. For this work, only the cup-1 product will be used. It is a map of degree
-1
, namely

\displaystyle\cup_{1}:\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,M_{1})\times\mathcal{C}^{m}(G,M_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{n+m-1}(G,M_{3})

(110)

Given a bilinear map
B
, the product
\cup_{1}
is defined by

\displaystyle w_{n}\cup_{1}w_{m}

\displaystyle(g_{1},...,g_{n+m-1})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{(n-i)(m+1)}

\displaystyle B[w_{n}(g_{1},...,g_{i},\prod_{j=1}^{m}g_{i+j},g_{m+i+1}...,g_{n+m-1}),

\displaystyle\quad w_{m}(g_{i+1},...,g_{i+m})].

(111)

Under the differential map, it satisfies

\displaystyle w_{n}\cup w_{m}-(-1)^{nm}w_{m}\cup w_{n}=(-1)^{n+m}\text{d}w_{n}\cup_{1}w_{m}

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{m}w_{n}\cup_{1}\text{d}w_{m}-(-1)^{n+m}\text{d}(w_{n}\cup_{1}w_{m})

(112)

Different from the cup product, we see that even if
w_{n}
and
w_{m}
are cocycles,
w_{n}\cup_{1}w_{m}
might not be a cocycle. Nevertheless, for equivalence classes of cocycles, this relation implies the super-commutative relation
[w_{n}]\cup[w_{m}]=(-1)^{nm}[w_{m}]\cup[w_{n}]
.

In this work, we mostly consider the case
M_{1}=M_{2}=M_{3}=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
with a trivial
G
action. Let
\mathbb{Z}_{N}=\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
and the group multiplication is the usual addition modulo
N
. We take the bilinear map
B
to be

\displaystyle B(a,b)=ab{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ N)},

(113)

where
ab
is the usual multiplication. Then,
w_{n}\cup w_{m}=w_{n}w_{m}
and
w_{n}\cup_{1}w_{m}
becomes

\displaystyle w_{n}\cup_{1}w_{m}(g_{1},...,g_{n+m-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{(n-i)(m+1)}w_{m}(g_{i+1},...,g_{i+m})

\displaystyle\quad\,\,w_{n}(g_{1},...,g_{i},\prod_{j=1}^{m}g_{i+j},g_{m+i+1},...,g_{n+m-1}).

(114)

For 2-cochains
w_{2}
and
v_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle w_{2}\cup_{1}v_{2}(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})=

\displaystyle v_{2}(g_{1},g_{2})w_{2}(g_{1}g_{2},g_{3})

\displaystyle-v_{2}(g_{2},g_{3})w_{2}(g_{1},g_{2}g_{3}).

(115)

where “modulo
N
” is assumed on the right-hand side.

A.3
Pontryagin square

Another cohomological operation used in the main text is the Pontryagin square, denoted as
\mathcal{P}
. It is a map

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{H}^{2m}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2N})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{4m}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{4N}).

(116)

To define
\mathcal{P}
, consider a cocycle
w_{2m}\in\mathcal{Z}^{2m}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2N})
. It can be viewed as a cochain in
\mathcal{C}^{2m}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{4N})
by embedding
\mathbb{Z}_{2N}=\{0,1,\dots,2N-1\}
into
\mathbb{Z}_{4N}=\{0,1,\dots,4N-1\}
. For clarity, let us denote
w_{2m}
as
\hat{w}_{2m}
when it is lifted to a cochain in
\mathcal{C}^{2m}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{4N})
. We have
\hat{w}_{2m}=w_{2m}
(mod
2N
) and
\text{d}\hat{w}_{2m}=2Nc_{2m+1}
, where
c_{2m+1}\in\mathcal{C}^{2m+1}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{4N})
. The Pontryagin square of
w_{2m}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(w_{2m})=\hat{w}_{2m}\cup\hat{w}_{2m}+\hat{w}_{2m}\cup_{1}\text{d}\hat{w}_{2m}.

(117)

According to Eqs.(
109
) and (
112
), we have

\displaystyle\text{d}\mathcal{P}(w_{2m})

\displaystyle=2\hat{w}_{2m}\cup\mathrm{d}\hat{w}_{2m}+\mathrm{d}\hat{w}_{2m}\cup_{1}\mathrm{d}\hat{w}_{2m}

\displaystyle=4N\hat{w}_{2m}c_{2m+1}+4N^{2}c_{2m+1}\cup_{1}c_{2m+1}

\displaystyle=0{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4N)}.

(118)

Accordingly,
\mathcal{P}(w_{2m})
is a cocycle in
\mathcal{Z}^{4m}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{4N})
. In addition, it can be checked that the cohomology class of
\mathcal{P}(w_{2m})
does not change under the coboundary transformation

Appendix C
Evaluating obstructions for Abelian
G

Here we present some calculations related to the
\mathcal{O}_{3}
and
\mathcal{O}_{4}
obstructions for Abelian group
G=\prod_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}}
. Without loss of generality, we take
N_{i}=2^{k_{i}}
,
i=1,2,\dots,K
. We also collect the topological invariants of
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2}),\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
for Abelian group
G
, which are very useful for the calculations in different parts of the paper.

C.1
Topological invariants

Let us discuss some general aspects of cohomology groups
\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
(
n=1,2,3
) and
\mathcal{H}^{4}(G,U(1))
. In particular, we discuss the so-called topological invariants, which are certain combinations of cocycles that are invariant under coboundary transformations. Many of them are discussed in the main text and we summarize them here. For a cochain
\omega\in\mathcal{C}^{n}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, we find it useful to define the differential operator
\hat{\mathrm{d}}
, which has the same expression as
\mathrm{d}
but without taking modulo
2
. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega=\hat{\mathrm{d}}\omega{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}.

(145)

We will use
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{K})
to denote the group elements of
G
, with
a_{i}=0,1,\dots,N_{i}-1
. We will also use
[a_{i}]
to denote “
a_{i}{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ N_{i})}
” for short.

First, the cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})=\prod_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. There are
K
root cocycles

\displaystyle v_{i}(a)=[a_{i}]{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)},

(146)

where
i=1,2,\dots,K
. A general cocycle can be constructed from these root cocycles

v(a)=\sum_{i}q_{i}v_{i}(a),

(147)

where an overall “modulo 2” is assumed, and
q_{i}=0,1
are the parameters for the general 1-cocycle. Equation (
147
) gives a complete parametrization of 1-cocycles in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

Next, the second cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})=\prod_{i}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\prod_{i<j}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. A general 2-cocycle can be generated by root cocycles, which are

\displaystyle w_{i}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{i}}([a_{i}]+[b_{i}]-[a_{i}+b_{i}])=\frac{\hat{\mathrm{d}}v_{i}(a,b)}{N_{i}},

\displaystyle w_{ij}(a,b)

\displaystyle=[a_{i}][b_{j}]=v_{i}\cup v_{j}(a,b),

(148)

where again an overall “modulo 2” is assumed. Note that
w_{ij}
and
w_{ji}
are equivalent cocycles. A general cocycle is given by

w(a,b)=\sum_{i}p_{i}w_{i}(a,b)+\sum_{ij}p_{ij}w_{ij}(a,b)

(149)

where
p_{i},p_{ij}=0,1
. One can define the following complete set of topological invariants:

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{i}-1}w(e_{i},ne_{i}),

\displaystyle\Omega_{ij}

\displaystyle=w(e_{i},e_{j})-w(e_{j},e_{i}),

(150)

where
e_{i}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)
with only the
i
th entry being
1
and others being
0
. One can straightforwardly check that
\Omega_{i}
and
\Omega_{ij}
are invariant under coboundary transformations. We remark that
\Omega_{ij}=-\Omega_{ji}
, so only one of them is independent. For the cocycle in (
149
), we have

\Omega_{i}=p_{i}+\frac{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)p_{ii}}{2},\quad\Omega_{ij}=p_{ij}-p_{ji}.

(151)

We observe that the possible values that
\{\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}\}
can take saturate
|\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})|
, so this is a complete set of topological invariants. By computing the topological invariants of a general cocycle (not necessarily parametrized as in (
149
)), one can easily assert its cohomology class.

The third cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})=\prod_{ij}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\prod_{i<j<k}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. A general cocycle can be generated by the following root cocycles

\displaystyle u_{ij}(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=\frac{[a_{i}]}{N_{j}}([b_{j}]+[c_{j}]-[b_{j}+c_{j}])

\displaystyle=v_{i}\cup w_{j}(a,b,c),

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{ij}(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=([a_{i}]+[b_{i}]-[a_{i}+b_{i}])\frac{[c_{j}]}{N_{i}}

\displaystyle=w_{i}\cup v_{j}(a,b,c),

\displaystyle u_{ijk}(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=[a_{i}][b_{j}][c_{k}]

\displaystyle=v_{i}\cup v_{j}\cup v_{k}(a,b,c).

(152)

This set of root cocycles is over-complete. In particular,
u_{ij}
and
\tilde{u}_{ji}
are equivalent cocycles. We list them here for later convenience. With these generators, a general cocycle is given by

\displaystyle u=\sum_{ij}(t_{ij}u_{ij}+\tilde{t}_{ij}\tilde{u}_{ij})+\sum_{ijk}t_{ijk}u_{ijk}

(153)

where
t_{ij},\tilde{t}_{ij},t_{ijk}=0,1
are the parameters. One can define the following topological invariants: let
\chi_{a}(b,c)=u(a,b,c)-u(b,a,c)+u(b,c,a)
, and define

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{j}-1}\chi_{e_{i}}(e_{j},ne_{j})

\displaystyle\Xi_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\chi_{e_{i}}(e_{j},e_{k})-\chi_{e_{i}}(e_{k},e_{j})

(154)

where again “modulo 2” is assumed. One can check that they are invariant under coboundary transformations. For the cocycle in (
153
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=t_{ij}+\tilde{t}_{ji}+\frac{N_{j}(N_{j}-1)}{2}(t_{ijj}-t_{jij}+t_{jji})

\displaystyle\Xi_{ijk}

\displaystyle=t_{ijk}-t_{ikj}+t_{jki}-t_{jik}+t_{kij}-t_{kji}.

(155)

By varying the parameters, one can see that the possible values that
\{\Xi_{ij},\Xi_{ijk}\}
can take saturate
\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, so this is a complete set of topological invariants. Note that
\Xi_{ij}
and
\Xi_{ji}
are independent and
\Xi_{ijk}
is a fully anti-symmetric tensor. Using topological invariants, it is sufficient to assert the cohomology class that a general 3-cocycle belongs to. In particular, if all these topological invariants are zero, it is a trivial cocycle.

\mathcal{O}_{3}
for
\nu=4k
, which is
\mathcal{O}_{3}=n_{1}\cup\lambda
. We will give the sufficient and necessary conditions on when
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction vanishes for Abelian
G
and show the condition on
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
for ESB to occur for some examples.

First, we give an explicit parameterization of the 1-cocycle
n_{1}
and 2-cocycle
\lambda
. According to Eq. (
147
) and Eq.(
149
), we take the following choices:

\displaystyle n_{1}=\sum_{i}q_{i}v_{i},\quad\lambda=\sum_{i}p_{i}w_{i}+\sum_{ij}p_{ij}w_{ij}

(156)

where
v_{i}
,
w_{i}
and
w_{ij}
are the root cocycles in (
146
) and (
148
). Note that the parametrization of
\lambda
is over-complete. The same parametrization is used in the main text.

Secondly, we make a simplification on the expression of
\mathcal{O}_{3}
such that it can be expressed in terms of the root cocycles given in (
152
). For a cocycle
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, one can show this very helpful relation:

\displaystyle\lambda\cup_{1}\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\mathrm{d}}\lambda.

(157)

(Recall that
\hat{\mathrm{d}}
is defined without taking modulo 2.) At the same time, one can check that
\hat{\mathrm{d}}w_{i}=0
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hat{\mathrm{d}}w_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\mathrm{d}}v_{i}\cup v_{j}+v_{i}\cup\hat{\mathrm{d}}v_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{j}}{2}v_{i}\cup w_{j}+\frac{N_{i}}{2}w_{i}\cup v_{j}.

(158)

Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{3}

\displaystyle=n_{1}\cup\lambda+\lambda\cup_{1}\lambda

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\left(q_{i}p_{j}v_{i}\cup w_{j}+p_{ij}\frac{N_{j}}{2}v_{i}\cup w_{j}+p_{ij}\frac{N_{i}}{2}w_{i}\cup v_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{ijk}q_{i}p_{jk}v_{i}\cup w_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\bigg[\left(q_{i}p_{j}+p_{ij}\frac{N_{j}}{2}\right)u_{ij}+p_{ij}\frac{N_{i}}{2}\tilde{u}_{ij}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{ijk}q_{i}p_{jk}u_{ijk}.

(159)

Comparing to (
153
) and using (
155
), we immediately have

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=q_{i}p_{j}+(p_{ij}+p_{ji})\frac{N_{j}}{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{N_{j}(N_{j}-1)}{2}(q_{i}p_{jj}-q_{j}p_{ij}+q_{j}p_{ji})

\displaystyle\Xi_{ijk}

\displaystyle=q_{i}(p_{jk}-p_{jk})+q_{j}(p_{ki}-p_{ik})+q_{k}(p_{ij}-p_{ji}).

(160)

Making use of the expressions of
\Omega_{i}
and
\Omega_{ij}
in (
151
), we have

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\Omega_{j}+\frac{N_{j}}{2}(q_{j}-1)\Omega_{ij},

\displaystyle\Xi_{ijk}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\Omega_{jk}+q_{j}\Omega_{ki}+q_{k}\Omega_{ij}

(161)

where “
{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}
” is assumed and
N_{i}=2^{k_{i}}
is used. For
\mathcal{O}_{3}=\lambda\cup n_{1}
at
\nu=4k
, the result is very similar:
\Xi_{ijk}
is the same, while
\Xi_{ij}
becomes

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\Omega_{j}+\frac{N_{j}}{2}q_{j}\Omega_{ij}.

(162)

The equations in (
161
) and (
162
) are the main results of the calculations in this subsection.

We can now assert that
\mathcal{O}_{3}
is trivial if and only if
\Xi_{ij}=\Xi_{ijk}=0
for all
i,j,k
. Recall that
\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}
(i.e.,
\lambda
) are given in our problem to generate the fermionic symmetry group
G_{f}
. For given
\{\Omega_{i},\Omega_{ij}\}
, the condition
\Xi_{ij}=\Xi_{ijk}=0
is a set of equations with
\{q_{i}\}
being the unknowns. Then,
G_{f}
is enforced to break if these equations have no solutions. That is, ESB of
G_{f}
occurs if
\mathcal{O}_{3}
is nontrivial regardless of
n_{1}
.

For
\nu=4k
, there is always a solution to
\Xi_{ij}=\Xi_{ijk}=0
:
q_{i}=0
for all
i
, i.e., a trivial
n_{1}
. So,
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction alone cannot lead to ESB. For
\nu=4k+2
, we do have situations that ESB can occur. With straightforward analysis on equations in (
161
), we find a few examples of ESB, which are summarized in Table
6
. Two general properties of (
161
) are: (1) If
\Omega_{ij}=0
for all
i
and
j
and
\Omega_{i}
is nonzero for at least one
i
, say
\Omega_{I}\neq 0
of a fixed
I
, the requirement
\Xi_{iI}=0
gives
q_{i}=0
for all
i
. That is, there is one and only one solution
n_{1}=0
. (2) If
N_{j}=0{\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)}
for all
j
, then
n_{1}=0
is always a solution.

C.3
A result for
\nu=4k+2

We have only considered
\mathcal{O}_{3}
obstruction above. In this subsection, we consider
\mathcal{O}_{4}
in a special case. We show the following corollary for finite Abelian groups.

Corollary 1
.

For 2D fermionic iTOs with
\nu=4k+2
and a finite Abelian group
G
, ESB can not occur if
\lambda
is a type-I 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

Recall that we have defined type-I 2-cocycles in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
as those with
\Omega_{ij}=0
for all
i,j
. If
\Omega_{i}=0
for all
i
, i.e.,
\lambda=0
, then ESB can never occur. If at least one
\Omega_{i}
is nonzero, then, according to the result at the end of Appendix
C.2
, we must have
q_{i}=0
for all
i
to have a vanishing
\mathcal{O}_{3}
. To show that ESB does not occur, it remains to check if
\mathcal{O}_{4}
vanishes when
n_{1}=0
and
\lambda
is type-I.

In that case, the
\mathcal{O}_{4}
obstruction formula in (
91
) is applicable. For type-I
\lambda
, we can write it as
\lambda=\sum_{i}p_{i}\hat{\mathrm{d}}v_{i}/N_{i}
. It leads to two consequences:
\hat{\mathrm{d}}\lambda=0
, and

\displaystyle\lambda\cup\lambda=\sum_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\hat{\mathrm{d}}\left(\frac{v_{i}\cup\hat{\mathrm{d}}v_{j}}{N_{i}N_{j}}\right),

(163)

which means the piece
e^{i\nu\pi\lambda\cup\lambda/8}
is a
U(1)
-valued 4-coboundary. Then, one can easily check that
n_{2}=0
makes the
\mathcal{O}_{4}
in (
91
) a
U(1)
-valued 4-coboundary. Accordingly, we find a solution that
n_{1}=n_{2}=0
such that both
\mathcal{O}_{3}
and
\mathcal{O}_{4}
vanish. Hence, there is no ESB and the corollary is proven.
