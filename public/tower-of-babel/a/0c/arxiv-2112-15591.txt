High-Order Statistical Functional Expansion and Its Application To Some Nonsmooth Problems The work of Cun-Hui Zhang was conducted as a consulting researcher at Baidu Research – 10900 NE 8th St. Bellevue, WA 98004, USA
Fan Zhou 1, Ping Li 1 and Cun-Hui Zhang 2

Abstract
Let 𝒙 j = 𝜽 + 𝜺 j , j = 1 , … , n \boldsymbol{x}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j},j=1,...,n , be observations of an unknown parameter 𝜽 \boldsymbol{\theta} in a Euclidean or separable Hilbert space ℋ {\cal H} , where 𝜺 j {\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j} are noises as random elements in ℋ {\cal H} from a general distribution. We study the estimation of f ⁡ ( 𝜽 ) f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) for a given functional f : ℋ → ℝ f:{\cal H}\rightarrow{{\mathbb{R}}} based on 𝒙 j \boldsymbol{x}_{j} ’s. The key element of our approach is a new method which we call High-Order Degenerate Statistical Expansion. It leverages the use of classical multivariate Taylor expansion and degenerate U U -statistic and yields an elegant explicit formula. In the univariate case of ℋ = ℝ {\cal H}={{\mathbb{R}}} , the formula expresses the error of the proposed estimator as a sum of order k k degenerate U U -products of the noises (with no tied index for each k k -product) with coefficient f ( k ) ​ ( 𝜽 ) / k ! f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/k! and an explicit remainder term in the form of the Riemann-Liouville integral as in the Taylor expansion around the true 𝜽 \boldsymbol{\theta} . For general ℋ {\cal H} , the formula expresses the estimation error in terms of the inner product of f ( k ) ​ ( 𝜽 ) / k ! f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/k! and the average of the tensor products of k k noises with distinct indices and a parallel extension of the remainder term from the univariate case. This makes the proposed method a natural statistical version of the classical Taylor expansion. The proposed estimator can be viewed as a jackknife estimator of an ideal degenerate expansion of f ⁡ ( ⋅ ) f(\cdot) around the true 𝜽 \boldsymbol{\theta} with the degenerate U U -product of the noises, and can be approximated by bootstrap. Thus, the jackknife, bootstrap and Taylor expansion approaches all converge to the proposed estimator. We develop risk bounds for the proposed estimator under proper moment conditions and a central limit theorem under a second moment condition (even in expansions of higher than the second order). We apply this new method to generalize several existing results with smooth and nonsmooth f f to universal 𝜺 j {\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j} ’s with only minimum moment constraints.

A straightforward approach to this problem is to use the plug-in estimator
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
with the sample mean
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}

One may think of

’s covariance operator

1.1
Related works

Several methods were proposed recently to address the problem in the Gaussian shift model.
One of them, developed in a series of works
[
24
,
28
,
27
]
,
is to use an iterative bootstrap technique to correct the bias of the plug-in estimator.
We briefly summarize the idea of iterative bootstrap as follows.
Denote by
{\cal T}
the linear operator given by

{\cal T}g(\boldsymbol{\theta}):={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}g(\boldsymbol{\theta})={\mathbb{E}}g(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}),

where
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
.
Let
{\cal I}
be the identity operator and
{\mathscr{B}}:={\cal T}-{\cal I}
.
To create an estimator
g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with small bias,
it is tempting to solve the integral equation

\displaystyle{\cal T}g(\boldsymbol{\theta})=({\cal I}+{\mathscr{B}})g(\boldsymbol{\theta})=f(\boldsymbol{\theta})

as accurately as possible.
However, solving such an integral equation itself is challenging. Instead, the authors used a natural finite approximation of the Neumann series
({\cal I}+{\mathscr{B}})^{-1}=({\cal I}-{\mathscr{B}}+{\mathscr{B}}^{2}-{\mathscr{B}}^{3}+\dots)
to create the following estimator

(1.2)

f_{k}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}):=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}{\mathscr{B}}^{j}f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})

with

(1.3)

{\mathscr{B}}^{j}f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}):={\mathbb{E}}_{\cdot|\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}\bigg[f^{(j)}\bigg(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\tau_{\ell}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}_{\ell}\bigg)(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}_{1},\dots,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}_{j})\bigg]

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{\cdot|\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is the conditional expectation taking over the i.i.d. random variables

\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{j}\sim U[0,1]
and i.i.d. bootstrap samples
\{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{j}
of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}
.
It was shown that (
1.3
) holds exactly for
{\mathscr{B}}={\cal T}-{\cal I}
so that
the bias of (
1.2
) is exactly
(-1)^{k}{\mathscr{B}}^{k+1}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Intuitively, the main idea is that given
the condition that the bootstrap operator
{\mathscr{B}}
is small,
for relatively small noise
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
and sufficiently smooth
f
,
the bias should also be small. Thus bias reduction can be achieved.
In practice, the expectation in (
1.3
) can be replaced by averaging samples obtained from Monte-Carlo simulations, of which performance can be guaranteed by law of large numbers
[
41
]
.

Another work
[
20
]
compared multiple jackknife, iterative bootstrap and Taylor expansion approaches
in a study of the estimation of

f(\theta)
for a given
f\in C[0,1]
based on Bernoulli
(\theta)
observations.
Although
[
20
]
studied the problem in a very specific classical model,
the methods they used to approach the problem are closely related to ours.
Especially, in Sec. A of their paper, they
proposed a multiple jackknife estimator

(1.4)

\hat{f}_{m}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}C_{k}\frac{n_{k}!}{n!}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n_{k}}\leq n}f\bigg(\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}x_{i_{j}}\bigg),

with sample sizes
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{m}\leq n
and constants
C_{k}
satisfying

\sum_{k=1}^{m}C_{k}/n_{k}^{\rho}=I\{\rho=0\}
for
\rho=0,\ldots,m-1
.
This estimator makes use of
U
-statistics from sub-sampling data to cancel each other’s bias
in order to achieve overall bias-reduction when the bias of
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
is a sum of

\sum_{\rho=1}^{m-1}\hbox{\rm bias}_{\rho}/n^{\rho}
and a higher order term. In Sec. C, they generalized the bias correction technique of first order Taylor expansion to higher orders in an iterative way.
However, as they noted in
[
20
]
, an iterative bias correction into Taylor series of higher orders can be tedious and computationally intensive even in the one dimensional case. As a result, they proposed to use the sample splitting technique and considered the Taylor expansion of
f(\hat{\theta})
at
\theta
:

(1.5)

\hat{f}_{m}:=\sum_{k=0}^{2m-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(\hat{\theta}^{(1)})}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}\hat{\theta^{j}}^{(2)}(-\hat{\theta}^{(1)})^{k-j}

where
\hat{\theta^{j}}^{(2)}
is an unbiased estimator of
\theta^{j}
,
and
\hat{\theta}^{(1)}
and
\hat{\theta^{j}}^{(2)}
are obtained from split samples
independent of each other.

In a series of works
[
11
,
7
,
8
,
6
]
,
the authors studied rate minimax estimation of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
under Gaussian shift model
when

f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{p}

or
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{p}^{p}
.
The main idea of their works is to use Hermite polynomials to approximate the given functional,
possibly with some delicate truncation on the magnitude of each observational coordinate to address sparsity presented in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Based on the seminal work on unbiased estimation
[
23
]
and the fruitful idea of Littlewood-Paley theory,
[
40
]
proposed a Fourier analytical estimator

(1.6)

\hat{f}_{R}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})e^{\langle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle/2n}e^{i\boldsymbol{\zeta}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}d\boldsymbol{\zeta},

where
\Omega:=\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}:\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|\leq R\}\subset{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
is a properly truncated region in the frequency domain. The main idea is that the factor
e^{\langle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle/2n}
in (
1.6
) exactly cancels the characteristic function
{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{i\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{T}\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}}\big]
of the noise which makes
\hat{f}_{R}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
an unbiased
estimator of the analytical part of
f
and yet the remainder can be uniformly small. Thus, overall bias reduction is achieved.

In addition to the setting when
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
resides in a finite-dimensional space, there are also some progress recently in the related problems in nonparametric domain where
\theta:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\rightarrow{{\mathbb{R}}}
belongs to some multivariate density class and
f(\theta)
is a known functional of
\theta
. For instance,
[
5
]
studied the estimation of differential entropy of
\theta

f(\theta)=-\int_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}\theta(\boldsymbol{x})\log\theta(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}

the Kozachenko-Leonenko estimator
[
21
]
by introducing a deliberately constructed weighted version, and proved its efficiency and asymptotic normality for arbitrary

while relaxed the support of
\theta
to be unbounded. In another work,
[
17
]
established the minimax optimal rate of the same problem over certain general Lipschitz balls with smoothness index
s\in(0,2]
.
Their results hold when
\theta
doesn’t need to be bounded away from zero and can have unbounded support. Their approach was based on using kernel smoothing estimator
\theta_{h}
of
\theta
, and then to estimate
f(\theta)
by constructing an estimator of
f(\theta_{h})
with polynomial approximation, Taylor expansion and
U
-statistic techniques.

1.2
Our contribution

Although the methods we mentioned above are versatile and feasible in each of their settings, they are also either computationally intensive when it comes to implementation or limited by the form of the functional and/or the distribution type of the noise. In this article, inspired by (
1.6
), we go back to the classical multivariate Taylor expansion and leverage the use of degenerate
U
-statistic, and propose a new method which we call High-Order Degenerate Statistical Expansion (HODSE). The new method leads to a unified estimator of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with a remarkably neat explicit formula for high-order expansions.
What’s more important, the resulting new estimator miraculously and systematically
cancels the bias and all other non-degenerate terms up to any preassigned order in
estimating the intermediate derivatives when using classical Taylor series to reduce bias,
with an explicit Riemann-Liouville integral formula for the remainder term
as in the analytical Taylor expansion. This makes HODSE a natural
statistical version of the classical Taylor expansion.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we introduce HODSE with the explicit formula for its degenerate expansion,
explain its differences as well as connections to the classical Taylor series which is commonly adopted,
and discuss its connection to jackknife and bootstrap.
In Section 3, we establish upper bounds on the
L_{p}
- risk (
p\geq 1
) for the estimation of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
under Hölder smoothness with only minimal moment constraints on the noise. These generalize the previous results in
[
28
]
under Gaussian shift model using iterative bootstrap which are shown to be asymptotically efficient and minimax optimal when
{\cal H}={{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
and
r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=d
. In Section 4, we establish the asymptotic normality of the HODSE estimator under only finite second moment of the noise, including the cases where higher moments appear in the statistical expansion.
In Section 5, we apply HODSE to the estimation of

f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{p}^{p}/d
in the non-smooth case
0<p\leq 1
to generalize the minimax rate optimal estimation results in the Gaussian shift model
[
11
]
to a wider class of noise distributions. Moreover, it shows that the power of HODSE is not limited to smooth functional estimation, but also it can be applied to non-smooth functional estimation.

To summarize, we proposed a new method that naturally
provides a statistical version of the classical Taylor expansion.
The resulting estimator not only yields a neat and elegant formula, but also can be applied to a broad range of smooth and non-smooth functional estimation problems and thus achieves universality.

2
High-order degenerate statistical expansion

We are interested in the estimation of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with a known function
f
and
a high- or infinite-dimensional unknown parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
based on observations
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
with mean
{\mathbb{E}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{j}]=\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Throughout this section we assume that

f(\cdot):{\cal H}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is
m
times
Fréchet differentiable at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
so that the following expansion holds,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{h})=f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{h}^{\otimes k}\big\rangle_{k}}{k!}+o(\|\boldsymbol{h}\|^{m})

for symmetric
k
-linear forms
f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with finite spectral norm
\big\|f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{\rm S}
,
1\leq k\leq m
.

Consider the estimation of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
based on independent data points
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in{\cal H},1\leq j\leq n
, with
{\mathbb{E}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{j}]=\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}/n
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
The plug-in estimator
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
has the Taylor expansion

(2.1)

\displaystyle f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{\otimes k}\big\rangle_{k}}{k!}+O(1)\|f\|_{(s)}\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|^{s}

with the Hölder norm
\|f\|_{(s)}
in (
1.7
).
When the remainder term is sufficiently small for
1<s\leq 2
,

f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
is asymptotically linear and the CLT holds under the Lindeberg condition.
We note that the Lindeberg condition is needed in our setting
even when
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
are i.i.d. with zero mean and finite variance
because the distribution of
\big\langle f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\big\rangle_{1}
typically changes
when
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
and
d=d_{n}\to\infty
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\mathbb{E}}[n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}]
,

\sigma=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{\rm S}^{1/2}
and
r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\hbox{\rm trace}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})/\sigma^{2}\geq 1
be the effective rank of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
,
where
\trace(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\langle\boldsymbol{I}_{{\cal H}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rangle_{2}
with the identity operator
\boldsymbol{I}_{{\cal H}}
in
{\cal H}
.
The plug-in estimator can be biased
when we use higher order approximations, say
s>2
in (
2.1
), to control the remainder term.
For example, by comparing the usual bounds for the standard deviation of the linear term and
the bias in the second order expansion when
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
’s are i.i.d. copies of an isotropic
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
,

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big\langle f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\big\rangle_{1}^{2}\big]\big\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\otimes f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}/n\big\rangle_{2}^{1/2}\asymp\|f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|\sigma/n^{1/2},\

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big\langle f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{\otimes 2}\big\rangle_{2}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big\rangle_{2}/n\leq\|f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\rm S}\sigma^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})/n,

we find that the bias is typically of smaller order than the linear term when

\displaystyle\|f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\rm S}\big(\sigma r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})/n^{1/2}\big)\ll\|f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|,

which requires
r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\ll n^{1/2}
when
\|f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\rm S}
and
\|f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|

are of the same order.
Thus, bias correction may be needed when
r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\gg n^{1/2}
.

To remove the bias in the case of
s>2
, one may
subtract from
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
some estimator
\widehat{\hbox{\rm bias}_{\,2}}
for

\hbox{\rm bias}_{\,2}=\big\langle f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}^{\otimes 2}/(2n^{2})\big\rangle_{2}
in
the expansion of
\big\langle f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{\otimes 2}\big\rangle_{2}/2
.
For
s\geq 3
, we also want to remove

\big\langle f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}^{\otimes 3}/(6n^{3})\big\rangle_{3}

and the third order bias term in the expansion of
-\widehat{\hbox{\rm bias}_{\,2}}
, so on and so forth.
This approach was discussed in detail in
[
20
]
in the one dimensional case
in the Bernoulli model. As the authors of
[
20
]
noted, even in the one dimensional case,
such an ad hoc process can be tedious and hard to keep track of when dealing with higher-order correction terms. Yet our case is more delicate and can be high dimensional.
One may also work with the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{\otimes k}\big\rangle_{k}}{(-1)^{k}k!}+O(1)\|f\|_{(s)}\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|^{s}

at the observable
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
but similar issues arise when we deal with the unobservable
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{\otimes k}
.

In this paper, we present a unified yet very neat formula for bias correction which was aimed at achieving

(2.2)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}\approx f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}}{k!},

with a remainder term of higher order than
m
, where

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}:=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}\neq\cdots\neq j_{k}\leq n}\frac{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{k}}}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}

are completely degenerate
U
-tensors of order
k
.
As
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}
are completely degenerate,
the bias of (
2.2
),
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big]
,
is identical to the expectation of the remainder term in this expansion
and thus is expected to be of higher order than the
m
-th order.
An estimator of form (
2.2
)
may also achieve variance reduction by removing terms like

\big\langle f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\sum_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{1}}^{\otimes 2}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{2}}/(6n^{3})\big\rangle_{3}

in the expansion of the plug-in estimator
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
.

Given the order
s
of the Hölder smoothness of
f
,
we propose to estimate
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}:=f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{1\leq k\leq m}\frac{\langle f^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}\rangle_{k}}{k!}

with
m=\lceil s\rceil-1
and
U
-statistics

(2.5)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}:=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}\neq\cdots\neq j_{k}\leq n}\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{1}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\otimes\cdots\otimes(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}.

We note that
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(1)}={\bf 0}
so that the sum in (
2.4
) actually runs from
k=2
to
k=m
.

We advocate the use of (
2.4
),
a plug-in estimator of the right-hand side of (
2.2
),
as the proper statistical expansion of the plug-in estimator
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
to higher orders.
A remarkable feature of this natural formula is that the resulting estimator
automatically cancels out the impacts of estimating
f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
by
f^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})

and
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
by
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
on the right-hand side of (
2.2
)
simultaneously for all orders
k=1,\ldots,m
.
Thus, the estimator (
2.4
) can be viewed as a degenerate statistical expansion
of function
f
.
This claim is formally justified by the following proposition which gives an explicit formula
for the remainder term in (
2.2
) when the estimator (
2.4
) is used.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\widehat{f}
be as in (
2.4
) with
n\geq m\geq 2
.
Then, (
2.2
) holds,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}=f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}}{k!}-\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m},

with the
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}
in (
2.3
) for the noise vectors
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\theta}

and with the remainder term

(2.7)

\displaystyle\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}:=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{\langle J^{m-k}\Delta^{(m)},\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{\otimes(m-k)}\otimes\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\rangle_{m}}{(-1)^{m-k}k!},

where
J^{0}h=h(1)
and
J^{\alpha}h=\int_{0}^{1}h(t)(1-t)^{\alpha-1}dt/\Gamma(\alpha)

give the Riemann-Liouville integral operator
for
h:[0,\infty)\to{\cal H}^{\otimes m}
and
\alpha>0
,

\Delta^{(m)}(t):=f^{(m)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+t(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}))-f^{(m)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
and

\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

While the expansion (
2.6
) looks similar to the expansion (
2.1
),
the difference is highly significant in high-order analysis
because
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}
in (
2.3
) are completely degenerate
U
-tensors.
For i.i.d. data,
{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}]={\bf 0}
for all integers
k\geq 1
but

{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\boldsymbol{v}^{\otimes 2k},\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{\otimes 2k}\rangle_{2k}]>0
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0
.
Moreover,
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}^{2}\big]
depends
on
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
only through its covariance operator for all
k
[See (
3.8
)].

We call (
2.4
) High-Order Degenerate Statistical Expansion (HODSE)
as it gives a natural statistical version of the Taylor expansion at
the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in the following sense: The
k
-th order term on the right-hand side of (
2.6
) has
zero mean and a standard deviation of the same form as the absolute value of the
k
-th
order term in the analytical Taylor expansion.
For
f:{{\mathbb{R}}}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
and independent noise with common standard deviation
n^{1/2}\sigma_{n}
,
the standard deviation of the
k
-th order term in (
2.6
) is

(1+o(1))|f^{(k)}(\theta)|\sigma_{n}^{k}/k!

whereas the absolute value of the
k
-th order term in the Taylor series of
f(\theta+t)

is
|f^{(k)}(\theta)|\times|t|^{k}/k!
.

In the following lemma we provide some analytic upper bounds for the remainder term.
For symmetric order
m
tensors
T^{(m)}
, define norms

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|T^{(m)}\|_{m-k,k}:=\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|=\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{k}=1}\langle T^{(m)},\boldsymbol{u}^{\otimes(m-k)}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{m},

where the supreme is taken over
\boldsymbol{u}\in{\cal H}
and order
k
tensors
\boldsymbol{v}
.
In particular,
\|T^{(m)}\|_{m,0}
is no greater than the spectral norm
\|T^{(m)}\|_{\rm S}

and
\|T^{(m)}\|_{0,m}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm

\|T^{(m)}\|_{\rm HS}=\langle T^{(m)},T^{(m)}\rangle_{m}^{1/2}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n\geq m=\lceil s\rceil-1\geq 2
and
\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}
be the remainder term in (
2.7
). Then,

(2.9)

\displaystyle|\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\bigg(\max_{0<t\leq 1}\frac{\|\Delta^{(m)}(t)\|_{m-k,k}}{(t\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|)^{s-m}}\bigg)\frac{\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|^{s-k}\langle\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)},\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\rangle_{k}^{1/2}}{\Gamma(s-k+1)k!}

with the tensor norm
\|\cdot\|_{m-k,k}
in (
2.8
) and

(2.10)

\displaystyle\qquad|\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(\max_{0<t\leq 1}\frac{\|\Delta^{(m)}(t)\|_{\rm S}}{(t\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|)^{s-m}}\bigg)\sum_{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{b}=\ell\leq k\leq m\atop k_{a}\geq 2\,\forall a;b\geq 0}\frac{(C_{k,n}/C_{b,n})C^{(k)}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{b}}}{\Gamma(s-k+1)k!}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{j_{1}\neq\ldots\neq j_{b}}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{1}}\|^{k_{1}}\cdots\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{b}}\|^{k_{b}}\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|^{s-\ell}}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-b+1)n^{\ell-b}}

with
C_{k,n}=n^{k}(n-k)!/n!
satisfying
C_{k,n}\leq\exp((k-1)k/n)

and certain positive integers
C^{(k)}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{b}}
satisfying

r_{k}=\sum_{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{b}=\ell\leq k\atop k_{a}\geq 2\,\forall a;b\geq 0}C^{(k)}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{b}}\leq k!
.

In the above lemma, (
2.9
) is typically sharper than (
2.10
) when
{\cal H}
is of low-dimension,
and vice versa.
For example, when
{\cal H}={{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
,
\Delta^{(m)}(t)
is a
d\times\cdots\times d
tensor,
so that
\|\Delta^{(m)}(t)\|_{m-k,k}\leq d^{k-1}\|\Delta^{(m)}(t)\|_{\rm S}

and (
2.9
) could be sharper when
d^{m-1}\leq m!
.
In (
2.10
), the
U
-tensor
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}
needs to be written
as a sum of rank-one tensors of proper forms to apply the spectrum norm,
and
r_{k}
is the number of different types of such rank-one tensors.
The order of constants
1/\{(m-k)!k!\}
and
r_{k}
in (
2.9
) and (
2.10
),
arising from the explicit remainder formula,
become crucial
when we need to use the estimator (
2.4
) with diverging order
m
,
see for example, Subsections 5.1 and 5.2. In our study of the asymptotic normality in Theorem
4.1
,
both (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) are used respectively under conditions on
the “effective rank” of
f
and the boundedness of the order
m
of the expansion.

2.1
Jackknife and bootstrap

The jackknife and bootstrap approaches converge to the Taylor expansion approach in HODSE.
The following discussion casts the estimator (
2.4
) as a plug-in jackknife estimator of

(2.11)

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}}{k!},

ideally with smaller estimation error for larger
m
.
Let
{\mathbb{E}}^{*}
be the conditional expectation given the data points
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}
in
{\cal H}
, and
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{m}\}
be sampled without replacement from
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}
under
{\mathbb{E}}^{*}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
be the mean of the resampled data and

{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{j}=\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
the resampled noise.
Because

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{*}\big[{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{k}\big]=\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}

with the
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
in (
2.5
), the estimator (
2.4
) can be written as

(2.12)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}}{k!}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{*}\bigg[f(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{k}\big\rangle_{k}}{k!}\bigg].

This can be viewed a jackknife estimator of (
2.11
)
as subsamples of size
k
are used to correct the biases of the estimates
for
\big\langle f^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(\ell)}\big\rangle_{\ell}
based on subsamples of sizes

\ell=1,\ldots,k-1
, and the average
{\mathbb{E}}^{*}
over all permutations is used to achieve noise cancellation.
It is different from the standard Jackknife as the same
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is plugged-in
in the estimation of
f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
to achieve systematic bias cancellation.
Bootstrap can be used to compute the estimator (
2.4
),

(2.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}\approx f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{B=1}^{N_{K}}\frac{\big\langle f^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{k}\big\rangle_{k}^{(B)}}{k!N_{K}},

where
\big\langle f^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{k}\big\rangle_{k}^{(B)},1\leq k\leq m
,
are computed using
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{m}\}^{(B)}
,
and
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{m}\}^{(B)}
are i.i.d. copies of

\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{m}\}
under
{\mathbb{E}}^{*}
.
We note that this bootstrap of the jackknife estimator (
2.12
)
is different from the standard empirical bootstrap
[
15
]
as
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{m}

are sampled without replacement for each bootstrap copy
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{m}\}^{(B)}

but the bootstrap copies
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{*}_{m}\}^{(B)}
for different
B
are sampled with replacement.

It is remarkable that the plug-in jackknife estimator (
2.12
) and its bootstrapped version
(
2.13
), with the population mean
\boldsymbol{\theta}
replaced by the jackknife/bootstrap mean

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
simultaneously in the nonlinear
f^{(k)}(\cdot)
,
automatically yields the non-degenerate expansion (
2.11
) with a remainder
term of higher order than
m
, akin to the automatic Edgeworth expansion adjustments
of resampling methods in the central limit theorem

[
36
,
1
,
38
]
.

3
Risk bounds

We provide in this section upper bounds for the bias and
L_{1}({\mathbb{P}})
and
L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})
risks of the estimator (
2.4
).
Unless otherwise stated, we assume in the rest of the paper that
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}

are independent but not necessarily identically distributed random elements in
{\cal H}
.
Recall that the covariance operator
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

for the noise, noise level
\sigma
and the effective rank of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
are defined respectively as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\bigg],\quad\sigma=\big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm S}^{1/2},\quad r=r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\frac{\trace(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})}{\sigma^{2}}.

Because
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}\big]=0
in the expansion
(
2.6
), the bias of the estimator (
2.4
) is no greater in absolute value than
the
L_{1}({\mathbb{P}})
norm of the remainder term in (
2.7
). In the following theorem, we develop
such bias bounds under a Hölder smoothness condition on
f
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\widehat{f}
be the estimator (
2.4
) with
n\geq m=\lceil s\rceil-1\geq 2

and
\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}
be the remainder term in (
2.7
).
Let
p\geq 1
and
C^{*}_{m,n}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}n^{k}(n-k)!/\{(m-k)!n!\}
. Then,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}\|_{L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})}\leq C_{m,n}^{*}\|f\|_{(s)}\max\bigg\{\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|_{L_{ps}({\mathbb{P}})}^{s},\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg\|^{s/2}_{L_{ps/2}({\mathbb{P}})}\bigg\}

for any (possibly with nonzero mean and dependent) random
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Moreover, if
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
are independent with
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}]={\bf 0}
,
then for some constant
C^{*}_{ps}
depending on
p,s
only

(3.3)

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\widehat{f}\big]-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|\leq\|\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}\|_{L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})}\leq C^{*}_{ps}\|f\|_{(s)}\bigg\{\big\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|_{L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})}^{s}+\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\|_{L_{ps}({\mathbb{P}})}^{ps}}{n^{ps}}\bigg)^{1/p}\bigg\}.

We note that the
L_{1}({\mathbb{P}})
norm of the remainder in (
2.7
)
is sufficient to bound the bias.
The
L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})
bounds in Theorem
3.1
will be useful
in the development below of bounds for the
L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})
risk of
\widehat{f}
.
It follows from the upper bound for
C_{k,n}
in Lemma
2.2
that

C^{*}_{m,n}\leq e^{1+(m-1)m/n}
.

Theorem
3.1
requires
s
-th moment of the noise for bias correction of the same order.
For i.i.d.
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
and
p=1
,
\big\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\|_{L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})}^{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}\|_{L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})}^{2}/n

so that (
3.3
) can be written as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\widehat{f}\big]-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|\leq\|\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m}\|_{L_{1}({\mathbb{P}})}\leq C^{*}_{s}\|f\|_{(s)}\bigg\{\frac{\big\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}\big\|_{L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})}^{s}}{n^{s/2}}+\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}\|_{L_{s}({\mathbb{P}})}^{s}}{n^{s-1}}\bigg\},

and the
s
-th moment term is subsumed into the second moment term
\big\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\big\|_{L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})}^{s}
iff

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}/\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}\|_{L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})}\right\|_{L_{s}({\mathbb{P}})}=O(n^{1/2-1/s}).

In the upper bounds in Theorem
3.1
, we may replace
\|f\|_{(s)}
by
2\|f\|_{(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}}
where

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{h}\|>0}\big\|f^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{h})-f^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{\rm S}/\|\boldsymbol{h}\|^{s-m}

with
m=\lceil s\rceil-1
. As
C^{*}_{s}
is implicit,
Theorem
3.1
is most useful when
{\cal H}
is high- or infinite-dimensional but
s>0
is fixed.

The
L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})
bounds for the remainder in (
3.3
) or (
3.4
)
naturally lead to
L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})
risk bounds for
\widehat{f}

given any
L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})
bound for the completely degenerate
U
variables

\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}
in (
2.6
).
However, as we are interested in the case where bias correction with
s>2
is needed to improve
the naive plug-in estimator
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
, the following
L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})
bounds will be used.

Write the degenerate
U
-variables in the expansion (
2.6
) as a sum of uncorrelated terms,

(3.7)

\displaystyle S_{k}=\big\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}^{(k)}\big\rangle_{k}=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq n}\frac{\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j_{k}}\rangle_{k}}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)/k!}.

For
k\geq 2
and i.i.d.
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
, the variance of
S_{k}
can be explicitly expressed as

(3.8)

\displaystyle\hbox{\rm Var}(S_{k})={\mathbb{E}}\big[S_{k}^{2}\big]=(C_{k,n}k!)V_{k}/n^{k},

with the constant
C_{k,n}=n^{k}(n-k)!/n!\leq e^{(k-1)k/n}
in Lemma
2.2
and

(3.9)

\displaystyle V_{k}=\langle f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\times_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{k}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rangle_{k},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is the covariance operator in (
3.1
)
and
\times_{\ell}
denotes the mode-
\ell
tensor product.
For independent not identically distributed (i.n.i.d.) noise
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\hbox{\rm Var}(S_{k})={\mathbb{E}}\big[S_{k}^{2}\big]\leq(C_{k,n}^{2}k!)V_{k}/n^{k}.

With the tensor spectrum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\rm S}
and the effective rank in (
3.1
),

V_{k}
is bounded by

(3.11)

\displaystyle V_{k}\leq\|f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\rm S}^{2}\sigma^{2k}(r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}))^{k-1}.

The following theorem is based on the
L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})
bound (
3.3
) for the remainder
and the
L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})
bounds for the degenerate
U
-tensors via (
3.11
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
n\geq m=\lceil s\rceil-1\geq 2\geq p\geq 1
and

\widehat{f}
be as in (
2.4
) with
independent observations
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\theta}
with
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}]={\bf 0}
. Then,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\quad\big\|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{C_{k,n}^{2}V_{k}}{n^{k}k!}\bigg)^{1/2}+C^{*}_{s}\|f\|_{(s)}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}r}{n}\bigg)^{s/2}

\displaystyle+C^{*}_{s}\|f\|_{(s)}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\|_{L_{ps}({\mathbb{P}})}^{ps}}{n^{ps}}\bigg)^{1/p}

with the
\sigma
and
r=r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
in (
3.1
),
V_{k}
in (
3.9
),

C_{k,n}\leq e^{(k-1)k/n}
and a constant
C^{*}_{s}
depending on
s
only.
Moreover, if
\max_{2\leq k<s}\|f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\rm S}\leq C_{0}
and
\|f\|_{(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq C_{0}
, then

(3.13)

\displaystyle\quad\big\|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{L_{p}({\mathbb{P}})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(V_{1}/n)^{1/2}+C_{0}C^{*}_{s}\max\big\{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{r}/n,(\sigma^{2}r/n)^{s/2}\big\}

\displaystyle+C_{0}C^{*}_{s}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\|_{L_{ps}({\mathbb{P}})}^{ps}}{n^{ps}}\bigg)^{1/p}.

Theorem
3.2
is proved in Section
6
along with proofs of
(
3.8
), (
3.10
) and (
3.11
).
Compared with
[
28
]
in the literature where
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}
are assumed to be i.i.d. Gaussian,
Theorem
3.2
requires the
ps
-th moment condition in the third component
on the right-hand side of (
3.12
).
In the i.i.d. Gaussian case, we may write
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1}=\sum_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}^{1/2}z_{\ell}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}

with i.i.d.
z_{\ell}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)

via the eigenvalue decomposition
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}
,
so that for even integers
ps\geq 2
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\|_{L_{ps}({\mathbb{P}})}^{ps}}{n^{ps}}=n^{1-ps}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}z_{\ell}^{2}\bigg)^{ps/2}\bigg]\leq n^{1-ps/2}(\sigma^{2}r/n)^{ps/2}(ps-1)!!.

Especially, when
r=o(n)
and we take
p=2
and
s>1
, (
3.14
) is of an asymptotic order smaller than
O((r/n)^{s/2}
). As a result, bound (
3.13
) reproduces the bound on MSE:
\|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}_{L_{2}({\mathbb{P}})}\lesssim(n^{-1}\vee(r/n)^{s})
, which was proved in
[
28
]
under the i.i.d. Gaussian assumption and shown to be minimax optimal under the standard Gaussian shift model.

4
Asymptotic normality

In this section we develop asymptotic normality theory for the proposed estimator (
2.4
)
of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

We have developed upper bounds for the bias and
L_{p}
risk when the

ps
-moment of the noise does not grow too fast. The moment condition is natural for the
L_{p}

risk with expansions of order
s
, and the risk bound can be used to remove higher order terms
in the asymptotic normality analysis. However, such an approach would
require higher moment condition than necessary.
In Theorem
4.1
below, asymptotic normality requires only the Lindeberg
condition on the linear term and a mild condition on the growth rate of the second moment,
provided the Hölder smoothness of
f(\cdot)
at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
In addition to the Hölder smoothness condition in the spectrum norm,
we shall consider its counterpart in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{(s),{\rm HS},\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{h}\|>0}\big\|f^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{h})-f^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{\rm HS}/\|\boldsymbol{h}\|^{s-m}.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
n\geq m=\lceil s\rceil-1\geq 2
and
\widehat{f}
be as in (
2.4
)
based on independent observations
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\cal H}
,
j\leq n
,
with
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}]={\bf 0}
.
Let
V_{1}=\langle f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\otimes f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rangle_{2}
be as in (
3.9
),

\sigma
and
r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
be the noise level and effective rank as in (
3.1
),
and
\|f\|_{(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be as in (
3.6
).
Suppose
\max_{2\leq k<s}\|f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\rm S}\leq C_{0}
and
\|f\|_{(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq C_{0}
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle C_{0}\max\big\{\sigma^{2}r^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})/n,(\sigma^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})/n)^{s/2}\big\}\ll(V_{1}/n)^{1/2},\quad s=O(1),

and the Lindeberg condition holds for
\{\langle f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}\rangle_{1},j\leq n\}
. Then,

(4.2)

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\big(\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\big/V_{1}^{1/2}\Rightarrow{\cal N}\big(0,1\big)\quad\hbox{ as $n\to\infty$.}

The asymptotic normality (
4.2
) still holds when the condition
s=O(1)
is weakened to

s^{2}\leq n
in (
4.1
) and the smoothness condition on
f
is
replaced by
\max_{2\leq k<s}\|f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{\rm S}/d^{k/2}\leq C_{0}
in the spectrum norm and

\|f\|_{(s),{\rm HS},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq C_{0}d^{m/2}
in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm with any fixed positive real number
d
.

In Theorem
4.1
, all quantities, including
{\cal H}
,
f:{\cal H}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
,
\sigma
,
r(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
C_{0}

are allowed to change with
n
.
While

is just a constant in Theorem
4.1
, for
{\cal H}={{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}

we have
\|f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{\rm HS}^{2}\leq\|f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{\rm S}^{2}d^{k-1}

and

because

f^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{x})\times_{k=1}^{m-2}\boldsymbol{e}_{i_{k}}
is a
d\times d
symmetric matrix given
\boldsymbol{x}
and canonical basis
vectors
\boldsymbol{e}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{i_{m-2}}
,
1\leq i_{k}\leq d
.
For general
{\cal H}
,
d
can be viewed as the effective rank of
f(\cdot)
.
Thus, the last statement of Theorem
4.1
asserts that
the boundedness condition
s=O(1)
in (
4.1
) on the order of the expansion
can be replaced by the boundedness condition
d=O(1)
on the effective rank of the functional
f(\cdot)
..

Both components on the left-hand side of the first condition in (

) are needed.
For example, when
C_{0}\asymp 1
and
\sigma\asymp 1
, the component

can be omitted when

but needed when

.
Note that in a previous work
[

]
, asymptotic normality was proved under i.i.d. Gaussian assumption on

’s. Here, only independence is required. Especially, an interesting result of
[
28
]
is that when

and

is sharp for (

5
Estimation of non-smooth additive functions

In this section we consider the estimation of additive functions of the form

(5.1)

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a=1}^{d}f_{0}(\theta_{a}),\quad\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})^{\top},

for a given but possibly non-smooth function
f_{0}(\cdot)
,
based on independent observations
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}=(x_{j,1},\ldots,x_{j,d})^{\top}
with
{\mathbb{E}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{j}]=\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

We allow general
f_{0}
satisfying a smoothness condition of small order,
including for example
f_{0}(x)=|x|^{p}
with small
p>0
.
Such functions are called non-smooth in the literature as their order of smoothness
is much smaller than the order of differentiability used in the analysis.

We assume the following conditions on the noise:

(5.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{j,a}\varepsilon_{j,b}]=0,\ \ 1\leq j\leq n,1\leq a<b\leq d,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[|n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}\varepsilon_{j,a}|^{2k}\big]\leq\sigma_{n}^{2k}2^{k-1}k!,\ \ 1\leq a\leq d,1\leq k\leq\lceil s\rceil,

where
\varepsilon_{j,a}=x_{j,a}-\theta_{a}
is the noise for the

a
-th component of the
j
-th observation, and

\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j},1\leq j\leq n\}
is a Rademacher sequence independent of
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j},1\leq j\leq n\}
.

We may set
\sigma_{n}=1
so that the model matches the Gaussian shift model
where only a single instance of
{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})
is observed.
Typically in this setting,
consistent estimation of a single
f_{0}(\theta_{a})
is infeasible
as there is only one
{\cal N}(\theta_{a},1)
observation,
and the consistent estimation of
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is achieved through high-order
bias correction and noise averaging over data components
a=1,\ldots,d
.
Thus, for
\sigma_{n}\asymp 1
and general
f_{0}
we assume
d\to\infty

and apply HODSE with
m\to\infty
in (
2.4
).
Two important features of our approach become more crucial when
m\to\infty
, namely
(a): the explicit description of the remainder term in Proposition
2.1
;
and (b) sharp control of constant factors in Lemma
2.2

for the analysis of the estimator (
2.4
).
Of course, our approach also allows
\sigma_{n}\to 0
and
\sigma_{n}\to\infty
.

The first line of (
5.2
) asserts that the elements
\varepsilon_{j,a}
of the noise vector
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{j}

are uncorrelated for each
j
in addition to the independence of
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{n}
.
The second line of (
5.2
) can be viewed as a sub-Gaussian condition
which holds when
{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{j,a}^{2k}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|{\cal N}(0,n\sigma_{n}^{2})|^{2k}]
for all positive integers
k\leq s

due to the independence of
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{n}
.
For example, (
5.2
) holds when
\varepsilon_{j,a}
are i.i.d. with
{\mathbb{P}}\{\varepsilon_{j,a}=\pm n^{1/2}\sigma_{n}\}=1/2
.
The i.i.d.
\varepsilon_{j,a}\sim{\cal N}(0,n)
assumption, which implies (
5.2
) with
\sigma_{n}=1
,
is equivalent to the standard Gaussian shift model due to the sufficiency of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\sim{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})
.
However, (
5.2
) is much weaker than the Gaussian shift model
as the components of
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
are only required to be uncorrelated, and non-Gaussian data
and heteroscedasticity are allowed.

In
[
11
]
the authors proved that based on a single
{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})
observation,
the minimax rate for the estimation of the length normalized
\ell_{1}
norm
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{1}/d
is

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\big(\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)^{2}\Big]\asymp(\log d)^{-1}.

We note that the plug-in estimator is inconsistent here as there is only one observation available
and
f_{0}(x)=|x|
is not analytic. As the
{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})
model is a special case of (
5.2
),
our results in this section will extend their upper bound to more general
f_{0}
and
heteroscedastic non-Gaussian data.

Our idea is to first apply kernel smoothing to the function
f_{0}
,

(5.3)

\displaystyle f_{h}(x):=(K_{h}*f_{0})(x)=\int K_{h}(x-t)f_{0}(t)dt,\ \ {\rm with}~~K_{h}(x):=h^{-1}K(x/h),

with proper a kernel
K(x)
and a bandwidth
h>0
, and then apply the proposed (
2.4
) to
estimate the individual
f_{h}(\theta_{a})
,
a=1,\ldots,d
. This leads to the estimator

(5.4)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bigg\{f_{h}(\bar{x}_{a})+\sum_{k=2}^{m}\frac{f_{h}^{(k)}(\bar{x}_{a})\bar{u}_{a}^{(k)}}{k!}\bigg\}

with
\bar{x}_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j,a}/n
and

\bar{u}_{a}^{(k)}=\{(n-k)!/n!\}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}\neq\cdots\neq j_{k}\leq n}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(x_{j_{\ell},a}-\bar{x}_{a})
.

Let
m=s-1\leq n
for some integer
s>2
. By Proposition
2.1
and (
2.9
) of Lemma
2.2
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a=1}^{d}f_{h}(\theta_{a})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\frac{f_{h}^{(k)}(\theta_{a})\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}^{(k)}}{k!}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m,a}

with completely degenerate
\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}^{(k)}=\{(n-k)!/n!\}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}\neq\cdots\neq j_{k}\leq n}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{j_{\ell},a}
and

(5.6)

\displaystyle|\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m,a}|\leq\big\|f_{h}\big\|_{(s)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{|\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}|^{s-k}|\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}^{(k)}|}{(s-k)!k!}

where
\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j,a}
is the average of the
a
-th noise component.

It can be seen from (
5.5
) that
while the kernel smoothing (
5.3
) introduces some bias,
correction of additional bias is achieved through the application of HODSE
in the estimation of individual
f_{h}(\theta_{a})
, and de-noising is
achieved through the averaging in the degenerate
U
-variables
\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}^{(k)}

and over the d-coordinates and terms of different orders in the second term.

By (
5.2
),

\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}^{(k)},1\leq a\leq d,1\leq k\leq m
,
are uncorrelated with each other and

(5.7)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{a=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{f_{h}^{(k)}(\theta_{a})\bar{\varepsilon}_{a}^{(k)}}{d(k!)}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\leq\sum_{a=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{m}C_{k,n}^{2}\frac{|f_{h}^{(k)}(\theta_{a})|^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2k}}{d^{2}k!}

with
C_{k,n}\leq e^{(k-1)k/n}
.
By the second line of (
5.2
), the remainder is bounded by

(5.8)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\hbox{\rm Rem}_{m,a}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\leq C_{s,n}^{2}\|f_{h}\|_{(s)}^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2s}2^{7s-1}/s!.

The benefit of the neat formulas (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) is evident
in this analysis in view of (
5.8
) in the case of
\sigma_{n}\asymp 1
.
It follows from (
5.5
), (
5.7
) and (
5.8
)
that for integers
s
satisfying
s>2
and
2(s-1)s/n\leq\log 2

the error of the estimator (
5.4
) is bounded by

(5.9)

\displaystyle\big\|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}\leq\big\{\hbox{\rm bias}_{h,d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\kappa_{s,h,n,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\}^{1/2}+\|f_{h}\|_{(s)}(2^{7/2}\sigma_{n})^{s}\big/\sqrt{s!}

for the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
risk given by
\|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\big[|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}\big]
,
where

(5.10)

\displaystyle\hbox{\rm bias}_{h,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a=1}^{d}(f_{0}-f_{h})(\theta_{a}),\ \kappa_{t,h,n,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil t\rceil-1}\frac{\|f_{h}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{2}^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2k}}{(d^{2}/2)k!}

are respectively the bias introduced by (
5.3
)
and an upper bound for the variance in (
5.7
).

The convergence rate and sharpness of (
5.9
) would heavily depend on
the smoothness properties of
f_{0}
and
f_{h}
through the choice of the kernel
K(\cdot)
in (
5.3
).
The following theorem provides an explicit
error bound in an “
\alpha
-smooth” scenario, allowing arbitrarily small smoothness index
\alpha
for the function
f_{0}(\cdot)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
s
be an integer satisfying
2<s\leq\sqrt{(n/2)\log 2}
.
Let
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
be as in (
5.1
) and
\widehat{f}
as in (
5.4
) with
m=s-1
.
If (
5.2
) holds, then (
5.9
) holds.
If (
5.2
) holds and

(5.11)

\displaystyle\|f_{0}^{(k)}-f_{h}^{(k)}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\eta_{\alpha}(h)/h^{k},\ 0\leq k<\alpha,\ \ \|f_{h}^{(k)}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\eta_{\alpha}(h)/h^{k},\ \alpha\leq k\leq s,\ \

for certain
\alpha>0
and
\eta_{\alpha}(h)>0

for the

and
f_{h}
in (

) with
(\sigma_{n}/h)^{2}\leq s/(2^{7}e)
, then

(5.12)

\displaystyle\qquad\big\|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big|\hbox{\rm bias}_{h,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|+\eta_{\alpha}(h)\big(s^{-1/4}+\sqrt{2/d}e^{(\sigma_{n}/h)^{2}/2}\big)+\kappa^{1/2}_{\alpha,0,n,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{\alpha}(h)\big(1+s^{-1/4}+\sqrt{2/d}e^{(\sigma_{n}/h)^{2}/2}\big)+\kappa^{1/2}_{\alpha,0,n,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

where
\hbox{\rm bias}_{h,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\kappa_{\alpha,h,n,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

are as defined in (
5.10
).

Corollary 5.2
.

Suppose (
5.2
) and (
5.11
) hold with
\eta_{\alpha}(h)=C_{\alpha}h^{\alpha}

for some constants
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
C_{\alpha}<\infty
.
When
(\sigma_{n}/h)^{2}=\log(d/\log d)
in (
5.12
),

(5.14)

\displaystyle\quad\big\|\widehat{f}-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big|\hbox{\rm bias}_{h,d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|+\big(s^{-1/4}+\sqrt{2/\log d}\big)C_{\alpha}\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}\big/(\log(d/\log d)\big)^{\alpha/2}

(5.15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(C_{\alpha}+o(1)\big)\sigma_{n}^{\alpha}/(\log d)^{\alpha/2}.

We notice that when
\varepsilon_{j,a}
are i.i.d.
{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, we have
\sigma_{n}=\sigma/\sqrt{n}
,
so that for
\alpha=1
the convergence rate in (

is faster than the usual parametric rate
due to the noise averaging over components

.
At high noise level
\sigma_{n}\asymp 1
, consistent estimation of

is still feasible
but the convergence rate is logarithmic for general

.

Interestingly, in (

in the construction of the estimator (

depends on

and

only, not on the noise level

,
while the choice of the bandwidth in (

We also note from the theorem that the risk is typically
dominated by the bias in the kernel smoothing step.
This phenomenon also presents in some other problems with logarithmic convergence rates
such as deconvolution
[
39
,
16
]
.

Theorem
5.1
is proved in Section
6
along with detailed derivations of
(

) and (

.

For

6
Proofs

An order
k
tensor
T^{(k)}
is symmetric if

\displaystyle\langle T^{(k)},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\rangle_{k}=\langle T^{(k)},\boldsymbol{v}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{j_{k}}\rangle_{k}

for all permutations of indices
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
By algebra and the definition of
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
,
for any symmetric tensor
T^{(k)}

(6.1)
