Controllability of continuous networks and a kernel-based learning approximation
Michael Herty, Chiara Segala, Giuseppe Visconti

Abstract
Residual deep neural networks are formulated as interacting particle systems leading to a description through neural differential equations, and, in the case of large input data, through mean-field neural networks. The mean-field description allows also the recast of the training processes as a controllability problem for the solution to the mean-field dynamics. We show theoretical results on the controllability of the linear microscopic and mean-field dynamics through the Hilbert Uniqueness Method and propose a computational approach based on kernel learning methods to solve numerically, and efficiently, the training problem. Further aspects of the structural properties of the mean-field equation will be reviewed.

Preliminaries: Neural differential equations and their mean-field limit

Assume a deep Residual Neural Network (ResNet) with
L
hidden layers and
N_{\kappa}
neurons in each layer
\kappa
. The indices
\kappa=0
and
\kappa=L+1
denote the input layer and the output layer, respectively.

Consider a data-set
\{x_{i}^{0},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
of size
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
M\gg 1
. Let each input data
x_{i}^{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and output or target data
y_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
contain
d\geq 1
features or measurements. The state of the
i
-th input data at the
\kappa
-th layer is denoted by
x_{i}(\kappa)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\kappa}}
, with
x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{0}
and
N_{0}=d
. The final state
x_{i}(L+1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{L+1}}
is called
output
or prediction.

A ResNet propagates each input data
x_{i}^{0}
according to the deterministic dynamics
He2015DeepRL
:

x_{i}(\kappa+1)=A(\kappa)x_{i}(\kappa)+\Delta t\,\sigma\big(w(\kappa)x_{i}(\kappa)+b(\kappa)\big),\quad\kappa=0,\dots,L

(1)

where
\Delta t>0
indicates a (pseudo)-time step,
w(\kappa)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\kappa+1}\times N_{\kappa}}
are the weights and
b(\kappa)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\kappa+1}}
the bias which are the parameters of the network. The matrix
A(\kappa)\in R^{N_{\kappa+1}\times N_{\kappa}}
will be specified later and the function
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the activation function of the neurons applied component-wise in (
1
). Examples
are the identity function
\sigma(x)=x
, the rectified linear unit (ReLU) function
\sigma(x)=\max\{0,x\}
, the sigmoid function
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}
, the hyperbolic tangent function
\sigma(x)=\tanh(x)
or the growing cosine unit (GCU) function
\sigma(x)=x\cos(x)
.

In

the parameters
w
and
b
are chosen by a
training process

aiming to minimize the distance
\ell:{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{L+1}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}_{0}
between given
targets

\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and the predictions:

\min_{(w(\cdot),b(\cdot))}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\ell(x_{i}(L+1)-y_{i}).

(2)

Several methods have been proposed to solve this optimization problem, e.g. stochastic gradient descent
haber2018look
or ensemble Kalman filter
Kovachki2019
;
Watanabe1990
;
Alper
.

2.1
Neural differential equations

In (
1

To recover the time-continuous formulation of (

Assumption 2.1

The number of neurons in each layer is given by
N_{\kappa}=N_{0}=d
,
\forall\,\kappa=1,\dots,L+1

The choice

is possible and, in addition, networks of this type have already been proved to satisfy different formulations of the universal approximation theorem

;
LuLu2020
;

Under Assumption
2.1
equation (

\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\sigma\big(w(t)x_{i}(t)+b(t)\big),\quad t\in[0,T]\\
x_{i}(t_{0})=x_{i}^{0},\end{cases}

(3)

for each
i=1,\dots,M
and time-step
\Delta t
. The system of differential equations (
3

and input data

In the continuous limit, the parameters of the network are time time-dependent functions, the weights

, and the bias

\min_{(w(t),b(t))}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\ell(x_{i}(T)-y_{i})\ \mbox{ subject to }\eqref{eq:micro}.

(4)

2.2
Mean-field neural networks

The computational and memory cost of the training of a neural network, increases with the dimension
M
of the data set. For this reason, an approach based on statistical mechanics leads to the mean-field limit of (
3
) for
M\to\infty
, see
HTV_MFResNet
. The time-dependent probability measure
\mu_{t}:=\mu(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
fulfills the nonlinear transport equation

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)+\nabla_{x}\cdot\Big(\sigma\big(w(t)x+b(t)\big)\mu_{t}(x)\Big)=0,\quad t>0.

(5)

The initial condition
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is obtained as limit (in the sense of measures) of
\lim_{M}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta(x-x_{i,0})
. The well-posedness of equation (
5
), the continuous dependence on
(w,b)
and the convergence of (
3
) to (
5
) in
1
-Wasserstein distance has been established in
HTTV_training
under the following assumptions:

(A1)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sigma\in C^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\ w,b\in C^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}}),

(A2)

\displaystyle\quad\quad|\sigma(x)|\leq C,\ \forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\ \text{ for some constant $C>0$}.

The training problem (
4
) allows for a mean-field description as an optimal transport problem

\min_{(w(t),b(t))}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\tilde{\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{T}(x)\ \mbox{ subject to }\eqref{eq:meso}.

(6)

Here,
\tilde{\ell}
is the mean-field limit of the loss function

\tilde{\ell}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\ell(x-y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y),

(7)

where
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is the probability measure of the target data for
M\to\infty
. The existence of solutions to (
6
) has been established in
HTTV_training
. Note that, the optimal solution
(w,b)^{*}(\cdot;M)
of problem (
4
) depends on the number of particles
M.
It still an open problem if the optimal solutions
(w,b)^{*}(\cdot;M)
also converge to the optimal solution to problem (
6
) for
M\to\infty.

3
Controllability problems

The optimal controls obtained as the solution to
(
4
) or (
6
), respectively, depend on the choice of
\ell.
In this section, we consider a different approach based on controllability. This aims to find a suitable mapping parameterized by
(w,b),
such that it’s possible to map the initial data on the target data. The corresponding definition
Coron
;
Zuazua
for linear time-variant systems is given below:

Definition 1
(Microscopic controllability)

Let
A(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Md\times Md}
and
B(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Md\times d}
be time-dependent matrices, and let
x\in C^{0}([t_{0},T];\mathbb{R}^{Md})
satisfy

\dot{x}=A(t)x+B(t)u,\quad t\in[t_{0},T].

(8)

Then, the linear time-varying control system (
8
) is controllable if, for every
(x^{0},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}\times\mathbb{R}^{Md}
, there exists a control
u\in L^{\infty}((t_{0},T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that the solution
x(t)
of the Cauchy problem defined by (
8
) with initial condition
x(t_{0})=x^{0}
satisfies
x(T)=y
.

The system (
8
) is a particular case of the neural ODEs in the setting when the activation function
\sigma(x)=x
is the identity as shown in the following in Corollary
1
. The controllability in this particular setting relies on the Hilbert Uniqueness Method (HUM), see e.g.
Coron
. We first state the general result before applying it to the system (
3
).

Let
\mathcal{R}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the reachable set, i.e., the set of all states
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Md}
such that there exists
u\in L^{\infty}((t_{0},T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that the solution
x
to the Cauchy problem defined by (
8
) with initial condition
x(t_{0})=0
satisfies
x(T)=y
.
For
\lambda^{T}
given in
\mathbb{R}^{Md}
, we consider the solution
\lambda:[t_{0},T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Md}
of the following adjoint problem:

\dot{\lambda}=-A(t)^{tr}\lambda,\quad\lambda(T)=\lambda^{T},\ t\in[t_{0},T].

(9)

Then, the following holds true:

Proposition 1

Let
u\in L^{2}((t_{0},T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
x:[t_{0},T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Md}
be the solution of the Cauchy problem defined by (
8
) with initial condition
x(t_{0})=0
. Let
\lambda^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}
and let
\lambda:[t_{0},T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Md}
be the solution of the Cauchy problem (
9
). Then

x(T)\cdot\lambda^{T}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}u(t)\cdot B(t)^{tr}\lambda(t)dt.

The proposition allows to define a closed system in terms of
(x,\lambda)
: let
\Gamma
be the map
\Gamma:\lambda^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}\mapsto x(T)\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}
, where
x
is the solution of the Cauchy problem

\dot{x}=A(t)x+B(t)\bar{u}(t),\quad x(t_{0})=0,\quad\bar{u}(t):=B(t)^{tr}\lambda(t).

The latter now allows to state a controllability result, see
Coron
.

Theorem 3.1

Under the assumptions of Proposition
1
the time-varying system (
8
) is controllable, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}=\Gamma(\mathbb{R}^{Md}).

Due to the linearity of the system (
8
), Proposition
1
and Theorem
3.1
generalize to the general initial condition
x(t_{0})=x^{0}
.

By applying Theorem
3.1
to the linear system of neural ODEs, we achieve the exact controllability of the system to an arbitrary final state, namely
x(T)=y
, using the bias
b(t)
as control which can be explicitly computed.

Corollary 1

Consider the neural ODE system (
3
) with
\sigma(x)=x
. Then, for any weights
w(t)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the system is controllable in the sense of Definition
1
for every
(x_{0},y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Md}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{Md}
. Further, the control is given only by the bias
b\in L^{\infty}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Proof

∎Since
\sigma(x)=x
, we note that
x(t)-x^{0}
fulfills a time-variant linear system of the type (
3
) with initial condition
z(0)=0\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Md}.

Then, equation (
3
) for
i=1,\dots,M
is given by system (
8
) using the following definitions

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{Md\times Md}\ni

\displaystyle\quad A(t)=I_{M\times M}\otimes w(t),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{Md\times d}\ni

\displaystyle\quad B(t)=\mathbbm{1}_{M\times 1}\otimes I_{d\times d}.

Then, Theorem
3.1
applies. Note that using the adjoint equation

\dot{\lambda}=-A(t)^{tr}\lambda,\quad\lambda(T)=\lambda^{T},

which fulfills

x(T)\cdot\lambda^{T}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}b(t)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}(t)dt,

allows to characterize the optimal bias by

{b}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}(t).

This concludes the proof.

&\quad-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3d}}\nabla_{x_{1}}\psi(x_{1})\,\bigl((w(t)x_{1}+b(t,x_{2},x_{3})\bigr)\,\bar{\mu}^{M}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\,d{x_{1}}d{x_{2}}d{x_{3}}\\

This shows that
\bar{\mu}^{M}
is a solution in distributional sense, provided that
\dot{x}_{i}(t)-w(t)x_{i}(t)-b(t,x_{i}^{0},y_{i})=0
.

4
A numerical approach for general training based on the kernel-based learning method

The previous results show that the continuous ResNets is controllable if the activation function is linear, see also
Zuazua:neuralODE
;
Osher:controllability
for additional results. This provides in simple cases explicit solutions. However, to also allow for training in the case of nonlinear activation functions and to incorporate the weights, we propose an efficient training method. The computational framework to determine optimal parameters
w
and
b
in continuous ResNets will be based on a kernel method, to approximate the loss function, in both the microscopic case (
4
) and the mean-field limit (
6
).

Kernel methods are supported by a well-developed theory
steinwart2008support
;
shawe2004kernel
, and come with efficient algorithms
scholkopf2002learning
;
williams2006gaussian
;
kanagawa2018gaussian
. From a mathematical point of view, these methods rest on the concept of kernels and function spaces generated by kernels, so-called reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. In recent years, there has been an increasing interest in the application of kernel methods approaches in the context of interacting particle systems, see e.g.
CFiedler2023KRM
.

Define the function
\mathcal{L}
on the space
X:={\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of possible pairs of control
(w,b)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:\ X\ \rightarrow\ {\mathbb{R}}^{+}_{0}\,,\quad(w,b)\ \mapsto\ \mathcal{L}((w,b))\,.

Since the loss function of a typical neural network contains the summation of possibly many target values, different techniques are known to efficiently compute its gradient, e.g. the stochastic gradient method. Here, we propose to approximate
\mathcal{L}
by a function
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
that is fast and simple to evaluate with respect to the parameters
(w,b).
Provided
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
as an accurate approximation
\mathcal{L}
, we expect that training of
\mathcal{L}
can be replaced by a more efficient training of
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}.
The approximation should be consistent on the microscopic and mean-field level.
Therefore, we consider kernel-based methods that have recently been shown to allow for an extension in the case of infinitely many agents (or data points
M
in our case)
CFiedler2023KRM
.

The approximation
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
is given by a weighted sum

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(w,b)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}\,k\left(\,(w,b)\,,\,\bigl(w,b\bigr)_{n}\right),

(22)

where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are coefficients,
\bigl(w,b\bigr)_{1},\ldots,\bigl(w,b\bigr)_{N}\in X
are given values and
k:X\times X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a kernel function, cf. e.g.
scholkopf2001generalized
. To specify the approximation we need to determine suitable choices for
(w,b)_{n}
and then compute the corresponding coefficients
\alpha_{n}
of the approximation. Both will be detailed below.

The set of all functionals
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
represented by the series (
22
) forms a Hilbert space. Hence, the question is closely related to the problem of describing the Hilbert space generated by the kernel
k
, the so-called reproducing kernel Hilbert space.
For the convenience of the reader, some definitions and concepts on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, see
(
steinwart2008support
, Chapter 4)
.

Definition 3

Let
\mathcal{X}\not=\emptyset
be an arbitrary set and
H\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{X}}
a real Hilbert space of functions. Then, the function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is called a kernel, if there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a map
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}

k(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\Phi(x^{\prime}),\Phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

The map
\Phi
is called the feature map and
\mathcal{H}
is the
feature space
.

Definition 4

The mapping
k
is called a
reproducing kernel
(for
H
) if

k(\cdot,x)\in H\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
and if
f(x)=\langle f,k(\cdot,x)\rangle_{H}\quad\forall f\in H,x\in\mathcal{X}.

If a kernel
k
has the previous property, then
k
is said to have the
reproducing property
.

Definition 5

H
is called a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS) if all evaluation functionals are continuous, i.e., if
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
the functionals
\delta_{x}:H\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\;\delta_{x}(f)=f(x)
are bounded.

Finally, we say that
k
is positive definite if for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k(x_{j},x_{i})\geq 0.

Note that only symmetric and positive definite functions
k
can be kernels. We, therefore, use Gaussian kernels with the particular choice

k_{\gamma}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}/2\gamma\right),

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
plays the role of a length scale.
For more details on this kernel and its associated RKHS, we refer to
(
steinwart2008support
, Section 4.4)
.

The idea of the approximation problem (
22
) is as follows. Assume we have
N
possibly noisy measurements of the feature map

\displaystyle z_{i}=\mathcal{L}((w,b)_{i})+\eta,\quad i=1,\dots,N,

(23)

for noise
\eta
. The latter can be distributed e.g.
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).
The regression problem is solved e.g. by support vector machines
steinwart2008support
or Gaussian process estimation
williams2006gaussian
, being two particular kernel methods.
Using kernel methods, problem (
23
) can be solved by
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
belonging to the RKHS
H
given as a solution to a minimization problem for a continuous, strictly convex function
{\bf L}
and
\lambda>0:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}=\mbox{argmin}_{\mathcal{L}\in H}\;{\bf L}\left(\mathcal{L}((w,b)_{1}),\mathcal{L}((w,b)_{2}),\dots,\mathcal{L}((w,b)_{N})\right)+\lambda\|\mathcal{L}\|^{2}_{H}\,,

(24)

with

\displaystyle{\bf L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\|z_{i}-\mathcal{L}((w,b)_{i})\|^{2}_{\Sigma}.

(25)

The norm in (
24
) is given by
\|\mathcal{L}\|_{H}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k\left(\,(w,b)_{i}\,,\,\bigl(w,b\bigr)_{j}\right)
and the parameter
\lambda>0
should be chosen according to a discrepancy principle.

In the numerical tests of sections
4.1
and
4.2
, we consider the noisy free case (i.e.

\Sigma=0
and
\lambda=0
), hence the unknown coefficients
\alpha_{n}
in (
22
), are obtained by interpolation. This leads to the linear system:

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}k\bigl(\,(w,b)_{m},\,(w,b)_{n}\,\bigr)=\mathcal{L}((w,b)_{m}),\qquad m=1,\ldots,N.

The training algorithm we propose consists of determining
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
and considering the optimization of the function
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
with respect to the variables
(w,b)
. Numerically, we apply a gradient descent method and also treat the case of nonlinear activation functions. Since both, the optimization problem, as well as the kernel approximation, allow for a mean–field description. Hence, we expect performance results independent of the number of data points
M
.

5
Conclusion

In this chapter, we have considered controllability problems associated with continuous limits of residual neural networks, both from a theoretical and computational point of view. In particular, the contribution of this work is twofold. We have established the exact controllability of linear neural differential equations and their mean-field reformulation. We have established controllability for bias as control of the system. Then, we proposed a computational framework to efficiently solve the general optimal control problem associated with the training process of a neural network. The numerical approach is based on the reproducing kernel-based method that allows reformulating the control problem as a reduced unconstrained minimization problem. So far, the technique has been applied to static controls only. Future work includes extending the algorithm to time-dependent control using model predictive control.
