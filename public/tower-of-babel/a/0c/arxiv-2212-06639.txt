The extremal point process for branching random walk with stretched exponential displacements Thanks: The research of PD was partially supported by the National Science Centre, Poland (Sonata, grant number 2020/39/D/ST1/00258)
Piotr Dyszewski, Nina Gantert

Abstract
. We investigate a branching random walk where the displacements are independent from the branching mechanism and have a stretched exponential distribution. We describe the positions of the particles in the vicinity of the rightmost particle in terms of point process convergence. As a consequence we give a new limit theorem for the position of the rightmost particle. Our methods rely on providing precise large deviations for sums of i.i.d. random variables with stretched exponential distributions outside the so-called one big jump regime. Keywords. branching random walk, limit theorem, point processes, stretched exponential distribution, precise large deviations AMS 2000 subject classification: 60F10, 60J80, 60G50.



1.
Introduction

Branching random walk (BRW) has been studied for more than forty years and during this time, due to its relation with the KPP equation, a
lot of effort was made to understand the behaviour of its extremes. In the existing literature one can distinguish two different
regimes depending on the laws of the
displacements, however not many results address BRW outside of this two classical cases.
The first one treats displacements with thin tails, i.e. possessing some exponential moments.
In this case the asymptotic behaviour of the extremes of BRW is determined by the contribution coming from many, typical particles,
see
[
12
,
13
,
4
,
2
,
15
]
.
In the second regime one investigates regularly varying steps, in this case the asymptotic behaviour of the extremes of BRW is determined by a few,
atypically big displacements, see
[
9
,
3
]
.
The contrast between these two cases raises the question about a regime in which both big jumps and typical displacements
contribute to the asymptotics
of the extremes of BRW.
It turned out that in the case of stretched exponential displacements one can see a clear transition between the two aforementioned
regimes
[
10
]
. We aim to give a complete picture of the extremes of BRW in this case.

In what follows we study BRW which is a branching process with a spatial component that can be described as follows.
The process will evolve at time
epochs
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
Suppose that at time
n=0
one particle is placed at the origin of the real line
\mathbb{R}
. At time
n=1
the initial particle splits into a
random number
of new particles which are randomly
displaced from their place of birth. We will always assume that all displacements are independent copies of a given random
variable
X
. From now on,
all particles independently evolve in the same way as the initial particle. Denote by

\Lambda_{n}
the point process obtained by putting a unit mass in the position of each particle present in the system at time
n
.
Note that
\Lambda_{n}
is not
necessarily simple, since two particles can occupy the same position.
The sequence
\{\Lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a branching random walk.
We refer to Section
2
for a more detailed set-up and to the recent monograph
[
21
]
for an introduction to the topic.

For a point measure
\eta=\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
a>0
denote the translation of
\eta
and scaling of
\eta
via

\mathfrak{T}_{b}\eta=\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}+b}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathfrak{S}_{a}\eta=\sum_{i}\delta_{ax_{i}}

respectively.
The aim of the present article is a description of the asymptotic behaviour of the extremes of
\{\Lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by finding sequences

\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for which
\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}

has a non-trivial limit in distribution in the sense of point process convergence, which we will make precise in Section
2
.
Such a choice is not unique by any means. We will pick the pair

a_{n}
and
b_{n}
which allows to describe the position of the rightmost particle. In other words, we will work with sequences
a_{n}
and
b_{n}

for which
the limiting configuration of
\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}
does
not contain a sequence diverging to infinity. Under some mild moment conditions on the number of offspring of the initial particle,
the asymptotic behaviour of

\{\Lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is determined mainly
by the law of the displacements, i.e. the law of
X
. In the classical case one works assuming Cramér’s condition

(1)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{s|X|}\right]<\infty\quad\mbox{for some $s>0$}.

Under condition (
1
) the contribution of a single displacement is negligible and the position of the rightmost particle
is concentrated
around its mean which moves at a linear speed.
One can show that there are
constants
{\rm C}_{1},{\rm C}_{2}
such that

\mathfrak{T}_{-{\rm C}_{1}n-{\rm C}_{2}\log n}\Lambda_{n}

converges to a non-trivial limit, see
[
15
]
. If the condition (
1
) is not satisfied, some additional
scaling is necessary.
For example, consider the case of
regularly varying tails, i.e. a random variable
X
such that as
t
tends to infinity

(2)

\mathbb{P}\left[X>t\right]\sim{\rm C}\cdot t^{-\gamma},

for some constants
\gamma,{\rm C}>0
. Here and throughout the article the above relation means that the quotient of the two
quantities tends to one
(see the end of this Section for a precise statement).
The regularly varying distributions follow the so called "one big jump principle" (see (
8
)). Using this fact one can observe a
different limiting behaviour of
\Lambda_{n}
.
Namely, if one denotes by
m>1
the expected
number of children of the initial particle, one can show that

\mathfrak{S}_{m^{-n/\gamma}}\Lambda_{n}

converges in law to a non-trivial limit, see
[
3
]
. The scaling present above is necessary to
compensate the contribution of atypical,
huge displacements which, in contrast to
the previous case, is not negligible.

We will investigate an intermediate case between (
1
) and (
2
), namely stretched exponential,
or Weibull, displacements
with tails of the form

\mathbb{P}\left[X>t\right]\sim e^{-t^{r}\ell(t)},

for
r\in(0,1)
and a slowly varying function
\ell
. Recall that a function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
is slowly varying (at infinity)
if for any
constant
{\rm C}>0
,
\ell({\rm C}t)\sim\ell(t)
as
t\to\infty
.
We will show in our main result that

\mathfrak{S}_{n^{1-1/r}\ell_{2}(n)}\mathfrak{T}_{-n^{1/r}\ell_{1}(n)}\Lambda_{n}

converges for an appropriate choice of slowly varying functions
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
. Moreover we will provide a detailed asymptotic expansion of

n^{1/r}\ell_{1}(n)
.
As a consequence one gets a new limit theorem for
M_{n}
, the position of the rightmost particle in generation
n
.
Namely, we will infer that

n^{1-1/r}\ell_{2}(n)\left(M_{n}-n^{1/r}\ell_{1}(n)\right)

converges in distribution to a random shift of the Gumbel law.
Such results were obtained recently for constant
\ell
in the case
r\leq 2/3
, see
[
10
]
.
Our proof relies on precise large deviation estimates for sums of i.i.d. random variables with stretched exponential tails. Since the corresponding
results established in the
sixties
[
17
,
18
]
were too
implicit for our needs, we revisit the problem and provide a more explicit description of the large deviation probabilities on the
polynomial scale.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give a precise description of the model and our assumptions.
Our main results, Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
, are presented in
Section
3
.
In Sections
4
and
5
we present the main steps of our arguments, namely trimming and
decoupling of the random weighted tree.
Lastly, in Section
6
we give the final arguments of our proof and in Section
7
we prove precise large deviations for sums of
stretched exponential random variables,
which may be of independent interest.

Throughout the article we write
g(t)\sim f(t)
for two functions
f,g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
whenever

\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)/g(t)=1

and we write
f(t)\gg g(t)
if
g(t)=o(f(t))
, i.e. if
f(t)/g(t)\to 0
.
We will denote by
{\rm C}
positive constants whose value is not important for us.
Note that the value of
"{\rm C}"
may change from line to line.

2.
Preliminaries

Let us now give a precise definition of our model. Since we will assume that the displacements and the reproduction mechanism are independent,
we will introduce both of these components separately.
If we denote by
Z_{n}=\Lambda_{n}(\mathbb{R})
the number of particles present in the system at time
n
, then the sequence

Z=\{Z_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a Galton-
Watson process starting from

Z_{0}=1
, i.e. with one particle, and a reproduction law with mean

m=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}\right].

It is well known from the classical literature, see
[
1
, Theorem I.A.5.1]
, that if
\mathbb{P}[Z_{1}=1]
is strictly less than
1
,

Z
survives with positive probability, that is

\mathbb{P}\left[\forall\>n\geq 0,\>Z_{n}>0\right]>0

if and only if
m>1
. Since our objects become trivial on the set of extinction of the underlying Galton-Watson process, one can work under the
conditional probability

\mathbb{P}^{*}\left[\>\cdot\>\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\>\cdot\>|\>\forall\>n\in\mathbb{N},\>Z_{n}>0\>\right].

In fact one can recast our main results in terms of weak convergence under the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{*}
. We will however, for notational simplicity,
continue to work under
\mathbb{P}
.
Under a mild integrability assumption, known as the Kesten-Stigum condition given below, the exponential growth rate of
Z
is strictly
positive on the event of survival (see (
3
) below).

Assumption 1
.

We have
\mathbb{P}[Z_{1}=1]<1
and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}\log^{+}Z_{1}]<\infty
and the Galton-Watson process
Z
is supercritical, that is
m>1
.

Note that by the martingale convergence theorem there is a random variable
W
such that
\mathbb{P}
- almost surely,

(3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}m^{-n}Z_{n}=W.

If Assumption
1
is satisfied then the convergence holds also in
L^{1}
and moreover
\mathbb{P}^{*}[W>0]=1
,
see for example
[
21
, Chapter 2]
. This means that

\mathbb{P}[W=0]=\mathbb{P}[\exists n\in\mathbb{N},\>Z_{n}=0].

The random variable
W
will play a crucial role in our results: it will be a shift parameter in the limit of
\Lambda_{n}
.

We will now describe the branching structure in more detail and introduce the displacements. Denote by

\mathbb{T}\subseteq\mathbb{U}=\{\emptyset\}\cup\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{N}^{n}
the Galton-Watson tree corresponding to

Z
with Ulam-Harris labelling. That is, label the initial particle with
\emptyset
and its children with

\{1,2,\ldots,Z_{1}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
and for each particle with a label
w\in\mathbb{T}
, label its children with labels of the form

(w,1),(w,2),\ldots,(w,N(w))
, where
N(w)
is the random variable denoting the number of children of
the individual labelled with
w
. Write
|w|=n
if
w
is a label of a particle from generation
w
, in other words if
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
Denote by
(\emptyset,w]
the unique vertex path from
\emptyset
to
w

(excluding
\emptyset
and including
w
) and write
w\wedge z
to denote
the last common ancestor of
w
and
z
, i.e. the unique element of
\mathbb{T}
for which

[\emptyset,w]\cap[\emptyset,z]=[\emptyset,w\wedge z]
.
Finally, write
w\leq z
whenever
w
is an ancestor of

z
, that is
w\in[\emptyset,z]
and
\mathbb{T}_{n}
for the particles from the
n
th generation present in the system, i.e.

\mathbb{T}_{n}=\{w\in\mathbb{T}\>:\>|w|=n\}
.

To model the displacements consider a family
\{X_{w}\>:\>w\in\mathbb{U}\}
of independent copies of
X
which are independent of
Z
.
The random variable
X_{w}
represents the displacement of the particle with label
w

from its place of birth. The position
of the particle with label
w
is therefore represented via

V(w)=\sum_{z\in(\emptyset,w]}X_{z}.

To study the collection
\{V(w)\}_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
define a sequence of point processes by

\Lambda_{n}=\sum_{|w|=n}\delta_{V(w)}.

We call the sequence
\{\Lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a branching random walk. We will regard
\Lambda_{n}
as a point process, that is a random element of

\mathcal{M}_{p}=\mathcal{M}_{p}((-\infty,\infty])
, the space of point measures on
(-\infty,\infty]
equipped with the topology
of vague convergence. Here
(-\infty,+\infty]
denotes the homomorphic image of
(0,1]
where closed neighbourhoods of
+\infty
are compact.
Recall that
\eta
is a point measure, i.e. an element of
\mathcal{M}_{p}
, if it can be written in the form

\eta=\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
for some sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i}\subset(-\infty,\infty]
without an accumulation point.
We refer to
[
19
, Chapter 3.1]
for a more
in-depth description of
\mathcal{M}_{p}
.
We equip
\mathcal{M}_{p}
with the topology of vague convergence, and say
that
\eta_{n}\to\eta
in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
(or vaguely) if for any function
f
from the set
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{+}=\mathcal{C}_{K}^{+}((-\infty,\infty])
of nonnegative,
continuous functions
with compact
support on
(-\infty,\infty]
,
\int f(x)\eta_{n}({\rm d}x)\to\int f(x)\eta({\rm d}x)
.
In our results we use weak convergence of random elements of

\mathcal{M}_{p}
(denoted by
\Rightarrow
), which is equivalent to the following condition, see
[
19
, Proposition 3.19]
.
For point processes
\Theta_{n}
,
\Theta
we have

\Theta_{n}\Rightarrow\Theta\quad\mbox{ in }\mathcal{M}_{p},

if and only if for any
f\in\mathcal{C}_{K}^{+}
,

\int f\left(x\right)\>\Theta_{n}({\rm d}x)\overset{d}{\to}\int f(x)\>\Theta({\rm d}x),

where
\overset{d}{\to}
denotes the convergence in law of random variables.
Our goal is to study weak convergence of

\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}=\sum_{|w|=n}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}(V(w)-b_{n})}

in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
. Aiming to find a correct choice of
b_{n}
’s and
a_{n}
’s we first note that the convergence of

\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}
in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
describes the behaviour of
\Lambda_{n}

near its right frontier, that is the location of the rightmost particle, which we can write as

(4)

M_{n}=\max_{|w|=n}V(w)

with the convention that the maximum of an empty set is
-\infty
.
The first order of magnitude of
M_{n}
can be predicted using the first moment method.
Take
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
to be i.i.d. copies of
X
and let
S_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}
denote the corresponding random walk.
For a sequence of real numbers
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
write

(5)

\mathbb{P}\left[M_{n}>x_{n}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\exists w\in\mathbb{T}_{n},\>V(w)>x_{n}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]\mathbb{P}[S_{n}>x_{n}]=m^{n}\mathbb{P}[S_{n}>x_{n}]\,.

For the estimate (
5
) to give any non-trivial information about the asymptotics of
M_{n}
, one needs to choose the sequence

\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the probabilities
\mathbb{P}[S_{n}>x_{n}]
decay as
m^{-n}
, that is
exponentially fast. Here large deviation estimates for the random walk
\{S_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
come into play.

In the classical case, under Cramér’s condition (
1
), it is well known that if one takes the sequence of the form

x_{n}=\rho n
for

\rho>\mathbb{E}[X]
, by the Bahadur-Ranga-Rao theorem,
see
[
7
, Theorem 3.7.4]
,

(6)

\mathbb{P}[S_{n}>\rho n]\sim{\rm C}(\rho)n^{-1/2}e^{-\psi^{*}(\rho)n},

where
\psi^{*}(\rho)=\sup_{s}\left[s\rho-\psi(s)\right]
, with
\psi(t)=\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right]
and a constant
{\rm C}(\rho)

depending on
\rho
.
Provided that
\rho<\sup\{\psi^{\prime}(s)\>:\>\psi(s)<\infty\}
, under some mild regularity assumptions, there exists

s_{\rho}
such that
\psi^{\prime}(s_{\rho})=\rho
and then
\psi^{*}(\rho)=\rho s_{\rho}-\psi(s_{\rho})
.
Now, our estimate for
\mathbb{P}[M_{n}>x_{n}]
suggests that one should find a constant
\rho
such that

\psi^{*}(\rho)=\rho s_{\rho}-\psi(s_{\rho})=\log m
.
Then one could expect that, after establishing the lower bound corresponding to (
5
),

\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}/n={\rm C}_{1}=\rho\qquad\mathbb{P}^{*}\text{- a.s. }

This is indeed true and dates back to
[
12
,
4
,
13
]
.
Since the seventies a lot of effort was devoted to a
precise description of the asymptotics of
M_{n}
with the ultimate result stating that, with
{\rm C}_{2}=3/(2s_{\rho})
,

M_{n}-{\rm C}_{1}n-{\rm C}_{2}\log n

converges in distribution to a random shift of the Gumbel law, see
[
2
]
. One can in fact argue that in this case
there is a relatively small portion of the
particles from generation
n
in the neighbourhood of
M_{n}
whose children are most likely to be the rightmost one in
generation
n+1
(see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
BRW with steps distributed uniformly on the interval (-1/2, 1/2) (left) and regularly varying steps with index
\gamma=2
(right).
The red lines indicate the displacements of the rightmost particles from its place of birth.

This is the scale to study the extremes of BRW. Indeed the point process

(7)

\mathfrak{T}_{-{\rm C}_{1}n-{\rm C}_{2}\log n}\Lambda_{n}

has a weak limit in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
, see
[
15
]
for details. It turns out that the limiting measure of (
7
)
is a randomly shifted
decorated Poisson process
[
23
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

\Theta
is a decorated Poisson point process
of intensity
v
and decoration
D

if
\Theta
is distributed as
\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathfrak{T}_{\zeta_{i}}D_{i}
where
\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{\zeta_{i}}
is a Poisson point
process with
intensity
v
and
D_{i}
are i.i.d. copies of a point process
D
.

Definition 2.2
.

\Theta
is a randomly shifted decorated Poisson point process
of intensity
v
, decoration
D
and shift
S

if
\Theta
is distributed as
\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathfrak{T}_{\zeta_{i}+S}D_{i}
where
\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{\zeta_{i}}
is a Poisson point
process with
intensity
v
and
D_{i}
are i.i.d. copies of a point process
D
. One then writes

\Theta\sim{\rm SDPPP}(v,D,S)
.

The weak limit of (
7
) is
{\rm SDPPP}({\rm C}_{3}e^{-x}{\rm d}x,N,-\log(D_{\infty}))
for some positive
{\rm C}_{3}
,
some point process
N
and
D_{\infty}
denoting the a.s. limit of the so-called
derivative martingale associated with the BRW
[
21
, Chapter 5]
.

The behaviour of
M_{n}
under (
2
) is very different. In this case the law of
X
obeys the "one big jump principle", that is

(8)

\mathbb{P}[S_{n}>x_{n}]\sim n\mathbb{P}[X>x_{n}]

provided that
x_{n}
is large enough (for example
x_{n}\gg\sqrt{n\log n}
if
\gamma>2
).
Going back to the estimate (
5
) one can expect, taking into account (
8
)
and considering
x_{n}=m^{n/\gamma}
, that

m^{-n/\gamma}M_{n}

converges in law. Indeed, this sequence converges in law to a random shift of the Fréchet distribution, see
[
9
]
.
In this case the rightmost particles are most likely to originate from the bulk of the population, i.e. the exponential number of
typically placed particles (see Figure
1
).
Similarly as before, one has convergence of the associated point process and one can show that
after an appropriate scaling
\Lambda_{n}
converges to so-called Cox cluster process, see
[
3
]
.
Due to the similarity with our main result we now describe the limiting point process in detail.
Assume for simplicity that the displacements are positive and let
\{T_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
be a collection of i.i.d. random variables with
common distribution given by

(9)

\mathbb{P}[T_{1}=k]=\frac{1}{\upsilon}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}m^{-i}\mathbb{P}[Z_{i}=k],\quad k=1,2,\ldots,

where

(10)

\upsilon=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}m^{-i}\mathbb{P}[Z_{i}>0]\,.

Next let
\{g_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the points of a Poisson point process with intensity

\gamma x^{-\gamma-1}\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x){\rm d}x
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{m^{-n/\gamma}}\Lambda_{n}\Rightarrow\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}T_{l}\delta_{(\upsilon W)^{1/\gamma}g_{l}},

in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
, where
W
is given by (
3
). The coefficients
\{T_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
represent the number of
descendants of particles that made a big jump.
As we will see, the limiting point process in the case of stretched exponential displacements has a very similar representation.

3.
Main result

We turn our attention to a case between (
1
) and (
2
), namely the case where the displacements have a
stretched exponential tail.
We will impose some regularity condition on the regularly varying function
R(x)=x^{r}\ell(x)
that appears in the tail of the displacement law.
One can usually circumvent this kind of
restrictions using the smooth variation theorem
[
5
, Theorem 1.8.2]
. However this is not sufficient for our
proposes since we would
need an approximation up to a certain order. We will thus impose sufficient smoothness on
R
from the beginning.
From now on we will assume that the law of the displacements is given as follows.

Assumption 2
.

The random variable
X
is centred
(\mathbb{E}[X]=0)
, has variance
1

(\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]=1)
and has a stretched exponential
upper tail with index

r\in(0,1)
, that is for some positive
x_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}[X>x]=a(x)e^{-R(x)},\qquad x>x_{0},

where
a,R\colon(x_{0},+\infty)\to[0,\infty)
,
a(x)\to a>0
as
x\to\infty
and
R
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}(x_{0},+\infty)
function
such that
R^{\prime}
is regularly varying with index
r-1
and differentiable with
R^{\prime\prime}(x)=O(x^{r-2+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.
Moreover we assume that

R(x)x^{-d}
is eventually
decreasing for some
d\in(0,1)
and if
r>2/3
we assume additionally that
R(x)x^{-1/2-\epsilon}
is eventually
increasing and
R(x)x^{-1+\epsilon}

is eventually decreasing for some
\epsilon>0
. Further, suppose that

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{k}]<\infty

for all
k\leq\left\lfloor(2-r)/(1-r)\right\rfloor
.

We note right away that since
a(\cdot)
is assumed to be bounded away from zero, the condition regarding regularity of
R

excludes discrete random variables,
i.e. taking values in a closed, countable set.
Recall that
R^{\prime}
is regularly varying at infinity with index
r-1
, if it can be written in the form

R^{\prime}(x)=x^{r-1}\ell_{R^{\prime}}(x),\quad x>x_{0}

for some slowly varying function
\ell_{R^{\prime}}
.
By Karamata’s Theorem
[
19
, Theorem 0.6]
this implies that
R
is also regularly varying
with index
r
, i.e. it is of the form

R(x)=x^{r}\ell_{R}(x),\qquad x>x_{0}

for some slowly varying function
\ell_{R}
. There is a natural relation between
\ell_{R}
and
\ell_{R^{\prime}}
, but it will be of
little importance for us and
thus we refrain from stating it explicitly.
Note that under Assumption
2
,
R^{\prime\prime}(x)/R^{\prime}(x)^{2}\to 0
as
x\to\infty
and thus
e^{-R(x)}
is a Von Mises function with an auxiliary
function
1/R^{\prime}(x)

[
19
, Proposition 1.1]
.
This further implies that under Assumption
2

the distribution of
X
lies in the max-domain of attraction of the Gumbel law
[
19
, Proposition 1.4]
.
We will use this fact on the exponential scale. For sufficiently large
n
we put

(11)

d_{n}=\inf\{x\in(x_{0},\infty)\>:\>R(x)>n\log m\}.

Note that by continuity of

, for sufficiently large

is the smallest solution to
R(d_{n})=n\log m
.
The sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is regularly varying with index
1/r
, i.e.

d_{n}=n^{1/r}\ell_{d}(n)

for some slowly varying function
\ell_{d}

[
19

. Let also

(12)

a_{n}=1/R^{\prime}(d_{n})

for

large enough.
Since a composition of two regularly varying function is regularly varying,
a_{n}
admits the representation

(13)

a_{n}=n^{1/r-1}\ell_{a}(n)

for some slowly varying function
\ell_{a}
. Extreme value theory asserts that, under Assumption
2
, the maximum of

m^{n}
independent copies of
X
shifted by
d_{n}
and
scaled by
a_{n}
converges in distribution to the Gumbel
law, that is for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}[X_{k}\leq d_{n}+a_{n}x,\>k\leq m^{n}]\to e^{-ae^{-x}}.

Note that here one uses the fact that
R^{\prime\prime}(x)/(R^{\prime}(x))^{2}\to 0
as
x\to\infty
. As we will soon see, the sequences

\{d_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
will drive the centring and
scaling for
\Lambda_{n}
. In fact the latter sequence is the scaling for
\Lambda_{n}
.

Large deviations for random walks under Assumption
2
date back to the late sixties,
see
[
17
,
18
,
20
,
14
]
or
[
8
]
for a
general set-up.
As it turns out (

) is satisfied also in this case provided that

x_{n}\gg n^{1/(2-2r)+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Such deviations are said to lie in the one big jump domain.
Once again the estimate (
5
)
suggests that in this case
M_{n}
should be of order

d_{n}=n^{1/r}\ell_{d}(n)
.
This causes some technical problems since
d_{n}\gg n^{1/(2-2r)+\varepsilon}
if and only if
r<2/3
.
Indeed for
r\geq 2/3
and
x_{n}
of the order
d_{n}
, the asymptotic of
\mathbb{P}[S_{n}>x_{n}]
differs from the one of

n\mathbb{P}[X>x_{n}]
. However the latter still provides the leading term in the asymptotic of the former. More precisely, for any
r\in(0,1)
,

(14)

\log\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n}>d_{n}\right]=-(\log m)n(1+o(1)),

see
[
11
, Theorem 3]
. In the sequel we will revisit this problem to provide a new description of the classical
results
[
17
]
on the precise asymptotic of
\mathbb{P}[S_{n}>x_{n}]
with

x_{n}\sim d_{n}
. The logarithmic asymptotics (
14
) suffices to predict the law of large numbers for
M_{n}
.
Indeed, as shown in
[
11
]
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}/d_{n}=1\qquad\mathbb{P}^{*}-\text{ a.s. }

The behaviour of
M_{n}-d_{n}
was recently studied in
[
10
]
for the special case
R(x)=\lambda x^{r}
for some
\lambda>0
.
In this case

d_{n}=((\log m)/\lambda)^{1/r}n^{1/r}\quad\mbox{and}\quad a_{n}=r((\log m)/\lambda)^{1/r-1}n^{1/r-1}.

It transpires that for
r\leq 2/3
the rightmost particle is most likely to originate from the bulk of the population (see Figure
2
)
and for
r>2/3
it typically originates from a subexponential number of particles that deviate from the bulk (located around
O(n^{2-1/r})
),
see Figure
2
. This in turn leads to a more balanced behaviour of
M_{n}
for big values of
r\in(0,1)
.

BRW with stretched exponential steps with

(left) and

More precisely, for this choice of

we have, for

,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{M_{n}-((\log m)/\lambda)^{1/r}n^{1/r}}{n^{2-1/r}}=\frac{r\lambda^{1/r}(\log m)^{1-1/r}}{2}\quad\mathbb{P}^{*}-\text{ a.s.}

and for
r\leq 2/3
, with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{*}
,

\frac{M_{n}-((\log m)/\lambda)^{1/r}n^{1/r}}{n^{1/r-1}}

converges in distribution to a random shift of the Gumbel law
[
10
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Here, we extend these results to distributions satisfying Assumption
2

and prove point process convergence.
The limit object of
\Lambda_{n}
will be described with the help of an auxiliary polynomial. However it comes into play only in the case

.
Let the sequence of cumulants of
X
,
\{k_{j}\}_{j\leq\kappa}
be defined recursively via
k_{1}=0
and for

,

(15)

k_{j}=\mathbb{E}\left[X^{j}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\binom{j-1}{i}k_{j-i}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{i}\right].

Define the truncated cumulant generating function via

(16)

K(x)=\sum_{j=2}^{\kappa}\frac{k_{j}}{j!}x^{j},\qquad\kappa=\left\lfloor\frac{2-r}{1-r}\right\rfloor.

The name truncated cumulant generating function will become clear after we state Lemma
4.1
below.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
consider a function
\Psi_{n}
given for
z\in\mathbb{R}
and sufficiently large

via

\Psi_{n}(z)=\inf_{s\in[0,1]}\{R(d_{n}+z-nK^{\prime}(s))+n(sK^{\prime}(s)-K(s))\}.

One can then check that
\Psi_{n}
is a nondecreasing and continuous function such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Psi_{n}(z)\to\infty

as
z\to\infty
.
Finally, since
\Psi_{n}(0)\leq R(d_{n})
, for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can define
\tau_{n}
to be the smallest real number solving

(17)

\Psi(\tau_{n})=R(d_{n}).

By a direct calculation using the regular variation of
R
and
R^{\prime}
, one gets that for some slowly varying function
\ell
,

\tau_{n}=nR^{\prime}(d_{n})/2+O(n^{3-2/r}\ell(n)).

It follows that
\tau_{n}
is regularly varying with index
2-1/r
and thus, recalling (
13
),
\tau_{n}=o(a_{n})
if
r<2/3
.
We will now describe the limiting measure. Let
\{T_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. with distribution given in (
9
) and take

\{\iota_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to be the points of a Poisson point process with intensity
ae^{-x}{\rm d}x
. Define
a random element of
\mathcal{M}_{p}
by

(18)

\Lambda=\mathds{1}_{\{W>0\}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T_{k}\delta_{\iota_{k}-\log(\upsilon W)},

recalling (
10
).
Then
\Lambda
is a Cox cluster process with the Laplace functional given via

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int f(x)\>\Lambda({\rm d}x)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-aW\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}m^{-j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\left[1-e^{-Z_{j}f(x)}\right]e^{-x}{\rm d}x\right\}\right]

for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{+}_{K}
. Note that even though
W
is the limiting random variable of
Z_{j}/m^{j}
, the
Z_{j}
’s present in the above formula
can be taken independent from
W
due to the presence of the expectation in the exponent. If we denote by

z\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
the probability generating function of the underlying reproduction law

z(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[s^{Z_{1}}\right]

then a standard application of the branching property yields that the probability generating function of
Z_{n}
is an
n
-fold composition

\mathbb{E}\left[s^{Z_{n}}\right]=z^{(\circ n)}(s)=z\circ z\circ\ldots\circ z(s),\qquad s\in[0,1].

The formula for the Laplace functional of
\Lambda
can be thus recast as

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int f(x)\>\Lambda({\rm d}x)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-aW\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}m^{-j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(1-z^{(\circ j)}\left(e^{-f(x)}\right)\right)e^{-x}{\rm d}x\right\}\right].

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are in force. Let
d_{n}
and
a_{n}
be given by (
11
)
and (
12
) respectively and take
b_{n}=d_{n}+\tau_{n}
,
where
\tau_{n}
is given in (

).
Then

\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}\Rightarrow\Lambda\quad\mbox{in }\mathcal{M}_{p},

with
\Lambda
given by (
18
).

Note that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{P}[M_{n}\leq x]=\mathbb{P}[\Lambda_{n}(x,+\infty]=\emptyset]
.
As a by-product of Theorem
3.1
we obtain a new result for

which complements the results
of
[
10
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then for
M_{n}=\max_{|w|=n}V(w)
and
b_{n}
and
a_{n}
as in the statement of
Theorem
3.1
,

\frac{M_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}\overset{d}{\to}V

with respect to
\mathbb{P}
, where the random variable
V\in[-\infty,\infty)
is distributed according to

\mathbb{P}[V\leq x]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-a\upsilon We^{-x}\right\}\right].

Note that
V
can have an atom at
-\infty
if
W
has an atom at
0
.
The point process convergence allows to treat upper order statistics of the positions of particles in generation
n
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let

M_{n}^{(k)}
denote the
k
-th order statistic of
\{V(w)\}_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
. That is for any

\{M_{n}^{(k)}\}_{k\leq\#\mathbb{T}_{n}}
is the non-decreasing enumeration of

. Then we have

\mathbb{P}\left[M_{n}^{(k)}\leq b_{n}+a_{n}x\right]=\mathbb{P}[\mathfrak{S}_{1/a_{n}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}(x,+\infty]\leq k-1]\to\mathbb{P}[\Lambda(x,+\infty]\leq k-1].

Following
[
3
]

we can express the limiting quantity on the right hand side in the following way. Consider a marked point process

\tilde{\Lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{T_{k}}\otimes\delta_{\iota_{k}-\log(\upsilon W)},

which conditioned on
W
is a Poisson point process with intensity

a\upsilon W\mathbb{P}[T_{1}\in{\rm d}x]\otimes e^{-y}{\rm d}y.

Denote by
\pi
a generic partition of some given integer, say
l
, of the form
l=i_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+i_{|\pi|}y_{|\pi|}
, where
each
i_{j}=i_{j}(\pi)
repeats
y_{j}=y_{j}(\pi)
times in the partition and the
i_{j}
are increasing.
Here
|\pi|
denotes the length of the partition, i.e. the number of its distinct elements.
Write
\Pi_{l}
for the collection of all possible partitions of

.
Then
\mathbb{P}[\Lambda(x,+\infty]\leq k-1]
is equal to

\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{l}}\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcap_{j=1}^{|\pi|}\{\tilde{\Lambda}(\{i_{j}\}\times(x+\infty]=y_{j}\}\right]=\\
\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{l}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{|\pi|}(a\upsilon We^{-x}\mathbb{P}[T_{1}=i_{j}])^{y_{j}}\frac{1}{y_{j}!}e^{-a\upsilon We^{-x}\mathbb{P}[T_{1}=i_{j}]}\right].

The right hand side is therefore the limit of
\mathbb{P}[M_{n}^{(k)}\leq b_{n}+a_{n}x]
.

Remark 3.3
.

The limiting point process (
18
) falls into the class of randomly shifted decorated Poisson point processes.
Comparing the Definition
2.2
with (
18
) one readily sees that

\Lambda\sim{\rm SDPPP}\left(ae^{-x}{\rm d}x,T_{1}\delta_{0},-\log(\upsilon W)\right).

Remark 3.4
.

The limiting point process
\Lambda
enjoys the superposability
[
6
, subsection 3.4]
: the distances from the
rightmost position of a union of realizations of
\Lambda
have the same distribution as those of a
single realization.
To state this in a more rigorous way let

\Lambda^{(i)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T_{k}^{(i)}\delta_{\ell_{k}^{(i)}-\log(\upsilon W_{i})}

denote i.i.d. copies of
\Lambda
. Since the martingale limit
W
is known to satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{1}}W_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}mW

where
W_{1},W_{1},\ldots
are i.i.d. copies of
W
independent of
Z_{1}
, one sees from studying the corresponding Laplace functionals that

\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{1}}\Lambda_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{T}_{\log m}\Lambda.

Now consider a mapping
\mathfrak{V}
on
\mathcal{M}_{p}
given by

\mathfrak{V}\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{j}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}-\max_{j}x_{j}}&\max_{j}x_{j}<\infty\\
o&\max_{j}x_{j}=\infty,\end{array}\right.

where
o
denotes the null measure.
Then
\mathfrak{V}\eta
is the point process
\eta
seen from the rightmost particle.
Since
\mathfrak{V}\circ\mathfrak{T}_{x}=\mathfrak{V}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, one readily sees that

\mathfrak{V}\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{1}}\Lambda_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{V}\Lambda.

Remark 3.5
.

One can show in a similar fashion that
\Lambda
is exponentially stable provided that
W=1
a.s. which is the case when the branching is deterministic, i.e.

\mathbb{P}[Z_{1}=k]=1
for some
k\geq 2
. More precisely
\Lambda
is exponentially

-stable if for any choice of
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

such that

we have

\mathfrak{T}_{\alpha}\Lambda+\mathfrak{T}_{\beta}\Lambda^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Lambda,

where
\Lambda^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
\Lambda
. One can check that this is true since in the case

a.s. one has

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int f(x)\mathfrak{T}_{\alpha}\Lambda({\rm d}x)}\right]=\exp\left\{-a(k-1)e^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}k^{-j-1}\left(1-e^{-k^{j}f(x)}\right)e^{-x}{\rm d}x\right\}

for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. It has been conjectured in
[
6
, subsection 6.1]
and proved in
[
16
]
that a

-stable point process is a decorated exponential Poisson process i.e. with intensity of the form
{\rm C}e^{-x}{\rm d}x
and a certain
decoration. This can be checked quite easily in our context since the aforementioned
SDPPP
representation of
\Lambda
boils down to a
DPPP

representation

\Lambda=\sum_{k}\mathfrak{T}_{\iota_{k}}D_{k},\qquad D_{k}=T_{k}\delta_{-\log\upsilon}.

with
\upsilon
defined in (
10

4.
Trimming lemmas

In our first steps towards the proof of Theorem
3.1
we will show which particles in
\mathbb{T}_{n}
contribute to the extremes of
\Lambda_{n}
.
To facilitate this we will
use several deviation estimates for sums of i.i.d. random variables with stretched exponential tails. We will often use the following expansion for
R
,

R\left(d_{n}+x\right)=R(d_{n})+R^{\prime}(d_{n})x+x^{2}R^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{d_{n},d_{n}+x})/2

for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and some
\xi_{d_{n},d_{n}+x}
that lies between
d_{n}
and
d_{n}+x
.

The first few steps of our arguments concentrate on pinpointing the particles that contribute to the point process which is the limit of

\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}
.
As we will see the positions of those particles will be asymptotically independent.
We will partition
\mathbb{T}_{n}
into four classes of particles and show which class contributes to the asymptotics of
\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}
.
The first one consists of those particles with no big jumps along their ancestral line, i.e.

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\left\{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\>:\>\max_{z\in(\emptyset,w]}X_{z}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\right\},

where
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon d_{n}
for some fixed
\varepsilon\in\left(2^{-r},1\right)
.
The positions of particles from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
consist of individual displacements of
typical particles and are therefore well controlled.
The point process given by the positions of the particles from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
will be denoted by

\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}(V(w)-b_{n})}.

As we will see in Lemma
4.2
below, the contribution of particles from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is negligible.
To control the contribution of
\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
we will need a deviation results for typical particles. For
L>0
take

\mathbb{P}_{L}
to be a probability measure such that the
X_{k}
’s are i.i.d. under
\mathbb{P}_{L}
with distribution given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{L}\left[X_{k}\leq t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\>X_{k}\leq t\>\left|\>X_{k}\leq L\>\right.\right].

One can use classical large deviation techniques to control the sum of the
X_{k}
’s under
\mathbb{P}_{L}
.
More precisely one can exploit the exponential change of measure and use the
cumulant generating function of the truncated variables

K_{L}(s)=\log\mathbb{E}_{L}\left[\exp\{sX_{1}\}\right],\quad s>0,

where
\mathbb{E}_{L}
denotes the expectation corresponding to
\mathbb{P}_{L}
. In order to be able to control the particles from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
we need to take

L=\varepsilon_{n}
in the above construction.
We will first check the asymptotic expansion of

K_{L}=K_{\varepsilon_{n}}
near
0
. For functions
f
, we write
f^{(0)}
,
f^{(1)}
and
f^{(2)}
for
f
,
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
respectively.
Recall the truncated cumulant generating function
K
given via (
16
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let Assumption
2
be in force and let
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon d_{n}
for some
\varepsilon\in(2^{-r},1)
.
Let

be such that
R(x)x^{-d}
is decreasing. Then for
\chi\in(0,1/d)
and

\sup_{0\leq s\leq\chi R^{\prime}(d_{n})}\left|K_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{(i)}(s)-K^{(i)}(s)\right|=o\left(n^{-1}\right).

The proof of Lemma
4.1
is quite standard and utilizes the fact that the
k_{m}
’s obey a similar recursive formula (

) as the cumulants
of
X_{1}
under
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon_{n}}
.
The details are given in the appendix.
One of the consequences of Lemma

is the fact that since
K(s)\sim s^{2}/2
as

we have

nK_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)=(1+o(1))ns^{2}/2,

as
n\to\infty
and

provided that
s\leq\chi R^{\prime}(d_{n})
.
For future reference denote

N_{n}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}X_{k}.

With Lemma

at hand we can state and prove the aforementioned lemma regarding the contribution of particles from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
for any
r\in(0,1)
, if
b_{n}\sim d_{n}
and
a_{n}=1/R^{\prime}(d_{n})
,

\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}\Rightarrow o

in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
, where

Proof.

Since the convergence is tested by integrating functions of compact support, it is enough to show that

\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}[L,\infty]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}0

for any constant
L\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}
is integer valued it is sufficient to estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}[L,\infty]\geq 1\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}[L,\infty]\right]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n}>b_{n}+La_{n},\>N_{n}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\right].

To tackle the second term on the right hand side note that

\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n}>b_{n}+La_{n},\>N_{n}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left[S_{n}>b_{n}+La_{n}\right].

Now use the exponential Markov inequality to get for
s>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left[S_{n}>b_{n}+La_{n}\right]\leq\exp\left\{-s(b_{n}+La_{n})+nK_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)\right\}.

Choose
s=(n\log m+n^{\xi})/(b_{n}+La_{n})
with
\xi\in(3-2/r,1)\cap(0,1)
. By Lemma
4.1
,
nK_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)=o(n^{\xi})
and thus

(20)

\exp\left\{-s(b_{n}+La_{n})+nK_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)\right\}\leq m^{-n}\exp\left\{-n^{\xi}/{\rm C}\right\}

which yields our claim.
∎

The next class of particles consist of those that had at least two big jumps along the ancestral line, that is

\mathcal{B}_{n}=\left\{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\>:\>\exists v,u\in(\emptyset,w],\>v\neq u,\>\>\min\{X_{v},X_{u}\}>\varepsilon_{n}\right\}.

By the choice of
\varepsilon\in(2^{-r},1)
,
\mathcal{B}_{n}=\emptyset
almost surely for sufficiently large

. Indeed, by a direct first moment bound

\mathbb{P}[|\mathcal{B}_{n}|\geq 1]\leq{\rm C}\>n^{2}\>m^{n}\mathbb{P}\left[X>\varepsilon_{n}\right]^{2}=o(1),

and
2\varepsilon^{r}>1
.
Thus

\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{B}}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{B}_{n}}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}(V(w)-b_{n})}\Rightarrow o

in

.
Particles that are not in

have exactly one big jump along their ancestral line.
We will need to distinguish whether this jump is greater or smaller than

d_{n}-nR^{\prime}(d_{n})&r\in\left(\frac{2}{3},1\right).\end{array}\right.

For

define

to be the element of

closest to the root such that

The next class of particles we will consider is given by

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\left\{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\>:\>X_{v^{*}(w)}\in\left(\varepsilon_{n},y_{n}\right]\mbox{ and }\max_{u\in(\emptyset,w]\setminus\{v^{*}(w)\}}\>X_{u}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\right\}.

Define the corresponding point process via

\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{C}}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}(V(w)-b_{n})}.

The second truncation sequence
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is chosen such that the contribution of the particles in

is also negligible.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2

Then for sequences

Proof.

We will proceed similarly as in the proof of Lemma
4.2
. In order to carry out this program we will first estimate

P_{n}=\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n-1}+X_{n}>b_{n}+La_{n},\>N_{n-1}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X_{n}\in\left(\varepsilon_{n},y_{n}\right]\right],

for
L\in\mathbb{R}
and
N_{n}
given in (
19
). We will first treat the case
r\leq 2/3
.
Note that with
h_{n}=R(d_{n})/d_{n}
, using the exponential Markov inequality,

(23)

P_{n}\leq{\rm C}e^{-h_{n}(b_{n}+La_{n})+nK_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left(h_{n}\right)}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h_{n}X}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X\in\left(\varepsilon_{n},y_{n}\right]\right\}}\right].

By Lemma
4.1
,
nK_{\varepsilon_{n}}(h_{n})=O(nR(d_{n})^{2}/d_{n}^{2})
and the expectation on the r.h.s. of (
23
), by
Lemma
A.3
from the appendix, has an upper
bound of the form

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h_{n}X}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X\in\left(\varepsilon_{n},y_{n}\right]\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{-(1+nR(d_{n})^{2}/d_{n}^{2})(\log n)^{2}/{\rm C}\right\}.

Finally for sufficiently large
n
,
h_{n}(b_{n}+La_{n})\geq R(d_{n})-|L/d|
as
R(x)x^{-d}
is assumed to be increasing for some
d\in(0,1)
.
Gathering all the estimates together shows that for
r\leq 2/3
, and sufficiently large
n
,

P_{n}\leq m^{-n}e^{-(\log n)^{2}/{\rm C}}.

For the case
r>2/3
we proceed in the same way and get (
23
) with
nK_{\varepsilon_{n}}(h_{n})=(1/2+o(1))nR(d_{n})^{2}/d_{n}^{2}
and by
Lemma
A.4
from the appendix

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h_{n}X}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X\in\left(\varepsilon_{n},y_{n}\right]\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{\frac{nR(d_{n})R^{\prime}(d_{n})}{d_{n}}\left(\frac{R^{\prime}(d_{n})d_{n}}{R(d_{n})}-1\right)(1+o(1))\right\}.

Recall that
b_{n}=d_{n}+\tau_{n}
with
\tau_{n}=(1+o(1))nR^{\prime}(d_{n})/2
and write

h_{n}(b_{n}+La_{n})=-R(d_{n})-(1+o(1))nR(d_{n})R^{\prime}(d_{n})/(2d_{n}).

Combining all the estimates yields

P_{n}\leq m^{-n}\exp\left\{\frac{nR(d_{n})R^{\prime}(d_{n})}{d_{n}}\left(\frac{R^{\prime}(d_{n})d_{n}}{R(d_{n})}-\frac{3}{2}+\frac{R(d_{n})}{2R^{\prime}(d_{n})d_{n}}\right)(1+o(1))\right\}.

Since we assume that for some
\epsilon>0
the functions
R(x)x^{-1/2-\epsilon}
and
R(x)x^{-1+\epsilon}
are eventually increasing and
decreasing respectively,
y=xR^{\prime}(x)/R(x)\in[1/2+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]

for sufficiently large
x
and thus the expression
y-3/2+1/(2y)
remains negative and bounded away from
0
.
We can therefore conclude that for
r>2/3
,

P_{n}\leq m^{-n}\exp\left\{-nR(d_{n})R^{\prime}(d_{n})/({\rm C}d_{n})\right\}.

Note that the sequence
nR(d_{n})R^{\prime}(d_{n})/d_{n}
is regularly varying with index
3-2/r>0
and thus it diverges to
+\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Finally to conclude our claim note that we have proven that for any
r\in(0,1)
, and
L\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{C}}[L,\infty)\geq 1\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{C}}[L,\infty)\right]\leq{\rm C}\>nm^{n}P_{n}\leq e^{-(\log n)^{2}/{\rm C}}\to 0.

∎

Our considerations up to this point show that the particles from
\mathcal{A}_{n}\cup\mathcal{B}_{n}\cup\mathcal{C}_{n}
do not affect the
asymptotic behaviour of
\mathfrak{S}_{a_{n}^{-1}}\mathfrak{T}_{-b_{n}}\Lambda_{n}
provided that the
latter has a non-trivial limit. In the rest of the article we will show that for

\mathcal{D}_{n}=\mathbb{T}_{n}\setminus(\mathcal{A}_{n}\cup\mathcal{B}_{n}\cup\mathcal{C}_{n})
the point process

\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}(V(w)-b_{n})}

has a non-trivial limit in distribution. For this reason we need to show that the extremes on
\mathcal{D}_{n}
decouple.

5.
Decoupling

Until now we investigated only the contribution of
big jumps which as it will soon turn out is not enough for our needs. Now we will be also investigating the contribution coming from typical particles.
This is when the precise large deviations beyond the so called one big jump domain
come into play. In this Section we will state the large deviation result in Theorem
5.1
and then use it to show that the extremal positions
of particles from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
decouple in Lemma
5.2
.

For functions
f_{n},g_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
we will say
that
f_{n-j_{n}}(z_{n})\sim g_{n-j_{n}}(z_{n})
uniformly with respect to
j_{n}\in J_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
z_{n}\in H_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{j_{n}\in J_{n}}\sup_{z_{n}\in G_{n}}\left|\frac{f_{n-j_{n}}(x_{n})}{g_{n-j_{n}}(x_{n})}-1\right|=0.

Equivalently,
f_{n-j_{n}}(z_{n})\sim g_{n-j_{n}}(z_{n})
for any choice of
x_{n}\in H_{n}
and
j_{n}\in J_{n}
.
Put

(24)

H_{n}=\left[d_{n}+nR^{\prime}(d_{n})/2-z_{n}^{*},d_{n}+nR^{\prime}(d_{n})/2+z_{n}^{*}\right],

where
z_{n}^{*}
is any sequence of real numbers that is regularly varying with index strictly less than
2-1/r
if
r>2/3
and less than

1/r-1/2
if
r\leq 2/3
.
Finally for
x_{n}\in H_{n}
we write

(25)

I(x_{n})=\inf_{s\in[0,1]}\left\{R(x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s))+n(sK^{\prime}(s)-K(s))\right\}.

Theorem
5.1
given below states that the probability that
S_{n}
exceeds
x_{n}\in H_{n}
decays as
ne^{-I(x_{n})}
. The form of
I(x_{n})
can be
heuristically derived in the following way.
Write
S_{n}=N_{n}+S_{n}-N_{n}
. Then
S_{n}-N_{n}
can be seen as the sum of
n-1
of the
X_{k}^{\prime}s
that are not the maximal among
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
In other words.
S_{n}-N_{n}
is the sum of typical values
X_{1},\ldots X_{n}
and
N_{n}

is the one atypical value among
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. Now the event
\{S_{n}>x_{n}\}
is a union of
\{N_{n}>a\}\cap\{S_{n}-N_{n}>b\}
along
a+b=x_{n}
.
For technical reasons it is most convenient to write
a=x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s)
and

b=nK^{\prime}(s)
for some
s>0
. Then the probability of
\{N_{n}=x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s)\}
decays as
ne^{-R(x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s))}
and
\{S_{n}-N_{n}>nK^{\prime}(s)\}
decays as

n^{-1/2}e^{n(K(s)-sK^{\prime}(s))}
(see (
6
)). Finally the infimum
present in the definition of
I(x_{n})
should be interpreted as taking the most probable choice of
s
and thus of

a=x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s)
and
b=nK^{\prime}(s)
.

Note that the infimum in (
25
) is attained for
s=s^{*}_{n}
which is the unique solution to

s^{*}_{n}=R^{\prime}(x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s_{n}^{*}))

in the interval
[0,1]
. By computing the derivative one can easily check that, for a sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
the function
s\mapsto R^{\prime}(x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s))
is a contraction on
[0,1]
and thus the existence and
uniqueness of
s^{*}_{n}
follows from the Banach fixed
point theorem. We thus have the following representation

I(x_{n})=R(x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s^{*}_{n}))+n(sK^{\prime}(s^{*}_{n})-K(s^{*}_{n})).

One then checks, using the regular variation of
R^{\prime}
that
s^{*}_{n}
is bounded from above by
(1+o(1))R^{\prime}(d_{n})
. It transpires that
if
r<2/3
then
I(x_{n})=R(x_{n})+o(1)
uniformly in
x_{n}\in H_{n}
and for
r\geq 2/3
we have
I(x_{n})=R(d_{n})+O(n^{4-3/r}\ell(n))

uniformly in
x_{n}\in H_{n}
for some slowly varying function
\ell
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let Assumption
2
be satisfied and let
J_{n}=\left[0,j_{n}^{*}\right]\cap\mathbb{N}
where
j_{n}^{*}
is any sequence of natural numbers
which is regularly varying with index
strictly less than
\min\{1,3/r-3\}
.
Then uniformly in
x_{n}\in H_{n}
and
j\in J_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}[S_{n-j_{n}}>x_{n}]\sim ne^{-I(x_{n})+O(j_{n}^{*}R^{\prime}(x_{n})^{2})}.

The case
r\leq 2/3
corresponds to
[
17
, Theorem 3]
, the case
r=2/3
to
[
17
, Theorem 5]
and
the case
r>2/3
to
[
17
, Theorem 2]
.
However the statement of the last one was too implicit for our needs and therefore we provide a new proof with a more explicit statement.
We will present a self-contained proof of Theorem
5.1
in Section
7
.
Note that the case
r<2/3
is covered in
[
8
]
.

We will use Theorem
5.1
in the proof of our decoupling lemma which we will state below. For
k<j
and
w\in\mathbb{T}
define

V_{k,j}(w)=\sum_{u\leq w,\>k<|u|\leq j}X_{u}.

The next lemma asserts that the particles in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
whose positions are around
b_{n}
are asymptotically independent.
The idea of the proof is the following. First, note that for
w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\setminus(\mathcal{A}_{n}\cup\mathcal{B}_{n}\cup\mathcal{C}_{n})

we have
X_{v^{*}(w)}>y_{n}
. One can use this information to infer estimates on
V_{k_{n}^{(1)},n}(w)
for sufficiently big
k_{n}^{(1)}
.
Then, using the branching property and the aforementioned estimates, one can get bounds on
V_{k_{n}^{(2)},n}(w)
for
k_{n}^{(2)}<k_{n}^{(1)}
. One can then
iterate this procedure
j
times until
k_{n}^{(j)}
gets small enough.

Lemma 5.2

(Decoupling lemma)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied. Then there exists a sequence of natural numbers
k_{n}

such that
k_{n}=o\left(n^{1-r-\epsilon}\right)
for some
\epsilon>0

and a sequence
l_{n}=o(b_{n})
such that
a_{n}=o(l_{n})
with the following property.
The sequence of events
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
A_{n}
occurs whenever there exist
w,z\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
such that

\min\{V_{k_{n},n}(w),V_{k_{n},n}(z)\}>b_{n}-l_{n},\>v^{*}(w)\neq v^{*}(z),\>|w\wedge z|>k_{n}

or if
r>2/3
and there exists
w\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
such that
V_{k_{n},n}(v^{*}(w))>b_{n}-l_{n}
and
|v^{*}(w)|\leq n-k_{n}

satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}\right]=0.

Proof.

We will first treat
r\leq 2/3
. In this case the event
A_{n}
is determined only by the first condition.
Take
k_{n}
to be the smallest integer grater than

(\log n)^{3}R^{\prime}(d_{n})(d_{n}-y_{n}+1),

take
l_{n}=0
and write,
for sufficiently large
n
,
a simple estimate

\mathbb{P}[A_{n}]\leq{\rm C}m^{2n-k_{n}}\mathbb{P}[X>y_{n}]^{2}\leq e^{-(\log n)^{3}/{\rm C}}

which secures our claim for
r\leq 2/3
.

The proof for
r>2/3
is more involved. In this case the event
A_{n}
is determined by both conditions mentioned in the statement.
For
j\geq 1
define the sequence of indexes
\{q_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
, where
q_{1}\in(3-2/r,1)
and

q_{j+1}=1+(q_{j}-1)/r
. Then
q_{j+1}=1-2r^{-j}(1-q_{1})\to-\infty
. Let
j_{0}
to be the smallest positive integer for which
q_{j_{0}}<(1-r)
.
Note that by the recursive formula for the
q_{j}
’s,
q_{j_{0}}\geq 0
. We can always choose the value of
q_{1}
such that the last inequality
is strict, so we have
q_{j_{0}}>0
.
Next define the approximating sequences
\{k_{n}^{(j)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\ell_{n}^{(j)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
via

k_{n}^{(1)}=n^{q_{1}}
,
k_{n}^{(j)}=d_{k_{n}^{(j-1)}}R^{\prime}(d_{n})\log n
and

\ell_{n}^{(j)}=d_{k_{n}^{(j)}}R^{\prime}(d_{n})(\log n)^{1/2}
. Then for each
j
,
\{k_{n}^{(j)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
regularly varying with index
q_{j}
and
\{\ell_{n}^{(j)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is regularly varying with index
q_{j}/r
.
In what follows we will make use of three asymptotic relations between the two sequences which follow from the construction.
Namely for each
j\geq 2
, as
n\to\infty
,

(26)

k_{n}^{(j)}\gg R^{\prime}(d_{n})\ell_{n}^{(j-1)},\quad\ell_{n}^{(j)}\gg d_{k_{n}^{(j)}},\quad\mbox{and}\quad k_{n}^{(1)}\gg nR^{\prime}(d_{n})^{2}.

Define
l_{n}=l_{n}^{(j_{0})}
and
k_{n}=k_{n}^{(j_{0})}
.
Consider the subsets of
\mathbb{T}_{n}
given via

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(0)}=\mathcal{D}_{n}
and for
j\geq 1
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j-1)}\>:\>|v^{*}(w)|\leq n-k_{n}^{(j)}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j-1)}\>:\>|v^{*}(w)|>n-k_{n}^{(j)},\>V_{k_{n}^{(j)},n}((v^{*}(w))\leq b_{n}-\ell_{n}^{(j)}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j-1)}\>:\>|v^{*}(w)|>n-k_{n}^{(j)},\>V_{k_{n}^{(j)},n}(v^{*}(w))>b_{n}-\ell_{n}^{(j)}\right\}.

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathcal{D}_{n}\>:\>|v^{*}(w)|>n-k_{n}^{(j)},\>V_{k_{n}^{(i)},n}(v^{*}(w))>b_{n}-\ell_{n}^{(i)},i<j\right\}.

We have

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(1)}\neq\emptyset\right]\leq{\rm C}m^{n-k_{n}^{(1)}}\mathbb{P}[X>y_{n}]\leq\exp\left\{-n^{q_{1}}/{\rm C}\right\}

and

\mathbb{P}\left[\exists w\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(1)},\>V_{k_{n},n}(w)>b_{n}-l_{n}\right]\leq\\
{\rm C}m^{n}\mathbb{P}[X>y_{n}]\mathbb{P}\left[S_{k_{n}^{(1)}-k_{n}}>l_{n}^{(1)}(1+o(1))\right]\leq\exp\left\{-n^{q_{1}}/{\rm C}\right\}.

Turning our attention to
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(m)}
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(m)}
with
j_{0}>m\geq 2
we use the uniform estimates in
Theorem
5.1
and the first condition in (
26
) to write

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(m)}\neq\emptyset\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\exists w\in\mathbb{T},\>n-k_{n}^{(m-1)}<|w|\leq n-k_{n}^{(m)},\>V_{k_{n},n}(w)>b_{n}-l_{n}^{(m-1)}\right]\\
\leq\sum_{j=k_{n}^{(m)}}^{k^{(m-1)}_{n}}m^{n-j}\mathbb{P}[S_{n-j-k_{n}}>b_{n}-l_{n}^{(m-1)}]\leq\exp\left\{-k_{n}^{(m)}/{\rm C}\right\}.

In a similar way, using the first two conditions in (
26
) and Theorem
5.1
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\exists w\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(m)},\>V_{k_{n},n}(w)>b_{n}-l_{n}\right]\leq\\
\leq m^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k_{n}^{(m)}}\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n-j-k_{n}}>b_{n}+La_{n}-l_{n}^{(m-1)}\right]\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left[S_{j}>l^{(m)}_{n}\right]\\
\leq\exp\left\{-k_{n}^{(m)}/{\rm C}\right\}.

From the above considerations

(27)

\mathbb{P}\left[\exists w\in\bigcup_{j=0}^{j_{0}-1}\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j)},\>V_{k_{n},n}(v^{*}(w))>b_{n}-l_{n},|v^{*}(w)|\leq n-k_{m}\right]\to 0.

Next consider the events

A_{n}^{(j)}=\left\{\exists w,\>z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j_{0})}\,:\min\{V_{k_{n},n}(w),V_{k_{n},n}(z)\}>b_{n}-l_{n},\right.\\
\left.v^{*}(w)\neq v^{*}(z),\>|v^{*}(w)\wedge v^{*}(z)|\geq k_{n}^{(j)}\right\}.

Then for
j=1
, and sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}^{(1)}\right]\leq{\rm C}m^{n}m^{n-k_{n}^{(1)}}\mathbb{P}[X>y_{n}]^{2}\leq\exp\left\{-n^{q_{1}}/{\rm C}\right\}

and for
j>1
,

(28)

\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}^{(j)}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}^{(j-1)}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}^{(j)}\setminus A_{n}^{(j-1)}\right].

On the event
\{k_{n}^{(j-1)}>|v^{*}(w)\wedge v^{*}(z)|\}
the random variables
V_{k_{n}^{(j-1)},n}(w)
and
V_{k_{n}^{(j-1)},n}(z)
are independent
given
\sigma\left(X_{v}\>:|v|\leq k_{n}^{(j-1)}\right)
.
Now by the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j-1)}
we use the aforementioned conditional independence and write

\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}^{(j)}\setminus A_{n}^{(j-1)}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\exists w,\>z\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(j_{0})},\>V_{k_{n},n}(w)>b_{n}-l_{n},\right.\\
V_{k_{n}^{j-1},n}(z^{*}(y))>b_{n}-l_{n}^{(j-1)}\left.k_{n}^{(j-1)}>|v^{*}(w)\wedge v^{*}(z)|\geq k_{n}^{(j)}\right]\\
\leq{\rm C}m^{n}m^{n-k_{n}^{(j)}}\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n-k_{n}^{(j-1)}-k_{n}}>b_{n}-l_{n}^{(j-1)}\right]\mathbb{P}[S_{n-k_{n}}>b_{n}-l_{n}].

The large deviation result for stretched exponential random variables in Theorem
5.1
implies that for sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}[S_{n-k_{n}^{(j-1)}}>b_{n}+La_{n}-l_{n}^{(j-1)}]\leq m^{n}\exp\left\{{\rm C}R^{\prime}(d_{n})l_{n}^{(j-1)}\right\}.

By our choice of sequences,
R^{\prime}(d_{n})l_{n}^{(j-1)}\ll k_{n}^{(j)}
as stated in (
26
). Going back to (
28
) gives

\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}^{(j)}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}^{(j-1)}\right]+\exp\left\{-k_{n}^{(j)}/{\rm C}\right\}

which by induction implies that for any
j\leq j_{0}
,
\mathbb{P}[A_{n}^{(j)}]\to 0
. The claim follows since
A_{n}
is a union of
A_{n}^{(j_{0})}
and the
event considered in (
27
).
∎

Lemma
5.2
will allow for an efficient decoupling. The position of each
w\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
can be written in the following way:

(29)

V(w)=H_{1}(w)+T(w)+X_{v^{*}(w)}+H_{2}(w),

where

\displaystyle H_{1}(w)=\sum_{z\leq w}X_{z}\mathds{1}_{\{|z|\leq k_{n}\}},

\displaystyle H_{2}(w)=\sum_{z\leq w}X_{z}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{z}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>|z|>n-k_{n}\}},

\displaystyle T(w)=\sum_{|z|\leq w}X_{z}\mathds{1}_{\{|z|\in[k_{n},n-k_{n}]\}}.

As one may expect, the first term on the r.h.s. of (
29
) is negligible.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
r\in(0,1)
,
\mathbb{P}
- a.s.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{w\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}H_{1}(w)/a_{n}=0.

Proof.

One can use the quite generous upper bound

\max_{w\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}|H_{1}(w)|\leq M_{k_{n}}.

Since
d_{k_{n}}=o\left(n^{1/r-1-\epsilon}\right)
for some
\epsilon>0
as in the claim of Lemma
5.2
, we get
M_{k_{n}}/a_{n}\to 0

as
a_{n}
is regularly varying with index
1/r-1
.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
5.3
in order to establish convergence of
\Lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}

it is sufficient to study

\Theta_{n}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}(T(w)+X_{v^{*}(w)}+H_{2}(w)-b_{n})}.

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied. Let
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
be chosen as in the statement of
Theorem
3.1
. Then as
n\to\infty
,

\Lambda^{\mathcal{D}}_{n}-\Theta_{n}\Rightarrow o

in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
.

We will present the proof of this lemma once we establish the convergence of
\Theta_{n}
.

6.
Convergence of the point processes

We now turn to the final arguments in in the proof of Theorem
3.1
. We will first show that the decoupled point process
\Theta_{n}
converges to
the desired limit. Then, using the tightness of
\Theta_{n}
, we will show Lemma
5.4
which will yield immediately Theorem
3.1
.
Note first that we can rewrite
\Theta_{n}
in the following way

\Theta_{n}=\sum_{n-k_{n}<|v|\leq n}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{v}>y_{n}\}}\sum_{z\geq v,\>|z|=n}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}(T(z)+X_{v}+H_{2}(z)-b_{n})}\mathds{1}_{E_{n}(v,z)},

where
E_{n}(v,z)=\left\{X_{w}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\>\mbox{ for all }w\in(\emptyset,z]\setminus\{v\},|w|\geq k_{n}\right\}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied. Then for sequences
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as in the
statement of Theorem
3.1
, and
\Lambda
given by (
18
),

\Theta_{n}\Rightarrow\Lambda

in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
.

Proof.

By the choice of the centring sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the scaling sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, by an appeal to Lemma
4.2
,
Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
5.1
we have for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}m^{n}\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n-2k_{n}}+X>b_{n}+xa_{n},\>N_{n-2k_{n}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X>y_{n}\right]=ae^{-x}.

Therefore if we denote

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[a_{n}^{-1}(S_{n-2k_{n}}+X-b_{n})\in\cdot\>,\>N_{n-2k_{n}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X>y_{n}\right]

the above observation implies that vaguely

m^{n}\eta_{n}({\rm d}x)\to\eta({\rm d}x)=ae^{-x}{\rm d}x.

Fix any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{+}_{K}
and take
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently small such that
{\rm supp}(f)\subseteq[-l_{n}/a_{n},\infty]
.
Recall the statement of Lemma
5.2
and the definition of the event
A_{n}
. By conditioning on

\mathbb{T}
and
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\sigma(X_{u},{|u|\geq n-k_{n}})
we can write on the set
A_{n}^{c}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left\{-\int f(x)\>\Theta_{n}({\rm d}x)\right\}\right|\mathbb{T},\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]=\\
\prod_{|v|\in(n-k_{n},n]}\!\!\!\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left\{-\mathds{1}_{E_{n}}\!\!\!\sum_{w\geq v,|w|=n}\!\!\!\mathds{1}_{E_{n}^{\prime}(v,w)}f\left(\frac{S_{n-k_{n}-1}+H_{2}(w)+X_{v}}{a_{n}}\right)\right\}\right|\mathbb{T},\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]

where
S_{n-k_{n}-1}
is independent from everything else,

E_{n}^{\prime}(v,w)=\left\{X_{z}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\>\mbox{ for all }z\in(\emptyset,w]\setminus\{v\},|z|\geq n-k_{n},X_{v}>y_{n}\right\}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}

and

E_{n}=\left\{N_{n-k_{n}-1}\leq\varepsilon_{n}\right\}
.
Integrating the conditional expectation over the set
A_{n}^{c}
and using the fact that
\mathbb{P}[A_{n}]=o(1)
we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\int f(x)\>\Theta_{n}({\rm d}x)\right\}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=n-k_{n}}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\int f(x)\>\Theta_{n,k}({\rm d}x)\right\}\right]^{Z_{k}}\right]+o(1)

where

\Theta_{n,k}=\mathds{1}_{E_{n}}\mathds{1}_{\{X>y_{n}\}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}_{n-k}}\delta_{a_{n}^{-1}\left(S_{n-2k_{n}-1}+X+V(w)-b_{n}\right)},

We will argue that for

,

(30)

Z_{n-j}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\exp\left\{-\int f(x)\>\Theta_{n,n-j}({\rm d}x)\right\}\right]\\
\to m^{-j}W\int\mathbb{E}\left[1-e^{-Z_{j}f(x)}\right]\eta({\rm d}x)

a.s. which implies

\prod_{j=0}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}^{*}\left[\exp\left\{-\int f(x)\>\Theta_{n,n-j}({\rm d}x)\right\}\right]^{Z_{n-j}}\to\\
\mathbb{E}^{*}\left[\exp\left\{-W\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}m^{-j}\int\mathbb{E}\left[1-e^{-Z_{j}f(x)}\right]\eta({\rm d}x)\right\}\right].

30

f
via

(31)

\omega_{f}(\epsilon)=\sup_{|x-y|\leq\epsilon}|f(x)-f(y)|,\quad\epsilon>0\,.

Denote

\Delta_{n-j}=\int f(x)\>\Theta_{n,n-j}({\rm d}x)-Z_{j}f\left(a_{n}^{-1}\left(S_{n-2k_{n}}+X-b_{n}\right)\right)

and notice that the law

is dominated in the sense of the stochastic order via

|\Delta_{n-j}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\leq}}Z_{j}\omega_{f}\left(M_{k_{n}}/a_{n}\right)

where the second term on the right hand side goes to

M_{k_{n}}/a_{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}0
(see the proof of Lemma
5.3

is necessarily bounded, the aforementioned convergence holds also in

Z_{n-j}\mathbb{E}\left[1-e^{-\int f(x)\>\Theta_{n,n-j}({\rm d}x)}\right]=Z_{n-j}\mathbb{E}\left[1-e^{-Z_{j}f\left(a_{n}^{-1}\left(S_{n-2k_{n}}+X-b_{n}\right)\right)}\right]\\
+Z_{n-j}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-Z_{j}f\left(a_{n}^{-1}\left(S_{n-2k_{n}}+X-b_{n}\right)\right)}(1-e^{-\Delta_{n-j}})\right].

The first term has the desired asymptotics, i.e. by an appeal to the vague convergence
m^{n}\eta_{n}(\cdot)\to\eta(\cdot)

Z_{n-j}\mathbb{E}\left[1-e^{-Z_{j}f\left(a_{n}^{-1}\left(S_{n-2k_{n}}+X-b_{n}\right)\right)}\right]=Z_{n-j}\int\mathbb{E}\left[1-e^{-Z_{j}f(x)}\right]\eta_{n}({\rm d}x)\\

The second term is negligible since

\leq\frac{Z_{n-j}}{m^{n-j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{Z_{j}}{m^{j}}\omega_{f}\left(\frac{M_{k_{n}}}{R^{\prime}(d_{n})}\right)\right]m^{n}\eta_{n}((\varepsilon,\infty]).

The convergence in (
30

.

Large deviations for stretched exponential tails

In this section we will revisit precise large deviations for sums of i.i.d. random variables with stretched exponential tails. This problem was addressed previously, see
[
17
,
18
]
. However the general
results in
[
17
]
were not sufficiently explicit for our purposes. For this reason we decided to revisit the problem and study the deviations on the scale
d_{n}
, where
d_{n}
is any regularly varying
function with index
1/r
.
We will study deviations uniformly in the time interval

n_{0}=n-j_{n},\quad j_{n}\in J_{n}=\left[0,j_{n}^{*}\right],

where
j_{n}^{*}
is any sequence of natural number which is regularly varying with index strictly less than
\min\{1,3/r-3\}
.
Recall
H_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
given in (
24
) and the rate function
I(x_{n})
given in (
25
).

The first lemma asserts that whenever
S_{n_{0}}>x_{n}
there has to be at least one jump grater than
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon d_{n}
for some fixed
\varepsilon\in(2^{-r},1)
.
The proof goes along the exact same lines as the proof of Lemma
4.2
(see (
20
))
and is therefore omitted.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. Then

\sup_{x_{n}\in H_{n}}\sup_{j_{n}\in J_{n}}\mathbb{P}[S_{n_{0}}>x_{n},\>N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n}]=o\left(ne^{-I(x_{n})}\right).

Next we will treat the possibility of having two jumps bigger than
\varepsilon_{n}
. The proof is straightforward, see (
21
), and is therefore also omitted.

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. Then

\sup_{x_{n}\in H_{n}}\sup_{j_{n}\in J_{n}}\mathbb{P}[S_{n_{0}}>x_{n},\>\exists i,j\leq n_{0},\>\min\{X_{i},X_{j}\}>\varepsilon_{n},\>i\neq j]=o\left(ne^{-I(x_{n})}\right).

Taking the last two lemmas into account, we see that in order to establish the asymptotics of
\mathbb{P}[S_{n_{0}}>x_{n}]
it is sufficient to study

\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n_{0}}+X>x_{n},\>N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X>\varepsilon_{n}\right].

In our next step we will show that
X
has to be sufficiently big. Recall the sequence
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by (
22
).

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. Then

\sup_{x_{n}\in I_{n}}\sup_{j_{n}\in J_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n_{0}}+X>x_{n},\>N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X\in\left(\varepsilon_{n},y_{n}\right]\right]=o\left(ne^{I(x_{n})}\right).

The proof goes along the same lines as the proof of Lemma
4.3
and is therefore omitted. In what follows we will investigate

P_{n}=\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n_{0}}+X>x_{n},\>N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X>y_{n}\right].

Consider
w_{n}>y_{n}
given via

(32)

w_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rr}d_{n}+(\log n)^{2}/R^{\prime}(d_{n})&r\in\left(0,2/3\right]\\
d_{n}+nR^{\prime}(d_{n})/3&r\in\left(2/3,1\right)\end{array}\right..

We will start by writing

P_{n}=\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n_{0}}>x_{n}-y_{n},\>N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X>y_{n}\right]\\
+\mathbb{P}\left[S_{n_{0}}<x_{n}-w_{n},\>N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>S_{n_{0}}+X>x_{n},\>X>y_{n}\right]\\
+\mathbb{P}\left[x_{n}-S_{n_{0}}\in[y_{n},w_{n}],\>N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>X>x_{n}-S_{n_{0}}\right]=A_{n}+B_{n}+C_{n}.

Lemma 7.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. We have

\sup_{x_{n}\in I_{n}}\sup_{j_{n}\in J_{n}}(A_{n}+B_{n})=o\left(ne^{-I(x_{n})}\right).

Proof.

We will first treat
A_{n}
. Simply write

A_{n}\leq\mathbb{P}[X>y_{n}]\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon_{n}}[S_{n_{0}}>x_{n}-y_{n}].

If
r\leq 2/3
the above estimate combined with an exponential Markov’s inequality with
s=(1+\epsilon)R^{\prime}(d_{n})
constitutes

A_{n}\leq\mathbb{P}[X>y_{n}]\exp\{n_{0}K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)-s(x_{n}-y_{n})\}.

Provided that
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small, we can invoke Lemma
4.1
and conclude that

A_{n}\leq\exp\left\{-R(d_{n})-\epsilon(\log n)^{2}\right\}.

For
r>2/3
we apply the same argument (with the same choice of
s
in the exponential Markov’s inequality) and get

A_{n}\leq\exp\left\{-R(d_{n})-\epsilon nR^{\prime}(d_{n})^{2}\right\}.

This proves our first claim.
For the second term we have a simple bound
B_{n}\leq\mathbb{P}[X>w_{n}]
which implies our claim.
∎

To treat
C_{n}
firstly condition on
X_{1},\ldots X_{n_{0}}
and write

(33)

C_{n}=\mathbb{E}[e^{-R(x_{n}-S_{n_{0}})}\mathds{1}_{\{N_{n_{0}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},\>x_{n}-S_{n_{0}}\in[y_{n},w_{n}]\}}].

Recall the measure
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon_{n}}
introduced in Section
4
and that we write
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{n}}
for
its expectation.
We have uniformly in
x_{n}\in H_{n}
and
j_{n}\in J_{n}
,

C_{n}=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{n}}[e^{-R(x_{n}-S_{n_{0}})}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{n}-S_{n_{0}}\in[y_{n},w_{n}]\}}].

We will use yet another change of measure.
For
s>0
denote by
\mathbb{P}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}
a probability measure with respect to which
X_{k}
are i.i.d. distributed according to

\mathbb{P}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}[X_{k}\leq t]=e^{-K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{X_{k}\leq t\}}e^{sX_{k}}\right].

Denoting by
\mathbb{E}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}
the expected value corresponding to
\mathbb{P}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}
we have
\mathbb{E}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}[X_{1}]=K^{\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)
and

\mathbb{E}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}\left[(X_{1}-\mathbb{E}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}[X_{1}])^{2}\right]=K^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)
.
Consider the distribution of normalized sums

F_{n}^{(s)}(y)=\mathbb{P}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}\left[\theta_{n}(S_{n_{0}})\leq y\right],\quad\text{where}\quad\theta_{n}(y)=(y-n_{0}K^{\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s))/\sqrt{n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)}.

Then
F_{n}^{(s)}
converge point wise to the cumulative distribution function of standard normal distribution.
and observe that for
D_{n}=[\theta_{n}(x_{n}-w_{n}),\theta_{n}(x_{n}-y_{n})]
we have

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{n}}[e^{-R(x_{n}-S_{n_{0}})}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{n}-S_{n_{0}}\in[y_{n},w_{n}]\}}]=\\
e^{n_{0}K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)}\mathbb{E}_{s,\varepsilon_{n}}\left[e^{-sS_{n_{0}}-R(x_{n}-S_{n_{0}})}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{n}-S_{n_{0}}\in[y_{n},w_{n}]\}}\right]\\
=\exp\left\{n_{0}K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)\right\}\int_{D_{n}}e^{-s\theta_{n}^{-1}(y)-R(x_{n}-\theta_{n}^{-1}(y))}\>F_{n}^{(s)}({\rm d}y).

Now use Taylor expansion for
R
to write, for
y\in\theta_{n}([x_{n}-w_{n},x_{n}-y_{n}])
, and
x_{n}(s)=x_{n}-n_{0}K_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{\prime}(s)
,

R(x_{n}-\theta_{n}^{-1}(y))=R(x_{n}(s))-yR^{\prime}(x_{n}(s))\sqrt{n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}(s)}+y^{2}R^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{y})n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}(s)/2

for some
\xi_{y}
that lies between
x_{n}(s)
and
x_{n}(s)-y\sqrt{n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}(s)}
.
Thus we arrive at

C_{n}=(1+o(1))e^{n_{0}K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)-n_{0}sK^{\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)-R(x_{n}(s))}\int_{D_{n}}e^{ye_{n}(s)-y^{2}R^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{y})n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)/2}\>F_{n}^{(s)}({\rm d}y)

with

e_{n}(s)=(R^{\prime}(x_{n}(s))-s)\sqrt{n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)}.

We want to take
s=s^{*}
in the above construction, where
s^{*}
is characterised by being the unique solution to (
5
). Then, by the merit of regular variation of
R^{\prime\prime}
, for some slowly varying function
\ell_{n}
,

|e_{n}(s^{*})|\leq|R^{\prime}(x_{n}(s^{*}))-R^{\prime}(x_{n}-nK^{\prime}(s^{*}))|\sqrt{nK^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s^{*})}\leq n^{-1/2}\ell_{n}.

Note that we used here the bound on
j_{n}^{*}
. In the sequel we will write
e_{n}=e_{n}(s^{*})
.
Finally by the regular variation of
R^{\prime\prime\prime}
for some slowly varying sequence
\ell_{n}

(34)

\sup_{z\in[x_{n}-w_{n},x_{n}-y_{n}]}|R^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{x_{n},x_{n}+z})-R^{\prime\prime}(x_{n})|\leq\epsilon_{n}=n^{3-4/r}\ell_{n}.

It is therefore sufficient to show the claim of our next lemma.

Lemma 7.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. Let

f_{n}=(R^{\prime\prime}(x_{n}(s^{*}))+\epsilon_{n})n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s^{*})
where
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is regularly varying with index
3-4/r
.
Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\sup_{x_{n}\in I_{n}}\sup_{j_{n}\in J_{n}}\left|\int_{D_{n}}e^{ye_{n}-y^{2}f_{n}/2}\>F_{n}^{(s^{*})}({\rm d}y)-1\right|\to 0.

Proof.

Firstly, for
r\geq 2/3
,
ye_{n}-y^{2}f_{n}/2\to 0
uniformly in
y\in D_{n}
,
x_{n}\in H_{n}
, and
j_{n}\in J_{n}
and so the claim follows from the central limit theorem. We thus treat only the case
r>2/3
.
We will utilize the following estimate, for
y\in[0,\theta_{n}(x_{n}-y_{n})]
and
h>0
we have

1-F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(y)=e^{-n_{0}K^{\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s^{*})}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{n}}[e^{s^{*}S_{n_{0}}}\mathds{1}_{\{\theta_{n}(S_{n_{0}})>y\}}]\leq\\
(1+o(1))\exp\left\{n_{0}(K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s^{*}+h)-K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s^{*})-hK^{\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s^{*}))-\sqrt{n_{0}K^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s^{*})}hy\right\}.

Take
h=\epsilon y/\sqrt{n_{0}}
for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that we can invoke Lemma
4.1
to get

(35)

1-F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(y)\leq{\rm C}\exp\left\{-y^{2}/{\rm C}_{\epsilon}\right\},

for some constant
{\rm C}_{\epsilon}
that depends on
\epsilon
.
Similarly, for
y\in[\theta_{n}(x_{n}-w_{n}),0]

F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(y)\leq{\rm C}\exp\left\{-y^{2}/{\rm C}_{\epsilon}\right\}.

Denote
D_{n}^{+}=\left[0,\theta_{n}(x_{n}-y_{n})\right]
.
We will first show that

\sup_{z_{n}\in I_{n}}\sup_{j_{n}\in J_{n}}\left|\int_{D_{n}^{+}}e^{ye_{n}-y^{2}f_{n}/2}\>F_{n}^{(s^{*})}({\rm d}y)-\frac{1}{2}\right|\to 0.

Using the integration by parts formula we arrive at

\int_{D_{n}^{+}}e^{ye_{n}-y^{2}f_{n}/2}\>F_{n}^{(s^{*})}({\rm d}y)=\int_{D_{n}^{+}}(e_{n}-yf_{n})e^{ye_{n}-y^{2}f_{n}/2}(1-F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(y))\>{\rm d}y\\
-e^{\theta_{n}(x_{n}-y_{n})e_{n}-\theta_{n}(x_{n}-y_{n})^{2}f_{n}/2}(1-F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(\theta_{n}(x_{n}-y_{n})))+(1-F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(0)).

One can show that the first two terms on the right hand side are negligible using the estimate (
35
). Indeed the behaviour of the second term follows directly from (
35
).
The integral can be treated by considering
y>n^{2/r-2-\epsilon}
and
y<n^{2/r-2-\epsilon}
. In the first case the
pre factor
e_{n}+f_{n}y
is bounded via
n^{-\epsilon}
. In the second case the exponent tends to

.
Our claim follows since
F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(0)\to 1/2
.
In a similar fashion we treat
D_{n}^{-}=\left[\theta_{n}(x_{n}-w_{n}),0\right]
, we write

\int_{D_{n}^{-}}e^{ye_{n}-y^{2}f_{n}/2}\>F_{n}^{(s^{*})}({\rm d}y)=\int_{D_{n}^{-}}(e_{n}-yf_{n})e^{ye_{n}-y^{2}f_{n}/2}(1-F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(y))\>{\rm d}y\\
-e^{\theta_{n}(x_{n}-w_{n})e_{n}-\theta_{n}(x_{n}-w_{n})^{2}f_{n}/2}(F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(\theta_{n}(x_{n}-w_{n})))+F_{n}^{(s^{*})}(0).

and conclude that the limit of the right hand side is
1/2
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Our considerations up to this point show that it is enough to justify that

given in (

) has the desired asymptotics. Note that with
\varepsilon_{n}^{*}
be given

\int_{D_{n}}e^{ye_{n}+y^{2}f_{n}^{\downarrow}/2}\>F_{n}^{(s^{*})}({\rm d}y)\leq\\

where

and

. The claim now follows from Lemma

∎

,

A=0

With this estimate we infer that for sufficiently large

\psi(\varepsilon_{n})\mathbb{P}[X>\varepsilon_{n}]\leq e^{-R(d_{n})/{\rm C}}.

For the integral we can write

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}}\psi^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}[X>t]{\rm d}t\leq{\rm C}\cdot d_{n}^{k}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}}\exp\left\{st-R(t)\right\}{\rm d}t\\
\leq{\rm C}\cdot d_{n}^{k+1}\int_{\delta+o(1)}^{1}\exp\left\{s\varepsilon_{n}t-R(\varepsilon_{n}t)\right\}{\rm d}t

As
R
is regularly varying with index
r\in(0,1)
,
R(\varepsilon_{n}t)\sim t^{r}R(\varepsilon_{n})
almost uniformly in
t\in(0,\infty)
, i.e. uniformly in
t\in G
for any compact
G\subseteq(0,+\infty)
.
Thus for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
there is
n
large enough such that
R(\varepsilon_{n}t)\geq t^{r}R(\varepsilon_{n})(1-\varepsilon)
for all
t\in[\delta/2,1]
. Thus the integral can be bounded in the following way

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}}\psi^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}[X>t]{\rm d}t\leq Cd_{n}^{k+1}\exp\{(\chi d-1+\varepsilon)R(\varepsilon_{n})\}.

Provided that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small the right hand side decays exponentially fast in
n
which in turn secures our claim.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Lemma
4.1
which we recall here for convenience.

Lemma A.2
.

Let Assumption
2
be in force. Let
d\in(0,1)
be such that
R(x)x^{-d}
is decreasing and let
\chi\in(0,1/d)
.
Then for any
i\in\{0,1,2\}
,

\sup_{0\leq s\leq\chi R^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{n})}\left|K_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{(i)}(s)-K^{(i)}(s)\right|=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Since under
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon_{n}}
,
X
is bounded a.s. for any fixed
s
,

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\kappa}\frac{s^{j}}{j!}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left[X^{j}\right]+\int_{0}^{s}\frac{(s-t)^{\kappa}}{(\kappa+1)!}F_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{(\kappa+1)}(t){\rm d}t,

where for any fixed
j
,
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left[X^{j}\right]=\mathbb{E}[X^{j}]+o\left(1/n\right)
. Let

F(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\kappa}\frac{s^{j}}{j!}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{j}\right].

By Lemma
A.1
and the choice of
\kappa
, for any
i\in\{0,1,2\}
,

(38)

\sup_{0\leq s\leq\chi R^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{n})}|F^{(i)}(s)-F_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{(i)}(s)|=o\left(1/n\right).

We continue with

(39)

K_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s)=\log(F_{\varepsilon_{n}}(s))=\log(F(s))+\widehat{\Delta}_{n}(s),

with
\widehat{\Delta}_{n}(s)
such that
\sup_{0\leq s\leq\chi R^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{n})}|\widehat{\Delta}_{n}(s)|=o\left(1/n\right)
.
Define
\phi(s)=\log(F(s))
. Following
[
22
]
we have
\phi(0)=0
and

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}F(s)=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}e^{\phi(s)}=\phi^{\prime}(s)e^{\phi(s)}=\phi^{\prime}(s)F(s).

We see that
\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
and after differentiating
m-1
times, where
m\leq\kappa
, we get

\frac{{\rm d}^{m}}{{\rm d}s^{m}}\phi(0)=\mathbb{E}[X^{m}]-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{j}\frac{{\rm d}^{m-j}}{{\rm d}s^{m-j}}\phi(0)\cdot\frac{{\rm d}^{j}}{{\rm d}s^{j}}F(0).

Comparing the above relation with (
15
) allows us to conclude that
\frac{d^{m}}{ds^{m}}\phi(0)=k_{m}
for
m\leq\kappa
.
Using the Taylor expansion of
\phi
we get finally that

\log F(s)=\phi(s)=\sum_{j=2}^{\kappa}\frac{k_{j}}{j!}s^{j}+\frac{s^{\kappa+1}}{(\kappa+1)!}\phi^{(\kappa+1)}(\vartheta s)=K(s)+\frac{s^{\kappa+1}}{(\kappa+1)}\phi^{(\kappa+1)}(\vartheta s)

for some
\vartheta\in[0,1]
with

\sup_{0\leq s\leq\chi R^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{n})}\left|\frac{s^{\kappa+1}}{(\kappa+1)}\phi^{(\kappa+1)}(s)\right|=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

by the choice of
\kappa
. This in combination with (
39
) yields our claim for
i=0
. The claims for
i=1
and
i=2
follow from a direct comparison of
K^{(i)}
and

K^{(i)}_{\varepsilon_{n}}
and an appeal to (
38
).
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let Assumption
2
be in force for some
r\leq 2/3
. Then for
y_{n}=d_{n}-(\log n)^{2}(d_{n}^{2}+nR(d_{n})^{2})/(d_{n}R(d_{n}))
,
h_{n}=R(d_{n})/d_{n}
and

\varepsilon_{n}=\delta d_{n}
for
\varepsilon\in(2^{-r},1)
the following inequality holds true for sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h_{n}X}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X\in\left(\varepsilon_{n},y_{n}\right]\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{-(\log n)^{2}(1+nR(d_{n})^{2}/d_{n}^{2})/{\rm C}\right\}.

Proof.

We will apply the integration by parts formula (
36
)
with
\psi(s)=e^{h_{n}s}
,
A=\varepsilon_{n}
and
B=y_{n}
. We have

(40)

\psi(\varepsilon_{n})\mathbb{P}\left[X>\varepsilon_{n}\right]=\exp\left(\delta R(d_{n})-R(\delta d_{n})\right)\leq\exp\left\{-n/{\rm C}\right\},

since
R(\varepsilon d_{n})\sim\varepsilon^{r}R(d_{n})\sim\varepsilon^{r}n
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Now note that since
R(x)x^{-d}
is assumed to be eventually decreasing for some
d\in(0,1)
, we have
R(x)/(R^{\prime}(x)x)>1/d>1
for sufficiently large
x
and thus

(41)

h_{n}y_{n}-R(y_{n})\leq(1-1/d)(\log n)^{2}(1+nR(d_{n})^{2}/d_{n}^{2}+o(1))

which in turn gives

\psi(y_{n})\mathbb{P}[X>y_{n}]\leq{\rm C}\exp\left\{-(\log n)^{2}(1+nR(d_{n})^{2}/d_{n}^{2})/{\rm C}\right\}.

We finally consider the integral on the r.h.s. of (
36
) for which we have

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{y_{n}}\psi^{\prime}(s)\mathbb{P}[X>s]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=h_{n}\int_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{y_{n}}\exp\left\{h_{n}s-R(s)\right\}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq{\rm C}\cdot n\int_{\delta+o(1)}^{1}\exp\left\{h_{n}y_{n}s-R(y_{n}s)\right\}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq{\rm C}\cdot n\int_{\delta+o(1)}^{1}\exp\left\{s(h_{n}y_{n}-R(y_{n}))\right\}{\rm d}s,

where in the last line we used the fact that
R(sy_{n})\sim s^{r}R(y_{n})
locally uniformly in
s\in(0,\infty)
.
We see that if we plug in (
41
) into the integral and use the fact that
s
is bounded away from
0
we will arrive at

\int_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{y_{n}}\psi^{\prime}(s)\mathbb{P}[X>s]{\rm d}s\leq\exp\left\{-(\log n)^{2}(1+nR(d_{n})^{2}/d_{n}^{2})/{\rm C}\right\}

which in turn yields our claim.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let Assumption

Proof.

The argument goes along the exact same lines as for Lemma
A.3
. Once again apply formula (

) with

,

and

. The estimate (
40
) is

∎
