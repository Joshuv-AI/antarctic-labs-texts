Favorite Downcrossing Sites of One-Dimensional Simple Random Walk
Chen-Xu Hao, Ze-Chun Hu, Ting Ma, Renming Song

Abstract




Abstract:
Random walk is a very important Markov process and has important applications in many fields. For a one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk
(S_{n})
, a site
x
is called a favorite downcrossing site at time
n
if its downcrossing local time at time
n
achieves the maximum among all sites. In this paper, we study the cardinality of the favorite downcrossing site set, and will show that with probability 1 there are only finitely many times at which there are at least four favorite downcrossing sites and three favorite downcrossing sites occurs infinitely often. Some related open questions will be introduced.

Keywords:
Random walk, favorite site, favorite edge, favorite downcrossing site, local time.

1
Introduction and main result

Let
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk with
S_{0}=0
. Following Tóth and Werner
[
37
]
, we define the number of upcrossings and downcrossings of the site
x
by time
n
to be

\displaystyle\xi_{U}(x,n):=\#\{0<k\leq n:S_{k}=x,S_{k-1}=x-1\},

\displaystyle\xi_{D}(x,n):=\#\{0<k\leq n:S_{k}=x,S_{k-1}=x+1\},

respectively. Hereafter,
\#D
denotes the cardinality of the set
D
. Define the local times of site
x
and edge
x
by

\displaystyle\xi(x,n):=\xi_{U}(x,n)+\xi_{D}(x,n),

\displaystyle L(x,n):=\xi_{U}(x,n)+\xi_{D}(x-1,n),

respectively, where edge
x
is between points
x-1
and
x
.

•

A site
x
is called a favorite (most visited) site of the random walk at time
n
if

\xi(x,n)=\max_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\xi(y,n).

The set of favorite sites of the random walk at time
n
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}(n)
.
(\mathcal{K}(n))_{n\geq 1}
is called the favorite site process of the one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk. We say that
three favorite sites
occurs at time
n
if
\#\mathcal{K}(n)=3
.

•

An edge
x
is called a favorite edge of the random walk at time
n
if

L(x,n)=\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}}L(y,n).

The set of favorite edges of the random walk at time
n
is denoted by
\mathcal{E}(n)
.
(\mathcal{E}(n))_{n\geq 1}
is called the favorite edge process of the one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk.
We say that
three favorite edges
occurs at time
n
if
\#\mathcal{E}(n)=3
.

•

A site
x
is called a favorite downcrossing site of the random walk at time
n
if

\xi_{D}(x,n)=\max_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\xi_{D}(y,n).

The set of favorite downcrossing sites of the random walk at time
n
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{D}(n)
.
(\mathcal{K}_{D}(n))_{n\geq 1}
is called the favorite downcrossing site process of the one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk. We say that
three favorite downcorssing sites
occurs at time
n
if
\#\mathcal{K}_{D}(n)=3
.

In
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
, we showed that with probability 1 three favorite edges occurs infinitely often, which complements the result in
[
37
]
(in which the authors showed that eventually there are no more than three favoriate edges), and disproves a conjecture mentioned in

[
37
, Remark 1, p. 368]
. In the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
,
we used the transience of the favorite downcrossing site process to show the transience of the favorite edge process. In fact, from
[
21
]
,
we can see that there is a close relation between favorite edges and favorite downcrossing sites, which can be regarded as one motivation of this paper.

In this paper, we study the cardinality of the favorite downcrossing site set, and
the main result of this paper is the following

Theorem 1.1
.

For one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk, with probability 1 there are only finitely many times at which there are at least four favorite downcrossing sites and three favorite downcrossing sites occurs infinitely often.

For the related problem of the number of favorite sites of one-dimensional simple symmetric random walks, there are many more references (see Shi and Tóth
[

]
for an overview).
This problem was posed by Erdős and Révész
[

]
. Tóth
[

]
proved that there are no more than three favorite sites eventually. Ding and Shen
[

proved that with probability 1 three favorite sites occurs infinitely often.

In addition to the study of the number of favorite sites,
a series of papers focus on the asymptotic behavior of favorite sites, see
[

,

,

,

,

]
. For papers on favorite sites of simple random walks in higher dimensions, we refer to
[

,

.
In addition, there are a number of papers
on favorite sites for other processes
including Brownian motion, symmetric stable process, Lévy processes, random walks in random environments and so on, see
[

.

In
[
21
, Proposition 2.4]
, we proved the following result.

Proposition 1.2
.

If
x\in\mathcal{E}(n)
, then
x-1\in\mathcal{K}_{D}(n)
.

By Proposition
1.2
and
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
,
it is obvious that in order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we need only to prove that with probability 1 there are only finitely many times at which there are at least four favorite downcrossing sites.

For
r\geq 1
, we define

\displaystyle f(r):=\#\left\{n\geq 1:S_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{D}(n),S_{n}=S_{n-1}-1,\#\mathcal{K}_{D}(n)=r\right\},

the number of times at which a new favorite downcrossing site appears, tied with
r-1
other favorite downcrossing sites. By definition
f(r)\geq f(r+1)
. Then in order to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, it is enough to show

Theorem 1.3
.

\mathbb{E}(f(4))<+\infty
.

Our proof is inspired by
[
36
]
and
[
37
]
. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present some preliminaries. In Section 3, we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In the final section,
we give some remarks and introduce several open questions.

Throughout the paper, we denote by
C
some positive constant whose value may vary even within one proof.

2
Preliminaries

We define the inverse local times by

\displaystyle T_{U}(x,k):=\min\{n\geq 1:\xi_{U}(x,n)=k\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{D}(x,k):=\min\{n\geq 1:\xi_{D}(x,n)=k\}.

We rewrite
f(4)
as

f(4)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}d(x),

where

\displaystyle d(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\textbf{1}_{\{S_{n-1}=x+1,S_{n}=x,x\in\mathcal{K}_{D}(n),\#\mathcal{K}_{D}(n)=4\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\textbf{1}_{\{T_{D}(x,k)=n,x\in\mathcal{K}_{D}(n),\#\mathcal{K}_{D}(n)=4\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\textbf{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{K}_{D}(T_{D}(x,k)),\#\mathcal{K}_{D}(T_{D}(x,k))=4\}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(f(4))=\sum_{x=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}(d(-x))+\sum_{x=0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}(d(x)),

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(d(x))=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(x\in\mathcal{K}_{D}(T_{D}(x,k)),\#\mathcal{K}_{D}(T_{D}(x,k))=4\right).

2.1
Branching processes and the Ray-Knight representation

In the remainder of this paper, we denote by
Y_{n}
a critical Galton-Watson branching process with geometric offspring distribution, and by
Z_{n},R_{n}
two related critical Galton-Watson branching processes with immigration in different ways.
More precisely, the definitions of these processes are as follows: Let
(X_{n,i})_{n,i}

be i.i.d. geometric variables with mean 1, that is, for all
k\geq 0,P(X_{n,i}=k)=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}
. We recursively define

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}X_{n,i},\ Z_{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{n}+1}X_{n,i},\ R_{n+1}=1+\sum_{i=1}^{R_{n}}X_{n,i}.

(2.2)

Then
Y_{n},Z_{n}
and
R_{n}
are Markov chains with state space

\mathbb{N}
and transition probabilities as follows:

\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}=j|Y_{n}=i)=\pi(i,j):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta_{0}(j),&\text{if~$i=0$},\\
&2^{-i-j}\frac{(i+j-1)!}{(i-1)!j!},&\text{if~$i>0$},\end{aligned}\right.

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n+1}=j|Z_{n}=i)=\rho(i,j):=\pi(i+1,j),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{n+1}=j|R_{n}=i)=\rho^{*}(i,j):=\pi(i,j-1).

(2.5)

These three processes are very useful tools for describing
the local time process of
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in the space variable, taken at certain stopping times. Let
x
be a fixed integer. We have the following two cases:

Case 1.

x\leq-1
. We define the following three processes:

•

(R_{n}^{(h)})_{0\leq n\leq-x-1}
is a Markov chain with transition probabilities
\rho^{*}(i,j)
and initial state
R^{(h)}_{0}=h
;

•

(Y_{n}^{(h-1)})_{0\leq n<\infty}
is a Markov chain with transition probabilities
\pi(i,j)
and initial state
Y^{(h-1)}_{0}=h-1
;

•

(Y_{n}^{{}^{\prime}(h)})_{0\leq n<\infty}
is a Markov chain with transition probabilities
\pi(i,j)
and initial state
Y_{0}^{{}^{\prime}(h)}=R_{-x-1}^{(h)}
.

We assume that the three processes are independent, except that
(Y_{n}^{{}^{\prime}(h)})
starts from

R_{-x-1}^{(h)}
.
We patch the above three processes together to a single process as follows:

\Delta_{x}^{(h)}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y_{y+1}^{{}^{\prime}(h)},&\text{if~$-1\leq y<+\infty$},\\
R_{y-x}^{(h)},&\text{if~$x\leq y\leq-1$},\\
Y_{x-y}^{(h-1)},&\text{if~$-\infty<y\leq x-1$}.\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

By the Ray-Knight theorem (
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
), we know that

\displaystyle(\xi_{D}(y,T_{D}(x,h)),y\in\mathbb{Z})\overset{{\rm law}}{=}(\Delta_{x}^{(h)}(y),y\in\mathbb{Z}).

(2.7)

Case 2.

x\geq 0
. Define the following three processes:

•

(Z_{n}^{(h-1)})_{0\leq n\leq x}
is a Markov chain with transition probabilities
\rho(i,j)
and initial state
Z_{0}^{(h-1)}=h-1
;

•

(Y_{n}^{(h)})_{0\leq n<\infty}
is a Markov chain with transition probabilities
\pi(i,j)
and initial state
Y_{0}^{(h)}=h
;

•

(Y_{n}^{{}^{\prime}(h-1)})_{0\leq n<\infty}
is a Markov chain with transition probabilities
\pi(i,j)
and initial state
Y_{0}^{{}^{\prime}(h-1)}=Z_{x}^{(h-1)}
.

In this case, we patch the three processes together by

\Gamma_{x}^{(h)}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y_{-y}^{{}^{\prime}(h-1)},&\text{if~$y\leq 0$},\\
Z_{x-y}^{(h-1)},&\text{if~$0\leq y\leq x-1$},\\
Y_{y-x}^{(h)},&\text{if~$y\geq x$}.\end{array}\right.

(2.8)

By
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
, we know that

\displaystyle(\xi_{D}(y,T_{D}(x,h)),y\in\mathbb{Z})\overset{{\rm law}}{=}(\Gamma_{x}^{(h)}(y),y\in\mathbb{Z}).

(2.9)

Remark 2.1
.

(i) In case 1, if
x=-1
, (
2.6
)should be modified as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}^{(h)}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y_{y+1}^{{}^{\prime}(h)},&\text{if~$-1\leq y<+\infty$},\\
Y_{x-y}^{(h-1)},&\text{if~$-\infty<y\leq-2$},\end{array}\right.

where
Y_{0}^{{}^{\prime}(h)}=h
.

(ii) In case 2, if
x=0
, (
2.8
) should modified as follows:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{x}^{(h)}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y_{-y}^{{}^{\prime}(h-1)},&\text{if~$y\leq-1$},\\
Y_{y-x}^{(h)},&\text{if~$y\geq 0$},\end{array}\right.

where
Y_{0}^{{}^{\prime}(h-1)}=h-1
.

(iii) In order to save space, we will not discuss the above two special cases separately in the corresponding proof below.

Note that the initial state of
(Y_{n})
(resp.
(R_{n})
) is
h-1
(resp.
h
) in the case 1, but the initial state of
(Y_{n})
(resp.
(Z_{n})
) is
h
(resp.
h-1
) in the case 2. For all the notations above, the initial state of other processes
(R_{n}),(Z_{n}),(Y_{n}^{\prime})
other than
(Y_{n})
will be omitted below. We will also use conditional probability

2.2
\mathbb{E}(d(x))
under the Ray-Knight representation

Let
Y_{n},Y^{\prime}_{n},Z_{n}
and
R_{n}
be the branching processes
defined in the above subsection.
For
h\in\mathbb{N}
, define the following stopping times:

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}:=\min\{n\geq 1:Y_{n}\geq h\},\ \sigma_{h}^{\prime}:=\min\{n\geq 1:Y_{n}^{\prime}\geq h\};

\displaystyle\tau_{h}:=\min\{n\geq 1:Z_{n}\geq h\},\ \tau_{h}^{\prime}:=\min\{n\geq 1:R_{n}\geq h\}.

(2.12)

Case 1:

Let
x\leq-1,h\geq 1,p\geq 0
be integers. We define

\displaystyle A_{h,p}:=\left\{\max_{1\leq n<\infty}Y_{n}^{(h-1)}\leq h,\#\{1\leq n<\infty:Y_{n}^{(h-1)}=h\}=p\right\},

(2.13)

\displaystyle A_{h,p}^{\prime}:=\left\{\max_{1\leq n<\infty}Y_{n}^{\prime}\leq h,\#\{1\leq n<\infty:Y_{n}^{\prime}=h\}=p\right\},

(2.14)

\displaystyle B_{x,h,p}:=\left\{\max_{1\leq n\leq-x-1}R_{n}\leq h,\#\{1\leq n\leq-x-1:R_{n}=h\}=p\right\}.

(2.15)

Note that the event
A_{h,p}^{\prime}
depends on
x
through the initial state of
(Y_{n}^{\prime})
. In the following, we will use conditional probabilities to indicate the initial state.

By (
2.7
), we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(d(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p+q+r=3}\sum_{h=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{h}\mathbb{P}(A_{h,p}|Y_{0}=h-1)\cdot\mathbb{P}(B_{x,h,q}\cap\{R_{-x-1}=l\}|R_{0}=h)

(2.16)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\mathbb{P}(A_{h,r}^{\prime}|Y_{0}^{\prime}=l).

For convenience, we substitute “
-x
” for “
x
”. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big(d(-x)\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{p+q+r=3}\sum_{h=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_{h,p}|Y_{0}=h-1)\cdot\left(\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{-x,h,q}|R_{0}=h)\right)

(2.17)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\left(\sup_{l\in[0,h]}\mathbb{P}(A_{h,r}^{\prime}|Y_{0}^{\prime}=l)\right).

Case 2:

Let
x\geq 0,h\geq 1,p\geq 0
be integers.
We define

\displaystyle C_{h,p}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\max_{1\leq n<\infty}Y_{n}^{(h)}\leq h,\#\{1\leq n<\infty:Y_{n}^{(h)}=h\}=p\right\},

(2.18)

\displaystyle C_{h,p}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\max_{1\leq n<\infty}Y_{n}^{\prime}\leq h,\#\{1\leq n<\infty:Y_{n}^{\prime}=h\}=p\right\},

(2.19)

\displaystyle D_{x,h,p}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\max_{1\leq n\leq x}Z_{n}\leq h,\#\{1\leq n\leq x:Z_{n}=h\}=p\right\}.

(2.20)

Note that the event
C_{h,p}^{\prime}
depends on
x
through the initial state of
(Y_{n}^{\prime})
.
In the following, we will use conditional probabilities to indicate the initial state.

By (
2.9
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(d(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p+q+r=3}\sum_{h=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{h}\mathbb{P}(C_{h,p}|Y_{0}=h)\cdot\mathbb{P}(D_{x,h,q}\cap\{Z_{x}=l\}|Z_{0}=h-1)

(2.21)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\mathbb{P}(C_{h,r}^{\prime}|Y_{0}^{\prime}=l),

which implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big(d(x)\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{p+q+r=3}\sum_{h=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(C_{h,p}|Y_{0}=h)\cdot\left(\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(D_{x,h,q}|Z_{0}=h-1)\right)

(2.22)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\left(\sup_{l\in[0,h]}\mathbb{P}(C_{h,r}^{\prime}|Y_{0}^{\prime}=l)\right).

2.3
Some lemmas

In order to give the proof of Theorem
1.3
in next section, we need several lemmas.

Lemma 2.2
.

(
[
36
, Side-lemma 3]
)
There exists a constant
C<\infty
such that for any
0\leq k\leq h
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{h}=\infty|Y_{0}=k)\leq\frac{h-k}{h}+Ch^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

By the above lemma and the definitions of the two processes
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(Y_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 0}
, we have

Lemma 2.3
.

There exists a constant
C<\infty
such that for any
0\leq k\leq h
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{h}^{\prime}=\infty|Y_{0}^{\prime}=k)\leq\frac{h-k}{h}+Ch^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Lemma 2.4
.

(
[
36
, Side-lemma 4]
)
There exists a constant
C<\infty
such that for any
0\leq k\leq h
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tau_{h}|Z_{0}=k)\leq(h-k)+Ch^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By following
[
36
, Corollary, p. 500]
,
we can get a similar result on
\tau_{h}^{\prime}
as follows.

Lemma 2.5
.

There exists a constant
C<\infty
such that for any
integer
h\geq 1
,
the following upper bound holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tau_{h}^{\prime}|R_{0}=h)\leq Ch^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.23)

Proof.

One can check that
(R_{t}-t)_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale. Then by the optional stopping theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tau_{h}^{\prime}|R_{0}=h)=\mathbb{E}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}|R_{0}=h)-h=-h+\sum_{u=h}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}\geq u|R_{0}=h).

(2.24)

We claim that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}\geq u|R_{0}=h)\leq\dfrac{\sum_{w=u}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,w)}{\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,v)}.

(2.25)

Note that, for
1\leq h\leq u
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}=u|R_{0}=h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{h-1}\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}=u,R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}-1}=l|R_{0}=h)

(2.26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{h-1}\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}-1}=l|R_{0}=h)\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}=u|R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}-1}=l,R_{0}=h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{h-1}\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}-1}=l|R_{0}=h)\cdot\dfrac{\rho^{*}(l,u)}{\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(l,v)}.

By (
2.3
) and (
2.5
), we know that for any
0\leq l<h\leq v
, it holds that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\rho^{*}(l+1,v)}{\rho^{*}(l,v)}=\dfrac{\pi(l+1,v-1)}{\pi(l,v-1)}=\frac{l+v-1}{2l}<\frac{l+v}{2l}=\dfrac{\pi(l+1,v)}{\pi(l,v)}=\dfrac{\rho^{*}(l+1,v+1)}{\rho^{*}(l,v+1)}.

It follows that for any
0\leq l<h\leq v<w
,

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(l+1,v)\cdot\rho^{*}(l,w)<\rho^{*}(l,v)\rho^{*}(l+1,w),

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(l,v)=\frac{2l}{l+v-1}\rho^{*}(l+1,v)\leq\rho^{*}(l+1,v),

which imply that for any
0\leq l<h\leq u
,

\displaystyle\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(l+1,v)\cdot\sum_{w=u}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(l,w)<\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(l,v)\cdot\sum_{w=u}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(l+1,w).

Then, by (
2.26
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}\geq u|R_{0}=h)\leq\max_{l^{\prime}\in[0,h]}\dfrac{\sum_{w=u}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(l^{\prime},w)}{\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(l^{\prime},v)}\leq\dfrac{\sum_{w=u}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,w)}{\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,v)},

i.e. (
2.25
) holds.

By (
2.25
) and some explicit computations,
we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{u=h}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}\geq u|R_{0}=h)\leq\dfrac{\sum_{u=h}^{\infty}\sum_{w=u}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,w)}{\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,v)}=\frac{\sum_{v=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,v)v}{\sum_{w=h}^{\infty}\rho^{*}(h,w)}\leq h+Ch^{1/2},

which together with (
2.24
) implies (
2.23
).
∎

(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}

Lemma 2.6
.

(
[
37

)
For any
\varepsilon>0

C(\varepsilon)<\infty
such that for

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{\tau_{h}}=h|Z_{0}=h)\leq C(\varepsilon)h^{-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}.

For
(R_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, by following
[
37
, Lemma 3.1(ii)]
, we can obtain the following result.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C(\varepsilon)<\infty
such that for
any integer
h>0
,

\displaystyle P(R_{\tau_{h}^{\prime}}=h|R_{0}=h)\leq C(\varepsilon)h^{-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}.

(2.27)

Proof.

We divide the proof into three steps:

Step 1.
Let
\frac{1}{2}\leq\alpha\leq 1
,
h-h^{\alpha}\leq z\leq h
, and
k\leq\sqrt{h}
. We have

\displaystyle\dfrac{\rho^{*}(z,h+k)}{\rho^{*}(z,h)}=\dfrac{\pi(z,h+k-1)}{\pi(z,h-1)}

\displaystyle=2^{-k}\dfrac{(h+z-1)(h+z+1)\cdots(h+z+k-2)}{h(h+1)\cdots(h+k-1)}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-k}\cdot\dfrac{(h+z-1)^{k}}{(h+k-1)^{k}}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-k}\cdot\dfrac{(2h-h^{\alpha}-1)^{k}}{(h+\sqrt{h}-1)^{k}}

\displaystyle\geq(1-2h^{\alpha-1})^{k},

where in the last inequality we used

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{2h-h^{\alpha}-1}{h+\sqrt{h}-1}\geq 1-2h^{\alpha-1}\Leftrightarrow\left(3h^{\alpha}-2\sqrt{h}\right)+4\left(h^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}-h^{\alpha-1}\right)+1\geq 0.

Then when
h
is large enough such that
1-2h^{\alpha-1}\in(0,1)
, we have

Remark 4.2

Define

We call

The set of favorite one-side sites of the random walk at time

It seems that it is more diffucult to understand the process

. As to one-dimensional simple symmetric random walk, we introduce the following questions:

Question 1.
With probability 1 there are only finitely many times at which there are at least four favorite one-side sites?

Question 2.
With probability 1 three favorite one-side sites occurs infinitely often?

Question 3.
Is the favorite one-side site process transient? If the answer is yes, how about the escape rate?
