CLAMP: Contrastive Language Model Prompt-tuning
Piotr Teterwak, Ximeng Sun, Bryan A. Plummer, Kate Saenko, Ser-Nam Lim

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful general-purpose interfaces for many machine learning problems. Recent work has adapted LLMs to visual tasks like image captioning, visual question answering, and visual chat, using a relatively small amount of instruction-tuning data. Surprisingly, as we show in this paper, the resulting multimodal-LLMs (mLLMs) are poor at classifying an image into a set of categories, and far worse than specialized models like CLIP. We explore whether modern LLMs can be adapted to perform this fundamental visual task, and propose an approach for paremeter-efficient fine-tuning of LLMs using the same contrastive image-caption matching objective as CLIP. Our results show that LLMs can, indeed, achieve good zero-shot image classification performance. Our approach beats state-of-the-art mLLMs by 13% and slightly outperforms contrastive learning with a custom text model (known as LiT), while also retaining the LLM’s generative abilities. LLM initialization appears to particularly help classification in domains under-represented in the visual pre-training data.

Introduction

In this paper, we present CLAMP, an approach to add classification abilities to a base LLM. Putting together prior mLLM adapter modules and CLAMP, LLM’s are now able to generate text, answer visually-grounded questions, chat interactively, and do zero-shot object classification.

In the past several years the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) have progressed from interesting to extremely useful. Recent work has adapted LLMs to multimodal inputs such as images,
by learning a mapping from a frozen visual backbone to a continuous token used as input to the LLM. Such multimodal LLMs (mLLMs) include LLaVA
[
28
]
, MiniGPT
[
57
]
, Instruct-BLIP
[
8
]
, and GPT-4V
[
33
]
and have impressive image captioning and question-answering capabilities. For example, they can use the LLM’s world knowledge to effectively answer common sense questions, such as why a photo is unusual (Fig.
1
left).

One might assume that the LLM’s world knowledge would similarly help in classifying objects into categories, such as what type of car is in the image (Fig.
1
). After all, an LLM can provide a detailed description of what different cars look like. In this paper we ask,
can multimodal LLMs also perform zero-shot classification tasks?
For instance, given a list of vehicle types and an image, can an mLLM identify which type of vehicle is present? We find that the answer is, surprisingly, not well. For example, state-of-the-art mLLMs get under 10% top-1 classification accuracy on Stanford Cars
[
21
]
, while CLIP achieves over 80% (Tab.
1
). This is despite the fact that the best classification algorithm for current mLLMs computes the likelihood for
each
image-label pair, inefficiently requiring many passes through the LLM (see Section
4.2
, Runtime). Poor classification performance of mLLMs is counter-intuitive; LLMs have been proven to encode extensive knowledge about the world. Furthermore, visual classification is a fundamental computer vision task, and is a capability we definitely want a foundation model to have.

We hypothesize that this weakness is due not to a lack of capability of the LLM but to improper training,
and that the generative captioning and instruction-tuning objectives do not enable effective classification. To build some intuition for this, consider that LLMs contain a powerful language prior which may improve visual-question answering even without image features; for example, the correct answer may simply have higher-likelihood in the language model, as illustrated by Visual GPT-Score
[
26
]
. In that work, it was shown that SoTA performance can be achieved on several vision-language benchmarks by ignoring the context image completely and just computing the likelihood of the text sequences using the language model.

Given our hypothesis that generative training is not sufficient for discriminative image tasks, but that LLMs still encode knowledge useful for those tasks, we propose to replace the text encoder of a vision-language pretrained model like CLIP
[
36
]
) with an LLM. We call our method
Contrastive LAnguage Model Prompt-tuning (CLAMP)
(Fig.

We find that by updating a minority of the LLM parameters, our method can approach the performance of zero-shot CLIP trained on much larger scale data while outperforming a text-encoder trained from scratch on the same data (LiT
[
54
]
). This finding highlights the benefits of using Large Language Models; LiT earlier found that initializing to pre-trained (but smaller scale) LMs is not helpful for zero-shot classification in English. We instead find that carefully finetuning modern, large language models is helpful relative to training a text-encoder from scratch, especially on evaluation datasets which have poor coverage in data used for contrastive training. Surprisingly, we’re
also
able to mostly retain the generative capabilities of the language model, pointing towards universal generative and discriminative models. Finally, because we use parameter-efficient finetuning methods for our training and the same base LLM as current instruction-tuned mLLMs, our method makes it possible to swap CLAMP’s finetuned parameters with those of mLLMs like LLaVA. As we illustrate in Fig.
1
, this enables captioning, VQA, chat and classification with the same foundational LLM.

In summary, our contributions are as the follows:

•

We show that SOTA multimodal LLMs are neither accurate nor efficient at zero-shot image classification.

•

We train a set of adapter modules with a contrastive loss, which enable an LLM to be used for visual discrimination tasks by replacing a text encoder in a contrastive vision-language model.

•

We show that the LLM initialization in CLAMP allows for improved performance in domains under-represented in the pre-training data, and that our CLAMP retains generative abilities despite our fine-tuning.

Related Work

Large Language Models.
Large Language Models are transformers
[
46
]
, scaled to billions of parameters, trained generatively on trillions of tokens. LLMs are commonly trained with next-token prediction. The success of LLM-based chatbots such as ChatGPT has inspired a great number of efforts to create both general
[
45
,
33
,
56
,
5
]
and domain specific
[
39
,
48
]
language models. Instruction-tuning additionally fine-tunes LLMs with (instruction, output) pairs, which makes LLMs more useful for specific tasks. In this work, we explore adapting an LLM to image classification.

Multimodal Large Language Models.
Until recently, large LMs have operated exclusively in the text domain. However, a plethora of models such as LLaVA
[
28
]
, GPT-4V
[
33
]
, MiniGPT
[
57
]
, Flamingo
[
1
]
, and BLIP
[
8
]
now can take images as input. These models typically use a pre-trained vision encoder and a small set of learnable parameters to map from vision encoder output to LLM input. They are typically trained with a captioning loss, and can additionally be tuned with an instruction-tuning objective. The base LLM can be frozen
[
57
]
or updated
[
45
]
. Multimodal LLMs work well for tasks such as visual question answering or captioning. However, we show that they are generally ineffective for zero-shot classification. But classification is a fundamental computer vision task, and one for which the world knowledge of LLMs is intuitively very useful. We show that with a Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, LLMs can perform discriminative visual reasoning.

Regularized Fine-tuning.
Also known as Parameter-Efficient Fine Tuning (PEFT), regularized fine-tuning aims to update only a subset of the parameters in a pre-trained model. This allows one to keep around only a small number of parameters for each specialized task a model is fine-tuned for, to use less memory in the training process, and to avoid unlearning knowledge present in the pre-trained model. Common approaches include low-rank updates of existing matrices (LoRA
[
17
]
), prompt and prefix tuning
[
25
,
29
,
24
]
, and inserting additional trainable layers into the middle of the network (Adapters
[
16
]
). In our work, we use prompt tuning and LoRA to adapt the LLM to the contrastive text-image task.

Contrastive Training for Vision-Language Models.
Contrastive training has become a popular training technique. It aims to align positive pairs in embedding space, while pushing negative pairs apart. Therefore, this type of method strongly depends on how positive and negative pairs are formed. Vision-Language models (
e.g
.
CLIP
[
36
]
) use a text-encoder and contrast against text captions
[
36
,
54
,
52
]
, therefore learning a joint image-text embedding. This joint embedding space enables Vision-Language models to classification in the zero-shot setting, without training data with explicit labels. In this work, we add a contrastive objective to fine-tune an LLM, and therefore enable the LLM to function as an effective zero-shot visual classifier.

3
CLAMP: Contrastive LAnguage Model Prompting

We propose
Contrastive LAnguage Model Prompt-tuning (CLAMP)
, a method to adapt Large Language Models to classification via prompting and contrastive learning.
Our approach is inspired by state-of-the-art vision-language models trained with the contrastive objective,
i.e
.
, CLIP. The objective encourages a strong alignment between text and image representations in a joint space, enabling effective-zero shot classification. We propose to align LLM representations with visual representations using a contrastive loss. However, the output embedding space of a frozen LLM may not be naively easy to align with a visual encoder for a discriminative task, because the LLM was trained for next-token prediction instead of cross-modal alignment. Instead of using a fully frozen LLM, CLAMP uses PEFT (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning) to lightly update the model to align it with CLIP visual encoders.

CLAMP accomplishes this in three main ways (Section
3.1
). First, it learns a pooling over the LLM’s output representations to aggregate information over the entire sequence. We implement this with attention-pooling, with a query token that learns to aggregate the output representations of an encoded caption. However, output pooling is insufficient to get the LLM to produce useful information that is embedded in the remaining network layers. To overcome this, CLAMP adds learned read-only prompts
[
24
]
, which serve as special-purpose discriminative tokens in a model pre-trained for generation. This enables CLAMP to extract information from intermediate network layers, to align LLM embeddings with visual embeddings. Finally, we find that slight modification of the parameters improves zero-shot performance further, accomplished with LoRA, which learns low-rank delta updates of certain layers.

Section
3.2
details the text prompt used in training and Section
3.3
describes the training objective, which includes distillation of a stronger CLIP model into our trained model, inspired by Dime-FM
[
42
]
.

3.1
Architecture

Our goal is to align an LLM with a pre-trained visual encoder contrastively (see Fig.
3
). In our experiments, we use the Vicuna-1.5 LLM
[
4
]
, which is a LLaMA-2 based instruction-tuned model. In order to avoid overfitting, we only add a small number of trainable modules including Output Attention Pooling at the output, Read-Only Prompts at the input, and LoRA in the intermediate layers. Similar to many mLLMs, we freeze the visual encoder.

Read-only Prompts

Frozen LLMs are generative models, therefore in order to add discriminative capabilities we need to modify the model. However, doing so without harming the good representations learned during pre-training requires care, and one method used to do so is prompt tuning. We append learnable prompts to the end of the text sequence, which function as special-purpose tokens for discriminative tasks:

x=[x_{\text{text}};p_{\text{learnable}}]

(1)

where
x
is the input sequence to the LLM,
x_{\text{text}}
is the tokenized language and
p_{\text{learnable}}
are the learnable prompts.
The LLM we use has causal attention, meaning each position’s representation can only attend to tokens previously seen in the sequence. When attention is bi-directional, the intermediate representations of
x_{\text{text}}
can shift with changes in
p_{\text{learnable}}
. With causal attention, however,
p_{\text{learnable}}
can change without updating intermediate representations of
x_{\text{text}}
. Such a technique is known as read-only prompting
[
24
]
, and it is shown to improve generalization of prompt-tuning of contrastive vision-language models to unseen classes, due to an effect of minimizing overfitting. To produce the text embedding, we perform output attention pooling over the output representations of the learnable prompt tokens.

Output Attention Pooling

Transformer networks take as input variable-length sequences, and output sequences of that same length. However, the joint image-text embedding space of vision-language models requires a representation of a fixed length. Therefore, some kind of pooling of tokens is needed. CLIP
[
36
]
accomplishes this by simply using the last token in the sequence as the representation of the text. We instead use the read-only prompts described in the previous section. However, since we use many of those prompts, we still need to pool them to a fixed length. We choose to leverage a form of pooling that uses the multi-headed attention mechanism, where
W_{Q}
,
W_{K}
,
W_{O}
, and
W_{V}
are parameter matrices.

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{Concat}(\text{head}_{1},\ldots,\text{head}_{h})W_{O}\\

(2)

where

\text{head}_{i}=\text{Attention}(QW_{Qi},KW_{Ki},VW_{Vi})\\

(3)

and

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{Softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V

(4)

In our attention pooling mechanism, we learn the query Q and the K and V are simply output tokens of the LLM. In this way, we can learn a per-sample linear combination of LLM outputs.

LoRA

While Attention Pooling and Read-Only Pooling are important architectural components in extracting discriminative features from text sequences, discriminative performance can further be improved if the network parameters can be lightly updated. One option is full-finetuning of the network parameters. This, however, has two large drawbacks. First, it has heavy memory requirements. In particular, for the large-batch training necessary for effective contrastive learning, full fine-tuning requires the storage of intermediate activation. Secondly, we go back to the motivation of not catastrophically forgetting information present in the LLM in our contrastive training. Therefore, we leverage LoRA, which learns low rank updates to pretrained models:

W=W_{0}+BA^{T}

(5)

where
W
are the updated weights in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and
B
and
A
are in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
.

As is common, we only update multi-headed attention layers with our LoRA updates. See the Supplementary for exact LoRA configuration.

3.2
Wrapper prompts

Vicuna is trained with a very specific prompt template:

“
A chat between a curious user and an artificial intelligence assistant. The assistant gives helpful, detailed, and polite answers to the user’s questions.

USER: Hello!

ASSISTANT: Hello!".

In order to follow this format of USER followed by ASSISTANT, we use the following prompt template for our training:

“USER: A photo of a
{}
. A
{}
has the following visual attributes.

ASSISTANT: 1.”

where the {} is replaced with the caption of a particular sample. At test time, {} is replaced with the class name. Then, this text becomes
x_{\text{text}}
and the final input to the CLAMP LLM is
x=[x_{\text{text}};p_{\text{learnable}}]
. The
ASSISTANT: 1
, would prompt the model to generate a response, if it were being using auto-regressively. Auto-regressive decoding of this prompt would result in a description of what the image with the caption may look like. Although auto-regressive decoding in the training loop is prohibitively expensive; Xiao
et al
.

[
49
]
show that much information about the decoded sequence is present in the initial tokens, and therefore prompting the LLM to describe the caption is a reasonable way to surface visual information.

3.3
Contrastive Training of LLMs

So far, we have discussed the architectural components of our method. We now turn our attention to the training methodology. Following CLIP
[
36
]
, we use image-text and text-image contrastive losses:

\mathcal{L}_{img2txt}=-\sum_{i}\log\frac{\exp(\tau v^{T}_{i}t_{i})}{\sum_{j}\exp(\tau v_{i}^{T}t_{j})}

(6)

\mathcal{L}_{txt2img}=-\sum_{i}\log\frac{\exp(\tau t^{T}_{i}v_{i})}{\sum_{j}\exp(\tau t_{i}^{T}v_{j})}

(7)

\mathcal{L}_{contrastive}=\mathcal{L}_{img2txt}+\mathcal{L}_{txt2img}

(8)

where
t
is a text representation,
v
is a visual representation, and
i
and
j
index the batch.
\tau
is a learned temperature parameter.

In addition, we add a distillation loss term to the training, similar to Dime-FM
[
42
]
. Following
[
42
]
, we first compute teacher similarity matrix
S_{t}
and student similarity matrix
S_{s}
using the vision and text encoders over samples in the batch, as in the contrastive loss. Defining row-wise indexing variable as
i
and column-wise indexing variable as
j
, the distillation loss is defined as:

\small\mathcal{L}_{dist}=\sum_{i}KL(\tau_{d}S_{t}||\tau_{d}S_{s})+\sum_{j}KL(\tau_{d}S_{t}^{T}||\tau_{d}S_{j}^{T})

(9)

Finally, our loss becomes:

\small\mathcal{L}_{tot}=\mathcal{L}_{contrastive}+\mathcal{L}_{dist}

(10)

During inference time, we simply take the argmax over classes of the dot product of an input image features
x
and the CLAMP representations of class labels
t_{c}
to produce predicted class label
y_{pred}
.

\small y_{pred}=\argmax_{c}{xt_{c}^{T}}

(11)

Training and Evaluation Setup:
For the text encoder, we use a pre-trained Vicuna13B-1.5
[
4
]
, which uses LLaMa2 as a base model. We enable Attention Pooling, Read-Only Prompting with 24 soft prompts, and LoRA (Section
3.1
) as the text encoder trainable parameters. For the vision encoder, we use an OpenClip pretrained ViT-B-16, and keep it frozen. We train with a data mixture of Conceptual Captions 3M
[
38
]
, Conceptual Captions 12M
[
2
]
, a 200-million scale subset of LAION-400M
[
37
]
, and ImageNet-21k
[
9
]
. All of these datasets, except for ImageNet21k, are image-caption datasets. In contrast, ImageNet21k is a dataset of approximately 21000 labels. In order to convert this to an image-caption dataset, we wrap each label in one of the 80 prompt templates used in the CLIP paper
[
36
]
. We train for 480 million seen examples, with a batch size of 8192. We use the standard image-text contrastive loss in addition to distilling a ViT-L-14 trained on DataComp XL
[
12
]
into the model. Additional training details are in the supplementary. When we do zero-shot evaluation, we use a prompt template matching the training prompt (Section
3.2
).

Baselines:

MiniGPT4
[
57
]
:
MiniGPT4 learns a linear mapping between the output of a pre-trained CLIP encoder and a frozen LLM, trained using Conceptual Captions. In our experiments, we use a MiniGPT4 with a 16-bit Vicuna13B model.

LLava-1.5
[
27
]
:
LLaVa-1.5 is similar to MiniGPT in architecture, but with a different data mixture. In particular, LLava-1.5 combines academic VQA datasets with an expansion of COCO by ChatGPT
[
28
]
to create a vision-language instruction-following dataset. LLaVA-1.5 also finetunes the LLM with LoRA.

LiT
[
54
]
:
To compare to state-of-the art contrastive vision-language models, we train a LiT model, which keeps the vision encoder frozen while updating all text encoder parameters. We initialize with a ViT-B-16 CLIP text encoder from scratch, and train on the same data and for the same length as our model. For LiT models, we use an evaluation prompt template of
‘A photo of
{
class
}
’
. We use a single prompt template for evaluating both LiT and CLAMP.

4.1
Multi-modal LLMs as Zero-shot Classifiers

We compare our method to the multimodal Large Language Models by computing the likelihood of an image-class caption sequence, following Flamingo
[
1
]
and VisualGPTScore
[
26
]
. In more detail, given an image
I
and a set of class labels, for each class label
c
consisting of one or more tokens, we compute the likelihood of sequence
P(\text{`A photo of {c}'}|I)
. We additionally normalize by the sequence length, because longer sequences generally have lower likelihood and this is not a sensible prior for classification. Then, the score for each class is:

\small\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(P(t_{i}|t_{0..i-1};I))

(12)

where
N
is the sequence length,
I
is the image to be classified, and
t
are the sequence tokens. To classify, we simply select the score with the highest normalized log-likelihood.

We present the performance of our model in Table

. We find that on average, we outperform generatively-trained LLaVA-v1.5 and miniGPT-4 by 13% and the LiT baseline by half a percent. This demonstrates that adding a contrastive loss to an LLM, together with Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, adds a visual discriminative ability to LLMs which current SOTA captioning models do not have. We further analyze where we outperform LiT in Section
4.2
, and find that our method is particularly useful when there is little target data present in the pre-training data (see
count
in
Coverage Analysis
).

Runtime:
Applying mLLMs using likelihoods (GPT-Score) to do zero-shot classification is extremely inefficient; for example evaluating LLaVA on the ImageNet test set takes 12 hours 52 minutes on 10 GPUs. This is because each image needs to be forwarded through the LLM N times, where N is the number of classes. In contrast, it takes our method minutes for the same evaluation, as we can precompute the text embeddings ahead of time and we avoid the combinatorial explosion of early fusion.

Concept Overlap:
One benefit of large scale pre-training is improved performance on data which is similar to the pre-training data, but less similar to the fine-tuning data. To check whether this is the case with our models, we utilize a metric from
[
51
]
called Concept Coverage to measure the presence of evaluation data in the pre-training dataset. The methodology uses spaCy
1
1

1

https://spacy.io/
. Following
[
51
]
, we iterate over all the captions in our LAION subset and extract noun chunks, adding them into a dictionary. Finally, we filter out all noun chunks which appear fewer than 5 times. Then, we compute two metrics for each downstream dataset. First, we check how many class labels are present in the concept dictionary, and report this as fraction of all labels which we call
coverage
. Second, we compute the average number of pre-training images per class label in the target set, and call this
count
. We present the results in Table
1
.

We can observe two things. First, there is a strong correlation between absolute performance and concept counts. When we split the evaluation datasets into “high count" and “low count", “high count" has much higher average performance. Second, in the
“low count"
regime , our model outperforms the LiT baseline on average. For example, Cars only has about 432 samples per vehicle type, and our model outperforms the LiT baseline by over 4%. Similarly, our pre-training data only has about 170 samples per aircraft type, and our model outperforms the LiT baseline by about 7%. In other words, the knowledge imported by the very large-scale text-based pre-training of an LLM allows us to generalize better than simply training on image-caption pairs. This highlights the benefits of LLM-based discriminative zero-shot classification. Furthermore, LiT doesn’t have any generative abilities.

Two datasets which appear to refute the rule are Kitti (with very high coverage and count, but lower performance) and PatchCamelyon (low coverage count, and also lower performance). PatchCamelyon is a dataset of histopathologic scans of lymph node sections annotated with a binary label indicating presence of cancer. This is a very difficult concept to describe in natural language; in fact humans trained to do this task must be highly specialized and undergo many years of
training. Therefore, a model pre-trained on language data may not be appropriate. The second dataset is Kitti. Looking more closely at the Kitti class labels, we have the set
\{
‘a photo of a photo i took of a car on my left or right side’,‘a photo i took with a car nearby’,‘a photo i took with a car in the distance.’,‘a photo i took with no car.
\}
. These labels have nouns which are not discriminative, ‘car’ and ‘photo’, so the concept coverage heuristic is not fully relevant for this dataset. Instead the descriminative labels are adjectives corresponding to distance.

4.3
Generative Abilities of Finetuned Models

As one of our aims is to take steps towards model universality, an important metric is whether we retain generative capabilities in our models even after our discriminative finetuning. One could expect that contrastive adaptation would ruin the generative abilities of the model.

We show qualitative examples in Table
2
. From inspection, the results are quite reasonable. We also measure performance on MMLU
[
15
]
( an LLM test of 57 tasks including elementary mathematics, US history, computer science, law, and more. To attain high accuracy on this test, models must possess extensive world knowledge and problem solving ability), DROP
[

On DROP the degradation is lesser, from 27.73 to 24.93. On BBH the metric falls from 38.42 to to 32.11. We emphasize that if the user wants to maximize generative performance, they can disable the LoRA parameters during inference.

4.4
Effect of Data Scale:

Data scale is an extremely important part of vision-language pretraining in general. We confirm that data scale remains very important even with our strong language prior by subampling our data and training. In Figure
4
, we show top-1 ImageNet performance as we scale data. As data grows, so does top-1 accuracy.

4.5
Regularized Fine-tuning Alternatives:

One of our aims is to retain the generative ability of the large language model; and as a result we regularize our fine-tuning so that our model does not catastrophically forget that capability. We choose to combine Read-Only Prompts (Section
3.1
) and LoRA (Section
3.1
). However, this is not the only choice. In fact, Qi et al.
[
35
]
show that combining LayerNorm tuning with prompt tuning is amongst the most effective tuning methods. LayerNorm tuning is even lighter weight than tuning LoRA parameters, and therefore if effective would be well-suited for our purposes. However, as we show in Table
3

Conclusion

In this work, we explore how to make multi-modal LLMs work more effectively for zero-shot classification. We start by showing that SOTA models such as LLaVA or miniGPT-4 do not perform well for zero-shot classification, and underperform models trained with a contrastive captioning loss by over 10%. We then show that we can add discriminative abilities to pre-trained Large Language Models by aligning an LLM with a pre-trained vision encoder with a contrastive loss, and only performing a lightweight finetuning. We show that our contrastively trained LLM is effective at zero-shot classification, and outperforms contrastively trained baselines in the regime where pre-training data does not overlap with the evaluation dataset. Finally, we show that, despite fine-tuning, our model retains generative capabilities.
