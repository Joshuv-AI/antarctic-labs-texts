Input Decoupling of Lagrangian Systems via Coordinate Transformation: General Characterization and its Application to Soft Robotics
Pietro Pustina, Cosimo Della Santina, Frédéric Boyer, Alessandro De Luca, Federico Renda

Abstract
Suitable representations of dynamical systems can simplify their analysis and control. On this line of thought, this paper aims to answer the following question: Can a transformation of the generalized coordinates under which the actuators directly perform work on a subset of the configuration variables be found? Not only we show that the answer to this question is yes , but we also provide necessary and sufficient conditions. More specifically, we look for a representation of the configuration space such that the right-hand side of the dynamics in Euler-Lagrange form becomes [ 𝑰 ​ 𝑶 ] T ​ 𝒖 [\boldsymbol{I}\;\boldsymbol{O}]^{T}\boldsymbol{u} , being 𝒖 \boldsymbol{u} the system input. We identify a class of systems, called collocated , for which this problem is solvable. Under mild conditions on the input matrix, a simple test is presented to verify whether a system is collocated or not. By exploiting power invariance, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions that a change of coordinates decouples the input channels if and only if the dynamics is collocated. In addition, we use the collocated form to derive novel controllers for damped underactuated mechanical systems. To demonstrate the theoretical findings, we consider several Lagrangian systems with a focus on continuum soft robots.



I
Introduction

Electrical, hydraulic, and mechanical systems, or their combinations, are Lagrangian systems that usually exhibit complex behavior. However, their physical nature displays special properties, such as symmetry and passivity, which have been exploited to solve many control problems
[
1
,

,

,

,

]
, otherwise difficult to address for generic nonlinear dynamics. To cope with their high nonlinearity and large number of degrees of freedom (DOF), representations with specific structures play a crucial role in simplifying analysis, as well as control design and synthesis. For example, coordinate transformations are often used to highlight some internal structure that simplify derivation of feedback controllers for robotic systems and prove their stability
[
6
,
7
,
8
,

]
.

\boldsymbol{u}

\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}=\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

This paper considers the Input Decoupling (ID) problem for input-affine Lagrangian systems. In particular, given a Lagrangian system whose inputs enter the second-order equations of motion through a configuration-dependent actuation matrix, we study under which conditions on the definition of generalized coordinates each input affects one and only one equation of motion.
The coordinates solving the ID problem constitute a set of variables that simplify control design and synthesis because the input directly affects the equations of motion in a decentralized form. If the decoupling coordinates are regarded as system outputs, any control law implemented in these coordinates automatically decouples also the input-output channels.
For fully actuated or overactuated Lagrangian systems, i.e., when the number of independent inputs is equal or larger than the number of generalized coordinates, the ID problem can be solved by a configuration-dependent input transformation that inverts (or pseudoinverts) the actuation matrix. On the other hand, in the case of underactuated systems, a solution is available so far if the actuation matrix is constant and requires only a linear change of generalized coordinates
[
10
]
.

For multi-input-multi-output (MIMO) plants described in state space form, the input-output decoupling problem is solved by an inversion-based feedback controller, both in the linear
[
11
]
and nonlinear case
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. For this, a feedback transformation is needed that requires a state and input transformation together with a feedback action.
In particular, input-output decoupling is possible by a static state feedback if and only if the system has well defined vector relative degree, namely a nonsingular decoupling matrix
[
15
,
16
]
. This result can be used to solve the ID problem for Lagrangian systems by taking the configuration variables (or a subset of them) as system outputs. In fact, in this way, the system has a vector relative degree, with all outputs having uniform relative degree two. It can be shown that, in case of underactuation, the necessary and sufficient condition is the involutivity of the distribution spanned by the columns of the actuation matrix
[
17
]
.
Unfortunately, involutivity is not easy to check, especially for high dimensional dynamics, and has to be evaluated case by case, see
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
. Furthermore, finding the input decoupling state variables in this case requires solving a system of nonlinear partial differential equations
[
16
, Chap. 5]
, which could be impractical for control synthesis.
Finally, in
[
22
]
, the authors have proposed input transformations for dynamic processes that achieve exact linearization and input decoupling, under complete model knowledge. However, the analysis is restricted to systems with equal number of states, inputs and outputs.

In this paper, we focus on Lagrangian systems and we show that the choice of particular coordinates, called
actuation coordinates
, solves the ID problem without requiring a configuration-dependent transformation of the input nor a state-feedback.
We derive necessary and sufficient conditions under which the actuation coordinates exist and constructively show that these coordinates solve the ID problem for fully actuated, overactuated, and underactuated Lagrangian systems. In particular, the unactuated coordinates remain arbitrary when the system is underactuated.

These results stem from power invariance under change of coordinates, as graphically illustrated in Fig.
1
for the fully actuated case. Remarkably, similar considerations hold also when the dynamics is underactuated.
We apply the results to several mechanical examples as archetypal Lagrangian systems. In addition, we prove that robotic systems driven by thread-like actuators, such as inelastic tendons or thin fluidic chambers, always admit actuation coordinates.
Our results have relevant consequences on the control of continuum soft robots and other underactuated mechanical systems. Indeed, recent control laws for planar underactuated soft robots
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
10
,
26
]
generalize to all collocated mechanical systems with damping, such as soft robots moving in 3D.
Interestingly, the energy-based regulator in
[
27
]
uses in fact actuation coordinates to decouple the equations of motion of a 3-DOF underactuated soft robotic system.
Similarly, in flexible link robots, the actuation coordinates are the clamped angles at the base of each beam, which have been extensively used in control design
[
28
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce the notation and formalize the ID problem. Section
III

collocated

In Sec.

\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{L}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}})

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right)^{T}-\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right)^{T}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\boldsymbol{u}),\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u},\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

{\boldsymbol{q}}

1

n

II-C

Problem statement

We look for a change of coordinates
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
from
\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{q}})\subset\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{N}
where each of the first
r
equations of motion in (
1
) is affected by one, and only one, independent actuator input, i.e., the right-hand side of the transformed equations of motion (see Property
2
in Appendix
-A
) takes the form

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{u}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\\
\boldsymbol{O}_{n-r\times r}\end{array}\right)\boldsymbol{u}.

(2)

We refer to such problem as the
Input Decoupling (ID) problem
for the Lagrangian dynamics (
1

) admits a

. Note that (

III
Collocated Lagrangian Systems

In this section, we characterize a new class of Lagrangian systems, which we call
collocated
because only such Lagrangian dynamics admit a collocated form under a change of generalized coordinates. In addition, a set of coordinates that solve the ID problem come for free without further system analysis.

To this end, we will exploit a concept that is known as the
passive output
in the context of passivity-based control
[
29
]
.
Consider the following vector function linear in the velocity

\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}},

which is called the passive output because (
1
) is passive with respect to the pair
(\boldsymbol{u},\dot{\boldsymbol{y}})
, with the storage function being the system Hamiltonian (see Appendix
-A
).
We will assume that (
1
) has
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}
integrable, i.e.,

Integrability assumption.

For all
{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a function
\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}}):\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{q}}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}({\boldsymbol{q}}).

(3)

If the passive output is integrable, then we say that the Lagrangian system (
1
) is collocated because it admits a collocated form as defined in Section
II-C
.
Furthermore, we define
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
as
actuation coordinates
because, in such coordinates,
\boldsymbol{u}
acts directly on the equations of motion according to (
2
).

Remark
.

Each component of
\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is defined up to a constant since any function
\bar{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})+\boldsymbol{k}
, with
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, satisfies the condition
\boldsymbol{J}_{\bar{\boldsymbol{g}}}=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{g}}
.

The integrability assumption requires each column of
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
to be the gradient of a scalar function of the configuration variables. If
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is constant, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}
, then
\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}{\boldsymbol{q}}
.
More in general, when the column
\boldsymbol{A}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})
has continuous partial derivatives,
\dot{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
is integrable
[
30
, Chap. 2]
if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{ji}}{\partial q_{k}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{ki}}{\partial q_{j}};\quad\forall j,k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}.

(4)

Note that this condition is equivalent to asking that, when
u_{i}
is constant, the generalized work done by
u_{i}
on
{\boldsymbol{q}}
does not depend on the system trajectories but only on the initial and final configurations
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{a}
and
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{b}
, respectively, i.e.,

W_{u_{i}}({\boldsymbol{q}}):=\int^{{\boldsymbol{q}}_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}_{a}}u_{i}\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})d{\boldsymbol{q}}=u_{i}\left[g_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}}_{b})-g_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}}_{a})\right],

where the last equality follows from the Gradient Theorem
[
30
, Prop. 1, Chap. 2]
.
In other words,
P_{i}(u_{i},{\boldsymbol{q}}):=u_{i}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})
plays the role of a potential energy for the dynamics. If the actuation matrix is obtained using a differential formalism, such as the virtual works principle, it is reasonable to expect–although without any guarantee–that the integrability holds due to the inherent differentiation involved. It is also worth observing the following.

Remark
.

If
\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is integrable according to (
3
), then the orthogonal complement to the co-distribution spanned by
\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
satisfies the Frobenius theorem.
However, in general, the inverse implication does not hold without also an input transformation.

Even when (
4
) is satisfied, it could be challenging to integrate the passive output
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}
in closed form. Nonetheless, it is always possible to perform the numerical integration online based on the measure of
{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
. Assuming an exact knowledge of
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
and neglecting integration errors, there is formally no difference in having
\boldsymbol{y}
in closed form or computing it online. Furthermore, in many cases, the numerical integration should not be necessary. This is because the actuation coordinates are inherently related to the system inputs and should be easily measurable.

In some cases, the integrability may come directly as a consequence of the physical nature of the system, as illustrated in the following.

Example 1 (Cartesian forces on a robot).

Inspired by
[
31
]
, consider a manipulator with
n
-DOF subject to
m
external forces applied on its structure. Assume that each force
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
changes its magnitude over time but keeps the same direction in the global Cartesian frame so that

\boldsymbol{f}_{i}=\boldsymbol{d}_{\boldsymbol{f},i}u_{i};\quad i\in\{1,\cdots,m\},

with
{\left\|{\boldsymbol{d}_{\boldsymbol{f},i}}\right\|}=1
, and where
\boldsymbol{d}_{\boldsymbol{f},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represents the direction of
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
its magnitude. If
\boldsymbol{p}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the point of application of
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
in the global frame, then the effect of the force in the dynamics is

\tau_{{\boldsymbol{q}},i}=\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{f}_{i}=\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{d}_{\boldsymbol{f},i}u_{i}=\boldsymbol{A}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})u_{i}.

Thus, one can integrate
\boldsymbol{A}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})
as
g_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{d}_{\boldsymbol{f},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{p}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})
.

IV
Change of Coordinates

for Fully Actuated Systems

In this section, we show that when the system is fully actuated, the existence of
n
actuator coordinates is necessary and sufficient to bring the dynamics to the collocated form. This case allows illustrating the results in the most simple scenario, where an input transformation is sufficient to solve the ID problem. However, the same arguments will be used also for underactuated systems.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose the system is fully actuated, i.e.,
m=n
. There exists a change of coordinates
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}}):\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{q}})\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
such that (
1
) takes the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)^{T}-\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{T}=\boldsymbol{u},

(5)

i.e.,
\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}
, if and only if the integrability assumption (
3
) holds.
Let
\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
be the integral of
\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
. Then, a possible choice for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
.

Proof.

Under the integrability assumption,
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
defines a change of coordinates because its Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
has rank
n
at
{\boldsymbol{q}}
.

Since the generalized power is coordinate invariant (Property
2
in Appendix
-A
), it follows

\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}.

Noting that
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
and using
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u}
, the above equation rewrites as

\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u},

or, equivalently,

\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u}\right)=0.

(6)

Since (
6
) holds for all
\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\in T_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\mathcal{M}
, it follows that

\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}.

Furthermore,
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}({\boldsymbol{q}})
leads to

\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{u}\right)=\boldsymbol{0}.

The above equation defines a homogeneous linear system in the unknown
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{u}
, which admits the unique solution
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{u}
since
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is nonsingular, thus yielding the sufficiency of (
5
).

As for the necessity, suppose that a change of coordinates
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
exists such that (
5
) holds. Property
2
implies that, for all
\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\in T_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\mathcal{M}
,

\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\right)\boldsymbol{u}=0,

leading to

\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\right)\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0};\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

If one chooses
\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{I}_{n})_{i};\ i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
, then

\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\right)\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}}))_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}.

Thus, it holds
\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
and
\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
.
∎

Outside of singularities

and the integrability test (

\displaystyle\small\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{11}}{\partial q_{2}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(-l_{1}\mathrm{s}_{1}\right)}{\partial q_{2}}=0=\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{21}}{\partial q_{1}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{21}}{\partial q_{2}}

The passive output is integrable as

IV-A

Overactuated case

The previous result extends to overactuated systems, namely dynamics with more inputs than generalized coordinates. Thus, we have
r=n<m
.
We partition
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
, which is a wide matrix, as

\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})&\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o}({\boldsymbol{q}})\end{array}\right),

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m-n)}
.
Without loss of generality, we can have that
\mathrm{rank}\left(\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\right)=n
and the integrability condition holds for
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})
.

Corollary 1
.

If the system is overactuated, i.e.,
r=n<m
and the same hypotheses of Theorem
1
hold for
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})
, then there exists a change of coordinates
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}}):\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{q}})\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
such that (
1
) takes the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)^{T}-\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{T}=\left(\boldsymbol{I}_{n}\quad\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{o,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)\boldsymbol{u},

(8)

where

\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{o,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1\!}_{a}\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{o}\left({\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{h}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m-n)}.

If
\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is the integral of
\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
, then
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be chosen as
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
.

Proof.

Choosing again
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
and following steps similar to those of the proof of Theorem
1
, one obtains

\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u}.

Expanding
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
into (
7
) and left-multiplying the above equation by
\boldsymbol{A}^{-1\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})
gives

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}&=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})&\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o}({\boldsymbol{q}})\end{array}\right)\boldsymbol{u}\\
&=(\,\,\boldsymbol{I}_{n}\quad\underbrace{\boldsymbol{A}^{-1\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o}({\boldsymbol{q}})}_{\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{o,\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\,\,)\boldsymbol{u},\end{split}

with
{\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

Now, assume a change of coordinates
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
exists such that (
8
) holds.
After some computations, power invariance leads to the algebraic system

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})({\boldsymbol{q}})-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})&\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o,\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{q}})-\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o}({\boldsymbol{q}})\end{array}\right)\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0},

which must hold for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. By taking
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{I}_{m})_{i};\ i\in\{1,\cdots,m\}
, it follows
\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{o,\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{q}})
. Hence, at least
n
passive outputs are integrable as
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
.
∎

Note that it is not possible, in general, to simplify the expression of both terms in the actuation matrix because there are too many input variables to be decoupled.

V
Change of Coordinates

for Underactuated Systems

We now focus on underactuated systems, specifically dynamics for which the number of independent actuator inputs is less than that of degrees of freedom. In the following, we show that also in this case the actuation coordinates solve the input decoupling problem. First, it is convenient to expand the actuation matrix as

\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\\
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{u}({\boldsymbol{q}})\end{array}\right),

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is nonsingular and
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{u}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-m)\times m}
. Note that such partition is always possible after reordering the linearly independent rows of
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})
. Furthermore, since
n>r=m
,
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}
is an
m
-dimensional vector.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that (
1
) is underactuated, i.e.,
m<n
. The integrability assumption is a necessary and sufficient condition for a change of coordinates
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}}):\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{q}})\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
to exist such that (
1
) becomes

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)^{T}-\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{u}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{n-m}\end{array}\right),

(10)

i.e.,
\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
has the form

\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{I}_{n}\\
\boldsymbol{O}_{(n-m)\times m}\end{array}\right).

Let
\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})
be the integral of
\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
. Then, a possible choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\theta}&=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{n-m}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{O}_{m\times m}&\boldsymbol{O}_{m\times(n-m)}\\
\boldsymbol{O}_{(n-m)\times m}&\boldsymbol{I}_{n-m}\end{array}\right){\boldsymbol{q}}.\end{split}

(11)

Proof.

The choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as given in (
11
)
qualifies as a change of coordinates because its Jacobian

\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})&\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}_{u}({\boldsymbol{q}})\\
\boldsymbol{O}_{(n-m)\times m}&\boldsymbol{I}_{n-m}\end{array}\right),

(12)

is nonsingular at
{\boldsymbol{q}}
.
Power invariance and (
12
) imply

\begin{split}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u}=\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}.\end{split}

Furthermore, being
\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
arbitrary, it follows

\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\boldsymbol{u},

which can be rewritten as

\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{a}({\boldsymbol{q}})&\boldsymbol{O}_{m\times(n-m)}\\
\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}_{u}({\boldsymbol{q}})&\boldsymbol{I}_{n-m}\end{array}\right)}_{\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}}-\boldsymbol{u}\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}}\end{array}\right)=\boldsymbol{0},

(13)

where we expanded
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
into the two vectors
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
performing work on
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}
, respectively.
Equation (
13

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}}-\boldsymbol{u}\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}}\end{array}\right)=\boldsymbol{0},

(14)

being that
\boldsymbol{J}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is nonsingular.

To prove the necessary part of the statement, suppose there exists
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
such that (
10
) holds and partition

\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{a}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\\
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{u}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\end{array}\right),

with
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{a}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{u}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-m)\times n}

or, equivalently,

\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{a}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}}).

Thus, the first
m
components of
\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
satisfy the integrability assumption.
∎

This section shows that the conclusions drawn in Examples

This type of actuation is growing in popularity because it allows creating lightweight structures with high power density, and precise and distributed actuation

) is

\begin{split}\dot{L}_{c_{i}}({\boldsymbol{q}})&=\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{t}^{T}_{i}[\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}]_{3}dX=\int_{0}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{a}(X,{\boldsymbol{q}}))^{T}_{i}\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathrm{d}X\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
&=(\boldsymbol{A}^{{}\!}({\boldsymbol{q}}))^{T}_{i}\dot{\boldsymbol{q}},\end{split}

which implies that the passive output
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
is integrable as

\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}L_{c_{1}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\\
\vdots\\
L_{c_{m}}({\boldsymbol{q}})\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Thus, the actuation coordinates correspond to the length of the actuators, as for rigid systems.
The above results are independent of the number of DOF and actuators.
In other words, finite-dimensional models of mechanical systems actuated via tendons always admit a collocated form, independently of being fully-, over-, or underactuated.
Recalling that the actuator coordinates are defined up to a constant, it is also possible to consider the actuator elongation
\delta L_{c_{i}}:=L_{c_{i}}-L^{*}_{c_{i}};\ i\in\{1,\cdots,m\}
, with respect to a reference length
L^{*}_{c_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
, such as that in the stress-free configuration. This way, proprioceptive sensors like encoders can easily measure the actuation coordinates. Consequently, the proposed change of coordinates is also helpful for control synthesis.

In Appendix
-C
, we show that similar arguments apply to soft robots with volumetric actuators.

However, to the best of Authors knowledge, it has never been clarified why these coordinates represent a better choice than others, such as the curvature and bending direction.
In the actuation coordinates, the dynamics is collocated, which is expected to simplify and robustify the closed loop, especially when the control law does not require significant system knowledge. When a controller is implemented in the actuation coordinates, explicit inversion of the actuation matrix is unnecessary because these coordinates inherently incorporate the inversion. It is also worth noting that any control problem formulated in the initial configuration space can be reformulated in the actuation coordinates.

VIII
Conclusions

This article has considered the input decoupling problem for Lagrangian systems. We have shown that there exists a class of Lagrangian dynamics, called
collocated
, for which a coordinate transformation decouples actuator inputs entering the equations of motion through a configuration-dependent actuation matrix. These coordinates have a physical interpretation and can be easily computed.
Under mild conditions on the differentiability of the actuation matrix, a simple test allows verifying if the dynamics is collocated or not.
As a consequence of power invariance, the results equally apply to fully actuated, overactuated and underactuated systems. In case of underactuated dynamics, inputs are collocated with the actuation coordinates, while some freedom in left in the definition of the unactuated coordinates.
Since we consider only coordinate transformations, the proposed method differs from the standard differential geometric approach used for controlling input-affine nonlinear systems, which typically makes use of complete feedback transformations.
As a byproduct of our approach, we have shown that all mechanical systems driven by thread-like actuators are collocated. Moreover, we were able to extend control laws recently developed for underactuated systems with constant actuation matrix to collocated mechanical systems with damping.

Future work will be devoted to the experimental validation of the proposed method and to special choices of the unactuated variables that further simplify the equations of motion, ease the check of conditions for obtaining input-state or input-output exact linearization via feedback, or even reveal the existence of flat outputs for the system. Additionally, we will consider relaxing the integrability hypothesis at the cost of transforming the input.

-A

Properties of Lagrangian systems

We recall two important properties of Lagrangian systems that play a key role in deriving the results in this paper.

Property 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}):=\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}}\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}-\mathcal{L}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}})
be the system Hamiltonian. For all
{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathcal{M},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\in T_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\mathcal{M}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, it holds

\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}})=\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\boldsymbol{u}).

(27)

Equation (
27
) states that the time rate of change of the Hamiltonian, i.e., the system total energy, equals the input power. It also follows from (
1
) and (
27
) that the dynamics is passive with respect to the pair
(\boldsymbol{u},\dot{\boldsymbol{y}})=\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\right)
with storage function
\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}})
.

Property 2
.

If
\boldsymbol{\theta}:\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{q}})\rightarrow\mathcal{N}=\boldsymbol{h}({\boldsymbol{q}})
is a (local) diffeomorphism, with
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{h}}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{q}}}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)^{T}-\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{T}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{u}),

(28)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{h}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

and

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{J}^{-T}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{h}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{u}).

This also implies that, for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{N},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in T_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{N},{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathcal{M},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\in T_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\mathcal{M}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, it holds

\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{u})=\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\boldsymbol{u})=\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}),

(29)

being
\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{h}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.

According to the above property, the Euler-Lagrange equations and the power are invariant, i.e., they do not depend on the choice of coordinates representing the dynamics.

Integrability of volumetric actuators

Following arguments similar to those of
[
45
]
, it is possible to extend the results of Sec.
VI
to robotic systems with volumetric actuators
[
46
]
.

Let
V_{i}^{*}
be the volume of the
i
-th actuator inside the robot when in the reference configuration. Similarly, denote with
V_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})
the volume in the current deformed configuration. The work performed by the actuator on the robot is

W_{u_{i}}=\left(V_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})-V_{i}^{*}\right)u_{i}.

To determine the effect of
u_{i}
on the generalized coordinates it is possible to apply the principle of virtual works

\delta W_{(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{{\boldsymbol{q}}})_{i}}=\delta{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}(\tau_{{\boldsymbol{q}}})_{i}=\delta{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\left(\frac{\partial V_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right)^{T}u_{i}=\delta W_{u_{i}},

obtaining

\boldsymbol{A}^{\!}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})=\left(\frac{\partial V_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right)^{T}.

The above equations imply that
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T\!}_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}
is integrable and the corresponding actuation coordinate can be chosen as
\delta V_{i}:=V_{i}({\boldsymbol{q}})-V_{i}^{*}
, which is the volume variation in the actuator chamber.
