Orthogonal Intertwiners for Infinite Particle Systems in the Continuum
Stefan Wagner

Abstract
This article focuses on a system of sticky Brownian motions, also known as Howitt-Warren martingale problem, and correlated Brownian motions and shows that infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials intertwine the dynamics of infinitely many particles and their n n -particle evolution. The proof is based on two assumptions about the model: information about the reversible measures for the n n -particle dynamics and consistency. Additionally, explicit formulas for the polynomials are used, including a new explicit formula for infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomials, the orthogonal polynomials with respect to the Pascal process. As an application of the intertwining relations, new reversible measures for the infinite-particle dynamics are obtained. Keywords : Correlated Brownian motions; sticky Brownian motions; intertwining relations; orthogonal polynomials; duality; consistency; point processes. Mathematics Subject Classification : 60J60, 60J25, 82C22.

Introduction

Intertwining relations are a powerful tool for establishing a connection between two Markov processes. A linear operator
\Lambda

intertwines
Markov processes
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with Markov semigroups
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
if

\Lambda P_{t}=Q_{t}\Lambda

holds for all
t\geq 0
.
The concept of
intertwining relations
in the context of Markov processes goes back to Dynkin
[
Dyn65
]
. He employed them for the construction of new Markov semigroups based on existing ones.
Building upon Dynkin’s work, Rogers and Pitman expanded these ideas in
[
RP81
]
, which led to the characterization of Markov functions—–measurable maps that preserve the Markov property. Furthermore, intertwining relations play a crucial role in the analysis of the convergence to equilibrium in the top-to-random shuffle, see
[
AD86
]
. In
[
Mic18
]
, intertwining relations are discussed in the context of the algebraic concept of similarity transformation. Moreover, they are used in
[
BO13
]
for the construction of Markov processes on infinite-dimensional spaces and in
[
DF90
]
in relation to strong stationary times. Additionally,
[
PS12
]
explores their connection with fractional operators, while
[
Dub04
]
,
[
PS11
]
,
[
AOW19
]
delve into their application in the context of diffusions. Intertwining relations for Ehrenfest, Yule and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes are studied in
[
MP22
]
. For additional examples of intertwining relations, we refer to
[
Bia95
]
and
[
JK14
]
.
Lastly, in
[
MP21
]
, the notion of interweaving relations was introduced.

Intertwining relations are closely related (see, e.g.,
[
RS18
, Section 5.2]
,
[
Gro19
, Lemma 2.1]
and
[
CPY98
]
) to the concept of stochastic duality that establishes a connection between two Markov processes using a common observable, known as the duality function (see e.g.
[
JK14
]
).
Stochastic duality has found widespread application in diverse fields, including interacting particle systems (see e.g.
[
Lig05
]
), population genetics models (see e.g.
[
EK86
]
,
[
Eth06
]
,
[
DG11
]
), and (stochastic) partial differential equations (see e.g.
[
Mue15
]
). In
[
KMP82
]
, duality serves as a tool for understanding the non-equilibrium steady state in the KMP model of heat conduction. Self-duality, specifically with orthogonal polynomials, is particularly valuable in studying fluctuation fields, the Boltzmann Gibbs principle, and cumulants in non-equilibrium steady states. This approach is elaborated upon in
[
DP91
]
,
[
ACR18
]
,
[
ACR21b
]
and
[
FRS22
]
.

In the realm of interacting particle systems, dualities and self-dualities are typically explored in the context of lattices.
Self-duality with respect to orthogonal polynomials has been demonstrated in three prominent classical discrete interacting particle systems: the exclusion process, inclusion process, and independent random walks, as explored in
[
GKR07
]
,
[
GKRV09
]
,
[
FG19
]
,
[
Gro19
]
,
[
CFG+19
]
, and
[
FRS22
]
. Self-duality means that the time evolution of an orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
can be expressed by the time evolution of
n
dual particles.
This is crucial because it allows us to characterize properties of a system of infinitely many particles by analyzing only a finite system. One shared property of these systems is consistency, which means that the system’s time evolution commutes with the action of randomly removing a particle from the system. In
[
CGR21
]
, the relationship between self-duality and consistency for reversible systems was investigated.

In
[
FJRW21
]
, a advance was made in the study of consistent particle systems. The authors developed a general method that shows how the orthogonal dualities of these systems rely solely on the conditions of consistency and reversibility. This method does not require an explicit formula of the polynomials and works not only for particle systems on discrete spaces but also on Borel spaces like the real line
\mathbb{R}
. This approach produces then self-intertwiners in terms of infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials.

However, the authors of
[
FJRW21
]
only consider particle systems with a finite number of particles. The arguments presented in
[
FJRW21
]
can apply to systems with infinitely many particles, subject to certain limitations. Specifically, to extend the analysis, it is necessary to have a reversible measure for the dynamics for such systems. Additionally, the concept of consistency alone is insufficient because removing a single particle does not link infinite and finite dynamics. However, it is worth noting that the intertwining relation
[
FJRW21
, Equation (3.5)]
in terms of generalized falling factorial polynomials, together with reversibility, implies the intertwining relation
[
FJRW21
, Equation (3.12)]
in terms of orthogonal polynomials.

In this article, we present a novel approach for infinite particle systems with particles on a non-discrete space that overcomes these obstacles. Our alternative setup has two key features: firstly, only reversible measures for the unlabeled
n
-particle dynamics are required, which are more manageable to obtain than reversible measures for infinite dynamics. Secondly, we define consistency in a manner that is compatible with an infinite number of particles. Given these requirements, we find that infinite-dimensional polynomials of degree
n
intertwine the dynamics of an infinite number of particles with the dynamics of
n
particles where
n<\infty
. Our proof relies on explicit formulas for the infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials.

Intertwiners for infinitely many particles have practical applications.
By using the intertwining relation that we take as the definition for consistency we obtain new invariant measures for the dynamics of infinitely many correlated or sticky Brownian motions.
Exploiting the orthogonality of the intertwining relations in terms of infinite-dimensional polynomials, allows us to enhance this result: we prove that these measures are, in fact, reversible.

We illustrate our procedure by presenting examples of strongly consistent systems, called compatibility by Le Jan and Raimond
[
LR04
, Definition 1.1]
. Such systems possess the property that the time-evolution of the process commutes with the removal of any deterministic particle. This characteristic, related to Kolmogorov’s extension theorem, enables the construction of an infinite dynamics in a straightforward manner.
Furthermore, there is a one-to-one correspondence between strongly consistent families and stochastic flows (see
[
LR04
]
). These flows can be interpreted as independent particles in randomly chosen environments. Brownian flows, such as the Arratia flow
[
Arr79
,
BGS15
]
, the Harris flow
[
Har84
]
, or the Howitt-Warren flow
[
HW09
]
, are prominent examples of strongly consistent models.

We focus on two examples. The first model is a system of correlated Brownian motions. We obtain intertwining relations in terms of infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials with respect to the distribution of the Poisson process. Their connection to the multiple Wiener-Itô integral was extensively studied in e.g.
[
Ogu72
]
or
[
Sur83
]
. The second example refers to a system of sticky Brownian motions, which were introduced by Howitt and Warren via a martingale problem (see
[
HW09
]
). They are observed as a scaling limit of random walks in random environments, a generalized exclusion process, or through a condensation rescaling of symmetric inclusion process (see e.g.
[
RS15
]
,
[
CGR20
]
, and
[
ACR21a
]
). Sticky interactions are used to model colloids in materials science (see
[
HC17
]
). In addition, these interactions have recently received attention in
[
DDP23
]
. We focus on a special case of the Howitt-Warren martingale problem called uniform sticky Brownian motions and studied in
[
BR20
]
and
[
BW23
]
. The multiparticle interactions in this model are entirely determined by two-particle interactions. The dynamics of this model consist of independent Brownian motions if they are apart. If they meet, they exhibit a slowly reflecting interaction behavior, as per Feller’s boundary classification
[
Fel52
]
. Specifically, the time during which they are equal has a positive Lebesgue measure with positive probability, but does not contain any interval. For this model, we investigate intertwining relations in terms of infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomials that are orthogonal with respect to the distribution of the Pascal process. For this latter family, we obtain a new explicit formula.
Both families of infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials belong to the Meixner class (
[
Mei34
]
,
[
Lyt03b
]
).

The article is organized as follows. In the first two sections, we introduce the two models: correlated Brownian motions (Section
2
) and sticky Brownian motions (Section
3
). We introduce infinite-dimensional polynomials and present our main theorems. In Section
4
, we generalize the concept of consistency for infinitely many particles and develop a general approach that works with models beyond the two we present. In Section
5
, we prove that strongly consistent systems, including our examples, satisfy the consistency condition. We then establish the intertwining relations using the explicit formula for infinite-dimensional polynomials, and in particular, prove the explicit formula for Meixner polynomials. Finally, we examine the reversible measures for
n
-particle dynamics of correlated and sticky Brownian motion.

2
Correlated Brownian Motions

This section focuses on intertwining relations in terms of the multiple Wiener-Itô integral with respect to the Poisson process. While previous works have focused on independent particle systems (see e.g.
[

]
,
[

]
and
[

]
) and consistent finite particle systems (see
[

We consider correlated Brownian motions, a simple example within the established setting of
[

]
. Notably, this example stands out for its simplicity, as the interaction of the Brownian motions does not depend on local interactions when the particles meet, unlike other correlated Brownian motion models such as the Harris flow
[

]

]

2.1
The Model

We define a family of real-valued stochastic processes
(X_{k,t})_{t\geq 0}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
to be a family of
correlated Brownian motions with pairwise correlation
0\leq a\leq 1
starting at a (deterministic) sequence
x=(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers if

•

The family
(X_{k,t})_{k\in\mathbb{N},t\geq 0}
is a Gaussian process.

•

For all
t\geq 0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
:
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k,t}\right]=x_{k}
.

•

For all
t,s\geq 0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
:
\mathrm{Cov}\left[X_{k,t},X_{k,s}\right]=\min{\left\{t,s\right\}}
.

•

For all
t,s\geq 0
and
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\neq l
:
\mathrm{Cov}\left[X_{k,t},X_{l,s}\right]=a\min{\left\{t,s\right\}}
.

If

, the
(X_{1,t})_{t\geq 0},(X_{2,t})_{t\geq 0},\ldots
are independent Brownian motions. On the other hand, if
a=1
, they are modifications of each other up to an additive constant: for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\geq 0
, we have
X_{k,t}=X_{k,1}-x_{1}+x_{k}
almost surely.

An explicit construction can be done as follows:
Let
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(B_{1,t})_{t\geq 0},(B_{2,t})_{t\geq 0},\ldots
be independent Brownian motions all starting at zero. From there, define

\displaystyle X_{k,t}:=\sqrt{1-a}B_{k,t}+\sqrt{a}B_{t}+x_{k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},t\geq 0.

(2.1)

A family of correlated Brownian motions satisfies the Markov property. We define the
n
-particle semigroup

\displaystyle P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n}(x):=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\sqrt{1-a}B_{1,t}+\sqrt{a}B_{t}+x_{1},\ldots,\sqrt{1-a}B_{n,t}+\sqrt{a}B_{t}+x_{n})\right]

(2.2)

for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is bounded and measurable.

Remark 2.1
.

The family of
n
-particle semigroups of correlated Brownian motions is strongly consistent (called
compatibility
by
[
LR04
]
). More precisely, a family of Markov semigroups
(P_{t}^{[n]})_{t\geq 0}
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is called

if the equation

\displaystyle P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=P_{t}^{[l]}g_{l}(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{l}}),\qquad x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}

(2.3)

holds for all
l\leq n
,
i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}\in{\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}}

bounded and measurable, where
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto g_{l}(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{l}})
.

We remark that for a general strongly consistent family
(P_{t}^{[n]})_{t\geq 0}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the existence of a Markov family
{\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(X_{t})_{t\geq 0},(\mathbb{P}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}}\right)}

, Section 1.5.3]
). Thereby, we denote by

the set of sequences of real numbers, and equip it with its cylindrical
\sigma
-algebra.
\mathbb{P}_{x}
is a probability measure on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
and
X_{k,t}
is a real-valued random variable for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\geq 0
.
X_{0}=x
holds
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-almost surely for all

is measurable for all measurable

and

, and

satisfies the Markov property with respect to its natural filtration. This family is such that each finite subconfiguration of

The correspondence between strongly consistent families and stochastic flows, as outlined in
[

]

be a random variable that follows the standard normal distribution and let

be the random probability kernel such that

is equal to the normal distribution with expected value

and

. By using (

\displaystyle P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int\cdots\int f_{n}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})K_{t}(x_{1},\mathrm{d}y_{1})\cdots K_{t}(x_{1},\mathrm{d}y_{1})\right]

For each

.

•

For each

,

2.3
Multiple Wiener-Itô Integrals for the Poisson Process

For the sake of completeness, let us recapitulate the multiple Wiener-Itô integral corresponding to the Poisson process, see e.g.
[
Ogu72
]
or
[
Sur83
]
. The
k
-th factorial measure
(see e.g.
[
LP17
, Eq. (4.5)]
) of
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}}\in\mathbf{N}
,

,

is given by

\displaystyle\mu^{(k)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=1\\
\text{pairwise different}\end{subarray}}^{m}\delta_{(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}})},

(2.7)

where an empty sum is considered to be equal to the zero measure. The
symmetrization
of a function

is defined by taking the average of

over all permutations of the coordinates (see e.g.
[

denotes the set of permutations of the set

\displaystyle\widetilde{f_{n}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}f_{n}(x_{s(1)},\ldots,x_{s(n)}),\qquad x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}.

A function

Let

\mathbb{R}
. The

with respect to the Poisson process with intensity measure

(see

]
) is defined by

\displaystyle I_{n}f_{n}(\mu):=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{n-k}

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\iint\widetilde{f_{n}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\lambda^{\otimes(n-k)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}))\mu^{(k)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}))

for
\mu\in\mathbf{N}_{\mathrm{lf}}
and

.

\mathbf{N}_{\mathrm{lf}}
denotes the set of
locally finite

\mu\in\mathbf{N}
, i.e.,
\mu(B)<\infty

while
\mathcal{C}_{n}
denotes the space of bounded, measurable functions

with bounded support.
Furthermore, when integrating with respect to

or

The orthogonality relation

\displaystyle\int(I_{n}f_{n})(I_{m}g_{m})\>\mathrm{d}\pi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{1}_{{\left\{n=m\right\}}}n!\int f_{n}g_{m}\>\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\otimes n}

,

(see e.g.

]
,
[

]

\pi_{\lambda}
denotes the distribution of the Poisson point process with intensity measure

. Hence,

can be uniquely extended to a bounded linear operator mapping square-integrable, symmetric functions, denoted by

, to

. We put

.

Additionally, for each
F\in L^{2}(\pi_{\lambda})
, there exists a unique sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
that belongs to the

Furthermore, it is worth noting that

The study of non-Gaussian white noise naturally involves infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials, as discussed in

The use of chaos decompositions is significant in the analysis of Lévy white noise and stochastic differential equations driven by Lévy white noise, as explored in

in the definition below as coefficients. The set of polynomials of degree less or equal to

(2.11)

2.4
Main Result

According to
[

, Corollary 3.7]
, there exists a proper Poisson process. Thus, let
\zeta
be a proper Poisson process with intensity measure given by the Lebesgue measure
\lambda

on the measurable space

from Lemma

and expected value

. Note that

.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose

. Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the multiple Wiener-Itô integral of degree
n
intertwines the dynamics of infinitely many correlated Brownian motions with pairwise correlation
a
and their
n
-particle evolution. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\left[I_{n}f_{n}(\eta_{t})\right]=I_{n}P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n}(\zeta)

holds almost surely for all

and for all functions

.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
relies on two key components. The notion of consistency, which we introduce along with the abstract framework after Theorem
4.4
in Section
4
below, and the following proposition, which follows directly from the definition of correlated Brownian motions.

Proposition 2.5
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the measure
\lambda^{\otimes n}
is reversible for
n
correlated Brownian motions with pairwise correlation
a
, i.e.,
\int(P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n})g_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\otimes n}=\int(P_{t}^{[n]}g_{n})f_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\otimes n}
for

,
f_{n},g_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
.

Theorem
2.4
is applicable in finding reversible measures for infinitely many particles. We say that the measure
\pi_{\lambda}
is

for
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\left[F(\eta_{t})\right]G(\zeta)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F(\zeta)\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\left[G(\eta_{t})\right]\right]

(2.13)

holds for all measurable, bounded functions

and

is measurable. Thus, the outer expected values in (

By applying standard arguments, we obtain the following corollary. Specifically, the family of
n
-particle Markov semigroups undergoes a unitary transformation, resulting in the Markov semigroup of unlabeled infinite dynamics. This transformation is the only significant step in the proof.

Corollary 2.6
.

The measure
\pi_{\lambda}
, which denotes the distribution of the Poisson process with intensity measure
\lambda
, is reversible for
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, which is an infinite system of unlabeled correlated Brownian motions with pairwise correlation

.

Proof.

Consider the unitary operator

\displaystyle\mathfrak{U}:\mathfrak{F}:=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\lambda^{\otimes n})\to L^{2}(\pi_{\lambda}),\qquad(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}I_{n}f_{n},

and for each

, define the operator
P_{t}:\mathfrak{F}\to\mathfrak{F}

(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mapsto(P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
where

,

By Proposition

is a well-defined, bounded, self-adjoint operator for every

is also self-adjoint. Therefore, to complete the proof, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{U}P_{t}\mathfrak{U}^{-1}F(\zeta)=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\left[F(\eta_{t})\right]

holds almost surely for all

implies that (

, where

In the proof of Theorem
4.4
below we show that the operator

obtained from the right-hand side of (

∎

3
Sticky Brownian Motions

The focus of this section is on sticky Brownian motions. Similar to correlated Brownian motions, this model leads us to infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials, specifically, infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomials. These polynomials serve as intertwiners, and they are orthogonal with respect to the distribution of the Pascal process. As an application of the intertwining relations, we obtain a new result for a system of infinitely many sticky Brownian motions: the distribution of the Pascal process is reversible.

Intertwining relations involving infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomials were already studied in
[
FJRW21
, Section 5]
. The authors examined a version of the symmetric inclusion processes in the continuum.

3.1
The Model

Feller considered in his boundary classification (see
[
Fel52
]
) a single reflected Brownian motion that is sticky at the origin at a rate
\theta>0
. However, it should be noted that this condition is more accurately described as a “slowly” reflecting boundary, rather than “true” stickiness. Specifically, the amount of time that the Brownian motion spends at zero has a strictly positive Lebesgue measure with positive probability, but contains no interval. In contrast, “true” stickiness is characterized by a process getting “stuck” at zero over a random interval of time, as shown e.g.
[
KKR07
]
for an interest rate process.

Based on that, a pair
(X_{1,t},X_{2,t})_{t\geq 0}
of Brownian motions with sticky interaction can be described, namely that
(X_{1,t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(X_{2,t})_{t\geq 0}
are both Brownian motions and
({\left|X_{1,t}-X_{2,t}\right|})_{t\geq 0}
is a single reflected Brownian motion that is sticky at zero, in the sense of Feller. They behave independently when they are apart and interact when they meet. The pair
(X_{1,t},X_{2,t})_{t\geq 0}
can be characterized by a martingale problem, as shown in
[
HW09
]
.

Howitt and Warren (see
[
HW09
]
) generalized this concept to a family of
n
diffusions on
\mathbb{R}
, which is commonly known as the
Howitt-Warren martingale problem
. This yields a family of
n
independent Brownian motions that move separately when they are far apart and coalesce when individual processes meet. A new non-negative quantity
\theta(i:j)
is introduced, which can be interpreted as the rate at which a group of
i+j
particles splits into a group of
i
particles and a group of
j
particles. Furthermore, the concept of strong consistency (see Remark
2.1
) corresponds to the condition
\theta(i+1:j)+\theta(i:j+1)=\theta(i:j)
, which has been characterized in
[
SSS14
, Lemma A.4]
by the existence of a finite measure
\nu
on the interval
[0,1]
, called
the characteristic measure
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\theta(i:j)=\int x^{i-1}(1-x)^{j-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}x),\qquad i,j\in\mathbb{N}.

For a full construction using the martingale problem, we refer to
[
HW09
]
. An alternative formulation is available in
[
SSS14
]
.

In this article, we examine uniform sticky Brownian motions with stickiness
\theta>0
and zero drift, which were studied by
[
BR20
]
in the context of large deviation analysis. This model is defined by choosing the characteristic measure
\nu=\frac{\theta}{2}\lambda_{[0,1]}
, where
\lambda_{[0,1]}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, leading to

\displaystyle\theta(i:j)=\frac{\theta}{2}\frac{(i-1)!(j-1)!}{(i+j-2)!}.

For this model, the multiparticle interactions are completely determined by the two-particle interactions. The authors of
[
BW23
]
derive the Kolmogorov backwards equation and demonstrate that, for this particular interaction, it can be solved exactly using the Bethe ansatz. This result is used to obtain the reversible measure for the
n
-particle dynamics. We provide the definition of the uniform sticky Brownian motion from
[
SSS14
, Definition 2.2]
for the sake of completeness.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=(X_{1,t},\ldots,X_{n,t})_{t\geq 0}
are
n
-particle uniform sticky Brownian motions with stickiness
\theta>0
if the following conditions are satisfied.

(i)

(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous, square-integrable semimartingale.

(ii)

The covariation of
X_{k}
and
X_{l}
is given by

\displaystyle[X_{k},X_{l}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{{\left\{X_{k,s}=X_{l,s}\right\}}}\>\mathrm{d}s,\qquad t\geq 0

for
k,l\in{\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}}
.

(iii)

For each
\Delta\subset{\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}}
,
f_{\Delta}(X_{t})-\theta\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{+}(g_{\Delta}(X_{s}))\>\mathrm{d}s
,
t\geq 0
is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
where

\displaystyle f_{\Delta}(x):=\max_{k\in\Delta}x_{k},\qquad g_{\Delta}(x):={\left|{\left\{k\in\Delta:x_{k}=f_{\Delta}(x)\right\}}\right|},\qquad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\beta^{+}(1):=0
and
\beta^{+}(m):=1+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\ldots\frac{1}{m-1}
,
m\geq 2
.

The existence of the
n
-particle uniform sticky Brownian motions starting at an arbitrary initial value
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
was proven in
[
HW09
, Theorem 2.1.]
together with the fact, that their distribution is unique. A family of sticky Brownian motions satisfies the Markov property. Additionally, it is shown that the corresponding
n
-particle semigroups form a strongly consistent family, as described in Remark
2.1
.

3.2
Pascal Process and Infinite-Dimensional Meixner Polynomials

Let
\alpha:=\theta\lambda
, where
\lambda
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
and
\theta>0
is fixed. The Pascal process, also known as the negative binomial process, is a well-studied point process (see
[
Ser90
]
,
[
Ber02
]
or
[
KP09
]
for further details). We briefly recall the construction of a Pascal process. A
Pascal process
with parameters
0<p<1
and
\alpha
is a random measure
\zeta\in\mathbf{N}
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

The random variables
\zeta(A_{1}),\ldots,\zeta(A_{N})
are independent if the sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}
are measurable and pairwise disjoint.

(ii)

For each measurable
A\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
0<\alpha(A)<\infty
, the random variable
\zeta(A)
follows the negative binomial distribution with parameters
p
and
\alpha(A)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\zeta(A)=k]=\alpha(A)(\alpha(A)+1)\cdots(\alpha(A)+k-1)\frac{p^{k}}{k!}(1-p)^{\alpha(A)},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

If
\alpha(A)=0
, then
\zeta(A)=0
almost surely. If
\alpha(A)=\infty
, then
\zeta(A)=\infty
almost surely.

The Pascal process is a compound Poisson process. Specifically, if we consider a Poisson process
\xi
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{N}
with intensity measure
\alpha\otimes\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p^{k}}{k}\delta_{k}
, then
\zeta(A):=\int_{A\times\mathbb{N}}y\xi(\mathrm{d}(x,y))
defines a Pascal process. In particular, the Pascal process can be constructed as a proper point process, meaning it has the form (
2.5
). For more details on Pascal processes, see also
[
Kin67
]
and
[
FJRW21
]
.

As a reminder, the symmetrization
\widetilde{f_{n}}
of a function
f_{n}
is defined as in (
2.8
). We put for each
\mu\in\mathbf{N}_{\mathrm{lf}}
and
f_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}f_{n}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}{\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)}^{k-n}\iint\widetilde{f_{n}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\kappa_{n,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\mathrm{d}(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}))\mu^{(k)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})),

(3.1)

the
infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomial of degree
n
. The kernel
\kappa_{n,n-1}:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty)\cup{\left\{\infty\right\}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\kappa_{n,n-1}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}),\,\cdot\,):=\alpha+\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n-1}},

while
\kappa_{n,k}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n-k})\to[0,\infty)\cup{\left\{\infty\right\}}
is defined by
\kappa_{n,k}:=\kappa_{k+1,k}\otimes\kappa_{k+2,k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\kappa_{n,n-1}
. In other words,

\displaystyle\kappa_{n,k}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\mathrm{d}(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}))=(\alpha+\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n-1}})(\mathrm{d}x_{n})

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt\cdots(\alpha+\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{k+1}})(\mathrm{d}x_{k+2})(\alpha+\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{k}})(\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}).

(3.2)

We interpret the case
n=k
in (
3.2
) as though the inner integral was not present. If
k=0
,
\kappa_{n,0}
reduces to a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we interpret the outer integral with respect to
\mu^{(0)}
as though it was not present. If
n=0
, we put
\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}f_{0}:=f_{0}
for
f_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denote the set of polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n
, as defined by (
2.11
).

Proposition
3.2
below demonstrates that the polynomials
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{p,\alpha}f_{n}
defined in (
3.2
) coincide with the infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials with respect to the distribution of the Pascal process. These polynomials are investigated, e.g., in
[
Lyt03a
, Section 5 and 6]
and
[
FJRW21
, Section 5.2]
. The proof of this proposition is provided in Section
5.3
below.
Equation (
3.2
) is a novel and explicit formula for the infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials following a format similar to that of the Wiener-Itô integrals (
2.3
). Notably, our formula differs from the one presented in
[
Lyt03a
, Equation (6.4)]
, which only provides a recursion.
Our explicit formula is crucial in the proof of Theorem
3.5
below, where we exploit its structure as a sum of integrals involving factorial measures and the kernels
\kappa_{n,k}
. Furthermore, we use Proposition
3.2
in that proof and in the proof of Corollary
3.7
below, more precisely, we exploit the orthogonality relation (
3.7
) below.

Proposition 3.2
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}f_{n}=\text{orthogonal projection of }{\left(\mu\mapsto\mu^{\otimes n}(f_{n})\right)}\text{ onto }\mathcal{P}_{n-1}^{\perp}

(3.3)

holds
\rho_{p,\alpha}
-almost surely.

Remark 3.3
.

The infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomials, given by (
3.2
), can be regarded as a natural extension of the monic univariate Meixner polynomials with parameters
0<p<1
and
\alpha>0
. The
monic Meixner polynomials
are expressed as follows (e.g.
[
KLS10
]
):

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}^{p,a}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}{\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)}^{k-n}(\alpha+k)^{(n-k)}(x)_{k},\qquad n,x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.4)

In the infinite-dimensional version of these polynomials, the
rising factorial
(also known as the
Pochhammer symbol
),
(a+k)^{(n-k)}=(a+n-1)\cdots(a+k+1)(a+k)
, where
(a+k)^{(0)}=1
, turns into an integration with respect to the kernel
\kappa_{n,k}
, while the
falling factorial
,
(x)_{k}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)
, where
(x)_{0}:=1
, occurs as an integration with respect to the factorial measure
\mu^{(k)}
. These relationships imply that if
f_{n}
is wisely chosen, (
3.2
) reduces to a product of univariate polynomials, as shown in Lemma
5.3
below.

We denote by
\Sigma_{n}
the set of partitions
\sigma
of the set
{\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}}
. For a function
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denote by
(f_{n})_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{{\left|\sigma\right|}}\to\mathbb{R}
the function gained by identifying the variables belonging to the same

in the order of occurrence. Put

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}{\left(\prod_{A\in\sigma}({\left|A\right|}-1)!\right)}\alpha_{\sigma}

(3.5)

where
\alpha_{\sigma}
is the measure defined by
\int f_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\alpha_{\sigma}=\int(f_{n})_{\sigma}\>\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\otimes{\left|\sigma\right|}}
. Note that

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\kappa_{n,0}=\lambda_{k}\otimes\kappa_{n,k}

(3.6)

for all
n\geq k\geq 0
.
The measure
\lambda_{n}
is closely related to the Pascal process in two ways. Firstly, the definition of the Pascal process implies the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4
.

The
n
-th factorial moment measure of the Pascal process with parameters

is given by
(\frac{p}{1-p})^{n}\lambda_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta^{(n)}(A)\right]=(\frac{p}{1-p})^{n}\lambda_{n}(A)
for

measurable.

Secondly, the orthogonality relation

\displaystyle\int(\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}f_{n})(\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{m}g_{m})\>\mathrm{d}\rho_{p,\alpha}=\mathbbm{1}_{{\left\{n=m\right\}}}\frac{p^{n}n!}{(1-p)^{2n}}\int f_{n}g_{m}\>\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n}

holds for symmetric
f_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
,

(see
[
Lyt03a
]
,
[
FJRW21
]
). Hence, the linear operator
\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}
can be extended continuously to symmetric, measurable, square-integrable functions, which are denoted by

. This extension allows us to decompose any function
F\in L^{2}(\rho_{p,\alpha})
into a series that converges in
L^{2}(\rho_{p,\alpha})
, given by
F=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-p)^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}f_{n}
. The sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is contained in a space called the
extended anyon Fock space
, as defined in
[
Lyt03a
]
,
[
Lyt03b
]
,
[
BLR15
]
. This space is defined as the set of sequences
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
that satisfy the condition
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{p^{n}}{n!}\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\lambda_{n})}^{2}<\infty
, where
L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\lambda_{0})
understood as
\mathbb{R}
.

3.3
Main Result

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
, consider a family of stochastic processes
(X_{k,t})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
t\geq 0
such that that for any pairwise different
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, the distribution of
(X_{i_{1},t},\ldots,X_{i_{n},t})
,
t\geq 0
is the same as that of the
n
-particle uniform sticky Brownian motions with stickiness
\theta>0
starting at
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{n}})
. That sequence exists using strong consistency and Kolmogorov’s theorem. Let
(P_{t}^{[n]})_{t\geq 0}
denote the Markov semigroup of the
n
-particle uniform sticky Brownian motions with stickiness
\theta
. Consider a Markov family
(\Omega
,
\mathcal{F}
,
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(\mathbb{P}_{\mu})_{\mu\in\mathbf{N}})
, which is an unlabeled version of
(X_{k,t})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
t\geq 0
. This construction is analogous to the one described in Lemma
2.2
. Let
\zeta
denote a Pascal process with parameters
p
and
\alpha
that is proper, meaning it has the form (
2.5
). Recall that
\lambda
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose
\theta>0
and put
\alpha=\theta\lambda
. Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<p<1
, the infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomial of degree
n
intertwines the dynamics of infinitely many uniform sticky Brownian motions with stickiness
\theta
and their
n
-particle evolution. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\left[\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}f_{n}(\eta_{t})\right]=\mathcal{M}^{p,\alpha}_{n}P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n}(\zeta)

(3.8)

holds almost surely for all
t\geq 0
and for all functions

.

Similar to Theorem
2.4
, the proof is a direct consequence of consistency and reversibility for the
n
-particle evolutions.

Proposition 3.6
.

For unlabeled uniform sticky Brownian motions
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with stickiness
\theta
, the push-forward measure of
\lambda_{n}
under the mapping
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\to\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n}}
is reversible.

To prove Proposition
3.6

[

, Theorem 4.17]
, which provides a reversible measure for

4
Strategy for the Proof

We prove the intertwining relations for correlated Brownian motions and sticky Brownian motions (Theorem
2.4
and Theorem
3.5
) by using the same techniques. First, in Section
4.1
, we adapt the notation of consistency from e.g.,
[
CGR21
]
and
[
FJRW21
]
to infinite particle systems. We then establish that the two models, correlated Brownian motions and sticky Brownian motions, are consistent, based only on the fact that the family of
n
-particle dynamics is a strongly consistent family according to Remark
2.1
.

Next, in Section
4.2
, we formulate a general theorem for both the Pascal and the Poisson case: if the push-forward of the factorial moment measures of the Poisson (or Pascal) process under the map
\iota_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{N}
,
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n}}
is reversible and the Markov processes are consistent, then we obtain the intertwining relations. The proof uses the explicit representation of Wiener-Itô integrals (
2.3
) and of infinite-dimensional Meixner polynomials (
3.2
).

Thus, we only need to verify the reversible measures for the finite systems. For correlated Brownian motions, this follows directly from the definition, while for sticky Brownian motions, we use
[
BW23
]
.

4.1
Consistency

In
[
CGR21
]
and
[
FJRW21
]
,
consistency
refers to the property that the removal of a particle uniformly at random commutes with the time-evolution of a process. This property can be formulated for particle systems with unlabeled particles. Specifically, if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\int F(\eta_{t}-\delta_{x})\eta_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\right]=\int\mathbb{E}_{\mu-\delta_{x}}\left[F(\eta_{t})\right]\mu(\mathrm{d}x),\qquad\mu\in\mathbf{N}_{<\infty}

(4.1)

holds for all measurable
F:\mathbf{N}_{<\infty}\to[0,\infty)
, then the system is called consistent. In (
4.1
), the left-hand side first evolves the system and then removes a particle uniformly at random. Conversely, the right-hand side of the equation first removes a particle uniformly at random from the initial configuration and then evolves the process from the resulting state.

In this article, we deliberately define consistency differently since (
4.1
) is not useful for extending to infinitely many particles. Equation (
4.1
) lacks a connection between the dynamics of infinitely many particles and finitely many particles. More precisely, if one particle is removed from an infinite configuration, the total number of particles in the configuration remains infinite, whereas removing a particle from a finite configuration results in a finite number of remaining particles. However, our objective is to obtain intertwining relations that reduce the infinite number of particles to a finite number. According to
[
FJRW21
, Theorem 3.5, Remark 3.6.]
, our new definition below is a natural extension of the conventional definition of consistency, which coincides with the established notation of consistency of finite particle systems.

Let
(\Omega
,
\mathcal{F}
,
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(\mathbb{P}_{\mu})_{\mu\in\mathbf{N}})
be a Markov family such that
\eta_{t}
is proper for each
t\geq 0
.

Definition 4.1
.

A stochastic process
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called
consistent
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\int F(\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n}})\eta_{t}^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt=\int\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n}}}\left[F(\eta_{t})\right]\mu^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})),\qquad\mu\in\mathbf{N}

(4.2)

holds for all
t\geq 0
, measurable
F:\mathbf{N}_{<\infty}\to[0,\infty)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We remark that, in general, integrability is not guaranteed on either the left-hand or the right-hand side in (
4.1
). In the case of non-integrability, we interpret the equation as
\infty=\infty
.

Interpreting (
4.1
), the left-hand side describes
n
particles chosen uniformly from the evolved state of the process starting at
\mu
. The right-hand side, on the other hand, describes
n
particles chosen uniformly from the initial state
\mu
and then evolved under the process.

Remark 4.2
.

We only define consistency for processes in which each
\eta_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, is proper. This assumption is motivated by a technical subtlety: in general, the mapping
\mu\mapsto\int F(\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n}})\mu^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))
for
n\geq 2
, even for measurable
F
, is not measurable.
By requiring each
\eta_{t}
to be proper, we ensure the measurability of
\omega\mapsto\int F(\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n}})\eta_{t}(\omega)^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))
.

Our definition can also be interpreted as self-intertwining property (see
[
FJRW21
, Equation (3.6)]
) using the
K
-transform
introduced by Lenard
[
Len73
]
,
[
Len75
]
.
See also
[
KKO+09
]
for
K
-transform intertwining relations in the context of
free Kawasaki dynamics
.

We say that a process
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is
conservative
if it conserves the number of particles, meaning that
\eta_{t}(\mathbb{R})=\mu(\mathbb{R})
holds
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
-almost surely for all
\mu\in\mathbf{N}
and
t\geq 0
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Both the unlabeled correlated Brownian motions with pairwise correlation
a
and the unlabeled sticky Brownian motions with stickiness
\theta
are consistent and conservative.

The proofs of both Lemma
2.2
and Proposition
4.3
rely solely on the strong consistency (picked up in Remark
2.1
) of the family of underlying
n
-particle evolutions. This demonstrates that from any strongly consistent family, a consistent, unlabeled process can be constructed. Therefore, strong consistency is aptly named, as it is indeed a stronger property compared to consistency. For models of finitely many configurations, see also
[
FJRW21
, Section 3.1]
.

A direct consequence of the intertwining relation (
4.1
), which we take as the definition of consistency, is that
\pi_{\lambda}
, the distribution of the Poisson process with intensity measure
\lambda
, is an invariant measure for an infinite system of unlabeled correlated Brownian motions with pairwise correlation
a
.
Indeed, we can use the fact that the moment problem for the Poisson process is uniquely solvable (as shown e.g. in
[
LP17
, Proposition 4.12]
). Therefore, it is enough to check that the factorial moment measures of a proper Poisson process
\zeta
, given by
\lambda^{\otimes n}
, and
\eta_{t}
starting at
\zeta
coincide. Since the push-forward measure of
\lambda^{\otimes n}
under
\iota_{n}:(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto\delta_{x_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{x_{n}}
is invariant for
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, see Proposition
2.5
, we use consistency to obtain

\displaystyle\int f_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\otimes n}=\int P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\otimes n}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int P_{t}^{[n]}f_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\zeta^{(n)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\left[\int f_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\eta_{t}^{(n)}\right]\right]

for measurable
f_{n}:E^{n}\to[0,\infty)
and
t\geq 0
which implies that
\pi_{\lambda}
is indeed invariant for
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

We emphasize that the argument for proving invariance of
\pi_{\lambda}
is a general principle:
Let each
n
-th factorial moment measure of an infinite point process
\zeta
be invariant for the
n
-particle dynamics of a consistent particle system. Assume that the factorial moment measures uniquely characterize the distribution of
\zeta
. Then, the distribution of
\zeta
is invariant for the infinite dynamics.

Thus, using Proposition
3.6
, we obtain that for each
0<p<1
, the distribution of the Pascal process with parameters
p
and
\alpha=\theta\lambda
is an invariant measure for an infinite system of unlabeled uniform sticky Brownian motions with stickiness
\theta
.
Here, the unique solvability of the moment problem follows by the criterion presented in
[
LP17
, Proposition 4.12]
: Fix a bounded, measurable set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
. Since

\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\alpha(B)^{(n)}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
, there exists a
D\geq 1
such that
\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\alpha(B)^{(n)}\leq D
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, we estimate the factorial moment measure as follows:

\displaystyle{\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)}^{n}\lambda_{n}(B^{n})

\displaystyle={\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)}^{n}\alpha(B)^{(n)}\leq{\left(2D\frac{p}{1-p}\right)}^{n}n!.

While the invariance follows easily by consistency, reversibility proves to be more challenging. In the following section, we conquer this issue by specifically exploiting the orthogonality of the intertwining relation in terms of infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials.

5.3

In this section, we prove Proposition

Fix a partition of measurable sets

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

holds true.

Thereby, we put
\infty^{(k)}:=\infty

Proof.

We prove the equation by induction over

\displaystyle\int\mathbbm{1}_{B_{i_{n+1}}\times\ldots B_{i_{m+1}}}(y_{n+1},\ldots,y_{m+1})\kappa_{m+1,n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\mathrm{d}(y_{n+1},\ldots,y_{m+1}))

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt=\int\mathbbm{1}_{B_{i_{n+1}}\times\ldots\times B_{i_{m}}}(y_{n+1},\ldots,y_{m})\kappa_{m+1,m}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},y_{n+1},\ldots,y_{m},B_{i_{m+1}})

\displaystyle\hskip 210.00032pt\kappa_{m,n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\mathrm{d}(y_{n+1},\ldots,y_{m}))

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt=(\alpha(B_{i_{m+1}})+c_{i_{m+1}}+e_{i_{m+1}})\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\left(\alpha(B_{k})+c_{k}\right)}^{(e_{k})}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\left(\alpha(B_{k})+c_{k}\right)}^{(e_{k}+\mathbbm{1}_{{\left\{k=i_{m+1}\right\}}})}.\qed

Lemma 5.2

We decompose the the integral into

\displaystyle\hskip 160.00024pt\kappa_{m,n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\mathrm{d}(y_{n+1},\ldots,y_{m})).

(5.9)

If

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{n}(-Y+x)g_{n}(x)\lambda^{\otimes n}(\mathrm{d}x)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int f_{n}(x)g_{n}(x+Y)\lambda^{\otimes n}(\mathrm{d}x)\right]

\displaystyle=\int f_{n}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[g_{n}(x+Y)\right]\lambda^{\otimes n}(\mathrm{d}x)

for all
f_{n}
,
g_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
∎

As a next step, we prove Proposition

. A reversible measure for a system of

Let

For each

To prove that the push-forward measure of the symmetrization

\displaystyle\int f_{n}(x)\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[g_{n}(Z_{t})\right]\widetilde{m}_{\theta}^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}x)

By using the fact that the Lebesgue measure is diffuse, meaning that

We observe that every permutation

\displaystyle\int f_{n}\>\mathrm{d}\widetilde{m}_{\theta}^{(n)}

,
