Tempered Calculus for ML: Application to Hyperbolic Model Embedding This paper is subsumed by paper ”Hyperbolic Embeddings of Supervised Models”, by Richard Nock, Ehsan Amid, Frank Nielsen, Alexander Soen and Manfred K. Warmuth, appearing at NeurIPS’24.
Richard Nock, Ehsan Amid, Frank Nielsen, Alexander Soen, Manfred K. Warmuth

Abstract
Most mathematical distortions used in ML are fundamentally integral in nature: f f -divergences, Bregman divergences, (regularized) optimal transport distances, integral probability metrics, geodesic distances, etc. In this paper, we unveil a grounded theory and tools which can help improve these distortions to better cope with ML requirements. We start with a generalization of Riemann integration that also encapsulates functions that are not strictly additive but are, more generally, t t -additive, as in nonextensive statistical mechanics. Notably, this recovers Volterra’s product integral as a special case. We then generalize the Fundamental Theorem of calculus using an extension of the (Euclidean) derivative. This, along with a series of more specific Theorems, serves as a basis for results showing how one can specifically design, alter, or change fundamental properties of distortion measures in a simple way, with a special emphasis on geometric- and ML-related properties that are the metricity, hyperbolicity, and encoding. We show how to apply it to a problem that has recently gained traction in ML: hyperbolic embeddings with a “cheap” and accurate encoding along the hyperbolic vs Euclidean scale. We unveil a new application for which the Poincaré disk model has very appealing features, and our theory comes in handy: model embeddings for boosted combinations of decision trees, trained using the log-loss (trees) and logistic loss (combinations).

Proper losses and the log loss

We briefly present the theory of
losses for class probability estimation
(CPE, which has been instrumental in DT induction). We follow the notations of
[
33
]
: A CPE
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is

\displaystyle\ell(y,p)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\llbracket y=1\rrbracket\cdot\ell_{1}(p)+\llbracket y=-1\rrbracket\cdot\ell_{-1}(p),

(20)

where
\llbracket.\rrbracket
is Iverson’s bracket
[
31
]
. Functions
\ell_{1},\ell_{-1}
are called
partial
losses. A CPE loss is

symmetric
when
\ell_{1}(p)=\ell_{-1}(1-p),\forall p\in[0,1]
. The
log-loss
is a symmetric CPE loss with
\ell^{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}_{1}(p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}-\log(p)
. The fundamental property of a CPE loss is
properness
, which enforces the optimality of Bayes rule for classification: letting
\underline{L}(\pi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\inf_{p}\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\pi}\ell(\mathsf{Y},p)
denote the minimal pointwise achievable risk (called Bayes risk) where we sample labels according to the
prior

\Pr[\mathsf{Y}=1]=\pi
, properness imposes
\underline{L}(\pi)=\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\pi}\ell(\mathsf{Y},\pi),\forall\pi\in[0,1]
and
strict
properness imposes in addition
\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\pi}\ell(\mathsf{Y},p)>\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{Y}\sim\pi}\ell(\mathsf{Y},\pi),\forall p\neq\pi
. The log-loss is strictly proper; its Bayes risk is very familiar in DT induction: it is the splitting criterion used in C4.5
[
43
]
:

\displaystyle{\underline{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-p\cdot\log p-(1-p)\cdot\log(1-p).

(21)

DT induction usually proceeds with a top-down induction of a big tree following the minimization of the Bayes risk, thus relying on a model that predicts a
posterior
at the leaves,
\hat{\Pr}[\mathsf{Y}=1|\mathsf{X}]
. There exists a connection between CPE classification and the real-valued classification setting familiar to
e.g.
deep learning: optimizing Bayes risk for the posterior is equivalent to minimizing its
convex surrogate
using the
canonical link
of the loss to compute real classification
[
41
, Theorem 1]
. The canonical link of the log-loss,
\psi_{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the inverse sigmoid, which has a very convenient form for our purpose,

\displaystyle\psi_{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}(p)=\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).

(22)

Notably, the absolute value
|\psi_{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}(p)|
is a
confidence
and the sign the class predicted. In the case of the log-loss, the convex surrogate is hugely popular in ML: it is the logistic loss.

the top-down induction of a DT in many popular packages for DT induction boils down to repeatedly minimizing the expected Bayes risk at the leaves of the current DT

\displaystyle\underline{L}(H,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i\sim[m]}[\underline{L}(H_{[0,1]}(\bm{x}_{i}))]\quad\left(=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda\sim\Lambda(H)}[\underline{L}(p^{+}_{\lambda})]\right),\quad H_{[0,1]}(\bm{x}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}p^{+}_{\lambda_{i}}

(23)

\displaystyle{\underline{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}}(H,\mathcal{S})=\mathbb{E}_{i}[\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\cdot H_{\mathbb{R}}(\bm{x}_{i})))],\quad H_{\mathbb{R}}(\bm{x}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\psi_{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}(p^{+}_{\lambda_{i}})=\log\left(\frac{p^{+}_{\lambda_{i}}}{1-p^{+}_{\lambda_{i}}}\right).

(24)

Let us put next to each other the absolute confidence of a leaf prediction and the hyperbolic distance to the origin of some

\displaystyle|\psi_{\mbox{\tiny{{log}}}}(p^{+}_{\nu})|=\log\left(\frac{1+r_{\nu}}{1-r_{\nu}}\right),\quad r_{\nu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}|2p^{+}_{\nu}-1|\quad;\quad d_{\mathbb{B}_{1}}(\bm{0},\bm{z})=\log\left(\frac{1+\|\bm{z}\|}{1-\|\bm{z}\|}\right).

Objective for hyperbolic geometric embedding of a DT

At the end of the top-down induction of a DT,
all
nodes have participated at some point during induction in the classification of examples, the root being the first leaf of a DT without arcs. A full mapping of this training history may help to interpret substantial local improvements, relate different such events if they appear in the same path, visualize options for subsequent pruning in their tree context
[
30
]
, etc. . So, a good embedding should map the largest number of nodes in a clean and readable way, representing, for each of them, how good or confident they are / were. Looking at (
25

\partial\mathbb{B}_{1}
. Here comes another side advantage of hyperbolic representation: for such nodes, the hyperbolic distance between them gives an approximate estimation of their joint absolute confidence
[

Embedding objective

(A)
the largest part of the tree in DT

(B)

is close to
\|\bm{z}_{\nu}\|
(

(C)

Satisfying accurately all three objectives
(A-C)
mean a very easy way to visually cross classification, confidence in classification and the semantic of the model. But it is not hard to show that this is hard for a

Monotonic Decision Trees and hyperbolic embeddings

Let us start by explaining why a complete DT embedding cannot even remotely guarantee
(A-C)
in general: the sequence of absolute confidences in a path from the root to a leaf is in general
not
monotonic. For example, one can have a root mapped to the center of the disk (
p^{+}_{\mbox{\tiny{root}}}=0.50
), with a child with
p^{+}_{\nu}\neq 0.50
and a grandchild with
p^{+}_{\nu^{\prime}}=0.50
, creating a double arc
\leftrightarrow
in the embedding. In the same way, loops can happen for longer paths, making tricky even approximate solutions to
(A-C)
of good quality. Note that the source of the disorder is the presence of nodes of poor predictive quality in the tree. Our solution is simple in principle:

Embed only the monotonic classification part of a DT

(meaning for each path from the root to a leaf in
H
, we end up embedding the subsequence of nodes whose absolute confidences are strictly increasing); to do so, we replace the DT by a path-monotonic approximation using a new class of models we introduce, called
Monotonic Decision Trees
(MDT).

Definition 5.1

A Monotonic Decision Tree (MDT) is a rooted tree with a Boolean test labeling each arc and a real valued prediction at each node. Any sequence of nodes in a path starting from the root is strictly monotonically increasing in absolute confidence. At any internal node, no two Boolean tests at its outgoing arcs can be simultaneously satisfied. The classification of an observation is obtained by the bottommost node’s prediction reachable from the root.

We now introduce an algorithm which takes as input a DT
H
and outputs a MDT
H^{\prime}
satisfying the following invariant:

(M)

for any observation
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the prediction
H^{\prime}(\bm{x})
is equal to the prediction in the path followed by
\bm{x}
in
H
of its deepest node in the strictly monotonic subsequence starting from the root of
H
.

The interesting link between distances in the Poincaré model of hyperbolic geometry and tree prediction in the CPE loss theory (

To preserve readability, we fully derive and describe the boosting algorithm, called

7
Discussion and conclusion

Our contributions start with a generalization of classical integration and derivation to a context encompassing the concept of additivity, upon which integration is built. This generalization is rooted in the theory governing nonextensive statistical mechanics, in which additivity needs to be replaced by a more general notion of
t
-additivity. The standard properties of integration and derivation that we have investigated are extended in a natural way, for many of them involving a generalization of the arithmetic over the reals also tied to the field of nonextensive statistical mechanics. That such properties can be obtained without additional technical “effort” offers a perspective of developments and applications in other subfields of ML where such tools could be used, not just in the context of hyperbolic embeddings or more broadly distortion measures on which we have focused. We note the recent developments of tools in connection with nonextensive statistical mechanics, that have been used in ML problems as diverse as clustering
[
4
]
, boosting
[
39
]
, optimal transport
[
3
]
, etc. .

More specific avenues for future work are related to our application. First, we experimentally observed that Mototonic Decision Trees (MDTs) can be substantially better than DTs even when using the DTs’ leveraging coefficients in boosting. This could be attributable to the fact that being smaller in general (and sometimes much smaller), they reduce the risk of overfitting on big models. This could make them a good alternative to DT for ML and deserves further investigation. Second, our embedding algorithm for MDTs, based on Sarkar’s approach, is heuristic and, while it does seem to provide good visual results in general (modulo some fine tuning of some of its parameters), it does not give any formal guarantees with respect to the embedding quality; this problem would deserve a full treatment of its own to get to a provably good embedding. This seems to be a very promising direction since popular packages to represent decision trees rely on a simple topological layer provided by the tree graph, to which various additional information are superimposed without link to the embedding. We believe our approach is the first to provide all layers of a full geometric embedding with a geometry directly interpretable in terms of classification performances, considering trees alone or in a boosted ensemble.

I.1
Primer on
t
-algebra

Classical arithmetic over the reals can be used to display duality relationships between operators using the
\log,\exp
functions, such as for example
\log(a/b)=\log a-\log b
,
\exp(a+b)=\exp(a)\cdot\exp(b)
, and so on. They can also be used to define one operator from another one. There is no difference between the operators appearing inside and outside functions. In the
t
-algebra, a difference appears and such relationships can be used to define the
t
-operators from those over the reals, as indeed one can define the tempered addition

\displaystyle x\oplus_{t}y

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\log_{t}(\exp_{t}(x)\cdot\exp_{t}(y)),

the tempered subtraction,

\displaystyle x\ominus_{t}y

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\log_{t}(\exp_{t}(x)/\exp_{t}(y)),

(both simplifying to the expressions we use), and of course the tempered product and division,

\displaystyle x\otimes_{t}y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\exp_{t}(\log_{t}(x)+\log_{t}(y))

;

\displaystyle x\oslash_{t}y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\exp_{t}(\log_{t}(x)-\log_{t}(y)),

whose simplified expression appears
e.g.
in
[
3
]
. See also
[
38
]
.

I.2
Functional
t
-additivity

As is well-known, Boltzman-Gibbs (and so, Shannon) entropy is additive while Tsallis entropy is
t
-additive. Note that additivity for BG requires being on the simplex, but
t
-additivity of Tsallis technically requires only positive measures – the simplex restriction ensures the limit exists for
t\rightarrow 1
and then T
\rightarrow
BG.

Divergences can also be
t
-additive
The KL divergence

\displaystyle D_{\textsc{kl}}(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k}p_{k}\log(p_{k}/q_{k})

is additive on the simplex but not
t
-additive: using a decomposition of
\bm{p},\bm{q}
as product of (independent) distributions (
\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2}
and
\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}
) using the cartesian product of their support, we indeed check
D_{\textsc{kl}}(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})=D_{\textsc{kl}}(\bm{p}_{1}\|\bm{q}_{1})+D_{\textsc{kl}}(\bm{p}_{2}\|\bm{q}_{2})
with
p_{ij}=p_{1i}p_{2j}
and
q_{ij}=q_{1i}q_{2j}
. On the other hand, Tsallis divergence
[
37
]
is
t
-additive on positive measures with such a decomposition, with

\displaystyle D_{\textsc{T}}(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k}p_{k}(p_{k}/q_{k})^{1-t}-1}{1-t},

and we check that
D_{\textsc{T}}(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})=D_{\textsc{T}}(\bm{p}_{1}\|\bm{q}_{1})+D_{\textsc{T}}(\bm{p}_{2}\|\bm{q}_{2})+(1-t)\cdot D_{\textsc{T}}(\bm{p}_{1}\|\bm{q}_{1})\cdot D_{\textsc{T}}(\bm{p}_{2}\|\bm{q}_{2})
(additional requirement to be on the simplex for convergence to
D_{\textsc{kl}}
as
t\rightarrow 1
). For
t\not\in(1,2)
and on the simplex, Tsallis divergence is an
f
-divergence with generator
z\mapsto(z^{2-t}-1)/(1-t)
. Similarly, the tempered relative entropy is
t
-additive
on the co-simplex
, where in this case

\displaystyle D_{t}(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1-\sum_{k}p_{k}q^{1-t}_{k}}{1-t},\bm{p},\bm{q}\in\tilde{\Delta}_{m}.

The tempered relative entropy is a Bregman divergence with generator
z\mapsto z\log_{t}z-\log_{t-1}z
.

II.8
Modifying Sarkar’s embedding in Poincaré disk

We refer to the concise and neat description of Sarkar’s embedding in
[
46
]
for the full algorithm. Our modification relies on changing one step of the algorithm, as described in Figure
6
. The key step that we change is step 5: in the description of
[
46
, Algorithm 1]
. This step embeds the children of a given node (and then the algorithm proceeds recursively until all nodes are processed). Sarkar’s algorithm corresponds to the simple case where all arcs to/from a node define a fixed angle, which does not change during reflection because Poincaré model is conformal. Hence, if the tree is binary, this angle is
2\pi/3
, which provides a very clean display of the tree. In our case however, some children may have just one arc to a leaf while others may support big subtrees. Also, arc lengths can vary substiantially. We thus design the region of the disk into which the subtrees are going to be embedded by choosing an angle proportional to the number of leaves reachable from the node, and of course lengths have to match the difference between absolute confidence between a node and its children. There is no optimization step to learn a clean embedding, so we rely on a set of hyperparameters to effectively compute this new step of the algorithm.

Figure 6:
Schematic description of our modification (bottom) of Sarkar’s algorithm (up). Our modification solely changes the step in which the children of a node (here,
a
) are computed, this node having been reflected back to the origin. Instead of using a fixed angle and length to position arcs (and thus children of the node), the angle depends on the number of leaves that can be reached from a children and the length depends on the difference between absolute confidence between the node and the corresponding child. We also define an angle which represents the domain (before reflecting back) in which the embedding is going to take place, shown with the thick dashed line (here, this angle is
\pi
).
