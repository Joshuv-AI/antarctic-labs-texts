Abstract

Specifically, we consider the computation of the 𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{OR} function of n n bits (where queries correspond to noisy readings of the bits) and the 𝖬𝖠𝖷 \mathsf{MAX} function of n n real numbers (where queries correspond to noisy pairwise comparisons). We show that an expected number of queries of ( 1 ± o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ 1 δ D 𝖪𝖫 ( p ∥ 1 − p ) (1\pm o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)} is both sufficient and necessary to compute both functions with a vanishing error probability δ = o ⁡ ( 1 ) \delta=o(1) , where D 𝖪𝖫 ( p ∥ 1 − p ) D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p) denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between 𝖡𝖾𝗋𝗇 ⁡ ( p ) \mathsf{Bern}(p) and 𝖡𝖾𝗋𝗇 ⁡ ( 1 − p ) \mathsf{Bern}(1-p) distributions. Compared to previous work, our results tighten the dependence on p p in both the upper and lower bounds for the two functions.



I
Introduction

Coping with noise in computing is an important problem to consider in large systems. With applications in fault tolerance
(
Hastad et al. 1987
,
Pease et al. 1980
,
Pippenger et al. 1991
)
, active ranking
(
Shah and Wainwright 2018
,
Agarwal et al. 2017
,
Falahatgar et al. 2017
,
Heckel et al. 2019
,
Wang et al. 2022
,
Gu and Xu 2023
)
, noisy searching
(
Berlekamp 1964
,
Horstein 1963
,
Burnashev and Zigangirov 1974
,
Pelc 1989
,
Karp and Kleinberg 2007
)
, among many others, the goal is to devise algorithms that are robust enough to detect and correct the errors that can happen during the computation. More concretely, the problem can be defined as follows: suppose an agent is interested in computing a function
f
of
n
variables with an error probability at most
\delta
, as quickly as possible. To this end, the agent can ask binary questions (referred to hereafter as
queries
) about the variables in hand. The binary response to the queries is observed by the agent through a binary symmetric channel (BSC) with crossover probability
p
. The agent can adaptively design subsequent queries based on the responses to previous queries. The goal is to characterize the relation between
n
,
\delta
,
p
, and the query complexity, which is defined as the minimal number of queries that are needed by the agent to meet the intended goal of computing the function
f
.

This paper considers the computation of the
\mathsf{OR}
function of
n
bits and the
\mathsf{MAX}
function of
n
real numbers. For the computation of the
\mathsf{OR}
function, the noisy queries correspond to noisy readings of the bits, where at each timestep, the agent queries one of the bits, and with probability
p
, the wrong value of the bit is returned. For the computation of the
\mathsf{MAX}
function, the noisy queries correspond to noisy pairwise comparisons, where at each timestep, the agent asks to compare two numbers in the given sequence, and with probability
p
, the incorrect result of the comparison is returned.

This model for the
\mathsf{OR}
and
\mathsf{MAX}
functions has been previously considered in
(
Feige et al. 1994
)
, where upper and lower bounds on the number of queries that are needed to compute the two functions are derived in terms of the number of variables
n
, the noise probability
p
and the desired error probability
\delta
. The upper and lower bounds in
(
Feige et al. 1994
)
are tight only in terms of dependence on
n
; however, the exact dependence of the query complexity on the parameters
p
and
\delta
is not very clear. In this paper, we derive new upper and lower bounds for the computation of the
\mathsf{OR}
and
\mathsf{MAX}
functions, with tight dependence on
p
and
\delta
.

I-A

Main Contribution

The main result of this paper can be stated as follows: it is both sufficient and necessary to use

(1\pm o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}

queries in expectation to compute the
\mathsf{OR}
and
\mathsf{MAX}
functions with a vanishing error probability
\delta=o(1)
, where
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
\mathsf{Bern}(p)
and
\mathsf{Bern}(1-p)
distributions. This result simultaneously improves the dependence on
p
in both the upper and lower bounds derived in
(
Feige et al. 1994
)
.

From a technical standpoint, our lower bound for both functions is based on Le Cam’s two point method, which transforms the noisy computing problem in hand into a proper binary testing problem between two instances of the input variables. By carefully designing the two instances to look at, Le Cam’s two point lemma
(
Yu 1997
)
then gives a non-asymptotic lower bound on the error probability of any estimator that wishes to distinguish the evaluation of the function over those two instances.

The algorithms that attain the upper bounds are executed in two steps. In the first step, a subset of the input variables of sublinear size is chosen. In the second step, an existing algorithm (e.g., one of the algorithms in
(
Feige et al. 1994
)
) is executed over the chosen subset. For the
\mathsf{OR}
function, the chosen subset corresponds to the input variables that, when queried, return a value of 1. For the
\mathsf{MAX}
function, the subset corresponds to the elements of the sequence that appear to be larger than the maximum of a random subsample of the input variables (of sublinear size). We show that
(1+o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}
queries are used on average in the first step, while the query complexity of the second step ends up being a lower order term.

I-B

Related Work

The noisy computation of the
\mathsf{OR}
function has been first studied in the literature of circuit with noisy gates
(
Dobrushin and Ortyukov 1977a
,
Dobrushin and Ortyukov 1977b
,
Von Neumann 1956
,
Pippenger et al. 1991
,
Gács and Gál 1994
)
and noisy decision trees
(
Feige et al. 1994
,
Evans and Pippenger 1998
,
Reischuk and Schmeltz 1991
)
. Since the querying model for the
\mathsf{OR}
function corresponds to noisy reading of the bits, it is closely related to the best arm identification problem, where the variables correspond to real-valued arms and the goal is to identify the arm with the largest value (i.e., reward)
(
Bubeck et al. 2009
,
Audibert et al. 2010
,
Garivier and Kaufmann 2016
,
Jamieson and Nowak 2014
,
Gabillon et al. 2012
,
Kaufmann et al. 2016
)
. Indeed, any best-arm identification algorithm can be converted to an
\mathsf{OR}
computation since the evaluation of the
\mathsf{OR}
function of
n
bits is equal to the evaluation of their maximum. This observation gives an upper bound
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{1-H(p)}\right)
on the number of queries needed for the computation of the
\mathsf{OR}
function
(
Audibert et al. 2010
)
, where
H(\cdot)
denotes the binary entropy function. However, as we shall see in this paper, this bound doesn’t give a tight dependence on
p
.

On the other hand, the noisy computation of the
\mathsf{MAX}
function has closely followed the literature on the active ranking problem (also known as “noisy sorting”) and the top-
k
selection problem
(
Mohajer et al. 2017
,
Shah and Wainwright 2018
,
Agarwal et al. 2017
,
Falahatgar et al. 2017
,
Heckel et al. 2019
,
Wang et al. 2022
,
Wang et al. 2023
,
Gu and Xu 2023
)
, where the ranking of a subset of the
n
items is required. Many of these works considered generalized noise models, where the noise probability can depend on the pair of elements to be compared. For our simplified noise model, however, the best known upper and lower bounds for the computation of the
\mathsf{MAX}
function are due to
(
Feige et al. 1994
)
which gives an upper bound based on the popular knockout tournament algorithm and a lower bound of
\Omega\left(\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{\log((1-p)/p)}\right)
queries. As we shall see, the dependence on
p
in both bounds can be improved.

II
Problem Formulation and Main Results

II-A

Noisy
\mathsf{OR}
Problem

Let us first consider the problem of computing the
\mathsf{OR}
function of
n
bits using noisy observations of the bits. To this end, let
{\bf X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be a sequence of
n
bits. The
\mathsf{OR}
function is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{OR}({\bf X})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }\exists\,i\in[n]:X_{i}=1\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1)

At the
k
th time step, an agent can submit a query
U_{k}
corresponding to one of the bits, i.e.,
U_{k}\triangleq X_{i}
for some
i\in[n]
. The response
Y_{k}
can be expressed as

Y_{k}=U_{k}\oplus Z_{k},

where
Z_{k}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables. Note that the queries are designed adaptively, i.e.,

U_{k}=f_{k}({\bf U}^{k-1},{\bf Y}^{k-1}),

for some (possibly stochastic) function
f_{k}
. Moreover, the querying strategy may have a variable stopping time that depends on the query responses, i.e., the number of queries
M
may be random. The goal of the agent is to estimate the
\mathsf{OR}
function using the noisy queries and responses.

II-B

Noisy
\mathsf{MAX}
Problem

With a slight abuse of notation, for the noisy
\mathsf{MAX}
problem, we use
{\bf X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a sequence of
n
real numbers. The
\mathsf{MAX}
function is defined as

\mathsf{MAX}({\bf X})=\argmax_{i\in[n]}\,X_{i}.

At the
k
th time step, the agent can submit a query
(U_{k},V_{k})\triangleq(X_{i},X_{j})
for some
i,j\in[n]
. The response
Y_{k}
can be expressed as

Y_{k}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{U_{k}<V_{k}\}}\oplus Z_{k},

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\{.\}}
denotes the indicator function, and
Z_{k}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables. As for the
\mathsf{OR}
problem, the queries can be designed adaptively, i.e.,

(U_{k},V_{k})=g_{k}({\bf U}^{k-1},{\bf V}^{k-1},{\bf Y}^{k-1}),

for some (possibly stochastic) function
g_{k}
. Moreover, the querying strategy may have a variable stopping time. The goal of the agent is to estimate the
\mathsf{MAX}
function using the responses to the noisy pairwise comparisons.

Remark II.1
.

Notice the difference in the querying models for the noisy
\mathsf{OR}
and
\mathsf{MAX}
problems. While the queries in the noisy
\mathsf{OR}
problem correspond to readings of the bits, the queries in the noisy
\mathsf{MAX}
problem correspond to pairwise comparisons between the elements in the given sequence. Due to this distinction, one cannot immediately translate any upper or lower bound on the number of queries from one problem to the other (despite the fact that, for example, the
\mathsf{OR}
of
n
bits is equal to their maximum).

II-C

Main Results

Our main results can be summarized in the following four theorems, which give tight upper and lower bounds on the expected number of queries needed to compute the noisy
\mathsf{OR}
and noisy
\mathsf{MAX}
functions.

Theorem II.1

(Lower bound for
\mathsf{OR}
)
.

For the noisy
\mathsf{OR}
problem, it holds that

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{OR}({\bf x}))\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{m\cdot D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}{n}\right),

where
m
is the expected number of queries under the worst-case input binary sequence.
Thus, the expected number of queries needed to compute the
\mathsf{OR}
function with worst-case error probability at most
\delta=o(1)
is lower bounded by
(1-o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}
.

Theorem II.2

(Upper bound for
\mathsf{OR}
)
.

For any vanishing
\delta=o(1)
, there exists an algorithm (Algorithm
3
in Section
III-B
) that computes the
\mathsf{OR}
function with an expected number of queries at most

(1+o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}

(2)

and worst-case error probability at most
\delta
.

Theorem II.3

(Lower bound for
\mathsf{MAX}
)
.

For the noisy
\mathsf{MAX}
problem, it holds that

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{MAX}({\bf x}))\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{m\cdot D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}{n}\right),

where
m
is the expected number of queries under the worst-case input sequence. Thus, the expected number of queries needed to compute the
\mathsf{MAX}
function with worst-case error probability at most
\delta=o(1)
is lower bounded by
(1-o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}
.

Theorem II.4

(Upper bound for
\mathsf{MAX}
)
.

For any vanishing
\delta=o(1)
, there exists an algorithm (Algorithm
6
in Section
IV-B
) that computes the
\mathsf{MAX}
function with an expected number of queries at most

(1+o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p||1-p)}

and worst-case error probability at most
\delta
.

III
Noisy
\mathsf{OR}
Problem

III-A

Lower Bound

First, we consider the lower bound for the noisy
\mathsf{OR}
problem (Theorem
II.1
). We give a proof of the theorem based on Le Cam’s two point method
(
LeCam 1973
,
Yu 1997
)
, which is stated in Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
for the reader’s convenience.

Proof of Theorem
II.1
.

Let
{\bf x}_{0}
be the length-
n
all-zero sequence, and let
{\bf x}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be such that
x_{jj}=1
and
x_{ji}=0
for
i\neq j
, where
x_{ji}
refers to the
i
-th element in the
j
th binary sequence
{\bf x}_{j}
. We can first verify that for any estimate
\hat{\mu}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathds{1}(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{OR}({\bf x}_{0}))+\mathds{1}(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{OR}({\bf x}_{j}))\geq 1.

By applying Le Cam’s two point lemma on
{\bf x}_{0}
and
{\bf x}_{j}
, we know that

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{OR}({\bf x}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\mathsf{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{j}})\right).

Here,
\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{j}}
denotes the distribution of the query responses in
m
rounds when the underlying binary sequence is
{\bf x}_{j}
. By taking maximum over
j
on the right-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{OR}({\bf x}))

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\mathsf{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{j}})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-D_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{j}})\right),

where
(a)
follows due to the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality
(
Bretagnolle and Huber 1979
)
(Lemma
A.2
in Appendix
A
). Now, we aim at computing
D_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{j}})
. Let
M_{j}
be the random variable that denotes the number of times the
j
th element is queried among all
m
rounds. From the divergence decomposition lemma
(
Auer et al. 1995
)
(Lemma
A.3
in Appendix
A
) and the fact that
{\bf x}_{0}
and
{\bf x}_{j}
only differ in the
j
th position, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\bf x}_{j}})=\E_{{\bf x}_{0}}[M_{j}]\cdot D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p).

Here,
\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}_{0}}[M_{j}]
denotes the expected number of times the
j
th element is queried when the underlying sequence is
{\bf x}_{0}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{OR}({\bf x}))\geq\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\E_{{\bf x}_{0}}[M_{j}]\cdot D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)\right).

Now, since
\sum_{j}\E_{{\bf x}_{0}}[M_{j}]=\E_{{\bf x}_{0}}[M]\leq\sup_{\bf x}\E_{\bf x}[M]\triangleq m
, there must exist some
j
such that
\E_{{\bf x}_{0}}[M_{j}]\leq m/n
. This gives

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{OR}({\bf x}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\frac{m\cdot D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}{n}\right).

Therefore, to achieve an error probability
\delta
for any input sequence
{\bf x}
, the number of queries must satisfy that

m\geq\frac{n\log\frac{1}{4\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p||1-p)}=(1-o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p||1-p)}.

∎

For the

\hat{\mu}

\mathsf{OR}({\bf x})=1
. Without loss of generality, assume that
x_{1}=1
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{1}\triangleq\{\hat{\mu}=0\}
be the error event in this case, and let
\mathcal{A}\triangleq\{x_{1}\notin\mathcal{S}\}
denote an auxiliary event that
x_{1}
is not appended into
{\bf y}
. By the union bound, we have

\P(\mathcal{E}_{1})\leq\P({\mathcal{A}})+\P({\mathcal{E}}_{1}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}).

For the first term, we notice that
\P({\mathcal{A}})\leq\delta
by Lemma
III.1
. For the second term, note that
|\mathcal{S}|\geq 1
on the event
{\mathcal{A}}^{c}
. Therefore, the only possibility for the algorithm to return
0
is that the function call of
Tournament_OR
on Line 11 incorrectly returns
0
. By Lemma
III.2
, we have that
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{1}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}^{c})\leq\delta
. This leads to the upper bound
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{1})\leq 2\delta
.

Next, we move on to consider the all-zero input sequence. Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\triangleq\{\hat{\mu}=1\}
denote the error event in this case, and define an auxiliary event
{\mathcal{B}}=\{|\mathcal{S}|\geq\max(\log n,\,n\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta})\}
. As before, we have

\P(\mathcal{E}_{0})\leq\P({\mathcal{B}})+\P({\mathcal{E}}_{0}|{\mathcal{B}}^{c}).

By Lemma
III.2
, we know that
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{0}|{\mathcal{B}}^{c})\leq\delta
. Moreover, we claim that
\P({\mathcal{B}})=o(\delta)
. These would together imply that
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{0})=(1+o(1))\delta
.

Now we move on to show the claim. By Lemma
III.1
, we know that the random variable
|\mathcal{S}|
is statistically dominated by
\mathrm{Binom}(n,\delta)
. We will separately consider two different regimes to show the claim.

Regime 1:
\delta\geq\frac{1}{n}
.
In this case,
\max(\log n,\,n\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta})=n\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}
. We have

\displaystyle\P\left(|\mathcal{S}|\geq n\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\P\left(\mathrm{Binom}(n,\delta)\geq n\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(-nD_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Big\|\delta\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-n\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}\log\log\frac{1}{\delta}(1-o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}o(\delta),

where
(a)
follows by the additive Chernoff bound (Lemma
A.4
in Appendix
A
), and
(b)
follows because
n\delta\geq 1
and
\log\log\frac{1}{\delta}=\omega(1)
.

Regime 2:
\delta<\frac{1}{n}
.
In this case,
\max(\log n,\,n\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta})=\log n
. We have

\displaystyle\P(|\mathcal{S}|\geq\log n)

\displaystyle\leq\P\left(\mathrm{Binom}(n,\delta)\geq\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-nD_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big\|\delta\right)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\exp\left(-\log n\log\frac{\log n}{n\delta}(1-o(1))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n\delta}{\log n}\right)^{(1-o(1))\log n},

where
(c)
follows because
\delta<1/n
. To complete the argument, first suppose that
\delta=n^{-\Theta(1)}
. In this case, because
n\delta<1
, we have

\left(\frac{n\delta}{\log n}\right)^{(1-o(1))\log n}<\frac{1}{(\log n)^{(1-o(1))\log n}}=n^{-\omega(1)}=o(\delta).

Secondly, suppose
\delta=n^{-\omega(1)}
. Then we have

\exp\left(-\log n\log\frac{\log n}{n\delta}(1-o(1))\right)=\exp\left(-\log n\log\frac{1}{\delta}(1-o(1))\right)=o(\delta).

∎

III-B
2
Query Analysis

Let
M
denote the number of queries made by the
NoisyOR
algorithm. The following lemma provides an upper bound on the expectation of
M
, and hence completes the proof of Theorem
II.2
.

Lemma III.4
.

For
\delta=o(1)
, the expected number of queries made by the
NoisyOR
algorithm satisfies that

\E[M]\leq(1+o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}.

Proof.

As before, let
{\mathcal{S}}
denote the set of bits appended to
{\bf y}
in Line 4 of the algorithm. To bound
\E[M]
, we only need to bound the expected number of queries made by the
n
calls of the
CheckBit
function on Line 3, and the single call of the
Tournament_OR
function on Line 11.
By Lemma
III.1
, we know that the expected number of queries spent at Line 3 is at most

\frac{n}{1-2p}\left\lceil\frac{\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}}{\log\frac{1-p}{p}}\right\rceil\leq\frac{n}{1-2p}\left(\frac{\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}}{\log\frac{1-p}{p}}+1\right)=(1+o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{(1-2p)\log\frac{1-p}{p}}=(1+o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}},

where the penultimate equality follows because
\delta=o(1)
and
p
is bounded away from
0
and
1/2
, and the last equality follows because
{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}\triangleq(1-2p)\log\frac{1-p}{p}
.
By Lemma
III.2
, we know that the expected number of queries spent at Line 11 is at most

C\left(\frac{\E[|\mathcal{S}|]}{1-H(p)}+\frac{\E[|\mathcal{S}|]\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}\right)=C(1+o(1))\E[|\mathcal{S}|]\log(1/\delta)=o(n\log(1/\delta)),

where the penultimate equality follows because
\delta=o(1)
and
p
is bounded away from
0
and
1/2
, and the last equality follows because we always have
|\mathcal{S}|=o(n)
on the event that Line 11 is executed.

Finally, by the linearity of expectation, we have
\E[M]\leq(1+o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
.
∎

IV
Noisy
\mathsf{MAX}
Problem

IV-A

Lower Bound

In this part, we turn our attention to the noisy
\mathsf{MAX}
problem. We first consider the lower bound established in Theorem
II.3
. As for the
\mathsf{OR}
problem, our proof of the lower bound is based on Le Cam’s two point method (see Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
).

Proof of Theorem
II.3
.

Consider an arbitrary sequence
{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
y_{1}<\cdots<y_{n}
. Clearly, in this case, we have
\mathsf{MAX}({\bf y})=n
. Now, for
i\in[n-1]
, consider the sequence
{\bf z}_{i}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i-1},y_{n},y_{i},\ldots,y_{n-1})
, i.e., we move the
n
th element in
{\bf y}
and insert it between
y_{i-1}
and
y_{i}
. Notice that
\mathsf{MAX}({\bf z}_{i})=i
for all
i\in[n-1]
.

Let
M
denote the total number of queries made by the estimator, and let
M_{i,j}
represent the number of pairwise comparisons made between the
i
th and
j
th elements in the
M
rounds. We first notice that, for any estimator
\hat{\mu}
and any
i\in[n-1]
,

\displaystyle\mathds{1}(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{MAX}({\bf y}))+\mathds{1}(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{MAX}({\bf z}_{i}))\geq 1.

Following a similar proof as Theorem
II.1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{MAX}({\bf x}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}\sup_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\mathsf{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf y}},\mathbb{P}_{{\bf z}_{i}})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geq}}\sup_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-D_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bf y}},\mathbb{P}_{{\bf z}_{i}})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geq}}\sup_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\E_{{\bf y}}[M_{i,j}]\cdot D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)\right),

where
\mathbb{P}_{{\bf z}_{i}}
denotes the distribution of query responses in
m
rounds when the underlying sequence is
{\bf z}_{i}
. Here,
(a)
follows by Le Cam’s two point lemma (Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
),
(b)
follows from the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality (Lemma
A.2
in Appendix
A
), and
(c)
follows from the divergence decomposition lemma (Lemma
A.3
in Appendix
A
) and the fact that the distributions
\mathbb{P}_{{\bf y}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{{\bf z}_{i}}
only differ when the
i
th element is compared with a subsequent element. Now, since
\sum_{i,j\in[n],i<j}\E_{{\bf y}}[M_{i,j}]=\E_{{\bf y}}[M]\leq\sup_{\bf x}\E_{\bf x}[M]\triangleq m
, there must exist some index
i
such that
\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\E_{{\bf y}}[M_{i,j}]\leq\frac{m}{n-1}
. This gives

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\P(\hat{\mu}\neq\mathsf{MAX}({\bf x}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\frac{m\cdot{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}{n-1}\right).

It follows that, to achieve an error probability at most
\delta=o(1)
for any input sequence
{\bf x}
, the number of queries must satisfy that

m\geq\frac{(n-1)\log\frac{1}{4\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}=(1-o(1))\frac{n\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}.

∎

Next, we prove the upper bound stated in Theorem

. To prove the theorem, we devise a querying strategy for the noisy
\mathsf{MAX}

The

Error Analysis

Let

denote the largest element in
{\bf x}
. In the following, we provide an upper bound on the error probability

.

Lemma IV.3

(Error probability of
NoisyMax
)
.

The error probability of the
NoisyMax
algorithm satisfies that

\P(\hat{x}\neq x^{*})\leq 3\delta.

Proof.

To prove Lemma
IV.3
, we first define a few error events. Let
{\mathcal{E}}=\{\hat{x}\neq x^{*}\}
denote the error probability of the
NoisyMax
algorithm. Let
y^{*}\triangleq\mathsf{MAX}({\bf y})
and
z^{*}\triangleq\mathsf{MAX}({\bf z})
denote the indices of the largest elements of
{\bf y}
and
{\bf z}
, where
{\bf y}
and
{\bf z}
are as constructed in Lines 3 and 9 of Algorithm
6
respectively. We define
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}=\{\hat{y}\neq y^{*}\}
as the event that the
Tournament_Max
algorithm outputs an incorrect estimate in Line 5. We let
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}=\{x^{*}\text{ is not contained in }{\bf z}\}
denote the event that the true maximum element
x^{*}
does not belong to the vector
{\bf z}
. Finally, we define
{\mathcal{E}}_{3}=\{\hat{x}\neq z^{*}\}
as the event that the
Tournament_Max
algorithm in Line 10 returns an incorrect estimate.

By the union bound, we clearly have

\P({\mathcal{E}})\leq\P({\mathcal{E}}_{1})+\P({\mathcal{E}}_{2}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c})+\P({\mathcal{E}}_{3}).

By Lemma
IV.2
, we have that
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{1})\leq\delta
and
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{3})\leq\delta
. To bound
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{2}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c})
, we consider two cases. First, in the case that
x^{*}
is appended to
{\bf y}
in Line 3, we have that
x^{*}=y^{*}
. Given the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c}
, we thus know that
\hat{y}=x^{*}
, which is appended to
{\bf z}
in Line 6. Hence, we have that
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{2}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c})=0
in this case. In the second case that
x^{*}
is not appended to
{\bf y}
in Line 3, we have that
x^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{c}
. It follows that
x^{*}
is not appended to
{\bf z}
only if the result of the noisy comparison of
\hat{y}
and
x^{*}
in Line 8 is incorrect. Since this happens with error probability at most
\delta
, we have that
\P({\mathcal{E}}_{2}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c})\leq\delta
in this case, and the statement of the lemma follows.

IV-B
2
Query Analysis

Let
M
denote the number of queries made by the
NoisyMax
algorithm. In the following lemma, we provide an upper bound on
\E[M]
.

Lemma IV.4
.

Suppose
\delta=o(1)
. The expected number of queries
\E[M]
of the
NoisyMax
algorithm satisfies that

\E[M]\leq(1+o(1))\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p||1-p)}.

Proof.

To prove Lemma
IV.4
, we first define two auxiliary sets. Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the set of elements
x_{i}
that were appended to
{\bf y}
(as defined in Line 4 of Algorithm
6
), and let
{\mathcal{T}}
be the set of elements
x_{i}
that were appended to
{\bf z}
in Lines 6 and 9. Consider the two events:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq\frac{n}{\log n}+n^{3/4}\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\{|{\mathcal{T}}|\leq\sqrt{n}+\delta n+n^{3/4}\}.

By the law of total expectation, we know that

\E[M]=\E[M\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}]\P({\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}})+\E[M\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c}]\P({\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c}).

In the following, we analyze the two conditional expectations. We notice that all the queries made by the
NoisyMax
algorithm are spent on the two calls of the
Tournament_Max
algorithm in Lines 5 and 10, and the
n-|{\mathcal{S}}|
calls of the
NoisyCompare
algorithm in Line 8.
By Lemma
IV.2
, we know that the expected number number of queries spent on Lines 5 and 10 are
O\left(\E\left[|{\mathcal{S}}|\right]\log(1/\delta)\right)
and
O\left(\E\left[|{\mathcal{T}}|\right]\log(1/\delta)\right)
respectively, because
\delta=o(1)
and
p
is a constant bounded away from
0
and
1/2
. By Lemma
IV.1
, we have that the expected number of queries spent on Line 8 is at most
(1+o(1))\frac{(n-\E\left[|{\mathcal{S}}|\right])\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
. Therefore, by the linearity of expectation, we have that

\E[M]=O\left(\E\left[|{\mathcal{S}}|\right]\log(1/\delta)\right)+O\left(\E\left[|{\mathcal{T}}|\right]\log(1/\delta)\right)+(1+o(1))\frac{(n-\E\left[|{\mathcal{S}}|\right])\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}.

Notice that on the event
{\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}
, we have that
\E\left[|{\mathcal{S}}|\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}\right]=o(n)
and
\E\left[|{\mathcal{T}}|\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}\right]=o(n)
(since
\delta=o(1)
). It follows that

\E\left[M\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}\right]\leq(1+o(1))\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}.

In the case when
{\mathcal{A}}
or
{\mathcal{B}}
does not hold, we still have that
\E\left[|{\mathcal{S}}|\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c}\right]=O(n)
and
\E\left[|{\mathcal{T}}|\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c}\right]=O(n)
. Thus, we have that

\E\left[M\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c}\right]=O\left(\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}\right).

It follows that

\displaystyle\E[M]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[M\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}\right]+\E\left[M\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c}\right]\P({\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}+O\left(\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}\right)\P({\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c}).

Hence, to complete the proof, it suffices to show that
\P({\mathcal{A}}^{c}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{c})=o(1)
. This is implied by the following two lemmas, whose proofs are deferred to Appendix

.

Lemma IV.5
.

The probability of event
{\mathcal{A}}^{c}
satisfies that

\P({\mathcal{A}}^{c})={\mathcal{O}}(\exp(-n^{c_{1}}))

for some positive constant

.

Lemma IV.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}=\{\hat{y}\neq y^{*}\}
denote the event that the
Tournament_Max
algorithm returns an incorrect estimate in Line 5 of Algorithm
6
. The conditional probability of event
{\mathcal{B}}^{c}

IV.4
. Along with Lemma
IV.3
, this implies the result stated in Theorem
II.4
.
∎

V
Extension to Varying
p

In our main results for the
\mathsf{OR}
problem and the
\mathsf{MAX}
problem, we established tight achievability and converse results under the assumptions that
\delta=o(1)
and
p
is a constant bounded away from
0
and
1/2
.
In this section, we maintain the premise of a diminishing error probability,
\delta=o(1)
, but explore scenarios where
p
converges to either zero or half as
n
approaches infinity. Within this context, we delve into the two problems while demonstrating that we can still derive matching achievability and converse results—except in the specific instance where
\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\log(1/p)}=\Theta(1)
. Our subsequent discussion will primarily revolve around the
\mathsf{OR}
problem, with a parallel argument applicable to the
\mathsf{MAX}
problem.

p\rightarrow 1/2
as
n\rightarrow\infty

In this case, we can still show that
(1\pm o(1))\frac{n\log{1/\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
queries in expectation are both necessary and sufficient for achieving a vanishing error probability
\delta
. Firstly, the proof of Theorem
II.1
does not use the constant assumption on
p
, so the lower bound
(1-o(1))\frac{n\log{1/\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
still holds for any
p
. Secondly, at most
(1+o(1))\frac{n\log{1/\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
in expectation can still be achieved by the proposed
NoisyOR
algorithm. To see this, we focus on the proof of Lemma
III.4
, where we analyze the expected number of queries of the algorithm. Recall that in this proof, we bounded the number of queries spent on Lines 3 and 11 using Lemmas
III.1
and
III.2
respectively. These two lemmas hold for any
p
, and in the case of
p\rightarrow 1/2
, it is not hard to see the the number of queries spent on Lines 3 and 11 are
(1+o(1))\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
and
o\left(\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}\right)
respectively. Therefore, the total expected number of queries is at most
(1+o(1))\frac{n\log{1/\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
.

p\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty

In the case of vanishing
p
, we will discuss three sub-cases separately.

Firstly, suppose that
\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\log(1/p)}=\omega(1)
. In this case, we still have that
(1\pm o(1))\frac{n\log{1/\delta}}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}
queries in expectation are both necessary and sufficient for achieving a vanishing error probability
\delta
. The lower bound follows because Theorem
II.1
holds for any
p
. The upper bounded is achieved by the proposed
NoisyOR
algorithm for the same reason as the case
p\rightarrow 1/2
.

Secondly, suppose that
\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\log(1/p)}=o(1)
. In this case, at least
n
queries are necessary for achieving a vanishing error probability
\delta
, while the proposed
NoisyOR
algorithm uses at most
(1+o(1))n
queries in expectation. To see the lower bound, we notice that trivially each of the
n
bits must be queried at least once to achieve any error probability
\delta<1/2
. To see the upper bound, we use Lemmas
III.1
and
III.2
to bound the number of queries spent on Lines 3 and 11 of the
NoisyOR
algorithm respectively. By Lemma
III.1
, we know that the expected number of queries spent on Line 3 is at most

\frac{n}{1-2p}\left\lceil\frac{\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}}{\log\frac{1-p}{p}}\right\rceil=\frac{n}{1-2p}=(1+o(1))n.

By Lemma
III.2
, we know that the expected number of queries spent on Line 11 is at most

C\left(\frac{\E[|\mathcal{S}|]}{1-H(p)}+\frac{\E[|\mathcal{S}|]\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}\right)=O(\E[|\mathcal{S}|])=o(n),

where
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of elements in
{\bf y}
, and the first equality follows because
1-H(p)=\Theta(1)
and
\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}=\Theta(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\log(1/p)})
. Therefore the expected number of queries made by
NoisyOR
algorithm is at most
(1+o(1))n
.

Finally, suppose
\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\log(1/p)}=\Theta(1)
. In this case, the expected number of queries for achieving error probability
\delta
is at least
\max\{n,(1-o(1))\frac{n\log(1/\delta)}{{D_{\mathsf{KL}}(p\|1-p)}}\}=\Theta(n)
, while the expected number of queries of the proposed
NoisyOR
algorithm is at most
\frac{(1+o(1))n}{1-2p}\left\lceil\frac{\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}}{\log\frac{1-p}{p}}\right\rceil=\Theta(n)
. So our upper and lower bounds are tight up to constant, but the exact constant is still unknown. This untightness is because when
\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\log(1/p)}
is a constant, the
\left\lceil\frac{\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}}{\log\frac{1-p}{p}}\right\rceil
term in Lemma
III.1
cannot be upper bounded by
(1+o(1))\frac{\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}}{\log\frac{1-p}{p}}
. How to get the exact constant in this case is still an open problem.

Appendix A
Useful Lemmas

Here, we introduce several important lemmas.

Lemma A.1

(Le Cam’s Two Point Lemma, see e.g.
(
Yu 1997
)
)
.

Let
\Theta
and
\hat{\Theta}
be arbitrary parameter spaces, and let
L:\Theta\times\hat{\Theta}\to\mathbb{R}
be any loss function. Suppose that
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\Theta
satisfy the following separation condition:

\displaystyle\forall{\hat{\theta}\in\hat{\Theta}},L(\theta_{0},\hat{\theta})+L(\theta_{1},\hat{\theta})\geq\Delta>0.

Then we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\,\sup_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[L(\theta,\hat{\theta})]\geq\frac{\Delta}{2}\left(1-\mathsf{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}})\right),

where
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{i}}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, denotes the sample distribution under the parameter
\theta_{i}
.

Lemma A.2

(Bretagnolle–Huber inequality
(
(
Bretagnolle and Huber 1979
)
, and Lemma 2.6 in
(
Tsybakov 2004
)
))
.

For any two distributions
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-D_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})\right).

Lemma A.3

(Divergence Decomposition (
(
Auer et al. 1995
)
, and Lemma 15.1 in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
))
.

Let
M_{i}
be the random variable denoting the number of times experiment
i\in[n]
is performed under some policy
\pi
, then for two distributions
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}
,
\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}
under policy
\pi
,

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}^{\pi},\mathbb{Q}^{\pi})=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}}[M_{i}]D_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\pi},\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{\pi}).

Lemma A.4

(Chernoff bound
(
Mitzenmacher and Upfal 2005
)
)
.

Let
Z\sim\mathrm{Binom}(n,p)
. For all
0<\delta<1
and
\epsilon>0
, it holds that

\P\left(Z\geq(1+\delta)np\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}np}{3}\right),

and

\P\left(Z\geq n(p+\epsilon)\right)\leq\exp\big(-nD_{\mathsf{KL}}(p+\epsilon\|p)\big).

Appendix B
Helper Lemmas for Theorem
II.4

In this appendix, we prove Lemmas
IV.5
and
IV.6
, which were used to complete the proof of Theorem
II.4
.

Proof of Lemma
IV.5
.

Notice that

\displaystyle\P({\mathcal{A}}^{c})

\displaystyle=\P\left(\mathrm{Binom}\left(n,\frac{1}{\log n}\right)>\frac{n}{\log n}+n^{3/4}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{1/2}\log n}{3}\right),

where
(a)
follows from the multiplicative Chernoff bound (Lemma
A.4
in Appendix
A
). Hence,
\P({\mathcal{A}}^{c})={\mathcal{O}}(\exp(-n^{c_{1}}))
for any constant
c_{1}<1/2
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
IV.6
.

Notice that on the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c}
, we have that
\hat{y}=y^{*}
. Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}^{+}

\displaystyle\triangleq\{x\in{\mathcal{S}}^{c}:x>y^{*}\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}^{-}

\displaystyle\triangleq\{x\in{\mathcal{S}}^{c}:x<y^{*}\}.

Notice that there are two types of elements in the set
{\mathcal{T}}
. The first type consists of the elements
x\in{\mathcal{X}}^{+}
appended into
{\mathcal{T}}
because
NoisyCompare
(y^{*},x,\delta)
correctly outputs true. The other type consists of the elements
x\in{\mathcal{X}}^{-}
appended into
{\mathcal{T}}
because
NoisyCompare
(y^{*},x,\delta)
incorrectly returns true. By Lemma
IV.1
, the
NoisyCompare
algorithm returns an incorrect estimate with probability at most
\delta
. Thus,
|{\mathcal{T}}|
is statistically dominated by the random variable
|{\mathcal{X}}^{+}|+\mathrm{Binom}(|{\mathcal{X}}^{-}|,\delta)
. Next, we derive large deviation bounds for
|{\mathcal{X}}^{+}|
and
\mathrm{Binom}(|{\mathcal{X}}^{-}|,\delta)
. First, notice that

\displaystyle\P(|{\mathcal{X}}^{+}|\geq\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log n}\right)^{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log n}\right),

where (a) follows because the event
\{|{\mathcal{X}}^{+}|\geq\sqrt{n}\}
is equivalent to the event that the largest
\sqrt{n}
elements are not sampled into
{\mathcal{S}}
, and (b) follows by
1+x\leq\exp(x)
. Next, we have that

\displaystyle\P(\mathrm{Binom}(|{\mathcal{X}}^{-}|,\delta)\geq\delta n+n^{3/4})

\displaystyle\leq\P(\mathrm{Binom}(n,\delta)\geq\delta n+n^{3/4})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{3}\right),

where
(c)
follows by the multiplicative Chernoff bound (Lemma
A.4
in Appendix
A
). Finally, the proof is completed by realizing that

\displaystyle\P({\mathcal{B}}^{c}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\P(|{\mathcal{X}}^{+}|+\mathrm{Binom}(|{\mathcal{X}}^{-}|,\delta)\geq\sqrt{n}+\delta n+n^{3/4})

\displaystyle\leq\P\left(\{|{\mathcal{X}}^{+}|\geq\sqrt{n}\}\cup\{\mathrm{Binom}(|{\mathcal{X}}^{-}|,\delta)\geq\delta n+n^{3/4}\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\P(|{\mathcal{X}}^{+}|\geq\sqrt{n})+\P(\mathrm{Binom}(|{\mathcal{X}}^{-}|,\delta)\geq\delta n+n^{3/4})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log n}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{3}\right),

which implies that
\P({\mathcal{B}}^{c}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{c})={\mathcal{O}}(\exp(-n^{c_{2}}))
for any constant
c_{2}<1/2
.
∎
