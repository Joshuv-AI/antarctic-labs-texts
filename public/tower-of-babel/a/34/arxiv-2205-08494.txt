Covariance Estimation: Optimal Dimension-free Guarantees for Adversarial Corruption and Heavy Tails
Pedro Abdalla, Nikita Zhivotovskiy

Abstract
We provide an estimator of the covariance matrix that achieves the optimal rate of convergence (up to constant factors) in the operator norm under two standard notions of data contamination: We allow the adversary to corrupt an η \eta -fraction of the sample arbitrarily, while the distribution of the remaining data points only satisfies that the L p L_{p} -marginal moment with some p ⩾ 4 p\geqslant 4 is equivalent to the corresponding L 2 L_{2} -marginal moment. Despite requiring the existence of only a few moments of the distribution, our estimator achieves the same tail estimates as if the underlying distribution were Gaussian. As a part of our analysis, we prove a non-asymptotic, dimension-free Bai-Yin type theorem in the regime p > 4 p>4 .

Introduction

Estimation of the covariance matrix is a classic topic. In high-dimensional statistics the role of sample covariance matrices is central to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and to linear least squares. Most of the existing work focuses on estimation of covariance matrices under different structural assumptions allowing minimax estimation in the high-dimensional setup. We refer to the line of work

A line of research on non-asymptotic guarantees for sample covariance matrices was initiated by a question of Kannan, Lovász and Simonovits
[
35
]
on the computation of the volume of a convex body. For the class of log-concave measures the optimal
\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}
(

[

[

[

who proved the optimal rate of convergence
\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}
for the sample covariance matrix assuming only the existence of

moments.

However, as discussed by Chen, Gao and Ren
[

, if

On the other hand, there is a growing interest in getting dimension-free guarantees for estimating the covariance matrix. Given a covariance matrix
\Sigma

\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)=\frac{\tr(\Sigma)}{\|\Sigma\|},

[

proved the optimal high probability bound for the sample covariance matrix in the Gaussian case that depends on the effective rank rather than the dimension. Since then, their result was recovered and extended multiple times and via different techniques
[

,

,

.

Finally, we mention the recent interest in getting the so-called
sub-Gaussian estimators
when the data is heavy-tailed. This direction was initiated by O. Catoni in
[
13
]
, where the sub-Gaussian estimation of the mean of a random variable is considered. To explain informally, one
aims to construct statistical estimators performing as good as the sample mean does for the Gaussian distribution, while making as weak assumptions on the distribution as possible. For a recent survey with focus on multivariate mean estimation, we refer to
[

Our goal is to provide an estimator that simultaneously achieves all the properties described above:

•

We allow the adversarial contamination and recover the optimal dependence on the contamination level based on the number of moments of the underlying distribution.

•

Our bounds do not contain unnecessary logarithmic factors and the convergence rates coincide with the classical asymptotic result of Bai and Yin
[
4
]
provided that there are at least four moments of the distribution.

•

The convergence rates scale with the effective rank
\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
rather than the dimension
d
.

•

We allow the distributions satisfying certain weak norm equivalence assumptions instead of more restrictive Gaussian/log-concave assumptions appearing in the literature. At the same time, we provide the same high probability bounds as if the data were Gaussian.

Theorem 1

(Simplified)

Assume that
X
is a zero mean random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with covariance
\Sigma

norm equivalence

\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}-\Sigma\right\|\leqslant C\|\Sigma\|\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\kappa(p)^{2}\eta^{1-2/p}\right),

\kappa(4)
and

\sqrt{\eta}

[
20

1

and
\eta

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}x,\quad&\textrm{for}\;x\in[-1,1],\\
\operatorname{sign}(x),\quad&\textrm{for}\;|x|>1.\end{cases}

(2)

Then, when

\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{\lambda N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2})-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\Sigma}v\right|,

where

5

One of the technical results used in this paper is a dimension-free version of the classical Bai-Yin theorem
[
4
]
. In a nutshell, the result of Bai and Yin implies that if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are independent copies of a zero mean random vector

\mathbb{R}^{d}
with unit covariance and independent identically distributed coordinates having four bounded moments, that is
p=4
, then

\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\otimes X_{i}-I_{d}\right\|\to 2\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}+\frac{d}{N}

almost surely as

so that

. Moreover, if the distribution only has
p<4
moments, no convergence at such a rate is possible. Our result is non-asymptotic and is somewhat stronger, since we do not require that the coordinates of
X_{i}
are independent. Moreover, our focus is on the dimension-free bound. At the same time, we require that
p>4
and the constant in the bound depends on how well

is separated from zero.

Theorem 2

(A non-asymptotic, dimension-free Bai-Yin type theorem)
.

Assume that
Y
is a zero mean random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying
L_{p}-L_{2}

norm equivalence
with
p>4
. Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
be a sample of independent copies of
Y
. Consider the truncated vectors
X_{i}=Y_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\|Y_{i}\|\leqslant(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4}\}
for

N\geqslant c(p)\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
, then it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left(X_{i}\otimes X_{i}-{\mathbb{E}}X_{i}\otimes X_{i}\right)\right\|\leqslant C(p)\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}},

where
c(\cdot)
and
C(\cdot)
are non-increasing and both satisfy
C(p),c(p)\to\infty
as
p\to 4
.

A natural attempt to prove this bound would be to apply the matrix Bernstein inequality (see the survey

On the breakdown point of our estimator.

To explain informally, the breakdown point of an estimator is defined as the largest proportion of outliers in
the data for which the estimator gives a non-vacuous result
[
30
]
. The form of the bounds we are interested in is a high probability bound of the form
\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|\leqslant\varepsilon\|\Sigma\|
, where
\widehat{\Sigma}
is our estimator and
\varepsilon>0
is precision parameter. Since
\widehat{\Sigma}=0
satisfies this inequality with
\varepsilon=1
, in Theorem

we are focusing on

, where

is a small enough constant so that for large enough sample size we guarantee

.

Practical considerations.

Our main focus is on the statistical properties of the estimator. In fact, we are aiming to achieve the best possible statistical performance and we left computational questions aside. While there is some algorithmic progress in the case of adversarial contamination (see the survey

contains the vertices of the same color. Following the proof of the dimension-dependent result, we provide a dimension-free bound on the chromatic number of this random graph.

Proposition 1
.

Let
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a zero mean random vector with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying the
L_{p}-L_{2}
norm equivalence for
p>4
with
\kappa(\cdot)
as in (
1
). Define, for an arbitrary positive
R
,
X=Y\mathbbm{1}\{\|Y\|\leqslant R\}
and let
\kappa=\kappa(4)
. Then, for any
H>0
and any integer
m>1
, with probability at least
1-(\kappa(p)^{p}\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}NH^{-p})^{m-1}N\kappa(p)^{4}\tr(\Sigma)^{2}H^{-4}
,

\chi(\mathcal{G}_{H})\leqslant m.

Proof.

We consider a greedy coloring process. Let
Y(1),Y(2),\ldots
be an auxiliary random process such that
Y(1)=1
and

Y(i)=\min\{r\in\mathbb{N}:\forall j<i\ \text{with}\ Y(j)=r\ \text{we have}\ |\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle|\leqslant H\|X_{j}\|\}.

Note that, by definition,
\chi(\mathcal{G}_{H})\leqslant\max_{i\in[N]}Y(i)
. Next, for each
i>1
and
m\geqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y(i)=m+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\text{There is}\ l\leqslant i-1\ \text{such that}\ |\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle|>H\|X_{j}\|\ \text{and}\ Y(l)=m)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{l=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{P}(|\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle|>H\|X_{j}\|\ \text{and}\ Y(l)=m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{P}(|\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle|>H\|X_{j}\|\ |\ Y(l)=m)\mathbb{P}(Y(l)=m)

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa(p)^{p}\frac{\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}}{H^{p}}\sum_{l=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{P}(Y(l)=m)

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa(p)^{p}\frac{\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}}{H^{p}}\mathbb{E}|\{j\leqslant N:Y(j)=m\}|,

where we used that since
X_{i}
is independent of
Y(l)
, and by Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle|>H\|X_{j}\|\ |\ Y(l)=m)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(|\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle|>H\|X_{j}\|\ \textrm{and}\ \|X_{j}\|\neq 0\ |\ Y(l)=m)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\kappa(p)^{p}\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}}{H^{p}}.

We obtain the following recursion,

\mathbb{E}|\{j\leqslant N:Y(j)=m+1\}|\leqslant\kappa(p)^{p}\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}N\frac{1}{H^{p}}\mathbb{E}|\{j\leqslant N:Y(j)=m\}|

Now we apply Lemma
1
and use the monotonicity of
\kappa(\cdot)
to obtain,

\mathbb{E}|\{j\leqslant N:Y(j)=2\}|\leqslant N\mathbb{P}(\|X\|>H)\leqslant N\kappa(p)^{4}\tr(\Sigma)^{2}H^{-4}.

Combining the estimates above, we get

\mathbb{E}|\{j\leqslant N:Y(j)=m+1\}|\leqslant(\kappa(p)^{p}\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}NH^{-p})^{m-1}N\kappa(p)^{4}\tr(\Sigma)^{2}H^{-4}.

Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\chi(\mathcal{G}_{H})\geqslant m+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\exists j\leqslant N:\ Y(j)=m+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}|\{j\leqslant N:Y(j)=m+1\}|

\displaystyle\leqslant(\kappa(p)^{p}\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}NH^{-p})^{m-1}N\kappa(p)^{4}\tr(\Sigma)^{2}H^{-4}.

∎

There exists an absolute constant

Lemma 2

Lemma 3

Let

, where

is a large enough absolute constant. Then there exits

We define

\mathcal{C}_{m}^{H}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi}\sum_{m=1}^{\chi}f(s,\mathcal{C}_{m}^{H})

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C\log^{2}\frac{N}{s}\left(H\log s\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|+p\kappa\|\Sigma\|^{1/2}\sqrt{s}\left(\frac{N}{s}\right)^{1/p}\sqrt{f(s,[N])}\right).

We now apply the dimension-free version of the coloring Proposition

\displaystyle(\kappa^{p}\|\Sigma\|^{p/2}NH^{-p})^{\chi-1}N\kappa^{4}\tr(\Sigma)^{2}H^{-4}

\displaystyle\qquad=(N^{1-2p/(p+4)}s^{2p/(p+4)-p/2})^{\chi-1}N^{1-8/(4+p)}s^{8/(4+p)-2}(\log s)^{p(\chi-1)+4}\mathbf{r}^{2}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\chi^{-1}(\mathcal{G_{H}})f(s,[N])

\displaystyle\leqslant\chi^{-1}(\mathcal{G_{H}})\sum_{m=1}^{\chi(\mathcal{G}_{H})}f(s,\mathcal{C}_{m}^{H})

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|^{2}+C\kappa\|\Sigma\|^{1/2}\left(\frac{N}{s}\right)^{2/(4+p)}\sqrt{s}\log^{2}\frac{N}{s}\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|

\displaystyle\qquad+Cp\kappa\|\Sigma\|\log^{2}\frac{N}{s}\sqrt{s}\left(\frac{N}{s}\right)^{1/p}\sqrt{f(s,[N])},

with probability at least

Theorem 3
.

Assume that for some large enough absolute constant
c>0
it holds that
N\geqslant c\mathbf{r(\Sigma)}
.
For large enough sample size
N
, simultaneously for all integers
s
satisfying
\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)\leqslant s\leqslant N/c
, with probability at least
1-\frac{c(p)}{N}
, it holds that

f(s,[N])\leqslant C(p)\left(\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|^{2}+\|\Sigma\|s\left(\frac{N}{s}\right)^{4/(4+p)}\log^{4}\frac{N}{s}\right),

where
C(p)
and

depend only on
p
and
\kappa(p)
.

Proof.

The proof is based on the application of Lemma
3
.
Let
s\geqslant\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
and fix
\chi=\max(10,\frac{4p}{p-4})
. Consider two cases:

•

If
\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)\leqslant 100
, we have

N^{(4-p)(\chi-2)/(p+4)}s^{(p^{2}(\chi-1)+4p)/(2p+8)}(\log s)^{p(\chi-1)+4}\mathbf{r}^{2}(\Sigma)\leqslant c(p)N^{-2}.

•

Otherwise, if

, then we have

and

. Therefore,

N^{(4-p)(\chi-2)/(p+4)}s^{-(p^{2}(\chi-1)+4p)/(2p+8)+(p(\chi-1)+4)/3}\mathbf{r}^{2}(\Sigma)\leqslant s^{-2}\mathbf{r}^{2}(\Sigma)N^{-2}\leqslant N^{-2}.

Therefore, for a fixed integer

, we have that

is sufficiently large so that, by Lemma
3
and the above calculations, with probability at least

f(s,[N])\leqslant C(p)\left(\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|^{2}+\|\Sigma\|s\left(\frac{N}{s}\right)^{4/(4+p)}\log^{4}\frac{N}{s}\right).

By taking the union bound with respect to the value of

Lemma 4

Assume that

{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{\rho}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}f(X_{i},\theta)-N{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\log({\mathbb{E}}_{X}\exp(f(X,\theta)))-\mathcal{KL}(\rho,\mu)\right)\leqslant 0.

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 4
.

Y
is a zero mean random vector with covariance

with

. Let

{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|\lesssim\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{\lambda N}+\lambda\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2},

\kappa=\kappa(4)

\lambda=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}\|\Sigma\|}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}

{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|\lesssim\kappa^{2}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}.

Proof.

Our aim is to choose the distributions
\mu
and
\rho
in Lemma
4
. Let

\Theta=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}.

We choose
\mu
to be a product of two zero mean multivariate Gaussians with mean zero and covariance

\beta^{-1}I_{d}

{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}(\theta,\nu)=(v,v)

\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{v},\mu)=\beta.

By the first part of Lemma
5
we have,

\displaystyle\psi\left(\lambda\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(\lambda{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\log\left(1+\lambda\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+\lambda^{2}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\min\{1,\lambda^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}/6\}.

(7)

Observe that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}=(\langle X,v\rangle^{2}+\beta^{-1}\|X\|^{2})^{2}\leqslant 2\langle X,v\rangle^{4}+2\beta^{-2}\|X\|^{4},

By the second part of Lemma

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\log\left(1+\lambda\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+\lambda^{2}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right)+8{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\min\{1,\lambda^{2}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}/6\}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\log\left(1+\lambda\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+c\lambda^{2}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right).

{\mathbb{E}}\|X\|^{4}\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\|Y\|^{4}\leqslant\kappa^{4}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\langle Y,v\rangle^{2}\leqslant\|\Sigma\|,

where

.

and Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\log{\mathbb{E}}\left(1+\lambda\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+c\lambda^{2}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+c\lambda^{2}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}\langle X,\nu\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}+2c\lambda^{2}({\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{4}+{\mathbb{E}}\beta^{-2}\|X\|^{4})\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:forthnorm})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}+2c\lambda^{2}\kappa^{4}(({\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2})^{2}+\beta^{-2}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2})\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:normforth})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}+2c\lambda^{2}\kappa^{4}(\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\beta^{-2}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2}).

We plug

f(X,\theta,\nu)=\log\left(1+\lambda\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+c\lambda^{2}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right)

in the second part of Lemma
4
. Choosing
\beta=\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
and dividing both sides by
N
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}\cup\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}f\left(X_{i},\theta,\nu\right)-\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}+4c\lambda^{2}\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2},

(10)

where we added the
0
vector by considering
\mu
as a posterior distribution and observing that
0=\mathcal{KL}(\mu,\mu)\leqslant\beta
. By adding the
0
vector we guarantee that the supremum in (
10
) is always non-negative. Adding the term

\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\min\{1,2\lambda^{2}(\mathbf{r}(\Sigma))^{-2}\|X_{i}\|^{4}/6\}\leqslant\lambda^{2}\frac{\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}}{3},

to the inequality (
10
) and using the derivations in the beginning of the proof, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}\cup\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X,v\rangle^{2})-\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}+\left(4c+\frac{1}{3}\right)\lambda^{2}\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}.

The same argument works for
-\lambda
instead. This leads to

{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{\lambda N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X,v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|\leqslant 2\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{\lambda N}+2\left(4c+\frac{1}{3}\right)\lambda\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}.

Our choice of
\lambda
concludes the proof.
∎

2.3
Combining peaky and spread parts

We conclude the proof of Theorem
2
. Using our decomposition we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}>1\}.

We choose
\lambda=\frac{1}{\kappa(4)^{2}\|\Sigma\|}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}
. By Proposition
4
we have

{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|\lesssim\kappa(4)^{2}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}.

We proceed with the remaining term. Define the random set
I_{v}=\{i\in[N]:\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}>\lambda^{-1}\}
. Let
m=\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}|I_{v}|
. By (
5
) we have

\frac{m}{N\lambda}\leqslant\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}>1\}\leqslant\frac{1}{N}f(m,[N]).

(11)

Observe that for any
p>4
, there is
C(p)>0
such that for all
x\geqslant 1
,

x^{1-4/(4+p)}\log^{4}x\leqslant C(p)\sqrt{x}.

We now want to apply Theorem
3
with the (random) value
m
. First, we assume that
\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)\leqslant m\leqslant N/c
.
In this case, by Theorem
3
, with probability at least
1-\frac{c(p)}{N}
, it holds that

\frac{1}{N}f(m,[N])\leqslant C(p)\left(\frac{1}{N}\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|^{2}+\|\Sigma\|\left(\frac{m}{N}\right)^{1/2}\right).

By (
11
) and since
\|X_{i}\|\leqslant(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4}
we have on the same event

m\leqslant C(p)\lambda\left(\max_{i\leqslant N}\|X_{i}\|^{2}+\sqrt{mN}\|\Sigma\|\right)\leqslant C(p)\frac{1}{\|\Sigma\|}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}\left(\sqrt{mN}\|\Sigma\|+\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|N}\right).

Solving the inequality above with respect to
m
we have on the same event

m\leqslant C(p)\mathbf{r}(\Sigma).

We consider the case where
m
does not satisfy
\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)\leqslant m\leqslant N/c
. If
m<\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
, then we recover the same upper bound as above. If
m>N/c
, then on the event of Theorem
3
for
k=\left\lfloor N/c-1\right\rfloor
by (
11
) and monotonicity we have

\frac{k}{\lambda}\leqslant f(k,[N])\leqslant C(p)\left(\sqrt{N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|}+\|\Sigma\|\left(kN\right)^{1/2}\right).

For our choice of
\lambda
this bound leads to contradiction if
N\geqslant c(p)\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
for large enough
c(p)
.
We are now ready to plug our bound
m\leqslant C(p)\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
in (
11
) to obtain that, with probability at least
1-\frac{c(p)}{N}
,

\frac{1}{N}f(C(p)\mathbf{r}(\Sigma),[N])\leqslant C(p)\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}.

Finally, for any
v\in S^{d-1}
, we have
|\langle X_{i},v\rangle|\leqslant\|X_{i}\|\leqslant(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}>1\}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(p)\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}+\frac{c(p)}{N}\sqrt{N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|}

\displaystyle=(C(p)+c(p))\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}.

The claim of Theorem
2
follows.
∎

3
Proof of Theorem
1
in the regime
p=4

First, we provide a high probability version of Proposition
4
. In the definition of our estimator we assume that both
\|\Sigma\|
and
\tr(\Sigma)
are known, so that our estimator depends on such quantities. In Section
3.1
, we provide optimal guarantees for estimating these parameters. The following result can be seen as a special case of Lemma 5 in
[
73
]
. We provide a detailed sketch of the proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 5
.

Assume that
X
is a zero mean random vector with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying the
L_{4}-L_{2}

norm equivalence
. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be a sample of independent copies of
X
. For a fixed truncation level
\lambda>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|\lesssim\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{\lambda N}+\lambda\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2},

where
\kappa=\kappa(4)
.

Proof.

We repeat the lines of the proof of Proposition
4
. However, instead we plug

f(X,\theta,\nu)=\log\left(1+\lambda\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+c\lambda^{2}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right)

in the first part of Lemma
4
. This implies that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}f\left(X_{i},\theta,\nu\right)-\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{N}+4c\lambda^{2}\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2},

(12)

where
c>0
is the same constant as in the proof of Proposition
4
. Furthermore, by the Bernstein inequality we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\min\{1,\lambda^{2}(\mathbf{r}(\Sigma))^{-2}\|X_{i}\|^{4}/3\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\min\{1,2\lambda^{2}(\mathbf{r}(\Sigma))^{-2}\|X\|^{4}/6\}

\displaystyle\ +\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\min\{1,2\lambda^{2}(\mathbf{r}(\Sigma))^{-2}\|X\|^{4}/6\}}+\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{3N}.

where we used that each summand belongs to the interval
[0,1]
and therefore the variance of each summand is bounded by its expectation. Following the proof of Lemma
1
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}\|X\|^{4}\leqslant\kappa^{4}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2}
. This implies that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\min\{1,2\lambda^{2}(\mathbf{r}(\Sigma))^{-2}\|X_{i}\|^{4}/6\}\leqslant\frac{2\lambda^{2}\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}}{3}+\frac{3\log(1/\delta)}{N}.

(13)

Using this line and the union bound together with (
12
) and the derivations in the proof of Proposition
4
, we obtain the one-sided version of our claim. Repeating the same lines by replacing
\lambda
by
-\lambda
and using the union bound again, we finish the proof.
∎

Our next result concludes the proof of Theorem 1 when
p=4
. Recall that
\kappa
denotes
\kappa(4)
.

Theorem 4
.

There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that the following holds.
Assume that
X
is a zero mean random vector with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying the
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence. Fix the corruption level
\eta\in[0,1]
and the confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
. Assume that
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N}
is an
\eta
-corrupted sample. Then there exists an estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}-\Sigma\right\|\leqslant C\kappa^{2}\|\Sigma\|\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\sqrt{\eta}\right).

We begin with presenting the estimator. As we mentioned, we assume that
\eta,\|\Sigma\|,\tr(\Sigma)
and the constant
\kappa=\kappa(4)
are known. In Section
3.1
, we discuss how to estimate
\|\Sigma\|
and
\tr(\Sigma)
up to multiplicative constant factors. The dependence on
\kappa
in our estimator can also be ignored, though in this case we obtain a slightly weaker dependence on this parameter in the final bound.

The estimator in Theorem
4

1.

Given
\delta,\tr(\Sigma),\|\Sigma\|,\kappa
, and an
\eta
-corrupted sample
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N}
we set

\lambda=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}\|\Sigma\|}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)+\eta N}{N}}.

2.

Define

\Gamma=\bigcap\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left\{A\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}:\left|\frac{1}{\lambda N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)-v^{\mathsf{T}}Av\right|\leqslant C\lambda\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}/2\right\}.

3.

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
be any matrix in the set
\Gamma
. If the set is empty, we output
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}=0
.

Proof.

Since the truncation function
\psi(\cdot)
is bounded, we have

\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|\leqslant\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right|+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}.

Combining this with Proposition
5
we have for some
C>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2})-v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v\right|\leqslant\frac{C}{4}\left(\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)+\eta N}{\lambda N}+\lambda\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}\right).

Using the triangle inequality, the definition of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
and the line above, we have on the same event

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}-\Sigma\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2})-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}v\right|+\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2})-v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\kappa^{2}\|\Sigma\|\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\sqrt{\eta}\right).

Our choice of
\lambda
concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3
.

It is straightforward to verify that the matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
defined by (
3
) satisfies the assumption of Theorem
4
and can be used as an estimator of the covariance matrix.

Finally, we show how to estimate the value of the truncation parameter
\lambda
using the corrupted observations.

3.1
Estimating the truncation level
\lambda
in the regime
p=4

We begin this section with a brief discussion of our contamination model. To simplify the technical aspects of our analysis, we impose a slight restriction on the strong contamination model described in the introduction. In our analysis, we require the estimation of multiple parameters, which can be accomplished by dividing the sample into several equal parts and estimating these parameters using independent sub-samples. This approach is standard, and has been analyzed, for example, in
[
54
]
in the same context. However, in the strong contamination model, the adversary has the ability to replace some observations within each sub-sample in a manner that depends on the entire sample. This can result in dependent sub-samples and a more complicated stability type analysis appearing in the literature (see
[
23
, Section 6]
). We therefore restrict the adversary from making the sub-samples dependent on each other. We note that this assumption is standard and has been made (implicitly) in some recent works
[
48
,
58
]
. Furthermore, it is automatically satisfied in standard contamination models, including the Huber model.

An important aspect of the proof of Theorem
4
is that we only need to know
\|\Sigma\|
and
\tr(\Sigma)
up to multiplicative constant factors. That is, we want to find two estimators
\widehat{\varphi}_{1}
and
\widehat{\varphi}_{2}
such that for some
c>1
, with high probability with respect to the realization of the corrupted sample
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N}
,

\frac{1}{c}\tr(\Sigma)\leqslant\widehat{\varphi}_{1}\leqslant c\tr(\Sigma)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{c}\|\Sigma\|\leqslant\widehat{\varphi}_{2}\leqslant c\|\Sigma\|.

If these estimators are available, we can assume, without the loss of generality, that our initial sample is of size
3N
. We use the first
2N
elements to estimate
\tr(\Sigma)
and
\|\Sigma\|
and then plug them into the truncation level
\lambda
in Theorem
4
. A similar sample splitting strategy is used in
[
54
]
. Our contribution is that we provide estimators
\widehat{\varphi}_{1}
and
\widehat{\varphi}_{2}
such that they take the adversarial corruption into account as well as do not contain an unnecessary logarithmic factor. For the rest of the section, we fix
\kappa=\kappa(4)
, where
\kappa(\cdot)
is defined in (
1
).

3.2
Estimating
\tr(\Sigma)

One can estimate
\tr(\Sigma)
using, for example, the trimmed mean estimator analyzed in
[
48
]
. Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
denote the canonical basis in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Observe that

\tr(\Sigma)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,e_{i}\rangle^{2}.

Using the
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence and the proof of Lemma
1
we have
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}\langle X,e_{i}\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant\kappa^{4}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2}
. Given an
\eta
-corrupted sample of size
2N
, the trimmed mean estimator
\widehat{\varphi}_{1}
applied to the random variable
\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}\langle X,e_{i}\rangle^{2}
implies that, for any
\delta\in(\exp(-N)/4,1)
, with probability at least
1-4\exp(-\varepsilon N/12)
,

|\widehat{\varphi}_{1}-\tr(\Sigma)|\leqslant 10\sqrt{\varepsilon}\kappa^{2}\tr(\Sigma),

where
\varepsilon=8\eta+12\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{N}
.
This bound is presented in
[
48
, Theorem 1]
.
For the sake of brevity, we do not provide the details of the multivariate trimmed mean estimator. Observe that for this choice of
\varepsilon
, it holds that
1-4\exp(-\varepsilon N/12)\geqslant 1-\delta
. Provided that
10\sqrt{\varepsilon}\kappa^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
we obtain that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma)\leqslant\widehat{\varphi}_{1}\leqslant 2\tr(\Sigma).

(14)

It is only left to check that it is sufficient to have
\eta\in\left[0,\frac{1}{40^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right]
and
N\geqslant 12\cdot 40^{2}\kappa^{4}\log(4/\delta)
.

3.3
Estimating
\|\Sigma\|

A simply way of estimating
\|\Sigma\|
is to first estimate the full covariance matrix
\Sigma
as in Theorem
4
and then compute the operator norm of the estimator.
However, the difficulty of estimating
\|\Sigma\|
is that we are not allowed to choose the truncation level
\lambda
depending on
\|\Sigma\|
. In particular, all previous results we are aware of lead to an additional logarithmic factor in the assumption on the sample size (see
[
54
,
15
]
). Instead we provide an adaptive estimator similar in spirit to the original robust estimator of O. Catoni
[
13
]
for estimating the mean of a random variable (see also
[
14
]
for related computations). To simplify the proof, we assume that the distribution of
X
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(X=0)=0
. This assumption is mild and can always be satisfied if we add a small Gaussian perturbation to
X
without changing the covariance matrix of
X
too much.

Proposition 6
.

Assume that
X
is a zero mean random vector with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying the
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence. Assume additionally that
\mathbb{P}(X=0)=0
. Let
c\geqslant 1
be a large enough absolute constant. Fix the corruption level
\eta\in[0,\frac{1}{300c\kappa^{4}}]
and the confidence level
\delta\in(0,1/4)
. Assume that
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N}
is an
\eta
-corrupted sample. Then there is a unique value
\widehat{\alpha}>0
satisfying

\frac{1}{N}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{20c\kappa^{4}}+\eta.

If
N\geqslant 100c\kappa^{4}\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+400c\kappa^{4}\log(1/\delta)
, then with probability at least
1-4\delta
, it holds that

\frac{1}{4}\|\Sigma\|\leqslant\frac{1}{24c\kappa^{4}\widehat{\alpha}^{2}}\leqslant 4\|\Sigma\|.

Proof.

We begin with the analysis of an uncorrupted sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
.
Our first aim is to choose the distributions
\mu
and
\rho
in Lemma
4
. Let

\Theta=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}

and choose
\mu
to be a product of two zero mean multivariate Gaussians with mean zero and covariance
\beta^{-1}I_{d}
. For
v\in S^{d-1}
and
\alpha>0
let
\rho_{\alpha,v}
be a product of two multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
\alpha v
and covariance
\beta^{-1}I_{d}
. Because of this, if
(\theta,\nu)
is distributed according to
\rho_{\alpha,v}
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{v}}(\theta,\nu)=(\alpha v,\alpha v)
. By the additivity of
\mathcal{KL}
-divergence for product measures and the standard formula, we have

\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{\alpha,v},\mu)=\alpha^{2}\beta.

For the rest of the proof we sometimes write
\rho
instead of
\rho_{\alpha,v}
. By the first part of Lemma
5
, we have

\displaystyle\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\psi\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\log\left(1+\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right)+\min\{1,{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}/6\}.

Observe that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}=(\alpha^{2}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}+\beta^{-1}\|X\|^{2})^{2}\leqslant 2\alpha^{4}\langle X,v\rangle^{4}+2\beta^{-2}\|X\|^{4},

and we can write

\min\{1,{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}/6\}\leqslant\min\{1,2\alpha^{4}\langle X,v\rangle^{4}/6\}+\min\{1,2\beta^{-2}\|X\|^{4}/6\}.

Repeating the lines of the proof of Proposition
4
, we get

{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\log{\mathbb{E}}\left(1+\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle+c\left(\langle X,\theta\rangle\langle X,\nu\rangle\right)^{2}\right)\leqslant\alpha^{2}v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v+2c\kappa^{4}(\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\beta^{-2}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2}),

where
c\geqslant 1
is an absolute constant. Using the first part of Lemma
4
we obtain that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, simultaneously for all
v\in S^{d-1}
and
\alpha>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha^{2}v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v+2c\kappa^{4}(\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\beta^{-2}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha^{2}\beta+\log(1/\delta)}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\min\{1,2\beta^{-2}\|X_{i}\|^{4}/6\}.

Using the Bernstein inequality as in (
13
) we have, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\min\{1,2\beta^{-2}\|X_{i}\|^{4}/6\}\leqslant\frac{2\kappa^{4}\beta^{-2}(\tr(\Sigma))^{2}}{3}+\frac{3\log(1/\delta)}{N}.

We choose
\beta=10c\kappa^{4}\tr(\Sigma)
and using the union bound, we obtain that, with probability at least
1-2\delta
, simultaneously for all
v\in S^{d-1}
and
\alpha>0
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant\alpha^{2}v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v+2c\kappa^{4}\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\frac{2}{75c\kappa^{4}}+\frac{10c\kappa^{4}\alpha^{2}\tr(\Sigma)}{N}+\frac{4\log(1/\delta)}{N}.

Since
N\geqslant 100c\kappa^{4}\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+400c\kappa^{4}\log(1/\delta)
, we have on the same event

\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant\alpha^{2}v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v+\frac{1}{10}\alpha^{2}\|\Sigma\|+2c\kappa^{4}\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\frac{11}{300c\kappa^{4}}.

Returning to the
\eta
-corrupted sample
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N}
and since
|\psi(\cdot)|\leqslant 1
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant\alpha^{2}v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v+\frac{1}{10}\alpha^{2}\|\Sigma\|+2c\kappa^{4}\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\frac{11}{300c\kappa^{4}}+\eta.

(15)

Observe that the function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

\varphi(\alpha)=\frac{1}{N}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)

is continuous (it is easy to check that
\varphi(\cdot)
is a supremum over equi-Lipschitz functions) and non-decreasing. Furthermore, there is
w\in S^{d-1}
such that
\langle X_{i},w\rangle\neq 0
for all
i\in[N]
. Indeed, to construct such a vector we pick a random vector
U
distributed as a zero mean multivariate Gaussian random vector with unit covariance. Since
X_{i}\neq 0
almost surely for all
i\in[N]
we have that
\langle U,X_{i}\rangle
is a zero mean Gaussian (conditionally on
X_{i}
) with non-zero covariance. Thus, taking
w=U/\|U\|
we have almost surely
\langle X_{i},w\rangle\neq 0
for all
i\in[N]
.
We have
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi\left(\frac{1}{\min_{i\in[N]}|\langle X_{i},w\rangle|}\right)\geqslant 1-\eta
since at most
\eta N
vectors
\widetilde{X}_{i}
can be orthogonal to
w
.
Therefore, since
\kappa,c\geqslant 1
and
\eta\leqslant\frac{1}{300c\kappa^{4}}
there is
\widehat{\alpha}>0
such that

\varphi(\widehat{\alpha})=\frac{1}{20c\kappa^{4}}+\eta.

Using (
15
), we get on the same event

2c\kappa^{4}\widehat{\alpha}^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\frac{11}{10}\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\|\Sigma\|-\frac{1}{75c\kappa^{4}}\geqslant 0.

Solving this as a quadratic equation (only the positive root plays a role) with respect to
\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\|\Sigma\|
we get

\frac{-11/10+\sqrt{(11/10)^{2}+8/75}}{4c\kappa^{4}\widehat{\alpha}^{2}}\leqslant\|\Sigma\|.

This provides a lower bound on
\|\Sigma\|
in terms of
\widehat{\alpha}^{2}
.

We proceed with an upper bound. For
v\in S^{d-1}
and
\alpha>0
let
\rho_{\alpha,-\alpha,v}
be a product of two multivariate Gaussian distributions with means
\alpha v
and
-\alpha v
respectively and the same covariance matrix
\beta^{-1}I_{d}
. Repeating the proof, we obtain the following analog of inequality (
15
). With probability at least
1-2\delta
, for all
v\in S^{d-1}
and
\alpha>0
,

-\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant-\alpha^{2}v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v+\frac{1}{10}\alpha^{2}\|\Sigma\|+2c\kappa^{4}\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\frac{11}{300c\kappa^{4}}+\eta.

(16)

Let
v_{1}
be a maximizer of
v^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v
in
S^{d-1}
. We have
v_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma v_{1}=\|\Sigma\|
. Furthermore, we have

-\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)\leqslant-\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\alpha^{2}\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v_{1}\rangle^{2}\right).

Since
-\varphi(\cdot)
is non-increasing, this implies that simultaneously for all
\alpha\in[0,\widehat{\alpha}]
on the event where (
16
) holds, we have

-\frac{1}{20c\kappa^{4}}-\eta\leqslant-\frac{9}{10}\alpha^{2}\|\Sigma\|+2c\kappa^{4}\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\frac{11}{300c\kappa^{4}}+\eta.

Since
\eta\leqslant\frac{1}{300c\kappa^{4}}
we reduce this equation to

2c\kappa^{4}\alpha^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}-\frac{9}{10}\alpha^{2}\|\Sigma\|+\frac{7}{75c\kappa^{4}}\geqslant 0.

(17)

As a function of
\alpha^{2}\|\Sigma\|
this quadratic equation has two non-negative roots:

x_{1}=\frac{9/10-\sqrt{(9/10)^{2}-56/75}}{4c\kappa^{4}}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{2}=\frac{9/10+\sqrt{(9/10)^{2}-56/75}}{4c\kappa^{4}}.

We show that since (
17
) holds for all
\alpha\in[0,\widehat{\alpha}]
, we should have that
\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\|\Sigma\|\leqslant x_{1}
. Indeed, assume that instead
\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\|\Sigma\|\geqslant x_{2}
. In this case
0\leqslant(x_{1}+x_{2})/2\leqslant\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\|\Sigma\|
and thus (
17
) should be satisfied for
\alpha^{2}\|\Sigma\|=(x_{1}+x_{2})/2
. The obtained contradiction proves that
\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\|\Sigma\|\leqslant x_{1}
. Finally, by union bound we have, with probability at least
1-4\delta
,

\frac{-11/10+\sqrt{(11/10)^{2}+8/75}}{4c\kappa^{4}\widehat{\alpha}^{2}}\leqslant\|\Sigma\|\leqslant\frac{9/10-\sqrt{(9/10)^{2}-56/75}}{4c\kappa^{4}\widehat{\alpha}^{2}}.

Algebraic computations conclude the proof.
∎

We end up by observing that the estimator proposed in this section admits a straightforward generalization for (uncontaminated) higher order tensors. In this setup one is aiming to estimate
{\mathbb{E}}\langle X,v\rangle^{s}
uniformly over
v\in S^{d-1}
for some integer
s\geqslant 2
, as in the recent work of Mendelson
[
51
]
. In order to provide this extension we apply
[
73
, Lemma 5]
together with the estimators of the trace and the operator norm provided above. We note that getting the optimal dependence on the contamination level
\eta
seems more subtle when
s>2
.

4
Proof of Theorem
1
in the regime
p>4

In this section, we prove Theorem
1
in the regime
p>4
. We begin with the following symmetrized version of Theorem
2
.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that
Y
is a zero mean random vector with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying the
L_{p}-L_{2}

norm equivalence
with
p>4
. Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
be a sample of independent copies of
Y
. Consider the truncated vectors
X_{i}=Y_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\|Y_{i}\|\leqslant(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4}\}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. Let
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N}
be independent Rademacher random signs. If
N\geqslant c(p)\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
, then it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}\right|\leqslant C(p)\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}},

where
c(p)
and
C(p)
depend only on
p
and
\kappa(p)
and the expectation is taken with respect to both
X_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
.

Remark 4
.

We highlight that the result of Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
admits extensions to higher order power in the spirit of
[
69
]
. For the related derivations in the light-tailed case, see the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
73
]
.

Proof.

We use the standard desymmetrization argument
[
38
, Section 2.1]
. Let
X^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{N}
be the independent copies of
X
and let
{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}
denote the expectation with respect to these random vectors. Using the triangle inequality, Jensen’s inequality and the contraction principle
[
42
, Theorem 4.4]
we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}\left\langle X^{\prime}_{i},v\right\rangle^{2})\right|+{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}-\left\langle X^{\prime}_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}\right|+\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{N}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2{\mathbb{E}}\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle^{2}\right|+\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{N}.

The bound of Theorem
2
concludes the proof.
∎

In the regime
p>4
, we show how to adapt the proposed estimator to obtain the optimal dependence on the contamination level
\eta
. The main insight is to interpret our estimator as a second order version of the trimmed mean estimator of Lugosi and Mendelson
[
48
]
. Our proof mainly follows their steps, however two important modifications are required: First, we need to exploit Theorem
2
when controlling the expected supremum of quadratic processes, while in
[
48
]
the control of the corresponding process follows from a simple application of the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. To do so, we also need to truncate the norms of our observations as explained after Proposition
7
. Second, since the quadratic forms of interest are non-negative, we consider only a one-sided truncation. The main theorem of this section is the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
X
is a zero mean random vector with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying the
L_{p}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with
p>4
. Fix the corruption level
\eta\in[0,1]
and the confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then there exists an estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}-\Sigma\right\|\leqslant C(p)\|\Sigma\|\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+\kappa(p)^{2}\eta^{1-2/p}\right).\

Here
C(p)
is a non-increasing function of
p
that satisfies
C(p)\to\infty
as
p\to 4
.

Let us first recall the form of our estimator in the case where
p=4
. For some specifically chosen
\lambda>0
, the estimator in Theorem
4
is defined by the following set

\Gamma=\bigcap\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left\{A\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}:\left|\frac{1}{\lambda N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)-v^{\mathsf{T}}Av\right|\leqslant C\lambda\kappa^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}/2\right\},

where
\psi(\cdot)
is the truncation function given by (
2
).
We defined
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
to be any matrix in
\Gamma
.
In the regime
p>4
, we introduce a more involved estimator that uses a direction dependent value of the one-sided threshold. For simplicity, we assume that we observe a sample of size
2N
. For a set of positive semi-definite matrices
\Gamma
, we define its diameter as
\Delta(\Gamma)=\sup_{A,B\in\Gamma}\|A-B\|
.

The estimator in Theorem
5

1.

Given the confidence level
\delta
, the corruption level
\eta
and the
\eta
-corrupted sample
\widetilde{Y}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{Y}_{2N}
, we split the sample in two parts of equal size: Truncate each vector
\widetilde{Z}_{i}=\widetilde{Y}_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\|\widetilde{Y}_{i}\|\leqslant R\}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
\widetilde{X}_{i}=\widetilde{Y}_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\|\widetilde{Y}_{i}\|\leqslant R\}
for
i\in\{N+1,\ldots,2N\}
, where
R=(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4}
.

2.

Set
\varepsilon=\max\left(20\eta,560\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{N}\right)
.

3.

For the first half of the sample, define
q_{v}=(\langle\widetilde{Z}_{i},v\rangle^{2})_{N\varepsilon/2}^{\ast}
.

4.

For the second half of the sample
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N}
, we proceed as follows: For every
Q>0,v\in S^{d-1}
, define the trimming level
\lambda_{v}(Q)=(q_{v}+Q)^{-1}
and set

\Gamma(Q)=\bigcap\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left\{A\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}:\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}(Q)\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)-v^{\mathsf{T}}Av\right|\leqslant 4\varepsilon Q\right\}.

5.

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
be any matrix in the set
\Gamma(2^{i^{\ast}})
, where
i^{\ast}
minimizes the diameter
\Delta(\Gamma(2^{i}))
over all integers
i
subject to be non-empty.

Remark 5
.

Due to the bounds in Section
3.1
, we explicitly assume the knowledge of the operator norm
\|\Sigma\|
and the effective rank
\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)
up to multiplicative constant factors.

Our first technical observation is that, by truncating the norms of the vectors at the level
R=(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4}
, the covariance matrix does not change too much. At the same time, the truncation allows us to apply Theorem
2
in our analysis.

Proposition 7
.

Assume that
Y
is a zero mean random vector with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying the
L_{p}-L_{2}

norm equivalence
with
p\geqslant 4
. Consider the truncated vector
X=Y\mathbbm{1}\{\|Y\|\leqslant(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4}\}
and define
\widetilde{\Sigma}={\mathbb{E}}X\otimes X
. It holds that

\|\Sigma-\widetilde{\Sigma}\|\leqslant\kappa(4)^{4}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}.

Moreover,
\|\widetilde{\Sigma}\|\leqslant\|\Sigma\|
and
\tr(\widetilde{\Sigma})\leqslant\tr(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the proof Lemma 2.1 in
[
54
]
. For the second part of the proof we observe that
\Sigma-\widetilde{\Sigma}
is positive semi-definite.
∎

The result of Proposition
7
allows us to focus on estimating the matrix of second moments
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. Indeed, by the triangle inequality, the estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}
satisfies

\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}-\Sigma\|\leqslant\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta,\delta}-\widetilde{\Sigma}\|+\kappa(4)^{4}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}},

and the last term only affects the multiplicative constant factors in our bound. We proceed by defining the following quantity

Q_{0}=C_{Q_{0}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)+\log(2/\delta)}{N}},\varepsilon^{-2/p}\kappa(p)^{2}\|\Sigma\|\right\}.

Here
C_{Q_{0}}=C_{Q_{0}}(p)>0
is to be chosen later, depends only on
p
and is non-increasing with respect to
p
. The first term in the definition above is responsible for the rate in the uncontaminated case and the second term captures the rate of
\eta
according to the value of
p
. We start with an important lemma that is the second order analog of
[
48
, Lemma 1]
. For the rest of this section,
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is the matrix of second moments of the truncated vector
X=Y\mathbbm{1}\{\|Y\|\leqslant(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4})\}
and
\varepsilon=\max\left(20\eta,560\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{N}\right)
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a mean zero random vector satisfying the
L_{p}-L_{2}
norm equivalence assumption with
p>4
. Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
be i.i.d. copies of
Y
and set
Z_{i}=Y_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\|Y_{i}\|\leqslant(N\tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|)^{1/4})\}
for every
i\in[N]
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\left\{i\in[N]:\langle Z_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\geqslant Q_{0}\right\}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}N.

Proof.

For simplicity of notation, we write
\overline{Z}_{i}(v)=\langle Z_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v
. The proof is a standard application of a small ball argument in empirical process theory. Consider a function
\xi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\xi(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\leqslant Q_{0}/2,\\
\frac{2x}{Q_{0}}-1,&x\in(Q_{0}/2,Q_{0}],\\
1,&x>Q_{0}.\end{cases}

It is clear that
\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{Z}_{i}(v)\geqslant Q_{0}\}\leqslant\xi(\overline{Z}_{i}(v))\leqslant\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{Z}_{i}(v)\geqslant Q_{0}/2\}
and
\xi
is a Lipschitz function with constant
2/Q_{0}
. By symmetrization
[
26
]
and the contraction lemma for Rademacher processes
[
42
, Theorem 4.4]
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{Z}_{i}(v)\geqslant Q_{0}\}&\leqslant\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi(\overline{Z}_{i}(v))\\
&\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\varepsilon_{i}\xi(\overline{Z}_{i}(v))\right|+\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\xi(\overline{Z}_{i}(v))\\
&\leqslant\frac{4}{Q_{0}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\overline{Z}_{i}(v)\right|+\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\xi(\overline{Z}(v)).\end{split}

Now, we define
C_{BY}=C_{BY}(p)>0
to be the constant in the conclusion of Corollary
1
. We remark that
C_{BY}
is at most an absolute constant for any
p\geqslant c>4
, where
c
is another absolute constant. We now apply Corollary
1
and the same arguments as in its proof and obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\varepsilon_{i}(\langle Z_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v)\right|&\leqslant\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\langle Z_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right|+\frac{\|\Sigma\|}{N}\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\right|\\
&\leqslant 2C_{BY}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}\leqslant\frac{Q_{0}\varepsilon}{128}.\end{split}

The last steps follow from the fact that
\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)\geqslant 1
and
\|\widetilde{\Sigma}\|\leqslant\|\Sigma\|
. By the definition of
Q_{0}
we conclude that the first term is at most
\frac{\varepsilon}{32}
if we choose a sufficiently large constant
C_{Q_{0}}>0
. We now proceed to bound the second term. We use Markov’s inequality together with the
L_{p}-L_{2}
norm equivalence to obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\xi\left(\langle Z,v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\langle Z,v\rangle^{2}\geqslant Q/2+v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\right)&\leqslant\frac{\kappa(p)^{p}}{\left(Q/2+v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\right)^{p/2}}(\mathbb{E}\langle Z,v\rangle^{2})^{p/2}\\
&\leqslant\left(\frac{2\kappa(p)^{2}\|\Sigma\|}{Q}\right)^{p/2}.\end{split}

Again, by the definition of
Q_{0}
, we can choose
C_{Q_{0}}>0
such that the right hand side above is at most
\frac{\varepsilon}{32}
. By Talagrand’s concentration inequality for supremum of empirical process (Massart’s version
[
49
]
), with probability at least
1-\exp(-x)
,

\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{Z}_{i}(v)\geqslant Q\}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{8}+\sqrt{\frac{8x}{N}}\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{8}+\frac{35x}{N}.

We choose
x=\varepsilon N/560
to conclude the proof.
∎

We now describe the first part of the estimation procedure. First, we assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon<1
. When the conclusion of the previous lemma holds, we immediately obtain that, for every
v\in S^{d-1}
and
Q\in(2Q_{0},4Q_{0})
,

\quad q_{v}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v+Q\leqslant 5Q_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{v}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v+Q\geqslant Q_{0}.

To see why the latter condition holds, suppose by contradiction that
q_{v}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v+Q\leqslant Q_{0}
, then
q_{v}\leqslant v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v-Q_{0}
. Since
\varepsilon<1
, by the choice of
Q_{0}
, we have
Q_{0}>\|\Sigma\|
and then we get
0\leqslant q_{v}\leqslant v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v-Q_{0}<0
, the latter is clearly a contradiction. Moreover, both inequalities above still hold for an
\eta
-corrupted sample, since
\varepsilon\geqslant 20\eta
and by the definition of
\varepsilon
, we have that the fraction of points greater than
Q_{0}
is at most
\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\eta\leqslant\varepsilon(\frac{1}{20}+\frac{1}{4})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Thus, we can use
\widetilde{Z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{Z}_{N}
to estimate
q_{v}
, and since they are independent from the second half of the sample, from now on we work conditionally on the event of Lemma
6
.

The second part of the analysis consists in proving that
\Gamma(v,Q)
is non-empty. The formal statement is the proposition below.

Proposition 8
.

Under the notation of Theorem
5
, fix
Q\in[2Q_{0},4Q_{0}]
and assume that the event of Lemma
6
holds. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that if
C_{Q_{0}}\geqslant C
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}(Q)\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\right|\leqslant 4\varepsilon Q.

It will be convenient to work with two a sided truncation function in the analysis. We define the following function for arbitrary positive
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}
,

\psi_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}},\quad x>\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}},\\
x,\quad x\in[\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}],\\
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},\quad x<\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}.\end{cases}

Clearly, if
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda
, our definition agrees with (
2
). That is,
\frac{1}{\lambda}\psi(\lambda x)=\psi_{\lambda,\lambda}(x)
.

Proof.

First, for every
v\in S^{d-1}
, due to the assumption that the event of Lemma
6
holds, the uncorrupted observations satisfy

\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}(Q)\langle\widetilde{X}_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}(Q)\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)\right|\leqslant 2\eta Q\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon Q}{10}.

We centralize the empirical process to obtain that

\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}(Q)\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v=\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}^{c}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}^{c}(Q)(\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v)\right),

where
\lambda_{v}^{c}(Q)=(q_{v}+Q-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v)^{-1}
. To see why this identity holds, observe that both sides are equal except when
\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\leqslant-q_{v}-Q+v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v
. In this case,
0\leqslant\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\leqslant-q_{v}-Q+2v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v
. However, since
Q\geqslant 2\|\Sigma\|
if
C_{Q_{0}}\geqslant 2
and
\varepsilon<1
, the latter inequality cannot be satisfied. Now, on the event of Lemma
6
,
Q_{0}\leqslant q_{v}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v+Q\leqslant 5Q_{0}
and therefore

\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}^{c}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}^{c}(Q)(\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v)\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{-\frac{1}{Q_{0}},\frac{1}{5Q_{0}}}\left(\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\right).

We define
\overline{U}_{Q}(v)
to be the right hand side in the inequality above. We first study how the empirical process of interest concentrates around the mean. To simplify the analysis, we consider a simpler quantity,
\overline{W}_{Q}(v)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{\frac{-1}{3Q},\frac{1}{3Q}}\left(\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\right)
. Our starting point is

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}(\overline{U}_{Q}(v)-\mathbb{E}\overline{U}_{Q}(v))

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}(\overline{U}_{Q}(v)-\overline{W}_{Q}(v))+\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}(\overline{W}_{Q}(v)-\mathbb{E}\overline{W}_{Q}(v))

\displaystyle\qquad+\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}(\mathbb{E}\overline{W}_{Q}(v)-\mathbb{E}\overline{U}_{Q}(v))

\displaystyle=(a)+(b)+(c).

For the first term, observe that it is non-zero if and only if the argument of the function is above
5Q_{0}
or below
-Q_{0}
. The latter cannot happen because of the same contradiction argument as above. The magnitude of the difference is at most
3Q
. By Lemma
6
, there are at most
\varepsilon N/4
points in the range
(5Q_{0},\infty)
, therefore, we obtain that the first term (a) is at most
\frac{3\varepsilon Q}{4}
. Similarly, we apply the Markov inequality together with the
L_{p}-L_{2}
norm equivalence assumption to obtain that

\begin{split}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left(\overline{W}_{Q}(v)-\overline{U}_{Q}(v)\right)&\leqslant 3Q\mathbb{P}(\langle X,v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v>5Q_{0})\\
&\leqslant 3Q\left(\frac{\kappa(p)^{2}\|\Sigma\|}{5Q_{0}}\right)^{p/2}\leqslant\frac{3\varepsilon Q}{32}.\end{split}

This concludes the bound for (c). The second term (b) is handled via Talagrand’s concentration inequality for empirical processes (Massart’s version
[
49
]
). Observe that, for every
v\in S^{d-1}
,

\left|\psi_{-\frac{1}{3Q},\frac{1}{3Q}}\left(\langle X,v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v\right)\right|\leqslant 3Q\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left|\psi_{-\frac{1}{3Q},\frac{1}{3Q}}\left(\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\right)\right|^{2}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle^{4}\leqslant\kappa(4)^{4}\|\Sigma\|^{2}.

Moreover, for every
Q>0
,
\psi(\cdot)_{-\frac{1}{Q},\frac{1}{Q}}
is a
1
-Lipschitz function that passes through the origin. By the same symmetrization and contraction arguments as in the proof of Lemma
6
, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\overline{W}_{Q}(v)-\mathbb{E}\overline{W}_{Q}(v)\right|\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right|\leqslant 2C_{BY}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}.

Therefore, by Talagrand’s concentration inequality for empirical processes, with probability at least
1-2e^{-x}
, it holds that

\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}|W_{Q}(v)-\mathbb{E}W_{Q}(v)|\leqslant 4C_{BY}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)}{N}}+\kappa(4)^{2}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{8x}{N}}+105Q\frac{x}{N}.

We choose
x=2\log(2/\delta)
to obtain that the right hand side above is less than
\frac{\varepsilon Q}{2}
. Now, the problem boils down to control uniformly the typical magnitude of
\overline{U}_{Q}(v)
. For simplicity, we define
\overline{X}_{i}(v)=\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v
and write

\begin{split}\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\overline{U}_{Q}(v)&=\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{-\frac{1}{Q_{0}},\frac{1}{5Q_{0}}}(\langle X,v\rangle^{2}-v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v))(\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{X}_{i}(v)\leqslant 5Q_{0}\}+\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{X}_{i}(v)>5Q_{0}\})]\\
&\leqslant\sup_{v\in S^{d-1}}5Q_{0}\mathbb{E}\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{X}(v)\geqslant 5Q_{0}\}\\
&\leqslant 5Q\frac{\varepsilon}{64}.\end{split}

The final bound for the first part becomes
\varepsilon Q(\frac{1}{10}+\frac{3}{4}+\frac{3}{32}+\frac{5}{64}+\frac{1}{2})\leqslant 2\varepsilon Q
. For the other side bound, observe that

\begin{split}v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v-\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(\lambda_{v}(Q)\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2})&=\frac{1}{\lambda_{v}^{c}(Q)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi\left(\lambda_{v}^{c}(Q)v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v-\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right)\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{-\frac{1}{Q_{0}},\frac{1}{5Q_{0}}}\left(v^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\Sigma}v-\langle X_{i},v\rangle^{2}\right).\end{split}

The same analysis follows and concludes the proof.
∎

The proof of Theorem
5
follows directly from Lemma
6
and Proposition
8
. Indeed, consider the event
\mathcal{E}
where the events of both Lemma
6
and Proposition
8
hold. It occurs with probability at least
1-\delta
as desired. Our next argument is standard in the literature
[
48
]
and is similar to Lepskii’s method
[
43
]
. Let
i_{0}
be the smallest integer satisfying
2^{i_{0}}\in(2Q_{0},4Q_{0})
. By Lemma
6
we know that at most
\frac{\varepsilon N}{4}
samples are outside of the range
q_{v}+2^{i_{0}}
. We conclude that the the difference between
U_{Q}(v)
and
U_{2Q}(v)
is at most
\frac{\varepsilon Q}{2}
. By induction, this implies that
\Gamma(2^{i_{0}})\subset\Gamma(2^{i_{0}+1})
for every
i\geqslant i_{0}
. This implies that corresponding sets are nested. The diameter of the set
\bigcap_{i\geqslant i^{\ast}}\Gamma(2^{i})
is at most
8\varepsilon Q
and the matrix of second moments
\widetilde{\Sigma}
belongs to an even smaller set. Thus,
\|\hat{\Sigma}-\widetilde{\Sigma}\|\leqslant 8\varepsilon Q
. We conclude by applying Proposition
7
. ∎

5
Optimality

It is natural to ask about the optimality of our statistical guarantees of Theorem
1
. It is well known that the term
C\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\mathbf{r}(\Sigma)/N}
appears in the minimax lower bound for covariance estimation
[
44
, Theorem 2]
as well as in the lower bound for the performance of the sample covariance matrix in the Gaussian case
[
40
, Theorem 4]
. Clearly, this term is necessary because the adversary can always leave the sample uncorrupted and the multivariate Gaussian distribution satisfies the norm equivalence assumption (
1
). Further, we show that the term
C\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}
cannot be improved in general. As shown in
[
54
]
(following the lower bound in
[
46
]
), the term scaling as
R\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}
appears in the lower bound for any estimator of the covariance matrix. Here
R^{2}
is the so-called
weak variance
defined by

R^{2}=\sup\nolimits_{v\in S^{d-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(v^{\mathsf{T}}(X\otimes X-{\mathbb{E}}X\otimes X)v\right)^{2}.

In the multivariate Gaussian case, using the standard relation between moments, we get

R^{2}\geqslant\sup\nolimits_{v\in S^{d-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\langle v,X\rangle^{4}-\|\Sigma\|^{2}=3\sup\limits_{v\in S^{d-1}}({\mathbb{E}}\langle v,X\rangle)^{2}-\|\Sigma\|^{2}=2\|\Sigma\|^{2}.

This shows the necessity of the term
C\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}
in Theorem
1
.

Finally, we can restrict the discussion to the convergence rate with respect to the fraction of corrupted samples
\eta
. Some closely related lower bounds with matching rates of convergence appear in the analysis of the sparse vector model
[
20
, Theorem 5 and Theorem 6]
. Their model can be seen as a special case of our setup when
d=1
. Instead of exploiting their techniques for the lower bounds, we provide a separate analysis using explicit examples.
Our key argument to derive the minimax optimality with respect to
\eta
is to reduce our problem to a mean estimation problem. For the mean estimation, a simple computation shows a lower bound with respect to
\eta
. To describe this result, we first consider a basic definition.

Definition 1
.

For a random variable
X
, we define the quantile

Q_{q}(X)=\sup\{M\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(X\geqslant M)\geqslant 1-q\}.

Formally, the following simple lower bound holds.

Proposition 9

(Inequality 2.3 in
[
48
]
)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma_{X}
, and with an absolutely continuous distribution. Suppose that
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N}
is an
\eta
-corrupted sample sampled according to the distribution of
X
.
Let
\overline{X}=X-\mathbb{E}X
and define
\varepsilon(\overline{X},\eta)
as follows

\varepsilon(\overline{X},\eta)=\max\left\{\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}-Q_{\eta/2}(\overline{X})|\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{X}\leqslant Q_{\eta/2}(\overline{X})\},\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}-Q_{1-\eta/2}(\overline{X})|\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{X}\geqslant Q_{1-\eta/2}(\overline{X})\}\right\}.

Then, no estimator
\widehat{\mu}=\widehat{\mu}(\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{N})
of the mean
\mu
can perform better than

|\widehat{\mu}-\mu|\leqslant\varepsilon(\overline{X},\eta).

To obtain the minimax rates with respect to
\eta
, it is enough to restrict our attention to the one dimensional case. Assume that the distribution of a zero mean random variable
X
satisfies the
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence (
1
). That is, for some
\kappa\geqslant 1
, it holds that
\left({\mathbb{E}}X^{4}\right)^{1/4}\leqslant\kappa\left({\mathbb{E}}X^{2}\right)^{1/2}
. Denote
Y=X^{2}
and observe that
Y
is non-negative. In this case, the estimation of the variance of
X
can be seen as the estimation of the mean of
Y
. The norm equivalence assumption can be rewritten as

\left({\mathbb{E}}Y^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\kappa^{2}{\mathbb{E}}Y.

Example 1

(Optimality of the
\sqrt{\eta}
-term when
p=4
)
.

First, a few intermediate random variables are considered before the definition of
Y
. Define

Y_{1}=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}},&\text{with probability}\ \frac{\eta}{2},\\
-1,&\text{with probability}\frac{1-\eta}{2},\\
1,&\text{with probability}\ \frac{1-\eta}{2},\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}},&\text{with probability}\ \frac{\eta}{2}.\end{cases}

Clearly
\mathbb{E}Y_{1}=0
. Let
\sigma_{Y_{1}}
denote the standard deviation of
Y_{1}
. It holds that

\sigma_{Y_{1}}=(\mathbb{E}Y_{1}^{2})^{1/2}=\sqrt{2-\eta}\leqslant\sqrt{2}.

Assume that
\eta\leqslant 1/4
. We have
Q_{\eta/2}(Y_{1})=-1
. Indeed,
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geqslant-1)=1-\frac{\eta}{2}
and
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geqslant 1)=\frac{1}{2}<1-\frac{\eta}{2}
. We conclude that

\varepsilon(Y_{1},\eta)\geqslant\mathbb{E}|Y_{1}+1|\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{1}\leqslant-1\}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}-1\right)\frac{\eta}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\eta}.

We now consider the random variable
Y_{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{2-\eta}}Y_{1}
, where
\sigma>0
is a positive real number. It still holds that
\mathbb{E}Y_{2}=0
, but now,
(\mathbb{E}Y_{2}^{2})^{1/2}=\sigma^{2}
. By homogeneity, we have

\varepsilon(Y_{2},\eta)\geqslant\frac{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\eta}}{4\sqrt{2-\eta}}\geqslant\frac{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\eta}}{4\sqrt{2}}.

Finally, observe that
Y=Y_{2}+\|Y_{2}\|_{\infty}
is a non-negative random variable and
\varepsilon(Y,\eta)=\varepsilon(Y_{2},\eta)
since the difference between
Y
and
Y_{2}
is a constant. We conclude by observing that
{\mathbb{E}}Y=\|Y_{2}\|_{\infty}
and
{\mathbb{E}}Y^{2}={\mathbb{E}}Y_{2}^{2}+\|Y_{2}\|^{2}_{\infty}\leqslant 2\|Y_{2}\|^{2}_{\infty}
. Thus, the norm equivalence assumption
\left({\mathbb{E}}Y^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\kappa^{2}{\mathbb{E}}Y
holds with
\kappa^{2}=\sqrt{2}
.

We now present an example that attains the sub-exponential minimax rate in the mean estimation and therefore confirms the optimality of our covariance estimation results in the sub-Gaussian case. Indeed, observe that if
X
is a sub-Gaussian random variable, then
Y=X^{2}
is a sub-exponential random variable (see
[
72
, Lemma 2.7.6]
). Therefore, when making a reduction from covariance estimation to a mean estimation problem as in Example
1
, we have to analyze the mean estimation problem in the sub-exponential regime. We note that a similar sub-Gaussian construction appears in
[
48
, Remark before Section 2.1]
2
2

2

To make their construction work in the Gaussian case, we changed the definition of
Q
, so that it becomes an
\eta/4
-quantile instead of an
\eta/2
-quantile claimed in
[
48
]
.
.

Example 2

(Optimality of
\eta\log(1/\eta)
-term in sub-Gaussian covariance estimation)
.

Let
Y=\min\{1,|g|^{2}\}\mathbbm{1}\{|g|^{2}<Q\}+|g|^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{|g|^{2}\geqslant Q\}
, where
g
is a standard Gaussian random variable and
Q
is such that
\mathbb{P}(|g|^{2}\geqslant Q)=\eta/4
. Consequently, we have
\mathbb{P}(|g|\geqslant\sqrt{Q})=\eta/4
. It is easy to see that for
\eta
sufficiently small, it holds that
Q>1
. Let us now prove that
Q_{1-\eta/2}(Y)=1
. Indeed, since
\mathbb{P}(Y\geqslant 1)=\mathbb{P}(|g|\geqslant 1)>\eta/2
for small enough
\eta
, then
Q_{1-\eta/2}(Y)\geqslant 1
. It is clear that if
Q_{1-\eta/2}(Y)>1
, then
Q_{1-\eta/2}(Y)\geqslant Q
, which contradicts
\mathbb{P}(|g|^{2}\geqslant Q)=\eta/4<\eta/2
.
Thus, we have

\varepsilon(\overline{Y},\eta)\geqslant\mathbb{E}|\overline{Y}-Q_{1-\eta/2}(\overline{Y})|\mathbbm{1}\{\overline{Y}\geqslant Q_{1-\eta/2}(\overline{Y})\}=\mathbb{E}(Y-1)\mathbbm{1}\{Y\geqslant 1\}.

This leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y-1)\mathbbm{1}\{Y\geqslant 1\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}Y\mathbbm{1}\{1\leqslant Y<Q\}+\mathbb{E}Y\mathbbm{1}\{Y\geqslant Q\}-\mathbb{P}(Y\geqslant 1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(1\leqslant|g|^{2}<Q)+\mathbb{E}|g|^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{|g|^{2}\geqslant Q\}-\mathbb{P}(|g|^{2}\geqslant 1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}|g|^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{|g|^{2}\geqslant Q\}-\mathbb{P}(|g|^{2}\geqslant Q).

Using the standard computation (see
[
72
, Exercise 2.1.4]
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}|g|^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{|g|^{2}\geqslant Q\}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{Q}\exp(-Q/2)+\mathbb{P}(|g|\geqslant\sqrt{Q}).

Therefore, since
\eta/4=\mathbb{P}(|g|\geqslant\sqrt{Q})
, and by the standard Gaussian integration
[
72
, Proposition 2.1.2]
we have

\varepsilon(\overline{Y},\eta)\geqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{Q}\exp(-Q/2)\geqslant Q\mathbb{P}(|g|\geqslant\sqrt{Q})=\frac{Q\eta}{4}.

Using the same Gaussian integration formula, we conclude that, in order to get
\mathbb{P}(|g|\geqslant\sqrt{Q})=\eta/4
, we need to choose
Q\sim\log\left(1/\eta\right)
. This implies that

\varepsilon(\overline{Y},\eta)\geqslant\frac{Q\eta}{4}\gtrsim\eta\log\left(1/\eta\right).

We claim that the rate
\eta\log\left(1/\eta\right)
is sharp. To do so, we compute the upper bound with respect to
\eta
in Theorem
1
. If the original random variable
X
is a sub-Gaussian, then
\kappa(p)^{2}\sim p
for every
p\geqslant 1
(see
[
72
, Proposition 2.5.2]
) and the upper bound scales as
\eta p/\eta^{2/p}
. We choose
p\sim\log\left(1/\eta\right)
to optimize the fraction and obtain the claimed rate.

Finally, we note that for the Gaussian case, it is possible to achieve a slightly improved dependence on
\eta
by eliminating the logarithmic factor
\log(1/\eta)
(as shown in the follow-up work of Minasyan and the second-named author of this paper
[
55
]
), owing to the rotational invariance property of the Gaussian distribution. As discussed in
[
55
]
, the estimator needs to be tailored to the Gaussian distribution and therefore it cannot be applied to the more general setup considered in our Theorem
1
.
