EXCHANGEABLE ARRAYS AND INTEGRABLE SYSTEMS FOR CHARACTERISTIC POLYNOMIALS OF RANDOM MATRICES
THEODOROS ASSIOTIS, MUSTAFA ALPER GUNES, JONATHAN P. KEATING, FEI WEI

Abstract
The joint moments of the derivatives of the characteristic polynomial of a random unitary matrix, and also a variant of the characteristic polynomial that is real on the unit circle, in the large matrix size limit, have been studied intensively in the past twenty five years, partly in relation to conjectural connections to the Riemann zeta-function and Hardy’s function. We completely settle the most general version of the problem of convergence of these joint moments, after they are suitably rescaled, for an arbitrary number of derivatives and with arbitrary positive real exponents. Our approach relies on a hidden, higher-order exchangeable structure, that of an exchangeable array. Using these probabilistic techniques, we then give a combinatorial formula for the leading order coefficient in the asymptotics of the joint moments, when the power on the characteristic polynomial itself is a positive real number and the exponents of the derivatives are integers, in terms of a finite number of finite-dimensional integrals which are explicitly computable. Finally, we develop a method, based on a class of Hankel determinants shifted by partitions, that allows us to give an exact representation of all these joint moments, for finite matrix size, in terms of derivatives of Painlevé V transcendents, and then for the leading order coefficient in the large-matrix limit in terms of derivatives of solutions of the σ \sigma -Painlevé III’ equation. Equivalently, we can represent all the joint moments of power sum linear statistics of a certain determinantal point process behind this problem in terms of derivatives of σ \sigma -Painlevé III’ transcendents. This gives an efficient way to compute all these quantities explicitly. Our methods can be used to obtain analogous results for a number of other models sharing the same features.

Introduction and main results

1.1

Random Matrix Theory and Riemann
\zeta
-function

Over the past fifty years, there has been significant interest in the surprising duality between problems in analytic number theory and analogous ones in random matrix theory. This connection dates back to the work of Montgomery
[
72
]
, who conjectured that the eigenvalues of
N\times N
unitary matrices, as the matrix size tends to infinity, can be used to model the behaviour of the zeros of the Riemann
\zeta
-function,
\zeta(z)
, high up the critical line,
\Re(z)=1/2
; see
[
22
,
24
,
23
,
81
,
40
]
for subsequent extensions of Montgomery’s work. It is natural to ask whether this connection between the zeros of the Riemann
\zeta
-function and those of the characteristic polynomials of random unitary matrices (i.e. the eigenvalues) extends to the values of these two functions. Keating and Snaith
[
67
]
suggested that the value distribution of the Riemann
\zeta
-function high on the critical line can be modeled by the value of the characteristic polynomial of a random unitary matrix at 1, when the matrix size is large enough. As an application, they conjectured the leading-order asymptotics of the moments of the
\zeta
-function on the critical line by considering the analogous moments for the characteristic polynomials of unitary random matrices.

To be more precise, let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{U}(N)
, where, here and throughout this paper,
\mathbb{U}(N)
denotes the group of
N\times N
unitary matrices, and let
e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{N}}
denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
. Consider the characteristic polynomial
V_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
of
\mathbf{A}
on the unit circle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, given by,

V_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)=\det\left(\mathbf{I}-e^{-\textnormal{i}\theta}\mathbf{A}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-e^{\textnormal{i}(\theta_{j}-\theta)}\right).

It was conjectured in
[
67
]
(see also
[
32
,
33
]
for
s=3,4
), that the
T\rightarrow\infty
asymptotics of

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+\textnormal{i}t\right)\right|^{2s}\mathrm{d}t

can be obtained from the
N\rightarrow\infty
asymptotics of

\int_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\left|V_{\mathbf{A}}(0)\right|^{2s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\mathbf{A}),

where
\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}
is the Haar measure on
\mathbb{U}(N)
. This conjecture was extended to later terms in the asymptotic expansion of the moments in
[
29
,
30
]
, and has been verified heuristically using correlations of divisor functions in
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.

Inspired by this, Hughes
[
57
]
considered a related quantity, aiming to obtain conjectural values for joint moments of the Riemann
\zeta
-function and its derivative. For a unitary matrix
\mathbf{A}
, instead of the characteristic polynomial itself, he considered the associated function

Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)=\exp\left(\textnormal{i}N\frac{\theta+\pi}{2}-\textnormal{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}\right)V_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)

which has the property that for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
,
Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}
and
|Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)|=|V_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)|
. This relation between
V_{\mathbf{A}}
and
Z_{\mathbf{A}}
is analogous to the relation between the
\zeta
function and Hardy’s
\mathcal{Z}
-function, defined as follows,

\mathcal{Z}(t)=\pi^{-\textnormal{i}t/2}\frac{\Gamma(1/4+\textnormal{i}t/2)}{|\Gamma(1/4+\textnormal{i}t/2)|}\zeta(1/2+\textnormal{i}t),

which also satisfies
|\mathcal{Z}(t)|=\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+\textnormal{i}t\right)\right|
, and
\mathcal{Z}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Hughes conjectured in
[
57
]
that, for arbitrary positive real parameters
s,h
, one should have asymptotic convergence of the joint moments as follows,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{s^{2}+2h}}\int_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\left|Z_{\mathbf{A}}(0)\right|^{2s-2h}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{\mathbf{A}}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\bigg|_{\theta=0}\right|^{2h}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\text{Haar}}(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{P}(s,h),

(1)

for some positive
\mathcal{P}(s,h)
, a claim he was able to prove for
s,h\in\mathbb{N}
, deriving an expression for
\mathcal{P}(s,h)
for these values of
s,h
. This, in turn, leads to the conjectural asymptotics for the analogous joint moments of
\mathcal{Z}(t)
, introduced by Ingham in 1926
[
58
]
,

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mathcal{Z}(t)\right|^{2s-2h}\left|\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}(t)\right|^{2h}\mathrm{d}t\sim(\log T)^{s+2h}\eta(s)\mathcal{P}(s,h)

where

\eta(s)=\prod_{\text{primes }p}(1-p^{-1})^{s^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p^{-k}\left[\frac{\Gamma(k+s)}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(s)}\right]^{2}.

We note that even though his initial work, and the majority of subsequent works, are centered around the joint moments of
Z_{\mathbf{A}}
, Hughes also conjectured analogous results for
V_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+\textnormal{i}t\right)
. Our goal in this paper is to completely solve the most general version of this problem on the random matrix side, by considering moments involving an arbitrary number of derivatives and positive real exponents, for both
V_{\mathbf{A}}
and
Z_{\mathbf{A}}
. First, we briefly review the literature.

1.2

A brief history of the problem

Over the years, there has been significant interest and progress in both proving the convergence in (
1
), and also obtaining explicit formulae for the leading order coefficient
\mathcal{P}(s,h)
. After Hughes’ proof for the case
s,h\in\mathbb{N}
, Conrey, Rubenstein and Snaith
[
39
]
gave an alternative proof for the case
s=h\in\mathbb{N}
, using representations of these moments via multiple contour integrals from
[
29
,
30
]
. They also gave an alternative expression for the coefficient
\mathcal{P}(s,s)
in terms of a determinant of Bessel functions, which was later shown to be related to the Painlevé equations by Forrester and Witte in
[
46
]
. Dehaye
[
41
,
42
]
gave an independent proof for
s,h\in\mathbb{N}
, and gave another representation of
\mathcal{P}(s,h)
as a certain combinatorial sum. Winn
[
84
]
gave the first proof for
s\in\mathbb{N},h\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
by expressing the moments in terms of Laguerre polynomials. Basor et al. in
[
21
]
took a different approach, using Riemann-Hilbert problem techniques to obtain a further alternative proof, and giving a representation for
\mathcal{P}(s,h)
in terms of Painlevé transcendents for
s,h\in\mathbb{N}
. Bailey et al
[
19
]
extended the methods developed in
[
39
]
to obtain the same Painlevé-representation of
\mathcal{P}(s,h)
. In
[
7
]
, the authors extended the method of
[
39
]
to the orthogonal and symplectic groups, including to moments of higher derivatives, and used the results to formulate conjectures for the corresponding moments of families of number-theoretic
L
-functions, along the lines of
[
66
]
.

It is important to point out that Basor et al.
[
21
]
showed that when the matrix size is fixed, the joint moments also have an exact representation in terms of Painlevé transcendents (not the same ones that describe the large-matrix limit).

The large-matrix asymptotics for general positive real parameters
s,h
were only recently proven for the first time in
[
16
]
. Moreover, it was understood in the same paper that there exists a random variable, defined via a determinantal point process, which serves as a bridge between the problem of joint moments and Painlevé equations. Even though this link was initially only proven for
s\in\mathbb{N}
in
[
16
]
, it was later shown in
[
14
]
that the connection indeed holds for real
s
. Finally, we note that for the case where
Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
is replaced by the characteristic polynomial
V_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
, which models the
\zeta
-function itself, Hughes’ conjecture remained unproven until now outside the parameter range
s,h\in\mathbb{N}
.

1.3

Joint moments of higher order derivatives

The main purpose of the present paper is to establish the
N\to\infty
asymptotics, and study in detail the leading order coefficient, of the joint moments of arbitrary numbers of higher order
derivatives with arbitrary positive real exponents. We also obtain exact formulae when
N
is fixed.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
n_{1}>\cdots>n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then, we define:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}_{N}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left|V_{\mathbf{A}}^{(n_{j})}(0)\right|^{2h_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\mathbf{A}),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}_{N}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left|Z_{\mathbf{A}}^{(n_{j})}(0)\right|^{2h_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\mathbf{A}).

(3)

Here, the superscript
n_{j}
in
V^{(n_{j})}_{\mathbf{A}}
or
Z^{(n_{j})}_{\mathbf{A}}
means the
n_{j}
-th derivative of
V_{\mathbf{A}}
or
Z_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Observe, that (
1
) is simply a statement for the asymptotics of
\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(1,0)}(h,s-h)
. Barhoumi in his monograph
[
20
]
was the first to establish the asymptotics for
\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})
for
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
using an approach based on symmetric function theory. More recently, the asymptotics for
k=2
, for
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, for both
\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(n_{1},n_{1})}(h_{1},h_{2})
and
\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},n_{2})}(h_{1},h_{2})
, were obtained in
[
68
]
using an intricate analysis of multiple contour integrals and an expression, different to the one in
[
20
]
, for the leading order coefficient that was shown in
[
69
]
, in the special case of
\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(2,0)}(h_{1},h_{2})
, to be connected to Painlevé equations. We note that the expressions for the leading order coefficients given in
[
20
,
69
,
68
]
do not make sense for generic real exponents
h_{i}
.

Our first main result gives the convergence of both of these quantities, after appropriate rescalings, for the
full parameter range (in particular without the integrality restrictions), and gives a probabilistic expression for the leading coefficient in terms of joint moments
of certain natural random variables that are defined through a determinantal point process. Thus, in order to
state our result, we need some definitions.

A determinantal point process on a Borel set
\mathfrak{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
with correlation kernel
\mathcal{K}:\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{C}
is a probability measure
\mathfrak{P}
on
\mathrm{Conf}(\mathfrak{X})
, the space of locally finite collections of points in
\mathfrak{X}
, endowed with a certain topology and corresponding Borel
\sigma
-algebra, see
[
25
,
60
]
for the details, such that for any
k\geq 1
and any measurable and bounded
F:\mathfrak{X}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support, we have

\int_{\mathrm{Conf}(\mathfrak{X})}\sum_{a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{k}}\in\mathsf{E}}F(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{k}})\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{P}(\mathsf{E})=\int_{\mathfrak{X}^{k}}F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\det_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\left[\mathcal{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}.

We will be interested in specific determinantal point processes defined via their correlation kernels
\mathfrak{E}^{(s)}
(we note here that by results of
[
26
,
70
]
, the kernels
\mathfrak{E}^{(s)}
, completely determine their corresponding point processes), where
s\geq 0
is a parameter.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then, we define
\mathbf{P}^{(s)}
to be the determinantal point process on
\left(-\infty,0\right)\times\left(0,\infty\right)
with the correlation kernel
\mathfrak{E}^{(s)}
given by,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}^{(s)}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\left(\Gamma(s+1)\right)^{2}}{\Gamma(2s+1)\Gamma(2s+2)}\frac{G^{(s)}(x)H^{(s)}(y)-G^{(s)}(y)H^{(s)}(x)}{x-y},

where the functions
G^{(s)}(x),H^{(s)}(x)
are given by the formulae

G^{(s)}(x)=2^{2s-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}}J_{s-1/2}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right),\ \ H^{(s)}(x)=\mathrm{sgn}(x)2^{2s+\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(s+\frac{3}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}}J_{s+1/2}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right),

where
J_{\nu}
denotes the Bessel function with parameter
\nu
.

This random point process first appeared, in a completely different setting, in influential work of Borodin and Olshanski
[
26
]
, and we will say more in the sequel. Moving on, certain elementary symmetric functions, the power sums and elementary symmetric functions, of the random points of
\mathbf{P}^{(s)}
will be one of the main ingredients in our first main result.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
s\geq 0
. Associated to the determinantal point process
\mathbf{P}^{(s)}
, we define random variables
\{\mathsf{q}_{n}(s)\}_{n\geq 1}
by, where the random point configuration
\mathsf{E}
is distributed according to
\mathbf{P}^{(s)}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{q}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{E}}x\mathbf{1}_{|x|>k^{-2}},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathsf{q}_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{E}}x^{n},\ \ \textnormal{for }n\geq 2.

(5)

Moreover, define random variables
\{\mathsf{Y}_{n}(s)\}_{n\geq 0}
by letting
\mathsf{Y}_{0}(s)\equiv 1
,
\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)=-\mathsf{q}_{1}(s)
and inductively, for
n\geq 2
,

\mathsf{Y}_{n}(s)=-(n-1)!\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(n-j)!}\mathsf{Y}_{n-j}(s)\mathsf{q}_{j}(s).

(6)

It is not immediately obvious that these infinite sums are
\mathbf{P}^{(s)}
-a.s. finite but this follows from the results of
[
26
,
79
]
. We note that the principal value sum in the definition of
\mathsf{q}_{1}(s)
is required, otherwise the series does not converge. The random variables
\{\mathsf{Y}_{n}(s)\}_{n\geq 1}
are really just the elementary symmetric functions of
\mathbf{P}^{(s)}
(up to explicit universal constants); the formula (
6
) is simply Newton’s identity, in that it expresses the elementary symmetric functions in terms of power sums. We could have defined them directly, but then one needs to introduce a cutoff in the definition as in (
4
) for every
n\geq 1
. Both families of random variables will appear in the results below, as in some cases one choice is better suited than the other. Below we normally denote by the symbol
\mathbb{E}
the expectation with respect to the underlying probability law, which will be clear from context.

With these definitions at hand, our first main result gives the leading order asymptotics of both quantities introduced in Definition
1.1
for the full parameter range.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Let
h_{1},\ldots h_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
s=\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}>0
. Then, we have the following finite limits,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})}{N^{s^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}h_{j}}}=\frac{G(s+1)^{2}}{G(2s+1)}2^{-2\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}n_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left|\mathsf{Y}_{n_{j}}(s)\right|^{2h_{j}}\right],

(7)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})}{N^{s^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}h_{j}}}=\frac{G(s+1)^{2}}{G(2s+1)}2^{-2\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}n_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg|\sum_{m_{j}=0}^{n_{j}}(-\textnormal{i})^{m_{j}}\binom{n_{j}}{m_{j}}\mathsf{Y}_{n_{j}-m_{j}}(s)\Bigg|^{2h_{j}}\right],

(8)

where
G(z)
is the Barnes G-function, with
\gamma
the Euler-Mascheroni constant,

G(1+z)=(2\pi)^{\frac{z}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{z+z^{2}(1+\gamma)}{2}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{z}{j}\right)^{j}\exp\left(\frac{z^{2}}{2j}-z\right).

We note that Theorem
1.4
, in the more natural case of the characteristic polynomial
V_{\mathbf{A}}
, is new whenever the exponent on any of the derivatives of
V_{\mathbf{A}}
is non-integer. Even in the simplest possible case of
k=2
,
n_{1}=1,n_{2}=0
, this confirms the prediction of Hughes
[
57
]
from more than 20 years ago; the somewhat simpler case of the asymptotics of
\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(1,0)}(h_{1},h_{2})
, also conjectured by Hughes, was in fact only proven in the last few years as well, see
[
16
]
.

The probabilistic expressions in (
3.1
) and (
8
) in terms of the random variables
\{\mathsf{Y}_{n}(s)\}_{n\geq 1}
are aesthetically rather pleasing and, although initially may seem to come out of nowhere, it will be clear from the proof that they are rather natural. On the other hand, using them to compute quantities more explicitly is far from trivial. In proving the results that follow we will not make direct use of these expressions. Nevertheless, we stress that (
3.1
) and (
8
) are the only expressions for the leading order coefficients available in the literature which make sense for generic non-integer exponents
h_{i}
, even in the simplest case of the moments of a single derivative.

Towards our goal of computing the leading order coefficient more explicitly, our next main result gives a representation of the expectation on the right-hand side of (
3.1
), for
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, as a finite linear combination of explicit finite-dimensional integrals. These integrals can all be computed explicitly (one simply expands the Vandermonde determinant and these integrals boil down to one-dimensional integrals which are basically moments of a Student’s t-distribution) but writing down a general expression becomes very tedious and we will not attempt to do it here; we will instead discuss an alternative recursive way to obtain such expressions via connections to integrable systems below. In particular, the formula in Theorem
1.5
gives a concrete expression for the joint moments whenever the exponent on the characteristic polynomial itself is a real number and the exponents on its derivatives are integers.

These finite-dimensional averages will be taken with respect to the Cauchy
[
47
,
85
]
(also called Hua-Pickrell
[
26
]
) measures
{\bf M}_{N}^{(s)}
, with
s\geq 0
, which are probability measures supported on the Weyl chamber,

\mathbb{W}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots\geq x_{N}\right\}

that are given by:

{\bf M}_{N}^{(s)}(\mathrm{d}x_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}x_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathrm{C}}_{N}^{(s)}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\left(1+x_{i}^{2}\right)^{s+N}}\Delta^{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{N},

(9)

where we denote by
\Delta
the Vandermonde determinant,

\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{1\leq i<k\leq N}(x_{i}-x_{k}),

and the normalization constant
\tilde{\mathrm{C}}_{N}^{(s)}
is given explicitly by

\tilde{\mathrm{C}}_{N}^{(s)}={\pi^{N}2^{-N(N+2s-1)}}\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{j!\Gamma(2s+N-j)}{\Gamma(s+N-j)^{2}}.

For the rest of the paper, we denote averages taken with respect to these measures as:

\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\left[f(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)})\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{W}_{N}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}){\bf M}_{N}^{(s)}(\mathrm{d}x_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}x_{N}).

Note that, whenever the function
f
is invariant under permutations of indices, and this is the case for all functions we will consider in this paper, one can equivalently write:

\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\left[f(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)})\right]=\frac{1}{N!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}){\bf M}_{N}^{(s)}(\mathrm{d}x_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}x_{N}).

(10)

We also observe that by analytic continuation the function
s\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\left[\bullet\right]
is holomorphic whenever the integrand is holomorphic in
s
and the integral on the right hand side of (
10
) exists. We note that although for
s\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{R}
the probabilistic interpretation breaks down, abusing notation we will still use the expectation sign
\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}
below. As the discussion preceding Theorem
1.4
suggests, throughout the rest of the paper, we will commonly use elementary symmetric polynomials and power sum polynomials, which we will denote by (
\mathrm{e}_{0}=\mathrm{p}_{0}\equiv 1
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}_{n}\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{n}\leq m}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}_{n}\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{n}.

We are now in a position to state our second main result.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
n_{1}>\cdots>n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and
h_{j}\in\frac{\mathbb{N}}{2}
, for
j=1,\ldots,k
. Moreover, define
L=\sum_{j}2h_{j}n_{j}
. Then, for any real number
s>\sum_{j}h_{j}-\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\mathsf{Y}_{n_{j}}(s)\right)^{2h_{j}}\right]=\frac{(n_{1}!)^{2h_{1}}\cdots(n_{k}!)^{2h_{k}}}{L!}\sum_{m=n_{1}}^{L}(-1)^{m}{L\choose m}\mathbb{E}_{m}^{(s)}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\mathrm{e}_{n_{j}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(m)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(m)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{m}^{(m)}\right)\right)^{2h_{j}}\right].

In particular, we obtain that the map

s\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\mathsf{Y}_{n_{j}}(s)\right)^{2h_{j}}\right]

is a rational function.

If this were the case, the asymptotics we established earlier would give a bound on the growth of the joint moments, but not the true order. 1.5

Connection to integrable systems

As remarked in Section
1.2
, over the past two decades, the problem of joint moments was realized to be closely related to the theory of the Painlevé equations (see
[
21
,
19
,
16
,
14
]
). In the case of joint moments with only the first derivative, it was discovered only recently that the random variable
\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)=-\mathsf{q}_{1}(s)
is in fact responsible for this connection between joint moments and integrable systems. To be more precise, it was shown in
[
16
]
and
[
14
]
that, for any
s\geq 0
, the function
\tau^{(s)}
defined by,

t\mapsto\tau^{(s)}(t)\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}t\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\textnormal{i}t\frac{\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)}{2}}\right]

satisfies the following form of the
\sigma
-Painlevé III’ equation:

\left(t\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\tau^{(s)}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\right)^{2}=-4t\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau^{(s)}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{3}+\left(4s^{2}+4\tau^{(s)}\right)\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau^{(s)}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+t\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau^{(s)}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\tau^{(s)},

(13)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, along with the initial conditions:

\begin{cases}\tau^{(s)}(0)=0,&\text{for}\ s>0,\\
&\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tau^{(s)}(t)\Bigr\rvert_{t=0}=0,&\text{for}\ s>\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

(14)

Here, we note that for the derivatives to exist at
t=0
, one requires further conditions on the parameter
s
. That is, in order to guarantee that

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\textnormal{i}t\frac{\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)}{2}}\right]\Big\rvert_{t=0}<\infty

one needs to have
s>\frac{m-1}{2}
. Moving on, we note that the result above can be useful in many ways. First of all, we can see immediately that all the even integer moments (when they exist) of
\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)=-\mathsf{q}_{1}(s)
have a representation in terms of the
\sigma
-Painlevé III’ equation, since we have,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)\right)^{2h}\right]=2^{2h}(-1)^{h}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2h}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2h}}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\tau^{(s)}(u)}{u}\mathrm{d}u\right)\right]\Bigg|_{t=0}.

But more ambitiously, if one can show that the characteristic function of
\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and if it admits an explicit expression (and this is a highly non-trivial, being connected to deep results on classical solutions of Painlevé equations; see
[
82
,
83
]
for what is meant by a classical solution in the context of Painlevé equations), then the expression for
\tau^{(s)}(t)
can be used, by means of Fourier inversion, to recover an explicit density for
\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)
. This is precisely what is done in
[
14
]
, for
s\in\mathbb{N}
, where the density is then used to give an explicit expression for any finite (including complex) moments of
\mathsf{Y}_{1}(s)
.

Given the previous results presented in our paper, it is then natural to wonder whether certain joint moments of the random variables
\{\mathsf{Y}_{n}(s)\}_{n\geq 1}
admit representations in terms of Painlevé transcendents. We answer this question in the affirmative in Theorem
1.11
below by representing such joint moments as linear combinations, with polynomial coefficients, of derivatives of solutions to the
\sigma
-Painlevé III’ equation. This is presented in terms of the allied family of random variables

which is more convenient. In fact, we show a more general result, related to a richer object, a certain one-dimensional function, from which the joint moments can be recovered.

At this stage it can be shown

1.8

Some questions

We end this introduction with some questions. There exists a natural generalisation of the law of eigenvalues of Haar-distributed unitary matrices called the circular
\beta

\beta

\beta\geq 0
(random unitary matrices correspond to
\beta=2

[
47

. The problem of establishing the asymptotics of the joint moments of the characteristic polynomial makes sense in the setting of C

[
48

15
]
in the special case of a joint moment with a single derivative. Our methods extend word-for-word for
\beta=1,4
to prove corresponding results for general joint moments since permutation-invariant matrix models exist in these cases (now with real and quaternion entries instead of complex numbers). We believe a variation of our methods will also be useful in tackling the problem for general

A different variant of the problem is to consider joint moments of characteristic polynomials of Haar-distributed orthogonal and symplectic matrices, see
[
6

8

53

for previous work. These asymptotics give conjectures for moments of L-functions in analytic number theory, see
[

6

53

. In the case of the second derivative, these joint moments were shown to be related to the sum of inverse points of the Jacobi ensemble, see
[
53
]
. Hence, one would expect that joint moments of higher order derivatives admit representations in terms of higher order Laurent polynomials in the eigenvalues of a Jacobi random matrix. It is foreseeable that the methods we use to prove Theorem
1.11
would allow one to obtain an expression for these higher order averages in terms of the lowest order one, whose asymptotics are already known. More generally, we expect that our methods can be used to show similar phenomena for all classical ensembles of random matrices; namely give an expression for averages of polynomials of arbitrary degrees in the eigenvalues in terms of averages of the first-order statistics.

In relation to integrable systems, it would be interesting if the joint characteristic function (or a variant) of several of the
\mathsf{Y}_{n}(s)
or

2.1

Exchangeable arrays

We present some general results on exchangeable arrays that will be applied to moments of random matrices in the sequel, this application being the main novel contribution of this part of the paper. The techniques we use are (unsurprisingly) rooted in the general theory of exchangeability
[
64
]
. We first fix some notation. For each
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and
J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, let
J^{(d)}
denote the set of subsets of
J
of size
d
. Also, let
\tilde{J}^{(k)}=\cup_{d\leq k}J^{(d)}
. Let
[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
and denote by
\mathsf{Sym}_{N}
the symmetric group acting on
[N]
. Finally, recall that for an arbitrary random variable
\mathcal{X}
taking values in a standard Borel space we write
\mathsf{Law}(\mathcal{X})
for its law.

An exchangeable array will be our main object of study and is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
. A collection of random variables
(\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}
, with
\mathsf{X}_{J}
taking values in a standard Borel space
(\mathcal{V},\mathfrak{V})
, is called an exchangeable array if for any permutation
\tau
of
\mathbb{N}
that fixes all but finitely many elements, we have that

\mathsf{Law}\left\{(\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\right\}=\mathsf{Law}\left\{\left(\mathsf{X}_{\tau(J)}\right)_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\right\},

(20)

where if
J=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\}
, we define
\tau(J)=\{\tau(a_{1}),\ldots,\tau(a_{d})\}
.

Let us comment on some features of this definition; for more details see Kallenberg’s monograph
[
64
]
. First, for
k=1
the definition is exactly that of an exchangeable sequence of random variables. In higher dimensions it is most common in the literature to index the random variables
\mathsf{X}_{J}
by sets
J
of size exactly
k
instead of size at most
k
. However, the richer object indexed by sets of sizes at most
k
is required if one wants to study joint moments. Finally, a more common terminology in the literature is that of a “jointly-exchangeable array” indicating that we are using a single permutation
\tau
to act on the elements of
J
. This is to distinguish from a “separately-exchangeable array” for which each element is acted on by a different permutation. Since separately-exchangeable arrays will not appear in this paper we have dropped this extra word from the terminology.

Initially our random variables
(\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}
are defined on some abstract probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mathbb{Q})
. It will, however, be more convenient for our proofs to define them instead on the canonical space,

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}{\mathcal{V}}^{\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}},

endowed with the
\sigma
-algebra
\tilde{\mathfrak{V}}
generated by all sets of the form

\prod_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}A_{J},

where all but finitely many
A_{J}\in\mathfrak{V}
equal
\mathcal{V}
, as follows. For
I\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}
, define the measurable function
\mathsf{X}_{I}
from
\left(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}\right)
to
\left(\mathcal{V},\mathfrak{B}\right)
by:

\mathsf{X}_{I}\left((v_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\right)=v_{I}.

Then, we consider the probability space
(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathfrak{V}},\mathbb{P})
, with
\mathbb{P}
the unique probability measure on
\left(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}\right)
defined via the relation:

\mathbb{P}\left(A_{J_{1}}\times A_{J_{2}}\times\cdots\times A_{J_{m}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{Q}\left(\left\{\mathsf{X}_{J_{1}}\in A_{J_{1}}\right\}\cap\left\{\mathsf{X}_{J_{2}}\in A_{J_{2}}\right\}\cap\cdots\cap\left\{\mathsf{X}_{J_{m}}\in A_{J_{m}}\right\}\right),

for
J_{1},\ldots,J_{m}\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}
and
A_{J_{1}},\dots,A_{J_{m}}\in\mathfrak{V}
.
By construction, for any measurable
B\in\tilde{\mathfrak{V}}
we have,

\mathbb{P}\left((\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\in B\right)=\mathbb{Q}\left((\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\in B\right).

Thus, from now on we assume that we are working on the canonical space
\left(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathfrak{V}},\mathbb{P}\right)
. We denote by
\mathbb{E}
, the averages taken with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. The following definition is fundamental for our purposes.

Definition 2.2
.

For each
N\geq 1
, we define the
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{E}_{N}
on
(\tilde{\mathcal{V}},\tilde{\mathfrak{V}})
by,

\mathcal{E}_{N}=\left\{A\in\tilde{\mathfrak{V}}:\left(v_{J}\right)_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\in A\Rightarrow\left(v_{\tau(J)}\right)_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\in A\text{
for every }\tau\in\mathsf{Sym}_{N}\right\}

and hence define the exchangeable
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{E}=\bigcap_{N\geq 1}\mathcal{E}_{N}
.

We then have the following natural higher-dimensional extension of De-Finetti’s Theorem
[
63
]
. Surprisingly, as already mentioned in the introduction, we were not able to locate the precise statement in the literature.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}
be an exchangeable array, and let
(J_{l})_{l=1}^{\infty}\subset\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}
be disjoint sets. Then, conditioned on
\mathcal{E}
,
(\mathsf{X}_{J_{l}})_{l=1}^{\infty}
are independent.

We will prove Theorem
2.3
in two steps. First, we will show the claimed result when
\mathcal{E}
is replaced by the tail
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
we define below. Then, we will show that even though
\mathcal{G}\subsetneq\mathcal{E}
, they coincide up to sets of measure zero. For the first step, we need some definitions.

Definition 2.4

(Tail
\sigma
-algebra)
.

Consider the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\to\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)},

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{l}\}

\displaystyle\mapsto\{\gamma_{1}+1,\ldots,\gamma_{l}+1\},

and with a slight abuse of notation, denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{m}\left((\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathcal{S}^{m}(J)})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}.

Then, for each
N\geq 1
, define the auxiliary
\sigma
-algebras:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{N}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sigma\left(\mathcal{S}^{N}\left((\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\right)\right)

and the associated tail
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{N}\mathcal{G}_{N}.

Observe that in proving Theorem
2.3
, there is no loss of generality in assuming that each
J_{l}
is given by

J_{l}=\{j_{l-1}+1,\ldots,j_{l}-1,j_{l}\},

(21)

for some increasing sequence
(j_{l})_{l\geq 1}
. Indeed, if one proves the result for this special form of
J_{l}
’s then using exchangeability, it follows that it holds for any disjoint sequence of
J_{l}
’s.

Next, to simplify the presentation, we define the random vector:

\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left(\mathsf{X}_{I}:I\subset J\right)

for each
J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}
and also write

\mathcal{L}\underset{\mathcal{K}}{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\!\perp}\mathcal{H},

whenever the
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{H}
are independent conditioned on the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{K}
. That is to say, whenever
A\in\mathcal{L},B\in\mathcal{H}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}\mathbf{1}_{B}\mid\mathcal{K}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}\mid\mathcal{K}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B}\mid\mathcal{K}\right].

We also consider the previously defined map
\mathcal{S}
as a map on
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
, defined by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:\tilde{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle\to\tilde{\mathcal{V}},

\displaystyle(v_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}

\displaystyle\mapsto(v_{\mathcal{S}(J)})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(j_{l})_{l=1}^{\infty}
be a strictly increasing sequence of integers and define the corresponding
J_{l}
as in (
21
). Then,
(\mathsf{X}_{J_{l}})_{l=1}^{\infty}
are independent conditioned on
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Firstly, note that by exchangeability, we have, for each
l
and
N\geq j_{l}
, the distributional equality of the random vectors

\left(\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J_{l}},\mathcal{S}^{j_{l}}\left((\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\right)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\left(\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J_{l}},\mathcal{S}^{N}\left((\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}\right)\right),

(with
\overset{\textnormal{d}}{=}
denoting equality in distribution) which, by means of Corollary 8.10 in
[
63
]
, gives that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J_{l}}

\displaystyle\underset{\mathcal{G}_{N}}{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\!\perp}\mathcal{G}_{j_{l}}.

In particular, for every
A\in\sigma(\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J_{l}})
, and all
N\geq j_{l}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf{1}_{A}\mid\mathcal{G}_{j_{l}}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf{1}_{A}

\displaystyle\mid\mathcal{G}_{N}\Bigr]\xrightarrow[\mathrm{a.s.}]{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}\mid\mathcal{G}\right],

where for the equality we used Theorem 8.9 from
[
63
]
. Since the first term appearing above is independent of
N
, we then have that it is precisely equal to the limit, which then implies that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J_{l}}

\displaystyle\underset{\mathcal{G}}{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\!\perp}\mathcal{G}_{j_{l}}.

Hence, applying this iteratively, we get that

\displaystyle\sigma(\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J_{1}},\ldots,\hat{\mathsf{X}}_{J_{l}})

\displaystyle\underset{\mathcal{G}}{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\!\perp}\mathcal{G}_{j_{l}},

for each
l
, from which the result follows.
∎

Next, we show that
\mathcal{G}
coincides with
\mathcal{E}
up to sets of measure
0
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
, thus proving Theorem
2.3
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{E}
. Then, there exists
B\in\mathcal{G}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}B\right)=0,

where
A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}B
denotes the symmetric difference between the sets
A
and
B
.

Proof.

We first claim that for each
A\in\mathcal{E}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-1}(A)\right)=0.

To prove the claim, we start by fixing
\delta>0
and noting that since
A\in\tilde{\mathfrak{V}}
, by means of Lemma 4.16 in
[
63
]
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
and a set
W\in\tilde{\mathfrak{V}}
of the form

W=Z\times\mathcal{V}^{K_{m}}

(22)

where
K_{m}\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}:\max(J)>m\}
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}W\right)<\delta.

Here,
Z
is a Borel subset of
\mathcal{V}^{L_{m}}
where
L_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}\setminus K_{m}
. Next, define the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{m}:\tilde{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle\to\tilde{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle(v_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}

\displaystyle\mapsto(v_{\tau_{m}(J)})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}},

where
\tau_{m}\in\mathsf{Sym}_{m+1}
is the
(m+1)-
cycle:
(m+1\;\;m\;\;\cdots\;\;2\;\;1)
.

Then, note that
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is a bijection such that for any
A\in\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{P}_{m}(A)=A
. In particular, we have

\mathcal{P}_{m}(W)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}A=\mathcal{P}_{m}(W)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{P}_{m}(A)=\mathcal{P}_{m}(W\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}A).

Combining this with the fact that
\mathbb{P}
is invariant under
\mathcal{P}_{m}
, we get that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{P}_{m}(W)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}A\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(W\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}A\right)<\delta.

Now, note that if
J\in L_{m}
, then

\mathcal{S}(J)=\tau_{m}^{-1}(J).

Hence, one easily sees that

\mathcal{S}^{-1}(W)=\Big\{(v_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}:(v_{\mathcal{S}(J)})_{J\in L_{m}}\in Z\Big\}=\Big\{(v_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}:(v_{\tau_{m}^{-1}(J)})_{J\in L_{m}}\in Z\Big\}=\mathcal{P}_{m}(W)

Thus, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}^{-1}(W)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}W\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{P}_{m}(W)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}W\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{P}_{m}(W)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}A\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}W\right)<2\delta.

Combining these, and using that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}^{-1}(E))=\mathbb{P}(E)
for any
E\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, we get that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}^{-1}(A)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}A\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}W\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(W\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-1}(W)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}^{-1}(W)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-1}(A)\right)<4\delta,

which proves the claim since
\delta>0
was arbitrary. Now, define inductively

\mathcal{S}^{-m}(A)\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\mathcal{S}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{S}^{-(m-1)}(A)\right),

and the corresponding set,

B\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\limsup_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{S}^{-m}(A).

Note that,
B\in\mathcal{G}
. Also note that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-m}(A))\leq\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{-(m-1)}(A))+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}^{-(m-1)}(A)\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-m}(A))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-(m-1)}(A))+\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-1}(A))=0,

where we used induction in the last step. Hence,

\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}B)=\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\cap_{n\geq 1}\cup_{m\geq n}\mathcal{S}^{-m}(A))\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(A\boldsymbol{\,\Delta\,}\mathcal{S}^{-m}(A))=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

From now on we assume that
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}
, namely the random variables
\mathsf{X}_{J}
are real-valued.
We use the results above to first prove an analogue of the law of large numbers for statistics of exchangeable arrays (which should not be surpsising), and then give a formula for joint moments of the limiting random variables in terms of finitely many random variables in the array. This general moments formula appears to be new.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}
be an exchangeable array, and let
\mathcal{E}_{N}
,
\mathcal{E}
be defined as before. Then, for each
d\leq k
and fixed
I\in\mathbb{N}^{(d)}
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d!}{N^{d}}\sum_{J\in[N]^{(d)}}\mathsf{X}_{J}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.},\;L^{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{X}_{I}\mid\mathcal{E}\right]

whenever
\mathsf{X}_{I}\in L^{1}
. If we further have
\mathsf{X}_{I}\in L^{p}
for
p>1
then convergence takes place in
L^{p}
as well.

Proof.

Define the partial sums, noting that we may change
\frac{N^{d}}{d!}
to
\binom{N}{d}
without affecting the result,

\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(d)}=\frac{1}{{N\choose d}}\sum_{J\in[N]^{(d)}}\mathsf{X}_{J}.

(23)

Then, note that
\mathsf{T}_{N}
is
\mathcal{E}_{N}
-measurable, and for every
I,L\in[N]^{(d)}
and
A\in\mathcal{E}_{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{X}_{I}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{X}_{L}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right],

so that we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{X}_{I}\mid\mathcal{E}_{N}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{X}_{L}\mid\mathcal{E}_{N}\right].

In particular, we have

\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(d)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(d)}\mid\mathcal{E}_{N}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{I}\mid\mathcal{E}_{N}].

Thus, we see that
\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(d)}
forms a backwards martingale with respect to
\mathcal{E}_{N}
. Combining this with the assumed integrability of
\mathsf{X}_{I}
and using the backwards martingale convergence theorem, the result follows.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}
be an exchangeable array, and define, for
d\leq k
, the random variables

\mathsf{T}^{(d)}\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(d)}

where
\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(d)}
is defined as in (
23
). Then, if for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{l}>0
and
k_{1},\ldots,k_{l}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{X}_{[k_{j}]}\right|^{p}\right]<\infty

for all
j=1,\ldots,l
, where we define
p\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\sum_{j}r_{j}
, then we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{\sum_{j}r_{j}k_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{l})}\right|^{r_{j}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathsf{T}^{(k_{l})}\right|^{r_{j}}\right].

Proof.

Note that under the given conditions, almost sure convergence holds via Proposition
2.7
. Thus, to prove the convergence of moments, it suffices to show that the sequence

\Bigg\{\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{j})}\right|^{r_{j}}\Bigg\}_{N\geq\max(k_{j})}

(24)

is uniformly integrable. To see that this follows from the assumptions, note that first applying Young’s inequality, and then applying Holder’s inequality with exponents
\frac{p}{r_{j}}
in both cases, we get that

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{j})}\right|^{r_{j}}\mathbf{1}\Bigg\{\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{j})}\right|^{r_{j}}\geq R\Bigg\}\right]\leq\sup_{N}\sum_{m=1}^{l}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{j})}\right|^{r_{j}}\mathbf{1}\Bigg\{\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{m})}\right|^{p}\geq\frac{pR}{lr_{m}}\Bigg\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{N}\sum_{m=1}^{l}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{m})}\right|^{p}\mathbf{1}\Bigg\{\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{m})}\right|^{p}\geq\frac{pR}{lr_{m}}\Bigg\}\right]^{r_{m}/p}\prod_{j\neq m}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{j})}\right|^{p}\right]^{r_{j}/p}.

(25)

By Proposition
2.7
, we know that for each
m
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{m})}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}^{(k_{m})}

(26)

and hence, the sequence

\displaystyle\left\{\left|\mathsf{T}_{N}^{(k_{m})}\right|^{p}\right\}

{}_{N\geq\max(k_{j})}

(27)

is uniformly integrable. Thus, taking
R\to\infty
in (
25
), we see that the sequence in (
24
) is also uniformly integrable, concluding the proof.
∎

Next, we give a seemingly new formula for the joint moments of the random variables
\mathsf{T}^{(d)}
in terms of joint moments of finitely many
\mathsf{X}_{J}
’s. Its usefulness will be clear in the sequel.

Proposition 2.9
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
k_{1}>\cdots>k_{l}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
. Define
L=\sum_{j}h_{j}k_{j}
and
p=\sum_{j}h_{j}
. Suppose
\{J_{m}\}_{m=1}^{p}
is a partition of the set
\{1,\ldots,L\}
such that for each
j
, it contains exactly
h_{j}
sets of size
k_{j}
; and further assume that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{X}_{[k_{j}]}\right|^{p}\right]<\infty.

Then, with the random variables
\mathsf{T}^{(d)}
defined as in Proposition
2.8
, we have that,

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(\mathsf{T}^{(k_{j})}\right)^{h_{j}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{m=1}^{p}\mathsf{X}_{J_{m}}\right].

Proof.

Note that combining the integrability assumption with the fact that
J_{m}
are disjoint, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{m=1}^{p}\mathsf{X}_{J_{m}}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{E}\right]=\prod_{m=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{X}_{J_{m}}\mid\mathcal{E}\right]=\prod_{m=1}^{p}\mathsf{T}^{(|J_{m}|)}=\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(\mathsf{T}^{(k_{j})}\right)^{h_{j}}.

Taking the expectation of both sides and using tower law gives the desired result.
∎

2.2

Consistent permutation-invariant ensembles

In this section, we prove a number of new results for consistent permutation-invariant ensembles using the theory of exchangeable arrays discussed in the previous subsection. We restrict our attention to complex Hermitian matrices because this is what we need for our applications to the problem of joint moments. However, essentially all our arguments go through verbatim if we consider ensembles over the reals or quaternions, or if we drop the self-adjoint constraint, as long as we have permutation-invariance. Before we formally define the class of permutation-invariant random matrix ensembles, let us introduce some notation that will be used throughout.

Let
\mathbb{H}(N)
denote the space of
N\times N
Hermitian matrices, equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{H}(N))
and the Lebesgue measure,

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{H}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{H}_{ii}\prod_{i<j}\mathrm{d}\Re\mathbf{H}_{ij}\mathrm{d}\Im\mathbf{H}_{ij}.

Define the projection maps,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}_{N}^{N+1}:\mathbb{H}(N+1)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{H}(N),

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{H}_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1,\dots,N+1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[\mathbf{H}_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1,\dots,N},

mapping each
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{H}(N+1)
to its top-left
N\times N
corner. Next, define
\mathbb{H}(\infty)
to be the projective limit of
\mathbb{H}(N)
under these maps, endowed with the projective limit topology, whose Borel sigma algebra we denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{H}(\infty))
. Finally, consider the corresponding natural projections:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}_{N}^{\infty}:\mathbb{H}(\infty)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{H}(N),

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{H}_{ij}\right]_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[\mathbf{H}_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1,\dots,N}.

We have the following definition.

Definition 2.10
.

A sequence of probability measures
\{\mu_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
, with
\mu_{N}
on
(\mathbb{H}(N),\mathcal{B}\left(\mathbb{H}(N)\right))
, is called a consistent permutation-invariant ensemble if:

•

For each
N\geq 1
,
\tau\in\mathsf{Sym}_{N}
, and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{H}(N))
,

\mu_{N}\left(\mathbf{H}\in B\right)=\mu_{N}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\tau}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_{\tau}^{*}\in B\right),

where recall that
\mathbf{P}_{\tau}
is the permutation matrix associated to
\tau
.

•

For each
N\geq 1
, the pushforward of
\mu_{N+1}
under
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{N}^{N+1}
is
\mu_{N}
.

We denote by
\mu_{\infty}
the unique probability measure on
(\mathbb{H}(\infty),\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{H}(\infty)))
such that for all
N\geq 1
, the pushforward image of
\mu_{\infty}
under
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{N}^{\infty}
is
\mu_{N}
.

Throughout this section, we will be considering arbitrary consistent permutation-invariant ensembles
\{\mu_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
, associated with the measure
\mu_{\infty}
which couples them. We denote expectation with respect to
\mu_{\infty}
by
\mathbb{E}
. We let
\mathbf{H}
denote an infinite random matrix sampled according to
\mu_{\infty}
and also define
\mathbf{H}_{N}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{N}^{\infty}(\mathbf{H})
which is distributed according to
\mu_{N}
. We denote the random eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}_{N}
by
\big(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\big)
; how we order the eigenvalues will not be important as we will only consider symmetric functions of them.

We now prove two general results on such matrices.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If

\mathbb{E}\left[|\det(\mathbf{H}_{k})|\right]<\infty

then there exists a random variable
\mathsf{T}^{(k)}
such that

\frac{k!}{N^{k}}\mathrm{e}_{k}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathsf{T}^{(k)}

almost surely and in
L^{1}
, where
\mathrm{e}_{k}
denotes the
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial. If we further have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\det\left(\mathbf{H}_{k}\right)\right|^{r}\right]<\infty,

for some
r>1
, then convergence takes place in
L^{r}
as well. Furthemore, if
for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{l}>0
and
k_{1},\ldots,k_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\det\left(\mathbf{H}_{k_{j}}\right)\right|^{s}\right]<\infty

for all
1\leq j\leq l
, where
s=\sum r_{j}
, then

\prod_{j=1}^{l}\frac{(k_{j}!)^{r_{j}}}{N^{k_{j}r_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathrm{e}_{k_{j}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)\right|^{r_{j}}\right]\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left|\mathsf{T}^{(k_{j})}\right|^{r_{j}}\right].

(28)

Proof.

We start by defining, for each
J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}
, the random variables

\mathsf{X}_{J}\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{J}\right),

where if
m_{1}<\cdots<m_{d}
are the elements of
J
, with
d\leq k
, we denote:

\mathbf{H}^{J}\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\left[\mathbf{H}_{ij}\right]_{i,j=m_{1},\ldots,m_{d}}.

Then, note that by permutation invariance of the law of
\mathbf{H}
, if
\tau
is any permutation of
\mathbb{N}
fixing all but finitely many elements, then

\mathsf{Law}(\mathbf{H})=\mathsf{Law}(\mathbf{P}_{\tau}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_{\tau}^{*}).

Write
\mathbf{H}_{\tau}=\mathbf{P}_{\tau}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_{\tau}^{*}
. Now, observe that for any
J
described as above, we have that

\mathbf{H}_{\tau}^{J}=\left[\mathbf{H}_{ij}\right]_{i,j=\tau(m_{1}),\ldots,\tau(m_{d})}

so that
\mathbf{H}_{\tau}^{J}
and
\mathbf{H}^{\tau(J)}
are two matrices that can be obtained by one another after a reordering of their indices. In particular, they are equivalent under unitary conjugation and hence have the same determinant. Combining these two observations, we see that
(\mathsf{X}_{J})_{J\in\tilde{\mathbb{N}}^{(k)}}
is an exchangeable array.

Next, observe that the elementary symmetric polynomials in the eigenvalues can be rewritten as:

\frac{d!}{N^{d}}\mathrm{e}_{d}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)=\frac{d!}{N^{d}}\sum_{J\in[N]^{(d)}}\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{J}\right)=\frac{d!}{N^{d}}\sum_{J\in[N]^{(d)}}\mathsf{X}_{J}.

Note that the first equality above easily follows by considering the characteristic polynomial
\det(t\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})
. Indeed, by means of Vieta’s formulas, one easily sees that the
d
-th symmetric polynomial in eigenvalues equals the coefficient of
t^{N-d}
, which then also equals the sum of the determinants given above that one sees by expanding the characteristic polynomial as a sum over the symmetric group. Combining all of these observations, we see that the result now follows by applications of Propositions
2.7
and
2.8
.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
k_{1}\geq\cdots\geq k_{l}
, and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
L=\sum_{j}k_{j}r_{j}
. Then, under the same assumptions as Proposition
2.11
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(\mathsf{T}^{(k_{j})}\right)^{r_{j}}\right]=\frac{(k_{1}!)^{r_{1}}\cdots(k_{l}!)^{r_{l}}}{L!}\sum_{m=k_{1}}^{L}(-1)^{m+L}{L\choose m}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(\mathrm{e}_{k_{j}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(m)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(m)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{m}^{(m)}\right)\right)^{r_{j}}\right].

Proof.

We start by noting that if we let

\displaystyle\left\{\mathrm{I}_{m}^{(j)}\right\}_{j,m}

(29)

be a partition of
[L]
such that for each
j,m
,
\mathrm{I}_{m}^{(j)}
contains
k_{j}
elements; then by means of Proposition
2.9
, we get that our claim amounts to showing:

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j,m}\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{I}_{m}^{(j)}}\right)\right]=\frac{(k_{1}!)^{r_{1}}\cdots(k_{l}!)^{r_{l}}}{L!}\sum_{m=k_{1}}^{L}(-1)^{m+L}{L\choose m}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(\sum_{J\in[m]^{(k_{j})}}\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{J}\right)\right)^{r_{j}}\right].

(30)

To prove the formula above, we will expand the right-hand side of the equation, and use permutation-invariance to note cancellations. With this aim, for each
k\in\{k_{1},\ldots,L\}
define the sets

A_{k}=\Bigg\{\mathcal{J}=\left(J_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,J_{r_{1}}^{(1)},J_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,J_{r_{2}}^{(2)},\ldots,J_{1}^{(l)},\ldots,J_{r_{l}}^{(l)}\right):J_{m}^{(j)}\in[L]^{(k_{j})},\left|\bigcup_{j,m}J_{m}^{(j)}\right|=k\Bigg\}.

Let the symmetric group
\mathsf{Sym}_{L}
act on
A_{k}
such that for any
\tau\in\mathsf{Sym}_{L}
, we have

\tau\left(J_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,J_{r_{1}}^{(1)},J_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,J_{r_{2}}^{(2)},\ldots,J_{1}^{(l)},\ldots,J_{r_{l}}^{(l)}\right)\\
=\left(\tau\left(J_{1}^{(1)}\right),\ldots,\tau\left(J_{r_{1}}^{(1)}\right),\tau\left(J_{1}^{(2)}\right),\ldots,\tau\left(J_{r_{2}}^{(2)}\right),\ldots,\tau\left(J_{1}^{(l)}\right),\ldots,\tau\left(J_{r_{l}}^{(l)}\right)\right).

Next, consider the terms appearing on the expansion of the right-hand side of (
30
) that are given, for any
\mathcal{J}\in\bigcup_{k=k_{1}}^{L}A_{k}
, by:

M_{\mathcal{J}}\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{J_{1}^{(1)}}\right)\cdots\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{J_{r_{1}}^{(1)}}\right)\cdots\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{J_{1}^{(l)}}\right)\cdots\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{J_{r_{l}}^{(l)}}\right)\right].

Then, note that by permutation-invariance we have that for any
\tau\in\mathsf{Sym}_{L}
, the average defining
M_{\mathcal{J}}
remains unchanged if the random matrix
\mathbf{H}
is replaced by
\mathbf{H}_{\tau}=\mathbf{P}_{\tau}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_{\tau}^{*}
. In addition, noting that for any
J\subseteq[L]
,
\mathbf{H}_{\tau}^{J}
and
\mathbf{H}^{\tau(J)}
are two matrices that are equal up to re-ordering of the indices, we see that they are equivalent under unitary conjugation and hence have the same determinant. Combining these two observations, we easily see that for any
\tau\in\mathsf{Sym}_{L}
, we have that
M_{\mathcal{J}}=M_{\tau(\mathcal{J})}
. Next, with a slight abuse of notation, for any
\mathcal{J}\in\bigcup_{k=k_{1}}^{L}A_{k}
, let
\mathsf{Orb}_{m}(\mathcal{J})
denote the number of times an average corresponding to the
\mathsf{Sym}_{L}
-orbit of
\mathcal{J}
appears on the expansion of the
m
-th summand on the right-hand side of (
30
), where
m=k_{1},\ldots,L
. Then, realize that if
\mathcal{J}\in A_{k}
, it follows that the terms of the form
M_{\tau(\mathcal{J})}
as
\tau
runs over
\mathsf{Sym}_{L}
only appear on the expansion of the
m
-th summand on the right-hand side of (
30
) if
k\leq m\leq L
, and we have the equality

\mathsf{Orb}_{m}(\mathcal{J})={m\choose k}\mathsf{Orb}_{k}(\mathcal{J}).

Thus, if
k\neq L
, we have that the contribution coming from the
\mathsf{Sym}_{L}
-orbit of
\mathcal{J}
equals

M_{\mathcal{J}}\mathsf{Orb}_{k}(\mathcal{J})\sum_{m=k}^{L}{L\choose m}(-1)^{m}{m\choose k}=0.

Furthermore, note that if
\mathcal{J}\in A_{L}
, then

\Big\{J_{m}^{(j)}\Big\}_{j,m}
forms a partition of
[L]
. In particular, we get by permutation-invariance that

M_{\mathcal{J}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j,m}\det\left(\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{I}_{m}^{(j)}}\right)\right],

for all such
\mathcal{J}
. Next, observe that the terms corresponding to
A_{L}
only appear in the sum in (
30
) when
m=L
, in which case the coefficient that multiplies the average is simply
1
. Hence, combining these observations with the simple combinatorial formula

\left|A_{L}\right|=\frac{L!}{(k_{1}!)^{r_{1}}\cdots(k_{l}!)^{r_{l}}},

gives the desired result.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Proposition
2.12
can be generalised to the setting of arbitrary real-valued exchangeable arrays and the proof is the same. In the final formula one simply replaces
\mathrm{e}_{k_{j}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(m)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(m)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{m}^{(m)}\right)
by
\sum_{J\in[m]^{(k_{j})}}\mathsf{X}_{J}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

This is a direct consequence of Propositions
2.11
and
2.12
above by virtue of Vieta’s formulae.
∎

3

Proofs of convergence and explicit formulae

3.1

Cauchy ensemble and convergence of moments

The main results of this section will be obtained by using various results from the last section on consistent permutation-invariant ensembles. The specific ensemble that will be used here is called the Cauchy
[
47
,
85
]
(also Hua-Pickrell
[
26
]
) random matrix ensemble, which is a sequence of probability measures
\mu_{N}
on
\mathbb{H}(N)
given by

\mu_{N}^{(s)}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{H})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\mathrm{const}\cdot\det\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}^{2}\right)^{-s-N}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{H},

where
s\geq 0
, and the explicit expression of the normalization constant will be ommitted as it will not play a role in what follows. Note that, via Weyl’s integration formula, one can compute that the eigenvalue distribution of a random matrix
\mathbf{H}
sampled according to
\mu_{N}^{(s)}
is given by
{\bf M}_{N}^{(s)}
from the introduction as defined in (
9
).

The reason these measures are closely related to the Haar measure on
\mathbb{U}(N)
is that under the transformation
x_{j}=\cot(\theta_{j}/2)
, averages over the eigenvalues of a Haar-unitary random matrix can be written as an average against
{\bf M}_{N}^{(s)}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
n_{1}>\cdots>n_{k}\geq 0
be integers. Let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in(0,\infty)
and
s=\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}
. Then, we have the following expression for
\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})}{\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(0)}(s)}={2^{-\sum_{j=1}^{k}2h_{j}n_{j}}}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left|\Xi_{n_{j}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)\right|^{2h_{j}}\right],

and for
\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})}{{\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(0)}(s)}}={2^{-\sum_{j=1}^{k}2h_{j}n_{j}}}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{j}=0\end{subarray}}^{n_{j}}(-\textnormal{i})^{m_{j}}\widetilde{\Xi}_{n_{j}-m_{j}}\right|^{2h_{j}}\right],

where the polynomials
\Xi_{m}
and
\widetilde{\Xi}_{m}
are given by,

\displaystyle\Xi_{n_{j}}(x_{1},x_{2}\ldots,x_{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j}}a_{n_{j},l}\mathrm{e}_{l}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}),

(31)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Xi}_{n_{j}-m_{j}}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})

\displaystyle=N^{m_{j}}\binom{n_{j}}{m_{j}}\Xi_{n_{j}-m_{j}}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N}),

with the constants
a_{n,l}
given as follows,

a_{n,l}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(-1)^{\frac{n+l}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}=n\\
\textnormal{only}~m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{l}~\textnormal{are}~\textnormal{odd~integers}\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N}}.

(32)

Proof.

By the definition of
Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
, we have

Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)=(-2)^{N}\prod_{n=1}^{N}\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_{n}}{2}\right).

(33)

Note that, for
l\geq 1
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{l}}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{l}}\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_{n}}{2}\right)=2^{-l}(-1)^{\frac{l-1}{2}}\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_{n}}{2}\right)\cot\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_{n}}{2}\right)

when
l
is an odd integer; and

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{l}}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{l}}\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_{n}}{2}\right)=2^{-l}(-1)^{\frac{l}{2}}\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_{n}}{2}\right)

when
l
is an even integer. So we can compute:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{(n_{j})}Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{n_{j}}}\Big|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=(-2)^{N}\sum_{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{N}=n_{j}}\binom{n_{j}}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N}}\prod_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m_{n}}\sin(\frac{\theta-\theta_{n}}{2})}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{m_{n}}}\Big|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\mathbf{A}}(0)}{2^{n_{j}}}\sum_{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{N}=n_{j}}\binom{n_{j}}{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
m_{n}~\text{is~even}\end{subarray}}^{N}(-1)^{\frac{m_{n}}{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
m_{n}~\text{is~odd}\end{subarray}}^{N}(-1)^{\frac{m_{n}+1}{2}}\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{n}}{2}\right).

(34)

Note that, if
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{N}=n_{j}
, then
\#\{i:m_{i}~\text{is~odd}\}\leq n_{j}
. Hence, the above can be simplified to

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{(n_{j})}Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{n_{j}}}\Big|_{\theta=0}=\frac{Z_{\mathbf{A}}(0)}{2^{n_{j}}}\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\dots,i_{l}=1\\
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\end{subarray}}^{N}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{l}}\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{i_{1}}}{2}\right)\cdots\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{i_{l}}}{2}\right),

(35)

where the constants
c_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}}
are given by,

c_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}}=(-1)^{\frac{n_{j}+l}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}=n_{j}\\
\text{only}~m_{i_{1}},m_{i_{2}},\ldots,m_{i_{l}}~\text{are}~\text{odd~integers}\end{subarray}}\binom{n_{j}}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N}}.\

(36)

But then one immediately sees that
c_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}}=a_{n_{j},l}
for any
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}
so that we have,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{(n_{j})}Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{n_{j}}}\Big|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\mathbf{A}}(0)}{2^{n_{j}}}\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j}}a_{n_{j},l}\mathrm{e}_{l}\Big(\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\right),\ldots,\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{N}}{2}\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\mathbf{A}}(0)}{2^{n_{j}}}\Xi_{n_{j}}\left(\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\right),\ldots,\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{N}}{2}\right)\right).

Next, by applying Weyl’s integration formula for the Haar measure on
\mathbb{U}(N)
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\big|Z_{\mathbf{A}}^{(n_{j})}(0)\big|^{2h_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\sum_{j=1}^{k}2h_{j}n_{j}}(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{N}}\prod_{1\leq j<m\leq N}|e^{{\textnormal{i}}\theta_{j}}-e^{{\textnormal{i}}\theta_{m}}|^{2}\prod_{n=1}^{N}|1-e^{{\textnormal{i}}\theta_{n}}|^{2s}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg|\Xi_{n_{j}}\left(\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\right),\ldots,\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{N}}{2}\right)\right)\Bigg|^{2h_{j}}\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\theta_{N}.

Then, applying the transformation
x_{j}=\cot(\theta_{j}/2)
gives that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})=\frac{\mathrm{K}_{N}(s)}{2^{\sum_{j=1}^{k}2h_{j}n_{j}}}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\Bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg|\Xi_{n_{j}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)\Bigg|^{2h_{j}}\Bigg],

for some constant
\mathrm{K}_{N}(s)
that is independent of
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
. Plugging in
h_{j}=0
for all
j
to both sides of the equation now gives the desired result for
\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
. To get the second part, simply note that

V_{\mathbf{A}}^{(n_{j})}(0)=e^{-\textnormal{i}N\frac{\pi}{2}+\textnormal{i}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\theta_{n}}{2}}\sum_{m_{j}=0}^{n_{j}}\binom{n_{j}}{m_{j}}\left(-\frac{\textnormal{i}N}{2}\right)^{m_{j}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{(n_{j}-m_{j})}Z_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{n_{j}-m_{j}}}\Bigg|_{\theta=0},

which gives the desired conclusion.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The fact that
\{\mu_{N}^{(s)}\}_{N\geq 1}
forms a consistent permutation-invariant (in fact it is unitarily invariant) ensemble was shown in Proposition 3.1 in
[
26
]
. We write
\mu_{\infty}^{(s)}
for the corresponding measure coupling them on infinite matrices, with respect to which all almost-sure statements below are understood. Now, using the fact that

\mathbb{E}^{(s)}\left[\left|\det\left(\mathbf{H}_{n_{j}}\right)\right|^{2s}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{n_{j}}^{(s)}\left[\prod_{m=1}^{n_{j}}\left|\mathsf{x}_{m}^{(n_{j})}\right|^{2s}\right]<\infty,

where
\mathbb{E}^{(s)}
denotes averages taken with respect to
\mu_{\infty}^{(s)}
,
we obtain, by means of Proposition
2.11
, that there exist random variables
\{\widetilde{\mathsf{Y}}_{n}(s)\}_{n\geq 0}
with
\widetilde{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}(s)=1
, such that

\frac{\Xi_{n_{j}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)}{N^{n_{j}}}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\widetilde{\mathsf{Y}}_{n_{j}}(s)

in
L^{2s}\big(\mu_{\infty}^{(s)}\big)
and also almost surely. Here, we crucially use the fact that

a_{n_{j},l}=\begin{cases}O\left(N^{\frac{n_{j}-l}{2}}\right),&\text{ if }n_{j}-l\text{
is even},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise},\par\end{cases}

and
a_{n_{j},n_{j}}=(-1)^{n_{j}}(n_{j})!
. Hence, by combining Proposition
3.1
with the asymptotic equivalence, see for example
[
67
]
,

\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(0)}(s)=\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(0)}(s)\sim N^{s^{2}}\frac{G(s+1)^{2}}{G(2s+1)}

and arguing as in Proposition
2.8
, we obtain convergence of all joint moments

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})}{N^{s^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}h_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{G(s+1)^{2}}{G(2s+1)}2^{-2\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}n_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left|\widetilde{\mathsf{Y}}_{n_{j}}(s)\right|^{2h_{j}}\right],

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})}{N^{s^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}h_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{G(s+1)^{2}}{G(2s+1)}2^{-2\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}n_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg|\sum_{m_{j}=0}^{n_{j}}(-\textnormal{i})^{m_{j}}\binom{n_{j}}{m_{j}}\widetilde{\mathsf{Y}}_{n_{j}-m_{j}}(s)\Bigg|^{2h_{j}}\right].

It only remains to identify the random variables
\widetilde{\mathsf{Y}}_{j}(s)
with the random variables
\mathsf{Y}_{j}(s)
from Definition
1.3
. For this we make use of some results from the integrable probability literature. Observe that, for all
j\geq 1
, we have the almost sure limits,

\widetilde{\mathsf{Y}}_{j}(s)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}j!\mathrm{e}_{j}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)}{N^{j}}.

(37)

It will then suffice, by the definition of the
\mathsf{Y}_{j}(s)
and Newton’s identities, to show that, for any
M\geq 1
, we have the convergence in distribution,

\left(N^{-j}\mathrm{p}_{j}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)\right)_{j=1}^{M}\overset{\textnormal{d}}{\longrightarrow}\left(\mathsf{q}_{j}(s)\right)_{j=1}^{M}.

(38)

It is known, by combining the results of
[
26
,
79
]
, that, with
\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\dots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)
distributed according to
{\bf M}_{N}^{(s)}
, and with the convention
{\mathsf{x}}_{i}^{(N)}\equiv 0
for
i<1
or
i>N
,

\displaystyle\left(\left(N^{-1}\max\left\{\mathsf{x}_{i}^{(N)},0\right\}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty},\left(N^{-1}\max\left\{-\mathsf{x}_{N+1-i}^{(N)},0\right\}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty},N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{x}_{i}^{(N)},N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathsf{x}_{i}^{(N)}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{d}}{\longrightarrow}\left(\left(\alpha_{i}^{+}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty},\left(\alpha_{i}^{-}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty},\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\left|\alpha_{i}^{\pm}\right|\geq k^{-2}}\left(\alpha_{i}^{+}-\alpha_{i}^{-}\right),\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left[\left(\alpha_{i}^{+}\right)^{2}+\left(\alpha_{i}^{-}\right)^{2}\right]\right),

(39)

(convergence in the product-space in (
39
) is coordinate-wise) where
\left\{\alpha_{i}^{+}\right\}\sqcup\left\{-\alpha_{i}^{-}\right\}\overset{\textnormal{d}}{=}\mathbf{P}^{(s)}
. Note that, this completely determines the law of the right hand side in (
39
), as the
\left(\alpha_{i}^{+}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}
,
\left(\alpha_{i}^{-}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}
are known to be strictly ordered, see
[
79
]
. Finally, by virtue of (
39
), along with Proposition 2.3 of
[
17
]
, which then implies that, for
k\geq 3
,
N^{-k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathsf{x}_{i}^{(N)}\right)^{k}
converges to
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\alpha_{i}^{+}\right)^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(-\alpha_{i}^{-}\right)^{k}
. We obtain (
38
) which concludes the proof.
∎

3.2

Explicit formulae

Next, we apply the combinatorial formulae proved in Section
2
for the even-integer moments of the limiting random variables to our current setting. Via this representation, we further deduce that our random variables
\{\mathsf{Y}_{n}(s)\}_{n\geq 1}
are non-trivial, which implies that Theorem
1.4
not only gives an upper bound, but rather gives a sharp order of growth of the joint moments, as expected.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

The first part of the result is a direct application of Proposition
2.12
. The last claim immediately follows from the formula combined with the fact that for any
k
and non-negative integers
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k}
,

s\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{k}^{(s)}\left[\sum_{\tau\in\mathsf{Sym}_{k}}\left(\mathsf{x}_{\tau(1)}^{(k)}\right)^{r_{1}}\left(\mathsf{x}^{(k)}_{\tau(2)}\right)^{r_{2}}\cdots\left(\mathsf{x}^{(k)}_{\tau(k)}\right)^{r_{k}}\right]

is a rational function, which can be shown by an elementary computation.
∎

The rationality claim of Theorem
1.5
turns out to be very useful in proving Corollary
1.6
. The strategy to prove the result above will be as follows: we will initially restrict to
s\in\mathbb{N}
and compute the averages in (
11
) by calculating the corresponding integrals over
\mathbb{U}(N)
and taking
N\to\infty
. We will then show that the expressions we obtain extend to rational functions, which, when combined with Theorem
1.5
, will give us two rational functions that agree on integers, which then implies they must agree everywhere. We will then show that the explicit expressions that appear in (
11
) are strictly positive, and conclude the final claim of the corollary.

To complete the aforementioned first step, we state and prove a sequence of lemmas regarding certain averages of eigenvalues of Haar-unitary random matrices. Recall that via Weyl’s integration formula, it is known that if
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
denote the eigenvalues of a Haar-unitary random matrix, ordered so that

\lambda_{j}=e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{j}}\;\;\;\textnormal{ with }\;\;\;\theta_{j}\in[0,2\pi),\;\;\;\theta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{N},

then the law of the
\theta_{j}
’s is given by:

\mathrm{d}\nu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\left|e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{j}}-e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{k}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\theta_{N}.

Thus, for the rest of this section, we denote

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\left[f(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int f\left(e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{N}}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N}).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
s,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, as
N\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{F}_{N}^{(n,0)}(1,s-1)}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial z_{1}^{n}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial w_{1}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{s}(z_{i}w_{i})^{-\frac{N}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{s}(\lambda_{j}-z_{i})(\bar{\lambda}_{j}-w_{i})\right]\Bigg|_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}=\cdots=z_{s}=1\\
w_{1}=\cdots=w_{s}=1\end{subarray}},

(40)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{G}_{N}^{(n,0)}(1,s-1)}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial z_{1}^{n}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial w_{1}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{s}(\lambda_{j}-z_{i})(\bar{\lambda}_{j}-w_{i})\right]\Bigg|_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}=\cdots=z_{s}=1\\
w_{1}=\cdots=w_{s}=1\end{subarray}},

(41)

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
are the random eigenvalues of a Haar-distributed
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{U}(N)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{U}(N)
and let
(e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{N}})=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
denote its eigenvalues. Then, define the characteristic polynomial, this time as a function on the unit circle instead of
[0,2\pi)
by:

\widetilde{V}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-ze^{-\textnormal{i}\theta_{j}})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-z\bar{\lambda}_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\lambda}_{j}(\lambda_{j}-z)

and its corresponding rescaled version:

\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)=e^{-\textnormal{i}N\frac{\pi}{2}+\textnormal{i}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\theta_{n}}{2}}z^{-\frac{N}{2}}\widetilde{V}_{\mathbf{A}}(z).

Then, direct computation gives that:

\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{A}^{*}}^{(n)}(1)=(-1)^{N}\overline{\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(n)}(1)}.

In particular, one may compute,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial z_{1}^{n}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial w_{1}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{s}(z_{i}w_{i})^{-\frac{N}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{s}(\lambda_{j}-z_{i})(\bar{\lambda}_{j}-w_{i})\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}=\cdots=z_{s}=1\\
w_{1}=\cdots=w_{s}=1\end{subarray}}

Lemma 3.3
.

For every

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}N!}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{s}(z_{m}-e^{i\theta_{j}})\right)\left(\prod_{m=1}^{s}(\overline{w}_{m}-e^{-\textnormal{i}\theta_{j}})\right)\Delta(e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{\textnormal{i}\theta_{N}})\Delta(e^{-\textnormal{i}\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{-\textnormal{i}\theta_{N}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

Lemma 3.4
.

For

By Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{s}(\lambda_{j}-z_{i})(\bar{\lambda}_{j}-w_{i})\right]=\frac{\det_{1\leq i,j\leq s}\left[\sum_{l=0}^{N+s-1}(z_{i}w_{j})^{l}\right]}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq s}(z_{j}-z_{i})\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq s}(w_{j}-w_{i})}.

Thus, letting

\displaystyle\lim_{w_{s-1}\rightarrow 1}\lim_{z_{s-1}\rightarrow 1}\frac{\det_{1\leq i,j\leq s}\left[\sum_{l=0}^{N+s-1}(z_{i}w_{j})^{l}\right]}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq s-1}(z_{j}-z_{i})\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq s-1}(w_{j}-w_{i})\prod_{1\leq i\leq s-1}(1-z_{i})(1-w_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq s-2}(z_{j}-z_{i})(w_{j}-w_{i})\prod_{1\leq i\leq s-2}(1-z_{i})^{2}(1-w_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\times\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{s-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{s-1}}\det_{1\leq i,j\leq s}\left[\sum_{l=0}^{N+s-1}(z_{i}w_{j})^{l}\right]\Bigg|_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{s-1}=w_{s}=1\\

where

. Thus, if one shows that the right-hand side of the above equation extends to a rational function on

\displaystyle\det_{1\leq i,j\leq s}\left[\frac{1}{p_{i}+p_{j}+1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq s}(p_{j}-p_{i})^{2}}{\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq s}(p_{i}+p_{j}+1)}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2s-1+2n}\prod_{i=2}^{s}\frac{(n+i-1)^{2}}{(2s+n-i)^{2}}\frac{\prod_{i=2}^{s}((s-i)!)^{2}}{\prod_{2\leq i,j\leq s}(2s+1-i-j)}.

Substituting this into the above expression and simplifying it, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[\Bigg|\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-\textnormal{i})^{m}\binom{n}{m}\mathsf{Y}_{n-m}(s)\Bigg|^{2}\right]=2^{2n}\frac{2s-1}{2s-1+2n}\prod_{l=1}^{n}\left(\frac{l+s-1}{l+2s-2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial z_{1}^{n}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial w_{1}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}(N)}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{s}(z_{i}w_{i})^{-\frac{N}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{s}(\lambda_{j}-z_{i})(\bar{\lambda}_{j}-w_{i})\right]\Bigg|_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}=\cdots=z_{s}=1\\

Hence, arguing similarly as above, we obtain the first equality in (

4.1

As explained in the introduction, Theorem

Lemma 4.1
.

Proof.

Let

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{e^{-\textnormal{i}\frac{t_{1}}{N}x_{j}}}{\left(1+x_{j}^{2}\right)^{s+N}}\Delta^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|\leq\frac{1}{N^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\Bigg|^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\left(1+x_{j}^{2}\right)^{l+N}}\Delta^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

for

and also that

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{q=2}^{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(x_{j}-\textnormal{i})^{q}\right)^{n_{q}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}

Now, observe that proving (

. Thus, using the bounds for the integrals above and the fact that

Computing the derivative explicitly gives the desired bound immediately.

4.3

Hankel determinants shifted by partitions

Following the strategy sketched earlier we define the Hankel determinants:

\Psi_{N}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\det_{0\leq i,j\leq N-1}\left[\sum_{m_{2},\dots,m_{k}=0}^{\infty}\frac{t_{2}^{m_{2}}\cdots t_{k}^{m_{k}}}{m_{2}!\cdots m_{k}!}\phi_{i+j+\sum_{l=2}^{k}lm_{l}}(t_{1})\right].

(45)

Next, to make the presentation clearer, we fix some notation that will be used throughout the rest of this section.
Firstly, let

\psi_{\gamma}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\sum_{m_{2},\ldots,m_{k}=0}^{\infty}\frac{t_{2}^{m_{2}}\cdots t_{k}^{m_{k}}}{m_{2}!\cdots m_{k}!}\phi_{\gamma+\sum_{l=2}^{k}lm_{l}}(t_{1}),

for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
.
Indeed, since we are mostly concerned about the
N\to\infty
behaviour of these determinants, it becomes more natural to consider the determinant given in (
45
) with the replacement
t_{1}\rightarrow{t_{1}}/{N},\ldots,t_{k}\rightarrow{t_{k}}/{N}
. Hence, we introduce

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\psi_{\gamma}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{N},\ldots,\frac{t_{k}}{N}\right),

and accordingly define,

\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\det_{0\leq i,j\leq N-1}\Big[\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i+j}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\Big].

Note that these functions are all well-defined due to Lemma
4.4
. Next, we define shifted and weighted versions of the Hankel matrix whose determinant defines
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}
. For each integer partition
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}>0)
, we let

\mathbf{A}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\left[\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i+j+\lambda_{N-j}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\right]_{i,j=0,\ldots,N-1},

where if
p\leq N-1
, we let

\lambda_{p+1}=\cdots=\lambda_{N}=0.
When
p>N
, we define
\mathbf{A}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=0
.
Accordingly, we let

\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\det[\mathbf{A}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}].

Note that in the special case,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\emptyset=(0,0,\ldots,0)
, one has
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\emptyset}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}
.
Related to these matrices, we also define their weighted version:

{\mathbf{A}}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(w)}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\left[(i+j+\lambda_{N-j})\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i+j+\lambda_{N-j}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\right]_{i,j=0,\ldots,N-1},

and let

\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(w)}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\det[\mathbf{A}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(w)}].

Finally, we define the main objects that will allow us to obtain recurrences for derivatives of
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, which will become very crucial for our induction argument.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
For each integer partition
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p})
with
p\leq N
, define its translated version:

\mathcal{S}_{h}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1}+h,\ldots,\lambda_{p}+h,h,\ldots,h).

Hence, we define the functions
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda},h}
by,

\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda},h}(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{A}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\mathbf{A}_{N,\mathcal{S}_{h}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right],

and
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda},h}^{(w)}
by,

\mathbf{\Psi}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda},h}^{(w)}(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{adj}\left({{\mathbf{A}}}_{N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right){\mathbf{A}}_{N,\mathcal{S}_{h}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(w)}\right],

where
\mathrm{adj}
denotes the adjugate of a matrix.

It turns out that in most of our computations we will have expressions which involve a certain distinguished partition.
These partitions are defined as follows:

Definition 4.6
.

Let
1\leq q\leq n
. Define the partition
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n,q}
of
n
to be the partition
(n-q+1,1,\ldots,1)
where the number of
1
’s that appear at the end is
q-1
.

In what follows, we will first show certain recursive and differential relations satisfied by the functions
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma}
, and then leverage these to obtain relations between derivatives of
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}
and its shifted versions defined above.

Lemma 4.7
.

For each
\gamma\geq 0

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+1}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma}}{2}-\frac{N}{2}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma}}{\partial t_{1}},

and also

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N+s-1-\gamma)}{2(t_{1}+t_{2})}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma}+\left(\frac{N(2-2N-2s+\gamma)}{2(t_{1}+t_{2})}+\frac{t_{1}}{t_{1}+t_{2}}\right)\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+1}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(t_{1}+t_{2})}\sum_{q=3}^{k}\big((q-1)t_{q-1}-qt_{q}\big)\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+q}+\frac{kt_{k}}{2(t_{1}+t_{2})}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+1+k}.

Proof.

This follows from the properties of the confluent hypergeometric functions

\displaystyle(b-a-1)\mathsf{U}

\displaystyle(a,b-1;t)-(b-1+t)\mathsf{U}(a,b;t)+t\mathsf{U}(a,b+1;t)=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{U}(a,b;t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathsf{U}(a,b;t)-\mathsf{U}(a,b+1;t),

which can be found in
[

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+2}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N+s-1-\gamma)}{2t_{1}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})-\frac{1}{2t_{1}}\sum_{q=2}^{k}qt_{q}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{N(2-2N-2s+\gamma)}{2t_{1}}+1\right)\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+1}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})+\frac{1}{2t_{1}}\sum_{q=2}^{k}qt_{q}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\gamma+1+q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}).

Now, note that the only term

appearing on the right-hand side above is the

Proof of Theorem
1.11
.

First, note that by an application of Lemma
4.1
, the differential equality (
15
) holds for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
s>2^{-1}(\sum_{q=2}^{k}qn_{q}-1)
, so that it now suffices to prove that for all
t_{1}\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{\sum_{m=2}^{k}mn_{m}}}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\left[e^{-\textnormal{i}\frac{t_{1}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathsf{x}^{(N)}_{j}}\prod_{m=2}^{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathsf{x}^{(N)}_{j}-\textnormal{i})^{m}\right)^{n_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\textnormal{i}t_{1}\mathsf{q}_{1}(s)}\prod_{m=2}^{k}\left(\mathsf{q}_{m}(s)\right)^{n_{m}}\right],

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{m}}\mathbb{E}_{N}^{(s)}\left[e^{-\textnormal{i}t_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\mathsf{x}^{(N)}_{j}}{N}}\right]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\textnormal{i}t_{1}\mathsf{q}_{1}(s)}\right].

The second limit follows immediately, for any
m<2s+1
, from our results in Section
3
. To prove the first one, on the other hand, note that one can write the power sums in terms of a combination of smaller power sums and elementary symmetric polynomials via Newton’s formula:

\mathrm{p}_{m}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=(-1)^{m-1}m\mathrm{e}_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-1+j}\mathrm{e}_{m-j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mathrm{p}_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Hence, using the convergence results from Section
3
for
\frac{1}{N^{k}}\mathrm{e}_{k}(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)})
and applying the identity above iteratively, we see that for all
m=2,\ldots,k
,

\frac{\mathrm{p}_{m}\left(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\mathsf{x}_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)}\right)}{N^{m}}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathsf{q}_{m}(s),

almost surely and in
L^{n_{m}}\Big(\mu_{\infty}^{(s)}\Big)
. In particular, the relation above still holds if
\mathrm{p}_{m}(\mathsf{x}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{N}^{(N)})
is replaced by
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\mathsf{x}^{(N)}_{j}-{\textnormal{i}}\right)^{m}
. Thus, arguing as in the proof of Proposition
2.8
we see that (
72
) indeed holds, where the polynomials
\mathcal{A}_{m}^{(s)}(t_{1})
are given by,

\mathcal{A}_{m}^{(s)}(t_{1})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{P_{m}^{(s,N)}(t_{1})}{N^{\sum_{q=2}^{k}(q-1)n_{q}}}.

This proves the result, except for the justification for the
t_{1}
-degrees of the polynomials
t_{1}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}_{m}^{(s)}(t_{1})
being at most
\sum_{q=2}^{k}(q-1)n_{q}-1
(which is smaller than the maximal degree that is given immediately by taking the limit of Theorem
1.9
). This is simply due to the fact that certain terms vanish while taking the limit. To be more precise, if one writes the expansion in
N
,

\mathcal{L}_{N,l,q}^{(n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})}(t_{1})=N^{l+\sum_{m=2}^{k}(m-1)n_{m}}\mathcal{M}_{N,l,q}^{(n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})}(t_{1})+O(N^{l+\sum_{m=2}^{k}(m-1)n_{m}-1}),

for each
1\leq q\leq l
, (and do the same for
\mathcal{L}_{N}^{(n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})}
)
then by comparing the leading order coefficients in
N
in Propositions
4.13
and
4.15
, one obtains analogous relations for
\mathcal{M}_{N,l,q}^{(n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})}
. Then, carefully running the same inductive process gives that the polynomials in
t_{1}
contained in the terms
\mathcal{M}_{N,l,q}^{(n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})}
have the desired degrees that are stated in Theorem
1.11
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
