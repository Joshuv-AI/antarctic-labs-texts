The Non-Cancelling Intersections Conjecture
Antoine Amarilli LTCI, Télécom Paris, Institut Polytechnique de Paris a3nm@a3nm.net, Mikaël Monet Université de Lille, CNRS, Inria, UMR 9189 - CRIStAL, F-59000 Lille, France mikael.monet@inria.fr, Dan Suciu University of Washington suciu@cs.washington.edu

Abstract
In this note, we present a conjecture on intersections of set families, and a rephrasing of the conjecture in terms of principal downsets of Boolean lattices. The conjecture informally states that, whenever we can express the measure of a union of sets in terms of the measure of some of their intersections using the inclusion-exclusion formula, then we can express the union as a set from these same intersections via the set operations of disjoint union and subset complement. We also present a partial result towards establishing the conjecture.

1
Introduction

We present in this note a conjecture on intersection lattices of set
families, which can be equivalently stated on the Boolean lattice.
The original motivation for the conjecture comes from a problem in database
theory about the existence of certain circuit representations for
probabilistic query evaluation (see
[
Monet, 2020a
]
), but in this note
we present the conjecture as a purely abstract claim without any database
prerequisites. A positive answer to this abstract conjecture implies a positive answer to
the database theory conjecture.

The conjecture can be understood in terms of the inclusion-exclusion formula.
Consider a family of sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
. The inclusion-exclusion formula
can be used to express a quantity on the union
S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{n}
(e.g.,
the cardinality, or the value of some measure) as a function of
the intersections of the
S_{i}
.
In general, some of these intersections may in fact be identical: we can in
fact define the
intersection lattice
of the set family to represent the
possible intersections that can be obtained. Further, we can
have
cancellations
, i.e., some of the possible intersections may end up
having a coefficient of zero in the inclusion-exclusion formula, due to
cancellations. Thus, in the general case, inclusion-exclusion allows us to
express the measure of
S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{n}
in terms of the measure of the
intersections
I_{1},\ldots,I_{m}
that have a non-zero coefficient.

Our conjecture asks whether, in this case, one can obtain the
set

S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{n}
from the intersections
I_{1},\ldots,I_{m}
, using the set
operations of disjoint union and subset-complement. If this is true, then it
implies in particular the inclusion-exclusion formulation, provided that our
measure is additive in the sense that the measure of
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}S^{\prime}
(where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}
denotes disjoint union and

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}
subset complement, as per Definition
3.1
) is the sum
of the measure of
S
and of
S^{\prime}
. Obtaining
such a set expression can sometimes be done simply by re-ordering the
inclusion-expression formula: for instance, if we write
|X\cup Y|=|X|+|Y|-|X\cap Y|
, we can reorder to
|X\cup Y|=|X|-|X\cap Y|+|Y|
, and then express
X\cup Y=(X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}(X\cap Y))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}Y
. However, in general, it is unclear whether such an expression can be
obtained.
Before continuing, let us give a first toy example.

Example 1.1
.

Consider the set family
\mathcal{F}=\{S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\}
with
S_{1}=\{a,b,d\}
,
S_{2}=\{a,b,c,e\}
,

S_{3}=\{a,c,f\}
. Then:

\displaystyle|S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup S_{3}|

\displaystyle=|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|+|S_{3}|

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-(|S_{1}\cap S_{2}|+|S_{1}\cap S_{3}|+|S_{2}\cap S_{3}|)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+|S_{1}\cap S_{2}\cap S_{3}|.

Since we have
S_{1}\cap S_{3}=S_{1}\cap S_{2}\cap S_{3}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup S_{3}|

\displaystyle=|\{a,b,d\}|+|\{a,b,c,e\}|+|\{a,c,f\}|-|\{a,b\}|-|\{a,c\}|.

The non-cancelling intersections are the ones that remain, i.e.,
\{a,b,d\}
,

\{a,b,c,e\}
,
\{a,c,f\}
,
\{a,b\}
, and
\{a,c\}
, while
\{a\}
(
=S_{1}\cap S_{3}=S_{1}\cap S_{2}\cap S_{3}
) is a cancelling term.

Then, we can express
S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup S_{3}=\{a,b,c,d,e,f\}
with
\big[\big((\{a,b,d\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\{a,b\})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}\{a,b,c,e\}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\{a,c\}\big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}\{a,c,f\}
: the reader can easily check that each

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}
(resp., each
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}
) is a valid disjoint union (resp., subset
complement), and that we have only used the non-cancelling intersections. Note
that this is not the only valid expression, for instance we can also obtain the
union with the expression
[\{a,b,d\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\{a,b\}]\cup[\{a,b,c,e\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\{a,c\}]\cup\{a,c,f\}
.

Our results imply that it is always possible when there are no cancellations
(Fact
4.8
), or exactly one cancellation
(in the equivalent formulation on Boolean lattices, Theorem
6.2
), but in general it seem challenging to do so while
avoiding those intersections which cancel out.
The goal of this note is to present the current status of our efforts in
attacking the conjecture, in particular showcasing some equivalent formulations,
presenting examples, and establishing a very partial result. After presenting
preliminaries in Section
2
, we formally state the conjecture and
give examples in Section
3
. We show in Section
4
that
it suffices to study the conjecture on specific intersection lattices, which we
call
tight
, where informally the set family ensures that every possible
intersection contains exactly one element that is “specific” to this
intersection (in the sense that it is present precisely in this intersection and
in larger intersections). We use this in Section
5
to give an
alternative formulation: instead of working with intersection lattices, we can
work with downsets on the Boolean lattice. We show that this is equivalent to
the original conjecture. The hope is that the setting of the Boolean lattice can
be more convenient to work with, as its structure is more restricted, and we can
define quantities such as the Euler characteristic that seem useful in
understanding the structure of the problem.

We next present in Section
6
a partial result that we can show
in the context of the Boolean lattice. In the rephrased problem, we must express
a downset of that lattice using disjoint union and subset complement on
principal downsets spanned by elements which do not “cancel out” in the sense
of having non-zero Möbius value. Our result establishes that the rephrased conjecture is true
when there is one single node of the downset that has such value; this non-trivially
extends the fact that the result is true when no such nodes exist
(Lemma
5.7
), but falls short of the goal as
in general many such zeroes can occur.

We conclude in Section
7
with further questions and
directions on the conjecture. We point out that the conjecture can be
strengthened, in two different ways, to restrict the shape of the disjoint-union
and subset-complement expressions: we also do not know the status of these
stronger conjectures. We briefly report on an unsuccessful experimental search
for counterexamples. We also hint at an incomplete proof of another partial
result where we can avoid more than one zero, provided the targeted downset
satisfies a certain decomposability condition.

2
Preliminaries

For a set
S
we write
2^{S}
its powerset.
In this work, by
family of sets
, or simply
set family
, we always mean
a finite set of (not necessarily finite) sets. We generally use
cursive letters to denote set families, uppercase letters to denote sets, and lowercase letters for elements of sets.
For a set family
\mathcal{F}
(resp., non-empty set family
\mathcal{F}
) we write
\bigcup\mathcal{F}
(resp.,
\bigcap\mathcal{F}
) for
\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{F}}X
(resp.,
\bigcap_{X\in\mathcal{F}}X
).
For a set
X
and two functions
f,g\colon X\to\mathbb{Z}
, we
write
f+g
(resp.,
f-g
) the function defined by
(f+g)(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(x)+g(x)

(resp.,
(f-g)(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(x)-g(x)
) for all
x\in X
.

Posets.

Recall that a
poset

P=(A,\leq)
is a pair consisting of a set
A
and a
binary
partial order
relation
\leq
over
A
that is reflexive,
antisymmetric and transitive.
By slight abuse of notation, we may write
x\in P
to mean
x\in A
(and
U\subseteq P
to mean
U\subseteq A
).

A
greatest
(resp.,
least
) element of
P
is an
element
x\in P
such that for all
x^{\prime}\in P
we have
x^{\prime}\leq x

(resp.,
x\leq x^{\prime}
). When such an element exists it is unique, and
we then denote it by
\hat{1}
(resp.,
\hat{0}
). We may write
\hat{0}_{P},\hat{1}_{P}
to avoid confusion when multiple posets are involved.

For
G\subseteq A
, we define the
upset of
P
generated by
G
(also called an
order
filter
),
denoted
\uparrow_{P}(G)
, by
\uparrow_{P}(G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{x\in P\mid\exists y\in G\text{~s.t.~}y\leq x\}
. We
also define the
downset generated by
G
(also called an
order
ideal
), denoted
\downarrow_{P}(G)
, by

\downarrow_{P}(G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{x\in P\mid\exists y\in G\text{~s.t.~}x\leq y\}
.
Note that
\uparrow_{P}(\emptyset)=\downarrow_{P}(\emptyset)=\emptyset
.
An
upset
(resp., a

downset
) of
P
is a subset of
P
of the form
\uparrow_{P}(G)

(resp.,
\downarrow_{P}(G)
) for
G\subseteq A
. When
|G|=1
we call
\uparrow_{P}(G)

(resp.,
\downarrow_{P}(G)
) a
principal upset
(resp.,
principal
downset
), and by slight abuse of notation we sometimes write
\uparrow_{P}(x)

for
x\in P
to mean
\uparrow_{P}(\{x\})
(resp.,
\downarrow_{P}(x)
to
mean
\downarrow_{P}(\{x\})
).

Two posets
P_{1}=(A_{1},\leq_{1})
and
P_{2}=(A_{2},\leq_{2})
are

isomorphic
if there exists a bijection
h\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that for
all
x,y\in P_{1}
we have
x\leq_{1}y
iff
h(x)\leq_{2}h(y)
. When this is the case we write
P_{1}\simeq P_{2}
.

Lattices.

A poset
P=(A,\leq)
is a
meet semilattice
if, for every
x,y\in P
,
there exists
z\in P
such that: (1)
z\leq x
and
z\leq y
; and (2) for
every
z^{\prime}\in P
such that
z^{\prime}\leq x
and
z^{\prime}\leq y
, we have
z^{\prime}\leq z
. This
element
z
is then unique and is called the
meet of
x
and
y
, and
written
x\mathrel{\text{\raisebox{1.07639pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}}}y
. We similarly define
join semilattices
in the
expected way, with the join denoted
x\mathrel{\text{\raisebox{1.07639pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\vee$}}}}y
, and then
lattices
as
posets that are both a meet semilattice and a join semilattice.

We recall that a finite join (resp., meet) semilattice that has a least
(resp., greatest) element is a lattice (see, e.g.,
[
Stanley, 2011
, Proposition
3.3.1]
).
We also recall the property that in a meet semilattice
L
, the intersection of two
principal downsets
\downarrow_{L}(x)
and
\downarrow_{L}(y)
generated by
x,y\in L
is the
principal downset generated by
x\mathrel{\text{\raisebox{1.07639pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}}}y
; the dual property holds about principal upsets in
join semilattices with the join operation.

For a set
S
we write
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
the Boolean lattice over
S
, by which we mean
the lattice
(2^{S},\subseteq)
, where join corresponds to set union and meet to
set intersection.

Definition 2.1
.

A set family
\mathcal{F}
is called
trivial
if
it is empty or if
there is
X\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\bigcup\mathcal{F}=X
, and
non-trivial
otherwise.

We define the standard notion of
intersection lattice
of a non-trivial set family:

Definition 2.2

(
[
Stanley, 2011
]
, Section 3.7.2)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a non-trivial set family. For
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{T}\neq\emptyset
, we define:

S_{\mathcal{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\bigcap\mathcal{T}
. We also define
S_{\emptyset}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\bigcup\mathcal{F}
with a slight abuse of notation as
S_{\emptyset}
depends on
the underlying set family
\mathcal{F}
.

Define the poset
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}}=(\mathcal{A},\subseteq)
, where
\mathcal{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{S_{\mathcal{T}}\mid\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\}
.
Then one can check
that
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}}
is a meet semilattice whose meet operation is set intersection.
We
call
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}}

the intersection lattice
of
\mathcal{F}
. We say that a
lattice
L
is
an intersection lattice
if it is of the form
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}}
for some non-trivial set family
\mathcal{F}
.
(Note that we might have
\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}=\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}
for
\mathcal{F}_{1}\neq\mathcal{F}_{2}
.)

Remark 2.3
.

Every finite lattice
L=(A,\leq)
is isomorphic to some intersection
lattice. Indeed, define
\mathcal{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\downarrow_{L}(\{U\})\mid U\in A,U\neq\hat{1}_{L}\}
, i.e., the set of principal
downsets of
L
except
\downarrow_{L}(\{\hat{1}_{L}\})
(as otherwise
\mathcal{F}
is
trivial). Then one can easily check that
L\simeq\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}}
, since

\downarrow_{L}(\{U\})\cap\downarrow_{L}(\{V\})=\downarrow_{L}(U\wedge_{L}V)

for
U,V\in A
.

Such a lattice
L=\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}}
has a
\hat{0}
, which is
S_{\mathcal{F}}=\bigcap\mathcal{F}
, and
a
\hat{1}
, which is
S_{\emptyset}=\bigcup\mathcal{F}
.
Notice that
\hat{1}\neq\hat{0}
because
\mathcal{F}
is non-trivial.
Since
L
is a finite meet-semilattice that
has a
\hat{1}
, we know that
L
is also a join-semilattice by the property mentioned
above, but for the purpose of this note we will not need to know what the join operation corresponds to.
Note that by definition
S_{\mathcal{T}_{1}\cup\mathcal{T}_{2}}=S_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\cap S_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}
for
any
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. Observe that we may have
S_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}=S_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}
with
\mathcal{T}_{1}\neq\mathcal{T}_{2}
, in which case
S_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}=S_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}=S_{\mathcal{T}_{1}\cup\mathcal{T}_{2}}
.

Möbius inversion formula and inclusion-exclusion.

We now introduce the
Möbius function
on finite posets.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
P=(A,\leq)
be a finite poset. The
Möbius function

of
P
is the function
\mu_{P}
with value in
\mathbb{Z}
and domain the set of pairs
(x,y)\in A\times A
such that
x\leq y
, defined,
for each
y\in A
, by induction over the elements
x
such that
x\leq y
:

•

\mu_{P}(y,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}1
;

•

\mu_{P}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-\sum_{x<z\leq y}\mu_{P}(z,y)
.
1
1

1

The Möbius
function is traditionally defined with
\mu_{P}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-\sum_{x\leq z<y}\mu_{P}(x,z)
, but the two definitions are
equivalent
[
Rota, 1964
, Proposition 3]
. We
use this version as it simplifies our presentation.

Proposition 2.7

(Möbius inversion formula; see, e.g., Prop. 3.7.1 of
[
Stanley, 2011
]
)
.

Let
P=(A,\leq)
be a finite poset,
and let
f,g\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
. Then we have:

g(x)=\sum_{y\leq x}f(y)\text{~for all~}x\in P\text{~~~~if and only if~~~~}f(x)=\sum_{y\leq x}\mu_{P}(y,x)g(y)\text{~for all~}x\in P.

One important application of the Möbius inversion formula is in
determining the
measure
of a union of sets from the measure
of some of their intersections. For our purposes, given a set
S
,
what we call a
measure on
2^{S}
is a
function
\xi\colon 2^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
which is nonnegative (i.e.,

\xi(U)\geq 0
for all
U\in 2^{S}
), which satisfies

\xi(\emptyset)=0
, and which is additive under countable disjoint
unions, i.e., for any countable collection
\{E_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of
pairwise disjoint subsets of
S
, we have

\xi\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}E_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi(E_{k})
. In particular,
\xi
is completely determined by
the images of the singleton sets
\{x\}
for
x\in S
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
L
be an intersection lattice,
and let
\xi
be a measure
on
2^{\hat{1}}
. Then we have

\xi(\hat{1})=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in L\\
U\neq\hat{1}\end{subarray}}\mu_{L}(U,\hat{1})\xi(U).

(1)

Proof.

This is a known result but, to be self-contained, we reproduce the proof from

[
Stanley, 2011
]
(end of Section 3.7, page 304).
Let us define two functions

Proof.

Useful facts

We now present useful facts about the

First, we observe that the generalized Möbius functions are linear (in their parameters)
under disjoint union and subset complement.

Fact 5.6
.

Let
S

\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
be two configurations of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
such

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\mathcal{C}_{2}}=\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{1}}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{2}}

Proof.

One can simply check by top-down induction that for all
X\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
we
have
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}\mathcal{C}_{2}}(X)=\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{1}}(X)+\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{2}}(X)
(resp.,
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\mathcal{C}_{2}}(X)=\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{1}}(X)-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}_{2}}(X)

Definition 5.9

Let

\mathcal{C}
a configuration of

for all

, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{C}}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}\\
X\subseteq X^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|X^{\prime}|-|X|}g(X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{X^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}\cap\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)}}(-1)^{|X^{\prime}|-|X|}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|X|}\times\sum_{X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\cap\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)}(-1)^{|X^{\prime}|}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|X|}\times\mathsf{e}(\mathcal{C}\cap\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X))

Partial result on
\mathtt{NCPD}
: avoiding a given zero

In this section we present a partial result on the
\mathtt{NCPD}
conjecture
intuitively saying that we can “avoid” any given non-trivial zero. We first
define what these are.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a downset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
. We define the
non-trivial zeros
of
\mathcal{I}

By opposition, the
trivial
zeros of
\mathcal{I}
are the elements

such that
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Z)=0
(notice
that these form an upset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
).

Observe that if
\mathcal{I}
does not have non-trivial zeros, i.e.,
\mathtt{NTZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})=\emptyset
, then
we have indeed that
\mathcal{I}\in\bullet(\mathtt{NCPD}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I}))
, that is, the
\mathtt{NCPD}

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{I}
in Lemma
5.7
.
So we can focus on downsets
\mathcal{I}

The partial result that we show in this section is that, for any given such non-trivial zero

to use all the non-trivial principal downsets
except the one generated by

\mathcal{I}
.
Formally we will prove the following:

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a downset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
such that
\mathtt{NTZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})
is not empty, and let
Z\in\mathtt{NTZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})

Then we have
\mathcal{I}\in\bullet(\{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)\mid X\in I\setminus\{Z\}\})

The proof reuses and extends some ideas and

We combine everything in Section

even that are
in

\ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{m}

Then we can successively teleport

to

It is then clear that

6.2
Lifting lemma and simulating adjacent pairs with principal downsets

A simple, yet important component of the proof of Theorem
6.2
will be what we
call the
lifting lemma
. We first state and prove it before
giving intuition.

Lemma 6.10

(Lifting lemma)
.

Let
Z\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)}
. Let

I_{\mathcal{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)}\mid X\in\mathcal{A}\}

and
I^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{A}\}
. Then, for any

\mathcal{C}\in\bullet(I_{\mathcal{A}})
, defining the
configuration

\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{X\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}\mid X\cup Z\in\mathcal{C}\},

we have that
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})\in\bullet(I^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Proof.

This is shown by induction on
\mathcal{C}\in\bullet(I_{\mathcal{A}})
. The
first base case is when
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
, but then we
have
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})=\emptyset
as well, which is
in
\bullet(I^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}})
by definition. The second base case is
when
\mathcal{C}={\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)}
for
some
X
in
\mathcal{A}
. In this case, it is easy to show
that
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})={\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)}
; this
uses in particular that set union is the join operation of the
lattice
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
. But then this implies

\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})\in\bullet(I^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}})
indeed.
For the inductive case, we focus on
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}
as
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}
works similarly. Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\in\bullet(I_{\mathcal{A}})
such that
\mathcal{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{C}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}\mathcal{C}_{2}
is well-defined, and let us show
that
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})\in\bullet(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}})
.
By induction hypothesis we have that

\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C}_{1})\in\bullet(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}})
and

\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C}_{2})\in\bullet(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}})
. Now,
observe that
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C}_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C}_{2})
is well defined, and furthermore
that we have
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}\mathcal{C}_{2})=\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C}_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\cup}}\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C}_{2})
.
This implies
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})\in\bullet(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}})
as wanted, and concludes the proof.
∎

The intuition behind the lifting lemma is the following. Notice
that
\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)
is isomorphic to the Boolean
lattice
\mathbbm{B}_{S^{\prime}}
with
S^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}S\setminus Z
. Consider a
witnessing tree
T^{\prime}
representing a configuration
\mathcal{C}
of

\mathbbm{B}_{S^{\prime}}
that is formed from principal downsets of
\mathbbm{B}_{S^{\prime}}
.
Consider the “lifted tree”
T^{\prime}
that is obtained from
T
by
replacing every leaf of
T
of the form
\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S^{\prime}}}(X)

for
X\subseteq S^{\prime}
by the principal downset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
that is

\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X\cup Z)
. The lifting lemma says that the
obtained tree
T^{\prime}
is valid (i.e., the internal nodes are well-defined disjoint unions and subset complements) and that it
represents the configuration of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
that
is
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{C})
.

This lemma will have two uses in the proof of Theorem
6.2
. The
first is that said proof will work by first constructing a configuration in
the principal upset generated by the non-trivial zero
Z
that is fixed
in the theorem statement, and then this lemma will be used to “lift” the result
to the whole Boolean lattice
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
. The second use of this lemma is that it
helps us show that adjacent pairs can be simulated by principal downsets, while
avoiding the principal downset that is at the bottom. We explain this
second use in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X,X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\{x\}\}
be an adjacent pair
of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
. Then we have
\mathcal{P}\in\bullet(\{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Y)\mid\emptyset\subsetneq Y\subseteq X\})
.

In this statement, pay attention that the principal downset

\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\emptyset)
is not in the base set of the
dot-algebra.
7
7

7

Recall that to alleviate the notation we write

\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\emptyset)
to mean

\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\{\emptyset\})
, which is then
\{\emptyset\}
.

In other words, we are allowed to use any principal downset that is
generated by an element that is below
X
and strictly
above
\emptyset
in
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
. The reason is that in the proof of
Theorem
6.2
,
Z
will correspond to
\emptyset
here
and it generates the only downset we are not allowed to use. We now
prove Lemma
6.11
.

Proof of Lemma
6.11
.

Clearly, it suffices to show the claim in the case that
X=S
.
To this end, it is enough to show that we have
\mathbbm{B}_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\mathcal{P}\in\bullet(\{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Y)\mid\emptyset\subsetneq Y\subseteq X\})
: indeed we can then obtain
\mathcal{P}

as
\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}(\mathbbm{B}_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{$\bullet$}}}{{\setminus}}\mathcal{P})
.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the configuration
\{X\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}\mid\{x\}\subseteq X\subsetneq S\}
.
Using Lemma
5.7
appropriately we have that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\in\bullet(\{{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Y)}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\{x\})}\mid\{x\}\subseteq Y\subsetneq S\})
. We now use the lifting lemma (invoked with

) to
obtain that
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\{x\}}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\in\bullet(\{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Y)\mid\{x\}\subseteq Y\subsetneq S\})
, hence in particular

\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\{x\}}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\in\bullet(\{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Y)\mid\emptyset\subsetneq Y\subseteq S\})
.
But notice that
\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\{x\}}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
is actually

\mathbbm{B}_{S}\setminus\mathcal{P}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

6.3
Proof of Theorem
6.2

We now prove Theorem
6.2
.
Fix the finite set
S
and the configuration
\mathcal{I}
that is a downset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
such that
\mathtt{NTZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})

By Proposition

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Z)=(-1)^{|Z|}\times\mathsf{e}(\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)})
,

\mathsf{e}(\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)})=0
.
We now instantiate Proposition

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)}

and
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\emptyset
to obtain
\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)}\simeq_{\mathtt{AP}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z))}}\emptyset
.
This gives us a left-linear tree

for

with disjoint unions and

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}=\mathtt{lift}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},Z}(\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z))}\setminus(\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Z)})

7
Extensions, Variants and Counterexample Search

In this section we present a generalization of Theorem
6.2
that
we believe to be true, present variants of the conjectures, and talk about our
experimental search for counterexamples.

Extension of Theorem
6.2
.

We sketch here a proposed generalization that allows us to avoid not one single zero,
but subsets of zeros while requiring that the lattice has a certain
structure. We first define a few notions.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a downset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
.
For
X\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
, the
non-trivial covering zeros of
X
relative
to
\mathcal{I}
are

\mathtt{NTCZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{Z\in\mathtt{NTZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})\mid X\subsetneq Z\text{ and there is no }Z^{\prime}\in\mathtt{NTZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})\text{ s.t. }X\subsetneq Z^{\prime}\subsetneq Z\}.

In other words,
\mathtt{NTCZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X)
simply consists of the minimal
non-trivial zeros of
\mathcal{I}
that are strictly above
X
.
Observe that we have
\mathtt{NTCZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X)=\emptyset
whenever
X\notin\mathcal{I}
. We now define
k
-decomposable
downsets.

Definition 7.2
.

A configuration
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
that is a downset is
called
k
-decomposable
if for every
X\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
we have

|\mathtt{NTCZ}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X)|\leq k
.

This intuitively means that, in every upset, there are at most two non-trivial
covering zeroes.
We believe that the following is true, but have not completely formalized the
proof.

Conjecture 7.3
.

For every finite
S
, for every configuration
\mathcal{I}

of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
that is a
2
-decomposable downset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
, we
have that
\mathcal{I}\in\bullet(\mathtt{NCPD}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I}))
.

Proof sketch.

For an element
X\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
, let
I_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{{\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(Y)}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)}\mid Y\in{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)},\allowbreak\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)\neq 0\}
. The idea of the proof would be to show by top-down induction on
\mathbbm{B}_{S}

that for every
X\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
, we have
\mathcal{I}\cap{\uparrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)\in\bullet(I_{X})}
. Applying this claim to
X=\emptyset
yields the desired
result. To show this, we use similar tools to those developed for the proof
of Theorem
6.2
, extended with an accounting of Euler
characteristics of diverse sets of configurations. It does not seem that the
proposed proof would directly generalize to the general case, or indeed to

3
-decomposable configurations.
∎

Strengthenings.

We present here two possible orthogonal ways in which we canstrengthen the conjecture; we do not know whether the stronger conjectures are
true, nor whether they are equivalent to the original conjecture.

First, in view of Proposition
4.7
, we could require that
the non-trivial intersections are only used positively or only negatively depending
on the sign of their Möbius value. Indeed,
Proposition
4.7
implies that, on tight intersection
lattices, the non-trivial intersections have total multiplicity equal to their
Möbius value, but they may be used both positively and negatively. Likewise, in
view of Proposition
5.8
, we could require that the principal
downsets are only used positively and negatively.

Second, we do not know if the witnessing trees can be required to be
left-linear, i.e., whether we can obtain
\hat{1}
(for the
\mathtt{NCI}
conjecture)
or the desired configuration (for the
\mathtt{NCPD}
conjecture) by a series of
operations where, at each step, we add a (disjoint) non-trivial intersection (or principal
downset), or subtract one (which must be a subset). For instance, this
formulation does not allow us to express a disjoint union of two configurations
themselves obtained with more complex witnessing trees. This stronger conjecture
is the topic of the question asked in
[
Amarilli, 2019
]
(up to replacing upsets by
downsets and to working in general lattices instead of only on Boolean lattices). This question also explains why the conjecture is false if asked
about general DAGs (instead of lattices), and shows that the conjecture can be
true for so-called
crown-free lattices
.

We note that the construction for the proof of Theorem
6.2
(or
the one that we have in mind for Conjecture
7.3
) does not satisfy either of
these strengthenings. However, the proof of Fact
4.8
can be
adjusted to satisfy the left-linear condition.

Counterexample search.

We have implemented a search for counterexamples of the
\mathtt{NCI}
conjecture. We
consider Sperner families
[
Monet, 2020b
]
, which give all sets of subsets of a
base set of
n
elements that are non-equivalent (i.e., that are not the same up
to permuting the elements). We have checked in a brute-force fashion that the conjecture holds up to

n=5
, i.e., on all intersection lattices such that
\hat{1}
has cardinality at
most
5
. Unfortunately, already for
n=6
there are intersection lattices that
are too large for the computation to finish sufficiently quickly. We also
generated random intersection lattices over larger sets of elements, but could
not find a counterexample (assuming that our code is correct). The code checks
the strong version of the
\mathtt{NCI}
conjecture in the previous terminology, i.e.,
it searches for left-linear trees and requires the non-trivial intersections to
be used precisely with the right polarity. The code is available
as-is
[
Amarilli, 2023
]
. We have also improved the implementation to search for
solutions on each lattice using a SAT-solver rather than a brute-force search:
this speeds up the computation but also did not yield any counterexample.

We had also implemented, earlier, a search for counterexamples of an
alternative phrasing of the
\mathtt{NCPD}
conjecture: see
[
Amarilli et al., 2020
]
. This also
illustrates that the
\mathtt{NCPD}
conjecture is false if the configuration to reach
is not a downset; see Figure 4 of
[
Amarilli et al., 2020
]
(up to reversing directions, i.e.,
considering downsets instead of upsets).

We show in this section that the
\mathtt{NCI}
and
\mathtt{NCU}
conjectures
are equivalent. We only sketch the proofs as details are easy to
fill.

Definition .4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a set family. For
S\in 2^{\bigcup\mathcal{F}}
,
let
\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(\bigcup\mathcal{F})\setminus S
, and

\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{T})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{T}\}

Observe then that

\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{F})

Fact .5
.

Let

be an intersection lattice, and let

be the union lattice
\mathbbm{U}_{\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{F})}

of
\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{F})

Proof sketch.

One can check that the function that sends

is an

1.

For any sets

2.

For a set

.∎

The dual version of this fact also holds, i.e., for a union

We can then show that the
\mathtt{NCU}

\mathtt{NCI}

L=\mathbbm{L}_{\mathcal{F}}
be an intersection

\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{F})

.5

[

Since the

, hence by (

\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(\hat{0}_{L^{\prime}})\in\bullet(\mathtt{NCI}(L))

\hat{0}_{L^{\prime}}=\bigcap_{X\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(X)
by
definition, which is equal to

So
\mathrm{dual}_{\mathcal{F}}(\hat{0}_{L^{\prime}})=\bigcup\mathcal{F}

.
Now for a node
n\in T
, let
X_{n}
be the subset of
\hat{1}

represented by the subtree of
T
rooted at
n
. We show by
bottom-up induction on
T
that for every node
n
of
T

and
U\in\mathtt{NTI}(L)
we have
\mathtt{mult}_{T_{n}}(U)=\check{\mu}_{L,X_{n}}(U)
. This will indeed imply the result by applying
it to the root of
T
and using item (
2
), since we then
have that
X_{n}=\hat{1}
. The base case, when
n
is a leaf
of
T
, follows from item (
4
) and item (
1
), while the inductive
case, when
n
is an internal node of
T
, follows
from (
3
).
∎

Lemma

(
5.5
)
.

Let
S
be a finite set and let
\mathcal{I}
be a downset of
\mathbbm{B}_{S}
of the form
\mathcal{I}={\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{F})}
for
\mathcal{F}
a non-trivial set family.
For
X\in\mathcal{F}
let
X^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\downarrow_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(X)
(which is
2^{X}
),
and let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{X^{\prime}\mid X\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Note that

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is non-trivial as well, and let then
L
be its intersection
lattice.
Then we have
\mathtt{NCPD}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})=\mathtt{NCI}(L)
.

Proof.

Observe that
\hat{1}_{L}=\mathcal{I}
. For
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, we
write
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
for the corresponding subset of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
,
i.e.,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\{X^{\prime}\mid X\in\mathcal{T}\}
, and vice versa. For

\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F},\mathcal{T}\neq\emptyset
let
X_{\mathcal{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\bigcap\mathcal{T}
. We show that the following hold:

1.

For
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
we have
S_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}={\downarrow_{B_{S}}(X_{\mathcal{T}})}
.

2.

Let
L^{\prime}
be the lattice
(\{X_{\mathcal{T}}\mid\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F},\mathcal{T}\neq\emptyset\}\cup\{\hat{1}_{L^{\prime}}\},\leq_{L^{\prime}})
where
\hat{1}_{L^{\prime}}
is a fresh element
and
Y\leq_{L^{\prime}}\hat{1}_{L^{\prime}}
for all
Y\in L^{\prime}
and
X_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\leq_{L^{\prime}}X_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}

for
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
if
X_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\subseteq X_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}
. Then
L\simeq L^{\prime}
via the isomorphism that sends
\hat{1}_{L}

to
\hat{1}_{L^{\prime}}
and
S_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
for
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
to
X_{T}
.

3.

For every
Y\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
, if
Y
is of the form
X_{\mathcal{T}}

then
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=-\mu_{L}(S_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}},\hat{1}_{L})
, otherwise
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=0
.

Notice that Item (
3
) together with Item (
1
)
implies that
\mathtt{NCPD}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S}}(\mathcal{I})=\mathtt{NCI}(L)
, which is what we need to
prove.
Item (
1
) follows from the chain of trivialities
S_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\bigcap\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\bigcap_{X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}X^{\prime}=\bigcap_{X\in\mathcal{T}}2^{X}=2^{\bigcap\mathcal{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}2^{X_{\mathcal{T}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}{\downarrow_{B_{S}}(X_{\mathcal{T}})}
, and Item (
2
) is easy to
prove using Item (
1
).
For Item (
3
), we show
that
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}
is as claimed. For
Y\in\mathbbm{B}_{S}
,

Y\notin\mathcal{I}
, it is clear
that
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=0
, by definition of
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}
and because
\mathcal{I}
is a downset, so we
can focus on
Y\in\mathcal{I}
. We show by top-down induction on
\mathcal{I}
that

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}
is as claimed on
\mathcal{I}
. The base case is
when
Y
is a maximal element of
\mathcal{I}
, i.e.,
Y=X
for
some maximal
X\in\mathcal{F}
. Then we have
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=1=-\mu_{L}(X^{\prime})
indeed.
The inductive case is when
Y
is not a maximal element of
\mathcal{I}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{X\in\mathcal{F}\mid Y\subseteq X\}
, which is non-empty. Notice
the following fact (
\dagger
): we have
Y\subseteq X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}
, and more specifically we
have
Y\subseteq X_{\mathcal{T}}
for
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F},\mathcal{T}\neq\emptyset
if
and only if
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{Y}
. We then distinguish two cases.

•

The first case is when
Y=X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}
. We
have
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=1-\sum_{Y\subsetneq Y^{\prime}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y^{\prime})
by definition. Now by what precedes, if
Y^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{I}
then
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y^{\prime})=0
, and by
induction hypothesis if
Y^{\prime}
is not of the form
X_{\mathcal{T}}

then
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y^{\prime})=0
. Hence, we have

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subsetneq Y^{\prime}\\
Y^{\prime}=X_{\mathcal{T}}\end{subarray}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X_{\mathcal{T}})
, and
the suitable
\mathcal{T}
are the subsets of
\mathcal{T}_{Y}
by
(
\dagger
), i.e.,
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=1-\sum_{Y^{\prime}\in\{X_{\mathcal{T}}\mid\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{Y}\}\setminus\{Y\}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X_{\mathcal{T}})
. Now, by induction
hypothesis, we have
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X_{\mathcal{T}})=-\mu_{L}(S_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}},\hat{1}_{L})
for all
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{Y}

such that
X_{\mathcal{T}}\neq Y
, therefore

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=1+\sum_{Y^{\prime}\in\{X_{\mathcal{T}}\mid\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{Y}\}\setminus\{Y\}}\mu_{L}(S_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}},\hat{1}_{L})
. Now by Item (
2
)
this is equal to
1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in L\\
U\neq\hat{1}_{L}\\
S_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}^{\prime}}\subsetneq U\end{subarray}}\mu_{L}(U,\hat{1}_{L})
, which is
equal to
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in L\\
S_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}^{\prime}}\subsetneq U\end{subarray}}\mu_{L}(U,\hat{1}_{L})
, which is
-\mu_{L}(S_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}^{\prime}})
by
definition. So indeed
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=-\mu_{L}(S_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}^{\prime}})
.

•

The second case is when
Y\neq X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}
. Then, the
nodes of
\mathcal{I}
in the principal upset of
Y
minus
\{Y\}
consists of
the nodes of
\mathcal{I}
in the principal upset of

X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}
, and of nodes that are not of the form

X_{\mathcal{T}}
by (
\dagger
) and that are strict supersets of
Y
. By induction hypothesis, the

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}
-value of the latter is zero. So

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=1-\sum_{X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}\subseteq Y^{\prime}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y^{\prime})=1-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}})-\sum_{X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}\subsetneq Y^{\prime}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y^{\prime})
, but by
definition of
\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}
we have

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}})=1-\sum_{X_{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}\subsetneq Y^{\prime}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y^{\prime})
, so indeed

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbbm{B}_{S},\mathcal{I}}(Y)=0
. This concludes the proof.∎
