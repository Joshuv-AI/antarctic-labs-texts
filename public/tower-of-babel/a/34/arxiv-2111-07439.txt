Improving Compound Activity Classification via Deep Transfer and Representation Learning
Vishal Dey, Raghu Machiraju, Xia Ning

Abstract
Recent advances in molecular machine learning, especially deep neural networks such as Graph Neural Networks ( 𝙶𝙽𝙽𝚜 \mathop{\mathtt{GNNs}}\limits ) for predicting structure activity relationships (SAR) have shown tremendous potential in computer-aided drug discovery. However, the applicability of such deep neural networks are limited by the requirement of large amounts of training data. In order to cope with limited training data for a target task, transfer learning for SAR modeling has been recently adopted to leverage information from data of related tasks. In this work, in contrast to the popular parameter-based transfer learning such as pretraining, we develop novel deep transfer learning methods 𝚃𝙰𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits and 𝚃𝙰𝚌 ​ - ​ 𝚏𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits to leverage source domain data and transfer useful information to the target domain. 𝚃𝙰𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits learns to generate effective molecular features that can generalize well from one domain to another, and increase the classification performance in the target domain. Additionally, 𝚃𝙰𝚌 ​ - ​ 𝚏𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits extends 𝚃𝙰𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits by incorporating novel components to selectively learn feature-wise and compound-wise transferability. We used the bioassay screening data from PubChem, and identified 120 pairs of bioassays such that the active compounds in each pair are more similar to each other compared to its inactive compounds. Overall, 𝚃𝙰𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits achieves the best performance with average 𝚁𝙾𝙲 ​ - ​ 𝙰𝚄𝙲 \mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits of 0.801; it significantly improves 𝚁𝙾𝙲 ​ - ​ 𝙰𝚄𝙲 \mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits of 83% target tasks with average task-wise performance improvement of 7.102%, compared to the best baseline 𝙵𝙲𝙽 ​ - 𝚍𝚖𝚙𝚗𝚊 ​ ( 𝙳𝚃 ) \mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits . Our experiments clearly demonstrate that 𝚃𝙰𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits achieves significant improvement over all baselines across a large number of target tasks. Furthermore, although 𝚃𝙰𝚌 ​ - ​ 𝚏𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits achieves slightly worse 𝚁𝙾𝙲 ​ - ​ 𝙰𝚄𝙲 \mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits on average compared to 𝚃𝙰𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits (0.798 vs 0.801), 𝚃𝙰𝚌 ​ - ​ 𝚏𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits still achieves the best performance on more tasks in terms of 𝙿𝚁 ​ - ​ 𝙰𝚄𝙲 \mathop{\mathtt{PR\text{-}AUC}}\limits and 𝙵𝟷 \mathop{\mathtt{F1}}\limits compared to other methods. In summary, 𝚃𝙰𝚌 ​ - ​ 𝚏𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits is also found to be a strong model with competitive or even better performance than 𝚃𝙰𝚌 \mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits on a notable number of target tasks.

Introduction

Drug discovery is a time consuming and expensive process
Dickson and Gagnon 2009
–
it takes at least 10 years and at least $1 billion to fully develop a drug
DiMasi et al. 2003
.
During the initial stages of this process, promising drug candidates
are identified by screening large library of chemical compounds,
and are then further investigated for specific properties.
In order to speed up this process,
computational approaches
Terstappen and Reggiani 2001
;
Sliwoski et al. 2014
have been adopted, particularly
for identifying potential drug candidates during the initial stages of
the drug discovery.
Computational approaches explore a much larger space of chemical compounds
to predict their physio-chemical properties and/or biological activities towards the target.
In this paper, we consider the problem of compound bioactivity classification,
where a compound is classified as active or inactive based on whether that compound
binds to the protein target.
Biological activities of compounds are initially examined in a bioassay by measuring
their binding affinities or dissociation constants toward the target.
Significant research
Hansch et al. 1962
;
Debnath et al. 1991
;
Superti-Furga and Courtneidge 1995
have established the relationship between
the chemical structures and biological activities of compounds, also known as
Structure-Activity relationships (SAR)
Hansch et al. 1962
.
Several computational approaches
Dudek et al. 2006
have been developed to model
SAR
and to predict compound bioactivities from their 2D/3D structures. However, most popular approaches such as deep neural networks require
large amounts of labeled data for effective SAR modeling.
Thus, limited availability of bioassay data for specific targets still
pose a major challenge in effective SAR modeling
Imrie et al. 2018
.

Over the years, several methods
Ning et al. 2009
;
Liu and Ning 2017
;
Liu and Ning 2017

aimed to improve SAR predictions for specific targets
by leveraging activity information from related targets.
These methods consider targets to be related,
based on the principles from
chemogenomics
Murcko 2001
;
Kubinyi and Müller 2005
;
Harris and Stevens 2006
;
Klabunde 2007
.

The key principle behind these methods is that
similar proteins tend to bind to structurally similar compounds.

In this work, we consider proteins belonging to the same protein family to be similar.
Thus, leveraging compound activity information
from bioassays corresponding to a set of proteins from the same protein family
(e.g., G-coupled protein receptors, kinases, peptidases, etc)
collectively might better inform the SAR model than the individual bioassays.
In essence, transfer learning can enable better SAR modeling by
leveraging information from such related bioassays.
However, existing methods are instance-based transfer learning methods
Pan and Yang 2010
.
They select a subset of data from related bioassays,
and then augment the training data for the target task with the selected subset.
Existing deep transfer learning based methods
Cai et al. 2020

for SAR modeling are either parameter-based (such as finetuning),
or feature-based; out of which parameter-based methods
are more popular.
However, such methods can lead to overfitting and negative transfer
Pan and Yang 2010
,
especially when the targets are not related.
In this regard, we believe that feature-based methods are better in
that they can learn the similarity/relatedness between the targets in the
latent space in a data-driven manner.

Primarily, we develop an instance-based transfer learning method
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
that leverages target information from related bioassays,
based on the key principle of chemogenomics as mentioned earlier.
We further extend
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
to a novel feature-based deep transfer learning method
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
that quantitatively measures transferability and explicitly learns what to
transfer in a fully data-driven manner.
To this end, we develop novel components to learn feature-wise and compound-wise
transferability in order to effectively encode the commonalities among compounds
of different tasks.
In order to represent compounds,
we leveraged the popular idea of Directed Message Passing Neural Network (
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
)
Yang et al. 2019
,
and add an attention-based pooling mechanism, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
.
We collected a set of confirmatory bioassays from PubChem
Kim et al. 2020

that have a single protein target,
and are tested on chemical substances.
We identified 120 bioassay pairs involving 59 protein targets
such that the active compounds in each pair
are more similar to each other compared to the inactive compounds.
We compared our methods
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
with several baselines with respect to two aspects: compound representation and
transfer mechanisms.
Overall,
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
achieves the best performance compared to all other methods.
Compared to
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
performs slightly worse, but the latter
still provides significant performance improvement on some target tasks.
This suggests that although the transfer mechanism in
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
performs the best overall,
the deep transfer mechanism with learned feature-wise and compound-wise transferability
can actually benefit some targets.
Furthermore, experimental results demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed attention mechanism
of
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
in learning better compound features.
We provide additional experiments on the compound prioritization problem
Liu and Ning 2017

where
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
clearly outperforms all other compound representation methods.

1.1.1
Computational Methods in Drug Discovery

The first step in the drug discovery process is to conduct bioassays
ur Rahman et al. 2001

that screens a large set of compounds for desirable properties
(e.g., activity, solubility, toxicity).
The findings from these bioassays guide the later steps
of the drug discovery process.
In order to speed up initial stages of the drug discovery process,
computational approaches have been adopted.
Computational approaches to predict activities/properties of
compounds from their molecular structures have been a significant
research area in cheminformatics
Dudek et al. 2006
;
Tareq Hassan Khan 2010
;
Lo et al. 2018
.
These approaches rely on the quantitative structure activity/property relationship
(QSAR/QSPR)
Hansch et al. 1962
;
Kat 2001

to predict compound activities/properties as expressed in bioassays.

In order to predict such activities/properties,
machine learning methods such as classification and regression are typically used.
Binary/real-valued observations from bioassay data
are used to train these classification/regression methods.
Popular conventional classification and regression methods to predict compound activities/properties
consist of
support vector machines
Czermiski et al. 2001
;
Zernov et al. 2003
;
Hou et al. 2007
;
Alvarsson et al. 2016
,
random forests
Svetnik et al. 2003
;
Zhang and Aires-de Sousa 2007
,
Bayesian models
Xia et al. 2004
;
Chen et al. 2011
, etc.
In these methods, compounds are typically represented by
hand-crafted molecular fingerprints
Rogers and Hahn 2010
;
Durant et al. 2002
or descriptors
Randić 1993
.
Recently, deep learning methods
Ma et al. 2015
;
Gawehn et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Hop et al. 2018

have demonstrated significant
performance improvement over conventional methods
across several activity/property prediction tasks
Klambauer et al. 2017
;
Wenzel et al. 2019
;
Wu et al. 2019
.
Unlike conventional methods, these methods do not require
careful and expensive design of
hand-crafted molecular fingerprints or descriptors by domain experts.
These methods learn the compound representations from
molecular graphs
Duvenaud et al. 2015
;
Kearnes et al. 2016
;
Gilmer et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2020
;
Withnall et al. 2020
and
SMILES strings
Zheng et al. 2019
;
Chakravarti and Alla 2019
;
Karpov et al. 2020
,
in a fully data-driven manner for each task.
Such learned representations are task-specific and can better encode relevant structures
for each task.
Thus, such learned representations are often more effective than
molecular fingerprints or descriptors.
While these deep learning models have achieved the state-of-the-art
performance on several molecular activity/property prediction tasks,
these models require large amount of labeled training data to encode
relevant patterns into learned representations.
Training these models with limited labeled data
for certain prediction tasks
often leads to sub-par performance.

1.1.2
Transfer Learning

In order to effectively train models with limited labeled data
for certain prediction tasks,
transfer learning between related tasks
has been widely explored in Computer Vision(CV)
and Natural Language Processing (NLP)
Tan et al. 2018
;
Zhuang et al. 2021
.
Transfer learning
Pan and Yang 2010
is an emerging research area
in which knowledge gained from auxiliary tasks
is transferred to improve the predictive performance
of the target task.
Instead of training a model for the target task from scratch,
a popular transfer learning technique, called finetuning
Yosinski et al. 2014
, finetunes the model
pretrained from other related tasks.
Pretraining does not explicitly learn what/when to transfer
and rather relies on the model parameters to encode and
transfer information across different tasks.
Although pretraining is the most popular transfer learning method,
it does not guarantee improvement (due to ‘negative transfer’
Wang et al. 2019
).
Moreover, finetuning a highly parameterized model with limited data
may lead to overfitting to the training data and
thus, the finetuned model might not generalize well to the test data.
Apart from pretraining, another area of deep transfer learning, called
domain adaptation has gained a lot of attention
Pan et al. 2011
;
Long et al. 2013
;
Wang and Deng 2018
.
Domain adaptation methods reduces the effect of
the domain shift
by learning domain-invariant representations that can generalize
well across different tasks.
In order to learn such representations,
domain adaptation methods
either minimize statistical measures
Tzeng et al. 2014
;
Long et al. 2015
;
Long et al. 2016
of domain shift
or use adversarial training
Liu et al. 2019
.
Following the success of
adversarial training in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
Goodfellow et al. 2014
,
adversarial domain adaptation methods
Ganin and Lempitsky 2015
;
Long et al. 2017
;
Sankaranarayanan et al. 2018
gained more attention and demonstrated
state-of-the-art performances
over benchmark CV and NLP datasets.
Adversarial domain adaptation methods use
adversarial training to learn domain-invariant representations
via a mini-max optimization
using a feature extractor, a domain classifier and a label predictor.
The principle of adversarial training is used to train the feature extractor
to learn domain-invariant representations which are indistinguishable by the domain classifier.
Seminal methods in adversarial domain adaptation
Ganin and Lempitsky 2015
;
Long et al. 2017
;
Tzeng et al. 2017

differ in the design choices such as
adversarial loss functions, optimization,
coupling of weights etc.
Other existing methods focus on
conditional feature alignment
Long et al. 2018
;
Monteiro et al. 2021
,
multi-source transfer
Pei et al. 2018
;
Zhao et al. 2018
, etc.
However, these methods have been specifically developed
for image domain adaptation or image translation problems.
To the best of our knowledge, none of these methods have been
widely adapted for graph-structured data.
In this work, following the idea of adversarial domain adaptation,
we proposed a novel transfer learning method that learns effective compound representations
from graph-structured data and transfers relevant information
from a related task to the target task.

1.1.3
Transfer Learning in SAR predictions

To alleviate the limited data problem in cheminformatics,
various transfer learning
Altae-Tran et al. 2017
;
Simões et al. 2018
;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Cai et al. 2020
;
Guo et al. 2021
and
multitask learning methods
Dahl et al. 2014
;
Ramsundar et al. 2017
;
Xu2 2017
;
Rodríguez-Pérez and
Bajorath 2019
;
Sosnin et al. 2019

have been recently developed.
Inspired by the success of pretraining followed by finetuning in CV and NLP,
Goh et al.
Goh et al. 2018
proposed ChemNet, where a deep neural network
is pretrained on a large set of compounds in a self-supervised manner
and then fine-tuned on individual activity/prediction tasks.
Following the same idea, Li and Fourches
Li and Fourches 2020

proposed MolPMoFit which trains a Long Short Term Memory (LSTM)
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
on
SMILES strings of compounds
and then fine-tunes the pretrained model on specific tasks.
Although pretraining has been widely studied,
existing work in cheminformatics do not demonstrate significant
performance improvement over the state-of-the-art
supervised models in a single-task setting.
Moreover, models trained on SMILES strings
do not explicitly leverage the topological information of compounds.
However, our methods use molecular graphs as inputs, and hence explicitly
leverage the topological information.

Adversarial transfer learning has been rarely explored for
SAR predictions and on graph-structured data.
To the best of our knowledge, only recently
Abbasi et al.
Abbasi et al. 2019
combined multitask networks and
adversarial domain adaptation
to learn transferable molecular representations from multiple source bioassays to improve the prediction performance on the target bioassay.
The authors evaluated their model on biophysics and physiology
data sets such as Tox21, SIDER, BACE, ToxCast and HIV.
Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed method outperforms
no-transfer methods only on a few target tasks.
Moreover, experimental results do not clearly demonstrate the
contribution of the adversarial domain adaptation component
to the overall performance.
Overall, prior work on transfer-learning based SAR modeling
do not clearly suggest a performance gain over conventional SAR models
over a wide-array of target tasks.

2.1
Materials

In this section, we describe the dataset generation, baseline methods

2.1.1
Dataset Generation

We used the real screening data from PubChem to test our methods.
PubChem
Kim et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2020
is one of the largest public
chemical database with more than 271M substances, 111M unique
chemical structures and 293M bioassay data.
We selected a set of bioassays from PubChem bioassays [accessed on 2020-12-25]
such that each bioassay has a sufficiently large number of active and inactive compounds.
Then, we generated pairs of bioassays for transfer learning in accordance
to the protocols in below sections
2.1.1.1
and
2.1.1.2
.

2.1.1.1
Initial Bioassay Selection and Pruning

We first selected a set of 7,284 confirmatory bioassays that have a single protein target,
and are tested on chemical substances. These bioassays have 1,279 unique protein targets in total.
Among these protein targets, we were able to identify the organism and protein family information for
961 protein targets within 435 protein families using UniProt
Bateman et al. 2021
.
Among the 435 protein families, we further combined them into 278 families (e.g., Peptidase A1, Peptidase C12, and Peptidase C13 families
were combined into peptidase family).
Among the 278 families, we selected 10 that have the most protein targets belonging to ‘Human’ organisms.
These top-10 protein families are 1) G-protein coupled receptor 1 family, 2) peptidase family,
3) protein kinase family, 4) nuclear hormone receptor family, 5) protein-tyrosine phosphatase family,
6) ABC transporter family, 7) Cytochrome P450 family, 8) Bcl-2 family, 9) G-protein coupled receptor 3 family,
and 10) Histone deacetylase family.
These protein families involved 269 unique protein targets and
covered the major drug targets in drug discovery
Santos et al. 2016
;
Zdrazil et al. 2020
.

According to the 10 protein families,
bioassays with targets from these protein families were then processed as follows:

1.

We combined bioassays of a same target into one bioassay, resulting in 269 combined bioassays.

2.

For each combined bioassay, we selected its compounds
that were tested for inhibition against the target (i.e., the corresponding PubChem activity type
specified by the depositor was “inhibitor").

3.

From those inhibitive compounds, we selected the compounds that were specified as either “active" or “inactive"
against the target, and discarded the compounds that were specified as
“inconclusive” or “undetermined".

4.

If the active/inactive compounds appeared multiple times in the bioassay with a same
activity label, we retained one of their records.
If the active/inactive compounds appeared multiple times in the bioassay with different activity labels,
we removed the compounds from the bioassays (in our data set, about only 2.08% of compounds for each bioassay on average
appear multiple times with different activity levels).
We use canonical SMILES strings to detect identical compounds.

After the above processing, each combined bioassay has on average 17,005 unique
compounds in total, with 188
active and 16,817 inactive.
Furthermore, out of the 269 combined bioassays, 95 bioassays have more than 50 active compounds.
Among the 10 protein families involved in the 95 bioassays,
2 protein families had only 1 target with more than 50 active compounds.
Thus, we removed these 2 protein families and only used the remaining 8 protein families and their 93 bioassays.
This set of 93 bioassays has on average 40,115 compounds, with 521 active
and 39,595 inactive.
This set of bioassays will be used to create bioassay pairs as will be described in the
next section.
Table S1 in the Supplementary Materials presents the statistics of each of the 93 bioassays.

2.1.1.2
Transferable Bioassay Pairing

From the 93 processed bioassays, we constructed 765 bioassay pairs such that in each pair,
the protein targets of the two bioassays are from the same protein family.
We selected targets from a same protein family because based on the key intuition of
chemical genomics
Harris and Stevens 2006
;
Klabunde 2007
, proteins from a same family tend to have similar binding pockets and bind to similar compounds
– this is the physicochemical foundation to enable possible information transfer across protein targets, and such targets
and their bioassays can be used to test transfer learning.
We first ensured that each of the 765 pairs of bioassays had balanced active and inactive compounds as follows:

1.

In each pair of bioassays, we removed the compounds that appeared in both bioassays but with different activity labels
(on average, 2.09% of all unique compounds in a pair of bioassays).
This is to avoid any conflicting information across bioassays,
which could adversely affect our transfer learning method.

2.

For compounds with same activity labels in both the bioassays (on average, 1.82% of all unique compounds in a pair of bioassays),
we randomly sampled half of them into one of the bioassays,
and the other half into the other.
This is to avoid duplication of compounds across bioassays, which could
lead to overestimation of predictive performance.

3.

After the above steps, for each bioassay of a pair,
we used all its active compounds,
and randomly sampled the same number of its inactive compounds.
If the inactive compounds were not sufficient,
we randomly sampled compounds from PubChem that were not active
in the bioassay as additional inactive compounds for the bioassays.
This is to ensure that each bioassay in a pair has equal number of active and inactive compounds, and thus
the learning will not be dominated by either active or inactive compounds. Please note that a bioassay involved in
two pairs may have different numbers of active and inactive compounds due to its paired, the other bioassay.

After the above steps, we selected the bioassay pairs such that each bioassay in each pair had
at least 50 active compounds retained. There were 635 such pairs and involved 92 bioassays in total.
Among the 635 pairs of bioassays, we further selected the pairs as follows
such that the active compounds in each pair are similar to each other compared to its inactive compounds:

1.

For a pair of bioassay
\mbox{$B$}_{i}
and
\mbox{$B$}_{j}
, and their respective active and inactive compounds,
denoted as
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+}
,
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{-}
,

\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+}
and
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{-}
, respectively,
we calculated the following two types of average compound similarities using Taminoto coefficient
Willett et al. 1998
over
Morgan-count fingerprints (with radius 3 and dimension = 2,048):
1) among compounds of same labels across the two bioassays:

\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})
, and

\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{-},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{-})
; and
2) among compounds of different labels across the two bioassays:

\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{-})
, and

\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{-},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})
.

2.

Based on the similarities, we selected a set of bioassay pairs, denoted as
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, such that
in each pair, the active compounds of the two bioassays are more similar, that is,

\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{(\mbox{$B$}_{i},\mbox{$B$}_{j})|\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})>\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{-})~~\text{and}~~\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})>\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{-},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})\}

We identified 329 such pairs.
From
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, we further selected a set of bioassay pairs, denoted as
\mathcal{P}
, such
that in each pair, the active compounds in the two bioassays have a similarity above a certain threshold, that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=

\displaystyle\{(\mbox{$B$}_{i},\mbox{$B$}_{j})|(\mbox{$B$}_{i},\mbox{$B$}_{j})\in\mathcal{P}_{0},

\displaystyle\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})-\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{-})+\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})-\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{-},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})\geq 0.026\},

where 0.026 is the average value of
\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})-\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{-})+\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{+},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})-\text{sim}(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{i}}^{-},\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}_{\scriptsize\mbox{$B$}_{j}}^{+})
among
all the pairs.

After the above process, we identified 120 pairs of bioassays in
\mathcal{P}
, involving 59 bioassays and
7 protein families, with
278 active and 278 inactive compounds in each bioassay on average.
Table S2 presents all the pairs and their compound statistics.

2.1.2
Baseline Methods

We tested our
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
methods with respect to two aspects:
1) compound representations, and 2) transfer mechanisms. Compound representation is
key to revealing information among compounds that can be leveraged to transfer across.
Transfer machenisms are critical to enable effective transfer of revealed information across
bioassays.

2.1.2.1
Compound Representation Methods

Specifically, we compared our compound representation method
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
(i.e., the feature learner in Section
4.2.2
)
with the following compound representation methods:

•

Binary Morgan fingerprint (
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
)
Rogers and Hahn 2010
:

\mathop{\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
uses a binary feature vector to present a compound, in which each dimension of the
feature vector corresponds to a pre-defined substructure, and the binary value in that dimension represents if
the compound has that substructure or not.

•

Morgan count fingerprints (
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan\textrm{-}c}}\limits
)
Rogers and Hahn 2010
:
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan\textrm{-}c}}\limits
is very similar to
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
except that the values in
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan\textrm{-}c}}\limits
represent how many corresponding substructures the compound has.

•

Directed Message Passing Network (
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
)
Yang et al. 2019
:
The
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
method learns molecular structures by passing
messages along directed edges over molecular graphs.
It produces two representations for each bond
through message passing through the two directions along the bond.
Then it learns atom representations from the incoming bond
representations and generates a compound representation using
mean pooling over the atom representations.
The
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits

1
1

1

https://github.com/chemprop/chemprop [accessed 2021-01-22]

method is the state-of-the-art compound embedding learning approach for compound
property prediction.

We generated
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan\textrm{-}c}}\limits
(with radius = 3 and size = 2,048) using RDKit
Landrum 2020
.
In order to only compare the different compound representation methods, not the transfer learning mechanisms,
we used a 2-layer fully-connected network as the classifier
\mathtt{S}
over the above baseline feature representations to predict activity labels.
We used cross-entropy as the loss function for these baseline methods.
The corresponding methods are denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{morganc}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}}\limits
,
respectively.
Note that these three baseline methods do not have information transfer mechanisms – they are
single-task compound prediction methods.

2.1.2.2
Learning Methods for Compound Prediction:

We compared
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
with a transfer learning baseline known as
domain-adversarial neural network, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits

Ganin and Lempitsky 2015
.
We selected
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
because, to the best of our knowledge, there are no existing transfer learning
methods over graph-structured data; and
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
is a standard transfer learning baseline method
used on other data (e.g., images)
Wang and Deng 2018
.
In particular, we adapted
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
to learn compound features
from graph-structured data via
\mathop{\mathtt{GNN}}\limits
(e.g.,
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
or
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
).

\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
consists of three components:
1) a feature extractor that
represents compounds via feature learning;
2) a label predictor that predicts activity labels from learned compound features;
3) a domain classifier that discriminates between the source and
target compounds during training.

\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
learns compound features that can generalize well
from one domain to another
such that the learned features contain little discriminative domain information, and
enable
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
to accurately predict activity labels.

The objective function in
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
consists of two losses:
domain classification loss, and label prediction loss.
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
uses a minimax optimization such that the domain classification loss
is minimized with respect to the domain classifier, and is
maximized with respect to the feature extractor.
Specifically, minimizing the domain classification loss will encourage
the domain classifier to correctly discriminate between the
source and target compounds.
On the other hand, maximizing the domain classification loss will
encourage the learning of generalizable compound features.

The feature learner and discriminators in
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
are learned via a minimax optimization similarly to how the
feature extractor and the domain classifier in
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
are learned.
However,
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
is different from
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
in that

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
learns feature-wise transferability and compound-wise transferability,
while
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
only learns compound-wise transferability.
Furthermore, following Ganin et al.
Ganin and Lempitsky 2015
,

\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
is trained on labeled data from the source domain
and unlabeled data from the target domain.

2.1.3
Experimental Protocols

2.1.3.1
Experimental Settings

In our experiments, we split each of the bioassays in a pair into 10 folds.
For the target bioassay, we used 1 fold for modeling training, 1 fold for validation and 8 folds for testing.
We did the above process 10 times, with a different
training fold each time, and reported the average performance over the 10 times.
The above 1:1:8 training-validation-testing ratio follows a typical setting in transfer learning
Wang et al. 2021
,
where it is assumed that the training data is limited so it is needed to
leverage other tasks via transfer.
We used this cross validation setting because we did not have a benchmark test set for each bioassay; and
10-fold across validation will reduce variance of the model performance.
When we transferred the information from the source bioassay to the target bioassay,
we used all the folds of the source bioassay and the training fold of the target bioassay in
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
in order
to maximize the information content in the source bioassay that could be leveraged.

If the baseline methods do not have an information transfer mechanism (e.g.,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
), we applied an additional setting
to simulate information transfer:
in addition to the target task
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
’s own training compounds, we also used all the compounds from the source task
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(S)}}\limits
as
training data of
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
.
Thus,
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(S)}}\limits
’s compounds will enrich
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
’s training data and bring (i.e., transfer) information from
\mathop{\mathtt{Src}}\limits
directly to
\mathop{\mathtt{Tgt}}\limits
. This setting is referred to as data transfer, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits
.
If we only use
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
’s compounds for training as in conventional single-task models, this setting is denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{noT}}\limits
.

We trained each model using ADAM
Kingma and Ba 2015
optimizer
with an initial learning rate 1e-3.
All the models are trained up to 40 epochs.
We used grid-search to tune all the hyper-parameters such as the
dimension
d
of the compound embedding
{\mathbf{r}}
,
hidden-layer dimension of the attention layer for
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
,
hidden-layer dimension in
\mathtt{L}
and
\mathtt{G}
,
and batch size.
We used the validation set to determine the optimal number of epochs.
During training, we evaluated the performance of each model on the validation set
at every epoch and choose the trained model at some epoch
k

that gives the best performance on the validation set;
thus we selected
k
as the optimal number of epochs.
We used
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits
metric for the above performance evaluation.
All evaluation metrics are discussed in the following section.
All the hyper-parameters are reported in Table S3 for reproducibility purposes.

2.1.3.2
Evaluation Metrics

We used the following evaluation metrics:
area under the precision-recall curve (
\mathop{\mathtt{PR\text{-}AUC}}\limits
), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits
),

\mathop{\mathtt{precision}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{sens}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{accuracy}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{F1}}\limits
score.

•

Area under the Precision-Recall curve (
\mathop{\mathtt{PR\text{-}AUC}}\limits
):
A Precision-Recall curve is generated by (precision, recall) value pairs
corresponding to variable thresholds.

\mathop{\mathtt{PR\text{-}AUC}}\limits
measures the area under the Precision-Recall curve, and
provides an aggregate measure of performance across all possible thresholds.

•

Area under the Receiver Operating Characteristics curve (
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits
):
A Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve is generated by
true positive rates against false positive rates at
various threshold values.

\mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits
measures the area under the ROC curve.

•

\mathop{\mathtt{precision}}\limits
: it is the ratio of correctly predicted
positive instances out of all predicted positive instances
(e.g. the ratio of predicted active compounds that are truly active).

•

\mathop{\mathtt{sens}}\limits
: it is the ratio of correctly predicted positive
instances out of all ground-truth positive instances
(e.g. the ratio of active compounds that are correctly predicted as active).

•

\mathop{\mathtt{accuracy}}\limits
: it is the ratio of correctly predicted
(positive and negative) instances out of all instances
(e.g. the ratio of compounds that are correctly predicted as active/inactive).

•

\mathop{\mathtt{F1}}\limits
-score: it is the harmonic mean of
\mathop{\mathtt{precision}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{sens}}\limits
.

If the above metrics have higher values, they indicate better performance.

\mathop{\mathtt{t\textrm{-}diff\%}}\limits
)
from
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
over
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
across each metric is
5.702%, 6.085%, 4.876%, 25.281%, 7.727%, and 18.464%,
respectively; and these differences are positive and statistically significant
(as indicated by their corresponding p-values in parentheses)
– hence suggesting that
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
significantly improves the task-wise performance over
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
.
In particular,
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
significantly improves the
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits
performance of 199 out of 240 (83%) target tasks
with an average task-wise improvement (i.e.
\mathop{\mathtt{t\textrm{-}impv\%}}\limits
) of 7.102%
(
\mathop{\mathtt{p\textrm{-}value}}\limits
: 5.56e-22).
Such consistent and significant improvement
(demonstrated by
\mathop{\mathtt{t\textrm{-}impv\%}}\limits
and their corresponding p-values)
across all evaluation metrics
on a large percentage of target tasks (demonstrated by
\mathop{\mathtt{N\textrm{-}impv}}\limits
)
provides strong evidence that
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
clearly outperforms
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
on majority of target tasks.
This further implies that the transfer mechanism in
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
is more effective than that in
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
.
While
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
pays equal attention to both the source and target tasks
during training,

\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
can differentially focus on the two tasks
by varying the weightage on the source classification loss
(i.e., the trade-off parameter
\alpha
in equation
3
).
Note that
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
with
\alpha=1
is
methodologically equivalent to
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
.
By varying
\alpha
,
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
can incorporate
different degrees of task relatedness between the source and target tasks during training.
If the two tasks are not that related, a lower
\alpha
will encourage the
learning to focus more on the target task.
In essence, learned compound features are more specific to the target task.
On the other hand,
\alpha
as high as 1 will enforce learning of compound features
that generalize well across the two tasks.
Such features may encode little target task-specific information, and hence,
are not effective.

Furthermore, our experimental results in Table
1
demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed attention
mechanism of
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
in learning better compound features.
Overall, both
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
-based methods (i.e.,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
)
outperform
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
-based methods
(i.e.,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
).
Particularly, compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
,

\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
improves about half of the
target tasks
improves
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC\text{-}AUC}}\limits
of 152 out of 240 (63%) target tasks
and gives significant
performance improvement of 3.443% (
\mathop{\mathtt{p\textrm{-}value}}\limits
: 2.64e-18) on those improved target tasks.
This demonstrates that the proposed attention mechanism in
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
enables more effective compound features
since it can differentially score atoms based on their relevance towards the final task.
However,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}}\limits
either achieves similar or slightly worse performance
compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}}\limits
.
This is because
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
– with slightly more parameters than
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
–
may struggle to capture relevant patterns during training,
and thus
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}}\limits
can easily overfit to limited training data of the target task.
In essence, this can lead to poor generalization performance on the test data.
On the other hand,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
can generalize well since it
is trained on the labeled source data along with the limited target data.
Overall, the attention mechanism can better learn and effectively score the atoms
in
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
but not in
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}}\limits
– thereby
achieving significant improvement in the former over
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
, and marginal improvement in the latter over
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}}\limits
.

We will further demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
in the compound prioritization problem detailed in Section
2.7
.

Furthermore, all
\mathop{\mathtt{GNN}}\limits
-based baselines
(i.e.,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
and

\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
)
significantly outperform
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
-based methods.
Our experimental results show that both
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
-based methods
yield poor or similar performance
compared to all other baseline methods.
Specifically, the best
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
method (i.e.
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
)
reduces the average performance by 3-4%
over the best baseline method
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}~(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{DT}}\limits$})}\limits
across all evaluation metrics.
Such poor performance may be due to the ineffectiveness of domain-invariant compound features
to encode necessary task-specific information.

Surprisingly,
\mathop{\mathtt{DANN}}\limits
even performs worse than the fingerprint-based methods
(i.e.
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{morganc}}\limits
).
As a matter of fact, overall, fingerprint-based methods
performs relatively well compared to all other baselines.
Compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{GNN}}\limits
-based methods,
fingerprint-based methods achieve competitive or even better performance in most evaluation metrics.
This could be due to potential overfitting of
\mathop{\mathtt{GNN}}\limits
-based methods
in low-data settings.
It is known that
\mathop{\mathtt{GNNs}}\limits
require large amounts of training data
to extract relevant molecular substructures
and to effectively encode meaningful task-specific information.
In contrast, fingerprint-based methods are not data-hungry owing to fewer learnable parameters
and thus, these methods can perform reasonably well in low-data setting
Wu et al. 2018
.

We also explored the potential of using

We develop a comprehensive

\mathop{\mathtt{gnnCP}}\limits
for effective compound prioritization
that jointly learns molecular graph representations via
\mathop{\mathtt{GNN}}\limits

using the representations.
The learning methods for compound prioritization is described in Section 4 in the Supplementary
Materials.

2.7.1
Materials

Baselines:

We compare
\mathop{\mathtt{gnnCP}}\limits
with the following feature vectors
using the same scoring and loss functions:
(i) binary Morgan fingerprints (
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan}}\limits
), (ii) morgan count fingerprints, (
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan\textrm{-}c}}\limits
),
(iii) bioassay-specific compound features
Liu and Ning 2017
computed using Tanimoto coefficient on binary Morgan fingerprints (
\mathop{\mathtt{morgan\textrm{-}ba}}\limits

(iv) 200-dimensional RDKit descriptors (
\mathop{\mathtt{RDKit200}}\limits
), and
(v) directed message passing network
Yang et al. 2019
(
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits

We generate binary Morgan fingerprints and Morgan-count fingerprints

Landrum 2020
.
Codes for computing the RDKit descriptors are available in the Descriptastorus package
des
.

Experimental Protocol:

In order to evaluate the overall ranking performance,
we perform 5-fold cross validation.
We randomly split each bioassay into five folds.
In each run, four folds of each bioassay are used for training and the other fold is used for testing.
We record optimal values of each performance metric averaged over the five folds. Finally, we report the average of all such recorded optimal values of each performance metric over all the bioassays.
For each bioassay, we train the models using Adam
Kingma and Ba 2015
optimizer
with an initial learning rate
\in~\{\text{5e-3, 1e-3, 5e-4}\}
.
We use grid-search to tune all the hyperparameters such as
the dimension of the graph representation
d
,
hidden dimension of the attention layer and batch size.
Specifically, we use
d~\in~\{\text{25, 50, 100}\}
for
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
,
hidden dimension of the attention layer
\in~\{\text{5, 10, 20}\}
for
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
.
We use batch size
\in~\{\text{128, 256, 512}\}
, and
\lambda\text{ = 1e-6}

for all the models.
All the models are trained for 50 epochs.

Evaluation:

We evaluate all the methods using a set of 105 single-target
confirmatory bioassays from PubChem
Kim et al. 2020
.
These bioassays all use IC
50
to measure compound binding affinities and have at least 50 active compounds.
For each bioassay, we only keep the active compounds, and remove duplicate compounds and those with identical IC
50
values.
We evaluate the ranking performance using

k
(R@
k
),
Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain@
k
(ndcg@
k
)
Liu and Ning 2017
, where
k=3,5,10

We also use R@
k

3
Conclusions

We have developed
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
that effectively leverages source bioassay data
to improve the performance of the target task.
We also proposed a variant of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
, i.e.,
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
that additionally
learns feature-wise and compound-wise transferability.
We conducted an exhaustive array of experiments and analyses that suggest
that
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
is the best-performing method on average across all target tasks.
The proposed variant is also a very strong method and even better compared to
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
on certain target tasks.
Furthermore, in ablation studies,
we also showed that
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}c\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}}\limits
can even improve
performance for more than half of the target tasks compared to
\mathop{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits$}\text{-}\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}\limits
.
Our analyses further demonstrated that learning compound-wise transferability via
\mathtt{G}
can
better encode the commonalities between compounds across bioassays.
We also provided a parameter study to demonstrate the effect of
\alpha
and
\lambda

on our proposed methods.
Additionally, we demonstrated the efficacy of our proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
in both compound activity and compound prioritization problems
since it performed better than
any other compound representation methods.

In this work, we paired the bioassays if their corresponding protein targets
belong to the same protein family.
In other words, when we paired the bioassays, the corresponding pair of tasks
are assumed to be related.
We assumed that leveraging activity information from related protein targets
(i.e., targets belonging to the same protein family)
can improve the target task performance.
However, we observed
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
did not improve all the targets compared
to the best no-transfer baseline method
\mathop{\mathtt{FCN}\text{-}\mathtt{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits$}}}\limits
.
This suggests occurrence of potential negative transfer.
In future works, we will focus on developing a more principled approach
to determine task-relatedness.
Given a target task, our current method only consider a single source task.
This severely limits the scope of transfer from only one related task, and can also
impact the performance on the target task if the learning is too focused on the source task.
Our future work will incorporate multiple source tasks for each target task
by simultaneously learning task-relatedness in a data-driven manner.

\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}=\{\mbox{$c$}_{i}=(\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i},\mbox{$y$}_{i})|\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i}\in\mbox{${\mathbb{X}}$},\mbox{$y$}_{i}\in\mbox{${\mathbb{Y}}$},i=1,...,|\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}|\}
,
where the compounds
\{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i}\}
are represented in a feature space
{\mathbb{X}}
,
and their activity labels
\{\mbox{$y$}_{i}\}
are represented in a label space
{\mathbb{Y}}
;

|\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}|
is the size of the domain (i.e., the number of
(\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i},\mbox{$y$}_{i})
pairs).
In our transfer learning, we will have two domains:
a source domain, denoted as
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(S)}}\limits
, and a target domain,
denoted as
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(T)}}\limits
.
In general, these two domains can have different numbers of compounds with different
compound feature representations, and also different label sets.
We use superscript
(S)
and
(T)
to represent information associated with the source domain and the target domain,
respectively. For example,
\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}^{(T)}
represents a compound from the target domain.
In addition, we use
\mathcal{X}
to represent the set of compound features
\{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i}\}
, that is,

\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}=\{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i}|\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i}\in\mbox{${\mathbb{X}}$}\}
,
and
\mathcal{Y}
to represent the set of compound labels, that is,
\mbox{$\mathcal{Y}$}=\{\mbox{$y$}_{i}|\mbox{$y$}_{i}\in\mbox{${\mathbb{Y}}$}\}
.
Thus,
{\mathcal{D}}
can also be represented as
\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}=(\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$},\mbox{$\mathcal{Y}$})
.

•

Task: Given a domain
\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}=\{(\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i},\mbox{$y$}_{i})_{i=1,...,|\scriptsize{\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}|}}\}
,
a task
{\mathcal{T}}
is to learn a model that maps each
\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}_{i}
to its corresponding
\mbox{$y$}_{i}
.
In our transfer learning, we will have two tasks: a source task, denoted as
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(S)}}\limits
, and a target task,
denoted as
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
.
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(S)}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
learn from the source domain
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(S)}}\limits
and
the target domain
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(T)}}\limits
, respectively.

•

Transfer Learning: Transfer learning learns and transfer information from source task
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(S)}}\limits
to
the target task
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
, and helps improvement the performance of
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
.
The underlying assumptions are 1) the target domain
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(T)}}\limits
does not have
sufficient information for
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
to learn a good model; and 2) there are commonalities between

\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(S)}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(T)}}\limits
and such commonalities can be transferred from
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(S)}}\limits
to
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(T)}}\limits
,
and used to improve
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
.

Methods

In this section, we present our two transfer learning methods:

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
.
We first introduce the overall architecture of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
in section
4.2.1
, and then
discuss each component in detail in subsequent sections
(i.e., sections
4.2.2
and
4.2.3
).
We discuss the end-to-end optimization process in section
4.2.4
.
We then introduce
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
with additional components
that learns feature-wise and compound-wise transferability, and finally discuss
the optimization process in section
4.2.5
.

4.2.1
Overall Architecture of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
learns to generate transferable features that can
generalize well from one domain to another,
and increase the predictive power for classification in the target domain.
Figure
6
presents the overall architecture of the proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
consists of two components:
1) a feature learner
\mathtt{F}
,
that learns to represent chemical compounds;
and
2) a domain-wise classifier
\mathtt{S}
,
that classifies chemical compounds of each domain.
Below, we discuss each component of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
in detail.

:
Proposed architecture of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
. The feature learner
\mathtt{F}
learns compound representations
{\mathbf{r}}

given the corresponding molecular graph.
The domain-wise classifier

classifies the compound as active/inactive.

4.2.2
Learning Compound Representations

This section describes how the feature representations
of compounds are learned.

In
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
, the feature representations of chemical compounds are learned in a data-driven fashion.
Compared to using static fingerprints or fixed feature representations of molecular structures
Rogers and Hahn 2010
,
such learned features will be more adapted to the learning task and enable optimal performance.
We leverage the popular idea of graph neural networks
Scarselli et al. 2009
, and use the Directed Message Passing Neural Network,
denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
, developed in Yang et al.
Yang et al. 2019
.
Given a molecular graph
\mbox{${\mathcal{G}}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$c$}}}=(\mbox{$\mathcal{A}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$c$}}},\mbox{$\mathcal{E}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$c$}}})

for a compound
c
,

\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
learns a feature vector, also called an embedding, of
c
using graph convolution,
by passing messages along directed edges over molecular graphs.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
, two representations for each bond are learned via message passing through the two directions along
the bond.
Then atom representations are learned from the representations of their incoming bonds.
In the end, the compound representation is generated via mean pooling over all the atom representations.
Details about
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
are presented in Section 1 in the Supplementary Materials, and also available
in Yang et al.
Yang et al. 2019

Based on
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
, we further improve the compound representation learning by
introducing an attention mechanism
inspired from Graph Attention Networks
Veličković et al. 2018
.
This new method is referred to as
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
with attention,
denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
.
Specifically, we replace the mean-pooling in
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
with an attention-based pooling mechanism as follows,

\mbox{${\mathbf{r}}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$c$}}}=\sum_{u\in\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathcal{A}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$c$}}}}}(\mathbbm{1}+w_{u})\odot\mbox{${\mathbf{s}}$}_{u},

(1)

where
\odot
is the element-wise product,

\mbox{${\mathbf{s}}$}_{u}
is the learned representation of atom
u
as in
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
,

w_{u}
is the attention weight on atom
u
calculated as follows,

w_{u}=\frac{\exp({\mbox{$f_{a}$}(\mbox{${\mathbf{s}}$}_{u})})}{\sum_{v\in\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathcal{A}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$c$}}}}}\exp({\mbox{$f_{a}$}(\mbox{${\mathbf{s}}$}_{v})})}

(2)

where
\mbox{$f_{a}$}(\cdot)
is a 2-layer feed-forward network
with a ReLU activation function after the hidden layer. That is, the attention learns a specific weight on each atom.
Thus, the attention mechanism in
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
can differentially focus on atoms based on the relevance of each atom
towards the final predictive task. The network to learn compound embeddings is denoted as
\mathtt{F}
(i.e.,
\mathtt{F}
is
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpn}}\limits
or
\mathop{\mathtt{dmpna}}\limits
).

4.2.3
Learning to Classify Compounds of Each Domain

This section describes how the compounds of each domain
are classified as active/inactive using the learned feature representations.

Given the compound embedding
{\mathbf{r}}
,
the domain-wise classifier classifies each compound in a given domain
as active or inactive with respect to that domain
using a two-layer fully-connected neural network
\mathtt{S}
as follows,

\hat{y}=\mbox{$\mathtt{S}$}(\mbox{${\mathbf{r}}$}),

(3)

with ReLU at the hidden layer and sigmoid at the output layer.
The outputs of
\mathtt{S}
are the probabilities of input compounds
from source/target domain being active in the
source/target domain.

To learn
\mathtt{S}
,
the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{(c)}
for the classifier
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(c)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Phi$})=&&-\alpha\frac{1}{n^{(S)}}\sum_{\scriptsize{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}^{(S)}}\in\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(S)}}}[y^{(S)}\log(\hat{y}^{(S)})+(1-y^{(S)})\log(1-\hat{y}^{(S)})]\\
&&&-\frac{1}{n^{(T)}}\sum_{\scriptsize{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}^{(T)}}\in\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(T)}}}[y^{(T)}\log(\hat{y}^{(T)})+(1-y^{(T)})\log(1-\hat{y}^{(T)})],\end{aligned}

(4)

where
y^{(S)}/y^{(T)}
is the ground-truth activity label of each compound in domain
S
/
T
,

n^{(S)}
/
n^{(T)}
is the number of compounds
in
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(S)}
/
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(T)}
(i.e.,
n=|\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}|
),

\alpha
is a hyperparameter to trade-off the two classification losses,
and

\Omega
and
\Phi
are learnable parameters of
\mathtt{F}
and
\mathtt{S}
,
respectively.
Please note that both the source domain and target domain use the same classifier
\mathtt{S}
.
Therefore, if the source and target domain have common compounds or very similar compounds,
when these compounds have same labels in the two domains, they will induce small classification errors in the both domains;
when these compounds have different labels in the two domains, they will induce large errors in one domain and small errors in the other.
By minimizing the loss
\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(c)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Phi$})
, it will encourage common or similar compounds that have
same labels in the two domains to be more focused on through learning, and prevent the transfer of conflicting
information across domains.

4.2.4
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
Model Optimization

This section presents the optimization process
of the proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
constructs an end-to-end transfer learning framework with the
above two components: 1) feature learner
\mathtt{F}
,
and 2) domain-wise classifier
\mathtt{S}
.
We solve for
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
through minimizing the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{(c)}
.
In other words, we solve the following optimization problem:

\widehat{\mbox{$\Omega$}},\widehat{\mbox{$\Phi$}}=\arg\min_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Phi$}}}\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(c)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Phi$}),

(5)

where
\Omega
and
\Phi
are the learnable parameters of
\mathtt{F}
and
\mathtt{S}
, respectively.
Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{(c)}
will minimize the classification error in each domain
while preventing transfer of conflicting information across domains,
hence enabling the feature learner
\mathtt{F}
to learn better compound features
for effective classification in each domain.
Since the same
\mathtt{F}
and
\mathtt{S}
are used for both the source and target tasks,
minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{(c)}
also enables transfer of relevant information
through the shared parameters of
\mathtt{F}
and
\mathtt{S}
.
Intuitively, the amount of transferable information from the source domain
to the target domain is determined by the degree of task-relatedness between those domains.
In this work, the degree of task-relatedness between the source and target domains
is essentially controlled through the hyperparameter
\alpha
.
We will consider learning task-relatedness or
\alpha
in
a data-driven manner in our future works.

4.2.5
Variant of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
:
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits

In this section, we propose a variant of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
where we
incorporate additional components to selectively learn feature-wise and compound-wise transferability.
We denote this variant as
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
. Figure
7
presents the overall architecture of the
proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
.
In addition to the feature learner
\mathtt{F}
and the label classifier
\mathtt{S}
,

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
consists of two more components:
1) a feature-wise discriminator
\mathtt{L}
,
that learns the transferability of each learned feature (Section
4.2.5.1
);
and
2) a compound-wise discriminator
\mathtt{G}
,
that separates chemical compounds into source and target domains (Section
4.2.5.2
).
We refer to
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
with the feature-wise discriminator only as
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}f}}\limits
,
and
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc}}\limits
with the compound-wise discriminator only as
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}c}}\limits
.
Below, we discuss each component in detail and also the optimization of the
proposed method.

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
.

\mathtt{F}
learns compound embedding
{\mathbf{r}}

given the corresponding molecular graph.
The feature-wise discriminator
\mathtt{L}
learns feature-wise transferability given

{\mathbf{r}}
.

is further scaled into

using its

.

.

4.2.5.1
Learning Transferability of Individual Features

Given the learned compound embedding

\mbox{${\mathbf{r}}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
out of
\mathtt{F}
(discussed in Section
4.2.2
), the feature-wise discriminator of
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
learns
the transferability of each embedding feature in
{\mathbf{r}}
using a two-layer neural network
\mathtt{L}
as follows,

\mathbf{p}=\mbox{$\mathtt{L}$}(\mbox{${\mathbf{r}}$}),

(6)

where
\mathtt{L}
has a hidden layer with ReLU, and an output layer with sigmoid.
Note that
\mathbf{p}=[p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{d}]
has the same
dimension as
{\mathbf{r}}
, and
p_{i}\in[0,1]
represents
the probability that the
i
-th embedding feature in
{\mathbf{r}}

is specific to the source domain.
Thus, the feature-wise discriminator determines whether the input compound features (not the input compounds) belong to the
source domain or not.
For bioactivity prediction problems, if
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(S)}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathcal{D}^{(T)}}\limits
have compounds for protein targets that are from
a same protein family, it is very likely that their active compounds are similar and share similar substructures (e.g., pharmacophores).
In this case, intuitively, the feature-wise discriminator here could learn and represent such similar substructures.

We further quantify the transferability of each embedding feature using its entropies as follows,

\mbox{${H}$}(p_{i})=-p_{i}\log p_{i}-(1-p_{i})\log(1-p_{i}).

(7)

If
p_{i}
is very large or very small and it will have a low entropy,
it indicates the
i
-th embedding feature is very likely or very unlikely to be specific to the source domain,
and thus it is less likely to be common across domains;
if
p_{i}
is close to 0.5 and with a high entropy,
the feature is less specific to any of the domains and more likely to be common across domains, and therefore
can be used for information transfer across domains.

We then scale compound embedding
{\mathbf{r}}
into
{\mathbf{z}}
using feature entropies as follows,

\mbox{${\mathbf{z}}$}=(\mathbbm{1}+\boldsymbol{\mbox{${H}$}})\odot\mbox{${\mathbf{r}}$},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\mbox{${H}$}}=[\mbox{${H}$}(p_{1}),\mbox{${H}$}(p_{2}),...,\mbox{${H}$}(p_{d})]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\odot
represents element-wise dot-product.
Each feature is scaled with its entropy and added with itself.
Intuitively, the self-addition reduces the loss of informative features
due to improper scaling.
Thus, in
{\mathbf{z}}
, domain-invariant embedding features are scaled larger than
domain-specific embedding features (
\boldsymbol{\mbox{${H}$}}\geq 0
).
We will use
{\mathbf{z}}
as input to the following components.

To learn the feature-wise discriminator, the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(l)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Theta$})&=&-\frac{1}{n^{(S)}}\sum_{\scriptsize{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}^{(S)}\in\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(S)}}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1...d}\log(p^{(S)}_{i})\\
&&&-\frac{1}{n^{(T)}}\sum_{\scriptsize{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}^{(T)}\in\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(T)}}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1...d}\log(1-p^{(T)}_{i}),\end{aligned}

(9)

where
n^{(S)}
/
n^{(T)}
is the number of compounds
in
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(S)}
/
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(T)}
(i.e.,
n=|\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}|
);

\Omega
and
\Theta
are learnable parameters of

\mathtt{F}
(compound representation learning network as in Section
4.2.2
)
and
\mathtt{L}
(feature-wise discriminator network as in Equation
6
), respectively;
and
d
is the dimension of compound feature embeddings.
Note that in Equation
9
,
p^{(S)}_{i}
and
p^{(T)}_{i}
both measure an embedding feature’s probability of being specific
to the
source domain
; superscripts
(S)
/
(T)
here indicate that the compounds,
whose features are measured, are from source/target domain, respectively.

To have an accurate feature-wise discriminator, embedding features specific to the source/target domain should have large/small probabilities
(i.e., large
p^{(S)}_{i}
and small
p^{(T)}_{i}
)
with respect to the source domain, and thus make the
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
value small.
Therefore, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
will encourage accurate probabilities.
Meanwhile, the feature learner
\mathtt{F}
should encourage the learning of more transferable embedding features, which will have probabilities close to 0.5
and thus make the
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
value large. Therefore, maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
will encourage more transferable embedding features
being learned and learned well.
To combine these two aspects, an adversarial optimization will be applied to
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
as will be described later in Section
4.2.5.3
.

4.2.5.2
Learning Transferability of Compounds

Inspired by the principle that similar compounds tend to bind to similar protein targets,
our method identifies such similar compounds that have
same activity labels across two targets, and hence learns compound-wise transferability.

Given the scaled compound embedding
{\mathbf{z}}
of compound
c
,
the compound-wise discriminator classifies whether the compound
is from the source domain using a two-layer fully-connected neural network
\mathtt{G}
as follows,

q=\mbox{$\mathtt{G}$}(\mbox{${\mathbf{z}}$}),

(10)

with ReLU at the hidden layer and sigmoid at the output layer.
If
q
is very large or very small,
c
is very likely or
very unlikely to belong to the source domain (it is equivalent to calculate the value with respect to the target domain, since
there are only two domains to consider).
If
q
is close to 0.5,
c
is likely to be common
across domains (e.g., identical or similar compounds in the two domains) and thus can
be used for information transfer across domains.

To learn the compound-wise discriminator, the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(g)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Psi$})=&-\frac{1}{n^{(S)}}\sum_{\scriptsize{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}^{(S)}}\in\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(S)}}}\log(q^{(S)})\\
&-\frac{1}{n^{(T)}}\sum_{\scriptsize{\mbox{${\mathbf{x}}$}^{(T)}}\in\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(T)}}}\log(1-q^{(T)}),\end{aligned}

(11)

where
n^{(S)}
/
n^{(T)}
is the number of compounds
in
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(S)}
/
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}^{(T)}
(i.e.,
n=|\mbox{${\mathcal{D}}$}|
);

\Omega
and
\Psi
are learnable parameters of

\mathtt{F}
(compound representation learning network as in Section
4.2.2
)
and
\mathtt{G}
(Equation
10
);
and
d
is the dimension of compound feature embeddings.
Note that in Equation
11
,

q^{(S)}
and
q^{(T)}
represent the probability of
\mbox{$c$}^{(S)}
and
\mbox{$c$}^{(T)}
belonging to the
source domain
.
Also, all the compounds from the source and target domains will be predicted using a same
\mathtt{G}
.

In order to identify similar compounds across domains, the discriminator needs to identify compounds with their
q
values close to 0.5;
when the
q
values are close 0.5,
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
will be maximized. Therefore, maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
will encourage more
transferable compounds being learned and learned well.
Meanwhile, to have an accurate compound-wise discriminator, compounds specific to the source/target domain should have large/small
probabilities (i.e., large
q^{(S)}_{i}
and small
q^{(T)}_{i}
) with respect to the source domain, and thus make the
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
value small.
Therefore, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
will encourage accurate probabilities.
To combine these two aspects, similarly as to
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
,
an adversarial optimization will be applied to
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
as will be described later in Section
4.2.5.3
.

According to
\mathtt{G}
, a compound that is common in the two domains, or is similar to compounds in the other domain,
could be transferable (
q
value close to 0.5; not specific to source or target domain).
However, such common or similar compounds may have different activity labels in the two domains.
Using transfered information
from common/similar compounds with conflicting labels in
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
will confuse any learners adversely.
The compound-wise discriminator
\mathtt{G}
does not consider activity label information in learning compound transferability, and thus
possibly induces conflicting information into
\mathop{\mathcal{T}^{(T)}}\limits
.
However, in the downstream domain-wise classification (Section
4.2.3
), the minimization of domain-specific
classification errors will prevent the transfer of conflicting information.

However, the input to the domain-wise classifier
\mathtt{S}
in
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
is
{\mathbf{z}}
instead of
{\mathbf{r}}
as in Section
4.2.3
.
Given the scaled compound embedding
{\mathbf{z}}
,
the domain-wise classifier classifies each compound in a given domain
as active or inactive with respect to that domain
using a two-layer fully-connected neural network
\mathtt{S}
as follows,

\hat{y}=\mbox{$\mathtt{S}$}(\mbox{${\mathbf{z}}$}),

(12)

with ReLU at the hidden layer and sigmoid at the output layer.
As discussed in Section
4.2.3
,
minimizing the loss
\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(c)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Phi$})

enables correct classification in each domain,
and prevent the transfer of conflicting information across domains.

4.2.5.3
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
Model Optimization

This section presents the optimization process
of the proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
constructs an end-to-end adversarial transfer learning framework with the
above four components: 1) compound feature presentation learning network
\mathtt{F}
,
2) feature-wise discriminator
\mathtt{L}
, 3) compound-wise discriminator
\mathtt{G}
, and 4) domain-wise classifier

\mathtt{S}
. We solve for
\mathop{\mathtt{TAc\text{-}fc}}\limits
through optimizing the following loss function:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mbox{$\mathcal{L}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Theta$},\mbox{$\Psi$},\mbox{$\Phi$})=-\lambda[(\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(l)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Theta$})+\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(g)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Psi$})]+{\mbox{$\mathcal{L}_{(c)}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Phi$})},\end{aligned}

(13)

where
\Omega
,
\Theta
,
\Psi
and
\Phi
are learnable
parameters of
\mathtt{F}
,
\mathtt{L}
,
\mathtt{G}
and
\mathtt{S}
, respectively,
and
\lambda
is a trade-off parameter. This loss function combines the three loss functions for
\mathtt{L}
,
\mathtt{G}
and
\mathtt{S}
, and will be optimized in an
adversarial way as follows:

(Step 1). Minimize
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\Omega
and
\Phi
via solving the following optimization problem:

\widehat{\mbox{$\Omega$}},\widehat{\mbox{$\Phi$}}=\arg\min_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Phi$}}}\mbox{$\mathcal{L}$}(\mbox{$\Omega$},\mbox{$\Theta$},\mbox{$\Psi$},\mbox{$\Phi$}).

(14)

By minimizing
\mathcal{L}
, we essentially minimize
\mathcal{L}_{(c)}
, and maximize

\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
and
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
. As discussed in
\mathtt{L}
(Section
4.2.5.1
) and

\mathtt{G}
(Section
4.2.5.2
), maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{(l)}
and
\mathcal{L}_{(g)}
will encourage
learning of transferable features and compounds that can be used to help the tasks; as discussed in
\mathtt{S}
(Section
4.2.3
), minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{(c)}
will prevent the transfer of conflicting information, in addition to
minimizing the classification errors in each task.

(Step 2). Maximize
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\Theta
and
\Psi
via solving the following optimization problem:
