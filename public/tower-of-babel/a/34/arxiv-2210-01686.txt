Asymptotic behavior of Markov complexity of matrices
Shmuel Onn, Apostolos Thoma and Marius Vladoiu

Abstract
. To any integer matrix A A one can associate a matroid structure consisting of a graph and another integer matrix A B A_{B} . The connected components of this graph are called bouquets. We prove that bouquets behave well with respect to the r r –th Lawrence liftings of matrices and we use it to prove that the Markov and Graver complexities of m × n m\times n matrices of rank d d may be arbitrarily large for n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 and d ≤ n − 2 d\leq n-2 . In contrast, we show they are bounded in terms of n n and the largest absolute value a a of any entry of A A .

1.
Introduction

Let
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m\times n}
be an integer matrix of rank
d
and
r\geq 2
.
The
r
–th
Lawrence lifting
of
A
is denoted by
A^{(r)}
and is the
(rm+n)\times rn
matrix

A^{(r)}=\begin{array}[]{c}\overbrace{\hskip 8.50012pt\hskip 8.50012pt\hskip 8.50012pt\hskip 8.50012pt\hskip 8.50012pt\ \ }^{r-\textrm{times}}\\
\begin{pmatrix}\ A\ &0&\ldots&0\\
0&\ A\ &\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&\ A\ \\
I_{n}&I_{n}&\cdots&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\end{array}.

We identify an element of
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A^{(r)})
with an
r\times n
matrix: each row of this matrix corresponds to an element of

\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
and the sum of its rows is zero. The
type
of an element of
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A^{(r)})
is the number of nonzero rows of this matrix.
The
Markov complexity
,
m(A)
, is the largest type of any vector in the universal Markov basis of
A^{(r)}
as
r
varies. The universal Markov basis
is the union of all minimal Markov bases,
[
11
]
.

Markov complexity measures the complexity of Markov bases for hierarchical models in Algebraic Statistics. The finiteness of Markov
complexity was first observed by Aoki and Takemura, in
[
1
]
,
and the theory of Markov complexity was developed by Santos and Sturmfels in
[
22
]
. Recent advances in understanding Markov complexity
[
13
]
and the development of the bouquets algebra
[
20
]
permit us to answer the problem
of the maximal Markov complexity for any
m\times n
integer matrix of rank
d
.

We will show that the maximal Markov complexity of
m\times n
integer matrices of rank
d
follows the following patern

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}0&2&3&\infty&\infty&\cdots&\infty\\
&0&2&\infty&\infty&\cdots&\infty\\
&&0&2&\infty&\cdots&\infty\\
&&&0&2&\cdots&\infty\\
\end{array}

where the columns correspond to
n\geq 1
, the rows to the rank
1\leq d\leq n
and
\infty
means that matrices of the corresponding dimensions may have arbitrary large Markov complexity.
A specific matrix has always finite Markov complexity since the toric ideals of Lawrence liftings are
positively graded, see
[
5
]

and therefore the Markov complexity is smaller than or equal to the Graver complexity, which is always finite, see
[
22
]
.

Remark that if
d=n
then the columns of
A
are linearly independent and the kernel of
A
and
A^{(r)}
, for every
r\geq 2
are zero, therefore the Markov complexity of those matrices is zero.
In the case that
d=n-1
the kernel of
A
is generated by one vector

{\bf u}
and the kernel of
A^{(r)}
is generated by matrices of type 2, making the Markov complexity equal to 2.

The case of
1\times 3
matrices was solved in
[
4
]
, where it was shown that the Markov complexity of an
1\times 3
matrix is equal to
two if the toric ideal
I_{A}
is complete intersection and equal to three otherwise.
Thus the maximal Markov complexity is three for
1\times 3
matrices.
Recently, in
[
13
]
, the case of
1\times n
,
n\geq 4
, was solved and it was proved that such
1\times n
matrices may have arbitrarily large Markov complexity. In this article one main result is that
2\times 4
matrices have arbitrarily large Markov complexity, see Theorem 5.1.
Then,
[
13
, Theorem 2.4]
, which states that elimination behaves well with Markov complexity, provides a way to move horizontally from
(d,n)
-matrices that have arbitrarily large Markov complexity to prove that
(d,n+k)
-matrices have arbitrarily large Markov complexity. The bouquet’s results of Section 3 move you diagonally from
(d,n)
-matrices that have arbitrarily large Markov complexity to prove that

(d+k,n+k)
-matrices have arbitrarily large Markov complexity. The four results,
[
13
, Theorem 4.1]
, Theorem
5.1
,
[
13
, Theorem 2.4]
and Theorem
3.6
, show that if we only bound the matrix dimensions then the Markov and Graver complexities can be arbitrarily large, the content of Theorem
6.1
. In contrast, based on recent results on sparse integer programming from
[
9
,
14
,
15
]
, we show in Theorem
7.2
that the Markov and Graver complexities are bounded in terms of
n
and the largest absolute value
a
of any entry of
A
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\Bbbk
be a field and
A=[{\mathbb{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{a}}_{n}]\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m\times n}
an integer matrix whose
column vectors are
{\mathbb{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{a}}_{n}
. We recall that the toric ideal of
A
is the ideal

I_{A}\subseteq\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, generated by all binomials of the form
x^{{\mathbb{u}}}-x^{{\mathbb{w}}}
,
where
{\mathbb{u}}-{\mathbb{w}}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
. The columns of
A
impose an
A
-grading
on
\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
by defining the
A
-degree of
x_{i}
to be
{\mathbb{a}}_{i}
.
The toric ideal
I_{A}
is
A
-homogeneous. Furthermore, if
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{n}=\{{\bf 0}\}
then this grading is positive.

A
Markov basis

{\mathcal{M}}
of
A
is a finite subset of
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
such that whenever
{\bf w},{\bf u}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
{\bf w}-{\bf u}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
, there exists a subset
\{{\bf v}_{i}:i=1,\ldots,p\}
of
{\mathcal{M}}
that connects
{\bf w}
to
{\bf u}
, that is
({\mathbb{w}}-{{\sum^{l}_{i=1}}}{{\mathbb{v}}_{i}})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
for all
1\leq l\leq p
and
{\bf w}-{\bf u}=\sum^{p}_{i=1}{\bf v}_{i}
. We call a Markov basis
{\mathcal{M}}
of
A

minimal
if no proper subset of
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Markov basis of
A
.
For a vector
{\bf u}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
, we denote by
\bf u^{+}
,
{\bf u}^{-}
the unique vectors in
{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
such that
{\mathbb{u}}={\mathbb{u}}^{+}-{\mathbb{u}}^{-}
. According to a classical result by Diaconis and Sturmfels, if
{\mathcal{M}}
is a minimal Markov basis of
A
, then the set
\{x^{\bf u^{+}}-x^{\bf u^{-}}:\ {\bf u}\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
is a minimal generating set of
I_{A}
(see
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
). The union of all minimal Markov bases of
A
, where we identify elements that differ by a sign, is called the
universal Markov basis
of
A
and is denoted by
{{\mathcal{M}}}(A)
(see
[
11
, Definition 3.1]
).

The
indispensable subset
of the universal Markov basis
{\mathcal{M}}(A)
, which is denoted by
{\mathcal{S}}(A)
, is the intersection of all minimal Markov bases of
A
via the same identification.
The
Graver basis
of
A
,
{\mathcal{G}}(A)
, is the subset of
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
whose elements have no
proper conformal decomposition
; namely, an element
{\bf u}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
belongs to the Graver basis
{\mathcal{G}}(A)
if whenever
{\bf u}
can be written in the form
{\bf v}+_{c}{\bf w}
,
where
{\bf v},{\bf w}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
and
{\bf u}^{+}={\bf v}^{+}+{\bf w}^{+}
,
{\bf u}^{-}={\bf v}^{-}+{\bf w}^{-}
,
we conclude that either
{\bf v}={\bf 0}
or
{\bf w}={\bf 0}
, see
[
23
, Chapter 4]
.
The
Graver basis
of
A
is always a finite set and in the case that the ideal
I_{A}
is homogeneous
for a positive grading it contains the universal Markov basis of
A
, see
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
. The
ideal
I_{A}
is homogeneous
for a positive grading if and only if

\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{n}=\{{\bf 0}\}
.
Note that disregarding if the ideal
I_{A}
is or is
not homogeneous for a positive grading the higher Lawrence liftings
A^{(r)}
of
A
always are,
since the row with all its entries 1 belongs to the row span of the matrix
A^{(r)}
. Therefore, we have the following inclusions

{\mathcal{S}}(A^{(r)})\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}(A^{(r)})\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(A^{(r)}).

For the later proofs we will need the algebraic characterization of indispensable elements:

Proposition 2.1
.

[
4
, Proposition 1.1]

The set of indispensable elements
{{\mathcal{S}}}(A)
of
A
consists of all nonzero vectors in
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}({A})
with no proper semiconformal decomposition.

We recall from
[
11
, Definition 3.9]
that for vectors
{\mathbb{u}},{\mathbb{v}},{\mathbb{w}}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
such that
{\mathbb{u}}={\mathbb{v}}+{\mathbb{w}}
, the sum is said to be a semiconformal decomposition of
{\mathbb{u}}
, written
{\mathbb{u}}={\mathbb{v}}+_{sc}{\mathbb{w}}
, if
v_{i}>0
implies that
w_{i}\geq 0
, and
w_{i}<0
implies that
v_{i}\leq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Here
v_{i}
denotes the
i^{th}
coordinate of the vector
{\mathbb{v}}
. As before, the decomposition is called
proper
if both
{\mathbb{v}},{\mathbb{w}}
are nonzero.
We remark that
\bf 0
cannot be written as the semiconformal sum of two nonzero vectors in the case that
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{n}=\{\bf 0\}
. Note also, that in a semiconformal sum
{\mathbb{u}}={\mathbb{v}}+_{sc}{\mathbb{w}}
the following situations occur coordinate-wise: 1) if
u_{i}>0
then
v_{i}
can take any value and
w_{i}\geq 0
, 2) if
u_{i}<0
then
v_{i}\leq 0
and
w_{i}
can take any value, and 3) if
u_{i}=0
then
v_{i}\leq 0
and
w_{i}\geq 0
. Resuming these three cases to symbols we have:
(\dots,+,\dots)=(\dots,*,\dots)+_{sc}(\dots,\oplus,\dots)
,
(\dots,-,\dots)=(\dots,\ominus,\dots)+_{sc}(\dots,*,\dots)
,
(\dots,0,\dots)=(\dots,\ominus,\dots)+_{sc}(\dots,\oplus,\dots)
, where the symbol
\ominus
means that the corresponding integer is non positive, the symbol
\oplus
non negative and the symbol
*
means that it can take any value.

3.
Markov complexity and Bouquet ideals

To any toric ideal
I_{A}\subseteq\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, encoded by an integer matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m\times n}
,
one can associate another toric ideal, called the “bouquet ideal”,
denoted by
I_{A_{B}}
. For details of this construction see
[
20
, Section 1]
,
and how relate in general the properties of the bouquet toric ideal
I_{A_{B}}
to
the starting toric ideal
I_{A}
, see
[
20
,
21
]
. Next, we revisit a few technical
details of the bouquet construction, needed for proofs. We recall that two column
vectors
{\mathbb{a}}_{i},{\mathbb{a}}_{j}
are in the same bouquet if there exists a vector
{\mathbb{v}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}

such that the matrix product
{\mathbb{v}}\cdot A
is a vector with only two nonzero components,
those appearing on the
i
-th and
j
-th positions. In other words, this is equivalent
to saying that the dot products
{\mathbb{v}}\cdot{\mathbb{a}}_{k}=0
for all
k\neq i,j
and
{\mathbb{v}}\cdot{\mathbb{a}}_{i}\neq 0
,
{\mathbb{v}}\cdot{\mathbb{a}}_{j}\neq 0
. Then, if
A
has
q
bouquets then, without loss of generality, we may assume that there exist positive integers
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{q}
such that the first
i_{1}
column vectors
{\mathbb{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{a}}_{i_{1}}
belong to the first bouquet, the next
i_{2}
column vectors belong to the second bouquet, and so on the last
i_{q}
vectors belong to the
q
-th bouquet, thus in particular
i_{1}+\cdots+i_{q}=n
.

Next, we recall from
[
20
]
, the definition of the
bouquet-index-encoding vector

{\mathbb{c}}_{B}
,
since the proof of Theorem
3.4
requires the basics of its definition and properties.
If the bouquet
B
is free then we set
{\mathbb{c}}_{B}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
to be any nonzero vector such that

\operatorname{supp}({\mathbb{c}}_{B})=\{i:{\mathbb{a}}_{i}\in B\}
and with the property that the first nonzero coordinate is positive.
For a non-free bouquet
B
of
A
, consider the Gale transforms of the elements in
B
.
All the elements are nonzero and pairwise linearly dependent, therefore there exists a nonzero
coordinate
j
in all of them. Let
g_{j}=\gcd(G({\bf a}_{i})_{j}|\ {\bf a}_{i}\in B)
and fix the smallest
integer
i_{0}
such that
{\bf a}_{i_{0}}\in B
. Let
{\bf c}_{B}
be the vector in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
whose
i
-th coordinate
is
0
if
{\bf a}_{i}\notin B
, and is
\varepsilon_{i_{0}j}G({\bf a}_{i})_{j}/g_{j}
if
{\bf a}_{i}\in B
, where
\varepsilon_{i_{0}j}
represents the sign of the integer
G({\bf a}_{i_{0}})_{j}
.
Thus the
\operatorname{supp}({\bf c}_{B})=\{i:{\bf a}_{i}\in B\}
. Note that the choice of
i_{0}
implies that the first nonzero coordinate of
{\bf c}_{B}
is positive. Since each
{\bf a}_{i}
belongs to exactly one bouquet the supports of the vectors
{\bf c}_{B_{i}}
are pairwise disjoint. In addition,
\cup_{i}\operatorname{supp}({\mathbb{c}}_{B_{i}})=[n]
.
For each bouquet
B
we define the vector

{\mathbb{a}}_{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{B})_{i}{\mathbb{a}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,
where
(c_{B})_{i}
is the
i
-th component of the vector
{\mathbb{c}}_{B}
. The matrix
A_{B}
whose column vectors are the vectors
{\mathbb{a}}_{B}

corresponding to the bouquets of
A
is called the
bouquet matrix
of
A
.

Finally, we recall that (see
[
20
, Theorem 1.9]
) the matrix
A_{B}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m\times q}
has its kernel
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{B})
isomorphic to
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
via the isomorphism
D:\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{B})\mapsto\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
given by

D(u_{1},\ldots,u_{q})=(c_{11}u_{1},\ldots,c_{1i_{1}}u_{1},c_{21}u_{2},\ldots,c_{2i_{2}}u_{2},\ldots,c_{q1}u_{q},\ldots,c_{qi_{q}}u_{q}),

where the integers
c_{11},c_{21},\ldots,c_{q1}
are the first nonzero coordinates of the vectors
{\mathbb{c}}_{B_{1}}
,
{\mathbb{c}}_{B_{2}},\ldots,{\mathbb{c}}_{B_{q}}
, and thus, all positive. Note that the vector in the image has
n
coordinates, and that
D
can be naturally extended to a linear map
D^{\prime}:{\mathbb{Q}}^{q}\mapsto{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
. The inverse of the map
D
we are going to denote it by
T
, and
T:\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)\mapsto\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{B})
is defined

T(v_{11},\ldots,v_{1i_{1}},v_{21},\ldots,v_{2i_{2}},\ldots,v_{q1},\ldots,v_{qi_{q}})=(v_{11}/c_{11},\ldots,v_{i1}/c_{i1},\ldots,v_{q1}/c_{q1}),

and admits, as
D
, a natural extension to a linear map
T^{\prime}:{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}\mapsto{\mathbb{Q}}^{q}
. Here, we used in the definition of
D
and
T
, the assumption that the first
i_{1}
column vectors of
A
belong to the first bouquet, and so forth.

The next theorem generalizes
[
20
, Theorem 1.11]
for Markov bases.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\{{\bf u}_{1},\ldots,{\bf u}_{t}\}
is a minimal Markov basis for
A
then
\{T({\bf u}_{1}),\ldots,T({\bf u}_{t})\}
is a Markov basis for
A_{B}
, not necessarily minimal.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=\{{\bf u}_{1},\ldots,{\bf u}_{t}\}
be a minimal Markov basis of
A
. Consider
{\bf w},{\bf u}\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
such that
{\bf w}-{\bf u}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{B})
, and we look then at the elements
D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-}
and
D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{-}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{+}
of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Since

(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-})-(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{-}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{+})=D^{\prime}({\bf w}-{\bf u})=D({\mathbb{w}}-{\mathbb{u}})\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A),

then there exists a subset
\{{\bf v}_{i}:i=1,\ldots,p\}
of
{\mathcal{M}}
that connects
D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-}
to
D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{-}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{+}
. By definition, this means that
(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-}-{{\sum^{l}_{i=1}}}{{\bf v}_{i}})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
for all
1\leq l\leq p
, and

(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-})-(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{-}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{+})(=D({\bf w}-{\bf u}))=\sum^{p}_{i=1}{\bf v}_{i}.

Applying
T^{\prime}
to the previous two equalities we have
T^{\prime}(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-})-{{\sum^{l}_{i=1}}}{T({\bf v}_{i}})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{q}
and
T^{\prime}(D({\bf w}-{\bf u}))=T(D({\bf w}-{\bf u}))=\sum^{p}_{i=1}{T(\bf v}_{i})
(here we used that
T^{\prime}({\mathbb{v}}_{i})=T({\mathbb{v}}_{i})
since
{\mathbb{v}}_{i}\in\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A)
).

Note that
T^{\prime}(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+})={\mathbb{w}}
since
{\bf w}\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
and
c_{11},\ldots,c_{q1}
are positive. In addition,
T^{\prime}(D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-})={\bf 0}
since the
i1
-th coordinate of
D^{\prime}({\mathbb{u}})^{-}
is
0
because
D^{\prime}({\mathbb{u}})
has all
i1
-th coordinates nonnegative (
{\bf u}\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
and
c_{i1}>0
for all
i
). Hence
T^{\prime}(D^{\prime}({\bf w})^{+}+D^{\prime}({\bf u})^{-})={\mathbb{w}}+{\bf 0}={\mathbb{w}}
, and therefore we obtain
{\bf w}-{{\sum^{l}_{i=1}}}{T({\bf v}_{i}})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{q}
and
{\bf w}-{\bf u}=\sum^{p}_{i=1}{T(\bf v}_{i})
. The latter relations imply that
\{T({\bf u}_{1}),\ldots,T({\bf u}_{t})\}
is a Markov basis for
A_{B}
.
∎

The following example shows that the minimality of a Markov basis is not preserved in general after applying
T
.

Example 3.2
.

Consider the integer matrix

A={\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}3&3&4&5\\
2&3&0&0\end{pmatrix}}.

Then
A
has three bouquets, with the first two columns being in the same bouquet while the other two are bouquets by themselves, and
{\mathbb{c}}_{B_{1}}=(3,-2,0,0)
,
{\mathbb{c}}_{B_{2}}=(0,0,1,0)
,
{\mathbb{c}}_{B_{3}}=(0,0,0,1)
(this implies that
c_{11}=3,c_{21}=1,c_{31}=1
). Hence the bouquet matrix is (see
[
20
, Example 1.10]
for detailed computation of the matrix
A_{B}
)

A_{B}={\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}3&4&5\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}}.

The map
T
is given by

T(u_{11},u_{12},u_{21},u_{31})=(u_{11}/3,u_{21}/1,u_{31}/1)
. Note that the set
{
(0,0,5,-4)
,
(3,-2,-2,1)
,
(3,-2,3,-3)
,
(6,-4,1,-2)
,
(9,-6,-1,-1)
}
represents a minimal Markov basis for
A
, and therefore by Theorem
3.1
the vectors
T(0,0,5,-4)=(0,5,-4)
,
T(3,-2,-2,1)=(1,-2,1)
,
T(3,-2,3,-3)=(1,3,-3)
,
T(6,-4,1,-2)=(2,1,-2)
,
T(9,-6,-1,-1)=(3,-1,-1)
form a Markov basis for

, but not minimal. Indeed, one can easily check that a minimal Markov basis of
A_{B}
consists only of the vectors
(1,-2,1)
,
(2,1,-2)
,
(3,-1,-1)
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{B})\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{n}=\{{\bf 0}\}
then if
{\bf u}
is an indispensable element of
A_{B}
then
D({\bf u})
is an indispensable element of
A
.

Proof.

Let
\{{\bf u}_{1},\ldots,{\bf u}_{t}\}
be a minimal Markov basis for
A
.
Applying Theorem
3.1
we obtain that {
T({\bf u}_{1}),\ldots,T({\bf u}_{t})\}
is a Markov basis for
A_{B}
.
By the homogeneous Nakayama Lemma a subset of it is a minimal Markov basis for
A_{B}
.
Since
{\bf u}
is an indispensable element of
A_{B}
then there exists an
i
such that
T({\bf u}_{i})={\bf u}
.
Then applying
D
we have
D({\bf u})=D(T({\bf u}_{i}))={\mathbb{u}}_{i}
, which means that
D({\bf u})

is an indispensable element of

.
∎

If
A_{B}
is the matrix obtained by the bouquet construction from
A
, then one can easily see that when passing to the
r
-th Lawrence liftings of these matrices, the map
D_{(r)}:\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{B}^{(r)})\mapsto\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A^{(r)})
defined by

D_{(r)}{\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}{\bf u}_{1}\\
{\bf u}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf u}_{r}\end{pmatrix}}={\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}D({\bf u}_{1})\\
D({\bf u}_{2})\\
\vdots\\
D({\bf u}_{r})\end{pmatrix},}

is well defined since
D
is linear (
{\bf u}_{1}+\cdots+{\bf u}_{r}={\bf 0}
implies that
D({\bf u}_{1})+\cdots+D({\bf u}_{r})={\bf 0}
). Similarly one can define for any
r
the map
T_{(r)}:\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A^{(r)})\mapsto\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{B}^{(r)})
, starting from
T
. On the other hand, we can also discuss about the bouquet matrix of the
r
-th Lawrence lifting of
A
, that is
(A^{(r)})_{B}
, and wonder how it relates to the one of
A_{B}^{(r)}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
A_{B}
be the bouquet matrix of
A
and
r\geq 3
an integer. Then,

\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}({(A^{(r)})_{B}})=\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}({A_{B}^{(r)}}),

and in particular
I_{(A^{(r)})_{B}}=I_{({A_{B}^{(r)}})}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbb{a}}_{i}(s)
denote the
i+(s-1)n
column of
A^{(r)}
for
s=1,\ldots,r
. We prove that each bouquet
B
of
A
gives rise to exactly
r
subbouquets of
A^{(r)}
, and those
r
subbouquets of
A
are bouquets if
B
is non-free.

Indeed, we first see that if
{\mathbb{a}}_{j},{\mathbb{a}}_{k}
belong to the same bouquet, then there exists a vector
{\mathbb{v}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}

such that
{\mathbb{v}}\cdot A
has the only nonzero components,

4.
The family of matrices

Let

and consider the family of matrices

that the vector

. With this observation we can identify in the next lemma some indispensable elements of
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{s})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The elements
(1,-1,-1,1),(2-s,s-1,-1,0)
and
(0,-1,s-1,2-s)
of
\operatorname{Ker}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(A_{s})
are indispensable.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbb{u}}=(1,-1,-1,1)
and
{\mathbb{t}}=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
an arbitrary vector of
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbb{u}}}
. Since
{\mathbb{t}}\in\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbb{u}}}
then

, which implies the vectorial equality

{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}s\\
2\end{bmatrix}}=\alpha{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}}+\beta{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}}+\gamma{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}s-1\\
1\end{bmatrix}}+\delta{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}s\\
1\end{bmatrix}}.

Note that
\delta\leq 1
. If
\delta=1
then we necessarily have
\alpha=1
and
\beta=\gamma=0
implying that
{\mathbb{t}}={\mathbb{u}}^{+}
. Otherwise
\delta=0
, and then we obtain
\alpha=0
and
\beta=\gamma=1
, hence
{\mathbb{t}}={\mathbb{u}}^{-}
. We conclude that
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbb{u}}}=\{{\mathbb{u}}^{+},{\mathbb{u}}^{-}\}
, and by the previous remark we get that
(1,-1,-1,1)
is indispensable.

Similarly, if

then we compute
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbb{u}}}
. Let

{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}s-1\\

. Therefore

∎

{\mathbb{u}}={\mathbb{v}}+_{sc}{\mathbb{w}}
is a proper semiconformal decomposition.

In conclusion, from the two cases, we obtain that
{\bf u}
is indispensable, and therefore
m(A_{s})\geq s
.
∎

6.
Markov complexity of
m\times n
matrices

We can state now the main result of this paper:

Theorem 6.1
.

Markov complexity of
m\times n
matrices of rank
d
may be arbitrarily large, for all
n\geq 4
and
d\leq n-2
.

Proof.

Let
n,d
that satisfy the hypotheses of the theorem. In particular, we have

be an

s
, see Theorem
5.1

for the first one and
[
13
, Theorem 4.1, Corollary 4.4]
, respectively, for the second one.
Next we apply
[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
to construct
L
a generalized Lawrence matrix
m\times n

of rank
d
such that its subbouquet ideal is the toric ideal of

. The matrix

has rank
d
since bouquets
preserve the codimension, as noticed in the introduction of Section 2.
Applying now Theorem
3.6
we obtain that the

matrix

of rank

has Markov complexity at least
s
.
∎

7.
Bounds on Markov complexity

The above results show that if we only bound the matrix dimensions then the Markov and Graver
complexities can be arbitrarily large. In contrast, we now show that if we also bound the
matrix entries, these complexities become bounded. We use recent results on sparse
integer programming from
[
9
,
14
,
15
]
. We need some definitions.
The
height
of a rooted tree is the maximum number of vertices on a path from the
root to a leaf. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, a rooted tree on
V
is
valid
for
G
if
for each edge
\{j,k\}\in E
one of
j,k
lies on the path from the root to the other.
The
tree-depth

{\rm td}(G)
of
G
is the smallest height of a rooted tree which is valid for
G
.
For instance, if
G=([2m],E)
is a perfect matching with
E=\{\{i,m+i\}:i\in[m]\}

then its tree-depth is
3
where a tree validating it rooted at
1
has edge set

E\uplus\{\{1,i\}:i=2,\dots,m\}
. The graph of an
m\times n
matrix
A
is the graph
G(A)
on
[n]

where
{j,k}
is an edge if and only if there is an
i\in[m]
such that
A_{i,j}A_{i,k}\neq 0
.
The
tree-depth
of
A
is the tree-depth
{\rm td}(A):={\rm td}(G(A))
of its graph.

The tree-depth is a parameter playing a central role in sparsity, see
[
17
]
.
The results of
[
9
,
14
,
15
]
assert that
sparse integer programming
,
where the defining matrix
A
has small tree-depth and hence is sparse, is efficiently solvable.
One of the key ingredients in the efficient solution is that there exists a bound on the
1
-norm
of the elements of the Graver basis of
A
which is a computable function depending only on

a:=\|A\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|
, the maximum absolute value of any entry of
A
,
and
t:={\rm td}(A^{T})
, the tree-depth of its transpose. The currently best bound is in

[
9
, Lemma 28]
and
[
12
, Lemma 11]
, as follows.

Proposition 7.1
.

For any integer matrix
A
,
any Graver basis element
x\in{\mathcal{G}}(A)
satisfies

\|x\|_{1}\ \leq\ (2a+1)^{{2^{t}}-1}\ .

We now prove the bound on the Markov complexity and Graver complexity.

Theorem 7.2
.

The Markov and Graver complexities of any integer
m\times n
matrix
A
satisfy

m(A)\ \leq\ g(A)\ \leq\ (2a+1)^{{4^{n}}-1}+1\ .

Proof.

Since
m(A)\leq g(A)
for any matrix, is suffices to prove the bound on
g(A)
.
Thus, we need to show that for any
r
, the type of any element
x\in{\mathcal{G}}(A^{(r)})
, that is,
the number of nonzero rows of
x
, regarded as an
r\times n
matrix with rows

x^{1},\dots,x^{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, satisfies the bound. First, note that if
a=0
, that is,
A
is the
zero
m\times n
matrix, then
{\mathcal{G}}(A^{(r)})
consists precisely of those matrices
x
which
for some
1\leq i\neq k\leq r
and some
1\leq j\leq n
, have
x^{i}
equal to the
j
th
unit vector in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and
x^{k}=-x^{i}
, and all other rows are zero. So the type
of each such matrix is
2
and therefore
g(A)=2
and obeys the claimed bound.

So assume from now on that
a\geq 1
. Removing redundant rows of
A
does not change the
kernel of
A^{(r)}
and hence also does not change
{\mathcal{G}}(A^{(r)})
, so we may assume
m\leq n
.
Now, consider any
r
and the matrix
B:=A^{(r)}
and its transpose
B^{T}
. We index the rows
of
B=A^{(r)}
naturally by
b^{i}_{j}
for
i=1,\dots,r
and
j=1,\dots,m
, followed by
c_{k}

for
k=1,\dots,n
. Then the
b^{i}_{j}
and
c_{k}
are the vertices of the graph
G(B^{T})

of the transpose of the matrix
B
.

We define a rooted tree
T
on the
b^{i}_{j}
and
c_{k}

as follows. We let the root be
c_{1}
. We include the path
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}
.
For
i=1,\dots,r
we include the edge
\{c_{n},b^{i}_{1}\}
and the path
b^{i}_{1},b^{i}_{2},\dots,b^{i}_{m}
.
We now claim that
T
is valid for the graph
G(B^{T})
. Consider any two distinct rows of
B
.
If they are some
c_{k},c_{l}
with
k<l
then
c_{k}
is on the path from the root to
c_{l}
.
If they are some
c_{k}
and
b^{i}_{j}
then
c_{k}
is on the path from the root to
b^{i}_{j}
.
If they are some
b^{i}_{k}
and
b^{i}_{l}
with
k<l
then
b^{i}_{k}
is on the path from the root to
b^{i}_{l}
.
Finally, suppose they are
b^{i}_{j}
and
b^{k}_{l}
with
i\neq k
. Then for any column
s
of

B=A^{(r)}
, either
B_{b^{i}_{j},s}=0
or
B_{b^{k}_{l},s}=0
, and therefore
\{b^{i}_{j},b^{k}_{l}\}
is

not
an edge of
G(B^{T})
. Therefore
T
is valid for
G(B^{T})
.

Since the height of
T
is
n+m\leq 2n
, we find that
{\rm td}(B^{T})={\rm td}(G(B^{T}))\leq 2n
.
Also, since
a\geq 1
, we have that
\|B\|_{\infty}=a
. Therefore, by Proposition
7.1

applied to
B
, any Graver basis element
x\in{\mathcal{G}}(A^{(r)})
satisfies

\|x\|_{1}\leq(2a+1)^{{2^{2n}}-1}=(2a+1)^{{4^{n}}-1}
. Regarding again
x\in{\mathcal{G}}(A^{(r)})
as an

r\times n
matrix with rows
x^{1},\dots,x^{r}
, and noting that
x
is integer matrix and therefore

x^{k}\neq 0
implies
\|x^{k}\|_{1}\geq 1
, we find that the type
|\{k:x^{k}\neq 0\}|
of
x
satisfies

|\{k:x^{k}\neq 0\}|\ \leq\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\|x^{k}\|_{1}\ =\ \|x\|_{1}\ \leq\ (2a+1)^{{4^{n}}-1}\ .

So the type of any element
x\in{\mathcal{G}}(A^{(r)})
for any
r
satisfies the claimed bound.
Since the Graver complexity
g(A)
is the maximum such type, it satisfies the bound as well.

∎

As the Markov complexity
m(A)
may be much smaller than the Graver complexity
g(A)
,
it is interesting to ask whether the bound on the Markov complexity can be substantially reduced,
say, to a bound which is singly, rather than doubly, exponential in
n
.

Question 7.3
.

What is the best upper bound
f(a,n)
on the Markov complexity
of any integer
m\times n
matrix with
\|A\|_{\infty}=a
?

Since applications of the Markov and Graver complexities are mainly for 0/1-matrices,
see
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
11
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
22
]
,
it is also interesting to find the best bound for
0/1
matrices.
Note that for
0/1
matrices
a:=\|A\|_{\infty}=1
by definition.

Question 7.4
.

What is the best upper bound
h(n)
on the Markov complexity of any
0/1

m\times n

We note that in
[
24

it is proved that certain

bounded above by
n-d
. We also note that, while the bound

for
