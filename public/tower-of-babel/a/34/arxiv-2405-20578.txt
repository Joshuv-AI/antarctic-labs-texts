Lopsided Rigid Dumbbell Rheology from Langevin Equation: A Graduate Tutorial
Nhan Phan-Thien, Dingyi Pan, Mona A. Kanso, Alan Jeffrey Giacomin

Abstract
The modelling of symmetric rigid dumbbell particles suspended in a Newtonian fluid, as a model of a rigid-rod polymeric solution, has been accomplished exclusively through the diffusion equation, which has been detailed elegantly by Bird et al. [Chapter 14 of Dynamics of Polymeric Liquids , Vol 2, Ed 2, (1987)]. In this tutorial, a straightforward approach for modelling a lopsided rigid dumbbell particle is presented by the Langevin analysis. The connector force between the dumbbell beads is obtained through the rigidity constraint of the center-to-center vector of the dumbbell using its Langevin equation. By directly averaging via the Langevin equation, the evolution of the center-to-center vector, and the configuration tensor are derived. The stress expressions for the dumbbell from the Langevin equation, and the diffusion equation for the orientation distribution function of the center-to-center vector of the dumbbell are also derived, and the final expressions agree with established results from other methods.

This Tutorial is for those teaching this elegant method. Within this Tutorial we arrive at the Giesekus expression for the stress, in a completely different way, starting with the Langevin equation. Those learning the dynamics of polymeric liquids will see the equivalence between kinetic macromolecular theory and the Langevin approach. ), where all the interactions between the solvent and the dumbbell beads are located at the bead centers, at position vectors
\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}
. Table
1

L

phantom
rigid rod. By
phantom

i=1,2
is associated with a radius
a_{i}
, a Stokes frictional coefficient

\zeta_{i}=6\pi\eta_{s}a_{i},

(1)

with
\eta_{s}

m_{i}

i=2

\nu=\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}.

(2)

\zeta_{2}=\nu\zeta_{1}

To maintain a constant distance between the beads, a connector force
\mathbf{F}^{(c)}
between them arises;
\mathbf{F}^{(c)}
is thus a constraint on the dynamical system to keep the dumbbell rigid. The beads undergo their Newtonian 2nd law of motions acted on by a fluid viscous resistance force,
\zeta_{i}\left(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\right)

\mathbf{u}_{i}=\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)

\pm\mathbf{F}^{(c)}

\mathbf{F}_{i}^{(b)}\left(t\right)
modelled by Brownian motion (
i=1,2

\begin{array}[c]{c}m_{1}\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{1}=\zeta_{1}\left(\mathbf{u}_{1}-\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{1}\right)+\mathbf{F}^{(c)}+\mathbf{F}_{1}^{(b)}\left(t\right),\\
m_{2}\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{2}=\zeta_{2}\left(\mathbf{u}_{2}-\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{2}\right)-\mathbf{F}^{(c)}+\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}\left(t\right).\end{array}

(3)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{F}_{j}^{(b)}\left(t\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{F}_{1}^{(b)}\left(t+s\right)\mathbf{F}_{1}^{(b)}\left(t\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=2kT\zeta_{1}\delta\left(s\right)\mathbf{I},

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}\left(t+s\right)\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}\left(t\right)\rangle

Let us now define the center of resistance

(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2})\mathbf{R}^{(c)}=\zeta_{1}\mathbf{r}_{1}+\zeta_{2}\mathbf{r}_{2}.

(6)

By

, we mean the point on the dumbbell where a single applied force results in pure translation. Since
\zeta_{2}=\nu\zeta_{1}
, the center of resistance is also defined by
\left(1+\nu\right)\mathbf{R}^{(c)}=\mathbf{r}_{1}+\nu\mathbf{r}_{2}

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{r}_{1}=\mathbf{R}^{(c)}-\mathbf{R}^{(1)},\;\mathbf{r}_{2}=\mathbf{R}^{(c)}+\mathbf{R}^{(2)},\\
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{(1)}+\mathbf{R}^{(2)},\end{gathered}

(7)

Then, by virtue of the definition of the center of resistance,

\zeta_{2}\mathbf{R}^{(2)}-\zeta_{1}\mathbf{R}^{(1)}=\zeta_{1}\mathbf{r}_{1}+\zeta_{2}\mathbf{r}_{2}-(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2})\mathbf{R}^{(c)}=\mathbf{0}.

(8)

On the time scales of interest the inertia of the beads can be ignored, as it is customarily done. The equations of motion for the center of resistance, and for the center-to-center vector become,

\begin{gathered}\left(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}\right)\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{(c)}=\zeta_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\zeta_{2}\mathbf{u}_{2}+\mathbf{F}^{(b,c)}\left(t\right),\\
\zeta_{2}\dot{\mathbf{R}}=\zeta_{2}\left(\mathbf{u}_{2}-\mathbf{u}_{1}\right)-\frac{\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}{\zeta_{1}}\mathbf{F}^{(c)}+\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right),\end{gathered}

(9)

where
\mathbf{F}^{(b,c)}\left(t\right)
is the Brownian force acting on the center of resistance, and
\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)
is the Brownian force acting on the center-to-center vector; they are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{(b,c)}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{1}^{(b)}\left(t\right)+\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}\left(t\right),

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}\left(t\right)-\nu\mathbf{F}_{1}^{(b)}\left(t\right).

Furthermore a Taylor’s series expansion about the center of resistance in

yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{(c)}-\mathbf{R}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}+\mathbf{R}^{(1)}\mathbf{R}^{(1)}\!:\!\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}\!+\!HOT,

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{(c)}+\mathbf{R}^{(2)}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}+\mathbf{R}^{(2)}\mathbf{R}^{(2)}\!:\!\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}\!+\!HOT,

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\zeta_{2}\mathbf{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}\right)\mathbf{u}^{(c)}+\zeta_{1}\mathbf{R}^{(1)}\mathbf{R}^{(1)}:\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}

(12)

\displaystyle+\zeta_{2}\mathbf{R}^{(2)}\mathbf{R}^{(2)}:\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}+HOT,

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{2}-\mathbf{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{R+R}^{(2)}\mathbf{R}^{(2)}:\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}

(13)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{R}^{(1)}\mathbf{R}^{(1)}:\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}+HOT,

where
\mathbf{L}=(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)})^{T}
is the velocity gradient at the center of resistance. Thus

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{(c)}+\left(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{F}^{(b,c)}\left(t\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{R}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L\cdot R}-\frac{\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}}\mathbf{F}^{(c)}+\zeta_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right),

where the statistics of the Brownian forces acting on the center of resistance and the center-to-center vector can be found from Eq. (

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{F}^{(b,c)}\left(t\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(15)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{F}^{(b,c)}\left(t+s\right)\mathbf{F}^{(b,c)}\left(t\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=2kT\left(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}\right)\delta\left(s\right)\mathbf{I},

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

\mathbf{R\cdot\dot{R}}=0=\mathbf{p\cdot\dot{R}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{Rp}-\frac{\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}}\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{(c)}+\zeta_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)=0,

\mathbf{F}^{(c)}=\frac{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}}{\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{Rpp}+\frac{\zeta_{1}}{\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}\mathbf{pp}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right).

(17)

Compared with the

\mathbf{F}^{(c)}=\zeta\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{Rpp}/2
, the effect of lopsidedness is seen to replace the effective coefficient of friction by

\frac{\zeta}{2}=\frac{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}}{\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}\ \mathrm{or\ }\frac{2}{\zeta}=\frac{1}{\zeta_{1}}+\frac{1}{\zeta_{2}}.

(18)

That is, the effective frictional coefficient is the harmonic mean of the beads’ frictional coefficients, a result noted by Abdel-Khalik and Bird.
[
18
]
Alternatively, since the frictional coefficient is proportional to the radius, this amounts to replacing both radii by an effective radius

given by the harmonic mean of the radii (Eq. (103) of Ref.
19

\frac{a}{2}=\frac{a_{1}a_{2}}{a_{1}+a_{2}}\ \mathrm{or\ }\frac{2}{a}=\frac{1}{a_{1}}+\frac{1}{a_{2}}.

(19)

In addition, the rigidity constraint also demands that only Brownian forces acting perpendicular to the center-to-center vector participate in the equation of motion for
\mathbf{R}
, the components parallel to
\mathbf{R}
cause a drift in the mean square of the center of resistance and are of no interest; the filter
\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{pp}\right)
on the Brownian force
\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)
effectively does that;
\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{pp}\right)\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)
can be justifiably called the rotational Brownian force, and the Langevin equation for the center-to-center vector is

\dot{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{ppR}+\zeta_{2}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{pp}\right)\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right),

(20)

which we will use presently to generate the diffusion equation for the center-to-center vector, from which entities related to
\mathbf{R}
can be averaged.

Now, from Eq. (

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{ppR}\right)\Delta t

and consequently

Furthermore, keeping to

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta R_{i}\Delta R_{j}\right\rangle

I.3
Average Motions and Configuration Tensor

If the flow is homogeneous, i.e.,
\mathbf{L}
is constant,
\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(r)}
and
HOT
are identically zero, the dumbbell’s center of resistance drifts just like a particle of fluid in the mean,

\langle\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{(c)}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{u}^{(c)}\rangle=\mathbf{L}\langle\mathbf{R}^{(c)}\rangle=\mathbf{u}\left(\langle\mathbf{R}^{(c)}\rangle\right).

(27)

A migration from the streamline of the center of resistance is therefore induced by a non-homogeneous flow field (or by other external forces on the beads, not considered here). This migration is proportional to
\nabla\nabla\mathbf{u}^{(c)}
and the
HOT
.

Also, the average center-to-center vector evolves in time according to

\langle\dot{\mathbf{R}}\rangle=\mathbf{L}\cdot\langle\mathbf{R}\rangle-\mathbf{L}:\langle\mathbf{ppR}\rangle.

(28)

This equation does not involve a material time constant, unlike in the elastic dumbbell case – the only time constant comes from the shear rate tensor. To solve for the average motion, higher-order statistics in
\mathbf{R}
are required, a classical closure problem.

Multiplying Eq. (
20
) by
\mathbf{R}
, averaging the resulting expression, the evolution of the configuration tensor
\langle\mathbf{RR}\rangle
is found,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbf{R}\dot{\mathbf{R}}+\mathbf{\dot{R}R}\right\rangle

(29)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}\cdot\left\langle\mathbf{RR}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathbf{RR}\right\rangle\cdot\mathbf{L}^{T}-2\mathbf{L}:\left\langle\mathbf{ppRR}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\zeta_{2}^{-1}\left\langle\mathbf{R}\left(\mathbf{I-pp}\right)\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)+\left(\mathbf{I-pp}\right)\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)\mathbf{R}\right\rangle.

The average of quantity like

\left\langle\mathbf{pp}\right\rangle
is

\lambda\frac{\delta}{\delta t}\langle\mathbf{pp}\rangle+2\lambda\mathbf{L}:\left\langle\mathbf{pppp}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathbf{pp}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I.}

(37)

II
Stress from Langevin equation

The stress contributed by the dumbbells is related to the configuration tensor by (Eq. D of Table 13.3-1 of Bird et al.
[
1
]
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{\left(p\right)}

\displaystyle=-nkT\mathbf{I}-3nkT\lambda\frac{\delta}{\delta t}\left\langle\mathbf{pp}\right\rangle

(38)

\displaystyle=-nkT\mathbf{I}+6nkT\lambda\mathbf{L}:\left\langle\mathbf{pppp}\right\rangle+3nkT\left\langle\mathbf{pp}\right\rangle

The first form in Eq. (
38
) is known as the Giesekus expression (Eq. D of Table 13.3-1 of Ref.
1
),
[
23
]
and the second, the Kramers expression (Eq. D of Table 13.3-1 of Ref.
1
),
[
24
]
for the dumbbell-contributed stress.

It is pedagogical to derive the stress expressions from Langevin equation of motion. The convenient starting point is the expression for the stress, from Bird et al.
[
1
]
(Eq. 13.3-14) specialized to this case:

\mathbf{S}^{(p)}=n\left\langle\mathbf{RF}^{(c)}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}n\left\langle\mathbf{RF}_{2}^{(b)}-\mathbf{RF}_{1}^{(b)}\right\rangle.

(39)

The first term on the right of Eq. (
39
) is called the contribution of intramolecular forces (due to the connector force), and the second term, the contribution of external forces (in this case they consist of Brownian forces) by Bird et al.
[
1
]
With
\mathbf{F}^{(c)}
given by Eq. (
17
), we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{RF}^{(c)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta L^{2}}{2}\left\langle\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{pppp}\right\rangle

(40)

\displaystyle+\frac{\zeta_{1}R}{\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}\left\langle\mathbf{ppp}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)\right\rangle.

Using the technique as detailed in Eqs. (
30
-
32
), and
\zeta L^{2}/2=6kT\lambda
, we find that

\left\langle\mathbf{RF}^{(c)}\right\rangle=6kT\lambda\left\langle\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{pppp}\right\rangle+2kT\left\langle\mathbf{pp}\right\rangle,

(41)

and

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{RF}_{2}^{(b)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\zeta_{2}^{-1}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\left(dt\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{pp}\right)\mathbf{F}^{(b,\mathbf{R})}\left(t\right)\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\zeta_{2}^{-1}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{pp}\right)\left(\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}-\nu\mathbf{F}_{1}^{(b)}\right)\mathbf{F}_{2}^{(b)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=kT\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{pp}\right)=-\left\langle\mathbf{RF}_{1}^{(b)}\right\rangle.

(42)

Putting results Eqs. (
41
-
42
) into Eq. (
39
), the dumbbell-contributed stress becomes (Eq. A of Table 13.3-1 of Ref.
1
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=6nkT\lambda\left\langle\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{pppp}\right\rangle+2kT\left\langle\mathbf{pp}\right\rangle-nkT\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{pp}\right)

\displaystyle=-nkT\mathbf{I}+3kT\left\langle\mathbf{pp}\right\rangle+6nkT\lambda\left\langle\mathbf{L}:\mathbf{pppp}\right\rangle,

(43)

which is exactly the Kramers expression for the stress in Eq. (
38
). This, using the evolution of the configuration tensor and the definition of the upper-convected derivative, leads to the Giesekus expression Eq. (
38
) (Eq. D of Table 13.3-1 of Ref.
1
).

Rather than starting with Eq. (
39
), one can start with the volume average concept for the stress, as detailed in Subsection 7.4 of Phan-Thien and Mai-Duy,
[
15
]
wherein it is set as an exercise for the student to derive the dumbbell-contributed stress as given exactly by Eq. (
43
) (Eq. A of Table 13.3-1 of Ref.
1
).

III
Conclusion

A lopsided rigid dumbbell model is developed by Langevin analysis of Brownian motion. The connector force between the dumbbell beads is obtained as a constraint to the rigidity requirement, and it is found that a harmonic mean of the friction coefficients (Eq. (
18
)), or the harmonic mean of the radii (Eq. (
19
)), of the lopsided beads can be used to replace the effective coefficient of friction of the symmetric rigid dumbbell model – all the previously derived results for the symmetric rigid dumbbell then apply with this replacement, a result uncovered by Abdel-Khalik and Bird using kinetic molecular theory (Rows 2 of TABLE 13.4-1 of Ref.
25
or TABLE 16.4-1 of Ref.
1
, Ref.
18
).

The Langevin equation for the center-to-center vector is then derived. By averaging the center-to-center vector, the evolution of the configuration tensor is found. The expressions for the dumbbell-contribution stress are also derived from the Langevin approach, and our final expressions Eqs. (
39
) and (
43
) are exactly those derived from the distribution function, detailed in Bird et al.
[
1
]
The diffusion equation for the probability distribution in the center-to-center vector can be derived from its Langevin equation. We consider this Tutorial a pedagogical contribution – indeed the Langevin analysis may bring out the role of lopsidedness more clearly, especially in the case where deterministic external forces are acting on the beads
[
26
]
(not considered in this Tutorial, and for which Eq. (
39
) may need to be modified). Our Langevin approach will be preferred by those having no interest in the orientation distributions of the macromolecules. It is less work. Our Langevin approach will also be preferred by students with engineering mechanics background. One can definitely have just as much fun with the Langevin approach for analyzing lopsided elastic dumbbells (see Ref.
27
).
