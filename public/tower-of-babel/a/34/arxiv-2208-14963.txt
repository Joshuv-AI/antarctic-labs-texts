Reconstruction of a Single String from a Part of its Composition Multiset
Zuo Ye and Ohad Elishco

Abstract
Motivated by applications in polymer-based data storage, we study the problem of reconstructing a string from part of its composition multiset. We give a full description of the structure of the strings that cannot be uniquely reconstructed (up to reversal) from their multiset of all of their prefix-suffix compositions. Leveraging this description, we prove that for all n ⩾ 6 n\geqslant 6 , there exists a string of length n n that cannot be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal. Moreover, for all n ⩾ 6 n\geqslant 6 , we explicitly construct the set consisting of all length n n strings that can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal. As a by product, we obtain that any binary string can be constructed using Dyck strings and Catalan-Bertrand strings. For any given string 𝐬 \mathbf{s} , we provide a method to explicitly construct the set of all strings with the same prefix-suffix composition multiset as 𝐬 \mathbf{s} , as well as a formula for the size of this set. As an application, we construct a composition code of maximal size. Furthermore, we construct two classes of composition codes which can respectively correct composition missing errors and mass reducing substitution errors. In addition, we raise two new problems: reconstructing a string from its composition multiset when at most a constant number of substring compositions are lost; reconstructing a string when only given its compositions of substrings of length at most r r . For each of these setups, we give suitable codes under some conditions.

Polymer-based data storage systems

, as an alternative to DNA-based data storage systems, are emerging as the next-generation data storage medium. Comparing to DNA-based data storage systems, they offer high storage densities at lower cost and lower readout latency. In such platforms, two molecules (phosphodiesters) with significantly different masses are synthesized, one of which represents the bit
0
while the other represents the bit
1
. In the write-in process, a string of binary information is encoded into a string (poly-phosphodiester) of these two molecules which are stitched together by means of phosphate bonds. In the readout process, a tandem mass (MS/MS) spectrometer is used to break some of these phosphate bonds, thereby creating fragments of various lengths, and then output the masses of the resulting fragments. Then the information of masses is utilized to reconstruct the whole string.

There have been several works on this topic. The first one is

Later, under the same assumptions, the authors of

Following

Recall that all of these works are based on

Obviously, if no error occurs in the (prefix-suffix) composition multiset, all the works mentioned above can be viewed as special cases of a more general problem: how to reconstruct strings from a part of their composition multisets? In this paper, we continue this line of research and we study three subclasses of this problem. The first one is how to reconstruct a single string from its prefix-suffix composition multiset. Intuitively, one can deem that this is a critical step to go further and fully understand the more general setup considered in

Question 1:

For which

Question 2:

Question 3:

Given

Question 4:

In this paper, we will completely solve these questions. Our basic method is the generating polynomial method first introduced in

In addition, we find that all binary strings can be constructed by Dyck strings and Catlan-Bertrand strings (see Corollary

Secondly, we turn our attention to the second subclass of the general problem: how to reconstruct a string from its composition multiset if at most a constant number of compositions were lost in the reading process. The third subclass of the general problem is how to reconstruct a string if we are only given its compositions of substrings of length at most

II
Preliminaries

Let
\Sigma_{2}\triangleq\{0,1\}
be the binary alphabet. For any string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, denote by
c(\mathbf{s})=0^{n-w}1^{w}
the
composition
of
\mathbf{s}
, where
w=wt_{H}(\mathbf{s})
is the Hamming weight of
\mathbf{s}
. If
\mathbf{s}=s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
, we denote
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{j}=s_{i}\cdots s_{j}
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant n
.
If
j\leqslant i-1
, we define
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{j}\triangleq\emptyset
. In particular,
\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}
and
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{n}
are called the prefix and suffix of
\mathbf{s}
respectively.

Definition II.1

For a string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})
the multiset of compositions of all prefixes and suffixes of
\mathbf{s}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s}):=\left\{([1,j],c(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}))~:~1\leqslant j\leqslant n\right\}\cup\Big\{([i,n],c(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{n}))~:~1\leqslant i\leqslant n\Big\}.

By abuse of notation, we sometimes refer only to the right arguments in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})
.
We call
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})
the prefix-suffix composition multiset of
\mathbf{s}
.

For example, if
\mathbf{s}=01010
, then

\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})=\left\{0,0,01,01,0^{2}1,0^{2}1,0^{2}1^{2},0^{2}1^{2},0^{3}1^{2}\right\}.

For any
\mathbf{s}=s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
, we denote
\mathbf{s}^{\star}=s_{n}s_{n-1}\cdots s_{1}
and call it the
reversal
of
\mathbf{s}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s}^{\star})
for any string
\mathbf{s}
. We say a string
\mathbf{s}
can be
uniquely reconstructed up to reversal
if there is no string
\mathbf{t}
other than
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\star}
, such that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{t})=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})
.

With a given string
\mathbf{s}=s_{1}\cdots s_{n}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, we can associate a bivariate polynomial
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
with coefficients in
\{0,1\}
.
In this paper, for a given bivariate polynomial
P(x,y)
, we always let
\left(P(x,y)\right)_{i}
denote the term of total degree
i
of
P(x,y)
.
The next polynomial was firstly introduced in
[
17
]
.

Definition II.2

Let
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
be a string. Define the generating polynomial of
\mathbf{s}
, denoted
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
, as
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)_{0}\triangleq 1

and for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,

P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)_{i}\triangleq\begin{cases}y\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i-1},&\mbox{if }s_{i}=0,\\
x\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i-1},&\mbox{if }s_{i}=1.\end{cases}

For example, if
\mathbf{s}=01010
, then
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)=1+y+xy+xy^{2}+x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}y^{3}
.
By definition, we can also write
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
as

P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{n}x^{a_{i}}y^{i-a_{i}},

(1)

where
a_{0}\triangleq 0
and
a_{i}=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i})
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. The
reciprocal
of
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
is defined to be the polynomial
P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=x^{a_{n}}y^{n-a_{n}}P_{\mathbf{s}}(\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{y})
. Thus we have

P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{n}x^{w-a_{n-i}}y^{i-(w-a_{n-i})},

(2)

where
w\triangleq a_{n}
.

The relationship between
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
and
P_{\mathbf{s}^{\star}}(x,y)
is described in the following lemma.

Lemma II.3

[
17
, Section 7]

For any string
\mathbf{s}
, we have
P_{\mathbf{s}^{\star}}(x,y)=P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)
.

Remark II.4

It is immediate to verify that
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}
if and only if
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{n-i}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{n-i}
for every
0\leqslant i\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
and
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma^{n}_{2}
.
By Lemma
II.3
and the relationship between a generating polynomial and its corresponding string, this is equivalent to

\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left((\mathbf{s}^{\star})_{1}^{i}\right)
if and only if
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{n-i}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left((\mathbf{s}^{\star})_{1}^{n-i}\right)
.
This, in turn, is equivalent to
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-i+1}^{n}\right)
if and only if
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{n-i}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{i+1}^{n}\right)
.

The following lemma, which can be easily verified, is key to our purpose.

Lemma II.5

For any two strings
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{t}
,
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{t})
if and only if
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)+P_{\mathbf{s}^{\star}}(x,y)=P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y)+P_{\mathbf{t}^{*}}(x,y)
, i.e.,
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)+P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y)+P_{\mathbf{t}}^{*}(x,y)
.

In the following section we use Lemma
II.5
to identify strings that are not uniquely reconstructible up to reversal.

III
Strings that cannot be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal

Let
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
be a string of length

, to find a string

such that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{t})

for some string
\mathbf{t}
with coefficients in
\{0,1\}
such that

P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)+P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=Q(x,y)+Q^{*}(x,y).

(3)

Recall that the coefficients of
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
and

are
0
or

Equation

3
holds, then either
\left(Q(x,y)\right)_{i}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}
or
\left(Q(x,y)\right)_{i}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}

must be obtained by replacing some
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}
’s with the corresponding
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}

\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}
’s with the corresponding
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}
’s. On the other hand, if
Q(x,y)
is obtained in this way,
Equation

3
clearly holds.

Of course, it is meaningless to swap
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}
if
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}
. Besides, it always holds that
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{n}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{n}
. So we define the set

D\triangleq\left\{i~:~1\leqslant i<n,\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}\right\}.

By Remark
II.4
,
i\in D
if and only if
n-i\in D
.
If
D=\emptyset
, then
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{i}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, which is equivalent to
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\star}
and thus
\mathbf{s}
can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal. So for our aim, we assume
D\neq\emptyset
. For a nonempty subset
S
of
D
, let
Q_{S,1}(x,y)
be obtained by replacing all the
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
’s with
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
’s, and let
Q_{S,2}(x,y)
be obtained by replacing all the
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
’s with
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
’s, where
j\in S
. If
S
is clear from the context, we can omit
S
in the subscript.

Example III.1

Let
\mathbf{s}=1000
. Then
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)=1+x+xy+xy^{2}+xy^{3}
and
P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=1+y+y^{2}+y^{3}+xy^{3}
. So
D=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
. Let
S=\left\{1\right\}
and
T=\left\{1,3\right\}
. Then

\begin{array}[]{l}Q_{S,1}(x,y)=1+y+xy+xy^{2}+xy^{3},\\
Q_{S,2}(x,y)=1+x+y^{2}+y^{3}+xy^{3},\\
Q_{T,1}(x,y)=1+y+xy+y^{3}+xy^{3},\\
Q_{T,2}(x,y)=1+x+y^{2}+xy^{2}+xy^{3}.\end{array}

Clearly,
Q_{S,1}(x,y)\neq Q_{S,2}^{*}(x,y)
and
Q_{T,1}(x,y)=Q_{T,2}^{*}(x,y)
.

Example
III.1
tells that we should carefully choose
S
to ensure

.

Lemma III.2

Q_{S,1}(x,y)=Q_{S,2}^{*}(x,y)
if and only if
S
satisfies the condition that
j\in S
if and only if

.

Proof:

The
\Leftarrow

and

.
Suppose that

is given in

. Then

Suppose on the contrary that there is a

_i=0^nx^d_iy^i-d_i.

Then

_i=0^nx^w-d_n-iy^i-(w-d_n-i).

In particular,
\left(Q_{S,1}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}=x^{w-d_{n-j}}y^{j-(w-d_{n-j})}

III-A

A Special Case

In this subsection, we study the special case when
\left|S\right|=1
. On the one hand, this special case inspires our discussion of the general case in next subsection. On the other hand, in this special case, we can obtain more accurate descriptions on the structures of strings.

Let
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
and
P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)
be given in
Equation

1
and
Equation

2
respectively.
Firstly, We should describe on which terms of
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
and
P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)
the “swapping” operation can be performed, such that the obtained polynomial
Q(x,y)
and is indeed a generating polynomial of some string.
Let
Q_{1}(x,y)
be obtained by replacing
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
with
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and
Q_{2}(x,y)
be obtained by replacing
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
with
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
, where
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. Then
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)+P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=Q_{1}(x,y)+Q_{2}(x,y)
. To make sure that
Q_{1}(x,y)
and
Q_{2}(x,y)
are both generating polynomials, the following inequalities must hold:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}w-a_{n-j+1}\leqslant a_{j}\leqslant w-a_{n-j-1}\\
j-1-(w-a_{n-j+1})\leqslant j-a_{j}\leqslant j+1-(w-a_{n-j-1})\\
a_{j-1}\leqslant w-a_{n-j}\leqslant a_{j+1}\\
j-1-a_{j-1}\leqslant j-(w-a_{n-j})\leqslant j+1-a_{j+1}\end{array}\right.

Simplifying these inequalities, we get

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}w-a_{n-j+1}\leqslant a_{j}\leqslant w-a_{n-j-1}\\
w-a_{n-j-1}-1\leqslant a_{j}\leqslant w-a_{n-j+1}+1\\
w-a_{j+1}\leqslant a_{n-j}\leqslant w-a_{j-1}\\
w-a_{j-1}-1\leqslant a_{n-j}\leqslant w-a_{j+1}+1\end{array}\right.

(4)

If
Equation

4
holds, we say
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
are
swappable
.
From symmetry, we obtain that
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
are swappable if and only if
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{n-j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{n-j}
are swappable.

Lastly, to ensure that
Q_{1}(x,y)=Q_{2}^{*}(x,y)
, we need to swap
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
,
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{n-j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{n-j}
simultaneously.

Putting everything together, we get the following lemma.

Lemma III.3

Let
\mathbf{s}
be a string and let
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
be its generating polynomial.
Then there exists a string
\mathbf{t}
such that

(ii)

(iii)

There exists some

, i.e.,
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{k}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-k+1}^{n}\right)

Proof:

By Lemma
II.5
, there exists a string
\mathbf{t}
such that

if and only if there is a polynomial

with coefficients in

such that

and

indeed gives a string. Condition (i) ensures the existence of such

The last two conditions exclude the cases
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{s}^{\star}
.
Indeed, let
Q(x,y)
be obtained by swapping
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
,
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{n-j}

be the string corresponding to

\mathbf{t}\neq\mathbf{s}
is equivalent to
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and

\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{k}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-k+1}^{n}\right)

Let

be a generating polynomial for a string
\mathbf{s}

Let
\mathbf{s}
be a string of length

\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\star}
.

Notice that if

gives a string, then this string is different from
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\star}
if and only if there exists a

IV
Strings that can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal

In the previous section we obtained that for any
n\geqslant 6

n
that cannot be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal.
Two natural questions arise:

1.

For a given
n
, how many strings of length
n
are there which can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal?

2.

Is there a unified method to construct all the uniquely reconstructible strings?

This section is devoted to solving these questions. Let
\mathcal{U}(n)\subseteq\Sigma_{2}^{n}
be the set of all
n
-length strings that can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal.

For any
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, we can always find
0=j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots<j_{\ell}\leqslant\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
, where
\ell\geqslant 0
, such that
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
for all
j=j_{i},n-j_{i}
, where
0\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
, and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
for all
j\neq j_{i},n-j_{i}
.
In other words,
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
j=j_{i},n-j_{i}
, and
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
j\neq j_{i},n-j_{i}
, which is the same as
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left((\mathbf{s}^{\star})_{1}^{j}\right)
for all
j=j_{i},n-j_{i}
, and
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left((\mathbf{s}^{\star})_{1}^{j}\right)
for all
j\neq j_{i},n-j_{i}
(see Remark
II.4
). Clearly,
\left\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\right\}=\left\{1,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}\setminus D
, where
D
is defined at the beginning of
Section

III
.

We give some examples to illustrate the above idea.

Example IV.1

1.

Let
n=6
and
\mathbf{s}=101010
. Then
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)=1+x+xy+x^{2}y+x^{2}y^{2}+x^{3}y^{2}+x^{3}y^{3}
and
P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=1+y+xy+xy^{2}+x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}y^{3}+x^{3}y^{3}
. We have
\ell=1
and
j_{1}=2
.

2.

Let
n=6
and
\mathbf{s}=010111
. Then
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)=1+y+xy+xy^{2}+x^{2}y^{2}+x^{3}y^{2}+x^{4}y^{2}
and
P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)=1+x+x^{2}+x^{3}+x^{3}y+x^{4}y+x^{4}y^{2}
which means that
\ell=0
.

3.

Let
n=7
and
\mathbf{s}=1011010
. Then
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)=1+x+xy+x^{2}y+x^{3}y+x^{3}y^{2}+x^{4}y^{2}+x^{4}y^{3}
and

and

.

Next we will solve the two questions raised at the beginning of this section. We will not give the general construction of
\mathcal{U}(n)
immediately. Instead, we first consider two special cases, which not only contain interesting results themselves, but also provide us a better understanding of the ideas behind.

IV-A

The case
\ell=0

We consider the case
\ell=0
. That is to say,
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. The authors of
[
18
]
constructed the set
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)&=\left\{\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}:\;s_{1}=0,s_{n}=1,\text{ and }\exists I\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}\text{ such that}\right.\\
&\quad\;\;s_{i}\neq s_{n-i+1}\text{ for all }i\in I;s_{i}=s_{n-i+1}\text{ for all }i\notin I;\\
&\quad\;\;\left.\mathbf{s}_{\left\{1,\dots,\frac{n}{2}\right\}\cap I}\text{ is a Catalan-Bertrand string}\right\}\end{array}

(6)

when
n
is even, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(n-1)}\left\{\mathbf{s}_{1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,0\,\mathbf{s}_{\frac{n+1}{2}}^{n-1}\;\;,\;\;\mathbf{s}_{1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,1\,\mathbf{s}_{\frac{n+1}{2}}^{n-1}\right\}

when
n
is odd. Here, a Catalan-Bertrand string is a binary string such that every prefix contains strictly more
0
s than
1
s.

Lemma IV.2

Let
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
. Then
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
if and only if
\mathbf{s}
or
\mathbf{s}^{\star}
is in
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)
.

Proof:

The
\Leftarrow
direction follows from
[
18
, Lemma 1]
. For the
\Rightarrow
direction, without loss of generality assume
s_{1}=0
and
s_{n}=1
.
Let
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
s_{i}\neq s_{n-i+1}
if and only if
i\in I
. Notice that
1,n\in I
.
Since any
i\notin I
does not affect
\left(\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j})-\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n})\right)
, together with the assumptions that
s_{1}=0,s_{n}=1
and that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
, we conclude that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)<\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. It follows that
\mathbf{s}_{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]\cap I}
is a Catalan-Bertrand string.
∎

The following corollary is a direct result from Lemma
IV.2
.

Corollary IV.3

For a given
n
, let
f(n)
be the number of strings
\mathbf{s}
of length
n
such that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. Then

f(n)=\begin{cases}2\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\right|,&\mbox{if }n\geqslant 2,\\
2,&\mbox{if }n=1.\end{cases}

(7)

The value of
\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\right|
was given in
[
18
]
:

\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\right|=\begin{cases}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\binom{\frac{n}{2}-1}{i}2^{\frac{n}{2}-1-i}\binom{i}{\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\rfloor}\geqslant\frac{2^{n-3}}{\sqrt{\pi n}},&\mbox{if }n\text{ is even},\\
2\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(n-1)\right|,&\mbox{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(8)

Here we define
\mathcal{S}_{R}(0)\triangleq\left\{\emptyset\right\}
and hence
\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(0)\right|=1
.
Therefore,

\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log_{2}\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)^{*}\right|}{n}=1,

where
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)^{*}\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{s}:\;\mathbf{s}^{\star}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\right\}
.

Lemma IV.4

Let
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
be a string such that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. Then
\mathbf{s}
is uniquely reconstructible up to reversal.

Proof:

When
n=1
,
\mathbf{s}=0
or
1
. So the conclusion is trivial. When
n\geqslant 2
, we apply
Theorem

III.11
.

If
n=2
,
\mathbf{s}=01
or
10
. we can only choose
S=\{1\}
, which does not satisfy condition (i) of
Theorem

III.11
.
When
n\geqslant 3
, we have
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor+1<n
. Therefore, it is impossible to find an interval
S
satisfying condition (i) of
Theorem

III.11
.
∎

There is another way to show the correctness of Lemma
IV.4
. Let
\mathbf{s}
be given in Lemma
IV.4
. We can conclude that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)>\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
, or
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)<\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
. So when given
s_{1}
, we can know the compositions of all prefixes of
\mathbf{s}
and thus reconstruct
\mathbf{s}
. There are two cases for
s_{1}
, which means that we can get two strings and they are
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\star}
.

To conclude the case of
\ell=0
, we give the following corollary, which is an immediate result of Lemma
IV.2
and Lemma
IV.4
, presents a set of strings which can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal.

Corollary IV.5

For all
n\geqslant 1
, any string in
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)^{*}
can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal.

IV-B

General
\ell

Now we are ready to deal with general
\ell
.
For convenience, in the sequel we denote
j_{\ell+1}:=n-j_{\ell}
(recall that
j_{\ell}\leqslant\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
).
The following theorem is a reformulation of
Theorem

III.11
.

Theorem IV.6

Let
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
and let
j_{0},\ldots,j_{\ell},j_{\ell+1}
be defined as above. Then
\mathbf{s}
cannot be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal if and only if there exist two different integers
i,i^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\ell+1\}
such that
j_{i}-j_{i-1}\geqslant 2
and
j_{i^{\prime}}-j_{i^{\prime}-1}\geqslant 2
. In particular, we have
\ell\geqslant 1
.

Indeed, in
Theorem

III.11
, we can choose
S=\{j_{i-1}+1,\ldots,j_{i}-1\}
. Then by the definition of
j_{0},j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell+1}
, condition (i) of
Theorem

III.11
is satisfied. Since
j_{i^{\prime}}-j_{i^{\prime}-1}\geqslant 2
, we can choose a
k
with
j_{i^{\prime}-1}<k<j_{i^{\prime}}
. Besides, we have
k\notin S
because
i\neq i^{\prime}
. Then condition (ii) of
Theorem

III.11
is satisfied.

The following is a construction of a subset of
\mathcal{U}(n)
for any given
n\geqslant 1
:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)&=\left\{\bm{u}_{1}\cdots\bm{u}_{\ell}\bm{u}_{\ell+1}\bm{u}_{\ell}\cdots\bm{u}_{1}:\;\ell\geqslant 0,j_{0}=0;\right.\\
&\quad\;\;1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots j_{\ell}\leqslant\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor,j_{\ell+1}=n-j_{\ell};\\
&\quad\;\;\bm{u}_{i}\text{ or }\bm{u}_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{i}-j_{i-1})\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell+1;\\
&\quad\;\;\left.j_{i}-j_{i-1}\geqslant 2\text{ for at most one }i.\right\}\end{array}

Theorem IV.7

For any
n\geqslant 1
, we have
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)\subseteq\mathcal{U}(n)
.

Proof:

The conclusion follows from
Theorem

IV.6
and Lemma
IV.2
. Indeed, Lemma
IV.2
ensures that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j})\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n})
except for the selected
j_{i}
s.
∎

Remark IV.8

Take
\ell=0
, we can see that
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)
.
Notice that for
n\geqslant 6
,
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)^{*}
is not necessarily a equal to
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)
.

It is possible to provide a formula for
\left|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)\right|
.
For
n\geqslant 1
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(n)

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{j}=(j_{0},j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell+1}):\ell\geqslant 0;j_{0}=0;1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots<j_{\ell}\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\right.

(9)

\displaystyle\quad\;\;\left.j_{\ell+1}=n-j_{\ell};\text{there is at most one }i\text{ such that }j_{i}-j_{i-1}\geqslant 2\right\}.

The size of
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)
is

\left|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)\right|=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{P}(n)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell+1}f(j_{i}-j_{i-1}),

where
f(m)
is given in
Equation

7
for
m\geqslant 1
and
f(0)\triangleq 1
.
Since
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)
, we obtain that
\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log_{2}\left|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)\right|}{n}=1
.

Example IV.9

1.

Let
n=6
. Then
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{6}{2}\right\rfloor=3
and

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{P}(6)=\left\{(0,6),(0,1,5),(0,3,3),(0,1,2,4),(0,1,3,3),(0,2,3,3),\right.\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.(0,1,2,3,3)\right\}.\end{array}

Therefore,
\left|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(6)\right|=52
.

2.

Let
n=7
. Then
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{7}{2}\right\rfloor=3
and

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{P}(7)=\left\{(0,7),(0,1,6),(0,3,4),(0,1,2,5),(0,1,3,4),(0,2,3,4),\right.\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.(0,1,2,3,4)\right\}.\end{array}

Therefore,
\left|\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(7)\right|=120
.

Comparing Example
IV.9
with Example
III.6
, we see that
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(6)\neq\mathcal{U}(6)
, but
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(7)=\mathcal{U}(7)
.
In general, for
n\geqslant 6
, it is not clear a priory when
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)=\mathcal{U}(n)
.
Thus, it is of interest to find a construction for the entire set of uniquely reconstructible (up to reversal) stings.

IV-C

further generalization

In this subsection, we build upon the ideas presented in the last two subsections and construct the entire set of uniquely reconstructible (up to reversal) strings.

Consider a string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
and its generating polynomial
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
with
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{k}=x^{a_{k}}y^{k-a_{k}}
.
For any
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell+1
, define

\begin{array}[]{rl}P_{\mathbf{s},1}^{(i)}(x,y)&\triangleq x^{-a_{j_{i-1}}}y^{-(j_{i-1}-a_{j_{i-1}})}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{k=j_{i-1}}^{j_{i}}\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{k}\\
&=x^{-a_{j_{i-1}}}y^{-(j_{i-1}-a_{j_{i-1}})}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{k=j_{i-1}}^{j_{i}}x^{a_{k}}y^{k-a_{k}},\\
P_{\mathbf{s},2}^{(i)}(x,y)&\triangleq x^{-w+a_{n-j_{i-1}}}y^{-j_{i-1}+w-a_{n-j_{i-1}}}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{k=j_{i-1}}^{j_{i}}\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{k}\\
&=x^{-w+a_{n-j_{i-1}}}y^{-j_{i-1}+w-a_{n-j_{i-1}}}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{k=j_{i-1}}^{j_{i}}x^{w-a_{n-k}}y^{k-(w-a_{n-k})}\end{array}

where
w=a_{n}=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s})
. Since both
P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)
and
P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)
are generating polynomials, we conclude that
P_{\mathbf{s},1}^{(i)}(x,y)
and
P_{\mathbf{s},2}^{(i)}(x,y)
are generating polynomials of length-
(j_{i}-j_{i-1})
strings.
Let us denote by
\bm{r}^{(i)},\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\in\Sigma_{2}^{j_{i}-j_{i-1}}
, the strings that correspond to
P_{\mathbf{s},1}^{(i)}(x,y),P_{\mathbf{s},2}^{(i)}(x,y)
respectively.
In particular, if
i=\ell+1
, then
\bm{r}^{(i)}=\left(\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\right)^{*}
.
From the definition of
j_{i}
, we obtain that
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j_{i-1}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j_{i-1}}
,
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j_{i}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j_{i}}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{k}

\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{k}
for all
j_{i-1}<k<j_{i}
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{r}^{(i)}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\right),\\
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\left(\bm{r}^{(i)}\right)_{1}^{k}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\left(\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\right)_{1}^{k}\right)\text{ for all }1\leqslant k<j_{i}-j_{i-1}.\end{array}

(10)

In particular, we have
\bm{r}^{(\ell+1)}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell})\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell})^{*}
.
Moreover, if
\bm{r}^{(i)}=\left(\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\right)^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{i}-j_{i-1})\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{i}-j_{i-1})^{*}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell+1
, then
Equation

10
holds for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell+1
.

Putting everything together, we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition IV.10

For any
n\geqslant 1
, it holds that

\begin{array}[]{l}\Sigma_{2}^{n}=\left\{\bm{r}^{(1)}\cdots\bm{r}^{(\ell)}\bm{r}^{(\ell+1)}\left(\mathbf{t}^{(\ell)}\right)^{*}\cdots\left(\mathbf{t}^{(1)}\right)^{*}:\;\ell\geqslant 0;j_{0}=0;\right.\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad 1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots j_{\ell}\leqslant\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor,j_{\ell+1}=n-j_{\ell};\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{r}^{(\ell+1)}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell})\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell})^{*}\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{r}^{(i)},\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\in\Sigma_{2}^{j_{i}-j_{i-1}}\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell;\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.\bm{r}^{(i)},\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\text{ satisfy \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq_lastgeneralization} for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\right\}.\end{array}

Combining Proposition
IV.10
with
Theorem

IV.6
, we obtain the following.

Proposition IV.11

For any
n\geqslant 1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{U}(n)=\left\{\bm{r}^{(1)}\cdots\bm{r}^{(\ell)}\bm{r}^{(\ell+1)}\left(\mathbf{t}^{(\ell)}\right)^{*}\cdots\left(\mathbf{t}^{(1)}\right)^{*}:\;\ell\geqslant 0;j_{0}=0;\right.\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad 1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots j_{\ell}\leqslant\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor,j_{\ell+1}=n-j_{\ell};\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{r}^{(\ell+1)}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell})\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell})^{*}\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{r}^{(i)},\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\in\Sigma_{2}^{j_{i}-j_{i-1}}\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell;\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{r}^{(i)},\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\text{ satisfy \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq_lastgeneralization} for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell;\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.\text{there is at most one }i\text{ such that }j_{i}-j_{i-1}\geqslant 2\right\}.\end{array}

Remark IV.12

If we take
\bm{r}^{(i)}=\left(\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\right)^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{i}-j_{i-1})\cup\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{i}-j_{i-1})^{*}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
we
obtain
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)
. Therefore,
\mathcal{U}(n)
is indeed a generalization of
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(n)
.

Our next aim is to explicitly construct
\bm{r}^{(i)}
and
\mathbf{t}^{(i)}
so that they satisfy
Equation

10
. To this end, let
\bm{u}=\bm{r}^{(i)}\left(\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\right)^{*}
. Then
Equation

10
is equivalent to

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{u}_{1}^{\frac{m}{2}}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{u}_{\frac{m}{2}+1}^{m}\right),\\
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{u}_{1}^{k}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{u}_{m-k+1}^{m}\right)\text{ for all }1\leqslant k<\frac{m}{2},\end{array}

(11)

where
m=2(j_{i}-j_{i-1})
.

Then the question is reduced to explicitly constructing strings that satisfies
Equation

11
.
To move further, we first introduce the well known
Dyck strings

[
22
, Section 1.4]
. A string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{2h}
is called a Dyck string if
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s})=h
and
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i})\geqslant\lceil\frac{i}{2}\rceil
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant 2h-1
. The number of Dyck strings of length
2h
is given by the Catlan number
[
22
, Theorem 1.4.1]
:

C_{h}\triangleq\frac{1}{h+1}\binom{2h}{h}.

For even
m\geqslant 4
, let

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}(m)=\left\{\bm{u}\in\Sigma_{2}^{m}:\;u_{1}=1,u_{\frac{m}{2}}=0,u_{\frac{m}{2}+1}=1,u_{m}=0;\right.\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\exists I\subseteq\{2,\ldots,\frac{m}{2}-1\}\text{ such that if }i\neq 1,\frac{m}{2},\text{ and }i\leqslant\frac{m}{2},\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\text{ then }u_{i}\neq u_{m-i+1}\text{ if and only if }i\in I;\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\left.\bm{u}_{I}\text{ is a Dyck string}\right\}.\end{array}

By definition, we have

\left|\mathcal{A}(m)\right|=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{4}-1\rfloor}\binom{\frac{m}{2}-2}{2i}2^{\frac{m}{2}-2-2i}\frac{1}{i+1}\binom{2i}{i}

(12)

If
m=2
, we define
\mathcal{D}(m)\triangleq\{00,11\}
. If
m\geqslant 4
is even, we define
\mathcal{D}(m)\triangleq\{\bm{u}:\;\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}(m)\text{ or }\bm{u}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}(m)\}
.

Lemma IV.13

Let
m\geqslant 2
be an even integer. Then a string
\bm{u}\in\Sigma_{2}^{m}
satisfies
Equation

11
if and only if
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{D}(m)
.

Proof:

The
\Leftarrow
direction follows immediately from symmetry around
m/2
in the construction.
Thus, we only need to prove the
\Rightarrow
direction.
If
m=2
, the conclusion is trivial so we may assume
m\geqslant 4
.

We first notice that since
m\geqslant 4
, the second row of
Equation

11
has a meaning. From the second row, we deduce
u_{1}\neq u_{m}
.
Assume that
u_{1}=1
and
u_{m}=0
(the case
u_{1}=0
and
u_{m}=1
is similar).
Notice that the second row of
Equation

11
implies that

Up to this point we studied the set of strings which can be uniquely reconstructed (up to reversals) from their prefix-suffix composition multiset.
However, we did not study how many sequences there are that share the same prefix-suffix composition multiset. This is the focus of the next section.

V
Mutually equivalent strings

In this section we focus our attention to the study of sets of strings with a common prefix-suffix composition multiset.
To clearly state the goals of this subsection, we define the following equivalence relation on the set of strings.

Definition V.1

Let
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
be two strings of length
n
. We say that
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{t}
are equivalent, denoted
\mathbf{s}\sim\mathbf{t}
,
if
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{t})
, i.e., if
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{t}
have the same prefix-suffix composition multiset.

It is clear that the relation ”
\sim
” is indeed an equivalence relation.
For a string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
of length
n
, we define

\displaystyle E\left(\mathbf{s}\right)\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{t}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}:\;\mathbf{t}\sim\mathbf{s}\right\}

to be the set of all strings that are equivalent to
\mathbf{s}
, and

\displaystyle E_{n}\triangleq\max\left\{\left|E\left(\mathbf{s}\right)\right|~:~\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}\right\}.

The goal of this subsection is to completely characterize the sets
E(\mathbf{s})
and
E_{n}
for any
\mathbf{s}
and
n
.
To that end, we build upon previous ideas. Specifically, we use similar ideas to those appeared in
Theorem

III.4
,
Theorem

IV.6
, Proposition
IV.10
and
Theorem

IV.16
.
Before diving into the calculations, we give some simple examples.

Example V.2

1.

Let
n=1
. Then
E\left(0\right)=\left\{0\right\}
,
E\left(1\right)=\left\{1\right\}
and hence
E_{1}=1
.

2.

Let
n=2
. Then
E\left(00\right)=\left\{00\right\}
,
E\left(11\right)=\left\{11\right\}
, and
E\left(01\right)=\left\{01,10\right\}
. Therefore,
E_{2}=2
.

3.

From Corollary
III.12
, we conclude that for
3\leqslant n\leqslant 5
,
\left|E(\mathbf{s})\right|=1
or
2
for any
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
and that
|E_{n}|=2
.

In the rest of this section, we always assume that
n\geqslant 2
, unless otherwise is stated.

Recall that for any
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, we can always find
0=j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots<j_{\ell}\leqslant\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor

\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}

\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}

In other words,
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
j=j_{i},n-j_{i}
, and
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\neq\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
j\neq j_{i},n-j_{i}
.

Given a string
\mathbf{s}
, we define

I_{\mathbf{s}}\triangleq\left\{i:\;1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell+1,j_{i}-j_{i-1}\geqslant 2\right\}.

Then
Theorem

IV.6
asserts that

cannot be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal if and only if
\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|\geqslant 2
. Lemma
III.10
and

tell us that
\mathbf{t}\in E(\mathbf{s})
if only if is obtained from
\mathbf{s}
by swapping all
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
’s and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
’s for all
j\in S
, where
S
is a union of maximal intervals. Therefore, we have the following natural definition.

Definition V.3

Let
A\subseteq I_{\mathbf{s}}
be a set. We define the set

\displaystyle\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},A)=\begin{cases}\biggl\{\mathbf{t}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}~:~\forall i\in A,\;\forall j_{i-1}<j<j_{i},\;(P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y))_{j}=(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y))_{j},(P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y))_{n-j}=(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y))_{n-j},\\
\text{ and }(P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y))_{j}=(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y))_{j}\text{ otherwise }\biggr\}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\text{ if }A\neq\emptyset\\
\left\{\mathbf{s}\right\}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{if }A=\emptyset,\end{cases}

and define
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)
as

\swap(\mathbf{s},A)=\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},A)\cup\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s}^{\star},A).

In words,
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)
is the set of all the strings obtained by swapping between
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
,
and between
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{n-j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{n-j}
, for all
j_{i-1}<j<j_{i}
for some
i\in A
, while keeping
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
and
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
intact for all other
j
’s.
If
A=\emptyset
, we define
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)=\left\{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\star}\right\}
.

Notice that
|\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},A)|=1
which implies that
1\leqslant\left|\swap(\mathbf{s},A)\right|\leqslant 2
. Indeed, if
I_{\mathbf{s}}=\emptyset
then
A=\emptyset
.
This means that
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\star}
and
\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},A)=\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s}^{\star},A)
which in turn, implies that
|\swap(\mathbf{s},A)|=1
.
If
I_{\mathbf{s}}\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathbf{s}\neq\mathbf{s}^{\star}
and
\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},A)\neq\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s}^{\star},A)
, implying that
|\swap(\mathbf{s},A)|=2
.

Lemma V.5

Let
A,B
be two subsets of
I_{\mathbf{s}}
such that
A\neq B
.

(i)

If
A\cup B\neq I_{\mathbf{s}}
, then
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)\cap\swap(\mathbf{s},B)=\emptyset
.

(ii)

If
A\cup B=I_{\mathbf{s}}
but
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
, then
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)\cap\swap(\mathbf{s},B)=\emptyset
.

(iii)

If
A\cup B=I_{\mathbf{s}}
and
A\cap B=\emptyset
, then
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)=\swap(\mathbf{s},B)
.

Proof:

(i)
Since
A\cup B\neq I_{\mathbf{s}}
,
there exists
i_{0}\in I_{\mathbf{s}}\setminus\left(A\cup B\right)
which implies that there is
j_{i_{0}-1}<j<j_{i_{0}}
for which
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
.
By definition,
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)=\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},A)\cup\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s}^{\star},A)
and
\swap(\mathbf{s},B)=\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},B)\cup\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s}^{\star},B)
.
Assume
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)\cap\swap(\mathbf{s},B)\neq\emptyset
, i.e., there exists
\mathbf{t}\in\swap(\mathbf{s},A)\cap\swap(\mathbf{s},B)
.
For
\mathbf{t}
, we have

\left(P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\right)_{j}\in\left\{\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j},\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}\right\}.

Assume
\left(P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\right)_{j}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j}
.
This implies that
\mathbf{t}\in\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},A)\cap\swap_{1}(\mathbf{s},B)
, which, in turn, implies that
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}}

for all
j_{i-1}<j^{\prime}<j_{i}
such that
i\in A\triangle B
where
\triangle
is the symmetric difference.
This is possible only if
A\triangle B=\emptyset
which contradicts the assumption.
A similar contradiction arises if we assume
\left(P_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\right)_{j}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j}
.

(ii)
Let
C=A\cap B
. Since
C\neq\emptyset
, we can choose an
i_{1}\in C
. Then
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}}\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}}
for all
j_{i_{1}-1}<j^{\prime}<j_{i_{1}}
. Since
A\neq B
, we have
A\setminus C\neq\emptyset
or
B\setminus C\neq\emptyset
.

Assume that
A\setminus C\neq\emptyset
and choose
i_{2}\in A\setminus C=A\setminus B
.
We have
\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}}\neq\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}}
for all
j_{i_{2}-1}<j^{\prime\prime}<j_{i_{2}}
.
Let
\mathbf{t}_{1}\in\swap(\mathbf{s},A)
and
\mathbf{t}_{2}\in\swap(\mathbf{s},B)
be arbitrary.
For
\mathbf{t}_{1}
, we have either

\left(P_{\mathbf{t}_{1}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}},\;\left(P_{\mathbf{t}_{1}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}},

or

\left(P_{\mathbf{t}_{1}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}},\;\left(P_{\mathbf{t}_{1}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}}.

On the other hand, for
\mathbf{t}_{2}
, we have either

\left(P_{\mathbf{t}_{2}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime}},\;\left(P_{\mathbf{t}_{2}}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}}=\left(P_{\mathbf{s}}^{*}(x,y)\right)_{j^{\prime\prime}},

\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}=A^{\text{\textcentoldstyle}}
, meaning that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}
is an open under complement set obtained from the power set of
I_{\mathbf{s}}
.
Of course
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}
is not unique.
For example, if
I_{\mathbf{s}}=\{1,4\}
, then the power set is
2^{I_{\mathbf{s}}}=\left\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{4\},\{1,4\}\right\}
.
We can take
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}=\left\{\emptyset,\{1\}\right\},\;\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}=\left\{\emptyset,\{4\}\right\},\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}=\left\{\{1\},\{1,4\}\right\}
, or

\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}=\left\{\{4\},\{1,4\}\right\}
.
However, if
I_{\mathbf{s}}\neq\emptyset
, it is easy to see that
\left|\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}\right|=2^{\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|-1}
,
regardless of the choice of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}
.

Corollary V.8

For any
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}
, we have
E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)=\bigcup\limits_{A\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}}\swap(\mathbf{s},A)
. In particular,
\left|E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)\right|=2^{\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|}
.

Proof:

The first equality follows from noticing that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{t})
for every
\mathbf{t}\in\swap(\mathbf{s},A)
for some
A\subseteq I_{\mathbf{s}}
and from Lemma
V.5
. Indeed, Lemma
V.5
asserts that if
A\cup B=I_{\mathbf{s}}
and
A\cap B=\emptyset
then
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)=\swap(\mathbf{s},B)
.
Thus, instead of going over all
A\subseteq I_{\mathbf{s}}
, it suffices to consider sets
A
without their complement.
Thus, we are left to prove the second equality.

If
I_{\mathbf{s}}=\emptyset
, then
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\star}\in\mathcal{U}(n)
, implying
\left|E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)\right|=1=2^{\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|}
.
Assume that
I_{\mathbf{s}}\neq\emptyset
, i.e.,
\left|\swap(\mathbf{s},A)\right|=2
for each
A\subseteq I_{\mathbf{s}}
.
In other words, each
A\subseteq I_{\mathbf{s}}
contributes
2
to
\left|E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)\right|
.
On the other hand, from the construction of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}
we have that for
A,B\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}
, either
A\cup B\neq I_{\mathbf{s}}
or
A\neq I_{\mathbf{s}}\setminus B
.
In either case, Lemma
V.5
asserts that
\swap(\mathbf{s},A)\cap\swap(\mathbf{s},B)=\emptyset
.
Therefore,
\left|E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)\right|=2\left|\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}\right|=2^{\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|}
.
∎

We present some examples to explain Corollary
V.8
.

Example V.9

1.

Let
n=6
and
\mathbf{s}=101010
. From Example
IV.1
, we already know that
\ell=1
,
j_{1}=2
, and
j_{\ell+1}=j_{2}=4
.
Therefore,
I_{\mathbf{s}}=\left\{1,2\right\}
since
j_{2}-j_{1}=j_{1}-j_{0}=2
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{s}}=\left\{\emptyset,\{1\}\right\}
. By definition, we have
\swap(\mathbf{s},\emptyset)=\left\{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\star}\right\}
and
\swap(\mathbf{s},\{1\})=\left\{011001,100110\right\}
. Then by Corollary
V.8
, we have

E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)=\swap(\mathbf{s},\emptyset)\cup\swap(\mathbf{s},\{1\})=\left\{101010,010101,011001,100110\right\}

and
\left|E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)\right|=2^{\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|}=2^{2}=4
.

2.

Let
n=6
and
\mathbf{s}=010111
. From Example
IV.1
, we know that
\ell=0
so
I_{\mathbf{s}}=\{1\}
.
By Corollary
V.8
, we have

E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)=\swap(\mathbf{s},\emptyset)=\left\{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\star}\right\}

and
\left|E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)\right|=2^{\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|}=2
.

3.

Let
n=7
and
\mathbf{s}=1011010
. From Example
IV.1
, we know that
\ell=1
and
j_{1}=2
,
so
I_{\mathbf{s}}=\{1,2\}
.
By definition, we have
\swap(\mathbf{s},\emptyset)=\left\{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\star}\right\}
and
\swap(\mathbf{s},\{1\})=\left\{0111001,1001110\right\}
.
Then by Corollary
V.8
, we have

E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)=\swap(\mathbf{s},\emptyset)\cup\swap(\mathbf{s},\{1\})=\left\{1011010,0101101,0111001,1001110\right\}

and
\left|E\left({\mathbf{s}}\right)\right|=2^{\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|}=4
.

We see that the results in Example
V.9
are consistent with those in Example
III.6
.

Our final goal for this section is to estimate the maximal size of
E(\mathbf{s})
, i.e., to calculate
E_{n}
.
From Corollary
V.8
, we see that in order to determine
E_{n}
, we should determine the maximum value of
\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|
.

Theorem V.10

For
n\geqslant 2
, we have
E_{n}=2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1}
.

Proof:

The proof is a case-by-case analysis.

1.

Assume
n
is even.

If
j_{\ell}=\frac{n}{2}
:

We have
j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell}=n-2j_{\ell}=0
. Then
|I_{\mathbf{s}}|\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n/2}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor
.

If
j_{\ell}=\frac{n}{2}-1
:

We have
j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell}=2
.
Then
\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\frac{n}{2}-1}{2}\right\rfloor+1=\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1
.
This bound can be achieved by taking
j_{i}-j_{i-1}=2
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell+1
with possibly one exception in which
j_{i}-j_{i-1}=1
.

If
j_{\ell}\leqslant\frac{n}{2}-2
:

We have
j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell}\geqslant 4
.
Thus,
\left|I_{\mathbf{s}}\right|\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\frac{n}{2}-2}{2}\right\rfloor+1=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor
.

Thus, if
n
is even, then
E_{n}=\max\left\{2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor},2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1}\right\}=2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1}
.

2.

Assume
n
is odd.

If
j_{\ell}=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
:

We have
j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell}=1
.
Then
|I_{\mathbf{s}}|\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{4}\right\rfloor
.

If
j_{\ell}\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-1
:

We have
j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell}\geqslant 3
.
Thus,
|I_{\mathbf{s}}|\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-1}{2}\right\rfloor+1=\left\lfloor\frac{n-3}{4}\right\rfloor+1=\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1
.
This bound can be achieved by taking
j_{\ell}=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-1
and
j_{i}-j_{i-1}=2
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
with possibly one exception in which
j_{i}-j_{i-1}=1
.

Therefore, if
n
is odd, then
E_{n}=\max\left\{2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{4}\right\rfloor},2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1}\right\}=2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1}
.

Overall, we obtain that
E_{n}=2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{4}\right\rfloor+1}
which is the wanted result.
∎

Remark V.11

Notice that Theorem
V.10
asserts that
E_{n}\geqslant 4>2
for all
n\geqslant 6
.
This means that there exists a string
\mathbf{s}
of length
n
that cannot be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal for every
n\geqslant 6
.

Up to now we studied strings that can (or cannot) be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal.
Although the condition of unique reconstruction up to reversal makes sense from a computational point of view, for application purposes, we prefer strings which can be uniquely reconstructed (not up to reversal). This is the main focus of the next section.

VI
Composition codes

In this section we study strings which can be uniquely reconstructed from their composition multiset. To that end, we define a
composition code
,

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, as a set of strings such that for
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{t})
implies
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{t}
.
That is to say, any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{C}
can be uniquely reconstructed.
For example, consider the set
\mathcal{C}_{0}
that contains the strings
\mathbf{s}
such that
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\star}
, or
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
implies
\mathbf{s}^{\star}\notin\mathcal{C}_{0}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{C}_{0}=\left\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{U}(n):\;\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\star},\text{ or exactly one of }\mathbf{s}^{\star}\text{ and }\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\right\}.

If follows from Theorem
IV.16
that
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is a composition code.

Before we present our construction, we mention some relevant results on composition codes.
The authors of
[
21
]
studied the composition multiset reconstruction in the general case for which several strings are to be reconstructed simultaneously.
In this work, we consider the case of reconstructing only one string. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the construction presented in
[
21
]
can be improved in the case of a single string reconstruction.
Indeed, using the construction in
[
21
, Theorem 10]
, we get a composition code
C(n,1)
of size whose order of magnitude is
2^{n-\sqrt{n}}
, where
n=m+\frac{17}{2}\sqrt{m}
for some positive integer
m
.
Considering the code
\mathcal{C}_{0}
above, we have
|\mathcal{C}_{0}|=\frac{|\mathcal{U}(n)|-|\mathcal{C}_{1}(n)|}{2}+|\mathcal{C}_{1}(n)|=\frac{|\mathcal{U}(n)|+|\mathcal{C}_{1}(n)|}{2}
where

\mathcal{C}_{1}(n)=\left\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{U}(n)~:~\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\star}\right\}
.
Hence
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is of order of magnitude of at least
\frac{2^{n-3}}{\sqrt{n}}
by Equation (
8
).
Thus, for the specific case of a single string reconstruction, in terms of code size,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
improves upon the construction given in
[
21
, Theorem 10]
.
Notice, however, that
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is not optimal in terms of code size since we can add strings
\mathbf{s}\notin\mathcal{U}(n)
for
n\geqslant 6
.

Our goals in this section is to study optimal composition codes (in terms of code size).
In the next subsections we present several constructions for composition codes, some of which are capable of correcting errors.
We start with constructions under the assumptions that there are no errors while reading the compositions.

VI-A

Error-free prefix-suffix composition multiset

In this subsection we present two constructions of composition codes.
We first notice that by definition, a subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\Sigma_{2}^{n}
is a composition code if and only if
|\mathcal{C}\cap E(\mathbf{s})|\leqslant 1
for any
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
.
Therefore, the following code construction is optimal.

Construction VI.1

For all
n\geqslant 6
, let
\mathcal{C}_{max}\subseteq\Sigma_{2}^{n}
be such that
|\mathcal{C}\cap E(\mathbf{s})|=1
for any
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
.

We can use Corollary
V.8
to explicitly determine
E(\mathbf{s})
for any
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
.
Therefore, Construction
VI.1
is indeed an explicit construction.
Note that the code
\mathcal{C}_{max}
is not unique in general.

Although any given
\mathcal{C}_{max}
is an optimal code in terms of code size,
we do not know how to uniquely reconstruct an arbitrary
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{max}
when given
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})
.
Fortunately, it is possible to construct a code such that every string
\mathbf{s}
in that code can be uniquely reconstructed from
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})
.

For a string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma^{n}
, let
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbf{s}),\mathcal{M}_{s}(\mathbf{s})
denote the multiset of all the compositions of prefixes of
\mathbf{s}
, and the multiset of
all suffixes of
\mathbf{s}
, respectively.
When given
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbf{s})
or
\mathcal{M}_{s}(\mathbf{s})
, it is straightforward to (uniquely) reconstruct
\mathbf{s}
,
so the idea in the next construction is to impose additional constraints on
\mathbf{s}
such that when given
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{s})
,
we can distinguish
\mathcal{M}_{p}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
from
\mathcal{M}_{s}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
.
With this idea and Corollary
IV.14
in mind, it is now natural to give the following construction.

Construction VI.2

For all
n\geqslant 6
, let

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)=\left\{\bm{r}^{(1)}\cdots\bm{r}^{(\ell)}\bm{r}^{(\ell+1)}\left(\mathbf{t}^{(\ell)}\right)^{*}\cdots\left(\mathbf{t}^{(1)}\right)^{*}:\;\ell\geqslant 0;j_{0}=0\right.\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad 1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots<j_{\ell}\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,j_{\ell+1}=n-j_{\ell};\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{r}^{(\ell+1)}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(j_{\ell+1}-j_{\ell})^{*};\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\bm{r}^{(i)},\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\in\Sigma_{2}^{j_{i}-j_{i-1}}\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell;\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.\bm{r}^{(i)}\left(\mathbf{t}^{(i)}\right)^{*}\in\mathcal{A}(2j_{i}-2j_{i-1})\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\right\}.\end{array}

It follows from the construction of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
that for every
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
,
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\geqslant\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)

for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
.
Recall that
\mathbf{t}\sim\mathbf{s}
if and only if
\mathbf{t}\in\swap(\mathbf{s},A)
for some
A\subseteq I_{\mathbf{s}}
.
It is straightforward to verify that if
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
,
\mathbf{t}\sim\mathbf{s}
, and
\mathbf{t}\neq\mathbf{s}
, then there exists some
j_{0}
such that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{t}_{1}^{j_{0}}\right)<\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{t}_{n-j_{0}+1}^{n}\right)
and hence
\mathbf{t}\notin\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
.
That is to say,
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
is a composition code.
Furthermore, since
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{j}\right)\geqslant\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{n-j+1}^{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, it is possible to distinguish
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbf{s})

from
\mathcal{M}_{s}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
when given
\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
.
Thus, any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
can be uniquely reconstructed from
\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
.
The reconstruction process is straightforward by the discussions above.
The size of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
is

\left|\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)\right|=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(n)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell+1}h(j_{i}-j_{i-1}),

(13)

where
j_{0}=0
,

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(n)\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell+1})~:~\ell\geqslant 0,1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots j_{\ell}\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,j_{\ell+1}=n-j_{\ell}\right\},

and

h(j_{i}-j_{i-1})\triangleq\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }j_{i}=j_{i-1}\\
2,&\mbox{if }j_{i}-j_{i-1}=1\\
\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(n-2j_{\ell})\right|,&\mbox{if }i=\ell+1\text{ and }n-2j_{\ell}\geqslant 2\\
\left|\mathcal{A}(2j_{i}-2j_{i-1})\right|,&\mbox{if }j_{i}-j_{i-1}\geqslant 2\text{ and }i\neq\ell+1.\end{cases}

By letting
\ell=0
, it follows from
Equation

13
that
\left|\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)\right|>\left|\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\right|\geqslant\frac{2^{n-3}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}
.
Hence, the redundancy of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
is less than
\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(n)+3+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\pi)
and the code
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
has asymptotic rate
1
.

It is worth noticing that Construction
VI.2
is under the assumption that the prefix-suffix composition
multiset
\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
is error-free.
Unfortunately, this assumption is false in practical applications because the MS/MS readout technique is error prone.
The authors of
[
21
]
considered the following two types of errors.

1.

Missing of prefixes or suffixes
. These type of errors occur when the masses of some prefixes and suffixes are not measured and/or reported.

2.

Mass reducing substitution errors
. These type of errors occur when a composition is mistakenly read out as a composition of the same length,
but with a smaller Hamming weight, i.e., a larger mass is reported as a smaller mass due to loss of atoms during the fragmentation process.

More details regarding the errors can be found in
[
21
, Section V]
. The following are examples for the type of errors mentioned above.

Example VI.3

Let
\mathbf{s}=101010
. One can check that
\mathbf{s}=101010\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(6)
with
\ell=1
.
Consider the erroneous prefix-suffix composition multiset

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)=\left\{1,01,01^{2},0^{2}1^{2},0^{3}1^{3},0^{3}1^{3},0^{3}1^{2},0^{2}1^{2},0^{2}1,01,0\right\},

one can see that the prefix composition
0^{2}1^{3}
is missing. Now consider the erroneous prefix-suffix composition multiset

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)=\left\{1,01,01^{2},0^{2}1^{2},0^{3}1^{2},0^{3}1^{3},0^{3}1^{3},0^{3}1^{2},0^{2}1^{2},0^{2}1,01,0\right\},

one can see that the prefix composition
0^{2}1^{3}
is mistakenly read out as
0^{3}1^{2}
.
Now let
\mathbf{t}=100110
and consider the erroneous prefix-suffix composition multiset

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{3}\left(\mathbf{t}\right)=\left\{1,01,0^{2}1,0^{2}1^{2},0^{3}1^{2},0^{3}1^{3},0^{3}1^{3},0^{3}1^{2},0^{2}1^{2},01^{2},01,0\right\},

one can see that the prefix composition
0^{2}1^{3}
is mistakenly read out as
0^{3}1^{2}
. This makes
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{3}(\mathbf{t})=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(\mathbf{s})
.

In the rest of this section, we will modify Construction
VI.2
to obtain composition codes that can correct these two type of errors.

VI-B

Missing of prefixes or suffixes

In this subsection, we aim to construct composition codes capable of correcting the first type of errors.
Suppose that there are at most
t
prefix compositions or suffix compositions are missing.
For a given
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
, even if the prefix composition of length
i
is missing but the suffix composition of length
i
remains unchanged
(or vice versa), we will still regard both of them are missing, due to the fact that we cannot identify whether the remained one is the prefix composition
or a suffix.
Therefore, this error corresponds to at most
2t
erasures in the reconstructed string
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
(see
[
21
]
).
With this in mind, we can encode strings in
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
by the following three steps:

Step 1:

Choose a systematic
[m,n,\geqslant 2t+1]
code
\mathcal{E}
over finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=2^{s}
is a power of 2. Fix a basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Step 2:

For each
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
, we firstly encode it using
\mathcal{E}
into
\mathbf{s}\bm{r}^{\prime}
, where
\bm{r}^{\prime}
is the string of redundancy bits. Secondly, according to
\mathcal{B}
, we convert each symbol of
\bm{r}^{\prime}
into a binary string of length
s
. Then we concatenate these length-
s
binary strings into
\bm{r}
. At last, we get an intermediary string
\mathbf{s}\bm{r}
.

Step 3:

The output codeword is
\bm{c}=\bm{r}^{*}\mathbf{s}\bm{r}\in\Sigma_{2}^{N}
, where
N=n+2sr(\mathcal{E})
and
r(\mathcal{E})
is the redundancy of
\mathcal{E}
.
This step is to ensure that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{c}_{1}^{j}\right)\geqslant\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{c}_{N-j+1}^{N}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant N
.

The decoding of
\mathbf{s}
from
\bm{r}^{*}\mathbf{s}\bm{r}
is straightforward. Denote the resulting code by
\mathcal{E}_{2}(n)
. Then
r\left(\mathcal{E}_{2}(n)\right)=r\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)\right)+2s\cdot r\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
.

Since for each cyclic code there is a systematic encoder
[
23
, Page 128]
and BCH codes are in most cases among the best cyclic codes, we can let
\mathcal{E}
be a narrow-sense primitive BCH code over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with designed distance
2t+1
, then
r(\mathcal{E})\leqslant t\log_{2}(m+1)

[
23
, Theorem 5.1.7]
. Then
m-t\log_{2}(m+1)\leqslant n\leqslant m-2t
and
m-t\log_{2}(m+1)+4t\leqslant N\leqslant m-2t+2t\log_{2}(m+1)
. Now we have

\frac{\log_{2}\left(\left|\mathcal{E}_{2}(n)\right|\right)}{N}=\frac{\log_{2}\left(\left|\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)\right|\right)}{N}>\frac{m-t\log_{2}(m+1)-3-\log_{2}(\sqrt{\pi})-\log_{2}(\sqrt{m-2t})}{m-2t+2t\log_{2}(m+1)}.

Therefore, the code
\mathcal{E}_{2}(n)
has asymptotic rate
1
.

VI-C

Mass reducing substitution errors

In this subsection, we aim to construct composition codes capable of correcting the second type of errors.
Throughout this part, we assume that errors are not in batches, i.e., if the composition of the prefix length
i
is erroneous, then the composition of the prefixes lengths
i-1
and
i+1
are read out correctly, and similarly, if the composition of the suffix length
i
is erroneous, then the composition of the suffixes length
i-1
and
i+1
are read out correctly.
Although this requirement seems kind of impractical, we believe that the ideas we used to obtain the results in this subsection
will shed some light into further study of this type of errors.

First, we study the different effects an error has on a string.
Assume
0^{a_{1}}1^{b_{1}}
,
0^{a_{2}}1^{b_{2}}
, and
0^{a_{3}}1^{b_{3}}
are the compositions of prefixes of length
i-1
,
i
and
i+1
, respectively, of
\mathbf{s}
,
where
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Here we define
a_{1}=b_{1}=0
if
i=0
, and
a_{3}=a_{2}+1
,
b_{3}=b_{2}
if
i=n
, thus
b_{1}\leqslant b_{2}\leqslant b_{1}+1
and
b_{3}-1\leqslant b_{2}\leqslant b_{3}
.
Suppose that the composition
0^{a_{2}}1^{b_{2}}
is mistakenly read out as
0^{a_{2}^{\prime}}1^{b_{2}^{\prime}}
, where
b_{2}^{\prime}<b_{2}
and
a_{2}^{\prime}+b_{2}^{\prime}=a_{2}+b_{2}=i
.
Furthermore, suppose that the two compositions
0^{a_{1}}1^{b_{1}}
and
0^{a_{3}}1^{b_{3}}
are error free.
Assume also that given the erroneous prefix-suffix composition multiset
\widetilde{M}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
, we can still distinguish
\widetilde{M}_{p}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
from
\widetilde{M}_{s}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
(this requirement will be fulfilled by the presented code).

Note that
b_{1}\leqslant b_{3}\leqslant b_{1}+2
.
If
b_{3}=b_{1}
or
b_{3}=b_{1}+2
, then the error can be detected and corrected directly.
If
b_{3}=b_{1}+1=b_{2}+1
or
b_{3}=b_{2}=b_{1}+1
, and
b_{2}-b_{2}^{\prime}\geqslant 2
, then the error can be detected.
If
b_{3}=b_{2}=b_{1}+1
and
b_{2}-b_{2}^{\prime}=1
, then
b_{1}\leqslant b_{2}^{\prime}\leqslant b_{1}+1
and
b_{3}-1\leqslant b_{2}^{\prime}\leqslant b_{3}
.
In this case, we cannot detect the error because
\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i+1}=\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i-1}10
will be reconstructed as
\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i-1}01

(recall that we define
s_{n+1}=0
if
i=n
).

According to the above analyses, we divide the mass reducing substitution errors into two types.
If
b_{3}=b_{2}=b_{1}+1
and
b_{2}-b_{2}^{\prime}=1
, we say this error is
compatible
. For instance, the prefix-suffix composition multiset
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{3}(\mathbf{t})
in Example
VI.3
is obtained by introducing a compatible error.
If
b_{3}=b_{2}=b_{1}+1
but
b_{2}-b_{2}^{\prime}\geqslant 2
, we say it is
incompatible
.
Note that a single compatible error will result in two adjacent substitution errors in the reconstructed string,
while a single incompatible error will result in two adjacent erasures in the reconstructed string.

We now define the error model. For given integers
e_{1},e_{2}\geqslant 1
and
t\geqslant 2
, we formulate an
(e_{1},e_{2},t)
-mass reducing substitution error model as follows:

•

If a composition
0^{a}1^{b}
is mistakenly read out as
0^{a+b-b^{\prime}}1^{b^{\prime}}
, then
1\leqslant b-b^{\prime}\leqslant t
;

•

There are at most
e_{1}
compatible errors;

•

There are at most
e_{2}
incompatible errors.

•

If the composition of the prefix length
i
is erroneous, then the composition of the prefixes length
i-1
and
i+1
are not erroneous;

•

If the composition of the suffix length
i
is erroneous, then the composition of the suffixes length
i-1
and
i+1
are not erroneous.

We can encode
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
as follows:

Step 1:

Choose a systematic
[m,n,d]
code
\mathcal{E}
over finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that can correct
2e_{1}
substitutions and
2e_{2}
erasures, where
q=2^{s}
is a power of 2. Fix a basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Step 2:

For each
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
, we firstly encode it using
\mathcal{E}
into
\mathbf{s}\bm{r}^{\prime}
, where
\bm{r}^{\prime}
is the string of redundancy bits. Secondly, according to
\mathcal{B}
, we convert each symbol of
\bm{r}^{\prime}
into a binary string of length
s
. Then we concatenate these length-
s
binary string into
\bm{r}
. At last, we get an intermediary string
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=\bm{r}^{*}\mathbf{s}\bm{r}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n_{1}}
. This step is to ensure that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\left(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\right)_{1}^{j}\right)\geqslant\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\left(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\right)_{n_{1}-j+1}^{n_{1}}\right)
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n_{1}
.

Step 3:

The output codeword is
\bm{c}=\bm{1}^{t}\bm{r}^{*}\mathbf{s}\bm{r}\bm{0}^{t}\in\Sigma_{2}^{N}
, where
N=n+2sr\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
and
r\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
is the redundancy of
\mathcal{E}
. This step is to ensure that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{c}_{1}^{j}\right)\geqslant\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{c}_{N-j+1}^{N}\right)+t
for all
t+1\leqslant j\leqslant N-t
.

Denote the resulting code by
\mathcal{E}_{3}(n)
. Then
r\left(\mathcal{E}_{3}(n)\right)=r\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)\right)+2s\cdot r\left(\mathcal{E}\right)+2t
.

Since the first
t
bits of
\bm{c}
are all
1
’s and the last
t
bits of
\bm{c}
are all
0
’s, we can distinguish
\widetilde{M}_{p}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
from
\widetilde{M}_{s}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
, due to the property that
\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{c}_{1}^{j}\right)\geqslant\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\bm{c}_{N-j+1}^{N}\right)+t
for all
t+1\leqslant j\leqslant N-t
. Now the decoding of
\mathbf{s}
from
\bm{1}^{t}\bm{r}^{*}\mathbf{s}\bm{r}\bm{0}^{t}
is straightforward. Therefore, the code
\mathcal{E}_{3}(n)
can correct
(e_{1},e_{2},t)
-mass reducing substitution errors.

Lemma VI.4

[
23
, Theorem 1.11.6]

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
[m,n,d]
code, Then
\mathcal{C}
is capable of
correcting any pattern of
2e_{1}
substitution errors and
2e_{1}
erasures if
4e_{1}+2e_{2}\leqslant d-1
.

With Lemma
VI.4
in hand, we can let
\mathcal{E}
be a narrow-sense primitive BCH code over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with designed distance
2\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)+1
, then
r(\mathcal{E})\leqslant\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)\log_{2}(m+1)

[
23
, Theorem 5.1.7]
. Then
m-\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)\log_{2}(m+1)\leqslant n\leqslant m-2\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)
and
N\leqslant m-2\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)+2\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)\log_{2}(m+1)+2t
. Now we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{\log_{2}\left(\left|\mathcal{E}_{3}(n)\right|\right)}{N}&=\frac{\log_{2}\left(\left|\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)\right|\right)}{N}\\
&>\frac{m-\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)\log_{2}(m+1)-3-\log_{2}(\sqrt{\pi})-\log_{2}\left(\sqrt{m-2\left(2e_{1}+e_{2}\right)}\right)}{m-2t+2t\log_{2}(m+1)}.\end{array}

Therefore, the code
\mathcal{E}_{3}(n)
has asymptotic rate
1
.

VII
Deleted compositions from the entire composition multiset

In this section, we turn our attention to the second subclass of the general problem: reconstructing a string from its composition multiset when at most a constant number of substring compositions are lost. Notice that in this section we consider the problem of string reconstruction from the entire composition multiset and not only from the prefix-suffix composition multiset.

Let us denote by
C(\mathbf{s})
the composition multiset of a string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, i.e.,
C(\mathbf{s})=\left\{c(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{j})~:~1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant n\right\}
. For each
1\leqslant l\leqslant n
, let
C_{l}(\mathbf{s})
be the multiset of compositions of
all
the substrings of length
l
, i.e.,
C_{l}(\mathbf{s})=\left\{c\left(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{i+l-1}\right):\>1\leqslant i\leqslant n-l+1\right\}
.
Then we have
C(\mathbf{s})=\mathop{\cup}\limits_{l=1}^{n}C_{l}(\mathbf{s})
.
Our problem can be formally stated as follows.

Problem :
Let
L\subseteq\left\{1,2,\dots,n\right\}
be a set of size
t_{1}
and assume that for every
l\in L
,
C_{l}(\mathbf{s})
may suffer the loss of at most
t_{2}
compositions. Here we assume that
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are both constants. Is there a code which is resilient to that error model?
In other words, a code such that each codeword can be reconstructed even if such an error happens?

If
\mathcal{C}
is such a code, we say
\mathcal{C}
is a
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion correcting composition code
.
We also say
\mathcal{C}
can correct a
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion error.
By definition,
\mathcal{C}
is a
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion correcting composition code if and only if for any two distinct strings
\mathbf{s},\bm{v}\in\mathcal{C}
,
there is no subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
of size at most
t_{1}
such that

•

C_{l}(\mathbf{s})\neq C_{l}(\bm{v})
if and only if
l\in\mathcal{I}
;

•

\left|C_{l}(\mathbf{s})\setminus C_{l}(\bm{v})\right|\leqslant t_{2}
for each
l\in\mathcal{I}
.

The authors of
[
19
]
studied stronger cases in which a finite number (say
t_{1}
) of
\mathcal{C}_{l}
’s are totally missing, and not just a part of their elements.
Their codes are naturally
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion correcting composition codes for any
t_{2}
.
Since the construction of
[
19
]
is resilient to any
t_{2}
, it is reasonable that their codes can be improved
(in terms of size) for a limited value of
t_{2}
.
We improve upon the construction of
[
19
]
, and present a
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion correcting compositions codes of larger size for the case
t_{2}=2
.
In the rest of this section, we always assume that
n
is an even integer.

For a string
\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}
, define
w_{l}(\mathbf{s})\triangleq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-l+1}\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{i+l-1}\right)
for any
1\leqslant l\leqslant n
.
For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant\frac{n}{2}
, denote
\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{s})=\mathsf{wt}_{H}\!(s_{i}s_{n-i+1})
, i.e., the number of ones in the pair of symbols
s_{i},s_{n-i+1}
.
Finally, let
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})\triangleq\left(\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{s}),\ldots,\sigma_{\frac{n}{2}}(\mathbf{s})\right)
.
It is straightforward to show that
w_{l}(\mathbf{s})=w_{n-l+1}(\mathbf{s})
for any
l
.
It was proved in
[
18
, Section II-A]
that given
w_{1}(\mathbf{s}),\dots,w_{\frac{n}{2}}(\mathbf{s})
,
it is possible to uniquely determine
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})
.
For an integer
t\geqslant 1
, a set
\mathcal{S}^{(t)}_{R}(n)
was constructed in
[
18
, Section IV-B]
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t)}(n)&=\left\{\mathbf{s}\in\Sigma_{2}^{n}:\;s_{1}^{t}=\mathbf{0},s_{n-t+1}^{n}=\mathbf{1},\text{ and }\exists I\subset\{t+1,\ldots,n-t\}\text{ such that when }t+1\leqslant i\leqslant n-t,\right.\\
&\quad\;\;\text{we have }s_{i}\neq s_{n-i+1}\text{ for all }i\in I\text{ and }s_{i}=s_{n-i+1}\text{ for all }i\notin I;\\
&\quad\;\;\left.\mathbf{s}_{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]\cap I}\text{ is a Catalan-Bertrand string}\right\}.\end{array}

Before we connect
\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t)}(n)
to
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion correcting composition codes, we need the following definition.
We say that
i
is a
prefix-suffix pair
shared by two
n
-length strings
\mathbf{s},\bm{v}
if
\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i}=\bm{v}_{1}^{i}
,
\mathbf{s}_{n-i+1}^{n}=\bm{v}_{n-i+1}^{n}
.
The
longest prefix-suffix pair
shared by
\mathbf{s}
and
\bm{v}
is the largest
i
such that
i
is a prefix-suffix pair and
i+1
is not, i.e.,
if
\mathbf{s}_{1}^{i}=\bm{v}_{1}^{i}
and
\mathbf{s}_{n-i+1}^{n}=\bm{v}_{n-i+1}^{n}
but
s_{i+1}\neq v_{i+1}
or
s_{n-i}\neq v_{n-i}
.
The following lemma was obtained in
[
18
]
.

Lemma VII.1

[
18
, Lemma 4]

Let
\mathbf{s},\bm{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t)}(n)
be two distinct strings with
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})=\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v})
.
If the longest prefix-suffix pair shared by
\mathbf{s}
and
\bm{v}
is of length
i
,
then
\left|C_{n-i-k}(\mathbf{s})\setminus C_{n-i-k}(\bm{v})\right|\geqslant 2
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant t+1
.

By taking
t=t_{1}
, we obtain that
\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t_{1})}(n)
is a
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion correcting composition code for any
t_{2}\geqslant 1
.
Comparing the definition of
(t_{1},t_{2})
-deletion correcting composition codes and Lemma
VII.1
, we see that
\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t_{1})}(n)
has a stronger-than-need property, that is,
C_{l}(\mathbf{s})\neq C_{l}(\bm{v})
for at least
t_{1}+1
such
l
’s.
Therefore, to increase the code size, we aim at adding strings to
\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t_{1})}(n)
.
To this end, recall the definition of the set
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)
given in Equation (
6
).
Clearly,
\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t)}(n)\subsetneq\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)
for all
t\geqslant 1
, so we try to pick suitable strings in

\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t)}(n)
and add them to
\mathcal{S}_{R}^{(t)}(n)
.

Let
\mathbf{s},\bm{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)
be two distinct strings and assume
\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})=\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v})=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\frac{n}{2}})
.
When
\sigma_{i}\in\{0,2\}
, we have
s_{i}=s_{n-i+1}=v_{i}=v_{n-i+1}
.
As a result, if
\sigma_{i}\in\{0,2\}
for all
i\geqslant 2
, then
\mathbf{s}=\bm{v}
, which is a contradiction.
Thus, there exists some
i\geqslant 2
such that
\sigma_{i}=1
and denote by
i_{1}
the smallest one.
By the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{R}(n)
, we obtain that
s_{1}s_{i_{1}}
and
v_{1}v_{i_{1}}
are both Catalan-Bertrand strings and hence
s_{1}s_{i_{1}}=00=v_{1}v_{i_{1}}
(recall that in a Catalan-Bertrand string the number of zeros is strictly larger than the number of ones in any prefix).
So there exists some
i>i_{1}
such that
\sigma_{i}=1
. Otherwise, we have
\mathbf{s}=\bm{v}
, which is a contradiction. Now we can assume the indices
2\leqslant i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{l}\leqslant\frac{n}{2}
being such that
\sigma_{i}=1
if and only if
i\in\{1,i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{l}\}
, where
l\geqslant 2
.

From above analysis, we know that
s_{i_{1}}=v_{i_{1}}
which, in turn, implies that if
s_{i}\neq v_{i}
then
i\in\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{l}\}
.
Let
k\geqslant 2
be the smallest integer such that
s_{i_{k}}\neq v_{i_{k}}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
s_{i_{k}}=0
and
v_{i_{k}}=1
.

Lemma VII.2

With the above notations, we have
\left|C_{n-i_{k}-1}(\mathbf{s})\setminus C_{n-i_{k}-1}(\bm{v})\right|\geqslant 3

if and only if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(i)

k<l
,
i_{k+1}-i_{k}=1
and
(s_{i_{k+1}},v_{i_{k+1}})\neq(0,0),(1,0)
;

(ii)

k<l
,
i_{k+1}-i_{k}\geqslant 2
;

(iii)

k=l<\frac{n}{2}
.

Proof:

In the following we estimate
\Delta\triangleq\left|C_{n-i_{k}-1}(\mathbf{s})\setminus C_{n-i_{k}-1}(\bm{v})\right|
.
To ease notation, let
\mathbf{u}=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{k-1}}=v_{i_{1}}\cdots v_{i_{k-1}}
.
Let
N_{0}\triangleq N_{0}(\mathbf{u})=\left|\left\{j:\;s_{i_{j}}=0,1\leqslant j\leqslant k-1\right\}\right|

is the number of zeros in
\mathbf{u}
.
Besides, let
N_{1}

\triangleq

N_{1}(\mathbf{u})=k-1-N_{0}(\mathbf{u})
.
Since
v_{1}v_{i_{1}}\cdots v_{i_{k-1}}v_{k}
is a Catalan-Bertrand string,
v_{1}=0
and
v_{i_{k}}=1
, we have that the number of zeros in
\mathbf{u}
is strictly larger than the number of ones in
\mathbf{u}
, i.e.,
N_{0}(\mathbf{u})\geqslant N_{1}(\mathbf{u})+1
.
Since we have
s_{j}=s_{n-j+1}=v_{j}=v_{n-j+1}
for every

, and since
s_{i_{j}}=v_{i_{j}}
for
1\leqslant j<k
,
it follows that the possible elements in
C_{n-i_{k}-1}(\mathbf{s})
that are not in

L={c(

are

R={c(

We first consider the case

\begin{array}[]{llllllllrlll}&&&i_{k}&&&&&&&&\\

.
Our aim is to compare between elements in

and elements in

. Therefore, we may disregard common entries that appear simultaneously in all the elements that in

and in

. As a result, if

\displaystyle s_{1}\mathbf{u}s_{i_{k}}s_{i_{k+1}}s_{i_{k+2}}\cdots\overline{s}_{i_{k+2}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}s_{i_{k}}s_{i_{k+1}}s_{i_{k+2}}\cdots\overline{s}_{i_{k+2}}\overline{s}_{i_{k+1}},

Similarly, if

VIII
r
-Length Limited Composition Multiset

In this section we suggest a new reconstruction model.
Recall that in order to store data, two molecules with different mass are synthesized to represent the 0 and 1 symbols.
These molecules are combined into a string to represent the data that the user wishes to store.
In order to read the data, the synthetic polymers are read by tandem mass (MS/MS) spectrometer.
As part of the reading process, the polymer molecule breaks in a position which is chosen at random, creating a prefix and a suffix.
The breaking of the molecule is usually made by collisions, bombardment of the molecule with electrons or photons, or a chemical reaction (a molecule can also break down due to instability).
It is reasonable to assume that longer molecule fragments tend to break more often than shorter fragments.
For example, using electron bombardment, it is more likely that the electron will hit a longer polymer fragment than hitting a short fragment.
As a result, when the mass readings of the fragments are obtained, the composition of long fragments will not appear in those readings.
Leaving us with a composition multiset of shorter strings.

Since our results in this section apply to general alphabets, we assume our alphabet is a
q
-ary alphabet
\Sigma_{q}=\{a_{i}~:~a_{i}=i,0\leqslant i\leqslant q-1\}

q\geqslant 2
is an integer. Let
\Sigma_{q}^{*}

\Sigma_{q}
of finite length. For a string

.
Indeed, notice that there are no Lyndon words of length
r
with
r
appearances of
a_{q}
.
This implies that
a_{q}^{r}
appears exactly once in
C_{r}(\mathbf{s})
.
From that point onward, we can construct
\mathbf{s}
backwards by considering compositions containing a decreasing number of
a_{q}
s.

The number of length-
r
Lyndon words over an alphabet
\Sigma_{q}
is given by Witt’s formula
[
33
]

|\mathcal{L}_{r}(q)|=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{d|r}\mu(d)q^{r/d}

where
\mu
is the Möebius function.

Since
\mu(\cdot)\in\left\{0,1,-1\right\}
, we can bound the size
|\mathcal{L}_{r}(q)|
.
Notice that
\mu(1)=1
which implies that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}_{r}(q)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}q^{r}+\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|r\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\mu(d)q^{r/d}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{r}q^{r}+q^{r/2}

and similarly,

|\mathcal{L}_{r}(q)|\geqslant\frac{1}{r}q^{r}-q^{r/2}.

Thus, the number of Lyndon words over an alphabet of size
q
is
|\mathcal{L}_{r}(q)|=\frac{q^{r}}{r}\left(1+o(1)\right)
.

The length of a codeword
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
is bounded above by
2r
.
Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\log_{q}|\mathcal{L}_{r}(q)|}{2r}

\displaystyle=\frac{r-\log_{q}(r)+\log_{q}(1+o(1)}{2r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\log_{q}(r)}{r}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+o(1)).

Remark VIII.5

A similar encoding process can be used to reconstruct a string
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{r}(n)
when
n\geqslant 2r
. Thus, it is possible to use
\mathcal{C}_{r}(n)
as a code of length
n
.
Applying similar calculations with
n
instead of
2r
, we obtain code rate
R=r/n
.
Thus, when
r=r(n)
is a linear function of
n
, such as
r(n)=n/\alpha
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain a rate of
1/\alpha
. If
r(n)=o(n)
the code rate is
0
.

IX
Conclusion

In this paper, we firstly solved the problem on whether a string can be uniquely reconstructed up to reversal or not, when only given its prefix-suffix composition multiset:

•

we completely characterized their structures;

•

we give an explicit formula of their numbers;

•

for any given string
\mathbf{s}
, we gave a method to find all the strings that share a common prefix-suffix composition multiset with
\mathbf{s}
, and we gave a formula of the number of such strings.

As applications of these results, we gave a construction of composition codes of maximum size. But we do not know whether or not there is an efficient reconstruction algorithm to reconstruct an arbitrary string in this kind of code. This will be an interesting problem for the future research. As a compromise, we constructed a subcode
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
of some composition code of maximum size. For this subcode, there does exist a reconstruction algorithm. It is shown that the redundancy of this subcode is less that
\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(n)+3+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\pi)
.

In addition, we studied two error models of composition errors. For each error model, we modified
\mathcal{E}_{1}(n)
to get a composition code that can correct the studied type of error; each code has asymptotic rate
1
.

The authors of
[
21
]
focused on the problem of uniquely reconstructing a set of strings from the union of their prefix-suffix composition multisets. We believe that our results in this paper might be a helpful step towards fully understanding this problem. It is also interesting to extend our results to this situation.
Moreover, in either this paper or
[
21
]
, the constructed codes uses the idea that we can distinguish the prefix composition multiset from the suffix composition multiset.
An interesting question is whether it is possible to construct codes without this property, such that a set of strings can be uniquely reconstructed from the union of their prefix-suffix composition multiset. We leave this problem for a future endeavour.

Secondly, we initiated the problem of reconstructing a string from its composition multiset under the condition that at most a constant number of its compositions are lost. We solved this problem for a special case when the error is asymmetric. It is interesting to introduce new ideas to solve this problem for more parameters and even without the ”asymmetric” assumption.

Lastly, we presented the problem of
r
-length limited composition multiset reconstruction,
and provided a simple coding scheme that obtains a rate of
1/2
.
This problem gives rise to many interesting problems.
For example, is it possible to improve the code presented in this problem for the case
r=r(n)
is a linear function of
n
? What about codes for a constant
r
or for a different functions
r(n)
?
Is it possible to find bounds on the capacity of such codes?
Also, some generalizations of the model itself are of interest. For example, consider the case in which compositions of substrings of lengths between
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are obtained,
Consider the appearances of errors, or even considering a scattered noise model in which the compositions are obtained according to a probability which is a function of their length.
As an example, consider the case that all the compositions up to length
r_{1}
are obtained, and the compositions of strings of length
r_{1}+j
are obtained with a probability of
1/2^{j-1}
.
