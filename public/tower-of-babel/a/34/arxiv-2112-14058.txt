Abstract
In this paper, we introduce open parity games, which is a compositional approach to parity games. This is achieved by adding open ends to the usual notion of parity games. We introduce the category of open parity games, which is defined using standard definitions for graph games. We also define a graphical language for open parity games as a prop, which have recently been used in many applications as graphical languages. We introduce a suitable semantic category inspired by the work by Grellois and Melliès on the semantics of higher-order model checking. Computing the set of winning positions in open parity games yields a functor to the semantic category. Finally, by interpreting the graphical language in the semantic category, we show that this computation can be carried out compositionally.

Introduction

Parity game
is a major tool in theoretical computer science. Many formal verification problems such as model checking, satisfiability, etc.—can be reduced to solving parity games
[
35
]
, where alternation of least and greatest fixed point operators in a specification is modeled by the parity winning condition. Efficient solutions of parity games, therefore, benefit many problems; recent algorithmic works include
[
10
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in
compositionality
in formal verification in general, and in parity games in particular. It means that the property of a big system can be deduced from those of its constituent parts. One benefit is
efficiency
: compositionality can yield an efficient divide-and-conquer algorithm. Another is
maintainability
: compositional verification explicates an assumption that each subsystem must satisfy for the safety of the whole system; a subsystem can then be replaced freely as long as the local assumption is satisfied.

Compositional methods in model checking have been pursued in the literature, such as
[
9
,
27
]
. Many of those methods require a user to provide interfaces between subsystems, either as systems
[
9
]
or as specifications
[
27
]
. The role of compositionality is stressed in
higher-order model checking (HOMC)

[
16
,
33
]
, too, where intermediate results are combined along typing rules.

In this paper, influenced by the semantical constructs from
[
16
]
, we introduce a categorical framework in which parity games are both presented and solved in a compositional manner. The presentation is by a
prop

[
29
]
(products and permutations category), a categorical notion of “monoidal” algebraic structure. This categorical presentation enables us to formulate compositionality as the preservation of suitable structures of certain functors. It also enables us to exploit general categorical structures (traced, compact closed, etc.) and properties (such as freeness).
The use of props as graphical languages for various mathematical structures has been actively pursued recently (such as signal flow diagrams, matrices, and network games)
[
6
,
5
,
28
]
; the current work adds a new item to the list, namely parity games.

to be a
free
compact
closed category in a suitable sense. The functors
\mathcal{R}_{M}
and
\left\llbracket{-}\right\rrbracket_{M}
arise by the freeness; the commutativity is proved by the freeness, too.

In this paper, we find a new application of props as graphical languages in parity games. It allows one to solve parity games in a compositional manner (Ex.
5.11
), thanks also to the identification of the right semantical domain (namely
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
) that retains the right level of information in intermediate results. Such compositional solution has multiple potential applications. Firstly,
the categorical structure we identify has a lot in common with those used for HOMC
[
16
,
33
]
. Therefore we expect we can streamline known HOMC algorithms and reveal their categorical essences.
Secondly, we will pursue algorithmic applications, such as efficient divide-and-conquer algorithms and those which accommodate blackbox components as part of a game.

Related Work.

We use
\mathbb{F}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M},E^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M})
as the graphical language for open parity games.
The use of monoidal categories as graphical languages dates back
to
[
30
]
.
There have been numerous such languages; see
[
32
]

for a survey.
Languages that compositionally describe graph-like structures are
of particular interest to us:
[
11
]
describes the
algebra of directed acyclic graphs but does not consider cyclic
structures;
[
3
]
describes open Petri nets, and
compositionality is achieved “externally” by the use of cospans.

In particular, props have been used extensively as graphical languages.
They define graphical languages as models for some
mathematical structures (signal flow diagrams
[
6
]
,
networks
[
2
]
,
Petri nets
[
4
]
,
automata
[
31
]
,
and the ZX-calculus
[
7
,
8
]

respectively) and prove that the graphical language is
equivalent to the category that they are studying.
They can therefore transfer properties of the graphical language (for
example, decidability of equivalence of diagrams) to the category they
are studying.
In our work, however, we use the graphical language for
expressing open parity games compositionally, and we are not necessarily
interested in equivalence between
\mathbb{F}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M},E^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M})
and
\mathbf{OPG}_{M}
(see also Rem.
3.16
).

Note that our work uses a 2-colored prop for modeling the two possible
directions for edges in an open parity game,
while
[
4
,
6
,
28
]
only have a single type
of edges, which are undirected.
In
[
31
]
, the authors use a colored prop to
model different kinds of edges, and in particular, they use two colors to model directed edges.

Kissinger gives a general construction of
the free traced symmetric monoidal categories
\mathbb{F}_{\textnormal{tr}}(\Sigma)
,
which are also props
[
25
]
.
This is related to the free compact closed category
\mathbb{F}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M},E^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M})
in the present paper,
as explained in §
3
.
Free traced monoidal categories are also given in
[
23
]

in the study of attribute grammars, where
many-to-one signatures are treated while Kissinger’s paper treats many-to-many signatures.

Another related work is
[
14
]
, which introduces the concept
of composing games.
Their approach is mainly applied to economic models, and they use a symmetric monoidal category for compositional game theories.
However, their framework is different from ours in the sense that the
objects along which games are composed have different meanings: in our
framework, games are composed along graph edges, while in theirs,
games are composed along interfaces describing player choices, game
utility, etc.

Categories of Open Parity Games

We introduce the notion of open parity games.
It extends parity games by adding

to the game,
which are used to define composition of parity games; specifically, we
obtain a compact closed category
\mathbf{OPG}_{M}
of open parity games
(Def.

).

In this paper, we often encounter situations where the structure of

traced symmetric monoidal
category (TSMC)
or as a
compact closed category (CpCC)
.
(Specifically, we have three such classes of structures, yielding three TSMCs and CpCCs.
See Fig.

While our applicational interests lie in the CpCC structures, we work

2.1
Open Parity Games

Recall that a
parity game
is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Q,E,\rho,\omega)
where

(Q,E)
is a finite directed graph of
positions
and

edges
,
\rho\colon Q\to\left\{{\exists,\forall}\right\}
is the
role
function
, and
\omega:Q\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
is the
priority
function
.
An
infinite play
on
\mathcal{A}
is an infinite sequence
q_{0}q_{1}\dots\in Q^{{\mathbb{N}}}
such that, for all
i\geq 0
,
(q_{i},q_{i+1})\in E
.
A
finite play
is defined similarly.
Let
\mathrm{Play}_{\exists}
and
\mathrm{Play}_{\forall}
be the sets of finite plays
q_{0}\ldots q_{n}
such
that
\rho(q_{n})=\exists
or
\rho(q_{n})=\forall
, respectively.
An infinite play
q_{0}q_{1}\dots
is
winning
for
\exists
if the
maximum priority appearing infinitely often in
\omega(q_{0})\omega(q_{1})\ldots
is even.
A finite play
q_{0}\dots q_{n}
is winning for
\exists
if
\rho(q_{n})=\forall
.
A
strategy
of
\exists
is a partial function
\sigma_{\exists}:\mathrm{Play}_{\exists}\rightharpoonup Q

such that
(q_{n},\sigma_{\exists}(q_{0}\dots q_{n}))\in E
if
\sigma_{\exists}(q_{0}\dots q_{n})
is defined.
For any position
q
and strategies
\sigma_{\exists}
and

\sigma_{\forall}
, we denote by
\mathrm{play}^{\sigma_{\exists},\sigma_{\forall}}_{q}
the unique play starting from
q
and consistent
with both
\sigma_{\exists}
and
\sigma_{\forall}
.
A strategy
\sigma_{\exists}
is
winning
for
\exists
from
q\in Q

if for all strategies
\sigma_{\forall}
,
\mathrm{play}^{\sigma_{\exists},\sigma_{\forall}}_{q}
is winning for
\exists
.
A position
q\in Q
is
winning
for
\exists
if there is a
strategy
\sigma_{\exists}
winning for
\exists
from
q
.
We define
open
parity games by extending parity games with open ends.

Definition 2.1

(open parity game)
.

An
open parity game
from
\overline{m}
to
\overline{n}
is a tuple

(\overline{m},\overline{n},Q,E,\rho,M,\omega)
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

\overline{m}=(m_{\mathbbm{r}},\ m_{\mathbbm{l}})
and
\overline{n}=(n_{\mathbbm{r}},\ n_{\mathbbm{l}})
are pairs of natural numbers,
where
\overline{m}
represents the domain interface of the
game and
\overline{n}
the codomain interface.

2.

Q
is a finite set, whose elements are called
internal positions
.

3.

E
is a relation

E\subseteq([m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]+Q)\times([n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}]+Q)
, whose element is called an
edge
.
Moreover,
for any
s\in[m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]
, there is a unique
s^{\prime}\in[n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}]+Q
such that
(s,s^{\prime})\in E
;
and similarly
for any
t\in[n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}]
, there is a unique
t^{\prime}\in[m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]+Q
such that
(t^{\prime},t)\in E
.

4.

\rho
is a function
\rho:Q\rightarrow\{\exists,\forall\}
, which assigns a
role
to each internal position.

5.

M\in{\mathbb{N}}
is called the
maximal rank
and
\omega:Q\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{M}
is called the
priority function
.

We call an element of
([m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]+[n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}])+Q
a
position
,
one of
[m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]+[n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}]
an
open end
,
one of
[m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]
an
entry position
, and
one of
[n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}]
an
exit position
.

We extend the priority function
\omega
to

\omega:([m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]+[n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}])+Q\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{M}

by
\omega(i)=0
for
i\in[m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}}]+[n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}}]
,
i.e., we define the priority of each open end to be 0.

2.3
A Compact Closed Category of Open Parity Games

To obtain the category
\mathbf{OPG}_{M}
of open parity games, we use the

Int construction

[
22
]
(see also
[
19
]
for some correction).
It is a
free
construction from a traced symmetric monoidal
category
\mathbb{C}
to a compact closed category
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{C})
.
We briefly explain how it is defined here, but see
the full version
or
[
22
]
for more details.

Let
\mathbf{CpCC}
be the 2-category of (locally small) CpCCs,
compact closed functors, and monoidal natural transformations.
Note that its 2-cells automatically respect compact closed structures
and are monoidal natural
isomorphisms

[
21
, Proposition 7.1]
.
Also, let
\mathbf{TrSMC}_{g}
be the 2-category of (locally small) TSMCs,
traced symmetric strong monoidal functors, and monoidal natural
isomorphisms
.

Then the Int construction is a left biadjoint to the embedding
\mathbf{TrSMC}_{g}\to\mathbf{CpCC}
.
Specifically, given a traced symmetric monoidal cateogry

(\mathbb{C},\otimes,\mathbf{I},\sigma,\mathrm{tr})
,
the category
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{C})
is defined as follows:
An object of
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{C})
is a pair
(X_{+},X_{-})
of objects of
\mathbb{C}
.
Then
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{C})\big((X_{+},X_{-}),\,(Y_{+},Y_{-})\big):=\mathbb{C}(X_{+}\otimes Y_{-},\,Y_{+}\otimes X_{-})
,
and
\mathrm{id}_{(X_{+},X_{-})}:=\mathrm{id}_{X_{+}\otimes X_{-}}
.
Notably, for
f\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{C})\big((X_{+},X_{-}),\,(Y_{+},Y_{-})\big)
and
g\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{C})\big((Y_{+},Y_{-}),\,(Z_{+},Z_{-})\big)
,
the composite of
f
and

of the notion of Lawvere theory whose use has been actively
pursued recently
[

,
2

It gives to open parity games introduced in §

Moreover, we find that the category

(i) as the prop induced by a theory

Definition 3.1

Due to the space limitation, we put the detailed information that is written here in the full version.

We need some definitions for proving that
\mathbb{F}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M},E^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M})

We call an object of the category
\mathbf{Set}^{\{\mathbbm{r},\mathbbm{l}\}^{\ast}\times\{\mathbbm{r},\mathbbm{l}\}^{\ast}}

(

Recall that the signature
\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M}
consists of nodes
\mathrm{n}^{w,u}_{r,p}
of open parity games
and the unit

and counit

We define a CCS

or

Definition 3.10

(CCS
\Sigma_{M}
)

We define a CCS

\Sigma

,

is

where

(

V^{\ast}_{d_{1}\ldots d_{n}}:=V_{d_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes V_{d_{n}}

where
V_{\mathbbm{l}}:=V_{\mathbbm{r}}^{\bot}
.

Given a compact closed functor

and

of
\Sigma
into
\mathbb{C}
,

on

(ii)

,

\phi^{F}_{w}
(and

Theorem 3.13

\mathbb{F}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M},E^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M})
)

3.13

\epsilon^{\square_{M}}_{A}:=\big\{\bigl((0,a),a^{\prime}\bigr)\in({\mathbb{N}}_{M}{\times}|A|)\times|A|\ \big|\ a^{\prime}\leq_{A}a\big\}
and

\delta^{\square_{M}}_{A}:=\big\{\bigl((\max(p,q),a),(p,(q,a^{\prime}))\bigr)\in({\mathbb{N}}_{M}{\times}|A|)\times({\mathbb{N}}_{M}{\times}({\mathbb{N}}_{M}{\times}|A|))\ \big|\ a^{\prime}\leq_{A}a\big\}
.

Remark 4.4
.

We expect that this comonad can be extended to a
graded comonad

[
13
,
24
]

so that its Kleisli category interprets all open parity games,
without fixing the parity bound
M
.
However, we do not take this approach because it is reasonably harmless
to fix the maximal parity, while
the use of the complex notion of graded comonad makes it hard to see the essential idea.

Combining the above notions, we define the comonad
!_{M}
.

Definition 4.5

(comonad
!_{M}

[
15
,
16
]
)
.

We define a distributive law

\lambda:\mathcal{P}\circ\square_{M}\Rightarrow\square_{M}\circ\mathcal{P}

on
\mathbf{FinScottL}
by

\lambda_{(A,\leq_{A})}:=\big\{\,(X,(p,Y))\,\in\,P({\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times|A|)\times({\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times P(|A|))\ \big|\ \forall y\in Y,\exists\ a\in A,(p,a)\in X\text{ and }y\leq_{A}a\,\big\}
,
and we define a comonad
!_{M}=(!_{M},\epsilon^{!_{M}},\delta^{!_{M}})
on
\mathbf{FinScottL}
by:
(i)
!_{M}:=\mathcal{P}\circ\square_{M}
,
(ii)
\epsilon^{!_{M}}:=\epsilon^{\mathcal{P}}\circ(\mathcal{P}*\epsilon^{\square_{M}})
, and
(iii)
\delta^{!_{M}}:=(\mathcal{P}*\lambda*\square_{M})\circ(\delta^{\mathcal{P}}*\delta^{\square_{M}})

where
*
is the horizontal composition of natural transformations.

In general, the Kleisli category of a comonad inherits the cartesian product from the original category,
and so the Kleisli category
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}
has a cartesian products given by

(|A|+|B|,\leq_{A}+\leq_{B})
.
(Furthermore,
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}
is cartesian
closed
[
15
,
16
]
, though we do
not use this fact.)

In order to give a model of higher-order model checking,
Grellois and Melliès introduced
a fixpoint operator
\mathrm{fix}^{\mathbf{GM}}
on
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}

to deal with infinite
plays
[
15
,
16
]
.
Its definition is based on the notion of
semantic run-tree
.
From
\mathrm{fix}^{\mathbf{GM}}
, we get a trace operator on
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}
,
because having a fixpoint operator is equivalent to having a trace
operator for a cartesian category
[
18
]
.
We then get a trace operator

\mathrm{tr}^{\mathbf{GM}}
on
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}
, since if
\mathbb{C}
is a
traced symmetric monoidal category, then so is
\mathbb{C}^{\mathit{op}}
canonically.

Now we give the definition of
\mathrm{tr}^{\mathbf{GM}}

(the definition of
\mathrm{fix}^{\mathbf{GM}}
can be found in
the full version).
In order to do this, we first adapt the notion of semantic run-tree by
Grellois and Melliès (which we also call
semantic run-tree
)
through the correspondance above between fixpoint operators and trace
operators.

Definition 4.6

(semantic run-tree for
\mathrm{tr}^{\mathbf{GM}}
)
.

Let
R\in\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}(D+A,D+B)
and
a\in|A|
;
then especially,
R\subseteq P({\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times(|D|+|B|))\times(|D|+|A|)
.
A
semantic run-tree

\psi
for
R
and
a
(for the trace operator) is
a possibly infinite (
{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times(|D|+|A|+|B|)
)-labeled tree
\psi
that satisfies the following conditions:

1.

The label of the root of
\psi
is
(0,a)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times|A|
.

2.

Any node of
\psi
that is neither a leaf nor the root has its label in
{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times|D|
.

3.

For any non-leaf node (possibly being the root that is not a leaf) of
\psi
with label
(p,x)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times(|D|+|A|)
,
let

X\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times(|D|+|A|+|B|)

be the set of the labels of all the children of the node. Then
(X,x)\in R
.

4.

For any leaf node (possibly being the root that is a leaf) of
\psi
such that its label belongs to
{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times(|D|+|A|)

(rather than
{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times|B|
) and is
(p,x)
, we have
(\emptyset,x)\in R
.

We write
\mathrm{SRT}(A,B,D,R,a)
for the set of semantic run-trees with respect to
A,B,D,R
and
a
.
For a semantic run-tree
\psi\in\mathrm{SRT}(A,B,D,R,a)
, we define

\mathrm{leaves}(\psi)\in|\mathcal{P}(\square_{M}(B))|=P({\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times|B|)
as the set of elements

(p,b)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times|B|
such that
there exists a leaf
\ell
of
\psi
such that:
(i) the label of leaf
\ell
is
(p^{\prime},b)
for some
p^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{M}
and
(ii)
p
is the maximal priority encountered on the path from the leaf
\ell
to the root of
\psi
.

A semantic run-tree is similar to a (usual) run for a parity game,
except that (i) its branching models
\forall
’s choices, and (ii) it is
induced by a suitable semantic construct
R
instead of a
graph-theoretic notion of game.
In our use of the notion (§
5
),
R
will
be a “summary” of an open parity game, which retains the necessary
data to decide who is winning yet is much smaller than the original
open parity game.

Definition 4.7

(trace operator
\mathrm{tr}^{\mathbf{GM}}

[
15
,
16
]
)
.

For every
A,B,D\in\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}
, we define a trace operator

\mathrm{tr}^{\mathbf{GM}}_{D;A,B}:\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}(D\otimes A,D\otimes B)\rightarrow\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}(A,B)
as follows:

\mathrm{tr}^{\mathbf{GM}}_{D;A,B}(R):=\{(\mathrm{leaves}(\psi),a)\mid\psi\in\mathrm{SRT}(A,B,D,R,a)\text{ that meets the
parity condition}\}

where a semantic run-tree meets the
parity condition
if for every infinite path
((p_{i},x_{i}))_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
the maximum priority met infinitely along the path is even
(i.e.,
\max\{q\mid\#\{i\mid p_{i}=q\}=\infty\}
is even).

Thus
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}
is a traced symmetric monoidal category.
The trace operator above is used in the (sequential) composition of
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
given below.

Now we define the semantic category
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
for open parity games.
In §
5
,
we explain how
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}
and
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
serve as
the semantic categories in the traced and compact closed structures, respectively,
by giving a suitable
winning-position
functor
\mathcal{W}^{\mathbbm{r}}_{M}
from
\mathbf{OPG}_{M}^{\mathbbm{r}}
to
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}

and then by inducing
\mathcal{W}_{M}
(see Fig.
3
).

Definition 4.8

(semantic category
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
)
.

By applying the Int construction to the traced symmetric monoidal category
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}
,
we obtain the compact closed category
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
.

Remark 4.9
.

We have
\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}\cong\mathbf{FinPreord}_{T}
, where

\mathbf{FinPreord}
is the category of finite preordered sets and monotonic functions, and
(a Kleisli morphism of) the monad
T
is of the following form:

\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}\big(A,B\big)\ \cong\ \mathbf{FinPreord}\Big(A,\big(P^{\uparrow}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{N}_{M}\times{|}B{|},\ \leq_{\square_{M}\!B})),\ \supseteq\big)\Big)
,
where
P^{\uparrow}
is the upward-closed powerset.
This description is closed to the double-powerset style semantics for 2-player games, e.g.,
in
[
20
]
.

We want to define an interpretation functor
\left\llbracket{-}\right\rrbracket_{M}\colon\mathbb{F}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M},E^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M})\to\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
that reflects the winning condition on open parity games.
The idea is that, if
((j_{k},p_{k})_{k\in n},i)\in\left\llbracket{G}\right\rrbracket_{M}
, then player
\exists
can force any play that
starts from the entry position corresponding to
i
in
G
to end in one of
the exit positions corresponding to the
j_{k}
’s while encountering a maximum
priority of
p_{k}
.
By Thm.
3.13
, we obtain this functor as:

Definition 4.10

(interpretation functor
\left\llbracket{-}\right\rrbracket_{M}
)
.

We define the
interpretation functor

\left\llbracket{-}\right\rrbracket_{M}:\mathbb{F}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M},E^{\mathrm{opg}}_{M})\rightarrow\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})

to be the compact closed functor whose action on objects is
generated by
\left\llbracket{\mathbbm{r}}\right\rrbracket_{M}=\big(([1],=),(\emptyset,=)\big)\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
and whose action on morphisms is generated by:

\displaystyle\left\llbracket{\mathrm{n}^{a,b}_{\exists,p}}\right\rrbracket_{M}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\emptyset&(\overrightarrow{b}+\overleftarrow{a}=0)\\
\big\{\,(T,i)\ \big|\ (j,p)\in T\text{ for some }j\in[\overrightarrow{b}+\overleftarrow{a}]\,\big\}&(\overrightarrow{b}+\overleftarrow{a}\neq 0)\end{cases}

\displaystyle\left\llbracket{\mathrm{n}^{a,b}_{\forall,p}}\right\rrbracket_{M}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}P(\emptyset)\times[\overrightarrow{a}+\overleftarrow{b}]&(\overrightarrow{b}+\overleftarrow{a}=0)\\
\big\{\,(T,i)\ \big|\ \{(j,p)\mid j\in[\overrightarrow{b}+\overleftarrow{a}]\}\subseteq T\,\big\}&(\overrightarrow{b}+\overleftarrow{a}\neq 0).\end{cases}

Both morphisms above are from

\left\llbracket{a}\right\rrbracket_{M}=([\overrightarrow{a}],[\overleftarrow{a}])

to
\left\llbracket{b}\right\rrbracket_{M}=([\overrightarrow{b}],[\overleftarrow{b}])

in
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}})
, i.e.,

P({\mathbb{N}}_{M}\times[\overrightarrow{b}+\overleftarrow{a}])-\mspace{-13mu}+\mspace{-18mu}\to[\overrightarrow{a}+\overleftarrow{b}]
in
\mathbf{FinScottL}
,
where powerset is ordered by inclusion.

Strategies and the Winning Position Functor

In §
5.1

we define notions of play and strategy for open parity games
(in the traditional style of graph game),
as well as winning, losing, and
pending
strategies and positions.
We use these definitions in
§
5.2
to define the

winning-position
functor
\mathcal{W}^{\mathbbm{r}}_{M}:\mathbf{OPG}_{M}^{\mathbbm{r}}\rightarrow\mathbf{FinScottL}_{!_{M}}^{\mathit{op}}
, which
gives information that allows compositional computation of winning
positions.
We show that the diagram in Figure

commutes,
which gives a justification of our compositional approach to parity games.
In this section, we assume that
a given rightward open parity game
\mathcal{A}
is of the form

\mathcal{A}=(m,n,Q,E,\rho,M,\omega)
.

5.1
Winning Strategies and Winning Positions for Open Parity Games

Here we give the notions of strategy and play.
We also define the
denotation
of a strategy/position, which is
how they win, lose, or are pending.
These definitions are given only for
rightward
open parity games,
but we can readily extend them to general open parity games,
because any open parity game is a rightward open parity game
by definition:
\mathbf{OPG}_{M}((m_{\mathbbm{r}},m_{\mathbbm{l}}),\,(n_{\mathbbm{r}},n_{\mathbbm{l}}))=\mathbf{OPG}_{M}^{\mathbbm{r}}(m_{\mathbbm{r}}+n_{\mathbbm{l}},n_{\mathbbm{r}}+m_{\mathbbm{l}})
.

First we define the notion of strategy.
For an open parity game
\mathcal{A}\colon[m]\to[n]
, a family

(s_{i})_{i\in I}
of positions in
[m]+[n]+Q
is called a
position sequence
if

I={\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 1}
or
I=\left\{{1,\ldots,k}\right\}
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 1}
,
(in that case, we also write
(s_{i})_{i\in I}
as
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
).

Definition 5.1

(
\exists
-strategy and
\forall
-strategy)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a rightward open parity game from
m
to

.
We define
\mathrm{Play}_{\exists}(\mathcal{A}):=\big\{\,s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\ \big|\ k\geq 1,\,s_{i}\in Q\ (i\in[k]),\ (s_{i},s_{i+1})\in E\ (i\in[k-1]),\,\rho(s_{k})=\exists\,\big\}
.
We often just write
\mathrm{Play}_{\exists}
if there is no confusion.
Then, an
\exists
-strategy
on
\mathcal{A}
is a partial function

\tau:\mathrm{Play}_{\exists}\rightharpoonup[n]+Q

where for any
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\in\mathrm{Play}_{\exists}
, (i) if
\tau(s_{1}\cdots s_{k})=s
, then
(s_{k},s)\in E
, and (ii) if
\tau(s_{1}\cdots s_{k})
is undefined, then for all
s\in[n]+Q
,
(s_{k},s)\notin E
.
A
\forall
-strategy on an open parity game
\mathcal{A}
is defined in the same way,
by replacing the occurrence of
\exists
with
\forall
in the above definition.
The sets of

\exists
-strategies and
\forall
-strategies on
\mathcal{A}
are
\mathrm{Str}_{\exists}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathrm{Str}_{\forall}(\mathcal{A})
, respectively.

A pair of an
\exists
-strategy and a
\forall
-strategy resolves the
non-determinism in a game to induce a unique play:

Definition 5.2
.

(induced play
\mathrm{play}^{\tau_{\exists},\tau_{\forall}}_{a}
)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a rightward open parity game from
m
to

.
The
induced play

\mathrm{play}^{\tau_{\exists},\tau_{\forall}}_{a}

\exists
-strategy

\tau_{\exists}
and a
\forall
-strategy

(s_{i})_{i\in I}
(for the prefix order) such that:
(i)
a\,E\,s_{1}
,
(ii) for any
i\in I
, if
\rho(s_{i})=\exists
and
\tau_{\exists}(s_{1}\cdots s_{i})
is defined,
then
\tau_{\exists}(s_{1}\cdots s_{i})=s_{i+1}
, and similarly
(iii) for any
i\in I
, if
\rho(s_{i})=\forall
and
\tau_{\forall}(s_{1}\cdots s_{i})
is defined,
then
\tau_{\forall}(s_{1}\cdots s_{i})=s_{i+1}

\left\llbracket{\mathcal{A}_{2}\circ\mathcal{A}_{1}}\right\rrbracket_{M}=\left\{{(T,i)}\,\middle|\,{i\in\left\{{1,2^{\prime}}\right\},(1,1^{\prime})\in T}\right\}
.

Similarly, to compose
\left\llbracket{\mathcal{A}_{3}}\right\rrbracket_{M}
with
\left\llbracket{\mathcal{A}_{2}\circ\mathcal{A}_{1}}\right\rrbracket_{M}
, we also have to compute the corresponding
semantic run-trees.
Here, there can be no leaves (no exit positions), and the run-tree
corresponding to taking the loop infinitely meets the parity
condition (because all its nodes are
(2,1^{\prime})
, except for the
root), so
(\emptyset,1)
is in the interpretation, whence

\left\llbracket{\mathcal{A}}\right\rrbracket_{M}=\left\{{(T,1)}\,\middle|\,{\text{true}}\right\}=\{(\emptyset,1)\}
. Therefore,
1
is a winning poistion in
G
.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

We have given a compositional approach to parity games by exhibiting
their underlying compact closed structure.
Parity games can be composed by considering open ends, and we
defined a prop that gives a graphical language to describe such open
parity games.
At the semantic level, we have given a notion of winning/losing
positions that takes open ends into account.
It retains enough information to be compositional, but is still
extensional, as it can be computed without referring to starategies.

The current semantic category is a
strategy-insensitive
model,
in that it only keeps track of the (non)existence of a winning strategy,
while
strategy-sensitive
models should keep track of all
strategies (perhaps up to some suitable equivalence).
Strategy-sensitive models can easily be obtained when restricting to

history-free
strategies.
One future work is to find some history-dependent strategy-sensitive model.

It could be fruitful to deepen the link between the existing body of
work on props and our use of props in this work.
For example, by showing equivalence between the graphical language and
the category of open parity games, we could get decidability results
on parity games from the syntax, as in
[
6
]
.

Another possible future work is the coalgebraic treatment of open parity games.
There seems a bijective correspondence between open parity games
up to some notion of bisimilarity and
pairs of functions

[m]\to Q
and

Q\to P(Q+[n])\times\{\exists,\forall\}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{M}

up to the bisimilarity of coalgebras.
Then there might exist a compact closed structure in the category of coalgebraic open parity
games up to the bisimilarity.
However, it seems subtle to give the same categorical structure in the category of,
say, the above form of coalgebras up to
isomorphism
.
Also, it might be interesting to give a functor (similar to
\mathcal{R}_{M}
) from some graphical category to
the category of coalgebras up to bisimilarity by using bialgebraic methods
[
26
,
34
]
.
We did not take the bisimilarity approach in this paper
because in game theory we basically consider the level up to isomorphism, say, for complexity.
