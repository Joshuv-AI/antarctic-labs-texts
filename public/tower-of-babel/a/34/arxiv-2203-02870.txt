Nonadiabatic Correction and Adiabatic Criteria of Noninteracting Quantum Dot Systems
Masahiro Hasegawa 1 and Takeo Kato 1

Abstract
We theoretically study nonadiabatic corrections for charge pumping in a noninteracting electron model of a single-level quantum dot. We derive a formula for the velocity limit of parameter driving to realize adiabatic pumping and illustrate its features in the wide-band limit. We discuss the effect of the nonadiabatic corrections in terms of a typical velocity limit, which is defined by averaging the velocity limits on the parameter contour. We also show that the typical velocity limit vanishes when the adiabaticity breakdown occurs in a quantum dot coupled to reservoirs with a band edge.

The geometric structure of adiabatic pumping is helpful for understanding non-equilibrium properties of nanoscale quantum engines.
However, in order to justify a geometric description of adiabatic pumping, its pumping protocol has to satisfy an adiabatic (or quasi-static) condition in the target systems.
In previous theoretical work, the adiabatic condition was not studied seriously and was roughly estimated intuitively or empirically.
However, in order to estimate the non-equilibrium properties of nanoscale engines, for example, the upper bound of efficiency of pumping, one has to evaluate the maximum velocity of parameter driving to satisfy the adiabatic condition because the parameter driving should be as fast as possible to increase the pumping power.
In particular, the upper limit of the driving velocity generally depends on the position in the parameter space, which is crucial to geometrical optimization of the pumping power
[
34
]
.
This indicates that estimation of the adiabatic condition is not a simple problem.
Therefore, to be able to understand the non-equilibrium nature of nanoscale engines, the adiabatic condition should be defined in a systematic way.

To this end, we theoretically derive a detailed condition for adiabaticity by taking the dependence of the parameter driving contour into account in this work.
As a minimum setup for adiabatic pumping, we choose a non-interacting electron system composed of a quantum dot and two reservoirs as shown in Fig.
1
and consider adiabatic pumping induced by periodic modulation of the dot-reservoir coupling.
Our formula constitutes a general procedure to calculate the upper limit of the driving velocity as a function of parameters and can be used to detect bottleneck regions in the parameter space at which the limit velocity is suppressed.
We examine the adiabatic condition by analyzing the leading nonadiabatic corrections for a quantum dot system coupled to reservoirs with a constant density of states by assuming an infinite bandwidth.
Then we examine a quantum dot coupled to reservoirs with band edges and discuss the problem of adiabaticity breakdown
[

in terms of our formulation of the adiabatic condition.

Model

2.1
Model Hamiltonian

We consider a noninteracting electron model of a single-level quantum dot coupled to two electric reservoirs (see Fig.
1
) described by the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H(t)=\epsilon_{d}d^{\dagger}d+\sum_{r,k}\left[\epsilon_{k}c_{rk}^{\dagger}c_{rk}+\gamma_{r}(t)(d^{\dagger}c_{rk}+c_{rk}^{\dagger}d)\right].

(1)

Here,
d
and
d^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators of an electron in the quantum dot with an energy level
\epsilon_{d}
, while
c_{rk}
and
c_{rk}^{\dagger}
are those of electrons with a wavenumber
k
and an energy
\epsilon_{k}
in the reservoir
r=\mathrm{L},\mathrm{R}
, respectively.
The time-dependent coupling constant between the dot and the reservoir
r
is denoted as
\gamma_{r}(t)
.
We assume that the reservoir temperature is zero and set the reservoir electrochemical potentials as
\mu_{r}
.
Then, the distribution function of the reservoir
r
can be described as

\displaystyle f_{r}(\omega)=\Theta(\mu_{r}-\omega),

(2)

where
\Theta(\omega)
is the Heaviside step function.

Throughout this paper, we assume that both reservoirs have the same continuous density of states
\rho(\omega)
.
In the wide-band limit, the strength of the dot-reservoir coupling is described by a time-dependent linewidth, as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{r}(t)=2\pi\rho_{0}|\gamma_{r}(t)|^{2},

(3)

where
\rho
is the density of states at the Fermi energy.
In Sect.
5
, where we discuss the breakdown of adiabaticity, we need to consider the dependence of the density of states carefully and use a time-dependent linewidth, defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{r}(\omega;t)=2\pi\rho(\omega)|\gamma_{r}(t)|^{2}.

(4)

2.2
Transferred charge in one cycle

We consider charge pumping induced by a time-dependent dot-reservoir coupling.
The charge transferred from the reservoir
r
into the quantum dot in one pumping cycle, denoted by
\delta Q_{r}
, is calculated as

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\Braket{I_{r}(t)},

(5)

\displaystyle I_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=-e\frac{d}{dt}\left[\sum_{k}c_{rk}^{\dagger}(t)c_{rk}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=(-i)e\gamma_{r}(t)\left(d^{\dagger}(t)c_{rk}(t)-c_{rk}^{\dagger}(t)d(t)\right),

(6)

where
T
is the pumping period.
The average of the charge current operator can be expressed in terms of Keldysh Green’s functions as

\displaystyle\Braket{I_{r}(t)}

\displaystyle=2e\int dt_{1}\ \mathrm{Re}\biggl[G^{R}(t,t_{1})\Sigma_{r}^{<}(t_{1},t)

\displaystyle\biggl.\hskip 28.45274pt+G^{<}(t,t_{1})\Sigma_{r}^{A}(t_{1},t)\biggr].

(7)

Here,
G
and
\Sigma
denote the Green’s function and the one-particle-irreducible self-energy, respectively, and the superscripts,
R
,
A
, and
<
indicate the retarded, advanced, and lesser components (see Appendix
A
).

3
Adiabatic pumping and nonadiabatic correction

3.1
Average-time approximation

To calculate the charge pumped in one cycle in the slow driving region, we employ the adiabatic approximation, that is called the average-time approximation in Ref.
\citen
Splettstoesser2005Dec.
In this approximation, the time-dependent Hamiltonian is expanded up to the first order at frozen time
t
:

\displaystyle H(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq H(t)+(t^{\prime}-t)\frac{dH(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=H(t)+(t^{\prime}-t)\sum_{r}F_{r}\frac{d\gamma_{r}(t)}{dt},

(8)

where
F_{r}=\partial H/\partial\gamma_{r}
is the response operator.
Pumped charge in one cycle is approximated into two terms,

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}

\displaystyle\simeq\delta Q_{r}^{(0)}+\delta Q_{r}^{(1)},

(9)

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\ \Braket{I_{r}^{(a)}(t)}_{t},\quad(a=0,1),

(10)

where
\Braket{\cdot}_{t}
denotes the steady-state ensemble average described by the Hamiltonian at frozen time
t
.
The current operators,
I_{r}^{(0)}
and
I_{r}^{(1)}
, are calculated as

\displaystyle I_{r}^{(0)}(t)

\displaystyle=I_{r}(t),

(11)

\displaystyle I_{r}^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r_{1}}\int dt_{1}\ \mathcal{T}\left[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})\right](t_{1}-t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}.

(12)

Here,
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator.
The former term,
\delta Q_{r}^{(0)}
, is referred to as the steady-state term, because it is just the integral of the steady-state charge current in one cycle.
The latter term,
\delta Q_{r}^{(1)}
, is referred to as the adiabatic pumping term, because it is independent of the pumping period
T
, while the steady state contribution is of order
O(T)
.
This term can be interpreted in a geometrical manner by substituting Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
10
) as

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(1)}_{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\ \sum_{r_{1}}A_{r,r_{1}}(t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt},

(13)

\displaystyle A_{r,r_{1}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int dt_{1}\,\langle{\cal T}[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})]\rangle(t_{1}-t).

(14)

3.2
Adiabatic expansion

In obtaining the pumped charge for adiabatic pumping in the previous subsection, the higher-order corrections, such as,
\frac{d^{2}H(t)}{dt^{2}}
and
\left(\frac{dH(t)}{dt}\right)^{2}
, were neglected.
In other words, adiabatic charge pumping is realized under the condition that these higher-order corrections are small enough compared to the adiabatic pumping term.
Here, in order to see the details of the condition for adiabatic pumping, we derive the higher-order corrections explicitly.

The pumped charge can be generally written as a series expansion with respect to the inverse of the pumping period,
1/T
:

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta Q_{r}^{(n)}

(15)

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\ \Braket{I_{r}^{(n)}(t)}_{t}

(16)

Here,
\delta Q^{(n)}
is of order
O((1/T)^{n-1})
and
I^{(n)}_{r}(t)
is referred to as the
n
th-order current operator.
The zeroth- and first-order current operators correspond to Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
), respectively.

The second-order current operator is calculated as

\displaystyle I^{(2)}_{r}(t)=I^{(2,1)}_{r}(t)+I^{(2,2)}_{r}(t),

(17)

\displaystyle I^{(2,1)}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r_{1}}\int dt_{1}\ \mathcal{T}\left[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times(t_{1}-t)^{2}\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt^{2}},

(18)

\displaystyle I^{(2,2)}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}\int dt_{1}dt_{2}\ \mathcal{T}\left[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})F_{r_{2}}(t_{2})\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times(t_{1}-t)(t_{2}-t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt}.

(19)

Substituting Eqs. (
17
)-(
19
) into Eq. (
16
), the second-order correction can be expressed in quadratic form,

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\ \Biggl(\sum_{r_{1}}B^{(1)}_{r,r_{1}}(t)\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}B^{(2)}_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt}\Biggr)

(20)

\displaystyle B_{r,r_{1}}^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle=\int dt_{1}\,\langle{\cal T}[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})]\rangle(t_{1}-t)^{2},

(21)

\displaystyle B_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle=\int dt_{1}dt_{2}\,\langle{\cal T}[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})F_{r_{2}}(t_{2})]\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times(t_{1}-t)(t_{2}-t).

(22)

Using integration by parts, the second-order correction becomes

\displaystyle\braket{I_{r}^{(2)}(t)}=\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}B_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt}.,

(23)

\displaystyle B_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)=B^{(2)}_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)-\frac{\partial B^{(1)}_{r,r_{1}}(t)}{\partial\gamma_{r_{2}}}.

(24)

The third-order current operator is calculated in the same way:

\displaystyle I^{(3)}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=I^{(3,1)}_{r}(t)+I^{(3,2)}_{r}(t)+I^{(3,3)}_{r}(t),

(25)

\displaystyle I^{(3,1)}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!}\sum_{r_{1}}\int\!dt_{1}\,\mathcal{T}\left[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})\right]

\displaystyle\times(t_{1}-t)^{3}\frac{d^{3}\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt^{3}},

(26)

\displaystyle I^{(3,2)}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2!}\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}\int\!dt_{1}\,\mathcal{T}\left[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})F_{r_{2}}(t_{2})\right]

\displaystyle\times(t_{1}-t)^{2}(t_{2}-t)\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt^{2}}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt},

(27)

\displaystyle I^{(3,3)}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!}\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\int\!dt_{1}\,\mathcal{T}\left[I_{r}(t)F_{r_{1}}(t_{1})F_{r_{2}}(t_{2})F_{r_{3}}(t_{3})\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-25.60747pt\times(t_{1}-t)(t_{2}-t)(t_{3}-t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{3}}(t)}{dt}.

(28)

Using integration by parts, the third-order correction can be summarized into two terms:

\displaystyle\braket{I_{r}^{(3)}(t)}=\braket{I_{r}^{(3v)}(t)}+\braket{I_{r,a}^{(3a)}(t)},

(29)

\displaystyle\braket{I_{r}^{(3v)}(t)}=\!\!\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\!\!C_{r,r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}(t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{3}}(t)}{dt},

(30)

\displaystyle\braket{I_{r}^{(3a)}(t)}=\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}D_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt^{2}}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt}.

(31)

The first term,
\langle I_{r}^{(3v)}(t)\rangle
, consists only of the first derivative of the driving parameter, whereas the second term,
\langle I_{r}^{(3a)}(t)\rangle
, includes the second derivative of the driving parameter.
In general, we can derive higher-order corrections by using a similar procedure for the
n
th-order operator, defined as

\displaystyle I^{(n)}_{r}(t)=\int dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n}\ \mathcal{T}\left[I_{r}(t)F_{n}(t_{1},\cdots,t_{n})\right],

(32)

\displaystyle F_{n}(t_{1},\cdots,t_{n})=\prod_{m=1}^{n}\sum_{l_{m}}\sum_{r^{\prime}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\gamma_{r^{\prime}}}(t_{m})\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{l_{m}}\gamma_{r^{\prime}}(t),

(33)

though their expressions become much more complex.

3.3
Adiabatic condition

We define the driving velocity of the parameters as

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}(t)=(v_{\rm L}(t),v_{\rm R}(t))=\biggl(\frac{d\gamma_{\rm L}}{dt},\frac{d\gamma_{\rm R}}{dt}\biggr).

(34)

To write down the adiabatic condition, it is convenient to introduce the normalized velocity, defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{v}}(t)=(\tilde{v}_{\rm L}(t),\tilde{v}_{\rm R}(t))=\frac{{\bm{v}}(t)}{|{\bm{v}}(t)|}.

(35)

Let us discuss how the driving velocity is restricted in order to realize adiabatic pumping.
Charge pumping can be regarded as ‘adiabatic’ when the higher-order (nonadiabatic) corrections of the charge current are much smaller than the adiabatic current:

\displaystyle|\langle{I_{r}^{(2)}(t)}\rangle|,|\langle{I_{r}^{(3v)}(t)}\rangle|,|\langle{I_{r}^{(3a)}(t)}\rangle|,\cdots\ll|\langle I_{r}^{(1)}(t)\rangle|.

(36)

Note that this condition is always satisfied in the slow-driving limit of
{\cal T}\rightarrow\infty
since
\langle I_{r}^{(n)}(t)\rangle
is proportional to
(1/T)^{n-1}
.
For a finite speed of driving, we introduce a small tolerance factor
\eta
(
\ll 1
) and describe the extent to which the ratio of the higher-order current corrections to the adiabatic current is allowed as follows:

\displaystyle|\langle I_{r}^{(2)}(t)\rangle|\leq\eta|\langle I_{r}^{(1)}(t)\rangle|,

(37)

\displaystyle|\langle I_{r}^{(3v)}(t)\rangle|\leq\eta|\langle I_{r}^{(1)}(t)\rangle|,

(38)

\displaystyle|\langle I_{r}^{(3a)}(t)\rangle|\leq\eta|\langle I_{r}^{(1)}(t)\rangle|.

(39)

We call these the adiabatic conditions.

The adiabatic condition for the second-order correction, Eq. (
37
), can be rewritten using Eqs. (
13
) and (
23
) as

\displaystyle|v_{r}(t)|\leq v^{(2)}_{{\rm lim},r}(t),

(40)

where the ‘velocity limit’
v^{(2)}_{{\rm lim},r}(t)
is given as

\displaystyle v_{{\rm lim},r}^{(2)}(t)=\eta\frac{|A_{r}(t)|}{|B_{r}(t)|},

(41)

\displaystyle A_{r}(t)=\sum_{r_{1}}A_{r,r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{1}}(t),

(42)

\displaystyle B_{r}(t)=\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}B_{r,r_{1},r_{2}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{2}}(t).

(43)

In a similar way, the adiabatic condition for the third-order correction, Eq. (
38
), can be rewritten as

\displaystyle|v_{r}(t)|\leq v_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(3)}(t),

(44)

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(3)}(t)=\sqrt{\eta\frac{|A_{r}(t)|}{|C_{r}(t)|}},

(45)

\displaystyle C_{r}(t)=\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}C_{r,r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{2}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{3}}(t).

(46)

The last condition, Eq. (
39
), imposes a restriction on the acceleration of the pumping parameter.
For simplicity, we will not discuss this condition and instead assume that the driving velocity is nearly constant (see Appendix
B
).

Combining these conditions, we obtain

\displaystyle v(t)\leq\mathrm{min}\left[v_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(2)}(t),v_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(3)}(t),\cdots\right].

(47)

One might guess that, as long as one considers slow driving, the strictest condition is imposed by a second-order correction, since the third- and higher-order corrections are much smaller than the second-order one in the adiabatic expansion.
Although this is true in most cases, there is an exception:
When the system is in equilibrium, that is, when all the reservoirs have the same temperature and chemical potential, the second-order correction is always zero for any driving of
\gamma_{r}(t)

(this exception will be discussed in the next subsection).
In this case, the velocity limit
v^{(2)}_{{\rm lim},r}(t)
becomes infinite, and there is no restriction on the pumping velocity.
Therefore, the third correction should be included in the estimate of the velocity limit.

3.4
Disappearance of the second-order correction

Comparing the charge transfer induced by a driving operation and its time-reversed operation, one can see that the
2n
-th-order correction disappears when the system is in equilibrium.
Let us consider a driving protocol
C
defined in the parameter space, such as,

\displaystyle C=\{(\gamma_{L}(t),\gamma_{R}(t))|t\in[0,T]\},

(48)

and its time-reversed protocol
\bar{C}
, such as,

\displaystyle\bar{C}=\{(\gamma_{L}(T-t),\gamma_{R}(T-t))|t\in[0,T]\}.

(49)

In general, the amount of charge transfer induced by
C
and
\bar{C}
can vary, when steady charge current flows between the reservoirs or an external magnetic field is applied.
This indicates that when the system is in equilibrium, that is, when the temperature and chemical potential are the same among all the reservoirs, there is no steady charge current and the amount of charge transfer induced by
C
and
\bar{C}
should satisfy the following relation:

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}[C]=-\delta Q_{r}[\bar{C}].

(50)

Here,
\delta Q[C]
denotes the amount of charge transfer under the driving protocol
C
.
Let us consider the adiabatic expansion of
\delta Q[C]
and
\delta Q[\bar{C}]
.

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}[C]=\sum_{n=0}\delta Q^{(n)}_{r}[C],

(51)

\displaystyle\delta Q_{r}[\bar{C}]=\sum_{n=0}\delta Q^{(n)}_{r}[\bar{C}]

(52)

Focusing on the first-order corrections (adiabatic pumping terms), they satisfy an anti-symmetric relation,

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(1)}_{r}[\bar{C}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\ \sum_{r_{1}}A_{r,r_{1}}(T-t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(T-t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}dt\ \sum_{r_{1}}A_{r,r_{1}}(t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\delta Q^{(1)}[C],

(53)

while the second-order corrections satisfy a symmetric relation,

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(2)}_{r}[\bar{C}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}B_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(T-t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(T-t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(T-t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}B_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)\frac{d\gamma_{r_{1}}(t)}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{r_{2}}(t)}{dt}=\delta Q^{(2)}_{r}[C].

(54)

In general, the
(2n)
-th-order corrections and
(2n+1)
-th-order corrections satisfy symmetric and anti-symmetric relations, respectively,

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(2n)}[C]

\displaystyle=\delta Q^{(2n)}[\bar{C}],

(55)

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(2n+1)}[C]

\displaystyle=-\delta Q^{(2n+1)}[\bar{C}].

(56)

Combining Eq. (
50
) and Eq. (
56
), we can conclude that the
(2n)
-th-order correction should be zero for any driving protocols when the system is in equilibrium.
In this case, the nonadiabatic correction starts with the third-order correction
\delta Q^{(3)}
, so the velocity limit is imposed by the third-order correction, not the second-order correction.

3.5
Typical velocity limit

As shown in Eqs. (
41
) and (
45
), the velocity limit may drop to zero under certain conditions, which is known as adiabaticity breakdown.
There are two cases in which the velocity limit is zero:
(i) the adiabatic pumping term (numerator of Eqs. (
41
) and (
45
)) is zero;
(ii) the nonadiabatic corrections (denominator of Eqs. (
41
) and (
45
)) are infinitely large.
The former case is not serious for adiabaticity, because the nonadiabatic corrections are finite and one can pass through such adiabaticity breaking points in finite velocity as long as the nonadiabatic corrections are negligible compared to the adiabatic pumping term in the whole cycle.
We refer to this case as ‘non-serious adiabaticity breakdown’.
On the other hand, the latter case is serious, because the nonadiabatic corrections diverge, so one should not pass through such points at any finite velocity.
We refer to this case as ‘serious adiabaticity breakdown’.

Unfortunately, it is difficult to distinguish serious and non-serious adiabaticity breakdown only by the velocity limit itself, and it is necessary to define an indicator that can distinguish them.
Here, we define a ‘typical’ velocity limit as such an indicator,

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(2/3)}=\int_{0}^{T}dt\ v_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(2/3)}(t)p^{(2/3)}(t),

(57)

where
p^{(2)}(t)
and
p^{(3)}(t)
are weight functions describing how large the nonadiabatic corrections are on the driving contour:

\displaystyle p^{(2)}(t)=\frac{\left|\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}B_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{2}}(t)\right|}{\int_{0}^{T}dt\left|\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}B_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{2}}(t)\right|},

(58)

\displaystyle p^{(3)}(t)=\frac{\left|\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}C_{r,r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{2}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{3}}(t)\right|}{\int_{0}^{T}dt\left|\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}C_{r,r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{2}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{3}}(t)\right|}.

(59)

The typical velocity limit becomes zero for the serious adiabaticity breakdown, while it remains finite for the non-serious adiabaticity breakdown.

5
Adiabaticity breakdown in band-edge system

When the system shows critical phenomena, for example, phase transition, transport changes drastically at the critical point and the adiabaticity easily breaks down in this regime.
This adiabaticity breakdown can be observed as a divergence of nonadiabatic corrections.
In this section, we examine this adiabaticity breakdown in a quantum dot system with the electron reservoirs that have a band edge.
There are two cases in which adiabaticity breaks down:
(i) the quantum dot is isolated from all the reservoirs,
\gamma_{L}=\gamma_{R}=0
, so adiabaticity trivially breaks down because the quantum dot never relaxes;
(ii) when the dot level is located near the band edge of the electron reservoirs, a bound state with an infinite lifetime appears in the quantum dot system and it violates adiabaticity.

To see the details, let us assume the reservois have the following density of states:

\displaystyle\rho(\omega)=\Theta(\omega)\rho_{0}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}\right)^{\alpha}\exp\left[-\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}\right]

(62)

Here, we assume that the band edge is at
\omega=0
and
\omega_{c}
and
\rho_{0}
are the cutoff frequency and amplitude of the density of states, respectively.
The bound state appears when the coupling constants between the dot and the reservoirs are larger than a critical value:
[
36
,
37
,
35
]

\displaystyle\pi\rho_{0}\sum_{r}|\gamma_{r}(t)|^{2}=\frac{\epsilon_{d}}{\Gamma(\alpha)},

(63)

where
\Gamma(\alpha)
is the Gamma function.
As a quantum state with an infinite lifetime emerges, the system cannot relax and any finite-velocity driving violates adiabaticity.
Such a violation would appear as a divergence of nonadiabatic corrections.

6
Summary

We studied nonadiabatic corrections in charge pumping and derived a formula for adiabatic criteria by employing a noninteracting electron model of a single-level quantum dot as a minimum model.
The upper velocity limit for the parameter for adiabatic pumping was derived from the
n
th-order term of the pumped charge, which is proportional to
T^{n-1}
where
T
is the period of the pumping cycle.
The leading nonadiabatic correction derived from the second term (
n=2
) imposes the strongest constraint in most cases.
However, the next leading correction derived from the third term (
n=3
) becomes important when the two reservoirs have the same electrochemical potential and the same temperature because the leading nonadiabatic correction vanishes in such a situation.
As a demonstration, we considered charge pumping induced by the time-dependent dot-reservoir coupling and calculated the velocity limit in the wide-band limit.
We found that the velocity limit indeed depends on the point in the parameter space; this feature is important for optimization to maximize the pumping power.
We also showed how the velocity limits imposed from the second- and third-order terms behave near thermal equilibrium.

To detect adiabatic breakdown, we defined a typical velocity limit by averaging the velocity limit on the parameter contour.
We showed that the typical velocity limit approaches zero
in the wide-band limit when the dot-reservoir coupling is weakened.
We also calculated the typical velocity limit for a quantum dot coupled to reservoirs with a band edge and showed that it approaches zero, corresponding to adiabatic breakdown caused by the appearance of a bound state in this model.

We expect that our findings will be helpful not only for accurate control of adiabatic pumping in quantum devices but also for the foundation of non-equilibrium statistics such as in the study of quantum heat engines.
While our work illustrates a general procedure for obtaining adiabatic criteria just in a simple model, it is a straightforward exercise to extend our formulation to more complex models.
An important future problem would be to extend our work to take the effect of Coulomb interactions between electrons into account.

Appendix B
Adiabatic condition for the acceleration

We define the acceleration of the driving parameters and its normalized value as

\displaystyle{\bm{a}}(t)=(a_{\rm L}(t),a_{\rm R}(t))=\biggl(\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{\rm L}(t)}{dt^{2}},\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{\rm R}(t)}{dt^{2}}\biggr),

(76)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{a}}(t)=(\tilde{a}_{\rm L}(t),\tilde{a}_{\rm L}(t))=\frac{{\bm{a}}(t)}{|{\bm{a}}|},

(77)

respectively.
The adiabatic condition, Eq. (
39
), can be rewritten as

\displaystyle|a_{r}(t)|\leq a_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(3)}(t),

(78)

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(3)}=\eta\frac{|A_{r}(t)|}{|D_{r}(t)|},

(79)

\displaystyle D_{r}(t)=\sum_{r_{1},r_{2}}D_{r,r_{1}r_{2}}(t)\tilde{a}_{r_{1}}(t)\tilde{v}_{r_{2}}(t).

(80)

Here
a_{\mathrm{lim},r}^{(3)}(t)
is the limit of the acceleration of the driving parameters.

In our work, we consider the driving contour shown in Fig.
2
with the protocol of constant velocity for simplicity, which leads to infinite acceleration at the corners of the driving contour.
This apparent singularity can be avoided by implicitly assuming a finite margin near the corners where the acceleration is finite.
For this finite acceleration, the corresponding nonadiabatic correction is considered to be much smaller than other corrections by the following reasons.
For rough estimate, we can assume that
D_{r,r_{1},r_{2}}(t)
is the same order with
C_{r,r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}(t)
unless it has a singularity.
Then, by the dimensional analysis, the nonadiabatic correction by finite acceleration becomes much smaller than that by the third corrections with respect to the velocity if the time scale of the parameter driving is longer than
\Gamma_{\rm min}^{-1}
(compare Eq. (
31
) with Eq. (
30
)).

The above discussion holds only when
D_{r,r_{1},r_{2}}(t)
has no singularity.
If it has a singularity such as the adiabaticity breakdown, the nonadiabatic correction by finite acceleration may be relevant.
However, in such a situation, the higher order corrections also become important.
The analysis for such a situation is beyond the scope of our work and is left as a future problem.
