Computing the Degenerate Ground Space of Gapped Spin Chains in Polynomial Time
Christopher T. Chubb and Steven T. Flammia

Abstract
Given a gapped Hamiltonian of a spin chain, we give a polynomial-time algorithm for finding the degenerate ground space projector. The output is an orthonormal set of matrix product states that approximate the true ground space projector up to an inverse polynomial error in any Schatten norm, with a runtime exponential in the degeneracy. Our algorithm is an extension of the recent algorithm of Landau, Vazirani, and Vidick [ 1 ] for the nondegenerate case, and it includes the recent improvements due to Huang [ 2 ] . The main new idea is to incorporate the local distinguishability of ground states on the half-chain to ensure that the algorithm returns a complete set of global ground states.



Strongly correlated quantum systems are at the heart of such diverse physical phenomena as frustrated magnets, high-temperature superconductors, and topological quantum phases, to name but a few, and understanding their low-temperature behaviour is a grand challenge at the interface of physics and computer science. The computational tools to study these systems numerically are however inevitably stymied by the exponential growth of the Hilbert space of an
n
-particle system.

Many numerical tools have been developed to surmount the computational difficulties surrounding the exponential growth of the state space. Tools like quantum Monte Carlo
[
3
,
4
]
, coupled cluster methods
[
5
]
, and the density-matrix renormalisation group (DMRG)
[
6
]
all exploit some underlying physical structure to perform heuristically efficient simulations of interacting quantum systems. However, like all heuristics they often lack basic theoretical guarantees except in certain special cases, and some of them have failure modes that are difficult to diagnose or characterise. This makes it hard to precisely determine which physical features enable efficient simulation and which features might be responsible in the cases where the heuristic fails.

Very recently, a success story has emerged in the rigorous understanding of gapped 1D spin chains. Hastings’ proof of the 1D area law
[
7
]
established that approximating the ground state of a gapped spin chain is in
\mathsf{NP}
. The proof technique crucially used the idea of matrix product states (MPS)
[
8
]
, an ansatz for quantum systems that exploits the area law structure of entanglement
[
9
]
. The first rigorous nontrivial algorithm for finding ground states, due independently to Schuch and Cirac
[
10
]
and Aharonov, Arad, and Irani
[
11
]
, used dynamic programming to find MPS ground states. The runtime of the algorithm was exponential in the bond dimension, and since Hastings’ result used a polynomial bond dimension to achieve a sufficiently accurate approximation, the runtime could only be guaranteed to be exponential for general gapped 1D systems. The first subexponential-time algorithm was found by Arad, Kitaev, Landau, and Vazirani
[
12
]
. They used the same algorithm as Refs.
[
10
,
11
]
, but showed that the ground state could be approximated to sufficient accuracy with an MPS of bond dimension
n^{o(1)}
, where
n
is the length of the chain. This line of research culminated in the breakthrough algorithm of Landau, Vazirani, and Vidick (LVV)
[
1
]
, who gave the first polynomial-time algorithm for approximating the ground state in the case of a non-degenerate ground space. This was subsequently improved by Huang
[
2
]
who gave a deterministic algorithm with an exponentially improved scaling with respect to the gap that also applied to highly frustrated systems.

In this paper, we extend the LVV algorithm and its subsequent improvements by Huang to the case where the Hamiltonian has a degenerate ground space. To give a precise statement of our results, we first introduce some notation.

Consider a 1D chain of
d
-level quantum systems (
qudits
) with
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
and a Hamiltonian
H
that is a sum of local terms on contiguous sites, and let
n
be the number of sites in the chain. After sufficient coarse-graining, shifting the energy, and dividing by the largest operator norm from the terms in the Hamiltonian, we can take the Hamiltonian to be of the form
H=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}H_{i}
, where
0\leq H_{i}\leq\mathbb{1}
and
H_{i}
acts non-trivially only on the
i
and
i+1
qudits. We additionally require that this transformed Hamiltonian has a constant lower bound
\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{0}\geq\epsilon\geq\Omega(1)
on the spectral gap, i.e. the difference between the first excited energy (
\epsilon_{1}
) and the ground state energy (
\epsilon_{0}
) of
H
is bounded from below by
\epsilon
independent of
n
. Our Hamiltonians will have a degenerate spectrum in the ground space with a degeneracy
g=\mathcal{O}(1)
. We say that such a Hamiltonian is in
standard form
, though this is not a unique normal form because of the ambiguity in the coarse-graining step.

Our main result is an approximation of the ground space in the form of an orthonormal basis of matrix product states that approximate the true ground space projector. More precisely, we have the following.

Theorem 1

(Main result)
.

Given an
n
-qudit Hamiltonian
H
in standard form and constant bounds
\epsilon
and
g
on the gap and degeneracy respectively, then for every sufficiently small approximation error

1/\eta=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, Algorithm
1
constructs the orthonormal approximate ground states
\{\left\lvert\gamma_{j}\right\rangle\}_{j}
in matrix product form for
1\leq j\leq g
in runtime

. The approximation error gives a bound in the Frobenius norm of

\bigl\lVert G-\Upsilon\bigr\rVert_{F}\leq\eta\,,

where

is the true ground space projector, and
\Upsilon:=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\left\lvert\gamma_{j}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\gamma_{j}\right\rvert
is the approximate ground space projector.

The proof of this statement is given in
Section

2.7
, and a more precise dependence of the runtime on the gap
\epsilon
and the ground space degeneracy
g
is made explicit in
Section

2.8
. Note that since both
G
and
\Upsilon
have rank
g
, we can get an approximation error in the trace norm at the cost of a factor of
\sqrt{2g}=\mathcal{O}(1)
increase in error.

Our result relies heavily on the ideas in LVV
[
1
]
and the improvements of Huang
[
2
]
, so let us recall briefly how this algorithm proceeds. The LVV algorithm proceeds by constructing a
viable set
, which roughly speaking is a small set of matrix product states on a half-chain that is guaranteed to contain the local Schmidt vectors of an approximate ground state on the full chain. In each step of the algorithm, the half-chain of the current viable set is extended by one site to create a new viable set. This initial extension is too large, but trimming away some states with too high an energy yields another suitably small set that is still viable.

The main new idea is an extension of the size trimming step to degenerate systems, based on the concept of
local distinguishability
of ground states. The algorithm works by iteratively sweeping across the chain, and finds the degenerate ground states one-by-one in a manner similar to that of LVV on each iteration. As we run these iterations, we must ensure that the viable set of potential ground states on the half-chain contains a witness that not only has low energy, but also does not simply reduce to something in the span of the previously found ground states. To ensure this, we break the analysis into two cases of
high-
and
low-
distinguishability.

In the low-distinguishability case, the Schmidt vectors on the left half-chain of the previously found ground states and the desired witness have large overlap. By recycling these Schmidt vectors, we therefore get a viable set for this new witness for free.

In the high-distinguishability case however, this recycling would not provide a viable set. In this case we need to trim the viable set in a way that not only ensures the existence of a low-energy witness, but a witness which has low overlap with the existing states. If the relevant Schmidt vectors of the desired witness and the existing states have low overlap, then we can optimise for a state of low energy
and
overlap efficiently.

2.3
Size trimming

For the size-trimming we are going to use two nets to compensate for our ignorance of two properties of the ground states: the local energy differences between them, and their entanglement structure across the
i|i+1
cut. The former is represented by the difference in the expectation value of
H_{L}
between our desired witness and (say) the first ground state found. The latter is represented by the boundary contraction (defined below) of the witness state in question. In both cases we are going to construct a net on the space of possible values of these unknowns, and perform the necessary optimisations in the neighbourhoods of these elements, thus allowing for error guarantees in spite of this ignorance.

2.3.1
Boundary contraction

Viable sets, like reduced density matrices, capture local information about a given state. As defined, viable sets are solely determined by the left Schmidt vectors of a witness state; the reduced density operator however also contains the Schmidt coefficients across the cut. As such, merely finding vectors of low energy on the half-chain
[1,i]
in no way guarantees consistency with a low-energy global state, as they might not be consistent with the entanglement structure across the
i|i+1
cut. As well as this the system may be frustrated, with a globally low-energy state appearing locally excited. Both of these problems are circumvented by performing the desired optimisations alongside
boundary contraction
constraints.

Given a state of Schmidt rank
B
and Schmidt decomposition across the
i|i+1
cut given by
\left\lvert v\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{B}\lambda_{j}\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rangle\left\lvert b_{j}\right\rangle
, let
U_{v}:\mathbb{C}^{B}\to\mathcal{H}_{R}
be the partial isometry specified by
U_{v}\left\lvert j\right\rangle=\left\lvert b_{j}\right\rangle
. Then we have the following.

Definition 4

(Boundary Contraction
[
1
]
)
.

The
left state
of
\left\lvert v\right\rangle
with respect to the
i|i+1
cut is defined as

\left\lvert\ls(v)\right\rangle:=U_{v}^{\dagger}\left\lvert v\right\rangle=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rangle\left\lvert j\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{L}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{B}\,.

The
boundary contraction
of
\left\lvert v\right\rangle
is defined as

\cont(v):=\Tr_{[1,i-1]}\left(\left\lvert\ls(v)\middle\rangle\middle\langle\ls(v)\right\rvert\right)=U_{v}^{\dagger}\Tr_{[1,i-1]}\left(\left\lvert v\middle\rangle\middle\langle v\right\rvert\right)U_{v}\,.

Note that the density matrix
\cont(v)
is supported on
\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{B}
.

The interpretation of a boundary contraction is the following. The difference in right energies between a candidate state and a valid witness state for the full chain is bounded by how close the boundary contractions of the states are. By extending a state by the as-yet-unknown witness state, we can argue the existence of a low energy solution within our extended viable set.

(

Lemma 13

(Overlap results
[
1

]

•

For any states for which
\left|\left\langle v\middle|w\right\rangle\right|\geq 1-\delta

\left|\left\langle v\middle|v^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|\geq 1-2(\delta+\delta^{\prime})

•

For any two states such that
\left|\left\langle v\middle|w\right\rangle\right|\geq 1-\delta

\left|\left\langle v\middle|O\middle|v\right\rangle-\left\langle w\middle|O\middle|w\right\rangle\right|\leq 2\sqrt{2\delta}
for any operator

\left\|O\right\|\leq 1

•

If
\left|\left\langle u\middle|\Gamma\right\rangle\right|\leq\omega
and
\left|\left\langle v\middle|u\right\rangle\right|\geq 1-\delta
then
\left|\left\langle v\middle|\Gamma\right\rangle\right|\leq\omega+\sqrt{2\delta}
.

•

For a pair of vectors with bounded overlap
\left|\left\langle u_{1}\middle|u_{2}\right\rangle\right|\leq\omega
, and a second pair
\left\lvert v_{1}\right\rangle
,
\left\lvert v_{2}\right\rangle
such that
\left|\left\langle u_{j}\middle|v_{j}\right\rangle\right|\geq 1-\delta
for

, then we have
\left|\left\langle v_{1}\middle|v_{2}\right\rangle\right|\leq\omega+\sqrt{10\delta}

Lemma 14

(Orthogonalising lemma)
.

Suppose you have two vectors
\left\lvert u\right\rangle
and
\left\lvert v\right\rangle
, such that
\left\langle u\middle|H\middle|u\right\rangle,\left\langle v\middle|H\middle|v\right\rangle\leq\epsilon_{0}+\Delta\epsilon
, with bounded overlap
\left|\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle\right|\leq\beta
. Then there exist orthogonal vectors
\left\lvert u^{\prime}\right\rangle,\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle\in\Span\{\left\lvert u\right\rangle,\left\lvert v\right\rangle\}
, such that
\left\langle u^{\prime}\middle|H\middle|u^{\prime}\right\rangle,\left\langle v^{\prime}\middle|H\middle|v^{\prime}\right\rangle\leq\epsilon_{0}+\Delta\epsilon(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)
.

Proof.

The proof is constructive, take the two vectors to be

\left\lvert u^{\prime}\right\rangle=\left\lvert u\right\rangle\,,\qquad\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle=\frac{\left\lvert v\right\rangle-\left\lvert u\right\rangle\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle}{\left\|\left\lvert v\right\rangle-\left\lvert u\right\rangle\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle\right\|}\,.

As
\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle\propto(I-\left\lvert u\middle\rangle\middle\langle u\right\rvert)\left\lvert v\right\rangle
this vector is clearly orthogonal to
\left\lvert u^{\prime}\right\rangle
. As for the energy bound, define
H^{\prime}:=H-\epsilon_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\left\langle v^{\prime}\middle|H^{\prime}\middle|v^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle v\middle|H^{\prime}\middle|v\right\rangle-2\Re\left\langle v\middle|H^{\prime}\middle|u\right\rangle\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle+\left\langle v\middle|u\right\rangle\left\langle u\middle|H^{\prime}\middle|u\right\rangle\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle}{\left\|\left\lvert v\right\rangle-\left\lvert u\right\rangle\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\left|\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle\right|}{1-\left|\left\langle u\middle|v\right\rangle\right|}\Delta\epsilon\leq\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\Delta\epsilon

After reintroducing the
\epsilon_{0}
term in
H
we get the final energy bound.
∎

If we apply the same argument to the projector orthogonal to the ground space, the same
(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)
growth can also be shown to occur in the overlap error. The Schmidt rank of the resulting orthogonalised vectors will in general be the sum of the two original vectors’ Schmidt ranks. If we can bound the overlap between a vector and
any
member of a vector space, then that vector can be similarly orthogonalised with respect to that whole space at the same cost.

As expectation values are bilinear in pure states, the optimisations in the
Trim

ApproxGroundState
are both performed over mixed states such that their objective functions are linear. To aid in the analysis of these optimisations, we will next show that the leading eigenvector of a low energy mixed state must be a low energy pure state.

Lemma 15

(Demixing)
.

If a mixed state
\sigma
has an energy at most
\epsilon_{0}+\Delta\epsilon/(2g+1)
where
\Delta\leq 1/3g
, then the leading eigenvector has energy at most
\epsilon_{0}+\Delta\epsilon

Proof.

First decompose the mixed state as
\sigma=\sum_{k>0}\lambda_{k}\left\lvert v_{k}\middle\rangle\middle\langle v_{k}\right\rvert
, where
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
. By the normalisation of
\sigma_{k}
we have
\sum\lambda_{k}=1
. Next define the set of indices
K
as

K=\left\{k\,\middle|\,\left\langle v_{k}\middle|H\middle|v_{k}\right\rangle\leq\epsilon_{0}+\Delta\epsilon\right\}\,.

By comparing the energy bound of the mixed state as a whole as well as those on indices in
K
, we get

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}+\Delta\epsilon/(2g+1)

\displaystyle\geq\Tr(\sigma H)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K}\lambda_{k}\left\langle v_{k}\middle|H\middle|v_{k}\right\rangle+\sum_{k\notin K}\lambda_{k}\left\langle v_{k}\middle|H\middle|v_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon_{0}+\Delta\epsilon\sum_{k\notin K}\lambda_{k}

which in turn gives
\sum_{k\in K}\lambda_{k}\geq 2g/(2g+1)
, analogous to a Markov inequality. As we required
\Delta\leq 1/3g
, each vector corresponding to a index in
K

\epsilon_{0}+\epsilon/3g
, and thus a ground space overlap at least

\left|K\right|\leq g
. Using this we can bound the largest eigenvalue corresponding to an index in

\displaystyle\max_{k\in K}\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\underset{k\in K}{\mathrm{mean}}\lambda_{k}=\frac{\sum_{k\in K}\lambda_{k}}{\left|K\right|}\geq\frac{2}{2g+1}

as well as that
not
in
K

\displaystyle\max_{k\notin K}\lambda_{k}

Lemma 16

3.2.2
Distinguishability

Viable sets capture local information about a witness state, specifically the Schmidt vectors. If two states are globally orthogonal, this can mean that their Schmidt vectors span orthogonal spaces, but they can also span identical regions. Indeed global orthogonality of witness states cannot be locally imposed for this reason.

When considering viable sets, a natural notion of distinguishability is the overlap of the desired witness state, and the Schmidt vectors of the already constructed states. If we take
\left\lvert w\right\rangle
to be a witness state which we wish to capture,
L_{h,i}
to be the set of Schmidt vectors on
[1,i]
of the
h
already constructed ground states, and
\Pi_{h,i}
the projector onto
\Span(L_{h,i})
, then the distinguishability is defined as

\displaystyle D_{h,i}

\displaystyle:=1-\left\langle w\middle|\Pi_{h,i}\middle|w\right\rangle.

From here we will omit the subscripts on
L
,
\Pi
, and
D
, since they will be clear from context. If we think of
\Pi
as being the projector onto the vectors on
[1,i]
which we already have, then
D
can be considered a measure of how much of the state is unaccounted for.

Clearly if
D
is low, then a viable set can be constructed entirely by recycling existing Schmidt vectors. The precise bounds for the quality of viable set constructed in this manner, as a function of
D
, are given in
20
.

In the case of a high
D
new vectors need to be found. In this case we know there is little overlap between the Schmidt vectors of our new witness and
\Span(L)
. Our procedure works by restricting to a low-energy subspace, and projecting away from the existing vectors, by minimising the expectation value of
\Pi
. In
21
we will show that this procedure must produce a low energy state. Given that our witness is highly distinguishable,
22
will show that this new witness after size trimming must also be highly distinguishable. This will culminate in
23
where we show that this therefore implies that the new witness has small overlap with the previous ground states, producing a good viable set.

We will conclude this subsection by showing in
24
that by performing both the low- and high-distinguishability procedures and combining the results, we can upper bound the error of our viable set in a distinguishability-independent manner.

Conclusion

We have shown the ground state approximation of Refs.
[
1
,
2
]
can be extended to degenerate systems with an approximation of the ground space projector that is up to inverse polynomial error in any Schatten norm.

AGSPs have proven powerful tools for relating the structural properties of gapped systems to various computational complexity bounds regarding them. Most AGSP constructions are either functions of the Hamiltonians
[
12
,
14
,
2
]
or parts of the Hamiltonian terms
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
1
]
. While we utilise such AGSPs in intermediate steps, our algorithm outputs a state-based AGSP, and more thoroughly exploits the underlying one-dimensional structure, allowing for a more powerful overall construction. While previous AGSP constructions have typically been full-rank, our AGSP takes the form of a minimal-rank projector, that moreover has a polynomially bounded bond dimension as a matrix product operator (MPO). It remains an open question for which systems there exist efficiently constructible AGSPs that are also
low-rank
AGSPs. Note that this is important for applications because while AGSPs that are close to the ground space in operator norm are easy to construct even with small bond dimension in MPO form, they do not generally allow for low-error estimation of expectation values of other MPO observables.

While running in polynomial-time, the specific runtime scaling of the above algorithm is highly suboptimal, leaving wide room for further optimisation. One potential avenue for optimisation is the level of redundancy within the viable sets that are generated. While there are rigorous upper bounds on the cardinality of these sets, no care has been taken to explore to possibility of high levels of linear dependence, opening the door to far better practical runtimes than our analysis would suggest.

Appendix A
Overlap lemma

In this appendix we want to relax the notion of a basis, and show that key properties thereof are robust to a small amount of allowed error.

Suppose we have some
g
-dimensional space, represented by a
g
-rank projector
G
onto this space. Take
\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rangle
for
1\leq i\leq g
to be an orthonormal set of vectors, each with
\left\langle v_{i}\middle|G\middle|v_{i}\right\rangle\geq 1-\delta/g
. First we will prove that all members of
S:=\Span\{\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rangle\}
have non-trivial overlap with
G
. We will then use this to show that the vectors
G\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rangle
span the space. Finally we will show that any vector orthogonal to
\Span\{\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rangle\}
has low overlap with the space.

Lemma A.1
.

Any vector
\left\lvert v\right\rangle\in S
has
\left\langle v\middle|G\middle|v\right\rangle\geq 1-\delta
.

Proof.

As
\left\lvert v\right\rangle
lies in
S
, we can write it as a linear combination of the form

\left\lvert v\right\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{g}c_{i}\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rangle\,.

Next we can bound the expectation value of the complementary projector
I-G
by using the triangle and Cauchy-Schwartz inequalities.

\displaystyle\left\langle v\middle|(I-G)\middle|v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}c_{i}^{*}c_{j}\left\langle v_{i}\middle|(I-G)\middle|v_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\left|c_{i}\right|\left|c_{j}\right|\left|\left\langle v_{i}\middle|(I-G)\middle|v_{j}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left|c_{i}\right|\left\|(I-G)\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rangle\right\|\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta

where we have used the normalisation
\sum_{i}\left|c_{i}\right|^{2}=1
and the resulting bound
\sum_{i}\left|c_{i}\right|\leq\sqrt{g}
. This in turn gives
\left\langle v\middle|G\middle|v\right\rangle\geq 1-\delta
.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Basis under projection)
.

The projections
G\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rangle
form a basis of
\mathrm{im}(G)
.

Proof.

As the number of vectors matches the dimension of the target space, proving linear independence is sufficient to prove the formation of a basis. If the projected vectors were linearly dependent then this would imply there exist a non-zero
\left\lvert v\right\rangle\in S
such that
G\left\lvert v\right\rangle=0
.
A.1
however gives
\left\langle v\middle|G\middle|v\right\rangle\geq 1-\delta\geq 0
for all such vectors, thus proving linear independence by contradiction.
∎

Next we want to generalise the concept of a
full
basis, specifically the fact that any vector perpendicular to a full basis is necessarily perpendicular to the entire space, meaning that there is a natural maximum to the size of any given basis.

Lemma A.3

(Fullness)
.

Any vector
\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle
which is orthogonal to
S
has
\left\langle v^{\prime}\middle|G\middle|v^{\prime}\right\rangle\leq\delta
.

Proof.

As the projection of this space is spanning, we can pick
\left\lvert v\right\rangle\in S
such that it projects into the same vector as
\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle
, i.e.
G\left\lvert v\right\rangle\propto G\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle
. As such we can decompose both states as

\displaystyle\left\lvert v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda}\left\lvert g\right\rangle+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\left\lvert h\right\rangle

\displaystyle\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\left\lvert g\right\rangle+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{\prime}}\left\lvert h^{\prime}\right\rangle

where
\left\lvert g\right\rangle\in\mathrm{im}(G)
and
\left\lvert h\right\rangle,\left\lvert h^{\prime}\right\rangle\in\mathrm{ker}(G)
. The orthogonality of
\left\lvert v\right\rangle
and
\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rangle
gives

0=\left\langle v\middle|v^{\prime}\right\rangle=\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{\prime}}\left\langle h\middle|h^{\prime}\right\rangle\,.

Using
\left|\left\langle h\middle|h^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|\leq 1
in turn gives
\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}\leq 1
.
A.1
gives that
\lambda\geq 1-\delta
, leading us to conclude that
\lambda^{\prime}\leq\delta
, and thus that
\left\langle v^{\prime}\middle|G\middle|v^{\prime}\right\rangle\leq\delta
generically for any vector orthogonal to
S
.
∎

Appendix B
Approximate AGSP construction

In this section we explicitly lay out the various errors associated with approximating the AGSP defined in Sections
2.5
and
3.4
. The scaling of these errors was stated in less detail in Ref.
[
2
]
. First we consider the AGSP itself

\displaystyle A:=\exp\left[-\frac{x(H-\epsilon_{0}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\right]=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}t^{2}}{2x}-i(H-\epsilon_{0}^{\prime})t\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

where
\epsilon_{0}^{\prime}
is the ground energy approximation given by
\left\lvert\gamma_{1}\right\rangle
. If we take
\eta,\zeta\leq 1/2
and assume that
\zeta\geq\eta^{2}/4f
then

\left|\epsilon_{0}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{0}\right|\leq\frac{\eta^{2}\epsilon}{4f}\leq\epsilon\zeta\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,.

The magnitude of this AGSP on the excited subspace, known as the
shrinking factor
, can be bounded

\left\|A\left\lvert\Gamma^{\perp}\right\rangle\right\|\leq\exp\left[-\frac{x(\epsilon/2)^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\right]=e^{-x/8}\,.

If we define
{x:=33-8\log\zeta}
, then this shrinking factor is at most
\zeta/2
. Further assuming that
\zeta\leq 1/25
, the magnitude of
A
on the ground space can be lower bounded

\displaystyle\left\|A\left\lvert\Gamma\right\rangle\right\|

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{x(\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{0}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{19}{20}\,.

Next we move on to the approximate AGSP, defined by

\displaystyle K:=\frac{2\epsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil T/\tau\rceil}\exp\left[i\epsilon_{0}^{\prime}\tau j-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}j^{2}}{2x}\right]U_{D}(\tau j)\,.

where
T
,
\tau
and
D
are parameters and
U_{D}
is some unitary operator all of which we shall define below. This definition relies on taking the Fourier decomposition of
A
, and approximating this by truncating and discretising the integral and approximating the propagators by a known algorithm
[
13
]
.

B.1
Time Truncation Error

The truncation error is due to the finite range of the integral, controlled by the parameter
T
. If we let
\delta_{T}
denote the truncation error (in the operator norm) then it can be bounded

\displaystyle\delta_{T}

\displaystyle=2\left\|\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\int_{T}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}t^{2}}{2x}-iHt\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\int_{T}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}t^{2}}{2x}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2x}}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{\epsilon T}{\sqrt{2x}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}T^{2}}{2x}\right]\,,

where the last inequality holds for sufficiently large
T
, e.g.
T\geq 1
. For the time-scale

\displaystyle T:=\frac{\sqrt{2x}}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\log 3/\zeta^{\prime}}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\zeta)\sqrt{\log(n/\zeta)}\bigr)

we can in turn upper bound
\delta_{T}
by
\delta_{T}\leq\zeta^{\prime}/3=\Omega(\zeta/n)
, where
\zeta^{\prime}
is defined in
Eq.

12
.

B.2
Discretisation Error

Next we consider the discretisation error associated with approximating this integral with a Riemann sum. The term being approximately integrated is a Gaussian centred on the origin and so its absolute value is even as a function of
t
, allowing us to consider only the
t>0
error and doubling it. On this range the absolute value is not only even, but monotonically decreasing. As such the concepts of left and right Riemann sums correspond to upper and lower Riemann sums. If we let the discretisation error be
\delta_{D}
then

\displaystyle\delta_{D}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil T/\tau\rceil}\tau\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}j^{2}}{2x}-iH\tau j\right]-\int_{0}^{T}\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}t^{2}}{2x}-iHt\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|\,.

This error can be upper bounded in turn by the difference between upper and lower Riemann sums which is equal to the left and right Riemann sums owing to the monotonicity of the integrand.

\displaystyle\delta_{D}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\epsilon\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil T/\tau\rceil}\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}j^{2}}{2x}-iH\tau j\right]-\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil T/\tau\rceil+1}\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}j^{2}}{2x}-iH\tau j\right]\right\|\,.

The difference between these two summations is telescopic. As such it equals the difference between the highest and lowest terms.

\displaystyle\delta_{D}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\epsilon\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\left\|1-\exp\left[-\frac{\epsilon^{2}T^{2}}{2x}-iHT\right]\right\|\,.

Using the trivial bound on the real exponential term, we get

\displaystyle\delta_{D}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\epsilon\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\left\|1-\exp\big[-iHT\big]\Big.\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{\epsilon\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\,.

Again this error can also be bounded by
\delta_{D}\leq\zeta^{\prime}/3
if we take the time-step

\displaystyle\tau^{-1}:=\frac{12}{\zeta^{\prime}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(3/\zeta^{\prime})}{\pi}}=\frac{12\epsilon}{\zeta^{\prime}\sqrt{2\pi x}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n}{\zeta\sqrt{\log 1/\zeta}}\right)\,.

B.3
Bond Truncation Error

To upper bound the total truncation error for the entire approximation by
\zeta^{\prime}/3
, it is sufficient to bound the truncation error for each propagator
\exp(-iH\tau j)
by
\zeta^{\prime}/3
since the total sum is normalized. Using the construction of Ref.
[
13
]
this error can be achieved if the bond dimension of each propagator is

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\exp{\left[\mathcal{O}\left(\tau j\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log(n^{2}/\zeta^{\prime})\right)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[\mathcal{O}\left(T\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log n/\zeta\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2^{\mathcal{O}(T)}{\mathsf{poly}}(n/\zeta)

\displaystyle=2^{\mathcal{O}\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\zeta)\sqrt{\log(n/\zeta)}\bigr)}{\mathsf{poly}}(n/\zeta)\,.

As bond dimension is additive, and there are
2T/(\tau+1)
terms, the total bond dimension for the AGSP
B
is

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\leq 2T\tau^{-1}D

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\zeta)\sqrt{\log(n/\zeta)}\bigr)\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n}{\zeta\sqrt{\log 1/\zeta}}\right)2^{\mathcal{O}\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\zeta)\sqrt{\log(n/\zeta)}\bigr)}{\mathsf{poly}}(n/\zeta)

\displaystyle=2^{\mathcal{O}\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\zeta)\sqrt{\log(n/\zeta)}\bigr)}{\mathsf{poly}}(n/\zeta)

\displaystyle=n^{\mathcal{O}(1+\epsilon^{-1}\sqrt{\log(1/\zeta)/\log(n)})}

where we have assumed
\zeta^{-1}=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Appendix C
Frustration bound

Consider a 1D system with nearest neighbour interactions, were each local Hamiltonian term
H_{i}

0\leq H_{i}\leq\mathbb{1}
. For a cut
i|i+1

H_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}H_{j}

H_{M}=H_{i}

H_{R}=\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}H_{j}

.

For a Hamiltonian normalised as above, the left-energies relative to any cut for any two states with an energy less than
\Delta E
over the ground, differ by no more than
1+\Delta E
