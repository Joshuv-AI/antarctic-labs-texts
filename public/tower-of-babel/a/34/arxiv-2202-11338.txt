Towards a general framework of Randomized Benchmarking incorporating non-Markovian Noise
Pedro Figueroa-Romero, K. Modi, Min-Hsiu Hsieh

Abstract
The rapid progress in the development of quantum devices is in large part due to the availability of a wide range of characterization techniques allowing to probe, test and adjust them. Nevertheless, these methods often make use of approximations that hold in rather simplistic circumstances. In particular, assuming that error mechanisms stay constant in time and have no dependence in the past, is something that will be impossible to do as quantum processors continue scaling up in depth and size. We establish a theoretical framework for the Randomized Benchmarking (RB) protocol encompassing temporally-correlated, so-called non-Markovian noise, at the gate level, for any gate set belonging to a wide class of finite groups. We obtain a general expression for the Average Sequence Fidelity (ASF) and propose a way to obtain average fidelities of full non-Markovian noise processes. Moreover, we obtain conditions that are fulfilled when an ASF displays authentic non-Markovian deviations. Finally, we show that even though gate-dependence does not translate into a perturbative term within the ASF , as in the Markovian case, the non-Markovian sequence fidelity nevertheless remains stable under small gate-dependent perturbations.

2.2
Non-Markovian Quantum Processes

Non-Markovianity generally refers to a dependence of subsequent outcomes on previous ones, and in the context of
RB
, it implies that the noise at a given step is temporally correlated with the noise that preceded it and that the data outputs cannot be obtained by modeling noise as local quantum channels. The functional form of the
ASF
for unitary 2-designs in the non-Markovian regime is not that of a decaying exponential in sequence length anymore, but rather that of a non-trivial function of the memory within the noise
[
29
]
: this makes the extraction of operationally meaningful figures of merit a more elaborated task than in the Markovian case, despite the simplicity of the
RB
protocol. Nevertheless, the mere fact that no physical system can be completely isolated from its surroundings requires considering the presence of temporal correlations.

Classically, non-Markovianity can be described by a stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}
where information is being sent between timesteps such that the state of the system is conditionally dependent on the past, i.e.

\mathds{P}(x_{k}|x_{k-1},\ldots,x_{0})=\mathds{P}(x_{k}|x_{k-1},\ldots,x_{k-\ell}),

(2)

for any integers
0\leq{\ell}\leq{k}
and sequences of event outcomes
x_{i}
, with
\mathds{P}(\cdot|\cdot)
denoting a conditional probability. In particular, when
\ell=1
, the process is called
Markovian
and when
\ell=0
it is called
random
; otherwise, the process is non-Markovian with Markov order
\ell
. The fact that the complexity in describing non-Markovian processes increases exponentially in increasing Markov-order can be seen from joint probabilities requiring up to
\ell
-point correlations within the respective conditional probabilities.

4
Average Process Fidelity

The main advantage of

5
Signature of non-Markovian noise in Randomized Benchmarking

A first signature of non-Markovianity in a
RB
experiment is the display of deviations from an exponential decay in the
ASF

[
29
]
. However, deviations might not be evident or significant, either statistically after short sequence lengths, or fundamentally due to the noise itself, making the experiment blind to non-Markovianity, or the noise might be time-dependent but Markovian, not displaying multi-time correlations but rather some arbitrary temporal dependence changing the exponential rates of decay of the
ASF
data. Similarly, gate-dependence generally generates deviations from an exponential
[
34
,
39
]
. Anyhow, using the framework established before, we may look for unique signatures of non-Markovianity.

In
[
39
]
it is pointed out that, in the context of
RB
,
if some of the
(quality)
parameters are observed to be greater than 1, the experimental noise must be non-Markovian
. Wallman and Flammia notice that the decay of the
ASF
for time-dependent Markovian noise is given by products of time-dependent quality parameters, so that these can only change the rate of decay, as all of them are upper-bounded by unity. Having an increase within the
ASF
in increasing sequence length thus points to non-Markovianity. As can be seen with the analysis presented above, the same will hold for
RB
with the finite group
\mathds{G}
, with the decay specified by
f_{(1),\pi}\cdots{f}_{(m),\pi}
, and with each quality factor satisfying
f_{(i),\pi}=\tr(\hat{\Lambda}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})/\tr(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\leq 1
for all
\pi
, due to
\Lambda
being trace non-increasing, or in particular trace-preserving.

That is, in general having
\mathcal{F}_{n}>\mathcal{F}_{m}
for any
n>m
must imply a non-Markovian effect, as this cannot be explained within the Markovian framework
5
5

5

The case of gate-dependence, discussed in the following section, can be regarded as a form of non-Makovianity.
. Indeed, in the following lemma we give sufficient and necessary conditions, which can only be satisfied in a non-Markovian framework, for the condition

In particular, consider time-independent noise,

While Markovian time-dependence can be obtained via a superfluous environment getting discarded between steps, non-Markovianity implies a time-dependence such that an environment correlates timesteps with each other. In particular, with Markovian time-dependence, one can estimate average gate fidelities over arbitrary sequence length intervals, as also shown in

Understanding such unique non-Markovian deviations more deeply could be relevant to further be able to use this to one’s own advantage, either for control, mitigation or otherwise, of the noise in question. Up to this point, however, we have assumed gate-independence for the noise: a remaining question is thus, what the impact of gate-dependence, and/or context errors, is when considered together with non-Markovianity?

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{m+1:1}+\hat{\Delta}_{m+1:1}+\kern-25.00003pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\ell_{i}=0\}\\[2.04861pt]
\setminus\{\text{all}\,\ell_{i}=0\,\text{or}\,\text{all}\,\ell_{i}=1\}\end{subarray}}^{1}\kern-25.00003pt\left(\prod_{i=m+1}^{1}\!\!\!\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{i}^{\ell_{i}}\hat{\Delta}_{i}^{1-\ell_{i}}\!\right),

(33)

where here
\Delta_{i}=\Delta_{g_{i}}
, and where where the second line follows from the (multi) binomial theorem. Of particular relevance is the rightmost term in the last line in Eq. (
33
), which mixes
\mathcal{J}
and
\Delta
terms: the result in
[
33
]
(and generalization in
[
32
]
) imply that for Markovian noise, such mixed terms do not contribute to the
ASF
, and that the term
\Delta_{m+1:1}
gives a contribution that vanishes exponentially in sequence length.

Generalizing this result with the group
\mathds{G}
, as in
[
32
]
, but when the noise is non-Markovian, would require for there to be
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
satisfying the properties

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g},

(34a)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathcal{R}}\,\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g}\hat{\mathcal{R}},

(34b)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g},

(34c)

where
\mathds{E}
here again is uniform averaging over the group
\mathbb{G}
, and with

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g}:=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi},

(35)

for some (length-1) quality map
\mathscr{Q}_{\pi}
, and where
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}
is a projector onto the irreducible subspace defined by the representation
\phi_{\pi}
of
\mathcal{G}
as in Eq. (
10
).

Following
[
32
]
, we start by plugging the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{g}
in Eq. (
34a
) and Eq. (
34b
), together with the multiplicity-free decomposition in Eq. (
10
), so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\mathds{E}\!\left[\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger}\right]\!

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\!\left(\!\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\!\otimes\!\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\!\right),

(36a)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\mathds{E}\!\left[\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{R}}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right]\!

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{\pi}\!\left(\!\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\!\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\!\right)\!\hat{\mathcal{R}}.

(36b)

Now both
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}
and
\phi(g)
act solely on
\mathsf{S}
, so we now take without loss of generality,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}:=\sum_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi},\quad\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\right)=\delta_{\pi\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi},

(37a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}:=\sum_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi},\quad\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\right)\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}=\delta_{\pi\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi},

(37b)

both for all
\lambda
, and hence, writing corresponding identities explicitly, we have the equations

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\left(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\right),

(38a)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger}\right)\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\right)\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi},

(38b)

and we can vectorize both sides
7
7

7

By means of
{\mathrm{vec}(AXB)=\left(A\otimes{B}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\mathrm{vec}(X)}
; this follows from the definition we employ here,
\mathrm{vec}\left(|i\rangle\!\langle{j}|\right):=|ij\rangle

, and reorder spaces to get

\displaystyle\llangle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}|\left\{\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathds{E}[(\phi_{\pi}(g)^{*}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)})^{\mathrm{T}}]\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt=\llangle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}|(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{S}}),

(39a)

\displaystyle\left\{\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathds{E}[(\phi_{\pi}(g)^{*}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)})^{\mathrm{T}}]\right\}|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\rrangle

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt=(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{S}})|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\rrangle.

At this point, for Markovian noise, the quality map

is just a quality factor
f_{\pi}
, thus giving right and left eigenvalue equations for the operator

\mathcal{R}

\mathsf{E}

39
) make manifest that average gate-dependent errors will get carried within
\mathsf{E}
through quality maps.

We may notice, however, that if the gate-dependence is small in the sense of the physical implementation being given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon,g)}:=\Lambda\circ\mathcal{G}+\epsilon\Delta_{g},

(40)

for an

\|\hat{\Delta}_{g}\|\leq 1
, then the
ASF
and its variance remain close to the unperturbed ones. This can be done as in
[
39
]
, as shown in Appendix
E.2
, so that
|\mathcal{F}_{m}-\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(\epsilon,g)}|\leq\delta_{\mathcal{F}}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(\epsilon,g)}
is the
ASF
corresponding to a sequence of perturbed gates
\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon,g)}_{m+1:1}
, whenever

\displaystyle\epsilon\lesssim\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{F}}}{(m+1)d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}},

(41)

where in particular, with non-Markovian noise, the scaling with increasing
d_{\mathsf{E}}
also requires a smaller
\epsilon
, in a sense also pointing out the relevance of the environment for general, not necessarily small, gate-dependence. For the variance, similarly, if for some
\delta_{\mathcal{V}}>0
,

\displaystyle\epsilon\lesssim\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{V}}}{4(m+1)d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}},

(42)

then this implies
|\mathcal{V}_{m}-\mathcal{V}_{m}^{(\epsilon,g)}|\leq\delta_{\mathcal{V}}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{m}
and
\mathcal{V}_{m}^{(g)}
are variances of the unperturbed and perturbed sequence fidelities, respectively. This bound would thus require a perturbation a quarter times smaller for a change in variance of the same magnitude as a change in average of the sequence fidelity. Both bounds are almost the same as in
[
39
]
, with the extra
d_{\mathsf{E}}
factor being a consequence of the noise acting on the whole
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
; the fact that
RB
is done only on subsystem
\mathsf{S}
is of no consequence, as pointed out in Appendix
E.2
.

The main upshot is that both gate-dependence or more general contextual effects, together with non-Markovian noise, in
RB
and almost surely in any other technique, would require a further dealing with the environment. One possible way forward could be to study non-Markovian gate-dependent
RB
with Fourier analysis, similar to
[
40
]
; this has the potential to be not only more compact and simple, but also to allow for more generality and to obtain deeper consequences.

7
Conclusions

We have established a
Randomized Benchmarking (RB)
framework for non-Markovian noise with gate sets forming a finite group which admits a multiplicity-free representation. Despite this being a more general and abstract case than that of unitary 2-designs
[
29
]
, it renders a much clearer functional form of the
Average Sequence Fidelity (ASF)
as described in terms of quality maps, which we identify as the objects carrying average noise through the environment that mediates temporal correlations. Quality maps naturally generalize the concept of quality parameters
[
32
]
, from Markovian to non-Markovian
RB
, as the central quantities capturing average noise rates within the
ASF
. The main difference between quality maps and quality factors, is that the first capture all temporal correlations within a
RB
experiment; as such, all information to be known about the intrinsic noise is carried through these, with all remaining quantities being experimental choices for the benchmarked gates, initial state and measurement.

The main obstacle to operationally extract average error rates from quality maps can be tracked back to the environment. Furthermore, the mere fact that prepared initial states and measurements can give rise to correlated
State Preparation and Measurement (SPAM)
errors, appears as a downside to employing the
RB
technique under this type of noise. Nevertheless, we have provided a means to bridge this gap and obtain an operational estimate of gate fidelities for full non-Markovian
RB
processes by simply extending the
RB
protocol to include a coherent averaging over initial states and measurements. This effectively removes the environmental functional dependence and renders a single figure of merit as an average fidelity of the full
RB
process, including the correlated
SPAM
. Further estimates of average noise of gates alone can then potentially be made through Character
RB

[
32
]
or otherwise.

In non-Markovian
RB
, given that noise rates at a given timestep will depend on all previous ones, gate fidelities for single gates make sense only when relative to a whole noise process. This prompts pointing out that purely non-Markovian behavior in
RB
can be detected, and accounted for as above, whenever fidelities are seen to increase in subsequent timesteps. We obtained conditions under which these deviations can be observed, and in particular we highlight that it is necessary for the noise at a given step to increase the purity of the full
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
state at such step by over
d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}
, together with having an environment with a size over that of the system,
d_{\mathsf{E}}>d_{\mathsf{S}}
, to witness such increase in a subsequent step.

Finally, arguably one of the major hurdles to be cleared in any benchmarking or characterization technique, is incorporating non-Markovianity together with gate-dependence or a more general context-dependence. We noticed that, while the results of
[
33
,
32
]
, showing that gate-dependence introduces a single perturbative term in the
ASF
(itself decaying exponentially in sequence length) do not extend trivially to the non-Markovian case, the stability bound of
[
39
]
on the variance of the sequence fidelity still holds in the non-Markovian case when incorporating the size of the environment, and similarly for the
ASF
.

An overall highlight in our analysis is that the role of the environment in non-Markovian
RB
is to carry temporal correlations within the noise, as well as gate-dependence and potentially more general context dependence, through quality maps: further studying these quantities could give relevant insights into non-Markovian error mitigation or other advantageous uses of memory effects. This could be done, e.g., for specific microscopic models of noise stemming from the open quantum systems literature
[
76
,
77
]
, or with general approaches, such as treating
RB
as convolution
[
40
]
. While non-Markovianity has stayed relatively in the dark when considered in the context of noise characterization techniques, it almost certainly can be smoothly incorporated into a generalized framework for
RB
in the spirit of
[
8
]
, and further allow for a more comprehensive understanding and control of memory effects in quantum technologies.

Appendix A
Background preliminaries

Here we present some of the essential background related to representation theory of finite groups as well as randomized benchmarking; for the first, references such as
[
78
,
79
]
can be consulted for an in-depth treatment, while for
RB
we mainly follow the results of
[
32
]
.

A.1
Representations of finite groups

A group is a set
\mathds{G}
and an operation
\cdot
satisfying:

\diamond

(Closure) For all
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathds{G}
, also
g_{1}\cdot{g}_{2}=\mathds{G}
.

\diamond

(Associativity) For all
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in\mathds{G}
,
(g_{1}\cdot{g}_{2})\cdot{g}_{3}=g_{1}\cdot(g_{2}\cdot{g}_{3})
.

\diamond

(Identity) There exists
e\in\mathds{G}
such that
e\cdot{g}=g\cdot{e}=g
for all
g\in\mathds{G}
.

\diamond

(Inverse) There exists
g^{-1}\in\mathds{G}
for all
g\in\mathds{G}
such that
g^{-1}\cdot{g}=g\cdot{g}^{-1}=e
.

Henceforth we omit the
\cdot
symbol and take group multiplication to be the group operation.

A representation of a group provides a way of dealing with abstract groups as linear transformations to a vector space. In particular, we restrict ourselves to representations by unitary linear operators on
\mathds{U}(V)
for a complex finite-dimensional vector space
V
, and we may define a representation of a group
\mathds{G}
as a map

\phi:\mathds{G}\to\mathds{U}(V):\,g\mapsto\phi(g),

(43)

being such that

\phi(g)\phi(h)=\phi(gh),\qquad\forall{g,h\in\mathds{G}},

(44)

i.e., with the group operation being preserved under matrix multiplication of
\phi
. Here we will normally take
V
as a vector space over
d\times{d}
matrices.

We call a representation
\phi
reducible if there exists some transformation
S
such that

\displaystyle S\phi(g)S^{-1}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(g)\oplus\phi_{2}(g)=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}(g)&0\\
0&\phi_{2}(g)\end{pmatrix},

(45)

for all
g\in\mathds{G}
. Otherwise, a representation is called irreducible. This implies that any reducible representation can be written as a direct sum of irreducible ones: more generally, Maschke’s theorem
[
79
]
ensures that, for all
g\in\mathds{G}

\displaystyle\phi(g)=\Moplus_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\phi_{\pi}^{\otimes\mu_{\pi}}(g),

(46)

where
R_{\mathds{G}}
is a set of labels for the irreducible representations, and
\mu_{\pi}
is a non-negative integer denoting the multiplicity (equivalent copies) of
\phi_{\pi}
. In this manuscript we deal solely with groups admitting multiplicity-free representations, meaning
\mu_{\pi}=1
for all
\pi
.

A.2
Schur’s lemma and twirling

A central result in representation theory is known as Schur’s lemma: in a nutshell, it states that
the only matrices that commute with all elements of an irreducible representation of a group are constant matrices (i.e., scalar multiples of
\mathds{1}
)
. This is stated as in Lemma 1 of
[
12
]
, which in turn refers to
[
78
]
for detailed proofs; similarly other standard literature can be consulted for an in-depth treatment, e.g.,
[
79
]
. Here we employ a consequence of Schur’s lemma, rather than the lemma itself, applying to the so-called twirl of an operator given a representation of a group.

Consider
\mathbb{G}
a finite group and
V
be some finite dimensional complex vector space as above. For a representation
\phi
of
\mathbb{G}
, the twirl
\mathcal{T}_{\phi}
is defined by

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}(A):=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{G}|}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{G}}\phi(g)\,A\,\phi(g)^{\dagger},

(47)

for all linear maps
A:V\to{V}
.

Lemma

(Lemma 1 in
[
32
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finite group and let
\phi
be a multiplicity-free representation of
\mathbb{G}
on a complex vector space
V
with decomposition

\phi(g)\simeq\Moplus_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\phi_{\pi}(g),\qquad\forall{g}\in\mathbb{G}

(48)

into inequivalent irreducible subrepresentations
\phi_{\pi}
. Then for any linear map
A:V\to{V}
the twirl of
A
over
\mathbb{G}
takes the form

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}(A)=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\frac{\tr(AP_{\pi})}{\tr(P_{\pi})}P_{\pi},

(49)

where
P_{\pi}
is the projector onto the support of the representation
\phi_{\pi}
.

The multiplicity-free requirement is relaxed in
[
8
]
in the context of Markovian randomized benchmarking, but here we will solely focus on the former case.

A.3
The superoperator representation

To apply Lemma
Lemma
, we may use the so-called
superoperator representation
(a.k.a. natural, Liouville or vectorized representation). The idea is to represent quantum channels as matrices acting on vectorized states (in an extended space of dimension squared). It is similar in spirit to the Choi-Jamiołkowski representation
[
67
]
, in the sense that it maps channels to matrices, although different in that it does not necessarily maps them to a quantum state (density matrix). Here we are mainly going to need the definition

\mathrm{vec}(|i\rangle\!\langle{j}|):=|ij\rangle,

(50)

so that generally

|X\rrangle:=\mathrm{vec}(X),

(51)

for any matrix
X
. This implies that for a
Completely Positive (CP)
map
\Phi
,

|\Phi(X)\rrangle:=\hat{\Phi}|X\rrangle,

(52)

where,

\Phi(\cdot):=\sum_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu}(\cdot)\,\varphi_{\mu}^{\dagger},\,\quad\text{implies}\quad\,\hat{\Phi}=\sum_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu}\otimes\varphi_{\mu}^{*},

(53)

for Kraus operators
\varphi_{\mu}
of
\Phi
, and where
*
here means entry-wise conjugate
8
8

8

This follows from
{\mathrm{vec}(AXB)=(A\otimes{B}^{\mathrm{T}})\mathrm{vec}(X)}
. Depending on the definition of the vec map, one may find this property with the right-hand side arranged differently. The definition in Eq. (
50
) effectively stacks the rows of the matrix in a vector. A definition that is also common is
{\mathrm{vec}(|a\rangle\!\langle{b}|)=|ba\rangle}
, which in braket notation looks odd but when looking at matrices looks sort of natural because it stacks columns of the matrix into the vector; this definition would lead to a slightly different Liouville rep of quantum maps.
. The Liouville representation of
\Phi
is here denoted by
\hat{\Phi}
, and we generally will distinguish maps,
\mathcal{X}
, when they are in the Liouville representation with a circumflex on top,
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
. The main point here is that quantum maps become matrices in this representation, which will let us work with them more easily without necessarily trying to extract properties of the maps in question. A clear exposition of this representation can be found e.g., in Section V.B of
[
39
]
.

A.4
The standard randomized benchmarking protocol

A standard
Randomized Benchmarking (RB)
protocol proceeds as follows:

1.

Prepare an initial state
\rho
on the system of interest
\mathsf{S}
.

2.

Sample
m
distinct elements,
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{m}
, uniformly at random from a given gate set
\mathds{K}
containing the corresponding inverse elements. Let
\mathcal{C}_{m+1}:=\Mcirc_{i=m}^{1}\mathcal{C}_{i}^{-1}=\mathcal{C}_{1}^{-1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{C}_{m}^{-1}
, where
\circ
denotes composition of maps. We refer to
\mathcal{C}_{m+1}
as an undo-gate.

3.

Apply the composition
\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}\mathcal{C}_{i}
on
\rho
. In practice, this amounts to applying a noisy sequence
\mathcal{S}_{m}:=\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}\mathcal{J}_{i}
of length
m
on
\rho
, where
\mathcal{J}_{i}
are the physical noisy gates associated to
\mathcal{C}
.

4.

Estimate the probability
f_{m}:=\tr\left[M{\mathcal{S}}_{m}\left(\rho\right)\right]
via a
Positive Operator Valued Measurement (POVM)
element
M
.

5.

Repeat
n
times the steps 1 to 4 for the same initial state
\rho
, same
POVM
element
M
, and different sets of gates chosen uniformly at random
\{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=1}^{m},\ldots,\{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{(n)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
from
\mathds{K}
to obtain the probabilities
f_{m}^{(1)},f_{m}^{(2)},\ldots{f}_{m}^{(n)}
. Compute the
Average Sequence Fidelity (ASF)
,
\mathcal{F}_{m}=1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{m}^{(i)}
.

6.

Examine the behavior of the
ASF
,
\mathcal{F}_{m}
, over different sequence lengths
m
.

A.5
Markovian finite-group time-independent average sequence fidelity

In standard, Markovian, time-independent and gate-independent
RB
, we have a sequence

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{m}

\displaystyle:=\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}\left(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{C}_{i}\right),

(54)

where
\mathcal{C}_{m+1}:=\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{C}_{m}^{\dagger}
with
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)=G(\cdot)G^{\dagger}
and here
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(\cdot)=G^{\dagger}(\cdot)G
, both for
G\in\mathbb{G}
unitary representations being the target gates and where the
Completely Positive Trace Preserving (CPTP)
map
\Lambda
models the noise. Now, consider a change of variables
\mathcal{G}_{j}=\Mcirc_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{C}_{i}
; this implies that, equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{m}=\Lambda\circ\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\dagger}\circ\Lambda\circ\mathcal{G}_{i}\right).

(55)

Thus we have, for an initial state
\rho
and a measurement with
POVM
element
M
, over a sequence length
m
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}(\rho,M)

\displaystyle:=\tr\left\{{M}\,\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\tr\left\{{M}\,\Lambda\circ\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\dagger}\circ\Lambda\circ\mathcal{G}_{i}\right]\rho\right\}

\displaystyle=\tr\left\{{M}\,\Lambda\circ\left(\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}\circ\Lambda\circ\mathcal{G}\right]\right)^{m}\rho\right\},

(56)

where
\mathds{E}
implicitly means uniform average over the gates
\mathcal{G}
. We will henceforth omit the explicit
\rho
and
M
dependence.

We can equivalently express Eq. (
56
) in the superoperator representation as

\mathcal{F}_{m}=\llangle{M}\,|\hat{\Lambda}\left(\mathds{E}[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger}\hat{\Lambda}\hat{\mathcal{G}}]\right)^{m}|\,\rho\rrangle.

(57)

Then it follows by Lemma
Lemma
, that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=\llangle{M}\,|\hat{\Lambda}\left(\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}f_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)^{m}|\,\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}f_{\pi}^{m}\,\llangle{M}\,|\hat{\Lambda}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}|\,\rho\rrangle,

(58)

where here, as done in the main text and as we do onward, we denote by
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}
the projector onto the irreducible space corresponding to
\phi_{\pi}
, and where

f_{\pi}:=\frac{\tr(\hat{\Lambda}\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})}{\tr(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})},

(59)

is called a
quality parameter
, as in
[
32
]
.

A.6
Standard case: Markovian, time-independent, 2-design

Consider the case of the group
\mathbb{G}
forming at least a 2-design, i.e., when uniformly averaging over it gives the same result as uniformly averaging over the whole unitary group. When we move to the superoperator representation, we may take the gates to the form
\hat{\mathcal{G}}=G\otimes{G}^{*}
for unitary
G
of dimension
d_{\mathsf{S}}
and where
G^{*}
denotes entry-wise complex conjugate; this is a representation with support on
\mathds{C}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\otimes\mathds{C}^{*d_{\mathsf{S}}}
, which can be decomposed into invariant subspaces having projectors (see e.g.
[
80
,
81
]
)

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\Psi,\qquad\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\mathds{1}-\Psi,

(60)

where
\Psi=\sum|ii\rangle\!\langle{jj}|/d_{\mathsf{S}}
can be thought of as a partial transpose after a swap (and normalized). Hence too, assuming that
\Lambda
is also trace-preserving,

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=\tr[\hat{\Lambda}{\Psi}]=\frac{\sum\langle{i}|\lambda_{\mu}|j\rangle\!\langle{j}|\lambda_{\mu}^{\dagger}|i\rangle}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}=1,

(61)

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tr[\hat{\Lambda}]-\tr[\hat{\Lambda}{\Psi}]}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}=\frac{\tr[\hat{\Lambda}]-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}:=p,

(62)

where here
\lambda_{\mu}
are the Kraus operators of the
\mathsf{S}
noise map
\Lambda
, and so as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}=f_{1}^{m}\llangle{M}|\hat{\Lambda}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{1}|\rho\rrangle+f_{2}^{m}\llangle{M}|\hat{\Lambda}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{2}|\rho\rrangle,

(63)

and noticing that
{\Psi}|\rho\rrangle=\Psi\,\mathrm{vec}(\rho)=\sum|ii\rangle/d_{\mathsf{S}}=|\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}}\rrangle
, which is simply the superoperator representation of
\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}}
, this gives the standard result

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=\llangle{M}|\hat{\Lambda}|\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\rrangle+p^{m}\llangle{M}|\hat{\Lambda}|\rho-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\rrangle=B+Ap^{m}.

(64)

B.3
From quality parameters to quality maps

In general, we can think of the whole term

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}:=\sum_{e,e^{\prime},\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|,

(81)

where
\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}
is the quality factor defined in Eq. (
68
), as the Liouville representation of an object that we may label as a
quality map
,

\mathcal{Q}_{m,\pi}(\cdot):=\sum_{e,e^{\prime},\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}|e\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon|(\cdot)|\varepsilon^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle{e}^{\prime}|,

(82)

or equivalently we can label this object generally as a
quality tensor
, given that each
\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}
can be thought of precisely as an
Matrix Product Operator (MPO)
with quality parameter nodes and environment bonds.

We may then equivalently write the full non-Markovian
ASF
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\llangle{M}\,|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\,|\,\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\tr[{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda\circ(\mathcal{Q}_{m,\pi}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\pi})\,\rho],

(83)

with
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
the map associated to the projector
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}
: these will depend on each particular group case and in general they are not projective maps (as one would initially be led to think), e.g., for the unitary 2-design case
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\Psi
corresponds to
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\cdot)=\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\tr(\cdot)
and
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\mathds{1}-\Psi
corresponds to
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\cdot)=(\cdot)-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\tr(\cdot)
.

B.4
Time-dependent noise

We can generalize the previous
ASF
to the case of time-dependent noise in a straightforward way. For the Markovian case,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}

\displaystyle=\llangle{M}|\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\,\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{E}[\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{\Lambda}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{i}]|\,\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}\llangle{M}|\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}|\,\rho\rrangle,

(84)

where here
\Lambda
acts solely on
\mathsf{S}
and where

\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}:=f_{(1),\pi}f_{(2),\pi}\cdots f_{(m),\pi}\quad\text{with}\quad f_{(i),\pi}:=\frac{\tr(\hat{\Lambda}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})}{\tr(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})}.

(85)

Similarly, for the non-Markovian case, now with
\Lambda
acting on the full
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{e,e^{\prime},\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{E}}}\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\!\left(|ee^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)|\,\rho\rrangle,

(86)

where now

\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}:=\sum_{\{\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{i}^{\prime}\}}f^{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}}_{(1),\pi}f^{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}}_{(2),\pi}\cdots{f}^{\epsilon_{m-2}\epsilon_{m-2}^{\prime}\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}}_{(m-1),\pi}f^{\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{(m),\pi},

(87)

with

f_{(i),\pi}^{\,ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}:=\frac{\tr(\hat{\Lambda}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{i,\mathsf{S}}\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})}{\tr(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})},\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\Lambda}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{i,\mathsf{S}}=\sum_{\mu}\langle{e}|\lambda_{\mu}^{(i)}|\varepsilon\rangle\otimes\langle{e^{\prime}}|\lambda_{\mu}^{(i)\,*}|\varepsilon^{\prime}\rangle,

(88)

where here
\lambda_{\mu}^{(i)}
are the Kraus operators of the respective
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
map
\Lambda_{i}
.

Notice that non-Markovianity is a time-dependence in itself, with the time-labels constituting an extra layer of time-dependence where the noise maps
\Lambda
can themselves differ arbitrarily between timesteps. The crucial difference between Markovian and non-Markovian time-dependence, however, is that the Markovian one simply refers to an explicit dependence on timesteps but not to a temporal correlation between these: this is clear if one compares Eq. (
85
) with its non-Markovian counterpart in Eq. (
87
): it is apparent that Markovian time-dependent
ASF
s can be reproduced by non-Markovian time-independent ones with a large enough environment.

B.5
The quality map in the Markov limit

Consider a single step,
m=1
, and two distinct
ASF
, one Markovian and the other non-Markovian. Suppose for the Markov case we have some
CP
map
\Phi
acting on
\mathsf{S}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{f}_{m=1,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}=f_{(1),\pi}=\frac{\tr[\hat{\Phi}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}{\tr[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}=\sum_{\mu}\frac{\tr[(\phi_{\mu}\otimes\phi_{\mu}^{*})\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}{\tr[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]},

\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{f}_{m=1,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}=f_{(1),\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tr[\hat{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{S}}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}{\tr[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\frac{\tr[(\langle{e}|\lambda_{\mu}^{(i)}|\varepsilon\rangle\otimes\langle{e^{\prime}}|\lambda_{\mu}^{(i)\,*}|\varepsilon^{\prime}\rangle)\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}{\tr[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}.

(90)

The two quantities are actually somewhat akin: let
\Phi
have a Stinespring dilation representation
{\Phi(\cdot)=\tr_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathcal{U}(\nu\otimes\cdot)]}

\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{U}
and a pure state

\mathcal{E}

\Phi
are
\phi_{\mu}=\langle\mu|\mathcal{U}|\nu\rangle
, so that

\mathbbm{f}_{m=1,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}=\sum_{\mu}\frac{\tr[(\langle\mu|\mathcal{U}|\nu\rangle\otimes\langle\mu|\mathcal{U}^{*}|\nu\rangle)\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}{\tr[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}.

(91)

The non-Markovian quality parameter will reduce to something of this form in the Markov limit: indeed, in such a case, within the full
ASF
, we can interchange the partial trace with the noise due to the undo gate, which would have the form
\Lambda_{2}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\Lambda_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}
, thus rendering a term
\delta_{ee^{\prime}}
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathbbm{f}_{m=1,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Markov}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sum_{e}\sum_{\mu}\frac{\tr[(\langle{e}|\lambda_{\mu}^{(i)}|\varepsilon\rangle\otimes\langle{e}|\lambda_{\mu}^{(i)\,*}|\varepsilon^{\prime}\rangle)\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]}{\tr[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}]},

(92)

reducing effectively to Eq. (
91

Markovianizing
the process tensor as described in
[
29
]
, i.e., tracing out the environment at every step.

Average sequence fidelity finite memory perturbation

Consider now noise maps that take a convex combination form,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{f}}_{m=3,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(\underaccent{\vec}{\Gamma}\right):=\sum_{\{\epsilon_{i}^{(\prime)}\}}f^{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}}_{(1)}(\Gamma_{1})f^{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}}_{(2)}(\Gamma_{2})f^{\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{(3)}(\Gamma_{3})

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{3}r_{n}\delta_{e\varepsilon}\delta_{e^{\prime}\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mathbbm{f}_{m=3}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}\left(\underaccent{\vec}{\Phi}\right)

\displaystyle+\left[q_{1}r_{2}r_{3}\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}(\Phi_{2},\Phi_{3})\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\!(\Lambda_{1})+r_{1}q_{2}r_{3}\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{3})\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\!(\Lambda_{2})+r_{1}r_{2}q_{3}\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\!(\Lambda_{3})\right]

\displaystyle+\left[r_{1}q_{2}q_{3}\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}(\Phi_{1})\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3})+q_{1}r_{2}q_{3}\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{3})\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}(\Phi_{2})+q_{1}q_{2}r_{3}\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2})\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}(\Phi_{3})\right]

\displaystyle+\prod_{n=1}^{3}q_{n}\,\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{f}}_{m=3}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(\underaccent{\vec}{\Lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{3}(1-q_{n})\delta_{e\varepsilon}\delta_{e^{\prime}\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mathbbm{f}_{m=3}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}\left(\underaccent{\vec}{\Phi}\right)+\binom{(1-q)^{2}q\,\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}}+\binom{(1-q)q^{2}\mathbbm{f}_{m=1}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{\mathbbm{f}_{m=2}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\prod_{n=1}^{3}q_{n}\,\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{f}}_{m=3}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(\underaccent{\vec}{\Lambda}\right),

(96)

where the notation
\displaystyle{\binom{(1-q)^{\ell}q^{m-\ell}\mathbbm{f}_{\ell}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}}}
for
\ell\leq{m}
here denotes the sum of possible combinations of the map arguments
\{q_{i}\Lambda_{i}\}
in
\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}
within a product
\mathbbm{f}_{\ell}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, conditioned with the map arguments to be time-ordered, i.e.,
(1-q_{i_{1}})\cdots(1-q_{i_{\ell}})q_{\ell+1}\cdots{q}_{i_{m}}\mathbbm{f}_{\ell}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}(\Phi_{i_{1}},\ldots,\Phi_{i_{\ell}})\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,\Lambda_{i_{m}})
must be such that
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell}<i_{\ell+1}<\cdots<i_{m}
. This is irrelevant in the Markovian quality parameters and could be relaxed but an extra factor would need to be added to avoid over-counting. The notation is suggestive because in the static case these will just correspond to binomial coefficients and powers of the Markovian quality parameters as
\binom{m}{\ell}(1-q)^{\ell}q^{m-\ell}f^{\ell}\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.

We may thus write the general
m
case as,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{f}}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(\underaccent{\vec}{\Gamma}\right)=\prod_{n=1}^{m}(1-q_{n})\delta_{e\varepsilon}\delta_{e^{\prime}\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}(\underaccent{\vec}{\Phi})+\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\binom{q^{m-\ell}(1-q)^{\ell}\mathbbm{f}_{\ell,\,\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{\mathbbm{f}_{m-\ell,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}},

(97)

which in turn implies

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e,e^{\prime},\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{m}(1-q_{n})\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\binom{q^{m-\ell}(1-q)^{\ell}\mathbbm{f}_{\ell,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m-\ell,\pi}}{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m-\ell,\pi}},

(98)

and thus the
ASF
for this noise model with any finite group can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Gamma}_{m+1}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})|\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{m}(1-q_{n})\!\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\mathbbm{f}_{m,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Gamma}_{m+1}(\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})|\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Gamma}_{m+1}\binom{q^{m-\ell}(1-q)^{\ell}\mathbbm{f}_{\ell,\pi}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m-\ell,\pi}}{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m-\ell,\pi}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}|\,\rho\rrangle,

(99)

where the first term is the single Markovian contribution, dominant whenever
q\approx 0
.

For static noise,
\Gamma:=\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma_{j}
, this reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Gamma}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})|\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{m}\!\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}f_{\pi}^{m}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Gamma}(\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})|\rho\rrangle+\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\binom{m}{\ell}\,q^{m-\ell}(1-q)^{\ell}\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}f_{\pi}^{\ell}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Gamma}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m-\ell,\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})|\,\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{m}\,\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\binom{m}{\ell}\,q^{m-\ell}(1-q)^{\ell}\,\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{m},

(100)

which more easily can be read as a correction to the Markovian
ASF
with contributions at each intermediate step. Here
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(\ell)}
means the
ASF
decay is exponential (Markovian) up to timestep
\ell
, specifically given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}f_{\pi}^{\ell}\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m-\ell,\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)|\rho\rrangle,

(101)

with
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m-\ell,\pi}:=\sum{f}_{(\ell+1),\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{\ell+1}\epsilon^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}\cdots{f}_{(m),\pi}^{\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon^{\prime}_{m-1}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|
and
\rho
being uncorrelated on
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
.

We can further relabel
\ell=m-\ell^{\prime}
to obtain a binomial expansion dominated by the leading
\ell^{\prime}
terms whenever
0\lesssim{q}\ll 1/2
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{m}\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{Mkv})}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\binom{m}{m-\ell}q^{\ell}(1-q)^{m-\ell}\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(m-\ell)}.

(102)

In this sense this constitutes a perturbative expansion with finite-memory corrections to the
ASF
.

B.7
Character randomized benchmarking

The character of a group
\mathds{G}
associated to the rep
\phi
is a function
\chi_{\phi}:\mathds{G}\to\mathds{R}
. In particular, it satisfies

\avg_{G\in\mathds{G}}\chi_{\phi}(G)\,\mathcal{G}=\frac{1}{|\phi|}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\phi},

(103)

with
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\phi}
the projector onto the support of all the subreps of
\mathcal{G}
equivalent to
\phi
and
|\phi|
is the dimension of the representation
\phi
.

In particular, the core idea in the Character
RB
protocol of
[
32
]
is that we can attach a gate from a subgroup of the
RB
group that we are benchmarking to isolate individual quality parameters.

Explicitly, fix a
\lambda^{\prime}\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}
; now consider
\mathds{H}\subset\mathds{G}
such that the Liouville representation has a subrepresentation
\hat{\phi}
, with character function
\chi_{\hat{\phi}}
, that has support inside the rep
\phi_{\lambda^{\prime}}
of
\mathds{G}
. This means
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\hat{\phi}}\subset\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. Then attaching some extra gate
h\in\mathds{H}
to the original
RB
sequence, we can estimate the weighed average

\displaystyle\kappa_{m}^{\lambda^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=|\hat{\phi}|\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}\left(\mathds{E}[(\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger})\hat{\Lambda}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{G}})]\right)^{m}\!\avg_{h\in\mathds{H}}\chi_{\hat{\phi}}(h)\hat{h}|\rho\rrangle,

\displaystyle=\sum_{e,e^{\prime},\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{E}}}\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{f}}_{m,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\llangle{M}\,|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}\left(|ee^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\hat{\phi}}\right)|\,\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{e,e^{\prime},\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{E}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{f}}_{m,\lambda^{\prime}}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\llangle{M}\,|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}\left(|ee^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\hat{\phi}}\right)|\,\rho\rrangle

\displaystyle=\llangle{M}\,|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\lambda^{\prime}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\hat{\phi}}\right)|\,\rho\rrangle,

(104)

which isolates the part of the
ASF
with quality tensor
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\lambda^{\prime}}
. Whereas in the Markovian case one can readily fit single exponentials and extract average gate fidelities, for the non-Markovian case a further analysis or adapted protocol to extract the noise maps would be required, e.g., as we show, the averaging over initial states and measurements.

Appendix C
Quality parameters and process fidelity

Single quality parameters arise from twirling via Schur’s lemma, and the usual average gate fidelity of some channel
\Phi
with the identity can also be directly related to a twirl. In fact, from the definition of the uniform average gate fidelity of a map
\Phi
with respect to the identity,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{\Phi}

\displaystyle:=\int{d}\psi\langle\psi|\Phi(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int{d}\mu(U)\,\langle\psi|U^{\dagger}\Phi(U|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|{U}^{\dagger})U|\psi\rangle,

(105)

where
\mu
is the Haar measure and
U
is a uniformly drawn random unitary, and given the formula

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle d\mu(U)\,U^{\dagger}AUXU^{\dagger}BU=\frac{d\tr(AB)-\tr(A)\tr(B)}{d(d^{2}-1)}\tr(X)\mathds{1}+\frac{d\tr(A)\tr(B)-\tr(AB)}{d(d^{2}-1)}X,

(106)

for operators on a
d
-dimensional space, one can deduce that

\mathfrak{F}_{\Phi}=\frac{d+\tr[\hat{\Phi}]}{d(d+1)}.

(107)

We may just employ invariance of the trace under a twirl with a subgroup
\mathbb{G}
of the unitary group so that via Schur’s lemma,

\mathfrak{F}_{\Phi}=\frac{d+\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}f_{\pi}\tr(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})}{d(d+1)}.

(108)

In the standard case of Markovian
RB
with unitary 2-designs this gives a simple relation of the average gate fidelity with the noise-strength
p
because the only quality parameters are
f_{1}=1
and
f_{2}=p
, the latter of which contains the term
\tr[\Phi]
such that

\mathfrak{F}_{\Phi}=p+\frac{1}{d}(1-p),

(109)

and for this reason, in this scenario it suffices to run a standard
RB
protocol to extract average gate fidelities of the noise. More generally for finite groups (with a multiplicity-free representation) character
RB
described in Section
B.7
extracts each quality factor, allowing to estimate the respective average gate fidelity.

For the non-Markovian case, however, after an
RB
protocol, the quality tensor is still cloistered between the noise due to the undo gate and the state preparation error, both of which can act on the full
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
. While in the Markovian case these two can be absorbed as
State Preparation and Measurement (SPAM)
errors not affecting the
ASF
decay, this is not the case for non-Markovian noise. Furthermore, non-Markovianity as a time-dependence is explicit in the quality map as all quality factors are correlated with each other through
\mathsf{E}
, as discussed in Appendix
B.4
.

Thus we propose quantifying the average fidelity of a noise process as a whole by means of
RB
. Whenever this average fidelity factorizes, we will recover the standard Markovian case; this points to the possibility as well that other clear functional forms of this
process fidelity
could be identified for certain types of noise. Consider then the following. Suppose we have a non-Markov
ASF
given as in Eq. (
83
); we care about how well the inputs are being mapped to outputs on average, thus let us define a map
\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}(\rho_{\mathsf{S}},M)=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\tr\left[M\,\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}(\rho_{\mathsf{S}})\right],

(110)

i.e., for some
CPTP
map
\Lambda_{0}
and a fiducial pure state
\varepsilon
of
\mathsf{E}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}(x):=\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ(\mathcal{Q}_{m,\pi}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\pi})\circ\Lambda_{0}(\varepsilon\otimes x).

(111)

The map
\Lambda_{0}
thus explicitly refers to a state preparation error correlating the input solely in space
\mathsf{S}
with the environment
\mathsf{E}
. The previous discussion then means we wish to extract the average gate fidelity of the map
\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
. The
ASF
contains only two free inputs which are chosen arbitrarily,
\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
and
{M}
: if we average over these, we could effectively extract the gate fidelity of
\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
. Of course, this added step in the
RB
protocol would only be relevant in the non-Markov case, i.e., when deviations from an exponential are observed, as otherwise it would simply amount of averaging the
SPAM
terms.

To extract an average gate fidelity we require a second moment over a given distribution, so consider

\displaystyle{M}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{N}_{m+1}\circ\mathcal{U}(|s\rangle\!\langle{s}|)

(112)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathsf{S}}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{N}_{0}\circ\mathcal{U}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|),

(113)

where
\mathcal{U}
is a unitary belonging to a 2-design, sampled at uniformly at random,
\{|s\rangle\}_{s=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}
is a basis vector of
\mathsf{S}
, so that
M
is the
s
th
-
POVM
element of such random
POVM

[
73
]
,
|\psi\rangle
is a pure state in
\mathsf{S}
, and each
\mathcal{N}_{x}
is some
CPTP
map modeling
SPAM
due to the randomization. Overall we would now have a distribution of
ASF
s specified by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}\to

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\tr\left[\mathcal{N}_{m+1}\circ\mathcal{U}(|s\rangle\!\langle{s}|)\,\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{0}\circ\mathcal{U}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\langle{s}|\,\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\circ\mathcal{N}_{m+1}^{\dagger}\circ\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{0}\circ\mathcal{U}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)\,|s\rangle.

(114)

Then we can average over these inputs and measurements so that denoting by
K_{\mu}^{(m,\pi)}
the Kraus operators of
\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
and by
N^{(x)}_{\mu}
the ones of
\mathcal{N}_{x}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{S}},{M}}[\mathcal{F}_{m}]

\displaystyle=\sum\mathds{E}_{U}\langle{s}|\,U^{\dagger}N^{\dagger\,(m+1)}_{\alpha}K_{\gamma}^{(m,\pi)}N^{(0)}_{\beta}U|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|{U}^{\dagger}N^{(0)\,\dagger}_{\beta}K^{(m,\pi)\,\dagger}_{\gamma}N^{(m+1)}_{\alpha}U\,|s\rangle

\displaystyle:=\sum\mathds{E}_{U}\langle{s}|\,U^{\dagger}\mathsf{K}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}^{(m,\pi)}U|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|{U}^{\dagger}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger\,(m,\pi)}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}U\,|s\rangle

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\pi\in\mathds{G}}\mathds{E}_{U}\langle{s}|\,U^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}\left(U|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|{U}^{\dagger}\right)U\,|s\rangle,

(115)

where we defined
\mathsf{K}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}^{(m,\pi)}:=N^{\dagger\,(m+1)}_{\alpha}K_{\gamma}^{(m,\pi)}N^{(0)}_{\beta}
as the Kraus operators of the composition

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}:=\mathcal{N}_{m+1}^{\dagger}\circ\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{0}.

(116)

The maps
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
are essentially equivalent, as we may simply absorb the
\mathcal{N}
on the respective
\Lambda
maps by defining

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}(x)

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ(\mathcal{N}^{\dagger}_{m+1}\!\!\circ\Lambda_{m+1})\circ(\mathcal{Q}_{m,\pi}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\pi})\circ(\Lambda_{0}\circ\mathcal{N}_{0})(\varepsilon\otimes x)

\displaystyle:=\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}^{\prime}\circ(\mathcal{Q}_{m,\pi}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\pi})\circ\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}(\varepsilon\otimes x),

(117)

where the
\mathcal{N}
maps act solely on
\mathsf{S}
.

Notice that if
\langle{s}|\psi\rangle=1
, then
\mathds{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{S}},M}[\mathcal{F}_{m}]=\sum_{\pi}\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}^{(m,\pi)}
by definition, where here
\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}^{(m)}
means the average gate fidelity of
\sum_{\pi}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
; we can now employ Eq. (
105
) to get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{S}},{M}}[\mathcal{F}_{m}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}\left[\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}\tr\left(\sum\mathsf{K}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}^{(m,\pi)}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger\,(m,\pi)}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}\right)-\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]}{d_{\mathsf{S}}(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1)}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]-\tr\left(\sum\mathsf{K}^{(m,\pi)}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger\,(m,\pi)}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}\right)}{d_{\mathsf{S}}(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1)}|\langle{s}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\right].

(118)

The full map
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
sends
\mathsf{S}
input states to
\mathsf{S}
output states, so it must be
CP
; we can now further demand the whole of
\sum_{\pi}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}
to be
CPTP
, so that
\tr[\sum_{\pi}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\mathsf{K}^{(m,\pi)}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger\,(m,\pi)}_{\alpha\gamma\beta}]=d_{\mathsf{S}}
; this implies the noise being trace-preserving, then

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{S}},{M}}[\mathcal{F}_{m}]

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-\sum_{\pi}\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]}{d_{\mathsf{S}}(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1)}+\frac{\sum_{\pi}\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}|\langle{s}|\psi\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sum_{\pi}\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}\right)\left(|\langle{s}|\psi\rangle|^{2}-\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}\,\mathfrak{F}^{(m)}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}-1}\right)\left(|\langle{s}|\psi\rangle|^{2}-\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}|\langle{s}|\psi\rangle|^{2}-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}-1}\right)\mathfrak{F}^{(m)}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}+\frac{1-|\langle{s}|\psi\rangle|^{2}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}-1}

\displaystyle:=A\mathfrak{F}^{(m)}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}+B

(119)

where the second line can be identified noticing that
\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-\sum_{\pi}\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}=1-\frac{\sum_{\pi}\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}
, the third line follows by Eq. (
107
), and
A
and
B
are constants. The form of the average in Eq. (
119
) follows from it being uniform with respect to either the full unitary group or a 2-design, while the gate fidelity
\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}^{(m)}
is still a quantity containing the
RB
protocol, i.e., average noise, with respect to any finite group.

The dependence in
m
will generally be non-trivial, as it is directly related to a dependence of the environment
\mathsf{E}
, except for specific cases or whenever there are assumptions on the noise, e.g., for Markov time-independent noise, and other particular cases might also display some useful functional structure.

Consider the definition in Eq. (
117
) and let
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varepsilon}:=(|\varepsilon\rangle\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}})\otimes(|\varepsilon\rangle\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}})^{*}
, then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\tr[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}\tr\left[\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{m+1}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{0}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\tr\left[\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{0}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varepsilon}\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{m+1}\,\sum_{\pi}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{0}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varepsilon}\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{m+1}\|\sum_{\pi}\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\right)\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle:=\|\hat{\mathscr{S}}\|\sum_{\pi}\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\right)\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right),

(120)

where here
\|\cdot\|
denotes operator norm, i.e., largest singular value, and for the first inequality we used the fact that all operators are positive semidefinite. Finally, we defined
\hat{\mathscr{S}}
as the
SPAM
-noise operator
\hat{\mathscr{S}}:=\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{0}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varepsilon}\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{m+1}
; in terms of Kraus operators, this is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{S}}=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\sum_{e}\left(\lambda_{0}^{\mu}|\varepsilon\rangle\!\langle{e}|\lambda_{m+1}^{\nu}\otimes\lambda_{0}^{*\mu}|\varepsilon\rangle\!\langle{e}|^{*}\lambda_{m+1}^{*\nu}\right).

(121)

Now we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\right)\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}[(d_{\mathsf{S}}+1)\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}^{(m)}-1]}{\|\hat{\mathscr{S}}\|},

(122)

so that Character
RB
can be employed to obtain estimates of
\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\right)\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)
, which is related to the gate fidelity of the map
\mathcal{Q}_{m,\pi}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
, as in Eq. (
107
). This can serve to estimate noise from gates separately from
SPAM
correlation errors.

C.1
Process fidelity - The Markovian case

Clearly when averaging over initial states and measurements, the
ASF
of a Markovian
RB
experiment should simply average the
SPAM
terms. This might still be useful in case average
SPAM
error rates are of interest. First, Eq. (
117
) turns into

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{(i),\pi}\right)\,\Lambda_{m+1}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\circ\Lambda_{0}^{\prime},

(123)

with all maps now acting solely on
\mathsf{S}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{S}},{M}}[\mathcal{F}_{m}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{(i),\pi}\right)\mathds{E}_{U}\tr\left[U|s\rangle\!\langle{s}|U^{\dagger}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\circ\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}(U|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|U^{\dagger})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{(i),\pi}\right)\mathds{E}_{M,\rho_{{}_{\mathsf{S}}}}\tr\left[M_{s}\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\circ\Lambda_{0}(\rho_{{}_{\mathsf{S}}})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathds{G}}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{(i),\pi}\right)\mathds{E}_{M,\rho_{{}_{\mathsf{S}}}}\llangle M_{s}|\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\hat{\Lambda}_{0}|\rho_{{}_{\mathsf{S}}}\rrangle,

(124)

which amounts exactly to uniformly averaging the
SPAM
terms over noisy measurement elements and initial states.

Now, however, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}+\sum_{\pi}\tr\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{F}}}_{m,\pi}^{\mathsf{S}}\right)}{d_{\mathsf{S}}(d_{\mathsf{S}}+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}+\sum_{\pi}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{(i),\pi}\right)\tr\left(\hat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\prime}\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)}{d_{\mathsf{S}}(d_{\mathsf{S}}+1)},

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\mathsf{S}}+\sum_{\pi}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{(i),\pi}\right)f_{\pi}^{(\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\prime})}\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)}{d_{\mathsf{S}}(d_{\mathsf{S}}+1)}

(125)

where
f_{\pi}^{(\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\prime})}
is a quality factor of the
SPAM
noise. Here clearly all of the
m
-steps are being taken into account and the
SPAM
errors are directly incorporated: this is because the average over initial states and measurements will always contain both the
\Lambda_{0}
and
\Lambda_{m+1}
maps.

For the unitary 2-design / Clifford case, with time-independence and
CPTP
noise, it can be seen that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{S}},{M}}[\mathcal{F}_{m}]

\displaystyle=p^{m}q\left(|\langle{s}|\psi\rangle|^{2}-\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)+\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}},

(126)

where

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\frac{\tr[\hat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\prime}\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}^{\prime}]-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1},

(127)

being the corresponding
SPAM
quality factor or noise-strength.

Appendix D
Non-Markovianity

Now, looking back at quality factors, we have

\displaystyle\tr[\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{e,\epsilon\}}{f}_{(1),\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}}{f}_{(2),\pi}^{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}}{f}_{(3),\pi}^{\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}\epsilon_{3}\epsilon_{3}^{\prime}}\cdots{f}_{(m),\pi}^{\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}ee^{\prime}}.

(128)

In the Markovian time-dependent case, it is possible to estimate average gate (in)fidelities on arbitrary sequence length intervals
[
39
]
by taking ratios of
ASF
s, precisely because these do not depend on all previous steps, i.e., the quality factors satisfy
\frac{f_{(1)}f_{(2)}\cdots{f}_{(m_{2})}}{f_{(1)}f_{(2)}\cdots{f}_{(m_{1})}}=f_{(m_{1}+1)}\cdots{f}_{(m_{2})}
for
m_{2}>m_{1}
. As we argue in Appendix
B.4
, time-dependent Markovian
ASF
s are contained in non-Markovian ones, either time-independent or time-dependent, a crucial difference, however, is that quality factors in time-dependent Markovian
ASF
s only change its rate of decay, so it is not possible to obtain a higher
ASF
in increasing sequence lengths, while there is virtually no reason why non-Markovian
ASF
s should also be constrained in this way.

Consider then
n>m
such that
\mathcal{F}_{n}>\mathcal{F}_{m}
for
RB
experiments with the exact same group and noise up to step
m
. This would imply that

\displaystyle\tr\left\{|\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}}\rrangle\!\!\llangle{M}|\hat{\tr}_{\mathsf{E}}\sum_{\pi}\left[\hat{\Lambda}_{n+1}\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\right)-\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\right)\right]\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)\hat{\Lambda}_{0}\right\}>0,

(129)

which will hold whenever

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{n+1}\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}\right)-\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\left(\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}\right)\succ 0

(130)

i.e. where the matrix difference on the left side is positive definite for all
\pi
, in turn implying

\displaystyle\tr\left[\tr_{\mathsf{S}}\left(\hat{\Lambda}_{n+1}\right)\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\pi}\right]-\tr\left[\tr_{\mathsf{S}}\left(\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\right)\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{m,\pi}\right]>0,

(131)

or equivalently, letting
\hat{\Lambda}_{i}^{\mathsf{E}}:=\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\hat{\Lambda}_{i})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{E}}\,\mathbbm{f}_{m-1,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathbbm{f}_{n-m+1,\pi}^{\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\hat{\Lambda}_{n+1}^{\mathsf{E}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle-f_{(m),\pi}^{\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}^{\mathsf{E}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle>0,

(132)

where the sum here is over all environment
e,\epsilon
indices. Let us first consider the simplest case,
n=m+1
, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{E}}\,\mathbbm{f}_{m-1,\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}}\left(f_{(m),\pi}^{\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}\epsilon_{m}\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}f_{(m+1),\pi}^{\epsilon_{m}\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\hat{\Lambda}_{m+2}^{\mathsf{E}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle-f_{(m),\pi}^{\epsilon_{m-1}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}^{\mathsf{E}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle>0,

(133)

and furthermore, consider finite memory such that effectively
m=1
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{E}}\left(f_{(1),\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}}f_{(2),\pi}^{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\hat{\Lambda}_{3}^{\mathsf{E}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle-f_{(1),\pi}^{ee^{\prime}\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}\langle\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}|\hat{\Lambda}_{2}^{\mathsf{E}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle>0,

(134)

which by definition means

\displaystyle 0<

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{E}}\left(\frac{\tr\left[\langle{ee^{\prime}\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}}|(\hat{\Lambda}_{1}\otimes\hat{\Lambda}_{2})\hat{\Lambda}_{3}^{\mathsf{E}}|\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}ee^{\prime}\rangle\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{\otimes 2}\right]}{\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{\otimes 2}\right)}-\frac{\tr\left[\langle{ee^{\prime}}|(\hat{\Lambda}_{1})\hat{\Lambda}_{2}^{\mathsf{E}}|ee^{\prime}\rangle\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right]}{\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\frac{\hat{\Lambda}_{1}(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\hat{\Lambda}_{2}(\hat{\Lambda}_{3}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})}{\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}_{\pi}}^{\otimes 2}\right)}-\frac{\hat{\Lambda}_{1}(\hat{\Lambda}_{2}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})}{\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}_{\pi}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{\otimes 2}\right)}\tr\left\{\hat{\Lambda}_{1}\left[(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\hat{\Lambda}_{2}(\hat{\Lambda}_{3}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})-(\hat{\Lambda}_{2}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\hat{\Lambda}_{1}(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\|}{\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}^{\otimes 2}\right)}\tr\left[\hat{\Lambda}_{2}(\hat{\Lambda}_{3}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})-(\hat{\Lambda}_{2}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}})\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}_{\pi}}\right)\right],

(135)

and so,

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{S}}\tr(\hat{\Lambda}_{2})\tr\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)<\tr\left[\hat{\Lambda}_{2}\left(\hat{\Lambda}_{3}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\right)\right],

(136)

where furthermore, we may use
\tr(XY)\leq\|X\|\tr(Y)
for positive semidefinite
X
,
Y
, on the right-hand-side, so that

\displaystyle\tr(\hat{\Lambda}_{3})\|\hat{\Lambda}_{2}\|>d_{\mathsf{S}}\tr(\hat{\Lambda}_{2}).

(137)

The general form of inequality (
135
) is now clear for any
m
and
n
as

\displaystyle\tr\left[\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}(\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\cdots\hat{\Lambda}_{n}(\hat{\Lambda}_{n+1}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})-(\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}^{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathds{1})\tr(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\right]>0,

(138)

simplified to

\|\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}\|\cdots\|\hat{\Lambda}_{n}\|\tr(\hat{\Lambda}_{n+1})>d_{\mathsf{S}}\tr(\hat{\Lambda}_{m+1}),

(139)

repeatedly employing
\tr(XY)\leq\|X\|\tr(Y)
for positive semidefinite
X
,
Y
. Both trace terms are lower-bounded by zero, although they realistically are less and close to
d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}
.

In particular, for time-independent noise this reduces to

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Lambda}\|^{n-m}>d_{\mathsf{S}},

(140)

which says that
\Lambda
must be able to increase the purity of its input in at least
d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2/(n-m)}
, ruling out e.g., coherent noise. Furthermore, by
\|\hat{\mathcal{X}}\|\leq\sqrt{d}
for
CPTP
map
\mathcal{X}
acting between
d
-dimensional spaces (theorem II.I in
[
75
]
), one gets
d_{\mathsf{E}}>d_{\mathsf{S}}^{\frac{2}{n-m}-1}
, which really just implies the necessity of an environment.

Appendix E
Gate-dependence

E.1
General case — gate-dependence is taken to the environment

Suppose now instead of having chosen to model the noisy gates through the maps
\Lambda
, we choose to employ
\mathcal{J}:=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{R}
for some given
CP
maps
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
. This is such that a time-independent and gate-independent
RB
sequence reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{m}=\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}\mathcal{J}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{G}_{m+1}\circ(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{L})\circ\mathcal{G}_{m}\circ(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{L})\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{G}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R},

(141)

which produces an equivalent
ASF
as the one using the noise maps
\Lambda
, here with the outermost noisy maps accounting for
SPAM
errors. We may now incorporate gate-dependence by taking

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(g)}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{J}+\Delta_{g}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{R}+\Delta_{g},

(142)

where
\Delta_{g}
is the map containing the gate-dependent contribution, which is not necessarily small. In the non-Markovian case,
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{R}
,
\Delta_{g}
, and
\mathcal{J}
all act on both
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
, while the ideal
\mathcal{G}
acts on
\mathsf{S}
alone.

Let us now denote
\mathcal{X}_{j:i}=\mathcal{X}_{j}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{X}_{i}
, and
X_{j:i}=X_{j}\cdots{X}_{i}
for any maps
\mathcal{X}_{i},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{j}
or matrices
X_{i},\ldots,X_{j}
. We can now expand the corresponding noisy sequence as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{m}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{(g)}_{m+1:1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\ell_{i}=0\}}^{1}\mathcal{J}_{m+1}^{\ell_{m+1}}\circ\Delta_{m+1}^{1-\ell_{m+1}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{m}^{\ell_{m}}\circ\Delta_{m}^{1-\ell_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\circ\Delta_{1}^{1-\ell_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{m+1:1}+\Delta_{m+1:1}+\kern-20.00003pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\ell_{i}=0\}\\
\setminus\{\text{all}\,\ell_{i}=0\,\text{or}\,\text{all}\,\ell_{i}=1\}\end{subarray}}^{1}\kern-20.00003pt\mathcal{J}_{m+1}^{\ell_{m+1}}\circ\Delta_{m+1}^{1-\ell_{m+1}}\circ\mathcal{J}_{m}^{\ell_{m}}\circ\Delta_{m}^{1-\ell_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\circ\Delta_{1}^{1-\ell_{1}},

(143)

where here
\Delta_{i}=\Delta_{g_{i}}
, where the second line follows from the
multi-binomial theorem
and the last line contains terms mixing
\mathcal{J}
and
\Delta
: the results in
[
33
,
32
]
imply that for Markovian noise, such mixed terms do not contribute to the
ASF
, and that the term
\Delta_{m+1:1}
gives a contribution that vanishes exponentially in sequence length.

Extending the previous result to a non-Markovian
ASF
in gate-dependent noise would require
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
to satisfy the properties

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g},

(144)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathcal{R}}\,\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g}\hat{\mathcal{R}},

(145)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g},

(146)

where averages again are uniform over the group
\mathbb{G}
, with

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{g}:=\sum_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi},

(147)

for some
\mathsf{E}
to
\mathsf{E}
maps
\mathscr{Q}_{\pi}
.

Following
[
32
]
, start by plugging the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{g}
in Eq. (
144
) and Eq. (
145
), together with the multiplicity-free decomposition of the Liouville representation
\hat{\mathcal{G}}:=\displaystyle{\Moplus_{\pi\in{R}_{\mathbb{G}}}\phi_{\pi}(g)}
, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\,\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}),

(148)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\mathds{E}\left[\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger}\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}\,\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi})\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}.

(149)

Now the difference is that both
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\pi}
and
\phi(g)
act solely on
\mathsf{S}
, i.e., we now take w.l.o.g.,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}:=\sum_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi},\qquad\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\right)=\delta_{\pi\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi},\,\forall\lambda,

(150)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}:=\sum_{\pi}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi},\qquad\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\right)\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}=\delta_{\pi\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi},\,\forall\lambda,

(151)

That is, writing the identities explicitly, then we have the equations

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\,(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger})\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\,(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}),

(152)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\phi_{\pi}(g)^{\dagger})\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\,\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right]

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}})\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi},

(153)

and we can vectorize both sides to get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\phi_{\pi}(g)^{*}\right]|\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\rrangle

\displaystyle=(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}\mathsf{S}}\otimes\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}^{\,\mathrm{T}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}})\,|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}\rrangle,

(154)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\phi_{\pi}(g)^{*}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)})^{\mathrm{T}}\right]|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\rrangle

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}\mathsf{S}})|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\rrangle,

(155)

or equivalently (transposing and reordering spaces),

\displaystyle\llangle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}|\left\{\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathds{E}\left[\left(\phi_{\pi}(g)^{*}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\llangle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\pi}|(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{S}}),

(156)

\displaystyle\left\{\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathds{E}\left[\left(\phi_{\pi}(g)^{*}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\right\}|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\rrangle

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathscr{Q}}_{\pi}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{S}})|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\pi}\rrangle.

(157)

In the Markov case, these are simply eigenvalue equations (as the quality factor is a scalar), so the solution of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
follows through them being eigenvectors of the average operator in the left-hand side.

E.2
Stability under gate-dependent perturbations

While this is not possible in the non-Markovian case, we may consider a perturbative gate-dependence and look at how the
ASF
and the variance of the sequence fidelity changes. As we now show, this can be done in a similar way as done for the Markov and Clifford case in
[
39
]
. That is, take the noisy gates to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(g)}=\mathcal{J}+\epsilon\Delta_{g}=\Lambda\circ\mathcal{G}+\epsilon\Delta_{g},

(158)

for some
\epsilon
scaled such that
\|\hat{\Delta}_{g}\|\leq 1
for all
g
, and denote the sequence fidelity by
\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{(g)}:=\llangle M|\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{(g)}_{m+1:1}|\rho\rrangle
for fixed sequence length
m
. Due to Eq. (
143
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{(g)}=\mathcal{Z}_{m}+\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\epsilon^{n}\,\llangle{M}|\,\hat{\Xi}_{m}^{(n)}|\,\rho\rrangle,

(159)

where
\mathcal{Z}_{m}
is the gate-independent sequence fidelity and where here
\Xi_{m}^{(n)}
contains gate-dependent contributions to the
n
th
order in
\epsilon
, i.e., all terms containing
n
amount of
\Delta
terms in the gate-sequence; specifically we can write

\displaystyle\Xi_{m}^{(n)}:=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m+1}:H(s)=n}\prod_{i=m+1}^{1}\mathcal{W}^{(g)}_{i,s},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{W}^{(g)}_{i,s}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{J}_{i},&\text{if}\,s=0\\
\Delta_{i},&\text{if}\,s=1\end{cases}

(160)

which is equivalent to the second and third terms of Eq. (
143
), just highlighting the amount
n
of
\Delta
terms; here
H(s)
is the number of bits equal to 1 in the bit string
s
(more generally, its Hamming weight) and
\Delta_{i}:=\Delta_{g_{i}}
. Now then, the gate-dependent
ASF
,
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(g)}
, has the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(g)}=\mathcal{F}_{m}+\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\epsilon^{n}\,\llangle{M}|\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\Xi}_{m}^{(n)}\right]|\,\rho\rrangle,

(161)

and the gate-dependent term satisfies the following,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\epsilon^{n}\llangle{M}|\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\Xi}_{m}^{(n)}\right]|\,\rho\rrangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\epsilon^{n}\,\left\|\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\Xi}_{m}^{(n)}\right]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\epsilon^{n}\,\sum_{s:H(s)=n}\|\mathds{E}\left[\prod_{i=m+1}^{1}\mathcal{W}^{(g)}_{i,s}\right]\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\epsilon^{n}\,\binom{m+1}{n}\,(d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}})^{(n+1)/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\,\binom{m+1}{n}\,\left(\epsilon\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}\left[\left(1+\epsilon\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)^{m+1}-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}(m+1)}-1\right],

(162)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes maximum singular value (operator norm) of the Liouville representation of
\Xi_{m}^{(n)}
, and on the third line we use
\|\hat{\mathcal{X}}\|\leq\sqrt{d}
for a
CPTP
map between
d
-dimensional spaces (theorem II.I in
[
75
]
), together with
\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\,\|B\|
, which using the assumption that
\|\Delta_{i}\|\leq 1
, gives only
(n+1)
terms
\|\hat{\mathcal{J}}\|
. We point out there is virtually no change from the derivation of
[
39
]
: in particular, the average in the second line and/or the fact that the gates act solely on
\mathsf{S}
, do not change anything, and the bound is (almost) the same derived there with
d\to d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}
; this rather has to do with the noise acting on the full
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
, rather than
RB
being done solely on
\mathsf{S}
.

From here, letting
\delta_{\mathcal{F}}:=|\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(g)}-\mathcal{F}_{m}|
we get

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq\frac{\log\left[1+\delta_{\mathcal{F}}/\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right]}{(m+1)\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}}}},

(163)

that is, we can say that
|\mathcal{F}_{m}^{(g)}-\mathcal{F}_{m}|\leq\delta
whenever
\epsilon\leq\log\left[1+\delta\,(d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}})^{-1/2}\right](m+1)^{-1}(d_{\mathsf{E}}d_{\mathsf{S}})^{-1/2}
. In the main text we further use
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x>-1
, or in particular
\log(1+x)\approx x
for small
x
.

Turning now to the variance
\mathds{V}
of the sequence fidelity,
\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{(g)}
, the way to obtain the bound as in
[
39
]
(instead of directly plugging in the perturbed sequence fidelities), is to perturb each term from the average, in the original unperturbed variance
\mathds{V}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}]:=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{2}]-\mathcal{F}_{m}^{2}
, as

\displaystyle\mathds{V}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{(g)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{2}\right]+\delta\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{2}\right]-\mathcal{F}_{m}^{2}-\delta\left(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{2}\right]+\delta\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{2}\right]-\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}]^{2}-\delta\left(\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}]^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{V}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}]+\delta\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{2}\right]-\delta\left(\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}]^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{V}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}]+\mathds{E}\left[\delta\left(\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{2}\right)\right]-\delta\left(\mathds{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{m}]^{2}\right)

(164)
