1.2
Multiplicative Properties of Zeons

Since

Lemma 1.2

(Cancellation)

Let

Suppose

\natural(uv)=\natural v
. Hence,

must be a scalar. The rest follows from the fact that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(uv)=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}v).

∎

Proposition 1.3

(Existence and Uniqueness of Inverses)
.

Let

1.3
Complex Zeon Roots: Existence and Recursive Formulations

Invertible zeons have roots of all orders; a recursive algorithm establishes their existence and provides a convenient method for their computation. The material below, taken from
[
17
]
was generalized from the original (real) context seen in
[
2
]

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
w\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\times}
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
\exists u\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\times}
such that

, provided
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}w\neq 0
. Further, writing
w=\varphi+\zeta_{\{n\}}\psi
, where
\varphi,\psi\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n-1}
,
u
is computed recursively by

u=w^{1/k}=\varphi^{1/k}+\zeta_{\{n\}}\frac{1}{k}\varphi^{-(k-1)/k}\psi.

As expected, any invertible complex zeon has
k
distinct complex
k
th roots.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\times}
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
\alpha
has exactly
k
distinct
k
th roots; i.e.,
\sharp\{u:u^{k}=\alpha\}=k
.

Given an invertible zeon
u
and positive integer
k
, the
principal
k
th root
of
u
is defined to be the zeon
k
th root of
u
whose scalar part is the principal complex root of
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u\in\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 1.6
.

Following the approach of
[
18
]
, an analytic complex-valued function

may be extended to a zeon-valued function

The Zeon Inner Product

Let
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
. Writing
x
and
y
as column matrices
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})^{\intercal}
and
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})^{\intercal}
, the
zeon inner product
of
x
and
y
is defined by

\langle x,y\rangle=y^{\dagger}x,

(2.1)

where
y^{\dagger}=\overline{y}^{\intercal}
denotes the complex conjugate transpose of
y
.

Some basic properties are established in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
x,y,z\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\langle\alpha x+y,z\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha\langle x,z\rangle+\langle y,z\rangle

(2.2)

\displaystyle\langle x,y\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle y,x\rangle}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle x,y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}x,\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}y\rangle

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle x,x\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 0

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle x,x\rangle

\displaystyle=0\text{ \rm iff }x\in({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ})^{m}.

(2.6)

Proof.

Proof is straightforward. Beginning with (
2.2
),

\displaystyle\langle\alpha x+y,z\rangle

\displaystyle=z^{\dagger}(\alpha x+y)=\alpha z^{\dagger}x+z^{\dagger}y=\alpha\langle x,z\rangle+\langle y,z\rangle,

while (
2.3
) is established as follows:

\displaystyle\langle x,y\rangle=y^{\dagger}x=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\overline{y_{\ell}}x_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\overline{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\overline{x_{\ell}}y_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle y,x\rangle}.

Turning to (
2.4
),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle x,y\rangle=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\overline{y_{\ell}}x_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\overline{y_{\ell}})(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}x_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}x,\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}y\rangle.

Properties (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) follow immediately from (
2.4
).
∎

The quantity
\langle x,x\rangle
is generally not scalar-valued, so it does not define a norm. However, the scalar part of
\langle x,x\rangle
defines a seminorm. Since
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle x,x\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
, we define the
spectral seminorm
of
x
by

|x|_{\star}=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle x,x\rangle^{1/2}.

Definition 2.2
.

A zeon vector
v\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
is said to be
null
if its spectral seminorm is zero, i.e.,
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle v,v\rangle=0
, or equivalently,
\langle v,v\rangle\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ}
. A non-null zeon vector
v
is said to be
normalized
if and only if
\langle v,v\rangle=1
.

Given non-null
x\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
, one
normalizes

x
via the mapping
x\mapsto\hat{x}
where

\hat{x}=(\langle x,x\rangle^{-1})^{1/2}x.

It is not difficult to verify that
\langle\hat{x},\hat{x}\rangle=1
whenever
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\langle x,x\rangle\neq 0
.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the following elements of
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{3}}^{3}
, :

\displaystyle v_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}+\zeta_{\{1\}}\\
\zeta_{\{2\}}-\zeta_{\{2,3\}}\\
2-\zeta_{\{1,2,3\}}\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle v_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta_{\{1,3\}}\\
\zeta_{\{2\}}\\
\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\zeta_{\{1,3\}}\end{pmatrix}.

Direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=5-4\zeta_{\{1,2,3\}}

\displaystyle\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=0.

Although
v_{2}
is null,
v_{1}
can be normalized by mapping

v_{1}\mapsto w_{1}=\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle^{-1/2}v_{1},

where

In this case, the normalized vector

The Determinant

The determinant of

is defined in the usual way by

|A|=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^{m}a_{j\,\sigma(j)},

where
\mathcal{S}_{m}
is the symmetric group of order

and
\sgn(\sigma)
is the signature of the permutation
\sigma

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
,
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}|A|=|\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A|
.

Proof.

Given arbitrary
u,v\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(uv)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\left((\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}u)(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}v+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}v)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\left(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}v+\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}v+\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}v\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}u+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}u\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}v\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}v.

Extending to arbitrary products in the determinant, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}|A|

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}a_{j\,\sigma(j)}+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}a_{j\,\sigma(j)})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}a_{j\,\sigma(j)}+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}a_{j\,\sigma(j)})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}a_{j\,\sigma(j)}.

∎

The next result, first established for real zeon matrices in
[
16
]
, is now generalized to matrices over
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The mapping
A\mapsto\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A
is an algebra homomorphism
{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})\to{\rm End}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
.

Proof.

Recognizing that
{\rm End}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
is isomorphic to the algebra of
m\times m
complex matrices, the result is established by verifying that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(A+B)=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A+\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}B
and that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(AB)=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A)(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}B)
for matrices
A,B\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
. ∎

By Proposition
3.3
,
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(A^{k})=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A)^{k}
. Hence, the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

A matrix
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
is singular if and only if
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A
is singular.

Lemma 3.5

(Properties of the determinant)
.

Let

and

be

, and let

\displaystyle=|A||B|,

\displaystyle|\alpha A|

\displaystyle=\alpha^{m}|A|.

In particular,

Proof.

The result follows directly from the determinant definition.
∎

Lemma 3.6

The Matrix Inverse

With Lemma

established, conditions for invertibility of zeon matrices can be discussed. The next proposition was established for real zeon matrices in

. The proof for the complex generalization differs only by substituting
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A

\mathfrak{R}\mspace{1mu}A

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})
be a square matrix having entries from
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
, and write
A=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A+\mathfrak{D}A
, where
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}a_{ij})

A^{-1}=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A)^{-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\kappa(\mathfrak{D}A(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A)^{-1})-1}(-1)^{\ell}(\mathfrak{D}A(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A)^{-1})^{\ell}.

Corollary 3.8
.

A matrix

Zeon Gaussian Elimination

It is easily verified that the following elementary (row) operations have the familiar effects on the matrix determinant. If
A\mapsto A^{\prime}
via an elementary operation, then the effects are as follows:

i.

exchanging two rows changes the sign, i.e.,
|A^{\prime}|=-|A|
;

ii.

multiplying a row by an invertible zeon constant
u
implies
|A^{\prime}|=u|A|
;

iii.

adding any zeon multiple of one row to another gives
|A|=|A^{\prime}|
.

Much as in the real case, elementary zeon matrices are defined as matrices obtained from the identity matrix by elementary zeon row operations. Moreover, the standard method of computing inverses via Gaussian elimination can be applied, and every zeon matrix can be placed in upper triangular form via a sequence of elementary operations.

Example 3.9
.

Consider the matrix
A
given by

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}2+\zeta_{\{1\}}&\zeta_{\{2\}}&0\\
\zeta_{\{2\}}&2-\zeta_{\{2\}}&3\zeta_{\{1,2,3\}}\\
0&3\zeta_{\{1,2,3\}}&1-\zeta_{\{1,2,3\}}+\zeta_{\{1\}}\\
\end{array}\right),

along with elementary matrix
E_{1}
exchanging rows 1 and 2, and the elementary matrix
E_{2}
that multiplies row 3 by the constant
2+3\zeta_{\{1,2\}}
, as given here

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{array}\right);\quad E_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&2+3\zeta_{\{1,2\}}\\
\end{array}\right).

Then,

E_{1}A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\zeta_{\{2\}}&2-\zeta_{\{2\}}&3\zeta_{\{1,2,3\}}\\

\end{array}\right)

and

E_{2}A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}2+\zeta_{\{1\}}&\zeta_{\{2\}}&0\\

Further,

\displaystyle|A|

\displaystyle=8+6\zeta_{\{1,2\}}-8\zeta_{\{1,2,3\}}+12\zeta_{\{1\}}-4\zeta_{\{2\}}=(2+3\zeta_{\{1,2\}})|A|.

The Adjugate and Cramer’s Rule

When
A
is a square matrix with entries in a commutative ring
R
(and thus in
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
), the
adjugate
of
A
is the transpose of the cofactor matrix of
A
.
When
A
is invertible, the adjugate satisfies
{\rm adj}(A)=|A|A^{-1}
and hence,
{\rm adj}(A)A=|A|\mathbb{I}
.

Consequently, Cramer’s Rule holds for invertible complex zeon matrices. If
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}})
is invertible and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})^{\top}
is a column vector with complex zeon entries, then solutions of
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
satisfy

\displaystyle{\rm adj}(A)A\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=|A|\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle={\rm adj}(A)\mathbf{b},

so that
\mathbf{x}=\displaystyle\frac{{\rm adj}(A)}{|A|}\mathbf{b}
.

4
Complex Zeon Polynomials

The interested reader is directed to the recent paper
[
17
]
for a detailed study of zeon polynomials and their zeros. For purposes of computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors of zeon matrices, only essential results are recalled here.

When the leading coefficient of a zeon polynomial is invertible, polynomial division is possible in the familiar sense. This is formalized as follows.

Theorem 4.1

(Zeon polyomial division algorithm)
.

Let
\varphi(u)
and
\psi(u)
be polynomials with zeon coefficients such that the leading coefficient of
\psi(u)
is invertible. Then, there exist unique zeon polynomials
q(u)
and
r(u)
such that
0\leq\deg r(u)<\deg\psi(u)
and

\varphi(u)=\psi(u)q(u)+r(u).

(4.1)

The following corollary follows immediately from Theorem
4.1
.

Corollary 4.2

(Zeon remainder theorem)
.

Let
\varphi(u)
be a polynomial with zeon coefficients and let
z\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}
. Then,
\varphi(z)
is the remainder after dividing
\varphi(u)
by the monic polynomial
u-z
.

Generally, the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra does not hold for zeon polynomials. That is, if
\varphi(u)
is a nonconstant complex zeon polynomial, then
\varphi(u)=0
does not necessarily have a complex zeon root. For example, it is straightforward to verify that
\varphi(u)=(u-1)^{2}+\zeta_{\{1\}}
has no zeon zeros.

Given a complex zeon polynomial
\varphi(u)=\alpha_{m}u^{m}+\cdots+\alpha_{1}u+\alpha_{0}
, a
complex polynomial

f_{\varphi}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is induced by

f_{\varphi}(z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\alpha_{\ell})z^{\ell}.

It follows that

\displaystyle f_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\alpha_{\ell})(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\varphi(u)),

so that
f_{\varphi}\circ\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\circ\varphi
.

Considering
\varphi(u)=(u-1)^{2}+\zeta_{\{1\}}
, one sees that the induced complex polynomial
f_{\varphi}(z)=(z-1)^{2}
, which has a zero of multiplicity 2. Thus,
\varphi
need not have zeros when
f_{\varphi}
has complex zeros.

On the other hand, it was shown in
[
2
]
that every invertible zeon element with positive scalar part has a real square root. Extending to complex zeons,
(u-1)^{2}+(a+b\zeta_{\{1\}})
has zeros for all
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
where
a\neq 0
. In this particular example, one sees that
u=i\sqrt{a}+\displaystyle\frac{ib}{2\sqrt{a}}\zeta_{\{1\}}
is a zero.

When
f_{\varphi}(z)
has a multiple root
w_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\varphi(u)
may or may not have a zero
w
satisfying
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}w=w_{0}
. For this reason, discussion is now restricted to simple zeros of polynomials with zeon coefficients.

4.1
Spectrally Simple Zeros of Complex Zeon Polynomials

Recalled in this section is a useful version of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra for zeon polynomials. It not only guarantees the existence of a simple zeon zero of
\varphi(u)
when the complex polynomial
f_{\varphi}(z)
has a simple complex zero, but it provides an algorithm for computing such a zero.

Letting
\varphi(u)
be a nonconstant monic zeon polynomial, we consider
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}
to be a
simple
zero of
\varphi
if
\varphi(u)=(u-\lambda)g(u)
for some zeon polynomial
g
satisfying
g(\lambda)\neq 0
. Recalling that the spectrum of an element
u
in a unital algebra is the collection of scalars
\lambda
for which
u-\lambda
is not invertible, it follows that when
u\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
, the spectrum of
u
is the singleton
\{\lambda=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u\}
.

Definition 4.3
.

A simple zero
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}
of
\varphi(u)
is said to be a
spectrally simple
if
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda_{0}
is a simple zero of the complex polynomial
f_{\varphi}(z)
.

With the notion of spectrally simple zeros in hand, the fundamental theorem of algebra for zeon polynomials was presented in
[
17
]
.

Theorem 4.4

(Fundamental Theorem of Zeon Algebra)
.

Let
\varphi(u)
be a monic polynomial of degree
m
over
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
, and let
f_{\varphi}(z)
be the complex polynomial induced by
\varphi
. If
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
is a simple zero of
f_{\varphi}(z)
, let
g
be the unique complex polynomial satisfying
f_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u-\lambda_{0})g(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(u))
. It follows that
\varphi(u)
has a simple zero
\lambda
such that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda=\lambda_{0}
. In particular, for
1\leq k\leq n
, the grade-
k
part of
\lambda
(denoted
\lambda_{k}
) is given by

\lambda_{k}=-\frac{1}{g(\lambda_{0})}\left<\varphi\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{j}\right)\right>_{k}.

Moreover, such a zero
\lambda
is unique.

Remark 4.5
.

The requirement that
\varphi(u)
be monic can be relaxed by simply requiring the leading coefficient
\alpha_{m}
of
\varphi
to be invertible. In that case, the proposition is applied to the monic polynomial
{\alpha_{m}}^{-1}\varphi(u)
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\varphi(u)
be a complex zeon polynomial of degree
m\geq 1
. If
f_{\varphi}(z)
is a nonconstant complex polynomial whose zeros are all simple, then
\varphi(u)
has exactly
m
complex zeon zeros. In this case, we say
\varphi
splits over
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.4
,
\varphi
has a zero of the form
w=w_{0}+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}w
, where
w_{0}\neq 0
is a zero of the complex polynomial
f_{\varphi}(z)
. If the zeros of
f_{\varphi}(z)
are simple, then there exist
m
such zeros.
∎

4.1.1
Computing spectrally simple zeon zeros

Presented here is an algorithm for finding a spectrally simple zeon zero
\lambda
of polynomial
\varphi(u)
.

input :
Zeon polynomial
\varphi(u)
over
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
and a simple nonzero root
\lambda_{0}
of the associated complex polynomial
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\varphi(u))
.

output :
Zeon zero
\lambda
of
\varphi(u)
with
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda=\lambda_{0}
.

Initialize complex polynomial
g(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)
.

g(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)\leftarrow\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\varphi(u))}{\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u-\lambda_{0}}
;

Note
g(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)
satisfies
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\varphi(u))=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u-\lambda_{0})g(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}u)
, where

.

\xi\leftarrow\,\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda_{0})_{\natural}/g(\lambda_{0})
;

\lambda\leftarrow\lambda_{0}-\xi
;

while

0<\natural\xi\leq n

do

\xi\leftarrow\,\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda)_{\natural}/g(\lambda_{0})
;

\lambda\leftarrow(\lambda-\xi)
;
end while

return

\lambda
;

Algorithm 1

Compute spectrally simple zeon zero.

Example 4.7
.

Consider the following zeon polynomial
\varphi(u)
over
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{4}
:

\displaystyle\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u^{4}-6u^{3}+\left(-\zeta_{\{1,2\}}-\zeta_{\{1,3\}}-\zeta_{\{1,4\}}+12\right)u^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(2\zeta_{\{1,2\}}+2\zeta_{\{1,3\}}+2\zeta_{\{1,4\}}-10\right)u+3-\zeta_{\{1,2\}}-\zeta_{\{1,3\}}-\zeta_{\{1,4\}}.

The associated complex polynomial is
f_{\varphi}(z)=z^{4}-6z^{3}+12z^{2}-10z+3
, which has zeros 3 (of multiplicity 1) and 1 (of multiplicity 3). The unique complex zeon zero
\lambda
such that
\lambda_{0}=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\lambda)=3
is

\lambda=3+\frac{1}{2}\left(\zeta_{\{1,2\}}+\zeta_{\{1,3\}}+\zeta_{\{1,4\}}\right).

Multiple and Non-spectrally Simple Zeon Zeros

Given any multiple or non-spectrally simple zeon zero
w
of
\varphi
, there are infinitely many zeon zeros
v
such that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}v=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}w
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\varphi(u)
be a monic polynomial over
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
with zeon zero
w\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
such that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}w
is a multiple zero of
f_{\varphi}
. Then,

\varphi
has infinitely many zeros
v
satisfying
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}v=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}w
.

Proof.

Suppose
\varphi
has a complex zeon zeros
w=\rho+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}w
such that
\varphi(u)=(u-w)g(u)
, where
g(u)
is a nonzero zeon polynomial satisfying
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}g(\rho)=0
. It follows immediately that
g(\rho+\lambda)\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ}
is nilpotent for any nilpotent
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ}
. Recalling that
\zeta_{[n]}=\zeta_{\{1,2,\ldots,n\}}=\zeta_{1}\cdots\zeta_{n}
, it thereby follows that for any nonzero scalar
a
,

\displaystyle\varphi(w+a\zeta_{[n]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(w+a\zeta_{[n]}-w)g(w+a\zeta_{[n]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\zeta_{[n]}g(\rho+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}w+a\zeta_{[n]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

∎

Remark 4.9
.

It is worth noting that Theorem

5
Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and the Characteristic Polynomial

Given a square matrix
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
, the
characteristic polynomial
of
A
is defined in the usual way as the zeon polynomial
\chi_{A}(t)=|t\mathbb{I}-A|
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
. The
zeon eigenvalues
of
A
are defined to be the spectrally simple zeros of the characteristic polynomial
\chi_{A}(t)
.

The fact that the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem holds for matrices over arbitrary commutative rings is well known and can be deduced from the “usual” formulation for matrices over fields.

Theorem 5.2

(Cayley-Hamilton Theorem)
.

Let
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
. Then,
A
satisfies its own characteristic equation; i.e.,

\chi_{A}(A)=\mathbf{0}.

It is easy to verify that if
\lambda
is a zeon eigenvalue of
A
, then
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda
is a complex eigenvalue of
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A
. The next lemma is an immediate consequence.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
A
is invertible, then all eigenvalues of
A
are invertible.

Proof.

If
A
is invertible, then all eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A
are nonzero. Hence, the scalar part of any zero of
\chi_{A}(u)
must be nonzero.
∎

The following theorem is an immediate corollary of Theorem
4.4
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
. If
\chi_{\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A}(u)
has simple complex zero
u_{0}
, then
A
has a unique zeon eigenvalue
\lambda
satisfying
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda=u_{0}
.

5.1
Zeon eigenvectors

With zeon eigenvalues in hand, attention now turns to eigenvectors. To begin, a few remarks concerning linear independence of vectors in
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
are in order. A subset
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
of
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
is said to be
linearly independent
if and only if for all coefficients
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
,

\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}v_{k}=\mathbf{0}

implies
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\cdots=\alpha_{k}=0
.

Note that for any nonzero
v\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
, the singleton
\{v\}
can only be linearly independent if it has at least one invertible component. That is,
\{v\}
linearly independent implies
v\notin({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ})^{m}
; equivalently,
\{v\}
is linearly independent if and only if
\zeta_{[n]}v\neq\mathbf{0}
.

Definition 5.5
.

Given a matrix
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
, a nonzero
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
is said to be a
zeon eigenvector
of
A
if the following are satisfied:

i.

there exists a zeon eigenvalue
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}
such that
A\xi=\lambda\xi
, and

ii.

\xi\notin({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ})^{m}
, i.e., at least one component of
\xi
is invertible.

Note that the requirement
\xi\notin({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m})^{\circ}
is necessary for linear independence of zeon eigenvectors.

Next, suppose
\xi
is a zeon eigenvector associated with zeon eigenvalue
\lambda
and let
f(t)=a_{k}t^{k}+a_{k-1}t^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{1}t+a_{0}
be an arbitrary zeon polynomial. The zeon matrix evaluation of
f
then satisfies

\displaystyle f(A)\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{k}A^{k}+a_{k-1}A^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{1}A+a_{0}\mathbb{I})\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{k}\lambda^{k}+a_{k-1}\lambda^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{1}\lambda+a_{0})\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\lambda)\xi.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
. If
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
is a spectrally simple zero of
\chi_{A}(u)
, then there exists
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}\setminus({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ})^{m}
such that
A\xi=\lambda\xi
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda
is a spectrally simple zero of the characteristic polynomial
\chi_{A}(u)
, it follows immediately that reducing
\lambda\mathbb{I}-A
to upper triangular form (via Gaussian elimination), one finds
m-1
invertible elements along the main diagonal. It follows immediately that the last row consists of all zeros, giving one free variable in a system of equations. It thereby follows that for each value of this free variable, the solutions of the remaining equations can be uniquely determined. Any such resulting vector serves as a basis for the corresponding eigenspace.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
. If
\lambda
is a zeon eigenvalue of
A
associated with zeon eigenvector
\xi
, then
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A
associated with eigenvector
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi
.

Proof.

Expanding
A\xi=\lambda\xi
as

(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}A)(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\xi)=(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\lambda)(\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\xi),

one finds that

\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}A\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}A\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\xi=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\lambda\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\xi+\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\lambda\mathfrak{D}\mspace{1mu}\xi.

Since
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ}
is an ideal, it is easy to see that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi
and
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi
are the only terms of the equation that exist in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. All other terms are elements of
({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ})^{m}
. Hence, the result:
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\xi
.
∎

6
The Zeon Spectral Theorem

In this section, linear independence of eigenvectors associated with distinct zeon eigenvalues is established and utilized to construct rank-one zeon projection operators. These operators are used to construct a resolution of the identity and, ultimately, to establish a spectral theorem for zeon matrices.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
. Let
v_{1},v_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
be zeon eigenvectors of
A
associated with zeon eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, respectively, where
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda_{1}\neq\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda_{2}
. Then,
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are linearly independent.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, that for some nonzero constants
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\alpha_{2}v_{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\alpha_{2}v_{2})\lambda_{1}.

(6.1)

Further, linearity of
A
implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=A(\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\alpha_{2}v_{2})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\lambda_{1}v_{2}+\alpha_{2}\lambda_{2}v_{2}.

(6.2)

Subtracting (
6.2
) from (
6.1
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})v_{2}.

Note that since
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are spectrally simple by Definition
5.1
, it follows that
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\neq 0
. Hence,
\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}
is invertible and not a zero divisor.
Hence,
\alpha_{2}v_{2}=\mathbf{0}
, which further implies
\alpha_{1}v_{1}=\mathbf{0}
by (
6.1
) so that
v_{1},v_{2}\in({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ})^{m}
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Note that the only zeon eigenvalue that can be associated with the nullspace of matrix
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
is zero. If
\lambda v=0
for nonzero
\lambda
, then
\lambda
is nilpotent and
v\in({\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{\circ})^{m}
, violating the requirement that an individual eigenvector be linearly independent.

Remark 6.3
.

In light of Theorem
4.8
and Corollary, the zeon eigenvalues of a matrix are restricted to spectrally simple zeros of the matrix’s characteristic polynomial. Suppose
v
is an eigenvector of
X
associated with distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
satisfying
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda_{1}=\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda_{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle Xv=\lambda_{1}v

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}v

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})v=\mathbf{0},

contradicting linear independence of
v
.

Resolution of the Identity

Two vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
are said to be
orthogonal
if and only if
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle=0
. Note that any collection of zeon vectors
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
spanning
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
can be orthogonalized via Gaussian elimination. Normalizing then yields an orthonormal basis for
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
be a normalized zeon vector. The matrix
vv^{\dagger}
represents orthogonal projection onto
\spn(\{v\})
.

Proof.

Given normalized
v
, let
\{u_{1},\ldots u_{m-1}\}
be an orthonormalized collection of zeon vectors orthogonal to
v
. Letting
x\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
be arbitrary, there exist zeon coefficients
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{m-1}
such that

x=\alpha_{0}v+\alpha_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{m-1}u_{m-1}.

It follows that

\displaystyle(vv^{\dagger})x

\displaystyle=vv^{\dagger}(\alpha_{0}v+\alpha_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{m-1}u_{m-1})

\displaystyle=v\alpha_{0}v^{\dagger}v+\alpha_{1}v^{\dagger}u_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{m-1}v^{\dagger}u_{m-1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}\langle v,v\rangle v+\alpha_{1}\langle u_{1},v\rangle v+\cdots+\alpha_{m-1}\langle u_{m-1},v\rangle v

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}v.

∎

It follows that when
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
is an orthonormalized collection of zeon vectors, a
resolution of the identity
is given by

\mathbb{I}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}{u_{j}}^{\dagger}.

A matrix
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
satisfying
A^{\dagger}=A
is clearly self-adjoint w.r.t. the inner product (
2.1
) because for arbitrary
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\langle Ax,y\rangle=y^{\dagger}(Ax)=y^{\dagger}(x^{\dagger}A^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=y^{\dagger}(x^{\dagger}A)^{\dagger}=y^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}x

\displaystyle=(Ay)^{\dagger}x

\displaystyle=\langle x,Ay\rangle.

Eigenvalues of self-adjoint zeon matrices are elements of the real zeon algebra
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
; i.e.,
\chi_{A}(\lambda)=0
implies
\lambda=\overline{\lambda}
when
A
is self-adjoint. Further, eigenvectors of self-adjoint zeon matrices associated with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Given distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
associated with eigenvectors
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, respectively,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Av_{1},v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v_{1},Av_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle

implies
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle=0
.

Remark 6.5
.

Consider zeon eigenvalues
\lambda
of
A
, where
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue of
\mathfrak{C}\mspace{1mu}A
.
Simple zeros give spectrally simple zeon eigenvalues. An
m\times m
matrix with
m
distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.

Theorem 6.6

(Zeon Spectral Theorem)
.

Let
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
be a self-adjoint zeon matrix with
m
spectrally simple eigenvalues. Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
denote normalized zeon eigenvectors associated with these eigenvalues and set
\pi_{j}=v_{j}{v_{j}}^{\dagger}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Then,

A=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\pi_{j}.

Proof.

Assuming that
A
is self-adjoint with spectrally simple eigenvalues, it follows that the corresponding normalized eigenvectors
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
are orthogonal, resulting in an orthonormal zeon basis of
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
. For arbitrary
z\in{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
, it follows that

\displaystyle Az

\displaystyle=A\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle z,v_{j}\rangle v_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\langle z,v_{j}\rangle v_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\pi_{j}z

\displaystyle=\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\pi_{j}\right)z.

∎

.

Consider the self-adjoint zeon matrix

7
Conclusion and Avenues for Further Research

Viewing a matrix
M\in{\rm Mat}(m,{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}})
as a linear operator on the
\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}
-module
{\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}}^{m}
, zeon eigenvalues of
M
have been defined as the spectrally simple zeon zeros of the characteristic polynomial of
M
. Further, essential notions of zeon eigenvectors and linear independence have been established and used to define orthogonal projections and a resolution of the identity.

Finally, it has been shown that given a self-adjoint zeon matrix
A\in{\rm Mat}(m,\mathbb{C}\mathfrak{Z}_{n})
having
m
spectrally simple eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
associated with normalized zeon eigenvectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
, the following holds:

A=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\pi_{j},

where
\pi_{j}=v_{j}{v_{j}}^{\dagger}
is an orthogonal projection onto
\spn\{v_{j}\}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

Looking toward future work, a number of promising avenues become apparent. Two are briefly described here.

Spectral properties of the zeon combinatorial Laplacian

It was shown in
[
16
]
that the zeon combinatorial Laplacian of a simple graph with no isolated vertices is always invertible and that the entries of the inverse matrix represent enumeration of paths in the graph. Spectral properties of the zeon combinatorial Laplacian are therefore of particular interest due to their potential combinatorial applications in graph theory and computer science.

Putzer’s method for evaluating matrix exponentials

Putzer’s method for analytically evaluating matrix exponentials uses only eigenvalues and components of the solution of a relatively simple linear system. The method is characterized by the following theorem
[
10
]
.

Theorem 7.1

(Putzer)
.

Let
A
be a square matrix of order
n
, and let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the (not necessarily distinct) eigenvalues of
A
in some arbitrary but specified order. Then

e^{At}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r_{j+1}(t)P_{j},

(7.1)

where
P_{0}=\mathbb{I}
and
P_{j}
is defined for
j=2,\ldots,n
by

P_{j}=\prod_{k=1}^{j}(A-\lambda_{k}\mathbb{I}).

(7.2)

Here,
r_{1}(t),\ldots,r_{n}(t)
is the solution of the triangular system

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}r_{1}

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{j}

\displaystyle=r_{j-1}+\lambda_{j}r_{j}

\displaystyle(j=2,\ldots,n)

with initial values
r_{1}(0)=1
and
r_{j}(0)=0
for
j=2,\ldots,n
.

It is not obvious that Putzer’s method extends to the case of zeon matrices, but it is worth noting that the number of terms contained in Putzer’s expansion corresponds to the degree of the minimal polynomial of the associated matrix, as opposed to an infinite series expansion.
