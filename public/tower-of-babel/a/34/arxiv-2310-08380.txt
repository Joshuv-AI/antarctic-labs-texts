Left adjoint to precomposition in elementary doctrines
Francesca Guffanti

Abstract
It is well-known in universal algebra that adding structure and equational axioms generates forgetful functors between varieties, and such functors all have left adjoints. The category of elementary doctrines provides a natural framework for studying algebraic theories, since each algebraic theory can be described by some syntactic doctrine and its models are homomorphism from the syntactic doctrine into the doctrine of subsets. In this context, adding structure and axioms to a theory can be described by a homomorphism between the two corresponding syntactic doctrines, and the forgetful functor arises as precomposition with this last homomorphism. In this work, given any homomorphism of elementary doctrines, we prove the existence of a left adjoint of the functor induced by precomposition in the doctrine of subobjects of a Grothendieck topos.



1
Introduction

In universal algebra, adding structure or equational axioms is a widely used technique: classical results say that for a given category of algebraic structure—e.g. monoids—, adding some structure or axioms—e.g. groups, commutative monoids—defines a forgetful functor from the new category to the original one, with a left adjoint. The 2-category
\mathbf{ED}
of elementary doctrines provides a natural framework for studying algebraic theories, with each theory
\mathbb{T}
for a particular algebraic language
{\Sigma}
described by some doctrine of formulae
\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}
.
From a categorical point of view, every variety is equivalent to a category of homomorphisms of elementary doctrines
\mathbf{ED}(\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}},\mathop{\mathscr{P}\kern-4.73611pt_{\ast}})
between a doctrine of formulae and the subsets doctrine
\mathop{\mathscr{P}\kern-4.73611pt_{\ast}}:\textnormal{Set}_{\ast}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
. Moreover, adding structure and equational axioms translates to a doctrine homomorphism
(E,\mathfrak{e}):\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}\to\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma^{\prime}}_{\mathbb{T^{\prime}}}
. Precomposition with this homomorphism induces a functor
\mathrm{-}\circ(E,\mathfrak{e}):\mathbf{ED}(\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma^{\prime}}_{\mathbb{T^{\prime}}},\mathop{\mathscr{P}\kern-4.73611pt_{\ast}})\to\mathbf{ED}(\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}},\mathop{\mathscr{P}\kern-4.73611pt_{\ast}})
, and it represents the forgetful functor between the correspondent varieties, hence it has a left adjoint.

In this work we extend this classical result in
\mathbf{ED}
by considering the subobject doctrine
\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}:\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
from a Grothendieck topos
\mathbb{E}
instead of the doctrine of subsets, and precomposition with any homomorphism
(F,\mathfrak{f}):P\to R
instead of the forgetful functor. The whole article is dedicated to the proof that also in this case the functor

\mathrm{-}\circ(F,\mathfrak{f}):\mathbf{ED}(R,\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})\to\mathbf{ED}(P,\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})

has a left adjoint, showing how the existence of free functors in universal algebra follows from a more general result that lives in the theory of elementary doctrines.

2
Elementary doctrines

We start by recalling the language of elementary doctrines introduced in
[
MR13
,
MR15
,
MPR17
]
, as a generalization of Lawvere’s hyperdoctrine
[
Law69a
,
Law69b
,
Law70
]
. While in general, doctrines are a way to generalize the posets of well-formed formulae ordered by provable consequence, the particular context of elementary doctrines is a suitable one in which we can interpret conjunctions and equality of formulae. We define the 2-category
\mathbf{ED}
of elementary doctrines, which will be the main protagonists of this work, and show some relevant examples.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{C}
be a category with finite products and let
\mathbf{Pos}
be the category of partially-ordered sets and monotone functions. A
primary doctrine
is a functor
P:\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
such that for each object
A
in
\mathbb{C}
, the poset
P(A)
has finite meets, and the related operations
\land:P\times P\xrightarrow{\cdot}P
and
\top:\mathbf{1}\xrightarrow{\cdot}P
yield natural transformations. The category
\mathbb{C}
is called
base category of
P
, each poset
P(X)
for an object
X\in\mathbb{C}
is called
fiber
, the function
P(f)
for an arrow
f
in
\mathbb{C}
is called
reindexing
.

We recall the definition of elementary doctrines, which are—informally speaking—doctrines in which we can interpret equality. The original definition of equality for hyperdoctrines was given by Lawvere in
[
Law70
]
: for any object
X
in the base category the equality is defined as
\Sigma_{\Delta_{X}}(\top_{X})
, where
\Sigma_{\Delta_{X}}:P(X)\to P(X\times X)
is the left adjoint of the reindexing
P(\Delta_{X}):P(X\times X)\to P(X)
of the usual diagonal map
\Delta_{X}:X\to X\times X
. Since Lawvere’s definition of doctrines with equality, there have been many equivalent definitions up to date—some can be found in
[
MR12
,
MR13
]
. The one we chose to deal with in this work is taken from the characterization in Proposition 2.5 of
[
EPR20
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

A primary doctrine
P:\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
is
elementary
if for any object
A
in
\mathbb{C}
there exists an element
\delta_{A}\in P(A\times A)
such that:

1.

\top_{A}\leq P(\Delta_{A})(\delta_{A})
;

2.

P(A)=\mathscr{D}es_{\delta_{A}}:=\{\alpha\in P(A)\mid P(\mathrm{pr}_{1})(\alpha)\land\delta_{A}\leq P(\mathrm{pr}_{2})(\alpha)\}
;

3.

\delta_{A}\boxtimes\delta_{B}\leq\delta_{A\times B}
, where
\delta_{A}\boxtimes\delta_{B}=P(\langle\mathrm{pr}_{1},\mathrm{pr}_{3}\rangle)(\delta_{A})\land P(\langle\mathrm{pr}_{2},\mathrm{pr}_{4}\rangle)(\delta_{B})
.

In 2.,
\mathrm{pr}_{1}
and
\mathrm{pr}_{2}
are the projections from
A\times A
in
A
; in 3., the projections are from
A\times B\times A\times B
. The element
\delta_{A}
will be called
fibered equality
on
A
.

Example 2.3
.

1.

The functor
\mathscr{P}:\textnormal{Set}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}

Definition 2.4
.

An
elementary doctrine homomorphism
—1-cell or 1-arrow—between two elementary doctrines
P:\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
and
R:\mathbb{D}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}

(F,\mathfrak{f})
where

\mathfrak{f}:P\xrightarrow{\cdot}R\circ F^{\mathrm{op}}

\mathfrak{f}_{A}(\alpha\land_{A}\alpha^{\prime})=\mathfrak{f}_{A}(\alpha)\land_{FA}\mathfrak{f}(\alpha^{\prime});\qquad\qquad\mathfrak{f}_{A}(\top_{A})=\top_{FA};\qquad\qquad\mathfrak{f}_{A\times A}(\delta_{A})=\delta_{FA}.

P
and
R

P
in

in
\mathbb{C}
and

. Elementary doctrines, elementary doctrine homomorphisms with 2-cells defined here form a
2
-category, that will be denoted

.

.

For a given category

with finite limits, the inclusion of

The definition of the left adjoint functor

Fix in the category
\mathbf{ED}
of elementary doctrines a homomorphism
(F,\mathfrak{f})
between two elementary doctrines
P
and

to obtain a homomorphism

\mathrm{-}\circ(F,\mathfrak{f}):\mathbf{ED}(R,\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})\to\mathbf{ED}(P,\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}).

.

\mathbb{E}
be a Grothendieck topos and
\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}:\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
be the subobject doctrine. Then, the functor induced by precomposition

\mathrm{-}\circ(F,\mathfrak{f}):\mathbf{ED}(R,\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})\to\mathbf{ED}(P,\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})

has a left adjoint.

Before we continue, we need
\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H
to be product preserving.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D},\mathbb{E}
be categories with finite products such that
\mathbb{C}
is small and
\mathbb{E}
is cocomplete and cartesian closed, and let
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}
and
H:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{E}
be finite product preserving functors. Then
\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{E}
preserves finite products.

This is a classical result in the theory of Kan extension that can be shown in different ways, see for instance Proposition 2.5 of
[
KL93
]
, or
[
KP12
]
.

If
\mathbb{E}
is a Grothendieck topos, the hypothesis of the proposition above are satisfied, so
\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H
preserves finite products.

Define now a natural transformation
\mathfrak{l}:R\xrightarrow{\cdot}\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)^{\mathrm{op}}
. For any object
D\in\mathbb{D}
, and any
\gamma\in R(D)
, write

\mathfrak{l}_{D}(\gamma)=\bigwedge_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}\widehat{\theta}_{D}^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma))

where
(K,\mathfrak{k}):R\to\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}
is an arrow in
\mathbf{ED}
and
\theta:(H,\mathfrak{h})\to(K,\mathfrak{k})(F,\mathfrak{f})
is a 2-arrow, i.e.
\mathfrak{h}_{A}(\alpha)\leq\theta^{*}_{A}(\mathfrak{k}_{FA}(\mathfrak{f}_{A}(\alpha)))
for all
A\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in P(A)
. Observe that
\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma)
is a subobject of
KD
,
\widehat{\theta}
is defined by the universal property of the left Kan extension, and
\widehat{\theta}_{D}^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma))
is the pullback of
\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma)
along
\widehat{\theta}_{D}:(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)(D)\to KD
, hence it is a subobject of
(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)(D)
. Since
\mathbb{E}
is a complete category, the infimum of
\{\widehat{\theta}_{D}^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma))\}_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}
exists, and we call it
\mathfrak{l}_{D}(\gamma)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The following properties hold:

1.

\mathfrak{l}:R\xrightarrow{\cdot}\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)^{\mathrm{op}}
is a natural transformation;

2.

\mathfrak{l}:R\xrightarrow{\cdot}\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)^{\mathrm{op}}
preserves finite meets;

3.

\mathfrak{l}_{D\times D}(\delta_{D})\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}\big((\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)(D)\times(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)(D)\big)
is an equivalence relation for any object
D\in\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

1.

Take an arrow
g:D^{\prime}\to D
in
\mathbb{D}
, we prove that
\big((\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)(g)\big)^{*}\mathfrak{l}_{D}(\gamma)=\mathfrak{l}_{D^{\prime}}R(g)(\gamma)
for any
\gamma\in RD
:

\displaystyle\big((\textstyle{\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H})(g)\big)^{*}\mathfrak{l}_{D}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\big((\textstyle{\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H})(g)\big)^{*}\left(\bigwedge_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}\widehat{\theta}_{D}^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma))\right)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}\big((\textstyle{\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H})(g)\big)^{*}\widehat{\theta}_{D}^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma))

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}\widehat{\theta}_{D^{\prime}}^{*}\big(K(g)\big)^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\gamma))

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}\widehat{\theta}_{D^{\prime}}^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D^{\prime}}R(g)(\gamma))=\mathfrak{l}_{D^{\prime}}(R(g)\gamma).

Note that the second equality follows from the fact that pullback functors between subobjects categories preserve arbitrary limits—since in a regular categories they have a left adjoint—; the other equalities follow from naturality of
\widehat{\theta}
and
\mathfrak{k}
.

2.

The top element
\top_{D}\in RD
for any object
D\in\mathbb{D}
is preserved by
\mathfrak{l}_{D}
since
\mathfrak{k}_{D}(\top_{D})
is
\OPRid_{KD}:KD\to KD
the top element in
\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}(KD)
by assumption, and its pullback along any
\widehat{\theta}_{D}
is the identity of
(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)(D)
. Similarly,
\mathfrak{l}_{D}
preserves binary meets since any
\mathfrak{k}_{D}
and any pullback functor do.

3.

Compute

\mathfrak{l}_{D\times D}(\delta_{D})=\bigwedge_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}\widehat{\theta}_{D\times D}^{*}(\mathfrak{k}_{D\times D}(\delta_{D}))=\bigwedge_{(K,\mathfrak{k}),\theta}{(\widehat{\theta}_{D}\times\widehat{\theta}_{D})}^{*}(\Delta_{KD}).

Note that each
{(\widehat{\theta}_{D}\times\widehat{\theta}_{D})}^{*}(\Delta_{KD})
is an equivalence relation on
(\mathop{\mathrm{Lan}}_{F}H)(D)
, since it is the kernel pair of the map
\widehat{\theta}_{D}
. So
\mathfrak{l}_{D\times D}(\delta_{D})
is an equivalence relation itself, being the infimum of equivalence relations.∎

Before we go on, we recall from Section 4 of
[
MR12
]
the
elementary quotient completion
of an elementary doctrine. Given any elementary doctrine
P:\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
one can build the category
\mathcal{R}_{P}
of equivalence relations of
P
: objects are pairs
(A,\rho)
, where
\rho\in P(A\times A)
is a
P
-equivalence relation on

—meaning that it satisfies reflexivity
\delta_{A}\leq_{A\times A}\rho
, symmetry
\rho\leq_{A\times A}P(\langle\mathrm{pr}_{2},\mathrm{pr}_{1}\rangle)(\rho)
and transitivity
P(\langle\mathrm{pr}_{1},\mathrm{pr}_{2}\rangle)(\rho)\land P(\langle\mathrm{pr}_{2},\mathrm{pr}_{3}\rangle)(\rho)\leq_{A\times A\times A}P(\langle\mathrm{pr}_{1},\mathrm{pr}_{3}\rangle)(\rho)
; arrows
f:(A,\rho)\to(B,\sigma)
in
\mathcal{R}_{P}
are arrows
f:A\to B
such that
\rho\leq_{A\times A}P(f\times f)(\sigma)
. Composition and identities are computed in
\mathbb{C}
. The product in the category
\mathcal{R}_{P}
of a pair of objects
(A,\rho)
and
(B,\sigma)
is
(A\times B,\rho\boxtimes\sigma)
, together with the projections from
A\times B
to
A
and
B
. Then, the elementary quotient completion
(P)_{q}:\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
will be given by

, and
(P)_{q}(f)=P(f)
. Finite meets in
\mathscr{D}es_{\rho}
are computed in
P(A)
. The fibered equality in
(P)_{q}\big((A,\rho)\times(A,\rho)\big)=\mathscr{D}es_{\rho\boxtimes\rho}
is
\rho
itself. In particular
(P)_{q}
is an elementary doctrine.

The elementary quotient completion comes with a 1-arrow
(J,j):P\to(P)_{q}
in
\mathbf{ED}
, where the functor
J:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{R}_{P}
maps a homomorphism

to

, and each component

is the identity of
P(A)
.

A
P
-quotient of a
P
-equivalence relation
\rho
on
A
is a homomorphism

in
\mathbb{C}
such that
\rho\leq P(q\times q)(\delta_{C})
and, for every homomorphism

, there exists a unique homomorphism
h:C\to B
such that
g=hq
. A homomorphism

in
\mathbb{C}
is descent if the functor

is full.

Then, let

be the 2-full 2-subcategory of

whose objects are elementary doctrines

-quotient that is a descent homomorphism; the 1-arrows are those arrows

Before we give some examples, we prove a general result for some first-order theories. This generalizes Example 2.5.a of
[
MR12
]
.

We refer to
[
Car17
]
for the definitions about first-order calculus. We slightly change the doctrine of well-formed formulae of Example
2.3
considering just the fragment of Horn logic. Moreover, a theory in this context is not a set of closed formulae, but is instead a set of Horn sequents over
\Sigma
. We write in this case
\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}:\mathrm{\mathbb{C}tx}_{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
for the elementary doctrine of Horn formulae: the base category is the same defined in Example
2.3
, each list of variable is sent to the poset reflection of Horn formulae—defined inductively as the smallest set containing relations, equalities, true constant and conjunctions of formulae—ordered by provable consequence in
\mathbb{T}
; reindexing are again defined as substitutions.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
{\Sigma}
be a first-order language and
\mathbb{T}
a first-order theory in the language
{\Sigma}
such that its axioms are Horn sequents. Then there exists an equivalence of categories:

\mathbf{ED}(\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}},\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})\cong\mathop{\mathrm{Mod}}^{{\Sigma}}_{\mathbb{T}}

where
\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}:\mathrm{\mathbb{C}tx}_{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
is the elementary doctrine of Horn formulae in the language
{\Sigma}
of the theory
{\mathbb{T}}
,
\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Pos}
is the elementary doctrine of subobject for a given category
\mathbb{C}
with finite limits, and
\mathop{\mathrm{Mod}}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}
is the category whose objects are models of the theory
\mathbb{T}
in the category
\mathbb{C}
, and whose arrows are
{\Sigma}
-homomorphism.

Proof.

Since there is no confusion, we write
\mathtt{HF}
instead of
\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Mod}}
instead of
\mathop{\mathrm{Mod}}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}
.
For any given
\theta:(H,\mathfrak{h})\to(H^{\prime},\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})
in
\mathbf{ED}(\mathtt{HF},\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})
, define
\theta_{(x)}:H(x)\to H^{\prime}(x)
.

Observe that
H(x)
is indeed a model of the theory
\mathbb{T}
: each
n
-ary function symbol
f
in the language defines an arrow
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\to(x)
in
\mathrm{\mathbb{C}tx}
, hence its image through
H
—that preserves products—defines a map
f^{H}:\big(H(x)\big)^{n}\to H(x)
, which is the interpretation of
f
in
H(x)
; each
n
-ary predicate symbol
R
defines
R^{H}=\mathfrak{h}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}(R(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})):\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}(R^{H})\rightarrowtail\big(H(x)\big)^{n}
. From now on, we write
\vec{x}
instead of the list
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.

Satisfiability of axioms follows by the fact that
\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}(\alpha(\vec{x}))
is the interpretation of
\alpha
in
H(x)
for each
\alpha(\vec{x})\in\mathtt{HF}(\vec{x})
, and
\mathfrak{h}
is monotone, so if we have an axiom
\alpha(\vec{x})\vdash\beta(\vec{x})
in
\mathbb{T}
we have
\alpha^{H}\leq\beta^{H}
, i.e.
H(x)
satisfies
\alpha\vdash\beta
. To check that
\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}(\alpha(\vec{x}))=\alpha^{H}
we work recursively on the complexity of
\alpha
:

•

\alpha=\top
:
\alpha^{H}=\OPRid_{\big(H(x)\big)^{n}}=\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}(\top)
trivially holds;

•

\alpha=R(\vec{x})
:
\alpha^{H}=R^{H}=\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}(R)
by the definition given above;

•

\alpha=\alpha_{1}\land\alpha_{2}
:
\alpha^{H}=\alpha^{H}_{1}\land\alpha^{H}_{2}=\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}(\alpha_{1})\land\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}(\alpha_{2})=\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}(\alpha_{1}\land\alpha_{2})
since
\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}
preserves meets;

•

\alpha=\big(t_{1}(\vec{x})=t_{2}(\vec{x})\big)
:
\alpha^{H}=\text{Eq}(t_{1}^{H},t_{2}^{H})
, the equalizer of the interpretations
t_{1}^{H},t_{2}^{H}
of the terms
t_{1},t_{2}
in
H(x)
.

It is left to prove then that if
\alpha=\big(t_{1}(\vec{x})=t_{2}(\vec{x})\big)
, we have
\alpha^{H}=\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}\big(t_{1}(\vec{x})=t_{2}(\vec{x})\big)
. Naturality of
\mathfrak{h}
with respect to the arrow
(t_{1}(\vec{x}),t_{2}(\vec{x})):\vec{x}\to(y_{1},y_{2})
in
\mathrm{\mathbb{C}tx}
, applied to the formula
\big(y_{1}=y_{2}\big)\in\mathtt{HF}(y_{1},y_{2})
gives:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathtt{HF}(y_{1},y_{2})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}((H(x))^{2})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathtt{HF}(\vec{x})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}}((H(x))^{n})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vec{t}(\vec{x})/\vec{y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{h}_{(y_{1},y_{2})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\langle t_{1}^{H},t_{2}^{H}\rangle^{*}}

in order to get:

\mathfrak{h}_{\vec{x}}\big(t_{1}(\vec{x})=t_{2}(\vec{x})\big)=\langle t_{1}^{H},t_{2}^{H}\rangle^{*}(\Delta_{H(x)})=\text{Eq}(t_{1}^{H},t_{2}^{H}),

as claimed. This proves that the association
\mathbf{ED}(\mathtt{HF},\mathop{\mathrm{Sub}})\to\mathop{\mathrm{Mod}}
is well defined on objects.

Concerning arrows, first of all observe that since
H,H^{\prime}
preserve products and
\theta
is a natural transformation,
\theta_{\vec{x}}=\theta_{(x)}\times\dots\times\theta_{(x)}
—
n

\theta
with respect to an arrow defined by an
n

gives the fact that
\theta_{(x)}
preserves the interpretation of the function symbol

\theta_{(x)}f^{H}=f^{H^{\prime}}\theta_{\vec{x}}=f^{H^{\prime}}(\theta_{(x)}\times\dots\times\theta_{(x)});

moreover, for any
n
-ary predicate symbol

so that

Example 4.5

Some quasi-algebraic examples.

Suppose we have an algebraic language
{\Sigma}

Example 4.6
.

Some non-algebraic example.

Let
{\Sigma}
be a first-order language with a binary relation
R
and
\mathbb{T}
a theory such that the only axiom in
\mathbb{T}
are reflexivity, transitivity, symmetry or antisymmetry.

We can easily recover some adjunction by adding axioms—of the kind defined above—to the theory, in the same way we did for the algebraic case: for example we can find the left adjoint to the forgetful functor from the category of sets with an equivalence relation to the category of sets with a reflexive and symmetric relation, or from the category of sets with a preorder to the category of sets with an order, and so on.

A little more work must be done to recover the adjunction between the category of posets and inf-semilattices. Recall that any inf-semilattice is a poset defining that an element is smaller than another one if their meet is the first element, so there is a forgetful functor from inf-semilattices to posets. This forgetful functor arises again from a precomposition between the doctrines of Horn formulae: take the language
{\Sigma}
with a binary predicate symbol, and a theory
\mathbb{T}
with axioms of reflexivity, transitivity and antisymmetry; then take the algebraic language
{\Sigma^{\prime}}
with a constant symbol
\top
and a binary function symbol

, and the algebraic theory
\mathbb{T^{\prime}}
that defines inf-semilattices. The functor
E:\mathrm{\mathbb{C}tx}_{{\Sigma}}\to\mathrm{\mathbb{C}tx}_{{\Sigma^{\prime}}}
maps projections in projections and is extended to lists of projections;
\mathfrak{e}_{\vec{x}}:\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{T}}(\vec{x})\to\mathtt{HF}^{\Sigma^{\prime}}_{\mathbb{T}^{\prime}}(\vec{x})
is defined recursively: the top element, equalities of variables and conjunctions are sent to themselves, while the formula

is sent to the formula
\big(\sqcap(x_{i},x_{j})=x_{i}\big)
.
