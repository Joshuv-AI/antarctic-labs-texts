Certified Hermite Matrices from Approximate Roots
Tulay Ayyildiz Akoglu, Agnes Szanto

Abstract
Let ℐ = ⟨ f 1 , … , f m ⟩ ⊂ ℚ ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] \mathcal{I}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}] be a zero dimensional radical ideal defined by polynomials given with exact rational coefficients. Assume that we are given approximations { z 1 , … , z k } ⊂ ℂ n \{z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n} for the common roots { ξ 1 , … , ξ k } = V ⁡ ( ℐ ) ⊆ ℂ n \{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}\}=V(\mathcal{I})\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{n} . In this paper we show how to construct and certify the rational entries of Hermite matrices for ℐ \mathcal{I} from the approximate roots { z 1 , … , z k } \{z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\} . When ℐ \mathcal{I} is non-radical, we give methods to construct and certify Hermite matrices for ℐ \sqrt{\mathcal{I}} from approximate roots. Furthermore, we use signatures of these Hermite matrices to give rational certificates of non-negativity of a given polynomial over a (possibly positive dimensional) real variety, as well as certificates that there is a real root within an ε \varepsilon distance from a given point z ∈ ℚ n z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n} .

Introduction

The development of numerical and symbolic techniques to solve systems of polynomial equations resulted in an explosion of applicability, both in terms of the size of the systems efficiently solvable and the reliability of the output. Nonetheless, many of the results produced by numerical methods are not certified. In this paper, we show how to compute exact Hermite matrices from approximate roots of polynomials, and how to certify that these Hermite matrices are correct.

Hermite matrices and Hermite bilinear forms were introduced by Hermite in 1850
[
14
]
for univariate polynomials and were extended to the multivariate zero-dimensional setting in
[
7
,
23
]
. Hermite matrices have many applications, including
counting real roots
[
7
,
15
,
16
]
and locating them
[
4
]
. Assume that we are given the ideal
\mathcal{I}:=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
generated by rational polynomials, and assume that
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}=k<\infty
. Hermite matrices have two kinds of definitions (see the precise formulation in Section
2.1
):

1.

The first definition of Hermite matrices uses the traces of
k^{2}
multiplication matrices, each of them of size
k\times k
. The advantage of this definition is that it can be computed exactly, working with rational numbers only. The disadvantage is that it requires the computation of the traces of
k^{2}
multiplication matrices.

2.

The second definition uses symmetric functions of the
k
common roots of
{\mathcal{I}}
, counted with multiplicity.
The advantage of this definition is that it gives a very efficient way to evaluate the entries of the Hermite matrix, assuming that we know the common roots of
{\mathcal{I}}
exactly. The disadvantage is that we need to compute the common roots exactly, which may involve working in field extensions of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

In this paper we propose to use the second definition to compute Hermite matrices, but instead of using exact roots, we use approximate roots that can be computed with numerical methods efficiently
[
12
]
. Once we obtain an approximate Hermite matrix, we use rational number reconstruction to construct a matrix with rational entries of bounded denominators. Finally, we give a symbolic method which certifies that the rational Hermite matrix we computed is in fact the correct one, corresponding to the exact roots of
{\mathcal{I}}
.

The novelty of this work and the difficulty of this problem is to certify the correctness of the Hermite matrix that we computed with the above heuristic approach. This part of the algorithm is purely symbolic. The main idea is that we use the relationship between multiplication matrices and Hermite matrices to compute a system of multiplication matrices from Hermite matrices and vice versa. As
multiplication matrices, which are also rational matrices, act as roots of the polynomial system, we can certify their correctness, which in turn gives us a method to certify Hermite matrices.

Note that both of the above definitions of Hermite matrices are continuous in the presence of root multiplicities. However, our use of approximate roots and multiplication matrices necessitate that we first consider the case when
{\mathcal{I}}
is radical. To handle the non-radical case, we use the fact that the maximal non-singular submatrix of the Hermite matrix of
{\mathcal{I}}
gives the Hermite matrix of
\sqrt{I}
, so this is what we compute and certify. In both the radical and non-radical cases we were able to give sufficient conditions in terms of the quality of the root approximations and the size of the rational numbers in the Hermite matrices that guarantee that our Hermite matrix construction and certification algorithms do not fail.

Another contribution of this paper is the presentation of
two novel applications using the signature of the certified Hermite matrices. The first application is to give a rational certificate that a polynomial
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is non-negative over a smooth real variety
V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}].
This application was inspired by
[
10
]
where the authors give a method to compute a degree
d
sum of squares (SOS) decomposition (if one exists) for a non-negative polynomial over a real algebraic set using a finite set of sample points from the (complex) algebraic set. While the SOS decomposition constructed in
[
10
]
is approximate, the construction we give here is exact, using Hermite matrices with rational entries.

The second application is to give a rational certificate that for a given point
z\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
,
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{+}

and
{\mathcal{I}}:=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
zero dimensional, there exists
\xi\in V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that

\|z-\xi\|_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon.

A natural question arises about the advantage of this hybrid symbolic–numeric approach over purely symbolic methods to compute Hermite matrices. The answer to this question is not black and white, it depends on the situation. For example, one could use a purely symbolic method computing a Gröbner bases, and then computing a system of multiplication matrices of the input polynomials and then the traces of certain multiplication matrices give the entries of Hermite matrices symbolically (just as in the first definition above). Instead, in this study, we use a symbolic-numeric approach as we assume that we have an efficient and parallelizable numerical method that can compute high precision approximations to all common complex roots for
square subsystems
of
f
, i.e. for
n
random linear combinations of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
. From these approximate roots we select in a certified manner a subset
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
(given with floating point numbers as coordinates) that are approximations solutions of
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{m}
. The worst case arithmetic complexity for both the purely symbolic and the hybrid methods are similar (asymptotically bounded by
D^{n}
where
D
is the maximum of the degrees of the input polynomials). In
[
5
,
6
]
the authors give some evidence that Gröbner basis techniques for highly overdetermined systems (
m>>n
) can be highly efficient. The underlying idea behind this is that if one is given many polynomials already, there is only a little more work needed to generate a Gröbner basis. On the other hand, for systems that are square or close to square, the limited accuracy and parallelizability of the numerical approach allows to handle larger polynomial systems in practice than with purely symbolic approaches. In
[
8
]
they compare the two different approaches to computing and representing the solutions of polynomial systems: numerical homotopy continuation and symbolic computation.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Hermite Matrices

In this section we give two definitions for the Hermite matrix of a zero-dimensional ideal. The first one defines the matrix from the common roots of the ideal, and in fact can be used to define Hermite matrices from any set of points. The second definition uses the traces of multiplication matrices of the factor ring of the ideal, and thus it gives a definition where the entries of the Hermite matrices are rational functions of the coefficients of the polynomial system.

Everything in this section is valid for polynomials over
{\mathbb{R}}
, so while in the rest of the paper we assume that our input polynomials are rational, in this section we present the preliminaries over
{\mathbb{R}}
. We use the following notation. Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{m}
with
\mathcal{I}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
a zero-dimensional ideal and
\mathcal{B}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}
be a monomial basis for
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/\mathcal{I}
.

Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be the common roots of
{\mathcal{I}}
, here each root listed as many times as their multiplicity. If
{\mathcal{I}}
is radical (which we assume in most of the later sections), each root is distinct. We denote by
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
approximations to the exact roots
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k}
.

The first definition of Hermite matrices is given for any multiset of points in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
\mathcal{B}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}

be a set of monomials in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. Let

z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be points, not necessary distinct. Then the
Hermite matrix
of
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k}
with respect to
g
, written in the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
is

H_{g}^{\mathcal{B}}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k}):=V^{T}GV

(1)

where

V:=V_{\mathcal{B}}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k})=[z_{i}^{\alpha_{j}}]_{i,j=1,\ldots,k}

is the Vandermonde matrix of
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with respect to a monomial set
\mathcal{B}
and
G
is a
k\times k
diagonal matrix with
[G]_{i,i}=g(z_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,k
. We may omit
{\mathcal{B}}
from the notation if it is clear from the context.

Example 2.2
.

When
{\mathcal{B}}=\{1,x_{1},\ldots,x_{1}^{k-1}\}
and
g=g(x_{1})
is also univariate, then the Hermite matrices are the same as in the univariate case. If
z_{l}=(z_{l,1},\ldots,z_{l,n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
for
l=1,\ldots,k
then the entries of the
k\times k
univariate Hermite matrices are defined by

[H^{\mathcal{B}}_{g}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})]_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}g(z_{l,1})z_{l,1}^{i+j-2}.

In particular, for
g(x)=1
, if
z_{1,1},\ldots,z_{k,1}
all distinct, the entries of the Hermite matrix are the power sum elementary symmetric functions of the first coordinates:

[H^{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})]_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}z_{l,1}^{i+j-2}.

(2)

The second definition of Hermite matrices implies that the the entries of the Hermite matrix are rational functions of the coefficients of the defining polynomials of
{\mathcal{I}}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a zero dimensional ideal and denote
A:={\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/I
, a finite dimensional vectors space over
{\mathbb{R}}
with
k:=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}A
. For any
f\in A
let
\mu_{f}:A\rightarrow A,

p+{\mathcal{I}}\mapsto p\cdot f+{\mathcal{I}}

be the multiplication map by
f
on
A
. Fix a monomial basis
{\mathcal{B}}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}
of
A
, and denote by
M^{\mathcal{B}}_{f}
the
k\times k
matrix of
\mu_{f}
in the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
. The
Hermite matrix
of
{\mathcal{I}}
with respect to
g
, written in the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
is

H_{g}^{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{I}})=\left[{\rm Tr}(\mu_{g\cdot x^{\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}}})\right]_{i,j=1}^{k}=\left[{\rm Tr}(M^{\mathcal{B}}_{g\cdot x^{\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}}})\right]_{i,j=1}^{k},

where
{\rm Tr}
denotes both the trace of a linear transformation and the trace of a matrix (note that the trace of a linear transformation is the trace of its matrix in
any
basis). We may omit
{\mathcal{B}}
from the notation when it is clear from the context.

The next theorem asserts that the two definitions give the same matrix if we take the exact common roots of a zero dimensional polynomial system.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a zero dimensional ideal and
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Let
k:=\dim{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}
be a monomial basis for
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}
.
Let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be the roots of
{\mathcal{I}}
, each root listed as many times as their multiplicity. Then

H_{g}^{\mathcal{B}}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})=H_{g}^{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{I}}).

Remark 2.5
.

Note that if
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m},g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
then for
{\mathcal{I}}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
we have
H_{g}({\mathcal{I}})\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{k\times k}
, thus
H_{g}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{k\times k}
, even if the roots are not rational.

Next we present the classical Hermite theorem that uses the
signature
of Hermite matrices to count real roots of a real polynomial system. First we define the signature of a matrix.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
A
be a real and symmetric matrix. Then the
signature
of A is

\sigma(A):=\#\{{\rm positive~eigenvalues~of}A\}-\#\{{\rm negative~eigenvalues~of}A\}

Definition
2.3
implies that Hermite Matrices of real polynomial ideals are real and symmetric. The classical univariate Hermite Theorem
[
16
]
was generalized to the multivariate case by Pedersen, Roy and Szpirglas
[
20
]
, and was also proved in
[
7
]
and
[
11
]
:

Theorem 2.7

(Multivariate Hermite Theorem)
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be zero dimensional and
{\mathcal{B}}
be a monomial basis of
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}
. If

H_{g}({\mathcal{I}})
is the Hermite matrix of
~{\mathcal{I}}
with respect to
g
in the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
, then

\sigma(H_{g}({\mathcal{I}}))=\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)>0\}-\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)<0\}.

Remark 2.8
.

There are several ways to obtain the signature of a
k\times k
real and symmetric matrix
M
without computing the eigenvalues explicitly. We will describe two of these methods here. Note that if
M
is a rational symmetric matrix, then both of these methods can be computed with exact arithmetic over the rationals.

1.

Using Descartes rule of signs:

Let
p(x)
be the characteristic polynomial of the given
k\times k
real and symmetric matrix. Since all eigenvalues of real symmetric matrices are real their characteristic polynomials have only real roots. Then the Descartes Rule of Signs provides that
\sigma(M)
is the difference between the number of sign variation of the coefficients of
p(x)
and the number of sign variations of the coefficients of
p(-x)
(see Proposition 8.4 in
[
7
]
). The complexity of this computation is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(k^{4}).

2.

Using
LU
decomposition:

The LU decomposition of real symmetric matrices can be written as

LDL^{T}
where
L
is a special lower triangular matrix with 1’s on the diagonal entries and
D
is a diagonal matrix with the entries
\{u_{11},\ldots,u_{kk}\}
. These entries are the diagonal entries of the upper triangular matrix
U
obtained from the LU decomposition.
By Sylvester Law of inertia, the signature of
M
is the difference between
\#\{u_{ii}~:~u_{ii}>0\}
and
\#\{u_{ii}~:~u_{ii}<0\}
. The complexity of this computation only comes from the cost of the
LU
decomposition which can be found via Gaussian Elimination. The complexity of Gaussian elimination is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(k^{3}).

We close this subsection with some definitions that will be used later in this paper. First, in our certification algorithm we need the following property of
{\mathcal{B}}
(see
[
19
]
):

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. We say that
{\mathcal{B}}
(or
{\rm span}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{B}})
) is
connected to 1
if for all
b\in{\rm span}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{B}})
there exists
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in{\mathcal{B}}
such that

b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}b_{i}

and
\deg(b_{i})<\deg(b)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Definition 2.10
.

\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a finite set of monomials. The
extension
of
\mathcal{B}
is defined by

\mathcal{B}^{+}:=\mathcal{B}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\mathcal{B}=\left\{b,x_{1}b,\ldots,x_{n}b~|~b\in\mathcal{B}\right\}.

(3)

Definition 2.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a finite set of monomials and assume that
|{\mathcal{B}}^{+}|=l
. The
extended Hermite matrix
associated to points

~z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
(not necessarily distinct) is

H^{+}_{g}:=H^{{\mathcal{B}}^{+}}_{g}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}):=(V^{+})^{T}GV^{+}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{l\times l}

(4)

where
V^{+}=V_{\mathcal{B}^{+}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k\times l}
and G is the
k\times k

[G]_{j,j}=g(z_{j})
for

2.2
Rational Number Reconstruction

The continued fraction method for a real number
\alpha>0
can be described as the computation of

\alpha=\gamma_{1}=\lfloor\gamma_{1}\rfloor+\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}=\lfloor\gamma_{1}\rfloor+\frac{1}{\lfloor\gamma_{2}\rfloor+\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}}=\cdots

where
\gamma_{1}=\alpha
and
\gamma_{i+1}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}-\lfloor\gamma_{i}\rfloor}
. We call the rational numbers

\lfloor\gamma_{1}\rfloor,\lfloor\gamma_{1}\rfloor+\frac{1}{\lfloor\gamma_{2}\rfloor},\lfloor\gamma_{1}\rfloor+\frac{1}{\lfloor\gamma_{2}\rfloor+\frac{1}{\lfloor\gamma_{3}\rfloor}},\ldots

the
convergents
for
\alpha
. The following theorem (c.f.
[
22
, Corollary 6.3a]
) gives bounds on the distance from
\alpha
that guarantees uniqueness of a rational number with bounded denominator, and shows that if such rational number exists, it is a convergent for
\alpha
.

Theorem 2.12
.

[
22
]

There exists a polynomial time algorithm which, for a given rational number
\alpha
and a natural number
B
tests if there exists a pair of integers
(p,q)
with
1\leq q\leq B
and

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|<\frac{1}{2B^{2}},

and if so, finds this unique rational number
\frac{p}{q}
as a convergent for
\alpha
.

Approximate solutions are floating point numbers which are obtained from numerical computations. Using an absolute error bound
E>0
on numerical computations, we can set the denominator bound
B
such that Theorem
2.12
provide the unique rational approximation of
\alpha
as follows:

Corollary 2.13
.

Given
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
E>0
in
{\mathbb{R}}
there is at most one rational number with its denominator bounded by
B:=\left\lceil(2E)^{-1/2}\right\rceil
within the distance
E
from
\alpha
.

2.3
Numerical certification of non-roots of overdetermined systems

In this subsection we summarize some numerical methods from the literature to compute approximations of a superset of the common roots of an overdetermined system of polynomials
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
where
m>n
and to give a method to certify that a given point
z
is
not
an approximate root of
f
. Here we need to assume that
{\mathcal{I}}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
is radical and zero dimensional. The difficulty lies in the fact that numerical methods, such as the homotopy continuation method
[
12
]
, are designed to compute the common roots of square, well-conditioned polynomial systems. Here we describe the approach presented in
[
13
]
to handle overdetermined systems.

The main idea is that even though the consistency of an overdetermined system is a non-continuous property, the converse, inconsistency of an overdetermined system is a continuous property that can be certified with numerical methods. Similarly, while certifying that a point is approximating an exact root of an overdetermined system with exact rational coefficients cannot be certified with purely numerical methods, certifying that a point is
not
an approximate root can be done numerically. This allows us to eliminate in a certified manner roots of a square subsystem of
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
that are not roots of all polynomials and to give a certified upper bound
k
on the number of common roots of
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
. With this upper bound we can guarantee that our certification algorithm of Hermite matrices in Section
4
is correct when it gives a certification (although it may also return “fail”).

More precisely,
[
13
]
suggests to consider two square systems, each are random linear combinations of the polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
: If
R_{1},R_{2}:{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
two linear maps (represented by two random matrices), then for
R_{i}(f):=R_{i}\circ f
we can assume that

V(f)=V(R_{1}(f))\cap V(R_{2}(f)),

which property is satisfied unless
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are from Zariski closed subsets of all linear transformations. Similarly, for
i=1,2
, we can assume that the roots of
V(R_{i}(f))
are finite and all distinct, this property is also satisfied unless
R_{i}
is from a Zariski closed subset of all linear transformations (c.f.
[
13
, Section 3]
).
Using numerical homotopy continuation methods and
\alpha
-theory, one can compute and certify all approximate roots of both
R_{1}(f)
and
R_{2}(f)
, see details on this part of the algorithm in
[
13
, Section 2]
.

The following idea is a slight modification of
[
13
, Section 3]
allowing to discard approximate roots of
R_{1}(f)
and
R_{2}(f)
that do not approximate roots in
V(f)
. First note that for any approximate root
z
approximating an exact root
\xi\in V(R_{i}(f))
for
i=1,2
, we can give upper bounds for
\|{z}-{\xi}\|
using two times the
\beta
function defined in
\alpha
-theory (c.f.
[
9
, Ch 8, Theorem 2]
), even without knowing the exact root. Thus, for
i=1,2
denote by

\tilde{V}(R_{i}(f))
the set of pairs
(z,\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
where
z
is one of the approximate roots computed for
R_{i}(f)
and
\varepsilon
is an upper bound of the distance of
z
from the exact root it approximates. Fix
(z,\varepsilon)\in\tilde{V}(R_{1}(f))
and define

S_{(z,\varepsilon)}:=\left\{(z^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})\in\tilde{V}(R_{2}(f))\;:\>\|z-z^{\prime}\|\leq\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}\right\}.

If
|S_{(z,\varepsilon)}|>1
then we need to refine
z
and all
z^{\prime}
such that
(z^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})\in S_{(z,\varepsilon)}
using Newton’s method w.r.t
R_{1}(f)
and
R_{2}(f)
respectively, until one gets
S_{(z,\varepsilon)}=\emptyset
or
|S_{(z,\varepsilon)}|=1
. If
S_{z}=\emptyset
then we can discard
(z,\varepsilon)
since it cannot approximate an exact root in
V(f)=V(R_{1}(f))\cap V(R_{2}(f))
. If
S_{z}
has one element
(z^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})
, suppose
z
approximates an exact root
\xi\in V(R_{1}(f))
,
z^{\prime}
approximates an exact root
\xi^{\prime}\in V(R_{2}(f))
but
\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}
. Then we can compute refinements
z_{k}
and
z^{\prime}_{k}
using
k
iterations of Newton’s method starting from
z
and
z^{\prime}
w.r.t
R_{1}(f)
and
R_{2}(f)
respectively, such that

\|{z}_{k}-{z^{\prime}}_{k}\|>\varepsilon_{k}+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{k}.

(5)

where
\varepsilon_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2^{2^{k}-1}}\varepsilon
is a bound on
\|z_{k}-\xi\|
, and
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{k}
is a bound for
\|z^{\prime}_{k}-\xi^{\prime}\|
. If we find a
k
such that the inequality (
5
) is satisfied then we discard
(z,\varepsilon)
, otherwise we keep it. We repeat the above procedure for all elements in
\tilde{V}(R_{1}(f))\cup\tilde{V}(R_{2}(f))
.

Note that the above method never eliminates points that were approximating roots in
V(f)
, but may leave in points that were not near
V(f)
. Thus, as a consequence, we can always guarantee that the input
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
for Algorithm
3.1
below, to compute an approximate Hermite matrix, is a
superset
of an approximation of
V(f)
, and in particular the above method gives a certification that
k\geq\dim{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}
. On the other hand, our main symbolic-numeric certification Algorithm
4.1
for Hermite matrices will always fail when there are superfluous points among the input. Thus, using the assumption
k\geq\dim{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}
, the Hermite matrices that we certify successfully will correspond to all roots of
V(f)
(see Theorem
4.2
).

In the rest of the paper we assume that we already computed a set
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
that contain an approximate root for each root in
V(f)
, i.e. if
V(f)=\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k^{\prime}}\}
then
k^{\prime}\leq k
and for
i=1,\ldots,k^{\prime}
there exists
j_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that the Newton iteration starting from
z_{j_{i}}
quadratically converges to
\xi_{i}
. Moreover, using the
\beta
function from
\alpha
-theory as above, we assume that we have a certified bound
E\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
that we call
accuracy
, such that

\left\|\xi_{i}-z_{j_{i}}\right\|_{2}\leq E\quad i=1,\ldots,k^{\prime}.

(6)

Algorithm 3.1

.

We need the following proposition to get the bounds in (

Proposition 3.2
.

Given
{\mathcal{I}}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
zero dimensional radical ideal with
V({\mathcal{I}})=\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}.
Suppose
E>0
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
are such that

\|\xi_{i}-z_{i}\|_{2}<E.

Let
M>0
such that for all
i=1,\ldots,k
and
y\in{\rm Ball}(z_{i},E)=\{x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:\|x-z_{i}\|_{2}<E\}

\|y\|_{\infty}\leq M.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
with
d:=|\alpha|
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{\alpha}\right|\leq EkndM^{d-1}.

(8)

Furthermore, there is at most one rational number within

EkndM^{d-1}
distance from
\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{\alpha}
with denominator bounded by

B:=\left\lceil(2EkndM^{d-1})^{-1/2}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
with
d:=|\alpha|
. First note that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{\alpha}
is a polynomial in the
nk
coordinates of
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
of degree
d
. Using a multivatiate version of Taylor’s Theorem
[
1
]
, there exist
R_{i,j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
for
i=1,\ldots,k
and
j=1,\ldots,n
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{\alpha}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{i,j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})(z_{i,j}-\xi_{i,j}).

Moreover,

|R_{i,j}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})|\leq\max_{s,t}\max_{y_{s}\in{{\rm Ball}}(z_{s},E)}\left|\frac{\partial z_{s}^{\alpha}}{\partial z_{s,t}}(y_{s})\right|\leq dM^{d-1}.

Thus we get

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{\alpha}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}dM^{d-1}\left|z_{i,j}-\xi_{i,j}\right|\leq kndM^{d-1}E.

The second claim is straightforward from Corollary
2.13
using
E^{\prime}=EkndM^{d-1}
.
∎

The next theorem gives sufficient conditions for Algorithm
3.1
to correctly compute the exact Hermite matrices
H_{1}^{+}
for
{\mathcal{I}}
from the approximate points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a zero dimensional radical ideal with
{\mathcal{B}}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}
a basis for
{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}.
Denote
V_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathcal{I}})=\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Let
E>0
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
there exists a unique
j_{i}
such that

\|z_{j_{i}}-\xi_{i}\|_{2}<E.

Assume further that
\|z_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq M-E
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
. Finally, assume that for
x^{\alpha}=x_{i}x_{j}x^{\alpha_{t}}\,x^{\alpha_{s}}
for
i,j=1,\ldots n
and
t,s=1,\ldots,k
, the denominator of
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
is at most
\lceil(2Ekn|\alpha|M^{|\alpha|-1})^{-1/2}\rceil
. Then Algorithm
3.1
computes the exact Hermite matrices
H_{1}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{+}}({\mathcal{I}})
.

Proof.

Since
\|z_{i}-\xi_{j_{i}}\|_{2}<E
and
\|z_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq M-E
, we have that
\|\xi_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq M
, thus the assumptions of Proposition
3.2
are satisfied. Therefore, for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
as in the claim, we have that there is at most one rational number within
Ekn|\alpha|M^{|\alpha|-1}
distance from
\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{\alpha}
with denominators bounded by
\lceil(2Ek|\alpha|M^{|\alpha|-1})^{-1/2}\rceil
. By Proposition
3.2
we can see that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}
is within that distance from
\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{\alpha}
, and using our assumption on the denominator of

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, by Theorem
2.12
the rational number reconstruction algorithm finds
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}
. Thus, the entries of
H_{1}^{+}
computed by Algorithm
3.1
are the same as the entries of
H_{1}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{+}}({\mathcal{I}})
as claimed.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The assumption of Theorem
3.3
that the denominator of
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
is at most
\lceil(2Ekn|\alpha|M^{|\alpha|-1})^{-1/2}\rceil
can be achieved by improving the accuracy
E
of the approximate roots
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
. If we assume that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
are all approximate roots for a square subsystem of
f
, we can use Newton iterations to quadratically converge to the exact roots, thus decrease
E
. Meanwhile, the other quantities in this bound (
k,n,|\alpha|,M
) are fixed, so with enough iterations we increase
\lceil(2Ekn|\alpha|M^{|\alpha|-1})^{-1/2}\rceil
to satisfy the condition of the Theorem. One could in theory study a priori bounds on how small
E
has to be to satisfy this condition, (we did a similar analysis in
[
3
]
), but in this paper we let our Hermite matrix certification algorithm reject cases when the accuracy of the root approximation is not sufficiently good.

4
Certification of the Exact Hermite Matrix

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{m}
be a rational polynomial system with zero dimensional radical ideal
\mathcal{I}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
. In the previous section we computed a matrix
H_{1}^{+}
with rows and columns corresponding to
{\mathcal{B}}^{+}
, where
{\mathcal{B}}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}
. In this section we certify if this matrix is the the extended Hermite matrix of
{\mathcal{I}}
and we also compute
H_{g}({\mathcal{I}})
for any polynomial
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, as long as

. Here we assume that
{\mathcal{B}}
is connected to 1 as in Definition
2.9
. The following algorithm is purely symbolic:

Algorithm 4.1

(Hermite Matrix Certification)
.

Input:

{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{m}
with

with rows and columns indexed by the elements of
{\mathcal{B}}^{+}
.

The certified

and

, or Fail.

1:

H_{1}\leftarrow k\times k
submatrix of

with rows and columns corresponding to
{\mathcal{B}}

M_{s}\leftarrow H_{1}^{-1}\cdot H_{1}^{x_{s}}
for

.

3:

For

s=1,\ldots,n

x_{s}x^{\alpha_{i}}=x^{\alpha_{j}}

Fail.

p(\lambda)\leftarrow
characteristic polynomial polynomial to

M_{i}\cdot M_{j}=M_{j}\cdot M_{i}\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n

.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

{\mathcal{I}}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

{\mathcal{B}}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}

is radical,

{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}

Assume Algorithm
4.1
did not fail, and let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}
be the matrices computed in Step 2. Define the set of polynomials
{\mathcal{F}}
from the columns of

{\mathcal{F}}:=\left\{x_{j}x^{\alpha_{t}}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}[M_{j}]_{i,t}\,x^{\alpha_{i}}\;:\;j=1,\ldots,n,t=1,\ldots,k\right\}.

. In
[

if the following three conditions are satisfied: 1. the map
N:{\rm span}({\mathcal{B}}^{+})\rightarrow{\rm span}({\mathcal{B}})

2. the space spanned by

is connected to
1;\;

\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}
is a set of pairwise commuting matrices. The first condition is certified in Step 3, the second is an assumption, and the third is certified in Step 5. Also by Step 5 we have

f_{i}(M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{n})=0\hbox{ for }i=1,\ldots,m,

\dim{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}\leq k

{\mathcal{I}}=J
and for
s=1,\ldots,n

is the transpose of the matrix of the multiplication map

\mu_{x_{s}}:{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}},\quad[p]\mapsto[x_{s}p],

with respect to
{\mathcal{B}}
and

is the diagonal matrix

. This gives

H_{1}\cdot g(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})=(V^{T}V)\cdot(V^{-1}GV)=V^{T}GV=H_{g}.

H_{1}
and
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}

Remark 4.3
.

For
{\mathcal{B}}=\{1,x_{1},\ldots,x_{1}^{k-1}\}
we can guarantee that

{\rm rank}(H_{1})={\rm rank}(H_{1}^{+})=k

by checking that the difference between the first coordinates of
z_{i}
and

is at least
2E
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
. In this case, since we assume that
\|z_{i}-\xi_{j_{i}}\|_{2}<E

\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}
are all distinct. Since
H_{1}({\mathcal{I}})=V^{T}V
where

is the usual univariate Vandermonde matrix of these first coordinates of

, we get that
H_{1}({\mathcal{I}})
is invertible. Since
H_{1}^{+}({\mathcal{I}})=V_{{\mathcal{B}}^{+}}^{T}V_{{\mathcal{B}}^{+}}

{\rm rank}(H_{1})={\rm rank}(H_{1}^{+})=k.

there are no such simple conditions, but the SVD of the Vandermonde matrix of
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
with respect to
{\mathcal{B}}
gives a good indication that
{\mathcal{B}}
is a basis for both
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/I(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
and
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/I(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
.
Also for
{\mathcal{B}}=\{1,x_{1},\ldots,x_{1}^{k-1}\}

has a companion matrix structure. In this case, in Step 6 we can certify

using the Newton-Girard formulae (c.f.

, and constructing the matrix

according to (

5
Extension to the non-radical case

So far in this paper we assumed that the ideal
{\mathcal{I}}
is radical and zero-dimensional. In this section, we describe what we can certify when we drop the assumption of radicality.

First note that Definition
2.1
for the Hermite matrix in terms of roots is well defined and continuous even if some of the roots are repeated. This is no longer true when we try to define the multiplication matrices from the roots. Using a notation similar to the previous section, for
{\mathcal{B}}
a basis for
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}
and
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, denote by
M^{\mathcal{B}}_{g}
the transpose of the matrix of multiplication
\mu_{g}:{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/{\mathcal{I}},\;[p]\mapsto[gp]
in the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
. While in the case when
{\mathcal{I}}
is radical we have

M^{\mathcal{B}}_{g}=V_{\mathcal{B}}\,G\,V_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1},

with
V_{\mathcal{B}}=[b(\xi)]_{\xi\in V({\mathcal{I}}),b\in{\mathcal{B}}}
and
G={\rm diag}(g(\xi)\,:\,\xi\in V({\mathcal{I}}))
,
M^{\mathcal{B}}_{g}
is not diagonalizable when
{\mathcal{I}}
is not radical. In particular,
V_{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{I}})
and
H_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}(I)
are not invertible, so we cannot use them to compute multiplication matrices in the certification algorithm as above.

To overcome this difficulty, we notice that while in the non-radical case
H_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{I}})
is not invertible, its maximal non-singular submatrix is the Hermite matrix
H_{1}^{\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}}(\sqrt{{\mathcal{I}}})
of the radical of
{\mathcal{I}}
, with respect to a subset
\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}
that is a basis for
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\sqrt{{\mathcal{I}}}
(c.f.
[
17
]
). Denote by
\bar{H}_{1}
this maximal non-singular submatrix of
H_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{I}})
, with rows and columns corresponding to
\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}
and by
\bar{H}_{1}^{x_{i}}
the submatrix of
H_{1}^{{\mathcal{B}}+}({\mathcal{I}})
corresponding to rows indexed by
\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}
and columns indexed by
x_{i}\cdot\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}
. Then we get the transpose of the matrix of multiplication by
x_{i}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\sqrt{{\mathcal{I}}}
w.r.t. the basis
\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}
by

\bar{M}_{i}:=M_{x_{i}}^{\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}}=\bar{H}_{1}^{-1}\cdot\bar{H}_{1}^{x_{i}}\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Thus we can use
\bar{H}_{1}^{\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}^{+}}
to compute the multiplication matrices of the radical
\sqrt{{\mathcal{I}}}
and certify them.

We modify Algorithm
3.1
to output the maximal non-singular submatrix
\bar{H}_{1}
of
H_{1}
with rows and columns corresponding to
\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}
, and its extension
\bar{H}^{+}_{1}
to the basis
\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}^{+}
.

Algorithm 5.1

(Hermite Matrix Computation - Non Radical Case)
.

Input:

k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathcal{B}}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}
connected to 1,

E,M\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that
E
is a bound on the accuracy of

Application: Rational certificate of non-negativity over real varieties

As mentioned in the Introduction, the following application was inspired by
[
10
]
where the authors give a method to compute a degree
d
sum of squares (SOS) decomposition (if one exists) for a non-negative polynomial over a real algebraic set using a finite set of sample points from the (complex) algebraic set. In
[
10
]
they also give bounds on the number of sample points needed to decide whether a degree
d
SOS decomposition exists. Their method uses semidefinite programming.

The application we present below also gives a certificate for the non-negativity of a polynomial over a real algebraic set, and also uses sample points of the complex algebaric set. However, it does not give an SOS decomposition, instead it uses
Hermite matrices reconstructed and certified from the sample points. While the SOS decomposition constructed in
[
10
]
is approximate, the construction we give here is exact, using rational numbers.

Consider the following problem:

Problem 6.1
.

Given
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{s}
and
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Decide if
g(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and give a rational certificate.

We use the following notion of critical points of
g
in
V(f)\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
:

Definition 6.2
.

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{s}
and
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
, and assume that
{\mathcal{I}}:=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\rangle
is a radical ideal.
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is a
critical point
of
g
in
V(f)
if

z\in V(f)
,

z
is non-singular in
V(f)
, i.e. the Jacobian matrix
Jf(z)
of
f
has rank
c:=n-\dim V(f)
,
and the Jacobian matrix
Jf(z)
augmented with the row vector
\nabla g(z)
has also rank
c
.

We can solve Problem
6.1
using the following assumptions on
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
and
g
:

Assumption 6.3
.

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{s}
and
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. We assume

1.

For all
z\in V(f)
, the Jacobian matrix
Jf(z)
of
f
has rank
s\leq n
, i.e.,
f
is a regular sequence and
V(f)
is smooth.

2.

V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is bounded,

3.

g
has finitely many critical points on
V(f)
.

We need the following lemma:

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{s}
and
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
satisfying Assumption
6.3
.
Then, the set of polynomials in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s}]

L(x,\lambda)=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\}\cup\left\{\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\;:\;i=1,\ldots,n\right\}

has finitely many roots in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+s}
, and their projections to the
x
coordinates contain all real points where
g
attains its extreme values on each connected component of
\,\left(V(f)\setminus V(g)\right)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

First we prove that the projection
V(L)\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n+s}
onto the
x
-coordinates are the critical points of
g
in
V(f)
. Let
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n+s}
. Then clearly
x\in V(f)
, and because of our first assumption and that
s\geq c=n-\dim V(f)
we have that
s=c
and
x
is non-singular in
V(f)
. By
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)
we also have that
Jf(x)
augmented with
\nabla g(x)
has rank at most
c
, thus
x
is a critical point. Conversely, let
x
be a critical point of
g
on
V(f)
. Then
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
vanishes at
x
, and since both
Jf(x)
and the augmented matrix
Jf(x)
by
\nabla g(x)
has rank
s
,
\nabla g(x)
is in the row space of
Jf(x)
, thus there exists
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s}
such that
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)
.

Next we prove that
V(L)\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n+s}
is finite. By assumption,
g
has finitely many critical points in
V(f)
, thus the projection of
V(L)
onto the
x
coordinates is finite. Since
Jf(x)
has full row rank for every
x\in V(f)
, for every critical point
x
there is a unique
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s}
such that
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)
. Thus
V(L)
is finite.

To prove the second claim, assume that
\,\left(V(f)\setminus V(g)\right)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\neq\emptyset
and let
C
be a connected component of
the set
(V(f)\setminus V(g))\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Since
V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is bounded,
C
is bounded as well.
Since
C\not\subset V(g)
, there exists
x\in C
with
g(x)\neq 0
.
Let
\overline{C}
be the Euclidean
closure of
C
so that
\overline{C}\subset V\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is closed and bounded,
and
g
vanishes identically on
\overline{C}\setminus C
. By the extreme value theorem,

g
attains both a minimum and a maximum on
\overline{C}
. Since
g
is not identically zero on
\overline{C}
,
either the minimum or the maximum value of
g
on
\overline{C}
must be nonzero, so
g

attains a non-zero extreme value on
C
. By the Lagrange multiplier theorem, using our assumptions, the points in
C
where
g
attains its extreme values are critical points of
g
in
V(f)
, which proves the claim.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main result of the section:

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{s},g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
and
L\subset{\mathbb{Q}}[x,\lambda]
as in Lemma
6.4
. Let
J
be the ideal generated by
L
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x,\lambda]
, and
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}[x,\lambda]
be any finite monomial basis for
{\mathbb{Q}}[x,\lambda]/J
. Then

\sigma(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{g})=\sigma(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{g^{2}})\text{ if and only if }g(x)\geq 0\text{ for all }x\in V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Proof.

First we prove that
g(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if and only if
g(x,\lambda)\geq 0
for all
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}
. Assume that
g(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}
. Then
x\in V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and since
g\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
,
g(x,\lambda)=g(x)\geq 0
. Conversely, assume
g(x,\lambda)\geq 0
for all
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}
. Suppose there exists
x\in V(f)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
g(x)<0
. Let
C
be the bounded connected component of
\,\left(V(f)\setminus V(g)\right)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that contains
x
and
\overline{C}
its Euclidean closure. Let
x^{*}\in\overline{C}
where
g
attains its minimum on
\overline{C}
. Then
g(x^{*})\leq g(x)<0
, so
x^{*}\in C
and
x^{*}
is a critical point of
g
in
V(f)
. Thus there exists a unique
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
, the solution of a linear system, such that
(x^{*},\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}
. But then
g(x^{*},\lambda)=g(x^{*})\geq 0
, a contradiction.

By Lemma
6.4
we have
V(L)
is finite, so
{\mathcal{B}}
is finite. Since
L
has real (rational) coefficients, by Hermite’s theorem

\displaystyle\sigma(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{g})=\#\{(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}\;:\;g(x)>0\}-\#\{(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}\;:\;g(x)<0\}

\displaystyle\sigma(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{g^{2}})=\#\{(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}\;:\;g(x)>0\}.

Thus,
\sigma(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{g})=\sigma(H^{\mathcal{B}}_{g^{2}})
if and only if
\#\{(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}\;:\;g(x)<0\}=0
, i.e. for all
(x,\lambda)\in V(L)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n+s}
we have
g(x)\geq 0
.
This proves the theorem.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

Let

and

and

Since

and

Remark 6.7

The size

Proposition 6.8

∎

Application: Real Root Certification with Hermite Matrices

Let
{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})

\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{m}

{\mathcal{I}}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
is a zero dimensional radical ideal. The certification problem is the following: We are given

and
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{+}

within the

In this section, we present how one can use the signature of Hermite matrices to answer this certification problem as an application of Hermite matrices.

Now we introduce the certification theorem as a corollary of the Multivariate Hermite Theorem
2.7

Corollary 7.1
.

Let
{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
,
f_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for all
i=1,\dots m
, and
{\mathcal{I}}=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
is a zero dimensional radical ideal. Given
z^{*}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
and
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{+}
, define
g(x):=\|x-z^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Then

\sigma(H_{1}({\mathcal{I}}))=\sigma(H_{g}({\mathcal{I}}))

if and only if
there is no real root within the closed ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of radius
\varepsilon
around
z^{*}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.7
,
\sigma(H_{g}({\mathcal{I}}))=\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)>0\}-\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)<0\}
. Since 1 is greater than 0, the signature of
H_{1}({\mathcal{I}})
gives the number of all real roots of
{\mathcal{I}}
. Thus, it can be written as

\sigma(H_{1}({\mathcal{I}}))=\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)>0\}+\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)<0\}+\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)=0\}

(9)

for any
g(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
Now, let
g(x)=\|x-z^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Assume that
\sigma(H_{1}({\mathcal{I}}))=\sigma(H_{g}({\mathcal{I}}))
. Using the definitions above
and cancelling the terms
\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)>0\}
from both sides, we yield
2\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)<0\}=-\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)=0\}.
The only solution to this equation is

\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)<0\}=0\text{ and }\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)=0\}=0,

since these terms are nonnegative integers by definition. This implies that there is no real solution to
{\mathcal{I}}
, when
g(x)<0
and
g(x)=0
. By the definition of
g(x)=\|x-z^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}
, there is no
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
\|x-z^{*}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}
. Thus we conclude that there is no real root within the closed ball of radius
\varepsilon
.

Assume that there is no real root to
{\mathcal{I}}
within the closed ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of radius
\varepsilon
around
z^{*}
. It implies that any
x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})
satisfies the inequality
\|x-z^{*}\|_{2}^{2}>\varepsilon^{2}
. Then

g(x)=\|x-z^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}
is always positive, and
\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)\leq 0\}=0
. By Theorem
2.7
and (
9
), we have

\sigma(H_{1}({\mathcal{I}}))=\sigma(H_{g}({\mathcal{I}}))=\#\{x\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})~|~g(x)>0\}.

∎

Algorithm 7.2

(Real Root Certification)
.

Input:

{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{m}
;

z^{*}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[i]^{n}
;

\varepsilon^{2}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{+}
;

{\mathcal{B}}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,x^{\alpha_{k}}\}
connected to 1 with
|{\mathcal{B}}^{+}|=l
for some
k,l\in{\mathbb{N}}

E,M\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that
\|z_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq M-E
for
i=1,\ldots,k
and the accuracy of
z_{i}
is at least
E
.

Output:

True:
\exists z\in V_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{I}})
such that
z
is in the closed ball of radius
\varepsilon
around
z^{*}

False: No real root of
{\mathcal{I}}
within the closed ball of radius
\varepsilon
around
z^{*}

or Fail.

1:

Define
g(x):=\|x-z^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

2:

H_{1}^{+}:=H_{1}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{+}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\leftarrow\text{\sc Hermite Matrix Computation}\left({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{B}}^{+},E,M,\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\}\right)

(see Algorithm
3.1
)

3:

For
I:=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
call

\text{\sc Hermite Matrix Certification}(f,g(x),B,H_{1}^{+})
to obtain certified
H_{1}(I)
and
H_{g}(I)
, that algorithm can also return Fail (see Algorithm
4.1
)

4:

Compute
\sigma(H_{1}({\mathcal{I}}))
and
\sigma(H_{g}({\mathcal{I}}))
. See Remark
2.8
for computational details.

5:

If

\sigma(H_{1}({\mathcal{I}}))=\sigma(H_{g}({\mathcal{I}}))

then
return False

else
return True.
