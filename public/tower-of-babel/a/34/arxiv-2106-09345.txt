Scaling Limits of Planar Symplectic Ensembles
Gernot AKEMANN a, Sung-Soo BYUN b and Nam-Gyu KANG b

Abstract
We consider various asymptotic scaling limits N → ∞ N\to\infty for the 2 ​ N 2N complex eigenvalues of non-Hermitian random matrices in the symmetry class of the symplectic Ginibre ensemble. These are known to be integrable, forming Pfaffian point processes, and we obtain limiting expressions for the corresponding kernel for different potentials. The first part is devoted to the symplectic Ginibre ensemble with the Gaussian potential. We obtain the asymptotic at the edge of the spectrum in the vicinity of the real line. The unifying form of the kernel allows us to make contact with the bulk scaling along the real line and with the edge scaling away from the real line, where we recover the known determinantal process of the complex Ginibre ensemble. Part two covers ensembles of Mittag-Leffler type with a singularity at the origin. For potentials Q ⁡ ( ζ ) = | ζ | 2 ​ λ − ( 2 ​ c / N ) ​ log ⁡ | ζ | Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2\lambda}-(2c/N)\log|\zeta| , with λ > 0 \lambda>0 and c > − 1 c>-1 , the limiting kernel obeys a linear differential equation of fractional order 1 / λ 1/\lambda at the origin. For integer m = 1 / λ m=1/\lambda it can be solved in terms of Mittag-Leffler functions. In the last part, we derive Ward’s equation for planar symplectic ensembles for a general class of potentials. It serves as a tool to investigate the Gaussian and singular Mittag-Leffler universality class. This allows us to determine the functional form of all possible limiting kernels (if they exist) that are translation invariant, up to their integration domain.

Introduction

In the pioneering work of Ginibre
[
31
]
, it was first discovered that the complex eigenvalues of Gaussian random matrices with quaternion elements (also known as the symplectic Ginibre ensemble) behave like equally charged particles with complex conjugation symmetry. They interact via the two-dimensional Coulomb repulsion, subject to a confining
Gaussian potential
Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2}
.
Below we will consider more general potentials
Q
, where the joint probability distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{N}
of the point process
\bm{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{P}}_{N}(\bm{\zeta}):=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}{\rm e}^{-{\mathbf{H}}_{N}(\bm{\zeta})}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}A(\zeta_{j}).

(1.1)

Here the Hamiltonian
{\mathbf{H}}_{N}
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}_{N}(\bm{\zeta}):=\sum_{j\neq k}\log\frac{1}{|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k}|\big|\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{k}\big|}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\log\frac{1}{\big|\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{j}\big|^{2}}+NQ(\zeta_{j})\right),

(1.2)

the normalisation constant
Z_{N}
is called the partition function, which turns
{\mathbf{P}}_{N}
into a probability measure, and
{\rm d}A(\zeta):=\tfrac{1}{\pi}{\rm d}^{2}\zeta
is the two-dimensional Lebesgue measure divided by
\pi
.

Compared to the eigenvalues of the complex Ginibre or more general random normal matrix ensembles, which correspond to a genuine two-dimensional Coulomb gas at specific inverse temperature
\beta=2
without further symmetries, one of the most distinguished features of the symplectic ensemble is the existence of a local repulsion from the real axis, which follows from the term
-\log\big|\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{j}\big|^{2}
in (
1.2
).
To be more precise, this local repulsion originates from complex conjugate eigenvalue pairs which repel each other when approaching the real axis.
For illustration, see Figure
1
(a) for some random samplings of eigenvalues from the symplectic Ginibre ensemble.

(a)

1024
samples for
N=8

(b)

a single sample for
N=8192

Figure 1
:
The plots show
2N
eigenvalues of random matrices from the symplectic Ginibre ensemble.
In (a), the local repulsion from the real axis is clearly visible. In (b), the rescaled process at the right endpoint of the spectrum clearly displays the complex conjugation symmetry.

Why are symplectic ensembles interesting, apart from their statistical mechanics interpretation?
Together with its real and complex counterparts, the symplectic Ginibre ensemble represents one of the few examples which is integrable, in the sense that it constitutes a Pfaffian point process where the matrix-valued kernel can be explicitly constructed.
This fact will be recalled in more detail in the next section.
It makes the asymptotic analysis of the kernel in various scaling limits possible, as will be the main topic of this work. The situation is much more difficult for Coulomb gases at general values of
\beta\neq 2
, see
[
9
,
24
,
47
]
and references therein for recent developments.

Moreover, it has been found rather recently that non-Hermitian random matrices enjoy a much wider class of universality than their Hermitian counterparts, in the sense that away from the real axis the limiting complex eigenvalue correlation functions of all three Ginibre ensembles agree

[
7
,
20
,
27
,
46
]
. The same phenomenon has been observed very recently when comparing the spectra of truncated unitary and symplectic random matrices
[
37
,
49
]
, respectively.

While random matrices with complex eigenvalues have many applications in physics in general, we provide some examples
where the symplectic ensemble yields unique predictions and differs from other non-Hermitian symmetry classes, notably at the origin. These include disordered non-Hermitian Hamiltonians with an imaginary magnetic field
[
40
]
,
thermal conduction in superconducting quantum dots
[
26
]
in the related circular quaternion ensemble,
or the spectrum of the Dirac operator in quantum chromodynamics with two colours at non-vanishing chemical
potential, for the symplectic ensemble with additional chiral symmetry
[
2
]
.
In the latter the complex eigenvalues of the Dirac operator in the vicinity of the origin are of particular importance because of their role in chiral symmetry breaking. The limiting random matrix predictions have been confirmed from field theory in
[
4
]
.

It is the goal of this article to derive further asymptotic results which are specific for the symplectic ensembles, notably at the edge of the real axis and in the presence of singularities at the origin. The representation for the limiting
edge kernel that we will derive provides a unifying picture that allows us to relate results at different parts of the spectrum. Before we present our main results in the next section, let us summarise what was previously known about symplectic ensembles in various parts of the spectrum. Here we include the elliptic symplectic Ginibre ensemble with the potential
Q(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\big(|\zeta|^{2}-\tau\operatorname{Re}\zeta^{2}\big)
, where the parameter
\tau\in[0,1)
controls the degree of non-Hermiticity. Its joint probability distribution (
1.1
) and kernel at finite-
N
were derived by Kanzieper
[
35
]
.

In the asymptotic analysis of the kernel at various points of the spectrum one has to distinguish local or microscopic from global or macroscopic scales.
Since the above-mentioned repulsion from the real line affects only the microscopic scale when the complex conjugate eigenvalues become close, it could be expected that the leading form of the macroscopic eigenvalue density is the same as that of the two-dimensional Coulomb gas in the symmetry class of the complex Ginibre ensemble, with external potential
Q/2
,
see, e.g.,
[
29
]
. Indeed, for a general
Q
satisfying complex conjugation symmetry
Q(\zeta)=Q\big(\bar{\zeta}\big)
and suitable potential theoretic assumptions, it was shown by Benaych-Georges and Chapon that as
N\to\infty
the empirical measure of
\bm{\zeta}
converges to Frostman’s equilibrium measure associated with
Q/2
, see
[
17
, Theorem 3.1]
.
In particular, the density of
\bm{\zeta}
tends to

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta Q(\zeta)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{S}(\zeta),\qquad\Delta:=\partial\bar{\partial},

(1.3)

where
S
is a certain compact set called the
droplet
. For the Ginibre ensemble this is the well-known circular (or elliptic) law.

In the local scaling limit at the origin at maximal non-Hermiticity (
\tau=0
), the limiting kernel of the symplectic Ginibre was derived in
[
35
,
44
]
. At weak non-Hermiticity, when
\tau
scales as
1-\tau=O\big(N^{-1}\big)
, a different limiting kernel was found at the origin by Kanzieper
[
35
]
. It interpolates between the former
at
\tau=0
and the sine kernel of the Gaussian symplectic ensemble in the limit
\tau\to 1
. Both limiting kernels are invariant under translations along the real line, and we will come back to this feature below.

At the edge of the spectrum, it was shown by Rider
[
46
]
and spelled out by Dubach
[
27
]
that the maximal modulus fluctuations of complex and symplectic Ginibre ensemble agree.
The agreement between the two ensembles was also studied in
[
7
]
. It was shown that in the bulk away from the real axis both ensembles yield the same determinantal point processes. In this work we will first focus on the edge on the real line. The local statistics along the real line for the elliptic symplectic Ginibre ensemble is found in
[
21
]
.

Secondly, we investigate what happens when a non-Gaussian potential develops a singularity by inserting a point charge at the origin. In the generic case of a potential of Mittag-Leffler type we will be able to provide an explicit expression for the limiting origin kernel,
that differs from the Ginibre universality class.
Our findings can be thought of as the counterparts for previous results in random normal matrix ensembles
[
15
,
25
]
.
(See also
[
16
,
18
,
42
]
for extensive studies on the orthogonal polynomials associated with Mittag-Leffler type potentials.)

As the third issue, we study the universality for kernels that are translation-invariant along the real line. In setting up Ward’s equation for the symplectic ensemble – an identity satisfied by limits of the rescaled one-point functions, under some natural assumptions, we can completely characterise the class of all such possible limiting kernels for general potentials by an integral representation. It is unique (if it exists) up to the integration domain, which is a connected interval symmetric around the origin, see
[
13
,
14
]
for analogous works in random normal matrix ensembles.

2
Main results

Let us now come to the main objects in this work. We denote by
\mathbb{D}(\eta,r)
the disc with centre
\eta\in{\mathbb{C}}
and radius
r
. For a given sequence of points
p_{N}
, the positive number
r_{N}=r_{N}(p_{N})
is called the
micro-scale
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}(p_{N},r_{N})}\frac{\Delta Q(\zeta)}{2}\,{\rm d}A(\zeta)=\frac{1}{N}.

(2.1)

We drop the subscript and write
p\equiv p_{N}
if the sequence does not depend on
N
.
By (
1.3
), the micro-scale
r_{N}
corresponds to the mean eigenvalue spacing in radial distance of the ensemble (
1.1
) at the point
p\in S

(cf.
[
21
]
for a situation where
p
is outside the droplet).
We define the rescaled process
\bm{z}=\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
as follows: for all
j
,

\displaystyle z_{j}:=\begin{cases}r_{N}^{-1}\cdot(\zeta_{j}-p)&\text{if }p\in\operatorname{int}(S),\\
{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}r_{N}^{-1}\cdot(\zeta_{j}-p)&\text{if }p\in\partial S,\end{cases}

(2.2)

distinguishing the interior and the boundary of the droplet
S
.
Here the angle
\theta\equiv\theta_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}
is chosen so that
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}
is outer normal to
\partial S
at
p
, see Figure
1
(b)
inset
for an illustration of the rescaled process.
The
k
-point correlation function of
the rescaled process
\bm{z}
is defined by

\displaystyle R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k}):=\lim_{{\varepsilon}\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\exists\text{ at least one particle in }\mathbb{D}(z_{j},{\varepsilon}),\,j=1,\dots,k)}{{\varepsilon}^{2k}}.

In (
3.2
) a more standard definition of the
k
-point correlation function before rescaling denoted by
\mathbf{R}_{N,k}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k})
is given.
Throughout the paper we distinguish these and all other objects (kernels, Hamiltonian, joint distribution) before rescaling by bold symbols.
For the precise relation on the level of the kernels see (
3.6
). It results into the following relation between the
k
-point correlation functions

\displaystyle R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=r_{N}^{2k}\mathbf{R}_{N,k}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k}).

It is well known that before and after rescaling the
set
\bm{z}
forms a Pfaffian point process (see
[
35
,
44
]
), i.e.,
R_{N,k}
is expressed in terms of a certain
2\times 2
matrix-valued kernel
K_{N}
as

\displaystyle R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\bar{z}_{j}-z_{j}){\operatorname{Pf}}\big[K_{N}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k},

(2.3)

where the kernel
K_{N}
is of the form

\displaystyle K_{N}(z,w):={\rm e}^{-\frac{N}{2}(Q(\zeta)+Q(\eta))}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{N}(z,w)&\kappa_{N}(z,\bar{w})\\
\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},w)&\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix}.

Here
{\operatorname{Pf}}
denotes a Pfaffian of the
2k\times 2k
skew-symmetric matrix and

\displaystyle\zeta=\begin{cases}p+r_{N}z&\text{if }p\in\textup{int}(S),\\
p+{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}r_{N}z&\text{if }p\in\partial S,\end{cases}\qquad\eta=\begin{cases}p+r_{N}w&\text{if }p\in\textup{int}(S),\\
p+{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}r_{N}w&\text{if }p\in\partial S,\end{cases}

(2.4)

where
\theta
is given as in (
2.2
).
The arguments (
2.4
) are given according to the rescaling (
2.2
).
For the spectral density at
k=1
, let us denote
R_{N}\equiv R_{N,1}
.

The primary goal of this work is to derive the large-
N
limit of the kernel
K_{N}
for various potentials
Q
.
For the Gaussian potential
Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2}
, where the associated ensemble (
1.1
) corresponds to the symplectic Ginibre ensemble, it follows from the circular law that
S=\mathbb{D}\big(0,\sqrt{2}\big)
.
In
[
35
]
, Kanzieper studied the elliptic potential
Q(\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\big(|\zeta|^{2}-\operatorname{Re}\zeta^{2}\big)
and derived the scaling limit
for the pre-kernel at the origin
p=0
in the almost-Hermitian regime when
1-\tau=O\big(\frac{1}{N}\big)
.
Also at
p=0
and for maximally non-Hermiticity at
\tau=0
,
he showed that the associated
\infty
-point process
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

of the symplectic Ginibre ensemble has the correlation kernel

\displaystyle K_{{\rm bulk}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,w)={\rm e}^{-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{{\rm bulk}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,w)&\kappa_{{\rm bulk}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,\bar{w})\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\kappa_{{\rm bulk}}^{\mathbb{R}}(\bar{z},w)&\kappa_{{\rm bulk}}^{\mathbb{R}}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix},

(2.5)

where the pre-kernel
\kappa_{{\rm bulk}}^{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

\displaystyle\kappa_{{\rm bulk}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,w):=\sqrt{\pi}{\rm e}^{z^{2}+w^{2}}\operatorname{erf}(z-w).

(2.6)

We also refer to
[
44
]
for an alternative derivation of (
2.6
).
Furthermore, it was shown in
[
21
,
38
]
that the kernel (
2.5
) also appears when
p\in\textup{int}(S)\cap{\mathbb{R}}

in the symplectic elliptic Ginibre ensemble
and in that sense is universal
(in
[
7
, Appendix B]
a different strategy at
\tau=0
was mentioned).

2.1

p\in\partial S\cap{\mathbb{R}}=\big\{{\pm}\sqrt{2}\big\}

Let

\displaystyle f_{z}(u):=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\big(\sqrt{2}(z-u)\big)

(2.7)

and write
W(f,g)
for the Wronskian of two functions
f
,
g
to formulate our first main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2}
and
p=\pm\sqrt{2}
.
Then
R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
converges uniformly for
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}
in compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
to

\displaystyle R_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\bar{z}_{j}-z_{j}){\operatorname{Pf}}\big[K_{{\rm edge}}^{{\mathbb{R}}}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k},

where

\displaystyle K_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,w)={\rm e}^{-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,w)&\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,\bar{w})\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(\bar{z},w)&\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix},

and

\displaystyle\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,w):=\sqrt{\pi}{\rm e}^{z^{2}+w^{2}}\int_{E}W(f_{w},f_{z})(u)\,{\rm d}u,\qquad E=(-\infty,0).

(2.8)

We remark that the pre-kernel
\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}
has the following alternative representation

\displaystyle\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z,w)={\rm e}^{2zw}\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\rm e}^{-t^{2}}\sinh(2t(w-z))\operatorname{erfc}(z+w-t)\,{\rm d}t.

As a consequence of Theorem
2.1
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2}
,
p_{N}=\pm\sqrt{2}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{N}}
, where
\theta_{N}=\frac{t}{\sqrt{N}}
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
converges uniformly for
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}
in compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
to

\displaystyle R_{k}^{t}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\bar{z}_{j}-z_{j}-2{\rm i}t){\operatorname{Pf}}\big[K_{{\rm edge}}^{t}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k},

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle K_{{\rm edge}}^{t}(z,w):={\rm e}^{-|z+{\rm i}t|^{2}-|w+{\rm i}t|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z+{\rm i}t,w+{\rm i}t)&\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z+{\rm i}t,\bar{w}-{\rm i}t)\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(\bar{z}-{\rm i}t,w+{\rm i}t)&\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(\bar{z}-{\rm i}t,\bar{w}-{\rm i}t)\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, as
t\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle R_{k}^{t}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\det\big[K_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{C}}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k}\cdot(1+o(1)),

(2.10)

where the
o(1)
-term is uniform on
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}
in compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
and

\displaystyle K_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,w):={\rm e}^{-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}+2z\bar{w}}\,\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}(z+\bar{w}).

(2.11)

We emphasise that the kernel (
2.11
) corresponds to that of the complex Ginibre ensemble at the edge of the spectrum (up to a trivial rescaling), which is known to form a determinantal point process, see, e.g.,
[
20
,
30
,
36
]
. Therefore the convergence (
2.10
) implies that away from the real axis, the local edge statistics of the symplectic and complex Ginibre ensemble are equivalent in the large-
N
limit.
We also refer the reader to
[
27
,
46
]
and
[
7
]
for the equivalence of the symplectic and complex Ginibre ensemble in the context of the scaled maximal modulus and the local bulk statistics away from the real axis, respectively.

Before moving on to our next topic, let us give some further remarks on Theorem
2.1
. These complete the asymptotic study of the symplectic Ginibre ensemble with the Gaussian potential.

Remark 2.3
.

(i)

Recently, the scaling limit of the symplectic Ginibre ensemble at the edge of the spectrum has been obtained independently by Khoruzhenko and Lysychkin
[
38
]
, see also
[
43
]
for more details. Contrary to our approach using a differential equation satisfied by the pre-kernel, their methods are based on contour integral representations, which yields the same result.

(ii)

For
p\in\big({-}\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}\big)
, the pre-kernel (
2.6
) can be obtained from the same method used in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Indeed, the pre-kernel (
2.6
) can be represented by (
2.8
) with
E=(-\infty,\infty)
using the well-known integral

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}{\rm e}^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\operatorname{erf}(ax+b)\,{\rm d}x=\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{a\mu+b}{\sqrt{1+2a^{2}\sigma^{2}}}\right).

The same integral identity holds for the complementary error function, replacing
\operatorname{erf}\to\operatorname{erfc}
.

(iii)

Theorem
2.1
can be generalised to a moving boundary point
p_{N}
. More precisely, if we set

\displaystyle p_{N}:=\sqrt{2}-a\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}},\qquad a\in{\mathbb{R}},

and rescale the process as (
2.2
), then the limiting correlation kernel is of the form (
2.8
) with
E=(-\infty,a).
When
a\gg 1
we recover the bulk limit due to (ii).

(iv)

It is well known that the local bulk/edge correlation kernel
K^{\mathbb{C}}
of the random normal matrix ensemble is given by

\displaystyle K^{\mathbb{C}}(z,w)={\rm e}^{z\bar{w}-|z|^{2}/2-|w|^{2}/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{E}{\rm e}^{-(z+\bar{w}-u)^{2}/2}\,{\rm d}u,

where
E=(-\infty,\infty)
for the bulk case and
E=(-\infty,0)
for the edge case, see
[
11
,
33
]
.
Thus one can interpret the integral representation (
2.8
) as a symplectic analogue of the above expression.

(v)

In
[
8
]
, the authors studied the ensemble (
1.1
) with the elliptic potential

\displaystyle Q(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\big(|\zeta|^{2}-\tau\operatorname{Re}\zeta^{2}\big),\qquad\tau\in[0,1).

For this model, the local correlation kernel at the right/left endpoint of the spectrum was derived in the regime of weak non-Hermiticity when
1-\tau=O\big(N^{-1/3}\big)
. The kernel (
2.8
) was derived there as well from the large argument limit of such an intermediate process, see
[
8
, equation (4.28)]
.

(vi)

It was proved in
[
5
]
that the limiting local kernel of the chiral complex Ginibre ensemble at multi-criticality is given by the edge kernel of the complex Ginibre ensembles in squared variables. A quaternion version of such a result will appear in future work.

Next, we investigate ensembles containing certain types of singularities at the origin. More precisely, we consider the
Mittag-Leffler ensemble
whose associated potential
Q
is of the form

\displaystyle Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2\lambda}-\frac{2c}{N}\log|\zeta|,\qquad\lambda>0,\quad c>-1.

(2.12)

Here the condition
c>-1
is required to guarantee
Z_{N}<\infty
, where
Z_{N}
is the partition function in (
1.1
). Note that when
c\not=0
, a “conical singularity” at the origin arises from an insertion of a point charge
c
.
On the other hand, since the limiting global density of the ensemble is
\Delta\tfrac{1}{2}Q(\zeta)=\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}|\zeta|^{2\lambda-2}
, when
\lambda>1
(resp.,
\lambda<1
) it vanishes (resp., diverges) at the origin.

We aim to discover the local statistics of the symplectic Mittag-Leffler ensemble, which provides “non-standard” or multi-critical universality classes (
2.14
) beyond (
2.6
).
Away from the origin we expect to be back in the Gaussian universality class of the Ginibre ensemble.
Note that the micro-scale at the origin is given here by

\displaystyle r_{N}=\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}N\right)^{-\frac{1}{2\lambda}}.

(2.13)

For each
\lambda>0
and
c>-1
, the rescaled limiting local kernel
K_{\lambda,c}
at
p=0
is of the form

Remark 2.9

(Ward’s equation and universality for the random normal matrix ensemble with translation invariant kernel)
.

We pause here to briefly introduce recent developments in Ward’s equation and its significance in the context of universality for the random normal matrix ensemble.
Hopefully this gives more intuition as to why we aim to examine Ward’s equation for symplectic ensembles as well.

For random normal matrix ensembles, the rescaled version of Ward’s equation was studied in
[
13
]
. An important feature of this equation is that in the large-
N
limit, it does not depend on the choice of potential
Q
if
p
is regular, in that sense that
0<\Delta Q(p)<\infty
is non-vanishing and bounded. Due to this property, Ward’s equation has been utilised to show the local universality conjectures in various situations, see, e.g.,
[
10
,
14
,
15
]
and references therein.
To be more precise, the overall strategy for the universality proof using Ward’s equation is as follows.

•

Derivation of Ward’s equation
(cf.
[
13
, Theorem 1.3]
,
[
14
, Lemma 2]
,
[
15
, Lemma 3.1]
). The first step is to show that for a
C^{2}
-smooth potential
Q
and a regular point
p
, the following form of Ward’s equation holds:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\bar{\partial}}\mathcal{C}(z)=\mathcal{R}(z)-1-\Delta\log\mathcal{R}(z),\\
&\mathcal{C}(z):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}(z)}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathcal{R}(z)\mathcal{R}(w)-\mathcal{R}_{2}(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w),\end{split}

(2.32)

where
\mathcal{R}\equiv\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
are the (limiting) rescaled
1
- and
2
-point functions of the random normal matrix ensemble with potential
Q
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}(z)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{(N\Delta Q(p))^{k}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N,k}\left(p+\frac{z}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q(p)}}\right).

Here
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N,k}
denotes the unscaled
k
-point functions.
We emphasise that due to the rescaling factor
\sqrt{N\Delta Q(p)}
chosen according to the mean eigenvalue spacing of the random normal matrix model, the equation (
2.32
) does not depend on the choice of
Q
.

•

Structure of the correlation kernel
(cf.
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
,
[
15
, Theorem 1.3]
). The next step is to show that for a general potential
Q
and a regular point
p
, the limiting correlation kernel
\mathcal{K}
exists and is of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(z,w)=\mathcal{G}(z,w)\Psi(z,w),\qquad\mathcal{G}(z,w):={\rm e}^{z\bar{w}-|z|^{2}/2-|w|^{2}/2},

where
\Psi
is Hermitian, i.e.,
\Psi(z,w)=\overline{\Psi(w,z)}
and
\Psi
is entire as a function of
z
and
\bar{w}
.

•

Characterisation of translation-invariant solution to Ward’s equation
(cf.
[
14
, Theorem 4]
). The third step is to show that the only non-trivial horizontally translation-invariant solutions
\mathcal{R}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{R}(z+t)=\mathcal{R}(z)
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}

along the real axis) to Ward’s equation (
2.32
) are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{I}{\rm e}^{-(2\operatorname{Im}z-t)^{2}/2}\,{\rm d}t,

where
I
is a connected interval. Due to this step, the possible translation-invariant scaling limit is determined only by one interval
I
.

•

Specifying the interval
(cf.
[
13
, Theorem 1.5]
,
[
10
, Theorem 3.8]
). The interval
I
depends on the situation. For instance,
I={\mathbb{R}}
if
p
is in the bulk
[
11
,
12
]
and
I=(-\infty,0)
if
p
is at the edge
[
13
,
33
]
of the droplet. Furthermore,
I=(-a,a)
for some
a>0
if one considers the almost-Hermitian limit
[
10
]
or
p
is close to a cusp type singularity
[
14
]
. Determining the interval in each situation requires a separate analysis.

•

Translation invariance of the correlation functions
. The translation invariance is easy to check if
Q
is radially symmetric. As a consequence, for a radially symmetric potential, edge universality was shown in
[
13
, Theorem 1.8]
.

We remark that for a more general class of non-radially symmetric potentials, edge universality was recently shown by Hedenmalm and Wennman in
[
33
]
.
The authors used a different approach based on the theory of quasi-orthogonal polynomials.
However, this theory is not directly applicable to the symplectic ensemble since it is far from obvious how to construct
skew-orthogonal polynomials using (quasi-)orthogonal polynomials in general
(see however
[
6
]
).

In contrast, the overall strategy using Ward’s equation described above can be applied in parallel to the symplectic ensemble.
This is the primary purpose of our remaining discussion.
In the rest of this section, as an analogue of
[
14
]
, we aim to particularly address the characterisation of the translation-invariant solution to Ward’s equation for the symplectic ensemble.

Our next result is the resulting Ward’s equation for symplectic ensembles. For this, let

\displaystyle C_{N}(z):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{B_{N}(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w).

Then, we obtain the following form of Ward’s equation at finite-
N
for general
Q
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Suppose that
Q
is
C^{2}
-smooth and
p\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then for each

we have

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}C_{N}(z)=R_{N}(z)-\frac{Nr_{N}^{2}}{2}\Delta Q(p+r_{N}z)-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\log R_{N}(z)+\frac{1}{(z-\bar{z})^{2}}.

(2.33)

Let us discuss the large-

limit of Ward’s equation.
Since the micro-scale

is given by (

), if

\displaystyle r_{N}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N\Delta Q(p)}}(1+o(1)).

(2.34)

This leads to

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{Nr_{N}^{2}}{2}\Delta Q(p+r_{N}z)=1.

Therefore if we formally take the large-

limit of Ward’s equation (

) and if

is non-trivial, for a general potential

and

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}C(z)=R(z)-1-\dfrac{1}{2}\Delta\log R(z)+\dfrac{1}{(z-\bar{z})^{2}},

\displaystyle C(z):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{B(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w).

One may compare Ward’s equation (

Remark 2.11

As an analogue of

3
Scaling limits of the Ginibre ensemble

In the first part of this section, we review the canonical representation of the correlation kernel in terms of skew-orthogonal polynomials. In the second part, we prove Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
.

3.1
Skew-orthogonal polynomials

For a given potential
Q
, the
anti-symmetric scalar product

\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle\langle h|g\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\bar{\zeta}-\zeta\big){\rm e}^{-NQ(\zeta)}\big(h(\zeta)\overline{g(\zeta)}-\overline{h(\zeta)}g(\zeta)\big)\,{\rm d}A(\zeta).

By definition, a family
\{q_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
of polynomials is called

skew-orthogonal polynomials
associated with
Q
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\langle q_{2k+1}|q_{2l}\rangle=-\langle q_{2l}|q_{2k+1}\rangle=s_{k}\,\delta_{kl},\qquad\langle q_{2k+1}|q_{2l+1}\rangle=\langle q_{2l}|q_{2k}\rangle=0,

where
\delta_{kl}
is the Kronecker delta and
s_{k}
is a positive number that depends only on
k
.

For a radially symmetric potential
Q
, let

\displaystyle h_{j}:=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\zeta|^{2j}{\rm e}^{-NQ(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}A(\zeta).

Then by
[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
(see also
[
34
, p. 7]
)

\displaystyle q_{2k+1}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2k+1},\qquad q_{2k}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2k}+\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\zeta^{2l}\prod_{j=0}^{k-l-1}\frac{h_{2l+2j+2}}{h_{2l+2j+1}}

(3.1)

form a family of skew-orthogonal polynomials associated with the potential
Q
. Here we have
s_{k}=2h_{2k+1}.

Recall that the particle system (
1.1
) forms a Pfaffian point process, namely, the
k
-point correlation function

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{N,k}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k}):=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\frac{N!}{(N-k)!}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N-k}}{\rm e}^{-{\mathbf{H}}_{N}(\bm{\zeta})}\prod_{j=k+1}^{N}{\rm d}A(\zeta_{j})

(3.2)

is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{N,k}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\big(\bar{\zeta}_{j}-\zeta_{j}\big){\operatorname{Pf}}\big[{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta_{j},\zeta_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k},

(3.3)

where the
2\times 2
matrix-valued kernel
{\mathbf{K}}_{N}
is of the form

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta,\eta)={\rm e}^{-NQ(\zeta)/2-NQ(\eta)/2}\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}(\zeta,\eta)&{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}(\zeta,\bar{\eta})\\
{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}(\bar{\zeta},\eta)&{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\eta})\end{pmatrix}.

(3.4)

In particular, we write
\mathbf{R}_{N}\equiv\mathbf{R}_{N,1}
for the
1
-point function.
It is well known that the pre-kernel
{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}
takes the form

\displaystyle{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}(\zeta,\eta)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{q_{2k+1}(\zeta)q_{2k}(\eta)-q_{2k}(\zeta)q_{2k+1}(\eta)}{s_{k}},

(3.5)

see
[
35
]
, where the quaternionic determinant
Q\det
is used instead of the Pfaffian
{\operatorname{Pf}}
. We also refer to
[
44
]
for the relation between
Q\det
and
{\operatorname{Pf}}
.

The relation between the pre-kernels
{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}
and
\kappa_{N}

for the change of variables (
2.4
)
at point
p\in S
is given as

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}(z,w):=r_{N}^{3}\begin{cases}{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}(p+r_{N}z,p+r_{N}w)&\text{if }p\in\textup{int}(S),\\
{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}\big(p+r_{N}z{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta},p+r_{N}w{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}\big)&\text{if }p\in\partial S.\end{cases}

(3.6)

Thus the rescaled process
\bm{z}
retains its Pfaffian structure in terms of the rescaled matrix-valued kernel

\displaystyle K_{N}(z,w):={\rm e}^{-\frac{N}{2}(Q(\zeta)+Q(\eta))}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{N}(z,w)&\kappa_{N}(z,\bar{w})\\
\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},w)&\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix},

(3.7)

cf. (
2.4
) for the unscaled variables
\zeta
and
\eta
inside the arguments of the potential
Q
. We remark that in the factor
r_{N}^{3}
in (
3.6
), one factor
r_{N}
comes from the term
\prod_{j=1}^{k}\big(\bar{\zeta}_{j}-\zeta_{j}\big)
in (
3.3
) (see (
2.3
)), whereas the other two factors originate from the change of variables for the pre-kernel. This leads precisely to (
2.3
) as follows

\displaystyle R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=r_{N}^{2k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\big(\bar{\zeta}_{j}-\zeta_{j}\big){\operatorname{Pf}}\big[{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta_{j},\zeta_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\bar{\zeta}_{j}-\zeta_{j}}{r_{N}}{\operatorname{Pf}}\big[K_{N}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k}.

3.2
Boundary Ginibre point processes

For the Gaussian case
Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2}
, by (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle q_{2k+1}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2k+1},\qquad q_{2k}(\zeta)=\frac{(2k)!!}{N^{k}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{N^{l}}{(2l)!!}\zeta^{2l},\qquad s_{k}=2\frac{(2k+1)!}{N^{2k+2}}.

Therefore it follows from (
3.5
) that the associated kernel
{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}
has an expression

\displaystyle{\bm{\varkappa}}_{N}(\zeta,\eta):=\bm{G}_{N}(\zeta,\eta)-\bm{G}_{N}(\eta,\zeta),

(3.8)

where

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{N}(\zeta,\eta):=\frac{N\sqrt{N}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{N}\zeta\big)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(\sqrt{N}\eta)^{2l}}{(2l)!!}.

This recovers the expression obtained in Mehta’s book
[
44
]
in a different approach, compared to the skew-orthogonal polynomials we use here from
[
35
]
.

By definition, a function
c(z,w)
is called a
cocycle
if there exists a (continuous) unimodular function
h
(i.e.,
h(z)=h(1/\bar{z})
) such that
c(z,w)=h(z)h(w).
Since cocycles cancel out when multiplying the pre-kernel and taking the Pfaffian, if
(c_{N})_{N=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of cocycles, then
c_{N}\kappa_{N}
is an equivalent realisation of the pre-kernel
\kappa_{N}

for the same point process
\bm{z}
. Let us denote by
c_{N}\cdot K_{N}
the correlation kernel associated with
c_{N}\kappa_{N}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle c_{N}\cdot K_{N}(z,w):={\rm e}^{-\frac{N}{2}(Q(\zeta)+Q(\eta))}\begin{pmatrix}c_{N}(z,w)\,\kappa_{N}(z,w)&c_{N}(z,\bar{w})\,\kappa_{N}(z,\bar{w})\\
c_{N}(\bar{z},w)\,\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},w)&c_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\,\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix}.

Now we prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We prove the theorem for
p=\sqrt{2}
only. The other case
p=-\sqrt{2}
can be proved in the same way.
Note that for
Q=|\zeta|^{2},
we have
r_{N}=\sqrt{2/N}
, see (
2.1
).

By (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle K_{N}(z,w)={\rm e}^{-|\sqrt{N}+z|^{2}-|\sqrt{N}+w|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{N}(z,w)&\kappa_{N}(z,\bar{w})\\
\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},w)&\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix}.

Here it follows from (
3.6
) and (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}(z,w)=G_{N}(z,w)-G_{N}(w,z),

where

\displaystyle G_{N}(z,w)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2l}}{(2l)!!}.

(3.9)

Let us write

\displaystyle\widehat{\kappa}_{N}(z,w):={\rm e}^{-2(\sqrt{N}+z)(\sqrt{N}+w)}\kappa_{N}(z,w).

(3.10)

Then we have

\displaystyle K_{N}(z,w)={\rm e}^{-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}c_{N}(z,w)\widehat{\kappa}_{N}(z,w){\rm e}^{2zw}&c_{N}(z,\bar{w})\widehat{\kappa}_{N}(z,\bar{w}){\rm e}^{2z\bar{w}}\\
c_{N}(\bar{z},w)\widehat{\kappa}_{N}(\bar{z},w){\rm e}^{2\bar{z}w}&c_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\widehat{\kappa}_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w}){\rm e}^{2\bar{z}\bar{w}}\end{pmatrix},

where the cocycle
c_{N}
is given by

c_{N}(z,w):={\rm e}^{2{\rm i}\sqrt{N}\operatorname{Im}(z+w)}
.

We claim that
\widehat{\kappa}:=\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\widehat{\kappa}_{N}
satisfies the differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\widehat{\kappa}(z,w)=2(z-w)\widehat{\kappa}(z,w)+\operatorname{erfc}(z+w)-\frac{{\rm e}^{(z-w)^{2}-2z^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\big(\sqrt{2}w\big).

(3.11)

Here the uniform convergence of
\widehat{\kappa}_{N}
on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
follows from the Weierstrass
M
-test, see
[
6
, Section 4]
for similar computations.

Differentiating (
3.9
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z}G_{N}(z,w)=2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2k}}{(2k-1)!!}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2l}}{(2l)!!}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial_z G_N(z,w)=}{}+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2k}}{(2k-1)!!}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2k}}{(2k)!!}.

Rearranging the terms, we rewrite this equation as

\displaystyle\partial_{z}G_{N}(z,w)=2\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2l}}{(2l)!!}

\displaystyle{}+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2k}}{(2k-1)!!}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2k}}{(2k)!!}-2\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2N}}{(2N-1)!!}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2l}}{(2l)!!}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\partial_{z}G_{N}(w,z)=2\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2l}}{(2l)!!}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial_z G_N(w,z)=}{}-2\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}w\big)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2k}}{(2k)!!}.

Now let us recall the definition of the (regularised) incomplete Gamma function
[
45
, Chapter 8]

\displaystyle Q(n,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{z^{k}{\rm e}^{-z}}{k!},\qquad n=1,2,\dots.

Combining all of the above equations with (
3.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\widehat{\kappa}_{N}(z,w)=2(z-w)\widehat{\kappa}_{N}(z,w)+2Q(2N,\lambda)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial_z \widehat{\kappa}_N(z,w)=}{}-2{\rm e}^{(z-w)^{2}}{\rm e}^{-(\sqrt{N}+z)^{2}}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2N}}{(2N-1)!!}Q\big(N,\widetilde{\lambda}\big),

(3.12)

where

\displaystyle\lambda:=2\big(\sqrt{N}+z\big)\big(\sqrt{N}+w\big),\qquad\widetilde{\lambda}:=\big(\sqrt{N}+w\big)^{2}.

We now derive (
3.11
) from (
3.12
). By combining Stirling’s formula with the elementary asymptotic

{\rm e}^{-2\sqrt{N}z}\big(1+\tfrac{z}{\sqrt{N}}\big)^{2N}={\rm e}^{-z^{2}}(1+o(1))
,
we have

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-(\sqrt{N}+z)^{2}}\frac{\big(\sqrt{2N}+\sqrt{2}z\big)^{2N}}{(2N-1)!!}=\frac{{\rm e}^{-2z^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}(1+o(1)),

(3.13)

where the
o(1)
-terms are uniform on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
.

On the other hand, the incomplete Gamma function satisfies the asymptotic

\displaystyle Q\big(a,a+\sqrt{2a}z\big)=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}(z)(1+o(1)),\qquad a\to\infty,

(3.14)

see
[
45
, equation (8.11.10)]
. Substituting (
3.13
), (
3.14
) to the equation (
3.12
), and taking the limit as
N\to\infty
, we obtain (
3.11
).

Let us define

\displaystyle f(z,w):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\rm e}^{-2(z-u)^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\big(\sqrt{2}(w-u)\big)-{\rm e}^{-2(w-u)^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\big(\sqrt{2}(z-u)\big)\,{\rm d}u.

Then integration by parts gives that

\displaystyle\partial_{z}f(z,w)=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\rm e}^{-2(z-u)^{2}-2(w-u)^{2}}\,{\rm d}u-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big[{\rm e}^{-2(z-u)^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\big(\sqrt{2}(w-u)\big)\big]_{-\infty}^{0},

which leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{z}f(z,w)={\rm e}^{-(z-w)^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(z+w)-\frac{{\rm e}^{-2z^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{erfc}\big(\sqrt{2}w\big).\end{split}

Therefore
\widehat{\kappa}(z,w):={\rm e}^{(z-w)^{2}}f(z,w)
is an anti-symmetric solution to (
3.11
).
Since (
3.11
) is a first-order differential equation, the anti-symmetry plays the role of the initial value, which determines the solution uniquely.
This completes the proof.
∎

We now prove Corollary
2.2
.

Proof of Corollary
2.2
.

By (
2.2
), for
p_{N}={\rm e}^{i\theta_{N}}
with
\theta_{N}=\frac{t}{\sqrt{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{j}=\sqrt{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}(z_{j}+{\rm i}t)}{\sqrt{N}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),\qquad\frac{\bar{\zeta}_{j}-\zeta_{j}}{r_{N}}=\bar{z}_{j}-z_{j}-2{\rm i}t+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

Therefore the first assertion (
2.9
) follows along the same lines of the proof of Theorem
2.1
by replacing
(z,w)\mapsto(z+{\rm i}t,w+{\rm i}t)
.

We now prove (
2.10
). By (
2.9
), we have

\displaystyle R_{k}^{t}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\sim{\operatorname{Pf}}\big[{-}2{\rm i}tK_{{\rm edge}}^{t}(z,w)\big]_{j,l=1}^{k}

(3.15)

as
t\to\infty
.
Here
f(x)\sim g(x)
means that
f(x)/g(x)
tends to
1
as
x\to\infty
.
Using the following asymptotic of the error function (see, e.g.,
[
45
, equation (7.12.1)]
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{erfc}(z)\sim\frac{{\rm e}^{-z^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,z},\qquad z\to\infty,

we have that as
t\to\infty,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-|z+{\rm i}t|^{2}-|w+{\rm i}t|^{2}}\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z+{\rm i}t,\bar{w}-{\rm i}t)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{{\rm i}}{\sqrt{\pi}t}{\rm e}^{-(z+{\rm i}t)(z+\bar{z})-(\bar{w}-{\rm i}t)(w+\bar{w})}\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\rm e}^{-4u^{2}+4u(z+\bar{w})}\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{4t}{\rm e}^{-|z|^{2}-|w|^{2}+2z\bar{w}}\operatorname{erfc}(z+\bar{w}){\rm e}^{-(z+\bar{z}){\rm i}t+(w+\bar{w}){\rm i}t}.

Therefore we obtain that as
t\to\infty
,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-|z+{\rm i}t|^{2}-|w+{\rm i}t|^{2}}\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z+{\rm i}t,\bar{w}-{\rm i}t)\sim\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}\widetilde{K}(z,w),

(3.16)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}(z,w):=c(z,w)\cdot K_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,w).

(3.17)

Here
c(z,w)={\rm e}^{-(z+\bar{z}){\rm i}t+(w+\bar{w}){\rm i}t}
is a cocycle for the determinantal point process.
On the other hand, it follows from similar computations that as
t\to\infty,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-|z+{\rm i}t|^{2}-|w+{\rm i}t|^{2}}\kappa_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{R}}(z+{\rm i}t,w+{\rm i}t)=O\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right).

(3.18)

Combining (
3.15
), (
3.16
) and (
3.18
), we have

\displaystyle R_{k}^{t}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\sim{\operatorname{Pf}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\widetilde{K}(z_{1},z_{1})&0&\widetilde{K}(z_{1},z_{2})&\dots\\
-\widetilde{K}(z_{1},z_{1})&0&-\widetilde{K}(z_{2},z_{1})&0&\dots\\
0&\widetilde{K}(z_{2},z_{1})&0&\widetilde{K}(z_{2},z_{2})&\dots\\
-\widetilde{K}(z_{1},z_{2})&0&-\widetilde{K}(z_{2},z_{2})&0&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}_{2k\times 2k}

as
t\to\infty.
The remaining argument is similar to that used in
[
7
]
. By a proper reordering, we have

\displaystyle R_{k}^{t}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\sim(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}{\operatorname{Pf}}\begin{pmatrix}0&M\\
-M^{\rm T}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\big(\widetilde{K}(z_{j},z_{l})\big)_{j,l=1}^{k}.

Now it follows from the identity

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pf}}\begin{pmatrix}0&M\\
-M^{\rm T}&0\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\det(M)

and (
3.17
) that

\displaystyle R_{k}^{t}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\sim\det\big[\widetilde{K}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k}=\det\big[K_{{\rm edge}}^{\mathbb{C}}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4
Scaling limit of the Mittag-Leffler ensemble

In this section, we construct a fractional differential equation satisfied by the local kernel of the Mittag-Leffler ensemble with potentials
Q(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2\lambda}-(2c/N)\log|\zeta|
,
\lambda>0
and
c>-1
(Proposition
4.1
). As a consequence, we prove Theorem
2.4
.

4.1
Christoffel–Darboux type formula

For the Mittag-Leffler potentials (
2.12
), the orthogonal norm
h_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle h_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\zeta|^{2k}{\rm e}^{-NQ(\zeta)}\,{\rm d}A(\zeta)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{k+c+1}{\lambda}\big)}{\lambda N^{\frac{k+c+1}{\lambda}}}.

Then by (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle q_{2k+1}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2k+1},\qquad q_{2k}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2k}+\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{\zeta^{2l}}{N^{\frac{k-l}{\lambda}}}\prod_{j=0}^{k-l-1}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+2j+3+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+2j+2+c}{\lambda}\big)},

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle s_{k}=2h_{2k+1}=\frac{2}{\lambda}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{2k+c+2}{\lambda})}{N^{\frac{2k+c+2}{\lambda}}}.

(4.2)

To describe the Christoffel–Darboux type formula, let us recall that the Caputo fractional derivative
D^{\nu}_{z}
is given by

\displaystyle D^{\nu}_{z}f(z):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n-\nu)}\int_{0}^{z}(z-u)^{n-\nu-1}f^{(n)}(u)\,{\rm d}u,\qquad n-1<\nu<n,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

see
[
39
, Section 2.4]
. Here
f^{(n)}
is the usual
n
:th derivative.

Recall that
p=0

in the rescaling (
2.4
) here,
and
K_{\lambda,c}
is given by (
2.14
).

Proposition 4.1
.

For each
\lambda>0
and
c>-1
, there exists a sequence of cocycles
(c_{N})_{N=1}^{\infty}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}c_{N}\cdot K_{N}(z,w)=K_{\lambda,c}(z,w)={\rm e}^{-\frac{|z|^{2\lambda}+|w|^{2\lambda}}{\lambda}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{\lambda,c}(z,w)&\kappa_{\lambda,c}(z,\bar{w})\\
\kappa_{\lambda,c}(\bar{z},w)&\kappa_{\lambda,c}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix}

uniformly for
z
,
w
in compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
, where

\displaystyle\kappa_{\lambda,c}(z,w)=\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\lambda}}(zw)^{\lambda-1}\widetilde{\kappa}_{\lambda,c}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}}z^{\lambda},\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}}w^{\lambda}\right).

Here
\widetilde{\kappa}_{\lambda,c}
is given in terms of a function
\widetilde{G}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\kappa}_{\lambda,c}(z,w)=\widetilde{G}(z,w)-\widetilde{G}(w,z)

and the following fractional differential equations hold:

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(z,w)=z^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(z,w)+(zw)^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}E_{\frac{2}{\lambda}\frac{1+c}{\lambda}}\big((zw)^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\big),

(4.3)

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{\kappa}_{\lambda,c}(z,w)=z^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{\kappa}_{\lambda,c}(z,w)+(zw)^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}E_{\frac{1}{\lambda},\frac{1+c}{\lambda}}\big((zw)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{D_z^{ \frac{1}{\lambda} } \widetilde{\kappa}_{\lambda,c}(z,w)=}{}-\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{c}{\lambda}\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{2+c}{\lambda}\big)}(zw)^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}(w/z)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}E_{\frac{1}{\lambda},2,3+c-\lambda}\big(w^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\big).

(4.4)

We call the equation (
4.4
) (generalised) Christoffel–Darboux formula.
Such a differential equation satisfied by the correlation kernel, that makes the asymptotic analysis possible, is broadly called Christoffel–Darboux type formula, see, e.g.,
[
19
,
23
,
41
]
.

We also remark that the inhomogeneous term
(zw)^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}E_{\frac{1}{\lambda},\frac{1+c}{\lambda}}\big((zw)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\big)
corresponds to the kernel of the complex Mittag-Leffler ensemble, see
[
15
]
.
Such a relation has been observed for other models as well, which include the elliptic Ginibre ensemble
[
21
]
and its chiral counter part
[
2
]
.
See also
[
1
,
48
]
for a similar relation for Hermitian ensembles, which gives an expression of the kernel of the symplectic ensemble in terms of a small number of orthogonal polynomials.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let us define

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{N}(z,w):=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}z^{\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}-1}\prod_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+2+c}{\lambda}\big)}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{w^{\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j-1+c}{\lambda}\big)}.

Here and henceforth we use the convention that if
l=0
, then
\prod_{j=1}^{l}\varphi=1
.
By (
4.1
), (
4.2
) and (
2.13
), the kernel
K_{N}
is given by

\displaystyle K_{N}(z,w)={\rm e}^{-\frac{|z|^{2\lambda}+|w|^{2\lambda}}{\lambda}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{N}(z,w)&\kappa_{N}(z,\bar{w})\\
\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},w)&\kappa_{N}(\bar{z},\bar{w})\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}(z,w):=G_{N}(z,w)-G_{N}(w,z),

\displaystyle G_{N}(z,w):=\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\lambda}}(zw)^{\lambda-1}\widetilde{G}_{N}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}}z^{\lambda},\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}}w^{\lambda}\right).

By definition of the Caputo derivative, we have that if
k>\lceil\nu\rceil

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\nu}z^{k}=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k-\nu+1)}z^{k-\nu}

(4.5)

and vanishes otherwise.
Let us write
\widetilde{G}:=\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\widetilde{G}_{N},
where the convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
. The existence of the limit follows from the Weierstrass
M
-test.
By direct computations using (
4.5
), we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(z,w)-\frac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2+c}{\lambda}\big)}z^{\frac{2+c}{\lambda}-1}w^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}-1}\prod_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+2+c}{\lambda}\big)}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{w^{\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j-1+c}{\lambda}\big)}.

(4.6)

Note in particular that as
z\to 0,

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(z,w)\sim\frac{w^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2+c}{\lambda}\big)}z^{\frac{2+c}{\lambda}-1}.

(4.7)

Applying the operator
D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}
to the identity (
4.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(z,w)-\frac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\big)}(zw)^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{\frac{2k+1+c}{\lambda}-1}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j-1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+c}{\lambda}\big)}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{w^{\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j-1+c}{\lambda}\big)}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=z^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}-1}\prod_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+2+c}{\lambda}\big)}\sum_{l=0}^{k+1}\frac{w^{\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j-1+c}{\lambda}\big)},

which leads to

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(z,w)=z^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(z,w)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(zw)^{\frac{2k+1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}.

Now (
4.3
) follows from (
2.15
).

Similarly, by rearranging the terms, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(w,z)-\frac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\big)}z^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}w^{\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}-1}\prod_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+2+c}{\lambda}\big)}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w^{\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}-1}\prod_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+2+c}{\lambda}\big)}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{z^{\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j-1+c}{\lambda}\big)}.

(4.8)

Then by (
2.16
), we have that as
z\to 0
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(w,z)\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2+c}{\lambda}\big)}w^{\frac{2+c}{\lambda}-1}E_{\frac{1}{\lambda},2,3+c-\lambda}\big(w^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\big)z^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}

Again, by applying the operator
D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}
to (
4.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(w,z)-\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{c}{\lambda}\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{2+c}{\lambda}\big)}(zw)^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}(w/z)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}E_{\frac{1}{\lambda},2,3+c-\lambda}\big(w^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=z^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w^{\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}-1}\prod_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+2+c}{\lambda}\big)}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{z^{\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2l+1+c}{\lambda}\big)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j+c}{\lambda}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2j-1+c}{\lambda}\big)}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=z^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(w,z)-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(zw)^{\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}-1}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+2+c}{\lambda}\big)}.

Then by (
2.15
), we have

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(w,z)=z^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\widetilde{G}(w,z)-(zw)^{\frac{2+c}{\lambda}-1}E_{\frac{2}{\lambda},\frac{2+c}{\lambda}}\big((zw)^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{D_z^{ \frac{1}{\lambda} } \widetilde{G}(w,z)=}{}+\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+c}{\lambda})}{\Gamma(\frac{c}{\lambda})\Gamma(\frac{2+c}{\lambda})}(zw)^{\frac{1+c}{\lambda}-1}(w/z)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}E_{\frac{1}{\lambda},2,3+c-\lambda}\big(w^{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\big).

(4.9)

Now (
4.4
) follows from (
4.3
), (
4.9
) and (
2.15
).
∎

4.2
Bulk singularities of the order
\bm{\lambda=\frac{1}{m}}
with
\bm{m\in\mathbb{N}}

A point
p
in which the eigenvalue density
\frac{1}{2}\Delta Q(p)
vanishes or diverges is called bulk singularity, see
[
15
]
and references therein. In this subsection, we shall consider the case
\lambda=\frac{1}{m}
, where
m
is a positive integer and prove Theorem
2.4
for general
c>-1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

By (
4.3
) and (
4.7
), the function
\widetilde{G}
is a unique solution to the
m
-th order linear (ordinary) differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{m}\widetilde{G}(z,w)=z^{m}\widetilde{G}(z,w)+F_{m,c}(zw),

(4.10)

which satisfies the asymptotic behaviour

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(z,w)\sim\frac{w^{m+mc-1}}{\Gamma(2m+mc)}z^{2m+mc-1},\qquad z\to 0.

(4.11)

Here the function
F_{m,c}
is given by (
2.17
). Note that the
m
initial conditions are provided by differentiating (
4.11
).

We aim to solve the initial value problem (
4.10
) and (
4.11
).
It is well known that the functions
g_{j,m}
given by (
2.18
) provide the solutions to the homogeneous equation of (
4.10
), see, e.g.,
[
32
, Theorem 5.18]
.
By (
2.16
), we have that

\displaystyle g_{j,m}(z)\sim z^{j-1},\qquad z\to 0.

(4.12)

Using this, it is easy to observe that

\displaystyle W(g_{1,m},\dots,g_{m,m})(z)|_{z=0}=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}j!=G(m+1),

where
G
is the Barnes
G
-function
[
45
, Section 5.17]
. Due to Abel’s identity, for
m>1
, this leads to

\displaystyle W(g_{1,m},\dots,g_{m,m})(z)\equiv G(m+1).

(4.13)

On the other hand for
m=1
, we have obviously
W(g_{1})(z)=g_{1}(z).

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, let us write

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(z,w):=\det\begin{bmatrix}g_{1,m}(z)&\dots&g_{j-1,m}(z)&0&g_{j+1,m}(z)&\dots&g_{m,m}(z)\\
g_{1,m}^{\prime}(z)&\dots&g_{j-1,m}^{\prime}(z)&0&g_{j+1,m}^{\prime}(z)&\dots&g_{m,m}^{\prime}(z)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
g_{1,m}^{(m-1)}(z)&\dots&g_{j-1,m}^{(m-1)}(z)&F_{m,c}(zw)&g_{j+1,m}^{(m-1)}(z)&\dots&g_{m,m}^{(m-1)}(z)\end{bmatrix}.

Note here that by (
2.19
), we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(z,w)=(-1)^{m-j}F_{m,c}(zw)W_{j,m}(z).

(4.14)

From now on, we shall only consider the case
m>1
. The other case
m=1
follows along the same lines. In this case, one can also easily solve the associated linear non-homogenous ODE of degree
1
employing an integrating factor.

By virtue of the method of variation of parameters and (
4.13
), one can write

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1}{W(g_{1,m},\dots,g_{m,m})(s)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(s,w)g_{j,m}(z)\,{\rm d}s+\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_{j,m}(w)g_{j,m}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G(m+1)}\int_{0}^{z}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(s,w)g_{j,m}(z)\,{\rm d}s+\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_{j,m}(w)g_{j,m}(z)

for some functions
C_{j,m}
. We shall show that in the above expression
\widetilde{G}
satisfies the asymptotic behaviour (
4.11
) if and only if
C_{j,m}\equiv 0
for every
j
.

By (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), direct computations of the determinant give that as
z\to 0
,

\displaystyle W_{j,m}(z)\sim\frac{G(m+1)}{(m-1)!}\binom{m-1}{j-1}z^{m-j}.

On the other hand, by (
2.15
), as
z\to 0
,

\displaystyle F_{m,c}(zw)\sim\frac{w^{m+mc-1}}{\Gamma(m+mc)}z^{m+mc-1}.

Combining these two equations, we have that as
z\to 0
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(z,w)\sim\frac{w^{m+mc-1}}{\Gamma(m+mc)}(-1)^{m-j}\frac{G(m+1)}{(m-1)!}\binom{m-1}{j-1}z^{2m+mc-1-j}.

This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G(m+1)}\int_{0}^{z}\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(s,w)g_{j,m}(z)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad{}\sim\frac{w^{m+mc-1}}{\Gamma(m+mc)}\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\binom{m-1}{j-1}\frac{(-1)^{m-j}}{2m+mc-j}z^{2m+mc-1}.

Next, we show the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m-1}{j-1}\frac{(-1)^{m-j}}{2m+mc-j}=\frac{\Gamma(m+mc)}{\Gamma(2m+mc)}.

(4.15)

By the binomial theorem, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{k}x^{m+mc-1+k}=x^{m+mc-1}(1+x)^{m-1}.

Integrating this identity, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{k}\frac{x^{m+mc+k}}{m+mc+k}=\int_{0}^{x}t^{m+mc-1}(1+t)^{m-1}\,{\rm d}t.

Letting
x=-1
and using the change of the variable
t=-s
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{k}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{m+mc+k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}s^{m+mc-1}(1-s)^{m-1}\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=B(m+mc,m)=(m-1)!\frac{\Gamma(m+mc)}{\Gamma(2m+mc)},

where
B
is the beta function. This gives (
4.15
).

Using (
4.15
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G(m+1)}\int_{0}^{z}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(s,w)g_{j,m}(z)\,{\rm d}s\sim\frac{w^{m+mc-1}}{\Gamma(2m+mc)}z^{2m+mc-1}.

Thus by (
4.11
), we obtain that
C_{j,m}\equiv 0
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.
Therefore by (
4.14
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G(m+1)}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(sz,w)zg_{j,m}(z)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G(m+1)}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\widetilde{W}_{j,m}(sz)zg_{j,m}(z)F_{m,c}(szw)\,{\rm d}s.

This completes the proof.
∎

5
Translation-invariant scaling limit and Ward’s equation

The following two subsections are devoted to proving Propositions
2.8
, the mass-one equation for the Ginibre
\infty
-point process, and
Ward’s equation at finite-
N
for general potentials, Proposition
2.10
.

5.1
Mass-one equation

In this subsection, we consider the Pfaffian
\infty
-point process with the correlation kernel (
2.28
).

By (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle R_{1}(z)=(\bar{z}-z){\rm e}^{-2|z|^{2}}\kappa(z,\bar{z})

and

\displaystyle R_{2}(z,w)=(\bar{z}-z)(\bar{w}-w){\rm e}^{-2|z|^{2}-2|w|^{2}}\big(\kappa(z,\bar{z})\kappa(w,\bar{w})-|\kappa(z,w)|^{2}+|\kappa(z,\bar{w})|^{2}\big).

Therefore the Berezin kernel is written as

\displaystyle B(z,w)=(\bar{w}-w){\rm e}^{-2|w|^{2}}\frac{|\kappa(z,w)|^{2}-|\kappa(z,\bar{w})|^{2}}{\kappa(z,\bar{z})}.

(5.1)

We now prove Proposition
2.8
.

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

By (
5.1
) and conjugation symmetry, the mass-one equation (
2.27
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\kappa(z,\bar{z})=2\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}(\bar{w}-w){\rm e}^{-2|w|^{2}}|\kappa(z,w)|^{2}\,{\rm d}A(w).

(5.2)

Note that the pre-kernel
\kappa\equiv\kappa_{a}^{\mathbb{R}}
given in (
2.29
) is written as

\displaystyle\kappa(z,w)=\sqrt{\pi}{\rm e}^{z^{2}+w^{2}}\int_{E}f^{\prime}(w-u)f(z-u)-f^{\prime}(z-u)f(w-u)\,{\rm d}u,

\displaystyle f(z):=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\big(\sqrt{2}z\big),

where
E\equiv E_{a}=(-\infty,a)
.
Using this expression, we rewrite the right-hand side of the equation (
5.2
) as

\displaystyle-4{\rm i}{\rm e}^{z^{2}+\bar{z}^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}\int_{E^{2}}F(u,v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y,

where

\displaystyle F(u,v)=\big(f^{\prime}(x+{\rm i}y-u)f(z-u)-f^{\prime}(z-u)f(x+{\rm i}y-u)\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{F(u,v) =}{}\times\big(f^{\prime}(x-{\rm i}y-v)f(\bar{z}-v)-f^{\prime}(\bar{z}-v)f(x-{\rm i}y-v)\big).

We now compute

\displaystyle g(u,v):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}f^{\prime}(x+{\rm i}y-u)f^{\prime}(x-{\rm i}y-v)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x.

Due to the translation invariance in
x
, it suffices to consider the case
v=0
. Then we have

\displaystyle g(u,0)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{-2(x+{\rm i}y-u)^{2}}{\rm e}^{-2(x-{\rm i}y)^{2}}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm e}^{-4x^{2}+4xu-2u^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}y{\rm e}^{4{\rm i}uy}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x.

Note that in the sense of distributions, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}y{\rm e}^{4{\rm i}uy}\,{\rm d}y=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}y{\rm e}^{-4{\rm i}uy}\,{\rm d}y=-\frac{1}{16}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}y{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}uy}\,{\rm d}y=\frac{\pi}{8{\rm i}}\delta^{\prime}(u).

Thus it follows from
\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{-4x^{2}}\,{\rm d}x=\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}
that

\displaystyle g(u,v)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{8{\rm i}}{\rm e}^{-(u-v)^{2}}\delta^{\prime}(u-v).

This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{8{\rm i}}{\sqrt{\pi}}{\rm e}^{(u-v)^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}F(u,v)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\qquad{}=f(z-u)f(\bar{z}-v)\delta^{\prime}(u-v)-f(z-u)f^{\prime}(\bar{z}-v)\delta(u-v)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+f^{\prime}(z-u)f(\bar{z}-v)\delta(u-v)-f^{\prime}(z-u)f^{\prime}(\bar{z}-v)\Theta(u-v),

where
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside theta function. Combining the above equations, interchanging the integrals, and using integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}(\bar{w}-w){\rm e}^{-2|w|^{2}}|\kappa(z,w)|^{2}\,{\rm d}A(w)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-4{\rm i}{\rm e}^{z^{2}+\bar{z}^{2}}\int_{E^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y\,{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}F(u,v)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sqrt{\pi}{\rm e}^{z^{2}+\bar{z}^{2}}\int_{E}f^{\prime}(\bar{z}-u)f(z-u)-f^{\prime}(z-u)f(\bar{z}-u)\,{\rm d}u=\kappa(z,\bar{z}),

which completes the proof.
∎

5.2
Ward’s equation at finite-
\bm{N}

In this subsection, we consider the symplectic ensemble (
1.1
) with general external potential
Q
and prove Proposition
2.10
. In the sequel, we assume that
\theta=0
without loss of generality.

Proof of Proposition
2.10
.

We denote by
{\mathbf{E}}_{N}
the expectation with respect to the Gibbs measure (
1.1
). Let
\psi_{N}
be a fixed test function. Integration by parts gives that, for each
j
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}[{\partial}\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j})]={\mathbf{E}}_{N}[{\partial}_{j}\mathbf{H}_{N}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N})\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j})],

where
\mathbf{H}_{N}
is the Hamiltonian given by (
1.2
).
Summing in
j
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbf{E}}_{N}[{\partial}\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j})]={\mathbf{E}}_{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j})\left[N{\partial}Q(\zeta_{j})-\frac{2}{\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle\hphantom{\sum_{j=1}^N{\mathbf{E}}_N [{\partial}\psi_N(\zeta_j)]=}{}-{\mathbf{E}}_{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j})\Bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
(k\neq j)\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k}}+\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{k}}\right)\Bigg].

Thus we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}{\mathrm{I}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]-{\mathbf{E}}_{N}{\mathrm{II}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]+{\mathbf{E}}_{N}{\mathrm{III}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]+{\mathbf{E}}_{N}\textup{IV}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=0,

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle{\mathrm{I}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k=1\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{N}\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j})\left(\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k}}+\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{k}}\right),\qquad

\displaystyle{\mathrm{II}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=N\sum_{j=1}^{N}[\psi_{N}{\partial}Q](\zeta_{j}),

\displaystyle{\mathrm{III}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\partial}\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j}),\qquad

\displaystyle\textup{IV}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{N}(\zeta_{j})\frac{2}{\zeta_{j}-\overline{\zeta}_{j}}.

Due to the conjugation symmetry
Q(\zeta)=Q\big(\bar{\zeta}\big)
, one can easily observe that for any
j
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{N}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{j},\dots,\zeta_{N})=\mathbf{H}_{N}\big(\zeta_{1},\dots,\bar{\zeta}_{j},\dots\zeta_{N}\big).

Then it follows from the definition (
3.2
) that
\mathbf{R}_{N,2}(\zeta,\eta)=\mathbf{R}_{N,2}(\zeta,\bar{\eta})
.
Using the change of variable
\eta\mapsto\bar{\eta}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathbf{R}_{N,2}(\zeta,\eta)}{\zeta-\bar{\eta}}\,{\rm d}A(\eta)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathbf{R}_{N,2}(\zeta,\eta)}{\zeta-\eta}\,{\rm d}A(\eta),

which leads to

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}\textup{I}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=2\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}\psi_{N}(\zeta)\frac{\mathbf{R}_{N,2}(\zeta,\eta)}{\zeta-\eta}\,{\rm d}A(\zeta)\,{\rm d}A(\eta).

Also it follows from the definition and integration by parts that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}\textup{II}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=N\int_{\mathbb{C}}\psi_{N}(\zeta)\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)\partial Q(\zeta)\,{\rm d}A(\zeta),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}\textup{III}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=-\int_{\mathbb{C}}\psi_{N}(\zeta)\partial\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}A(\zeta),\qquad{\mathbf{E}}_{N}\textup{IV}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=2\int_{\mathbb{C}}\psi_{N}(\zeta)\frac{\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)}{\zeta-\overline{\zeta}}\,{\rm d}A(\zeta).

To describe the rescaled version of Ward’s equation, let
\psi(z):=\psi_{N}(\zeta)
, where
\zeta=p+r_{N}z.
By the relation

\displaystyle R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=r_{N}^{2k}\,\mathbf{R}_{N,k}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k}),\qquad\zeta_{j}=p+r_{N}z_{j}

and the change of variables
\zeta\mapsto p+r_{N}z
,
\eta\mapsto p+r_{N}w
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}{\mathrm{I}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=\frac{2}{r_{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\psi(z)\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{R_{N,2}(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)\,{\rm d}A(z).

Similarly,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}{\mathrm{II}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=N\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\psi(z)\partial Q(p+r_{N}z)R_{N}(z)\,{\rm d}A(z),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{N}{\mathrm{III}}_{N}[\psi_{N}]=-\frac{1}{r_{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\psi(z)\partial R_{N}(z)\,{\rm d}A(z),\qquad{\mathbf{E}}_{N}\textup{IV}[\psi_{N}]=\frac{2}{r_{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\psi(z)\frac{R_{N}(z)}{z-\bar{z}}\,{\rm d}A(z).

Therefore by (
5.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{R_{N,2}(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)=\frac{Nr_{N}}{2}\partial Q(p+r_{N}z)R_{N}(z)+\frac{\partial R_{N}(z)}{2}-\frac{R_{N}(z)}{z-\bar{z}}.

Dividing by
R_{N}(z)
, this identity is rewritten in terms of Berezin kernel
B_{N}
as

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{B_{N}(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{R_{N}(w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)-\frac{Nr_{N}}{2}\partial Q(p+r_{N}z)-\frac{\partial\log R_{N}(z)}{2}+\frac{1}{z-\bar{z}}.

By taking
\bar{\partial}
derivative on both sides of this equation, Proposition
2.10
follows. ∎

In the next two subsections, we characterise translation-invariant scaling limits of symplectic ensembles, first, by solving the mass-one equation, and second, by the solution of the limiting Ward’s equation. This leads to the proof of Theorem
2.12
. By (
2.37
) together with (
2.38
), it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\Psi(z)=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{E}{\rm e}^{-u^{2}}\big({\rm e}^{2{\rm i}uz}-{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}uz}\big)\,\frac{{\rm d}u}{u},\qquad\widehat{J}(u)=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{{\rm i}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-u^{2}/4}}{u}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2),

(5.4)

where
E
is of the form
(-a,a)
with
a\in[0,\infty]
.

5.3
Translation-invariant solutions to the mass-one equation

In this subsection, we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Translation-invariant solution to the mass-one equation (
2.27
) are of the form (
5.4
), where
E
is some symmetric Borel set.

It follows from this proposition that the specified form of the pre-kernel (
2.39
) with any symmetric Borel set satisfies the mass-one equation (
2.27
).
However, in the following subsection, it will be shown that the if
E
is not a single interval, then the pre-kernel (
2.39
) does not satisfy Ward’s equation.
(An analogous result for the random normal matrix ensemble was obtained in
[
14
]
.)

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

In terms of
\Psi
, the mass-one equation (
5.2
) is written as

\displaystyle\Psi(z-\bar{z})=2\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}(\bar{w}-w){\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}|\Psi(z-w)|^{2}\,{\rm d}A(w).

By translation invariance, it is equivalent to the fact that for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\Psi(2{\rm i}t)=\frac{4}{\pi{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y\,{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}|\Psi(x+{\rm i}(y-t))|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y.

(5.5)

In terms of
\widehat{J}
, the equation (
5.5
) is written as

\displaystyle\Psi(2{\rm i}t)=\frac{1}{\pi^{3}{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}y\,{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(u+v)x}{\rm e}^{-(u-v)(y-t)}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(u+v)x}\,{\rm d}x=\delta(u+v),

the above equation is simplified to

\displaystyle\Psi(2{\rm i}t)=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{-2u(y-t)}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(-u)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u=\frac{2{\rm i}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{-2u(y-t)}\widehat{J}(u)^{2}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u.

Moreover, it follows from

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}-2uy}\,{\rm d}y=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{8}\,u\,{\rm e}^{u^{2}/4}

that the mass-one equation holds if and only if

\displaystyle f(t)=g(t),

(5.6)

where

\displaystyle f(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm e}^{-2ut}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u,\qquad g(t):=\frac{{\rm i}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm e}^{-2ut}\,u\,{\rm e}^{u^{2}/4}\widehat{J}(u)^{2}\,{\rm d}u.

Therefore we can readily see that the kernel (
2.39
) satisfies the mass-one equation.

Since
\widehat{J}\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
, we can extend the domain of
f
,
g
to the complex plane. Then by (
5.6
) we have
f(z)=g(z)
for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, which in particular leads to
f({\rm i}t)=g({\rm i}t)
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}ut}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u=\frac{{\rm i}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}ut}u{\rm e}^{u^{2}/4}\widehat{J}(u)^{2}\,{\rm d}u.

By Fourier inversion, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(u)\left(1+\frac{u{\rm e}^{u^{2}/4}}{2{\rm i}\sqrt{\pi}}\widehat{J}(u)\right)=0,\qquad\text{a.e.}

It gives rise to

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(u)=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{{\rm i}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-u^{2}/4}}{u}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2),\qquad\text{a.e.}

for some Borel set
E,
which is symmetric with respect to the origin since
\widehat{J}
is odd. Here the argument
u/2
is merely used for convenience.
∎

5.4
Translation-invariant solution of Ward’s equation

In this subsection, we characterise the translation-invariant solutions to Ward’s equation (
2.35
) and complete the proof of Theorem
2.12
.

Recall that by (
2.37
), we have

\displaystyle R(z)=-2{\rm i}\operatorname{Im}z{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}z)^{2}}\Psi(z-\bar{z}),

\displaystyle B(z,w)=-2{\rm i}\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}\frac{|\Psi(z-w)|^{2}-|\Psi(z-\bar{w})|^{2}}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}.

Let us first observe the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

Ward’s equation (
2.35
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\Psi\Psi^{\prime\prime}(z-\bar{z})-\Psi^{\prime}(z-\bar{z})^{2}+\dfrac{\Psi(z-\bar{z})^{2}}{(z-\bar{z})^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{4}{{\rm i}}\Psi(z-\bar{z})\int_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}\Psi(z-w)\overline{\Psi^{\prime}(z-w)}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}+\frac{1}{z-\bar{w}}\right){\rm d}A(w)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+\frac{4}{{\rm i}}\Psi^{\prime}(z-\bar{z})\int_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}|\Psi(z-w)|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}+\frac{1}{z-\bar{w}}\right){\rm d}A(w).

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle{\bar{\partial}}_{z}C(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{{\bar{\partial}}_{z}B(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)+\int B(z,w){\bar{\partial}}_{z}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}\right){\rm d}A(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{{\bar{\partial}}_{z}B(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)+B(z,z).

Since
B(z,z)=R(z)
, Ward’s equation (
2.35
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{{\bar{\partial}}_{z}B(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)=-1-\dfrac{1}{2}\Delta\log R(z)+\dfrac{1}{(z-\bar{z})^{2}}.

Notice also that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\Delta\log R(z)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\log\big(\operatorname{Im}z{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}\,z)^{2}}\Psi(z-\bar{z})\big)=\dfrac{1}{2(z-\bar{z})^{2}}-1+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\log\Psi(z-\bar{z}).

Thus one can rewrite above equation as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{{\bar{\partial}}_{z}B(z,w)}{z-w}\,{\rm d}A(w)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(z-\bar{z})}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(z-\bar{z})}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}\right)^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2(z-\bar{z})^{2}}.

Using the change of variable
w\mapsto\bar{w}
, the left-hand side of this equation is computed as

\displaystyle\frac{2}{{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}}{z-w}{\bar{\partial}}_{z}\left(\frac{|\Psi(z-w)|^{2}-|\Psi(z-\bar{w})|^{2}}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}\right){\rm d}A(w)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{2}{{\rm i}}\frac{1}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}\,w)^{2}}\Psi(z-w)\overline{\Psi^{\prime}(z-w)}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}+\frac{1}{z-\bar{w}}\right){\rm d}A(w)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+\frac{2}{{\rm i}}\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(z-\bar{z})}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}|\Psi(z-w)|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}+\frac{1}{z-\bar{w}}\right){\rm d}A(w).

Thus Ward’s equation is rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(z-\bar{z})}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(z-\bar{z})}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}\right)^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2(z-\bar{z})^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{2}{{\rm i}}\frac{1}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}\Psi(z-w)\overline{\Psi^{\prime}(z-w)}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}+\frac{1}{z-\bar{w}}\right){\rm d}A(w)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+\frac{2}{{\rm i}}\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(z-\bar{z})}{\Psi(z-\bar{z})^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Im}w\,{\rm e}^{-4(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}|\Psi(z-w)|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{z-w}+\frac{1}{z-\bar{w}}\right){\rm d}A(w).

∎

Let us write

\displaystyle L(t):=\frac{4}{\pi{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}|\Psi({\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y)|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{{\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y}+\frac{1}{{\rm i}t-x+{\rm i}y}\right){\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y,

\displaystyle K(t):=\frac{4}{\pi{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}\Psi({\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y)\overline{\Psi^{\prime}({\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y)}\left(\frac{1}{{\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y}+\frac{1}{{\rm i}t-x+{\rm i}y}\right){\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y.

We now show the following lemma, the key reduction of Ward’s equation.

Lemma 5.3
.

Ward’s equation (
2.35
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{L(t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}\right]=-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}+\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}\right]-8t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t).

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
, Ward’s equation holds if and only if for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle\Psi\Psi^{\prime\prime}(2{\rm i}t)-\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)^{2}-\dfrac{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)^{2}}{4t^{2}}=\Psi(2{\rm i}t)K(t)+\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)L(t).

(5.7)

Note that

\displaystyle\Psi(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}zu}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u,\qquad\Psi^{\prime}(z)=\frac{{\rm i}}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}zu}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u.

Using this, we have

\displaystyle L(t)=\frac{1}{\pi^{3}{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(u+v)x}{\rm e}^{-(u-v)(y-t)}\frac{\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)}{{\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{L(t) =}{}+\frac{1}{\pi^{3}{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(u+v)x}{\rm e}^{-(u-v)(y-t)}\frac{\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)}{{\rm i}t-x+{\rm i}y}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

and

\displaystyle K(t)=-\frac{1}{\pi^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(u+v)x}{\rm e}^{-(u-v)(y-t)}v\frac{\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)}{{\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{K(t) =}{}-\frac{1}{\pi^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}y\,{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(u+v)x}{\rm e}^{-(u-v)(y-t)}v\frac{\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)}{{\rm i}t-x+{\rm i}y}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v.

Let us first compute
L(t)
. It follows from the well-known Fourier transform of the following rational function that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(u+v)x}}{{\rm i}t-x-{\rm i}y}\,{\rm d}x=\operatorname{sgn}(u+v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{(u+v)(y-t)<0\}}{\rm e}^{(u+v)(y-t)}.

This leads to

\displaystyle L(t)=-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\operatorname{sgn}(u+v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{(u+v)(y-t)<0\}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}+2vy}{\rm e}^{-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{L(t) =}{}-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\operatorname{sgn}(u+v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{(u+v)(y+t)>0\}}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}-2uy}{\rm e}^{-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v.

Notice here that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}+2vy}\,{\rm d}y=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{16}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{v}{2}-2t\right)-\frac{1}{8}{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2vt},

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}+2vy}{\rm d}y=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{16}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{v}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{8}{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2vt}

and

\displaystyle\int_{-t}^{\infty}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}-2uy}\,{\rm d}y=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{16}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{u}{2}-2t\right)+\frac{1}{8}{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2ut},

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-t}y{\rm e}^{-4y^{2}-2uy}\,{\rm d}y=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{16}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{u}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{8}{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2ut}.

Using this, the function
L
is computed as

\displaystyle L(t)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v<0}\left(\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{v}{2}\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{L(t)=}{}+\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v>0}\left(-\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{v}{2}-2t\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{L(t)=}{}+\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v>0}\left(\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{u}{2}-2t\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{L(t)=}{}+\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v<0}\left(-\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{u}{2}\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle K(t)=\frac{-{\rm i}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v<0}v\left(\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{v}{2}\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{K(t) =}{}+\frac{{\rm i}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v>0}v\left(\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{v}{2}-2t\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{K(t) =}{}-\frac{{\rm i}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v>0}v\left(\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{u}{2}-2t\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{K(t) =}{}+\frac{{\rm i}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v<0}v\left(\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{u}{2}\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v.

Observe here that

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{v}{2}\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-2v\left(\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{v}{2}\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)-16t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{v}{2}-2t\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-2v\left(\sqrt{\pi}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{v}{2}-2t\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\right)+16t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{u}{2}-2t\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-2v\left(\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{u}{2}-2t\right)-2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)+16t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}

and

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\frac{u}{2}\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=-2v\left(\sqrt{\pi}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\operatorname{erfc}\left(2t-\tfrac{u}{2}\right)+2{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}\right)-16t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}+2(u-v)t}.

Combining the above equations, we obtain

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(t)=-2{\rm i}K(t)+M(t),

(5.8)

where

\displaystyle M(t):=\frac{2t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\rm e}^{2(u-v)t}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v=-8t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t)^{2}.

Substituting (
5.8
) into (
5.7
), we obtain that Ward’s equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Psi\Psi^{\prime\prime}(2{\rm i}t)-\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)^{2}-\dfrac{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)^{2}}{4t^{2}}=-\Psi(2{\rm i}t)\frac{L^{\prime}(t)-M(t)}{2{\rm i}}+\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)L(t).

Dividing each side of the identity by
\Psi(2{\rm i}t)^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}+\frac{1}{4t}\right]=-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{L(t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}\right]+\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\frac{M(t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)},

which completes the proof.
∎

In the following lemma, we analyse the structure of the function
L
by rewriting

\displaystyle L=L_{1}+L_{2},

(5.9)

where

\displaystyle L_{1}(t):={\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t)^{2}+\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{u}{2}-2t\right)\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u

(5.10)

and

\displaystyle L_{2}(t):=-\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{u+v>0}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\hphantom{L_2(t) :=}{}-\frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{u+v<0}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v.

(5.11)

Lemma 5.4
.

Ward’s equation (
2.35
) holds if and only if there exists a constant
c
such that

\displaystyle L_{2}(t)=-\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)-\left(\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}+c\right)\Psi(2{\rm i}t).

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3
and (
5.9
), we have shown that Ward’s equation (
2.35
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{L_{1}(t)+L_{2}(t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}\right]=-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}+\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}\right]-8t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t).

Notice that since

\displaystyle-2\pi{\rm i}\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u{\rm e}^{-2ut}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u,

we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{u}{2}-2t\right)\right]\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u=\frac{{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u{\rm e}^{2ut}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u=-2{\rm i}{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t).

Therefore by (
5.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{L_{1}(t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}\right]=-8t{\rm e}^{-4t^{2}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t).

Thus Ward’s equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{L_{2}(t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}\right]=-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left[\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)}{\Psi(2{\rm i}t)}+\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}\right].

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.12
. It results from this part of the proof that
E=(-a,a)
for some
a>0
.

Proof of Theorem
2.12
.

By Proposition
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(u)=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{{\rm i}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-u^{2}/4}}{u}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)

for some symmetric Borel set
E
. Suppose that
E=(-\alpha/2,\alpha/2)
for some
\alpha>0.
We shall compute
L_{2}
in (
5.11
).
Then we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v>0}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{v>-u}{\rm e}^{-2vt}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(v/2)\,{\rm d}v\,\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)\,\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}\frac{{\rm e}^{2ut}-{\rm e}^{-2\alpha t}}{2t}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u=\frac{1}{2t{\rm i}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-2ut}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u=-\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}\Psi(2{\rm i}t).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v<0}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{u<-v}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm e}^{-2vt}\,\mathbbm{1}_{E}(v/2)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}(\alpha-v){\rm e}^{-2vt}\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}v=-\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)-\frac{\alpha}{{\rm i}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t).

Combining the above equations, we obtain that

\displaystyle L_{2}(t)=-\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)-\left(\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}+\frac{\alpha}{{\rm i}}\right)\Psi(2{\rm i}t).

Then by Lemma
5.4
, we conclude that Ward’s equation holds.

It is also easy to see that if
E
is not connected, Ward’s equation does not hold due to the discontinuity of the functions
\int_{v>-u}{\rm e}^{-2vt}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(v/2)\,{\rm d}v
and
\int_{u<-v}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)\,{\rm d}u
. For instance let
E=(-\alpha/2,-\beta/2)\cup(\beta/2,\alpha/2)
for some
\alpha>\beta>0
.
Note that

\displaystyle\int_{v>-u}{\rm e}^{-2vt}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(v/2)\,{\rm d}v=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{{\rm e}^{2ut}-{\rm e}^{-2\alpha t}}{2t}&\text{if }u\in(-\alpha,-\beta),\\
\displaystyle\frac{{\rm e}^{2ut}-{\rm e}^{-2\alpha t}+{\rm e}^{-2\beta t}-{\rm e}^{2\beta t}}{2t}&\text{if }u\in(\beta,\alpha).\end{cases}

Thus we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v>0}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v=-\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}\Psi(2{\rm i}t)+\frac{1}{\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\sinh(2\beta t)}{2t}\int_{\beta}^{\alpha}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u.

Also since

\displaystyle\int_{u<-v}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)\,{\rm d}u=\begin{cases}\alpha-v&\text{if }v\in(\beta,\alpha),\\
\alpha-v-2\beta&\text{if }v\in(-\alpha,-\beta),\end{cases}

we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v<0}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{-\alpha}^{-\beta}(\alpha-v){\rm e}^{-2vt}\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}v-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\beta}^{\alpha}(\alpha-v-2\beta){\rm e}^{-2vt}\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)-\frac{\alpha}{{\rm i}}\Psi(2{\rm i}t)+\frac{\beta}{\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\beta}^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-2vt}\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}v.

Combining above equations, we obtain

\displaystyle L_{2}(t)=-\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)-\left(\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}+\frac{\alpha}{{\rm i}}\right)\Psi(2{\rm i}t)+\frac{1}{\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\sinh(2\beta t)}{2t}\int_{\beta}^{\alpha}\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u+\frac{\beta}{\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\beta}^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-2vt}\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}v.

Thus by Lemma
5.4
, one can see that Ward’s equation does not hold.

For a general
E
it follows from similar computations that

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v>0}v{\rm e}^{\frac{v^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v+\frac{{\rm i}}{2t}\Psi(2{\rm i}t)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{v>-u}{\rm e}^{-2(u+v)t}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(v/2)\,{\rm d}v+\frac{1}{2t}\right){\rm e}^{2ut}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)\widehat{J}(u)\,{\rm d}u

and

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{u+v<0}u{\rm e}^{\frac{u^{2}}{4}-2vt}\widehat{J}(u)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}u\,{\rm d}v+\Psi^{\prime}(2{\rm i}t)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{u<-v}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)\,{\rm d}u+v\right){\rm e}^{-2vt}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(v/2)\widehat{J}(v)\,{\rm d}v.

Therefore by Lemma
5.4
, Ward’s equation holds if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{v>-u}{\rm e}^{-2(u+v)t}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(v/2)\,{\rm d}v+\frac{1}{2t}=c_{1},\qquad u\textrm{ a.e.},

\displaystyle\int_{u<-v}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(u/2)\,{\rm d}u+v=c_{2},\qquad v\textrm{ a.e.}

for some constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
. This gives that
E
is connected (up to a null set). Now the proof is complete.
∎
