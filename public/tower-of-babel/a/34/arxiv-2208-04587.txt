On exact computation of Tukey depth central regions
Vít Fojtík 1, Petra Laketa 1, Pavlo Mozharovskyi 2, Stanislav Nagy 1

Abstract
. The Tukey (or halfspace) depth extends nonparametric methods toward multivariate data. The multivariate analogues of the quantiles are the central regions of the Tukey depth, defined as sets of points in the d d -dimensional space whose Tukey depth exceeds given thresholds k k . We address the problem of fast and exact computation of those central regions. First, we analyse an efficient Algorithm (A) from Liu et al., 2019 , and prove that it yields exact results in dimension d = 2 d=2 , or for a low threshold k k in arbitrary dimension. We provide examples where Algorithm (A) fails to recover the exact Tukey depth region for d > 2 d>2 , and propose a modification that is guaranteed to be exact. We express the problem of computing the exact central region in its dual formulation, and use that viewpoint to demonstrate that further substantial improvements to our algorithm are unlikely. An efficient C++ implementation of our exact algorithm is freely available in the R package TukeyRegion .

Denote by
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
all the facets of
P
. The

of
F_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,m
, is

(1)

\hat{F}_{j}=\left\{x\in P^{\circ}\colon\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=1\mbox{ for all }y\in F_{j}\right\}.

By

is a point in

, and

A pair of vertices

and

of

is joined by an edge on the boundary of

if and only if the facets
F_{j}
and

share a

of

[

determined by facets of
C
are mutually reachable in
\mathcal{H}(1)
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is given in Section
A.2
in the Appendix. We now turn our attention to the more interesting case
k=2
. In a series of auxiliary lemmas stated and proved in Section
A.3
in the Appendix, we obtain a proof of exactness of Algorithm
(A)
for
k=2
.

Theorem 3
.

Algorithm
(A)
finds all relevant halfspaces for
k=1,2
and any dimension
d=1,2,\dots
. Consequently, Algorithm
(A)
gives an exact solution for
k=1,2
.

Again as a consequence of our proof of Theorem
3
we are able to reduce the initial set of ridges
\mathcal{Q}
in Algorithm
(A)
for
k=2
to
d
, still keeping the algorithm exact. Indeed, take a single facet of
C
and consider any collection of
d
halfspaces from
\mathcal{H}(2)
, each of these halfspaces cutting off one of the
d
vertices of
C
. The set of all ridges in the boundaries of these halfspaces is placed in the initial queue
\mathcal{Q}
. Then the proof of Theorem
3
guarantees that for
k=2
the procedure
RidgeSearch
gives the exact central region
hD_{2}(X)
.

2.3.
Algorithm
(A)
is not exact in general

The output of Algorithm
(A)
is a collection of relevant halfspaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset\mathcal{H}(k)
at level
k
. Their intersection is therefore always a superset of the corresponding central region
hD_{k}(X)
. In the following example we demonstrate that for
k>2
and
d>2
, Algorithm
(A)
does not always recover the central region exactly.

Example
.

Consider a dataset
X
of
n=12
points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in general position. Each of these points is labeled by a colour: red, blue or green. We start with red points positioned in the vertices of a regular tetrahedron

r_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(e_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1,1,1\right)\right)\mbox{ for }i=1,2,3,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad r_{4}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8}}\left(1,1,1\right),

for
e_{1}=(1,0,0)
,
e_{2}=(0,1,0)
and
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
. The blue and the green points are respectively placed at
b_{i}=-0.3\,r_{i}
and
g_{i}=0.15\,r_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,4
. To satisfy the condition of the points being in general position, we rotate a bit the vertices of the blue and the green tetrahedrons, each in a slightly different way. The data is constructed so that the convex hull of
X
is formed only by the four red points, and so that the green vertices lie outside the blue tetrahedron. An example of such a configuration of points is in Figure
2
and in the supplementary
Mathematica
notebook, where our whole construction is visualised in an interactive display.

. The green vertices

Consider the depth level

. Algorithm
(A)
begins in step

with a ridge

, i.e. an edge of the convex hull of

, which must consist of two red points. The algorithm then proceeds in step

by finding two relevant planes

Note that any plane determined by three blue points is relevant for

— it cuts off exactly two points from

. The last claim is seen by considering a halfspace passing through a green point whose boundary plane is parallel to the adjacent blue facet. That halfspace contains only a single red point in its interior. As we intended to show, Algorithm
(A)
fails to recover the exact central region
hD_{3}(X)
.

3.
Algorithm
(B)
: Exact computation of central regions

We now turn to the problem of exact computation of the central regions by means of the ridge-wise search strategy
RidgeSearch
. Our intention is to find an initial set of ridges

guaranteeing the exactness of the procedure. As demonstrated in our example from Section

directly, it is unlikely that an initial set of much less than all

(C)

We therefore approach the problem in a different way, and argue that

is feasible to be run recursively. We show that given the set of all relevant halfspaces

using

\mathcal{Q}
in

are placed into
\mathcal{Q}
.

The complete Algorithm
(B)

\mathcal{Q}
in step

We have implemented the exact Algorithm

this yields the same complexity as

, namely
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\log(n)\right)
.

For several combinations of

and

we have drawn
100

from the

. Since such samples

are in general position almost surely, the maximum Tukey depth of a full-dimensional central region is bounded from above by

. For each

,

(A)
–

. We kept a record of

at level

;

(ii)

the number of ridges visited by the algorithm; and

(iii)

the total execution time for computing the first

central regions of the data.

The full study was run for the following combinations of
(n,d)
:
(50,3)
,
(50,4)
,
(50,5)
,
(100,3)
,

, and

for

for

. In the complete numerical study, we generated

5.
Dual graph: (Non-)Exactness of Algorithm
(A)
and negative results

We saw that Algorithm
(A)
does not always find the central region. On the other hand, the exact Algorithm
(B)
proposed in Section
3
is typically slower than Algorithm
(A)
if a single central region
hD_{k}(X)
is to be evaluated. In the present section we first explore the negative example of Section
2.3
in view of polarity considerations. We present a different vantage point on the search for relevant halfspaces based on the duality theory. It is shown that in the so-called dual graph of the dataset
X
, the search for an exact algorithm manifests itself as a natural problem in the theory of graphs. This analysis serves us to show that multiple promising simplifications of our Algorithm
(B)
along the lines of Algorithm
(A)
fail to recover the exact Tukey depth central region.

5.1.
Dual graph: Definition

We have seen in Section
1.2
that any polytope
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose interior contains the origin can be represented in its dual form as a polytope
P^{\circ}
. The facets
F_{j}
of
P
correspond to the vertices
\hat{F}_{j}
of
P^{\circ}
, and facets
F_{j}
and
F_{k}
of
P
are mutually neighbouring if and only if the vertices
\hat{F}_{j}
and
\hat{F}_{k}
share an edge on
P^{\circ}
. Instead of working with polytopes, we now generalise the polarity paradigm directly toward a dataset
X
. To visualise our problem of finding all relevant halfspaces at a given level
k=1,2,\dots
of
X
, we introduce the dual graph of
X
, and show how the search strategy employed in
RidgeSearch
translates into a problem of graph connectivity in the dual space.

Any
d
distinct points
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
from
X
uniquely determine a hyperplane
\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket
. We suppose that
X
is such that none of these
\binom{n}{d}
hyperplanes passes through the origin; in the other case we shift the dataset
X
slightly. The
polar
to
\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket
is defined as

(3)

\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket^{\circ}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=1\mbox{ for all }y\in\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket\right\}.

This definition is analogous to that of a conjugate face from (
1
). Indeed, for a face
F=\convOp\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\right)
we have
\hat{F}=\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket^{\circ}
. In particular, each polar to an observational hyperplane is a single point in the dual space. The polar from (
3
) is easy to express analytically. Writing
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
for a unit normal vector of
\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket
, we have

(4)

\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket^{\circ}=\left\{\frac{u}{\left\langle a_{1},u\right\rangle}\right\}.

Note that by our assumption that
\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket
does not contain the origin, the single point set above is well defined as
\left\langle a_{1},u\right\rangle
is the distance of the hyperplane
\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket
from the origin. Analogously as in Section
1.2
, for two different observational hyperplanes
\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket
and
\llbracket b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\rrbracket
we join the pair of vertices
\llbracket a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\rrbracket^{\circ}
and
\llbracket b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\rrbracket^{\circ}
in the dual space by an edge if and only if the two hyperplanes are
(mutually) neighbouring
, meaning that their defining sets of data points share a common subset of exactly
d-1
elements (a ridge)

\#\left(\left\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\right\}\cap\left\{b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\right\}\right)=d-1.

This definition is an extension of the notion of neighbouring facets of a polytope from Section
2.2

that pass through
d-1
points from
\{x_{j},x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{d-1}}\}
(step
(A3)
).

This includes for each
l\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
a halfspace in
\mathcal{H}(2)
that cuts off
x_{i_{l}}
.

The points
x_{j},x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{d-1}}
are all vertices of a facet of
C
. If
H\in\mathcal{H}(2)
is a relevant halfspace cutting off
x_{h}\in X
, by Lemma
5
we know that
x_{h}
is vertex-reachable from one of the points

. By Lemma

,

is reachable from the initial relevant halfspace that cuts off

finds all relevant halfspaces reachable from the initial set in step

A.4.
Proof of Theorem
4

To prove that
(B1)
does indeed guarantee exactness, we introduce additional notation. For a subset
A\subset X
of
k-1
points we write
\mathcal{H}(A)
for the collection of all the halfspaces
H\in\mathcal{H}(k)
that cut off
A
, i.e. that satisfy
X\setminus H=A
. Technically,
\mathcal{H}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}(k)
depends also on
k
and this should be emphasized in our notation; we shall not do this because
k
is, in fact, implicitly present, as
\#A=k-1
. We start with a simple but useful lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
S=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d+1}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be in general position. Denote by
p
the projection of
a_{d+1}
into the hyperplane
H=\llbracket S\setminus\{a_{d+1}\}\rrbracket
. Then there exists
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
such that
p
and
a_{j}
lie on the same side of the
(d-2)
-dimensional hyperplane inside
H
determined by the
d-1
points
S\setminus\{a_{j},a_{d+1}\}
.

Proof.

The set of
d
points
S\setminus\{a_{d+1}\}
lies in general position. Thus, it forms vertices of a
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex inside the hyperplane
H
. Consider now only the
(d-1)
-dimensional (affine) space
H
. Every vertex of the simplex lies inside a halfspace in
H
bounded by the
(d-2)
-dimensional affine subspace (that is, a hyperplane in
H
) containing the remaining vertices. These
d
halfspaces cover
H
, and in particular, one of them contains
p
. We found a
(d-2)
-dimensional hyperplane in
H
determined by
d-1
points from
S\setminus\{a_{d+1}\}
that bounds a halfspace containing
p
and a single point from
S\setminus\{a_{d+1}\}
, as desired.
∎

For the next lemma we need to consider also the metric structure of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Writing
\left\|x\right\|
for the Euclidean norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the distance of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a hyperplane
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\dist(x;G)=\min_{y\in G}\left\|x-y\right\|
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
H\in\mathcal{H}(k+1)
be determined by points
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in X
, and denote by
A=X\setminus H
the set of
k
points cut off from
X
by
H
. Let
a\in A
be any point of minimum distance to the hyperplane
\partial H
, that is, any
a\in A
that satisfies
\dist(a;\partial H)=\min_{x\in A}\dist(x;\partial H)
. Then there exists an observational halfspace
H_{1}\neq H
that shares a ridge with
H
in its boundary and either

(i)

H_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(k)
, or

(ii)

H_{1}
cuts off from
X
the same
k
points as
H
does, that is,
H_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(A)\cap\mathcal{H}(k+1)
, and at the same time
\dist(a;\partial H_{1})<\dist(a;\partial H)
.

Proof.

We start by applying Lemma
6
with
a_{d+1}=a
. Let
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
be the index from Lemma
6
. Denote by
G
the unique halfspace that satisfies the following three conditions:

(i)

the boundary of
G
is orthogonal to the boundary of
H
in the sense that the unit normals of
G
and
H
are orthogonal;

(ii)

G
contains the
d-1
points
M=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{j-1},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{d}\}
in its boundary; and

(iii)

a_{j}
lies in the interior of
G
.

Then by Lemma
6
we have that also
a\in G
, because the projection
p
of
a
into
\partial H
and
a_{j}
lie on the same side of the projection of
\partial G
into
\partial H
. Denote by
W
a two-dimensional plane orthogonal to the
(d-2)
-dimensional affine hull of
M
. Write
o\in W
for the projection of
M
onto
W
and for any other point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote by
x^{\prime}\in W
its projection into
W
. Since
M\subset\partial H
is orthogonal to
W
, the projection of
H
into
W
is a halfplane
H^{\prime}
that does not contain
a^{\prime}
and its boundary is the line
\llbracket o,a^{\prime}_{j}\rrbracket
. Likewise, because
M\subset\partial G
, also the projection of
G
is a halfplane
G^{\prime}
in
W
that contains
o
on its boundary and has inner normal
a^{\prime}_{j}-o
. Because
a\in G
, we obtain that
a^{\prime}\in G^{\prime}
. For any data point
b\in X
denote by
\theta(b)
the value of the smaller angle between the lines
\llbracket o,b^{\prime}\rrbracket
and
\llbracket o,a^{\prime}_{j}\rrbracket
inside the plane
W
. This whole setup is visualised in Figure
7
.

. The regions
D_{1}
and

are projected to the quadrants given by
D_{1}^{\prime}
and

. We search for a point
b\in D\cap X
that minimizes the value of the angle
\theta(b)
between the line
\partial H^{\prime}
and the line joining

.

Denote

D_{1}=G\setminus H
,
and
D_{2}=H\setminus G
,
and set
D=D_{1}\cup D_{2}
. Let
c\in D\cap X
be the point that satisfies

\theta(c)=\min\left\{\theta(b)\colon b\in D\cap X\right\}.

Such a point is necessarily unique, as
M
, which projects to

, already contains
d-1
points from

, and if there were two different points
c_{1},c_{2}
from

with the same angle

, there would necessarily exist a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
passing through

and both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is impossible due to the assumption of
X
being in general position.

Denote by
H_{1}
the halfspace in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with boundary determined by
M\cup\{c\}
that does not contain
a
in its interior. Note that
H_{1}
is actually a halfspace obtained by rotating
H
around
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the direction that “keeps”

in
H_{1}
, until its boundary
\partial H_{1}
hits the first new point from

. Also,
H_{1}
obviously shares the ridge

with

.

We distinguish two cases.

(i)

c\in D_{1}
: In this case, we know that
c
is one of the

. Consequently,
H_{1}
cuts off exactly

, that is,

(ii)

cuts off the same

. What remains to be proved is that
\dist(a;\partial H)>\dist(a;\partial H_{1})
. To see this, note that because the plane

is parallel to normal vectors of both

\dist(a^{\prime};\llbracket o,a^{\prime}_{i}\rrbracket)>\dist(a^{\prime};\llbracket o,c^{\prime}\rrbracket)
, which follows directly from

and from the fact that

.

∎

Everything is now ready for the main proof of Theorem
4
.

Main proof of Theorem
4
.
Denote by
A=X\setminus H
the set of
k
points cut off from
X
by
H
. We apply Lemma
7
to the halfspace
H
and obtain a halfspace
H_{1}
. If
H_{1}
cuts off
k-1
points from
X
, then we set
H^{\prime}=H
and
\widetilde{H}=H_{1}
. Otherwise, we know that
H_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(k+1)\cap\mathcal{H}(A)
and for
a\in A
from the statement of Lemma
7
we have
\min_{x\in A}\dist(x;\partial H)=\dist(a;\partial H)>\dist(a;\partial H_{1})\geq\min_{x\in A}\dist(x;\partial H_{1})
. We apply again Lemma
7
to the halfspace
H_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(k+1)
and obtain another halfspace
H_{2}
. Note that
H_{2}
is reachable from
H
by our construction. Again, if
H_{2}
cuts off
k-1
points, then we set
H^{\prime}=H_{1}
and
\widetilde{H}=H_{2}
; otherwise, we continue and apply Lemma
7
iteratively. We obtain a sequence
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},\dots
. Because in each step we obtain a halfspace with a strictly smaller distance from the closest point of the set
A
, it is not possible for any two halfspaces from the sequence to coincide. Because there are only finitely many observational halfspaces, there must exist an integer
m\geq 2
such that
H_{m}\in\mathcal{H}(k)
. Taking
m
to be the lowest such integer, we set
H^{\prime}=H_{m-1}
and
\widetilde{H}=H_{m}
. Note that
H_{m-1}
is a relevant halfspace that cuts off
k
points from
X
and also is reachable from
H
. The proof is concluded.
