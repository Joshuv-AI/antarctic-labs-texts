Introduction and main results

In recent years, there has been a renewed and increasing
interest in the study of boundary value problems involving linear and nonlinear integro-differential operators.
This growing interest is fueled both by important applications and seminal advances in the
understanding of nonlocal phenomena from a PDE point of view, see e.g.

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
16
,
17
,
19
]
and the references therein.
Among nonlocal operators of positive differential order, fractional powers of the Laplacian are receiving the most attention.
Recall that, for
s\in(0,1)
, the fractional Laplacian of a function
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is defined by

\mathcal{F}((-\Delta)^{s}u)(\xi)=|\xi|^{2s}\widehat{u}(\xi)\qquad\text{for all $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$},

(1.1)

where both
\mathcal{F}
and
\widehat{\cdot}
denote the Fourier transform. Equivalently, we can write
(-\Delta)^{s}
as a singular integral operator, i.e.

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=c_{N,s}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(x)}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dy=c_{N,s}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}\frac{u(x)-u(x+z)}{|z|^{N+2s}}dz,

(1.2)

where
c_{N,s}=2^{2s}\pi^{-\frac{N}{2}}s\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N+2s}{2})}{\Gamma(1-s)}
and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function, see e.g.
[
17
]
.

It is well known that the fractional Laplacian has the following limiting
properties when
s
approaches the values zero and
1
:

\lim_{s\to 1^{-}}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=-\Delta u(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=u(x)\qquad\text{for $u\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$,}

(1.3)

see e.g.
[
8
]
. On the other hand, one might guess that the function
z\mapsto|z|^{-N}
also appears in
a suitably renormalized limit of the integral kernels
z\mapsto\frac{c_{N,s}}{|z|^{N+2s}}
appearing in (
1.2
) as
s\to 0
. However, due to lack of integrability, the zero order kernel
|\cdot|^{-N}
has to be cut off at infinity in order to give rise to a singular integral operator defined analogously as in (
1.2
). Integral operators given by kernels with a singularity of the order
-N
have received growing interest recently, as they give rise to interesting limiting regularity properties and Harnack inequalities without scaling invariance, see e.g.
[
14
]
.

As we shall see in the present paper, operators of such limiting order appear also within a first order expansion of the second limit in (
1.3
). More precisely, we find that for
u\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=u(x)+sL_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u(x)+o(s)\quad\text{as\ \, $s\to 0^{+}$,}

where, formally, the operator
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!:=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}(-\Delta)^{s}
is given as a
logarithmic Laplacian
. Indeed, this operator has a logarithmic symbol, since, at least formally, (
1.1
) gives rise to the representation

\mathcal{F}(L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u)(\xi)=(2\log|\xi|)\,\widehat{u}(\xi),\qquad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

The purpose of the present paper is to study qualitative and functional analytic properties of the extremal nonlocal operator
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
and related Dirichlet problems. An important motivation for this study is the fact that, as we shall observe, the operator
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
appears in the asymptotic description of the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
in bounded domains for small
s
and of corresponding eigenfunctions.

In the following, we present the main results of the present paper. Our first result provides an integral representation of the logarithmic Laplacian.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u\in C^{\beta}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
\beta>0
. Then we
have

\displaystyle L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}[(-\Delta)^{s}u](x)=c_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)1_{B_{1}(x)}(y)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy+\rho_{N}u(x)

(1.4)

\displaystyle=c_{N}\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy-c_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy+\rho_{N}u(x)\quad\text{for \,$x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$,}

where

c_{N}:=\pi^{-N/2}\Gamma(N/2)=\frac{2}{|S^{N-1}|},\qquad\rho_{N}:=2\log 2+\psi(\frac{N}{2})-\gamma

(1.5)

and
\gamma=-\Gamma^{\prime}(1)
is the Euler Mascheroni constant. Here
\psi=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}
is the Digamma function. Moreover,

(i)

for
1<p\leq\infty
, we have
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}u-u}{s}\to L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as
s\to 0^{+}
;

(ii)

\mathcal{F}(L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u)(\xi)=(2\log|\xi|)\,\widehat{u}(\xi)
for a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Remark 1.2
.

We note that

\rho_{N}=-2\gamma+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{(N-1)/2}\frac{2}{2k-1}
for
N
odd and

\rho_{N}=2\bigl(\log 2-\gamma\bigr)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{(N-2)/2}\frac{1}{k}
for
N
even. We also note that, in distributional sense, the integral representiation (
1.4
) coincides, up to a constant, with the convolution with the inverse Fourier transform of the symbol
2\log|\cdot|
, see e.g.
[
11
, Chapter II.3]
.

The formula (
1.4
) allows to define
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u
for a fairly large class of functions
u
. For this we recall that, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the space
L^{1}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
denotes the space of locally integrable functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\|u\|_{L^{1}_{s}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|}{(1+|x|)^{N+2s}}\,dx<+\infty.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. If
u
is Dini continuous at some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then
[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u](x)
is well-defined by the formula (
1.4
). Moreover, if
u
is uniformly Dini continuous in some open subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u
is continuous in
\Omega
.

For the definition of (uniform) Dini continuity, see Section
2
below. Our next aim is to study the eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u=\lambda u\quad\ {\rm in}\quad\Omega,\\[5.69054pt]
\phantom{L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!}u=0\quad\ {\rm{in}}\ \quad\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

in a bounded domain
\Omega
. We consider corresponding eigenfunctions in weak sense. The corresponding functional analytic framework is given as follows.
Let
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
denote the space of all measurable functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with
u\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
and

\int\!\!\!\!\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}}{{\text{\tiny$|x-y|\!\leq\!1$}}}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy<+\infty.

We shall see that
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space with inner product

\mathcal{E}(u,w)=\frac{c_{N}}{2}\int\!\!\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}}{{\text{\tiny$|x-y|\!\leq\!1$}}}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(w(x)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy

and the induced norm
\|u\|_{{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}=\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(u,u)}
, where
c_{N}
is given in (
1.5
). By
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
, the embedding
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact. Here and in the following, we identify
L^{2}(\Omega)
with the space of functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
u\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
. Moreover, the quadratic form associated with
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
is well-defined on
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
by

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}:{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\times{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,w)=\mathcal{E}(u,w)-c_{N}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}}{{\text{\tiny$|x-y|\!\geq\!1$}}}}\frac{u(x)w(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+\rho_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}uw\,dx.

A function
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
will then be called an eigenfunction of (
1.6
) corresponding to the eigenvalue

\lambda
if

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,\phi)=\lambda\int_{\Omega}u\phi\,dx\qquad\text{for all $\phi\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)$.}

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then problem (
1.6
) admits a sequence of eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)<\lambda_{2}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{k+1}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\cdots

and corresponding eigenfunctions
\xi_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds:

(i)

\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)=\min\{{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,u)\>:\>u\in{\mathbb{H}}_{k}(\Omega)\>:\>\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\}
, where

{\mathbb{H}}_{1}(\Omega):={\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{H}}_{k}(\Omega):=\{u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\>:\>\text{$\int_{\Omega}u\xi_{i}\,dx=0$ for $i=1,\dots k-1$}\}\quad\text{for $k>1$.}

(ii)

\{\xi_{k}\>:\>k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

(iii)

\xi_{1}
is strictly positive in
\Omega
. Moreover,
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
is simple, i.e., if
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
satisfies (
1.6
) in weak sense with
\lambda=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
, then
u=t\xi_{1}
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iv)

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)=+\infty
.

Our next theorem highlights the role of
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
and the corresponding eigenfunction
\xi_{1}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let
\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)
denote the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
on
\Omega
for
s\in(0,1)
. Then we have

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega).

Moreover, if
u_{s}
is the unique nonnegative
L^{2}
-normalized Dirichlet eigenfunction
of
(-\Delta)^{s}
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)
, then we have

u_{s}\to\xi_{1}\qquad\text{in $L^{2}(\Omega)$,}

where
\xi_{1}
is the unique nonnegative
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunction of
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
.

Since
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
contains competing nonlocal terms of different signs, it seems unclear how the quadratic form
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
changes under Schwarz symmetrization or other types of rearrangements. Nevertheless, as we shall see in Section
3
, Theorem

allows to deduce the Faber-Krahn-inequality for the logarithmic Laplacian from the corresponding one for the fractional Laplacian due to Bañuelos et al., see
[

Corollary 1.6
.

(Faber-Krahn-inequality for the logarithmic Laplacian)

Let
\rho>0
. Among all bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega
with
|\Omega|=\rho

B=B_{r}(0)
with
|B|=\rho

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
.

Next, we wish to discuss the maximum principle for the operator
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u
on bounded domains
\Omega

For this we let

\int\!\!\!\!\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\Omega}}{{\text{\tiny$|x-y|\!\leq\!1$}}}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\,dx<+\infty.

We shall see in Section

Definition 1.7

For a function

Let

We begin this section with the derivation of the integral representation (

Proof of Theorem

Let

. For
0<s<\min\{\frac{\beta}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}
, the principal value in the definition of
[(-\Delta)^{s}u](x)
reduces to a standard Lebesgue integral. Let

R>4
be chosen such that
\supp u\subset B_{\frac{R}{4}}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we then have

[(-\Delta)^{s}u](x)=c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+z)}{|z|^{N+2s}}\,dz=A_{R}(s,x)+D_{R}(s)\,u(x)

with

A_{R}(s,x):=c_{N,s}\Bigl(\int_{B_{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+z)}{|z|^{N+2s}}\,dz-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{R}}\frac{u(x+z)}{|z|^{N+2s}}\,dz\Bigr)

and

D_{R}(s):=c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{R}}|z|^{-N-2s}dz=\frac{c_{N,s}|S^{N-1}|R^{-2s}}{2s}.

Here we recall that
B_{R}:=B_{R}(0)
. If
|x|\geq\frac{R}{2}
, we have that
u(x)=0
and
|z|\geq\frac{|x|}{2}\geq 1
whenever
x+z\in\supp u
, and therefore

|A_{R}(s,x)|=c_{N,s}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x+z)}{|z|^{N+2s}}\,dz\Bigr|\leq c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x+z)|}{|z|^{N}}\,dz\leq 2^{N}c_{N,s}\|u\|_{L^{1}}|x|^{-N}.

Consequently,

\|A_{R}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\frac{R}{2}})}\leq 2^{2N-\frac{N}{p}}c_{N,s}\|u\|_{L^{1}}\Bigl(\frac{|S^{N-1}|}{(p-1)N}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{p}}{R}^{\frac{N}{p}-N}

(2.1)

for
1<p<\infty
and

\|A_{R}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\frac{R}{2}})}\leq 4^{N}c_{N,s}\|u\|_{L^{1}}{R}^{-N}.

(2.2)

We now write

c_{N,s}=sd_{N}(s)\qquad\text{with}\qquad d_{N}(s):=\frac{c_{N,s}}{s}=\pi^{-\frac{N}{2}}2^{2s}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N}{2}+s)}{\Gamma(1-s)},

(2.3)

and we note that

d_{N}(0)=\pi^{-\frac{N}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{N}{2})=c_{N}\quad\text{and}\quad d_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\pi^{-\frac{N}{2}}\Bigl((2\log 2-\gamma)\Gamma(\frac{N}{2})+\Gamma^{\prime}(\frac{N}{2})\Bigr)=c_{N}\rho_{N}.

From (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we then deduce that

\|A_{R}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\frac{R}{2}})}\leq sm_{p}R^{\frac{N}{p}-N}

(2.4)

for
1<p\leq\infty
with a constant
m_{p}>0
depending on
u
but not on
R
and
s
.

On the other hand, for
x\in B_{\frac{R}{2}}
, we have
|z|\leq B_{R}
whenever
x+z\in\supp u
and therefore the second integral in the definition of
A_{R}(s,x)
vanishes. Since
u\in C^{\beta}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, it is thus easy to see that

\displaystyle\frac{A_{R}(s,x)}{s}

\displaystyle=d_{N}(s)\int_{B_{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+z)}{|z|^{N+2s}}\,dz

\displaystyle\to\tilde{A}_{R}(x):=c_{N}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+z)}{|z|^{N}}\,dz\qquad\text{as $\ s\to 0^{+}$,}

(2.5)

and this convergence is uniform in
x\in B_{\frac{R}{2}}
. Next we note that

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}D_{R}(s)=d_{N}(s)\frac{|S^{N-1}|}{2}R^{-2s}=\frac{c_{N}|S^{N-1}|}{2}=1

and

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{D_{R}(s)-1}{s}=\frac{|S^{N-1}|}{2}\bigl(d_{N}^{\prime}(0)-2d_{N}(0)\log R)=\rho_{N}-c_{N}|S^{N-1}|\log R=:\kappa_{R}.

Since
u\in C_{c}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we then conclude that

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\Bigl\|\frac{D_{R}(s)u-u}{s}-\kappa_{R}u\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=0

(2.6)

for
1<p\leq\infty
. By (
2.5
) and the definition of
\kappa_{R}
above, we also find that

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{R}(x)

\displaystyle+\kappa_{R}u(x)=c_{N}\Bigl(\int_{B_{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+z)}{|z|^{N}}\,dz-u(x)\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N}}\,dz\Bigr)+\rho_{N}u(x)

\displaystyle=c_{N}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{u(x)1_{B_{1}}(z)-u(x+z)}{|z|^{N}}dz+\rho_{N}u(x)

\displaystyle=[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u](x)+F(x)\qquad\text{with}\quad F(x):=c_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{R}}\frac{u(x+z)}{|z|^{N}}dz

(2.7)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. By the same estimates as for
A_{R}(s,x)
, we see that
F(x)=0
for
|x|\leq\frac{R}{2}
and

\|F\|_{L^{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\frac{R}{2}})}\leq M_{p}R^{\frac{N}{p}-N}

(2.8)

for
1<p\leq\infty
with a constant
M_{p}>0
depending on
u
but not on
R
and
s
.
Combining (
2.4
), (
2.5
), (
2.6
), (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we see that

\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}\Bigl\|\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}u-u}{s}-\,L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq(m_{p}+M_{p})R^{\frac{N}{p}-N}\qquad\text{for every $R>0$, $p\in(1,\infty]$}

and therefore

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\Bigl\|\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}u-u}{s}-\,L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=0\qquad\text{for every $p\in(1,\infty]$.}

In particular, this holds for
p=2
, and therefore, using the continuity of the Fourier transform as a map
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we find that

\widehat{L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u}=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\widehat{(-\Delta)^{s}u}-\hat{u}}{s}=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\Bigl(\frac{|\cdot|^{2s}-1}{s}\Bigr)\hat{u}=2\log|\cdot|\,\hat{u}\quad\text{in \,$L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$}.

From this we infer that

\widehat{L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u}(\xi)=2\log|\xi|\,\hat{u}(\xi)\qquad\text{for almost every $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$,}

as claimed.
∎

It is convenient to introduce the kernel functions

\mathbf{k}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\mathbf{k}(z)=c_{N}1_{B_{1}}(z)|z|^{-N}

(2.9)

and

{\mathbf{j}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad{\mathbf{j}}(z)=c_{N}1_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}}(z)|z|^{-N}.

(2.10)

Then the integral representation (
1.4
) can be rewritten as

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dy-[{\mathbf{j}}*u](x)+\rho_{N}u(x).

(2.11)

We now analyze for which functions
u
and points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the expression
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u(x)
is well-defined by this formula. We need to recall some definitions. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a measurable subset and
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. The module of continuity of
u
at a point
x\in\Omega

is defined by

\omega_{u,x,\Omega}:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty),\qquad\omega_{u,x,\Omega}(r)=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y\in\Omega}}{{|y-x|\leq r}}}|u(y)-u(x)|.

The function
u
is called
Dini continuous
at
x
if
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega_{u,x,\Omega}(r)}{r}\,dr<+\infty
.
If

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega_{u,\Omega}(r)}{r}\,dr<\infty\qquad\text{for the uniform continuity module $\omega_{u,\Omega}(r):=\sup\limits_{x\in\Omega}\omega_{u,x,\Omega}(r)$,}

then we call
u

uniformly Dini continuous
in
\Omega
. In the following, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we also let
L^{1}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
denote the space of locally integrable functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\|u\|_{L^{1}_{s}}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|u(x)|}{(1+|x|)^{N+2s}}\,ds<+\infty.

We need the following observation.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
v:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable with
|v(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{-N}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with some
C>0
.
Then the convolution
v*u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is well-defined and continuous.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Lebesgue’s theorem if

u\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Moreover, if
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is compact, we see that

\displaystyle|[v*u](x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|v(x-y)||u(y)|\,dy\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(y)|}{(1+|x-y|)^{N}}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(y)|}{(1+|y|)^{N}}\,dy=C_{K}\|u\|_{L^{1}_{0}}\qquad\text{for $x\in K$ with a constant $C_{K}>0$},

and therefore
\|v*u\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{K}\|u\|_{L^{1}_{0}}
. Hence, using the fact that
C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is dense in
L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, a standard approximation argument shows that
v*u
is continuous on
K
for arbitrary
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Since
K
is chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that
v*u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous.
∎

The following is an extension of Proposition
1.3
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

i)

If
u
is Dini continuous at some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then
[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u](x)
is well-defined by the formula (
1.4
). Moreover, if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an open subset and
x\in\Omega
, then we have the alternative representation

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u(x)=c_{N}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy-c_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}\,dy+[h_{\Omega}(x)+\rho_{N}]u(x),

(2.12)

where
h_{\Omega}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is given by (
1.7
).

ii)

If
u
is uniformly Dini continuous in some open subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u
is continuous in
\Omega
.

Proof.

i) Since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)1_{B_{1}(x)}(y)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{N}}dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{|u(y)|}{|x-y|^{N}}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq|S^{N-1}|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega_{u,x,\Omega}(r)}{r}\,dr+C_{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(y)|}{(1+|y|)^{N}}\,dy<+\infty

with a constant
C_{x}>0
by assumption, it follows that
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u(x)
is well-defined by (
1.4
). Next, we let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open subset such that
x\in\Omega
. Starting from (
1.4
), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u(x)-\rho_{N}u(x)}{c_{N}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)1_{B_{1}(x)}(y)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)1_{B_{1}(x)}(y)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\frac{u(x)1_{B_{1}(x)}(y)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy-u(x)\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy+u(x)\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}\,dy+\frac{h_{\Omega}(x)u(x)}{c_{N}}.

This yields (
2.12
).

ii) We start with a preliminary remark. Let
{\varepsilon}_{k}:=2^{-2^{k}}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\omega_{u,\Omega}({\varepsilon}_{k})\log\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}_{k}}=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\omega_{u,\Omega}({\varepsilon}_{k})\log\frac{{\varepsilon}_{k-1}}{{\varepsilon}_{k}}\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}^{{\varepsilon}_{k-1}}\frac{\omega_{u,\Omega}(r)}{r}\,dr=2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\omega_{u,\Omega}(r)}{r}\,dr<\infty

by assumption. Hence

\omega_{u,\Omega}({\varepsilon}_{k})\log\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}_{k}}\to 0\qquad\text{as $k\to\infty.$}

(2.13)

Next, we write
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u=c_{N}f_{1}+f_{2}
with

f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad f_{1}(x)=\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy,\qquad f_{2}(x)=-[{\mathbf{j}}*u](x)+\rho_{N}u(x).

By Lemma
2.1
, we see that
f_{2}
is continuous on
\Omega
. To see the continuity of
f_{1}
in
\Omega
, we let
x\in\Omega
,

r:=\min\{1,\frac{\dist(x,\partial\Omega)}{4}\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
be chosen such that
{\varepsilon}_{k}<r
. For
y\in\Omega
with
|x-y|<{\varepsilon}_{k}
, we then have

\displaystyle|f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(y)|=\Bigl|\int_{B_{1}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+z)-[u(y)-u(y+z)]}{|z|^{N}}dz\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x+z)|+|u(y)-u(y+z)|}{|z|^{N}}dz+\Bigl|\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)+u(x+z)-u(y+z)}{|z|^{N}}dz\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}\frac{\omega_{u,\Omega}(|z|)}{|z|^{N}}dz+\omega_{u,\Omega}(|x-y|)\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N}}dz+\Bigl|\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}\frac{u(x+z)-u(y+z)}{|z|^{N}}dz\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq|S^{N-1}|\delta_{k}+\Bigl|\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}\frac{u(x+z)-u(y+z)}{|z|^{N}}dz\Bigr|

with
\delta_{k}:=2\int_{0}^{{\varepsilon}_{k}}\frac{\omega_{u,\Omega}(\tau)}{\tau}\,d\tau+\omega_{u,\Omega}({\varepsilon}_{k})\log\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}_{k}}
.
We note that
\delta_{k}\to 0
by assumption and (
2.13
). Moreover, defining

v_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad v_{k}(z)=1_{B_{1}\setminus B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}(z)|z|^{-N},

we see by Lemma
2.1
that

\Bigl|\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{{\varepsilon}_{k}}}\frac{u(x+z)-u(y+z)}{|z|^{N}}dz\Bigr|=\bigl|[v_{k}*u](x)-[v_{k}*u](y)\bigr|\to 0\qquad\text{as $y\to x$}

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We thus conclude that

\limsup_{y\to x}\bigl|f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(y)\bigr|\leq|S^{N-1}|\delta_{k}\qquad\text{for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$,}

and this implies that
\lim\limits_{y\to x}|f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(y)|=0
. Hence
f_{1}
is continuous in
x
, and so is
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u
.
∎

3
Functional analytic framework for the Dirichlet problem

In this section, we set up the functional analytic framework for Dirichlet problems related to
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
in weak sense.
Throughout this section, let
\Omega
be a bounded domain. Using (
2.11
), we observe that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u]vdx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl({\mathbf{j}}*u-\rho_{N}u\bigr)v\,dx

(3.1)

for uniformly Dini continuous functions
u,v\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with the kernel functions
\mathbf{k},{\mathbf{j}}
defined in (
2.9
) and (
2.10
). We let
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
denote the space of all measurable functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with
u\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy<+\infty.

Then
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
contains the space of all uniformly Dini continuous functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}

with
u\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
.
On
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, we define the inner product

\mathcal{E}(u,w)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(x)-u(y))(w(x)-w(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy

and the induced norm
\|u\|_{{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}=\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(u,u)}
.
By
[
10
, Lemma 2.7]
, we have that

\inf_{u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}\frac{\mathcal{E}(u,u)}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}>0,

(3.2)

and from this it can be deduced that
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space. Moreover,

the embedding
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact

(3.3)

by
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
. We note that, for
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, we have

{\mathcal{E}}(u,u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy+\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\Omega}(x)u^{2}(x)\,dx

(3.4)

with

\kappa_{\Omega}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dy=c_{N}\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy.

(3.5)

The function
\kappa_{\Omega}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is usually called the
Killing measure
associated with the kernel
\mathbf{k}
.
For
x\in\Omega
with
r:=\rho(x)=\dist(x,\partial\Omega)<1
, we have

\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\leq c_{N}\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus B_{r}(x)}|x-y|^{-N}\,dy=c_{N}|S^{N-1}|\log\frac{1}{r}=2\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}.

(3.6)

Moreover, if
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz, then a standard computation also gives the lower bound

\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\geq C_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\qquad\text{for $x\in\Omega$ with a constant $C_{\Omega}>0$.}

(3.7)

A more precise estimate is available if
\Omega
has a uniform
C^{2}
-boundary, see Remark
5.5
below.
The following density result will be useful in the sequel.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, if
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
is nonnegative, we have:

(i)

There exists a sequence of functions
u_{n}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
with compact support in
\Omega
,
0\leq u_{n}\leq u_{n+1}\leq u
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

u_{n}\to u
in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
as
n\to+\infty
.

(ii)

There exists a sequence of nonnegative functions
u_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
u_{n}\to u
in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
as
n\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Since for
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
we have
u^{\pm}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, it suffices to consider a nonnegative function
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
and to prove (i) and (ii).

(i) For
r>0
, we define the Lipschitz function

\phi_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\phi_{r}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&&\quad\quad\rho(x)\geq 2r,\\
&2-\frac{\rho(x)}{r},&&\quad\quad r\leq\rho(x)\leq 2r,\\
&1,&&\quad\quad\rho(x)\leq r.\end{aligned}\right.

Clearly, we then have that
\phi_{s}\leq\phi_{r}
whenever
0<s\leq r
. We shall show that

u\phi_{r}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\;\text{for $r>0$ sufficiently small}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|u\phi_{r}\|_{{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{as $r\to 0$.}

(3.8)

As a consequence, we then have
u(1-\phi_{r})\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
for
r>0
sufficiently small and
u(1-\phi_{r})\to u
in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
as
r\to 0
. We also note that
0\leq u(1-\phi_{r})\leq u(1-\phi_{s})\leq u
for
0<s\leq r
, and that
u(1-\phi_{r})
has compact support in
\Omega
. Hence the claim follows by choosing
u_{n}:=u(1-\phi_{r_{n}})
for a decreasing sequence of numbers
r_{n}>0
with
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=0
.

To prove (
3.8
), we consider constants
C>0
which may change from line to line. Let
A_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega\>:\rho(x)\leq t\}
for
t>0
. Since
u\phi_{r}
vanishes in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus A_{2r}
,
0\leq\phi\leq 1
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and

|{\phi_{r}}(x)-{\phi_{r}}(y)|\leq\min\{\frac{|x-y|}{r},1\}\qquad\text{for $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$,}

we have that for
r\in(0,\frac{1}{4})

\displaystyle\|{\phi_{r}}u\|^{2}_{{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}={\mathcal{E}}(\phi_{r}u,\phi_{r}u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[u(x){\phi_{r}}(x)-u(y){\phi_{r}}(y)]^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{4r}}\int_{A_{4r}}[u(x){\phi_{r}}(x)-u(y){\phi_{r}}(y)]^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx+\int_{A_{2r}}u(x)^{2}{\phi_{r}}(x)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus A_{4r}}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{4r}}\int_{A_{4r}}\bigl[u(x)\bigl({\phi_{r}}(x)-{\phi_{r}}(y)\bigr)+{\phi_{r}}(y)\bigl(u(x)-u(y)\bigr)\bigr]^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{A_{2r}}u(x)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\dist(x,\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus A_{4r})}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A_{4r}}u^{2}(x)\int_{A_{4r}}({\phi_{r}}(x)-{\phi_{r}}(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx+\int_{A_{4r}}\int_{A_{4r}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{A_{2r}}u(x)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{r^{2}}\int_{A_{4r}}u^{2}(x)\int_{B_{r}(x)}|x-y|^{2-N}dydx+C\int_{A_{4r}}u^{2}(x)\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus B_{r}(x)}|x-y|^{-N}dydx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{4r}}\int_{A_{4r}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx+C\int_{A_{2r}}u(x)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{A_{4r}}\Bigl(1+\log\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)u^{2}(x)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{4r}}\int_{A_{4r}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{A_{2r}}u(x)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{A_{4r}}u^{2}(x)\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{4r}}\int_{A_{4r}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx.

Next we note that

\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\kappa_{\Omega}(x)dx<+\infty

by (
3.4
), and therefore

\int_{A_{4r}}u^{2}(x)\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}dx\to 0\qquad\text{as $r\to 0$}

by (
3.7
). Here we used the fact that
\Omega
has a Lipschitz boundary.
Moreover, since
{\mathcal{E}}(u,u)<+\infty
, we have

\int_{A_{4r}}\int_{A_{4r}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dydx\to 0\qquad\text{as $r\to 0$.}

We thus obtain (
3.8
), and this finishes the proof of (i).

(ii) By (i), we may assume that
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
is nonnegative function with compact support in
\Omega
.
Let
u_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the usual mollification of
u
given by

u_{\epsilon}:=\eta_{\epsilon}\ast u=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\eta_{\epsilon}(z)u(\cdot-z)\ dz,

where
\eta_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a nonnegative radial function supported in
B_{1}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\eta_{0}(x)\,dx=1
and

\eta_{\epsilon}(x)=\epsilon^{-N}\eta(\frac{x}{{\varepsilon}})
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
{\varepsilon}>0
. By
[
13
, Proposition 4.1]
, we then have that
u_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
for
{\varepsilon}>0
sufficiently small and

\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}^{2}=\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}{\mathcal{E}}(u-u_{\varepsilon},u-u_{\varepsilon})=0.

Hence it suffices to choose
u_{n}:=u_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}
for a sequence
{\varepsilon}_{n}\to 0^{+}
. The proof is thus finished.
∎

The Hilbert space
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
provides the appropriate framework to study the Poisson problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u=f\quad\ {\rm in}\quad\Omega,\\[5.69054pt]
\phantom{L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!}u=0\quad\ {\rm{in}}\ \quad\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega\end{array}\right.

(3.9)

with given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
in weak sense. For this it is convenient to introduce the quadratic form

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}:{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\times{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,w)=\mathcal{E}(u,w)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl({\mathbf{j}}*u-\rho_{N}u\bigr)w\,dx.

Here we note that, since
{\mathbf{j}}\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for
r>1
and
\Omega
is bounded,

the map
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to L^{2}(\Omega)
,
u\mapsto{\mathbf{j}}*u
is well-defined and continuous,

(3.10)

and thus
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
is well-defined. Theorem
3.1
in particular implies that, if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, then the space of uniformly Dini continuous functions
u\in C_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
. It thus follows from (
3.1
) that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u]vdx=\mathcal{E}_{L}(u,w)

(3.11)

for every uniformly Dini continuous function
u\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and every
v\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
. Now, by definition, a function
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of (
3.9
) if

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,w)=\int_{\Omega}fw\,dx\qquad\text{for all $w\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)$.}

Since
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
contains competing nonlocal terms of different sign, at first glance it appears difficult to compare the values of
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,u)
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(|u|,|u|)
for
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
. For this, an alternative representation of
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
will turn out to be useful.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,u)=\frac{c_{N}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{[u(x)-u(y)]^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+\int_{\Omega}[h_{\Omega}(x)+\rho_{N}]u^{2}(x)\,dx

(3.12)

with
h_{\Omega}
given in (
1.7
). Moreover, if
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz, we have

h_{\Omega}(x)\geq C_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}-\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\qquad\text{for $x\in\Omega$ with constants $C_{\Omega},\,\tilde{C}_{\Omega}>0$.}

(3.13)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,u)-\rho_{N}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\,dx={\mathcal{E}}(u,u)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[{\mathbf{j}}*u]u\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N}}{2}\int\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\Omega}}{{|x-y|<1}}}\frac{[u(x)-u(y)]^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+c_{N}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dydx

\displaystyle\quad-c_{N}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{u(x)u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dydx

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N}}{2}\int\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\Omega}}{{|x-y|<1}}}\frac{[u(x)-u(y)]^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+c_{N}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dydx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c_{N}}{2}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\Omega}}{{|x-y|\geq 1}}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dydx-c_{N}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\Omega}}{{|x-y|\geq 1}}}\frac{u^{2}(x)}{|x-y|^{N}}dydx

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{[u(x)-u(y)]^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+c_{N}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\Bigl[\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy-\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy\Bigr]dx

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{[u(x)-u(y)]^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+c_{N}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)h_{\Omega}(x)\,dx.

Moreover, (
3.13
) is a direct consequence of (
3.7
).
∎

The following is a rather immediate corollary of Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, we also have
|u|\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, and

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(|u|,|u|)\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,u).

(3.14)

Moreover, equality holds in (
3.14
) if and only if
u
does not change sign.

Our next aim is to study the eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u=\lambda u\quad\ {\rm in}\quad\Omega,\\[5.69054pt]
\phantom{L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!}u=0\quad\ {\rm{in}}\ \quad\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(3.15)

We consider corresponding eigenfunctions in weak sense, i.e., as weak solutions of (
3.9
) with
f=\lambda u
. We restate Theorem
1.4
from the introduction for the reader’s convenience.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then problem (
1.6
) admits a sequence of eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)<\lambda_{2}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{k+1}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\cdots

and corresponding eigenfunctions
\xi_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds:

(i)

\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)=\min\{{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,u)\>:\>u\in{\mathbb{H}}_{k}(\Omega)\>:\>\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\}
, where

{\mathbb{H}}_{1}(\Omega):={\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{H}}_{k}(\Omega):=\{u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\>:\>\text{$\int_{\Omega}u\xi_{i}\,dx=0$ for $i=1,\dots k-1$}\}\quad\text{for $k>1$.}

(ii)

\{\xi_{k}\>:\>k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

(iii)

\xi_{1}
is strictly positive in
\Omega
. Moreover,
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
is simple, i.e., if
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
satisfies (
3.15
) in weak sense with
\lambda=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
, then
u=t\xi_{1}
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iv)

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)=+\infty
.

Proof.

By (
3.10
),

the functional

L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
,
u\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[{\mathbf{j}}*u]u\,dx
is continuous.

(3.16)

Consequently, by (
3.3
), the functional

{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad u\mapsto\Phi(u):={\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,u)

is weakly lower semicontinuous. Moreover, setting
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}:=\{u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega),\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\}
, we have that

m_{1}:=\sup\limits_{u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[{\mathbf{j}}*u]u\,dx<+\infty

(3.17)

by (
3.16
), which implies that

{\mathcal{E}}(u,u)\leq\Phi(u)+m_{1}-\rho_{N}<+\infty\qquad\text{for $u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}$.}

(3.18)

Put
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega):=\inf_{{\mathcal{M}}_{1}}\Phi
. From (
3.16
), (
3.18
) and the weak lower semicontinuity of
\Phi
, it then follows that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
is attained by a function
\xi_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. Consequently, there exists a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{1},\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{\prime}(u)\varphi=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\xi_{1}\varphi\,dx\qquad\text{for all $\varphi\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)$.}

Choosing
\varphi=\xi_{1}
yields
\lambda=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
, hence
\xi_{1}
is an eigenfunction of (
3.15
) corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
. Next we proceed inductively and assume that
\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
and
\lambda_{2}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)
are already given for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
with the properties that for
i=2,\dots,k
, the function
\xi_{i}
is a minimizer
of
\Phi
within the set

{\mathcal{M}}_{i}:=\{u\in{\mathbb{H}}_{i}(\Omega)\>:\>\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\}=\{u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\>:\>\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1,\>\text{$\int_{\Omega}u\xi_{j}\,dx=0$ for $j=1,\dots i-1$}\},

\lambda_{i}^{L}(\Omega)=\inf_{{\mathcal{M}}_{i}}\Phi=\Phi(\xi_{i})
, and

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{i},\varphi)=\lambda_{i}^{L}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}\xi_{i}\varphi\,dx\qquad\text{for all $\varphi\in\mathbb{H}(\Omega)$.}

(3.19)

We then put

\displaystyle{\mathbb{H}}_{k+1}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\>:\>\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1,\>\text{$\int_{\Omega}u\xi_{i}\,dx=0$ for $i=1,\dots k$}\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{k+1}:=\left\{u\in{\mathbb{H}}_{k+1}(\Omega),\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\right\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}^{L}(\Omega):=\inf_{{\mathcal{M}}_{k+1}}\Phi.

By the same weak lower semicontinuity argument as above, the value
\lambda_{k+1}^{L}(\Omega)
is attained by a function
\xi_{k+1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k+1}
. Consequently, there exists a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with the property that

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{k+1},\varphi)=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\xi_{k+1}\varphi\,dx\qquad\text{for all $\varphi\in{\mathbb{H}}_{k+1}(\Omega)$.}

(3.20)

Choosing
\varphi=\xi_{k+1}
, it yields
\lambda=\lambda_{k+1}^{L}(\Omega)
. Moreover, for
i=1,\dots,k
, we have, by (
3.19
) and the definition of
{\mathbb{H}}_{k+1}(\Omega)
,

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{k+1},\xi_{i})={\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{i},\xi_{k+1})=\lambda_{i}^{L}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}\xi_{i}\xi_{k+1}\,dx=0=\lambda_{k+1}^{L}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}\xi_{k+1}\xi_{i}\,dx.

Hence (
3.20
) holds with
\lambda=\lambda_{k+1}^{L}(\Omega)
for all
\varphi\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
.
Inductively, we have now constructed an
L^{2}
-normalized sequence
(\xi_{k})_{k}
in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
and a nondecreasing sequence
(\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega))_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}
such that property (i) holds and such that
\xi_{k}
is an eigenfunction of (
3.15
) corresponding to
\lambda=\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, by construction, the sequence
(\xi_{k})_{k}
forms an orthonormal system in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Next we show property (iv), i.e.,
\lim\limits_{k\to+\infty}\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)=+\infty.
Supposing by contradiction that
c:=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)<+\infty
, we deduce that

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{k},\xi_{k})\leq c
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and therefore

{\mathcal{E}}(\xi_{k},\xi_{k})\leq c+m_{1}-\rho_{N}<+\infty\qquad\text{for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$}

by (
3.18
). Hence the sequence
(\xi_{k})
is bounded in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, and therefore it
contains a convergent subsequence
(\xi_{k_{j}})_{j}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by (
3.3
).
This however is impossible since the functions
\{\xi_{k_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are
L^{2}
-orthonormal. Hence (iv) is proved.

Next, to prove that
\{\xi_{k}\>:\>k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we first suppose by contradiction that there exists
v\in\mathbb{H}(\Omega)
with
\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1

and
\int_{\Omega}v\xi_{k}dx=0
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{k}^{L}(\Omega)=+\infty
, there exists an integer
k_{0}>0
such that

\Phi(v)<\lambda_{k_{0}}^{L}(\Omega)=\inf_{{\mathcal{M}}_{k_{0}}}\Phi(u)
, which by definition of
{\mathcal{M}}_{k_{0}}
implies that

\int_{\Omega}v\xi_{k}dx\not=0
for some
k\in\{1,\dots,k_{0}-1\}
. Contradiction. We conclude that
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
is contained in the
L^{2}
-closure of the span of
\{\xi_{k}\>:\>k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Since
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we conclude that the span of
\{\xi_{k}\>:\>k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and hence
\{\xi_{k}\>:\>k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Finally, we show property (iii), which also implies that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)<\lambda_{2}^{L}(\Omega)
. Let
w\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
be a
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunction of (
3.15
) corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
, i.e. we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(w,\varphi)=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}w\varphi\,dx\qquad\text{for all $w\in\mathbb{H}(\Omega)$.}

(3.21)

We show that
w
does not change sign. Indeed, choosing
\phi=w
in (
3.21
), we see that
w
is a minimizer of
\Phi\big|_{{\mathcal{M}}_{1}}
. On the other hand, we also have
|w|\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and

\Phi(|w|)={\mathcal{E}}_{L}(|w|,|w|)\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(w,w)=\Phi(w)=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega),

by Lemma
3.3
. Hence equality holds by definition of
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
, and then Lemma
3.3
implies that
w
does not change sign. In particular, we may assume that
\xi_{1}
is nonnegative, which implies that

{\mathcal{E}}(\xi_{1},\phi)-[\rho_{N}+\lambda_{1}]\int_{\Omega}\xi_{1}\phi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}[{\mathbf{j}}*\xi_{1}]\phi\,dx\geq 0\qquad\text{for all $\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$, $\phi\geq 0$.}

Hence
\xi_{1}
is a nontrivial, nonnegative weak supersolution of the equation
I\xi_{1}-(\rho_{N}+\lambda_{1})\xi_{1}=0
in
\Omega
in the sense of
[
13
]
, where
I
is the integral operator associated with the kernel
\mathbf{k}
defined in (
2.9
). Therefore,
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
applies and yields that
\xi_{1}>0
in
\Omega
.

Now suppose by contradiction that there is a function
w\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
satisfying (
3.21
) and such that
w\not=t\xi_{1}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then there exist a linear combination
\tilde{w}
of
w
and
\xi_{1}
which changes sign, and we may also assume that
\tilde{w}
is
L^{2}
-normalized. Since
\tilde{w}
also satisfies (
3.21
) in place of
w
, we arrive at a contradiction. Thus the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
is simple, and property (iii) holds.
∎

Note that the logarithmic Laplacian has the same structure of Dirichlet eigenvalues and eigenfunctions as the fractional Laplacian, see
[
18
, Section 3]
(cf. also
[
12
]
). A remarkable relationship between the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
and
(-\Delta)^{s}
for
s>0
close to
0
is given by Theorem
1.5
, which we prove now and restate here for the reader’s convenience.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let
\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)
denote the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
on
\Omega
for
s\in(0,1)
. Then we have

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega).

(3.22)

Moreover, if
u_{s}
is the unique nonnegative
L^{2}
-normalized Dirichlet eigenfunction
of
(-\Delta)^{s}
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)
, then we have

u_{s}\to\xi_{1}\qquad\text{in $L^{2}(\Omega)$,}

(3.23)

where
\xi_{1}
is the corresponding unique nonnegative
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunction of
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}^{L}
.

Proof.

We first recall that, for
0<s<1
, the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
is given by

\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)=\inf\{{\mathcal{E}}_{s}(u,u)\>:\>u\in C^{2}_{c}(\Omega),\>\|u\|_{L^{2}}=1\},

(3.24)

where the quadratic form
{\mathcal{E}}_{s}
is defined by

(u,v)\mapsto{\mathcal{E}}_{s}(u,u)=\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\bigl(u(x)-u(y)\bigr)\bigl(v(x)-v(y)\bigr)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dxdy.

From the variational characterization (
3.24
) and the fact that
(-\Delta)^{s}\psi\to\psi
as
s\to 0
for
\psi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
we observe that

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)=1.

Let

\Gamma_{s}:C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\qquad\Gamma_{s}u=\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}u-u}{s}.

For
w\in C^{2}_{c}(\Omega)
with
\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1
, we have that

\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)-1}{s}\leq\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{s}(w,w)-\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}}}{s}=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\langle\Gamma_{s}w,w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!w,w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

by Theorem
1.1
(i), and consequently,

\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)-1}{s}\leq\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w\in C^{2}_{c}(\Omega)}}{{\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1}}}\langle L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!w,w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}}{{\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1}}}{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(w,w)=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega).

(3.25)

Here we used (
3.11
) and the fact that
C^{2}_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
by Theorem
3.1
. Next we wish to prove (
3.23
). For this we first prove that the functions
u_{s}
remain uniformly bounded in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
as
s\to 0^{+}
. Indeed, by (
3.25
) we have, as
s\to 0^{+}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)+o(1)\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)-1}{s}=\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{s}(u_{s},u_{s})-1}{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N,s}}{2s}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{(u_{s}(x)-u_{s}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dxdy-\frac{c_{N,s}}{s}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{u_{s}(x)u_{s}(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dxdy

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{s}\Bigl(c_{N,s}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{u_{s}(x)^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dydx-1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N,s}}{2s}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{(u_{s}(x)-u_{s}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dxdy-\frac{c_{N,s}}{s}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{u_{s}(x)u_{s}(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dxdy+f_{N}(s)

with

f_{N}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\Bigl(c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}|x-y|^{-N-2s}\,dy-1\Bigr)=\frac{1}{s}\Bigl(\frac{c_{N,s}|S^{N-1}|}{2s}-1\Bigr).

Here we used that
\|u_{s}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)+o(1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{N,s}}{2s}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{(u_{s}(x)-u_{s}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy-\frac{c_{N,s}}{s}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{u_{s}(x)u_{s}(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy+f_{N}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N,s}}{sc_{N}}\Bigl({\mathcal{E}}(u_{s},u_{s})-\int_{\Omega}[{\mathbf{j}}*u_{s}]u_{s}dx\Bigr)+f_{N}(s).

Therefore, writing again
c_{N,s}=sd_{N}(s)
as in (
2.3
) and using that

d_{N}(s)\to c_{N},\quad f_{N}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\Bigl(\frac{d_{N}(s)|S^{N-1}|}{2}-1\Bigr)\to\frac{|S^{N-1}|}{2}d_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{d_{N}^{\prime}(0)}{c_{N}}=\rho_{N}\;\quad\text{as \,$s\to 0^{+}$,}

we infer that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(u_{s},u_{s})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{N}}{d_{N}(s)}\Bigl(\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)+o(1)-f_{N}(s)\Bigr)+\int_{\Omega}[{\mathbf{j}}*u_{s}]u_{s}dx

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(1+o(1))\Bigl(\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)-\rho_{N}+o(1)\Bigr)+m_{1}\qquad\text{as $s\to 0^{+}$,}

where
m_{1}
is given in (
3.17
). We thus conclude that the functions
u_{s}
remain uniformly bounded in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
as
s\to 0^{+}
.
Now, to prove (
3.23
), we argue by contradiction and suppose that there exists
{\varepsilon}>0
and a sequence
s_{k}\to 0
with

\|u_{s_{k}}-\xi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all $k\in\mathbb{N}$.}

(3.26)

Since the sequence
\{u_{s_{k}}\}_{k}
is bounded in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, we may, by (
3.3
), pass to a subsequence such that

u_{s_{k}}\rightharpoonup u_{0}
in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
,

u_{s_{k}}\to u_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)

and

\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s_{k}}(\Omega)-1}{s_{k}}\to\lambda^{*}\in[-\infty,\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)]

as
k\to\infty
.

In particular, it follows that
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=1
. For
\psi\in C^{2}_{c}(\Omega)
, we now find that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s_{k}}(\Omega)-1}{s_{k}}\langle u_{s_{k}},\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{s_{k}}(u_{s_{k}},\psi)-\langle u_{s_{k}},\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}}{s_{k}}

(3.27)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle u_{s_{k}},\Gamma_{s_{k}}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle u_{0},L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}={\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u_{0},\psi).

Here we used Theorem
1.1
(i) with
p=2
. Since also
\langle u_{s_{k}},\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}\to\langle u_{0},\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}
as
k\to\infty
, and since we may choose
\psi\in C^{2}_{c}(\Omega)
with
\langle u_{0},\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}>0
, it follows from (
3.27
) that
\lambda^{*}>-\infty
and

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u_{0},\psi)=\lambda^{*}\langle u_{0},\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}\qquad\text{for all $\psi\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)$.}

Thus
u_{0}
is an eigenfunction of
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda^{*}
. Since
\lambda^{*}\leq\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
, it follows from the definition of
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
that
\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
. Since moreover
u_{0}\geq 0
and
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=1
,
u_{0}=\xi_{1}
is the unique nonnegative
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunction of
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
. This contradicts (
3.26
), and hence (
3.23
) is proved. Next, let
\lambda_{*}:=\liminf\limits_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)-1}{s}
, and let
s^{k}\to 0^{+}
be a sequence such that
\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s^{k}}(\Omega)-1}{s^{k}}\to\lambda_{*}
. We have already seen that
u_{s^{k}}\to\xi_{1}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. By the same argument as above, we thus find that
\lambda_{*}>-\infty
and that

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{1},\psi)=\lambda_{*}\langle\xi_{1},\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}\qquad\text{for all $\psi\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)$}.

Consequently, we have
\lambda_{*}=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
, and together with (
3.25
) this implies
(
3.22
). The proof is finished.
∎

We may now deduce the Faber-Krahn-inequality for the logarithmic Laplacian, which we restate here for the reader’s convenience.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\rho>0
. Among all bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega
with
|\Omega|=\rho
, the ball
B=B_{r}
with
|B|=\rho
minimizes
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain with
|\Omega|=\rho
. By
[
1
, Theorem 5]
, we have

\lambda_{1}^{s}(B)\leq\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)\qquad\text{for $s\in(0,1)$.}

Consequently, we have

\lambda_{1}^{L}(B)=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s}(B)-1}{s}\leq\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)-1}{s}=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega).

∎

Remark 3.7
.

We note that we do not have a direct proof of Corollary
3.6
, which is merely based on symmetrization arguments applied to the quadratic form
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
. Moreover, even though we have, with the notation above,
\lambda_{1}^{s}(B)<\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)
in the case where
\Omega\not=B
, the argument above does not imply that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(B)<\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
when
\Omega\not=B
. The characterization of the equality case remains an open problem.

4
The maximum principle on bounded domains

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded domain. In this section, we present maximum principles for the operator
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
on
\Omega
. We start with a strong version of the maximum principle for pointwise solutions, which turns out to be a rather direct consequence of the representation (
2.12
).

Proposition 4.1
.

(Strong Maximum Principle for pointwise solutions)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded domain with
h_{\Omega}+\rho_{N}\geq 0
on
\Omega
. Moreover, let
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a function which is continuous on
\bar{\Omega}
, Dini continuous in
\Omega
, and satisfies

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0\quad\text{in $\Omega$,}\qquad u\geq 0\qquad\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$}.

Then
u>0
in
\Omega
or
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that
u
is not positive in
\Omega
. Since
u
is continuous on
\bar{\Omega}
, there exists a point
x_{0}\in\Omega
with

u(x_{0})=\min_{x\in\Omega}u(x)\leq 0,

(4.1)

whereas, by (
2.12
),

0\leq[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u](x_{0})=c_{N}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x_{0})-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy-c_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}\,dy+[h_{\Omega}(x_{0})+\rho_{N}]u(x_{0})\leq 0,

since all three terms are nonpositive by assumption and (
4.1
). It follows that

0=\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x_{0})-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}\,dy=[h_{\Omega}(x_{0})+\rho_{N}]u(x_{0}).

(4.2)

The first two equalities and (
4.1
) imply that
u\equiv u(x_{0})\leq 0
in
\Omega
and
u\equiv 0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
. Then the preceding arguments are still valid if we choose
x_{0}
sufficiently close to the boundary so that
[h_{\Omega}(x_{0})+\rho_{N}]>0
by (
3.7
), which then implies that
u(x_{0})=0
by (
4.2
). Hence
u\equiv 0
in
\Omega
, and therefore
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
∎

In the following, we wish to extend the maximum principle to functions which satisfy the inequality
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in weak sense. This also allows us to formulate necessary and sufficient spectral conditions on the validity of the maximum principle as given in Theorem
1.8
. We need to introduce an appropriate space of functions
u
which may have nonzero values outside of
\Omega
and which allows to give a meaning to the inequality
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in
\Omega
in weak sense.
For this, we first let
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
denote the space of all functions
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

{\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy<+\infty.

Then
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space with scalar product given by

\langle u,v\rangle_{{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)}:={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v,\Omega)+\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where

{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v,\Omega):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy.

We also observe that, if
u\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
satisfies

\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\,dx<+\infty,

then the trivial extension
\tilde{u}
of
u
to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is contained in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
, and

{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u},\tilde{u})={\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)+\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\,dx.

(4.3)

This follows immediately from (
3.4
). In the following, we often identify
u
with
\tilde{u}
.
We shall need the following analogue of Theorem
3.1
for the space
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, if
u\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
is nonnegative, we have:

(i)

There exists a sequence of functions
u_{n}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
with compact support in
\Omega
,
0\leq u_{n}\leq u_{n+1}\leq u
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
u_{n}\to u
in
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
.

(ii)

There exists a sequence of nonnegative functions
u_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
u_{n}\to u
in
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Since for
u\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
we have
u^{\pm}\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
, it suffices again to consider a nonnegative function
u\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
u_{k}:=\min\{u,k\}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. Then we have that
0\leq u_{k}\leq u_{k+1}\leq u
on
\Omega

and

|u_{k}(x)-u_{k}(y)|\leq|u(x)-u(y)|\qquad\text{for all $x,y\in\Omega$.}

From this it readily follows that
u_{k}\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, for
v_{k}:=u-u_{k}=(u-k)^{+}
we have that

v_{j}(x)\leq v_{k}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad|v_{j}(x)-v_{j}(y)|\leq|v_{k}(x)-v_{k}(y)|\qquad\text{for $x,y\in\Omega$, $k<j$.}

Since
v_{k}\to 0
a.e. in
\Omega
, it follows by monotone convergence that

\|u-u_{k}\|_{{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)}^{2}=\|v_{k}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}(v_{k}(x)-v_{k}(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dxdy+\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{2}(x)\,dx\to 0

as
k\to\infty
. Hence it suffices from now on to consider the case where
u
is bounded, which implies that
u\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega,\log\frac{1}{\dist(x,\partial\Omega)})
. We extend
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by setting
u\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
. As remarked above, we then have that
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
. Consequently, since
\|v\|_{{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)}\leq C\|v\|_{{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)}
for every
v\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
with some constant
C>0
, the claim now follows from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. From a logarithmic boundary Hardy inequality which we will prove in Proposition
6.1
in the Appendix, it follows that

\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\Omega}(x)u^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all $u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$}

with a constant
C>0
, see Corollary
6.2
below. Since moreover
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
by Proposition
4.2
, we deduce a posteriori from (
3.2
) and (
4.3
) that the spaces
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
and
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
are isomorphic in this case – with equivalent norms – via the trivial extension map
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
,
u\mapsto\tilde{u}
.
This fact may be of independent interest, but we will not use it in the paper since it does not simplify the proofs.

Next, we let
{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
denote the space of all measurable functions
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that the restriction of
u
to
\Omega
is contained in
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
. The following observation will allow us to define a weak notion of the inequality
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
for functions in
{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

The quantities
{\mathcal{E}}(u,v)
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,v)
are well defined for
u\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
and
v\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
with

\supp v\subset\subset\Omega
.

Proof.

Setting
\Omega^{\prime}:=\supp v
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u(x)-u(y)|\cdot|v(x)-v(y)|\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\dots+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\dots

\displaystyle\leq\bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(v,\Omega)\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}|v(x)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}|u(x)-u(y)|\mathbf{k}(x-y)dydx,

where

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}|v(x)|

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}|u(x)-u(y)|\mathbf{k}(x-y)dydx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}|v(x)||u(x)|\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\,dx+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}|v(x)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}|u(y)|\mathbf{k}(x-y)dydx

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+c_{2}\|v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{1}_{0}}

with

c_{1}:=\sup_{x\in\Omega^{\prime}}\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{2}:=\sup_{x\in\Omega^{\prime},y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\mathbf{k}(x-y)(1+|y|)^{N}.

Since
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
, the values
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are finite. It thus follows that
{\mathcal{E}}(u,v)
is well-defined in Lebesgue sense with

|{\mathcal{E}}(u,v)|\leq\bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(v,\Omega)\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}+c_{1}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+c_{2}\|v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{1}_{0}}

and constants
c_{1},c_{2}
depending only on
\Omega
and
\supp v\subset\subset\Omega
.
Moreover, we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u(x)||v(y)|{\mathbf{j}}(x-y)\,dxdy=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}|v(y)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u(x)|{\mathbf{j}}(x-y)\,dxdy\leq c_{3}\|v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{1}_{0}}

with

c_{3}:=\sup_{y\in\Omega^{\prime},x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\mathbf{j}}(x-y)(1+|x|)^{N}.

Hence
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,v)
is also well-defined with

|{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,v)|\leq\bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(v,\Omega)\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}+(c_{1}+|\rho_{N}|)\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(c_{2}+c_{3})\|v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{1}_{0}}.

The proof is complete.∎

We now recall the following definition from the introduction.

Definition 4.5
.

i)

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
, we say that
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in
\Omega
(in weak sense) if
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,\phi)\geq 0
for all nonnegative
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

ii)

We say that
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
satisfies the maximum principle on
\Omega
if for every
u\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
with

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in
\Omega
and
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
, we have
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Remark 4.6
.

If
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is uniformly Dini continuous on
\bar{\Omega}
, then
u\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, if
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in pointwise sense, then this also holds in weak sense. This follows since

\int_{\Omega}[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u]\varphi\,dx={\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,\varphi)

for functions
u\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
which are uniformly Dini continuous on
\bar{\Omega}
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. Indeed, in this case we have

\int_{\Omega}|\varphi(x)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u(x)-u(y)|\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dydx\leq\int_{\Omega}|\varphi(x)|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega_{u,\Omega}(r)}{r}\,dr\,dx<+\infty,

and therefore, by Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\varphi(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)

\displaystyle\,dydx=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\varphi(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dydx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\varphi(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(y)-u(x))\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dydx\Bigr)={\mathcal{E}}(u,\varphi),

which yields that

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,\varphi)=\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl({\mathbf{j}}*u-\rho_{N}u\bigr)\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}[L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u]\varphi\,dx.

In the proof of weak maximum principles, testing the equation in weak sense with
u^{-}
is usually a key step. Related to this, we have the following useful property.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and suppose that
u\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
satisfies
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in
\Omega
and
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
. Then

u^{-}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
and

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u^{-},u^{-})\leq 0.

Proof.

We first note that

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,w)\geq 0
for all nonnegative
w\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
with
\supp w\subset\subset\Omega
.

(4.4)

Indeed, for every such
w
, Theorem
3.1
(ii) yields a sequence of nonnegative functions
w_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with
w_{n}\to w
in
{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, an inspection of the proof of Theorem
3.1
(ii) shows that there exists a compact set
K\subset\Omega
with
\supp w_{n}\subset K
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence the estimates in the proof of Lemma
4.4
show that

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,w)=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,w_{n})\geq 0,

as claimed in (
4.4
).

Next we note that
u^{-}\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
since
u\in{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
. Hence, by Proposition
4.2
(i),
there exists a sequence of nonnegative functions
v_{n}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
with compact support in
\Omega
,

0\leq v_{n}\leq v_{n+1}\leq u^{-}
in
\Omega
and
v_{n}\to u^{-}
in
{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
as
n\to\infty
. Then

{\mathcal{E}}(u,v_{n})={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v_{n},\Omega)+\int_{\Omega}v_{n}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy,

where, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v_{n},\Omega)\to{\mathfrak{b}}(u,u^{-},\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))\cdot(u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dxdy

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\ dxdy=-{\mathfrak{b}}(u^{-},\Omega)

and, since
0\leq v_{n}\leq u^{-}
in
\Omega
and
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v_{n}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)dydx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}v_{n}(x)u(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\mathbf{k}(x-y)dydx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}v_{n}(x)u^{-}(x)\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\,dx.

With the help of (
4.4
), we thus infer that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}v_{n}(x)u^{-}(x)\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\,dx\leq-\int_{\Omega}v_{n}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)dydx

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v_{n},\Omega)-{\mathcal{E}}(u,v_{n})={\mathfrak{b}}(u,v_{n},\Omega)-{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u,v_{n})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl({\mathbf{j}}*u-\rho_{N}u\bigr)v_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq{\mathfrak{b}}(u,v_{n},\Omega)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl({\mathbf{j}}*u-\rho_{N}u\bigr)v_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq o(1)-{\mathfrak{b}}(u^{-},\Omega)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl({\mathbf{j}}*u-\rho_{N}u\bigr)u^{-}\,dx

\displaystyle=o(1)-{\mathfrak{b}}(u^{-},\Omega)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[{\mathbf{j}}*u]u^{-}\,dx-\rho_{N}\|u^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq o(1)-{\mathfrak{b}}(u^{-},\Omega)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[{\mathbf{j}}*u^{-}]u^{-}\,dx-\rho_{N}\|u^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

By monotone convergence, we thus conclude that

\int_{\Omega}[u^{-}(x)]^{2}\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\,dx\leq-{\mathfrak{b}}(u^{-},\Omega)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[{\mathbf{j}}*u^{-}]u^{-}\,dx-\rho_{N}\|u^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

and therefore
u^{-}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
with

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u^{-},u^{-})={\mathfrak{b}}(u^{-},\Omega)+\int_{\Omega}[u^{-}(x)]^{2}\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}[{\mathbf{j}}*u^{-}]u^{-}\,dx+\rho_{N}\|u^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

The proof is complete.∎

We may now complete the proof of Theorem
1.8
, which we restate here for the reader’s convenience.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
satisfies the maximum principle on
\Omega
if and only if
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)>0
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)>0
, and let
u\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
satisfy
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in
\Omega
and
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
. By Proposition
4.7
, we then have
u^{-}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
and

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)\|u^{-}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u^{-},u^{-})\leq 0.

Consequently,
u^{-}\equiv 0
, and therefore
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Thus
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
satisfies the maximum principle on
\Omega
. Suppose now that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)\leq 0
, and let
\xi_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\subset{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
be the unique positive
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunction of
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
.
With
u:=-\xi_{1}
, we then have
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
,
u<0
in
\Omega
and
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)u\geq 0
in
\Omega
.
Hence
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
does not satisfy the maximum principle on
\Omega
.
∎

It is now easy to deduce Corollary
1.10
, which again we restate for the reader’s convenience.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then we have

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)\leq\log\lambda_{1}^{1}(\Omega)
, where
\lambda_{1}^{1}(\Omega)
is the first eigenvalue of the Dirichlet Laplacian
-\Delta
on
\Omega
. Moreover, if
\lambda_{1}^{1}(\Omega)\leq 1
, then
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
does not satisfy the maximum principle on
\Omega
.

Proof.

By
[
15
, Corollary 4]
, we have

\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)<\bigl[\lambda_{1}^{1}(\Omega)\bigr]^{s}\qquad\text{for $s\in(0,1)$.}

Consequently,

\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{s}(\Omega)-1}{s}\leq\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\bigl[\lambda_{1}^{1}(\Omega)\bigr]^{s}-1}{s}=\log\lambda_{1}^{1}(\Omega).

If
\lambda_{1}^{1}(\Omega)\leq 1
, we conclude that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)\leq 0
, and hence
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
does not satisfy the maximum principle on
\Omega
by Theorem
1.8
.
∎

The following corollary is a variant of Proposition
4.1
, which applies to functions
u\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
satisfying the inequality
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u\geq 0
in weak sense.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and suppose that
h_{\Omega}+\rho_{N}\geq 0
on
\Omega
. Then
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)>0
, and thus
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
satisfies the maximum principle on
\Omega
.

Proof.

By definition of
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)
and (
3.12
), it follows that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)\geq 0
.
We claim that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)>0
. Assuming by contradiction that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)=0
, we let, as before,
\xi_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)
be the corresponding nonnegative
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunction. Then, by (
3.12
),

0=\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)\|\xi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}={\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\xi_{1},\xi_{1})={\mathfrak{b}}(\xi_{1},\Omega)+\int_{\Omega}[h_{\Omega}(x)+\rho_{N}]\xi_{1}^{2}(x)\,dx\geq{\mathfrak{b}}(\xi_{1},\Omega).

From the definition of
{\mathfrak{b}}(\xi_{1},\Omega)
, we then deduce that
\xi_{1}\equiv c
in
\Omega
with a constant
c>0
. Then the above estimate gives

0\geq c^{2}\int_{\Omega}[h_{\Omega}(x)+\rho_{N}]\,dx

and therefore
h_{\Omega}+\rho_{N}\equiv 0
in
\Omega
by assumption. This however contradicts the fact that
h_{\Omega}(x)\to\infty
as
\dist(x,\partial\Omega)\to 0
. The proof is finished.
∎

Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
4.10
show that it is useful to have estimates for
h_{\Omega}
from below. In the following lemma, we consider the special case
\Omega=B_{r}
for some
r>0
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\Omega=B_{r}
for some
r>0
. Then we have

h_{\Omega}(x)\geq 2\log\frac{1}{r}\qquad\text{for $x\in\Omega$,}

and equality is attained at
x=0
.
Consequently,
h_{\Omega}+\rho_{N}\geq 0
on
\Omega
if and only if

r\leq r_{N}:=2e^{\frac{1}{2}(\psi(\frac{N}{2})-\gamma)}.

Proof.

We first show that
h_{\Omega}(x)\geq h_{\Omega}(0)
for
x\in\Omega
. Indeed, let
x\in\Omega
and
r_{1}(x)=\Bigl(\frac{|\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)|}{|B_{1}|}+1\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{N}}
, so that
|\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)|=|B_{r_{1}(x)}\setminus B_{1}|
. It is then easy to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{[\Omega-x]\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N}}dz\leq\int_{B_{r_{1}(x)}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N}}dz=|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|\log r_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=|B_{1}|\log\Bigl(\frac{|\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)|}{|B_{1}|}+1\Bigr)=|B_{1}|\log\frac{|B_{1}|+|\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)|}{|B_{1}|}.

Moreover, we choose
r_{2}(x):=\Bigl(1-\frac{|B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega|}{|B_{1}|}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{N}}
, so that
|B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega|=|B_{1}\setminus B_{r_{2}(x)}|
. We then have, for
x\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}\setminus[\Omega-x]}\frac{1}{|z|^{N}}dz\geq\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r_{2}(x)}}\frac{1}{|z|^{N}}dz=-|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|\log r_{2}

\displaystyle=-|B_{1}|\log\Bigl(1-\frac{|B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega|}{|B_{1}|}\Bigr)=|B_{1}|\log\frac{|B_{1}|}{|B_{1}|-|B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega|}.

Consequently,

h_{\Omega}(x)\geq c_{N}|B_{1}|\Bigl(\log\frac{|B_{1}|}{|B_{1}|-|B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega|}-\log\frac{|B_{1}|+|\Omega\setminus B_{1}(x)|}{|B_{1}|}\Bigr)=:\tilde{h}_{\Omega}(x).

Now the quantity
\tilde{h}_{\Omega}
is minimized at points
x\in\Omega
where
|B_{1}(x)\setminus\Omega|
is minimal.
Thus, in the case where
\Omega=B_{r}
for some
r>0
, then
\tilde{h}_{\Omega}
is minimized at
x=0
, and we have

\tilde{h}_{\Omega}(0)=h_{\Omega}(0)
since all inequalities in the above estimate are equalities in the case
x=0
.
Moreover, we compute that

h_{\Omega}(0)=-c_{N}|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|\log r=c_{N}|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|\log\frac{1}{r}=2\log\frac{1}{r}

in this case.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain with

\frac{|\Omega|}{|B_{1}|}\leq 2^{N}e^{\frac{N}{2}(\psi(\frac{N}{2})-\gamma)}.

(4.5)

Then
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
satisfies the maximum principle on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
r:=\Bigl(\frac{|\Omega|}{|B_{1}|}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{N}}
, so that
|\Omega|=|B_{r}|
. By assumption (
4.5
) we have
r\leq r_{N}
, which implies that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(B_{r})>0
by Theorem
4.8
, Corollary
4.10
and Lemma
4.11
.

By Corollary
3.6
, it then follows that
\lambda_{1}^{L}(\Omega)>0
, and thus
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
satisfies the maximum principle on
\Omega
by Theorem
4.8
.
∎

Now Corollary
1.9
readily follows from Corollaries
4.10
and
4.12
.

5
Regularity and boundary decay

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.11
. In the following, we fix a function
\ell:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with

\ell(t)\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\log\ell(t)}{\Bigl(-\log t\Bigr)^{\sigma}}\to 0\qquad\text{as $t\to 0\quad$ for any $\sigma>0$.}

(5.1)

A possible choice for
\ell
is a positive extension of the function
t\mapsto-\frac{1}{\log t}
,
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. We also define the constant

\kappa_{N}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\frac{1}{\bigl(1+|z^{\prime}|^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{N}{2}}}\,dz^{\prime}\quad\text{for $N\geq 2$,}\qquad\kappa_{1}:=1.

(5.2)

We need the following integral estimates.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let the function
h:[0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}\to(0,\infty)
be defined by

h(t):=\int_{S^{N-1}}|te_{N}-y|^{-N}d\sigma(y).

Then
h(t)=\frac{h_{0}(t)}{|t-1|}
for
t\in[0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}
with a bounded continuous function
h_{0}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
h_{0}(1)=\kappa_{N}
.

Proof.

For
\tau>0
, we consider the rescaled and translated sphere
S_{\tau}:=\tau(S^{N-1}-e_{N})
with radius
\tau
which touches the origin and is contained in the half space
\{x_{N}\leq 0\}
. For
t\in[0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}
we then have, with
\tau_{t}:=\frac{1}{|t-1|}
,

|t-1|h(t)=|t-1|^{N}\int_{S_{\tau_{t}}}\bigl|te_{N}-\bigl(|t-1|y+e_{N}\bigr)|^{-N}d\sigma(y)=\int_{S_{\tau_{t}}}\Bigl|\frac{t-1}{|t-1|}e_{N}-y\Bigr|^{-N}d\sigma(y)=:h_{0}(t).

Observe that this defines a continuous function
h_{0}:[0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}\to\mathbb{R}
which is bounded on
[0,\infty)\setminus(1-{\varepsilon},1+{\varepsilon})
for any
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
. It thus remains to show that
h_{0}(t)\to\kappa_{N}
as
t\to 1
, i.e., that

\int_{S_{\tau}}\bigl|\pm e_{N}-y\bigr|^{-N}d\sigma(y)\to\kappa_{N}\qquad\text{as $\tau\to\infty$.}

(5.3)

For this we choose
\frac{N-1}{N}<\alpha<1
, and we let
\tau>1
. Moreover, we consider the local graph parametrization

\psi_{\tau}:B_{\tau^{\alpha}}\to S_{\tau},\qquad\psi_{\tau}(z^{\prime})=(z^{\prime},\phi_{\tau}(z^{\prime}))\quad\text{with $\phi_{\tau}(z^{\prime})=\sqrt{\tau^{2}-|z^{\prime}|^{2}}-\tau$},

where
B_{\tau^{\alpha}}:=B_{\tau^{\alpha}}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
, and we let
A_{\tau}:=S_{\tau}\setminus\psi_{\tau}(B_{\tau^{\alpha}})
. Then we have
|y\pm e_{N}|\geq\tau^{\alpha}
for every
y\in A_{\tau}
and therefore

\int_{A_{\tau}}|e_{N}\pm y|^{-N}d\sigma(y)\leq|S_{\tau}|\tau^{-\alpha N}=O(\tau^{N-1-\alpha N})\to 0\qquad\text{as $\tau\to\infty.$}

(5.4)

Moreover,

\int_{\psi_{\tau}(B_{\tau^{\alpha}})}|e_{N}\pm y|^{-N}d\sigma(y)=\int_{B_{\tau^{\alpha}}}h_{\tau}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\quad\text{with}\quad h_{\tau}^{\pm}(z^{\prime}):=\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}-|z^{\prime}|^{2}}}\bigl(|z^{\prime}|^{2}+[\phi_{\tau}(z^{\prime})\pm 1]^{2}\bigr)^{-\frac{N}{2}}.

Note that
0\leq\tau-\sqrt{\tau^{2}-|z^{\prime}|^{2}}\leq 1-\sqrt{1-|z^{\prime}|^{2}}\leq 1
for
|z^{\prime}|\leq 1
,
\tau\geq 1
, which implies that

[\phi_{\tau}(z^{\prime})\pm 1]^{2}=\Bigl(\pm 1-\bigl(\tau-\sqrt{\tau^{2}-|z^{\prime}|^{2}}\,\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}\geq 1-|z^{\prime}|^{2}\qquad\text{for $|z^{\prime}|\leq 1$, $\tau\geq 1$.}

Furthermore,
\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}-|z^{\prime}|^{2}}}\leq\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}-\tau^{2\alpha}}}\leq 2
for
z^{\prime}\in B_{\tau^{\alpha}}
if
\tau\geq\bigl(\frac{4}{3}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}}
. Consequently,

|h_{\tau}(z^{\prime})|\leq 2\Bigl(|z^{\prime}|^{2}+[\phi_{\tau}(z^{\prime})\pm 1]^{2}\Bigr)^{-\frac{N}{2}}\leq 2\Bigl(1_{\{|z^{\prime}|\geq 1\}}|z^{\prime}|^{-N}+1_{\{|z^{\prime}|\leq 1\}}\Bigr)

(5.5)

for
\tau\geq\bigl(\frac{4}{3}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}}
,
|z^{\prime}|\leq B_{\tau^{\alpha}}
, where the RHS of (
5.5
) defines an integrable function on
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
.
Furthermore, we see that
h_{\tau}(z^{\prime})\to\bigl(1+|z^{\prime}|^{2}\bigr)^{-\frac{N}{2}}
as
\tau\to\infty
for every
z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
. Combining this pointwise convergence property with (
5.5
) and Lebesgue’s theorem, we find that

\int_{\psi_{\tau}(B_{\tau^{\alpha}})}|e_{N}\pm y|^{-N}d\sigma(y)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\bigl(1+|z^{\prime}|^{2}\bigr)^{-\frac{N}{2}}\,dz^{\prime}=\kappa_{N}\qquad\text{as $\tau\to\infty$.}

(5.6)

Now (
5.4
) and (
5.6
) yield (
5.3
), as required.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
R>0
and
\rho(x)={\rm dist}(x,\partial B_{R})=|x|-R
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{R}
. Then we have that

\int_{B_{R}}|x-y|^{-N}\,dy\geq-(\kappa_{N}+o(1))\log\rho(x)\qquad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{R}$ as $\rho(x)\to 0$.}

(5.7)

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{R}
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}|x-y|^{-N}\,dy=\int_{0}^{R}\tau^{N-1}\int_{S^{N-1}}|x-\tau y|^{-N}\,dy=\int_{0}^{R}\tau^{-1}\int_{S^{N-1}}|\frac{x}{\tau}-y|^{-N}\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}\tau^{-1}\frac{h_{0}(\bigl|\frac{x}{\tau}\bigr|)}{||\frac{x}{\tau}|-1|}\,d\tau=\int_{0}^{R-\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{h_{0}(\bigl|\frac{x}{\tau}\bigr|)}{|x|-\tau}\,d\tau+\int_{R-\ell(\rho(x))}^{R}\frac{h_{0}(\bigl|\frac{x}{\tau}\bigr|)}{|x|-\tau}\,d\tau.

(5.8)

Since
\ell(\rho(x))\to 0
as
|x|\to R
, we have

\sup_{R-\ell(\rho(x))\leq\tau\leq R}\>\>\Bigl|h_{0}(\bigl|\frac{x}{\tau}\bigr|)-\kappa_{N}\Bigr|\to 0\qquad\text{as $|x|\to R$}

by Lemma
5.1
and therefore, by (
5.1
),

\displaystyle\int_{R-\ell(\rho(x))}^{R}

\displaystyle\frac{h_{0}(\bigl|\frac{x}{\tau}\bigr|)}{|x|-\tau}\,d\tau=\bigl(\kappa_{N}+o(1)\bigr)\int_{R-\ell(\rho(x))}^{R}\frac{1}{|x|-\tau}d\tau=\bigl(\kappa_{N}+o(1)\bigr)\int_{\rho(x)}^{\rho(x)+\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{1}{\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=\bigl(\kappa_{N}+o(1)\bigr)\bigl(\log\bigl(\rho(x)+\ell(\rho(x))\bigr)-\log(\rho(x))\bigr)\geq\bigl(\kappa_{N}+o(1)\bigr)\bigl(\log\bigl(\ell(\rho(x))\bigr)-\log(\rho(x))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\kappa_{N}+o(1)\bigr)\bigl(-\log(\rho(x))\bigr)\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)\to 0$.}

(5.9)

Moreover, since
h_{0}
is a bounded function,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{R-\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{h_{0}(\bigl|\frac{x}{\tau}\bigr|)}{|x|-\tau}\,d\tau\Bigr|\leq\|h_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\rho(x)+\ell(\rho(x))}^{|x|}\frac{1}{\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=\|h_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigl(\log(|x|)-\log\bigl(\rho(x)+\ell(\rho(x))\bigr)\bigr)\leq\|h_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigl(\log(|x|)-\log\bigl(\ell(\rho(x))\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle=o(1)\bigl(-\log(\rho(x))\bigr)\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)=|x|-R\to 0$.}

(5.10)

Combining (
5.8
), (
5.9
) and (
5.10
), we get (
5.7
).
∎

Next, we construct a barrier function which will allow us to prove Theorem
1.11
with the help of the weak maximum principle.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\tau\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
0<R<\frac{1}{2}
. Then there exists
\delta>0
and a continuous function
V\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with the following properties:

(i)

V\equiv 0
in
B_{R}
and
V>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{B_{R}}
;

(ii)

V
is (locally) Dini continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{B_{R}}
;

(iii)

V(x)=\bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\bigr)^{-\tau}
for
x\in A_{R,\delta}
;

(iv)

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!V(x)\geq\frac{(1-2\tau)\kappa_{N}c_{N}}{2(1-\tau)}\bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\bigr)^{1-\tau}
for
x\in A_{R,\delta}
.

Here
c_{N}=\pi^{-\frac{N}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{N}{2})
as before,
\kappa_{N}
is given in (
5.2
),
A_{R,\delta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\>:R<|x|<R+\delta\}
and

\rho(x):=|x|-R=\dist(x,B_{R})
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
|x|>R
.

Proof.

We consider the function
r\mapsto\bigl(-\log r\bigr)^{-\tau}
which is positive, strictly increasing and concave in the interval
(0,\delta_{\tau}]
with
\delta_{\tau}:=e^{-(\tau+1)}
. Indeed, this follows since

\frac{d}{dr}\frac{1}{(-\log r)^{\tau}}=\frac{\tau}{r(-\log r)^{\tau+1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}\frac{1}{(-\log r)^{\tau}}=-\frac{\tau}{r^{2}(-\log r)^{\tau+1}}\Bigl(1+\frac{\tau+1}{\log r}\Bigr).

Let
g:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive extension of class
C^{1}
of the function
(0,\delta_{\tau}]\to\mathbb{R}
,
r\mapsto\bigl(-\log r\bigr)^{-\tau}
such that
g
decays to zero exponentially as
r\to\infty
.
In particular, we have

g(b)-g(a)\leq\min\{g(b-a),g^{\prime}(a)(b-a)\}\qquad\text{for \,$0<a\leq b<\delta_{\tau}$.}

(5.11)

We now let
R>0
and consider the radial function

V\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\qquad V(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0&&\quad\text{for \, $|x|\leq R$},\\
&g(\rho(x))&&\quad\text{for \, $|x|>R$}.\end{aligned}\right.

Then properties (i), (ii) and (iii) are obviously satisfied with
\delta=\delta_{\tau}
. It remains to show that (iv) holds after making
\delta
smaller if necessary. To see this, we consider
x\in A_{R,\delta_{\tau}}
from now on, and we write

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!V(x)=c_{N}\Bigl(I_{1}(x)+I_{2}(x)+I_{3}(x)\Bigr)

with

I_{1}(x)=\int_{B_{1}(x)\setminus B_{R}}\frac{V(x)-V(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy,\qquad I_{2}(x)=-[{\mathbf{j}}*V](x)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{V(y)}{|x-y|^{N}}\,dy

and

I_{3}(x)=\Bigl[\rho_{N}+\int_{B_{1}(x)\cap B_{R}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy\Bigr]V(x),

By Lemma
5.2
and since
0<R<\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\int_{B_{1}(x)\cap B_{R}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy=\int_{B_{R}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N}}dy=-(\kappa_{N}+o(1))\log\rho(x)\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)\to 0$,}

which implies that

I_{3}(x)=(\kappa_{N}+o(1))(-\log\rho(x))^{1-\tau}\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)\to 0$.}

(5.12)

Moreover, by a standard estimate as in the proof of Lemma
2.1
, there exists
C>0
such that

|I_{2}(x)|\leq C\|V\|_{L^{1}_{0}}\qquad\text{for any $x\in A_{R,\delta_{\tau}}$.}

(5.13)

In view of (
5.12
) and (
5.13
), property (iv) follows for
\delta>0
sufficiently small once we have shown that

-I_{1}(x)\leq\Bigl(\frac{\tau\kappa_{N}}{1-\tau}+o(1)\Bigr)(-\log\rho(x))^{1-\tau}\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)\to 0$.}

(5.14)

Using the definition of
V
and the fact that
g
is increasing on
(0,\delta_{\tau})
, we find that

-I_{1}(x)\leq\int_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!\leq\!|y|\!\leq\!|x|+1$}}\frac{V(y)-V(x)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy=I_{1,1}(x)+I_{1,2}(x)+I_{1,3}(x)

with

I_{1,1}(x)=\int_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!\leq\!|y|\!\leq\!|x|+\rho(x)$}}\frac{V(y)-V(x)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy,\quad I_{1,2}(x)=\int_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!+\!\rho(x)\!\leq\!|y|\!\leq\!|x|\!+\!\ell(\rho(x))$}}\frac{V(y)-V(x)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

and

I_{1,3}(x)=\int_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!+\!\ell(\rho(x))\!\leq\!|y|\!\leq\!|x|+1$}}\frac{V(y)-V(x)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy,

where, as before, the function
\ell
is given with the properties (
5.1
). We compute that

\displaystyle I_{1,2}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!+\!\rho(x)\!\leq\!|y|\!\leq\!|x|\!+\!\ell(\rho(x))$}}\frac{g(|y|-R)-g(|x|-R)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{|x|+\rho(x)}^{|x|+\ell(\rho(x))}\Bigl(g(t-R)-g(|x|-R)\Bigr)t^{N-1}\int_{S^{N-1}}|x-t\theta|^{-N}\,d\theta dt,

where, by Lemma
5.1
,

t^{N-1}\int_{S^{N-1}}|x-t\theta|^{-N}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{t}\int_{S^{N-1}}|\frac{x}{t}-\theta|^{-N}\,d\theta=\frac{h_{0}(|\frac{x}{t}|)}{t\bigl||\frac{x}{t}|-1\bigr|}=\frac{h_{0}(|\frac{x}{t}|)}{t-|x|}

(5.15)

for
t>|x|
. Since
|x|\to R
and
\ell(\rho(x))\to 0
as
\rho(x)\to 0
, we have

\sup_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!+\!\rho(x)\!\leq\!t\!\leq\!|x|+\ell(\rho(x))$}}\Bigl|h_{0}(|\frac{x}{t}|)-\kappa_{N}\Bigr|\to 0\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)\to 0$},

by Lemma
5.1
and therefore,

\displaystyle I_{1,2}(x)

\displaystyle=(\kappa_{N}+o(1))\int_{|x|+\rho(x)}^{|x|+\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{g(t-R)-g(|x|-R)}{t-|x|}\,dt

\displaystyle=(\kappa_{N}+o(1))\Bigl(\int_{\rho(x)}^{\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{g(t+\rho(x))}{t}\,dt-g(\rho(x))\Bigl(\ln\ell(\rho(x))-\ln\rho(x)\Bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq(\kappa_{N}+o(1))\Bigl(\int_{\rho(x)}^{\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{g(2t)}{t}\,dt-(1+o(1))\Bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\Bigr)^{1-\tau}\Bigr),

where, by (
5.1
),

\int_{\rho(x)}^{\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{g(2t)}{t}\,dt=\int_{\rho(x)}^{\ell(\rho(x))}\frac{1}{t\Bigl(-\ln t-\ln 2\Bigr)^{\tau}}\,dt\\
=-\frac{\bigl(-\ln t-\ln 2\bigr)^{1-\tau}}{1-\tau}\Big|_{\rho(x)}^{\ell(\rho(x))}=\frac{1+o(1)}{1-\tau}\Bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\Bigr)^{1-\tau}

as
\rho(x)\to 0
. Consequently,

I_{1,2}(x)\leq\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{N}\tau}{1-\tau}+o(1)\Bigr)\Bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\Bigr)^{1-\tau}.

(5.16)

To estimate
I_{1,1}(x)
, we use (
5.11
), (
5.15
) and Lemma
5.1
to find that

\displaystyle I_{1,1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!\leq\!|y|\!\leq\!|x|\!+\!\rho(x)$}}\frac{g(|y|-R)-g(|x|-R)}{|x-y|^{N}}dy\leq g^{\prime}(|x|-R)\int_{\text{\tiny$|x|\!\leq\!|y|\!\leq\!|x|\!+\!\rho(x)$}}\frac{|y|-|x|}{|x-y|^{N}}dy

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(\rho(x))\int_{|x|}^{|x|+\rho(x)}(t-|x|)t^{N-1}\int_{S^{N-1}}|x-t\theta|^{-N}\,d\theta dt=g^{\prime}(\rho(x))\int_{|x|}^{|x|+\rho(x)}h_{0}(|\frac{x}{t}|)dt

\displaystyle=(\kappa_{N}+o(1))g^{\prime}(\rho(x))\rho(x)=\frac{\kappa_{N}\tau+o(1)}{\bigl(-\log\rho(x)\bigr)^{\tau+1}}=o(1)\Bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\Bigr)^{1-\tau}\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)\to 0$.}

(5.17)

Finally, we estimate
I_{1,3}(x)
with the help of (
5.1
), (
5.15
) and Lemma
5.1
, finding that

\displaystyle I_{1,3}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{|x|+\ell(\rho(x))}^{|x|+1}t^{N-1}\int_{S^{N-1}}|x-ty|^{-N}dy=\int_{|x|+\ell(\rho(x))}^{|x|+1}\frac{h_{0}(|\frac{x}{t}|)}{t-|x|}dt

\displaystyle\leq 2\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\|h_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\Bigl(-\log\ell(\rho(x))\Bigr)=o(1)\Bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\Bigr)^{1-\tau}\quad\ \text{as \,$\rho(x)\to 0$.}

(5.18)

Combining (
5.16
), (
5.17
) and (
5.18
), we get (
5.14
), and thus the claim follows.
∎

We now complete the proof of Theorem
1.11
, which we restate here for the reader’s convenience.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be Lipschitz domain which satisfies a uniform exterior sphere condition,

f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
u\in{\mathbb{H}}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a weak solution of the Poisson problem

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!u=f\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},\qquad u\equiv 0\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega.$}

(5.19)

Then
u\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and

|u(x)|=O\bigl(\log^{-\tau}\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\bigr)\qquad\text{for every $\tau\in(0,\frac{1}{2})$ as $\rho(x)\to 0$.}

(5.20)

Proof.

Let

f_{1}:=f+{\mathbf{j}}*u-\rho_{N}u\ \in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),

where
{\mathbf{j}}
is defined in (
2.10
). Then (
5.19
) is – in weak sense – equivalent to

Iu=f_{1}\quad\text{in \,$\Omega$},\qquad u\equiv 0\quad\text{in \,$\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega,$}

where
I
is the integral operator associated with the kernel
\mathbf{k}
defined in (
2.9
), which is given by

[Iu](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dy
. Consequently, we have
u\in C_{loc}(\Omega)
by the regularity result
[
14
, Theorem 3]
of Kassmann and Mimica and a straightforward approximation argument. It thus remains to prove the estimate (
5.20
) for fixed
\tau\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Since
\Omega
satisfies a uniform exterior sphere condition, there exists a radius
0<R_{0}<\frac{1}{2}
such that for every point
x_{*}\in\partial\Omega
there exists a ball
B^{x_{*}}
of radius
R_{0}
contained in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
and
tangent to
\partial\Omega
at
x_{*}
. Let
c(x_{*})
denote the center of
B^{x_{*}}
. We now apply Lemma
5.3
with the value
R:=\frac{R_{0}}{2}
. This yields a function
V\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\delta>0
such that the properties (i)-(iv) of Lemma
5.3
are satisfied. By Lemma
5.3
(iv), we may assume that
\delta>0
is chosen sufficiently small so that

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!V(x)\geq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\quad\text{for $x\in A_{R,\delta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\>:R<|x|<R+\delta\}$.}

It now suffices to show that

|u(x)|\leq c\bigl(-\log\rho(x)\bigr)^{-\tau}\qquad\text{for all $x\in\Omega_{\delta}$ with some constant $c>0$},

(5.21)

where
\Omega_{\delta}=\{x\in\Omega:\,\rho(x)<\delta\}
.
For this we consider, for
t\in[0,1]
and
x_{*}\in\partial\Omega
, the ball
B_{R}(z(t,x_{*}))\subset B^{x_{*}}
of radius
R
centered at
z(t,x_{*}):=x_{*}+(t+R)\frac{c(x_{*})-x_{*}}{|c(x_{*})-x_{*}|}
. Moreover, we define the translated functions

V_{t,x_{*}}\in L^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\qquad V_{t,x_{*}}(x)=V(x-z(t,x^{*}))\qquad\text{for $x_{*}\in\partial\Omega$, $t\in[0,1]$.}

For
t\in(0,\delta)
, the intersection
\Omega_{t,x_{*}}
of
\Omega
with the translated annulus
z(t,x_{*})+A_{R,\delta}
is nonempty. Moreover, making
\delta>0
smaller if necessary, we may assume that the measure of
\Omega_{t,x_{*}}
is small enough so that
L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!
satisfies the maximum principle on
\Omega_{t,x_{*}}
(for all
x_{*}\in\partial\Omega
). Moreover, since
\Omega
is bounded, there exists
R_{1}>R
such that

\Omega\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\>:R<|x-z(t,x_{*})|<R_{1}\}\qquad\text{for all $x_{*}\in\partial\Omega$, $t\in(0,\delta)$},

which implies that

\Omega\setminus\Omega_{t,x_{*}}\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\>:R+\delta<|x-z(t,x_{*})|<R_{1}\}\qquad\text{for all $x_{*}\in\partial\Omega$, $t\in(0,\delta)$.}

Hence, since
V
is positive on
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\>:R+\delta<|x|<R_{1}\}
by Lemma
5.3
(i), we may choose
c>1
sufficiently large such that

cV_{t,x_{*}}\geq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\qquad\text{in $\Omega\setminus\Omega_{t,x_{*}}$ for all $x_{*}\in\partial\Omega$, $t\in(0,\delta)$.}

Consequently, we have, in weak sense,

L_{\text{\tiny$\Delta\,$}}\!\bigl(cV_{t,x_{*}}\pm u\bigr)\geq 0\quad\text{in $\Omega_{t,x_{*}}$,}\qquad cV_{t,x_{*}}\pm u\geq 0\quad\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega_{t,x_{*}}$.}

Here we note that
V_{t,x_{*}}\in{\mathcal{V}}(\Omega)
for
x_{*}\in\partial\Omega
,
t\in(0,\delta)
since this function is uniformly Dini continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
(for this we need
t>0
!). Applying the weak maximum principle to the function

cV_{t,x*}\pm u
on the set
\Omega_{t,x_{*}}
, we see that

\pm u\leq cV_{t,x_{*}}\qquad\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}\qquad\text{for every $x_{*}\in\partial\Omega$, $t\in(0,\delta)$.}

With
x_{*}
being fixed, we may then pass to the limit
t\to 0
and deduce that

\pm u\leq cV_{0,x_{*}}\qquad\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}\qquad\text{for every $x_{*}\in\partial\Omega$.}

Next, we let
x\in\Omega
with
\rho(x)<\delta
, and we let
x_{*}\in\partial\Omega
with
\rho(x)=|x-x_{*}|
. It then also follows that

0<\dist(x,B_{R}(z(0,x_{*})))=\rho(x)<\delta

and therefore, by Lemma
5.3
,

x\in\Omega_{0,x*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pm u(x)\leq cV_{0,x_{*}}(x)=c\bigl(-\ln\rho(x)\bigr)^{-\tau}.

Hence (
5.21
) is true, and the proof is finished.
∎

Appendix: A logarithmic boundary Hardy inequality

The aim of this section is to prove the following logarithmic boundary Hardy inequality. It is not used in the present paper but might be of independent interest as it is related to the function spaces introduced in the previous sections, see Remark
4.3
above. Here, as before, we use the notation
\rho(x)=\dist(x,\partial\Omega)
if the ambient domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is fixed.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then there exists a constant
C=C(\Omega)>0
with

\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\,dx\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all $u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$,}

where, as before,

{\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy=\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\!\!\int_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x,y\in\Omega}}{{\text{\tiny$|x-y|\!\leq\!1$}}}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N}}dxdy.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then there exists a constant
C=C(\Omega)>0
with

\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\Omega}(x)u^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all $u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$,}

where
\kappa_{\Omega}
denotes the Killing measure corresponding to the kernel
\mathbf{k}
defined in (
3.5
).

Proof.

This simply follows from Proposition
6.1
by noting that, since
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, we have that

\kappa_{\Omega}(x)\leq 2\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}+C
for
x\in\Omega
with a constant
C>0
by (
3.6
).
∎

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
6.1
. The starting point of the proof is the following variant of Beckner’s logarithmic inequality (
1.8
).

Proposition 6.3
.

We have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Bigl[\psi(\frac{1}{4})-\log(\pi|x_{N}|)\Bigr]u^{2}(x)dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\log|\xi|\,|\widehat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\qquad\text{for Schwarz functions $u\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$.}

Proof.

We write
x=(z,t),\>\xi=(\tau,\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
z,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
,
t,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, and we recall the one-dimensional version of Beckner’s inequality (
1.8
):

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl[\psi(\frac{1}{4})-\log(\pi|t|)\Bigr]u^{2}(t)dt\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(u)(\zeta)|^{2}\log|\zeta|d\zeta\qquad\text{for $u\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})$.}

(6.1)

Here
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
denotes the one-dimensional Fourier transform. Next, let
u\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Then (
6.1
) implies that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Bigl[\psi(\frac{1}{4})-\log(\pi|x_{N}|)\Bigr]u^{2}(x)dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl[\psi(\frac{1}{4})-\log(\pi|t|)\Bigr]u^{2}(z,t)dtdz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(u(z,\cdot))(\zeta)|^{2}\log|\zeta|d\zeta dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log|\zeta|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(u(z,\cdot))(\zeta)|^{2}dzd\zeta,

where, by the Plancherel theorem in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(u(z,\cdot))(\zeta)|^{2}dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}|\hat{u}(\tau,\zeta)|^{2}d\tau\qquad\text{for $\zeta\in\mathbb{R}$.}

Using the fact that
\log
is an increasing function, we conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Bigl[\psi(\frac{1}{4})-\log(\pi|x_{N}|)\Bigr]u^{2}(x)dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log|\zeta|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}|\hat{u}(\tau,\zeta)|^{2}d\tau d\zeta\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\log|\xi|\,|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi.

∎

In the remainder of this section, the latter
C
always stands for a positive constant, and the value of
C
may change in every step.

Corollary 6.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}u^{2}(x)\log\frac{1}{x_{N}}\,dx\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})}^{2}\Bigr)\quad\text{for all $u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})$.}

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})
be nonnegative, and let
v
be the even extension of
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with respect to the reflection
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
at the hyperplane
\{x_{N}=0\}
. By Proposition
6.3
,

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Bigl[\psi(\frac{1}{4})-\log(\pi|x_{N}|)\Bigr]v^{2}(x)dx\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\log|\xi||\widehat{v}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi={\mathcal{E}}_{L}(v,v)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{E}}(v,v)-c_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}v(x)v(y){\mathbf{j}}(x-y)\,dxdy+\rho_{N}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{2}.

We thus infer that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}v^{2}(x)\log\frac{1}{|x_{N}|}dx\leq C\Big({\mathcal{E}}(v,v)+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Bigr).

Moreover,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(v,v)

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{b}}(v,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})+{\mathfrak{b}}(v,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{-})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}(v(x)-v(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)\,dxdy

\displaystyle=2{\mathfrak{b}}(u,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-\sigma(y))\,dxdy\leq 4{\mathfrak{b}}(u,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}).

Here we used in the last step that
\mathbf{k}(x-\sigma(y))\leq\mathbf{k}(x-y)
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
. We thus conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}u^{2}(x)\log\frac{1}{|x_{N}|}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}v^{2}(x)\log\frac{1}{|x_{N}|}dx

\displaystyle\leq C\Big({\mathcal{E}}(v,v)+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})}\Bigr),

as claimed.
∎

In the following, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\phi\in C^{0,1}(\Omega)
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(\phi)>0
with

{\mathfrak{b}}(\phi u,\Omega)\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all $u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$.}

Proof.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. Since

\bigl([\phi u](x)-[\phi u](y)\bigr)^{2}\leq 2\Bigl(\|\phi\|_{C^{0,1}(\Omega)}u^{2}(x)(x-y)^{2}+\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\bigl(u(x)-u(y)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)

for
x,y\in\Omega
, we have

{\mathfrak{b}}(\phi u,\Omega)\leq 2\Bigl(\|\phi\|_{C^{0,1}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|z|^{2}\mathbf{k}(z)dzdx+\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}{\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)\Bigr)\leq C\Bigl(\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+{\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)\Bigr).

The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then there exist
r>0
and
C>0
such that for any
z\in\partial\Omega
,

\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\,\phi^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(\phi,\Omega)+\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\quad\text{for all $\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega)$.}

Proof.

Let

Q:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\>:\>|x|<1\},\qquad Q_{+}:=Q\cap\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{0}:=\{x\in Q\>:\>x_{N}=0\}.

Since
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, there exists
r>0
such that for fixed
z\in\partial\Omega
there exists a bilipschitz map
T:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with

T(z)=0,\ \ T(B_{2r}(z)\cap\partial\Omega)=Q_{0}\ \ {\rm and}\ \ T(B_{2r}(z)\cap\Omega)=Q_{+}.

For simplicity, let
B:=B_{r}(z)
and
\tilde{B}:=B_{2r}(z)
in the following. Since
\rho(x)=\dist(x,\partial\Omega)=\dist(x,\partial\Omega\cap B)
for
x\in\tilde{B}
, we have

(Tx)_{N}\leq C\rho(x)\quad\text{and therefore}\quad\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\leq\log\frac{1}{(Tx)_{N}}+\log C\qquad\text{for $x\in\tilde{B}\cap\Omega$.}

(6.2)

Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B\cap\Omega)
, and put
\psi:=\phi\circ T^{-1}\in C^{1,0}_{c}(Q_{+})
. Then (
6.2
) and Corollary
6.4
imply that

\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\phi^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\int_{Q_{+}}\Bigl(\log\frac{1}{y_{N}}+1\Bigr)\psi^{2}(y)\,dy\leq C\Bigl(\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(Q_{+})}^{2}+{\mathfrak{b}}(\psi,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})\Bigr).

(6.3)

Moreover, since
\supp\psi\subset T^{-1}(\tilde{B})\subset\subset Q_{+}
, we have

{\mathfrak{b}}(\psi,\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(\psi,Q_{+})+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(Q_{+})}^{2}\Bigr).

(6.4)

Next we note that
\mathbf{k}(x-y)\leq C\bigl(\mathbf{k}(T^{-1}x-T^{-1}y)+1\bigr)
for
x,y\in Q_{+}
and therefore

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{b}}(\psi,Q_{+})=\int_{Q_{+}}\int_{Q_{+}}(\psi(x)-\psi(y))^{2}\mathbf{k}(x-y)dxdy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(\phi(x)-\phi(y))^{2}\Bigl(\mathbf{k}(x-y)+1\Bigr)dxdy

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(\phi,\Omega)+\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr).

(6.5)

The claim now follows by combining (
6.3
), (
6.4
) and (
6.5
).
∎

We may now complete the

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Let
r>0
be chosen as in Lemma
6.6
. Since
\partial\Omega
is compact, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
such that
\partial\Omega\subset\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}
with
U_{j}:=\tilde{B}_{r}(x_{j})
. Setting
U_{0}:=\Omega
gives rise to an open covering
\overline{\Omega}\subset\bigcup\limits_{j=0}^{n}U_{j}
. We consider a subordinated partition of unity given by functions
\phi_{0},\dots,\phi_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, i.e.,

0\leq\phi_{j}\leq 1,\quad\supp\phi_{j}\subset U_{j}\qquad\text{for $j=0,\dots,n$}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{$\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}\phi_{j}\equiv 1$ on $\overline{\Omega}$.}

Now let
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. By Lemma
6.5
and Lemma
6.6
,

\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}(u\phi_{j})^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u\phi_{j},\Omega)+\|u\phi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\quad\text{for $j=1,\dots,n$.}

Moreover, since
\supp\phi_{0}\subset\subset\Omega
, we have that

\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}(u\phi_{0})^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\|u\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Combining these inequalities, we conclude that

\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}u^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{\rho(x)}(\phi_{j}u)^{2}(x)\,dx\leq C\Bigl({\mathfrak{b}}(u,\Omega)+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr),

as claimed.
∎
