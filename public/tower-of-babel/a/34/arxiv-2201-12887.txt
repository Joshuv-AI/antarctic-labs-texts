On Graphs of Sets of Reduced Words
Jennifer Elder

Abstract
. Any permutation in the finite symmetric group can be written as a product of simple transpositions s i = ( i ​ i + 1 ) s_{i}=(i~i+1) . For a fixed permutation σ ∈ 𝔖 n \sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} the products of minimal length are called reduced decompositions or reduced words, and the collection of all such reduced words is denoted ℛ ⁡ ( σ ) \mathcal{R}(\sigma) . Any reduced word of σ \sigma can be transformed into any other by a sequence of commutation moves or long braid moves. One area of interest in these sets are the congruence classes defined by using only braid or only commutation relations. The set ℛ ⁡ ( σ ) \mathcal{R}(\sigma) can be drawn as a graph, G ⁡ ( σ ) G(\sigma) , where the vertices are the reduced words, and the edges denote the presence of a commutation or braid move between the words. This paper presents new work on subgraph structures in G ⁡ ( σ ) G(\sigma) , as well as new formulas to count the number of braid edges and commutation edges in G ⁡ ( σ ) G(\sigma) . We also include work on bounds for the number of braid and commutation classes in ℛ ⁡ ( σ ) \mathcal{R}(\sigma) .

2.1.
The weak order

The generating relations for
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
can also be used to produce the lattice for the weak order poset of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Definition 2.3
.

The
right weak order poset
is a partial order defined on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, denoted
W(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
.
W(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
is a bounded lattice for all
n\geq 2
. The minimal element in this lattice is the identity, while the maximal element is
w_{0}=[n~n-1~\ldots 2~1]
.

The cover relations are defined on the addition or removal of a single simple transposition on the right hand side. That is,
\tau\lessdot\sigma
if and only if
\tau s_{i}=\sigma
for some
i\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
. Then
\ell(\tau)=\ell(\sigma)-1
.

In general, if
\tau\leq\sigma
, then there exists a collection of simple transpositions such that
\tau s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{k}}=\sigma
, where
\ell(\tau)=\ell(\sigma)-k
.

For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the set
\text{Des}(\sigma)
gives us all the elements that
\sigma
covers in
W(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
. That is, the set of all elements covered by a permutation
\sigma
is
\{\sigma s_{i}\mid i\in\text{Des}(\sigma)\}
.

Much work has been done on subposet structures in this lattice. For example, the number of 4-cycles in
W(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
is known (OEIS
A317487
). In Figure
2
, we have the weak order of
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
, with one such 4-cycle highlighted.

\mathfrak{S}_{4}
.

Similarly, the number of 6-cycles in
W(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
is well known (OEIS
A317486
). These results are unsurprising, as a 4-cycle is produced whenever
i,j\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
are such that
|i-j|>
, and a 6-cycle is produced whenever

.

In
[

2.2.
Sets of reduced words

The following recursions are well known results for sets of reduced words and braid classes.

Theorem 2.5
.

For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}

|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|=\sum_{i\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{i})|.

For

|\mathcal{B}(\sigma)|=\left(\sum_{i\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}|\mathcal{B}(\sigma s_{i})|\right)-\sum_{i,i+1\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}|\mathcal{B}(\sigma s_{i}s_{i+1}s_{i})|.

Additional work has been done by Stanley

2.3.
Graphs of sets of reduced words

First, we give the formal definition of the graph
G(\sigma)
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We produce an undirected graph whose vertex set is
\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
. If two words are associated by commutation moves, we give a solid edge in the graph, and if they are associated by braid moves, we give a dashed edge. We call this graph
G(\sigma)
.

These edges give us straight forward methods of seeing the commutation and braid classes in the set
\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
for some
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We say that
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are in the same commutation class if we can perform a series of commutation moves on
r_{1}
to produce
r_{2}
, and vice versa. We denote the collection of commutation classes of
\sigma
as
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
. Similarly, we say that
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are in the same braid class if we can perform a series of braid moves on
r_{1}
to produce
r_{2}
, and vice versa. We denote the collection of braid classes of
\sigma
as
\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
.

We are also interested in graphs constructed from these congruence classes, so we need some notation for those as well.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and consider
G(\sigma)
.

(1)

The graph
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma)
is defined on the vertex set
V(G(\sigma))
and the edge set, denoted
E_{\mathcal{B}}
, is formed from the braid edges of
G(\sigma)
.

(2)

The graph
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{C}}(\sigma)
is defined on the vertex set
V(G(\sigma))
and the edge set, denoted
E_{\mathcal{C}}
, is formed from the commutation edges of
G(\sigma)
.

Note that these are not the same as the traditional congruence class graphs, where the vertices are the classes, which are formed from graph theoretic contraction and deletion procedures.

The following result from Reiner gives a relationship between the number of braid edges in
G(\sigma)
and the size of the set
\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
8
]
, Theorem 1)
.

Let
d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)
be the number of braid edges incident to the vertex
v
in
G(\sigma)
. For
\sigma=w_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,

\sum_{v\in G(\sigma)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)=|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|.

Theorem
2.12
was used by Tenner in
[
15
]
to find the average number of commutation edges incident to any vertex in
G(w_{0})
. And Schilling et al.
[
11
]
used this result to find the average number of braid edges that are incident to the connected components in
G(w_{0})
once the braid edges are deleted. We use this result to provide bounds on the number of braid edges in
G(\sigma)
for more general classes of permutations.

G(\tau)

G(\sigma)
. If there is a commutation edge from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
in
G(\tau)
, then there is a commutation edge from

to
t_{2}i
in

. The same applies for braid edges in

. Thus we can map edges from

into

.

Additionally, we can look at

as a subset of
V(G(\sigma))
, and consider an edge in

incident to two vertices contained in

. This edge must already be contained in

, because any commutation or braid move is happening between words in

, since these vertices are of the form

and
\tau_{2}s_{i}
.

Therefore,
G(\tau)
is an induced subgraph of

.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
n\geq 2
such that
\tau\leq\sigma
in the weak order lattice. Then,

(1)

G(\tau)
is an induced subgraph of
G(\sigma)
,

(2)

G(\tau)
is an induced subgraph of
G(w_{0})
.

Proof.

Consider the chain in the weak order lattice:

\tau\lessdot\tau_{1}\lessdot\tau_{2}\lessdot\ldots\lessdot\tau_{k}\lessdot\sigma,

where each element in the chain covers the previous element. We know such a chain exists because
\tau\leq\sigma
. Note that every element in
W(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
is comparable to
w_{0}
, so for any
\tau
, we could set
\sigma=w_{0}
.

From the previous proposition, we can construct the following chain of induced subgraphs:

G(\tau)\leq G(\tau_{1})\leq G(\tau_{2})\leq\ldots\leq G(\tau_{k})\leq G(\sigma).

So we have that
G(\tau)\leq G(\sigma)
, as desired.
∎

We wish to combine our previous results into a method of partitioning the vertex set of

Theorem 4.4

As noted in Theorem

Corollary 4.9
.

Let

This is a brand new way to count the number of braid edges in a graph of a set of reduced words. Now we have methods of breaking

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,

such that
\tau\lessdot\sigma
in the weak order lattice. Suppose that
\tau s_{i}=\sigma
, where

.

(1)

If

, then there are
\sum_{j\in I_{i}}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{i}s_{j})|

G(\tau)
that are not contained in the induced subgraph.

(2)

If

G(\tau)
are the commutation edges that are incident to two vertices from
G(\tau)
.

Proof.

Consider
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\tau\lessdot\sigma
. Suppose that

are such that

, and
\sigma s_{i}=\tau
.

Since
i,j\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
, and letters
i
and
j
commute with each other, there exist reduced decompositions of
\sigma
such that
\sigma=\alpha s_{i}s_{j}
and
\sigma=\alpha s_{j}s_{i}
. We want to be able to count the number of reduced words of
\sigma
that have a commutation move in those last two positions that use the letters
i
and
j
. This means we really need to count the number of ways we can write
\alpha
. There are
|\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{i}s_{j})|
ways to write
\alpha
.

We note that all vertices that end in the letters
j~i
are contained in the subgraph
G(\sigma s_{i})
, where
\tau=\sigma s_{i}
. All vertices that end in the letters

are contained in the subgraph
G(\sigma s_{j})
. Then the number of commutation edges between these subgraphs are equal to
|\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{i}s_{j})|

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Suppose that
\text{Des}(\sigma)
contains a string of
m
consecutive elements, with smallest element in the string
i
:
\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+m-1\}
. Then there exists a
u\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
u\leq\sigma
, and
u^{-1}\sigma=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, for
k=m+1
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=[a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n}]\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We recall that
i\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
if and only if
a_{i}>a_{i+1}
. We also recall that for any
j
,
\sigma s_{j}=[a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{j+1}a_{j}\ldots a_{n}]
.

For any element
i\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
, we recall that
s_{i}=w_{0}^{1,i}
. Suppose that
\{i,i+1,\ldots i+m-1\}\subset\text{Des}(\sigma)
is a string of consecutive elements where
m\geq 2
. This means that

a_{i}>a_{i+1}>\ldots>a_{i+m-1}.

Let
\tau_{\sigma}^{(j,k)}
be defined as follows:

\tau_{\sigma}^{(j,k)}:=\sigma s_{j}s_{j+1}\ldots s_{j+k-1}=[a_{1}\ldots a_{j-1}a_{j+1}\ldots a_{j+k}a_{j}\ldots a_{n}],

where
\tau_{\sigma}^{(0,0)}=\sigma
, and
\tau_{\sigma}^{(j,1)}=\sigma s_{j}
.

We note that for the set
\{i,i+1,\ldots i+m-1\}\subset\text{Des}(\sigma)
, if
j=i
and
k=m
, we have

\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,m)}=\sigma s_{i}s_{i+1}\ldots s_{i+m-1}=[a_{1}\ldots a_{i-1}a_{i+1}a_{i+2}\ldots a_{i+m}a_{i}\ldots a_{n}].

We see that
\{i,i+1,\ldots i+m-2\}\subset\text{Des}(\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,m)})
, while
i+m-1\notin\text{Des}(\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,m)})
.

Furthermore,
\ell(\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,m)})=\ell(\sigma)-m
. Since
i\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
,
\ell(\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,1)})=\ell(\sigma)-1
. We still have
i+1\in\text{Des}(\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,1)})
, and we can see that
\ell(\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,2)})=\ell(\sigma)-2
. Inductively, this process continues until we have
\ell(\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,m)})=\ell(\sigma)-m
.

Let
\sigma^{(1)}=\tau_{\sigma}^{(i,m)}
. We now consider

\tau_{\sigma^{(1)}}^{(i,m-1)}=\sigma^{(1)}s_{i}s_{i+1}\ldots s_{i+m-2}=[a_{1}\ldots a_{i-1}a_{i+2}\ldots a_{i+m}a_{i+1}a_{i}\ldots a_{n}].

We note that

i+m-1,i+m-2\notin\text{Des}(\tau_{\sigma^{(1)}}^{(i,m-1)})\mbox{ and }\{i,i+1,\ldots i+m-3\}\subset\text{Des}(\tau_{\sigma^{(1)}}^{(i,m-1)}).

We also have
\ell(\tau_{\sigma^{(1)}}^{(i,m-1)})=\ell(\sigma^{(1)})-(m-1)=\ell(\sigma)-m-(m-1)
using the same argument on descents as before. Let
\sigma^{(2)}=\tau_{\sigma^{(1)}}^{(i,m-1)}
.

Inductively, for
1\leq x\leq m-3
, we have that the permutation
\sigma^{(x+1)}=\tau_{\sigma^{(x)}}^{(i,m-x)}
is such that

\sigma^{(x+1)}=\sigma^{(x)}s_{i}s_{i+1}\ldots s_{i+m-(x+1)}=[a_{1}\ldots a_{i-1}a_{i+x}\ldots a_{i+m}a_{i+x-1}\ldots a_{i+1}a_{i}\ldots a_{n}],

where
\{i,\ldots,i+m-(x+2)\}\subset\text{Des}(\sigma^{(x+1)})
,
\{i+m-(x+1),\ldots,i+m-1\}\cap\text{Des}(\sigma)=\emptyset
, and
\ell(\sigma^{(x+1)})=\ell(\sigma)-\sum_{b=0}^{m-x}m-b
.

Now consider
\sigma^{(m-2)}=\tau_{\sigma^{(m-3)}}^{(i,m-(m-3))}
:

\sigma^{(m-2)}=[a_{1}\ldots a_{i-1}a_{i+m-2}a_{i+m-1}a_{i+m}a_{i+m-3}\ldots a_{i+1}a_{i}\ldots a_{n}].

From the above one line notation, we see that
\sigma^{(m)}:=\sigma^{(m-2)}s_{i}s_{i+1}s_{i}
such that
\{i,i+1,\ldots i+m-2\}\cap\text{Des}(\sigma^{(m)})=\emptyset
. Additionally,

\ell(\sigma^{(m)})=\left(\ell(\sigma)-\sum_{b=0}^{m-3}m-b\right)-3=\ell(\sigma)-\binom{m+1}{2}.

Let
u\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{(m)})
. Note that if
\sigma^{(m)}
is the identity, then
u
is simply the empty word. In either case, by tracing our products from
\sigma^{(m)}
back to
\sigma^{(1)}
, let
v
be the word

i(i+1)i~(i+2)(i+1)i\ldots(i+k-1)(i+k-2)\ldots(i+1)i.

We see that
\ell(v)=\binom{m+1}{2}
, and that we can verify that
v\in\mathcal{R}(w_{0}^{(m+1,i)})
.
By construction, we have
uv\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
as desired.
∎

This result leads us to the following, more general theorem.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Suppose that
\text{Des}(\sigma)
can be partitioned into maximal blocks of consecutive entries,
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots B_{m}
. Let
\min(B_{j})=b_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq m
. Then

\sigma=\tau\prod_{i=1}^{m}w_{0}^{(|b_{j}|+1,b_{j})}

for some
\tau\leq\sigma
.

Proof.

For each block of consecutive descents, we apply Lemma
5.2
to produce

\sigma=\tau_{1}w_{0}^{(|b_{1}|+1,b_{1})}.

We apply Lemma
5.2
again to arrive at

\sigma=\tau_{2}w_{0}^{(|b_{2}|+1,b_{2})}w_{0}^{(|b_{1}|+1,b_{1})}.

We note that as the descent sets are disjoint, then

w_{0}^{(|b_{2}|+1,b_{2})}w_{0}^{(|b_{1}|+1,b_{1})}=w_{0}^{(|b_{1}|+1,b_{1})}w_{0}^{(|b_{2}|+1,b_{2})}

so the order does not matter.

We repeat this process inductively until we have worked our way down the lattice to
\tau
, and the product

\sigma=\tau\prod_{i=1}^{m}w_{0}^{(|b_{j}|+1,b_{j})}

as desired.
∎

Using Theorem
5.3
, we have the following classification of the shortest permutation with a fixed descent set.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Suppose that
\text{Des}(\sigma)
can be partitioned into maximal blocks of consecutive entries,
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots B_{m}
. Then

\sigma^{\prime}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}w_{0}^{(|b_{j}|+1,b_{j})}

is the permutation with minimal length such that
\text{Des}(\sigma^{\prime})=\text{Des}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

From Theorem
5.3
, we note that for
\text{Des}(\sigma)
, we must have

\prod_{i=1}^{m}w_{0}^{(|b_{j}|+1,b_{j})}

as part of a reduced decomposition of
\sigma
. Then
\tau=e
produces the permutation with minimal length with this descent set.
∎

Using ideas from the previous section, we now discuss how to consider subgraphs in

that may not be of the form

,

.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be such that
[i,i+k-2]\subset\text{Des}(\sigma)
is a set of consecutive elements. From Lemma
5.2
, we note that there are permutations
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\beta=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, and
u=u_{1}u_{2}\ldots u_{l(\alpha)}\in\mathcal{R}(\alpha)
and
v=v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{l(\beta)}\in\mathcal{R}(w_{0}^{(k,i)})
can be concatenated to produce
uv\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
. That is,
\sigma=\alpha\beta
. In order to distinguish the letters, we color the letters of
u
blue, and the letters of
v
red.

A
shuffle
of the letters of

Much like the other research done by Tenner

Theorem 7.3

For

|\mathcal{B}(w_{0})|\geq\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|-1

Proof.

We consider the graph

, as defined in Definition

Suppose to the contrary that there are less than
\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|-1

|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|
is even, we have at most
\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|-2

|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|
is odd, then there are at most
\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|-\dfrac{3}{2}

We can partition our vertex set over the connected components. Suppose there are
m
connected components, and let
V_{i}
be the vertex set for one of these components, for

V(G_{b}^{\prime}(w_{0}))=\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq m}V_{i}.

We note that a graph with

edges. We assume there are

We also see that each of the connected components in

are at minimum tree graphs, which means we have at least

\sum_{v\in V(G(w_{0}))}d_{G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{B}}(w_{0})}(v)\geq 2|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|-2.

Thus, for

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G(w_{0}))}d_{G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{B}}(w_{0})}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2E(G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|-2-2\left[\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|-i\right]

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|,

for either choice of

This contradicts Reiner’s result in Theorem

that states that the sum of the braid degrees exactly equals the size of the set of reduced words.

for
w_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, the result is true for more than one permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
when
n>3
.

7.2.
Bounds on the number of braid classes for certain families of permutations

We now use our results from Sections
4
and
5
to prove results similar to Theorem
7.3
for more general families of permutations.

In the following lemma, we refer to certain
\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as being
completely commutative
permutations. In this case we do not mean fully commutative, we simply mean that a reduced decomposition of
\alpha
is formed from commuting generators. For example,
\sigma=s_{1}s_{3}s_{5}
would be completely commutative, while
\tau=s_{4}s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}
would be fully commutative.

Lemma 7.4
.

Suppose that
\text{Des}(\sigma)=[i,i+k-1]\cup J
, where
k
is the length of the longest string of consecutive elements in
\text{Des}(\sigma)
. Then

(1)

If
k=1
,

A(\sigma):=|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|-\left(\sum_{v\in G(\sigma)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)\right)>0.

(2)

If
J=\{j\}
and
\sigma=s_{j}w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, then
A(\sigma)>0
.

(3)

If
J\neq\emptyset
,
\alpha
is completely commutative with
\text{Des}(\alpha)=J
, and
\sigma=\alpha w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, then
A(\sigma)>0
.

(4)

If
J\neq\emptyset
,
\alpha
is fully commutative with
\text{Des}(\alpha)=J
, every
s_{i}
in the support of
\alpha
commutes with all
s_{i}
’s in the support of
w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, and
\sigma=\alpha w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, then
A(\sigma)>0
.

(5)

If
J=\emptyset
and
\sigma=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, then
A(\sigma)=0
.

Proof.

First, we note that point five above is exactly Theorem
2.12
. Thus we focus on proving the other three parts of our lemma.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
where
l(\sigma)=l
and
\text{Des}(\sigma)=[i,i+k-1]\cup J
for some
k,i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that no string of consecutive elements in
J
has length greater than
k
.

Suppose that for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
where
l(\tau)<l
, exactly one of the following is true:

(1)

\tau=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, and
A(\tau)=0
,

(2)

\tau\neq w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, and
A(\tau)>0
.

We want to show that
A(\sigma)>0
as well.

Case 1:
Let
k=1
. That is, suppose that
\text{Des}(\sigma)
does not contain any consecutive elements. Then we select any
j\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
, and consider the word
aj\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
where
a\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{j})
.

If
\text{Des}(\sigma)=\{j\}
, we note that
|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|=|\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{j})|
. Then
A(\sigma)=A(\sigma s_{j})>0
by our induction hypothesis.

If
|\text{Des}(\sigma)|>1
, Proposition
3.3
notes that at most one
j\in\text{Des}(\sigma)
produces
\sigma s_{j}=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
. Using Corollary
4.9
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in G(\sigma)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}\sum_{u\in G(\sigma s_{x})}d_{\mathcal{B}}(u)\right)+2\cdot\sum_{x,x+1\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{x}s_{x+1}s_{x})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}\sum_{u\in G(\sigma s_{x})}d_{\mathcal{B}}(u),

because there are no consecutive elements in the set
\text{Des}(\sigma)
.

We assume that
A(\sigma s_{x})=0
for at most one descent
x
, and
A(\sigma s_{y})>0
for all other descents
y
. There are at least two elements in
\text{Des}(\sigma)
, and therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in G(\sigma)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}\sum_{u\in G(\sigma s_{x})}d_{\mathcal{B}}(u)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\text{Des}(\sigma)}\left(|\mathcal{R}(\sigma s_{x})|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|,

and therefore,
A(\sigma)>0
.

Case 2:
Let
\ell(\alpha)=1
. Suppose that
\alpha=s_{j}
,
\beta=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, so that
jv\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
for
v\in\mathcal{R}(w_{0}^{(k,i)})
. Because
\text{Des}(\sigma)=[i,i+k-1]\cup\{j\}
, we know that
j\notin\{i-1,i+k\}
. Then
j
commutes with every letter in
u\in\mathcal{R}(\beta)
, so we can split up
G(\sigma)
into subgraphs
H_{t}
as defined in Definition
6.7
, where
1\leq t\leq\ell(\beta)+1
.

Since
j
commutes with everything in
u\in\mathcal{R}(\beta)
, we know that
j
cannot be used in a braid move, and that
|V(H_{t})|=|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|
. Thus each of the
H_{t}
’s is joined to another
H_{t-1}
and
H_{t+1}
by commutation edges.

In any three consecutive positions in
\beta
, there are
\sum_{m,m+1\in\text{Des}(\beta)}|\mathcal{R}(\beta s_{m}s_{m-1}s_{m})|
braid moves, corresponding to braid edges. Since
\beta
is a
w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, using notation from Definition
2.11
, we also know that

|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|=2\cdot|E_{\mathcal{B}}(\beta)|=\sum_{v\in G(\beta)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v),

which means that

|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|=2\cdot\left((l(\beta)-2)\sum_{m,m+1\in\text{Des}(\beta)}|\mathcal{R}(\beta s_{m}s_{m-1}s_{m})|\right)=\sum_{v\in G(\beta)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v).

For
t=1,l(\beta)+1
, the subgraph
H_{t}
has exactly
|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|
vertices, and
\sum_{v\in H_{t}}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)=|V(H_{t})|
.

For
t=2,l(\beta)
, the subgraph
H_{t}
has exactly
|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|
vertices, but

\sum_{v\in H_{t}}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)=2\cdot\left((l(\beta)-3)\sum_{m,m+1\in\text{Des}(\beta)}|\mathcal{R}(\beta s_{m}s_{m-1}s_{m})|\right)<|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|.

For all other
3\leq t\leq l(\beta)-1
, the subgraph
H_{t}
has exactly
|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|
vertices, but

\sum_{v\in H_{t}}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)=2\cdot\left((l(\beta)-4)\sum_{m,m+1\in\text{Des}(\beta)}|\mathcal{R}(\beta s_{m}s_{m-1}s_{m})|\right)<|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|.

Thus

\sum_{v\in G(\sigma)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)<|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|,

so that
A(\sigma)>0
as desired.

Case 3 and 4:
Let
\ell(\alpha)=m
. Let
\alpha=s_{a_{1}}\ldots s_{a_{m}}
,
\beta=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
, so that
a_{1}\ldots a_{m}v\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
for
v\in\mathcal{R}(w_{0}^{(k,i)})
. Because
\text{Des}(\sigma)=[i,i+k-1]\cup J
, we know that
a_{j}\notin\{i-1,i+k\}
for any
1\leq j\leq m
. Then
a_{j}
commutes with every letter in
u\in\mathcal{R}(\beta)
, so we can split up
G(\sigma)
into subgraphs
H_{t}
as defined in Definition
6.7
, where
1\leq t\leq\binom{\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\beta)}{\ell(\alpha)}
.

Since each
a_{j}
commutes with everything in
u\in\mathcal{R}(\beta)
, and
\alpha
is fully commutative, we know that each
a_{j}
cannot be used in a braid move, and that
|V(H_{t})|=|\mathcal{R}(\alpha)|\cdot|\mathcal{R}(\beta)|
. Thus each of the
H_{t}
’s is joined to another
H_{t-1}
and
H_{t+1}
by commutation edges. Additionally, each of the graphs
H_{t}
are a subgraph of
G(\alpha)\times G(\beta)
, where
V(H_{t})=V(G(\alpha)\times G(\beta))
, and
E(H_{t})\subset E(G(\alpha)\times G(\beta))
. We also know from
6.11
that the only braid edges are in the copies of
G(\beta)
.

Using the same argument as Case 2, we have the following facts about our subgraphs
H_{t}
:

For any subgraph
H_{t}
where the letters of
\beta
sit in consecutive positions,
H_{t}
has
|\mathcal{R}(\alpha)||\mathcal{R}(\beta)|
vertices, and
\sum_{v\in H_{t}}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)=|V(H_{t})|
.

For any subgraph
H_{t}
where at least two letters of
\beta
are separated by at least one letter of
\alpha
,
H_{t}
still has exactly
|\mathcal{R}(\alpha)||\mathcal{R}(\beta)|
vertices, but
\sum_{v\in H_{t}}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)<|\mathcal{R}(\alpha)||\mathcal{R}(\beta)|

Thus

\sum_{v\in G(\sigma)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)<|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|,

so that
A(\sigma)>0
as desired.
∎

Note that the cases we have proven in Lemma
7.4
relied heavily on the work we did in Sections
4
and
5
. This Lemma also covers all the cases we have been able to prove.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be one of the permutations covered by Lemma
7.4
. Then

|\mathcal{B}(\sigma)|\geq\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|-1\quad\mbox{ and }\quad|\mathcal{C}(\sigma)|\leq\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|+2.

Proof.

We can follow all the steps of the proof of Lemma
7.3
, replacing
w_{0}
with
\sigma
. The proof is dependent on arriving at

\sum_{v\in G(w_{0})}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)>|\mathcal{R}(w_{0})|,

which is a contradiction for
w_{0}
. If
\sigma
is one of the permutations in Lemma
7.4
, then
A(\sigma)>0
, and this is also a contradiction for
\sigma
.

Thus
|\mathcal{B}(\sigma)|\geq\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|-1
. By Theorem
2.8
,

|\mathcal{B}(\sigma)|+|\mathcal{C}(\sigma)|-1\leq|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|.

We can now rewrite this as

\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|-1+|\mathcal{C}(\sigma)|-1\leq|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|,

which means that

|\mathcal{C}(\sigma)|\leq\dfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|+2,

as desired.
∎

While not complete for arbitrary permutations, this theorem represents all current knowledge of the relationship between
|\mathcal{B}(\sigma)|
and
|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|
. We used all of our new tools related to subgraphs to arrive at this result, and believe that additional work on our new families of subgraphs are required to prove any more cases.

8.
Future Work

Finally, we detail some future directions of study for the work done in this paper.

(1)

There are two possible results that would prove Theorem
7.5
for all permutations:

Conjecture 8.1
.

For any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,

A(\sigma)=|\mathcal{R}(\sigma)|-\left(\sum_{v\in G(\sigma)}d_{\mathcal{B}}(v)\right)\geq 0.

Conjecture 8.2
.

For all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, when
\sigma=w_{0}^{(k,i)}
for some
k,i\in\mathbb{N}
, then
G(\sigma)
has the highest proportion of braid edges to vertices in any graph of
\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

We have spent considerable time attempting to prove these conjectures, but as of the writing of this paper, the cases present in Lemma
7.4
represents all of our progress on Theorem
7.5
.

(2)

Theorem
6.5
allows us to break permutations into more parts than we consider in Lemma
7.4
. That is, more parts that are equivalent to
w_{0}^{(k,i)}
’s. Can we add cases to Lemma
7.4
using a more general division of
\sigma
?

(3)

If Theorem
7.5
can be proven for all permutations, we still have Conjectures
7.1
and
7.2
, which we believe are the true limits of these ratios. Are there other methods of proving these conjectures without our work on subgraphs?

(4)

Since these families of subgraphs have not been studied, we intend to continue to investigate what they can tell us about
\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
apart from congruence classes and number of edges in
G(\sigma)
.

(5)

As noted in Section
6
, 12 and 21 inflations have been well studied. Since shuffles are a more general method of looking at permutations, we continue to study what they can tell us about
\mathcal{R}(\sigma)
and
G(\sigma)
.
