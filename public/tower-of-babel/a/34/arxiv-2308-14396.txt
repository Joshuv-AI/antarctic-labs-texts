New Hindman spaces
Rafał Filipów, Krzysztof Kowitz, Adam Kwela, Jacek Tryba

Abstract
. We introduce a method that allows to turn topological questions about Hindman spaces into purely combinatorial questions about the Katětov order of ideals on ℕ \mathbb{N} . We also provide two applications of the method. (1) We characterize F σ F_{\sigma} ideals ℐ \mathcal{I} for which there is a Hindman space which is not an ℐ \mathcal{I} -space under the continuum hypothesis. This reduces a topological question of Albin L. Jones about consistency of existence of a Hindman space which is not van der Waerden to the question whether the ideal of all non AP-sets is not below the ideal of all non IP-sets in the Katětov order. (2) Under the continuum hypothesis, we construct a Hindman space which is not an ℐ 1 / n \mathcal{I}_{1/n} -space. This answers a question posed by Jana Flašková at the 22nd Summer Conference on Topology and its Applications.

In our paper we consider topological spaces which satisfy some properties connected with well known combinatorial theorems, in particular van der Waerden’s arithmetical progressions theorem and Hindman’s finite sums theorem. Research into this topic was initiated by Kojman and later continued by Shelah and others in

,

.

A topological space
X
is called
van der Waerden

[
16
]
if for every sequence

\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
there exists a convergent subsequence

\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in A}
with
A
being an AP-set (i.e.
A
contains arithmetic progressions of arbitrary finite length).

A nonempty family
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
of subsets of
\mathbb{N}
is an
ideal on
\mathbb{N}
if it is closed under taking subsets and finite unions of its elements,
\mathbb{N}\notin\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}
contains all finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
. It is easy to see that the family
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}=\{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}:\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n+1}<\infty\}

is an ideal on
\mathbb{N}
, and it follows from van der Waerden’s theorem
[
20
]
that the family

\mathcal{W}=\{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}:\text{$A$ is not an AP-set}\}
is an ideal on
\mathbb{N}
. An ideal on
\mathbb{N}
is called
F_{\sigma}
if it is an
F_{\sigma}
subset of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
with a topology induced from the Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
(recall: a set is
F_{\sigma}
if it is a countable union of closed sets).
It is not difficult to check that both
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
and
\mathcal{W}
are
F_{\sigma}
.

A topological space
X
is called an
\mathcal{I}
-space

[
8
]
if for every sequence

\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
there exists a converging subsequence

\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in A}
with
A\notin\mathcal{I}
.
In particular, van der Waerden spaces coincide with
\mathcal{W}
-spaces.

A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is an
IP-set
if there exists an infinite set
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\FS(D)\subseteq A
where
\FS(D)
denotes the set of all finite non-empty sums of distinct elements of
D
.
The family
\mathcal{H}=\{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}:\text{$A$ is not an IP-set}\}
is an ideal on
\mathbb{N}
(it follows from Hindman’s theorem
[
11
]
).

In
[
15
]
, Kojman proved that only finite spaces are
\mathcal{H}
-spaces, and to obtain a meaningful topological counterpart of Hindman’s finite sums theorem he utilized the
notion of IP-convergence introduced by Furstenberg and Weiss in
[
9
]
.

An
IP-sequence
in
X
is a sequence indexed by
\FS(D)
for some infinite
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.
An IP-sequence
\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\FS(D)}
in a topological space
X

IP-converges

[
9
]
to a point
x\in X
if for every neighborhood
U
of the point
x
there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\{x_{n}:n\in\FS(D\setminus\{0,1,\dots,m\})\}\subseteq U
(point
x
is called the
IP-limit
of the sequence).

A topological space
X
is called
Hindman

[
15
]
if for every sequence

\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
there exists an infinite set
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that the subsequence
\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\FS(D)}
IP-converges to some
x\in X
.

In
[
14
]
, Katětov introduced the following order to study convergence
in topological spaces.
For ideals
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}
we say that
\mathcal{I}
is below
\mathcal{J}
in the
Katětov order

if there is a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathcal{J}
for every
A\in\mathcal{I}
. We denote it by
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{J}
. When
\mathcal{I}
is not below
\mathcal{J}
in Katětov order, we denote it by
\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{J}
.

In 2003, Kojman and Shelah
[
17
]
constructed under the continuum hypothesis a van der Waerden space (i.e.
\mathcal{W}
-space) which is not Hindman. Later Flašková
[
8
]
extended their result by constructing an
\mathcal{I}
-space which is not Hindman for every
F_{\sigma}
ideal
\mathcal{I}
under the assumption of Martin’s axiom. In particular, she constructed
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
-space which is not Hindman and posed a question
[
7
]
whether it is consistent that there is a Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
-space. In Section
3
we show that the answer to her question is positive under the continuum hypothesis. This follows from our main result: in Section
2
we show that
if an ideal
\mathcal{I}
is not below the ideal
\mathcal{H}
in the Katětov order
then there exists a Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}
-space.

The spaces constructed by Kojman, Shelah and Flašková are examples of Mrówka spaces. In Section
1
we show that Mrówka spaces are not Hindman, therefore the spaces we construct in Section
2
are not Mrówka spaces.

In
[
13
]
Jones posed a question whether there is a Hindman space which is not van der Waerden.
In fact, we show that, in the realm of
F_{\sigma}
ideals, the consistency of existence of a Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}
-space is equivalent to
\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
.
Thus, the topological question of Jones is reduced to a purely combinatorial question whether
\mathcal{W}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
.

1.
Mrówka spaces are not Hindman

In the sequel we use
\omega
to denote the set of all natural numbers, and we identify
n\in\omega
with the set
\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
(in particular
A\setminus n=A\setminus\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
).

We say that sets
A,B
are
almost disjoint
if
A\cap B
is finite.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an infinite pairwise almost disjoint family of infinite subsets of
\omega
.
Define a topological space
\Psi(\mathcal{A})
as follows:
the underlying set of
\Psi(\mathcal{A})
is
\omega\cup\mathcal{A}
, the points of
\omega

are isolated and a basic neighborhood of
A\in\mathcal{A}
has the form
\{A\}\cup(A\setminus F)
, with
F
finite. (The space
\Psi(\mathcal{A})
was introduced in

[
19
]
.)

Let
\Phi(\mathcal{A})=\Psi(\mathcal{A})\cup\{\infty\}
be the one-point compactification of
\Psi(\mathcal{A})
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a
mad family on
\omega
(i.e. infinite maximal pairwise almost disjoint family of infinite subsets of

\omega
), then the space
\Phi(\mathcal{A})

is called a
Mrówka space
.

In
[
15
]
, Kojman proved that if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, then the Mrówka space
\Phi(\mathcal{A})
is not Hindman, and using this observation various spaces were shown to be not Hindman (see e.g.
[
17
,
13
,
8
]
). Below we show that a Mrówka space is not Hindman even without assuming that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
.

Proposition 1.1
.

No Mrówka space is Hindman.

Proof.

Fix a Mrówka space
\Phi(\mathcal{A})
defined by a mad family
\mathcal{A}
.

Let

P_{k}=\{n2^{k+1}+2^{k}:n\in\omega\}

for each
k\in\omega
and notice that for any infinite
D\subseteq\omega
we have:

(1)

\FS(D)\cap P_{k}
is infinite for some
k\in\omega
,

(2)

\FS(D)\setminus P_{k}
is infinite for each
k\in\omega
.

Indeed, we have two cases:
D\cap P_{k}
is infinite for some
k
or
D\cap P_{k}
is finite for each
k
.

In the first case, take
k_{0}
such that
D\cap P_{k_{0}}
is infinite.
Then
\FS(D)\cap P_{k_{0}}
is infinite as it contains
D\cap P_{k_{0}}
.
Moreover
for
k\neq k_{0}
we have
\FS(D)\setminus P_{k}
is infinite as
P_{k}\cap P_{k_{0}}=\emptyset
and
D\cap P_{k_{0}}\subseteq\FS(D)
, and

\FS(D)\setminus P_{k_{0}}
is infinite as
a+b\in\FS(D)\setminus P_{k_{0}}
for any
a,b\in D\cap P_{k_{0}}
.

In the second case, take
k_{0}
such that
D\cap P_{k_{0}}\neq\emptyset
. Then

\FS(D)\cap P_{k_{0}}
is infinite as
a+b\in\FS(D)\cap P_{k_{0}}
for any
a\in D\cap P_{k_{0}}
and
b\in D\cap P_{l}
with
l>k_{0}
.
Moreover,

\FS(D)\setminus P_{k}
is infinite as
D\subseteq\FS(D)
and
D\setminus P_{k}
is infinite for any
k
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},\dots
be a sequence of distinct sets from
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
f:\omega\to\Phi(\mathcal{A})
be a sequence such that
f\restriction P_{k}:P_{k}\to A_{k}\setminus\bigcup_{i<k}A_{i}
is a bijection for each
k\in\omega
.
We claim that
f
has no IP-convergent IP-subsequence.

We take any infinite
D\subseteq\omega
and show that
f\restriction\FS(D)
is not IP-convergent to any
x\in\Phi(\mathcal{A})
.

If
x\in\omega
, then
f\restriction\FS(D)
is not IP-convergent to
x
as
\omega
is discrete in
\Phi(\mathcal{A})
and
f
is one-to-one.

Suppose that
x=\infty
. Take
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
f[D]\cap A
is infinite (there is one such
A
since
\mathcal{A}
is a mad family) and notice that for each
n\in\omega
the set
f[D\setminus n]\cap A
is also infinite.
Then
U=\Phi(\mathcal{A})\setminus(\{A\}\cup A)
is an open neighborhood of
\infty

such that
f[\FS(D\setminus n)]\setminus U
is infinite for each
n
. Hence
f\restriction\FS(D)
is not IP-convergent to
\infty
.

Suppose that
x\in\mathcal{A}
. We have two cases:

x\neq A_{k}
for any
k\in\omega
or
x=A_{k_{0}}
for some
k_{0}\in\omega
.

In the first case,

U=\{x\}\cup x
is an open neighborhood of
x
such that
f[\FS(D\setminus n)]\setminus U
is infinite for each
n
and therefore
f\restriction\FS(D)
is not IP-convergent to
x
.

In the second case,
U=\{A_{k_{0}}\}\cup(A_{k_{0}}\setminus\bigcup_{i<k_{0}}(A_{i}\cap A_{k_{0}}))
is an open neighborhood of
x

such that
f[\FS(D\setminus n)]\setminus U
is infinite for each
n

and therefore
f\restriction\FS(D)
is not IP-convergent to
x
.
∎

2.
New Hindman spaces

In this section we prove our main result that under the continuum hypothesis if
\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
then there is a Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}
-space.

First we prove some lemmas we are going to use in the proof of the main theorem.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a topological space.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

Limits of convergent sequences in
X
are unique.

(2)

IP-limits of IP-convergent IP-sequences in
X
are unique.

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)

Follows from the fact that if
\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\FS(D)}
is an IP-sequence IP-convergent to
x
then
\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in D}
is a sequence convergent to
x
.

(2)\implies(1)

Suppose that there is a sequence
x_{n}\in X
with two distinct limits
x,y\in X
.
Let

P_{k}=\{n2^{k+1}+2^{k}:n\in\omega\}

for each
k\in\omega
.
Now we define a sequence
y_{n}\in X
by

y_{n}=x_{k}\iff n\in P_{k}
.
Let
E=\{2^{k}:k\in\omega\}
. Then it is not difficult to see that

\langle y_{n}\rangle_{n\in\FS(E)}
is IP-convergent to both
x
and
y
.
∎

An infinite set
D\subseteq\omega
is
sparse
if for every
x\in\FS(D)
there is a unique nonempty finite set
F\subseteq D
with
x=\sum_{i\in F}i
.
A sparse set
D
is
very sparse
if additionally for each
F,F^{\prime}\in[D]^{<\omega}
, if
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
then
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\notin\FS(D)
.
(The term a “sparse set” was coined by Kojman in
[
15
]
, however the sparsity of sets was used earlier as this notion is crucial in Hindman’s original proof
[
11
]
. The same notion has been recently renamed “apartness” in some other contexts
[
2
]
.)

Lemma 2.2

(Essentially
[
10
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

For every infinite
D
there is an infinite very sparse
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
.

Proof.

Let
D
be infinite. Inductively pick
d_{i}\in D
in such a way that
d_{n}>2\sum_{i<n}d_{i}
.
Define
D^{\prime}=\{d_{i}:i\in\omega\}
. Obviously,
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
. Moreover,
D^{\prime}
is sparse. Indeed, if

x\in\FS(D)
and
F,F^{\prime}\in[D]^{<\omega}
are such that
x=\sum_{i\in F}i=\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i
, then
\sum_{i\in F\setminus F^{\prime}}i=\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}\setminus F}i
. Assume to the contrary that
F\neq F^{\prime}
. Since
(F\setminus F^{\prime})\cap(F^{\prime}\setminus F)=\emptyset
, without loss of generality we may assume that
d_{n}=\max(F\setminus F^{\prime})>\max(F^{\prime}\setminus F)
. Observe that
\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}\setminus F}i\leq\sum_{i<n}d_{i}<d_{n}\leq\sum_{i\in F\setminus F^{\prime}}i
. Thus,
F=F^{\prime}
.

Finally, we show inductively that
D^{\prime}
is very sparse. For the first inductive step note that if
F,F^{\prime}\subseteq\{d_{0}\}
and
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
then
F=F^{\prime}=\{d_{0}\}
and
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i=2d_{0}<d_{1}
. Thus,
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}\notin\FS(D^{\prime})
. Assume now that for some
n\in\omega
, if
F,F^{\prime}\subseteq\{d_{i}:i<n\}
and
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
then
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}\notin\FS(D^{\prime})
. Fix
F,F^{\prime}\subseteq\{d_{i}:i\leq n\}
such that
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

There are two possibilities: either
\max(F\cup F^{\prime})<d_{n}
and then
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\notin\FS(D^{\prime})
by the inductive assumption, or
\max(F\cup F^{\prime})=d_{n}
.
In the latter case note that
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\leq 2\sum_{i\leq n}d_{i}<d_{n+1}
.
Moreover, if
d_{n}\in F\cap F^{\prime}
then
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\geq 2d_{n}>\sum_{i\leq n}d_{i}
and thus
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\notin\FS(D^{\prime})
.
On the other hand, if
d_{n}\in F\setminus F^{\prime}
, then suppose to the contrary that
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\in\FS(D^{\prime})
.
In this case, since
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i<d_{n+1}
,

\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\in FS(\{d_{i}:i\leq n\})=\{d_{n}\}\cup FS(\{d_{i}:i<n\})\cup(d_{n}+FS(\{d_{i}:i<n\}))
.
As
d_{n}>2\sum_{i<n}d_{i}
and
d_{n}\in F\setminus F^{\prime}
, we get that
\sum_{i\in F}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\in d_{n}+FS(\{d_{i}:i<n\})
i.e.
\sum_{i\in F\setminus\{d_{n}\}}i+\sum_{i\in F^{\prime}}i\in\FS(\{d_{i}:i<n\})
.
But this contradicts the inductive assumption. Thus,
D^{\prime}
is very sparse.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(D_{i})\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}
be such that
D_{0}
is very sparse and
\FS(D_{0})\supseteq\FS(D_{1})\supseteq\ldots
. Then there is an infinite
E
such that
\FS(E)\subseteq\FS(D_{0})
and for each
n\in\omega
there is
k\in\omega
with
\FS(E\setminus k)\subseteq\FS(D_{n})
.

Proof.

Since
D_{0}
is very sparse, for each
x\in\FS(D_{0})
there is a unique
F(x)\in[D_{0}]^{<\omega}
with
x=\sum_{i\in F(x)}i
. Let
e_{0}\in D_{0}
,
e_{1}\in\FS(D_{1})\setminus\{x\in\FS(D_{0}):F(x)\cap F(e_{0})\neq\emptyset\}
and
e_{n}\in\FS(D_{n})\setminus\{x\in\FS(D_{0}):(\exists i<n)\,F(x)\cap F(e_{i})\neq\emptyset\}
for all
n>1
. This is possible as
\{x\in\FS(D_{0}):(\exists i<n)\,F(x)\cap F(e_{i})\neq\emptyset\}\in\mathcal{H}\restriction\FS(D_{0})
for each
n\in\omega
.

Define
E=\{e_{i}:i\in\omega\}
. Then
E
is infinite. We need to check that
E
is as needed.

Let
x\in\FS(E)
. Then there is a finite
F\subseteq\omega
such that
x=\sum_{i\in F}e_{i}
. As
F(e_{i})
are pairwise disjoint, we get that
x\in\FS(D_{0})
.

Fix
n\in\omega
and let
x\in\FS(E\setminus e_{n})
. Then there is a finite
F\subseteq\omega\setminus n
such that
x=\sum_{i\in F}e_{i}
. Recall that each
e_{i}
for
i\geq n
is in
\FS(D_{n})
. Thus, for each
i\in F
we can find finite
T_{i}\subseteq D_{n}
with

e_{i}=\sum_{d\in T_{i}}d
. Observe that
F(d)\cap F(d^{\prime})=\emptyset
for all
d,d^{\prime}\in D_{n}
as otherwise we would get
d+d^{\prime}\in\FS(D_{n})\setminus\FS(D_{0})
which contradicts
\FS(D_{0})\supseteq\FS(D_{n})
. We claim that the sets
T_{i}
are pairwise disjoint. Indeed, if
d\in T_{i}\cap T_{j}\subseteq D_{n}
for some
i,j\in F
then
F(d)\subseteq\bigcup_{d^{\prime}\in T_{i}}F(d^{\prime})=F(e_{i})
and
F(d)\subseteq\bigcup_{d^{\prime}\in T_{j}}F(d^{\prime})=F(e_{j})
(as
F(x)
are unique). This leads to
F(e_{i})\cap F(e_{j})\neq\emptyset
which implies
e_{i}+e_{j}\notin\FS(D_{0})
and contradicts the fact that
F(e_{i})
are pairwise disjoint. Since the sets
T_{i}\in[D_{n}]^{<\omega}
are pairwise disjoint,
x=\sum_{i\in F}\sum_{d\in T_{i}}d\in\FS(D_{n})
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
D\subseteq\omega
be infinite and
A_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
for each
n\in\omega
. Then there is an infinite
D^{\prime}
such that
\FS(D^{\prime})\subseteq\FS(D)
and for each
n\in\omega
there is
k\in\omega
with
\FS(D^{\prime}\setminus k)\cap A_{n}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, without loss of generality we may assume that
D
is very sparse. Since
A_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, the set
\FS(D)\setminus A_{0}
contains
\FS(D_{0})
for some infinite
D_{0}\subseteq\omega
. Similarly, the set
\FS(D_{0})\setminus A_{1}
contains
\FS(D_{1})
for some infinite
D_{1}\subseteq\omega
. Thus, we can inductively define a sequence of sets
D_{n}\in[\omega]^{\omega}
such that
\FS(D)\supseteq\FS(D_{0})\supseteq\FS(D_{1})\supseteq\ldots
and
\FS(D_{i})\cap A_{i}=\emptyset
for all
i\in\omega
. Applying Lemma
2.3
we get an infinite
E
such that for each
n\in\omega
there is
k\in\omega
with
\FS(E\setminus k)\subseteq\FS(D_{n})
. Let
k_{0}
be such that
\FS(E\setminus k_{0})\subseteq\FS(D_{0})
and define
D^{\prime}=E\setminus k_{0}
. Then
\FS(D^{\prime})=\FS(E\setminus k_{0})\subseteq\FS(D_{0})\subseteq\FS(D)
. Moreover, given
n\in\omega
, as there is
k\in\omega
with
\FS(E\setminus k)\subseteq\FS(D_{n})
, we have that
\FS(D^{\prime}\setminus k)\subseteq\FS(E\setminus k)\subseteq\FS(D_{n})
, so
\FS(D^{\prime}\setminus k)\cap A_{n}=\emptyset
.
∎

Finally, we are ready for the main result of this section.
To some extent, the idea of the proof of the following theorem shares some similarity with Kojman-Shelah’s construction of van der Waerden space which is not Hindman (
[
17
]
).

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume the continuum hypothesis. If
\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
,
then there is a
T_{1}
separable Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}
-space. Moreover, limits of convergent sequences and IP-limits of IP-convergent IP-sequences in this space are unique.

Proof.

Using the continuum hypothesis, we only need countable ordinals to fix a list
\langle(H_{\alpha},f_{\alpha}):\alpha<\omega_{1}\rangle
of all sets
H_{\alpha}\notin\mathcal{H}
and functions
f_{\alpha}:H_{\alpha}\to\omega
satisfying
f_{\alpha}^{-1}[\{n\}]\in\mathcal{H}
for all
n\in\omega
.

We will construct a sequence
\langle D_{\alpha}:\alpha<\omega_{1}\rangle
of infinite subsets of
\omega
such that for every
\alpha<\omega_{1}

we have
\FS(D_{\alpha})\subseteq H_{\alpha}

and
f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha})]\in\mathcal{I}

and
one of the following conditions holds:

(W1)

\forall{\beta<\alpha}\,\forall{i\in\omega}\,\exists{k\in\omega}\,\left(f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]\cap(i\cup f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)])=\emptyset\right)

or

(W2)

\exists{\beta<\alpha}\,\forall{k\in\omega}\,\exists{n\in\omega}\,\,\left(f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus n)]\subseteq f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]\setminus k\right).

Suppose that
\alpha<\omega_{1}
and that
D_{\beta}
have been chosen for all
\beta<\alpha
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is homogeneous (see
[
18
, Example 2.6]
) and
\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
, there is
C\in\mathcal{I}
such that
f^{-1}_{\alpha}[C]\notin\mathcal{H}\restriction H_{\alpha}
.
Find infinite
D\subseteq\omega
with
\FS(D)\subseteq H_{\alpha}
and
f_{\alpha}[\FS(D)]\subseteq C
.
By Lemma
2.2
we can assume that
D
is very sparse.

We have 2 cases:

(P1)

\forall{D^{\prime}\in[\omega]^{\omega}}\,\forall{\beta<\alpha}\,\left(\FS(D^{\prime})\subseteq\FS(D)\implies\exists{k\in\omega}\,\left(\FS(D^{\prime})\setminus f_{\alpha}^{-1}[f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]]\notin\mathcal{H}\right)\right)

or

(P2)

\exists{D^{\prime}\in[\omega]^{\omega}}\,\exists{\beta<\alpha}\,\left(\FS(D^{\prime})\subseteq\FS(D)\land\forall{k\in\omega}\,\left(\FS(D^{\prime})\setminus f_{\alpha}^{-1}[f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]]\in\mathcal{H}\right)\right).

In the first case, let
\alpha\times\omega=\{(\beta_{n},i_{n}):n<\omega\}
, taking into account that
\alpha
is countable.
Using condition (
P1
) repeatedly
and the fact that
f_{\alpha}^{-1}[i]\in\mathcal{H}
for every
i\in\omega
,
one can easily construct a sequence
\langle E_{n}:n\in\omega\rangle
of infinite subsets of
\omega
such that

(1)

\FS(E_{0})\subseteq\FS(D)
,

(2)

\forall{n\in\omega}\,(\FS(E_{n+1})\subseteq\FS(E_{n}))
,

(3)

\forall{n\in\omega}\,\exists{k\in\omega}\left(\FS(E_{n})\cap f_{\alpha}^{-1}[i_{n}\cup f_{\beta_{n}}[\FS(E_{\beta_{n}}\setminus k)]]=\emptyset\right)
.

Now using Lemma
2.3
we find an infinite set
E^{\prime}\subseteq\omega
such that

\FS(E^{\prime})\subseteq\FS(D)

and for every
n\in\omega
there is
l\in\omega
with
\FS(E^{\prime}\setminus l)\subseteq\FS(E_{n})
.

It is not difficult to see that
D_{\alpha}=E^{\prime}
satisfies
\FS(D_{\alpha})\subseteq H_{\alpha}
and (
W1
).

Consider the second case.
Let
D^{\prime}\in[\omega]^{\omega}
and
\beta<\alpha
be such that

\FS(D^{\prime})\subseteq\FS(D)
and
\FS(D^{\prime})\setminus f_{\alpha}^{-1}[f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]]\in\mathcal{H}
for each
k\in\omega
.
Since
f_{\alpha}^{-1}[i]\in\mathcal{H}
for every
i\in\omega
,
we also have

\FS(D^{\prime})\setminus f_{\alpha}^{-1}[f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]\setminus k]\in\mathcal{H}
for each
k\in\omega
.
Using Lemma
2.4
, we find an infinite set
D^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\omega

such that
\FS(D^{\prime\prime})\subseteq\FS(D^{\prime})
and for every
k\in\omega
there is
n\in\omega
with

\FS(D^{\prime\prime}\setminus n)\cap(\FS(D^{\prime})\setminus f_{\alpha}^{-1}[f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]\setminus k])=\emptyset
.

It is not difficult to see that
D_{\alpha}=D^{\prime\prime}
satisfies
\FS(D_{\alpha})\subseteq H_{\alpha}
and (
W2
).

The construction of sets
D_{\alpha}
is finished.

We are ready to define the required space. Let

T=\{\alpha<\omega_{1}:\text{$D_{\alpha}$ satisfies \eqref{eq:W1}}\}

and

X=\omega\cup\{\FS(D_{\alpha}):\alpha\in T\}\cup\{\infty\}.

For every
x\in X
we define the family
\mathcal{B}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
as follows:

•

\mathcal{B}(n)=\left\{\{n\}\right\}
for
n\in\omega
,

•

\mathcal{B}(\FS(D_{\alpha}))=\left\{\{\FS(D_{\alpha})\}\cup f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]\setminus k:k\in\omega\right\}
for
\alpha\in T
,

•

\mathcal{B}(\infty)=\left\{\{\infty\}\cup\bigcup_{\alpha\in T\setminus F}U_{\alpha}:F\in[T]^{<\omega}\land U_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}(FS(D_{\alpha}))\text{ for $\alpha\in T\setminus F$}\right\}
.

It is not difficult to check that
the family
\mathcal{N}=\{\mathcal{B}(x):x\in X\}

satisfies:

•

for each
x\in X
we have
\mathcal{B}(x)\neq\emptyset
and if
U\in\mathcal{B}(x)
then
x\in U
;

•

for each
x\in X
, if
U,U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(x)
then there is
V\in\mathcal{B}(x)
with
V\subseteq U\cap U^{\prime}
;

•

if
x,y\in X
are such that
y\in U
for some
U\in\mathcal{B}(x)
then there is
V\in\mathcal{B}(y)
with
V\subseteq U
.

Hence
\mathcal{N}
is a neighborhood system (see e.g.
[
3
, Proposition 1.2.3]
).
We claim that
X
with the topology generated by
\mathcal{N}
is a topological space we are looking for.

First, observe that
X
is separable as
\omega
is a countable dense subset of
X
.

Second, it is not difficult to see that
X
is
T_{1}
. In fact
X\setminus\{\infty\}
is
T_{2}
(i.e. Hausdorff), however it is unlikely to be able to separate points
x=\infty
and
y=\FS(D_{\alpha})
.

Now we show that
X
is a Hindman space. Fix any
f:\omega\to X
. If there is
x\in X
with
f^{-1}[\{x\}]\notin\mathcal{H}
then find
D\in[\omega]^{\omega}
with
FS(D)\subseteq f^{-1}[\{x\}]
and observe that
\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in FS(D)}
IP-converges to
x
. Thus, we can assume that
f^{-1}[\{x\}]\in\mathcal{H}
for all
x\in X
. There are two possible cases:

f^{-1}[X\setminus\omega]\notin\mathcal{H}
or

f^{-1}[\omega]\notin\mathcal{H}
.

If
f^{-1}[X\setminus\omega]\notin\mathcal{H}
then find
D\in[\omega]^{\omega}
with
FS(D)\subseteq f^{-1}[X\setminus\omega]
. As
f^{-1}[\{x\}]\in\mathcal{H}
for all
x\in X

and

f^{-1}[\{x\}]\neq\emptyset
only for countably many
x\in X
, using Lemma
2.4
we can find
D^{\prime}\in[\omega]^{\omega}
with
FS(D^{\prime})\subseteq FS(D)
and such that for each
x\in X\setminus\omega
there is
k\in\omega
with
FS(D^{\prime}\setminus k)\cap f^{-1}[\{x\}]=\emptyset
. Since for each
U\in\mathcal{B}(\infty)
there are only finitely many
\alpha\in T
with
\FS(D_{\alpha})\notin U
,
\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in FS(D^{\prime})}
IP-converges to
\infty
.

If
f^{-1}[\omega]\notin\mathcal{H}
then there is
\alpha<\omega_{1}
with
(f^{-1}[\omega],f_{\upharpoonright f^{-1}[\omega]})=(H_{\alpha},f_{\alpha})
.
We have two subcases:
\alpha\in T
and
\alpha\notin T
.

Assume
\alpha\in T
.
Using Lemma
2.4
, we find an infinite set
D^{\prime}\subseteq\omega
such that

\FS(D^{\prime})\subseteq\FS(D_{\alpha})
and
for each
k\in\omega
there is
n\in\omega
with

\FS(D^{\prime}\setminus n)\cap f^{-1}[k]=\emptyset
.
Since each
U\in\mathcal{B}(\FS(D_{\alpha}))
is of the form
\{\FS(D_{\alpha})\}\cup f[FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]\setminus k
for some
k\in\omega
,
the subsequence

\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in FS(D^{\prime})}
IP-converges to
\FS(D_{\alpha})
.

Assume
\alpha\notin T
.
Then there is
\beta<\alpha
such that for each
k\in\omega
there is
n\in\omega
with
f_{\alpha}[FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus n)]\subseteq f_{\beta}[FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]\setminus k
.
If we take the smallest
\beta<\alpha
with the above property, then
\beta\in T

and
\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in FS(D_{\alpha})}
IP-converges to
\FS(D_{\beta})\in X
.

Now we check that
X
is not an
\mathcal{I}
-space. Define
f:\omega\to X
by
f(n)=n
and fix any
B\notin\mathcal{I}
. We claim that
\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in B}
does not converge. Clearly,
\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in B}
cannot converge to any
x\in\omega
. Moreover, it cannot converge to any
\FS(D_{\alpha})
as

U=\{\FS(D_{\alpha})\}\cup f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha})]
is an open neighborhood of
\FS(D_{\alpha})
and
f[B]\setminus U=B\setminus U
is infinite (since
B\notin\mathcal{I}
and
f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha})]\in\mathcal{I}
).

We will show that
\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in B}
cannot converge to
\infty
. Since
B
is infinite, there is
\alpha<\omega_{1}
with

H_{\alpha}=\omega
and
f_{\alpha}:H_{\alpha}\to B
being the increasing enumeration of
B
.
In particular,
f_{\alpha}[H_{\alpha}]=B
.
We have 2 cases:
\alpha\in T
or
\alpha\notin T
.

Suppose first that
\alpha\in T
. Then
f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha})]\subseteq B
.
Since
f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]

is infinite for every
k
, we can inductively take
c_{k}\in f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]\setminus\{c_{i}:i<k\}
to obtain an infinite set
C=\{c_{k}:k\in\omega\}

such that

C\subseteq B
and
C\setminus f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]
is finite for all
k\in\omega
.
Now observe that for each
\beta\in T\setminus\{\alpha\}
there is
U_{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}(\FS(D_{\beta}))
with
U_{\beta}\cap C=\emptyset
. Indeed, fix
\beta\in T\setminus\{\alpha\}
. By condition (
W1
) there is
k\in\omega
such that
f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]\cap f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]=\emptyset
.
Let
k_{\beta}=\max(k,\max(C\setminus f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]))
.
Then
U_{\beta}=\{\FS(D_{\beta})\}\cup f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k_{\beta})]\setminus k_{\beta}
is in
\mathcal{B}(\FS(D_{\beta}))
and
U_{\beta}\cap C=\emptyset
.
Therefore,
U=\{\infty\}\cup\bigcup_{\beta\in T\setminus\{\alpha\}}U_{\beta}
is an open neighborhood of
\infty
which is disjoint with
C
. Since
C\in[B]^{\omega}
, this shows that
\langle f(n)\rangle_{n\in B}
cannot converge to
\infty
.

Suppose now that
\alpha\notin T
. Then there is
\beta<\alpha
such that for each
k\in\omega
there is
n\in\omega
with
f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus n)]\subseteq f_{\beta}[FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]
.
If we take the smallest
\beta<\alpha
with the above property, then
\beta\in T
.
Then similarly as in the first case, we find an infinite
C\subseteq B
with
C\setminus f_{\beta}[FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k)]
finite for all
k\in\omega
, and consequently we can find an open neighborhood
U
of
\infty
disjoint with
C
.

To finish the entire proof we have to check that limits of convergent sequences and IP-limits of IP-convergent IP-sequences in
X
are unique.
By Proposition
2.1
we only have to show the uniqueness of limits.

Fix a convergent sequence
x_{n}\in X
. Since
X\setminus\{\infty\}
is
T_{2}
(and limits in
T_{2}
space are unique) and
\omega
is discrete in
X
(so sequences with limits in
\omega
have unique limits) we only need to check that if
\langle x_{n}\rangle
converges to some
\FS(D_{\alpha})
then it cannot converge to
\infty
. So suppose that
\langle x_{n}\rangle
converges to
A_{\alpha}
.

If
x_{n}=FS(D_{\alpha})
for infinitely many
n

then
U=\{\infty\}\cup\bigcup_{\beta\in T\setminus\{\alpha\}}\{\FS(D_{\beta})\}\cup f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta})]
is an open neighborhood of
\infty
which omits infinitely many elements of
\langle x_{n}\rangle
. Hence
\langle x_{n}\rangle
cannot converge to
\infty
.

Suppose now that
x_{n}\neq FS(D_{\alpha})
for all but finitely many
n
. Then the set
C=\omega\cap\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}
is infinite.
Since
\langle x_{n}\rangle
converges to
FS(D_{\alpha})
,
C\setminus f_{\alpha}[\FS(D_{\alpha}\setminus k)]
is finite for any
k\in\omega
.
Then, using condition (
W1
), for each
\beta\in T\setminus\{\alpha\}
we can find
k_{\beta}\in\omega
with
C\cap(f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k_{\beta})]\setminus k_{\beta})=\emptyset
.
Therefore,
U=\{\infty\}\cup\bigcup_{\beta\in T\setminus\{\alpha\}}\{\FS(D_{\beta})\}\cup f_{\beta}[\FS(D_{\beta}\setminus k_{\beta})]\setminus k_{\beta}
is an open neighborhood of
\infty
which is disjoint with
C
. Hence
\langle x_{n}\rangle
cannot converge to
\infty
.
∎

At this point a natural conjecture would be that if
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
then every Hindman space is an
\mathcal{I}
-space. We were able to prove it only under one additional assumption on the ideal
\mathcal{I}
.

An ideal
\mathcal{I}
is called
P
+
-ideal
if for every decreasing sequence
A_{1},A_{2},\dots\notin\mathcal{I}
there is
A\notin\mathcal{I}
such that
A\setminus A_{n}
is finite for each
n\in\omega
(
[
12
, p. 32]
).

Proposition 2.6
.

If
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{I}
is a P
+
-ideal, then every Hindman space is an
\mathcal{I}
-space.
In particularly, if
\mathcal{I}
is an
F_{\sigma}
ideal and
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
then every Hindman space is an
\mathcal{I}
-space.

Proof.

The “in particular” part follows
from the first part as it is well known that each
F_{\sigma}
ideal is a P
+
-ideal
(see e.g.
[
5
, Proposition 5.1]
).

Let
X
be a Hindman space and fix a sequence
\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\omega}
in
X
. Let
f:\omega\to\omega
be a witness for
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
and define
y_{n}=x_{f(n)}
for all
n\in\omega
. Then there is an infinite
D\subseteq\omega
and
x\in X
such that for each open neighborhood
U
of the point
x
there is
m\in\omega
with
\{y_{n}:n\in\FS(D\setminus m)\}\subseteq U
. We have
f[\FS(D\setminus n)]\notin\mathcal{I}
for each
n\in\omega
. Consider the decreasing sequence of
\mathcal{I}
-positive sets given by
A_{i}=f[\FS(D\setminus i)]
for all
i\in\omega
. Since
\mathcal{I}
is a P
+
-ideal, there is
B\notin\mathcal{I}
with
B\setminus A_{i}
finite for all
i\in\omega
. We claim that
\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in B}
converges. Indeed, let
U
be an open neighborhood of
x
. Then there is
k\in\omega
with
\{x_{n}:n\in f[\FS(D\setminus k)]\}=\{y_{n}:n\in\FS(D\setminus k)\}\subseteq U
. Thus,
\{n\in B:x_{n}\notin U\}\subseteq B\setminus f[FS(D\setminus k)]=B\setminus A_{k}
and the latter set is finite.
∎

Example 2.7
.

The family
\mathcal{F}=\{A\subseteq\omega:\exists k\in\omega\,\forall D\in[\omega]^{k}\,(\FS(D)\setminus A\neq\emptyset)\}
is an ideal on
\mathbb{N}
(it follows from a version of Folkman’s theorem see e.g.
[
1
, a remark before Theorem 1.3 at page 43]
).
(This ideal was introduced in
[
8
]
, where the author proved that consistently there is an
\mathcal{F}
-space which is not an
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
-space. This ideal was also considered in
[
18
]
, where the authors posed a question whether it is homogeneous.)
It is not difficult to show that
\mathcal{F}
is
F_{\sigma}
.
It is obvious that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, so
\mathcal{F}\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
and each Hindman space is an
\mathcal{F}
-space.

Combining Theorem
2.5
and Proposition
2.6
we get the following result.

Corollary 2.8
.

If
\mathcal{I}
is an
F_{\sigma}
ideal on
\omega
, then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

\mathcal{I}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
.

(2)

The continuum hypothesis implies that there is
a Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}
-space.

(3)

It is consistent that there is
a Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}
-space.

In
[
13
]
Jones posed a question whether it is consistent that there is a Hindman space which is not van der Waerden. Since the ideal
\mathcal{W}
is
F_{\sigma}
and
\mathcal{W}
-spaces coincide with van der Waerden spaces, the question of Jones is reduced to a purely combinatorial question whether
\mathcal{W}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
.

3.
A Hindman space which is not an
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
-space

From the previous section we know that there are
F_{\sigma}
ideals below
\mathcal{H}
in the Katětov order. At this point one may ask about existence of
F_{\sigma}
ideals that are not below
\mathcal{H}
in the Katětov order. In this section we show that
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
is an example of such ideal. By the main theorem from the previous section, this answers in positive a question posed by Jana Flašková at the 22nd Summer Conference on Topology and its Applications
[
7
]
about existence of a Hindman space which is not
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
-space.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
D\in[\omega]^{\omega}
is very sparse and
E\in[\omega]^{\omega}
is such that
\FS(E)\subseteq\FS(D)
, then for every
d\in\omega
there is
e\in\omega
such that
\FS(E\setminus e)\subseteq\FS(D\setminus d)
.

Proof.

Suppose that there is
d\in\omega
such that for every
e\in\omega
we have
\FS(E\setminus e)\setminus\FS(D\setminus d)\neq\emptyset
.
Then we pick inductively numbers
x_{n}
such that
x_{n}\in\FS(E\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{n-1}\})\setminus\FS(D\setminus d)
.
Then
x_{n}+x_{k}\in\FS(E)
for any
n\neq k
.
Since
x_{n}\in\FS(E)\subseteq\FS(D)
and
D
is sparse, there is a unique set
F_{n}\subseteq D
with
x_{n}=\sum F_{n}
. Since
x_{n}\notin\FS(D\setminus d)
, we have
F_{n}\cap d\neq\emptyset
.
Thus, there is
n\neq k
with
F_{n}\cap F_{k}\neq\emptyset
.
But
D
is very sparse, so
x_{n}+x_{k}\notin\FS(D)
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

(1)

\mathcal{I}_{1/n}\not\leq_{K}\mathcal{H}
i.e. there is no function
f:\omega\to\omega
such that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathcal{H}
for every
A\in\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
.

(2)

Assume the continuum hypothesis. There is a Hindman space which is not
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
-space.

Proof.

Since (2) follows from (1) and Theorem
2.5
, below we show only (1).

Let
f:\omega\to\omega
be any function. If there is
n\in\omega
with
f^{-1}[\{n\}]\notin\mathcal{H}
then we are done, so we can assume that
f^{-1}[\{n\}]\in\mathcal{H}
for all
n\in\omega
.

Let
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots\in\omega
be such that

\sum_{n\in\omega}\frac{2^{n+1}}{a_{n}+1}<\infty.

Since
f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{n}\}]\in\mathcal{H}
for all
n\in\omega
, there is a very sparse infinite
D\subseteq\omega
such that for each
n\in\omega
there is
k\in\omega
with
FS(D\setminus k)\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{n}\}]=\emptyset
(by Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
).
By shrinking and re-enumerating
D
if necessary, we can assume that
\FS(D\setminus d_{n})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{n}\}]=\emptyset
, where
\langle d_{i}:i\in\omega\rangle
is the increasing enumeration of
D
.

We will inductively pick
x_{n}\in\omega
,
D_{n}\in[\omega]^{\omega}
and auxiliary functions
g_{n}
such that for each
n\in\omega
:

•

x_{n}<x_{n+1}
,
x_{n}\in D_{n}
and
\FS(D_{n}\setminus x_{n})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{n}\}]=\emptyset
;

•

\FS(D)\supseteq\FS(D_{n})\supseteq\FS(D_{n+1})
,

•

g_{n}:\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n\})\to\{0,1\}
,

and for each
y\in\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n\})
:

•

if
g_{n}(y)=1
then
(y+\FS(D_{n}))\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{n}\}]=\emptyset
(in particular,
f(y+x_{n})>a_{n}
),

•

if
g_{n}(y)=0
and either
g_{n-1}(y)=0
or
y\in(x_{n-1}+(\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n-1\})\cap g^{-1}_{n-1}[\{0\}]))
then
f\restriction(y+\FS(D_{n}))
is a constant function whose value is some
c\in f[\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n\})]
,

•

if
g_{n}(y)=0
and either
g_{n-1}(y)=1
or
y\in(x_{n-1}+(\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n-1\})\cap g^{-1}_{n-1}[\{1\}]))
or
y=x_{n-1}
then
f\restriction(y+FS(D_{n}))
is a constant function whose value is some
c>a_{n-1}
.

(The initial step for
n=0
)
We start with
D_{0}=D
,
g_{0}=\emptyset
and any
x_{0}\in D_{0}
.
Since
d_{0}\leq x_{0}
, we have

\FS(D_{0}\setminus x_{0})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{0}\}]=\emptyset
.

(The initial step for
n=1
)
We have 2 cases.

Case 1.

There is
c\in\omega
such that
f^{-1}[\{c\}]
contains
x_{0}+\FS(C)
for some infinite
C
with
\FS(C)\subseteq\FS(D_{0}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{0}\})
.
We define
D_{1}=C
,
g_{1}(x_{0})=0
and, using Lemma
3.1
, pick
x_{1}\in D_{1}

such that

\FS(D_{1}\setminus x_{1})\subseteq\FS(D\setminus d_{1})
.

Since

\FS(D\setminus d_{1})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{1}\}]=\emptyset
,

\FS(D_{1}\setminus x_{1})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{1}\}]=\emptyset
.

Since
x_{0}+\FS(D_{1})\subseteq x_{0}+\FS(D_{0}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{0}\})\subseteq\FS(D_{0}\setminus x_{0})
and

\FS(D_{0}\setminus x_{0})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{0}\}]=\emptyset
, we obtain
c>a_{0}
.

Case 2.

For each
c\in\omega
the set
f^{-1}[\{c\}]
does not contain any
x_{0}+\FS(C)
for infinite
C
with

\FS(C)\subseteq\FS(D_{0}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{0}\})
, so using Lemma
2.4
we can find an infinite
C
with
\FS(C)\subseteq\FS(D_{0}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{0}\})
such that for each
m\in\omega
there is
k_{m}\in\omega
with
(x_{0}+\FS(C\setminus k_{m}))\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{m}\}]=\emptyset
. Put
D_{1}=C\setminus k_{1}
,

g_{1}(x_{0})=1
and, using Lemma
3.1
, pick
x_{1}\in D_{1}

such that

\FS(D_{1}\setminus x_{1})\subseteq\FS(D\setminus d_{1})
.

Since

\FS(D\setminus d_{1})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{1}\}]=\emptyset
,

\FS(D_{1}\setminus x_{1})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{1}\}]=\emptyset

Since
x_{1}\in\FS(D_{1})\subseteq\FS(D_{0}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{0}\})
, we obtain
x_{0}<x_{1}
.

Moreover,
(x_{0}+\FS(D_{1}))\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{1}\}]=(x_{0}+\FS(C\setminus k_{1}))\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{1}\}]=\emptyset
.

(The inductive step)
Suppose now that
x_{i}
,
g_{i}
and
D_{i}
for all
i<n
are already defined.
Let

\begin{split}\{t_{0},\ldots,t_{k}\}&=\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n\})\setminus\\
&\left(g_{n-1}^{-1}[\{0\}]\cup(x_{n-1}+(\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n-1\})\cap g_{n-1}^{-1}[\{0\}]))\right)\end{split}

and define
g_{n}(y)=0
for each
y\in g_{n-1}^{-1}[\{0\}]\cup(x_{n-1}+(\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n-1\})\cap g_{n-1}^{-1}[\{0\}]))
.

We will inductively construct auxiliary sets
E_{-1},E_{0},\ldots,E_{k}\in[\omega]^{\omega}
such that
\FS(D_{n-1}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{n-1}\})\supseteq\FS(E_{-1})\supseteq\dots\supseteq\FS(E_{k})
.

We start with
E_{-1}=D_{n-1}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{n-1}\}
. Suppose now that
i\leq k
and
E_{j}
for all
j<i
are defined. We have 2 cases.

Case 1.

There is
c\in\omega
such that
f^{-1}[\{c\}]
contains
t_{i}+\FS(C)
for some infinite
C
with
\FS(C)\subseteq\FS(E_{i-1})
.
We define
E_{i}=C
and
g_{n}(t_{i})=0
.

Note that
c>a_{n-1}
in this case. Indeed, we have 3 cases:

(1)

t_{i}=x_{n-1}
,

(2)

t_{i}\in x_{n-1}+(\FS(\{x_{j}:j<n-1\})\cap g_{n-1}^{-1}[\{1\}])
,

(3)

t_{i}\in\FS(\{x_{j}:j<n-1\})
.

In the first case,

t_{i}+\FS(E_{i})\subseteq t_{i}+\FS(D_{n-1}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{n-1}\})

and

\FS(D_{n-1}\setminus x_{n-1})\cap f^{-1}[\{0,\ldots,a_{n-1}\}]=\emptyset
, so
c>a_{n-1}
.

In the second case,

t_{i}=y+x_{n-1}
for some
y\in\FS(\{x_{j}:j<n-1\})
with
g_{n-1}(y)=1
.
Then
t_{i}+\FS(E_{i})\subseteq t_{i}+\FS(D_{n-1}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{n-1}\})=y+x_{n-1}+\FS(D_{n-1}\setminus\{0,1,\dots,x_{n-1}\})\subseteq y+\FS(D_{n-1}\setminus x_{n-1})
.
Since

g_{n-1}(y)=1
,
(y+\FS(D_{n-1}))\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{n-1}\}]=\emptyset
. Thus
c>a_{n-1}
.

In the third case,

g_{n-1}(t_{i})=1
,
so
(t_{i}+\FS(D_{n-1}))\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{n-1}\}]=\emptyset
.
Since

t_{i}+\FS(E_{i})\subseteq t_{i}+\FS(D_{n-1})
, we obtain
c>a_{n-1}
.

Case 2.

For each
c\in\omega
the set
f^{-1}[\{c\}]
does not contain any
t_{i}+\FS(C)
for infinite
C
with
\FS(C)\subseteq\FS(E_{i-1})
, so using Lemma
2.4
we can find an infinite
C
with
\FS(C)\subseteq\FS(E_{i-1})
such that for each
m\in\omega
there is
k_{m}\in\omega
with
(t_{i}+\FS(C\setminus k_{m}))\cap f^{-1}[\{0,1,\ldots,a_{m}\}]=\emptyset
. Put
E_{i}=C\setminus k_{n}
and
g_{n}(t_{i})=1
.

Once all
E_{i}
for
i\leq k
are constructed, put
D_{n}=E_{k}
and, using Lemma
3.1
, pick
x_{n}\in D_{n}

such that

\FS(D_{n}\setminus x_{n})\subseteq\FS(D\setminus d_{n})
.

This finishes the inductive construction of the sequences
\langle x_{n}\rangle
,
\langle D_{n}\rangle
and
\langle g_{n}\rangle
.

Define
X=\{x_{i}:i\in\omega\}
. Obviously,
\FS(X)\notin\mathcal{H}
. To end the proof we need to check that
f[\FS(X)]\in\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
i.e. we have to show that

\sum_{i\in f[\FS(X)]}\frac{1}{i+1}=\sum_{i\in f[\FS(X_{0})]}\frac{1}{i+1}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i\in f[\FS(X_{n})]\setminus f[\FS(X_{n-1})]}\frac{1}{i+1}\right)<\infty,

where

X_{n}=\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
for every
n\in\omega
.

Let
n\geq 1
. If
i\in f[\FS(X_{n})]\setminus f[\FS(X_{n-1})]
, then either
i=f(x_{n})
or
i=f(y+x_{n})
for some
y\in\FS(X_{n-1})
.
It follows from the properties of the sequence
\langle x_{n}\rangle
that
f(x_{n})>a_{n}
(hence
1/(i+1)<1/(a_{n}+1)
) and for the value
f(y+x_{n})
we have three cases.

•

If
g_{n}(y)=1
then
f(y+x_{n})>a_{n}
, so
1/(i+1)<1/(a_{n}+1)
.

•

If
g_{n}(y)=0
and
either
g_{n-1}(y)=0
or
y\in(x_{n-1}+(\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n-1\})\cap g^{-1}_{n-1}[\{0\}]))
,
then
f\restriction(y+\FS(D_{n}))
is a constant function whose value is some
c\in f[\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n\})]
. Note that this case is not possible here because
i\notin f[\FS(X_{n-1})]
.

•

If
g_{n}(y)=0
and
either
g_{n-1}(y)=1
or
y\in(x_{n-1}+(\FS(\{x_{i}:i<n-1\})\cap g^{-1}_{n-1}[\{1\}]))
or
y=x_{n-1}
,
then
f\restriction(y+FS(D_{n}))
is a constant function whose value is some
c>a_{n-1}
, so
1/(i+1)<1/(a_{n-1}+1)
.

Thus,

\begin{split}\sum_{i\in f[\FS(X_{n})]\setminus f[\FS(X_{n-1})]}\frac{1}{i+1}&\leq\frac{1}{a_{n}+1}+\frac{|\FS(X_{n-1})|}{a_{n-1}+1}\leq\frac{2^{n}}{a_{n-1}+1}.\end{split}

All in all,

\sum_{i\in f[\FS(X)]}\frac{1}{i+1}\leq\sum_{i\in f[\FS(X_{0})]}\frac{1}{i+1}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n}}{a_{n-1}+1}=\frac{1}{f(x_{0})+1}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n}}{a_{n-1}+1}<\infty.

This finishes the proof.
∎
