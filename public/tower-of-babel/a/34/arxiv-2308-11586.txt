1.
Introduction and statement of main results

By an
algebra norm
on a (real or complex) algebra
\mathscr{A}
, we understand
a norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
on
\mathscr{A}
that is submultiplicative in the sense that
\lVert ab\rVert\leqslant\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert
for every
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
. A
normed algebra
is an algebra equipped with an algebra norm. We say that a normed algebra
\mathscr{A}
has a
unique algebra norm
if every algebra norm on
\mathscr{A}
is equivalent to the given norm.

In the case where
\mathscr{A}
is a Banach algebra (which will be the case for us), this notion should not be confused with the weaker notion of having a unique
complete
algebra norm; see
Remark

2.3
for a more detailed discussion of the difference between these two notions.

We shall focus on the Banach algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)
of bounded operators on a Banach space
X
and its quotients by closed ideals. One of the earliest results about
this Banach algebra (predating even the term “Banach algebra”) is due to Eidelheit
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, who showed that
\mathscr{B}(X)
has a unique complete algebra norm for every Banach space
X
. In fact, building on Eidelheit’s methods, one can deduce a stronger conclusion, which we shall state in
Theorem

2.7
below, once we have established the necessary terminology.

Many related results have subsequently been obtained;
[
50
, §0]
contains an excellent survey of what was known around the turn of the millenium. We would like to highlight the following results, which provide the most important context for our work:

(i)

Meyer
[
37
]
proved that the Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{K}(X)
has a unique algebra norm for each of the classical sequence spaces
X=c_{0}
and
X=\ell_{p}
, where
1\leqslant p<\infty
. For later reference, we recall the classical result of Gohberg, Markus and Feldman
[
17
]
that the ideal
\mathscr{K}(X)
of compact operators is the unique non-trivial closed ideal of
\mathscr{B}(X)
for each of these Banach spaces
X
.

(ii)

In his dissertation
[
51
]
, Ware launched a systematic attack on the uniqueness-of-algebra-norm question for Calkin algebras, generalizing the aforementioned results of Meyer by showing that the Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{K}(X)
has a unique algebra norm for a wide range of Banach spaces
X
, including the following
[
51
, §§5.2–5.5]
:

•

every finite direct sum of spaces from the family
\{c_{0}\}\cup\{\ell_{p}:1\leqslant p<\infty\};

•

the infinite direct sums
\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{p}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
and
\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{p}^{n}\bigr)_{\ell_{q}}
for
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
and
1\leqslant q<\infty;

•

the Tsirelson space
T
and its dual
T^{*}
;

•

the quasi-reflexive James spaces
J_{p}
for
1<p<\infty
.

(iii)

In a somewhat different direction, Ware
[
51
, Section 6]
generalized Meyer’s results to the non-separable setting as follows. Take an uncountable index set
\Gamma
, and set
X=c_{0}(\Gamma)
or
X=\ell_{p}(\Gamma)
for some
1\leqslant p<\infty
. Then the quotient
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{I}
has a unique algebra norm for every closed ideal
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{B}(X)
. Daws’ classification
[
10
]
of the closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
for these Banach spaces
X
plays a key role in this work.

(iv)

More recently, Johnson and Phillips
[
26
]
have answered one of the main questions left open in Ware’s dissertation
[
51
]
by showing that the Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(L_{p}[0,1])/\mathscr{K}(L_{p}[0,1])
has a unique algebra norm whenever
1<p<\infty
. Furthermore, for
p\neq 2
, they have shown that
\mathscr{B}(L_{p}[0,1])
contains a closed ideal
\mathscr{I}
for which the quotient
\mathscr{B}(L_{p}[0,1])/\mathscr{I}
admits at least two inequivalent algebra norms.

Motivated by these results, especially
(i)
,
(iii)
and
(iv)
, we asked ourselves for which Banach spaces
X
other than
c_{0}(\Gamma)
and
\ell_{p}(\Gamma)
for
1\leqslant p<\infty
is it true that every quotient of
\mathscr{B}(X)
by a closed ideal has a unique algebra norm? Our focus has been on the (relatively few) Banach spaces
X
for which all of the closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
have been classified. The following theorem summarizes our main findings.

Theorem 1.1
.

Every quotient of
\mathscr{B}(X)
by one of its closed ideals has a unique algebra norm for each of the following Banach spaces
X\colon

(i)

X=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
and
X=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}};

(ii)

X=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}\oplus c_{0}(\Gamma)
and
X=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}}\oplus\ell_{1}(\Gamma)
for an uncountable index set

(iii)

X=C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}})
, the Banach space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity on the Mrówka space
K_{\mathcal{A}}
induced by an uncountable, almost disjoint family

of infinite subsets of

We refer to

for details of the terminology used in

\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\Bigr)_{c_{0}}\cong\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\Bigr)_{c_{0}}\oplus c_{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\Bigr)_{\ell_{1}}\cong\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\Bigr)_{\ell_{1}}\oplus\ell_{1}.

2.
Preliminaries, including a skeleton proof of
Theorem

1.1

All normed spaces and algebras are over the same scalar field
\mathbb{K}
, either the real or the complex numbers.
As usual, for a locally compact Hausdorff space
K
,
C_{0}(K)
denotes the Banach space of continuous functions
f\colon K\to\mathbb{K}
which “vanish at infinity” in the sense that the set
\{k\in K:\lvert f(k)\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon\}
is compact for every
\varepsilon>0
.

The term “operator” means a bounded linear map between normed spaces.
We write
B_{X}
for the closed unit ball of a normed space
X
, while
I_{X}
(or simply
I
when
X
is clear from the context) denotes the identity operator on
X
. For a Banach space with a basis and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
P_{n}
will usually denote the
n^{\text{th}}
basis projection. Other notation will be introduced as and when required.

Equivalence of norms plays a central role in this investigation. The definition splits naturally in two parts, an upper and a lower bound. This is the motivation behind the following terminology.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert)
be a normed algebra. We say that the given norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
on
\mathscr{A}
is
maximal
if, for every algebra norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
on
\mathscr{A}
, there is a constant
C_{1}>0
such that
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|a\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leqslant C_{1}\|a\|
for every
a\in\mathscr{A}
. Analogously, we say that

\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
is
minimal
if, for every algebra norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
on
\mathscr{A}
, there is a constant
C_{2}>0
such that
\|a\|\leqslant C_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|a\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
for every
a\in\mathscr{A}
.
We say that
\mathscr{A}
has a
unique algebra norm
if the given norm is both maximal and minimal, or in other words if every algebra norm on
\mathscr{A}
is equivalent to the given norm.

Remark 2.2
.

One usually requires that an algebra norm on a unital algebra
\mathscr{A}
must take the value
1
at the multiplicative identity
1_{\mathscr{A}}
. We shall not adhere to this convention. This will not cause any problems because, given any algebra norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
on
\mathscr{A}
,

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|a\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}=\sup\bigl\{\lVert ab\rVert:b\in\mathscr{A},\,\lVert b\rVert\leqslant 1\bigr\}\qquad(a\in\mathscr{A})

defines an equivalent algebra norm on
\mathscr{A}
which satisfies
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|1_{\mathscr{A}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}=1
.

Remark 2.3
.

For a Banach algebra
\mathscr{A}
, there is a related notion of having a
unique complete algebra norm,
which asserts that every complete algebra norm on
\mathscr{A}
is equivalent to the given norm. The Banach Isomorphism Theorem implies that whenever the given norm on a Banach algebra
\mathscr{A}
is either minimal or maximal, then it is automatically equivalent to any other complete norm on
\mathscr{A}
, so
\mathscr{A}
has unique complete algebra norm.

It is well-known that maximality of the norm can be rephrased in terms of automatic continuity of homomorphisms, which is one of the oldest topics in the theory of Banach algebras. Here, and elsewhere, the term “homomorphism” means a linear and multiplicative map between two algebras. There are many ways to express this relationship. We have chosen the following, which reflects the applications we have in mind. A much more comprehensive result can be found in
[
42
, Theorem 6.1.5]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

The following three conditions are equivalent for a normed algebra
(\mathscr{A},\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert)\colon

(a)

The given norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
on
\mathscr{A}
is maximal.

(b)

Every homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a normed algebra is continuous.

(c)

Every injective homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a Banach algebra is continuous.

Now suppose that
(\mathscr{A},\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert)
is a Banach algebra. Then conditions
(a)
–
(c)
are equivalent to the following two conditions:

(d)

The quotient norm on
\mathscr{A}/\mathscr{I}
is maximal for every closed ideal
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{A}
.

(e)

Every homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}/\mathscr{I}
into a normed algebra is continuous for every closed ideal
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

This is standard. For example, it is easy to adapt the proof of
[
8
, Proposition 2.1.7]
to verify that conditions
(a)
–
(c)
are equivalent, and therefore conditions
(d)
and
(e)
are also equivalent. This part of the proof does not require completeness of
\mathscr{A}
. Completeness (in the shape of the Open Mapping Theorem) is, however, required to prove that
(b)
implies
(e)
; see
[
8
, Proposition 2.1.5]
for details. Finally, the implication
(e)
\Rightarrow
(b)
is trivial.
∎

B. E. Johnson
[
24
]
proved what has become the classical automatic continuity result for homomorphisms from the Banach algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)
. We state it in a simplified non-technical form, as it will suffice for our purposes.

Theorem 2.5

(Johnson)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space which is isomorphic to its square
X\oplus X
. Then every homomorphism from
\mathscr{B}(X)
into a Banach algebra is continuous.

Proposition

2.4
has a counterpart for minimality. Its statement involves the following notion: a linear map
T\colon X\to Y
between normed spaces
X
and
Y
is
bounded below
if there is a constant
\eta>0
such that
\lVert Tx\rVert\geqslant\eta\lVert x\rVert
for every
x\in\ X
. If we wish to specify the constant, we say that
T
is
bounded below by

\eta
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert)
be a normed algebra. Then its norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
is minimal if and only if every injective homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a normed algebra is bounded below.

Proof.

Straightforward; we refer to the proof of
[
42
, Theorem 6.1.5(a)–(b)]
for details. ∎

The following result originates in the work of Eidelheit
[
13
, Lemma 1 and Theorem 1]
, as already mentioned in the Introduction. It can for instance be found in
[
8
, Theorem 5.1.14]
or
[
42
, Theorem, page 107]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
\mathscr{A}
be a subalgebra of
\mathscr{B}(X)
which contains the ideal of finite-rank operators. Then the operator norm on
\mathscr{A}
is minimal.

In a forthcoming paper
[
26
]
, W. B. Johnson and N. C. Phillips introduce and explore the following two notions.

Definition 2.8
.

A normed algebra
\mathscr{A}
is:

•

incompressible
if every continuous, injective homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a normed algebra is bounded below;

•

uniformly incompressible
if there is a function
f\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that every continuous, injective homomorphism
\varphi
from
\mathscr{A}
into a normed algebra is bounded below by
f(\lVert\varphi\rVert)
.

Lemma

2.6
shows that minimality of the norm implies incompressibility and that the two notions are equivalent if every homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a normed algebra is continuous. More precisely, we have the following result.

Lemma 2.9
.

A normed algebra
(\mathscr{A},\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert)
has a unique algebra norm if and only if
\mathscr{A}
is incompressible and the given norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
is maximal.

Proof.

This is immediate from
Lemma

2.6
because, by
Proposition

2.4
, maximality of the norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
means that every homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a normed algebra is continuous.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

The following three conditions are equivalent for a Banach algebra
\mathscr{A}\colon

(a)

Every quotient algebra of
\mathscr{A}
by a closed ideal has a unique algebra norm.

(b)

Every homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a Banach algebra is continuous and has closed range.

(c)

Every homomorphism from
\mathscr{A}
into a Banach algebra is continuous, and
\mathscr{A}/\mathscr{I}
is incompressible for every closed ideal
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
. Let
\varphi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
be a homomorphism into a Banach algebra
\mathscr{B}
.

Proposition

2.4
shows that
\varphi
is continuous because the norm on
\mathscr{A}
is maximal.
In particular, the ideal
\ker\varphi
is closed, and the quotient norm on
\mathscr{A}/\ker\varphi
is minimal by hypothesis.
Let
\widetilde{\varphi}\colon\mathscr{A}/\ker\varphi\to\mathscr{B}
be the induced homomorphism given by
\widetilde{\varphi}(a+\ker\varphi)=\varphi(a)
; it satisfies:

(i)

\widetilde{\varphi}[\mathscr{A}/\ker\varphi]=\varphi[\mathscr{A}]
,

(ii)

\widetilde{\varphi}
is injective and therefore bounded below by
Lemma

2.6
,

(iii)

\widetilde{\varphi}
is continuous, hence it has closed range by
(ii)
, and therefore so does
\varphi
by
(i)
.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c)
. Let
\mathscr{I}
be a closed ideal of
\mathscr{A}
, and consider a continuous, injective homomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathscr{A}/\mathscr{I}\to\mathscr{B}
into a normed algebra
\mathscr{B}
. By hypothesis, the composite homomorphism
\iota\varphi\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\widehat{\mathscr{B}}
has closed range, where
\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}/\mathscr{I}
is the quotient map and
\iota\colon\mathscr{B}\to\widehat{\mathscr{B}}
is the isometric embedding of
\mathscr{B}
into its completion
\widehat{\mathscr{B}}
. It follows that the injection
\iota\varphi
has closed range because
\pi
is surjective, so
\iota\varphi
is bounded below, and therefore the same is true for
\varphi
.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(a)
. This follows from
Proposition

2.4
and
Lemma

2.9
.
∎

When verifying (
2.1
), we shall follow the lead of Johnson and Phillips
[
26
]
and use “quantitative factorizations” of idempotent operators to deduce that the quotient algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{I}
is uniformly incompressible. This is a natural tool to bring to bear on the problem at hand because factorizations of idempotent operators already play a key role in the classifications of the closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
for each of the Banach spaces
X
we consider. The information we need to add to these results is that the factorizations can be carried out with uniform norm bounds on the auxiliary operators. We believe that the results we obtain in this regard will be of interest to researchers working on problems very different from uniqueness of algebra norm.
The following notion is at the heart of our approach.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
C\geqslant 1
.
A normed algebra
\mathscr{A}
has the
idempotent factorization property with constant

C
(abbreviated
C
-IFP) if, for every
a\in\mathscr{A}
of norm
1
, there are
b,c\in\mathscr{A}
with
\lVert b\rVert\,\lVert c\rVert\leqslant C
such that
bac
is a non-zero idempotent.

It is sometimes more convenient to use the following equivalent formulation of this definition:
\mathscr{A}
has the
C
-IFP if, for every
a\in\mathscr{A}\setminus\{0\}
, there are
b,c\in\mathscr{A}
with
\lVert b\rVert\,\lVert c\rVert\leqslant C/\lVert a\rVert
such that
bac
is a non-zero idempotent.

Using this terminology, we can express the basic observation of Johnson and Phillips
[
26
]
as follows; see also
[
38
]
for an earlier related, more abstract result. We include a short proof for ease of reference.

Proposition 2.12
.

Every normed algebra which has the
C
-IFP for some constant
C\geqslant 1
is uniformly incompressible.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathscr{A}
is a normed algebra with the
C
-IFP for some
C\geqslant 1
, and let
\varphi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
be a continuous, injective homomorphism into some normed algebra
\mathscr{B}
. For every
a\in\mathscr{A}\setminus\{0\}
, we can find
b,c\in\mathscr{A}
with
\lVert b\rVert\,\lVert c\rVert\leqslant C/\lVert a\rVert
such that
p=bac
is a non-zero idempotent. Then
\varphi(p)
is a non-zero idempotent in
\mathscr{B}
, so

1\leqslant\lVert\varphi(p)\rVert\leqslant\lVert\varphi(b)\rVert\,\lVert\varphi(a)\rVert\,\lVert\varphi(c)\rVert\leqslant\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}\,\frac{C}{\lVert a\rVert}\,\lVert\varphi(a)\rVert.

Rearranging this inequality, we see that
\varphi
is bounded below by
1/(C\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2})
, and consequently
the definition of uniform incompressibility is satisfied with respect to the function
f\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
given by
f(t)=1/(Ct^{2})
.
∎

We shall usually apply a variant of

2.12

, an

is an assignment

which designates to every pair

of Banach spaces a subspace

of

satisfying:

•

\mathscr{J}(X,Y)\neq\{0\}
for some Banach spaces
X
and
Y
;

•

UTS\in\mathscr{J}(W,Z)
whenever
W
,
X
,
Y
and
Z
are Banach spaces and
S\in\mathscr{B}(W,X)
,
T\in\mathscr{J}(X,Y)
and

are operators.

An operator ideal
\mathscr{J}
is

if the subspace
\mathscr{J}(X,Y)
is closed in the operator norm for every pair of Banach spaces
(X,Y)
. As usual, we abbreviate
\mathscr{J}(X,X)
to
\mathscr{J}(X)
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
\mathscr{J}
a closed operator ideal. Suppose that there exists a constant
C\geqslant 1
such that, for every
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)\setminus\mathscr{J}(X)
with
\lVert T+\mathscr{J}(X)\rVert=1
, there exist a Banach space
Y
and operators
R\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
and
S\in\mathscr{B}(Y,X)
with
\lVert R\rVert\,\lVert S\rVert\leqslant C
such that
RTS+\mathscr{J}(Y)
is a non-zero idempotent in
\mathscr{B}(Y)/\mathscr{J}(Y)
. Then
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{J}(X)
has the
C^{2}
-IFP and is therefore uniformly incompressible.

Proof.

If
\mathscr{J}(X)=\mathscr{B}(X)
, there is nothing to prove, so suppose that the closed ideal
\mathscr{J}(X)
is proper, and choose
C\geqslant 1
as specified. Then, for every
t=T+\mathscr{J}(X)\in\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{J}(X)
with
\lVert t\rVert=1
, we can find a Banach space
Y
and operators
R\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
and
S\in\mathscr{B}(Y,X)
with
\lVert R\rVert\,\lVert S\rVert\leqslant C
such that
P+\mathscr{J}(Y)
is a non-zero idempotent, where
P=RTS\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
. Then
P\notin\mathscr{J}(Y)
, and we have

P^{3}-P=(I_{Y}+P)(P^{2}-P)\in\mathscr{J}(Y)
,
so the operator
Q=(TSR)^{2}\in\mathscr{B}(X)
satisfies

RQTS=P^{3}\notin\mathscr{J}(Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q^{2}-Q=TS(P^{3}-P)R\in\mathscr{J}(X).

It follows that
q=Q+\mathscr{J}(X)
is a non-zero idempotent in
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{J}(X)
. Therefore its image under the injective homomorphism
\varphi
is also a non-zero idempotent, which implies that
\lVert\varphi(q)\rVert\geqslant 1
. Set
u=SR+\mathscr{J}(X)\in\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{J}(X)
, and observe that
\lVert u\rVert\leqslant\lVert S\rVert\,\lVert R\rVert\leqslant C
and
q=(tu)^{2}
. Combining these facts, we conclude that
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{J}(X)
has the
C^{2}
-IFP.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

In our applications of
Lemma

2.13
, a stronger hypothesis will be satisfied: we can always take the Banach space
Y
and the operators
R
and
S
with
\lVert R\rVert\,\lVert S\rVert\leqslant C
such that the non-zero idempotent
RTS+\mathscr{J}(Y)
is equal to
I_{Y}+\mathscr{J}(Y)
. In this case we can draw the stronger conclusion that
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{J}(X)
has the
C
-IFP. The reason is that, in the notation already introduced in the proof, the hypothesis that
I_{Y}-RTS\in\mathscr{J}(Y)
implies that

q=(tu)^{2}=TSRTSR+\mathscr{J}(X)=TSI_{Y}R+\mathscr{J}(X)=tu,

and
\lVert u\rVert\leqslant C
as before.

Suppose that the operator ideal
\mathscr{J}
is the ideal of compact operators, so that we consider the Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{K}(X)
of
X
. A result of Barnes
[
4
, Lemma 1, including the remark following it]
implies that idempotents lift from the Calkin algebra to
\mathscr{B}(X)
. Our next lemma will allow us to establish quantitative versions of this result in certain cases. More precisely, under the hypothesis of
Remark

2.14
that we can factor
I_{Y}+\mathscr{K}(Y)
through
T+\mathscr{K}(X)
with control over the norms of the factors, it turns out that we can lift the factorization to obtain an analogous quantitative factorization for the operator
T
itself, provided that the Banach space
Y
is sufficiently “nice” (which it will be in our applications, where
Y=c_{0}
or
Y=\ell_{1}
; see also
Remark

2.16
for how to handle some other cases). As well as formally strengthening our conclusions, we believe that presenting them in this way will make them more accessible to researchers from other fields.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
Y
be a Banach space with a normalized, bimonotone basis
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and suppose that

(2.2)

\biggl\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{n}b_{n}\biggr\rVert=\biggl\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{n}b_{n+1}\biggr\rVert\qquad(m\in\mathbb{N},\,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{K}).

Then, for every
C>1
and every operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
such that
I_{Y}-T
is compact,
there are operators
U,V\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
with
\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert\leqslant C
such that
UTV=I_{Y}
.

Proof.

Equation (
2.2
) shows that the linear map
R
given by
Rb_{n}=b_{n+1}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
is an isometry. Now consider the linear map
L
given by
Lb_{1}=0
and
Lb_{n+1}=b_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. It satisfies

\displaystyle\biggl\lVert L\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\alpha_{n}b_{n}\Bigr)\biggr\rVert=\biggl\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{n+1}b_{n}\biggr\rVert

\displaystyle=\biggl\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{n+1}b_{n+1}\biggr\rVert=\biggl\lVert(I_{Y}-P_{1})\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\alpha_{n}b_{n}\Bigr)\biggr\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\biggl\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{m+1}\alpha_{n}b_{n}\biggr\rVert\qquad(m\in\mathbb{N},\,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m+1}\in\mathbb{K})

by (
2.2
) and bimonotonicity. Hence
Y
admits shift operators
R,L\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
, both having norm
1
, and

(2.3)

L^{n}R^{n}=I_{Y}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R^{n}L^{n}=I_{Y}-P_{n}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Let
C>1
, and suppose that
S=I_{Y}-T
is compact. Then
P_{n}S\to S
as
n\to\infty
, so we can find
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lVert(I_{Y}-P_{n})S\rVert\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{C}
. Combining this with (
2.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert I_{Y}-L^{n}TR^{n}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert L^{n}(I_{Y}-T)R^{n}\rVert=\lVert L^{n}SR^{n}\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert L^{n}S\rVert=\lVert R^{n}L^{n}S\rVert=\lVert(I_{Y}-P_{n})S\rVert\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{C}.

This implies that the operator
L^{n}TR^{n}
is invertible by the Neumann series, and its inverse has norm at most
C
.
Consequently we can define operators
U=(L^{n}TR^{n})^{-1}L^{n}\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
and
V=R^{n}\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
with
\lVert U\rVert\leqslant C
and
\lVert V\rVert=1
, and
UTV=I_{Y}
by definition.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

We shall also require the counterpart of
Lemma

2.15
for
Y=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{E}
, where
E=c_{0}
or
E=\ell_{1}
. Clearly
Y
has a bimonotone basis, but it does not satisfy (
2.2
). One could set up a more abstract, unified framework which covers both these spaces and those in
Lemma

2.15
. However, we find it more instructive to explain directly how to modify the proof of
Lemma

2.15
to obtain the desired conclusion for
Y=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{E}
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
L_{n}\in\mathscr{B}(\ell_{2}^{n+1},\ell_{2}^{n})
and
R_{n}\in\mathscr{B}(\ell_{2}^{n},\ell_{2}^{n+1})
by

L_{n}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n+1})=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}),\qquad R_{n}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n},0)\qquad(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n+1}\in\mathbb{K}),

and then define
L,R\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
by

Ly=(L_{n}y_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\qquad Ry=(0,R_{1}y_{1},R_{2}y_{2},\ldots)\qquad(y=(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in Y).

As in the proof of
Lemma

2.15
, these operators satisfy that
R
is an isometry,
L
has norm
1
,
L^{n}R^{n}=I_{Y}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
L^{n}S\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for every
S\in\mathscr{K}(Y)
. Using these facts, we can now repeat the final part of the argument verbatim: Let
C>1
, and suppose that
T\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
is an operator for which
S=I_{Y}-T
is compact. Then, choosing
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lVert L^{n}S\rVert\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{C}
, we have

\lVert I_{Y}-L^{n}TR^{n}\rVert=\lVert L^{n}SR^{n}\rVert\leqslant\lVert L^{n}S\rVert\,\lVert R\rVert^{n}\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{C},

so the operator
L^{n}TR^{n}
is invertible, and
\lVert(L^{n}TR^{n})^{-1}\rVert\leqslant C
. Hence the operators
U=(L^{n}TR^{n})^{-1}L^{n}\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
and
V=R^{n}\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
satisfy
UTV=I_{Y}
and
\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert\leqslant C
, as desired.

Remark 2.17
.

The closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
have been classified for several other Banach spaces
X
than those specified in
Theorem

1.1
. All of these Banach spaces originate from the famous Argyros–Haydon space
X_{{\text{AH}}}
, which was created to solve the scalar-plus-compact problem
[
1
]
. We do not know whether
\mathscr{B}(X_{{\text{AH}}})
has a unique algebra norm because we do not know whether every homomorphism from
\mathscr{B}(X_{{\text{AH}}})
into a Banach algebra is continuous. This question is also open for the variants of
X_{{\text{AH}}}
whose closed operator ideals have been classified in
[
48
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
28
, Theorem 1.4]
, respectively. (We shall consider the latter Banach space in more detail in
Example

2.20
below.)

The situation is even more intriguing for the family of Banach spaces
X_{K}
, where
K
is an infinite compact metric space, having the property that the Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(X_{K})/\mathscr{K}(X_{K})
is isometrically isomorphic to the Banach algebra
C(K)
of continuous, scalar-valued functions on
K
. A Banach space
X_{K}
with this property was originally constructed by Motakis, Puglisi and Zizimopoulou
[
40
]
in the case where
K
is countably infinite, while Motakis
[
39
]
has subsequently found a new approach which handles the general case.

It is undecidable in
ZFC
whether every homomorphism from
C(K)
into a Banach algebra is continuous; more precisely, Dales
[
7
]
and Esterle
[
14
]
independently proved that discontinuous homomorphisms from
C(K)
exist under the Continuum Hypothesis, whereas Solovay and Woodin constructed a different model of
ZFC
in which all such homomorphisms are continuous; see
[
9
]
for an exposition of this result. Consequently it is also undecidable in
ZFC
whether every homomorphism from
\mathscr{B}(X_{K})
into a Banach algebra is continuous. Note that we have a complete classification of the closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X_{K})
, as described in
[
28
, Remark 1.5(vi)]
and
[
39
, Proposition 8.6]
.

We conclude this section with three examples which use results from the literature to illustrate how quotient algebras of
\mathscr{B}(X)
may fail to possess certain combinations of the properties we consider. The first two examples concern the
weak Calkin algebra,
that is, the quotient
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{W}(X)
, where
\mathscr{W}(X)
denotes the ideal of weakly compact operators on
X
.

Example 2.18
.

Let
X
be a Banach space, identified with its canonical image in the bidual
X^{**}
. Since an operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)
is weakly compact if and only if
T^{**}[X^{**}]\subseteq X
, the formula

\varphi(T+\mathscr{W}(X))(x^{**}+X)=T^{**}(x^{**})+X\qquad(x^{**}\in X^{**},\,T\in\mathscr{B}(X))

defines an injective and contractive homomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{W}(X)\to\mathscr{B}(X^{**}/X)
, which is non-zero if and only if
X
is non-reflexive. González, Saksman and Tylli studied this homomorphism in
[
18
]
, showing in particular that when
X=(J_{2}\oplus J_{2}\oplus\cdots)_{\ell_{2}}
is the
\ell_{2}
-sum of countably many copies of James’ quasi-reflexive Banach space
J_{2}
, the range of
\varphi
is not closed
[
18
, Theorem 2.6]
. Hence the weak Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{W}(X)
fails to be incompressible in this case. Clearly
X\cong X\oplus X
, so
Proposition

2.4
and
Theorem

2.5
imply that the quotient norm on
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{W}(X)
is maximal.

Our second example involves the following piece of notation. For a Banach space
E
, we write
E^{\sim}
for the Banach algebra which is the unitization of
E
equipped with the trivial product; that is,
E^{\sim}=E\times\mathbb{K}
as a vector space, with the norm and product defined by

\lVert(x,s)\rVert=\lVert x\rVert+\lvert s\rvert\qquad\text{and}\qquad(x,s)(y,t)=(sy+tx,st)\qquad(x,y\in E,\,s,t\in\mathbb{K}),

respectively. Given a linear map
\theta\colon E\to F
into some Banach space
F
, we define its unitization
\widetilde{\theta}\colon E^{\sim}\to F^{\sim}
by
\widetilde{\theta}(x,s)=(\theta(x),s)
for
x\in E
and
s\in\mathbb{K}
. This is a unital homomorphism which is continuous if and only if
\theta
is continuous.

Example 2.19
.

Let
X_{\text{R}}
denote the Banach space which Read constructed in
[
45
]
with the following remarkable property: the weak Calkin algebra of
X_{\text{R}}
is isomorphic as a Banach algebra to the unitization
\ell_{2}^{\sim}
of the Hilbert space
\ell_{2}
equipped with the trivial product. (In fact,
X_{{\text{R}}}
has a slightly stronger property, namely: there exists a continuous, surjective homomorphism
\psi\colon\mathscr{B}(X_{{\text{R}}})\to\ell_{2}^{\sim}

with
\ker\psi=\mathscr{W}(X_{{\text{R}}})
such that the extension

\textstyle{\{0\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathscr{W}(X_{{\text{R}}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathscr{B}(X_{{\text{R}}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\displaystyle{\psi}}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}^{\sim}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\{0\}}

splits in the category of Banach algebras, as shown in
[
33
, Theorem 1.2]
.)

We claim that the norm on
\ell_{2}^{\sim}
is neither maximal nor incompressible, and hence the same is true for the weak Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(X_{{\text{R}}})/\mathscr{W}(X_{{\text{R}}})
.

To see that the norm on
\ell_{2}^{\sim}
fails to be maximal, we must show that there exists a discontinuous homomorphism from
\ell_{2}^{\sim}
into a Banach algebra. This is well known and not difficult to verify. Indeed, since
\ell_{2}
is infinite-dimensional, it admits a discontinuous linear functional
\theta\colon\ell_{2}\to\mathbb{K}
, so its unitization
\widetilde{\theta}\colon\ell_{2}^{\sim}\to\mathbb{K}^{\sim}
is a discontinuous homomorphism.

To verify that
\ell_{2}^{\sim}
fails to be incompressible, take
p\in(2,\infty)
and consider the unitization
\widetilde{\varphi}\colon\ell_{2}^{\sim}\to\ell_{p}^{\sim}
of the formal inclusion map
\varphi
from
\ell_{2}
into
\ell_{p}
. This is a continuous, injective homomorphism with dense range, but it is not surjective, so it cannot be bounded below. (In fact, it is strictly singular, as observed in
[
34
, page 76]
, for instance.)

Our third and final example displays a Calkin algebra which is incompressible without being uniformly incompressible.

Example 2.20
.

This example is based on the Banach space
Z
studied in
[
28
]
. It is given by

(2.4)

Z=X_{{\text{AH}}}\oplus_{\infty}Y,

where
X_{{\text{AH}}}
denotes the Banach space of Argyros and Haydon already mentioned in
Remark

2.17
,
Y
is a certain closed, infinite-dimensional subspace of
X_{{\text{AH}}}
constructed in
[
28
, Theorem 1.2]
, and the subscript
\infty
indicates that we equip
Z
with the norm

\lVert(x,y)\rVert=\max\{\lVert x\rVert,\lVert y\rVert\}\qquad(x\in X_{{\text{AH}}},\,y\in Y).

The key property of
Z
that we require is that,
according to
[
28
, Equation (1.2)]
, every operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(Z)
has the form

(2.5)

T=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1,1}I_{X_{{\text{AH}}}}&\alpha_{1,2}J\\
0&\alpha_{2,2}I_{Y}\end{pmatrix}+S,

where
\alpha_{1,1},\alpha_{1,2},\alpha_{2,2}\in\mathbb{K}
,

J\colon Y\to X_{{\text{AH}}}
denotes the inclusion map and
S\in\mathscr{K}(Z)
. (We note in passing that
Z
has a basis, so the ideal of finite-rank operators is dense in
\mathscr{K}(Z)
.)

Johnson and Phillips
[
26
]
observed that the subalgebra

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}:\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{K}\right\}

of the algebra
M_{2}(\mathbb{K})
of scalar-valued
2\times 2
matrices, equipped with the spectral norm, is incompressible, but not uniformly incompressible. Building on this example, we shall show that the same conclusion holds true for the Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(Z)/\mathscr{K}(Z)
.

First, (
2.5
) shows that
\mathscr{B}(Z)/\mathscr{K}(Z)
is finite-dimensional and therefore incompressible.

Second, we observe that the scalars
\alpha_{1,1}
,
\alpha_{1,2}
and
\alpha_{2,2}
in (
2.5
) are uniquely determined by
T
because the operators
I_{X_{{\text{AH}}}}
,
J
and
I_{Y}
are non-compact, so for each
\delta\in(0,1)
, we can define a map
\varphi_{\delta}\colon\mathscr{B}(Z)\to M_{2}(\mathbb{K})
by

\varphi_{\delta}(T)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1,1}&\delta\alpha_{1,2}\\
0&\alpha_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}.

Straightforward calculations show that
\varphi_{\delta}
is a unital homomorphism. Furthermore,
\varphi_{\delta}
is continuous with norm
1
provided that we equip
M_{2}(\mathbb{K})
with the norm induced by identifying it with the Banach algebra
\mathscr{B}(\ell_{\infty}^{2})
, that is,

\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1,1}&\alpha_{1,2}\\
\alpha_{2,1}&\alpha_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert=\max\bigl\{\lvert\alpha_{1,1}\rvert+\lvert\alpha_{1,2}\rvert,\lvert\alpha_{2,1}\rvert+\lvert\alpha_{2,2}\rvert\bigr\}.

Since
\ker\varphi_{\delta}=\mathscr{K}(Z)
, the Fundamental Isomorphism Theorem implies that
\varphi_{\delta}
induces a continuous, injective, unital homomorphism
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\delta}\colon\mathscr{B}(Z)/\mathscr{K}(Z)\to M_{2}(\mathbb{K})
, also of norm
1
, by the formula
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\delta}(T+\mathscr{K}(Z))=\varphi_{\delta}(T)
.

Consequently, if
\mathscr{B}(Z)/\mathscr{K}(Z)
were uniformly incompressible, there would be a constant
\eta>0
such that
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\delta}
is bounded below by
\eta
for every
\delta\in(0,1)
. However, we have

\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}0&J\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\mathscr{K}(Z)\right\rVert=\lVert J+\mathscr{K}(Y,X_{{\text{AH}}})\rVert=1

because
Y
is infinite-dimensional, and therefore

\eta\leqslant\left\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}_{\delta}\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&J\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\mathscr{K}(Z)\right)\right\rVert=\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}0&\delta\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert=\delta\qquad(\delta\in(0,1)),

contradicting that the same constant
\eta>0
should work for every
\delta\in(0,1)
. This contradiction proves that the Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(Z)/\mathscr{K}(Z)
fails to be uniformly incompressible.

Proposition

2.12
implies that
\mathscr{B}(Z)/\mathscr{K}(Z)
cannot have the
C
-IFP for any
C\geqslant 1
. This is also clear from the fact that the inclusion operator
J
is inessential, as observed in
[
28
]
, because every inessential, idempotent operator has finite rank.

A similar conclusion can be drawn for the Banach space
X=\ell_{p}\oplus\ell_{q}
for
1\leqslant p<q<\infty
. As already mentioned on page
(ii)
, Ware
[
51
, Corollary 5.2.3]
showed that the Calkin algebra of
X
has a unique algebra norm and is therefore incompressible. However,
\mathscr{B}(X)
contains many non-compact operators which no idempotent operator of infinite rank factors through because
[
15
, Corollaries 9 and 12]
shows that there are
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
many closed ideals between the ideals of compact and strictly singular operators on
X
, and every strictly singular, idempotent operator has finite rank. Hence the Calkin algebra of
X
cannot have the
C
-IFP for any
C\geqslant 1
.

3.
A quantitative factorization result for the identity on
c_{0}

The aim of this section is to establish the following characterization of when the identity operator on
c_{0}
factors through an operator defined on a Banach space of the form
C_{0}(K)
for a locally compact Hausdorff space
K
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K),Y)
, where
K
is a locally compact Hausdorff space and
Y
is a Banach space for which the unit ball of
Y^{*}
is weak* sequentially compact, and let
C>0
. Then
C_{0}(K)
contains a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(3.1)

\sup_{k\in K}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lvert f_{n}(k)\rvert\leqslant 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert Tf_{n}\rVert>\frac{1}{C}

if and only if there are operators
U\in\mathscr{B}(Y,c_{0})
and
V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},C_{0}(K))
such that

(3.2)

UTV=I_{c_{0}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert<C.

Our main application of
Theorem

3.1
will be in
Section

6
, where it will provide the foundation for the proof of
Theorem

1.1
(iii)
. However, a simple special case of it will also help us establish
Theorem

1.1
(ii)
, which is why we state and prove it at this point.

To aid the presentation of the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, we have split it into a number of simpler statements, beginning with one which explains the significance of the left-hand part of (
3.1
). For an index set
\Gamma
, we write
(e_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
for the unit vector basis of
c_{0}(\Gamma)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
C_{0}(K)
for some locally compact Hausdorff space
K
. There is an operator
V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},C_{0}(K))
such that
Ve_{n}=f_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if

(3.3)

\sup_{k\in K}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lvert f_{n}(k)\rvert<\infty.

If one, and hence both, of these conditions are satisfied, the norm of the operator
V
is equal to the supremum (
3.3
).

Proof.

Let
C\in[0,\infty]
denote the supremum in (
3.3
), and observe that

C=\sup\biggl\{\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lvert f_{n}(k)\rvert:k\in K,\,m\in\mathbb{N}\biggr\}.

\Rightarrow
. Suppose that
V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},C_{0}(K))
is an operator with
Ve_{n}=f_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and take
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in K
. For each
n\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, choose
\sigma_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
such that
\lvert\sigma_{n}\rvert=1
and
\sigma_{n}f_{n}(k)\geqslant 0
. Then
x=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\sigma_{n}e_{n}\in c_{0}
has norm
1
, so

\lVert V\rVert\geqslant\lVert Vx\rVert_{\infty}=\biggl\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\sigma_{n}f_{n}\biggr\rVert_{\infty}\geqslant\biggl\lvert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\sigma_{n}f_{n}(k)\biggr\rvert=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\sigma_{n}f_{n}(k)=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lvert f_{n}(k)\rvert.

\Leftarrow

\biggl\lVert V\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{n}e_{n}\Bigr)\biggr\rVert_{\infty}=\biggl\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{n}f_{n}\biggr\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{k\in K}\biggl\lvert\sum_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{n}f_{n}(k)\biggr\rvert\leqslant\sup_{k\in K}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lvert\alpha_{n}\rvert\,\lvert f_{n}(k)\rvert\leqslant C.

Hence

Theorem 3.3

(Rosenthal)

Let

Rosenthal stated the result without specifying for which values of

, Theorem 5.5(iii)]
. (The bounds on the norms of the operators
U
and
V
are not stated explicitly in
[
31
]
, but follow from the proof of the result.)

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)
be an operator with locally finite matrix, and suppose that the set
\{m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}T):j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then there are operators
U,V\in\mathscr{B}(X)
with
\lVert U\rVert\leqslant 1/\varepsilon
and
\lVert V\rVert\leqslant 1
such that
UTV=I_{X}
.

In order to apply this result, we require specific values of
\varepsilon>0
for which the set
\{m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}T):j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded. Our next lemma addresses this point.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)\setminus\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{c_{0}}(X)
be an operator with locally finite matrix, and suppose that the set
\{m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}T):j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is bounded for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then
\varepsilon\geqslant\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{c_{0}}(X)\rVert
.

Consequently the set
\{m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}T):j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded whenever
0<\varepsilon<\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{c_{0}}(X)\rVert
.

Proof.

Set
m=\sup\{m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}T):j\in\mathbb{N}\}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Our proof uses the machinery from
[
31
, Construction 4.2]
, so we begin by introducing the necessary notation. For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, define
N_{j}=\{k\in\mathbb{N}:T_{j,k}\neq 0\}
, which is finite, and
B_{j}=\bigl(\bigoplus_{k\in N_{j}}\ell_{2}^{k}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
, and
let
L_{j}\colon B_{j}\to X
be the natural inclusion map. Furthermore, set
B=\bigl(\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{N}}B_{j}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
and
\widetilde{T}=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{j}TL_{j}\in\mathscr{B}(B,X)
. Then, as shown in
[
31
, Construction 4.2]
, there exists an operator
V\in\mathscr{B}(X,B)
with
\lVert V\rVert=1
such that
T=\widetilde{T}V
.

The convention described after
Definition

4.3
means that
m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}TL_{j})=m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}T)\leqslant m
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore we can find orthogonal projections
P_{j,k}\in\mathscr{B}(\ell_{2}^{k})
for
k\in N_{j}
, each having rank at most
m+1
, such that

(4.3)

\Bigl\lVert Q_{j}TL_{j}\circ\bigoplus_{k\in N_{j}}(I_{\ell_{2}^{k}}-P_{j,k})\Bigr\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon.

Set
R_{j}=\bigoplus_{k\in N_{j}}P_{j,k}\in\mathscr{B}(B_{j})
and
R=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{N}}R_{j}\in\mathscr{B}(B)
. Since each of the projections
P_{j,k}
has rank at most
m+1
, the image of
R
embeds isometrically into a direct sum of the form
(\ell_{2}^{m+1}\oplus\ell_{2}^{m+1}\oplus\cdots)_{c_{0}}
, which in turn is isomorphic to
(\ell_{\infty}^{m+1}\oplus\ell_{\infty}^{m+1}\oplus\cdots)_{c_{0}}\equiv c_{0}
. This shows that
R
factors through
c_{0}
, and therefore the same is true for the composite operator
\widetilde{T}RV
. Hence we have

\displaystyle\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{c_{0}}(X)\lVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert T-\widetilde{T}RV\rVert=\lVert\widetilde{T}(I_{B}-R)V\rVert\leqslant\Bigl\lVert\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{j}TL_{j}(I_{B_{j}}-R_{j})\Bigr\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon,

where the final estimate follows because
\lVert V\rVert=1
and
\lVert Q_{j}TL_{j}(I_{B_{j}}-R_{j})\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
by (
4.3
). The final clause is immediate.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
for
E=c_{0}
.

Take a constant
C>1
and an operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)
with
\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{c_{0}}(X)\rVert=1
. By
[
31
, Lemma 2.7(iii)]
, we can find
S\in\mathscr{K}(X)
such that
T-S
has locally finite matrix. Since

C^{-\frac{1}{2}}<1=\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{c_{0}}(X)\rVert=\lVert T-S+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{c_{0}}(X)\rVert,

Lemma

4.5
shows that the set
\{m_{C^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(Q_{j}(T-S)):j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded. Consequently, applying
Lemma

4.4
, we can find operators
U_{1},V_{1}\in\mathscr{B}(X)
with
\lVert U_{1}\rVert\leqslant C^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\lVert V_{1}\rVert\leqslant 1
such that
U_{1}(T-S)V_{1}=I_{X}
. Since
U_{1}SV_{1}
is compact,
Remark

2.16
implies that there are operators
U_{2},V_{2}\in\mathscr{B}(X)
with
\lVert U_{2}\rVert\,\lVert V_{2}\rVert\leqslant C^{\frac{1}{2}}
such that
U_{2}(U_{1}TV_{1})V_{2}=I_{X}
. Now the conclusion follows by taking
U=U_{2}U_{1}
and
V=V_{1}V_{2}
.
∎

The case
E=\ell_{1}
.

We now turn our attention to the Banach space
X=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}}
. It identifies naturally with the dual space of the Banach space
\bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
that we considered in the previous subsection, and our proof is essentially a dual version of the proof we have just completed. As observed in
[
32
, Remark 2.13]
, the dualization is not entirely straightforward because we can no longer perturb operators to arrange that they have locally finite matrix; only finite columns can be achieved. Fortunately, that will suffice to carry out the necessary steps, beginning with the following index originally introduced in
[
32
, Definition 2.4]
, which is the dual version of
Definition

4.3
above.

Definition 4.6
.

For
\varepsilon>0
and an operator
T\in\mathscr{B}\bigl(Y,\bigl(\bigoplus_{j\in M}H_{j}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}})
, where
Y
can be any Banach space, the index set
M
is finite and
H_{j}
is a Hilbert space for each
j\in M
, we define

n_{\varepsilon}(T)=\sup\biggl\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\biggl\lVert\Bigl(\bigoplus_{j\in M}(I_{H_{j}}-P_{G_{j}})\Bigr)T\biggr\rVert>\varepsilon\ \text{for every subspace}\ G_{j}\ \text{of}\ H_{j}\biggr.\\[-11.38109pt]
\biggl.\text{with}\ \dim G_{j}\leqslant n\ \text{for each}\ j\in M\biggr\}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\pm\infty\},

where
P_{G_{j}}
denotes the orthogonal projection of
H_{j}
onto the subspace
G_{j}
, as before.

Hence
n_{\varepsilon}(T)
is the largest number of dimensions that we can remove from each of the Hilbert spaces in the codomain of
T
and still obtain an operator with norm greater than
\varepsilon
.

When
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)
has finite columns, we can apply this definition to the operator
TJ_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
by simply ignoring the Hilbert spaces
\ell_{2}^{j}
in the codomain of
TJ_{k}
corresponding to the cofinite set of indices
j\in\mathbb{N}
for which
Q_{j}TJ_{k}=0
. Then, as one would hope, the quantities
n_{\varepsilon}(TJ_{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
measure how close the identity operator on
X
is to factoring through
T
. More precisely, we have the following counterpart of
Lemma

4.4
, originally proved as part of
[
32
, Proposition 2.10]
. (The norm bounds on
U
and
V
are not stated explicitly in
[
32
]
, but follow easily from the proof of the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii).)

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)
be an operator with finite columns, and suppose that the set
\{n_{\varepsilon}(TJ_{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then there are operators
U,V\in\mathscr{B}(X)
with
\lVert U\rVert\leqslant 1/\varepsilon
and
\lVert V\rVert\leqslant 1
such that
VTU=I_{X}
.

Our next lemma serves the same purpose as
Lemma

4.5
, and its proof is similar.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)\setminus\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{\ell_{1}}(X)
be an operator with finite columns, and suppose that
\sup\{n_{\varepsilon}(TJ_{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is finite for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then
\varepsilon\geqslant\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{\ell_{1}}(X)\rVert
.

Consequently the set
\{n_{\varepsilon}(TJ_{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded whenever
0<\varepsilon<\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{\ell_{1}}(X)\rVert
.

Proof.

Set
n=\sup\{n_{\varepsilon}(TJ_{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The set
M_{k}=\{j\in\mathbb{N}:T_{j,k}\neq 0\}
is finite for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
because
T
has finite columns. Define
Y_{k}=\bigl(\bigoplus_{j\in M_{k}}\ell_{2}^{j}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}}
, and
let
L_{k}\colon Y_{k}\to X
and
S_{k}\colon X\to Y_{k}
denote the natural inclusion map and projection, respectively. Furthermore, define
Y=\bigl(\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}Y_{k}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}}
and
\widetilde{T}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}S_{k}TJ_{k}\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
. Then, for each element
y=(y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in Y
, the series
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}L_{k}y_{k}
converges absolutely in
X
, and the operator
W\in\mathscr{B}(Y,X)
given by
Wy=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}L_{k}y_{k}
satisfies
\lVert W\rVert=1
and
T=W\widetilde{T}
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}(TJ_{k})=n_{\varepsilon}(S_{k}TJ_{k})
, so we can find orthogonal projections
P_{j,k}\in\mathscr{B}(\ell_{2}^{j})
for
j\in M_{k}
, each having rank at most
n+1
, such that

(4.4)

\biggl\lVert\Bigl(\bigoplus_{j\in M_{k}}(I_{\ell_{2}^{j}}-P_{j,k})\Bigr)S_{k}TJ_{k}\biggr\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon.

Set
R_{k}=\bigoplus_{j\in M_{k}}P_{j,k}\in\mathscr{B}(Y_{k})
and
R=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}R_{k}\in\mathscr{B}(Y)
, and observe that
R
factors through
\ell_{1}
because the image of
R
embeds isometrically into
(\ell_{2}^{n+1}\oplus\ell_{2}^{n+1}\oplus\cdots)_{\ell_{1}}
, which is isomorphic to
(\ell_{1}^{n+1}\oplus\ell_{1}^{n+1}\oplus\cdots)_{\ell_{1}}\equiv\ell_{1}
. Consequently we have

\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{\ell_{1}}(X)\rVert\leqslant\lVert T-WR\widetilde{T}\rVert=\lVert W(I_{Y}-R)\widetilde{T}\rVert\leqslant\lVert W\rVert\,\Bigl\lVert\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(I_{Y_{k}}-R_{k})S_{k}TJ_{k}\Bigr\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon

by (
4.4
), as required. The final clause is immediate.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
for
E=\ell_{1}
.

Take
C>1
and
T\in\mathscr{B}(X)
with
\lVert T+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{\ell_{1}}(X)\rVert=1
, and apply
[
31
, Lemma 2.7(i)]
to find
S\in\mathscr{K}(X)
such that
T-S
has finite columns. Then
C^{-\frac{1}{2}}<1=\lVert T-S+\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{\ell_{1}}(X)\rVert
, and using Lemmas
4.8
and
4.7
as well as
Remark

2.16
, we can complete the proof in the same way as we did for

above.
∎

5.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
(ii)

The aim of this section is to show that every quotient of
\mathscr{B}(X)
by one of its closed ideals has a unique algebra norm for each of the Banach spaces

(5.1)

X=\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\Bigr)_{c_{0}}\oplus c_{0}(\Gamma)\qquad\text{and}\qquad X=\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\Bigr)_{\ell_{1}}\oplus\ell_{1}(\Gamma),

where the index set
\Gamma
is an uncountable cardinal number. As before, it suffices to show that the quotient of
\mathscr{B}(X)
by each of its non-trivial closed ideals is incompressible, and in line with our previous approach, we shall in fact prove the stronger result that each of these quotients has the
C
-IFP for some
C>1
; see
Theorem

5.4
and
Corollary

5.6
for details.

The closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
were classified in
[
2
]
. Unsurprisingly, this result relies heavily on the ideal classifications for each of the two summands of
X
. The first of these played the main role in
Section

4
and was stated explicitly in (
4.1
).
Let us now review the second. The following definition is at the heart of it.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal number.
An operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
between Banach spaces
X
and
Y
is
\kappa
-compact
if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the closed unit ball
B_{X}
of
X
contains a subset
X_{\varepsilon}
with
\lvert X_{\varepsilon}\rvert<\kappa
such that

\inf\bigl\{\lVert T(x-y)\rVert:y\in X_{\varepsilon}\bigr\}\leqslant\varepsilon\qquad(x\in B_{X}).

We write
\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X,Y)
for the subset of
\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
consisting of
\kappa
-compact operators. This is a closed operator ideal. The name “
\kappa
-compact operator” refers to the fact that it extends the usual notion of compactness for operators (which corresponds to
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
) to uncountable cardinal numbers. We call the quotient algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X)
the
\kappa
-Calkin algebra
.

Generalizing a theorem of Gramsch
[
19
]
and Luft
[
36
]
in the non-separable Hilbertian case, Daws
[
10
, Theorem 7.4]
showed that the lattice of closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
for each of the long sequence spaces
X=c_{0}(\Gamma)
and
X=\ell_{p}(\Gamma)
, where
1\leqslant p<\infty
and the index set
\Gamma
is an uncountable cardinal number, is given by

(5.2)

\begin{split}\{0\}\subsetneq\mathscr{K}(X)=\mathscr{K}_{\aleph_{0}}(X)\subsetneq\mathscr{K}_{\aleph_{1}}(X)\subsetneq&\cdots\subsetneq\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X)\subsetneq\mathscr{K}_{\kappa^{+}}(X)\subsetneq\cdots\\
&\cdots\subsetneq\mathscr{K}_{\Gamma}(X)\subsetneq\mathscr{K}_{\Gamma^{+}}(X)=\mathscr{B}(X),\end{split}

where
\kappa^{+}
denotes the cardinal successor of a cardinal number
\kappa
. Johnson, Kania and Schechtman
[
25
, Theorem 1.5]
gave an alternative statement and proof of this result, with additional details provided in
[
22
, Theorem 3.7]
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be Banach spaces. As in Notation
4.2
, we equip their direct sum
X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
with the
E
-norm, where
E=c_{0}
or
E=\ell_{1}
, and for
m\in\{1,2\}
,
J_{m}\colon X_{m}\to X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
and
Q_{m}\colon X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\to X_{m}
denote the
m^{\text{th}}
coordinate embedding and projection, respectively.

For a subset
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})
and
j,k\in\{1,2\}
, we define the
(j,k)^{\text{th}}

quadrant
of
\mathscr{I}
by

\mathscr{I}_{j,k}=\{Q_{j}TJ_{k}:T\in\mathscr{I}\}\subseteq\mathscr{B}(X_{k},X_{j}).

Conversely, given subsets
\mathscr{I}_{j,k}
of
\mathscr{B}(X_{k},X_{j})
for
j,k\in\{1,2\}
, we set

\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{I}_{1,1}&\mathscr{I}_{1,2}\\
\mathscr{I}_{2,1}&\mathscr{I}_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}T_{1,1}&T_{1,2}\\
T_{2,1}&T_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}:T_{j,k}\in\mathscr{I}_{j,k}\ \bigl(j,k\in\{1,2\}\bigr)\right\}\subseteq\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2}).

The significance of this notation is due to
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
, which states that

(5.3)

\mathscr{I}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{I}_{1,1}&\mathscr{I}_{1,2}\\
\mathscr{I}_{2,1}&\mathscr{I}_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}

for every ideal
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})
, and
\mathscr{I}_{j,j}
is an ideal of
\mathscr{B}(X_{j})
for each
j\in\{1,2\}
. Furthermore,
\mathscr{I}_{j,k}
is closed in
\mathscr{B}(X_{k},X_{j})
for each
j,k\in\{1,2\}
if and only if
\mathscr{I}
is closed in
\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})
.

Now, to state the classification of the closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
for each of the Banach spaces
X
given by (
5.1
) in a unified way and align our notation with that used in the previous paragraph, set
(E,E_{\Gamma})=(c_{0},c_{0}(\Gamma))
or
(E,E_{\Gamma})=(\ell_{1},\ell_{1}(\Gamma))
, where
\Gamma
is an uncountable cardinal number, and observe that

(5.4)

X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\qquad\text{for}\qquad X_{1}=\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ell_{2}^{n}\Bigr)_{E}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X_{2}=E_{\Gamma}.

In this notation, the main result of
[
2
]
states that the non-trivial closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
are

(5.5)

\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X)\quad(\aleph_{0}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\Gamma)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{J}_{\kappa}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{E}(X_{1})&\mathscr{B}(X_{2},X_{1})\\
\mathscr{B}(X_{1},X_{2})&\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X_{2})\end{pmatrix}\quad(\aleph_{1}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\Gamma^{+}).

We split the proof that the quotient of
\mathscr{B}(X)
by each of these ideals has the
C
-IFP for some
C>1
in two cases, beginning with those ideals that are strictly larger than the ideal of compact operators (which we recall is labelled
\mathscr{K}_{\aleph_{0}}(X)
in the list above). This relies on the following elementary general result.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be Banach spaces, and suppose that
\mathscr{I}
is a closed ideal of
\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})
which satisfies:

(i)

There are constants
C_{1},C_{2}\geqslant 1
such that
\mathscr{B}(X_{j})/\mathscr{I}_{j,j}
has the
C_{j}
-IFP for
j\in\{1,2\}
.

(ii)

\mathscr{I}_{j,k}=\mathscr{B}(X_{k},X_{j})
whenever
j,k\in\{1,2\}
are distinct.

Then
\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})/\mathscr{I}
has the
C
-IFP for
C=\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
.

Proof.

Take
T=(T_{j,k})_{j,k=1}^{2}\in\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})
with
\lVert T+\mathscr{I}\rVert=1
. Condition
(ii)
implies that
\lVert T+\mathscr{I}\rVert=\max\bigl\{\lVert T_{1,1}+\mathscr{I}_{1,1}\rVert,\lVert T_{2,2}+\mathscr{I}_{2,2}\rVert\bigr\}
, so we can choose
j\in\{1,2\}
such that
\lVert T_{j,j}+\mathscr{I}_{j,j}\rVert=1
. Now condition
(i)
shows that there are operators
R,S\in\mathscr{B}(X_{j})
with
\lVert R+\mathscr{I}_{j,j}\rVert\,\lVert S+\mathscr{I}_{j,j}\rVert\leqslant C_{j}
such that
RT_{j,j}S+\mathscr{I}_{j,j}
is a non-zero idempotent.

Set
U=J_{j}RQ_{j}
and
V=J_{j}SQ_{j}
. Then
U,V\in\mathscr{B}(X_{1}\oplus X_{2})
satisfy

\lVert U+\mathscr{I}\rVert\,\lVert V+\mathscr{I}\rVert=\lVert R+\mathscr{I}_{j,j}\rVert\,\lVert S+\mathscr{I}_{j,j}\rVert\leqslant C_{j}\leqslant\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\},

and

(U+\mathscr{I})(T+\mathscr{I})(V+\mathscr{I})=J_{j}RT_{j,j}SQ_{j}+\mathscr{I}
is an idempotent, which is non-zero because
RT_{j,j}S\notin\mathscr{I}_{j,j}
.
∎

In order to apply this lemma, we require the following result, which Ware
[
51
, Propositions 6.3.1–6.3.2]
observed was established in Daws’ proofs of
[
10
, Theorems 6.2 and 7.3]
. (We note in passing that we do not know whether the constant
4
in
(i)
is optimal.)

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\Gamma
and
\kappa
be uncountable cardinal numbers with
\kappa\leqslant\Gamma
. Then:

(i)

The
\kappa
-Calkin algebras of
c_{0}(\Gamma)
and
\ell_{p}(\Gamma)
for
1<p<\infty
have the
4
-IFP.

(ii)

The
\kappa
-Calkin algebra of
\ell_{1}(\Gamma)
has the
C
-IFP for every constant
C>1
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
X
be given by (
5.1
). Then, for every proper closed ideal
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{B}(X)
such that
\mathscr{K}(X)\subsetneq\mathscr{I}
, the quotient algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{I}
has the
4
-IFP and is therefore uniformly incompressible.

Proof.

Inspecting the list (
5.5
) of proper closed ideals of
\mathscr{B}(X)
which are strictly larger than
\mathscr{K}(X)
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\overline{\mathscr{G}}_{E}(X_{1})\ &\text{for}\ \mathscr{I}=\mathscr{J}_{\kappa}(X),\ \text{where}\ \aleph_{1}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\Gamma^{+},\\
\mathscr{B}(X_{1})\ &\text{for}\ \mathscr{I}=\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X),\ \text{where}\ \aleph_{1}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\Gamma,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{2,2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X_{2})\ &\text{for}\ \mathscr{I}=\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X)\ \text{or}\ \mathscr{I}=\mathscr{J}_{\kappa}(X),\ \text{where}\ \aleph_{1}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\Gamma,\\
\mathscr{B}(X_{2})\ &\text{for}\ \mathscr{I}=\mathscr{J}_{\Gamma^{+}}(X),\end{cases}

and

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{B}(X_{k},X_{j})\qquad(j,k\in\{1,2\},\,j\neq k).

Indeed, this is clear from the definition if
\mathscr{I}=\mathscr{J}_{\kappa}(X)
for some
\aleph_{1}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\Gamma^{+}
. Otherwise
\mathscr{I}=\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X)
for some
\aleph_{1}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\Gamma
, so
\mathscr{I}_{j,k}=\mathscr{K}_{\kappa}(X_{k},X_{j})
, and the above identities follow from the fact that every operator with separable range is
\aleph_{1}
-compact.

Hence Theorems
4.1
and
5.3
imply that the quotient algebras
\mathscr{B}(X_{j})/\mathscr{I}_{j,j}
for
j\in\{1,2\}
have the 4-IFP whenever they are non-zero, and (
5.6
) ensures that
Lemma

5.2
applies. It follows that
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{I}
has the 4-IFP if
\mathscr{B}(X_{1})/\mathscr{I}_{1,1}
and
\mathscr{B}(X_{2})/\mathscr{I}_{2,2}
are both non-zero. Otherwise we have
\mathscr{I}_{j,j}=\mathscr{B}(X_{j})
for
j=1
or
j=2
, and
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{I}\cong\mathscr{B}(X_{k})/\mathscr{I}_{k,k}
, where
k=2
if
j=1
and
k=1
if
j=2
, so
\mathscr{B}(X)/\mathscr{I}
also has the 4-IFP in this case.
∎

It remains to tackle the Calkin algebra of
X
. For brevity, we write
\lVert T\rVert_{e}
for the essential norm of an operator

; that is,

(5.7)

\lVert T\rVert_{e}=\lVert T+\mathscr{K}(X,Y)\rVert.

Furthermore, for a set
\Gamma
,
[\Gamma]^{<\omega}
denotes the collection of finite subsets of
\Gamma
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
X=\bigl(\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}F_{\gamma}\bigr)_{E}
and
Y=\bigl(\bigoplus_{\xi\in\Xi}G_{\xi}\bigr)_{E}
, where
E=c_{0}
or
E=\ell_{1}
, the index sets
\Gamma
and
\Xi
are infinite and
F_{\gamma}
and
G_{\xi}
are non-zero, finite-dimensional Banach spaces for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
\xi\in\Xi
.
Then, for every non-compact operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
with
\lVert T\rVert_{e}=1
and every constant
C>1
, there are operators
U\in\mathscr{B}(Y,E)
and
V\in\mathscr{B}(E,X)
such that

(5.8)

UTV=I_{E}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert\leqslant C.

Proof.

For
A\in[\Gamma]^{<\omega}
, set
R_{A}=\sum_{\gamma\in A}J_{\gamma}Q_{\gamma}\in\mathscr{B}(X)
.
This is a finite-rank projection, and both
R_{A}
and
I_{X}-R_{A}
have norm
1
(provided
A\neq\emptyset
). (In the second part of the proof, we shall also use this notation for the Banach space
Y
; this should not cause any confusion.)

In the case where
E=c_{0}
, take
\eta\in(C^{-1},1)
. Combining the density of the subspace
\operatorname{span}\{J_{\gamma}(F_{\gamma}):\gamma\in\Gamma\}
in
X
with the fact that
\lVert T(I_{X}-R_{A})\rVert\geqslant\lVert T\rVert_{e}=1
for each
A\in[\Gamma]^{<\omega}
, we can recursively construct a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of non-empty disjoint sets in
[\Gamma]^{<\omega}
such that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a unit vector
x_{n}\in\operatorname{span}\{J_{\gamma}(F_{\gamma}):\gamma\in A_{n}\}
for which
\lVert Tx_{n}\rVert>\eta
.
Since the sets
A_{n}
are disjoint and
\lVert x_{n}\rVert=1
, we can define a linear isometry
V_{1}\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},X)
by
V_{1}e_{n}=x_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We can now apply
Theorem

3.1
to the operator
TV_{1}\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},Y)
, noting that the unit vector basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
c_{0}
is a sequence which satisfies (
3.1
) because
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert TV_{1}e_{n}\rVert\geqslant\eta>1/C
, and that the unit ball of
Y^{*}\equiv\bigl(\bigoplus_{\xi\in\Xi}G_{\xi}^{*}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}}
is weak* sequentially compact, for instance because its elements have countable support, so every sequence is contained in a subspace of the form
\bigl(\bigoplus_{\xi\in\Xi_{0}}G_{\xi}^{*}\bigr)_{\ell_{1}}
for some countable subset
\Xi_{0}
of
\Xi
. It follows that there are operators
U\in\mathscr{B}(Y,c_{0})
and
V_{2}\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0})
such that
UTV_{1}V_{2}=I_{c_{0}}
and
\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V_{2}\rVert<C
, so (
5.8
) is satisfied for the operator
V=V_{1}V_{2}
.

In the case where
E=\ell_{1}
, we no longer have a Rosenthal-style result such as
Theorem

3.1
, so we build the factorization from scratch. Set
\eta=\frac{C-1}{3C}\in(0,\frac{1}{3})
. We shall require two elementary facts about operators
S\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
between
\ell_{1}
-direct sums of finite-dimensional spaces:

(5.9)

\displaystyle\lVert S\rVert_{e}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{\lVert(I_{Y}-R_{A})S\rVert:A\in[\Xi]^{<\omega}\bigr\},

(5.10)

\displaystyle\lVert S\rVert

\displaystyle=\sup\bigl\{\lVert SJ_{\gamma}w\rVert:\gamma\in\Gamma,\,w\in B_{F_{\gamma}}\bigr\}.

(In fact, (
5.9
) holds true no matter what form the domain
X
of
S
has, while (
5.10
) holds true irrespective of the codomain
Y
.)
Applying (
5.9
) to the given operator
T
with essential norm
1
, we can find
A_{0}\in[\Xi]^{<\omega}
such that

(5.11)

\lVert(I_{Y}-R_{A_{0}})T\rVert\leqslant 1+\eta.

Now, using (
5.10
) together with the fact that
\lVert(I_{Y}-R_{A})T(I_{X}-R_{B})\rVert\geqslant\lVert T\rVert_{e}>1-\eta
whenever
A\in[\Xi]^{<\omega}
and
B\in[\Gamma]^{<\omega}
,
we can recursively choose a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of disjoint sets in
[\Xi\setminus A_{0}]^{<\omega}
and a sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of distinct elements in
\Gamma
such that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a unit vector
w_{n}\in F_{\gamma_{n}}
for which the vector
y_{n}=R_{A_{n}}TJ_{\gamma_{n}}w_{n}\in Y
satisfies

(5.12)

\lVert y_{n}\rVert\geqslant 1-\eta.

Then
(J_{\gamma_{n}}w_{n})
is a sequence of disjointly supported unit vectors in
X
, so we can define a linear isometry
V\in\mathscr{B}(\ell_{1},X)
by
Ve_{n}=J_{\gamma_{n}}w_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and choose
f_{n}\in Y^{*}
of norm
1
such that
\langle y_{n},f_{n}\rangle=\lVert y_{n}\rVert
. Since
y_{n}=R_{A_{n}}y_{n}
and
\lVert R_{A_{n}}\rVert=1
, we may suppose that
R_{A_{n}}^{*}f_{n}=f_{n}
. This implies that
\langle y_{m},f_{n}\rangle=0
for
m\neq n
, and we can define an operator
U_{1}\in\mathscr{B}(Y,\ell_{1})
by
U_{1}y=\bigl(\langle y,f_{n}\rangle/\lVert y_{n}\rVert\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
; it satisfies
U_{1}y_{n}=e_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\lVert U_{1}\rVert\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\eta}
by (
5.12
).

The definition of
y_{n}
together with the fact that
A_{n}\subseteq\Xi\setminus A_{0}
means that
y_{n}
is the component of the vector
(I_{Y}-R_{A_{0}})TJ_{\gamma_{n}}w_{n}
supported on the set
A_{n}
, so by the definition of the
\ell_{1}
-norm and (
5.11
)–(
5.12
), we have

\lVert(I_{Y}-R_{A_{0}})TJ_{\gamma_{n}}w_{n}-y_{n}\rVert=\lVert(I_{Y}-R_{A_{0}})TJ_{\gamma_{n}}w_{n}\rVert-\lVert y_{n}\rVert\leqslant 1+\eta-(1-\eta)=2\eta.

Consequently the operator
U_{2}=U_{1}(I_{Y}-R_{A_{0}})TV\in\mathscr{B}(\ell_{1})
satisfies

\lVert(U_{2}-I_{\ell_{1}})e_{n}\rVert=\lVert U_{1}((I_{Y}-R_{A_{0}})TJ_{\gamma_{n}}w_{n}-y_{n})\rVert\leqslant\frac{2\eta}{1-\eta}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

This implies that
\lVert U_{2}-I_{\ell_{1}}\rVert\leqslant 2\eta/(1-\eta)<1
by the well-known counterpart of (
5.10
) for operators defined on the space
\ell_{1}
itself. Therefore
U_{2}
is invertible, and

\lVert U_{2}^{-1}\rVert\leqslant\Bigl(1-\frac{2\eta}{1-\eta}\Bigr)^{-1}=\frac{1-\eta}{1-3\eta}.

Set
U=U_{2}^{-1}U_{1}(I_{Y}-R_{A_{0}})\in\mathscr{B}(Y,\ell_{1})
. Then

by the definition of

,

\lVert U\rVert\leqslant\frac{1-\eta}{1-3\eta}\frac{1}{1-\eta}=C\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert V\rVert=1.\qed

The proof of
Theorem

Let

be the set of all infinite subsets of

, recall that
[\Gamma]^{<\omega}
denotes the set of all finite subsets of a general set
\Gamma

\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
which is

in the sense that

whenever

are distinct. Then the closed subspace

of

spanned by the indicator functions

for

and
\mathbbm{1}_{\{n\}}

is a self-adjoint subalgebra. Hence, assuming complex scalars, we see that

is isometrically isomorphic to

for a locally compact Hausdorff space

(also known as the Alexandroff–Urysohn space, the

Alternatively, one can define

explicitly as follows. As a set,

(6.1)

K_{\mathcal{A}}=\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{y_{A}:A\in\mathcal{A}\},

where
x_{n}
is an isolated point for each

, while a neighbourhood basis of

, for each

, is given by the collection of sets of the form

, where

. It follows easily from this description that

is

Our goal is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
K_{\mathcal{A}}
be the Mrówka space induced by an uncountable, almost disjoint family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
.
For every
C>4
and every non-compact operator

Consequently the Calkin algebra of

C
-IFP and is uniformly incompressible.

Before embarking on the proof of
Theorem

6.1
, let us explain how

1.1
(iii)
follows from it.

The setting is as follows: for an uncountable, almost disjoint family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, we say that

admits

if every operator on

is the sum of a scalar multiple of the identity operator and an operator which factors through

of operators factoring through

has codimension

Assuming the Continuum Hypothesis, Koszmider
[
29
]

constructed an uncountable, almost disjoint family

for which

admits few operators; a construction within

is given in
[
30
]
.
Kania and Kochanek

proved that when

admits few operators,
\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))
contains only four closed ideals, namely

(6.2)

\{0\}\subsetneq\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))\subsetneq\mathscr{G}_{c_{0}}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))\subsetneq\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}})).

This result was also obtained independently by Brooker (unpublished).

In view of (
2.1
) and (

), to prove

(iii)
, we need only establish incompressibility of the quotients
\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))/\mathscr{G}_{c_{0}}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))\cong\mathbb{K}
and
\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))/\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))
. The former is finite-dimensional, so trivially has a unique algebra norm, while
Theorem

6.1
gives the conclusion for the latter.

It remains to prove
Theorem

6.1
. Our strategy is to verify that
C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}})
contains a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
which satisfies (

) and then apply
Theorem

3.1
. We begin by explaining why the latter result applies to the space

.

Theorem 6.2
.

The unit ball of
C_{0}(K)^{*}
is weak* sequentially compact for every scattered, locally compact Hausdorff space

.

The following well-known observation will be useful in the proof.

Let

be a scattered, compact Hausdorff space. Then

contains a complemented subspace which is isomorphic to

, so

is isomorphic to its hyperplanes.

As an application of this fact, suppose that

is only

compact (as well as scattered and Hausdorff). Then its one-point compactification

is scattered. Since

is a hyperplane in

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

In view of

and the isomorphic nature of this result, we may suppose that

is compact by replacing it with its one-point compactification if necessary. Now the conclusion follows by combining two famous results: on the one hand, Hagler and Johnson
[

have shown that the unit ball of

is weak* sequentially compact for every Asplund space

∎

To facilitate the presentation of our proof of

, we introduce some more notation. Throughout the following list,
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
denotes an almost disjoint family.

•

A (finite or infinite) sequence

of scalar-valued functions, all defined on the same set

•

For a function

For

Proof of Theorem

We carry out the recursive construction in two distinct cases, depending on the norms of the restrictions of a certain family of operators to the subspace
X_{00}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{n}\}}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Case 1.
Suppose that

(6.4)

\lVert T(I-\operatorname{Proj}(F,\emptyset))|_{X_{00}}\rVert>\delta\qquad(F\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}).

In this case, it is quite easy to construct a disjoint sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying (
6.3
) inside the unit ball of
X_{00}
.

Indeed, to start the recursion, we apply (
6.4
) with
F_{0}=\emptyset
to see that
\|T|_{X_{00}}\|>\delta
, so we can select
f_{1}\in B_{X_{00}}
such that
\|Tf_{1}\|>\delta
.

Now assume recursively that, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have chosen a disjoint sequence of functions
(f_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
in
B_{X_{00}}
for which
\|Tf_{j}\|>\delta
for each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Set
F_{n}=\bigcup^{n}_{j=1}r(f_{j})
, and observe that
F_{n}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
because
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in X_{00}
. Hence we may apply (
6.4
) to find a function
g_{n+1}\in B_{X_{00}}
for which

\lVert T(I-\operatorname{Proj}(F_{n},\emptyset))g_{n+1}\rVert>\delta.

Define
f_{n+1}=(I-\operatorname{Proj}(F_{n},\emptyset))g_{n+1}\in X_{00}
. Then we have
\lVert Tf_{n+1}\rVert>\delta
,
r(f_{n+1})\subseteq\mathbb{N}\setminus F_{n}
, and
\|f_{n+1}\|\leqslant\|g_{n+1}\|\leqslant 1
by
Lemma

6.4
(iv)
, so the recursion continues.

Case 2.
Suppose that (
6.4
) fails, so that
\lVert T(I-\operatorname{Proj}(F,\emptyset))|_{X_{00}}\rVert\leqslant\delta
for some
F\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
, and set
S=T(I-\operatorname{Proj}(F,\emptyset))\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}}))
. Since
\operatorname{Proj}(F,\emptyset)
is a finite-rank operator, we have
\|S\|_{e}=\|T\|_{e}=1
.
In this case, we shall construct the disjoint sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying (
6.3
) within the unit ball of the subspace

Y_{00}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{n}\}},\,\mathbbm{1}_{U(A,\emptyset)}:n\in\mathbb{N},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\},

which is dense in
C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}})
. This argument is somewhat more involved, with the main part consisting of a recursive construction of a disjoint sequence of functions
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
B_{Y_{00}}
and a sequence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
for which

(6.5)

\|Sg_{n}\|>\delta,\quad\ D\Bigl(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}s(g_{j})\Bigr)\subseteq G_{n}\quad\ \text{and}\quad\ g_{n}=\sum_{A\in s(g_{n})}g_{n}(y_{A})\mathbbm{1}_{U(A,G_{n})}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

To begin this recursion, observe that there is a function
h_{1}\in B_{Y_{00}}
with
\|Sh_{1}\|>2\delta
because
\|S\|\geqslant\|S\|_{e}=1>2\delta
and
Y_{00}
is dense in
C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}})
. Applying
Lemma

6.5
(i)
, we can find a set
H_{1}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
such that

D(s(h_{1}))\subseteq H_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h_{1}\in\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{m}\}},\,\mathbbm{1}_{U(A,H_{1})}:m\in H_{1},\,A\in s(h_{1})\}.

Define
G_{1}=H_{1}
,
k_{1}=\operatorname{Proj}(H_{1},\emptyset)h_{1}\in B_{X_{00}}
and
g_{1}=h_{1}-k_{1}=(I-\operatorname{Proj}(H_{1},\emptyset))h_{1}\in B_{Y_{00}}
. Then
\|Sk_{1}\|\leqslant\delta
by the choice of
S
, so that

\|Sg_{1}\|\geqslant\|Sh_{1}\|-\|Sk_{1}\|>2\delta-\delta=\delta,

while the second and third identities in (
6.5
) both follow from
Lemma

6.5
(ii)
. This completes the base step.

Now assume that, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have selected disjointly supported functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in B_{Y_{00}}
and sets
G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
satisfying (
6.5
). The definition of
Y_{00}
implies that the set
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}s(g_{j})
is finite, so
Q_{n}=\operatorname{Proj}(G_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n})
is a finite-rank projection, and
\lVert S(I-Q_{n})\rVert\geqslant\lVert S\rVert_{e}=1>4\delta
. Hence we can find a function
\zeta_{n+1}\in B_{Y_{00}}
for which
\|S(I-Q_{n})\zeta_{n+1}\|>4\delta
.

By hypothesis,
D(\mathcal{A}_{n})\subseteq G_{n}
, so
Lemma

6.4
(iii)
implies that
\lVert Q_{n}\rVert=1
. Consequently the function
h_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}(I-Q_{n})\zeta_{n+1}
belongs to
B_{Y_{00}}
. With another application of
Lemma

6.5
(i)
, we produce a set
H_{n+1}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
for which
D(s(h_{n+1}))\subseteq H_{n+1}
and

h_{n+1}\in\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{m}\}},\,\mathbbm{1}_{U(A,H_{n+1})}:m\in H_{n+1},\,A\in s(h_{n+1})\}.

For every
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, we have

h_{n+1}(y_{A})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\zeta_{n+1},(I-Q_{n})^{*}\delta_{y_{A}}\rangle=0

because
Q_{n}^{*}\delta_{y_{A}}=\delta_{y_{A}}
by
Lemma

6.4
(ii)
, and therefore

s(h_{n+1})\cap\mathcal{A}_{n}=\emptyset
.
Define

\displaystyle G_{n+1}=H_{n+1}\cup D(\mathcal{A}_{n}\cup s(h_{n+1}))\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega},\qquad k_{n+1}=\operatorname{Proj}(G_{n+1},\emptyset)h_{n+1}\in B_{X_{00}},

\displaystyle g_{n+1}=h_{n+1}-k_{n+1}=(I-\operatorname{Proj}(G_{n+1},\emptyset))h_{n+1}\in B_{Y_{00}}.

Then we have
\lVert Sk_{n+1}\rVert\leqslant\delta
, which implies that

\lVert Sg_{n+1}\rVert\geqslant\lVert Sh_{n+1}\rVert-\delta=\frac{1}{2}\lVert S(I-Q_{n})\zeta_{n+1}\rVert-\delta>\frac{4\delta}{2}-\delta=\delta.

Furthermore,
Lemma

6.5
(ii)
shows that

(6.6)

s(g_{n+1})=s(h_{n+1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{n+1}=\sum_{A\in s(g_{n+1})}g_{n+1}(y_{A})\mathbbm{1}_{U(A,G_{n+1})}.

In particular, we see that
D\bigl(\bigcup^{n+1}_{j=1}s(g_{j})\bigr)\subseteq G_{n+1}
, bearing the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
in mind. Thus (
6.5
) is satisfied for
n+1
.

It remains to verify that
g_{n+1}
is disjoint from
g_{j}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Since

s(g_{n+1})\cap s(g_{j})\subseteq s(h_{n+1})\cap\mathcal{A}_{n}=\emptyset,

we need only check that
r(g_{n+1})\cap r(g_{j})=\emptyset
. Take
m\in r(g_{n+1})
, so that
m\in A\setminus G_{n+1}
for some
A\in s(g_{n+1})
by (
6.6
). Then, for each
B\in s(g_{j})
, we see that
m\notin B
because

A\cap B\subseteq D\biggl(\bigcup^{n+1}_{i=1}s(g_{i})\biggr)\subseteq G_{n+1}.

Hence the final identity in (
6.5
) for the function
g_{j}
implies that

g_{j}(x_{m})=\sum_{B\in s(g_{j})}g_{j}(y_{B})\mathbbm{1}_{U(B,G_{j})}(x_{m})=0,

and therefore
m\notin r(g_{j})
. This completes the recursive construction of the sequences
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

We conclude the proof by defining
f_{n}=(I-\operatorname{Proj}(F,\emptyset))g_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\lVert f_{n}\rVert\leqslant\lVert g_{n}\rVert\leqslant 1
by
Lemma

6.4
(iv)
,
\lVert Tf_{n}\rVert=\lVert Sg_{n}\rVert>\delta
by the left-hand part of (
6.5
), and the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is disjoint because
s(f_{n})=s(g_{n})
and
r(f_{n})\subseteq r(g_{n})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where the sequence
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is disjoint. ∎

7.
Incompressibility of the Calkin algebra of
C_{0}(K)
for scattered
K

In each of the cases we encountered in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, we deduced incompressibility from quantitative factorizations of idempotent operators, which in view of
Example

2.20
would appear to be a much stronger property than necessary. Notably, in the previous section we used
Theorem

3.1
to show that the Calkin algebra of
C_{0}(K_{\mathcal{A}})
is (uniformly) incompressible for every uncountable, almost disjoint family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
.

The aim of this section is to prove that for a scattered, locally compact Hausdorff space
K
, the idempotent factorization property is not only a sufficient, but also a necessary condition for incompressibility of the Calkin algebra of
C_{0}(K)
.
The significance of this result is that when somebody seeks to determine whether the Calkin algebra of
C_{0}(K)
is incompressible, they will know that verifying condition
(a)
below (as we did in the previous section for
K=K_{\mathcal{A}}
) is most likely the optimal route. We consider the case where
K
equals the ordinal interval
[0,\omega^{\omega})
particularly interesting.

Recall from (
5.7
) that
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{e}
denotes the essential norm.

Theorem 7.1
.

The following conditions are equivalent for a scattered, locally compact Hausdorff space
K\colon

(a)

There is a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
such that, for every non-compact operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))
with
\lVert T\rVert_{e}=1
,
C_{0}(K)
contains a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with

(7.1)

\sup_{k\in K}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lvert f_{n}(k)\rvert\leqslant 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert Tf_{n}\rVert>\delta.

(b)

There is a constant
C>1
such that, for every non-compact operator
T\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))
with
\lVert T\rVert_{e}=1
, there are operators
U\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K),c_{0})
and
V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},C_{0}(K))
with
\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert<C
such that
UTV=I_{c_{0}}
.

(c)

The Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))/\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))
is uniformly incompressible.

(d)

The Calkin algebra
\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))/\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))
is incompressible.

(e)

The algebra norm
\nu
given by (
7.2
) below dominates the essential norm in the sense that there is a constant
\eta>0
such that

\nu(T+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\geqslant\eta\lVert T\rVert_{e}\qquad(T\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))).

The proof of
Theorem

7.1
has two main ingredients. The first is
Theorem

3.1
, which was already established in
Section

3
and has the equivalence of conditions
(a)
and
(b)
as an immediate consequence. The other is the algebra norm
\nu
on the Calkin algebra of
C_{0}(K)
that appears in condition
(e)
.

To define it, we note that according to the proof of
[
27
, Proposition 5.4(ii)]
, the identity operator on
c_{0}
factors through every non-compact operator on
C_{0}(K)
whenever
K
is a scattered, locally compact Hausdorff space. (This will also follow from our results, as we shall explain in detail at the beginning of the proof of
Proposition

7.2
below.) Therefore we can define a map
\nu\colon\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))/\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))\to[0,\infty)
by

(7.2)

\nu(T+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if}\ T\in\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)),\\
\sup\biggl\{\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert}}:I_{c_{0}}-UTV\in\mathscr{K}(c_{0})\biggr\}\ &\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

As already indicated, our aim is to establish the following result.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
K
be a scattered, locally compact Hausdorff space. Then
\nu
defined by (
7.2
) is an algebra norm on
\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))/\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))
, and

(7.3)

\nu(T+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))\leqslant\lVert T\rVert_{e}\qquad(T\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))).

Remark 7.3
.

Recall from
Remark

6.3
that
C_{0}(K)
is isomorphic to
C(\alpha K)
whenever
K
is a scattered, locally compact Hausdorff space and
\alpha K
denotes its one-point compactification, which is also scattered.
Consequently, in view of the of the isomorphic invariance of
Theorem

7.1
, we could have stated it only for compact
K
without formally losing any generality. We have nevertheless chosen to state it in the locally compact case for the following reasons:

(i)

The isomorphism between
C_{0}(K)
and
C(\alpha K)
is not isometric (as the example of
c_{0}
and
c
illustrates; see
[
5
]
for details), and the constants
\delta
,
C
and
\eta
in conditions
(a)
,
(b)
and
(e)
may therefore change when passing from one space to the other. We consider the values of these constants to be of some interest in the context of quantitative factorizations.

(ii)

The present version includes the case
K=\mathbb{N}
(or in other words
C_{0}(K)=c_{0}
) explicitly, which seems natural given the role
c_{0}
plays in the result. Perhaps more importantly, when proving
Theorem

7.1
(specifically, in the proof of
Proposition

7.2
), we shall apply various lemmas to
c_{0}
as well as
C_{0}(K)
. If the latter class did not contain
c_{0}
, some statements would become more complicated.

(iii)

When we applied a precursor of
Theorem

7.1
in
Section

6
, it was for a locally compact space; the “vanishing at infinity” property was very helpful in that proof.

Before initiating the preparations for the proof of
Proposition

7.2
, let us point out that the definition of
\nu
and the proof that it is an algebra norm are heavily inspired by a similar result in
[
26
]
concerning operators that the identity operator on
\ell_{2}
factors through. Our version, which involves replacing
\ell_{2}
with
c_{0}
, is somewhat harder to prove because
c_{0}
has a much richer subspace structure than
\ell_{2}
.

We begin with a variant of a standard result about non-compact operators defined on
c_{0}
, tailored to our particular needs.
Combining it with
Lemma

3.7
, we arrive at
Corollary

7.6
, which we shall apply in a number of slightly different contexts in the proof of
Proposition

7.2
. To ensure that it covers all these applications, we have stated the exact details we require in each case, even though this means there is some redundancy in the statement.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
R\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
be a non-compact operator between Banach spaces
X
and
Y
, where
X
is
C
-isomorphic to
c_{0}
for some constant
C\geqslant 1
. Then, for each
\eta\in(0,1)
,
X
contains a
C
-complemented subspace
W
which is
C
-isomorphic to
c_{0}
and satisfies

(7.4)

(1+\eta)C\lVert R\rVert_{e}\lVert w\rVert\geqslant\lVert Rw\rVert\geqslant\frac{1-\eta}{C}\lVert R\rVert_{e}\lVert w\rVert\qquad(w\in W).

Proof.

Take an isomorphism
U\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},X)
with
\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert U^{-1}\rVert\leqslant C
, and set
S=RU\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},Y)
. Choose
T\in\mathscr{K}(c_{0},Y)
such that
\lVert S+T\rVert\leqslant(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\lVert S\rVert_{e}
. We have
TP_{n}\to T
as
n\to\infty
because the basis of
c_{0}
is shrinking, so
\lVert T(I_{c_{0}}-P_{m_{0}})\rVert\leqslant\frac{\eta}{2}\lVert S\rVert_{e}
for some
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
.

On the other hand,
\lVert S(I_{c_{0}}-P_{m})\rVert>(1-\frac{\eta}{2})\lVert S\rVert_{e}
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and therefore we can find an integer
m^{\prime}>m
and a unit vector
w\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{j}:m<j\leqslant m^{\prime}\}
such that
\lVert Sw\rVert>(1-\frac{\eta}{2})\lVert S\rVert_{e}
. Using this, we can recursively choose integers
m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots
with
m_{1}>m_{0}
and unit vectors
w_{n}\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{j}:m_{n-1}<j\leqslant m_{n}\}
such that

\lVert Sw_{n}\rVert>\Bigl(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\Bigr)\lVert S\rVert_{e}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Since the linear map on
c_{0}
determined by
e_{n}\mapsto w_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
is an isometry,
Theorem

3.3
implies that we can find a subsequence
(w_{n_{j}})
of
(w_{n})
such that the restriction of
S
to the subspace
W_{0}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{w_{n_{j}}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is bounded below by
(1-\eta)\lVert S\rVert_{e}
. Furthermore,
(w_{n_{j}})
is a normalized block basic sequence of
(e_{n})
, so
W_{0}
is isometrically isomorphic to
c_{0}
, and we can take a projection
Q
of
c_{0}
onto
W_{0}
with
\lVert Q\rVert=1
. Consequently the subspace
W=U[W_{0}]
is
C
-isomorphic to
c_{0}
and complemented in
X
via the projection
UQU^{-1}
, which has norm at most
C
.

Finally, to verify (
7.4
), take
w\in W
, and set
w_{0}=U^{-1}w\in W_{0}
. Then
Rw=Sw_{0}
and
w_{0}=(I_{c_{0}}-P_{m_{0}})w_{0}
, so

\displaystyle\lVert Rw\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert(S+T)w_{0}\rVert+\lVert T(I_{c_{0}}-P_{m_{0}})w_{0}\rVert\leqslant(1+\eta)\lVert S\rVert_{e}\lVert w_{0}\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\eta)\lVert R\rVert_{e}\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert U^{-1}\rVert\,\lVert w\rVert\leqslant(1+\eta)C\lVert R\rVert_{e}\lVert w\rVert

and

\displaystyle\lVert Rw\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\eta)\lVert S\rVert_{e}\lVert w_{0}\rVert\geqslant(1-\eta)\frac{\lVert SU^{-1}\rVert_{e}}{\lVert U^{-1}\rVert}\frac{\lVert Uw_{0}\rVert}{\lVert U\rVert}\geqslant\frac{1-\eta}{C}\lVert R\rVert_{e}\lVert w\rVert.\qed

Definition 7.5
.

Let
X
,
Y
and
Z
be Banach spaces. An operator
R\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)

fixes a copy of
Z
if there is an operator
V\in\mathscr{B}(Z,X)
such that the composite operator
RV
is bounded below.

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces for which the unit ball of
Y^{*}
is weak* sequentially compact, and let
R\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
be an operator which fixes a copy of
c_{0}
. Then, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exist a constant
\xi>0
and a closed subspace
W
of
X
such that
W
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
,

(7.5)

(1+\varepsilon)\xi\lVert w\rVert\geqslant\lVert Rw\rVert\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\xi\lVert w\rVert\qquad(w\in W),

the subspace
R[W]
is
(1+\varepsilon)
-complemented in
Y
and
(1+\varepsilon)
-isomorphic to
c_{0}
, and there are operators
U\in\mathscr{B}(Y,c_{0})
and
V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},X)
such that

(7.6)

URV=I_{c_{0}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert\leqslant\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\xi}.

Proof.

Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, choose
\eta>0
such that
\eta^{2}+2\eta\leqslant\varepsilon
. Since
R
fixes a copy of
c_{0}
, we can take a closed subspace
X_{1}
of
X
such that
X_{1}
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
and the restriction of
R
to
X_{1}
is bounded below. By James’ Distortion Theorem
[
23
]
,
X_{1}
contains a closed subspace
X_{2}
which is
(1+\eta)
-isomorphic to
c_{0}
. The restriction of
R
to
X_{2}
is non-compact because it is bounded below, so in view of
Lemma

7.4
, we can find a closed subspace
X_{3}
of
X_{2}
such that
X_{3}
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
and

(7.7)

(1+\eta)^{2}\lVert R|_{X_{2}}\rVert_{e}\lVert w\rVert\geqslant\lVert Rw\rVert\geqslant\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta}\lVert R|_{X_{2}}\rVert_{e}\lVert w\rVert\qquad(w\in X_{3}).

Now, applying
Lemma

3.7
to the restriction of
R
to
X_{3}
and with the constant
C=1+\varepsilon
, we obtain a closed subspace
Z
of
R[X_{3}]
such that
Z
is
(1+\varepsilon)
-complemented in
Y
and
(1+\varepsilon)
-isomorphic to
c_{0}
. Set
W=R^{-1}[Z]\cap X_{3}
and
\xi=\lVert R|_{X_{2}}\rVert_{e}
. Then
R
maps
W
isomorphically onto
Z
, so in particular
W
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
, and (
7.5
) follows from (
7.7
) because the choice of
\eta
implies that
1+\varepsilon\geqslant(1+\eta)^{2}
and
1-\varepsilon\leqslant(1-\eta)/(1+\eta)
. Finally the existence of the operators
U
and
V
satisfying (
7.6
) also follows from
Lemma

3.7
because the restriction of
R
to
X_{3}
is bounded below by
(1-\varepsilon)\xi
.
∎

When applying this result to
C_{0}(K)
for
K
scattered, the following variant of a classical theorem of Pełczyński will be helpful.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
K
be a scattered, locally compact Hausdorff space. An operator from
C_{0}(K)
into a Banach space is compact if and only if it is weakly compact, if and only if it does not fix a copy of
c_{0}
.

Proof.

In view of
Remark

6.3
and the isomorphic nature of this result, we may suppose that
K
is compact by replacing it with its one-point compactification if necessary.
Pełczyński
[
43
]
showed that an operator defined on a
C(K)
-space is weakly compact if and only if it does not fix a copy of
c_{0}
, and of course every compact operator is weakly compact.

To complete the proof, suppose that
R
is a weakly compact operator defined on
C(K)
. By Gantmacher’s Theorem, its adjoint
R^{*}
is also weakly compact. This implies that
R^{*}
is compact because a famous result of Rudin
[
47
]
states that its codomain
C(K)^{*}
is isomorphic to
\ell_{1}(K)
, which has the Schur property. Hence
R
is compact by Schauder’s Theorem.
∎

Finally, before proving
Proposition

7.2
, we establish an elementary lemma that will help us shorten a couple of steps in the proof.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
X
,
Y
and
Z
be infinite-dimensional Banach spaces, and suppose that the operators
R\in\mathscr{B}(X,Y)
,
T\in\mathscr{B}(Y,Z)
and
U\in\mathscr{B}(Z,X)
satisfy

(7.8)

I_{X}-UTR\in\mathscr{K}(X).

Then
R[X]
is closed and isomorphic to a closed subspace of finite codimension in
X
, and the restriction of
T
to any closed, infinite-dimensional subspace of
R[X]
is non-compact.

Proof.

Equation (
7.8
) implies that
UTR
is a Fredholm operator. Therefore
R
is an upper semi-Fredholm operator by
[
6
, Corollary 1.3.4]
, so
R
has closed range and finite-dimensional kernel. Let
W
be a closed, complementary subspace of
\ker R
; that is,
W+\ker R=X
and
W\cap\ker R=\{0\}
. Then the restriction of
R
to
W
is an isomorphism onto
R[X]
.

Suppose that
Y_{0}
is a closed subspace of
R[X]
such that
T|_{Y_{0}}
is compact, set
X_{0}=R^{-1}[Y_{0}]
, and regard the restriction
\widetilde{R}
of
R
to
X_{0}
as an operator into
Y_{0}
; it satisfies
UTR|_{X_{0}}=UT|_{Y_{0}}\widetilde{R}
, which implies that
UTR|_{X_{0}}
is compact. Hence
X_{0}
is finite-dimensional by (
7.8
), so
R[X_{0}]=Y_{0}
is also finite-dimensional.
∎

Remark 7.9
.

The same proof as above will work if one replaces
\mathscr{K}(X)
with the larger ideal of inessential operators in (
7.8
).

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

We shall apply
Corollary

7.6
several times in this proof, in each case to a non-compact operator
R
whose domain and codomain are (isomorphic to) either
c_{0}
or
C_{0}(K)
. To avoid repetition, let us once and for all state that Theorems
7.7
and
6.2

ensure that the hypotheses of
Corollary

7.6
on
R
and its codomain are satisfied in this case.

This observation shows in particular that the identity operator on
c_{0}
factors through every non-compact operator on
C_{0}(K)
, so the definition of
\nu
makes sense. Furthermore, it is clear that
\nu
is faithful and absolutely homogeneous.

In the remainder of the proof, suppose that
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K))
.

Subadditivity.
Let us begin by observing that the inequality

\nu(T_{1}+T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\leqslant\nu(T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))+\nu(T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))

is trivial if
T_{1}+T_{2}
is compact. Otherwise, for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we can find operators
U\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K),c_{0})
and
V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},C_{0}(K))
such that

(7.9)

I_{c_{0}}-U(T_{1}+T_{2})V\in\mathscr{K}(c_{0})

and

\frac{1}{\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\nu(T_{1}+T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))).

Set
R_{i}=UT_{i}V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0})
for
i=1,2
. By (
7.9
), at least one of these operators is non-compact, say
R_{1}
(relabelling them if necessary).
Therefore we can apply
Corollary

7.6
to find a constant
\xi_{1}>0
and a closed subspace
W_{1}
of
c_{0}
such that
W_{1}
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
,

(7.10)

(1+\varepsilon)\xi_{1}\lVert w\rVert\geqslant\lVert R_{1}w\rVert\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\xi_{1}\lVert w\rVert\qquad(w\in W_{1})

and
I_{c_{0}}=U_{1}R_{1}V_{1}
for some operators
U_{1},V_{1}\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0})
with
\lVert U_{1}\rVert\,\lVert V_{1}\rVert\leqslant\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\xi_{1}}
.

Now we split in two cases, beginning with the case where the restriction of
R_{2}
to
W_{1}
is non-compact. Since
W_{1}
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
, we can apply
Corollary

7.6
once more, this time to the operator
R_{2}|_{W_{1}}
, to find a constant
\xi_{2}>0
and a closed, infinite-dimensional subspace
W_{2}
of
W_{1}
such that

(7.11)

(1+\varepsilon)\xi_{2}\lVert w\rVert\geqslant\lVert R_{2}w\rVert\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\xi_{2}\lVert w\rVert\qquad(w\in W_{2})

and
I_{c_{0}}=U_{2}R_{2}V_{2}
for some operators
U_{2},V_{2}\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0})
with
\lVert U_{2}\rVert\,\lVert V_{2}\rVert\leqslant\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\xi_{2}}
.

Then, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have
I_{c_{0}}=U_{i}R_{i}V_{i}=(U_{i}U)T_{i}(VV_{i})
, so

(7.12)

\displaystyle\nu(T_{i}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{\lVert U_{i}\rVert\,\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert\,\lVert V_{i}\rVert}\geqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\xi_{i}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}\nu(T_{1}+T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))).

The left-hand inequalities in (
7.10
)–(
7.11
) and the fact that
W_{2}\subseteq W_{1}
imply that

(7.13)

(1+\varepsilon)(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})\geqslant\lVert(R_{1}+R_{2})|_{W_{2}}\rVert\geqslant\lVert(R_{1}+R_{2})|_{W_{2}}\rVert_{e}=\lVert I_{c_{0}}|_{W_{2}}\rVert_{e}=1,

where the penultimate equality follows from (
7.9
).
Adding up the estimates (
7.12
) for
i=1
and
i=2
and substituting the lower bound on
\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}
from (
7.13
) into this sum, we obtain

(7.14)

\nu(T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))+\nu(T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\geqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{3}}\nu(T_{1}+T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))).

We claim that this inequality also holds true in the case where
R_{2}|_{W_{1}}
is compact. Indeed, the estimate (
7.12
) remains valid for
i=1
, while we can modify (
7.13
) in the following way:

(1+\varepsilon)\xi_{1}\geqslant\lVert R_{1}|_{W_{1}}\rVert\geqslant\lVert R_{1}|_{W_{1}}\rVert_{e}=\lVert(R_{1}+R_{2})|_{W_{1}}\rVert_{e}=\lVert I_{c_{0}}|_{W_{1}}\rVert_{e}=1.

Now (
7.14
) follows by using the trivial lower bound
0
on
\nu(T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))
.

Since (
7.14
) holds true for arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we conclude that
\nu
is subadditive.

Submultiplicativity.
The inequality

\nu(T_{2}T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\leqslant\nu(T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\nu(T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))

is trivial if
T_{2}T_{1}
is compact.
Otherwise, for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we can find operators
U\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K),c_{0})
and
V\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},C_{0}(K))
such that

(7.15)

I_{c_{0}}-UT_{2}T_{1}V\in\mathscr{K}(c_{0})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\nu(T_{2}T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))).

Applying
Corollary

7.6
and
Lemma

7.8
twice, we obtain constants
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}>0
, closed subspaces
W_{1}\subseteq V[c_{0}]
and
W_{2}\subseteq T_{1}[W_{1}]
and operators
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathscr{B}(C_{0}(K),c_{0})
and
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathscr{B}(c_{0},C_{0}(K))
such that
T_{1}[W_{1}]
and
T_{2}[W_{2}]
are isomorphic to
c_{0}
and

(7.16)

(1+\varepsilon)\xi_{i}\lVert w\rVert\geqslant\lVert T_{i}w\rVert\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\xi_{i}\lVert w\rVert,\quad U_{i}T_{i}V_{i}=I_{c_{0}},\quad\lVert U_{i}\rVert\,\lVert V_{i}\rVert\leqslant\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\xi_{i}}

for
w\in W_{i}
and
i\in\{1,2\}
. (To explain this construction in detail, for
i=1
we apply
Corollary

7.6
to the operator
T_{1}|_{V[c_{0}]}
; this is justified because
Lemma

7.8
and the first part of (
7.15
) show that the domain
V[c_{0}]
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
and
T_{1}|_{V[c_{0}]}
is non-compact. Next, for
i=2
, we apply
Corollary

7.6
to the operator
T_{2}|_{T_{1}[W_{1}]}
; by construction its domain
T_{1}[W_{1}]
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
, and since
T_{1}[W_{1}]\subseteq T_{1}V[c_{0}]
, we can apply
Lemma

7.8
and (
7.15
) once more to deduce that
T_{2}|_{T_{1}[W_{1}]}
is non-compact.)

The second and third identity in (
7.16
) show that

(7.17)

\nu(T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\nu(T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\geqslant\frac{1}{\lVert U_{2}\rVert\,\lVert V_{2}\rVert\,\lVert U_{1}\rVert\,\lVert V_{1}\rVert}\geqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{4}}.

Set
W_{0}=V^{-1}[T_{1}^{-1}[W_{2}]\cap W_{1}]
. This is a closed subspace of
c_{0}
such that

V[W_{0}]=T_{1}^{-1}[W_{2}]\cap W_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T_{1}V[W_{0}]=W_{2}.

The latter identity implies that
W_{0}
is infinite-dimensional. Combining this with the first part of (
7.15
) and the left-hand inequality in (
7.16
), we find

1=\lVert I_{c_{0}}|_{W_{0}}\rVert_{e}=\lVert UT_{2}T_{1}V|_{W_{0}}\rVert_{e}\leqslant\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert T_{2}|_{W_{2}}\rVert\,\lVert T_{1}|_{W_{1}}\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert.

This produces a lower bound on
\xi_{1}\xi_{2}
, which we can substitute into (
7.17
) to obtain

\displaystyle\nu(T_{2}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))\nu(T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K)))

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{6}\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert V\rVert}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{3}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{6}}\nu(T_{2}T_{1}+\mathscr{K}(C_{0}(K))).

Since this inequality holds true for arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we conclude that
\nu
is submultiplicative.

Finally, the inequality (
7.3
) follows from the fact that if
I_{c_{0}}-UTV\in\mathscr{K}(c_{0})
, then

1=\lVert I_{c_{0}}\rVert_{e}=\lVert UTV\rVert_{e}\leqslant\lVert U\rVert\,\lVert T\rVert_{e}\,\lVert V\rVert.\qed

6.2

.
