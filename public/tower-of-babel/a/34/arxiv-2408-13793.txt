Abstract
. We describe a new method to reconstruct the permittivity distribution, of an object to image, from the remotely measured electromagnetic field. We propose to use the remote fields measured before and after injecting locally in the medium plasmonic nano-particles. Such a technique is known in the framework of imaging using contrast agents where, in optical imaging, the nano-particles play the role of these contrast agents. The plasmonic nano-particles are known to enjoy resonant effects, as enhancing the applied incident field, while excited at certain particular frequencies called plasmonic resonances. These resonant frequencies encode the values of the unknown permittivity at the location of the injected nano-particles. The imaging methods we propose mainly use this resonant effect. We show that the imaging functional build up from contrasting the fields before and after injecting the nano-particles, measured at one single back-scattered direction, and in an explicit band of incident frequencies, reaches its maximum values, in terms of the incident frequency, precisely at the mentioned plasmonic resonances. Such a behavior allows us to recover these plasmonic resonances from which we recover the point-wise values of the permittivity distribution. In this work, we describe the method and provide the mathematical justification of this resonant effect and its use for the optical inversion using plasmonic nano-particles as contrast agents.

Introduction and statement of the results

In quantitative optical imaging, the goal is to estimate the optical properties, as the permittivity function, from the optical signal response of the object to image after excitation with coherent or incoherent incident electromagnetic fields, see
[
7
,
30
,
48
,

,

. The optical response is measured at receivers located away from the object to image. Such inversion requires multiple emitters-receivers, i.e. many measurements, and it is known to be highly unstable
[
32
]
and hence one is unable to recover low contrasting permittivity functions using such remote measurements. To overcome such difficulties, it is proposed in the engineering literature, see

,

,

,

,
44
,
42
,

, to perturb the medium with small-scaled inhomogeneities to create such missing contrast to render the imaging more accurate. In optics, such contrast agents are given by nano-particles which enjoy appropriate contrasting properties. We have two classes of such nano-particles: dielectric and plasmonic nano-particles. The dielectric nano-particles are highly localized as they are nano-scaled and have high contrast permittivity. Under these scales, we can choose the incident frequency so that we excite the dielectric resonances which are related to the eigenvalues of the vector Newtonian operator, see
[
5
,
10
]
for a mathematical justification. Moreover, in practice, optically induced waves by dielectric nano-particles have been examined and extensively studied, see for instance
[
49
,
35
,
51
,
52
]
. The main feature of the plasmonic nano-particles is that they enjoy negative values of the real part of their permittivity if we choose incident frequencies close to the plasmonic frequencies of the nano-particle. With such negative permittivity, we can excite the plasmonic resonances which are related to the eigenvalues of the Magnetization operator, see
[
4
,
21
]
. These plasmonic nano-particles show an ability to manipulate light at the nano-scaled size due to their support for these resonant optical modes, see
[

,
38
,

,

,

,

,

[
39
]
. These optical properties of plasmonic nano-particles have been utilized in numerous applications, including bio-sensors, thermo-therapy, and solar cells, etc. To learn more on these two types of nano-particles about their common as well as different properties, the interested reader can see the references
[

To describe the material properties of these nano-particles, we use the Lorentz model where the permeability
\mu
is kept constant as the one of the homogeneous background while the permittivity has the form:

(1.1)

\epsilon_{p}(\omega)\,=\,\epsilon_{\infty}\left(1+\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{\omega^{2}_{0}-\omega^{2}-i\gamma\omega}\right)

where
\omega_{p}
is the electric plasma frequency,
\omega_{0}
is the undamped frequency, and
\gamma
is the electric damping frequency which is assumed to be small, i.e.,
0\leq\gamma\ll 1
, and its order of smallness will be discussed later, see (
3.34
). We refer
[
2
, Formula (4)]
or
[
15
, Formula (1.3)]
for the derivation of this model. It is observed that if we choose the incident frequency
\omega
so that
\omega^{2}
is larger than
\omega_{0}^{2}
, then the real part becomes negative. For such choices of the incident frequency, the nano-particle behaves as a plasmonic nano-particle. In the current work, we focus on the use of these properties for quantitative imaging. As mentioned before, these nano-particles are used as contrast agents. Few scenarios and assumptions on the distribution of the contrast agents can be considered. These scenarios are of course not exhaustive as the reality might be more complicated. Nevertheless, we state the following classes of distributions under which we can perform rigorous analysis.

(1)

The contrast agents are injected in isolation, i.e. they are injected one after another. In addition, we need to assume them well separated to insure weak multiple scattering between them.

(2)

They are injected as a cluster, i.e. all-at-once. In this case we have full strong multiple scattering between them. Here also, we can handle the following situations.

(a)

The distribution is regular, i.e. periodic or following a given density of distribution. In this case, we can expect deterministic estimations.

(b)

The distribution is random. In this case, we aim for probabilistic estimations.

The approach we propose to analyse these families of imaging modalities using contrast agents can be summarized as follows.
Contrasting the measured fields collected before and after injecting the small-agents, we propose the following solutions.

(1)

Case when we inject the contrast agents one after another, under the weak scattering assumption.

(a)

We can recover the
related resonances
. From these resonances, we derive the values of the ’high order’ coefficient (i.e. the mass density in acoustics). This idea works in the time-harmonic regimes.

(b)

We can recover the internal values of
the travel time function
. Using the Eikonal equation, we recover the speed of propagation. This idea works in time-domain regimes.

(c)

In addition, we can recover the internal values of the
total fields
generated only by the background medium. This allows to recover the lower order coefficients (as the bulk modulus in acoustics). This idea works for both the time-harmonic and the time-domain regimes.

These ideas were applied to the time-harmonic as well as the time domain imaging for acoustics, i.e. ultrasound imaging using bubble as contrast agents, see
[
25
,
46
,
47
]
.

(2)

Case when we inject the contrast agents
all-at-once
, as a cluster.

(a)

using the resonant character of the contrast-agents,
we can
linearize
the measured boundary-map, i.e. the Dirichlet-Neumann map.

(b)

then we solve the linearized inverse problem.

This idea was applied in
[

to the Calderón problem using resonant perturbations modeling the acoustic imaging with droplets as contrast agents.

V^{Inc}\left(z,d,q\right)\,=\,\left(d\times q\right)\,e^{i\,k\,z\cdot d},\quad\text{for}\quad d,q\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

we deduce, from
(\ref{SEq1})
, the following equation

(4.10)

\left(G_{k}^{\infty}\right)^{\top}(\hat{x},z)\cdot\left(\hat{x}\times q\right)\,=\,-\frac{1}{4\,\pi}\,V^{Inc}(z,-\hat{x},q)\,-\,\frac{1}{4\,\pi}\int_{\Omega}\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}^{\top}(y,z)\cdot V^{Inc}(y,-\hat{x},q)\,dy.

The total field equation should be recalled to analyze the second term on the R.H.S of the above equation. We have,

\underset{z}{\nabla}\times\underset{z}{\nabla}\times\left(V(z,\hat{x},q)\right)\,-\,k^{2}(z)\,V(z,\hat{x},q)\,=\,0,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{x},q\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

and, by knowing that
V(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\,=\,V^{s}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\,+\,V^{Inc}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
with

\underset{z}{\nabla}\times\underset{z}{\nabla}\times(V^{Inc}(z,\hat{x},q))\,-\,k^{2}\,V^{Inc}(z,\hat{x},q)\,=\,0,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

we deduce

\underset{z}{\nabla}\times\underset{z}{\nabla}\times\left(V^{s}(z,\hat{x},q)\right)\,-\,k^{2}(z)\,V^{s}(z,\hat{x},q)\,=\,\left(k^{2}(z)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,V^{Inc}(z,\hat{x},q).

The solution to the above equation will be given by

(4.11)

V^{s}(z,\hat{x},q)\,=\,\int_{\Omega}G_{k}(z,y)\,\cdot\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,V^{Inc}(y,\hat{x},q)\,dy.

Then, as we have

(4.12)

G_{k}(z,y)\;=\;G^{\top}_{k}(y,z),\quad y\neq z,

see Subsection
4.4
, we rewrite
(\ref{Equa0826})
as

(4.13)

V^{s}(z,\hat{x},q)\,=\,\int_{\Omega}G_{k}^{\top}(y,z)\cdot\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,V^{Inc}(y,\hat{x},q)\,dy.

By gathering
(\ref{Equa0817})
and
(\ref{Equa0828})
we end up with the following equation

\left(G_{k}^{\infty}\right)^{\top}(\hat{x},z)\cdot\left(\hat{x}\times q\right)\,=\,-\,\frac{1}{4\,\pi}\,V^{Inc}(z,-\hat{x},q)\,-\,\frac{1}{4\,\pi}\,V^{s}(z,-\hat{x},q)\,=\,-\,\frac{1}{4\,\pi}\,V(z,-\hat{x},q).

This proves
(\ref{MERFormula})
and justifies the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

4.2.
A-priori estimate

Proposition 4.2
.

Under the condition

3\,-\,3\,t\,-s\,-\,h\,>\,0,

we have,

(4.14)

\left\|u_{1}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}\,\lesssim\,a^{-h}\,\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}.

where the parameter
h
is such that
0<h<1
, the parameter

fulfills
\aleph\sim a^{-s}
and the parameter
t
is such that
d\sim a^{t}
.

Proof.

We assume that
D\,=\,\overset{\aleph}{\underset{j=1}{\cup}}D_{j}
with
\aleph\,\sim\,a^{-s}
. To investigate Proposition
4.2
, we recall, from
(\ref{SK})
, that the total field
u_{1}(\cdot)
is the solution to

u_{1}(x)+\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\int_{D}G_{k}(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,dy=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Now, we let
x\in D_{m}
and we rewrite the above equation as

(4.15)

\displaystyle u_{1}(x)\,

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\int_{D_{m}}G_{k}(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,dy

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\int_{D_{j}}G_{k}(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,dy\,=\,u_{0}(x),\quad x\in D_{m}.

In addition, thanks to
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
, we know that

(4.16)

G_{k}(x,y)\,=\,\frac{1}{\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\epsilon_{0}(y)}\,\underset{y}{\nabla}\,\underset{y}{\nabla}\Phi_{0}(x,y)\,+\,\Gamma(x,y),\qquad x\neq y.

Then, by plugging
(\ref{GkDecomposition})
into
(\ref{SK-appendix})
, we obtain

(4.17)

\displaystyle u_{1}(x)\,

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\nabla M_{D_{m}}\left(u_{1}(\cdot)\,\tau(\cdot)\right)(x)\,+\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\int_{D_{j}}G_{k}(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,u_{0}(x)\,-\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\int_{D_{m}}\Gamma(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,dy,\qquad x\in D_{m},

where

\tau(y)\,:=\,\frac{(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))}{\epsilon_{0}(y)},

and
\nabla M_{D_{m}}\left(\cdot\right)
is the Magnetization operator defined by
(\ref{DefNDefM})
. Furthermore, using Taylor expansion, the equation
(\ref{Equa1134})
becomes,

\displaystyle\left[I\,-\,\tau(z_{m})\,\nabla M_{D_{m}}\right]\left(u_{1}\right)(x)\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,u_{0}(x)\,-\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}(\epsilon_{0}(z_{j})\,-\,\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\cdot\int_{D_{j}}u_{1}(y)\,\,dy

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}(\epsilon_{0}(z_{j})\,-\,\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x}G_{k}(z_{m}+t(x-z_{m}),z_{j})\cdot(x-z_{m})\,dt\cdot\int_{D_{j}}u_{1}(y)\,\,dy

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}(\epsilon_{0}(z_{j})\,-\,\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,\int_{D_{j}}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{y}G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j}+t(y-z_{j}))\cdot(y-z_{j})\,dt\cdot u_{1}(y)\,\,dy

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\int_{D_{j}}G_{k}(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\epsilon_{0}(z_{m}+t(y-z_{m}))\cdot(y-z_{m})\,dt\,dy

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,\nabla M_{D_{m}}\left(u_{1}(\cdot)\,\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\tau(z_{m}+t(\cdot-z_{m}))\cdot(\cdot-z_{m})\,dt\right)(x)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\int_{D_{m}}\Gamma(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,dy,\qquad x\in D_{m}.

Besides, by scaling the above equation from the domain
D_{m}
to the domain
B
, i.e. we let
x\,=\,z_{m}\,+a\,\eta
and
y\,=\,z_{j}\,+a\,\xi
with
\eta,\,\xi\,\in\,B\,\subset\,B(0,1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left[I\,-\,\tau(z_{m})\,\nabla M_{B}\right]\left(\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right)(\eta)\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\tilde{u}_{0,m}(\eta)\,-\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,a^{3}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}(\epsilon_{0}(z_{j})\,-\,\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\cdot\int_{B}\tilde{u}_{1,j}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,a^{4}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}(\epsilon_{0}(z_{j})\,-\,\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\eta}G_{k}(z_{m}\,+\,t\,a\eta,z_{j})\cdot\eta\,dt\cdot\int_{B}\tilde{u}_{1,j}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,a^{4}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}(\epsilon_{0}(z_{j})\,-\,\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,\int_{B}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\xi}G_{k}(z_{m},\,z_{j}+t\,a\,\xi)\cdot\xi\,dt\cdot\tilde{u}_{1,j}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,a^{4}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\int_{B}G_{k}(z_{m}\,+\,a\,\eta,z_{j}\,+\,a\,\xi)\cdot\tilde{u}_{1,j}(\xi)\,\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\epsilon_{0}(z_{m}\,+\,t\,a\,\xi)\cdot\xi\,dt\,d\xi

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,a\,\nabla M_{B}\left(\tilde{u}_{1,m}(\cdot)\,\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\tau(z_{m}\,+\,t\,a\,\cdot)\cdot\,dt\right)(\eta)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\omega^{2}\,\mu\,a^{3}\,\int_{B}\Gamma(z_{m}+a\eta,z_{m}+a\xi)\cdot\tilde{u}_{1,m}(\xi)\,(\epsilon_{0}(z_{m}\,+\,a\,\xi)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,d\xi,\qquad\eta\in B,

where
\tilde{u}_{k,j}(\eta)\,:=\,u_{k}(z_{j}\,+\,a\,\eta)
, for
k\,=\,0\,,\,1
and
1\leq j\leq\aleph
. For the above equation, we successively take the inverse of the operator
\left[I\,-\,\tau(z_{m})\,\nabla M_{B}\right]
, and then the
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}
-norm on the both sides, by using the fact that
\left\|\nabla M_{B}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbb{L}^{2}(B);\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)\right)}\,=\,1
and

\left\|\left[I\,-\,\tau(z_{m})\,\nabla M_{B}\right]^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbb{L}^{2}(B);\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)\right)}\,=\,\mathcal{O}\left(a^{-h}\right),

see for instance
[
21
, Subsection 4.1]
, to obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,a^{-h}\,\Bigg[\left\|\tilde{u}_{0,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,a^{3}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\left|G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\right|\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,a^{4}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\left|\nabla G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\right|\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,a\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,a^{3}\,\left\|\int_{B}\Gamma(z_{m}+a\cdot,z_{m}+a\xi)\cdot\tilde{u}_{1,m}(\xi)\,(\epsilon_{0}(z_{m}\,+\,a\,\xi)-\epsilon_{p}(\omega))\,d\xi\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\Bigg].

In addition, knowing that
h\,<\,1
and

\Gamma(z_{m}+a\cdot,z_{m}+a\xi)\,\sim\,\Phi_{0}^{2}(z_{m}+a\cdot,z_{m}+a\xi)\,=\,a^{-2}\,\frac{1}{\left|\cdot\,-\,\xi\right|^{2}},

from singularity analysis point of view, see for instance
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,a^{-h}\,\Bigg[\left\|\tilde{u}_{0,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,a^{3}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\left|G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\right|\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,a^{4}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\left|\nabla G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\right|\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,a\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\Bigg].

Moreover, using the fact that

\left|G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\right|\,\sim\,\frac{1}{d^{3}_{mj}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\nabla G_{k}(z_{m},z_{j})\right|\,\sim\,\frac{1}{d^{4}_{mj}},

we deduce that

\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,\lesssim\,a^{-h}\,\left[\left\|\tilde{u}_{0,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\left(\frac{a^{3}}{d^{3}_{mj}}\,+\,\frac{a^{4}}{d^{4}_{mj}}\right)\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\right].

Besides, we have

\left(\frac{a^{3}}{d^{3}_{mj}}\,+\,\frac{a^{4}}{d^{4}_{mj}}\right)\,=\,\frac{a^{3}}{d^{3}_{mj}}\,\left(1\,+\,\frac{a}{d_{mj}}\right)\ \leq\,\frac{a^{3}}{d^{3}_{mj}}\,\left(1\,+\,\frac{a}{d}\right)\,=\,\frac{a^{3}}{d^{3}_{mj}}\,\left(1\,+\,a^{1-t}\right)\,=\,\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^{3}}{d^{3}_{mj}}\right),

where the last equality is due to the fact that
t\,<\,1
. Then,

\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,\lesssim\,a^{-h}\,\left[\left\|\tilde{u}_{0,m}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,a^{3}\,\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\frac{1}{d^{3}_{mj}}\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\right].

By taking the square and summing up with respect to the index
m
on the both sides, we end up with the following estimation

\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,\lesssim\,a^{-2h}\,\left[\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{0,m}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,a^{6}\,\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\frac{1}{d^{6}_{mj}}\,\sum_{j=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\right].

Since

\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\sum_{j=1\atop j\neq m}^{\aleph}\frac{1}{d^{6}_{mj}}\,\leq\,d^{-6}\,\aleph^{2}\,=\,\mathcal{O}\left(a^{-6t-2s}\right).

Then,

\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,\lesssim\,a^{-2h}\,\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{0,m}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,+\,a^{6-6t-2s-2h}\,\sum_{j=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,j}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)},

and, under the condition

3\,-\,3\,t\,-\,s\,-\,h\,>\,0,

we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{1,m}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,a^{-2h}\,\sum_{m=1}^{\aleph}\left\|\tilde{u}_{0,m}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{u}_{1}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}\,

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,a^{-h}\,\left\|\tilde{u}_{0}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)}.

This implies, by scaling back to the domain
D
,

\left\|u_{1}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}\,\lesssim\,a^{-h}\,\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}.

This proves
(\ref{Equa1359})
and completes the proof of Proposition
4.2
.

4.3.
Comprehending the formula
(\ref{Equa0805})
.

In order to finish the proof of Proposition
3.1
, it is necessary to comprehend
(\ref{Equa0805})
. Recall
(\ref{Equa0805})
that

\left(G_{k}\,-\,\Pi_{k}\right)(x,z)\,=\,\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{k}(x,y)\cdot\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(y,z)\,dy.

To give sense to the R.H.S of the above equation, we use the explicit expression of the Green dyadic function
\Pi_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
given by
(\ref{KernelP})
, to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{k}(x,y)\cdot\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(y,z)\,dy\,

\displaystyle\,-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\underset{x}{\nabla}\int_{\Omega}\underset{y}{\nabla}\,\Phi_{k}(x,y)\cdot\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(y,z)\,dy

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{k}(x,y)\,\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(y,z)\,dy,

which, by using the definition of the Magnetization operator and the Newtonian operator, see

(4.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{k}(x,y)\cdot\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(y,z)\,dy\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-\,\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x).

Now, for an arbitrary fixed point
z
, write

(4.19)

G_{k}(\cdot,z)\,=\,\Pi_{k}(\cdot,z)\,+\,\left(G_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\cdot,z),

in
\Omega
with

being a regular function, see for instance
[
21

\left(G_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\cdot,z)\,\in\,\mathbb{L}^{\frac{3}{2}-\delta}(\Omega),\quad\text{with}\;\delta\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}.

Then, using
(\ref{G=P+(G-P)})
, the equation
(\ref{G-P})
takes the following form,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{k}(x,y)\cdot\left(k^{2}(y)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,G_{k}(y,z)\,dy\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-\,\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\Pi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\Pi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\left(G_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\cdot,z)\right)(x)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\left(G_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\cdot,z)\right)(x).

The third term and the fourth term on the R.H.S are well defined, as we know that
\left(G_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\cdot,z)
is a regular function and both the Magnetization operator and the Newtonian operator are bounded from
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
to
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, with

. Consequently, based on
(\ref{IA1})
, we deduce that

(4.22)

T_{0}(x,z)\,:=-\,\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\left(G_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\cdot,z)\right)(x)+N_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\left(G_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\cdot,z)\right)(x)\;\in\;\mathbb{L}^{\frac{3}{2}-\delta}(\Omega).

Hence, to give sense of the L.H.S of
(\ref{LHSEq1})
, it is enough to study the first term and the second term on the R.H.S accordingly. To accomplish this, we set

\displaystyle T_{1}(x,z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\Pi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{KernelP})}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\nabla\nabla\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)+\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)I_{3}\right)(x).

Observe that the second term on the R.H.S is well defined as
\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)I_{3}\in\mathbb{L}^{3-\delta}(\Omega)
and
\nabla M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\cdot\right)
is bounded from
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
to
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, with
p>1
. Then,

(4.23)

T_{1,2}(\cdot,z)\;:=\;\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)I_{3}\right)\,\in\,\mathbb{L}^{3-\delta}(\Omega),

which implies,

\displaystyle T_{1}(x,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\nabla\nabla\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)\,+\,T_{1,2}(x,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\,\frac{1}{k^{2}}\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\underset{z}{\nabla}\nabla\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)\,+\,T_{1,2}(x,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\,\frac{1}{k^{2}}\underset{z}{\nabla}\,\left(\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\nabla\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)\right)\,+\,T_{1,2}(x,z).

Next, we need to analyze the regularity of the term

(4.24)

T_{1,1}(x,z)\,:=\,-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\underset{z}{\nabla}\left(\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\left(k^{2}(\cdot)\,-\,k^{2}\right)\,\nabla\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)\right).

(4.25)

\displaystyle T_{1,1,r}(x,z)\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\underset{z}{\nabla}\left(\underset{x}{\nabla}M_{\Omega}^{k}\left(\nabla\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\right)(x)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{DefNDefM})}{=}

\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\,I_{3}\,=\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\langle\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\,I_{3},e_{n}^{(j)}\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\otimes e_{n}^{(j)}(\cdot)\overset{(\ref{DefNDefMk})}{=}\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(e_{n}^{(j)}\right)(z)\otimes e_{n}^{(j)}(\cdot).

N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(\Phi_{k}(\cdot,z)\,I_{3}\right)(x)\,=\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(e_{n}^{(j)}\right)(z)\otimes N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(e_{n}^{(j)}\right)(x).

Then, by plugging
(\ref{PhikDecomposition})
into
(\ref{T11r})
, we deduce

\displaystyle T_{1,1,r}(x,z)\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\underset{x}{\nabla}\left(\underset{z}{\nabla}\left(\underset{z}{\nabla}\cdot\left(\underset{x}{\nabla}\cdot\left(N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(e_{n}^{(j)}\right)(z)\otimes N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(e_{n}^{(j)}\right)(x)\right)\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\underset{x}{\nabla}\left(\underset{z}{\nabla}\left(\underset{z}{\nabla}\cdot\left(N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(e_{n}^{(j)}\right)(z)\right)\underset{x}{\nabla}\cdot\left(N^{k}_{\Omega}\left(e_{n}^{(j)}\right)(x)\right)\right)\right),

and, using the fact that for an arbitrary vector field
F
