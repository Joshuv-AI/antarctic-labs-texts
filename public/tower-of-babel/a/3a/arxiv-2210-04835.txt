Factoring numbers with elliptic curves
Jorge Jiménez Urroz, Jacek Pomykała

Abstract
. In the present paper we provide a probabilistic polynomial time algorithm that reduces the complete factorization of any squarefree integer n n to counting points on elliptic curves modulo n n , succeeding with probability 1 − ε 1-\varepsilon , for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 .



1.
introduction

The importance of factorization is something already we all agree. Since the creation of RSA cryptosystem, and its strong consequences in society, not only mathematicians, but even the public community follow the updates in the problem with great enthusiasm. The security of our systems depend heavily in the difficulty of finding the factors of certain known integer
n
.

Despite the efforts of the best mathematicians in the area, the fastest algorithms for factoring, the general number field sieve, suceeds in finding the factors only in subexponential time which, in particular, makes nowadays extremely hard to factor numbers of
1024
digits. It would need more than
1
year of continuos working of
1,5
million computers with
2.1
Ghz of speed. And still, given the delicacy of the matter, the NIST recommends keys of
2048
bits at least since
2015
.

One way of dealing with the factorization is to find problems with equivalent difficulty, i.e. mathematical problems which solutions would give a polynomial time algorithm to factor integer numbers. The first of these instances is due to Miller
[
10
]
, who proved under GRH that factoring an integer
n
is equivalent to compute the Euler function
\varphi(n)
.

Since then, there have been many attempts to find equivalent problems. Bach, et all in
[
3
]
relate factorization of an integer
n
with the problem of counting the sum of the divisors of
n
, in
[
2
]
consider the computation of cyclotomic polynomials, and in
[
1
]
provides a probabilistic polynomial time algorithm to reduce factorization to the discrete logarithm problem on composite integers
n
. It is worth to note that in all these problems, isolating certain multiple of
(p-1)
is of interest, for some
p|n
.

In
1987
the strategy changes with the introduction of elliptic curves into the problem with the extraordinary paper by Lenstra
[
8
]
, in which he produces an algorithm to factor the integer
n
by using the number of points of an elliptic curve modulo
n
. Since then, many articles relate elliptic curves with factorization. In
[
7
]
the authors prove that counting points on elliptic curves modulo
n
is randomly computationally equivalent to factoring
n
, assuming somehow uniform distribution of the order of points of random elliptic curves over the Hasse interval. In
[
9
]
the authors relate the problem of factoring with computing the order of points on elliptic curves modulo
n
and give a random polynomial time reduction between both problems, while
[
11
]
provides an algorithm reducing in polynomial time factorization to the computation of the exponent of the group of points
E(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
for elliptic curves modulo
n
.

In
[
4
]
the authors prove a deterministic algorithm to reduce the factorization of any RSA modulus,
n
, to counting affine points on elliptic curves modulo
n
. For general squarefree the authors of
[
5
]
, among other things, give an algorithm reducing the factorization of
n
to counting points on elliptic curves modulo
n
, valid for
n
outside an exceptional set depending on the number of prime factors of
n
. For the particular case of RSA modulus this set is of size
o(x\log\log x/\log x)
. The argument is based on the idea of finding traces of elliptic curves coprime to
p+1
for some prime factor
p|n
. However, to exploit the idea, they also need to assume uniform distribution of the traces of elliptic curves, in a particular way as
p
varies.

The goal of this paper is to give a probabilistic polynomial time algorithm succesfull with probability
1-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
, valid for all squarefree integers
n
with no exception. Concretely we prove the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n
be a squarefree integer. Then, assuming GRH, counting the number of points on elliptic curves modulo
n
allows to find the complete factorization of
n
with probability bigger than
1-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

The argument also uses the idea of counting traces coprime to
p+1
where
p|n
, but instead we do not need to assume extra hypothesis on the traces. Instead, our argument uses the unconditional bounds of Lenstra for the distribution of the number of points as
E
varies randomly over all elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. To do so, Lenstra uses the class number formula, and the bounds for the special value
L(1,\chi)
where
\chi
is the quadratic character associated to the Frobenius field when varying the curve. In this sense one is tempted to assume that no Siegel zero exists, but this only would save a logarithm with no effect in the result. On the other hand, we do need to assume the general Riemann Hypothesis, GRH, to bound the size of the least non square residue modulo
p
.

2.
Lemmata

We want to factorize
n=p_{1}\dots p_{r}
an squarefree integer. As usual, for
E/\mathbb{Z}
we let
E_{n}=\prod_{p|n}E_{p}
, where
E_{p}
is the reduction of
E
modulo
p
. Clearly we can give to
E_{n}
group structure isomorphic to
\prod_{p|n}E_{p}
as groups. We denote
|E_{n}|
the number of points of the elliptic curve modulo
n
. Now, for any
(d,n)=1
consider
E_{n}^{d}
the quadratic twist of
E_{n}
and let
|E_{n}^{d}|
its number of points modulo
n
. Then,

|E_{n}|=\prod_{p|n}(p+1-a_{p}),\quad|E_{n}^{d}|=\prod_{p|n}\left(p+1-\left(\frac{d}{p}\right)a_{p}\right),

where
\left(\frac{d}{p}\right)
is the Legendre symbol, and
a_{p}
is the trace of the Frobenius endomorphism
\varphi_{p}:E_{p}\to E_{p}
which verifies
|a_{p}|\leq 2\sqrt{p}
by Hasse’s Theorem
[
6
]
. We will denote
I_{p}=[p+1-2\sqrt{p},p+1+2\sqrt{p}]
the Hasse’s interval. For the proof of the theorem we need the following lemmata.

Lemma 2
.

Let
m
be an integer and
p
a prime
p\nmid m
. Assuming GRH, there exist
d\ll(\log mp)^{2}
such that
\left(\frac{d}{p}\right)=-1
and
\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)=1
.

Proof.

This is just a consequence of Theorem 1.4 of
[
13
]
, considering the coset
aH
of the subgroup
H\subset(\mathbb{Z}/pm)^{*}
of the quadratic residues modulo
pm
, and
a
a quadratic non-residue modulo
p
and a quadratic residue modulo
m
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
,
p
a prime number and
S_{p,D}
be the number of isomorphic classes over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
of elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with trace
a
such that the gcd
(a,p+1)\leq D
. Then

S_{p,D}\geq C\max\left\{\frac{p}{\log p}-\frac{p}{D\log p}\tau(p+1)-\tau(p+1)^{2}\frac{\sqrt{p}}{\log p},\frac{p}{(\log p)^{2}}\right\},

for some explicit constant
C
.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.9
of
[
8
]
, we know that given any set
S\subset I_{p}
with
|S|\geq 3
, then the number of isomorphism classes over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
of elliptic curves modulo
p
with number of points in
S
is bigger than
|S|\frac{c\sqrt{p}}{\log p}
, for some explicit constant
c
. So we just have to count the number of integers
1\leq a\leq 2\sqrt{p}
such that
(a,p+1)=d
for some
d\leq D
. Let us call
\varphi_{p,D}
to this number. Note that the number of traces with
(a_{p},p+1)\leq D
is exactly 2
\varphi_{p,D}
since
(a,p+1)=d
if and only if
(-a,p+1)=d
. Now,

\displaystyle\varphi_{p,D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq 2\sqrt{p}\\
(a,p+1)=d\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq 2\sqrt{p}/d\end{subarray}}\sum_{k|(a,(p+1)/d)}\mu(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|(p+1)/d\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{kd}\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|(p+1)/d\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\left[\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{kd}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|(p+1)/d\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|(p+1)/d\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{kd}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{p+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\varphi((p+1)/d)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|(p+1)/d\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{kd}\right\},

and noting that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\end{subarray}}\varphi((p+1)/d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\end{subarray}}\varphi(d)=p+1.

we get

(1)

\varphi_{p,D}=2\sqrt{p}-\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{p+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d>D\end{subarray}}\varphi((p+1)/d)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|(p+1)/d\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{kd}\right\}.

Using the trivial upper bound
\varphi(n)<n
we get for the first sum in (
1
)

\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{p+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d>D\end{subarray}}\varphi((p+1)/d)<{2\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d>D\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}<\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{D}\tau(p+1).

The last sum in (
1
) can be bounded in the following way

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|(p+1)/d\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{kd}\right\}\right|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\leq D\end{subarray}}2^{\omega((p+1)/d)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|p+1\\
d\geq(p+1)/D\end{subarray}}2^{\omega(d)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{q^{\alpha}||p+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|q^{\alpha}\end{subarray}}2^{\omega(d)}=\prod_{q^{\alpha}||p+1}(1+2\alpha)=\tau((p+1)^{2}).

and hence

(2)

\varphi_{p,D}\geq 2\sqrt{p}-\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{D}\tau(p+1)-\tau((p+1)^{2}).

On the other hand, we trivially have

\varphi_{p,D}\geq\varphi_{p,1}

and

\displaystyle\varphi_{p,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq 2\sqrt{p}\\
(a,p+1)=1\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq 2\sqrt{p}\\
(a,p+1)=1\\
2\nmid a\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq 2\sqrt{p}\\
2\nmid a\end{subarray}}\sum_{k|(a,p+1)}\mu(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|p+1\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{k}\\
2\nmid a\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|p+1\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\left[\frac{\sqrt{p}}{k}+\frac{1}{2}\right]=\sqrt{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|p+1\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|p+1\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}\mu(k)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k|p+1\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{d}+\frac{1}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{p}\frac{\varphi{(P)}}{P}+\sum_{k|P}\mu(k)\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{d}+\frac{1}{2}\right\}

where
p+1=2^{k}P
with
2\nmid P
. As before, for the last sum we have trivially the upper bound

\left|\sum_{k|P}\mu(k)\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{p}}{d}+\frac{1}{2}\right\}\right|\leq 2^{\omega(P)}

since the sum only counts squarefree integers, and hence

(3)

\varphi_{p,D}\geq\sqrt{p}\frac{\varphi(P)}{P}-2^{\omega(P)}.

Now, if
\omega(P)=l
, then
P\geq\prod_{p\leq p_{l}}p
. From Corollary
2
of Rosser and Shoenfeld
[
12
]
we obtain that
p_{l}>el
for any
p_{l}>113
, so in particular
\prod_{p\leq p_{l}}p\geq l^{l}
. Indeed we just need Maple to check it is true for
13\leq l\leq 31
and for
l>31
we will prove it by induction. So, suppose it is true that
\prod_{p\leq p_{l-1}}p\geq(l-1)^{(l-1)}
. Then,

\prod_{p\leq p_{l}}p=p_{l}\prod_{p\leq p_{l-1}}p\geq p_{l}(l-1)^{(l-1)}>el(l-1)^{(l-1)},

but since
a_{l}=(1+\frac{1}{l})^{l}
is an increasing sequence with
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{l}=e
, we have

el(l-1)^{(l-1)}>l(1+\frac{1}{l-1})^{l-1}(l-1)^{(l-1)}=l^{l}

Hence, taking
l=16
we see than
P\geq l^{l}>16^{l}=(2^{l})^{4}
so, in particular, we have
P>(2^{\omega(P)})^{4}
and

\varphi_{p,1}>\sqrt{p}\frac{\varphi(P)}{P}-p^{1/4}\geq\frac{\sqrt{p}}{8\log p},

for any
p\geq 10^{8}
, where we have used the trivial inequality
\varphi(n)>\frac{n}{4\log n}
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1

Let
D\leq(\log n)^{k}
for some fixed
k
. We select an elliptic curve
E_{n}:=y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b
modulo
n
at random. Suppose
(a_{p_{1}},p_{1}+1)=d_{E}\leq D
. Take
d
as in Lemma
2
, for
p=p_{1}
and
m=n/p_{1}
. Then,
|E_{p_{1}}^{d}|=p_{1}+1+a_{p_{1}}
while

|E_{p_{i}}^{d}|=p_{i}+1-a_{p_{i}}
for any
i=2\dots r
and, hence

\frac{|E_{n}|}{|E_{n}^{d}|}=\frac{p_{1}+1-a_{p_{1}}}{p_{1}+1+a_{p_{1}}}.

Let
\frac{|E_{n}|}{|E_{n}^{d}|}=\frac{a}{b}
where
(a,b)=1
. Then

\frac{a}{b}=\frac{\frac{p_{1}+1-a_{p_{1}}}{d}}{\frac{p_{1}+1+a_{p_{1}}}{d}},

and hence
a=(p_{1}+1-a_{p_{1}})d
,
b=(p_{1}+1+a_{p_{1}})d
. So we just have to mulptiply
a
by all the integers up to
D
in order to recover
p_{1}+1-a_{p_{1}}
and mulptiply
b
by all the integers up to
D
to recover
p_{1}+1+a_{p_{1}}
. Adding both number we get
2(p_{1}+1)
and, hence, we have found a factor of
n
.

If the algorithm does not return
p_{1}
, in
(\log n)^{k}
steps, then stop, and select a new elliptic curve
E^{\prime}_{n}:=y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
non isomorphic to
E_{n}
. Note that we can assume
A\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
neither
B\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
for
p|n
since otherwise we find a factor of
n
. Then, if we would get an elliptic curve isomorphic to
E_{n}
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for some
p|n
then
\lambda^{4}A\equiv a\pmod{p}
and
\lambda^{6}B\equiv b\pmod{p}
, so
\lambda^{4}\equiv\frac{a}{A}
and
\lambda^{6}\equiv\frac{b}{B}\pmod{p}
, or
\lambda^{2}\equiv\frac{bA}{aB}\pmod{p}
and then
b^{2}A^{3}-a^{3}B^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and we would find a factor.

Now, repeating the process
k(\log n)^{2}
times, for non isomorphic classes of elliptic curves, the probality that none of them has the trace
a
so that
(a,p+1)\leq D
is bounded above by

\left(1-\frac{c}{(\log p)^{2}}\right)^{k(\log n)^{2}}<\ \left(1-\frac{c}{(\log p)^{2}}\right)^{k(\log p)^{2}}<e^{-\frac{k}{c}}<\frac{c}{k}<\varepsilon,

for
k\gg(1/\varepsilon)
. Finally note that checking that none of them is isomorphic to the previous ones would take
O\left(k^{2}(\log n)^{4}\right)
steps.
