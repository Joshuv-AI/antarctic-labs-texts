On the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem
Lorenzo Mella

Abstract
. Heffter arrays are partially filled arrays that have been introduced in [ 1 ] as a tool to construct regular embeddings of graphs on surfaces. These constructions can be achieved from the solution of a tour problem on the filled cells of the array, introduced in [ 3 ] and called Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem . In particular, the knight’s move is seen as the composition of an horizontal move and a vertical one, where the directions (leftward or rightward, upward or downward respectively) are prescribed in advance for each row and each column of the array and each filled cell is mapped to the first filled cell encountered along that direction. Then, a solution to the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem is a set of directions such that the resulting move function is a tour over the array. Here, we consider a particular class of square arrays, and we construct solutions to the tour problem in some infinite families of these arrays.

05B20; 05B30; 05C10

1.
Introduction

Tour problems of chess pieces over chessboards have always been an interesting topic in Discrete Mathematics, that have been widely studied over the years due to their vast variety of applications, like Hamiltonian cycles and graph domination, and due to the fact that many combinatorial problems can be translated in this setting, like graph labelings and embeddings, see
[
9
]
. Moreover, there are many variations that can be proposed and studied, such as considering different chess pieces
[
2
,
5
]
, or changing the structure of the chessboard, that for instance might have holes
[
7
]
, or be embedded inside a surface
[
6
]
.

In this article, we consider a tour problem introduced in
[
3
]
, called
Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem
, that arised from a particular class of combinatorial arrays, called Heffter arrays, firstly introduced by Archdeacon in
[
1
]
, and then widely studied in successive works, see
[
8
]
. A solution to this tour problem allows the construction of
2
-face colorable embeddings of complete graphs on orientable surfaces (this can be extended more in general to Cayley graphs), see
[
3
]
for details. As shown below, the tour problem can be defined more in general on the filled cells of any array, while the content of each cell is not relevant for the problem. For this reason, in this work we are not going to report notions and results relative to Heffter arrays, and we address the interested reader to the survey
[
8
]
.

In what follows, we consider a chessboard that might have some holes, that we represent as an array where some cells are allowed to be empty, i.e. that it is partially filled. From now on, all arrays are considered to be partially filled by default, and to avoid trivial cases we assume that every row and every column contains at least one filled cell. Moreover, unless explicitely stated, every array that we consider is toroidal, hence if we have an
m\times n
array the row and column indexes of its filled cells are written with their residue class modulo
m
and modulo
n
, respectively.

Given two positive integers
a
and
b
with
a\leq b
, we denote by
[a,b]
the set of integers
\{a,a+1,a+2,\dotsc,b-1,b\}
. For an
m\times n
array
A
, let
F(A)
denote the set of pairs
(i,j)
, with
i\in[1,m]
and
j\in[1,n]
, such that the
(i,j)
-th cell of
A
is filled. Then, a
move function

\phi
over
A
is a permutation of
F(A)
, while a
tour
is a move function that is also a permutation of length
|F(A)|
. Given
(i,j)\in F(A)
, we say that its
orbit
is the cycle of
\phi
that contains it. The problem of finding a tour in
A
is denoted by
T(A)
, and if
\phi
is a move function that is a tour of
A
, we say that
\phi
is a
solution
of
T(A)
. More in general, we also use the term move function when considering a permutation over a set, especially if the set has a natural association with a set of cells of an array.

We now describe the Crazy Knight’s move function introduced in
[
3
]
: for
(i,j)\in F(A)
let
s_{r}(i,j)
be the
row successor
of
(i,j)
, namely
(i,j+k)
, where
k
is the minimum strictly positive integer such that
(i,j+k)\in F(A)
. Similarly,
s_{c}(i,j)=(i+k,j)
denotes the
column successor
of
(i,j)
, where again
k
is the minimum strictly positive integer such that
(i+k,j)\in F(A)
.
Let
R=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{m})\in\{-1,1\}^{m}
and
C=(c_{1},\dotsc,c_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
be two sequences of prescribed directions. Then, for
(i,j)\in F(A)
the
row move
is
s_{r}^{r_{i}}
, while the
column move
is
s_{c}^{c_{j}}
, where again
r_{i},c_{j}\in\{-1,1\}
. Hence,
s_{r}^{1}(i,j)
is the row successor of
(i,j)
, while
s_{r}^{-1}(i,j)
is the filled cell such that
(i,j)
is its row successor (a similar reasoning holds for the column move). We then define the Crazy Knight’s move function
CN_{RC}
as the composition of a row move with a column move; in particular, if
(i,j^{\prime})=s_{r}^{r_{i}}(i,j)
, then:

(1.1)

CN_{RC}(i,j)=s_{c}^{c_{j^{\prime}}}(i,j^{\prime}).

We then recall the statement of the Crazy Knight’s tour problem:

Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem
.

Given an
m\times n
array
A
, determine whether there exist
R\in\{-1,1\}^{m}
and
C\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
such that
CN_{RC}
is a tour.

Given an array
A
, we denote by
P(A)
the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem.
Among the possible partially filled arrays that can be considered, we focus on the so-called cyclically
k
-diagonal square arrays, as many Heffter arrays that have been constructed in the literature share this structure, and in general it is not easy to provide a solution to the tour problem. Given a square array
A
of size
n
, for every
i\in[1,n]
we define the
i
-th diagonal
D_{i}
as the set of cells:

D_{i}=\{(i,1),(i+1,2),\dotsc,(i-1,n)\}.

Then, an
n\times n
array
A
is
cyclically
k
-diagonal
if its filled cells are precisely
k
consecutive diagonals: since the array is toroidal, it is not restrictive to assume that the filled diagonals are the first
k
ones, namely
F(A)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}
.

In Section
2
we report
the main results on the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem obtained in
[
3
]
for cyclically diagonal arrays, that comprehend necessary conditions for the existence of a solution to
P(A)
, and a study of a particular class of solutions. In Section
3
we proceed with the study of this particular case, showing that under some conditions the problem is equivalent to a tour problem of another generalized knight in a partially filled toroidal array. In Section
4
we prove some auxiliary statements that we use in Section
5
to produce new solutions to
P(A)
for some infinite classes of cyclically
k
-diagonal square arrays.

2.
Known results

In this section we report some results obtained in
[
3
]
, regarding the solution of the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem in the case of cyclically diagonal square matrices. We begin with the following necessary condition, see Corollary 2.8 of
[
3
]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a square array with
|F(A)|
filled cells. If there exists a solution to
P(A)
, then
|F(A)|
is odd.

It has moreover been proven that the necessary conditions are also sufficient in the case of completely filled arrays, indeed (Theorem 3.3 of
[
3
]
):

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
totally filled array. Then there exists a solution to
P(A)
if and only if
m
and
n
are not both even.

The authors of
[
3
]
then focused on cyclically
k
-diagonal arrays of size
n
(notice that by Proposition
2.1
both
n
and
k
must be odd): in particular, they studied the case when one of the two vectors
R
and
C
is identically equal to
1
. This was done in order to obtain equivalent conditions for the existence of a solution to
P(A)
, that moreover rely on a covering problem of a smaller set of cells, as the next statement shows. We remark that in
[
3
]
the authors always considered
R\equiv 1
and
C\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, but due to the natural symmetries of the array the same results hold when
C\equiv 1
and
R\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
. Given then
R=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, let
E
be the set of indexes
\{e_{1},\dotsc,e_{t}\}
, with
e_{1}<e_{2}<\dotsc<e_{t}
, such that:

(2.1)

r_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-1&\text{ if $i\in E$,}\\
&1&\text{ if $i\not\in E$.}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

We then have the following necessary and sufficient conditions, see Lemma 4.7 of
[
3
]
:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an odd integer and let
A
be a cyclically
k
-diagonal array of odd size
n>k
. If
E
is a subset of
[1,n]
, then
C=(1,\dotsc,1)
and
R=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, where
r_{i}=-1
if and only if
i\in E
, are a solution of
P(A)
if and only if:

(1)

E
covers all the congruence classes modulo
\gcd(n,k-1)
;

(2)

the orbit of
(1,1)
contains the set of cells
\{(e,e)|e\in E\}
.

In particular, from Proposition
2.3
we can find a solution to
P(A)
whenever
n
and
k-1
are coprime, indeed (see Proposition 4.8 of
[
3
]
):

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an odd integer, and let
A
be a cyclically
k
-diagonal array of size
n>k
. If
n
and
k-1
are coprime, then
R=(-1,1,\dotsc,1)
and
C=(1,\dotsc,1)
are a solution to
P(A)
.

We conclude this section regarding the known main results by reporting this extension theorem, that highly reduces the spectrum of cases to study:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an odd integer, and let
A
be a cyclically
k
-diagonal array of size
n>k
. Assume that the vectors
C=(1,\dotsc,1)
and
R\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
are a solution of
P(A)
. Then there
exists a solution of
P(A^{\prime})
for any cyclically
k
-diagonal array
A^{\prime}
of size
n^{\prime}=n+\lambda(k-1)
for any
integer
\lambda\geq 0
.

Through the aid of a computer, in
[
3
]
the authors were able to find solutions to the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem for cyclically
k
-diagonal arrays of size
n
for many pairs of odd integers
(n,k)
. It seems then natural to conjecture that for every pair of odd integer
(n,k)
, with
n\geq k>3
, there exists a solution to
P(A)
.
Moreover, from Theorem
2.5
it follows that, for any fixed
n
, it would be sufficient to find a solution to
P(A)
whenever
k\in\left[\frac{n+1}{2},n-2\right]
.

3.
Generalized knight equivalence

For the whole of this section
A
is a fixed
n\times n
cyclically
k
-diagonal array, for
n>k\geq 3
positive odd integers. Here we show that, under some hypotheses on the vectors
R
and
C
, we can give some necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a solution to
P(A)
. As discussed in Section
2
, we choose
C=(1,\dotsc,1)
and
R=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, where
E\subset[1,n]
is the set of indexes
\{e_{1},\dotsc,e_{t}\}
, with
e_{1}<e_{2}<\dotsc<e_{t}
, such that:

r_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-1&\text{ if $i\in E$,}\\
&1&\text{ if $i\not\in E$.}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

If
E
is a set of indexes such that
C\equiv 1
and the corresponding sequence
R
solve
P(A)
, then we say that
E

solves

P(A)
.
For convenience of notation, let
m
and
\ell
be the positive integers such that

(3.1)

n=m\gcd(n,k-1),\qquad k-1=\ell\gcd(n,k-1).

We now construct two permutations, acting on
E
and
[1,n]
respectively, in order to produce an equivalent formulation of a particular restriction of the Crazy Knight’s move function defined in Equation (
1.1
). We remark that in Example
3.4
we give an explicit construction of what follows, hoping that this might help the reader. The first permutation is defined on the set
E
as:

\Omega:E\rightarrow E,\quad e_{i}\mapsto e_{i+(k-1)}.

The second permutation, denoted as
\Theta
, is defined on a partition into intervals of size
\gcd(n,k-1)
of
[1,n]
, and is constructed as follows:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\text{$\bullet$ for every $j\in[1,m]$, let $I_{j}=[1+(j-1)\gcd(n,k-1),j\gcd(n,k-1)]$; }

\displaystyle\text{$\bullet$ let $\theta=(j_{1},\dotsc,j_{m})$ be the permutation of $[1,m]$ such that }

\displaystyle\text{ \, $j_{i+1}-j_{i}\equiv-\ell\pmod{m}$ for every $i\in[1,m]$;}

\displaystyle\text{$\bullet$ let $\Theta=(I_{j_{1}},I_{j_{2}},\dotsc,I_{j_{m}})$ be a permutation on $\{I_{j_{1}},I_{j_{2}},\dotsc,I_{j_{m}}\}$.}

Observe that by Equation (
3.1
)
\ell
and
m
are coprime, hence
-\ell\pmod{m}
is a generator of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and
\theta
is indeed a permutation of
[1,m]
. With a slight abuse of notation, if
d
is an integer in
I_{j_{i}}
for some
i\in[1,m]
, then by
\Theta(d)
we mean the element in
I_{j_{i+1}}
that is equal to
d
modulo
\gcd(n,k-1)
(or, equivalently, the integer
d+n-(k-1)
modulo
n
).

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
d\in[1,n]
, let
(d,d)
be a cell of the main diagonal
D_{1}
of the array
A
, and let
\pi:D_{1}\rightarrow[1,n],(d,d)\mapsto d
. Moreover, let
CN_{R,C}|_{D_{1}}:D_{1}\rightarrow D_{1}
be the restriction on
D_{1}
of the Crazy Knight’s move function. Then,
\pi CN_{R,C}|_{D_{1}}\equiv S_{D}
, where:

(3.3)

S_{D}(d)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Theta(\Omega(d))\quad\text{ if $d\in E$,}\\
&\Theta(d)\quad\text{ otherwise.}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

If
d\not\in E
, then the application of the Crazy Knight’s move function on the cell
(d,d)
results in the
(d+n-(k-1),d+n-(k-1))
-th cell of
A
, that is the cell having row and column indexes equal to
\Theta(d)
, hence
\pi CN_{R,C}|_{D_{1}}(d,d)=\Theta(d)=S_{D}(d)
. If
d\in E
, then the Crazy Knight’s move function maps the cell
(d,d)
to the diagonal
D_{2}
of
A
. Then, in order to return to the main diagonal
D_{1}
we first need to traverse
k-1
rows such that their relative indexes belong to
E
, that corresponds to the map
\Omega
; we then arrive to the
(k-1)
-th diagonal, and by applying
s_{c}s_{r}^{-1}
we return to the main diagonal. This last operation corresponds to an application of the map
\Theta
, thus the statement follows.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

A set
E\subset[1,n]
of row indexes solves
P(A)
if and only if
S_{D}
is a permutation of
[1,n]
of maximum length.

The following remark shows that we may simplify the study of the move function of Equation (
3.3
) to intervals that are not disjoint from
E
.

Remark 3.3
.

Assume that there exists an interval
I_{j_{i}}
such that
E\cap I_{j_{i}}=\emptyset
. Define the following permutation on the set of intervals
\{I_{j_{1}},I_{j_{2}},\dotsc,I_{j_{i-1}},I_{j_{i+1}},\dotsc,I_{j_{m}}\}
:

\hat{\Theta}=(I_{j_{1}},I_{j_{2}},\dotsc,I_{j_{i-1}},I_{j_{i+1}},\dotsc I_{j_{m}}).

Again, by an abuse of notation if
d
is an integer in
I_{a}
for some
a\in[1,m-1]
, then by
\hat{\Theta}(d)
we mean the element in
\hat{\Theta}(I_{{a}})
that is equal to
d
modulo
\gcd(n,k-1)
. Let then
\widehat{S_{D}}
be:

(3.4)

\widehat{S_{D}}(d)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\hat{\Theta}(\Omega(d))\quad\text{ if $d\in E$,}\\
&\hat{\Theta}(d)\quad\text{ otherwise.}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

It then easily follows that, for every
d\in I_{j_{i-1}}
,
\widehat{S_{D}}(d)=S_{D}^{2}(d)
, while for
d\in\{I_{j_{1}},I_{j_{2}},\dotsc,I_{j_{i-2}},I_{j_{i+1}},\dotsc,I_{j_{m}}\}
the two functions coincide.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
A
be a cyclically
k
-diagonal array of size
n
,
n>k\geq 3
. Let
E
be a subset of
[1,n]
satisfying Conditions (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), and let
C=(1,\dotsc,1)
and
R=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, where
r_{i}=-1
if and only if
i\in E
. Then
R
and
C
are a solution to
P(A)
if and only if a
(k-1-|E|)
-knight is a solution to
T(D)
, where
D
is the array defined in (
3.8
).

Proof.

For the sake of notation, let
h:=k-1-|E|
and
g:=\gcd(n,k-1)
. We divide the proof of the statement in two steps: we first show that there is a natural bijection
\phi
between the set
E
and the filled cells of a suitable subarray
B
of
D
; in the second step, we show that for every
e\in E
it holds:

(3.9)

S_{E}(e)=\phi^{-1}N_{h}^{\alpha}\phi(e),

where
\alpha\geq 1
is the minimum integer such that
N_{h}^{\alpha}(e)\in B
. From Equation (
3.9
) it is then clear that the Crazy Knight’s move function restricted to the set
E
is equivalent to an
h
-knight over the array
D
, thus proving the necessary and sufficient condition of the statement.

Step 1: Building the function
\phi
.

From Condition (
3.6
) we know that
E
is the union of
i
intervals
I_{j_{1}},\dotsc,I_{j_{i}}
of size
g:=\gcd(n,k-1)
, and a subinterval of size
g-f
of
I_{j_{i+1}}
. Let
B=(b_{uv})
be the subarray of
D
containing its last
g
columns.

For every
I_{j_{k}}=\{e_{k,1},\dotsc,e_{k,g}\}
with
k\in[1,i]
, and for
I_{j_{i+1}}=\{e_{i+1,1},\dotsc,e_{i+1,g-f}\}
, where
e_{x_{1},y}<e_{x_{2},y}\Leftrightarrow x_{1}<x_{2}
for each
y\in[1,i+1]
, let
\phi
be the map that assigns
e_{u,v}
to the
(u,v)
-th cell of
B
, that is filled from the definition of
B
. Moreover, since the intervals
I_{j_{1}},\dotsc,I_{j_{i+1}}
are pairwise disjoint and
|F(B)|=|E|
, we have that
\phi
is a bijection. As a final remark, notice that if we endow the indexes of the filled cells of the array
B
with the following partial ordering:

(u,v)\prec(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow u<u^{\prime},

then the map
\phi
preserves the natural ordering of the set
\{j_{1},\dotsc,j_{i+1}\}
, i.e.
j_{u}<j_{v}
if and only if
\phi(e_{u})\prec\phi(e_{v})
for each
e_{u}\in I_{j_{u}}
,
e_{v}\in I_{j_{v}}
.

Step 2: Equivalence.

We begin by making some further considerations on Equation (
3.9
). Indeed, note that both
S_{E}
and
N_{h}
are the composition of two functions, namely
\Theta^{\gamma}
and
\Omega
for
S_{E}
, as shown in Equation (
3.7
), and
s_{C}
and
s_{R}^{h}
for
N_{h}
. Moreover, by Condition (
3.5
) the sequence
(j_{1},\dotsc,j_{i+1})
is both ordered with respect to the natural ordering of the integers and with respect to the action of the map
\Omega
. Since
\phi
is order-preserving, for any
e\in E
it holds:

\phi^{-1}s_{C}\phi(e)=\Theta^{\gamma}(e).

Thus, Equation (
3.9
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle S_{E}(e)=\phi^{-1}N_{h}^{\alpha}\phi(e)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\Theta^{\gamma}(\Omega(e))=\phi^{-1}s_{c}s_{r}^{h}N_{h}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\Theta^{\gamma}(\Omega(e))=\phi^{-1}s_{c}\phi\phi^{-1}s_{r}^{h}N_{h}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e).

Hence, in order to prove Equation (
3.9
) it is sufficient to verify that:

(3.10)

\phi^{-1}s_{r}^{h}N_{h}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)=\Omega(e).

Now, let
h=pg+f
for some integer
p\geq 0
, and let
D_{p}
be the
(i+1)\times(h+g)
array defined in (
3.8
). We prove Equation (
3.10
) by induction on
p
.
Assume first that
p=0
, hence
h=f
and
D_{0}
is an
(i+1)\times(f+g)
array. Let
e_{j}\in E
and
\phi(e_{j})=(u,v)
, we distinguish between two cases:

•

if
v+f\leq g
and
u\neq i+1
, or if
v+2f\leq g
and
u=i+1
, then

s_{R}^{f}N_{f}^{0}\phi(e_{j})=s_{R}^{f}\phi(e_{j})\in B,

hence
s_{R}^{f}\phi(e_{j})=\phi(e_{j+f})=\phi\Omega(e_{j})
;

•

in the other cases, it is easy to see that:

s_{R}^{f}N_{f}^{1}\phi(e_{j})\in B,

since
s_{R}^{f}N_{f}^{1}\phi(e_{j})
is the
(u+2f,v+1)
-th cell in the array
D_{0}
, this corresponds to the
(u+f,v+1)
-th cell of the array
B
, that is exactly
\phi\Omega(e_{j})
.

Assume now that Equation (
3.10
) holds for
h=pg+f
; we prove that this implies that it holds for
h^{\prime}=(p+1)g+f
. Let
D_{p}
and
D_{p+1}
be the two arrays relative to
h
and
h^{\prime}
respectively. It can be easily seen that
D_{p+1}
is composed by the concatenation of a completely filled
(i+1)\times g
array with
D_{p}
, hence we refer to
D_{p}
as being contained inside
D_{p+1}
. Then, let
\Omega_{p}
and
\Omega_{p+1}
be the functions on
E
relative to
h
and
h^{\prime}
respectively.

Remark 3.7
.

In
D_{p}
(resp.
D_{p+1}
) an
h
-N (resp.
h^{\prime}
-N) move is equivalent to a
(-g)
-N move for all rows, except for the last one where it corresponds to a
(-g+f)
-N move.

Assume first that
e\in E
is such that
s_{c}\phi\Omega_{p}(e)=\phi\Omega_{p+1}(e)
. By inductive hypothesis, on
D_{p}
it holds
s_{r}^{h}N_{h}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)=\phi\Omega(e)
. Let
(u,v)=N_{h}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)
in
D_{p}
, that is also
N_{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)
in
D_{p+1}
by previous remark: it is easy to see that, since
s_{r}^{h}(u,v)
in
D_{p}
belongs to the array
B
, the move
s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}(u,v)
is contained in
D_{p+1}\setminus D_{p}
, hence we apply
N_{h^{\prime}}
to
(u,v)
. The resulting cell belongs to
D_{p+1}\setminus D_{p}
, and it follows that
s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}(u,v)
is contained in
B
. Hence, in
D_{p+1}
:

s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}(u,v)=s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)=s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\phi(e)\in B.

Comparing this formula to the one that holds for
D_{p}
, we have applied one more time an
h^{\prime}
-N move. A simple counting argument shows that
s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\phi(e)
in
D_{p+1}
belongs to the same column of
s_{r}^{h}N_{h}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)=\phi\Omega(e)
in
D_{p}
, and as it is contained in the successive row it follows that:

s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\phi(e)=s_{c}\phi\Omega_{p}(e)=\phi\Omega_{p+1}(e).

Let now
e\in E
be such that
s_{c}\phi\Omega_{p}(e)\neq\phi\Omega_{p+1}(e)
. Notice that this happens if and only if
\Omega_{p}(e)=e_{u}
, where
u\in[|E|-g+1,|E|]
. Also in this case, let
(u,v)=N_{h}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)
in
D_{p}
, that is also
N_{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha-1}\phi(e)
in
D_{p+1}
. It easily follows that
v\in\{i,i+1\}
, and that
s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}(u,v)
does not belong to
B
, hence we apply the
h^{\prime}
-N move function.

If
v=i+1
, then
N_{h^{\prime}}(u,v)\in D_{p+1}\setminus D_{p}
(in particular, it belongs to the first row of
D_{p+1}
), hence
s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}(u,v)=(x_{1},y_{1})\in B
.
If
v=i
, then
N_{h^{\prime}}(u,v)\in D_{p+1}\setminus D_{p}
(in particular, it belongs to the last row of
D_{p+1}
). Since
s_{c}\phi\Omega_{p}(e)\neq\phi\Omega_{p+1}(e)
, it follows that
s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N_{h^{\prime}}(u,v)\not\in B
, hence we apply the
h^{\prime}
-N move function another time, thus
s_{r}^{h^{\prime}}N^{2}_{h^{\prime}}(u,v)=(x_{2},y_{2})
belongs to
B
. Let
\eta
be the map that associates to each
e\in E
such that
s_{c}\phi\Omega_{p}(e)\neq\phi\Omega_{p+1}(e)
its corresponding cell, that is either
(x_{1},y_{1})
or
(x_{2},y_{2})
.

It is easy to see that
x_{1}=x_{2}=1
, and notice that
|[|E|-g+1,|E|]|=g
, hence
\eta
is a bijection between the elements of
[|E|-g+1,|E|]
and the first row of
B
. To conclude, it is sufficient to check that
\eta
is order-preserving, where the ordering of the cells of the row is intended as the natural one, i.e.
(1,y_{1})\prec(1,y_{2})\Leftrightarrow y_{1}<y_{2}
. Let
(u_{\gamma},v_{\gamma})=N_{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha_{\gamma}-1}\phi(e_{\gamma})
for
\gamma\in\{1,2\}
, where
e_{1}<e_{2}
. Clearly, if
u_{1}=u_{2}
, then the order is preserved. If
u_{1}=i
and
u_{2}=i+1
, then
N_{h}^{\prime}(u_{1},v_{1})<(u_{2},v_{2})
: in fact,
N_{h}^{\prime}(u_{1},v_{1})
belongs to
D_{p+1}\setminus D_{p}
, while
(u_{2},v_{2})
belongs to
D_{p}
.
Thus,
\eta
is order-preserving, concluding the proof.

∎

4.
Auxiliary lemmas

In this section we prove some auxiliary statements that we use in Section
5
to find solutions to the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem on cyclically diagonal square arrays. In particular, our goal is to show that the generalized knight’s tour problem on the array
D
introduced in (

) is equivalent to a bishop’s tour problem on another array, say
D^{*}
. To this aim, after having introduced some notation, we state a list of statements that, applied sequentially to

, will construct the desired array

. For this reason, we try to keep the notation of the various parameters as close as possible to the one of Theorem
3.6
.

Given two arrays
A
and
B

A\otimes B
the Kroenecker product between
A
and
B

J_{m,n}

A^{T}
denotes the transpose of
A
.

.

An important tool that is used in the remaining of this section is the concept of equivalence of two arrays
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
with respect to another given array
A_{2}
. The idea is to establish a condition such that, when considering a move function on
[A_{1}|A_{2}]^{T}
,
A_{1}
can be replaced with the array
B_{1}
.

Let
\phi
be a move function on an array
A=[A_{1}\mid A_{2}]^{T}
, and for each
(i,j)\in F(A_{2})
let
\phi_{2}:F(A_{2})\rightarrow F(A_{2})
be the function:

(4.1)

\phi_{2}(i,j)=\phi^{k}(i,j),

where
k
is the minimum strictly positive integer such that
\phi^{k}(i,j)\in A_{2}
. Similarly, define
\sigma
to be a move function on an array
B=[B_{1}\mid A_{2}]^{T}
, and for each
(i,j)\in F(A_{2})
let
\sigma_{2}:F(A_{2})\rightarrow F(A_{2})
be the function:

(4.2)

\sigma_{2}(i,j)=\sigma^{k}(i,j),

where
k
is the minimum strictly positive integer such that
\sigma^{k}(i,j)\in A_{2}
. We then say that
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are
(\phi,\sigma)
-equivalent with respect to
A_{2}
if for every
(i,j)\in F(A_{2})
it holds:

\displaystyle\phi(i,j)\not\in A_{2}\Leftrightarrow\sigma(i,j)\not\in A_{2},

\displaystyle\phi(i,j),\sigma(i,j)\not\in A_{2}\Rightarrow\phi_{2}(i,j)=\sigma_{2}(i,j).

Moreover, when the move functions
\phi
and
\sigma
are essentially the same, e.g. an
a
-knight for some nonzero integer
a
, we simply say that
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are
\phi
-equivalent with respect to
A_{2}
, and we may omit
A_{2}
if it is clear from the context.

The functions
\phi_{2}

\sigma_{2}
applied to the
(i,j)
-th cell of
A_{2}
.

We begin with the following proposition:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\phi
be the move function of a bishop, and let
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
be
\phi
-equivalent with respect to a completely filled
m\times n
array
A_{2}:=J_{m,n}
. Then,
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are
\phi
-equivalent with respect to every
m\times n
array.

Proof.

Let
A_{1}
,
B_{1}
and
A_{2}
be as in the statement, and let
B_{2}
be any other
m\times n
array. For every
C\in\{A_{1},B_{1}\}
and
D\in\{A_{2},B_{2}\}
, let:

•

\phi_{C,D}
be the bishop’s move function on the array
[C\mid D]^{T}
;

•

\phi_{2,C,D}
be the function defined in Equation (
4.1
) from the array
[C\mid D]^{T}
.

Assume by contradiction that
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are not
\phi
-equivalent with respect to
B_{2}
, i.e. there exists
(i,j)\in F(B_{2})
such that
\phi_{2,A_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)\neq\phi_{2,B_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)
. Since the bishop’s move function is defined as the composition of an horizontal move and a vertical move, we have that:

\phi_{A_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)=(a_{1},a_{2})\text{ and }\phi_{B_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)=(b_{1},b_{2})\Rightarrow b_{2}=a_{2}.

The array
A_{2}
is completely filled, thus there exists
(u,v)\in F(A_{2})
such that
\phi_{A_{1},A_{2}}(u,v)=(a_{1},a_{2})
and
\phi_{B_{1},A_{2}}(u,v)=(b_{1},a_{2})
. Now, since
\phi_{2,A_{1},A_{2}}\equiv\phi_{2,B_{1},A_{2}}
, we have that for some
(x,y)\in F(A_{2})
it holds:

(x,y)=\phi_{2,A_{1},A_{2}}(u,v)=\phi_{2,B_{1},A_{2}}(u,v).

Hence:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}_{A_{1},A_{2}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\phi^{-1}_{A_{1},B_{2}}\phi_{2,A_{1},B_{2}}(i,j),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}_{B_{1},A_{2}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\phi^{-1}_{B_{1},B_{2}}\phi_{2,B_{1},B_{2}}(i,j).

If
\phi_{2,A_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)\neq\phi_{2,B_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)
, then in particular
\phi_{2,A_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)
and
\phi_{2,B_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)
have different column indexes (otherwise, by the definition of the bishop’s move function, they would be the same cell). However, if we apply an horizontal move
s_{R,A}
(resp.
s_{R,B}
) to both sides of the first (resp. second) equation in (
4.3
), we can see that the column index must be
y
. We have then proved that
\phi_{2,A_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)=\phi_{2,B_{1},B_{2}}(i,j)
.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

If two arrays are
\phi
-equivalent with respect to
J_{1,n}
, where
\phi
is the bishop’s move function, then they are
\phi
-equivalent with respect to every other
m\times n
array, for every
m\geq 1
(recall that we always assume that every row and every column of an array contains at least one filled cell).

Let
I_{1}\subset[1,m]
and
I_{2}\subset[1,n]
be intervals. Then, given an
m\times n
array
A
such that
F(A)=\{[1,m]\times[1,n]\}\setminus\{I_{1}\times I_{2}\}
, we say that
A
is an
(m,n;I_{1},I_{2})
-holed array
, that we denote as
HA(m,n;I_{1},I_{2})
. In this study, we only consider the case where
I_{1}=[m-a+1,m]
and
I_{2}
is either
[1,b]
or
[n-b+1,n]
for some positive integers
a<m
and
b<n
. In this cases, we write
A
as an
HA^{sw}(m,n;a,b)
and a
HA^{se}(m,n;a,b)
respectively, where
s
stands for
south
, while
w
and
e
stand for
west
and
east
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
respectively be an
HA^{sw}(a,a+b;f,a)
and an
HA^{se}(b+f,a+b;f,b-f)
, where
a,b
and
f
are positive integers, with
f<\min\{a,b\}
. For a positive integer
d
, let
A_{2}:=J_{d,a+b}
be a completely filled array. Let
\phi
be the bishop’s move function on
[A_{1}\mid A_{2}]^{T}
, and let
\sigma
be the move function on
[B_{1}\mid A_{2}]^{T}
that is:

•

the bishop’s move function on
A_{2}
;

•

the reversed bishop’s move function on
B_{1}
.

Then,
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are
(\phi,\sigma)
-equivalent with respect to
A_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
A_{1},B_{1}
and
A_{2}
be as in the statement, and let
R_{1}=(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{a+b})
and
R_{d}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{a+b})
respectively denote the first and the
d
-th row of
A_{2}
. Since the array
A_{2}
is completely filled, the maps
\phi_{2}
and
\sigma_{2}
are bijections between
R_{d}
and
R_{1}
, in particular:

\phi_{2}(r_{i})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &s_{i+1+a-f}\quad&\text{ if $i\geq b+f$ or $i\leq f-1$,}\\
&s_{i+1+a-b}\quad&\text{ if $b\leq i\leq b+f-1$,}\\
&s_{i+1+a}\quad&\text{ otherwise. }\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Similarly, for
\sigma_{2}(r_{i})
:

\sigma_{2}(r_{i})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &s_{i+1-b-f}\quad&\text{ if $i\geq b+f$ or $i\leq f-1$,}\\
&s_{i+1+a-b}\quad&\text{ if $b\leq i\leq b+f-1$,}\\
&s_{i+1-b}\quad&\text{ otherwise. }\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Since indexes have to be read modulo
a+b
, it follows that
a\equiv-b
, hence for each
i\in[1,a+b]
it holds
\phi_{2}(r_{i})=\sigma_{2}(r_{i})
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
D
be an
HA^{se}((i+1)h,h+g;h,f)
, where
i\geq 1
,
f<g
and
f\leq h
. Then a bishop is a solution to
P(D)
if and only if it is a solution to
P(D^{\prime})
, where
D^{\prime}
is an
HA^{sw}(ih,h+g;g-f,h)
.

Proof.

Consider the subarray
D_{1}
of
D
containing its last
h+g-f+1
rows, that is an
HA^{se}(h+g-f+1,h+g;h,f)
, and the subarray
D_{1}^{\prime}
of
D^{\prime}
containing its last
g-f+1
rows, that is a
HA^{sw}(g-f+1,h+g;g-f,h)
. Let
\phi
be the bishop’s move function, we show that
D_{1}
and
D_{1}^{\prime}
are
\phi
-equivalent with respect to the remaining part of
D
and
D^{\prime}
, that are the same completely filled array.

Let
R_{1}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{h+g})
and
R_{2}=(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{h+g})
respectively be the last and the first row of
D
. We then have:

\phi_{2}(r_{i})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &s_{i+2}\quad&\text{for $i\leq h-2$ or $i\geq h+g-1$,}\\
&s_{i+g-f+2}\quad&\text{for $h-1\leq i\leq h+f-2$,}\\
&s_{i+h+g-f+2}\quad&\text{for $h+f-1\leq i\leq h+g-2$.}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Let then
R_{1}^{\prime}=(r_{1}^{\prime},\dotsc,r_{h+g}^{\prime})
and
R_{2}^{\prime}=(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{h+g})
respectively be the last and the first row of
D^{\prime}
, we have:

\phi_{2}(r_{i}^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &s_{i+2}^{\prime}\quad&\text{for $i\leq h-2$ or $i\geq h+g-1$,}\\
&s_{i+g-f+2}^{\prime}\quad&\text{ for $h-1\leq i\leq h+f-2$, }\\
&s_{i-f+2}^{\prime}\quad&\text{for $h+f-1\leq i\leq h+g-2$.}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Recalling that the arrays have
h+g
columns, and indexes have to be read modulo
h+g
, we get that:

\phi_{2}(r_{i})=s_{j}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\phi_{2}(r_{i}^{\prime})=s_{j}^{\prime},

hence,
D_{1}
and
D_{1}^{\prime}
are
\phi
-equivalent with respect to any completely filled array, thus the statement follows.
∎

D^{\prime\prime}
is an
HA((i+1)g-f,h+g;g-f,f)
.

Proof.

The statement directly follows by applying Lemma
4.4
to the subarray of
D^{\prime}
containing the last
h
rows of
D^{\prime}
and Corollary
4.6
to the remaining rows of the array. The resulting array is an
HA((i+1)g-f,h+g;g-f,f)
, that we denote by
D^{\prime\prime}
, and the move function is a reversed bishop on
D^{\prime\prime}
. Since it is clear that a reversed bishop is a solution to
T(D^{\prime\prime})
if and only if a standard bishop solves
T(D^{\prime\prime})
, we have proven the statement.
∎

We remark that in the next statement the various parameters and arrays follow the notation of Theorem
3.6
.

Theorem 4.11
.

A
(k-1-|E|)
-knight is a solution to
T(D)
if and only if a bishop is a solution of
T(D^{*})
, where
D^{*}
is an
HA((i+1)g-f,h+g;g-f,f)
.

We now consider the following auxiliary lemma, that we use in the following section:

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
A
be an
HA(m,n;a,b)
. If
\gcd(m,n)=a+b
and
\gcd(a,b)=1
, then a bishop is a solution to
T(A)
.

Proof.

Let
A
be as in the statement, where we assume without loss of generality that
A
is an
HA^{se}(m,n;a,b)
, and let
A^{\prime}=J_{m,n}
. It has been proven in
[
4
]
that in an
m\times n
toroidal board we need exactly the orbits of
\gcd(m,n)=a+b
bishops to cover each filled cell of the array
A^{\prime}
once, where here we always refer to the bishop as a
1
-knight, i.e. the chessboard piece that moves by one square on the right and by one square downwards. Let then
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{1},B_{2},\dotsc,B_{a+b}\}
be such a set of bishops. Note that if we fix
a+b
consecutive cells in a row of
A^{\prime}
, say
(c_{1},c_{2},\dotsc,c_{a+b})
, then each cell belongs to the orbit of a different bishop.

Let
(c_{1},c_{2},\dotsc,c_{a+b})
be consecutive cells in the
(m-a)
-th row of
A
, so that
c_{a+b}=(m-a,n-1)
, and place the indexes of the bishops so that the cell
c_{i}
belongs to the orbit of
B_{i}
. Let
\phi
denote the bishop’s move function, and define
\eta:[1,a+b]\rightarrow[1,a+b]
to be the following map:

\eta(i)=j\Leftrightarrow\text{ $\phi^{q}(c_{i})$ is in the orbit of $B_{j}$,}

where
q
is the minimum strictly positive integer such that
\phi^{q}(c_{i})
is either in the first column or in the first row of
A
.
It is not hard to verify that:

\eta(i)=\left\{\begin{aligned} i-a&\text{ if $a+1\leq i$,}\\
i+b&\text{ otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

We now prove that
\eta
is a permutation on
[1,a+b]
of maximum length. We have that
\eta(1)=b+1
, and through successive applications of
\eta
we cover all the elements in
[a,a+b]
that are equal to
b+1
modulo
a
; in particular, the last visited element that belongs to this congruence class is precisely the residue class of
b+1
modulo
a
. Proceeding inductively, it follows that we cover all the elements that are equal to
\ell b+1
modulo
a
, for
\ell=1,2,\dotsc,a
; since
\gcd(a,b)=1
, this corresponds to the whole interval
[1,a+b]
. Thus,
\eta
is a permutation of
[1,a+b]
of maximum length.

This proves that a bishop is a solution to
T(A)
: indeed, if we start from the cell
c_{i}
, after
\phi^{q}
steps (where
q
is defined as before) we arrive in the orbit of the bishop
B_{\eta(i)}
. Then, we visit all the cells that belong to the orbit of
B_{\eta(i)}
, and we arrive at the cell labelled as
c_{\eta(i)}
. By repeating the iteration inductively, it immediately follows that the whole array
A
is covered by the orbit of one bishop, hence the statement.
∎

5.
Main results

In this section we use Theorems
3.6
and
4.11
to construct new solutions to the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem for cyclically diagonal arrays. In what follows, in order to simplify the notation and to be coherent with the previous sections, let
A
be a cyclically
k
-diagonal array of size
n
and set
g=\gcd(n,k-1)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
\gcd(g+1,k-1)=2
, then there exists a solution to
P(A)
.

Proof.

Choose
E\subset[1,n]
such that
|E|=g+1
and Conditions (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) are satisfied (note that this can always be done). Following the notation of Theorem
3.6
, we have
h=k-2-g
and
f=g-1
. From Theorem
3.6
and Lemma
4.8
, we know that
E
solves the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem if and only if a bishop’s move function solves
T(D^{*})
, where
D^{*}
is a
HA^{sw}(h,h+g;1,h)
.

Now, let
B
be the subarray of
D^{*}
containing its first
h
columns, and respectively define
C=(c_{1},\dotsc,c_{h})
and
C^{\prime}=(c_{1}^{\prime},\dotsc,c_{h}^{\prime})
to be the
(h+1)
-th and the
(h+g)
-th columns of
D^{*}
. Finally, let
\phi
be the bishop’s move function on
D
, and
\phi_{2}
be the map from
C^{\prime}
to
C
such that:

\phi_{2}(c_{i}^{\prime})=c_{j},

where
c_{j}
is the first cell of
C
that is visited by the move function after
c_{i}^{\prime}
.

It is easy to see that for each
i\in[1,h-2]
we have
\phi_{2}(c_{i}^{\prime})=c_{i+2}
, while
\phi_{2}(c_{h}^{\prime})=c_{1}
and
\phi_{2}(c_{h-1}^{\prime})=c_{2}
. Hence, if
D^{*}_{1}
is the array obtained from
D^{*}
by replacing
B
with an
HA^{sw}(h,1;1,1)
, a bishop’s move function is a solution of
T(D^{*})
if and only if it solves
T(D^{*}_{1})
. We have that
D^{*}
is an
HA(g+1,k-2-g;1,1)
, hence the statement follows from Lemma
4.12
.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
g
,
m
and
\ell
be positive integers such that:

•

g
and
m
are odd,
\ell
is even,
m>\ell
and
g,m,\ell>1
;

•

\gcd(g+1,\ell)=2
and
\gcd(m,\ell)=1
.

If
n=mg
and
k=1+\ell g
, then there exists a solution to
P(A)
, where
A
is a cyclically
k
-diagonal
n\times n
array.

Proof.

From the conditions on the set of parameters, we can prove that
\gcd(g+1,\ell g-(g+1))=2
. Indeed, if
g+1=2q
for some positive integer
q
, then:

\ell g-(g+1)=\ell(2q-1)-2q=2\ell q-\ell-2q.

It then immediately follows that
\gcd(2q,2\ell q-\ell-2q)=\gcd(2q,\ell)
, that is equal to
2
by hypothesis. Since
k-2-g=k-1-(g+1)=\ell g-(g+1)
, the statement follows from Proposition
5.1
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

If there exists
E\subset[1,n]
satisfying Conditions (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) with
|E|=\frac{k-1}{2}+\frac{g-1}{2}
, then there exists a solution to
P(A)
.

Proof.

Let
E
be as in the statement. By Theorems
3.6
and
4.11
we have that a solution to
P(A)
exists if and only if a bishop is a solution to
T(D^{*})
, where
D^{*}
is an
HA^{se}(\frac{k-1}{2}+\frac{g-1}{2},\frac{k-1}{2}+\frac{g+1}{2};\frac{g-1}{2},\frac{g+1}{2})
. Let
B
be the subarray of
D^{*}
containing its first
\frac{k-1}{2}
columns, and respectively denote by
C=(c_{1},\dotsc,c_{\frac{k-1}{2}})
and
C=(c_{1}^{\prime},\dotsc,c_{\frac{k-1}{2}}^{\prime})
the
(\frac{k+1}{2})
-th and the
(\frac{k-1}{2}+\frac{g+1}{2})
-th column of
D^{*}
.

Let
\phi
be the bishop’s move function on
D
, and let
\phi_{2}
be the map from
C^{\prime}
to
C
such that:

\phi_{2}(c_{i}^{\prime})=c_{j},

where
c_{j}
is the first cell of
C
that is visited by the move function after
c_{i}^{\prime}
.

We then distinguish between three different cases:

•

if
i>\frac{g+1}{2}
, in order to arrive to
C
we need to apply
\frac{k+1}{2}
times the function
\phi
. Since the column
C
has empty cells, the element
c_{i}^{\prime}
is mapped by
\phi_{2}
to the element
c_{j}
, where
j=i+\frac{k+1}{2}-\frac{g-1}{2}
.

•

if
i\leq\frac{g-3}{2}
, then we need to apply
2\frac{k-1}{2}+1=k
times
\phi
, hence the element
c_{i}^{\prime}
is mapped by
\phi_{2}
to the element
c_{j}
, where
j=i+2-g
;

•

if
i=\frac{g-1}{2}
, then we apply
k
times the function
\phi
; however, we have to consider that we need to remove
\frac{k-1}{2}
, hence the element
c_{i}^{\prime}
is mapped by
\phi_{2}
to
c_{1}
.

Now, if we apply from
C
the bishop’s move function
\frac{g+1}{2}
times, we arrive again at the column
C^{\prime}
; it is not hard to see that for every
i\in[1,\frac{k-1}{2}]
:

\phi^{\frac{g+1}{2}}\phi_{2}(c_{i}^{\prime})=c_{i+1}^{\prime}.

Note that this is exactly equivalent to a bishop’s move function on the completely filled
\frac{k-1}{2}\times 1
array, that is the column
C
. This proves that a bishop is a solution to
T(D^{*})
if and only if it is a solution to
T(C)
, and it is trivial to see that this is always the case.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
m
and
g
be positive odd integers,
m,g\geq 3
. Let
A
be a cyclically
k
-diagonal
n\times n
array, where:

n=mg\qquad k=1+(m-1)g.

Then there exists a solution to
P(A)
.

Proof.

Following the notation of the previous sections, we have
n=mg
and
k-1=\ell g
, where
\ell=m-1
. Since
-\ell\equiv 1\pmod{m}
, we can easily construct the permutation
\Theta
of the intervals
I_{1},\dotsc,I_{m}
, that is simply
\Theta=(I_{1},I_{2},\dotsc,I_{m})
. Let now
E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}I_{i}\setminus[\frac{m+1}{2}g-\frac{g-1}{2},\frac{m+1}{2}]
.

It is easy to see that
E
satisfies Conditions (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), and moreover
|E|=\frac{k-1}{2}+\frac{g-1}{2}
. By Proposition
5.4
, we conclude that there exists a solution to
P(A)
.
∎

We remark that from Corollaries
5.3
and
5.6
we can construct solutions to the Crazy Knight‘s Tour Problem for infinite families of cyclically
k
-diagonal
n\times n
arrays. Indeed, for instance to apply Corollary
5.3
one can choose
g+1=2p
for some odd prime
p
, and any even
\ell
that is not divisible by
p
, while to apply Corollary
5.6
it is only necessary to choose
m,g\geq 3
to be positive odd integers.
