Spinors and conformal correlators
Marc Gillioz

Abstract
In conformal field theory, momentum eigenstates can be parameterized by a pair of real spinors, in terms of which special conformal transformations take a simpler form. This well-known fact allows to express 2-point functions of primary operators in the helicity basis, exposing the consequences of unitarity. What is less known is that the same pair of spinors can be used, together with a pair of scalar quantities, to parameterize 3-point functions. We develop this formalism in 3 dimensions and show that it provides a simple realization of the operator product expansion (OPE) for scalar primary operators acting on the vacuum.

Introduction

Conformal field theory is remarkable in the sense that it admits a non-perturbative definition in terms of CFT data characterizing 2- and 3-point correlation functions only.
The problem of constructing all these 2- and 3-point functions in the position-space representation is in some sense completely solved: since conformal Ward identities are first-order differential equations in position space, it essentially reduces to the classification of all tensors transforming covariantly

,

,

.

A related problem is the construction of all 2- and 3-point correlation functions in the momentum-space representation. One could expect this problem to be solved as well, since the two representations are related by a Fourier transform. But this Fourier transform is very difficult to perform explicitly — so difficult in fact that most results in momentum space do not rely on it in an essential way
[

,

In this work we introduce a new approach for the construction of 2- and 3-point conformal correlation function in momentum space, making use of the spinor formalism. This method works specifically in Minkowski space-time and with Wightman functions, i.e. with correlators that are not time-ordered.
Unlike other recent works that use similar methods
[
6
,
7
]
, we do not focus on (higher-spin) conserved currents but treat all conformal primary operators on the same footing.
To keep the argument simple, our results are limited to 3 space-time dimensions and, in the case of 3-point functions, to correlators involving two scalar operators. The generalization to more complicated correlators and to 4 space-time dimensions (or higher) will be presented elsewhere.
As a consequence, the correlators computed here are not new and can in fact be found in the literature
[

,

Nevertheless, this work is more than an academic exercise: the use of spinors in Minkowski space-time allows to encode both the momentum and the polarization information of the operators

2
2-point functions

We begin by considering states created by a single local primary operator acting on the vacuum, and work in the momentum-space representation such that

P^{\mu}\mathcal{O}(p)\left|0\right\rangle=p^{\mu}\mathcal{O}(p)\left|0\right\rangle,

(2.1)

where
P^{\mu}
is the generator of translations and
p^{\mu}
its eigenvalue.

\left|0\right\rangle
is the unique vacuum state of the theory, invariant under conformal transformations.
We shall also consider the inner product of such states, which by translation symmetry is proportional to a delta function,

\left\langle 0\right|\mathcal{O}(p^{\prime})\mathcal{O}(p)\left|0\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(p^{\prime}+p)\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}(-p)\mathcal{O}(p)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}.

(2.2)

The object inside double brackets is a function of a single 3-momentum
p^{\mu}
.

2.1
Spinors and helicity eigenstates

Standard assumptions of quantum field theory require states of the form of eq. (
2.1
) to only have support in the forward light cone.
In other words, the function in double bracket in eq. (
2.2
) vanishes unless

p^{2}=(p^{0})^{2}-\mathbf{p}^{2}\geq 0,\qquad\qquad p^{0}\geq 0.

(2.3)

Any momentum of this sort can be parameterized by a pair of real spinors, or equivalently by a unique complex spinor
\lambda^{a}
and its complex conjugate
\bar{\lambda}^{a}
through

p^{\mu}=\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\,\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}.

(2.4)

Note that this parameterization is redundant: it uses a complex spinor with 4 components (two real and two imaginary) for a real 3-vector.
This redundancy is encoded in the little group symmetry

\lambda^{a}\to e^{i\theta}\lambda^{a},\qquad\qquad\bar{\lambda}^{a}\to e^{-i\theta}\bar{\lambda}^{a},

(2.5)

under which
p
transforms trivially. We say that
\lambda^{a}
carries helicity
+\frac{1}{2}
and
\bar{\lambda}^{a}
helicity
-\frac{1}{2}
.
In the rest frame in which
\mathbf{p}=0
, this corresponds to spatial rotations.

Spinors are particularly powerful when dealing with operators
\mathcal{O}
that transform in non-trivial representations of the Lorentz group. In 3 space-time dimensions, all such operators are symmetric tensors in spinor indices.
We shall denote with
2j
the number of spinor indices,
j
taking either integer or half-integer values. A complete and orthogonal basis of polarization tensors is given by

\varepsilon^{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}_{m}=\lambda^{a_{1}}\cdots\lambda^{a_{j+m}}\bar{\lambda}^{a_{j+m+1}}\cdots\bar{\lambda}^{a_{2j}},

(2.6)

where
m
takes values between
-j
and
+j
in integer steps.
There are
2j+1
such polarization tensors for each spin
j
, and all of them are eigenstates of helicity
m
, i.e. they transform as
\varepsilon^{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}_{m}\to e^{2im\theta}\varepsilon^{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}_{m}
under the little group scaling (
2.5
).

To simplify the notation even further, we introduce an auxiliary spinor
\zeta^{a}
and define the index-free polarization

\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)=(\zeta\cdot\lambda)^{j+m}(\zeta\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{j-m}.

(2.7)

The explicit form (
2.6
) of the polarization tensor can be unequivocally recovered from this expression acting with spinor derivatives.
2
2

2

This contrasts with the use of polarization
vectors
that require subtraction of trace terms
[
2
,
16
]
; there are no such trace terms in the spinor formalism.

The same auxiliary spinor can be used to express any operator carrying spinor indices in the index-free notation

\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\left|0\right\rangle\equiv\zeta^{a_{1}}\cdots\zeta^{a_{2j}}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}(p)\left|0\right\rangle.

(2.8)

The completeness of the basis of polarization tensors implies that we can further decompose this operator into helicity eigenstates as

\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\left|0\right\rangle=\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(p)\left|0\right\rangle.

(2.9)

Note that these last two equations involve a primary operator
\mathcal{O}
acting on the vacuum, as it is important to realize that they are only valid when
p
takes values inside the forward light cone. The decomposition does not make sense at the level of operators.

2.2
Conformal Ward identities

The norm of these states is computed by the 2-point correlation function

\left\langle 0\right|\mathcal{O}_{j}(p^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\left|0\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(p^{\prime}+p)\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}},

(2.10)

where we are now making use of two distinct auxiliary polarization spinors
\zeta
and
\zeta^{\prime}
to encode the information about indices.
The object in double brackets is a correlation function in its own right: taking the Fourier transform of this equation with respect to
p^{\prime}
, one gets

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=e^{ip\cdot x}\left\langle 0\right|\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{j}(x,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\left|0\right\rangle.

(2.11)

This mixed position/momentum representation is particularly well-suited to examine the constraints imposed by conformal symmetry: when evaluated at
x=0
, the primary operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(0,\zeta^{\prime})
has trivial transformation properties under the conformal group. This implies that conformal Ward identities for this correlation function can be obtained in the form of differential equations with respect to the momentum
p^{\mu}
. For instance, for scale and Lorentz symmetry, we have

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{D}-2\Delta+3\right]\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{M}_{\mu\nu}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}(\zeta^{\prime})-\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}(\zeta)\right]\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.13)

where

\widehat{D}=p^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}},\qquad\qquad\widehat{M}_{\mu\nu}=p_{\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}-p_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\nu}}.

(2.14)

\Delta
is the scaling dimension of
\mathcal{O}
, and
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}
the spin operator acting on its Lorentz indices: in terms of the auxiliary spinor
\zeta
, it can be written as

\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}(\zeta)=-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\sigma^{\rho}_{ab}\,\zeta^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta_{b}}.

(2.15)

It is a simple exercise to verify using the relation (
2.4
) and the chain rule for derivatives that the scale and Lorentz generators can be written in terms of spinors as

\widehat{D}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{a}}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{a}}\right)

(2.16)

and

\widehat{M}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\sigma^{\rho}_{ab}\left(\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{b}}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{b}}\right).

(2.17)

These results can be obtained enumerating the handful of operators that are first-order in derivatives, invariant under the little group scaling (
2.5
), and that transform respectively as a scalar and as a vector

\widehat{M}_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)=\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}(\zeta)\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta).

The same strategy can be applied to determine the constraints imposed by special conformal symmetry, encoded in the Ward identity

\widehat{K}_{\mu}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=0,

The fact that eq. (

Alternatively, this Ward identity could be obtained commuting the generator of special conformal transformation with the delta function imposing momentum conservation

\widehat{K}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}p_{\mu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{\nu}\partial p_{\nu}}-p^{\nu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{\nu}\partial p^{\mu}}+(\Delta-3)\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}+\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}(\zeta)\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{\nu}}.

(2.21)

In this case the expression for
\widehat{K}_{\mu}
in terms of spinors is more complicated because the Ward identity involves second-order derivatives in the momenta.
Nevertheless, it can be determined straightforwardly using

\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{\mu}}=\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}}{4\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\left(\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{b}}-\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{b}}\right),

(2.22)

together with the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}p^{\mu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{\nu}\partial p_{\nu}}-p^{\nu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{\nu}\partial p_{\mu}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{\mu}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda_{a}\partial\bar{\lambda}_{b}},

(2.23)

in which we note that the combination of second-order derivatives precisely matches that of eq. (
2.21
).

This is sufficient to write down a differential equation for the correlation function in terms of spinors only.
But the effectiveness of the spinor formalism goes further, as it also implies that we can decompose this equation into 3 components of definite helicity, writing

\widehat{K}^{\mu}=\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}}{2(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\left(\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{K}_{0}+\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}\widehat{K}_{-}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{K}_{+}\right).

(2.24)

To give explicit expressions for the differential operators
\widehat{K}_{0}
and
\widehat{K}_{\pm}
, it is convenient to construct all scalar, first-order derivative operators in spinors. There are four independent operators: one is the scale generator (
2.16
), the other three can be written

\widehat{H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda^{a}}-\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}^{a}}\right),\qquad\qquad\widehat{H}_{+}=\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}^{a}},\qquad\qquad\widehat{H}_{-}=\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda^{a}}.

(2.25)

\widehat{H}_{0}
counts the helicity, while
\widehat{H}_{+}
and
\widehat{H}_{-}
are ladder operator that raise and lower the helicity by one unit. Together, they form an
\text{su}(2)
algebra

\left[\widehat{H}_{0},\widehat{H}_{\pm}\right]=\pm\widehat{H}_{\pm},\qquad\qquad\left[\widehat{H}_{+},\widehat{H}_{-}\right]=2\widehat{H}_{0}.

(2.26)

Note that
\widehat{H}_{+}
and
\widehat{H}_{-}
are complex-conjugate to each other, and also that all three commute with
\widehat{D}
.
Any differential operator can then be expressed in terms of these: this is the case of the Lorentz generator

\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\widehat{M}_{\nu\rho}=\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\left(2\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{H}_{0}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{H}_{+}-\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}\widehat{H}_{-}\right),

(2.27)

and of the derivative with respect to
p

\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{\mu}}=\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}}{2(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\left(-2\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{D}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{H}_{+}+\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}\widehat{H}_{-}\right).

(2.28)

To express the components of
\widehat{K}^{\mu}
in this manner, one also needs to decompose the spin operator
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}
into scalar components. This can be done defining

\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{+}=\frac{(\zeta\cdot\lambda)\lambda^{a}}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta^{a}},\qquad\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{-}=-\frac{(\zeta\cdot\bar{\lambda})\bar{\lambda}^{a}}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta^{a}},\qquad\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{0}=-\frac{(\zeta\cdot\bar{\lambda})\lambda^{a}+(\zeta\cdot\lambda)\bar{\lambda}^{a}}{2\,\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta^{a}}.

(2.29)

These 3 operators obey the same
\text{su}(2)
algebra as the
\widehat{H}_{i}
, but act only on functions of the spinor
\zeta
. A set of differential operators acting on the auxiliary spinor
\zeta^{\prime}
can be defined similarly, denoted
\widehat{H}^{\zeta^{\prime}}_{i}
.
With these tools, the Ward identity (
2.13
) for Lorentz transformations can be projected onto components of definite helicity, leading to the three simple equations

\left[\widehat{H}_{i}-\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{i}-\widehat{H}^{\zeta^{\prime}}_{i}\right]\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=0,\qquad\qquad i\in\{0,+,-\}.

(2.30)

The same can be done with the components of
\widehat{K}_{\mu}
defined in eq. (
2.24
). We find

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{H}_{+}\widehat{H}_{-}-\widehat{H}_{+}\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{-}-\widehat{H}_{-}\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{+}+\widehat{D}(\widehat{D}-2\Delta+3)-\widehat{H}_{0}(\widehat{H}_{0}+1),

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{+}

\displaystyle=\widehat{H}_{+}(-\widehat{D}-\widehat{H}_{0}+\widehat{H}_{0}^{\zeta}+\Delta-2)+\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{+}(\widehat{D}+1),

(2.31)

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{-}

\displaystyle=\widehat{H}_{-}(-\widehat{D}+\widehat{H}_{0}-\widehat{H}_{0}^{\zeta}+\Delta-2)+\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{-}(\widehat{D}+1).

This is a major improvement compared to the momentum-space Ward identity (
2.21
): on the one hand, the helicity projection disentangles the equation into three independent components; on the other hand, it is very simple to apply the elementary differential operators in eq. (
2.31
) to
any
function of the spinors.
The scalar quantity
p^{2}=-(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}
is for instance annihilated by all
\widehat{H}_{i}
. Moreover, the polarization tensors (
2.6
) have simple transformation rules, being eigenstates of
\widehat{H}_{0}
,

\widehat{H}_{0}\,\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)=m\,\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta),

(2.32)

and satisfying ladder identities

\widehat{H}_{+}\,\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)=(j-m)\,\varepsilon_{j,m+1}(\zeta),\qquad\qquad\widehat{H}_{-}\,\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)=(j+m)\,\varepsilon_{j,m-1}(\zeta).

(2.33)

The numerical factors in these last two equations guarantee that
\widehat{H}_{\pm}
annihilate the highest-helicity polarizations
m=\pm j
respectively.
The other differential operators
\widehat{H}^{\zeta}_{i}
and
\widehat{H}^{\zeta^{\prime}}_{i}
act likewise, but only on polarization tensors defined in terms of the corresponding auxiliary spinor.

2.3
Correlation function

Now that the conformal Ward identities have been split into simple equations in terms of elementary ladder operators, it is straightforward to obtain the most general solution for the 2-point correlator (
2.10
).
Note that it is the overlap of two states that can each be decomposed into helicity eigenstates following eq. (
2.9
).
Moreover, since the correlation function (
2.10
) transform covariantly under the little group, its total helicity is zero. This implies that the decomposition into helicity eigenstates is diagonal, and that one can write

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\varepsilon_{j,-m}(\zeta^{\prime})\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j,-m}(-p)\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(p)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}.

(2.34)

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j,-m}(-p)\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(p)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
is a scalar function of the spinors
\lambda^{a}
and
\bar{\lambda}^{a}
, which means that it must necessarily be a function of
p^{2}=-(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}
only.
Its overall scaling dimension is determined by the Ward identity (
2.12
) to be
2\Delta-2j-3
.
We obtain immediately that

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j,-m}(-p)\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(p)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=(p^{2})^{\Delta-j-3/2}C_{\Delta,j,m},

(2.35)

where
C_{\Delta,j,m}
is a numerical coefficient that remains to be determined. Note that unitarity requires this coefficient to be non-negative.
Together, eqs. (
2.34
) and (
2.35
) form the most general ansatz for the 2-point function that is consistent with Poincaré and scale symmetry, i.e. that satisfies the Ward identities (
2.12
) and (
2.30
).

In fact, the zero-helicity component
\widehat{K}_{0}
of the special conformal Ward identity (
2.20
) is also readily satisfied by this ansatz.
This is not the case of the components
\widehat{K}_{\pm}
, however:
using

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{+}

\displaystyle\left[(p^{2})^{\Delta-j-3/2}\varepsilon_{j,-m}(\zeta^{\prime})\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)\right]=(p^{2})^{\Delta-j-3/2}

\displaystyle\times\left[(j+m)(\Delta-m-1)\varepsilon_{j,-m}(\zeta^{\prime})\varepsilon_{j,m-1}(\zeta)-(j-m)(\Delta+m-1)\varepsilon_{j,-m-1}(\zeta^{\prime})\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)\right],

(2.36)

or a similar identity for
\widehat{K}_{-}
related to this one by complex conjugation,
one obtains the condition

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-j}^{j-1}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j,-m-1}(\zeta^{\prime})\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)

\displaystyle\times\left[(j+m+1)(\Delta-m-2)C_{\Delta,j,m+1}-(j-m)(\Delta+m-1)C_{\Delta,j,m}\right]=0.

(2.37)

For the Ward identity to be satisfied, all individual terms in the sum must vanish separately. This gives a recursion relation for the coefficient
C_{\Delta,j,m}
, solved by

C_{\Delta,j,m}=\frac{(2j)!}{(j-m)!(j+m)!}\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+m-1)\Gamma(\Delta-m-1)}{\Gamma(\Delta+j-1)\Gamma(\Delta-j-1)}\,C_{\Delta,j}.

(2.38)

C_{\Delta,j}
is a normalization constant that is arbitrary up to its sign:
since
C_{\Delta,j,\pm j}=C_{\Delta,j}
corresponds to the norm of the eigenstate of helicity
\pm j
, it must therefore be positive.
Despite the appearance of gamma functions in eq. (
2.38
), the
C_{\Delta,j,m}
are in fact rational functions of
\Delta
multiplying this normalization constant: they can alternatively be written using the Pochhammer symbol as

C_{\Delta,j,m}=\frac{(2j)!}{(j-m)!(j+m)!}\frac{(\Delta-j-1)_{j-m}}{(\Delta+m-1)_{j-m}}\,C_{\Delta,j}.

(2.39)

Requiring that all helicity eigenstates have positive norm,
one obtains the unitarity bound

\Delta\geq j+1\qquad\qquad\text{for all}\quad j>\tfrac{1}{2}.

(2.40)

This is a well-known result, particularly transparent in this formalism.
When the bound is saturated, i.e. when
\Delta=j+1
, the operator only admits transverse polarizations with helicity
m=\pm j
: it is then a conserved current transforming in a short representation of the conformal group.
5
5

5

p_{a_{1}a_{2}}\varepsilon^{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}_{\pm j}=0
.

In all other cases the operator admits

Distinct operators.

We focused so far on 2-point functions of identical operators, but the methods presented in this section can also be used to examine the correlation function of two distinct operators. Based on Poincaré and scale symmetry, we can make the following ansatz:

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\sum_{m}C_{\Delta,j^{\prime},j,m}\varepsilon_{j^{\prime},-m}(\zeta^{\prime})\varepsilon_{j,m}(\zeta)(p^{2})^{(\Delta^{\prime}+\Delta-j^{\prime}-j-3)/2}.

(2.43)

\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta
are the scaling dimensions of
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{O}
respectively. The sum over
m
runs over
\left|m\right|\leq\min(j^{\prime},j)
, and in this way invariance under the little group is also satisfied.
When it comes to special conformal transformation, however, this ansatz is constrained further: the zero-helicity component
\widehat{K}_{0}
of the Ward identity (
2.20
) now gives

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(2.44)

\displaystyle=\big[(\Delta^{\prime}-\Delta)(\Delta^{\prime}+\Delta-3)+(j^{\prime}-j)(j^{\prime}+j+1)\big]\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}(-p,\zeta^{\prime})\mathcal{O}_{j}(p,\zeta)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}.

For identical spins
j^{\prime}=j
, special conformal symmetry requires that the two operators have either identical scaling dimension
\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta
, or that their scaling dimensions add up as
\Delta^{\prime}+\Delta=3
. This latter condition is only compatible with the unitarity bounds for scalar operators.
When the spins are not equal,
j^{\prime}\neq j
, the other Ward identities generated by
\widehat{K}_{\pm}
require the highest-helicity coefficient with
m=\pm\min(j^{\prime},j)
to vanish, and all other coefficients must therefore vanish as well (again assuming that the unitarity bounds are enforced).
This means that 2-point functions of distinct operators generically vanish in conformal field theory.
7
7

7

A possible exception concerns scalar operators with scaling dimensions adding up to 3, which is usually not realized in physical theories. The other exception concerns theories that have multiple operators in the same conformal representation: it is usually possible to diagonalize the basis of such operators, but not always
[
19
]
.
We shall not consider this possibility further.

This concludes the discussion on 2-point correlations functions. The results obtained here are not new: they have recently appeared in the bootstrap literature as an efficient tool for the construction of conformal blocks in position space
[
18
]
, and a similar construction in 4 space-time dimensions dates back to early days of conformal field theory half a century ago
[
10
,
11
]
.
8
8

8

See also refs.
[
20
,
21
]
for a recent use of the spinor-helicity formalism combined with conformal symmetry in the context of free theories.

The probable reason why this approach is absent from modern reviews on the topic is that it is too remote from the position-space representation in which the OPE is usually formulated, and in terms of which conformal blocks can be simply expressed as functions of invariant cross-ratios.
The goal of this paper is precisely to show that the spinor formalism can be carried through the whole construction of conformal blocks, and that it is a valuable tool in doing so.

3
3-point functions

Let us consider next 3-point correlation functions, specifically those involving two scalar operators, which we will denote by
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, and one arbitrary tensor operator
\mathcal{O}
(only integer-spin operators will in fact appear, as explained below). As before, translation symmetry implies that we can write

\left\langle 0\right|\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}(-p)\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\left|0\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(p-p_{2}-p_{1})\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}(-p)\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}.

(3.1)

One way to understand this Wightman function is as the overlap of two states, one obtained as the product of two operators acting successively on the vacuum,

\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\left|0\right\rangle,

(3.2)

and the other of the form discussed in the previous section,

\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}\ldots a_{2j}}(p_{1}+p_{2})\left|0\right\rangle.

(3.3)

Both are momentum-eigenstates, with eigenvalue
p=p_{1}+p_{2}
that we can assume to be in the forward light cone. We will also see that both can be expanded into helicity eigenstates: for the state (
3.3
) the helicity labels the spin of the operator, carried by its spinor indices, while for the state (
3.2
) it corresponds to angular momentum.
Before discussing the constraints of conformal symmetry that will determine this 3-point function up to a single OPE coefficient, we need to introduce a unified language that can account for these two distinct manifestations of helicity.

3.1
From momenta to spinors

Once the delta function imposing momentum conservation has been taken out of its definition, the correlator in eq. (
3.1
) is a function of two 3-momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
only.
The spectral condition on their sum is such that one can always parameterize it with a complex spinor as

p^{\mu}=p_{1}^{\mu}+p_{2}^{\mu}=\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\,\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b},

(3.4)

in complete analogy with eq. (
2.4
). However, the 3-point function does not only depend on the sum
p_{1}+p_{2}
, but also on the difference
p_{1}-p_{2}
. Depending on the kinematics, this difference can either be space-like, time-like, or even light-like. It is therefore not immediately well-suited to be expressed in terms of spinors.
Instead, let us consider the vector
9
9

9

In the particle-physics literature, and particularly in the context of bound states of 2-particles,
q
is sometimes called the
relative momentum in the center-of-mass frame
. It is purely spatial (
q^{0}=0
) in a Lorentz frame in which
\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2}=0
. Moreover, if the “constituent masses”
p_{1}^{2}
and
p_{2}^{2}
are equal, then
\mathbf{q}\propto\mathbf{p}_{1}-\mathbf{p}_{2}
. Eq. (
3.5

q^{\mu}=\frac{\big[(p_{1}+p_{2})\cdot p_{2}\big]p_{1}^{\mu}-\big[(p_{1}+p_{2})\cdot p_{1}\big]p_{2}^{\mu}}{\sqrt{(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})^{2}-p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2}}}.

(3.5)

This is the unique combination of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
(up to a sign) that is orthogonal to
p
, i.e.
p\cdot q=0
, and that has the same norm in absolute value,
q^{2}=-p^{2}
(

These two conditions are sufficient to give it a parameterization in terms of the same complex spinor:

q^{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\left(\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\right).

(3.6)

q
is a real vector, but unlike
p
, it is not invariant under the little group (
2.5
).

Figure 1:
Given a pair of momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
that add up to
p

inside the forward light cone, shown here in the center-of-mass
frame (
\mathbf{p}=0
), one can always define two null momenta

q_{1}
and
q_{2}
that are the projections of

onto the light cone
in the plane spanned by
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
.
The space-like vector
q=q_{1}-q_{2}

satisfies
p\cdot q=0

.

A different perspective of the same parameterization goes as follows: there is a unique pair of null vectors
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
pointing in the forward direction that is formed of linear combinations of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
and adds up to

\displaystyle q_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{p_{2}\cdot(p_{1}+p_{2})}{\sqrt{(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})^{2}-p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2}}}\right)p_{1}^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}\cdot(p_{1}+p_{2})}{\sqrt{(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})^{2}-p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2}}}\right)p_{2}^{\mu},

(3.7)

\displaystyle q_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p_{2}\cdot(p_{1}+p_{2})}{\sqrt{(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})^{2}-p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2}}}\right)p_{1}^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{p_{1}\cdot(p_{1}+p_{2})}{\sqrt{(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})^{2}-p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2}}}\right)p_{2}^{\mu}.

This is illustrated in figure
1
.
Each of these two null vectors can then be written using a real spinor,

q_{1}^{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\lambda_{1}^{a}\lambda_{1}^{b},\qquad\qquad q_{2}^{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\lambda_{2}^{a}\lambda_{2}^{b}.

(3.8)

Both the parameterization (
3.4
) for
p=q_{1}+q_{2}
and (
3.6
) for
q=q_{1}-q_{2}
are recovered upon identifying
\lambda^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\lambda_{1}^{a}+i\lambda_{2}^{a}\right)
. Note that this procedure does not depend whether
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are space-like or time-like
(even though the spectral condition also requires
p_{1}
to be in the forward light cone).
The only requirement is that the two vectors are not colinear, so that

(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})^{2}-p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2}>0
.

It is important to realize that
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are
projections
of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
onto the light cone, and as such the map is not invertible: to reconstruct
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
from of the massless vectors
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, we also need information about their length.
To keep track of these, we introduce the dimensionless scalar quantities

x
and
y
, defined by the equations

x(1-y)=\frac{p_{1}^{2}}{(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}},\qquad\qquad(1-x)y=\frac{p_{2}^{2}}{(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}},

(3.9)

in terms of which we can write

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\left[(1+x-y)\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,(1-x-y)\left(\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\right)\right],

(3.10)

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\left[(1+x-y)\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(1-x-y)\left(\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\right)\right].

This is the central observation of this work: any 3-point function can be parameterized with a
unique
complex spinor, like the 2-point function, together with a pair of scalar quantities. The map

\left\{\lambda^{a},\bar{\lambda}^{a},x,y\right\}\qquad\to\qquad\left\{p_{1}^{\mu},p_{2}^{\mu}\right\}

(3.11)

is surjective, with both sides described by six real parameters. It is also regular as long as
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are not colinear, i.e. for
(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})^{2}-p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}(1-x-y)^{2}\neq 0
.
If we restrict the range of
x
and
y
to the half-plane
x+y<1
, then the map (
3.11
) is in fact one-to-one.
10
10

10

The parameterization in terms of
x
and
y
provides in fact a double cover of the real kinematic domain in terms of
p_{1}^{2}/(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}
,
p_{2}^{2}/(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}
, but it has the advantage of avoiding the square root that is otherwise present in the definition (
3.5
) of
q
.

The problem of expressing the conformal Ward identities in this new set of parameters can therefore essentially be reduced to the computation of a Jacobian.

Before moving forward, let us emphasize once more that this construction is very specific to Minkowski space-time (spinors parameterize null vectors), and also to Wightman functions (the spectral condition is needed to guarantee that at least one momentum sits inside the forward light cone). It is not at all obvious how this could be extended to Euclidean space, or even to time-ordered correlation functions in Minkowski space-time.

3.2
Conformal symmetry

One of the great features of the map (
3.11
) is that the two quantities
x
and
y
are
scalar
and
dimensionless
.
Only the spinor
\lambda^{a}
and its complex conjugate are dimensionful and transform under Lorentz symmetry. This implies that it is straightforward to construct objects that transforms covariantly under these symmetries, up to arbitrary functions of
x
and
y
.

In particular, all dependence on spinor indices can be expressed in terms of the polarization tensors (
2.6
), or their index-free form (
2.7
). This also implies that the decomposition (
2.9
) into helicity eigenstates is still valid, and that one can write

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j}(-p,\zeta)\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\varepsilon_{j,-m}(\zeta)\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(-p)\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}},

(3.12)

where the correlator on the right-hand side is now a function of scalar quantities only, namely
x
,

and
\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}

p_{1}^{2}
,
p_{2}^{2}
and
p^{2}
.
Moreover, since the overall scaling dimension of the correlator is known, we can further deduce that

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(-p)\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=(p^{2})^{(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta-j-6)/2}F_{j,m}(x,y),

and

,
\phi_{2}
and

3.3
Solutions

In addition to this recursion relation, the two equations (

3.25

\displaystyle w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}w_{2}^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,j}(x,y),

\displaystyle w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}f_{\Delta_{1},3-\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,j}(x,y),

\displaystyle w_{2}^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}f_{3-\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,j}(x,y),

\displaystyle\quad\times F_{4}\left(\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2},\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta-3+j}{2},\Delta_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2},\Delta_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2};x(1-y),(1-x)y\right).

\displaystyle f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,j}^{(a,b)}(x,y)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2}\right)_{r}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta-3+j}{2}\right)_{r}\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right)_{r}}{r!\left(\Delta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}+a\right)_{r}\left(\Delta_{2}-\frac{1}{2}+b\right)_{r}}x^{a+r}y^{b+r}

\displaystyle\times{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2}+r,\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta-3+j}{2}+r,\Delta_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}+a+r;x\right)

\displaystyle\times{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2}+r,\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta-3+j}{2}+r,\Delta_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}+b+r;y\right),

(3.30)

\displaystyle\chi_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,m}^{(n,i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{j+m+n+i}}{i!(n-i)!}\frac{4^{j-m}(m+1)_{j-m}}{(2m+1)_{j-m-n}}\frac{\left(\frac{\Delta+j-\Delta_{12}}{2}-n+i\right)_{n-i}\left(\frac{\Delta+j+\Delta_{12}}{2}-i\right)_{i}}{\left(\Delta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{n-i}\left(\Delta_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{i}(\Delta+m-1)_{j-m}}

Some important properties of the 3-point function can be deduced from this general solution:

\circ

If

3.4
Correlation function

We are now armed with a set of functions that satisfy all constraints imposed by conformal symmetry. But what linear combination of solutions corresponds to the Wightman correlation function?

Conformal Ward identities alone are not enough to answer this question. In fact, for generic (non-integer) value of the scaling dimensions, different correlation functions obey the same set of identities: Wightman functions, time-ordered products, and retarded products can all be expressed as linear combinations of the solutions that we just found.
Additional information is needed to distinguish between these various cases. It can be obtained using analyticity properties that follow from the micro-causality condition
[
24
]
, or in the specific case of Wightman functions, studying some OPE limits
[
9
]
.
We refer the reader to the relevant work for more details, and focus here on the result.

In the case where both
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are inside the forward light cone, one finds

\displaystyle F_{j,m}(x,y)

\displaystyle=4\pi\mathcal{N}\,\frac{\sin\left[\pi\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j+3}{2}\right]}{\Gamma\left(\Delta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg[\Gamma\left(\tfrac{3}{2}-\Delta_{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j-3}{2}\right)w_{2}^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,m}(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\Gamma\left(\Delta_{2}-\tfrac{3}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j+3}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j}{2}\right)f_{\Delta_{1},3-\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,m}(x,y)\bigg].

(3.34)

This is the unique linear combination of solutions that is on the one hand proportional to
w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}
as
p_{1}^{2}\to 0
(the other two solutions are analytic in

\phi_{1}(p_{1})\left|0\right\rangle

1-w_{2}
as
w_{2}\to 1_{-}

p_{1}^{\mu}\to 0

The constant

[

in which the OPE coefficient

is defined from the position-space correlation function, we have

\mathcal{N}=\frac{(4\pi)^{d}(-i)^{j}(\Delta-1)_{j}\,\lambda_{12\mathcal{O}}}{2^{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta}\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1}+\Delta+j}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j-3}{2}\right)}.

(3.35)

When
p_{2}
is instead space-like, we have

\displaystyle F_{j,m}(x,y)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}\mathcal{N}\,\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\Delta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}-\Delta_{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j-3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2}\right)}(-w_{2})^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,m}(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\frac{\Gamma\left(\Delta_{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j+3}{2}\right)}f_{\Delta_{1},3-\Delta_{2},\Delta,j,m}(x,y)\bigg].

(3.36)

This only differs from eq. (

m=+j
takes a particularly simple form thanks to a property of the Appell
F_{4}
function: it can be written

\displaystyle F_{j,j}(x,y)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}\mathcal{N}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j-3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\Delta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}(-w_{2})^{-(\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j)/2}f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta,\Delta_{2},j,j}\left(\frac{x}{x-1},\frac{1}{y}\right),

(3.37)

in terms of a unique Appell
F_{4}
hypergeometric function in which the roles of
\Delta_{2}
and
\Delta
have been exchanged. In this case the correlation function is still proportional to
w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}
as

, but it has also the scaling limit of a state created by the operator
\mathcal{O}
when
(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}\to 0

Unfortunately, the type of transformation property relating series expansions around

and

is special to the Appell

12

12

In fact, solving the conformal Ward identities in the case where all 3 operators carry spin will contribute to answering this question, as one can then always choose the reference momentum — the one expressed in terms of spinors as in eq. (

The results presented in this section are the main output of this work. We have shown how the parameterization of the 3-point function with spinors helps express the conformal Ward identities in terms of simple ladder operators, how the various helicity eigenstates are related by the action of a differential operator, and we even arrived at a closed-form solution valid when

Before concluding, we want to illustrate in the next section how this 3-point function can be used as elementary building block for the construction of higher-point Wightman functions.

4
The OPE for higher-point functions

Our results for the 2- and 3-point correlation functions implicitly define a momentum-space OPE for conformal primary operators acting on the vacuum, and the goal of this short section is to illustrate its conceptual simplicity.

The existence of an operator product expansion in quantum field theory is intrinsically related to the completeness of the Hilbert space: if all the states created by a single local operator acting on the vacuum span the entire Hilbert space, then the product of two (or more) operators must also be expressible in that basis. In other words, we must be able to write

\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\left|0\right\rangle\approx\sum_{\mathcal{O}}C_{12\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{O}(p_{1}+p_{2})\left|0\right\rangle,

(4.1)

where
C_{12\mathcal{O}}
is an object that depends on the momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
.
This statement can be made sharp in conformal field theory thanks to radial quantization in Euclidean position space.
13
13

13

Another salient feature of radial quantization is that the convergence rate of the OPE is controlled by the separation of operators in space, and this can be used to show that the OPE is absolutely convergent over most of the configuration space. There is no such argument in momentum space, and therefore we generically only expect convergence in the sense of distributions (see however ref.
[
25
]
for a counterexample in 2 dimensions).

In this case however the sum does not only involve primary operators on the right-hand side, but also descendants.
This inconvenience disappears when working in momentum space, as there is a unique momentum eigenstate in each conformal family that can appear on the right-hand side of eq. (
4.1
).

The other difficulty in defining the OPE has to do with spin: even when the operators appearing on the left-hand side of eq. (
4.1
) are scalars, the fact that they are inserted at separated points — or come with distinct momenta — means that the state carries angular momentum, and thus that the operator on the right-hand side might have spin.
This is where the spinor-helicity formalism becomes extremely useful, as it allows to further decompose the OPE (
4.1
) into helicity eigenstates.
14
14

14

Strictly speaking the spinor-helicity formalism is not necessary to do so: when the two operators are scalars, the total spin is given by the angular momentum, which means that the polarization tensors can be constructed out of the momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
only. This fact has been used in ref.
[
23
]
to write down the OPE for a scalar 4-point function in general space-time dimension without making use of spinors.

The decomposition (
2.9
) can be used to absorb the polarization tensors into the object
C_{12\mathcal{O}}
and write

\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\left|0\right\rangle=\sum_{\mathcal{O}}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}C^{(j,m)}_{12\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(p_{1}+p_{2})\left|0\right\rangle.

(4.2)

C_{12\mathcal{O}}
is now a mere function of the momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, which can be computed taking the overlap of both sides of this equation with a helicity eigenstate
\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(p)\left|0\right\rangle
.
We find

C^{(j,m)}_{12\mathcal{O}}=\frac{\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(-p)\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}}{\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(-p)\mathcal{O}_{j,m}(p)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}}=(p^{2})^{(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j-3)/2}C_{\Delta,j,m}^{-1}F_{j,m}(x,y),

(4.3)

where the coefficient
C_{\Delta,j,m}
is given in eq. (
2.38
), the function
F_{j,m}
in eq. (
3.34
), and
x
,
y
are defined in terms of the momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in eq. (
3.9
).
The dynamical OPE coefficient is contained in the definition of
F_{j,m}
.

As it is, this result is valid when the helicity of the operator
\mathcal{O}_{j,m}
on the right-hand side of eq. (
4.2
) is defined with respect to the same spinors
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
used to parameterized the momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
as in eq. (
3.10
).
This is not necessary, however, and in fact it is not wanted in the perspective of iterating the OPE.
Thankfully, the freedom to choose a different basis of spinors is easy to implement in eq. (
4.2
):
any other basis will be related to
\{\lambda^{a},\bar{\lambda}^{a}\}
by a little group transformation (
2.5
). If we define this new basis by

\lambda^{\prime a}=e^{i\theta}\lambda^{a},\qquad\qquad\bar{\lambda}^{\prime a}=e^{-i\theta}\bar{\lambda}^{a},

(4.4)

then we can again compute the overlap of eq. (
4.2
) with an eigenstate of helicity given in the original basis.
This time, the computation involves contracting the indices of two polarization tensors of the form (
2.6
) that satisfy

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{-m^{\prime}}\cdot\varepsilon_{m}=\delta_{m^{\prime}m}e^{i\theta m}(-1)^{j}(p^{2})^{j}.

(4.5)

In other words, the change of basis only accounts for a phase
e^{i\theta m}
in the coefficient (
4.3
), which is trivial to implement in a computation.

To give a concrete example, this OPE can be used to decompose the 4-point correlation function of scalar primary operators into conformal partial waves. We find immediately

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\phi_{4}(p_{4})\phi_{3}(p_{3})\phi_{2}(p_{2})\phi_{1}(p_{1})\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=s^{(\Delta_{\Sigma}-9)/2}\sum_{\mathcal{O}}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}C_{\Delta,j,m}^{-1}e^{im\theta}F_{j,-m}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{*}F_{j,m}(x,y)

(4.6)

where
s=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}=(p_{3}+p_{4})^{2}
is the square of the center-of-mass energy, the variables
x
,
y
,
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
are defined by

x(1-y)=\frac{p_{1}^{2}}{s},\qquad(1-x)y=\frac{p_{2}^{2}}{s},\qquad(1-x^{\prime})y^{\prime}=\frac{p_{3}^{2}}{s},\qquad x^{\prime}(1-y^{\prime})=\frac{p_{4}^{2}}{s},

(4.7)

and
\theta
is the angle formed by the space-like vector
q
defined in terms of momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in eq. (
3.5
) and a similar vector defined in terms of
p_{3}
and
p_{4}
. We have also denoted
\Delta_{\Sigma}=\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}+\Delta_{4}
.
Note that because of the symmetry relation (
3.32
) the phase always appears in the linear combination
e^{im\theta}+e^{-im\theta}=2\cos(m\theta)
, which is (twice) the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{m}(\cos\theta)
corresponding to spherical harmonics in 3 space-time dimensions. This result precisely matches the conformal partial waves obtained in ref.
[
23
]
in the special case
d=3
.
A significant improvement with respect to that work is that the 3-point functions are now known exactly, whereas they were given by a recursion relation there.
15
15

15

The two-step recursion relation for the 3-point function given in ref.
[
23

5
Conclusions

This work develops a new formalism for conformal correlators in terms of spinor variables.
We have emphasized the usefulness of spinors to decompose the states created by a single conformal primary operator acting on the vacuum into helicity eigenstates, and extended this formalism to the product of two operators acting on the vacuum.
While the 3-point functions involved in these computation are not completely new, the novel formalism that we used makes their derivation particularly clear when compared with other methods.

This work is also an important milestone in a larger research program: the construction of all conformal Wightman functions in Minkowski momentum space. The state-of-the-art in this area can be summarized as follows:

1.

Wightman 2-point functions in momentum space have been known since the early days of conformal field theory, and used in the constructions of all unitary representations
[
11
]
.
They recently re-appeared as an efficient way of computing Euclidean conformal blocks in ref.
[
18
]
.
For tensor operators, a construction valid in any space-time dimension has been presented in ref.
[
8
]
.

2.

The Wightman 3-point function of scalar operators was constructed for the first time in closed-form in ref.
[
9
]
.
An integral representation particularly useful for holographic applications is also available
[
26
]
.

3.

The 3-point function of two scalars and one symmetric tensor operator was also presented in ref.
[
9
]
, but its projection onto spin eigenstates only appeared in ref.
[
23
]
. This allowed for a convenient decomposition of the 4-point function into partial waves in the same work.
16
16

16

An interesting feature of all these results is that they are analytic in the space-time dimension
d
. This is a peculiarity of working only with tensor representations of the Lorentz group that exist in any
d
.

The obvious next step is to compute Wightman 3-point functions of operators carrying arbitrary Lorentz representations. This would not only grant access to partial wave expansions for generic 4-point functions, but also for any higher-point function through the momentum-space OPE.
17
17

17

The current formalism only allows for the computation of higher-point partial waves in which one of the intermediate operator is a scalar.

The present work does not yet present results for these generic 3-point function, but it takes an important step in this direction. The derivation of conformal Ward identities in terms of helicity ladder operators in not conceptually different from what has been presented here. It is however vastly more complicated to solve the resulting partial differential equations, and for this reason the results will be presented in a separated work.
The goal of the present paper is therefore to introduce the method, and illustrate its simplicity when applied to 3-point correlation functions involving two scalar operators.
For the same reason, we have chosen to work here exclusively in 3 space-time dimensions. The results will be generalized to conformal field theory in 4 and higher dimensions elsewhere.

The informed reader will also wonder what is the physical purpose of this program, besides its mathematical interest. There is in fact already a large body of literature on the construction of correlation functions that have the isometries of the Euclidean conformal group
\text{SO}(d+1,1)
,
both for CFT in flat space, of for QFT in AdS. Many works have been specifically dedicated to the study of operators transforming in various non-trivial representations
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.
In fact, the problem has essentially been solved by the introduction of weight-shifting operators
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
.
One aspect in which our approach differs from the standard literature is that it relies on the Lorentzian conformal group
\text{SO}(d,2)
. However, one might argue that the two are substantially equivalent thanks to the simple analytic continuation that is possible between Euclidean correlators and Wightman functions in Minkowski space-time
[
35
]
.

Nevertheless, there are at least three directions of research that will actively benefit from the development of this program.
The first one is the study of higher-point correlation functions, both in CFT and in AdS. The computation of conformal blocks for functions of more than 4 points is quite difficult, and various methods have been used
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
.
It is obvious that the factorization observed in the Minkowski momentum space OPE present important advantages, even though it only applies to Wightman functions.
The second direction of research is Hamiltonian truncation in infinite volume
[
44
,
45
]
, where one of the computational bottlenecks is the evaluation of matrix elements that correspond to a particular limit of the Wightman 3-point functions
[
46
]
.
Finally, the last area in which this work can have an impact is quantum field theory in de Sitter space-time. Many recent works have exploited the Euclidean conformal symmetry of late-time correlators
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51

]
, but the Lorentzian conformal group also plays an important role in the in-in formalism recently put forward
[

Appendix A
Conventions for spinors

We work in 3-dimensional Minkowski space-time with the mostly-minus metric
18
18

18

While the mostly-plus metric is arguably the best choice in position space, as it makes the analytic continuation between Euclidean and Minkowski spaces obvious, the mostly-minus metric is definitely more convenient to work with in momentum space.

\eta_{\mu\nu}=\diag(1,-1,-1)_{\mu\nu}
, where
\mu=0,1,2
.
Let
z^{\mu}
be a null vector pointing in the forward direction,

z^{2}=(z^{0})^{2}-(z^{1})^{2}-(z^{2})^{2}=0,\qquad\qquad z^{0}>0.

(A.1)

z^{\mu}
is conveniently parameterized in terms of a two-component real spinor
\zeta^{a}
as

z^{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\zeta^{a}\zeta^{b},

(A.2)

where repeated indices
a,b=1,2
are summed over, and
\sigma^{\mu}
are the
2\times 2
symmetric matrices

\sigma^{0}_{ab}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)_{ab},\qquad\sigma^{1}_{ab}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)_{ab},\qquad\sigma^{2}_{ab}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)_{ab}.

(A.3)

We may sometimes also use the 2-index notation for a vector,
z^{\mu}\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}z^{ab}
.
Introducing

\varepsilon_{ab}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right)_{ab},

(A.4)

we have the identity

\eta_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\sigma^{\nu}_{cd}=\varepsilon_{ac}\varepsilon_{bd}+\varepsilon_{ad}\varepsilon_{bc}.

(A.5)

Lorentz-invariant quantities can therefore be constructed out of spinors with upper indices contracted with the matrix
\varepsilon
with lower indices: for instance, the antisymmetric scalar product of two spinors is defined by

\zeta\cdot\xi\equiv\varepsilon_{ab}\zeta^{a}\xi^{b}=-\xi\cdot\zeta.

(A.6)

For
z^{\mu}
defined in eq. (
A.2
) and another vector
x^{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\xi^{a}\xi^{b}
, we have

z\cdot x=\eta_{\mu\nu}z^{\mu}x^{\nu}=\tfrac{1}{4}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\sigma^{\nu}_{cd}\zeta^{a}\zeta^{b}\xi^{c}\xi^{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon_{ac}\varepsilon_{bd}\zeta^{a}\zeta^{b}\xi^{c}\xi^{d}=\frac{1}{2}(\zeta\cdot x)^{2}.

(A.7)

It is sometimes convenient to introduce the contravariant spinor

\zeta_{a}\equiv\varepsilon_{ab}\zeta^{b},

(A.8)

in terms of which the scalar product can be written

\zeta\cdot\xi=\zeta^{a}\xi_{a}=-\zeta_{a}\xi^{a}.

(A.9)

A lower spinor index can be raised with the inverse tensor
\varepsilon^{ab}=-\varepsilon^{ba}
satisfying

\varepsilon^{ac}\varepsilon_{cb}=\varepsilon_{bc}\varepsilon^{ca}=\delta^{a}_{b},

(A.10)

so that
\varepsilon^{ab}\zeta_{b}=\zeta^{a}
.
Note that both the (inverse) metric
\eta^{\mu\nu}
and the Levi-Civita symbol
\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}
can be expressed in terms of
\sigma^{\mu}
through the identity

(\sigma^{\mu}\cdot\sigma^{\nu})_{ab}\equiv-\sigma^{\mu}_{ac}\varepsilon^{cd}\sigma^{\nu}_{db}=\eta^{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{ab}+\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}(\sigma_{\rho})_{ab}.

(A.11)

Complex spinors.

Since spinors have two components, any pair of linearly independent spinors can be used as a basis. We shall take the real spinors
\lambda_{1}^{a}
and
\lambda_{2}^{a}
to form such a basis, or more conveniently the complex spinors
\lambda^{a}
and
\bar{\lambda}^{a}
that are conjugate to each other, defined by

\lambda^{a}=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{a}+i\lambda_{2}^{a}}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad\qquad\bar{\lambda}^{a}=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{a}-i\lambda_{2}^{a}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(A.12)

This means that we can write

\varepsilon^{ab}=\frac{\bar{\lambda}^{a}\lambda^{b}-\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}},

(A.13)

or express any other spinor in this basis through

\zeta^{a}=\frac{(\zeta\cdot\bar{\lambda})\lambda^{a}-(\zeta\cdot\lambda)\bar{\lambda}^{a}}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}.

(A.14)

These two spinors can be used to parameterize a momentum inside the forward light cone as in eq. (
2.4
),

p^{\mu}=\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\,\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\left(\lambda_{1}^{a}\lambda_{1}^{b}+\lambda_{2}^{a}\lambda_{2}^{b}\right).

(A.15)

Note that the energy
p^{0}=\left|\lambda_{1}\right|^{2}+\left|\lambda_{2}\right|^{2}
is positive, and likewise

p^{2}=-(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}=(\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{2})^{2}>0.

(A.16)

With our conventions the scalar product
\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}
is purely imaginary.

Spinor derivatives.

The spinor derivative is defined by the equation

\frac{\partial\zeta^{b}}{\partial\zeta^{a}}=\delta_{a}^{b},

(A.17)

or equivalently

\frac{\partial\zeta^{b}}{\partial\zeta_{a}}=\varepsilon^{ab},

(A.18)

which implies for the scalar product

\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta^{a}}(\zeta\cdot\xi)=\xi_{a},\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{a}}(\zeta\cdot\xi)=-\zeta_{a}.

(A.19)

When applied to a function of
z^{\mu}
, the chain rule gives

\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial\zeta^{a}}=\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\zeta^{b}\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z^{\mu}}.

(A.20)

Using the identity (
A.11
), we can form the combination

\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\zeta^{a}\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial\zeta_{b}}=\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}z_{\nu}\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z^{\rho}}.

(A.21)

This is precisely the derivative operator appearing in the definition (
2.15
) of the spin operator: when applied to a correlation function in which Lorentz indices are contracted with an auxiliary vector
z^{\mu}
, the spin operator takes the familiar form
[
2
,
16
]

\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu\nu}(z)=z_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\nu}}-z_{\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\mu}}.

(A.22)

Derivatives with respect to momenta.

Expressing derivatives with respect to momenta in terms of spinors is the key element in the computation of the conformal Ward identities for 2- and 3-point functions. In the presence of a single momentum
p
, the form of this derivative was given in eq. (
2.22
), and its projection onto helicity components in eq. (
2.28
).
This led to the representations (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) for the generators of scale and Lorentz transformations. For special conformal transformations, the operator
\widehat{K}^{\mu}
defined in eq. (
2.21
) is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\bigg[\,

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda_{a}\partial\bar{\lambda}_{b}}+\frac{\Delta-2}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\left(\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{b}}-\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{b}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\left(\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{c}}+\bar{\lambda}^{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{a}}-\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{c}}-\lambda^{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{a}}\right)\left(\zeta^{b}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta^{c}}+\zeta_{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta_{b}}\right)\bigg].

(A.23)

Its helicity components have been reported in eq. (
2.31
).

For functions of the two momenta
p_{1}^{\mu}
and
p_{2}^{\mu}
defined in eq. (
3.10
), the algebra is slightly more complicated but the logic is the same. One can show that derivatives with respect to
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{1\mu}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\left(\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{b}}-\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{b}}\right)-\frac{\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}-\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}}{(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-y}\,\widehat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle-2\frac{p_{1}^{ab}}{(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\frac{1}{1-x-y}\left((1-x)^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+y^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)

(A.24)

\displaystyle+2\frac{p_{2}^{ab}}{(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\frac{1}{1-x-y}\left(x(1-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+y(1-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)\bigg],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{2\mu}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda}}\left(\bar{\lambda}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{b}}-\lambda^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{b}}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}-\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}}{(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\frac{1+x-y}{1-x-y}\,\widehat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle+2\frac{p_{1}^{ab}}{(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\frac{1}{1-x-y}\left(x(1-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+y(1-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)

(A.25)

\displaystyle-2\frac{p_{2}^{ab}}{(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\frac{1}{1-x-y}\left(x^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+(1-y)^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)\bigg],

where we have used the operator
\widehat{H}_{0}
defined in eq. (
2.25
).
They can equivalently be rewritten in the helicity basis as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{1\mu}}=\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}}{2(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\bigg[

\displaystyle-2\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\left(\widehat{D}+(1-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)

\displaystyle+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\left(\widehat{H}_{+}-(1-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-y}\widehat{H}_{0}\right)

(A.26)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}\left(\widehat{H}_{-}-(1-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}-\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-y}\widehat{H}_{0}\right)\bigg],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{2\mu}}=\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}}{2(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\bigg[

\displaystyle-2\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\left(\widehat{D}-x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+(1-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)

\displaystyle+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\left(\widehat{H}_{+}+x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+(1-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}-\frac{1+x-y}{1-x-y}\widehat{H}_{0}\right)

(A.27)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}\left(\widehat{H}_{-}+x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+(1-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\frac{1+x-y}{1-x-y}\widehat{H}_{0}\right)\bigg].

Note that when they are applied to functions that are independent of
x
and
y
and that carry zero total helicity, both differential operators reduce to
\partial/\partial p_{\mu}
given in eq. (
2.22
). Moreover, one can form some linear combinations of the two operators that do not involve derivatives with respect to
x
or
y
, and recover in this way the generators of scale and Lorentz transformations:

\sum_{i=1}^{2}\,p_{i}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}^{\mu}}=\widehat{D},\qquad\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[p_{i\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}^{\mu}}-p_{i\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}^{\nu}}\right]=\widehat{M}_{\mu\nu}.

(A.28)

This shows that the 3-point correlation function obeys the simple Ward identities (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) that are independent of
x
or
y

On the contrary, the Ward identity for special conformal transformations involves derivatives with respect to
x
and
y
.
If we denote with
\widehat{k}^{\mu}
the part of the differential operator (
3.17
) that is second-order in derivatives of

, we can write

\widehat{k}^{\mu}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}p_{i}^{\mu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p_{i}^{\nu}\partial p_{i\nu}}-p_{i}^{\nu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p_{i}^{\nu}\partial p_{i\mu}}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ab}}{2(\lambda\cdot\bar{\lambda})^{2}}\left(\lambda^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{k}_{0}+\bar{\lambda}^{a}\bar{\lambda}^{b}\widehat{k}_{+}+\lambda^{a}\lambda^{b}\widehat{k}_{-}\right),

(A.29)

and we find

\displaystyle\widehat{k}_{0}

\displaystyle=2\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}_{3,3;0,0}+\frac{1}{2}(\widehat{H}_{+}\widehat{H}_{-}+\widehat{H}_{-}\widehat{H}_{+})+\widehat{D}(\widehat{D}-3)-\frac{(1-x+y)(1+x-y)}{(1-x-y)^{2}}\widehat{H}_{0}^{2},

\displaystyle\widehat{k}_{+}

\displaystyle=\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}_{3,3}-\widehat{H}_{+}(\widehat{D}+\widehat{H}_{0}-1)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{1-x-y}\left[2x(1-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-2y(1-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+(x-y)(2-\widehat{H}_{0})\right]\widehat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle\widehat{k}_{-}

\displaystyle=\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}_{3,3}-\widehat{H}_{-}(\widehat{D}-\widehat{H}_{0}-1)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{1-x-y}\left[2x(1-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-2y(1-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+(x-y)(2+\widehat{H}_{0})\right]\widehat{H}_{0}.

(A.30)

\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}
and
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}
are differential operators in
x
and

, corresponding to the definitions (
3.19
) and (
3.20

\Delta=m=0

Appendix B
Generalized hypergeometric series

This appendix discusses in some detail the algebra of partial differential equations generated by the operators
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}
and
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}
defined in eqs. (
3.19
) and (
3.20
), and whose indices we omit for compactness of the notation.
For simplicity, we will also use

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{6-\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2},

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{2}-\Delta_{1},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{3-\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j}{2},

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{2}-\Delta_{2}.

(B.1)

The first observation is that, when acting on a function of the two variables

w_{1}=x(1-y),\qquad\qquad w_{2}=(1-x)y,

(B.2)

the differential operators
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}
and
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}
satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}

\displaystyle+\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-y}\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}\bigg)f(w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle=\bigg[

\displaystyle w_{1}(1-w_{1})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{1}^{2}}-2w_{1}w_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{1}\partial w_{2}}-w_{2}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{2}^{2}}

(B.3)

\displaystyle+\left(\gamma_{1}-(\alpha+\beta+1)w_{1}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{1}}-(\alpha+\beta+1)w_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{2}}-\alpha\beta\bigg]f(w_{1},w_{2}),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}

\displaystyle-\frac{1+x-y}{1-x-y}\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}\bigg)f(w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle=\bigg[

\displaystyle w_{2}(1-w_{2})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{2}^{2}}-2w_{1}w_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{1}\partial w_{2}}-w_{1}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{1}^{2}}

(B.4)

\displaystyle+\left(\gamma_{2}-(\alpha+\beta+1)w_{2}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{2}}-(\alpha+\beta+1)w_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{1}}-\alpha\beta\bigg]f(w_{1},w_{2}).

This is the system of Appell
F_{4}
differential equations in its standard form. It is solved by the double hypergeometric series

f(w_{1},w_{2})=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{i+j}(\beta)_{i+j}}{i!j!(\gamma_{1})_{i}(\gamma_{2})_{j}}w_{1}^{i}w_{2}^{j}\equiv F_{4}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2};w_{1},w_{2}).

(B.5)

Alternatively, if one did not know about the Appell
F_{4}
series, a solution to this system of differential equations could be obtained in the following way. Note that
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}
and
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}
look very much like the sum and difference of the differential operators generating the ordinary hypergeometric series in
x
and
y
respectively: if one denotes with

\widehat{Q}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;x)=x(1-x)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+\left[\gamma-(\alpha+\beta+1)x\right]\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-\alpha\beta

(B.6)

the operator that annihilate the ordinary hypergeometric function,

\widehat{Q}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;x)\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;x)=0,

(B.7)

then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}

\displaystyle=\widehat{Q}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{1};x)+\widehat{Q}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{2};y)

\displaystyle\quad-(\alpha+\beta-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}+1)\left[\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-y}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\frac{1+x-y}{1-x-y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right]

(B.8)

and

\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}=\widehat{Q}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{1};x)-\widehat{Q}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{2};y)+(\alpha+\beta-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}+1)\left[x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right].

(B.9)

If it were not for the last term that involves both
x
and
y
, these operators would simply generate decoupled systems of hypergeometric equations in
x
and
y
.
Therefore, this suggests looking for a solution that can be written as a product of
{}_{2}F_{1}
functions in
x
and
y
.
If we introduce

f_{r}(x,y)=x^{r}y^{r}{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{1}+r;x){}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{2}+r;y),

(B.10)

then we can use properties of the
{}_{2}F_{1}
function to show that

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}f_{r}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(\alpha+r)(\alpha+\beta-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}+1+r)f^{\,\rm I}_{r}(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{r(\gamma_{1}+r-1)(\gamma_{2}+r-1)}{\beta+r-1}f^{\,\rm I}_{r-1}(x,y),

(B.11)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}f_{r}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(\alpha+r)(\alpha+\beta-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}+1+r)f^{\,\rm II}_{r}(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{r(\gamma_{1}+r-1)(\gamma_{2}+r-1)}{\beta+r-1}f^{\,\rm II}_{r-1}(x,y),

(B.12)

where we have denoted

\displaystyle f^{\,\rm I}_{r}(x,y)=x^{r}y^{r}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{2}{1-x-y}{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{1}+r;x){}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{2}+r;y)

\displaystyle-\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-y}{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r+1,\beta+r,\gamma_{1}+r;x){}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{2}+r;y)

\displaystyle-\frac{1+x-y}{1-x-y}{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{1}+r;x){}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r+1,\beta+r,\gamma_{2}+r;y)\bigg]

\displaystyle f^{\,\rm II}_{r}(x,y)=x^{r}y^{r}\bigg[

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r+1,\beta+r,\gamma_{1}+r;x){}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{2}+r;y)

\displaystyle-{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{1}+r;x){}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r+1,\beta+r,\gamma_{2}+r;y)\bigg].

(B.13)

Since eqs. (
B.11
) and (
B.12
) are differences of consecutive terms in
r
, the infinite sum

f(x,y)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{r}(\beta)_{r}(\alpha+\beta-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}+1)_{r}}{r!(\gamma_{1})_{r}(\gamma_{2})_{r}}f_{r}(x,y),

(B.14)

satisfies

\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}f(x,y)=\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}f(x,y)=0.

(B.15)

The series (
B.14
) is in fact a well-known representation of the Appell
F_{4}
function, completely equivalent to the definition (
B.5
).

The reason why we discuss this representation here is that it is a convenient starting point to solve the recursion relation (
3.23
).
Upon acting on the series (
B.14
) with
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}
, one encounters a generalization of that series, which we denote

\displaystyle f^{(a,b)}(x,y)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha)_{r}(\beta)_{r}(\alpha+\beta-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}+1)_{r}}{r!(\gamma_{1}+a)_{r}(\gamma_{2}+b)_{r}}x^{a+r}y^{b+r}

\displaystyle\times{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{1}+a+r;x){}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha+r,\beta+r,\gamma_{2}+b+r;y).

(B.16)

This is an infinite series in
x
and
y
that begins at order
x^{a}y^{b}
. The case
a=b=0
corresponds to the Appell
F_{4}
series,
f^{(0,0)}(x,y)=f(x,y)
.
The general solution to the recursion equation could be guessed after examining the first few steps, and then verified to hold at higher orders.
It is given by the linear combination

f(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{j-m}(1-x-y)^{j-n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\chi^{(n,i)}f^{(n-i,i)}(x,y),

(B.17)

with coefficients

\displaystyle\chi^{(n,i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!(n-i)!}\frac{4^{j-m}(m+1)_{j-m}}{(2m+1)_{j-m-n}}\frac{(\gamma_{1}-\beta)_{n-i}(\gamma_{2}-\beta)_{i}}{(\gamma_{1})_{n-i}(\gamma_{2})_{i}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}-2\beta)_{j-m}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{k=0}^{j-m-n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)_{j-m-n-k}\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)_{k}}{k!(j-m-n-k)!}(\gamma_{1}-\alpha)_{j-m-i-k}(\gamma_{2}-\alpha)_{i+k}.

(B.18)

These are precisely the results reported in eqs. (
3.29
), (
3.30
), and (
3.31
).

This construction so far is specific to the solution of the system of differential equations that is analytic in
x
and
y
around
(x,y)=(0,0)
.
However, the other solutions can easily be extracted from it, noting that the differential operators
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}
and
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}
obey

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,m}\left[w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}f(x,y)\right]

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}_{3-\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,m}f(x,y)

(B.19)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}}\left[w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}f(x,y)\right]

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{\Delta_{1}-3/2}\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}_{3-\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}}f(x,y)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta,m}\left[w_{2}^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}f(x,y)\right]

\displaystyle=w_{2}^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}\widehat{A}^{\,\rm I}_{\Delta_{1},3-\Delta_{2},\Delta,m}f(x,y)

(B.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}}\left[w_{2}^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}f(x,y)\right]

\displaystyle=w_{2}^{\Delta_{2}-3/2}\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}_{\Delta_{1},3-\Delta_{2}}f(x,y)

This explains immediately why the four functions (
3.27
) are related by the “shadow transform”
\Delta_{i}\leftrightarrow 3-\Delta_{i}
for
i=1,2
. But since the recursion relation (
3.23
) also simply depends on
\widehat{A}^{\,\rm II}
, the construction of the solution follows the same rule, and the result is directly given by the linear combination (
B.17
) in which the transformation
\Delta_{i}\leftrightarrow 3-\Delta_{i}
is applied both to the functions
f^{(n-i,i)}(x,y)
and to the coefficients
\chi^{(n,i)}
.

Colinear limit.

Note that in the colinear limit in which
1-x-y=0
, the function
f(x,y)
vanishes unless
m=0
. Moreover, at
m=0
, only the terms with
n=j
contribute to the sum (
B.17
). This means that we have

f(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\chi^{(j,i)}f^{(j-i,i)}(x,y),

(B.21)

with coefficients taking the simpler form

\chi^{(j,i)}=\frac{(-1)^{i}4^{j}j!}{i!(j-i)!}\frac{(\gamma_{1}-\alpha)_{j-i}(\gamma_{1}-\beta)_{j-i}(\gamma_{2}-\alpha)_{i}(\gamma_{2}-\beta)_{i}}{(\gamma_{1})_{j-i}(\gamma_{2})_{i}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}-2\beta)_{j}}.

(B.22)

Light-cone limit.

Finally, it should be noted that our formulation of the solution is particularly well-suited to examine either of the limits
x\to 0
or
y\to 0
, corresponding respectively to
p_{1}^{2}\to 0
or
p_{2}^{2}\to 0
. Let us describe the latter limit,
y\to 0
.
Since the function
f^{(a,b)}(x,y)
is a series of non-negative powers of
y
starting with
y^{b}
, only the functions with
b=0
contribute to that limit. Moreover, only the leading term with
r=0
contributes in the sum, so that we find

F_{j,m}(x,0)=\sum_{n=0}^{j-m}(1-x)^{j-n}\chi^{(n,0)}x^{n}{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{1}+n;x).

(B.23)

Using the properties of the coefficient
\chi^{(n,0)}
, this can be rewritten

\displaystyle F_{j,m}(x,0)=\sum_{n=0}^{j-m}

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{n}4^{j-m}j!\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)_{j-m-n}(\Delta-j-1+n)_{j-m-n}\left(\frac{\Delta-j-1-\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}{2}\right)_{n}}{m!n!(j-m-n)!(2m+1)_{j-m-n}(\Delta+m-1)_{j-m}}

\displaystyle\times(1-x)^{j-n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\tfrac{6-\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}-\Delta+j}{2}-n,\tfrac{3-\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\Delta+j}{2},\tfrac{5}{2}-\Delta_{1};x\right).

(B.24)

This matches the result obtained in arbitrary space-time dimension in ref.
[
23
]
.
