Abstract
. We compute explicitly the group of connected components π 0 ​ G ​ ( ℝ ) \pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R}) of the real Lie group G ⁡ ( ℝ ) G(\mathbb{R}) for an arbitrary (not necessarily linear) connected algebraic group G G defined over the field ℝ \mathbb{R} of real numbers. In particular, it turns out that π 0 ​ G ​ ( ℝ ) \pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R}) is always an elementary Abelian 2-group. The result looks most transparent in the cases where G G is a linear algebraic group or an Abelian variety. The computation is based on structure results on algebraic groups and Galois cohomology methods.

Introduction

Let
G
be an arbitrary algebraic group defined over the field
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers. If
G
is connected (in the Zariski topology), then its complex locus
G(\mathbb{C})
is also a connected (in the classical Hausdorff topology) complex Lie group, while the real locus
G(\mathbb{R})
is a real Lie group, which is no longer connected in general:
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
may serve as an example. It is natural to address a question about the structure of the group
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})=G(\mathbb{R})/G(\mathbb{R})^{\circ}
of connected components of
G(\mathbb{R})
, where
G(\mathbb{R})^{\circ}
denotes the identity component.

First results in this direction go back to É. Cartan: if
G
is semisimple and simply connected, then
G(\mathbb{R})
is connected, i.e., the component group is trivial; see, e.g.,
[
BT72
, Cor. 4.7]
. H. Matsumoto proved in 1964 that,
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})
is an elementary Abelian 2-group whenever
G
is a linear algebraic group; see
[
Mat64
, Thm. 1.2]
. Recently M. Borovoi and the author obtained a combinatorial description of the component group for a linear algebraic group
G
as the stabilizer of a point for an explicitly given action of a certain finite group on a certain finite set, which allows one to compute the group
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})
in any given case; see
[
BT23
, Thm. 9.2]
.

A weak point in this description is that it provides a method for computing the component group but does not lead to an explicit formula for the latter group. This defect was corrected by the author in
[
Tim22
]
, where an explicit formula for
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})
was obtained representing this group as a quotient of the cocharacter lattice of a maximal split torus in
G
; see also
[
BG22
]
. In this paper, a similar formula is obtained for an arbitrary (not necessarily linear) connected algebraic group. This gives a final answer to the above question about the component group.

Here is the structure of the paper. We start by providing necessary material on algebraic groups (in Section
1
) and Galois cohomology (in Section
2
). In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
3
, which is the main result of the paper. In Section
4
, we illustrate our formula for the component group by an example of the real locus of an elliptic curve.

1.
Structure of connected algebraic groups

1.1.

We fix a square root of
-1
in the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers and denote it by
{\boldsymbol{i}}
.

We consider algebraic groups defined over
\mathbb{R}
. We shall identify such an algebraic group
G
with its complex locus
G(\mathbb{C})
equipped with an anti-regular involutive group automorphism
\sigma
(the
real structure
) defining the action of the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
on
G(\mathbb{C})
. (The name “anti-regular” means that
\sigma
sends regular functions on Zariski open subsets in
G
to functions that are complex conjugate to regular ones.) In this setting,
G(\mathbb{R})=G^{\sigma}
is the fixed point subgroup of
\sigma
.

For an arbitrary algebraic or Lie group
H
, denote by
H^{\circ}
the connected component of identity in
H
and by
\mathfrak{h}
(the same lowercase German letter) the Lie algebra of
H
.

We say that an algebraic or Lie group
H
decomposes into an
almost direct product
of subgroups
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, and denote it by

H=H_{1}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}H_{2},

if
H={H_{1}\cdot H_{2}}
, the subgroups
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
commute with each other, and the intersection
{H_{1}\cap H_{2}}
is finite (but not necessarily trivial).

1.2.

We shall need some general structural results on connected algebraic groups. We use a survey paper of M. Brion
[
Bri17
]
as a source.

The most widely known are
affine
algebraic groups, i.e., those which are affine varieties, from the algebro-geometric viewpoint. Each affine algebraic group is
linear
, i.e., admits a faithful linear representation. The converse is also true: a linear algebraic group is isomorphic to a Zariski closed subgroup in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
(for some
n
) and therefore is an affine variety, whose coordinate algebra is generated by the matrix entries of the faithful linear representation.

An opposite class of algebraic groups is constituted by
anti-affine
groups, which are the algebraic groups having no non-constant regular functions. Obviously, anti-affine algebraic groups are connected. Examples of anti-affine groups are given by Abelian varieties, i.e., those algebraic groups which are irreducible projective varieties. However the class of anti-affine groups is wider; see
[
Bri17
, 5.5]
.

For arbitrary connected algebraic groups, there is the following
Rosenlicht decomposition
:

Theorem 1

(
[
Bri17
, 5.1.1]
)
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group. There exist the largest connected affine subgroup
G_{\text{aff}}
and the largest (connected) anti-affine subgroup
G_{\text{ant}}
in
G
. The subgroup
G_{\text{aff}}
is normal and
G_{\text{ant}}
is central in
G
, and

(1)

G=G_{\text{aff}}\cdot G_{\text{ant}}.

The structure of affine (i.e., linear) algebraic groups is well known. There is a
Levi decomposition

[
Hoc81
, Thm. VIII.4.3]
:

(2)

G_{\text{aff}}=G_{\text{uni}}\rtimes G_{\text{red}},

where
G_{\text{uni}}
is the largest normal unipotent subgroup (
unipotent radical
) of
G
and
G_{\text{red}}
is a maximal connected reductive subgroup (
Levi subgroup
). A Levi subgroup is not unique, but all Levi subgroups are conjugate to each other. There is an almost direct product decomposition:

(3)

G_{\text{red}}=G_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}S,

where
G_{\rm ss}=[G_{\text{red}},G_{\text{red}}]
is a connected semisimple group and
S=Z(G_{\text{red}})^{\circ}
is an algebraic torus.

The structure of anti-affine algebraic groups is described in
[
Bri17
, 5.5]
. There is an exact sequence

1\longrightarrow(G_{\text{ant}})_{\text{aff}}=(G_{\text{ant}}\cap G_{\text{aff}})^{\circ}\longrightarrow G_{\text{ant}}\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow 1,

where
A
is an Abelian variety and
(G_{\text{ant}})_{\text{aff}}
is a connected commutative linear algebraic group. There is a decomposition

(G_{\text{ant}})_{\text{aff}}=S_{0}\times V,

where
S_{0}
is an algebraic torus and
V
is a commutative unipotent group, i.e., the additive group of a vector space (a
vector group
). Theorem
1
and the decompositions (
2
) and (
3
) imply inclusions
S_{0}\subseteq S
and
V\subseteq G_{\text{uni}}
.

The decompositions (
1
)–(
3
) imply that
Z=S\cdot G_{\text{ant}}
is a connected commutative algebraic subgroup in
G
and

(4)

G=G_{\text{uni}}\cdot\bigl(G_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z\bigr).

1.3.

Let
T
be an algebraic torus of dimension
n
defined over
\mathbb{R}
. The torus
T
is called
split
if there exists a coordinate system (i.e., an isomorphism
T(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\dots\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
) in which the real structure is given by the formula

\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=(\bar{t}_{1},\dots,\bar{t}_{n}),

and
T
is called
anisotropic
if there exists a coordinate system in which

\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=(\bar{t}_{1}^{-1},\dots,\bar{t}_{n}^{-1}).

(The bar here denotes complex conjugation.) If
T
is split, then
{T(\mathbb{R})\simeq(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}}
. If
T
is anisotropic, then
T(\mathbb{R})\simeq(U_{1})^{n}
is a compact real torus; here
U_{1}
denotes the unit circle on the complex plane. In general, there exist the largest split subtorus
T_{\text{s}}\subseteq T
and the largest anisotropic subtorus
T_{\text{c}}\subseteq T
, so that there is an almost direct product decomposition
T=T_{\text{s}}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}T_{\text{c}}
.

Let
\mathsf{X}^{\vee}=\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T)=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{C}^{\times},T)
be the cocharacter lattice of
T
. By identifying cocharacters with their differentials at the unity, we may (and will) regard
\mathsf{X}^{\vee}
as a lattice in
\mathfrak{t}
. There is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{X}^{\vee}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{t}\overset{\mathcal{E}}{\longrightarrow}T\longrightarrow 1,

where
\mathcal{E}
is the scaled exponential map given by the formula
\mathcal{E}(x)=\exp(2\pi{x})
. This map can be regarded as the universal covering of the complex Lie group
T(\mathbb{C})
.

The involution
\sigma
acts on
\mathfrak{t}
preserving the lattice
\mathsf{X}^{\vee}
. The sublattices
\mathsf{X}^{\vee}_{+}
and
\mathsf{X}^{\vee}_{-}
consisting of cocharacters preserved and multiplied by
-1
under the action of
\sigma
are just the cocharacter lattices of
T_{\text{s}}
and
T_{\text{c}}
, respectively.

2.
Galois cohomology

As in our previous paper
[
Tim22
]
, we use techniques of Galois cohomology over real numbers for computation of the component group. For the reader’s convenience, we recall here briefly necessary material on Galois cohomology by following
[
Tim22
, §2]
. We refer the reader to the classical book of J.-P. Serre
[
Ser97
]
for a detailed exposition.

2.1.

Let
A
be an arbitrary group on which the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
acts by group automorphisms. Such an action is defined by an involutive automorphism
\sigma
of
A
.automorphism

An element
z\in A
is called a
1
-cocycle
with coefficients in
A
if
\sigma(z)=z^{-1}
. The group
A
acts by twisted conjugation on the set
\operatorname{Z}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)
of 1-cocycles:

z\overset{a}{\longmapsto}a\cdot z\cdot\sigma(a)^{-1}.

The orbit
[z]
of the cocycle
z
is called its
cohomology class
and the orbit set

\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)=\operatorname{Z}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)/A

is called the
first cohomology set
of
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
with coefficients in
A
or, for short, the first Galois cohomology set of
A
. This set contains a base point, which is the class
[1]
of the trivial cocycle, also denoted by
\operatorname{B}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)
and called the set of
1
-coboundaries
. In case
A
is Abelian the sets
\operatorname{Z}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)
,
\operatorname{B}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)
, and
\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)=\operatorname{Z}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)/\operatorname{B}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)
are Abelian groups.

Any short exact sequence of groups

(5)

1\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow C\longrightarrow 1

on which
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
acts in a compatible way gives rise to a long exact cohomology sequence

(6)

1\longrightarrow A^{\sigma}\longrightarrow B^{\sigma}\longrightarrow C^{\sigma}\longrightarrow\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)\longrightarrow\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},B)\longrightarrow\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},C).

The maps in the sequence (
6
) are obvious, except for the fourth one from the left, which is given by the formula

c\longmapsto[b^{-1}\sigma(b)],\qquad\forall c\in C^{\sigma},

where
b\in B
is an arbitrary preimage of
c
, so that
b^{-1}\sigma(b)\in A
. Exactness is understood in the sense of maps of pointed sets.

In the case where the exact sequence (
5
) splits, i.e.,
B=A\times C
, the picture simplifies: it is easy to see that

\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},B)\simeq\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},A)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},C).

2.2.

If
U
is a unipotent linear algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{R}
, then
\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},U)=\{[1]\}
. An elementary proof of this well-known fact (see
[
BT23
, Lemma 6.2(ii)]
) is based on an observation that any element in a unipotent group possesses a unique square root (this follows from bijectivity of the exponential map for unipotent groups). Specifically, each
z\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},U)
is represented as
z=z^{1/2}\cdot\sigma(z^{-1/2})
(since
z^{1/2}
is also a cocycle), i.e., is a coboundary.

3.
Component group

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{R}
. Our goal is to compute the group of connected components
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})=G(\mathbb{R})/G(\mathbb{R})^{\circ}
of the real locus
G(\mathbb{R})
. We use the notation from Section
1
.

3.1.

The decomposition (
4
) extends to real loci.

Lemma 2
.

G(\mathbb{R})=G_{\text{uni}}(\mathbb{R})\cdot\bigl(G_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z\bigr)(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Take any
g\in G(\mathbb{R})
. We can decompose it as
g=u\cdot h
, where
u\in G_{\text{uni}}(\mathbb{C})
,
h\in\bigl(G_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z\bigr)(\mathbb{C})
, so that
g=\sigma(g)=\sigma(u)\cdot\sigma(h)
. Hence
v:=u^{-1}\cdot\sigma(u)=h\cdot\sigma(h)^{-1}\in V(\mathbb{C})
and
\sigma(v)=v^{-1}
, i.e.,
v
is a Galois 1-cocycle. Since
V
is a vector group, there exists a unique square root
v^{1/2}\in V(\mathbb{C})
, which is also a 1-cocycle, i.e.,
\sigma(v^{1/2})=v^{-1/2}
. By replacing
u
with
u\cdot v^{1/2}
and
h
with
v^{-1/2}\cdot h
, we get
u\in G_{\text{uni}}(\mathbb{R})
,
h\in\bigl(G_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z\bigr)(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

The exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{g}_{\text{uni}}\to G_{\text{uni}}
is an isomorphism of algebraic varieties over
\mathbb{R}
and therefore yields a diffeomorphism between
\mathfrak{g}_{\text{uni}}(\mathbb{R})
and
G_{\text{uni}}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus,
G_{\text{uni}}(\mathbb{R})
is a connected Lie group and Lemma
2
implies a canonical isomorphism

\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})\simeq\pi_{0}(G_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z)(\mathbb{R}).

For this reason, we may (and will) assume in the sequel without loss of generality that
G=G_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z
, where
G_{\rm ss}=[G,G]
is semisimple, and
Z=Z(G)^{\circ}
is the connected center of
G
.

3.4.

The maximal torus

\sigma

3.5.

Here is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 3
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{R}
,
G_{\text{ant}}\subseteq G
be the largest anti-affine subgroup,
G_{\text{red}}\subseteq G
be a maximal connected reductive subgroup,
S=Z(G_{\text{red}})^{\circ}
be its connected center,
G_{\rm ss}=[G_{\text{red}},G_{\text{red}}]
be its derived subgroup,
T_{\rm ss}\subseteq G_{\rm ss}
be a maximal torus containing a maximal split torus
T_{\text{s}}\subseteq G_{\rm ss}
, and
Z=S\cdot G_{\text{ant}}
. Then the group of connected components of the real locus
G(\mathbb{R})
is computed by the following formula (in the notation of Subsection
3.4
):

\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})\simeq\Lambda_{+}/(2\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}+\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}_{+}).

Here
\Lambda
is the lattice in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}_{\rm ss}\oplus\mathfrak{z}
of the algebraic group
T_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z
such that
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda
is the kernel of the universal covering
\mathcal{E}:\mathfrak{t}_{\rm ss}\oplus\mathfrak{z}\to T_{\rm ss}\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}Z
,
\mathcal{E}(y)=\exp(2\pi{y})
, and
\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}
is the coroot lattice of
G_{\rm ss}
with respect to
T_{\rm ss}
. The connected component of
G(\mathbb{R})
corresponding to the coset of
\lambda\in\Lambda_{+}
is represented by

\exp(\pi{\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda)=\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda/2).

The proof.

is similar to the proof of Theorem 6 in
[
Tim22
]
. So we skip some technical details.

We use the exact sequence (

). Similarly to the proof of
[
Tim22
, Lemma 2]
, we have:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Z}^{1}(\mathbb{R},{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda\,/\,{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda\cap({\boldsymbol{i}}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}+\tfrac{{\boldsymbol{i}}}{2}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}_{-})/{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee},

\displaystyle\operatorname{B}^{1}(\mathbb{R},{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda\,/\,{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee})

\displaystyle=(2{\boldsymbol{i}}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}+{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee})/{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee},

\displaystyle\operatorname{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda\,/\,{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee})

\displaystyle\simeq\Lambda\cap(\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}_{-})/(2\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}+\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}).

The image of a cohomology class
[{\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda+{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}]
under the map
\iota
is the cocycle
\mathcal{E}_{\rm sc}({\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda_{\rm sc})\in Z(G_{\rm sc})
. Since
Z(G_{\rm sc})
has finite order
N
, we have
N\lambda_{\rm sc}\in\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}\subseteq\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T_{\rm ss})\subseteq\Lambda
, whence
\lambda_{\rm sc}\in\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T_{\rm ss})_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
and
(\lambda_{\rm sc})_{+}\in\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T_{\rm ss})_{\mathbb{R}}^{\sigma}=\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T_{\text{s}})_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Similarly to
[
Tim22
, Lemma 4]
,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm sc}({\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda_{\rm sc})
is a coboundary in
G_{\rm sc}
if and only if it belongs to
(T_{\rm sc})_{\text{s}}
, i.e.,
\lambda_{\rm sc}\in{\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T_{\text{s}})_{\mathbb{R}}+\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}}
. Since
(\lambda_{\rm sc})_{-}=\lambda_{-}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}_{-}
, the coboundary condition is equivalent to
\lambda_{-}\in\widetilde{\mathsf{Q}}^{\vee}_{-}
. As
\lambda
is defined up to the shift by a vector in
\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\lambda_{-}=0
, i.e.,
\lambda\in\Lambda_{+}
.

Thus

\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})\simeq\operatorname{Ker}(\iota)\simeq\Lambda_{+}/(2\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}+\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}).

For
\lambda\in\Lambda_{+}
,
\tilde{g}=\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(-{\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda/2)
is a cocycle in
\widetilde{G}
, whence

\tilde{g}^{-1}\sigma(\tilde{g})=\tilde{g}^{-2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}({\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda)

and

g=\tau(\tilde{g})=\mathcal{E}(-{\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda/2)=\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{i}}\lambda/2)

represents the connected component in
G(\mathbb{R})
corresponding to the coset of
\lambda
.
∎

A straightforward consequence of Theorem
3
generalizes Matsumoto’s theorem
[
Mat64
, Thm. 1.2]
:

Corollary 4
.

\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})
is an elementary Abelian 2-group of finite order.

3.6.

Theorem
3
takes the most transparent form in the cases where
G
is a linear algebraic group or an Abelian variety.

The case of linear algebraic groups is examined in detail in
[
Tim22
]
. In this case,
Z=S
is an algebraic torus,
\Lambda=\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T)
is the cocharacter lattice of a maximal torus
T=S\mathbin{\smash[b]{\underset{\text{\raisebox{6.02777pt}{$\smash{\centerdot}$}}}{\times}}}T_{{\rm ss}}
in
G
containing a maximal split torus
T_{\text{s}}
(here
T_{\text{s}}
denotes a maximal split torus in
G
, not in
G_{\rm ss}
!), and
\Lambda_{+}=\mathsf{X}^{\vee}(T_{\text{s}})
.

In the case where
G
is an Abelian variety, the groups
G_{\text{aff}}
,
G_{\text{red}}
,
G_{\rm ss}
,
G_{\rm sc}
,
T_{\rm ss}
,
T_{\rm sc}
,
T_{\text{s}}
,
S
are trivial and
Z=G
. The exact sequence (
7
) reads as

0\longrightarrow\Lambda\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}\overset{\mathcal{E}}{\longrightarrow}G\longrightarrow 1,

i.e.,
\Lambda
is the (scaled) period lattice of the Abelian variety
G

\mathsf{Q}^{\vee}=0
and
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})\simeq\Lambda_{+}/2\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}
.

4.
Example

Examples of computation of component groups for linear algebraic groups were considered in
[
Tim22
, §4]
. Here we consider the “opposite” case of Abelian varieties. As an example, we consider one-dimensional Abelian varieties, i.e., elliptic curves.

4.1.

Let
G
be an elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{R}
. In this case,
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{C}
is the complex plane, the lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
has rank 2, and
G\simeq\mathbb{C}/{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda
. Since
G
is defined over
\mathbb{R}
, the lattices
\Lambda
and
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda
are preserved by complex conjugation and, in particular,
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda
intersects the real axis. After rescaling we may assume without loss of generality that
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\omega
, where
-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\operatorname{Re}(\omega)<\tfrac{1}{2}
. By invariance under complex conjugation, there are only two possibilities for the fundamental period
\omega
: either
\omega\in{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathbb{R}
or
\omega\in-\tfrac{1}{2}+{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathbb{R}
.

In the first case,
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda_{+}={\boldsymbol{i}}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}=\mathbb{Z}\omega
, hence
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.,
G(\mathbb{R})
has two connected components.

In the second case,
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda_{+}=\mathbb{Z}(\omega-\bar{\omega})
, while
{\boldsymbol{i}}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{+}=\mathbb{Z}(\omega-\bar{\omega})/2
. Hence
\pi_{0}G(\mathbb{R})=0
, i.e.,
G(\mathbb{R})
is connected.

4.2.

One can see that differently. Let us define
G
in the Weierstrass normal form by the equation

y^{2}z=x^{3}+pxz^{2}+qz^{3}.

(Here
x,y,z
are homogeneous coordinates on a projective plane). It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
Kob88
, Chap. I, §6]
) that the coefficients
p,q
are given by Eisenstein series:

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=-15\sum_{\lambda\in{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}\;,

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=-35\sum_{\lambda\in{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{6}}\;.

Consider the cubic trinomial
f(x)=x^{3}+px+q
and its discriminant
D(f)=-4p^{3}-27q^{2}
.

If
\omega\in{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathbb{R}
, then, without loss of generality,
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)>0
and
D(f)>0
.

Indeed, since
G
is a smooth curve,
D(f)\neq 0
and therefore has constant sign on the ray
{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Thus it suffices to consider a single value of
\omega\in{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, e.g.,
\omega={\boldsymbol{i}}
.

In this case,
G
possesses complex multiplication: the period lattice is preserved by multiplication with
{\boldsymbol{i}}
. On the other hand, when the period lattice is multiplied by
{\boldsymbol{i}}
, the series
q
is multiplied by
-1
. Hence
q=0
.

In this case,
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}[{\boldsymbol{i}}]
is the ring of Gaussian numbers, which is a Euclidean ring. Hence the series
p
can be written in the form of an Euler product:

p=-15\cdot\bigl(1^{4}+(-1)^{4}+{\boldsymbol{i}}^{4}+(-{\boldsymbol{i}})^{4}\bigr)\cdot\prod_{\rho}\frac{1}{1-1/\rho^{4}}\;,

where the product is taken over all Gaussian primes
\rho
up to multiplication by invertible elements
\pm 1,\pm{\boldsymbol{i}}
of
\mathbb{Z}[{\boldsymbol{i}}]
. Gaussian primes which are not integers can be grouped in pairs of mutually conjugate numbers, whence

p=-60\cdot\prod_{\operatorname{Im}(\rho)>0}\frac{1}{|1-1/\rho^{4}|^{2}}\cdot\prod_{\operatorname{Im}(\rho)=0}\frac{1}{1-1/\rho^{4}}<0\;.

It follows that
D(f)=-4p^{3}>0
.

Removing the point at infinity
g_{\infty}=(0:1:0)
, we can define
G(\mathbb{R})\setminus\{g_{\infty}\}
by the equation
y^{2}=f(x)
on the real coordinate plane. Since
f
has three distinct real roots, it is clear that
G(\mathbb{R})
has two connected components (see Fig.
1
).

If now
\omega\in-\tfrac{1}{2}+{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathbb{R}
, then
D(f)<0
. The proof is similar to the previous case. Since
D(f)
has constant sign on the ray
-\tfrac{1}{2}+{\boldsymbol{i}}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\omega=-\tfrac{1}{2}+{\boldsymbol{i}}\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
is a cube root of
1
.

In this case
G
still possesses complex multiplication: the period lattice is preserved by multiplication with
\omega
. However, when the period lattice is multiplied by
\omega
, the series
p
is multiplied by
\omega^{-1}=\bar{\omega}
, hence
p=0
.

Here
{\boldsymbol{i}}\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
is the ring of Eisenstein numbers, which is Euclidean again. Therefore, as in the previous case, the series
q
can be written as an Euler product:

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=-35\cdot\bigl(1^{6}+\omega^{6}+\bar{\omega}^{6}+(-1)^{6}+(-\omega)^{6}+(-\bar{\omega})^{6}\bigr)\cdot\prod_{\rho}\frac{1}{1-1/\rho^{6}}\;,

(the product is taken over all Eisenstein primes up to multiplication by invertible elements
\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\bar{\omega}
of
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
)

\displaystyle=-210\prod_{\operatorname{Im}(\rho)>0}\frac{1}{|1-1/\rho^{6}|^{2}}\cdot\prod_{\operatorname{Im}(\rho)=0}\frac{1}{1-1/\rho^{6}}<0\;.

It follows that
D(f)=-27q^{2}<0
.

In this case the trinomial
f
has a single real root and therefore
G(\mathbb{R})
is connected (see Fig.
2
).
