Learning Decision-Focused Uncertainty Sets in Robust Optimization
Irina Wang, Cole Becker, Bart Van Parys, Bartolomeo Stellato

Abstract
We propose a data-driven technique to automatically learn the uncertainty sets in robust optimization. Our method reshapes the uncertainty sets by minimizing the expected performance across a parametric family of problems, subject to guaranteeing constraint satisfaction. Our approach is very flexible, and can learn a wide variety of uncertainty sets while preserving tractability. We solve the constrained learning problem using a stochastic augmented Lagrangian method that relies on differentiating the solutions of the robust optimization problems with respect to the parameters of the uncertainty set. Due to the nonsmooth and nonconvex nature of the augmented Lagrangian function, we apply the nonsmooth conservative implicit function theorem to establish convergence to a critical point, which is a feasible solution of the constrained problem under mild assumptions. Using empirical process theory, we show finite-sample probabilistic guarantees of constraint satisfaction for the resulting solutions. Numerical experiments show that our method outperforms traditional approaches in robust and distributionally robust optimization in terms of out-of-sample performance and constraint satisfaction guarantees.



1
Introduction

Over the past years,
robust optimization (RO)
has become a widely adopted efficient tool for decision-making under uncertainty.
A robust approach first defines an uncertainty set where the uncertain vector lives, and subsequently hedges its decision against any worst-case realization in this set
(
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski, 2008
;
Ben-Tal et al., 2009
;
Bertsimas et al., 2011
;
Bertsimas and den Hertog, 2022
)
.
To attain proper solution quality and practical robustness the choice of uncertainty set is paramount.

Traditionally, uncertainty sets are shaped and sized to guarantee they contain an appropriately large fraction of the probability mass of the unknown distribution generating the samples (
i.e.
, high coverage). The main appeal of this approach is that the uncertainty set is independent of the subsequent optimization problem considered.
In this vein, many approaches to designing uncertainty sets assume structural information of the underlying distributions, and rely on these a priori assumptions to build guarantees of constraint satisfaction
(
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski, 2000
;
Bertsimas et al., 2011
;
Bandi and Bertsimas, 2012
)
.
In particular, a large amount of existing literature studies how to bound the probability of constraint satisfaction based on the size and shape of the uncertainty sets
(
Li et al., 2012
;
Guzman et al., 2017
;
Bertsimas et al., 2021
)
, where the aforementioned structural information is exploited, but the optimization problem itself is not leveraged.
By decoupling the design of the uncertainty set from the subsequent optimization problem, the resulting theoretical guarantees can often be quite conservative
(
Roos and den Hertog, 2020
;
Gao and Kleywegt, 2023
)
.

The trend of constructing uncertainty sets separately from the subsequent optimization problem has continued in the current era of data-driven
RO
, where the explosion in the availability of data has lead to new paradigms where uncertainty sets are designed directly from data
(
Bertsimas et al., 2018
)
.
Whether by combining a priori assumptions with the confidence regions of statistical hypothesis tests on the data
(
Bertsimas et al., 2018
)
, or by approximating a high-probability-region using quantile estimation
(
Hong et al., 2021
)
, significant attention has been given to the probability distribution generating the data and less so to the subsequent optimization problem.
More recently in
distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
, the constructed ambiguity set of distributions has historically been required to contain the true data-generating distribution with a certain level of confidence
(
Esfahani and Kuhn, 2018
)
.
Again, the intuitive appeal of this approach is that the design of the ambiguity set can be carried out in isolation of the subsequent optimization problem.

Unfortunately, decoupling the uncertainty set construction from the subsequent optimization problem in a two-step approach may lead to very conservative decisions.
In
DRO
with Wasserstein ambiguity sets, the two-step approach suffers from the
curse of dimensionality
as their radius must scale with the dimension of the uncertainty
(
Weed and Bach, 2019
)
.
On the other hand, one can overcome the curse of dimensionality by exploiting Lipschitz conditions on the objective function of the subsequent optimization problem when designing the ambiguity set
(
Gao, 2023
)
.
The situation in
DRO
with
f
-divergence ambiguity sets is even more striking.
For instance, Kullback-Leibler ambiguity sets around an empirical distribution can structurally not contain any continuous distribution however large its radius is chosen.
Consequently, if the data-generating distribution is continuous a two-step approach is not possible here. Nevertheless, the Kullback-Leibler can be shaped to ensure that the solution has optimal statistical properties when one is willing to exploiting the fact that the subsequent optimization problem has a continuous loss function
(
Van Parys et al., 2021
)
.
In both cases, significant reduction in conservatism can be achieved by designing the uncertainty set not only based on the distributions generating the data, but also by exploiting structural properties of the subsequent optimization problem.

The drive for even better performance in
RO
, then, leads us to a new direction: a method of constructing uncertainty sets that deviates from the conventional decoupled approach and explicitly takes into account the optimization problem as well.
This idea has been around for multiple years; as stated by
(
Ben-Tal et al., 2009
, page 34)
, the requirement of containing a high probability mass is only a sufficient (and often restrictive and not needed) condition to guarantee high probability of constraint satisfaction.
However, standardized methodology to exploit the notion have seldom been developed.
The customary restriction, in essence, allows the uncertainty set developed to hold for all classes of optimization problems, which brings about high levels of conservatism.
However, the uncertainty set need only provide high solution quality for the desired optimization problems, and in turn need only include worst-case realizations of uncertainty for these instances.

In this work, we propose a new approach to calibrate uncertainty sets for a particular
family
of parametric optimization instances.
This is a common scenario in many applications, including inventory management, where we solve similar optimization problems with varying initial inventory levels (
i.e.
, the parameters), while satisfying uncertain demand with high probability.
Our method minimizes an expected objective over a
family
of parametric problems, while ensuring data-driven probabilistic guarantees enforced as a conditional value-at-risk constraint
(
Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2002
)
.
We construct the uncertainty set, which is the family-specific region of worst-case realizations, by learning its shape and size automatically.
Specifically, we develop a stochastic augmented Lagrangian approach, relying on nonsmooth implicit differentiation techniques
(
Agrawal et al., 2019b
;
Agrawal et al., 2019a
;
Bolte et al., 2021
;
Bertrand et al., 2022
)
to compute the derivative of the solution of robust optimization problem with respect to the shape and size of the uncertainty sets.
By incorporating the problem objective and the data-driven constraint satisfaction into the learning problem, we control the trade-off between performance and robustness.
Our approach offers significant performance improvements over traditional techniques constructing uncertainty sets with high probability mass requirements, and is more flexible than data-driven
RO
methods that only tune the radius of the uncertainty sets (often via grid search), and not its shape.

1.1
Our contributions

In this work, we present a new approach to automatically learn the uncertainty sets in
RO
. We obtain high-quality solutions across a
family
of parametric optimization problems while ensuring probabilistic guarantees of constraint satisfaction.
By directly taking into account the quality of the
RO
solution,
the sets found are
decision-focused
and eliminate the unnecessary conservatism common in traditional data-driven
RO
methods.

•

We minimize the expectation of the objective function over a family of parametric
RO
problems, while ensuring an appropriate probability level of constraint satisfaction. The latter is formulated as constraint on the conditional-value-of-risk (
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
) of the problem family’s uncertain constraints.

•

The aforementioned objective and constraints rely on the solutions of the parametric
RO
problems. The overall formulation is thus a constrained bi-level problem, with the inner level represented by the parametric
RO
problems.

•

We develop a stochastic augmented Lagrangian algorithm to solve the constrained learning problem to find the optimal parameters of the uncertainty set. Due to the potential nonsmooth, nonconvex, and therefore nondifferentiable nature of the constructed problem, we show
path-differentiability
of the Lagrangian, and apply nonsmooth implicit differentiation. We show convergence to a critical point under step-size and boundedness conditions, and establish the critical point as a feasible solution of the constrained problem under mild assumptions.

•

Using empirical process theory, we give finite sample probabilistic guarantees of constraint satisfaction for the resulting solution.

•

We benchmark our method on examples in portfolio optimization and multi-stage inventory management, outperforming data-driven robust and distributionally robust approaches in terms of out-of-sample objective value and probabilistic guarantees. The code is available at
https://github.com/stellatogrp/lropt_experiments
.

1.2
Related work

Data-driven robust optimization.

Data-driven robust optimization has gained wide popularity, by constructing uncertainty sets through data-driven approximations of the unknown data-generating distribution.
Using hypothesis testing,
Bertsimas et al., 2018
pair a priori assumptions on the distribution with different statistical tests, and obtain various uncertainty sets with different shapes, computational properties, and modeling power.
Similar approaches construct data-driven sets using quantile estimation
(
Hong et al., 2021
)
, type-
\infty
Wasserstein distance
(
Bertsimas et al., 2022
)
, intersections of multiple single-measure-based ambiguity sets
(
Tanoumand et al., 2023
)
, and deep learning clustering techniques
(
Goerigk and Kurtz, 2023
)
.
All these methods rely on a two-step procedure which separates the construction of the uncertainty set from the resulting robust optimization problem, and often enforces a high coverage requirement on the selected uncertainty sets.
As noted by
Ben-Tal et al., 2009
, however, this requirement may be unnecessary.
Recently,
Gupta, 2019
proves that while convex problems require
DRO
ambiguity sets with high coverage to guarantee constraint satisfaction, traditional
DRO
ambiguity sets for concave problems are significantly larger than the asymptotically optimal sets.
In order to find non coverage-based and potentially smaller uncertainty sets that offer improved performance for targeted robust optimization problems, we turn to ideas in differentiable optimization.
Recent developments in differentiable convex optimization have enabled a popular
end-to-end
approach of training a predictive model to minimize loss on a downstream optimization task
(
Donti et al., 2017
;
Demirović et al., 2019
;
Wilder et al., 2019a
;
Wilder et al., 2019b
;
Cameron et al., 2022
;
Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022
)
, for which we also point to the survey by
Kotary et al., 2021
.
Our approach adapts this idea, training instead the shape of the uncertainty set on its performance on parametric robust optimization problems.
While developing this work, we became aware of a concurrent work with a similar goal of fitting decision-focused uncertainty sets. In their work,
Schuurmans and Patrinos, 2023
construct
DRO
ambiguity sets which constrain only the worst-case distribution along the direction in which the expected cost of an approximate solution increases most rapidly.
Our work, however, considers a broader class of optimization problems and uncertainty sets, and can directly tackle joint chance constraints using hyperparameter tuning.

Contextual optimization.

Contextual optimization and end-to-end approaches, beginning from the Smart
Predict, then Optimize
framework
(
Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022
)
have been shown to improve performance as opposed to traditional
two-stage
approaches, where the model is trained separately from the optimization problem
(
Costa and Iyengar, 2023
)
.

Costa and Iyengar, 2023
propose an end-to-end distributionally robust approach for portfolio construction, where they integrate a
prediction layer
that predicts asset returns with data on financial features and historical returns, and use these predictions in a
decision layer
that solves the robust optimization problem.
Concurrent to our work,
Chenreddy and Delage, 2024
propose an end-to-end training algorithm to produce contextual uncertainty sets that achieve a certain conditional coverage.
This is to decrease the conservatism of their previous two-stage approach, conditional robust optimization, where they learn a contextually adapted uncertainty set known to contain with high probability the realized uncertainty
(
Chenreddy et al., 2022
)
.
We, however, adopt a different notion for context parameters, denoting them
family
parameters that parameterize different instances of the downstream optimization problem, instead of the uncertain parameter.
Our learned uncertainty set therefore does not learn a direct mapping from the context parameters to the uncertainty set (as in contextual and conditional
RO

(
Sadana et al., 2023
)
), as it may be computationally expensive and hard to interpret.
We do not enforce coverage constraints, which may introduce unnecessary conservatism, and instead freely shape the uncertainty sets based on their performance on the entire parametralized family of robust problems.
As pointed out in the recent survey by
Sadana et al., 2023
, current work in the end-to-end framework seldom focus on uncertainty in the downstream optimization problem, especially in the constraints.
Our approach thus fills this gap, where the process of constructing the uncertainty set is linked in an end-to-end fashion to the solution of the inner robust optimization problem. Our formulation is flexible, as it allows for a wide variety of uncertain constraints and uncertainty sets.

Modeling languages for robust optimization.

Several open-source packages facilitate building models for
RO
, including as ROME
(
Goh and Sim, 2009
)
in Matlab, RSOME
(
Xiong and Chen, 2021
)
in Python, JuMPeR
(
Dunning et al., 2017
)
in Julia, and ROmodel
(
Wiebe and Misener, 2022
)
for the Pyomo modeling language in Python.
These packages mostly support
RO
problems with affine uncertain constraints, which are solved via reformulations and/or cutting plane procedures.

Schiele et al., 2023
recently introduced a Python extension of cvxpy
(
Diamond and Boyd, 2016
)
for solving the larger class of convex-concave saddle point problems, by automating a conic dualization procedure reformulating min-max type problems into min-min problems which can then be solved efficiently
(
Juditsky and Nemirovski, 2022
)
.
In the paper, they describe a set of rules termed
disciplined saddle programming (DSP)
detailing when a saddle point optimization problem can be equivalently formulated as convex optimization ones, and efficiently be solved.
In this work, we build a Python extension to cvxpy tailored to formulating and solving
RO
problems.
While some
RO
problems can be described in terms of the
DSP
framework, our library augments
DSP
capabilities in
RO
by supporting max-of-concave uncertain constraint and structure to intuitively describe well known uncertainty sets for ease of use.
Finally, our method automatically learns the uncertainty set through differentiable optimization via a tight integration with the works by
Agrawal et al., 2019a
;
Agrawal et al., 2019b
.

Differentiable convex optimization.

There has been extensive work on embedding differentiable optimization problems as layers within deep learning architectures
(
Amos and Kolter, 2017
)
.

Agrawal et al., 2019a
propose an approach to differentiating through disciplined convex programs, and implement their methodology in cvxpy. Specifically, they implement differentiable layers for disciplined convex programs in PyTorch and TensorFlow 2.0, in the package cvxpylayers.
They convert the convex programs into conic programs, then implicitly differentiate the residual map of the homogeneous self-dual embedding associated with the conic program.
These developments have fueled the recent advances in end-to-end approaches, and is also the tool of choice in this work.

Nonsmooth implicit differentiation.

Traditional techniques in differentiable convex optimization rely on the implicit function theorem, which requires derivatives to exist almost everywhere.
Recently, a nonsmooth variant has been established, using conservative Jacobians, provided that a nonsmooth form of the classical invertibility condition is fulfilled
(
Bolte et al., 2021
)
.
As a key ingredient, definable and locally Lipschitz continuous functions are shown to admit conservative Jacobians, which follow the chain rule, and are thus compatible with backpropagation techniques in stochastic gradient descent
(
Davis et al., 2018
;
Bolte et al., 2021
)
.
These techniques can be used to obtain convergence for nonsmooth problems.

Automatic hyperparameter selection.

Implicit differentiation techniques can greatly accelerate tuning the regularization parameters in regression and classification problems
(
Bertrand et al., 2020
;
Bertrand et al., 2022
;
Blanchet et al., 2019
)
.
However, most literature focuses on tuning hyperparameters of the objective function in regression and classification tasks, such as hyper-parameters for Lasso regularization
(
Bertrand et al., 2022
)
.
In this work, we tune various hyperparameters of the uncertainty sets in
RO
using implicit differentiation techniques.

Risk measures.

Value at risk (
\mathbf{VaR}
) and conditional value at risk (
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
) are common risk measures for quantifying costs encountered in the tails of distributions.
A
\mathbf{VaR}_{\eta}
constraint is a chance constraint that measures the maximum cost incurred with respect to a given confidence level
\eta
, but while intuitive, the function is often intractable, and does not quantify costs beyond the found threshold value.
Conditional value at risk, on the other hand, measures the
expected
cost given that the cost exceeds
\mathbf{VaR}
, and is known to have a tractable formulation
(
Uryasev and Rockafellar, 2001
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2002
)
.
Since its development,
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
optimization has been a common method for controlling risk in portfolio optimization and option hedging problems, and has also been explored in risk-constrained Markov decision problems, where the risk is presented as a constraint on the
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
of the cumulative cost
(
Chow et al., 2015
)
.
The use of
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
constraints to substitute chance constraints has also been explored in reliability engineering, where the
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
constraint has been given a different name: buffered failure probability
(
Rockafellar and Royset, 2010
)
.
A related concept for controlling risk is the buffered probability of exceedance (bPOE)
(
Mafusalov and Uryasev, 2018
)
, which is equivalent to 1 minus the inverse of
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
, and has a level set equivalence with
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
.
In our work, we constrain risk, which in
RO
is the probability of constraint violation, by constraining the
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
of the uncertain constraint functions.

2
The robust optimization problem

We consider a
family
of parametric
RO
problems, indexed by the
family parameter

y\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{p}
, where each instance takes the form:

x(\theta,y)\in\begin{array}[t]{ll}\mathop{\rm argmin}&f(x)\\
\mbox{subject to}&g(x,u,y)\leq 0\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta),\end{array}

(1)

where
x\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
is the optimization variable,
u\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
is the
uncertain parameter
,
f:{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}\to{\mbox{\bf R}}
is the convex objective function, and
g:{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}\times{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}\times{\mbox{\bf R}}^{p}\to{\mbox{\bf R}}
is the uncertain constraint which we assume to be the maximum of functions concave in
u
and convex in
x
and
y
,
e.g.
,
g(x,u,y)=\max_{l=1,\dots,L}g_{l}(x,u,y)
.
This structure allows us to express
L
joint uncertain constraints using the maximum of each component
l
.
Problem (
1
) guarantees that, for a given
y
, the solution
x(\theta,y)
satisfies the constraints for any value of the uncertain parameter
u
in
{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\subseteq{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
, a convex uncertainty set that depends on the
set parameter

\theta\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{q}
.
For example,
\theta
can represent the center, size, and shape of
{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)
.
In this setting, the family parameter
y
represents information available when we make our decisions (
e.g.
, current allocations in portfolio optimization). The uncertain parameter
u
, however, is not known (
e.g.
, future returns in portfolio optimization).
Importantly, we do not assume that
y
affects
u
, which is common in several practical applications (
e.g.
, when our portfolio allocations do no affect future market returns).

Here, the set
{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)
does not depend on individual
y
as this would require either learning a different uncertainty set for every instance of the family, which may be costly, or learning a mapping from
y
to the uncertainty set (as in conditional
RO

(
Sadana et al., 2023
)
), which may be hard to interpret.
Instead, we construct a fixed set that works for all realizations of
y
encountered in target application.

Our goal is to find a set parameter
\theta
in a
decision-focused
manner,
i.e.
, such that the solutions
x(\theta,y)
perform well in terms of objective value and constraint satisfaction guarantees across the family of problems.
Specifically, we choose the uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)
such that the solution
x(\theta,y)
from (
1
) implies that the uncertain constraint is satisfied with probability
1-\eta
with
\eta>0
, across all instances of problem (
1
):

{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y)\leq 0)\geq 1-\eta.

(2)

Here,
{\mathbf{P}}_{y}
is the distribution of
y
with domain
\mathcal{D}_{y}\subseteq{\mbox{\bf R}}^{p}
,
{\mathbf{P}}_{u}
is the distribution of
u
with domain
\mathcal{D}_{u}\subseteq{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
, and
{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}
is their joint distribution, all of which are unknown.
We note that this is an aggregate probabilistic guarantee for the parametric family of problems, and not conditional guarantee which holds for all instances
y
.
Whereas such conditional guarantee
(
Chenreddy and Delage, 2024
)
are perhaps more desirable they require stringent assumptions on the distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{y}
which are typically hard to verify in practice.
We can formulate constraint (
2
) in terms of the corresponding
value at risk
,
i.e.
,

\mathop{\bf VaR}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y),\eta)=\inf\{\gamma\mid{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y)\leq\gamma)\geq 1-\eta\}\leq 0.

Unfortunately, the value at risk function often leads to intractable formulations
(
Uryasev and Rockafellar, 2001
)
.
To get a tractable approximation, we can adopt the
conditional value at risk

(
Uryasev and Rockafellar, 2001
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2002
)
, defined as

\mathop{\bf CVaR}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y),\eta)=\inf_{\alpha}\left\{\frac{{\mbox{\bf E}}_{(u,y)}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y)-\alpha)_{+}}{\eta}+\alpha\right\},

(3)

where
(a)_{+}=\max\{a,0\}
. It is well known
(
Uryasev and Rockafellar, 2001
)
that, for any
x\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
, the relationship between these probabilistic guarantees of constraint satisfaction is

\mathop{\bf CVaR}(g(x,u,y),\eta)\leq 0~~\Longrightarrow~~\mathop{\bf VaR}(g(x,u,y),\eta)\leq 0~~\Longleftrightarrow~~{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}(g(x,u,y)\leq 0)\geq 1-\eta.

(4)

Therefore, if the solution
x(\theta,y)
of (
1
) satisfies
\mathop{\bf CVaR}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y),\eta)\leq 0
, we have the desired probabilistic guarantee (
2
).
This constraint, together with the goal of minimizing the expected objective value, are constructed into an evaluation metric for updating
\theta
; see Figure
1
.
Typically, by constructing uncertainty sets that contain at least
1-\eta
probability mass,
i.e.
,
{\mathbf{P}}_{u}(u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta))\geq 1-\eta
, we can ensure that any feasible solution of (
1
) implies a probabilistic guarantee (
2
).
However, this sufficient condition may lead to overly conservative solutions
(
Ben-Tal et al., 2009
, page 34)
, as it works for any
RO
problem and any constraint using the uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)
.
In this work, instead, we avoid such conservatism by taking into account the cost of the robust solutions
x(\theta,y)
while constructing the uncertainty sets.

:
Our high-level procedure. We evaluate the performance of the decision
x(\theta,y)
in terms of the expected objective value and constraint satisfaction. The evalution metric is defined in Section
3.2
.

2.1
Motivating example

We consider a 2-product newsvendor problem where each day, the vendor
orders
x\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{2}

products at price
k\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{2}
, and we allow fractional values for simplicity. The products are sold at price
p\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{2}
, until either the demand
u\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{2}
or inventory
x
is exhausted. The objective function to minimize is the sum of the ordering cost minus the revenue,

f(x,u,k,p)=k^{T}x-p^{T}\min\{x,u\}=k^{T}x+\max(-p_{1}x_{1}-p_{2}x_{2},-p_{1}x_{1}-p_{2}u_{2},-p_{1}u_{1}-p_{2}x_{2},-p_{1}u_{1}-p_{2}u_{2}).

We obtain the
RO
problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&t\\
\mbox{subject to}&f(x,u,k,p)\leq t\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&x\geq 0.\end{array}

(5)

We parametrize the problem (
5
) by
y=(k,p)
, the buying and selling prices of the products, and would like to construct uncertainty sets without knowing the distributions of
y
and
u
.

Problem setup.

The uncertain parameter
u
is distributed as a log-normal distribution, with parameters

\mu=\left[\begin{array}[]{l}1.1\\
1.7\end{array}\right],\quad\Sigma=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\phantom{-}0.6&-0.3\\
-0.3&\phantom{-}0.1\end{array}\right],

and we have access to
50
independent samples.
We generate 10 values of
(k,p)
, where each
k=[4,5]+\delta_{1}
and
p=k+\max\{0,\delta_{2}\}
, with
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
standard normal random variables.
We repeat the data generation for
u
and
y
detailed above, for 15 repetitions of the experiment. Below, we show the uncertainty sets obtained for one such repetition.

Mean-Variance uncertainty set.

Standard methods in
RO
methods construct uncertainty sets based on
the empirical mean
\hat{\mu}
and covariance
\hat{\Sigma}
of the uncertainty,

{\mathcal{U}}{(\theta)}=\{u=\hat{\mu}+\hat{\Sigma}^{1/2}z\mid\|z\|_{2}\leq\rho\}=\{b^{\rm mv}+A^{\rm mv}z\mid\|z\|_{2}\leq\rho\},

(6)

where
\theta=(A^{\rm mv},b^{\rm mv},\rho)
. The parameter
\rho
represents the size of the uncertainty set, and, for one set of data, we have

A^{\rm mv}=\hat{\Sigma}^{1/2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\phantom{-}3.08&-0.83\\
-0.83&\phantom{-}1.49\end{array}\right],\quad b^{\rm mv}=\hat{\mu}=\left[\begin{array}[]{l}3.96\\
5.82\end{array}\right].

) with different shape,
i.e.
,

{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)=\{u=b^{\rm re}+A^{\rm re}z\mid\|z\|_{2}\leq\rho\}
, where
\theta=(A^{\rm re},b^{\rm re},\rho)
and

A^{\rm re}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}0.12&2.05\\

Comparison.

In Figure

, we compare the two uncertainty sets where we vary
\rho
while
A^{\rm st},b^{\rm st}
and

are fixed.
For each value of
\rho
we compare the average out-of-sample objective value at the optimizers
x(\theta,y)
against the out-of-sample empirical probability of constraint violation. The average is taken across problem instances
y
’s, testing datasets of
N=450
uncertainty realizations, and over all repetitions of the experiment.
We note that the reshaped uncertainty set achieves data-driven solutions that give, on average, better trade-offs.
While the mean-variance uncertainty set conforms to the shape of data, it might be conservative because it does not take into account the structure of the optimization problem.
In contrast, the reshaped uncertainty set gives better (lower) out-of-sample costs for the same probability of constraint satisfaction, even though it does not conform as well to the shape of the data, and covers a lower percentage of the data points.

In the next sections, we introduce an automatic technique to find such sets with better performance, by tuning their shape and size such that the problem objective is minimized while guaranteeing constraint satisfaction across the family of parametric
RO
problems (
1
).

3
Learning the uncertainty set

3.1
Constrained learning formulation

Consider a
RO
problem (
1
).
We have a dataset of
N
independent samples of the uncertain parameter
u
,
U^{N}=\{u^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{u}
, governed by the product distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{N}_{u}
, and a dataset of
J
independent samples of the family parameter
y
,
Y^{J}=\{y^{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{y}
, governed by the product distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{y}^{J}
.
We define the combined samples
(u^{i},y^{j})
for
i=1,\dots,N
and
j=1,\dots,J
, and the combined dataset
U^{N}\times Y^{J},
with product distribution defined as
{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}
.
Our main requirement is to identify a set parameter
\theta
for which the solutions
x(\theta,y)
to problem (
1
) with uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}(\theta)
, satisfy the following
finite-sample probabilistic guarantee

{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}\left\{{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y)\leq 0)\geq 1-\eta\right\}\geq 1-\beta,

(7)

where
\beta>0
is the target confidence level across draws of the dataset
U^{N}\times Y^{J}
.
Instead of working with the probability
{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}
, which often leads to intractable formulations
(
Uryasev and Rockafellar, 2001
)
as discussed in Section
2
, we adopt a tractable approximation based on the
conditional value at risk

(
Uryasev and Rockafellar, 2001
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2002
)
defined in (
3
), obtaining the constraint

\mathop{\bf CVaR}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y),\eta)\leq\kappa\quad\iff\quad\inf_{\alpha}\left\{\frac{{\mbox{\bf E}}_{(u,y)}(g(x(\theta,y),u,y)-\alpha)_{+}}{\eta}+\alpha\right\}\leq\kappa,

where
\kappa>0
is a threshold value that we define in Section
4
to obtain the finite sample guarantees (
7
).
Our goal is to find the set parameter
\theta
that minimizes the average objective of the
RO
problem across realizations of the family parameter
y
, which can be written as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&{\mbox{\bf E}}_{y}\left(f(x(\theta,y))\right)\\
\mbox{subject to}&{\mbox{\bf E}}_{(u,y)}\left((g(x(\theta,y),u,y)-\alpha)_{+}/\eta+\alpha\right)\leq\kappa,\\
\end{array}

(8)

where
x(\theta,y)
are solutions of the inner
RO
problems, defined as in (
1
).
We given in Appendix
A
examples of parameterized uncertainty sets and the corresponding convex reformulations of (
1
), which we use to solve for
x(\theta,y)
.
The expectation constraint corresponds to the
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
constraint where
\alpha
is shifted to become a minimization variable.
When the functions
f
and
g
are convex in
x
, this shift in
\alpha
has no impact on the optimal solution
(
Sarykalin et al., 2008
)
.
With this formulation, we tune
\alpha
and the set parameter
\theta
to improve the quality of the solutions
x(\theta,y)
of the inner
RO
problem (
1
).
Solving the training problem (
8
) is challenging because of the stochasticity in the objective and the constraints, and the nonconvex bi-level structure introduced by the inner
RO
problem.
We address these challenges using a stochastic augmented Lagrangian algorithm.

3.2
Training the uncertainty set using a Stochastic augmented Lagrangian algorithm

As the distributions
{\mathbf{P}}_{u}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{y}
are unknown, we are unable to obtain the true expectation in (
8
). As such, we work with sampled versions of the expectations. We define a condition on the sampled
\mathop{\bf CVaR}

\widehat{\mathop{\bf CVaR}}(\theta)=\inf_{\alpha}\frac{1}{NJ}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{(g(x(\theta,y^{j}),u^{i},y^{j})-\alpha)_{+}}{\eta}+\alpha\right)\leq\kappa,

(9)

and require this condition to hold for feasible
\theta

To find such a

F(\theta)=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}f(x(\theta,y^{j}))\quad\text{and}\quad H(\alpha,\theta)=\frac{1}{NJ}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(g(x(\theta,y^{j}),u^{i},y^{j})-\alpha)_{+}/\eta+\alpha,

evaluated over the dataset

U^{N}\times Y^{J}
. We use these approximations to define the sampled version of problem (

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&F(\theta)\\
\mbox{subject to}&H(\alpha,\theta)+s=\kappa\\
&s\geq 0,\\
\end{array}

(10)

where

is the non-negative slack variable in

to represent the inequality constraint as an equality.
We can now write the augmented Lagrangian function as

\displaystyle L(\alpha,s,\theta,\lambda,\mu)=F(\theta)

Using the stochastic augmented Lagrangian approach outlined in Algorithms

where

3.2.1
Path differentiability and conservative Jacobians

In order to apply Algorithms
1
and
2
, we need to establish the existence of
J_{L}
and
J_{\hat{L}}
, the conservative Jacobians of the augmented Lagrangian functions
L
and
\hat{L}
.
Conservative Jacobians, introduced in
(
Bolte and Pauwels, 2019
)
, are a generalized form of Jacobians for functions that are almost everywhere differentiable.

Definition 3.1

(Conservative Jacobian
(
Bolte et al., 2021
)
)
.

Given a locally Lipschitz continuous function
F:{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}\rightarrow{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
, we call
J_{F}:{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times m}
its conservative Jacobian if
J_{F}
has a closed graph, is locally bounded, and is nonempty with

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}F(\gamma(t))\in J_{F}(\gamma(t))\dot{\gamma}(t)~~\text{a.e.},

for any absolutely continuous curve
\gamma
in
{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
.
A locally Lipschitz function
F
that admits a conservative Jacobian
J_{F}
is called path differentiable.

To obtain the conservative Jacobians
J_{L}
and
J_{\hat{L}}
, we note the following assumptions.

Assumption 3.1

(Definability)
.

All functions
f
and
g_{l}
in the robust optimization problem (
1
) are
definable

(
van den Dries and Miller, 1999
;
Coste, 2000
;
Bolte et al., 2021
)
.

The class of definable functions is very wide and includes semi-algebraic functions; for an overview, see the works by
Bolte and Pauwels, 2019
and
Bolte et al., 2021
. For a foundational work, see
van den Dries and Miller, 1999
.
Assuming the definability of the objective and constraint functions, it follows that
F
and
H
, and subsequently
L
, as compositions of definable pieces, are also definable
(
Bolte et al., 2021
)
.

Assumption 3.2

(Local Lipschitzness)
.

The Lagrangian function
L(\alpha,s,\theta,\lambda,\mu)
is locally Lipschitz in variables
\alpha,s,\theta,\lambda,\mu
. A function
F:{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
is locally Lipschitz if, for each
x\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
, there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{V}
of
x
such that
F
is Lipschitz on
\mathcal{V}
.

We also require the augmented Lagrangian to be locally Lipschitz continuous, as definable locally Lipschitz functions are shown to be path differentiable, meaning they admit conservative Jacobians and follow the chain rule
(
Bolte et al., 2021
)
.
Admittance of the chain rule is imperative, as

L
depends on
\theta
indirectly through the solution
x(\theta,y)
of the inner robust problem (
1
).
In order to establish the conservative Jacobians of
L
in the variable
\theta
, we thus need to show the path differentiability of the inner problem and compute
J_{x}(\theta)
, for which we require a uniqueness assumption.

Assumption 3.3

(Uniqueness)
.

The robust optimization problem (
1
) has a convex conic reformulation with a unique solution.

We can then make use of the results of
Agrawal et al., 2019b
and
Bolte et al., 2021
, which enables us to find conservative Jacobians of optimal solutions for conic problems by differentiating through the KKT optimality conditions.
We state the theorem below, with the proof delayed to Appendix
C
.

Theorem 3.1

(Path differentiability of the
RO
solution)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.3
holds. Let
x(\theta,y)
be the unique solution, for a given input pair
(\theta,y)
, to the convex conic reformulation of the inner optimization problem (
1
).
This solution is path differentiable with nonempty conservative Jacobian
J_{x}(\theta)\neq\emptyset
.

We can then evaluate the conservative Jacobians of the augmented Lagrangian function.

Proposition 3.1

(Conservative Jacobians of the augmented Lagrangian (
11
))
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
hold, and for each given input pair
(\theta,y)
, the solution
x(\theta,y)
of the optimization problem (
1
) is path differentiable with conservative Jacobian
J_{x}(\theta)
. Then, the Lagrangian function
L(\alpha,s,\theta,\lambda,\mu)
, from equation (
11
), and its batched version
\hat{L}(\alpha,s,\theta,\lambda,\mu)
, are path differentiable with conservative Jacobians
J_{L}(\alpha,s,\theta)
and
J_{\hat{L}}(\alpha,s,\theta)
.

The specific forms of the conservative Jacobians established in Proposition
3.1
are given in Appendix
B
.

3.2.2
Convergence analysis

To show convergence of the augmented Lagrangian algorithm, given in Algorithm
1
, we require the convergence of the inner loop, Algorithm
2
.
The convergence of Algorithm
2
relies on the path-differentiability of the Lagrangian function, as well as the boundedness of the iterates and the Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda

Assumption 3.4

(Boundedness)
.

There exists a constant
\alpha^{\rm max}>0
and compact set
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\subset{\mbox{\bf R}}
, such that, for
\alpha^{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
,
\|\alpha^{t}\|\leq\alpha^{\rm max}
almost surely.
Similarly, there exist constants
\theta^{\rm max},s^{\rm max},x^{\rm max},y^{\rm max}>0
, compact sets
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\subset{\mbox{\bf R}}^{q}
,
\mathcal{D}_{s}\subset{\mbox{\bf R}}
, and
\mathcal{X}\subset{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
, such that, for
\theta^{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
,

, and

, we have

,

, and

almost surely.
In addition, the optimal Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda^{\star}\in[\lambda^{\rm{min}}
,
\lambda^{\rm{max}}]
.

Lastly, we require that the step sizes are nonnegative, square summable, but not summable.

Assumption 3.5

(Step sizes)
.

The step sizes
\{\delta^{t}\}_{t\in\mathbf{N}}
are chosen such that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\delta^{t}=\infty,\quad\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(\delta^{t})^{2}<\infty,\quad\delta^{t}=o(1/\log(t)).

Under these assumptions, by Theorem 6.2 of
Davis et al., 2018
and Theorem 3 of
Bolte et al., 2021
,
the sequence

in Algorithm

converges to a stationary point of the Lagrangian function (

) with arbitrary
\lambda
and
\mu
.

We note that
\lambda^{k}
and
\mu^{k}
in Algorithm
1
are potentially unbounded, in which case they do not converge.
The stationary point
(\alpha^{k},s^{k},\theta^{k})
of the augmented Lagrangian function for any
\lambda^{k}
and
\mu^{k}

is, therefore, not guaranteed to be a feasible solution of the
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
-constrained problem (

).
However, with the use of the safeguards

,

(
Birgin and Martinez, 2009

More specifically, below we show that the algorithm finds a feasible point of condition (

The proof is delayed to Appendix

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
to

4
Probabilistic guarantees of constraint satisfaction

In the previous section, we have proved convergence to a feasible solution of the sampled problem (
10
), which does not exactly correspond to the probabilistic guarantees of constraint satisfaction (
7
).
To arrive at our desired guarantees, where the inner probability is with respect to the unknown joint distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}
, we need to analyze the generalization behavior of the robust solutions on out-of-sample data.
We begin with a boundedness assumption on the function inside the expectation from the
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
definition, which appears in (
8
).

Assumption 4.1
.

The function
g(x(\theta,y),u,y)-\alpha)_{+}/\eta+\alpha
maps to values in
(-c^{\rm min},c^{\rm max})
, where
0\leq c^{\rm min},c^{\rm max}<\infty
, for all
\theta,\alpha,u,y
in its support.

Let
C=c^{\rm min}+c^{\rm max}
and define the normalized function

\psi_{z}(u,y)=\frac{1}{C}\left(c^{\rm min}+\frac{(g(x,u,y)-\alpha)_{+}}{\eta}+\alpha\right),

(13)

where
z=(x,\alpha)\in\mathcal{Z}\subset{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n+1}
.
Note that when Assumption
3.4
holds, we have
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
being a compact set.
The function
\psi_{z}
then maps to values in
(0,1)
, and we consider the entire function class

\Psi=\{\psi_{z}\}_{z\in\mathcal{Z}},

which we use to acquire the finite sample probabilistic guarantee in Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.1

(Finite sample probabilistic guarantee)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
to
4.1
hold.
For the function class
\Psi
defined above, suppose the following condition on the covering number
N
holds with constants

and

, relative to the

\sup_{Q}N(\delta,\Psi,L_{2}(Q))\leq\left(\frac{K}{\delta}\right)^{V},

(14)

1

\epsilon

{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}\left({\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}(g(x,u,y)\leq 0)\geq 1-\eta\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\right)\geq 1-\beta,

(15)

where
\beta=(D\sqrt{N}C\tau/\sqrt{V})^{V}\exp(-2NC^{2}\tau^{2})

\tau\leq(c^{\min}+\kappa+\epsilon)/C

Proof.

By the definition of the function class
\Psi
and Assumptions
3.4
and
4.1

{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}\left(\sup_{\psi_{z}\in\Psi}\left\|\frac{1}{NJ}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{z}(u^{i},y^{j})-{\mbox{\bf E}}_{(u,y)}[\psi_{z}(u,y)]\right\|\leq\tau\right)\geq 1-\left(\frac{D\sqrt{N}C\tau}{\sqrt{V}}\right)^{V}\exp(-2NC^{2}\tau^{2}).

By the convergence of Algorithm

),

{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}\left(\sup_{\psi_{z}\in\Psi}{\mbox{\bf E}}_{(u,y)}[\psi_{z}(u,y)]\leq(1/C)(c^{\min}+\kappa+\epsilon)+\tau\right)\geq 1-\beta,

{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}\left(\mathop{\bf CVaR}(g(x,u,y),\eta)\leq 0\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\right)\geq 1-\beta,

{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}\left({\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}(g(x,u,y)\leq 0)\geq 1-\eta\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\right)\geq 1-\beta.

(16)

∎

x

\mathcal{X}

.

Corollary 4.1.1
.

Suppose Assumption
3.4

\theta^{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\theta}

{\mathbf{P}}^{N\times J}\left\{{\mathbf{P}}_{(u,y)}(g(x(\theta^{\star},y),u,y)\leq 0)\geq 1-\eta\right\}\geq 1-\beta.

|g(x,u,y)-g(x^{\prime},u,y)|\leq\bar{L}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}\quad\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},~u\in\mathcal{D}_{u},~y\in\mathcal{D}_{y},

Covering number for
L

When
\psi_{z}

In this case, the covering number condition (
14
) reduces to

\sup_{Q}N(\delta,\Psi,L_{2}(Q))\leq N(\delta/L,\mathcal{Z},\|\cdot\|_{2})\leq\left(1+\frac{(x^{\rm max}+\alpha^{\rm max})L}{\delta}\right)^{n+1}\leq\left(\frac{K}{\delta}\right)^{n+1},

for every
0<\delta<K
, where
K=\delta+(x^{\rm max}+\alpha^{\rm max})L
.
Then,
\beta=(D\sqrt{N}C\tau/\sqrt{n+1})^{n+1}\exp(-2NC^{2}\tau^{2})
.

Problem description.

We consider the portfolio management problem where we select a portfolio
x\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
of stocks to maximize
u^{T}x
, where
u\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
are the uncertain asset returns.
The trading costs are proportional to the deviations from our nominal holdings
x^{\text{nom}}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
,
i.e.
,
\lambda^{\rm trade}\|x-x^{\text{nom}}\|_{1}
with
\lambda^{\rm trade}>0
.
We seek to minimize the objective

-u^{T}x+\lambda^{\rm trade}\|x-x^{\text{nom}}\|_{1}.

We parametralize the problem with the family parameter
y=x^{\text{nom}}
, and solve the
RO
problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&t+\lambda^{\text{trade}}\|x-x^{\text{nom}}\|_{1}\\
\mbox{subject to}&-u^{T}x\leq t\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&\mathbf{1}^{T}x=1,\quad x\geq 0,\end{array}

where the uncertain parameter
u
stands for the unknown returns.

Data.

We consider the case where
n=5
and
n=10
stocks. We let
\lambda^{\text{trade}}=0.2
. The uncertain returns
u
is sampled from a multivariate normal distribution
N(\mu,\Sigma)
, where
\mu
is generated uniformly on
[0.5,1]^{n}
, and
\Sigma
is generated as
0.1FF^{T}
, where
F
is generated on
[0,1]^{n\times 2}
.
The nominal holdings
\bar{x}^{\text{nom}}
is generated from a Dirichlet distribution with parameter values equal to
\mu
. We consider 10 instances of
x^{\text{nom}}
, distributed normally around
\bar{x}^{\text{nom}}
, with a variance of
0.05
.
Each sample is then projected to be non-negative, and normalized to sum to 1.
The total training sample size is
N=500
, with a testing sample size of
20000
.

Uncertainty set.

We learn both a

RO
and a
DRO
uncertainty set. For
RO
, the set is ellipsoidal with with norm
p=2
, and for
DRO
, we train the shape of a

DRO
set. See Appendix
A
for details on the

By choosing

, we speed up the training procedure with minimal performance degradation compared to training with Wasserstein
DRO

(
Wang et al., 2022
)
.
For both
n=5

\eta\in\eta^{\text{list}}=(0.01,0.03,0.05,0.08,0.10,0.15,0.2,0.3)

5.2
Inventory management

Problem description.

We consider a two-stage adjustable
RO
problem on inventory management
(
Iancu and Trichakis, 2014
, Section 5.2.1)
. There exist a retail network consisting of a single warehouse and
n
different retail points, indexed by
i=1,\dots,n
. For simplicity, only a single product is sold.
There are a total of
C
units of the product available, and each retail point starts with
0
inventory and is capable of stocking at most
c_{i}
units.
The transportation costs for distributing the items at the
i
th retail point is
t_{i}
currency units per unit of inventory, and the operating cost is
h_{i}
per unit.
The revenues per unit is
r_{i}
, which we take as our family parameter,
{\it i.e.},~y=r
, as these values are known but may be subject to minor changes across problem instances.
Customer demand is uncertain, with a nominal demand plus an additional amount dependent on
m
market factors.
The formula for the demand at retail point
i
is

a_{i}=\bar{a}_{i}+q_{i}^{T}u,

where
\bar{a}_{i}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}
denotes the nominal demand, and
q_{i}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
denotes the exposure of demand
a_{i}
to the market factors.
We denote with
u
the unknown market factors, which belong to an uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)
.
The decision variable is the stocking decisions for all retail points, denoted
s\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
.
In addition, the problem involves a vector
w(u)
of realized sales at each point, which is dependent on stocking decision
s
and the unknown factors
u
affecting the demand.
We have

w_{i}(u)=\min\{s_{i},a_{i}\},

for each retail point.
The manager makes stocking decisions to maximize worst-case profits, which is equivalent to minimizing worst-case loss,

-r^{T}w(u)+(t+h)^{T}s
,
\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)
.
For each problem instance, the
RO
formulation is given as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&\tau\\
\mbox{subject to}&-r^{T}w(u)+(t+h)^{T}s\leq\tau,\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&w_{i}(u)\leq s_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&w_{i}(u)\leq\bar{a}_{i}+q_{i}^{T}u,\quad i=1,\dots,n,\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&\mathbf{1}^{T}s=C,\quad 0\leq s\leq c,\\
\end{array}

(17)

where variables
s\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
,
w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
, and
\tau\in{\mbox{\bf R}}
. To get a tractable approximation, we replace the adjustable decision
w_{i}(u)
with their affine adjustable robust counterparts
z^{0}_{i}+v_{i}^{T}u
, where
z^{0}_{i}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}
and
v_{i}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{f}
are auxiliary introduced as part of the linear decision rules
(
Ben-Tal et al., 2009
)
. The new formulation is

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&\tau\\
\mbox{subject to}&-r^{T}z^{0}-r^{T}Vu+(t+h)^{T}s\leq\tau,\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&z^{0}_{i}+v_{i}^{T}u\leq s_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&z^{0}_{i}+v_{i}^{T}u\leq\bar{a}_{i}+q_{i}^{T}u,\quad i=1,\dots,n,\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{U}}(\theta)\\
&\mathbf{1}^{T}s=C,\quad 0\leq s\leq c,\\
\end{array}

(18)

where
V=[v_{1}\cdots v_{n}]^{T}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times f}
.
We treat the multiple uncertain constraints as a single joint chance constraint represented as the maximum of concave functions.

Data.

As in the work by
Iancu and Trichakis, 2014
, we let
n=10
. We consider the cases where the number of uncertain market factors is
m=4
and
m=8
. We set
C=200
, and all other problem data are independently sampled from uniformly distributed random variables.
Inventory capacities
c
are between 30 and 50.
Transportation and operation costs are between 1 and 3.
Sales revenues
r
are between 2 and 4, and nominal demand values are between 10 and 20.
We consider 10 instances for
r
, perturbing each by random sample of a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation
0.05
.
The market factor exposure coefficients
q_{i}
are between
-2
and
2
.
We generate uncertain market factors
u
from 3 normal distributions, to simulate 3 uncertain market environments.
For distribution
j
, the mean is
\mu_{i}=i\gamma_{j}
for all
i=1,\dots,m
, with
\gamma=(0.3,0.6,0.9)
, and the variance is
\sigma_{i}=0.02^{2}+(0.1i)^{2}
for all distributions.
The total training sample size is
N=100
, split evenly across the 3 distributions.
The testing sample size is
20000
.

Uncertainty set.

We train the shape and size of an ellipsoidal uncertainty set, with norm
p=2
, and for
\eta\in\eta^{\text{list}}=(0.01,0.03,0.05,0.08,0.10,0.15,0.2,0.3)

6
Conclusion

We develop a data-driven method to learn the shape and size uncertainty sets in robust optimization in a decision-focused manner,
i.e.
, using the structure and the solution of the robust counterpart.
By formulating the learning problem as a stochastic optimization problem with
\mathop{\bf CVaR}
constraints, we choose the shape and size of the uncertainty sets to optimize the data-driven performance while enforcing desired probabilistic guarantees.
We show convergence of our algorithm using nonsmooth implicit differentiation, and provide finite sample probabilistic guarantees on unseen data.
Through numerical examples, we demonstrate the performance gain achieved by our method compared to traditional manual tuning methods.
