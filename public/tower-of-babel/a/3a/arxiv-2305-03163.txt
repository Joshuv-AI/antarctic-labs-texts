Homogeneous isosceles-free spaces
Christian Bargetz, Adam Bartoš, Wiesław Kubiś, Franz Luggin

Abstract
We study homogeneity aspects of metric spaces in which all triples of distinct points admit pairwise different distances; such spaces are called isosceles-free . In particular, we characterize all homogeneous isosceles-free spaces up to isometry as vector spaces over the two-element field, endowed with an injective norm. Using isosceles-free decompositions, we provide bounds on the maximal number of distances in arbitrary homogeneous finite metric spaces. MSC (2020): 03C50, 20B25, 51F99, 54E35, 05C15, 05E18. Keywords: Isosceles-free metric space, homogeneity, isometry group.

Introduction

A mathematical structure is called
ultrahomogeneous
if every isomorphism between its finite (or, more generally, finitely generated), substructures extends to an automorphism. Adding bounds on the cardinality of the substructures we obtain
n
-homogeneity
, where
n\geqslant 1
is a natural number. Countable (or, more generally, countably generated) ultrahomogeneous structures are known in model theory as
Fraïssé limits
(see e.g. Hodges
[
5
]
) and they are fully characterized as unique countable ultrahomogeneous structures generated by a given class of finite (or finitely generated) structures satisfying some natural axioms, where the most important one is the
amalgamation property
. Metric spaces can be easily viewed as first order structures, for instance, replacing the metric by countably many binary relations saying that “the distance is less than a fixed positive rational number”. In this setting, isomorphisms are just bijective isometries and a metric space is ultrahomogeneous if every isometry between its finite subsets extends to a bijective auto-isometry of the space. Perhaps the first and arguably most important example is the
Urysohn space

[
22
]
, the unique separable complete ultrahomogeneous metric space
\mathbb{U}
containing isometric copies of all separable metric spaces. The space
\mathbb{U}
contains a dense countable ultrahomogeneous subspace in which all distances are rational, this is actually
the
Fraïssé limit of the class of all finite metric spaces with rational distances.

In this paper we consider the special class of metric spaces without isosceles triangles, called
isosceles-free
, in connection with homogeneity.
It turns out this class is a nice source of examples in the context of Fraïssé theory as well as in the context of finite combinatorics and the question of how many distinct distances a finite homogeneous spaces of a fixed size can have.

Our main results include:

(1)

Realizing that every
1
-homogeneous isosceles-free metric space is already ultrahomogeneous (Proposition
3.4
), and that homogeneous isosceles-free spaces are exactly
uniquely
2
-homogeneous
spaces (Proposition
3.6
).

(2)

Showing that the class of all finite isosceles-free metric spaces is a hereditary class without the
weak amalgamation property
(Theorem
3.16
).

(3)

Characterizing all homogeneous isosceles-free spaces up to isometry as normed
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-linear spaces with an injective norm (Theorem
4.9
), using an auxiliary notion of a
Boolean metric space
.

(4)

Studying more general
1
-homogeneous spaces through the lens of
singleton distances
(i.e. locally non-repeating distances) and related invariant decompositions, showing that every
1
-homogeneous metric space is Boolean or
isosceles-generated
or a
rainbow duplicate
of an isosceles-generated space (Theorem
5.19
).
In the case of
2
-homogeneous spaces, this further reduces to being isosceles-generated or isosceles-free.

(5)

Giving bounds on the maximal number of distances in a homogeneous finite metric space of size
n
.
In the case of a
2
-homogeneous space, we have the optimal bound
2^{m}(k+1)
for
n=2^{m}(2k+1)
, realized even by ultrahomogeneous spaces (Theorem
6.4
and Example

).
This bound is optimal also for
1
-homogeneous spaces whose size is odd or a power of two.
In the case of an even-sized
1
-homogeneous space of size

we give a better lower bound
2^{m}(3k+2)
(Example
6.10
).

Homogeneity

A metric space

•

n

•

•

with

;

•

if it is uniquely
n
-homogeneous for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Note that

is uniquely

-homogeneous if and only if it is
n
-homogeneous and uniquely

-homogeneous. In model theory, uniquely

We usually avoid the term

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
Y\subseteq X
be a quasi-invariant subspace.
If
X
is (uniquely)
n
-homogeneous for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
or ultrahomogeneous, then so is

, and this is witnessed by restrictions of automorphisms of

.

Proof.

Let

.
If

.
We have
\emptyset\neq B\subseteq Y\cap F[Y]
, and hence

since

is quasi-invariant.
Moreover, if

is the unique automorphism of

, then

is the unique automorphism of
Y
extending
f
.
∎

For metric spaces
X
and
Y
let
X\times_{1}Y
denote the product space endowed with the
\ell_{1}
-metric:
d(\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle,\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle)=d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})
.
Also for every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
let
f\times g
denote the map
\langle x,y\rangle\mapsto\langle f(x),g(y)\rangle
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are nonempty metric spaces such that the map
+\colon\operatorname{Dist}(X)\times\operatorname{Dist}(Y)\to\operatorname{Dist}(X\times_{1}Y)\subseteq[0,\infty)
is injective (and so bijective).
Then
\langle f,g\rangle\mapsto f\times g
is a group isomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\times\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to\operatorname{Aut}(X\times_{1}Y)
.
Moreover,
X\times_{1}Y
is (uniquely)
n
-homogeneous/ultrahomogeneous if and only if
X
and
Y
are.

Proof.

Distances in
X\times_{1}Y
are of the form
d(\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle,\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle)=d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})
. Therefore, since sums of the form
d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})
injectively map into
[0,\infty)
, there will be a one-to-one correspondence between
\operatorname{Dist}(X\times_{1}Y)
and
\operatorname{Dist}(X)\times\operatorname{Dist}(Y)
.

For every
f,f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
g,g^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
we have

\displaystyle d((f\times g)(x_{1},y_{1}),(f\times g)(x_{2},y_{2}))

\displaystyle=d_{X}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))+d_{Y}(g(y_{1}),g(y_{2}))

\displaystyle=d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})=d(\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle,\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle),

and
(f\times g)\circ(f^{\prime}\times g^{\prime})=(f\circ f^{\prime})\times(g\circ g^{\prime})
.
Hence,
f\times g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X\times_{1}Y)
and
\langle f,g\rangle\mapsto f\times g
is a group homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\times\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to\operatorname{Aut}(X\times_{1}Y)
.

Let
\pi_{X}\colon X\times_{1}Y\to X
and
\pi_{Y}\colon X\times_{1}Y\to Y
denote the projections.
For every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
,
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
,
x\in X
, and
y\in Y
we have
\pi_{X}((f\times g)(x,y))=f(x)
and
\pi_{Y}((f\times g)(x,y))=g(y)
, and hence the homomorphism
\langle f,g\rangle\mapsto f\times g
is injective.

To show that it is also surjective and to show the remaining claims, let
\phi\colon A\to B
be an isometry of some subspaces
A,B\subseteq X\times_{1}Y
.
We prove that
\phi=(\phi_{X}\times\phi_{Y})|_{A}
for some isometries
\phi_{X}\colon\pi_{X}[A]\to\pi_{X}[B]
and
\phi_{Y}\colon\pi_{Y}[A]\to\pi_{Y}[B]
.
To that end, let us look at

d(\phi(x_{1},y_{1}),\phi(x_{2},y_{2}))=d(\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle,\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle)=d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})

and note that if
\phi(x_{1},y_{1})=:\langle a,b\rangle
and
\phi(x_{2},y_{2})=:\langle c,d\rangle
, then

d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})=d_{X\times_{1}Y}(\langle a,b\rangle,\langle c,d\rangle)=d_{X}(a,c)+d_{Y}(b,d)

and it follows from our injectivity assumption of
+
on
\operatorname{Dist}(X)\times\operatorname{Dist}(Y)
that
d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=d_{X}(a,c)
and
d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})=d_{Y}(b,d)
.
In particular, if
x_{1}=x_{2}
, then
a=c
, so for all pairs of points
\langle x,y_{1}\rangle,\langle x,y_{2}\rangle
with identical
X
-components, we get that the
X
-components of the images under
\phi
coincide as well:
\pi_{X}(\phi(x,y_{1}))=\pi_{X}(\phi(x,y_{2}))=:\phi_{X}(x)
.
Also,
d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=d_{X}(a,c)
shows that
\phi_{X}
is an isometry.
Analogously, we obtain the isometry
\phi_{Y}
.

Hence,
\times\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\times\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to\operatorname{Aut}(X\times_{1}Y)
is surjective.
It also follows that every subspace
X\times\{y\}\subseteq X\times_{1}Y
(which is isometric to
X
) is quasi-invariant, and so if
X\times_{1}Y
is (uniquely)
n
-homogeneous/ultrahomogeneous, so is
X
by Proposition
2.3
, and similarly for
Y
.
Finally, if
\phi_{X}
and
\phi_{Y}
have (unique) extensions
\Phi_{X}\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
\Phi_{Y}\in\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
, then
\Phi_{X}\times\Phi_{Y}
is a (unique) extension of
\phi
, so if
X
and
Y
are (uniquely)
n
-homogeneous/ultrahomogeneous, then so is
X\times_{1}Y
.
∎

Example 2.5
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, let
C_{n}:=\langle V_{n},E_{n},d_{n}\rangle
be the
n
-point circle graph with simple graph distance, where
V_{n}:=\{0,\dotsc,n-1\}
is the vertex set, the set of edges
E_{n}:=\{\{i,i+1\}\mod n\colon 0\leqslant i<n\}
only connects consecutive vertices as well as the first and last vertex with each other, and

d_{n}\colon V_{n}\times V_{n}\to\left[0,\frac{n}{2}\right]\cap\mathbb{N}\colon\langle i,j\rangle\mapsto\min\{|i-j|,n-|i-j|\}

counts the minimum number of edges in
E_{n}
you have to cross to get from
i
to
j
.

The metric space
C_{n}
is ultrahomogeneous.

Proof.

For any
C_{n}
, it is clear that the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n})
contains all rotations around the vertex set
\Phi_{i}(k)=(i+k\mod n)
and all reflections across a vertex
i\in V_{n}
,
\Psi_{i}(k)=(2i-k\mod n)
.

Each isometry
\phi\colon A\to B
with
A,B\subseteq C_{n}
can be extended to at least one such rotation or reflection since, after choosing any two points
x\neq y\in A
(that are not antipodal in the case of even
n
), all other points
z\in C_{n}
can be uniquely determined from their distances to
x
and
y
, and thus the same holds for
\phi(z)
.
Therefore, it suffices to consider
\lvert A\rvert\leqslant 2
, and in those cases it is easy to see that rotating one point
x
onto its image and then potentially reflecting across
\phi(x)
will give an automorphism mapping
A
to
B
.
∎

3
Isosceles-free spaces

In the following, we will study metric spaces
X
with the property that all distances from a given point are distinct, i.e.
d(x,y)\neq d(x,z)
for all distinct
x,y,z\in X
. We will refer to such spaces as
isosceles-free
spaces since the condition is equivalent to “X does not contain any isosceles triangles”.
The isosceles-free spaces were introduced under the name
star-rigid
by Janoš and Martin
[
9
]
.

Observation 3.1
.

Every isosceles-free space is zero-dimensional, as observed by Hattori
[
4
, Theorem 2]
:
Every ball
B(x,r)
has at most one point at the boundary, and every subspace
C(x,y):=\{z:d(z,x)<d(z,y)\}
is clopen.
Hence, if
B(x,r)
has exactly one point
y
at the boundary,
B(x,r)\cap C(x,y)
is a basic clopen set, and otherwise
B(x,r)
is already a basic clopen set.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
X
is any metric space and
Y
is isosceles-free, then for every
x\in X
,
y\in Y
there exists at most one isometric embedding
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
which maps
x
to
y
.

Proof.

Pick two isometric embeddings
f,g
such that
f(x)=y=g(x)
.
Hence for any
x^{\prime}\in X
we have that
d(y,f(x^{\prime}))=d(x,x^{\prime})=d(y,g(x^{\prime}))
. But this means that
f(x^{\prime})=g(x^{\prime})
since otherwise, we would have a non-trivial isosceles triangle in
Y
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Every
1
-homogeneous isosceles-free space
X
is uniquely
1
-homogeneous, i.e. for every
x,y\in X
there exists precisely one
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
such that
f(x)=y
.

Proof.

Since
X
is
1
-homogeneous, there exists at least one
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
mapping
x
to
y
, and according to Proposition
3.2
, there exists at most one.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Every
1
-homogeneous isosceles-free space
X
is ultrahomogeneous.

Proof.

Let
i\colon A\rightarrow B
be an isometry from a finite subset
A\subset X
to
B\subset X
. Choose any
x\in A
and find the automorphism
f
which maps
x
to
i(x)
. Then,
i
and
f|_{A}
are both isometric embeddings from
A
into
X
which map
x
to
i(x)
. According to Proposition
3.2
, this means they are equal.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Observe that the proof of Proposition
3.4
shows that we have homogeneity not only for finite substructures, but for all substructures of
X
. This is called
absolute homogeneity
by Piotr Niemiec, studied in his recent preprint
[
17
]
.

Since
1
-homogeneity and ultrahomogeneity are equivalent for isosceles-free spaces, we will call them just
homogeneous isosceles-free spaces
.

Proposition 3.6
.

A metric space
X
is homogeneous isosceles-free if and only if it is uniquely
2
-homogeneous.

Proof.

Recall that being uniquely
2
-homogeneous is equivalent to being
2
-homogeneous and uniquely
1
-homogeneous.
Suppose
X
is
1
-homogeneous and isosceles-free.
By Proposition
3.4
,
X
is even ultrahomogeneous.
By Corollary
3.3
,
X
is uniquely
1
-homogeneous.

On the other hand, suppose that
X
is uniquely
2
-homogeneous, and let
x,y,z\in X
be such that
d(x,y)=d(x,z)
.
By
2
-homogeneity there is
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
with
f(x)=x
and
f(y)=z
.
By unique
1
-homogeneity,
f
is the unique automorphism fixing
x
, and so
f=\operatorname{id}_{X}
and
y=z
, so
X
is isosceles-free.
∎

Recall that a
Boolean group
is a group
G
such that
g^{2}=1
, i.e.
g^{-1}=g
, for every
g\in G
.
It follows that
G
is Abelian as
ghg^{-1}h^{-1}=(gh)(gh)=1
and so
gh=hg
for every
g,h\in G
.

Proposition 3.7
.

For every isosceles-free space
X
the isometry group
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is Boolean.

Proof.

For every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
x\in X
we have
d(x,f(x))=d(f(x),f(f(x)))
, and hence
x=f(f(x))
since
X
is isosceles-free.
Hence
f^{2}=\operatorname{id}_{X}
.
∎

Observation 3.8
.

Let
X
be a metric space.
For every
a\in X
let

•

D_{a}\colon X\to\operatorname{Dist}(X)
denote the
distance map

x\mapsto d(x,a)
,

•

E_{a}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to X
denote the
evaluation map

f\mapsto f(a)
.

We have the following reformulation of the properties considered.

(1)

X
is isosceles-free if and only if the maps
D_{a}
are injective, and in that case it follows that the maps
E_{a}
are injective.

(2)

X
is
1
-homogeneous if and only if the maps
E_{a}
are surjective, and in that case it follows that the maps
D_{a}
are surjective.

(3)

X
is uniquely
1
-homogeneous if and only if the maps
E_{a}
are bijective.

(4)

X
is homogeneous isosceles-free if and only if the maps
D_{a}
and
E_{a}
are bijective.

Proof.

Clearly the maps
D_{a}
being injective is essentially the definition of being isosceles-free.
The maps
E_{a}
are injective and surjective if and only if for every
x,y\in X
there exists at most and at least, respectively, one
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
such that
f(x)=y
.
Proposition
3.2
says that the first option is true for isosceles-free spaces.
Also for every
r\in\operatorname{Dist}(X)
there are
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)=r
, and so if
X
is
1
-homogeneous, for every
a\in X
there is
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
with
f(x)=a
, and so
d(a,f(y))=r
and the maps
E_{a}
are surjective.
The rest is clear.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

For every finite homogeneous isosceles-free metric space
X
we have
\lvert X\rvert=2^{m}
for some
m\in\omega
.

Proof.

We have a bijection
E_{a}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to X
and
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is a Boolean group by Proposition
3.7
.
∎

The next result exhibits a universality property of the automorphism groups of homogeneous isosceles-free spaces. Let us note that a countable ultrahomogeneous structure
U
, the Fraïssé limit of a given class of finite/finitely generated structures
\mathcal{F}
, is universal in the sense that it contains isomorphic copies of all countable structures that are unions of chains from
\mathcal{F}
. So, it is natural to ask whether
\operatorname{Aut}(U)
contains isomorphic copies of
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
for every
X\in\mathcal{F}
or, even better, for every
X
that is the union of a countable chain in
\mathcal{F}
. This universality question had been explicitly asked by Jaligot
[
7
]
and it turns out that for most classical Fraïssé classes the answer is positive
[
13
]
, however there exist relational homogeneous structures whose automorphism groups are far from being universal, see
[
14
]
. The next result gives a positive answer to the question above in the case of isosceles-free metric spaces.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
e\colon X\to Y
be an isometric embedding of metric spaces.
By an
extension operator
along
e
we mean a group homomorphism
e_{*}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
(which is necessarily injective) such that
e_{*}(f)\circ e=e\circ f
for every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
. In the case that
e
is the inclusion
X\subseteq Y
, this means simply that
e_{*}(f)
extends
f
for every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
e\colon X\to Y
be an isometric embedding of an isosceles-free space into a homogeneous isosceles-free space.

(1)

There is a unique extension operator
e_{*}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
.

(2)

For any
a\in X
we have
e_{*}=E_{e(a)}^{-1}\circ e\circ E_{a}
.

(3)

The assignment
X\mapsto\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
e\mapsto e_{*}
defines a functor from the category of homogeneous isosceles-free metric spaces and isometric embeddings to the category of Boolean groups and injective group homomorphisms.

Proof.

If
e_{*}
is an extension operator and
a\in X
, then for every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
we have
e_{*}(f)(e(a))=e(f(a))
, and by unique
1
-homogeneity of
Y
,
e_{*}(f)
is the unique automorphism of
Y
mapping
e(a)
to
e(f(a))
, so
E_{e(a)}(e_{*}(f))=e(E_{a}(f))
.
This shows
(2)
and uniqueness in
(1)
if
X\neq\emptyset
.
For
X=\emptyset
, we have
\operatorname{Aut}(X)=\{\operatorname{id}_{X}\}
and
(1)
holds.

To show existence of the extension operator for
X\neq\emptyset
, we fix any
a\in X
and for
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
we let
e_{*}(f)
be the unique automorphism of
Y
mapping
e(a)\mapsto e(f(a))
.
Both
e_{*}(f)\circ e
and
e\circ f
map
a\mapsto e(f(a))
, and so they are equal by Proposition
3.2
.
For
f,g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
we have
(e_{*}(f)\circ e_{*}(g))\circ e=e_{*}(f)\circ e\circ g=e\circ(f\circ g)
, and hence
e_{*}
is a group homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
.
Moreover the assignment is injective: if
e_{*}(f)=e_{*}(g)
, then
e\circ f=e\circ g
and
f=g
since
e
is an embedding.

To show
(3)
, consider two isometric embeddings between homogeneous isosceles-free spaces
i\colon X\to Y
and
j\colon Y\to Z
.
For every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
we have

(j_{*}\circ i_{*})(f)\circ(j\circ i)=j_{*}(i_{*}(f))\circ j\circ i=j\circ i_{*}(f)\circ i=j\circ(i\circ f),

and clearly
j_{*}\circ i_{*}
is a group homomorphism.
Hence,
j_{*}\circ i_{*}=(j\circ i)_{*}
.
Clearly also
(\operatorname{id}_{X})_{*}=\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Aut}(X)}
.
∎

In the following, we will talk about classes of metric spaces (with isometric embeddings as morphisms), and we define the weak amalgamation property, which was formally introduced by Ivanov
[
6
]
and independently by Kechris and Rosendal
[
10
]
in connections with generic automorphisms of Fraïssé limits. It was recently explored in the context of Fraïssé limits by Krawczyk and Kubiś
[
11
]
; a purely category-theoretic framework was developed in
[
12
]
and for more information we refer to these two sources.
(WAP) is crucial for the existence of (weak) Fraïssé sequences and thus for the construction of an object
M
which is
generic
over
\mathcal{K}
, roughly speaking, the most common (or perhaps most complicated) object (a metric space, in our case) that can be built as the union of a chain in
\mathcal{K}
.

In the context of metric spaces, it seems to be rather difficult to find easy-to-describe classes that lack the weak amalgamation property. Note that graphs can be seen as metric spaces, with distance set
\{0,1,2\}
depending on whether two points are connected by an edge or not. Then, graph embeddings correspond to isometric embeddings and thus some nontrivial examples of hereditary classes without (WAP) are given in
[
11
]
and
[
19
]
.

Theorem 3.16
.

The class of all finite isosceles-free spaces does not have the weak amalgamation property.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{K}
denote the class of all finite isosceles-free spaces, let
A:=\{0,1\}
, let
\langle A,d_{A}\rangle
be our base space in
\mathcal{K}
, and let
\langle B,d_{B}\rangle\in\mathcal{K}
be any extension of
\langle A,d_{A}\rangle
.
In order to show a failure of (WAP), we will define two one-point extensions
X:=B\cup\{x\}
and
Y:=B\cup\{y\}
of
B
, with distance functions
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
, respectively, such that no space
Z
exists in
\mathcal{K}
which allows
X
and
Y
to be embedded into it in such a way that the images of
A
coincide.

To this end, define
r_{0}
as a distance larger than any distance in
B
, and let
r_{1}
be a distance very close to
r_{0}
:

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle:=\max\{d_{B}(a,b)\colon a,b\in B\}+1,

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\min\{|d_{B}(a,0)-d_{B}(b,1)|>0\colon a,b\in B\},

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle:=r_{0}-\varepsilon.

Note that
B
is a finite metric space and thus the minimum in the definition of
\varepsilon
really is a minimum rather than an infimum (in particular,
\varepsilon>0
).

Since they are supposed to be extensions of
B
, let
d_{X}(a,b):=d_{B}(a,b)=:d_{Y}(a,b)
for any
a,b\in B
. Moreover, for any
a\in B
, let

d_{X}(a,x):=d_{B}(a,0)+r_{0},\qquad d_{Y}(a,y):=\min\{d_{B}(a,0)+r_{0},d_{B}(a,1)+r_{1}\}

Clearly, this way,
X
is a valid finite metric space and both choices of distances
\rho(a,y):=d_{B}(a,0)+r_{0}
and
\rho^{\prime}(a,y):=d_{B}(a,1)+r_{1}
within the minimum in the definition of
d_{Y}
would define a valid metric on
Y
(again with
\rho=\rho^{\prime}=d_{B}
on
B\times B
). So to show that
d_{Y}
is valid as well, observe that the minimum of two metrics always satisfies all conditions for a metric except potentially the triangle inequality. However, the two metrics
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
only differ when
y
is one of the two arguments, so we need to check whether

\displaystyle d_{Y}(a,y)=\min\{\rho(a,y),\rho^{\prime}(a,y)\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\min\{\rho(a,b)+\rho(b,y),\rho^{\prime}(a,b)+\rho^{\prime}(b,y)\}

\displaystyle=d_{B}(a,b)+\min\{\rho(b,y),\rho^{\prime}(b,y)\}.

But since
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are valid metrics on
Y
, the only cases in which this might fail are when (w.l.o.g.)
\rho(a,y)<\rho^{\prime}(a,y)
and
\rho(b,y)>\rho^{\prime}(b,y)
. But in that case,

\displaystyle d_{Y}(a,y)<\rho^{\prime}(a,y)\leqslant\rho^{\prime}(a,b)+\rho^{\prime}(b,y)=d_{Y}(a,b)+d_{Y}(b,y).

Lastly, when
y
only shows up on the right-hand side of the triangle inequality, we have to show that:

\displaystyle d_{Y}(a,b)\leqslant\min\{\rho(a,y),\rho^{\prime}(a,y)\}+\min\{\rho(y,b),\rho^{\prime}(y,b)\}=d_{Y}(a,y)+d_{Y}(y,b).

If
\rho(a,y)<\rho^{\prime}(a,y)
or
\rho(b,y)<\rho^{\prime}(b,y)
this is clear since
\rho(\,\cdot\,,y)\geqslant r_{0}>d_{B}(a,b)=d_{Y}(a,b)
on
B
. If, on the other hand,
\rho(a,y)>\rho^{\prime}(a,y)
and
\rho(b,y)>\rho^{\prime}(b,y)
, then

d_{Y}(a,y)+d_{Y}(b,y)=\rho^{\prime}(a,y)+\rho^{\prime}(b,y)\geqslant\rho^{\prime}(a,b)=d_{Y}(a,b).

So
(X,d_{X}),(Y,d_{Y})
are both valid metric spaces. Let us additionally show that they are isosceles-free: since
B
already contains no isosceles triangles, the only way to add an isosceles triangle to
X
would be if
d_{X}(a,x)=d_{X}(b,x)
for some
a,b\in B
, but in that case, it would follow that
d_{B}(a,0)=d_{B}(b,0)
, a clear contradiction.

For
Y
, the situation is slightly more complicated. If
a,b\in B
and
d_{Y}(a,y)=d_{Y}(b,y)
, then we need to consider four cases, however, if both
d_{Y}(a,y)=d_{B}(a,0)+r_{0}
and
d_{Y}(b,y)=d_{B}(b,0)+r_{0}
or
d_{B}(a,1)+r_{1}=d_{Y}(b,y)=d_{B}(b,1)+r_{1}
, then the same argument as in the previous paragraph leads to the conclusion that
B
is not isosceles-free. Thus, up to re-labelling
a
and
b
, we only need to consider the case where

d_{Y}(a,y)=d_{B}(a,0)+r_{0}=d_{Y}(b,y)=d_{B}(b,1)+r_{1}.

However, in this case,

\displaystyle d_{B}(a,0)+r_{0}=d_{B}(b,1)+r_{0}-\varepsilon\Leftrightarrow\varepsilon=d_{B}(b,1)-d_{B}(a,0).

Clearly, this cannot be the case due to our definition of
\varepsilon
since the right-hand side is always either
0
, negative or at least double the value of
\varepsilon
.

It follows that
X
and
Y
are both in
\mathcal{K}
. However, in order for (WAP) to hold, we would need to find a
Z\in\mathcal{K}
such that both
X
and
Y
embed into
Z
in such a way that
0
and
1
in
A
are mapped to the same points in
Z
no matter whether they are mapped via
X
or via
Y
.

So any such space
Z
would need to satisfy that there exist
\mathcal{K}
-embeddings
\pi_{X},\pi_{Y}
such that
\pi_{X}(0)=\pi_{Y}(0)=:0
and
\pi_{X}(1)=\pi_{Y}(1)=:1
.

However, in such a case, due to isometry of
\mathcal{K}
-embeddings, we get

d_{Z}(0,\pi_{X}(x))=d_{X}(0,x)=r_{0}=\min\{r_{0},d_{B}(0,1)+r_{1}\}=d_{Y}(0,y)=d_{Z}(0,\pi_{Y}(y)).

This contradicts our assumption that
Z\in\mathcal{K}
since for that,
Z
would have to be isosceles-free and yet
\pi_{X}(x)\neq\pi_{Y}(y)
(their distances to
1
are different, for example) and
\{0,\pi_{X}(x),\pi_{Y}(y)\}
is a non-trivial isosceles triangle in
Z
.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

Note that the result above is valid when the distance set is restricted to a dense subgroup
A
of
\mathbb{R}
, which includes the case of rational distances.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{A}
denote the class of all countable isosceles-free spaces
X
with
\operatorname{Dist}(X)\subseteq A
.

One of the properties of a Fraïssé limit is that it is universal for the associated class of countable structures. When (WAP) fails, not only is there no Fraïssé limit, but there is not even a universal structure; in fact by
[
11
, Corollary 6.3]
the universality number of
\mathcal{F}_{A}
, that is the minimal cardinality of a subfamily
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{A}
such that every
X\in\mathcal{F}_{A}
embeds isometrically into a member of
\mathcal{C}
, is the continuum.

Remark 3.18
.

Note that by classical Fraïssé theory
[
5
, Theorem 7.1.7]
, for every countable homogeneous isosceles-free metric space
X
, the family of all finite spaces embeddable into
X
(denoted by
\operatorname{Age}(X)
) has even the
amalgamation property
(AP).
This is no contradiction with the previous result –
\operatorname{Age}(X)
is a much more restrictive class of finite isosceles-free spaces than
\mathcal{F}_{A}
from the previous remark.
In fact, in a homogeneous isosceles-free space
X
for every positive
p\neq q\in\operatorname{Dist}(X)
there is
r\in\operatorname{Dist}(X)
such that every triangle in
X
with distances
p
and
q
is completed by the distance
r
.
Hence, every one-point extension
F\cup\{x\}\subseteq X
is uniquely determined by
d_{F}
and a single distance
d(a,x)
for a fixed point
a\in F
since every
d(b,x)
for
b\neq a\in F
is the unique distance completing the distances
d(a,x)
and
d(a,b)
.
This also shows that the class of finite homogeneous isosceles-free spaces does not have the
joint embedding property
(JEP), while it is easy to see that the class of finite isosceles-free spaces has (JEP).
We will give a precise description of
\operatorname{Age}(X)
for a homogeneous isosceles-free space
X
in Proposition
4.18
and Corollary
4.21
.

Boolean metric spaces

Definition 4.1
.

By a
Boolean metric space
we mean a nonempty
1
-homogeneous metric space
X
such that
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is a Boolean group.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that the notion of Boolean metric space we use here is not related to the notion where the metric itself takes values in a Boolean algebra, as used by, for example, Melter in
[
15
]
or Avilés in
[
1
]
.

We have shown that every homogeneous isosceles-free space is Boolean (Proposition
3.7
) and uniquely
1
-homogeneous (Corollary
3.3
).
It turns out that every Boolean metric space is uniquely
1
-homogeneous and that it is in fact enough to suppose that the automorphism group is Abelian (Corollary
4.4
).
Moreover, Boolean metric spaces can be viewed as normed
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-linear spaces, which gives us a concrete representation of every homogeneous isosceles-free space.

By a
norm
on an Abelian group
X
we mean a map
\lVert\cdot\rVert\colon X\to[0,\infty)
such that

(1)

\lVert x\rVert=0
if and only if
x=0
, for
x\in X
,

(2)

\lVert x+y\rVert\leqslant\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert
, for
x,y\in X
,

(3)

\lVert-x\rVert=\lVert x\rVert
, for
x\in X
.

It is a more general version of an F-norm in linear vector spaces, cf. Rolewicz
[
20
, p. 4]
, and is nowadays present in several aspects of group theory.
It is well-known that every norm induces an
invariant metric
on
X
(i.e. a metric such that
d(x+z,y+z)=d(x,y)
for every
x,y,z\in X
) by putting
d(x,y):=\lVert x-y\rVert
.
Then we have
\lVert x\rVert=d(x,0)
.
On the other hand, the previous formula gives a norm for any invariant metric on
X
.
Altogether, norms and invariant metrics on
X
are in one-to-one correspondence.
Similarly, norm-preserving maps
X\to Y
between normed Abelian groups are in one-to-one correspondence with isometric embeddings preserving
0
.

If the Abelian group
X
is Boolean,
X
is a linear space over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and the norm is trivially a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-norm, i.e. it also satisfies
\lVert\alpha\cdot x\rVert=\lvert\alpha\rvert\cdot\lVert x\rVert
for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
x\in X
.
Altogether, a Boolean group endowed with an invariant metric is the same thing as a normed
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-linear space, and isometric embeddings preserving
0
are linear.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
X
be a
1
-homogeneous space such that
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is Abelian.

(1)

For every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
, the displacement
d(x,f(x))
does not depend on the point
x\in X
.

(2)

Putting
\lVert f\rVert:=d(x,f(x))
for any
x\in X
defines a norm on
\langle\operatorname{Aut}(X),\circ\rangle
.

(3)

X
is uniquely
1
-homogeneous.

(4)

The evaluation map
E_{a}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to X
is an isometry for every
a\in X
.

(5)

\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is a Boolean group.

Proof.

(1)

Let
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
x,y\in X
.
By
1
-homogeneity there is a
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
with
g(x)=y
.
We have
d(y,f(y))=d(g(x),f(g(x)))=d(g(x),g(f(x)))=d(x,f(x))
.

(2)

We have
\lVert f\rVert=0
if and only if
d(x,f(x))=0
for every
x\in X
, i.e. if and only if
f=\operatorname{id}_{X}
.
For every
f,g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
x\in X
we have
\lVert f\circ g\rVert=d(x,f(g(x)))\leqslant d(x,f(x))+d(f(x),f(g(x)))=d(x,f(x))+d(x,g(x))=\lVert f\rVert+\lVert g\rVert
.
Finally,
\lVert f^{-1}\rVert=d(x,f^{-1}(x))=d(f(x),x)=\lVert f\rVert
.

(3)

If
f(x)=g(x)
for
f,g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and some
x\in X
, then
\lVert f^{-1}\circ g\rVert=d(x,f^{-1}(g(x)))=d(f(x),g(x))=0
, and so
f^{-1}\circ g=\operatorname{id}_{X}
by the first property of the norm.

(4)

We have
d(E_{a}(f),E_{a}(g))=d(f(a),g(a))=d(a,f^{-1}(g(a)))=\lVert f^{-1}\circ g\rVert=d_{\operatorname{Aut}(X)}(f,g)
.
Hence,
E_{a}
is an isometric embedding.
It is onto since
X
is
1
-homogeneous (see Observation
3.8
)

(5)

By
(4)
,
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is isometric to
X
, and so is uniquely
1
-homogeneous by
(3)
.
By the third property of the norm, the map
\phi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
,
f\mapsto f^{-1}
, is norm-preserving, and hence an isometry fixing
\operatorname{id}_{X}
.
Therefore
\phi(\operatorname{id}_{X})=\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Aut}(X)}(\operatorname{id}_{X})
, and so
\phi=\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Aut}(X)}
since
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is uniquely
1
-homogeneous.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

For a
1
-homogeneous metric space
X
,
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is Abelian if and only if
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is Boolean, and in this case,
X
is uniquely
1
-homogeneous.

Corollary 4.5
.

Definition 4.10

Remark 4.11
.

The triangle inequality of the space
\langle 2^{(I)},\lVert\cdot\rVert\rangle
expressed using the norm is

\lVert x\mathbin{\triangle}z\rVert\leqslant\lVert x\mathbin{\triangle}y\rVert+\lVert y\mathbin{\triangle}z\rVert\quad\text{for every $x,y,z\in 2^{(I)}$},

but it reduces to

\lVert x^{\prime}\mathbin{\triangle}y^{\prime}\rVert\leqslant\lVert x^{\prime}\rVert+\lVert y^{\prime}\rVert\quad\text{for every $x,y\in 2^{(I)}$}

in every Boolean group with an invariant metric since we can put
x^{\prime}=x\mathbin{\triangle}y
and
y^{\prime}=y\mathbin{\triangle}z
.

A different simplification of the original inequality is the fact that it is enough to verify it only for triples of pairwise disjoint sets.
For every

we put
w:=\{i\in I:\lvert\{k<3:i\in x_{k}\}\rvert\geqslant 2\}
.
Then

is a finite set, the sets

k<3

The triangle inequality then reduces to

\lVert x\cup z\rVert\leqslant\lVert x\cup y\rVert+\lVert y\cup z\rVert\quad\text{for every pairwise disjoint $x,y,z\in 2^{(I)}$.}

In particular, the norm is

Proposition 4.16
.

Let

be homogeneous isosceles-free spaces.

(1)

For every isometric embedding

(2)

Every isometric embedding
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(Y)

\operatorname{id}_{X}
to
\operatorname{id}_{Y}
is an extension operator and hence linear, and every isometric embedding

is affine.

Proof.

For every
a\in X
and

,
e_{*}(f)
maps
e(a)
to

, and so we have
\lVert e_{*}(f)\rVert=d(e(a),e(f(a)))=d(a,f(a))=\lVert f\rVert
.
As a group homomorphism,

is linear.
Together, a norm-preserving linear map is an isometric embedding.

For every embedding

we have that

by Proposition

, and hence is affine as a composition of affine maps.
On the other hand, every isometric embedding

such that

is of the form

, and so is linear.
Namely, we take any
a\in X
and

and put

.
Since

, we have

, and so

-homogeneity.
∎

Example 4.17
.

There is a homogeneous isosceles-free space

In the last part of this section we describe amalgamation classes associated to homogeneous isoceles-free spaces, as promised in Remark

Let us call a triple

of positive real numbers a

if
p\leqslant q+r
and
q\leqslant r+p
and
r\leqslant p+q
.
If
p=q
, then

is the only

Similarly, if
p=0
, then
r=q
is the only choice completing the triangle.
These triangles are called

, while triangles with

are

.
Let

denote the space

with the metric

,
d(a,c)=q
,
d(b,c)=r
.
This notation automatically implies that if
p=0
, then
a=b
, and so on.
We write just
T_{p,q,r}
when the supporting set is irrelevant.

For a class
\mathcal{F}
of metric spaces we denote the set of distances
\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{F}}\operatorname{Dist}(X)\subseteq[0,\infty)
by
\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
.
We also call
\mathcal{F}

hereditary
if for every isometric embedding
e\colon A\to B
with
B\in\mathcal{F}
we have
A\in\mathcal{F}
.
This automatically means that
\mathcal{F}
is closed under isomorphic copies.

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of isosceles-free metric spaces such that for every
p,q\in\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
there is
A\in\mathcal{F}
and
a,b,c\in A
such that
d(a,b)=p
and
d(a,c)=q
.
Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

\mathcal{F}
can be extended to a hereditary class
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
of isosceles-free spaces with the amalgamation property such that
\operatorname{Dist}(\overline{\mathcal{F}})=\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
.

(2)

We have

(2a)

for every
p,q\in\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
there is a unique
t(p,q)\in\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
such that
\langle p,q,t(p,q)\rangle
is a triangle and

embeds into a member of
\mathcal{F}
,

(2b)

for every
p,q,p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
with
t(p,q)=t(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
we have
t(p,p^{\prime})=t(q,q^{\prime})
.

(3)

There is a homogeneous isosceles-free space
X_{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\operatorname{Dist}(X_{\mathcal{F}})=\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
and
\operatorname{Age}(X_{\mathcal{F}})\supseteq\mathcal{F}
.

Moreover, the class
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
and the space
X_{\mathcal{F}}
are unique, and
\overline{\mathcal{F}}=\operatorname{Age}(X_{\mathcal{F}})
.

Proof.

Suppose
(1)
and let
p,q\in\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
. We show that
(2)
holds.
By the assumption there is some
r
such that
T_{p,q,r}
is embedded into a member of
\mathcal{F}
.
Since
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is hereditary, we have
T_{p,q,r}(a,b,c)\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.
If
T_{p,q,r^{\prime}}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}
for some
r^{\prime}\neq r
, without loss of generality, we have
T_{p,q,r^{\prime}}(a,b,c^{\prime})\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, necessarily with
c^{\prime}\neq c
.
We view
T_{p,q,r}(a,b,c)
and

as one-point extensions of
\{a,b\}
.
By the amalgamation property it is possible to define

so that

would form an isosceles triangle as

.
Hence,
t(p,q)
is well-defined and unique.

Next, let
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
with
t(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})=t(p,q)=r
.
Hence, without loss of generality,
T_{p,q,r}(a,b,c),T_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r}(a^{\prime},b,c)\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.
By the amalgamation property, their union is contained in a space
A
containing also the triangles

and
T_{q,q^{\prime},t(q,q^{\prime})}(c,a,a^{\prime})
.
Hence,
t(p,p^{\prime})=d_{A}(a,a^{\prime})=t(q,q^{\prime})
.

Now suppose
(2)
in order to show that it implies
(3)
.
We put
X_{\mathcal{F}}:=\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
, and
p+q:=t(p,q)
and
\lVert p\rVert:=p
for

.
It is enough to show that
+
is a commutative associative addition with the neutral element

, and that
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is an injective norm.
By considering degenerate triangles, it is easy to see that
0
is the neutral element and that
p=-p
for every
p\in X_{\mathcal{F}}
.
Clearly,
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is injective and we have
\lVert p\rVert=0
if and only if
p=0
.
The triangle inequality for
\lVert\cdot\rVert
follows from the fact that every
\langle p,q,t(p,q)\rangle
is a triangle.
The only missing property is the associativity of

.
For every
p,q,r\in\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
we have

, and so by
(2)(2b)

t(p,q+r)=t(p+q,r)
.

Finally, suppose
(3)
and put
\overline{\mathcal{F}}:=\operatorname{Age}(X_{\mathcal{F}})
. We show that
(1)
holds.
Clearly,
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is a hereditary class of isosceles-free spaces extending
\mathcal{F}
with
\operatorname{Dist}(\overline{\mathcal{F}})=\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})
.
The amalgamation property is also easy to see and follows from the homogeneity of

as in classical Fraïssé theory
[

.

This finishes the proof of the equivalences.

Next we show the uniqueness of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.
On one hand, every

satisfies

for every

On the other hand, by induction, every such metric space

\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.
Since
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is hereditary and
\operatorname{Dist}(\overline{\mathcal{F}})=\operatorname{Dist}(\mathcal{F})

\mathcal{F}
.
For every

as the disjoint union

\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.
This is because in any such amalgamation we have

The uniqueness of

follows from the fact that for any

the map
\lVert x\rVert:=d(x,0)
is a bijection

and we have

for every

.
∎

It is easy to check that the properties of

, and hence

and we can apply Proposition
4.18
.
Since

, we have

if the amalgamation extension exists.
Hence the claim follows from Proposition
4.18

(2)
.
∎

5
Decompositions of homogeneous spaces

In this section we study two invariant decompositions of homogeneous spaces based on non-repeating distances.
This will give more insight into the structure of homogeneous spaces that are close to being isosceles-free and will be applied in Section
6
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that an equivalence
\sim
on a metric space
X
and the corresponding decomposition
X/{\sim}
are
invariant
if for every automorphism
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and a component
C\in X/{\sim}
we have that
f[C]
is a component.
Equivalently,
x\sim y
implies
f(x)\sim f(y)
for every
x,y\in X
.

By
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
we denote the family of all automorphisms
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
setwise fixing all components, i.e.
f[C]=C
for every
C\in X/{\sim}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\sim
be an invariant decomposition of a metric space
X
.

(1)

\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
is a normal subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
.

(2)

The restriction map
\rho_{C}\colon\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(C)
,
f\mapsto f|_{C}
, is a group homomorphism.

(3)

If
X
is
1
-homogeneous, all components
C\in X/{\sim}
are isometric.

(4)

If
X
is
n
-homogeneous or ultrahomogeneous, so is every component
C\in X/{\sim}
, and this is witnessed by the subgroup
\operatorname{im}(\rho_{C})\leqslant\operatorname{Aut}(C)
.

Proof.

(1)

Clearly,
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is a subgroup.
Also for every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
,
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
, and a component
C
, we have
g[f[g^{-1}[C]]=:g[f[C^{\prime}]]=g[C^{\prime}]=g[g^{-1}[C]]=C
, and so
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
is a normal subgroup.

(2)

This is clear.

(3)

For every two components
C,C^{\prime}
we pick points
x\in C
and
y\in C^{\prime}
.
Since
X
is
1
-homogeneous there is
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
such that
f(x)=y
and so
f[C]=C^{\prime}
, showing that
C
and
C^{\prime}
are isometric.

(4)

This follows from Proposition
2.3
since every component is quasi-invariant.
∎

In the following, we define two invariant decompositions of homogeneous spaces related to isosceles-free spaces.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
X
be a
1
-homogeneous space.
We say that a distance
s\in\operatorname{Dist}(X)
is
singleton
if for every
x\in X
there exists a unique
y\in Y
such that
d(x,y)=s
,
i.e. there is no isosceles triangle with side lengths
\langle s,s,t\rangle
in
X
(for
t>0
).
By
1
-homogeneity, it is enough if the defining condition holds at a single point
x\in X
.

Let
S_{X}\subseteq\operatorname{Dist}(X)
be the set of all singleton distances in
X
.
Clearly,
X
is isosceles-free if and only if
S_{X}=\operatorname{Dist}(X)
.

Theorem 5.4

(Decomposition into isosceles-free components)
.

Let
X
be a
2
-homogeneous space and let
x\sim y
if
d(x,y)\in S_{X}
for
x,y\in X
.
We have that
\sim
is an invariant equivalence relation inducing a decomposition of
X
into pairwise isometric homogeneous isosceles-free spaces.

Proof.

Symmetry and reflexivity are trivial, transitivity follows from the fact that if we assume
x\sim y\sim z
and
x\nsim z
, this means that
d(x,z)\notin S
, therefore (due to homogeneity) there exists some point
w\in X
with
d(x,w)=d(x,z)
, meaning the function
f\colon\{x,z\}\rightarrow\{x,w\}
which fixes
x
and maps
z
to
w
is an isometry. However, due to
2
-homogeneity, we now know that there exists an automorphism
F
which extends
f
. It follows that
d(x,y)=d(x,F(y))
, but since we assumed
x\sim y
, this means
y=F(y)
. Analogously,
d(y,z)=d(y,F(z))=d(y,w)
, but because we assumed
y\sim z
, this implies
z=w
.

Clearly, the equivalence
\sim
is invariant since isometries preserve distances, and the components
C\in X/{\sim}
are isosceles-free.
By Proposition
5.2
, they are also pairwise isometric and
2
-homogeneous, and hence ultrahomogeneous by Proposition
3.4
.
∎

A simple example of the decomposition into isosceles-free components follows.
It also shows that the map
\rho_{C}\colon\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(C)
is not necessarily injective.

Example 5.5
.

Let
C_{4}=\{1,2,3,4\}
be the four element circle graph considered in Example
2.5
.
Then the isosceles-free components of
C_{4}
are
C=\{1,3\}
and
C^{\prime}=\{2,4\}
, the pairs of antipodal points in
C_{4}
, with all distances between two components being equal to one.

Now
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(C_{4})=\{(1\ 3)^{i}(2\ 4)^{j}\in\Sigma_{4}:i,j\in\{0,1\}\}
is a
4
-element group, whereas
\operatorname{Aut}(C)=\{(1\ 3)^{i}:i\in\{0,1\}\}
and
\operatorname{Aut}(C^{\prime})=\{(2\ 4)^{i}:i\in\{0,1\}\}
are both
2
-element groups.
Hence, the restriction maps
\rho_{C},\rho_{C^{\prime}}
are surjective, but not injective.

We define another decomposition, which is in a sense dual to the previous one.

Theorem 5.6

(Decomposition into isosceles-generated components)
.

Let
X
be a
1
-homogeneous space.
Let
\sim
be the equivalence generated by the relation
x\sim y
if there is
z\neq y
such that
d(x,y)=d(x,z)
, i.e. we identify points along non-singleton distances, or equivalently collapse all non-degenerate isosceles triangles.

(1)

The equivalence
\sim
induces an invariant decomposition into isometric
1
-homogeneous components.
In particular, automorphisms map components onto components.

(2)

If
\lvert X/{\sim}\rvert\geqslant 2
, then
X
is uniquely
1
-homogeneous.

(3)

For every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
either all components
C
are fixed by
f
(setwise), or none of them are.
In the latter case we have
f\circ f=\operatorname{id}
.

(4)

For every
C\in X/{\sim}
the homomorphism
\rho_{C}\colon\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(C)
is an embedding.

(5)

For every
h\in\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
and
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)\setminus\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
we have
f\circ h\circ f^{-1}=h^{-1}
.

(6)

If
\lvert X/{\sim}\rvert\geqslant 3
, then
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is Boolean, i.e.
X
is a Boolean metric space.

(7)

If
\lvert X/{\sim}\rvert\geqslant 2
, then
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
is Abelian.

Proof.

(1)

Clearly, a distance
d(x,y)
is non-singleton from the point of view of
x
if and only if it is non-singleton from the point of view of
y
as the space
X
is
1
-homogeneous.
Also then,
d(f(x),f(y))
is non-singleton for every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
.
Hence, the generating symmetric relation and so its reflexive transitive closure is preserved by automorphisms, and we have an invariant decomposition.
The rest follows from Proposition
5.2
.

(2)

Let
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
such that
f(x)=y
.
For any
x^{\prime}
from a different component than
x
, we have that
d(x,x^{\prime})
is a singleton distance, and so
f(x^{\prime})
is the unique point
y^{\prime}
such that
d(y,y^{\prime})=d(x,x^{\prime})
.
For every
x^{\prime\prime}\sim x
we have that
x^{\prime\prime}
is from a different component than
x^{\prime}
, and we can apply the same argument for
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}
.

(3)

Suppose
y:=f(x)\nsim x
for some
x\in X
.
Since
d(x,y)=d(y,f(y))
is a singleton distance, we have
f(y)=x
.
Hence,
f
swaps the components
C_{x}
and
C_{y}
of
x
and
y
.
We will show that
f[C]\neq C
for every component
C
, and
f\circ f=\operatorname{id}
will follow as we can repeat the above argument for any point
x\in X
.

Let
C_{z}\ni z
be a component different from
C_{x}
and
C_{y}
.
The distances
\langle a,b,c\rangle:=\langle d(x,y),d(y,z),d(z,x)\rangle
are singleton, and hence pairwise different.
Moreover, by
1
-homogeneity for every point
w\in X
and all distinct distances
\{i,j\}\subseteq\{a,b,c\}
there are unique points
w_{i},w_{j}
with
d(w,w_{i})=i
and
d(w,w_{j})=j
, and necessarily
d(w_{i},w_{j})=k
where
\{i,j,k\}=\{a,b,c\}
.
By applying this to
w=y
, we have
d(y,z)=b
,
d(y,f(z))=d(f(x),f(z))=d(x,z)=c
, and so
d(z,f(z))=a
.
Hence, the unique automorphism
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
mapping
z\mapsto x
also maps
f(z)\mapsto y
.
It follows that
z\nsim f(z)
since otherwise we would have
x\sim y
as
g
preserves the equivalence
\sim
.

(4)

The map
\rho_{C}
is injective by
(2)
if there are at least two components and trivially if there is only one component.

(5)

We have
h\circ f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)\setminus\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
, and so
(h\circ f)^{2}=\operatorname{id}
by
(3)
. On the other hand,
f^{2}=\operatorname{id}
and
(h\circ f)^{2}=h\circ f\circ h\circ f=h\circ(f\circ h\circ f^{-1})
.
Together, this shows
h\circ(f\circ h\circ f^{-1})=\operatorname{id}
, and so
f\circ h\circ f^{-1}=h^{-1}
.

(6)

We already know that
f^{2}=\operatorname{id}
for
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)\setminus\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
.
Let
h\in\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
.
Suppose
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}
are distinct components of
X
.
There are
f_{1},f_{2}\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
swapping
C_{0}
with
C_{1}
and
C_{0}
with
C_{2}
, respectively.
Hence,
f_{2}\circ f_{1}
moves
C_{1}
onto
C_{2}
, and so is not a member of
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
.
On one hand,
(f_{2}\circ f_{1})\circ h\circ(f_{2}\circ f_{1})^{-1}=h^{-1}
.
On the other hand,
(f_{2}\circ f_{1})\circ h\circ(f_{2}\circ f_{1})^{-1}=f_{2}\circ(f_{1}\circ h\circ f_{1}^{-1})\circ f_{2}^{-1}=(h^{-1})^{-1}=h
.
Hence,
h^{-1}=h
and
h^{2}=\operatorname{id}
.

(7)

Let
h,k\in\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
. Since
\lvert X/{\sim}\rvert\geqslant 2
, there is an
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)\setminus\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
.
We have
h\circ f\notin\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)
.
On one hand,
(h\circ f)\circ k\circ(h\circ f)^{-1}=k^{-1}
.
On the other hand,
(h\circ f)\circ k\circ(h\circ f)^{-1}=h\circ(f\circ k\circ f^{-1})\circ h^{-1}=h\circ k^{-1}\circ h^{-1}
.
Together,
h\circ k^{-1}\circ h^{-1}=k^{-1}
and
k\circ h=h\circ k
.
∎

Definition 5.7
.

We say that a metric space is
isosceles-generated
if its decomposition into isosceles-generated components has at most one component.

Example 5.8
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we define the space
D_{n}
as follows. We take two copies of the cyclic space
C_{n}
from Example
2.5
and define the distances in
D_{n}=C_{n}\times 2
between the copies as follows:

d(\langle i,0\rangle,\langle j,1\rangle):=q_{j-i}
for every
i,j\in C_{n}
, where
q_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, are distinct distances not in
\operatorname{Dist}(C_{n})=\{0,\ldots,\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor\}
suitable for the triangle inequality, i.e.
0<\lvert q_{i}-q_{j}\rvert\leqslant 1
and
q_{i}>\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
for every
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
We choose e.g.
q_{k}:=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor+1+k/n
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

The space
D_{n}
is
1
-homogeneous:
We know that
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n})
consists of rotations
\Phi_{k}\colon i\mapsto i+k
and reflections
\Psi_{k}\colon i\mapsto k-i
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
Let
\sigma\colon 2\to 2
denote the unique transposition.
For every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
the maps
\Phi_{k}\times\operatorname{id}
and
\Psi_{k}\times\sigma
are automorphisms of
D_{n}
, as the following computations show for every
i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
x\in 2
,
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n})
,
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(2)
:

\displaystyle d\bigl(\langle f(i),g(x)\rangle,\langle f(j),g(x)\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=d_{C_{n}}(f(i),f(j))=d_{C_{n}}(i,j)=d(\langle i,x\rangle,\langle j,x\rangle),

\displaystyle d\bigl(\langle\Phi_{k}(i),\operatorname{id}(0)\rangle,\langle\Phi_{k}(j),\operatorname{id}(1)\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=q_{\Phi_{k}(j)-\Phi_{k}(i)}=q_{(k+j)-(k+i)}=q_{j-i}=d(\langle i,0\rangle,\langle j,1\rangle),

\displaystyle d\bigl(\langle\Psi_{k}(i),\sigma(0)\rangle,\langle\Psi_{k}(j),\sigma(1)\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=q_{\Psi_{k}(i)-\Psi_{k}(j)}=q_{(k-i)-(k-j)}=q_{j-i}=d(\langle i,0\rangle,\langle j,1\rangle).

We consider the decomposition into isosceles-generated components.
Since in
C_{n}
every point has the same distance to both its “neighbours”, the space
C_{n}
is isosceles-generated.
As
D_{n}
retains the distances within the copies of
C_{n}
and as the distances
q_{k}
are singleton, the isosceles-generated components of
D_{n}
are the sets
C_{n}\times\{0\}
and
C_{n}\times\{1\}
. Hence
D_{n}
consists of two isosceles-generated components, and so is uniquely
1
-homogeneous.

We see that
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(D_{n})=\{\Phi_{k}\times\operatorname{id}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\}
, and so the natural map
\rho\colon\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(D_{n})\to\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n})
is not surjective.
Also for
n\geqslant 3
,
\mathbb{Z}_{n}\leqslant\operatorname{Aut}(D_{n})
is not a Boolean group, and so
D_{n}
is not a Boolean metric space.

Remark 5.9
.

The two extreme cases of the decomposition of a
1
-homogeneous space into isosceles-generated components are as follows.
The components are singletons if and only if the space is isosceles-free, and if there is only one component, the space is isosceles-generated.
Note that for a
2
-homogeneous metric space (so both decompositions make sense) the situation is always extreme, see Theorem
5.19
.

Next we consider the question whether for the decomposition into isosceles-generated components the normal subgroup
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)\leqslant\operatorname{Aut}(X)
induces a decomposition of
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
into a semi-direct product.
In the following we show that it is indeed the case, and moreover we endow the set of components
X/{\sim}
with a metric turning it into a homogeneous isosceles-free space.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
X
be a
1
-homogeneous space, and let
X/{\sim}
be its decomposition into isosceles-generated components.
For
r,q\in\operatorname{Dist}(X)
let us put
r\sim q
if there are points
x,x_{r},x_{q}\in X
such that
d(x,x_{r})=r
,
d(x,x_{q})=q
, and
x_{r}\sim x_{q}
.
For every
R\in\operatorname{Dist}(X)/{\sim}
let us pick a distance
q_{R}\in[0,\infty)
such that
q_{0/{\sim}}=0
,
q_{R}\in[a,2a]
for every
R\neq 0/{\sim}
and fixed
a>0
, and such that
R\mapsto q_{R}
is injective.
For all components
C,C^{\prime}\in X/{\sim}
we put
d(C,C^{\prime}):=q_{R}
where
d(x,y)\in R
for any
x\in C
and
y\in C^{\prime}
.

(1)

The relation
\sim
is a well-defined equivalence on
\operatorname{Dist}(X)
, and for every
x,y\in X
we have
x\sim y
if and only if
d(x,y)\sim 0
.

(2)

We can always choose the distances
q_{R}
as described, and the induced map
d
is a well-defined metric on
X/{\sim}
turning it into a homogeneous isosceles-free space.

For every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
let
\tilde{f}
denote the induced bijection on
X/{\sim}
.

(3)

The map
Q\colon f\mapsto\tilde{f}
is a surjective homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})
inducing an isomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(X)/{\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)}\to\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})
.

By a
section
we mean a group homomorphism
S\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
(necessarily an embedding) such that
Q\circ S=\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})}
, i.e. a section selects a representative
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
for every
\tilde{f}\in\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})
in a coherent way.

(4)

For every section
S
we have that
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is the semidirect product
\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)\rtimes\operatorname{im}(S)
.

(5)

For every
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-linear base
B\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})
and every map
\beta\colon B\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
choosing a representative
\beta(f)\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
with
Q(\beta(f))=f
, there is a unique group embedding
S_{\beta}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
extending
\beta
, which is necessarily a section.
Hence,
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\cong\operatorname{Aut}_{*}(X)\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(X/{\sim})
.

Proof.

(1)

The relation
\sim
on
\operatorname{Dist}(X)
is clearly reflexive and symmetric.
To show transitivity, suppose that a triple
\langle x,x_{r},x_{q}\rangle
witnesses
r\sim q
and that
\langle y,y_{q},y_{p}\rangle
witnesses
q\sim p
.
By
1
-homogeneity there is an automorphism
f
such that
f(x)=y
.
Since
d(y,f(x_{q}))=d(x,x_{q})=q=d(y,y_{q})
, we have
f(x_{r})\sim f(x_{q})\sim y_{q}\sim y_{p}
, and so
r\sim p
.

Finally, for every
x,y\in X
, if
x\sim y
, then
\langle x,x,y\rangle
witnesses that
0=d(x,x)\sim d(x,y)
.
On the other hand, if
d(x,y)\sim 0
, then there are
x^{\prime}\sim y^{\prime}\in X
such that
d(x,y)=d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
By
1
-homogeneity there is
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
such that
f(x)=x^{\prime}
.
We have
d(x^{\prime},f(y))=d(x,y)=d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, and so
f(y)\sim y^{\prime}\sim x^{\prime}=f(x)
and
x\sim y
.

(2)

We can always pick the distances
q_{R}
as described since
\lvert\operatorname{Dist}(X/{\sim})\rvert\leqslant\lvert\operatorname{Dist}(X)\rvert\leqslant\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert=\lvert\{0\}\cup[a,2a]\rvert
for any
a>0
.

The map
d
is well-defined since for every
x,x^{\prime}\in C
and
y,y^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}
we have
d(x,y)\sim d(x,y^{\prime})
as witnessed by
\langle x,y,y^{\prime}\rangle
and
d(x,y^{\prime})\sim d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
as witnessed by
\langle y^{\prime},x,x^{\prime}\rangle
.
Clearly,
d
is symmetric, and
d(C,C)=0
for every component
C
since
q_{0/{\sim}}=0
.
Also if
d(C,C^{\prime})=0
, then for any
x\in C
and
y\in C^{\prime}
we have
d(x,y)\sim 0
, and so
x\sim y
by
(1)
and
C=C^{\prime}
.
Finally,
d
trivially satisfies the triangle inequality since
\operatorname{Dist}(X/{\sim})\subseteq\{0\}\cup[a,2a]
.

To show that the space
X/{\sim}
is isosceles-free, suppose
d(C,C^{\prime})=d(C,C^{\prime\prime})>0
.
For any
x\in C
,
x^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}
, and
x^{\prime\prime}\in C^{\prime\prime}
we have
d(x,x^{\prime})\sim d(x,x^{\prime\prime})
, and so there is a witnessing triple
\langle y,y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\rangle
.
By

(4)

This follows from standard group-theoretic facts.

\displaystyle=d(\langle x,1\rangle,\langle y,1\rangle)=d_{X}(x,y),

\displaystyle d(\langle x,0\rangle,\langle y,1\rangle)

(2)

(2)

This is clear.

(3)

This is also clear.

(4)

(2)

6
Maximal number of distances

We have seen that restricting distances of finite metric spaces by a coherent triangle scheme
t\colon R^{2}\to R
(Corollary
4.21
) leads to an isosceles-free Fraïssé limit
X
.
If the set of distances
R
is finite, this forces
X
to be finite as well, despite
X
being the “largest” and “most complicated” structure associated to the given class of finite structures.
In fact, we get
\lvert X\rvert=\lvert R\rvert
.
This is in contrast with the situation when distances are restricted just to a given finite set satisfying the four-values condition, where the Fraïssé limit is clearly infinite.

In this section, instead of limiting the set of distances and asking about the cardinality of the Fraïssé limit, we start with a general homogeneous metric space of a given finite cardinality and ask how homogeneity limits the number of attained distances.
Namely, we consider the following question:

What is the maximal number of different distances attained in a
k
-homogeneous metric space of cardinality
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
?

It turns out that spaces with the highest ratio of the number of attained distances to the number of points are somewhat close to being isosceles-free and that it is useful to view them through the lens of decompositions into isosceles-free and isosceles-generated components, developed in the previous section.

For every metric space
X
let
\delta(X)
denote the number of distinct distance values used in
X
, i.e.
\delta(X):=\lvert\operatorname{Dist}(X)\rvert
.
Moreover let

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}(n)

\displaystyle:=\max\{\delta(X):\text{$X$ a $k$-homogeneous space with $\lvert X\rvert=n$}\},\text{for $k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}$},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\omega}(n)

\displaystyle:=\max\{\delta(X):\text{$X$ an ultrahomogeneous space with $\lvert X\rvert=n$}\}.

Clearly, we have
\Delta_{\omega}(n)\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\Delta_{2}(n)\leqslant\Delta_{1}(n)
for every

.

Example 6.1
.

The circle graph space

Recall that by

we mean the set of singleton distances from Definition

Observation 6.2
.

We have
\delta(X)\leqslant(\lvert S_{X}\rvert+\lvert X\rvert)/2
for every finite

-homogeneous space
X
.
This is because for any

is a surjection where exactly the elements of

have a unique preimage.
Hence,
\lvert X\rvert\geqslant\lvert S_{X}\rvert+2(\delta(X)-\lvert S_{X}\rvert)
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
X
be a finite
1
-homogeneous
n
-point space.
Clearly,
\delta(X)\leqslant n
.
We have
\delta(X)=n
if and only if

is isosceles-free, and in this case

is ultrahomogeneous and

In other words,
\Delta_{\omega}(n)=\Delta_{1}(n)=n
if

\Delta_{1}(n)<n

Proof.

By Observation

, for every

,
X\to\operatorname{Dist}(X)

In this case,

.
A homogeneous isosceles-free space of cardinality

.
∎

).

Proposition 6.11
.

The number of distances in a
1
-homogeneous space
X
of cardinality
n=2(2k+1)
with
2k+1
prime is bounded from above by
3k+2
.
Hence,
\Delta_{1}(n)=\alpha_{n}
for such
n
.

Proof.

Assume
\delta(X)\geqslant 3k+2
. Since
\lvert X\rvert=4k+2
, this means there are at least
2k+2
singleton distances in
X
. Therefore it is possible to pick two non-zero singleton distances
s_{1}>s_{2}>0
and define
f_{1}
as the function which maps each
x\in X
to the unique point of distance
s_{1}
from
x
, and analogously define
f_{2}(x)
by
d(f_{2}(x),x)=s_{2}
for all
x
.

Now, define a sequence starting at an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in X
as:

\displaystyle x_{2i+1}

\displaystyle:=f_{1}(x_{2i}),

\displaystyle x_{2i+2}

\displaystyle:=f_{2}(x_{2i+1}),

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\in\omega.

Clearly, for each
x_{i}
,
f_{j}(x_{i})\neq x_{i}
since they have distance
s_{j}>0
from each other, and for
\ell\neq j
we have
f_{\ell}(f_{j}(x_{i}))\neq x_{i}
since their distances from
f_{j}(x_{i})
are distinct.

So we have a non-trivial sequence
\langle x_{n}\rangle_{n\in\omega}
in the finite space
X
and we want to find the smallest
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that
x_{m}=x_{i}
for some
i<m
. Note that since
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are singleton distances and
d(x_{2i},x_{2i+1})=s_{1}
and
d(x_{2i+2},x_{2i+1})=s_{2}
for all
i
, the first
m
points
x_{i}
form a ‘chain’ where each point in said chain except
x_{0}
and
x_{m}
are already connected to their unique partners of distance
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, respectively. So the only possible way to achieve
x_{m}=x_{i}
for some
i<m
is if
i=0
and thus the sequence is
m
-periodic.

Moreover, changing the starting point to
x_{0}^{\prime}\not\in\{x_{n}\colon n\in\omega\}
will yield a disjoint set
\{x_{n}^{\prime}\colon n\in\omega\}
, again because no point
x_{i}
has more than one point
y
of distance
s_{j}
to
x_{i}
in
X
. Due to
1
-homogeneity, it follows that
\lvert\{x_{n}\colon n\in\omega\}\rvert=m\mid 2(2k+1)=\lvert X\rvert
. Since we have already shown that
m>2
, it follows that
m=2k+1
or
m=4k+2
, however, if
m
were
2k+1
and therefore odd, this would imply that
x_{0}=x_{m}=x_{2k+1}=f_{1}(x_{2k})
, meaning
x_{1}=x_{m-1}
since they both have distance
s_{1}
to
x_{0}
, contradicting the minimality of
m
. It follows that
m=4k+2=\lvert X\rvert
and thus
\{x_{n}:n\in\omega\}=X
.

We can use this observation to find
k
distances which are repeated in
X
. To do this, first notice that each automorphism
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
acts on
X
like a rotation or reflection, i.e. there always exists an
i\in\omega
such that either
g(x_{j})=x_{i+j}
for all

(if
i
is even), or
g(x_{j})=x_{i-j}
(if
i
is odd). This is because
g
preserves distances, so it, in particular, preserves pairs
\{x,f_{j}(x)\}
of distance
s_{j}
. This means
g
commutes with both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.

It follows that if
g
maps
x_{0}
to
x_{i}
, then

\displaystyle g(x_{j})=g(f_{\ell_{j}}\circ\dotsc\circ f_{1}(x_{0}))=f_{\ell_{j}}\circ\dotsb\circ f_{1}(x_{i})=\begin{cases}x_{i+j},&i\text{ even,}\\
x_{i-j},&i\text{ odd.}\end{cases}

This is because for even

,
f_{1}(x_{i})=x_{i+1}
whereas, for odd

, it is
x_{i-1}
, and from that point onwards
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
alternate.

Let
g_{i}\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
be the automorphism satisfying
g_{i}(x_{0})=x_{i}
. Then, for each even
i
, notice that

d(x_{m-i},x_{0})=d(g_{i}(x_{m-i}),g_{i}(x_{0}))=d(x_{0},x_{i})=:r_{i}
, and the function

therefore assumes at most

distinct values on the

points in

with an even index

, and of course at most

distinct values on the remaining

.
∎

Remark 6.14
.

Note that when searching for
\Delta_{1}(n)
where
n
is not a power of two, a space
X
of cardinality
n
cannot be Boolean, and hence by Theorem
5.19
is either isosceles-generated, or a rainbow duplicate of an isosceles-generated space
Y
, and in the latter case
\delta(X)=\delta(Y)+n/2
.
Hence,
\Delta_{1}(n)
ultimately depends on numbers of distances of isosceles-generated spaces.

Remark 6.15
.

Let us note that the question of maximal number of distances in a finite homogeneous metric space can be rephrased in the language of colorings of complete graphs.
Let
X
be a set and let
c\colon X\times X\to C_{X}\ni 0
be a surjective map onto a set
C_{X}
such that

•

c(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
, i.e.
0
is a special color reserved for the diagonal,

•

c(x,y)=c(y,x)
, i.e.
c
is symmetric.

The map
c
can be naturally viewed as a (not necessarily proper) edge coloring of the complete graph on
X
.
Let us call pairs
\langle X,c\rangle

(edge-)colored complete graphs
.

Note that for every metric space
X
, the distance
d\colon X\times X\to\operatorname{Dist}(X)
is a valid coloring, and that the notions of automorphisms and
n
-homogeneity are exactly the same when we view
X
as a colored graph instead of a metric space.

1
-homogeneity of the metric would be more traditionally called
vertex-transitivity
of the induced coloring.
Also note that for every finite colored complete graph
X
we may consider an embedding
e\colon C_{X}\to\{0\}\cup[a,2a]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
e(0)=0
for some
a>0
, and put
d(x,y)=e(c(x,y))
. This defines a metric on
X
inducing an equivalent coloring.
Since we take positive distances in
[a,2a]
, the triangle inequality becomes trivial.
This is what we have done in Theorem
5.10
.

Altogether, the question of maximal number of distances can be reformulated as:
“What is the maximal number of colors used by a vertex-transitive coloring of a complete graph of cardinality
n
