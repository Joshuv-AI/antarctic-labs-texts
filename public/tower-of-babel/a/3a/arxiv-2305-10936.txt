Asymptotic covariances for functionals of weakly stationary random fields
Leonardo Maini

Abstract
. Let ( A x ) x ∈ ℝ d (A_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}} be a measurable, weakly stationary random field, i.e. 𝔼 ⁡ [ A x ] = 𝔼 ⁡ [ A y ] \mathbb{E}[A_{x}]=\mathbb{E}[A_{y}] , Cov ⁡ ( A x , A y ) = K ⁡ ( x − y ) {\rm Cov}(A_{x},A_{y})=K(x-y) , ∀ x , y ∈ ℝ d \forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , with covariance function K : ℝ d → ℝ K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} . Assuming only that the integral covariance function w t := ∫ { | z | ≤ t } K ( z ) d z w_{t}:=\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}K(z)dz is regularly varying (which encompasses the classical assumptions found in the literature), we compute lim t → ∞ Cov ⁡ ( ∫ t ​ D A x ​ 𝑑 x t d / 2 ​ w t 1 / 2 , ∫ t ​ L A y ​ 𝑑 y t d / 2 ​ w t 1 / 2 ) \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}A_{x}dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}A_{y}dy}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right) for D , L ⊆ ℝ d D,L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} belonging to a certain class of compact sets. As an application, we combine this result with existing limit theorems to obtain multi-dimensional limit theorems for non-linear functionals of stationary Gaussian fields, in particular proving new results for the Berry’s random wave model. At the end of the paper, we also show how the problem for A A with a general continuous covariance function K K can be reduced to the same problem for a radial, continuous covariance function K iso K_{\text{iso}} . The novel ideas of this work are mainly based on regularity conditions for (cross) covariograms of Euclidean sets and standard properties of regularly varying functions.

1.2.
Main result

In order to state our main result, we need to introduce some notations, quantities and assumptions.

We denote by
{\rm"Vol"}
the Lebesgue measure and by
{\rm"Per"}
the perimeter in generalized sense in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined for
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
measurable as (see
[
13
]
)

{\rm Per}(D)=\sup\left\{\int_{D}{\rm div}\phi(x)\,dx:\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|{\phi}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\},

where
C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set of continuously differentiable functions with compact support. Note that, denoting by
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial D)
the
(d-1)
-Hausdorff measure of the topological boundary
\partial D
, we have
{\rm Per}(D)\leq\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial D)
, which becomes an equality under additional assumptions on
D
(see
[
13
]
for more details).

Furthermore, for
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
,

\int_{S^{d-1}}f(r\theta)d\theta

is the integral of
\theta\mapsto f(r\theta)
(when defined) with respect to the uniform measure on
S^{d-1}
and
\omega_{d-1}=\int_{S^{d-1}}d\theta
is the surface measure of
S^{d-1}
. (For
d=1
we use the formalism

(11)

\int_{S^{0}}f(r\theta)d\theta=f(r)+f(-r),\quad\quad\omega_{0}=2.\text{)}

For
D,L\in\mathcal{D}
, we consider the
cross covariogram

g_{D,L}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

(12)

g_{D,L}(z)={\rm Vol}(D\cap(L+z)),

which, by Proposition
3
, is such that all its directional derivatives

\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,L}(l\theta),\quad\theta\in S^{d-1},

are well-defined in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and bounded by
{\rm Per}(D)\wedge{\rm Per}(L)
.

Finally, we will always assume to be in one of the following cases:

Case 1.

K
is
radial
and
\mathcal{D}
is the class of all compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
finite perimeter
, i.e. for some
k:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

(13)

K(z)=k(|z|),\quad\mathcal{D}=\{D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}:D\text{ compact, }{\rm Per}(D)<\infty\}.

If
d=1
,
K
is always radial.

Case 2.

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}}
is the class of the
closed balls centered
at a fixed point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, namely

(14)

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-x_{0}|\leq r\right\}:r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\right\}.

Theorem 1

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a collection of compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
A=(A_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a measurable, weakly stationary random field with covariance function
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

and

satisfy the assumption (
13
) or the assumption (
14
), and assume that

in (
1
) is
regularly varying
with index
\alpha\in(-1,d]
, that is

(15)

w_{t}=\ell(t)t^{\alpha}\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

where
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is slowly varying (see Definition

Then, for all
D,L\in\mathcal{D}

(16)

{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}A_{x}dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}A_{x}dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right)\to\frac{1}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dl\left(-\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,L}(l\theta)\right)l^{\alpha}.

Remark 1
.

(Restriction of the parameter
\mathbf{\alpha}
)

Note that in Theorem
1
we consider
\alpha\in(-1,d]
instead of
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. This happens for different reasons:

•

\alpha>-1
is assumed for technical reasons. For example, if
D=L=\{|z|\leq 1\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have for all
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and some constant
c_{d}>0

g_{D,D}(l\theta)=c_{d}\int_{\frac{l}{2}}^{1}\left(1-r^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}dr\,\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,2]}(l),\quad\quad\quad-\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,D}(l\theta)=\frac{c_{d}}{2}\left(1-\left(\frac{l}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,2]}(l),

implying that the RHS of (
16
) is not a finite integral if
\alpha\leq-1
. Anyway, if (
15
) holds with
\alpha\leq-1
, another scaling could yield a different limit.

•

Since
|K(z)|\leq K(0)<\infty
for every

, we have

|w_{t}|\leq\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}|K(z)|dz\leq{\rm const}\,K(0)t^{d},

implying that
w_{t}
can not be regularly varying with index
\alpha>d
.

Remark 2
.

(Comparison to known covariance structures)

Before going on, it is worth discussing analogies and differences with some known covariance structures, highlighting when (
16
) can be seen as their generalization. First of all, note that when
d=1
,
D=[0,s]
and
L=[0,r]
with
r>s>0
, then for

we have

\displaystyle g_{D,L}(l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s-l)\mathbf{1}_{[0,s]}(l)

\displaystyle g_{D,L}(-l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s\mathbf{1}_{[0,r-s]}(l)+(r-l)\mathbf{1}_{[r-s,r]}(l)

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dl}(g_{D,L}(l)+g_{D,L}(-l))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[0,s]}(l)+\mathbf{1}_{[r-s,r]}(l)

and the RHS of (
16
) becomes

(17)

\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{s}l^{\alpha}dl+\int_{r-s}^{r}l^{\alpha}dl\right)=\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}\left(s^{\alpha+1}+r^{\alpha+1}-|r-s|^{\alpha+1}\right).

In (

with Hurst index

. Note that this is also the covariance structure of other (non-Gaussian) stochastic processes, like the Hermite process (see e.g.
[

).

For
\alpha=0
, the RHS of (

(18)

{\rm Vol}(D\cap L),

which is the covariance structure of a Gaussian noise (a set-indexed generalization of the Brownian motion, see e.g.
[

). Note that if

is fixed and

which is, up to a scaling factor, the covariance structure of a Brownian motion (see also (

[

and the discussion in Section

For
\alpha>0
, the RHS of (

(19)

\frac{1}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dl\left(-\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,L}(l\theta)\right)l^{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dlg_{D,L}(l\theta)l^{\alpha-1}=\frac{\alpha}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{D,L}(z)|z|^{\alpha-d}dz,

where the first equality follows integrating by parts and the second passing from polar to standard coordinates. Note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{D,L}(z)|z|^{\alpha-d}dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{D}(x)\mathbf{1}_{L}(x-z)dx\right)|z|^{\alpha-d}dz=\int_{D}\int_{L}|x-y|^{\alpha-d}dxdy.

Thus, (

, which is perfectly consistent with the discussion in Section

.

Finally, note that choosing

and

•

If
\alpha=0

•

If

, then the RHS of (

2.1.
Well-posedness of (
2
) and proof of (
4

The following proposition explains why (
2

4
) holds.

Proposition 2
.

Fix
d\geq 1
and let
A=(A_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a measurable (in the sense that
(\omega,x)\mapsto A_{x}(\omega)
is measurable), weakly stationary random field, with covariance function
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then:

(i)

We have

\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{D}|A_{x}|dx<\infty,\quad\forall\text{ $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ compact}\right)=1

which implies that (
2
) is well defined.

(ii)

For every
D,L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact we have

{\rm Cov}\left(\int_{D}A_{x}dx,\int_{L}A_{y}dy\right)=\int_{D}\int_{L}K(x-y)dxdy,

which implies (
4
).

Proof.

For simplicity, since
A
is weakly stationary, let us use the notation
m:=\mathbb{E}[A_{x}]
,
\forall

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that the function
(\omega,x,y)\mapsto|A_{x}(\omega)-m||A_{y}(\omega)-m|
is measurable because we are assuming that
(\omega,x)\mapsto A_{x}(\omega)
is measurable. Moreover, we have

|A_{x}-m||A_{y}-m|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(|A_{x}-m|^{2}+|A_{y}-m|^{2}\right),

which implies

(20)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{D}\int_{L}\left|A_{x}-m\right||A_{y}-m|dxdy\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\left({\rm Var}(A_{x})+{\rm Var}(A_{y})\right){\rm Vol}(D){\rm Vol}(L)=K(0){\rm Vol}(D){\rm Vol}(L)<\infty.

In particular, for
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{D}|A_{x}-m|dx\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{D}|A_{x}-m|dx\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{D}\int_{D}\left|A_{x}-m\right||A_{y}-m|dxdy\right]^{1/2}<\infty,

implying that for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
the function
x\mapsto A_{x}(\omega)
is integrable on the compact
D
. To prove that for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
the function
x\mapsto A_{x}(\omega)
is integrable on every compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(i.e. the almost sure local integrability stated in (i)), note that as
n\rightarrow\infty

\Omega_{n}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\int_{\{|x|\leq n\}}|A_{x}(\omega)|dx<\infty\right\}\searrow\Omega_{\infty}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\int_{D}|A_{x}(\omega)|dx<\infty,\forall D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ compact}\right\}.

Thus, since
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{n})=1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\infty})=1
and (i) is proved.

Since
(
2
)
is now well defined by (i) and (
20
) holds, by Fubini-Tonelli we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\left(\int_{D}A_{x}dx,\int_{L}A_{y}dy\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{D}\int_{L}(A_{x}-m)(A_{y}-m)dxdy\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\int_{L}{\rm Cov}(A_{x},A_{y})dxdy=\int_{D}\int_{L}K(x-y)dxdy,

for every
D,L
compact domains, which concludes the proof of (ii).
∎

2.2.
Cross covariograms

First of all, let us recall the definition (

g_{D,L}
of two compact sets

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, when
D=L

the

g_{D}(z):=g_{D,D}(z)={\rm Vol}(D\cap(D+z)),\quad\quad\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Moreover, for a bounded set

, recall the definition of diameter

(21)

{\rm diam}(E):=\sup_{x,y\in E}|x-y|<\infty.

Let us now list some properties related to cross covariograms, which are easily derived from the results in
[
13
]
and will be needed in the sequel.

Proposition 3
.

Consider
D,L
compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then we have:

(i)

g_{D,L}(z)=g_{L,D}(-z)
and in particular
g_{D}
is symmetric.

(ii)

For every
z,h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

|g_{D,L}(z+h)-g_{D,L}(z)|\leq 2\min\{g_{D}(0)-g_{D}(h),g_{L}(0)-g_{L}(h)\}.

(iii)

g_{D,L}(z)=0
if
|z|>{\rm diam}(D\cup L)
. In particular, since
{\rm diam}(D\cup L)<\infty
,
g_{D,L}
has compact support.

(iv)

{\rm Per}(D)<\infty
if and only if
g_{D}
is Lipschitz continuous (with Lipschitz constant
{\rm Per}(D)/2
).

(v)

If
{\rm Per}(D)<\infty
or
{\rm Per}(L)<\infty
, then
g_{D,L}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
{\rm Per}(D)\wedge{\rm Per}(L)
. Moreover, all the directional derivatives of
g_{D,L}
exist almost everywhere and are bounded by
{\rm Per}(D)\wedge{\rm Per}(L)
. In addition, for every
\theta\in S^{d-1}
the function
r\rightarrow g_{D,L}(r\theta)
is absolutely continuous and we have

(22)

g_{D,L}(r\theta)=\int_{r}^{{\rm diam}(D\cup L)}\left(-\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,L}(l\theta)\right)dl,\quad\quad\quad\forall r>0.

Proof.

(i) follows from
{\rm Vol}(D\cap(L+z))={\rm Vol}((D-z)\cap L)

and (ii) from

\displaystyle|{\rm Vol}(D\cap(L+z+h))-{\rm Vol}(D\cap(L+z))|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{1}_{L+z}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{L+z+h}(x)|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{1}_{L+z}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{L+z+h}(x)|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{L+z}(x)+\mathbf{1}_{L+z+h}(x)-2\mathbf{1}_{L+z}(x)\mathbf{1}_{L+z+h}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=2(g_{L}(0)-g_{L}(h))

and using (i).
(iii) simply follows by definition, since
g_{D,L}(z)\neq 0
is possible only if
z\in D-L\subseteq(D\cup L)-(D\cup L)
, and (iv) is proved in
[
13
, Theorem 14]
. Regarding (v), the fact that
g_{D,L}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
{\rm Per}(D)\wedge{\rm Per}(L)
easily follows from (iv) and (ii). As a consequence, for every fixed
\theta\in S^{d-1}
the function

l\mapsto g_{D,L}(l\theta)

is Lipschitz, its derivative exists almost everywhere and is bounded by
{\rm Per}(D)\wedge{\rm Per}(L)
. Moreover, the fact that every Lipschitz function is absolutely continuous, together with (iii), implies (
22
).

∎

2.3.
Regularly varying functions

Definition 1
.

A measurable function

In particular, if

slowly varying

Definition
1
is one of the equivalent definitions used for regularly varying functions, as explained in Lemma

(see
[

Lemma 4
.

Consider a measurable function
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

h
is regularly varying.

(ii)

h
is regularly varying with limit
g(l)=l^{\alpha}
, for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

h(l)=\ell(l)l^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
slowly varying.

If (one of) the three statements hold, we say that

is
regularly varying with index

\mathbf{\alpha}
.

The following proposition says that the condition (
15

1
is more general than the classical assumption ”
K(z)=k(|z|)
with

regularly varying with index
\beta\in(-d,0)
”, discussed in Section
1.1

Proposition 5
.

[
6
, Proposition 1.5.11]
If
k:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is regularly varying with index
-\beta\in(-d,0)
and locally bounded on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then

\frac{t^{d}k(t)}{\int_{0}^{t}k(r)r^{d-1}dr}\rightarrow(d-\beta)\quad\quad\text{as }t\rightarrow\infty

and
w_{t}:=\omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{t}k(r)r^{d-1}dr
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=d-\beta
.

A fundamental tool for the proof of Theorem

[

Theorem 6
.

(Potter’s Theorem) If
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is regularly varying with index
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, then for every
A>1
,
\delta>0
,
\exists

X=X(h,A,\delta)>0
such that

|h(lt)/h(t)|\leq A\max\{l^{\alpha+\delta},l^{\alpha-\delta}\}\quad\quad\forall l>\frac{X}{t},\quad\forall t>X.

Remark 3
.

(Justifying equation (
9
))

Note that if
k:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is measurable, bounded and regularly varying with index
-\beta\in(-d,0)
, by Theorem
6
we have for

\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|\geq X/t\}}\mathbf{1}_{D}(x)\mathbf{1}_{L}(y)\frac{k(t|x-y|)}{k(t)}\leq c\,\mathbf{1}_{D}(x)\mathbf{1}_{L}(y)\max\left\{|x-y|^{-\beta-\delta},|x-y|^{-\beta+\delta}\right\}

are suitable constants. Moreover, since

small enough, the function on the RHS is integrable in

. Therefore, by dominated convergence theorem, we have as

\int_{D}\int_{L}\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|\geq X/t\}}\frac{k(t|x-y|)}{k(t)}dxdy\rightarrow\int_{D}\int_{L}|x-y|^{-\beta}dxdy.

This last fact, together with

\int_{D}\int_{L}\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|\leq X/t\}}\frac{k(t|x-y|)}{k(t)}dxdy\leq c\,\frac{1}{k(t)t^{d}}=\frac{1}{\ell(t)t^{d-\beta}}\rightarrow 0,\quad\quad\text{as $t\rightarrow\infty$,}

concludes the proof of (
9

Corollary 7
.

Consider
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

Proof.

First of all, observe that by Potter’s Theorem

and

with

integrable on

Moreover, since

25
]
,
[
27
]
.

Theorem 8
.

(Breuer-Major)
If
C\in L^{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

(33)

t^{-d/2}\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx\overset{\rm law}{\to}N(0,\sigma^{2}{\rm Vol}(D))

where

\sigma^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(z)dz=\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}C(z)^{q}dz\geq 0.

Note that also multi-dimensional (see e.g.
[
27
, Theorem 1.2]
) and stronger type (see e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
) central limit theorems of the form

(34)

\left(t^{-d/2}\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx\right)_{D\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\overset{\rm f.d.d.}{\to}\sigma G=(\sigma G(D))_{D\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}

have been proved in the literature, where
G
is a Gaussian noise, with

{\rm Cov}(G(D),G(L))={\rm Vol}(D\cap L),

and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is a suitable class of compact sets. In particular, considering
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{Du^{1/d},u\in[0,1]\}
with fixed
D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|\leq 1\}
, we have (see
[
16
, Theorem 2.3.1]
)

(35)

\left(t^{-d/2}\int_{tD{u^{1/d}}}\varphi(B_{x})dx\right)_{u\in[0,1]}\overset{\rm f.d.d.}{\to}\sigma{\rm Vol}(D)W=\left(\sigma{\rm Vol}(D)W_{u}\right)_{u\in[0,1]},

where
W
is a standard Brownian motion.

If
\sigma^{2}>0
, then (
15
) holds with
\alpha=0
and Theorem
1
implies

(36)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}\varphi(B_{y})dy}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right)={\rm Vol}(D\cap L),

which is (
32
). Moreover, Theorem
8
(and its multi-dimensional generalizations) ensure Gaussian fluctuations, which are not guaranteed by Theorem
1
. Nevertheless, our Theorem
1
allows to obtain the asymptotic covariances (
36
) in many other situations (see e.g. Example
3
and
4
), whenever (
15
) holds with
\alpha=0
, i.e.
w_{t}
is slowly varying.

Conversely, if
\sigma^{2}=0
, (
32
) and (
33
) only imply that
t^{d/2}
is not the correct normalization if we hope in a non-degenerate limit in distribution (see also the discussion in Section
1.1
). However,
w_{t}
could be regularly varying, allowing us to apply Theorem
1
. This is exactly what happens in the following example.

Example 1
.

(Breuer-Major case,
\mathbf{\sigma^{2}=0}
.)

Fix
d=1
, consider a fractional Brownian motion
(W_{x}^{H})_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
with Hurst index
H\in(0,1/2)
and recall its covariance structure

{\rm Cov}(W_{x}^{H},W_{y}^{H})=\frac{1}{2}\left(|x|^{2H}+|y|^{2H}-|x-y|^{2H}\right).

Furthermore, consider the associated continuous, stationary Gaussian process
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, defined by

B_{x}:=W^{H}_{x+1}-W^{H}_{x}.

Note that we consider the
1
-increment process
B
instead of
W^{H}
because
B
is stationary (hence weakly stationary, and we can apply Theorem
1
), while
W^{H}
is not even weakly stationary (it only has stationary increments).
A standard computation yields that the covariance function
C:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of
B
is given by

C(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(|1+z|^{2H}+|1-z|^{2H}-2|z|^{2H}\right)

and
C\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, because as
|z|\rightarrow\infty
we have (by Taylor expansion)

C(z)\sim 2H(2H-1)|z|^{2H-2},\quad\quad 2H-2\in(-2,-1).

If we choose
\varphi(x)=x
, then
\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx=\int_{tD}B_{x}dx
is Gaussian for every
t>0
. The important fact to note in this example is that

\sigma^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(z)dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}C(z)dz=0,

implying that (
32
) and the Breuer-Major theorem only say that
t^{d/2}=t^{1/2}
is not the correct normalization. Indeed, as
t\rightarrow\infty
we have

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{1,t}=2\int_{0}^{t}C(z)dz=\int_{0}^{t}|1+z|^{2H}+|1-z|^{2H}-2|z|^{2H}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2H+1}\left((t+1)^{2H+1}+(t-1)^{2H+1}-2t^{2H+1}\right)\sim 2Ht^{2H-1},

which means that
w_{t}
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=2H-1\in(-1,0)
. Therefore, applying Theorem
1
we obtain that the correct normalization is
t^{1/2}w_{t}^{1/2}\sim\sqrt{2H}t^{H}
and
\forall D,L\in\mathcal{D}

{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}B_{x}dx}{t^{1/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}B_{y}dy}{t^{1/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right)\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}-\frac{d}{dl}(g_{D,L}(l)+g_{D,L}(-l))l^{2H-1}dl

which, if
D=[0,r]
and
L=[0,s]
, is the covariance function of a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H\in(0,1/2)
(see Remark
2
).

4.2.
The long memory case.

Another frequent assumption is
C(z)=\rho(|z|)
radial with
\rho
regularly varying with index
-\beta\in(-d/R,0)
(see e.g.
[
18
]
for some examples), that is:

\rho(r)=\frac{\ell(r)}{r^{\beta}},\quad\quad r>0,

with
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
slowly varying (see Section
2.3
).
In this case, we say that we are in the long-memory case. As
|z|\rightarrow\infty
, we have

K(z)=k(|z|)=\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}\rho^{q}(|z|)\sim R!a_{R}^{2}\rho^{R}(|z|)=R!a_{R}^{2}\frac{\ell^{R}(|z|)}{|z|^{R\beta}},

implying that
k:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is regularly varying with index
-R\beta\in(-d,0)
. Therefore, reasoning as in (
9
) we obtain

(37)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d}k(t)^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}\varphi(B_{y})dy}{t^{d}k(t)^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{D,L}(z)|z|^{-R\beta}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{L}|x-y|^{-R\beta}dxdy,

and a correct normalization turns out to be

t^{d}k(t)^{1/2}\sim\sqrt{R!\,a_{R}^{2}}\,t^{d-\frac{R\beta}{2}}\ell^{R/2}(t),\quad\quad\text{as }t\rightarrow\infty,

i.e. the one usually observed in the long memory context (see e.g.
[
19
]
).
Note that
C\notin L^{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so we are not in the Breuer-Major case, and

(38)

w_{t}=\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\sim a_{R}^{2}w_{R,t}\quad\quad\text{as $t\rightarrow\infty$},

which by Proposition
5
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=d-R\beta\in(0,d)
. For this reason, we can apply Theorem
1
, obtaining

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}\varphi(B_{y})dy}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right)=\frac{\alpha}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{D}\int_{L}|x-y|^{\alpha-d}dxdy,

which is exactly (
37
) (indeed,
w_{t}\sim\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{\alpha}t^{d}k(t)
by Proposition
5
).

The first limit theorems in the long memory context were proved in the seminal works
[
11
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
33
]
and then extended in many directions. What happens in this case is that (
28
) (suitably normalized by
t^{d}k(t)
) has asymptotically an Hermite distribution of order
R
, which is not Gaussian if
R\geq 2
. Moreover, the result can be extended to multi-dimensional (and stronger type) central limit theorems, see e.g.
[
19
]
.

Summarizing, if
B
has a radial covariance function
C(z)=\rho(|z|)
with
\rho
regularly varying with index
-\beta\in(-d/R,0)
, then we can compute the asymptotic covariances of
B
using (
37
) or Theorem
1
(since
w_{t}
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=d-R\beta
). Moreover, the forementioned limit theorems apply, yielding an Hermite limiting distribution. However, Theorem
1
allows to obtain the asymptotic covariances in many other situations in which (
37
) does not hold, whenever
w_{t}
is regularly varying with index
\alpha>0
and
k
is not regularly varying (see Example
5
).

4.3.
The Berry’s case.

The Berry’s random wave model is a Gaussian field which arises as the local scaling limit of a variety of random fields on two-dimensional manifolds, see
[
10
]
for the details. It was first introduced by Berry in
[
5
]
, and is used in quantum mechanics to model the Laplace eigenfunctions of classically chaotic billiards with large eigenvalue (see also
[
26
]
and the references therein). It is defined as a smooth, stationary, centered Gaussian field
B
on the plane (
d=2
) with radial covariance function

C(z)=\rho(|z|)=J_{0}(|z|),

where
J_{0}
is the Bessel function of the first kind of order
0
(see e.g.
[
17
]
), with series expansion

J_{0}(r)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2j}

and asymptotic behavior

(39)

J_{0}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\cos\left(r-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+O\left({r^{-\frac{3}{2}}}\right)\quad\mbox{as $r\to\infty$}.

Note that
\rho^{q}
is not regularly varying. This means that we can not use Proposition
5
to prove that
w_{q,t}
is regularly varying, as we did in Section
4.2
. Nevertheless, this latter fact is given by the following lemma (for
q\neq 1
) and is a consequence of the results in
[
14
]
.

Remark 4
.

For
q=1
,
w_{1,t}
is not regularly varying. To prove this fact, consider
J_{1}
, the Bessel function of the first kind of order
1
(see e.g.
[
17
]
), with series expansion

J_{1}(r)=\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!(j+1)!}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2j}

and asymptotic behavior

J_{1}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\cos\left(r-\frac{3}{4}\pi\right)+O\left({r^{-\frac{3}{2}}}\right)\quad\mbox{as $r\to\infty$}.

Note that using the series expansions of
J_{0}
and
J_{1}
we have

\frac{d}{dr}\left(J_{1}(r)r\right)=J_{0}(r)r.

Therefore, since

w_{1,t}=2\pi\int_{0}^{t}J_{0}(r)r\,dr=J_{1}(t)t=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,t^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\cos\left(t-\frac{3}{4}\pi\right)+O\left({t^{-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\quad\mbox{as $t\to\infty$},

w_{1,t}
is not a regularly varying function, and Theorem
1
can not be applied to compute the asymptotic covariances of
\int_{tD}H_{1}(B_{x})dx=\int_{tD}B_{x}dx
and
\int_{tL}H_{1}(B_{x})dx=\int_{tL}B_{x}dx
(recall that
H_{1}(x)=x
is the first Hermite polynomial).

Nevertheless, with a different argument, something can be said about the variance of
\int_{tD}B_{x}dx=\int_{tD}H_{1}(B_{x})dx
. Indeed, we have the formula (see e.g.
[
32
]
)

C(z)=J_{0}(|z|)=\frac{1}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}e^{i\langle x,\theta\rangle}d\theta.

and after applying Fubini-Tonelli theorem we obtain

{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}B_{x}dx\right)=\int_{tD}\int_{tD}J_{0}(|x-y|)dxdy=\frac{1}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}|\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{tD}]|^{2}(\theta)d\theta=\frac{t^{2d}}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}|\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}]|^{2}(t\theta)d\theta,

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform.
Therefore, if
|\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}](x)|=o(|x|^{-d/2})
, we have

{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}B_{x}dx\right)=o(t^{d}),\quad\text{as }t\rightarrow\infty.

This fact will be needed in Example
6
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
be the Berry’s random wave model, and recall the notation introduced in the first part of Section
4
. Then for
q\geq 2
, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, we have

w_{q,t}\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}c\,t&&\mbox{if $q=2$}\\
c\log(t)&&\mbox{if $q=4$}\\
c&&\mbox{if $q=3$ or $q\geq 5$}\end{array}\right.,

where
c\in(0,\infty)
depends on
q
, but does not depend on
t
.

Proof.

In the sequel, let
c
be a positive constant which may change value.
For
q=2
even, by (
39
) and the trigonometric identity
2\cos^{2}(x)=1+\cos(2x)
we have

\displaystyle w_{2,t}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{t}J_{0}^{2}(r)r\,dr=c\,\left(\int_{1}^{t}{\cos^{2}(r-\pi/4)}dr+O\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{dr}{r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c\,\left(t-1+\int_{1}^{t}{\cos(2r-\pi/2)}dr+O\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{dr}{r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c\,\left(t+O(\log(t))\right)\sim ct.

For
q=4
, by (
39
) and the trigonometric identity
8\cos^{4}(x)=2(1+\cos(2x))^{2}=2+4\cos(2x)+2\cos^{2}(2x)=2+4\cos(2x)+1+\cos(4x)
, we have

\displaystyle w_{4,t}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{t}J_{0}^{4}(r)r\,dr=c\,\left(\int_{1}^{t}\frac{\cos^{4}(r-\pi/4)}{r}dr+O\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{dr}{r^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c\,\left(3\log(t)+4\int_{1}^{t}{\cos(2r-\pi/2)}dr+\int_{1}^{t}{\cos(4r-\pi)}dr+O\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{dr}{r^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c\,\left(\log(t)+O(1)\right)\sim c\log(t).

If
q\geq 6
is even,
w_{q,t}>0
,
w_{q,t}
increasing in
t
, and
w_{q,t}=O(1)
(see Remark
5
).
Therefore, we have
w_{q,t}\sim c>0
for some positive constant
c>0
.

If
q\geq 3
is odd, the fact that
w_{q,t}
converges to a positive constant is more difficult to see, and is proved in
[
14
]
by means of Pearson’s random walks.
∎

Remark 5
.

Note that since
|C(z)|=|J_{0}(|z|)|\leq 1
and (
39
) holds, we have

\left|J_{0}^{5}(r)\right|\leq c\,\left(1\,\wedge\frac{1}{r^{5/2}}\right),\quad r>0,

where
c
is a positive constant. Moreover, since
|C(z)|=|J_{0}(|z|)|\leq 1
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|C^{q}(z)\right|dz\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|C^{5}(z)\right|dz=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|J_{0}^{5}(r)\right|r\,dr\leq c\,\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^{3/2}}<\infty,\quad\quad q\geq 5.

Therefore, we obtain the uniform bound

(40)

\sum_{q=5}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\leq\|{\varphi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\gamma(dx))}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|C^{5}(x)\right|dx<\infty.

Combining Lemma
9
with Theorem
1
, we are now able to compute the asymptotic covariances

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}\varphi(B_{y})dy}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right),

where
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
is the Berry’s random wave model,
for a large class of functions
\varphi
and compact domains
D,L
. If we further apply
[
21
, Theorem 2]
and the Peccati-Tudor multi-dimensional fourth moment theorem (see
[
28
]
), we are able to prove multi-dimensional central limit theorems for functionals of the Berry’s random wave model. All these facts are explained in the upcoming Examples
2
-
6
. Before we move on to the latter, to ease the exposition, let us state a simplified version of the Peccati-Tudor theorem in our context.

Theorem 10
.

[
28
, Proposition 1]

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a continuous, centered, stationary Gaussian field with covariance function
C
. Let
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
be a class of compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assume that the following three conditions hold:

(i)

{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}H_{q}(B_{x})dx\right)>0
, for every
D\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
and
t
large enough.

(ii)

For every
D\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}

\frac{\int_{tD}H_{q}(B_{x})dx}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}H_{q}(B_{x})dx\right)}}\overset{law}{\rightarrow}N,

where
N\sim N(0,1)
has a standard Gaussian distribution.

(iii)

For every
D,L\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
, we have

{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}H_{q}(B_{x})dx}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}H_{q}(B_{x})dx\right)}},\frac{\int_{tL}H_{q}(B_{y})dy}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tL}H_{q}(B_{y})dy\right)}}\right)\rightarrow b(D,L),

where
b(D,L)
is a constant depending only on
D
and
L
.

Then, as
t\rightarrow\infty
we have the multi-dimensional central limit theorem

\left(\frac{\int_{tD}H_{q}(B_{x})dx}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}H_{q}(B_{x})dx\right)}}\right)_{D\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}}\overset{f.d.d.}{\rightarrow}G=\left(G(D)\right)_{D\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}}

where
G
is a centered, set-indexed Gaussian field with covariances

{\rm Cov}(G(D),G(L))=b(D,L).

in the class

of compact sets with finite perimeter (see (

(45)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}\varphi(B_{y})dy}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{S^{1}}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dl\left(-\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,L}(l\theta)\right)l=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{D}\int_{L}\frac{dxdy}{|x-y|}.

For the last equality, see Remark
2
.
Note that the integral on the RHS of (

) is finite, since

has bounded derivative and compact support if

are compact sets with finite perimeter (see Proposition
3
).

Let us define the following class of compact sets:

\mathcal{D}_{O}:=\left\{D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ compact }:{\rm Vol}(D)>0,|\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}](x)|=O(|x|^{-d/2})\text{ as $|x|\rightarrow\infty$}\right\},

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform. For example,
D\in\mathcal{D}_{O}
when

is compact,

and
\partial D
is smooth with non-vanishing Gaussian curvature, see
[

]
.
By using reduction techniques (see e.g. the proof of
[

, Proposition 4]
) in (
46
) and the spectral CLT
[

, Theorem 2]
, we obtain for every

:

(46)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx\right)}}-sgn(a_{2})\frac{\int_{tD}H_{2}(B_{x})dx}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}H_{2}(B_{x})dx\right)}}\right)^{2}\right]\rightarrow 0

and

(47)

\frac{\int_{tD}H_{2}(B_{x})dx}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}H_{2}(B_{x})dx\right)}}\overset{law}{\rightarrow}N(0,1).

Moreover, combining (

), (
46
), (

) and Theorem

with
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}=\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{O}
, we obtain the multi-dimensional central limit theorem

(48)

\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right)_{D\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{O}}\overset{f.d.d.}{\to}(G^{\prime}(D))_{D\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{O}},

where the set-indexed Gaussian field

has covariances given by (

). Note that the restriction on
\mathcal{D}
in (

can not be applied as in (

.

Example 6
.

(Berry with
R=1
.)

If

is

, where

. Therefore, we can not apply Theorem

to compute the asymptotic covariances.

Let us now assume that
\varphi(x)
is
not linear
, splitting the functional as follows

\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx=a_{1}\int_{tD}H_{1}(B_{x})dx+\int_{tD}\hat{\varphi}(B_{x})dx,

where
\hat{\varphi}:=\varphi-a_{1}H_{1}\neq 0
because
\varphi
is not linear (i.e.
\exists\,q\geq 2
with

). In addition, recall the notation
\mathcal{D}
for the class of compact sets with finite perimeter, and consider

\mathcal{D}_{o}:=\left\{D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ compact }:{\rm Vol}(D)>0,|\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}](x)|=o(|x|^{-d/2})\text{ as $|x|\rightarrow\infty$}\right\}.

For example,
D\in\mathcal{D}_{o}
when

is compact,

and

is smooth with non-vanishing Gaussian curvature, see
[
7
]
.

Then, we can study the asymptotic covariances and multi-dimensional fluctuations of
\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx
, as we did in the previous examples, following two steps:

(i)

We observe that as
t\rightarrow\infty
(note that the equality holds by definition of
\hat{\varphi}
)

(49)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}\left(\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\right)^{1/2}}-\frac{\int_{tD}\hat{\varphi}(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}\left(\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\right)^{1/2}}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{{\rm Var}\left(\int_{tD}a_{1}H_{1}(B_{x})dx\right)}{t^{d}\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}}\rightarrow 0,\quad\quad\forall D\in\mathcal{D}_{o}.

In fact, by Remark
4
we have (note that
t^{d}=O\left(t^{d}\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\right)
by Lemma

, since

with
a_{q}\neq 0
)

{\rm Var}\left(a_{1}\int_{tD}H_{1}(B_{x})dx\right)=o\left(t^{d}\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\right),\quad\quad\forall D\in\mathcal{D}_{o}.

(ii)

Since the asymptotic

-equivalence (
49
) holds, the problem of studying the asymptotic covariances and the multi-dimensional Gaussian fluctuations of
\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx
is reduced to the same problem for
\int_{tD}\hat{\varphi}(B_{x})dx
. Hence, since
\hat{\varphi}
has Hermite rank greater or equal than

, one can solve the problem using the results in the previous examples. For instance, if the Hermite rank of

is
2
, then by Example
5
we have

\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\hat{\varphi}(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}\left(\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\right)^{1/2}}\right)_{D\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{O}}\overset{f.d.d.}{\to}(G^{\prime}(D))_{D\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{O}},

and combining the latter with (
49
) we obtain (note that
\mathcal{D}_{o}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{O}
, so we add an additional restriction on the class of domains in order to use (

)).

\left(\frac{\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx}{t^{d/2}\left(\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}w_{q,t}\right)^{1/2}}\right)_{D\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{o}}\overset{f.d.d.}{\to}(G^{\prime}(D))_{D\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{o}},

where the set-indexed Gaussian field
G^{\prime}=(G^{\prime}(D))_{D\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{D}_{o}}
has covariances given by (
45
).

Remark 6
.

Note that all the arguments above could be generalized to prove analogous multi-dimensional central limit theorems for the
d
-dimensional Berry’s random wave model (and partially extended to a larger class of fields, see
[
21
, Section 6]
). We only focused on the case
d=2
for the sake of brevity.

5.
Non-radial covariance functions.

When
d\geq 2
, the covariance function
K
of the field
A
could be non-radial, but Theorem
1
can still be applied taking
\mathcal{D}
as the set of balls centered at a fixed point, see (
14
).

The goal of this section is showing that the problem (
3
) for non-radial, continuous
K
can be reduced (for
D,L
balls centered at a fixed point) to the same problem for a radial covariance function
K_{\text{iso}}
, which will be defined in the sequel.
This reduction principle will be proved in Proposition
11
.

In order to state Proposition
11
, we need to introduce some additional quantities.

Let
A=(A_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a measurable, weakly stationary random field with
continuous
covariance function
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
K(0)=1
. By Bochner’s theorem, there exists a unique, symmetric, Borel probability measure
G
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

K(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\langle x,\lambda\rangle}G(d\lambda).

G
is called the
spectral measure
of
A
(or associated to
K
).
In other words,
K
is the characteristic function of
\Lambda
, where
\Lambda\sim G
is a random variable with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Note that we can always write

K(z)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\langle z,\Lambda\rangle}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i|\Lambda|\langle z,\hat{\Lambda}\rangle}\right],

where
|\Lambda|\sim\mu
and
\hat{\Lambda}:=\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda\neq 0}\,\Lambda/|\Lambda|\sim\sigma
are respectively the random norm and the random direction of
\Lambda
. We will refer respectively to the probability measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\sigma
on
S^{d-1}
as the
isotropic spectral measure
and
spherical spectral measure
of
A
(or associated to
K
or
G
).

Remark 7
.

Let us denote by
\nu
the uniform probability measure on
S^{d-1}
. Note that
K
is radial if and only if
\sigma=\nu
.
Indeed,
K
is radial, i.e.
K(z)=K(Pz)
for every
P
orthogonal matrix, if and only if
P\Lambda\sim\Lambda
for every
P
orthogonal matrix, that is
\hat{\Lambda}
is uniformly distributed on
S^{d-1}
(i.e.
\sigma=\nu
). In particular, given a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, there is a unique continuous, radial covariance function with isotropic spectral measure
\mu
(and spherical spectral measure
\nu
).

Thanks to Remark
7
, we can now give the crucial definition of this section.

Definition 2
.

Let
A=(A_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a weakly stationary random field with continuous covariance function
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, isotropic spectral measure
\mu
and spherical spectral measure
\sigma
, that is

K(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(dr)\int_{S^{d-1}}\sigma(d\theta)\,e^{i\langle r\theta,z\rangle}.

The
isotropic covariance function
of
A
is the only continuous, radial covariance function
K_{\text{iso}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with isotropic spectral measure
\mu
, namely (see Remark
7
)

K_{\text{iso}}(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(dr)\int_{S^{d-1}}\nu(d\theta)\,e^{i\langle r\theta,z\rangle},

where
\nu(d\theta)=d\theta/\omega_{d-1}
is the uniform probability measure on
S^{d-1}
.

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 11
.

Let
A=(A_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a measurable, weakly stationary random field with continuous covariance function
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and isotropic covariance function
K_{\text{iso}}
. Then we have (recall the definition (
1
) of
w
)

(50)

w_{t}^{\text{iso}}:=\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}K_{\text{iso}}(z)dz=\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}K(z)dz=w_{t},\quad\quad t>0,

and for
D,L
balls centered at the same point

(51)

\int_{D}\int_{L}K(x-y)dxdy=\int_{D}\int_{L}K_{\text{iso}}(x-y)dxdy.

In particular, if
w_{t}=w_{t}^{\text{iso}}
is regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(-1,d]
(see (
15
)), by Theorem
1
we have, for
D,L
balls centered at the same point (see (
14
)), as
t\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}A_{x}dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}A_{x}dx}{t^{d/2}w_{t}^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{tD}\int_{tL}K(x-y)dxdy}{t^{d}w_{t}}

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{tD}\int_{tL}K_{\text{iso}}(x-y)dxdy}{t^{d}w_{t}^{\text{iso}}}\to\frac{1}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dl\left(-\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,L}(l\theta)\right)l^{\alpha}.

Proof.

By Fubini, we have

w_{t}=\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}K(z)dz=\int_{S^{d-1}}\sigma(d\theta)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(dr)\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}e^{i\langle z,r\theta\rangle}=\int_{S^{d-1}}\sigma(d\theta)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(dr)\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{\{|z|\leq t\}}](r\theta),

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform. Since
\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{\{|z|\leq t\}}](r\theta)
is a radial function (i.e. does not depend on
\theta
) and
\sigma,\nu
are probability measures on
S^{d-1}
, we have

\int_{S^{d-1}}\sigma(d\theta)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(dr)\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{\{|z|\leq t\}}](r\theta)=\int_{S^{d-1}}\nu(d\theta)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(dr)\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{\{|z|\leq t\}}](r\theta)=\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}K_{\text{iso}}(z)dz=w_{t}^{\text{iso}},

where the last equality follows again by Fubini. Therefore, (
50
) is proved. As a consequence of (
50
) and (
25
), we also obtain (
51
). Moreover, (
11
) follows by (
50
), (
51
) and a direct application of Theorem
1
.
∎

Proposition
11
allows to reduce the problem (
3
) for
K
non-radial (and
D,L
balls centered at the same point) to the easiest problem (
3
) for
K_{\text{iso}}
radial.

Note that we can also make the argument in the opposite direction: if
K_{\text{iso}}
is a radial covariance function and we can check (
15
) for
K_{\text{iso}}
, then by Proposition
11
we can apply Theorem
1
not only for random fields with covariance function
K_{\text{iso}}
, but for every random field with isotropic covariance function
K_{\text{iso}}
.

To be more concrete about the possible applications of Proposition
11
, we conclude with the following example.

Example 7
.

Fix
d\geq 2
and consider the
non-Gaussian
random field
A=(A_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
given by the rescaled sum of
N
independent random waves with random phases
\phi_{i}
, random directions
\theta_{i}
and random wavenumbers
k_{i}
. Namely, we have

(53)

A_{x}:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{{N}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\cos\left(k_{i}\langle x,\theta_{i}\rangle+\phi_{i}\right),

where
\phi_{1},\theta_{1},k_{1},\dots,\phi_{N},\theta_{N},k_{N}
are all independent and for all
i=1,\dots,N
we have:
\phi_{i}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,2\pi]
;
\theta_{i}\sim\sigma
symmetric probability measure on
S^{d-1}
;
k_{i}\sim\mu
probability measure on
(0,\infty)
.

Note that when
\sigma
is the uniform distribution on
S^{d-1}
and
\mu=\delta_{k}
for some constant
k>0
, random fields of the form (
53
) are called random superposition of independent waves, and have been extensively studied as good local models for wavefunctions (i.e. eigenfunctions of the Laplacian), see e.g.
[
5
,
26
,
29
,
36
]
.

Moreover, note that
A
is
not stationary
, since
A_{x}
and
A_{y}
have not the same distribution in general. For instance, if
N=1
, in general
A_{0}=\cos(\phi_{1})
has not the same distribution of
A_{x}=\cos(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle+\phi_{1})
for every
x\neq 0
.

However,
A
is
weakly stationary
, implying that Theorem
1
can be applied to compute the asymptotic covariances of functionals of
A
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{x}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2N}\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle+\phi_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2N}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\cos(\phi_{1})\right]}_{=0}-\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sin(\phi_{1})\right]}_{=0}\right)=0

and

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}(A_{x},A_{y})=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{x}A_{y}\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle+\phi_{1}\right)\cos\left(k_{1}\langle y,\theta_{1}\rangle+\phi_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\cos\left(k_{1}\langle y,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\cos^{2}(\phi_{1})\right]}_{=1/2}

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\sin\left(k_{1}\langle y,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sin^{2}(\phi_{1})\right]}_{=1/2}

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\sin\left(k_{1}\langle y,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\cos(\phi_{1})\sin(\phi_{1})\right]}_{=0}

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(k_{1}\langle x,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\cos\left(k_{1}\langle y,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sin(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{1})\right]}_{=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(k_{1}\langle x-y,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right].

Therefore, denoting by
K
the covariance function of
A
, by the symmetry of
\theta_{1}
we have

K(z)=\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(k_{1}\langle z,\theta_{1}\rangle\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{ik_{1}\langle z,\theta_{1}\rangle}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{d-1}}e^{i\langle z,r\theta\rangle}(\mu\times\sigma)(dr,d\theta),

implying that the isotropic (resp. spherical) spectral measure of
A
is
\mu
(resp.
\sigma
). By Proposition
11
the integral covariance function
w
defined in (
1
) does not depend on the spherical spectral measure. In other words, whatever is the distribution of the random directions
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N}
fixed in the definition (
53
) of
A
, one can apply Theorem
1
with the isotropic covariance function of
A
, i.e.

K_{\text{iso}}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{d-1}}e^{i\langle z,r\theta\rangle}(\mu\times\nu)(dr,d\theta),

where
\nu
is the uniform probability measure on
S^{d-1}
.
More precisely, whatever is the covariance function
K
, whatever is the spherical spectral measure
\sigma
, if
K_{\text{iso}}
satisfies

w^{\text{iso}}_{t}=\int_{\{|z|\leq t\}}K_{\text{iso}}(z)dz=\ell(t)t^{\alpha},\quad\quad\alpha\in(-1,d],

with
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
slowly varying, we have for
D,L
balls centered at the same point
(see (
14
)), as
t\rightarrow\infty

{\rm Cov}\left(\frac{\int_{tD}A_{x}dx}{t^{d/2}(w^{\text{iso}}_{t})^{1/2}},\frac{\int_{tL}A_{x}dx}{t^{d/2}(w^{\text{iso}}_{t})^{1/2}}\right)\to\frac{1}{\omega_{d-1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}dl\left(-\frac{d}{dl}g_{D,L}(l\theta)\right)l^{\alpha}.
