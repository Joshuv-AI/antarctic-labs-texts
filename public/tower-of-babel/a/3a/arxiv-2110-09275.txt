Double Robust Mass-Imputation with Matching Estimators
Ali Furkan Kalay

Abstract
This paper proposes using a method named Double Score Matching (DSM) to do mass-imputation and presents an application to make inferences with a nonprobability sample. DSM is a k k -Nearest Neighbors algorithm that uses two balance scores instead of covariates to reduce the dimension of the distance metric and thus to achieve a faster convergence rate. DSM mass-imputation and population inference are consistent if one of two balance score models is correctly specified. Simulation results show that the DSM performs better than recently developed double robust estimators when the data generating process has nonlinear confounders. The nonlinearity of the DGP is a major concern because it cannot be tested, and it leads to a violation of the assumptions required to achieve consistency. Even if the consistency of the DSM relies on the two modeling assumptions, it prevents bias from inflating under such cases because DSM is a semiparametric estimator. The confidence intervals are constructed using a wild bootstrapping approach. The proposed bootstrapping method generates valid confidence intervals as long as DSM is consistent.

Introduction

This paper proposes a novel mass-imputation method to integrate two datasets which we label Double Score Matching (DSM). DSM is a consistent estimator as long as one of the two modeling assumptions is correct - this property is known as
double robustness
. Even if the consistency assumptions fail, DSM could prevent bias from inflation under certain scenarios as matching estimators are semiparametric. The paper presents an application of DSM mass-imputation to make inferences with a sample not representative of the population (a nonprobability sample).

Nonprobability samples are increasingly becoming the dominant type of data and becoming available for research purposes such as internet surveys, administrative records, and most types of big data. Various methods are available to make inferences with a nonprobability sample

5
Conclusion

Data integration methods are becoming increasingly popular to enhance the research prospects of new types of datasets. We propose to use DSM to do mass-imputation and consider a specific use of it: Making inference with a nonprobability sample. Our methodology is doubly robust in the sense that as long as one of the two modeling assumptions is correct, mass-imputation is asymptotically unbiased.

Mass-imputation methods create a complete dataset, hence making any statistical analysis is straightforward with the imputed dataset. We also showed that DSM inflates the bias slower than weighting methods when models are misspecified, i.e., the data generating process possesses some degree of nonlinearity unknown to the researcher. Also, DSM is less sensitive to extreme propensity and prognostic scores because matching estimators can only interpolate the data.

While DSM has good robustness properties, it is challenging to work on its asymptotic properties. We provide consistency results and construct the confidence intervals using the residual bootstrapping approach. An essential feature of the proposed bootstrapping method is the low computational burden as it does not requires re-sampling. Our simulations show that wild bootstrapping performs well under various relative sample sizes and the number of matchings.

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}Pr\left\{N_{B}\left(\dfrac{1}{h_{N}(e^{L_{i}(\tilde{\theta})/h_{N}})}\right)^{2}>K\right\}

(A.45)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}Pr\left\{\log N_{B}-2\left(\log h_{N}+L_{i}(\tilde{\theta})/h_{N})\right)>\log K\right\}

(A.46)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}Pr\left\{L_{i}(\tilde{\theta})<\dfrac{\log N_{B}-\log K+6\log N_{B}+\log b_{N_{B}M}}{2N_{B}^{3}b_{N_{B}M}}\right\}

(A.47)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}Pr\left\{D_{i(M+1)}(\theta)-D_{i(M)}(\theta)<\dfrac{-\log K+7\log N_{B}+\log b_{N_{B}M}}{N_{B}^{3}b_{N_{B}M}}\right\}

(A.48)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}b_{N_{B}M}f_{i}(x)\left[F_{i}\left(x+\dfrac{-\log K+7\log N_{B}+\log b_{N_{B}M}}{N_{B}^{3}b_{N_{B}M}}\right)-F_{i}(x)\right]\partial x

(A.49)

Using the Results 1 and 2 along with the mean value theorem as previously,
F_{i}(x+u)=F_{i}(x)+uf_{i}(x^{*})
where
x^{*}\in[x,x+u]
, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}Pr

\displaystyle\left\{N_{B}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\phi^{2}_{h_{N}}(L_{i}(\tilde{\theta}))>K\right\}

(A.50)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}b_{N_{B}M}f_{i}(x)\left[\left(\dfrac{-\log K+7\log N_{B}+\log b_{N_{B}M}}{N_{B}^{3}b_{N_{B}M}}\right)f_{i}(x^{*})\right]\partial x

(A.51)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\dfrac{-\log K+7\log N_{B}+\log b_{N_{B}M}}{N_{B}^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i}^{2}(x)\partial x

(A.52)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\dfrac{-\log K+7\log N_{B}+\log b_{N_{B}M}}{N_{B}^{3}}C

(A.53)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\dfrac{-\log K+5\log N_{B}+\log\left(\dfrac{N_{B}!}{(M-1)!(N_{B}-M-1)!}\right)}{N_{B}^{3}}C

(A.54)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\dfrac{-\log K+7\log N_{B}+\sum_{N=N_{B}-M}^{N_{B}}\log N}{N_{B}^{3}}C

(A.55)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}\dfrac{-\log K+7\log N_{B}+N_{B}\log N_{B}}{N_{B}^{3}}C

(A.56)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N_{B}\to\infty}\dfrac{-N_{B}\log K-N_{B}5\log N_{B}-N_{B}^{2}\log N_{B}}{N_{B}^{3}}C

(A.57)

\displaystyle\leq 0

(A.58)

Thus,

\left|\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\theta}}\mu_{\Phi}(\tilde{\theta};h_{N})\right|=o_{p}(1).

Assuming that convergence rate of
\hat{\theta}
to
\tilde{\theta}
is
\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{N}}
, then it must be:

\mu_{\Phi}(\hat{\theta};h_{N})-\mu_{\Phi}(\tilde{\theta};h_{N})=O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2})

Proof of # 4
: The last step directly follows from lemma
3
:

\mu_{B}(\tilde{\theta})-\mu_{B}=O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2})

.

All steps combined implies that

\mu(\hat{\theta})-\mu=O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2})

which completes the proof.

A.4
Theorem 2

Proof.

Let’s decompose
\mu_{DSM}(\hat{\theta})-\mu
as follows:

(\mu_{DSM}(\hat{\theta})-\mu_{DSM}(\tilde{\theta}))+(\mu_{DSM}(\tilde{\theta})-\mu_{\Psi})+(\mu_{\Psi}-\mu)

The first component consists of the bias terms and error terms associated with matching outcomes.

\displaystyle\mu_{DSM}(\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle-\mu_{DSM}(\tilde{\theta})=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{M\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{j\in J_{M}(i;\hat{\theta})}y_{j}-\dfrac{1}{M\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{j\in J_{M}(i;\tilde{\theta})}y_{j}

(A.59)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{j\in J_{M}(i;\hat{\theta})}y_{j}-\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{j\in J_{M}(i;\tilde{\theta})}y_{j}

(A.60)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{m=1}^{M}y_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}-\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{m=1}^{M}y_{j_{m}(i;\tilde{\theta})}

(A.61)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\left[\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(E[y_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}|{\bm{z}}_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}]-E[y_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}|{\bm{z}}_{j_{m}(i;\tilde{\theta})}]+\epsilon_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}-\epsilon_{j_{m}(i;\tilde{\theta})})\right]

(A.62)

As the error terms are assumed to be uncorrelated with the sample weights, they will converge at rate
O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2})
:

\displaystyle\mu_{DSM}(\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle-\mu_{DSM}(\tilde{\theta})=

(A.63)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\left[\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(E[y_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}|{\bm{z}}_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}]-E[y_{j_{m}(i;\hat{\theta})}|{\bm{z}}_{j_{m}(i;\tilde{\theta})}])\right]+O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2}).

(A.64)

The remaining one is the bias term that is known converging at rate
O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2})
. Hence,

\mu_{DSM}(\hat{\theta})-\mu_{DSM}(\tilde{\theta})=O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2})

(A.65)

The second component consists of the difference between imputed
y_{i}
estimated with
\tilde{\theta}
and known
y_{i}
. The difference between two terms are equal to error terms because their conditional means are equal. It follows from that fact that
\bm{z}_{j_{m}(i;\tilde{\theta})}\to\bm{z}_{i}
, and
E[y_{i}|\bm{z}_{j_{m}(i;\tilde{\theta})}]=E[y_{i}|\bm{z}_{i}]
as
N_{B}\to\infty
(and as
N_{A}\to\infty)
.

\displaystyle\mu_{DSM}(\tilde{\theta})-\mu_{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{j\in J_{M}(i;\tilde{\theta})}y_{j}-\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}y_{i}

(A.66)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{j\in J_{M}(i;\tilde{\theta})}(y_{j}-y_{i})

(A.67)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{M\hat{N}}\sum_{i\in S_{B}}d_{i}\sum_{j\in J_{M}(i;\tilde{\theta})}(\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{i})

(A.68)

\displaystyle=O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2})

(A.69)

The last component
(\mu_{\Psi}-\mu)
is the well-known Hajek Estimator
(
Hajek (1964)
)
. Consequently,

\mu_{DSM}(\hat{\theta})-\mu=O_{p}(N_{B}^{-1/2}).

(A.70)

∎
