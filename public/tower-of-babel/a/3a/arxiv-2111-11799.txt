Non-simple polarised abelian surfaces and genus 3 curves with completely decomposable Jacobians
Robert Auffarth and Paweł Borówka

Abstract
. We study the space of non-simple polarised abelian surfaces. Specifically, we describe for which pairs ( m , n ) (m,n) the locus of polarised abelian surfaces of type ( 1 , d ) (1,d) that contain two complementary elliptic curve of exponents m , n m,n , denoted ℰ d ​ ( m , n ) \mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n) is non-empty. 1.
Introduction

The moduli space of non-simple principally polarised complex abelian varieties is well understood. Indeed, the space of all non-simple principally polarised abelian varieties that contain an abelian subvariety of fixed dimension and exponent is irreducible and can be described as the quotient of a product space of polarised abelian varieties (see for instance
[
Deb88
]
,
[
Auf16
]
,
[
Bor16
]
).
In particular, for principally polarised surfaces, exponents of complementary elliptic curves coincide and the periods of such surfaces have to satisfy so called Humbert singular equations (see for example
[
ALR17
,
Bor16
,
vdG88
]
).

On the other hand, the moduli space of polarised abelian varieties of a given (non-principal) type is much harder to deal with. The main obstacle to extend the results of the principally polarised case is the fact that two complementary abelian subvarieties of a non-principally polarised abelian variety do not necessarily have the same exponent. This inevitably leads to the conclusion that the moduli space of non-simple polarised abelian varieties that contain an abelian subvariety of a fixed dimension is not necessarily irreducible, and it is not clear a priori how to describe the irreducible components.

In
[
vdG88
, Ch. IX]
, G. van der Geer recalls Humbert’s singular equations and states that they can be generalised to non-principally polarised surfaces in
[
vdG88
, Th. IX.2.4]
.
However the result only states that there are many irreducible components of non-simple surfaces but does not give any insight into what the exponents of the elliptic curves are.

In
[
G06
]
, L. Guerra constructs moduli spaces of non-simple abelian varieties and especially their irreducible components. However, due to the fact that the construction is general and not very explicit, the author concludes his paper with questions about whether the constructed spaces are actually non-empty
[
G06
, page 334]
.

In
[
ALR17
]
the authors have found equations for non-simple polarised varieties. However, since they are defined over
\mathbb{Q}
they do not provide any information about irreducible components and possible exponents of subvarieties.

The aim of this paper is to understand the case of non-simple polarised abelian surfaces. Let
\mathcal{A}_{2}(d)
be a moduli of
(1,d)
polarised abelian surfaces. Denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n):=\left\{(A,\mathcal{L})\in\mathcal{A}_{2}(d):\begin{array}[]{l}A\text{ contains a pair of complementary}\\
\text{ elliptic curves of exponents }m,n\end{array}\right\}.

The first main result of the paper can be summarised in the following theorem

Theorem

(see Theorem
4.2
)
.

For
d,m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, the moduli space
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
is a non-empty (2-dimensional) subvariety of
\mathcal{A}_{2}(d)
if and only if

\lcm(m,n)\cdot\gcd(m,n,d)=\gcd(m,n)d.

The idea of the proof is to use certain cyclic isogenies from principally polarised abelian surfaces in order to reduce the problem to working in the principally polarised case. Since we understand the situation for principally polarised surfaces, we need to carefully compute the degrees of the induced restrictions of the isogeny to each elliptic curve. To show the irreducibility (for a square free
d
) we consider modular curves as moduli spaces of elliptic curves with level structure and show that our objects of interest are equivalent under the action of the symplectic group.

However, if there is an odd square that is a divisor of
d
, then we are able to show that there are multiple components of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
. We show the following result.

Theorem

(see Theorem
5.9
)
.

If
p^{2}|d
for some odd
d
, then the locus
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
has at least
\sigma(p)
irreducible components, where
\sigma(p)
is the number of divisors of
p
.

The idea to distinguish irreducible components is to consider the subgroup
E\cap F
and show that they may be parametrised by distinct finite abelian groups.

We conclude this part of the paper with a natural question.

Question

(see Question
5.11
)
.

Does the number of irreducible components of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
equal
\sigma(p)
, where
p
is the maximal odd number satisfying
p^{2}|d
?

The next part of the paper involves finding a “normalised” equation in the Siegel space of periods of abelian varieties, similar to Humbert equations. We prove the following theorem.

Theorem

(see Theorem
6.4
)
.

For any
d,m,n
, we can do the following construction:

•

find coprime
a,b,c
such that
m=\frac{cd}{a},\ n=\frac{cd}{b}
,

•

find
u,v
such that
au-bv=c
,

•

define
x=\frac{du}{b},\ y=\frac{dv}{a}
(they automatically satisfy
xy|d
).

Then
the set of matrices satisfying
z_{3}=(x+y)z_{2}-xyz_{1}
with
\Delta=(\frac{cd}{ab})^{2}
maps into an irreducible component of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
.

As an application of the investigation of non-simple polarised abelian surfaces, we consider completely decomposable Jacobians of genus 3 curves. We show the following theorem.

Theorem

(see Theorem
7.2
)
.

Let
C
be a genus 3 curve such that
JC=E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}
with
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
complementary abelian subvarieties (so each elliptic curve
E_{i}
induces a unique symmetric idempotent
\iota_{i}\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{Q}}(JC)
and
\iota_{1}+\iota_{2}+\iota_{3}=\mathrm{id}
). Then there exist maps
f_{i}:C\rightarrow E_{i},\ i=1,2,3
that satisfy

\lcm(\deg(f_{1}),\deg(f_{2}))=\lcm(\deg(f_{1}),\deg(f_{3}))=\lcm(\deg(f_{2}),\deg(f_{3})).

Moreover, if
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}
satisfy the above equations then there exists a 3–dimensional family of curves
C
with maps
f_{i}
satisfying
\deg(f_{i})=d_{i}
.

The structure of the paper is the following: In Section
2
we go over some preliminaries of abelian surfaces and find important conditions on the exponents of elliptic curves, in Section
3
we prove crucial results on finite abelian groups with symplectic action that are applied in Section
4
to prove the non-emptiness of the moduli spaces we are studying. In Section
5
we show when
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
is irreducible and when the lower bound of components is large and in Section
6
we show some equations for
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
. Finally in Section
7
we apply the results to genus 3 curves.

2.
Preliminaries on abelian surfaces

Let
(A,{\mathcal{L}})
be a complex polarised abelian surface of type
D:=(1,d)
. That is,
{\mathcal{L}}
is an ample line bundle and the map

\varphi_{{\mathcal{L}}}:A\to A^{\vee}=:\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(A)

x\mapsto t_{x}^{*}{\mathcal{L}}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}

is an isogeny with kernel isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
. Moreover let
\mathcal{A}_{2}(d)
denote the space of polarised abelian surfaces of type
(1,d)
. If
d=1
we will simply use the standard notation
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. We wish to study the moduli space

\mathcal{E}_{d}(n):=\{(A,\mathcal{L})\in\mathcal{A}_{2}(d):A\text{ contains an elliptic curve of exponent }n\},

where the exponent of an elliptic curve
E\subseteq A
is by definition the degree of
{\mathcal{L}}|_{E}
, and in particular we want to understand what its irreducible components are.

If
E\subseteq A
is an elliptic curve, then it corresponds to the image of a symmetric idempotent
\epsilon_{E}\in\mathrm{End}(A)\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
(where symmetric means that it is fixed by the Rosati involution given by
{\mathcal{L}}
). We can define the
complementary elliptic curve
of
E
with respect to
{\mathcal{L}}
as

F:=\mathrm{Im}(1-\epsilon_{E}),

where
\epsilon_{F}:=1-\epsilon_{E}
is also a symmetric idempotent. We have that
E\cap F
is finite and
A=E+F
, and therefore the natural addition map

E\times F\to A

is an isogeny.

We define another moduli space

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n):=\left\{(A,\mathcal{L})\in\mathcal{A}_{2}(d):\begin{array}[]{l}A\text{ contains a pair of complementary}\\
\text{ elliptic curves of exponents }m,n\end{array}\right\}.

Note that for a general pair
(m,n)
the space
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
will be empty, and that

\mathcal{E}_{d}(n)=\bigcup_{m}\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n).

Remark 2.1
.

It is a classical result (see
[
BL06
, Cor 12.1.2]
) that if
d=1
then
m=n
. In the non–principally polarised case the complementary polarisation types can be quite unexpected. For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, consider a product
E\times F
with the product polarisation
{\mathcal{O}}_{E}(a)\boxtimes{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(b)
. The kernel of the isogeny associated to the polarisation is
\mathbb{Z}_{a}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{b}^{2}
, and by elementary group theory, this group is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{gcd(a,b)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{lcm(a,b)}
. In particular, the product polarisation is of type
(gcd(a,b),lcm(a,b))
, and so
E\times F
can naturally be seen to belong to the moduli space
\mathcal{A}_{2}\left(d\right)
where
d=lcm(a,b)/gcd(a,b)
. Moreover, with this primitive polarisation
E
has exponent
a/gcd(a,b)
and
F
has exponent
b/gcd(a,b)
, so they are coprime.

Going back to the original problem, we would like to find conditions on the pair
(n,m)
for it to correspond to complementary polarisation types on a polarised abelian surface of type
(1,d)
. In other words, we want to know when
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)\neq\varnothing
. An obvious necessary condition that follows from
[
BL06
, Cor 2.4.6.d]
is that
d|lcm(m,n)
.

Write
A=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda
where
\Lambda
is a lattice in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. If
\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\}
is a symplectic basis for
\Lambda
, then we see that the sublattice given by
\langle d\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\rangle
gives an abelian variety
X
and a cyclic isogeny

f:X\to A

of degree
d
. Moreover, we see that
f^{*}{\mathcal{L}}\equiv d\theta
where
\theta
is a principal polarisation and
\equiv
stands for numerical equivalence. Let

E^{\prime}:=f^{-1}(E)^{0},\hskip 14.22636ptF^{\prime}:=f^{-1}(F)^{0}

be the corresponding elliptic curves on
X
. By
[
ALR17
, Corollary 2.5]
, we have that
E^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}
are complementary elliptic curves on
X
with respect to
d\theta
, and therefore with respect to
\theta
. In particular, since
\theta
is a principal polarisation, we have that
E^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}
have the same exponent which we will denote by
c
.

Define

a:=|E^{\prime}\cap\ker f|=\deg f|_{E^{\prime}},\hskip 14.22636ptb:=|F^{\prime}\cap\ker f|=\deg f|_{F^{\prime}}.

Now, we are ready to show the necessary condition for the exponents
m,n
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A
be
(1,d)
polarised abelian surface that is non-simple. Let
E,F
be a pair of complementary elliptic curves with exponents
n
and
m
, respectively. Then
m
,
n
and
d
satisfy the following equation:

(1)

lcm(m,n)\cdot gcd(m,n,d)=gcd(m,n)d.

Proof.

Let
f:X\to A
be a cyclic isogeny with
ker(f)=<P>
and let
E^{\prime}=f^{-1}(E)^{0},F^{\prime}=f^{-1}(F)^{0}
.
Assume
E^{\prime}
is of exponent
c
in
X
. Then
E^{\prime}\cap F^{\prime}=E^{\prime}[c]=F^{\prime}[c]
.
Let
a=|E^{\prime}\cap ker(f)|
, i.e
\frac{d}{a}P
generates the intersection and let
b=|F^{\prime}\cap ker(f)|
(i.e.
\frac{d}{b}P
generates the intersection).

Then we claim that
(a,b)=(a,c)=(b,c)
.
To show this, assume
(a,b)=q
. Then
\frac{d}{q}P
is a multiple of both
\frac{d}{a}P
and
\frac{d}{b}P
, so
\frac{d}{q}P\in E^{\prime}[q]\cap F^{\prime}[q]
hence
q|c
, so
q|(a,c)
.
On the other hand, if
(a,c)=r
then
\frac{d}{r}P\in ker(f)\cap E^{\prime}[r]
, so
\frac{d}{r}P\in ker(f)\cap F^{\prime}[r]
since
E^{\prime}[c]=F^{\prime}[c]
, and hence
r|b
and
r|(a,b)
. An analogous argument shows that
(a,b)=(b,c)
. This shows that indeed
(a,b)=(a,c)=(b,c)=q
for some
q|d
.

Note that
exp(E)=\frac{cd}{a}
and
exp(F)=\frac{cd}{b}
. Since
gcd(\frac{cd}{a},\frac{cd}{b})=\frac{cdq}{ab}
and
gcd(\frac{cd}{a},\frac{cd}{b},d)=\frac{dq^{2}}{ab}
both sides of Equation
1
equal
\frac{cd^{2}q}{ab}
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

In the previous proof, we have found a possible relation
(a,b)=(a,c)=(b,c)=q
for some
q|d
.
It is worth to note that although
q
may vary, the exponents
m,n
do not change.
This is an indicator that
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
may have multiple irreducible components.

Since our aim is to show that
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
is non-empty, we assume from now on that
q=1
and hence
a,b,c
are pairwise coprime. We will comment on other cases in Section
5
.

2.1.
A number of possible complementary exponents

We define the function

\mu:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}

as follows: If
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
we can write
n=\tilde{n}t
and
d=\tilde{d}t
where
t
is the maximal integer such that
gcd(\tilde{n},t)=gcd(\tilde{d},t)=1
. Then we define

\mu(n,d):=\sigma(t),

where
\sigma
is the function that counts the number of positive divisors of a number.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, then the number of
m\in\mathbb{N}
that satisfy Equation (
1
) is
\mu(n,d)
.

Proof.

Write

n=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}},\hskip 14.22636ptd=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{\beta_{i}},\hskip 14.22636ptm=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\gamma_{i}\geq 0
and
\beta_{i}\leq\max\{\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i}\}
. Now Equation (
1
) turns into

\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{i}+\min\{\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\gamma_{i}\}=2\min\{\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i}\}+\beta_{i}.

If
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
(only when
p\mid t
), we get the equation

\gamma_{i}+\min\{\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i}\}=2\min\{\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i}\},

which is satisfied if and only if
\gamma_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}
. Therefore
\gamma_{i}
can take on
\alpha_{i}+1
possibilities.

If
\alpha_{i}>\beta_{i}
, then we get the equation

\alpha_{i}+\min\{\beta_{i},\gamma_{i}\}=\gamma_{i}+\beta_{i}.

This can only be satisfied if
\gamma_{i}=\alpha_{i}
and so there is one possibility.

If
\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}
, then we get the equation

\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{i}=\min\{\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i}\}+\beta_{i}

which has the unique solution
\gamma_{i}=\beta_{i}
. By counting the total number of possibilities for each
i
, we get the number
\mu(n,d)
.
∎

The aim of the rest of the paper is to show that the restriction of Proposition
2.2
is in fact sufficient for the existence of elliptic curves with the given exponents. Finally, as a consequence Lemma
2.4
computes the number of possible complementary exponents for
\mathcal{E}_{d}(n)
.

3.
Symplectic modules over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}

Now we need some results from the theory of abelian groups. Let
d>1
be a positive integer. Our main object of interest is the group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{4}
equipped with a non-degenerate symplectic form
\omega
over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}
(i.e.
\omega
is bilinear, alternating and
\omega(x,\cdot)=0
if and only if
x=0
). We will use the perspective that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{4}
is a free module over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}
. The results can be stated in a more general setting, but it is not necessary for our purposes.

We start with a useful fact that we will use quite often:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k|d
and let
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{n}
be of order
k
. Then there exists
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{n}
of order
d
such that
x=\frac{d}{k}y
.

Proof.

Let
x
be of order
k
and consider the canonical projection
\pi\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{n}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{n}/\langle x\rangle
. Note that by the fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{n}/\langle x\rangle\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{n-1}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{\frac{d}{k}}
. Now,
\pi^{-1}(0\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{\frac{d}{k}})
is a cyclic group of order
d
that contains
x
. Hence, there exists a generator
y
such that
x=\frac{d}{k}y
.
∎

As usual, for
k\in\mathbb{N},E\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{n}
we denote
kE=\{kx:x\in E\}
and
E[k]=\{x\in E:kx=0\}
. Then, the lemma yields an immediate corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
E\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{4}
and
k\mid d
, then
E[k]=\frac{d}{k}E
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\frac{d}{k}E\subseteq E[k]
is obvious. If
x\in E[k]
is of order
\ell
(and so
\ell\mid k
), then the previous lemma tells us that
x=\frac{d}{\ell}y
for some
y
of order
d
. Now

x=\frac{d}{k}\frac{k}{\ell}y

which completes the proof.
∎

The first result deals with the case of a direct sum.

Lemma 3.3
.

For an integer
d>1
, let
E\cong F\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{2}
with the canonical bases
e_{1},e_{2}
and
f_{1},f_{2}
be equipped with the alternating forms given by
\omega_{E}(e_{1},e_{2})=\omega_{F}(f_{1},f_{2})=1
. Consider
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{4}=E\oplus F
with the direct sum of forms
\omega_{E}+\omega_{F}
. Let
a,b
be coprime divisors of
d
.
Then, there exists a cyclic subgroup
G
of order
d
such that
|G\cap E|=a
and
|G\cap F|=b
. Moreover, all such groups are equivalent under the action of a (symplectic) group
Sp(E,\omega_{E})\times Sp(F,\omega_{F})
.

Proof.

Firstly, consider
P=be_{1}+af_{1}
. Since
e_{1},f_{1}
are independent, for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}
we have
kP=0
if and only if
kb=ka=0\ mod\ d
. Since
a,b
are coprime, then
lcm(\frac{d}{a},\frac{d}{b})=d
, so
d|k
. Hence
P
is of order
d
.
Now, we need to prove that
G=\langle P\rangle
satisfies the assertion. Firstly, note that
kP\in E
if and only if
\frac{d}{a}|k
. Hence, we can write
k=\frac{d}{a}l
, for
l=1,\ldots,a
. There are exactly
a
possible values of
l
and
\frac{ld}{a}e_{1}=0
if and only if
l=a
. This shows
|G\cap E|=a
. Completely analogously, one can show that
|G\cap F|=b
.

Let
H
be another such group. Let
Q=k_{1}e_{1}+k_{2}e_{2}+zf_{1}+tf_{2}
be a generator of
H
. Since
H\cap E
is of order
a
, we know that
\frac{d}{a}z=\frac{d}{a}t=0
. Hence
z=az^{\prime},\ t=at^{\prime}
. for some
z^{\prime},t^{\prime}
. Let
f_{1}^{\prime}=z^{\prime}f_{1}+t^{\prime}f_{2}
. Since
Q
is of order
d
and therefore
zf_{1}+tf_{2}
is of order
\frac{d}{a}
, we get that
f_{1}^{\prime}
is of order
d
. Let
f_{2}^{\prime}\in F
be chosen so that
\omega_{F}(f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime})=1
. Now, for a symplectic basis
e_{1},e_{2},f^{\prime}_{1},f^{\prime}_{2}
we have
Q=k_{1}e_{1}+k_{2}e_{2}+af_{1}^{\prime}
. Analogously, one can find symplectic
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}\in E
such that
Q=be_{1}^{\prime}+af_{1}^{\prime}
. Since both bases are symplectic, the transition matrix belongs to
Sp(E,\omega_{E})\times Sp(F,\omega_{F})
and hence
H
is equivalent to
G
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

For a non-prime integer
d
, having
E\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{2},E\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{4}
does not imply the existence of
F
such that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{4}=E\oplus F
. Therefore, we need a more general and more technical construction that will involve working over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{cd}
, for some
c>1
.

For an integer
d>1
, let
a,b,c
be pairwise coprime integers, such that
a,b
are divisors of
d
and
c>1
. Let
E\cong F\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{cd}^{2}
with the canonical bases
e_{1},e_{2}
and
f_{1},f_{2}
be equipped with the alternating forms given by
\omega_{E}(e_{1},e_{2})=\omega_{F}(f_{1},f_{2})=1
. Consider
{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}^{4}=E\oplus F
with the direct sum of forms
\omega_{E}+\omega_{F}
. Assume, there is an isotropic subgroup
K\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{c}^{2}
such that
K\cap E=K\cap F=\{0\}
and let
X=(E\oplus F)/K
with
\pi:E\oplus F\to X
be the quotient projection. Such group is an example of so called allowable isotropic subgroup in
[
Bor16
]
.

Note that since
K\subset(E\oplus F)[c]
we can write every element of
K
as
dP
and instead of using restricted symplectic form (that may be trivial), we use the induced symplectic form that is given by
\omega_{K}(dP,dQ)=\omega(P,Q)\ mod\ c
for which
K
has to be isotropic, too.

We will start with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
K
satisfying the condition above there exists a symplectic basis
e_{1},e_{2}\in E,f_{1},f_{2}\in F
such that
K=\langle dbe_{1}+daf_{1},dae_{2}-dbf_{2}\rangle
.

Proof.

Firstly, note that
K=\langle dbe_{1}+daf_{1},dae_{2}-dbf_{2}\rangle
satisfies the above properties. Certainly
K\subset(E\oplus F)[c]
. Moreover, since
a,b
are coprime, both generators are of order exactly
c
and they are linearly independent.
K
is obviously isotropic since
ab=ba
. Moreover, since
a,c
(and
b,c
) are coprime, elements
dbe_{1},dae_{2}
generates
E[c]
and hence
K\cap E=\{0\}

Now, consider the multiplication map

are coprime. Moreover, for any generator

Now, let

and

are independent and hence generate

.

Now, let

. Then

is a symplectic basis and

are generators of

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
E^{\prime}=\pi(E\times\{0\}),F^{\prime}=\pi(\{0\}\times F)
. Then
E^{\prime}\cap F^{\prime}=\pi(dE\times\{0\})=\pi(\{0\}\times dF)
.
In particular, we have that
\pi^{-1}(E^{\prime}\cap F^{\prime})=dE+dF
.

Proof.

Firstly, note that for every

and all elements of

are of this form.

Hence, for any

, we have

, so there exist

and

On the other hand if

Note that by comparing the orders, one gets

∎

Let

be the set of

.

Lemma 3.7
.

The set of cyclic groups

On the other hand, if

4.
Non-emptiness of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)

We would like to describe the moduli of non-simple abelian surfaces
\mathcal{E}_{d}
. Our natural starting point is a moduli space of products of elliptic curves with a chosen
d
-torsion point which in the literature are sometimes called abelian surfaces with a root (cf.
[
BL95
,
HW89
]
).

Since a non-simple principally polarised surface is only isogenous to a product, we also use a construction from
[
Bor16
]
that involves so-called allowable isotropic subgroups.

In order to make this precise, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
X(N)
denote the modular curve associated to the congruence subgroup
\Gamma(N):=\ker(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{N}))
, i.e. the moduli space of triples
(E,e_{1},e_{2})
where
e_{1},e_{2}\in E
is a symplectic basis of torsion points of order
N
.

Now let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
be coprime integers with
a|d
,
b|d
and
\tilde{c}=\frac{c}{\gcd(c,d)}
and consider the moduli map

\Phi_{a,b,c}:X(cd)\times X(cd)\to\mathcal{A}_{2}(d)

((E,e_{1},e_{2}),(F,f_{1},f_{2}))\mapsto((E\times F)/\langle\tilde{c}be_{1}+\tilde{c}af_{1},dbe_{1}+daf_{1},dae_{2}-dbf_{2}\rangle),H)

where
H
is the polarisation that pulls back to
cd
times the principal product polarisation on
E\times F
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The map
\Phi_{a,b,c}
is a well-defined morphism and its image lies in
\mathcal{E}_{d}(\frac{cd}{a},\frac{cd}{b})
.

Proof.

We start with
(E\times F,\mathcal{O}_{E}(cd)\boxtimes\mathcal{O}_{F}(cd))
with a
(cd,cd)
product polarisation.
We define
K=\langle dbe_{1}+daf_{1},dae_{2}-dbf_{2}\rangle
and we see that
K
is an allowable isotropic subgroup of the set of
c
-torsion points, so we can define a quotient
\pi_{c}:E\times F\to(E\times F)/K
and by
[
BL06
, Cor 6.3.5]
and Riemann-Roch we can compute that
X=(E\times F)/K
has a
(d,d)
polarisation and the quotient map
\pi_{c}
is polarised. Now if
P=\tilde{c}be_{1}+\tilde{c}af_{1}
then
\pi_{c}(P)\in X[d]
and

is indeed (again by
[
BL06
, Cor 6.3.5]
) a
(1,d)
-polarised abelian surface with the quotient map
\pi_{d}
being a polarised isogeny.

The last assertion follows from the fact that
\pi_{c}|_{E\times\{0\}}
is injective, whereas
\pi_{d}|_{\pi_{c}(E\times\{0\})}
is of order
a
and analogously
(\pi_{d}\circ\pi_{c})|_{\{0\}\times F}
is of order
b
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
m,n,d
be positive integers that satisfy Equation
1
.
Then, one can write
m=\frac{cd}{a},\ n=\frac{cd}{b}
for some
a,b
coprime divisors of
d
and
c
coprime to
a,b
and the image of
\Phi_{a,b,c}
is contained in
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
. This shows that
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
contains an irreducible component of dimension 2, hence it is non-empty.

Proof.

By dividing Equation
1
by
gcd(m,n)
one gets
lcm(m,n)|gcd(m,n)d
, hence by elementary number theory we can write
lcm(m,n)=gcd(m,n)\cdot s
for some
s|d
and so
m=gcd(m,n)\cdot b,\ n=gcd(m,n)\cdot a
for some
a,b
that are coprime and satisfy

. In particular
a,b
are divisors of
s
and hence divisors of
d
. Substituting

we get
gcd(m,n,d)=\frac{d}{ab}
, so
m=\frac{cd}{a},\ n=\frac{cd}{b}
for

coprime to
a,b
.
Moreover, the triple
(a,b,c)
of pairwise coprime numbers is uniquely determined by

and all such triples
(a,b,c)
appear in this way.
Now, Lemma
4.1
shows that
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
is non-empty.∎

The following corollary computes possible complementary exponents if we fix a pair

.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
n,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We can write uniquely

, where

and

. Let

be a divisor of

such that

and

are pairwise coprime. Note that

Then

satisfies Theorem

is non-empty and the number of possible complementary exponents equals

(see Lemma

).

Remark 4.4
.

In extreme cases, we have the following. If

then the only possibility is

and both exponents equal

.
If
(d,n)=1
, then

, so

and we have exponents

.
On the other hand, if

for some

then

and there are as many complementary types as divisors of

(of the form

for

).

Polarised products of elliptic curves

In the case of principally polarised abelian surfaces, the polarised product of two elliptic curves is a distinguished irreducible divisor that is the complement of the locus of Jacobians of smooth genus 2 curves.

We would like to describe the locus of polarised products of two elliptic curves also in the
(1,d)
polarised case.

Corollary
[
BL06
, 5.3.6]
asserts that a polarised abelian surface
A
is a polarised product of two complementary elliptic curves E,F if and only if the addition map
g:E\times F\to A
is a (polarised) isomorphism.
Then, Remark
2.1
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 4.5
.

A
(1,d)
polarised abelian surface
A
is a polarised product of two elliptic curves E,F of exponents
m,n
if and only if
m,n
are coprime and
mn=d
.

Note that every such pair

satisfies Equation
1
.

Now, the loci
\{(E\times F,{\mathcal{O}}_{E}(m)\boxtimes{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(n)):E,F\text{ elliptic curves}\}
are all irreducible components of the locus of products of elliptic curves. The number of such components is equal to
2^{\omega(d)-1}
where
\omega(d)
is the function counting all prime factors of
d
.

Corollary 5.3
.

For a square-free positive integer

, the locus of non-simple polarised abelian surfaces in

is equal to

5.1.
Many components

The reduction lemma does not assert that
h
is cyclic. Now, we would like to provide an explicit example that shows that
\mathcal{E}_{9}(9,9)
has at least two components.
We will use the following notation (that appears in Lemma
6.1
). Let
e_{1},e_{2}
denote canonical basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and
f_{1},f_{2}
be the first and the second column of the period matrix. Then
\omega(e_{1},f_{1})=\omega(e_{2},f_{2})=1
is the alternating form that defines a polarisation.

Example 5.4
.

Let
A_{0,9}={\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Lambda
with the lattice
\Lambda
generated by the columns of

\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}z_{1}&z_{2}&1&0\\
z_{2}&9z_{2}&0&9\end{array}\right]

for

such that the imaginary part of the period matrix is positive definite.

Lemma
6.1
shows that there exist elliptic curves
E_{0},\ E_{9}
that are embedded in

via maps of analytic representations
[1\ 9]
with the primitive lattice vectors
f_{2},e_{1}+9e_{2}
and
[1\ 0]
with the primitive lattice vectors

. In particular, exponents of both curves are equal to 9, so
A_{0,9}\in\mathcal{E}_{9}(9,9)
.
One can also compute that
E_{0}\cap E_{9}=<\frac{1}{9}f_{2}>\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{9}
.

Example 5.5
.

Let
A_{3,6}={\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Lambda
with the lattice
\Lambda
generated by the columns of

\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}z_{1}&z_{2}&1&0\\
z_{2}&9z_{2}-18z_{1}&0&9\end{array}\right]

for
z_{1},z_{2}
such that the imaginary part of the period matrix is positive definite.

Now, we can explicitly compute that there exist elliptic curves
E_{3},\ E_{6}
that are embedded in
A_{3,6}
via maps of analytic representations
[1\ 3]
with the primitive lattice vectors
f_{2}-6f_{1},3e_{1}+9e_{2}
and
[1\ 6]
with the primitive lattice vectors

. In particular, exponents of both curves are equal to 9, so

Note that
E_{3}\cap E_{6}=<3e_{2},\frac{f_{2}}{3}>\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
.

Corollary 5.6
.

Examples
5.4
and
5.5
explicitly show that the locus
\mathcal{E}_{9}(9,9)
has at least two irreducible components.

An explanation of this phenomenon is as follows. Example
5.4
is constructed by taking the product
E\times F
(with the polarisation type
(9,9)
) and dividing by an allowable cyclic subgroup of order

.

On the other hand, in Example
5.4
one takes the product
E\times F
(with the polarisation type
(9,9)
) and divides by an allowable

(3,3)

(9,9)
. Such a choice forces the quotient surface to be
(1,9)
polarised.
One can check that if one wants to find a cyclic isogeny

from a principally polarised

then
X
will contain elliptic curves of exponent 3 and reduction lemma will give you

that will essentially be a quotient by
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
and hence it will never be cyclic in this case.

Now, we would like to generalise the above phenomenon in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let

Let

is a symplectic basis of
X[d]
. Let
g_{1}=2e_{1}+f_{1},g_{2}=e_{2}-f_{2}
. Since
d
is odd, we see that
<g_{1}>\cap F=\{0\}
.
Now, let
G=<kg_{1},\frac{d}{k}g_{2}>
. Since
g_{1},g_{2}
are linearly independent, we get that
G\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{k}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{\frac{d}{k}}
and
G\cap E=G\cap F=\{0\}
. Moreover, since
\omega(g_{1},g_{2})=1\mod d
, we get that
\omega(kg_{1},\frac{d}{k}g_{2})=d
. In particular,
G
is isotropic with respect to
(d,d)
polarisation. Now, consider
(d^{\prime},d^{\prime})
type and denote by
l=\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}>1
. Then
le_{1},le_{2},lf_{1},lf_{2}\in X[d^{\prime}]
is a symplectic basis with
\omega^{\prime}=\frac{1}{l^{2}}\omega
being the symplectic form. Then, either
l\nmid k
and in this case
kg_{1}\notin X[d^{\prime}]
, so
G
is not a subgroup of the kernel of the polarising isogeny or
l\mid k
. In this case
\omega^{\prime}(kg_{1},\frac{d}{k}g_{2})=\frac{d}{l^{2}}=\frac{d^{\prime}}{l}\neq 0\mod d^{\prime}
, hence
G
is not isotropic with respect to
(d^{\prime},d^{\prime})
polarisation type.

The fact that
X/G
is of type
(1,d)
follows from
[
BL06
, Corollary 6.3.5]
. Assuming by contradiction that
X/G
is of type
(l,\frac{d}{l})
for some
l>1
we could have found a polarisation of type
(1,\frac{d}{l^{2}})
on
X/G
and pull it back to
X
to get a polarisation of type
(\frac{d}{l},\frac{d}{l})
for which
G
would have to be isotropic.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

Note that we cannot extend the statement of Lemma
5.7
to
p=2
and
d=4
. It is because if
G\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
satisfies the condition
G\cap E[2]=G\cap F[2]=\{0\}
then you can find a symplectic basis
e_{1},e_{2},f_{1},f_{2}
such that
G=<e_{1}+f_{1},e_{2}+f_{2}>
and hence
G
is always isotropic with respect to
(2,2)
polarisation type. That is why we assume
p_{i}>2
.

Theorem 5.9
.

If
p^{2}|d
for some odd
d
, then the locus
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
has at least
\sigma(p)
irreducible components, where
\sigma(p)
is the number of divisors of
p
.

Proof.

A general surface
A\in\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
contains precisely two elliptic curves
E,F
, so one can consider a (rational) map
A\mapsto E\cap F\in A[d]
that is continuous. In particular, the groups
E\cap F
are abstractly isomorphic for any general abelian surface in the same irreducible component of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
.

On the other hand, one can easily check that in Lemma
5.7
the surfaces
(E\times F)/G
have the property that
E\cap F\simeq G
that are non-isomorphic to each other for various
k|p
.
∎

5.2.
A natural question

We have shown a lower bound for the number of irreducible components of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
.
Let us observe the following fact.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
A
be an abelian surface and
E,F\subset A
elliptic curves. The only possible groups that appear as intersections of
E\cap F
are of the form
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{\prime}}
for
q|q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
f:E\times F{\rightarrow}A
be an addition isogeny. Then
f(E)\cap f(F)\simeq ker(f)
. It is because
f(x,0)=f(0,-y)\iff f(x,y)=0
.

Now, the result follows from the fact that
ker(f)
is an allowable subgroup of
E[k]\times F[k]\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{k}^{2}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{k}^{2}
, hence it cannot be of the form
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{\prime}}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{\prime\prime}}
for
q|q^{\prime},q^{\prime}|q^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Proposition
5.10
shows that the intersection of elliptic curves should be of the form
{\mathbb{Z}}_{k}\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{\frac{d}{k}}
and all such groups can appear. We have shown that if
d
is square free, then there is one possible intersection group and only one irreducible component. Therefore one can ask whether fixing an intersection group gives in fact an irreducible component of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
in general, hence we state the following question.

Question 5.11
.

Does the number of irreducible components of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(d,d)
equal
\sigma(p)
, where
p
is the maximal odd number satisfying
p^{2}|d
?

6.
Equations for
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
for square free
d

By Theorem
5.2
, for a square free
d
, the moduli space
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
is a surface in
\mathcal{A}_{d}
, so one can compute a possible equation for it in the Siegel space in the following way:

Using
[
ALR17
, Prop 4.4]
and
[
K94
, Prop 5.4]
one sees that preimages of
\mathcal{E}_{d}
in

\mathfrak{h}_{2}=\left\{Z=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}z_{1}&z_{2}\\
z_{2}&z_{3}\end{array}\right]:Im(Z)>0\right\}

satisfy singular relations
da_{1}z_{1}+a_{2}z_{2}+a_{3}z_{3}+a_{4}(z_{2}^{2}-z_{1}z_{3})+da_{5}=0
for some integers
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5})
without a common divisor satisfying
\Delta=a_{2}^{2}-4da_{1}a_{3}-4da_{4}a_{5}=p^{2}
for some integer
p
.
Note that
[
vdG88
, Chapter IX]
uses slightly different notation that results in a different formula.

The equations come from the fact that being non-simple is invariant under isogenies. Hence, one can consider a map that takes an extended period matrix (i.e. the matrix whose columns consist of a symplectic basic for the lattice)
[Z\ D]
to
[Z\ id_{2}]
and use the pullback of a differential form to get equations of the desired form.

Before proceeding, we would like to set up a (usual) convention that
lcm(0,t)=0
,
gcd(0,t)=t
and more importantly
\frac{lcm(0,t)}{0}=1
because
=\frac{lcm(0,t)}{0}=\frac{t}{gcd(0,t)}
.

We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Fix
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying
x\neq y
and
d|xy
.
Let
A_{x,y,z_{1},z_{2}}={\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Lambda
with
\Lambda
generated by the columns of

\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}z_{1}&z_{2}&1&0\\
z_{2}&(x+y)z_{2}-xyz_{1}&0&d\end{array}\right]

for
z_{1},z_{2}
such that the imaginary part of the period matrix is positive definite. Then there exist elliptic curves
E_{x},\ E_{y}
that are embedded in
A_{x,y,z_{1},z_{2}}
via maps of analytic representations
[1\ x]
with the primitive lattice vectors
f_{2}-yf_{1},\frac{lcm(d,x)}{x}e_{1}+lcm(d,x)e_{2}
and
[1\ y]
with the primitive lattice vectors
f_{2}-xf_{1},\frac{lcm(d,y)}{y}e_{1}+lcm(d,y)e_{2}
. In particular, the exponents are equal to
|lcm(d,x)-\frac{ylcm(d,x)}{x}|
and
|lcm(d,y)-\frac{xlcm(d,y)}{y}|
.

Proof.

By symmetry, it is only necessary to do the case
E_{y}
. A direct computation shows that
[1\ y]^{t}(z_{2}-xz_{1})=f_{2}-xf_{1}
which is a primitive vector in
\Lambda
. A vector
\frac{lcm(d,y)}{y}e_{1}+lcm(d,y)e_{2}
is indeed primitive in
\Lambda
because the coefficients
\frac{lcm(d,y)}{y},\frac{lcm(d,y)}{d}
are coprime. The exponent is the absolute value of a symplectic form on primitive vectors in the image, hence we get the assertion.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Note that for general
z_{1},z_{2}
, the curves are non-isogenous to each other. In such a case
A
contains only these two elliptic curves, so they have to be complementary to each other. One may also explicitly compute the norm maps and associated idempotents using analytic representations.

If we multiply the exponents of
E_{x},\ E_{y}
by
gcd(d,x)
and
gcd(d,y)
respectively, we get the same number
dx-dy
(here, we assume
x>y
). The following lemma is then crucial to find equations.

Lemma 6.3
.

For any pair
m,n
of complementary exponents there exist
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}
(with
d|xy
) satisfying

\begin{cases}dx-dy=m\cdot gcd(d,x)\\
dx-dy=n\cdot gcd(d,y)\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
m=\frac{cd}{a},\ n=\frac{cd}{b}
and assume
a,b,c
are pairwise coprime.
By Bezout’s identity, there exist
u,v
such that
au-bv=c
. Let
x=\frac{du}{b},\ y=\frac{dv}{a}
. Note that
b,u
and
a,v
have to be coprime, so
gcd(d,x)=\frac{d}{b}
and
gcd(d,y)=\frac{d}{a}
. Therefore
dx-dy=\frac{d^{2}}{ab}(au-bv)=\frac{cd^{2}}{ab}=m\frac{d}{b}=n\frac{d}{a}
and we get the assertion.
∎

To summarize, we have proved the following theorem.

Theorem 6.4
.

For any
d,m,n
, we can do the following construction.

•

find coprime
a,b,c
such that
m=\frac{cd}{a},\ n=\frac{cd}{b}
,

•

find
u,v
such that
au-bv=c
,

•

define
x=\frac{du}{b},\ y=\frac{dv}{a}
(they automatically satisfy
xy|d
).

Then
the set of matrices satisfying
z_{3}=(x+y)z_{2}-xyz_{1}
with
\Delta=(\frac{cd}{ab})^{2}
maps into an irreducible component of
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
.

Remark 6.5
.

If
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
is irreducible then we found one of Humbert surfaces that lies above
\mathcal{E}_{d}(m,n)
. The equation
z_{3}=(x+y)z_{2}-xyz_{1}
can be seen as a normalized one, similarly to the principally polarised case considered in
[
BW03
, Prop 4.5]
or
[
Bor16
, Thm 4.(6)]
.

Moreover, having explicit periods
\tau_{E},\tau_{F}
of elliptic curves
E,\ F
, one can solve a system of linear equations to get explicit
z_{1},z_{2}
and hence an explicit period matrix of a surface
A_{x,y,z_{1},z_{2}}
containing
E
and
F
.

Remark 6.6
.

Note that one can discard the assumption that
(a,b,c)
are pairwise coprime in the construction given by Theorem
6.4
as long as we have
xy|d
. For example, for

, we have taken

and

.

Since, unfortunately, we do not know how many irreducible components there are for a general

.

At the end of this section, we would like to compare our results to the known ones.

Remark 6.7

In

We can answer his questions (see

7.
Application to genus 3 curves

Let us call a curve
multielliptic
if its Jacobian is totally decomposable, i.e it is isogenous to a product of elliptic curves. It is still an open question whether in every genus there exists a multielliptic curve (see for example
[
ES93
]
,
[
PR17
]
).

For genus 2 and 3, since a (very) general principally polarised abelian variety is a Jacobian, there are infinitely many families of multielliptic curves. Assuming we have a multielliptic curve, a natural question is to ask what the degrees of the elliptic coverings are. For genus 2, we know that a general multielliptic curve is a covering of two elliptic curves and the degrees coincide. The locus of multielliptic curves equals the locus of Humbert surfaces of square discriminant not equal to 1.

For genus 3, multielliptic curves appear in some constructions. Examples of curves that are double coverings of three different elliptic curves may be found in
[
HLP00
]
or
[
BO20
]
.

From now on,
C
will be a multielliptic genus 3 curve and
E
will stand for an elliptic curve. In this section we would like to answer the question what triples can be obtained as possible degrees of coverings
f_{i}:C{\rightarrow}E_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
). As usual, we will only consider
indecomposable
coverings, i.e. coverings
f:C{\rightarrow}E
that cannot be decomposed as
f:C{\rightarrow}E^{\prime}{\rightarrow}E
for some other elliptic curve
E^{\prime}
. Moreover, if
E_{i}{\rightarrow}JC
are embeddings then we write
JC=E_{1}\boxplus E_{2}\boxplus E_{3}
if and only if their associated symmetric idempotents satisfy
\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}=1
(i.e.
E_{1},e_{2},E_{3}
are complementary).

Lemma 7.1
.

A map
f:C{\rightarrow}E
is indecomposable if and only if
f^{*}:E{\rightarrow}JC
is an embedding. In such a case we have
deg(f)=exp(f^{*}E)
.

Proof.

It is a straightforward application of
[
BL06
, Proposition 11.4.3]
using the Hurwitz formula.
∎

The following theorem is a direct corollary of Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
C
be a multielliptic curve such that
JC=E_{1}\boxplus E_{2}\boxplus E_{3}
. Let
f_{i}:C{\rightarrow}E_{i}
be respective coverings with degrees
d_{i}
. Then the
d_{i}
satisfy Equation
1
(see Proposition
2.2
).

Moreover, for every triple
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}
satisfying Equation
1
(with the assumption that
d_{i}>1
), there exists a multielliptic genus 3 curve with elliptic curves of exponents
d_{i}
’s.

Proof.

For the first part, it is enough to note that
E_{2}\boxplus E_{3}
is the complementary surface to
E_{1}
and therefore it is of type
(1,d_{1})
.

For the second part, it is enough to construct such a threefold. If we fix
d_{1}
, there exists a
(1,d_{1})
polarised surface with
E_{2},E_{3}
of exponents
d_{2},d_{3}
. Then one uses 3rd remark from page 1548 of
[
Bor16
]
to construct a principally polarised threefold having
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
as complementary elliptic curves with exponents
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}
.
Since
d_{i}>1
, the threefold is not a polarised product of these curves, hence by
[
BL06
, Cor 11.8.2]
it is a Jacobian of a smooth genus 3 curve.
∎
