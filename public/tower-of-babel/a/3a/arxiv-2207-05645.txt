Speed limits on correlations in bipartite quantum systems
Vivek Pandey, Divyansh Shrimali, Brij Mohan, Siddhartha Das, Arun Kumar Pati

Abstract
Quantum speed limit is bound on the minimum time a quantum system requires to evolve from an initial state to final state under a given dynamical process. It sheds light on how fast a desired state transformation can take place which is pertinent for design and control of quantum technologies. In this paper, we derive speed limits on correlations such as entanglement, Bell-CHSH correlation, and quantum mutual information of quantum systems evolving under dynamical processes. Our main result is speed limit on an entanglement monotone called negativity which holds for arbitrary dimensional bipartite quantum systems and processes. Another entanglement monotone which we consider is the concurrence. To illustrate efficacy of our speed limits, we analytically and numerically compute the speed limits on the negativity, concurrence, and Bell-CHSH correlation for various quantum processes of practical interest. We are able to show that for practical examples we have considered, some of the speed limits we derived are actually attainable and hence these bounds can be considered to be tight.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we briefly review some of the standard notations and results common in the literature of quantum information theory.

Let
\mathcal{H}
represents a separable Hilbert space with
\dim(\mathcal{H})
that can be either finite (
<\infty
) or infinite (
=\infty
). Let
\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
denote the algebra of bounded linear operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
with
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
denoting the identity operator. State of a quantum system is described by a density operator defined on the Hilbert space. Let
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of density operators acting on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. If
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
then it satisfies following properties:
\rho=\rho^{\dagger},\,\rho\geq 0,\,\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
denote the Hilbert space associated with a quantum system
A
. The state of quantum system
A
is denoted by density operator
\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{H}}_{A})
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{AB}:=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
the Hilbert space of a bipartite system
AB
. The state of bipartite system
AB
is denoted by density operator
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{AB})
and the density matrix of a subsystem, say
A
, which is given by the partial trace over the other subsystem
B
, i.e.,
\rho_{A}=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho_{AB})
. A quantum system whose state is exactly known is said to be in pure state. The density operator representing a pure state
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
is a rank-one projection operator i.e.
\psi=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, where
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
. Otherwise,
\rho
is in a mixed state and can be written as
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\psi_{i}
, where each
\psi_{i}
is a pure density matrix with
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
and
p_{i}>0
for all
i
.

The von-Neumann entropy of a quantum state
\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
is defined as:

S(A)_{\rho}:=S(\rho_{A})=-\operatorname{tr}({\rho\ln{\rho}}).

(1)

The von-Neumann entropy of a state
\rho_{A}
is always non-negative and
S(A)_{\rho}=0
if and only if the state
\rho_{A}
is pure.

The quantum relative entropy between any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
and
\sigma\geq 0
is defined as
Umegaki 1962

D(\rho\|\sigma):=\begin{cases}\operatorname{tr}(\rho(\ln{\rho}-\ln{\sigma}))&\text{if}\ \operatorname{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\sigma),\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2)

where
\operatorname{supp}(\rho)
and
\operatorname{supp}(\sigma)
are the supports of
\rho
and
\sigma
, respectively.

The Schatten-p norm of an operator
O\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is defined as:

\norm{O}_{p}=\left(\operatorname{tr}\left|O\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p},

(3)

where
\left|O\right|=\sqrt{O^{\dagger}O}
,
p\geq 1,\ p\in\mathbb{R}
. The operator norm, the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, and the trace norm corresponds to
p=\infty,2,1
respectively and satisfy the inequality
\norm{A}_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\norm{A}_{\mathrm{2}}\leq\norm{A}_{\mathrm{1}}
.

II.1
Quantum dynamics

In the Schrödinger picture, the observables of the system are fixed and density operator evolves with time
Sudarshan et al. 1961
. The evolution of the density operator is governed by a linear, completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map called quantum channel. Although quantum channels provide a general way to describe the evolution of density operators for pure and mixed states both but sometimes it is convenient to use differential equations to describe the evolution instead of quantum channels. For example, evolution of pure states can be described by unitary transformations which can easily be written in the form of differential equation (Schrödinger equation). Under the assumption that evolution of density operator is smooth, the evolution of density operator can be written in the form of linear differential equation (called master equation):

\dot{\rho_{t}}:=\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\rho_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\rho_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\rho_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\rho_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}=\mathcal{L}_{t}(\rho_{t}),

(4)

where
\rho_{t}
is the state of the system at time
t
and
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is the Liouvillian super-operator
Rivas and Huelga 2012
which in general can be time independent or time-dependent.

Let us define initial and final states of time evolving quantum system as
\rho_{0}:=\rho_{t=0}
and
\rho_{T}:=\rho_{t=T}
, respectively. We can drop the suffix
t
if
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is time independent and in this case the above evolution equation has the following formal solution:

\rho_{t}=\operatorname{e}^{\mathcal{L}t}(\rho_{0}).

(5)

Let
\Phi
denote a linear CPTP map which maps density operator to density operators. The map
\Phi
is called unital if
\Phi(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}})=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
. The adjoint map
\Phi^{\dagger}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
of
\Phi
is a unique linear map that satisfies
\operatorname{tr}({\mathcal{O}\Phi(\rho)})=\operatorname{tr}({\Phi^{\dagger}}(\mathcal{O})\rho)
,
\forall\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}),\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The adjoint of a trace preserving map is unital.

In the Heisenberg picture the density operator of the system is fixed and the observable evolves with time
Sudarshan et al. 1961
. The evolution is given by the adjoint (or dual) map
{\Phi^{{\dagger}}}
which keeps density operator fixed and takes an input observable to an output observable. Note that
{\Phi}^{{\dagger}}
may not be a quantum channel because in the case of observables, trace preserving condition is not necessary. In differential equation form the time evolution of an observable is given by adjoint-master equation:

\dot{\mathcal{O}}_{t}:=\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{O}_{t}),

(6)

where
\mathcal{O}_{t}
is the observable of the system at time
t
and
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{t}
is adjoint of the Liouvillian super-operator. We define initial and final observable of time evolving quantum system as
\mathcal{O}_{0}:=\mathcal{O}_{t=0}
and
\mathcal{O}_{T}:=\mathcal{O}_{t=T}
, respectively.

II.2
Correlations in bipartite quantum systems

Quantum entanglement is a type of (non-classical) correlation found in bipartite and multipartite quantum systems. The concurrence
Wootters 1998
;
Wootters 2001
, the negativity
Peres 1996
;
Vidal and Werner 2002
and the entanglement entropy
Bennett et al. 1996a
are some of the widely discussed entanglement monotones. In the sequel, we define some other correlation quantifiers that we will be using for our purpose.

Concurrence.—
The concurrence quantifies the entanglement present in a two-qubit quantum system. The square of concurrence of a two-qubit pure state
\psi_{AB}=\outerproduct{\psi_{AB}}{\psi_{AB}}
is given as
Wootters 1998
;
Wootters 2001
;
Rungta et al. 2001

\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{AB}):={{\operatorname{tr}(\psi\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}}))}},

(7)

where,
\mathcal{R}(\psi):=(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y})\psi(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y})
,
\sigma_{y}
is the Pauli operator and
∗
is the complex conjugation operation. The value of concurrence lies between 0 to 1 (i.e.,
0\leq\mathscr{C}(\psi)\leq 1
). For maximally entangled states (i.e., Bell states)
\mathscr{C}(\psi)=1
, while for product states
\mathscr{C}(\psi)=0
.

The square of I-concurrence for a pure two-qudit state
\psi_{AB}=\outerproduct{\psi_{AB}}{\psi_{AB}}
is given as
Rungta et al. 2001

\mathscr{C}_{I}^{2}(\psi_{AB}):=2\nu_{d_{A}}\nu_{d_{B}}[1-\operatorname{tr}(\rho^{2}_{A})],

(8)

where
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=d_{A}
,
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d_{B}
, and
\nu_{d_{A}},\nu_{d_{B}}
are constants. The I-concurrence reduces to concurrence for
\nu_{d_{A}}=1
, and
\nu_{d_{B}}=1
. The I-concurrence varies from 0 for pure product state to
\sqrt{2(d-1)/d}
, where
d=\min\{\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})\}
, for a maximally entangled state.

Negativity.—
The negativity is an entanglement monotone to quantify the amount of entanglement present in an arbitrary bipartite quantum state. It is derived from the positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion for the separability of a bipartite quantum states. For an arbitrary bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
, its negativity is defined as
Vidal and Werner 2002

\mathscr{N}(\rho):=\frac{\norm{\rho_{AB}^{\Gamma_B}}_{1}-1}{2},

(9)

where
\rho_{AB}^{\Gamma_{B}}=\Gamma^{B}(\rho_{AB})
., and
{\Gamma_{B}}
is partial transpose operation (see Appendix
A
).

Note that, the negativity can be zero even when the state is entangled because the PPT criterion is only necessary but not sufficient for bipartite state to be separable. However, for two-qubit states (and also for bipartite state where one system is qutrit while the other is qubit), the set of PPT states and separable states coincide (are same). The negativity is a convex function i.e,
\mathscr{N}(\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{i})\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathscr{N}(\rho_{i})
, where
\{p_{i}\}_{i}
is probability distribution (hence,
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
and
p_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
) and
\rho_{i}
’s are density operators. All states that are not PPT are called NPT states. NPT states are entangled. Therefore, the non-zero negativity implies that the given bipartite state is entangled.

Entanglement entropy.—-

The entanglement entropy is an entanglement monotone and quantifies the amount of entanglement present between subsystems
A
and
B
when the joint state of
AB
is pure, i.e.,
S(AB)_{\psi}=0
. The entanglement entropy of composite system
AB
in a pure state
\psi_{AB}
is defined as
Bennett et al. 1996a

E_{S}(\psi_{AB}):=S(\rho_{A})=S(\rho_{B})=-\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{A}\ln\rho_{A}),

(10)

where
\rho_{A}:=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{B}}(\psi_{AB})
.

Bell-CHSH observable.—
The non-classical correlation existing in a bipartite quantum state is detected by the violation of Bell-CHSH inequality. These correlations appear to be stronger than anything explained by classical physics. The violation of Bell-CHSH inequality for a given bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
can be checked by estimating the expectation value of the Bell observable
\mathcal{B}
. For a two-qubit systems, which may be represented as a pair of spin-1/2 particles, the Bell-CHSH observable has the following
general form
Clauser et al. 1969
;
Horodecki et al. 1995

\mathcal{B}:=\hat{a}.\vec{\sigma}\otimes\left(\hat{b}+\hat{b^{\prime}}\right).\vec{\sigma}+\hat{a^{\prime}}.\vec{\sigma}\otimes\left(\hat{b}-\hat{b^{\prime}}\right).\vec{\sigma},

(11)

where
\hat{a}
,
\hat{a^{\prime}}
,
\hat{b}
and
\hat{b^{\prime}}
are unit vectors in a
3
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\vec{\sigma}
is the Pauli spin vector operator. The state
\rho_{AB}
violates Bell-CHSH inequality for
\absolutevalue{\langle\cal{B}\rangle_\rho}>2
. The optimal Bell measurement settings for Bell observable for any given bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
has been derived in Ref.
Horodecki et al. 1995
. Any state that violates the Bell-CHSH inequality is said to be nonlocal as no local-realistic hidden variable models can depict such correlations.

Quantum mutual information.—
The correlation present in bipartite quantum system can be quantified by quantum mutual information. The quantum mutual information of a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
is defined as

{I}(A;B)_{\rho}:=S({A})_{\rho}+S({B})_{\rho}-S({AB})_{\rho},

(12)

where
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}
are reduced density matrices of
\rho_{AB}
. The quantum mutual information can also be stated in
terms of the quantum relative entropy as

\displaystyle{I}(A;B)_{\rho}:=\min_{\omega_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\omega_{B}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{B})}D(\rho_{AB}||\omega_{A}\otimes\omega_{B}).

(13)

The quantum mutual information
I(A;B)
of any bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
is a non-negative quantity i.e.,
I(A;B)\geq 0
(where equality holds if and only if the state is factorize i.e., a product state) and upper bounded by
2\ln\left({\mathrm{m}in}\{\mathrm{d}im(\mathcal{H}_{A}),dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})\}\right)
.

II.3
Speed limits on observable

The speed limit on observables are defined as a bound on the maximum evolution speed of the expectation value of a given observable of a quantum system undergoing dynamical evolution, which might be unitary or non-unitary. It sets the lower bound on the evolution time of the quantum system needed to evolve between different expectation values of a given observable. The bound on evolution time of expectation value of an observable for an arbitrary dynamics reads as (see Appendix
C
)

T\geq T_{\mathrm{O}QSL}=\frac{\left|\langle\mathcal{O}_{T}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle\mathcal{O}_{0}\rangle_{\rho}\right|}{\norm{\rho}_{1}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{\infty}},\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{1}},\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{2}}\right\},

(14)

where
\Lambda_{T}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\norm{\cal{L}_{t}^{\dagger}(\cal{O}_{t})}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\{1,2,\infty\}
is the evolution speed of the observable of the given system under the dynamics
\dot{\mathcal{O}}_{t}:=\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{O}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{O}_{t})
.

Note that speed limits on observables have been previously derived in Refs.
Mohan and Pati 2022
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022
using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Our derivation employs the Hölder’s inequality, and therefore, our bound is more general than the previous ones.
In Refs.
Mohan and Pati 2022
;
Carabba et al. 2022
, state-independent speed limits on observables are derived by considering the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product for observables. Furthermore, Ref.
Hörnedal et al. 2022
formulates the speed limit of super-operators, which are operators in the operator space, and showed some applications in many-body physics.

III
Quantum Speed Limits

In general, quantum speed limits represent fundamental constraints imposed by the quantum theory on the evolution speed of quantum systems. Entangling abilities of quantum interactions are of wide interest from both fundamental and applied aspects, see e.g.,
Das 2018
;
Das et al. 2021
. Quantum correlations are fundamental in the quantum information theory as they act as resources for several quantum information processing tasks. For instances, entanglement and nonlocal quantum correlations are useful properties (resources) for the tasks of teleportation, quantum key distribution, quantum communication, quantum sensing, etc.
Bennett et al. 1993
;
Ekert 1991
;
Colbeck and Renner 2012
;
Dowling 2008
;
Das et al. 2021
.

In this section, we discuss limitations on the minimal time taken for changes in some desirable correlation measures of bipartite quantum systems undergoing bipartite dynamical processes. Our main focuses are speed limits on the negativity and concurrence, which are entanglement monotones useful in the resource theories of entanglement, see e.g.,
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Bäuml et al. 2018
;
Bäuml et al. 2019
. We also inspect speed limits on other correlations, namely Bell-CHSH observable
\mathcal{B}
and quantum mutual information
{I}(A;B)_{\rho}
.

We note that speed limits on entanglement for unitary dynamics has been studied earlier using geometric measure of entanglement
Bera et al. 2013
;
Rudnicki 2021
. In Refs.
Campaioli et al. 2022
, speed limits on entanglement has been obtained using divergence based measure for open quantum dynamics. The bounds obtained in Refs.
Bera et al. 2013
;
Rudnicki 2021
;
Campaioli et al. 2022
;
Paulson and Banerjee 2022
are challenging to calculate in general as it requires optimization over all separable sates. Here, we have obtained speed limits on entanglement using the negativity and the concurrence, which is arguably easier to calculate. Moreover, speed limits on entanglement obtained using the negativity is applicable for arbitrary dynamics, i.e., for both closed and open dynamical processes. Speed limits obtained for negativity also provide limitations on the minimal time required for transitions of a PPT state to NPT (non-negative under partial transposition) and NPT state to PPT under any given dynamical processes.

III.1
Speed limit on the negativity

We now discuss the first main result of this work that provides a lower bound
T_{\mathrm{N}SL}
on the time taken for the change in the value of negativity of a bipartite quantum system evolving under a given dynamical processes.

Theorem 1
.

Consider any bipartite quantum system
AB
, where each of
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})
can be either finite or infinite. The minimal time
T
taken for the bipartite system to bring certain amount of change in its negativity by evolving under an arbitrary given quantum dynamical process with associated Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is lower bounded by

T\geq T_{\mathrm{N}SL}=\frac{2\absolutevalue{\mathscr{N}(\rho_{T})-\mathscr{N}(\rho_{0})}}{\Lambda^{N}_{T}},

(15)

where
\rho_{0}
is the initial state (at
t=0
),
\rho_{T}
is the final state (at
t=T
), and
\Lambda^{N}_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\norm{\cal{L}_t(\rho^{T_{B}})}_{1}
.
\Lambda^{N}_{T}
can be interpreted as the evolution speed of the negativity of the given system and process.

Proof.

Consider the evolution of bipartite state
\rho
in the time interval interval
\mathscr{I}:=[0,T]
.
The negativity of time evolved bipartite state
\rho_{t}
given by

\mathscr{N}(\rho_{t})=\frac{\norm{\rho^{T_{B}}_t}_{1}-1}{2}.

(16)

Now consider the
\epsilon
neighbourhood of
t\in\mathscr{I}
(i.e. an interval
(t-\epsilon,t+\epsilon)
where
\epsilon
is a number arbitrarily close to zero), and the following difference

\mathscr{N}(\rho_{t+\epsilon})-\mathscr{N}(\rho_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t+\epsilon}\right|-\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|\right).

(17)

We assume that evolution of density operator
\rho
is smooth, i.e.,
\rho_{t}
is differentiable at each
t\in\mathscr{I}
, which also implies that
\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_{t}
and
\operatorname{tr}\left|\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|
are also well defined at each
t\in\mathscr{I}
. We further assume that negativity is differentiable in
\mathscr{I}
, so the left hand derivative and the right hand derivative of negativity must be equal at each point in
\mathscr{I}
and also equal to the derivative of negativity. Now, multiplying by
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
on both the sides of above equation and taking limit
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{N}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t+\epsilon}\right|-\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|}{\epsilon}.

(18)

Using the Taylor expansion we have:

\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t+\epsilon}\right|=\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t}+\epsilon\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_{t}+o(\epsilon^{2})\right|,

(19)

Let us now take the absolute value on both the sides of Eq. (
18
) and use Eq. (
19
) to calculate the limit, we then get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{N}(\rho_{t})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t}+\epsilon\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_{t}+o(\epsilon^{2})\right|-\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|}{\epsilon}\right|.

(20)

Now, we leave the terms of
o(\epsilon^{2})
in the Taylor expansion of
\rho^{T^{B}}_{t+\epsilon}
and use the triangular inequality
\operatorname{tr}\left|A+B\right|\leq\operatorname{tr}\left|A\right|+\operatorname{tr}\left|B\right|
to further simplify above equation. We then obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{N}(\rho_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|+\epsilon\operatorname{tr}\left|\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|-\operatorname{tr}\left|\rho^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|}{\epsilon}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left({\left|\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_{t}\right|}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\norm{\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_t}_{1}.

(21)

The above inequality (
21
) is the upper bound on that the rate of change of the negativity of the quantum system evolving under given dynamics. After integrating the above equation with respect to time
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{N}(\rho_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\norm{\dot{\rho}^{T_{B}}_t}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\norm{\cal{L}_t(\rho^{T_{B}})}_{1}.

(22)

From the above inequality, we get the desired bound:

T\geq\frac{2\left|\mathscr{N}(\rho_{T})-\mathscr{N}(\rho_{0})\right|}{\Lambda^{N}_{T}}.

(23)

We also provide an alternative proof of Theorem
1
(see Appendix
E
).
∎

The bound (
15
) holds for both the generation and degradation of entanglement due to quantum dynamical processes. The negativity is monotone under local operation and classical communication (LOCC). Therefore, whenever bipartite dynamical process from initial time to final time can be represented as an LOCC map, we have
\mathscr{N}(\rho_{T})\leq\mathscr{N}(\rho_{0})
; see also Remark 4.4 of Ref.
Das 2018
in this context. It is a trivial observation that that
T_{\mathrm{N}SL}=0
if and only if there is no difference between the negativity between initial and final states, where we assume that the dynamics is such that
\Lambda_{T}
is finite. There are multiple scenarios under which no change in the negativity and hence
T_{\mathrm{N}SL}=0
may occur. For instances,
(i)
if the initial state is a fixed point of the dynamical process,
(ii)
if the dynamical process is PPT-preserving map, i.e., processes that map PPT states to PPT states,
(iii)
if we are choosing evolution duration of the state under dynamical process such that negativity at the initial and final time points are same.

III.2
Speed limit on the concurrence

The concurrence was first introduced for pure two-qubit states in Ref.
Wootters 1998
and later a generalized version of concurrence called I-concurrence for pure two-qudit states was introduced in Ref.
Rungta et al. 2001
. We now discuss lower bounds on the minimal time for the certain amount of change in the concurrence and the I-concurrence for two-qubit and two-qudit systems, respectively, evolving under time-dependent Hamiltonians
H_{t}
. For a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H_{t}
, subscript
t
is to denote time-point
t
.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a closed two-qubit quantum system
AB
which is in a pure state. The minimal time
T
taken for the (closed) system to evolve for a certain amount of change in the square of its concurrence under unitary dynamics generated by a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H_{t}
is lower bounded by

T\geq T_{\mathrm{C}SL}=\frac{\hbar}{4}\frac{\absolutevalue{ \mathscr{C}^2(\psi_T)-{ \mathscr{C}^2(\psi_0)}}}{\Lambda^{C}_{T}},

(24)

where
\psi_{t}=\mathcal{T}\exp(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{t'}{\rm d}t')\psi_{0}
, with
\psi_{0}
denoting the initial state (at
t=0
),
\psi_{T}
denoting the final state (at
t=T
),
\mathcal{T}
is time ordering operator, and
\Lambda^{C}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H_{t}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}}t
.
\Lambda^{C}_{T}
can be interpreted as the evolution speed of the square of the concurrence of the given system and process.

See Appendix
G
for the detailed proof of the above theorem. An immediate consequence of the above theorem is the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Consider a closed two-qubit quantum system
AB
which is in a pure state. The minimal time
T
taken for the (closed) system to evolve for a certain amount of change in the square of its concurrence under unitary dynamics generated by a time-independent Hamiltonian
H
is lower bounded by

T\geq T_{\mathrm{C}SL}=\frac{\hbar}{4}\frac{\absolutevalue{ \mathscr{C}^2(\psi_T)-{ \mathscr{C}^2(\psi_0)}}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{0}H^{2})}},

(25)

where
\psi_{t}=\exp(\frac{\iota Ht}{\hbar})\psi_{0}\exp(\frac{-\iota Ht}{\hbar})
, with
\psi_{0}
denoting the initial state (at
t=0
) and
\psi_{T}
denoting the final state (at
t=T
).

We now derive speed limits on the I-concurrence.

Proposition 1
.

Consider a finite-dimensional bipartite quantum system
AB
initially in a pure state. The minimal time
T
taken for the closed system to bring certain amount of change in the square of its I-concurrence under unitary dynamics generated by a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H_{t}
is lower bounded by

T\geq T_{\mathrm{I}CSL}=\frac{\absolutevalue{{ \mathscr{C}_{I}^2(\psi_T)}-{ \mathscr{C}_{I}^2(\psi_0)}}}{\Lambda_{T}^{I}},

(26)

where
\psi_{0}
is the initial state (at
t=0
),
\psi_{T}
is the final state (at
T=0)
,
\Lambda_{T}^{I}:=4\nu_{d_{A}}\nu_{d_{B}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\rho^{A}_t}_{\mathrm{2}}\norm{\tr_{\rm B}\left(\cal{L}_t(\psi_{t})\right)}_{\mathrm{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t
for the given unitary dynamics
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\psi_{t})=-\frac{\iota}{\hbar}[\psi_{t},H_{t}]
, and
\rho^{A}_{t}:=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\psi_{t}
.

Proof.

The square of I-concurrence of bipartite state
\rho_{t}
is given as

\mathscr{C}_{I}^{2}(\psi_{t})=2\nu_{d_{A}}\nu_{d_{B}}[1-\operatorname{tr}((\rho^{A}_{t})^{2})],

(27)

where
\rho^{A}_{t}:=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\psi_{t}
. After differentiating above equation with respect to time
t
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}_{I}^{2}(\psi_{t})

\displaystyle=-4\nu_{d_{A}}\nu_{d_{B}}\operatorname{tr}({\rho_{t}^{A}\dot{\rho}_{t}^{A}})

\displaystyle=-4\nu_{d_{A}}\nu_{d_{B}}\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{t}^{A}\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathcal{L}_{t}(\psi_{t})).

(28)

Now let us take absolute value of above equation and applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we then obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}_{I}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|\leq 4\nu_{d_{A}}\nu_{d_{B}}\norm{\rho_t^{A}}_{\mathrm{2}}\norm{\tr_{\rm B}\left(\cal{L}_t(\psi_{t})\right)}_{\mathrm{2}}.

(29)

The above inequality is the upper bound on that the rate of change of square of the concurrence of the quantum system evolving under given dynamics.
After integrating above equation with respect to time
t
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}_{I}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|{\mathrm{d}}t\leq\nu_{AB}\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\rho^{A}_t}_{\mathrm{2}}\norm{\tr_{\rm B}\left(\cal{L}_t(\psi_{t})\right)}_{\mathrm{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t,

(30)

where
\nu_{AB}:=4\nu_{d_{A}}\nu_{d_{B}}
. From the above inequality, we get the bound

T\geq\frac{\left|{\mathscr{C}_{I}^{2}(\psi_{T})}-{\mathscr{C}_{I}^{2}(\psi_{0})}\right|}{\Lambda_{T}^{I}}.

(31)

∎

The QSL on concurrence and I-concurrence applies to both entanglement generation and degradation processes. In particular, our bounds (
25
) and (
26
) can determine the minimal time required to prepare a pure bipartite entangled state from a pure product state via unitary dynamics. It is a trivial observation that under local (i.e., separable) unitary dynamics concurrence and I-concurrence are invariant, therefore under such dynamics both
T_{\mathrm{C}SL}
and
T_{\mathrm{I}CSL}
are zero. Note that for any two-qubit system in a pure state the I-concurrence reduces to the concurrence for
\nu_{d_{A}}=1
and
\nu_{d_{B}}=1
but
T_{\mathrm{C}SL}
and
T_{\mathrm{I}CSL}
may not be equal because the evolution speed for a given dynamics could be different for both the bounds. For example, in the case of time-independent Hamiltonian, the evolution speed of concurrence is independent of the time interval while the evolution speed of I-concurrence depends on the time interval.

III.3
Speed limits on other correlations

Quantum mechanics allow for correlations between systems that cannot be depicted by any classical systems. It is known that no local-realistic hidden variable theories can predict all the outcomes exhibited by quantum correlations
Einstein et al. 1935
;
Bell 1964
;
Brunner et al. 2014
(see also Refs.
Bancal et al. 2013
;
Home et al. 2015
). Entangled states with no local-realistic hidden variable models are deemed nonlocal states. Bell-CHSH observables are used to test nonlocality of bipartite quantum states
Clauser et al. 1969
;
Hensen et al. 2015
. Apart from quantum corrrelations like entanglement and nonlocality, there is also interest in quantifying total amount of correlations between two systems. A quantifier of total amount of correlations present in arbitrary bipartite quantum system is quantum mutual information. It captures both the classical and truly quantum correlations present in a bipartite quantum system. In this section, we provide speed limits on the Bell-CHSH observable and the quantum mutual information for certain classes of quantum dynamics and speed limits on the von-Neumann entropy for arbitrary dynamics.

Speed limit on Bell-CHSH correlation.—
In the Heisenberg picture, it is the operator which changes in time while the density operator remains fixed. For any dynamics of two-qubit quantum system with initial state
\rho
, the minimum time needed for the Bell-CHSH observable
\mathcal{B}_{t}
to attain expectation value
\langle\mathcal{B}_{T}\rangle_{\rho}
, starting with the initial expectation value
\langle\mathcal{B}_{0}\rangle_{\rho}
, is lower bounded by (see Appendix
C
)

T\geq T_{\mathrm{B}QSL}=\frac{|\langle\mathcal{B}_{T}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle\mathcal{B}_{0}\rangle_{\rho}|}{\norm{\rho}_{1}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{\infty}},\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{1}},\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{2}}\right\},

(32)

where
\Lambda_{T}^{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t||\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{B}_{t})||_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\{1,2,\infty\}
is the evolution speed of Bell-CHSH observable of the given system,
\langle\mathcal{B}_{0}\rangle_{\rho}
and
\langle\mathcal{B}_{T}\rangle_{\rho}
are expectation value of Bell-CHSH observable at
t=0
and
t=T
, respectively. In Appendix
H
, we also derive speed limit on Bell-CHSH correlation for bipartite quantum dynamics describable as separable maps.

We note that the sharpest bound is the operator norm-based bound. However, determining the Hilbert-Schmidt norm is comparatively easier to compute for general quantum dynamics.

Speed limit on quantum mutual information.—

Consider quantum systems
A
and
B
which are of arbitrary dimensions. We assume that the systems are initially uncorrelated before they interact. Starting from a product state
\omega^{AB}_{0}:=\rho^{A}_{0}\otimes\rho^{B}_{0}
, the minimal time
T
required to bring a certain amount of change in the quantum mutual information of the system evolving under an arbitrary quantum dynamics with time-dependent Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is lower bounded by

T\geq T_{\mathrm{M}ISL}=\frac{I(A;B)_{\omega_{T}}}{\Lambda^{M}_{T}},

(33)

where
\omega_{t}
denotes the state at time
t
with
\omega_{0}
being the initial state (at
t=0
) and
\omega_{T}
being the final state (at
t=T
) and
\Lambda^{M}_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\norm{\mathcal{L}_{t}({\omega_t})}_{2}\norm{\ln\omega_t-\ln{\omega_0})}_{2}
(see Appendix
I
for proof). Here we are implicitly assuming dynamics for which
\operatorname{supp}(\omega_{t_{2}})\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\omega_{t_{1}})
for all valid time points
t_{2}\geq t_{1}
(see Appendix
B
).
\Lambda_{T}
can be interpreted as the evolution speed of the quantum mutual information of the given system and process. This bound (
33
) only holds for quantum mutual information generation process. The bound (
33
) provides the minimal time required to prepare a bipartite correlated state from a uncorrelated state.

Speed limit on the entropy.—
Consider a quantum system
A
, where
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})\leq\infty
. The minimal time
T
taken for the quantum system to bring certain amount of change in the entropy under an arbitrary given quantum dynamical process with associated Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is lower bounded by

T\geq T_{{\mathrm{E}SL}}=\frac{\left|S(\rho_{T})-S(\rho_{0})\right|}{{\Lambda}^{S}_{T}},

(34)

where
\rho_{t}
denotes the state at time
t
with
\rho_{0}
being the initial state (at
t=0
) and
\rho_{T}
being the final state (at
t=T
) and
\Lambda^{S}_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\norm{\mathcal{L}_{t}({\rho_t})}_{2}\norm{\ln\rho_t}_{2}
(see proof in Appendix
J
).

Several entanglement measures such as the entanglement of formation
Bennett et al. 1996a
;
Wootters 2001
, the distillable entanglement
Bennett et al. 1996a
;
Bennett et al. 1996b
, and the relative entropy of entanglement
Vedral et al. 1997
;
Vedral and Plenio 1998
reduce to the entanglement entropy in the case of a closed bipartite system
Osborne and Nielsen 2002
. Thus, in this case, the above bound (
34
) also sheds light on speed limits on entanglement for bipartite systems in a pure state (cf.
Das et al. 2018
;
Das 2018
). The above bound (
34
) is valid for both the entropy generation and degradation processes, and also tighter than that obtained in the Theorem 1 of Ref.
Mohan et al. 2022
.

Open quantum dynamics.—
Before we analytically and numerically compute speed limits on the negativity (

In case of open quantum systems we assume that dynamics of extended system (system + environment) is unitary and after tracing out the environment, we get the evolution equation for open quantum system. Under Markovian approximation, Eq. (
4

\displaystyle\dot{\rho_{t}}=-\iota[H,\rho_{t}]+\sum_{\alpha}\left(2L_{\alpha}\rho_{t}L^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}-\{L^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}L_{\alpha},\rho_{t}\}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{O}_{t}}=\iota[H,\mathcal{O}_{t}]+\sum_{\alpha}\left(2L^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}\mathcal{O}_{t}L_{\alpha}-\{L^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}L_{\alpha},\mathcal{O}_{t}\}\right),

\displaystyle\rho_{0}

\displaystyle=p\ket{00}\bra{00}+\sqrt{\left(1-p\right)p}(\ket{00}\bra{11}+\ket{11}\bra{00})

(47)

Pure dephasing process.—
We first consider pure dephasing channel as an example of quantum correlation degradation process. The Lindbladian operators for pure dephasing process are given as

V
Conclusion

An important aspect of developing quantum devices is linked to the understanding of the rates at which quantum resources are consumed or generated
Aifer and Deffner 2022
;
Das 2018
;
Das et al. 2021
. The rate at which correlations change for any given quantum process is critical for designing the architecture of quantum processors as well as to control the dynamical evolution of quantum systems for desired information processing or computation tasks
Ladd et al. 2010
;
Dowling and Milburn 2003
;
Jones et al. 2012
. In this work, we introduced speed limits on some of the entanglement monotones like negativity and concurrence and also some other correlations like quantum mutual information and Bell-CHSH observable. These speed limits provide lower bound on the minimal time required to bring certain amount of change in these correlations for bipartite quantum systems undergoing time-evolution. We have also discussed a few cases of practical processes for which some of these speed limits are attainable and hence can be considered to be tight. As a byproduct of the scenarios we consider in this paper, we are also able to improve upon the speed limit on the von Neumann entropy derived in Ref.
Mohan et al. 2022
. It is important to note that standard speed limits answer the fundamental question of how fast quantum systems can evolve over time, while our work provides limits on the rates at which we can create or destroy correlations through physical processes or how fast quantum devices can consume these correlations to perform a given task (as correlations can be valuable resources in quantum information and computing tasks. As a future direction, we leave open questions to derive (tight) speed limits on correlational measures beyond those discussed in this paper, for an instance, multipartite nonlocal quantum correlations
Bancal et al. 2013
;
Home et al. 2015
;
Horodecki et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Partial Transpose operation

For an arbitrary operator
X_{AB}
defined on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
and a fixed orthonormal basis
\{\ket{\alpha}\}_{A}
, the partial transpose
\Gamma^{A}
is defined as the following linear map

X_{AB}^{\Gamma_{A}}:=\Gamma^{A}(X_{AB})=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B})X_{AB}(\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B}),

(64)

where
\Gamma^{A}
is tensor product of two maps
T^{A}
(transpose operator on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
) and
\mathbbm{1}_{B}
. Map
\Gamma^{B}
can also be defined in similar way.

A bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
is called separable if it can be written in the following form:

\rho_{AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_{i}}{\psi_{i}}_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{\phi_{i}}{\phi_{i}}_{B},

(65)

for some probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i}
and sets
\{\ket{\psi_{i}}_{A}\}_{i}
and
\{\ket{\phi_{i}}_{B}\}_{i}
of pure states. Let
\operatorname{SEP}(A;B)
denotes the set of separable states defined on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
. Under the action of partial transpose map, set of separable states remains a closed set i.e.,
\Gamma^{A}(\rho_{AB})\in\operatorname{SEP}(A;B),\forall\rho_{AB}\in\operatorname{SEP}(A;B)
. If the action of partial transposition map on a state yields operator which is positive semidefinite, then the state is said to be positive partial transposition (PPT) (see ). That is,
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{AB})
is a PPT state if
\Gamma^{B}(\rho_{AB})\geq 0
, which also implies that
\Gamma^{A}(\rho_{AB})\geq 0
. If a quantum state
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{AB})
corresponds to a separable bipartite state then
\Gamma^{B}(\rho_{AB})\geq 0
but the converse statement is true if and only if both the quantum systems
A
and
B
are either qubit or one is qubit and the other is qutrit
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Peres 1996
. For a
1\oplus 1
-mode Gaussian state
\rho_{AB}
, the positive partial transposition is necessary and sufficient criterion for separability
Simon 2000
. If
\rho_{AB}
is not separable then it is entangled. There are some special type of entangled states called “maximally entangled” states. If
\rho_{AB}
is maximally entangled and we perform any local measurement on subsystem
A
or
B
, then we gain no information about the preparation of the state; instead we merely generate a random bit.

Let
\mathcal{O}

\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}^{{\dagger}}
. The kernel of
\mathcal{O}

is the span of the eigenvectors of
\mathcal{O}
corresponding to its zero eigenvalues. The support
\operatorname{supp(\mathcal{O})}
of
\mathcal{O}

\mathcal{H}
orthogonal to its kernel. Let
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}
be the set of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{O}
and
\{\ket{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i}
be the corresponding set of eigenvectors of
\mathcal{O}
. Then
\mathcal{O}
can be written as follows:

\mathcal{O}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\outerproduct{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}},

(66)

which is called a spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{O}
. The projection onto the support
\operatorname{supp}(\mathcal{O})
of
\mathcal{O}
is denoted by

\Pi_{\mathcal{O}}=\sum_{i:\alpha_{i}\neq 0}\outerproduct{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}.

(67)

If
f
is a real valued function with domain
{\mathrm{D}om}(f)
, then
f(\mathcal{O})
is defined as

f(\mathcal{O})=\sum_{i:\alpha_{i}\in{\mathrm{D}om}(f)}f(\alpha_{i})\outerproduct{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}.

(68)

Appendix C
Speed limit for observable

The expectation value of the observable
\mathcal{O}_{t}
is given as

\langle\mathcal{O}_{t}\rangle_{\rho}={\mathrm{t}r}(\mathcal{O}_{0}\Phi_{t}(\rho))={\mathrm{t}r}(\Phi^{\dagger}_{t}(\mathcal{O}_{0})\rho),

(69)

where
\Phi_{t}
is generator of dynamics,
\mathcal{O}_{t}=\Phi_{t}(\mathcal{O}_{0})
and
\rho
is the given state of bipartite quantum system. The time evolution of the observable
\mathcal{O}
is given by the following equation,

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{O}_{t}}={\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}[\mathcal{O}_{t}].

(70)

Let us take the average of Eq (
70
) in the bipartite state
\rho
and its absolute value. By applying the Hölder’s inequality inequality, we obtain the following inequality

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\langle\mathcal{O}_{t}\rangle_{\rho}}\right|\leq\norm{\rho}_{1}\norm{{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}[\cal{O}_{t}]}_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\norm{\rho}_{1}\norm{{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}[\cal{O}_{t}]}_{\mathrm{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\rho}_{1}\norm{{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}[\cal{O}_{t}]}_{\mathrm{1}}.

(71)

The above inequality (
C
) is the upper bound on that the rate of change of expectation value of the observable evolving under given dynamics. After integrating the above equation with respect to time
t
, we then obtain the following unified bound

T\geq T_{\mathrm{O}QSL}=\frac{|\langle\mathcal{O}_{T}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle\mathcal{O}_{0}\rangle_{\rho}|}{\norm{\rho}_{1}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{\infty}},\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{1}},\frac{1}{\Lambda_{T}^{2}}\right\},

(72)

where
\Lambda_{T}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\norm{\cal{L}_{t}^{\dagger}(\cal{O}_{t})}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\{1,2,\infty\}
is the evolution speed of the observable of the given system. For the given dynamics, the bound given in (
72
) determines how fast the expectation value of the observable changes in time (cf.
Mohan and Pati 2022
).

Appendix D
Hermiticity after partial transposition

Consider a bipartite system
AB
with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
and density matrix
\rho_{AB}
. If
\{\ket{\alpha_{i}}_{A}\}_{i}
are the orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\{\ket{\beta_{i}}_{B}\}_{i}
are the orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{B}
then the density matrix of
AB
takes the general form

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}}a^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha'}_{A}\otimes\ket{\beta}\bra{\beta'}_{B},

(73)

\displaystyle\rho^{\dagger}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}}\left(a^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}\right)^{*}\ket{\alpha'}\bra{\alpha}_{A}\otimes\ket{\beta'}\bra{\beta}_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}}\left(a^{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha}_{\beta^{\prime},\beta}\right)^{*}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha'}_{A}\otimes\ket{\beta}\bra{\beta'}_{B}.

(74)

The density operator
\rho_{AB}
is a hermitian i.e.
\rho^{\dagger}_{AB}=\rho_{AB}
. Now, comparing coefficients of
\rho_{AB}
and
\rho^{\dagger}_{AB}
, we find the following condition on the coefficient
a^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}

a^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}=\left(a^{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha}_{\beta^{\prime},\beta}\right)^{*}.

(75)

The partial transpose
\rho^{AB}
can be written as

\displaystyle\rho^{T_{B}}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}}a^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha'}_{A}\otimes\ket{\beta'}\bra{\beta}_{B},

(76)

\displaystyle(\rho^{T_{B}}_{AB})^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}}\left(a^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}}\right)^{*}\ket{\alpha'}\bra{\alpha}_{A}\otimes\ket{\beta}\bra{\beta'}_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}}\left(a^{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha}_{\beta^{\prime},\beta}\right)^{*}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha'}_{A}\otimes\ket{\beta'}\bra{\beta}_{B}.

(77)

Using (
75
) it can be shown that partial transpose of a density operator is hermitian.

Let us now consider the absolute value of the above equation and apply triangular inequality
|A+B|\leq|A|+|B|
. We then obtain the following inequality

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|\leq\left|\operatorname{tr}\big(\dot{\psi_{t}}\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}})\big)\right|+\left|\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t})\dot{\psi_{t}}^{*}\big)\right|.

(86)

Using Liouville-von Neumann equation
\dot{\psi}_{t}=\frac{\iota}{\hbar}[\psi_{t},H_{t}]
for unitary dynamics, the above inequality can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\hbar}[\left|\operatorname{tr}([\psi_{t},H_{t}]\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}}))\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 9.95863pt+\left|\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t})([\psi_{t},H_{t}])^{*})\right|].

(87)

By applying triangular inequality once more on the above inequality, we then obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\hbar}[\left|\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H_{t}\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}}))\right|+\left|\operatorname{tr}(H_{t}\psi_{t}\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}}))\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t})(\psi_{t}H_{t})^{*})\right|+\left|\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t})(H_{t}\psi_{t})^{*})\right|].

(88)

Let us apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
|{\mathrm{t}r}(AB)|\leq\sqrt{{\mathrm{t}r}(A^{\dagger}A){\mathrm{t}r}(B^{\dagger}B)}
. We then obtain the following inequality

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\hbar}[\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}})^{\dagger}\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}}))}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H_{t}^{2})}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}({\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t})}^{\dagger}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t}))}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H_{t}^{2})^{*}}.

(89)

Since
\psi_{t}
is pure state, it implies that
\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}})^{\dagger}\mathcal{R}({\psi^{*}_{t}})}=1
,
\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t})^{\dagger}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{t}))}=1
and
\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H^{2})^{*}=\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H^{2})
.
Therefore, we can rewrite the above equation as

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|\leq\frac{4}{\hbar}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H_{t}^{2})}.

(90)

The above inequality (
90
) is the upper bound on that the rate of change of square of the concurrence of the quantum system evolving under unitary dynamics. After integrating the above equation with respect to time
t
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{t})\right|\leq\frac{4}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\psi_{t}H_{t}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}}t.

(91)

From the above inequality, we get the desired bound:

T\geq\frac{\hbar}{4}\frac{\left|{\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{T})}-{\mathscr{C}^{2}(\psi_{0})}\right|}{\Lambda^{C}_{T}}.

(92)

∎

Appendix H
Alternative speed limit on Bell-CHSH correlations

In this section, we derive speed limits for Bell-CHSH observable whose dynamics governed by separable map. The separable dynamics degrade the Bell-CHSH correlation.
Expectation value of Bell-CHSH observable at time
t
is given by

\operatorname{tr}(\phi_{t}(\rho)\mathcal{B})=\operatorname{tr}(\phi_{t}(\rho)a_{1}\otimes b_{1})+\operatorname{tr}(\phi_{t}(\rho)a_{2}\otimes b_{2}),

(93)

where
a_{1}=\hat{a}.\vec{\sigma}
,
a_{2}=\hat{a^{\prime}}.\vec{\sigma}
,
b_{1}=(\hat{b}+\hat{b^{\prime}}).\vec{\sigma}
and
b_{2}=(\hat{b}-\hat{b^{\prime}}).\vec{\sigma}
.
The above equation can be rewritten as

\operatorname{tr}(\rho\phi^{\dagger}_{t}(\mathcal{B}))=\operatorname{tr}(\rho\phi^{\dagger}_{t}(a_{1}\otimes b_{1}))+\operatorname{tr}(\rho\phi^{\dagger}_{t}(a_{2}\otimes b_{2})).

(94)

Let us differentiate above equation with respect to time t, we then obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\operatorname{tr}(\rho\mathcal{B}_{t})=\operatorname{tr}(\rho\mathcal{L}_{A}(a_{1t})\otimes\mathcal{L}_{B}(b_{1t}))+\operatorname{tr}(\rho\mathcal{L}_{A}(a_{2t})\otimes\mathcal{L}_{B}(b_{2t})),

(95)

where
\mathcal{L}_{AB}=\mathcal{L}_{A}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{B}+\operatorname{id}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{B}
,
\operatorname{id}_{A}
and
\operatorname{id}_{B}
are the identity super-operators corresponding to system
A
and
B
respectively. Let us take the absolute value of the above equation and applying the triangular inequality, we get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\operatorname{tr}(\rho\mathcal{B}_{t})\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{tr}(\rho\mathcal{L}_{A}(a_{1t})\otimes\mathcal{L}_{B}(b_{1t}))\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\operatorname{tr}(\rho\mathcal{L}_{A}(a_{2t})\otimes\mathcal{L}_{B}(b_{2t}))\right|.

(96)

Let us apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we then obtain the following inequality

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\operatorname{tr}(\rho\mathcal{B}_{t})\right|\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\rho^{2})}(\norm{\cal{L}_{A}(a_{1t})\otimes\cal{L}_{B}(b_{1t})}_{2}

\displaystyle+\norm{ \cal{L}_{A}(a_{2t})\otimes\cal{L}_{B}(b_{2t})}_{2}).

(97)

After integrating the above equation with respect to time
t
, we then obtain the following bound

T\geq T_{\mathrm{B}QSL}=\frac{|\langle\mathcal{B}_{T}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle\mathcal{B}_{0}\rangle_{\rho}|}{\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\rho^{2})}\Lambda^{B}_{T}},

(98)

where
\Lambda^{B}_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t(\norm{\cal{L}_{A}(a_{1t})\otimes\cal{L}_{B}(b_{1t})}_{2}+\norm{ \cal{L}_{A}(a_{2t})\otimes\cal{L}_{B}(b_{2t})}_{2})
.

The above abound describes how fast Bell-CHSH correlation degrades under separable dynamics.

Appendix J
Proof of speed limit on the entropy (
34
)

Consider the state of a quantum system
A
evolving under quantum dynamics at time
t
is given by
\rho_{t}
. Then the entropy of the quantum system at time
t

(105)

It is standard convention that

.
After differentiating the above equation with respect to time
t
, we obtain
Das et al. 2018
;
Spohn 1978

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}S(\rho_{t}^{A})=-{\mathrm{t}r}\{\dot{\rho}_{t}^{A}\ln\rho_{t}^{A}\}=-{\mathrm{t}r}\{\mathcal{L}_{t}({\rho_{t}^{A}})\ln\rho_{t}^{A}\}.

(106)

Let us now consider the absolute value of the above equation and apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. We then obtain the following inequality

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}S(\rho_{t}^{A})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathrm{t}r}\{\mathcal{L}_{t}({\rho_{t}^{A}})\ln\rho_{t}^{A}\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\mathcal{L}_{t}({\rho_t^{ A}})}_{2}\norm{ \ln\rho_t^{ A}}_{2},

(107)

where
\ln\rho_{t}
is defined on the support of
\rho_{t}
.
The above inequality (
J
) is the upper bound on that the rate of change of the entropy of the quantum system evolving under given dynamics. After integrating above equation with respect to time
t
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}t\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}S(\rho_{t}^{A})\right|\leq\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\mathcal{L}_{t}({\rho_t^{ A}})}_{\mathrm{2}}\norm{\ln\rho_t^{ A}}_{\mathrm{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t.

(108)

(109)

where
