On the Uncertainty Principle for Metaplectic Transformations
Nicolas Lerner

Abstract
. We explore the new proofs and extensions of the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle introduced by A. Widgerson & Y. Widgerson in [ 27 ] , developed in [ 7 ] by N.C. Dias, F. Luef and J.N. Prata and also in [ 24 ] by Y. Tang. In particular we give here a proof of the Uncertainty Principle for operators in the Metaplectic group in any dimension.

Introduction

1.1.
Foreword

The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle,
a cornerstone of Quantum Mechanics,
could somehow be summarized by the fact that
Operators do not usually commute

and the calculation of the commutator of two operators
A,B,

say from a Hilbert space into itself,

(1.1.1)

[A,B]=AB-BA,

can unravel important properties linked to stability of matter. In particular the fact that, in one dimension,

(1.1.2)

\Bigl[\frac{d}{dx},x\Bigr]=1,

implies readily that

(1.1.3)

\Bigl\|{\frac{du}{dx}}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\|xu\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\geq\operatorname{Re}\langle\frac{du}{idx},ixu\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\langle[\frac{d}{dx},x]u,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

and is closely related to the fact that the Harmonic
Oscillator

\mathcal{H}=-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x^{2},

is bounded from below by the positive constant
\hbar
, whereas the Hamiltonian function
\xi^{2}+x^{2}

(
quantized
by
\mathcal{H}
)
is actually vanishing at the origin.
Similarly, we have in
n
dimensions with
n\geq 2
,
\kappa>0
,

\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}\Delta}{2}-\frac{\kappa}{|x|}\geq-\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}\hbar^{2}}>-\infty,

whereas the Hamiltonian function

\frac{|\xi|^{2}}{2}-\frac{\kappa}{|x|}

(quantized by
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}
)
is unbounded from below.
The
Heisenberg inequality
(
1.1.3
) can be written in
n
dimensions as

(1.1.4)

\bigl\||D|u\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigl\||x|u\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\geq\frac{n}{4\pi}\bigl\|u\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2},

and follows easily from the one-dimensional (
1.1.3
).
(see e.g. Section
4.2.1
in our Appendix.) One could say as well as for (
1.1.3
)
that (
1.1.4
) is a consequence of the non-commutation of the Fourier multiplier
|D|

with the operator of multiplication by
|x|
.
It is also interesting to notice
the following result (see e.g. Lemma 1.2.29 in
[
20
]
).

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
J,K\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{H})
; then the commutator
[J,K]\not=\operatorname{Id}
.

That simple result, whose proof is elementary, is showing that
Quantum Mechanics must deal with unbounded operators to have properties like (
1.1.2
)
or creation-annihilation properties such as

\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\frac{d}{idx}-ix,\frac{d}{idx}+ix\Bigr]=1.

E. Lieb’s article
[
21

and E. Lieb & R. Seiringer’ book
[
22

are providing a whole theory of stability of matter based upon the non-commutation of operators quantifying the kinetic energy with the operators of multiplication by a potential depending on the configuration variables.

2.3.2.
The Weyl quantization

The main goal of Hermann Weyl in his seminal paper
[
26
]
was to give a simple formula, also providing symplectic covariance, ensuring that real-valued Hamiltonians
a(x,\xi)
get quantized by formally self-adjoint operators. The standard way of dealing with differential operators does not achieve that goal since for instance the standard quantization of the Hamiltonian

x\xi
(indeed real-valued) is the operator
xD_{x}
, which is not symmetric
(
D_{x}=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial x})
; H. Weyl’s choice in that case was

x\xi\ \text{should be quantized by the operator\quad}\frac{1}{2}(xD_{x}+D_{x}x),\quad\text{(indeed symmetric),}

and more generally, say for

a\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}),u\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the quantization of the Hamiltonian
a(x,\xi)
, denoted by
{\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}(a)}
,
should be
given by the formula

(2.3.8)

({\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}(a)}u)(x)=\iint e^{2i\pi(x-y)\cdot\xi}a\Bigl(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\Bigr)u(y)dyd\xi.

For
v\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we may consider

\langle{\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}(a)}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\iiint a(x,\xi)e^{-2i\pi z\cdot\xi}u(x+\frac{z}{2})\bar{v}(x-\frac{z}{2})dzdxd\xi\\
=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x,\xi)\mathcal{W}(u,v)(x,\xi)dxd\xi,

and the latter formula allows us to give the following definition.

Definition 2.31
.

Let
a\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. We define the Weyl quantization
{\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}(a)}
of the Hamiltonian
a
, by the formula

(2.3.9)

({\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}(a)}u)(x)=\iint e^{2i\pi(x-y)\cdot\xi}a\Bigl(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\Bigr)u(y)dyd\xi,

to be understood weakly as

(2.3.10)

\langle{\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}(a)}u,\bar{v}\rangle_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\langle a,\mathcal{W}(u,v)\rangle_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}),\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})}.

We note that the sesquilinear mapping

\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ni(u,v)\mapsto\mathcal{W}(u,v)\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}),

is continuous so that the above bracket of duality

makes sense.
We note as well that a temperate distribution

from

\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

into

\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
This very general framework is not really useful since we want to compose our operators

A first step in this direction is to look for sufficient conditions

is bounded on

.

and

a
polynomial

4.1.1.
Fourier transform.

We use in this paper the following normalization for the Fourier transform and inversion formula: for

,

(4.1.1)

\hat{u}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-2i\pi x\cdot\xi}u(x)dx,\quad u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2i\pi x\cdot\xi}\hat{u}(\xi)d\xi,

a formula that can be extended to

,
with defining the distribution
\hat{u}
by the duality bracket

(4.1.2)

\langle\hat{u},\phi\rangle_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\langle u,\hat{\phi}\rangle_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Checking (
4.1.1
) for

is then easy, that is

(4.1.3)

\check{\hat{\hat{u}}}=u,

where the distribution
\check{u}
is defined by

(4.1.4)

\langle\check{u},\phi\rangle_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\langle u,\check{\phi}\rangle_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\quad\text{with}\ \check{\phi}(x)=\phi(-x).

It is useful to notice that for

,

\check{\hat{u}}=\hat{\check{u}}.

Our normalization yields simple formulas for the Fourier transform of Gaussian functions: for

a real-valued symmetric positive definite
n\times n
matrix, we define the function
v_{A}
in the Schwartz space by

(4.1.6)

v_{A}(x)=e^{-\pi\langle Ax,x\rangle},\quad\text{and we have }\quad\widehat{v_{A}}(\xi)=(\det A)^{-1/2}e^{-\pi\langle A^{-1}\xi,\xi\rangle}.

Similarly when
B
is
a real-valued symmetric non-singular
n\times n
matrix, the function

defined by

w_{B}(x)=e^{i\pi\langle Bx,x\rangle}

is in
L^{{\infty}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and thus a tempered distribution and we have

(4.1.7)

\widehat{w_{B}}(\xi)={|\det B|}^{-1/2}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}\operatorname{sign}B}e^{-i\pi\langle B^{-1}\xi,\xi\rangle},

where
\operatorname{sign}B

stands for the

B
that is, with
\mathtt{E}
the set of eigenvalues of
B

(4.1.8)

\operatorname{sign}B=\underbrace{\operatorname{Card}(\mathtt{E}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+})}_{\nu_{+}(B)}-\underbrace{\operatorname{Card}(\mathtt{E}\cap\mathbb{R}_{-})}_{\nu_{-}(B)=\operatorname{index}{(B)}}.

The integer
\nu_{-}(B)
is called the

of

, noted
\operatorname{index}{(B)}
; Formula

since

(as

We note also that

Let

Remark 4.3
.

The notation
|T|
could be misleading. In particular we may have
|T|\not=|T^{*}|
:
take for instance the right shift operator

defined on

by

The operator

is isometric since we have obviously
\|S_{r}x\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}=\|x\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}
and thus

On the other hand we have with

\langle S_{l}\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{k}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}=\langle\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{k+1}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}=\delta_{j,k+1},

so that

S_{l}\mathbf{e}_{0}=0
, and
\forall j\geq 1,S_{l}\mathbf{e}_{j}=\mathbf{e}_{j-1},

i.e.

x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k},\dots)\mapsto(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},\dots)=S_{l}x.

We have thus

S_{l}^{*}S_{l}=S_{r}S_{r}^{*}

and

is the orthogonal projection onto
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})

since

\forall j\geq 1,\ S_{r}S_{r}^{*}(\mathbf{e}_{j})=S_{r}(\mathbf{e}_{j-1})=\mathbf{e}_{j},\quad S_{r}S_{r}^{*}(\mathbf{e}_{0})=0,

implying that

|S_{r}^{*}|=I-\mathbb{P}_{0},\quad\text{where $\mathbb{P}_{0}$ is the orthogonal projection onto $\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{e}_{0}).$}

More generally if

(4.2.1)

2\operatorname{Re}\langle D_{t}u,itu\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\langle D_{t}u,itu\rangle+\langle itu,D_{t}u\rangle=\langle D_{t}itu-itD_{t}u,u\rangle\\
=\langle[D_{t},it]u,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

implying in particular

(4.2.2)

\|D_{t}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\|tu\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\geq\frac{1}{4\pi}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

which is an equality for
u(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}2^{1/4}
; moreover we infer also from
(
4.2.1
) that

(4.2.3)

\displaystyle\langle\pi(D_{t}^{2}+t^{2})u,u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

proving that

(4.2.4)

\min\bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}{\pi(D_{t}^{2}+t^{2})}\bigr]=\frac{1}{2}.

The core argument in the proof of (
4.2.2
) is the integration by parts given by the third equality,
taking advantage of the non-commutation of the operators of momentum
D_{t}
and of position
t
.
We can infer a non-trivial generalization of (
4.2.2
) by noticing that for
u\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

2\operatorname{Re}\langle D_{x_{j}}u,ix_{j}u\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

which implies for
p\in[1,+{\infty}]
,
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
,

\displaystyle\frac{n}{4\pi}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\operatorname{Re}\langle D_{x_{j}}u,ix_{j}u\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\|D_{x_{j}}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|x_{j}u\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\|D_{x_{j}}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Bigr)^{1/p}\Bigl(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\|x_{j}u\|^{{p^{\prime}}}_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Bigr)^{1/{p^{\prime}}}=\|Du\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|xu\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where for a function
\mathbb{R}^{n}\ni x\mapsto v(x)=\bigl(v_{j}(x)\bigr)_{1\leq j\leq n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we have defined

(4.2.5)

\|v\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left(\int\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}{|v_{j}(x)|}^{p}dx\right)^{1/p}=\left(\int\|v(x)\|_{p}^{p}dx\right)^{1/p}.

We get thus

(4.2.6)

\bigl\|Du\bigr\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigl\|xu(x)\bigr\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\geq\frac{n}{4\pi}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

and in particular for
p=2
,
since we have with the standard Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the vector

(Du)(x)=\bigl((D_{x_{j}}u)(x)\bigr)_{1\leq j\leq n}
,

\bigl\|Du\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\int\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}{|(D_{{x_{j}}}u)(x)|}^{2}dx\\
=\int\langle(Du)(x),(Du)(x)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}dx=\bigl\||Du|\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2},

we get

(4.2.7)

\bigl\||D|u\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigl\||x|u(x)\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\geq\frac{n}{4\pi}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

with the Fourier multiplier

(4.2.8)

|D|=\bigl(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}D_{x_{j}}^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}.

The inequality (
4.2.7
) can be written as

(4.2.9)

\Bigl(\int{|\xi|}^{2}{|\hat{u}(\xi)|}^{2}d\xi\Bigr)\Bigl(\int{|x|}^{2}{|u(x)|}^{2}dx\Bigr)\geq\frac{n^{2}}{2^{4}\pi^{2}}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

and is an equality for the Gaussian function

(4.2.10)

u(x)=2^{n/4}e^{-\pi\|x\|_{2}^{2}}.

Indeed we have

\hat{u}(\xi)=2^{n/4}e^{-\pi\|\xi\|_{2}^{2}},

and

\displaystyle 2^{n}\Bigl(\int{|y|}^{2}e^{-2\pi|y|^{2}}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=2^{n}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{+{\infty}}r^{n+1}e^{-2\pi r^{2}}dr\Bigr)^{2}\frac{4\pi^{n}}{\Gamma(n/2)^{2}}

(using
r=s^{1/2}(2\pi)^{-1/2}
)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\frac{4\pi^{n}}{\Gamma(n/2)^{2}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n+1}}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{+{\infty}}s^{n/2}e^{-s}\frac{ds}{2^{3/2}\sqrt{\pi}}\Bigr)^{2}

(4.2.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\Gamma(n/2)^{2}}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+1)^{2}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{n^{2}}{4}=\frac{n^{2}}{2^{4}\pi^{2}}.

N.B
.

4.2.7
) in dimensions larger than

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and let
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
. Let
Q(x_{1},x_{2},\xi_{2})
be a non-negative quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
. We define

(4.2.13)

\lambda_{0}(x_{1})=\inf\Bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}\bigl\{{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}\bigl(Q(x_{1},\cdot,\cdot)\bigr)}\bigr\}\Bigr],

where
{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}(b)}
stands for the Weyl quantization of the Hamiltonian
b

defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
.
Then we have

(4.2.14)

\inf\Bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}\bigl\{{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}\bigr\}\Bigr]=\inf_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{0}(x_{1}).

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}})
: we have

\displaystyle\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}v,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\iiint\hskip-5.0pt\iiint e^{2i\pi(\langle x_{1}-y_{1},\xi_{1}\rangle+\langle(x_{2}-y_{2}),\xi_{2}\rangle)}

\displaystyle\hskip 108.0pt\times Q\bigl(\frac{x_{1}+y_{1}}{2},\frac{x_{2}+y_{2}}{2},\xi_{2}\bigr)v(y_{1},y_{2})\overline{v(x_{1},x_{2})}dy_{1}dy_{2}d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}dx_{1}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=\iint\hskip-5.0pt\iint e^{2i\pi\langle x_{2}-y_{2},\xi_{2}\rangle}Q\bigl(x_{1},\frac{x_{2}+y_{2}}{2},\xi_{2}\bigr)v(x_{1},y_{2})\overline{v(x_{1},x_{2})}dy_{2}dx_{2}d\xi_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}\bigl(Q(x_{1},\cdot,\cdot)\bigr)}v_{x_{1}},v_{x_{1}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}dx_{1},

with

v_{x_{1}}(x_{2})=v(x_{1},x_{2}).

As a consequence, we have

\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}v,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\lambda_{0}(x_{1})\|v_{x_{1}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}^{2}dx_{1}\\
\geq\inf_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{0}(x_{1})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\|v_{x_{1}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}^{2}dx_{1}=\bigl(\inf_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{0}(x_{1})\bigr)\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2},

proving that

(4.2.15)

\inf\Bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}\bigl\{{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}\bigr\}\Bigr]\geq\inf_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{0}(x_{1}).

Let
z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
.
We check with
\varepsilon>0
,

v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,x_{1})=\phi\bigl((x_{1}-z_{1})/\varepsilon\bigr)\varepsilon^{-n_{1}/2},\quad\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}),\quad\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})}=1,

using the first computation in this proof, for
v_{2}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
,

\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}(v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,\cdot)\otimes v_{2}),(v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,\cdot)\otimes v_{2})\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}\bigl(Q(x_{1},\cdot,\cdot)\bigr)}v_{2},v_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}{|\phi\bigl((x_{1}-z_{1})/\varepsilon\bigr)|}^{2}\varepsilon^{-n_{1}}dx_{1}\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}\bigl(Q(z_{1}+\varepsilon t_{1},\cdot,\cdot)\bigr)}v_{2},v_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}{|\phi\bigl(t_{1}\bigr)|}^{2}dt_{1},

so that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}}\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}(v_{1}\otimes v_{2}),(v_{1}\otimes v_{2})\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}\bigl(Q(z_{1},\cdot,\cdot)\bigr)}v_{2},v_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}{|\phi\bigl(t_{1}\bigr)|}^{2}dt_{1}\\
=\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}\bigl(Q(z_{1},\cdot,\cdot)\bigr)}v_{2},v_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}.

Let
\gamma>0
be given.
We may choose

v_{2}(x_{2})=w(z_{1},\gamma,x_{2})
such that

\|v_{2}(z_{1},\gamma,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}=1,

and verifying

\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n_{2}}\bigl(Q(z_{1},\cdot,\cdot)\bigr)}v_{2},v_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})}\leq\gamma+\lambda_{0}(z_{1}).

As a consequence, we find that

1=\|v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,\cdot)\otimes v_{2}(z_{1},\gamma,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}}\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}(v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,\cdot)\otimes w(z_{1},\gamma,\cdot)),v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,\cdot)\otimes w(z_{1},\gamma,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
\leq\gamma+\lambda_{0}(z_{1}),

so that

\langle{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}(v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,\cdot)\otimes w(z_{1},\gamma,\cdot)),v_{1}(z_{1},\varepsilon,\cdot)\otimes w(z_{1},\gamma,\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
\leq\gamma+\lambda_{0}(z_{1})+\beta(z_{1},\gamma,\varepsilon),\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}}\beta(z_{1},\gamma,\varepsilon)=0.

The previous inequality implies that for
\gamma>0,\varepsilon>0,z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,

(4.2.16)

\inf\Bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}\bigl\{{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}\bigr\}\Bigr]\leq\gamma+\lambda_{0}(z_{1})+\beta(z_{1},\gamma,\varepsilon).

\mathtt{\limsup}

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}

4.2.16

(4.2.17)

\inf\Bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}\bigl\{{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}\bigr\}\Bigr]\leq\gamma+\lambda_{0}(z_{1}).

Taking then the
\mathtt{\limsup}
with respect to

on both sides of
(
4.2.17
),
we get for

(4.2.18)

\inf\Bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}\bigl\{{\text{Op}_{w,n}\bigl(Q\bigr)}\bigr\}\Bigr]\leq\lambda_{0}(z_{1}).

proving the converse inequality of (

)

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
C
be a symmetric real
n\times n
matrix and let
\Omega
be a symmetric

n\times n
matrix. We define the operator

(4.2.19)

\mathcal{H}_{C,\Omega}={\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}\bigl(\|\Omega x\|^{2}+\|C\xi+x\|^{2}\bigr)},

where

stands for the canonical Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then we have

(4.2.20)

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\\

Proof.

We may assume that
C
is a symmetric matrix with rank
r\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
.
We may as well suppose that

C=\begin{pmatrix}[l]\Lambda&0_{r,n-r}\\

\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})

\lambda_{j}\not=0

\Omega
symmetric positive-definite, which implies in particular that
\beta_{11}
is positive-definite.
Now the Weyl symbol of
\mathcal{H}_{C,\Omega}
is

a(x_{1},x_{2},\xi_{1})=\|\beta_{11}x_{1}+\beta_{12}x_{2}\|^{2}_{r}+\|\beta_{12}^{*}x_{1}+\beta_{22}x_{2}\|_{n-r}^{2}+\|\Lambda\xi_{1}+x_{1}\|^{2}_{r}+\|x_{2}\|^{2}_{n-r}.

Since it is a quadratic form independent of
\xi_{2}
,
we may apply
Proposition
4.9
.
We have for instance

a(x_{1},0,\xi_{1})=\|\beta_{11}x_{1}\|^{2}_{r}+\|\beta_{12}^{*}x_{1}\|_{n-r}^{2}+\|\Lambda\xi_{1}+x_{1}\|^{2}_{r}\\
=\langle(\beta_{11}^{2}+\beta_{12}\beta_{12}^{*})x_{1},x_{1}\rangle_{r}+\|\Lambda\xi_{1}+x_{1}\|^{2}_{r}.

As a warm-up,

(4.2.24)

We obtain thus that

\mu(\mathcal{M})\geq\frac{|\beta|}{4\pi}.

\mu(\mathcal{M})=\frac{|\beta|}{4\pi}.

With

given by (

), we follow the calculations of Section

in our Appendix

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\omega,c
be real numbers and let

\mathcal{H}_{c,\omega}={\text{{O}p}_{\text{{w}}}((\omega x)^{2}+(c\xi+x)^{2})}.

\inf\bigl[\operatorname{Spectrum}\mathcal{H}_{c,\omega}\bigr]=\frac{|\omega c|}{2\pi}.

.

1.3
.)

\alpha=\sqrt{|\omega/c|},
