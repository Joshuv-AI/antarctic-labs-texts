Multivariable pseudospectrum in C ∗ C^{*} -algebras
Alexander Cerjan, Vasile Lauric, Terry A. Loring

Abstract
. We look at various forms of spectrum and associated pseudospectrum that can be defined for noncommuting d d -tuples of Hermitian elements of a C ∗ C^{*} -algebra. The emphasis is on theoretical calculations of examples, in particular for noncommuting pairs and triple of operators on infinite dimensional Hilbert space. In particular, we look at the universal pair of projections in a C ∗ C^{*} -algebra, the usual position and momentum operators, and triples of tridiagonal operators.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
47A13, 46L05

1.
Introduction

Given
\mathbb{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
,
a noncommuting
d
-tuple of bounded linear Hermitian operators on
Hilbert space, so
A_{j}=A_{j}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, there
are many competing notions of a joint spectrum. The one that has been
involved in recent developments in many areas of physics
[
3
,
4
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
20
,
25
]
is the Clifford
spectrum
\Lambda(\mathbb{A})
. This is a closed, bounded subset
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To define it, we define first the
spectral
localizer

L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(A_{j}-\lambda_{j}I)\otimes\Gamma_{j}.

Here
(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{d})
are matrices that satisfy the
relations

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{j},\quad(j=1,\dots,d)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=1,\quad(j=1,\dots,d)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{k}\Gamma_{j},\quad(j\neq k)

that we call a
representation of the Clifford relations
.

It does not matter what representation we use
[
5
, Lemma 1.2]
,
so generally we take an irreducible representation, which means these matrices
are of size
2^{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}
. For
d=2
a standard
choice is

(1.1)

\Gamma_{1}=\sigma_{x},\,\Gamma_{2}=\sigma_{y}

and for
d=3
standard choice is

(1.2)

\Gamma_{1}=\sigma_{x},\,\Gamma_{2}=\sigma_{y},\,\Gamma_{3}=\sigma_{z}.

Here we are using the Pauli spin matrices

\sigma_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\,\sigma_{y}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},\,\sigma_{z}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

The
Clifford spectrum
is then

(1.3)

\Lambda(\mathbb{A})=\left\{\left.\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right|L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})\text{ is not invertible}\right\}.

We will see that an interesting subset of this is the
essential
Clifford spectrum
, defined as

\Lambda^{e}(\mathbb{A})=\left\{\left.\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right|L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})\text{ is not Fredholm}\right\}.

From a mathematical point of view, there is much that is unknown about the
Clifford spectrum. We are not even sure if it can be the empty set
[
5
, §8]
,
although a lot of evidence says this does not happen. Most of the examples
examined so far have required computer calculations, so most examples
have been finite-dimensional. Here we move on to infinite-dimensional
examples. In most cases, we work inside the Toeplitz algebra, so that
we work with easy
C^{*}
-algebras inside the Calkin algebra to calculate
most of the Clifford spectrum, and then reduce the search for the
rest of the Clifford spectrum to a calculation involving some difference equations.
We are then able to find infinite-dimensional examples where the Clifford spectrum looks
very different from the Clifford spectra that arise from similar examples finite-dimensions.

If
\mathbb{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
, where each
A_{j}
is an Hermitian element
of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
, the definition of Clifford
spectrum still makes sense. Taking the
\Gamma_{j}
to be matrices in
M_{2^{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}}(\mathbb{C})
, we treat

L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})
as an element of
M_{2^{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}}(\mathcal{A})\cong\mathcal{A}\otimes M_{2^{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}}(\mathbb{C})
. We now are treating
I
as the identity element of
\mathcal{A}
.
As
\Lambda(\mathbb{A})
is defined in Eq.
1.3
,
in term of invertiblity, we find we have spectral permanence. That
is, if
\mathcal{A}
is a unital
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{B},

then we get the same result if we compute
\Lambda(\mathbb{A})
working
in
M_{2^{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}}(\mathcal{B})
as we do
if working in
M_{2^{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}}(\mathcal{A})
.

2.
Symmetries and
*
-homomorphisms

We collect here some basic lemmas on the Clifford spectrum in
C^{*}
-algebras. We already discussed spectral permanence, so we already know the behavior of the Clifford spectrum with respect to an embedding of
C^{*}
-algebras.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
\varphi:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
is a unital
*
-homomorphism between unital
C^{*}
-algebras.
If
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are Hermitian elements of
\mathcal{A}

then

(2.1)

\Lambda(\varphi(A_{1}),\dots,\varphi(A_{d}))\subseteq\Lambda(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}).

If
\varphi
is one-to-one, then the inclusion in Equation
2.1
becomes an equality.

Proof.

Since

\left(\varphi\otimes I\right)\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)\right)=L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\varphi(A_{1}),\dots,\varphi(A_{d})\right),

we know that if
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)

is invertible then
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\varphi(A_{1}),\dots,\varphi(A_{d})\right)

is also invertible.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

If
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}
) are Hermitian
and
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
then

\Lambda(UA_{1}U^{*},\dots,UA_{d}U^{*})=\Lambda(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})

and

\Lambda^{\textup{e}}(UA_{1}U^{*},\dots,UA_{d}U^{*})=\Lambda^{\textup{e}}(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}).

For many purposes, such as proving that a symmetry in
\mathbb{A}
leads to a symmetry in
\Lambda(\mathbb{A})
, we need to know that we have complete flexibility in selecting the
\Gamma_{j}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{d}
form a representation of the Clifford relations in
\boldsymbol{M}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
and
\Gamma^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\Gamma^{\prime}_{d}
form a representation of the Clifford relations in
\boldsymbol{M}_{s}(\mathbb{C})
.
If
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are Hermitian elements of unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
then

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(A_{j}-\lambda_{j}\right)\otimes\Gamma_{j}

is invertible if, and only if,

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(A_{j}-\lambda_{j}\right)\otimes\Gamma^{\prime}_{j}

is invertible.

The proof of this lemma is essentially the same as the proof of
[
5
, Lemma 1.2]
and is omitted. We need also the following lemmas from
[
5
]
, also generalized to the
C^{*}
-algebra setting. Again the proofs are almost identical to the matrix case and are omitted. We will denote by

O(d)
the real-valued orthogonal matrices of size
d.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)

is a
d
-tuple of Hermitian elements of unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}

and that
U\in O(d)
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The
d
elements

\hat{A}_{j}=\sum_{s}u_{js}A_{s}

are also Hermitian and

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{\lambda}}\in\Lambda\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)\iff U\boldsymbol{\mathbb{\lambda}}\in\Lambda(\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{d}).

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)
are Hermitian elements in the unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and that
U\in O(d)
.
Let

\hat{A}_{j}=\sum_{s}u_{js}A_{s}.

If there exists a unitary
Q
in
\mathcal{A}
such that

Q\hat{A_{j}}Q^{*}=A_{j}
for all
j
then

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{\lambda}}\in\Lambda\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)\iff U\boldsymbol{\mathbb{\lambda}}\in\Lambda\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right).

3.
The commuting and essentially commuting cases

We know that a single operator’s spectrum can look very different in the infinite-dimensional case when compared with the spectrum of a finite-dimensional counterpart. In the finite-dimensional case, any operator (or matrix)
T
will have finite spectrum. In contrast, in infinite dimensions we can get any nonempty closed and bounded subset of the complex plane. This phenomenon gives us our first examples where the Clifford spectrum looks different from how things looked in finite dimensions
[
10
,
28
]
.

In the case
d=2
the Clifford spectrum is a minor variation on the ordinary spectrum. With the standard
\Gamma
matrices, as in Eq.
1.1
, the spectral localizer is

L_{(x,y)}(A_{1},A_{2})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&A_{1}-iA_{2}-(x-iy)I\\
A_{1}+iA_{2}-(x+iy)I&0\end{array}\right].

This tells us immediately that
(x,y)\in\Lambda(A_{1},A_{2})
exactly when
x+iy
is in the ordinary spectrum of
A_{1}+iA_{2}
. For example, we can have
A_{1}+iA_{2}
be the bilateral shift and so have an example where

(3.1)

\Lambda(A_{1},A_{2})=\mathbb{T}^{1}.

In finite dimensions, we have conjectured
[
5
, §8]
that one-dimensional manifolds cannot arise as the Clifford spectrum of three matrices. In infinite dimensions, we can get the circle as the Clifford spectrum of three operators with commutative and noncommutative examples.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are pair-wise commuting Hermitian elements
of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
then the Clifford spectrum
of
\mathbb{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
equals the standard joint spectrum.

Proof.

Because of spectral permanence and Corollary
2.2
, we can assume that
\mathcal{A}=C(X)
for some compact
Hausdorff space and that
A_{j}=f_{j}
for some continuous
f_{j}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
In this case, we can work pointwise and we find

L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})=g:X\rightarrow M_{2^{\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor}}(\mathbb{C})

where

g(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(f_{j}(x)-\lambda_{j})\Gamma_{j}.

An easy calculation shows that, for scalars
\alpha_{j}
,

\sigma\left(\sum\alpha_{j}\Gamma_{j}\right)=\left\{\pm\sqrt{\sum\alpha_{j}^{2}}\right\}.

Thus
g
is singular only when there is a point
x_{0}
in
X

such that
f_{j}(x_{0})=\lambda_{j}
for all
j
. Thus the Clifford
spectrum is just the joint spectrum.
∎

Example 3.2
.

If
X
is a compact nonempty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
then there
is a commutative example of
d
Hermitian operators on separable
Hilbert space whose Clifford spectrum equals
X
.
Notice that
X
is metrizable so
C(X)
is separable and so can be represented on a separable
Hilbert space.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are Hermitian elements of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and that
1\leq r<d
. If
A_{j}
commutes with
A_{k}
whenever
j\leq r
and
k>r
then

\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d})\subseteq\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{r})\times\Lambda(A_{r+1},\cdots,A_{d}).

Proof.

We always have

\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d})\right)^{2}=\sum(A_{j}-\lambda_{j})^{2}\otimes I+\sum_{j<k}\left[A_{j},A_{k}\right]\otimes\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}

but with the given assumptions many commutators vanish. Here we obtain

\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d})\right)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(A_{j}-\lambda_{j})^{2}\otimes I+\sum_{j<k\leq r}\left[A_{j},A_{k}\right]\otimes\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}+\sum_{r\leq j<k}\left[A_{j},A_{k}\right]\otimes\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{k}

which implies

(3.2)

\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d})\right)^{2}=\left(L_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{r})\right)^{2}+\left(L_{(\lambda_{r+1},\dots,\lambda_{d})}(A_{r+1},\cdots,A_{d})\right)^{2}.

If
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})\notin\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{r})

then there is a positive
a
such that

a\leq\left(L_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{r})\right)^{2}.

We always have

0\leq\left(L_{(\lambda_{r+1},\dots,\lambda_{d})}(A_{r+1},\cdots,A_{d})\right)^{2}

and so

a\leq\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d})\right)^{2}.

Thus

(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})\notin\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{r})\implies(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})\notin\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}).

By symmetry,

(\lambda_{r+1},\dots,\lambda_{d})\notin\Lambda(A_{r+1},\cdots,A_{d})\implies(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})\notin\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}).

∎

Notice that the reverse inclusion in Thereom
3.3
is already false in the case where
all the
A_{j}
commute. We do get
equality in a simple special case.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose
A_{1},\dots,A_{d-1}
are Hermitian elements of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
. For any real scalar
\alpha
we have

\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d-1},\alpha I)=\Lambda(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d-1})\times\{\alpha\}.

Proof.

Equation
3.2
here becomes

\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d-1},\alpha I)\right)^{2}=\left(L_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d-1})}(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d-1})\right)^{2}+\left(\alpha-\lambda_{d}\right)^{2}I

and the result follows.
∎

The following provides more evidence to support two conjectures from
[
5
]
,
that when the Clifford spectrum of a
d
-tuple of
n
-by-
n
matrices is nonempty, and that when it is finite, it must have cardinality at most
n
.

Theorem 3.5
.

If
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
are
n
-by-
n
Hermitian matrices
and
A_{1}
commutes with both
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
then the Clifford
spectrum of
(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})
is a set containing at least one point and at most
n
points.

Proof.

First we look at the special case where the first matrix is scalar. Here Theorem
3.4

tells us that
\Lambda(\alpha I,A_{2},A_{3})
equals
\{\alpha\}\times\sigma(A_{2}+iA_{3})
which is nonempty and can have no more than
n
points.

In the general case, let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
denote the distinct eigenvalues of
A_{1}
.
We can conjugate all the matrices by a unitary to ensure that

A_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\alpha_{1}I\\
&\alpha_{2}I\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&\alpha_{r}I\end{array}\right].

The fact that the other matrices commute with
A_{1}
forces them
to be block diagonal,

A_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}B_{1}\\
&B_{2}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&B_{r}\end{array}\right],\quad A_{3}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}C_{1}\\
&C_{2}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&C_{r}\end{array}\right].

Therefore

\Lambda(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})=\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\Lambda(\alpha_{j}I,B_{j},C_{j})

and the result follows.
∎

4.
Singular values in infinite dimensions

The spectrum of a matrix is generally less informative when that matrix
is not normal. In essence, this is why we cannot build as wide-ranging
functional calculus in the nonnormal case as in the normal case. Some
additional information can be found in the pseudospectum of a nonnormal
matrix. The pseudospectrum
[
29
]
of a square matrix
X
is based on look
formally at the function

(4.1)

\alpha\mapsto\left\|\left(X-\alpha I\right)^{-1}\right\|^{-1}

which takes on a gradation of values for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, including

0
by default when
X-\lambda
is not invertible. This function
can be seen as the pseudospectrum, but traditionally one looked at
the inverse image of
[0,\epsilon)
and called that the
\epsilon
-pseudospectrum.

In applied math, the pseudospectrum is only defined for a single, typically non-normal, square matrix. One can easily translate this into a theory applying to two Hermitian matrices by considering
X=A_{1}+iA_{2}
. The norm in Eq.
4.1
can be interpreted in many ways.
Here we are only interested in the operator norm. We will find in more convenient to compute
\|X^{-1}\|^{-1}
as the smallest singular value of a matrix.
Various papers have looked at the pseudospectrum of a single nonnormal operator on separable Hilbert space, including

[
2
,
15
,
16
]
in operator theory and
[
18
]
in physics.

We need a replacement for the smallest singular value of a non-square matrix that works for a bounded linear operator

T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}.

For now, we are content to deal with the bounded linear operators on separable Hilbert space. In the finite-dimensional case, one characterization of the smallest singular value is the minimum value of
\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|
as
\boldsymbol{v}
ranges over all unit vectors.
We take this as a definition in the infinite-dimensional case, except we use infimum,

s_{\min}(T)=\inf_{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=1}\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|.

In the special case of a compact operator, there is a spectral decomposition
[
13
]
and so all singular
values are defined. We need only the smallest, and of course the largest since we are looking at the spectral norm.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}
is invertible, then

s_{\min}(T)=\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}.

Proof.

By homogeneity we can compute
s_{\min}(T)
as

\displaystyle s_{\min}(T)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0}\frac{\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}.

If
T\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{w}
then

\left(\frac{\left\|T\boldsymbol{v}\right\|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\left\|T^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}\right\|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\right\|}

and the result now follows.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
T:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is a bounded linear operator.
If
T
is normal, then

s_{\min}(T)=\min\left\{\left.|\lambda|\,\right|\lambda\in\sigma(T)\right\}.

Proof.

Suppose
\lambda
is an element in the spectrum of
T
of smallest absolute value. Since
T
is normal,
\lambda
is in the approximate point spectrum so there is a sequence of unit vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
such that

\|T\boldsymbol{v}_{n}-\lambda\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Thus

\|T\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|\leq|\lambda|+\frac{1}{n}

and so
s_{\min}(T)\leq|\lambda|
.

Now we prove the opposite inequality. Since
s_{\min}(T)
and
\sigma(T)
are invariant under conjugation
by a unitary, we can use the spectral theorem
[
14
]
to reduce to the case where
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(X,\mu)
and there is some bounded measurable
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
so that
T\xi(x)=f(x)\xi(x)
. Let
[a,b]
denoted the smallest closed interval that contains the essential range of
|f|
. Then
a
is the smallest element in the spectrum of
|f|
, which by the spectral mapping theorem equals the
smallest absolute value of an element of the spectrum of
f
. Since

\left\|T\xi\right\|^{2}=\int|f(x)|^{2}|\xi(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq a^{2}\int|\xi(x)|^{2}\,dx=a^{2}\left\|\xi\right\|^{2}

we know that
s_{\min}(T)\geq a
.
∎

Lemma
4.2
allows us to extend the definition of
s_{\min}
to normal elements of a
C^{*}
-algebra. In particular, we can now extend the definition of the Clifford pseudospectrum for the
C^{*}
-algebra setting.

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are Hermitian elements of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
. The
Clifford pseudospectrum

for
\mathbb{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
is the function
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\mapsto\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{C}}(\mathbb{A})
where

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{C}}(\mathbb{A})=s_{\min}\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})\right).

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
S,T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}
are both bounded
linear operators, then

\left|s_{\min}(S)-s_{\min}(T)\right|\leq\left\|S-T\right\|.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given. Then there is a unit vector
\boldsymbol{v}

so that

\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq s_{\min}(T)+\epsilon.

Then

s_{\min}(S)\leq\|S\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|+\|(S-T)\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq s_{\min}(T)+\epsilon+\|S-T\|

proving

s_{\min}(S)-s_{\min}(T)\leq\|S-T\|+\epsilon.

As this is true for all
\epsilon>0
, we have shown

s_{\min}(S)-s_{\min}(T)\leq\|S-T\|.

We are done, by symmetry.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Unlike the case in finite dimensions,
s_{\min}(T)\neq 0
does not
always imply
T
is invertible. However, when
T^{*}=T
it is true
that
s_{\min}(T)\neq 0
if and only if
T^{-1}
exists.

Lemma 4.6
.

If
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
S:\mathcal{H}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{3}

are bounded linear operators then

\displaystyle s_{\min}(ST)

\displaystyle\geq s_{\min}(S)s_{\min}(T)

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle s_{\min}(T)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0}\frac{\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}.

If
T
has no kernel then, for every nonzero
\boldsymbol{v}
, we have

\frac{\|ST\boldsymbol{v}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}=\frac{\|ST\boldsymbol{v}\|}{\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|}\frac{\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|}\geq s_{\min}(S)s_{\min}(T)

so

\displaystyle s_{\min}(ST)

\displaystyle\geq s_{\min}(S)s_{\min}(T).

If
T
has a kernel then
ST
has a kernel so both
s_{\min}(ST)

and
s_{\min}(T)
are zero and we are done for trivial reasons.
∎

Remark
.

One might think that
T
and
T^{*}
and

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&T^{*}\\
T&0\end{array}\right]

all have the same
s_{\min}
. After all, this is true in the finite-dimensional case. However, if we let
T
be the forward
shift, then
s_{\min}(T)=1

since

\left\|T\boldsymbol{v}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|

for all
\boldsymbol{v}
(that is,
T
is an isometry). In contrast,
there is a unit vector
\boldsymbol{v}
so that that the backwards
shift sends
\boldsymbol{v}
so zero, so that
\left\|T^{*}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|=0
.
Thus also

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&T^{*}\\
T&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\boldsymbol{v}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\end{array}\right]

so we can conclude

s_{\min}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&T^{*}\\
T&0\end{array}\right]=s_{\min}(T^{*})=0.

Finally, notice that
\sqrt{T^{*}T}=I
while
\sqrt{TT^{*}}

has a kernel, so

s_{\min}\left(|T^{*}|\right)\neq s_{\min}\left(|T|\right)

in infinite dimensions.

For two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{i},\ i=1,2,

let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
denote
the set of all bounded linear operators from

\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}.

Recall that for
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}),

|T|\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
denotes the modulus of
T
,
that is the unique positive semi-definite operator so that

|T|^{2}=T^{*}T
and
\ker(T)=\ker(|T|).

Lemma 4.7
.

If
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}

is a bounded linear operator then

s_{\min}(T)=s_{\min}(|T|)=\sqrt{s_{\min}(T^{*}T)}.

Proof.

Since
\|T\boldsymbol{v}\|=\|\,|T|\boldsymbol{v}\,\|
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},

the consequence that
s_{\min}(T)=s_{\min}(|T|)
is straightforward. The equality of
s_{\min}(|T|)
with
\sqrt{s_{\min}(T^{*}T)}
follows from Lemma
4.2
and the spectral mapping
theorem.

∎

With these lemmas, we can now extend some of the result on Clifford spectrum and pseudospectrum that have
been previously worked out in the case of finite matrices
[
7
,
21
]
.
Theorem
4.8

is a generalization of Lemma 2.3 of
[
26
]
. That result handles one general operator, or equivalently two Hermitian operators.

Theorem 4.8
.

Suppose
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are Hermitian elements of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
. The Clifford pseudospectrum
of
\mathbb{A}
is Lipschitz, specifically with

(4.2)

\left|\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{C}(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})-\mu_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{C}(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})\right|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\|

where the norm is taken to be the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover

(4.3)

\left|\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{C}(\mathbb{A})-\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|\right|\leq\left\|L_{\boldsymbol{0}}(\mathbb{A})\right\|.

Proof.

These statements all follow from the fact that
s_{\min}(A)
is Lipshitz in the operator norm (Lemma
4.4
). This result is a generalization of results in Section 7 of
[
21
]
, and is proven by related methods.
The essential calculations are

\sigma\left(L_{\boldsymbol{0}}\left(\lambda_{1}I,\cdots,\lambda_{d}I\right)\right)=\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right\|,-\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right\|\right\}

and its corollary

\left\|L_{\boldsymbol{0}}\left(\lambda_{1}I,\cdots,\lambda_{d}I\right)\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right\|.

For (
4.2
) note that

L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\right)-L_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\right)=L_{\boldsymbol{0}}\left((\nu_{1}-\lambda_{1})I,\cdots,(\nu_{d}-\lambda_{d})I\right)

which implies that

\left\|L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\right)-L_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\right)\right\|=\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\|.

For (
4.3
), we know that

s_{\min}\left(L_{\boldsymbol{0}}\left(\lambda_{1}I,\cdots,\lambda_{d}I\right)\right)=\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|

and

\left\|L_{\boldsymbol{0}}\left(\lambda_{1}I,\cdots,\lambda_{d}I\right)+L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\right)\right\|=\left\|L_{\boldsymbol{0}}\left(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\right)\right\|.

∎

Corollary 4.9
.

The Clifford spectrum for a
d
-tuple of Hermitian elements of a
C^{*}
-algebra is always compact.

Proof.

Since Lipshcitz implies continuous we get continuity from Equation
4.2
. Equation
4.3
tells us that then

\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|>\|L_{\boldsymbol{0}}(\mathbb{A})\right\|

we cannot have
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{C}(\mathbb{A})=0
. Therefore
the Clifford spectrum of
(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
is a closed subset of the ball at the origin of radius

\left\|L_{\boldsymbol{0}}(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})\right\|
.
∎

5.
Other forms of multivariable pseudospectrum

5.1.
The quadratic pseudospectrum and more

There are many ways to define a pseudospectrum for

\mathbb{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
that are Hermitian. If these are operators on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, Mumford
[
24
]

suggests associating to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
the number

(5.1)

\min\left\{\left.\max\left\|A_{j}\boldsymbol{v}-\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|\,\right|\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=1\text{ and }\textup{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}[A_{j}]=\lambda_{j}\text{ for all }j\right\}

where
\textup{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}[A_{j}]=\left\langle A_{j}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle

is the expectation for the observable
A_{j}
when the system is
in state
\boldsymbol{v}
. This is expected
[
21
, §1]
to be a very difficult, but important, minimization problem related to joint measurement.

Fortunately, there
is a slight modification that can be made to Equation
5.1
that turns it into something very computable, as the minimization problem leads to a number equal to the smallest spectral value of a Hermitian matrix. The
quadratic pseudospectrum

[
7
]
is defined as

(5.2)

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{Q}}(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})=\min\left\{\left.\sqrt{\sum_{j}\left\|A_{j}\boldsymbol{v}-\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}}\,\right|\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=1\right\}.

The practical way to compute this is to use the known equality
[
7
]

(5.3)

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{Q}}(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})=\sqrt{s_{\min}\left(Q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\mathbb{A}\right)\right)}

where

(5.4)

Q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(\mathbb{A}\right)=\sum_{j}\left(A_{j}-\lambda_{j}\right)^{2}.

This can be defined also in the case where the
A_{j}
are Hermitian elements in
a unital
C^{*}
-algebra.

We can also select a bump function
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

with
0\leq g\leq 1
and
g(0)=1
, some manner of a windowing function.
Lin (in [19]) looks at

1-\left\|W(\mathbb{A})\right\|

where

W(\mathbb{A})=g\left(A_{1}-\lambda_{1}\right)g\left(A_{2}-\lambda_{2}\right)\cdots g\left(A_{d}-\lambda_{d}\right).

Notice there needs to be some choice of the order in the product.
This is related to the windowed LDOS

[
22
]
that looks at the trace of

\left(W(\mathbb{A})\right)^{*}W(\mathbb{A})
. It can be difficult to compute
g\left(A_{j}-\lambda_{1}\right)
, but it should be should be possible to numerically compute this in settings
where some of the matrices are diagonal.
For theoretical calculations, this can be a practical form of
pseudospectrum
[
19
]
.

5.2.
Relation between the Clifford and quadratic pseudospectrum

It is expected that all variations on multivariable operator pseudospectrum will be related somehow. Indeed, they should all be equal in the commutative case, and be close when the
A_{j}
have small commutators. This is known in one case, in that the Clifford and quadratic are
of bounded distance apart. That is, we know
[
7
]
that

(5.5)

\left|\biggl(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{C}\left(\mathbb{A}\right)\biggr)^{2}-\biggl(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{Q}\left(\mathbb{A}\right)\biggr)^{2}\right|\leq\sum_{j<k}\|[A_{j},A_{k}]\|.

It is becoming clear that one cannot make do with a single form of pseudospectrum when studying physical systems.
The Clifford pseudospectrum is related to
K
-theory and
is finding applications in photonics
[
4
,
6
]
, acoustics
[
9
]
, aperiodic structures
[
12
]
, nonlinear systems
[
30
]
, and even non-Hermitian systems
[
3
,
8
,
11
,
20
,
25
]
.
On the other hand, showing that points in the Clifford spectrum correspond to states approximately localized in energy and position seems to require using something like Equation
5.5
.

5.3.
Symmetry in the quadratic pseudospectrum

There are symmetry properties in the Clifford spectrum that must follow from symmetry properties in
\mathbb{A}

[
5
]
. Here we prove the same implication, but for the quadratic case.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)
is a
d
-tuple of Hermitian
elements in the unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and that
U\in O(d)
.
Suppose
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
d

element

\hat{A}_{j}=\sum_{s}u_{js}A_{s}

are also Hermitian, and

\mu_{U\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{Q}\left(\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{d}\right)=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{Q}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right).

Proof.

The elements
\hat{A}_{j}
can be shown to be Hermitian, by the same
proof
[
5
, §2]
as for the matrix case.
We find

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\hat{A}_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\sum_{s}\sum_{j}u_{jr}u_{js}A_{r}A_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}A_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}A_{j}^{2}.

Setting

\tilde{\lambda}_{j}=\sum_{s}u_{js}\lambda_{s}.

we next compute

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}\hat{A}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\sum_{s}\sum_{j}u_{js}u_{jr}\lambda_{s}A_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}A_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}A_{j}

Finally

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\sum_{s}\sum_{j}u_{rj}u_{sj}\lambda_{r}\lambda_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}^{2}.

Thus

\displaystyle Q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\left(\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\hat{A}_{j}^{2}-2\sum_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}\hat{A}_{j}+\sum_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}^{2}I

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}A_{j}^{2}-2\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}A_{j}+\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}I

\displaystyle=Q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right).

∎

If
Q
is a unitary and we let

\mathbb{B}=(QA_{1}Q^{*},\dots,QA_{1}Q^{*})

then
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{B})
is unitarily equivalent to
L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})
and
Q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{B})
is unitarily equivalent to
Q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbb{A})
. Thus we can use Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
5.1

to obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose
\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)
are Hermitian elements in the unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and that
U\in O(d)
.
Let

\hat{A}_{j}=\sum_{s}u_{js}A_{s}.

If there exists a unitary
Q
in
\mathcal{A}
such that

Q\hat{A_{j}}Q^{*}=A_{j}
for all

then

\mu^{\textup{C}}_{U\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)=\mu^{\textup{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)

and

\mu^{\textup{Q}}_{U\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right)=\mu^{\textup{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left(A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\right).

Theorem 6.2
.

For the universal pair of unitary operators of order two, so

7.
A hemisphere as Clifford spectrum

Example
3.2
tells us we can find essentially any

In the finite-dimensional case we need to introduce noncommutativity to get Clifford spectrum in dimension greater that zero. a_{2n}=-\frac{z}{x}a_{2n-1}+\frac{1}{x}a_{2n-2}

and

\displaystyle a_{2n+1}

\displaystyle=-za_{2n}+xa_{2n-1}

\displaystyle=-z\left(-\frac{z}{x}a_{2n-1}+\frac{1}{x}a_{2n-2}\right)+xa_{2n-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{z^{2}}{x}+x\right)a_{2n-1}-\frac{z}{x}a_{2n-2}.

If we set

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{x}&-\frac{z}{x}\\
-\frac{z}{x}&\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}}{x}\end{array}\right]

the relation on
\boldsymbol{a}
is

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{2n}\\
a_{2n+1}\end{array}\right]=M\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{2n-2}\\
a_{2n-1}\end{array}\right].

We also know

a_{2}=\frac{1-z}{x}

and

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=x+(1-z)a_{2}

\displaystyle=x+(1-z)\frac{1-z}{x}

so have an initial condition

\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{2}\\
a_{3}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1-z}{x}\\
x+\frac{\left(1-z\right)^{2}}{x}\end{array}\right].

Now let’s pause to take care of the case of
z=1
and
x>0
. In
that situation, we have

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{x}&-\frac{1}{x}\\
-\frac{1}{x}&\frac{x^{2}+1}{x}\end{array}\right]

so that

\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
x\end{array}\right]

is not going to be an eigenvalue. Thus, the only Clifford spectrum
on the line
z=1
is the point
(x,0,z)=(0,0,1)
that we found above.

We need to know about the eigenvalues of the real symmetric matrix

M
. a see that

\textup{Tr}(M)=\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}+1}{x}

and
\det(M)=1
we find that the eigenvalues of the real symmetric
matrix
M
are

\frac{1}{2}\left(\textup{Tr}(M)\pm\sqrt{\left(\textup{Tr}(M)\right)^{2}-4}\right).

We get a double real eigenvalue when

\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}+1}{x}=2

but this leads to
x=1
and
z=0
which we have excluded. We get
two complex eigenvalues when

\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}+1}{x}<2

which is equivalent to

\left(x-1\right)^{2}+z^{2}<0

and that cannot happen. What we need look at then is where
M
has
two positive eigenvalues, one outside of
(0,1)
and one inside.
The only way to have
\boldsymbol{a}
square-summable is if the initial
vector lands in the eigenspace for the eigenvalue closer to
0
.

Let us find out when
M\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
is parallel to
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
.
Since

M\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left(1-z\right)-z\left(x^{2}+z^{2}-2z+1\right)\\
-z\left(1-z\right)+\left(x^{2}+z^{2}\right)\left(x^{2}+z^{2}-2z+1\right)\end{array}\right]

we need to solve

\frac{x^{-2}\left(\left(1-z\right)-z\left(x^{2}+z^{2}-2z+1\right)\right)}{x^{-1}\left(1-z\right)}-\frac{x^{-2}\left(-z\left(1-z\right)+\left(x^{2}+z^{2}\right)\left(x^{2}+z^{2}-2z+1\right)\right)}{x^{-1}\left(x^{2}+z^{2}-2z+1\right)}=0.

For
x\neq 0
and
z\neq 1
this is equivalent to

-\frac{x^{4}-z^{4}+3z^{3}-4z^{2}+3z-1}{x^{2}+z^{2}-2z+1}=0

so we get the curve

e(x,z)=0
where

e(x,z)=x^{4}-z^{4}+3z^{3}-4z^{2}+3z-1.

? First, we deal with the case where

will be positive when

As

8.
Momentum and Position – an unbounded example

We hope to push the idea of joint pseudospectrum into the realm of unbounded operators. Here we
consider a classic example, and do not attempt a general theory. The example is a classic interpretation of position and momentum.

We will work on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and in particular
on the subspace
\mathcal{S}
of Schwartz functions, where we conisder only
f
for which
x^{m}D^{n}f(x)

is always bounded. Here we use

D(f)(x)=\frac{d}{dx}(f(x))

to denote the standard differential operator.
We consider two unbounded operators,
P,Q:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}

where
P=-iD
and
Q(f)(x)=xf(x).

These are both symmetric (but probably not self-adjoint, so
Q\subseteq Q^{*}

is all that is claimed, due to the small domain).

We can consider

Q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)=\left(P-\lambda_{1}I\right)^{2}+\left(Q-\lambda_{2}I\right)^{2}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}.

and define

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)=\sqrt{\min_{\|f\|=1}\|Q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)(f)\|}.

The Clifford pseudospectrum is also easy to define,
as

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{C}}(P,Q)=\min_{\|f\|=1}\|L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)(f)\|

where

L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\left(P-\lambda_{1}I\right)-i\left(Q-\lambda_{2}I\right)\\
\left(P-\lambda_{1}I\right)+i\left(Q-\lambda_{2}I\right)&0\end{array}\right]

is taken to be an operator on
{S}\oplus\mathcal{S}
.
In
[
10
]
, this example of Clifford pseudospectrum was computed, with

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{C}}(P,Q)\equiv 0

being the result.

We will need some alternate characterizations of the quadratic pseudospectrum of
P
and
Q
, as proven in
[
7
]
for the matrix case.
We can consider

M_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}P-\lambda_{1}I\\
Q-\lambda_{2}I\end{array}\right]:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}\oplus\mathcal{S}.

and can check that

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{Q}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\min_{\|f\|=1}\left\|\left(M_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)(f)\right)^{*}\left(M_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)(f)\right)\right\|}

\displaystyle=\min_{\|f\|=1}\|M_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(P,Q)(f)\|

\displaystyle=\min_{\|f\|=1}\sqrt{\left\|Pf-\lambda_{1}f\right\|^{2}+\left\|Qf-\lambda_{2}f\right\|^{2}}.

Since

\left\|A\boldsymbol{v}-\lambda\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{2}A+\left(\textup{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(A)-\lambda\right)^{2}

for any Hermitian operator, we can find lower bounds on the quadratic
pseudospectrum by utilizing results on lower bounds on the sum of
uncertainty. For example, there are lower bounds on
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{2}A+\Delta_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{2}B

in
[
23
]
. For simplicity, we give a simplification of a proof from

[
23
]
and directly prove the following.

Lemma 8.1
.

If
\boldsymbol{v}
is a unit
vector and
A
and
B
are Hermitian operators then

\left\|A\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}+\left\|B\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}\geq\left|\left\langle i\left[A,B\right]\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\right|.

Proof.

We first calculate

\left\|\left(A\mp iB\right)\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}=\left\|A\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}+\left\|B\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}\mp\left\langle i\left[A,B\right]\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle

so we have

\left\|A\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}+\left\|B\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}=\left\|\left(A\mp iB\right)\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}\pm\left\langle i\left[A,B\right]\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle.

An easy consequence is then

\left\|A\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}+\left\|B\boldsymbol{v}\right\|^{2}\geq\pm\left\langle i\left[A,B\right]\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle.

∎

Theorem 8.2
.

For
P
and
Q
as above,

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)=1

for all
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

Proof.

We start by establishing that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)

is constant. The shift operator
f(x)\mapsto f(x-\lambda_{2})
gives a
unitary
U
that takes
\mathcal{S}
onto
\mathcal{S}
. Since

\displaystyle U^{*}PU(f(x))

\displaystyle=-iU^{*}(f^{\prime}(x-\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle=-if^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=P(f(x))

and

\displaystyle U^{*}QU(f(x))

\displaystyle=U^{*}(xf(x-\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle=(x+\lambda_{2})f(x)

we find

U^{*}PU=P
and
U^{*}QU=Q+\lambda_{2}
.
Therefore
Q_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(P,Q)

is unitarily equivalent to

Q_{(\lambda_{1},0)}(P,Q)
and so

\mu_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)=\mu_{(\lambda_{1},0)}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q).

Likewise, we can use
f(x)\mapsto e^{2\pi i\lambda_{1}x}f(x)
so set up
a unitary that shows

\mu_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)=\mu_{(0,\lambda_{2})}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q).

Taken together, these facts prove that the quadratic
pseudospectrum is constant.

Since
\left[P,Q\right](f)=-if
,
Lemma
8.1
says

\left\|Pf\right\|^{2}+\left\|Qf\right\|^{2}\geq 1.

Therefore
\mu_{(\boldsymbol{0})}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)\geq 1
and so

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)\geq 1

Now consider

g(x)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}
.
Clearly

Q^{2}(g)(x)=x^{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}=x^{2}g(x).

We also find

\displaystyle P^{2}(g)(x)

\displaystyle=P\left(ixe^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-i\frac{d}{dx}\left(ixe^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\left(xe^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}-x^{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}

\displaystyle=g(x)-x^{2}g(x)

and so find

\left(P^{2}+Q^{2}\right)g=g.

Having found an eigenvector
Q_{(0,0)}(P,Q)=P^{2}+Q^{2}
we have proven

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textup{Q}}(P,Q)\leq 1.

∎
