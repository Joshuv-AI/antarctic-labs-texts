Novel mass-based multigrid relaxation schemes for the Stokes equations
Yunhui He

Abstract
In this work, we propose three novel block-structured multigrid relaxation schemes based on distributive relaxation, Braess-Sarazin relaxation, and Uzawa relaxation, for solving the Stokes equations discretized by the mark-and-cell scheme. In our earlier work [ 11 ] , we discussed these three types of relaxation schemes, where the weighted Jacobi iteration is used for inventing the Laplacian involved in the Stokes equations. In [ 11 ] , we show that the optimal smoothing factor is 3 5 \frac{3}{5} for distributive weighted-Jacobi relaxation and inexact Braess-Sarazin relaxation, and is 3 5 \sqrt{\frac{3}{5}} for σ \sigma -Uzawa relaxation. Here, we propose mass-based approximation inside of these three relaxations, where mass matrix Q Q obtained from bilinear finite element method is directly used to approximate to the inverse of scalar Laplacian operator instead of using Jacobi iteration. Using local Fourier analysis, we theoretically derive the optimal smoothing factors for the resulting three relaxation schemes. Specifically, mass-based distributive relaxation, mass-based Braess-Sarazin relaxation, and mass-based σ \sigma -Uzawa relaxation have optimal smoothing factor 1 3 \frac{1}{3} , 1 3 \frac{1}{3} and 1 3 \sqrt{\frac{1}{3}} , respectively. Note that the mass-based relaxation schemes do not cost more than the original ones using Jacobi iteration. Another superiority is that there is no need to compute the inverse of a matrix. These new relaxation schemes are appealing.



Keywords.

Stokes equations, local Fourier analysis, staggered finite-difference method, mass matrix, block-structured relaxation, multigrid

2
Discretization and relaxation schemes

In this work, we consider the following Stokes equations in two dimensions

\displaystyle-\triangle\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f},

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{u}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}
is the velocity vector, and
p
is the scalar pressure of a viscous fluid.

We apply the standard staggered finite-difference discretization to (
1
) and (
2
), known as the mark-and-cell (MAC) scheme, see
[
18
]
, where the unknowns
u,v,p
, are placed in different locations, see Figure
1
.
The velocity
u
is located on the middle points of vertical edges (
\Box
), the velocity
v
is located on middle points of horizontal edges (
\lozenge
), and the pressure
p
is located in the center of each cell (
\bigstar
).

Figure 1:
The staggered location of unknowns:
\Box-u,\,\,\lozenge-v,\,\,\bigstar-p

In this paper, we deal with uniform meshes with
h_{x}=h_{y}=h
. The discrete momentum equations is given as
[
18
]

\displaystyle-\triangle_{h}u_{h}+(\partial_{x})_{h/2}\,p_{h}

\displaystyle=f_{1,h},

\displaystyle-\triangle_{h}v_{h}+(\partial_{y})_{h/2}\,p_{h}

\displaystyle=f_{2,h}.

The Laplacian
-\triangle_{h}
is discretized by the standard five-point scheme as follows

\displaystyle(\partial_{x})_{h/2}\,p_{h}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\bigg(p_{h}\big(x+h/2,y\big)-p_{h}\big(x-h/2,y\big)\bigg),

\displaystyle(\partial_{y})_{h/2}\,p_{h}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\bigg(p_{h}\big(x,y+h/2\big)-p_{h}\big(x,y-h/2\big)\bigg).

The discrete conservation of mass equation is given by

(\partial_{x})_{h/2}\,u_{h}(x,y)+(\partial_{y})_{h/2}\,v_{h}(x,y)=0.

The staggered discretization of the Stokes equations then reads
[
18
]

\mathcal{L}_{h}=\begin{pmatrix}-\triangle_{h}&0&(\partial_{x})_{h/2}\\
0&-\triangle_{h}&(\partial_{y})_{h/2}\\
-(\partial_{x})_{h/2}&-(\partial_{y})_{h/2}&0\end{pmatrix},

(3)

where the computational stencils are given by

-\triangle_{h}=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}&-1&\\
-1&4&-1\\
&-1&\end{bmatrix},\quad(\partial_{x})_{h}=\frac{1}{h}\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad(\partial_{y})_{h}=\frac{1}{h}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
-1\end{bmatrix}.

2.1
Relaxation schemes

The resulting linear system of (
1
) and (
2
) is

\mathcal{L}_{h}x=\begin{pmatrix}A&B^{T}\\
B&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\\
p_{h}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}\\
0\end{pmatrix}=b,

(4)

where
A
is the discretized vector Laplacian,
B
is the negative of the discrete divergence operator, and
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{h}\\
v_{h}\end{pmatrix}
.

To solve (
4
), we consider the following relaxation scheme: given a current approximation
x_{k}
, we updates this approximation via

\hat{x}_{k}=x_{k}-\omega M^{-1}(b-\mathcal{L}_{h}x_{k})=x_{k}-\omega\delta x,

(5)

where
M
is an approximation to
\mathcal{L}_{h}
, and
\delta x=M^{-1}(b-\mathcal{L}_{h}x_{k})
. Then, the error-propagation for the relaxation scheme (
5
) is

\mathcal{S}_{h}=I-\omega{M}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{h}.

(6)

The spectral radius of
\mathcal{S}_{h}
often determines the convergence speed in the multigrid methods. In this work, we will employ local Fourier analysis to minimize the spectral radius of
\mathcal{S}_{h}
, which will be presented in Section
3
.

Note that
[
11
]
discussed three block-structured relaxation schemes, that is, distributive weighted-Jacobi relaxation, Braess-Sarazin relaxation, and Uzawa-type
relaxation within multigrid methods for solving (
4
). We first give a brief introduction to these three relaxations, then propose our improved relaxation schemes. For comparison purposes, we borrow some notations from
[
11
]
. For simplicity, throughout the rest of this paper, we drop the subscript

h
, except when necessary for clarity.

Distributive relaxation
: We detail the idea of distributive relaxation
[
6
]
as follows. To relax the equation
\mathcal{L}x=b
, we consider a transformed system
\mathcal{K}\hat{x}=\mathcal{L}P\hat{x}=b
, where
P\hat{x}=x
and
P
is chosen so that
\mathcal{K}

is suitable for decoupled relaxation. For the Stokes equations,
P
is taken to be

P=\begin{pmatrix}I_{h}&0&(\partial_{x})_{h/2}\\
0&I_{h}&(\partial_{y})_{h/2}\\
0&0&\triangle_{h}\end{pmatrix},

whose discrete matrix form is

P=\begin{pmatrix}I&B^{T}\\
0&-A_{p}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
A_{p}
is the the standard 5-point stencil of the Laplacian operator discretized at cell centers. Accordingly, the distributed operator has the form

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}P=\begin{pmatrix}-\triangle_{h}&0&0\\
0&-\triangle_{h}&0\\
-(\partial_{x})_{h/2}&-(\partial_{y})_{h/2}&-\triangle_{h}\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Then, we can apply block relaxation to the transformed system. For instance, distributive Gauss-Seidel relaxation
[
6
,
16
]
(DGS) is well-known to be highly efficient for the MAC discretization. Distributive weighted-Jacobi (DWJ) relaxation has been discussed in
[
11
]
, where one needs to solve the following system

M_{D}\delta\hat{x}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{D}C&0\\
B&\alpha_{D}E\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\delta\mathcal{\hat{U}}\\
\delta\hat{p}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r_{\mathcal{U}}\\
r_{p}\end{pmatrix},

(8)

where
C={\rm diag}(A)
, and
E={\rm diag}(A_{p})
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{\hat{U}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha_{D}C)^{-1}r_{\mathcal{U}},

\displaystyle\delta\hat{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\alpha_{D}E\big)^{-1}(r_{p}-B\delta\mathcal{\hat{U}}).

Using
\delta x=\mathcal{P}\delta\hat{x}
gives

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{\hat{U}}+B^{T}\delta\hat{p},

\displaystyle\delta p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-A_{p}\delta\hat{p}.

The error propagation operator for the distributive relaxation scheme is
I-\omega_{D}PM^{-1}_{D}\mathcal{L}
.

Braess-Sarazin relaxation
: In Braess-Sarazin relaxation,
M
is given by

M_{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha C&B^{T}\\
B&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(9)

where
C
is an approximation of
A
, whose inverse is easy to apply, and
\alpha>0
is a chosen relaxation parameter.

Using
M=M_{B}
in (
5
), one needs to compute the update
\delta x=(\delta\mathcal{U},\delta p)
in (
5
) by the following two stages

\displaystyle(BC^{-1}B^{T})\delta p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle BC^{-1}r_{\mathcal{U}}-\alpha r_{p},

(10)

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}C^{-1}(r_{\mathcal{U}}-B^{T}\delta p),

where
(r_{\mathcal{U}},r_{p})=b-\mathcal{L}x_{k}
.

In
[
11
]
, we considered
C={\rm diag}(A)
. In practice, an approximate solve for (
10
) is sufficient
[
23
]
, such as using a simple sweep of a Gauss-Seidel
[
15
]
or weighted Jacobi iteration
[
11
]
.

\sigma
-Uzawa relaxation

[
11
]
: Uzawa-type relaxation is a popular family of algorithms for solving saddle-point systems
[
8
,
14
]
, which can be treated as a lower triangular approximation, where
M
is taken to be

M_{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha C&0\\
B&-D\\
\end{pmatrix},

(11)

where
\alpha C
is an approximation of
A
, for example,
C={\rm diag}(A)

[
11
]
, and
D
is an approximation to the Schur complement
BC^{-1}B^{T}
. One possible choice of
D
is
D=\sigma^{-1}I
, see
[
11
]
. The resulting relaxation is referred to
\sigma
-Uzawa relaxation.

In
\sigma
-Uzawa relaxation,
\delta x
in (
5
) with
M=M_{U}
defined in (
11
) is equivalent to computing updates as

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha C)^{-1}r_{\mathcal{U}},

\displaystyle\delta p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{-1}(B\delta\mathcal{U}-r_{p}).

Note that, in all these relaxation schemes, the key point is to find a good approximation,
C
, to
A
, and we particularly need
C^{-1}
. This inspires us to consider an approximation of
C^{-1}
directly. In our recently work
[
10
]
, we have shown that the mass matrix
Q
derived from bilinear finite elements is a very good approximation to the inverse of scalar Laplacian discretized by finite difference method. Therefore, we will consider

C^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}Q&0\\
0&Q\end{pmatrix}

(12)

for the above three relaxation schemes. We call the resulting schemes
mass-based relaxations
, for example, mass-based Braess-Sarazin relaxation.

3
Smoothing analysis

LFA
[
18
]
is a very useful tool for analyzing the convergence speed of multigrid methods, by examining the spectral radius of
symbol
of the underlying operator. In general, the smoothing factor,
\mu
, of LFA offers sharp predictions of actual multigrid convergence. In this work, we employ LFA to examine the block-structured multigrid relaxation schemes proposed in Section
2
by exploring the LFA smoothing factor. We consider geometric multigrid methods for staggered discretizations with standard coarsening, that is, we construct a sequence of coarse grids by doubling the mesh size in each spatial direction. High and low frequencies for standard coarsening are given by

\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm low}}=\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}=\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)^{2}\bigg\backslash\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}.

In the following,
\widetilde{L}
denotes the symbol of operator
L
. We omit the details on how to compute the symbols. For more details, see
[
18
]
and
[
11
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The smoothing factor for the error-propagation for the relaxation scheme
\mathcal{S}
, see (
6
), is defined as

\mu_{{\rm loc}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}\left\{\left|\lambda(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right|\,\,\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{p}
is algorithmic parameters, and
\lambda\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)
denotes the eigenvalue of symbol
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

Often, one can minimize
\mu_{{\rm loc}}(\boldsymbol{p})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{p}
to obtain fast convergence speed. Thus, we define the optimal smoothing factor as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

We define the optimal smoothing factor for the error-propagation for the relaxation scheme
\mathcal{S}
as

\mu_{{\rm opt}}=\min_{\boldsymbol{p}\in\Theta}{\mu_{{\rm loc}}}(\boldsymbol{p}),

where
\Theta
is the set of allowable parameters.

We note that
\boldsymbol{p}
may contains many components depending on the selection of the relaxation scheme. In our setting,
\boldsymbol{p}
has dimension one, two or three.

The symbol of operator
\mathcal{L}
, see (
3
), is given by
[
11
]

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})&0&i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\\
0&4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})&i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}\\
-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}&-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
i^{2}=-1
and

m(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{4-2\cos\theta_{1}-2\cos\theta_{2}}{4}=\sin^{2}(\theta_{1}/2)+\sin^{2}(\theta_{2}/2).

(13)

Let
A_{s}
be the scalar Laplacian operator. It is easily seen that the symbol of the scalar Laplacian is

\widetilde{A}_{s}=\frac{4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{h^{2}}.

(14)

The error-propagation symbol for a relaxation scheme,
\mathcal{S}
, applied to MAC scheme is

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\theta})=I-\omega\widetilde{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},

where
\boldsymbol{p}
represents parameters within
M
and
\omega
.

Before we analyze the smoothing factor with
C^{-1}
being the vector mass matrix defined in (
12
), we present some properties for the mass operator
Q
. Recall that the mass matrix stencil using bilinear finite elements in two dimensions is

Q=\frac{h^{2}}{36}\begin{bmatrix}1&4&1\\
4&16&4\\
1&4&1\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

It easily follows that the symbol of
Q
is
\widetilde{Q}=\frac{h^{2}}{9}(2+\cos\theta_{1})(2+\cos\theta_{2})
.
Let us denote

m_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\frac{1}{9}(2+\cos\theta_{1})(2+\cos\theta_{2})\right)^{-1},

(16)

which will be useful for our discussion later. Using (
14
) and (
16
), we have

\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{A}_{s}=\frac{4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{m_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}=:m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(17)

Lemma 3.1
.

[
10
]

For
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{high}
,
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,16/9]=:[\eta_{1},\eta_{2}]
.

We restate the optimal smoothing factor for solving the Poisson problem using the mass-based relaxation
[
10
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If we consider the mass stencil
Q
to approximate the inverse of the scalar Laplacian,
A_{s}
, and
S_{s}=I-\omega QA_{s}
, then the optimal smoothing factor for
S_{s}
is

\mu_{s,\rm opt}=\min_{\omega}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}{|1-\omega\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{A}_{s}|}=\frac{1}{3},

(18)

where the minimum is achieved if and only if
\omega=\omega_{\rm opt}=\frac{3}{4}
.

Lemma
3.2
plays an important role in analyzing the optimal smoothing factor for the mass-based relaxation schemes for the Stokes equations because block relaxation schemes include
S_{s}
as a piece of the overall relaxation. Therefore, the value
\frac{1}{3}
offers a lower bound on the optimal smoothing factor for the mass-based relaxation schemes. To distinguish the mass-based Braess-Sarazin relaxation, distributive relaxation, and Uzawa relaxation, subscripts, such as
B,D
and
U
, will be used in
S,\omega
and
\mu_{\rm opt}
throughout the rest of this paper.

3.1
Mass-based Distributive relaxation

The symbol of operator
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}P
, see (
7
), is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})&0&0\\
0&4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})&0\\
-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}&-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}&4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})\end{pmatrix}.

The symbol of the block relaxation operator (
8
) with
C^{-1}
given by the mass approximation (
12
) is

\widetilde{M}_{D}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{s}&0&0\\
0&m_{s}&0\\
-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}&-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}&m_{s}\end{pmatrix}.

It can be easily shown that all of the eigenvalues of the error-propagation symbol,
\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{D}(\alpha_{D},\omega_{D},\boldsymbol{\theta})=I-\omega_{D}\widetilde{P}\widetilde{M}_{D}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, are

1-\omega_{D}\frac{4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{m_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}.

Theorem 3.1
.

The optimal smoothing factor for
Q
-DR is

\mu_{{\rm opt},D}=\min_{\omega_{D}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}{\big|\lambda(\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{D}(\alpha_{D},\omega_{D},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|}=\frac{1}{3},

where the minimum is uniquely achieved at
\omega_{D}=\frac{3}{4}
.

Proof.

Note that
\frac{4m}{m_{s}}=\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{A}_{s}
. As shown in Lemma
3.2
, we know that
\mu_{\rm opt}(I-\omega_{D}\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{A}_{s})=\frac{1}{3}
with
\omega_{D}=\frac{3}{4}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\min_{\omega_{D}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}\big|\lambda(\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{D}(\omega_{D},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|=\frac{1}{3}.

∎

Compared to Lemma
3.2
, Theorem
3.1
shows that
\frac{1}{3}
is preserved for the block relaxation scheme. A similar property is also observed in
[
11
]
for the distributive weighted Jacobi relaxation for the Stokes equations.

Remark 3.1
.

We point out some existing results for variants of distributive relaxation for MAC discretization of the Stokes equations. For example, LFA predicts a two-grid convergence factor for DGS which requires a single sweep of GS on the velocity equations plus one on the pressure unknowns, of 0.4 using 6-point interpolation and 12-point restriction in
[
15
]
. In
[
11
]
, LFA predicts a two-grid convergence factor for DWJ of 0.6 for both linear and bilinear interpolation.

3.2
Mass-based Braess-Sarazin-type relaxation schemes

Next, we examine Braess-Sarazin-type algorithms, which were originally developed as a relaxation scheme for the Stokes equations
[
4
]
. In practice, (
10
) is not solved exactly, see
[
11
]
. Thus, in the following, we first consider exact solve for (
10
), then inexact one.

Exact Braess-Sarazin relaxation
: In (
9
), for simplicity, we consider
\alpha=1
and
C^{-1}
being the vector mass matrix, see (
12
). Later,
we will see that the choice of
\alpha>0
does not affect the optimal smoothing factor. We first derive the corresponding optimal smoothing factor for the exact mass-based Braess-Sarazin relaxation. We refer to the relaxation scheme as
Q
-BSR.

Theorem 3.2
.

The optimal smoothing factor for
Q
-BSR is

\mu_{{\rm opt},B}=\displaystyle\min_{\omega_{B}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}{\big|\lambda(\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{B}(\omega_{B},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|}=\frac{1}{3},

where the minimum is uniquely achieved at
\omega_{B}=\frac{3}{4}
.

Proof.

Using (
16
), the symbol of
M_{B}
is

\widetilde{M}_{B}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{s}&0&i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\\
0&m_{s}&i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}\\
-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}&-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The symbol of the error-propagation matrix for
Q
-BSR is
\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{B}(\omega_{B},\boldsymbol{\theta})=I-\omega_{B}\widetilde{M}_{B}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
.
To minimize the spectral radius of
\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{B}
with respect to high frequencies, we first compute the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{M}_{B}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. It can be easily shown that the determinant of
\widetilde{L}-\lambda\widetilde{M}_{B}
is

4mm_{s}\big(\lambda-1\big)^{2}\left(\lambda-\frac{4m}{m_{s}}\right).

Thus, the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{M}_{B}^{-1}\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}
are
1,1
, and
\displaystyle\frac{4m}{m_{s}}.

Note that
\frac{4m}{m_{s}}=\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{A}_{s}
. As in Lemma
3.2
, we know that
\mu_{\rm opt}(I-\omega_{B}\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{A}_{s})=\frac{1}{3}
with
\omega_{B}=\frac{3}{4}
. For the eigenvalue 1, we have
|1-\omega_{B}1|<\frac{1}{3}
, where
\omega_{B}=\frac{3}{4}
. This means that

\displaystyle\min_{\omega_{B}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}\big|\lambda(\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{B}(\omega_{B},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|=\frac{1}{3}
with
\omega_{B}=\frac{3}{4}
.
∎

We stress that the optimal smoothing factor

Remark 3.2
.

One can consider weighted mass approximations, that is,
\alpha
is varying in (

). However, from the proof of Theorem
3.2
, the scaling does not affect the optimal smoothing factor, which only changes the optimal damping parameter
\omega_{B}
.

Inexact Braess-Sarazin relaxation
: The exact Braess-Sarazin algorithm requires an exact inversion of the Schur complement (
10
), which is very expensive. Our previous work
[
11
]
shows that inexact BSR can achieve the same convergence factor as the exact BSR for MAC discretization of the Stokes equations.
Therefore, we explore mass-based inexact Braess-Sarazin relaxation, using a single sweep of weighted Jacobi iteration with weight
\omega_{J}
to approximate the solution of (
10
). We are asking whether the inexact version is able to achieve the same smoothing factor of
\frac{1}{3}
. Here, we do not pursue theoretical analysis. Instead, we numerically check the performance of the inexact BSR with the requirement
\frac{\omega_{B}}{\alpha_{B}}=\frac{3}{4}
because
1-\frac{\omega_{B}}{\alpha_{B}}\frac{m}{m_{s}}
is the eigenvalue for both exact and inexact version. We report the results in Section
4
. The results show that inexact BSR of a two-grid method can obtain the same convergence factor,
\frac{1}{3}
, with
\alpha_{B}=1.4,\omega_{B}=\frac{3}{4}\alpha_{B}
and
\omega_{J}=1
. Since the inexact Braess-Sarazin relaxation is simple to implement, avoiding computing the exact inversion of the Schur complement, we recommend inexact mass-based Braess-Sarazin relaxation.

3.3
Mass-based Uzawa-type relaxation

In this subsection, we consider mass-based
\sigma
-Uzawa (
Q
-
\sigma
-Uzawa ), that is, the inverse of
C
in (
11
) is defined as (
12
) and
D=\sigma^{-1}I
. The symbol of
M_{U}
is given by

\widetilde{M}_{U}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{U}m_{s}&0&0\\
0&\alpha_{U}m_{s}&0\\
-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}&-i2h\sin\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}&-\sigma^{-1}h^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Furthermore, the determinant of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}-\lambda\widetilde{M}_{U}
is

\frac{(\alpha_{U}m_{s})^{2}}{\sigma}\big(\lambda-\frac{4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{m_{s}\alpha_{U}}\big)\bigg(\lambda^{2}-\frac{1+\sigma}{\alpha_{U}m_{s}}4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})\lambda+\frac{4m(\boldsymbol{\theta})\sigma}{\alpha_{U}m_{s}}\bigg).

Using
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{4m}{m_{s}}
defined in (
17
), we can rewrite the above determinant as

\frac{(\alpha_{U}m_{s})^{2}}{\sigma}\big(\lambda-\frac{m_{r}}{\alpha_{U}}\big)\bigg(\lambda^{2}-\frac{(1+\sigma)m_{r}}{\alpha_{U}}\lambda+\frac{m_{r}\sigma}{\alpha_{U}}\bigg),

(19)

which will be more convenient for a later discussion. We follow
[
11
]
to carry out the analysis of Uzawa relaxation scheme.

From (
19
), we see one eigenvalue of
\widetilde{M}_{U}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is
\lambda_{*}:=\frac{m_{r}}{\alpha_{U}}
. From Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
, we know the optimal smoothing factor for these modes is

\left|1-\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\eta_{1}\right|=\left|1-\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\eta_{2}\right|=\frac{1}{3},

with
\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}=\frac{3}{4}
.

Next, we explore the optimal smoothing factor for other two eigenvalues of
\widetilde{M}_{U}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
in (
19
). We denote by
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
the roots of

d(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-\frac{(1+\sigma)m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\alpha_{U}}\lambda+\frac{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\sigma}{\alpha_{U}}.

(20)

The discriminant of
d(\lambda)
is

\Delta(\alpha_{U},\sigma)=\frac{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})(1+\sigma)^{2}}{\alpha_{U}^{2}}\bigg(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\bigg).

Let the two roots of
\Delta=0
with respect to
m_{r}
be

m_{1}=0,\quad m_{2}=\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}.

From (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})(1+\sigma)}{\alpha_{U}},

(21)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\sigma}{\alpha_{U}},

(22)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\sigma)m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{2\alpha_{U}}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{m_{2}}{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\right).

(23)

The value
m_{2}
plays an important role in determining the optimal smoothing factor with respect to eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
. There are three cases for
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
:

1.

only complex eigenvalues, that is,
m_{2}>\max{m_{r}}=\eta_{2}
.

2.

only real eigenvalues, that is,
m_{2}<\min{m_{r}}=\eta_{1}
.

3.

giving both real and complex eigenvalues, that is,
\frac{8}{9}=\eta_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq\eta_{2}=\frac{16}{9}
.

To minimize the smoothing factor, we explore the optimal smoothing factor for the above three cases. Then, we choice the best one.

In order to discuss the complex eigenvalues, we set
\Upsilon(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
to be the magnitude of
1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{1}
at frequency

\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Using (
21
) and (
22
) gives

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle=(1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{1})(1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=1-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\omega_{U}+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\omega_{U}^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that
m_{2}\geq\frac{8}{9}
and let
\gamma=\min\{m_{2},16/9\}
. For
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,\gamma]
, eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are complex conjugates and the smoothing factor for these modes is

\mu^{C}=\max_{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,\gamma]}\Upsilon(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))=\sqrt{1+\frac{8\omega_{U}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)}{9\alpha_{U}}}\geq\sqrt{1-\frac{8}{9\gamma}},

where the equality is achieved provided that

\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}.

Proof.

When
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,\gamma]
,
\Delta(\alpha_{U},\sigma)\leq 0
and
|1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{1}|=|1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{2}|=\Upsilon(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
. To guarantee convergence, we require
\Upsilon^{2}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))<1
, which leads to
\frac{\omega_{U}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)}{\alpha_{U}}<0
. Since
\gamma=\min\{m_{2},16/9\}
, we have

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)\gamma

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)m_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2\omega_{U}\sigma}{1+\sigma}\right)^{2}\geq 0.

It follows that

\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)\geq-\frac{1}{\gamma}.

Furthermore,

\max_{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,\gamma]}\Upsilon(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))=\Upsilon(8/9)=\sqrt{1+\frac{8\omega_{U}}{9\alpha_{U}}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)}\geq\sqrt{1-\frac{8}{9\gamma}},

where the equality is achieved if and only if
\frac{\omega_{U}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)}{\alpha_{U}}=\frac{-1}{\gamma}
.
∎

Based on the above results, we are ready to give a lower bound on the smoothing factor for the case that
m_{2}>\eta_{2}=\frac{16}{9}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

If
m_{2}=\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}>\eta_{2}=\frac{16}{9}
, then the optimal smoothing factor for
Q
-
\sigma
-Uzawa relaxation is larger than
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.3
with
\gamma=\frac{16}{9}
, we know the smoothing factor for the complex modes is

\mu^{C}=\Upsilon(8/9)\geq\sqrt{1-\frac{8}{9\gamma}}=\sqrt{1-\frac{8}{9\cdot\frac{16}{9}}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},

(24)

where the equality is achieved on condition that
\Upsilon^{2}(\eta_{2})=0
. Next, we show that
\Upsilon^{2}(\eta_{2})>0
.
We proceed to prove this by contradiction. Supposing that
\Upsilon^{2}(\eta_{2})=1+\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)\frac{16}{9}=0
, we have
\frac{\alpha_{U}}{\omega_{U}(\sigma+1-\omega_{U}\sigma)}=\frac{16}{9}
. Using
m_{2}=\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}>\frac{16}{9}
gives

\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}>\frac{\alpha_{U}}{\omega_{U}(\sigma+1-\omega_{U}\sigma)},

which is equivalent to

\bigg(\frac{\omega_{U}\sigma}{1+\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)^{2}<0.

Thus,
\mu^{C}
is large than
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
in (
24
).
∎

Next, we explore the case where
m_{2}\leq\eta_{2}=\frac{16}{9}
. For
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[m_{2},16/9]
, the two roots,
\lambda_{1,2}
, are real. Using (
23
) gives

\displaystyle|1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{1}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{2\alpha_{U}}m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{m_{2}}{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\bigg)\right|,

\displaystyle|1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{2}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{2\alpha_{U}}m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{m_{2}}{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\bigg)\right|.

Define

\displaystyle\chi_{+}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{m_{2}}{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\right),

\displaystyle\chi_{-}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{m_{2}}{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\right).

It can be shown that function
\chi_{+}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
is an increasing function of
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
for
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[m_{2},16/9]
, and
\chi_{+}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
is a decreasing function, see
[
11
]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\chi_{+}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))_{\rm max}

\displaystyle=\chi_{+}(16/9)=\frac{8}{9}\left(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{9m_{2}}{16}}\right)=:\chi_{1},

\displaystyle\chi_{-}(m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))_{\rm min}

\displaystyle=\chi_{-}(16/9)=\frac{8}{9}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{9m_{2}}{16}}\right)=:\chi_{2}.

It follows that the smoothing factor for the two real eigenvalues in this case is

\displaystyle\mu^{R}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}\big|\lambda(\mathcal{\widetilde{S}}_{U}(\alpha_{U},\omega_{U},\sigma,\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|,

\displaystyle={\rm max}\bigg\{\big|1-\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\chi_{1}\big|,\big|1-\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\chi_{2}\big|\bigg\}.

The above expression can be further simplified to

\mu^{R}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\chi_{1}-1,\quad{\rm if}\quad\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\geq\frac{9}{8}.\\
&1-\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\chi_{2},\quad{\rm if}\quad\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\leq\frac{9}{8}.\end{aligned}\right.\\

(25)

We are now able to give a lower bound on the optimal smoothing factor for the case where
m_{2}\leq\frac{8}{9}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

If
m_{2}=\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\leq\frac{8}{9}
, then the optimal smoothing factor for
Q
-
\sigma
-Uzawa relaxation is not less than
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
.

Proof.

When
m_{2}\leq\frac{8}{9}
,
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are all real. From (
25
), the smoothing factor for
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,16/9]
is given by

\mu^{R}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{8(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{9\alpha_{U}}(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2})-1,\quad{\rm if}\quad\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\geq\frac{9}{8}.\\
&1-\frac{8(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{9\alpha_{U}}(1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}),\quad{\rm if}\quad\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\leq\frac{9}{8}.\end{aligned}\right.\\

Note that when
\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha}=\frac{9}{8}
,
\mu^{R}
achieves its minimum value of
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
. However, the conditions that
\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha}=\frac{9}{8}
and
m_{2}=\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\leq\frac{8}{9}
might not be satisfied at the same time. This implies that the optimal smoothing factor may be larger than
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
.
∎

From previous discussions, we know that when
m_{2}>\frac{16}{9}
or
m_{2}\leq\frac{8}{9}
, the optimal smoothing factor is at least
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
. Next, we will show that the global optimal smoothing factor for all choices of

occurs when
\frac{8}{9}\leq m_{2}\leq\frac{16}{9}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

The optimal smoothing factor for
Q
-
\sigma
-Uzawa relaxation over all possible parameters is

\displaystyle\mu_{{\rm opt},U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{(\alpha_{U},\omega_{U},\sigma)}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}\left\{\left|1-\omega_{U}\lambda_{*}\right|,\,\mu^{R},\,\mu^{C}\right\}=\sqrt{1-\frac{9m_{{\rm opt}}}{16}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}\approx 0.577,

where the minimum is attained provided that
m_{2}=m_{{\rm opt}}=\frac{32}{27}
with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3\mu_{{\rm opt},U}}\leq

\displaystyle\omega_{U}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3(1-\mu_{{\rm opt},U})},

\displaystyle\alpha_{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\omega^{2}_{U}}{3(3\omega_{U}-1)},

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\omega_{U}-1}.

Proof.

We first consider
m_{2}\in[8/9,16/9]
and
\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}=\frac{9}{8}
. In this situation, the two expressions in (
25
) coincide and
m_{2}=\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}=\frac{4\cdot 8^{2}}{9^{2}}\frac{\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{\alpha_{U}}
.

For
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[m_{2},16/9]
, from (
25
), we have

\mu^{R}=\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\chi_{1}-1=\sqrt{1-\frac{9m_{2}}{16}}=\sqrt{1-\frac{16\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{9\alpha_{U}}}.

For
m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,m_{2}]
, Theorem
3.3
tells us that

\mu^{C}=\sqrt{1+\frac{8\omega_{U}(\omega_{U}\sigma-\sigma-1)}{9\alpha_{U}}}=\sqrt{\frac{8\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{9\alpha_{U}}}.

Since
\mu^{R}
is a decreasing function of
\frac{\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{\alpha_{U}}
and
\mu^{C}
is an increasing function of
\frac{\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{\alpha_{U}}
, the optimal smoothing factor over the modes bounded by these factors is achieved if and only if
\mu^{R}=\mu^{C}
and is given by

\mu_{{\rm opt},U}=\displaystyle\min_{(\alpha_{U},\omega_{U},\sigma)}\max_{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in[8/9,16/9]}\left\{\sqrt{1-\frac{16\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{9\alpha_{U}}},\,\sqrt{\frac{8\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{9\alpha_{U}}}\right\}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}},

provided that

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{\alpha_{U}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{8},

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{9}{8}.

(27)

Furthermore,
m_{{\rm opt}}:=m_{2}=\frac{4\cdot 8^{2}}{9^{2}}\frac{\omega^{2}_{U}\sigma}{\alpha_{U}}=\frac{32}{27}
. Since
\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
, it means that
m_{2}\in[8/9,16/9]
gives a better smoothing factor.

Next, we show the above choice,
\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}=\frac{9}{8}
, gives the best possible bound over these two modes, then we go back to consider the eigenvalues
1-\omega_{U}\frac{m(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\alpha_{U}}
.

Let

and

. Then,
m_{2}=\frac{4\alpha_{U}\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}=\frac{4y}{x^{2}}

\mu^{R}
under the condition that

\displaystyle\mu^{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\frac{8}{9}\left(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{9m_{2}}{16}}\right)-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{9}\left(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-9y/4}\right)-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{8}{9}\sqrt{x^{2}-\frac{9}{4}(x-3/4)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{9}\sqrt{(x-9/8)^{2}+27/64}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}.

This means that when

\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}
.

If
x<\frac{9}{8}

), we have

\displaystyle\mu^{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{(1+\sigma)\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}\frac{8}{9}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{9m_{2}}{16}}\right)

\displaystyle 1-\frac{8}{9}x+\frac{8}{9}\sqrt{x^{2}-9y/4}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{8}{9}\sqrt{x^{2}-9/4\cdot(x-3/4)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{9}\sqrt{(x-9/8)^{2}+27/64}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}.

for

with

Now, we need to include
\lambda_{*}=\frac{m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\alpha_{U}}

\lambda_{*}

\min_{(\alpha_{U},\omega_{U},\sigma)}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in T^{{\rm high}}}\big|1-\frac{\omega_{U}}{\alpha_{U}}m_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|=\frac{1}{3}<\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}.

To obtain optimal result for all modes, we need

(28)

\displaystyle\alpha_{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\omega^{2}_{U}}{3(3\omega_{U}-1)},

(29)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3\omega_{U}-1}.

(30)

Substituting (

(31)

Note that parameters

), (

), and (

5
Conclusion

In this work, we consider multigrid methods for solving the Stokes equations discretized by the staggered finite difference method. We use mass matrix derived from finite element methods to approximate the inverse of the Laplacian coming from the Stokes equations. Based on this approximation, we propose three novel mass-based block-structured relaxation schemes: mass-based distributive relaxation, mass-based Braess-Sarazin relaxation, and mass-based
\sigma
-Uzawa relaxation. Then, we present a theoretical analysis of the optimal smoothing factor of LFA for multigrid methods for these three relaxation schemes. We obtain fast efficient smoothing properties. Specifically, our theoretical results show that the mass-based distributive relaxation and Braess-Sarazin relaxation have optimal smoothing factor
\frac{1}{3}
, and the mass-based Uzawa relaxation has optimal smoothing factor
\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}
. Numerical results validate our theoretical predictions, showing that the two-grid convergence factors are the same as the optimal smoothing factors. The main advantages of these mass-based relaxation schemes are that the mass matrix is sparse, the implementation is easy, and there is no need to compute the inverse of a matrix. Our findings here give us a better understanding of the construction of efficient multigrid methods for the Stokes equations. It may be useful for more complex problems, such as Navier-Stokes equations, which remains a topic for future work.
