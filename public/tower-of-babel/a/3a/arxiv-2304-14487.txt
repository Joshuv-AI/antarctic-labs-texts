Continued fractions using a Laguerre digraph interpretation of the Foata–Zeilberger bijection and its variants
Bishal Deb, [3mm] Department of Mathematics, University College London, London WC1E 6BT, United Kingdom, [2mm] Sorbonne Université, Université Paris Cité, CNRS, Laboratoire de Probabilités Statistique et Modélisation, 75005 Paris, France[2mm] bishal@gonitsora.com

Abstract
In the combinatorial theory of continued fractions, the Foata–Zeilberger bijection and its variants have been extensively used to derive various continued fractions enumerating several (sometimes infinitely many) simultaneous statistics on permutations (combinatorial model for factorials) and D-permutations (combinatorial model for Genocchi and median Genocchi numbers). A Laguerre digraph is a digraph in which each vertex has in- and out-degrees 0 0 or 1 1 . In this paper, we interpret the Foata–Zeilberger bijection in terms of Laguerre digraphs, which enables us to count cycles in permutations. Using this interpretation, we obtain Jacobi-type continued fractions for multivariate polynomials enumerating permutations, and also Thron-type and Stieltjes-type continued fractions for multivariate polynomials enumerating D-permutations, in both cases including the counting of cycles. This enables us to prove some conjectured continued fractions due to Sokal and Zeng (2022 Advances in Applied Mathematics) in the case of permutations, and Randrianarivony and Zeng (1996 Electronic Journal of Combinatorics) and Deb and Sokal (2024 Advances in Applied Mathematics) in the case of D-permutations.



Key Words:
Permutations, D-permutations, continued fraction, Foata–Zeilberger bijection, S-fraction, J-fraction, T-fraction, Dyck path, almost-Dyck path, Motzkin path, Schröder path, Laguerre digraphs

1.2
Classical continued fractions: S-fractions, J-fractions
and T-fractions

If
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a sequence of combinatorial numbers or polynomials
with
a_{0}=1
, it is often fruitful to seek to express its
ordinary generating function as a continued fraction.
The most commonly studied types of continued fractions
are Stieltjes-type (S-fractions),

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;,

(1.6)

and Jacobi-type (J-fractions),

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\gamma_{0}t-\cfrac{\beta_{1}t^{2}}{1-\gamma_{1}t-\cfrac{\beta_{2}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;.

(1.7)

A less commonly studied type of continued fraction is
the Thron-type (T-fraction):

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\delta_{1}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;.

(1.8)

(Both sides of all these expressions are to be interpreted as
formal power series in the indeterminate
t
.)
Flajolet
[
12
]
showed that
any S-fraction (resp. J-fraction)
can be interpreted combinatorially as a generating function
for Dyck (resp. Motzkin) paths with suitable weights for each rise and fall
(resp. each rise, fall and level step).
More recently, several authors

[
18
,
27
,
21
,
10
]

have found a similar combinatorial interpretation
of the general T-fraction:
namely, as a generating function for Schröder paths with suitable
weights for each rise, fall and long level step.
These interpretations will be reviewed in
Section
5.1
below.

Given a permutation

•

\sigma^{-1}(i)<i>\sigma(i)

•

(cval) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)>i<\sigma(i)
;

•

cycle double rise
(cdrise) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)<i<\sigma(i)
;

•

cycle double fall
(cdfall) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)>i>\sigma(i)
;

•

fixed point
(fix) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)=i=\sigma(i)
.

Clearly every index
i
belongs to exactly one of these five types;
we refer to this classification as the
cycle classification
.

On the other hand, an index
i\in[N]
is called a

•

record
(rec) (or
left-to-right maximum
)
if
\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)
for all

[note in particular that the indices 1 and
\sigma^{-1}(N)

•

antirecord

right-to-left minimum
)
if
\sigma(j)>\sigma(i)
for all

[note in particular that the indices
N

\sigma^{-1}(1)

are always antirecords];

•

exclusive record
(erec) if it is a record and not also
an antirecord;

•

exclusive antirecord
(earec) if it is an antirecord
and not also a record;

•

record-antirecord
(rar) (or
pivot

if it is both a record and an antirecord;

•

neither-record-antirecord
(nrar) if it is neither a record
nor an antirecord.

Every index
i
thus belongs to exactly one of the latter four types;
we refer to this classification as the

1.7
Overview of proof for results on permutations

We now summarise our proof for permutations (described in Section
6
).
Our results for D-permutations are also obtained by using
very similar ideas (described in Section
7
).
We first provide an overview of the Foata–Zeilberger bijection,
and then briefly mention how we reinterpet it
to obtain the count of cycles in a permutation.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a permutation on
n
letters.
This permutation
\sigma
partitions the set
[n]

into excedance indices (
F=\{i\in[n]:\sigma(i)>i\}
),
anti-excedance indices (
G=\{i\in[n]:\sigma(i)<i\}
), and fixed points (
H
).
Similarly,
\sigma
also partitions
[n]
into
excedance values (
F^{\prime}=\{i\in[n]:i>\sigma^{-1}(i)\}
),
anti-excedance values (
G^{\prime}=\{i\in[n]:i<\sigma^{-1}(i)\}
),
and fixed points.
Clearly,
\sigma\upharpoonright F\colon\>F\to F^{\prime}
,

\sigma\upharpoonright G\colon\>G\to G^{\prime}
,
and
\sigma\upharpoonright H\colon\>H\to H

are bijections,
and the permutation
\sigma
can be obtained from the following data:

•

Two partitions of the set
[n]\;=\;F\,\cup\,G\,\cup\,H\;=\;F^{\prime}\,\cup\,G^{\prime}\,\cup\,H
.

•

The two subwords of
\sigma
:
\sigma(x_{1})\sigma(x_{2})\ldots\sigma(x_{m})

and
\sigma(y_{1})\sigma(y_{2})\ldots\sigma(y_{l})
, where
G=\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{m}\}

and
F=\{y_{1}<y_{2}<\ldots<y_{l}\}
.

In their construction, Foata and Zeilberger
[
15
]

use these data to describe a bijection from
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

to a set of labelled Motzkin paths of length
n

(these will be completely defined in Section
5
).
One then uses Flajolet’s theorem
[
12
]

to obtain continued fractions from this bijection
while keeping track of various simultaneous permutation statistics.

The Foata–Zeilberger bijection consists of the following steps
(following
[
34
]
):

•

Step 1: A 3-coloured Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}

is constructed from
\sigma

(each level step is one of three colours).
The path
\overline{\omega}
is fully determined by the sets

F,F^{\prime},G,G^{\prime},H
.

•

Step 2: The labels
\xi
associated to
\overline{\omega}

are constructed from
\sigma
.
It turns out that the labels depend on
the maps

\sigma\upharpoonright F\colon\>F\to F^{\prime}

and
\sigma\upharpoonright G\colon\>G\to G^{\prime}

and the set
H
, separately.

•

Step 3: This step describes the construction of the inverse map

(\overline{\omega},\xi)\mapsto\sigma
.
This step is broken down as follows:

–

Step 3(a): The sets
F,F^{\prime},G,G^{\prime},H
are read off from the 3-coloured
Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}
.

–

Step 3(b): This description is the crucial part of the construction (at least for our purposes).
We use
inversion tables
to
construct the words
\sigma(x_{1})\sigma(x_{2})\ldots\sigma(x_{m})

and
\sigma(y_{1})\sigma(y_{2})\ldots\sigma(y_{l})
;
the former is constructed using a “right-to-left” inversion table
and the latter is constructed using a “left-to-right” inversion table.

It is, a priori, unclear how one might be able to track
the number of cycles of
\sigma
in this construction.
We resolve this issue by reinterpreting Step 3(b).
We describe a “history” of this construction using Laguerre digraphs.

Recall that a Laguerre digraph of size
n
is a directed graph
where each vertex has a distinct label from the label set
[n]

and has indegree
0
or
1
and outdegree
0
or
1
.
It follows that the connected components in a Laguerre digraph are
either directed paths or directed cycles.
Clearly, any subgraph of a Laguerre digraph is also a Laguerre digraph.
A permutation
\sigma
in cycle notation
is equivalent to a Laguerre digraph
L
with no paths
(
[
35
, pp. 22-23]
).
The directed edges of
L
are precisely
u\to\sigma(u)
.
We will interpret Step 3(b) of the Foata–Zeilberger construction
as building up a permutation as a sequence of Laguerre digraphs,
starting from the empty digraph in which all vertices are isolated
(i.e., have no adjacent edges),
and ending with the digraph of the permutation
\sigma

in which there are no paths.

For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
,
we let
\left.L\right|_{S}
denote the subgraph of
L
,
containing the same set of vertices
[n]
, but only the edges
u\to\sigma(u)
,
with
u\in S
(we are allowed to have
\sigma(u)\not\in S
).
Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
be a rewriting of
[n]
.
We consider the “history”

\left.L\right|_{\emptyset}\subset\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1}\}}\subset\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1},u_{2}\}}\subset\ldots\subset\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}=L

as a process of building up the permutation
\sigma

by successively considering the status of vertices
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}
.
At step
u
, we construct the edge
u\to\sigma(u)
.
Thus, at each step we insert a new edge into the digraph,
and at the end of this process,
the resulting digraph obtained is the digraph of
\sigma
.

The crucial part of our construction is that
we use a very special order
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
:
we first go through
H
in increasing order (we call this stage (a)),
we then go through
G
in increasing order (stage (b)),
finally we go through
F
but in decreasing order (stage (c)).
This total order is suggested by the inversion tables.
On building up the permutation
\sigma
using this history,
we will see that the cycles can only be obtained during
stage (c) and we can now count the number of cycles.

Our total order on
[n]
only depends on the sets
F,G,H
,
and hence, only on the
3-coloured
Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}

and not on the full description of the labels
\xi
.
This is crucial for our proof to work.

We now define (following

)
some permutation statistics that count

First we associate to each permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}

a pictorial representation
by placing vertices
1,2,\ldots,N
along a horizontal axis
and then drawing an arc from
i
to
\sigma(i)

above (resp. below) the horizontal axis
in case
\sigma(i)>i
[resp.
\sigma(i)<i
];
if
\sigma(i)=i
we do not draw any arc.
This idea was first introduced by Corteel
in
[
4
]
.
See Figures
1
and
2

for our two running examples.

Figure 1:

Pictorial representation of the permutation

\sigma=9\,\,3\,\,7\,\,4\,\,6\,\,11\,\,5\,\,8\,\,10\,\,1\,\,2=(1,9,10)\,(2,3,7,5,6,11)\,(4)\,(8)\in\mathfrak{S}_{11}

.

\sigma

Each vertex thus has either
out-degree = in-degree = 1 (if it is not a fixed point) or
out-degree = in-degree = 0 (if it is a fixed point).
Of course, the arrows on the arcs are redundant,
because the arrow on an arc above (resp. below) the axis
always points to the right (resp. left);
we therefore omit the arrows for simplicity.

We then say that a quadruplet
i<j<k<l
forms an

•

upper crossing
(ucross) if
k=\sigma(i)
and
l=\sigma(j)
;

•

lower crossing
(lcross) if
i=\sigma(k)
and
j=\sigma(l)
;

•

upper nesting
(unest) if
l=\sigma(i)
and
k=\sigma(j)
;

•

lower nesting
(lnest) if
i=\sigma(l)
and
j=\sigma(k)
.

We also consider some “degenerate” cases with
j=k
,
by saying that a triplet
i<j<l
forms an

•

upper joining
(ujoin) if
j=\sigma(i)
and
l=\sigma(j)

[i.e. the index
j
is a cycle double rise];

•

lower joining
(ljoin) if
i=\sigma(j)
and
j=\sigma(l)

[i.e. the index
j

A powerful way to prove continued fraction results is Flajolet’s

in terms of Motzkin and Dyck paths
and its generalisation

[
18
,
27
,

,
32
,
10
]

to Schröder paths.
One then uses a bijection from
the combinatorial object of study to labelled paths.
In our situtation,
the later bijections are
the variant Foata–Zeilberger bijection used in

[
34
, Section 6.1]
(for permutations),
and the two bijections in
[
6
, Section 6]
(for D-permutations).
We begin by reviewing briefly these two ingredients.

Our exposition in this section will closely follow

,

5.1
Combinatorial interpretation of continued fractions

Recall that a
Motzkin path
of length
n\geq 0

is a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n})

in the right quadrant
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
,
starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
and ending at
\omega_{n}=(n,0)
,
whose steps
s_{j}=\omega_{j}-\omega_{j-1}

are
(1,1)
[“rise” or “up step”],
(1,-1)
[“fall” or “down step”]
or
(1,0)
[“level step”].
We write
h_{j}
for the
height
of the Motzkin path at abscissa
j
,
i.e.
\omega_{j}=(j,h_{j})
;
note in particular that
h_{0}=h_{n}=0
.
We write
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
for the set of Motzkin paths of length
n
,
and
{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
.
A Motzkin path is called a
Dyck path
if it has no level steps.
A Dyck path always has even length;
we write
{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}
for the set of Dyck paths of length
2n
,
and
{\mathcal{D}}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}
.

Let
{\bf a}=(a_{i})_{i\geq 0}
,
{\bf b}=(b_{i})_{i\geq 1}

and
{\bf c}=(c_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be indeterminates;
we will work in the ring
\mathbb{Z}[[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}]]

of formal power series in these indeterminates.
To each Motzkin path
\omega
we assign a weight

W(\omega)\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}]

that is the product of the weights for the individual steps,
where a rise starting at height
i
gets weight
a_{i}
,
a fall starting at height
i
gets weight
b_{i}
,
and a level step at height
i
gets weight
c_{i}
.
Flajolet
[
12
]
showed that
the generating function of Motzkin paths
can be expressed as a continued fraction:

Theorem 5.1

(Flajolet’s master theorem)
.

We have

\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{M}}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-c_{0}-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}}{1-c_{1}-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}}{1-c_{2}-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.1)

as an identity in
\mathbb{Z}[[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}]]
.

In particular, if
a_{i-1}b_{i}=\beta_{i}t^{2}
and
c_{i}=\gamma_{i}t

(note that the parameter
t
is conjugate to the length of the Motzkin path),
we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\gamma_{0}t-\cfrac{\beta_{1}t^{2}}{1-\gamma_{1}t-\cfrac{\beta_{2}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}\;\,,

(5.2)

so that the generating function of Motzkin paths with height-dependent weights
is given by the J-type continued fraction (
1.7
).
Similarly, if
a_{i-1}b_{i}=\alpha_{i}t
and
c_{i}=0

(note that
t
is now conjugate to the semi-length of the Dyck path), we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\,,

(5.3)

so that the generating function of Dyck paths with height-dependent weights
is given by the S-type continued fraction (
1.3
).

Let us now show how to handle Schröder paths within this framework.
A
Schröder path
of length
2n
(
n\geq 0
)
is a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{2n})

in the right quadrant
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
,
starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
and ending at
\omega_{2n}=(2n,0)
,
whose steps are
(1,1)
[“rise” or “up step”],

(1,-1)
[“fall” or “down step”]
or
(2,0)
[“long level step”].
We write
s_{j}
for the step starting at abscissa
j-1
.
If the step
s_{j}
is a rise or a fall,
we set
s_{j}=\omega_{j}-\omega_{j-1}
as before.
If the step
s_{j}
is a long level step,
we set
s_{j}=\omega_{j+1}-\omega_{j-1}
and leave
\omega_{j}
undefined;
furthermore, in this case there is no step
s_{j+1}
.
We write
h_{j}
for the height of the Schröder path at abscissa
j

whenever this is defined, i.e.
\omega_{j}=(j,h_{j})
.
Please note that
\omega_{2n}=(2n,0)
and
h_{2n}=0

are always well-defined,
because there cannot be a long level step starting at abscissa
2n-1
.
Note also that a long level step at even (resp. odd) height
can occur only at an odd-numbered (resp. even-numbered) step.
We write
{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}
for the set of Schröder paths of length
2n
,
and
{\mathcal{S}}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}
.

There is an obvious bijection between Schröder paths and Motzkin paths:
namely, every long level step is mapped onto a level step.
If we apply Flajolet’s master theorem with

a_{i-1}b_{i}=\alpha_{i}t
and
c_{i}=\delta_{i+1}t

to the resulting Motzkin path
(note that
t
is now conjugate to the semi-length
of the underlying Schröder path),
we obtain

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\delta_{1}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\,,

(5.4)

so that the generating function of Schröder paths
with height-dependent weights
is given by the T-type continued fraction (
1.8
).
More precisely, every rise gets a weight 1,
every fall starting at height
i
gets a weight
\alpha_{i}
,
and every long level step at height
i
gets a weight
\delta_{i+1}
.
This combinatorial interpretation of T-fractions in terms of Schröder paths
was found recently by several authors

[
18
,
27
,
21
,
32
]
.

5.2
Labelled Dyck, Motzkin and Schröder paths

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=({\mathcal{A}}_{h})_{h\geq 0}
,
{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}=({\mathcal{B}}_{h})_{h\geq 1}

and
{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}}_{h})_{h\geq 0}
be sequences of finite sets.
An

({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled Motzkin path of length
\bm{n}

is a pair
(\omega,\xi)

where
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n})

is a Motzkin path of length
n
,
and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is a sequence satisfying

\xi_{i}\>\in\>\begin{cases}{\mathcal{A}}(h_{i-1})&\textrm{if step $i$ is a rise (i.e.\ $h_{i}=h_{i-1}+1$)}\\[2.84526pt]
{\mathcal{B}}(h_{i-1})&\textrm{if step $i$ is a fall (i.e.\ $h_{i}=h_{i-1}-1$)}\\[2.84526pt]
{\mathcal{C}}(h_{i-1})&\textrm{if step $i$ is a level step (i.e.\ $h_{i}=h_{i-1}$)}\end{cases}

(5.5)

where
h_{i-1}
(resp.
h_{i}
) is the height of the Motzkin path
before (resp. after) step
i
.
[For typographical clarity
we have here written
{\mathcal{A}}(h)
as a synonym for
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}
, etc.]
We call
\xi_{i}
the
label
associated to step
i
.
We call the pair
(\omega,\xi)

an
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})
-labelled Dyck path

if
\omega
is a Dyck path (in this case
{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}
plays no role).
We denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})

the set of
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled Motzkin paths of length
n
,
and by
{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})

the set of
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})
-labelled Dyck paths of length
2n
.

We define a
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled
Schröder path

in an analogous way;
now the sets
{\mathcal{C}}_{h}
refer to long level steps.
We denote by
{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})

the set of
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled Schröder paths
of length
2n
.

Let us stress that the sets
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}
,
{\mathcal{B}}_{h}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{h}
are allowed
to be empty.
Whenever this happens, the path
\omega
is forbidden to take a step
of the specified kind starting at the specified height.

Following Flajolet
[
12
, Proposition 7A]
,
we can state a “master J-fraction” for

({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled Motzkin paths.
Let
{\bf a}=(a_{h,\xi})_{h\geq 0,\;\xi\in{\mathcal{A}}(h)}
,

{\bf b}=(b_{h,\xi})_{h\geq 1,\;\xi\in{\mathcal{B}}(h)}

and
{\bf c}=(c_{h,\xi})_{h\geq 0,\;\xi\in{\mathcal{C}}(h)}

be indeterminates;
we give an
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled Motzkin path
(\omega,\xi)

a weight
W(\omega,\xi)

that is the product of the weights for the individual steps,
where a rise starting at height
h
with label
\xi
gets weight
a_{h,\xi}
,
a fall starting at height
h
with label
\xi
gets weight
b_{h,\xi}
,
and a level step at height
h
with label
\xi
gets weight
c_{h,\xi}
.
Then:

Theorem 5.2

(Flajolet’s master theorem for labelled Motzkin paths)
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\!\!\sum_{(\omega,\xi)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})}\!\!\!W(\omega,\xi)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-c_{0}t-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}t^{2}}{1-c_{1}t-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}t^{2}}{1-c_{2}t-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.6)

as an identity in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}][[t]]
, where

a_{h}\;=\;\sum_{\xi\in{\mathcal{A}}(h)}a_{h,\xi}\;,\qquad b_{h}\;=\;\sum_{\xi\in{\mathcal{B}}(h)}b_{h,\xi}\;,\qquad c_{h}\;=\;\sum_{\xi\in{\mathcal{C}}(h)}c_{h,\xi}\;.

(5.7)

This is an immediate consequence of Theorem
5.1

together with the definitions.

By specialising to
{\bf c}={\bm{0}}
and replacing
t^{2}
by
t
,
we obtain the corresponding theorem
for
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})
-labelled Dyck paths:

Corollary 5.3

(Flajolet’s master theorem for labelled Dyck paths)
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\!\!\sum_{(\omega,\xi)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})}\!\!\!W(\omega,\xi)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}t}{1-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}t}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.8)

as an identity in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bf a},{\bf b}][[t]]
, where

a_{h}
and
b_{h}
are defined by (
5.7
).

Similarly, for labelled Schröder paths we have:

Theorem 5.4

(Flajolet’s master theorem for labelled Schröder paths)
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\!\!\sum_{(\omega,\xi)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})}\!\!\!W(\omega,\xi)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-c_{0}t-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}t}{1-c_{1}t-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}t}{1-c_{2}t-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}t}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.9)

as an identity in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}][[t]]
, where

a_{h},b_{h},c_{h}
are defined by (
5.7
),
with
c_{h,\xi}
now referring to long level steps.

6.1
Sokal–Zeng variant of the Foata–Zeilberger bijection

Sokal and Zeng employed a variant of the Foata–Zeilberger bijection
to prove
[
34
, Theorems 2.1(a), 2.2, 2.5, 2.7, and 2.9]
,
i.e., their “first theorems” for permutations.
We begin by recalling this bijection
which is a correspondence between
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and the set of

({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled
Motzkin paths of length
n
, where the labels
\xi_{i}
lie in the sets

{\mathcal{A}}_{h}&=\{0,\ldots,h\}\,\>\;\;\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\hbox{for $h\geq 0$}\\
{\mathcal{B}}_{h}=\{0,\ldots,h-1\}\>\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\hbox{for $h\geq 1$}\\
{\mathcal{C}}_{h}=\left(\{1\}\times C_{h}^{(1)}\right)\,\cup\,\left(\{2\}\times C_{h}^{(2)}\right)\,\cup\,\left(\{3\}\times C_{h}^{(3)}\right)\qquad\hbox{for $h\geq 0$}

(6.1)

where

C_{h}^{(1)}&=\{0,\ldots,h-1\}\qquad\hbox{for $k\geq 0$}\\
C_{h}^{(2)}=\{0,\ldots,h-1\}\qquad\hbox{for $k\geq 0$}\\
C_{h}^{(3)}=\{0\}\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\>\hbox{for $k\geq 0$}

(6.2)

Notice that our convention for labels in this paper are slightly different from

[
34
]
and
we instead follow those in

[
6
, Section 5]
. A key difference
is that our label set starts at
0
and not at
1
.
A level step that has label

\xi_{h}\in\{i\}\times C_{h}^{(i)}

will be called a level step of type
i
(
i=1,2,3
).
By a 3-coloured Motzkin path, we will refer to a Motzkin path
\omega

where each of its level steps is assigned a type.
We will use
\overline{\omega}
to denote a 3-coloured Motzkin path.

We will begin by recalling how the Motzkin path
\omega
is defined;
then we will explain how the labels
\xi
are defined;
next we sketch the proof that the mapping is indeed a bijection.
There were two more steps in
[

, Section 6.1]

which we will not recall;
these were the translation of the various statistics from

to labelled Motzkin paths;
and summing over labels
\xi
to obtain the weight

Step 1: Definition of the Motzkin path.

Given a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
we classify the indices
i\in[n]

according to the cycle classification.
We then define a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n})

starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
and ending at
\omega_{n}=(n,0)
,
with steps
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
, as follows:

•

If
i
is a cycle valley, then
s_{i}
is a rise.

•

If
i
is a cycle peak, then
s_{i}
is a fall.

•

If
i
is a cycle double fall, then
s_{i}
is a level step of type 1.

•

If
i
is a cycle double rise, then
s_{i}
is a level step of type 2.

•

If
i
is a fixed point, then
s_{i}
is a level step of type 3.

The fact that the resulting path is indeed a Motzkin path was proved by
providing an interpretation of the height
h_{i}
which we recall here:

Lemma 6.1

(
[
34
, Lemma 6.1]
)
.

For

we have

h_{i-1}&=\#\{j<i\colon\>\sigma(j)\geq i\}\\
=\#\{j<i\colon\>\sigma^{-1}(j)\geq i\}.

(6.3)

We also recall (
[
34
, eq. (6.4)]
)
which is an equivalent formulation of Equation (
6.3
):

h_{i}&=\#\{j\leq i\colon\>\sigma(j)>i\}\\
=\#\{j\leq i\colon\>\sigma^{-1}(j)>i\}.

(6.4)

Notice that if
i
is a fixed point,
then by comparing (
6.3
)/(
6.4
)
with (

)
we see that the height of the Motzkin path after (or before) step

equals the number of pseudo-nestings of the fixed point:

h_{i-1}\;=\;h_{i}\;=\;{\rm psnest}(i,\sigma)\;.

(6.5)

Step 2: Definition of the labels
\bm{\xi_{i}}
.

We now recall the definition of the labels

\xi_{i}\;=\;\begin{cases}\#\{j\colon\>j<i\hbox{ and }\sigma(j)>\sigma(i)\}&\textrm{if $\sigma(i)>i$ \quad i.e. $i\in{\rm Cval}\cup{\rm Cdrise}$}\\[2.84526pt]
\#\{j\colon\>j>i\hbox{ and }\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\}&\textrm{if $\sigma(i)<i$ \quad i.e. $i\in{\rm Cpeak}\cup{\rm Cdfall}$}\\[2.84526pt]
0&\textrm{if $\sigma(i)=i$ \quad i.e. $i\in{\rm Fix}$}\end{cases}

(6.6)

where recall that
{\rm Cval}
is the set of all cycle valleys of
\sigma

and likewise for the others.
For sake of brevity, we are abusing notation
for fixed points, cycle double rises and cycle double falls
by dropping the first index of
\xi_{i}
.

Compare our definition (
6.6
) with
[
34
, (6.5)]

and notice the shift from
1
to
0
.
These definitions have a simple interpretation in terms of the
nesting statistics defined in (
2.3
b,d):

\xi_{i}\;=\;\begin{cases}{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)&\textrm{if $\sigma(i)>i$ \quad i.e. $i\in{\rm Cval}\cup{\rm Cdrise}$}\\[2.84526pt]
{\rm lnest}(i,\sigma)&\textrm{if $\sigma(i)<i$ \quad i.e. $i\in{\rm Cpeak}\cup{\rm Cdfall}$}\\[2.84526pt]
0&\textrm{if $\sigma(i)=i$ \quad i.e. $i\in{\rm Fix}$}\end{cases}

(6.7)

To verify that the inequalities (
5.5
)/(
6.1
)
are satisfied; to do this, we interpret
h_{i-1}-\xi_{i}

in terms of the crossing statistics defined in (
2.3
a,c):

Lemma 6.2

(Crossing statistics)
.

We have

\displaystyle h_{i-1}-\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma)\quad\textrm{\rm if $i\in{\rm Cval}$}

(6.8)

\displaystyle h_{i-1}-1-\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma)\quad\textrm{\rm if $i\in{\rm Cdrise}$}

(6.9)

\displaystyle h_{i-1}-1-\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm lcross}(i,\sigma)\quad\>\textrm{\rm if $i\in{\rm Cpeak}\,\cup\,{\rm Cdfall}$}

(6.10)

Again compare Lemma
6.2
with

[
34

to see how the shift of
1

affects these quantities.

Since the quantities (

)–(

Step 3: Proof of bijection.

We recall the description of the inverse map for the mapping

\sigma\mapsto(\omega,\xi)
.

First, some preliminaries:
Given a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
we define five subsets of
[n]
:

F&=\{i\colon\>\sigma(i)>i\}\;=\;\hbox{positions of excedances}\\
F^{\prime}=\{i\colon\>i>\sigma^{-1}(i)\}\;=\;\hbox{values of excedances}\\
G=\{i\colon\>\sigma(i)<i\}\;=\;\hbox{positions of anti-excedances}\\
G^{\prime}=\{i\colon\>i<\sigma^{-1}(i)\}\;=\;\hbox{values of anti-excedances}\\
H=\{i\colon\>\sigma(i)=i\}\;=\;\hbox{fixed points}

(6.11)

Let us observe that

F\cap F^{\prime}&=\hbox{cycle double rises}\\
G\cap G^{\prime}=\hbox{cycle double falls}\\
F\cap G^{\prime}=\hbox{cycle valleys}\\
F^{\prime}\cap G=\hbox{cycle peaks}\\
F\cap G=\emptyset\\
F^{\prime}\cap G^{\prime}=\emptyset

(6.12)

and of course
H
is disjoint from
F,F^{\prime},G,G^{\prime}
.

Let us also recall the notion of an
inversion table
:
Let
S
be a totally ordered set of cardinality
k
,
and let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
be an enumeration of
S
;
then the (left-to-right) inversion table corresponding to
\bm{x}

is the sequence
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
of nonnegative integers
defined by
p_{\alpha}=\#\{\beta<\alpha\colon\>x_{\beta}>x_{\alpha}\}
.
Note that
0\leq p_{\alpha}\leq\alpha-1
for all
\alpha\in[k]
,
so there are exactly
k!
possible inversion tables.
Given the inversion table
\bm{p}
,
we can reconstruct the sequence
\bm{x}

by working from right to left, as follows:
There are
p_{k}
elements of
S
larger than
x_{k}
,
so
x_{k}
must be the
(p_{k}+1)
th largest element of
S
.
Then there are
p_{k-1}
elements of
S\setminus\{x_{k}\}

larger than
x_{k-1}
,
so
x_{k-1}
must be the
(p_{k-1}+1)
th largest element
of
S\setminus\{x_{k}\}
.
And so forth.
[Analogously, the right-to-left inversion table corresponding to
\bm{x}

is the sequence
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
of nonnegative integers
defined by
p_{\alpha}=\#\{\beta>\alpha\colon\>x_{\beta}<x_{\alpha}\}
.]

With these preliminaries out of the way,
we can now describe the map
(\omega,\xi)\mapsto\sigma
.
Given a Motzkin path along with an assignment of types for its level steps
\overline{\omega}
,
we read off which indices
i
correspond to cycle valleys,
cycle peaks, cycle double falls, cycle double rises, and fixed points;
this allows us to reconstruct the sets
F,F^{\prime},G,G^{\prime},H
.
We now use the labels
\xi
to reconstruct the maps

\sigma\upharpoonright F\colon\>F\to F^{\prime}
and

\sigma\upharpoonright G\colon\>G\to G^{\prime}
, as follows:
Let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
be the elements of
F
written in increasing order;
then the sequence
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}
defined by
j_{\alpha}=\sigma(i_{\alpha})

is a listing of
F^{\prime}
whose inversion table is given by

p_{\alpha}=\xi_{i_{\alpha}}
:
this is the content of (
6.6
) in the case
\sigma(i)>i
.
So we can use
\xi\upharpoonright F
to reconstruct
\sigma\upharpoonright F
.
In a similar way we can use
\xi\upharpoonright G

to reconstruct
\sigma\upharpoonright G
,
but now we must use the right-to-left inversion table
because of how (
6.6
) is written in the case
\sigma(i)<i
.

Remark 6.1

In the original version of the bijection by Foata and Zeilberger

6.2
Combinatorial interpretation using Laguerre digraphs

We begin with a Motzkin path
\omega

and an assignment of labels
\xi
satisfying
(
5.5
)/(
6.1
).
The inverse bijection
(Section
6.1
Step 3),
gives us a permutation
\sigma
.
We will now break this process into several intermediate steps
and reinterpret it using Laguerre digraphs.
Recall that a Laguerre digraph of size
n
is a directed graph
where each vertex has a distinct label from the label set
[n]

and has indegree
0
or
1
and outdegree
0
or
1
.
Clearly, any subgraph of a Laguerre digraph is also a Laguerre digraph.
The connected components in a Laguerre digraph are
either directed paths or directed cycles,
where a path with one vertex is called an isolated vertex
and a cycle with one vertex is called a loop.

Notice that a Laguerre digraph with no paths
is a diagrammatic representation of a permutation in cycle notation
(see
[
35
, pp. 22-23]
).
Let
L^{\sigma}
denote this Laguerre digraph
corresponding to a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. The directed edges of
L^{\sigma}

are precisely
u\to\sigma(u)
. Also for
S\subseteq[n]
,
we let
\left.L^{\sigma}\right|_{S}
denote the subgraph of
L^{\sigma}
,
containing the same set of vertices
[n]
, but only the edges
u\to\sigma(u)
,
with
u\in S
(we are allowed to have
\sigma(u)\not\in S
).
Thus,
\left.L^{\sigma}\right|_{[n]}=L^{\sigma}
, and

\left.L^{\sigma}\right|_{\emptyset}
is the digraph containing
n
vertices
and no edges. Whenever the permutation
\sigma
is understood,
we shall drop the superscript and
use
\left.L\right|_{S}
to denote this digraph.

Recall that the inverse bijection in
Step 3 Section
6.1
,
begins by obtaining the sets
F,F^{\prime},G,G^{\prime},H

from the 3-coloured Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}
.
We then construct
\sigma\upharpoonright F\colon\>F\to F^{\prime}

and
\sigma\upharpoonright G\colon\>G\to G^{\prime}
separately
by using the labels
\xi\upharpoonright F
and
\xi\upharpoonright G
respectively.
(Note that just knowing the set
H
suffices
to reconstruct
\sigma\upharpoonright H
.)

In this interpretation, we start with the digraph
\left.L\right|_{\emptyset}
.
We then go through the set
[n]
.
However, the crucial part of our approach is that
we use the following unusual total order on
[n]

(notice that
[n]=F\cup G\cup H
):

•
Stage (a):

We first go through the set
H
in increasing order.

•
Stage (b):

We then go through the set
G
in increasing order.

•
Stage (c):

Finally, we go through the set
F

but in
decreasing
order.

As
F,G
and
H
are entirely determined by the 3-coloured path

\overline{\omega}
,
we call the above order the FZ order on
[n]
with respect to
the 3-coloured Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}
.
Thus, the FZ order corresponding to two different permutations
\sigma

and
\sigma^{\prime}
coming from the same 3-coloured Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}
are the same.

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
be a rewriting of
[n]
as per the FZ order.
We now consider the “FZ history”

\left.L\right|_{\emptyset}\subset\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1}\}}\subset\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1},u_{2}\}}\subset\ldots\subset\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}=L

as a process of building up the permutation
\sigma

by successively considering the status of vertices
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}
.
Thus, at step
u

(where the step number is given by the vertex
u\in[n]
)
we use the inversion tables to construct the edge
u\to\sigma(u)
.
Thus, at each step we insert a new edge into the digraph,
and at the end of this process,
the resulting digraph obtained is the permutation
\sigma
in cycle notation.

Let us now look at the intermediate Laguerre digraphs obtained
during stages (a), (b) and (c) more closely.

Stage (a): Going through
\bm{H}
:

For each vertex
u\in H
, we introduce a loop edge
u\to u

thus creating a new loop at the end of each step.
After all steps
u\in H
have been carried out,
the resulting Laguerre digraph
\left.L\right|_{H}
consists of loops
at all vertices in
H
.
All other vertices are isolated vertices, i.e., have no adjacent edges.

Stage (b): Going through
\bm{G}
:

From (
6.12
), we know that
G={\rm Cdfall}(\sigma)\cup{\rm Cpeak}(\sigma)
where
\sigma
is the resulting permutation obtained at the end
of the inverse bijection.
Let us recall the construction for this case.
We construct

\sigma\upharpoonright G\colon\>G\to G^{\prime}
using
the right-to-left inversion table.
Let
G=\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{k}\}

be the elements of
G
arranged in increasing order,
and similarly let
G^{\prime}=\{x_{1}^{\prime}<x_{2}^{\prime}<\ldots<x_{k}^{\prime}\}
.
Then the
(p_{j}+1)
th smallest element of

G^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(x_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(x_{j-1})\}

is chosen to be
\sigma(x_{j})
where

p_{j}=\xi_{x_{j}}
.

Let us reinterpret this in terms of Laguerre digraphs.
At this stage, the vertices in
G
(resp.
G^{\prime}
)
are our designated starting vertices (ending vertices)
arranged in increasing order.
We then look through the starting vertices
in increasing order.
At the end of step
x_{j-1}
,
directed edges
x_{1}\to\sigma(x_{1}),\ldots,x_{j-1}\to\sigma(x_{j-1})

have been inserted,
the available starting vertices are
x_{j},\ldots,x_{k}

and the available ending vertices belong to

G^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(x_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(x_{j-1})\}
.
We then pick the smallest available starting vertex,
which is
x_{j}
,
and connect it to the

(p_{j}+1)
th smallest ending vertex available,
i.e., the
(p_{j}+1)
th smallest element of the set

G^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(x_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(x_{j-1})\}
.
(This is analogous to the partial interpretation
of the labels in terms of bipartite digraph
for cycle double falls and cycle peaks on

[
34
, p. 96]
.)

The vertices in
\left.L\right|_{H\,\cup\,\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{j}\}}

have out-edges in the following situations:

•

if
u\in H
,
u
is a loop.

•

if
u
is one of the
j
smallest
elements of
G
,
it has an edge going to some vertex
v\neq u
and
v\in G^{\prime}
.

•

All other vertices have no out-edges.

Also, from definition of
G
in (
6.11
c),
it follows that for any non-loop edge
u\to v
,
we must have
u>v
.
Thus, the Laguerre digraph
\left.L\right|_{H\,\cup\,\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{j}\}}

only consists of decreasing directed paths (including isolated vertices)
and loops; there are no non-loop cycles.

Lemma 6.3
.

The Laguerre digraph
\left.L\right|_{H\,\cup\,G}

consists of the following connected components:

•

loops on vertices
u\in H
,

•

directed paths with at least two vertices,
in which the initial vertex of the path
is a cycle peak in
\sigma

(i.e. contained in the set
F^{\prime}\,\cap\,G
),
the final vertex is a cycle valley in
\sigma

(i.e. contained in the set
F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
),
and the intermediate vertices (if any)
are cycle double falls
(i.e. contained in the set
G\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
).

•

isolated vertices at
u\in F\,\cap\,F^{\prime}={\rm Cdrise}(\sigma)
.

Furthermore, it contains no directed cycles.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that for a directed path with
at least two vertices, the initial vertex is a cycle peak in
\sigma

(i.e. contained in the set
F^{\prime}\,\cap\,G
)
and the final vertex is a cycle valley in
\sigma

(i.e. contained in the set
F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
).

Notice that the initial vertex
u
of such a path must already have an out-neighbour
and hence must belong to the set
G
as only the elements of the sets
G
and
H

have been assigned out-neighbours.
As all vertices in
G^{\prime}
have already been assigned in-neighbours,

u\not\in G^{\prime}
and using the fact that

G={\rm Cdfall}\,\cup\,{\rm Cpeak}
(from (
6.12
)),
we get that
u\in{\rm Cpeak}=F^{\prime}\,\cap\,G
.
The proof for the final vertex is similar and we omit it.
∎

Stage (c): Going through
\bm{F}
:

Similar to both the previous cases,
at each step we introduce edges
u\to\sigma(u)
.
However, now we go through elements of
F
in decreasing order.

From (
6.12
), we know that
F={\rm Cdrise}\cup{\rm Cval}
.
Let us now recall the construction for this case.
We construct

\sigma\upharpoonright F\colon\>F\to F^{\prime}
using
the left-to-right inversion table.
Let
F=\{y_{1}<y_{2}<\ldots<y_{l}\}

be the elements of
F
arranged in increasing order.
Then the
(p_{j}+1)
th largest element of

F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}

is chosen to be
\sigma(y_{j})
where

p_{j}=\xi_{y_{j}}
.

We now reinterpret this in terms of Laguerre digraphs.
At this stage, the vertices in
F
(
F^{\prime}
)
are our designated starting vertices (ending vertices).
We look through the starting vertices
in decreasing order.
At the end of step
y_{j+1}
,
directed edges
y_{l}\to\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,y_{j+1}\to\sigma(y_{j+1})

have been inserted,
the available starting vertices are
y_{j},\ldots,y_{1}

and the available ending vertices belong to

F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}
.
We then pick the largest available starting vertex,
which is
y_{j}
and connect it to the

(p_{j}+1)
th largest ending vertex available,
i.e., the
(p_{j}+1)
th largest element of the set

F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
u
be the final vertex of a path
with at least two vertices in

\left.L\right|_{H\,\cup\,G\,\cup\,\{y_{l},\ldots,y_{j}\}}
.
Then
u\in{\rm Cval}=F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since all vertices in
H\,\cup\,G
were already assigned out-neighbours
during stages (a) and (b), it must be that
u\in F
.
As
F={\rm Cdrise}\cup{\rm Cval}
,
u

is either a cycle valley or a cycle double rise in
\sigma
.

Let us assume that
u
is a cycle double rise in
\sigma
, i.e.,

\sigma^{-1}(u)<u<\sigma(u)
.
We know that
u
must have an in-neighbour
v

as its path has atleast two vertices.
Thus,
v=\sigma^{-1}(u)<u
, which implies
v\in F

(from definition of
F
in (
6.11
a)).
However, this is a situation where we have two vertices
u,v\in F

with
u>v
such that the smaller vertex has an out-neighbour
even though the larger vertex does not.
This clearly cannot happen as we assign out-neighbours
to vertices in
F
in descending order.
This is a contradiction and thus
u\in{\rm Cval}=F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
. ∎

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
be the elements of
[n]
rearranged as per the FZ order
with respect to the 3-coloured Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}
.
We say that
u_{j}\in[n]\backslash H
is a
cycle closer

if the edge
u_{j}\to\sigma(u_{j})

is introduced in
\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{j-1}\}}

as an edge between the two ends of a path
turning the path into a cycle.
The following lemma classifies all cycle closers:

Lemma 6.5
.

(Classifying cycle closers) Given a permutation
\sigma
,
an element
u\in[n]
is a cycle closer
if and only if it is a cycle valley minimum,
i.e.,
it is the smallest element in its cycle.

Proof.

Let
u_{j}
be a cycle closer.
Notice that
u_{j}
must have been the final vertex of a path
in
\left.L\right|_{\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{j-1}\}}

that contains at least two vertices.
From Lemma
6.4
,

u_{j}\in F\cap G^{\prime}={\rm Cval}
.

Any other cycle valley
v\neq u_{j}
in the resulting cycle
must have already been present in this path
and hence must have an out-neighbour.
Hence, it must be that
v>u_{j}
as
{\rm Cval}\subseteq F

and we assign out-neighbours
to vertices belonging to
F
in descending order.
Thus, the cycle closer
u_{j}
is the smallest cycle valley
in its cycle in
\sigma
.
∎

As each non-singleton cycle has exactly one cycle closer,
counting cycle closers will give us the number of
non-singleton cycles.
This is what we do next.
But before doing that, we require a technical lemma.
However, before going into the lemma, recall that if

y_{j}\in F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}={\rm Cval}(\sigma)
,
step
s_{y_{j}}
must be a rise from height
h_{y_{j}-1}
to height
h_{y_{j}}

and hence,
h_{y_{j}-1}+1=h_{y_{j}}
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Given a permutation
\sigma
and associated sets
F,F^{\prime},G,G^{\prime},H

with
F=\{y_{1}<y_{2}<\ldots<y_{l}\}
,
and an index
j
(
1\leq j\leq l
) such that
y_{j}\in F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
.
Then the following is true:

\left|\{u\in F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}\colon\>u>y_{j}\}\right|=h_{y_{j}-1}+1=h_{y_{j}}

(6.18)

where
h_{i}
denotes the height at position
i
of the Motzkin path
\omega

associated to
\sigma

in Step 1.

Proof.

We first establish the following equality of sets:

\{u>y_{j}\colon\>\sigma^{-1}(u)\leq y_{j}\}\;=\;\{u\in F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}\colon\>u>y_{j}\}.

(6.19)

Whenever
u\in F^{\prime}
, we have that
\sigma^{-1}(u)\in F

(by description of
F
,
F^{\prime}
in (
6.11
a,b)).
Additionally, if
u\not\in\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}

then it must be that
\sigma^{-1}(u)\leq y_{j}
.
This establishes the containment
\{u>y_{j}\colon\>\sigma^{-1}(u)\leq y_{j}\}\;\supseteq\;\{u\in F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}\colon\>u>y_{j}\}
.

On the other hand, if
u>y_{j}
and
\sigma^{-1}(u)\leq y_{j}
,
then
u>\sigma^{-1}(u)
and hence
u\in F^{\prime}
.
As
\sigma^{-1}(u)\leq y_{j}<y_{j+1}<\ldots<y_{l}
,

u
cannot be one of
\sigma(y_{j+1}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{l})
.
Therefore,
u\in F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}
.
This establishes (
6.19
).

To obtain Equation (
6.18
),
it suffices to show that the cardinality of the set

\{u>y_{j}\colon\>\sigma^{-1}(u)\leq y_{j}\}

is
h_{y_{j}}
.
To do this, recall the interpretation of heights
in Equation (
6.4
) and observe that

\displaystyle h_{y_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\{u\leq y_{j}\colon\>\sigma(u)>y_{j}\}

(6.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\{u>y_{j}\colon\>\sigma^{-1}(u)\leq y_{j}\}

where the second equality is obtained by replacing
u
with
\sigma^{-1}(u)
.
∎

We are now ready to count the number of cycle closers.

Lemma 6.7

(Counting of cycle closers for permutations)
.

Fix a 3-coloured Motzkin path
\overline{\omega}
of length
n

and construct the sets
F,F^{\prime},G,G^{\prime},H

(these are totally
determined by
\overline{\omega}
).
Let
F=\{y_{1}<y_{2}<\ldots<y_{l}\}
and
fix an index
j
(
1\leq j\leq l
) such that
y_{j}\in F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
.
Also fix labels
\xi_{u}
for vertices

u\in H\,\cup\,G\,\cup\,\{y_{l},y_{l-1},\ldots,y_{j+1}\}

satisfying (
5.5
)/(
6.1
).
Then

(a)

The value of
\xi_{y_{j}}
completely determines
if
y_{j}
is a cycle closer or not.

(b)

There is exactly one value
\xi_{y_{j}}\in\{0,1,\ldots,h_{y_{j}-1}\}

that makes
y_{j}
a cycle closer, and conversely.

Proof.

As
y_{j}\in F\,\cap\,G^{\prime}
, it must be that
y_{j}

is a cycle valley in
\sigma
.
As
y_{j}
does not have an out-neighbour in the Laguerre digraph

\left.L\right|_{H\,\cup\,G\,\cup\,\{y_{l},\ldots,y_{j+1}\}}
,
it must be the final vertex of a path.
Let
v
be the initial vertex of this path.
During step
y_{j}
, we choose one of the available ending vertices
from the set
F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}

to be
\sigma(y_{j})
.

For
y_{j}
to be a cycle closer, it must be that
\sigma(y_{j})=v

(so that inserting the edge
y_{j}\to v
turns its path into a cycle).
As each value for label
\xi_{y_{j}}\in\{0,1,\ldots,h_{y_{j}-1}\}

assigns a different vertex to be
\sigma(y_{j})
,
there is at most one label for which
y_{j}
is a cycle closer.

Next we observe that
v\not\in G^{\prime}
.
This is because
v
does not have an in-neighbour
in
\left.L\right|_{H\,\cup\,G\,\cup\,\{y_{l},\ldots,y_{j+1}\}}

whereas all
v\in G^{\prime}
had in-neighbours at the end of stage (b).
Therefore,
v\in F^{\prime}
and
y_{j}\neq v
as
y_{j}\in G^{\prime}
.
To show that there is a
\xi_{y_{j}}\in\{0,1,\ldots,h_{y_{j}-1}\}

which can connect
y_{j}
to
v
,
we must show that
v
is among the largest
h_{y_{j}-1}+1
elements
of
F^{\prime}\backslash\{\sigma(y_{l}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{j+1})\}
.
However, from Lemma
6.6
,
we only need to show that
v>y_{j}
.
The remainder of the proof does this.

Let
v=v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{\alpha}=y_{j}
be the vertices
of the path containing
y_{j}
and
v

with edges

v_{i}\to v_{i+1}
.
Let
\beta
be the smallest index such that
v_{\beta}\in F
.
Using the description of
G
in
(
6.11
c),
we get
v=v_{0}>\ldots>v_{\beta}
when
\beta>0
. Thus,

v=v_{0}\leq v_{\beta}

(6.21)

with equality if and only if
\beta=0
.
On the other hand, if
\beta<\alpha
,
then
v_{\beta}
already has an out-neighbour
and thus
v_{\beta}>y_{j}

(as we are going through elements of
F
in descending order).
Thus,

v_{\beta}\leq v_{\alpha}=y_{j}

(6.22)

with equality if and only if
\beta=\alpha
.
Using (
6.21
), (
6.22
)
and the fact that
v\neq y_{j}
, we obtain

v>y_{j}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

6.4
Computation of weights

To each index
i\in[n]
of a given permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
we assign a weight
{\rm wt}(i)
as follows:

•

if
i
is a cycle valley minimum, we set

{\rm wt}(i)=\lambda\,{{\sf a}}_{{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma)+{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)}
.
For all other cycle valleys, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf a}}_{{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma)+{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

if
i
is a cycle peak, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf b}}_{{\rm lcross}(i,\sigma),\,{\rm lnest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

if
i
is a cycle double fall, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf c}}_{{\rm lcross}(i,\sigma),\,{\rm lnest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

if
i
is a cycle double rise, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf d}}_{{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma),\,{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

and finally, if
i
is a fixed point, we set

{\rm wt}(i)=\lambda\,{{\sf e}}_{{\rm psnest}(i,\sigma)}
.

We then set the weight of the permutation
\sigma
to be

{\rm wt}(\sigma)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\rm wt}(i)
.
It is clear that our polynomial
\widehat{Q}_{n}
defined in (
3.6
)
is simply

\widehat{Q}_{n}({\mbox{{{a}}}},{\mbox{{{b}}}},{\mbox{{{c}}}},{\mbox{{{d}}}},{\mbox{{{e}}}},\lambda)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}{\rm wt}(\sigma)
.

We now use the bijection
\sigma\mapsto(\omega,\xi)

to transfer the weights
{\rm wt}(i)
onto the pair
(\omega,\xi)
.
Let
k=h_{i-1}
the height at which step
s_{i}
starts.
From
6.5
,
6.7
, and
Lemma
6.2
, we immediately see that

•

if
s_{i}
is a rise, then

{\rm wt}(i)=\bigl(1+{\rm I}[\text{$i$ is a cycle valley minimum}](\lambda-1)\bigr)\,{{\sf a}}_{k}
.

(Here
{\rm I}[\hbox{\sl proposition}]=1
if
proposition
is true,
and 0 if it is false.)

•

if
s_{i}
is a fall, then
{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf b}}_{k-1-\xi_{i},\,\xi_{i}}
,

•

if
s_{i}
is a level step of type 1 (here
i
is a cycle double fall),

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf c}}_{k-1-\xi_{i},\,\xi_{i}}
,

•

if
s_{i}
is a level step of type 2 (here
i
is a cycle double rise),

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf d}}_{k-1-\xi_{i},\,\xi_{i}}
,

•

if
s_{i}
is a level step of type 3 (here
i
is a fixed point),

{\rm wt}(i)=\lambda{{\sf e}}_{k}
,

Next, we fix a Motzkin path
\omega
and sum over all labels
\xi

to obtain the weight
W(\omega)
.
This weight is then factorised over the individual steps
s_{i}

to obtain the weight contributed by each step
s_{i}
.
As all the variables involved commute,
we can consider the weights for the steps
s_{i}
in any order.
We do this in the FZ order as follows:

•

if
s_{i}
is a rise, then
i
is a cycle valley.
Among the possible choices of labels
\xi\in[0,k]

there is exactly one which closes a cycle and the others don’t
(Lemma
6.7
).
Therefore, we obtain

a_{k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\sum_{\xi_{i}}\bigl(1+{\rm I}[\text{$i$ is a cycle valley minimum}](\lambda-1)\bigr)\,{{\sf a}}_{k}\;=\;(\lambda+k){{\sf a}}_{k}.

(6.23)

•

if
s_{i}
is a fall, then the weight is

b_{k}\;=\;\sum_{\xi_{i}}{{\sf b}}_{k-\xi_{i}-1,\xi_{i}}

(6.24)

•

if
s_{i}
is a level step of type 1, then the weight is

c_{k}\;=\;\sum_{\xi_{i}}{{\sf c}}_{k-\xi_{i}-1,\xi_{i}}

(6.25)

•

if
s_{i}
is a level step of type 2, then the weight is

d_{k}\;=\;\sum_{\xi_{i}}{{\sf d}}_{k-\xi_{i}-1,\xi_{i}}

(6.26)

•

if
s_{i}
is a level step of type 3, then the weight is

e_{k}\;=\;\lambda{{\sf e}}_{k}

(6.27)

Setting
\beta_{k}={{\sf a}}_{k-1}{{\sf b}}_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}={{\sf c}}_{k}+{{\sf d}}_{k}+{{\sf e}}_{k}

as instructed in Theorem
5.2
,
we obtain the weights
3.8
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
3.3
. ∎

7
D-permutations: Proofs

Motivated by Randrianarivony’s
[
29
]
bijection
for D-o-semiderangements,
Deb and Sokal came up with two different bijections
for D-permutations.
One of them
[
6
, Sections 6.1-6.3]
was used
to prove
[
6
, Theorems 3.3, 3.9, 3.11]
,
i.e., their “first T-fractions” for D-permutations.
The second bijection
[
6
, Section 6.5]
was used to prove

[
6
, Theorems 3.12, 3.13]
,
i.e. their “variant forms of the first T-fractions”
for D-permutations.
We will provide new interpretations to both of these bijections
and use them to prove our theorems for D-permutations.
As both bijections have the same path but different description of labels,
we state the description of the paths for both bijections here.

An
almost-Dyck path
of length
2n
is defined
to be a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{2n})

in the right half-plane
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
and ending at
\omega_{2n}=(2n,0)
,
using the steps
(1,1)
and
(1,-1)
,
that stays always at height
\geq-1
.
Thus, an almost-Dyck path is like a Dyck path
except that

a down step from height 0 to height
-1
is allowed;
note, however, that it must be immediately followed by
an up step back to height 0.
Each non-Dyck part of the path is therefore of the form

(h_{2i-2},h_{2i-1},h_{2i})=(0,-1,0)
.
We write
{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{2n}
for the set of almost-Dyck paths of length
2n
.

A
0-Schröder path
is defined
to be a Schröder path in which long level steps, if any,
occur only at height 0.
We write
{\mathcal{S}}^{0}_{2n}
for the set of 0-Schröder paths of length
2n
.
There is an obvious bijection
\psi\colon{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{2n}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{0}_{2n}

from almost-Dyck paths to 0-Schröder paths:
namely, we replace each down-up pair starting and ending at height 0
with a long level step at height 0.

Both bijections
(
[
6
, Sections 6.1-6.3]

and

[
6
, Section 6.5]
)
are correspondences between
{\mathfrak{D}}_{2n}
and the set of

({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labelled
0-Schröder paths of length
2n
, where
the labels
\xi_{i}
lie in the sets

[
6
, eq. (6.1)]

{\mathcal{A}}_{h}&=\{0,\ldots,\lceil h/2\rceil\}\qquad\quad\;\;\hbox{for $h\geq 0$}\\
{\mathcal{B}}_{h}=\{0,\ldots,\lceil(h-1)/2\rceil\}\quad\hbox{for $h\geq 1$}\\
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\{0\}\\
{\mathcal{C}}_{h}=\emptyset\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\hbox{for $h\geq 1$}

(7.1)

The Schröder path was obtained by
first constructing an almost-Dyck path
\omega

and then transforming it into a 0-Schröder path
{\widehat{\omega}}=\psi(\omega)
.
The almost-Dyck path
\omega
is described as follows:

Step 1: Definition of the almost-Dyck path.

Given a D-permutation
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{2n}
,
we define a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{2n})

starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
,
with steps
s_{1},\ldots,s_{2n}
as follows:

•

If
\sigma^{-1}(i)
is even, then
s_{i}
is a rise.
(Note that in this case we must have
\sigma^{-1}(i)\geq i
,
by definition of D-permutation.)

•

If
\sigma^{-1}(i)
is odd, then
s_{i}
is a fall.
(Note that in this case we must have
\sigma^{-1}(i)\leq i
,
by definition of D-permutation.)

An alternative way of saying this is:

•

If
i
is a cycle valley, cycle double fall or even fixed point,
then
s_{i}
is a rise.

•

If
i
is a cycle peak, cycle double rise or odd fixed point,
then
s_{i}
is a fall.

7.1.5
Computation of weights

The steps in this subsection will be similar to that in
Section
6.4
.
We first assign weights to each index

i\in[2n]
of a given D-permutation
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{2n}
,
and then the weight of a permutation is defined
to be the weights of its indices.
We then use the bijection to transfer the weights to 0-Schröder paths
and then factorise the total weight for each of the steps
which then finally leads us to Theorem
4.4
.

The index weights are assigned as follows:

•

if
i
is a cycle valley minimum, we set

{\rm wt}(i)=\lambda\,{{\sf a}}_{{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma)+{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)}
.
For all other cycle valleys, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf a}}_{{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma)+{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

if
i
is a cycle peak, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf b}}_{{\rm lcross}(i,\sigma),\,{\rm lnest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

if
i
is a cycle double fall, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf c}}_{{\rm lcross}(i,\sigma),\,{\rm lnest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

if
i
is a cycle double rise, we set

{\rm wt}(i)={{\sf d}}_{{\rm ucross}(i,\sigma),\,{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)}
,

•

and finally, if
i
is a fixed point, we set

{\rm wt}(i)\;=\;\begin{cases}\lambda\,{{\sf e}}_{{\rm psnest}(i,\sigma)}\;\;\;\text{if $i$ is even}\\
\lambda\,{{\sf f}}_{{\rm psnest}(i,\sigma)}\;\;\;\text{if $i$ is odd}\end{cases}

We set the weight of the D-permutation
\sigma

to be
{\rm wt}(\sigma)=\prod_{i=1}^{2n}{\rm wt}(i)
.
It is clear that our polynomial
\widehat{Q}_{n}
defined in (
4.9
)
is simply

\widehat{Q}_{n}({\mbox{{{a}}}},{\mbox{{{b}}}},{\mbox{{{c}}}},{\mbox{{{d}}}},{\mbox{{{e}}}},{\mbox{{{f}}}},\lambda)=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{2n}}{\rm wt}(\sigma)
.

We now use the bijection
\sigma\mapsto({\widehat{\omega}},\xi)

to transfer the weights
{\rm wt}(i)
onto the pair
({\widehat{\omega}},\xi)
.
As our polynomial
\widehat{Q}

is almost the same as the polynomial introduced in

[
6
, eq. (3.30)]

except for the extra factor
\lambda^{{\rm cyc}(\sigma)}

and the index of
{{\sf a}}
,
the dependence on cycle peaks, cycle double rises, cycle double falls,
are same as in
[
6
, eq. (3.30)]

but the treatment of cycle valleys and also fixed points is different.
As we use the same bijection used to obtain
the continued fraction (
[
6
, eq. (3.31)/(3.32)]
)
the computation of weights corresponding to
the variables
{{\sf b}},{{\sf c}},{{\sf d}}
are going to be exactly the same.
The weights corresponding to the fixed points are almost same
except
{{\sf e}}_{k}
and
{{\sf f}}_{k}
are now replaced with

\lambda{{\sf e}}_{k}
and
\lambda{{\sf f}}_{k}
, respectively.
Hence, we skip the details for all cycle types and the corresponding steps
in the 0-Schröder path
{\widehat{\omega}}

except for cycle valleys.

Let us now compute the weights contributed by cycle valleys.
These correspond to steps
s_{i}
in
{\widehat{\omega}}
where
s_{i}
is a rise
starting at height
h_{i-1}=2k
.
Then from Equations (
7.6
)/(
7.8
)
we get
\lceil(h_{i-1}-1)/2\rceil=\lceil h_{i}/2\rceil={\rm ucross}(i,\sigma)+{\rm unest}(i,\sigma)
(
=k
).
Also among the possible choices of labels
\xi\in[0,k]

there is exactly one which closes a cycle and the others don’t
(Lemma
7.8
).
Therefore, we obtain

a_{2k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\sum_{\xi}a_{2k,\xi}\;=\;(\lambda+k){{\sf a}}_{2k}.

(7.21)

This completes the proof of Theorem
4.4
. ∎

Step 3: Proof of bijection.

The proof is similar to that presented in
Step 3 Section
7.1.1
,
but using a value-based rather than position-based notion
of inversion table.
Recall that if
S=\{s_{1}<s_{2}<\ldots<s_{k}\}

is a totally ordered set of cardinality
k
,
and
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is a permutation of
S
,
then the (left-to-right) (position-based) inversion table
corresponding to
\bm{x}

is the sequence
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
of nonnegative integers
defined by
p_{\alpha}=\#\{\beta<\alpha\colon\>x_{\beta}>x_{\alpha}\}
.
We now define the (left-to-right)
value-based
inversion table

\bm{p}^{\prime}
by
p^{\prime}_{x_{i}}=p_{i}
;
note that
\bm{p}^{\prime}
is a map from
S
to
\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
,
such that
p^{\prime}_{x_{i}}
is the number of entries to the left of
x_{i}

(in the word
\bm{x}
) that are larger than
x_{i}
.
In particular,
0\leq p^{\prime}_{s_{i}}\leq k-i
.
Given the value-based inversion table
\bm{p}^{\prime}
,
we can reconstruct the sequence
\bm{x}

by working from largest to smallest value,
as follows
[
22
, section 5.1.1]
:
We start from an empty sequence, and insert
s_{k}
.
Then we insert
s_{k-1}
so that the resulting word has
p^{\prime}_{s_{k-1}}

entries to its left.
Next we insert
s_{k-2}
so that the resulting word has
p^{\prime}_{s_{k-2}}

entries to its left, and so on.
[The right-to-left value-based inversion table
\bm{q}^{\prime}

is defined analogously, and the reconstruction proceeds from
smallest to largest.]

We now recall the definitions

F&=\{2,4,\ldots,2n\}\;=\;\hbox{even positions}\\
F^{\prime}=\{i\colon\sigma^{-1}(i)\text{ is even}\}\;=\;\{\sigma(2),\sigma(4),\ldots,\sigma(2n)\}\\

(resp.

\omega
.

We can now describe the map

.
Given the almost-Dyck path
\omega
,

.

\widehat{\xi}

).

p^{\prime}_{i}=\widehat{\xi}_{i}

.

Let

).

(b)

(

)

Remark 7.5
.

For this bijection, a comment similar to Remark
7.2
can be made.
As mentioned earlier in Remark
6.1
,
Foata and Zeilberger used value-based inversion tables for their presentation of the bijection.
We think that it is possible to write a variant of the Foata–Zeilberger bijection

Let
u
be the initial vertex of a path
with at least two vertices in
\left.L^{\prime}\right|_{G^{\prime}\,\cup\,\{y_{n},\ldots,y_{j+1}\}}

for some index
j
(
1\leq j\leq n
). Then
u\in{\rm Cval}
.

Our definition of cycle closers here is different.
Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2n}
be the vertices
[2n]
arranged according to the
variant DS order.
We say that
u_{j}\in[2n]
is a
cycle closer

if the edge
\sigma^{-1}(u_{j})\to u_{j}

is introduced in
\left.L^{\prime}\right|_{\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{j-1}\}}

as an edge between the two ends of a path
turning the path to a cycle.
The following lemma classifies all cycle closers.

Lemma 7.15
.

(Classifying cycle closers) Given a D-permutation
\sigma
,
an element
u\in[2n]
is a cycle closer
if and only if it is a cycle valley minimum,
i.e.,
it is the smallest element in its cycle.

Next, we will count the number of cycle closers.
But before doing that, we require a technical lemma similar to
Lemmas
6.6
,
7.7
.
(As before, recall that if

i\in G\,\cap\,F^{\prime}={\rm Cval}(\sigma)
,
step
s_{i}
must be a rise from height
h_{i-1}
to height
h_{i}

and hence,
h_{i-1}+1=h_{i}
.
Also, from the interpretation of the heights in Lemma
7.1
,
we must have
\lceil h_{i-1}/2\rceil+1=\lceil(h_{i}+1)/2\rceil=f_{i}
.)

Lemma 7.16
.

Given a D-permutation
\sigma
and associated sets
F,G,F^{\prime},G^{\prime}

where
F^{\prime}=\{y_{1}<\ldots<y_{n}\}
,
and an index
j
(
1\leq j\leq n
)
such that
y_{j}\in G\,\cap\,F^{\prime}={\rm Cval}
.
Then the following is true:

\#\{u\in F\backslash\{\sigma^{-1}(y_{n}),\ldots,\sigma^{-1}(y_{j+1})\}\colon\>u>y_{j}\}=\lceil h_{y_{j}-1}/2\rceil+1=\lceil(h_{y_{j}}+1)/2\rceil=f_{y_{j}}

(7.37)

where
h_{i}
denotes the height at position
i
of the almost-Dyck path
\omega

associated to
\sigma

in Step 1
and
f_{i}
is defined in (
7.3
).

Proof.

Notice the equality of the following sets:

\{u\in F\backslash\{\sigma^{-1}(y_{n}),\ldots,\sigma^{-1}(y_{j+1})\}\colon\>u>y_{j}\}\;=\;\{u>y_{j}\>\colon\sigma(u)\leq y_{j}\}.

(7.38)

Next use (
7.3
)
to notice that the set on
the right hand side of (
7.38
)
has cardinality
f_{y_{j}}
.
∎

We are now ready to count the number of cycle closers.

Lemma 7.17

(Counting of cycle closers for D-permutations using variant labels)
.

Fix an almost-Dyck path
\omega
of length
2n
and
construct
F^{\prime},G^{\prime}
(these are completely determined by
\omega
).
Let
y_{j}\in G\cap F^{\prime}
.
Also fix labels
\widehat{\xi}_{u}
for vertices

u\in G^{\prime}\,\cup\,\{y_{n},\ldots,y_{j+1}\}

satisfying (
7.31
).
Then

(a)

The value of
\widehat{\xi}_{y_{j}}
completely determines
if
y_{j}
is a cycle closer or not.

(b)

There is exactly one value
\widehat{\xi}_{y_{j}}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\lceil h_{y_{j}-1}/2\rceil\}

that makes
y_{j}
a cycle closer, and conversely.

8
Final remarks

We began this work only hoping to prove
[
34
, Conjecture 2.3]
.
Our initial guess was that this would involve constructing a new bijection
from permutations to labelled Motzkin paths,
possibly by tweaking the Biane bijection
[
3
]
.
However, on discovering our proof,
we were surprised to see that not only did we not construct any new bijection,
but we used the same variant of the Foata–Zeilberger bijection,
which Sokal and Zeng use to prove their “first” continued fractions for permutations.
As the proofs of the “first” theorems in
[
6
]
for D-permutations
are
parallel
to the proofs of the “first” theorems in
[
34
]
for permutations
(as mentioned in
[
6
, Section 8]
),
we managed to then prove
[
30
, Conjecture 12]

and
[
6
, Conjecture 4.1]
.

We had to introduce a new total order on
[n]
(
[2n]
for D-permutations)
to describe histories for these bijections.
The crucial reason why our proof works is that the
total order is the same for any given Motzkin path (almost-Dyck path for D-permutations),
and also because the associated weights are commutative.
Thus, the order in which we multiply them has no effect on
the product as long as we stick to the same order for any given path.

On the other hand,
Flajolet
[
12
]
provides a more general combinatorial
interpretation with non-commutative weights,
as long as these weights are multiplied using the natural order of the path.
With this in mind,
we think that it will probably not be too difficult to generalize
the “second” theorems
for permutations in

[
34
]
,
and for D-permutations
in
[
6
]

to obtain continued fractions with non-commutative weights.
We predict that this will also be possible for continued fractions obtained using
the Françon–Viennot bijection
[
17
]
.
However, why such continued fractions
might be of interest is not immediate to us,
and at the present moment, we refrain from working out the details.
