Assumption-Lean Honest Inference for Z Z -functionals
Woonyoung Chang, Arun Kumar Kuchibhotla

Abstract
We develop a general assumption-lean framework for constructing uniformly valid confidence sets for functionals defined by moment equalities, referred to as Z Z -functionals. Our approach combines self-normalized statistics with a test inversion principle, enabling honest inference under mild regularity conditions and without explicit variance estimation. To enhance geometric tractability, we propose novel split-normalized and Gateaux-normalized statistics that yield computationally feasible and interpretable confidence sets. A central contribution of this work is a comprehensive non-asymptotic width analysis: we derive high-probability upper bounds on the diameter of the proposed confidence sets, and quantify their proximity to Wald intervals under minimal assumptions. Applications to high-dimensional non-sparse linear and generalized linear regression demonstrate that our procedures achieve valid coverage and near-optimal rate of convergence for the width/diameter, while the classical methods including Wald and bootstrap fail.

Introduction

This paper studies the problem of inference for parameters defined implicitly through a system of population-level equations or so-called moment equalities. Given a measurable function
\psi:\mathcal{Z}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is a class of distribution on a sample space
\mathcal{W}
, our inferential target is a functional, defined as

\theta_{0}:=\theta_{0}(P)\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P}[\psi(Z,\theta_{0})]=0_{d}.

(1)

We refer to such a map
P\mapsto\theta_{0}(P)
as a
Z
-functional. This formulation includes many classical and modern statistical problems such as smooth
M
-estimation and the generalized method of moments, and further supports model-agnostic perspectives in which the target parameter is defined in the absence of any statistical model
(
Buja et al., 2019a
;
Buja et al., 2019b
)
. It is conceptually related to partially identified models defined by moment inequalities, an area of large interest in empirical economics
(
Tamer, 2003
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2007
;
Andrews and Soares, 2010
;
Romano and Shaikh, 2010
)
, where the target may be set-valued. Even with moment equalities (as in our setting), the target
\theta_{0}(P)
need not be unique, especially if the moment equation is obtained as the derivative of a non-convex function. All our coverage guarantees are valid with the interpretation
\mathbb{P}_{P}(\theta_{0}(P)\notin\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})=\sup_{\theta^{*}\in\theta_{0}(P)}\mathbb{P}_{P}(\theta^{*}\notin\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})
. Note that, under such non-uniqueness, the diameters of the proposed confidence sets do not converge to zero in probability. For brevity, throughout, we assume
\theta_{0}(P)
is a singleton.

The primary goal of this paper is to construct valid confidence sets for
\tau(P)=\theta_{0}(P)
or
\tau(P)=\xi^{\top}(\theta_{0}(P))
(for a known contrast vector
\xi
), under minimal assumptions. We focus on honest confidence sets
\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha}
over a class of distributions
\mathcal{P}
, i.e., for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\mathbb{P}_{P}(\tau(P)\notin\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})-\alpha\big)_{+}=0,

where
n
is the sample size. This notion is stronger than pointwise validity, which requires

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\mathbb{P}_{P}(\tau(P)\notin\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})-\alpha\big)_{+}=0.

Pointwise-valid procedures can severely under-cover along some distribution sequences
(
Romano and Wolf, 2000
;
Pötscher, 2002
)
.

While valid coverage is the primary focus of confidence sets, their utility crucially depends on their shape and size. Our secondary goal is to analyze the worst-case diameter of the proposed confidence sets along with their “asymptotic” shape. Given that the proposed confidence sets are implicit (unlike the traditional Wald or resampling ones), this secondary goal is also of importance. Specifically, we seek a sequence
s_{n}
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}({\rm diam}_{\|\cdot\|}(\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})\geq s_{n})=0,

(2)

where
{\rm diam}_{\|\cdot\|}(\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})=\sup\{\|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}\|:\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha}\}
and
\|\cdot\|
is an appropriate norm, often the Euclidean norm. Additionally, we seek a set
\widehat{\mathrm{S}}_{n,\alpha}
with an interpretable shape such as a hyperrectangle or an ellipse, and a sequence
\gamma_{n}=o(s_{n})
as
n\to\infty
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\,\mathbb{P}_{P}({\rm Haus}_{\|\cdot\|}(\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha},\widehat{\mathrm{S}}_{n,\alpha})\geq\gamma_{n})=0,

(3)

where
{\rm Haus}_{\|\cdot\|}(A,B)
is the Hausdorff distance between sets
A
and
B
measured in terms of the norm
\|\cdot\|
. The convergence in (
2
) ensures that the confidence set contracts at rate
s_{n}
uniformly over
\mathcal{P}
, while the convergence in (
3
) ensures that the confidence set has approximately the same shape as that of
\widehat{\mathrm{S}}_{n,\alpha}
. It is important that
\gamma_{n}
converges to zero faster than
s_{n}
; for any sequence of sets
\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{n}
with
\mbox{diam}_{\|\cdot\|}(\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{n})=o_{P}(s_{n})
and
\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{n}\cap\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha}\neq\emptyset
, we have
\mbox{Haus}_{\|\cdot\|}(\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha},\widehat{\mathrm{E}}_{n})=o_{P}(s_{n})
. We refer to such Hausdorff convergence of confidence sets as a second-order analysis of the confidence set.

We remark that in the context of duality of confidence sets and hypothesis tests, the analysis of the diameter yields a stronger statement than the power analysis. The diameter is more closely related to the separation rate for distinguishable hypotheses. We refer the reader to Section 6 of
Robins et al. (2006)
and also to
Nickl and van de Geer (2013)
.

1.1
Literature Review

Standard Inferences Based on Estimators and Their Limitations.

A common route in inference for a general functional
\tau:=\tau(P)
is to rely on pointwise asymptotics of an estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{n}
,

b_{n}^{-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{n}-\tau)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{L}(\omega_{P})\quad\mbox{for all}\quad P\in\mathcal{P},

(4)

for some convergence rate
b_{n}
, the limit distribution
\mathcal{L}
, and a nuisance parameter
\omega_{P}
. A canonical example is asymptotic normality where
b_{n}=n^{-1/2}
and
\mathcal{L}(\omega_{P})=\sigma_{P}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Asymptotic normality with root-n consistency is by no means the only possible setting. For instance, many estimators enjoy cube-root asymptotics with non-Gaussian limits
(
Kim and Pollard, 1990
;
Pflug, 1995
;
Delgado et al., 2001
;
Cattaneo et al., 2020
;
Berthet and Einmahl, 2022
)
.

Two standard approaches to construct a pointwise valid confidence set are: (1) the Wald method and (2) resampling methods. The Wald method, assuming a consistent estimator
\widehat{\omega}_{P}
, yields a confidence interval
[\widehat{\tau}_{n}-b_{n}q_{\alpha/2}(\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\omega}_{P})),\widehat{\tau}_{n}-b_{n}q_{1-\alpha/2}(\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\omega}_{P}))]
where
q_{\gamma}
denotes the upper
\gamma
-th quantile functional. In contrast, resampling methods do not require additional estimation procedures for nuisance parameters. For instance,
m_{n}
-out-of-
n
bootstrap (or sub-sampling) relies on approximating the distribution of
b_{n}^{-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{n}-\tau)
by the conditional distribution of
b_{m_{n}}^{-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{m_{n}}-\widehat{\tau}_{n})
, whereas the bootstrap validity typically requires the suitable stability of the functional
\tau(P)
in
P
, such as Hadamard differentiability
(
Dümbgen, 1993
;
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
)
and sub-sampling requires knowledge of the convergence rate and a careful calibration of the subsample size
(
Politis et al., 1999
;
Bickel et al., 1997
)
.

While these are foundational methods, yielding pointwise valid inferences, they do not guarantee uniform validity in general
(
Andrews and Guggenberger, 2009
;
Andrews and Guggenberger, 2010
)
. Among others, one source of complications arises when the convergence rate
b_{n}
itself depends on the unknown features of
P
. For example, the sample median converges at rate
b_{n}=n^{-1/(2\beta)}
where
\beta
denotes the local Hölder smoothness exponent of the underlying distribution function
(
Knight, 1998
;
Knight, 2002
)
. In such cases, the rate
b_{n}
should be estimated (see, for e.g.,
Bertail et al. (1999)
), but achieving uniform consistency of a rate estimator is generally difficult.

In response to such irregular scenarios, recent work has developed robust inference procedures that maintain uniform validity under minimal assumptions
(
Wasserman et al., 2020
;
Kuchibhotla et al., 2023
;
Park et al., 2023
;
Kim and Ramdas, 2024
;
Takatsu and Kuchibhotla, 2025
;
Kim and Ramdas, 2025
)
. A common strategy in these frameworks is to decouple estimation from inference by sample splitting
(
Wasserman and Roeder, 2009
;
Meinshausen et al., 2009
;
Rinaldo et al., 2019
;
Wasserman et al., 2020
;
Takatsu and Kuchibhotla, 2025
)
or cross-fitting
(
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2022
;
Kim and Ramdas, 2024
)
, allowing valid inference often at a small price in diameters. Not surprisingly, the uniform validity of these methods sometimes yields conservative inference in regular settings
(
Nguyen et al., 2020
;
Shi and Drton, 2024
)
, reflecting a standard notion of trade-off between robustness and efficiency. A possible refinement to mitigate the conservativeness was studied in
Takatsu (2025)
.

Validity and Width Analysis in Increasing Dimensions.

Validity of the traditional confidence sets is suspect when the dimension is allowed to grow with the sample size. For example, in the context of linear regression, asymptotic normality of the traditional
Z
-estimator holds only when the dimension
d
grows slower than
\sqrt{n}
(i.e.,
d=o(\sqrt{n})
) and thus the validity of the Wald interval is limited to such low-dimensional regimes. Similarly, validity guarantees for resampling methods relying on asymptotic distribution could fail for larger dimensions. Only a handful of works provide asymptotically valid confidence sets for the general range of
d=o(n)
without relying on
well-specified linear model
and/or
sparsity
.
Takatsu and Kuchibhotla (2025)
is one such example. In addition, none of these works provides sharp (root-n) inference for linear contrasts of the projection parameter. Although not related to our work, we mention here that there are numerous inference methods in the literature for high-dimensional
Z
-estimators that rely on sparsity assumptions and derive minimax optimal confidence sets
(
Belloni et al., 2015
)
. In the context of non-sparse fitting of linear regression with increasing dimension and (potential) misspecification,
Mammen (1993)
proved the validity of bootstrap confidence intervals when
d=o(n^{3/4})
assuming a finite 24-th moment on the covariates.
Chang et al. (2024)
construct honest intervals for linear contrasts, proving
1/\sqrt{n}
width and validity when
d=\tilde{o}(n^{2/3})
*
*

*

Here,
\tilde{o}
denotes the small-o notation, which disregards the poly-logarithmic dependency on
n
.
, assuming a finite 12-th moment on the covariates. Recently,
Lin et al. (2024)
constructed honest confidence sets for general
Z
-estimators assuming thrice differentiability and sub-Gaussianity of
\psi
along with a restrictive assumption of compact parameter space. The existence of honest confidence intervals for general
Z
-functionals that remain valid for the range of
d=o(n)
remains unknown.

1.2
Main Contributions

This paper adheres to an assumption-lean framework for inference on
Z
-functionals, imposing minimal assumptions and providing non-asymptotic guarantees. Our contributions are as follows.

Honest, Nuisance-Free, and Computationally Tractable Confidence Sets.

We propose a family of self-normalized and sample-splitting-based statistics
SeN Statistic
,
SpN Statistic
, and
GaN Statistic
for honest inference. Unlike the traditional inference methods, our methods do not depend on the limiting distribution of an estimator and, hence, avoid the estimation of asymptotic variance or quantile. With a purely data-driven quantile estimate using a preliminary estimator, the resulting confidence sets are both computationally efficient and geometrically tractable. We derive non-asymptotic coverage guarantees under weak regularity conditions, moment assumptions, and the consistency of the preliminary estimator. When applied to high-dimensional linear and generalized linear regression, these confidence sets remain uniformly valid provided that
d=\tilde{o}(n)
.

Rate-Optimal Concentration for
Z
-Estimators.

By construction, all the proposed confidence sets contain a
Z
-estimator,
\widehat{\theta}
, the solution to an empirical version of (
1
). Given the asymptotic validity of the confidence sets, we get that the expected diameter of our confidence sets is asymptotically larger than
(1-\alpha)\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}
. Given the lack of results on the rate of convergence of
Z
-estimators under our mild conditions, we provide finite-sample concentration inequality (Theorem
7
) for
Z
-estimators, significantly generalizing the results of
Lin et al. (2024)
and
Gauss and Nagler (2024)
by removing their restrictive compact parameter space assumption, among others. Applied to generalized linear models, Theorem
11
proves
\sqrt{n/d}
-consistency under finite
(2+\varepsilon)
-moment assumptions and without strong regularity. To our knowledge, this is the first such result that accommodates both heavy-tailed covariates and unbounded parameter spaces. The rate closely mirrors that of least squares estimators in linear regression
(
Oliveira, 2016
;
Kuchibhotla et al., 2020
)
, and therefore cannot be substantially improved in general. See also
Chardon et al. (2024)
for the
\sqrt{n/d}
-rate optimality results for the MLE in misspecified logistic regression.

Non-Asymptotic Width Control.

We provide bounds on the diameter of the confidence set constructed via the SpN statistic for general
Z
-functionals under a mild curvature and a (one-sided quantitative) stochastic equicontinuity condition. When applied to misspecified generalized linear models, Theorem
13
shows that the diameter scales as
\sqrt{d\log(ed)/n}
with probability converging to 1, matching the convergence rate of the
Z
-estimator up to logarithmic factors. This result holds under finite moment assumptions and does not rely on model specification or strong regularity conditions. In misspecified linear regression, we further show that the SeN, SpN, and GaN procedures yield confidence sets with a minimax optimal diameter scaling of
\sqrt{d/n}
with a high probability.

Honest confidence intervals for linear contrasts and second order width analysis.

We introduce a general framework for constructing confidence intervals for one-dimensional contrasts via directional projection. These intervals are uniformly valid and, under suitable regularity conditions, converge to the classical Wald interval in Hausdorff distance. In high-dimensional linear regression, the resulting confidence intervals are honest under finite-moment assumptions and dimension growth
d=o(n)
. Moreover, they achieve a width scaling of
1/\sqrt{n}+d/n
with a high probability. To our knowledge, these are the first confidence intervals to attain this rate under moment assumptions and model misspecification.

Outline

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the proposed confidence sets based on self-normalized (SeN), split-normalized (SpN), and Gateaux-normalized (GaN) statistics, and establish their non-asymptotic coverage guarantees under weak moment and regularity conditions. Section 3 focuses on inference for one-dimensional linear contrasts and develops uniformly valid confidence intervals with sharp second-order width bounds. Section 4 applies the framework to high-dimensional non-sparse linear and generalized linear regression, deriving explicit rates for coverage and width. Section 5 presents numerical studies that evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed procedures in various data-generating processes. Section 6 concludes with a comprehensive summary and a discussion of potential extensions and open problems. Proofs of theorems, corollaries, and lemmas are included in Appendix.

Inference for high-dimensional
Z
-functional

The motivation for our approach is the reduction from functional inference to high-dimensional mean inference problems via the inversion principle. This principle has widely used in the construction of confidence sets for partially identified models
(
Imbens and Manski, 2004
;
Romano and Shaikh, 2014
;
Andrews and Mikusheva, 2016
)
. The defining property of the
Z
-functional is that
\psi(Z,\theta_{0}(P))
is a
d
-dimensional mean zero random vector under
P
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. For a fixed
\theta\in\Theta
, let
\widehat{\mathfrak{C}}_{n,\alpha}(\theta)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a confidence set constructed from
\psi(Z_{1},\theta),\ldots,\psi(Z_{n},\theta)
for
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi(Z,\theta_{0}(P)]
and define the inversion-based confidence set
\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha}:=\{\theta\in\Theta:0_{d}\in\widehat{\mathfrak{C}}_{n,\alpha}(\theta)\}.
From the construction, it follows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}(\theta_{0}(P)\notin\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}(0_{d}\notin\widehat{\mathfrak{C}}_{n,\alpha}(\theta_{0}(P))).

Thus, honest inference for
Z
-functionals reduces to the construction of an honest confidence set for the high-dimensional mean
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi(Z,\theta)]
, evaluated at
\theta=\theta_{0}(P)
.

Another property of
Z
-functional is its affine equivariance under linear transformations. For any
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi(Z,\theta)]=0_{d}
implies that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[Q\psi(Z,\theta)]=0_{d}
. Consequently, an honest confidence set for the transformed vector
\mathbb{E}_{P}[Q\psi(Z,\theta)]
can be pulled back as the preimage of the map
u\mapsto Qu
, yielding an honest confidence set for
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi(Z,\theta)]
. This is valid even if
Q
is rank-deficient; in such cases, the resulting preimage is an affine cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and does not constrain the directions orthogonal to the row space of
Q
. Nevertheless, introducing a linear transformation can be beneficial in shaping the resulting confidence set. Suitable choices of
Q
may allow the construction of approximately rectangular confidence sets, which are both interpretable and efficient for coordinate-wise inference. See Figure
1
for an illustration of how different choices of
Q
yield different shapes; see

for details on the data generating scheme for this figure.

2.1
Construction based on Self-Normalization

Given a sample of
n
i.i.d. observations
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\sim P
, we adopt a sample-splitting scheme with subsets
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{Z_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}=\{Z_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\}
where
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
form a partition of indices
[n]
. Let
n_{1}=|\mathcal{I}_{1}|
and
n_{2}=|\mathcal{I}_{2}|
, and define, for
\theta\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z,\theta)],

\displaystyle\quad V(\theta)={\rm Var}(\psi(Z,\theta)),

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\psi(Z_{i},\theta),

\displaystyle\quad\widehat{V}_{1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\psi(Z_{i},\theta)-\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta))(\psi(Z_{i},\theta)-\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta))^{\top}.

We use the subscript
\cdot_{1}
to denote data-driven quantities computed using
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and
\cdot_{2}
to denote those computed using
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. Moreover, we write
\widehat{\cdot}
to denote a specific estimator, for e.g.,
Z
-estimator, while we write
\widetilde{\cdot}
to denote an arbitrary estimator.

For any
\mathcal{D}_{2}
-measurable matrix
\widetilde{Q}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the self-normalized (SeN) statistic is defined as

T^{\rm SeN}(\theta)=T^{\rm SeN}(\theta;\widetilde{Q}_{2}):=\max_{j\in[d]}\frac{\sqrt{n_{1}}|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta)|}{\sqrt{\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\widehat{V}_{1}(\theta)\widetilde{Q}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{j}}}.

(SeN Statistic)

This construction can be used without sample splitting by setting
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\emptyset
and choosing a non-stochastic matrix
Q
.
In the univariate case (
d=1
), the SeN statistics converge in distribution to the standard normal law under weak conditions
(
Giné et al., 1997
)
. In multivariate settings (
d\geq 2
), the distribution can be approximated with the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of a
d
-dimensional mean zero Gaussian whose variance is a correlation matrix.

Due to non-linear dependence of the SeN statistic on
\theta
, the geometry of the resulting confidence set is generally intractable. To improve tractability, we introduce a split-normalized (SpN) statistic. Given any estimator
\widetilde{\theta}_{2}\in\Theta
obtained from
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, we define

T^{\rm SpN}(\theta)=T^{\rm SpN}(\theta;\widetilde{\theta}_{2},\widetilde{Q}_{2}):=\max_{j\in[d]}\frac{\sqrt{n_{1}}|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta)|}{\sqrt{\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\widehat{V}_{1}(\widetilde{\theta}_{2})\widetilde{Q}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{j}}}.

(SpN Statistic)

Unlike the SeN statistic, the SpN statistic has an explicit dependence on a preliminary estimator computed from
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. This preliminary estimator is used to remove the dependence on
\theta
in the denominator of the SeN statistic.

Distributional approximations of the SeN and SpN statistics with
\theta=\theta_{0}(P)
depend heavily on the relative growth of dimension with respect to the sample size, and also on the moment assumptions. For example, if
\psi(Z,\theta_{0})
is sub-Gaussian, then
d
can be as large as
\exp(n^{1/3-\varepsilon})
, but if
\psi(Z,\theta_{0})
has only a finite fourth moment, then
d
can at most be
n^{1-\varepsilon}

(
Chernozhukov et al., 2023
, Corollary 2.1)
. Such a phase transition in the Gaussian approximation depending on moment conditions has been studied in
Kock and Preinerstorfer (2024)
. Rather surprisingly, it is possible to consider a statistic whose distributional approximation is independent of the dimension
d
. We finally introduce the GaN statistic:

T^{\rm GaN}(\theta):=T^{\rm GaN}(\theta;\widetilde{\theta}_{2},\widetilde{Q}_{2})=\frac{\sqrt{n_{1}}|(\theta-\widetilde{\theta}_{2})^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta)|}{\sqrt{(\theta-\widetilde{\theta}_{2})^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\widehat{V}_{1}(\widetilde{\theta}_{2})\widetilde{Q}_{2}^{\top}(\theta-\widetilde{\theta}_{2})}}.

(GaN Statistic)

When
\psi
corresponds to the gradient of a loss
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\theta)
, the GaN statistic is a normalized Gateaux directional derivative of
\theta\mapsto n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\mathcal{L}(X_{i},\theta)
along the direction
\widetilde{Q}_{2}^{\top}(\theta-\widetilde{\theta}_{2})
, motivating its name. The associated confidence set is then defined as

\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}^{\rm GaN}_{\alpha}:=\{\theta\in\Theta:T^{\rm GaN}(\theta)\leq z_{\alpha/2}\},

where
z_{\alpha/2}
denotes the upper
\alpha/2
-th quantile of standard normal distribution.

Self-normalization combined with and without sample splitting has been widely used in the hypothesis testing literature.
Chernozhukov et al. (2019)
use an analogue of the SeN statistic for testing in moment inequality models.
Kim and Ramdas (2024)
developed a cross-fitted studentized statistic (with sample splitting) for dimension-agnostic inference. Although there is a duality between hypothesis tests and confidence sets, these methods yield computationally and geometrically intractable confidence sets when applied directly. To elaborate, when testing the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\theta_{0}=\theta^{*}
using a non-negative statistic
T(\theta)
, the level
\alpha
-test would reject
H_{0}
if
T(\theta^{*})>K_{\alpha}(\theta^{*})
, for some data-driven (usually bootstrap-based) quantile
K_{\alpha}(\theta^{*})
. The test-inversion based confidence set would be
\{\theta\in\Theta:\,T(\theta)\leq K_{\alpha}(\theta)\}
. The computation of such a confidence set requires the computation of the quantile for each
\theta\in\Theta
, while for hypothesis testing, the computation of the quantile is only required at the hypothesized value of
\theta_{0}
. Hence, for tractability, we use sample splitting to compute a preliminary estimator
\tilde{\theta}_{2}
from
\mathcal{D}_{2}
and use that for the computation of the quantile. Note that such a substitution with a preliminary estimator is not needed for the GaN statistic because
T^{\mathrm{GaN}}(\theta_{0})
can be approximated by the folded standard Gaussian random variable. For the SeN and SpN statistics, the quantile is computed as follows: Let

\Gamma(\theta;Q):=\mathrm{Corr}(QV(\theta)Q^{\top})\quad\mbox{for}\quad\theta\in\theta~\mbox{and}~Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Define the method of moment estimator for this correlation matrix, obtained from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, as

\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}(\theta;Q)={\rm Corr}(Q\widehat{V}_{1}(\theta)Q^{\top})\quad\mbox{for}\quad\theta\in\theta~\mbox{and}~Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, define the Gaussian multiplier bootstrap
(
Chen, 2018
;
Rinaldo et al., 2019
)
quantile function
K^{\rm b}_{\alpha}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
as

K^{\rm b}_{\alpha}(\theta)=K^{\rm b}_{\alpha}(\theta;\widetilde{Q}_{2}):=\inf\big\{t\geq 0:n^{-1}{\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\|W_{i}^{\rm b}\|_{\infty}>t)\leq\alpha\big\},

where
W_{i}^{\rm b}\mid Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{d},\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}(\theta;\widetilde{Q}_{2}))
for
i\in[n]
. Let
\widehat{K}^{\rm b}_{\alpha}:=K^{\rm b}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\theta}_{2})
evaluated only at a preliminary estimator. We then define the SeN and SpN confidence sets as

\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SeN}:=\{\theta\in\Theta:T^{\rm SeN}(\theta)\leq\widehat{K}^{\rm b}_{\alpha}\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SpN}:=\{\theta\in\Theta:T^{\rm SpN}(\theta)\leq\widehat{K}^{\rm b}_{\alpha}\}.

In the following subsections, we provide results on the miscoverage errors and diameter of the confidence sets. Throughout, we assume that
n
is even and
n_{1}=n_{2}=n/2
for convenience. This supposition is not required for proof techniques and is only made to reduce the notational burden.

2.2
Non-asymptotic Coverage Guarantees

In this section, we establish finite-sample coverage guarantees for the proposed confidence sets under general moment conditions and weak regularity conditions.

To describe the required moment conditions, we introduce Lyapunov ratios. For any
\theta\in\Theta
, recall
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z,\theta)]
, and define

L_{q}(\theta):=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{(\mathbb{E}[|u^{\top}(\psi(Z,\theta)-\mu(\theta))|^{q}])^{1/q}}{(\mathbb{E}[|u^{\top}(\psi(Z,\theta)-\mu(\theta))|^{2}])^{1/2}}\quad\mbox{for}~q\geq 2.

We have
1\leq L_{q}(\theta)
for all
\theta
and
q\geq 2
, and we write
L_{q}:=L_{q}(\theta_{0})
for the shorthand. Lyapunov ratio is closely related to
\mathcal{L}^{q}
–
\mathcal{L}^{2}
moment equivalence for one-dimensional projections, a significant weakening of exponentially decaying tail assumptions. Moreover, this ratio is invariant under affine transformations of
\psi
, aligning with the invariance of the target
\theta_{0}
.

To characterize the regularity conditions, let
\mathbb{B}(\theta_{0},\delta)=\{\theta\in\Theta:\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\leq\delta\}
be a
\delta
-neighborhood of
\theta_{0}
. For
V(\theta)={\rm Var}(\psi(Z,\theta))
, we define, for
\delta>0
,

\phi(\delta):=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{B}(\theta_{0},\delta)}\left\|V(\theta_{0})^{-1/2}(V(\theta)-V(\theta_{0}))V(\theta_{0})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\rm op}.

Moreover, we define
\gamma_{q}(\delta):=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{B}(\theta_{0},\delta)}L_{q}(\theta)/L_{q}(\theta_{0})
for
q\geq 2
.

Remark 1
.

The function
\phi
measures the local smoothness of the variance map
\theta\mapsto V(\theta)
around
\theta_{0}
. If
\psi
is local Lipschitz in
\theta
, then
\phi(\delta)=O(\delta)
as
\delta\to 0
. In particular,
\phi
encodes moment-level regularity and can be controlled without the continuity of
\psi
itself. Similarly,
\gamma_{q}
captures the variation in the moment level and remains uniformly bounded around
\theta_{0}
, that is,
\gamma_{q}(\delta)=O(1)
as
\delta\to 0
, under mild conditions, such as the local continuity of
\theta\mapsto L_{q}(\theta)
.

Theorem
1
provides non-asymptotic bounds on the coverage of the SeN confidence set under minimal assumptions.

Theorem 1
.

For
q>2
, there exists a constant
C=C(q)
, depending only on
q
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\left(\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0}\notin\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SeN})-\alpha\right)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{L_{4}\log^{5/4}(ed)}{n^{1/4}}+C\gamma_{q}(\delta)\left(\frac{L^{2}_{q}\log^{3}(ed)d^{2/q}}{n^{1-2/q}}\right)^{\frac{q}{2q+2}}

\displaystyle+C\log(en)\sqrt{\phi(\delta)}+\mathbb{P}(\|\widetilde{\theta}_{2}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\geq\delta),

(5)

for any
\delta>0
. Suppose further that there exists
\lambda_{\circ}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{\rm min}(\Gamma(\theta_{0},\widetilde{Q}_{2}))\geq\lambda_{\circ})=1.

(6)

For
q\geq 4
, there exists a constant
C=C(q,\lambda_{\circ})
such that for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\left(\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0}\notin\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SeN})-\alpha\right)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq C\log^{2}(en)\gamma_{q}(\delta)\left(\frac{L^{2}_{q}\log^{3}(ed)d^{2/q}}{n^{1-2/q}}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+2}\wedge\frac{q}{2(q-2)}}

\displaystyle+C\log^{2}(en)\phi(\delta)+\mathbb{P}(\|\widetilde{\theta}_{2}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\geq\delta).

(7)

The general bound (
5
) requires finite fourth moments, but is particularly useful when
L_{q}

(q\in(2,4))
is sufficiently smaller than
L_{4}
. Although implicit, the quantities on the right hand side of (
5
) and (
7
) depend on
P
, the data generating distribution. Hence, Theorem
1
implies uniform validity of SeN confidence set under mild regularity conditions by taking the supremum on both sides of (
5
) and (
7
) over
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

As highlighted in Remark
1
, under mild regularity conditions, we have
\phi(\delta)\to 0
and
\gamma_{q}(\delta)=O(1)
as
\delta\to 0
. Additionally, if
L_{4}
is uniformly bounded and a preliminary estimator
\widetilde{\theta}_{2}
is uniformly consistent over a distribution class
\mathcal{P}
, then the SeN confidence set is honest over
\mathcal{P}
provided
d\log^{6}(en)=o(n)
. While uniform consistency may appear strong, Theorem
4
establishes such a property under mild conditions; see also Theorem
11
. For distribution classes allowing for higher moments, the dimension condition can be relaxed further.

The bounds in Theorem
1
constitute two sources of approximation error: (i) one-sided Berry–Esséen bounds for the self-normalized sums, and (ii) quantile estimation error from the estimated correlation matrix at a preliminary estimator. The former relies on recent quantitative high-dimensional CLTs for standardized averages where the minimal eigenvalue condition (
6
) is crucial. When this condition is satisfied, sharper rates follow from CLTs tailored to non-degenerate Gaussian approximations
(
Lopes, 2022
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2023
)
; otherwise, the general rate in (
5
) applies, relying only on control of the diagonal entries of the covariance matrix
(
Chernozhuokov et al., 2022
)
. In our case, the diagonals are ones, so no further assumption is needed. The quantile approximation error is controlled by Gaussian comparison inequalities
(
Lopes, 2022
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2023
)
, and the applicability of sharper results again depends on the validity of the condition (
6
).

The dimension dependence in (
5
) and (
7
) arises from both sources of approximations. From the approximation error in Gaussian approximation (high-dimensional CLT), we use the bound:

\mathbb{E}^{1/q}[\max_{j\in[d]}|U_{j}|^{q}]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/q}[{\textstyle\sum}_{j=1}^{d}|U_{j}|^{q}]\leq d^{1/q}L_{q}~\mbox{where}~U_{j}=\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}V(\theta_{0})^{-1/2}\psi(Z,\theta_{0}),~j\in[d].

(8)

While this appears to be crude, the dimension factor cannot be improved under only the finite
q
-th moment assumptions. Under a stronger distributional assumption, this can be relaxed to logarithmic dependence; for e.g., if
U_{j}
admits a sub-Weibull(
\beta
) tail, then
d^{1/q}
in (
8
) improves to
\log^{1/\beta}(ed)
up to constants in
\beta
and
q
; see
Vladimirova et al. (2020)
and
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty (2022)
for details on the sub-Weibull random variables. On the other hand, the approximation error from the quantile appears via the estimation error of
\tilde{\theta}_{2}
which often restricts the growth rate of
d
, unless additional structural assumptions are available. For example, with sparsity, a consistent estimator can be obtained for
\theta_{0}
even if
d
grows faster than
n
. In a similar vein, if the correlation matrix
\Gamma(\theta;Q)=\mbox{Corr}(QV(\theta)Q^{\top})
is sparse or banded, then this structure can be used to reduce the dimension requirement of this approximation error.

Next, we establish the coverage bounds for the SpN confidence sets.

Theorem 2
.

The miscoverage bound in (
5
) applies to the confidence set
\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SpN}
. Under minimum eigenvalue condition in (
6
), the sharper bound (
7
) applies.

The SpN statistic shares the same “limiting” distribution as the SeN statistic. The only modification lies in normalization, and this, in turn, is absorbed into the existing bounds in Theorem
1
up to constants. Therefore, the SpN confidence set inherits the same uniform validity guarantees as the SeN confidence set, without requiring additional conditions.

We now turn to the GaN statistic. A key feature of the GaN construction is that it reduces inference to a one-dimensional problem. This simplification removes the explicit dependence on the dimension
d
in the distributional approximation. The following result provides a two-sided coverage bound for the GaN confidence set under weaker moment conditions.

Theorem 3
.

For
q>2
, let
\widebar{q}=q\wedge 4
. There exists a constant
C:=C(q)>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\left|\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0}\notin\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}^{\rm GaN})-\alpha\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{L_{\widebar{q}}^{2}\gamma_{\widebar{q}}^{2}(\delta)}{n^{1-2/\widebar{q}}}\right)^{\frac{\widebar{q}}{\widebar{q}+2}}+C\phi(\delta)+\mathbb{P}(\|\widetilde{\theta}_{2}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}>\delta)~\mbox{for any }\delta>0.

Although the bound in Theorem
3
does not explicitly depend on the dimension
d
, the tail behavior of the preliminary estimator
\widetilde{\theta}_{2}
may still induce a dimension dependence.

2.3
Rate of Convergence of
Z
-estimator and Width Analysis of Confidence Sets

This section analyzes the width properties of honest confidence sets, with a focus on the SpN confidence set, which is designed to facilitate geometric tractability. While our results are stated in general form, explicit rates can be derived in more structured settings; see Section
4.2
for an application to generalized linear regression.

Defining
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
as the
Z
-estimator from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, i.e.,
\bar{\psi}_{1}(\widehat{\theta}_{1})=0_{d}
, it is clear that
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
belongs to all our confidence sets. Because each of them contains
\theta_{0}
with an asymptotic probability of at least
1-\alpha
, we get that, under mild regularity conditions,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\mbox{diam}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha})\geq\|\widehat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\big)\geq 1-\alpha.

Because of this, as a preliminary but instructive step, we first analyze the rate of convergence of the
Z
-estimator.

The study of
Z
-estimators in increasing dimensions dates back to the seminal work of
Huber (1973)
, who analyzed
M
-estimators in linear models. This was extended by
Yohai and Maronna (1979)
;
Portnoy (1984)
;
Portnoy (1985)
;
Portnoy (1986)
;
Portnoy (1988)
;
Mammen (1993)
, who established consistency and asymptotic normality under various conditions. An approach based on stochastic equicontinuity was introduced by
Pakes and Pollard (1989)
and later refined by
He and Shao (1996)
;
He and Shao (2000)
for the convex
M
-estimation, although the conditions presented are often implicit. Recently,
Gauss and Nagler (2024)
showed consistency for
d\log(en)=o(n)
and asymptotic normality for
d^{2}\log(en)=o(n)
, under weak moment assumptions and compact parameter space.
All of these works are primarily qualitative.

In the modern high-dimensional setting, the finite-sample guarantee is essential, and several works fall within the scope of a model-agnostic framework. In linear regression,
Oliveira (2016)
proved the
\sqrt{n/d}
-consistency of least squares under the fourth moment conditions
and this rate is minimax optimal
(
Mourtada, 2022
)
.
Kuchibhotla et al. (2020)
established the Berry-Esséen bound for the studentized least squares, where their bound vanishes if
d^{2}=\tilde{o}(n)
. For general
Z
-estimators, the key technical challenge lies in controlling local curvature, often tied to the Jacobian of the estimating equation. A relatively weak condition is self-concordance
(
Bach, 2010
)
, which has been leveraged in finite-sample risk bounds
(
Bach, 2010
;
Ostrovskii and Bach, 2021
)
and deterministic approximations of estimators
(
Kuchibhotla, 2018
)
. In particular,
Ostrovskii and Bach (2021)
showed
\sqrt{n/d}
-consistency of estimators that minimize self-concordant loss under sub-Gaussianity and
d^{2}\log(en)=o(n)
. Recently,
Lin et al. (2024)
showed the
\sqrt{n/d}
-consistency of smooth
Z
-estimators under sub-Gaussianity and compact parameter space.

We propose the following assumptions.

(M)

For
V(\theta_{0})={\rm Var}(\psi(Z,\theta_{0}))
, there exists
\overline{\lambda}_{V}>0
such that
\lambda_{\rm max}(V(\theta_{0}))\leq\overline{\lambda}_{V}<\infty
.

(C1)

There exists a real-valued function
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{Z}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\theta)
is convex and differentiable, and
\psi(\cdot,\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\theta)
. For
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z,\theta)]
, there exist
\delta_{0}>0
and non-decreasing function
\underline{\mu}(\cdot):(0,\delta_{0})\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\underline{\mu}(\delta)\leq\inf_{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\eta^{\top}\mu(\theta_{0}+\delta\eta)
.

(C2)

For
n\geq 1
, there exists
u_{1,n}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that, for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}^{*}\bigg(\sqrt{n/2}\sup_{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}-\eta^{\top}\left(\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta_{0}+\delta\eta)-\mu(\theta_{0}+\delta\eta)-\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta_{0})\right)\geq u_{1,n}(\delta;\varepsilon)\bigg)\leq\varepsilon.

We adopt the outer probability
\mathbb{P}^{*}
to account for possible measurability issues; see Section 1.2 of
van der Vaart and Wellner (1996)
for a comprehensive discussion.

Assumption
(C1)
links the problem of
Z
-estimation with the convex
M
-estimation. We note that for
\eta=(\theta-\theta_{0})/\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{2}
, the difference in population objectives satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(X,\theta)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(X,\theta_{0})]=\int_{0}^{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{2}}\eta^{\top}\mu(\theta_{0}+\delta\eta)\,d\delta\geq\int_{0}^{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{2}}\underline{\mu}(\delta)\,d\delta.

Hence,
(C1)
ensures that the parameter
\theta_{0}
is the unique minimizer of the population objective
\theta\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(X,\theta)]
, as well as the unique root of the moment equation
\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z,\theta)]=0_{d}
. Assumption
(C2)
ensures that the local empirical process remains controlled, providing a finite-sample analogue of the stochastic equicontinuity conditions studied in
Pakes and Pollard (1989)
,
He and Shao (1996)
, and
He and Shao (2000)
.

The following theorem establishes a non-asymptotic concentration of the
Z
-estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume
(M)
,
(C1)
, and
(C2)
. For
q>2
, there exists a constant
C=C(q)>0
such that: for any
\varepsilon>0
, let
r_{n}:=r_{n}(\varepsilon)
satisfy the inequalities:
0<r_{n}<\delta_{0}
and

\underline{\mu}(r_{n})>\frac{u_{1,n}(r_{n};\varepsilon)}{\sqrt{n}}+C\overline{\lambda}_{V}\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2/\varepsilon)}{n}}+\frac{L_{q}d^{1/2}}{n^{1-1/q}\varepsilon^{1/q}}\bigg].

(9)

Then,
\mathbb{P}(\|\widehat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}>r_{n})\leq 2\varepsilon
.

Hence, the convergence rate is determined implicitly by the local curvature, via
\underline{\mu}(\cdot)
, and the local complexity of empirical processes through
u_{1,n}(\cdot;\cdot)
.

To analyze the width of the SpN confidence set, we impose a spectral condition on
\widetilde{Q}_{2}
.

(S)

There exist constants
\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\underline{\sigma}\leq\sigma_{\rm min}(\widetilde{Q}_{2})\leq\sigma_{\rm max}(\widetilde{Q}_{2})\leq\overline{\sigma})=1
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}_{2}
be a
Z
-estimator obtained from
\mathcal{D}_{2}
and we denote

\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}^{\rm SpN}(K_{n}):=\{\theta:T^{\rm SpN}(\theta;\widehat{\theta}_{2},\widetilde{Q}_{2})\leq K_{n}\}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\{K_{n}>0:n\geq 1\}.

(10)

We now establish a non-asymptotic bound for the diameter of SpN confidence sets.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose the assumptions made in Theorem
4
are met, and assume
(S)
. For
q\geq 4
, there exists a constant
C=C(q)>0
such that: for any
\varepsilon>0
, let
r_{n}:=r_{n}(\varepsilon)
be such that
\mathbb{P}(\|\widehat{\theta}_{2}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}>r_{n})\leq 2\varepsilon
, and let
s_{n}:=s_{n}(\varepsilon)
satisfy that
0<s_{n}<\delta_{0}
and

\underline{\mu}(s_{n})>\frac{u_{1,n}(s_{n};\varepsilon)}{\sqrt{n}}+C\frac{\overline{\sigma}^{2}\overline{\lambda}_{V}(1\vee\phi(r_{n}))}{\underline{\sigma}^{2}}\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{K_{n}^{2}d+\log(2/\varepsilon)}{n}}+\frac{\gamma_{q}(r_{n})L_{q}K_{n}d^{1/2}}{n^{1-1/q}\varepsilon^{1/q}}\bigg].

(11)

Then,
\mathbb{P}({\rm diam}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}^{\rm SpN}(K_{n}))>2s_{n})\leq 4\varepsilon
.

The SpN confidence set contracts at a rate of
s_{n}
, depending on the estimation rate
r_{n}
, the quantile
K_{n}
and the complexity of empirical processes
u_{1,n}
, all against curvature
\underline{\mu}
. It is easy to show that the bootstrap quantile used for our construction scales as
\sqrt{\log(ed/\alpha)}
.

Lemma 1
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, if
\sqrt{6\log(en)/n}\leq\alpha
, then for any
\mathcal{D}_{1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}
-measurable quantities
\widetilde{\theta}\in\Theta
and
\widetilde{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\mathbb{P}(K_{\alpha}^{\rm b}(\widetilde{\theta};\widetilde{Q})\leq\sqrt{2\log(4d/\alpha)})\geq 1-1/n
.

3
Inference for One-Dimensional Contrasts

We now consider a different inferential target:
\xi^{\top}\theta_{0}
for a non-stochastic unit vector
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. This includes inference on marginal coefficients and non-sparse linear contrasts. The goal of this section is to construct honest confidence intervals for a scalar
\xi^{\top}\theta_{0}
. From the confidence set for
\theta_{0}
, a confidence set for
\xi^{\top}\theta_{0}
readily follows by the idea of projection, i.e.,

\{\xi^{\top}\theta:\,\theta\in\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha}\},

is an asymptotically
(1-\alpha)
confidence set for
\xi^{\top}\theta_{0}
whenever
\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}_{n,\alpha}
is an asymptotically
(1-\alpha)
confidence set for
\theta_{0}
. This projection set is, in general, extremely conservative and wide because it is simultaneously valid for all
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Hence, it is conceivable that a much shorter confidence set can be constructed for
\xi^{\top}\theta_{0}
.

Our constructions are based on directional self-normalization and split-normalization, respectively. Let the
Z
-estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{2}
be our preliminary estimator and consider two sets:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SeN}=\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SeN}(\widetilde{Q}_{2})

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\theta:\frac{\sqrt{n_{1}}|\xi^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta)|}{\sqrt{\xi^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\widehat{V}_{1}(\theta)\widetilde{Q}_{2}^{\top}\xi}}\leq z_{\alpha/2}\Bigg\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SpN}=\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SpN}(\widehat{\theta}_{2},\widetilde{Q}_{2})

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\theta:\frac{\sqrt{n_{1}}|\xi^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{1}(\theta)|}{\sqrt{\xi^{\top}\widetilde{Q}_{2}\widehat{V}_{1}(\widehat{\theta}_{2})\widetilde{Q}_{2}^{\top}\xi}}\leq z_{\alpha/2}\Bigg\}.

(12)

These sets define valid confidence sets for
\theta_{0}
, but are, in general, unbounded. Hence, we intersect them with the SpN confidence set whose target coverage
1-\alpha/n
converges to 1 as
n\to\infty
. Our confidence intervals are given as projections of intersected sets:

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm CI}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SeN}=\{\xi^{\top}\theta:\theta\in\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{\rm SeN}_{\alpha,\xi}\cap\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}^{\rm SpN}_{\alpha/n}\},\qquad\widehat{\rm CI}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SpN}=\{\xi^{\top}\theta:\theta\in\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SpN}\cap\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}^{\rm SpN}_{\alpha/n}\}.

Theorem
6
establishes the validity of these confidence intervals under mild conditions.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose assumptions in Theorem
4
are met and assume
(S)
and (
6
). As per Theorem
4
, let
r_{n}
be such that
\mathbb{P}(\|\widehat{\theta}_{2}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\geq r_{n})\leq 1/n
. Define

\mathcal{E}_{n,q}=\gamma_{q}(r_{n})\log^{2}(en)\left(\frac{L^{2}_{q}\log^{3}(ed)d^{2/q}}{n^{1-2/q}}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+2}\wedge\frac{q}{2(q-2)}}+\log^{2}(en)\phi(r_{n})+\frac{1}{n}.

For
q\geq 4
, there exists a constant
C=C(q)>0
such that

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\big(\mathbb{P}(\xi^{\top}\theta_{0}\notin\widehat{\rm CI}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SeN})-\alpha\big)_{+}\leq C\mathcal{E}_{n,q}.

The same miscoverage guarantee holds for
\widehat{\rm CI}_{\alpha,\xi}^{\rm SpN}
.

Thus, the SeN and SpN confidence intervals are valid under finite fourth moments, provided that
\gamma_{4}(r_{n})=O(1)
,
\phi(r_{n})=\tilde{o}(1)
and
d=\tilde{o}(n)
, as
n\to\infty
.

In the context of linear regression, we demonstrate that the proposed confidence intervals exhibit favorable width properties: they scale as
1/\sqrt{n}+d/n
, which recovers the parametric rate when
d\ll\sqrt{n}
. The width analyses of the SeN and SpN confidence intervals for the general setting are provided in Appendix A.

4
Applications

In this section, we apply the general results from the previous sections to linear and generalized linear regression.

4.1
High-dimensional Misspecified Linear Model

Linear regression is among the most widely used statistical tools in science. Define the projection parameter
(
Buja et al., 2019a
)
as the minimizer of the population least squares criterion:

\beta_{0}:=~\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[(Y-X^{\top}\theta)^{2}],

(13)

where
Y\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar response and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a random covariate vector. Under finite second moments,
\beta_{0}
solves the moment condition
\mathbb{E}[\psi((X,Y),\theta)]=0_{d}
where
\psi((X,Y),\theta)=-X(Y-X^{\top}\theta)
, and is uniquely given by
\beta_{0}=\Sigma^{-1}\mathbb{E}[XY]
, if population Gram matrix
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}]
is invertible. The ordinary least squares estimator (OLSE) is the conventional estimator for
\beta_{0}
and takes the closed-form
\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}Y_{i}
when the sample Gram matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}
is almost surely invertible.

Under model misspecification and moment conditions, the OLSE remains consistent in
\|\cdot\|_{2}
if
d=o(n)

(
Oliveira, 2016
)
, and linear contrasts are asymptotically normal as long as
d=\tilde{o}(\sqrt{n})

(
Kuchibhotla et al., 2020
)
. The dimension requirement for asymptotic normality is sharp: when
d\gg\sqrt{n}
, the OLSE incurs non-negligible bias, invalidating OLSE-based Wald inference
(
Mammen, 1993
;
Lin et al., 2024
)
. Recent works
(
Cattaneo et al., 2019
;
Chang et al., 2024
;
Lin et al., 2024
)
have proposed bias correction procedures to extend the validity range of Wald inference to
d=\tilde{o}(n^{2/3})
.

We consider the following assumptions throughout this section.

(LM1)

Suppose that there exists
q_{x},q_{y},K_{x},K_{y}\geq 1
such that
q_{xy}:=(1/q_{x}+1/q_{y})^{-1}>2
,
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[|u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X|^{q_{x}}]\leq K_{x}^{q_{x}}
, and
\mathbb{E}[|Y-X^{\top}\beta_{0}|^{q_{y}}]\leq K_{y}^{q_{y}}
.

(LM2)

For
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}]
and
V=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}(Y-X^{\top}\beta_{0})^{2}]
, there exist constants
\underline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
,
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
, and
\underline{\lambda}_{V}
such that
0<\underline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}\leq\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma)\leq\lambda_{\rm max}(\Sigma)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}<\infty
and
0<\underline{\lambda}_{V}\leq\lambda_{\rm min}(V)
.

Assumption
(LM1)
imposes only finite-moment assumptions on the covariates and the population residual (termed by
Buja et al. (2019a)
), allowing for discrete and heavy-tailed distribution. This relaxes the exponentially decaying tail assumptions, fairly common in the high-dimensional linear regression literature. Assumption
(LM2)
serves as a technical condition to ensure the well-conditioned Gram matrix and non-degeneracy of the “meat part” of the sandwich variance
(
White, 1980
;
Buja et al., 2019a
)
.

4.1.2
Inference for Linear Contrasts

In this section, we apply the procedure described in Section
3
to construct confidence intervals for
\xi^{\top}\beta_{0}
for a fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Section
3
employs the SpN confidence set as a bounding set due to its favorable width guarantees in general settings. In linear regression, we use the SeN confidence set with the Bonferroni quantile as our bounding set, since it attains the desired width with a better coverage guarantee. We recall
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SeN}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SpN}
from (
12
), and define

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm CI}_{\alpha}^{\rm SeN}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0})=\left\{\xi^{\top}\theta:\theta\in\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SeN}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1})~\mbox{and}~T^{\rm SeN}(\theta;\widehat{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1})\leq z_{\frac{1}{2nd}}\right\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm CI}_{\alpha}^{\rm SpN}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0})=\left\{\xi^{\top}\theta:\theta\in\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{\alpha}^{\rm SpN}(\widehat{\beta}_{2},\widehat{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1})~\mbox{and}~T^{\rm SeN}(\theta;\widehat{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1})\leq z_{\frac{1}{2nd}}\right\},

where the sample precision matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1}
serves as the rotation matrix. We note that the construction of the SeN confidence interval here does not require preliminary estimators.

Theorem 9
.

Suppose that Assumption
(LM2)
holds.

1.

Assume
(LM1)
and let
s=q_{xy}\wedge 3
, then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0}\notin\widehat{\rm CI}^{\rm SeN}_{\alpha}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0})\right)-\alpha\right)_{+}\leq C\frac{\log^{s/2}(end)}{n^{s/2-1}}.

(18)

2.

(LM1)
with

(19)

The SeN confidence interval is honest over a distribution class that admits finite
(2+\delta)
-th moments as long as
d\leq n/2
and
n\to\infty
. The SpN confidence interval is honest over a class that further allows a higher moment of covariates, provided that
d=o(n)
.

We next analyze their width properties. The standard distribution approximation for OLSE under model misspecification
(
Buja et al., 2019a
)
suggests
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}_{1}-\beta_{0})\approx\mathcal{N}(0_{d},\Sigma^{-1}V\Sigma^{-1})
. This approximation leads to a canonical Wald interval for
\xi^{\top}\theta_{0}
:

\widehat{\rm CI}^{\rm Wald}_{\alpha}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0})=\big[\xi^{\top}\widehat{\beta}_{1}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\xi^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}V\Sigma^{-1}\xi/n}\big].

To formalize this geometric similarity, we consider the one-side Hausdorff distance between intervals
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, defined as

{\rm Haus}_{|\cdot|}(A\to B):=\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}|a-b|.

Theorem
10
reports the second-order width analyses for the SeN and SpN confidence intervals toward the Wald’s, in terms of the Hausdorff distance.

Theorem 10
.

Assume
(LM1)
with
q\geq 4
and
(LM2)
. There exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n/2}~{\rm Haus}_{|\cdot|}\left(\widehat{\rm CI}^{\rm SeN}_{\alpha}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0})\to\widehat{\rm CI}^{\rm Wald}_{\alpha}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0})\right)\geq\frac{C_{1}d}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq C_{2}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}+\left(\frac{d\log^{3}(en)}{n^{1-4/q_{x}}}\right)^{q_{x}/4}\bigg].

The same bound applies to
{\rm Haus}_{|\cdot|}(\widehat{\rm CI}^{\rm SpN}_{\alpha}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0})\to\widehat{\rm CI}^{\rm Wald}_{\alpha}(\xi^{\top}\beta_{0}))
.

Thus, the widths of the SeN and SpN confidence intervals scale as
1/\sqrt{n}+d/n
with high probability, matching the scaling of
|\xi^{\top}(\widehat{\beta}_{1}-\beta_{0})|
under model misspecification
(
Mammen, 1993
;
Chang et al., 2024
)
. Consequently, any valid confidence interval containing OLSE cannot improve on this rate up to constants. When
d=o(\sqrt{n})
, the SeN and SpN intervals converge to the Wald interval faster than the Wald interval shrinks to a point, with widths comparable to the full-sample Wald interval up to a factor of
\sqrt{2}
. Notably, while the Wald interval loses validity when
d\gg\sqrt{n}

(
Mammen, 1993
;
Kuchibhotla et al., 2020
)
, the SeN and SpN intervals remain valid under the weaker conditions
d\leq n/2
and
d=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
, respectively.

4.2
High-dimensional Misspecified Generalized Linear Model

We now extend to generalized linear models (GLMs). Consider the canonical form of a loss:

\mathcal{L}((y,x),\theta)=\ell(x^{\top}\theta)-yx^{\top}\theta,

where
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and thrice differentiable. The gradient and Hessian of the loss function are given by
\psi((y,x),\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}((y,x),\theta)=-x^{\top}(y-\ell^{\prime}(x^{\top}\theta))
and
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\mathcal{L}((y,x),\theta)=xx^{\top}\ell^{\prime\prime}(x^{\top}\theta)\succeq 0
. In this setup, the target
\theta_{0}
is the solution to the following moment equation:

\theta_{0}~:~\mathbb{E}[X(Y-\ell^{\prime}(X^{\top}\theta_{0}))]=0_{d}.

(20)

We reemphasize that
\theta_{0}
in (
20
) is defined as a statistical functional and does not require the data-generating process to be a parametric GLM.

We impose the following assumptions throughout this section.

(GLM1)

There exists a positive constant
b_{1}>0
such that
\sup_{x}|\ell^{\prime\prime}(x)|\vee\sup_{x}|\ell^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)|\leq b_{1}
.

(GLM2)

Suppose that there exists
q_{x},q_{y},K_{x},K_{y}\geq 1
such that
q_{xy}:=(1/q_{x}+1/q_{y})^{-1}>2
,
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[|u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X|^{q_{x}}]\leq K_{x}^{q_{x}}
, and
\mathbb{E}[|Y-\ell^{\prime}(X^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{y}}]\leq K_{y}^{q_{y}}
.

(GLM3)

For
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}]
and
V=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}\ell^{\prime\prime}(X^{\top}\theta_{0})]
, there exist constants
\underline{\lambda}_{\Sigma},\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma},\underline{\lambda}_{V}\in(0,\infty)
such that
0<\underline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}\leq\lambda_{\rm min}(\Sigma)\leq\lambda_{\rm max}(\Sigma)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}<\infty
and
\lambda_{\rm min}(V)\geq\underline{\lambda}_{V}>0
.

Assumption
(GLM1)
encompasses a wide range of commonly used loss functions, including squared loss, logistic loss, probit loss, and several smoothed robust proxies, such as smoothed hinge or bisquare loss. Assumption
(GLM2)
generalizes
(LM1)
to the GLM setting, requiring finite moments of standardized covariates and population residuals. Assumption
(GLM3)
ensures the well-conditioned Gram matrix and the Hessian matrix. Importantly,
(GLM3)
is a pointwise condition at
\theta_{0}
and does not impose uniform strong convexity in a neighborhood.

We first establish a non-asymptotic concentration bound for
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
under mild assumptions.

Theorem 11
.

Assume
(GLM1)
,
(GLM2)
, and
(GLM3)
. Then, there exist constants
c>0
and
C>0
such that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
,

C^{-1}\|\widehat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(2/\varepsilon)}{n}}+\frac{d^{1/2}}{n^{1-1/q_{xy}}\varepsilon^{1/q_{xy}}},

(21)

provided that the right-hand side is no greater than
c
.

Therefore,
Z
-estimators for misspecified GLMs are
\sqrt{n/d}
-consistent under
(2+\epsilon)
-th moments if
d=o(n)
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first non-asymptotic result that accommodates heavy-tailed covariates and responses, unbounded parameter spaces, and model misspecification. The rate comprises a sub-Gaussian term with a polynomially decaying remainder, reflecting a structure analogous to classical Fuk–Nagaev inequalities
(
Einmahl and Li, 2008
;
Rio, 2017
)
. As per Theorem
11
, the
Z
-estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{2}
from
\mathcal{D}_{2}
also has the convergence rate of
\sqrt{d/n}.
The next result establishes that
\widehat{\theta}_{2}
serves as a valid preliminary estimator for constructing uniformly valid confidence sets.

Assume
(GLM1)
and
(GLM3)

Assume
(GLM2)
with

Therefore, the SeN, SpN, and GaN confidence sets are honest over a distribution class that admits finite moments as long as
d=\tilde{o}(n)

(S)

We recall

5
Numerical Studies

This section evaluates the finite sample performance of the proposed confidence sets in linear regression across a range of data-generating processes (dgps), chosen to reflect both well-specified and misspecified scenarios.

(dgp1)

Well-specified linear model with independent covariates.
Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim(X,Y)
for
i\in[n]
where
Y=X^{\top}\beta_{0}+\varepsilon_{i}
. Here, the covariate
X
and the error
\varepsilon
are independently drawn from
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{d},I_{d})
and
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. We let
\beta_{0}=1_{d}/\sqrt{d}
.

(dgp2)

Mis-specified linear model with dependent covariates.
The covariates are defined as
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}X_{i}=U_{ij}Z_{j}
for
i\in[n]
and
j\in[d]
where
U_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
Z_{i}\sim{\rm unif}[1,3]
. The responses were put as
Y_{i}=Q_{i}+0.25Q_{i}V_{i}+V_{i}
where
Q_{i}=\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}/d
and
V_{i}
is drawn from a mixture of Gaussian:
1/2\mathcal{N}(1/2,(1.2)^{2})+1/2\mathcal{N}(-1/2,(0.7)^{2})
for
i\in[n]
. The variables

are independent.

The sample size is fixed at

with a split ratio of

, and the number of covariates varies as

for

. For

, the true projection parameters are approximated by Monte Carlo simulation with

replicates.

6
Conclusions

This paper develops a general framework for constructing uniformly valid confidence sets for functionals defined by moment equations, adopting a model-agnostic, assumption-lean approach that requires only weak moment and regularity conditions.

The methodological contribution lies in the introduction of self-normalized (SeN), split-normalized (SpN), and Gateaux-normalized (GaN) statistics. These are constructed through a combination of test inversion and sample splitting, enabling both statistical robustness and computational tractability. We show that the resulting confidence sets are honest over broad distribution classes and admit worst-case width guarantees. In particular, the proposed procedures remain valid under heavy-tailed distributions, model misspecification, and unbounded parameter spaces.

When applied to high-dimensional linear and generalized linear regression, our theory yields the non-asymptotic width and coverage guarantees for confidence sets that are robust to model and distribution misspecification. We establish that the SpN sets attain uniform coverage with diameters scaling as
\sqrt{d\log(ed)/n}
for generalized linear regression. In linear regression, the SeN, SpN, and GaN sets achieve honesty with the minimax optimal diameter scaling
\sqrt{d/n}
. Furthermore, we show that directional inference procedures based on these sets yield valid confidence intervals for linear contrasts, with second-order width analysis toward the classical Wald interval. Numerical studies support our theoretical findings, demonstrating that the proposed methods achieve reliable coverage across a range of nonlinear data-generating processes. Compared with classical procedures, the SeN, SpN, and GaN constructions exhibit better robustness and comparable width behavior in high-dimensional settings.

Future directions include extending the framework to settings with dependent data, such as time series or spatial models, as well as to partially identified parameters defined through moment inequalities. The combination of geometric tractability, finite-sample validity, and distributional robustness makes the proposed approach a promising foundation for honest inference in modern high-dimensional applications.

Appendix C

Lemma C.1
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be not necessarily independent centered Gaussian random variables. Let
\sigma_{\rm max}^{2}=\max_{i\in[N]}{\rm Var}(X_{i})
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[N]}|X_{i}|>t\right)\leq Ne^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma_{\rm max}^{2})},

for all
t>0
.

proof of Lemma
C.1
.

Let
\sigma_{i}^{2}={\rm Var}(X_{i})
. It follows from the union bound and that
1-\Phi(t/\sigma_{i})\leq e^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma_{i}^{2})}/2
for
t>0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}|X_{i}|>t\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{i}|>t\right)=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\Phi(t/\sigma_{i})\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma_{i}^{2})}\leq Ne^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma_{\rm max}^{2})}.

∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent non-negative random variables and let
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{1+\varepsilon}]<\infty
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, for some
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\mu_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
and
B_{n,\varepsilon}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{1+\varepsilon}])^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}
. Then,
for
0\leq x\leq\mu_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leq x\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}-x}{B_{n,\varepsilon}}\right)^{1+1/\varepsilon}\right).

(C.1)

proof of Lemma
C.2
.

We follow the proof of lemma 2.19 of
de la Peña et al. (2009)
. Note that
e^{-a}\leq 1-a+(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}a^{1+\varepsilon}
for all
a>0
. For any
t\geq 0
and
0\leq x\leq\mu_{n}
, we have from Chernoff inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leq x\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{tx}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-tX_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{tx}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[1-tX_{i}+\frac{(tX_{i})^{1+\varepsilon}}{1+\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(t(x-\mu_{n})+\frac{(B_{n,\varepsilon}t)^{1+\varepsilon}}{1+\varepsilon}\right).

Taking
t=((\mu_{n}-x)/B_{n,\varepsilon}^{1+\varepsilon})^{1/\varepsilon}
yields (
C.1
).
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
where
X_{i}=(X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ip})^{\top}
and
X_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. For
\varepsilon>0
, define

L_{1+\varepsilon}=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}^{1+\varepsilon}])^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}}{(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}])}.

For
c\in(0,1)
, one has

\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}]}\geq 1-c\right)\geq 1-p\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{c}{L_{1+\varepsilon}}\right)^{1+1/\varepsilon}\right).

Hence, if
c_{n}:=L_{1+\varepsilon}((1+1/\varepsilon)\log(en)/n)^{\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)}\leq 1
, then one has

\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}]}\geq 1-c_{n}\right)\geq 1-\frac{p}{n}.

proof of Lemma
C.3
.

For any
c\in(0,1)
, Lemma
C.2
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\leq(1-c)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon c^{1+1/\varepsilon}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}])^{1+1/\varepsilon}}{(1+\varepsilon)(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}^{1+\varepsilon}])^{1/\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{c}{L_{1+\varepsilon}}\right)^{1+1/\varepsilon}\right),

for all
j=1,\ldots,p
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}]}\geq 1-c\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}]}<1-c\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}]}<1-c\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-p\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{c}{L_{1+\varepsilon}}\right)^{1+1/\varepsilon}\right).

∎

Proposition C.1
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be i.i.d with
\mathbb{E}[X]=0_{d}
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}]=\Sigma
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\bar{X}_{n}\right\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}\geq\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{2d+3\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{q>2}+C_{q}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{1/q}[\|X\|^{q}_{\Sigma^{-1}}]}{\delta^{1/q}n^{1-1/q}}\bigg]\right)\leq\delta,~\mbox{for}~q\geq 2

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]]}\|X_{i}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}\geq d+\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbb{E}[\|X\|^{4}_{\Sigma^{-1}}]\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{q>4}+C_{q}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{2/q}[\|X\|^{q}_{\Sigma^{-1}}]}{\delta^{2/q}n^{1-2/q}}\bigg]\right)\leq\delta~\mbox{for}~q\geq 4.

Moment conditions on norms are generally weaker than those on projections. For e.g., one can show that
\mathbb{E}^{1/q}[\|X\|^{q}_{\Sigma^{-1}}]\leq\sqrt{d}\sup_{j\in[d]}\mathbb{E}^{1/q}[|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X|^{q}]
while the reverse does not hold in general. See
Mourtada (2022)
for related discussions on norm kurtosis assumption.

Proof.

The
q=2
case follows from Chebyshev inequality. For
q>2
, Theorem 4 of
Einmahl and Li (2008)
applies with
\delta=\eta=1
and yields

\mathbb{P}(\|\bar{X}_{n}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}\geq 2\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{X}_{n}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}]+t)\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{3\Lambda_{n}}}+C_{q}\frac{\mathbb{E}\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{q}}{t^{q}n^{q-1}},

for any
q>2
where
\Lambda_{n}=n^{-1}\lambda_{\rm max}(\mathbb{E}[\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{1}X_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}])=1/n
. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{X}_{n}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/2}[\|\bar{X}_{n}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}]=\sqrt{d/n}
. Hence, taking the following
t=\sqrt{\frac{3\log(2/\delta)}{n}}+(2C_{q})^{1/q}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{1/q}[\|X\|^{q}_{\Sigma^{-1}}]}{\delta^{1/q}n^{1-1/q}}
proves the first part of the proposition. Now, let
Y_{i}=\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1
, and apply Equation (1.9) of
Rio (2017)
to get, for
q>4
,

\mathbb{P}(\bar{Y}_{n}\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)}+C_{q}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{q/2}[|Y_{1}|^{q/2}]}{n^{1-2/q}\delta^{2/q}})\leq\delta,

where
\sigma^{2}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]/n=\mathbb{E}[\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{4}]-1
, and
\mathbb{E}^{q/2}[|Y_{1}|^{q/2}]\leq\mathbb{E}^{q/2}[\|X_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{q}]+1
. This proves the second part where
q=4
case follows from Chebyshev’s ineqaulity.
∎

Lemma C.6

R\geq\bar{R}(\Omega)^{1/2}\sigma_{*}(\Omega)^{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\bar{R}(\Omega)
where
\varepsilon_{R}(t)=(1+\sqrt{2})\sigma_{*}(\Omega)t^{1/2}+R^{1/3}\sigma(X)^{2/3}t^{2/3}+Rt
.
Now, we will analyze the matrix parameters in (
C.14
):

\displaystyle\sigma(\Omega)^{2}=\|\mathbb{E}[(\Omega-I_{d})^{2}]\|_{\rm op}=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}[u^{\top}(\Omega-I_{d})^{2}u]\right|

\displaystyle~~=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\top}\left({\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\right)^{2}u\right]\right|=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\top}\left({\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{2}\right)u\right]\right|

\displaystyle\quad=n^{-1}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}[u^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1/2}XX^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d})^{2}u]\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq n^{-1}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbb{E}[(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X)^{2}\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}]-1\right|\leq\frac{K^{4}d}{n},

where we have used
Z_{i}
’s are mean-zero and independent across
i\in[n]
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}(\Omega)^{2}=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[|u^{\top}({\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i})u|^{2}]=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[{\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{n}(u^{\top}Z_{i}u)^{2}]

\displaystyle\quad=n^{-1}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X)^{2}-1)^{2}]=n^{-1}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}[(u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X)^{4}]-1\leq\frac{K^{4}}{n}.

In order to analyze
\bar{R}(\Omega)
, we note that
\|Z_{i}\|_{\rm op}=n^{-1}|\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1|
. Therefore,

\bar{R}(\Omega)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\|X_{i}\|^{4}_{\Sigma^{-1}}\right].

Moreover, we get from Jensen’s inequality and union bound that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\|X_{i}\|^{4}_{\Sigma^{-1}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{4/q}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\|X_{i}\|^{q}_{\Sigma^{-1}}\right]\leq n^{4/q}\mathbb{E}^{4/q}\left[\|X_{i}\|^{q}_{\Sigma^{-1}}\right]\leq K^{4}n^{4/q}d^{2}.

Hence,
\bar{R}(\Omega)^{2}\leq 1/n^{2}+K^{4}d^{2}/n^{2-4/q}\leq 2K^{4}d^{2}/n^{2-4/q}
. Following the proof of Proposition B.6 of
Chang et al. (2024)
, we let

R(\delta)=2K^{2}\left[\frac{d}{\delta^{2/q}n^{1-2/q}}+\frac{d^{3/4}}{n^{3/4-1/q}}\right],

for
\delta\in(0,1]
. This ensures that
R(\delta)\geq\bar{R}(\Omega)^{1/2}\sigma_{*}(\Omega)^{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\bar{R}(\Omega)
for all
\delta\in(0,1]
. Moreover, it follows from the union bound and Markov inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}\|Z_{i}\|_{\rm op}\geq R(\delta)\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\|X_{i}\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1\right|\geq nR(\delta)\right)\leq n\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1\right|\geq nR(\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\leq n(nR(\delta))^{-q/2}\mathbb{E}[|\|X\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}-1|^{q/2}]\leq\frac{2^{q/2-1}K^{q}d^{q/2}}{n^{q/2-1}R(\delta)^{q/2}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

(C.20)

From Young’s inequality, it follows that
3R(\delta)^{1/3}\sigma(\Omega)^{2/3}t^{2/3}\leq 2\sigma(\Omega)t^{1/2}+R(\delta)t
, and this implies that for some universal constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{R(\delta)}(t)=(1+\sqrt{2})\sigma_{*}(\Omega)t^{1/2}+R(\delta)^{1/3}\sigma(\Omega)^{2/3}t^{2/3}+R(\delta)t

\displaystyle\quad\leq CK^{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{dt}{n}}+t\left[\frac{d}{\delta^{2/q}n^{1-2/q}}+\frac{d^{3/4}}{n^{3/4-1/q}}\right]\right).

Moreover, we note that

\frac{d^{3/4}t}{n^{3/4-1/q}}=\left(\sqrt{\frac{dt}{n}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{t^{3/2}d}{n^{1-q/2}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{dt}{n}}+\frac{t^{3/2}d}{n^{1-2/q}}.

Therefore, the inequality in (
C.19
) becomes

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}\geq CK^{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{dt}{n}}+\frac{t^{3/2}d}{n^{1-2/q}}+\frac{td}{\delta^{2/q}n^{1-2/q}}\right),~\max_{i\in[n]}\|Z_{i}\|_{\rm op}\leq R(\delta)\right)\leq de^{-t},

for all
t\geq 0
. Taking
t=\log(2d/\delta)
and combining this with (
C.20
) leads to the desired results.
∎

Lemma C.7
.

Assume
(GLM2)
. For any
0<q_{1},q_{2}
such that
q_{1}/q_{x}+q_{2}/q_{y}\leq 1
, it holds that

\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}|^{q_{1}}|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{2}}\right]\leq K_{x}^{q_{1}}K_{y}^{q_{2}},

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{1}/2}|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{2}}\right]\leq K_{x}^{q_{1}}K_{y}^{q_{2}}d^{q_{1}/2}.

Proof.

Let
s=(q_{1}/q_{x}+q_{2}/q_{y})^{-1}\geq 1
. It follows from Jensen’s inequality and Hölder’s inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{1}}|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{sq_{1}}|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{sq_{2}}\right]\right)^{1/s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{x}}\right]\right)^{q_{1}/q_{x}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{y}}\right]\right)^{q_{2}/q_{y}}.

Applications of Jensen’s inequality and Hölder’s inequality once more leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{x}}\right]\right)^{q_{1}/q_{x}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{y}}\right]\right)^{q_{2}/q_{y}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{x}}\right]\right)^{sq_{1}/q_{x}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{y}}\right]\right)^{sq_{2}/q_{y}}\right)^{1/s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{x}}\right]\right\}^{sq_{1}/q_{x}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{y}}\right]\right\}^{sq_{2}/q_{y}}\right)^{1/s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{x}}\right]\right\}^{q_{1}/q_{x}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{i}-\ell^{\prime}(X_{i}^{\top}\theta_{0})|^{q_{y}}\right]\right\}^{q_{2}/q_{y}}\leq K_{x}^{q_{1}}K_{y}^{q_{2}}d^{q_{1}/2},

where the last inequality follows from
(GLM2)
. In particular, one has from Jensen’s inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{q_{x}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}|^{2}\right)^{q_{x}/2}\right]=\frac{d^{q_{x}/2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}|^{2}\right)^{q_{x}/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{d^{q_{x}/2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}|^{q_{x}}\right]\leq K_{x}^{q_{x}}d^{q_{x}/2}.

∎

Proposition C.5
.

Grant
(LM1)
and
(LM2)
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})^{2}\geq K_{x}^{2}K_{y}^{2}\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\delta}}\right)\right)\leq\delta,

(C.21)

for all
\delta>0
. Moreover, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle(\beta_{0}-\widehat{\beta})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{1}(\beta_{0}-\widehat{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-9K_{x}\sqrt{\frac{d+2\log(4/\delta)}{n}}\right)_{+}^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(8\frac{\overline{\lambda}_{V}}{\underline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}}\frac{d+2\log(4/\delta)}{n}+C_{q_{xy}}K_{x}^{2}K_{y}^{2}\frac{d}{\delta^{2/q_{xy}}n^{2-2/q_{xy}}}\right)

(C.22)

where the constant
C_{q_{xy}}
only depends on
q_{xy}
and
\widehat{\beta}
is the OLSE computed from
\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i\in[n]\}
.

Proof.

Note that (
C.21
) follows from Chebyshev’s inequality. In particular, an application of Lemma
C.7
with
q_{1}=2
and
q_{2}=2
leads to

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})^{2}\right]\leq K_{x}^{2}K_{y}^{2}\frac{d}{n}.

In addition, an application of Lemma
C.7
with
q_{1}=4
and
q_{2}=4
gives

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Var}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}\|_{2}^{4}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{x}^{4}K_{y}^{4}\frac{d^{2}}{n^{3}}.

This proves (
C.21
). To prove (
C.22
), we note from the definition of
\widehat{\beta}
that

\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0}=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0}).

Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle(\beta_{0}-\widehat{\beta})^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{1}(\beta_{0}-\widehat{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}\right)}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{0})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence, (
C.22
) follows from Theorem 4.1 of
Oliveira (2016)
and Proposition
C.4
.
∎
