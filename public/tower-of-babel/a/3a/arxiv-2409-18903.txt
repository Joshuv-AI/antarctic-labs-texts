On the convergence rate of a numerical method for the Hunter–Saxton equation
Thomas Christiansen

Abstract
. We derive a robust error estimate for a recently proposed numerical method for α \alpha -dissipative solutions of the Hunter–Saxton equation, where α ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in[0,1] . In particular, if the following two conditions hold: i) there exist a constant C > 0 C>0 and β ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \beta\in(0,1] such that the initial spatial derivative u ¯ x \bar{u}_{x} satisfies ∥ u ¯ x ( ⋅ + h ) − u ¯ x ( ⋅ ) ∥ 2 ≤ C h β \|\bar{u}_{x}(\cdot+h)-\bar{u}_{x}(\cdot)\|_{2}\leq Ch^{\beta} for all h ∈ ( 0 , 2 ] h\in(0,2] , and ii) , the singular continuous part of the initial energy measure is zero, then the numerical wave profile converges with order 𝒪 ⁡ ( Δ ​ x β / 8 ) \mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{8}}}) in L ∞ ​ ( ℝ ) L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}) . Moreover, if α = 0 \alpha=0 , then the rate improves to 𝒪 ⁡ ( Δ ​ x 1 / 4 ) \mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}) without the above assumptions, and we also obtain a convergence rate for the associated energy measure – it converges with order 𝒪 ⁡ ( Δ ​ x 1 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}) in the bounded Lipschitz metric. These convergence rates are illustrated by several examples.

1.
Introduction

The Cauchy problem for the Hunter–Saxton (HS) equation reads

(1.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)+uu_{x}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{x}u_{x}^{2}(t,z)dz-\frac{1}{4}\int_{x}^{\infty}u_{x}^{2}(t,z)dz,\quad u|_{t=0}=\bar{u},

where
(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. This equation was proposed in
[
28
]
as an asymptotic model for nonlinear instabilities in the director field of nematic liquid crystals.

The left-hand side of equation (
1.1
) contains a Burgers nonlinearity, while the right-hand side is a nonlocal source term. In contrast to what one would typically expect for the inviscid Burgers equation, where shock formations are to be anticipated, see e.g.,
[
27
]
, the source term has a regularizing effect on the solution
u
, preventing shocks from occurring, see
[
10
,
21
]
. As a consequence,
u(t,\cdot)
remains Hölder continuous, and, in addition,
u_{x}(t,\cdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for all
t\geq 0
.

However, classical solutions to (
1.1
) will in general cease to exist past finite time since the spatial derivative,
u_{x}
, blows up pointwise – a phenomenon referred to as
wave breaking
. If wave breaking takes place at
t=t_{c}
, then there exists at least one point
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
u_{x}(t,x)\rightarrow-\infty
as
t\uparrow t_{c}
. This leads to energy concentrations on sets of zero measure as the following example illustrates.

Example 1.1
.

Consider the following weak solution of (
1.1
), valid for
0\leq t<2
,

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{8}t+\frac{1}{2},&x<\frac{1}{16}(8-t)t,\\
\frac{8x-(t+4)}{4(t-2)},&\frac{1}{16}(8-t)t\leq x\leq\frac{1}{16}(8+t^{2}),\\
\frac{1}{8}t,&\frac{1}{16}(8+t^{2})<x,\end{cases}

and assume that the associated cumulative energy is described by

(1.2)

F(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}u_{x}^{2}(t,z)dz.

The pair
(u,F)
is visualized in Figure
1
for
t=0
,
1
, and
1.95
. As
t\uparrow 2
we observe that the interval
[\frac{1}{16}(8-t)t,\frac{1}{16}(8+t^{2})]
shrinks to the single point
x=\frac{3}{4}
, while
u_{x}(t,\frac{3}{4})\rightarrow-\infty
. Moreover,
u_{x}^{2}(t)dx
formally tends to the Dirac measure
\frac{1}{2}d\delta_{\frac{3}{4}}
as
t\uparrow 2
, and hence (
1.2
) is inadequate for describing the energy at
t=2
. In other words, the energy cannot be described by an absolutely continuous function for all
t\geq 0
.

Figure 1
.
The solution
u
(left) and the associated energy
F
(right) from Example
1.1
at the times
t=0
,
1
, and
1.95
.

To properly describe the solution in Example
1.1
and solutions to (
1.1
) in general, one has to augment the wave profile
u
with a finite, nonnegative Radon measure
\mu
that represents the energy density. This measure satisfies
d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}dx
and encodes all the information about wave breaking. In particular, the singular part
\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}
tells us where energy has concentrated, as was the case in Example
1.1
, but the absolutely continuous part
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}
can also give rise to wave breaking which manifests itself in the form of infinitesimal energy concentrations, see e.g.,
[
9
, Sec. 5]
and
[
22
, Sec. 3]
.

In order to extend local solutions of (
1.1
) into global ones, one has to make a choice about how to manipulate the concentrated energy at wave breaking. There is a plethora of possible energy modifications, leading to a whole continuum of weak solutions. This has been a subject of extensive research, see for instance
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
21
,
24
,
28
,
30
]
. In this work however, we are concerned with the particular notion of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions, for fixed
\alpha\in[0,1]
. As the name suggests, such solutions are obtained by removing a fixed
\alpha
-fraction of the concentrated energy at every wave breaking occurrence. Existence of weak
\alpha
-dissipative solutions was established in
[
21
]
via a generalized method of characteristics in the more general setting of
\alpha\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R},[0,1))\cup\{1\}
. Uniqueness on the other hand, has only been proved in the particular cases of
conservative
(
\alpha=0
) and
dissipative
(
\alpha=1
) solutions, see
[
17
]
and
[
11
]
, respectively.

The approach in
[
21
]
is based on introducing a nonlinear mapping
L
, which transforms the Eulerian initial data
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})
into an initial triplet
(\bar{y},\bar{U},\bar{V})
in Lagrangian coordinates, whose time evolution is governed by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} y_{t}(t,\xi)&=U(t,\xi),\\
\lx@nobreakspace U_{t}(t,\xi)&=\frac{1}{2}V(t,\xi)-\frac{1}{4}V_{\infty}(t),\\
V(t,\xi)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\left(1-\alpha\chi_{\{\omega:t\geq\tau(\omega)>0\}}(\eta)\right)\!\bar{V}_{\xi}(\eta)d\eta.\end{aligned}

Here
\displaystyle V_{\infty}(t)=\lim_{\xi\rightarrow\infty}V(t,\xi)
is the total cumulative Lagrangian energy and
\tau\!:\!\mathbb{R}\!\rightarrow\![0,\infty]
is the
wave breaking function
defined by

\displaystyle\tau(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\bar{U}_{\xi}(\xi)=\bar{y}_{\xi}(\xi)=0,\\
-2\frac{\bar{y}_{\xi}(\xi)}{\bar{U}_{\xi}(\xi)},&\bar{U}_{\xi}(\xi)<0,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is natural to interpret
\xi
as a label representing a particle, in which case
y(\cdot,\xi)
represents the particle trajectory,
U(\cdot,\xi)
its velocity, and
V_{\xi}(\cdot,\xi)
its associated energy. In addition,
\tau(\xi)
is the collision time of the particle, such that
\tau(\xi)=\infty
indicates that the particle does not engage in any collisions.

Naturally, several different types of numerical methods have been proposed for the HS equation, e.g., finite difference schemes of upwind-type
[
25
]
, discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
38
,
39
]
, geometrical methods
[
33
]
, and schemes based on the method of characteristics and its generalizations
[
8
,
9
,
22
]
. Yet there are very few results regarding convergence rates. The author is only aware of two results in this direction: Let
{\Delta x}
denote a spatial discretization parameter for a uniform mesh on
\mathbb{R}
, then the first result is the
\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
-rate shown in
[
22
]
for conservative solutions under the assumption that
u\in W^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
. This regularity does however break down at wave breaking. The second result, shown in
[
31
]
and
[
35
]
, is concerned with the
vanishing viscosity method
which converges with order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
towards dissipative solutions, where
\epsilon>0
is the viscosity parameter. Again, this result is valid either prior to wave breaking, or under the assumption that
\bar{u}_{x}\geq 0
, but this prevents wave breaking from taking place for any
t>0
.

We contribute to this line of research by deriving a
robust error estimate
(valid also after wave breaking) for the numerical scheme proposed in
[
9
]
for
\alpha
-dissipative solutions. This method takes advantage of the fact that if the initial data
\bar{u}
in (
1.1
) is piecewise linear, then the corresponding solution,
u(t,\cdot)
, remains piecewise linear for all
t\geq 0
, see
[
1
,
29
]
. Motivated by this observation, the authors in
[
9
]
derive a piecewise linear projection operator,
P_{{\Delta x}}
, which preserves the relation
d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}dx
. This is essential in order to ensure that the numerical solutions
\{(u_{{\Delta x}},\mu_{{\Delta x}})(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
, which are obtained by solving (
1.1
) exactly with initial data
\{P_{{\Delta x}}((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
via the outlined generalized method of characteristics, are in fact
\alpha
-dissipative solutions, i.e., they dissipate the correct amount of energy. It is then established that

u_{{\Delta x}}\rightarrow u\text{ in}\lx@nobreakspace C\big([0,\infty),L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\big)\hskip 8.5359pt\text{ as }{\Delta x}\rightarrow 0,

and we aim to strengthen this result, by proving that
\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\!\leq\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\gamma})
for some
\gamma>0
. With this in mind, let
\{(y_{{\Delta x}},U_{{\Delta x}},V_{{\Delta x}})(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
denote the family of numerical solutions in Lagrangian coordinates. Thanks to
[
9
, Lem. 4.11]
, we have, for any
t\geq 0
,

\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\!\leq\!\|U(t)-U_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{\bar{\mu}(\mathbb{R})}\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

It therefore enough to derive convergence rates for
\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}
and

\|U(t)-U_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}=\|u(y(t))-u_{{\Delta x}}(y_{{\Delta x}}(t))\|_{\infty}
. However, this is a delicate issue, because the relation between the Eulerian and Lagrangian variables is highly nonlinear. To be more precise, the initial energy density
\bar{\mu}
may have a singular part,
\bar{\mu}_{\mathrm{sing}}
, which generates a set
\mathcal{A}
of positive measure when mapping into Lagrangian coordinates. Since there is no natural way to relate the differentiated Eulerian and Lagrangian variables on
\mathcal{A}
, we only have a convergence rate for
\bar{V}_{\xi}-\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi}
over
\mathbb{R}\lx@nobreakspace\!\!\setminus\!\mathcal{A}
. This is problematic, because
\|U(t)-U_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}
and
\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}
heavily depend on
V_{\xi}(t)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t)
on all of
\mathbb{R}
, cf. (
1.3
).

To overcome this issue, we introduce a transformation which enables us to prove that the set
\mathcal{A}
contributes at most with order
\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\beta})
to the aforementioned norms, provided the following two conditions hold:
i)
there exists some constant
C>0
and
\beta\in(0,1]
such that

(1.4)

\|\bar{u}_{x}(\cdot+h)-\bar{u}_{x}(\cdot)\|_{2}\leq Ch^{\beta}\hskip 11.38092pt\text{for all }h\in(0,2],

and
ii)
, the singular continuous part of
\bar{\mu}
is zero. The latter condition is needed to avoid issues with measurability. The remaining contribution to
\|U(t)-U_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}
and
\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}
can be bounded in terms of the Lagrangian initial data over
\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{A}
. One is therefore just left to relate the initial convergence rate in Eulerian coordinates to a rate of convergence for the Lagrangian variables on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{A}
, from which one finally infers that
\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|\leq\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{8}}})
for all
t\geq 0
.

Before we illustrate the convergence rate on some important examples, we take a detour and investigate the particular case of conservative solutions. In this case we find that the rate can be improve significantly without the supplemented conditions on the initial data. Additionally, one obtains a convergence rate for the family
\{\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
of numerical energy densities in the bounded Lipschitz metric.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we remind the reader of the generalized method of characteristics based on
[
6
]
and
[
21
]
. In addition, we recall the numerical method from
[
9
]
. Thereafter, in Section
3
, we study how (
1.4
) enables us to deduce a convergence rate initially in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for the numerical derivative
\bar{u}_{{\Delta x},x}
. This is essential, when we derive a convergence rate for
\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}
in Section
4
. Section
5
is devoted to the particular case of conservative solutions and we end by studying the convergence rates for three explicit examples in Section
6
. This culminates in an
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
-error estimate of order
\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{48}}})
for the cusped initial data considered in
[
9
, Sec. 5]
.

2.
Preliminaries and the numerical method

In this section, we present the numerical method introduced in
[
9
]
. Since it relies on the construction of
\alpha
-dissipative solutions via the generalized method of characteristics from
[
6
]
and
[
21
]
, we start by recalling the main steps of this construction.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
be fixed and denote by
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
, the space of nonnegative, finite Radon measures on
\mathbb{R}
. In order to define the set of admissible data to (
1.1
) we have to recall some function spaces from
[
6
]
and
[
21
]
. To this end, let
E
be the Banach space

E:=\{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\},

equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{E}:=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{2}
. Furthermore, let

H_{d}^{1}(\mathbb{R})=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 8.5359ptd=1,2,

and decompose
\mathbb{R}
into the overlapping union
\mathbb{R}\!=(-\infty,1)\cup(-1,\infty)
. We pick a partition of unity, i.e., a pair
\chi^{+},\chi^{-}
belonging to
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, satisfying
\chi^{+}+\chi^{-}=1
,
0\leq\chi^{\pm}\leq 1
, in addition to
\mathrm{supp}(\chi^{+})\subset(-1,\infty)
and
\mathrm{supp}(\chi^{-})\subset(-\infty,1)
. This pair allows us to define the following linear, continuous, and injective maps

\displaystyle R_{1}:H_{1}^{1}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\rightarrow E,\hskip 28.45274pt(\bar{f},a)\mapsto f=\bar{f}+a\chi^{+},

\displaystyle R_{2}:H_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\rightarrow E,\hskip 28.45274pt(\bar{f},a,b)\mapsto f=\bar{f}+a\chi^{+}+b\chi^{-}.

Accordingly, we define the Banach spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
as

E_{1}:=R_{1}(H_{1}^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptE_{2}:=R_{2}(H^{1}_{2}(\mathbb{R})),

endowed with the norms

\displaystyle\|f\|_{E_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+a^{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\hskip 14.22636pt\lx@nobreakspace\text{and}\lx@nobreakspace\hskip 14.22636pt\|f\|_{E_{2}}:=\left(\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+a^{2}+b^{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\!\!\!.

At this point, it is worth emphasizing that the spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
do not depend on the particular choice of
\chi^{+}
and
\chi^{-}
​, see
[
20
]
. Furthermore,
R_{1}
is also well-defined when applied to functions in
L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\!:=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}
, motivating the following space

E_{1}^{0}:=R_{1}(L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\hskip 8.5359pt\text{with}\hskip 11.38092pt\|f\|_{E_{1}^{0}}:=\left(\|\bar{f}\|_{2}^{2}+a^{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\!\!\!.

At last, we can define the set of Eulerian coordinates
\mathcal{D}
, which contains the admissible initial data, as well as the numerical solutions (to be defined).

Definition 2.1
.

The space
\mathcal{D}
consists of all pairs
(u,\mu)
satisfying

(i)

u\in E_{2}
,

(ii)

\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
,

(iii)

\mu\left((-\infty,\cdot)\right)\!\in E_{1}^{0}
,

(iv)

d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}=u_{x}^{2}dx
.

It follows by
[
14
, Thm. 1.16]
that there is a one-to-one relationship between
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and the cumulative energy
F(x)=\mu((-\infty,x))
, which justifies our upcoming, interchangeable use of both
(u,\mu)
and
(u,F)
to refer to the same element of
\mathcal{D}
. Moreover, recall that
F
is an increasing, left continuous, and bounded function satisfying

\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}F(x)=0\hskip 9.95863pt\text{and}\hskip 9.95863pt\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}F(x)=\mu(\mathbb{R}),

and that it can be decomposed into two parts,

(2.1)

F(x)=F_{\mathrm{ac}}(x)+F_{\mathrm{sing}}(x):=\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}((-\infty,x))+\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}((-\infty,x)),

where
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}
are absolutely continuous and singular, respectively.

Associated with
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
, we define the Lagrangian coordinates

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\bar{y}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x+\bar{\mu}((-\infty,x))<\xi\},

(2.2b)

\displaystyle\bar{U}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\bar{u}(\bar{y}(\xi)),

(2.2c)

\displaystyle\bar{V}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi-\bar{y}(\xi),

and we let
L
denote the mapping which is given by
(\bar{y},\bar{U},\bar{V})=L((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))
. Furthermore, the time evolution in Lagrangian coordinates is governed by, see
[
21
, Sec. 2]
,

(2.3a)

\displaystyle y_{t}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=U(t,\xi),

(2.3b)

\displaystyle U_{t}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}V(t,\xi)-\frac{1}{4}V_{\infty}(t),

(2.3c)

\displaystyle V(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\!\!\left(1-\alpha\chi_{\{\omega:t\geq\tau(\omega)>0\}}(\eta)\right)\!\bar{V}_{\xi}(\eta)d\eta.

Here
V_{\infty}(t)
denotes the right asymptote of
V(t,\xi)
and
\tau:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is defined by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\tau(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\bar{U}_{\xi}(\xi)=\bar{y}_{\xi}(\xi)=0,\\
-2\frac{\bar{y}_{\xi}(\xi)}{\bar{U}_{\xi}(\xi)},&\bar{U}_{\xi}(\xi)<0,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and tells us when the solution experiences wave breaking in Lagrangian coordinates. In particular, the Lagrangian energy density
V_{\xi}(t,\xi)
may drop abruptly at
t=\tau(\xi)
, cf. (
2.3c
), and the right-hand side of (
2.3
) may therefore become discontinuous at the strictly positive, finite times given by
\tau
. In spite of that, it has been established in
[
34
, Lem. 2.2.1]
, that (
2.3
) is globally well-posed for initial data belonging to the set of Lagrangian coordinates
\mathcal{F}
, which in fact is an
invariant set
for (
2.3
).

For its definition, we introduce the Banach space
B\!:=E_{2}\times E_{2}\times E_{1}
, equipped with the norm

\|(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})\|_{B}:=\|f_{1}\|_{E_{2}}+\|f_{2}\|_{E_{2}}+\|f_{3}\|_{E_{1}}.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
t\geq 0
. The solution
(y,U,V)(t)
of (
2.3
) with initial data
(\bar{y},\bar{U},\bar{V})
is said to belong to the space
\mathcal{F}
, if the following holds for all
t\geq 0
.

(i)

(y-\text{{id}},U,V)(t)\in\big[W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})\big]^{3}\cap B
,

(ii)

y_{\xi}(t),\bar{V}_{\xi}\geq 0
a.e., and there exists
c>0
such that
y_{\xi}(t)+\bar{V}_{\xi}\geq c
holds a.e.,

(iii)

y_{\xi}V_{\xi}(t)=U_{\xi}^{2}(t)
a.e.,

(iv)

0\leq V_{\xi}(t)\leq\bar{V}_{\xi}
a.e.,

(v)

If
\alpha=1
,
y_{\xi}(t,\xi)\!=0
implies
V_{\xi}(t,\xi)\!=0
a.e., and
y_{\xi}(t,\xi)>0
implies
aa

V_{\xi}(t,\xi)\!=\bar{V}_{\xi}(\xi)
a.e.,

(vi)

If
\alpha\in[0,1)
, then there exists a function
\kappa\!:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\{1-\alpha,1\}
such that
V_{\xi}(t,\xi)=\kappa(\xi)\bar{V}_{\xi}(\xi)
a.e., and
\kappa(\xi)=1
whenever
U_{\xi}(t,\xi)<0
.

In addition, based on (
2.3
), we also define the Lagrangian solution operator
S_{t}
.

Definition 2.3
.

For any
t\geq 0
and
\bar{X}\in\mathcal{F}
, let
S_{t}(\bar{X})\!=X(t)
, where
X(t)
is the unique
\alpha
-dissipative solution of (

) satisfying
X(0)\!=\bar{X}
.

At last, in order to obtain the sought solution
(u,\mu)
in Eulerian coordinates, one applies the mapping
M:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
given by
(u,\mu)=M((y,U,V))
, where

(2.5a)

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=U(\xi)\lx@nobreakspace\text{ for any }\xi\text{ such that }x=y(\xi),

(2.5b)

\displaystyle d\mu

\displaystyle=y_{\#}\left(V_{\xi}d\xi\right)\!.

Here
y_{\#}(V_{\xi}d\xi)
denotes the pushforward measure given by

\mu(A)=\int_{y^{-1}(A)}V_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi,\hskip 9.95863pt\text{for any Borel set }A\subseteq\mathbb{R}.

To summarize, the
\alpha
-dissipative solution in Eulerian coordinates is obtained by combining the Lagrangian solution operator
S_{t}
with the mappings
L
and
M
, defined in (
2.2
) and (
2.5
), respectively.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
,
t\geq 0
, and define
T_{t}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
by
T_{t}:=M\circ S_{t}\circ L
. The
\alpha
-dissipative solution at time
t
is then given by

(u,\mu)(t):=T_{t}\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)=M\circ S_{t}\circ L\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)\!.

Let us pause for a moment and compare the outlined approach with the one presented in e.g.,
[
9
,
21
,
34
]
as there are some differences. More precisely, we have chosen to represent Eulerian solutions as pairs
(u,\mu)
rather than triplets
(u,\mu,\nu)
as considered in the aforementioned works. The reason is that
\nu
is a measure added exclusively for technical reasons, in the sense that one recovers the same Eulerian solution
(u,\mu)(t)
for all
t\geq 0
irregardless of which
\nu
we pick initially, see
[
23
, Lem. 2.13]
for the precise statement. However, this comes at the cost of losing the semigroup property of
T_{t}
, but from a numerical viewpoint this is of no concern, because we only map into Lagrangian coordinates initially and back again to Eulerian coordinates at the time for which we seek the numerical solution. In other words, viewing Eulerian coordinates as triplets, with the appropriate modifications of Definition
2.1
, (
2.2
), Definition
2.2
, and (
2.5
), does not influence the upcoming error analysis, but it leads to more cumbersome notation.

The procedure outlined in
[
9
, Def. 3.1]
for obtaining numerical solutions is closely related to that of Definition
2.4
, but it involves one additional step: projecting the initial data onto a uniform mesh. In particular, by projecting the initial data
(\bar{u},\bar{F})
into the space of piecewise linear functions, the solution operator
T_{t}
can be implemented exactly, as the solution associated with this projected initial data will remain piecewise linear at all later times, have a look at
[
1
,
29
]
for more details.

To present the projection, let
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
denote a uniform discretization of
\mathbb{R}
, with
x_{j}=j{\Delta x}
for
{\Delta x}>0
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, let
f_{j}=f(x_{j})
denote the values attained at the gridpoints
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
for any function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and introduce the difference operator

Df_{2j}=\frac{f_{2j+2}-f_{2j}}{2{\Delta x}},

in addition to the quantity

(2.6)

q_{2j}:=\sqrt{DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-(Du_{2j})^{2}}.

The projection operator
P_{{\Delta x}}
is then defined as follows.

Definition 2.5

(
[
9
, Def. 3.2]
)
.

Define
P_{\Delta x}\!:\!\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}
by
P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F)\right)\!=(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x})
, where

(2.7)

\displaystyle u_{\Delta x}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}u_{2j}+\left(Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}\right)(x-x_{2j}),&x_{2j}<x\leq x_{2j+1},\\
u_{2j+2}+\left(Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}\right)(x-x_{2j+2}),&x_{2j+1}<x\leq x_{2j+2},\end{cases}

and

(2.8)

F_{\Delta x}(x)=F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)+F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x),\hskip 12.80365pt\lx@nobreakspace\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, the absolutely continuous part of
F_{\Delta x}
is given by

(2.9)

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{ac}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F_{\mathrm{ac},2j}+\left(Du_{2j}\mp q_{2j}\right)^{2}\left(x-x_{2j}\right)\!,&x_{2j}<x\leq x_{2j+1},\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(F_{\mathrm{ac},2j+2}+F_{\mathrm{ac},2j}\right)\mp 2q_{2j}Du_{2j}\Delta x\\
\quad+\left(Du_{2j}\pm q_{2j}\right)^{2}(x-x_{2j+1}),&x_{2j+1}<x\leq x_{2j+2},\end{cases}

while the singular part is defined as

(2.10)

\displaystyle F_{\Delta x,\mathrm{sing}}(x)

\displaystyle=F_{2j+2}-F_{\mathrm{ac},2j+2}=F_{\mathrm{sing},2j+2},\qquad x_{2j}<x\leq x_{2j+2}.

This particular projection operator is tailored to preserve the total energy
F_{\infty}
, the continuity of
u
, and Definition
2.1

(iv)
– it therefore maps
\mathcal{D}
into itself.

The observant reader may have noticed that there is an ambiguity in (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), i.e., a choice of sign that has to be made. In the upcoming analysis the choice of sign is irrelevant, but in practical applications one would typically impose a sign-selection criterion, see
[
9
, Sec. 3.1]
for further details.

Finally, the numerical solution is defined in the following way.

Definition 2.6

(
[
9
, Def. 3.1]
)
.

The numerical solution
(u_{{\Delta x}},F_{{\Delta x}})(t)
at
t\geq 0
is for any
(\bar{u},\bar{F})\in\mathcal{D}
defined by

(u_{{\Delta x}},F_{{\Delta x}})(t):=T_{t}\circ P_{{\Delta x}}((\bar{u},\bar{F})).

The interested reader is referred to
[
9
, Sec. 3.2]
for a detailed account about the numerical implementation of the solution operator
T_{t}
. Here we would like to emphasize that the mapping
L
, defined by (
2.2
), maps the uniform mesh in Eulerian coordinates
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
into a nonuniform discretization in Lagrangian coordinates
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where each pair
[x_{2j},x_{2j+1}]
and
[x_{2j+1},x_{2j+2}]
possibly is mapped into three Lagrangian gridcells with endpoints given by

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \xi_{3j}&=x_{2j}+\bar{F}_{{\Delta x}}(x_{2j}),\hskip 39.83368pt\xi_{3j+1}=x_{2j}+\bar{F}_{{\Delta x}}(x_{2j}+),\\
\xi_{3j+2}&=x_{2j+1}+\bar{F}_{{\Delta x}}(x_{2j+1}),\hskip 19.91684pt\xi_{3j+3}=x_{2j+2}+\bar{F}_{{\Delta x}}(x_{2j+2}).\end{aligned}

The notation
x_{2j}+
is used to denote the right limit at
x=x_{2j}
and we observe that
\xi_{3j+1}\!=\xi_{3j}
if
\bar{F}_{{\Delta x}}
does not admit a jump discontinuity at
x=x_{2j}
. Furthermore, (
2.2
) and Definition
2.5
imply that the numerical Lagrangian initial data,

(2.12)

\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}}=L\circ P_{{\Delta x}}((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))

is continuous and piecewise linear with nodes situated at
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. The value of
\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)
, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, can therefore be computed with linear interpolation based on the values attained at
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. As a consequence, the derivatives
\bar{X}_{{\Delta x},\xi}=(\bar{y}_{{\Delta x},\xi},\bar{U}_{{\Delta x},\xi},\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi})
are piecewise constant functions that may have jump discontinuities at the Lagrangian gridpoints
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Furthermore, the numerical wave breaking function
\tau_{{\Delta x}}
is computed via (
2.4
), when we replace
\bar{X}
with the projected data
\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}}
from (
2.12
). Consequently, one infers that the times and points for which wave breaking occurs for the exact and numerical solution in general differ. This complicates the upcoming error analysis.

3.
Convergence rate initially in Eulerian coordinates

Assume from now on that
{\Delta x}\leq 1
. We start by investigating the rate of convergence for the projected initial data
(u_{{\Delta x}},F_{{\Delta x}})=P_{{\Delta x}}((u,F))
. To this end, let us recall the following result.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
9
, Prop. 4.1 and Lem. 4.2]
)
.

Given
(u,F)\in\mathcal{D}
, let
(u_{\Delta x},F_{\Delta x})=P_{\Delta x}\left((u,F)\right)
, then

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\!F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\Delta x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)\!F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta x,

(3.1c)

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\Delta x}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2F_{\infty}\Delta x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}},\quad\mathrm{for}\hskip 4.83691ptp=1,2.

Furthermore, as
{\Delta x}\rightarrow 0
we have

(3.2)

u_{{\Delta x},x}\rightarrow u_{x}\text{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

The aim of this section is to strengthen (
3.2
), namely derive a bound of the form
\|u_{x}-u_{{\Delta x},x}\|_{2}\!\leq\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\gamma})
for some
\gamma>0
. This will be essential in the next section when we examine how the time evolution in Lagrangian coordinates affects the initial convergence rate in (
3.1a
). In particular, if we lack a convergence rate for
u_{{\Delta x},x}
initially, then the initial order of convergence from (
3.1a
) will be completely deteriorated by the solution operator
S_{t}
from Definition
2.3
for
\alpha
-dissipative solutions. This derives from the fact that, for any
t>0
, the set

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:u_{x}(y(\xi))=-\frac{2}{t}\Bigr\},

completely determines all the characteristics that are involved in wave breaking at time
t
in Lagrangian coordinates, and thereby the amount of energy removed at this time upon wave breaking. The set

E_{{\Delta x},t}:=\Bigl\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:u_{{\Delta x},x}(y_{{\Delta x}}(\xi))=-\frac{2}{t}\!\lx@nobreakspace\Bigr\},

plays a similar role for the numerical solution. Thus, in order to avoid that the error between the exact- and numerical Lagrangian solution becomes too large, which would prevent us from obtaining a convergence rate for
t>0
, one needs some way of controlling the difference in measure between
E_{s}
and
E_{{\Delta x},s}
for all
s\in(0,t]
. This is provided by the stronger
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-convergence rate for
u_{{\Delta x},x}
.

Since
u_{{\Delta x}}
, given by (
2.7
), is piecewise linear, we are approximating
u_{x}
by the piecewise constant function,
u_{{\Delta x},x}
. A convergence rate in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is therefore only obtainable if we impose additional regularity on
u_{x}
.

The following family of spaces

(3.3)

\displaystyle B_{2}^{\beta}\!:=\!\left\{f\!\in\!L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\!:\exists\hskip 1.42271ptC\!>0\text{ s.t. }\!\|\delta_{h}f\|_{2}\!\leq Ch^{\beta}\hskip 1.42271pt\text{for all }h\in(0,2]\right\}\!,

appears naturally in this context. Here
\beta\in(0,1]
is fixed and
\delta_{h}f(x):=f(x+h)-f(x)
. Furthermore, the smallest admissible constant
C>0
is denoted by
|f|_{2,\beta}
and defines a seminorm, namely

(3.4)

|f|_{2,\beta}:=\sup_{t\in(0,2]}\Big(t^{-\beta}\sup_{h\in[0,t]}\|\delta_{h}f\|_{2}\Big).

These spaces are closely related to the
Besov spaces
,
B_{\infty}^{\beta}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, and the
Lipschitz spaces
,
\mathrm{Lip}(\beta,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, see e.g.,
[
4
,
12
,
13
,
36
]
. Moreover,
B_{2}^{\beta}
contains all the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-functions that optimally can be approximated with order
\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\beta})
by piecewise constant functions on equidistant grids, see
[
12
, Sec. 3]
and
[
13
, Chp. 12]
. Hence these spaces are well-suited in our setting. Also note that we have restricted ourselves to
\beta\leq 1
, because when
\beta>1
, (
3.4
) is finite only for functions that are almost everywhere constant, see
[
13
, Chp. 2]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\beta\in(0,1]
and
(u,\mu)\in\mathcal{D}
with
u_{x}\in B_{2}^{\beta}
, let
(u_{{\Delta x}},F_{{\Delta x}})=P_{{\Delta x}}((u,F))
, then there exists a constant
D
dependent on
\beta
,
F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}
and
|u_{x}|_{2,\beta}
such that

\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}\leq D\Delta x^{\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{2}}}.

Proof.

Combining (
2.7
) with the estimate
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
where
a=u_{x}-Du_{2j}
and
b=\pm q_{2j}=\pm\sqrt{DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-(Du_{2j})^{2}}
leads to

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\|u_{x}-u_{\Delta x,x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(u_{x}(x)-Du_{2j}\right)^{2}dx

(3.5b)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-(Du_{2j})^{2}\right)dx,

and by further decomposing (
3.5b
) we get

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-(Du_{2j})^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left|DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-u_{x}^{2}(x)\right|dx

(3.6b)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left|u_{x}^{2}(x)-(Du_{2j})^{2}\right|dx.

We start by estimating (
3.5a
), to which we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz (CS) inequality and argue as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(u_{x}(x)-Du_{2j}\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}(u_{x}(x)-u_{x}(z))dz\bigg)^{\!2}\!dx

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny CS}}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x-2\Delta x}^{x+2\Delta x}|u_{x}(x)-u_{x}(z)|^{2}dzdx

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{-2\Delta x}^{2\Delta x}|u_{x}(x)-u_{x}(x+h)|^{2}dhdx

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny Tonelli}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2{\Delta x}}\int_{-2{\Delta x}}^{2{\Delta x}}\|\delta_{h}u_{x}\|_{2}^{2}dh

(3.7)

\displaystyle\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\eqref{eq:LipSpaces}}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{-2\Delta x}^{2\Delta x}|u_{x}|_{2,\beta}^{2}|h|^{2\beta}dh=\frac{2^{1+2\beta}|u_{x}|_{2,\beta}^{2}}{2\beta+1}\Delta x^{2\beta}.

Note that (
3.6a
) can be recast on the form

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left|DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-u_{x}^{2}(x)\right|dx=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\bigg|\frac{1}{2{\Delta x}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left(u_{x}^{2}(z)-u_{x}^{2}(x)\right)dz\bigg|dx,

and, in addition, by the CS inequality,

(3.8)

\|\delta_{h}u_{x}^{2}\|_{1}\leq\|u_{x}(\cdot+h)+u_{x}(\cdot)\|_{2}\|\delta_{h}u_{x}\|_{2}\leq 2F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|u_{x}|_{2,\beta}|h|^{\beta},

which in combination with (
3.7
) implies

(3.9)

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left|DF_{\mathrm{ac},2j}-u_{x}^{2}(x)\right|dx\leq\frac{2^{2+\beta}F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|u_{x}|_{2,\beta}}{\beta+1}\Delta x^{\beta}.

It therefore remains to consider (
3.6b
). By proceeding as in (
3.8
), we find

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}

\displaystyle\bigl|u_{x}^{2}(x)-(Du_{2j})^{2}\bigr|dx

\displaystyle\hskip-9.95863pt\leq\!\bigg(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left|u_{x}(x)-Du_{2j}\right|^{2}dx\!\bigg)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigg(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\left|u_{x}(x)+Du_{2j}\right|^{2}dx\!\bigg)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\hskip-9.95863pt\leq 2^{1+\beta}\sqrt{\frac{2}{2\beta+1}}F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|u_{x}|_{2,\beta}\Delta x^{\beta}\!,

where we used that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}|

\displaystyle u_{x}(x)+Du_{2j}|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}^{2}(x)dx+2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\biggl(\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}(z)dz\biggr)^{\!2}dx

\displaystyle\leq 2F_{\text{ac},\infty}+2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}\int_{x_{2j}}^{x_{2j+2}}u_{x}^{2}(z)dzdx=4F_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}.

A combination of (
3.7
) and (
3.9
)–(
3
) now yields the result.
∎

Looking at the proof, one observes that all the terms that appear when estimating
\|u_{x}-u_{{\Delta x},x}\|_{2}
involve the factor
\|\delta_{h}u_{x}\|_{2}
. It is therefore to be expected, that the convergence rate in Lemma
3.2
deteriorates with decreasing values of
\beta
, because
u_{x}
behaves more poorly with respect to
L^{2}
-translates as
\beta
becomes smaller.

Remark 3.3
.

As functions in
B_{2}^{\beta}
optimally can be approximated of order
\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\beta})
by piecewise constants, the convergence rate obtained in Lemma
3.2
is suboptimal. The proof reveals that
q_{2j}
, defined in (
2.6
), is the cause of this suboptimality, but this term is crucial in order to ensure that
d\mu_{\Delta x,\textrm{ac}}=u_{\Delta x,x}^{2}dx
is preserved.

If this property is violated, then as pointed out in
[
9
,
22
]
, the numerical approximations would be solutions of the two-component HS system which is accompanied by a density
\rho
. It has been established in
[
19
, Thm. 6.1]
, that if
\rho
is nonzero on an interval
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, then it induces a repulsive force that prevents characteristics starting within
[x_{1},x_{2}]
from meeting each other such that no wave breaking occurs.

4.
Convergence rate in the
\alpha
-dissipative case,
\alpha\in[0,1]

According to
[
9
, Lem. 4.11]
, we have

(4.1)

\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|U(t)-U_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}+\bar{F}_{\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Hence, in order to derive a convergence rate in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for the family
\{u_{\Delta x}(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
, it is enough to obtain a convergence rate for
(y_{{\Delta x}}\!-\!\text{{id}},U_{{\Delta x}})(t)
in
[L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})]^{2}
.
However, there might exist a set
\mathcal{A}
of positive measure on which we have no initial convergence rate for
\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi}
which is problematic, because both
U_{{\Delta x}}(t)
and
y_{{\Delta x}}(t)
heavily depend on
\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi}
, cf. (
2.3
). To resolve this issue, we introduce a transformation that enables us to treat the error on the set
\mathcal{A}
, while we develop an a priori estimate for the remaining error on
\mathcal{A}^{c}
.

Let us start by recalling the following error estimates for the Lagrangian data.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
9
, Lem. 4.4]
)
.

Given
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
, let
\bar{X}=\left(\bar{y},\bar{U},\bar{V}\right)=L\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
, and
\bar{X}_{\Delta x}=(\bar{y}_{\Delta x},\bar{U}_{\Delta x},\bar{V}_{\Delta x})=L\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
, then

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\|\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Delta x,

(4.2b)

\displaystyle\|\bar{U}-\bar{U}_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+2\sqrt{2}\right)\!\bar{F}_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

(4.2c)

\displaystyle\|\bar{V}-\bar{V}_{\Delta x}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Delta x.

Furthermore, we collect two estimates that readily follow from (
2.3
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Given
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
and
t\geq 0
, let
\bar{X}=L\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
and

\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}}=L\circ P_{{\Delta x}}\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
. In addition, define
(y_{{\Delta x}},U_{{\Delta x}},V_{{\Delta x}})(t)=S_{t}(\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}})
and
(y,U,V)(t)=S_{t}(\bar{X})
, then

(4.3)

\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{{\Delta x}}\|_{\infty}+\int_{0}^{t}\|U(s)-U_{{\Delta x}}(s)\|_{\infty}ds.

Moreover, the following pointwise estimate holds for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle|U(t,\xi)

\displaystyle-U_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi)|\leq\|\bar{U}-\bar{U}_{{\Delta x}}\|_{\infty}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt+\!\frac{1}{4}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\!\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\!\!\left(V_{\xi}(s,\eta)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(s,\eta)\right)\!\operatorname{d}\!{\eta}-\!\int_{\xi}^{\infty}\!\!\left(V_{\xi}(s,\eta)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(s,\eta)\right)\!d\eta\bigg)ds\bigg|.

Due to Lemma
4.1
and (
4.3
), it only remains to estimate the second term in (
4.2
). To this end, let us recall the function
g(X)
from
[
21
, Def. 4.3]
which anticipates the future energy loss. This function is based on disjointly partitioning
\mathbb{R}
into a set of points where no wave breaking will take place in the future,
\Omega_{c}
, and its complement,
\Omega_{d}
, containing all the points that eventually will experience wave breaking. More precisely, let

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Omega_{c}(X)

\displaystyle=\{\xi:U_{\xi}(\xi)\geq 0\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{d}(X)

\displaystyle=\{\xi:U_{\xi}(\xi)<0\},

and define

(4.6)

\displaystyle g(X)(\xi)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)V_{\xi}(\xi),&\xi\in\Omega_{d}(X),\\
V_{\xi}(\xi),&\xi\in\Omega_{c}(X).\end{cases}

The following result has been established in
[
9
, Thm. 4.9]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
and
t\geq 0
, let
\bar{X}=L\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
and

\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}}=L\circ P_{\Delta x}\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
. Moreover, introduce
X(t)=S_{t}(\bar{X})
and
X_{\Delta x}(t)=S_{t}(\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}})
, then

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\!\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\!\!\left(V_{\xi}(s,\eta)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(s,\eta)\right)\!\operatorname{d}\!{\eta}

\displaystyle-\!\int_{\xi}^{\infty}\!\!\left(V_{\xi}(s,\eta)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(s,\eta)\right)\!d\eta\bigg)ds\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha)t\|\bar{V}_{\xi}-\bar{V}_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+t\|g(\bar{X})-g(\bar{X}_{\Delta x})\|_{1}\lx@nobreakspace

(4.7)

\displaystyle\quad+2\alpha\sqrt{2\Big(1+\frac{1}{4}t^{2}\Big)\bar{F}_{\infty}}\|\bar{U}_{\xi}-\bar{U}_{\Delta x,\xi}\|_{2}.

Combining Lemma
4.1
, Proposition
4.2
, and Lemma
4.3
reveals that the task of obtaining a convergence rate for
(y_{{\Delta x}}\!-\!\text{{id}},U_{{\Delta x}})(t)
in
[L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})]^{2}
is reduced to understanding how the mapping
L
transports the convergence rate in Lemma
3.2
for
\bar{u}_{{\Delta x},x}
to the variables
(\bar{U}_{{\Delta x},\xi},\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi},g(\bar{X}_{{\Delta x}}))
. Unfortunately, there is no natural way to obtain a convergence rate for these variables on the set where either the exact or the numerical solution experience wave breaking initially. In particular, (
2.4
) combined with Definition
2.2
imply that the exact solution breaks initially in Lagrangian coordinates at the points,

(4.8)

\mathcal{S}:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\bar{y}_{\xi}(\xi)=0\}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\bar{V}_{\xi}(\xi)=1\}.

Analogously, let
S_{{\Delta x}}
denote the corresponding set of points where wave breaking takes places initially for the numerical solution,

(4.9)

\mathcal{S}_{{\Delta x}}\!:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\bar{y}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)\!=0\}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)=1\}.

The reason we fail to obtain a convergence rate over
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{{\Delta x}}
can be explained as follows. Introduce
B=\bar{y}(\mathcal{S})
. From the proof of
[
18
, Thm. 27]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{3N}^{c}\cap\mathcal{S}_{{\Delta x},3N}^{c}}\!\!\!\!\big|V_{\xi}(s,\eta)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(s,\eta)\big|d\eta ds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\!\bigl|u_{{\Delta x},x}^{2}(y)-u_{{\Delta x},x}^{2}

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose

Remark 4.12
.

Mapping into and evolving in Lagrangian coordinates reduces the order
\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{2}}})
from Lemma
3.2
by a half, see Figure
2
. The main reason for this reduction is the term
\|\bar{U}_{\xi}-\bar{U}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(f)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{c})}
, which accounts for the energy discrepancy which arises when either
X
or
X_{{\Delta x}}
experiences wave breaking before the other solution, cf. (
4.3
).

Mapping back to Eulerian coordinates induces an additional reduction in the rate of convergence, because
u
is in general only Hölder continuous with exponent
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, we therefore end up with
\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{8}}})
.

5.
Convergence rate for
\alpha=0

For conservative solutions there is no energy dissipation, and mapping to and evolving in Lagrangian coordinates does therefore not deteriorate the rate of convergence. On the other hand, by exploiting that
V(t,\xi)\!=\!\bar{V}(\xi)
and
V_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi)\!\!=\!\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)
for all
t\!\geq\!0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, one achieves the following much stronger result.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\alpha=0
,
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
, and introduce
(y,U,V)(t)=S_{t}\circ L\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
and
(y_{{\Delta x}},U_{{\Delta x}},V_{{\Delta x}})(t)=S_{t}\circ L\circ P_{{\Delta x}}\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
for
t\in[0,\infty)
, then

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\|U(t)-U_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1+2\sqrt{2})\bar{F}_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+t{\Delta x},

(5.1b)

\displaystyle\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1+2\sqrt{2})\bar{F}_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}t\Delta x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\big(2+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\big){\Delta x}.

Furthermore, let the exact and numerical conservative solution be denoted by
(u,\mu)(t)=T_{t}\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
and
(u_{{\Delta x}},\mu_{{\Delta x}})(t)=T_{t}\circ P_{{\Delta x}}\left((\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\right)
, respectively, then

\displaystyle\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\lx@nobreakspace

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\big(1+2\sqrt{2}\big)}\bar{F}_{\infty}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}t^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}{\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\!1+2\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{\big(2+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\big)}\Big)\!\bar{F}_{\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}{\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+t{\Delta x}.

Proof.

Note that
V(t,\xi)=\bar{V}(\xi)
and
V_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi)\!=\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
. Thanks to Definition
2.5
, this implies
V_{\infty}(t)=\bar{V}_{\infty}=\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\infty}=V_{{\Delta x},\infty}(t)
. It therefore follows from (
2.3
) and Lemma
4.1
that

\displaystyle|U(t,\xi)-U_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq|\bar{U}(\xi)-\bar{U}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\left(\!V(s,\xi)-V_{{\Delta x}}(s,\xi)-\frac{1}{2}\big(V_{\infty}(s)-V_{{\Delta x},\infty}(s)\big)\!\right)\!ds

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{U}-\bar{U}_{{\Delta x}}\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}t\|\bar{V}-\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1+2\sqrt{2})\bar{F}_{\mathrm{ac},\infty}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}{\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+t{\Delta x},

and from here on we can argue precisely as in the proof of Theorem
4.11
. ∎

Next we address the issue of deriving a rate of convergence for the family
\{\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
of numerical energy measures. Recall that for any two nonnegative and finite Borel measures
\lambda
and
\nu
, we define the
bounded Lipschitz
metric (also known as
Fortet–Mourier
metric) by

(5.3)

d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\lambda,\nu):=\sup\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\big(d\lambda(x)-d\nu(x)\big)\!:\psi\in\mathrm{BL}(\mathbb{R}),\hskip 1.42271pt\lx@nobreakspace\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1\Big\},

where
\mathrm{BL}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the space of bounded Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the norm

(5.4)

\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|+\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|}{|x-y|}=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}.

The interested reader is referred to e.g.,
[
2
, Chp. 3]
or
[
3
, Chp. 8]
for a detailed exposition on this metric.

Lemma 5.2
.

Given
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\!\in\mathcal{D}
,
\alpha=0
, and
t\geq 0
, let
(u,\mu)(t)=T_{t}((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))
and
(u_{{\Delta x}},\mu_{{\Delta x}})(t)=T_{t}\circ P_{{\Delta x}}((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))
, then

(5.5)

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu(t),\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t))

\displaystyle\leq(1+2\sqrt{2})t\bar{F}_{\infty}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}{\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\Big(2+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}+4e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\Big)\bar{F}_{\infty}{\Delta x}.

Proof.

Let
(y,U,V)(t)=S_{t}\circ L((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))
and
(y_{{\Delta x}},U_{{\Delta x}},V_{{\Delta x}})(t)=S_{t}\circ L\circ P_{{\Delta x}}((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))
, and pick any
\psi\in\mathrm{BL}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1
. It follows from (
2.5
) that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\big(d\mu(t)-d\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t)\big)\right|\!\!\lx@nobreakspace

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(y(t,\xi))V_{\xi}(t,\xi)d\xi-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi))V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t,\xi)d\xi\right|

(5.6a)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\psi(y(t,\xi))-\psi(y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi))\right|\!V_{\xi}(t,\xi)d\xi

(5.6b)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi))\left(V_{\xi}(t,\xi)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t,\xi)\right)\!d\xi\right|\!,

and since
\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1
and
V_{\xi}(t,\xi)=\bar{V}_{\xi}(\xi)
, we have the following estimate for (
5.6a
),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\psi(y(t,\xi))-\psi(y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi))\big|V_{\xi}(t,\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|y(t,\xi)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi)\right|\!\bar{V}_{\xi}(\xi)d\xi

(5.7)

\displaystyle\leq\|y(t)-y_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\bar{F}_{\infty}.

For the term (
5.6b
), we integrate by parts, which yields

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi))\bigl(V_{\xi}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t,\xi)\bigr)d\xi\biggr|\lx@nobreakspace

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi^{\prime}(y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi))y_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t,\xi)\big(\bar{V}(\xi)-\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)\big)d\xi\right|\!,

because the boundary terms vanish as Definition
2.5
implies that left and right asymptotes of
\bar{V}
and
\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}
coincide. Moreover, by following the proof of
[
21
, (2.15)]
, we find

y_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t,\xi)+\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)\leq\left(\bar{y}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)+\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)\right)e^{\frac{1}{2}t},

which together with Lemma
4.1
and the fact that
\bar{V}
and
\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}
are increasing and satisfy
\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi_{3j})=\bar{F}(x_{2j})=\bar{V}(\xi_{3j})
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
by Definition
2.5
, implies the following upper bound on (
5
),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\psi^{\prime}(y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi))\right|

\displaystyle y_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t,\xi)\!\left|\bar{V}(\xi)-\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)\right|\!d\xi

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\bar{V}(\xi)-\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)\right|\!\bar{V}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\bar{V}(\xi)-\bar{V}_{{\Delta x}}(\xi)\right|\!\bar{y}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\bar{F}_{\infty}{\Delta x}+e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\xi_{3j}}^{\xi_{3j+3}}\!\left|\bar{V}(\xi_{3j+3})-\bar{V}(\xi_{3j})\right|\bar{y}_{{\Delta x},\xi}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\bar{F}_{\infty}{\Delta x}+2e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\!\left(\bar{F}(x_{2j+2})-\bar{F}(x_{2j})\right)\!{\Delta x}

\displaystyle\leq 4e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\bar{F}_{\infty}{\Delta x}.

At last, combining this estimate with Lemma
5.1
and (
5
), and thereafter taking the supremum over all functions in
\mathrm{BL}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1
, gives (
5.5
).
∎

Remark 5.3
.

If we impose a first order moment condition on
\bar{\mu}
, i.e., assume that
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathbb{R})\!:=\{\lambda\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{R})\!:\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|d\lambda<\infty\}
, then one can argue in precisely the same way using the 1-Wasserstein distance. This distance, which is defined for any two measures
\lambda
and
\nu
that belong to
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and have the same total mass, can be expressed in the following two equivalent ways, see
[
37
, Sec. 1 and 7]
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\lambda,\nu):=\sup_{\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\big(d\lambda(x)-d\nu(x)\big)\right\}\!=\|F_{\lambda}-F_{\nu}\|_{1}.

Here
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
denotes the Lipschitz seminorm introduced in (
5.4
) and
F_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda((-\infty,x))
and
F_{\nu}(x)=\nu((\infty,x))
are the cumulative functions associated with
\lambda
and
\nu
, respectively.

This metric can be applied in the case of conservative solutions, because (
3.1c
) implies
\bar{\mu}_{{\Delta x}}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
, and the proof of
[
7
, Thm. 2.11]
reveals that both
\mu(t)
and
\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t)
carry the first moment forward in time, i.e., they belong to
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover, Definition
2.5
guarantees that
\bar{\mu}_{{\Delta x}}(\mathbb{R})=\bar{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mu(t,\mathbb{R})=\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t,\mathbb{R})
when
\alpha=0
. As a consequence, we have

\|F(t)-F_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{1}\leq(1+2\sqrt{2})t\bar{F}_{\infty}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}{\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\Big(2+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}+4e^{\frac{1}{2}t}\Big)\bar{F}_{\infty}{\Delta x},

whenever
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha=0
.

In the nonconservative case (
\alpha\neq 0
), there is no reason to expect that
\mu(t)
and
\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t)
have the same total mass for a fixed
t>0
, because we lack sufficient control over the size of the following sets

\left\{x\!:\bar{u}_{x}(x)\leq-\frac{2}{t}<\bar{u}_{{\Delta x},x}(x)\right\}\bigcup\left\{x\!:\bar{u}_{{\Delta x},x}(x)\leq-\frac{2}{t}<\bar{u}_{x}(x)\right\}\!.

The former set contains the points in Eulerian coordinates for which the exact solution breaks within
[0,t]
, whereas the numerical solution either breaks at some later time or does not break at all. The second set consists of those points for which the opposite holds. Note that the
L^{2}
-error bound on
u_{{\Delta x},x}
in Lemma
3.2
provides some control on the difference in measure of these sets, as we have by the Chebychev inequality

\Big|\big\{x:|\bar{u}_{x}(x)-\bar{u}_{{\Delta x},x}|\geq\Delta x^{\gamma}\big\}\Big|\lx@nobreakspace\leq{\Delta x}^{-2\gamma}\|\bar{u}_{x}-\bar{u}_{{\Delta x},x}\|_{2}\leq D{\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{(\beta-4\gamma)}}{{2}}},

which tends to zero as
{\Delta x}\rightarrow 0
if
\gamma<\frac{\beta}{4}
. Yet, one is unable to use this for proving that
\{\mu_{{\Delta x}}(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
converges weakly or even vaguely for every fixed
t>0
. The reason is that one needs to control
\|V_{\xi}(t)-V_{{\Delta x},\xi}(t)\|_{p}
or
\|V(t)-V_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{p}
, for some
p\in[1,\infty]
, and this is hard. On the contrary, when one derives a convergence rate for
\{u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
one needs an error estimate for
\{U_{{\Delta x}}(t)\}_{{\Delta x}>0}
, in which case one only needs to control the time integral appearing in (
4.2
).

Remark 5.4
.

A fully discrete numerical method for conservative solutions was proposed in
[
22
]
. This method is based on successively evolving the numerical solution time steps of length
\Delta t
forward along characteristics (
\Delta t
is related to
{\Delta x}
through a CFL-like condition), and after each time increment a piecewise linear projection operator is applied. Let
(\tilde{u}_{{\Delta x}},\tilde{F}_{{\Delta x}})
denote the resulting numerical solution computed this way on a fixed mesh. Then it is shown that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|\!\left(\tilde{u}_{{\Delta x}}-u\right)\!(t)\|_{\infty}+\|\!\left(\tilde{F}_{{\Delta x}}-F\right)\!(t)\|_{\infty}\right)\!\leq\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}),

provided
(u,F)\in[W^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})]^{2}
, i.e., before wave breaking takes place, see
[
22
, Thm. 3]
. This result can be improved for our numerical method. In particular, if
(u,F)\in[W^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})]^{2}
, then

(5.9)

u_{{\Delta x}}(t,y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi_{3j}))\!=\!u(t,y(t,\xi_{3j}))\hskip 7.11317pt\text{and}\lx@nobreakspace\hskip 7.11317pt\lx@nobreakspace F_{{\Delta x}}(t,y_{{\Delta x}}(t,\xi_{3j}))\!=\!F(t,y(t,\xi_{3j})),

for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, since we only apply
P_{{\Delta x}}
from Definition
2.5
once, following the proof of
[
22
, Thm. 3]
and using (
5.9
) reveals that our method satisfies

(5.10)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big(\|(u_{{\Delta x}}-u)(t)\|_{\infty}+\|(F_{{\Delta x}}-F)(t)\|_{\infty}\big)\!\leq\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}).

In practice, this means that our numerical method converges in the case of Lipschitz initial data with order
\mathcal{O}({\Delta x})
prior to wave breaking. This is also true when
\alpha\neq 0
, provided the numerical solutions do not experience wave breaking.

6.
Explicit examples with convergence rates

We complement the previous three sections by examining three explicit examples that satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4.11
. They are inspired by the examples explored numerically in
[
9
, Sec. 5]
. For each example, we perform one or several tests of the numerical convergence rate. More precisely, we compute a sequence
\{(u_{{\Delta x}_{k}},F_{{\Delta x}_{k}})\}_{k}
of numerical approximations,
where

{\Delta x}_{k}=2^{-2k}\hskip 9.95863pt\lx@nobreakspace k\geq 1,

and thereafter compute the relative errors

\mathrm{Err}_{k}(T):=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}_{k}}(t)\|_{\infty}}{\|u(t)\|_{\infty}},

and the experimental orders of convergence (EOCs)

\mathrm{EOC}_{k}(T):=\frac{\ln(\nicefrac{{\mathrm{Err}_{k-1}(T)}}{{\mathrm{Err}_{k}(T)}})}{\ln(\nicefrac{{{\Delta x}_{k-1}}}{{{\Delta x}_{k}}})}.

Example 6.1

(Multipeakon data)
.

Firstly, let us consider a general multipeakon, i.e., a piecewise linear and continuous wave profile
\bar{u}
. This constitutes an important family of initial data, because the piecewise linear nature of the data is naturally preserved by the HS equation. Moreover, it is also one of the few classes of solutions for where explicit expressions can be computed.

Let
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}
,
\{b_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N}
, and
\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N+1}
be real-valued sequences with
z_{k}<z_{k+1}
for
k\in\{0,..,N\}
, and consider the multipeakon

(6.1)

\displaystyle\bar{u}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}b_{0},&x<z_{0},\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}x+b_{n},&z_{n}\leq x<z_{n+1},\\
a_{n+1}x+b_{n+1},&z_{n+1}\leq x<z_{n+2},\\
\vdots\\
b_{N},&z_{N+1}\leq x.\end{cases}

The derivative is a bounded, piecewise constant function with finitely many jumps, and its total variation is therefore the sum of the absolute value of these jumps, namely

TV(\bar{u}_{x})=|a_{1}|+\sum_{n=1}^{N-2}|a_{n+1}-a_{n}|+|a_{N-1}|<\infty.

Consequently, for any
h\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bar{u}_{x}(x)-\bar{u}_{x}(x-h)\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{j\in\{1,...,N-1\}}|a_{j}|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\bar{u}_{x}(x)-\bar{u}_{x}(x-h)\right|dx

\displaystyle=2\|\bar{u}_{x}\|_{\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{jh}^{(j+1)h}\left|\bar{u}_{x}(x)-\bar{u}_{x}(x-h)\right|dx

\displaystyle=2\|\bar{u}_{x}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{h}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\bar{u}_{x}(z+jh)-\bar{u}_{x}(z+(j-1)h)\right|dz

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\bar{u}_{x}\|_{\infty}TV(\bar{u}_{x})h,

or, in other words,
\bar{u}_{x}
belongs to
B_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\mathbb{R})
for any multipeakon data. In fact, this is a particular case of the more general inclusion

(6.2)

\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subset B_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\mathbb{R}).

As a consequence, Theorem
4.11
implies, that for any
(\bar{u},\bar{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}
with
\bar{u}_{x}\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\bar{\mu}_{\mathrm{sc}}=0
, we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle\|u(t)-u_{{\Delta x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{O}({\Delta x}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{16}}}),

where
(u,\mu)(t)=T_{t}((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))
and
(u_{{\Delta x}},\mu_{{\Delta x}})(t)=T_{t}\circ P_{{\Delta x}}(((\bar{u},\bar{\mu}))
. Moreover, if
\bar{u}
is given by (

for all

, such that the conservative and

) gives the improved bound,

[
9
, Sec. 5]
and
[
22
, Sec. 3]
.

Since
C_{c}^{0,\beta}\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for all
\beta\in(0,1]
, the case
\bar{u}_{x}\in C_{c}^{0,\beta}
excludes wave breaking times from accumulating initially, the same can be said for
\bar{u}_{x}\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R})
, but it can occur when
\bar{u}_{x}\in B_{2}^{\beta}
. Indeed, introduce the Besov space

\tilde{B}_{2}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})\!:=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\!:\!\exists\lx@nobreakspace C>0\text{ s.t. }\|\delta_{h}f\|_{2}\leq Ch^{\beta}\text{ for all }\!\lx@nobreakspace h\geq 0\big\},

then, clearly
\tilde{B}_{2}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq B_{2}^{\beta}
. Moreover, the following imbedding property was proved in
[
36
, Corollary 33]
,

(6.4)

\tilde{B}_{2}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{q}(\mathbb{R}),

with
q\in[2,\infty]
for
\beta>\frac{1}{2}
, and
q\in[2,p^{*})
in the case
\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}
, where

(6.5)

p^{*}=\frac{2}{1-2\beta}.

Again,
\tilde{B}_{2}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
whenever
\beta>\frac{1}{2}
. However, infinitesimal energy concentrations can happen initially if
\bar{u}_{x}\in\tilde{B}_{2}^{\beta}
for
\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}
. In fact, the cusped initial data studied in
[
9
, Sec. 5]
and
[
22
, Sec. 3]
belongs to
\tilde{B}_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{6}}}(\mathbb{R})
, which we now prove.

Example 6.3

(Cusp data)
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\leq b
and consider the initial data

\displaystyle\bar{u}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left|a\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}\!,&x<a,\\
\left|x\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}\!,&a\leq x\leq b,\\
\left|b\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}\!,&b<x,\end{cases}

with derivative given by

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x<a,\\
\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{sgn}(x)\left|x\right|^{-\frac{1}{3}}\!,&a\leq x\leq b,\\
0,&b<x.\end{cases}

By a direct calculation, one verifies that
\bar{u}_{x}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for
p\in(0,3)
. Hence, if
\bar{u}_{x}
is to belong to a certain Besov space
\tilde{B}_{2}^{\beta}
, then (
6.4
) reveals that
p^{*}=3
provides an upper bound on its
q
-integrability, which in particular implies, due to (
6.5
), that
\beta\leq\frac{1}{6}
. In other words, the best we can hope to prove is
\bar{u}_{x}\in\tilde{B}_{2}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\beta\leq\frac{1}{6}
. Before analyzing this in more detail, let us consider two specific situations, for which we obtain a better convergence rate than that guaranteed by Theorem
4.11
.

•

If
a>0
, then
\bar{u}_{x}(x)\geq 0
everywhere, and no wave breaking takes place in the future. Moreover
\bar{u}_{x}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that (
5.10
) applies.

•

If
b<0
, then
\bar{u}_{x}
is monotonically decreasing and bounded over
[a,b]
. It therefore belongs to
\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R})
, and hence
\bar{u}_{x}\in B_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
by (
6.2
).

It remains to consider
a\leq 0<b
and
a<0\leq b
, in which case wave breaking happens for each
t\in\big[0,3|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\big]
.

Suppose
h>0
and note that
\mathrm{supp}(\bar{u}_{x}(\cdot+h))\subseteq[a-h,b-h]
. Hence, there is no overlap between the support of
\bar{u}_{x}(\cdot+h)
and
\bar{u}_{x}
when
h\geq b-a=b+|a|
. In this case, recall
\delta_{h}f(x)=f(x+h)-f(x)
, we therefore get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\delta_{h}\bar{u}_{x})^{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9}\int_{a-h}^{b-h}|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx+\frac{4}{9}\int_{a}^{b}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\Big(|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}+b^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\Big)\leq\frac{8}{3}h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}.

Assume from now on that
h<b-a
, and decompose
\|\delta_{h}\bar{u}_{x}\|_{2}^{2}
in the following way

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\delta_{h}\bar{u}_{x})^{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9}\int_{a-h}^{a}|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx+\frac{4}{9}\int_{b-h}^{b}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx

(6.6)

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84544pt+\frac{4}{9}\int_{a}^{b-h}\left(\mathrm{sgn}(x+h)|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}-\mathrm{sgn}(x)|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\right)^{2}dx.

In order to estimate the two first integrals we exploit the following inequality.

Proposition 6.4
.

Given
q\in(0,1]
, then for any
c,d,\in\mathbb{R}
one has

\left||c|^{q}-|d|^{q}\right|\leq|c-d|^{q}.

Proof.

Let us start by defining the function
f(t)=(1+t)^{q}-1-t^{q}
and observe that
f(0)=0
. Moreover, for any
t>0
, we have
f^{\prime}(t)=q((1+t)^{q-1}-t^{q-1})<0
such that
f(t)<0
for all
t\in(0,\infty)
. Pick any
\tilde{a},\tilde{b}>0
. Substituting
t=\frac{\tilde{b}}{\tilde{a}}
yields

0>\Big(1+\frac{\tilde{b}}{\tilde{a}}\Big)^{q}-1-\Big(\frac{\tilde{b}}{\tilde{a}}\Big)^{q}=\Big(\frac{\tilde{a}+\tilde{b}}{\tilde{a}}\Big)^{q}-1-\Big(\frac{\tilde{b}}{\tilde{a}}\Big)^{q},

which in turn implies

(6.7)

(\tilde{a}+\tilde{b})^{q}\leq\tilde{a}^{q}+\tilde{b}^{q}\hskip 9.95863pt\lx@nobreakspace\text{for all }\tilde{a},\tilde{b}\geq 0.

Pick any
c,d\in\mathbb{R}
. By (
6.7
) and the fact that
|\cdot|^{q}
is increasing, we obtain

|c|^{q}=||c-d+d||^{q}\leq(|c-d|+|d|)^{q}\leq|c-d|^{q}+|d|^{q}

In other words,
|c|^{q}-|d|^{q}\leq|c-d|^{q}
, by arguing similarly with the roles of
c
and
d
reversed, the inequality follows.
∎

By using Proposition
6.4
we find that

\displaystyle\int_{a-h}^{a}|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx=3(|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}+\mathrm{sgn}(a+h)|a+h|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})\leq\begin{cases}3h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}},&|a|\geq h,\\
6h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}},&|a|<h,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\int_{b-h}^{b}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx=3(|b|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}-\mathrm{sgn}(b-h)|b-h|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}3h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}},&b\geq h,\\
6h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}},&b<h.\end{cases}

It remains to consider the last term in (
6.6
). Suppose
h<\min\{|a|,b\}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b-h}\!\bigl(\mathrm{sgn}(x+h)|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}-\mathrm{sgn}(x)|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\bigr)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{-h}\left(|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}-|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\right)^{2}dx

(6.8)

\displaystyle+\int_{-h}^{0}\!\left(|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}+|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{b-h}\!\left(|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}-|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\right)^{2}dx.

Let us estimate the first integral. By a direct calculation

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{-h}\!\left(|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}-|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{-h}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx+\int_{a}^{-h}|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{a}^{-h}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}dx

\displaystyle=3\Big(|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}-h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}+||a|-h|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{a}^{-h}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}dx.

The last term is negative and since
|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\geq|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}
for
x\in[a,-h]
, we get

(6.9)

\displaystyle-2\int_{a}^{-h}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}dx\leq-2\int_{-|a|}^{-h}|x|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}dx=6\Big(h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}-|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\Big).

Combining (

) with the fact that
|\cdot|^{q}
is increasing and (
6.9
) therefore yields

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{-h}\!\left(|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}-|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq 3\Big(|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}-h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}+\underbrace{||a|-h|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}}_{\leq|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}+h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}}\Big)+6\Big(h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}-|a|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\Big)\vskip-5.69046pt

One can argue similarly for the last integral in (
6.3
). Moreover, using
(c+d)^{2}\leq 2(c^{2}+d^{2})
with

and
d=|x|^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}
for the second term in (
6.3
) gives

\int_{-h}^{0}\!\left(|x|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}+|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{3}}}\right)^{2}\!dx\leq 2\int_{-h}^{0}\!\left(|x|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}+|x+h|^{\nicefrac{{-2}}{{3}}}\right)\!dx\leq 12h^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}},

and we thus end up with

\|\delta_{h}\bar{u}_{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{40}{3}|h|^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}},\hskip 14.22636pt\lx@nobreakspace\text{when }\lx@nobreakspace h<\min\{|a|,b\}.

The cases
|a|\leq h\leq b
and
b<h<|a|
can be treated similarly, and, moreover, as one of the terms in (

Appendix A
Exact solution to the multipeakon example

Given some
\alpha\in[0,1]
, consider the Cauchy problem with initial data

\displaystyle\bar{u}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2},&x<0,\\
\frac{1}{2}-x,&0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
0,&\frac{1}{2}<x,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\bar{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\
x,&0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
\frac{1}{2},&\frac{1}{2}<x.\end{cases}

Applying the mapping
L
from (
2.2
) gives the Lagrangian triplet
(\bar{y},\bar{U},\bar{V})
given by

\displaystyle\bar{y}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\xi,&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{2}\xi,&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\xi-\frac{1}{2},&1<\xi,\end{cases}\lx@nobreakspace

\displaystyle\bar{U}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2},&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-\xi),&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
0,&1<\xi\end{cases}

\displaystyle\bar{V}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{2}\xi,&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\frac{1}{2},&1<\xi,\end{cases}

and by inserting into (
2.4
) one finds

(A.1)

\displaystyle\tau(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2,&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In other words, no wave breaking occurs inside the time interval
[0,2)
, and the solution to (
2.3
) with initial data
(\bar{y},\bar{U},\bar{V})
therefore reads, for
t\in[0,2)
,

(A.2a)

\displaystyle y(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\xi-\frac{1}{16}t^{2}+\frac{1}{2}t,&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{8}(t-2)^{2}\xi-\frac{1}{16}t^{2}+\frac{1}{2}t,&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\xi+\frac{1}{16}t^{2}-\frac{1}{2},&1<\xi,\end{cases}

(A.2b)

\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace U(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{8}t+\frac{1}{2},&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{4}(t-2)\xi-\frac{1}{8}t+\frac{1}{2},&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\frac{1}{8}t,&1<\xi,\end{cases}

(A.2c)

\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace V(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\bar{V}(\xi).

Observe that
y(t,\cdot)
is strictly increasing for each
t\in[0,2)
, such that its inverse can be computed by solving
x=y(t,\xi)
in terms of
\xi
. It therefore follows from (
2.5
) that

(A.3)

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{8}t+\frac{1}{2},&x<\frac{1}{16}(8-t)t,\\
\frac{1}{4(t-2)}(8x-(t+4)),&\frac{1}{16}(8-t)t\leq x\leq\frac{1}{16}(t^{2}+8),\\
\frac{1}{8}t,&\frac{1}{16}(t^{2}+8)<x,\end{cases}

(A.4)

\displaystyle F(t,x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x<\frac{1}{16}(8-t)t,\\
\frac{1}{4(t-2)^{2}}(16x+t^{2}-8t),&\frac{1}{16}(8-t)t\leq x\leq\frac{1}{16}(t^{2}+8),\\
\frac{1}{2},&\frac{1}{16}(t^{2}+8)<x.\end{cases}

Wave breaking takes place at time
t=2
for all
\xi\in[0,1]
, cf. (
A.1
). Thus, as some energy possibly is lost at this time, the Lagrangian energy has to be rescaled. In particular, it takes the following form for
t\geq 2
,

\displaystyle V(t,\xi)=\begin{cases}0,&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)\xi,&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha),&1<\xi.\end{cases}

Solving (
2.3
) with data
\lim_{t\uparrow 2}(y,U)(t)=(y,U)(2)
thus yields

(A.5a)

\displaystyle y(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\xi-\frac{1}{16}(1-\alpha)t^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}\alpha,&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{8}(1-\alpha)(t-2)^{2}\xi-\frac{1}{16}(1-\alpha)t^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}\alpha,&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\xi+\frac{1}{16}(1-\alpha)t^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\alpha t-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\alpha,&1<\xi,\end{cases}\lx@nobreakspace

(A.5b)

\displaystyle U(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{8}(1-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha),&\xi<0,\\
\frac{1}{4}(1-\alpha)(t-2)\xi-\frac{1}{8}(1-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha),&0\leq\xi\leq 1,\\
\frac{1}{8}(1-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}\alpha,&1<\xi.\end{cases}

No more wave breaking occurs, such that (
A.2a
) together with (
A.5
) provide a global solution to (
2.3
). Moreover,
y(t,\cdot)
is strictly increasing for
t>2
. Its inverse can therefore be computed, which due to (
2.5
) implies

(A.6a)

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{8}(1-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha),&x<x_{1}(t),\\
\frac{2}{t-2}\big(x-\frac{1}{8}(t+4)\big),&x_{1}(t)\leq x\leq x_{2}(t),\\
\frac{1}{8}(1-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}\alpha,&x_{2}(t)<x,\end{cases}

(A.6b)

\displaystyle F(t,x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&x<x_{1}(t),\\
\frac{4}{(t-2)^{2}}\big(x+\frac{1}{16}(1-\alpha)t^{2}-\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha)t-\frac{1}{4}\alpha\big),&x_{1}(t)\leq x\leq x_{2}(t),\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha),&x_{2}(t)<x,\end{cases}

where we introduced, for
t\geq 2
,

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)\!:

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{16}(1-\alpha)t^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha)t+\frac{1}{4}\alpha,

\displaystyle x_{2}(t)\!:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}(1-\alpha)t^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\alpha t+\frac{1}{4}(2-\alpha).

Combining (
A.3
) and (
A.6
) yields the globally defined
\alpha
-dissipative solution
(u,F)(t,\cdot)
.
