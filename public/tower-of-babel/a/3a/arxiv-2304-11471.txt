The congruence properties of Romik’s sequence of Taylor coefficients of Jacobi’s theta function θ 3 \theta_{3} Thanks: † Research partially supported by the Austrian Science Foundation FWF, grant S50-N15, in the framework of the Special Research Program “Algorithmic and Enumerative Combinatorics”

Abstract
. In [ Ramanujan J. 52 (2020), 275–290], Romik considered the Taylor expansion of Jacobi’s theta function θ 3 ​ ( q ) \theta_{3}(q) at q = e − π q=e^{-\pi} and encoded it in an integer sequence ( d ⁡ ( n ) ) n ≥ 0 (d(n))_{n\geq 0} for which he provided a recursive procedure to compute the terms of the sequence. He observed intriguing behaviour of d ⁡ ( n ) d(n) modulo primes and prime powers. Here we prove (1) that d ⁡ ( n ) d(n) eventually vanishes modulo any prime power p e p^{e} with p ≡ 3 p\equiv 3 (mod 4), (2) that d ⁡ ( n ) d(n) is eventually periodic modulo any prime power p e p^{e} with p ≡ 1 p\equiv 1 (mod 4), and (3) that d ⁡ ( n ) d(n) is purely periodic modulo any 2-power 2 e 2^{e} . Our results also provide more detailed information on period length, respectively from when on the sequence vanishes or becomes periodic. The corresponding bounds may not be optimal though, as computer data suggest. Our approach shows that the above congruence properties hold at a much finer, polynomial level.

In order to carry out this programme, we need an explicit formula
for the entries of this inverse matrix. It turns out that this can best be achieved if one
defines the (larger) infinite matrix
\mathbf{R}=(R(n,k))_{n,k\geq 0}
by

R(n,k)=2^{(n-k)/2}\frac{n!}{k!}\left\langle t^{n}\right\rangle U^{k}(t).

(2.10)

Since
U(t)
is a power series in which even powers of
t
do not
occur, the matrix
\mathbf{R}
has a “checkerboard pattern”; more
precisely,
R(n,k)\neq 0
if, and only if,
n
and
k
have the same parity.
The entries
R(n,k)
are in fact all integers. This follows from this
checkerboard pattern of
\mathbf{R}
(implying that
2^{(n-k)/2}
on
the right-hand side of (
2.10
) is always an integer) and from the
exponential formula of combinatorics as Romik explains in
[
10
, Proof
of Theorem 7]
in a special case (namely in the case where
both
n
and
k
are even; the general case can be treated in the
same manner).
The matrix
\big(r(n,k)\big)_{n,k\geq 0}
is a submatrix of
\mathbf{R}

since
R(2n,2k)=r(n,k)
for all
n
and
k
(compare with (
2.7
)).

Proposition 2
.

The inverse
\mathbf{R}^{-1}
of
\mathbf{R}
is given by

(R^{-1}(n,k))_{n,k\geq 0}
with

R^{-1}(n,k)=2^{(n-k)/2}\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!}\left\langle t^{-k}\right\rangle U^{-n}(t).

(2.11)

Here, the case
k=0
has to be interpreted as
R^{-1}(0,0)=1
and

R^{-1}(n,0)=0
for
n\geq 1
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{R}^{-1}
has integer entries and

R^{-1}(n,k)\neq 0
only if
n
and
k
have the same parity.

Proof.

That
\mathbf{R}^{-1}
has integer entries is obvious since it is the
inverse of a triangular matrix with integer entries
(cf.
[
10
, Theorem 7]
) and
1
’s on the main diagonal.

The first assertion is a consequence of Lagrange inversion (cf.
[
12
, Theorem 5.4.2]
): if
F(t)
is a formal power series with

F(0)=0
and
F^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, and
F^{(-1)}(t)
is its compositional
inverse, then

\left\langle t^{n}\right\rangle\big(F^{(-1)}(t)\big)^{k}=\frac{k}{n}\left\langle t^{-k}\right\rangle F^{-n}(t).

In order to apply this theorem to our situation, it must be observed
that under the above conditions the matrix

\big(\left\langle t^{n}\right\rangle F^{k}(t)\big)_{n,k\geq 0}
is inverse to

\big(\left\langle t^{n}\right\rangle\big(F^{(-1)}(t)\big)^{k}\big)_{n,k\geq 0}
.
If one applies this observation with
F(t)=U(t)
, then the assertion
of the proposition follows upon little manipulation.

With the formula (
2.11
) established,
the parity condition follows again from the fact that
U(t)
is a
power series in which only odd powers of
t
occur.
∎

Using the entries of the matrix
\mathbf{R}
, the system of
equations (
2.8
) can be rewritten as

v(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}R(2n,2k)d(k),\quad\text{for all }n\geq 0.

If we invert this relation using the inverse matrix
\mathbf{R}^{-1}
,
then we obtain

d(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}R^{-1}(2n,2k)v(k).

(2.12)

We are going to use this formula in the proofs in Sections
10

and
12
.

3.
Classical criteria for divisibility of factorials and binomial
coefficients by prime powers

Here and in the sequel, for a prime number
p
,
let
v_{p}(\alpha)
denote the
p
-adic valuation of
the integer (or rational number)
\alpha
, defined as
the maximal exponent
e
such that
\alpha/p^{e}

is an integer (respectively a rational number with numerator and denominator
coprime to
p
).

There are essentially two formulae for the
p
-adic valuation of a
factorial. The one that we need in this article is Legendre’s formula.

Lemma 3

(
Legendre’s formula
[
7
, p. 12]
)
.

Let
N
be a positive integer and
p
a prime number.
Then

v_{p}(N!)=\frac{N-s_{p}(N)}{p-1},

where
s_{p}(N)
denotes the sum of digits in the
p
-adic
representation of
N
.

Similarly, there are essentially two formulae for the
p
-adic
valuation of a binomial coefficient, one in terms of the number of
carries when adding the involved numbers, the other in terms of digit sums;
we shall need the former one.

Lemma 4

(
Kummer’s theorem
[
5
, pp. 115–116]
)
.

Let
N
and
K
be positive integers and
p
a prime number.
Then

v_{p}\left(\binom{N}{K}\right)

equals the number of carries when
K
and
N-K
are added in terms of
their
p
-adic representations.

The following relation forms the link between the previous two lemmas,
and it provides, at the same time, the previously mentioned alternative
in computing the
p
-adic valuation of a binomial coefficient.

Lemma 5
.

Let
A
and
B
be positive integers and
p
a prime number.
Then

\frac{1}{p-1}\Big(s_{p}(A)+s_{p}(B)-s_{p}(A+B)\Big)\\
=\#(\text{carries when adding $A$ and $B$ in their $p$-adic representations}).

4.
The sequence
(u(n))_{n\geq 0}
modulo odd prime powers

In this section, we analyse the numbers
u(n)
modulo odd prime
powers
p^{e}
. The first result is Theorem
9
which states that,
for primes
p
congruent to 3 modulo 4 and all integers
e\geq 2
,
the number
u(n)
vanishes modulo
p^{e}
for
n\geq\left\lfloor(e-1)p^{2}/2\right\rfloor
.
The proof of the theorem is inductive, the
start of the induction being given in Proposition
8
,
which is itself based on auxiliary results in Lemma
7
.
In case the prime
p
should be congruent to
1

modulo 4, however, a much stronger result holds; see
Theorem
11
. The proof of that theorem requires two
auxiliary results which we state and prove separately; cf. Lemmas
13
and
14
.

We begin by providing lower bounds on
the
p
-adic valuations of the products
\Pi_{1}(N)
and
\Pi_{3}(N)

defined in (
2.3
) that will be ubiquitously used in this and
the next section.

Lemma 6
.

For all odd primes
p
and non-negative integers
N,
we have

v_{p}\big(\Pi_{1}(N)\big)\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{N}{p}\right\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad v_{p}\big(\Pi_{3}(N)\big)\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{N}{p}\right\rfloor.

(4.1)

If
p\equiv 1
(mod
4
), then we even have

v_{p}\big(\Pi_{3}(N)\big)\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{N+\frac{3}{4}(p-1)}{p}\right\rfloor.

(4.2)

Next we state and prove the announced auxiliary results,
which afterwards lead to Proposition
8
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
p
be an odd prime number,
and let
(u(n))_{n\geq 0}
be defined by the recurrence (
2.2
).
Then we have

u(ap+b)\equiv 0~(\text{mod }p),\quad\text{for }1\leq a\leq\tfrac{p-1}{2}\text{ and\/ }0\leq b\leq a-1,

(4.3)

and

u\left(ap+\tfrac{p-1}{2}+b\right)\equiv 0~(\text{mod }p),\quad\text{for }1\leq a\leq\tfrac{p-1}{2}\text{ and\/ }0\leq b\leq a.

(4.4)

Moreover, if
p\equiv 3
(mod
4
),
the second congruence also holds for
a=0
, that is,
u\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)\equiv 0
(mod
p
) for primes
p

with
p\equiv 3
(mod
4
).

Proof.

We prove the assertions by induction on the size of
ap+b

and of
ap+\frac{p-1}{2}+b
, simultaneously. The induction
is based on the recurrence (
2.2
).

Now let first
n=ap+b
with
a
and
b
satisfying the conditions
in (
4.3
). We want to prove that
u(n)\equiv 0
(mod
p
).

It is clear that the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.2
),
the product
\Pi_{1}(n)=\Pi_{1}(ap+b)
, is
divisible by
p
because of (
4.1
).

From Lemma
4
we infer that
the binomial coefficient
\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}=\binom{2ap+2b+1}{2m+1}
is divisible by
p
, except when

2m=cp+d
with both
0\leq c\leq 2a
and
0\leq d\leq 2b
.
It is important to note that at this point the upper bounds of

\frac{p-1}{2}
for
a
and
b
enter crucially.

If
c=0
(and hence
d
even since
2m=cp+d=d
), then

\Pi_{3}(n-m)=\Pi_{3}\left(ap+b-\frac{d}{2}\right)
is divisible by
p

according to (
4.1
).
From now on we may assume that
c\geq 1
.

We distinguish whether
c
and
d
are both even or both odd.
(There are no other cases since
2m=cp+d
is even.)

If both
c
and
d
are even, then, provided
c>d
, we may use the
induction hypothesis (
4.3
) to infer that

u(m)=u\left(\frac{c}{2}p+\frac{d}{2}\right)
is divisible by
p
. On the other hand, if
c\leq d
,
then we have

n-m=\left(a-\tfrac{c}{2}\right)p+b-\tfrac{d}{2}.

Since, by assumption, we have
a>b
, we have

a-\tfrac{c}{2}>b-\tfrac{d}{2}\geq 0.

Consequently, again using (
4.1
), we
obtain that
\Pi_{3}(n-m)
is divisible by
p
.

Now let both
c
and
d
be odd. Here we may rewrite

m=\frac{c}{2}p+\frac{d}{2}
as

m=\tfrac{c-1}{2}p+\tfrac{p-1}{2}+\tfrac{d+1}{2}.

Furthermore, we
may write

n-m=\left(a-\tfrac{c+1}{2}\right)p+\tfrac{p-1}{2}+b-\tfrac{d-1}{2}.

It should be noted that, if
c\leq d
, we have
a-\tfrac{c+1}{2}>b-\tfrac{d+1}{2}\geq 0.

As a consequence,
by (
4.1
),
we have
\Pi_{3}(n-m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
).
If, on the other hand, we have
c>d
, then

u(m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
), again
by the induction hypothesis (
4.4
).

Next we discuss the case where

n=ap+\frac{p-1}{2}+b
with
a
and
b
satisfying the conditions
in (
4.4
). We want to prove that
u(n)\equiv 0
(mod
p
).

Again it is clear that the first term on the right-hand side
of (
2.2
),
the product
\Pi_{1}(n)=\Pi_{1}(ap+\frac{p-1}{2}+b)
, is
divisible by
p
because of (
4.1
).

In the current case, the binomial coefficient on the right-hand side
of (
2.2
) becomes
\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}=\binom{(2a+1)p+2b}{2m+1}
. Here it must be observed that,
because of the upper bounds on
a
and
b
in (
4.4
), we
have
2a+1\leq p
and
2b\leq p-1
.
If
a=\frac{p-1}{2}
, so that
2a+1=p
, then by Lemma
4

\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}=\binom{p^{2}+2b}{2m+1}
is divisible by
p
, except
when
1\leq 2m+1\leq 2b
. In this exceptional case, we have

\Pi_{3}(n-m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
) by (
4.1
).

We assume from now on that
a<\frac{p-1}{2}
.
From Lemma
4
we infer that
the binomial coefficient
\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}=\binom{(2a+1)p+2b}{2m+1}
is divisible by
p
, except when

2m=cp+d
with both
0\leq c\leq 2a+1
and
0\leq d\leq 2b-1
.

We distinguish whether
c
and
d
are both even or both odd.
(There are no other cases since
2m=cp+d
is even.)

If both
c
and
d
are even, then, provided
c>d
, we may use the
induction hypothesis (
4.3
) to infer that

u(m)=u\left(\frac{c}{2}p+\frac{d}{2}\right)
is divisible by
p
. On the other hand, if
c\leq d
,
then we have

n-m=\left(a-\tfrac{c}{2}\right)p+\tfrac{p-1}{2}+b-\tfrac{d}{2}.

Since, by assumption, we have
a\geq b
, we have

a-\tfrac{c}{2}\geq b-\tfrac{d}{2}\geq 1.

Consequently, again using (
4.1
), we
obtain that
\Pi_{3}(n-m)
is divisible by
p
.

Now let both
c
and
d
be odd. We may again rewrite

m=\frac{c}{2}p+\frac{d}{2}
as

m=\tfrac{c-1}{2}p+\tfrac{p-1}{2}+\tfrac{d+1}{2}.

Furthermore, we
may write

n-m=\left(a-\tfrac{c-1}{2}\right)p+b-\tfrac{d+1}{2}.

Hence, according to the induction hypothesis (
4.4
),
we have
u(m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
) if
c>d
.
Otherwise we have

a-\tfrac{c-1}{2}>b-\tfrac{d+1}{2}\geq 0.

Hence,
\Pi_{3}(n-m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
) due to (
4.1
).

Finally, the congruence for
u\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)

in the case where
p\equiv 3
(mod
4
) holds because, as is seen by
inspection, the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.2
),
the product
\Pi_{1}(n)=\Pi_{1}(\frac{p-1}{2})
, is divisible
by
p^{2}
(here, the condition
p\equiv 3
(mod
4
) enters crucially).
∎

Proposition 8
.

Let
(u(n))_{n\geq 0}
be defined by the recurrence (
2.2
).
Then, given a prime
p\equiv 3
(mod
4
) and a positive integer
e,

the number
u(n)
is divisible by
p^{2}
for
n\geq\left\lfloor\frac{p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
.

Proof.

Let
n\geq\left\lfloor\frac{p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor=\frac{p^{2}-1}{2}
.
As in the proof of Lemma
7
, we use again induction on
n
.
Also here, the induction will be based on (
2.2
), and it
proceeds by showing that
each summand on the right-hand side is divisible by
p^{2}
.

For the start of the induction, we consider
n=\frac{p^{2}-1}{2}
,
so that
2n+1=p^{2}
. In that case, there are two carries when
adding
2m+1
and
2(n-m)
for
0\leq m\leq n-1
, except when
both of
2m+1
or
2(n-m)
are divisible by
p
. In the former case,
Lemma
4
implies that
the binomial coefficient
\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}
is divisible by
p^{2}
.
In the latter case, there is only one carry and so the binomial
coefficient
\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}
is only divisible by
p
.
In its turn, we see that
u(m)
is divisible by
p

due to (
4.4
) with
b=0
and the last assertion in
Lemma
7
.
In either case,
each summand on the right-hand side
of (
2.2
) is divisible by
p^{2}
.
Furthermore, it is again clear that the first term on the right-hand side,
the product
\Pi_{1}(n)
, is
divisible by
p
for all
n\geq\left\lfloor\frac{p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor

because of (
4.1
).

We will assume
n\geq\frac{p^{2}+1}{2}
from now on.

We may restrict our attention to
n\leq p^{2}
because otherwise
either
m\geq\frac{p^{2}+1}{2}
or

n-m\geq\frac{p^{2}+1}{2}
; whence, either

u(m)\equiv 0
(mod
p^{2}
)
or
\Pi_{3}(n-m)\equiv 0
(mod
p^{2}
). Consequently, in this case
each term on the
right-hand side of (
2.2
) would be divisible by
p^{2}
.

To summarise the discussion so far: we may write
2n
as

2n=p^{2}+ap+b
for some
a
and
b
not of the same parity
and with
0\leq a,b\leq p-1
.

If there are two carries when adding
2m+1
and
2(n-m)
, then
by Lemma
4

the binomial coefficient
\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}
is divisible by
p^{2}
.
Hence the corresponding summand on the right-hand side
of (
2.2
) is divisible by
p^{2}
.

Now let us assume that there is only one carry when adding
2m+1
and

2(n-m)
. As earlier, if either
m
or
n-m
are larger
than
\frac{p^{2}}{2}
, then
u(m)
or
\Pi_{3}(n-m)
are divisible
by
p^{2}
, and thus as well the corresponding summand on the right-hand
side of (
2.2
). We may therefore assume without loss of
generality that
2m=cp+d
with
c\leq\frac{p-1}{2}
. We may
furthermore assume that
d\leq b
since, otherwise, there would be
two carries when adding
2m+1
and
2(n-m)
.

We distinguish again whether
c
and
d
are both even or both odd.

Let first
c
and
d
be even. We write
m=\frac{c}{2}p+\frac{d}{2}
.
If
c>d
, then by (
4.3
),
we infer
u(m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
). If
c\leq d
, then we may write

n-m=\left(\tfrac{p+a-c}{2}\right)p+\tfrac{b-d}{2}=\left(\tfrac{p+a-c-1}{2}\right)p+\tfrac{p-1}{2}+\tfrac{b-d+1}{2}

and, depending on the parities of

and

, use the alternative
which has an integer coefficient in front of

.
By our assumptions, we have

\tfrac{p+a-c}{2}>\tfrac{b+a-c}{2}\geq\tfrac{b-c}{2}\geq\tfrac{b-d}{2}\geq 0.

If
a=0
then

must be odd, implying that the last inequality is
strict so that
\tfrac{p+a-c-1}{2}=\tfrac{p-c-1}{2}
is also positive.
By (

), regardless whether

or not, this implies that

\Pi_{3}(n-m)\equiv 0
(mod

).
In total, in both cases this shows that the
corresponding summand in (

) is divisible by
p^{2}
.

Now let
c
and
d
be odd.
Here we write
m=\frac{c-1}{2}p+\frac{p-1}{2}+\frac{d+1}{2}
.
If
c>d
, then by (
4.4
),
we infer
u(m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
). If
c\leq d
, then we may again write

n-m=\left(\tfrac{p+a-c}{2}\right)p+\tfrac{b-d}{2}=\left(\tfrac{p+a-c-1}{2}\right)p+\tfrac{b-d+1}{2}.

Arguing as before, we conclude that

\Pi_{3}(n-m)\equiv 0
(mod
p
).
This shows again that the
corresponding summand in (

) is divisible by
p^{2}
.

If there is no carry when adding
2m+1
and

, then
necessarily one of
2m+1
or

is at least

.
So, again, one of

or
\Pi_{3}(n-m)
is divisible by
p^{2}

due to the induction hypothesis, respectively due to (

).

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

The following theorem proves Conjecture 18(1) in
[
13
]
for

.

Theorem 9
.

Let
(u(n))_{n\geq 0}
be defined by the recurrence (
2.2
).
Then, given a prime
p\equiv 3
(mod
4
) and an integer
e\geq 2,

the number
u(n)
is divisible by
p^{e}
for
n\geq\left\lfloor\frac{(e-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
.

Proof.

We proceed by a double induction on
e

and
n
, the outer induction being on
e
.
For the start of the induction, we use Proposition
8

which proves the assertion of the theorem for
e=2
.
From now on let
e\geq 3
.

We assume that
\left\lfloor\frac{(e-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor\leq n<\left\lfloor\frac{ep^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
.
We claim that
the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.2
),
namely
\Pi_{1}(n)
, is always
divisible by
p^{e}
under this assumption. Indeed,
by (
4.1
) we have

v_{p}\left(\Pi_{1}(n)\right)\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{n}{p}\right\rfloor\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{1}{p}\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor\right\rfloor\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-1)p}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2p}\right\rfloor\geq 2\left(\tfrac{3(e-1)}{2}-1\right)\geq e

(4.5)

for
e\geq 3
. The conclusion
v_{p}\left(\Pi_{1}(n)\right)\geq e
holds
however as well for
e=2
as shown by a modification of the final
part of the estimation:

v_{p}\left(\Pi_{1}(n)\right)\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{n}{p}\right\rfloor\geq\dots\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{p}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2p}\right\rfloor=2\left(\tfrac{p}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\geq 2.

(4.6)

Next we consider the summand on the right-hand side
of (
2.2
) for
\left\lfloor\frac{(f-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor\leq m<\left\lfloor\frac{fp^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
with
2\leq f\leq e-2
.
By the induction hypothesis applied to
u(m)
, we have
v_{p}(u(m))\geq f
.
Furthermore we have

n-m>\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\tfrac{fp^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-f-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor.

Therefore, by replacing
n
by
n-m
and
e
by
e-f

in (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), we get

v_{p}\left(\Pi_{3}(n-m)\right)\geq e-f

(4.7)

since
e-f\geq 2
.
We infer that
v_{p}\big(u(m)\Pi_{3}(n-m)\big)\geq f+(e-f)=e
, and hence the
corresponding summand in (
2.2
) is divisible by
p^{e}
.

Let now
0\leq m<\left\lfloor\tfrac{p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
.
In that case, the previous argument yields that

n-m>\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-2)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
, with the consequence that we only
have
v_{p}(\Pi_{3}(n-m))\geq e-1
.
It may be that actually
n-m\geq\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
.
Then the estimation (
4.7
) implies that

v_{p}(\Pi_{3}(n-m))\geq e
, so that
the corresponding summand in (
2.2
) is divisible by
p^{e}
.
On the other hand, if

\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-2)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor\leq n-m<\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
,
then we may write

2n=(e-1)p^{2}+ap+b
and
2(n-m)=(e-2)p^{2}+cp+d
for some

with
0\leq a,b,c,d\leq p-1
. Since

,
there is (at least) one carry
when adding

and
2m+1

4
,
the binomial coefficient

is divisible by

.
Together with
the previously observed fact that
v_{p}(\Pi_{3}(n-m))\geq e-1
this shows that the
corresponding summand in (

) is divisible by
p^{e}
.

Finally, let

\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-2)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor\leq m<\left\lfloor\tfrac{(e-1)p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor
.
The induction hypothesis (in
n
) implies that
in this case we have
v_{p}(u(m))\geq e-1
.
We write

2n+1=(e-1)p^{2}+ap+b
and
2m+1=(e-2)p^{2}+cp+d
for some
a,b,c,d

with
0\leq a,b,c,d\leq p-1
.
If
n-m\geq\left\lfloor\frac{p^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor

we obtain

If, on the other hand, we have

and

in their

Therefore, by Lemma

, the binomial coefficient

\binom{2n+1}{2m+1}
is divisible by
p
.
In either case, together with
the previously observed fact that
v_{p}(u(m))\geq e-1
this shows that the
corresponding summand in (
2.2
) is divisible by
p^{e}
.

This concludes the induction step, and, thus, the proof of the theorem.
∎

Theorem 11
.

Let
(u(n))_{n\geq 0}
be defined by the recurrence (

).
Then, given a prime
p\equiv 1
(mod

) and a positive integer

for

.
Moreover, the number
u\big(\frac{p-1}{2}\big)
is a quadratic
residue modulo

Proof.

We begin with the second assertion.
Setting
n=\frac{p-1}{2}
in (
2.2
)/(
2.4
), we obtain

u\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)=\Pi_{1}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)-\sum_{m=0}^{(p-3)/2}\binom{p}{2m+1}\Pi_{3}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)u(m).

Due to the range of the sum on the right-hand side, the binomial
coefficient is always divisible by
p
. Therefore, we have

u\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)\equiv\Pi_{1}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)\pmod{p}.

(4.8)

The assertion now follows by observing that
\Pi_{1}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)
is a square not divisible by
p

For the proof of the first assertion,
we proceed again by a double induction on

and
n

e
.
The theorem is trivial for

of the induction.

We divide the proof into subtasks. At many places, the argument

from its

\left\lceil\frac{ep}{2}\right\rceil
.

Task 1: The first term on the left-hand side of (

for even

By (

as desired, since

is even.

Task 2: The assertion holds for
n\geq\left\lceil\frac{(e+1)p}{2}\right\rceil
.

Under this assumption we have

v_{p}\big(\Pi_{1}(n)\big)\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{n}{p}\right\rfloor\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{\left\lceil\frac{(e+1)p}{2}\right\rceil}{p}\right\rfloor\geq 2\left\lfloor\tfrac{e+1}{2}\right\rfloor\geq e,

which shows that the first term on the right-hand side of
(
2.4
) vanishes modulo
p^{e}
.

Next we consider the summand on the right-hand side of
(
2.4
). For
m\geq\left\lceil\frac{ep}{2}\right\rceil
, the term
u(m)
is
divisible by
p^{e}
by the inductive hypothesis (with respect to
n
),
hence the corresponding summand vanishes modulo
p^{e}
.
Now let
\left\lceil\frac{(f-1)p}{2}\right\rceil\leq m<\left\lceil\frac{fp}{2}\right\rceil
with
1\leq f\leq e
.
Our conditions imply that

n-m\geq\left\lceil\frac{(e+1)p}{2}\right\rceil-\left\lceil\frac{fp}{2}\right\rceil+1\geq\left\lceil\frac{(e+1-f)p}{2}\right\rceil.

Therefore, by using (

which shows that also in this case the corresponding summand
vanishes modulo
p^{e}
.

From now on we assume that
n<\left\lceil\frac{(e+1)p}{2}\right\rceil
or, more precisely,
integrating the overall assumption, that
\left\lceil\frac{ep}{2}\right\rceil\leq n<\left\lceil\frac{(e+1)p}{2}\right\rceil
.

Task 3:
p
-adic analysis of the summand in (

) for

\left\lceil\frac{ep}{2}\right\rceil\leq n<\left\lceil\frac{(e+1)p}{2}\right\rceil
.

As before, by the induction hypothesis of the induction on

, the term

in the sum in (

) is divisible

for

Next we consider the summand on the right-hand side of

with

.
Therefore, by using (

is odd and

.

of

and

(this condition is essential for the first line below), these are

\displaystyle(2m+1)_{p}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(f-1)_{p}\ *

\displaystyle\big(2n-2m\big)_{p}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(e-f)_{p}\ *

In order to summarise our findings so far:

(1)

By Task 2 the

(2)

Task 4:

By Item (3) above,

By the induction hypothesis, we know that

(4.11)

Furthermore, by (

(4.12)

since

.

.
Since the assumptions of the case in which we are imply

Thus, we see that the congruence (

.

\displaystyle\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{n-\frac{ep-1}{2}}(4j-3)^{2}\Bigg)\Pi_{1}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}+\alpha_{0}p\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{n-\frac{ep-1}{2}}(4j+2p+4\alpha_{0}p-3)^{2}\Bigg)\Pi_{1}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}+\alpha_{0}p\right)

Lemma 15

\times 2^{n-k}\frac{(2n+a2^{e})!\,c_{1}!}{(2n)!\,2^{a2^{e-1}}(c_{1}+a2^{e-1})!}\frac{(2n)!}{2!^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\,4!^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots n!^{c_{n}}c_{n}!}\prod_{i=1}^{n}{x^{c_{i}}(i)}\quad(\text{mod }2^{e}).

We have

Since, again by elementary number theory,

we may conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{(2n+a2^{e})!\,c_{1}!}{(2n)!\,2^{a2^{e-1}}(c_{1}+a2^{e-1})!}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{(n+a2^{e-1})!\,c_{1}!}{n!\,(c_{1}+a2^{e-1})!}\quad(\text{mod }2^{e})

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{i=c_{1}+1}^{n}\frac{i+a2^{e-1}}{i}\quad(\text{mod }2^{e})

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{i=c_{1}+1}^{n}\frac{(i\cdot 2^{-v_{2}(i)})+a2^{e-v_{2}(i)-1}}{i\cdot 2^{-v_{2}(i)}}\quad(\text{mod }2^{e}).

If this is substituted back in (

S(n+a2^{e-1},k+a2^{e-1},(\tilde{c}_{i}))\\[2.84526pt]
\equiv(-1)^{k-1}\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(2j-1)\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{i=c_{1}+1}^{n}\frac{(i\cdot 2^{-v_{2}(i)})+a2^{e-v_{2}(i)-1}}{i\cdot 2^{-v_{2}(i)}}\Bigg)\\[2.84526pt]
\cdot 2^{n-k}\frac{(2n)!}{2!^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\,4!^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots n!^{c_{n}}c_{n}!}\prod_{i=1}^{n}{x^{c_{i}}(i)}\quad(\text{mod }2^{e}).

(8.35)

in the denominator are odd numbers.

At this point, we would like to simplify the terms

For, assuming the validity of this simplification, the first product

on the right-hand side of (

We claim that this simplification is indeed allowed. For, by Lemma

know that the second line in (

) is an integer which
is divisible by

Hence, instead of computing modulo

Proof of (

Theorem 32
.

Let

\chi(\mathcal{A})=0

In this section, we prove periodicity of the matrix entries

(given by Proposition

)
modulo powers of 2, when considered as a sequence

. Moreover, this result comes again with a precise

Our starting point is a modified version of the

),

). Also here, the main result of this

,

, with

.

Theorem 33
.

Let

j=0,1,2,\dots,
be integers with

.
Furthermore, define coefficients

by

R^{-1}_{y}(n,k)=2^{(n-k)/2}\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!}\left\langle t^{-k}\right\rangle U^{-n}_{y}(t),

(9.1)

where

U_{y}(t):=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{y(j)}{(2j+1)!}t^{2j+1}.

Here again,
the case
k=0
has to be interpreted as
R^{-1}_{y}(0,0)=1
and

R^{-1}_{y}(n,0)=0
for
n\geq 1
.
Then, for fixed
n
and any
2
-power
2^{e},

the sequence
\big(R^{-1}_{y}(k+2n,k)\big)_{k\geq 0}
is purely periodic modulo

with (not necessarily minimal) period length
2^{e}
.

Proof.

By the definition (
9.1
) and the computation
in (
6.1
) with

replaced by
y
,
n
replaced by
n+k
,
and
k
replaced by

, in this order, we obtain

\cdot\frac{(2n+m)!}{3!^{c_{3}}c_{3}!\,5!^{c_{5}}c_{5}!\cdots(2n+1)!^{c_{2n+1}}c_{2n+1}!}\prod_{i=1}^{2n+1}{y^{c_{i}}\big(\tfrac{i-1}{2}\big)},

(9.2)

with
\mathcal{P}^{o}_{N,K}
having been defined below (
6.1
).
Here, we observe that
\mathcal{P}^{o}_{2n+m,m}
is empty for

.
Indeed, for
(c_{i})\in\mathcal{P}^{o}_{2n+m,m}
with
c_{1}=0
, we have

3m=3\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}c_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}ic_{i}=2n+m,

(9.3)

and hence

. Consequently, we may restrict the sum over
m
in
(

) to

.

We want to consider
R^{-1}_{y}(2n+k,k)
for fixed

as a function
in
k
. Inspection of the last expression in (

) reveals
that, since the sum over

is finite,

is a
polynomial in

with rational coefficients. Hence, it is certainly

(and, more generally, modulo any modulus

).
In order to find a bound on the period length, we must perform a

-adic analysis of the expression in (

).

We may rewrite (

) as follows:

\displaystyle R^{-1}_{y}(2n+k,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}\underset{c_{1}=0}{\sum_{(c_{i})\in\mathcal{P}^{o}_{2n+m,m}}}(-1)^{m}2^{n}\frac{(2n+m+k-1)(2n+m+k-2)\cdots k}{(2n)!}

\displaystyle\kern 85.35826pt\cdot\frac{(2n)!}{2!^{c_{3}}c_{3}!\,4!^{c_{5}}c_{5}!\cdots(2n)!^{c_{2n+1}}c_{2n+1}!}\prod_{i=1}^{2n+1}\left(\frac{y\big(\tfrac{i-1}{2}\big)}{i}\right)^{c_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}\underset{c_{1}=0}{\sum_{(c_{i})\in\mathcal{P}^{o}_{2n+m,m}}}(-1)^{m}\frac{(2n+m+k-1)(2n+m+k-2)\cdots k}{n!\,(2n-1)!!}

\displaystyle\kern 71.13188pt\cdot\frac{(2n)!}{2!^{c_{3}}c_{3}!\,4!^{c_{5}}c_{5}!\cdots(2n)!^{c_{2n+1}}c_{2n+1}!}\prod_{i=1}^{2n+1}\left(\frac{y\big(\tfrac{i-1}{2}\big)}{i}\right)^{c_{i}}.

(9.4)

We want to consider (
9.4
) modulo
2^{e}
, and our goal is
to show that, modulo
2^{e}
, this expression is periodic with period
length
2^{e}
as a (polynomial) function in
k
.

Now, the first term in the last line of (

) is an
integer due to the fact that

\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}(2i)c_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}(2i+1)c_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}c_{i}=(2n+m)-m=2n,

(c_{i})

replaced by

The product over

in the last line of (

) is a

for all even

. Thus,
the last line of (
9.4
) represents a rational number with

-adic valuation.
Likewise, the term

in the denominator of the expression in

We substitute

\displaystyle\kern 14.22636pt=\binom{2n+m+k+2^{e}-1}{n}{(n+m+k+2^{e}-1)(n+m+k+2^{e}-2)\cdots(k+2^{e})}

\displaystyle\kern 14.22636pt\equiv\binom{2n+m+k+2^{e}-1}{n}{(n+m+k-1)(n+m+k-2)\cdots k}\pmod{2^{e}}
