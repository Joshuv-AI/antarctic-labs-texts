On nonhomogeneous elliptic equations with


Abstract
In this paper, we present some suitable distributional identities of solutions for nonhomogeneous elliptic equations involving the Hardy-Leray potentials and study qualitative properties of the solutions to the corresponding nonhomogeneous problems in this distributional sense. We address some applications on the nonexistence of some nonhomogeneous problems with the Hardy-Leray potentials and the nonexistence principle eigenvalue with some indefinite potentials.

Introduction

Throughout this paper, we assume that
\Omega
is an open domain containing the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
N\geq 2
,
\mu\geq\mu_{0}:=-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}:=-\Delta+\frac{\mu}{|x|^{2}}.
The elliptic operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}

involves the inverse square potential, which is also called as Hardy potential, and related semilinear elliptic problems have been done mainly by variational methods
[
14
,
19
,
20
,
24
]
,
due to the Hardy type inequalities, see the references
[
1
,
10
,
12
,
26
]
.
A direct extension of definition for the distributional solution to the Hardy problem
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u=g\ {\rm in}\ \Omega

may be proposed as

\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}_{\mu}\xi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}g\xi\,dx,\quad\forall\,\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega),

(1.1)

where
g
is a nonlinearity of
x
and
u
. When
N\geq 3
and
\mu\in[\mu_{0},0)
,
[
7
,
20
]
make use of this notation of distributional solution to
show the existence of distributional solutions of (
1.1
) for particular nonlinearity depending on
u
under some restriction for
\mu
.
Later on, the authors in
[
22
]
considered the related Hardy problem in the distributional sense of (
1.1
) replaced
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)

by
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega\setminus\{0\})
in some range of
\mu
.
Chaudhuri and Cîrstea in
[
16
]
, Cîrstea in
[
18
]
classified the isolated singular classical solution of
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}u+b(x)h(u)=0
in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
, where
both
b
and
h
consist of regularly varying and slowly varying parts (see their definitions in §1.2.2 of
[
18
]
). There a solution is considered as a
C^{1}(\Omega\setminus\{0\})
-solution in the sense of distributions in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
, that is,

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\varphi dx-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\lambda}{|x|^{2}}u\varphi dx+\int_{\Omega}b(x)h(u)\varphi dx=0,\quad\forall\,\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega\setminus\{0\})

(1.2)

holds. Recall that a solution
u
has a ”removable” singularity at the origin if
u
can be extended to a
C^{1}
-solution in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
. Another important
attempt is done in
[
20
]
to consider the consider the solutions of semilinear Hardy equation in classical sense, in variational sense and in a distributional sense.
This subject has been extended into the elliptic equation with Hardy
potential with singularities on the boundary, for instance,
[
4
,
23
,
27
,
37
,
36
,
31
]
and the references therein.

However, we observe that the distributional identity (
1.2
) loses the information at the origin in the case that the test functions are in
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega\setminus\{0\})
,
and on the other hand, it does not work even for the fundamental solutions by the test functions in
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
if
\mu>0
large enough when we consider the distributional identity (
1.1
), because of high singularity at the origin of the corresponding fundamental solutions. Furthermore,
it loses the huge convenience in dealing with semi-linear elliptic equations involving the Laplacian from the expression of singularities by Dirac mass, for instances
[
9
,
34
,
39
]
, where
the authors obtained classical solutions of
-\Delta u=f
with precise isolated singularities by dealing with
-\Delta u=f+k\delta_{0}
in models:
f
being nonhomogeneous term,
f(u)=u^{p}
or
f(u)=u^{p}-u
.

Therefore, it is important for theoretical advances and also for application’s interests to obtain an improved version of distributional idnetity (
1.1
). To this end, we start our analysis form the fundamental solutions of Hardy operators. It is known that when
\mu\geq\mu_{0}
, the problem

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u=0\quad{\rm in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}

(1.3)

has two branches of radially symmetric solutions with the explicit formulas that

\Phi_{\mu}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}|x|^{\tau_{-}(\mu)}&{\rm if}\quad\mu>\mu_{0},\\[2.84526pt]
\phantom{}-|x|^{\tau_{-}(\mu)}\ln|x|&{\rm if}\quad\mu=\mu_{0}\end{array}\right.\quad\quad{\rm and}\quad\Gamma_{\mu}(x)=|x|^{\tau_{+}(\mu)},

(1.4)

where

\tau_{-}(\mu)=-\frac{N-2}{2}-\sqrt{\mu-\mu_{0}}\;\;\;{\rm and}\;\;\;\tau_{+}(\mu)=-\frac{N-2}{2}+\sqrt{\mu-\mu_{0}}.

Here
\tau_{-}(\mu)
and
\tau_{+}(\mu)
are two roots of
\mu-\tau(\tau+N-2)=0
. Moreover,
\Phi_{\mu}
is a regular solution of
(
1.3
) in the sense that
\mu|\cdot|^{-2}\Phi_{\mu}(\cdot)\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and we have that

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}\Phi_{\mu}=0\quad{\rm in}\quad\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

(1.5)

Remark that the mentioned fundamental solution of (
1.3
) with
\mu=0
is

\Phi_{0}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}|x|^{2-N}&{\rm if}\quad N\geq 3,\\[2.84526pt]
\phantom{}-\ln|x|&{\rm if}\quad N=2\end{array}\right.

and
\Phi_{0}
is a distributional solution of

\mathcal{L}_{0}u=c_{N}\delta_{0}\quad\rm{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N},

(1.6)

where

c_{N}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}(N-2)|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|&{\rm if}\quad N\geq 3,\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{}2\pi&{\rm if}\quad N=2\end{array}\right.

and
\mathcal{S}^{N-1}
is the sphere of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|
is the area of the unit sphere. We observe that the second branch of
fundamental solution
\Gamma_{0}\equiv 1
for
\mu=0
, which is always omitted in the study of semilinear elliptic equation. Without special stating, we always assume that
\mu\geq\mu_{0}
.

Our motivation of this paper is to provide suitable distributional identities for Hardy problems and to answer the basic question whether it is able to express the isolated singularities by the Dirac mass, in particular, to find out some distributional identity to distinguish the fundamental solutions of
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
.

Our first result on the distributional identities of the fundamental solutions can be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1

Let
d\mu(x)=\Gamma_{\mu}(x)dx
and

\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}=-\Delta-\frac{2\tau_{+}(\mu)}{|x|^{2}}\,x\cdot\nabla.

(1.7)

Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Phi_{\mu}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}(\xi)\,d\mu=c_{\mu}\xi(0),\quad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1.1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),

(1.8)

where

c_{\mu}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}2\sqrt{\mu-\mu_{0}}\,|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|&{\rm if}\quad\mu>\mu_{0},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{}|\mathcal{S}^{N-1}|&{\rm if}\quad\mu=\mu_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(1.9)

Here and in what follows, we always take the notation

d\mu(x)=\Gamma_{\mu}(x)dx\quad{(\rm\,or}\ d\mu=\Gamma_{\mu}dx).

The distributional identity (
1.8
) is derived from the observation that
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\Gamma_{\mu}\xi)=\Gamma_{\mu}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}(\xi)
.
In the
d\mu
-distributional sense,
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}
is the duality operator of
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
and we may say that the fundamental solution
\Phi_{\mu}
is a
d\mu-
distributional solution of

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}\Phi_{\mu}=c_{\mu}\delta_{0}\quad\rm{in}\quad\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),

which coincides (
1.6
) when
\mu=0
, since
d\mu(x)=dx
in this case and
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{*}=\mathcal{L}_{0}=-\Delta
. Furthermore, an alternative form of
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}

could be given as

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}(\xi)=-\Gamma_{\mu}^{-1}{\rm div}(\Gamma_{\mu}\nabla\xi),

which plays an essential role in the study of heat kernel for the Hardy operator, see
[
25
,
35
]
. We remark that
when
\mu=0
,
c_{\mu}
coincides with the coefficient
c_{N}
, while for
\mu=\mu_{0}
there is a jump for the parameter
c_{\mu}
.

It is known that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
with
\mu\in[\mu_{0},+\infty)\setminus\{0\}
, is self-adjoint in the normal distributional sense. However, identity (
1.8
) gives a new distributional sense with respect to a specific weighted measure,
which makes the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
not self-adjoint.
To the best of our knowledge, identity (
1.8
) is new.

We next continue to extend this identity for bounded domains and apply it for the classification of
isolated singularities of nonhomogeneous Hardy problem. Given a bounded
C^{2}
domain
\Omega
containing the origin, the Hardy problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u=0\qquad{\rm in}\quad{\Omega}\setminus\{0\},\\[2.84526pt]
\phantom{L_{\mu}\,}\displaystyle u=0\qquad{\rm on}\quad\partial{\Omega},\\[2.84526pt]
\phantom{}\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}u(x)\Phi_{\mu}^{-1}(x)=1\end{array}\right.

(1.10)

has solution
G_{\mu}
with isolated singularity as
\Phi_{\mu}
at origin, i.e.

\lim_{|x|\to 0}\frac{G_{\mu}(x)}{\Phi_{\mu}(x)}=1.

(1.11)

Then we have the following distributional identity.

Theorem 1.2

Let
G_{\mu}
be the solution of (
1.10
) verifying (
1.11
), then

\int_{\Omega}G_{\mu}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}(\xi)\,d\mu=c_{\mu}\xi(0),\quad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1.1}_{0}(\Omega).

(1.12)

A deeper knowledge of distributional identities allows us to draw a complete picture of the existence, non-existence and the singularities
for the nonhomogeneous problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u=f\qquad{\rm in}\quad{\Omega}\setminus\{0\},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{L_{\mu}}\displaystyle u=0\qquad{\rm on}\quad\partial{\Omega}\textsterling\textlnot\end{array}\right.

(1.13)

where
f:\bar{\Omega}\setminus\{0\}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a Hölder continuous locally in
\bar{\Omega}\setminus\{0\}
.

Motivated by (
1.8
) and (
1.12
), we shall classify the isolated singularities of (
1.13
) by building the connection with

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u=f+c_{\mu}k\delta_{0}&{\rm in}\quad{\Omega},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{L_{\mu}}\displaystyle u=0&{\rm on}\quad\partial{\Omega},\end{array}\right.

(1.14)

in the
d\mu
-distributional sense that
u\in L^{1}(\Omega,d\mu)
,

\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}(\xi)\,d\mu=\int_{\Omega}f\xi\,d\mu+c_{\mu}k\xi(0),\quad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1.1}_{0}(\Omega),

(1.15)

where

.
Note that the solution
G_{\mu}
of (
1.10
) verifying (
1.11
), from Theorem
1.2
, is a
d\mu
-distributional solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u=c_{\mu}\delta_{0}&{\rm in}\quad{\Omega},\\[2.84526pt]
\phantom{L_{\mu}}\displaystyle u=0&{\rm on}\quad\partial{\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

For nonhomogeneous problem (
1.13
), we have the following results.

Theorem 1.3

Let
f
be a function in
C^{\gamma}_{loc}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{0\})
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

(i)
Assume that

f\in L^{1}(\Omega,d\mu)\qquad{\rm i.\,e.}\quad\int_{\Omega}|f|\,d\mu<+\infty,

then for any

, problem (

then we have the asymptotic behavior

\lim_{x\to 0}u_{k}(x)\Phi_{\mu}^{-1}(x)=k.

(1.18)

(ii)
Assume that

is a weak solution of (

) for some
k\geq 0
.

(iii)
Assume that

and

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{r}(0)}f\,d\mu=+\infty,

(1.19)

then problem (
1.13
) has no nonnegative solutions.

Note that Theorem

(i)

build a one to one connection between problems (

1.14

Assumption (

It worth noting that our weak solution

Preliminary

2.1
Comparison Principle for
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
and weak singularity

In this subsection, we introduce the Comparison Principle for the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
which is one basic tool in our analysis.

Lemma 2.1

Let
O
be a bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
L:O\times[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
be a continuous function such that for any
x\in O
,

L(x,s_{1})\geq L(x,s_{2})\quad{\rm if}\quad s_{1}\geq s_{2},

then
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}+L
with
\mu\geq\mu_{0}
verifies the Comparison Principle,
that is, if

u,\,v\in C^{1,1}(O)\cap C(\bar{O})

satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u+L(x,u)\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mu}v+L(x,v)\quad{\rm in}\quad O\qquad{\rm and}\qquad u\geq v\quad{\rm on}\quad\partial O,

then

u\geq v\quad{\rm in}\quad O.

Proof.
Let
w=u-v
and
w_{-}=\min\{w,0\}
, then
w\geq 0
on
\partial O
by the assumption that
u\geq v
on
\partial O
and then

w_{-}=0
on
\partial O
.
We will prove that
w_{-}\equiv 0
.
If
O_{-}:=\{x\in O:\,w(x)<0\}
is not empty, then it is a bounded

C^{1,1}
domain in
O
. From Hardy inequality with
\mu\geq\mu_{0}
, for some
C>0
, there holds,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{O_{-}}(-\Delta w_{-}+\frac{\mu}{|x|^{2}}w_{-})w_{-}dx+\int_{O_{-}}[L(x,u)-L(x,v)](u-v)_{-}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{O_{-}}\left(|\nabla w_{-}|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{|x|^{2}}w_{-}^{2}\right)dx\geq C\int_{O_{-}}w_{-}^{2}dx,

then
w_{-}=0
in a.e.
O_{-}
, which is impossible with the definition of
O_{-}
.
\Box

We remark that when
L(s)=s^{p}
, Lemma
2.1
could be seen in
[
16
, Lemma 2.1]
.
The following lemma plays an important role in the obtention of uniqueness for classical solution.

Lemma 2.2

Assume that
\Omega
is a bounded
C^{2}
domain and
L
is a continuous function stated in Lemma
2.1
and satisfying

L(x,0)=0,\;\forall x\in\Omega
, then the homogeneous problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u+L(x,u)=0\qquad{\rm in}\quad{\Omega}\setminus\{0\},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{L_{\mu}+L(x,u)}\displaystyle u=0\qquad{\rm on}\quad\partial{\Omega},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{}\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}u(x)\Phi_{\mu}^{-1}(x)=0\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

has only zero solution.

Proof.
Let
u
be a solution of (
2.1
), then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
r_{\epsilon}>0
converging to zero as
\epsilon\to 0
such that

u\leq\epsilon\Phi_{\mu}\quad{\rm in}\quad\overline{B_{r_{\epsilon}}(0)}\setminus\{0\}.

We see that

u=0<\epsilon\Phi_{\mu}\quad{\rm on}\quad\partial\Omega,

then by Lemma
2.1
, we have that

u\leq\epsilon\Phi_{\mu}\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega\setminus\{0\}.

By the arbitrary of
\epsilon
, we have that
u\leq 0
in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}.

The same way to obtain that
u\geq 0
in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}.

This ends the proof.
\Box

Now let

\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}
be defined by (
1.7
).
As a consequence of the above result, an important test function we use often in the

distributional sense is the solution
\xi_{0}
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}u=1\qquad{\rm in}\quad\Omega,\\[5.69054pt]
\phantom{\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}}\displaystyle u=0\qquad{\rm on}\quad\partial{\Omega}\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

satisfies

0<\xi_{0}\leq c\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega.

(2.3)

In fact, without loss of the generality, let

and denote

, then

So

domain, there exists

Next, we build the distributional identity for the classical solution of the nonhomogeneous problem with ”removable” singularity, i.e.

Lemma 2.3

Assume that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}u=f\qquad{\rm in}\quad{\Omega}\setminus\{0\},\\[2.84526pt]

\phantom{}\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}u(x)\Phi_{\mu}^{-1}(x)=0\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

\int_{\Omega}u_{f}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}(\xi)\,d\mu=\int_{\Omega}f\xi\,d\mu,\quad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1.1}_{0}(\Omega).

Proof.

The case

.

4.3

In this subsection, we are devoted to classify the isolated singular solutions of (

)
in the distributional sense. When
\mu=0

Proposition 4.2

Assume that
f
is a function in
C^{\gamma}_{loc}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{0\})
satisfying (
1.16
) and

u
is a nonnegative solution of (
1.13
).
Then there exists some
k\geq 0
such that
u
is a the
d\mu
-distributional solution of (
1.15
).

Proof.
Let

\bar{u}(r)=|\mathcal{S}^{N}|^{-1}r^{1-N}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}u(x)d\omega(x).

For
r\in(0,r_{0})
, we have that

-\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{N-1}{r}\bar{u}^{\prime}(r)+\frac{\mu}{r^{2}}\bar{u}(r)\geq\bar{f}(r),

where

\bar{f}(r)=r^{1-N}\int_{\partial B_{r}(0)}f(x)d\omega(x).

Denote

\bar{u}(r)=r^{\tau_{+}(\mu)}v(r),

then

-v^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{N+2\tau_{+}(\mu)-1}{r}v^{\prime}(r)\geq r^{-\tau_{+}(\mu)}\bar{f},

where
N+2\tau_{+}(\mu)
plays the dimensional role. By (
1.16
), we have that

\int_{0}^{r_{0}}r^{-\tau_{+}(\mu)}\bar{f}(r)r^{N+2\tau_{+}(\mu)-1}dr=\int_{B_{r_{0}}(0)}|x|^{-\tau_{+}(\mu)}|f(x)|dx<+\infty,

where
r_{0}>0
such that
B_{r_{0}}(0)\subset\Omega
.
Following the step 1 in the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
9
]
, there exists
c>0
such that

v(r)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}c|x|^{2-(N+2\tau_{+}(\mu))}&{\rm if}\quad N+2\tau_{+}(\mu)\geq 3,\\[2.84526pt]
\phantom{}-c\ln|x|&{\rm if}\quad N+2\tau_{+}(\mu)=2,\end{array}\right.

that is,
\bar{u}\leq c\Phi_{\mu}
.
So for
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, it is well-defined that

\displaystyle|\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}(\xi)\,d\mu|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\xi\|_{C^{2}}\int_{B_{r_{0}}(0)}u(x)|x|^{-1}\Gamma_{\mu}(x)dx+c

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\|\xi\|_{C^{2}}\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\bar{u}(r)r^{-1+\tau_{+}(\mu)}r^{N-1}dr+c

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\|\xi\|_{C^{2}}\int_{0}^{r_{0}}r^{\tau_{-}(\mu)+\tau_{+}(\mu)+N-2}dr+c

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle+\infty.

We observe that for
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega\setminus\{0\})
, it follows by Divergence theorem that

\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}(\xi)\Gamma_{\mu}dx-\int_{\Omega}f\,\xi\Gamma_{\mu}dx=0.

By Schwartz Theorem (
[
40
, Theorem XXXV]
), there exists a multiple index
p
,

u\Gamma_{\mu}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}-f\Gamma_{\mu}=\sum_{|a|=0}^{p}k_{a}D^{a}\delta_{0},

i.e. for any
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)

\int_{\Omega}u(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}\xi-f\xi)\,d\mu=\sum_{|a|=0}^{|p|}k_{a}D^{a}\xi(0).

(4.10)

We are left to show that
k_{a}=0
for
|a|\geq 1
.
For multiple index
\bar{a}\not=0
, taking
\xi_{\bar{a}}(x)=x_{i}^{\bar{a}_{i}}\eta_{n_{0}}
and denoting
\xi_{\bar{a},\varepsilon}(x)=\xi_{\bar{a}}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
, we have that
\xi_{\bar{a}}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
and then for
\varepsilon\in(0,\,\frac{1}{2})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}\xi_{\bar{a},\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(-\Delta)\xi_{\bar{a}}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\cdot\nabla\xi_{\bar{a}}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}),

and on the one side,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}(\xi_{\bar{a},\varepsilon})d\mu\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B_{2\varepsilon}(0)}u_{\mu}\Gamma_{\mu}(-\Delta)\xi_{\bar{a}}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})dx-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{B_{2\varepsilon}(0)}u_{\mu}\Gamma_{\mu}\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\cdot\nabla\xi_{\bar{a}}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B_{2\varepsilon}(0)}u_{\mu}\Gamma_{\mu}dx+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{B_{2\varepsilon}(0)}\frac{u_{\mu}\Gamma_{\mu}}{|x|}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}c&{\rm if}\quad N\geq 3,\\[2.84526pt]
\phantom{}-c\ln\varepsilon&{\rm if}\quad N=2,\end{array}\right.

where
c
is independent of
\varepsilon
. Moreover, we have that

\left|\int_{\Omega}u_{\mu}\xi_{\bar{a},\varepsilon}d\mu\right|\leq\|\xi_{\bar{a}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{B_{2\varepsilon}(0)}f_{\mu}\Gamma_{\mu}dx\to 0\quad{\rm as}\ \ \varepsilon\to 0^{+}.

On the other side,

\sum_{|a|=0}^{|p|}k_{a}D^{a}\xi_{\bar{a},\varepsilon}(0)=\frac{k_{\bar{a}}}{\varepsilon^{|{\bar{a}}|}}|{\bar{a}!}|,

where

|{\bar{a}}|=\sum\bar{a}_{i}\quad{\rm and}\quad\bar{a}!=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\bar{a}_{i})!\geq 1.

So if
k_{\bar{a}}\not=0
, we have that

\left|\sum_{|a|=0}^{|p|}k_{a}D^{a}\xi_{\bar{a},\varepsilon}(0)\right|\to+\infty\quad{\rm as}\ \ \varepsilon\to 0^{+},

that is, the right hand of (
4.10
) with
\xi=\xi_{\bar{a},\varepsilon}
blows up with the rate
\varepsilon^{-|\bar{a}|}
,
while the left hand of (
4.10
) keeps bounded for
N\geq 3
or blows up in
N=2
, but controlled by
-\ln\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.
This is a contradiction and
so
k_{a}=0
for
|a|\geq 1
.

Therefore, we have that

\int_{\Omega}(u_{\mu}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mu}\xi-f\xi)\,d\mu=k_{0}\xi(0),\quad\forall\,\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

(4.12)

For
\xi\in C^{1.1}_{0}(\Omega)
, take a sequence of functions in
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
converging to
\xi
, then the identity (
4.12
) holds
for any
\xi\in C^{1.1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
\Box

5.1
d\mu
-distributional solution

In this subsection, we prove existence of
d\mu
-distributional solution of (

Proposition 5.1

Assume that
\Omega
is a smooth bounded domain containing the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\rho(x):={\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
and
f
is a measurable function satisfying

\int_{\Omega}|f|\rho\,d\mu<+\infty.

Then for any
k\in\mathbb{R}

1.14

d\mu
-distributional solution.

Proof.
By the linearity of
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
, we find out the solutions
u_{+}
and
u_{-}

1.14

f_{-}+k_{-}\delta_{0}

a_{\pm}=\max\{\pm a,0\}

is the solution of (
1.14
).
So we assume now that
f
is nonnegative and
k\geq 0
. We observe that there exists a increasing nonnegative sequence

f_{n}\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
such that

f_{n}\to f\quad{\rm as}\quad n\to\infty\quad{\rm in}\ L^{1}(\Omega,\,\rho d\mu).

From Theorem
1.3
, we have that problem (
1.13
), subject to
\lim_{x\to 0}u(x)\Phi_{\mu}^{-1}(x)=k
, has a unique solution of

u_{n}
satisfying

\int_{\Omega}u_{n}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}(\xi)\,d\mu=\int_{\Omega}f_{n}\xi\,d\mu+c_{\mu}k\xi(0).

(5.1)

By the Comparison Principle, we have that

0\leq u_{n}\leq u_{n+1}\quad\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega\setminus\{0\}.

Now take
\xi=\xi_{0}
, the solution of (
2.2
), we get that

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega,\,d\mu)}\leq\int_{\Omega}|f|\rho\,d\mu+ck,

, we have that

\int_{\Omega}u_{n}\,|x|^{-1}d\mu\leq c\int_{\Omega}|f|\rho\,d\mu.

Since

by Lebesgue’s monotone convergence theorem, we have that

\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{*}(\xi)\,d\mu=\int_{\Omega}f_{n}\xi\,d\mu+c_{\mu}k\xi(0),\qquad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1.1}_{0}(\Omega).

.
This ends the proof.

5.3
Nonexistence of the principle eigenvalue

In this subsection, our aim is to study the nonexistence of principle eigenvalue of the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=\lambda Vu\qquad{\rm in}\quad{\mathcal{O}},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{}\displaystyle u\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O}),\end{array}\right.

(5.4)

where
\mathcal{O}
is an smooth open set containing
\{0\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
V
is a measurable function.

It was proved in
[
42
]
(Theorem 2.5 ) that problem (
5.4
) admits a simple principle eigenvalue under the following
hypotheses
(V_{1})
and
(V_{2})
:

(V_{1})

V\in L^{p}_{loc}(\mathcal{O}\setminus\{0\})
with
p>\frac{N}{2}
;

(V_{2})

Let

V^{+}:=\max\{0,V\}\not=0,\quad V^{+}=V_{1}+V_{2},

(5.5)

where
V_{1}\in L^{\frac{N}{2}}(\mathcal{O})
and for any
y\in\bar{\mathcal{O}},

\lim_{x\in\mathcal{O},\,x\to y}|x-y|^{2}V_{2}(x)=0,\quad\lim_{x\in\mathcal{O},\,x\to\infty}|x|^{2}V_{2}(x)=0\ ({\rm if}\ \mathcal{O}\ {\makebox{ is unbounded}}).

More references on the principle eigenvalue with indefinite potential also could be seen in
[
13
,
15
,
41
,
46
]
.

Our aim here is to the nonexistence of the principle eigenvalue if
V
is the Hardy-Leray potential in bounded domain. We will see
that the assumptions on
V
above seem to be ”optimal” in some sense.
More precisely, our result can be stated as follows:

Theorem 5.1

Assume that
\mathcal{O}=\Omega
is a bounded
C^{2}
domain containing the origin, the potential
V\in C^{\gamma}_{loc}(\bar{\Omega}\setminus\{0\})
verifies

V(x)\geq\frac{a_{0}}{|x|^{2}},\quad\forall\,x\in\Omega\setminus\{0\}

for some constants
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
a_{0}>0
.
Then problem (
5.4
) has no principle eigenvalue.

Proof.
By contradiction, we assume that problem (
5.4
) has a principle eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
, and denote by
\varphi_{1}

the nonnegative corresponding eigenfunction.

Since
V
is nonnegative and
V\in C^{\gamma}(\bar{\Omega}\setminus\{0\})
, it is standard to prove that
\lambda_{1}>0
and
\varphi_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is a nonnegative classical solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=\lambda_{1}Vu&{\rm in}\quad{\Omega}\setminus\{0\},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{-\Delta}\displaystyle u=0&{\rm on}\quad\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(5.6)

So
\varphi_{1}
a super solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=\lambda_{1}a_{0}\frac{u}{|x|^{2}}&{\rm in}\quad{\Omega}\setminus\{0\},\\[4.2679pt]
\phantom{-\Delta}\displaystyle u=0&{\rm on}\quad\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(5.7)

Let
\psi_{1}
be the first positive eigenfunction of
-\Delta u=\lambda u
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega\setminus B_{r}(0))
with
r>0
small.
Then there exists
\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that
\epsilon\psi_{1}
is a sub solution of (
5.7
). By the super and sub solutions methods,
problem (
5.7
) has a nonnegative nontrivial solution
v_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

In the case that
\lambda_{1}a_{0}\in(0,-\mu_{0}]
, it follows by Theorem
1.3

(iii)
with
f=0
, we have that

v_{1}=kG_{-\lambda_{1}a_{0}}
for some
k\geq 0
. If
k=0
,
v_{0}=0
is trivial, which is impossible.
If

In the case that
