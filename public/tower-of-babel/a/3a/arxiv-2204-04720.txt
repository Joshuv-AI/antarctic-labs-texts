The crossover from the Macroscopic Fluctuation Theory to the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang equation controls the large deviations beyond Einstein’s diffusion
Alexandre Krajenbrink, Pierre Le Doussal

Abstract
We study the crossover from the macroscopic fluctuation theory (MFT) which describes 1D stochastic diffusive systems at late times , to the weak noise theory (WNT) which describes the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation at early times . We focus on the example of the diffusion in a time-dependent random field, observed in an atypical direction which induces an asymmetry. The crossover is described by a non-linear system which interpolates between the derivative and the standard non-linear Schrodinger equations in imaginary time. We solve this system using the inverse scattering method for mixed-time boundary conditions introduced by us to solve the WNT. We obtain the rate function which describes the large deviations of the sample-to-sample fluctuations of the cumulative distribution of the tracer position. It exhibits a crossover as the asymmetry is varied, recovering both MFT and KPZ limits. We sketch how it is consistent with extracting the asymptotics of a Fredholm determinant formula, recently derived for sticky Brownian motions. The crossover mechanism studied here should generalize to a larger class of models described by the MFT. Our results apply to study extremal diffusion beyond Einstein’s theory.



Introduction
For one-dimensional stochastic systems with a diffusive scaling at large time, such as the symmetric exclusion process (SEP),
the macroscopic fluctuation theory (MFT)

provides a powerful framework to describe
the large deviations of the density and current

Upon introduction of an asymmetry or driving, such as in the asymmetric
exclusion process (ASEP)

and the large scale behavior of the model is usually described by the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class

A paradigmatic member of this class is the KPZ equation

asymmetry

using the so-called weak noise theory (WNT)

;
Baruch
;

of large-deviation rate functions to solving saddle point partial non-linear differential equations, not always an easy task.
A natural question is to understand, in presence of a small but relevant asymmetry, the nature of the crossover
from the MFT to the WNT. We can expect that is should be somewhat subtle since the MFT describes the large deviations at large time, while the WNT describes the large deviations for the KPZ equation at short time.

Recently, exact solutions of the WNT equations were obtained by us

Extremal diffusions.

Consider the rightmost of
N
independent particles in the same random field, of position

Y_{N}(T)=\max_{i=1,\dots,N}Y_{i}(T)
. Without random field and for

a "thermal" fluctuation part
\simeq G\sqrt{\frac{T}{\log N}}

for

\mathcal{O}(1)

with a Tracy-Widom distribution

In the more accessible regime

;
GBPLDModerate

the KPZ equation. These phenomena go beyond the Gaussian nature of
Einstein’s diffusion. They allow for a detectable fingerprint of the random medium.
Recently, these two regimes have been observed numerically

The present results allow to study yet another regime,
\log N\ll\sqrt{T}
,
where diffusive scaling holds and the scaled position

of the maximum
converges to

where for typical environments

random variable with a computable variance

computed here and

Conclusion.

We have elucidated here in great details the crossover upon adding an asymmetry, between the MFT for diffusive systems
and the WNT of the KPZ equation. We have focused on the example of the diffusion of a tracer in a time-dependent random medium
in an atypical direction and a "droplet" type initial condition. We have obtained the large-deviation functions
in the context of classical integrability using simple, standard and versatile inverse scattering methods.
For this model it was based on the integrable crossover between the DNLS and NLS equations.
Obtaining the complete solution of this interpolating system (
11
)
beyond the large-deviation observable requires further efforts involving the use of Fredholm determinants
similarly to what we have achieved in
UsWNT2021
;
UsWNTFlat2021
for the complete solution of the WNT.
This is one open question that we leave to subsequent works
UsInterpolatingNext
, together
with other outstanding questions, such as investigating the MFT-KPZ crossover for more general
models, or within the present model, to study (
2
) for other initial conditions,
in particular those identified in
TTPLD
to converge in atypical directions to solutions of the
KPZ equation for flat and stationary geometries
UsWNTFlat2021
.

VI
Analytic continuation in the case of the WNT for the KPZ equation

Before discussing the intricacies of the analytic continuations for the present problem, we
recall here how it works in the case of the WNT for the KPZ equation. It is necessary to do so since
we show below that at large
\xi
the results for KPZ equation are recovered. We present further details
than given in Ref.
UsWNT2021
as they will be very useful below. Indeed the situation in the present paper is already
quite similar to the one for the KPZ equation
where
\Psi_{\rm KPZ}(\tilde{z})
admits a second branch for
-1\leqslant\tilde{z}<0
.

For the KPZ equation one first obtains for
\tilde{z}\in[0,+\infty)

(S113)

which can be continued for
\tilde{z}\in[-1,+\infty)

The polylogarithm function
\mathrm{Li}_{5/2}(-\tilde{z})
is analytic in the complex
\tilde{z}
plane except on
a branch cut for
\tilde{z}\in(-\infty,-1]
. Across this branch cut it has a jump, which leads to

\Psi_{\rm KPZ,0}(\tilde{z}+\mathbf{i}0^{+})-\Psi_{\rm KPZ,0}(\tilde{z}-\mathbf{i}0^{+})=\Delta_{\rm KPZ}(\tilde{z})\quad,\quad\Delta_{\rm KPZ}(\tilde{z})=\frac{4}{3}\mathbf{i}(\log(-\tilde{z}))^{3/2}

(S114)

for
z\in(-\infty,-1]
.

Analogy with the logarithm.
This situation is analogous to the study of the logarithm which admits different determination in the complex plane. Indeed, along the negative real axis, the logarithm has a jump of value
2\mathbf{i}\pi
. A better understanding of the logarithm is done by considering its domain of definition not in the complex plane but rather on a Riemann surface, see Fig.
S2
, where it does not have any jump. In the case of the logarithm, the Riemann surface is composed of different sheets joined by winding around the origin and the correct definition of the logarithm on the
n
-th sheet is
\log z+2\mathbf{i}\pi n
where
\log z
is the principal determination or main branch.

Figure S2:
Plot of the Riemann surface of the logarithm

. This surface is composed of different sheets continuously connected is a staircase manner.

Pursuing the construction of
\Psi_{\rm KPZ}(\tilde{z})
on a Riemann surface rather than on the complex plane, we extend continuously the definition
\Psi_{\rm KPZ,0}(\tilde{z})
to the first Riemann sheet along the branch cut as follows

\Psi_{\rm KPZ}(\tilde{z})=\begin{cases}\Psi_{\rm KPZ,0}(\tilde{z}),&\Im(\tilde{z})>0\\
\Psi_{\rm KPZ,0}(\tilde{z})+\Delta_{\rm KPZ}(\tilde{z}),&\Im(\tilde{z})<0\\
\end{cases}

On the real axis it is multi-valued, i.e. there is a first branch for

given by the first line, and
a second branch given by the second line. One can now continue these two branches for

\tilde{z}\in]-1,0]

(S116)

(S117)

For

becomes negative hence the branch cut along
the positive imaginary axis intersects the real axis at

This is represented in Fig.

(top right). In the case however, i.e. for

, it is always possible (i.e. for any

)
to deform the integration contour of

away from the real axis to pass
below the branch cut (as represented on the Figure). We call this new contour

\begin{split}z\Psi^{\prime}(z)&=\int_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{2\pi}\frac{\log(1-z(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})e^{-q^{2}-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}})}{(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})^{2}}\\
&=z\Psi^{\prime}_{0}(z)+z\Delta_{1}^{\prime}(z)\quad,\quad z\Psi^{\prime}_{0}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{2\pi}\frac{\log(1-z(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})e^{-q^{2}-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}})}{(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})^{2}}\end{split}

(S129)

In the second line we have split the integral into an integral over the real axis which passes right through the branch cut, and a contribution denoted
z\Delta_{1}^{\prime}(z)
which represents the contribution of a contour around the branch cut (taking into account the
2\mathbf{i}\pi
discontinuity of the logarithm), which reads

with

is by definition the integral over

\mathbb{R}
computed "naively", that is with a jump of the integrand when the argument of the logarithm crosses the

\mathbf{i}q\to-\mathbf{i}q
ensures that the result is real
(in other words one can e.g. replace
\int_{\mathbb{R}}=2{\Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}
) and not worry about the branch cut.

We can repeat the same procedure for the formula for
\Psi(z)
itself in
(
S128
), the branch cut of
the
{\rm Li}_{2}
function being identical to the one of the logarithm with however a different value of the jump

{\rm Li}_{2}(t+\mathbf{i}0^{+})-{\rm Li}_{2}(t-\mathbf{i}0^{+})=-2\mathbf{i}\pi\log t

\begin{split}\Psi(z)&=-\int_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})e^{-q^{2}-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}})}{(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})^{2}}\\

\displaystyle=\hat{\Delta}(p_{1}(z,\xi))\quad,\quad\hat{\Delta}(p)=\frac{1}{\xi}\left[-(\xi^{2}+2)(\log(\xi)-\log(\xi+2p))+2p(p-\xi)-\frac{4p}{\xi+2p}\right]

which will be useful below.

and write

\begin{split}\Delta_{1}(z)&=-\int_{z}^{z_{c}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}{z^{\prime}}\frac{4p_{1}(z^{\prime})}{\xi(2p_{1}(z^{\prime})+\xi)}=\int_{0}^{p_{1}}\mathrm{d}p\frac{2p}{\xi}\frac{1+2p(p+\frac{\xi}{2})}{(p+\frac{\xi}{2})^{2}}\end{split}

.

Next, as discussed in a previous subsection, see Fig.

, there is an interval of values of

, where there are three real zeroes

For

something nasty happens, the upper and lower branch cuts meet and form a cross

(bottom right). The same happened
for the KPZ equation, as discussed in the previous section.
In that case, it is not possible anymore to deform the integration
contour to avoid these branch cuts. We can now use the "jump" function
\Delta_{1}(z)
obtained in the previous section
to propose the proper analytical continuations and the ensuing new branches.

When

reaches
z_{c1}
the zeroes

and
p_{2}
annihilate (corresponding to the merging

the only remaining zero is

.

where
\hat{\Delta}(p)
was defined in (

). This branch appears indeed
in the Table

. However since

it is not a continuous extension
of
\Psi_{0}(z)+\Delta_{1}(z)
. This means that there are other branches that will allow a continuous extension. As we now discuss, they will be constructed
by first decreasing
z
from
+\infty
down to a turning point, increasing it up to a second turning point, and finally decreasing it
again down to
-\infty
.

We will thus consider the point
z=z_{c1}
as the first turning point and start to follow the second zero

. One then proposes the continuous extension
defined for

\displaystyle\Psi(z)=\Psi_{0}(z)+\Delta_{2}(z)\quad,\quad\Delta_{2}(z)=\hat{\Delta}(p_{2}(z))

(S138)

It is a new branch and since
\Psi_{0}(z)+\Delta_{1}(z)
also exists in the same interval

the function
\Psi(z)
is multi-valued in that interval.

When
z
reaches
z_{c2}
the zeroes

and

annihilate (corresponding to the disappearance of the lower branch-cut as seen in Fig.

(top right)). We then again consider

. The candidate for the next continuous extension is therefore

which is precisely the one in (

).

This procedure is sufficient for

and leads the third column in the Table

. Using the two turning points
one thus obtains a continuous extension, which is multi-valued in the interval
]z_{c1},z_{c2}[
.

In the case
\xi>\xi_{2}
this however is insufficient.
Indeed, there is a last feature of the branch cuts to take into account. When

increases from

to

and then decreases from

to

it can cross the value

. This is the case when

. When

Conversely, crossing

shown

\begin{split}A(v,\zeta)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}r\frac{u}{u+e^{-r}}e^{-(\zeta-v)r}\,\frac{g(v)}{g(\zeta)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}r\sigma(r)A_{1}(v,r)A_{2}(r,\zeta)=(A_{1}\sigma A_{2})(v,\zeta)\end{split}

(S196)

where the kernels
A_{1},A_{2}
and the function
\sigma
read

A_{1}(v,r)=g(v)e^{vr},\quad A_{2}(r,\zeta)=\frac{e^{-\zeta r}}{g(\zeta)},\quad\sigma(r)=\frac{u}{u+e^{-r}}.

(S197)

Hence using Sylvester’s identity

\begin{split}\mathrm{Det}(I-K_{u})|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(C)}&=\mathrm{Det}(I-A\tilde{A})\\
&=\mathrm{Det}(I-A_{1}\sigma A_{2}\tilde{A})\\
&=\mathrm{Det}(I-\sigma A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\end{split}

(S198)

This last Fredholm determinant has the typical structure for which the first cumulant method, developed in
KrajLedou2018
;
ProlhacKrajenbrink
;
krajenbrink2019beyond
to study the relevant asymptotics (here large
T
), applies. Defining a determinantal point process
\{a_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
associated to the kernel
A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1}
, the following identity holds

\begin{split}\mathrm{Det}(I-\sigma A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})&=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1-\sigma(a_{\ell}))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}e^{-\varphi(a_{\ell})}\right]\end{split}

(S199)

where
e^{-\varphi}=1-\sigma
. The first cumulant approximation asserts
(
KrajLedou2018
, Section 6)
that as some parameter goes to infinity (here it will be
T
, see below),
we expect the point process to self-average, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}e^{-\varphi(a_{\ell})}\right]\sim e^{-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(a)]}=e^{-{{\rm Tr}}(\varphi A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})}

(S200)

If the first cumulant method works, we aim to have under the right scaling

\mathrm{Det}(I-K_{u})|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(C)}\sim\exp\left[-{{\rm Tr}}(\varphi A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})\right]

(S201)

The explicit expression of the kernel
A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1}
is obtained as

(A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})(r,r^{\prime})=\int_{1/2+2{\sf R}+\mathbf{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\int_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\frac{g(v^{\prime})}{g(\zeta)}\frac{1}{\zeta-v^{\prime}}e^{r^{\prime}v^{\prime}-r\zeta}

(S202)

taking into account that the measure on the variables
v
is
\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}
. Using that

\varphi(r)=\log(1+ue^{r})=-\mathrm{Li}_{1}(-ue^{r})

(S203)

to apply the first cumulant method we need to calculate the following quantity which only involves the diagonal part of the kernel
A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1}

{{\rm Tr}}(\varphi A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}r\int_{1/2+2{\sf R}+\mathbf{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\int_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\mathrm{Li}_{1}(-ue^{r})\frac{g(v^{\prime})}{g(\zeta)}\frac{1}{\zeta-v^{\prime}}e^{r(v^{\prime}-\zeta)}

(S204)

We recall that
\Re(\zeta-v^{\prime})>0
by construction. We further proceed to an integration by part with respect to
r
to obtain

{{\rm Tr}}(\varphi A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}r\int_{1/2+2{\sf R}+\mathbf{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\int_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-ue^{r})\frac{g(v^{\prime})e^{rv^{\prime}}}{g(\zeta)e^{r\zeta}}

(S205)

The boundary terms of the integration by part are zero since
e^{r(1+v^{\prime}-\zeta)}\to 0
for
r\to-\infty
and the polylogarithms behave as
{\rm Li}_{s}(e^{r})\sim r^{s}
at
r\to+\infty
.

At this stage we proceed to the large-time rescaling to the diffusive regime using the rescaled variables

\{Y=\xi\sqrt{T}\quad,\quad v=w\sqrt{T}\quad,\quad\zeta=\omega\sqrt{T}\quad,\quad u=z\sqrt{T}\}

(S206)

We then rewrite (
S204
) as

{{\rm Tr}}(\varphi A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-ue^{r})I(r)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-ze^{r})I(r-\log\sqrt{T})

(S207)

where we have shifted the variable
r
by
-\log\sqrt{T}
, and defined

\begin{split}I(r-\log\sqrt{T})=&\int_{1/2+2{\sf R}+\mathbf{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\int_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\frac{g(v^{\prime})e^{(r-\log(\sqrt{T})))v^{\prime}}}{g(\zeta)e^{(r-\log(\sqrt{T}))\zeta}}\\
&=\int_{1/2+2{\sf R}+\mathbf{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}e^{-(\log g(\zeta)+(r-\log\sqrt{T})\zeta)}\int_{C}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}e^{(\log g(v)+(r-\log\sqrt{T})v)}\end{split}

(S208)

The large-time expansion of the function
g(v)
given in Eq. (
S192
) reads

\displaystyle\log g(v)=aY\psi_{0}(v)+bT\psi_{1}(v)+\log\Gamma(v)

(S209)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{T}\left(\phi(w)+(w+a\xi)\log\sqrt{T}\right)+\chi(w)-\frac{1}{2}\log(\sqrt{T})+o(T)

where we defined

\phi(w)=\frac{b}{w}-w+(w+a\xi)\log(w)\quad,\quad\chi(w)=\frac{b}{2w^{2}}-\frac{a\xi}{2w}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi w)

(S210)

At this stage we will choose the radius of the circle
C
conveniently to be equal to
{\sf R}=T/Y
so that its mapping under the large-
T
limit is a circle
C^{\prime}
of radius
1/\xi
centered at
1/\xi
(we assume here and below that
\xi>0
).
Upon the change of variable (
S206
) in the large
T
limit, inserting (
S209
) into (
S208
)
and noting that constant terms cancel from the two integrals we obtain

\begin{split}I(r-\log\sqrt{T})&\simeq T\int_{2/\xi+0^{+}+\mathbf{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}e^{-\sqrt{T}(\phi(\omega)+r\omega)-\chi(\omega)}\int_{C^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}e^{\sqrt{T}(\phi(w)+rw)+\chi(w)}\end{split}

(S211)

In the large-
T
limit these integrals are dominated by saddle points. The saddle point equations read

\phi^{\prime}(w)=-\frac{1}{w^{2}}+\frac{\xi}{w}+\log(w)=-r

(S212)

and the same for
\omega
. Since
w
is on the circle
C^{\prime}
we can parameterize it in the following way

\frac{1}{w}=-\mathbf{i}q+\frac{\xi}{2}\quad,\quad q\in\mathbb{R}

(S213)

The saddle point equation becomes

e^{r}=(-\mathbf{i}q+\frac{\xi}{2})e^{-q^{2}-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}}

(S214)

which is very reminiscent of Eq. (
37
). To make this saddle point easily attainable, one way is to deform the integration contour of
r

which is not
\mathbb{R}
anymore but the image of (
S214
) as
q
varies on the real axis, which we call
\gamma
. We will assume that this is possible.
This is a closed curve for
e^{r}
, touching the real axis at values
e^{r}=0
and
e^{r}=\frac{\xi}{2}e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}}
. The solution of (
S213
) and (
S214
) defines a function
w(r)
so that the saddle point evaluation of (
S211
) gives

I(r-\log\sqrt{T})\simeq-\frac{\sqrt{T}}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(w(r))}

(S215)

where we have also assumed that the integration contour of
\omega
could be deformed to be folded around
C^{\prime}
.
This ensures that the dominant exponential at the saddle point cancel.

To summarize, the first cumulant (
S207
) of the Fredholm determinant reads in the large

-limit

{{\rm Tr}}(\varphi A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})=\frac{\sqrt{T}}{2\mathbf{i}\pi}\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-ze^{r})\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(w(r))}

(S216)

We will now perform the change of variable (
S214
). Using the saddle point equation
\phi^{\prime}(w(r))=-r
we obtain upon derivation
the Jacobian of this change of variable

\phi^{\prime\prime}(w(r))\frac{\mathrm{d}w(r)}{\mathrm{d}q}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}r}=-1,\qquad\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(w(r))}\mathrm{d}r=-\mathbf{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})^{2}}

(S217)

Inserting into (
S216
) we finally obtain

\Psi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}{{\rm Tr}}(\varphi A_{2}\tilde{A}A_{1})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})e^{-q^{2}-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}})}{(\mathbf{i}q-\frac{\xi}{2})^{2}}\\

(S218)

which is in agreement with Eq. (
32
) in the text.

XI
Extension to the extremal diffusion beyond Einstein’s diffusion theory

In this section we study the position of the maximum of
N
walkers in the same random field
(by sample below we mean one given environment, i.e. random field).
Previous works started with Ref.
BarraquandCorwinBeta
;
BarraquandThesis
which studied the Beta random walk
and pointed out that for
N\gg 1
, and in the regime
\log N\sim T
, the position
of the maximum has sample to sample fluctuations given by the Tracy-Widom distribution.
Another regime,
\log N\sim\sqrt{T}
, was obtained in
TTPLD
and
GBPLDModerate
where these
fluctuations are described by the solution of the KPZ equation at finite time. Numerical simulations
which confirm these regimes have been performed recently
CorwinPrivate
.
Extending these arguments, our present work allows to study another regime,
\log N\ll\sqrt{T}
,
not studied previously.

Consider
N
independent particles in the same environment. One denotes
Y_{N}(T)=\max_{i}Y_{i}(T)
with
i=1,\dots,N

and
Z_{N}(Y,T)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}(T)>Y)
. One has the exact relation

1-Z_{N}(Y,T)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}(T)<Y)=\mathbb{P}(Y(T)<Y)^{N}=(1-Z(Y,T))^{N}

(S219)

We focus below on the diffusive scaling
Y\sim\sqrt{T}
at large
T
, not considered previously in the
discussion of the extremal diffusion. We will thus denote
y_{N}(T)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}Y_{N}(T)
.
There are several observables of interest.

Large deviations of the CDF of the maximum
.
The first observable is
Z_{N}=\mathbb{P}(y_{N}(T)>\xi)
, which is simply the analog of
Z
for the maximum position of
N
particles.
One can ask, for any finite
N
, what are the large deviations of the PDF of
Z_{N}
for
T\gg 1
. From the above simple
relation (
S219
) one finds for

{\cal P}(Z_{N})\sim\exp\left(-\sqrt{T}\hat{\Phi}_{\xi}\left(1-(1-Z_{N})^{1/N}\right)\right)

(S220)

where the rate function
\hat{\Phi}_{\xi}(Z)
is the one obtained in the present work (for
N=1
). Here and below we indicate explicitly the dependence in
\xi
of the rate functions.

Averaged CDF of the maximum
. Another observable is the following average over the environment

\overline{\mathbb{P}(y_{N}(T)<\xi)}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}Z\overline{e^{N\log(1-Z)}}\sim\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}Ze^{N\log(1-Z)-\sqrt{T}\hat{\Phi}_{\xi}(Z)}

(S221)

where in the last equation we have substituted the large deviation form. Note that considering instead of the average moments of order
q
is equivalent to
substitute
n\to nq
.

There are several regimes depending on
N
. If
N\ll\sqrt{T}
then the
second term dominates and implies that
Z\approx Z_{\rm typ}(\xi)
so that

\overline{\mathbb{P}(y_{N}(T)<\xi)}\simeq e^{N\log(1-Z_{\rm typ}(\xi))}

(S222)

and the result is identical as the CDF of the maximum position for
N
particles in the absence of random field.

If
N=n\sqrt{T}\gg 1
with
n=\mathcal{O}(1)
fixed, the two terms can balance each others and one finds
that this observable takes the large deviation form

\overline{\mathbb{P}(y_{N}(T)<\xi)}\sim e^{-\sqrt{T}\Sigma_{\xi}(n)}\quad,\quad\Sigma_{\xi}(n)=\min_{Z}(\hat{\Phi}_{\xi}(Z)-n\log(1-Z))

(S223)

with a rate function obtained from a non trivial variational formula.
Here for a given
\xi
the value of
Z
which realizes the optimum is different from
Z_{\rm typ}(\xi)

and thus involves rare environments. Upon some simple manipulations, recalling that

Z=\Psi^{\prime}(z)
and
\hat{\Phi}^{\prime}(Z)=-z
we obtain

z=\frac{n}{1-Z}
leading to the parametric representation

\Sigma_{\xi}(n)=\Psi_{\xi}(z)-z+n-n\log(\frac{n}{z})\quad,\quad z(1-\Psi_{\xi}^{\prime}(z))=n

(S224)

Note that the approximation
N\log(1-Z)\simeq-NZ
valid for
Z=\Psi_{\xi}^{\prime}(z)\ll 1

would instead lead to
z\simeq n
and
\Sigma_{\xi}(n)\simeq\Psi_{\xi}(z)
. Although we leave this study to the future, it is
quite likely that a phase transition similar to the one of
\Psi(z)
for
\xi>\xi_{1}
for
and for some values of
z
should also occur here.
For
n\to 0
one has
n\simeq z(1-\Psi_{\xi}^{\prime}(0))=z(1-Z_{\rm typ}(\xi))
and one recovers (
S222
).
More precisely one has the expansion

\Sigma_{\xi}(n)=-n\log(1-\Psi_{\xi}^{\prime}(0))+\frac{\Psi_{\xi}^{\prime\prime}(0)n^{2}}{2(1-\Psi_{\xi}^{\prime}(0))^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(n^{3})\quad,\quad\Psi_{\xi}^{\prime}(0)=Z_{\rm typ}(\xi)

(S225)

Position of the maximum: typical behavior
. One can ask about the position of the maximum and its fluctuations. Let us
introduce
N
i.i.d exponential random variables
g_{i}
of PDF
P(g)=e^{-g}\Theta(g)
, and
call
G_{N}=\max_{i}g_{i}-\log N
. At large
N
,
G_{N}\to G
a Gumbel random variable
with
\mathbb{P}(G<g)=e^{-e^{-g}}
. For any
N
one has
\mathbb{P}(G<g)=(1-\frac{1}{N}e^{-g})^{N}
.
In a given environment one can write

\mathbb{P}(Y_{N}(T)<Y)=e^{N\log(1-Z(Y,T))}=\overline{\Theta(G_{N}+\log N+H(Y,T)<0)}^{G_{N}}

(S226)

This formula is valid for any
N
and for large
N
one obtains the same formula with
G_{N}\to G

by approximating
e^{N\log(1-Z)}\simeq e^{-NZ}
. Note that
G_{N}
and
G
in this formula
are independent of
H(Y,T)
. As discussed below, the approximation
Z\ll 1

is also realized for any
N
with large probability when
\xi=Y/\sqrt{T}
is large.
The random position of the maximum
Y_{N}(T)
, in a given environment is then given by

G_{N}+\log N+H(Y,T)<0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad Y_{N}(T)<Y

(S227)

Note that
G_{N}+\log N
is a positive random
variable. Since
Z(Y,T)
and thus
H(Y,T)
is a positive decreasing function of
Y
in any sample,
one may argue (by taking a derivative w.r.t.
Y
in (
S226
)) that (
S227
) is equivalent to

G_{N}+\log N+H(Y_{N}(T),T)=0

(S228)

This formula generalizes
(
GBPLDModerate
, Eq. (50))
to any
N
.

Until now this is exact. Let us again consider the diffusive scaling regime
Y\sim\sqrt{T}
at large
T
.
In a typical environment, one has
H(Y,T)\simeq H_{\rm typ}(\xi)
where

\xi=Y/\sqrt{T}
and
H_{\rm typ}(\xi)=\log\left(\int_{\xi}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-x^{2}/4}}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\right)=\log(\frac{1}{2}{\rm Erfc}(\frac{\xi}{2}))=-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}-\log(\sqrt{\pi}\xi)+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-1})
.
Note that
H_{\rm typ}(\xi)
varies from
0
for
\xi\to-\infty
to

-\infty
for
\xi\to+\infty
. Let us denote
y_{N}^{\rm typ}
the scaled position of the maximum in a typical environment.
At large
T
it reaches a finite limit in distribution such that

G_{N}+\log N+H_{\rm typ}(y_{N}^{\rm typ})=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad y_{N}^{\rm typ}=H_{\rm typ}^{-1}(-G_{N}-\log N)

(S229)

where
H_{\rm typ}^{-1}(h)=\xi
is the reciprocal function of
H_{\rm typ}(\xi)=h
. This is correct
for any
N
.
The distribution of
y_{N}^{\rm typ}
is exactly the same as the one for the maximum of
N
Brownian motions
at time
t=1
, performing each diffusion
\mathrm{d}B_{i}(t)^{2}=2\mathrm{d}t
, started at
B_{i}(0)=0
at
t=0
,
i.e. for the problem without the quenched random field. For
N\gg 1
, using the asymptotics
of
H_{\rm typ}(\xi)
one finds the standard result

y_{N}^{\rm typ}\simeq 2\sqrt{\log N}+\frac{G-\frac{1}{2}\log(4\pi\log N)}{\sqrt{\log N}}+\dots

(S230)

We can now study the typical fluctuations from sample to sample. To lowest order one should take into
account the typical fluctuations of
H(Y,T)
, which are
\delta H=\mathcal{O}(T^{1/4})
. The variance was obtained in (
S160
)
as
\overline{H^{2}}^{c}=C_{2}(\xi)T^{-1/2}
, where the function
C_{2}(\xi)
was given there. The position of the maximum is now determined by

G_{N}+\log N+H_{\rm typ}(y_{N})+\sqrt{C_{2}(y_{N})}T^{-1/4}\omega=0

(S231)

where
\omega
is a Gaussian random variable of unit variance. Inverting to leading
order at large time we find (an equation valid for any
N
)

\displaystyle y_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{\rm typ}^{-1}\left(-G_{N}-\log N-\sqrt{C_{2}(y_{N}^{\rm typ})}T^{-1/4}\omega\right)+o(T^{-1/4})

(S232)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{N}^{\rm typ}-\frac{\sqrt{C_{2}(y_{N}^{\rm typ})}}{H_{\rm typ}^{\prime}(y_{N}^{\rm typ})}T^{-1/4}\omega+o(T^{-1/4})

(S233)

If
N\gg 1
one finds

y_{N}=2\sqrt{\log N}+\frac{G-\frac{1}{2}\log(4\pi\log N)+\sqrt{C_{2}(y_{N}^{\rm typ})}T^{-1/4}\omega}{\sqrt{\log N}}+\dots

(S234)

where we recall that the
\omega
term represents the sample-to-sample fluctuations and the Gumbel variable

the "thermal" fluctuations, the

We can compare this result with Ref.
(
GBPLDModerate
, Eqs. (57-58))
setting

and
r_{0}=2
there, which were obtained
when
N
and
T
are large with the parameter

kept fixed. The KPZ time there is

. This agrees perfectly with the

from (

). For the matching to

GBPLDModerate

to be perfect we need
the variance of the KPZ height field at very short time (i.e. in the Edward-Wilkinson regime for droplet initial condition)
which is given by
le2016exact

\overline{h(0,T_{\rm KPZ})^{2}}^{c}\simeq C_{2}^{\rm KPZ}T_{\rm KPZ}^{1/2}\quad,\quad C_{2}^{\rm KPZ}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}

(S235)

One then easily checks that it exactly matches the amplitude of the fluctuating term
\sim\omega
in (
S234

using the large

S161
),

To summarize, (
S232
) and (

) extend the results
of

about "typical" extremal diffusion to the diffusive regime

.
In that new regime
\log N\ll\sqrt{T}
, i.e.

Position of the maximum: large deviations
.
Finally, our results yield additional information about the large deviations of extremal diffusion, i.e. for rare environments such that

XII
Extension to general quadratic models in the MFT: diffusion in random medium and the
symmetric simple exclusion process

One definition of the MFT is as the Langevin equation of a diffusive gas with particle density
q(x,t)

SpohnFluct

\partial_{t}q=\partial_{x}[D(q)\partial_{x}q-\sqrt{\sigma(q)}\xi(x,t)]

(S239)

where
\xi(x,t)
is a standard space-time white noise. The model solved in this present paper corresponds to
\sigma(q)=2q^{2}
and
D(q)=1
.
Averages of solutions of (
S239
) over the noise can be obtained from the dynamical action

S[q,p]=\iint\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\,[p\partial_{t}q-\mathcal{H}(q,p)]
with Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(q,p)=-D(q)\partial_{x}q\partial_{x}p+\frac{1}{2}\sigma(q)(\partial_{x}p)^{2}
,
and where
p(x,t)
is the response field. At large time these averages can be
obtained from the solutions to the saddle point equations
\partial_{t}q=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta p}

and
\partial_{t}p=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta q}
, which admit the conservation law

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}(p,q)=0
.

We will focus below on a subclass of models within the MFT called quadratic models and show how the work of this present paper is relevant to solve them.

A
Mapping of quadratic models in the MFT to the coupled DNLS system

Consider here the quadratic MFT models which have a noise variance parameterized as

\sigma(q)=2Aq(B-q)

(S240)

and a diffusion constant
D(q)=1
. This class contains both the SSEP and the present model of diffusion in random medium.
The MFT hydrodynamic equations (i.e. the saddle point equations) read

\begin{split}\partial_{t}q&=\partial_{x}[\partial_{x}q-2Aq(B-q)\partial_{x}p]\\

(S241)

We introduce the generalized derivative Cole-Hopf transform

R(x,t)=A\partial_{x}p(x,t)e^{ABp(x,t)},\quad Q(x,t)=q(x,t)e^{-ABp(x,t)}\,.

(S242)

The variables

-\partial_{t}R&=\partial_{x}^{2}R-2\partial_{x}(QR^{2})\end{split}

(S243)

Gauge transformation between NLS and DNLS and relation with the non-local transformation of

Change of variable of Wadati and Sogo.
Wadati and Sogo proved in 1982

that the non-linear Schrodinger equation and the derivative non-linear Schrodinger equation were gauge equivalent. Indeed, consider the following systems in the conventions of
wadati1983gauge
, firstly the coupled NLS

\begin{split}&\mathbf{i}q_{1t}+q_{1xx}-2r_{1}q_{1}^{2}=0\\
&\mathbf{i}r_{1t}-r_{1xx}+2r_{1}^{2}q_{1}=0\end{split}

(S244)

and secondly the coupled DNLS

\begin{split}&q_{2t}-\mathbf{i}q_{2xx}-(r_{2}q_{2}^{2})_{x}=0\\
&r_{2t}+\mathbf{i}r_{2xx}-(r_{2}^{2}q_{2})_{x}=0\end{split}

(S245)

Wadati and Sogo showed that the following change of variables allows to map the coupled DNLS system to the coupled NLS system.

\begin{split}&q_{1}=\frac{q_{2}}{2}\exp\big(-\mathbf{i}\int_{-\infty}^{x}r_{2}q_{2}\big)\\
&r_{1}=(-\mathbf{i}r_{2x}+r_{2}^{2}q_{2}/2)\exp\big(\mathbf{i}\int_{-\infty}^{x}r_{2}q_{2}\big)\end{split}

(S246)

(S247)

We obtain that (

C
Stationary measure

The stochastic equation (
S239
) admits generically a family of stationary measures. For instance if one fixes the boundary conditions as
q(0)=q(L)=\varrho
, and if the problem is taken on a finite-size interval,
the stationary measure is
DerridaGershenfeld
;
BertiniMFT2009
;
SpohnFluct
;
DerridaMFTReview2007

\mathcal{P}_{\rm eq}(\{q(x)\})\sim e^{-\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}x\left(f(q(x))-f(\varrho)-(q(x)-\varrho)f^{\prime}(\varrho)\right)}

(S251)

where
f^{\prime\prime}(q)=\frac{2D(q)}{\sigma(q)}
. The linear term is determined so that the maximum probability is for
q=\varrho
.

Consider the model of diffusion in a random environment studied here in Eq. (
2
), with a more general amplitude
for the noise. In that case one has
D(q)=1
and
\sigma(q)=2\alpha q^{2}
, hence
f^{\prime\prime}(q)=1/(\alpha q^{2})
.
This leads to
f(q)=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log q+kq+c
, and to the stationary measure

\mathcal{P}_{\rm eq}(\{q(x)\})\propto e^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}x\left(-\log(q(x)/\varrho)+\frac{q-\varrho}{\varrho})\right)}

(S252)

Remark
. The stationary measure (
S252
) is the analog in the continuum of
a discrete measure on a lattice defined as a product of independent Gamma variables at each site, i.e.
\prod_{x}w_{x}
,
with PDF
p(w)\propto w^{\gamma-1}e^{-w}
. Indeed that measure appeared as a stationary measure
in the Beta polymer problem, in a (long time) one point version in

TTPLDBeta
, and for a more general discussion see
BarraquandBetaHalfSpace
.
For the more general quadratic model parametrized as (
S240
), in particular for the SSEP, the corresponding discrete
stationary measures are instead
factorized Bernoulli.

Remark
. For the diffusion model, one has
B=0
in (
S240
). Hence
R=A\partial_{x}p

and
Q=q
satisfy the DNLS system with
\beta=1
. By choosing here
A=-\alpha
one can vary
the exponent

E
Discussion on the quench and annealed initial conditions

The quadratic models of MFT have been investigated through the spectrum of classical integrability in three works and two contexts of initial conditions:

•

Reference
mallick2022exact
considered the SSEP with an initial condition in the annealed class and solved the problem through the mapping to the coupled NLS
\{P,Q\}
system and the use of its scattering theory. The remarkable feature of that work is that the annealed initial condition for the SSEP admits a simple quenched
\delta,\delta
mixed-time boundary conditions interpretation in the coupled NLS
\{P,Q\}
system.

•

The present work as well as Ref.
NaftaliDNLS
considered the diffusion in random media, equivalent to the KMP model, with a quenched initial condition and solved the problem using the scattering theory of the coupled DNLS
\{R,Q\}
system.

At this stage, the observation is that depending on whether the quench or annealed initial condition is considered, a specific integrable model might be more suited to obtain the exact solution of the problem. Since other gauge transformations between integrable models have been proposed in
wadati1983gauge
, it would be interesting to investigate whether mappings to other integrable models would allow to answer new questions.
