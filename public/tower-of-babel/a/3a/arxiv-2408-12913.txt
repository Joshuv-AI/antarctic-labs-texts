Blowups of triangle-free graphs
António Girão, Zach Hunter, Yuval Wigderson

Abstract
A highly influential result of Nikiforov states that if an n n -vertex graph G G contains at least γ ​ n h \gamma n^{h} copies of a fixed h h -vertex graph H H , then G G contains a blowup of H H of order Ω γ , H ​ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega_{\gamma,H}(\log n) . While the dependence on n n is optimal, the correct dependence on γ \gamma is unknown; all known proofs yield bounds that are polynomial in γ \gamma , but the best known upper bound, coming from random graphs, is only logarithmic in γ \gamma . It is a major open problem to narrow this gap. We prove that if H H is triangle-free, then the logarithmic behavior of the upper bound is the truth. That is, under the assumptions above, G G contains a blowup of H H of order Ω H ​ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ ( 1 / γ ) ) \Omega_{H}(\log n/{\log(1/\gamma)}) . This is the first non-trivial instance where the optimal dependence in Nikiforov’s theorem is known. As a consequence, we also prove an upper bound on multicolor Ramsey numbers of blowups of triangle-free graphs, proving that the dependence on the number of colors is polynomial once the blowup is sufficiently large. This shows that, from the perspective of multicolor Ramsey numbers, blowups of fixed triangle-free graphs behave like bipartite graphs.

Given an integer

One interesting feature of

If
n\leq 32/(\rho\varepsilon^{2})
, then we are again done, as we may take
A,B
to both be singletons, namely the endpoints of some edge, which exists since
e(G)\geq\rho\binom{n}{2}>0
. We thus assume henceforth that
(\varepsilon/4)n\geq 1
.
We let
B_{0}\subseteq V
be a uniformly random set of size
\lfloor(\varepsilon/4)n\rfloor
.
We claim that for every
v\in V_{\mathrm{heavy}}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{V\setminus B_{0}}(v)<(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)~\middle|~v\in B_{0}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(4)

Indeed, fix some
v\in V_{\mathrm{heavy}}
, and let
D=d(v)
. The event that
d_{V\setminus B_{0}}(v)<(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)
is precisely the event that among the
D
neighbors of
v
, more than
D-(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)
of them are included in
B_{0}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{V\setminus B_{0}}(v)<(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)~\middle|~v\in B_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert(B_{0}\setminus\{v\})\cap N(v)\rvert>D-(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)~\middle|~v\in B_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\lvert(B_{0}\setminus\{v\})\cap N(v)\rvert\mid v\in B_{0}]}{D-(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)},

by Markov’s inequality.
Conditional on the event
v\in B_{0}
, the distribution of
B_{0}\setminus\{v\}
is that of a uniformly random subset of
V\setminus\{v\}
of size exactly
\lfloor(\varepsilon/4)n\rfloor-1
.
As
\lfloor(\varepsilon/4)n\rfloor-1\leq(\varepsilon/4)(n-1)
, a given vertex of
V\setminus\{v\}
is included in
B_{0}\setminus\{v\}
with probability at most
\varepsilon/4
, and therefore

\mathbb{E}[\lvert(B_{0}\setminus\{v\})\cap N(v)\rvert\mid v\in B_{0}]\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\lvert N(v)\rvert=\frac{\varepsilon D}{4},

by linearity of expectation and the assumption
\lvert N(v)\rvert=D
. Continuing the computation above, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{V\setminus B_{0}}(v)<(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)~\middle|~v\in B_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\lvert(B_{0}\setminus\{v\})\cap N(v)\rvert\mid v\in B_{0}]}{D-(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon D/4}{D-(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)}.

Recall that
D\geq(1-\varepsilon/2)\rho(n-1)
, since
v\in V_{\mathrm{heavy}}
.
This final expression is a decreasing function of
D
, hence it is maximized when
D=(1-\varepsilon/2)\rho(n-1)
. Plugging this in, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{V\setminus B_{0}}(v)<(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)~\middle|~v\in B_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon D/4}{D-(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\varepsilon/4)(1-\varepsilon/2)\rho(n-1)}{(1-\varepsilon/2)\rho(n-1)-(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varepsilon/4)(1-\varepsilon/2)\rho(n-1)}{(\varepsilon/2)\rho(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},

proving (
4
).

Now, define
B
to comprise all vertices
v\in B_{0}\cap V_{\mathrm{heavy}}
with
d_{V\setminus B_{0}}(v)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)
.
From (
4
), we find that

\mathbb{E}[\lvert B\rvert]=\sum_{v\in V_{\mathrm{heavy}}}\mathbb{P}(v\in B_{0})\mathbb{P}(v\in B\mid v\in B_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V_{\mathrm{heavy}}}\mathbb{P}(v\in B_{0})\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{16}\lvert V_{\mathrm{heavy}}\rvert\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\rho}{32}n,

using the fact that each vertex is included in
B_{0}
with probability at least
\varepsilon/8
(since
\lfloor(\varepsilon/4)n\rfloor\geq 1
) and that
\lvert V_{\mathrm{heavy}}\rvert\geq(\varepsilon\rho/2)n

Fix an outcome where
\lvert B\rvert\geq(\varepsilon^{2}/32)\rho n
, and let
A=V\setminus B_{0}
. Note that
\lvert A\rvert=n-\lfloor(\varepsilon/4)n\rfloor\geq(\varepsilon^{2}/32)\rho n
, as claimed. Finally, by the definition of
B
, we have that

d_{A}(y)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho(n-1)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho\lvert A\rvert

for all
y\in B
, where we use the fact that
\lvert A\rvert\leq n-1
.
∎

Although our goal in
Lemma

4.1
is only to find an inflation of
K_{h}
in some color, it will be convenient to inductively maintain a richer structure, which we now define.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
V_{1},\dots,V_{h}
be pairwise disjoint vertex subsets of some graph
G
.
We say
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h})
is a
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-rich inflation of

if

and
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h})
is a
((1-\varepsilon)\rho)^{e(K_{h})}
-inflation of
K_{h}
. If additionally
\lvert V_{i}\rvert\geq n
for all
i
, we say it is a
(\rho,\varepsilon,\geq n)
-rich inflation of
K_{h}
.

The next lemma is the key step in our density increment strategy. It shows that at every step, we may either improve a rich inflation of
K_{h}
to a rich inflation of
K_{h+1}
, or perform a density-increment step.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h})
is a
(\rho,\varepsilon,\geq n)
-rich inflation of
K_{h}
for some
h\geq 1
and some
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{9}]
and
\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, and let
Y=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}V_{i}
. Let
X
be some set of vertices, disjoint from
Y
, with
d_{X}(y)\geq p|X|
for all
y\in Y
, where
p\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho
. Then at least one of the following holds.

•

The tuple
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h},X)
is a
(\rho,2\varepsilon,\geq\min\{n,\lvert X\rvert\})
-rich inflation of
K_{h+1}
, or

•

there exist
X^{\prime}\subseteq X,Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
with
|X^{\prime}|\geq\varepsilon\rho^{h^{2}}|X|,|Y^{\prime}|\geq\varepsilon\rho^{h^{2}}n
which satisfy
d_{X^{\prime}}(y)\geq(1+\varepsilon\rho^{3h})p|X^{\prime}|
for all
y\in Y^{\prime}
.

The proof of
Lemma

4.5
is fairly technical, but the main idea is simple, so we explain it before proceeding with the formal proof. We pick uniformly random vertices
y_{1}\in V_{1},\dots,y_{h}\in V_{h}
, where we think that we make these choices sequentially. Throughout, the key random variable that we track is the size
\lvert N_{i}\rvert
of the common neighborhood of
y_{1},\dots,y_{i}
in
X
, and we track the evolution of this random variable as
i
increases from
1
to
h
.

By the assumption that
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h})
is a rich inflation of
K_{h}
, we have a good probability that
y_{1},\dots,y_{h}
form a copy of
K_{h}
; let us henceforth condition on this event. If, with good probability,
\lvert N_{h}\rvert
is large at the end of the process, then this means that there are many vertices in
X
which complete
(y_{1},\dots,y_{h})
to a copy of
K_{h+1}
; in this case, we have the first outcome of
Lemma

4.5
, namely that
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h},X)
is a rich inflation of
K_{h+1}
. Therefore, we may assume that with good probability,
\lvert N_{h}\rvert
is small at the end of the process. Since we started with
\lvert N_{0}\rvert=\lvert X\rvert
, there must be some step
i
at which, with good probability,
\lvert N_{i}\rvert
shrinks significantly relative to
\lvert N_{i-1}\rvert
. But this precisely means that there are many choices of
y_{i}\in V_{i}
which have few neighbors in
N_{i-1}
, i.e. we have found large subsets
Y^{\prime}\subseteq V_{i}\subseteq Y
and
X^{*}\subseteq N_{i-1}\subseteq X
with few edges between them. Applying
Lemma

4.2
allows us conclude that in this case, we have the second outcome of
Lemma

4.5
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.5
.

We begin by handling the case
h=1
. In this case, the fact that
d_{X}(y)\geq p\lvert X\rvert
for all

implies that the number of canonical copies of
K_{2}
in
(V_{1},X)
is at least
p\lvert X\rvert\lvert Y\rvert\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho\lvert X\rvert\lvert V_{1}\rvert
. Hence the tuple
(V_{1},X)
a
(\rho,\varepsilon,\geq n)
-rich inflation of
K_{2}
, and therefore a
(\rho,2\varepsilon,\geq n)
-rich inflation as well. This is one of the two claimed outcomes, and thus we may assume henceforth that

.

By deleting arbitrary edges, we may assume that
d_{X}(y)=p|X|
for all
y\in Y
. Indeed, both claimed outcomes are monotone under adding edges, so if we prove the result after these edge deletions we have also proved it for the original graph.
We suppose henceforth that
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h},X)
is not a
(\rho,2\varepsilon)
-rich inflation of
K_{h}
, and seek to prove the existence of the claimed sets

.

Recall that we assumed that
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h})

(\rho,\varepsilon)
-rich inflation, implying that

; hence the fact that
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h},X)
is not a
(\rho,2\varepsilon)
-rich inflation implies that the number of canonical

is less than
((1-2\varepsilon)\rho)^{e(K_{h+1})}\prod_{i=1}^{h}\lvert V_{i}\rvert\cdot\lvert X\rvert
.

Let
(y_{1},\dots,y_{h})\in V_{1}\times\dots\times V_{h}
be chosen uniformly at random, and let
N_{h}\subseteq X
be their common neighborhood in
X
.
Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the event that
y_{1},\dots,y_{h}
form a copy of
K_{h}
, and note that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq((1-\varepsilon)\rho)^{e(K_{h})}
by assumption.
Let
x\in X

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

(y_{1},\dots,y_{h},x)

K_{h+1}

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})<((1-2\varepsilon)\rho)^{e(K_{h+1})}

((1-2\varepsilon)\rho)^{h}=\frac{((1-2\varepsilon)\rho)^{e(K_{h+1})}}{((1-2\varepsilon)\rho)^{e(K_{h})}}>\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})}{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{E}).

Moreover, note that conditional on the event
\mathcal{E}
, the probability of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is exactly
\lvert N_{h}\rvert/\lvert X\rvert
. Therefore, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}[\lvert N_{h}\rvert\mid\mathcal{E}]\leq((1-2\varepsilon)\rho)^{h}\lvert X\rvert.

Noting that
(1-2\varepsilon)\rho\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\rho\leq(1-\varepsilon)p
, where the final step is by our assumption on

, we find that
\mathbb{E}[\lvert N_{h}\rvert\mid\mathcal{E}]\leq((1-\varepsilon)p)^{h}\lvert X\rvert
. This in turn implies that

((1-\varepsilon)p)^{h}\lvert X\rvert\geq\mathbb{E}[\lvert N_{h}\rvert\mid\mathcal{E}]\geq(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}\lvert X\rvert\cdot\mathbb{P}(\lvert N_{h}\rvert\geq(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}\lvert X\rvert\mid\mathcal{E}),

\mathbb{P}(\lvert N_{h}\rvert\geq(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}\lvert X\rvert\mid\mathcal{E})\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{h-1}\leq 1-\varepsilon

\mathbb{P}(\lvert N_{h}\rvert\leq(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}\lvert X\rvert\mid\mathcal{E})\geq\varepsilon

\mathbb{P}(|N_{h}|\leq(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}|X|)\geq\varepsilon\delta
.

We note now that if

, then

(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}>(1-\varepsilon)(1-1/(2h))^{h}\geq(9/16)(1-\varepsilon)\geq\frac{1}{2}

\varepsilon\leq 1/9

\mathbb{P}(\lvert N_{h}\rvert\leq(1-\varepsilon)\rho^{h}\lvert X\rvert)=0

.

Let
N_{0}\coloneqq X
and for

. Note that this agrees with our earlier definition of
N_{h}
. We now observe that if
\lvert N_{\tau}\rvert\geq(1-\varepsilon/(2h))p\lvert N_{\tau-1}\rvert

\frac{\lvert N_{h}\rvert}{\lvert X\rvert}=\prod_{\tau=1}^{h}\frac{\lvert N_{\tau}\rvert}{\lvert N_{\tau-1}\rvert}\geq\left(\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2h}\right)p\right)^{h}>(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}.

In other words, whenever the event
\lvert N_{h}\rvert\leq(1-\varepsilon)p^{h}\lvert X\rvert
occurs, there

where

\tau\coloneqq\infty
.

By the pigeonhole principle, there must be some index

\mathbb{P}(\tau=i^{*})\geq\varepsilon\delta/h

|Y^{\prime}|\geq(\varepsilon\delta/h)|V_{i^{*}}|\geq(\varepsilon\delta/h)n
.

Additionally, we have that
|X^{*}|\geq((1-\varepsilon/(2h))p)^{i^{*}-1}|X|\geq\frac{1}{2}p^{h-1}|X|
, where the final inequality uses that

and that
(1-1/(2h))^{h}\geq\frac{1}{2}
for all

. At the same time, by our minimum degree assumption, we cannot have

, whence
|X^{*}|\leq d_{X}(y_{1}^{*})=p|X|
. We now apply

4.2
with

and

to conclude that for

, we have

for all

. The claimed result then follows by noting that

\lvert X^{\prime}\rvert=\lvert X\rvert-\lvert X^{*}\rvert\geq(1-p)\lvert X\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2h}\lvert X\rvert\geq\varepsilon\rho^{h^{2}}\lvert X\rvert,

that

\lvert Y^{\prime}\rvert\geq\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{h}n\geq\frac{\varepsilon((1-\varepsilon)\rho)^{e(K_{h})}}{h}n\geq\varepsilon\rho^{h^{2}}n,

and that

c\xi\geq\frac{\varepsilon p^{h-1}}{4h}\geq\frac{\varepsilon((1-\varepsilon)\rho)^{h-1}}{4h}\geq\varepsilon\rho^{3h}.\qed

4.2
The density increment argument

Definition 4.6
.

Let
q,h\geq 2
be integers and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
a parameter. We define

\eta(q,h,\varepsilon)
to be the maximum
\eta\in[0,1]
so that for all
N\geq 1/\eta
, and for every
q
-coloring of
E(K_{N})
, there exists a monochromatic
(1/q,\varepsilon,\geq\eta N)
-rich inflation of
K_{h}
.

We note that the set of such
\eta\subseteq[0,1]
is a closed subset of
[0,1]
, hence we really can define
\eta(q,h,\varepsilon)
as a maximum, rather than a supremum. The next result gives a recursive lower bound on
\eta(q,h+1,\varepsilon)
in terms of
\eta(q,h,\varepsilon/2)
.

Proposition 4.7
.

For every
q\geq 2,h\geq 1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{9}]
, we have

\eta(q,h+1,\varepsilon)\geq\left(\varepsilon^{4}q^{-h^{2}}\eta(q,h,\varepsilon/2)\right)^{q^{6h}/\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Fix some
q,h,\varepsilon
and consider
N\geq 1
and some coloring
\chi:E(K_{N})\to[q]
. For a color

we let
G_{i}\subseteq K_{N}
denote the graph of edges receiving color

\rho\coloneqq 1/q
,
\eta_{0}\coloneqq\eta(q,h,\varepsilon/2)
, and
k\coloneqq q^{6h}/\varepsilon

(1+\varepsilon\rho^{3h})^{k/q}\geq\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\rho^{3h}k}{2q}\right)\geq\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon k}{q^{3h+2}}\right)\geq e^{q}>2q\geq\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\rho},

(5)

where we use the inequality

in the second step, the definition of

Corollary 4.8
.

For every
q\geq 2,h\geq 1

\eta(q,h,\varepsilon)\geq q^{-q^{4h^{2}}/\varepsilon^{2h}}

\eta(q,h+1,\varepsilon)\geq\left(\varepsilon^{4}q^{-h^{2}}\eta(q,h,\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})\right)^{q^{6h}/\varepsilon}\geq\left(\varepsilon^{4}q^{-h^{2}}q^{-q^{4h^{2}}/(\varepsilon/2)^{2h}}\right)^{q^{6h}/\varepsilon}\geq\left(q^{-(4/\varepsilon+h^{2}+q^{4h^{2}}/(\varepsilon/2)^{2h})}\right)^{q^{6h}/\varepsilon},

where the final step uses that

\varepsilon^{4}\geq 2^{-4/\varepsilon}\geq q^{-4/\varepsilon}
since
x\geq 2^{-1/x}
for all
x\in(0,1]
. Therefore, if we denote by
\zeta\coloneqq-\log_{q}\eta(q,h+1,\varepsilon)
the negative of the exponent above, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta\leq\frac{q^{6h}}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}+h^{2}+\frac{q^{4h^{2}}}{(\varepsilon/2)^{2h}}\right)=\frac{q^{6h}}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}+h^{2}+2^{2h}\cdot\frac{q^{4h^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2h}}\right).

Note that
4/\varepsilon\leq q^{4h^{2}}/\varepsilon^{2h}
, and similarly
h^{2}\leq q^{4h^{2}}/\varepsilon^{2h}
. Therefore,

\zeta\leq\frac{q^{6h}}{\varepsilon}\left((2^{2h}+2)\frac{q^{4h^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2h}}\right)\leq 2^{2h+1}\frac{q^{4h^{2}+6h}}{\varepsilon^{2h+1}}\leq\frac{q^{4h^{2}+8h+1}}{\varepsilon^{2h+2}}\leq\frac{q^{4(h+1)^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2(h+1)}}.

Recalling the definition of
\zeta
, this implies that

\eta(q,h+1,\varepsilon)\geq q^{-q^{4(h+1)^{2}}/\varepsilon^{2(h+1)}},

completing the inductive step.
∎

We are now finally ready to prove
Lemma

4.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

By
Corollary

4.8
, we have that
\eta(q,h,1/10)\geq q^{-100^{h}q^{4h^{2}}}=\eta
, where we recall the definition of
\eta
from the statement of
Lemma

4.1
. Additionally, our assumption on
N
implies that
N\geq 1/\eta\geq 1/\eta(q,h,1/10)
. Therefore, by the definition of
\eta(q,h,\varepsilon)
, we conclude that every
q
-coloring of
E(K_{N})
contains a monochromatic
(1/q,1/10,\geq\eta N)
-rich inflation of
K_{h}
. In particular, this is a
(\gamma,\geq\eta N)
-inflation of
K_{h}
, where

\gamma=\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{10}\right)\frac{1}{q}\right)^{e(K_{h})}\geq(2q)^{-e(K_{h})},

which is precisely what we wanted to prove.
∎

5
Concluding remarks

We remark that, although we stated
Theorem

3.3
under the assumption
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}
, essentially the same result actually holds for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
, including when
\gamma=1-o(1)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
G
contains a
(\gamma,n)
-inflation of
H
, then
H[k]\subseteq G
, where

k=\Omega_{H}\left(\frac{\log((1-\gamma)n)}{\log(1/\gamma)}\right).

Note that this bound is again best possible, and generalizes
Theorem

3.3
; in particular, the extra factor of
1-\gamma
in the numerator is immaterial when
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}
as in
Theorem

3.3
, but is necessary and sharp when
\gamma\to 1
(indeed, already for
H=K_{2}
, a random bipartite graph of edge density
1-n^{-(1-c)}
contains no
K_{k,k}
with
k=10c\log(n)n^{1-c}
).

To prove Proposition
5.1
, one argues as follows.
Recall that for small
\varepsilon
,
\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))=\Theta(\varepsilon)
, so writing
\gamma=1-\varepsilon
we now wish to prove that
k=\Omega_{H}(\frac{\log(\varepsilon n)}{\varepsilon})
for
\varepsilon<1/2
.
To do so, we shall establish a more precise version of Lemma
3.2
, where we now have
|V_{h}^{*}|=\Omega_{H}(\frac{\log(\varepsilon n)}{\log(1/\gamma)}),n^{\prime}=\frac{(\varepsilon n)^{1/3}}{\varepsilon}
and
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma^{C_{H}}\geq 1-C_{H}\varepsilon
. Once this is established, the result follows by induction on
h
as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
(the important point is that we still have
\log((1-\gamma^{\prime})n^{\prime})=\Omega_{H}(\log((1-\gamma)n))
, so passing to this subgraph is still ‘cheap’ — this would not be the case if
n^{\prime}=\sqrt{n}<1/\varepsilon
).

The refined version of Lemma
3.2
can be split into two regimes. First, we note that if
\gamma\leq 1-\Omega(n^{-1/2})
, then the proof from Section
3
works essentially unchanged (giving
|V_{h}^{*}|\geq\Omega(\frac{\log(n)}{\log(1/\gamma)}),n^{\prime}=\sqrt{n}
). Indeed, the only place where we used the assumption
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}
is in estimating
\mathbb{E}[Z]
in the proof of
Lemma

3.2
, and a similar estimate (which is sufficiently strong for the rest of the proof) holds for all
\gamma\leq 1-\Omega(n^{-1/2})
. In particular, if
\gamma\leq 1-n^{-1/4}
, we have that
n^{\prime}=\sqrt{n}\geq\Omega(\frac{(\varepsilon n)^{1/3}}{\varepsilon})
, so we are done if
\gamma\leq 1-n^{-1/4}

So now we may assume
\gamma=1-\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon\leq n^{-1/4}
. Here, given a
(\gamma,n)
-inflation
(V_{1},\dots,V_{h})
of
H
, we begin by deleting all vertices in any
V_{i}
with fewer than
(1-h^{2}\varepsilon)n
neighbors in any other
V_{j}
with
ij\in E(H)
. Since any such vertex lies in at most
(1-h^{2}\varepsilon)n^{h-1}
canonical copies of
H
, after deleting them we still have a
(1-O_{H}(\varepsilon),\geq n/2)
-inflation of
H
. We now apply the same dependent random choice argument as in the proof of
Lemma

3.2
, with two changes: we now sample
s=\Theta(\log(\varepsilon n)/\varepsilon)
random vertices, chosen so that
\gamma^{s}n=\Theta((\varepsilon n)^{1/3}/\varepsilon)
, and we no longer keep track of the random variable
Y
. The rest of the argument goes through unchanged, giving us the set
V_{h}^{*}
of size
\lvert V_{h}^{*}\rvert\geq s/2
and subsets
V_{1}^{\prime},\dots,V_{t}^{\prime}
, each of size at least
\gamma^{s}n=(\varepsilon n)^{\Omega(1)}/\varepsilon
. Moreover, although we no longer have the random variable
Y
to guarantee that we preserve many canonical copies of
H^{\prime}=H\setminus\{h\}
, the minimum degree condition we established does guarantee this. Noting that

\lvert V_{h}^{*}\rvert\geq\frac{s}{2}=\Omega_{H}\left(\frac{\log(\varepsilon n)}{\varepsilon}\right)=\Omega_{H}\left(\frac{\log((1-\gamma)n)}{\log(1/\gamma)}\right),

we see that we get the desired result.

While our work gives optimal results for triangle-free graphs, a number of important problems remain open,
the most significant of which is to prove results like
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
for other graphs
H
; in particular, as discussed in the introduction, such results for
H=K_{3}
would have far-reaching consequences.

A more modest question, which we nonetheless find interesting, is to obtain the correct bounds for
Corollary

4.8
. It would be nice to show that
\eta(q,h,q^{-C})\geq q^{-O_{h}(q)}
, or possibly even
\eta(q,H,q^{-C})\geq r(H;q)^{-O_{H}(1)}
for general graphs
H
. Such bounds would give essentially optimal bounds on how large
k
needs to be for
Theorem

1.3
to hold. In the case of
h=3
, one can use recent work of Kelley, Lovett, and Meka
[
22
]
in order to do the density-increment argument more efficiently and prove that
\eta(q,3,q^{-C})\geq q^{-O(q\log^{2}(q))}
. However, we already hit a stumbling block at
h=4
, and do not know how to establish
\eta(q,4,q^{-C})\geq q^{-q^{1+o(1)}}
.
