Parametric Lie group structures on the probabilistic simplex and generalized Compositional Data
Petre Birtea, Ioana Gavra

Abstract
In this paper we build a set of parametric quotient Lie group structures on the probabilistic simplex that can be extended to real vector space structures. In particular, we rediscover the main mathematical objects generally used when treating compositional data as elements associated to the quotient Lie group with respect to the equivalence relation induced by the scale invariance principle. This perspective facilitates the adaptation of the statistical methods used for classical compositional data to data that follows a more general equivalence relation.



Keywords:
Quotient Lie groups, probabilistic simplex, Compositional Data, parametric distances, Fréchet means, principal components analysis.

MSC classification:
22E15, 62H

Introduction

The unit simplex can be regarded as the set of discrete probability distributions for random variables with a fixed finite number of possible values. It is also used as a model space for Compositional Data analysis (
[
5
]
,
[
11
]
), for word frequencies in text classification (
[
15
]
), portfolio selection problems (
[
16
]
) and a myriad of other problems.
From a geometric point of view, it can be regarded as a constrained sub-manifold of the open half-cone.

The open half-cone
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}|~x_{i}>0\right\}
can be endowed with an Abelian Lie group structure

{\bf x}\oplus{\bf y}=(\dots,x_{i}y_{i},\dots),

with neutral element
{\bf 1}=(\dots,1,\dots)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
.

The Lie group exponential map is given by the
global diffeomorphism

\mathrm{Exp}:\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
,

\mathrm{Exp}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=(\dots,e^{\xi_{i}},\dots)

and its inverse, the logarithmic map
{\bf\mathrm{Log}}:\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
,

{\mathrm{Log}}({\bf x})=(\dots,\ln x_{i},\dots).

Using the global diffeomorphism property of
{\bf\mathrm{Exp}}
and
{\bf\mathrm{Log}}
, we can introduce a scalar multiplication on
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
as follows,

c\odot{\bf x}:=\mathrm{Exp}(c{\mathrm{Log}}(\bf x)).

(1)

Writing in coordinates, we have

c\odot{\bf x}=(\dots,{x_{i}}^{c},\dots),

for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. The set
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
endowed these two operations becomes a real vector space

(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0},\oplus,\odot).

The probabilistic open simplex

\Delta_{N}^{0}=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}\ |\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}x_{i}=1\}

is a constraint manifold, which also has an Abelian group structure :

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}*{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}}(\dots,\lambda_{i}\mu_{i},\dots).

This group operation, also called perturbation, lays at the core of the Compositional Data analysis methods. It was also used together with the Fisher Riemannian metric on the simplex in the problem of metric learning for text classification in
[
15
]
.

A first observation is that
(\Delta_{N}^{0},*)
is not a subgroup of
(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0},\oplus).
A natural question that arises is
how can one geometrically explain the group operation
*
in relation with the group operation
\oplus
.

We prove that
(\Delta_{N}^{0},*)
can be regarded as a quotient Lie group structure of
(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0},\oplus)
relative to the one-dimensional subgroup
H=\left\{\mathrm{Exp}(t{\bf 1})=\right.

\left.(\dots,e^{t},\dots)|~t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
. Furthermore, this structure can be extended to a vector space one. This allows, for the first time to give a Lie group explanation for several of the main objects encountered in Compositional Data analysis. For example, the Centered-Log ratio transformation is non other than the Lie logarithmic map of the quotient structure
(\Delta_{N}^{0},*)
(for details, see sub-section
3.1
). It also facilitates a Lie group interpretation to the famous Boltzmann distribution, also known as softmax function in the machine learning community.

In section
2
, we give a follow up of the above constructions to the case where the Lie group structure and the associated objects depend on a parameter. In particular we obtain a parametric family of distances on the simplex. Parametric families of distances are an important tool in metric learning problems (
[
14
]
,
[
21
]
). Our idea is to embed this parameter dependence into the family of one-dimensional subgroups, with respect to which we construct the quotient structure on the probabilistic simplex.

In Section
3
, we detail the link between the quotient Lie group structure induced by
H
and the Aitchison geometry. Sometimes the nature of the data might require a different quotient geometry, like for example data that comes from exponential decay phenomena
[
20
]
. In Section
4
, we extend the main principles of Compositional Data analysis to this more general framework. Finally, in Section
5
, we present the adaptation of some basic objects of descriptive statistics and of the multivariate normal distribution to the probabilistic simplex endowed with this new geometric structures.

4
Generalised compositional data

As we have seen before, the main concepts and mathematical objects used in compositional data arise naturally in the Lie group theory, when considering the simplex as a space model for the quotient group
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}/H_{\bf 1}
. If the nature of the data implies a different link between the components, considering a different equivalence relation, determined by a general
H_{\bf a}
, might be a more appropriate choice. There are several principles, introduced by Aitchison, that are considered being fundamental in an large part of the compositional data literature. A detailed historical account of these principles and an analysis of their usefulness when studying data is given in
[
19
]
. In what follows, we explain what these principles become in the more general framework when the equivalence relation is generated by
H_{\bf a}
.

Scale invariance

The scale invariance principle used in compositional data corresponds to the equivalence relation determined by
H_{\bf 1}
. We give a straightforward generalisation of this principle when the equivalence class is determined by
H_{\bf a}
. In this case, two specimens
\bf{v}
and
\bf{w}
belong to the same class determined by
H_{\bf a}
,
i.e.

\bf{v}\sim\bf{w}
, iff there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
{\bf{v}}=(w_{1}\alpha^{a_{1}},\ldots,w_{N+1}\alpha^{a_{N+1}})

or equivalently,
\dfrac{{v_{i}}^{a_{j}}}{v_{j}^{a_{i}}}=\dfrac{{w_{i}}^{a_{j}}}{w_{j}^{a_{i}}}
, for all
i,j\in\overline{1,N+1}
.

If the two specimens are two different instances of a variable
X:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N+1}
, i.e.
{\bf v}=X(\tau_{1})
and
{\bf w}=X(\tau_{2})
, where
\tau
is some parameter describing mass, or volume, time, concentration, etc., then the equivalence relation above can be restated as
\dfrac{\delta}{\delta\tau}X(\tau):=X(\tau)^{-1}\dot{X}(\tau)=\bf{a}
. The operation
\dfrac{\delta}{\delta\tau}
is the logarithmic derivative in the Abelian group
(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N+1},\oplus)
.
This leads to considering the quotient structure with respect to
H_{\bf a}
.
Such situations are encountered in practice, for example, when analyzing the mass evolution of components in a system, see
[
20
]
. For instances, when the data comes from an exponential decay dynamic, a situation also described in
[
20
]
, the Aitchison geometry (case
\bf{a}=\bf{1}
) might not be the best approach when dealing with general equivalence classes.

Another case when a more general geometry is better adaptive is for data in which some components are measured in
unit
and other, for example, in
unit^{2}
or
unit^{3}
(for instance one component that represents a length and another an area or a volume).

Permutation invariance

This principle refers to the fact that the results of an analysis of the data should be invariant under any permutation of the components, as long as the same permutation is applied to the whole data set. This property still holds for the generalized framework corresponding to any
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
, as soon as the quotient structure is adapted accordingly : if a permutation
\sigma
is used on the components of the data, then the proper quotient structure is the one with respect to
H_{\sigma({\bf a})}
.

Subcompositional coherence

In what follows, we define a subcomposition procedure in the previously proposed general framework and show that the subcomposition procedure is a linear map between two vector spaces.

\textstyle{\left(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0},\oplus,\odot\right)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathrm{A}_{k}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}
\textstyle{\left(\mathbb{R}^{k+1}_{>0},\oplus,\odot\right)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\left(\Delta_{N}^{0},\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}},\odot_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\right)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

We denote by
\mathrm{A}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}^{k+1}_{>0}
the map that assigns to each
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
a sub-part of
k+1

(not necessarily in the initial order) and set
{\bf a}^{\prime}:=\mathrm{A}_{k}({\bf a})
. Then, for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta_{N}^{0}
we can define the subcomposition corresponding to the
k+1

\mathrm{A}_{k}
as :

\mathrm{Sub}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}):=\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}(\mathrm{A}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})).

The well definiteness of the operator is a consequence of the fact that
\forall{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}(\mathrm{A}_{k}({\bf x}))=\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}(\mathrm{A}_{k}(\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf x})))
.
Indeed, let
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf x})=(x_{1}e^{t_{\bf a}({\bf x})a_{1}},\ldots,x_{N+1}e^{t_{\bf a}({\bf x})a_{N+1}})
, where

is the solution of the equation (
4

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{k}(\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf x}))

\displaystyle=(x_{\sigma_{1}}e^{t_{\bf a}({\bf x})a_{\sigma_{1}}},\ldots,x_{\sigma_{N+1}}e^{t_{\bf a}({\bf x})a_{\sigma_{N+1}}})

\displaystyle=(x_{\sigma_{1}}e^{t_{\bf a}({\bf x})a_{1}^{\prime}},\ldots,x_{\sigma_{N+1}}e^{t_{\bf a}({\bf x})a_{N+1}^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{A}_{k}({\bf x})H_{\bf a^{\prime}}.

In other words, we have that
\mathrm{A}_{k}(\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf x}))
is in the same equivalence class as
\mathrm{A}_{k}({\bf x})
with respect to

and thus by definition
\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}(\mathrm{A}_{k}(\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf x})))=\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}(\mathrm{A}_{k}({\bf x}))

The fact that
\mathrm{Sub}

\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}

Subcompositional coherence principle is generally described as the fact that the results obtained for a sub-part of a compositional data (studied after closure) should remain coherent with the results obtained for the full composition. A classical example of subcompositional incoherence, shows that when compositional data framework is not taken into account, we can obtain a positive correlation between two variables when they are studied as composites of a specimen with
n

[
5

). Of course, not all results obtained on a compositional data are preserved by the

\mathrm{Sub}\left(\frac{1}{n}\odot_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\ldots\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{n}\odot_{\bf a^{\prime}}\left(\mathrm{Sub}\left({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}})\oplus_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}}\ldots\oplus_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a^{\prime}}}\mathrm{Sub}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}\right)\right).

Centrality for generalized compositional data

Once we endow the simplex with a distance
\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
, a natural first notion of centrality that can be considered is the corresponding Fréchet mean. Fréchet means were introduced by M Fréchet in
[
10
]
and represent a generalization of the classical expected value to arbitrary metric spaces.
For a random variable
X
, defined on a metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
, a Fréchet mean is a minimizer of
y\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[d^{2}(y,X)\right]
. When there exists a unique minimizer
m(X)
, we say that
m(X)
is the Fréchet mean of
X
with respect to
d
.
Aitchison used this notion to define the geometric mean as a measure of central tendency (see for example
[
2
]
) for compositional data.
A similar result can be obtained in a straightforward way for a more general distance
\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
. Let
X
be a random variable on
\Delta_{N}^{0}
. Then a Fréchet mean of
X
with respect to
\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
is a minimizer of the real valued functional:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},X)\right].

(9)

Now, for any
\boldsymbol{\lambda,\mu}\in\Delta_{N}^{0}
, the definition of the distance
\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
implies that
\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda,\mu})=d(\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))
. We also have that, for all
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta_{N}^{0}
,
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},X)]=\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(X)]
, thus a minimizer of (
9
) is a Fréchet mean of
\mathrm{Log}_{a}(X)
and therefore, as soon as
\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(X)
is integrable, we have that
m_{a}(X)
, the Fréchet mean of
X
with respect to
\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
, is unique and that :

m_{\bf a}(X)=\mathrm{Exp}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(X)\right]\right).

This last result can also be seen as a consequence of Proposition 1 of
[

since

Since the simplex is endowed with additional mathematical structure we can also construct the expected value of a random variable. Let
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{N+1}
and denote
\mathcal{B}_{\bf a}=\mathrm{Exp}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\mathcal{B})

\mathcal{B}

and obtain that :

\mathbb{E}_{\bf a}[X]=\mathrm{Exp}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(X)]\right).

It is well known that in

PCA

Proposition
1
implies in particular that
\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
can be seen as the equivalent of crl map for generalized compositional data.
With this is mind, all the statistical methods based on log-ratios used for compositional data can be extended in a straightforward way to generalized compositional data. Take for example principal component analysis (PCA). This is a well known multivariate statistical analysis method, often used for dimensionality reduction. The main idea of PCA is to synthesize a data set consisting in a large number of correlated variables, while retaining as much as possible the variation present in
it. To achieve this, PCA computes new uncorrelated variables, called principal components. For more details see
[
13
]
.

In an Euclidean framework, PCA can be reduced to the study of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a positive-semidefinite symmetric matrix, and is generally treated in this manner.
In the context of generalized compositional data, principal component analysis can be applied on the matrix obtained through a
\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
transformation of the data set. This is the equivalent of logratio analysis used for classical compositional data.

To highlight the link between the PCA on the image of a data set through
\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
and an analysis directly on the simplex, it is convenient to look at the variational formulation of the PCA.

Indeed, principal components can be described as a sequence of nested affine sub-spaces of increasing dimension that maximize the variance of the projections or minimize the sum of norms of projection residuals. For example, consider the case of
m
points
{\bf x}_{1},\ldots{\bf x}_{m}
, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote:

S_{\bf v}=\{{\bf\bar{x}}_{m}+t{\bf v},t\in\mathbb{R}\},\mbox{ where }{\bf\bar{x}}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bf x}_{i}

A first principal component of the data can be described as
S_{{\bf v}_{1}}
, where
{\bf v}_{1}
is such that:

{\bf v}_{1}\in\argmin\limits_{\|{\bf v}\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d^{2}({\bf x}_{i},S_{\bf v}).

This formulation is at the root of several generalizations of PCA to non-euclidean framework (like on the Wasserstein space of probability measures over
\mathbb{R}
in
[
6
]
, for a survey of methods employed in Riemannian settings see
[
12
]
).

Now consider the equivalent objects on
(\Delta_{N}^{0},\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}},\odot_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}})
. Suppose we have
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\ldots\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}\in\Delta_{N}^{0}
and for all
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\Delta_{N}^{0}
denote :

S_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\{\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m}\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}t\odot_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\boldsymbol{\mu},\ t\in\mathbb{R}\},\mbox{ with }\ \bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\odot_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\left[\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\ldots\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}\right]

A first principal component becomes
S_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}}
, with
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
such that :

{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}}\in\argmin\limits_{\|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}=1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},S_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}).

For

, set

. Let

and denote

Keeping in mind that

, we have that

{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}}\in\argmin\limits_{\|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}=1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},S_{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\Longleftrightarrow\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})\in\argmin\limits_{\|{\bf v}\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d^{2}({\bf x}_{i},S_{\bf v})

And thus,

is a first principal component for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\ldots\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}\in\Delta_{N}^{0}
if and only if

is a principal component for
\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}),\ldots\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})
.

This argument can be easily extended to principal components of higher order, showing thus that applying PCA on the data transformed through
\mathrm{Log}_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}}
is the same as using it directly on the simplex with the appropriate operations.

We give a proof for the commutativity of Diagram (
6
). First, we recall the necessary theoretical elements from Marsden and Ratiu
[
17
]
.

Let
(G,\cdot)
be a Lie group and
\mathfrak{G}
its Lie algebra. One can regard the group multiplication as the left action of the group to itself,
g\cdot h=L_{g}(h)
. The corresponding infinitesimal action is defined by
(\boldsymbol{\xi},g)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}(g):=DL_{g}(e_{{}_{G}})~\boldsymbol{\xi}
, where
DL_{g}(e_{{}_{G}})
is the differential of the left translation computed at the neutral element
e_{{}_{G}}\in G
.

The exponential map
Exp_{{}_{G}}:\mathfrak{G}\rightarrow G
is defined by

Exp_{{}_{G}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\gamma_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(1),

where
\gamma_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(1)
is the solution at time
t=1
for the system of differential equations
\dot{\gamma}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(t)=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}(\gamma_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(t))
with initial condition
\gamma_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(0)=e_{{}_{G}}
.

For the case of Lie group
(\Delta_{N}^{0},\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}})
, the left translation is defined by the maps composition

L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{{}^{\Delta_{N}^{0}}}\circ\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}=\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}\circ L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{{}^{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}}}.

By differentiation, we obtain the operator equality,

DL_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{{}^{\Delta_{N}^{0}}}({\bf e}_{{\bf a}})~\circ~D\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf{1}})=D\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})~\circ~DL_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{{}^{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}}}(\bf{1})

and consequently, for any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in T_{\bf{e_{a}}}\Delta_{N}^{0}
, we have the following computation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{{}^{\Delta_{N}^{0}}}

\displaystyle=DL_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{{}^{\Delta_{N}^{0}}}({\bf e}_{{\bf a}})\boldsymbol{\xi}=DL_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{{}^{\Delta_{N}^{0}}}({\bf e}_{{\bf a}})\left(D\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf{1}}){\bf v}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)

\displaystyle=D\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\left(DL_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{{}^{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}}}(\bf{1}){\bf v}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)

\displaystyle=D\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\left({\bf v}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)_{L}^{{}^{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}}},

where
{\bf v}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in T_{\bf 1}\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0}
such that
\boldsymbol{\xi}=D\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}({\bf{1}}){\bf v}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.This shows that the left invariant vector fields on
(\Delta_{N}^{0},\oplus_{{}_{\mathcal{C}_{\bf a}}})
are the push forward of the left invariant vector fields on
(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{>0},\oplus)
. Using the definition of the exponential map, we obtain the commutativity of Diagram
