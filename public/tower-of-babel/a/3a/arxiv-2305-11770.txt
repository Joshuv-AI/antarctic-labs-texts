Edifices: Building-like spaces associated to linear algebraic groups
Michael Bate, Benjamin Martin, Gerhard Röhrle

Abstract
. Given a semisimple linear algebraic k k -group G G , one has a spherical building Δ G \Delta_{G} , and one can interpret the geometric realisation Δ G ​ ( ℝ ) \Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}}) of Δ G \Delta_{G} in terms of cocharacters of G G . The aim of this paper is to extend this construction to the case when G G is an arbitrary connected linear algebraic group; we call the resulting object Δ G ​ ( ℝ ) \Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}}) the spherical edifice of G G . We also define an object V G ​ ( ℝ ) V_{G}({\mathbb{R}}) which is an analogue of the vector building for a semisimple group; we call V G ​ ( ℝ ) V_{G}({\mathbb{R}}) the vector edifice . The notions of a linear map and an isomorphism between edifices are introduced; we construct some linear maps arising from natural group-theoretic operations. We also devise a family of metrics on V G ​ ( ℝ ) V_{G}({\mathbb{R}}) and show they are all bi-Lipschitz equivalent to each other; with this extra structure, V G ​ ( ℝ ) V_{G}({\mathbb{R}}) becomes a complete metric space. Finally, we present some motivation in terms of geometric invariant theory and variations on the Tits Centre Conjecture.

1.
Introduction

Jacques Tits showed in his seminal 1974 monograph
[
33
]
how to associate a spherical building
\Delta_{G}
to an isotropic semisimple linear algebraic group
G
over a field

: the simplices of the building correspond to the

-parabolic subgroups of
G
, ordered by reverse inclusion. This has led to a rich and fruitful interaction between the theory of algebraic groups and the theory of buildings; for example, Serre has formulated his theory of
G
-completely reducible subgroups of
G
in building-theoretic language
[
30
]
,
[
31
]
. Tits’s celebrated Centre Conjecture for spherical buildings—whose proof is the culmination of a series of papers involving several authors
[
23
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
26
]
—has consequences for algebraic groups: for instance, it implies the Borel-Tits Theorem
[
13
, §3]
. The authors in previous and current work have studied the interactions among the Centre Conjecture,
G
-complete reducibility and geometric invariant theory (GIT), e.g., see
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
, §5]
,
[
10
, §5.4]
, and
[
9
]
. The link with geometric invariant theory comes via a rational version of the Hilbert-Mumford Theorem, used to study orbits of an action of
G
on an affine variety, see
[
3
]
,
[
10
, §3]
.

The geometric realisation
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
of
\Delta_{G}
has a striking interpretation in terms of cocharacters of
G
. Given a cocharacter
\lambda
of
G
, one can associate to it a parabolic subgroup
P_{\lambda}
of
G
in a standard way, see
[
27
, §2]
,
[
32
, §8.4]
. Roughly speaking, points in
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
correspond to formal
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear combinations of cocharacters, modulo a natural equivalence relation. If
x\in\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
is the point corresponding to
\lambda
then
x
belongs to the geometric realisation of the simplex corresponding to
P_{\lambda}
. This construction is based on ideas of Tits and Chevalley and is described in

[
16
]
,
[
9
]
and below. See
[
24
, Ch. 2, Sec. 2]
for further discussion (note that
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{Q}})
is called the
rational flag complex
in
loc. cit.
). For applications to geometric invariant theory, one must work with this geometric realisation rather than the simplicial building, since in the Hilbert-Mumford Theorem one is concerned with the behaviour of individual cocharacters; see Section
7
.

Our purpose in this paper is to generalise the construction of a building via cocharacters to the case when
G
is an arbitrary connected linear algebraic
k
-group, not necessarily semisimple. This was achieved for reductive groups in
[
16
]
; in that case one can still form a spherical building from the
k
-parabolic subgroups of
G
, which is canonically isomorphic to the spherical building of the semisimple group
G/Z(G)^{0}
, but one sees a difference at the level of cocharacters as
G
may admit non-trivial cocharacters with image in
Z(G)^{0}
. We define spaces
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\Delta_{G}
for arbitrary
G
in Section
3
, which coincide with the spaces described above when
G
is semisimple or reductive. We call
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
the
spherical edifice
associated to
G
and
\Delta_{G}
the
combinatorial edifice
associated to
G
. We also define a space
V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
, which we call the
vector edifice
associated to
G
; this is our generalisation for arbitrary
G
of the vector building for a semisimple group defined in
[
28
]
. In fact, we concentrate on vector edifices rather than spherical edifices as the former have some technical advantages (see Remark
4.30
).

In general
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
is not the geometric realisation of
\Delta_{G}
(or of any spherical building); nonetheless it enjoys some of the same properties. For instance,
G(k)
acts on
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
and we may endow
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
with a
G(k)
-invariant complete metric: see Section
6
. We prove the following key property (Proposition

). Let

be a homomorphism of

-groups. Then

gives rise in a natural way to a map
\kappa_{f}\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})\to V_{H}({\mathbb{R}})
, and
\kappa_{f}
is continuous with respect to the metric topologies (Corollary

). For example, if

is reductive and

is a parabolic subgroup of

then

is a bijection, where

is inclusion (see Example

). We also obtain an induced map from
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
to
\Delta_{H}({\mathbb{R}})
. Even when

and
H
are reductive,

need not map parabolic subgroups of

to parabolic subgroups of

, so

does not give rise to a map

of simplicial buildings (see Remark

). Our constructions therefore provide extra flexibility: maps between the geometric objects exist even when maps between the corresponding combinatorial objects do not. We rely on this heavily in our forthcoming work
[
9
]
.

While edifices are worth studying as objects in their own right, we have two further motivations for investigating them. First, assume
G
is reductive. Let
P
be a parabolic subgroup of
G
and let
L
be a Levi subgroup of
P
. For reasons discussed in Section
7
, we wish to construct a map with certain properties from
V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
to
V_{L}({\mathbb{R}})
. It is well known that such a map exists at the level of the simplicial building: this is the so-called projection map from
\Delta_{G}
to
\Delta_{L}
, see Section
4.6
. We need a map on the geometric realisations. Our constructions give us what we want: we define the map to be
\kappa_{\pi}\circ\kappa_{i}^{-1}
, where

is the inclusion of
P
in
G
from above and
\pi\colon P\to L
is the canonical projection.

Second, edifices give us a framework for studying
G
-complete reducibility and geometric invariant theory for non-reductive
G
: they play the role that the geometric realisation of the spherical building plays when
G
is semisimple. We mention here in particular the case of pseudo-reductive
G
.
As explained in
[
25
]
, one can associate a spherical Tits system to
G

[
15
, Thm. 4.1.7]
, and one can then construct a spherical building
\Delta_{G}
using the pseudo-parabolic subgroups of
G
;
however, there does not seem to be an analogue of the “geometric realisation by cocharacters” previously known here.
Moreover, when working with pseudo-reductive
G
, one often needs to extend scalars to a purely inseparable field extension

; if the base change
G_{k^{\prime}}
has non-trivial unipotent radical over
k^{\prime}
then
\Delta_{G}
need no longer be a spherical building, but the formalism of edifices is flexible enough to handle this situation. The authors and their collaborators will pursue these ideas in forthcoming work.

The paper is set out as follows. In Section
2
we review some background material on algebraic groups and cocharacters, and we define the key notion of an R-parabolic subgroup. We define the vector edifice
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, the spherical edifice
\Delta_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and the combinatorial edifice
\Delta_{G}
in Section
3
, where
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and introduce some related notions: apartments (certain subsets of the vector edifice), opposites, addition, and convexity. One substantial difference from the reductive group case is that the “common apartment property” can fail: not every pair of points in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
need belong to a common apartment. In Section
4
we introduce the notions of linear maps and isomorphisms between vector edifices. We show that an algebraic group homomorphism
f\colon G\to H
gives rise to a linear map
\kappa_{f}\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
, and that
\kappa_{f}
is injective if
\ker(f)
is finite. In Section
5
we construct linear maps of vector edifices arising from base change of the ground field
k
.

We turn to metrics in Section
6
. We define the notion of an
admissible metric
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. The idea is to do this first for reductive groups, where we can work inside individual apartments; for an arbitrary
G
, we can embed
G
inside some reductive
G^{\prime}
, which gives an embedding of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
inside
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
, then pull back a metric from
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
to
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Admissible metrics on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
are not unique, but we prove that any two are bi-Lipschitz equivalent (Corollary
6.23
); this takes some work. In the final section (Section
7
), we discuss applications of our formalism to the Tits Centre Conjecture and to geometric invariant theory.

gTg^{-1}=T
.
Now write
g=lu
with

and

.
Note that

\lim_{a\to 0}\lambda(a)\cdot g
normalises

\lambda(a)
and

do, thus so does

, and hence

Now the morphism

(by definition of the limit),
and this morphism has connected image contained in the normaliser of

and

.
Rigidity of tori implies that the identity component of

is the identity component of

,

[

, and since

.
But we also have

, so this implies

.
Thus

Now suppose

normalises

.
Then

is another maximal split torus of

, and hence is conjugate to

by some element of

.
But this says that we may multiply

so we’re done.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
P=P_{\lambda}
be an R-parabolic subgroup of
G
, and let
T
be a maximal split torus of
G
such that
\lambda\in Y_{T}
.

(i)

Given any other maximal split torus

of
G
contained in

, there is a
\mu\in Y_{T^{\prime}}
with

and

. In fact, we can take

for some

.

(ii)

The R-Levi subgroup

is the unique

-conjugate of
L_{\lambda}
containing

.

(iii)

Let
\mu\in Y_{G}
such that
P_{\mu}=P_{\lambda}
and
L_{\mu}=L_{\lambda}
. Then
U_{\mu}=U_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

(i). Since
T
and

are maximal split tori of the smooth connected
k
-group

, they are
P(k)
-conjugate.
Let
g\in P(k)
be such that

. Write
g=ul
with
u\in U_{\lambda}(k)
and
l\in L_{\lambda}(k)
.
Then
u\cdot\lambda=g\cdot\lambda\in Y_{T^{\prime}}
,
P_{u\cdot\lambda}=uP_{\lambda}u^{-1}=P_{\lambda}
, and
U_{u\cdot\lambda}=uU_{\lambda}u^{-1}=U_{\lambda}
, as required.

(ii). Suppose we have
g\in P(k)
such that
T\subseteq gL_{\lambda}g^{-1}
.
Then
T
and
g^{-1}Tg
are maximal split tori of

, so are conjugate by an element of

.
Hence, by adjusting

with an element of

if necessary, we may assume that

normalises

.
Now Lemma
2.3
implies that

(iii). Since
{\IM}(\mu)\subseteq Z(L_{\lambda})^{0}\subseteq T\subseteq L_{\lambda}
,
\mu
belongs to

. It follows easily that

. So let

for some

and some

∎

If

is reductive then the R-parabolic subgroups of

are precisely the parabolic subgroups, and the R-Levi subgroups of a parabolic subgroup are precisely its Levi subgroups; furthermore, we have

. These facts mean that the result above has a rather simpler formulation and proof for reductive

: the content of parts (i) and (ii) of Lemma
2.4
for reductive

are the well-known facts that each maximal torus of a parabolic subgroup is contained in a unique Levi subgroup, and
the unipotent radical of the parabolic subgroup acts simply transitively on its Levi subgroups.

If

is not reductive then things are more complicated. We have

since

is a connected normal unipotent subgroup of

, but the inclusion can be proper. We can have cocharacters
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{G}
such that

but

. In fact, there can exist
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{G}
such that

but

and

is not conjugate to

. For instance, take

and let

be a Borel subgroup of a non-trivial connected semisimple
k
-group

. Choose a maximal torus

of
G
such that
\mu\in Y_{T}
. Let

. Then

but

.

We do, however, have the following result.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
P
be an R-parabolic subgroup of
G
. Suppose

and
U_{\lambda}\subseteq U_{\mu}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{G}
. Let

be a subgroup of

such that

. Then:

(i)

H
is
U_{\mu}(k)
-conjugate to
L_{\mu}
.

(ii)

H
is
U_{\mu}(k)
-conjugate to a subgroup of
L_{\lambda}
. In particular,
L_{\mu}
is conjugate to a subgroup of
L_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

Since

is a complement to
U_{\mu}
in
P_{\mu}
,

is isomorphic to
L_{\mu}
under the (restriction of the) projection
P_{\mu}\to L_{\mu}
, and hence

contains a maximal split torus

of

.
Using Lemma
2.4
(i), by conjugating

with some element in

, we may assume that

evaluates in

as well.
Now

, but

is trivial, so

.
This implies that

, thus

since

is isomorphic to

and both are smooth and connected, proving (i).

The proof of (ii) is similar: by Lemma

(i) and the fact that

, we may also conjugate

by an element of

so that the maximal split torus

of

.
Then

and

. ∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{G}
such that
P_{\lambda}=P_{\mu}
and

.
Then there is a unique

such that

.

The existence of

(i) with

. If

. This proves uniqueness.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

We need some notation. Let

be a rational

for

Now we consider an important special case of this set-up. By

, Lem. 1.1(a)]
, there is a

of

(as a

Remark 2.8
.

There are only finitely many R-parabolic subgroups

containing a fixed maximal torus

of

of

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
T
be a maximal split torus of
G
and let
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{T}
. Suppose
P_{\lambda}=P_{\mu}
. Then
P_{\lambda+\mu}=P_{\lambda}=P_{\mu}
.

Proof.

Set
P=P_{\lambda}=P_{\mu}
. We prove first that
P_{\lambda+\mu}\supseteq P
. Choose a
G
-equivariant embedding
\rho
of
G
into a rational
G
-module
V
, and fix a maximal split torus
T
of
G
. For any
\nu\in Y_{T}
and any

, we have

if and only if

\min\{\langle\nu,\chi\rangle\mid\chi\in\supp(\rho(g))\}\geq 0.

So let

. Then

\min\{\langle\lambda,\chi\rangle\mid\chi\in\supp(\rho(g))\}\geq 0

and

\min\{\langle\mu,\chi\rangle\mid\chi\in\supp(\rho(g))\}\geq 0,

so

\min\{\langle\lambda+\mu,\chi\rangle\mid\chi\in\supp(\rho(g))\}\geq 0.

Hence

. As

and

are smooth, this shows that

.

To complete the proof, it is enough to show that
\Lie(P_{\lambda+\mu})=\Lie(P)
. Let
\Phi
be the set of roots of

with respect to

. By
[

, Prop. 2.1.8]
, if

then
\Lie(P_{\nu})=\mathfrak{g}_{\nu,\geq 0}
. Since

, we deduce that

, so we have
\Phi_{\lambda+\mu,\geq 0}=\Phi_{\lambda,\geq 0}=\Phi_{\mu,\geq 0}

∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
P
be an R-parabolic subgroup of
G
and let
T
be a maximal torus of

. Then there exists

such that

.

Proof.

Choose a finite Galois extension

such that

is
k^{\prime}
-split and set

. By Lemma

(i) there exists

. Now

belongs to

∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
P
and

be R-parabolic subgroups of

. Suppose

contains a maximal split torus

of

. Then

We can write

and

(i).

∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
P
and

be R-parabolic subgroups of

. The following are equivalent.

(i)

contains a maximal split torus of

.

(ii)

contains a maximal torus of

.

(iii)

.

Clearly (ii) implies (iii). If

is smooth by Lemma

, so

is smooth; then

contains a maximal torus and this is a maximal torus of

. This shows that (iii) implies (ii). Now suppose

is a maximal split torus of

contained in

. We can choose

such that

and

. Let

∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Suppose
P
and
Q
are two R-parabolic subgroups of a
k
-group

. Identify
Y_{P}
and
Y_{Q}
as subsets of

in the usual way.
Then

if and only if

.

Proof.

That
Y_{P}\subseteq Y_{Q}
when

is obvious.
For the other implication,
first observe that for any
\lambda\in Y_{G}
such that

, and any

,

does not evaluate in

.
To see this, note that since the image of

,
for every

we can write

for some

Since

does not centralise

, and hence there exists

Now if we assume that

, then we can find a maximal split torus

in the intersection

:
for any split torus of

and

∎

2.2.
Spherical buildings

As general references on buildings we refer to
[
1
]
and
[
33
]
. We regard a spherical building — as opposed to its geometric realisation — as a combinatorial object. It is a simplicial complex with a distinguished set of subcomplexes called apartments. If
G
is reductive then we can form the spherical building
\Delta_{G}
as follows: the simplices of
\Delta_{G}
are the parabolic subgroups ordered by reverse inclusion, and an apartment of
\Delta_{G}
is the subcomplex consisting of all parabolic subgroups containing a fixed maximal split torus
T
of
G
. Two simplices are opposite if the corresponding parabolic subgroups are opposite. There is a notion of convexity for subcomplexes
[
31
, Prop. 3.1]
. An automorphism of a spherical building
\Delta
is an automorphism of simplicial complexes that maps apartments bijectively to apartments. We write
\Aut(\Delta)
for the group of automorphisms of
\Delta
. For instance, any algebraic group automorphism of
G
— e.g., an inner automorphism — gives rise to an automorphism of
\Delta_{G}
. There are also automorphisms of
\Delta_{G}
arising from automorphisms of the ground field
k
. Both these kinds of automorphism have counterparts for vector edifices: see Section
4.2
and Section
5.2
.

3.
Edifices

In order to define vector edifices, we need to understand how to glue together the
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector spaces
Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
for the maximal split tori of a
k
-group
G
in a suitable way, where
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
This first requires us to attach an R-parabolic subgroup to an arbitrary element of
Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
(above, we have defined
P_{\lambda}
just for
\lambda\in Y_{T}
).
This work is achieved in the next few subsections.

First we clarify some notation. When we write, say,
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
then it is understood that we are working with a fixed choice of
T
. If
\mu
is an actual cocharacter — that is, if
\mu\in Y_{G}
— then there can exist many different maximal split tori
T
such that
\mu\in Y_{T}
, so there is potential for misunderstanding. This does not cause problems, however, for we show below that our constructions when applied to such a
\mu
don’t depend on the choice of
T
: see the paragraph following Definition
3.7
.

3.1.
Linear maps induced by homomorphisms of tori

Suppose
f\colon T\to T^{\prime}
is a homomorphism of split
k
-tori.
Then we get an induced map
Y_{T}\to Y_{T^{\prime}}
via
\lambda\mapsto f\circ\lambda
,
and this extends to a
{\mathbb{K}}
-linear map
f_{*}\colon Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
.
This map is injective if
f
has finite kernel.
In particular, if
T\subseteq G
for some
k
-group
G
and
g\in G(k)
then the conjugation map
\Inn_{g}
induces a linear isomorphism from
Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
to
Y_{gTg^{-1}}({\mathbb{K}})
; we denote the image of
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
by
g\cdot\lambda
.
If in fact
\lambda\in Y_{T}
, then
g\cdot\lambda\in Y_{gTg^{-1}}
as just defined agrees with the usual definition of
g\cdot\lambda
given in Section
2
.
We also have functoriality: if
f^{\prime}\colon T^{\prime}\to T^{\prime\prime}
is another homomorphism of split
k
-tori then
(f^{\prime}\circ f)_{*}=(f^{\prime})_{*}\circ f_{*}
.
In particular, if
f\colon G\to H
is a homomorphism of
k
-groups then, given split
k
-tori
T\subseteq G
and
S\subseteq H
such that
f(T)\subseteq S
, we have a map
h_{*}\colon Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
, where
h\colon T\to S
is the restriction of
f
.
Often we abuse notation slightly and write
f_{*}\colon Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
for this map.

We return to the subject of linear maps induced by group homomorphisms in Section
4.2
below.

3.2.
More on R-parabolic subgroups

Now we can define
P_{\lambda}
,
L_{\lambda}
and
U_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
, where
T\subseteq G
is a split torus.
We may as well assume that
T
is a maximal split torus of
G
and
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Then we may choose a
G
-equivariant embedding
\rho
of
G
into a rational
G
-module
V
. Using the notation from Remark
2.7
,
we define a subspace
W
of

by

W=\{\mu\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\mid\langle\mu,\chi\rangle=0\textrm{ for all }\chi\in\Phi_{\lambda,0}\}.

Clearly,

\lambda\in W
, by the definition of
\Phi_{\lambda,0}
.
Since
\Phi_{\lambda,0}\subseteq X_{T}
,

is defined by equations with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
and hence is defined over

3.3.
The vector edifice

Now we can define
Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. We work with the disjoint union
\bigsqcup_{T}Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
, where

ranges over the maximal split tori of
G
. It is convenient to write elements of

explicitly as pairs
(T,\lambda)
, where

is a maximal split torus of
G
and
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
.
The group

acts on

We define two relations on

(T_{1},\lambda_{1})\sim(T_{2},\lambda_{2})\ \mbox{if there exists $l\in L_{\lambda_{1}}(k)$ such that $T_{2}=lT_{1}l^{-1}$ and $\lambda_{2}=l\cdot\lambda_{1}$}

and

(T_{1},\lambda_{1})\approx(T_{2},\lambda_{2})\ \mbox{if there exists $g\in P_{\lambda_{1}}(k)$ such that $T_{2}=gT_{1}g^{-1}$ and $\lambda_{2}=g\cdot\lambda_{1}$}.

Lemma 3.6
.

With the notation as above, we have the following.

(i)

If
(T_{1},\lambda_{1})\sim(T_{2},\lambda_{2})
then
L_{\lambda_{1}}=L_{\lambda_{2}}
.

(ii)

If
(T_{1},\lambda_{1})\approx(T_{2},\lambda_{2})

.

(iii)

The relations

and

are equivalence relations.

Proof.

Parts (i) and (ii) are a consequence of Lemma
3.3
(ii), and (iii) follows from (i) and (ii).
∎

\displaystyle P=Q

\displaystyle\implies f^{-1}(P)=f^{-1}(Q)\implies((f_{\bar{k}})^{-1}(P_{\bar{k}}))_{\red}=((f_{\bar{k}})^{-1}(Q_{\bar{k}}))_{\red}

\displaystyle\implies(P_{\lambda})_{\bar{k}}=(P_{\mu})_{\bar{k}}\implies P_{\lambda}=P_{\mu}.

A similar argument works for
L_{\lambda}
and
L_{\mu}
. The final assertion now follows from part (i).
∎

3.4.
Apartments

It follows from Remark
3.10
that the restriction of
\phi_{G}
gives rise to a bijection from
Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
onto its image in

V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
;
we denote this image by

and we call this an

of

. The set

inherits the structure of a

-vector space from

.
We denote the common zero of all the apartments by
0
.

Remark 3.18
.

Our new notation has introduced a potential inconsistency — given a split torus
T
in
G
, we have two objects labelled

: the apartment of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
corresponding to
T
, and the vector edifice of
T
as a
k
-group in its own right.
It is not hard to see that these two objects are essentially the same, but we leave a proper discussion of this until
we have introduced linear maps below; see Remark
4.12
.

Lemma 3.19
.

The map
T\mapsto V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
gives a bijection between the set of maximal split tori in
G
and the set of apartments in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Proof.

The given map is surjective by definition, so we just need to show that it is injective. Suppose
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are maximal split tori in
G
,
and suppose that
V_{T_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})=V_{T_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Then for all
\lambda\in Y_{T_{1}}
there exists
\mu\in Y_{T_{2}}
such that
(T_{1},\lambda)\approx(T_{2},\mu)
.
This means that
T_{2}\subseteq P_{\lambda}
for every
\lambda\in Y_{T_{1}}
,
and hence
T_{2}\subseteq P_{\lambda}\cap P_{-\lambda}=L_{\lambda}
for every
\lambda\in Y_{T_{1}}
.
Hence
T_{2}
commutes with every cocharacter of
T_{1}
, which implies that
T_{2}
commutes with
T_{1}
,
and we conclude that

, by maximality of

and
T_{2}
.
∎

3.5.
Common apartments, addition, opposites

As detailed in the introduction, the structures we have just defined are generalisations to arbitrary

However, we cannot import all of the structure/properties we see in the reductive case to the case of arbitrary

Remark 3.21

In particular, taking

Example 3.22
.

Let

The preceding discussion motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.23
.

We say that

has the

In fact,

Returning to the example of the Borel subgroup

in Example

,
the only pseudo-parabolic subgroup of

An immediate consequence of this lack of common apartment is that there is no obvious addition law on

One can, however, define addition when the two points one considers live in a common apartment.

Lemma 3.24
.

(i)

Let
T
be a maximal split torus of
G

are contained in the common apartment

Since

is a vector space, there is an element

.

is independent of the choice of apartment containing

and
y
.

(ii)

Let
x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and let

. The element

is independent of the choice of apartment containing
x
.

Proof.

(i). The common apartments containing
x
and
y
correspond to the maximal split tori of
G
contained in the intersection
P_{x}\cap P_{y}
(Lemma
3.20
). By hypothesis, there is at least one such

. Let
T^{\prime}
be another. By Lemma
2.11
,

is smooth, so there exists
g\in P_{x}(k)\cap P_{y}(k)
such that
gTg^{-1}=T^{\prime}
. Choose
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
x=\phi_{G}(\lambda)
and
y=\phi_{G}(\mu)
. Then
x=\phi_{G}(g\cdot\lambda)
and
y=\phi_{G}(g\cdot\mu)
.
Fix a
G
-equivariant embedding of
G
in a rational
G
-module
V
, and let
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\mu^{\prime}
be cocharacter approximations (with respect to

to
\lambda
and

Then

is a cocharacter approximation to

, and so

.
Since

and

, we see that

(ii). Let

be maximal split tori of

such that

to be a subset of the form

of

3.7.
The combinatorial edifice

Definition 3.35
.

We define the

to be the poset formed by the R-parabolic subgroups of

Given

,
\sigma_{\lambda}

\sigma_{Q}\leq\sigma_{P}

.
We let

The elements of the combinatorial edifice can be realised geometrically inside

:
given an R-parabolic subgroup

of

\widetilde{\sigma}_{P}=\{x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\mid P_{x}=P\}.

(We also define
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\lambda}:=\widetilde{\sigma}_{P_{\lambda}}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}:=\widetilde{\sigma}_{P_{x}}
.) By choosing a maximal split torus

of

as a subset of

(Lemma

.

Let
x,y\in\widetilde{\sigma}_{P}

ax+by\in\widetilde{\sigma}_{P}
for all
a,b\in{\mathbb{K}}^{+}
.

Proof.

Fix a maximal split torus
T
of
P
. We can choose
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
x=\phi_{G}(\lambda)
and
y=\phi_{G}(\mu)
; then
P_{\lambda}=P_{\mu}=P
. Recall that
P_{\nu}=\rho^{-1}(V_{\nu,\geq 0})
for any
\nu\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
(Remark
3.4
(iii)). It follows that
P_{a\lambda}=P_{\lambda}
and
P_{b\mu}=P_{\mu}
for any

. So it is enough to show that
P_{\lambda+\mu}=P
.

Fix a
G
-equivariant embedding
\rho^{\prime}
of
G
in a rational

-module
V^{\prime}
. Let

be the rational
G
-module
V^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
and let
\rho
be

followed by the obvious inclusion of

in

. Since

for any

, the argument of the first part of the proof of Lemma

shows that
P_{\lambda+\mu}\supseteq P
.

Let
\nu\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
and let

be a cocharacter approximation to

\Lie(P_{\nu})=\Lie(P_{\nu^{\prime}})=\mathfrak{g}_{\nu^{\prime},\geq 0}=\mathfrak{g}_{\nu,\geq 0},

where the middle equality is from
[

. The argument of the second part of the proof of Lemma

shows that

∎

It is well known that if

is semisimple then

is a spherical building; in particular, it is a simplicial complex. The same is true for reductive

For some of the later work (especially in Section
5
) it is important to consider maps between edifices for algebraic groups defined over possibly different fields,
so that is how our definitions are framed in Section
4.1
.

4.1.
Definition and first properties

Definition 4.1
.

Suppose
V_{1}=V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and
V_{2}=V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
are two vector edifices,
where
G
is a
k
-group and
H
is a
k^{\prime}
-group for two (possibly different) fields
k
and
k^{\prime}
.
A function
\kappa\colon V_{1}\to V_{2}
is called a
linear map of vector edifices

if for every apartment
A_{1}
of
V_{1}
, there exists an apartment
A_{2}
of
V_{2}
such that
\kappa(A_{1})\subseteq A_{2}
and
\kappa|_{A_{1}}\colon A_{1}\to A_{2}
is a
{\mathbb{K}}
-linear map.

It is immediate that the composition of linear maps is a linear map.

Remark 4.2
.

The reader is warned that the inverse of a bijective linear map of vector edifices is
not
necessarily itself a linear map.
The reason for this is that even if
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are in bijection with each other, it might not be the case that their systems of apartments are in bijection with each other.
See Example
4.14
below.

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
V_{1}=V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and
V_{2}=V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
are two vector edifices as above.
A linear map
\kappa\colon V_{1}\to V_{2}
is an
isomorphism of vector edifices

if it is bijective and the inverse map is also a linear map of vector edifices. If
V_{1}=V_{2}
then we call
\kappa
an
automorphism of vector edifices
. We write
\Aut(V)
for the group of automorphisms of a vector edifice
V
.

In practice, we often wish to construct linear maps of vector edifices from corresponding maps on cocharacters.
The following result shows that this process can be reversed.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G
be a
k
-group and
H
a
k^{\prime}
-group for two fields
k
and
k^{\prime}
.
Let
\kappa\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
be a linear map.
Then
\kappa
lifts uniquely to a map

\widehat{\kappa}\colon Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{H}({\mathbb{K}})

with the following property: for any maximal split torus
T
of
G
and for any maximal split torus
S
of
H
such that
\kappa(V_{T}({\mathbb{K}}))\subseteq V_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
, we have
\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}}))\subseteq Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
, and the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi_{G}}
\scriptstyle{\widehat{\kappa}}
\textstyle{Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi_{H}}
\textstyle{V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\kappa}
\textstyle{V_{S}({\mathbb{K}})}

Proof.

First off, it follows from Remark
3.10
(ii) that
\widehat{\kappa}
is unique if it exists.

Let
\lambda\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, and set
x=\phi_{G}(\lambda)
and
y=\phi_{G}(-\lambda)
.
Since
\kappa
is linear on apartments,
\kappa(x)+\kappa(y)=\kappa(x+y)=\kappa(0)=0
, so
\kappa(x)
and
\kappa(y)
are opposite points in (the corresponding apartment of)
V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
.
By Lemma
3.27
we can find a unique
\mu\in Y_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
\phi_{H}(\mu)=\kappa(x)
,
\phi_{H}(-\mu)=\kappa(y)
.
We set
\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda):=\mu
.

Now let
T
be a maximal split torus of
G
such that
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
and let
S
be any maximal split torus of
H
such that
\kappa(V_{T}({\mathbb{K}}))\subseteq V_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
. Set
x=\phi_{G}(\lambda)
and
y=\phi_{G}(-\lambda)
. Since
\kappa(x),\kappa(y)\in V_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
by construction and
\kappa(x)+\kappa(y)=0
, there exists a unique
\nu\in Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
\phi_{H}(\nu)=\kappa(x)
and
\phi_{H}(-\nu)=\kappa(y)
.
We see that
\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda)=\mu=\nu\in Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
, so that
\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}}))\subseteq Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
, and the commutativity of the given diagram follows.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

It is immediate from the construction that
\widehat{\kappa}
restricts to a linear map from each
Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
to the corresponding
Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
.
We have obvious functoriality:
\widehat{\rm id}={\rm id}
and
\widehat{\tau\circ\kappa}=\widehat{\tau}\circ\widehat{\kappa}
.

4.2.
Linear maps induced by group homomorphisms

Let
f\colon G\to H
be a homomorphism of connected
k
-groups. We show that
f
gives rise to a linear map

\kappa_{f}\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
T
be a maximal split torus of
G
and let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be maximal split tori of
H
such that
f(T)\subseteq S_{1}\cap S_{2}
.
Let
f_{1}\colon T\to S_{1}
and
f_{2}\colon T\to S_{2}
be the maps induced by
f
.
Let
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
and set
\lambda_{1}=(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda)
,
\lambda_{2}=(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda)
.
Then
(S_{1},\lambda_{1})\sim(S_{2},\lambda_{2})
.

Proof.

By Remark
3.4
(ii),
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are maximal split tori of
L_{\lambda_{1}}
,
and hence
lS_{1}l^{-1}=S_{2}
for some
l\in L_{\lambda_{1}}(k)
. The conjugation map
\Inn_{l}
gives rise to a linear map
\omega
from
Y_{S_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})
to
Y_{S_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
, and we have
\omega\circ(f_{1})_{*}=(f_{2})_{*}
by functoriality (Section
3.1
). The result follows.
∎

For each maximal split torus
T
of
G
, choose a maximal split torus
S_{T}
of
H
such that
f(T)\subseteq S_{T}
.
The maps
f_{*}\colon Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{S_{T}}({\mathbb{K}})
for each
T
give rise to a map from
\bigsqcup_{T}Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
to
\bigsqcup_{S}Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
. Consider the compositions
\bigsqcup_{T}Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to\bigsqcup_{S}Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varpi_{H}}}{{\to}}Y_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
and
\bigsqcup_{T}V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to\bigsqcup_{S}V_{S}({\mathbb{K}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\omega_{H}}}{{\to}}V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
. By Lemma
4.6
, these maps do not depend on the choices of the maximal split tori
S_{T}
for each
T
.

Proposition 4.7
.

The maps above descend to give well-defined maps
Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
and

\kappa_{f}\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{H}({\mathbb{K}}).

Moreover,
\kappa_{f}
is a linear map of vector edifices.

Proof.

We give the proof for
\kappa_{f}
.
Suppose
(T_{1},\lambda_{1})\approx(T_{2},\lambda_{2})
for
(T_{1},\lambda_{1}),(T_{2},\lambda_{2})\in\bigsqcup_{T}Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Then
T_{2}=gT_{1}g^{-1}
and
g\cdot\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
for some
g\in P_{\lambda_{1}}(k)
. Let
f_{i}\colon T_{i}\to S_{T_{i}}
be the map induced by
f
. It is enough to prove that
(S_{T_{1}},(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{1}))\approx(S_{T_{2}},(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2}))
.
By Lemma
4.6
, there is no harm in taking
S_{T_{2}}
to be
f(g)S_{T_{1}}f(g)^{-1}
.
Observe
that this means that
f_{2}\circ\Inn_{g}=\Inn_{f(g)}\circ f_{1}
.
It follows from functoriality
that

f(g)\cdot(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{1})=(f_{2})_{*}(g\cdot\lambda_{1})=(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2}).

Since
f(g)
belongs to
P_{(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{1})(H)}(k)
by Lemma
3.17
(i), we obtain the desired result.
It is also clear that
\kappa_{f}
is linear — for each maximal split torus
T
of
G
, the corresponding map
f_{*}\colon Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{S_{T}}({\mathbb{K}})

is
{\mathbb{K}}
-linear.

The proof for the map
Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
is very similar: it just uses a conjugating element
l\in L_{\lambda_{1}}(k)
instead.
∎

Remarks 4.8
.

(i). If
\lambda\in Y_{G}
then
\widehat{\kappa_{f}}(\lambda)=f\circ\lambda
. It is clear that
\kappa_{{\rm id}_{G}}={\rm id}_{V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})}
and if
f\colon G\to H
and
l\colon H\to K
are homomorphisms of connected
k
-groups then
\kappa_{l\circ f}=\kappa_{l}\circ\kappa_{f}
. In particular, if
f\in\Aut(G)
then
\kappa_{f}
belongs to
\Aut(V_{G}({\mathbb{K}}))
and has inverse
\kappa_{f^{-1}}
.

(ii). Let
f\colon G\to H
be a surjective homomorphism of connected
k
-groups and let
x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. It follows from Lemma
3.17
(i) that
P_{\kappa_{f}(x)}=f(P_{x})
.

Example 4.9
.

Let
g\in G(k)
. Untangling the definitions, we see that
\kappa_{\Inn_{g}}(x)
is just
g\cdot x
in the sense of Section
3.3
. By Remark
4.8
(i),
\kappa_{\Inn_{g}}
is invertible with inverse
\kappa_{\Inn_{g^{-1}}}
. Remark
4.8
(ii) implies that
P_{g\cdot x}=P_{\kappa_{\Inn_{g}}(x)}=\Inn_{g}(P_{x})=gP_{x}g^{-1}
.

Remark 4.10
.

Let
f\colon G\to H
be a homomorphism. Then
\kappa_{f}
is equivariant in the following sense: if
x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and
g\in G(k)
then
\kappa_{f}(g\cdot x)=f(g)\cdot\kappa_{f}(x)
. To see this, note that if
\lambda\in Y_{G}
and
g\in G(k)
then
f\circ(g\cdot\lambda)=f(g)\cdot(f\circ\lambda)
; the result follows from linearity. This shows that
\kappa_{f}
is type-preserving (see Definition
3.30
).

4.3.
Homomorphisms which induce injective linear maps

We can now show that inclusions of
k
-groups give inclusions of vector edifices. In fact, we can do better.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
f\colon G\to H
be a homomorphism of connected
k
-groups with finite kernel.
Then the maps
Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
and
\kappa_{f}\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
are injective.

Proof.

Again, we give the proof for
\kappa_{f}
; the other proof is very similar.
Let
(T_{1},\lambda_{1}),(T_{2},\lambda_{2})\in\bigsqcup_{T}Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
be such that

(S_{T_{1}},f_{*}(\lambda_{1}))\approx(S_{T_{2}},f_{*}(\lambda_{2}))
. Let
f_{i}\colon T_{i}\to S_{T_{i}}
be the map induced by
f
for each
i
.
Then
P_{(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{1})}(H)=P_{(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2})}(H)
, so
P_{(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{1})}(f(G))=P_{(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2})}(f(G))
by Corollary
3.16
, and hence by Lemma
3.17
(i) we have
f(P_{\lambda_{1}})=f(P_{\lambda_{2}})
. It follows from Lemma
3.17
(ii) (applied to the surjective homomorphism
G\to f(G)
) that

P_{\lambda_{1}}=P_{\lambda_{2}}
. Thus
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are maximal split tori of
P_{\lambda_{1}}
, which means we can find

g\in P_{\lambda_{1}}(k)
such that
gT_{2}g^{-1}=T_{1}
.
Since
f(g)\in P_{(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{1})}(H)(k)=P_{(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2})}(H)(k)
, we have
P_{(f_{2})_{*}(g\cdot\lambda_{2})}(H)=P_{f(g)\cdot(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2})}(H)=f(g)P_{(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2})}(H)f(g)^{-1}=P_{(f_{2})_{*}(\lambda_{2})}(H)
by Remark
4.10
and Lemma
3.3
(ii). Hence, replacing
\lambda_{2}
with
g\cdot\lambda_{2}
, we may assume that
T_{1}=T_{2}
.
Now, since
f
has finite kernel, the map
(f_{1})_{*}
is injective on
Y_{T_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})
, as observed in Section
3.1
.
Since we have reduced to the case that
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in Y_{T_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})
with

(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{1})=(f_{1})_{*}(\lambda_{2})
, we conclude that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

We can now resolve the potential ambiguity noted in Remark
3.18
above.
If
T
is a maximal split torus of
G
then the notation
V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
could mean the corresponding apartment of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
or it could mean the vector edifice associated to
T
in its own right. But we see that if
i\colon T\to G
is the inclusion then
\kappa_{i}
gives a linear isomorphism from the latter object to the former, so the abuse of notation is harmless.
Indeed, this observation works for any subgroup
H
of
G
— we may identify the vector edifice
V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
as a subobject of the vector edifice
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
via the injective linear map
\kappa_{i}\colon V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
arising from the inclusion
i\colon H\to G
.
We often do this without further comment in the rest of the paper.

Remark 4.13
.

If
\lambda\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
then we may regard
\lambda
as an element of
Y_{L_{\lambda}}({\mathbb{K}})
and
x:=\phi_{G}(\lambda)
as an element of
V_{L_{\lambda}}({\mathbb{K}})
and
V_{P_{\lambda}}({\mathbb{K}})
in the obvious way.
Since all maximal split tori in
L_{\lambda}
are
L_{\lambda}(k)
-conjugate, it follows that if
T
is a maximal split torus of
L_{\lambda}
then
x\in V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Example 4.14
.

Suppose that
G
is such that
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
has the common apartment property, and
P
is an R-parabolic subgroup of
G
.
Then the inclusion
i\colon P\to G
actually induces a
bijection

\kappa_{i}\colon V_{P}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
To see this, note that for every maximal split torus
T
of
G
and every
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
, there is a maximal split torus
S_{T,\lambda}
contained in
P_{\lambda}\cap P
, because of the common apartment property,
and hence
(T,\lambda)\approx(S_{T,\lambda},\mu)
, where
\mu=g\cdot\lambda
for
g\in P_{\lambda}(k)
conjugating
T
to
S_{T,\lambda}
.
Therefore, if we let
x=\phi_{P}(\mu)\in V_{P}({\mathbb{K}})
and
y=\phi_{G}(\lambda)\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, we have
\kappa_{i}(x)=y
.
Thus
\kappa_{i}
is surjective, and we already know it is injective by Proposition
4.11
. Note that, even though
\kappa_{i}
is a bijective linear map of vector edifices, it is
not
an isomorphism in general: e.g., in the setting of Example
3.22
we have points
x_{1},x_{2}\in V_{P}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
do not lie in a common apartment of
V_{P}({\mathbb{K}})
, but
\kappa_{i}(x_{1})
and
\kappa_{i}(x_{2})
do lie in a common apartment of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, because
G
is reductive in that case (and hence
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
has the common apartment property).

4.4.
Homomorphisms which induce surjective linear maps

Next we
investigate the conditions under which a homomorphism
f\colon G\to H
gives rise to a surjective linear map. It turns out that the obvious condition (that
f
is surjective) is not sufficient; this situation is made more complicated by, for example, the existence over imperfect fields of inseparable isogenies. This motivates the following definition. See also Remark
4.17
(i) below.

Definition 4.15
.

Suppose
f\colon G\to H
is a homomorphism of connected
k
-groups.
We say
f
is
apte
if every maximal split torus
S
of
H
has the form
S=f(T)
for a maximal split torus
T
of
G
.

With the key definition in hand, the following result is easy.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
f\colon G\to H
be an apte homomorphism of connected
k
-groups. Then
\kappa_{f}
is surjective.

Proof.

Let
S
be a maximal split torus of
H
. The assumption that
f
is apte means we can find a maximal split torus
T
of
G
with
S=f(T)
. Let
h\colon T\to S
be restriction of
f
. Since the map of character groups
h^{*}\colon X_{S}\to X_{T}
is injective, the dual map
h_{*}\colon Y_{T}\to Y_{S}
has finite cokernel. This implies that
f_{*}\colon Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{S}({\mathbb{K}})
is surjective, and the result follows.
∎

Remarks 4.17
.

(i). The lemma does not hold without the assumption that
f
is apte. For a concrete example, let
G=H=\SL_{2}
over an imperfect field
k
in characteristic

, and let

be the standard Frobenius map which squares the entries of

That

.
Now let

The torus

.

Suppose

We finish with some results related to the common apartment property.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
N
be a connected normal unipotent subgroup of
G
and let
\pi\colon G\to G/N
be the canonical projection. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Then
\kappa_{\pi}(x_{1})=\kappa_{\pi}(x_{2})
if and only if
x_{2}=n\cdot x_{1}
for some
n\in N(k)
.

Proof.

If
n\in N(k)
and
y=n\cdot x
then
\kappa_{\pi}(y)=\kappa_{\pi}(n\cdot x)=\pi(n)\cdot\kappa_{\pi}(x)=\kappa_{\pi}(x)
where the second equality is from Remark
4.10
. Conversely, suppose
\kappa_{\pi}(x_{1})=\kappa_{\pi}(x_{2})
; call this common value
y
. For each
i
, let
G_{i}=P_{x_{i}}N
, let
Q_{i}=\pi(G_{i})
and let
\pi_{i}\colon G_{i}\to Q_{i}
be the restriction of
\pi
. Then
\pi_{i}
is surjective, so
Q_{i}=\pi(P_{x_{i}})=P_{y}(G/N)
by Remark
4.8
(ii).

Choose a maximal split torus
T
of
G/N
such that

. Then
T
is a maximal split torus of
P_{y}(G/N)
by Remark
3.21
. For each

, since
\pi_{i}
is smooth,
\pi_{i}
is apte (Remark
4.17

T_{i}
of
G_{i}
such that
\pi(T_{i})=T
. So
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are maximal split tori of
H:=T_{1}N\cong T_{1}\ltimes N
. Hence there exists

such that
T_{2}=nT_{1}n^{-1}
. Now

belongs to

for each

, so

and

belong to

. We have
\kappa_{\pi}(n\cdot x_{1})=\kappa_{\pi}(x_{2})=y
by Remark
4.10
. But
\pi
induces an isomorphism from

onto
T

Lemma 4.24
.

Let

5.
Linear maps arising from base change

Let
\alpha\colon k\to k^{\prime}
be a homomorphism of fields — for example,
\alpha

k^{\prime}/k
— and let

be a

Base change along

gives rise to a

-group

from the functorial point of view, given a

-algebra

{{}^{\alpha}}G(A^{\prime})=G(A^{\prime}\otimes_{\alpha}k)
, which makes sense

Note that when

Identifying a point

\mathrm{Spec}(k^{\prime})\to{{}^{\alpha}}G

{{}^{\alpha}}x\in{{}^{\alpha}}G(k^{\prime})

The corresponding map

\psi_{\alpha}\colon G(k)\to{{}^{\alpha}}G(k^{\prime}),\quad x\mapsto{{}^{\alpha}}x

{{}^{\alpha}}G
in the rational
{{}^{\alpha}}G
-module
{{}^{\alpha}}V
. The weights of
{{}^{\alpha}}V
with respect to
{{}^{\alpha}}T
are the characters of the form
{{}^{\alpha}}\chi
, where
\chi
runs over the set of weights of
V
with respect to
T
. We see that if
\lambda^{\prime}
is a cocharacter approximation to
\lambda
with respect to
V
then
{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda^{\prime}
is a cocharacter approximation to
{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda
with respect to
{}^{\alpha}V
. We have
P_{{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda}={{}^{\alpha}}P_{\lambda}
,
L_{{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda}={{}^{\alpha}}L_{\lambda}
and
U_{{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda}={{}^{\alpha}}U_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
;
this holds if
\lambda\in Y_{G}
by
[
14
, Lem. 2.1.5]
and the discussion that precedes it, and for arbitrary
\lambda
we get the result by taking cocharacter approximations. It follows that
\psi_{\alpha}(P_{\lambda}(k))\subseteq P_{{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda}(k^{\prime})
,
\psi_{\alpha}(L_{\lambda}(k))\subseteq L_{{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda}(k^{\prime})
and
\psi_{\alpha}(U_{\lambda}(k))\subseteq U_{{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda}(k^{\prime})
for all
\lambda\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
\alpha\colon k\to k^{\prime}
is a homomorphism of fields.
Then the map
\lambda\mapsto{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda
gives rise to well-defined injective maps

Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to Y_{{{}^{\alpha}}G}({\mathbb{K}})
and
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{{{}^{\alpha}}G}({\mathbb{K}})
, and the latter map is a linear map of vector edifices.

Proof.

Since we have already observed bijectivity and linearity at the level of apartments above, the essential point is well-definedness.
What we need follows from functoriality of base change:
if
T_{1},T_{2}
are maximal split tori of
G
and
g\in G(k)
is such that
gT_{1}g^{-1}=T_{2}
, then we have

\psi_{\alpha}(g)({{}^{\alpha}}T_{1})\psi_{\alpha}(g)^{-1}={{}^{\alpha}}T_{2}
.
Similarly, if
g\cdot\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
for
\lambda_{1}\in Y_{T_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})
and
\lambda_{2}\in Y_{T_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
,
then
\psi_{\alpha}(g)\cdot{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda_{1}={{}^{\alpha}}\lambda_{2}
.
We can now check that the map
\lambda\mapsto{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda
behaves well with respect to the relations
\sim
and
\approx

before and after base change.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Since it is not necessarily true that every maximal split torus of
{{}^{\alpha}}G
is of the form
{{}^{\alpha}}T
for some maximal split torus
T
of
G

(for example, take
\alpha
a Frobenius endomorphism of a non-perfect field
k
), the maps in Lemma
5.1
are not necessarily surjective.

Definition 5.3
.

Suppose
\alpha\colon k\to k^{\prime}
is a homomorphism of fields, and
G
is a
k
-group.
We denote the corresponding injective linear map of vector edifices by

\nu_{\alpha}:=\nu_{G,\alpha}\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{{{}^{\alpha}}G}({\mathbb{K}}).

We note in particular that this formalism allows us to identify the vector edifice
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
as a subset of the vector edifices
V_{G_{k_{s}}}({\mathbb{K}})
and
V_{G_{\overline{k}}}({\mathbb{K}})
,
which is helpful for later arguments on metrics.

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose
G
and
H
are
k
-groups, and let
\alpha\colon k\to k^{\prime}
be a field homomorphism.
For any homomorphism
f\colon G\to H
, we have
\kappa_{({{}^{\alpha}}f)}\circ\nu_{G,\alpha}=\nu_{H,\alpha}\circ\kappa_{f}
.

Proof.

This is basically just functoriality of base change: it is clear that for
\lambda\in Y_{G}
we have
{{}^{\alpha}}(f\circ\lambda)={{}^{\alpha}}f\circ{{}^{\alpha}}\lambda
,
and the result follows by chasing through the definitions.
∎

5.2.
Field automorphisms and vector edifice automorphisms

First observe that if
\alpha\colon k\to k^{\prime}
is an isomorphism of fields and
G
is a
k
-group, then the linear maps
\nu_{G,\alpha}
and
\nu_{{{}^{\alpha}}G,\alpha^{-1}}

are inverses of each other, and hence
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and
V_{{{}^{\alpha}}G}({\mathbb{K}})
are isomorphic.

We also wish to consider the possibility that
{{}^{\alpha}}G
and
G
are isomorphic as
k
-groups.
For ease of exposition, suppose
\alpha\in\Aut(k)
— i.e.,
\alpha
is given to us as a field
automorphism
— so that

G
and
{{}^{\alpha}}G
are both
k
-groups.
Then if there exists an isomorphism
f\colon{{}^{\alpha}}G\to G
,
we obtain a linear automorphism

\kappa_{\alpha,f}:=\kappa_{f}\circ\nu_{\alpha}

of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.

We discuss this further in
[
9
]
. Here we give two examples of this construction, showing how it captures naturally occurring phenomena.

Example 5.5
.

Suppose
\alpha\in\Aut(k)
and let
k^{\alpha}
be the fixed field of
\alpha
.
Suppose that
G
has a
k^{\alpha}
-descent — that is, that
G=H_{k}
for some
k^{\alpha}
-group
H
.
Then we claim that
{{}^{\alpha}}G
and
G
are
naturally
isomorphic as
k
-groups via a homomorphism
f_{\alpha}
(say).
To see this, note that for a
k
-algebra
A
, when we base change and form the algebra
A\otimes_{\alpha}k
(recall, the
k
-multiplication happens in the second factor),
we have an isomorphism of these two as
k^{\alpha}
-algebras
A\otimes_{\alpha}k\mapsto A
given by
a\otimes 1\to a
.
This gives rise to the corresponding natural isomorphisms

f_{\alpha}(A)\colon{{}^{\alpha}}G(A)=G(A\otimes_{\alpha}k)=H(A\otimes_{\alpha}k)\to H(A)=G(A)

for each
k
-algebra
A
, defining the isomorphism
f_{\alpha}
.

A particular instance of this set-up is if
k/k_{0}
is a Galois extension,
and
G
is a
k
-group for which we can fix a
k_{0}
-descent
G_{0}
.
Then we obtain from the various elements
\kappa_{\alpha,f_{\alpha}}

an action of the Galois group
\Gamma=\Gal(k/k_{0})
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Moreover, the set of fixed points
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})^{\Gamma}
naturally identifies
with the vector edifice
V_{G_{0}}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Example 5.6
.

Suppose
k
is a perfect field of characteristic
p
, so that the Frobenius map
\alpha_{p}\colon k\to k,x\mapsto x^{p}
is a field automorphism.
Let
G=\GL_{n}
be the general linear group over
k
.
Then this is a situation as in the previous example, where the group
{{}^{\alpha}}G
is naturally isomorphic to
G
, say via a map
f_{p}
,
so we obtain an automorphism of the vector building
\kappa_{p}:=\kappa_{\alpha_{p},f_{p}}
.
We also have the apte homomorphism
F_{p}\colon G\to G
which is the standard Frobenius morphism raising matrix entries to the
p^{\rm th}
power
(i.e., acting by
\alpha_{p}
in each coordinate), which gives rise to a linear automorphism
\kappa_{F_{p}}
of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
We can compare the effects of
\kappa_{p}
and
\kappa_{F_{p}}
by looking at the standard diagonal maximal torus
T
.

First, it is clear
\kappa_{F_{p}}(\lambda)=p\lambda
for all
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Second, because the natural isomorphism
f_{p}\colon{{}^{\alpha}}G\to G
is induced by the maps on
A
-points
G(A\otimes_{\alpha_{p}}k)\to G(A)
given by
a\otimes 1\mapsto a
in each coordinate (as described in the previous example), the net effect on a diagonal cocharacter after precomposing with base change along
\alpha_{p}
is to do nothing! Note (and this is important) that we are
not
claiming that
\kappa_{p}
is the identity map on all of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
For a general split maximal torus
T^{\prime}
, there exists
g\in G(k)
such that
T^{\prime}=gTg^{-1}
.
Then for
x\in V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
,
\kappa_{F_{p}}
sends
g\cdot x\in V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
to
F_{p}(g)\cdot(px)\in V_{F_{p}(g)TF_{p}(g)^{-1}}({\mathbb{K}})
, by Remark
4.10
.
On the other hand, it can be shown that
\kappa_{p}
sends
g\cdot x
to
F_{p}(g)\cdot x
— the key point is that for
g\in\GL_{n}(k)
, applying

f_{p}\circ\psi_{\alpha_{p}}
has the same effect as applying
F_{p}
.
Thus we see that both
\kappa_{F_{p}}
and
\kappa_{p}
permute the apartments of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
,
in the same way, but
\kappa_{F_{p}}
also scales by
p
.

Finally, we note that if
H
is a subgroup of
\GL_{n}
such that
F_{p}(H)=H
, then this conclusion naturally extends to the
corresponding linear maps on
V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
given by restriction from
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.

We finish this section by recalling that in the case where
G
is a simple
k
-group of rank at least
2
, Tits has shown
[
33
, Cor. 5.9, Cor. 5.10]
that
all automorphisms of the spherical building
\Delta_{G}
can be constructed by considering those induced by automorphisms of the group
G
together
with suitable automorphisms of the field
k
and certain exceptional Frobenius isogenies in characteristics
2
and
3
.
Our constructions in the previous two sections show how to realise all these automorphisms on the level of the edifice
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
as well.

6.
Admissible metrics

In this section we show how to put a metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Recall that two metrics
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
on a set
X
are said to be
bi-Lipschitz equivalent
if there exist
c,C>0
such that
d_{1}(x,y)\leq c\cdot d_{2}(x,y)\leq C\cdot d_{1}(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
; in this case,
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
induce the same topology on
X
. When
G
is reductive then (following
[
20
, Sec. 2]
,
[
7
]
) we can equip
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
with a metric, as follows.
Fix a maximal split torus
T
of
G
.
The space
V_{T}({\mathbb{R}})
can be equipped with a
W_{k}
-invariant metric (this is standard: just take any positive-definite bilinear form on
V_{T}({\mathbb{R}})
, average over the finite group
W_{k}
, and take the metric defined by the associated norm).
Now for any other maximal split torus
T^{\prime}
in
G
, there exists
g\in G(k)
such that
gT^{\prime}g^{-1}=T
— this choice of
g
is not unique, but any two such differ by an element of
W_{k}
, and so we can translate the
W_{k}
-invariant metric on
V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
to
V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
. This allows us to define
d(x,y)
for any
x,y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, since the common apartment property holds. We thus obtain a metric
d
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
with the property that
d(x,y)=d(g\cdot x,g\cdot y)
for all
x,y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
,
g\in G(k)
;
we call such a metric arising from a
W_{k}
-invariant bilinear form on
V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})

admissible
.

As explained in Section
3.5
, for general
G
the vector edifice
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
may fail to have the
common apartment property, so
we cannot immediately metrise
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
using this construction.
However, with Proposition
4.11
in hand, we can make the following definition.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
i\colon G\to G^{\prime}
be an embedding of
G
in a reductive
k
-group
G^{\prime}
.
Choose an admissible metric
d^{\prime}
on
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Recall that the map
\kappa_{i}\colon V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
is injective (Proposition
4.11
).
We obtain a pullback metric
d
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
defined by

d(x,y):=d^{\prime}(\kappa_{i}(x),\kappa_{i}(y))

for all
x,y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
We also denote this metric
by
i^{*}(d^{\prime})
, and call a metric obtained in this way an
admissible metric
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Note that the equivariance of
\kappa_{i}
observed in Remark
4.10
, together with the
G^{\prime}(k)
-invariance of
d^{\prime}
, implies that
d
is
G(k)
-invariant and the restriction of
d
to any apartment of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
is given by a positive-definite bilinear form. An admissible metric
d
gives rise to a topology on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Below we write “open” instead of “
d
-open” if
d
is understood.

Given an admissible metric
d
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
we let
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{d}
denote the corresponding norm:
\|x\|:=d(x,0)
for
x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Remarks 6.2
.

(i). Since any
G
can be embedded into some
\GL_{n}
, admissible metrics always exist.

(ii). It is clear that if
G
is reductive then the two notions of admissible metric coincide.

(iii). Let
H
be a connected subgroup of
G
and let
d
be an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, arising from an embedding of
G
in a reductive group
G^{\prime}
and an admissible metric
d^{\prime}
on
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
. Then the restriction of
d
to
V_{H}({\mathbb{K}})
(regarded as a subset of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
) is admissible: for it is the metric obtained from
d^{\prime}
via the inclusion of
H
in
G^{\prime}
.

(iv). Let
d
be an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{Q}})
. It is easily checked that there is a unique extension of
d
to an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
.

(v). If
d
is an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
arising from an embedding
i:G\to G^{\prime}
and an admissible metric
d^{\prime}
on
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
,
then we have

d(ax,ay)=d^{\prime}(\kappa_{i}(ax),\kappa_{i}(ay))=d^{\prime}(a\kappa_{i}(x),a\kappa_{i}(y))=ad^{\prime}(\kappa_{i}(x),\kappa_{i}(y))=ad(x,y)

for all
a\in{\mathbb{K}}^{+}
and
x,y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
This uses the linearity of
\kappa_{i}
and the fact that the edifice
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
has the common apartment property (because
G^{\prime}
is reductive).

(vi). If
d
is an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, then the subspace topology on any apartment
V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
is just the Euclidean topology induced by the
positive-definite bilinear form obtained by restricting the metric to
V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Remark 6.3
.

Note that since an admissible metric is
G(k)
-invariant, so is the norm arising from it: in particular, if
(x_{n})
is a bounded sequence in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
and
(g_{n})

is a sequence of elements of
G(k)
, then the sequence
(g_{n}\cdot x_{n})
is also bounded. We use this fact often in what follows.

Remark 6.4
.

If
G
has the common apartment property — e.g., if
G
is pseudo-reductive — then any two admissible metrics on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
are bi-Lipschitz equivalent — this follows from the common apartment property, the conjugation-invariance of the metrics and the standard fact that any two positive-definite bilinear forms on a finite-dimensional vector space over
{\mathbb{K}}
induce bi-Lipschitz equivalent metrics. In particular, the induced topology on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
does not depend on the choice of metric. Below we extend these results to arbitrary connected
k
-groups (Proposition
6.21
).

Definition 6.5
.

Given an admissible metric
d
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
, we define
\Isom_{d}(V_{G}({\mathbb{K}}))
to be the subgroup of
\Aut(V_{G}({\mathbb{K}}))
consisting of the isometries with respect to the
metric
d
.

Example 6.6
.

Not every automorphism is an isometry: for a simple example, choose
a\in{\mathbb{K}}^{+}
with
a\neq 1
and take
f_{a}\in\Aut(V_{G}({\mathbb{K}}))
to be the map that sends
x
to
ax
. See also the paragraph following Conjecture
7.4
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Fix an admissible metric
d
on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Let
(x_{n})
be a Cauchy sequence in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
such that all the
x_{n}
have the same type. Then
(x_{n})
is eventually constant.

Proof.

Clearly we can assume without loss that
G
is reductive. Let
x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
,
and define

\epsilon(x)=\inf\{d(x,y)\mid\mbox{$x\neq y$ and $x$ and $y$ have the same type}\}.

Remark
3.31
implies that if we choose an apartment
V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
with
x\in V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
,
then

\inf\{d(x,y)\mid\mbox{$x\neq y\in V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})$ and $x$ and $y$ have the same type}\}>0.

But given any other apartment
V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
containing
x
,
T^{\prime}
is conjugate to
T
by an element fixing
x
by Remark
3.21
,
and so this lower bound does not vary from apartment to apartment containing
x
.
Since
G
has the common apartment property we conclude that
\epsilon(x)>0
.
Further,
\epsilon(x^{\prime})=\epsilon(x)
if
x
and
x^{\prime}
have the same type, as
d
is conjugation-invariant, so
\epsilon(x_{n})
does not depend on
n
.
The result follows.
∎

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
G
be a
k
-group and let
d
be an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Then
(V_{G}({\mathbb{R}}),d)
is a complete metric space.

Proof.

The argument of
[
1
, Prop. 12.10]
establishes this result when
G
is semisimple; our proof is a slight variation on that theme.
By definition, there exist an embedding
i
of
G
in some reductive
k
-group
G^{\prime}
and an admissible metric
d^{\prime}
on
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}})
such that
d=i^{*}(d^{\prime})
.
To ease notation, we regard
V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
as a subset of
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}})
via
\kappa_{i}
, so that
d
is just the restriction of
d^{\prime}
.
Let
(x_{n})
be a Cauchy sequence in
V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Fix a maximal split torus
T
of
G
.
By conjugacy of maximal split tori in
G
, for each
n
we can choose
h_{n}\in G(k)
such that
z_{n}:=h_{n}\cdot x_{n}
belongs to the apartment
V_{T}({\mathbb{R}})
of
V_{G}({\mathbb{R}})
. Note that the sequence
(z_{n})
is bounded, because
(x_{n})
is (see Remark
6.3
). Fix a maximal split torus
S
of
G^{\prime}
such that
T\subseteq S
. Then each
z_{n}
belongs to the apartment
V_{S}({\mathbb{R}})
of
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}})
.
There is no harm in passing to a subsequence of
(x_{n})
, so without loss we can assume that the parabolic subgroups
P_{z_{n}}(G^{\prime})
are all equal by Remark
2.8
.
Since the restriction of
d^{\prime}
to
V_{S}({\mathbb{R}})
yields a complete metric and any closed ball in
V_{S}({\mathbb{R}})
is compact, we can assume after passing to a subsequence again that
(z_{n})
converges to some limit
z\in V_{S}({\mathbb{R}})
. Each
z_{n}
belongs to
V_{T}({\mathbb{R}})
and
V_{T}({\mathbb{R}})
is a subspace of the vector space
V_{S}({\mathbb{R}})
, so
z\in V_{T}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Define a sequence
(y_{n})
in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
by
y_{n}=h_{n}^{-1}\cdot z
. Then
d^{\prime}(x_{n},y_{n})=d^{\prime}(h_{n}\cdot x_{n},h_{n}\cdot y_{n})=d^{\prime}(z_{n},z)\to 0
; hence
(y_{n})
is Cauchy, since
(x_{n})
is. But the
y_{n}
all have the same type as each other as elements of
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
, so
(y_{n})
must eventually become constant by Lemma
6.7
: say,
y_{n}=y
for sufficiently large
n
. We see that
x_{n}\to y
, so we are done.
∎

We now consider admissible metrics on the edifice for a product of groups. Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be
k
-groups. The maximal split tori of
G_{1}\times G_{2}
are precisely the subgroups of the form
T_{1}\times T_{2}
, where
T_{i}
is a maximal split torus of
G_{i}
. There is an obvious linear isomorphism from
V_{T_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})\oplus V_{T_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
to
V_{T_{1}\times T_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
. For
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in V_{T_{1}\times T_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
, we claim that
P_{\lambda}(G_{1}\times G_{2})=P_{\lambda_{1}}(G_{1})\times P_{\lambda_{2}}(G_{2})
,
L_{\lambda}(G_{1}\times G_{2})=L_{\lambda_{1}}(G_{1})\times L_{\lambda_{2}}(G_{2})
and
U_{\lambda}(G_{1}\times G_{2})=U_{\lambda_{1}}(G_{1})\times U_{\lambda_{2}}(G_{2})
. This is clear if
\lambda_{1}\in Y_{T_{1}}
and
\lambda_{2}\in Y_{T_{2}}
. To see this in general, choose a
G_{i}
-equivariant embedding of
G_{i}
in a rational
G_{i}
-module
V_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then embed
G_{1}\times G_{2}
in
V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
in the obvious way. Then
(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})
is a cocharacter approximation to
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
if and only if
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}
is a cocharacter approximation to
\lambda_{i}
for
i=1,2
, and the claim follows. It is now easily checked that the maps
V_{T_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})\oplus V_{T_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{T_{1}\times T_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
paste together to give a bijection from
V_{G_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})\times V_{G_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
to
V_{G_{1}\times G_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Now let
d_{1}=i_{1}^{*}(d_{1}^{\prime})
and
d_{2}=i_{2}^{*}(d_{2}^{\prime})
, where
i_{j}
is an embedding of
G_{j}
in a reductive
k
-group
G_{j}^{\prime}
and
d_{j}^{\prime}
is an admissible metric on
V_{G_{j}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
. We get an admissible metric
d^{\prime}
on
V_{G_{1}^{\prime}\times G_{2}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})=V_{G_{1}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})\times V_{G_{2}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
given by

d^{\prime}((x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}),(y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime}))=\sqrt{d_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime})^{2}+d_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime})^{2}}:

on each apartment
V_{T_{1}^{\prime}\times T_{2}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})=V_{T_{1}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})\oplus V_{T_{2}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
, the positive-definite bilinear form corresponding to
d^{\prime}
is the orthogonal direct sum of the positive-definite bilinear forms on
V_{T_{j}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
corresponding to
d_{j}^{\prime}
for
j=1,2
. We have an embedding
i_{1}\times i_{2}
of
G_{1}\times G_{2}
in
G_{1}^{\prime}\times G_{2}^{\prime}
and we define an admissible metric
d
on
V_{G_{1}\times G_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
by
d=(i_{1}\times i_{2})^{*}(d^{\prime})
. We write
d=d_{1}\times d_{2}
and we call a metric of this form a
product metric
. We have

d((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))=\sqrt{d_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})^{2}+d_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})^{2}}

for all
(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in V_{G_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})\times V_{G_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})=V_{G_{1}\times G_{2}}({\mathbb{K}})
.

6.1.
Central tori

Suppose
G
has a non-trivial split central torus
Z
. We need an argument for reducing from
G
to
G/Z
. Let
d
be an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Then there exist an embedding
i
of
G
in a reductive group
G^{\prime}
, and an admissible metric
d^{\prime}
on
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
d=i^{*}(d^{\prime})
. Set
Z^{\prime}=i(Z)
. We can replace
G^{\prime}
with the reductive group
C_{G^{\prime}}(Z^{\prime})
if necessary, so without loss we can assume that
Z^{\prime}
is central in
G^{\prime}
.

Now let
T^{\prime}
be any maximal split torus of
G^{\prime}
. Since
T^{\prime}
contains
Z^{\prime}
, we may regard
V_{Z^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
as a subspace of
V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
. Set
W_{T^{\prime}}=V_{Z^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})^{\perp}
, where
⟂
denotes the orthogonal complement corresponding to the bilinear form that defines
d^{\prime}
. Given
x^{\prime}\in V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
, we have a unique decomposition
x^{\prime}=x^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}+x^{\prime}_{\perp}
, where
x^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}\in V_{Z^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
and
x^{\prime}_{\perp}\in W_{T^{\prime}}
.
Note that since any other maximal split torus
S^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
with
x^{\prime}\in V_{S^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
is conjugate to
T^{\prime}
by an element of
G^{\prime}(k)
fixing
x^{\prime}
(essentially by definition of
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
), and
Z^{\prime}
is central in
G^{\prime}
, the elements
x^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}
and
x^{\prime}_{\perp}
do not depend on the choice of
T^{\prime}
with
x^{\prime}\in V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Given
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
, we define
d^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}},y^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}})
and
d^{\prime}_{\perp}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{\perp},y^{\prime}_{\perp})
. For any
g^{\prime}\in G^{\prime}(k)
,
g^{\prime}
fixes
V_{Z^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
pointwise (since it fixes
Y_{Z^{\prime}}
pointwise), so
W_{g^{\prime}T^{\prime}(g^{\prime})^{-1}}=g^{\prime}\cdot W_{T^{\prime}}
and for any
x^{\prime}\in V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
,

(6.9)

(g^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime})_{Z^{\prime}}=g^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}=x^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}\ \mbox{\ and\ }\ (g^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime})_{\perp}=g^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}_{\perp}.

It follows easily that
d^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}
and
d^{\prime}_{\perp}
give well-defined
G^{\prime}(k)
-invariant functions from
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})\times V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. We define
d_{Z}(x,y)=d^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}(\kappa_{i}(x),\kappa_{i}(y))
and
d_{\perp}(x,y)=d^{\prime}_{\perp}(\kappa_{i}(x),\kappa_{i}(y))
for
x,y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. We also have
d(x,y)=d^{\prime}(\kappa_{i}(x),\kappa_{i}(y))
, by definition, so we can conclude that

(6.10)

d(x,y)=\sqrt{d_{Z}(x,y)^{2}+d_{\perp}(x,y)^{2}}\ \mbox{for all $x,y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})$},

because the corresponding equation holds for
d^{\prime}
,
d^{\prime}_{Z^{\prime}}
,
d^{\prime}_{\perp}
and for all
\kappa_{i}(x),\kappa_{i}(y)\in V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Note that
d
and
d_{Z}
agree on
V_{Z}({\mathbb{K}})\times V_{Z}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Now let
G_{1}=G/Z
and let
G_{1}^{\prime}=G^{\prime}/Z^{\prime}
. Let
\pi\colon G\to G_{1}
and
\pi^{\prime}\colon G^{\prime}\to G_{1}^{\prime}
denote the canonical projections. Then
i
gives rise to an embedding
i_{1}
of
G_{1}
in
G_{1}^{\prime}
. Choose an admissible metric
d_{1}^{\prime}
on
V_{G_{1}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
and let
d_{1}=i_{1}^{*}(d_{1}^{\prime})
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let the notation be as above. Then there exist
c,C>0
such that

(6.12)

d_{1}(\kappa_{\pi}(x),\kappa_{\pi}(y))\leq c\cdot d_{\perp}(x,y)\leq C\cdot d_{1}(\kappa_{\pi}(x),\kappa_{\pi}(y))

for all
x,y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that there exist
c,C>0
such that

(6.13)

d^{\prime}_{1}(\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}),\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}(y^{\prime}))\leq c\cdot d^{\prime}_{\perp}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq C\cdot d^{\prime}_{1}(\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}),\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}(y^{\prime}))

for all
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
. So fix a maximal split torus
T_{1}^{\prime}
of
G_{1}^{\prime}
. Since
\pi^{\prime}
is smooth and surjective,
\pi^{\prime}
is apte by Remark
4.17
(iii), so there is a maximal split torus
T^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
such that
\pi^{\prime}(T^{\prime})=T_{1}^{\prime}
. It is straightforward to check that
\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}
gives an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector spaces from
V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})/V_{Z^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
to
V_{T_{1}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
, so
\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}
gives an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector spaces from

to

. Since any two positive-definite bilinear forms on a vector space yield equivalent norms, there exist
c,C>0
such that (

) holds for all

. It follows from the

-invariance of

and

that the same

and

work for any maximal split torus of

. This gives the result.
∎

Consider the product group
G_{1}\times Z
. The maximal split tori of

are precisely the subgroups of the form

, where
T_{1}
is a maximal split torus of
G_{1}
. Keeping the notation above, we endow

with the product metric

, where

is the restriction of
d
to
V_{Z}({\mathbb{K}})
. Given a maximal split torus

of
G_{1}
, pick a maximal split torus

of
G
such that
\pi(T)=T_{1}
. Since

contains

,

is in fact unique. Define

\beta_{Z,T}\colon V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\to V_{T_{1}}({\mathbb{K}})\times V_{Z}({\mathbb{K}})=V_{T_{1}\times Z}({\mathbb{K}})

by
\beta_{Z,T}(x)=(\kappa_{\pi}(x),x_{Z})
.

Lemma 6.14
.

The maps
\beta_{Z,T}
paste together to give a well-defined isomorphism

. Moreover,

is a bi-Lipschitz map from

to
(V_{G_{1}\times Z}({\mathbb{K}}),\widetilde{d})

Proof.

That

) and Remark

. Since each

and

∎

6.2.
Bi-Lipschitz equivalence of norms

We have seen that the common apartment property can fail for
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. We can, however, prove a weaker local version.

Lemma 6.15
.

Let
d
be an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Let
x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Then there exists a
d
-open neighbourhood
O
of
x
in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
such that

(i)

P_{y}\subseteq P_{x}
for all
y\in O
;

(ii)

O\subseteq V_{L_{\lambda}}({\mathbb{K}})
for any
\lambda\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
\phi_{G}(\lambda)=x
.

Proof.

First suppose
G
is reductive. Recall (see Lemma
5.1
, Definition
5.3
) that we have an embedding of
Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
in
Y_{G_{k_{s}}}({\mathbb{K}})
and of
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
in
V_{G_{k_{s}}}({\mathbb{K}})
; we denote the image of
\mu\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
in
Y_{G_{k_{s}}}({\mathbb{K}})
by
\widetilde{\mu}
, and of
y\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
in
V_{G_{k_{s}}}({\mathbb{K}})
by
\widetilde{y}
. Choose a maximal split torus
T
of
G
such that
x\in V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
, and choose a maximal torus
\widetilde{T}
of
G_{k_{s}}
such that
T_{k_{s}}\subseteq\widetilde{T}
: then
\widetilde{x}\in V_{\widetilde{T}}({\mathbb{K}})
. Choose
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
x=\phi_{G}(\lambda)
, and recall that in this case we can determine the R-parabolic subgroup
P_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
by considering the pairing of
\widetilde{\lambda}
with roots of
G_{k_{s}}
(see Example
3.13
).
Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s}
be the roots of
U_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
with respect to
\widetilde{T}
. The set
\{\mu\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})\mid\langle\widetilde{\mu},\alpha_{i}\rangle>0\ \mbox{for $1\leq i\leq s$}\}
is open and contains
\lambda
, so its image
\widetilde{O}
in
V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
is an open neighbourhood of
x
. Hence there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
y\in\widetilde{O}
for all
y\in V_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
d(x,y)<\epsilon
.

Let
O
be the open ball
B(x,\epsilon)
in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Let
y\in O
. Since
G
is reductive, the common apartment property holds, so there is a maximal split torus
T_{0}
of
G
such that
x,y\in V_{T_{0}}({\mathbb{K}})
. Now
T_{0}
is a maximal split torus of
P_{x}
, so there exists
g\in P_{x}(k)
such that
gT_{0}g^{-1}=T
. We have
g\cdot x=x
, so
d(x,g\cdot y)=d(g\cdot x,g\cdot y)=d(x,y)<\epsilon
. It follows from Example
3.13
and by construction that
U_{g\cdot y}\supseteq U_{x}
, so
P_{g\cdot y}\subseteq P_{x}

(this conclusion holds because
G
is reductive). Hence
P_{y}=g^{-1}P_{g\cdot y}g\subseteq g^{-1}P_{x}g=P_{g^{-1}\cdot x}=P_{x}
by Example
4.9
. This proves part (i) when
G
is reductive.

Now let
G
be arbitrary and let
x\in V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. There exist an embedding
i
of
G
in a reductive
k
-group
G^{\prime}
and an admissible metric
d^{\prime}
on
V_{G^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
d=i^{*}(d^{\prime})
. By the reductive case, there is a
d^{\prime}
-open neighbourhood
O^{\prime}
of
\kappa_{i}(x)
such that
P_{w}(G^{\prime})\subseteq P_{\kappa_{i}(x)}(G^{\prime})
for all
w\in O^{\prime}
. By construction,
O:=\kappa_{i}^{-1}(O^{\prime})
is a
d
-open neighbourhood of
x
. Let
y\in O
. Then
P_{\kappa_{i}(y)}(G^{\prime})\subseteq P_{\kappa_{i}(x)}(G^{\prime})
, so
P_{y}\subseteq P_{x}
by Corollary
3.16
. This finishes the proof of (i).

To prove (ii), let
y\in O
. Choose
\lambda,\mu\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
\phi_{G}(\lambda)=x
and
\phi_{G}(\mu)=y
. Since
P_{y}\subseteq P_{x}
,
\mu
belongs to
Y_{P_{x}}({\mathbb{K}})
, so by Remark
3.8
there exist a maximal split torus
T
of
P_{x}
and
u\in U_{\lambda}(k)
such that
\lambda,u\cdot\mu\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
.
Since
P_{\kappa_{i}(u\cdot y)}(G^{\prime})=P_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(u\cdot\mu)}(G^{\prime})
is contained in
P_{\kappa_{i}(x)}(G^{\prime})=P_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\lambda)}(G^{\prime})
, it follows from the description of R-parabolic subgroups and R-Levi subgroups of reductive groups given in Example
3.13
that
L_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(u\cdot\mu)}(G^{\prime})
is contained in
L_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\lambda)}(G^{\prime})
. We deduce from Corollary
3.16
that
L_{u\cdot\mu}
is contained in
L_{\lambda}
, so

. Now
U_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\lambda)}(G^{\prime})\subseteq U_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\mu)}(G^{\prime})
because
P_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\mu)}(G^{\prime})\subseteq P_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\lambda)}(G^{\prime})
and

is reductive. It follows that
U_{\lambda}=U_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\lambda)}(G^{\prime})\cap G\subseteq U_{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}(\mu)}(G^{\prime})\cap G=U_{\mu}

by Corollary
3.16
, so

, so

. Hence

, and we are done.
∎

Lemma 6.16
.

Let
d
be an admissible metric on
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
. Let

. Then there is a
d
-open neighbourhood
O
of

such that for all

,

and

lie in a common apartment.

Proof.

We use induction on

. The statement is vacuous if

. If

then every

lies in a common apartment with

, so

will do. Suppose

. Pick
\lambda\in Y_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
\phi_{G}(\lambda)=x
. We identify

with its image in
V_{G}({\mathbb{K}})
;
the restriction

of

to

is an admissible metric. By Lemma

, there is an open neighbourhood

of

such that

. Since

by Lemma

(ii), our induction hypothesis implies that there is an open neighbourhood

of

in

such that for all

. But an apartment of

.
∎

Lemma 6.17
.

Let
T,T^{\prime}
be maximal split tori of
G
. Let
(\lambda_{n})
and
(\lambda^{\prime}_{n})
be sequences in
Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
and
Y_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
, respectively, and let
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{K}})
,
\lambda^{\prime}\in Y_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{K}})
such that
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}_{n}\to\lambda^{\prime}
. Suppose
(T,\lambda_{n})\approx(T^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}_{n})
for all

. Then
(T,\lambda)\approx(T^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
.

Proof.

For each

, there exists
g_{n}\in P_{\lambda_{n}}(k)
such that
g_{n}Tg_{n}^{-1}=T^{\prime}
and
g_{n}\cdot\lambda_{n}=\lambda^{\prime}_{n}
. The conjugation map
\Inn_{g_{n}}
gives rise to a linear map

. Since

is finite, the set

is finite. By passing to subsequences, we can assume that the

are all equal — say, to

. So

by the continuity of

. We have

∎

Lemma 6.18
.

Let
\kappa
be a linear map from

to

. Then there is a unique extension of

to a linear map

We can extend

from

to

(it is clear that the map is linear if it is well-defined, and uniqueness is also clear). Recall from Lemma

that

lifts to a map
\widehat{\kappa}\colon Y_{G}({\mathbb{Q}})\to Y_{H}({\mathbb{Q}})
. Let

be maximal split tori of

and choose maximal split tori

and

of

such that
\widehat{\kappa}
maps
Y_{T}({\mathbb{Q}})
into

and

into
Y_{S^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Q}})
. Let
\widehat{\kappa}_{T,S}\colon V_{T}({\mathbb{R}})\to V_{S}({\mathbb{R}})
(resp.,
\widehat{\kappa}_{T^{\prime},S^{\prime}}\colon V_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}})\to V_{S^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}})
) be the extension of

. Pick
\lambda\in Y_{T}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\lambda^{\prime}\in Y_{T^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}})
such that

. It is enough to show that

Pick a rational approximating sequence

to

Definition 6.19
.

G

Remarks 6.20
.

(i). Let
G
and
G^{\prime}
be connected

-groups and let

be an admissible metric on

(ii). It follows from Remark

-group and let

is a strongly type-preserving linear map; note that

as a map

We can now prove:

.

be a connected

-group, where

,

be admissible metrics on

Proof.

We may assume that
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}

by Lemma

(iv).
Let

.
Then

for some maximal split torus

of

.
The restriction of

(resp.,

) to

(resp.,

) is a metric arising from a symmetric positive-definite bilinear form, so there exists

but independent of
