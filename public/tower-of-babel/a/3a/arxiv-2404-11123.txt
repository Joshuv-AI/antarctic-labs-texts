Rational curves on complete intersections and the circle method
Tim Browning, Pankaj Vishe, Shuntaro Yamagishi

Abstract
. We study the geometry of the space of rational curves on smooth complete intersections of low degree, which pass through a given set of points on the variety. The argument uses spreading out to a finite field, together with an adaptation to function fields of positive characteristic of work by Rydin Myerson on the circle method. Our work also allows us to handle weak approximation for such varieties.

1.
Introduction

Geometry of rational curves

Let
d\geqslant 2
be an integer and let

k
be a field
whose characteristic exceeds
d
if it is positive.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth complete intersection over
k
,
cut out by
R
hypersurfaces of the same degree
d
. We will always assume that

n\geqslant Rd
, so that
X
is Fano.
In this paper we shall be interested in the geometry of the moduli space of degree
e
rational curves on
X
that pass through a given set of points on
X
.

To begin with, let
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
be the moduli space
of degree
e
rational curves on
X
.
As explained by Harris, Roth and Starr
[
15
, Lemma 4.2]
,
basic deformation theory shows that every irreducible component of

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
has dimension at least

\mu(e,R)=e(n-Rd)+n-R-4,

which we refer to as the expected dimension.
In the special case
R=1
of smooth degree
d
hypersurfaces
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, the space

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
has been the focus of a great deal of study.
For
d=2
,
Kim and Pandharipande
[
17
, Cor. 1]
have addressed the irreducibility and dimension of

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
. For
d=3
, similar results hold thanks to work of Coskun and Starr
[
10
]
if

n\geqslant 5
. Using
a version of Bend-and-Break, the best result for generic degree
d
hypersurfaces
X
is due to
Riedl and Yang
[
23
]
, who establish that

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is irreducible and of the expected dimension
\mu(e,1)
, provided that

n\geqslant d+3
.
Recent work of Browning and Sawin
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
achieves the same conclusion for any smooth hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of degree
d
, provided that

n>(2d-1)2^{d-1}
.
The latter result is proved using analytic number theory and builds on an approach
employed by Browning and Vishe
[
8
]
.
The idea is to study
the moduli space of maps

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)
, whose expected dimension is
\mu(e,R)+3
, since

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is obtained from
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)
on
taking into account an action by
\mathrm{PGL}_{2}
.
To calculate the dimension of
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)
,
one begins by working over a finite field
k=\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
>d
and counts
the number of points on it that are defined over a finite extension of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Finally, one combines this count with the
Lang–Weil estimate to control the irreducibility and the dimension.
In this paper we shall use the analytic number theory point of view to treat
complete intersections with
R\geqslant 1
, beginning with the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and let

k
be a field whose characteristic exceeds
d
if it is positive.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth complete intersection over
k
,
cut out by
R
hypersurfaces of degree
d
.
Assume that
e\geqslant 1
and

(1.1)

n\geqslant\begin{cases}d(d-1)2^{d+1}R+R&\text{ if $d\geqslant 3$,}\\
33R&\text{ if $d=2$.}\end{cases}

Then

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)

is an irreducible locally complete intersection of dimension
\mu(e,R)
.

For comparison, it follows from work of Beheshti and Kumar
[
1
, Thm. 7.3]
that a similar statement holds for
generic
complete intersections under the assumption
dR<(2n+R-1)/3
, which is equivalent to

n>\frac{1}{2}(3dR-R+1)
.

Let
X
be a smooth complete intersection over a field
k
, as in
the statement of Theorem
1.1
.
Let
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
be the closed subscheme formed from

p_{1},\dots,p_{b}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Consider the map

(1.2)

\mathrm{red}:\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)\to\operatorname{Mor}(B,X)

that is obtained by reducing to the subscheme
B
, where

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)
is the
moduli space of maps and
\operatorname{Mor}(B,X)
is isomorphic to
X^{b}
.
For any

y_{1},\dots,y_{b}\in X
, we define

(1.3)

\mathrm{M}_{e,b}=\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X;p_{1},\dots,p_{b};y_{1},\dots,y_{b})

to be the moduli space of degree
e
morphisms

g:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
such that

g(p_{j})=y_{j}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant b
. Thus

\mathrm{M}_{e,b}
is the fibre of the map (
1.2
) over

(y_{1},\dots,y_{b})\in X^{b}
.
(Note that
\mathrm{M}_{e,0}=\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)

when
b=0
.)
As explained by Debarre
[
11
, § 2.3]
,
viewed as a fibre of the reduction map,
the expected dimension of
\mathrm{M}_{e,b}
is

\displaystyle\dim\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)-b\dim X

\displaystyle=\mu(e,R)+3-b(n-1-R)

\displaystyle=e(n-Rd)+(n-1-R)(1-b).

In his lecture at the Banff workshop
“Geometry via Arithmetic” in July 2021, Will Sawin raised the question of
tackling the irreducibility of the space
\mathrm{M}_{e,b}
and calculating its dimension, pointing out that methods from
algebraic geometry haven’t yet been made to yield this information, even for generic complete intersections
X
.
This is the object of the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and let

k
be a field whose characteristic exceeds
d
if it is positive.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth complete intersection over
k
,
cut out by
R
hypersurfaces of degree
d
.
Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{b}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
and let
y_{1},\dots,y_{b}\in X
, for
b\geqslant 1
.
Assume that

e\geqslant(d+1+R)b

and (
1.1
) holds.
Then

\mathrm{M}_{e,b}

is irreducible and has the expected dimension.

When
d=3
and
n\geqslant 10
, work of
Mânză

t

,

eanu
[
21
, Cor. 1.4]
addresses the
geometry of the space

\mathrm{M}_{e,2}
in the special case
R=1
of hypersurfaces.
Since
X
is smooth, the Hessian covariant is non-vanishing.
Assuming that

y_{1},y_{2}\in X
don’t both vanish on the Hessian, and that

e\geqslant 19-\frac{2}{n-9}
, it is shown that

\mathrm{M}_{e,2}

is irreducible and of the expected dimension.
Theorem
1.2
arrives at the same conclusion for any
e\geqslant 10
,
provided that
n\geqslant 97
.

Arithmetic over function fields

Our work on the geometry of
\mathrm{M}_{e,b}
goes via a spreading out argument, as in
[
7
,
8
]
, which will lead us to study the size of

\#\mathrm{M}_{e,b}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
for a suitable finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
This in turn will be accessed through
a version of the
Hardy–Littlewood circle method
over the function field
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}

be a smooth complete intersection
cut out by
R
hypersurfaces of equal degree
d\geqslant 2
,
all of which are defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. In suitable circumstances we shall be able to count
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
-rational points on
X
of bounded height via the circle method.
Over
\mathbb{Q}
, this is carried out in a classic paper of Birch
[
2
]
, which
is capable of proving that the Hasse principle and weak approximation hold, provided that
n\geqslant R+R(R+1)2^{d-1}(d-1)
, a result that has already been extended to
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
by
Lee
[
20
]
. In fact,
as described in
[
13
, Thms. 3.4 and 3.6]
,
it follows from
the Lang–Nagata–Tsen theorems that
X(\mathbb{F}_{q}(t))\neq\emptyset
whenever
n>Rd^{2}
.

The particular version of the circle method we shall use is a function field analogue of the work recently carried out over
\mathbb{Q}
by Rydin Myerson
[
24
,
25
]
. It has the key feature that it only depends linearly on the number
R
of equations, rather than quadratically as in the case of Birch’s result.
To be precise, if
n\geqslant d2^{d}R+R
and the system of forms
f_{1},\dots,f_{R}
is
suitably generic
, then one can conclude the Hasse principle and weak approximation over
\mathbb{Q}
.
(Note that the genericity condition on the system of forms can be removed when
d=2

[
24
]
and when
d=3

[
25
]
.)
When working in positive characteristic, it turns out that we can
remove the genericity condition for every
d
, provided that the finite field is large enough and we make a stronger assumption on the number of variables.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d,n,R\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d\geqslant 2
and

n>\begin{cases}d(d-1)2^{d}R+R&\text{ if $d\geqslant 3$,}\\
17R&\text{ if $d=2$.}\end{cases}

Assume that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a finite field such that

\chara(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d

and
q\geqslant(d-1)^{n}
.
Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be forms of degree
d
cutting out a
smooth complete intersection
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.
Then
X
satisfies weak approximation over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
.

Our assumption on the number of variables is more stringent than the
bound
n\geqslant d2^{d}R+R
obtained by Rydin Myerson for generic forms, although it retains the feature that it is linear in the number
R
of equations. This can be traced to the nature of the saving in the main new technical ingredient, provided in Theorem

2.5
, which allows us to handle
all
forms defining a smooth complete intersection in the function field setting.

Low degree rational curves

Let us return to the setting of Theorem
1.1
, specialised to the case
k=\mathbb{F}_{q}
, a finite field whose characteristic exceeds
d
.
Under the assumptions of the theorem our work ensures that

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)

is an irreducible locally complete intersection of dimension
\mu(e,R)
, for each
e\geqslant 1
.
In the setting
d=3
and
R=1
of smooth cubic hypersurfaces,
Kollár
[
18
]
ask about an explicit lower bound on
e
, sufficient to ensure that
X
contains a degree
e
rational curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Building on the proof of Theorem
1.3
,
our final result does exactly this in the broader setting of complete intersections, provided that
q
is sufficiently large.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a constant
c_{d,n}>0
depending only on
d
and
n
such that the following holds.
Let
d\geqslant 2
and let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field such that

\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d
and
q\geqslant c_{d,n}
.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth complete intersection over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
cut out by
R
hypersurfaces of degree
d
.
Assume that (
1.1
) holds and

e\geqslant 2(d-1)R+3d
.
Then

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq\emptyset
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth cubic hypersurface over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
In
[
18
, Example 7.6]
, Kollár proves that for any
q\geqslant c_{3,n}
and any point
y\in X(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, there exists a
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational curve of degree at most
216
on
X
passing through
y
.
Although it will not be pursued here, it would be possible to
adapt the proof of Theorem
1.4
to yield explicit conditions on
e
under which

\mathrm{M}_{e,b}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\neq\emptyset
, for any given
b\geqslant 1
and any smooth complete intersection
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
for which
(
1.1
) holds.

2.
Background on finite fields and function fields

2.1.
Points on varieties over finite fields

The Lang–Weil estimate can be used to give an upper bound for the number of

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points on a variety over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
We will need a general version of this that features convenient explicit constants. The following bound is proved in
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
, but we include a full proof here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an affine variety of dimension
r
and degree
\delta
. Then

\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q})\leqslant\delta q^{r}.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
r
. When
r=0
then
X
consists of at most
\delta
points and the lemma is trivial.
Assume now that we have
r\geqslant 1
.
Let
Z
be an irreducible component of
X
, with
\dim Z\geqslant 1
.
We shall show that there is an index
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
such that
the intersection of
Z
with the hyperplane
H_{\alpha}=\{x_{i}=\alpha\}
satisfies

\dim(Z\cap H_{\alpha})<\dim Z,

for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Suppose otherwise, so that there exists
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, such that

Z\subset\{x_{j}=\alpha_{j}\}

for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
.
However, this means that
Z=\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\}
, which contradicts the assumption that
\dim Z\geqslant 1
.
Since
Z\cap H_{\alpha}
has dimension at most
\dim Z-1
and degree at most
\deg Z
, it follows by the induction hypothesis that

\#Z(\mathbb{F}_{q})\leqslant\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\#(Z\cap H_{\alpha})(\mathbb{F}_{q})\leqslant\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\deg Z)q^{\dim Z-1}\leqslant(\deg Z)q^{\dim Z}.

Therefore, if we denote by
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{u}
the irreducible components of
X
, then we obtain

\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q})\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant u}\#Z_{i}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant u}(\deg Z_{i})q^{\dim Z_{i}}\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant u}(\deg Z_{i})q^{\dim X}.

Since
\deg X=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant u}\deg Z_{i}
, the result follows.
∎

2.2.
Function field notation

In this section we collect together some notation and basic facts concerning the function field
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
.
Let

\Omega
be the set of places of
K
. These correspond to either monic irreducible polynomials
\varpi
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, which we call the
finite primes
, or the
prime at infinity

t^{-1}
which we usually denote by
\infty
.
The associated absolute value
|\cdot|_{v}
is either
|\cdot|_{\varpi}
for some prime
\varpi\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
or
|\cdot|_{\infty}
, according to whether
v
is a finite or infinite place, respectively.
These are given by

|a/b|_{\varpi}=\left(\frac{1}{q^{\deg\varpi}}\right)^{\ord_{\varpi}(a)-\ord_{\varpi}(b)}\quad\text{ and }\quad|a/b|_{\infty}=q^{\deg a-\deg b},

for any
a/b\in K^{*}
, where
\ord_{\varpi}(a)
is the non-negative integer
\ell
satisfying

\varpi^{\ell}\|a
. We extend these definitions to
K
by taking
|0|_{\varpi}=|0|_{\infty}=0.

We will usually just write
|\cdot|=|\cdot|_{\infty}
.

For
v\in\Omega
we let
K_{v}
denote the completion of
K
at
v
with respect to
|\cdot|_{v}
,
together with its ring of integers
\mathscr{O}_{v}
.
We can extend the absolute value at the infinite place to
K_{\infty}
to get a non-archimedean
absolute value

|\cdot|:K_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}

given by
|\alpha|=q^{\ord\alpha}
, where
\ord\alpha
is the largest
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
a_{i}\neq 0
in the
representation
\alpha=\sum_{i\leqslant N}a_{i}t^{i}
.
In this context we adopt the convention
\ord 0=-\infty
and
|0|=0
.
We extend this to vectors by setting

|\mathbf{x}|=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|,

for any
\mathbf{x}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
. It satisfies the ultrametric inequality

|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}|\leqslant\max\{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|\},

for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
. For given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
and
m\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
we will write
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{b}\bmod{m}
to mean that

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}+m\mathbf{y}
for some
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
.

We may identify
K_{\infty}
with the set

\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1}))=\left\{\sum_{i\leqslant N}a_{i}t^{i}:\mbox{for $a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$ and some $N\in\mathbb{Z}$}\right\}

and put

\mathbb{T}=\{\alpha\in K_{\infty}:|\alpha|<1\}=\left\{\sum_{i\leqslant-1}a_{i}t^{i}:\mbox{for $a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$}\right\}.

Since
\mathbb{T}
is a locally compact
additive subgroup of
K_{\infty}
it possesses a unique Haar measure
\mathrm{d}\alpha
, which is normalised so that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{d}\alpha=1.

We can extend
\mathrm{d}\alpha
to a (unique) translation-invariant measure on
K_{\infty}
in
such a way that

\int_{\{\alpha\in K_{\infty}:|\alpha|<q^{N}\}}\mathrm{d}\alpha=q^{N},

for any
N\in\mathbb{Z}
.
These measures also extend to
\mathbb{T}^{n}
and
K_{\infty}^{n}
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Given
\alpha\in K_{\infty}
we denote
\|\alpha\|=|\{\alpha\}|
, where

\{\alpha\}\in\mathbb{T}
is the
fractional part
of
\alpha
. The following result will prove useful.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and any non-zero
h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, we
have

\meas\{\beta\in\mathbb{T}:\|h\beta\|<q^{-N}\}=q^{-N}.

Proof.

We may suppose that

h=c_{s}t^{s}+\cdots+c_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, with
c_{s}\neq 0
and
s\geqslant 0
.
Note that

\{h\beta\}=\sum_{i\leqslant-1}(c_{0}\beta_{i}+c_{1}\beta_{-1+i}+\cdots+c_{s}\beta_{-s+i})t^{i},

where we write
\beta=\sum_{i\leqslant-1}\beta_{i}t^{i}
. Then the restriction

\|h\beta\|=|\{h\beta\}|<q^{-N}

is equivalent to
(\beta_{-1},\ldots,\beta_{-s-N})
satisfying

\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&\cdots&c_{s}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&\cdots&c_{s}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&\cdots&c_{s}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{-1}\\
\beta_{-2}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{-s-N}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}.

Since
c_{s}\neq 0
, the matrix on the left hand side has rank
N
.
Therefore, there are precisely
q^{s}
choices for
(\beta_{-1},\ldots,\beta_{-s-N})
.
It follows that

\meas\{\beta\in\mathbb{T}:\|h\beta\|<q^{-N}\}=q^{s}\meas\{\gamma\in\mathbb{T}:|\gamma|<q^{-s-N}\}=q^{-N},

as required.
∎

2.3.
Characters

There is a non-trivial additive character
e_{q}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
defined
for each
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
by taking

e_{q}(a)=\exp(2\pi i\tr(a)/p)
, where
\tr:\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}
denotes the
trace map.
This character induces a non-trivial (unitary) additive character
\psi:K_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
by defining
\psi(\alpha)=e_{q}(a_{-1})
for any

\alpha=\sum_{i\leqslant N}a_{i}t^{i}
in
K_{\infty}
.
We have the basic orthogonality property

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
|b|<q^{N}\end{subarray}}\psi(\gamma b)=\begin{cases}q^{N}&\mbox{if $|\gamma|<q^{-N}$,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{T}
and any integer
N\geqslant 0
, as proved in
[
19
, Lemma 7]
.
We also have

(2.1)

\int_{\{\alpha\in K_{\infty}:|\alpha|<q^{N}\}}\psi(\alpha\gamma)\mathrm{d}\alpha=\begin{cases}q^{N}&\text{ if $|\gamma|<q^{-N}$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

for any
\gamma\in K_{\infty}
and
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, as proved in
[
19
, Lemma 1(f)]
.

2.4.
Covering a box by boxes

Given
\mathbf{z}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
and
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote

B_{N}(\mathbf{z})=\{\bm{\alpha}\in K_{\infty}^{R}:|\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}|<q^{N}\}.

Let
M\in\mathbb{Z}
with
M\leqslant N
. We claim that we can always
cover
B_{{N}}(\mathbf{z})
by
at most
q^{R(N-M)}

boxes of the form
B_{{M}}(\mathbf{y})
. If
M=N
there is nothing to prove.
Suppose
M<N
and assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
. Then
we have

B_{{N}}(\mathbf{0})=\bigcup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant R}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{(i)}_{M},\ldots,a^{(i)}_{N-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\end{subarray}}B_{{M}}\left(\sum_{j=M}^{N-1}a^{(1)}_{j}t^{j},\ldots,\sum_{j=M}^{N-1}a^{(R)}_{j}t^{j}\right),

and the number of boxes used in this cover
is precisely
q^{R(N-M)}.

2.5.
An auxiliary estimate

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be forms of degree
d
defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
We will assume that
\chara(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d
throughout this section. Consider the affine variety

(2.2)

V=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}:\rank\left(\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant k\leqslant R\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant n\end{subarray}}<R\right\}.

Note that
V
cuts out the
Birch singular locus
occuring in the work of Birch
[
2
]
.
As shown by Lee
[
20
]
, the function field version of Birch’s work allows one to count
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
-points of bounded height in the system
f_{1}=\dots=f_{R}=0
, provided that

n-\dim V>R(R+1)2^{d-1}(d-1).

Remark 2.3
.

Let
V
be given by (
2.2
) and
let
\sigma=\dim V
. If we assume that
the system
f_{1},\dots,f_{R}
cuts out a smooth complete intersection
X
in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, then
we claim that
\sigma\leqslant R-1
.
This follows from the argument in

[
5
, Lemma 3.1]
for
optimal
systems of forms, this being an argument that is valid over fields of arbitrary characteristic. In characteristic
0
, it is shown in

[
3
, Lemma 3.1]
that
X
can be defined by an equivalent optimal system
of forms, but use is made of Bertini’s theorem, which is not generally available in positive characteristic. Fortunately, the same conclusion can be arrived at in positive
characteristic, as explained by Glas in his proof of
[
12
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Let

(2.3)

V_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}:h_{1}\nabla f_{1}(\mathbf{x})+\dots+h_{R}\nabla f_{R}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}\},

for any
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
.
Then it will be convenient to define

(2.4)

\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
\mathbf{h}\neq\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\dim V_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that
V_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}
is contained in the variety
V
defined in (
2.2
). Hence, if the system
f_{1},\dots,f_{R}
cuts out a smooth complete intersection in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, then
Remark
2.3
implies that
\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}\leqslant\sigma\leqslant R-1
.

Suppose that

f_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}^{(k)}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{d}},

for
1\leqslant k\leqslant R
,
with coefficients
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
which are symmetric in the indices.
Associated to each
f_{k}
are the multilinear forms

(2.5)

\Psi_{i}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{({d-1})})=d!\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}x_{i_{1}}^{(1)}\dots x_{i_{d-1}}^{(d-1)},

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Given
J\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{\beta}=(\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(R)})\in K_{\infty}^{R}
,
we shall be interested in the size of the counting function

(2.6)

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta})=\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{u}^{(1)}|,\dots,|\mathbf{u}^{(d-1)}|<q^{J}\\
\left|\sum_{k=1}^{R}\beta^{(k)}\Psi_{i}^{(k)}(\underline{\mathbf{u}})\right|<q^{(d-2)J}\\
\text{for $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$}\end{array}\right\},

where

\underline{\mathbf{u}}=(\mathbf{u}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{u}^{(d-1)})
.
(This is a function field variant of the quantity considered in
[
24
, Def. 1.1]
,
in which the upper bound
|\bm{\beta}|q^{(d-2)J}
is replaced by

q^{(d-2)J}
.)
The trivial bound for this quantity is

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta})\leqslant q^{Jn(d-1)}.

Our main result in this section offers the following improvement for suitable
\bm{\beta}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
J\in\mathbb{N}
,
M\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
\sigma=\dim V
, where
V
is the variety cut out by
(
2.2
). Let
\bm{\beta}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
such that
|\bm{\beta}|=q^{M}
. Assume that

M\geqslant d-2
.
Then

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta})\leqslant(d-1)^{\min\{\frac{J+M}{d-1},J\}n+n}q^{Jn(d-1)-\min\{\frac{J+M}{d-1},J\}(n-\sigma)}.

Proof.

Let
M^{*}\geqslant 0
and
define

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J,M^{*};\bm{\beta})=\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{u}^{(1)}|,\dots,|\mathbf{u}^{(d-1)}|<q^{J}\\
\left|\sum_{k=1}^{R}\beta^{(k)}\Psi_{i}^{(k)}(\underline{\mathbf{u}})\right|<q^{(d-2)J-M^{*}}\\
\text{for $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$}\end{array}\right\},

for any
\bm{\beta}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
such that
|\bm{\beta}|=1
.
Assuming that
M^{*}
lies in the range

(2.7)

d-2\leqslant M^{*}\leqslant J(d-2),

we shall prove that

(2.8)

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J,M^{*};\bm{\beta})\leqslant(d-1)^{\frac{(J+M^{*})n}{d-1}+n}q^{Jn(d-1)-\frac{(J+M^{*})(n-\sigma)}{d-1}}.

This will suffice for the theorem, since

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta})\leqslant N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J,\min\{M,J(d-2)\};\bm{\beta}t^{-M}),

on recalling that
|\bm{\beta}|=q^{M}
.

Assume henceforth that
|\bm{\beta}|=1
and
let

\beta^{(k)}=\sum_{r\geqslant 0}b_{r}^{(k)}t^{-r},

for
1\leqslant k\leqslant R
and
b_{r}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We may assume without loss of generality that
b_{0}^{(1)}\neq 0
, since
|\bm{\beta}|=1
.
Since
|\mathbf{u}^{(j)}|<q^{J}
, for
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-1
, each component of
\mathbf{u}^{(j)}
can be written

u_{i}^{(j)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{J-1}z_{i,\ell}^{(j)}t^{\ell},

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
z_{i,\ell}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Expanding everything out, it therefore follows that

\sum_{k=1}^{R}\beta^{(k)}\Psi_{i}^{(k)}(\underline{\mathbf{u}})
is equal to

d!\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sum_{r\geqslant 0}b_{r}^{(k)}t^{-r}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d-1}=0}^{J-1}z_{i_{1},\ell_{1}}^{(1)}\dots z_{i_{d-1},\ell_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}t^{\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{d-1}},

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. The components
z_{i,\ell}^{(j)}
are therefore counted by
N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J,M^{*};\bm{\beta})
if and only
if
the coefficient of
t^{(d-2)J-M^{*}+v}
vanishes in this expression for all

0\leqslant v\leqslant J+M^{*}-(d-1).

Note that this interval is non-empty, since
J\geqslant 1
and
M^{*}\geqslant d-2
, by the lower bound
in (
2.7
), whence
J+M^{*}\geqslant d-1
.

By collecting together the coefficients of
t^{(d-2)J-M^{*}+v}
,
for
0\leqslant v\leqslant J+M^{*}-(d-1)
,
we see that
N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta})
is equal to the number of elements

z_{i,\ell}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant J-1
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-1
, such that

\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}\hskip-11.38092ptc_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}\hskip-28.45274pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d-1}=0\\
\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{d-1}\geqslant v+(d-2)J-M^{*}\end{subarray}}^{J-1}\hskip-28.45274ptb_{\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{d-1}-(d-2)J-v+M^{*}}^{(k)}z_{i_{1},\ell_{1}}^{(1)}\dots z_{i_{d-1},\ell_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}=0,

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and

0\leqslant v\leqslant J+M^{*}-(d-1).

In total we have
Jn(d-1)
variables and
n(J+M^{*}-d+2)
equations. We let
\widetilde{Z}\subset\mathbb{A}^{J(d-1)n}
be the algebraic variety cut out by this system.
For each choice of
v
, we let
X_{v}\subset\mathbb{A}^{J(d-1)n}
denote the algebraic variety defined by the system of
n
equations associated to it.

Let
g=\lfloor(J+M^{*})/(d-1)\rfloor\geqslant 1
.
We are unable to extract anything useful from many of the equations, but we will be able to exploit information when
v=J+M^{*}-h(d-1)
for integers
1\leqslant h\leqslant g
, and also when

v=J+M^{*}-g(d-1)-1
, if

(J+M^{*})/(d-1)\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For this purpose we define

Z=X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)}\cap X_{J+M^{*}-2(d-1)}\cap\dots\cap X_{J+M^{*}-g(d-1)}\cap X_{J+M^{*}-g(d-1)-\epsilon_{J}},

where

\epsilon_{J}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $d-1\mid J+M^{*}$,}\\
1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

In order to establish (
2.8
), it will suffice to prove that

(2.9)

\dim Z\leqslant Jn(d-1)-(g+\epsilon_{J})(n-\sigma).

Note that our assumption (
2.7
) on
M^{*}
implies that

(g+\epsilon_{J})(n-\sigma)\leqslant\left(\frac{J+M^{*}}{d-1}+1-\frac{1}{d-1}\right)(n-\sigma)\leqslant Jn(d-1),

so that the right hand side of (
2.9
) is indeed non-negative.

It follows from Bézout’s theorem, in the form
[
14
, Example 8.4.7]
, that the degree of
Z
satisfies

\deg Z\leqslant(d-1)^{(g+\epsilon_{J})n}.

Noting that
\widetilde{Z}\subset Z
, (
2.8
)
follows from
an application of Lemma
2.1
and the observation that

\frac{J+M^{*}}{d-1}\leqslant g+\epsilon_{J}\leqslant\frac{J+M^{*}}{d-1}+1.

It therefore remains to prove (
2.9
).

The case
g=1
.

In the definition of
X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)}
, only
the indices
\ell_{1}=\dots=\ell_{d-1}=J-1
are possible. It follows that

X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)}

is given by the system of equations

G_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})=0,

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, where

(2.10)

G_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})=\sum_{k=1}^{R}b_{0}^{(k)}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}z_{i_{1},J-1}^{(1)}\dots z_{i_{d-1},J-1}^{(d-1)}.

If
\varepsilon_{J}=1
then,
in the definition of

X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)-1}
, we see that either

\ell_{1}=\dots=\ell_{d-1}=J-1
, or else
precisely one of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d-1}
is equal to
J-2
, with all the others equal to
J-1
.
It follows that

X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)-1}

is given by the system of equations

A_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})+B_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-1)},\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})=0,

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, where

(2.11)

A_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})=\sum_{k=1}^{R}b_{1}^{(k)}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}z_{i_{1},J-1}^{(1)}\dots z_{i_{d-1},J-1}^{(d-1)}

and
B_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-1)},\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})
is given by

(2.12)

\sum_{m=1}^{d-1}\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}\hskip-8.5359ptc_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}b_{0}^{(k)}z_{i_{1},J-1}^{(1)}\dots z_{i_{m-1},J-1}^{(m-1)}z_{i_{m},J-2}^{(m)}z_{i_{m+1},J-1}^{(m+1)}\dots z_{i_{d-1},J-1}^{(d-1)}.

Let
Y_{J-1}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n(d-1)}
be the variety that is obtained by projecting

X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)}
to the
(\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})
variables,
being defined by

G_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})=0
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
We shall prove (
2.9
), for
g=1
, by projecting to
Y_{J-1}
, which we denote by
\pi_{0}:Z\to Y_{J-1}
, and considering the dimension of the fibres.
The particular version of the fibre dimension theorem we use is the Corollary to Theorem 2 in Mumford
[
22
, §I.8]
.
Thus

(2.13)

\dim Z\leqslant\dim\overline{\pi_{0}(Z)}+\Delta_{0}\leqslant\dim Y_{J-1}+\Delta_{0},

where
\Delta_{0}
is the dimension of the fibre above any chosen point in the image of
\pi_{0}
.
We claim that

(2.14)

\dim Y_{J-1}\leqslant n(d-2)+\sigma.

To see this, we follow Birch
[
2
, Lemma 3.3]
and proceed by intersecting the system of equations defining
Y_{J-1}
with the diagonal

D=\{(\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{n(d-1)}:\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)}=\dots=\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)}\}.

This produces an algebraic variety cut out by the system

\sum_{k=1}^{R}b_{0}^{(k)}\frac{\partial f_{k}(\mathbf{z})}{\partial z_{i}}=0,

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Since
b_{0}^{(1)}\neq 0
these relations imply that
\mathbf{z}
must satisfy (
2.2
). Hence
the affine dimension theorem implies that

\sigma=\dim V\geqslant\dim(Y_{J-1}\cap D)\geqslant\dim Y_{J-1}+\dim D-n(d-1).

The diagonal has dimension
n
and so it follows that (
2.14
) holds, as claimed.

It remains to bound
\Delta_{0}
.
Suppose first that
\varepsilon_{J}=0
. Then we define the variety

T_{J-1}=\{(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{Jn(d-1)}:\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)}=\dots=\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)}=\mathbf{0}\}.

It follows that

\Delta_{0}\leqslant\dim(Z\cap T_{J-1})\leqslant\dim T_{J-1}\leqslant(J-1)n(d-1).

In this case, the desired bound (
2.9
) follows immediately
from
(
2.13
) and (
2.14
).

Suppose next that
\varepsilon_{J}=1
.
Fix a point

\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
,
to be determined in due course.
Then we define the variety

S_{J-1}(\mathbf{w})=\{(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{Jn(d-1)}:\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(1)}=\dots=\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-2)}=\mathbf{w},\penalty\ \mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)}=\mathbf{0}\}.

Note that

G_{i}(\mathbf{w},\dots,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{0})=A_{i}(\mathbf{w},\dots,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{0})=0
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. It follows that

\Delta_{0}\leqslant\dim(Z\cap S_{J-1}(\mathbf{w}))=\dim(X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)-1}\cap S_{J-1}(\mathbf{w})),

and from (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) that

X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)-1}\cap S_{J-1}(\mathbf{w})
is cut out by the system of equations

\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}b_{0}^{(k)}w_{i_{1}}\dots w_{i_{d-2}}z_{i_{d-1},J-2}^{(d-1)}=0,

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. But this is just the system of equations

H_{\mathbf{b}.\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{w})\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-1)}=\mathbf{0},

where

\mathbf{b}.\mathbf{f}=b_{0}^{(1)}f_{1}+\dots+b_{0}^{(R)}f_{R}
and

H_{f}
is the Hessian matrix of second derivatives associated to a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
Suppose that
H_{\mathbf{b}.\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{w})
has rank
\varrho
.
Then, on returning to

X_{J+M^{*}-(d-1)-1}\cap S_{J-1}(\mathbf{w})
, we see that there are no constraints on the vectors

\mathbf{z}_{0}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-2)}
, but
\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-1)}
is constrained to lie in a linear space of dimension
n-\varrho
.
Thus

\Delta_{0}\leqslant(J-1)n(d-1)-\varrho.

We now want to choose
\mathbf{w}
to make
\varrho
as large as possible.
Let
T_{\varrho}
be the variety of
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
such that

\rank H_{\mathbf{b}.\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{w})\leqslant\varrho
. Then it follows from
precisely the same argument in

[
4
, Lemma 2]
, together with Remark
2.4
,
that

\dim T_{\varrho}\leqslant\varrho+\sigma,

since
b_{0}^{(1)}\neq 0
.
Let
\varrho
be the least non-negative integer such that
\dim T_{\varrho}=n
. We then choose

\mathbf{w}\in T_{\varrho}\setminus T_{\varrho-1}
, and therefore deduce that

\Delta_{0}\leqslant(J-1)n(d-1)-(n-\sigma).

The desired bound (
2.9
) now follows
from
(
2.13
) and (
2.14
).

The case
g\geqslant 2

It will be convenient to define the variety

T_{i}=\{(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{Jn(d-1)}:\mathbf{z}_{i}^{(1)}=\dots=\mathbf{z}_{i}^{(d-1)}=\mathbf{0}\},

for each
i\in\{0,\dots,J-1\}
.
Let

Y_{J-2}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n(d-1)}
be the variety obtained by projecting

X_{J+M^{*}-2(d-1)}\cap T_{J-1}
to the

(\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-1)})
variables.
Then we may define the projection

\pi_{1}:(Z\cap T_{J-1})\to Y_{J-2}.

Note that

X_{J+M^{*}-2(d-1)}\cap T_{J-1}

is defined by the system of equations

\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}\hskip-5.69046ptc_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}^{(k)}\hskip-22.76228pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d-1}=0\\
\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{d-1}\geqslant(J-2)(d-1)\end{subarray}}^{J-2}\hskip-22.76228ptb_{\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{d-1}-(J-2)(d-1)}^{(k)}z_{i_{1},\ell_{1}}^{(1)}\dots z_{i_{d-1},\ell_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}=0,

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. The summation conditions
force
\ell_{1}=\dots=\ell_{d-1}=J-2
in this expression, so that we have the system

G_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-1)})=0
,
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, in the notation of (
2.10
).
Hence

Y_{J-2}
is defined by exactly the same set of equations that was used to define the variety
Y_{J-1}
, from which it follows that
\dim Y_{J-2}=\dim Y_{J-1}
.
By the same fibre dimension theorem as above, we obtain

\dim(Z\cap T_{J-1})\leqslant\dim Y_{J-1}+\Delta_{1},

where
\Delta_{1}
is the dimension of the fibre above any
point in
\pi_{1}(Z\cap T_{J-1})
. Taking the point

(\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-2}^{(d-1)})=(\mathbf{0},\dots,\mathbf{0})\in\pi_{1}(Z\cap T_{J-1}),

we see that

\Delta_{1}\leqslant\dim(Z\cap T_{J-2}\cap T_{J-1}).

Continuing inductively in this way, we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\dim Z

\displaystyle\leqslant\dim Y_{J-1}+\dim(T_{J-1}\cap Z)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\dim Y_{J-1}+\dim(T_{J-2}\cap T_{J-1}\cap Z)

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69046pt\vdots

\displaystyle\leqslant(g-1)\dim Y_{J-1}+\dim(T_{{J}-(g-1)}\cap\dots\cap T_{J-1}\cap Z).

Since
M^{*}\leqslant J(d-2)
, it follows that

J-g\geqslant 0
.
If
\epsilon_{J}=0
then we repeat this once more to deduce that

\displaystyle\dim Z

\displaystyle\leqslant g\dim Y_{J-1}+\dim(T_{J-g}\cap\dots\cap T_{J-1}\cap\mathbb{A}^{nJ(d-1)})

\displaystyle=g\dim Y_{J-1}+(J-g)n(d-1).

But then

\displaystyle\dim Z

\displaystyle\leqslant(J-g)n(d-1)+g\left(n(d-2)+\sigma\right)=Jn(d-1)-g(n-\sigma),

by (
2.14
), which is satisfactory for (
2.9
).

On the other hand, if
\epsilon_{J}=1
then we
deduce that

\dim(T_{{J}-(g-1)}\cap\dots\cap T_{J-1}\cap Z)\leqslant\dim Y_{J-1}+\Delta_{g},

where
\Delta_{g}
is the
dimension of the fibre above any
point in the image of the projection

\pi_{g}:Z\cap T_{J-1}\cap\dots\cap T_{J-(g-1)}\to Y_{J-g},

where
Y_{J-g}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n(d-1)}
is obtained by projecting

X_{J+M^{*}-g(d-1)}\cap T_{J-1}\cap\cdots\cap T_{J-(g-1)}
to the
(\mathbf{z}_{J-g}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-g}^{(d-1)})
variables.
We argue as in the case
g=1
, by picking a point
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
similarly to before and deducing that

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}

\displaystyle\leqslant\dim\left(Z\cap T_{J-1}\cap\cdots\cap T_{J-(g-1)}\cap S_{J-g}(\mathbf{w})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\dim\left(X_{J+M^{*}-g(d-1)-1}\cap T_{J-1}\cap\cdots\cap T_{J-(g-1)}\cap S_{J-g}(\mathbf{w})\right),

where

S_{J-g}(\mathbf{w})=\left\{(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{J-1}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{Jn(d-1)}:\mathbf{z}_{J-g}^{(1)}=\cdots=\mathbf{z}_{J-g}^{(d-2)}=\mathbf{w},\penalty\ \mathbf{z}_{J-g}^{(d-1)}=\mathbf{0}\right\}.

Thus
\Delta_{g}\leqslant(J-g)n(d-1)-(n-\sigma)
, as previously, from which
it follows that

\dim Z\leqslant g\dim Y_{J-1}+(J-g)n(d-1)-(n-\sigma)\leqslant Jn(d-1)-(g+1)(n-\sigma),

by (
2.14
).
This too is satisfactory for (
2.9
), which thereby completes
the proof of
Theorem

2.5
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Assume that
the system
f_{1},\dots,f_{R}
cuts out a smooth complete intersection in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.
Let
J\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\bm{\beta}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
such that

|\bm{\beta}|\geqslant q^{d-1}
.
Then

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta})\leqslant(d-1)^{n}q^{Jn(d-1)}\left(q^{n\left(1-\log_{q}(d-1)\right)-R+1}\right)^{-\theta_{d}(J)},

where

\theta_{d}(J)=\begin{cases}\frac{J}{d-1}+1&\text{ if $d\geqslant 3$ and $J\geqslant 2$,}\\
1&\text{ if $d\geqslant 3$ and $J=1$,}\\
J&\text{ if $d=2$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Recall from Remark
2.3
that
\sigma\leqslant R-1
.
Suppose that
|\bm{\beta}|=q^{M}
, where
M\geqslant d-1
.
Theorem
2.5
then implies that

N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta})\leqslant(d-1)^{n}q^{Jn(d-1)}\left(\frac{(d-1)^{n}}{q^{n-R+1}}\right)^{\min\{\frac{J+M}{d-1},J\}}.

The statement follows on noting that
d-1=q^{\log_{q}(d-1)}
and

\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{J+M}{d-1},J\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{J}{d-1}+1,J\right\}=\theta_{d}(J),

under the assumption
M\geqslant d-1
.
∎

2.6.
A further technical estimate

Now that Theorem
2.5
is established, our final result will also prove useful.
Let
\bm{\beta}=(\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(R)})\in K_{\infty}^{R}
.
For each
0\leqslant v\leqslant d-1
, let

(2.15)

N^{(v)}(J;\bm{\beta})=\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{u}^{(1)}|,\dots,|\mathbf{u}^{(v)}|<q^{J}\\
|\mathbf{u}^{(v+1)}|,\dots,|\mathbf{u}^{(d-1)}|<q^{P}\\
\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{R}\beta^{(k)}\Psi^{(k)}_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{u}})\right\|<q^{-(v+1)P+vJ}\\
\text{for all $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$}\end{array}\right\},

where
\underline{\mathbf{u}}=(\mathbf{u}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{u}^{(d-1)})
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
J,P\in\mathbb{N}
such that
J\leqslant P
and let
\bm{\beta}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
.
Then precisely one of the following two alternatives must happen:

(i)

we have

q^{-J}\leqslant\max\left\{q^{-dP+d-2}|\bm{\beta}|^{-1},|\bm{\beta}|^{\frac{1}{d-1}}q^{-1}\right\};

(ii)

we have

q^{J-dP+d-1}\leqslant|\bm{\beta}|\leqslant q^{-J(d-1)+d-2}

and

N^{(d-1)}(J;\bm{\beta})=N^{\textnormal{aux}}(J;\bm{\beta}t^{dP-J}),

in the notation of (
2.6
).

Proof.

Suppose that alternative (i) fails, so that
|\bm{\beta}|
satisfies the inequalities in alternative (ii).
Then

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{R}\beta^{(k)}\Psi^{(k)}_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{u}})\right|\leqslant|\bm{\beta}|q^{(d-1)(J-1)}\leqslant q^{d-2}\cdot q^{-(d-1)}<1,

for any
|\mathbf{u}^{(1)}|,\dots,|\mathbf{u}^{(d-1)}|<q^{J}
.
But then the statement clearly follows.
∎

3.
Singular series and singular integral

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be forms of degree
d
defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We will assume that the system of equations

f_{1}=\dots=f_{R}=0
defines a smooth complete intersection in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.
Let

m\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be a monic polynomial and let
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
be such that
\gcd(\mathbf{b},m)=1

and
f_{i}(\mathbf{b})\equiv 0\bmod{m}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
.
We define

(3.1)

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}),

where we write
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{R})
for the vector of polynomials, and similarly for
\mathbf{F}
. In Section
4
we shall initiate the application of the circle method. The purpose of this section is to discuss, in isolation, the singular series and singular integral that will arise in our analysis.
In traditional applications of the circle method it is customary to recycle estimates from the minor arcs in order to establish the convergence of the singular series and the singular integral.
In the setting of smooth complete intersections defined over the constant field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, however, a direct treatment is more efficient.

3.1.
Singular series

Given a non-zero polynomial
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}
,
we define

(3.2)

S_{g}(\mathbf{a})=|g|^{-n}\sum_{|\mathbf{y}|<|g|}\psi\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}.\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})}{g}\right),

where
\mathbf{F}
is given in (
3.1
) and

\psi
is defined in Section
2.3
.
In this section we study the
singular series

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\setminus\{0\}\\
\text{$g$ monic}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a}),

with the aim of establishing its convergence under relatively mild hypotheses.
The singular series is slightly awkward to work with, since the inner sum
sum over
\mathbf{a}
runs modulo
gm^{d}
, but only over those
\mathbf{a}
which are coprime to
g
.

Define

(3.4)

A(g)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|g|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{g}(\mathbf{a}),

for any monic
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. This is exactly the sum
\mathscr{A}(g)
that appears in
the work of Lee
[
20
, Eq. (4.6.24)]
, and
the multiplicativity of
A(g)
follows from

[
20
, Lemma 4.7.2]
.
Moreover, it is easy to check that

(3.5)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\mid m^{d}\\
\gcd(h,g)=1\end{subarray}}A(gm^{d}/h).

For now, we proceed to collect together some facts about
A(g)
for an
arbitrary monic
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
assume that
n\geqslant dR
. Suppose that
\gcd(\mathbf{b},m)=1
and

f_{i}(\mathbf{b})\equiv 0\bmod{m}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
.
Then
there exists a constant
C>0
that
depends only on
d
and
n
, such that

|A(g)|\leqslant C^{\omega(g)}|g|^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)}|\gcd(g,m)|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})\frac{n}{d}+\frac{R}{d}}.

By multiplicativity it suffices to bound
A(g)
when
g=\pi^{e}
is a prime power, with
e\geqslant 1
.
To begin with, orthogonality of characters yields

(3.6)

\begin{split}A(\pi^{e})&=|\pi|^{-en}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|\pi^{e}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},\pi)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{|\mathbf{y}|<|\pi^{e}|}\psi\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}.\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})}{\pi^{e}}\right)\\
&=|\pi|^{-e(n-R)}\left(N(\pi^{e})-|\pi|^{n-R}N(\pi^{e-1})\right),\end{split}

where

(3.7)

N(\pi^{e})=\#\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{y}|<|\pi^{e}|,\penalty\ F_{i}(\mathbf{y})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{e}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}.

Recall that
F_{i}
is defined in terms of
f_{i}
via (
3.1
), for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
.
We shall need to differentiate according to whether or not
\pi
divides
m
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
assume that
n\geqslant dR
.
Assume that
e\geqslant 1
and

\pi\nmid m
. Then

A(\pi^{e})\ll|\pi|^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)e},

where the implied constant depends only on
d
and
n
.

Proof.

Since
\pi\nmid m
we can make a non-singular change of variables
\mathbf{z}=m\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}
modulo
\pi^{e}
, finding that

N(\pi^{e})=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}|<|\pi^{e}|,\penalty\ f_{i}(\mathbf{z})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{e}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}.

It will be convenient to also define

N^{*}(\pi^{e})=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}|<|\pi^{e}|,\penalty\ \pi\nmid\mathbf{z},\penalty\ f_{i}(\mathbf{z})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{e}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}.

In view of the fact that
f_{1}=\dots=f_{R}=0
defines a smooth complete intersection in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
,
Hensel lifting yields

(3.8)

N^{*}(\pi^{e})=|\pi|^{n-R}N^{*}(\pi^{e-1})\quad\text{ if $e\geqslant 2$}.

Our analysis of
N(\pi^{e})
will depend on the relative size of
e
to
d
.
Suppose first that
e=1
.
As explained in the appendix by Katz to
[
16
]
,
Deligne’s resolution of the Riemann hypothesis now yields

(3.9)

N(\pi)=|\pi|^{n-R}+O(|\pi|^{\frac{n-R+1}{2}}),

whence

A(\pi)=|\pi|^{-(n-R)}\left(N(\pi)-|\pi|^{n-R}\right)\ll|\pi|^{-\frac{n-R-1}{2}},

by (
3.6
),
which is satisfactory for the lemma.

Suppose now that
2\leqslant e\leqslant d
.
Then we have

N(\pi^{e})=N^{*}(\pi^{e})+|\pi|^{n(e-1)}.

On appealing to (
3.6
) and (
3.8
), it therefore follows that

A(\pi^{e})=|\pi|^{eR-n}(1-|\pi|^{-R})=O(|\pi|^{eR-n}).

It is easy to check that
eR-n\leqslant-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)e
if
e\leqslant d

and
n\geqslant dR
.
Thus this too is satisfactory for the lemma.

Finally we must deal with the case
e>d
.
On sorting according to the
\pi
-adic valuation of
\mathbf{z}
, we obtain

\displaystyle N(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leqslant j\leqslant e}\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}|<|\pi^{e}|,\penalty\ \gcd(\mathbf{z},\pi^{e})=\pi^{j},\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z})\equiv\mathbf{0}\bmod{\pi^{e}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leqslant j\leqslant e}\hskip-2.84544pt\#\left\{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}^{\prime}|<|\pi^{e-j}|,\penalty\ \gcd(\mathbf{z}^{\prime},\pi^{e-j})=1,\pi^{dj}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\equiv\mathbf{0}\bmod{\pi^{e}}\right\}.

When
dj\geqslant e
we bound the inner cardinality by
|\pi|^{(e-j)n}
.
When
dj<e
, the congruence only depends on the value of
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
modulo
\pi^{e-dj}
, whence

(3.10)

N(\pi^{e})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j<e/d\end{subarray}}|\pi|^{(d-1)jn}N^{*}(\pi^{e-dj})+O\left(|\pi|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})en}\right),

since

\sum_{e/d\leqslant j\leqslant e}|\pi|^{(e-j)n}\ll|\pi|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})en}.

Note that
n-R+(1-\frac{1}{d})(e-1)n\geqslant(1-\frac{1}{d})en
.
Returning to
(
3.6
), we see that

\displaystyle|\pi|^{e(n-R)}A(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle=N(\pi^{e})-|\pi|^{n-R}N(\pi^{e-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j<e/d\end{subarray}}|\pi|^{(d-1)jn}N^{*}(\pi^{e-dj})

\displaystyle\quad-|\pi|^{n-R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j<(e-1)/d\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69046pt|\pi|^{(d-1)jn}N^{*}(\pi^{e-1-dj})+O\left(|\pi|^{\frac{n}{d}-R+(1-\frac{1}{d})en}\right).

The first sum runs over
j
such that
e-dj\geqslant 1
and the second sum only involves
j
with

e-dj\geqslant 2
. It follows from (
3.8
) that all terms cancel apart from a possible term with
e-dj=1
, provided that
d\mid e-1
. It now follows from (
3.9
) that

\displaystyle A(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle\ll|\pi|^{-e(n-R)}\left(|\pi|^{(d-1)\frac{(e-1)n}{d}+n-R}+|\pi|^{\frac{n}{d}-R+(1-\frac{1}{d})en}\right)

\displaystyle\ll|\pi|^{-e(n-R)}\cdot|\pi|^{\frac{n}{d}-R+(1-\frac{1}{d})en}

\displaystyle=|\pi|^{-(\frac{n}{d}-R)(e-1)}.

But
e-1>e(1-\frac{1}{d})
if
e>d
, whence

A(\pi^{e})\ll|\pi|^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)e}.

This finally completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
is such that
\pi^{\mu}\|m
, with
\mu\geqslant 1
.
Assume that
e\geqslant 1
and

f_{i}(\mathbf{b})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{\mu}}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
, with
\gcd(\mathbf{b},\pi)=1
.
Then

A(\pi^{e})=0\quad\text{ if $e>\mu$}

and

|A(\pi^{e})|\leqslant 2|\pi|^{eR}\quad\text{ if $e\leqslant\mu$}.

In particular, we have

|A(\pi^{e})|\leqslant 2|\pi|^{\min\{e,\mu\}R}.

Proof.

It follows from
(
3.1
) and
(
3.6
) that

\displaystyle A(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle=|\pi|^{-e(n-R)}\left(N(\pi^{e})-|\pi|^{n-R}N(\pi^{e-1})\right),

where

N(\pi^{e})=\#\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{y}|<|\pi^{e}|,\penalty\ \mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b})\equiv\mathbf{0}\bmod{\pi^{e}}\right\}.

If
e\leqslant\mu
we simply take

|N(\pi^{e})-|\pi|^{n-R}N(\pi^{e-1})|\leqslant N(\pi^{e})+|\pi|^{n-R}N(\pi^{e-1})\leqslant 2|\pi|^{en},

whence

|A(\pi^{e})|\leqslant 2|\pi|^{eR}
.

Suppose now that
e>\mu
and write
m=\pi^{\mu}m^{\prime}
, where
m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is coprime to
\pi
.
Then, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
, the
congruence

f_{i}(\pi^{\mu}m^{\prime}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{e}}
only depends on the value of
\mathbf{y}
modulo
\pi^{e-\mu}
.
Hence

\displaystyle N(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle=|\pi|^{\mu n}\#\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{y}|<|\pi^{e-\mu}|,\penalty\ \mathbf{f}(\pi^{\mu}m^{\prime}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b})\equiv\mathbf{0}\bmod{\pi^{e}}\right\}

\displaystyle=|\pi|^{\mu n}N^{\dagger}(\pi^{e}),

where

(3.11)

N^{\dagger}(\pi^{e})=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}|<|\pi^{e}|,\penalty\ \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z})\equiv\mathbf{0}\bmod{\pi^{e}},\penalty\ \mathbf{z}\equiv\mathbf{b}\bmod{\pi^{\mu}}\right\}.

We note that
e\geqslant 2
, since we are assuming that
\mu\geqslant 1
.
Making the change of variables
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{v}+\pi^{e-1}\mathbf{w}
, for
\mathbf{v}
modulo
\pi^{e-1}
and
\mathbf{w}
modulo
\pi
, we deduce that

\displaystyle N^{\dagger}(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle=\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{v}|<|\pi^{e-1}|\\
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v})\equiv\mathbf{0}\bmod{\pi^{e-1}}\\
\mathbf{v}\equiv\mathbf{b}\bmod{\pi^{\mu}}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt\#\left\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{w}|<|\pi|\\
\mathbf{w}.\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{v})\equiv-f_{i}(\mathbf{v})/\pi^{e-1}\bmod{\pi}\\
\text{for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\end{array}\right\}.

Since
\gcd(\mathbf{b},\pi)=1
, we have
\gcd(\mathbf{v},\pi)=1
in the outer sum. Thus
the
R\times n
matrix
(\nabla f_{1}(\mathbf{v}),\dots,\nabla f_{R}(\mathbf{v}))
has full rank,
since
f_{1}=\dots=f_{R}=0
defines a smooth complete intersection in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.
We conclude that the inner quantity is precisely
|\pi|^{n-R}
, whence

(3.12)

N^{\dagger}(\pi^{e})=|\pi|^{n-R}N^{\dagger}(\pi^{e-1}).

We have therefore shown that

\displaystyle A(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle=|\pi|^{-e(n-R)+\mu n}\left(N^{\dagger}(\pi^{e})-|\pi|^{n-R}N^{\dagger}(\pi^{e-1})\right)=0.

The statement of the lemma follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We write
g=hg^{\prime}
where
h=\gcd(g,m)
and
g^{\prime}
is coprime to
m
.
Once combined with Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
,
it follows from
the multiplicativity of
A(g)
that

\displaystyle|A(g)|

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi^{e}\|g^{\prime}\end{subarray}}|A(\pi^{e})|\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi^{e}\|h\end{subarray}}|A(\pi^{e})|

\displaystyle\leqslant C^{\omega(g)}|g^{\prime}|^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)}|h|^{R}

\displaystyle=C^{\omega(g)}|g|^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)}|h|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)+R},

for some constant
C>0
that depends only on
d
and

.
Now clearly

\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{n}{d}-R\right)+R=\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\frac{n}{d}+\frac{R}{d},

and so the statement of
Proposition
3.1
follows.
∎

We may now return to the treatment of the singular series
(
3.3
). We begin by proving the following result.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(g,m)=1}|m|^{R}A(g).

Proof.

It follows from (
3.5
) that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\mid m^{d}\\
\gcd(h,g)=1\end{subarray}}A(g\tilde{m}),

where
\tilde{m}=m^{d}/h
.
Next, we factorise
\tilde{m}=m_{0}m_{1}
, where

m_{0}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi^{\nu}\|\tilde{m}\\
\pi\mid g\end{subarray}}\pi^{\nu},\quad m_{1}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi^{\nu}\|\tilde{m}\\
\pi\nmid g\end{subarray}}\pi^{\nu}.

Then we may write
g\tilde{m}=g_{0}m_{1}
, where

g_{0}=gm_{0}
. In particular,
\gcd(m_{0},m_{1})=1
and

\gcd(g_{0},m_{1})=1
, whence

A(g\tilde{m})=A(g_{0})A(m_{1}),

by multiplicativity.
The first part of Lemma
3.3
implies that the first product vanishes
as soon as
g
shares a common prime factor with
m
.
Therefore
m_{0}=1
and we deduce that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(g,m)=1}A(g)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\mid m^{d}\end{subarray}}A(\tilde{m}),

since the condition
\gcd(h,g)=1
is implied by the condition
\gcd(g,m)=1
when
h\mid m^{d}
.
But

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\mid m^{d}\end{subarray}}A(\tilde{m})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|m^{d}|\end{subarray}}S_{m^{d}}(\mathbf{a})=\frac{N(m^{d})}{|m|^{d(n-R)}},

by orthogonality of characters. Using the Chinese remainder theorem we see that

N(m^{d})=\prod_{\pi^{\mu}\|m}N(\pi^{d\mu})=\prod_{\pi^{\mu}\|m}|\pi|^{\mu n}N^{\dagger}(\pi^{d\mu}),

in the notation of (
3.11
). Appealing to (
3.12
), it now follows that

N(m^{d})=|m|^{n}\prod_{\pi^{\mu}\|m}|\pi|^{(n-R)(d-1)\mu}N^{\dagger}(\pi^{\mu})=|m|^{R+d(n-R)},

since
N^{\dagger}(m)=1
.
We deduce that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\mid m^{d}\end{subarray}}A(\tilde{m})=|m|^{R},

from which the lemma follows.

We define the truncated singular series

(3.13)

\mathfrak{S}(B)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
\text{$g$ monic}\\
0<|g|\leqslant q^{B}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a}),

for any
B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
. It follows from Lemma
3.4
that

(3.14)

\mathfrak{S}(B)=|m|^{R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$g$ monic}\\
0<|g|\leqslant q^{B}\\
\gcd(g,m)=1\end{subarray}}A(g).

Formally extending the sum to infinity, the singular series
(
3.3
) can also be written

(3.15)

\mathfrak{S}=|m|^{R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$g$ monic}\\
\gcd(g,m)=1\end{subarray}}A(g).

We shall need to establish the convergence of

\mathfrak{S}
, and show how well we can approximate it by

\mathfrak{S}(B)
.
For this we are led to study the quantity

(3.16)

\mathscr{S}(T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$g$ monic}\\
|g|=q^{T}\\
\gcd(g,m)=1\end{subarray}}\left|A(g)\right|,

for any
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
assume that
n>dR
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose that
\gcd(\mathbf{b},m)=1
and

f_{i}(\mathbf{b})\equiv 0\bmod{m}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
.
Then,
for any
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, we have

\mathscr{S}(T)\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{T(1+\varepsilon)-T(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)}.

Proof.

We shall use Proposition
3.1
to prove this result. First,
for any
\varepsilon>0
,
it follows from the divisor sum bound in function fields that

C^{\omega(g)}=O_{\varepsilon}(|g|^{\varepsilon})
, where the implied constant depends only on
\varepsilon
and
C
. (Note that,
as explained in
[
21
, Lemma 2.3]
,
the implied constant in this estimate is independent of
q
.)
We deduce from Proposition
3.1
that

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(T)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
\text{$g$ monic}\\
|g|=q^{T}\end{subarray}}|g|^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)+\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{T(1+\varepsilon)-T(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)}.

The statement of the lemma follows.
∎

The following result summarises our treatment of the singular series
\mathfrak{S}
and its approximation by the truncated singular series
\mathfrak{S}(B)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
assume that
(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)>1
. Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose that
\gcd(\mathbf{b},m)=1
and

f_{i}(\mathbf{b})\equiv 0\bmod{m}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
.
Then, for any
B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{S}-\mathfrak{S}(B)|\ll_{\varepsilon}\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{R\deg m+(B+1)(1+\varepsilon-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R))}.

Moreover,
\mathfrak{S}
is absolutely convergent and satisfies

0<\mathfrak{S}\ll q^{R\deg m}
.
The implied constants in these estimates depend only on
d,n,R
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

It follows from (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) that

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{S}-\mathfrak{S}(B)|

\displaystyle\leqslant q^{R\deg m}\sum_{k=B+1}^{\infty}\mathscr{S}(k),

in the notation of (
3.16
).
Since
(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)>1
, Lemma
3.5
yields

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{S}-\mathfrak{S}(B)|

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{R\deg m}\sum_{k=B+1}^{\infty}q^{k(1+\varepsilon)-k(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{R\deg m+(B+1)(1+\varepsilon-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R))}.

The first part of the lemma readily follows.
Next, we may reapply
Lemma
3.5
to deduce the upper bound

\mathfrak{S}\ll q^{R\deg m}
.

It remains to establish the positivity of
\mathfrak{S}
under the assumptions of the lemma, having already established that
\mathfrak{S}
is absolutely convergent.
The argument in
[
20
, Corollary 4.7.7]
ensures that

\mathfrak{S}>0

provided that there exists a smooth
\mathscr{O}_{\pi}
-point on
the variety
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{R}=0
,
for every finite prime
\pi\nmid m
. But this follows from the assumptions of the lemma, since
the completion of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
at
\pi
is a
C_{1}
field.
∎

3.2.
Singular integral

For any
\bm{\gamma}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
, we define

(3.17)

S_{\infty}(\bm{\gamma})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\psi(\bm{\gamma}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}.

In analogy to (
3.16
), let

(3.18)

\mathscr{I}(T)=\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|=q^{T}}S_{\infty}(\bm{\gamma})\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma},

for any
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
.
We begin by proving the following upper bound.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and assume that
n\geqslant dR
. For any
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
we have

\mathscr{I}(T)\ll\min\left\{q^{R(T+1)},q^{(1-\frac{2}{d})n+R-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)T}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
S\in\mathbb{Z}
and define

m(S)=\meas\left\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}:|f_{i}(\mathbf{v})|<q^{-S}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}.

We claim that

(3.19)

m(S)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $S<d$,}\\
q^{(d-1-S)n}N(t^{S+1-d})&\text{ if $S\geqslant d$,}\end{cases}

where

N(\pi^{e})=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}|<|\pi^{e}|,\penalty\ f_{i}(\mathbf{z})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{e}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\},

for any prime
\pi\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
.

Taking (
3.19
) on faith for the moment, let us see how it suffices to complete the proof of the lemma. Taking the trivial upper bound
|S_{\infty}(\bm{\gamma})|\leqslant 1
, we see that
|\mathscr{I}(T)|\leqslant q^{R(T+1)}
. This is less than or equal to

q^{n-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)T}
if

T\leqslant d-1
.
Assume henceforth that
T\geqslant d
.
It follows from (
2.1
) and (
3.19
) that

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}(T)

\displaystyle=q^{R(T+1)}\left(m(T+1)-q^{-R}m(T)\right)

\displaystyle=q^{R(T+1)+(d-2-T)n}\left(N(t^{T+2-d})-q^{n-R}N(t^{T+1-d})\right).

Taking
\pi=t
and
e=T+2-d
, it now follows from (
3.6
) and
Lemma
3.2
that

\displaystyle N(t^{T+2-d})-q^{n-R}N(t^{T+1-d})

\displaystyle=q^{(T+2-d)(n-R)}A(t^{T+2-d})

\displaystyle\ll q^{(T+2-d)(n-R)}\cdot q^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)(T+2-d)}.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}(T)

\displaystyle\ll q^{R(T+1)+(d-2-T)n}\cdot q^{(T+2-d)(n-R)}\cdot q^{-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)(T+2-d)}

\displaystyle=q^{(d-1)R-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)(T+2-d)}

\displaystyle=q^{g-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)T},

where

g=(d-1)R+(d-2)\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{n}{d}-R\right)\leqslant\left(1-\frac{2}{d}\right)n+R,

on taking
1-\frac{1}{d}\leqslant 1
.

We now turn to the proof of (
3.19
).
If
S<d
then the condition

|f_{i}(\mathbf{v})|<q^{-S}
is vacuous for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
.
In this case
m(S)=1
. Suppose next that
S\geqslant d
. Then

\displaystyle m(S)

\displaystyle=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\\
|\mathbf{w}|<q^{-S}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69046pt\#\left\{(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{S})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{nS}:|f_{i}(t^{-S}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\dots+t^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{S}+\mathbf{w})|<q^{-S}\right\}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=q^{-Sn}\#\left\{(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{S})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{nS}:|f_{i}(t^{-S}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\dots+t^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{S})|<q^{-S}\right\},

since the integrand doesn’t depend on
\mathbf{w}
.
Let
\mathbf{u}=t^{-S}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\dots+t^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{S}
be
one of the vectors appearing in the latter cardinality and suppose that

f_{i}(\mathbf{u})=A_{0}+A_{1}t^{-1}+\dots+A_{N}t^{-N},

for some
A_{0},\dots,A_{N}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
N\geqslant 0
. Then the condition
|f_{i}(\mathbf{u})|<q^{-S}
is equivalent to demanding that
A_{0}=\dots=A_{S}=0
.
We now consider the effect of writing
t^{-1}=\tau
and working over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\tau]
.
Then
\mathbf{u}
becomes
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\tau^{S}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\dots+\tau\mathbf{u}_{S}

and
f_{i}(\mathbf{u})
becomes

f_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})=A_{0}+A_{1}\tau+\dots+A_{N}\tau^{N}
, with the condition

A_{0}=\dots=A_{S}=0
now being equivalent to
f_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\equiv 0\bmod{\tau^{S+1}}
. Hence, on recalling that
S\geqslant d
, it follows that

\displaystyle m(S)

\displaystyle=q^{-Sn}\#\left\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[\tau]^{n}:|\mathbf{u}|<q^{S},\penalty\ f_{i}(\tau\mathbf{u})\equiv 0\bmod{\tau^{S+1}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}

\displaystyle=q^{-Sn}\#\left\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[\tau]^{n}:|\mathbf{u}|<q^{S},\penalty\ f_{i}(\mathbf{u})\equiv 0\bmod{\tau^{S+1-d}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}.

Note that
S>S+1-d
, since
d\geqslant 2
.
Breaking the
\mathbf{u}
into residue classes modulo
\tau^{S+1-d}
,
the claim (
3.19
) easily follows, which thereby completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We define the truncated singular integral

(3.20)

\mathfrak{I}(B)=\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|<q^{B}}S_{\infty}(\bm{\gamma})\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma},

for any
B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, together with the completed
singular integral

(3.21)

\mathfrak{I}=\int_{K_{\infty}^{R}}S_{\infty}(\bm{\gamma})\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}.

We are now ready to compare these two quantities, as summarised in the following result.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and assume that
n\geqslant dR
.
Let
B\geqslant 0
.
Then

|\mathfrak{I}-\mathfrak{I}(B)|\ll q^{n-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)B}.

Moreover,
\mathfrak{I}
is absolutely convergent and satisfies

0<\mathfrak{I}\ll q^{n}
.
The implied constants in these estimates depend only on
d,n,R
.

Proof.

We clearly have
(1-\frac{2}{d})n+R\leqslant n
if
n\geqslant dR
.
It follows from Lemma
3.7
that

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{I}-\mathfrak{I}(B)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant B}|\mathscr{I}(j)|\ll q^{n-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)B},

as claimed in the first part of the lemma.
The second part follows on taking

|\mathfrak{I}|\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant 0}|\mathscr{I}(j)|

and reapplying Lemma
3.7
.
Finally, the last part follows from

[
20
, Lemma 4.8.3]
, using the fact that

K_{\infty}
is a
C_{1}
field.
∎

4.
Application of the circle method

Throughout this section, let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of characteristic
>d
and let

f_{1},\dots,f_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be forms of degree
d
, cutting out a smooth complete intersection in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. In particular it follows from Remark
2.3

that
\sigma\leqslant R-1
, in the notation of (
2.2
).

Let
m\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
with
\deg b_{i}<\deg m
and

\gcd(\mathbf{b},m)=1
.
Our primary goal in this section is to adapt the work of Rydin
Myerson
[
24
]
to
produce an estimate for the quantity

(4.1)

N(\mathbf{f};P,m,\mathbf{b})=\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}|<q^{P+\deg m}\text{ and }\mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{b}\bmod{m}\\
f_{1}(\mathbf{g})=\dots=f_{R}(\mathbf{g})=0\end{array}\right\},

for any
P\in\mathbb{N}
. It will be crucial to have an estimate that depends explicitly on
P
and on
q
.

4.1.
Fourier analytic interpretation

Recall the definition (
3.1
) of

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}),

where we write
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{R})
for the vector of polynomials, and similarly for
\mathbf{F}
.
Letting

(4.2)

S(\bm{\alpha};P)=S(\bm{\alpha};P,m,\mathbf{b})=\sum_{|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P}}\psi(\bm{\alpha}.\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})),

for
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}
and
P\in\mathbb{N}
, we may now write

(4.3)

N(\mathbf{f};P,m,\mathbf{b})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{R}}S(\bm{\alpha};{P})\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an arbitrary real parameter that satisfies

\mathscr{C}>dR
. (We will make an acceptable choice of
\mathscr{C}
in (
5.1
).)
We will need to work under the following assumption. Given
\bm{\alpha}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
, we put
\|\bm{\alpha}\|=|\{\bm{\alpha}\}|
, where

\{\bm{\alpha}\}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}
is the fractional part of
\bm{\alpha}
.

Hypothesis 4.1
.

There exists
C\geqslant 1
such that

\min\left\{\frac{|S(\bm{\alpha};{P})|}{q^{nP}},\frac{|S(\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta};{P})|}{q^{nP}}\right\}\leqslant C\max\{q^{-dP+d-1}\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|^{-1},\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|^{\frac{1}{d-1}}q^{-1}\}^{\mathscr{C}},

for any
\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
and any
P\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following result is a function field analogue of
[
24
, Prop. 2.1]
, with the added feature
that the dependence on
q
is made precise.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be forms of degree
d\geqslant 2
.
Let
\mathscr{C}>dR
and suppose that Hypothesis
4.1
holds for
C\geqslant 1
.
Assume that

P>R(d-1)+d\deg m

and
(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)>1
.
Then

N(\mathbf{f};P,m,\mathbf{b})=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}q^{(n-dR)P-dR\deg m}+O_{\varepsilon}\left(E\right),

for any
\varepsilon>0
,
with

\displaystyle E=\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{(n-\mathscr{C})P}+q^{1+dR(1+\deg m)+\delta_{0}(1+d\deg m)(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})+(n-dR-\delta_{1})P}

\displaystyle\quad+q^{(n-dR-\delta_{2}+\varepsilon)P-(d-1)R\deg m+n+\delta_{2}(1+d\deg m+R(d-1))},

and where

(4.4)

\displaystyle\delta_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}}{(d-1)2^{d-1}R},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}\left(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\right),

and
\delta_{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{n}{d}-R\right)-1
.
Moreover, the singular series
\mathfrak{S}
is defined in (
3.3
) and the singular integral
\mathfrak{I}
in (
3.21
).
Finally, the implicit constant depends only on

d,n,R,\mathscr{C},C
and
\varepsilon
.

For each integer
J
in the range
0\leqslant J\leqslant P
, define

(4.6)

\mathfrak{M}(J)=\mathfrak{M}_{m}(J;P)=\hskip-5.69046pt\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\text{ monic}\\
0<|g|\leqslant q^{J}\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}:\left|\bm{\alpha}-\frac{\mathbf{a}}{gm^{d}}\right|<\frac{q^{J-dP+d}}{|gm^{d}|}\right\}

to be the
major arcs
of level
J
.
The corresponding
minor arcs
are defined to be

\mathfrak{m}(J)=\mathbb{T}^{R}\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J).

With this notation we may break the integral (
4.3
) into major and minor arcs, obtaining

(4.7)

N(\mathbf{f};P,m,\mathbf{b})=\int_{\mathfrak{M}(\Delta(J))}S(\bm{\alpha};P)\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}+\int_{\mathfrak{m}(\Delta(J))}S(\bm{\alpha};P)\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}.

We claim that the major arcs of level
J
are non-overlapping if

(4.8)

J\leqslant\frac{d(P-1)}{2}.

To see this, if we have overlapping arcs associated to distinct
\frac{\mathbf{a}}{gm^{d}}
and

\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}{g^{\prime}m^{d}}
, then we would be able to deduce that

\frac{1}{|m|^{d}|gg^{\prime}|}\leqslant\left|\frac{\mathbf{a}}{gm^{d}}-\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}{g^{\prime}m^{d}}\right|<\frac{q^{J-dP+d}}{|m|^{d}\min\{|g|,|g^{\prime}|\}},

by the ultrametric inequality. Since
|g|,|g^{\prime}|\leqslant q^{J}
, this leads to a contradiction under the inequality
(
4.8
).

The first step in the treatment of the minor arcs will be the following result, which is a function field analogue of
[
24
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}\subset\mathbb{T}^{R}

be a measurable subset.
Let
\mathscr{C}>dR
and assume that Hypothesis
4.1
holds with
C\geqslant 1
.
Suppose further that

\sup_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{B}}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\leqslant C_{0}q^{(n-\delta)P},

for some
\delta>0
and
C_{0}\geqslant 1
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{B}}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\ll\penalty\

\displaystyle C_{0}q^{(n-\mathscr{C})P}+C_{0}q^{1+dR(1+\deg m)+(n-dR-\delta(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}))P},

where the implicit constant depends only on
C,\mathscr{C},d
and
R
.

If
\delta
is such that
\delta P\geqslant\mathscr{C}\left(1-\frac{d\deg m}{d-1}\right)
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{B}}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\ll\penalty\

\displaystyle C_{0}q^{(n-\mathscr{C})P}+C_{0}q^{1+dR\deg m+(n-dR-\delta(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}))P}.

Proof.

Throughout this proof we allow any implied constant to depend on
C,\mathscr{C},d
and
R
.
For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we set

D(j)=\{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{B}:|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|>q^{j}\}

and we put
L(j)=\meas D(j)
.
Note that
L(j)\leqslant 1
, since
\mathbb{T}^{R}
has measure
1
.
Then, for any integers
J\leqslant K
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{B}}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}=\penalty\

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{B}\setminus D(J)}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}+\sum_{j=J}^{K-1}\int_{D(j)\setminus D(j+1)}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\int_{D(K)}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{J}+\sum_{j=J}^{K-1}L(j)q^{j+1}+L(K)\sup_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{B}}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{J}+\sum_{j=J}^{K-1}L(j)q^{j+1}+C_{0}q^{(n-\delta)P}L(K),

by the hypothesis in the lemma.
We shall take

J=nP-\lceil\mathscr{C}P\rceil\quad\text{ and }\quad K=nP-\lceil\delta P\rceil.

If
\delta P\geqslant\mathscr{C}P
then the upper bound in the lemma is a trivial consequence of
the hypothesis of the lemma, since
\mathfrak{B}
has measure at most
1
.
Hence we may proceed under the assumption that
J\leqslant K
.

We note that
q^{J}\leqslant q^{(n-\mathscr{C})P}
and so the first term is satisfactory.
The second and third term combine to give

\displaystyle\sum_{j=J}^{K-1}L(j)q^{j+1}+C_{0}q^{(n-\delta)P}L(K)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{0}\sum_{j=J}^{(n-\delta)P}L(j)q^{j+1}.

In particular, we have
j\leqslant nP-1
in this sum.
We claim that

(4.9)

L(j)\ll\begin{cases}q^{dR(\deg m-P-\frac{(j-nP)}{\mathscr{C}})}&\text{ if $j\leqslant nP+\mathscr{C}\left(\frac{d\deg m}{d-1}-1\right)$,}\\
q^{dR(1-P-\frac{j-nP}{d\mathscr{C}})}&\text{ if $j>nP+\mathscr{C}\left(\frac{d\deg m}{d-1}-1\right)$,}\end{cases}

noting that the second case only occurs when
\deg m=0
.
Taking this on faith for the moment, let us see how it suffices to complete the proof of the lemma.

When

\delta P\geqslant\mathscr{C}\left(1-\frac{d\deg m}{d-1}\right)
, we are always in the first case of
(
4.9
) and we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=J}^{(n-\delta)P}L(j)q^{j+1}

\displaystyle\ll q^{dR(\deg m-P)}\sum_{j=J}^{(n-\delta)P}q^{j+1-\frac{(j-nP)dR}{\mathscr{C}}}

\displaystyle=q^{1+dR(\deg m-P)+\frac{dnRP}{\mathscr{C}}}\sum_{j=J}^{(n-\delta)P}q^{j(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})}

\displaystyle\ll q^{1+dR(\deg m-P)+\frac{dnRP}{\mathscr{C}}}q^{(nP-\delta P)(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})}

\displaystyle\leqslant q^{1+dR\deg m+(n-dR)P-\delta(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})P},

since
\mathscr{C}>dR
, by assumption. This is satisfactory for the second part of the lemma.
Alternatively, when the assumption

\delta P\geqslant\mathscr{C}\left(1-\frac{d\deg m}{d-1}\right)
is not made, we can combine the estimates in
(
4.9
) to get the general upper bound

L(j)\ll q^{dR(1+\deg m-P-\frac{(j-nP)}{\mathscr{C}})}
,
whence

\displaystyle\sum_{j=J}^{(n-\delta)P}L(j)q^{j+1}

\displaystyle\ll q^{dR(1+\deg m-P)}\sum_{j=J}^{(n-\delta)P}q^{j+1-\frac{(j-nP)dR}{\mathscr{C}}}

\displaystyle\ll q^{1+dR(1+\deg m-P)+\frac{dnRP}{\mathscr{C}}}q^{(nP-\delta P)(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})}

\displaystyle\leqslant q^{1+dR(1+\deg m)+(n-dR)P-\delta(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})P},

which completes the claim in the first part of the lemma.

It remains to establish (
4.9
) for any
j\leqslant nP-1
.
For any
\bm{\alpha},\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}\in D(j)
, it follows from Hypothesis
4.1
that

\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|<C^{1/\mathscr{C}}q^{-dP+d-1-\frac{j-nP}{\mathscr{C}}}\quad\text{or}\quad C^{-(d-1)/\mathscr{C}}q^{(j-nP)\frac{d-1}{\mathscr{C}}+d-1}<\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|.

We set

\displaystyle r_{1}(j)

\displaystyle=-dP+d-1-d\deg m-\frac{j-nP}{\mathscr{C}}+\frac{\log_{q}C}{\mathscr{C}},

\displaystyle r_{2}(j)

\displaystyle=(j-nP)\frac{d-1}{\mathscr{C}}-d\deg m+d-1-\frac{(d-1)\log_{q}C}{\mathscr{C}},

in which notation the above conclusion can be written as

(4.10)

\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|<q^{\min\{0,r_{1}(j)+d\deg m\}}\quad\textnormal{ or }\quad q^{r_{2}(j)+d\deg m}<\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|.

Recall the definition of the boxes
from Section
2.4
.
As explained there, since
\mathfrak{B}\subset\mathbb{T}^{R}
,
we can cover
D(j)
by
at most

\max\left\{1,q^{R(-\lceil r_{2}(j)\rceil)}\right\}\leqslant\frac{1}{q^{R\min\{r_{2}(j),0\}}}

boxes of the form
B_{\lceil r_{2}(j)\rceil}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})
. Suppose that we are given any such box, with
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
. Then we claim that

(4.11)

\meas\left(B_{\lceil r_{2}(j)\rceil}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\cap D(j)\right)\leqslant q^{R(r_{1}(j)+d\deg m)},

from which
it follows that

L(j)\ll q^{R(r_{1}(j)+d\deg m-\min\{0,r_{2}(j)\})}.

Suppose first that

j\leqslant nP+\mathscr{C}\left(\frac{d\deg m}{d-1}-1\right)
.
Then
\min\{0,r_{2}(j)\}=r_{2}(j)
and it follows that

L(j)\ll q^{R(d\deg m-dP-\frac{(j-nP)d}{\mathscr{C}})}
,
as claimed. In the opposite case we have

\min\{0,r_{2}(j)\}\geqslant-\frac{(d-1)\log_{q}C}{\mathscr{C}}
and it follows that

\displaystyle L(j)

\displaystyle\ll q^{dR(1-P)-\frac{(j-nP)R}{\mathscr{C}}}.

The bound
claimed in (
4.9
) is now an easy consequence.

It remains to prove (
4.11
).
Clearly there is nothing to prove if the intersection is empty.
Thus, let us fix
\bm{\alpha}\in B_{\lceil r_{2}(j)\rceil}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\cap D(j)

and we suppose that
\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}\in B_{\lceil r_{2}(j)\rceil}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\cap D(j)
,
for some
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}
.
Then

\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|=\|m^{d}(\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime})-m^{d}(\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\|<q^{\lceil r_{2}(j)\rceil+d\deg m}.

Therefore, it follows from (
4.10
) that

\|m^{d}\bm{\beta}\|<q^{\min\{0,r_{1}(j)+d\deg m\}}
.
The proof of (
4.11
) now follows on appealing to Lemma
2.2
.
∎

4.2.
Treatment of the minor arcs

Recall from (
2.5
) the definition of the multilinear form associated to each form
f_{k}
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant R
.
Then
the multilinear form associated to
F_{k}
is

m^{d}\Psi^{(k)}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})
, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant R
.
Recall the definition of
N^{(v)}(J;\bm{\beta})
from (
2.15
).
The following result is a version of
[
20
, Lemma 4.3.4]
in which the implied constant has been made explicit.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
J,P\in\mathbb{N}
such that
J\leqslant P
and let
\bm{\alpha}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
.
Then we have

|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|^{2^{d-1}}\leqslant q^{n(2^{d-1}P-(d-1)J)}N^{(d-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha}).

Proof.

We begin with an application of
[
20
, Cor. 4.3.2]
, which leads to the inequality

|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|^{2^{d-1}}\leqslant q^{(2^{d-1}-d+1)nP}N^{(0)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha}),

for any
\bm{\alpha}\in K_{\infty}^{R}
.
Therefore, it suffices to prove

(4.12)

N^{(0)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha})\leqslant q^{-n(J-P)(d-1)}N^{(d-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha}),

which in turn follows by showing that

N^{(v-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha})\leqslant q^{-n(J-P)}N^{(v)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha}),

for each
1\leqslant v\leqslant d-1
.

This will be a straightforward consequence of the version of the shrinking lemma
found in work of Browning and Sawin
[
6
, Lemma 6.4]
. To see this,
fix
1\leqslant v\leqslant d-1
and
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
, for each
i\neq v
, satisfying

(4.13)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\mathbf{x}^{(v-1)}|<q^{J}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad|\mathbf{x}^{(v+1)}|,\ldots,|\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)}|<q^{P}.

Let

L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{R}\alpha^{(k)}m^{d}\Psi^{(k)}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(v-1)},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{(v+1)}\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)}),

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
We write

M(b;Z)=\#\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{2n}:|\mathbf{x}|<q^{b+Z},\penalty\ |L_{i}(\mathbf{x})+y_{i}|<q^{-b+Z},1\leqslant i\leqslant n\},

for any
b,Z\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Now choose
b,Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(4.14)

\displaystyle Z_{2}\leqslant 0,\quad b+Z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z},\quad b-Z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad Z_{2}-Z_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}.

Taking
a=b+Z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c=b-Z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
s=Z_{2}-Z_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
in

[
6
, Lemma 6.4]
, it follows
that

\frac{M(b;Z_{1})}{M(b;Z_{2})}\geqslant q^{n(Z_{1}-Z_{2})},

for each
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
with
i\neq v
, such that
(
4.13
) holds.
Setting

b=P\frac{v+1}{2}-J\frac{v-1}{2},\quad Z_{1}=-P\frac{v+1}{2}+J\frac{v+1}{2}

and

Z_{2}=-P\frac{v-1}{2}+J\frac{v-1}{2},

we clearly have

b+Z_{2}=P,\quad b-Z_{2}=Pv-J(v-1)\quad\textnormal{and}\quad Z_{2}-Z_{1}=P-J,

so that (
4.14
) holds.
Summing the above inequality over
all
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
with
i\neq v
, we arrive at the
inequality

N^{(v)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha})\geqslant q^{n(J-P)}N^{(v-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha}),

which is what we were supposed to prove.
∎

Given a form
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t][x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
, we put

\mathscr{M}_{f}=\left\{(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{(d-1)n}:\Psi_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})=0,\penalty\ 1\leqslant j\leqslant n\right\},

where
\Psi_{j}
are the multilinear forms associated to
f
, and we let

\mathscr{M}_{f}(J)=\mathscr{M}_{f}\cap\{(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{(d-1)n}:|\mathbf{x}^{(1)}|,\ldots,|\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)}|<q^{J}\}.

Let
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}
.
Since

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{M}_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}\cap\{(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{(d-1)n}:\mathbf{x}^{(1)}=\cdots=\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)}\},

in the notation of (
2.3
),
it follows that

\dim V_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}+(d-2)n\geqslant\dim\mathscr{M}_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}},

by the affine dimension theorem. Hence

(4.15)

\displaystyle\#\mathscr{M}_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}(J)\ll q^{((d-2)n+\dim V_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}})J},

where the implicit constant depends only on
d
and
n
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
J,P\in\mathbb{N}
such that
J\leqslant P
and let
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha^{(1)},\dots,\alpha^{(R)})\in K_{\infty}^{R}
. Then one of the following two alternatives holds:

(\textnormal{i})

We have

S(\bm{\alpha};P)\ll q^{nP-\frac{(n-\sigma_{\mathbf{f}})}{2^{d-1}}J},

where the implicit constant depends only on
d,n,R
, and
\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}
is defined in (
2.4
).

(\textnormal{ii})

There exist
g,a_{1},\ldots,a_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, with
g
monic, such that
\gcd(g,\mathbf{a})=1
,

0<|g|\leqslant q^{R(d-1)(J-1)}

and

|gm^{d}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{a}|<q^{-dP+R(d-1)J-(R-1)(d-1)}.

Proof.

For
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
,
let
\mathbf{M}_{j}
be the matrix whose columns are

\begin{pmatrix}\Psi^{(1)}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})\\
\vdots\\
\Psi^{(R)}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})\end{pmatrix},

for each
(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})
counted by
N^{(d-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha})
,
in the notation of
(
2.15
).
We then put these matrices together and define
\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{M}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{M}_{n})
.
We consider two cases depending on the rank of
\mathbf{M}
.

Suppose first that
\rank\mathbf{M}=R
. Then there exists a non-singular
R\times R
submatrix

\mathbf{M}_{0}=(\Psi^{(i)}_{j_{\ell}}(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{(d-1)}))_{1\leqslant i,\ell\leqslant R}.

By the definition of
N^{(d-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha})

there exist
s^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and
|\gamma^{(\ell)}|<q^{-dP+(d-1)J}
satisfying

(4.16)

\sum_{i=1}^{R}m^{d}\alpha^{(i)}\Psi^{(i)}_{j_{\ell}}(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{(d-1)})=s^{(\ell)}+\gamma^{(\ell)},

for each
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant R
.
Let
\mathbf{C}=({c}_{i,\ell})_{1\leqslant i,\ell\leqslant R}
be the cofactor matrix of
\mathbf{M}_{0}
, so that

\mathbf{C}
has entries in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and satisfies

\mathbf{M}_{0}\mathbf{C}^{T}=(\det\mathbf{M}_{0})\mathbf{I}_{R}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}_{0}^{T},

where
\mathbf{I}_{R}
is the
R\times R
identity matrix.
Any entry in
\mathbf{C}
has absolute value at most
q^{(R-1)(d-1)(J-1)}
,
since each entry of
\mathbf{M}_{0}
has absolute value

\leqslant q^{(d-1)(J-1)}
.
On multiplying both sides of
(
4.16
) by
\mathbf{C}
on the left, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(\det\mathbf{M}_{0})m^{d}\alpha^{(1)}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{R}c_{1,\ell}s^{(\ell)}\\
\vdots\\
(\det\mathbf{M}_{0})m^{d}\alpha^{(R)}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{R}c_{R,\ell}s^{(\ell)}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{C}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma^{(R)}\end{pmatrix}.

We therefore obtain

\displaystyle\left|(\det\mathbf{M}_{0})m^{d}\alpha^{(i)}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{R}c_{i,\ell}s^{(\ell)}\right|

\displaystyle<q^{(R-1)(d-1)(J-1)}q^{-dP+(d-1)J}

\displaystyle=q^{-dP+R(d-1)J-(R-1)(d-1)},

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
.
Set

g=\frac{\det\mathbf{M}_{0}}{D}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad a_{i}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{\ell=1}^{R}c_{i,\ell}s^{(\ell)},

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
,
where

D=\gcd\left(\det\mathbf{M}_{0},\sum_{\ell=1}^{R}c_{1,\ell}s^{(\ell)},\ldots,\sum_{\ell=1}^{R}c_{R,\ell}s^{(\ell)}\right).

On dividing through by an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
we can further assume that
g
is monic.
Noting that

0<|\det\mathbf{M}_{0}|\leqslant q^{R(d-1)(J-1)}
,
we therefore establish alternative (ii) of the lemma.

Next we suppose that
\rank\mathbf{M}<R
.
Then there exists
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{R}h_{i}\Psi^{(i)}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})=0,

for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
and every

(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})
counted by
N^{(d-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha})
.
Since

\sum_{i=1}^{R}h_{i}\Psi^{(i)}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)})=\Psi_{j}^{(\mathbf{h})}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(d-1)}),

for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
,
where
\Psi_{j}^{(\mathbf{h})}
are the multilinear forms associated to the form
\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}
,
it then follows from (
4.15
) that

N^{(d-1)}(J;m^{d}\bm{\alpha})\leqslant\#\mathscr{M}_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}}(J)\ll q^{J((d-2)n+\dim V_{\mathbf{h}.\mathbf{f}})}\leqslant q^{J((d-2)n+\sigma_{\mathbf{f}})},

where the implicit constant depends only on
d
and
n
, and
\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}
is given in (
2.4
).
Thus, by Lemma
4.4
, it follows that

S(\bm{\alpha};P)\ll q^{nP-\frac{(n-\sigma_{\mathbf{f}})}{2^{d-1}}J},

which is precisely the bound in alternative (i).
∎

Let us set

(4.17)

\Delta(J)=R(d-1)(J-1)

and recall the definition (
4.4
) of
\delta_{0}
.
Since the set of
\bm{\alpha}
satisfying alternative (ii) of Lemma
4.5
is contained
in the set of major arcs
\mathfrak{M}(\Delta(J))
, in the notation of (
4.6
), the following result is an immediate consequence of Lemma
4.5
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
J\leqslant P
be integers. Then

\sup_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{m}(\Delta(J))}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\ll q^{nP-(d-1)R\delta_{0}J},

where the implicit constant depends only on
d
,
n
and
R
, and where
\delta_{0}
and
\Delta(J)
are given by (
4.4
) and (
4.17
), respectively.

4.3.
A major arc approximation

Recall the notation (
3.2
) for
S_{g}(\mathbf{a})
and
(
3.17
) for
S_{\infty}(\bm{\beta})
.
We proceed by recording the following result, which refines work of Lee

[
20
, Lemma 4.6.2]
. (To be precise, Lee’s version operates under the slightly more stringent assumption that

|\bm{\beta}|<q^{-(d-1)P}|g|^{-1}|m|^{-d}
.)

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{N}
, let
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}
. Put
\bm{\alpha}=\mathbf{a}/g+\bm{\beta}
and assume
that
0<|g|\leqslant q^{P}
and

|\bm{\beta}|<q^{-(d-1)(P-1)}|g|^{-1}|m|^{-d}.

Then

S\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{g}+\bm{\beta};P\right)=q^{nP}S_{g}(\mathbf{a})S_{\infty}(m^{d}t^{dP}\bm{\beta}).

Proof.

We begin by deducing from (
4.2
) that

S\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{g}+\bm{\beta};P\right)=\sum_{|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P}}\psi\left(\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{g}+\bm{\beta}\right).\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right).

Let us write

\mathbf{x}=g\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}
, where

|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P}|g|^{-1}
and
|\mathbf{z}|<|g|.

Then it follows that

\displaystyle S\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{g}+\bm{\beta};P\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P}|g|^{-1}}\sum_{|\mathbf{z}|<|g|}\psi\left(\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{g}+\bm{\beta}\right).\mathbf{f}(m(g\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{b})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{z}|<|g|}\psi\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}.\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b})}{g}\right)\sum_{|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P}|g|^{-1}}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m(g\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{b})).

We claim that

\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m(g\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{b}))=\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(mg\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}))

in the inner sum,
under the assumptions of the lemma,
from which it will follow that

(4.18)

S\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{g}+\bm{\beta};P\right)=|g|^{n}S_{g}(\mathbf{a})\sum_{|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P}|g|^{-1}}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(mg\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b})).

To check the claim it will suffice to show that

(4.19)

\ord(\bm{\beta}.(\mathbf{f}(m(g\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{b})-\mathbf{f}(mg\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b})))<-1,

for any
|\mathbf{z}|<|g|
.
Note that
|mg\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}|\leqslant q^{\deg m+P-1}
. Hence it follows from Taylor expansion that
the left hand side of this expression is

\displaystyle\ord(\bm{\beta}.

\displaystyle(\mathbf{f}(m(g\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{b})-\mathbf{f}(mg\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b})))

\displaystyle\leqslant\ord\bm{\beta}+\max\left(\ord(m\mathbf{z})+(d-1)\ord(mg\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}),d\ord(m\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ord\bm{\beta}+\max\left(\deg m^{d}+\deg g-1+(d-1)(P-1),\deg m^{d}+d(\deg g-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\ord\bm{\beta}+(d-1)(P-1)+\deg g+\deg m^{d}-1

\displaystyle<-1,

as required.
Now that we have verified (
4.18
), a further application of (
4.19
) reveals that

\displaystyle\sum_{|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P}|g|^{-1}}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(mg\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b}))

\displaystyle=|g|^{-n}\sum_{|\mathbf{z}|<|g|}\sum_{|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P}|g|^{-1}}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m(g\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{b}))

\displaystyle=|g|^{-n}\sum_{|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P}}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})).

Hence

(4.20)

\displaystyle S\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{g}+\bm{\beta};P\right)=S_{g}(\mathbf{a})S(\bm{\beta};P).

Arguing as in (
4.19
), it is easy to check that

\ord(\bm{\beta}.(\mathbf{f}(m(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{b})-\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})))<-1,

for any
|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P}
and
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
.
Thus

\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}))=\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b})),

for any
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}|<1
. It now follows that

\displaystyle S(\bm{\beta};P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P}}\int_{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}|<1}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P}}\int_{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}|<1}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{|\mathbf{v}|<q^{P}}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=q^{nP}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\psi(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(t^{P}m\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v},

on making a change of variables.
Next, we claim that

\ord(t^{dP}\bm{\beta}.(\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}t^{-P})-\mathbf{f}(m\mathbf{v})))<-1,

for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Taking this on faith for the moment, this will imply that

\displaystyle S(\bm{\beta};P)=q^{nP}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\psi(m^{d}t^{dP}\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}=q^{nP}S_{\infty}(m^{d}t^{dP}\bm{\beta}),

which will complete the proof of the lemma, on substituting back into (
4.20
).
To check the claim, we note that the left hand side is

\displaystyle\leqslant dP+\ord\bm{\beta}+\max\left(\ord(\mathbf{b}t^{-P})+(d-1)\ord(m\mathbf{v}),d\ord(\mathbf{b}t^{-P})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant dP+\ord\bm{\beta}+\max\left(\deg m-1-P+(d-1)(\deg m-1),d(\deg m-1-P)\right)

\displaystyle=dP+\ord\bm{\beta}-P+d(\deg m-1)

\displaystyle=\ord\bm{\beta}+(d-1)(P-1)+\deg m^{d}-1

\displaystyle<-1,

as required.
∎

4.4.
Deduction of Theorem
4.2

Let
\mathscr{C}>dR
.
We now have everything in place to complete the proof of
Theorem
4.2
, which we tackle under the assumption that
Hypothesis
4.1
holds and

P>R(d-1)+d\deg m
.
Recall the notation for
\Delta(J)
in (
4.17
)
and the definition of the major arcs

\mathfrak{M}(\Delta(J))=\mathfrak{M}_{m}(\Delta(J);P)

in
(
4.6
).
We require the major arcs to be non-overlapping, which in view of (
4.8
), means that we need

(4.21)

\Delta(J)\leqslant\frac{d(P-1)}{2}.

We shall apply Lemma

4.7
with
gm^{d}
in place of
g
.
We also need to choose
J
in order that
this lemma is applicable on the major arcs
\mathfrak{M}(\Delta(J))
.
Thus we need
|gm^{d}|\leqslant q^{P}
and
|gm^{d}\bm{\beta}|<q^{-(d-1)(P-1)}|m|^{-d}
; in other words, we need

|g|\leqslant q^{P-d\deg m}\quad\text{ and }\quad|g\bm{\beta}|<q^{-(d-1)(P-1)-2d\deg m}.

But

\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{M}(\Delta(J))
implies that
|g|\leqslant q^{\Delta(J)}
and

. Thus we need

\Delta(J)\leqslant P-d\deg m
and
\Delta(J)-dP+d\leqslant-(d-1)(P-1)-d\deg m.

These two inequalities are satisfied if
\Delta(J)\leqslant P-1-d\deg m
,
which in the light of (
4.17
),
we can achieve by setting

(4.22)

J=\left\lfloor\frac{P-1-d\deg m}{R(d-1)}\right\rfloor+1\geqslant\frac{P-1-d\deg m}{R(d-1)}.

This is also enough to ensure that (
4.21
) holds.

We break the integral into major and minor arcs, as in (
4.7
).
For the minor arcs, we apply the first part of Lemma
4.3
with
\mathfrak{B}=\mathfrak{m}(\Delta(J))

and

\delta=(d-1)R\delta_{0}J/P
, and where
C_{0}
is the implicit constant from
Corollary
4.6
.
Hence it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{m}(\Delta(J))}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\ll\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{(n-\mathscr{C})P}+q^{1+dR(1+\deg m)+(n-dR)P-(d-1)R\delta_{0}J(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})}.

Recalling that
\mathscr{C}>dR
, together with the lower bound
(
4.22
),
we see that

\displaystyle 1+dR

\displaystyle(1+\deg m)+(n-dR)P-(d-1)R\delta_{0}J\left(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\leqslant 1+dR(1+\deg m)+(n-dR-\delta_{1})P+\delta_{0}(1+d\deg m)\left(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\right),

where

\delta_{1}
is given by
(
4.5
).
Hence

(4.23)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathfrak{m}(\Delta(J))}|S(\bm{\alpha};P)|\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}&\ll q^{(n-\mathscr{C})P}+q^{1+dR(1+\deg m)+\delta_{0}(1+d\deg m)(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})+(n-dR-\delta_{1})P}.\end{split}

Turning to
the major arcs, it follows from Lemma
4.7

that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{M}(\Delta(J))}S(\bm{\alpha};P)\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\text{ monic}\\
|g|\leqslant q^{\Delta(J)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}\int_{|\bm{\beta}|<\frac{q^{\Delta(J)-dP+d}}{|gm^{d}|}}S\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{gm^{d}}+\bm{\beta};P\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle=q^{(n-dR)P-dR\deg m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\text{ monic}\\
|g|\leqslant q^{\Delta(J)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})\mathfrak{I}(B),

where

\mathfrak{I}(B)
is given by
(
3.20
), with

B=\Delta(J)+d-\deg g
.
According to the first part of Lemma
3.8
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{I}-\mathfrak{I}(B)|

6.4
.
For now, let
e\geqslant 1
be an arbitrary integer.
In all the estimates that follow, the implied constants are only allowed to depend on
d,n
and
R
, unless indicated otherwise.
We shall drop the coprimality condition from the definition of
N(q,e,b)
, together with the constraint
that at least one of the polynomials in the tuple
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
has exact degree
e
.
Moreover, for each index
1\leqslant j\leqslant b
,
the condition

\mathbf{g}(c_{j})=\mathbf{a}_{j}
is equivalent to the congruence condition

\mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{a}_{j}\bmod{(t-c_{j})}.

In this way, we see that

N(q,e;\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{b})\leqslant\widehat{N}(q,e,b),

where

\widehat{N}(q,e,b)=\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}\deg g_{1},\dots,\deg g_{n}\leqslant e\\
f_{1}(\mathbf{g})=\dots=f_{R}(\mathbf{g})=0\\
\text{$\mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{a}_{j}\bmod{(t-c_{j})}$ for $1\leqslant j\leqslant b$}\end{array}\right\}.

Here we recall that
c_{1},\dots,c_{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and

\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

are given, with
c_{1},\dots,c_{b}
all distinct.
Since
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{b}
are vectors representing
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points on
X
, we also have
f_{k}(\mathbf{a}_{j})=0
for
1\leqslant k\leqslant R
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant b
.
Let

m=(t-c_{1})\cdots(t-c_{b}).

This is a square-free monic polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
of degree
b
.
It follows from the Chinese remainder theorem that there exists
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
, with

\gcd(\mathbf{s},m)=1
and

\deg s_{i}<b
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, such that
f_{i}(\mathbf{s})\equiv 0\bmod{m}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
and

\widehat{N}(q,e,b)=\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}\deg g_{1},\dots,\deg g_{n}\leqslant e\\
f_{1}(\mathbf{g})=\dots=f_{R}(\mathbf{g})=0\\
\mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{s}\bmod{m}\end{array}\right\}.

It is now clear that
\widehat{N}(q,e,m)=N(\mathbf{f},e+1-b,m,\mathbf{s})
, in the notation of (
4.1
). It follows from
(
4.3
) that

\widehat{N}(q,e,m)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{R}}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha},

where
S(\bm{\alpha})=S(\bm{\alpha};e+1-b,m,\mathbf{s})
is given by (
4.2
).

Recall the definition of the major arcs of level
J
that were introduced in (
4.6
).
Taking
P=e+1-b
, these take the shape

(6.2)

\mathfrak{M}(J)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\text{ monic}\\
0<|g|\leqslant q^{J}\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}:\left|gm^{d}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{a}\right|<q^{J-de+db}\right\}.

Note that
\mathfrak{M}(-1)=\emptyset
. Moreover, the function field
version of Dirichlet’s theorem in
[
20
, Lemma 4.5.1]

shows that for any
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}
there exist
monic
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and a vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}
,
with
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|
and
\gcd(g,\mathbf{a})=1
,
such that

|g|\leqslant q^{J}
and

\left|gm^{d}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{a}\right|^{R}<q^{-J}
. Hence it follows that
\mathfrak{M}(J)=\mathbb{T}^{R}
if
J\geqslant Rd(e-b)/(R+1)
.
We proceed by considering the contribution from the major arc of level
J=0
.

Lemma 6.5
.

We have

\int_{\mathfrak{M}(0)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}=q^{e(n-dR)+(n-R)(1-b)}\left(1+O(q^{-(n-R-1)/2})\right).

Proof.

We begin by observing that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{M}(0)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|m^{d}|\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e+1}\\
\mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{s}\bmod{m}\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g})}{m^{d}}\right)\int_{|\bm{\beta}|<q^{-de}}\psi\left(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g})\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|m^{d}|\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<|m^{d}|\\
\mathbf{h}\equiv\mathbf{s}\bmod{m}\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{h})}{m^{d}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e+1}\\
\mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{h}\bmod{m^{d}}\end{subarray}}\int_{|\bm{\beta}|<q^{-de}}\psi\left(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g})\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta},

on breaking into residue classes modulo
m^{d}
.
The inner sum over
\mathbf{g}
becomes

\displaystyle q^{-dRe}\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e+1},\penalty\ \mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{h}\bmod{m^{d}}\\
|f_{k}(\mathbf{g})|<q^{de}\text{ for $1\leqslant k\leqslant R$}\end{array}\right\},

by
[
19
, Lemma 1(f)]
. Any
\mathbf{g}
in the cardinality to be estimated can be written
as
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}_{0}t^{e}+\mathbf{k}
where
f_{k}(\mathbf{g}_{0})=0
, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant R
, and
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
has norm

|\mathbf{k}|<q^{e}
. Recall our assumption that
e\geqslant db+1
in the statement of Theorem
6.4
. For
fixed
\mathbf{g}_{0}
, we must count the number of
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
with

|\mathbf{k}|<q^{e}
, such that

\mathbf{k}
lies in a fixed residue class modulo
m^{d}
. The number of such
\mathbf{k}
is

(q^{e}/|m^{d}|)^{n}=q^{n(e-db)}
, since
\deg m=b
. Finally, the number of
\mathbf{g}_{0}
is

q^{n-R}+O(q^{(n-R+1)/2})
by Deligne’s resolution of the Weil conjectures, as presented in
Hooley
[
16
, Thm. 2 and Sec. 5]
, for example.
Moreover, the implied constant in this estimate only depends on
d
and
n
.
It therefore follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e+1}\\
\mathbf{g}\equiv\mathbf{h}\bmod{m^{d}}\end{subarray}}\int_{|\bm{\beta}|<q^{-de}}\psi\left(\bm{\beta}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g})\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle=q^{-dRe}\cdot q^{n(e-db)}\cdot\left(q^{n-R}+O(q^{(n-R+1)/2})\right)

\displaystyle=q^{e(n-dR)+n-dbn-R}\left(1+O(q^{-(n-R-1)/2}\right).

Finally, on applying orthogonality of characters, we see that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|m^{d}|\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<|m^{d}|\\
\mathbf{h}\equiv\mathbf{s}\bmod{m}\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}.\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{h})}{m^{d}}\right)=q^{dbR}N^{\dagger}(m^{d}),

in the notation of (
3.11
). But it follows from
(
3.12
) that

N^{\dagger}(m^{d})=q^{(d-1)(n-R)b}N^{\dagger}(m)=q^{(d-1)(n-R)b}.

Combining everything together readily leads to the statement of the lemma.
∎

In order to complete the proof of Theorem

6.4
, it therefore suffices to show that

(6.3)

\lim_{q\to\infty}q^{-\left(e(n-dR)+(n-R)(1-b)\right)}\sum_{J=0}^{\lceil Rd(e-b)/(R+1)\rceil-1}\left|\int_{\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\right|<1.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let

\mathscr{C}
be given by
(
5.1
), where
\nu(d)
is given by (
5.2
).
The work in Section
5

shows that
Hypothesis
4.1
holds with

.
It follows from (
6.1
) that

(6.4)

\frac{n-R+1}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}>\mathscr{C}>\frac{n-R+1}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}-\frac{n\varepsilon}{2^{d}(d-1)}.

Hence,
on assuming that

\varepsilon>0
is chosen to be sufficiently small,
it follows that
\mathscr{C}>dR

if

n\geqslant(d(d-1)2^{d+\nu(d)+1}+1)R
, which we henceforth assume.
(Although it actually suffices to take
n\geqslant(d(d-1)2^{d+\nu(d)}+1)R
here,
this would lead to a significantly worse lower bound for

in Theorem
6.4
.)

We need to study the integral

(6.5)

I_{J}=\int_{\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha},

where
\mathfrak{M}(J)
is given by (

).
We shall proceed differently, according to the size of

. Our treatment for larger

is summarised in the following result and is based on the treatment of the minor arcs in Section

.

Lemma 6.6
.

Assume that

n\geqslant(d(d-1)2^{d+\nu(d)+1}+1)R
, where
\nu(d)
is given by (
5.2
).
Then

\displaystyle I_{J}\ll\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{\left(n-\frac{n+1-R}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}\right)(e+1-b)+O(\varepsilon)}+q^{1+dRb+(n-dR)(e+1-b)-\frac{n-R+1}{2^{d}}\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{J}{R(d-1)}\right\rfloor\right)+O(\varepsilon)},

where the implied constant in the
O(\varepsilon)
term depends only on
d,e,n,R
and
b
.

Proof.

The plan is to recycle our treatment of the minor arcs from Section

4
with
P=e+1-b
.
We begin by applying Lemma
4.5
. Suppose that

\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)
and that alternative (ii) holds for some
J^{\prime}\leqslant e+1-b
. Then there exist
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}
, with
g

\gcd(g,\mathbf{a})=1
, such that

0<|g|\leqslant q^{R(d-1)(J^{\prime}-1)}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad|gm^{d}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{a}|<q^{-d(e+1-b)+R(d-1)J^{\prime}-(R-1)(d-1)}.

We shall apply this with

(6.6)

J^{\prime}=1+\left\lfloor\frac{J}{R(d-1)}\right\rfloor,

which would
imply that
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{M}(J)
, which is a contradiction.
We also need to check that
J^{\prime}\leqslant e+1-b
, which certainly holds if

\frac{J}{R(d-1)}\leqslant e-b.

Since
J\leqslant\lceil Rd(e-b)/(R+1)\rceil-1
, we see that it suffices to have

\frac{Rd(e-b)}{R+1}\leqslant(e-b)R(d-1),

which is equivalent to demanding that

d\leqslant(d-1)(R+1)
, an inequality that always holds for
d\geqslant 2
and
R\geqslant 1
.
Hence we find ourselves in alternative (i) of
Lemma
4.5
and
it follows from Remark
2.4
that

S(\bm{\alpha})\ll q^{n(e+1-b)-\frac{(n-R+1)J^{\prime}}{2^{d-1}}}=q^{(n-\delta)(e+1-b)},

for any
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{T}^{R}\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)
,
with

\delta=\frac{(n-R+1)J^{\prime}}{(e+1-b)2^{d-1}}>0.

We would now like to apply the second part of
Lemma
4.3
with
P=e+1-b
, for which we note that the assumed bound holds with

C_{0}\ll 1
. It remains to check that
\delta(e+1-b)\geqslant\mathscr{C}\left(1-\frac{db}{d-1}\right)
.
If
b\geqslant 1
then this is trivial, since the
right hand side is negative. Assuming that
b=0
, on the other hand, then it follows from (
6.4
) that
it suffices to have

\frac{(n-R+1)J^{\prime}}{2^{d-1}}\geqslant\frac{n-R+1}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)},

which is valid, since
J^{\prime}\geqslant 1
.
We finally deduce from the
second part of
Lemma
4.3
that

(6.7)

I_{J}\ll q^{(n-\mathscr{C})(e+1-b)}+q^{1+dRb+(n-dR-\delta(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}))(e+1-b)}.

Now
it follows from (
6.4
) that

q^{(n-\mathscr{C})(e+1-b)}\leqslant q^{\left(n-\frac{n+1-R}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}+O(\varepsilon)\right)(e+1-b)}.

This is satisfactory for the lemma.
Next,
(
6.4
) yields

\delta\left(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\right)(e+1-b)\geqslant\frac{(n-R+1)J^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\right)}{2^{d-1}},

where

(6.8)

1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}=1-\frac{dR2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}{n+1-R}+O(\varepsilon)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+O(\varepsilon),

under our assumption on
n
.
The lemma follows on recalling the expression (
6.6
) for
J^{\prime}

and putting these estimates together in (
6.7
).
∎

Our second estimate for
I_{J}
is more appropriate for smaller values of

and draws on the major arc analysis in Section

Lemma 6.7
.

Assume that
n\geqslant dR
and

0\leqslant J\leqslant e-db-b-1.

Then

\displaystyle I_{J}\ll_{\varepsilon}\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)+dR-R(d-1)b-(J+1)\left((1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)-1\right)+O(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\quad+q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)-R(d-1)b+(1-\frac{2}{d})n+R-(J+d)\left((1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)-1\right)+O(\varepsilon)},

where the implied constant in the
O(\varepsilon)
term depends only on
d,e,n,R
and
b
.

Proof.

Recall the definition
(
6.2
) of the major arcs. By (
4.8
), the major arcs of level
J+1
are non-overlapping if
J+1\leqslant\frac{1}{2}d(e-b)
. Our assumption

J\leqslant e-db-b-1
is sufficient to ensure this property.
Hence

(6.9)

I_{J}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\text{ monic}\\
0<|g|\leqslant q^{J+1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}\int_{R_{J}\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}

in (
6.5
),
where

R_{J}=\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{T}^{R}:\left|\bm{\alpha}-\frac{\mathbf{a}}{gm^{d}}\right|<\frac{q^{J+1-de}}{|g|}\right\}.

Since
\bm{\alpha}\not\in\mathfrak{M}(J)
, we may assume in (
6.9
) that

(6.10)

|g|=q^{J+1}\quad\text{ or }\quad\left|\bm{\beta}\right|=\frac{q^{J-de}}{|g|},

where
\bm{\beta}=\bm{\alpha}-\frac{\mathbf{a}}{gm^{d}}
.
We proceed by applying Lemma
4.7
, which yields

S(\bm{\alpha})=q^{n(e+1-b)}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})S_{\infty}(m^{d}t^{d(e+1-b)}\bm{\beta}),

provided that the hypotheses of the lemma are met. The first hypothesis is the upper bound
|gm^{d}|\leqslant q^{e+1-b}
, which is
clearly met if
J\leqslant e-db-b
. The second hypothesis is the bound

|\bm{\beta}|<q^{-(d-1)(e-b)}|g|^{-1}|m|^{-2d},

for which it suffices to have

q^{J+1-de}\leqslant q^{-(d-1)(e-b)}|m|^{-2d}=q^{-de-db+e-b}.

Thus we see that both hypotheses hold if
J\leqslant e-db-b-1
, which is what we assumed in the statement of the lemma.

We have therefore shown that

I_{J}=q^{n(e+1-b)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\text{ monic}\\
0<|g|\leqslant q^{J+1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})\int_{|\bm{\beta}|<q^{J+1-de}/|g|}S_{\infty}(m^{d}t^{d(e+1-b)}\bm{\beta})\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta},

where

and
\bm{\beta}
are constrained to satisfy (

).
Making a change of variables, we see that

\int_{|\bm{\beta}|<q^{J+1-de}/|g|}S_{\infty}(m^{d}t^{d(e+1-b)}\bm{\beta})\mathrm{d}\bm{\beta}=q^{-dRb-dR(e+1-b)}\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|<q^{J+1+d}/|g|}S_{\infty}(\bm{\gamma})\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}.

Hence

I_{J}=q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)-dRb}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\text{ monic}\\
0<|g|\leqslant q^{J+1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|<q^{J+1+d}/|g|}S_{\infty}(\bm{\gamma})\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma},

where
g
and
\bm{\gamma}
are constrained to satisfy

|g|=q^{J+1}\quad\text{ or }\quad\left|\bm{\gamma}\right|=\frac{q^{J+d}}{|g|}.

Recalling the notation (
3.4
) for

,
it follows from the proof of (

) that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{R}\\
|\mathbf{a}|<|gm^{d}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},g)=1\end{subarray}}S_{gm^{d}}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(g,m)=1}|m|^{R}A(g).

Hence, on recalling the notation
(
3.16
) and (

|I_{J}|\leqslant q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)-R(d-1)b}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathscr{E}_{1}\cup\mathscr{E}_{2}}\mathscr{S}(i)\cdot|\mathscr{I}(j)|,

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{(J+1,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:j\leqslant d-1\right\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{(i,J+d-i)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:0\leqslant i\leqslant J+1\right\}.

Lemma
3.5
implies
that

\mathscr{S}(i)\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{i\left(1-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)+O(\varepsilon)\right)},

where the implied constant in

term depends only on

and

.
Likewise,
Lemma

\mathscr{I}(j)\ll\begin{cases}q^{R+jR}&\text{ if $j\leqslant d-1$,}\\
q^{(1-\frac{2}{d})n+R-j(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)}&\text{ if $j\geqslant d-1$.}\end{cases}

On noting that

\displaystyle I_{J}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)+R-R(d-1)b}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,j)\in\mathscr{E}_{1}\end{subarray}}q^{-i\left((1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)-1+O(\varepsilon)\right)}\cdot q^{jR}

\displaystyle I_{J}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)-R(d-1)b+(1-\frac{2}{d})n+R}

∎

It is now time to use this pair of results to prove that (

It follows from Lemma

\displaystyle\sum_{J=J_{0}+1}^{J_{1}}I_{J}\ll_{\varepsilon}\penalty\

\displaystyle eq^{\left(n-\frac{n+1-R}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}\right)(e+1-b)+O(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\quad+eq^{1+dRb+(n-dR)(e+1-b)+O(\varepsilon)}q^{-\frac{n-R+1}{2^{d}}\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{J_{0}+1}{R(d-1)}\right\rfloor\right)}.

Likewise, Lemma
6.7
implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{J=0}^{J_{0}}I_{J}\ll_{\varepsilon}\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)+dR-R(d-1)b+1-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)+O(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle+q^{(n-dR)(e+1-b)-R(d-1)b+(1-\frac{2}{d})n+R+d-(d-1)(\frac{n}{d}-R)+O(\varepsilon)}.

Putting these together,
we may conclude that (

) holds, as desired,
provided that our parameters

and

are chosen in such a way that

But, on choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, this is equivalent to demanding the veracity of the four inequalities

(6.11)

\left(n-\frac{n+1-R}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}\right)(e+1-b)<(n-dR)(e+1-b)+(d-1)R(1-b),

(6.12)

1+dRb-\frac{n-R+1}{2^{d}}\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{J_{0}+1}{R(d-1)}\right\rfloor\right)<(d-1)R(1-b),

(6.13)

1-\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{n}{d}-R\right)<-R,

and

(6.14)

\left(1-\frac{2}{d}\right)n+R+d-(d-1)\left(\frac{n}{d}-R\right)<(d-1)R.

Our assumptions on
d
and
n
plainly ensure that both
(
6.13
) and
(
6.14
) hold.
Moreover,
(
6.11
) holds if and only if

(d-1)R(b-1)<(e+1-b)\left(\frac{n+1-R}{2^{d+\nu(d)}(d-1)}-dR\right).

Since

e\geqslant(d+1)b+1\geqslant 2b+1
and

n\geqslant(d(d-1)2^{d+\nu(d)+1}+1)R
, the right hand side is

>(d-1)R(b-1)
, as required.
Thus (
6.11
) holds.

It remains to check (
6.12
), for which we
note that

1+\left\lfloor\frac{J_{0}+1}{R(d-1)}\right\rfloor\geqslant\frac{J_{0}+2}{R(d-1)}.

Recalling that
J_{0}=e-db-b-1

and
n\geqslant(d(d-1)2^{d+1}+1)R
,
we see that (
6.12
) holds if

2d(e-db-b+1)\geqslant 1-R(d-1)+b(2d-1)R.

Since
d\geqslant 2
, it suffices to have

e\geqslant\left(d+1+R\right)b.

The proof of Theorem
6.4
is now complete.

7.
Low degree rational curves

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.4
.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth complete intersection over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
cut out by
R
hypersurfaces of degree
d
.
We shall always assume that

\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d
and that
n
satisfies the bound in (
1.1
).
As usual, we denote by

f_{1},\ldots,f_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
the relevant forms of degree
d\geqslant 2
.

Recall

N(q,e)
from
Definition
6.1
, which counts the number of tuples
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
, of degree at most
e
, at least one of degree exactly
e
, with no common zero, such that
f_{1}(\mathbf{g})=\dots=f_{R}(\mathbf{g})=0
. It follows from Proposition
6.3
that

(7.1)

\#\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\frac{N(q,e)}{(q-1)(q^{3}-q)}.

In order to prove
Theorem
1.4
it will suffice to show that the right hand side is positive if
q

is sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
n
, and
e\geqslant 2(d-1)R+3d
.

7.1.
Counting in the affine cone

Let
P\in\mathbb{N}
. For now we simply assume that
q\geqslant(d-1)^{n}
and we fix the same choice of

\mathscr{C}
in (
5.1
). In particular, we have
\mathscr{C}>dR
under the assumption (
1.1
),
and we saw at the close of Section
5
that
Hypothesis
4.1
holds with
C=O(1)
.

We begin by focusing on the counting function

N(\mathbf{f};P)=N(\mathbf{f};P,1,\mathbf{0})
defined in (
4.1
), with
m=1
and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}
.
Assume that

(7.2)

P>(d-1)R.

Then
it follows from Theorem
4.2
that

(7.3)

N(\mathbf{f};P)=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}q^{(n-dR)P}+O_{\varepsilon}\left(E(P)\right),

for any
\varepsilon>0
,
with

\displaystyle E(P)=\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{(n-\mathscr{C})P}+q^{1+dR+\delta_{0}(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})+(n-dR-\delta_{1})P}+q^{(n-dR-\delta_{2}+\varepsilon)P+n+\delta_{2}(1+R(d-1))},

and where

(7.4)

\delta_{0}=\frac{n-\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}}{(d-1)2^{d-1}R},\quad\delta_{1}=\delta_{0}\left(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\right),\quad\delta_{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{n}{d}-R\right)-1.

Here, the implicit constant depends only on

d,n,R
and
\varepsilon
.

The singular series
\mathfrak{S}
is defined in (
3.3
) and the singular integral
\mathfrak{I}
in (
3.21
).
We shall need to develop precise estimates for these quantities.

Lemma 7.1
.

Assume
n>2(d-1)R
and that
q
is sufficiently large with respect to
d
and
n
. Then

\mathfrak{S}=1+O(q^{-\frac{n-R-3}{2}}).

Proof.

For any prime
\pi\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, define

\sigma(\pi)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathbf{a}|<|\pi^{j}|\\
\gcd(\mathbf{a},\pi)=1\end{subarray}}S_{\pi^{j}}(\mathbf{a}),

in the notation of (
3.2
). In the light of the notation (
3.4
), and the assumed
lower bound
n>2(d-1)R
,
it follows from
Lemma
3.2
that this sum converges. Moreover,
we clearly have

\mathfrak{S}=\prod_{\pi}\sigma(\pi)

in (
3.3
).
Using Ramanujan sums and recalling that
m=1
, it is routine to check that

\sigma(\pi)=\lim_{k\to\infty}|\pi|^{-k(n-R)}N(\pi^{k}),

in the notation of (
3.7
),
where

N(\pi^{k})=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}|<|\pi^{k}|,\penalty\ f_{i}(\mathbf{z})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{k}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}.

It will also be convenient to recall that

N^{*}(\pi^{k})=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:|\mathbf{z}|<|\pi^{k}|,\penalty\ \pi\nmid\mathbf{z},\penalty\ f_{i}(\mathbf{z})\equiv 0\bmod{\pi^{k}}\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant R$}\right\}.

We proceed by adapting the proof of Lemma
3.2
to prove that

(7.5)

N(\pi^{e})=\frac{N^{*}(\pi)}{|\pi|^{n-R}}C_{\infty}(\pi)|\pi|^{(n-R)e}+O\left(|\pi|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})en}\right),

where

C_{\infty}(\pi)=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}|\pi|^{-j(n-dR)}.

First, we deduce from (
3.8
) that

N^{*}(\pi^{k})=|\pi|^{(n-R)(k-1)}N^{*}(\pi),

for any
k\geqslant 1
. It now follows from (
3.10
)
that

\displaystyle N(\pi^{e})

\displaystyle=N^{*}(\pi)|\pi|^{(n-R)(e-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j<e/d\end{subarray}}|\pi|^{(d-1)jn-(n-R)dj}+O\left(|\pi|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})en}\right)

\displaystyle=N^{*}(\pi)|\pi|^{(n-R)(e-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j<e/d\end{subarray}}|\pi|^{-j(n-dR)}+O\left(|\pi|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})en}\right),

for
e>d
.
It is clear that

\left|C_{\infty}(\pi)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j<e/d\end{subarray}}|\pi|^{-j(n-dR)}\right|\ll|\pi|^{-\frac{e}{d}(n-dR)}.

Putting these together therefore establishes the claimed bound (
7.5
).

Note that

(7.6)

C_{\infty}(\pi)=1+O(|\pi|^{-(n-dR)}).

Moreover, according to (
3.9
), we have

N^{*}(\pi)=|\pi|^{n-R}(1+O(|\pi|^{-\frac{n-R-1}{2}}))
.
It now follows
from
(
7.5
) and (
7.6
) that

\displaystyle\sigma(\pi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}|\pi|^{-k(n-R)}\left(\frac{N^{*}(\pi)}{|\pi|^{n-R}}C_{\infty}(\pi)|\pi|^{(n-R)k}+O(|\pi|^{(1-\frac{1}{d})kn})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{*}(\pi)}{|\pi|^{n-R}}C_{\infty}(\pi)

\displaystyle=1+J(\pi),

where
J(\pi)=|\pi|^{-\frac{n-R-1}{2}}
.
Moreover, the implicit constant depends only on
d
and
n
.
Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}J(\pi)\ll\sum_{\pi}q^{-(\frac{n-R-1}{2})\deg\pi}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q^{j-(\frac{n-R-1}{2})j}\ll q^{-\frac{n-R-3}{2}}.

Let us assume
q
is sufficiently large with respect to
d
and
n
, so that we can use Taylor expansion to deduce that

\log(1+J(\pi))=O(|J(\pi)|),

for an absolute implied constant.
Then it follows that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\pi}\left(1+J(\pi)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{\pi}\log(1+J(\pi))\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(O\left(\sum_{\pi}|J(\pi)|\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(O(q^{-\frac{n-R-3}{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=1+O(q^{-\frac{n-R-3}{2}}),

where we again used the fact that
q
is sufficiently large to make use of the Taylor series expansion of
\exp
.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Assume
n\geqslant 2(d-1)R
. Then

\mathfrak{I}=q^{R(d-1)}(1+O(q^{-\frac{n-R-1}{2}})).

Proof.

Let
T>2d-1
. Firstly, it follows from Lemma
3.8
that

|\mathfrak{I}-\mathfrak{I}(T)|\ll q^{n-(1-\frac{1}{d})(\frac{n}{d}-R)T}.

It also follows from combining the proof of Lemma
3.7
with
(
7.5
) that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(T)

\displaystyle=q^{RT}m(T)

\displaystyle=q^{(d-1-T)n+RT}N(t^{T+1-d})

\displaystyle=q^{(d-1-T)n+RT}\left(\frac{N^{*}(t)}{q^{n-R}}C_{\infty}(t)q^{(n-R)(T+1-d)}+O\left(q^{(1-\frac{1}{d})(T+1-d)n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{*}(t)}{q^{n-R}}C_{\infty}(t)q^{R(d-1)}+O\left(q^{-\frac{T}{d}(n-dR)-\frac{1}{d}(1-d)n}\right).

But (
3.9
) and (
7.6
) yield

\frac{N^{*}(t)}{q^{n-R}}C_{\infty}(t)=1+O(q^{-\frac{n-R-1}{2}}),

under the assumption on
n
in the lemma.
Therefore, on choosing
T
to be sufficiently large, we easily arrive at the desired estimate.
∎

7.2.
Möbius inversion

Now that we have an estimate for
N(\mathbf{f};P)
in
(
7.3
), we may go on to deduce an estimate for the
quantity

N^{*}(\mathbf{f};P)=\#\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}_{\textnormal{prim}}:1\leqslant|\mathbf{g}|<q^{P},f_{1}(\mathbf{g})=\cdots=f_{R}(\mathbf{g})=0\},

in which
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}_{\textnormal{prim}}
means that

\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=1
.
As usual we shall assume that
q
is sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
n
.
We shall also need to assume that
P
satisfies the inequality
(
7.2
).

Let
\mu:\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\to\{0,\pm 1\}
denote the function field version of the Möbius function.
Then Möbius inversion yields

\displaystyle N^{*}(\mathbf{f};P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
k\,\textnormal{monic}\\
|k|<q^{P}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\#\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}:1\leqslant|\mathbf{g}|<q^{P-\deg k},f_{1}(\mathbf{g})=\cdots=f_{R}(\mathbf{g})=0\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
k\,\textnormal{monic}\\
|k|<q^{P}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)(N(\mathbf{f};P-\deg k)-1).

We will need to truncate the sum over
k
, so that
P-\deg k
satisfies the constraint
(
7.2
). Thus

\displaystyle N^{*}(\mathbf{f};P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
k\,\textnormal{monic}\\
|k|<q^{P-(d-1)R}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)N(\mathbf{f};P-\deg k)+O\left(q^{P}+L(P)\right),

where

L(P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
k\,\textnormal{monic}\\
|k|\geqslant q^{P-(d-1)R}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359ptN(\mathbf{f};P-\deg k)\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
k\,\textnormal{monic}\\
|k|\geqslant q^{P-(d-1)R}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt\left(\frac{q^{P}}{|k|}\right)^{n}\ll q^{(d-1)R(n-1)+P}.

On appealing to (
7.3
), we therefore deduce that

N^{*}(\mathbf{f};P)=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}q^{(n-dR)P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
k\,\textnormal{monic}\\
|k|<q^{P-(d-1)R}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)q^{-(n-dR)\deg k}+O\left(q^{(d-1)R(n-1)+P}+E(P)\right),

since

\sum_{k}E(P-\deg k)\ll E(P)
.

We see that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\\
k\,\textnormal{monic}\\
|k|<q^{P-(d-1)R}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)q^{-(n-dR)\deg k}=1+O\left(\sum_{1\leqslant j<P}q^{j-(n-dR)j}\right)=1+O(q^{1-(n-dR)}).

Our assumption (
1.1
) implies that
n>2(d-1)R
. Hence, on combining these estimates with Lemmas
7.1
and
7.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle N^{*}(\mathbf{f};P)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}q^{(n-dR)P}(1+O(q^{1-(n-dR)}))+O(q^{(d-1)R(n-1)+P}+E(P))

\displaystyle=q^{(n-dR)P+(d-1)R}(1+O(q^{-\frac{n-R-3}{2}}))+O(q^{(d-1)R(n-1)+P}+E(P))

\displaystyle=q^{(n-dR)P+(d-1)R}\left(1+O(\widetilde{E}(P))\right),

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}(P)=\penalty\

\displaystyle q^{(d-1)R(n-2)-(n-dR-1)P}+q^{-\frac{n-R-3}{2}}+q^{(dR-\mathscr{C})P-(d-1)R}

\displaystyle+q^{1+R+\delta_{0}(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}})-\delta_{1}P}+q^{-\delta_{2}(P-1-(d-1)R)+\varepsilon P+n-(d-1)R}.

7.3.
Completion of the proof

We are now ready to conclude the proof
of Theorem
1.4
, based on the expression (
7.1
).
Assuming that
q
is sufficiently large with respect to
d
and
n
,
it is clear from our work above that

\displaystyle N(q,e)

\displaystyle=N^{*}(\mathbf{f};e+1)-N^{*}(\mathbf{f};e)

\displaystyle=q^{(n-dR)(e+1)+(d-1)R}\left(1-q^{-(n-dR)}\right)+O(q^{(n-dR)(e+1)+(d-1)R}\widetilde{E}(e+1)),

under the assumption that
e>(d-1)R
.

Clearly
n-dR>0
,
\mathscr{C}>dR
and

\frac{n-R-3}{2}>0
under our assumption on
n
in (
1.1
).
Thus, in the light of our remarks on the size of
q
,
we may conclude that

\#\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)(\mathbb{F}_{q})>0
if the three inequalities

(7.7)

\begin{split}(n-dR-1)(e+1)&>(d-1)R(n-2),\\
\delta_{1}(e+1)&>1+R+\delta_{0}\left(1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\right),\\
\delta_{2}(e-(d-1)R)+(d-1)R&>\varepsilon(e+1)+n,\end{split}

all hold, for any
\varepsilon>0
. Recalling (
7.4
), the second inequality is equivalent to

e>\frac{1+R}{\delta_{1}}.

Now it follows from our assumption (
1.1
) that

n\geqslant d(d-1)2^{d+\nu(d)+1}R+R
, where
\nu(d)
is given by (
5.2
).
In particular
1-\frac{dR}{\mathscr{C}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+O(\varepsilon)
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, by (
6.8
).
But then, since
Remark
2.4
yields

\sigma_{\mathbf{f}}\leqslant R-1
,
it follows from (
7.4
) that

\delta_{1}\geqslant d2^{\nu(d)+1}+O(\varepsilon)\geqslant d,

on choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small. We conclude that the second inequality in (
7.7
) is satisfied if
e\geqslant R+1
.
This is implied by our assumption
e>(d-1)R
.
We now make the more stringent assumption

e\geqslant 2(d-1)R
, under which the first inequality is obvious.
Finally, we
make the even more stringent assumption
that

e\geqslant 2(d-1)R+3d
and we check the third inequality in
(
7.7
)
with
\varepsilon=\delta_{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}
, for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, which is then equivalent to

\delta_{2}\left(e(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})-(d-1)R-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)+(d-1)R>n.

It follows from (
7.4
) that

\delta_{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{n}{d}-R\right)-1\geqslant\frac{n-dR-2d}{2d},

since
1-\frac{1}{d}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}

for
d\geqslant 2
. Hence,
since
e\geqslant 2(d-1)R+3d
, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{2}\left(e(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})-(d-1)R-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{n-dR-2d}{2d}\cdot\left((1-2\varepsilon^{\prime})(d-1)R+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})3d-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right).

Taking
R\geqslant 1

and

\varepsilon^{\prime}
sufficiently small,
it therefore follows that

\displaystyle\delta_{2}\left(e(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})-(d-1)R-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)+(d-1)R

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{(4d-1)(n-dR-2d)}{2d}-O(\varepsilon^{\prime}).

This is greater than
n
under our assumptions on
n
.
This therefore completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
