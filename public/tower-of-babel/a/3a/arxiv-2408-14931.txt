Adaptive Mesh Construction for the Numerical Solution of Stochastic Differential Equations with Markovian Switching
Cónall Kelly, Kate O’Donovan

Abstract
. We demonstrate an approach to the numerical solution of nonlinear stochastic differential equations with Markovian switching. Such equations describe the stochastic dynamics of processes where the drift and diffusion coefficients are subject to random state changes according to a Markov chain with finite state space. We propose a variant of the Jump Adapted-Adaptive approach introduced by K, Lord, & Sun (2025) to construct nonuniform meshes for explicit numerical schemes that adjust timesteps locally to rapid changes in the numerical solution and which also incorporate the switching times of an underlying Markov chain as meshpoints. It is shown that a hybrid scheme using such a mesh that combines an efficient explicit method (to be used frequently) and a potentially inefficient backstop method (to be used occasionally) will display strong convergence in mean-square of order δ \delta if both methods satisfy a mean-square consistency condition of the same order in the absence of switching. We demonstrate the construction of an order δ = 1 \delta=1 method of this type and apply it to generate empirical distributions of a nonlinear SDE model of telomere length in DNA replication.



1.
Introduction

We examine a method of constructing adaptive meshes for the efficient numerical solution of
d
-dimensional system of stochastic differential equations with Markovian switching (SDEwMS) of the form

(1)

\begin{split}dX(t)&=f(X(t),r(t))dt+g(X(t),r(t))dW(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,\\
X(0)&=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{split}

In (
1
), the drift and diffusion coefficients f and g take the form

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\text{and}\quad g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}.

The stochastic dynamics of a process governed by (
1
) are influenced by two independent sources of noise. The process
r
dictates changes occurring at random intervals in the functional form of
f
and
g
, and is described by a scalar continuous time Markov chain taking values in the set
\mathbb{S}=\{1,2,..,L\}
. The process
W
is an
m
-dimensional Brownian motion and captures the effect of a diffusion process on trajectories. We characterise both mathematically in Section
2
.

We are motivated by the following example where a scalar SDE is used to model the shortening over time of telomere length in DNA replication in jackdaws:

(2)

dL(t)=-\left(c+aL(t)^{2}\right)dt+\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}aL(t)^{3}}dW(t),

where
W
is a scalar standard Brownian motion. As presented in
[
2
]
, the model arises as the Langevin equation associated with the Fokker-Planck PDE

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial l}\left\{\left(c+\frac{1}{2}al^{2}\right)p\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial l^{2}}\left\{\left(\frac{1}{3}al^{3}\right)p\right\},

which governs the probability density function
p(t,l)
for the telomere length
l
at time
t
. By simulating an ensemble of trajectories of (
2
) we can produce a density histogram of lengths
l=L
at a fixed time
t=T
which serves to numerically approximate
p(T,L)
. The parameters
c
and
a
control the underlying decay rate of the length and the intensity at which random breaks occur in the telomere respectively.

The general form of the SDE (
1
) without Markovian switching, can be written

(3)

\begin{split}dX(t)&=f(X(t))dt+g(X(t))dW(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,\\
X(0)&=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{split}

We can see that the telomere length SDE (
2
) is a special case of (
3
) with
d=m=1
,
f(x)=-(c+ax^{2})
, and
g(x)=(ax^{3}/3)^{1/2}
.

Suppose we wish to solve (
3
) numerically over an arbitrary mesh
\{t_{n}\}=\{0=t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{N-1},t_{N}=T
. Over any step
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
, solutions of (
3
) satisfy the integral equation

(4)

X(s)=X(t_{n})+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}f(X(r))dr+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}g(X(r))dW(r),\quad s\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}].

By fixing the solution at the beginning of the interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
, we achieve a continuous form of the explicit Euler-Maruyama approximation

(5)

\bar{X}(s)=X_{n}+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}f\left(\bar{X}(t_{n})\right)dr+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}g\left(\bar{X}(t_{n})\right)dW(r),\quad s\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}].

Setting
X_{n}:=\bar{X}(t_{n})
, we can write the scheme in its computational form as

(6)

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+h_{n+1}f(X_{n})+g(X_{n})\Delta W_{n+1},\quad n=0,\ldots,N-1,

where
h_{n+1}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
and
\Delta W_{n+1}=W(t_{n+1})-W(t_{n})
. Suppose there exists a constant maximum stepsize
h_{\max}\in(0,\infty)
such that
h_{n}\leq h_{\max}
for all
n=0,\ldots,N
. We say that solutions of (
5
) (or equivalently (
6
)) converge strongly to solutions of (
3
) in
L_{p}
with order
\gamma
if and only if

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|X(t)-\bar{X}(t)\|^{p}\right]\right)^{1/p}\leq Ch_{\max}^{\gamma}.

If
p=2
then we say that (
5
) converges strongly in
L_{2}
, in mean-square. It is the case that the Euler-Maruyama method (
5
) converges in mean-square to (
3
) with order
\gamma=1/2
when f, g are globally Lipschitz and
h_{n}\equiv h
.

Hutzenthaler et al
[
6
]
confirmed that solutions of (
6
) with constant stepsize
h_{n}\equiv h
fails to converge strongly in the strong sense to solutions of (
3
) if either
f
or
g
grow superlinearly in norm. Indeed this is the case for the telomere model (
2
). To address this issue, many novel explicit methods have been proposed that converge strongly even for highly nonlinear drift and diffusion coefficients; these include tamed, projected, truncated, and adaptive variants of both Euler-Maruyama and Milstein methods (see, for example
[
1
,
3
,
5
,
13
,
15
]
). In particular we highlight the adaptive timestepping approach that was introduced for the Euler-Maruyama method in
[
9
]
and extended to the Milstein method in
[
10
]
, where it was used to construct a numerical method converging strongly with order
\gamma=1
and to generate trajectories of the telomere SDE (
2
).

We wish to extend this capability to models where the coefficients
f
and
g
undergo Markovian switching. For example in the case of the telomere SDE (
2
) it may be that each of the model parameters in the pair
(c,a)
undergo transition between two distinct values according to a Markov process. Alternatively one can view Markovian switching in the model parameters as a form of stress testing to investigate the robustness of sample distributions to random changes in these parameters.

We take as our template the approach to mesh construction in
[
8
]
, where it was shown that an adaptive numerical scheme that satisfies a particular mean-square consistency bound can be extended to ensure strong convergence in mean-square for SDEs with jump perturbations (SJDEs) as long as the jump times are incorporated into the adaptive mesh in a particular way. In such a case the mesh is adapting both to the evolving behaviour of the approximate trajectory and directly to jumps when they occur. We refer to these as Jump Adapted-Adaptive Methods (JA-AMs).

In this article, we show that an analogue of the type of mesh used for JA-AMs also ensures convergence of explicit numerical schemes satisfying a similar mean-square consistency condition to SDEwMS governed by (
1
), as long as we include the times at which the Markov chain
r
switches between distinct states. We prove strong convergence of such schemes in mean-square, noting that the major challenge of this analysis is dealing with the fact that the number of Markov transitions on any interval is finite but unbounded, and we handle it in the same manner as in the jump case
[
8
]
. Finally we demonstrate the practical application of this approach on a variant of SDE model of telomere shortening (
2
) with stochastic dynamics governed by coefficients that undergo transitions at random times.

In Section
2
we provide a complete mathematical description of the setting, including of
W
and
r
, and provide conditions on
f
and
g
for which unique solutions of (
1
) exist on
[0,T]
for
T<\infty
. In Section 3 we describe how an adaptive mesh can be constructed that adapts both to the nonlinear functional response affecting the numerical solutions of (
1
) and which incorporate the switching times of
r
. We describe the general form of our adaptive scheme and present a version of it based upon the Milstein method. In Section
4
we state and prove our man strong convergence theorem, which in particular confirms that the Milstein scheme applied over this class of adaptive meshes will converge strongly in
L_{2}
with order
\delta=1
to solutions of (
1
). Finally in Section
5
we describe the practical implementation of the scheme and demonstrate its use to generate sample histograms of telomere length in base pairs using a nonlinear SDEwMS model.

2.
Mathematical Preliminaries

In this section, we mathematically characterise the random processes
W
and
r
influencing the dynamics of (
1
), then specify additional constraints on the coefficients
f
and
g
that are necessary for our main result.

2.1.
Characterisation of random inputs

First, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space with a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
which is right continuous and where
\mathcal{F}_{0}
contains all
\mathbb{P}
-null sets. The process
W(t)=(W(t_{1}),...,W(t_{m}))^{T}
is an
m
-dimensional Brownian motion defined on this probability space such that each
W_{i}
is independent of
W_{j}
for
i\neq j
, and each
(W_{i})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a scalar stochastic process with
W_{i}(0)=0
, a.s. continuous trajectories, and stationary increments that are independent on non-overlapping intervals.

Let
r(t)
,
t\geq 0
be a right-continuous Markov chain on the same probability space, taking values in
\mathbb{S}
with generator
\Gamma=(\gamma_{ij})_{L\times L}
given by

(7)

\mathbb{P}[r(t+\Delta)=j|r(t)=i]=\begin{cases}\gamma_{ij}\Delta+o(\Delta),&\text{if $i$ $\neq$ $j$};\\
1+\gamma_{ii}\Delta+o(\Delta),&\text{if $i$ = $j$};\end{cases}

where
\Delta>0
and
o(\Delta)
are higher order terms of
\Delta
. Here
\gamma_{ij}\geq 0
is the transition rate from
i
to
j
if
i\neq j
while

(8)

\gamma_{ii}=-\sum_{j\neq i}\gamma_{ij}

We assume that the standard Brownian Motion
W(\cdot)
and the Markov chain
r(\cdot)
are both
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted, and for the latter the initial value
r(0)=r_{0}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable taking values in
\mathbb{S}
.

The generator matrix
\Gamma
for a state space
\mathbb{S}=\{1,...,L\}
is

(9)

\Gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{11}&\gamma_{12}&...&\gamma_{1L}\\
\gamma_{21}&\gamma_{22}&...&\gamma_{2L}\\
&&.\\
&&.\\
\gamma_{L1}&\gamma_{L2}&...&\gamma_{LL}\end{bmatrix}

From (
8
) we see that
\sum_{j=i}^{L}\gamma_{ij}=1
for all
i=1,...,L
.
Denote the
k^{th}
time at which the Markov chain switches between distinct states as
\tau_{k}
. Then
(\tau_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is an increasing sequence of non-negative random variables which can be written

(10)

(\tau_{i})_{i\geq 0}=\sum_{k=0}^{i}\pi_{k},

where
\pi_{k}
denotes the waiting time between the
k^{th}
and
(k+1)^{th}
such transitions. In practice, as long as we keep track of the current state
i\in\mathbb{S}
of the Markov chain, these waiting times may be computed as the holding times associated with each
i
, and these are modelled as exponentially distributed random variables with state-dependent rates
\lambda_{i}
: see Section
5
.

Associated with the Markov chain
r(t)
is a process
(\bar{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that counts the number of switches that take place over the interval
[0,T]
for all
t\geq 0
, written

(11)

\bar{N}_{t}:=\#\{i\geq 1,\tau_{i}\in[0,T]\}.

Since the state space
\mathbb{S}
is a finite set,
\bar{N}_{t}
is almost surely (a.s) finite on any interval
[0,T]
: see for example
[
14
, Theorem 2.7.1]
. However
\bar{N}_{t}(\omega)
is not uniformly bounded from above across the sample space
\Omega
for any
t\in[0,T]
, and this presents a significant challenge in our main convergence result. A key to overcoming this challenge is the following.

Assumption 1
.

We suppose that
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can be decomposed such that
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\left(\mathcal{G}_{t}\cup\mathcal{H}_{T}\right)
for all
t\geq 0
, where
W
is adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
r
is adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and moreover the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is independent of
\mathcal{H}_{T}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Assumption
1
holds if
r(\cdot)
is independent of
W(\cdot)
, and in practice it allows us to simulate trajectories of (
1
) by precomputing trajectories of
r
and incorporating switching times into an adaptive mesh.

2.2.
Minimal conditions on the drift and diffusion coefficients
f
and
g
.

We identify conditions on
f
and
g
that ensure the existence of unique solutions over each interval
[0,T]
. We require that both satisfy a local Lipschitz condition:

Assumption 2
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
, there exists a positive constant
h_{k}
such that, for all
i\in\mathbb{S}
and those
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|x|\vee|y|\leq k

(12)

\|f(x,i)-f(y,i)\|^{2}\vee\|g(x,i)-g(y,i)\|^{2}\leq\bar{h}_{k}\|x-y\|^{2},

Consider also the following linear growth condition:

Assumption 3
.

f
and
g
satisfy a linear growth bound if for all
(x,i)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}
there exists a constant
0<K_{3}<\infty
such that

(13)

\|f(x,i)\|^{2}\vee\|g(x,i)\|^{2}\leq K_{3}(1+\|x\|^{2}).

Under Assumptions
2
and
3
there exists a unique solution to (
1
) on each
[0,T]
: See Mao & Yuan
[
11
, Theorem 3.16]
.

It is possible to replace the linear growth condition with a weaker constraint. If Assumption
2
holds, so that
f
and
g
satisfy a local Lipschitz condition, and they also satisfy the following monotonicity condition

Assumption 4
.

There exists a positive constant K such that for all
(x,i)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{S}
,

(14)

x^{T}f(x,i)+\frac{1}{2}\|g(x,i)\|^{2}\leq K(1+\|x\|^{2}).

Then there exists a unique solution
X
to Eq. (
1
) on each
[0,T]
. Under the same conditions (Assumption
2
and either Assumption
3
or
4
) solutions of (
1
) also satisfy uniform moment bounds and Hölder regularity bounds: for details see
[
11
]
.

In our main strong convergence proof we will make use of the following continuous form of the Gronwall inequality (see, for example Mao
[
12
, Theorem 8.1]
):

Lemma 1
.

Let
T>0
and
c\geq 0
. Let
u
be a Borel measurable bounded nonnegative function on
[0,T]
, and let
v
be a nonnegative integrable function on
{0,T}
. If

u(t)\leq c+\int_{0}^{t}v(s)u(s)ds\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

then

(15)

u(t)\leq c\exp\Big( \int_0^t v(s) ds \Big)\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

3.
Adaptive timestepping for an SDEwMS

In this section we define our adaptive mesh as a variant of the construction in
[
8
]
, and characterise the Euler-Maruyama and Milstein methods method used to generate the approximate solutions for the class of SDEsWMS.

3.1.
Structure of an adaptive mesh

Let
\{h_{n+1}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of strictly positive random timesteps with corresponding random times
\{t_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}}
, where
t_{0}=0
. We set up a framework by which nodes in this mesh can be generated procedurally as a function of the solution at the start of each step, while also ensuring that the timepoints at which the Markov chain switches between distinct states are included.

Assumption 5
.

For each term of the sequence of random timesteps
\{h_{n+1}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
at least one of the following three possibilities holds:

(1)

there are constant values
h_{\text{max}}>h_{\text{min}}>0
,
\rho>1
such that
h_{\text{max}}=\rho h_{\text{min}}
, and

0<h_{\text{min}}\leq h_{n+1}\leq h_{\text{max}}\leq 1;

(2)

there exists a switching time
\tau_{k}
, as defined in Section 2, such that
t_{n+1}=\tau_{k}
, so that

0<h_{n+1}=\tau_{k}-t_{n}\leq h_{\max}\leq 1;

(3)

t_{n+1}=T
, so that

0<h_{n+1}=T-t_{n}\leq h_{\max}\leq 1.

In addition we assume each
h_{n+1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-measurable.

It was shown in
[
8
]
that as long as
h_{1}
can be computed from the initial data, and successive adaptive timesteps
h_{k+1}
are computed on each trajectory as a deterministic function of the numerical approximation at time
t_{k}
, then each
t_{n}
on a mesh satisfying the conditions of Assumption
5
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-stopping time.

Definition 2
.

Let
N^{(t)}
be a random integer such that

(16)

N^{(t)}:=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ \{0\}:t_{n-1}<t\},

and let
N=N^{(t)}
and
t_{N}=T
, so that T is always the last point on the mesh

Let Definition
2
be satisfied with
N_{\min}^{(t)}:=\lfloor t/h_{\max}\rfloor
and
N_{\max}^{(t)}:=\lceil t/h_{\min}+\bar{N}_{t}\rceil

We ensure that we reach the final time by taking
h_{N}=T-t_{N-1}
as our final step, and use the backstop method if
h_{N}<h_{\min}
. Recall from Section
1
that
\bar{N}_{t}<\infty
a.s for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
\mathbb{S}
is a finite set, therefore
N_{\max}^{(t)}
is an a.s finite random variable.

3.2.
An explicit discretisation scheme for an SDEwMS

While the main result in this article applies for any strongly convergent numerical method, in this section we give the example of the explicit Euler-Maruyama and Milstein methods, and show how may be adapted to numerically solve an SDEwMS.

3.2.1.
The explicit Euler-Maruyama scheme

We may extend (
6
), presented in Section
1
, to solve an SDEwMS over an adaptive mesh
\{t_{n}\}
as

(17)

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+h_{n+1}f(X_{n},r(t_{n}))+g(X_{n},r(t_{n}))\Delta W_{n+1}.

Then the continuous approximation for all
s\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
is

(18)

\bar{X}(s)=X_{n}+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}f(\bar{X}(t_{n}),r(t_{n}))dr+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}g(\bar{X}(t_{n}),r(t_{n}))dW(r).

Notice that since switching times are included in the adaptive mesh as constructed in Section
3.1
we can simply fix the value of the Markov chain
r
to the value observed at the beginning of each step.

3.2.2.
The Milstein scheme

In the case where
d=m=1
, a continuous form of the Milstein scheme for the SDE (
4
) may be given as

(19)

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+h_{n+1}f(X_{n})+g(X_{n})\Delta W_{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime}(X_{n})g(X_{n})\left(\Delta W_{n+1}^{2}-h_{n+1}\right),

with corresponding continuous version

(20)

\bar{X}(s)=X_{n}+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}f(\bar{X}(t_{n}))dr\\
+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}g(\bar{X}(t_{n}))dW(r)+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}\int_{t_{n}}^{r}g^{\prime}(\bar{X}(t_{n}))g(\bar{X}(t_{n}))dW(p)dW(r).

Again, we extend (
19
) to solve an SDEwMS over an adaptive mesh
\{t_{n}\}
as

(21)

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+h_{n+1}f(X_{n},r(t_{n}))+g(X_{n},r(t_{n}))\Delta W_{n+1}\\
+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime}(X_{n},r(t_{n}))g(X_{n},r(t_{n}))\left(\Delta W_{n+1}^{2}-h_{n+1}\right),

with corresponding continuous version

(22)

\bar{X}(s)=X_{n}+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}f(\bar{X}(t_{n}),r(t_{n}))dr+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}g(\bar{X}(t_{n}),r(t_{n}))dW(r)\\
+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}\int_{t_{n}}^{r}g^{\prime}(\bar{X}(t_{n}),r(t_{n}))g(\bar{X}(t_{n}),r(t_{n}))dW(p)dW(r).

A full description of the Milstein scheme applied to (
4
) in the more general
d
-dimensional case with
m
independent Brownian noise terms requires the simulation of Lévy areas. Details are in
[
7
]
. When
m=d=1
, the Milstein method (
20
) may be expressed as a map
\psi\,:\,\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}\times[0,T]\times[0,h_{\max}]\to\mathbb{R}
over each step
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
:

(23)

\psi(x,i,t_{n},s-t_{n}):=x+(s-t_{n})f(x,i)\\
+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}g(x,i)dW(v)+\int_{t_{n}}^{s}\int_{t_{n}}^{v}g^{\prime}(x,i)g(x,i)dW(p)dW(v).

Finally, note that if we modify the second term on the RHS of (
19
) to be
h_{n+1}f(X_{n+1})
we arrive at the implicit Milstein scheme, which will be used later in Section
5
. A strong convergence analysis of this scheme for SDEs with non-globally Lipschitz continuous coefficients is contained in
[
4
]
.

3.3.
A hybrid adaptive numerical scheme: general form

Constructing our scheme we will use two maps
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}\times[0,T]\times[0,h_{\text{max}}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{B}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}\times[0,T]\times[0,h_{\text{max}}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
\mathcal{M}
is the main or default map and
\mathcal{B}
the backstop map.
\mathcal{M}
corresponds to an efficient scheme with is convergent as an adaptive method and
\mathcal{B}
corresponds to a method which is convergent as a fixed step method but which may be inefficient or may induce distortions in solution dynamics, with the aim that
\mathcal{B}
will be rarely used.

We characterise
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{B}
through their action over a single step, requiring them to satisfy a certain mean-square consistency bound.

Definition 3
.

Let
\{h_{n+1}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy Assumption (
5
). We define the continuous form of hybrid adaptive numerical scheme associated with the timestepping strategy
\{h_{n+1}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to be

(24)

Y(s)=\mathcal{M}(Y(t_{n}),r(t_{n}),t_{n},s-t_{n})\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\{h_{\text{min}}<h_{n+1}\leq h_{\text{max}}\}}\\
+\mathcal{B}(Y(t_{n}),r(t_{n}),t_{n},s-t_{n})\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\{h_{n+1}\leq h_{\text{min}}\}}.

Now define the global error of the scheme

Definition 4
.

For any
s\in[0,T]
, we define the global error acquired by the scheme (
24
) to be

(25)

E(s):=X(s)-Y(s)

where
X(s)
satisfies (
1
) and
Y(s)
satisfies (
24
).

Then we make the following assumption

Assumption 6
.

For
s\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

(26)

\begin{split}&E_{\mathcal{M}}^{x,y,i}(s):=x-\mathcal{M}(y,i,t_{n},s-t_{n});\\
&E_{\mathcal{B}}^{x,y,i}(s):=x-\mathcal{B}(y,i,t_{n},s-t_{n}).\end{split}

Assume that
E_{\mathcal{M}}^{x,y,i}
and
E_{\mathcal{B}}^{x,y,i}
satisfy, for some
\delta>0
, both
\psi=\mathcal{M}
or
\psi=\mathcal{B}
, any
i\in\mathbb{S}
, and any a.s. finite
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-measurable
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variables
A_{n},B_{n}

(27)

\mathbb{E}\bigr[||E_{\psi}^{A_{n},B_{n},i}(t_{n+1})||^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\bigr]\leq||A_{n}-B_{n}||^{2}\\

4.
Main result

For any adaptive numerical scheme composed of main and backstop maps that satisfy the bound (
27
) in Assumption
6
, our main result demonstrates strong

convergence when extended to an SDEwMS via a switching-time adapted mesh. The proof is a modification of that of
[

, Theorem 4.1]
, though the change of setting from SJDEs to SDEwMS allows for the analysis to be simplified in places. By using the Milstein method (
20

Theorem 6
.

Let
(X(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a solution of (
1
) with initial value
X(0)=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
hold. Let
Y
be an adaptive numerical scheme as characterised in Definition
3
with
Y_{0}=x_{0}
such that Assumption
6
holds with some
\delta>0
. Then there exists a constant
C(T)>0
such that

(30)

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\norm{X(t)-Y(t)}^{2}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C(T)h_{\max}^{\delta},

where C does not depend on
h_{\max}
.

Proof.

The proof naturally divides into four steps which successively show how conditional mean-square errors accumulate, first over the steps between successive switching times, then from the initial time to the final switching time before a fixed time
t
, then from the last switching time to
t
, and finally over the full interval
[0,t]
. We may then apply the Gronwall inequality (see Lemma
1
) to complete the proof.

Step 1: cumulative error bound over the interval between successive switches.
Over a single step, we obtain from (
27
) in Assumption
6
the following error bound regardless of whether the scheme uses the main map or the backstop map over a single step of length
0<h_{n+1}\leq h_{\max}
, and regardless of whether or not a change in the state of the Markov chain
r
occurs at either endpoint of the timestep: a.s,

(31)

\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E (t_{n+1})}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\leq\norm{E(t_n)}^{2}+\Gamma_{1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E (u)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]du+\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}^{2\delta+1}.

Let
\tau_{k}
and
\tau_{k+1}
be successive switching times. Subtract the first term on the RHS of (
31
) from both sides of the inequality and sum both sides over all the steps from
\tau_{k}
to
\tau_{k+1}
. Then we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{k}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t_{n+1}}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-\norm{E(t_n)}^{2}\bigg)\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}>n\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\bigg(\Gamma_{1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}[\norm{E(u)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}]du+\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}^{2\delta+1}\bigg)\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}>n\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}>n\}}du

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}^{2\delta+1}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}>n\}},\quad a.s.

By Definition
2
, the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
may be written as
\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(u)}-1}}
, for all
u\in[t_{n},t_{n+1})
and
n=0,\ldots,N^{(u)}-1
, so we can rewrite the inequality as

(32)

\mathcal{K}_{k}\leq\Gamma_{1}\int_{\tau_{k}}^{\tau_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(u)}-1}}\right]dr+\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}^{2\delta+1},\quad a.s,

where all indicator variables on the RHS have been bounded above by
1
.

Step 2: cumulative error from time
t=0
to the time of the last switch before time
t
.

Fix any
t\in[0,T]
. Summing over
k
for all switching times on the interval
(0,t]
and denoting
\mathcal{Q}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\mathcal{K}_{k}
, we have a.s,

(33)

\mathcal{Q}(t)\leq\Gamma_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\int_{\tau_{k}}^{\tau_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(u)}-1}}\right]du+\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}^{2\delta+1}.

Set
\tau_{0}=0
to include time from
0
to the first switch
\tau_{1}
and take the expectation conditional upon
\mathcal{H}_{T}

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}(t)|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\leq\Gamma_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\int_{\tau_{k}}^{\tau_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(u)}-1}}\right]du\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\\
+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]h_{\max}^{2\delta},\quad a.s.

Since
\bar{N}_{t}
is
\mathcal{H}_{T}
measurable we can take all of the summations on the RHS out of the conditional expectations.

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}(t)|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\leq\Gamma_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{k}}^{\tau_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(u)}-1}}\right]du\big|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\\
+\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]h_{\max}^{2\delta},\quad a.s.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(u)}-1}}
for any
u\in[0,T]
, an application of the tower property of conditional expectations yields

(34)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}(t)|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\leq\Gamma_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{\bar{N}_{t}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]du\\
+\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}-1}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]h_{\max}^{2\delta},\quad a.s.

Step 3: error bound from the time of the last switch to time
t
.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
. If
t_{N^{(t)}}=t
this step can be omitted. Suppose that
t_{N^{(t)}}<t
. Replacing
t_{n}
with
t_{N^{(t)}-1}
and
t_{n+1}
with
t
in (
31
), we have a bound on the error over the last step to time
t
as

(35)

\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E (t)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(t)}-1}}\right]\leq\norm{E(t_{N^{(t)-1}})}^{2}\\
+\Gamma_{1}\int_{t_{N^{(t)}-1}}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\norm{E (u)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(t)}-1}}]du+\Gamma_{2,n}|t-t_{N^{(t)}-1}|^{2\delta+1}.

Now summing up to
t
with the error bound over the last step satisfying (
35
) and taking the expectation conditioned on
\mathcal{H}_{T}
we have, for all
t\in[0,T]
and a.s,

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=N^{({\tau_{\bar{N}_{t}}})}}^{N^{(t)}-2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E (t_{n+1})}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-\norm{E(t_n)}^{2}\right)\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}>n+1\}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E (t)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(t)}-1}}\right]-\norm{E(t_{N^{(t)}-1})}^{2}\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=N^{({\tau_{\bar{N}_{t}}})}}^{N^{(t)}-2}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}>n+1\}}du\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad+\int_{t_{N^{(t)}-1}}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E (u)}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(t)}-1}}\right]du\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]

(37)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+h_{\max}^{2\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=N^{({\tau_{\bar{N}_{t}}})}}^{N^{(t)}-2}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}+\Gamma_{2,N^{(t)-1}}|t-t_{N^{(t)}-1}|\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right].

Summing the integrals on the RHS of (
37
), bounding the indicators on the RHS above by
1
, and again applying the tower property of conditional expectations we get

(38)

\mathcal{R}(t)\leq\Gamma_{1}\int_{\tau_{\bar{N}_{t}}}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]du\\
+h_{\max}^{2\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=N^{({\tau_{\bar{N}_{t}}})}}^{N^{(t)}-2}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}+\Gamma_{2,N^{(t)-1}}|t-t_{N^{(t)}-1}|\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right],\,t\in[0,T],\,a.s.

Step 4: cumulative error bound from time
t=0
to time

.

Adding the error bound associated with the steps that occur after the last switch (

) to the error bound at the last switch (

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}(t)|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]+\mathcal{R}(t)\leq\Gamma_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]du\\
+h_{\max}^{2\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N^{(t)}-2}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}+\Gamma_{2,N^{(t)-1}}|t-t_{N^{(t)}-1}|\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right],\quad t\in[0,T].

Next lets simplify the LHS of (
39
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}(t)\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]+\mathcal{R}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{N}_{t}-1}\left(\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{k})}}^{N^{(\tau_{k})}-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t_{n+1})}^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-\norm{E(t_n)}^{2}\right)\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(\tau_{k+1})}>n\}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\left.\left.+\sum_{n=N^{(\tau_{\bar{N}_{t}})}}^{N^{(t)}-2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t_{n+1})}^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-\norm{E(t_n)}^{2}\right)\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}>n+1\}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t)}^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(t)}-1}}\right]-\norm{E(t_{N^{(t)}-1})}^{2}\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N^{(t)}-2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t_{n+1})}^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-\norm{E(t_n)}^{2}\right)\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}>n+1\}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t)}^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(t)}}-1}\right]-\norm{E(t_{N^{(t)}-1})}^{2}\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right],\,t\in[0,T],\,a.s.

As

\mathcal{H}_{T}
measurable, we can bound

by
N_{\max}^{(t)}
and move the sum out of the conditional expectation. Again applying the tower property and now the telescoping sum with

we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}(t)|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]+\mathcal{R}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\max}^{(t)}-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t_{n+1})}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}>n+1\}}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-\norm{E(t_n)}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}>n+1\}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t)}^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{N^{(t)}}-1}\right]-\norm{E(t_{N^{(t)}-1})}^{2}\Big|\mathcal{H}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right],\,t\in[0,T],\,a.s.

Substituting (
4
) into (

) we have

(40)

\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\leq\Gamma_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]du\\
+h_{\max}^{2\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N^{(t)}-2}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}+\Gamma_{2,N^{(t)-1}}|t-t_{N^{(t)}-1}|\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right],\,t\in[0,T],\,a.s.

Now let’s consider the second term on the RHS of (

\omega\in\Omega
we can define a step function
\bar{\Gamma}_{2}(s)(\omega)
,

,

Then write the summation as an integral and take the expectation to get

\mathbb{E}\left[h_{\max}^{2\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N^{(t)}-2}\Gamma_{2,n}h_{n+1}+\Gamma_{2,N^{(t)-1}}\left|t-t_{N^{(t)}-1}\right|\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\right]\\
=h_{\max}^{2\delta}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\Gamma}_{2}(s)ds\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\bigg]\bigg].

Since
\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{2,n}]<\Gamma_{2}<\infty
and by construction of the step function we see that

(41)

h_{\max}^{2\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\Gamma}_{2}(s)ds\bigg|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\right]=h_{\max}^{2\delta}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{\Gamma}_{2}(s)\right]ds\right]\leq h_{\max}^{2\delta}t\Gamma_{2}.

Substitute (

) and take expectations on both sides to get

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(t)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\Gamma_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{E(u)}^{2}\big|\mathcal{H}_{T}\right]du\right]+h_{\max}^{2\delta}t\Gamma_{2},

Now we can apply the Gronwall inequality (see Lemma

\begin{split}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[\norm{E(t)}^{2}\Big]\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}&\leq\bigg(h_{\max}^{2\delta}t\Gamma_{2}\exp{\Gamma_1 \int_{0}^{t} dr}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\

5.1.
Implementation of an adaptive numerical method for SDEwMS

First, we simulate the trajectory of a given Markov chain
r
with state space
\mathbb{S}
and generator matrix
\Gamma
(see Eq. (
9
)). For each sampled
\omega\in\Omega
, we wish to compute the value of
r(\tau_{i},\omega)
, along with the respective switching times
\tau_{i}
, for
i=0,1,...,\bar{N}_{T}
. For a trajectory that has just transitioned to state
S_{i}
, the holding time before transitioning to a different state is an exponentially distributed random variable with parameter
\lambda_{i}
, where
\lambda_{i}=\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}+...+\gamma_{iL}-\gamma_{ii}
. The conditional transition probabilities are computed using the entries of
\Gamma
as

p_{ij}=\frac{\gamma_{ij}}{\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}+...+\gamma_{iL}-\gamma_{ii}}.

Then for each state
i
we can create a probability mass function with these transition probabilities and sample from it to simulate which state
S_{j}
is chosen at each switching time. We store the switching times
(\tau_{i})_{i\geq 0}
and the states selected
(S_{i})_{i\geq 0}
as two separate vectors for each trajectory
\omega
to use in the main algorithm.

Then we can begin to construct the mesh, with specific timestepping strategy as described in (
29
). The step size,
h_{n}
, taken for each step dictates whether we implement the main map
\mathcal{M}
or the backstop map
\mathcal{B}
as per (
24
). In this section we choose as our main map the Milstein map (
23
) and as our backstop map the map associated with the implicit Milstein method. Therefore our adaptive method will be strongly convergent in mean-square with order
\gamma=1
.

Running this method for a specified number of trajectories
\omega
with initial starting point
L_{0}
, we plot the final values on each trajectory
L_{T}(\omega)
as a density histogram.
