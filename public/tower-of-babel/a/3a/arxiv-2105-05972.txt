On angles between convex cones
Heinz H. Bauschke 1,∗ , Hui Ouyang 1 , Xianfu Wang 1

Abstract




1
Department of Mathematics, University of British Columbia, Kelowna, B.C. V1V 1V7, Canada.

Abstract.

There are two basic angles associated with a pair of linear subspaces:
the Diximier angle and the Friedrichs angle.
The Dixmier angle of the pair of orthogonal complements is the same as
the Dixmier angle of the original pair provided that the original pair
gives rise to a direct (not necessarily orthogonal) sum of the underlying Hilbert space.
The Friedrichs angles of the original pair
and the pair of the orthogonal complements always coincide.
These two results are due to Krein, Krasnoselskii, and Milman and
to Solmon, respectively. In 1995, Deutsch provided a very nice survey
with complete proofs and interesting historical comments.
One key result in Deutsch’s survey was an inequality for Dixmier angles provided by Hundal.

In this paper, we present extensions of these results to the case when the linear subspaces
are only required to be convex cones. It turns out that Hundal’s result
has a nice conical extension while the situation is more technical
for the results by Krein et al. and by Solmon. Our analysis is based
on Deutsch’s survey and our recent work on angles between convex sets.
Throughout, we also provide examples illustrating the sharpness of our results.

Keywords.

Angle between closed convex cones;
Dixmier angle; dual cone; Friedrichs angle; polar cone.

Introduction

Throughout this paper, we assume that

\text{$\mathcal{H}$ is a real Hilbert space},

with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and induced norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Following Deutsch and Hundal
[
5
]
, we now recall
two notions of angles between two nonempty convex sets:

Definition 1.1
.

(Dixmier and Friedrichs angle)

[
5
, Definitions 2.3 and 3.2]

Let
C
and
D
be nonempty convex sets in
\mathcal{H}
.
The
minimal angle
or
Dixmier angle
between
C
and
D
is the angle in

\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
whose cosine is given by

(1.1)

c_{0}(C,D):=\sup\big\{\langle x,y\rangle~\big|~x\in\overline{\operatorname{cone}}(C)\cap\mathbf{B}_{\mathcal{H}},~y\in\overline{\operatorname{cone}}(D)\cap\mathbf{B}_{\mathcal{H}}\big\},

where the
\operatorname{cone}
and
\overline{\operatorname{cone}}
operator returns
the smallest cone and closed cone containing its argument, respectively, and
where
\mathbf{B}_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the closed unit ball in
\mathcal{H}
.
In addition, the
angle
or
Friedrichs angle

between
C
and
D
is the angle in
\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
whose cosine is given by

(1.2)

c(C,D):=c_{0}\big((\operatorname{cone}C)\cap\overline{C^{\ominus}+D^{\ominus}},~(\operatorname{cone}D)\cap\overline{C^{\ominus}+D^{\ominus}}\big),

and where
C^{\ominus}:=\{y\in\mathcal{H}~|~\sup\langle C,y\rangle\leq 0\}
is the polar cone of
C

and
C^{\oplus}:=-C^{\ominus}
is the dual cone of
C
.

The cosine of these angles plays a key role in describing convergence rates
for projection methods such as the standard cyclic projection algorithm.
For a taste of such results, we refer the reader to, e.g.,

[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
and
[
4
, Example 9.40]
.

When
M
and
N
are closed linear subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
,
then the cosines of their Dixmier and Friedrichs angles — which were first studied
in
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
— can be written more succinctly as

c_{0}(M,N)=\sup\big\{\langle x,y\rangle~\big|~x\in M\cap\mathbf{B}_{\mathcal{H}},~y\in N\cap\mathbf{B}_{\mathcal{H}}\big\}

and

c(M,N):=c_{0}\big(M\cap(M\cap N)^{\perp},~N\cap(M\cap N)^{\perp}\big),

respectively.
The following three key results in this linear setting
were beautifully presented and proved in Deutsch’s survey
[
3
]
to which
we also refer the reader for interesting historical comments:

Fact 1.1
.

(Hundal’s Lemma)

(see
[
3
, Lemma 14]
)

Let
M
and
N
be closed linear subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
such that

c_{0}(M,N)<1
, and let
X
be a closed linear subspace such that
M+N\subseteq X\subseteq\mathcal{H}
. Then

c_{0}(M,N)\leq c_{0}(M^{\perp}\cap X,N^{\perp}\cap X).

Fact 1.2
.

(Krein, Krasnoselskii, and Milman)

(see
[
3
, Theorem 15]
)

Let
M
and
N
be closed linear subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
such that

M\cap N=\{0\}
and
M+N=\mathcal{H}
. Then

c_{0}(M,N)=c_{0}(M^{\perp},N^{\perp}).

Fact 1.3
.

(Solmon)

(see
[
3
, Theorem 16]
)

Let
M
and
N
be closed linear subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
.
Then

c(M,N)=c(M^{\perp},N^{\perp}).

We are now in a position to describe
the aim of this paper:

We will study the possibility of generalizing
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.3

from linear subspaces to convex cones.

While we initially anticipated nice generalizations, it turned out that only

1.1
appears to admit a natural and nice generalization.
The situation is more complicated for
1.2
and
1.3
;
our results and examples show that there are ostensibly no “nice” conical variants.
Our analysis relies on Deutsch’s exposition
[
3
]
as well as
our recent work
[
2
]
.

) are

The (somewhat negative) results concerning

.

[

3.
Positive results

We begin with a simple generalization of

[
3
, Lemma 10.2]
from two linear subspaces to one cone and one
linear subspace.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
K
be a nonempty closed convex cone in
\mathcal{H}
and let
M
be a
closed linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. Then

\displaystyle(\forall x\in K)(\forall y\in M)\quad\lvert\langle x,y\rangle\rvert\leq c_{0}(K,M)\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert.

Proof.

Let
x\in K
and
y\in M
. Then
-y\in M
as well because
M
is linear.
Applying

2.6
Item

(ii)
with
C=M
and

D=K
to obtain that

\pm\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x,\pm y\rangle\leq c_{0}(K,M)\lVert x\rVert\lVert\pm y\rVert=c_{0}(K,M)\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert

and the result follows.
∎

When one cone is contained in another, then the following pleasing result holds:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
be nonempty closed convex cones in
\mathcal{H}

such that
K_{1}\subseteq K_{2}
.
Then

c(K_{1},K_{2})=0=c(K^{\ominus}_{1},K^{\ominus}_{2}).

Proof.

Because
K_{1}\subseteq K_{2}
, we have
K_{1}\cap K_{2}=K_{1}
and
(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{\ominus}=K_{1}^{\ominus}
. Now
K_{1}
is a nonempty closed
cone and so
0\in K_{1}
. Hence, by
2.3
, we have that
K_{2}=\{0\}+K_{2}\subseteq K_{1}+K_{2}\subseteq K_{2}+K_{2}=K_{2}
,
which implies that
K_{1}+K_{2}=K_{2}
.
Combine this with
2.8
and
2.1
Item

(iv)
to see that
(K_{1}^{\ominus}\cap K_{2}^{\ominus})^{\ominus}=\overline{K_{1}^{\ominus\ominus}+K_{2}^{\ominus\ominus}}=\overline{K_{1}+K_{2}}=\overline{K_{2}}=K_{2}
.
Moreover, using
2.9
and
Definition

1.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle c(K_{1},K_{2})

\displaystyle=c_{0}\left(K_{1}\cap(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{\ominus},K_{2}\cap(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{\ominus}\right)=c_{0}\left(K_{1}\cap K_{1}^{\ominus},K_{2}\cap K_{1}^{\ominus}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{0}\left(\{0\},K_{2}\cap K_{1}^{\ominus}\right)=0

and

\displaystyle c(K_{1}^{\ominus},K_{2}^{\ominus})

\displaystyle=c_{0}\left(K_{1}^{\ominus}\cap(K_{1}^{\ominus}\cap K_{2}^{\ominus})^{\ominus},K_{2}^{\ominus}\cap(K_{1}^{\ominus}\cap K_{2}^{\ominus})^{\ominus}\right)=c_{0}\left(K_{1}^{\ominus}\cap K_{2},\{0\}\right)=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready for our first main result — the concial extension of
Hundal’s Lemma (
1.1
):

Theorem 3.1
.

(concial extension of Hundal’s Lemma)

Let
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
be nonempty closed convex cones in
\mathcal{H}
.
Suppose that
c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})<1
and that
X
is a convex subset of
\mathcal{H}
which contains
K_{1}-K_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})\leq c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus}\cap X,K_{2}^{\ominus}\cap X)\leq c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus},K_{2}^{\ominus}).

Proof.

2.7
Item

(i)
yields

\displaystyle c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}),K_{2}^{\ominus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}))\leq c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus}\cap X,K_{2}^{\ominus}\cap X)\leq c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus},K_{2}^{\ominus}).

Hence, it suffices to prove that
c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})\leq c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}),K_{2}^{\ominus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}))
.

If
c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})=0
, then
c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})\leq c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}),K_{2}^{\ominus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}))
is trivial.

Now assume that
c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})\in\left]0,1\right[\,
.
Then, by
2.5
, there exist sequences
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
K_{1}\cap\mathbf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}
and
(y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
K_{2}\cap\mathbf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\langle x_{k},y_{k}\rangle\to c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})
.

Clearly,
K_{1}^{\oplus}=-K_{1}^{\ominus}
.
Moreover, by
2.4
,
(\forall k\in\mathbb{N})

x_{k}-\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}\in K^{\ominus}_{2}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2})
and

\operatorname{P}_{K_{1}}\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}-\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}\in K_{1}^{\oplus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2})
.
Set

c_{0}:=c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})
and

(\forall k\in\mathbb{N})

\alpha_{k}:=\lVert\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}\rVert
.
Hence, using
2.7
Item

(i)
,
2.4
,
and the same techniques employed
in the proof of
[
3
, Lemma 14]
, we obtain that
\alpha_{k}\to c_{0}

and that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}),K_{2}^{\ominus}\cap(K_{1}-K_{2}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\big\langle\operatorname{P}_{K_{1}}\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}-\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k},x_{k}-\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}\big\rangle}{\lVert\operatorname{P}_{K_{1}}\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}-\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}\rVert\lVert x_{k}-\operatorname{P}_{K_{2}}x_{k}\rVert}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{k}-\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}_{0}-\alpha^{2}_{k}}{1-c^{2}_{0}}}\to c_{0},

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

If
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are linear in
Theorem

3.1
,
then
K_{1}-K_{2}=K_{1}+K_{2}
,
K_{1}^{\oplus}=K_{1}^{\perp}
,

K_{2}^{\ominus}=K_{2}^{\perp}

.

which are based
on the following example.

Example 3.1
.

[

, Example 4.13]

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Set
K_{1}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and
K_{2}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~|~-x_{1}\geq x_{2}\}
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

,
K_{2}^{\ominus}=\mathbb{R}_{+}(1,1)
, and

.

(ii)

,

(iii)

.

(iv)

c_{0}(K_{1}^{\ominus},K_{2}^{\oplus})=1
,

,
c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})<c_{0}(K_{1}^{\ominus},K_{2}^{\oplus})
, and

.

Remark 3.2
.

(importance of using dual-polar pair)

Let
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
be as in
Example

3.1
.
Then
c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})<1
yet

c_{0}(K_{1},K_{2})=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}>0=c_{0}(K_{1}^{\ominus},K_{2}^{\ominus})=c_{0}(K_{1}^{\oplus},K_{1}^{\oplus}).

This illustrates that in the conical extension of Hundal’s Lemma
(

), we indeed must work with a pair of
dual-polar cones of the original pair — substituting with
a polar-polar or dual-dual pair will not work!

4.
Negative results

We start with characterizations for differences of convex cones to be linear subspaces.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
be nonempty closed convex cones in
\mathcal{H}
such that

K_{1}\cap K_{2}=\{0\}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

K_{1}-K_{2}
is a linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.

(ii)

(-K_{1})\cup K_{2}\subseteq K_{1}-K_{2}
.

(iii)

K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are linear subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

“
Item

(i)
\Rightarrow
Item

(ii)
”:
We have

K_{1}=K_{1}-\{0\}\subseteq K_{1}-K_{2}
and

-K_{2}=\{0\}-K_{2}\subseteq K_{1}-K_{2}
.
Hence

(-K_{1})\cup K_{2}=-(K_{1}\cup(-K_{2}))\subseteq-(K_{1}-K_{2})=K_{1}-K_{2}
.

“
Item

(ii)
\Rightarrow
Item

(iii)
”:
Let
x\in-K_{1}
. Then there exist
y_{1}\in K_{1}
and
y_{2}\in K_{2}
such that

x=y_{1}-y_{2}
.
Then
y_{2}=y_{1}-x\in K_{1}-(-K_{1})=K_{1}+K_{1}=K_{1}
.
Hence
y_{2}\in K_{1}\cap K_{2}=\{0\}
.
