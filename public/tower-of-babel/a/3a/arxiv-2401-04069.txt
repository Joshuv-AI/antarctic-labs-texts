Proof of the Nernst heat theorem Preprint: English p. 1-3; Español págs. 4-6
José-María Martín-Olalla

Abstract
The Nernst heat theorem is probed from purely thermodynamic arguments connected with the second law of thermodynamics, and alien to the vanishing of the specific heats, or to the unattainability of the zeroth isotherm. If the proof is accepted the second law of thermodynamics would extend its applicability and the third postulate of thermodynamics would be narrowed to the fact that the entropy of a finite-density, chemically homogeneous body must not be negative.



1
Introduction

In classical thermodynamics, the Nernst heat theorem states that
the isothermal change of entropy
(\Delta S)_{T}
associated with a process between two states of a system in internal equilibrium vanishes as the temperature vanishes
.
Epstein 1937
;
Pippard 1957
;
Hatsopoulos and Keenan 1965
;
Kestin 1968
It is the first general property of matter in the vicinity of the zeroth isotherm. The theorem became a postulate after the proof presented by Nernst in 1912 was refuted by Einstein
Nernst 1912
(
administrative de l’Institut
Solvay 1921
, p. 293)
, paving the way for the third law of thermodynamics.

Nernst presented his theorem in 1905 after empirical evidences related to chemical equilibrium and to the expansion coefficients at very low temperatures.
Nernst 1906
Soon afterwards, he collected evidences on the vanishing of the specific heats as the temperature vanishes, a second general property of matter at very low temperatures, further completed by Simon.
Simon 1937
Eventually Nernst proposed the unattainability of the zeroth isotherm as a summary of his evidences.
Nernst 1912
In his proof, Nernst maintained that since a Carnot engine operating at the zeroth isotherm would negate the second law, the unattainability of the zeroth isotherm is then deduced. Einstein refuted the proof noting that at
T=0
the isothermal process becomes also adiabatic and therefore no practical procedure would be able to accomplish this process. Hence the engine would not operate and could not possibly challenge the second law.
Epstein 1937
;
Kestin 1968
;
Kox 2006
No other attempt to probe the theorem is known.

The isothermal change of entropy plays a pivotal role in reversible Carnot engines, which on their own play a pivotal role in the second law of thermodynamics. A reversible Carnot engine extracts an equivalent heat
\mathsf{Q_{h}}/T_{h}
(sans-serif letters will be used to identify exchanges in reversible processes) from a heater at temperature
T_{h}
and pours it into the cooler. For that to occur the substance undergoing the cycle must be able to sustain an isothermal change of entropy
(\Delta S)_{T}=\mathsf{Q_{h}}/T_{h}
at the temperature of the heater and at the temperature of the cooler
T_{c}
. The engine produces a work
\mathsf{W}=(\Delta S)_{T}\times(T_{h}-T_{c})
.

This article shows a simple proof of the Nernst theorem purely based on classical thermodynamic arguments. The proof does not require the vanishing of the specific heat. It is not a proof by contradiction, like the proof presented by Nernst, but of consistency of the second law.

2
The proof

The second law of thermodynamics states that
it is impossible to construct an engine which will work in a complete cycle, and produce no effect except the raising of a weight and the cooling of a heat-reservoir
(Planck’s statement
Planck 1897
). The statement is alien to the concept of temperature. Instead, temperature follows from the statement and the conservation of energy through the Carnot theorem, which set the fundamental equation of a reversible Carnot engine:

\frac{T_{c}}{T_{h}}=-\frac{\mathsf{Q}_{c}}{\mathsf{Q}_{h}},

(1)

where the labels refer to the heater (
h
) and the cooler (
c
), the reservoirs with which the engine operates. The minus sign takes care that in a reversible Carnot engine heat fluxes point in opposition, so that the ratio of the heat exchanges is always negative. Energy conservation mandates
\mathsf{Q}_{h}>-\mathsf{Q}_{c}
, therefore
T_{h}>T_{c}
, which connects with the intuitive idea of hotness.

In this context, equation (
1
) provides a universal thermometer and a natural zero for temperature (
\mathsf{Q}_{c}=0
). Therefore, if
T=0
is assigned to a finite-density substance, then a reversible Carnot engine operating with a zero temperature “cooler” and a heater must be conceptually admitted. Otherwise, the assignment
T=0
would be inconsistent with the framework of the second law.

This reversible Carnot engine must exchange no heat with the cold reservoir since if
T_{c}=0
, then
\mathsf{Q}_{c}=0
. It then follows that the engine would result in a perpetuum mobile of the second kind unless no heat were exchanged with the heater:
\mathsf{Q}_{h}=0
. Hence, the reversible engine would extract no entropy from the heater because
\mathsf{Q}_{h}/T_{h}=0
as well. Therefore the engine must only pour zero entropy in the zero temperature reservoir (
\mathsf{Q}_{c}/T_{c}=0
).

Because of the universality of Carnot theorem, this must apply irrespective of the body of finite density that undergoes the cycle and, given a body, irrespective of the specific mechanical or chemical configuration under which the engine operates. Because
\mathsf{Q}/T
exchanged by the reversible engine matches with
(\Delta S)_{T}
in the substance undergoing the cycle, it then follows that every
(\Delta S)_{T}
at
T=0
must be zero for every body of finite density, irrespective of the change in the configuration of the body. Continuity then requires that
(\Delta S)_{T}
vanishes as the temperature vanishes, which is the Nernst theorem.
\blacksquare

3
Corollaries

Two corollaries of the Nernst theorem would now follow from the second law, if the proof is accepted. In what follows
X
is a suitable mechanical parameter such as the pressure or the magnetic field that completes the description of the entropy of the system
S(T,X)
.

First,
S(0,X)
is unique, irrespective of
X
, should
\lim_{T\to 0}S(T,X)
exist. It would be the only circumstance in which the second law points toward absolute
S
, instead of
\Delta S
, albeit incompletely.

Second, irrespective of
\lim_{T\to 0}S(T,X)
, it is the case that
S(T,X)>S(0,X)
for any non-zero
T
. Since the entropy must not decrease adiabatically, no adiabatic cooling can bring the temperature of a finite density system arbitrarily close to
T=0
. This is the statement of unattainability of the zeroth isotherm.

4
Remark on the absolute value of entropy

The most comprehensive statement of the third law is due to Planck who summarized Nernst empirical observations saying that
as the temperature diminishes indefinitely, the entropy of a chemical homogeneous body of finite density approaches indefinitely near to the absolute zero
(
Planck 1911
, Part
IV
, Chapter
VI
)
Hatsopoulos and Keenan 1965
;
Kestin 1968
It refers to the absolute entropy, instead to a change of entropy.

The statement encompasses the two general properties of matter in the vicinity of the zeroth isotherm: (1) the entropy is unique (as deduced from the vanishing of
(\Delta S)_{T}
), and (2) the entropy has a floor, which can be set at zero. The second property follows the fast vanishing of the specific heats
c_{x}
as the temperature vanishes in view that the integrand in
S=\int c_{x}/T\mathrm{d}T
remains bounded.

The fast vanishing of
c_{x}
is alien the arguments presented in the proof of the Nernst theorem. Therefore it is the one and only general property of finite density systems that remains independent from the second law. It can be summarized by the following statement:
the entropy of a finite-density, chemically homogeneous body is not negative
.

5
Discussion

The burden of the proof lies in the meaning of the assignment
T=0
. Earlier in the 18th century the absolute zero was empirically conceived as a scenario where the volume or the pressure of a gas vanished. In the argument between Nernst and Einstein, the absolute zero meant
p=0
(
administrative de l’Institut
Solvay 1921
, page 294)
. From a classical kinematic point of view
T=0
is conceived as the state where motion, kinetic energy, vanishes. In some axiomatic presentations of the thermodynamics
T=0
is conceived as a scenario where
(\partial U/\partial S)_{X}
vanishes (
U
is the energy of the system).
Callen 1985
;
Beretta and Gyftopoulos 2015
Yet, in the classical framework provided by the second law
T=0
is associated with
\mathsf{Q}_{c}=0
from equation (
1
). This is a formal assignment of general validity, beyond the empirical
p=0
or
V=0
, and prior to the definition of
S
in the classical presentation of thermodynamics. The above proof makes a consistent use of that and runs in line with the proof of the theorems that are deduced from the second law of thermodynamics, such as Carnot theorem or Clausius theorem.

In contrast, under Einstein’s rebut of Nernst’s proof —a Carnot engine operating at
T=0
can not be constructed— it follows that
T=0
can not be determined by equation (
1
).

In summary,
T=0
would be assigned to a finite system only if the reversible Carnot engine that attempt to assess the temperature of the system consists in a round trip connecting a given state
\mathrm{E}_{1}
of the working fluid with
\mathrm{E}_{0}
, see figure
1
. The loop, purely conceptual, will enclose no area, will produce no work, and will exchange no entropy.

The interplay between Planck’s statement of the second law (P) and the Nernst theorem (N) is also worthy to discuss. In the form of propositional logic Planck’s statement is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle(W>0\Rightarrow Q_{c}<0),

(2a)

\displaystyle\neg\mathrm{P}\Longleftarrow

\displaystyle(W>0\land Q_{c}=0).

(2b)

It reads: Planck’s statement implies that if a weight raises (
W>0
, antecedent), then a reservoir must be heated (
Q_{c}<0
, consequent); if a weight raises and (
\land
) no reservoir is heated, then Planck’s statement is negated (
\neg
).

The Nernst theorem can be described by quantities related to reversible Carnot engine. First the limit
T\to 0
is the limit
\mathsf{Q}_{c}\to 0^{-}
, as per Eq. (
1
). Second, the observation
(\Delta S)_{T}\to 0
is translated into
\mathsf{Q}_{h}\to 0^{+}
and into
\mathsf{W}\to 0^{+}
. Therefore the Nernst theorem expresses the following relations:

\displaystyle\mathrm{N}\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\big((\mathsf{Q}_{c}\to 0^{-})\Rightarrow(\mathsf{W}\to 0^{+})\big),

(3a)

\displaystyle\neg\mathrm{N}\Longleftarrow

\displaystyle\big((\mathsf{Q}_{c}\to 0^{-})\land\mathsf{W}>0\big).

(3b)

Comparing with the right material implication in (
2a
), the right material implication in (
3a
) is saying: the consequent (
\mathsf{Q}_{c}
) vanishes only if the antecedent (
\mathsf{W}
) vanishes as well.

On the other hand, the left material implications in (
2b
) and in (
3b
) differ slightly. In (
2b
),
Q_{c}
is seen as a categorical variable: if
Q_{c}=0
and
W>0
, then the statement is negated; if
Q_{c}
is non-zero, then the statement survives, no matter how close to zero
Q_{c}
might be. Einstein refuted Nernst’s proof is in line with this view: it is impossible to construct an engine operating at
T=0
but, for any
T>0
, no matter how close to zero it might be, the engine could be constructed as the categorical argument presented by Einstein no longer sustains; nonetheless Planck’s statement would sustain as long as
Q_{c}<0
.
