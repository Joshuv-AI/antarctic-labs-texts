On the Goncharov depth conjecture and a formula for volumes of orthoschemes
Daniil Rudenko

Abstract
. We prove a conjecture of Goncharov, which says that any multiple polylogarithm can be expressed via polylogarithms of depth at most half of the weight. We give an explicit formula for this presentation, involving a summation over trees that correspond to decompositions of a polygon into quadrangles. Our second result is a formula for volume of hyperbolic orthoschemes, generalizing the formula of Lobachevsky in dimension three to an arbitrary dimension. We show a surprising relation between the two results, which comes from the fact that hyperbolic orthoschemes are parametrized by configurations of points on ℙ 1 . \mathbb{P}^{1}. In particular, we derive both formulas from their common generalization.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Depth reduction for multiple polylogarithms

Multiple polylogarithms are multivalued functions on
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
defined by Goncharov in
[
Gon95b
]
for
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
by the power series

\begin{split}&\textup{Li}_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})=\sum_{m_{1}>m_{2}>\dots>m_{k}>0}\frac{a_{1}^{m_{1}}a_{2}^{m_{2}}\dots a_{k}^{m_{k}}}{m_{1}^{n_{1}}m_{2}^{n_{2}}\dots m_{k}^{n_{k}}}\ \ \text{for}\ \ |a_{1}|,|a_{2}|,\dots,|a_{k}|<1.\\
\end{split}

The number
n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}
is called the weight of the multiple polylogarithm, and the number
k
is called its depth. The case of
k=1
is of particular interest and is known as the classical polylogarithm:

\textup{Li}_{n}(a)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{m}}{m^{n}}\ \ \text{for}\ \ |a|<1.

Classical polylogarithms appeared in the 18th and 19th centuries under different guises in the works of Leibniz, Euler, Spence, Abel, Kummer, Lobachevsky, and many others. It was noticed early on that polylogarithms satisfy functional equations of an algebraic nature. Here is the famous five-term relation for the
dilogarithm obtained by Abel:

\begin{split}&\textup{Li}_{2}(a_{1})+\textup{Li}_{2}(a_{2})+\textup{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{1-a_{1}}{1-a_{1}a_{2}}\right)+\textup{Li}_{2}\left(1-a_{1}a_{2}\right)+\textup{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{1-a_{2}}{1-a_{1}a_{2}}\right)\\
&=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\log(a_{1})\log(1-a_{1})-\log(a_{2})\log(1-a_{2})+\log\left(\frac{1-a_{1}}{1-a_{1}a_{2}}\right)\log\left(\frac{1-a_{2}}{1-a_{1}a_{2}}\right).\\
\end{split}

The structure of this equation can be clarified with the following two observations. First, all the terms in the RHS are products of polylogarithms of lower weight. We introduce the symbol
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\shuffle}}{{=}}
for “equal modulo products of polylogarithms of lower weight.” The precise meaning of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\shuffle}}{{=}}
is explained in §
2.2
. Second, define the cross-ratio of four points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
by the formula

[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}]=\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})(x_{3}-x_{4})}{(x_{1}-x_{4})(x_{3}-x_{2})}.

The five-term relation implies that:

(1.1)

\sum_{i=0}^{4}(-1)^{i}\textup{Li}_{2}([x_{0},\dots,\widehat{x_{i}},\dots,x_{4}])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\shuffle}}{{=}}0.

Finding equations for classical polylogarithms of higher weight is notoriously difficult as their length grows very fast. The first equation for
\textup{Li}_{4}
in more than two variables was found by Gangl in
[
Gan16
]
with computer-assisted search and contains more than 1000 terms; similar equations for
\textup{Li}_{5}
are not known yet. It seems that a more manageable goal is to write down equations for multiple polylogarithms and deduce from them equations for
\textup{Li}_{n}.
Here is an example in weight two:

\begin{split}&\textup{Li}_{1,1}(a_{1},a_{2})+\textup{Li}_{1,1}(a_{2},a_{1})+\textup{Li}_{2}(a_{1}a_{2})=\textup{Li}_{1}(a_{1})\textup{Li}_{1}(a_{2});\\
&\textup{Li}_{1,1}(a_{1},a_{2})=\textup{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{1-a_{1}\ \ }{1-a_{2}^{-1}}\right)-\textup{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{2}-1}\right)-\textup{Li}_{2}\left(a_{1}a_{2}\right).\\
\end{split}

The first of these equations can be generalized to an arbitrary weight and is a part of a family of so-called quasi-shuffle relations (also called “second shuffle relations”) for multiple polylogarithms. The second relation is more intricate. In combination, these relations imply the five-term relation and allow expression of any polylogarithm of weight two via the dilogarithm
\textup{Li}_{2}(a)
and products of logarithms.

In
[
Gon01
, Conjecture 7.6]
Goncharov formulated the depth conjecture giving a criterion for when a sum of polylogarithms can be expressed using polylogarithms of lower depths. For polylogarithms of depth larger than half of the weight, the criterion is always satisfied. We prove this part of the depth conjecture.

Theorem 1.1
.

Any multiple polylogarithm
of weight
n\geq 2
can be expressed as a linear combination of multiple polylogarithms of depth at most
\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor

and products of polylogarithms of lower weight.

This result is sharp as it is easy to show using a coproduct (discussed in §
2.3
) that a general multiple polylogarithm of weight
n
cannot be expressed via multiple polylogarithms of depth strictly less than
\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor.
Theorem
1.1
was known for
n\leq 5
; see
[
Cha17
]
,
[
CGR19b
]
, and
[
CGR19a
]
for further results on the depth reduction problem for multiple polylogarithms.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on the construction of so-called quadrangular polylogarithms, which are certain multivalued functions on the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{0,2n+2}
of configurations of
2n+2
distinct points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}.
We show that multiple polylogarithms can be expressed via quadrangular polylogarithms. On the other hand, we provide an explicit formula for quadrangular polylogarithms via multiple polylogarithms of depth at most
n.
The combination of these results implies Theorem
1.1
.

Our work came out of an attempt to understand the results of Coxeter in
[
Cox36
]
.
Coxeter found a relation between non-Euclidean orthoschemes, which are higher-dimensional generalizations of right triangles, and configurations of points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}.
Based on Coxeter’s results Böhm outlined in
[
Böh64
]
an integration procedure, from which one can deduce that the volume of a non-Euclidean orthoscheme in any dimension can be expressed via multiple polylogarithms. After a simple adjustment, our formula for quadrangular polylogarithms gives an explicit formula for the volume of a non-Euclidean orthoscheme.

Quadrangular polylogarithms

The proof of Theorem

We show that multiple polylogarithms can be expressed (modulo products) as linear combinations of the functions
\textup{QLi}_{n,n}

On the other hand, the quadrangular polylogarithm

on
\mathfrak{M}_{0,2n+2}.
The quadrangular polylogarithm
\textup{QLi}_{n,k}
is equal to a sum of multiple polylogarithms of depth at most
n
, whose arguments are certain products of cross-ratios, corresponding to disjoint quadrangles in
\mathrm{P}.

Consider a rooted tree
\mathrm{t}
with
n

which is a certain sum of multiple polylogarithms of weight
n+k
and depth at most
n
evaluated at products of numbers labeling the vertices of
\mathrm{t}
. Every decomposition of
\mathrm{P}
into disjoint quadrangles (we call such decompositions “quadrangulations”) determines such a tree:
\mathrm{t}
is the dual graph of the triangulation with vertices labeled by the cross-ratios of the corresponding points. Then the following theorem holds.

1.3.
Volumes of non-Euclidean polytopes and a theorem of Böhm

Multiple polylogarithms appear in computations of volumes of non-Euclidean polytopes, starting with the work of Lobachevsky at the dawn of the hyperbolic geometry era. In
[
Lob36
]
Lobachevsky was able to express the volume of an orthoscheme in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
as a sum of seven dilogarithms evaluated at certain functions of dihedral angles of an orthoscheme. Every polytope in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
can be dissected into orthoschemes, so its volume can be expressed via the dilogarithm. Lobachevsky applied this argument to a pair of scissors congruent polytopes and obtained a nontrivial identity between integrals, which he was able to check using classical integration techniques. To Lobachevsky, this was a strong argument in favor of his “Imaginary Geometry.”

It is natural to ask if volumes of non-Euclidean polytopes in higher dimensions could be expressed via multiple polylogarithms of higher weight. After we had completed our work, we discovered that a positive answer to this question could have been extracted from “Coxeters Integrationmethode” developed by Böhm in
[
Böh64
]
based on the results of Coxeter (see
[
Cox35
]
,
[
Cox36
]
).

Theorem 1.3

(Böhm, 1962)
.

The volume of a hyperbolic orthoscheme of dimension
2n-1
or
2n
can be expressed
1
1

1

For a precise statement see Theorem
1.5
.
via multiple polylogarithms of weight
n
evaluated at algebraic functions in exponents of dihedral angles of the orthoscheme.

We want to make a few remarks about the significance and the peculiar history of Theorem
1.3
. Using the Schläfli formula, one can easily see that the volume of a non-Euclidean simplex is an iterated integral of
d\log
-forms on a non-rational variety, which was written explicitly (for the spherical case) in
[
Aom77
, Theorem 1]
. Not all such integrals can be expressed via multiple polylogarithms (see
[
BD20
]
), so Theorem
1.3
is a much more subtle result. Goncharov showed in
[
Gon99
]
that the volume of a non-Euclidean simplex is a period of a mixed Hodge structure of mixed Tate type of geometric origin. The universality conjecture of Goncharov (
[
Gon01
, Conjecture 7.4]
) implies that any such period can be expressed via multiple polylogarithms.

It was conjectured in
[
BG47
]
and
[
Mül54
]
that the volume of an orthoscheme can be expressed via classical polylogarithms. In
[
Böh64
]
Böhm found that in seven-dimensional space, polylogarithms of weight four and depth two are unavoidable, raising doubts in this conjecture. Using the coproduct defined in §
2.3
, it is easy to show that the conjecture is indeed false, see also
[
Wec91
, Theorem 8.10]
. In the same work, Böhm shows how Coxeter’s approach in
[
Cox36
]
to the proof of the Lobachevsky formula could be generalized to higher dimensions (see also
[
BH80
, §5.4-5.9]
for a detailed exposition). With modern techniques, the method of Böhm can be used to prove Theorem
1.3
. Böhm neither formulates Theorem
1.3
explicitly nor gives a formula for the volume of an orthoscheme. Such formula for orthoschemes of dimensions up to five can be found in
[
BG47
]
,
[
Böh60
]
,
[
Mül54
]
,
[
Kel92
]
, and
[
Kel95
]
.

In §
6
we revisit these questions from a new perspective. We show that non-Euclidean orthoschemes of dimension
m-1
are parametrized by a certain abelian cover of
\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}
and deduce the results of Coxeter from
[
Cox36
]
. Next, we derive an explicit formula for the volume of an orthoscheme, which implies Theorem
1.3
. This formula is almost identical to (
1.2
) and we deduce both formulas from a more general statement, see §

.

1.5.
Philosophy of mixed Tate motives

The philosophy of mixed Tate motives and their relation to multiple polylogarithms and volumes of hyperbolic polytopes was developed by Goncharov, see
[
Gon95b
]
for an overview. According to this philosophy, there should exist a graded Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
of framed mixed Tate motives over an arbitrary field
\mathrm{F}.
Currently, the existence of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
is known only for some fields, see
[
DG05
]
. Next, Goncharov conjectures that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
is generated as a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space by
elements

\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{M}}_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})\text{\ \ for\ \ }a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathrm{F},

called
motivic multiple polylogarithms
, see
[
Gon01
, Conjecture 7.4]
. All relations between multiple polylogarithms in the usual sense are expected to originate from the corresponding relations in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}.

Let

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}/(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M},>0}\cdot\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M},>0})

be the corresponding Lie coalgebra of indecomposable elements and denote by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
its coideal of elements of weight greater than one.
Denote the graded dual Lie algebra to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}
by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}.
Consider a filtration on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}
defined by the formula
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee},

\mathcal{F}_{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}
and

\mathcal{F}_{-m-1}\mathcal{L}^{\vee}_{\mathcal{M}}=[\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee},\mathcal{F}_{-m}\mathcal{L}^{\vee}_{\mathcal{M}}]\text{\ \ for\ \ }m\geq 1.

The Goncharov depth conjecture
[
Gon01
, Conjecture 7.6]
states that the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{\bullet}\mathcal{L}^{\vee}_{\mathcal{M}}
is dual to the filtration on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}
by the depth of motivic multiple polylogarithms. The Lie coalgebra
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}
is graded by weight, from where it is easy to see that (for
m\geq 1
) elements in
(\mathcal{F}_{-m}\mathcal{L}^{\vee}_{\mathcal{M}})^{\vee}
have weight at least
2m,
so motivic multiple polylogarithms of weight
2m
and
2m+1
should lie in the subspace spanned by multiple polylogarithms of depth at most
m.
We prove this result unconditionally, see Theorem
1.1
.

The philosophy of mixed Tate motives predicts that there is a way to construct elements of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
out of cohomology groups of algebraic varieties, which carry mixed Hodge structure of mixed Tate type, see
[
Del71a
]
,
[
Del74
]
. For a projective simplex
S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{2n})
in
\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}
consider the following cohomology group with rational coefficients:

(1.5)

H^{2n-1}\left(\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}\fgebackslash Q,\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{2n}H_{i}\right)\fgebackslash Q;\mathbb{Q}\right).

Goncharov showed in
[
Gon99
, §3.4]
that it has mixed Tate type and thus is supposed to define an element
m(S)\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

The existence of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(F)
is only known for some classes of fields, including finite fields and number fields, but not
\mathbb{C}.
Because of that, we work not with mixed Tate motives, but with their
Hodge realizations
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hopf algebra of framed mixed Hodge-Tate structures, see §
2.2
. Conjecturally,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{C})
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{H}
, which consists of mixed Hodge-Tate structures of geometric origin, i.e., coming from cohomology groups of algebraic varieties. Goncharov constructed elements in
\mathcal{H}
called Hodge multiple polylogarithms

\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})\text{\ for \ }a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}.

For an oriented projective simplex
S
he also constructed an element
h(S)\in\mathcal{H}.
We derive Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.5
from the corresponding statements about elements of
\mathcal{H}.

The main tool for working with mixed Hodge-Tate structures is the coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}}.
On one hand, the coproduct is very explicit, see §
2.2
. On the other hand, the coproduct can be used to prove identities between elements of
\mathcal{H}
by reducing them to identities of lower weight, see
2.4
. Goncharov found the coproduct of Hodge multiple polylogarithms and the coproduct of
h(S),
which is related to the Dehn invariant of the scissors congruence class of a projective simplex
S.

1.6.
On proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.5

Our main result is Theorem
4.14
, which we call the “formal quadrangulation formula” and which we now motivate. Consider a convex
(2n+2)
-gon
\mathrm{P}
with vertices labeled by points
x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}.
Recall that for every convex quadrangle
(i_{0},i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
in
\mathrm{P}
we defined a cross-ratio
[x_{i_{0}},x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},x_{i_{3}}]
. For a quadrangulation
Q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{P})
consider a multiple polylogarithm, whose arguments are products of cross-ratios corresponding to quadrangles in
Q
. For example, for
n=3
we could take

(1.6)

\textup{Li}_{1,1,1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}],[x_{0},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}],[x_{0},x_{5},x_{6},x_{7}]).

The coproduct of (
1.6
) in
\mathcal{H}
is quite complicated. It has a “simple part”, which looks like a deconcatenation coproduct

\displaystyle 1\otimes\textup{Li}_{1,1,1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}],[x_{0},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}],[x_{0},x_{5},x_{6},x_{7}])

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\textup{Li}_{1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])\otimes\textup{Li}_{1,1}([x_{0},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}],[x_{0},x_{5},x_{6},x_{7}])

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\textup{Li}_{1,1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}],[x_{0},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}])\otimes\textup{Li}_{1}([x_{0},x_{5},x_{6},x_{7}])

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\textup{Li}_{1,1,1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}],[x_{0},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}],[x_{0},x_{5},x_{6},x_{7}])\otimes 1,

while other terms form the “complicated part.” The element

\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{Li}(\mathrm{t}_{Q})

appearing in Theorems
1.2
and
1.5
is characterized by the fact that the “complicated part” of the coproduct vanishes.

To formalize this idea, we define a Hopf algebra
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}
, whose elements are symbols like

[\textup{cr}(0,1,2,3),1|\textup{cr}(1,3,4,7),1|\textup{cr}(4,5,6,7),1]

which we think of as formal versions of polylogarithms of like (
1.6
). This is a very simple object: essentially, a free commutative Hopf algebra with the quasi-shuffle product and the deconcatenation coproduct
{\Delta^{\mathcal{PP}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}.}
Using the Connes-Kreimer Hopf algebra of rooted trees, we construct a special element
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}
whose coproduct can be expressed via elements of a similar type for subpolygons of
\mathrm{P}.
We define a coaction
{\Delta^{\mathcal{HP}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}}
which is related to the “complicated part” of the coproduct of multiple polylogarithms. The formal quadrangulation formula states that elements
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}
are coinvariants of this coaction.

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
we construct a collection of maps
\textup{Li}_{k}
for
k\geq 0
from
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}
to the Lie coalgebra
\mathcal{L}
of indecomposable elements of
\mathcal{H}.

\textup{Li}_{0}
is just the multiple polylogarithm and
\textup{Li}_{k}
for
k>0
is a slight generalization of it. We deduce from the formal quadrangulation formula that the “complicated part” of the coproduct of
\textup{Li}_{k}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})
vanishes, and the remaining part is identical to the coproduct for the quadrangular polylogarithm
\textup{QLi}_{n,k}.
This implies Theorem
1.2
.

Similarly, in §
6.5
we construct a map
ALi
from
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}
to
\mathcal{H},
which we call alternating polylogarithm. The formal quadrangulation formula implies that the “complicated part” of the coproduct of
\textup{ALi}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})
vanishes and the remaining part is identical to the coproduct of the class of an oriented orthoscheme
h(\textup{ort}(x^{s}))
for
x^{s}\in\mathfrak{M}^{s}_{0,2n+2},
which implies Theorem
1.5
.

2.
Multiple polylogarithms and mixed Hodge-Tate structures

2.1.
Multiple polylogarithms and iterated integrals

From the most naive point of view, multiple polylogarithm is an analytic function, defined in the polydisc

|a_{1}|,|a_{2}|,\dots,|a_{k}|<1

by the power series

\textup{Li}_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})=\sum_{m_{1}>m_{2}>\dots>m_{k}>0}\frac{a_{1}^{m_{1}}a_{2}^{m_{2}}\dots a_{k}^{m_{k}}}{m_{1}^{n_{1}}m_{2}^{n_{2}}\dots m_{k}^{n_{k}}}.

The properties of multiple polylogarithms, such as shuffle and quasi-shuffle relations, were studied by Goncharov, see
[
Gon01
, §2]
. The key idea is the relation between multiple polylogarithms and iterated integrals, which we now recall.

For a smooth complex manifold
M
consider a collection of
1
-forms
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}
on
M
and a piecewise smooth path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow M.
Denote by
\gamma^{*}\omega_{i}=f_{i}(t)dt

the pull-back of the form
\omega_{i}
to the segment
[0,1].
The iterated integral of
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
along
\gamma
is defined as follows:

\int_{\gamma}\omega_{1}\circ\dots\circ\omega_{n}\\
=\int_{0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}\leq 1}f_{1}(t_{1})dt_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}(t_{n})dt_{n}.

One can show (see
[
Gon01
, Theorem 2.1]
) that multiple polylogarithms can be presented as iterated integrals:

(2.1)

\begin{split}&\textup{Li}_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})\\
=&(-1)^{k}\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\frac{dt}{t-(a_{1}\dots a_{k})^{-1}}\circ\frac{dt}{t}\circ\dots\circ\frac{dt}{t}}_{n_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\underbrace{\frac{dt}{t-a_{k}^{-1}}\circ\frac{dt}{t}\circ\dots\circ\frac{dt}{t}}_{n_{k}}.\end{split}

Formula (
2.1
) allows defining multiple polylogarithms outside of the polydisc by analytic continuation. Moreover, it shows that multiple polylogarithms are
motivic periods
, see
[
KZ01
]
and
[
Bro17
]
. The theory of motivic periods is largely conjectural and may be viewed as an extension of the Galois theory to certain classes of transcendental numbers. The main advantage of this point of view is that one can study motivic periods via
motivic coaction
.

For our purposes, it will be convenient not to view polylogarithms as motivic periods, but rather as
framed mixed Hodge-Tate structures
. Intuitively, this corresponds to working modulo an ideal of the ring of periods, generated by
2\pi i.
In this setting, the motivic coaction is substituted with the motivic coproduct.

For a detailed exposition of the background material on mixed Hodge structures and multiple polylogarithms, see
[
Gon01
, §5-6]
. In the next few sections, we briefly review the key definitions and facts that we will use later. Also, there is a way to read the paper without working with mixed Hodge structures, see §
2.5
.

2.2.
Mixed Hodge-Tate structures

Mixed Hodge structures were introduced in
[
Del71a
]
,
[
Del71b
]
, and
[
Del74
]
. Let
H
be a finite-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{Q}.
A
pure Hodge structure
of weight
n\in\mathbb{Z}
on
H
is a decreasing filtration
F^{\bullet}
on the complexification
H_{\mathbb{C}}

\dots\supseteq F^{p}H_{\mathbb{C}}\supseteq F^{p+1}H_{\mathbb{C}}\supseteq\dots

such that

F^{p}H_{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\overline{F^{q}H_{\mathbb{C}}}=H_{\mathbb{C}}\text{ for any }p+q=n+1.

A
mixed Hodge structure
on
H
is a pair of an increasing filtration
W_{\bullet}
on
H
(the weight filtration) and a decreasing filtration
F^{\bullet}
on
H_{\mathbb{C}}
such that the filtration, induced by
F^{\bullet}
on every graded piece

\textup{gr}_{n}^{W}(H)=\frac{W_{n}H}{W_{n-1}H},

is a pure Hodge structure of weight
n.
Mixed Hodge structures form a category, which turns out to be abelian; moreover, it is a Tannakian category.

The unique
1
-dimensional pure Hodge structure
H
of weight
2n
with
F^{n}H=H
and
F^{n+1}H=0
is denoted by
\mathbb{Q}(-n)
and is called
pure Tate
; we have
\mathbb{Q}(-1)=\mathbb{Q}(1)^{\vee}
and
\mathbb{Q}(n)=\mathbb{Q}(1)^{\otimes n}.
A mixed Hodge structure
H
is called
mixed Hodge-Tate
if its graded pieces
\textup{gr}^{W}_{2n+1}(H)
vanish and graded pieces
\textup{gr}^{W}_{2n}(H)
are sums of pure Tate structures
\mathbb{Q}(-n).
The category of mixed Hodge-Tate structures is a Tannakian subcategory of the category of mixed Hodge structures.

Deligne showed that singular cohomology groups of algebraic varieties carry a canonical mixed Hodge structure; so do relative cohomology groups of algebraic pairs. For instance, both
H^{2n}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathbb{Q})
and
H^{2n-1}\left(\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}\fgebackslash Q,\mathbb{Q}\right)
for a smooth quadric
Q
in
\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}
equal to
\mathbb{Q}(-n)
and are pure Tate. Here is an example of a mixed Hodge-Tate structure, which is not pure.

Example 2.1
.

For
a\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\fgebackslash\{1\}
consider the cohomology group
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\fgebackslash\{0,\infty\},\{1,a\}).
From the long exact sequence of pairs one can deduce that the corresponding mixed Hodge structure is mixed Hodge-Tate and defines an extension

(2.2)

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}(-1)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(\mathbb{P}^{1}\fgebackslash\{0,\infty\},\{1,a\}\right)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}(0)\longrightarrow 0.

One can show that all extensions of
\mathbb{Q}(0)
by
\mathbb{Q}(-1)
are obtained in this way. Moreover,

\textup{Ext}^{1}(\mathbb{Q}(0),\mathbb{Q}(-1))=\mathbb{C}^{\times}_{\mathbb{Q}}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}_{\mathbb{Q}}
corresponds to the extension (
2.2
). Here we define the rationalization of an abelian group
A
by
A_{\mathbb{Q}}:=A\otimes\mathbb{Q}.

A general family of examples of mixed Hodge-Tate structures comes from non-Euclidean geometry. Goncharov showed that for a projective simplex
S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{2n})
in
\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}
the following cohomology group is mixed Hodge-Tate:

H(S)=H^{2n-1}\left(\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}\fgebackslash Q,\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{2n}H_{i}\right)\fgebackslash Q;\mathbb{Q}\right).

The dimension of
H(S)
depends on the relative position of the hyperplanes and the quadric. For instance, for a generic projective tetrahedron
S
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
we have

\textup{gr}_{0}^{W}\left(H(S)\right)=\mathbb{Q}(0),\ \ \ \textup{gr}_{2}^{W}\left(H(S)\right)=\mathbb{Q}(-1)^{\oplus 6},\ \ \ \textup{gr}_{4}^{W}\left(H(S)\right)=\mathbb{Q}(-2).

The category of mixed Hodge-Tate structures is Tannakian, so by Tannakian duality, it can be described via its Hopf algebra of framed objects, which we denote by
\mathcal{H}
(see
[
BMS87
]
and
[
BGSV90
]
). Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}
is graded:
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{n}.
The elements of
\mathcal{H}
can be described explicitly as follows.

An
n
-framed mixed Hodge-Tate structure is a triple
[H;v,f],
where
H
is a mixed Hodge-Tate structure,
v
is a vector in
\textup{gr}_{2n}^{W}(H)
, and
f
is a vector in
\left(\textup{gr}_{0}^{W}(H)\right)^{\vee}.
Consider the coarsest equivalence relation on the set of all
n
-framed Tate structures for which
H_{1}\sim H_{2}
if there is a morphism of mixed Hodge structures
H_{1}\rightarrow H_{2}
respecting the frames. Then
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is the set of equivalence classes;
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathbb{Q}
.

Addition in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is defined by the following rule:

[H_{1};v_{1},f_{1}]+[H_{2};v_{2},f_{2}]=[H_{1}\oplus H_{2};v_{1}\oplus v_{2},f_{1}+f_{2}].

The tensor product of mixed Hodge structures induces a commutative product. Next, the
(n-k,k)
-component of the coproduct

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\colon{\mathcal{H}_{n}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{n-k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{k}}

is defined by formula

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}_{n-k,k}[H;v,f]=\sum_{i}[H\otimes\mathbb{Q}(k);v,e^{i}]\otimes[H;e_{i},f]

for a basis
(e_{i})
of
\textup{gr}_{2k}^{W}(H)
and the dual basis
(e^{i})
of
\left(\textup{gr}_{2k}^{W}(H)\right)^{\vee}
.

Example 2.2
.

The Hodge logarithm
\log^{\mathcal{H}}(a)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the triple
[H,v,f]
, where

H=H^{1}\left(\mathbb{P}^{1}\fgebackslash\{0,\infty\},\{1,a\}\right),

vector
v
is the generator of

\textup{gr}_{2}^{W}(H)=H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\fgebackslash\{0,\infty\})
represented by the form
\dfrac{dz}{z}
, and vector
f
is the generator of
\left(\textup{gr}_{0}^{W}(H)\right)^{\vee}=H_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\{1,a\})
represented by a
path from
1
to
a.
Hodge logarithms generate
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathbb{C}^{\times}_{\mathbb{Q}}.
It is easy to see that Hodge logarithms are primitive elements for the coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}:

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\log^{\mathcal{H}}(a)=\log^{\mathcal{H}}(a)\otimes 1+1\otimes\log^{\mathcal{H}}(a).

The usual logarithm
\log(a)
is defined only up to a multiple of
2\pi i.
Notice that Hodge logarithm
\log^{\mathcal{H}}(a)
is defined canonically. In particular, there does not exist an evaluation morphism from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathbb{C}.
Remarkably, there exists a canonical morphism

(2.3)

\mathrm{per}_{\mathbb{R}}\colon\mathcal{H}_{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}(n-1),

where
\mathbb{R}(n-1)=\mathbb{R}(2\pi i)^{n-1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}.
It was defined by Goncharov in
[
Gon99
, §4]
and is called “real period.” The construction of the real period is explicit, but rather tricky, we do not repeat it here (see
[
GZ18
, §3]
for a more conceptual explanation). For
n=1
we have

\mathrm{per}_{\mathbb{R}}(\log^{\mathcal{H}}(a))=\log(|a|)\in\mathbb{R}.

Example 2.3
.

Consider an nondegenerate oriented projective tetrahedron
S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{4})
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined in §
6.1
; let
q=0
be an equation of the quadric
Q
. We associate to
S
a framed mixed Hodge structure

h(S)=[H(S),v,f]\in\mathcal{H}_{2},

where
H(S)=H^{3}\left(\mathbb{P}^{3}\fgebackslash Q,\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}H_{i}\right)\fgebackslash Q\right),

v
is a generator of
H^{3}\left(\mathbb{P}^{3}\fgebackslash Q\right),

and
f
is a generator of
H_{3}\left(\mathbb{P}^{3},\bigcup H_{i}\right).
The coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}h(S)\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
is a version of the Dehn invariant.

Notice that both
H_{3}\left(\mathbb{P}^{3},\bigcup H_{i}\right)=\mathbb{Q}(0)
and
H^{3}(\mathbb{P}^{3}\fgebackslash Q)=\mathbb{Q}(-2)
have two natural generators. The choice of
f
is fixed by the ordering of the hyperplanes
H_{1},\dots,H_{4}
and the choice of

v=\pm\sqrt{\textup{disc}(q)}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{3}(-1)^{i}x_{i}dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widehat{dx_{i}}\wedge\ldots\wedge dx_{4}}{q^{2}}

depends on the orientation of
S,
see
[
Gon99
, §3.3]
for the details.
If the projective tetrahedron
S
comes from a hyperbolic simplex
\mathcal{S}
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
then the real period of
h(S)
equals to the hyperbolic volume of
\mathcal{S}.

We will use the same symbol
\mathcal{H}
for the completion of
\mathcal{H}
with respect to the
\mathcal{I}
-adic topology, where
\mathcal{I}
is the fundamental ideal
\bigoplus_{n>0}\mathcal{H}_{n}.
The Lie coalgebra of indecomposable elements in
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}_{>0}/(\mathcal{H}_{>0}\cdot\mathcal{H}_{>0}).

For
A,B\in\mathcal{H}_{>0}
we say that
A
and
B

equal modulo products
if their projections coincide in
\mathcal{L};
we write
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\shuffle}}{{=}}B.
We denote the Lie cobracket in
\mathcal{L}
by
\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}.

Sometimes we work not with framed mixed Hodge-Tate structures but with framed unipotent variations of mixed Hodge-Tate structures over an algebraic variety
S
; we denote the corresponding Hopf algebra by
\mathcal{H}[S]
. Not much changes in this setting, see
[
Gon01
, §5.2]
and
[
Bro17
, §7]
for more details.

2.3.
Hodge iterated integrals, correlators and multiple polylogarithms

Goncharov constructed elements of
\mathcal{H}
called Hodge iterated integrals in
[
Gon01
]
,
[
Gon02
]
and
[
Gon05
]
. For every collection of points
x_{0},\dots,x_{n+1}\in\mathbb{C}
Goncharov constructed
Hodge iterated integrals

(2.4)

\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{0};x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\in\mathcal{H}_{n},

which are Hodge versions of iterated integrals

\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{n+1}}\frac{dt}{t-x_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\frac{dt}{t-x_{n}}=\int_{x_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}\leq x_{n+1}}\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}-x_{1}}\wedge\frac{dt_{2}}{t_{2}-x_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge\frac{dt_{n}}{t_{n}-x_{n}}.

Note that unlike usual iterated integrals, Hodge iterated integrals do not depend on the paths from
x_{0}
to
x_{n+1}.
We give a brief outline of their construction for
x_{i}\notin\{x_{0},x_{n+1}\}.
For the general case one needs to work with tangential base points, see
[
Gon01
, §5.3]
.

Let

\pi^{un}=\pi^{un}(\mathbb{C}\fgebackslash\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\};x_{0},x_{n+1})

be the prounipotent completion to the topological torsor of paths from

2.4.
Proving identities between periods

Some of the main results of our work, i.e., Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, are certain identities between multiple polylogarithms “modulo products of polylogarithms of lower weight.” Polylogarithms are multivalued functions, which are defined only up to a product of
2\pi i
and a function of lower weight, so writing explicit “numerically checkable” identities between multiple polylogarithms is possible only locally.

Instead of working with actual functions, we work with framed unipotent variations of mixed Hodge-Tate structures: elements of the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}[S]
. Given an element
[H;v,f]\in\mathcal{H}[S]
, one can recover the multivalued function by choosing a (local) splitting of the weight filtration
W_{\bullet}H_{\mathbb{Q}}
and taking a flat section of the corresponding variation. For details, see the definition of the
complex period
in
[
Gon99
, §4.1]
. The resulting function is defined up to a product of
2\pi i
and a complex period of a framed variation of mixed Hodge-Tate structure of lower weight.

So, every identity between elements of
\mathcal{H}[S]
implies an identity between the corresponding multivalued functions. Similarly, every identity between elements of
\mathcal{L}[S]
leads to an identity between the corresponding multivalued functions modulo product of functions of lower weight.

The general method of proving such identities is discussed in
[
Gon02
, §8]
; here we sketch the main idea. If we need to check an identity
A=B
between actual functions, we can proceed in two steps. First, we prove that the differentials of both sides coincide (
dA=dB
), thus showing that the identity is valid up to a constant. Then we specialize the functions to a point and check that the constant
A-B
is zero.

A similar method works for an identity
A=B
between elements of
\mathcal{H}_{n}.
In most examples that we encounter, terms in
A
and
B
are Hodge iterated integrals (
2.4
) with arguments being rational functions on a smooth connected algebraic variety
S.
It means that equality
A=B
is equality of framed unipotent variations of mixed Hodge-Tate structures on
S.
To check that
A=B
, we first check equality of coproducts
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}A=\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}B
. Note that this step is parallel to checking the equality of differentials of functions, as the
(1,n-1)
-component of the coproduct is closely related to the differential.

By the Rigidity Lemma
[
Gon02
, Lemma 8.2]
, vanishing of the coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}(A-B)
implies that
A-B
is a framed constant variation on
S.
Thus, to prove the equality
A=B
it suffices to prove it for any specialization to a point in
S.
Specialization of framed variations of mixed Hodge structures is discussed in
[
Gon02
, §4]
. Notice that one can prove identities in the Lie coalgebra
\mathcal{L}
in a similar way, see
[
GR18
, Lemma 2.7]
.

2.5.
Working with polylogarithms without knowledge of mixed Hodge structures

In §§
2.2
–
2.4
we gave the background material on mixed Hodge-Tate structures and their relation to polylogarithms. The notion of a mixed Hodge structure is neither intuitive nor an easy one to learn; in this section, we sketch an alternative way to read the paper. We construct a Hopf algebra of polylogarithms in a way similar to the formal definition of higher Bloch groups, see
[
Gon95a
, §1.9]
.

Let
\mathrm{F}
be a field of zero characteristic. Our goal is to define a commutative graded Hopf algebra
\mathbb{G}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F})
of multiple polylogarithms. Conjecturally,
\mathbb{G}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F})
is isomorphic to the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
from §
1.5
.

First, we define a commutative Hopf algebra
\mathbb{I}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F})
generated by symbols

(2.15)

\mathbb{I}(x_{0};x_{1},\dots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{I}_{n}(\mathrm{F})

for
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1}\in\mathrm{F}
subject to the following relations:

(1)

\mathbb{I}(x_{0};x_{1})=1;

(2)

\mathbb{I}(x_{0};x_{1},\dots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=0
for
x_{0}=x_{m+1};

(3)

\mathbb{I}(x_{0};x_{1},\dots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\mathbb{I}(x_{0};x_{1},\dots,x_{r};x)\mathbb{I}(x;x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

The cobracket

\Delta\colon\mathbb{I}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F})\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F})

is defined by the formula

\begin{split}&\Delta\mathbb{I}(x_{0};x_{1},\dots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\\
=&\sum_{(i_{0},i_{1},\dots,i_{r+1})}\left(\prod_{p=0}^{r}\mathbb{I}(x_{i_{p}};x_{i_{p}+1},\dots,x_{i_{p+1}-1};x_{i_{p+1}})\right)\otimes\mathbb{I}(x_{i_{0}};x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}};x_{i_{r+1}}),\end{split}

where the summation goes over all sequences
0=i_{0}<i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}<i_{r+1}=n+1.

It is easy to see that
\mathbb{I}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F})
is a commutative Hopf algebra, see
[
Gon05
, Proposition 2.2]
.

Next, we define the space of relations
R_{n}(\mathrm{F})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{n}(\mathrm{F})
inductively; the coalgebra of multiple polylogarithms is defined as the quotient

\mathbb{G}_{n}(\mathrm{F})=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{n}(\mathrm{F})}{R_{n}(\mathrm{F})}.

In weight one we define
R_{1}(\mathrm{F})
to be the kernel of the map sending
\mathbb{I}(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})\in\mathbb{G}_{1}(\mathrm{F})
to
\dfrac{x_{2}-x_{0}}{x_{0}-x_{1}}\in\mathrm{F}^{\times}_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\mathrm{F}
for distinct
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}
and to zero otherwise. By definition,
\mathbb{G}_{1}(\mathrm{F})
. Next, assume that spaces
R_{i}(\mathrm{F})
are defined for
i<n.
The space
R_{n}(\mathrm{F})
is spanned by elements

(2.16)

r(0)-r(1)\in\mathbb{I}_{n}(\mathrm{F})

for elements
r(t)\in\mathbb{I}_{n}(\mathrm{F}(t)),
which are primitive in
\mathbb{G}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F}(t))\otimes\mathbb{G}_{\bullet}(\mathrm{F}(t)).
Proving that this construction is well-defined requires some work; we will develop this approach elsewhere.

Elements (
2.15
) are analogs of Hodge iterated integrals. More precisely, one can show that there exists a homomorphism of Hopf algebras from
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{C})
to
\mathcal{H}
sending
\mathbb{I}
to
\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}.
We can easily define elements of

corresponding to Hodge correlators and Hodge multiple polylogarithms using formulas like (

) and (
2.14
). Theorem
1.2
can be proven for these elements as the rigidity argument can be also performed in

thanks to (

).

3.
Hopf algebra of formal polylogarithms

3.1.
Quasi-shuffle algebra and quasi-shuffle relation

Recall the definition of a quasi-shuffle algebra from
[
Hof00
]
,
[
HI17
]
. Consider a set of letters
\mathcal{A}
which carries a structure of a commutative semigroup. We denote the product of
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
by
a\cdot b\in\mathcal{A}.
Next, consider a set of words
\mathcal{A}^{*}
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. For a word
\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
we denote by
l(\omega)
the number of letters in
\omega,
called its length. The only word of length
0
is the empty word
1\in\mathcal{A}^{*}.
For two words
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
we denote their concatenation by
\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}.
Next, for a word
\omega=a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
we denote the word
(a\cdot a_{1})a_{2}\dots a_{n}
by
a\cdot w
; we have
a\cdot 1=0.

Let
A
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space with a basis given by
\mathcal{A}^{*}.
The tensor algebra on
A
carries a commutative Hopf algebra structure with quasi-shuffle product and the deconcatenation coproduct which we denote by
\textup{QSh}^{\mathcal{A}}
and call a free quasi-shuffle algebra on
\mathcal{A}.
We use the bar notation for its elements, i.e., we denote
v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{n}
by
[v_{1}|v_{2}|\dots|v_{n}].
Next, we use the same notation for a word
\omega=a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
and an element
[a_{1}|\dots|a_{n}]
of the quasi-shuffle algebra; such elements form a basis of
\textup{QSh}^{\mathcal{A}}.
The quasi-shuffle product of the basis elements
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\textup{QSh}^{\mathcal{A}}
is defined recursively:

\begin{split}&\omega\star 1=1\star\omega=\omega,\\
&(a_{1}\omega_{1})\star(a_{2}\omega_{2})=a_{1}(\omega_{1}\star(a_{2}\omega_{2}))+a_{2}((a_{1}\omega_{1})\star(\omega_{2}))+(a_{1}\cdot a_{2})(\omega_{1}\star\omega_{2}).\\
\end{split}

The deconcatenation coproduct is defined by the formula

(3.1)

\Delta(\omega)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{1}\dots a_{i}\otimes a_{i+1}\dots a_{n}.

As an abstract Hopf algebra,
\textup{QSh}^{\mathcal{A}}
does not depend on the semigroup structure on
\mathcal{A}.
In particular, if we take the product on
\mathcal{A}
to be zero, we obtain the free shuffle algebra. Also, we will use the fact that the space of primitive elements in
\textup{QSh}^{\mathcal{A}}
is spanned by the words of length 1.

Here is a particular example which we will use. Let
S
be an irreducible algebraic variety over
\mathbb{C}.
Consider the set of letters
(\varphi,n)
where
\varphi\in\mathbb{C}(S)^{\times}
is a nonzero rational function on
S
and
n
is a positive integer. The product

(\varphi_{1},n_{1})\cdot(\varphi_{2},n_{2})=(\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2},n_{1}+n_{2})

defines a semigroup structure on the alphabet
(\varphi,n)
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{S}
be the corresponding quasi-shuffle algebra with deconcatenation coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{S}
be the inductive limit of Hopf algebras
\mathcal{H}[U]
of framed unipotent variations of mixed Hodge-Tate structures over all nonempty open subset
U\subseteq S.
Consider a map
\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{S}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{S}
defined by the rule

\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}([\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\varphi_{2},n_{2}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}])=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k}).

This map is very far from being a homomorphism of coalgebras but remarkably is a homomorphism of algebras. This statement is known as the quasi-shuffle relation for multiple polylogarithms
[
Gon02
, Theorem 1.2]
, see also
[
Gon01
, §2.5]
for an analytic version (where it is called the first shuffle relation).

Example 3.1
.

For
S=\mathbb{A}^{2}
the equality

[a_{1},n_{1}|a_{2},n_{2}]+[a_{2},n_{2}|a_{1},n_{1}]+[a_{1}a_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}]=[a_{1},n_{1}]\star[a_{2},n_{2}]

implies that

\textup{Li}_{n_{1},n_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}}(a_{1},a_{2})+\textup{Li}_{n_{2},n_{1}}^{\mathcal{H}}(a_{2},a_{1})+\textup{Li}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}}(a_{1}a_{2})=\textup{Li}_{n_{1}}^{\mathcal{H}}(a_{1})\textup{Li}_{n_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}}(a_{2}).

The quasi-shuffle relation is almost immediate for the corresponding power series but is much harder to prove on the Hodge level. A cohomological proof is explained in
[
Gon02
, §9]
and a proof using the rigidity argument (§
2.4
) of a similar statement for Hodge correlators can be found in
[
Mal20
]
. In this section we give another proof of the quasi-shuffle relation, deriving it from a more general Proposition
3.8
, which we call “formal quasi-shuffle relation.” The proof of Proposition
3.8
uses the same technique as a more complicated formal quadrangulation formula (Theorem
4.14
).

3.2.
Smash coproduct Hopf algebras

Let
(\mathcal{F},\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}},m_{\mathcal{F}})
and
(\mathcal{H},\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}},m_{\mathcal{H}})
be commutative Hopf algebras. In applications we will take
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{S}
and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}
defined in §
3.1
. In this section, we recall the construction of a smash coproduct Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}
from
[
Mol77
]
, which plays a major role in what follows. Commutative Hopf algebras are dual to pro-affine algebraic groups. In the “dual” language a smash coproduct Hopf algebra corresponds to a split extension of the corresponding groups.

A coaction of
\mathcal{H}
on
\mathcal{F}
is a map

\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}\colon\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{F},

which gives
\mathcal{F}
a structure of a comodule over
\mathcal{H}.
Comodules over
\mathcal{H}
form a tensor category; in particular, we have a comodule structure on
\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F}

\Delta^{\mathcal{HFF}}\colon\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{F}.

defined by a map

\Delta^{\mathcal{HFF}}(x\otimes y)=(m_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes 1\otimes 1)(1\otimes T\otimes 1)(\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x)\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(y)),

where
T
denotes the twist map
T(t_{1}\otimes t_{2})=t_{2}\otimes t_{1}
. Assume that the coaction
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}
commutes with the product
m_{\mathcal{F}}
and coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}},
as well as with the unit and the counit. Under these assumptions we can construct the smash coproduct Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F},
see
[
Mol77
, Theorem (2.14)]
. As an algebra,
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}
is the tensor product
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{F}.
The coproduct

(3.2)

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}\colon\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F})\otimes(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F})

in
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}
is defined by a rather complicated formula

(3.3)

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}=(1\otimes T\otimes 1)(1\otimes m_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes 1\otimes 1)(1\otimes 1\otimes T\otimes 1)(\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\otimes 1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}})(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}).

The space of coinvariants

\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{H}}=\{x\in\mathcal{F}\mid\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}x=1\otimes x\}

is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{F};
it is closed under the product
m_{\mathcal{F}}.
Let
i\colon\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}
be an embedding
i(x)=1\otimes x.
The map
i
is an algebra homomorphism, but (in general) it does not commute with coproducts. Nevertheless, it is easy to see from formula (
3.2
) that if
x\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{H}}
and
\Delta(x)\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{H}},
we have

(3.4)

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}(i(x))=(i\otimes i)\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(x).

We use the same symbol for
x\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{H}}
and
i(x)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}.

Next, we give a group-theoretic interpretation of the smash coproduct Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}
. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
be pro-affine algebraic groups dual to
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{H}.
Then the coaction
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}
that commutes with product, coproduct, unit, and counit gives a homomorphism

\varphi\colon\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\longrightarrow\textup{Aut}(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}).

The dual of
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}
is the semidirect product
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\ltimes_{\varphi}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}.
Finally,
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{H}}
is the algebra of functions on
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
that are invariant under the action of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}.

We use the following notation for the reduced coaction:

\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x)=\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x)-1\otimes x

and for the reduced coproduct:

\begin{split}&\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(x)=\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(x)-x\otimes 1-1\otimes x,\\
&\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}(x)=\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}(x)-x\otimes 1-1\otimes x.\\
\end{split}

It is easy to see that it suffices to prove that for

We prove this statement by induction on

where

The base case of

Denote the difference by

First, we show that the difference

Indeed, assume that

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}x=\sum x_{i}^{(1)}\otimes x_{i}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{F}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{S},\\
&\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}y=\sum y_{j}^{(1)}\otimes y_{j}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{F}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{S}.\end{split}

Then

By induction, we know that

unless

and

Thus the following equality holds in

\displaystyle=\sum\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(x_{i}^{(1)}\star y_{j}^{(1)})\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(x_{i}^{(2)}\star y_{j}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(x\star y)\otimes 1\otimes 1+1\otimes 1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(x\star y)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(x)\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(y)\right)\otimes 1\otimes 1-1\otimes 1\otimes\left(\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(x)\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}(y)\right)

\displaystyle(\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}})\Delta^{\mathcal{FF}}(x\star y)

\displaystyle\quad+\left((\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}})\Delta^{\mathcal{FF}}(x)\right)\left((\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}})\Delta^{\mathcal{FF}}(y)\right).

Using the definition of

(\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{HH}}\otimes 1)D=(1\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}})D.

Equivalently, we need to show that

(\Delta^{\mathcal{HH}}\otimes 1)D-(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}})D=1\otimes D.

(\Delta^{\mathcal{HH}}\otimes 1)\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x\star y)-(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x\star y)=0.

Assume that

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x)=\sum f_{i}^{(1)}\otimes x_{i}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{F},\\
&\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(y)=\sum g_{j}^{(1)}\otimes y_{j}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{F}.\end{split}

Then

\displaystyle(\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\otimes 1)(\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x)\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(y))=

\displaystyle\sum\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\bigl(f_{i}^{(1)}g_{j}^{(1)}\bigr)\otimes\bigl(x_{i}^{(2)}\star y_{j}^{(2)}\bigr)

\displaystyle\sum\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\bigl(f_{i}^{(1)}\bigr)\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\bigl(g_{j}^{(1)}\bigr)\otimes\bigl(x_{i}^{(2)}\star y_{j}^{(2)}\bigr)

) and (

lies in

, and it is easy to see that only words in

which may appear in

have the form

corresponding to the words

as the last term. So, we have

Assume that it is not the case; let

We have

we get that

From (

is a linear combination of iterated integrals

3.6.
Quasi-shuffle relation for multiple polylogarithms

Using the results of §§
3.2
-
3.5
we construct the smash coproduct Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}_{S}\times\mathcal{F}_{S}
. Consider a map

{\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{S}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{S}}
defined by the formula

\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\varphi_{2},n_{2}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}]=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k}).

This map can be extended to an
\mathcal{H}_{S}
-linear map

{\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathcal{H}_{S}\times\mathcal{F}_{S}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{S}}.

Proposition 3.9
.

The map

{\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathcal{H}_{S}\times\mathcal{F}_{S}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{S}}
is a homomorphism of Hopf algebras. In particular, the quasi-shuffle relation for multiple polylogarithms

\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x\star y)=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(y)

holds for
x,y\in\mathcal{F}_{S}.

Proof.

It is easy to see that the map
\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}
commutes with the unit and the counit. We need to check that it commutes with the product and the coproduct. For elements
h\otimes 1\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{S}
this is obvious. It suffices to check these statements on elements of type
1\otimes x\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{S}.

First, we check compatibility with the coproduct, namely the equality

(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}(1\otimes x)=\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(1\otimes x)

for
x=[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\varphi_{2},n_{2}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}].
From (
3.3
) it is easy to see that we have

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}(1\otimes x)=1\otimes\bigl((1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}})\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}x\bigr)\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{S}.

Recall the presentation of
\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)
as an iterated integral

\begin{split}&\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})\\
&=(-1)^{k}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(0;1,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}}_{n_{1}},\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}}_{n_{2}},\dots,\underbrace{0,\dots,0;\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\dots\varphi_{k}}_{n_{k}}).\end{split}

Let

(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+1})=(0,1,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}}_{n_{1}},\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}}_{n_{2}},\dots,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\dots\varphi_{k}}_{n_{k}})

be the sequence of arguments of the corresponding iterated integral. From here, we see that

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)=(-1)^{k}\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{0};x_{1},\dots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\\
&=(-1)^{k}\sum_{(i_{0},\dots,i_{r+1})\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\prod_{p=0}^{r}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{i_{p}};x_{i_{p}+1},\dots,x_{i_{p+1}-1};x_{i_{p+1}})\right)\otimes\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{i_{0}};x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}};x_{i_{r+1}})\end{split}

where the summation goes over the set
\mathcal{I}
of sequences

0=i_{0}<i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}<i_{r+1}=n+1.

We break the set
\mathcal{I}
into subsets
\mathcal{I}_{j}
with
i_{1}=j
. Since
x_{0}=0
, the iterated integral
\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{i_{0}};x_{i_{0}+1},\dots,x_{i_{1}-1};x_{i_{1}})
vanishes if
x_{i_{1}}=0,
so all the terms in the formula above corresponding to sequences in
\mathcal{I}_{j}
vanish unless

j\in\{1,1+n_{1},1+n_{1}+n_{2},\dots,1+n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{k}\}.

For
j=1+n_{1}+\dots+n_{s}
where
0\leq s\leq k
we have

(-1)^{s}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{i_{0}};x_{i_{0}+1},\dots,x_{i_{1}-1};x_{i_{1}})=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\varphi_{2},n_{2}|\dots|\varphi_{s},n_{s}]

and

\begin{split}&(-1)^{k-s}\sum_{(i_{0},\dots,i_{r+1})\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}\left(\prod_{p=1}^{r}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{i_{p}};x_{i_{p}+1},\dots,x_{i_{p+1}-1};x_{i_{p+1}})\right)\otimes\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{i_{0}};x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}};x_{i_{r+1}})\\
&=(1\otimes\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\varphi_{s+1},n_{s+1}|\varphi_{s+2},n_{s+2}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}].\end{split}

It follows that

(3.20)

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)\\
&=\sum_{s=0}^{k}\left(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{s},n_{s}]\otimes 1)\left((1\otimes\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\varphi_{s+1},n_{s+1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}]\right)\right).\end{split}

Compatibility of the map
{\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathcal{H}_{S}\times\mathcal{F}_{S}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{S}}
with the coproduct follows from here.

To check compatibility with the product, it is sufficient to prove that
for
x,y
defined by (
3.12
) we have

\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x\star y)=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(y)\in\mathcal{H}_{S},

namely the quasi-shuffle relation for multiple polylogarithms. We apply the rigidity argument from §
2.4
. By Proposition
3.8
we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\left(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x\star y)-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}(\textup{Li}(x\star y))-\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}(\textup{Li}(x))\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}(\textup{Li}(y))

\displaystyle=(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}})\left(\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}(x\star y)-\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}(x)\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Specializing
\left(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x\star y)-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(y)\right)
to the point
a_{1}=\dots=a_{k}=a_{1}^{\prime}=\dots=a_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}=0
we conclude that
\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x\star y)-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(y)=0.

This finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Proposition

can be extended to generalized multiple polylogarithms in the following way. To simplify the exposition, we work in the Lie coalgebra
\mathcal{L}
instead of

Consider a map

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{0}=0}^{\infty}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{0};n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k}).

Proposition 3.10
.

The generalized quasi-shuffle relation

holds for homogeneous elements

Proof.

Similarly to (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k})\\

&\quad+\sum_{s=0}^{k}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{s})\wedge\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{s+1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{s+1},\dots,\varphi_{k})\\
&\quad+(1\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}].\\
\end{split}

Here is the brief explanation.
Let

(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+1})=(0,\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{n_{0}},1,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}}_{n_{1}},\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}}_{n_{2}},\dots,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\dots\varphi_{k}}_{n_{k}})

be the sequence of arguments of the iterated integral corresponding to the generalized multiple polylogarithm
\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{0};n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}
.
The terms of the coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{0};n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}
correspond to pairs
0\leq i<j\leq n+1.
Indeed, in (
2.5
) only the terms with

in (

. The term

The term

(1\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}]

comes from pairs with
n_{0}<i<j\leq n.
The remaining terms vanish in
\Lambda^{2}\mathcal{L}.

Now, we finish the proof of the generalized quasi-shuffle relation. For

\begin{split}&x=[a_{1},n_{1}|\dots|a_{k},n_{k}]\in\mathcal{F}_{S},\\
&y=[a^{\prime}_{1},n^{\prime}_{1}|\dots|a_{k}^{\prime},n^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}]\in\mathcal{F}_{S}\\
\end{split}

let
a=\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}
and
b=\prod_{j=1}^{k^{\prime}}b_{j}.

We argue by induction on
k+k^{\prime}.
The base case is trivial.
Then

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(x\star y)

\displaystyle=\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(x\star y)\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}\left(ab\right)+(\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})(\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(x\star y))+(1\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x\star y))

\displaystyle=\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(x\star y)\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}\left(ab\right)+(\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})(\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(x)\star\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(y))+(1\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})(\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x)\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(y)).

The terms
(\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})(\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(x)\star\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(y))
and
(1\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})(\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(x)\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(y))
vanish by induction. We get

\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(x\star y)=\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(x\star y)\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}\left(ab\right),

or, equivalently,

\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}_{n_{0}+1}^{\mathcal{L}}(x\star y)=\textup{Li}_{n_{0}}^{\mathcal{L}}(x\star y)\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}\left(ab\right)\text{ for }n_{0}\geq 0.

The statement follows by induction on
n_{0}
and the rigidity argument.
∎

4.1.
Alternating polygons

An alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}=(p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{2n+1})
is an increasing sequence of positive integers such that
p_{i+1}-p_{i}
is odd for
0\leq i\leq 2n.
It is convenient to draw terms
p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{2n+1}
in the vertices of a convex
(2n+2)
-gon, which we will denote by the same letter
\mathrm{P}.
Note that
p_{0}-p_{2n+1}
is also odd, so the condition of being alternating is “cyclically invariant”. Alternating polygons are objects of a category
\underline{\textup{Alt}}
with morphisms being parity- and order-preserving injective maps of the corresponding sequences. There are two classes of isomorphisms of objects in
\underline{\textup{Alt}}
for every
n,
represented by sequences
(0,1,\dots,2n+1)
and
(1,2,\dots,2n+2).
The polygon
\mathrm{P}
is called even if
p_{0}
is even and odd if
p_{0}
is odd.

An alternating subpolygon of
\mathrm{P}
is a subsequence
(p_{i_{0}},p_{i_{1}},\dots,p_{i_{2k+1}})
such that indices
p_{i_{r}}
have alternating parity for
0\leq r\leq 2k+1
. Two subpolygons of
\mathrm{P}
are called disjoint if their interiors do not intersect. Every diagonal of
\mathrm{P}
with ends of different parity decomposes
\mathrm{P}
into a pair of alternating polygons
\mathrm{P}_{1}
and
\mathrm{P}_{2}.
We say that
\mathrm{P}
is a disjoint union of
\mathrm{P}_{1}
and
\mathrm{P}_{2}
and denote it by
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{P}_{1}\sqcup\mathrm{P}_{2}.
More generally, every collection of such diagonals which do not intersect pairwise decomposes
\mathrm{P}
into disjoint alternating polygons. Denote by
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{P})
the set of all such decompositions of
\mathrm{P}.

The set
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{P})
is partially ordered:
D_{1}\leq D_{2}
if every polygon in the decomposition
D_{2}
is contained in some polygon in the decomposition
D_{1}.
The poset
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{P})
has
\{\mathrm{P}\}
as its smallest element. Maximal elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{P})
are called
quadrangulations
. In other words, a quadrangulation
Q
of
\mathrm{P}
is a decomposition of
\mathrm{P}
into disjoint quadrangles, which will necessarily be alternating subpolygons of
\mathrm{P}
(see Figure
7
). We denote by
\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{P})\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{P})
the set of quadrangulations of
\mathrm{P}.
The number of quadrangulations of a
(2n+2)
-gon is equal to

|\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{P})|=\frac{(3n)!}{n!(2n+1)!}.

4.2.
Arborification map

Recall the definition of the Hopf algebra of rooted trees, introduced by Connes and Kreimer in
[
CK99
]
in order to clarify the renormalization procedure in quantum field theory.
A rooted tree is a finite connected graph without cycles with a special vertex, called the root. A decorated rooted tree is a rooted tree with vertices labeled by elements of some set
\mathcal{A}.
Consider a free commutative unitary
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}
generated by isomorphism classes of decorated rooted trees. A
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of this algebra is given by decorated rooted forests. The product in
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}
is given by the concatenation of rooted forests; the unit
1
is represented by the empty forest. The weight of a forest is the number of vertices in it;
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}
is graded by weight. We define the bialgebra structure on
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}
in the following way. A cut
c
of a decorated rooted tree
\mathrm{t}
is a nonempty subset of the set of edges of
\mathrm{t}.
A cut is called admissible if any shortest path in the tree from a vertex to the root meets at most one edge in the cut. Denote by
\textup{Adm}(\mathrm{t})
the set of admissible cuts. After cutting all edges of
\mathrm{t}
in
C
we obtain a rooted forest. Its connected component containing the root of
\mathrm{t}
is denoted
R^{c}(\mathrm{t}).
The product of the remaining connected components is denoted
P^{c}(\mathrm{t}).
Then the coproduct

\Delta^{\mathcal{T}}\colon\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}

is given by the formula

\Delta^{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{t})=1\otimes\mathrm{t}+\mathrm{t}\otimes 1+\sum_{c\>\in\textup{Adm}(\mathrm{t})}R^{c}(\mathrm{t})\otimes P^{c}(\mathrm{t}).

For
a\in\mathcal{A}
we define the “grafting” operator

B_{a}^{+}\colon\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}

associating to every labeled rooted forest
\mathrm{t}_{1}\dots\mathrm{t}_{n}
a tree obtained by grafting the roots of
\mathrm{t}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{t}_{n}
on the common new root labeled by
a\in\mathcal{A}.
This operator satisfies the following equation: for
\mathrm{t}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}
we have

\Delta^{\mathcal{T}}\circ B_{a}^{+}(\mathrm{t})=(B_{a}^{+}\otimes\textup{id})\circ\Delta^{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{t})+1\otimes B_{a}^{+}(\mathrm{t}).

In other words,
B_{a}^{+}
is a family of Hochschild
1
-cocycles on the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}.

The Connes-Kreimer Hopf algebra has the following universal property. Let
(H,\Delta,m)
be a Hopf algebra with a collection of Hochschild
1
-cocycles
L_{a}
for
a\in\mathcal{A},
namely linear maps
L_{a}\colon H\longrightarrow H
such that

\Delta L_{a}=(L_{a}\otimes\textup{id})\Delta+1\otimes L_{a}\text{\ \ for \ \ }a\in\mathcal{A}.

Then there exists a unique Hopf algebra homomorphism
\phi\colon\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}\longrightarrow H
such that

\phi\circ B^{+}_{a}=L_{a}\circ\phi.

Let
\textup{QSh}_{\mathcal{A}}
be the quasi-shuffle on an alphabet
\mathcal{A},
defined in §
3.1
. Recall that the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
has the structure of a commutative semigroup. One can easily check that operators
L_{a}(\omega)=-a\omega
and
L_{a}(\omega)=a\omega+a\cdot\omega
are Hochschild
1
-cocycles on
\textup{QSh}_{\mathcal{A}}.

Now, assume that a “parity” map
p\colon\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}
is given. We call vertices labeled by
a\in\mathcal{A}

even
if
p(a)=0
and
odd
if
p(a)=1.
From the universal property of the Connes-Kreimer Hopf algebra we have the following.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists a unique Hopf algebra homomorphism

\textup{Arb}\colon\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{A}}\longrightarrow\textup{QSh}_{\mathcal{A}}
such that

(4.3)

\textup{Arb}(B_{a}^{+}(\mathrm{t}))=\begin{cases}-a\textup{Arb}(\mathrm{t})&\text{\ if \ }p(a)=0,\\
\ \>\,a\textup{Arb}(\mathrm{t})+a\cdot\textup{Arb}(\mathrm{t})&\text{\ if \ }p(a)=1.\\
\end{cases}

Example 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
be an alphabet with
p(a_{1})=1,\ p(a_{2})=p(a_{3})=0.
Consider a tree
\mathrm{t}
with a root labelled by
a_{1}
and two leaves labelled by
a_{2}
and
a_{3}.
Then

\mathrm{t}=B_{a_{1}}^{+}\left(B_{a_{2}}^{+}(1)B_{a_{3}}^{+}(1)\right).

By (
4.3
) we have

\textup{Arb}\left(B_{a_{2}}^{+}(1)\right)=-[a_{2}]
and
\textup{Arb}\left(B_{a_{3}}^{+}(1)\right)=-[a_{3}],

so

\textup{Arb}\left(B_{a_{2}}^{+}(1)B_{a_{3}}^{+}(1)\right)=(-[a_{2}])\star(-[a_{3}])=[a_{2}|a_{3}]+[a_{3}|a_{2}]+[a_{2}\cdot a_{3}].

It follows that

\textup{Arb}(\mathrm{t})=[a_{1}|a_{2}|a_{3}]+[a_{1}|a_{3}|a_{2}]+[a_{1}|a_{2}\cdot a_{3}]+[a_{1}\cdot a_{2}|a_{3}]+[a_{1}\cdot a_{3}|a_{2}]+[a_{1}\cdot a_{2}\cdot a_{3}].

For a
(2n+2)
-alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}
with
n\geq 2
we have

\textup{pr}_{n}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if\ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is even,}\\
[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n]&\text{if\ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is odd.}\end{cases}

Proof.

First, we consider the case of an even
\mathrm{P}
. From (
4.7
) it follows that each word in
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
is obtained by concatenation of a word of length one and a word of length at least one. Thus the concatenation has length at least two, so
\textup{pr}_{n}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=0.

For an odd polygon
\mathrm{P}=(1,\dots,2n+2)
we proceed by induction. Only terms in the second sum in formula (
4.8
) may contribute to
\textup{pr}_{n}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})
by the same reason as in the even case. Terms with
j=i+1
give a contribution
[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n]
and terms with
j=i+3
give a contribution
-[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n].
It is easy to see that words coming from other terms have length at least two, so do not contribute to the projection
\textup{pr}_{n}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})
. Thus

\textup{pr}_{n}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=n[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n]+(n-1)(-[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n])=[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n].

∎

Next, notice that for all words
[\textup{cr}_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\textup{cr}_{k},n_{k}]
in
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
we have
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\textup{cr}_{i}=\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}=n,
so in words of type (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) each side determines the other.

Lemma 4.11
.

The following equalities hold for an even polygon

\mathrm{P}=(0,1,\dots,2n+1)
for
n\geq 3:

\displaystyle\textup{pr}_{1,n-1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=[\textup{cr}(0,1,2n-2,2n+1),1|*]-[\textup{cr}(0,1,2n,2n+1),1|*]

\displaystyle\quad-[\textup{cr}(0,3,2n-2,2n+1),1|*]+[\textup{cr}(0,3,2n,2n+1),1|*],

\displaystyle\textup{pr}_{n-1,1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=0.

Proof.

The statement follows from (
4.7
) and Lemma
4.10
.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

The following equalities hold for an odd polygon
\mathrm{P}=(1,2,\dots,2n+2)
for
n\geq 3
:

\begin{split}&\textup{pr}_{n-1,1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\sum_{i=0}^{2n-2}(-1)^{i}[*|\textup{cr}(i+1,i+2,i+3,i+4),1],\\
&\textup{pr}_{1,n-1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\textup{cr}(1,2i,2i+1,2n+2),1|*]-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}[\textup{cr}(1,2i,2i+3,2n+2),1|*].\end{split}

Proof.

The statement follows from (
4.8
) and Lemma
4.10
.
∎

Corollary 4.13
.

For an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}
we have

\pi_{1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=0.

Proof.

Cases
n=1,2
can be easily checked by hand. For an even
\mathrm{P}=(0,1,\dots,2n+1)
and
n\geq 3
by Lemma
3.6
and Lemma
4.11
we need to check that

\displaystyle\log^{\mathcal{H}}\left(1-\textup{cr}(0,1,2n-2,2n+1)^{-1}\right)-\log^{\mathcal{H}}\left(1-\textup{cr}(0,1,2n,2n+1)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\log^{\mathcal{H}}\left(1-\textup{cr}(0,3,2n-2,2n+1)^{-1}\right)+\log^{\mathcal{H}}\left(1-\textup{cr}(0,3,2n,2n+1])^{-1}\right)=0,

which follows from an equality

[x_{0},x_{2n-2},x_{2n+1},x_{1}][x_{0},x_{2n},x_{2n+1},x_{1}]^{-1}[x_{0},x_{2n-2},x_{2n+1},x_{3}]^{-1}[x_{0},x_{2n},x_{2n+1},x_{3}]=1.

The proof for an odd polygon is similar though more tedious.
∎

4.5.
Quadrangulation formula

Theorem 4.14

(Quadrangulation Formula)
.

For an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}
the formal quadrangular polylogarithm
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
lies in
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{P}}.

Proof.

We need to show that
\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=0.
We proceed by induction on
n.
If
n=1
then
\mathrm{P}
is a
4
-gon and the statement is obvious. By induction and Proposition
4.7
element
\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
lies in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathcal{H}},
so we have

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{FF}}\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=(T\otimes 1)(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}))+\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\otimes 1+1\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}).

On the other hand, we have

\begin{split}&(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\\
=&(1\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})+(T\otimes 1)(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}))+\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\otimes 1\\
=&(1\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}})\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})+(T\otimes 1)(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}))+\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\otimes 1+1\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}).\end{split}

Lemma
3.4
implies that

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{FF}}\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}\left(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}\right)=(1\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\left(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}\right),

thus

(1\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}})\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=0.

In other words,
\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
lies in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{P}}\otimes\textup{Prim}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}).

For all words
[\textup{cr}_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\textup{cr}_{k},n_{k}]

appearing in
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
we have
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\textup{cr}_{i}=\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}=n.
Thus a primitive word appearing in
\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
equals
[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),i]
for
1\leq i\leq n-1.
It follows that there exist elements
h_{1},\dots,h_{n-1}\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}h_{i}\otimes[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n-i].

Let
r
be the smallest index such that
h_{r}\neq 0.
From Corollary
4.13
we know that
\pi_{1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=h_{1}=0,
so
r>1.

By Lemma
3.3
we have

(\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{HH}}\otimes 1)\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=(1\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}})\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}},

so

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left((\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{HH}}h_{i})\otimes\left[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n-i\right]\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(h_{i}\otimes\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{HF}}\left[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n-i\right]\right).

Comparing coefficients in front of
[\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}),n-r]

we get that

\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{HH}}(h_{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}h_{r-i}\otimes\frac{(\log^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}))^{i}}{i!},

so since
h_{1}=\dots=h_{r-1}=0
we have
\widetilde{\Delta}^{\mathcal{HH}}(h_{r})=0.
Consider the specialization to a point
p
in the Deligne-Mumford compactification
\overline{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathrm{P}}
where points
x_{2i}
and
x_{2i+1}
collide for
0\leq i\leq n
. For an alternating subpolygon
\mathrm{Q}
of
\mathrm{P}
the function
\textup{cr}(\mathrm{Q})
vanishes at
p.
From (
3.5
) it is clear that
h_{r}
is a linear combination of Hodge iterated integrals

\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(0;1,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,m_{1}}_{d_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,m_{1}m_{2}\dots m_{s-1}}_{d_{s-1}},\underbrace{0,\dots,0;m_{1}m_{2}\dots m_{s}}_{d_{s}}),

where
m_{i}
are monomials in variables
\textup{cr}(\mathrm{Q})
of degree at least
1
for alternating subpolygons
\mathrm{Q}
of
\mathrm{P}
. All such integrals specialize to zero at
p,
so by the rigidity argument (§
2.4
)
h_{r}=0,
which contradicts our assumption. This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

In §
4.3
we defined a collection of alternating subpolygons
\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})
of an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}.
By Theorem
4.14
we can view the element
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
as an element in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}.

Corollary 4.15
.

The following equality holds:

(4.11)

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{\mathrm{S}\in\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\left(\prod_{i=0}^{2r}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}^{i}}\right).

Proof.

The statement follows from Theorem
4.14
and (
3.4
).
∎

5.
Quadrangular polylogarithms

5.1.
The formula for quadrangular polylogarithms via Hodge correlators

For
n\geq 0
consider a collection of points
x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1}\in\mathbb{C}.
For any
k\geq 0
we define the quadrangular polylogarithm
\textup{QLi}_{n,k}
as a certain element of the Lie coalgebra of framed mixed Hodge-Tate structures of weight
n+k

\textup{QLi}_{n,k}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})\in\mathcal{L}_{n+k}.

Quadrangular polylogarithm is defined by an explicit formula, see Definition
5.2
. This definition looks very ad hoc; more naturally, quadrangular polylogarithm can be defined inductively as the unique element with the coproduct given by formula (
5.2
).

Consider a set
\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
of all nondecreasing sequences
\bar{s}=(i_{0},\dots,i_{n+k})
of indices

0\leq i_{0}\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{n+k}\leq 2n+1

such that every even number
1\leq s\leq 2n+1
appears in the sequence
s
at most once and the sequence
s
contains at least one element in each pair
\{2i,2i+1\}
for
0\leq i\leq n.
For a sequence
\bar{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
we define

\textup{sign}(\bar{s})=\begin{cases}-1&\text{\ if\ }\bar{s}\text{\ contains an odd number of even elements,}\\
\ \ 1&\text{\ if\ }\bar{s}\text{\ contains an even number of even elements.}\\
\end{cases}

Example 5.1
.

We have

\mathcal{C}_{1,0}=\{(0,2),\ (0,3),\ (1,2),\ (1,3)\}

and

\mathcal{C}_{1,1}=\{(0,1,2),\ (0,2,3),\ (0,1,3),\ (0,3,3),\ (1,2,3),\ (1,1,2),\ (1,1,3),\ (1,3,3)\}.

Definition 5.2
.

For
x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1}\in\mathbb{C}
we define the quadrangular polylogarithm of weight
n+k
by the formula

(5.1)

\begin{split}&\textup{QLi}_{n,k}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})=(-1)^{n+1}\sum_{\bar{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}}\textup{sign}(\bar{s})\textup{Cor}(x_{i_{0}},\dots,x_{i_{n+k}})\in\mathcal{L}_{n+k}.\\
\end{split}

Example 5.3
.

In weight
1
we have

\textup{QLi}_{0,1}(x_{0},x_{1})=\textup{Cor}(x_{0},x_{1})=\log^{\mathcal{L}}(x_{0}-x_{1})\\

and

\displaystyle\textup{QLi}_{1,0}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=\textup{Cor}(x_{0},x_{2})-\textup{Cor}(x_{0},x_{3})-\textup{Cor}(x_{1},x_{2})+\textup{Cor}(x_{1},x_{3})

\displaystyle=\log^{\mathcal{L}}(1-[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])=-\textup{Li}_{1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]).

Here is a more interesting example; the computation below uses the five-term relation (
1.1
).

Example 5.4
.

For
n=1,k=1
we recover the dilogarithm:

\begin{split}&\textup{QLi}_{1,1}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\textup{Cor}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})+\textup{Cor}(x_{0},x_{2},x_{3})-\textup{Cor}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{3})\\
&\quad-\textup{Cor}(x_{0},x_{3},x_{3})-\textup{Cor}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})-\textup{Cor}(x_{1},x_{1},x_{2})+\textup{Cor}(x_{1},x_{1},x_{3})+\textup{Cor}(x_{1},x_{3},x_{3})\\
&=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{2}([\infty,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}])+\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{2}([\infty,x_{0},x_{2},x_{3}])-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{2}([\infty,x_{0},x_{1},x_{3}])-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{2}([\infty,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])\\
&=-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{2}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{1;1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]).\\
\end{split}

The coproduct of a quadrangular polylogarithm can be computed inductively. We assemble quadrangular polylogarithms with fixed
n
and different
k
into a series

\textup{QLi}_{n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\textup{QLi}_{n,k}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})\in\mathcal{L}.

We introduce the following notation:

\textup{QLi}_{n}^{(-)^{s}}(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{2n},x_{2n+1})=\begin{cases}\textup{QLi}_{n}(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{2n},x_{2n+1})&\text{\ if \ }s\text{\ is even, \ }\\
-\textup{QLi}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2n+1},x_{0})&\text{\ if \ }s\text{\ is odd. \ }\\
\end{cases}

Theorem 5.5
.

We have the following formula for the coproduct of quadrangular polylogarithms:

(5.2)

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{QLi}_{n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})=\\
&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq 2n+1\\
j-i=2s+1\end{subarray}}\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i},x_{j},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{(-)^{i}}(x_{i},\dots,x_{j}).\\
\end{split}

Proof.

For a sequence
\bar{s}=(i_{0},\dots,i_{n+k})
we put

\textup{Cor}(\bar{s})=\textup{Cor}(x_{i_{0}},\ldots,x_{i_{n+k}})\in\mathcal{L}_{n+k}.

After replacing the quadrangular polylogarithms in (
5.2
) with corresponding sums of correlators and applying (
2.13
) both sides of (
5.2
) contain only terms of the form

\pm\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})

for certain nondecreasing admissible sequences
\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,\bullet}.
Below we show that after all cancellations the terms in the left-hand side (LHS) and in the right-hand side (RHS) of (
5.2
) are the same. For that we will use only (
2.12
).

For each term
\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})
appearing in (
5.2
) both sequences
\bar{s}_{1}
and
\bar{s}_{2}
contain each even index from
0
to
2n
at most once. It is easy to see that the set-theoretic intersection
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
contains at most two indices. We look at all possibilities for the cardinality of the set
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
and show that in each case the corresponding terms in the LHS and the RHS coincide after all the cancellations are performed.

Case 1

The intersection
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
is empty.

In the LHS such terms do not appear since in the coproduct of a correlator
\textup{Cor}(x_{i_{0}},\dots,x_{i_{n+k}})
the sequences
\bar{s}_{1}
and
\bar{s}_{2}
contain at least one index in common. Thus we need to show that in the RHS such terms cancel out. Consider a term coming from

\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i},x_{j},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{(-)^{i}}(x_{i},\dots,x_{j})

for
0\leq i<j\leq 2n+1,\ j-i=2s+1.
This term has a form
\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})
for a subsequence
s_{1}
of
0,1,\dots,i,j,\dots,2n+1
and a subsequence
s_{2}
of
i,\dots,j.
Indices
i
and
j
have different parity. Assume that
i
is even and
j
is odd; the other case is similar. By the second condition in the definition of
\mathcal{C}_{n,k},
sequence
s_{1}
contains
i
or
j
.

(a)

Case 1a:
i\in\bar{s}_{1}
and
j\notin\bar{s}_{1}
.
In this case the same term appears in exactly one other place in the RHS coming from

\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i+1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{-}(x_{i+1},\dots,x_{j+1})

with the opposite sign.

(b)

Case 1b:
i\notin\bar{s}_{1}
and
j\in\bar{s}_{1}
.
In this case the same term appears in

\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{j-1},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{-}(x_{i-1},\dots,x_{j-1})

with the opposite sign.

(c)

Case 1c:
i,j\in\bar{s}_{1}
.
In this case the same term appears in

\textup{QLi}_{n-s+1}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i+1},x_{j-1},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s-1}^{-}(x_{i+1},\dots,x_{j-1})

with the opposite sign.

Case 2

The intersection
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
contains two even indices.

Such terms do not appear. Indeed, in the LHS, all the terms come from cutting a sequence
\bar{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
. One even index in
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
has to come from the beginning point of the cut. Any even index appears in
\bar{s}
at most once, so the second index in the intersection must be odd. Next, in in a term in the RHS coming from

\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i},x_{j},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{(-)^{i}}(x_{i},\dots,x_{j})

the indices in the intersection
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
have to be equal to
i
or
j
where
j-i=2s+1.
Thus, one of them has to be odd.

Case 3

The intersection
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
consists of an even index
i
or a pair of an even index
i
and an odd index
j.
Consider a term
\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})
in the LHS. Let
j
be the largest odd number such that
\bar{s}_{1}
contains
j-1
or
j.
The same term (with the same sign) comes from

\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i},x_{j},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{(-)^{i}}(x_{i},\dots,x_{j})

in the RHS.

Case 4

The intersection
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
consists of an index
i
, which is odd.

Consider a term
\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})
in the LHS coming from a cut beginning with
i.
Since
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}=\{i\},
the end point of the cut is located between two distinct indices, which we call
j_{1}
and
j_{2}.
Such term comes from

\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i},x_{j},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{(-)^{i}}(x_{i},\dots,x_{j}),

where
j
is the largest even number less than
j_{2}.

Case 5

The intersection
\bar{s}_{1}\cap\bar{s}_{2}
consists of two odd indices
i
and
j
.

Such terms do not appear in the RHS by the same reason as in the Case 2. We need to show that in the LHS such terms cancel out. Consider a term
\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})
of this type obtained from a cut of a sequence
\bar{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
beginning at
i
and ending between
j
’s. The term
\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})
appears in the coproduct of another admissible sequence
\bar{s}^{\prime}
obtained from
s
by adding an extra
i
and deleting
j.
For this, one should take a cut beginning at
j
and ending between
i
’s. Since

\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})+\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{2})\wedge\textup{Cor}(\bar{s}_{1})=0,

the statement follows.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
5.5
. ∎

5.2.
Some properties of quadrangular polylogarithms

It is easy to see that

(5.3)

\textup{QLi}_{0}(x_{0},x_{1})=\log^{\mathcal{L}}(x_{0}-x_{1}),

because

\textup{Cor}(x_{0},\underbrace{x_{1},x_{1},\dots,x_{1}}_{m})=0\text{\ for \ }m\geq 2.

Here is a more interesting computation.

Proposition 5.6
.

We have

\displaystyle\textup{QLi}_{1,k}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])

\displaystyle=-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k;1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]).

Proof.

We have

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{QLi}_{1}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\\
&=\textup{QLi}_{1}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\wedge\left(\textup{QLi}_{0}(x_{0},x_{1})+\textup{QLi}^{-}_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})+\textup{QLi}_{0}(x_{2},x_{3})\right)\\
&\quad+\textup{QLi}_{0}(x_{0},x_{3})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{1}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\\
&=\textup{QLi}_{1}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}\left(\frac{(x_{0}-x_{1})(x_{2}-x_{3})}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(x_{3}-x_{0})}\right),\\
\end{split}

so for
k\geq 1
we have

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{QLi}_{1,k}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\textup{QLi}_{1,k-1}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}].\end{split}

It is easy to see that for
k\geq 1
we have

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}(a)=\log^{\mathcal{L}}(a)\wedge\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(a),\\
&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k;1}(a)=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k-1;1}(a)\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}(a).\end{split}

From here and the fact that
\textup{QLi}_{1,0}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\log^{\mathcal{L}}(1-[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])
both the equality

\textup{QLi}_{1,k}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(-1)^{k}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])

and the equality

\textup{QLi}_{1,k}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{k;1}([x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}])

follow by induction.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Quadrangular polylogarithms
\textup{QLi}_{n,k}
for
n\geq 1
are invariant under projective transformations, i.e., for
\psi\in\textup{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
we have

(5.4)

\textup{QLi}_{n,k}(\psi(x_{0}),\ldots,\psi(x_{2n+1}))=\textup{QLi}_{n,k}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{2n+1}).

Proof.

From (
5.3
) we see that for
n\geq 1
the formula (
5.2
) can be rewritten in the following way:

(5.5)

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{QLi}_{n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})=\textup{QLi}_{n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}[x_{0},x_{2i-1},x_{2i},x_{2i+1}]\right)\\
&\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq 2n+1\\
j-i=2s+1\\
s\geq 1\end{subarray}}\textup{QLi}_{n-s}(x_{0},\dots,x_{i},x_{j},\dots,x_{2n+1})\wedge\textup{QLi}_{s}^{(-)^{i}}(x_{i},\dots,x_{j}).\\
\end{split}

To show the projective invariance, we proceed by induction. For
n=1
the statement follows from Proposition
5.6
. From (
5.5
) we see that the coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{QLi}_{n,k}\in\Lambda^{2}\mathcal{L}
is invariant under projective transformations. Now the statement follows by the rigidity argument (§
2.4
), because (
5.4
) is true for
\psi=\textup{Id}\in\textup{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
∎

5.3.
The formula for quadrangular polylogarithms via multiple polylogarithms

Let
\mathrm{P}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{2n+1})
be an alternating polygon defined in §
4.1
. Consider the following element of the Lie coalgebra
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{P}}
of framed unipotent variations of mixed Hodge-Tate structures on
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}:

\textup{QLi}_{k}(\mathrm{P})=\begin{cases}\textup{QLi}_{n,k}(x_{p_{0}},\dots,x_{p_{2n+1}})&\text{\ if \ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is even,}\\
\textup{QLi}_{n,k}^{-}(x_{p_{0}},\dots,x_{p_{2n+1}})&\text{\ if \ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is odd.}\end{cases}

We define

\textup{QLi}(\mathrm{P})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\textup{QLi}_{k}(\mathrm{P})\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{P}}.

Next, consider a map
\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{P}}
defined by formula

\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}]=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k})

for functions
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}\in\mathbb{C}(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}})^{\times}
and
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\geq 1.
Also, in §
4.3
we constructed an element
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{P}}
called formal quadrangular polylogarithm.

Theorem 5.8
.

The following equality holds for an alternating
(2n+2)-
gon
\mathrm{P}
and
k\geq 0
:

(5.6)

\textup{QLi}_{k}(\mathrm{P})=\textup{Li}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}).

Proof.

To show that
\textup{QLi}(\mathrm{P})=\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})
we proceed by induction on
n
. For
n=1
the statement follows from (
4.5
) and Proposition
5.6
.

Assume that
n>1.
Recall that
\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})
is the set of all alternating subpolygons
(p_{i_{0}},p_{i_{1}},\dots,p_{i_{2r+1}})
of
\mathrm{P}
with
i_{0}=0
and
i_{2r+1}=2n+1
. Let
\textup{alt}_{0}(\mathrm{P})
be the subset of
\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})
consisting of alternating subpolygons

S=(p_{0},\dots,p_{i},p_{j},\dots,p_{2n+1}).
Then for
S^{\prime}=(p_{i},\dots,p_{j})
we have a decomposition
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{S}\sqcup\mathrm{S}^{\prime}
.

For an even
\mathrm{P}
formula (
5.5
) is equivalent to the following statement:

(5.7)

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{QLi}(\mathrm{P})=\textup{QLi}(\mathrm{P})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}))+\sum_{\mathrm{S}\in\textup{alt}_{0}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{QLi}(\mathrm{S})\wedge\textup{QLi}(\mathrm{S}^{\prime}).\\
\end{split}

Both parts of the formula (
5.7
) change sign after a cyclic shift, so the same formula holds for an odd polygon
\mathrm{P}
as well.

On the other hand, recall that in the proof of Proposition
3.10
we found that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k})\\
&=\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}(\varphi_{1}\dots\varphi_{k})\\
&\quad+\sum_{s=0}^{k}\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{s})\wedge\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bullet;n_{s+1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{s+1},\dots,\varphi_{k})\\
&\quad+(1\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}].\\
\end{split}

It follows that

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}))+(\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{FF}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}+(1\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}.\\
\end{split}

By Proposition
3.8
we have
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=0,
so

(5.8)

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}))+(\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{FF}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}.\\
\end{split}

By formula
(\ref{FormulaCoproductFormalPolylogarithm})
we have

(\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}})\Delta^{\mathcal{FF}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{S\in\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}})\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{2r}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}^{i}}\right)=\sum_{S\in\textup{alt}_{0}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}})\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}^{\prime}}).

The second equality holds because for polygons
S
in
\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})
but not in
\textup{alt}_{0}(\mathrm{P})
the term

\textup{Li}_{\bullet}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{2r}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}^{i}}\right)

vanishes in
\mathcal{L}_{P}
by the generalized quasi-shuffle relation (Proposition
3.10
). Now, from (
5.8
) we have

(5.9)

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\wedge\log^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}))+\sum_{S\in\textup{alt}_{0}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{Li}_{\bullet}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}})\wedge\textup{Li}_{\bullet}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}^{\prime}}).\\
\end{split}

Comparing formulas (
5.7
) and (
5.9
) we conclude by induction that

\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{QLi}(\mathrm{P}))=\Delta^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{Li}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})).

It is easy to see that for both framed variations, the specialization to a point with

x_{p_{0}}=x_{p_{1}},x_{p_{2}}=x_{p_{3}},\dots,x_{p_{2n}}=x_{p_{2n+1}}

vanishes, so the conclusion follows by the rigidity argument (§
2.4
).
∎

Identities between variations of framed mixed Hodge-Tate structures imply identities between the corresponding multivalued functions, see §
2.4
. In view of that, Theorem
5.8
implies Theorem
1.2
.

Corollary 5.9
.

The depth of the quadrangular polylogarithm
\textup{QLi}_{n,k}
is less than or equal to
n.

Proof.

We need to show that for an alternating
(2n+2)-
gon
\mathrm{P}
the depth of
\textup{Li}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})
is less than or equal to
n.
From Lemma
4.8
we conclude that
\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}
is the sum of words of length less than or equal to
n.
Since the map
\textup{Li}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}
sends a word of length
r
to a multiple polylogarithm of depth
r,
the statement follows.
∎

5.4.
Universality of quadrangular polylogarithms

Proposition 5.10
.

The following formula holds:

(5.10)

\textup{Cor}_{a}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n})=\sum_{s=0}^{2n+2}\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\leq 2n}(-1)^{s}\textup{QLi}_{n,n}(x_{0},\dots,a,\dots,a,\dots,x_{2n},a).

Here the
s
-th term is
(-1)^{s}
times a sum over
0\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\leq 2n
of quadrangular polylogarithms obtained from the quadrangular polylogarithm
\textup{QLi}_{n,n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n},a)
by substituting the point
a
instead of points
x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{s}}.

Similarly,

(5.11)

\textup{Cor}_{a}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})=\sum_{s=0}^{2n+2}\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\leq 2n+1}(-1)^{s}\textup{QLi}_{n,n+1}(x_{0},\dots,a,\dots,a,\dots,x_{2n+1}).

Here the
s
-th term is
(-1)^{s}
times a sum over
0\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\leq 2n+1
of quadrangular polylogarithms obtained from the quadrangular polylogarithm
\textup{QLi}_{n,n+1}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})
by substituting the point
a
instead of points
x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{s}}.

Proof.

We prove (
5.11
); the proof of (
5.10
) is similar. Consider a vector space of functions depending on variables
(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1}).
Consider an endomorphism of this space given by the formula

\textup{T}(f)(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})=\sum_{s=0}^{2n+2}\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{s}\leq 2n+1}(-1)^{s}f(x_{0},\dots,a,\dots,a,\dots,x_{2n+1}).

Any function which does not depend on at least one variable
x_{i}
lies in the kernel of
\textup{T}.
According to Definition
5.2

\textup{QLi}_{n,n+1}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})=\textup{Cor}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})+(\text{correlators with repeating argument}),

so

\textup{T}(\textup{QLi}_{n,n+1}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1}))=\textup{T}(\textup{Cor}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1})).

On the other hand, by (
2.10
) we have

\textup{T}(\textup{Cor}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1}))=\textup{Cor}_{a}(x_{0},\dots,x_{2n+1}).

∎

6.
Volume of non-Euclidean orthoschemes

6.1.
Projective simplices and scissors congruence

Let
E
be a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
of dimension
m
and consider a quadratic form
q\in\mathbb{S}^{2}E^{\vee}.
The set of zeros of
q
in the projectivization
\mathbb{P}(E)
is a quadric, which we denote by
Q
. The quadric
Q
is smooth if and only if the quadratic form
q
is nondegenerate. A smooth quadric defines a duality between subspaces of
\mathbb{P}(E),
known as the polar duality. For a subspace
\pi\subseteq\mathbb{P}(E)
the polar subspace
\pi^{\perp}
has dimension
m-2-\textup{dim}(\pi).
Two hyperplanes
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
in
\mathbb{P}(E)
are called orthogonal if
H_{1}^{\perp}\in H_{2}
(equivalently,
H_{2}^{\perp}\in H_{1}.
)

A smooth quadric
Q
contains projective subspaces of dimension
\left\lfloor\dfrac{m}{2}\right\rfloor-1
parametrized by a certain Fano variety. This variety is irreducible if
\textup{dim}(Q)
is odd and has two connected components if
\textup{dim}(Q)
is even. We call the choice of an irreducible component of this variety an
orientation
of the quadric.

Definition 6.1
.

A projective simplex
S=(Q;\mathcal{H})
in
\mathbb{P}(E)
is a configuration, consisting of a quadric
Q
and an ordered set of hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},H_{2},\dots,H_{m}\}
in
\mathbb{P}(E).

Two projective simplices
S_{1}=(Q_{1};\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
S_{2}=(Q_{2};\mathcal{H}_{2})
are called isometric if there exists a projective transformation sending
Q_{1}
to
Q_{2}
and hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to the corresponding hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}_{2}.
Denote by
h_{i}
hyperplanes in
E
with projectivization
H_{i}
and for any subset
I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,m\}
define

h_{I}=\bigcap_{i\in I}h_{i}\subseteq E
and
H_{I}=\bigcap_{i\in I}H_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{P}(E).
A projective simplex
S
is called
nondegenerate
if intersections
Q_{I}=H_{I}\cap Q
are smooth for every subset
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
(in particular,
Q
itself is smooth). An orientation of
S
is an orientation of
Q_{I}
for every
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
. A nondegenerate projective simplex has
2^{(2^{m-1}-1)}
orientations.

Let
S
be a nondegenerate simplex. For every subset
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
we define the projective simplex
S_{I}
, called the
I
-face of
S.
Simplex
S_{I}
is a configuration of the quadric
Q\cap H_{I}
in the projective space
H_{I}
and hyperplanes
H_{I}\cap H_{j}
for
j\notin I.
Next, we define projective simplex
S^{I}
, called
I
-angle of
S.
It is a configuration of the quadric
Q\cap(H_{I})^{\perp}
in the projective space
(H_{I})^{\perp}
and a collection of hyperplanes
H_{I\fgebackslash\{i\}}\cap(H_{I})^{\perp}
for
i\in I.
It is easy to see that for an odd-dimensional simplex
S
an orientation of
S
induces an orientation of all its faces and angles.

Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be a pair of projective simplices in spaces
\mathbb{P}(E)
and
\mathbb{P}(E^{\prime})
defined by quadratic forms
q
and
q^{\prime}
and hyperplanes
h_{1},\dots,h_{m}
and
h_{1}^{\prime},\dots,h_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}.
Their join
S_{1}\cdot S_{2}
is a projective simplex in
\mathbb{P}(E\oplus E^{\prime})
defined by hyperplanes
h_{i}\oplus E^{\prime},1\leq i\leq m
and
E\oplus h_{j}^{\prime},1\leq j\leq m^{\prime}.
If
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are nondegenerate, then
S_{1}\cdot S_{2}
is nondegenerate. If
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are oriented, then
S_{1}\cdot S_{2}
has a canonical orientation.

Next, we recall a construction of the Hopf algebra of polytopes modulo scissors congruence, following Goncharov
[
Gon99
, §3.4]
. We define a commutative graded Hopf algebra

\mathcal{G}
over
\mathbb{Q}
by generators and relations in the following way. We have
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathbb{Q}
and for
n\geq 1
the component
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is generated by isometry classes
[S]
of oriented nondegenerate
(2n-1)
-dimensional projective simplices, subject to the following relations:

(1)

For the class
[S]
of a simplex
S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{2n})
in
\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{2n}
we have

[(Q;H_{\sigma(1)},\dots,H_{\sigma(2n)})]=(-1)^{\sigma}[(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{2n})].

The class
[S]
does not depend on orientations of the quadrics
Q_{I}
for
I\neq\{1,\ldots,m\}.
If
\bar{S}
is the same simplex with the opposite orientation of
Q
then
[\bar{S}]=-[S].

If
Q
hyperplanes
H_{i}
are not in general position, we put
[S]=0.

(2)

Consider a collection of hyperplanes
H_{0},H_{1},H_{2}\dots,H_{2n}
and an oriented quadric
Q
in
\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}.
Then

\sum_{i=0}^{2n}(-1)^{i}[(Q;H_{0},\dots\widehat{H_{i}},\dots,H_{2n})]=0.

The product in
\mathcal{G}
is defined on generators as the join:

[S_{1}][S_{2}]=[S_{1}\cdot S_{2}].

Clearly, it is commutative. The coproduct is defined by the formula

(6.1)

\Delta^{\mathcal{G}}[S]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,\dots,2n\}\\
|I|\text{ is even}\end{subarray}}[S_{I}]\otimes\left[S^{I}\right].

The coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{G}}
is a projective counterpart of the Dehn invariant, used by Dehn to show that a regular tetrahedron is not scissors congruent to a cube of the same volume.

In
[
Gon99
, §3]
Goncharov constructed the Hodge realization map

h\colon\mathcal{G}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H},

assigning a framed mixed Hodge structure to a projective tetrahedron. For an oriented projective tetrahedron
S
, the framed mixed Hodge-Tate structure
h(S)
is equal to

H^{2n-1}\left(\mathbb{P}^{2n-1}\fgebackslash Q,\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{2n}H_{i}\right)\fgebackslash Q\right)

with a certain framing. Goncharov proved
[
Gon99
, Theorem 3.11]
that
h
is a homomorphism of commutative Hopf algebras.

Example 6.2
.

In weight one we have
\mathcal{H}_{1}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}_{\mathbb{Q}}.
For a
1
-dimensional simplex
S=(Q;H_{1},H_{2})
a quadric
Q
is a pair of points
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}.
An orientation of the quadric is the choice of an ordering of points
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}.
We have

h(S)=-\frac{1}{2}\log^{\mathcal{H}}\left([Q_{1},H_{1},Q_{2},H_{2}]\right)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}.

For every hyperbolic simplex
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2n-1}
of dimension
2n-1
one can construct the corresponding projective simplex
S
, see
[
Gon99
, §1.5]
. In
[
Gon99
, §4]
Goncharov defined a real period map

(6.2)

\mathrm{per}_{\mathbb{R}}\colon\mathcal{H}_{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}(n-1)

where
\mathbb{R}(n-1)=\mathbb{R}(2\pi i)^{n-1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}.
Goncharov proved in
[
Gon99
, Theorem 4.7]
that the following equality holds:

\textup{Vol}(\mathcal{S})=\mathrm{per}_{\mathbb{R}}(h(S))\in\mathbb{R}(n-1).

Notice that our normalization of the hyperbolic volume is different from the standard one by a factor of
(2\pi i)^{n}i/(2n-2)!.

6.2.
Orthoschemes and Maslov index

Definition 6.3
.

A projective simplex
S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{m})
in
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
is called an orthoscheme if
Q
is smooth, the hyperplanes
H_{1},\dots,H_{m}
are in general position and the hyperplanes
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
are orthogonal for
|i-j|>1.

From the point of view of the theory of scissors congruence, orthoschemes are important because
they generate the scissors congruence Hopf algebra
\mathcal{G}
as a vector space. One can easily see that by considering an orthogonal version of the barycentric subdivision. Every simplex in
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
is scissors congruent to a sum of
m!
orthoschemes. A related question of presenting a simplex as a union of disjoint orthoschemes is much harder and is related to a conjecture of Hadwiger, see
[
Had56
]
.

For a projective orthoscheme
S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{m})
in
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
we define two more hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}

(6.3)

H_{0}=\left(\bigcap_{i=2}^{m}H_{i}\right)^{\perp}\text{ \ and \ }H_{m+1}=\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{m-1}H_{i}\right)^{\perp}.

We label hyperplanes
H_{0},\dots,H_{m+1}
by an index
i\in\mathbb{Z}/(m+2)\mathbb{Z}
and denote by
h_{0},\dots,h_{m+1}
the corresponding hyperplanes in
E.
Distinct indices
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}/(m+2)\mathbb{Z}
are called adjacent if
(i-j)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{m+2}.
Clearly, the hyperplanes
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
are orthogonal for any pair of nonadjacent indices.

Definition 6.4
.

An orthoscheme
S
is called generic if hyperplanes
H_{0},\dots,H_{m+1}
are in general position (the intersection of any
m
of them is empty).

We will show that a generic orthoscheme is a nondegenerate projective simplex, see Corollary
6.10
. If
S
is a generic orthoscheme, then the formula (
6.3
) generalizes to

H_{r}=\left(\bigcap_{i\not\in\{r-1,r,r+1\}}H_{i}\right)^{\perp}\text{ \ for \ }r\in\mathbb{Z}/(m+2)\mathbb{Z}.

Remark 6.5
.

If
S
is a generic orthoscheme, then simplices

S^{[i]}=(Q;H_{i+1},H_{i+2},\dots,H_{i+m}),\ \ i\in\mathbb{Z}/(m+2)\mathbb{Z},

are also generic orthoschemes. This sequence of orthoschemes is classically known as the Napier cycle.

Our first result is a bijection between isometry classes of generic orthoschemes and points of
\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}.
This result is inspired by Coxeter’s spectacular work
[
Cox36
]
, but our construction seems to be new. It is based on an algebraic approach to the Maslov index from an unpublished work by Kashiwara, see also
[
LV80
]
.

Consider a configuration
(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})\in\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}
of points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}=\mathbb{P}(V)
and let
l_{0},\dots,l_{m+1}
be lines in
V
corresponding to points
x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}.
Let
\omega\in\Lambda^{2}V^{\vee}
be a symplectic form. The vector space

(6.4)

E=\textup{Ker}\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m+1}l_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sum}}{{\longrightarrow}}V\right)

has dimension
m
and carries a non-degenerate quadratic form defined on a vector

v=(v_{0},\dots,v_{m+1})\in E

by the formula

q(v)=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m+1}\omega(v_{i},v_{j}).

We denote by
q(v_{1},v_{2})
the symmetric bilinear form, associated to
q.

Example 6.6
.

Consider the case
m=1.
Fix nonzero vectors
e_{0}\in l_{0},e_{1}\in l_{1},e_{2}\in l_{2}.
The vector space
E
is spanned by the vector

v=\left(\omega(e_{1},e_{2})e_{0},\omega(e_{2},e_{0})e_{1},\omega(e_{0},e_{1})e_{2}\right).

Then

(6.5)

q(v,v)=\omega(e_{1},e_{2})\omega(e_{2},e_{0})\omega(e_{0},e_{1}).

For any subset
I=\{i_{0},i_{1}\dots,i_{r+1}\}\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,m+1\}
we define a vector space

E_{I}=\textup{Ker}\left(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{r+1}l_{i_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sum}}{{\longrightarrow}}V\right),

which is a subspace of
E.
As a quadratic space it is isometric to the space obtained from the configuration
(x_{i_{0}},\dots,x_{i_{r+1}})\in\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}
by the same construction as above.

Lemma 6.7
.

For
0\leq i<j\leq m+1
consider subsets
I=\{0,\dots,i,j,\dots,m+1\}
and
I^{\prime}=\{i,i+1,\dots,j\}
of
\{0,\dots,m+1\}.
Then we have an orthogonal decomposition
E=E_{I}\oplus E_{I^{\prime}}.

Proof.

We have
\textup{dim}(E)=\textup{dim}(E_{I})+\textup{dim}(E_{I^{\prime}})
and
E_{I}\cap E_{I^{\prime}}=0,
so
E=E_{I}\oplus E_{I^{\prime}}.
Also, consider
v\in E_{I}
and
v^{\prime}\in E_{I^{\prime}}
such that

v=(v_{0},\dots,v_{m+1}),v^{\prime}=(v_{0}^{\prime},\dots,v_{m+1}^{\prime})\in E.
Then we have
v_{r}=0
for
i<r<j
and
v_{r}^{\prime}=0
for
r<i
and for
r>j.
We have

(6.6)

\begin{split}&q(v,v^{\prime})=\sum_{r_{1}<r_{2}}\omega(v_{r_{1}},v_{r_{2}}^{\prime})=\sum_{r_{1}\leq r_{2}}\omega(v_{r_{1}},v_{r_{2}}^{\prime})=\sum_{r_{1}\leq i\leq r_{2}\leq j}\omega(v_{r_{1}},v_{r_{2}}^{\prime})\\
&=\omega\left(\sum_{r_{1}\leq i}v_{r_{1}},\sum_{i\leq r_{2}\leq j}v_{r_{2}}^{\prime}\right)=\omega\left(\sum_{r_{1}\leq i}v_{r_{1}},0\right)=0.\end{split}

This implies that
E_{I}
and
E_{I^{\prime}}
are orthogonal subspaces of
E,
so the decomposition
E=E_{I}\oplus E_{I^{\prime}}
is orthogonal.
∎

Theorem 6.8
.

For a configuration
(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})
consider a projective simplex

\textup{ort}(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{m+1})

in
\mathbb{P}(E),
where
Q
is the quadric defined by a quadratic form
q
and
H_{i}=\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i-1,i,i+1}^{\perp}\right)
for
1\leq i\leq m.
Then
\textup{ort}(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})
is a generic projective orthoscheme. This gives a bijection between points of
\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}
and isometry classes of generic projective orthoschemes.

Proof.

To prove that
S=\textup{ort}(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})
is an orthoscheme first notice that by Lemma
6.7
we have

E_{i-1,i,i+1}^{\perp}=E_{\{0,\dots,i-1,i+1,\dots,m+1\}}.

From here it is obvious that for
|i-j|>1
we have
E_{j-1,j,j+1}\subseteq E_{i-1,i,i+1}^{\perp},
so
S
is an orthoscheme. By the same argument,
h_{0}=E_{0,1,m+1}^{\perp}
and
h_{m+1}=E_{0,m,m+1}^{\perp}.

It is easy to see that

(6.7)

\bigcap_{r\notin\{i,j\}}h_{r}=0

for any
0\leq i\neq j\leq m+1.
Indeed, by Lemma
6.7
if
v=(v_{0},\dots,v_{m+1})
is a vector in
\bigcap_{r\notin\{i,j\}}h_{r}
then we have an equality
v_{r}=0
for any
r\notin\{i,j\}.
Since
\sum_{r=0}^{m+1}v_{r}=0,
we conclude that
v_{i}+v_{j}=0,
which contradicts our assumption that the lines
l_{0},\dots,l_{m+1}
are distinct. The formula (
6.7
) implies that
S
is generic.

Next, we give a construction of the map in the other direction. For a generic orthoscheme

S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{m})

in
\mathbb{P}(E)
we consider the sum of projection maps

(6.8)

p\colon E\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m+1}(E/h_{i}).

Since
H_{0},\dots,H_{m+1}
are in general position,
p
is injective and images of the lines
E/h_{i}
in the space
\textup{Coker}(p)
are distinct. We define
\textup{conf}(S)
to be the configuration of these lines in
\mathbb{P}^{1}=\mathbb{P}(\textup{Coker}(p)).

To see that
\textup{conf}(\textup{ort}(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1}))=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})
notice that for a configuration
(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})
we have an exact sequence

(6.9)

0\longrightarrow E\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m+1}l_{i}\longrightarrow V\longrightarrow 0.

By Lemma
6.7
we have a canonical isomorphism
{l_{i}\cong E_{i-1,i,i+1}\cong E/h_{i}}
for
0\leq i\leq m+1.
Moreover, it is easy to see that the embedding
E\hookrightarrow\oplus_{i=0}^{m+1}l_{i}
is identified with the sum of projections (
6.8
).

Finally, we need to show that the generic orthoschemes

S=(Q;H_{1},\dots,H_{m})

and

{\textup{ort}(\textup{conf}(S))=(Q^{\prime};H_{1}^{\prime},\dots,H_{m}^{\prime})}

are isometric. By the same argument as above, the configurations of hyperplanes
H_{0},\dots,H_{m+1}
and
H_{0}^{\prime},\dots,H_{m+1}^{\prime}
are projectively equivalent. The fact that
Q
is uniquely determined by the hyperplanes
H_{0},\dots,H_{m+1}
can be easily checked in coordinates and is left to the reader.
∎

The faces and the angles of a generic orthoscheme are joins of generic orthoschemes.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
S=\textup{ort}(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})
be a generic orthoscheme. For a subset
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
we define
i_{0}=0,i_{r+1}=m+1.
Then we have

(6.10)

S_{I}=\textup{ort}(x_{i_{0}},x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r+1}}),

and

(6.11)

S^{I}=\prod_{p=0}^{r}\textup{ort}(x_{i_{p}},x_{i_{p}+1},\dots,x_{i_{p+1}-1},x_{i_{p+1}}).

Proof.

Let
J=I\cup\{0,m+1\}\subseteq\{0,\dots,m+1\}
and
J_{p}=\{i_{p},i_{p}+1,\dots,i_{p+1}-1,i_{p+1}\}
for
0\leq p\leq r.
Applying Lemma
6.7
consequently we deduce that we have an orthogonal decomposition

E=E_{J}\oplus\bigoplus_{p=0}^{r}E_{J_{p}}.

From here, the statement can be deduced easily; we leave the details to the reader.
∎

Corollary 6.10
.

A generic orthoscheme is a nondegenerate projective simplex.

Proof.

For a generic orthoscheme
S=(Q,H_{1},\dots,H_{m})
the quadric
Q
is smooth, and every face
S_{I}
is a generic orthoscheme, so quadrics
H_{I}\cap Q
are also smooth.
∎

Theorem
6.8
and Proposition
6.9
imply Theorem
1.4
.

6.3.
Hyperbolic orthoschemes

In this section, we discuss hyperbolic orthoschemes, i.e., projective orthoschemes coming from hyperbolic geometry.

Definition 6.11
.

The hyperbolic locus
\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}^{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}
is the connected component of the set of real points of the variety
\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}
consisting of configurations projectively equivalent to

x=(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})

with
x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1}\in\mathbb{R}
and

(6.12)

x_{m+1}<x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{m-1}<x_{m}<x_{0}.

Proposition 6.12
.

For
x\in\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}^{h}
the orthoscheme
\textup{ort}(x)
is the projectivization of a hyperbolic orthoscheme. This gives a bijection between points of
\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}^{h}
and isometry classes of hyperbolic orthoschemes.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathfrak{M}_{0,m+2}^{h}
the vector space
V
is a complexification of a real vector space. Also, the quadratic space
E
defined by (
6.4
) is a complexification of a real quadratic space. In both cases, we denote the corresponding real vector spaces by the same letters. We choose the symplectic form
\omega\in\Lambda^{2}V^{\vee}
to be the usual area form. Denote the index of a quadratic space
E
by
\textup{Ind}(E).
Since index is additive, Lemma
6.7
implies that

\textup{Ind}(E)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\textup{Ind}(E_{0,i,i+1}).

From the condition (
6.12
) and the formula (
6.5
) we see that

\textup{Ind}(E_{0,i,i+1})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if\ }i=m,\\
-1&\text{if\ }i\neq m,\\
\end{cases}

so
\textup{Ind}(E)=m-2
and
q
has signature
(1,m-1).
The hyperboloid
\{v\in E\mid q(v)=1\}
has two connected components and we identify one of them with
\mathbb{H}^{m-1}.
Since

\textup{Ind}(E_{0,i,m+1})=1\text{\ for \ }1\leq i\leq m,

we can define a hyperbolic tetrahedron with vertices

A_{i}=E_{0,i,m+1}\cap\mathbb{H}^{m-1}

for
1\leq i\leq m.
In this way we get a hyperbolic orthoscheme with projectivization
\textup{ort}(x).

To prove the implication in the other direction, consider a projective orthoscheme
S
, obtained from a hyperbolic orthoscheme by projectivization. The orthoscheme
S
is generic, because hyperplanes
H_{0},H_{m+1}
defined by (
6.3
) do not intersect
\mathbb{H}^{m-1}.
The map
(\ref{FormulaSumOfProjections})
is a complexification of the corresponding map of real vector spaces, so the configuration
\textup{conf}(S)
is equivalent to a configuration
(x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1})
with
x_{0},\dots,x_{m+1}\in\mathbb{R}.
Without loss of generality,
x_{m+1}<x_{0}.
Since
\textup{ort}(\textup{conf}(S))
is isometric to
S
by Theorem
6.8
, we know that

\textup{Ind}(E_{0,i,m+1})=1\text{\ for \ }1\leq i\leq m,

so
x_{m+1}<x_{i}<x_{0}
for
1\leq i\leq m.
On the other hand, for any
1\leq i<j\leq m
the quadratic space
E_{0,i,j,m+1}
must have signature
(1,1),
so

\textup{Ind}(E_{0,i,j})=\textup{Ind}(E_{0,i,j,m+1})-\textup{Ind}(E_{0,j,m+1})=-1\text{\ for \ }1\leq i\leq m,

thus
x_{i}<x_{j}
by (
6.5
).
∎

6.4.
Orientations of orthoschemes

Let

\mathrm{P}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{2n+1})

be an alternating polygon. The variety
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}
has an étale covering
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}.
This covering can be characterized by the fact that for an alternating subpolygon
\mathrm{P}^{\prime}
of
\mathrm{P}
the square root of the cross-ratio
\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P}^{\prime})}
is a regular function on
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}.

By Theorem
6.8
we have a generic orthoscheme
\textup{ort}(x)
for every
x\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}};
let
Q_{x}
be the corresponding quadric. Since
Q_{x}
has two orientations, we have an action of the spherical pure braid group
\pi_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}})
on the set with two elements. Consider the corresponding cohomology class

\textup{Or}_{\mathrm{P}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\right).

For an alternating subpolygon
\mathrm{P}^{\prime}
of
\mathrm{P}
we have the forgetful morphism
f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}^{\prime}}\colon\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}^{\prime}}.

Lemma 6.13
.

For every decomposition
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{P}_{1}\sqcup P_{2}
the following equality holds:

\textup{Or}_{\mathrm{P}}=(f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}_{1}})^{*}\textup{Or}_{\mathrm{P}_{1}}+(f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}_{2}})^{*}\textup{Or}_{\mathrm{P}_{2}}.

Proof.

The statement follows immediately from the following observations. By Lemma
6.7
for a point
x\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}
the quadratic space
E_{x}
defined by (
6.4
) is the orthogonal sum of the corresponding spaces
E_{f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}_{1}}(x)}
and
E_{f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}_{2}}(x)}.
It is easy to see that a choice of orientations of quadrics
Q_{f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}_{1}}(x)}
and
Q_{f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}_{1}}(x)}
defines a choice of an orientation of the quadric
Q_{x},
from where the statement can be easily deduced.
∎

Definition 6.14
.

For an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}
consider a subgroup

\bigcap_{\mathrm{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{P}}\textup{Ker}(\textup{Or}_{\mathrm{P}^{\prime}})\subseteq\pi_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}).

We denote by
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}
the corresponding étale cover of
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}.

Remark 6.15
.

The group
\bigcap_{\mathrm{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{P}}\textup{Ker}(\textup{Or}_{\mathrm{P}^{\prime}})
contains the level two congruence subgroup of the pure spherical braid group, so the volume of an orthoscheme is a function on the pro-unipotent completion of this group. It would be interesting to interpret this function in the language of
[
KM19
]
.

By Definition
6.14
in order to define an orientation of
\textup{ort}(x)
for each point
x^{s}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}
over
x\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}
it is sufficient to fix a choice of an orientation of
\textup{ort}(x_{0})
for just one “base point”
x_{0}^{s}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}.
The hyperbolic locus
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}
is simply connected, and hyperbolic simplices have a canonical orientation, so any point of
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{h}
can be taken as the base point. If
\mathrm{P}
is odd, we define
\textup{ort}_{P}(x^{s})
as the scissors congruence class of the oriented orthoscheme
\textup{ort}(x^{s})
with the canonical orientation of the hyperbolic orthoscheme. If
\mathrm{P}
is even, we use the orientation opposite to the canonical.

Our next goal is to compute the “Dehn invariant” of an oriented orthoscheme, namely the coproduct
\Delta^{\mathcal{G}}[\textup{ort}(x^{s})]
in
\mathcal{G}.
Recall that we have defined a set of alternating subpolygons
\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})
in §
4.3
.

Proposition 6.16
.

For an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}
the following equality holds

\Delta^{\mathcal{G}}\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{\mathrm{S}\in\textup{alt}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\left(\prod_{i=0}^{2r}\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{S}^{i}}\right).

Proof.

By (
6.1
), we have

\Delta^{\mathcal{G}}\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,\dots,2n\}\\
|I|\text{ is even}\end{subarray}}[(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})_{I}]\otimes\left[(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})^{I}\right].

It is easy to see that
\left[(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})^{I}\right]=0
if
I
is not alternating. Indeed,
by Proposition
6.9
, if
I
is even and not alternating, then
\left[(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})^{I}\right]
is a product of classes of orthoschemes, among which at least two are even-dimensional. The product of classes of even-dimensional simplices vanishes, see
[
Gon99
, Lemma 3.10]
. From here the statement follows.
∎

6.5.
Alternating polylogarithms

For
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
fix the choice of square roots
\sqrt{a_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{a_{n}}\in\mathbb{C}.
An alternating polylogarithm is a framed mixed Hodge structure defined by the following formula:

\begin{split}&\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=\\
&\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\in\{-1,1\}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}\right)\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\epsilon_{1}\sqrt{a_{1}},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\sqrt{a_{k}}).\end{split}

Similar functions appeared in
[
Cha21
, Definition 3.12]
under the name
multiple
t
-polylogarithms
.

Example 6.17
.

Here are some examples in weights
1
and
2:

\begin{split}&\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(a_{1})=\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(\sqrt{a_{1}})-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(-\sqrt{a_{1}}))=\frac{1}{2}\log^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{a_{1}}}{1-\sqrt{a_{1}}}\right);\\
&\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{2}(a_{1})=\frac{1}{2}(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{2}(\sqrt{a_{1}})-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{2}(-\sqrt{a_{1}}));\\
&\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(a_{1},a_{2})\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\left(\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(\sqrt{a_{1}},\sqrt{a_{2}})-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(-\sqrt{a_{1}},\sqrt{a_{2}})-\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(\sqrt{a_{1}},-\sqrt{a_{2}})+\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(-\sqrt{a_{1}},-\sqrt{a_{2}})\right).\\
\end{split}

Assume that
S
is an irreducible algebraic variety over
\mathbb{C}.
For
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}\in\mathbb{C}(S)^{\times}
consider a covering
\widetilde{S}
of
S
such that
\sqrt{\varphi_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{\varphi_{k}}
are regular on
\widetilde{S}.
We define

\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\varphi_{2},n_{2}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}]=\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k})\in\mathcal{H}_{\widetilde{S}}.

Proposition 6.18
.

The coproduct of alternating polylogarithms can be computed by the following formula:

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}([\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}])\\
&=\sum_{s=0}^{k}\left((\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{s},n_{s}]\otimes 1)\left((1\otimes\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\varphi_{s+1},n_{s+1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}]\right)\right).\end{split}

Proof.

It is sufficient to show that

(6.13)

\begin{split}&(1\otimes\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}})\left(\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\in\{-1,1\}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}\right)\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\epsilon_{1}\sqrt{\varphi_{1}},n_{1}|\dots|\epsilon_{k}\sqrt{\varphi_{k}},n_{k}]\right)\\
&=(1\otimes\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}})\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}[\varphi_{1},n_{1}|\dots|\varphi_{k},n_{k}];\\
\end{split}

after that the statement would follow from (
3.20
).

For
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k})
consider a sequence

\begin{split}&x^{\epsilon}=(x_{0}^{\epsilon},\dots,x_{n+1}^{\epsilon})\\
&=(0,1,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\epsilon_{1}\sqrt{\varphi_{1}}}_{n_{1}},\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\sqrt{\varphi_{1}}\sqrt{\varphi_{2}}}_{n_{2}},\dots,\underbrace{0,\dots,0,\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\dots\epsilon_{k}\sqrt{\varphi_{1}}\sqrt{\varphi_{2}}\dots\sqrt{\varphi_{k}}}_{n_{k}}).\end{split}

By (
3.5
) we have

\begin{split}&\Delta^{\mathcal{HF}}[\epsilon_{1}\sqrt{\varphi_{1}},n_{1}|\dots|\epsilon_{k}\sqrt{\varphi_{k}},n_{k}]\\
&=(-1)^{l(x^{\epsilon})-l(x_{I}^{\epsilon})}\sum_{I=(i_{0},\dots,i_{r+1})}\left(\prod_{p=1}^{r}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{i_{p}}^{\epsilon};x_{i_{p}+1}^{\epsilon},\dots,x_{i_{p+1}-1}^{\epsilon};x_{i_{p+1}^{\epsilon}})\right)\otimes x_{I}^{\epsilon}\end{split}

where the summation goes over all sequences
0=i_{0}<i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}<i_{r+1}=n+1
with
i_{1}=1.

From (
2.9
) it follows that for any sequence
I
we have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\in\{-1,1\}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}\right)\left(\prod_{p=1}^{r}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(x_{i_{p}}^{\epsilon};x_{i_{p}+1}^{\epsilon},\dots,x_{i_{p+1}-1}^{\epsilon};x_{i_{p+1}}^{\epsilon}\right)\right)\otimes\textup{Li}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{I}^{\epsilon})\\
&=\left(\prod_{p=1}^{r}\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(x_{i_{p}}^{2};x_{i_{p}+1}^{2},\dots,x_{i_{p+1}-1}^{2};x_{i_{p+1}}^{2}\right)\right)\otimes\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{I}^{\epsilon}),\end{split}

which implies (
6.13
).
∎

Example 6.19
.

We have

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mathcal{H}}\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(a_{1},a_{2})

\displaystyle=1\otimes\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(a_{1},a_{2})+\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(a_{1})\otimes\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(a_{2})+\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(a_{1},a_{2})\otimes 1

\displaystyle\quad-\textup{I}^{\mathcal{H}}(1,a_{1},a_{1}a_{2})\otimes\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(a_{1}a_{2}).

Corollary 6.20
.

Alternating polylogarithms satisfy the quasi-shuffle relation

\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(x\star y)=\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(x)\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(y)

for
x,y\in\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{S}}.

Proof.

The statement follows from Proposition
6.18
, see the proof of Proposition

.

6.6.
Volumes of orthoscheme

Our goal in this section is to give an explicit formula for the unipotent variation of framed mixed Hodge-Tate structures
h(\textup{ort}_{P})
over
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}
in terms of alternating polylogarithms. For that, we first construct a collection of functions on
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s},
which are square roots of cross-ratios.

We start with the case
n=1.
For an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}=(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
consider a point
x^{s}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}
over

x=(x_{p_{0}},x_{p_{1}},x_{p_{2}},x_{p_{3}})\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}.

Then
\textup{ort}(x^{s})=(Q;H_{1},H_{2}),
where
Q
is a quadric of dimension zero, thus a pair of points
Q=\{Q_{1},Q_{2}\}
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}.
Since
Q
is oriented, the pair
\{Q_{1},Q_{2}\}
is ordered. It follows from the proof of Theorem
6.8

that the configuration
(x_{p_{0}},x_{p_{1}},x_{p_{2}},x_{p_{3}})
is projectively equivalent to the configuration
(H_{0},H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}),
where
H_{0}=H_{2}^{\perp}
and
H_{3}=H_{1}^{\perp}.
A direct computation shows that

[x_{p_{0}},x_{p_{1}},x_{p_{2}},x_{p_{3}}]=[H_{0},H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}]=\left(\frac{[Q_{1},H_{1},Q_{2},H_{2}]+1}{[Q_{1},H_{1},Q_{2},H_{2}]-1}\right)^{2}.

We put

(6.14)

\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{[Q_{1},H_{1},Q_{2},H_{2}]+1}{[Q_{1},H_{1},Q_{2},H_{2}]-1}&\text{\ if\ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is even, }\\
\\
\dfrac{[Q_{2},H_{1},Q_{1},H_{2}]-1}{[Q_{2},H_{1},Q_{1},H_{2}]+1}&\text{\ if\ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is odd}.\end{cases}

Lemma 6.21
.

Consider an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}.
The product

\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{Q}_{i})}

does not depend on the choice of a quadrangulation
Q=\{\mathrm{Q}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{Q}_{n}\}\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathrm{P}).

Proof.

Since
\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})
does not depend on the choice of a quadrangulation, the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{Q}_{i})}
can depend on the quadrangulation only up to a sign. Thus, it is enough to check the statement for any particular oriented orthoscheme, e.g., hyperbolic. Assume that
\mathrm{P}
is odd, in which case the orthoscheme has the canonical orientation. By (
6.14
)
\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{Q}_{i})}
is real and positive if
\mathrm{Q}_{i}
corresponds to the edge length. Similarly,
\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{Q}_{i})}
equals to
\lambda i
for positive
\lambda
if
\mathrm{Q}_{i}
corresponds to an angle. The statement follows because every quadrangulation has one quadrangle corresponding to an edge of the orthoscheme and
n-1
.quadrangles corresponding to angles.
∎

For an alternating polygon
\mathrm{P}
we put
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}
to be
\mathcal{H}_{S}
for
S=\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}.
Since square roots of cross-ratios are regular functions on
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s},
we have an element

\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}.

Theorem 6.22
.

We have the following equality of framed variations on
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}:

(6.15)

h(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s})=\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}).

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
n.
We start with the case
n=1.

Consider a point

x^{s}=(x_{p_{0}},x_{p_{1}},x_{p_{2}},x_{p_{3}})\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}.

From (
6.14
) we have an equality

[Q_{1},H_{1},Q_{2},H_{2}]=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})}+1}{\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})}-1}&\text{\ if\ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is even, }\\
\\
\dfrac{1-\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})}}{1+\sqrt{\textup{cr}(\mathrm{P})}}&\text{\ if\ }\mathrm{P}\text{\ is odd}.\end{cases}

Recall that by Example
6.2
we have an equality

h[\textup{ort}(x^{s})]=-\frac{1}{2}\log^{\mathcal{H}}\left([Q_{1},H_{1},Q_{2},H_{2}]\right).

For an even polygon
\mathrm{P}=(0,1,2,3)

\begin{split}&\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=-\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}([(0,1,2,3),1])=-\frac{1}{2}\log^{\mathcal{H}}\frac{1+\sqrt{\textup{cr}(0,1,2,3)}}{1-\sqrt{\textup{cr}(0,1,2,3)}},\\
\end{split}

so
h(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})=\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}).
For an odd polygon
\mathrm{P}=(0,1,2,3)
we have

\begin{split}&\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}_{1}([(1,2,3,4),1])\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\log^{\mathcal{H}}\frac{1+\sqrt{\textup{cr}(1,2,3,4)}}{1-\sqrt{\textup{cr}(1,2,3,4)}}=-\frac{1}{2}\log^{\mathcal{H}}\frac{1-\sqrt{\textup{cr}(1,2,3,4)}}{1+\sqrt{\textup{cr}(1,2,3,4)}},\\
\end{split}

so again
h(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})=\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}).
This finishes the proof for
n=1.

Next, assume that
n>1.
From Theorem
4.14
we know that
\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=0.
Thus Proposition
6.18
and Corollary
6.20
imply that

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=(\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}})\Delta^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{S\in\textup{Alt}(\mathrm{P})}\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}})\otimes\left(\prod_{i=0}^{2r}\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{S}_{i}})\right).

Comparing it with Proposition
6.16
we see that by induction we have

\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})=\Delta^{\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}}h(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}}),

so the variation
\textup{ALi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{T}_{\mathrm{P}})-h(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})
is constant on
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{P}}^{s}.
On the divisor
x_{p_{0}}=x_{p_{1}}
both sides equal to zero. Indeed, this is obvious for alternating polylogarithms. For
h(\textup{ort}_{\mathrm{P}})
it follows from the fact that in this specialization the quadric
Q
becomes singular, which can be easily deduced from (
6.5
). This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Applying the real period map (
6.2
) to (
6.15
) we obtain Theorem
1.5
.
