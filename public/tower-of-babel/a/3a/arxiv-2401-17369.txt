Construction of diagonal quintic threefolds with infinitely many rational points
Maciej Ulas

Abstract
. In this note we present a construction of an infinite family of diagonal quintic threefolds defined over ℚ \mathbb{Q} each containing infinitely many rational points. As an application, we prove that there are infinitely many quadruples B = ( B 0 , B 1 , B 2 , B 3 ) B=(B_{0},B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}) of co-prime integers such that for a suitable chosen integer b b (depending on B B ), the equation B 0 ​ X 0 5 + B 1 ​ X 1 5 + B 2 ​ X 2 5 + B 3 ​ X 3 5 = b B_{0}X_{0}^{5}+B_{1}X_{1}^{5}+B_{2}X_{2}^{5}+B_{3}X_{3}^{5}=b has infinitely many positive integer solutions.

11D41, 13P15

1.
Introduction

Let
A=(A_{0},A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},A_{4})\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
be given and assume that
\gcd(A)=1
. We consider the diagonal quintic form in five variables given by

F_{A}(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})=\sum_{i=0}^{4}A_{i}X_{i}^{5},

and a related diagonal quintic threefold defined as

\mathcal{V}_{A}:\;F_{A}(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})=0.

The set of integer points on the variety
\mathcal{V}_{A}
is denoted by
\mathcal{V}_{A}(\mathbb{Z})
. We say that a point
(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})\in\mathcal{V}_{A}(\mathbb{Z})
is non-trivial if the greatest common divisor of the entries
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}
is equal to 1, and no proper sub-sum in the expression
\sum_{i=0}^{4}A_{i}X_{i}^{5}
vanish. In the sequel, by an integer point on
\mathcal{V}_{A}
we will mean a non-trivial one.

The question concerning the existence of integer points, or equivalently rational points, on
\mathcal{V}_{A}
is very difficult. In particular, if
I=(1,1,1,1,1)
, the variety
\mathcal{V}_{I}
is called the Fermat quintic and it is an open question whether
\mathcal{V}_{A}(\mathbb{Z})
is infinite. Euler conjectured that there are no non-trivial points in
\mathcal{V}_{I}(\mathbb{Z})
. However, in 1966, Lander and Parkin in
[
4
]
found that
(27,84,110,133,-144)\in\mathcal{V}_{A}
, that is the following equality holds

27^{5}+84^{5}+110^{5}+133^{5}=144^{5}.

The computer search was extended by others and two additional points were find on
\mathcal{V}_{I}
. In 1997 Scher and Seidl found the point
(5027,6237,14068,-220,-14132)
. The other one
(55,3183,28969,85282,-85359)
was found by Frey. In fact, according to our best knowledge, there is no known example of a vector
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
such that
\mathcal{V}_{A}
contains infinitely many non-trivial integer points and this motivated us to work on this problem. Note that
\mathcal{V}_{A}\simeq\mathcal{V}_{I}
over
\mathbb{C}
. The geometry of
\mathcal{V}_{I}
was investigated in many papers. In particular, the geometry of lines on
\mathcal{V}_{I}
was investigated by Albano and Katz
[
1
]
. The question concerning the existence of (complex) conics on
\mathcal{V}_{I}
was the subject of PhD dissertation of Xu
[
9
]
, some experimental work of Testa
[
7
]
and recent work of A. M. Mustaţǎ
[
5
]
. In this context let us recall that a generic quintic threefold contains 2875 lines and 609,250 conics. However, the findings of the mentioned authors do not shed light on the question concerning non-emptiness of
\mathcal{V}_{A}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Let us describe the content of the paper in some details. In Section
2
we quickly eliminate the possibility of the existence of
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
with nonzero entries, such that the variety
\mathcal{V}_{A}
contains a line defined over
\mathbb{Q}
giving infinitely many nontrivial rational points. In Section
3
we present construction of a quadratic parametric solution of the equation defining the variety
\mathcal{V}_{A}
for suitable chosen values of
A
. As an immediate application we get lower bound for the number of those
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
satisfying
\gcd(A)=1
and such that
\mathcal{V}_{A}
has infinitely many integer points. More precisely, the number
\mathcal{P}(N)
, of such
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
with
\operatorname{max}(|A|)\leq N
and
\gcd(A)=1
, satisfies
\mathcal{P}(N)\gg N^{1/10}
. Finally, in the last section we prove that there are infinitely many quadruples
B=(B_{0},B_{1},B_{2},B_{3})
of co-prime integers such that for a suitable chosen integer
b
(depending on
B
), the equation
B_{0}X_{0}^{5}+B_{1}X_{1}^{5}+B_{2}X_{2}^{5}+B_{3}X_{3}^{5}=b
has infinitely many positive integer solutions.

In our work we extensively used symbolic calculations, especially Gröbner bases computations. All Gröbner bases were computed with the help of Mathematica 13.2 computational package
[
8
]
running on a standard machine with i7 type processor and 32 GB of RAM.

2.
Warm up

The first thing which comes to mind is to find linear polynomials and an appropriate
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
such that on
\mathcal{V}_{A}
there is a line defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. In other words, for variables
u,v
, we take
X_{i}=a_{i}u+b_{i}v
for
i=0,\ldots,4
and look for rational numbers
a_{i},b_{i}
such that

\sum_{i=0}^{4}A_{i}(a_{i}u+b_{i}v)^{5}=0

in the ring
\mathbb{Q}[u,v]
. In other words, we have a line lying on
\mathcal{V}_{A}
. Because we are interested in the existence of nontrivial points, there are
i,j\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
such that
i\neq j
and linear forms
a_{i}u+b_{i}v,a_{j}u+b_{j}v
are not proportional. Thus, after linear change of variables, without loss of generality, we can assume that
i=0,j=1
, and
a_{0}=1,b_{0}=0,a_{1}=0,b_{1}=1
. Let
C_{i}
be the coefficient of
u^{i}v^{5-i}
in the expansion of
\sum_{i=0}^{4}A_{i}(a_{i}u+b_{i}v)^{5}
and consider the ideal
J
generated by the polynomials
C_{i},i=0,1,\ldots,5
. Moreover, let
\operatorname{Gb}(J)
be a Gröbner basis of the ideal
J
. Then, we have that

\operatorname{Gb}(J)\cap\mathbb{Q}[a_{4},b_{4},A_{0},A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},A_{4}]=\{a_{4}^{2}b_{4}^{3}A_{0}A_{1}A_{4},a_{4}^{3}b_{4}^{2}A_{0}A_{1}A_{4}\},

and quick computation reveals that any solution of the system
C_{i}=0,i=0,\ldots,5
, leads to lines which generate only trivial points on
\mathcal{V}_{A}
. Let us note that essentially the same result can be obtained by using
[
1
, Proposition 1.3]
, which says that if
L
is a line contained in
\mathcal{V}_{A},A=(1,1,1,1,1)
, then
L

goes through a point with 3 coordinates equal to 0.

In any way, the failure of the above approach suggests that if we want to construct parametric solution of the equation defining
\mathcal{V}_{A}
, or equivalently, a rational curve lying on
\mathcal{V}_{A}
, we need to look for parametric solutions given by polynomials of degrees
>1
.

3.
The construction of a quadratic parametrization

In this section we will find one parameter family of 5-tuples
A
such that
\mathcal{V}_{A}
contains the curve defined by quadratic forms. It is tempting to work with the general form of such a curve, i.e., try to find values
A\in\mathbb{Z}_{5}
and
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
X_{i}(u,v)=a_{i}u^{2}+b_{i}uv+c_{i}v^{2},i=0,\ldots,4
, satisfy the identity

0=\sum_{i=0}^{4}A_{i}(a_{i}u^{2}+b_{i}uv+c_{i}v^{2})^{5}=\sum_{j=0}^{10}D_{j}u^{10-j}v^{j},

in the ring
\mathbb{Q}[u,v]
. Treating
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},A_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,4,
as independent variables, we have that
D_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[A_{0},\ldots,A_{4},a_{0},\ldots,c_{4}]
. Thus, we are interested in finding rational solutions of the system

\mathcal{S}:\;D_{j}=0,\quad j=0,1,\ldots,10.

To be more specific note that we are playing with a system involving 20 variables and 11 homogeneous polynomials. A naive count
20-11-1=8
suggests that the dimension of the variety defined by the system
\mathcal{S}
is 8 and the expectation that
\mathcal{S}
has solutions in rational numbers is reasonable. However, the resulting system is too complicated to be fully investigated. Although we were trying quite hard, we were unable to solve
\mathcal{S}
without any additional assumptions on the shape of the form
X_{i}(u,v)
. Thus, instead of working with the general quadratic forms, we decided to work with forms

\displaystyle X_{0}=s_{0}(u,v)=uv,

\displaystyle X_{1}=s_{1}(u,v)=u^{2}-auv+v^{2},

\displaystyle X_{2}=s_{2}(u,v)=u^{2}+auv+v^{2},

\displaystyle X_{3}=s_{3}(u,v)=u^{2}+buv+cv^{2},

\displaystyle X_{4}=s_{4}(u,v)=u^{2}+duv+ev^{2},

where
a,b,c,d,e\in\mathbb{Q}
need to be determined. Thus, let
C_{i}
be the
i
-th coefficient in the expansion

\sum_{i=0}^{4}A_{i}s_{i}(u,v)^{5}=\sum_{i=0}^{10}C_{i}u^{i}v^{10-i}.

We are interested in the rational solutions of the system

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:\;C_{i}=0,\quad i=0,1,\ldots,10,

and, without loss of generality, we can assume that
A_{4}=1
.

Let us observe that the variable
A_{0}
appears only in one polynomial, i.e., in
C_{5}
, and with the exponent 1. That means that it is enough to find solutions of the system
C_{i}=0
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,10\}\setminus\{5\}
, and then compute the value of
A_{0}
from
C_{5}=0
. We are thus working with the polynomials lying in the ring
\mathbb{Q}[A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},a,b,c,d,e]
.

Let
I=<C_{0},\ldots,C_{4},C_{6},\ldots,C_{10}>
denote the ideal generated by the polynomials
C_{i}
for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,10\}\setminus\{5\}
. One can compute the Gröbner basis
\operatorname{Gb}(I)
of the ideal
I
. It contains 32 polynomials. The computation of the full basis took around 13 minutes. However, for our purposes it is better to work with the basis containing polynomials in variables
A_{3},e
only. We have that

\operatorname{Gb}(I)\cap\mathbb{Z}[A_{3},e]=\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\},

where

F_{1}=\left(A_{3}+1\right)(e-1)^{6}\left(e^{2}+e+1\right)\left(3e^{2}+2e+3\right).

Because
A_{3},e
need to be rational we have that
(A_{3}+1)(e-1)=0
. First, we consider the case
A_{3}+1=0
. Adding the polynomial
A_{3}+1
to the ideal
I
we get the ideal
I^{\prime}
. The Gröbner basis
\operatorname{Gb}(I^{\prime})
of the ideal
I^{\prime}
takes the form:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gb}(I^{\prime})=\{

\displaystyle c-e,(b-d)(e^{4}-1)(b-d),(b-d)(e^{2}-1)(d^{2}+2e),b^{2}-d^{2},

\displaystyle(b-d)(a^{2}-d^{2}-2e+2),A_{3}+1,(b-d)(e^{2}-1)(ad-2A_{2}),

\displaystyle(b-d)(ad+A_{2}d^{2}+2eA_{2}-2A_{2}),2aA_{2}-b+d,A_{1}+A_{2}\}.

It is clear that the necessary condition for the existence of rational solutions of the system
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is the existence of rational solutions of the system, say
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
, defined by the vanishing of each entry from
\operatorname{Gb}(I^{\prime})
. Simple computation with the Mathematica procedure
Solve
reveals that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
has only one, up to sign changes and permutations, solution which may be useful. This solution has the following form

\begin{array}[]{llll}a=-\sqrt{d^{2}-4},&b=-d,&c=-1,&e=-1,\\
A_{0}=64d(d^{2}-2),&A_{1}=\frac{d}{\sqrt{d^{2}-4}},&A_{2}=-A_{1},&A_{3}=-1.\end{array}

There are also other solutions but they are defined over nontrivial algebraic extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
or lead to non interesting equalities
s_{1}(u,v)=s_{2}(u,v),s_{3}(u,v)=s_{4}(u,v)
. To make the expression
a=-\sqrt{d^{2}-4}
rational one can use the substitution
d=(-1-t^{2})/t
, where
t
is a rational parameter. Thus, after necessary simplifications we obtain
A(t)=(A_{0}(t),A_{1}(t),A_{2}(t),A_{3}(t),A_{4}(t))
, where

A_{0}(t)=64t^{2}(1-t^{8}),\quad A_{1}(t)=-A_{2}(t)=t^{2}+1,\quad A_{3}(t)=-A_{4}(t)=t^{2}-1

and note that on the quintic threefold
\mathcal{V}_{A(t)}
there is a rational curve defined by the quadratic forms

(1)

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{0}&=&s_{0}(u,v)=uv,\\
X_{1}&=&s_{1}(u,v)=tu^{2}+(t^{2}-1)uv+tv^{2},\\
X_{2}&=&s_{2}(u,v)=tu^{2}-(t^{2}-1)uv+tv^{2},\\
X_{3}&=&s_{3}(u,v)=tu^{2}-(t^{2}+1)uv-tv^{2},\\
X_{4}&=&s_{4}(u,v)=tu^{2}+(t^{2}+1)uv-tv^{2}.\end{array}\end{cases}

We are left with the case
e-1=0
. We apply exactly the same procedure as in the case
A_{3}+1=0
and find that there are no solutions which lead to nontrivial identities. Summing up, we thus proved

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
and put
A(t)=(A_{0}(t),\ldots,A_{4}(t))
. The variety
\mathcal{V}_{A(t)}
contains infinitely many non-trivial integer points.

Remark 3.2
.

In this context one can recall that there are quadratic parametrizations living on the diagonal surface
X_{0}^{k}+X_{1}^{k}=X_{2}^{k}+X_{3}^{k}
, for
k=5,6,7
. However, these parametrizations are defined over non-trivial algebraic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
(see the discussion in
[
2
]
and the work of Reznick concerning linear relations between powers of binary quadratic forms
[
6
]
).

We define the following counting function

\mathcal{P}(N)=\#\{A=(A_{0},\ldots,A_{4}):\;\operatorname{max}\{|A_{i}|\}\leq N,\gcd(A)=1,\;\mbox{and}\;\mathcal{V}_{A}(\mathbb{Z})\;\mbox{is infinite}\}.

Our findings allow us to deduce the following

Theorem 3.3
.

We have
\mathcal{P}(N)\gg N^{1/10}
.

Proof.

For a given
t\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
we take
A(t)=(A_{0}(t),\ldots,A_{4}(t))
and observe that
\gcd(A_{0}(t),\ldots,A_{4}(t))=1
for even values of
t
. If
t
is odd, we have that
\gcd(A_{0}(t),\ldots,A_{4}(t))=2
. We clearly have
\operatorname{max}\{|A_{i}(t)|:\;i=0,1,2,3,4\}=64t^{2}(t^{8}-1)
. Thus the number of
A
’s such that
\operatorname{max}\{|A_{i}|:\;i=0,1,2,3,4\}\leq N
and
\mathcal{V}_{A}
has infinitely many non-trivial integer points is not smaller than the number of solutions of the inequality
64t^{2}(t^{8}-1)\leq N
, i.e., we have at least
t\gg N^{1/10}
positive integer values satisfying required properties. We thus have
\mathcal{P}(N)\gg N^{1/10}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Let us observe that the solutions of
F_{t}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{4})=0
given by
X_{i}=s_{i}(u,v),i=0,\ldots,4
, we already constructed, satisfy two additional equations, i.e.,

L(X_{0},\ldots,X_{4})=Q(X_{0},\ldots,X_{4})=0,

where

L=4X_{0}+X_{1}-X_{2}+X_{3}-X_{4},\quad Q=X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}-X_{3}^{2}-X_{4}^{2}.

It is interesting to note that the forms
L,Q
do not depend on
t
. Thus, for given
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
, it is natural to define the variety

\mathcal{C}_{A}:\;F_{A}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{4})=L(X_{0},\ldots,X_{4})=Q(X_{0},\ldots,X_{4})=0.

One can check that for a generic choice of the vector
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
, the variety
\mathcal{C}_{A}
is a curve of genus
>1
. However, as we have seen the genus of
\mathcal{C}_{A}
can drop to 0. From Faltings theorem (see
[
3
]
) we know that the necessary condition for
\mathcal{C}_{A}
to have infinitely many rational points is when the genus is
\leq 1
.
We thus formulate the following

Problem 3.5
.

Characterize all 5-tuples of integers
A=(A_{0},\ldots,A_{4})
such that the curve
\mathcal{C}_{A}
has genus
\leq 1
.

4.
An application

In this section we present a curious application of our construction of solutions of the equation defining
\mathcal{V}_{A(t)}
. More precisely, we construct infinitely many diagonal quintic forms
G_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
, in four variables, and such that the equation
G_{t}(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=R(t)
has infinitely many integer solutions for suitable chosen integer
R(t)
.

Before we go on, let us recall the following well know property of Pell type equation
V^{2}-dU^{2}=a
, where
a\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
is not a square. If
(u,v)
, where
uv\neq 0
, is an integer solution of this equation, and
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is the fundamental solution of the Pell equation
Y^{2}-dX^{2}=1
, then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, the pair
(U_{n},V_{n})
defined by the equality

V_{n}+U_{n}\sqrt{d}=(v+u\sqrt{d})(Y_{0}+X_{0}\sqrt{d})^{n}

is a solution of the equation
V^{2}-dU^{2}=a
. In particular, the existence of one solution
(u,v),uv\neq 0
, of the equation
V^{2}-dU^{2}=a
implies the existence of infinitely many solutions.

Lemma 4.1
.

The equation

(2)

s_{3}(u,v)=s_{3}(1,1)=-t^{2}-1,

where
s_{3}(u,v)
is given in (
1
), has infinitely many solutions
u,v
in the ring
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{2},t\right]
.

Proof.

Let us note the equation (
2
) can be written in the following form

(3)

V^{2}-(t^{4}+6t^{2}+1)U^{2}=4t(t^{2}+1),

where
V=2tv+(1+t^{2})u,U=u
. Let us write
P(t)=t^{4}+6t^{2}+1
. Thus, we consider Pell type equation and look for solutions
U,V
satisfying congruence condition
V\equiv(1+t^{2})U\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu2t)
.

Let us note that (
3
) has a non trivial solution
(V,U)=((t+1)^{2},1)
. Moreover, the Pell equation

Y^{2}-P(t)X^{2}=1

has the fundamental solution

Y_{0}=Y_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{4}(t^{2}+1)(t^{2}+5),\quad X_{0}=X_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{4}(t^{2}+3).

As a consequence, we get that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the pair
(V_{n},U_{n})
defined by

V_{n}+U_{n}\sqrt{P(t)}=\left((t+1)^{2}+\sqrt{P(t)}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{4}(t^{2}+1)(t^{2}+5)+\frac{1}{4}(t^{2}+3)\sqrt{P(t)}\right),

is a solution of (
3
). Next, to obtain solutions of (
2
) we need to know that
(V_{n}\equiv(1+t^{2})U_{n})/2t\in\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{2},t\right]
. However, a simple induction on
n
confirms that this is the case. Finally, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the pair
(v_{n},u_{n})
, where

(4)

u_{n}=U_{n},\quad v_{n}=\frac{1}{2t}(V_{n}-(1+t^{2})U_{n}),

is a solution of (
2
). To see an example we take
n=1
and get

u_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(t^{4}+t^{3}+5t^{2}+3t+4),\quad v_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(t^{3}+t^{2}+3t+1).

For
n=2
we get

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(t^{8}+t^{7}+11t^{6}+9t^{5}+39t^{4}+23t^{3}+49t^{2}+15t+16\right),

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(t^{7}+t^{6}+9t^{5}+7t^{4}+23t^{3}+11t^{2}+15t+1\right).

∎

Remark 4.2
.

Using standard methods it is not difficult to prove that the following continued fraction expansion

\sqrt{(2t+1)^{4}+6(2t+1)^{2}+1}=[4t^{2}+4t+3;\overline{1,t^{2}+t-1,1,2(4t^{2}+4t+3)}]

holds. Thus, if we replace
t
by
2t+1
in (
2
), then the resulting equation has infinitely many solutions in the ring
\mathbb{Z}[t]
.

We are ready to prove the following.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G_{t}(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=(t^{2}+1)(X_{0}^{5}-X_{1}^{5})+(1-t^{2})X_{2}^{5}+64t^{2}(1-t^{8})X_{3}^{5}
. For each
t\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu2)
, the Diophantine equation

G_{t}(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=(t^{2}-1)(t^{2}+1)^{5}

has infinitely many solutions in integers.

Proof.

To get the proof it is enough to use the polynomials
u_{n},v_{n}
given by (
4
). Indeed, we know that
s_{3}(u_{n},v_{n})=-t^{2}-1
. Thus, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
we can take

X_{0,n}=s_{1}(u_{n},v_{n}),X_{1,n}=s_{2}(u_{n},v_{n}),X_{2,n}=s_{4}(u_{n},v_{n}),X_{3,n}=s_{0}(u_{n},v_{n})

and observe that
G_{t}(X_{0,n},X_{1,n},X_{2,n},X_{3,n})=(t^{2}-1)(t^{2}+1)^{5}
.
∎

For
B=(B_{0},B_{1},B_{2},B_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
let us put

G_{B}(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{4})=B_{0}X_{0}^{5}+B_{1}X_{1}^{5}+B_{2}X_{2}^{5}+B_{3}X_{3}^{5}

and define the counting function

C_{b,B}(N)=\#\{(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})\in\mathbb{N}:\;|X_{i}|\leq N\;\mbox{and}\;G_{B}(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=b\}.

From Theorem
4.3
we immediately get the following.

Corollary 4.4
.

There are infinitely many
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
satisfying
\gcd(B)=1
and such that there is an integer
b
depending on
B
, such that

C_{b,B}(N)\gg\log N.

Proof.

For
t\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
take
B=(t^{2}+1,-t^{2}-1,1-t^{2},64t^{2}(1-t^{8}))
and
b=(t^{2}-1)(t^{2}+1)^{5}
.
∎

Example 4.5
.

We take
t=3
. From Theorem
4.3
, we know that the equation

(5)

5X_{0}^{5}-5X_{1}^{5}-4X_{2}^{5}-1889280X_{3}^{5}=2^{7}5^{5}

has infinitely many solutions in positive integers. Using the formulas for
X_{i,n},i=0,1,2,3
, from the proof of Theorem
4.3
, one can compute small solutions of our equation. The solutions corresponding to
n=2,3
are

(37526,6982,38170,1909),\quad(183773534,34226638,186933610,9346681).

Note that the obtained family of integer solutions does not cover all integer solutions of (
5
). Indeed, due to reducibility of the quintic form
5(X_{0}^{5}-X_{1}^{5})
, one can find all integer solutions of (
5
) satisfying the condition
\operatorname{max}\{|X_{2}|,|X_{3}|\}\leq 10^{3}.
In this range we have the solutions

\displaystyle(-166,-38,-170,-8),(38,166,-170,-8),(2,-14,10,1),\;(14,-2,10,1),

\displaystyle(-10,-90,90,5),\;(90,10,90,5),(-94,-542,550,28),\;(542,94,550,28).

Remark 4.6
.

According to our best knowledge, in the above result we have the first explicit example of a diagonal quintic form
F
in four variables and such that there is an integer
b
for which the Diophantine equation
F(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=b
has infinitely many integer solutions satisfying the condition
X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}\neq 0
.

Coefficients

are given by:

\displaystyle C_{0}

\displaystyle=A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}+1,

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=-aA_{1}+aA_{2}+A_{3}b+d,

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=(2a^{2}+1)A_{1}+2a^{2}A_{2}+(2b^{2}+c)A_{3}+2d^{2}+e,

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=-a(a^{2}+2)A_{1}+a(a^{2}+2)A_{2}+b(b^{2}+c)A_{3}+d^{3}+2de,

\displaystyle C_{5}

\displaystyle=A_{0}+a(a^{4}+20a^{2}+30)(A_{2}-A_{1})

\displaystyle\quad+b(b^{4}+20b^{2}c+30c^{2})A_{3}+d(d^{4}+20d^{2}e+30e^{2}),

\displaystyle C_{6}

\displaystyle=(a^{4}+6a^{2}+2)(A_{1}+A_{2})+c(b^{4}+6b^{2}c+2c^{2})A_{3}+e(d^{4}+6d^{2}e+2e^{2}),

\displaystyle C_{7}

The computation of the Gröbner basis
\operatorname{Gb}(I)
of the ideal

.
