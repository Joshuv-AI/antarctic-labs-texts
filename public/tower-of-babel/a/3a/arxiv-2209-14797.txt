A non-linear second-order difference equation related to Gibbs measures of a SOS model
U.A. Rozikov

Abstract
. For the SOS (solid-on-solid) model with an external field and with spin values from the set of all integers on a Cayley tree each (gradient) Gibbs measure corresponds to a boundary law (an infinite-dimensional vector function defined on vertices of the Cayley tree) satisfying a non-linear functional equation. Recently some translation-invariant and height-periodic (non-normalisable) solutions to the equation are found. Here our aim is to find non-height-periodic and non-normalisable boundary laws for the SOS model. By such a solution one can construct a non-probability Gibbs measure. We find explicitly several non-normalisable boundary laws. Moreover, we reduce the problem to solving of a non-linear, second-order difference equation. We give analytic and numerical analysis of the difference equation.

These vectors satisfy a non-linear equation, which is equivalent to a compatibility condition of finite-dimensional Gibbs distributions.

By the non-linearity, infinite-dimensionality and tree-indexness of the equation its analysis is very complicated. In above mentioned papers, for a class of Hamiltonians, this equation mainly solved in class of translation-invariant (i.e. the vectors do not depend on vertices of the tree) and height-periodic vectors (i.e. the coordinates of an infinite-dimensional vector is periodic).

This paper is devoted to Gibbs measures of a SOS model with a countable set of spin values on Cayley trees.
Our aim is to find non-periodic and non-normalisable boundary laws for the SOS model and give corresponding to them non-probability Gibbs measures. First we find explicitly several non-normalisable boundary laws and then reduce the problem to solving of a non-linear, second-order difference equation. We give analytic and numerical analysis of the difference equation.

1.1.
Definitions and useful facts

We consider SOS model, where spin-configuration
\omega
is a function from the
vertices of the Cayley tree
\Gamma^{k}=(V,\vec{L})
(of order

) to the set
\mathbb{Z}
of integer numbers, where

is the set of oriented edges (bonds) of the tree
(see
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
for properties of the Cayley tree and theory of Gibbs measures for models with finitely many spin values on trees).

For any configuration
\omega=(\omega(x))_{x\in V}\in\mathbb{Z}^{V}
and edge
b=\langle x,y\rangle
of
\Gamma^{k}

the
difference
along the edge

is given by
\nabla\omega_{b}=\omega(y)-\omega(x)
, where
\omega_{b}
is a configuration on

, i.e.,
\omega_{b}=\{\omega(x),\omega(y)\}
. The configuration
\nabla\omega
is called the
gradient field
of

(see
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
).

The gradient spin variables are now defined by
\eta_{\langle x,y\rangle}=\omega(y)-\omega(x)
for each
\langle x,y\rangle
.

The space of
gradient configurations
is denoted by
\Omega^{\nabla}
. The measurable structure on the space
\Omega^{\nabla}
is given by
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{F}^{\nabla}:=\sigma(\{\eta_{b}\,|\,b\in\vec{L}\}).

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}^{\nabla}
be the sigma-algebra of gradient configurations outside of the finite volume
\Lambda
is generated by all gradient variables outside of
\Lambda
and the relative height-difference on the boundary of
\Lambda
.

For
\omega:x\in V\mapsto\omega(x)\in\mathbb{Z}
, consider Hamiltonian of SOS model with external field
\Phi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

(1.1)

H(\omega)=-J\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle:\atop x,y\in V}|\omega(x)-\omega(y)|+\sum_{x\in V}\Phi(\omega(x)),

where
J>0
.

For
b=\langle x,y\rangle
and
\omega_{b}=\{\omega(x),\omega(y)\}
define transfer operator

(1.2)

Q_{b}(\omega_{b})=\exp\left(-\beta J|\omega(x)-\omega(y)|+{1\over k+1}(\Phi(\omega(x))+\Phi(\omega(y)))\right),

and introduce the Markov (Gibbsian) specification as

\gamma_{\Lambda}^{\Phi}(\sigma_{\Lambda}=\omega_{\Lambda}|\omega)=(Z_{\Lambda}^{\Phi})(\omega)^{-1}\prod_{b\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset}Q_{b}(\omega_{b}).

If external field
\Phi(\cdot)\equiv 0
then for any bond
b=\langle x,y\rangle
the transfer operator
Q_{b}(\omega_{b})
is
a function of gradient spin variable
\zeta_{b}=\omega(y)-\omega(x)
, i.e., is
a
gradient interaction potential
.

Boundary law
(see
[
10
]
,
[
28
]
) which allow to describe the subset

of all Gibbs measures is defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

•

A family of vectors
\{l_{xy}\}_{\langle x,y\rangle\in\vec{L}}

l_{xy}=\left(l_{xy}(i):i\in\mathbb{Z}\right)\in(0,\infty)^{\mathbb{Z}}

there exists a constant

such that the consistency equation

l_{xy}(i)=c_{xy}\prod_{z\in\partial x\setminus\{y\}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{zx}(i,j)l_{zx}(j)

holds for every

, where

•

A boundary law

is said to be normalisable if and only if

(1.4)

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(\prod_{z\in\partial x}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{zx}(i,j)l_{zx}(j)\Big)<\infty

at any

.

•

A boundary law is called

-height-periodic (or

l_{xy}(i+q)=l_{xy}(i)

for every oriented edge

and each

.

Definition 2
.

The gradient Gibbs specification is defined as the family of probability kernels
\left(\gamma_{\Lambda}^{\prime}\right)_{\Lambda\Subset V}
from
\left(\Omega^{\nabla},\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}^{\nabla}\right)
to
\left(\Omega^{\nabla},\mathcal{F}^{\nabla}\right)
such that

\int F(\rho)\gamma_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(d\rho\mid\zeta)=\int F(\nabla\varphi)\gamma_{\Lambda}(d\varphi\mid\omega)

for all bounded

-measurable functions

\omega\in\Omega
is any height-configuration with
\nabla\omega=\zeta
.

Definition 3
.

A probability measure

\Omega^{\nabla}
is called a Gradient Gibbs Measure (GGM) if it satisfies the

equation

\int\nu(d\zeta)F(\zeta)=\int\nu(d\zeta)\int\gamma_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(d\tilde{\zeta}\mid\zeta)F(\tilde{\zeta})

for every finite

and for all bounded functions

\Omega^{\nabla}
.

1.2.
The boundary law equation for the SOS model with an external field.

In this paper we are interested to the translation-invariant boundary laws of the SOS model with external field, which are infinite-dimensional vectors of the form

3.1.
Fixed points of (
3.9
)

Fixed points are solutions to the following system of equations

(3.11)

\begin{array}[]{ll}x=(y_{0}+x_{1}-\tau)hx^{k}+\tau x-y\\[5.69054pt]
y=x.\end{array}

It is easy to see that this equation has two solutions:

{\rm Fix}(F)=\{P_{0},P_{1}\}
, with

(3.12)

P_{0}=(0,0),P_{1}=(x^{*},x^{*}),\ \ \mbox{where}\ \ x^{*}=\left({\tau-2\over h(\tau-y_{0}-x_{1})}\right)^{{1\over k-1}}.

Now we shall examine the type of the fixed points.

Definition 4
.

(see
[
3
]
). A fixed point
v
of an operator
M
is called hyperbolic if
its Jacobian
J
at
v
has no eigenvalues on the unit circle.

A hyperbolic fixed point
v
is called:

•

attracting if all the eigenvalues of the Jacobi matrix
J(v)
are less than 1 in
absolute value;

•

repelling if all the eigenvalues of the Jacobi matrix
J(v)
are greater than 1 in
absolute value;

•

a saddle point otherwise.

To find the type of a fixed point of the operator (
3.9
) we write
the Jacobi matrix:

J(x,y)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(y_{0}+x_{1}-\tau)hkx^{k-1}+\tau&-1\\[5.69054pt]
1&0\end{array}\right).

Note that both fixed points are saddle (or non-hyperbolic), because the equation for eigenvalues is

\lambda^{2}-((y_{0}+x_{1}-\tau)hkx^{k-1}+\tau)\lambda+1=0

solutions of which satisfy
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}=1
.
The eigenvalues for
P_{0}
are:

\lambda_{1,2}={1\over 2}\left(\tau\mp\sqrt{\tau^{2}-4}\right),

and for
P_{1}
are:

\lambda_{1,2}={1\over 2}\left(2k-(k-1)\tau\mp\sqrt{(k-1)(\tau-2)[(k-1)\tau-2(k+1)]}\right).

Therefore we obtain the following proposition

Proposition 3
.

For
k\geq 2
and
\tau>2
the following assertions hold

1.

Eigenvalues corresponding to the fixed point
P_{0}
are real and
0<\lambda_{1}<1
,
\lambda_{2}>1
.

2.

For the eigenvalues corresponding to
P_{1}
the following hold

2.1.

If
2<\tau<{2(k+1)\over k-1}
then the eigenvalues are complex numbers, and (surprisingly)
|\lambda_{1,2}|=1
independently on values of the parameters
\tau
and
k
.

2.2.

If
\tau={2(k+1)\over k-1}
then

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=-1
.

2.3.

If
\tau>{2(k+1)\over k-1}
then both eigenvalues are real and
|\lambda_{1}|<1
,
|\lambda_{2}|>1
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the formulas of
\lambda_{1,2}
given above. Let us give proof of the item 2.1. Under condition of this item the eigenvalues are complex:

\lambda_{1,2}={1\over 2}\left(2k-(k-1)\tau\mp i\sqrt{(k-1)(\tau-2)[2(k+1)-(k-1)\tau]}\right).

Therefore, simple computations show that

|\lambda_{1,2}|^{2}={1\over 4}\left((2k-(k-1)\tau)^{2}+\left((k-1)(\tau-2)[2(k+1)-(k-1)\tau]\right)^{2}\right)=1,

independently on parameters
\tau
and
k
satisfying the condition of this item.
∎

Thus
P_{0}
is always saddle. But
P_{1}
is saddle if
\tau>{2(k+1)\over k-1}
and non-hyperbolic if
2<\tau\leq{2(k+1)\over k-1}
.

From the known theorem about stable and unstable manifolds (see
[
3
]
)
we get the following result

Theorem 2
.

For the saddle fixed points
P_{i}
the following assertions hold

a.

There are curves
denoted by
W^{s}(P_{0})
,
W^{s}(P_{1})
such that for any initial
vector
v\in{\displaystyle W^{s}(P_{i})}
one has
F(v)\in{\displaystyle W^{s}(P_{i})}
(invariance) and

\lim_{n\to\infty}F^{n}(v)=P_{i},\,i=0,1.

b.

There are curves denoted by
W^{u}(P_{0})

(resp.
W^{u}(P_{1})
) and a neighborhood
V(P_{i})
of
P_{i}
such that for any initial vector
v\in W^{u}(P_{i})\cap V(P_{i})
, there exists
k=k(v)\in\mathbb{N}
that
F^{k}(v)\notin V(P_{i})
,
i=0,1
.

The curves
W^{s}(P_{i})
are known as stable and
W^{u}(P_{i})
as unstable manifolds (see
[
8
]
).

Open problem:
Find explicit formula of the curves
W^{s}(P_{i})
,
W^{u}(P_{i})
.

3.2.
An invariant set

For given parameters and fixed
x_{1}
,
y_{0}
we introduce

\psi(x)=(y_{0}+x_{1}-\tau)hx^{k}+\tau x,

a=\left({\tau-1\over h(\tau-y_{0}-x_{1})}\right)^{1/(k-1)}.

Proposition 4
.

If
2<\tau\leq 1+{k^{k/(k-1)}\over k-1}
then the set

I=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:0\leq x\leq a,\ \ \max\{0,\psi(x)-a\}\leq y\leq\psi(x)\},

is invariant with respect to operator
F
given in (
3.9
), i.e.,
F(I)\subset I
.

Proof.

We take an arbitrary
(x,y)\in I
and show that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=F(x,y)\in I
. Since
y_{0}+x_{1}<\tau
the function
\psi(x)
is monotone increasing in
(0,\hat{x})
, (where
\hat{x}=\hat{x}_{0}\sqrt[k-1]{1/k}
,
\hat{x}_{0}=\sqrt[k-1]{\tau\over h(\tau-y_{0}-x_{1})}
) and decreasing in
(\hat{x},+\infty)
. Note that
\hat{x}_{0}>\hat{x}
,
\psi(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,\hat{x}_{0})
,
\psi(\hat{x}_{0})=0
and
\psi(x)<0
for all
x>\hat{x}_{0}
.

We have
a<\hat{x}_{0}
and therefore
\psi(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,a)
. Using these inequalities we obtain

0\leq x^{\prime}\leq a\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ 0\leq\psi(x)-y\leq a\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ (\mbox{since}\ \ y\geq 0)\ \ \max\{0,\psi(x)-a\}\leq y\leq\psi(x).

For the second coordinate we have

\max\{0,\psi(x)-a\}\leq y^{\prime}\leq\psi(x)\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \max\{0,\psi(x)-a\}\leq x\leq\psi(x)\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\psi(x)-x\geq 0\\[5.69054pt]
\psi(x)-a-x\leq 0\\[5.69054pt]
\psi(x)-a\geq 0\end{array}\right.\ \ \mbox{or}\ \ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\psi(x)-x\geq 0\\[5.69054pt]
x\geq 0\\[5.69054pt]
\psi(x)-a\leq 0\end{array}\right.

Note that
\psi(x)-x\geq 0
iff
x\in[0,a]
. Moreover,
if
2<\tau\leq 1+{k^{k/(k-1)}\over k-1}
then

\max_{x\in[0,a]}(\psi(x)-x)=\psi(x_{*})-x_{*}=(\tau-1){k-1\over k}x_{*}<a,

where

x_{*}=\left({\tau-1\over hk(\tau-y_{0}-x_{1})}\right)^{1/(k-1)}.

Hence,
\psi(x)-a-x\leq 0
, and the proof is completed.
∎

3.3.
Inverse of the operator (
3.9
)

Recall the following definition. Let

f\colon X\to X
,
g\colon Y\to Y
, and
\ell\colon Y\to X
are continuous functions on topological spaces,
X
and
Y
.

The mapping
f
is called topologically semiconjugate to
g
if
\ell
is a surjection such that
f\circ\ell=\ell\circ g
.

f
and
g
are topologically conjugate if they are topologically semiconjugate and
\ell
is a homeomorphism.

Proposition 5
.

Operator (
3.9
) is invertible and its inverse
is

(3.13)

F^{-1}:\ \ \begin{array}[]{ll}x^{\prime}=y\\[5.69054pt]
y^{\prime}=(y_{0}+x_{1}-\tau)hy^{k}+\tau y-x.\end{array}

Moreover, the operator
F
is topological conjugate to its inverse.

Proof.

To find inverse of the operator (
3.9
) from the second equation of the operator one can find
x=y^{\prime}
and then putting it in the first equation one gets

y=(y_{0}+x_{1}-\tau)h(y^{\prime})^{k}+\tau y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}.

Thus we get
F^{-1}
. To see that
F
and
F^{-1}
are conjugate
take mapping
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by

\ell(x,y)=(y,x)
, i.e., permutation of the coordinates. Note that
\ell^{-1}=\ell
.
Then it is easy to see that

F(x,y)=\ell\circ F^{-1}\circ\ell(x,y).

∎
