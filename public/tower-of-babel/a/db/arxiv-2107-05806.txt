On regular ∗ -algebras of bounded linear operators: A new approach towards a theory of noncommutative Boolean algebras Thanks: The author is supported by RIKEN Special Postdoctoral Researcher Program.
Michiya Mori

Abstract
. We study (von Neumann) regular ∗ -subalgebras of B ⁡ ( H ) B(H) , which we call R ∗ -algebras. The class of R ∗ -algebras coincides with that of “E ∗ -algebras that are pre-C ∗ -algebras” in the sense of Z. Szűcs and B. Takács. We give examples, properties and questions of R ∗ -algebras. We observe that the class of unital commutative R ∗ -algebras has a canonical one-to-one correspondence with the class of Boolean algebras. This motivates the study of R ∗ -algebras as that of noncommutative Boolean algebras. We explain that seemingly unrelated topics of functional analysis, like AF C ∗ -algebras and incomplete inner product spaces, naturally arise in the investigation of R ∗ -algebras. We obtain a number of results on R ∗ -algebras by applying various famous theorems in the literature.

1.
Introduction

Throughout the paper,
H
is a complex Hilbert space, and
B(H)
denotes the von Neumann algebra of bounded linear operators on
H
.
A motivation for our research comes from a problem raised by Kadison.
In the celebrated conference in Baton Rouge in 1967, Kadison proposed a list of 20 problems on operator algebras.
The list was shared among operator algebraists worldwide, which brought a great influence to the development of the theory of operator algebras over the last half-century.
In 2003, Ge published a survey article
[
23
]
on Kadison’s list of problems.
Among the 20 problems, 19 are about C
∗
- or von Neumann algebras.
The only exception is listed as the 15th problem in Ge’s article, which asks the following.

Problem 1.1

(Kadison)
.

If a self-adjoint operator algebra is finitely generated (algebraically) and each self-adjoint operator in it has finite spectrum, is it finite-dimensional?

In the recent paper
[
48
]
, Szűcs and Takács discovered that for a
∗
-subalgebra
A
of
B(H)
several conditions are equivalent to the condition every self-adjoint element of
A
has finite spectrum.
In this paper, we first observe in Theorem
2.1
that these conditions are also equivalent to the condition
A
is regular in the sense of von Neumann.
We call a
∗
-subalgebra of
B(H)
that satisfies these equivalent conditions an R
∗
-algebra (“R” meaning “regular”).
The abundance of the conditions for a
∗
-subalgebra of
B(H)
to be an R
∗
-algebra implies that R
∗
-algebras form more or less an important class of
∗
-algebras.

Even though we assume no completeness, it turns out that R
∗
-algebras share some important properties with C
∗
-/von Neumann algebras.
For example, we may freely use the technique of functional calculus.
Recall that there exists a correspondence between commutative C
∗
-algebras and locally compact Hausdorff spaces (Gelfand–Naimark theorem), and similarly, between commutative von Neumann algebras and certain measure spaces.
Is there such a correspondence for R
∗
-algebras?
By von Neumann’s study of
∗
-regular rings in
[
43
, Part II]
, projections of a general R
∗
-algebra form a lattice.
It follows that the lattice of projections of a commutative R
∗
-algebra is a generalized Boolean algebra.
In fact, there exists a complete correspondence between the class of commutative R
∗
-algebras and that of generalized Boolean algebras.
By restricting ourselves to the unital case, we see that unital commutative R
∗
-algebras correspond to Boolean algebras.
In that sense (the projection lattice of) an R
∗
-algebra can be considered as a noncommutative (generalized) Boolean algebra.
This correspondence is closely tied with Stone’s theorem on the duality between Boolean algebras and Boolean spaces.

Are there plenty of noncommutative R
∗
-algebras?
The answer is yes.
The class of ultramatricial R
∗
-algebras, that is, directed unions of finite-dimensional C
∗
-algebras, provides us with many examples of noncommutative R
∗
-algebras.
It turns out that Problem
1.1
, which remains to be open, is equivalent to asking whether every R
∗
-algebra is ultramatricial.
Ultramatricial R
∗
-algebras with countable Hamel dimension are highly relevant to the theory of separable AF C
∗
-algebras, whose classification by Elliott
[
19
]
is one of the best-known results of the theory of C
∗
-algebras.
Another subclass of ultramatricial R
∗
-algebras, which we call purely atomic R
∗
-algebras, is constructed from inner product spaces.
We give a complete classification of purely atomic R
∗
-algebras in terms of linear isometries of inner product spaces.
By applying Banach space theory and results on operator ranges, we prove that there are many mutually nonisomorphic purely atomic R
∗
-algebras.

The literature of regular rings, Boolean algebras, and inner product spaces in connection with Banach spaces, as well as C
∗
-/von Neumann algebras provides us with many deep results and further questions.
The paper intends to collect some of them in order to give supporting evidence for the author’s belief that the study of R
∗
-algebras is interesting enough to be considered in more depth.
This paper should give much motivation to the research on Problem
1.1
, which seems to have gotten less attention among operator algebraists compared to other problems in the list
[
23
]
.
The choice of materials in this paper is more or less biased by the author’s preference.

This paper is formulated in the following manner.
In the next section, we give the definition and basic properties of R
∗
-algebras.
In particular, we characterize R
∗
-algebras by closedness of the ranges of operators, the uniqueness of C
∗
-norm of
∗
-subalgebras as well as von Neumann regularity (Theorems
2.1
,
2.12
).
Section
3
provides examples of R
∗
-algebras and discusses Problem
1.1
.
The correspondence between commutative R
∗
-algebras and Boolean algebras is given in Section
4
.
Section
5
is devoted to giving the relation between purely atomic R
∗
-algebras and inner product spaces.
Some results that arise by comparing the projection lattice of an R
∗
-algebra with that of a von Neumann algebra or a Baer
∗
-ring are given in Section
6
.
The consequence of Elliott’s classification theorem of AF C
∗
-algebras is summarized in Section
7
.
We also revisit Elliott’s theorem from a lattice theoretic viewpoint (Theorem
7.5
).
In Section
8
we collect results that derive from von Neumann, Dye and Gleason’s theorems.
We close this paper by proposing questions on R
∗
-algebras for further research (Section

In the paper, we frequently use basic results on C
∗
-/von Neumann algebras as in
[
31
,

, and von Neumann’s theory on complemented modular lattices and regular rings as in (the first four chapters of)

None of the proofs is dependent on any results by Szűcs and Takács in

2.
Regular
∗
-subalgebras of
B(H)

If there is no confusion, for each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we often write
z
instead of the scalar multiplication operator
z\cdot\operatorname{id}_{H}\in B(H)
.
In particular,
1
means the unit of
B(H)
.
An
algebra
means an algebra over the field of complex numbers, possibly without unit.
A subspace or a subalgebra in this paper need not be closed.
For a subset
S\subset B(H)
, the symbol
\operatorname{span}S
means the linear space spanned by
S
, that is, the smallest complex-linear subspace of
B(H)
that contains
S
.
Similarly,
{\mathcal{A}}(S)
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(S)
denote the algebra and the
∗
-algebra generated by
S
, respectively.
Namely,
{\mathcal{A}}(S)
(resp.
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(S)
) is the smallest subalgebra (resp.
∗
-subalgebra) of
B(H)
that contains
S
.
For a finite subset
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset B(H)
, we define
{\mathcal{A}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):={\mathcal{A}}(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):={\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})
.
A
projection
of
B(H)
is an operator
p\in B(H)
with
p=p^{2}=p^{*}
.

Let
x\in B(H)
.
The range of
x
is written as
\operatorname{ran}x
or
xH
, and the kernel as
\ker x
.
The symbol
\sigma(x)
stands for the spectrum of
x
, i.e.,
\sigma(x):=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid z-x\text{ is not invertible in }B(H)\}
.
Let
l(x)
and
r(x)
denote the left and right support projections of
x
.
That is,
l(x)
is the projection onto the closure of
\operatorname{ran}x
, and
r(x)
is the projection onto
(\ker x)^{\perp}
.
If in addition
x
is self-adjoint, then
s(x):=l(x)=r(x)
is called the
support
of
x
.
Recall that every
x\in B(H)
has the unique polar decomposition
x=v\lvert x\rvert
such that
\lvert x\rvert=(x^{*}x)^{1/2}
and
vv^{*}=l(x)
,
v^{*}v=r(x)
.

Although the theorem below can be obtained as a corollary of
[
48
, Theorems 3.8 and 4.1]
by Szűcs and Takács, our discovery of (or at least the emphasis on) its relation with von Neumann regularity is new.
This viewpoint motivates a deeper study on this class of algebras in connection with Boolean algebras in the subsequent sections.
For the convenience of the reader, we give a proof that is independent of
[
48
]
.
Our proof needs no background beyond the standard first course of functional analysis.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
A
be a
∗
-subalgebra of
B(H)
. The following are equivalent.

(1)

A
is regular (in the sense of von Neumann), i.e., for each
x\in A
there exists
y\in A
with
xyx=x
.

(2)

A
is algebraic, i.e., for each
x\in A
the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}(x)
generated by
x
has finite dimension.

(3)

Each operator in
A
has closed range.

(4)

Each operator in
A
has finite spectrum.

(5)

Each self-adjoint operator in
A
has closed range.

(6)

Each self-adjoint operator in
A
has finite spectrum.

Before proving it, let us first give a preliminary result.
Let
x,y\in B(H)
.
Then
y
is called a
Moore–Penrose inverse
of
x
if
y
satisfies
l(y)=r(x)
,
r(y)=l(x)
,
yx=r(x)
and
xy=l(x)
.
It is easy to see that such an operator (if there exists) is unique.

Lemma 2.2
.

For an operator
x\in B(H)
the following are equivalent.

•

The range of
x
is closed.

•

There exists
y\in B(H)
with
xyx=x
.

•

x
has a Moore–Penrose inverse.

Proof.

Let
x\in B(H)
have closed range.
Then
x
restricts to a bounded bijection
x_{0}
from
(\ker x)^{\perp}
onto
\operatorname{ran}x
, so
x_{0}
has a bounded inverse by the inverse mapping theorem.
We define
y
as the unique operator in
B(H)
with
y=x_{0}^{-1}
on
\operatorname{ran}x
and
y=0
on
(\operatorname{ran}x)^{\perp}
.
Then we have
xyx=x
by a direct calculation.
If
xyx=x
, then we have
x=xyx=xr(x)yl(x)x
, and it is easy to show that the operator
r(x)yl(x)
is the Moore–Penrose inverse of
x
.
If
x
has a Moore–Penrose inverse, the restriction of
x
as a mapping from
(\ker x)^{\perp}
into the closure of
\operatorname{ran}x
has a bounded inverse, which implies the closedness of
\operatorname{ran}x
.
∎

.

The implications
(2)\Rightarrow(4)\Rightarrow(6)

The above lemma implies
(1)\Rightarrow(3)

Recall that a self-adjoint operator

Take an accumulation point

By the spectral mapping theorem, the self-adjoint operator

(6)\Rightarrow(1)

Since

We thus have

Let

.
Take self-adjoint operators

For

If

Let

Let

Proposition 2.9

Let

Let

Let

Proof.

(1)

Theorem 2.12
.

Let

be a
∗
-subalgebra.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

A
is an R
∗
-algebra.

(2)

Every
∗
-subalgebra of

-norm.

(3)

For every positive element

the
∗
-subalgebra
{\mathcal{A}}(a)={\mathcal{A}}^{*}(a)\subset A
has a unique C

-norm.

(4)

satisfies
(E^{*})
.

Proof.

Proposition
2.11
combined with Corollary
2.3
shows
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
, and
(2)\Rightarrow(3)
is clear.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
Assume that there is a self-adjoint operator
a\in A
with infinite spectrum.
By replacing
a
with

we may assume that
a
is positive.
We prove that
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(a)
has more than one C
∗
-norm.
Let
A_{0}
denote the algebra of polynomials
P(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}t^{k}
with
n\geq 1

.
It is clear that

is a
∗
-isomorphism.
The algebra of polynomials

has more than two C

-norms, thus

has the same property.
Indeed, define e.g.
\lVert P(t)\rVert_{j}:=\sup_{\lambda\in[j-1,j]}\lvert P(\lambda)\rvert\in[0,\infty)
,

.
Then
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
are distinct C
∗
-norms of
A_{0}
.

(1)\Rightarrow(4)
Let
B\subset A
be a
∗
-subalgebra,
K_{1}
a Hilbert space,
\pi\colon B\to B(K_{1})
a
∗
-homomorphism,

a vector and assume that

is dense in

.
Corollary
2.3
combined with Proposition

implies that the mapping

It follows by the Hahn–Banach theorem (see

) that

By the Gelfand–Naimark–Segal (GNS) construction

there are a Hilbert space

, a
∗

and a vector

such that

It follows that

for every

.

(4)\Rightarrow(1)
Assume that there is a self-adjoint operator

We prove that

does not satisfy (E

), which clearly implies that

), either.
Let

.
Take a
∗
-homomorphism

.
(Note that

Assume that there are a Hilbert space

Then

∎

3.
Examples

Although the definition of R
∗
-algebra might seem somehow restrictive, we can construct various examples of R
∗
-algebras.
In this section, we give a number of examples of R
∗
-algebras, as well as means of obtaining new examples from known ones.
More examples can be found in Sections
4
and
5
.

First of all, for each
n\geq 1
, the
n\times n

full matrix algebra

{\mathbb{M}}_{n}={\mathbb{M}}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is the collection of all
n\times n
complex matrices, which is an example of a finite-dimensional C
∗
-algebra and also an R
∗
-algebra.

Example 3.1

(Finite-dimensional R
∗
-algebras)
.

Every finite-dimensional pre-C
∗
-algebra is a complete R
∗
-algebra (thus a C
∗
-algebra).
In fact, a finite-dimensional (pre-)C
∗
-algebra is the direct sum of finitely many full matrix algebras.

Non-example 3.2
.

An infinite-dimensional C
∗
-algebra is never an R
∗
-algebra.
Indeed, it is well-known that every infinite-dimensional C
∗
-algebra has an operator with infinite spectrum. See for example
[
31
, Exercise 4.6.14]
.

It follows that the intersection of C
∗
- and R
∗
-algebras consists of finite-dimensional C
∗
-algebras.

Example 3.3

(Sequence space and direct sums)
.

Let
c_{00}
denote the algebra of complex sequences with finitely many nonzero terms.
This acts on the sequential
\ell_{2}
space by termwise multiplication, and it is clear that
c_{00}
is an R
∗
-algebra in
B(\ell_{2})
.

More generally, let
I
be a set, and let
A_{i}\subset B(H_{i})
be an R
∗
-algebra for each
i\in I
.
Let
H
be the direct sum of
H_{i}
, and let
p_{i}
be the projection of
H
onto
H_{i}
.
Each operator
x\in B(H_{i})
determines an operator
p_{i}xp_{i}\in B(H)
.
Then

\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}:={\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\{p_{i}xp_{i}\mid x\in A_{i},\,\,i\in I\})\subset B(H)

forms an R
∗
-algebra.

Example 3.4

(Algebras of operators with finite rank)
.

Let
F(H)
denote the algebra of bounded linear operators with finite rank on a Hilbert space
H
.
Then
F(H)
is an R
∗
-algebra in
B(H)
.
On the other hand, the collection
{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}
of infinite matrices
(a_{ij})_{i,j\geq 1}
with finitely many nonzero entries forms an R
∗
-algebra, which acts on the sequential
\ell_{2}
space in the usual manner.
It is clear that
{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}\subset F(\ell_{2})
.
Let us observe that these are nonisomorphic.
Indeed, it is clear that
{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}
has countable Hamel dimension.
However,
F(\ell_{2})
does not.
(To prove it, consider for example the subspace of
F(\ell_{2})
that consists of the collection of operators
x
with
x^{*}(\ell_{2})\subset{\mathbb{C}}h
for a fixed unit vector
h\in\ell_{2}
.
This subspace is linearly isometric to
\ell_{2}
by the map
x\mapsto xh
.
Recall that
\ell_{2}
has uncountable Hamel dimension.)
It is worth mentioning that both of the completions of
{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}
and
F(\ell_{2})
are the C
∗
-algebra
K(\ell_{2})
of compact operators.
In Section
5
we will take a closer look at examples of this type.

Example 3.5

(Directed unions)
.

Let
I
be a directed set and
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
be an increasing net of abstract R
∗
-algebras.
Since every injective
∗
-homomorphism between R
∗
-algebras is isometric, the union
A=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}
forms an R
∗
-algebra.
We say such an
A
is
ultramatricial
provided that each
A_{i}
is finite-dimensional.
If in addition
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
is a sequence (rather than a net), then we say
A
is
sequentially ultramatricial
.

In this paper, a C
∗
-algebra that arises as the completion of an ultramatricial R
∗
-algebra is called an
AF
(approximately finite-dimensional) C
∗
-algebra.
A C
∗
-algebra that arises as the completion of a sequentially ultramatricial R
∗
-algebra is called a
sequentially AF
C
∗
-algebra.
It is well-known that a separable AF C
∗
-algebra is sequentially AF (see
[
16
, Theorem III.3.4]
).
CAUTION: In C
∗
-algebra theory it is common to consider only separable C
∗
-algebras, so an AF C
∗
-algebra often means a separable one in the literature, which is different from our choice of terminology.

Proposition 3.6

(Matrix algebras)
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra.
For
n\geq 1
, the algebra
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)=A\otimes{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
of the collection of
n\times n
matrices with entries in

forms an R
∗
-algebra.

Proof.

It is well-known that the algebraic tensor product
A\otimes{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
is a regular ring for every unital regular ring
A
.
See for example
[
43
, Theorem II.2.13]
.
If
A\subset B(H)
is a unital R
∗
-algebra, then
A\otimes{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
can be considered as a

.
Thus

forms an R

-algebra.
If

is nonunital, consider

as a subalgebra of
\widetilde{A}\otimes{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
.
∎

In
[
48
, Proof of Proposition 4.7]
, the above proposition was shown by means of the fact that the matrix algebra over an algebraic algebra is algebraic
[
1
, Theorem 9]
.

Example 3.7

(Tensor products with ultramatricial algebras)
.

Let
A
,
B
be R
∗
-algebras.
Suppose that
B
is ultramatricial.
Take a directed set
I
and an increasing net
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
of finite-dimensional R

-algebras with

.
Since a finite-dimensional R

-algebra decomposes into a direct sum of matrix algebras, the preceding proposition implies that for each

forms an R

-algebra.
Moreover, for any

we may take a canonical inclusion from

into

(as algebraic tensor products).
We define

to be the union of the increasing net
(A\otimes B_{i})_{i\in I}
.

In particular, for an R

-algebra

,
{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}(A)=A\otimes{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}
is an R

-algebra, which can be considered as the collection of

This R

-group in Section

Example 3.8

(Infinite tensor products)
.

Let
I
be a set and
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of unital ultramatricial R

-algebras.
Then the above argument implies that for each finite subset

\bigotimes_{i\in F}A_{i}
.
Moreover, if

.

Example 3.9

(Group algebras)
.

Let
G
be a discrete group and consider the group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
.
As usual, we endow
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
with the
∗
-operation that is determined by
(zg)^{*}=\overline{z}g^{-1}
,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

.
Since the study of C

plays a dominant role in the theory of operator algebras, it is natural to ask when
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
forms an R

-algebra.
Recall that
G
is said to be

if every finite subset generates a finite subgroup of

.
It is easy to see that the group algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
forms an R

-algebra if and only if

is locally finite.
Indeed, if
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
forms an R

-algebra, then it is algebraic, which plainly leads to local finiteness of

.
(For a finite subset

The converse implication is even easier:
The left regular representation gives a C

{\mathbb{C}}[G]
, and it is clear that every element in
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
is algebraic if

is locally finite.
More generally, in
[

it was proved that the convolution
∗
-algebra of complex-valued compactly supported continuous functions on a locally compact group

is an R
∗
-algebra if and only if
G
is discrete and locally finite.
Von Neumann regularity of more general group algebras (not necessarily over
{\mathbb{C}}
) is discussed in
[
36
, Problem 8]
.

3.1.
Problems of Kurosh and Kadison

In
[
48
]
, Szűcs and Takács found that Kadison’s Problem
1.1
is related to Kurosh’s problem.
Let us revisit it.
Recall that an algebra
A
is defined to be
locally finite-dimensional
if every finite subset of
A
generates a finite-dimensional subalgebra of
A
.
The classical Kurosh’s problem asks whether every algebraic algebra
A
(i.e., such that every single element of
A
generates a finite-dimensional subalgebra of
A
) is locally finite-dimensional.
This problem has a negative answer in the general setting.
However, it is known that for certain classes of algebras (e.g., the class of PI-algebras), Kurosh’s problem has a positive answer.
See Kaplansky’s list of problems
[
36
]
for references and several related topics.
We would like to consider Kurosh’s problem for the class of R
∗
-algebras.
Since an R
∗
-algebra is algebraic by Theorem
2.1
, this is equivalent to asking whether every R
∗
-algebra is locally finite-dimensional.
This problem clearly coincides with Kadison’s Problem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.10
.

An R
∗
-algebra
A
is locally finite-dimensional if and only if
A
is ultramatricial.

Proof.

Let
A
be locally finite-dimensional.
Consider the directed set
\mathcal{F}
made of all finite subsets of
A
, ordered by inclusion.
Then
A
is the union of the increasing net
({\mathcal{A}}(F\cup F^{*}))_{F\in\mathcal{F}}
of finite-dimensional R
∗
-algebras.
Conversely, let
A
be equal to the union of an increasing net
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
of finite-dimensional R
∗
-subalgebras.
For each finite subset
F\subset A
, we may find some
i\in I
such that
F\subset A_{i}
, and thus
{\mathcal{A}}(F)\subset A_{i}
is
finite-dimensional.
∎

Therefore, Problem
1.1
, as well as Kurosh’s problem for R
∗
-algebras, is equivalent to asking whether every R
∗
-algebra is ultramatricial.

4.
Lattice of projections as noncommutative Boolean algebras

Let us recall some definitions of lattice theory for beginners.
A
lattice
is a partially ordered set
{\mathcal{P}}
such that every pair
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}
has the least upper bound
p\vee q\in{\mathcal{P}}
and the greatest lower bound
p\wedge q\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
A mapping (resp. bijection)
\psi
between two lattices is called a
lattice homomorphism
(resp. a
lattice isomorphism
) if it satisfies
\psi(p\vee q)=\psi(p)\vee\psi(q)
and
\psi(p\wedge q)=\psi(p)\wedge\psi(q)
for every pair of elements
p,q
.
A lattice
{\mathcal{P}}
with greatest element
1
and least element
0
is
complemented
if for every
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
there exists
q\in{\mathcal{P}}
with
p\vee q=1
and
p\wedge q=0
. Such a
q
is called a
complement
of
p
.
A lattice
{\mathcal{P}}
is
modular
if
p\vee(q\wedge r)=(p\vee q)\wedge r
for all
p,q,r\in{\mathcal{P}}
with
p\leq r
.

An
orthocomplemented lattice
is a complemented lattice
{\mathcal{P}}
with a bijection
{\mathcal{P}}\ni p\mapsto p^{\perp}\in{\mathcal{P}}
(called the orthocomplementation) with the following properties.

•

(p^{\perp})^{\perp}=p
for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

•

p^{\perp}
is a complement of
p
for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

•

p\leq q\iff p^{\perp}\geq q^{\perp}
for all
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

An
orthoisomorphism

\psi
between two orthocomplemented lattices is a lattice isomorphism such that
\psi(p^{\perp})=\psi(p)^{\perp}
for all
p
.

A family
\emptyset\neq{\mathcal{S}}\subset 2^{X}
of subsets of a set
X
is called a
field of sets
if
A\cap B
,
A\cup B
,
A^{c}\in{\mathcal{S}}
for any
A,B\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
A field of sets forms an orthocomplemented lattice relative to the order by inclusion and the orthocomplementation by set-complement.
A
Boolean algebra
is an orthocomplemented lattice that is orthoisomorphic to a field of sets.
It is clear that each element of a Boolean algebra has a unique complement.
It follows that a lattice isomorphism between two Boolean algebras is an orthoisomorphism.
The book
[
25
]
is a gentle introduction to the theory of Boolean algebras.
A
generalized field of sets
is a family
\emptyset\neq{\mathcal{S}}\subset 2^{X}
of subsets of a set
X
such that
A\cap B
,
A\cup B
,
A\setminus B\in{\mathcal{S}}
for any
A,B\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
A lattice that is lattice isomorphic to a generalized field of sets is called a
generalized Boolean algebra
.

Recall that for a von Neumann algebra
M
the collection
{\mathcal{P}}(M)
of projections forms an orthocomplemented lattice.
It is well-known that the projection lattice of a von Neumann algebra is modular if and only if the algebra is of finite type.
Indeed, if a von Neumann algebra
M
is not finite, then
M
has
B(\ell_{2})
as a
\sigma
-weakly closed
∗
-subalgebra, but
{\mathcal{P}}(B(\ell_{2}))
is not modular by
[
30
, Solution 14]
.
The proof of modularity of
{\mathcal{P}}(M)
when
M
is of finite type can be found in e.g.
[
49
, Theorem V.1.37]
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra with unit.
By von Neumann’s theory in
[
43
, Part II]
, the collection of principal right ideals of
A
, ordered by inclusion, forms a complemented modular lattice.
Moreover, by
[
43
, Theorem II.4.5]
together with Lemma
2.4
, this lattice can be identified with the lattice of projections
{\mathcal{P}}(A)=\{p\in A\mid p=p^{2}=p^{*}\}
.
Here
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is endowed with the usual order, namely, for two projections
p,q\in B(H)
,
p\leq q
means
pH\subset qH
, or equivalently,
pq=p
.
The correspondence between principal right ideals and projections can be described in the following manner:
Each principal right ideal of
A
coincides with
pA
for a unique
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.
Observe that
p^{\perp}:=1-p
,
p\in A
, determines an orthocomplementation of
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.

Let
A
be a unital R
∗
-algebra in
B(H)
.
Then each element in
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
can be identified with its range
pH
, which is a closed subspace of the Hilbert space
H
.
The lattice structure of
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
can be written more explicitly:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
A
be a unital R
∗
-algebra in
B(H)
with
\operatorname{id}_{H}\in A
.
For
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
,

•

p\vee q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is the projection onto
pH+qH
;

•

p\wedge q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is the projection onto
pH\cap qH
; and

•

p^{\perp}\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is the projection onto the orthogonal complement
(pH)^{\perp}=\{h\in H\mid\langle h,k\rangle=0\text{ for all }k\in pH\}
.

Proof.

Let us prove the first item.
The projection
p\vee q
is determined as the smallest projection in
A
with
p\leq p\vee q
,
q\leq p\vee q
.
Therefore, it is clear that the range of
p\vee q
contains
pH+qH
.
It suffices to show that
pH+qH
is closed and the projection onto
pH+qH
belongs to
A
.
Since
A
is an R
∗
-algebra, the positive operator
p+q
has closed range by Theorem
2.1
(3).
Since
0\leq p\leq p+q
, we have
p=s(p)\leq s(p+q)
, which implies
pH\subset(p+q)H
.
Similarly, we have
qH\subset(p+q)H
.
It is clear that
(p+q)H\subset pH+qH
.
Thus we obtain
pH+qH=(p+q)H
.
Recall that
(p+q)H
is closed and the projection onto
(p+q)H
is equal to
s(p+q)\in A
.
This completes the proof of the first item.

To prove the second item, check that the projection onto
pH\cap qH
is equal to
\chi_{\{1\}}(pqp)
, which belongs to
A
by Proposition
2.6
(2).
The third item is easy.
∎

This property is very similar to a basic property of the lattice of projections of a von Neumann algebra.
However, there is a big difference: For projections
p,q
of a von Neumann algebra in
B(H)
,
p\vee q
is the projection onto the
closure
of
pH+qH
, and taking closure is inevitable.

In that respect, the lattice
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
resembles that of operator ranges, namely, the lattice formed of ranges of bounded linear operators on a fixed Hilbert space.
It is a curious fact that the sum and intersection of any pair of operator ranges are operator ranges.
However, the lattice of operator ranges is never complemented unless
H
is finite-dimensional.
In fact, an operator range has a complement if and only if it is closed.
See
[
22
]
for the proofs of these facts and a more detailed study of operator ranges.

Remark 4.2
.

In the above argument, we discussed only the case of unital R
∗
-algebras, just for simplicity.
All of these results carry over to the nonunital case after a suitable change.
For example, for a nonunital R
∗
-algebra
A
, the collection of projections forms a modular lattice that is not complemented.
(To show this, consider the unitization of
A
.)

Although the following theorem is easy to prove, it gives an enormous motivation to the research on R
∗
-algebras and their lattices of projections.
For a locally compact Hausdorff space
K
, let
C_{0}(K)
denote the commutative C
∗
-algebra of continuous complex-valued functions on
K
that vanish at infinity.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
A
is a commutative R
∗
-algebra then the lattice
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
forms a generalized Boolean algebra.
Every generalized Boolean algebra arises in this way.
Two commutative R
∗
-algebras are
∗
-isomorphic if and only if their projection lattices are isomorphic.
The commutative R
∗
-algebra
A
is unital if and only if
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is a Boolean algebra.

Proof.

It is a basic fact that the lattice of principal right ideals of a commutative regular ring forms a generalized Boolean algebra. See for example
[
43
, Theorem II.2.10]
, in which unital regular rings are considered.
However, let us give a proof of this fact for the particular case of R
∗
-algebras.
The proof below visualizes the relation among commutative R
∗
-algebras, Boolean algebras and commutative C
∗
-algebras.

Let
A
be a commutative R
∗
-algebra.
Take the completion
\widehat{A}
of
A
, which is a commutative C
∗
-algebra.
By the Gelfand–Naimark theorem there exists a locally compact Hausdorff space
K
such that
\widehat{A}\cong C_{0}(K)
.
Then each projection
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
can be identified with a projection
f_{p}
in
C_{0}(K)
, which is a continuous function with value in
\{0,1\}
, and
f_{p}
is in turn identified with a compact open subset
f_{p}^{-1}(1)\subset K
.
Set
{\mathcal{S}}:=\{f_{p}^{-1}(1)\mid p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)\}\subset 2^{K}
.
Then it follows from a quite straightforward argument that the map
p\mapsto f_{p}^{-1}(1)
is a lattice isomorphism from
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
onto
{\mathcal{S}}
, which forms a generalized field of sets.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subset 2^{X}
be a generalized field of sets.
Consider the Hilbert space
\ell_{2}(X)
, which is the space of square-summable complex-valued functions on
X
.
Then the space of functions
R({\mathcal{S}}):=\{f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}\mid\#f(X)<\infty,\,\,f^{-1}(z)\in{\mathcal{S}}\text{ for every }z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}\}
acts on
\ell_{2}(X)
by pointwise multiplication.
It is clear that
R({\mathcal{S}})
is a commutative R
∗
-algebra.
Moreover, for each
f\in{\mathcal{P}}(R({\mathcal{S}}))
, we have
f^{-1}(1)\in{\mathcal{S}}
, and this gives an identification
{\mathcal{P}}(R({\mathcal{S}}))={\mathcal{S}}
as generalized Boolean algebras.

Let
A,B
be commutative R
∗
-algebras.
It is obvious that if
A
is
∗
-isomorphic to
B
then
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is lattice isomorphic to
{\mathcal{P}}(B)
.
Assume that there is a lattice isomorphism
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
.
We concretely construct a
∗
-isomorphism from
A
onto
B
.
Let
x\in A
.
By Theorem
2.1
,
x
has finite spectrum.
Since
A
is commutative,
x
is a normal operator.
Thus there exist
n\geq 1
, complex numbers
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots z_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and projections
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
such that
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}p_{k}
.
We define
\phi(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}\psi(p_{k})
.
It is a routine exercise to show that
\phi
is well-defined (independent of the choice of
z_{k}
,
p_{k}
) and is a
∗
-isomorphism from
A
onto
B
.

It is clear that
A
is unital if and only if
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is a Boolean algebra.
∎

For a generalized Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{S}}
, let
R({\mathcal{S}})
denote the commutative R
∗
-algebra whose projection lattice is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{S}}
.
By the preceding theorem, such an R
∗
-algebra exists uniquely up to
∗
-isomorphism.

The above theorem is highly reminiscent of a similar characterization of commutative C
∗
-/von Neumann algebras.
The Gelfand–Naimark theorem tells us that the research of C
∗
-algebras is considered as “noncommutative locally compact Hausdorff spaces”.
Similarly, von Neumann algebras can be considered as “noncommutative measure spaces”.
The author believes that the theory of R
∗
-algebras can be considered as a theory of “noncommutative (generalized) Boolean algebras”.

It is known as
Stone’s duality theorem
that the category of Boolean algebras is dually equivalent to that of Boolean spaces.
Recall that a
Boolean space
(also known as a
Stone space
) is a zero-dimensional compact Hausdorff space.
The lattice of clopen subsets of a Boolean space forms a Boolean algebra, which gives one direction of the correspondence between Boolean spaces and Boolean algebras.

More generally, the category of generalized Boolean algebras is dually equivalent to that of zero-dimensional locally compact Hausdorff spaces.
A zero-dimensional locally compact Hausdorff space is also called a locally compact Boolean space, and can be characterized as a locally compact Hausdorff space such that every connected component is a singleton.
Let us revisit this correspondence from the viewpoint of R
∗
- and C
∗
-algebras.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a generalized Boolean algebra.
Then the lattice of projections of the commutative R
∗
-algebra
R({\mathcal{S}})
is
{\mathcal{S}}
.
We consider the completion
\widehat{R({\mathcal{S}})}
of
R({\mathcal{S}})
, which is a commutative C
∗
-algebra.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a generalized Boolean algebra.
Then every projection in
\widehat{R({\mathcal{S}})}
is an element of
{\mathcal{P}}(R({\mathcal{S}}))
, which can be identified with
{\mathcal{S}}
.
In other words, if one takes a locally compact Hausdorff space
K
with
\widehat{R({\mathcal{S}})}\cong C_{0}(K)
(whose existence is guaranteed by the Gelfand–Naimark theorem), then

•

K
is zero-dimensional, and

•

the generalized Boolean algebra formed of compact open subsets of

coincides with
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

Let
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(\widehat{R({\mathcal{S}})})
.
Then there exists a sequence
x_{n}\in R({\mathcal{S}})
such that
x_{n}\to p
(in norm).
Regard
p
and
x_{n}
as functions in
C_{0}(K)
.
Then
p
is a function with
p(K)\subset\{0,1\}
, and

means that
x_{n}
converges to
p
uniformly.
Take an open disc
D
with center
1
and radius
1/2
in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
It follows that
\chi_{D}(x_{n})\to p
.
Remark that
R({\mathcal{S}})
is a commutative R
∗
-algebra, so Proposition
2.6
(2) implies that
\chi_{D}(x_{n})\in{\mathcal{P}}(R({\mathcal{S}}))
.
Moreover,
\chi_{D}(x_{n})
can be regarded as a function in

with
\chi_{D}(x_{n})(K)\subset\{0,1\}
.
It follows by
\chi_{D}(x_{n})\to p
that
\chi_{D}(x_{n})=p
for a sufficiently large

, which implies

.
Therefore, the generalized Boolean algebra formed of compact open subsets of

coincides with
{\mathcal{P}}(R({\mathcal{S}}))={\mathcal{S}}
.
Since every function in
C_{0}(K)
can be uniformly approximated by elements in

, it is easy to see that each connected component of the locally compact Hausdorff space

is a singleton, which means that
K
is zero-dimensional.
∎

Using this, we may interpret Stone’s theorem as the equivalence of the following three categories:

•

the category of generalized Boolean algebras with lattice homomorphisms that preserve
0
,

•

the category of commutative R
∗
-algebras with
∗
-homomorphisms,

•

the category of all
C_{0}(K)
for zero-dimensional locally compact Hausdorff spaces

, with
∗
-homomorphisms.

We may give a proof of this equivalence straightforwardly.
The details are omitted, but let us just observe how the morphisms correspond.
Let
A
,
B
be commutative R
∗
-algebras.
Let
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
be a lattice homomorphism such that
\psi(0)=0
.
The corresponding
∗
-homomorphism
\phi\colon A\to B
can be described just as in Proof of Theorem
4.3
:
Each element
x
of

can be written as
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}p_{k}
for some
n\geq 1
, complex numbers
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.
Define
\phi\colon A\to B
by
\phi(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}\psi(p_{k})
, and check that it is a well-defined
∗
-homomorphism.
Secondly, given a
∗
-homomorphism
\phi\colon A\to B
(which is contractive), we may take its continuous extension
\varphi\colon\widehat{A}\to\widehat{B}
to completions, which is clearly a
∗
-homomorphism.
Finally, a
∗

\varphi\colon\widehat{A}\to\widehat{B}
maps

into

, thus restricts to a lattice homomorphism that sends

to

.

Is it possible to generalize this equivalence to the setting of noncommutative R
∗
-algebras?
Apparently, there is no that beautiful correspondence applicable to general R

-algebras.
Indeed, there exist distinct R

-algebras

with

(see Example

.
Moreover, in Proposition

we prove that there exists a pair of noncommutative R

Thus the structure of an R

-algebra cannot be fully recovered from the lattice of projections.
Nonetheless, there do exist several nice correspondence results that apply to certain classes of R

-algebras and their lattices of projections.
See Sections

and
8
.

5.
Purely atomic R
∗
-algebras and inner product spaces

Recall that a von Neumann algebra can be classified into several types by means of projections.
In particular, a von Neumann algebra is said to be atomic if the least upper bound of minimal projections is the unit.
It is a well-known fact, which follows from e.g. the type decomposition theorem of von Neumann algebras, that an atomic von Neumann algebra is an
\ell_{\infty}
-direct sum of type I factors.

Let us define a class of R
∗
-algebras that is similar to the class of atomic von Neumann algebras.
Since it is hard to consider the greatest lower bound of an infinite set of projections in an R
∗
-algebra, we want a definition that involves only finitely many projections.
An
atom
of an R
∗
-algebra
A
is a minimal projection in
{\mathcal{P}}(A)\setminus\{0\}
.
Note that
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is an atom if and only if the R
∗
-algebra
pAp
is one-dimensional.
We say an R
∗
-algebra
A
is
purely atomic
if every projection of
A
is a sum of finitely many atoms.
We give a complete description of purely atomic R
∗
-algebras.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra and
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
atoms.
Then the linear space
pAq
is at most one-dimensional.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in pAq
be elements with norm one.
Then
xx^{*}\in pAp
is positive and
\lVert xx^{*}\rVert=\lVert x\rVert^{2}=1
.
Since
pAp
is one-dimensional, we have
xx^{*}=p
.
Similarly, we obtain
yy^{*}=p
and
x^{*}x=y^{*}y=q
.
It follows that
x,y
are partial isometries that share initial and final spaces.
Since
xy^{*}\in pAp
, there is a complex number
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\lvert\lambda\rvert=1
such that
xy^{*}=\lambda p
.
It follows that
x=\lambda y
, proving that
x
and
y
are linearly dependent.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra.
Then
A
is purely atomic if and only if
pAp
is finite-dimensional for every
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.

Proof.

If
pAp
is finite-dimensional for every
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
, it is clear that
A
is purely atomic.
Suppose that
A
is purely atomic and let
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.
Take a collection of atoms
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
with
p=p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n}
.
For each pair
k,l\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the preceding lemma implies that
p_{k}Ap_{l}
is at most one-dimensional.
Therefore,
pAp=\operatorname{span}\{p_{k}Ap_{l}\mid k,l\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}
is finite-dimensional.
∎

It follows that a purely atomic R
∗
-algebra is ultramatricial.
Indeed, for a purely atomic R
∗
-algebra
A
and a collection
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A
, define
p:=\bigvee_{1\leq i\leq n}{l(x_{i})}\vee\bigvee_{1\leq i\leq n}{r(x_{i})}
, then
pAp
is finite-dimensional and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in pAp
.

Let
V
be an inner product space.
Let
H
be the completion of
V
.
Consider the collection
F(V)
of operators
x
in
B(H)
with finite rank such that
r(x)H,l(x)H\subset V
(namely, such that the ranges of
x
and
x^{*}
are included in
V
).
It is easy to see that
F(V)
is a purely atomic R
∗
-algebra.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
(V_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of complex inner product spaces.
Then the R
∗
-algebra
\bigoplus_{i\in I}F(V_{i})
is purely atomic.
Every purely atomic R
∗
-algebra is
∗
-isomorphic to some R
∗
-algebra that can be obtained in this manner.

Proof.

It is clear that the space
\bigoplus_{i\in I}F(V_{i})
as in the statement of this theorem forms a purely atomic R
∗
-algebra.
We prove that every purely atomic R
∗
-algebra is of this form.
The following proof is based on the theory of von Neumann algebras.

Let
A
be a purely atomic R
∗
-algebra.
Realize
A
as a
∗
-subalgebra of some
B(H)
.
Take the
\sigma
-weak closure (or equivalently, the
\sigma
-strong closure)
M
of
A
in
B(H)
.
Consider
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
as an increasing net of projections in
M
.
The fact that for every
x\in A
the projection
p=l(x)\vee r(x)\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
satisfies
pxp=x
implies that
\bigvee_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)}p\in{\mathcal{P}}(M)
is equal to the unit of
M
.
Moreover, for each
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
, the finite-dimensionality of
pAp
implies that
pMp=pAp
.
It follows that
M
is an atomic von Neumann algebra.
Thus we may find a collection
(H_{i})_{i\in I}
of Hilbert spaces such that

is
∗
-isomorphic to the
\ell_{\infty}
-direct sum of
(B(H_{i}))_{i\in I}
.
Since
pAp=pMp
is finite-dimensional for each

, each operator in

can be thought of as an operator of finite rank in the
\ell_{\infty}
-direct sum of
(B(H_{i}))_{i\in I}
.
Therefore, we may and do identify
A
with a
∗
-subalgebra of the R
∗
-algebra
\bigoplus_{i\in I}F(H_{i})
(
\subset B(K)
, where
K:=\bigoplus_{i\in I}H_{i}
).

Let us check that there exists a dense subspace
V_{i}\subset H_{i}
for each

such that
A
is identified with
\bigoplus_{i\in I}F(V_{i})\,\,(\subset\bigoplus_{i\in I}F(H_{i}))
.
Fix
i\in I
.
Define
V_{i}:=\{h\in H_{i}\mid h\in\operatorname{ran}p\text{ for some }p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)\}
.
Let
p_{i}
be the projection from
K
onto
H_{i}
, which lies in the center of
M
.
Observe that for each
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
we have
pp_{i}\in pMp=pAp
.
Thus we see that
V_{i}
is equal to
\{h\in H_{i}\mid h\in\operatorname{ran}p\text{ for some }p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)\text{ with }p\leq p_{i}\}
.
By the fact that
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
forms a lattice, we see that
V_{i}
is actually a linear subspace of
H_{i}
.
Since
A
is
\sigma
-strongly dense in

, we also see that
V_{i}
is dense in
H_{i}
.
For each
x\in A
, there exist

such that
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{i_{k}}x
.
Set
q_{k}=p_{i_{k}}(l(x)\vee r(x))
,

Then we have

This shows that

.

implies that there is a projection

Corollary 5.4
.

A purely atomic simple R
∗
-algebra is
∗
-isomorphic to

Therefore, the classification of purely atomic R

Theorem 5.5

Let

Since

Then the set

Since a

[

) to show that there exists a linear or conjugate-linear surjective isometry

In particular, the classification of purely atomic simple R
∗
-algebras is equivalent to that of inner product spaces.
How rich is the class of inner product spaces?
If we restrict ourselves to inner product spaces with countable Hamel dimension, then there is nothing interesting:
Every inner product space with countably infinite Hamel dimension is linearly isometric to

as a subspace of

. (Proof: Consider the Gram–Schmidt procedure.)
On the other hand, we know that complete inner product spaces (

In contrast, the behavior of incomplete inner product spaces with uncountable Hamel dimension is mysterious (even in the separable case).
A class of inner product spaces with uncountable Hamel dimension is given by ranges of operators:
If

of

Proposition 5.6
.

(1)

For every separable Banach space

For convenience, we give a proof.

(1) Take a sequence

By the Hahn–Banach theorem for each

Let us show that purely atomic R

What if one considers ascending chain condition instead of descending one?

Proposition 5.9
.

For an R
∗
-algebra
A
the following three conditions are equivalent.

•

There exists no strictly increasing infinite sequence of projections

in

It is clear that the first and the second conditions are equivalent and led by the third condition.

6.
R
∗

-rings and continuous geometries

Again let us recall the theory of von Neumann algebras.
In the type classification of von Neumann algebras, two important concepts appear: abelian projections and finite projections.
Recall that a projection
p
of a von Neumann algebra

is finite if every partial isometry
v\in M
with
vv^{*}\leq v^{*}v=p
satisfies
vv^{*}=p
.
Finiteness of projections in R
∗
-algebras is automatic.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra and
v\in A
be a partial isometry.
If
vv^{*}\leq v^{*}v
, then
vv^{*}=v^{*}v
.

Proof.

Assume that
v\in A
is a partial isometry with
vv^{*}\leq v^{*}v
and
vv^{*}\neq v^{*}v
.
Then the operator
v
restricted to
(\ker v)^{\perp}
is a proper isometry into
(\ker v)^{\perp}
.
It is well-known that a proper isometry has infinite spectrum.
(To prove it, use e.g. the Wold–von Neumann decomposition theorem and the fact that the unilateral shift has infinite spectrum.)
This contradicts the assumption that
A
is an R
∗
-algebra.
∎

In what follows we consider abelian projections.
For a von Neumann algebra
M
, recall that
M
is of type I if and only if there is a family of abelian projections whose least upper bound is the unit.

We say a projection

of an R
∗
-algebra

is

if the R
∗
-algebra

is commutative.
Clearly, an atom is an abelian projection.
The author is not aware whether there is a natural class of R
∗
-algebras that plays a role similar to that played by the class of type I von Neumann algebras in the theory of von Neumann algebras.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra.
Two projections

are said to be

and

.
This is an equivalence relation in

.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra.
For

.

Proof.

Let
x=v\lvert x\rvert
be the polar decomposition.
By Proposition

is a partial isometry in

.
Since

and

.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra and

.
Then

is nonabelian if and only if there exists a pair of nonzero subprojections

of

p_{1}p_{2}=0

p_{1}\sim p_{2}
.

Proof.

If the latter condition is satisfied then the algebra

Suppose that

is nonabelian, which means that

Since

.
It follows that there exists a pair of projections

.
Thus

Then

∎

The

Lemma 6.4

Let

be an R

-algebra and

Proof.

If

, so there is no pair

Assume that

Since

Recall that a ring

The structure of Baer

Which R

Recall that a lattice

is said to be

(2)

Recall that every principal right ideal of a

It is also common to consider completeness with respect to countably infinite collections.

is said to be

It should be also mentioned that a

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
Easy.
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
This is trivial.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)

First we claim that there exists no sequence

Thus we get the desired claim.

Consider the collection

of all projections in

Let

7.

In this section, we give a classification result on locally finite-dimensional R

Clearly, an R
∗
-algebra has a countable Hamel basis if and only if it is countably generated as a
∗
-algebra.
It follows that the class of locally finite-dimensional R

-algebras.

It was shown by Bratteli

11

(after Glimm’s classification of UHF algebras
[

∗
-algebras is equivalent to that of sequentially ultramatricial R

-algebras.
The classification of sequentially ultramatricial R

[

There are at least two formulations of this result:

Although the former formulation appears in Elliott’s original paper

Let us briefly recall both of these two formulations because the former formulation will be essentially used in the proof of Theorem

Let

Let

For

For

We define the partial binary operation

in

For

This is actually a well-defined partially defined binary operation.

The pair

Consider the set

For

Observe that for every pair of projections

Then

Theorem 7.1

Let

-isomorphic to

We say two projections

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra.
Let

.
The following are equivalent.

•

.

•

p\vee q-p\sim p\vee q-q

•

Proof.

By restricting our attention to the unital R

There is a partial isometry

We may consider

Since

such that

Fix a sufficiently large

It is an elementary exercise to show that

Let

Then it is easy to see that the operator

.

In this section, we give several results concerning mappings on the lattice of projections of an R

First, we give a consequence of von Neumann’s result.
A
ring isomorphism
is an additive and multiplicative bijection.
It is not necessarily linear.
Indeed, there exist many nonlinear ring automorphisms of

Let
A,B
be R
∗
-algebras.
Recall that each principal right ideal of

for a unique projection

Therefore, if
\phi\colon A\to B
is a ring isomorphism, then there exists a unique lattice isomorphism
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
such that

\phi(pA)=\psi(p)B

for all

.
(Note that this equation implies
l(\phi(p))=\psi(p)
.)
Von Neumann’s theorem states that the converse also holds under a certain condition.

Theorem 8.1

(von Neumann’s theorem for R
∗
-algebras)
.

Let
A,B
be unital R
∗
-algebras, and
n\geq 3
an integer.
If
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
is a lattice isomorphism, then there exists a ring isomorphism
\phi\colon{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)\to B
such that

\phi(p{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))=\psi(p)B

for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
.

Proof.

This is a special case of
[
43
, Theorem II.4.2]
.
∎

The uniqueness of
\phi
in the above theorem can be obtained from the proposition below.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
A\subset B(H)
be an R
∗
-algebra and
e\in A
an idempotent, i.e.,
e^{2}=e
.
If
r(e)\leq l(e)
, then
e
is a projection.

Proof.

By
e^{2}=e
, we have
l(e)=ee^{\dagger}=e^{2}e^{\dagger}=el(e)
.
Since
r(e)\leq l(e)
, we have
el(e)=e
.
Thus
e=l(e)
is a projection.
∎

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra, and let
n\geq 2
be an integer.
If
\phi\colon{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)\to{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
is a ring automorphism with

\phi(p{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))=p{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)

for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
, then
\phi
is the identity mapping on
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
.

Proof.

If
A
is unital, then the desired conclusion is a special case of
[
43
, Theorem II.4.1]
.
Let
p\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
.
It is clear that
\phi(p)
is an idempotent.
The equation
\phi(p{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))=p{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
implies
l(\phi(p))=p
.
Moreover, for every
q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
with
pq=0
, we have
\phi(p)q{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)=\phi(p)\phi(q{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))=\phi(pq{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))=\{0\}
.
Therefore, we have
\phi(p)q=0
for every
q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
with
pq=0
.
This means
r(\phi(p))\leq p=l(\phi(p))
.
The preceding lemma implies that
\phi(p)
is a projection and hence
\phi(p)=l(\phi(p))=p
.
It follows that
\phi
restricts to a ring automorphism of
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(qAq)
for every
q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.
Using the proposition for the unital case, we see that
\phi(x)=x
for every
x\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(qAq)
.
Since
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
is the directed union of
({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(qAq))_{q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)}
, we have
\phi(x)=x
for every
x\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
.
∎

If
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
are ring isomorphisms from
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
onto
B
that satisfy the condition of Theorem
8.1
, then
\phi^{-1}\circ\phi^{\prime}
maps each principal right ideal of
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
onto itself.
Thus the preceding proposition implies
\phi^{-1}\circ\phi^{\prime}=\operatorname{id}_{{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)}
, and
\phi^{\prime}=\phi
.
A somewhat surprising fact is that we cannot drop the assumption of the existence of unit in Theorem
8.1
.

Example 8.4
.

For an inner product space
V
, the lattice
{\mathcal{P}}(F(V))
can be identified with the lattice of finite-dimensional subspaces of
V
(ordered by inclusion).
Therefore, if two inner product spaces
V,W
have the same Hamel dimension, then
{\mathcal{P}}(F(V))
is lattice isomorphic to
{\mathcal{P}}(F(W))
.
Let
H
be an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, and let
f\colon H\to H
be any linear bijection that is not bounded.
Remark that
F(H)
is
∗
-isomorphic to
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(F(H))
for every
n\geq 1
.
Since
f
maps each finite-dimensional subspace of
H
onto another finite-dimensional subspace of
H
,
f
determines a lattice automorphism
\psi
of the lattice of finite-dimensional subspaces of
H
, which is identified with
{\mathcal{P}}(F(H))
.
Let us prove that there exists no ring automorphism
\phi\colon F(H)\to F(H)
that satisfies
\phi(pF(H))=\psi(p)F(H)
for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(F(H))
.
Assume for a contradiction that there exists such a ring automorphism
\phi\colon F(H)\to F(H)
.
By unboundedness of
f
, we may find a nonzero vector
h\in H
such that
f(\{h\}^{\perp})
is dense in
H
.
Take the rank-one projection
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(F(H))
onto
{\mathbb{C}}h
.
For each projection
q\in{\mathcal{P}}(F(H))
of rank one with
pq=0
, we see that
\phi(q)\in F(H)
is an idempotent with
\phi(p)\phi(q)=\phi(pq)=0
.
However, the equation
\phi(qF(H))=\psi(q)F(H)
implies
\phi(q)H=f(qH)
.
It follows that
\phi(p)|_{f(\{h\}^{\perp})}=0
.
Since
f(\{h\}^{\perp})
is dense in
H
, the boundedness of
\phi(p)
implies
\phi(p)=0
, a contradiction.

Recall that a mapping
\phi\colon A\to B
between
∗
-algebras is called a
Jordan
∗
-homomorphism
(resp. a
Jordan
∗
-isomorphism
) if it is a linear mapping (resp. a linear bijection) such that
\phi(x)^{*}=\phi(x^{*})
and
\phi(x^{2})=\phi(x)^{2}
for every
x\in A
.
Clearly, if
A
and
B
are R
∗
-algebras,
\phi\colon A\to B
is a Jordan
∗
-homomorphism and
x\in A
is self-adjoint (resp. positive, a projection), then so is
\phi(x)\in B
.

Jordan
∗
-isomorphisms appear quite often in studying mappings between operator algebras that preserve certain structures.
See Molnár’s recent article
[
40
]
for more about Jordan
∗
-isomorphisms between C
∗
-algebras.

Example 8.5
.

Let
A
be the unitization of
\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
, which is formed of all bi-infinite sequences
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
of
2\times 2
matrices
x_{n}
with the following property: There exists
N\geq 1
such that
x_{n}=x_{N}\in{\mathbb{C}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
for all
n
with
\lvert n\rvert\geq N
.
Define
\phi\colon A\to A
by
\phi((x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}})=(y_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
y_{n}=x_{n}
if
n\geq 0
, and
y_{n}
is the transpose of
x_{n}
otherwise.
Then
\phi
is a Jordan
∗
-automorphism of

.

Proposition 8.6
.

Let
A,B
be R
∗
-algebras.
Let
\phi\colon A\to B
be a linear map such that
\phi({\mathcal{P}}(A))\subset{\mathcal{P}}(B)
.
Then
\phi
is a Jordan
∗
-homomorphism.

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\phi
maps mutually orthogonal projections to mutually orthogonal projections.
Since every self-adjoint operator
a
in
A
can be written as a real-linear combination of mutually orthogonal projections, the linearity of
\phi
implies that
\phi(a)^{*}=\phi(a)
and
\phi(a^{2})=\phi(a)^{2}
.
It follows that
\phi
preserves involution.
If
a,b
are self-adjoint, then so is
a+b
, hence we have
\phi((a+b)^{2})=\phi(a+b)^{2}
, which together with
\phi(a^{2})=\phi(a)^{2}
and
\phi(b^{2})=\phi(b)^{2}
implies that
\phi(ab+ba)=\phi(a)\phi(b)+\phi(b)\phi(a)
.
Thus we obtain

\begin{split}\phi((a+ib)^{2})&=\phi(a^{2})+i\phi(ab+ba)-\phi(b^{2})\\
&=\phi(a)^{2}+i(\phi(a)\phi(b)+\phi(b)\phi(a))-\phi(b)^{2}\\
&=\phi(a+ib)^{2}.\end{split}

∎

By strengthening the assumption of Theorem
8.1
, we obtain a Feldman–Dye type result.
Let
A,B
be R
∗
-algebras.
A bijection
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
is called an orthoisomorphism if
pq=0\iff\psi(p)\psi(q)=0
for every pair
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.
It is easy to verify that an orthoisomorphism
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
is a lattice isomorphism and
\psi(p^{\perp})=\psi(p)^{\perp}
if
A
and
B
are unital, so this definition is consistent with the definition of orthoisomorphism in Section
4
.
Dye
[
18
]
(see also Feldman’s
[
21
]
) proved that every orthoisomorphism between the projection lattices of two von Neumann algebras extends to a Jordan
∗
-isomorphism if one of the von Neumann algebras is without type I
2
direct summands.
To obtain this theorem, Dye needed to appropriately modify the proof of von Neumann’s
[
43
, Theorem II.4.2]
.
In the setting of R
∗
-algebras, we may directly apply Theorem
8.1
to consider orthoisomorphisms of the projection lattices.
The proof below is partly based on
[
21
, Proof of Theorem 3]
.
It should be worth mentioning that we do not need the assumption of the existence of unit in this theorem.

Theorem 8.7

(Dye’s theorem for R
∗
-algebras)
.

Let
A,B
be R
∗
-algebras.
If
\phi\colon A\to B
is a Jordan
∗
-isomorphism, then it restricts to an orthoisomorphism from
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
onto
{\mathcal{P}}(B)
.
Let
n\geq 3
.
If
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
is an orthoisomorphism, then there exists a unique Jordan
∗
-isomorphism
\phi\colon{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)\to B
such that
\phi(p)=\psi(p)
for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
.

Proof.

It is clear that a Jordan
∗
-isomorphism restricts to an orthoisomorphism between the projection lattices.
In what follows, let
n\geq 3
and suppose that
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
is an orthoisomorphism.

Assume that
A
is unital.
Then
B
is also unital because
\psi
preserves the maximal projection.
Since
\psi
is a lattice isomorphism, there exists a ring isomorphism
\phi\colon{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)\to B
such that
\phi(p{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))=\psi(p)B
for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
.
Let
p\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))
.
It is clear that
\phi(p)
is an idempotent.
The equation
\phi(p^{\perp}{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A))=\psi(p^{\perp})B=\psi(p)^{\perp}B
implies that
l(\phi(p^{\perp}))=\psi(p)^{\perp}
.
Since
pp^{\perp}=0
, we have
\phi(p)\phi(p^{\perp})=0
.
Thus we have
0=r(\phi(p))l(\phi(p^{\perp}))=r(\phi(p))\psi(p)^{\perp}
and hence
r(\phi(p))\leq\psi(p)=l(\phi(p))
.
By Lemma
8.2
, we see that
\phi(p)
is a projection, and we have
\phi(p)=l(\phi(p))=\psi(p)\in{\mathcal{P}}(B)
.

Consider the ring automorphism
x\mapsto\phi^{-1}(\phi(x^{*})^{*})
of
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
.
This fixes every projection, hence Proposition
8.3
implies that
x=\phi^{-1}(\phi(x^{*})^{*})
, or equivalently,
\phi(x)^{*}=\phi(x^{*})
for each
x\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
.
It follows that
\phi
maps the self-adjoint part of
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
onto that of
B
.
Since
\phi
preserves squares,
\phi
restricted to self-adjoint parts is an order isomorphism.
It follows that
\phi(t)=t
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Define the complex-linear map
\Phi\colon A\to B
by
\Phi(a+ib)=\phi(a)+i\phi(b)
for self-adjoint
a,b\in A
.
By Proposition
8.6
,
\Phi
is a Jordan
∗
-homomorphism that extends
\psi
.
Since projections of
B
linearly span
B
,
\Phi
is surjective.

If
A
is not unital, then for each
p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
,
\psi
restricts to an orthoisomorphism from
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(pAp))
onto
{\mathcal{P}}(\psi(p\otimes 1)B\psi(p\otimes 1))
.
Since
pAp
is a unital R
∗
-algebra, it follows that
\psi
restricted to
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(pAp))
extends to a Jordan
∗
-isomorphism from
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(pAp)
onto
\psi(p\otimes 1)B\psi(p\otimes 1)
.
Moreover, since
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(pAp))
linearly spans
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(pAp)
, this is the unique linear extension of
\psi|_{{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(pAp))}
.
Since
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
is the directed union of
({\mathbb{M}}_{n}(pAp))_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)}
, it follows that
\psi
extends to a linear mapping, and this extension is a Jordan
∗
-isomorphism from
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
onto
B
.
The uniqueness of the extension is again the consequence of the fact that an R
∗
-algebra is linearly spanned by projections.
∎

The assumption
n\geq 3
is essential in Theorem
8.7
(and Theorem
8.1
).
Indeed, it is easy to find wild examples of orthoisomorphisms from
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{M}}_{2})
onto itself.

A
∗
-antiisomorphism

\phi\colon A\to B
between
∗
-algebras is a complex-linear bijection with
\phi(x^{*})=\phi(x)^{*}
,
\phi(xy)=\phi(y)\phi(x)
for every pair
x,y\in A
.
Clearly, a
∗
-antiisomorphism is a Jordan
∗
-isomorphism.
Theorem
8.7
motivates the following question:
How far are Jordan
∗
-isomorphisms from
∗
-isomorphisms?
In particular, how different are
∗
-antiisomorphisms from
∗
-isomorphisms?
A more specific question is:
Is there a pair of mutually
∗
-antiisomorphic R
∗
-algebras that are not
∗
-isomorphic, or equivalently, is there an R
∗
-algebra that is not
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite R
∗
-algebra?
Here, for an R
∗
-algebra
A
, its
opposite R
∗
-algebra

A^{\mathrm{op}}
is defined in the following manner.
As a complex normed space
A^{\mathrm{op}}
is equal to
A
.
For each
x\in A
we write the corresponding element in
A^{\mathrm{op}}
as
x^{\mathrm{op}}
in order to distinguish
A
from
A^{\mathrm{op}}
.
We define the
∗
-algebra structure of
A^{\mathrm{op}}
by
(x^{\mathrm{op}})^{*}:=(x^{*})^{\mathrm{op}}
and
x^{\mathrm{op}}y^{\mathrm{op}}:=(yx)^{\mathrm{op}}
for
x,y\in A
.
Then it is easy to show that
A^{\mathrm{op}}
forms an R
∗
-algebra that is
∗
-antiisomorphic to
A
.

It is a trivial fact that every commutative R
∗
-algebra is
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite.
By the Elliott classification theorem, it is plain that every sequentially ultramatricial R
∗
-algebra is
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite.
How about general R
∗
-algebras?
Banach space theory gives an answer.
For a complex normed space
X
, its
complex conjugate normed space

\overline{X}
is defined in the following manner.
As a real normed space
\overline{X}
is equal to
X
.
For each
v\in X
we write the corresponding element in
\overline{X}
as
\overline{v}
in order to distinguish
X
from
\overline{X}
.
We define the complex-linear structure of
\overline{X}
by
\lambda\overline{v}:=\overline{\overline{\lambda}v}

v\in X
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proposition 8.8
.

There is a purely atomic simple separable R
∗
-algebra
F(V)
that is not
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite.

Proof.

It is well-known (see for example
[
10
,
33
]
) that there exists a separable Banach space
X
that is not isomorphic (as complex Banach space) to its complex conjugate
\overline{X}
.
For such an
X
, take an injective bounded linear operator
T\colon X\to\ell_{2}
with dense range (see Proposition
5.6
), and set
V:=TX
.
Consider the complex conjugate
\overline{T}\colon\overline{X}\to\overline{\ell_{2}}
of
T
(defined by
\overline{T}(\overline{v}):=\overline{T(v)},\,\,v\in X
), which is clearly a bounded linear operator with dense range, and observe that the range is canonically identified with
\overline{V}
.
It is easy to see that the opposite R
∗
-algebra of
F(V)
is
∗
-isomorphic to
F(\overline{V})
by the mapping
x^{\mathrm{op}}\mapsto\overline{x}^{*}
,
x\in F(V)
.
If
F(V)
is
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite, then it follows by Proposition
5.6
that
X
and
\overline{X}
are isomorphic as complex Banach spaces, which contradicts our choice of
X
.
∎

The difference between an algebra and its opposite for operator algebras has been considered by many hands.
In the setting of von Neumann algebras, Connes
[
14
]
discovered an example of a II
1
factor which is not
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite, giving a negative solution to Problem 4 of Kadison’s list
[
23
]
.
By now many examples of C
∗
-algebras that are not
∗
-isomorphic to their opposites are known.
However, it remains as an open problem whether there exists a simple nuclear separable C
∗
-algebra that is not
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite.
The case of AF C
∗
-algebras is still under investigation in the literature.
Indeed, in
[
20
]
Farah and Hirshberg proved the existence of a (nonseparable) AF C
∗
-algebra that is not
∗
-isomorphic to its opposite under some set-theoretic axiom that is consistent with ZFC.
It is an open problem whether such an example can be constructed inside ZFC.
See
[
20
]
for more details about the state-of-the-art on this topic.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra or a von Neumann algebra.
A function
\mu\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called a
finitely additive
(signed)
measure
if it satisfies

\mu(p+q)=\mu(p)+\mu(q)

for every pair of mutually orthogonal projections
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.

Mackey’s problem asks whether a finitely additive measure extends to a linear mapping under a suitable assumption.
This question is motivated by the theory of quantum logic.
Gleason proved that every positive countably-additive measure on the projection lattice of
B(H)
, except for the case
H
is
2
-dimensional, extends to a bounded normal functional
[
26
]
.
More generally, it was proved by Bunce and Wright that every finitely additive measure with bounded range extends uniquely to a bounded linear functional in the setting of general von Neumann algebras without type I
2
direct summands
[
12
,
13
]
.
We give a Gleason-type result for a certain class of R
∗
-algebras.
Let
A
be an ultramatricial R
∗
-algebra.
We say
A
satisfies the property (N2) if
A
can be written as the union of an increasing net
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
of finite-dimensional R
∗
-algebras such that no
A_{i}
has
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
as its direct summand.

Let
A
be an R
∗
-algebra and
\mu
a finitely additive measure on
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.
Since every operator
x\in A
has cartesian decomposition
x=a+bi
with
a,b\in A
self-adjoint,
x
is written as a finite linear combination of projections.
Thus the uniqueness of linear extension of a finitely additive measure is clear.
If in addition
\lVert x\rVert\leq 1
, then
0\leq a_{\pm},b_{\pm}\leq 1
(see Proposition
2.6
(2)) and they are in the convex hull of
{\mathcal{P}}(A)
.
If
\mu
extends to a linear functional
f
on
A
, then we have
f(x)=f(a_{+})-f(a_{-})+if(b_{+})-if(b_{-})
, and
f(a_{+}),f(a_{-}),f(b_{+}),f(b_{-})
belong to the convex hull of
\mu({\mathcal{P}}(A))
.
Therefore, if
\mu({\mathcal{P}}(A))
is a bounded subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
then the linear extension, if exists, is bounded.

Theorem 8.9

(Gleason’s theorem for ultramatricial R
∗
-algebras)
.

Let
A
be an ultramatricial R
∗
-algebra with (N2).
If
\mu\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a finitely additive measure such that
\mu({\mathcal{P}}(A))
is a bounded subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
, then
\mu
extends uniquely to a bounded linear functional on
A
.

Proof.

Assume that
A
is the union of an increasing net
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
of finite-dimensional R
∗
-algebras such that no
A_{i}
has
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
as its direct summand.
Then Gleason’s theorem for a finite-dimensional von Neumann algebra (see
[
12
, Section 2]
, which is based on Gleason’s result
[
26
]
) implies that the map
\mu
restricted to each
{\mathcal{P}}(A_{i})
extends uniquely to a linear mapping on
A_{i}
.
It follows that
\mu
extends uniquely to a linear mapping on
A
, and the extension is bounded by the observation preceding this theorem.
∎

Corollary 8.10
.

Let
A
and
B
be R
∗
-algebras.
Assume that
A
is ultramatricial and satisfies (N2).
If
\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathcal{P}}(B)
is a map such that
\psi(p)+\psi(q)=\psi(p+q)
for every pair of mutually orthogonal projections
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
, then
\psi
uniquely extends to a Jordan
∗
-homomorphism
\phi\colon A\to B
.

Proof.

For each bounded linear functional
f
on
B
, the preceding theorem implies that the map
f\circ\psi\colon{\mathcal{P}}(A)\to{\mathbb{C}}
extends uniquely to a bounded linear functional of
A
.
As a consequence, we may easily prove the following:
If complex numbers
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and projections
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}(A)
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}p_{k}=0
, then
\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}\psi(p_{k})=0
.
It follows that
\psi
extends (uniquely) to a linear mapping
\phi\colon A\to B
.
By Proposition
8.6
, we conclude that
\phi
is a Jordan
∗
-homomorphism.
∎

9.
Questions

In this section, we propose naive questions on R
∗
-algebras.
These questions are not complete: some of them might be trivially easy to solve, and some might be totally meaningless to consider, but some should have essential importance.

First of all, the author suspects that the theory of R
∗
-algebras falls into one of the following two possibilities:

•

If every R
∗
-algebra is locally finite-dimensional or ultramatricial, then chances are that we can capture a lot about the structure of general R
∗
-algebras. Indeed, we already have a complete classification result of locally finite-dimensional R
∗
-algebras with countable Hamel dimension (Section
7
).

•

If not, then the theory of R
∗
-algebras should be so deep that the full understanding might be far out of the reach.
That is because once we get one wild example of an R
∗
-algebra we may easily construct many other wild examples by Section
3
.
In that case, R
∗

Every statement about (generalized) Boolean algebras gives rise to a question about R

Below we propose several questions in this direction.

Before that, we remark that there are several other attempts to give a framework for the theory of noncommutative Boolean algebras or noncommutative Boolean spaces.
One natural way is to detect the class of commutative C
∗
-algebras that arise as
C_{0}(K)
for zero-dimensional (locally) compact Hausdorff spaces
K
.
This class coincides with that of commutative C
∗
-algebras with real rank zero (i.e., every self-adjoint operator is in the norm closure of the collection of self-adjoint operators with finite spectra).
Thus one may consider C
∗
-algebras with real rank zero as noncommutative analogs of zero-dimensional locally compact Hausdorff spaces.
See
[
8
, Section V.3]
for more information about real and stable rank.

Another way of formulating noncommutative Boolean algebras is to consider a certain class of inverse semigroups, which in connection with groupoids can be related to the theory of C
∗
-algebras.
See
[
39
]
for the details.
Yet another direction of research is the theory of quantum logic initiated by Birkhoff and von Neumann
[
5
]
,
see
[
17
]
.
The main target of this theory is an orthomodular lattice (usually assumed to be
\sigma
-complete), which is an orthocomplemented lattice
{\mathcal{P}}
such that
p\vee(q\wedge p^{\perp})=q
for any
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}
with
p\leq q
.
This class of lattices includes the projection lattices of von Neumann algebras.

By Stone’s theorem, zero-dimensional locally compact Hausdorff spaces are in one-to-one correspondence with generalized Boolean algebras.
Recall that our main discovery in this paper is that R
∗
-algebras form noncommutative analogs of generalized Boolean algebras.
For this reason, the author guesses that those C
∗
-algebras obtained by completing R
∗
-algebras can be considered as noncommutative analogs of zero-dimensional locally compact Hausdorff spaces.
(However, we need to be aware of the fact that the completion, e.g.

∗
-subalgebras.)

If every R

Let us summarize several facts on Boolean algebras.
Although Theorem

Proposition 9.10

Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subset 2^{X}
be a generalized field of sets.
As in Section
4

with the space of functions
\{f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}\mid\#f(X)<\infty,\,\,f^{-1}(z)\in{\mathcal{S}}\text{ for all }z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}\}

•

R({\mathcal{S}})
has countable Hamel dimension if and only if
R({\mathcal{S}})
is separable if and only if
{\mathcal{S}}
has countable cardinality.

•

K_{0}(R({\mathcal{S}}))
is isomorphic to
\{f\in R({\mathcal{S}})\mid f(X)\subset{\mathbb{Z}}\}
, and
K_{0}(R({\mathcal{S}}))_{+}
to
\{f\in K_{0}(R({\mathcal{S}}))\mid f\geq 0\}
.

•

d(R({\mathcal{S}}))
is the same as
{\mathcal{S}}
as a set, and for

, the sum

.

We omit the proof because it is easy and these facts should be well-known.
There exist many wild examples of Boolean algebras and Boolean spaces, which implies the existence of wild commutative R

Example 9.11

In
