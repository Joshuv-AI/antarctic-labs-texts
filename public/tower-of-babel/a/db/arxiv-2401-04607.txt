Abstract
. The directed Cayley diameter of a finite group is investigated in terms of the monoid of product-one sequences over the group, via the new notion of directed geodesic atoms. Two quantities associated to the set of directed geodesic atoms provide lower and upper bounds for the directed Cayley diameter. An algorithm for computing the directed geodesic atoms is implemented in GAP, and is applied to determine the above mentioned quantities for all non-abelian groups of order at most 42 42 , and for the alternating group of degree 5 5 . Furthermore, the small and large Davenport constants of all these groups are computed (excepting the large Davenport constant for A 5 A_{5} ), extending thereby the formerly obtained results on the groups of order less than 32 32 . Along the way the directed Cayley diameter of a finite abelian group is expressed in terms of its invariants.

1.
Introduction

It was proved recently in
[
10
]
that if the monoid of product-one sequences
over a finite group
G_{1}
is isomorphic to the monoid of product-one sequences over a finite group
G_{2}
, then the group
G_{1}
is isomorphic to the group
G_{2}
.
It is therefore not surprising that much information on a finite group
G
can be
read off from the set of atoms in the monoid of product-one sequences over

G
. The present paper gives further instances of this principle. We shall
relate the problem of computing the directed Cayley diameter of a finite group to the study of
the atoms in the monoid of product-one sequences
over the group.
Note that the directed Cayley diameter is an obvious upper bound for the undirected Cayley diameter (referred to simply as the “Cayley diameter”). Conversely, a general explicit bound for the directed Cayley diameter in terms of the Cayley diameter is proved in
[
1
]
.
The Cayley diameter of finite groups has received much attention in the literature. In particular, several prominent papers are motivated by the conjecture from
[
2
]
stating
that the Cayley diameter of a finite non-abelian simple group
G
is bounded above by

(\log|G|)^{c}
with some absolute constant

c
, see for example
[
17
]
,

[
4
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
16
]
,

[
7
]
.
Other areas where Cayley graphs have relevance are surveyed in
[
18
]
.

Recall that the directed Cayley diameter of a finite group
G
is the maximal diameter of its Cayley graphs associated with the various irredundant generating systems of
G
.
The starting point of this paper is the observation
[
5
, Proposition 5.6]
, asserting that the large Davenport constant
\mathsf{D}(G)
of a finite group
G
(i.e. the maximal length of an atom in
the monoid of product-one sequences over
G
) is strictly greater than the diameter of any of the Cayley digraphs of
G
.
In fact this statement can be strengthened by saying that
\mathsf{D}(G)
is strictly greater than the directed Cayley diameter of any subgroup of
G
(see
Corollary
4.3
).
By our Lemma
5.5
, the maximum of the directed Cayley diameters of the subgroups of
G
is one less than
\mathsf{GD}(G)
, the
directed geodesic Davenport constant of

G

introduced in Definition
5.4
as the maximal length of a so-called
directed geodesic atom
(see Definition
5.1
) in the monoid

\mathcal{B}(G)
of product-one sequences over
G
.
A characterisation of the directed geodesic atoms,
given in Section
5
,
together with Theorem
6.1
, offers an algorithm to build up the set of directed geodesic atoms, using a bottom-up approach (see Section
7
).
This algorithm is related to the algorithm given in
[
5
, Section 6]
, computing
\mathsf{D}(G)
and all atoms in

\mathcal{B}(G)
. Finally, once a list of directed geodesic atoms is produced, one can read off bounds for
the directed Cayley diameter of
G

(see Lemma
5.5
).

After recalling concepts related to Cayley graphs and the monoid of product-one sequences in Section
2
,
we turn to finite abelian groups and determine
their directed Cayley diameter in
Theorem
3.2
.
The materials on the relation between the directed Cayley diameter and the atoms in the monoid of product-one sequences as well as the algorithms are contained in
Sections
4
,
5
,
6
, and
7
.
The results of our computer calculations
are presented in Section
8
.
The small and large Davenport constants of the non-abelian groups of order less than
32
were computed in
[
5
]
. Here
we give the Davenport constants of the remaining non-abelian groups of order at most
42
as well, and for all non-abelian
groups
G
with
|G|\leq 42
we compute also the directed geodesic Davenport constant of
G
(which in several cases yields
the directed Cayley diameter of
G
).

Finally, in Section
9
we review some recent results on the Davenport constants of finite non-abelian
groups.

We note that in a follow-up paper we shall adapt the results of this paper to the problem of computing the (undirected) Cayley diameter of a finite group.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Directed Cayley graphs

Let
G
be a finite group (written multiplicatively unless explicitly stated otherwise) with identity element
1_{G}
and let
B\subseteq G
be a generating system of
G
.
The
directed Cayley graph

\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
of
G
with respect to the generating system
B

is the digraph whose vertices are labeled by the elements of
G
, and
the pair
(g,h)
is a directed edge in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
if there exists an element

b\in B
with
h=gb
.
We say that a directed path
g_{0},g_{1},\dots,g_{d}
in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
is

geodesic
if its length
d
is minimal among the lengths of the directed paths in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
from
g_{0}
to
g_{d}
.
The
diameter

\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))
of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
is
the maximal possible length of a geodesic path in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
. As the action of
G
on itself via left multiplication embeds
G
into the automorphism group of the digraph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
,
\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))
is the same as the maximal length of a geodesic path in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
starting at
1_{G}
.
The
directed Cayley diameter
of
G
is defined as

\mathsf{dcd}(G):=\max\{\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))\mid B\subseteq G\mbox{ generates }G\}.

The number
\mathsf{dcd}(G)
can be introduced also without a reference to Cayley graphs. Namely, for a subset
B\subseteq G
and an element
g\in\langle B\rangle

(the subgroup of
G
generated by
B
)
we set

\ell_{B}(g):=\min\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid\exists b:\{1,\dots,n\}\to B\text{ with }g=b(1)\cdots b(n)\}

(with the convention
\ell_{B}(1_{G})=0
).
Clearly,
a directed path
1_{G}=g_{0},g_{1},\dots,g_{d}=g
from
1_{G}
to
g
in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,B)
is
geodesic
if
and only if
d=\ell_{B}(g)
.
Therefore

(1)

\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))=\max\{\ell_{B}(g)\mid g\in G\},

and

(2)

\mathsf{dcd}(G)=\max\{\ell_{B}(g)\mid g\in G,\ B\subseteq G,\ \langle B\rangle=G\}.

2.2.
The monoid of product-one sequences

Denote by
(\mathcal{F}(G),\boldsymbol{\cdot})
the free abelian monoid
with basis
G
. Its element will be called a
sequence over
G
, because
the element

S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}(G)

can be thought of as the sequence
g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell}
, where repetition is allowed and the order of terms is disregarded. The number
\ell
is called the
length
of
S
, denoted by
|S|
.
Note that the product of
g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}
and
h_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}h_{k}
in
the monoid
\mathcal{F}(G)
is
g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\cdot}h_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}h_{k}

(concatenation of sequences).
For
S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}(G)
we shall use the notation

\mathsf{v}_{g}(S):=|\{i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}\mid g_{i}=g\}|.

For
g\in G
and a positive integer
n
we shall write
g^{[n]}
for the sequence

\underbrace{g\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g}_{n}
.
Define the
support
of
S\in\mathcal{F}(G)
as

\mathrm{supp}(S):=\{g\in G\mid\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)>0\}.

For
g\in\mathrm{supp}(S)
denote by
S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]}
the element of

\mathcal{F}(G)
defined by the equality

S=g\boldsymbol{\cdot}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]}).

The sequence
g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}
is a
product-one sequence
if
there exists a permutation
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\ell}
such that

g_{\pi(1)}\cdots g_{\pi(\ell)}=1_{G}\in G
. Product-one sequences form a submonoid

\mathcal{B}(G)
of
\mathcal{F}(G)
.
An element
S\in\mathcal{B}(G)
is called an
atom
if it can not be written
as
S=T\boldsymbol{\cdot}U\in\mathcal{F}(G)
with
T,U\in\mathcal{B}(G)
and
|T|>0
,
|U|>0
. Write
\mathcal{A}(G)
for the set of atoms in
\mathcal{B}(G)
.
The maximal length of an atom in the monoid
\mathcal{B}(G)
is denoted by
\mathsf{D}(G)
and is called the

large Davenport constant of
G
.
A sequence
S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}(G)
is
product-one free
if
S
can not be written as
S=T\boldsymbol{\cdot}U\in\mathcal{F}(G)
where
T\in\mathcal{B}(G)
and
|T|>0
.
The maximal length of a product-one free sequence is denoted by
\mathsf{d}(G)
and is
called the
small Davenport constant of
G
.
The small and large Davenport constants are related by the following inequality:

\mathsf{D}(G)\geq 1+\mathsf{d}(G)
.

3.
Abelian groups

When
G
is abelian, we shall use the additive notation, and write
0_{G}
for the zero element of
G
. A product-one sequence

S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}

in this case is rather called a
zero-sum sequence
. It means that

g_{1}+\cdots+g_{\ell}=0_{G}\in G
.
A product-one free sequence is called a zero-sum free sequence in this case.
So
S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{\ell}
is
zero-sum free
if there is no
non-empty subset
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,\ell\}
with

g_{i_{1}}+\cdots+g_{i_{k}}=0_{G}\in G
.
The small and large Davenport constants of a finite abelian group
G

are related by the equality
\mathsf{D}(G)=\mathsf{d}(G)+1
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For a finite abelian group
G
we have

\mathsf{d}(G)\geq\mathsf{dcd}(G)
.

Proof.

Write
d:=\mathsf{dcd}(G)
. If
d=0
, then

G
is the one-element group, and
both
\mathsf{d}(G)
and
\mathsf{dcd}(G)
are equal to zero. From now on we assume that

d>0
.
By (
2
) there exists an equality
g=g_{1}+\cdots+g_{d}
, with

\{g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\}\subseteq B
, where
B
is an irredundant generating system of
G

(i.e.
B
generates
G
, but no proper subset of
B
generates
G
), and

d=\ell_{B}(g)
. Then
g\neq 0_{G}
,
and
g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{d}
is a zero-sum free sequence, and so
\mathsf{d}(G)\geq d
.
Indeed, otherwise after a possible renumbering we have that for some
k<d
we have
g_{k+1}+\cdots+g_{d}=0_{G}
, implying that
g_{1}+\cdots+g_{k}=g
. Thus

\ell_{B}(g)\leq k<d
, contrary to the assumption that
\ell_{B}(g)=d
.
∎

For an abelian group
G=C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}}
with

2\leq n_{1}\mid\dots\mid n_{r}
, set

\mathsf{d}^{*}(G):=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(n_{i}-1).

By
[
14
, Lemma 4.1]
(see also
[
12
, Exercise 1.6]
) we have

(3)

\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)\geq\mathsf{d}^{*}(H)+\mathsf{d}^{*}(G/H)\text{ for any subgroup }H\text{ of }G.

In particular,

(4)

\mathsf{d}^{*}(H)+|G:H|-1\leq\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)\quad\mbox{ when }G/H\mbox{ is cyclic}

(see also
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
for an alternative proof).

Note that
\mathsf{d}(G)\geq\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)
for all
G
.
Therefore Proposition
3.1
follows from the sharper
Theorem
3.2
below.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any finite abelian group
G
we have the equality

\mathsf{dcd}(G)=\mathsf{d}^{*}(G).

Proof.

For
i=1,\dots,r
denote by
e_{i}
the generator of the
i
th direct summand
C_{n_{i}}
of
G=C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}}
, where
2\leq n_{1}\mid\dots\mid n_{r}
. Consider the generating system
B:=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{r}\}
of
G
.
Then
\ell_{B}(\sum_{i=1}^{r}(n_{i}-1)e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(n_{i}-1)
, implying
the inequality
\mathsf{dcd}(G)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{r}(n_{i}-1)=\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)
.

We prove the reverse inequality
\mathsf{dcd}(G)\leq\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)
by induction on
|G|
.
For
|G|=1,2,3
the statement is obvious.
Suppose
|G|\geq 4
, and take a generating system
B
of
G
such that

d:=\mathsf{dcd}(G)=\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))
.
For any subset
C\subseteq B

it is obvious that

\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,C))\geq\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))
, therefore by omitting redundant elements from
B
,
we may assume that
B
is irredundant.
Pick some
b\in B
. Then
B\setminus\{b\}
generates a proper subgroup
H
of

G
, therefore
\mathsf{dcd}(H)\leq\mathsf{d}^{*}(H)
by the induction hypothesis.
Now take an element
g\in G
with
\ell_{B}(g)=d
.
Denote by
\bar{g}
the image of
g
in the factor group
G/H
. The factor group

G/H
is generated by the image
\bar{b}
of
b
. So there exists a

k\in\{0,1,\dots,|G:H|-1\}
such that
\bar{g}=k\bar{b}
, or equivalently,

g-kb\in H
.
Now

\ell_{B\setminus\{b\}}(g-kb)\leq\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(H,B\setminus\{b\}))\leq\mathsf{dcd}(H)\leq\mathsf{d}^{*}(H),

hence

d=\ell_{B}(g)\leq\ell_{B\setminus\{b\}}(g-kb)+k\leq\mathsf{d}^{*}(H)+k\leq\mathsf{d}^{*}(H)+|G:H|-1.

Finally, by (
4
) we conclude

\mathsf{dcd}(G)=d\leq\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

(i)

An easy short argument yielding an upper bound for

\mathsf{dcd}(G)
in the case when
G
is the direct sum of prime order groups is given in
[
3
, Lemma 5.2]
.

(ii)

The undirected Cayley diameter of a finite abelian group is determined in
[
22
, Lemma 3.3]
. For
G=C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}}
with

2\leq n_{1}\mid\dots\mid n_{r}
, it is

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lfloor n_{i}/2\rfloor
.

It is well known that the equality
\mathsf{d}(G)=\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)
holds for
abelian groups of rank at most
2
, for abelian
p
-groups, and for several other
infinite classes of abelian groups, and this equality is conjectured to hold for
abelian groups of rank
3
and for the groups of the form
C_{n}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n}
.
Therefore
for a notable class of abelian groups the inequality in the statement of
Proposition
3.1
holds with equality.

Example 3.4
.

Consider the group
G:=C_{3}\oplus C_{3}\oplus C_{3}\oplus C_{6}
with generators

e_{i}

(i=1,\dots,4)
such that
\mathrm{ord}(e_{1})=\mathrm{ord}(e_{2})=\mathrm{ord}(e_{3})=3
and
\mathrm{ord}(e_{4})=6
.
Set
g_{1}:=-e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}+e_{4}
,

g_{2}:=e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{3}+e_{4}
,

g_{3}:=e_{1}+e_{2}-e_{3}+e_{4}
,

g_{4}:=-e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}-e_{4}
,

g_{5}=e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}+e_{4}
,

g_{6}:=e_{2}-e_{3}-e_{4}
,

g_{7}:=-e_{2}+e_{3}-e_{4}
.
It is shown (as a special case of a more general statement) in

[
11
]
that the sequence

g_{1}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{2}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{3}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{4}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{5}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{6}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{7}
is zero-sum free.
As this sequence has length
12
, we conclude that

\mathsf{d}(G)\geq 12>11=\mathsf{d}^{*}(G)
.
Any ordering of the elements in this sequence gives a path of length
12
from
0
to

g:=2(g_{1}+g_{2}+g_{3}+g_{4}+g_{5})+g_{6}+g_{7}
in the Cayley graph

\mathrm{Cay}(G,\{g_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,7\})
.
Such a path can not be a geodesic path from
0
to
g
in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,\{g_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,7\})

by Theorem
3.2
.
Indeed, the equality

2(g_{1}+g_{2}+g_{3}+g_{4}+g_{5})+g_{6}+g_{7}=4g_{1}+g_{2}+g_{3}+2g_{4},

shows that there exists a path of length
4+1+1+2=8
from
0_{G}
to
g

in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,\{g_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,7\})
, hence
\ell_{\{g_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,7\}}(g)\leq 8
.

4.
The relation between the Davenport constant and the directed Cayley diameter

In view of Proposition
3.1
it is natural to pose the following question: does the inequality
\mathsf{dcd}(G)\leq\mathsf{d}(G)
hold for all non-abelian finite groups
G
?

The computer calculations presented in Section
8
answer
this question in the negative, see for example the group with GAP-identifier
[16,3]
in the table in
Section
8
.
What we know instead is that the directed Cayley diameter can be bounded in terms of the
large Davenport constant, since
in
[
5
, Proposition 5.6]
the following inequality was pointed out:

Proposition 4.1
.

We have
\mathsf{D}(G)>\mathsf{dcd}(G)

for all finite groups
G
.

Proposition
4.1
follows from the following observation:

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that

\ell_{\supp(b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d})}(b_{1}\cdots b_{d})=d
for a sequence
b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}\in\mathcal{F}(G)
.
Then setting
b_{d+1}:=(b_{1}\cdots b_{d})^{-1}
, we have that
b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d+1}
is an atom in
\mathcal{B}(G)
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that

b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d+1}=S\boldsymbol{\cdot}T
for some
S,T\in\mathcal{B}(G)
with
|S|>0
and
|T|>0
.
By symmetry we may assume that
\mathsf{v}_{b_{d+1}}(T)\geq 1
,
so
T=c_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}c_{m}\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d+1}
for some
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\in\supp(b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d})
.
Moreover, after a possible renumbering we have
c_{1}\cdots c_{m}b_{d+1}=1_{G}
.
Thus
c_{1}\cdots c_{m}=b_{d+1}^{-1}=b_{1}\cdots b_{d}
, hence

\ell_{\supp(b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d})}(b_{d+1}^{-1})\leq\ell_{\supp(c_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}c_{m})}(b_{d+1}^{-1})\leq m<d
.
This contradicts the assumption

\ell_{\supp(b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d})}(b_{1}\cdots b_{d})=d
.
∎

Lemma
4.2
implies the following strengthening of Proposition
4.1
:

Corollary 4.3
.

For any finite group
G
we have the inequality

(5)

\mathsf{D}(G)>\max\{\mathsf{dcd}(H)\mid H\leq G\}.

Remark 4.4
.

Corollary
4.3
is stronger than
Proposition
4.1
, since
in some cases it happens that
\mathsf{dcd}(H)>\mathsf{dcd}(G)
for a subgroup
H
of
G
(see Remark
5.8
and Remark
8.2
).

5.
Directed geodesic atoms and their applications

The number on the right hand side of the inequality (
5
) can be characterized in terms of the lengths
of some special atoms in
\mathcal{B}(G)
.

Definition 5.1
.

An element
S\in\mathcal{B}(G)

is called a
directed geodesic atom
if
there exists a
g\in\supp(S)
such that

\ell_{\supp(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})}(g^{-1})=|S|-1
.

Write

\mathcal{GA}(G)
for the set of directed geodesic atoms in
\mathcal{B}(G)
.

Remark 5.2
.

The terminology “directed geodesic atom” is motivated by the following:

(i)

S\in\mathcal{B}(G)
is a directed geodesic atom in the sense of Definition
5.1
if
and only if
S
can be written as

b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d+1}
,
where
1_{G},b_{1},b_{1}b_{2},\dots,b_{1}\cdots b_{d}

is a geodesic path from
1_{G}
to
b_{1}\cdots b_{d}
in
the directed Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\rangle,\supp(b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}))
, and
b_{d+1}^{-1}=b_{1}\cdots b_{d}
.

(ii)

By Lemma
4.2
a directed geodesic atom in
\mathcal{B}(G)
is an atom in
\mathcal{B}(G)
,
so

(6)

\mathcal{GA}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{A}(G).

Example 5.3
.

Keeping the notation of Example
3.4
, set

g_{8}:=-(2g_{1}+2g_{2}+2g_{3}+2g_{4}+2g_{5}+g_{6}+g_{7})
.
The sequence
g_{1}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{2}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{3}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{4}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{5}^{[2]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{6}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{7}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{8}
is an atom in
\mathcal{B}(C_{3}\oplus C_{3}\oplus C_{3}\oplus C_{6})
,
but our considerations in Section
3
show that it is not a directed geodesic atom.

In analogy with the large Davenport constant, we define the following quantities:

Definition 5.4
.

(i)

The
geodesic large Davenport constant of

G
is

\mathsf{GD}(G):=\max\{|S|\mid S\in\mathcal{GA}(G)\}.

(ii)

Set

\mathsf{dcd}^{*}(G):=\max\{|S|\colon S\in\mathcal{GA}(G),\ \langle\supp(S)\rangle=G\}-1.

As an immediate consequence of
(
2
) and Definition
5.1
we
get the following:

Lemma 5.5
.

We have

(i)

\mathsf{dcd}(G)\geq\mathsf{dcd}^{*}(G)
.

(ii)

\max\{\mathsf{dcd}(H)\mid H\leq G\}=\mathsf{GD}(G)-1
.

Corollary 5.6
.

For any finite group
G
we have

\mathsf{dcd}^{*}(G)\leq\mathsf{dcd}(G)\leq\mathsf{GD}(G)-1\leq\mathsf{D}(G)-1.

Proof.

The first two inequalities follow from
Lemma
5.5
, the third inequality follows from (
6
).
∎

Lemma
5.5
(ii) implies that

\mathsf{dcd}(G)

\mathsf{GD}(G)
are tightly
linked for certain groups as follows:

.

The following conditions are equivalent
for a finite group
G
:

(i)

\mathsf{dcd}(G)\geq\mathsf{dcd}(H)
for all

of

.

(ii)

\mathsf{dcd}(G)=\mathsf{GD}(G)-1

Remark 5.8
.

The equivalent conditions (i) and (ii) of

However, they do not hold for all non-abelian finite groups. The smallest examples are the groups with

Generating systems of
G
can also be read off from
\mathcal{GA}(G)

(and hence from
\mathcal{A}(G)
):

Proposition 5.9
.

A subset
B
generates a finite group
G
if and only if for all
g\in G
there exists an

S\in\mathcal{GA}(G)
with
g\in\mathrm{supp}(S)
and

\mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq B
.

Proof.

Suppose that
B
generates
G
. Then for each
g\in G
there exist

b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in B
with
g^{-1}=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}
, and
n=\ell_{B}(g^{-1})
.
Now
S:=b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{n}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g
belongs to
\mathcal{GA}(G)
and
S
satisfies

g\in\mathrm{supp}(S)
and

\mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq B
.

Conversely, let
B
be a subset of
G
such that for all
g\in G
there exists an

S\in\mathcal{GA}(G)
with
g\in\mathrm{supp}(S)
and

\mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq B
. Then in particular, for all
g\in G
there exist elements
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in B
with
b_{1}\cdots b_{n}g=1_{G}
, i.e.

g^{-1}=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}
. That is,
B
generates
G
.
∎

Given the set
\mathcal{GA}(G)
and a fixed generating system
B
of
G
,
it is in principle an easy matter to determine
\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))
.
Select the following subsets of
\mathcal{GA}(G)
:

\mathcal{GA}(G,B,0):=\{S\in\mathcal{GA}(G)\mid\mathrm{supp}(S)\subseteq B\}.

For
g\in G\setminus B
set

\mathcal{GA}(G,B,g,1):=\{S\in\mathcal{GA}(G)\mid\mathsf{v}_{g}(S)=1,\ \mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq B\},

and set

\mathcal{GA}(G,B,g):=\{S\in\mathcal{GA}(G,B,g,1)\mid\forall T\in\mathcal{GA}(G,B,g,1):|T|\geq|S|\}.

Finally,

\mathcal{GA}(G,B):=\mathcal{GA}(G,B,0)\cup\bigcup_{g\in G\setminus B}\mathcal{GA}(G,B,g).

Proposition 5.10
.

We have the equality

(7)

\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))=\max\{|S|\mid S\in\mathcal{GA}(G,B)\}-1.

Proof.

Set
d:=\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,B))
and write
m
for the number on the right hand side of (
7
). First we show that
m\geq d
. By definition of
d
(see (
1
)) there exists
an element
h:=b_{1}\cdots b_{d}
, where

b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\in B
and
\ell_{B}(h)=d
.
Then for
g:=h^{-1}
we have that

b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g
is a directed geodesic atom, and it belongs to
\mathcal{GA}(G,B,0)
if
g\in B
, and it belongs to
\mathcal{GA}(G,B,g)
if
g\in G\setminus B
. This shows that
m\geq d
.

By definition of
m
there exists a directed geodesic atom

, where
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\in B
and
\ell_{B}(g^{-1})=m
, showing that the reverse inequality

holds.
∎

6.
Characterization of directed geodesic atoms

The results of Section
5
show that if we are given the set

\mathcal{GA}(G)
of directed geodesic atoms for
G
, then we can read off generating systems of
G
, diameters of Cayley digraphs of
G
, and we can give upper and lower bounds for the directed Cayley
diameter of
G
. This motivates our interest in the
following characterization of the directed geodesic atoms in the set of all atoms
\mathcal{A}(G)
:

Theorem 6.1
.

The following conditions are equivalent for an element
S\in\mathcal{B}(G)
:

(i)

S
is not a directed geodesic atom.

(ii)

For all
g\in\mathrm{supp}(S)

there exists a
T\in\mathcal{GA}(G)
with
|T|<|S|
,
such that
g\in\mathrm{supp}(T)
and

\mathrm{supp}(T\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})
.

(iii)

For all
g\in\mathrm{supp}(S)

there exists a
T\in\mathcal{A}(G)
with
|T|<|S|
,
such that
g\in\mathrm{supp}(T)
and

\mathrm{supp}(T\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})
.

Proof.

(i)\Longrightarrow(ii)
:
Assume
S\in\mathcal{B}(G)\setminus\mathcal{GA}(G)
.
Pick an arbitrary
g
from
\mathrm{supp}(S)
. Then we can write

S=b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g
, with
g^{-1}=b_{1}\cdots b_{d}
.
Since
S\notin\mathcal{GA}(G)
, necessarily we have
\ell_{B}(g^{-1})<d
, where

B=\supp(b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d})
.
It follows that setting
k:=\ell_{B}(g^{-1})
, there exist elements

c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in B
such that
g^{-1}=c_{1}\cdots c_{k}
.
Set
T:=c_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}c_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g
.
Then
T\in\mathcal{GA}(G)
by
Definition
5.1
.
Moreover,
|T|=k+1<d+1=|S|
,
g\in\mathrm{supp}(T)
, and
\mathrm{supp}(T\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq B=\mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})
.

(ii)\Longrightarrow(iii)
: This is
trivial, since
\mathcal{GA}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{A}(G)
by (
6
).

(iii)\Longrightarrow(i)
:
Suppose for contradiction that (iii) holds for
S
and
S\in\mathcal{GA}(G)
.
Then we can write
S=b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d}\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d+1}
, where
b_{1}\cdots b_{d}b_{d+1}=1_{G}
, and setting

B:=\supp(b_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}b_{d})
, we have

\ell_{B}(b_{d+1}^{-1})=d
.
Now for
g:=b_{d+1}
we have
\mathrm{supp}(S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})=B
.
Take any
T\in\mathcal{A}(G)
with
g\in\mathrm{supp}(T)
,
\mathrm{supp}(T\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]})\subseteq B
and
|T|<|S|
.
We have
T=c_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}c_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g
with
c_{i}\in B
. With an appropriate numbering of the
c_{i}
, we have
c_{1}\cdots c_{k}g=1_{G}
, so
c_{1}\cdots c_{k}=g^{-1}=b_{d+1}^{-1}
. It follows that
k\geq\ell_{B}(b_{d+1}^{-1})=d
, implying in turn that
|T|=k+1\geq d+1=|S|
. This contradiction proves our claim.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

and

Recall the following definition from

:

Definition 7.1
.

Let
S,T\in\mathcal{F}(G)
.
The sequence
T
is called
a splitting
of the sequence
S
if
T=S\boldsymbol{\cdot}g^{[-1]}\boldsymbol{\cdot}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y

.

Let us denote simply by
\mathcal{GA}_{k}
the set of all directed geodesic atoms of length

in
\mathcal{GA}(G)
and by
\mathcal{GR}_{k}
a set of

\Aut(G)
-orbit representatives of
\mathcal{GA}_{k}

(here
\Aut(G)
stands for the automorphism group of

).
The possibility to build up
\mathcal{GA}_{k}
recursively is based on the following lemma:

Lemma 7.2
.

For any
k\geq 1
we have
\mathcal{GA}_{k+1}\subseteq\gamma(\mathcal{GA}_{k})
.

Proof.

All product-one sequences of length one or two (except the sequence
1_{G}\boldsymbol{\cdot}1_{G}
) are directed geodesic atoms. Assume that
k\geq 2
, and take an arbitrary
S\in\mathcal{GA}_{k+1}
. Then

S=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{k+1},\mbox{ where }g_{1}\dots g_{k}g_{k+1}=1_{G}\mbox{ and }\ell_{\supp(g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{k})}(g_{k+1}^{-1})=k.

Set

T:=g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{k-1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}h,\quad\mbox{where}\quad h:=g_{k}g_{k+1}.

Then obviously we have
g_{1}\dots g_{k-1}h=1_{G}
. We claim that

T
is a directed geodesic atom.
Suppose for contradiction that

h^{-1}=c_{1}\cdots c_{m}\mbox{ for some }m<k-1\mbox{ and }c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\in\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k-1}\}.

Taking into account
h^{-1}=g_{k+1}^{-1}g_{k}^{-1}
we conclude

g_{k+1}^{-1}=c_{1}\dots c_{m}g_{k},\quad\mbox{where}\quad c_{1},\dots,c_{m},g_{k}\in\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}.

Thus

\ell_{\supp(g_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\dots\boldsymbol{\cdot}g_{k})}(g_{k+1}^{-1})\leq m+1<k,

a contradiction.
Therefore
T\in\mathcal{GA}_{k}
and

T\prec S
, hence
S\in\gamma(\mathcal{GA}_{k})
.
Since
S
above was an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{GA}_{k+1}
, the statement is proved.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

Observe that by this lemma if
\mathcal{GA}_{k}=\emptyset
for some
k\geq 1
then
\mathcal{GA}_{l}=\emptyset
for every
l>k
. So the smallest
k
such that
\mathcal{GA}_{k}=\emptyset
equals
\mathsf{GD}(G)+1
.

Lemma 7.4
.

For any
k\geq 1
let
\mathcal{GR}_{k}
be a set of
\Aut(G)
-orbit representatives in
\mathcal{GA}_{k}
.
Then there is a system of
\Aut(G)
-orbit representatives
\mathcal{GR}_{k+1}
in
\mathcal{GA}_{k+1}
such that
\mathcal{GR}_{k+1}\subseteq\gamma(\mathcal{GR}_{k})
.

Proof.

Observe that the relation
\prec
is compatible with the
\Aut(G)
-action in the sense that
S\prec T
holds if and only if
\alpha(S)\prec\alpha(T)
for some
\alpha\in\Aut(G)
if and only if
\alpha(S)\prec\alpha(T)
for each
\alpha\in\Aut(G)
.

Now we prove that for any sequence
T\in\mathcal{GA}_{k+1}
its
\Aut(G)
-orbit
\Orb(T)
has a non-empty intersection with
\gamma(\mathcal{GR}_{k})
.
By Lemma
7.2
there exists a sequence
S\prec T
so that
S\in\mathcal{GA}_{k}
.
As
\mathcal{GR}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{GA}_{k}
is a complete set of

\Aut(G)
-orbit representatives, there is some

such that
R=\alpha(S)
for some
\alpha\in\Aut(G)
.
Hence by the compatibility of
\prec
it follows that
R=\alpha(S)\prec\alpha(T)
. As a result
\alpha(T)\in\Orb(T)\cap\gamma(\mathcal{GR}_{k})
.
∎

Example 8.1
.

The table above shows that
for
3\leq n\leq 21
the dihedral group
D_{2n}
has directed Cayley diameter
n
. It is quite easy to see
that
\mathsf{dcd}(D_{2n})\geq n
for all
n\geq 3
. Indeed,
D_{2n}
has the presentation with generators and relations as

D_{2n}=\langle a,b\mid a^{2}=b^{2}=(ab)^{n}=1\rangle
.
Now when
n
is even, then

\ell_{\{a,b\}}((ab)^{n/2})=n
,
and when
n
is odd,
then

\ell_{\{a,b\}}((ab)^{(n-1)/2}a)=n
.

Remark 8.2
.

The quantities
\mathsf{D}(G)
,

\mathsf{d}(G)
,
\mathsf{GD}(G)
are obviously monotone in the sense that for any subgroup
H
of
G
we have

\mathsf{D}(H)\leq\mathsf{D}(G)
,
\mathsf{d}(H)\leq\mathsf{d}(G)
,

\mathsf{GD}(H)\leq\mathsf{GD}(G)
.
The quantity
\mathsf{dcd}(G)
is also monotone for abelian groups
G
by
(
3
) and Theorem
3.2
.
On the other hand, the quantity
\mathsf{dcd}(G)
is not monotone for all finite groups
G
.
This follows from some known cases of Babai’s Conjecture from
[
2
]
mentioned in the Introduction,
because a non-abelian simple group
may contain a “large” cyclic subgroup, having “large” directed Cayley diameter.
The above table provides small examples, e.g.
if
G
is the order
16
group with GAP identifier
[16,6]
, then
\mathsf{dcd}(G)=6
, and
G
has an order
8
cyclic subgroup
H
, for which
\mathsf{dcd}(H)=7
.

Remark 8.3
.

The equality
\mathsf{dcd}(G)=\mathsf{dcd}^{*}(G)

holds for all abelian groups
G
by
Theorem
3.2
, and for all
non-abelian groups of order at most
42
, as one can observe inspecting the above table. We do not know whether the equality

\mathsf{dcd}(G)=\mathsf{dcd}^{*}(G)
holds for all finite groups
G
.

Remark 8.4
.

The computer calculations were performed on a server with
3.6\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
processors and
1\text{\,}\mathrm{TB}
of RAM memory, running single-threaded implementations in GAP of Algorithm
1
, Algorithm
2
, respectively the algorithms computing the small and large Davenport constants given in
[
5
]
.

The results for the groups of order at most
32
have been obtained in
30
hours of computing time. The results for the remaining groups up to order
39
have been obtained in about two weeks’ time. Computations for groups of order
40
and
42
took about three months, the group with GAP identifier
[40,8]
being the most time-consuming (computation for this group alone took almost 64 days).

Computing the large Davenport constant (using Algorithm 2 from
[
5
]
) was the most resource-consuming (in terms of computing-time and memory). Unfortunately, the algorithm did not finish in time to provide the value for
\mathsf{D}(A_{5})
. We are working on a parallel implementation and further optimizations to compute all these constants for groups of order above
42
.

9.
Some known results on the Davenport constants

The small Davenport constant for non-abelian groups was introduced in
[
19
]
, where it was shown that the inequality
\mathsf{d}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}|G|
holds for any non-cyclic group
G
, with equality when
G
has a cyclic index
2
subgroup.
The large Davenport constant was introduced in
[
9
]
, where it was proved that
if
G
has a cyclic index
2
subgroup, then

\mathsf{D}(G)=\mathsf{d}(G)+|G^{\prime}|
;
here
G^{\prime}
stands for the commutator subgroup of
G
.
Bounds on
\mathsf{d}(G)
in terms of
|G|
and the smallest prime divisor
p
of
|G|
were given in a sequence of papers (see
[
13
]
,
[
8
]
).
To the best of our knowledge, the strongest published bound of this type is given in
[
21
]
,
where it is proved that

\mathsf{d}(G)\leq|G|/p+p-2
.
