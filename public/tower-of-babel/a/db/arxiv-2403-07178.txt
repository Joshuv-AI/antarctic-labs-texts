Phase transitions and minimal interfaces on manifolds with conical singularities August 8, 2026
Daniel Grieser, Sina Held, Hannes Uecker, Boris Vertman

Abstract
Using Γ \Gamma –convergence, we study the Cahn-Hilliard problem with interface width parameter ε > 0 \varepsilon{>}0 for phase transitions on manifolds with conical singularities. We prove that minimizers of the corresponding energy functional exist and converge, as ε → 0 \varepsilon{\to}0 , to a function that takes only two values with an interface along a hypersurface that has minimal area among those satisfying a volume constraint. In a numerical example, we use continuation and bifurcation methods to study families of critical points at small ε > 0 \varepsilon{>}0 on 2D elliptical cones, parameterized by height and ellipticity of the base. Some of these critical points are minimizers with interfaces crossing the cone tip. On the other hand, we prove that interfaces which are minimizers of the perimeter functional, corresponding to ε = 0 \varepsilon=0 , never pass through the cone tip for general cones with angle less than 2 ​ π 2\pi . Thus tip minimizers for finite ε > 0 \varepsilon{>}0 must become saddles as ε → 0 \varepsilon{\to}0 , and we numerically identify the associated bifurcation, finding a delicate interplay of ε > 0 \varepsilon{>}0 and the cone parameters in our example.

1.6
Some related problems

By Cahn–Hilliard
problem
we denote the elliptic equation (
1.4
a)
together with the mass constraint (
1.4
b). The dynamic Cahn–Hilliard
equation
(in Euclidean space)
is the mass conserving flow

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}u=\nabla\cdot[\nabla\delta_{u}E_{\varepsilon}(u)]=-\Delta[\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u-W^{\prime}(u)],

(1.7)

also called
H^{-1}
gradient flow,
where
\delta_{u}
denotes the variational derivative.
For zero-flux boundary conditions, i.e.,
{\partial}_{n}u={\partial}_{n}\Delta u=0
on
{\partial}\Omega
,
this conserves the mass
\int_{\Omega}u\,{\rm d}x
, and steady states of (
1.7
)
fulfill
\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u-W^{\prime}(u)=\lambda
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and hence
are solutions of (
1.4
).

See
[
Ell89
]
for basic results
on existence of solutions of (
1.7
) (in 2D flat domains),
their numerical approximation,
and their basic dynamical behavior, which can roughly be characterized as
follows: Starting from essentially random initial data (with mass
0
),
the solution rapidly evolves to a fine grained structure with complex
interfaces between the phases
u=\pm 1
, also aptly called “fat spaghettis”.
After this initial phase, a slow coarsening process sets in, during
which interfaces move and disappear (regions of pure phases
u\approx 1
or
u\approx-1
coming together), on longer and longer time scales.
See also
[
Mir19
,
DF20
]
for comprehensive reviews of
other Cahn–Hilliard type
equations used to describe diffusive interfaces in a variety of
settings and applications, and of their analytical and numerical treatment.

The parabolic Allen-Cahn equation is given by

\partial_{t}u-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u+W^{\prime}(u)=0,

again with Neumann boundary conditions at
\partial M
.
For small
\varepsilon
, the level sets of
u
concentrate around an interface that
evolves in time under a generalized mean curvature flow
[
ESS92
]
. However,
the mass is in general not conserved. This holds for

\partial_{t}u-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u+(W^{\prime}(u)-\langle W^{\prime}(u)\rangle)=0,

for which solutions converge to a volume preserving mean curvature flow

[
CHL10
]
.
It should be interesting to transfer such results to the case
of manifolds with conical singularities too. See

[
RS13
,
Ver16
]
for well–posedness results for the Allen–Cahn
and Cahn–Hilliard
equations on singular manifolds.

2.1
Existence and regularity of solutions

The results of this subsection in fact hold for general incomplete

(M,g)

This goes well beyond compact manifolds with boundary and conical singularities.
Note that, when we talk about manifolds with boundary here then the letter

2.1.1
Self-adjoint extensions of the Laplacian

Consider the gradient
\nabla
on
(M,g)
, mapping smooth functions
C^{\infty}_{0}(M)

to smooth sections of the tangent bundle
C^{\infty}_{0}(M,TM)
. We write
\Delta=-\nabla^{t}\nabla
for the (negative)
Laplace Beltrami operator. If
M
is a closed compact manifold, then the usual Sobolev spaces

\displaystyle H^{1}(M):=\{u\in L^{2}(M):\nabla u\in L^{2}(TM)\},

\displaystyle H^{2}(M):=\{u\in H^{1}(M):\nabla^{2}u\in L^{2}(\Lambda^{2}TM)\},

define unique closed, and in the latter case unique self-adjoint, extensions of

\nabla
and
\Delta
, respectively. If
(M,g)
is non-compact, the extensions may not longer
be unique and we shall now employ the formalism of minimal and maximal
extensions, as in the seminal work by Brüning-Lesch
[
BL92
]
.

The maximal and minimal closed (with respect to the graph norm)
extensions
\nabla_{\max},\nabla_{\min}
are defined by the respective domains

\begin{split}&\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max}):=\{u\in L^{2}(M):\nabla u\in L^{2}(TM)\}=H^{1}(M),\\
&\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min}):=\{u\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max}):\exists\{u_{n}\}_{n}\subset C^{\infty}_{0}(M):\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad u_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}u,\nabla u_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\nabla u\ \textup{in}\ L^{2}\}.\end{split}

(2.1)

The two extensions define ideal boundary conditions in the sense of Cheeger
[
Che83
]
and Brüning-Lesch
[
BL92
]
and yield
self adjoint extensions of the Laplace Beltrami operator on
(M,g)

\begin{split}&\Delta_{D}:=-\nabla^{*}_{\min}\nabla_{\min},\quad\mathscr{D}(\Delta_{D})=\mathscr{D}(\nabla^{*}_{\min}\nabla_{\min}),\\
&\Delta_{N}:=-\nabla^{*}_{\max}\nabla_{\max},\quad\mathscr{D}(\Delta_{N})=\mathscr{D}(\nabla^{*}_{\max}\nabla_{\max}).\end{split}

(2.2)

The two extensions define the well-known Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions in case
M
is the interior of a compact Riemannian manifold with boundary,
\overline{M}=M\cup\partial M
. We shall be precise: the trace theorem asserts that the obvious restriction,
mapping any
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M})
to
u|_{\partial M}\in C^{\infty}(\partial M)

admits a continuous extension

\displaystyle\textup{tr}:\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max})\to L^{2}(\partial M),

(2.3)

where continuity holds with respect to the graph norm on
\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max})

and the
L^{2}
space on the right is defined with respect to the restriction of
g
to
\partial M
.
Similarly, if
\partial_{\nu}
is the unit normal inward vector field on
\partial M
,
extended smoothly to the interior, then
u\mapsto\partial_{\nu}u\restriction\partial M

admits a continuous extension

\displaystyle\textup{tr}\circ\partial_{\nu}:\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max})\to H^{-1/2}(\partial M).

(2.4)

By continuity of the trace we observe

\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})\subseteq\{u\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max})=H^{1}(M):\textup{tr}\,u=0\}=:H^{1}_{0}(M).

(2.5)

The following result is observed in
[
BL92
, §4]
.
If
\overline{M}
is non-compact, e.g. it has ’interior singularities’, and if
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M})
is compactly supported, i.e. supported
away
from the singularities in the interior, then

\begin{split}&\phi\mathscr{D}(\Delta_{D})=\phi\{u\in H^{2}(M):\textup{tr}\,u=0\},\\
&\phi\mathscr{D}(\Delta_{N})=\phi\{u\in H^{2}(M):\textup{tr}\circ\partial_{\nu}\,u=0\}.\end{split}

(2.6)

This motivates the choice of the subscripts D and N that stand for Dirichlet and Neumann
boundary conditions.

2.1.2
Existence of minimizers

We begin with the existence of minimizers. For coercive
functionals on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, this is a classical result, see
e.g.
[
Eva98
, Chapter 8]
. The
proof presented here holds on any finite volume Riemannian manifold,
possibly incomplete, including those with conical singularities.

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider the
function spaces

\begin{split}&\mathscr{A}_{D}:=\{u\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})\cap L^{4}(M):\langle u\rangle=m\},\\
&\mathscr{A}_{N}:=\{u\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max})\cap L^{4}(M):\langle u\rangle=m\}.\end{split}

(2.7)

Then for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
there exist minimizers
u^{\varepsilon}_{D}\in\mathscr{A}_{D}
and
u^{\varepsilon}_{N}\in\mathscr{A}_{N}
such that

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}_{D})=\inf_{u\in\mathscr{A}_{D}}E_{\varepsilon}(u),\quad E_{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}_{N})=\inf_{u\in\mathscr{A}_{N}}E_{\varepsilon}(u).

(2.8)

Proof.

We prove existence of a minimizer
u_{D}\equiv u_{D}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathscr{A}_{D}
. The argument
for the other case is exactly the same, with
u^{\varepsilon}_{D},\nabla_{\min},\mathscr{A}_{D}

replaced by
u^{\varepsilon}_{N},\nabla_{\max},\mathscr{A}_{N}
, respectively.
First, consider a sequence
(u_{n})\subset\mathscr{A}_{D}
such that

\inf_{u\in\mathscr{A}_{D}}E_{\varepsilon}(u)=\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon}(u_{n}).

We want to use the Banach-Alaoglu theorem which states that in every
reflexive Banach space, every bounded sequence admits a weakly convergent subsequence.
First,
\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})
is indeed a reflexive Banach space:
the map
u\mapsto(u,\nabla u)
identifies
\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})

with a closed subspace of
L^{2}(M)\times L^{2}(M,TM)
; the latter is
a reflexive Banach space and thus any closed subspace, such as
\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})
, is a reflexive
Banach space as well.

Second,
(u_{n})\subset\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})
is indeed a bounded
sequence:
E_{\varepsilon}(u)\geq 0
for any
u\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})\cap L^{4}(M)
and hence
the infimum of
E_{\varepsilon}(u)
over
u\in\mathscr{A}_{D}
is finite; therefore
(E_{\varepsilon}(u_{n}))_{n}
has finite limit and in particular
is uniformly bounded. This shows boundedness of
(u_{n})\subset\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})

in the graph norm, where we have used that the
L^{2}
-norm is bounded by the
L^{4}
-norm on manifolds
of finite volume.

Thus by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists a subsequence

(u_{n_{k}})\subset\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})
, which is weakly convergent, i.e.
there exists
u_{*}\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})
such that for any
\phi\in L^{2}(M)
and
\psi\in L^{2}(M,TM)

\begin{split}\int_{M}u_{n_{k}}\phi\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\int_{M}u_{*}\phi\quad\int_{M}g\bigl(\nabla u_{n_{k}};\psi\bigr)\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\int_{M}g\bigl(\nabla u_{*};\psi\bigr).\end{split}

(2.9)

Since
(u^{2}_{n_{k}}-1)\subset L^{2}(M)
is a bounded sequence in a reflexive
Banach space as well, we can apply Banach-Alaoglu again and assume by passing to a
subsequence, that

\int_{M}(u^{2}_{n_{k}}-1)\phi\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\int_{M}(u_{*}^{2}-1)\phi.

Taking the test function
\phi\equiv 1
, we find that weak convergence preserves the mass
constraint (
1.2
), i.e.
\langle u\rangle=m
. Since
L^{2}
-norms are weakly lower semi-continuous, we conclude

\begin{split}&\|\nabla u_{*}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla u_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{2}},\\
&\|u_{*}^{2}-1\|_{L^{2}}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|u^{2}_{n_{k}}-1\|_{L^{2}}.\end{split}

(2.10)

We conclude that
u_{*}\in\mathscr{A}_{D}
is indeed a minimizer, since by (
2.10
)

E_{\varepsilon}(u_{*})=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\sigma}\|\nabla u_{*}\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\|u_{*}^{2}-1\|_{L^{2}}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon}(u_{n_{k}})=\inf_{u\in\mathscr{A}_{D}}E_{\varepsilon}(u).

∎

The same result holds if we impose additional, e.g. generalized Neumann boundary
conditions in the definition of
\mathscr{A}_{N}
, since the trace operator

in (
2.3
)
is continuous in the operator norms and in particular weakly continuous. Thus, weak convergence
preserves boundary conditions.

The existence in Proposition
2.2
is sufficient to
proceed with the convergence of

, and also to justify
the numerics in §

. Thus, rather for completeness
our next result shows that minimizers are in fact strong solutions of the Allen-Cahn equation.
We do not proceed as in Evans

, but rather present an approach
adapted to the present possibly singular setting. Consider the Lagrangian

as in (

)
and note that a minimizer

satisfies

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}L(u+s\phi,\lambda)\equiv\varepsilon^{2}\int_{M}g(\nabla u,\nabla\phi)+\int_{M}W^{\prime}(u)\phi-\int_{M}\lambda\phi=0,

(2.11)

where we vary among

if

In other words,

\mathscr{A}_{D}
or

is a weak solution to the Allen-Cahn equation (

),
with the test functions
\phi

\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min})
or
\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max})
, respectively.
Note that the intersection with

is no longer needed for the test functions.

Proposition 2.3
.

Consider any
u\in H^{1}(M)\cap L^{4}(M)
.

1.

Let
u
be a weak solution to the Allen-Cahn equation (
1.4
),
with test functions
\phi\in\mathscr{D}(\ \nabla_{\min})\subseteq H^{1}_{0}(M)

and in particular,
\textup{tr}\,u=0
.

2.

Let
u
be a weak solution to the Allen-Cahn equation (
1.4
),
with test functions
\phi\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max})\equiv H^{1}(M)
. Then
u\in\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\max}^{*}\nabla_{\max})

and in particular,
\textup{tr}\circ\partial_{\nu}\,u=0
.

Proof.

We shall prove the first statement, the second one being verbatim with
\nabla_{\min}
and
\Delta_{D}

replaced by
\nabla_{\max}
and
\Delta_{N}
, respectively.
We may rewrite the Allen-Cahn equation (

) as

\Delta u=\varepsilon^{-2}\Big(W^{\prime}(u)-\lambda\Big)
. Hence

is a weak stationary (i.e. time independent)
solution to the inhomogeneous heat equation

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\Delta)\,\omega=-\varepsilon^{-2}\Big(W^{\prime}(u)-\lambda\Big).

(2.12)

Consider the self-adjoint extension
\Delta_{D}=-\nabla_{\min}^{*}\nabla_{\min}

of the Laplace Beltrami operator on
(M,g)
, with domain
\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min}^{*}\nabla_{\min})
.
Consider the heat semigroup
e^{t\Delta_{D}}
generated by
\Delta_{D}
.
A solution to (
2.12
) with initial condition
\omega(0)=u
is given
in terms of the heat semigroup by

\displaystyle\omega:=e^{t\Delta_{D}}u-\varepsilon^{-2}e^{t\Delta_{D}}*\Big(W^{\prime}(u)-\lambda\Big),

(2.13)

where

indicates convolution in time. The proof now proceeds by studying regularity of
\omega

and then proving
\omega\equiv u
. Since

and the domain of
\Delta_{D}
is dense in

\omega
is a mild solution to

[

[

, we conclude
that

is in fact a classical solution, i.e. for any

\displaystyle\omega\in C\Big((0,T],\mathscr{D}(\Delta_{D})\Big)\cap C^{1}\Big((0,T],L^{2}(M)\Big).

(2.14)

Since
\mathscr{D}(\Delta_{D})=\mathscr{D}(\nabla_{\min}^{*}\nabla_{\min})
, we can integrate by
parts for any

\displaystyle\int_{M}g\big(\Delta_{D}\,\omega,\phi\big)=-\int_{M}g\big(\nabla\omega,\nabla\phi\big).

(2.15)

It remains to show

. By construction,

solves the following equation

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)(\omega-u)=0,\quad\omega(0)=u,

weakly, i.e. for any

we have (cf. (

From here we compute by plugging

into (

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\Big\|\,\omega-u\,\Big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}&=2\int_{M}\big(\partial_{t}\,\omega\big)(\omega-u)\\
&=-\int_{M}g\Big(\nabla(\omega-u),\nabla(\omega-u)\Big)=-\Big\|\nabla(\omega-u)\Big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq 0.\end{split}

Thus, if

is continuous as

, then

) together with

implies that

). Hence it remains to establish

at

\displaystyle\Big\|e^{t\Delta_{D}}*\Big(W^{\prime}(u)-\lambda\Big)\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\Big\|e^{(t-\widetilde{t})\Delta_{D}}*\Big(W^{\prime}(u)-\lambda\Big)\Big\|_{L^{2}}d\widetilde{t}\xrightarrow{t\to 0}0.

By the Lumer-Phillips theorem
[

, Theorem 3.1]
, the heat operator

is a strongly continuous semigroup, generated by
\Delta_{D}
. Indeed,
\Delta_{D}\leq 0
is dissipative

[
LP61
, (1.1)]
and the image of

is

\Delta_{D}

by strong continuity of

\displaystyle\Big\|e^{t\Delta_{D}}u-u\Big\|_{L^{2}}\xrightarrow{t\to 0}0.

In view of (

The statement now follows from (

∎

We want to extend the Modica-Mortola Theorem in

Let

Theorem 2.5

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}

4.1
The setup, and basic notions from bifurcation theory

To illustrate Theorem

and study

in a singular setting we
use the truncated cones of height
h
and
semi axes
1
and
a\geq 1

as in (

The metric determinant is
g=a^{2}+h^{2}(x^{2}+ay^{2})/r^{2}
,
and
dS=\sqrt{g}\,{\rm d}(x,y)
, and the corresponding
Laplace Beltrami operator is given by

\displaystyle\Delta u(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\biggl[

\displaystyle{\partial}_{x}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}(1+h^{2}y^{2}r^{-2}){\partial}_{x}u)-{\partial}_{y}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}h^{2}xyr^{-2}{\partial}_{x}u)

We choose the standard double well potential

problem thus is

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}{\rm(a)}\ \ G(u):=-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u+W^{\prime}(u)-\lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad\partial_{\nu}u=0\text{ at }\partial\Omega,\\
{\rm(b)}\ \ q(u):=\langle u\rangle-m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0.\end{array}

We use the software

[

,
Uec23b
]
to discretize (

obtained algebraic system as a continuation and bifurcation problem.
More specifically, we consider the weak form of (

find critical points of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}(M)
.
For all
m
, (

u\equiv m
,
\lambda=W^{\prime}(m)
, i.e., we have the “trivial branch”
u\equiv m
,
with

. Starting at
m=1
we
first find branch points (BPs) from this trivial branch, and
continue the bifurcating branches to

at fixed

and

,
thus obtaining a
selection of critical points
u
of

at

.
Subsequently we
continue some of these solutions

in other parameters,
including

to check the formula

,
and to altogether identify minimal interfaces (depending on

and

By varying

and

we have a circular base, and
altogether an

equivariant problem, which inter alia means that

•

all bifurcations from the spatially homogeneous branch with
angular dependence are double, i.e. the linearization at the branch point has a
two-dimensional kernel;

•

to continue such branches we need a phase condition to uniquely
choose solutions from the group orbit of rotations.

has methods to deal with these issues, but the situation
is somewhat simpler and more generic if the rotational symmetry is broken.
For elliptic cones, the BPs from the trivial branch are generically simple

[

]

to obtain the bifurcating branches, while
for circular cones we would need equivariant bifurcation theory

[

However, we still have three discrete symmetries:

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}:\quad

\displaystyle(u(x,y),m,\lambda)\mapsto(u(-x,y),m,\lambda),\quad{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\text{ symmetry
over the $y$ axis;}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}:\quad

\displaystyle(u(x,y),m,\lambda)\mapsto(u(x,-y),m,\lambda),\quad{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\text{ symmetry
over the $x$ axis;}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}:\quad

\displaystyle(u(\cdot,\cdot),m,\lambda)\mapsto(-u(\cdot,\cdot),-m,-\lambda),\quad\text{ \begin{tabular}[]{l}${\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$ symmetry in $u$, mass\\
and Lagrange multiplier.\end{tabular}}

of the
problem via composition.

\gamma_{3}
can essentially be exploited to restrict to

the two mirror symmetries

can be used to

Remark 4.2
.

In terms of the notions recalled in Remark
4.1
, the symmetries
\gamma_{1}

and
\gamma_{2}
have the following consequences for bifurcations of nontrivial
branches from the trivial branch
u\equiv m
in (

•

If the solutions
u
on the bifurcating branch satisfy exactly one of the conditions

\gamma_{1}u=u
,
\gamma_{2}u=u
then the bifurcation must be a pitchfork,
as together with
u
also its partner
\gamma_{j}u
must bifurcate,

. This
for instance applies to the branches yielding the tip (T1) and winding

and
\gamma_{2}

cannot bifurcate from the homogeneous branch in a simple BP, i.e.,

\gamma_{1}u=u=\gamma_{2}u
(e.g., “horizontal” solutions like in Fig.

Appendix: Auxiliary aspects of geometric measure theory

A.1
Functions of bounded variation and the co-area formula

Functions of bounded variation are suitable for the study of our

\Gamma
–convergence.
They are defined on so-called
good
metric measure spaces, and
there are two different gradient norms that can both be put into relation.
With both gradient norms and the Hausdorff measure,
one can define the perimeter and establish the co-area formula. Our main sources are
[
Vol10
,
Mir03
,
Nic11
,
FR60
]
.

A.1.1
Metric measure spaces and Hausdorff measure

Definition A.1
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{d},\mu)
be a metric measure space with metric
\mathfrak{d}
and measure
\mu
. It is called good if

(X,\mathfrak{d})
is complete, and
(X,\mathfrak{d},\mu)
is doubling, i.e. if
B_{R}(x)
denotes a ball in
X
of radius
R

centered at
x
, then there exists a uniform constant
c>0
such that for any
x\in X
and any
R>0

\mu(B_{2R}(x))\leq c\cdot\mu(B_{R}(x)).

A central example of such a good metric measure space in our context is
a compact Riemannian manifold. The Riemannian metric defines the distance
\mathfrak{d}_{g}

and the Riemannian Lebesgue measure
d\text{vol}_{g}
. Then
(M,\mathfrak{d}_{g},d\text{vol}_{g})
is a good metric measure space
in the sense of the definition above.
More generally, compact manifolds with boundary and conical singularities, see Definition
B.1
, are good metric measure spaces.
More generally, compact stratified spaces yield good metric measure spaces.

Definition A.2

(Hausdorff measure)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{d},\mu)
be a metric measure space. For any subset
\Omega\subset X
, any
s\in[0,\infty)
define the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{s}(\Omega):=\displaystyle\sup_{\delta>0}\left[\inf\left\{\frac{\pi^{s/2}}{\Gamma(3/2)}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\text{diam}_{\mathfrak{d}}\Omega_{j}}{2}\right)^{s}:\Omega\subset\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{j};\,\text{diam}_{\mathfrak{d}}\Omega_{j}\leq\delta\right\}\right],

where
\text{diam}_{\mathfrak{d}}\Omega_{j}
is the diameter of the smallest metric ball containing
\Omega_{j}
, and
\Gamma(s)
is the Gamma-function.

The constant
\frac{\pi^{s/2}}{\Gamma(3/2)}
ensures that the Hausdorff measure corresponds to the Lebesgue measure
on smooth Riemannian manifolds
(M,g)
if
s=\dim M
. In that case, the Hausdorff measure

\mathcal{H}_{g}^{s}(\Omega)
coincides with
the Riemannian measure of a Borel set
\Omega\subset M
, cf. e.g.
[
Vol10
, Theorem 2.17]
.

A.1.2
Functions of bounded variation

Definition A.3

(Gradient for locally Lipschitz functions)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{d},\mu)
be a metric measure space. For any open
\Omega\subset X

consider the space
\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
of locally Lipschitz functions

u:{\Omega}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. For such
u
and any
x\in X
we define

\lVert\nabla u\rVert(x):=\displaystyle\liminf_{\rho\rightarrow 0}{\left[\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\overline{B_{\rho}(x)}}\frac{\lvert u(x)-u(y)\rvert}{\rho}\right]}.

One can define the gradient norm for locally integrable functions
L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega)

on some open
\Omega
.

Definition A.4

(Gradient of locally integrable functions)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{d},\mu)
be a metric measure space. Let
\Omega{\subset}X
be open and bounded, and

u{\in}L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega)
. Then

\begin{split}\lVert\nabla u\rVert(\Omega)&:=\inf\left\{\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lVert\nabla u_{n}\rVert(x)\,d\mu(x)}:(u_{n})_{n}\subset\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega);u_{n}\xrightarrow{L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)}u\right\}\\
\end{split}

Now, we can define functions of (locally) bounded variation.

Definition A.5
.

The space of functions with (locally) bounded total variation is

•

BV(\Omega):=\left\{u\in L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega):\lVert\nabla u\rVert(\Omega)<\infty\right\}

•

BV_{\text{loc}}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega):\lVert\nabla u\rVert(A)<\infty\text{ for any }A\subset\Omega\text{ open},\right.\\
\left.\overline{A}\subset\Omega\text{ compact }\right\}

The gradient can be computed as a limit for an approximating sequence.

Proposition A.6

(Approximating
\lVert\nabla u\rVert(\Omega)
)
.

For any
u{\in}BV(\Omega)
with
\lVert\nabla u\rVert(\Omega)<\infty
there exists
(u_{n})\subset\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
such that

•

u_{n}\longrightarrow u
in
L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega)
,

•

\lVert\nabla u\rVert(\Omega)=\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lVert\nabla u_{n}\rVert(x)=\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert\nabla u_{n}\rVert(\Omega)
.

Proof.

The statement can be found in
[
Mir03
, p. 984]
.
∎

We will also need the following result.

Theorem A.7

(lower semi-continuity)
.

Suppose
(u_{n})\subset BV(\Omega)
, and we have convergence

u_{n}\longrightarrow u
in
L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega)
as
n\to\infty
. Then

\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{g}(\Omega)\leq\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lVert\nabla u_{n}\rVert_{g}(\Omega)}.

(A.1)

The proof of Theorem
A.7
, see e.g.
[
Vol10
, Theorem 2.38]
,
does not require any conditions on
\Omega
. In particular
\overline{\Omega}\subset M
does not need to be compact.

Definition A.8

(Caccioppoli sets)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{d},\mu)
be a good metric measure space, arising from a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)

of dimension
d
.
Let
E\subset M
be a
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{d}}^{d}
-measurable set.

P_{g}(E,\Omega):=\lVert\nabla\chi_{E}\rVert(\Omega),

\chi_{E}:M\rightarrow\{0,1\}

[

P_{g}(E,M)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{d}}^{d-1}(\partial E).

(A.2)

A.2
\Gamma
-convergence

\Gamma
-convergence is a powerful tool from the calculus of variations in order to study convergence of minimizers.
We will recall the most important definitions and properties of
\Gamma
-convergence based on
[
Rin18
, Ch. 13.1]
.

Definition A.9

(Abstract
\Gamma
-Convergence)
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space. The functional
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called
(sequential)
\Gamma
-limit of the functionals
\mathcal{F}_{k}:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
(denoted by

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:=\Gamma-\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}
),
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if the following two conditions are satisfied.

•

For all sequences
(u_{k})\subset X
the ”
\liminf
-inequality” holds

u=\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}{u_{k}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{F}_{\infty}[u]\leq\displaystyle\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{F}_{k}[u_{k}]}

(A.3)

•

For all
u\in X
there exists as

recovery sequence

(u_{k})\subset X
such that

u=\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}{u_{k}},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\infty}[u]=\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}[u_{k}].

(A.4)

Remark A.10
.

If the first condition in Definition
A.9
is satisfied, then the second condition can be altered to the so
called ”
\limsup
-inequality”: For all
u\in X
there exists a sequence
u_{k}\longrightarrow u
in
X
as
k\rightarrow\infty
such that

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}[u]\geq\displaystyle\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{F}_{k}[u_{k}]}.

(A.5)

An important consequence of
\Gamma
-convergence is that the limit
functional
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\Gamma-\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}
is lower semi-continuous,
cf.
[
Rin18
, Proposition 13.2]
. Moreover,
\Gamma
-convergence implies convergence of minima and the corresponding minimizers.
More specifically we have the following results.

Theorem A.11
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space. Consider a sequence of functionals

\mathcal{F}_{k}:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and assume
that the
\Gamma
-limit

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\Gamma-\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}
exists.

1.

Assume that the functionals
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
are equicoercive,
i.e. there exists a compact set
K\subset X
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{X}\mathcal{F}_{k}=\displaystyle\inf_{K}\mathcal{F}_{k}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then,
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
has a minimizer and

\displaystyle\min_{X}\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\displaystyle\inf_{X}\mathcal{F}_{k}.

In addition, all accumulation points of any precompact sequence
(u_{k})\subset X
with the property

\liminf_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}[u_{k}]=\liminf_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\inf_{X}\mathcal{F}_{k}
are minimizers of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
.

2.

Without the assumption of equicoercivity,
a minimizer for
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
need not exist a priori and we only have: If
a sequence
\{u_{k}\}\subset X
of minimizers for
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}

converges to

, then
u

\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
and
\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow.\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}[u_{k}]=\mathcal{F}_{\infty}[u]
.

Proof.

See e.g.
[
Rin18
, Theorem 13.3]
for the first statement and
[
Mod87
, Proposition 4]
for the second statement.
