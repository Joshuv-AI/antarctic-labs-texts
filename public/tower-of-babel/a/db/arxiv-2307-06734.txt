The cubic Szegő equation on the real line: explicit formula and well-posedness on the Hardy class
Patrick Gérard, Alexander Pushnitski

Abstract
. We establish an explicit formula for the solution of the the cubic Szegő equation on the real line. Using this formula, we prove that the evolution flow of this equation can be continuously extended to the whole Hardy class H 2 H^{2} on the real line.

Primary 47B35, secondary 37K20

1.
Introduction and main result

1.1.
The cubic Szegő equation on
{\mathbb{R}}

For
p\geq 1
, let
H^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
be the standard Hardy class in the upper half-plane, defined as the set of all functions
f=f(z)
, analytic in
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0
and having the finite norm

\lVert f\rVert_{H^{p}({\mathbb{R}})}^{p}=\sup_{y>0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert f(x+iy)\rvert^{p}dx.

We will routinely identify functions
f\in H^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
with their boundary values on the real line, and with this identification one has
H^{p}({\mathbb{R}})\subset L^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
as a closed subspace. If
p=2
, this is a Hilbert space with the inner product (linear in the first argument and anti-linear in the second argument) denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
; this is identical to the restriction of the
L^{2}
inner product.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be the orthogonal projection onto
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.

In
[
19
,
20
]
Pocovnicu, by analogy with the unit circle case
[
5
,
6
]
introduced and studied the
cubic Szegő equation

(1.1)

i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mathbb{P}(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u),\quad u=u(x,t),\quad x,t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Here, for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the function
u(\cdot,t)
belongs to a suitable Sobolev class of functions in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
More precisely, in
[
19
]
it was proven to be well-posed on the intersection of
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
with the Sobolev class
W^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}})
with the smoothness exponent
s\geq 1/2
.
The cubic Szegő equation is easily seen to be the Hamiltonian equation with respect to the symplectic form

\omega(u,v)=\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{2}}

on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and the Hamiltonian

E(u)=\frac{1}{4}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{4}({\mathbb{R}})}^{4}.

Furthermore, following the strategy of the unit circle case
[
5
,
6
]
, it was proven
in
[
19
,
20
]
that this equation is completely integrable and possesses a Lax pair. The Lax pair involves
Hankel operators
on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and will be discussed in Section
2
.

1.2.
Main results

The purpose of this paper is two-fold: to establish an
explicit formula for the solution
of the cubic Szegő equation (
1.1
) and to extend its flow to the whole Hardy space
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
The formula we establish (see Theorem
2.2
) is heavily based on the Lax pair structure for the cubic Szegő equation. It involves the Hankel operator with the symbol
u(\cdot,0)
.
It will be convenient to state this formula in Section
2
after all the ingredients have been introduced.

Here we describe the second main result.
Let
\Phi(t)
,
t>0
, be the (non-linear) flow map of (
1.1
), defined on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap W^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}})
with
s\geq 1/2
, i.e. if
u=u(x,t)
is a solution to (
1.1
), then

\Phi(t)u(\cdot,0)=u(\cdot,t).

Theorem 1.1
.

The flow map
\Phi(t)
can be extended to
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
such the following properties are satisfied:

(i)

\lVert\Phi(t)u\rVert_{H^{2}}=\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}
for all
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
;

(ii)

if
\lVert u_{n}-u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
, then
\lVert\Phi(t)u_{n}-\Phi(t)u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
;

(iii)

the map
t\mapsto\Phi(t)u
is continuous in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
for all
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
;

(iv)

if
u\in H^{4}({\mathbb{R}})
, then
\Phi(t)u\in H^{4}({\mathbb{R}})
and
E(\Phi(t)u)=E(u)
.

An important ingredient of the proof is the fact that our explicit formula for the flow map
\Phi(t)
makes sense on the whole Hardy class
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
This forces us to consider unbounded Hankel operators with symbols in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
in Section
3
. It is convenient to postpone the description of the structure of the rest of the paper until Section
2.6
.

1.3.
The unit circle case

The Szegő equation (
1.1
) was originally introduced in
[
5
,
6
]
, where it was considered for functions
u
defined on the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
.
In this case,
\mathbb{P}
is the orthogonal projection in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
onto the Hardy class
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
.
In fact,
[
5
,
6
]
provided the blueprint for the study of the Lax pair structure of
[
19
,
20
]
.
More precisely, in the unit circle case the Szegő equation is completely integrable and possesses a Lax pair,
which involves a Hankel operator in the Hardy class of the unit disk. There is an explicit formula
[
7
]
for the flow map of the cubic Szegő equation on the unit circle, which involves Hankel operators and is based on the Lax pair structure. Using this formula, in
[
10
]
we have extended the flow map of the cubic Szegő equation on the unit circle to the whole Hardy class
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
.
We were also able to prove that this is sharp in the sense that the flow map does not extend to any Sobolev class with a negative smoothness exponent. We do not know whether Theorem
1.1
is sharp in the same sense.

1.4.
Methods used in the proof

The proof of the explicit formula is based on the Lax pair structure of the cubic Szegő equation and on the commutation properties of Hankel and Toeplitz operators with the infinitesimal generator of the Lax-Beurling semigroup in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, see Section
2
.
It has a different nature compared to the analogous formula
[
7
]
on the unit circle and is not reducible to it.

This difference reflects the fact that despite the similarities between the cubic Szegő equation on the unit circle and on the real line, the dynamics have some very different features. For example, on the real line, for a dense subset of rational initial data solutions, have Sobolev norms tending to infinity as
t\to\infty

[
11
]
, while on the circle, solutions with rational initial data are quasi-periodic
[
7
]
.

Similarly, Theorem
1.1
is not reducible to the corresponding result of
[
10
]
(even though we are using some elements of
[
10
]
).
The main new ingredients include spectral theory of dissipative operators and semigroup theory, combined with the Wold decomposition for contractions.

1.5.
Relation to other results.

Even for integrable PDEs, the existence of an explicit formula for the general solution is a rare phenomenon. However, such a formula has been recently proved in the case of the Benjamin–Ono equation
[
4
]
, both on the unit circle and on the line. Another example is the Calogero–Moser derivative nonlinear Schrödinger equation
[
9
]
and its periodic version, the Calogero–Sutherland equation
[
1
]
, where the explicit formula was used to prove global wellposedness in critical regularity. In the latter case, the explicit formula was used to display growth of Sobolev norms for multisoliton solutions.
Indeed one expects that such explicit formulae can help understand the long time dynamics of integrable PDEs, which is still a widely open problem for many of these equations, in particular for the Szegő equation on the line.

The sharp regularity wellposedness for other integrable Hamiltonian PDEs has recently stimulated the development of new methods based on the Lax pair structure, see in particular
[
18
]
for the Davey–Stewartson equation,
[
17
,
12
,
16
]

for cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equations,
[
13
]
for the derivative nonlinear Schrödinger equation, and
[
8
,
15
]
for the Benjamin–Ono equation.

Finally, let us mention that the cubic Szegő equation on the line recently appeared
[
2
]
in the description, through modified scattering, of the long time dynamics of the weakly dispersive nonlinear cubic Schrödinger equation on the plane
{\mathbb{R}}_{x}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{y}
,

i\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}^{2}u-|D_{y}|u=\lambda|u|^{2}u\ .

1.6.
Notation

The upper half-plane is denoted by
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0\}
, and the unit disk by
{\mathbb{D}}=\{\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}:\lvert\zeta\rvert<1\}
.
The Hardy class in the unit disk
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
is defined as the set of all functions
f
analytic in the unit disk and having the finite norm

\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})}^{2}:=\sup_{r<1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lvert f(re^{i\theta})\rvert^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}.

We denote by
S
the standard shift operator in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, acting by
Sf(\zeta)=\zeta f(\zeta)
, and let
S^{*}
is its adjoint.

We denote by
\mathcal{R}
the class of rational functions in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
; explicitly, elements of
\mathcal{R}
are functions of the form
p(z)/q(z)
, where
p
and
q
are polynomials with
\deg p<\deg q
and all zeros of
q
are in the lower half-plane
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z<0
.
For any
a\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we denote by
v_{a}
the reproducing kernel of
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
:

v_{a}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{\overline{a}-x},

so that
f(a)=\langle f,v_{a}\rangle
for any
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Observe that

\lVert v_{a}\rVert^{2}=\langle v_{a},v_{a}\rangle=v_{a}(a)=\frac{1}{4\pi\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,a}.

The Fourier transform on the real line is defined by

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-ix\xi}dx\,.

The identity operator is denoted by
I
. For elements
\psi,\varphi
in a Hilbert space, we denote by
\langle\cdot,\psi\rangle\varphi
the rank one operator in this space acting as
f\mapsto\langle f,\psi\rangle\varphi
.

We freely use the operator theoretic background collected in Appendix A.

2.
Explicit formula for the flow map
\Phi(t)

The main result of this section is Theorem
2.2
, which gives an explicit formula for the flow map
\Phi(t)
.
We start with some preliminaries, introducing the objects that enter this explicit formula.

2.1.
The Lax-Beurling semigroup

In the space
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, we consider the Lax-Beurling semigroup
S_{\tau}
,

(S_{\tau}f)(z)=e^{i\tau z}f(z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+},\quad\tau>0,

and its adjoint
S_{\tau}^{*}
. We denote by
A
the infinitesimal generator of
S_{\tau}
; it has the domain

\Dom A=\{f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):\tfrac{d}{d\xi}\widehat{f}(\xi)\in L^{2}(0,\infty),\ \widehat{f}(0)=0\}\,,

and acts by
Af(z)=zf(z)
for
f\in\Dom A
.
The adjoint
A^{*}
has the domain

\Dom A^{*}=\{f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):\tfrac{d}{d\xi}\widehat{f}(\xi)\in L^{2}(0,\infty)\}\,.

Remark
.

One can understand the difference between
\Dom A
and
\Dom A^{*}
by looking at the Fourier images
\widehat{S}_{\tau}
and
\widehat{S}_{\tau}^{*}
, which act as right and left shifts on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
. Their generators correspond to differentiation. Now observe that if
f
is smooth on
[0,\infty)
, extended by zero to
(-\infty,0)
, and
\tau\to 0
, then
\frac{1}{\tau}(f(x+\tau)-f(x))
converges to
f^{\prime}(x)
for all
x>0
, while

\frac{1}{\tau}(f(x-\tau)-f(x))
has a
\delta
-type singularity near
x=0
unless
f(0)=0
.

We also illustrate the difference between
\Dom A
and
\Dom A^{*}
by looking at the set of rational functions
\mathcal{R}\subset H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. We have
\mathcal{R}\subset\Dom A^{*}
, while
\mathcal{R}\not\subset\Dom A
; indeed,
f\in\mathcal{R}
belongs to
\Dom A
if and only if
f(x)=O(1/x^{2})
as
x\to\infty
.

For any point
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, the operators
A-\overline{z_{0}}
and
A^{*}-z_{0}
are boundedly invertible; we will need formulas for the inverses:

(2.1)

(A-\overline{z_{0}})^{-1}f(z)=(z-\overline{z_{0}})^{-1}f(z),\quad(A^{*}-z_{0})^{-1}f(z)=\frac{f(z)-f(z_{0})}{z-z_{0}}.

2.2.
Hankel and Toeplitz operators

For a
symbol

u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
we define the anti-linear Hankel operator
H_{u}
on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
by

H_{u}f=\mathbb{P}(u\overline{f}),\quad f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}});

the conjugate of
f
is always taken on the real line.
It is clear that the boundedness of
u
ensures that the operator
H_{u}
is bounded on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Observe that
H_{u}
satisfies the symmetry relation

\langle H_{u}f,g\rangle_{H^{2}}=\langle\mathbb{P}(u\overline{f}),g\rangle_{H^{2}}=\langle u\overline{f},g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle u\overline{g},f\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle H_{u}g,f\rangle_{H^{2}}.

We will often use the square
H_{u}^{2}
; while
H_{u}
is anti-linear, its square is linear. Moreover, by the above symmetry relation we have

\langle H_{u}^{2}f,f\rangle=\langle H_{u}(H_{u}f),f\rangle=\langle H_{u}f,H_{u}f\rangle\geq 0,

and so
H_{u}^{2}
is a self-adjoint positive semi-definite operator.

For a symbol
\varphi\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
, the Toeplitz operator
T_{\varphi}
on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is defined by

T_{\varphi}f=\mathbb{P}(\varphi f),\quad f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}).

Again, the boundedness of
\varphi
ensures that
T_{\varphi}
is bounded on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.

2.3.
Lax pair for the cubic Szegő equation

We use the following result of
[
19
]
:

Theorem 2.1
.

[
19
]

Let
u_{t}(x)=u(x,t)
be a solution to the cubic Szegő equation with
u_{0}\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then the Hankel operator
H_{u_{t}}
satisfies the equation

(2.2)

\frac{d}{dt}H_{u_{t}}=[B_{u_{t}},H_{u_{t}}]\ ,

where
B_{u_{t}}
is the skew-adjoint operator

B_{u}=-iT_{|u|^{2}}+\frac{i}{2}H_{u}^{2}\ .

2.4.
The functional
I_{+}

For
f\in\Dom A^{*}
, one may define

I_{+}(f):=\widehat{f}(0^{+})\ .

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
be the
approximate identity

\chi_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{1-i\varepsilon x}\ .

Then by Plancherel’s theorem we have

\langle f,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{-\xi/\varepsilon}d\xi,

and therefore

(2.3)

I_{+}(f)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\langle f,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

for any
f\in\Dom A^{*}
.

Here is another way of expressing
I_{+}
on the domain of
A^{*}
. Fix
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and write an arbitrary element in
\Dom A^{*}
as
(A^{*}-z)^{-1}f
for
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
; then by (
2.1
),

\displaystyle I_{+}((A^{*}-z)^{-1}f)

\displaystyle=x\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\langle(A^{*}-z)^{-1}f,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\langle f,(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

(2.4)

\displaystyle=2\pi i\langle f,v_{z}\rangle=2\pi if(z).

2.5.
Explicit formula for the flow map

We fix
t>0
once and for all.
For a symbol
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
we introduce the notation

(2.5)

L_{u}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\cdot,\mathrm{e}^{-isH_{u}^{2}}u\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-isH_{u}^{2}}u\,ds\,.

Here the integrand is a self-adjoint rank one operator, and therefore
L_{u}(t)
is a self-adjoint trace class operator.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Phi(t)
be the flow map defined on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap W^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}})
with
s>1/2
.
Then, for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and every
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap W^{s,2}({\mathbb{R}})
,

(2.6)

\boxed{(\Phi(t)u_{0})(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}I_{+}\left[\left(A^{*}+L_{u_{0}}(t)-zI\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0}\right].}

Some remarks are in order concerning the meaning of formula (
2.6
). Since
L_{u_{0}}(t)
is bounded and self-adjoint, the operator
A+L_{u_{0}}(t)
, defined on
\Dom A
, is maximal dissipative (see e.g.
[
3
, Section 3.1]
). It follows that
-(A^{*}+L_{u_{0}}(t))
is also maximal dissipative and therefore the operator
A^{*}+L_{u_{0}}(t)-zI
in (
2.6
) has a bounded inverse. Furthermore,

\Ran(A^{*}+L_{u_{0}}(t)-zI)^{-1}=\Dom(A^{*}+L_{u_{0}}(t)-zI)=\Dom A^{*}

and so the functional
I_{+}
in (
2.6
) is well-defined.

We also observe that in the context of this theorem, we require
s>1/2
because in the course of the proof we need the boundedness of
u
.

2.6.
The structure of the rest of the paper

2.7.
Two auxiliary identities

The following lemma can be found in
[
23
,
9
]
, but for completeness we give an alternative proof below.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
b\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then for every
f\in\Dom A^{*}
, we have
T_{b}f\in\Dom A^{*}
and

[A^{*},T_{b}]f=\frac{i}{2\pi}I_{+}(f)\mathbb{P}b\ .

Proof.

Step 1:

Assume
b\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. Let us prove that for every
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
,

[T_{b},(A^{*}-i)^{-1}]f(z)=-f(i)(A^{*}-i)^{-1}b(z).

In this case for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we have
T_{b}f(z)=b(z)f(z)
and using the explicit formula (
2.1
) for the resolvent of
A^{*}
, we find

\displaystyle T_{b}(A^{*}-i)^{-1}f(z)

\displaystyle-(A^{*}-i)^{-1}T_{b}f(z)=b(z)\frac{f(z)-f(i)}{z-i}-\frac{b(z)f(z)-b(i)f(i)}{z-i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{b(z)-b(i)}{z-i}f(i)=-f(i)(A^{*}-i)^{-1}b(z),

as claimed.

Step 2:
Assume
b\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. Let us prove that

[T_{\overline{b}},(A^{*}-i)^{-1}]=0.

Indeed, for any
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and for any
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we find

\displaystyle\langle[T_{\overline{b}},(A^{*}-i)^{-1}]f,v_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T_{\overline{b}}(A^{*}-i)^{-1}f,v_{z}\rangle-\langle(A^{*}-i)^{-1}T_{\overline{b}}f,v_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(A^{*}-i)^{-1}f,bv_{z}\rangle-\langle T_{\overline{b}}f,(A+i)^{-1}v_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,(A+i)^{-1}bv_{z}\rangle-\langle f,b(A+i)^{-1}v_{z}\rangle=0,

as claimed.

Step 3:

Let
b\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. Let us prove that for any
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
,

(2.7)

[T_{b},(A^{*}-i)^{-1}]f=-f(i)(A^{*}-i)^{-1}\mathbb{P}b.

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
b_{\varepsilon}
be the convolution of
b
with the Poisson kernel,

b_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{b(y)}{(x-y)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}dy.

Then for each
\varepsilon>0
, we have
b_{\varepsilon}=b_{\varepsilon,+}+b_{\varepsilon,-}
, where
b_{\varepsilon,+}=\mathbb{P}b_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\overline{b_{\varepsilon,-}}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. Combining the two previous steps, we find

(2.8)

[T_{b_{\varepsilon}},(A^{*}-i)^{-1}]f=-f(i)(A^{*}-i)^{-1}\mathbb{P}b_{\varepsilon}.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have
\lVert\mathbb{P}b_{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{P}b\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
and
T_{b_{\varepsilon}}\to T_{b}
in the weak operator topology. Passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0_{+}
in (
2.8
), we arrive at (
2.7
).

Step 4:

Let us apply (
2.7
) to the vector
f=(A^{*}-i)g
for some
g\in\Dom A^{*}
. We find

T_{b}g-(A^{*}-i)^{-1}T_{b}(A^{*}-i)g=-f(i)(A^{*}-i)^{-1}\mathbb{P}b,

and by (
2.4
)

f(i)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}I_{+}(g).

It follows that
T_{b}g\in\Dom A^{*}
and

A^{*}T_{b}g-T_{b}A^{*}g=(A^{*}-i)T_{b}g-T_{b}(A^{*}-i)g=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}I_{+}(g)\mathbb{P}b,

as required.
∎

Below
B_{u}=-iT_{|u|^{2}}+\frac{i}{2}H_{u}^{2}
is the operator from Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
; then for every
f\in\Dom A^{*}
, we have

B_{u}f\in\Dom A^{*}
and
H_{u}^{2}f\in\Dom A^{*}
and

[{A^{*}},B_{u}]f=-\frac{i}{2}[{A^{*}},H_{u}^{2}]f+\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle f,u\rangle u\,.

Proof.

Let us first check the identity

H_{u}^{2}=T_{|u|^{2}}-T_{u}T_{\overline{u}}.

Evaluating the quadratic forms, we find

\displaystyle\langle H_{u}^{2}f,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathbb{P}(u\overline{f})\rVert^{2}=\lVert u\overline{f}\rVert^{2}-\lVert\mathbb{P}(\overline{u}f)\rVert^{2}=\lVert\overline{u}f\rVert^{2}-\lVert\mathbb{P}(\overline{u}f)\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\lvert u\rvert^{2}f,f\rangle-\lVert T_{\overline{u}}f\rVert^{2}=\langle T_{\lvert u\rvert^{2}}f,f\rangle-\langle T_{u}T_{\overline{u}}f,f\rangle,

as claimed.

By Lemma
2.3
, it follows that both
H_{u}^{2}
and
B_{u}
map
\Dom A^{*}
into itself.
Furthermore

B_{u}+\frac{i}{2}H_{u}^{2}=-iT_{|u|^{2}}+iH_{u}^{2}=-iT_{u}T_{\overline{u}}

and so for
f\in\Dom A^{*}
,

[{A^{*}},B_{u}]f=-\frac{i}{2}[{A^{*}},H_{u}^{2}]f-i[{A^{*}},T_{u}T_{\overline{u}}]f\,.

Now using Lemma
2.3
again, we find

[A^{*},T_{u}T_{\overline{u}}]f=[A^{*},T_{u}]T_{\overline{u}}f+T_{u}[A^{*},T_{\overline{u}}]f=\frac{i}{2\pi}I_{+}(T_{\overline{u}}f)u,

and

I_{+}(T_{\overline{u}}f)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle T_{\overline{u}}f,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle f,u\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\langle f,u\rangle\,.

Putting this together gives the required identity.
∎

2.8.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Proceeding as in
[
7
]
, we introduce the family
U(t)
of unitary operators on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
defined by the linear initial value problem

U^{\prime}(t)=B_{{u_{t}}}U(t)\ ,\ U(0)=I\ .

Using the Lax pair equation (
2.2
), we observe that

\frac{d}{dt}U(t)^{*}H_{{u_{t}}}U(t)=-U(t)^{*}B_{u_{t}}H_{u_{t}}U(t)+U(t)^{*}[B_{u_{t}},H_{u_{t}}]U(t)+U(t)^{*}H_{u_{t}}B_{u_{t}}U(t)=0

and therefore

U(t)^{*}H_{{u_{t}}}U(t)=H_{u_{0}}.

Next, we have

(2.9)

\frac{d}{dt}U(t)^{*}\chi_{\varepsilon}=-U(t)^{*}B_{u_{t}}\chi_{\varepsilon}=iU(t)^{*}(T_{\lvert u_{t}\rvert^{2}}\chi_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}H_{u_{t}}^{2}\chi_{\varepsilon}).

For the r.h.s. here we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}T_{\lvert u_{t}\rvert^{2}}\chi_{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{P}(\lvert u_{t}\rvert^{2})=H_{u_{t}}u_{t},

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}H_{u_{t}}^{2}\chi_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}H_{u_{t}}(H_{u_{t}}\chi_{\varepsilon})=H_{u_{t}}u_{t},

and therefore the r.h.s. of (
2.9
) converges to

\frac{i}{2}U(t)^{*}H_{u_{t}}u_{t}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}U(t)^{*}H_{u_{t}}^{2}\chi_{\varepsilon}=\frac{i}{2}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}H_{u_{0}}^{2}U(t)^{*}\chi_{\varepsilon}.

By integrating in time, we infer

U(t)^{*}\chi_{\varepsilon}-\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}\chi_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0

in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Applying
H_{u_{0}}
to both sides here, we find

\displaystyle H_{u_{0}}U(t)^{*}\chi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=U(t)^{*}H_{u_{t}}\chi_{\varepsilon}\to U(t)^{*}u_{t}\,,

\displaystyle H_{u_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}\chi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}H_{u_{0}}\chi_{\varepsilon}\to\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0}\,,

and therefore

(2.10)

U(t)^{*}u_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0}\,.

Then using Lemma
2.4
and (
2.10
), we calculate

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}U(t)^{*}A^{*}U(t)

\displaystyle=U(t)^{*}[{A^{*}},B_{{u_{t}}}]U(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}U(t)^{*}[{A^{*}},H_{{u_{t}}}^{2}]U(t)+\frac{1}{2\pi}U(t)^{*}\bigl(\langle\cdot,{u_{t}}\rangle{u_{t}}\bigr)U(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}[U(t)^{*}A^{*}U(t),H_{u_{0}}^{2}]+\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle\cdot,\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0}\ .

Integrating this ODE and recalling the definition (
2.5
) of
L_{u_{0}}(t)
as an integral of rank one operators, we get

U(t)^{*}A^{*}U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}\left({A^{*}}+L_{u_{0}}(t)\right)\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}\ .

Plugging this information into formula (
2.4
) with
f=u_{t}
, we infer

\displaystyle u_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\langle\left({A^{*}}-zI\right)^{-1}{u_{t}},\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\langle\left(U(t)^{*}A^{*}U(t)-zI\right)^{-1}U(t)^{*}{u_{t}},U(t)^{*}\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\langle\left(\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}\left({A^{*}}+L_{u_{0}}(t)\right)\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}-zI\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0},\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{t}{2}H_{u_{0}}^{2}}\chi_{\varepsilon}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\langle\left({A^{*}}+L_{u_{0}}(t)-zI\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0},\chi_{\varepsilon}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}I_{+}\left[\left({A^{*}}+L_{u_{0}}(t)-zI\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0}\right]\ .

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is complete. ∎

3.
Unbounded Hankel operators in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})

3.1.
The set-up and the key result

We need to extend the notion of Hankel operators
H_{u}
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
to the case of symbols
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
In this case the operators
H_{u}
may become unbounded and so we need to discuss the issues of domains, closability etc.

Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. We define the Hankel operator
H_{u}f=\mathbb{P}(u\overline{f})
on the domain

(3.1)

\Dom H_{u}=\{f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):\mathbb{P}(u\overline{f})\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\}.

Here condition
\mathbb{P}(u\overline{f})\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
should be understood as follows: for the product
h:=u\overline{f}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, the Fourier transform
\widehat{h}
, restricted onto the positive semi-axis, belongs to
L^{2}(0,\infty)
.

We will also need a dense in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
subset of “nice” functions that are in the domain of every Hankel operator; we use the set of all rational functions
\mathcal{R}\subset H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
for this purpose.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, and let
H_{u}f=\mathbb{P}(u\overline{f})
be the anti-linear Hankel operator with the domain (
3.1
). Then:

(i)

H_{u}
is closed, i.e.
\Dom H_{u}
is closed with respect to the graph norm of
H_{u}
,

\lVert f\rVert_{H_{u}}:=\bigl(\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}+\lVert H_{u}f\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\,;

(ii)

the set of rational functions
\mathcal{R}
is dense in
\Dom H_{u}
with respect to the graph norm;

(iii)

for any
f,g\in\Dom H_{u}
, we have

\langle H_{u}f,g\rangle=\langle H_{u}g,f\rangle;

(iv)

suppose for some
f,h\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
we have

\langle H_{u}g,f\rangle=\langle h,g\rangle,\quad\forall g\in\Dom H_{u}.

Then
f\in\Dom H_{u}
and
H_{u}f=h
.

The proof is given at the end of this section.

3.2.
Hankel operators in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})

In
[
10
]
, we proved analogous results for Hankel operators acting on the Hardy space of the unit disk
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
.
Our proof of Theorem
3.1
consists of mapping the results of
[
10
]
onto our situation using the conformal map from the unit disc to the upper half-plane. We start by introducing relevant notation for the unit circle case.

Let
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
be the standard Hardy class of the unit disk, and let
\overset{\circ}{\mathbb{P}}:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})\to H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
be the orthogonal projection onto this space (we will supply some notation related to the unit circle case with a circle above the letter). For a symbol
\overset{\circ}{u}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, we denote by
\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
the (possibly unbounded) anti-linear Hankel operator in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, defined by

(3.2)

\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}f=\overset{\circ}{\mathbb{P}}(\overset{\circ}{u}\overline{f})\,,

with the domain

(3.3)

\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}=\{f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}}):\overset{\circ}{\mathbb{P}}(\overset{\circ}{u}\overline{f})\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})\}.

Here condition
\overset{\circ}{\mathbb{P}}(\overset{\circ}{u}\overline{f})\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
should be understood as follows: for the function
h=\overset{\circ}{u}\overline{f}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
, the Fourier coefficients
\widehat{h}_{n}
with
n\geq 0
are square-summable. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all polynomials in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
; it plays the role of the dense set of “nice” functions in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, which are in the domain of every Hankel operator.

We quote the relevant results of
[
10
]
in this notation.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\overset{\circ}{u}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, and let
\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
be the Hankel operator defined by (
3.2
) with the domain (
3.3
).
Then:

(i)

\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
is closed, i.e.
\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
is closed with respect to the graph norm of
\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
;

(ii)

the set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}
is dense in
\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
with respect to the graph norm;

(iii)

for any
f,g\in\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
, we have

\langle\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}f,g\rangle=\langle\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}g,f\rangle;

(iv)

suppose for some
f,h\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
we have

\langle\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}g,f\rangle=\langle h,g\rangle,\quad\forall g\in\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}.

Then

and
\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}f=h
.

3.3.
Conformal map

Let
\varphi
be the standard conformal map from the upper half-plane onto the unit disk,
\varphi(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i}
, and let
W
be the corresponding unitary map from
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
onto
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
,

(3.4)

(Wf)(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}(z+i)}f(\varphi(z)),\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}.

We denote by
S
be the standard shift operator in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and let
\overset{\circ}{u}=S^{*}(u\circ\varphi^{-1})
. Then
for any
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, the inclusions
f\in\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
and
Wf\in\Dom H_{u}
are equivalent.
Furthermore, for any
f\in\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
, the identity

(3.5)

W^{*}H_{u}Wf=\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}f

holds true.

Before embarking on the proof, we note that by a change of variable,

(3.6)

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lvert u\circ\varphi^{-1}(e^{i\theta})\rvert^{2}d\theta=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\lvert u(x)\rvert^{2}}{1+x^{2}}dx,

we see that
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
implies that
u\circ\varphi^{-1}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
and therefore
\overset{\circ}{u}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
and
h\in\mathcal{P}
; by a change of variable we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle u(x)\overline{Wf(x)}\overline{Wh(x)}dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(x)\overline{f(\varphi(x))}\overline{h(\varphi(x))}\frac{dx}{\pi(x-i)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x+i}{x-i}u(x)\overline{f(\varphi(x))}\overline{h(\varphi(x))}\frac{dx}{\pi(x^{2}+1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-i\theta}u(\varphi^{-1}(e^{i\theta}))\overline{f(e^{i\theta})}\overline{h(e^{i\theta})}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\overset{\circ}{u}(e^{i\theta})\overline{f(e^{i\theta})}\overline{h(e^{i\theta})}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,.

Observe that
\mathcal{P}
is dense in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
and as a consequence,
W\mathcal{P}
is dense in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Now the inclusion
f\in\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
is equivalent to the estimate

\left\lvert\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\overset{\circ}{u}(e^{i\theta})\overline{f(e^{i\theta})}\overline{h(e^{i\theta})}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\right\rvert\leq\lVert h\rVert_{H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})},\quad\forall\ h\in\mathcal{P}

while the inclusion
Wf\in\Dom H_{u}
is equivalent to the estimate

\left\lvert\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(x)\overline{Wf(x)}\overline{Wh(x)}dx\right\rvert\leq C\lVert Wh\rVert_{H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad\forall\ h\in\mathcal{P}.

By (
3.7
), the left hand sides of these two estimates coincide, and by the isometricity of
W
the right hand sides coincide as well. Thus, the inclusions
f\in\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
and
Wf\in\Dom H_{u}
are equivalent to one another. For
f\in\Dom\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}}
, identity (
3.7
) yields (
3.5
).
∎

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Items (i), (iii), (iv) of Theorem
3.1
follow from the corresponding items of
Theorem
3.2
. By Theorem
3.2
, the set
W\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{R}
is dense in

with respect to the graph norm of

; it follows that

is also dense in the same sense.
∎

3.5.
Strong resolvent convergence

For
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, we define the linear operator
H_{u}^{2}
on the domain

\Dom H_{u}^{2}=\{f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):f\in\Dom H_{u}\text{ and }H_{u}f\in\Dom H_{u}\}.

This operator is closed; furthermore, it is self-adjoint and positive semi-definite. It can be alternatively described as the self-adjoint operator corresponding to the quadratic form
\lVert H_{u}f\rVert^{2}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of elements of
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
with
\lVert u_{n}-u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then

H_{u_{n}}^{2}\to H_{u}^{2}

in the strong resolvent sense.

Proof.

The proof follows from Lemma
3.3
and from the corresponding statement
[
10
, Theorem 2.2]
about Hankel operators in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
:

if
\{\overset{\circ}{u}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of elements of
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
with
\lVert\overset{\circ}{u}_{n}-\overset{\circ}{u}\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then

(\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}_{n}})^{2}\to(\overset{\circ}{H}_{\overset{\circ}{u}})^{2}

in the strong resolvent sense.

We only need to comment that by (
3.6
), convergence

\lVert u_{n}-u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
implies the convergence

\lVert\overset{\circ}{u}_{n}-\overset{\circ}{u}\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
.
∎

4.
The explicit formula for initial data from
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})

4.1.
The flow preserves the norm in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})

We keep
t>0
fixed and continue using the notation
L_{u}(t)
for the integral (
2.5
) with any symbol
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
; here
H_{u}
is the closed operator as defined in the previous section.
By the remarks following Theorem
2.2
, the r.h.s. of (
2.6
) still makes sense in this context. Where no confusion arises, we write
L_{u}
in place of
L_{u}(t)
.
The main ingredient of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and
t>0
, let
\Phi(t)u
be the holomorphic function in the
upper half-plane, defined by

(4.1)

(\Phi(t)u)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}I_{+}\left[\left(A^{*}+L_{u}(t)-z\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u\right],\quad\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0.

Then
\Phi(t)u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and

\lVert\Phi(t)u\rVert_{H^{2}}=\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}.

In the rest of this Section, assuming Theorem
4.1
, we prove Theorem
1.1
.
Theorem
4.1
is proved in Sections
5
–
7
.

We emphasize that in Theorem
4.1
, formula (

) is regarded as a

of a non-linear map
\Phi(t)
on

. The fact that

, thus defined, coincides with the flow map of the cubic Szegő equation is established later.

4.2.
Rewriting

without

Below we recast the definition (
4.1
) in an alternative way, avoiding the use of the functional
I_{+}
.
Recall that
v_{a}
is the reproducing kernel of
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
at the point
a
in the upper half-plane. In particular, below we use
v_{i}
which corresponds to the choice

.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and any
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0
, the r.h.s. of (
4.1
) can be written as

(4.2)

(\Phi(t)u)(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\langle(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,q\rangle,

where

q:=\sqrt{4\pi}(I-(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}L_{u})v_{i}.

We note that the reason for the “strange” normalisation of the vector
q
is that
\lVert q\rVert=1
(as we shall see later). This vector plays an important role in what follows.

Proof.

By formula (
2.1
) for the resolvent of
A
, we have

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}(A+i)^{-1}\chi_{\varepsilon}=-v_{i}\,.

Next, we recall that
\Dom A^{*}=\Dom(A^{*}+L_{u})
(this will be used in the calculation below). Using (
2.3
), we find

\displaystyle(\Phi(t)u)(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}I_{+}\left[\left(A^{*}+L_{u}-z\right)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,(A+i)(A+i)^{-1}\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle(A^{*}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,(A+i)^{-1}\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(A^{*}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,v_{i}\rangle\,.

Finally, substituting

\displaystyle(A^{*}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(A^{*}+L_{u}-i-L_{u})(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I-L_{u}(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)^{-1})(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}

in the previous identity, we obtain the required result.
∎

4.3.
A convergence argument

We isolate a convergence argument as a lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
u_{n}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
be a convergent sequence,
\lVert u_{n}-u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have the
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
-convergence

\lVert e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}u_{n}-e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0

and the operator norm convergence

\lVert L_{u_{n}}-L_{u}\rVert\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.

In fact, the above convergence also holds true in the trace norm, but we will not need this fact.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.4
we have the strong resolvent convergence

H_{u_{n}}^{2}\to H_{u}^{2}.

This implies
[
22
, Theorem VIII.20]
that for all bounded continuous functions
f
, we have
f(H_{u_{n}}^{2})\to f(H_{u}^{2})
in the strong operator topology. In particular,

e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}\to e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}

for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
strongly as
n\to\infty
.
It follows that

\lVert e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}u_{n}-e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0

for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
as
n\to\infty
and therefore we have the operator norm convergence of rank one operators

\lVert\langle\cdot,e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}u_{n}\rangle e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}u_{n}-\langle\cdot,e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u\rangle e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u\rVert\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Also, the operator norms of
\langle\cdot,e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}u_{n}\rangle e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}u_{n}
are uniformly bounded. Integrating over
t
and using the operator norm valued dominated convergence theorem, we find that

\lVert L_{u_{n}}-L_{u}\rVert\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

The proof is complete.
∎

4.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Assuming Theorem
4.1
, let us prove Theorem
1.1
.
We extend the map
\Phi(t)
to the whole of
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
as indicated in Theorem
4.1
.
Clearly, (i) follows directly from Theorem
4.1
.

Let us prove (ii). Let
\lVert u_{n}-u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
.
Fix

and let us pass to the limit in (

). We have

(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}=I+(z-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}

and by the resolvent identity,

(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-z)^{-1}-(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}=(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-z)^{-1}(L_{u}-L_{u_{n}})(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}.

Applying the resolvent norm estimate (

) of a maximal dissipative operator to the r.h.s. of (

\lVert(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-z)^{-1}e^{-itH_{u_{n}}^{2}}u_{n}-(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u\rVert\to 0

as

. Finally, let

q=\sqrt{4\pi}(I-(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}L_{u})v_{i},\quad q_{n}=\sqrt{4\pi}(I-(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}L_{u_{n}})v_{i}.

Again by the resolvent identity and by Lemma
4.3
, we find that
\lVert q_{n}-q\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
We conclude that we can pass to the limit in (
4.2
), i.e.

\lvert(\Phi(t)u_{n})(z)-(\Phi(t)u)(z)\rvert\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,

for all
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0
, and so
\Phi(t)u_{n}\to\Phi(t)u
weakly in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
as
n\to\infty
.
On the other hand, by Theorem
4.1
we have

\lVert\Phi(t)u_{n}\rVert_{H^{2}}=\lVert u_{n}\rVert_{H^{2}}\to\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}=\lVert\Phi(t)u\rVert_{H^{2}}

and so we conclude that
\lVert\Phi(t)u_{n}-\Phi(t)u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let us prove (iii). Let
t_{n}\to t
as
n\to\infty
.
Following exactly the same argument as in the previous step of the proof (or simply rescaling by writing
u_{n}=t_{n}u
), we find that
\Phi(t_{n})u\to\Phi(t)u
weakly as
n\to\infty
and on the other hand, by Theorem
4.1

\lVert\Phi(t_{n})u\rVert_{H^{2}}=\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}=\lVert\Phi(t)u\rVert_{H^{2}}

and so again we conclude that
\lVert\Phi(t_{n})u-\Phi(t)u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let us prove (iv). We split the proof into four steps.

Step 1:

Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
; consider the Fourier transform of
\lvert u\rvert^{2}
, evaluated on the real axis. We have

\widehat{\lvert u\rvert^{2}}(-\xi)=\overline{\widehat{\lvert u\rvert^{2}}(\xi)},\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}},

and therefore, using Plancherel’s theorem, we find that
u\in H^{4}
iff

\lVert\mathbb{P}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lvert\widehat{\lvert u\rvert^{2}}(\xi)\right\rvert^{2}d\xi<\infty.

Furthermore, this argument also shows that if
u\in H^{4}({\mathbb{R}})
, then

\lVert u\rVert_{H^{4}}^{4}=2\lVert\mathbb{P}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}.

We conclude that
u\in H^{4}({\mathbb{R}})
iff
u\in\Dom H_{u}
and in this case

\lVert u\rVert_{H^{4}}^{4}=2\lVert H_{u}u\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}.

Step 2:

For
x>0
and
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, denote

J(x,u):=\langle(I+xH_{u}^{2})^{-1}u,u\rangle.

The function
x\mapsto J(x,u)
is
C^{\infty}
smooth on
(0,\infty)
, with

\partial_{x}J(x,u)=-\langle(I+xH_{u}^{2})^{-1}H_{u}^{2}(I+xH_{u}^{2})^{-1}u,u\rangle=-\lVert H_{u}(I+xH_{u}^{2})^{-1}u\rVert^{2}.

Observe that
u\in\Dom H_{u}
iff

\sup_{x>0}\lVert H_{u}(I+xH_{u}^{2})^{-1}u\rVert<\infty,

i.e. iff the derivative
\partial_{x}J(x,u)
remains bounded as
x\to 0_{+}
, and in this case

\partial_{x}J(0_{+},u)=-\lVert H_{u}u\rVert^{2}.

Combining this with the previous step, we see that
u\in H^{4}({\mathbb{R}})
iff
\partial_{x}J(x,u)
remains bounded as
x\to 0_{+}
, and in this case

\partial_{x}J(0_{+},u)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{4}}^{4}.

Step 3:

Let us check that

(4.4)

J(x,\Phi(t)u)=J(x,u),\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{R},\quad\forall x>0.

Denote
u_{t}(z)=(\Phi(t)u)(z)
; since
u
is rational, we can freely use the cubic Szegő equation (
1.1
) and the Lax pair formulation (
2.2
), and so it suffices to check that

\partial_{t}J(x,u_{t})=0.

The cubic Szegő equation (
1.1
) can be written as

\partial_{t}u_{t}=-i\mathbb{P}(\lvert u_{t}\rvert^{2}u_{t})=-iT_{\lvert u_{t}\rvert^{2}}u_{t}=B_{u_{t}}u_{t}-\tfrac{i}{2}H_{u_{t}}^{2}u_{t}.

On the other hand, from the Lax pair equation (
2.2
) by induction in
m
we find

\partial_{t}H_{u_{t}}^{m}=[B_{u_{t}},H_{u_{t}}^{m}]

and therefore

\partial_{t}(I+xH_{u_{t}}^{2})^{-1}=[B_{u_{t}},(I+xH_{u_{t}}^{2})^{-1}]\,.

Putting this together, using the fact that
B_{u}^{*}=-B_{u}
and that
H_{u_{t}}^{2}
commutes with
(I+xH_{u_{t}}^{2})^{-1}
, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}J(x,u_{t})=

\displaystyle\langle[B_{u_{t}},(I+xH_{u_{t}}^{2})^{-1}]u_{t},u_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle(I+xH_{u_{t}}^{2})^{-1}(B_{u_{t}}-\tfrac{i}{2}H_{u_{t}}^{2})u_{t},u_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle(I+xH_{u_{t}}^{2})^{-1}u_{t},(B_{u_{t}}-\tfrac{i}{2}H_{u_{t}}^{2})u_{t}\rangle=0,

as claimed.

Step 4:

By the already proven part (ii) of Theorem
1.1
, we can extend (
4.4
) from the dense set of
u\in\mathcal{R}
to all
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Now we can put it all together. In steps 1 and 2, we have characterised the inclusion
u\in H^{4}({\mathbb{R}})
in terms of the function
J(x,u)
. In step 3, we proved that this function is invariant under the map
\Phi(t)
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
(iv). ∎

5.
In the land of contractions

In the rest of the paper, our task is to prove Theorem
4.1
.
Formula (
4.1
) for
\Phi(t)
is stated in terms of the (adjoint of) dissipative operator
A+L_{u}
.
Our strategy is to rewrite this formula in terms of a contraction
\Sigma_{u}
which is a Cayley transform of
A+L_{u}
and then to use (i) a commutation relation between
\Sigma_{u}
and a certain auxiliary Hankel-like operator
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and (ii) a general operator theoretic lemma about contractions from our previous paper
[
10
]
.

Figuratively speaking, at this point in the paper we are crossing the bridge from the land of dissipative operators to the land of contractions.

5.1.
Contractions
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{u}

Here we define the Cayley transforms of
A
and
A+L_{u}
and discuss their basic properties.
Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and let
L_{u}
be the trace-class self-adjoint operator (
2.5
).
We define

(5.1)

\Sigma_{0}:=(A-i)(A+i)^{-1},\quad\Sigma_{u}:=(A+L_{u}-i)(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}.

We will regard
\Sigma_{u}
as a “perturbation” of
\Sigma_{0}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The operator
\Sigma_{0}
is a completely non-unitary contraction, unitarily equivalent to the shift operator
S
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
and satisfying

(5.2)

\Sigma_{0}^{*}\Sigma_{0}=I,\quad\Sigma_{0}\Sigma_{0}^{*}=I-4\pi\langle\cdot,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}.

Proof.

By (
2.1
), the operator
\Sigma_{0}
is the operator of multiplication by the function
\frac{x-i}{x+i}
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Let
W:H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})\to H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
be the unitary operator (
3.4
).
Changing the variable, we find that

W^{*}\Sigma_{0}W=S,

i.e. the usual shift operator in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
. In particular,
\Sigma_{0}
is a completely non-unitary contraction with defect indices
(0,1)
. For
S
we have

SS^{*}=I-\langle\cdot,\mathbbm{1}\rangle\mathbbm{1},

where
\mathbbm{1}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
is the function identically equal to
1
. By a direct calculation,
W\mathbbm{1}=-2\sqrt{\pi}iv_{i}
, and so (
5.2
) follows.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

The operator
\Sigma_{u}
is a contraction, satisfying

\Sigma_{u}^{*}\Sigma_{u}=I,\quad\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{u}^{*}=I-\langle\cdot,q\rangle q,

where
q
is as in Lemma
4.2
; moreover,
\lVert q\rVert=1
.

Proof.

Step 1: representation for
\Sigma_{u}
.
We would like to represent
\Sigma_{u}
in terms of
\Sigma_{0}
and
L_{u}
. First we represent the operator
A+L_{u}
in terms of
\Sigma_{0}
and
L_{u}
. We recall that

\Dom(A+L_{u})=\Dom A=\Ran(I-\Sigma_{0})

and

A=i(I+\Sigma_{0})(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}\text{ on $\Dom A$.}

It follows that on
\Dom A
we have

\displaystyle A+L_{u}\pm i

\displaystyle=i(I+\Sigma_{0})(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}+L_{u}\pm i

\displaystyle=\bigl(i(I+\Sigma_{0})+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0})\pm i(I-\Sigma_{0})\bigr)(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}\,.

Choosing the plus sign in the above expression, we would like to check that the operator in brackets, viz.

\bigl(i(I+\Sigma_{0})+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0})+i(I-\Sigma_{0})\bigr)=2i+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}),

has a bounded inverse. Since
L_{u}
is compact, by the Fredholm alternative it suffices to check that the kernel is trivial. Suppose
(2i+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))f=0
for some
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
; then for
g=(I-\Sigma_{0})f
we have

(A+L_{u}+i)g=(2i+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}g=(2i+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))f=0,

which is impossible because
A
is maximal dissipative and therefore
\Ker(A+L_{u}+i)
is trivial.

Now we compute:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{u}

\displaystyle=(A+L_{u}-i)(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(2i\Sigma_{0}+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}\biggl((2i+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}\biggr)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(2i\Sigma_{0}+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))(2i+L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))^{-1}

(5.3)

\displaystyle=(\Sigma_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))(I+\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))^{-1}.

In the same way, starting from

A=i(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}(I+\Sigma_{0})\text{ on $\Dom A$,}

we find

(5.4)

\Sigma_{u}=(I+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u})^{-1}(\Sigma_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u})\,,

where
I+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u}
has a bounded inverse.

Step 2: computing
\Sigma_{u}^{*}\Sigma_{u}
.

Using (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), we write

(5.5)

\Sigma_{u}^{*}\Sigma_{u}=(I-\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u})^{-1}M(I+\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))^{-1}\,,

where

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=(\Sigma_{0}^{*}-\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u})(\Sigma_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{0}^{*}\Sigma_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2i}\Sigma_{0}^{*}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0})-\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u}\Sigma_{0}-\tfrac{1}{(2i)^{2}}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u}^{2}(I-\Sigma_{0}).

Using that
\Sigma_{0}^{*}\Sigma_{0}=I
, we can rewrite this as

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=I+\tfrac{1}{2i}\Sigma_{0}^{*}L_{u}-\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}\Sigma_{0}-\tfrac{1}{(2i)^{2}}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u}^{2}(I-\Sigma_{0})

\displaystyle=I-\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u}+\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0})-\tfrac{1}{(2i)^{2}}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u}^{2}(I-\Sigma_{0})

\displaystyle=(I-\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})L_{u})(I+\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}))

and substituting this back into (
5.5
), we find
\Sigma_{u}^{*}\Sigma_{u}=I
.

Step 3: computing
\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{u}^{*}
.

We have

(5.6)

\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{u}^{*}=(I+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u})^{-1}M_{*}(I-\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*}))^{-1}

where

\displaystyle M_{*}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u})(\Sigma_{0}^{*}-\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*}))

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{0}\Sigma_{0}^{*}+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u}\Sigma_{0}^{*}-\tfrac{1}{2i}\Sigma_{0}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})-\tfrac{1}{(2i)^{2}}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u}^{2}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*}).

Using that
\Sigma_{0}\Sigma_{0}^{*}=I-4\pi\langle\cdot,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}
, we rewrite this as

\displaystyle M_{*}

\displaystyle=I-4\pi\langle\cdot,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}\Sigma_{0}^{*}-\tfrac{1}{2i}\Sigma_{0}L_{u}-\tfrac{1}{(2i)^{2}}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u}^{2}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})

\displaystyle=I-4\pi\langle\cdot,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u}-\tfrac{1}{(2i)^{2}}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u}^{2}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*})

\displaystyle=(I+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u})(I-\tfrac{1}{2i}L_{u}(I-\Sigma_{0}^{*}))-4\pi\langle\cdot,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}.

Substituting back into (
5.6
), we find

\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{u}^{*}=I-\langle\cdot,q\rangle q,

with

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\pi}(I+\tfrac{1}{2i}(I-\Sigma_{0})L_{u})^{-1}v_{i}=2i\sqrt{4\pi}(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}(I-\Sigma_{0})^{-1}v_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\pi}(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}(A+i)v_{i}=\sqrt{4\pi}(I-(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}L_{u})v_{i},

as required.

Step 4: computing the norm of
q
.

Since
\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{u}^{*}\geq 0
, we have
\lVert q\rVert\leq 1
. Since the spectra of
\Sigma_{u}^{*}\Sigma_{u}
and
\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{u}^{*}
outside zero coincide and
q\not=0
, we find that
\lVert q\rVert=1
.
∎

5.2.
Rewriting
\lVert\Phi(t)u\rVert
in terms of
\Sigma_{u}

We would like to recast the statement of Theorem
4.1
in terms of the contraction
\Sigma_{u}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\Phi(t)
be defined as in (
4.1
), and
q
be as in Lemma
4.2
. Then for any
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, we have

(5.7)

\sup_{y>0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\lvert(\Phi(t)u)(x+iy)\right\rvert^{2}dx=\sup_{0<r<1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lvert\langle(I+re^{i\theta}\Sigma_{u}^{*})^{-1}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,q\rangle\rvert^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}.

Proof.

We use the following well-known statement.
Let
G=G(z)
be a function holomorphic in the open upper half-plane and
g=g(\zeta)
be a function holomorphic in the open unit disc such that

G(z)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\tfrac{1}{z+i}g\left(\tfrac{z-i}{z+i}\right),\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}.

Then

\sup_{y>0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert G(x+iy)\rvert^{2}dx=\sup_{0<r<1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lvert g(re^{i\theta})\rvert^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,.

Using the definition of
\Sigma_{u}
as a Cayley transform of
A+L_{u}
, we compute

(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)(A^{*}+L_{u}-z)^{-1}=\tfrac{2i}{z+i}(I+\tfrac{z-i}{z+i}\Sigma_{u}^{*})^{-1},

and therefore, by Lemma
4.2
,

(\Phi(t)u)(z)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\tfrac{2i}{z+i}\langle(I+\tfrac{z-i}{z+i}\Sigma_{u}^{*})^{-1}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,q\rangle.

Now taking

G(z)=(\Phi(t)u)(z)\quad\text{ and }\quad g(\zeta)=i\langle(I+\zeta\Sigma_{u}^{*})^{-1}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u,q\rangle,

we obtain the required identity.
∎

5.3.
Discussion: direction of further exposition

In order to prove Theorem
4.1
, we need to check that the r.h.s. in (
5.7
) equals
\lVert u\rVert^{2}
.
Denote
p=e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u
.
For the integrand in the right hand side of (
5.7
), we have

\langle(I+re^{i\theta}\Sigma_{u}^{*})^{-1}p,q\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(re^{i\theta})^{n}\langle p,\Sigma_{u}^{n}q\rangle

and therefore

\sup_{r<1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\lvert\langle(I+re^{i\theta}\Sigma_{u}^{*})^{-1}p,q\rangle\rvert^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lvert\langle p,\Sigma_{u}^{n}q\rangle\rvert^{2}.

Thus, our task is to show that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lvert\langle p,\Sigma_{u}^{n}q\rangle\rvert^{2}=\lVert p\rVert^{2},\quad p=e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u.

From Lemma
5.2
we find that
\lVert\Sigma_{u}^{m}q\rVert=\lVert q\rVert=1
for all
m
and
\Sigma_{u}^{*}q=0
.
It follows that for
m>n\geq 0

\langle\Sigma_{u}^{m}q,\Sigma_{u}^{n}q\rangle=\langle\Sigma_{u}^{m-n}q,q\rangle=\langle q,(\Sigma_{u}^{*})^{m-n}q\rangle=0,

and therefore
\{\Sigma_{u}^{m}q\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal set in the Hardy space
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
We need to prove that
p
belongs to the closed linear span of this orthonormal set.

Below we use the Wold decomposition for
\Sigma_{u}
, see Theorem
A.1
in the Appendix.
By this theorem, it suffices to prove that
p
belongs to the c.n.u. subspace in the Wold decomposition (
A.2
) of
\Sigma_{u}
.
Now observe that

p=e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}u=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}\chi_{\varepsilon}.

This suggests considering the operator
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}:=e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}
in this context.
In the next section, we study
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and prove that it satisfies a simple commutation relation with
\Sigma_{u}
. Using this and some abstract results from
[
10
]
, in Section
7
we will prove that
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
is
diagonal
in the Wold decomposition (
A.2
) of
\Sigma_{u}
. From here it will be very easy to conclude that
p
is in the c.n.u. subspace of
\Sigma_{u}
, as required.

6.
A commutation relation between
\Sigma_{u}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}

6.1.
The auxiliary operator
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}

Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Motivated by the previous discussion, here we introduce an auxiliary anti-linear operator whose properties mirror those of
H_{u}
. We set

(6.1)

{\mathcal{H}}_{u}=e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}=H_{u}e^{itH_{u}^{2}},\quad\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}=\Dom H_{u}.

For the ease of further reference, we state the properties of
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
as a lemma which mirrors Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, and let
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
be the anti-linear operator defined by (
6.1
).
Then:

(i)

{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
is closed, i.e.
\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
is closed with respect to the graph norm of
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
;

(ii)

the set of rational functions
\mathcal{R}
is dense in
\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
with respect to the graph norm;

(iii)

for any
f,g\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
, we have

\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f,g\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}g,f\rangle;

(iv)

suppose for some
f,h\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
we have

\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}g,f\rangle=\langle h,g\rangle,\quad\forall g\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}.

Then
f\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f=h
.

Proof.

Since
H_{u}
is closed, it is clear that
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
is closed as well.
Item (ii) follows directly from Theorem
3.1
(ii).
Next, using Theorem
3.1
(iii), we find

\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f,g\rangle=\langle e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}f,g\rangle=\langle H_{u}f,e^{itH_{u}^{2}}g\rangle=\langle H_{u}e^{itH_{u}^{2}}g,f\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}g,f\rangle

for all
f,g\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
.
Finally, under the hypothesis of (iv) we have

\langle H_{u}g,e^{itH_{u}^{2}}f\rangle=\langle e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}g,f\rangle=\langle h,g\rangle

and so from Theorem
3.1
(iv) we find that
e^{itH_{u}^{2}}f\in\Dom H_{u}
and

H_{u}e^{itH_{u}^{2}}f=h
, as required.
∎

6.2.
The main result of this section

Our task in this section is to prove the following commutation relation between
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and
\Sigma_{u}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

We have
\Sigma_{u}\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}\subset\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and on
\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
the relation

(6.2)

\Sigma_{u}^{*}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}={\mathcal{H}}_{u}\Sigma_{u}

holds true.

Proving this requires some preparation. Essentially, this is a consequence of the analogous formula for
H_{u}
and
\Sigma_{0}
,

(6.3)

\Sigma_{0}^{*}H_{u}=H_{u}\Sigma_{0}

(see Remark
6.4
below),
the definitions of operators
\Sigma_{u}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and properties of
\Sigma_{0}
. We will also use an approximation argument, because it is easier to deal with bounded Hankel operators until the last moment.

For much of this section, we deal with Hankel operators
H_{u}
with rational symbols
u\in\mathcal{R}
.
By a version of Kronecker’s theorem (a detailed proof can be found, e.g. in
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
) in this case we have

\Ran H_{u}\subset\mathcal{R},\quad u\in\mathcal{R}.

In particular,
\Ran H_{u}\subset\Dom A^{*}
and so the operator
A^{*}H_{u}
is well-defined and bounded on
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.

6.3.
Commutation relations between
A
and
H_{u}

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{R}
; on
\Dom A
we have the relation

A^{*}H_{u}=H_{u}A.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the identity

(6.4)

H_{u}(A+i)^{-1}f=(A^{*}-i)^{-1}H_{u}f

for all
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Let us evaluate the inner products with the reproducing kernel
v_{z}
,
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0
:

\displaystyle\langle H_{u}(A+i)^{-1}f,v_{z}\rangle_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle-\langle(A^{*}-i)^{-1}H_{u}f,v_{z}\rangle_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u\overline{(A+i)^{-1}f},v_{z}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle H_{u}f,(A+i)^{-1}v_{z}\rangle_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u,v_{z}(A+i)^{-1}f\rangle_{H^{2}}-\langle u\overline{f},(A+i)^{-1}v_{z}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u,v_{z}(A+i)^{-1}f\rangle_{H^{2}}-\langle u,f(A+i)^{-1}v_{z}\rangle_{H^{2}}.

Using (
2.1
), we observe that
(A+i)^{-1}
commutes with multiplication, and therefore the right hand side here vanishes, as required.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{R}
; we have the identity

[A^{*},H_{u}^{2}]H_{u}=\frac{i}{2\pi}(\langle u,\cdot\rangle H_{u}u-\langle H_{u}u,\cdot\rangle u).

Proof.

Let us use the identity
H_{u}^{2}=T_{\lvert u\rvert^{2}}-T_{u}T_{\overline{u}}
and Lemma
2.3
.
We find

\displaystyle[A^{*},H_{u}^{2}]H_{u}f

\displaystyle=[A^{*},T_{\lvert u\rvert^{2}}]H_{u}f-[A^{*},T_{u}T_{\overline{u}}]H_{u}f

\displaystyle=[A^{*},T_{\lvert u\rvert^{2}}]H_{u}f-[A^{*},T_{u}]T_{\overline{u}}H_{u}f

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi}\biggl(I_{+}(H_{u}f)\mathbb{P}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})-I_{+}(T_{\overline{u}}H_{u}f)u\biggr).

Now let us compute all entries in the r.h.s.; we have

I_{+}(H_{u}f)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle H_{u}f,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle u\overline{f},\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle u,f\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\langle u,f\rangle,

and similarly

\displaystyle I_{+}(T_{\overline{u}}H_{u}f)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle T_{\overline{u}}H_{u}f,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle{\overline{u}}H_{u}f,\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}\langle H_{u}f,u\chi_{\varepsilon}\rangle=\langle H_{u}f,u\rangle=\langle H_{u}u,f\rangle\,.

Finally,
\mathbb{P}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})=H_{u}u
. Putting this together yields the required identity.
∎

6.4.
Commutation relations between
A+L_{u}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{R}
; we have the identity

(6.5)

(A^{*}+L_{u}){\mathcal{H}}_{u}={\mathcal{H}}_{u}(A+L_{u})

on
\Dom A
.

Proof.

Step 1:

Let us prove the identity

(6.6)

(e^{itH_{u}^{2}}A^{*}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}-A^{*})H_{u}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{t}e^{isH_{u}^{2}}\biggl(\langle u,\cdot\rangle H_{u}u-\langle H_{u}u,\cdot\rangle u\biggr)e^{isH_{u}^{2}}ds\,.

Differentiating the l.h.s. of (
6.6
) with respect to
t
, we find

\frac{d}{dt}e^{itH_{u}^{2}}A^{*}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}=-ie^{itH_{u}^{2}}[A^{*},H_{u}^{2}]H_{u}e^{itH_{u}^{2}}.

Using the previous lemma and integrating, we arrive at (
6.6
).
Multiplying (
6.6
) by
e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}
on the left, we obtain

A^{*}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}-e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}A^{*}H_{u}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)H_{u}^{2}}\biggl(\langle u,\cdot\rangle H_{u}u-\langle H_{u}u,\cdot\rangle u\biggr)e^{isH_{u}^{2}}ds\,.

Step 2:

By the definition of
L_{u}
, we find

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}L_{u}-L_{u}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}=

\displaystyle e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}L_{u}-L_{u}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)H_{u}^{2}}(\langle u,\cdot\rangle H_{u}u)e^{isH_{u}^{2}}ds

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-isH_{u}^{2}}(\langle H_{u}u,\cdot\rangle u)e^{i(t-s)H_{u}^{2}}ds\,;

changing the variable in the second integral, we find

Comparing this with the final identity of the previous step, we arrive at

A^{*}e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}H_{u}-e^{-itH_{u}^{2}}A^{*}H_{u}={\mathcal{H}}_{u}L_{u}-L_{u}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}\,.

Finally, on

∎

6.5.
Approximation arguments

Lemma 6.7
.

For any
u\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and
f\in\mathcal{R}
, we have the identity

(6.7)

(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f={\mathcal{H}}_{u}(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}f\,.

Proof.

Step 1:

Take a sequence
u_{n}\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\lVert u_{n}-u\rVert_{H^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Since
f\in\mathcal{R}\subset H^{2}\cap H^{\infty}
, we have
\lVert(u_{n}-u)\overline{f}\rVert_{L^{2}}\to 0
and therefore
H_{u_{n}}f\to H_{u}f
in
H^{2}
.
Furthermore, by Theorem
3.4
, we have the strong resolvent convergence
H_{u_{n}}^{2}\to H_{u}^{2}
. It follows that
{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}f\to{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f
. Furthermore, by Lemma
4.3
we have the operator norm convergence

\lVert L_{u_{n}}-L_{u}\rVert\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Using the resolvent identity, we find that

\lVert(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}-(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}\rVert\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Step 2:

We write the commutation relation (
6.5
) with
u_{n}
in place of
u
and take inverses; this yields

(6.8)

(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}f={\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}f

for each
n
. Our aim is to pass to the limit here.
Consider both sides of this identity. By Step 1 of the proof, we have

\displaystyle\lVert(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}f-(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}f\rVert

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle\lVert(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}f-(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f\rVert

\displaystyle\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
.
Take
g\in\mathcal{R}
and consider the inner product of (
6.8
) with
g
:

\langle(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}f,g\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}f,g\rangle.

By the symmetry of
{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}
(Lemma
6.1
(iii)), the r.h.s. here rewrites as

\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}f,g\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}g,(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}f\rangle.

Putting this together and taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we find

\displaystyle\langle(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle(A^{*}+L_{u_{n}}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u_{n}}g,(A+L_{u_{n}}+i)^{-1}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}g,(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}f\rangle,

resulting in

(6.9)

\langle(A^{*}+L_{u}-i)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f,g\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{u}g,(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}f\rangle.

By Lemma
6.1
(i) and (ii), the operator
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
is closed and rational functions are dense in
\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
with respect to the graph norm. It follows that (
6.9
) can be extended from
g\in\mathcal{R}
to all
g\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
.
From here, by Lemma
6.1
(iv) we find that
(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}f\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and (
6.7
) holds.
∎

6.6.
Proof of Theorem
6.2

Let
f\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
; we need to prove that
\Sigma_{u}f\in\Dom{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}\Sigma_{u}f=\Sigma_{u}^{*}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}f

Let

Now using the symmetry of

7.3.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

As discussed in Section

let us check the identity

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}(I-\Sigma_{u})\chi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=2i\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}\chi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=2i\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0_{+}}(I-(A+L_{u}+i)^{-1}L_{u})(A+i)^{-1}\chi_{\varepsilon}

Appendix A
Operator theoretic background

We recall key operator theoretic statements that we use throughout the text. For details, see
[
14
]
and
[
3
]
for semigroup theory and
[
21
]
for the theory of contractions and dissipative operators.

A.1.
Dissipative operators

Let
X
be a Hilbert space with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. An operator
\mathcal{A}
with a domain
\Dom\mathcal{A}\subset X
is called
dissipative
, if
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,\langle\mathcal{A}f,f\rangle\geq 0
for all
f\in\Dom\mathcal{A}
. A dissipative operator
\mathcal{A}
is called
maximal
, if it has no dissipative extensions.
Every dissipative operator has a maximal dissipative extension. A maximal dissipative operator is closed.
(The reader may wish to keep in mind that the distinction between dissipative and maximal dissipative operators is similar to the distinction between symmetric and self-adjoint operators.)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a dissipative operator.
A simple calculation shows that

\lVert(\mathcal{A}+z)f\rVert\geq\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z\lVert f\rVert

for any
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0
and
f\in\Dom\mathcal{A}
, and in particular the kernel of
\mathcal{A}+z
is trivial. Furthermore,
\mathcal{A}
is maximal if and only if
\Ran(\mathcal{A}+z)=X
for some (and then for all)
\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0
; in this case, by the above estimate,
\mathcal{A}+z
has a bounded inverse and the resolvent norm estimate

(A.1)

\lVert(\mathcal{A}+z)^{-1}\rVert\leq 1/\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z,\quad\hbox{{\rm Im}}\,z>0

holds true.

If
\mathcal{A}
is maximal dissipative, then so is
-\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a maximal dissipative operator, and let
L
be a bounded dissipative operator (in particular,
L
may be self-adjoint). Then the operator
\mathcal{A}+L
, defined on
\Dom\mathcal{A}
, is also maximal dissipative (see e.g.
[
3
, Section 3.1]
).

A.2.
Semigroups and their generators

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
,
\tau\geq 0
, be a strongly continuous semigroup of contractions (operators of norm
\leq 1
) on a Hilbert space
X
. The
infinitesimal generator
of
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
is the maximal dissipative operator

\mathcal{A}f=\lim_{\tau_{\to}0_{+}}\tfrac{1}{i\tau}(\mathcal{S}_{\tau}f-f)

(where the limit is in the norm of
X
) defined on the dense domain of elements
f
where the above limit exists.
Conversely, if
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal dissipative operator, then there exists a unique semigroup of contractions
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
such that
\mathcal{A}
is the generator of
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
; notation:
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}=e^{i\tau\mathcal{A}}
.
If
\mathcal{A}
is the infinitesimal generator of
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
, then
-\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the infinitesimal generator of
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}^{*}
.

A.3.
Cayley transform

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a maximal dissipative operator on
X
.
The
Cayley transform
of
\mathcal{A}
(corresponding to the point
i\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
) is the contraction

\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{A}-i)(\mathcal{A}+i)^{-1}.

The contraction
\mathcal{T}
has the property that
1
is not an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{T}
and
\Ran(I-\mathcal{T})=\Dom\mathcal{A}
.
Finally,

\mathcal{T}^{*}=(\mathcal{A}^{*}+i)(\mathcal{A}^{*}-i)^{-1}

is the Cayley transform of
-\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

A.4.
Contractions and the Wold decomposition

Let
\Sigma
be a contraction on a Hilbert space, i.e. an operator with the norm
\leq 1
.
The
defect indices
of
\Sigma
is the (ordered) pair of integers

\bigl(\rank(I-\Sigma^{*}\Sigma),\rank(I-\Sigma\Sigma^{*})\bigr).

In particular, any unitary operator has the defect indices
(0,0)
, any isometry has the defect indices
(0,k)
with
k\geq 0
and the shift operator
S
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
has the defect indices
(0,1)
.

A contraction
\Sigma
is called
completely non-unitary
(c.n.u.), if
\Sigma
is not unitary on any of its invariant subspaces. For example, the shift operator
S
is c.n.u.
The following result is a particular case of the
Wold decomposition
of an isometry (see e.g.
[
21
, Theorem I.1.1]
).

Theorem A.1

(Wold decomposition)
.

Let
\Sigma
be an isometry on a Hilbert space
X
with defect indices
(0,1)
, and let
q\in\Ran(I-\Sigma\Sigma^{*})
,
q\not=0
.
Then
X
can be represented as an orthogonal sum

X=X^{\rm(u)}\oplus X^{\rm(cnu)}
, such that

(A.2)

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma^{\rm(u)}&0\\
0&\Sigma^{\rm(cnu)}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ in $X^{\rm(u)}\oplus X^{\rm(cnu)}$,}

where
\Sigma^{\rm(u)}
is unitary and
\Sigma^{\rm(cnu)}
is c.n.u.
Moreover,
X^{\rm(cnu)}
coincides with the closed linear span of
\{\Sigma^{m}q\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
and
\Sigma^{\rm(cnu)}
is unitarily equivalent to the shift operator
S
on
H^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
.
