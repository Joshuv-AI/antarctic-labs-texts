Iterated magnitude homology
Emily Roff

Abstract
Magnitude homology is an invariant of enriched categories which generalizes ordinary categorical homology—the homology of the classifying space of a small category. The classifying space can also be generalized in a different direction: it extends from categories to bicategories as the geometric realization of the geometric nerve. This paper introduces a hybrid of the two ideas: an iterated magnitude homology theory for categories with a second- or higher-order enrichment. This encompasses, for example, groups equipped with extra structure such as a partial ordering or a bi-invariant metric. In the case of a strict 2-category, iterated magnitude homology recovers the homology of the classifying space; we investigate its content and behaviour when interpreted for partially ordered groups, normed groups, and strict n n -categories for n > 2 n>2 .

Introduction

The homology of a small category—meaning the homology of its classifying space—is a well-established construction which unifies various even more classical theories. Specialized to groups, it recovers group homology; specialized to posets, it recovers the homology of the order complex. The homology of small categories can, in turn, be generalized in two quite different directions. On the one hand, it can be lifted to a higher categorical dimension: the classifying space functor can be extended along the inclusion of categories into bicategories as the geometric realization of the
geometric nerve
. On the other hand, the theory can be enriched.
Magnitude homology
extends the homology of small categories—which are categories enriched in sets—to categories enriched in some more exotic monoidal category
\mathcal{V}
.

The scheme for constructing magnitude homology runs as follows. First, one must choose a functor
\Sigma
from
\mathcal{V}
to some abelian category
\mathbb{A}
. (We will refer to this as a choice of coefficients; conventionally,
\Sigma
is thought of as recording information about the ‘size’ of objects in
\mathcal{V}
.) The category
\mathbb{A}
should carry a closed monoidal structure, and
\Sigma
should be strong symmetric monoidal. There is then a recipe to lift
\Sigma
to a functor on the category
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
of
\mathcal{V}
-categories, valued in the category of simplicial objects in
\mathbb{A}
; this is the
magnitude nerve
functor. The magnitude homology of a
\mathcal{V}
-category is defined to be the homology of the Moore complex associated to its magnitude nerve. (This terminology is explained and more details provided in Sections
2
and
3
, below.)

Compared to the geometric nerve and the classifying space of a bicategory—which date back to work of Duskin and Street in the 1980s
[
44
]
—magnitude homology is a young theory. First defined for graphs by Hepworth and Willerton in 2017
[
24
]
, it was extended shortly thereafter by Leinster and Shulman
[
31
]
to encompass any category with a semicartesian enrichment, meaning that the monoidal unit in
\mathcal{V}
is terminal. Despite its relatively recent emergence, the theory has already shown itself to be productive. In the
\mathbf{Set}
-enriched setting it recovers ordinary categorical homology: if we take
\Sigma
to be the free abelian group functor on
\mathbf{Set}
, then the magnitude nerve of a small category is the simplicial abelian group freely generated by the ordinary nerve, so the magnitude homology of a small category is the homology of its classifying space. In other contexts, however, it offers something altogether new.

It has proved to be an especially rich invariant of metric spaces, regarded as categories enriched in the poset of nonnegative real numbers. With a suitable choice of coefficient functor, the magnitude homology in low degrees of a metric space captures specifically geometric information about convexity, curvature and the existence and uniqueness of geodesics (e.g.
[
1
,
21
,
25
]
). In the context of directed graphs, Asao has established a close connection between magnitude homology and Grigor’yan–Muranov–Yau
path homology
—appearing, respectively, on the first and second pages of a certain spectral sequence—and this relationship has already been exploited to prove new results about path homology and cast light on the homotopy theory of graphs
[
2
,
11
,
12
]
. More recently still, Tajima and Yoshinaga have made use of causal posets to define a notion of ‘magnitude homotopy type’ for metric spaces
[
45
]
.

This grounding in metric geometry and graph theory gives the applications of magnitude homology to date quite a different flavour from the abstract homotopy-theoretic or higher category-theoretic settings in which one typically encounters the bicategorical classifying space. Yet, of course, some bicategories are also enriched categories: a strict 2-category is precisely a category enriched in
\mathbf{Cat}
, the category of small categories. It makes sense, then, to ask whether the classifying space of a strict 2-category can be related to its magnitude homology with respect to some choice of coefficient functor on
\mathbf{Cat}
.

To answer that question, it is helpful to know that the classifying space of a strict 2-category has an alternative construction, essentially due to Segal
[
42
]
and given an especially convenient description by Bullejos and Cegarra in
[
9
]
. Instead of forming the geometric nerve of a strict 2-category
\mathbf{X}
—which is a simplicial set—the idea is to build a
bi
simplicial set by iterating the ordinary nerve construction for small categories: applying the nerve functor first locally (to each hom-category in
\mathbf{X}
) and then globally, to the resulting simplicial category. Bullejos and Cegarra prove that the diagonal of this bisimplicial set is naturally homotopy equivalent, after geometric realization, to the geometric nerve (
[
9
]
, Theorem 1).

This paper takes the bisimplicial approach as inspiration to construct a modified ‘magnitude homology’ theory for categories with a second- or higher-order enrichment—categories enriched in some category of
\mathcal{V}
-categories—where a coefficient functor on the base
\mathcal{V}
has been chosen. In the framework of magnitude homology, the analogue of Segal’s approach is to treat the magnitude nerve itself as a coefficient functor on
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
. This produces, for each
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-enriched category
\mathbf{X}
, a bisimplicial object in the abelian category
\mathbb{A}
, whose diagonal we call the
iterated magnitude nerve
of
\mathbf{X}
. The
iterated magnitude homology
of
\mathbf{X}
is defined to be the homology of the Moore complex associated to its iterated magnitude nerve. (Details are given in
Section

5
.)

By design—and by Bullejos and Cegarra’s Theorem 1—the iterated magnitude homology of a strict 2-category coincides with the topological homology of its classifying space; this is our
5.3
. Because we have the freedom to vary the base category
\mathcal{V}
and the coefficient functor
\Sigma
, we also have the freedom to extend the theory to new settings.

As it turns out, higher-order enrichment occurs in nature more often than one might imagine. In particular, various commonly-encountered structures on groups can be described as second-order enrichments in familiar base categories. Any congruence on a group (equivalently, any normal subgroup) gives rise to an enrichment in
\mathbf{Cat}
and thus a second-order enrichment in
\mathbf{Set}
. A group is called ‘partially ordered’ not simply when its underlying set is partially ordered, but rather when it has an enrichment in the category
\mathbf{Poset}
of posets and monotone maps, and thus a second-order enrichment in the category of Boolean truth values. And a group equipped with a norm that is constant on conjugacy classes—equivalently, a translation-invariant metric—is precisely one with an enrichment in the category
\mathbf{Met}
of metric spaces and 1-Lipschitz maps, and thus a second-order enrichment in the poset of nonnegative reals. (These examples are explained in detail in
Section

6
.)

To learn more about a partially ordered group or a group with a conjugation-invariant norm, one could choose to analyse its ordinary group homology, or alternatively to analyse the homology of its order complex or its magnitude homology as a metric space. The first option means neglecting the partial ordering or the metric; the others mean neglecting the group operation. In order to keep both forms of structure in view, one might prefer instead to take the magnitude homology of the group as a one-object
\mathbf{Poset}
- or
\mathbf{Met}
-enriched category—this is where the iterated theory comes in.

We investigate iterated magnitude homology for each of these examples. We begin by considering the special case of a strict 2-group, for which the well-known correspondence with homotopy 2-types makes the analysis particularly straightforward, and then extend that analysis to any
\mathbf{Cat}
-enriched group. Applied to a partially ordered group, we learn that iterated magnitude homology is a rather insensitive invariant, failing to distinguish (at least in its first two groups) between a partial order and the equivalence relation it generates. In the case of a normed group, though, things become more interesting. Here we find that the iterated theory captures both topological and metric features, recovering ordinary group homology and at the same time bearing information about the geometry of the group under the norm.

Finally we take the iteration further, constructing an iterated magnitude homology theory for strict
n
-categories for
n\geq 2
. For a certain special class of examples we are able to describe the homology completely, proving a theorem formally analogous to the suspension theorem of classical algebraic topology.

2
Enriched categories

The basic idea of enriched category theory is to describe category-like structures in which, instead of a set of morphisms between every pair of objects, one has an ‘object of morphisms’ drawn from some other category of interest. For this to make sense, as we shall see, it is necessary that the ‘enriching’ category should be
monoidal
. A
monoidal category
is a category
\mathcal{V}
equipped with an associative binary operation
\otimes
on its objects which extends to a functor
\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
, and an object
I
in
\mathcal{V}
which is a unit for
\otimes
. These data must satisfy associativity and unit axioms up to coherent isomorphism; for details, see Chapter VII in
[
32
]
.

We will only consider monoidal structures that are
symmetric
, meaning there are isomorphisms
\gamma_{u,v}:u\otimes v\to v\otimes u
, natural in
u,v\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{V})
and satisfying appropriate coherence conditions. A symmetric monoidal category
(\mathcal{V},\otimes,I)
is said to be
semicartesian
if
I
is the terminal object in
\mathcal{V}
, and
cartesian
if
\otimes
is the categorical product in
\mathcal{V}
.

Example 2.1

•

The cartesian product of sets makes the category
\mathbf{Set}
of sets and functions into a cartesian monoidal category. The unit object for this monoidal structure is the one-element set:
I=\{*\}
.

•

The category of Boolean truth values has two objects,
T
and
F
, and a single non-identity arrow
F\to T
. We will call this category
\mathbf{Truth}
. The operation of conjunction—denoted by
\wedge
—is the categorical product here, making
\mathbf{Truth}
into a cartesian monoidal category with unit object
T
.

•

Any preorder can be regarded as a category in which every hom-set has at most one element. In particular this is true of the poset
[0,\infty]
of nonnegative real numbers, extended to infinity. The objects of the corresponding category are the elements of
[0,\infty]
, with an arrow
u\to v
if and only if
u\geq v
. This category carries many monoidal structures, but in this paper we consider just one of them: the structure in which
u\otimes v=u+v
. The unit for
+
is
0
, which is terminal in
[0,\infty]
, so
([0,\infty],+,0)
is semicartesian.

Let
(\mathcal{V},\otimes,I)
be a monoidal category. A (small)
category enriched in
\mathcal{V}
, or
\mathcal{V}
-category
, consists of a set
\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})
of objects and for each pair
x,y\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})
an object
\mathbf{X}(x,y)
in
\mathcal{V}
. For each
x\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})
there must be a morphism
\mathrm{Id}_{x}:I\to\mathbf{X}(x,x)
in
\mathcal{V}
—thought of as ‘selecting the identity’ on
x
—and for each triple
x,y,z
there must be a morphism
\circ:\mathbf{X}(y,z)\otimes\mathbf{X}(x,y)\to\mathbf{X}(x,z)
in
\mathcal{V}
, performing ‘composition’. These data must satisfy axioms mirroring those in the definition of an ordinary category; details can be found in
[
26
]
.

Example 2.2

•

A category enriched in
(\mathbf{Set},\times,\{*\})
is an ordinary small category. For instance, any group can be regarded as a
\mathbf{Set}
-category with a single object, in which every morphism is invertible.

•

A category enriched in
(\mathbf{Truth},\wedge,T)
is a set equipped with a reflexive, transitive relation—in other words, a preorder. In particular, every poset is a
\mathbf{Truth}
-category.

•

A category enriched in
([0,\infty],+,0)
consists of a set
X
and, for every pair
x,y\in X
, a number
d(x,y)\in[0,\infty]
. The existence of ‘identity morphisms’ here says that for every
x\in X
we have
0\geq d(x,x)
and so
d(x,x)=0
; ‘composition’ gives us, for every triple
x,y,z\in X
, the inequality

d(y,z)+d(x,y)\geq d(x,z).

Thus, as was first observed by Lawvere
[
28
]
, a category enriched in
([0,\infty],+,0)
is a
generalized metric space
: one whose metric need not be separated or symmetric, and may take infinite values.

Any directed graph can be made into a generalized metric space by declaring the distance from a vertex
u
to a vertex
v
to be the length of the shortest directed path from
u
to
v
, or infinity if no such path exists. This metric will always be separated but is typically
not
symmetric nor finite-valued in general. Because we often have such examples in mind, we adopt the nonstandard convention that the term
metric space
means a generalized metric space whose metric is separated. We will use the term
classical metric space
to refer to a space whose metric is separated, symmetric and always finite-valued.

We will sometimes refer to the
underlying (ordinary) category
of a
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathbf{X}
. This is the category
\mathbf{X}_{0}
whose objects are those of
\mathbf{X}
, with hom-sets
\mathbf{X}_{0}(x,y)=\mathcal{V}(I,\mathbf{X}(x,y))
. For instance, the underlying ordinary category of a metric space is the discrete category on its set of points.

There is a notion of
enriched functor
between
\mathcal{V}
-categories, and thus for every monoidal category
(\mathcal{V},\otimes,I)
there is a category of
\mathcal{V}
-categories, which we denote by
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}

[
26
]
. If
(\mathcal{V},\otimes,I)
is symmetric monoidal, then
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
also carries a symmetric monoidal structure: given two
\mathcal{V}
-categories
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, their tensor product
\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y}
has object set
\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})\times\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{Y})
, with hom objects

(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y})((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\mathbf{X}(x,x^{\prime})\otimes\mathbf{Y}(y,y^{\prime}).

(The symmetry of the monoidal structure in
\mathcal{V}
is used to define composition in
\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y}
.) The unit for this monoidal structure is the one-object
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathbf{I}
whose unique hom-object is the unit
I
in
\mathcal{V}
.

Example 2.3

•

The monoidal category of categories enriched in
\mathbf{Set}
is just the ordinary category
\mathbf{Cat}
of small categories, with tensor product the categorical product and unit the terminal category.

•

A
\mathbf{Truth}
-functor between preorders is precisely a monotone map. The tensor product of preorders
P
and
Q
is the product set
P\times Q
equipped with the preorder in which
(p,q)\leq(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
if and only if
p\leq p^{\prime}
and
q\leq q^{\prime}
. We will denote the monoidal category of preorders and monotone maps by
\mathbf{PreOrd}
, and its full subcategory of posets by
\mathbf{Poset}
.

•

An enriched functor between generalized metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
is a function
f:X\to Y
such that
d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
; in other words, a 1-Lipschitz map from
X
to
Y
. Hereafter we will refer to these as
short maps
. The tensor product
X\otimes Y
is the product set
X\times Y
equipped with the
\ell^{1}
product metric:

d_{X\otimes Y}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}).

We will denote the monoidal category of generalized metric spaces and short maps by
\mathbf{GMet}
, and its full subcategory of metric spaces by
\mathbf{Met}
.

Given monoidal categories
(\mathcal{V},\otimes,I_{\mathcal{V}})
and
(\mathcal{W},\bullet,I_{\mathcal{W}})
, a functor
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{W}
is
monoidal
if there are morphisms
\phi:I_{\mathcal{W}}\to F(I_{\mathcal{V}})
and
\phi_{u,v}:F(u)\bullet F(v)\to F(u\otimes v)
, natural in
u,v\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{V})
and satisfying certain coherence conditions. If these morphisms are isomorphisms,
F
is said to be
strong monoidal
. If the monoidal structures on
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
are symmetric then one can ask for
F
to be
strong symmetric monoidal
, meaning, of course, that it respects the symmetric structure: for all
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
we have
\phi_{v,u}\circ\gamma_{F(u),F(v)}=F(\gamma_{u,v})\circ\phi_{v,u}
.

Conventions

Throughout this paper,
(\mathcal{V},\otimes,I)
will always denote a semicartesian monoidal category, and
(\mathbb{A},\otimes,\mathbb{I})
will always denote an abelian category with a symmetric monoidal structure which is
closed
, meaning that for every object
C
in
\mathbb{A}
the functor
-\otimes C:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
has a right adjoint. Since left adjoints preserve colimits, the closed structure on
\mathbb{A}
(along with the symmetry) guarantees that the tensor product preserves arbitrary coproducts in each variable.

We denote the simplex category by
\Delta
. For any symmetric monoidal closed abelian category
\mathbb{A}
, the category
[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]
of simplicial objects in
\mathbb{A}
is also abelian, and carries a symmetric monoidal closed structure inherited pointwise from
\mathbb{A}
, which we denote by
([\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}],\otimes_{\mathrm{pw}},I_{\mathrm{pw}})
.

3
Magnitude homology

All definitions in this section are due to Leinster and Shulman
[
31
]
(following Hepworth and Willerton
[
24
]
). They define the
magnitude homology
of a category with a semicartesian enrichment to be the homology of the Moore complex associated to a certain simplicial object, the
magnitude nerve
.

Definition 3.1

Let
(\mathcal{V},\otimes,I)
be a semicartesian monoidal category, and
(\mathbb{A},\otimes,\mathbb{I})
a symmetric monoidal closed abelian category. Let
\Sigma:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{A}
be a strong symmetric monoidal functor. The
magnitude nerve
of a
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathbf{X}
with respect to
\Sigma
is a simplicial object
MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})
in
\mathbb{A}
given for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
by

MB_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=\bigoplus_{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbf{X}}\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x_{0},x_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x_{n-1},x_{n}).

In particular, since the empty tensor product is the unit object, we have
MB_{0}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=\bigoplus_{x\in\mathbf{X}}\mathbb{I}
.
The simplicial structure is defined as follows:

•

For
1\leq i\leq n-1
the face map
\delta_{n}^{i}:MB_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\to MB_{n-1}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})
discards the object
x_{i}
from the tuple indexing each summand, using maps provided by the monoidal structure of
\Sigma
and composition at the object
x_{i}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x_{i-1},x_{i})\otimes\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x_{i},x_{i+1})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\cong}\Sigma(\mathbf{X}(x_{i-1},x_{i})\otimes\mathbf{X}(x_{i},x_{i+1}))

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\Sigma(\circ)}\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x_{i-1},x_{i+1}).

The face map
\delta_{n}^{0}
discards the object
x_{0}
using the monoidal structure of
\Sigma
and the terminal map
\mathbf{X}(x_{0},x_{1})\to I
in
\mathcal{V}
, which exists because
\mathcal{V}
is semicartesian. The map
\delta_{n}^{n}
discards
x_{n}
in the same way.

•

For each
i
, the degeneracy map
\sigma_{n}^{i}:MB_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\to MB_{n+1}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})
is induced by ‘inserting the identity’ on
x_{i}
, using the monoidal structure of
\Sigma
:

\mathbb{I}\xrightarrow{\cong}\Sigma I\xrightarrow{\Sigma(\mathrm{Id}_{x_{i}})}\Sigma X(x_{i},x_{i}).

To show that these maps satisfy the simplicial identities makes use of the fact that the monoidal structure of
\Sigma
is symmetric and strong.

From any simplicial object
B
in an abelian category one can form two chain complexes. The
unnormalized
chain complex
C(B)
has
C_{n}(B)=B_{n}
, with differential given by an alternating sum of face maps:
\partial_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\delta_{i}
. The
normalized
chain complex
N(B)
, also known as the
Moore complex
, is obtained by quotienting
C(B)
by a subcomplex ‘spanned by the degenerate simplices’:

N(B)=C(B)/D(B)

where
D_{n}(B)=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(C_{n-1}(B))
. The complex
D(B)
is always acyclic, so the unnormalized and normalized complexes are naturally quasi-isomorphic:

H_{\bullet}(N(B))\cong H_{\bullet}(C(B)).

This is proved, for example, as Theorem 8.3.8 in Weibel
[
46
]
.

Definition 3.2

Let
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathbb{A}
and
\Sigma
be as in
3.1
. The
unnormalized magnitude complex
of a
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathbf{X}
with respect to
\Sigma
is

\widetilde{MC}_{\bullet}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=C(MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}))

and the
normalized magnitude complex
is

MC_{\bullet}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=N(MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})).

The
magnitude homology
of
\mathbf{X}
with respect to
\Sigma
is

MH_{\bullet}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=H_{\bullet}(MC^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}))\cong H_{\bullet}(\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})).

In this paper we will be interested in the magnitude homology of categories enriched in each of the three monoidal categories discussed in
Section

2
, namely
(\mathbf{Set},\times,\{*\})
,
(\mathbf{Truth},\wedge,T)
and
([0,\infty],+,0)
. The category
\mathbf{Truth}
can be embedded into
\mathbf{Set}
by sending the object
T
to
\{*\}
and
F
to the empty set, and this embedding is strong symmetric monoidal. On
\mathbf{Set}
, we will always take as coefficients the free abelian group functor
\Sigma:\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Ab}
, and we obtain our coefficient functor on
\mathbf{Truth}
by restriction of
\Sigma
. Thus, in practice, we treat preorders as ordinary categories.

Example 3.3

The simplex category
\Delta
can be identified with the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Cat}
on nonempty finite linear orders; we write
[n]
for the linear order on
n+1
objects. The
nerve
of a small category
\mathbf{X}
is then the simplicial set defined by

S(\mathbf{X})_{n}=\mathbf{Cat}([n],\mathbf{X}).

Concretely, the
n
-simplices of
S(\mathbf{X})
are
n
-tuples of composable morphisms in
\mathbf{X}
. The
classifying space
of
\mathbf{X}
, denoted
\mathbb{B}\mathbf{X}
, is the geometric realization
|S(\mathbf{X})|
.

The magnitude complex of
\mathbf{X}
with respect to the free abelian group functor
\Sigma
is precisely the complex of simplicial chains in the nerve of
\mathbf{X}
. It follows that
MH_{\bullet}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})
coincides with the singular homology of the classifying space
\mathbb{B}\mathbf{X}
. In particular, the magnitude homology of a group (regarded as a one-object category) is its ordinary group homology, and the magnitude homology of a poset is the homology of its order complex.

In simple cases the normalized magnitude complex has the following convenient description.

Lemma 3.4

Suppose
\mathbf{X}
is a
\mathcal{V}
-category such that
\mathbf{X}(x,x)\cong I
for every
x\in\mathbf{X}
. Then the normalized magnitude complex of
\mathbf{X}
is given in degree
n
by

MC_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=\bigoplus\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x_{0},x_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x_{n-1},x_{n})

where the sum is over tuples
x_{0},\ldots x_{n}
such that
x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}
for all
i
.

Proof.

The condition on
\mathbf{X}
implies that the map
\mathrm{Id}_{x}:I\to\mathbf{X}(x,x)
is an isomorphism for every
x\in\mathbf{X}
; the same is of course true of the induced map
\mathbb{I}\to\Sigma\mathbf{X}(x,x)
, and therefore of every degeneracy map. Taking the quotient of
\widetilde{MC}_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})
by the image of every degeneracy map thus sends to 0 every summand associated to a tuple
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
in which
x_{i}=x_{i+1}
for some
i
.
∎

We record a miniature example for illustration and for later reference.

Example 3.5

Let
\mathbb{S}^{1}
denote the category

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

(Identity arrows not pictured.) The complex

MH_{\bullet}^{\Sigma}(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&*=0,1\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Magnitude homology is designed to categorify
magnitude
: a numerical invariant of enriched categories with a remarkable web of connections to notions of ‘size’ of relevance in different corners of mathematics. The study of magnitude precedes the construction of magnitude homology by almost a decade—originating with Leinster in
[
29
]
—and is especially well developed in the context of metric spaces. The magnitude of a metric space is best understood not just as a single number but as a real-valued function or power series
[
30
]
, which has been shown to carry information about the intrinsic volumes of a space (e.g.
[
4
,
19
,
36
]
), curvature and the Willmore energy
[
20
]
, and Minkowski dimension
[
35
]
. (These references are far from exhaustive.)

The desire for magnitude homology to recover, via its Euler characteristic, the magnitude of a (finite) metric space motivates a particular choice of coefficient functor on
([0,\infty],+,0)
, which we now describe.

Definition 3.6

Let
\mathbf{Ab}^{[0,\infty)}
denote the category of abelian groups graded by the nonnegative reals. Given
A\in\mathbf{Ab}^{[0,\infty)}
we write
A^{\ell}
for the value of
A
at
\ell\in[0,\infty)
, and refer to
\ell
as the
length grading
of
A^{\ell}
.

The category
\mathbf{Ab}^{[0,\infty)}
inherits, grading-wise, many of the valuable properties of
\mathbf{Ab}
—in particular, since
\mathbf{Ab}
is abelian, so is
\mathbf{Ab}^{[0,\infty)}
. It carries a closed symmetric monoidal structure given by the
convolution product

\circledast
, for which

(A\circledast B)^{\ell}=\bigoplus_{r+s=\ell}A^{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}B^{s}

for each
\ell\in[0,\infty)
. The unit object has
\mathbb{Z}
in length grading 0, and 0 in all other gradings. There is a strong symmetric monoidal functor
\Sigma^{*}:[0,\infty]\to\mathbf{Ab}^{[0,\infty)}
given by
\Sigma^{*}(\infty)=0
and, for
t<\infty
,

\Sigma^{\ell}(t)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&\ell=t\\
0&\ell\neq t.\end{cases}

The magnitude homology of a metric space is defined by Leinster and Shulman to be its magnitude homology with respect to
\Sigma^{*}

[
31
]
. In the case of a graph, this agrees with the earlier definition given by Hepworth and Willerton
[
24
]
.
3.8
will describe the magnitude chain complex explicitly. To simplify notation we suppress
\Sigma^{*}
, writing
MC_{k}^{\ell}(X)
and
MH_{k}^{\ell}(X)
for the magnitude complex—respectively, magnitude homology—of a metric space
X
in length grading
\ell
and homological degree
k
.

Definition 3.7

A point
z
in a metric space
X
is said to be
between
points
x
and
y
if
d(x,y)=d(x,z)+d(z,y)
. If, in addition,
x\neq z\neq y
, we say
z
is
strictly between

x
and
y
. Points
x,y\in X
are
non-adjacent
if there exists a point
z\in X
lying strictly between them, and
adjacent
otherwise.

Proposition 3.8

(
[
31
]
, Definition 3.3)

The normalized magnitude chain complex of a metric space
X
is given for
\ell\in[0,\infty)
and

by

MC_{n}^{\ell}(X)=\mathbb{Z}\cdot\left\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\,\middle|\,x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}\text{ for all $i$ and }\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d(x_{i},x_{i+1})=\ell\right\}.

The differential is
\partial=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}\delta_{i}
where
\delta_{i}
is specified on generators by

\delta_{i}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})

if
x_{i}
is between
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i+1}
, and
\delta_{i}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})=0
otherwise. ∎

Since
\partial_{1}=0
, we have
MH_{0}^{\ell}(\mathbf{X})=MC_{0}^{\ell}(\mathbf{X})
. In length grading 0 this group is freely generated by the set of points in
X
, while in length gradings
\ell>0
it vanishes. Magnitude homology in degree 1 is also straightforward to describe:

Theorem 3.9

(
[
31
]
, Theorem 4.3)

Let

be a metric space. Then

is the free abelian group on the set of ordered pairs
(x,y)
of distinct points in

such that
x
and

are adjacent and

.

The theorem implies, for example, that the first magnitude homology of a closed subset

of

vanishes if and only if

is convex (
[
31
]
, Corollary 4.9); otherwise, magnitude homology records quantitative information about the ‘size’ of its concavities. The higher magnitude homology groups similarly capture geometric, as opposed to topological, information. For instance, Asao has established that the second magnitude homology of a metric space detects the presence of closed geodesics, and that the curvature of a space strongly influences the gradings in which its magnitude homology of any degree can be non-vanishing (Theorem 5.3 and Corollary 4.6 in
[
1
]
).

To close this section, and to prepare the ground for the rest of the paper, we return to the general setting of semicartesianly enriched categories. The proof of

4
The Künneth theorem

As a first application of the monoidal structure of the magnitude nerve (
3.10
), in this section we prove a general Künneth theorem for the magnitude homology of semicartesianly enriched categories.

The classical Künneth theorem relates the singular homology of a product of two topological spaces
X
and
Y
to the tensor product of their homologies. It is proved as the composite of two results: the topological Eilenberg–Zilber theorem, which says that the singular chain complex
C_{\bullet}(X\times Y)
is quasi-isomorphic to the tensor product
C_{\bullet}(X)\otimes C_{\bullet}(Y)
, and an algebraic Künneth theorem, which relates the homology of a tensor product of chain complexes to the tensor product of their homologies. (E.g. Hatcher
[
23
]
, §3.B.)

Our proof of the Künneth theorem for magnitude homology has the same structure. Using the monoidal structure of the magnitude nerve, we first prove an Eilenberg–Zilber-type theorem for the magnitude chain complex, and from there we can derive the main result via the algebraic Künneth theorem. Underlying our Eilenberg–Zilber theorem is the following fundamental fact about double complexes arising from bisimplicial objects, which is proved as Theorem 8.5.1 in Weibel
[
46
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(Bisimplicial Eilenberg–Zilber theorem)

Let
B
be a bisimplicial object in an abelian category. Then there
is a natural isomorphism

H_{\bullet}N(\mathrm{diag}(B))\cong H_{\bullet}\mathrm{Tot}(CB).

Moreover, there exist natural chain maps

C(\mathrm{diag}(B))\rightleftarrows\mathrm{Tot}(CB)

which realize this isomorphism on homology. ∎

Eventually we will want to use the full strength of
4.1
—indeed, it will be central to many of the arguments and computations in later sections. For now, though, we are concerned with a special case which is perhaps more immediately suggestive of its connection to the topological Eilenberg–Zilber theorem. Here,
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})
denotes the category of chain complexes in
\mathbb{A}
, and
\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}
denotes the standard tensor product of chain complexes.

Corollary 4.2

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a symmetric monoidal abelian category. For every
A,A^{\prime}\in[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]
there is an isomorphism

H_{\bullet}C(A\otimes_{\mathrm{pw}}A^{\prime})\cong H_{\bullet}(C(A)\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}C(A^{\prime}))

natural in
A
and
A^{\prime}
. Moreover, there exist natural chain maps

C(A\otimes_{\mathrm{pw}}A^{\prime})\rightleftarrows C(A)\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}C(A^{\prime})

realizing this isomorphism on homology.

Proof.

Given any two simplicial objects
A
and
A^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{A}
, the composite

\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\times\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\xrightarrow{A\times A^{\prime}}\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\xrightarrow{\otimes}\mathbb{A}.

is a bisimplicial object. Denote it by
A\boxtimes A^{\prime}
; so
(A\boxtimes A^{\prime})_{nm}=A_{n}\otimes A^{\prime}_{m}
, and the vertical face and degeneracy operators are those of
A
, while the horizontal operators are those of
A^{\prime}
. Taking the diagonal of this bisimplicial object yields the pointwise tensor product of the functors
A
and
A^{\prime}
:

A\otimes_{\mathrm{pw}}A^{\prime}=\mathrm{diag}(A\boxtimes A^{\prime}).

Meanwhile, the total complex of
C(A\boxtimes A^{\prime})
is given in degree
n
by

\mathrm{Tot}_{n}C(A\boxtimes A^{\prime})=\bigoplus_{j+k=n}A_{j}\otimes A^{\prime}_{k}

and on comparing differentials one sees there is a chain complex isomorphism

\mathrm{Tot}\>C(A\boxtimes A^{\prime})\cong C(A)\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}C(A^{\prime}).

The result now follows from
4.1
, taking
B=A\boxtimes A^{\prime}
and making use of the fact that the normalized and unnormalized chain complexes of any simplicial object in
\mathbb{A}
are naturally quasi-isomorphic (Theorem 8.3.8 in
[
46
]
).
∎

Combining
4.2
with our
3.10
delivers the Eilenberg–Zilber theorem for the magnitude chain complex, as follows.

Theorem 4.3

(Eilenberg–Zilber theorem for magnitude chains)

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a semicartesian category, let
\mathbb{A}
be a symmetric monoidal closed abelian category and let
\Sigma:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{A}
be a strong symmetric monoidal functor. Given any two
\mathcal{V}
-categories
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
there exist chain maps

\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y})\rightleftarrows\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})

natural in
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, inducing an isomorphism on homology:

MH_{\bullet}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y})\cong H_{\bullet}\left(\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})\right).

(1)

Proof.

The strong monoidal structure of the magnitude nerve (
3.10
) supplies an isomorphism

MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y})\cong MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{\mathrm{pw}}MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y}),

natural in
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
. Taking the unnormalized chain complex on both sides and applying
4.2
on the right yields natural chain maps

\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y})\rightleftarrows C(MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{\mathrm{pw}}MB^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y}))\rightleftarrows\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})

inducing the claimed isomorphism on homology.
∎

To relate the right hand side of the isomorphism in (
1
) to a tensor product of homologies requires an algebraic Künneth theorem. We will employ it in the following form—proved, for instance, as Theorem 2.4 in May
[
34
]
.

Theorem 4.4

Let
R
be a principal ideal domain and
\mathbb{A}=\mathbf{Mod}_{R}
its category of modules. Let
C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})
be a chain complex of flat modules. Then given any other complex
D\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is a short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to\bigoplus_{k}H_{k}(C)\otimes_{R}H_{n-k}(D)

\displaystyle\to H_{n}\left(C\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A})}D\right)

\displaystyle\to\bigoplus_{k}\mathrm{Tor}\left(H_{k}(C),H_{n-k-1}(D)\right)\to 0

natural in
C
and
D
. The sequence splits, but the splitting is not natural. ∎

Combining
4.4
with
4.3
establishes the Künneth theorem for magnitude homology. As
4.4
is stated for categories of modules over a principal ideal domain, that is the context in which our theorem holds.

Theorem 4.5

(Künneth theorem for magnitude homology)

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a semicartesian category and
R
a principal ideal domain, and let
\Sigma:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbf{Mod}_{R}
be a strong symmetric monoidal functor. Suppose
\mathbf{X}
is a
\mathcal{V}
-category with the property that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
R
-module
{MC}_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})
is flat. Then given any
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathbf{Y}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is a short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to\bigoplus_{k}MH_{k}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{R}MH_{n-k}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\to MH_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\to\bigoplus_{k}\mathrm{Tor}\left(MH_{k}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}),MH_{n-k-1}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})\right)\to 0

natural in
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
. The sequence splits, but the splitting is not natural.

Proof.

4.4
says there is a natural short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to\bigoplus_{k}MH_{k}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{R}MH_{n-k}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\to H_{n}\left(\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\otimes_{\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbf{Mod}_{R})}\widetilde{MC}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})\right)

\displaystyle\to\bigoplus_{k}\mathrm{Tor}\left(MH_{k}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}),MH_{n-k-1}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{Y})\right)\to 0

which splits.
4.3
tells us the middle term is naturally isomorphic to
MH_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y})
, and this gives the result.
∎

Because the usual choice of coefficient functor
\Sigma^{*}
on
[0,\infty]
is not valued in a category of modules over a ring but in a category of graded modules,
4.5
does not immediately specialize to metric spaces. However, the Eilenberg–Zilber theorem for magnitude chains still applies in this setting, providing a Künneth formula for generalized metric spaces, as follows. In the case that
X
and
Y
are classical metric spaces (in particular, symmetric),
4.6
recovers Proposition 4.3 of
[
7
]
, itself a generalization of Theorem 5.1 in
[
24
]
(the Künneth formula for undirected graphs).

Theorem 4.6

Let
X
be a generalized metric space. Given any other generalized metric space
Y
, there is in each length grading
0\leq\ell<\infty
a short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}r+s=\ell\\
j+k=n\end{subarray}}MH_{j}^{r}({X})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}MH_{k}^{s}({Y})

\displaystyle\to MH_{n}^{\ell}(X\otimes Y)

\displaystyle\to\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}r+s=\ell\\
j+k=n\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tor}\left(MH_{j}^{r}({X}),MH_{k-1}^{s}({Y})\right)\to 0

natural in
X
and
Y
. The sequence splits, but the splitting is not natural.

Proof.

4.3
tells us that for spaces
X
and
Y
there is a natural isomorphism of bigraded abelian groups

MH_{\bullet}^{*}({X}\otimes{Y})\cong H_{\bullet}\left(\widetilde{MC}({X})\circledast_{\mathrm{Ch}}\widetilde{MC}({Y})\right)

(2)

where
\circledast_{\mathrm{Ch}}
denotes the tensor product of chain complexes in
\mathbf{Ab}^{[0,\infty)}
. The magnitude complex of any generalized metric space is free, and hence flat, in every length grading. We can therefore apply the algebraic Künneth theorem grading-wise on the right of (
2
) to obtain the result.
∎

5
Iterated magnitude homology

We turn now to the main theme of the paper, which is to construct a modified ‘magnitude homology’ theory applicable to categories with a second- or higher-order enrichment—categories enriched in some category of
\mathcal{V}
-categories—where a coefficient functor on
\mathcal{V}
has already been chosen. Our template for this construction will be the
classifying space

\mathbb{B}\mathbf{X}
of a bicategory
\mathbf{X}
.

In modern texts, the classifying space of a bicategory is usually defined to be the geometric realization of its
geometric nerve
: the simplicial set
S(\mathbf{X})
specified by

S(\mathbf{X})_{n}=\mathbf{Bicat}([n],\mathbf{X})

where
\mathbf{Bicat}
is the 1-category of bicategories whose morphisms are lax 2-functors that preserve identities strictly. This definition is found implicitly in Street
[
44
]
in 1987 and given its succinct description by Duskin
[
16
]
in 2002.

This construction has the virtue that it evidently extends the ordinary classifying space functor along the inclusion of small categories into bicategories. When it comes to strict 2-categories, though, there is at least one other way one might think about constructing something like a classifying space. Segal
[
42
]
observed in 1968 that the nerve
N\mathbf{C}
of any topological category
\mathbf{C}
(i.e. any category internal to
\mathbf{Top}
) naturally carries the structure of a simplicial space, and he defined the classifying space of a topological category to be the geometric realization of that simplicial space,
|N\mathbf{C}|
. Meanwhile, if
\mathbf{X}
is a strict 2-category, then applying the ordinary classifying space functor locally—that is, to each hom-category in
\mathbf{X}
—transforms
\mathbf{X}
into a topological category
\mathbb{B}_{*}\mathbf{X}
with discrete space of objects. Segal’s construction then gives an alternative ‘classifying space’ functor

|N\mathbb{B}_{*}(-)|:2\mathbf{Cat}\to\mathbf{Top}

where
2\mathbf{Cat}
is the category of strict 2-categories.

Although the Segal construction and the Duskin–Street construction look rather different, where both are applicable they give equivalent results: Bullejos and Cegarra prove in Theorem 1 of
[
9
]
that for any strict 2-category
\mathbf{X}
there is a natural homotopy equivalence
\mathbb{B}\mathbf{X}\simeq|N\mathbb{B}_{*}\mathbf{X}|
.

We are going to follow Segal’s lead, using the magnitude nerve in place of the ordinary classifying space functor, to define a homology theory for categories enriched in
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
, where
\mathcal{V}
is any semicartesian category. First we need to verify that
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
is semicartesian, provided
\mathcal{V}
is.

Lemma 5.1

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a semicartesian category. Then the monoidal structure on
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
induced by that of
\mathcal{V}
is semicartesian.

Proof.

The unit for the monoidal product on
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
is the one-object
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathbf{I}
whose sole hom-object is the unit
I
in
\mathcal{V}
. Given any
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathbf{C}
we can define a
\mathcal{V}
-functor
T:\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{I}
by sending each object in
\mathbf{C}
to the unique object in
\mathbf{I}
; as
I
is terminal, there is for each
a,b\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{C})
a unique map
\mathbf{C}(a,b)\to\mathbf{I}(Ta,Tb)=I
. This determines
T
uniquely, so
\mathbf{I}
is terminal in
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
.
∎

Now let
\mathbb{A}
be a symmetric monoidal closed abelian category and
\Sigma:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{A}
a strong symmetric monoidal functor. Let
\mathbf{X}
be a category enriched in
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
. As the magnitude nerve functor
MB^{\Sigma}:\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}\to[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]
is strong symmetric monoidal (
3.10
), we can consider the magnitude nerve of
\mathbf{X}
with respect to
MB^{\Sigma}
. According to
3.1
, this is a simplicial object in
[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]
; in other words,
MB^{MB^{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{X})
is a bisimplicial object in
\mathbb{A}
.

Definition 5.2

The
double magnitude nerve
of a
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-category
\mathbf{X}
is

MB^{MB^{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{X})\in[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\times\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}].

The
iterated magnitude nerve
of
\mathbf{X}
is the simplicial object in
\mathbb{A}

MB^{2\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=\mathrm{diag}\>MB^{MB^{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{X}),

the
iterated magnitude complex
of
\mathbf{X}
is the chain complex

MC^{2\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})=C(MB^{2\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})),

and the
iterated magnitude homology
of
\mathbf{X}
is its homology:

MH^{2\Sigma}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{X})=H_{\bullet}(MC^{2\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})).

The bisimplicial Eilenberg–Zilber theorem (
4.1
) implies that iterated magnitude homology can equally well be realized as the total homology of the chains in the double magnitude nerve: there is a natural isomorphism

MH_{\bullet}^{2\Sigma}(-)\cong H_{\bullet}\mathrm{Tot}\>C\left(MB^{MB^{\Sigma}}(-)\right).

(3)

It will often be this description that we use in computations. However, it is the definition in terms of the iterated magnitude nerve that lets us compare the iterated magnitude homology of a strict 2-category with the homology of its classifying space.

Theorem 5.3

Let
\Sigma:\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Ab}
be the free abelian group functor. Then for every strict 2-category
\mathbf{X}
we have
MH_{\bullet}^{2\Sigma}(\mathbf{X})\cong H_{\bullet}(\mathbb{B}\mathbf{X})
, naturally in
\mathbf{X}
.

Proof.

By design, the double magnitude nerve of
\mathbf{X}
with respect to
\Sigma
is the bisimplicial abelian group freely generated by the ‘double nerve’
NN\mathbf{X}
that Bullejos and Cegarra describe in
[
9
]
(pages 9–10). It is shown in the proof of their Theorem 1 that the geometric realization of
\mathrm{diag}(NN\mathbf{X})
is naturally homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{B}\mathbf{X}
. Since
MC^{2\Sigma}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{X})
is the Moore complex of
MB^{2\Sigma}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{Z}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(NN\mathbf{X})
, this gives the result.
∎

6
Enriched groups

The remainder of the paper is dedicated to investigating the information carried by iterated magnitude homology when it is interpreted in two rather different contexts. The first of these is drawn from group theory, where second-order enrichment occurs quite naturally; in this section we outline three classes of examples. The main definition is as follows.

Definition 6.1

A
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-group
is a one-object
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-category—equivalently, a monoid object in
(\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat},\otimes,\mathbf{I})
—whose underlying ordinary category is a group.

In other words, a
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-group is a group whose set of elements also carries the structure of a
\mathcal{V}
-category, and in which multiplication is a
\mathcal{V}
-functor. Inversion is
not
required to be a
\mathcal{V}
-functor.

If the monoidal structure on
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
happens to be cartesian, one can also talk about its
internal group objects
, and these can be characterized as those
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-groups in which inversion is a
\mathcal{V}
-functor. If
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
is not cartesian—for instance, if
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
is
\mathbf{GMet}
—the absence of diagonal maps means that internal groups are not defined (see
6.7
). Internal groups are a more widely familiar concept, well-motivated by many examples in geometry and topology, but the notion of
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-enrichment is also a natural one to consider, as our examples will illustrate.

The study of
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-groups has recent precedent. Clementino, Martins-Ferreira and Montoli’s 2019 paper
[
14
]
studies the category of preordered groups, which are groups with a second-order enrichment in
(\mathbf{Truth},\wedge)
. Clementino and Montoli
[
13
]
have since extended the investigation to groups with a second-order enrichment in any quantale, which encompasses the case of groups equipped with a translation-invariant metric (see
6.5
, below) but not, for instance, any strict 2-group whose category of elements is not a preorder (see
6.3
).
What we call
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-groups, Clementino and Montoli call ‘
\mathcal{V}
-groups’—we have chosen the slightly more unwieldy term to emphasise the second-order enrichment and specifically to avoid referring to strict 2-groups as ‘
\mathbf{Set}
-groups’. The following statement is proved as Proposition 3.1 in
[
13
]
.

Proposition 6.2

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a quantale, and let
G
be a group whose set of elements carries the structure of a
\mathcal{V}
-category. That structure makes
G
a
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-group if and only if it is
translation invariant
, meaning that for every
g\in G
the maps
g\cdot-:G\to G
and
-\cdot g:G\to G
are both
\mathcal{V}
-functors. ∎

In the next section we will analyse the iterated magnitude homology in low degrees of a
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-group where
\mathcal{V}=\mathbf{Set}
(so
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}=\mathbf{Cat}
),
\mathcal{V}=\mathbf{Truth}
(so
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}=\mathbf{PreOrd}
), or
\mathcal{V}=[0,\infty]
(so
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}=\mathbf{GMet}
). To make the analysis as concrete as possible, we will first describe three types of naturally occurring example: strict 2-groups, partially ordered groups, and any group equipped with a norm that is constant on conjugacy classes.

Example 6.3

The class of
\mathbf{Cat}
-groups encompasses all groups internal to
\mathbf{Cat}
. These are usually called
strict 2-groups

[
3
]
or
strict
gr
-categories

[
43
]
. Explicitly, a strict 2-group is a category
\mathbf{G}
equipped with functors
m:\mathbf{G}\times\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}
(performing multiplication),
e:\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{G}
(selecting the identity) and
i:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}
(taking inverses) which satisfy axioms imitating those for an ordinary group. In particular, the action of
m
,
e
and
i
on objects makes the set
\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{G})
into a group—and these functors make the set
\mathrm{arr}(\mathbf{G})
of arrows into a group as well. Given arrows
p:g_{1}\to g_{2}
and
q:h_{1}\to h_{2}
, the function

(\mathbf{G}\times\mathbf{G})((g_{1},h_{1}),(g_{2},h_{2}))=\mathbf{G}(g_{1},g_{2})\times\mathbf{G}(h_{1},h_{2})\xrightarrow{m_{(g_{1},h_{1}),(g_{2},h_{2})}}\mathbf{G}(g_{1}h_{1},g_{2}h_{2})

provided by the functoriality of
m
tells us how to multiply
p
and
q
. The identity in
\mathrm{arr}(\mathbf{G})
is the identity arrow on the identity object
e
; the inverse of an arrow
g_{1}\to g_{2}
is an arrow
g_{1}^{-1}\to g_{2}^{-1}
.

So a strict 2-group really does comprise two groups—a group of objects and a group of arrows—which are intertwined in a specific way. The rules for this intertwining are captured by the data of a
crossed module
. A crossed module is a group homomorphism
\phi:H\to G
along with an action of
G
on
H
by automorphisms, such that
\phi
is equivariant with respect to conjugation in
G
and satisfies an equation known as the
Pfeiffer identity
. These conditions ensure, in particular, that
\mathrm{im}(\phi)
is a normal subgroup of
G
. Indeed, the simplest example of a crossed module is given by the inclusion of a normal subgroup
N\hookrightarrow G
. In the corresponding strict 2-group
\mathbf{G}_{N}
, the objects are the elements of
G
and an arrow
g\to h
is a pair
(k,g)
such that
k\in N
and
kg=h
. Composition of arrows is given by multiplication in
N
, and the functor
m:\mathbf{G}_{N}\times\mathbf{G}_{N}\to\mathbf{G}_{N}
is defined on objects by multiplication in
G
and on arrows by multiplication in the semidirect product
N\rtimes G
.

We can travel in the other direction, too, to construct a crossed module from any strict 2-group
\mathbf{G}
. This makes use of the source and target maps
s,t:\mathrm{arr}(\mathbf{G})\rightrightarrows\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{G})
, both of which are group homomorphisms. Let
G=\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{G})
and
H=\ker(s)
; that is,
H=\{p:e\to g\mid g\in G\}
. The target map restricts to a homomorphism
t:H\to G
and one can define an action of
G
on
H
making
t
into a crossed module. This construction extends to an equivalence between the category of strict 2-groups and a certain category of crossed modules; for a textbook account, see §12.8 of Mac Lane
[
32
]
.

That equivalence is of interest—and will be useful in the next section—because crossed modules have played an important role historically in homotopy theory. They were introduced by Whitehead in the 1940s as an algebraic model for
homotopy 2-types
: spaces whose homotopy groups
\pi_{n}
vanish for
n>2

[
47
]
. From any crossed module
H\xrightarrow{\phi}G
one can build a space
\mathbb{B}(H\xrightarrow{\phi}G)
with the property that
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{B}(H\xrightarrow{\phi}G))\cong G/\mathrm{im}(\phi)
,
\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B}(H\xrightarrow{\phi}G))\cong\ker(\phi)
and
\pi_{n}(\mathbb{B}(H\xrightarrow{\phi}G))=0
for all
n>2
. This, in turn, extends to an equivalence between a certain homotopy category of crossed modules and the homotopy category of pointed, connected homotopy 2-types
[
33
]
.

For example, given a normal subgroup
N\hookrightarrow G
, the space
\mathbb{B}(N\hookrightarrow G)
has fundamental group
G/N
. Given any strict 2-group
\mathbf{G}
, the fundamental group of the space
\mathbb{B}(H\xrightarrow{t}G)
is
G/E
, where
E=\{g\in G\mid\exists\,e\xrightarrow{p}g\}
.

Example 6.4

A
partially ordered group
is a group
G
equipped with a partial order on its elements that is translation invariant in the sense that for all
g\leq h
and all
k\in G
we have

gk\leq hk\text{ and }kg\leq kh.

The study of partially ordered groups has a rich history going back to work of Birkhoff, Clifford and others in the 1940s (e.g.
[
6
,
15
,
18
]
), and further still in the case of ordered fields.

Since monotone maps are
\mathbf{Truth}
-functors,
6.2
tells us that every partially ordered group is a
\mathbf{PreOrd}
-group. In particular, every partially ordered group is a
\mathbf{Cat}
-group—but the interesting partially ordered groups are
not
strict 2-groups. Indeed, a partially ordered group is a strict 2-group if and only if inversion is a monotone map, in which case for all
g,h\in G
such that
g\leq h
we have
g^{-1}\leq h^{-1}
and thus, by translation invariance,
h=hg^{-1}g\leq hh^{-1}g=g
. In other words, only the trivial partial order on
G
makes
G
into a strict 2-group.

In
[
14
]
, Clementino
et al
study
\mathbf{PreOrd}
-groups in generality. Central to their analysis is the fact that to give a group
G
a
\mathbf{PreOrd}
-enrichment is equivalent to specifying a submonoid of
G
which is closed under conjugation (
[
14
]
, Proposition 2.1). This submonoid is the
positive cone

P_{G}=\{g\in G\mid e\leq g\}
. A preorder makes
G
into a strict 2-group if and only if
P_{G}
is a normal subgroup, which holds if and only if the preorder is symmetric and therefore a congruence on
G
.

Example 6.5

When group theorists speak of a ‘left-invariant’ or ‘right-invariant’ metric on a group, they mean a metric with respect to which left-translation (respectively, right-translation) by any given element is an isometry. A ‘bi-invariant’ metric is one which is both left- and right-invariant in this sense. This appears stronger than the notion of translation invariance in
6.2
, which only requires that translation be an enriched functor—in the case of metric spaces, this means a short map. In fact, the two notions are equivalent: if right-translation by every element is a short map, then for every
g
,
h
and
k
in
G
we have

d(g,h)=d(gkk^{-1},hkk^{-1})\leq d(gk,hk)\leq d(g,h)

so right-translation by
k
is an isometry, and in the same way so is left-translation. By
6.2
, then, a bi-invariant metric on a group is the same thing as an enrichment in the category
\mathbf{Met}
of metric spaces and short maps.

In
Section

7
we will see that the iterated magnitude homology of a
\mathbf{Met}
-group is easiest to describe in terms of the associated group norm. A
group norm
on a group
G
with identity element
e
is a function
|-|:G\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

1.

|e|=0
and
|g|>0
for all
g\in G\backslash\{e\}
;

2.

|gh|\leq|g|+|h|
for all
g,h\in G
.

If in addition
|ghg^{-1}|=|h|
for all
g,h\in G
—equivalently,
|gh|=|hg|
for all
g
and
h
—one says that
|-|
is
conjugation invariant
.

Any group norm on
G
induces a metric by setting
d(g,h)=|h^{-1}g|
: separatedness follows from condition
1
in the definition, and the triangle inequality from condition
2
, just as in the case of a vector space norm. It is well known and elementary that this metric is guaranteed always to be left-invariant, and will be right-invariant too if the norm is conjugation invariant. Conversely, any left-invariant metric
d
on
G
induces a norm on
G
by setting
|g|=d(g,e)
, and if
d
is also right-invariant then this norm will be conjugation invariant. Thus, we have:

Proposition 6.6

A
\mathbf{Met}
-group is exactly a group equipped with a conjugation-invariant norm. ∎

Conjugation-invariant norms and their associated metrics have been well studied since work of Klee in the 1950s. Klee proved that every abelian group which is Hausdorff, topologically complete and metrizable admits a bi-invariant metric
[
27
]
. The existence and properties of such metrics on interesting classes of groups remains an active topic of research due to its significance in fields such as symplectic topology and contact geometry
[
22
,
41
]
and in rigidity theory
[
38
]
.

In a sense the simplest sort of example is the ‘word norm’ determined by a subset
S\subset G
that normally generates
G
(meaning that the subgroup generated by conjugates of elements in
S
is
G
). The norm of
g\in G
with respect to
S
is the length of the shortest word expressing
g
in conjugates of elements of
S
. Such norms are always integer-valued, but this is not the case in general: examples of ‘fine’ conjugation-invariant norms—those which can take values arbitrarily close to zero—are discussed in
[
10
]
, Examples 1.19 and 1.21.

Remark 6.7

A bi-invariant metric on a group
G
is symmetric (thus, a classical metric) if and only if the function
G\to G
taking each element to its inverse is a short map. Even in this case, though,
G
cannot be interpreted as an internal group object in
\mathbf{Met}
(which is not a cartesian category). The issue is that the function
G\to G\times G
which
pairs
each element with its inverse is not a short map unless
G
is the trivial group.

7
Magnitude homology of enriched groups

In this section we analyse the iterated magnitude homology, in low degrees, of each of the three classes of examples described in
Section

6
: strict 2-groups, partially ordered groups, and any group with a conjugation-invariant norm.

In each case, a coefficient functor on the base category has already been chosen: on
\mathbf{Set}
, we take
\Sigma
to be the free abelian group functor; to obtain a coefficient functor on
\mathbf{Truth}
we restrict
\Sigma
along the inclusion
\mathbf{Truth}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{Set}
; and on
[0,\infty]
we take as coefficients the functor
\Sigma^{*}
described in
3.6
. Since the coefficients and the categorical dimension are understood, we will simplify the notation by omitting the superscript
2\Sigma
, writing
MH_{\bullet}(\mathbf{G})
for the iterated magnitude homology of a
\mathbf{Cat}
- or
\mathbf{PreOrd}
-group
\mathbf{G}
, and
MH_{\bullet}^{*}(\mathbf{G})
for the iterated magnitude homology of a
\mathbf{Met}
-group.

We begin by considering strict 2-groups, as in this case the equivalence with crossed modules allows for an especially efficient analysis. Baues and Ellis have defined the (co)homology of a crossed module
H\xrightarrow{\phi}G
to be the (co)homology of the space
\mathbb{B}(H\xrightarrow{\phi}G)
and have studied its properties
[
5
,
17
]
. From Ellis’s description on pages 5–6 of
[
17
]
it is clear that the space
\mathbb{B}(H\xrightarrow{\phi}G)
coincides with the classifying space of the corresponding strict 2-group. Hence, we have:

Theorem 7.1

The iterated magnitude homology of a strict 2-group
\mathbf{G}
is the Baues–Ellis homology of the corresponding crossed module. ∎

Because the homotopy theory of crossed modules is completely understood, the low-degree homology groups of a strict 2-group are easy to describe.

Example 7.2

Given a group
G
with a normal subgroup
N
, let
\mathbf{G}_{N}
denote the strict 2-group whose construction is described in
6.3
. By the discussion in that example, the space
\mathbb{B}(N\hookrightarrow G)
has fundamental group
G/N
. The Hurewicz theorem implies, then, that
MH_{1}(\mathbf{G}_{N})
is the abelianization
(G/N)_{\mathrm{ab}}
, and applying Proposition 5(iv) in Ellis
[
17

we see that
MH_{0}(\mathbf{G}_{N})=\mathbb{Z}
.

More generally, if
\mathbf{G}
is any strict 2-group then, by Ellis’s Proposition 5(iv) and the discussion in

MH_{0}(\mathbf{G})=\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }MH_{1}(\mathbf{G})=(G/E)_{\mathrm{ab}}

where

Not all interesting
\mathbf{Cat}

\mathbf{Cat}

Example 7.7

Given a partially ordered group
\mathbf{G}=(G,\leq)
, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}=(G,\sim)
denote the same group equipped with the equivalence relation generated by
\leq
. The connected components do not change when we pass from
\mathbf{G}
to
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
. In particular, the connected component of the identity in
\mathbf{G}
is

E=\{g\in G\mid g\sim e\}=P_{\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}},

the positive cone of
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
as defined in
6.4
. Thus,
7.6
says that

MH_{0}(\mathbf{G})=\mathbb{Z}=MH_{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}})\;\text{ and }\;MH_{1}(\mathbf{G})=\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}/P_{\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}}\right)_{\mathrm{ab}}=MH_{1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}),

so the first two homology groups do not distinguish
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
from
\mathbf{G}
.

For readers familiar with the homotopy theory of ordinary categories this may not come as a surprise, since, at least in the case of a
finite
preorder
Q
, the ordinary classifying space does not distinguish
Q
from the equivalence relation
\widetilde{Q}
that it generates. Indeed, in the finite case it is not hard to see that the inclusion
Q\hookrightarrow\widetilde{Q}
satisfies the conditions of Quillen’s Theorem A and so induces a homotopy equivalence on the classifying space
[
39
]
. Note, however, that the only translation-invariant partial order on a
finite
group is the trivial one (for an explanation, see
[
37
]
), so the interesting examples are necessarily infinite. In the case of an
infinite
poset it is less clear whether Quillen’s Theorem A applies.

7.7
suggests that iterated magnitude homology may be rather insensitive as an invariant of partially ordered groups, and preordered groups more generally. By contrast, in the case of a group with a conjugation-invariant norm we will find evidence that iterated magnitude homology does capture information not just about the structure of the group itself but about its geometry under the norm. We begin by describing the double magnitude nerve, which requires the following definition.

Definition 7.8

Let
\mathbf{G}=(G,|-|)
be a group with a conjugation-invariant norm, and let
d
be the associated metric on
G
. Given a matrix

\mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{00}&\cdots&g_{0n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
g_{m0}&\cdots&g_{mn}\end{bmatrix}

of elements of
\mathbf{G}
, the
total length
of
\mathbf{g}
is the sum of the lengths of its columns:

L(\mathbf{g})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}d(g_{ij},g_{i,j+1})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}|g_{ij}g_{i,j+1}^{-1}|.

In particular, if
m=0
, then
L(\mathbf{g})=0
. The total length of the empty matrix is
0
.

Since the magnitude nerve of a metric space is a real-graded simplicial abelian group, the double magnitude nerve
B^{*}_{\bullet\bullet}=MB^{MB^{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{G})
is a real-graded bisimplicial abelian group. Unravelling

in this case yields the following description.

Proposition 7.9

In grading

, for each

and

, the abelian group

is freely generated by

whose total length is

. Meanwhile,
B_{0q}^{\ell}=0
for all

, and

. The horizontal face maps
\delta_{j}^{h}
, which are defined when

, are specified on generators as follows:

•

The map
\delta_{0}^{h}
deletes the first column of a generating matrix

provided the length of this column is

, which holds if and only if all its entries are equal. Otherwise,
\delta_{0}^{h}(\mathbf{g})=0
. The map
\delta_{p}^{h}
acts in the same way with respect to the final column of

.

•

For

the map

is induced by multiplication at column

\delta_{i}^{h}\left(\begin{bmatrix}g_{00}&\cdots&g_{0,p-1}\\

.

When

inserts a column of identity elements after column

takes the element

) of identity elements.

When

•

The map
\delta_{0}^{v}
deletes the top row of a generating matrix

Recall from

Definition 7.10

Let
\mathbf{G}=(G,|-|)
be a normed group. An element

In terms of the associated metric on

7.11
says that if

counts (up to conjugacy) its indecomposable elements of every length. The proof, which is similar in strategy to that of

Theorem 7.11

Let
\mathbf{G}=(G,|-|)
be a group with a conjugation-invariant norm. In length grading

MH_{\bullet}^{0}(\mathbf{G})\cong H_{\bullet}(G).

In length gradings

MH_{2}^{\ell}(\mathbf{G})\cong\mathbb{Z}\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\text{conjugacy classes of indecomposable}\\
\text{elements of norm $\ell$ in $G$}\end{array}\right\}.

8
Magnitude homology of strict
n
-categories

Since the magnitude nerve functor with respect to a strong symmetric monoidal size functor is always strong symmetric monoidal, this is in particular true when we take the size functor to be
MB^{\Sigma}
itself. That is, the double magnitude nerve

MB^{MB^{\Sigma}}:\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}\to[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\times\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]

is strong symmetric monoidal. As the functor
\mathrm{diag}:[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\times\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]\to[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]
is strong symmetric monoidal too, so is their composite
MB^{2\Sigma}:\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}\to[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]
. This suggests going a step further, to define a magnitude nerve for categories enriched in the category of
\mathcal{V}\mathbf{Cat}
-categories:

MB^{3\Sigma}(-)=\mathrm{diag}\>MB^{MB^{2}}(-).

We will pursue this idea only in the case that
\mathcal{V}=\mathbf{Set}
, where repeated iteration allows us to define a sequence of homology theories: a magnitude homology theory on the category of strict
n
-categories, for every
n\geq 1
.

Conventions

Throughout this section, ‘
n
-category’ will mean ‘strict
n
-category’. Since we consider only small enriched categories in this paper, an
n
-category will be assumed to have a set of objects. We refer to the objects of an
n
-category as
0-cells
, and its
k
-morphisms as
k
-cells
. The category of
n
-categories is denoted by
n\mathbf{Cat}
.

Definition 8.1

Let
\Sigma:\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Ab}
be the free abelian group functor. The
magnitude nerve
functor on the category of
n
-categories is defined as follows.

•

Set
MB^{1}=MB^{\Sigma}:\mathbf{Cat}\to[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbf{Ab}]
.

•

For each
n>1
, define

MB^{n}=\mathrm{diag}\left(MB^{MB^{n-1}}(-)\right):n\mathbf{Cat}\to[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbf{Ab}].

The
magnitude chain complex
of an
n
-category
\mathbf{X}
is

MC^{n}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{X})=C(MB^{n}(\mathbf{X})),

and the
magnitude homology
of
\mathbf{X}
is

MH^{n}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{X})=H_{\bullet}(MC^{n}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{X})).

We will illustrate the magnitude homology of
n
-categories by drawing out a certain pleasing feature—namely, its behaviour with respect to an operation analogous to the two-point suspension of topological spaces.

Definition 8.2

Given an
n
-category
\mathbf{X}
, the

of
\mathbf{X}
is an
(n+1)
-category
\Gamma\mathbf{X}
with two objects,

n
-category
\Gamma\mathbf{X}(A,B)
is equivalent to
\mathbf{X}
, while
\Gamma\mathbf{X}(B,A)
is empty and the other two hom objects are given by the terminal

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot}
\mathbf{X}

Below, we will see that magnitude homology behaves with respect to this form of suspension very much as unreduced singular homology behaves with respect to the two-point suspension of topological spaces.

The topological story goes as follows. Given a nonempty space
Y
, the
suspension
of
Y
, denoted
SY
, is the quotient of
Y\times[0,1]
obtained by collapsing
Y\times\{0\}
to one point and collapsing
Y\times\{1\}
to another point. After decomposing
SY

H_{k}(SY)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&k=0\\
H_{k-1}(Y)&k\geq 2\end{cases}

while
H_{1}(SY)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\cong H_{0}(Y)
.

Our theorem is formally very similar to the topological one:

Theorem 8.3

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a nonempty strict
n
-category. Then

MH_{k}^{n+1}(\Gamma\mathbf{X})\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&k=0\\
MH_{k-1}^{n}(\mathbf{X})&k\geq 2\end{cases}

while

MH_{1}^{n+1}(\Gamma\mathbf{X})\oplus\mathbb{Z}\cong MH_{0}^{n}(\mathbf{X}).

Unlike the classical statement, which expresses a property of a single homology theory, ours expresses a relationship between members of a family of homology theories (namely the family
(MH_{\bullet}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
). Accordingly, the proof of
8.3
, which occupies the remainder of this section, is very different from the standard proof of the topological result. Rather than decomposing the
(n+1)
-category
\Gamma\mathbf{X}
into smaller pieces, we compute its homology directly, as the total homology of the double complex
C(MB^{MB^{n}}(\Gamma\mathbf{X}))
.

We begin by describing the
0^{\textrm{th}}
magnitude homology group of any
n
-category—not necessarily one obtained by suspension. The
0^{\textrm{th}}
homology group captures the same information irrespective of the categorical dimension.

Definition 8.4

The set of

in an
n
-category
\mathbf{X}
is the set of equivalence classes in
\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})
under the equivalence relation generated by the preorder ‘

\mathrm{Con}(\mathbf{X})
.

Proposition 8.5

Let
\mathbf{X}
be an
n
-category. Then
MH_{0}^{n}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{Z}\cdot\mathrm{Con}(\mathbf{X})
.

The proof comes down to a lemma describing the first two groups in the iterated magnitude nerve and the face maps between them;

follows on taking homology of the associated chain complex. To state the lemma, we note that for

-category
\mathbf{X}
is a

of

For any

\mathbf{X}

MB_{0\bullet}^{MB^{n-1}}(\mathbf{X})=\bigoplus_{x\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})}I_{\mathrm{pw}},

where

is unit for the pointwise tensor product in

in every degree, and all its face and degeneracy maps are identities. Thus, for each

MB_{0k}^{MB^{n-1}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{Z}\cdot\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{Z}\cdot\{0\text{-cells in }\mathbf{X}\}

Meanwhile

MB_{1\bullet}^{MB^{n-1}}(\mathbf{X})=\bigoplus_{x,y\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})}MB_{\bullet}^{n-1}(\mathbf{X}(x,y))

and so

\displaystyle MB_{10}^{MB^{n-1}}(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{x,y\in\mathrm{ob}(\mathbf{X})}\mathbb{Z}\cdot\{0\text{-cells in }\mathbf{X}(x,y)\}

(5)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}\cdot\{1\text{-cells in }\mathbf{X}\}

where (

\displaystyle MB_{11}^{MB^{n-1}}(\mathbf{X})

Proof of

In the proof of

, it will simplify matters to replace a double complex by one in which the rows have been normalized. The next lemma explains how this can be done.

Any bisimplicial object
B
in an abelian category
\mathbb{A}
can be regarded (in either of two ways) as a simplicial object of
[\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}]
, so we can take its normalized chain complex to obtain an object
NB
of
\mathrm{Ch}([\Delta^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{A}])
. Now applying the unnormalized chain complex functor in every degree yields an object of
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathrm{Ch}(\mathbb{A}))
. Equivalently, this is the double complex obtained from
B
by taking normalized complexes in every row and unnormalized complexes in every column (or vice versa). We will denote this double complex by

.

Lemma 8.7

Let
B
be a bisimplicial object in an abelian category. Then

H_{\bullet}\mathrm{Tot}\>CB\cong H_{\bullet}\mathrm{Tot}\>C^{v}N^{h}B.

Proof.

Taking quotients by the horizontal degeneracies in
B
defines a map of double complexes
CB\to C^{v}N^{h}B
. As the quotient map in each row is a quasi-isomorphism, the induced morphism of row-filtration spectral sequences is an isomorphism on page
E^{1}=H^{h}CB
, and thus induces an isomorphism on total homology. (See, e.g., Brown
[
8
]
, VII, Proposition 2.6.)
∎

It remains to prove

Lemma A.1

Let

be a first quadrant double complex with the property that every column is exact in vertical degrees greater than 0. Then

H_{k}\mathrm{Tot}(C)=H^{h}_{k}C_{\bullet 0}\text{ for all }k\geq 0.

Lemma A.2

Let

for all

H_{k}\mathrm{Tot}(C)\cong\begin{cases}0&k=0,1\\
H_{1}^{h}H_{1}^{v}C&k=2.\end{cases}

The third lemma is an elementary fact about bi-invariant metrics on groups.

Lemma A.3

Let
G
be a group equipped with a bi-invariant metric
d
. Elements
g,h\in G
are non-adjacent if and only if they can be factored as
g=g_{0}g_{1}
and
h=h_{0}h_{1}
in such a way that
g_{0}\neq h_{0}
,
g_{1}\neq h_{1}
, and

d(g,h)=d(g_{0},h_{0})+d(g_{1},h_{1}).

Proof.

First, suppose
g
and
h
are non-adjacent: there exists some
k\neq g,h
such that
d(g,h)=d(g,k)+d(k,h)
. We can factor
g
as
(gk^{-1})k
and
h
as
eh
; since
k\neq g
, we know
gk^{-1}\neq e
. Multiplying
gk^{-1}
and
e
on the right by
k
and using the right-invariance of the metric, we find that

d(gk^{-1},e)+d(k,h)=d(g,k)+d(k,h)=d(g,h).

Conversely, suppose we have a factorization
g=g_{1}g_{0}
and
h=h_{1}h_{0}
with the relevant property. I claim that
h_{1}g_{0}
lies strictly between
g
and
h
. Indeed,

\displaystyle d(g,h_{1}g_{0})+d(h_{1}g_{0},h)

\displaystyle=d(g_{1}g_{0},h_{1}g_{0})+d(h_{1}g_{0},h_{1}h_{0})

\displaystyle=d(g_{1},h_{1})+d(g_{0},h_{0})

\displaystyle=d(g,h)

where the second equality uses the bi-invariance of the metric. This says that
h_{1}g_{0}
lies
between

g
and
h
. By assumption,
g_{0}\neq h_{0}
and thus
h_{1}g_{0}\neq h_{1}h_{0}=h
. Similarly,
h_{1}g_{0}\neq g
, and hence
h_{1}g_{0}
lies
strictly
between
g
and
h
.
∎

With these, we can prove the theorem.
