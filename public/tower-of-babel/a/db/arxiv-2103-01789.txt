Cone and paraboloid points of arbitrary subsets of Euclidean space
Matthew Hyde and Michele Villa

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper we characterise cone points of arbitrary subsets of Euclidean space. Given
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x\in E
is a cone point of
E
if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(B(x,r))^{2}\frac{dr}{r}<\infty,

up to a set of zero
d
-measure. The coefficients
\beta_{E}^{d,2}
are a variation of the Jones coefficients. This is a high dimensional counterpart of a theorem of Bishop and Jones from 1994. We also prove similar results for
\alpha
-paraboloid points, which are the
C^{1,\alpha}
rectifiability counterparts to cone points:
x\in E
is an
\alpha
-paraboloid point if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,2}(B(x,r))^{2}}{r^{2\alpha}}\,\frac{dr}{r}<\infty

up to a set of zero
d
-measure. Here,
\overline{\beta}^{d,2}_{E}
is another variant of the Jones coefficients, introduced by Azzam and Schul.

1.
Introduction

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
d<n
an integer. One says that
E
is
d
-rectifiable if there are
d
-dimensional Lipschitz graphs
\Gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2,...
, so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}\left(E\setminus\bigcup_{i}\Gamma_{i}\right)=0.

On the other hand, a set is said to be purely
d
-unrectifiable if
\mathcal{H}^{d}(E\cap F)=0
for any
d
-rectifiable set
F
. Rectifiability is a central notion in geometric measure theory, and characterising rectifiable sets is one of its main objectives.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
d
-dimensional affine plane. For a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the truncated cone with radius
r>0
and aperture
\lambda>0
by

\displaystyle X(x,V,\lambda,r):=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,|\Pi_{V^{\perp}}(x-y)|<\lambda|\Pi_{V}(x-y)|\right\}\cap B(x,r),

where
B(x,r)=\{|x-y|<r\}
. Also let
X^{c}(x,V,\lambda,r):=B(x,r)\setminus X(x,V,\lambda,r)
. One of the best known characterisations of rectifiable sets is the following (see
[
Mat95
]
Theorem 15.19).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be so that
0<\mathcal{H}^{d}(E)<\infty
. Then
E
is
d
-rectifiable if and only if there exists a unique
d
-dimensional affine plane
V
so that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}(X^{c}(x,V,\lambda,r)\cap E)}{r^{d}}=0\mbox{ for }\mathcal{H}^{d}-a.e.\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A
d
-plane
V
satisfying (
1.1
) is called an approximate tangent plane, while an
x
for which such a
V
exists, is an approximate tangent point. Other classical examples are characterisations in terms of densities, tangent measures, or orthogonal projections. For proofs of these influential results, see the monograph
[
Mat95
]
.
More recently, there has been a need to quantify the notion of rectifiability, due to its connection with singular integrals and analytic capacity. This, starting with the pioneering works of Jones
[
Jon90
]
and David and Semmes
[
DS91
]
, has lead to the study of connections between rectifiability properties of sets and measures and boundedness of geometric square functions (see e.g.
[
BJ94
]
,
[
Dav98
]
,
[
Lég99
]
,
[
NTV14
]
,
[
AT15
]
,
[
JTV19
]
).

Given a set
E
, we say that
x\in E
is a
d
-cone point if there is a
d
-plane
V
so that for all
\lambda>0
we can find an
r>0
such that
E\cap X^{c}(x,V,\lambda,r)=\emptyset
. It is easy to see that the subset of
E
of cone points is
d
-rectifiable (
[
Mat95
]
, Lemma 15.13).

A prototypical and influential result connecting geometric square functions to rectifiability is the following theorem by Bishop and Jones from 1994.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
BJ94
]
, Theorem 2)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}
be a Jordan curve. Then, up to set of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
measure zero,
x\in\Gamma
is a cone point if and only if

(1.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{\Gamma}^{\infty}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}<+\infty.

Here
\beta_{\Gamma}^{\infty}(x,r):=\inf_{L}\sup_{y\in B(x,r)\cap\Gamma}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,L)}{r}
, where the infimum is taken over all lines in the plane. These are the so-called Jones
\beta
-numbers; they, and their variants, have been widely employed in geometric measure theory and analysis over the past thirty years.
Our main result is a full
d
-dimensional analogue of Theorem
1.2
. In fact, even with
d=1
and
n=2
, our theorem is new, since it applies to any set, and not only Jordan curves.

Theorem A
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1\leq d<n
. Then, up to a set of
\mathcal{H}^{d}
measure zero,
x\in E
is a
d
-cone point if and only if

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}\frac{dr}{r}<+\infty.

Here,
p\in[1,p(d)],
where

(1.4)

\displaystyle p(d):=\begin{cases}\infty&\mbox{ if }d=1\mbox{ or }d=2.\\
\frac{2d}{d-2}&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 1.3

(Finiteness assumption)
.

Classical characterisations of rectifiability such as Theorem
1.1
(or Besicovitch projection theorem, or Preiss’ results on tangent measures) usually assume
\mathcal{H}^{d}(E)<+\infty
(at least locally); this assumption is needed for the limit (
1.1
) to even make sense.
This is in contrast to Theorems
1.2
and
A
, which instead quantify how
E
should behave near a
d
-cone point
x
, regardless of the dimension and measure of
E
.

Remark 1.4

(
\beta
’s coefficients)
.

The coefficients appearing in (
1.3
) are an ‘
L^{p}
’ variant of the coefficients
\beta_{E}^{\infty}
, introduced in
[

]
. They are defined as

(1.5)

\displaystyle\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r):=\inf_{V}\left(\frac{1}{r^{d}}\int_{B(x,r)\cap E}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,V)}{r}\right)^{p}\,d{\mathcal{M}}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Here the infimum is over all
d
-dimensional affine planes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The integration is with respect to
{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}^{d}
, a modified Hausdorff content (see Section
2
).
It is well known that the coefficients
\beta_{E}^{\infty}
are not suitable when
d>2

Fang
[

constructed a Lipschitz graph where (

) is infinite on a positive measure set.
, thus to prove a theorem like Theorem

Remark 1.5

(Comparisons to literature)
.

There are several results that classify rectifiability in terms of geometric square functions. Most of them, however, are closer to Theorem
1.1
than to Theorem
A
, since they assume the existence of a (locally) finite measure.
A particularly similar-looking result which falls in this class is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
AT15
,
Tol15
,
ENV16
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a finite Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and suppose that
0<\theta^{*}(\mu,x)
and
\theta_{*}(\mu,x)<\infty
for
\mu
-almost all

. Then
\mu
is
d
-rectifiable if and only if

(1.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{\mu,2}^{d}(x,r)^{2}\,\frac{dr}{r}<\infty\mbox{ for }\mu-a.e.x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Here

(1.7)

\displaystyle\beta_{\mu,p}^{d}(x,r):=\inf_{V}\left(\frac{1}{r^{d}}\int_{B(x,r)}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,V)}{r}\right)^{p}\,d\mu(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
\mu
is a Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This
L^{p}
variant of Jones’
\beta^{E}_{\infty}
was introduced by David and Semmes
[
DS91
]
in their work on uniform rectifiability and singular integrals. It was first proven in
[
AT15
,
Tol15
]
with the stronger assumption
0<\theta^{*}(\mu,x)<\infty

\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and then improved to its current form in
[
ENV16
,
Tol19
]
.
There are several other pointwise results that classify rectifiability of Radon measures via geometric square functions. For example, see
[
Dab20
]
for a nice quantification of Theorem
1.1
in terms of ‘conical energy’. A closely related result is that of
[
BN20
]
, where the authors study

in terms of conical defect. See also
[
Ler03
,
BS15
,
MO18
]
.
For a more comprehensive list, we refer to the nice survey
[

]
.

Perhaps the closest result to date to Theorem

is a theorem by the second author in
[

]
, where, however, it was assumed

that

is lower content

-regular

Remark 1.7

(Approximate tangents and cone points)
.

A further difference between theorems
à la
Federer (e.g. Theorem
1.2
, Theorem
1.6
) and theorems
à la
Bishop-Jones (e.g. Theorem
1.2
, Theorem
A
) is that the latter type characterise
cone points
, rather than
approximate tangents
. If we have some kind of lower regularity (be it Ahlfors-David or content), the two notions coincide (whenever the notion of approximate tangent makes sense). However, in the absence of lower regularity, cone point implies approximate tangent, but not vice versa.

Remark 1.8

(Range of
p
)
.

The
\beta
numbers (
2.12
) (as (
2.8
) below) have been introduced to study quantitative rectifiability beyond the Ahlfors regular setting (see
[

,

,

is expected. Note however that Theorem

holds only for

We now introduce our second main result. One says that
E
is
(d,k,\alpha)
-rectifiable if there are
d
-dimensional

graphs
\Gamma_{i}
,

so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}\left(E\setminus\bigcup_{i}\Gamma_{i}\right)=0.

Higher order rectifiability was, to the best of our knowledge, first considered in
[

]
.
Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
d
-dimensional affine plane. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the truncated paraboloid with smoothness parameter
\alpha>0
, radius
r>0
and aperture
\lambda>0
by

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}(x,V,\lambda,r):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,|\Pi_{V^{\perp}}(x-y)|<\lambda|\Pi_{V}(x-y)|^{1+\alpha}\}\cap B(x,r).

Define
X^{c}_{\alpha}(x,V,\lambda,r):=B(x,r)\setminus X_{\alpha}(x,V,\lambda,r)
. For a set
E
, we say that

is a
(d,\alpha)
-paraboloid point (or just paraboloid point) if there is a
d
-plane
V
so that for all
\lambda>0
, we can find an

so that

. Define

(1.8)

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,p}(x,r):=\inf_{V}\left(\frac{1}{r^{d}}\int_{B(x,r)\cap E}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,V)}{r}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where the infimum is over all affine
d
-planes.
Our second main result is the following.

Theorem B
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq d<n
,
1\leq p\leq p(d)
and

. Then, up to a set of
\mathcal{H}^{d}

(1)

(1.9)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}\frac{dr}{r^{1+2\alpha}}<\infty,

then

is an
\alpha
-paraboloid point.

(2)

On the other hand,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}\frac{dr}{r^{1+2\alpha}}<\infty,

whenever

is an
\alpha
-paraboloid point

Theorem

and Theorem

(see below), imply immediately the following corollary.

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If (

) holds for

-almost all

is
(d,1,\alpha)
-rectifiable.

There is another corollary of Theorem

if

.

Let

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}\frac{dr}{r^{1+2\alpha}}<\infty.

Remark 1.11

(
\beta
coefficients)
.

Note that the integration in (

) is with respect to Hausdorff content (rather than

as in Theorem

). The numbers

were introduced by Azzam and Schul

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(B(x,r)\cap E)\geq cr^{d}\mbox{ for }x\in E\mbox{ and }r>0.

We could not prove Theorem

1.1.
Sketch of the proof

Theorem
B
(1) and hence the “if” direction of Theorem
A
is proved in Section
3
. We split the Christ-David cubes intersecting the points satisfying (
1.9
) (or (
1.3
)) into stopping-time regions for which we have good control on a Bishop-Jones type square function and each cube
Q\in S
has sufficiently many separated points from
E
. Because we can control angles between best approximating planes when there are sufficiently many separates points (Lemma
3.8
), we can apply the parameterisation theorems of Ghinassi (Theorem
2.5
) to produce a
C^{1,\alpha}
-surface
\Sigma_{S}
which well approximates
E
inside
S.
For
\mathcal{H}^{d}
-almost every
x
satisfying (
1.9
) (or (
1.3
)), we can find a stopping-time region
S
so that
x\in\Sigma_{S}
and so that
x
is a paraboloid point for
\Sigma_{S}.
The final part of the proof is to show that these are actually paraboloid points for
E
.

That being a cone point (resp. a paraboloid point) implies (
1.3
) (resp. (
1.10
)) is proved in Section
4
. We consider subsets of cone/paraboloid points that can be covered by Lipschitz or
C^{1,\alpha}
graphs. Then, in the case of Theorem
A
we construct a
d
-LCR set
E_{0}
containing
E
and which have the same cone points. This ultimately permit us to compute the
\beta
coefficients. This strategy is somewhat inspired by Bishop and Jones original one, even if the final technique is very different. The methods for Theorem
B
are similar, with some technical differences.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

We gather here some notation and some results which will be used later on.
We write
a\lesssim b
if there exists a constant
C
such that
a\leq Cb
. By
a\sim b
we mean
a\lesssim b\lesssim a
.
In general, we will use
n\in\mathbb{N}
to denote the dimension of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while we will use
d\in\mathbb{N}
, with
d\leq n-1
, to denote the dimension of a subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For two subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(A,B):=\inf_{a\in A,b\in B}|a-b|.

For a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,A):=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(\{x\},A)=\inf_{a\in A}|x-a|.

We write

B(x,r):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,|x-y|<r\},

and, for
\lambda>0
,

\lambda B(x,r):=B(x,\lambda r).

At times, we may write
\mathbb{B}
to denote
B(0,1)
. When necessary we write
B_{n}(x,r)
to distinguish a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
from one in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we may denote by
B_{d}(x,r)
.

2.2.
Christ-David and Whitney cubes.

In this section we briefly recall two ‘cubes’-tools which will be needed later on.

2.2.1.
Christ-David cubes

David in
[
Dav91
]
and then Christ in
[
Chr90
]
, introduced a construction which allows for a partition (up to a set of measure zero) of any space of homogeneous type into open subsets which resemble the behaviour of dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The formulation below is due to Hytönen and Martikainen
[
HM12
]
and forms an exact partition of any doubling metric space
X.

Theorem 2.1

(Christ-David cubes)
.

Let
X
be a doubling metric space and
X_{k}
be a sequence of maximal
\rho^{k}
-separated nets, where
\rho=1/1000
and let
c_{0}=1/500.
Then, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, there is a collection
\mathscr{D}_{k}
of cubes such that the following hold.

(1)

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z},\ X=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{D}_{k}}Q.

(2)

If
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathscr{D}=\bigcup_{k}\mathscr{D}_{k}
and
Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}\not=\emptyset,
then
Q_{1}\subseteq Q_{2}
or
Q_{2}\subseteq Q_{1}.

(3)

For
Q\in\mathscr{D},
let
k(Q)
be the unique integer so that
Q\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
and set
\ell(Q)=5\rho^{k}.
Then there is
x_{Q}\in X_{k}
such that

\displaystyle B(x_{Q},c_{0}\ell(Q))\subseteq Q\subseteq B(x_{Q},\ell(Q)).

The
k^{th}
-generation children of
Q\in\mathcal{D}
, denoted by
\text{Child}_{k}(Q),
are the cubes
R\subseteq Q
so that
\ell(R)=\rho^{k}\ell(Q).
We also need the notion of a stopping-time region.

Definition 2.2
.

A collection of cubes
S\subseteq\mathcal{D}
is called a
stopping-time region
if the following hold.

(1)

There is a cube
Q(S)\in S
such that
Q(S)
contains all cubes in
S
.

(2)

If
Q\in S
and
Q\subseteq R\subseteq Q(S),
then
R\in S
.

(3)

If
Q\in S
, then all siblings of
Q
are also in
S
.

2.2.2.
Whitney cubes

We follow
[
SM93
]
, Lemma 2 of Chapter 1. Let
F
be a closed non-empty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the standard euclidean distance. Then there exists a collection
\{S_{k}\}
of closed cubes with disjoint interiors which covers
F^{c}
(the complement of
F
) and whose side lengths are comparable to their distance to

\displaystyle A^{-1}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(S_{k},F)\leq\ell(S_{k})\leq A\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(S_{k},F).

2.3.
Parameterisation Theorems

Two main tools that we shall need are the parameterisation results of David and Toro
[
DT12
]
and Ghinassi
[
Ghi20
]
. To state these results, we need to introduce some more definitions.

Definition 2.3
.

Set the normalised local Hausdorff distance between two sets
E,F
to be given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle d_{x,r}(E,F):=\frac{1}{r}\max\left\{\sup_{y\in E\cap B(x,r)}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,F)\,;\,\sup_{y\in F\cap B(x,r)}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E)\right\}.

Moreover, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
r>0
, we set

\displaystyle\vartheta^{d}_{E}(B(x,r)):=\inf\{d_{B(x,r)}(E,L)\,|\,L\mbox{ is a }d\mbox{-dimensional plane in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

Definition 2.4
.

A subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
(d,\epsilon)
-Reifenberg flat (or simply
\epsilon
-Reifenberg flat if the dimension is understood) if

\displaystyle\vartheta_{E}^{d}(B(x,r))<\epsilon\mbox{ for all }x\in E\mbox{ and }r>0.

We will implement the parameterisation results
[
DT12
,
Ghi20
]
via the following reformulation in terms of Christ-David cubes. This formulation is proven in the Appendix of
[
Azz19
]
for the bi-Lipschitz case and the proof is the same (modulo replacing the improved estimates in
[

]
) for the

Theorem 2.5

(
[
DT12
]
,
[
Ghi20
]
)
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}>1.
Let

and let

be the Christ cubes for

For

, declare

if

and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(Q,R)\leq C_{2}\min\{\ell(Q),\ell(R)\}.
Then, for

large enough and
\epsilon\ll C_{1}^{-1},C_{2}^{-1},
the following holds. Let

be a stopping-time region with top cube

so that

and for all

d
-plane

such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{Q},L_{Q})<\epsilon\ell(Q).

Moreover, if

(2.3)

\displaystyle\epsilon(Q)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in S\\
R\sim Q\end{subarray}}d_{C_{1}B_{Q}}(L_{Q},L_{R})

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\subseteq R\subseteq Q(S)}\epsilon(R)^{2}<\epsilon^{2}.

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in F\cap 2C_{1}B_{Q}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(z,L_{Q})\leq\epsilon\ell(Q)\ \text{for all}\ Q\in S

3.
Fast decay of
\beta

In this section we will show the first part of Theorem

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\diam(E)}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}}{r^{2\alpha}}\,\frac{dr}{r}<+\infty,

Lemma 3.1

and suppose
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q)
is not a singleton. For
\delta>0
small enough (depending on
\kappa
), there exists a
(1+C\epsilon)
-bi-Lipschitz map

(invertible on its image) so that
\Sigma_{Q}=g_{Q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
C\epsilon
-Reifenberg flat. If

then

is
C^{1,\alpha}.
Furthermore,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in E\cap C_{1}B_{R}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Sigma_{R})\lesssim\delta^{\frac{1}{d+1}}\ell(R)\quad\text{for all}\ R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q).

Proof.

The proof consists of checking the hypotheses of Theorem
2.5
. Let
C_{1},C_{2}>1
be chosen large enough so that Theorem
2.5
holds and let
M
be as in Remark
3.9
. For each
T\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q)
choose
L_{T}
to be a
d
-plane through
x_{T}
such that

\beta^{d,1}_{E}(M^{2}B_{T},L_{T})\leq 2\beta^{d,1}_{E}(M^{2}B_{T}).

In this case, (
2.2
) is trivial. Let
T\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q).
We begin by estimating
\epsilon(T).
Let
T^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q)
such that
T^{\prime}\sim T.
By Remark
3.9
and our choice of

we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle C_{1}B_{T}\subseteq 2C_{1}B_{T}\subseteq\tfrac{M}{2}B_{T^{\prime}}\subseteq MB_{T^{\prime}}\subseteq M^{2}B_{T}.

Since
T
has
(d+1,\kappa)
-separated points, we can apply Lemma
3.8
and Lemma
2.13
to get

(3.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{C_{1}B_{T}}(L_{T},L_{T^{\prime}})&\lesssim_{\kappa,C_{1},C_{2}}\beta_{E}^{d,1}(MB_{T^{\prime}},L_{T^{\prime}})+\beta_{E}^{d,1}(2C_{1}B_{T},L_{T})\\
&\lesssim\beta_{E}^{d,1}(MB_{T^{\prime}},L_{T^{\prime}})+\beta_{E}^{d,1}(MB_{T},L_{T})\\
&\lesssim\beta_{E}^{d,1}(M^{2}B_{T}).\end{split}

Taking the max over all

gives

(3.12)

\displaystyle\epsilon(T)\lesssim\beta^{d,1}_{E}(M^{2}B_{T}).

So then, for any

we have

\sum_{R\subseteq T\subseteq Q}\frac{\epsilon(T)}{\ell(T)^{2\alpha}}\lesssim\sum_{R^{\prime}\subseteq T\subseteq Q}\frac{\beta^{d,1}_{E}(M^{2}B_{T})}{\ell(T)^{2\alpha}}<\delta^{2}.

Taking
\delta>0
small enough with respect to

we can apply Theorem
2.5
to produce the map

and surface
\Sigma_{Q}.

Let us prove (

). By Lemma

, we know

for each

This implies

\sup_{y\in E\cap 2C_{1}B_{R}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,L_{R})\lesssim\delta^{\frac{1}{d+1}}\ell(R)

and (

) holds by Theorem

.
∎

We also have the following packing condition for cubes in
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}.

Lemma 3.11
.

\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}}\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim\ell(Q_{0})^{d}.

Proof.

Let
Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}.
We begin by showing that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Stop}(Q)_{\mathscr{B}}}\ell(R)^{d}\lesssim\ell(Q)^{d}.

Indeed, if
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q)=\{Q\}
then
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Stop}(Q)_{\mathscr{B}}
is just the children of
Q
in
\mathscr{B}
(or
Q
itself, in the case that
Q\in\mathscr{B}
). Since
Q
has a bounded number of the children, the statement is obvious in this case. Assume then,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q)
is not a singleton and let
\Sigma_{Q}
be the surface from Lemma
3.10
. By (
2.7
) we have

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{R},\Sigma_{Q})\lesssim\delta^{\frac{1}{d+1}}\ell(R)

for each
R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Stop}(Q)_{\mathscr{B}}.
Hence, for
\delta>0
small enough
c_{0}B_{R}
carves out a large portion of
\Sigma_{Q}
which implies
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma_{Q}\cap c_{0}B_{R})\gtrsim\ell(R)^{d}.
Since the
c_{0}B_{R}
are disjoint, this gives (
3.13
) since

\sum_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Stop}(Q)_{\mathscr{B}}}\ell(R)^{d}\lesssim\sum_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Stop}(Q)_{\mathscr{B}}}\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma_{Q}\cap c_{0}B_{R})\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Sigma_{Q}\cap B_{Q})\lesssim\ell(Q)^{d}.

With the help of (
3.13
), we will show

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Next}(Q)}\ell(R)^{d}\leq\frac{1}{2}\ell(Q)^{d}.

First, by definition,

(3.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Next}(Q)}\ell(R)^{d}=\sum_{T\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Stop}(Q)_{\mathscr{B}}}\sum_{R\in\text{Child}_{k^{*}}(T)}\ell(R)^{d}.

If
T\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Stop}(Q)_{\mathscr{B}}
then
T
does not have
(d+1,\kappa)
-separated points (in the case that
T\in\mathscr{B})
or there exists a child
T^{\prime}
of
T
so that
T^{\prime}
does not have
(d+1,\kappa)
-separated points. This mean that there exists a
(d-1)
-plane
L
such that

\beta^{d-1}_{E,\infty}(C_{1}B_{T^{\prime}},L)\leq\kappa.

For
C_{1}
large enough
T\subseteq C_{1}B_{T^{\prime}}
, which implies

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,L)\leq C_{1}\kappa\ell(T^{\prime})=C_{1}\kappa\rho\ell(T)

for all
y\in T.
Combing this with our choice for
k^{*}
(see (
3.8
)), we observe that if
R\in\text{Child}_{k^{*}}(T)
then

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x_{R},L)\leq C_{1}\kappa\rho\ell(T)=C_{1}\kappa\rho\rho^{-k^{*}}\ell(R)\leq\frac{c_{0}}{2}\ell(R).

In particular,
c_{0}B_{R}
carves out a large portion of
L
which implies

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(L\cap c_{0}B_{R})\gtrsim\ell(R)^{d-1}.

Since the
c_{0}B_{R}
are disjoint and contained in
B_{T}
,

\sum_{R\in\text{Child}_{k^{*}}(T)}\ell(R)^{d-1}\lesssim\sum_{R\in\text{Child}_{k^{*}}(T)}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(L\cap c_{0}B_{R})\leq\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(L\cap B_{T})\leq C\ell(T)^{d-1}

which after multiplying both sides by
\rho^{k^{*}}\ell(T)
gives

\sum_{R\in\text{Child}_{k^{*}}(T)}\ell(R)^{d}\leq C\rho^{k^{*}}\ell(T)^{d}.

By taking
\kappa
small enough we can guarantee that
C\rho^{k^{*}}<\tfrac{1}{2}
. By plugging this into (
3.15
) and using (
3.13
), we get

\sum_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Next}(Q)}\ell(R)^{d}\leq C\rho^{k^{*}}\ell(Q)^{d}\leq\frac{1}{2}\ell(Q)^{d}.

Finally, for each
k\geq 1
we have

\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}_{k+1}}\ell(Q)^{d}=\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}_{k}}\sum_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Next}(Q)}\ell(R)^{d}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}}\ell(Q)^{d}

and so, by induction,

\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}_{k}}\ell(Q)^{d}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}\ell(Q_{0})^{d}.

This proves the lemma since

\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}}\ell(Q)^{d}=\ell(Q_{0})^{d}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}_{k}}\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim\ell(Q_{0})^{d}.

∎

3.3.
Tangent points

We partition

as follows. For

, let

be the set of points in

that are contained in exactly

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}
, that is,

Clearly

\displaystyle E_{0}=E_{0}^{\infty}\cup\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{0}^{N}.

Lemma 3.12
.

We have
\mathcal{H}^{d}(E_{0}^{\infty})=0.

Proof.

Let us define a sequence of covers for
E_{0}^{\infty}.
Let
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}
be those cubes in
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}
which contain an infinite number of other cubes from
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}.
Let
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{0}
be a maximal collection of cubes in
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}.
By maximality,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{0}
forms a cover for
E_{0}^{\infty}.
Then, supposing
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{k}
has been defined for some
k\geq 0,
let
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{k+1}
be a maximal collection of cubes from
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}
contained in those cubes from
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{k}.
Again, by maximality,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{k}
forms a cover for
E_{0}^{\infty}
for each

Using the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{i}\cap\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{j}=\emptyset
for

along with Lemma
3.11
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}_{k}^{\infty}}\ell(Q)^{d}\leq\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}}\ell(Q)^{d}<+\infty

which implies

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{k}}\ell(Q)^{d}=0.

Since each
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{k}
forms a cover for

and
\ell(Q)\leq\rho^{k}\ell(Q_{0})
for all

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}(E_{0}^{\infty})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\rho^{k}\ell(Q_{0})}(E_{0}^{\infty})\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Top}^{\infty}_{k}}\ell(Q)^{d}=0

as required.
∎

.

N\geq 1
and

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tree}(Q)

We will show

for all siblings

of

.

\geq N+1

At

Let

. Let

and its image under

has full measure in

So, if we can show

is a tangent point for

, we are done. This is the goal for the remainder of the proof.

Let
\tau>0.
We know
x_{0}
is a tangent point for
\Sigma
so there exists a
d
-plane
L_{0}
and a scale

so that if

then

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,L_{0})\leq\tau r^{1+\alpha},\quad\text{for all}\ y\in\Sigma\cap B(x_{0},r).

By the Lebesgue density theorem, there exists

)

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed set so that
E\subset B(0,1)
. Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Except for a set of zero
\mathcal{H}^{d}
measure, if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}}{r^{2\alpha}}\frac{dr}{r}<+\infty,

then
x
is a tangent point of
E
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that for any
x\in E
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r_{k})^{2}}{r_{k}^{2\alpha}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}}{r^{2\alpha}}\,\frac{dr}{r}.

Let
r_{k}\leq r\leq 10r_{k}
. Then recall from Lemma
2.13
, that

\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,t)^{2}\geq\left(\frac{r_{k}}{t}\right)^{2(1+d/p)}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r_{k})^{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{r_{k}}^{10r_{k}}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}}{r^{2\alpha}}\,\frac{dr}{r}\geq\int_{r_{k}}^{10r_{k}}\left(\frac{r_{k}}{r}\right)^{2(\alpha+1+d/p)}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r_{k})^{2}}{r_{k}^{2\alpha}}\,\frac{dr}{r}\geq\frac{\ln(10)}{10^{2(\alpha+1+d/p)}}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r_{k})^{2}}{r_{k}^{2\alpha}}.

This lets us conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r_{k})^{2}}{r_{k}^{2\alpha}}\lesssim_{p,d,\alpha}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{r_{k}}^{10r_{k}}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}}{r^{2\alpha}}\,\frac{dr}{r}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,r)^{2}}{r^{2\alpha}}\,\frac{dr}{r}.

The corollary then follows from Proposition
3.2
.
∎

Given any bounded set
E
, we can translate and dilate it so that it is contained in the unit ball centred at
0
. Hence, Corollary
3.16
immediately gives the first implication of Theorem
B
.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad=:C(d,p,c)\left[\overline{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(2B^{\prime})^{2}+E_{2}^{d,p}(4B,\Gamma,E_{0})^{2}\right].

Now, it is easily seen that for each
B^{\prime}
given as above, there exists a bounded number of Christ-David cubes for
\Gamma
(constructed as in Theorem
2.1
) which intersect it and with approximately the same radius. Using this, and Lemma
2.13
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}\overline{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(2B^{\prime})^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\approx\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}\\
\ell(Q)\leq 1\end{subarray}}\overline{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(Q)\ell(Q)^{d}.

Since
\Gamma
is a Lipschtiz graph (thus a bi-Lipschitz image of a
d
-plane), it follows from Dorronsoro’s theorem (
[
Dor85
]
) that the David-Semmes (see
[
DS91
]
)
\beta
-numbers (which we denote by
\widetilde{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}
) define a ‘Carleson measure’. This in particular implies that

(4.25)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}\\
Q\subset B(0,1)\end{subarray}}\overline{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(Q)^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\approx\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}\\
Q\subset B(0,1)\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(Q)^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim 1.

Lemma 4.16
.

We have

(4.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}E_{1}^{d,p}(2B,E_{0},E)^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}<+\infty.

Proof.

By Jensen’s inequality (Lemma
2.7
), we assume that
p\geq 2
, since
E_{1}^{d,p}(2B,E_{0},E)\lesssim E_{1}^{d,2}(2B,E_{0},E)
for any
1\leq p\leq 2
. Then, for
Q\in{\mathcal{S}}
, and keeping the notation as above, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{2B\cap E_{0}}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\leq\int_{2B\cap(E_{0}\setminus E)}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y).

If
y\in(E_{0}\setminus E)\cap 2B
, then
y\in F
, and thus
y\in T_{S}
for some
S\in\mathsf{W}_{n}
. Thus, by Lemma
4.14
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,K)\lesssim\diam(T_{S}).

We then compute

\displaystyle\int_{2B\cap(E_{0}\setminus E)}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}\\
T_{S}\cap 2B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\int_{T_{S}}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,E)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}\\
T_{S}\cap 2B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(T_{S})\diam(S)^{p}}{\ell(Q)^{p}}.

Then we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}\\
T_{S}\cap 2B_{Q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(T_{S})\diam(S)^{p}}{\ell(Q)^{p}}\ell(Q)^{d\left(\frac{p-2}{2}\right)}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}

(4.27)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}\\
T_{S}\cap 2B_{Q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\diam(S)^{p+d}}{\ell(Q)^{p+d}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}\,\ell(Q)^{d}\lesssim\sum_{T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{S}\\
2B_{Q}\cap T_{S}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\diam(S)^{\frac{2d}{p}+2}}{\ell(Q)^{\frac{2d}{p}-d+2}}

Notice that,

(4.28)

\displaystyle\mbox{if }d=1\mbox{ or }d=2\,\mbox{ then }\enskip 2-\frac{2}{p}d\left(\frac{p-2}{2}\right)>0\enskip\forall p\geq 2.

On the other hand,

(4.29)

\displaystyle\mbox{if }d\geq 3\mbox{ then }\enskip 2-\frac{2}{p}d\left(\frac{p-2}{2}\right)>0\mbox{ for }2\leq p<\frac{2d}{d-2}.

Given the hypotheses in Proposition
4.1
, in either case we may set

(4.30)

\displaystyle 2-\frac{2}{p}d\left(\frac{p-2}{2}\right)=:\alpha>0.

Now let
z\in T_{S}\cap B_{Q}
. Then we see, using (
4.21
) and recalling that, by definition of
\mathcal{S}
,
K\cap Q\neq\emptyset
,

(4.31)

\displaystyle\diam(S)\approx\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(T_{S},K)\lesssim|z-c_{Q}|\lesssim\ell(Q),

where
c_{Q}
is the center of
B_{Q}
.
Thus, given Whitney cube
T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}
, the number of cubes
Q\in{\mathcal{S}}
so that
T_{S}\cap 2B_{Q}\neq\emptyset
is bounded above by a universal constant
C
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{S}\\
2B_{Q}\cap T_{S}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\diam(S)^{\frac{2d}{p}+2}}{\ell(Q)^{\frac{2d}{p}-d+2}}

\displaystyle=\diam(S)^{\frac{2d}{p}+2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{S}\\
2B_{Q}\cap T_{S}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{\frac{2d}{p}-d+2}}

(4.32)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{C}\frac{\diam(S)^{\frac{2d}{p}+2}}{\diam(S)^{\frac{2d}{p}-d+2}}\approx\diam(S)^{d}.

Hence, we obtain

(4.33)

(
4.27
)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(T_{S})\lesssim\sum_{S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(S)\lesssim 1.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.17
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}E_{2}^{d,p}(4B,E_{0},\Gamma)^{2}\ell(Q)^{d}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{d}}\int_{E_{0}\cap 4B_{Q}}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Gamma)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}\ell(Q)^{d}<+\infty.

Proof.

As before, we assume
p\geq 2
. Note that if
y\in E_{0}\setminus\Gamma
, then
y\in F
. Thus, if
y\in E_{0}\setminus\Gamma
, then
y\in T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}
. Using the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Gamma)\lesssim\diam(T_{S})\approx\diam(S)
(Lemma
4.14
) we compute

\displaystyle\int_{\left(E_{0}\setminus\Gamma\right)\cap 2B}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Gamma)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}\\
T_{S}\cap 2B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\int_{T_{S})}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Gamma)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}\\
T_{S}\cap 2B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(T_{S})\diam(S)^{p}}{\ell(Q)^{p}}

The remainder of the proof is precisely as in Lemma
4.16
.
∎

Now (
4.2
) together with Lemma
4.9
, Lemma
4.16
, Lemma
4.17
and (
4.25
) give

\displaystyle\int_{K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D},\,\ell(Q)\leq 1\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(Q)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)<\infty

and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{D},\,\ell(Q)\leq 1\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\beta_{E}^{d,2}(Q)^{2}<\infty\enskip\enskip\mbox{for}\enskip\enskip\mathcal{H}^{d}\mbox{-almost all}\enskip x\in K.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
4.5
and therefore of Proposition
4.1
.

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
E\subset B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
d=1
or
d=2
, let
1\leq p<\infty
. If
d\geq 3
, let
1\leq p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
. Then, except for a set of zero
\mathcal{H}^{d}
measure, if
x\in E
is a tangent point of
E
then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,t)^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}<\infty.

Proof.

Let
x\in E
be a tangent point of
E
.
Recall Lemma
2.13
. Then, for
1\leq p<\infty
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(x,t)^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(Q)^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{Q\ni x}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(Q)^{2}\,\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{t\in[0,\infty)|C^{-1}\ell(Q)\leq t\leq C\ell(Q)\}}\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\ni x}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(Q)^{2}\,\int_{C^{-1}\ell(Q)}^{C\ell(Q)}\,dt/t

(4.34)

\displaystyle=\ln(C^{2})\sum_{Q\ni x}\beta_{E}^{d,p}(Q)^{2}.

This sum is bounded by Theorem (
4.1
).
∎

Now, any bounded set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be dilated and translated so that it is contained in
B(0,1)
. Thus, Theorem
A
follows immediately from Corollaries
3.16
and
4.18
.

4.4.
The sum over small cubes:
C^{1,\alpha}
case.

Throughout this subsection, we let
\alpha\in[0,1)
and we will use the
\overline{\beta}
coefficients defined in (
2.8
).

Recall the notation
K_{\alpha}
in (
4.2
) and that we want to prove Lemma
4.5
. We use Lemma
4.3
to obtain a map
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
(this is a graph map), so that
g(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
C^{1,\alpha}
. Set
G_{\alpha}:=\Pi(K_{\alpha})
.
Just like in the Lipschitz case, we find a Whitney decomposition of
B_{d}(0,1)\setminus G_{\alpha}
, which we denote by
\mathsf{W}_{d}
. We also define

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}:=\bigcap_{x\in K_{\alpha}}\overline{X_{\alpha}(x)},\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}:=E_{\alpha}\setminus K_{\alpha}.

For each
S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}
, we define
T_{S}:=\Pi^{-1}(S)\cap E_{0}
, and then set
\mathsf{W}_{n}:=\{T_{S}\,|\,S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}\}
.

Remark 4.19
.

It is immediate to see that (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) hold in this case, too. Lemmas
4.10
and
4.11
and
4.12
are still valid, with the same proofs.

Lemma 4.20
.

For
T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}
, we have

(4.35)

\displaystyle\diam(T_{S})\approx_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Lip}(f)}\diam(S)\mbox{ and }

(4.36)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(T_{S},K_{\alpha})\approx\diam(S).

Proof.

Both equation (
4.35
) and (
4.36
) follow from Lemma
4.13
, since a point admitting an
\alpha
-paraboloid is in particular a cone point, and
f
, being
C^{1,\alpha}
, is in particular Lipschitz.
∎

The improvement that we get with respect to the Lipschitz case, is in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
T_{S}\in\mathsf{W}_{n}

(
so
S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}
)
. Then if
y\in T_{S}
,

(4.37)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Gamma)\lesssim\diam(S)^{1+\alpha}.

Proof.

If
y\in T_{S}
, then
y\in F_{\alpha}
.
Recall that
F_{\alpha}=\cup_{S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}}T_{S}
. We claim that

(4.38)

\displaystyle g\left(\bigcup_{S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}}S\right)\subset F_{\alpha}.

Indeed, suppose that for some
p\in S
, we have
g(p)\in B(0,1)\setminus F_{\alpha}
. Since
p\notin K_{\alpha}
, then
g(p)\in B(0,1)\setminus E_{\alpha}
. Hence,
f(p)\in X_{\alpha}^{c}(x)
for some
x\in K_{\alpha}
. This contradicts the fact that
g
is the graph map of a
C^{1,\alpha}
extension of
f
. Thus (
4.38
) holds.
We also claim that

\displaystyle g(S)\subset T_{S}

for each
S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}
. We know that
g(S)\subset F_{\alpha}
. But by definition, if
g(S)\cap T_{R}
, for some
S\neq R\in\mathsf{W}_{d}
, then
g
maps a point
p\in S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\Pi(x)\neq p
. This is impossible since
g
is the graph map of a function whose range is the orthogonal complement of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is then clear that
\Pi|_{\Gamma}(T_{S})=S
.

Now let
y\in T_{S}
. Then
\Pi(y)\in S
. By the above, there exists a point
x\in\Gamma
so that
\Pi(x)=\Pi(y)
. Thus,
|x-y|=|Q(x)-Q(y)|
, where
Q
is the orthogonal projection onto
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\perp}
. Since
p=\Pi(x)=\Pi(y)\in S
, then there is a point
q\in G_{\alpha}
with
|q-p|\approx\diam(S)
. Moreover, we have
x,y\in X_{\alpha}(z)
, where
z=g(q)
. Thus,

\displaystyle|Q(x)-Q(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|Q(x)-Q(z)|+|Q(z)-Q(y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\theta}

\displaystyle|\Pi(x-z)|^{1+\alpha}+|\Pi(y-z)|^{1+\alpha}\approx_{\theta}|q-p|^{1+\alpha}\approx_{\theta}\diam(S)^{1+\alpha}.

∎

Let
Q\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Let
B^{Q}
be a ball centered on
K_{\alpha}
so that
r(B^{Q})\approx\ell(Q)
and
B_{Q}\subset B^{Q}\subset 3B_{Q}
. Then
\beta_{E}^{d,p}(Q)\approx\beta_{E}^{d,p}(B^{Q})
.
Note that

\displaystyle E\subset E_{\alpha},

since if
x\in E\setminus E_{\alpha}
, then there exists a point
y\in K_{\alpha}
so that
x\in X_{\alpha}^{c}(y)
, which contradicts
y\in K_{\alpha}
. Let
L_{2B^{Q}}
be the plane infimising
\overline{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(2B^{Q})
. By containment, we then have

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,p}(B^{Q})\leq\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,p}(B^{Q},L_{2B^{Q}})\leq\overline{\beta}_{E_{\alpha}}^{d,p}(B^{Q},L_{2B^{Q}}).

Moreover, by Lemma
4.12
(and Remark
4.19
),
E_{\alpha}
is
d
-LCR. Further, since
x_{B}\in K_{\alpha}\subset E_{\alpha}
, then
x_{B}\in\Gamma
. Moreover,
\Gamma
is
d
-LCR. Thus, we can apply Lemma

2.16
with
E_{1}=E_{\alpha}
and
E_{2}=\Gamma
. We have

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,p}(Q)\approx\overline{\beta}_{E}^{d,p}(B)\leq\overline{\beta}_{E_{\alpha}}^{d,p}(B,L_{2B})

\displaystyle\lesssim\overline{\beta}_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(2B)+\left(\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{d}}\int_{E_{\alpha}\cap 4B}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Gamma)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=:\beta_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(2B)+E(Q,E_{\alpha},\Gamma).

The sum
\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{\beta_{\Gamma}^{d,p}(2B^{Q})^{2}}{\ell(Q)^{2\alpha}}\ell(Q)^{d}
can be shown to be finite as in (
4.25
).
On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle E(Q,E_{\alpha},\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\left(\ell(Q)^{-d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}\\
T_{S}\cap 4B^{Q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\int_{T_{S}\cap E_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\Gamma)}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\ell(Q)^{-d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}\\
T_{S}\cap 4B^{Q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\int_{T_{S}}\left(\frac{\diam(S)^{1+\alpha}}{\ell(Q)}\right)^{p}d\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}\\
T_{S}\cap 4B^{Q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(T_{S})\diam(S)^{p(1+\alpha)}}{\ell(Q)^{p+d}}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}E(Q,E_{\alpha},\Gamma)\ell(Q)^{d-2\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}\ell(Q)^{d-2\alpha}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}\\
T_{S}\cap 4B^{Q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\infty}(T_{S})\diam(S)^{p(1+\alpha)}}{\ell(Q)^{p+d}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}\\
T_{S}\cap 4B^{Q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\diam(S)^{d+p(1+\alpha)}}{\ell(Q)^{p+d}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}\ell(Q)^{d-2\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{S\in\mathsf{W}_{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in{\mathcal{S}}\\
4B^{Q}\cap T_{S}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{\diam(S)^{\frac{2d}{p}+2(1+\alpha)}}{\ell(Q)^{2(1+\alpha)-d+\frac{2d}{p}}}

It is easy to see that this sum turns out to be a geometric series: as in (
4.28
), (
4.29
) and (
4.30
), the choice of the exponents
p
guarantees that
2(1+\alpha)-d+\frac{2d}{p}>0
. Then one can argue as in (
4.31
), (
4.32
) and (
4.33
). This estimates, together with Lemmas
4.9
and (
4.8
), conclude the proof of Lemma
4.6
and thus of Proposition
4.2
.
One can then obtain one direction of Theorem
B
as in Corollary
4.18
.
