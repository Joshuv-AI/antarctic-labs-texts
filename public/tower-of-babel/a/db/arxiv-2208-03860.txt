Rankability and Linear Ordering Problem: Probabilistic Insight and Algorithms
Leszek Szczecinski, Harsh Sukheja

Abstract
The linear ordering problem (LOP) , which consists in ordering M M objects from their pairwise comparisons, is commonly applied in many areas of research. While efforts have been made to devise efficient LOP algorithms, verification of whether the data are rankable , that is, if the linear ordering problem (LOP) solutions have a meaningful interpretation, received much less attention. To address this problem, we adopt a probabilistic perspective where the results of pairwise comparisons are modeled as Bernoulli variables with a common parameter and we estimate the latter from the observed data. The brute-force approach to the required enumeration has a prohibitive complexity of O ⁡ ( M ! ) O(M!) , so we reformulate the problem and introduce a concept of the Slater spectrum that generalizes the Slater index, and then devise an algorithm to find the spectrum with complexity O ⁡ ( M 3 ​ 2 M ) O(M^{3}2^{M}) that is manageable for moderate values of M M . Furthermore, with a minor modification of the algorithm, we are able to find all solutions of the LOP with the complexity O ⁡ ( M ​ 2 M ) O(M2^{M}) . Numerical examples are shown on synthetic and real-world data, and the algorithms are publicly available.



Keywords—
Linear ordering problem, Combinatorial optimization, Ranking, Rankability, Bayesian estimation

1
Introduction

In this work, we propose models and methods to analyze the rankability of the data which tells us if it is possible to find the order among objects by solving the
LOP
. The
LOP
has found wide use in many different areas, e.g., for the aggregation of the preferences of individual judgments or for ranking in sports where the teams (or players) are compared through the game result. Many more applications rely on the
LOP
formulation, see
(
Ceberio et al. 2015
, Sec. 1)
,
(
Martí et al. 2012
, Sec. 1)
, or
Charon and Hudry 2010
; so, while the wording can change from one application to another, the fundamental problem remains the same.

The effort in the area of the
LOP
was mainly focused on devising efficient numerical solutions as, even if the
LOP
can be solved exactly
(
deCani 1969
)
, for large problems, approximations are unavoidable due to its NP-hardness; see
(
Charon and Hudry 2010
)
,
(
Martí et al. 2012
)
,
(
Ceberio et al. 2015
)
for an overview of the literature.

Recent works e.g.,
Anderson et al. 2019
,
Anderson et al. 2021
,
Cameron et al. 2021
addressed an important issue of data
rankability
, that is, deciding if the data can be ordered. This is a critical step because, while objects can be ordered algorithmically, we need to know whether the ranking obtained can be meaningfully interpreted.

We continue this line of research, but, unlike
Anderson et al. 2019
or other works, we use a probabilistic approach, explicitly modeling the results of pairwise comparisons as Bernoulli variables. This follows the spirit of the early analysis in
Slater 1961
or
Remage and Thompson 1966
. In particular,
Slater 1961
proposed a hypothesis testing approach, which assumes that all Bernoulli variables have the same distribution with parameter
p
, and verifies if hypothesis
p=0.5
(meaning that the data is purely random and not related to the order) can be rejected. However, this method is too complex to be practical in most cases.

We also use the assumption that
p
is common to all variables, but instead of testing the hypothesis, we estimate
p
from the data. The estimation problem relies on finding the so-called Slater spectrum, which generalizes the well-known Slater index and tells us how “far” all possible orders are from the results we observe. We devise an algorithm that allows us to obtain the Slater spectrum efficiently. This is our main contribution. Incidentally, the algorithm we develop also allows us to find all the solutions of the
LOP
, which is itself an interesting contribution because we show that it can be done with the complexity
O(M2^{M})
which is known to be the bound on the complexity of finding only one solution
(
Remage and Thompson 1966
)
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
we describe the problem, define the model, show the relationship between the
LOP
and its probabilistic counterpart, and briefly describe the approach suggested in
Slater 1961
. The estimation problem is defined in Sec.
3
while Sec.
4
focuses on the proposed algorithm and evaluates the related numerical complexity. Interestingly, as a “byproduct” of the algorithm developed, we are able to recover all the solutions of the
LOP
and this comes with virtually no additional cost; the additional algorithmic steps required to do that are explained in Sec.
4.2
. The algorithms implemented in Python are available at
our GitHub repository
. Numerical examples are shown in Sec.
5
where we use synthetic data, as well as the results of cricket games in the
Indian Premier League (IPL)
and the volleyball games of the Italian SuperLega. We conclude the work in Sec.
6
.

2
Model and problem definition

Consider objects indexed with
M
integers from the set
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,M\}
and assume that they can be ordered, e.g.,
2\succ 5\succ 1\succ\ldots\succ M\succ 4
, where
m\succ n
means that object
m
is ordered
before
object
n
and the usual meaning is that “
m
is preferred to
n
” (in social science), or, in sports, that “team
m
is better than team
n
”. The order will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{\rho}=[\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{M}]\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
, where
\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{I},i=1,\ldots,M
,
\rho_{1}\succ\rho_{2}\succ\ldots,\succ\rho_{M}
, and
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
is the space of permutations of
\mathcal{I}
,
|\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}|=M!
. It essentially means that the object
\rho_{l}
is ordered at position
l
, e.g., in sport context,
\rho_{1}
is the “best” team/player. In the previous example, we have
\rho_{1}=2
,
\rho_{2}=5
,
\rho_{3}=1
,
\rho_{M-1}=M
, and
\rho_{M}=4
.

The order
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, being unknown, can only be inferred from the observations and the inference result,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is called a
ranking
.
We assume that we can observe the results of comparison between a pair of objects, where the simplest case occurs if the result of the comparison is binary, i.e., either
m
wins against
n
, or
n
wins against
m
, that is, the ties are not allowed. In this case, the results can be stored in the
observation
matrix
W
with the entries
w_{m,n}\in\mathbb{N}
indicating how many times
m
won against
n
. Diagonal entries in the matrix are ignored or, equivalently, set to zero,
w_{m,m}=0
. The total number of comparisons is
T=\sum_{m,n}w_{m,n}
and the number of comparisons between
m
and
n
is given by
K_{m,n}=K_{n,m}=w_{m,n}+w_{n,m}
.

The total number of distinct pairs is given by
{{M}\choose{2}}
. In general, not all pairs need to be compared, and the comparison may be performed multiple times for the same pair. For example, in sports, some players face each other very rarely or even never, while others compete frequently.

If knowing
m\succ n
implied that
w_{n,m}=0
, the pairwise comparison would be in perfect agreement with the underlying order of the objects. In practice, however, we may have both
w_{m,n}>0
and
w_{n,m}>0
, which means that, in some comparisons (between
m
and
n
)
m
wins and, in others,
n
does. This inconsistency of observations with the underlying order may be interpreted as a manifestation of randomness that may be modelled using Bernoulli variables:

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{m{\textnormal{ wins against }}n|m\succ n\right\}

\displaystyle=p

(1)

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{m{\textnormal{ wins against }}n|m\prec n\right\}

\displaystyle=1-p,

(2)

where
p\in[0.5,1]
. At the extremes,
p=1
means that observations are always in agreement with the underlying order, while
p=0.5
means that the win of
m
over
n
is independent of the order: in this case, searching for the order is obviously useless, which is synonymous with the data being not rankable.

We may estimate the ranking via
maximum likelihood (ML)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}J(\boldsymbol{\rho})

(3)

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left\{{\textnormal{{W}}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}\right\}

(4)

where, in general, (
3
) may have many equivalent solutions
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

(
Anderson et al. 2022
)
.

For a known/given order
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, the results (comparisons) are mutually independent; this conventional modeling assumption, which allows us to write (
4
) as follows:

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\neq n}\Pr\left\{w_{m,n}|\boldsymbol{\rho}\right\}

(5)

\displaystyle=p^{T-S(\boldsymbol{\rho})}(1-p)^{S(\boldsymbol{\rho})},

(6)

\displaystyle S(\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle=T-C(\boldsymbol{\rho}),

(7)

\displaystyle C(\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=m+1}^{M}w_{\rho_{m},\rho_{n}}

(8)

where the notation
\Pr\left\{w_{m,n}|\boldsymbol{\rho}\right\}
should be read as the probability of observing
w_{m,n}
wins of
m
over
n
conditioned on the given order
\boldsymbol{\rho}

The
inconsistency index

S(\boldsymbol{\rho})
denotes the number of observations that are in disagreement with the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}
. For example, in sports,
S(\boldsymbol{\rho})
is the number of games in which favorite teams lose against the underdogs (where, according to
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, the favorite is ordered before the underdog). Similarly, the
consistency index

C(\boldsymbol{\rho})
measures the number of observations in agreement with the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}
.

An alternative formulation of the
LOP
, known as
matrix triangulation
, sees (
9
) as a search for a permutation of rows and columns of the matrix
W
, which maximizes
C(\boldsymbol{\rho})
which is the sum of elements in the upper triangular part of the permuted matrix
W
; this can be seen directly in (
8
).

As long as
p>0.5
, we may write (
3
) as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}S(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}C(\boldsymbol{\rho})

(9)

and, the inconsistency index of the optimal ranking
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle\hat{S}

\displaystyle=S(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}),

(10)

is known as the Slater index
(
Charon and Hudry 2010
)
.

Therefore, for all
p>0.5
, there is a one-to-one mapping between problems (
9
) and (
3
), which explains the appeal of the model (
1
)-(
2
): we do not need to
know

p
to find the ranking
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, and then we may focus on finding numerically efficient methods to solve (
9
).

It should be noted that the literature may formulate a more general version of (
1
)-(
2
), by assuming that each comparison of
m
vs.
n
has its own parameter
p_{m,n}
which replaces
p
in (
1
)
(
deCani 1969
)
,
(
Flueck and Korsh 1975
)
,
(
Tiwisina and Külpmann 2019
)
. However, then, to find
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, we
must
know or estimate
M(M-1)/2
parameters
p_{m,n}

(
deCani 1969
)
– a problem compounded by the fact that additional transitivity conditions must be satisfied
m\succ k\succ n\implies p_{m,n}\geq p_{m,k}\wedge p_{m,n}\geq p_{k,n}
,
(
Tiwisina and Külpmann 2019
)
.

The optimization is then quite difficult, e.g.,
Tiwisina and Külpmann 2019
needed heuristic optimization tools. However, in addition to numerical problems, difficulty may appear due to the fact that the number of parameters to estimate may be relatively large compared to the number of observations
T
. For the sake of argument, assuming (very unrealistically) that we
know

\boldsymbol{\rho}
, with
K_{m,n}=K
, there are
K
observations per parameter. This affects the reliability of the estimate as, in practice,
K
may be small. For example, using football data, as done in
Tiwisina and Külpmann 2019
, we would have
K=2
observations per each Bernoulli parameter
p_{m,n}
; then, the reliability of the estimation of
p_{m,n}
may and should be questioned.

Taking into account the above, using a model (
1
)-(
2
) may be seen as a pragmatic middle ground, striking a balance between estimation complexity/reliability and the generality of a model. However, our goal is not to discuss the modeling assumption, but rather to point out the fact that many
LOP
s are of the form (
9
) and thus, implicitly, are related to the model (
1
)-(
2
).

2.1
Rankability

The equivalence between (
9
) and (
3
) requires
p>0.5
. On the other hand, for
p=0.5
, i.e., when the data is not rankable, we have
J(\boldsymbol{\rho})\equiv 2^{-T}
. Then, we can still find solutions of (
9
), but they do not solve (
3
) in any meaningful way simply because
J(\boldsymbol{\rho})
is independent of
S(\boldsymbol{\rho})
. Therefore, we conclude that the rankability of the data
W
is captured by the parameter
p
, and our work focuses on this very issue; we will calculate the statistics of
p
, and more generally obtain its posterior distribution,
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
.

This approach to rankability follows the postulate of
Anderson et al. 2019
which requires the rankability criterion to be agnostic with respect to the ranking algorithm. Indeed, to conclude if the data
W
is rankable, we find
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
and this does not presuppose how the ranking
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
should be found. In fact, as we shall see, the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
found in (
9
) does not play a particular role, and we scan the whole permutation space
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Before explaining our approach, we can revisit some previous takes on rankability.

2.1.1
Slater’s method

The rankability has been addressed in
Slater 1961
through hypothesis testing where the hypothesis
H_{0}
is that all
T
observations are obtained with probability
p=0.5
, that is, are drawn uniformly from the space of
2^{T}
elements, each represented by the observation matrix which we can treat as a random variable
\underline{{\textnormal{{W}}}}
.

The rankings obtained from random observations
\underline{{\textnormal{{W}}}}
are also random

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\underline{{\textnormal{{W}}}}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}S(\boldsymbol{\rho};\underline{{\textnormal{{W}}}})

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{S}}

\displaystyle=S(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\underline{{\textnormal{{W}}}}};\underline{{\textnormal{{W}}}}),

(12)

where
S(\boldsymbol{\rho},{\textnormal{{W}}})
is the same as (
7
) and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{{\textnormal{{W}}}}
is the same as (
3
) with the dependence on the observation matrix explicitly indicated.

If, under hypothesis
H_{0}
, the probability of obtaining the Slater index
\hat{\underline{S}}
smaller than
\hat{S}
is negligible, we reject
H_{0}
, i.e., the data is considered rankable. More precisely, we calculate

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{val}}=\mathrm{Pr}_{\underline{\hat{S}}}\{\underline{\hat{S}}\leq\hat{S}\}

(13)

and compare it to a small threshold
\epsilon>0
, e.g., it is common to use
\epsilon=0.05
. Then

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{val}}>\epsilon\implies\textrm{reject $H_{0}$}.

(14)

We note that
Slater 1961
suggested calculating (
13
) by brute force, i.e., by (i) enumerating all possible outcomes, (ii) forming the corresponding observation matrices
W
, (iii) solving (
9
) for each of the matrices, and (iv) counting all solutions whose Slater index is smaller than
\hat{S}
.

The computational bottleneck lies in steps (i)-(iii): solving the
LOP
for all realizations of the matrices
W
, is too complex to be practical. For example, with the simplest scheduling
K_{m,n}=1
(i.e., one comparison per pair), we have
T={{M}\choose{2}}=M(M-1)/2
and thus we have to solve the
LOP
for
2^{T}
matrices
W
. Even if
M
is moderate, e.g., for
M=20
, when (
9
) is easily solved, solving it
2^{T}\approx 10^{57}
times is an impossible challenge.

Thus, the above explicit implementation is not applicable in practice. Alternatively, we may evaluate the probability in (
13
) via the Monte Carlo method, by mimicking steps (i)-(iv) shown above. Then, in steps (i)-(ii), instead of enumeration, we will randomly generate
N_{\textrm{MC}}
matrices
W
with probability
p=0.5
. Of course, we still have to solve (
9
)
N_{\textrm{MC}}
times.

The main theoretical issue with Slater’ method is that
\hat{S}

is not
a sufficient statistic for
p
, which simply means that, using only
\hat{S}
, we lose information about
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
– the posterior distribution of
p
. Consequently, any inference about
p
, made using only
\hat{S}
, will be an approximation. This will become obvious in
3.1
where we will show a sufficient statistic.

2.1.2
Optimal solutions

Because Slater’s method is difficult to apply in practice, the rankability problem has been addressed using the quantitative metrics related to the properties of the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, e.g., in
Anderson et al. 2019
,
Anderson et al. 2021
,
Cameron et al. 2021
,
Anderson et al. 2022
.

The simplest to understand is the
degree of linearity

(
Cameron et al. 2021
, Sec. 3.1)
:

\displaystyle\lambda=1-\frac{\hat{S}}{T},

(15)

which expresses the proportion of observations in
W
which are consistent with the optimal ranking
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
.

Other metrics, e.g., shown in
Anderson et al. 2019
,
Anderson et al. 2021
,
Cameron et al. 2021
,
Anderson et al. 2022
are more difficult to calculate and we do not detail them here. Nevertheless, from the perspective of our work, these metrics
r
may be written as

\displaystyle r=r(\hat{\mathcal{R}})

(16)

where
\hat{\mathcal{R}}=\{\boldsymbol{\rho}:S(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\hat{S}\}
is the set of all optimal solutions
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
. In particular, some of these metrics
r(\hat{\mathcal{R}})
take into account not only
\hat{S}
but also all solutions
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\hat{\mathcal{R}}
.

Although this should offer an improvement over the criterion in (
15
), and may provide an interpretation to the existence of multiple solutions
Anderson et al. 2022
, it shares the main problem of Slater’s method by using insufficient statistics of
p
.

3
Probabilistic inference

The approach we propose can be seen as an estimation of the Bernoulli distribution parameter,
p
, from the observation matrix
W
.

We quickly note that it is tempting to solve the joint
ML
problem

\displaystyle\hat{p},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{p,\boldsymbol{\rho}}J_{p}(\boldsymbol{\rho}),

(17)

where
J_{p}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
is the same as (
6
) with the dependence on
p
explicitly indicated.

But since
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
does not depend on
p
, we have
\hat{S}=S(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})
so

\displaystyle\hat{p}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{p}p^{T-\hat{S}}(1-p)^{\hat{S}}

(18)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\hat{S}}{T}=\lambda;

(19)

thus, the degree of linearity,
\lambda
, shown in (
15
) is the estimate of
\hat{p}
in the joint
ML
problems (
17
).

As we said in Sec.
2.1
,
\hat{S}
and
\lambda
are not, in general, sufficient statistics for
p
.

The problem with the joint
ML
estimation is that, focusing on the mode, it ignores the form of the underlying distribution. Here, we propose to find the latter

\displaystyle f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})

\displaystyle\propto\Pr({\textnormal{{W}}}|p)f(p)

(20)

\displaystyle\propto\Pr({\textnormal{{W}}}|p)

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}\Pr\left\{{\textnormal{{W}}}|p,\boldsymbol{\rho}\right\}\Pr\left\{\boldsymbol{\rho}\right\}

(22)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}p^{T-S(\boldsymbol{\rho})}(1-p)^{S(\boldsymbol{\rho})}

(23)

\displaystyle=\phi(p),

(24)

where
f(p)
is the prior distribution of the parameter
p
and we assume a uniform distribution, i.e.,
f(p)=2,p\in[0.5,1]
which explains (
21
).

The difference with the approaches described in Sec.
2.1.2
appears in (
22
): unlike
Anderson et al. 2019
or
Cameron et al. 2021
which assign a particular role to the optimal solutions
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\hat{\mathcal{R}}
, we treat the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}
as a random variable and marginalize over it, assuming that it is uniformly distributed over the set of permutations
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
(this is done in (
23
)).

3.1
Slater spectrum

The explicit order enumeration in (
23
) has a complexity of
M!
that is prohibitive even for moderate
M
. Our goal is to reduce complexity by exploiting the structure of solutions.

First, we note that the number of comparisons that are inconsistent with the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is obviously a non-negative integer that cannot be greater than
T
, i.e.,
S(\boldsymbol{\rho})\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
. It is possible to rewrite (
23
) as follows:

\displaystyle\phi(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{t}p^{T-t}(1-p)^{t}

(25)

where

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}\mathds{I}\big[{S(\boldsymbol{\rho})=t}\big],\hskip 9.24994ptt=0,\ldots,T

(26)

is the number of orders
\boldsymbol{\rho}
(in the set
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
) with the inconsistency index equal to
S(\boldsymbol{\rho})=t
. The sequence
\{a_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
will be called
the Slater spectrum
and, by construction, satisfies the conditions:
\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{t}=M!
. Moreover, for any order
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, it is enough to reverse it (i.e., enumerate its elements backward) and, for the obtained order
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}=[\rho_{M},\rho_{M-1},\ldots,\rho_{1}]
, all disagreements with the order are transformed into agreements, and vice versa, i.e.,
S(\boldsymbol{\rho})=T-S(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})
,
a_{T-t}=a_{t}
, and, using (
8
) yields

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}\mathds{I}\big[{C(\boldsymbol{\rho})=t}\big],\hskip 9.24994ptt=0,\ldots,T;

(27)

in other words, the Slater spectrum and consistency spectrum are the same, the choice of using (
26
) or (
27
) is a matter of convenience, and the name is given due to historical reasons.

Due to (
10
) and the symmetry we also have

\displaystyle t<\hat{S}\hskip 9.24994pt\textrm{or}\hskip 9.24994ptt>T-\hat{S}\implies a_{t}\equiv 0,

(28)

and we can rewrite (
25
) as

\displaystyle\phi(p)=\sum_{t=\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}a_{t}p^{T-t}(1-p)^{t}

(29)

that is, the first non-zero element
a_{t}
is indexed with
t=\hat{S}
(and the last non-zero element is indexed with
t=T-\hat{S}
).

The problem is now reduced to finding
\{a_{t}\}
and we show in Sec.
4
the algorithm which avoids explicit enumeration over
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
which appear in (
27
).

With the Slater spectrum known, we can easily find the posterior
probability density function (PDF)
by normalizing the function
\phi(p)
as follows:

\displaystyle f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\phi(p),

(30)

where the normalization factor is given by

1
1

1

From the property of the beta distribution

\displaystyle\int_{0.5}^{1}p^{T-t}(1-p)^{t}\,\mathrm{d}p=\frac{I_{0.5}(t+1,T-t+1)}{(T+1){{T}\choose{t}}},

(31)

where
I_{x}(\alpha,\beta)={{\alpha+\beta-2}\choose{\alpha-1}}(\alpha+\beta-1)\int_{0}^{x}p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}\,\mathrm{d}p
is the regularized incomplete beta function, the normalization factor in (
35
) is calculated as

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int_{0.5}^{1}\phi(p)\,\mathrm{d}p=\sum_{t=\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}a_{t}\int_{0.5}^{1}p^{T-t}(1-p)^{t}\,\mathrm{d}p

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}\frac{a_{t}I_{0.5}(t+1,T-t+1)}{{{T}\choose{t}}}

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{0.5}{T+1}\sum_{t=\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}\frac{a_{t}I_{0.5}(t+1,T-t+1)}{{{T}\choose{t}}}+\frac{a_{T-t}I_{0.5}(T-t+1,t+1)}{{{T}\choose{T-t}}},

(34)

where, in (
34
) we add the series enumerating them backwards (hence the multiplication by
0.5
).
From the property
1-I_{x}(\alpha,\beta)=I_{1-x}(\beta,\alpha)
, we obtain
I_{0.5}(\alpha,\beta)+I_{0.5}(\beta,\alpha)=1
which, plugged in (
34
), yields (
35
) if we exploit the symmetry
a_{t}=a_{T-t}
.

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int_{0.5}^{1}\phi(p)\,\mathrm{d}p=\frac{0.5}{T+1}\sum_{t=\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}\frac{a_{t}}{{{T}\choose{t}}};

(35)

We easily see that (
35
) the terms under summation are identical for two indices
t
and
T-t
. However, we keep this redundancy for the sake of simplified notation (when
T
is even, the symmetry breaks and the term for
a_{T/2}
would require a separate notation).

3.2
Slater index vs Slater spectrum

The main conclusions from the above development are summarized in the following, which is obvious from the construction of the Slater spectrum.

Proposition 1
(Sufficient/insufficient statistics)

1. The Slater spectrum is the sufficient statistic for
p
.

2. The Slater index
\hat{S}
is
not
a sufficient statistic for
p
.

Consequently, we may use the Slater spectrum in standard probabilistic and statistical tools to draw conclusions about
p
without losing any information that can be provided by the matrix
W
.

Conversely, using only the Slater index
\hat{S}
, conclusions about
p
(thus, also conclusions about rankability) will be approximate. The corollary conclusion is that the methods described in Sec.
2.1.1
and Sec.
2.1.2
are approximate.

However, although relying only on the Slater index must lead to approximate conclusions, this is not to say that it is not useful. Indeed, it is better to be approximately correct than to not be able to say anything exactly, which may be the case if the calculation of the Slater spectrum is too complex to be practical.

Moreover, since the Slater index (or its transformation, e.g., (
15
)) has often been used in the literature, e.g.,
Slater 1961
,
Charon and Hudry 2010
,
Anderson et al. 2019
, it is interesting to look at its role.

From the perspective offered by Proposition
1
, using only the Slater index to estimate
p
, may be seen as using a “degenerate” form of the Slater spectrum

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{t}=a_{t}\mathds{I}\big[{t=\hat{S}\vee t=T-\hat{S}}\big],

(36)

and the corresponding “degenerate” posterior
PDF
is obtained by replacing
a_{t}
with
\tilde{a}_{t}
in (
29
)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{Z}}\tilde{\phi}(p)

(37)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(p)

\displaystyle=a_{\hat{S}}\left[p^{T-\hat{S}}(1-p)^{\hat{S}}+p^{\hat{S}}(1-p)^{T-\hat{S}}\right]

(38)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{\hat{S}}}{(T+1){{T}\choose{\hat{S}}}}

(39)

where, we immediately see that, due to normalization by
\tilde{Z}
, we might ignore the value of the coefficient
a_{\hat{S}}
. This would mean that knowing the number of optimal solutions
a_{\hat{S}}=|\hat{\mathcal{R}}|
does not help to determine the rankability of
W
under the assumed spectrum form (
36
).

Thanks to (
37
), we may work in the domain of the posterior distributions
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
and this may provide us with an intuitive understanding of the conditions under which the approximations based on the Slater index, that is, using the degenerate spectrum
\{\tilde{a}_{t}\}
instead of
\{a_{t}\}
, may be useful.

We do it considering two limiting cases. First, we assume that
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
is “concentrated” in the vicinity of
p=0.5
, e.g.,
\frac{1}{Z}\int_{0.5}^{0.5+\epsilon}f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})\,\mathrm{d}p\approx 1
, where
\epsilon
is a small number. Intuitively, this would mean that the data is not rankable. In the second case, we assume that
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
is concentrated close to
p=1
,
\frac{1}{Z}\int_{1-\epsilon}^{1}f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})\,\mathrm{d}p\approx 1
which would be a good indicator of rankability of
W
.

To deal with the second case, changing variable
p=1-\epsilon
, with inconsequential abuse of notation, we write (
29
) as a function of
\epsilon

\displaystyle\phi(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}a_{t}(1-\epsilon)^{T-t}\epsilon^{t}\approx a_{\hat{S}}(1-\epsilon)^{T-\hat{S}}\epsilon^{\hat{S}}

(40)

\displaystyle\approx\tilde{\phi}(\epsilon)

(41)

where the approximation in (
40
) is valid if
\epsilon^{t+1}a_{t+1}\ll\epsilon^{t}a_{t}
(which will hold for sufficiently small
\epsilon
). The same approximation applied to (
38
) allows us to write (
41
) which essentially means that the Slater’s index is sufficient to describe the posterior pdf
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
for sufficiently small
\epsilon
. Actually, in this case, we say that the entire posterior
PDF
may be approximated using only one term of
\phi(p)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})\propto p^{T-\hat{S}}(1-p)^{\hat{S}}.

(42)

Similarly, to deal with the case when the
PDF
is concentrated in the vicinity of
p=0.5
, after the change of variable
p=0.5+\epsilon
, we can write (
29
) as

\displaystyle\phi(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=(0.5-\epsilon)^{T}\sum_{\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}a_{t}\left(\frac{0.5+\epsilon}{0.5-\epsilon}\right)^{t}.

(43)

In this case, for small
\epsilon
, the entire spectrum
\{a_{t}\}
is required to define
\phi(\epsilon)
, which, therefore, cannot be well approximated using only the Slater index.

Therefore, if
W
is not rankable (the
PDF
concentrated in the vicinity of
p=0.5
), the Slater index is not sufficient to characterize the posterior
PDF
, while, if the data is rankable, the Slater index can, indeed, be used to describe well the most significant part of the posterior pdf
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
.

However, leveraging this observation is not immediately obvious because, to know if we can rely on the Slater index alone, we need to know if the data is rankable beforehand which, of course, defeats the purpose of using the Slater index to analyze the rankability.

Visualizing the relationship between the degenerate and the true distributions may be difficult and, in practice, it is preferable to use parameters of the posterior
PDF

f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
such as the mode or the posterior mean of
p
.

3.3
Mean

A simple way to characterize the posterior distribution
f(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})
is through the posterior mean

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[p]

\displaystyle=\int_{0.5}^{1}pf(p|{\textnormal{{W}}})\,\mathrm{d}p

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{t=\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}a_{t}\int_{0.5}^{1}p^{T-t+1}(1-p)^{t}\,\mathrm{d}p

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(T+2)}\sum_{t=\hat{S}}^{T-\hat{S}}\frac{a_{t}I_{0.5}(t+1,T-t+2)}{{{T+1}\choose{t}}},

(46)

where the regularized incomplete beta function
I_{x}(\alpha,\beta)
is available in popular numerical packages.

The degenerate posterior mean is obtained using degenerate Slater spectrum (
36
)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\tilde{p}]

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{\hat{S}}}{\tilde{Z}(T+2)}\sum_{t\in\{\hat{S},T-\hat{S}\}}\frac{I_{0.5}(t+1,T-t+2)}{{{T+1}\choose{t}}}

(47)

\displaystyle=\frac{(T+1){{T}\choose{\hat{S}}}}{(T+2)}\sum_{t\in\{\hat{S},T-\hat{S}\}}\frac{I_{0.5}(t+1,T-t+2)}{{{T+1}\choose{t}}}.

(48)

Again, we keep the formulas simple at the expense of some redundancy.

3.4
Mode

The posterior distribution (
30
) can be also characterized by its mode

\displaystyle\check{p}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{p\in[0.5,1]}\phi(p).

(49)

We may then use it in a way somewhat similar to what is done in Slater’s methods: if the most likely explanation of the matrix
W
is
\check{p}=0.5
, we may declare
W
not rankable. However, unlike in Slater’s method, we do not test the binary hypothesis, and obtaining
\check{p}\approx 0.5
also may be treated as evidence for non-rankability.

To comment on the location of the mode, let us calculate the first and the second derivatives of
\phi(p)
:

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{t}p^{t}(1-p)^{T-t}\frac{t-pT}{p(1-p)}

(50)

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{t}p^{t}(1-p)^{T-t}\frac{(t-pT)^{2}+[t(2p-1)-Tp^{2}]}{p^{2}(1-p)^{2}}.

(51)

From the symmetry of the spectrum
a_{T-t}=a_{t}
, we easily obtain the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{t}t=\frac{T}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{t}

(52)

which allows us to calculate the first derivative at
p=0.5
as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(0.5)

\displaystyle=0.5^{T-2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{t}(t-0.5T)=0;

(53)

this means that the function
\phi(p)
has an extremum at
p=0.5
and we would like to verify if it is a maximum or the minimum of
\phi(p)
.

The results we observed are similar in simulations and in empirical data, cf. Fig.
3
, Fig.
6
, lead to the following:

Conjecture 1
(Maximum of
\phi(p)
)

The function
\phi(p)
has only one maximum on the interval
[0.5,1)
.

We can express now the following sufficient conditions for determining the location of the mode:

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\ddot{\phi}(0.5)

(54)

\displaystyle\sigma>0

\displaystyle\implies\check{p}>0.5

(55)

\displaystyle\sigma<0

\displaystyle\implies\check{p}=0.5,

(56)

where Conjecture
1
is necessary to guarantee that, if
p=0.5
is a local maximum, it is also the global one; this is expressed in (
56
). Then, the most likely explanation for the matrix
W
is the model with
\check{p}=0.5
, and we may declare
W
not rankable. On the other hand, when
\sigma>0
we know that
p=0.5
is not a maximum and we may declare
W
rankable even without Conjecture
1
.

Note that the conditions in (
55
)-(
56
) are only sufficient because, if
\sigma=0
, the ambiguity about
p=0.5
being minimum or maximum cannot be resolved and we need higher-order derivatives of
\phi(p)
, which makes the derivations slightly more involved.

To find the mode
\check{p}
, we may proceed in two steps. First, we verify if the conditions (
56
) are satisfied, as then we immediately obtain
\check{p}=0.5
. If, on the other hand, (
55
) is satisfied, we need to numerically solve equation
\dot{\phi}(p)=0,p\in(0.5,1)
.

Although finding the mode
\check{p}
can be done numerically, the main point of the above analysis is to find conditions for the rankability
without
solving numerical equations.

This is possible if we use the degenerate spectrum
\tilde{a}_{t}
from (
36
): we find
\sigma
to be a quadratic function of the Slater index
\hat{S}

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}=\ddot{\tilde{\phi}}(0.5)=\frac{a_{\hat{S}}}{2^{T-4}}\Big[(\hat{S}-0.5T)^{2}-T0.25\Big]

(57)

so, (
55
)-(
56
) become

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}>0

\displaystyle\iff\hat{S}<\hat{S}_{\textrm{th}},

(58)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}<0

\displaystyle\iff\hat{S}>\hat{S}_{\textrm{th}},

(59)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\textrm{th}}

\displaystyle=0.5(T-\sqrt{T})

(60)

Relationship to the degree of linearity

Using (
15
), the relationships (
58
)-(
59
) may be expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}>0

\displaystyle\iff\lambda>\lambda_{\textrm{th}},

(61)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}<0

\displaystyle\iff\lambda<\lambda_{\textrm{th}},

(62)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textrm{th}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}).

(63)

However, it seems that in most practical cases, condition
\lambda>\lambda_{\textrm{th}}
will be easily satisfied, especially for large
T
(e.g., for
T=100
, we have
\lambda_{\textrm{th}}=0.55
). Therefore, although
\lambda_{\textrm{th}}
provides a quantitative condition for estimating rankability using only the degree of linearity
\lambda
, this condition is very weak, which is the consequence of using the degenerate Slater spectrum
\{\tilde{a}_{t}\}
.

Assuming that the posterior
PDF
is concentrated around
p=1
, (
42
) holds and is identical to the term under optimization in (
18
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\check{p}\approx\lambda.

(64)

In other words, if (
42
) holds, the degree of linearity,
\lambda
, can be treated as a mode of posterior distribution.

3.5
Inference from multiple observations

Let us assume that we deal with
L
comparisons of different objects; i.e., we observe the matrices
\{{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, which depend on orders
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{l},l=1,\ldots,L
, where
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k}\neq\boldsymbol{\rho}_{l},l\neq k
. In this case, if we want to find the rankings
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{l}
, we deal simply with multiple instances of the
LOP
.

However, if we assume that the observation matrices
{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}
, conditioned on the orders
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{l}
, are generated according to the model (
1
)-(
2
), and that the orders
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{l}
are independent, then the probabilistic approach we propose confers us another advantage because we can use all the matrices to obtain the posterior
PDF
of
p

\displaystyle f(p|\{{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})

\displaystyle\propto\Pr\left\{{\textnormal{{W}}}_{1},\ldots,{\textnormal{{W}}}_{L}|p\right\}\propto\prod_{l=1}^{L}\phi(p|{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}).

(65)

Of course, multiple comparisons of
different
objects may share the same model (
1
)-(
2
). Such an assumption may be justified if the comparisons are done on the same “type” of objects and the results are affected by the same uncertainty. For example, in sport leagues, the comparisons between the teams (made through games) are made according to the same rules. Also, in the sport context, the assumption of independence of orders
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{l}
(between seasons) can be justified if the levels of the teams are equalized through the annual exchange of players.

Since
\phi(p|\{{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})
is a product of functions, finding its mean and/or mode analytically seems difficult, but is done straightforwardly numerically.

If, on the other hand, we use the approximation (
42
), it is easy to see that (
65
) may be expressed as

\displaystyle f(p|\{{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{l=1}^{L}p^{T_{l}-\hat{S}_{l}}(1-p)^{\hat{S}_{l}}

(66)

\displaystyle=p^{L\overline{T}-L\overline{\hat{S}})}(1-p)^{L\overline{\hat{S}}},

(67)

where
\overline{\hat{S}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{S}_{l}
is the average Slater index, and
\overline{T}=\frac{1}{L}T_{l}
is average number of observations. Here,
\hat{S}_{l}
and
T_{l}
are, respectively, the Slater index and the number of observations in
{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}
(that is, we recognize that problems may have different structure); for example, in sports, it may happen that some seasons have games canceled or number of teams increased.

By analogy to (
18
), we conclude that the mode of the joint
PDF
(
65
) is then given by

\displaystyle\check{p}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda_{\textrm{joint}}=1-\frac{\overline{\hat{S}}}{\overline{T}};

(68)

in other words, the ratio of the average Slater index and the average number of observations should be treated as the “degree of linearity” index for the joint estimation. Note that,
\lambda_{\textrm{joint}}
is
not
the same as the average of individual linearity indices
\lambda_{l}=1-\frac{\hat{S}_{l}}{T_{l}}
. The equivalence appears only if all
T_{l}
are equal.

4
Algorithms

In order to evaluate the rankability criteria shown in Sec.
3.3
or Sec.
3.4
, we may need to obtain the spectrum
\{a_{t}\}
. We show how to do it efficiently, by exploiting the structure of the problem.

4.1
Recursive calculation of Slater spectrum

For any subset
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{I}
, let
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}
be the space of permutation of elements in
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, and
C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})
– the consistency index of the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}
, where, taking into account the cardinality of
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, we redefine (
8
) as follows:

\displaystyle C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|}\sum_{n=m+1}^{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|}w_{\rho^{\prime}_{m},\rho^{\prime}_{n}}.

(69)

Let us define a mapping from the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
onto a polynomial in an auxiliary variable
u

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\mapsto u^{C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})},

(70)

and, since the degree of the polynomial in (
70
) gives us the consistency index of the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}}u^{C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})}=\sum_{t=0}^{T}a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},t}u^{t}

(71)

is a polynomial whose coefficients
a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},t}
have meaning of the Slater spectrum and tell us how many orders
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
with consistency index
C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})=t
there are in the set of permutations
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}
. We call
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
the Slater spectrum (in the polynomial domain). In particular, for
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, the coefficients
a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},t}
define the Slater spectrum for the entire set
\mathcal{I}
. Therefore, the idea is to use the relationship between the spectra of the subsets and, recursively, arrive at the spectrum
G(u;\mathcal{I})
.

Let
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e
be a subset of
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, obtained by removing the element
e
from
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
(thus,
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}
has
|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|-1
elements). Then, any order
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}
can be represented as a concatenation of an element
\rho^{\prime}_{1}=e
, with an order
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}=[e,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}]
. Thus, the summation over all the orders
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
, required to calculate (
71
), can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}}u^{C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e}}u^{C([e,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}])}.

(72)

The consistency index of the order
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}=[e,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}]
in (
72
) is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle C([e,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|}\sum_{n=m+1}^{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|}w_{\rho^{\prime}_{m},\rho^{\prime}_{n}}

(73)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|-1}w_{e,\rho^{\prime}_{n}}+\sum_{m=1}^{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|-1}\sum_{n=m+1}^{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|-1}w_{\rho^{\prime\prime}_{m},\rho^{\prime\prime}_{n}}

(74)

\displaystyle=d_{e}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})+C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}),

(75)

where

\displaystyle d_{e}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}w_{e,n}.

(76)

We can now rewrite (
71
) using (
72
) and (
75
)

\displaystyle G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}u^{d_{e}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e}}u^{C(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime})}

(77)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}u^{d_{e}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})}G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e),

(78)

where (
78
) uses the definition of the spectrum from (
71
) that is valid for any subset of
\mathcal{I}
.

To generalize the above approach, let
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}:\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{I},|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|=k\}
denote the family of all distinct subsets of
\mathcal{I}
that have
k
elements.
2
2

2

A family defined in this way is a set, but we use a different name to make a distinction between its elements, which are themselves the sets.
Obviously, there are
|\mathcal{F}_{k}|={{M}\choose{k}}
subsets in a family
\mathcal{F}_{k}
. In particular, there is only one subset with
M
elements – the set
\mathcal{I}
itself, thus
\mathcal{F}_{M}=\{\mathcal{I}\}
. Similarly, there are
M
one-element subsets in
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\{\{1\},\{2\},\ldots,\{M\}\}
.

Applying (
77
), to calculate the spectrum
G(u;\mathcal{I})
we need to know the spectra
G(u;\mathcal{I}\backslash{e})
for all subsets
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}\backslash{e}\in\mathcal{F}_{M-1}
. Next, to calculate the spectrum
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
, we need the spectra
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime})
for all subsets
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash{e}\in\mathcal{F}_{M-2}
, etc. This leads to the recursion described with pseudo-code shown in Algorithm

.

We initialize the algorithm by calculating the spectra for all subsets composed of one element
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
; this is trivial:
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime})\equiv u^{0}=1
and means that for one element, no comparison is possible, so the consistency index is equal to zero.

Next, we apply (
78
) for each
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
for
k=2,3,\ldots,M
, where the final step

Some implementation comments are in order.

1.

The spectra are indexed with sets
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, that is, the ordering of elements in
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
does not matter. For example, the spectrum
G(u;\{1,2\})
is exactly the same as
G(u;\{2,1\})
. Since we do not want to store the same data with different indices, in practice, it is preferable to order the elements in
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, e.g., lexicographically, so that a subset obtained by removing one element
e
, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e
, becomes a valid index to the spectrum
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime})
.

2.

The spectrum
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
can be represented as vectors
\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}=[a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},0},\ldots,a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},T}]
so the multiplication by
u^{d_{e}(\mathcal{I})}
in (
78
) (see line
13
of Algorithm
1
) amounts to increasing the degree of all terms in
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e)
by
d_{e}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
. That is, before being added, all coefficients must be right-shifted as follows:

\displaystyle a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},k}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e,k-d_{e}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})},\hskip 9.24994ptk=0,\ldots,T,

(79)

where, for convenience, we set
a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},k}\equiv 0
for
k<0
.

3.

It is possible to obtain (
76
) recursively, saving calculation. This is done as follows for any
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{k}\}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}

\displaystyle d_{e^{\prime}_{1}}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d_{e^{\prime}_{1}}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e^{\prime}_{2})+w_{e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2}}

(80)

\displaystyle d_{e^{\prime}_{j}}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d_{e^{\prime}_{j}}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e^{\prime}_{1})+w_{e^{\prime}_{j},e^{\prime}_{1}},\hskip 9.24994ptj=2,\ldots,k.

Complexity evaluation

The complexity of the Algorithm
1
is due to three nested loops (a) over
k=2,\ldots,M
(line
8
), (b) over
{{M}\choose{k}}
elements of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
(line
10
), and (c) over
k
elements
e
of
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
(line
12
). In the most inner loop, over
e
, we (i) calculate (
76
), which requires a summation of
1
elements of the matrix
W
(using (
80
)-(
81
)), and (ii) add
k-1
vectors (representing polynomials), each with
T
elements.

The total number of operations may be calculated as follows:

CMPLX

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{M}{{M}\choose{k}}\big[k+T(k-1)\big]

(86)

\displaystyle=O\Big(M^{3}2^{M}\Big),

(87)

where we use the relationship
\sum_{k=0}^{M}{{M}\choose{k}}k=2^{M}M/2
and assume that
T\sim M^{2}
, e.g., if we make
K_{m,n}=K
comparisons for each pair, then
T=KM(M-1)/2
.

For example, with
M=20
, we have the complexity
\textrm{CMPLX}=O(10^{10})
which is within reach of any modern computer, while the explicit enumeration in (
23
) with the complexity of
O(M!)=10^{18}
– is not.

The algorithm implemented in Python is available at
our GitHub repository
.

An interesting feature of the algorithm is that even though we do not explicitly find the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
to the ordering problem, we immediately know how many such solutions there are: this is the coefficient
a_{\mathcal{I},\hat{S}}=a_{\mathcal{I},T-\hat{S}}
, where
\hat{S}
is the optimal Slater index in (
10
). Furthermore, with a minor modification of the above algorithm, and with much lower complexity, we can recover
all
,
a_{\mathcal{I},\hat{S}}
solutions
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
; this is shown below.

4.2
Recovering all rankings
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

Let
q(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}\{C(\boldsymbol{\rho})\}
be the maximal consistency index, i.e., the degree of the spectrum polynomial
G(u;\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{t}\{a_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime},t}>0\}

(88)

which, from (
78
) and (
76
), is calculated as

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\max_{e\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}\{d_{e}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})+q(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash e)\},

(89)

and we are able to keep track of
q(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
when executing the steps of the recursion (
78
), which, at the final step yields
q(\mathcal{I})=T-\hat{S}
.

All optimal rankings may be written as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=[\hat{e}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}}]
for some
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{I}\backslash{\hat{e}}_{1}
, and, from (
75
),
C(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})=d_{\hat{e}_{1}}(\mathcal{I})+C(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, by definition, the consistency index of an optimal ranking is maximal, i.e.,
C(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})=q(\mathcal{I})
which implies that the consistency index of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1}
is also maximal in
\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1}
, i.e.,
C(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\prime})=q(\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1})
.

Therefore, the element
e=\hat{e}_{1}
which maximizes the expression in (
89
) and thus satisfies the following:

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1})=q(\mathcal{I})-d_{\hat{e}_{1}}(\mathcal{I}),\hskip 9.24994pt\hat{e}_{1}\in\mathcal{I},

(90)

is the first element of the optimal ranking
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
. We say that node
\mathcal{I}\backslash{\hat{e}_{1}}
is
degree-compatible
with
\mathcal{I}
. Note that (
90
) may have many solutions
\hat{e}_{1}
, and therefore there may be more than one degree-compatible node
\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1}
.

Conversely, if
e\in\mathcal{I}
does not maximize expression in (
89
) and therefore does not satisfy (
90
), it means that
e
is
not
the first element of the ranking
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
.

Therefore, although the spectrum
G(u;\mathcal{I})
does not give us direct information about the elements of the optimal rankings
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, this information may be retrieved from the edges connecting the node
\mathcal{I}
to its parents
\mathcal{I}\backslash{e}
and allows us to unveil a ranking associated with the node
\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1}}=\{[\hat{e}_{1},\cdot,\ldots,\cdot]\},

(91)

which is “preliminary” in the sense that the elements
\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{M}
are yet unknown as indicated by “
\cdot
” placed at
M-1
corresponding positions.

In fact, we may say that the preliminary ranking associated with
\mathcal{I}
is
\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{I}}=\{[\cdot,\ldots,\cdot]\}
, that is, none of the elements is known. Then, (
91
) places element
\hat{e}_{1}
in the first position, which was not known in
\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{I}}
. There may be many preliminary rankings associated with a node
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, so
\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}
is, in general, a
list
of preliminary rankings.

We gather all degree-compatible nodes
\mathcal{I}\backslash\hat{e}_{1}
in the subfamilies
\mathcal{A}_{M-1}\subset\mathcal{F}_{M-1}
. Next, for each node
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{M-1}
we look for the degree-compatible parent nodes
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash\hat{e}_{2}
, all of which we gather in
\mathcal{A}_{M-2}
. Each of the parent nodes
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}
will “inherit” the list of preliminary rankings from its children, and each preliminary rankings on such list will have
\hat{e}_{2}
placed at the second position:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\textrm{tmp}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash\hat{e}_{2}}

(92)

\displaystyle\rho_{2}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\hat{e}_{2},\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\textrm{tmp}},

(93)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash\hat{e}_{2}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\backslash\hat{e}_{2}}\cup\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\textrm{tmp}},

(94)

where (
92
)-(
94
) take care of the situation when a parent node has many degree-compatible children. If this happens, the parent node inherits all lists of preliminary rankings from its children, sets the corresponding element of each ranking on the list via (
93
), and then merges the modified lists via (
94
).

Complexity

Note that the entire algorithm we describe does not make any reference to the spectrum. It only uses the orders of the polynomials and is truly an aside result obtained due to the particular formulation of the problem.

Removing the calculation of the spectra, it is quite easy to see that the complexity may be evaluated as

CMPLX

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{M}{{M}\choose{k}}k

(95)

\displaystyle=O\Big(M2^{M}\Big),

(96)

and the complexity of the backward recursion in which we recover the solutions is neglected because we only must visit order-compatible nodes from the subfamilies
\mathcal{A}_{k}
, and these are significantly smaller than
\mathcal{F}_{k}
.

Using the method shown in
(
Remage and Thompson 1966
, Sec. 3)
, the complexity of finding only
one
solutions
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is
O(M2^{M})
. Therefore, it is interesting to see that it is possible to find
all
the solutions with the same cost. Of course, we ignore all the cost related to indexing, as also done in
Remage and Thompson 1966
.

\mathcal{I};q(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})

The backward phase is more interesting and starts with the node

is then a suitable indicator of rankability.

On the other hand, using only the Slater index to detect non-rankable
W
seems difficult when
\overline{p}
is small. Indeed, in Fig.
4
b, all cases where
\check{p}=0.5
yield
\lambda\in(0.62,0.7)
while the approximate threshold (
63
), which allows us to assume that
\check{p}>0.5
is given by
\lambda_{\textrm{th}}=0.55
.

It is also worthwhile to note the significant overlap between the solutions obtained for
\overline{p}=0.5
and
\overline{p}=0.6
, which begs a question: should rankability be determined from a particular realization of
W
, or rather, concluded, when possible, from multiple realizations of
{\textnormal{{W}}}_{l}
? We do not have an answer to that conundrum.

Clearly, the issue of finding the suitable threshold to declare the data as non-rankable requires more research but the pragmatic, although conservative (avoiding false positives) solution is to consider data rankable only for sufficiently large
\lambda
, e.g.,
\lambda>0.8
in the examples we have shown.

We terminate the exploration of the synthetic data by investigating the multiplicity of the solutions, i.e., the value of the coefficient
a_{\hat{S}}
. To this end, we reproduce the experiments we run for
L=1000
and show the quartiles (as a box-plot) in Fig.
5
; note the logarithmic scale.

We can easily see that the probability of having a large multiplicity
a_{\hat{S}}
decreases with
\overline{p}
. By correlating this observation with Fig.
4
we may conclude that the intuition of
Anderson et al. 2019
is correct: not only
\hat{S}
, but also
a_{\hat{S}}
are evidence of rankability. This empirical observation clashes with our discussion about the degenerate spectrum (see text after (
39
)) which concluded that
a_{\hat{S}}
is irrelevant. Therefore, it is possible that there are other more informative forms of the Slater spectrum that can be inferred from the knowledge of the pair
(\hat{S},a_{\hat{S}})
. We must leave this question as an open problem.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we addressed the issue of defining the rankability of the data where, essentially, the goal is to make a statement about the reliability of solutions in the
linear ordering problem (LOP)
.

Our approach is probabilistic: we model pairwise comparison observations as Bernoulli variables characterized by a common parameter
p
and find its estimate, and more generally, we obtain its posterior distribution. To this end, we defined the so-called Slater spectrum, which tells us how many orders share a given value of the consistency index; this is done for all possible values of the index. By construction, the Slater spectrum is a sufficient statistic for
p
, while the Slater index, often used in the literature, is not. An efficient algorithm is developed to find the Slater spectrum, which may be easily used to calculate the posterior distribution of the parameter
p
, which, in turn, tells us whether the
LOP
solution can be treated as a meaningful answer to the ordering problem.

To simplify the analysis, we deal with the posterior mean and mode, and it allows us to link to the Slater index and a related parameter - the degree of linearity,
\lambda
, also often used in the literature. It turns out that
\lambda
may be treated as the mode of the posterior distribution of
p
if the latter is concentrated in the vicinity of
p=1
(which would be also an appropriate definition of rankability). In simple terms, and without surprise, sufficiently large
\lambda
is a good indicator of rankability; our analysis endows it with a probabilistic interpretation.

We show numerical examples using synthetic and real-world data that illustrate our theoretical findings.

As an additional result, thanks to the new formulation of the estimation problem, we can find
all
optimal solutions of the
LOP
. When ordering
M
objects, this is done with the complexity
O(M2^{M})
which, notably, is the same maximum complexity reported in
Remage and Thompson 1966
to find only one solution. The algorithms we develop and use are implemented in Python and made available at
our GitHub repository
.

Since our work is methodological in nature and aims at providing theoretical tools to analyze the
LOP
, it does not immediately address all the practical/implementation issues. Indeed, the complexity of finding the Slater spectrum grows very quickly with
M
and becomes impractical for
M>30
, while many practical problems deal with size
M>100

(
Anderson et al. 2019
)
,
(
Ceberio et al. 2015
)
.

Although we made some steps to link our framework to the practical setup (e.g., by reinterpreting, in probabilistic terms, the degree of linearity, which can be calculated for large problems), the possibility of finding the Slater spectrum for large
M
(exactly or approximately), or, at least, to calculate some statistics of
p
remains the main challenge for wider applicability of the proposed methods.
