Introduction

Hell and Zhu introduced the adaptable chromatic number
[
19
]
. This variant on the chromatic number was also introduced independently (under different names) by Erdös and Gyárfás
[
7
]
, Cochand and Duchet
[
3
]
, and Archer
[
1
]
.

The basic idea is this: we are given an edge labelling of a graph
G
and we assign colours to the vertices of
G
such that there is no edge
e=uv
where
u
,
v
, and
e
are all the same colour. Such a colouring is called an
adaptable colouring
of
G
. I.e. in conventional graph colouring each edge forbids its endpoints from both receiving the same colour, whereas in adaptable colouring each edge forbids its endpoints from both receiving a particular colour, namely its edge label. Note that an adaptable colouring may be improper in the sense that two adjacent vertices may both receive the colour red, so long as the edge connecting them is not also labelled with the colour red.

We can take this a step further by including multiple edges between adjacent vertices, to further constrain the colours assigned to them. In fact, a conventional colouring problem with
k
colours can be reduced to an adaptable colouring problem by passing to the corresponding multigraph with multiplicity
k
, and such that each edge between two endpoints is given a unique colour from
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
. Therefore it is natural—and in fact important—to allow multiple edges in our graphs.

For a formal definition: let
G=(V,E)
be a multigraph. Let
\tau:E\to\mathbb{N}
be an edge labelling of
G
, we say that a vertex colouring,
\sigma:V\to\mathbb{N}
, of
G
is a
proper adaptable colouring
if there is no edge
e=uv
such that
\sigma(u)=\sigma(v)=\tau(e)
. The
adaptable chromatic number
of
G
, denoted
\chi_{a}(G)
, is the minimum integer
k
such that every edge labelling of
G
using labels from
[k]
permits a proper adaptable colouring of
G
from
[k]
. Since every proper vertex colouring of
G
is a proper adaptable colouring of
G
for any edge labelling, we can see that
\chi_{a}(G)\leq\chi(G)
, where
\chi(G)
is the usual
chromatic number
of
G
. Applications of this problem include job scheduling
[
21
]
, matrix and list partitions of graphs
[
12
,
11
]
, as well as full constraint satisfaction problems
[
10
]
.

Here we present a result for adaptable list colouring, which is defined naturally. Suppose each
v\in G
is assigned a list
L(v)\subset\mathbb{N}
of candidate colours. Then a
proper adaptable list colouring
of
G
is a proper adaptable colouring where each vertex
v
is assigned a colour from
L(v)
. The
adaptable choosability
of
G
, denoted
\mathrm{ch}_{a}(G)
, is the smallest integer
k
such that any edge labelling of
G
and any assignment of lists of size
k
to the vertices of
G
permits a proper adaptable list colouring of
G
. If
\mathrm{ch}_{a}(G)=k
, then assigning
L(v)=[k]
for each
v\in G
permits a proper adaptable colouring, hence
\chi_{a}(G)\leq\mathrm{ch}_{a}(G)
. Moreover, since any proper vertex colouring of
G
from lists of size
k
permits a proper adaptable colouring of
G
for any edge labelling, we have
\mathrm{ch}_{a}(G)\leq\mathrm{ch}(G)
, where
\mathrm{ch}(G)
is the usual
choosability
of
G
. Adaptable colouring and adaptable list colouring have been studied in
[
18
,
25
,
19
,
27
,
16
,
9
]
.

In the case of conventional graph colouring, much attention has been given to colouring graphs of high girth
[
4
,
15
,
17
]
, as typically fewer colours are required. We will see that the same phenomenon can be observed with adaptable list colouring.

Two results in particular are of interest to us. The first is by Kim
[
20
]
who showed that if
\Delta
is the maximum degree of a simple graph
G
with girth at least 5 (i.e. no cycles of length 4 or less), then
\mathrm{ch}(G)\leq(1+o(1))\frac{\Delta}{\ln\Delta}
. The second is by Molloy and Thron
[
26
]
, who showed that for a general multigraph
G
with maximum degree
\Delta
,
\mathrm{ch_{a}}(G)\leq(1+o(1))\sqrt{\Delta}
. In a sense, we combine these results by presenting an upper bound on the adaptable choosability, which we show for essentially the same class of graphs considered by Kim.

Recall that it is important to allow multiple edges in the graphs we consider. So if we would like to study adaptable colouring in a high girth setting, we must define a notion of high girth for multigraphs. The most natural course of action is to permit 2-cycles, i.e. multiple edges, while disallowing other short cycles in our graphs. In particular we consider multigraphs with no cycles of length 3 or 4, which is the most natural analogue to Kim’s setting. We get the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a multigraph with no cycles of length 3 or 4 and maximum degree
\Delta
, then
\mathrm{ch}_{a}(G)\leq(2\sqrt{2}+o(1))\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
.

We comment briefly on the tightness of Theorem
1.1
. For any
g
and
\Delta\geq 3
, random
\Delta
-regular graphs have girth at least
g
and satisfy
\chi(G)\geq(\frac{1}{2}+o(1))\frac{\Delta}{\ln\Delta}
with high probability
[
14
]
. Moreover it is known that for any graph
G
,
\chi_{a}(G)\geq(1+o(1))\sqrt{\chi(G)}
by
[
22
]
. Combining these two facts implies the existence of a graph
G
with maximum degree
\Delta
and no 3 or 4-cycles, satisfying
\chi_{a}(G)\geq(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+o(1))\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
. And since
\mathrm{ch}_{a}(G)\geq\chi_{a}(G)
, this implies the bound in Theorem
1.1
is within a factor of 4 of the best possible (note that Kim’s result is within a factor of 2 of the best possible, see remark
3.5
to understand why this changes to a 4 in our case).

We achieve Theorem
1.1
by showing a more general result for a problem called conflict colouring. In adaptable colouring, we are given an edge labelling
\tau
so that each edge
e=uv
constrains the colours assigned to
u
and
v
symmetrically. I.e. the edge colour
c=\tau(e)
acts as a constraint preventing
u
and
v
from both receiving the same colour
c
. In conflict colouring we allow for the possibility of an asymmetric constraint, meaning that instead of the edge label being a single colour, it can now be an ordered pair of colours
(c,c^{\prime})
which is meant to prevent
u
from receiving
c
and
v
from receiving
c^{\prime}
simultaneously. The problem was introduced independently by Dvořák and Postle
[
6
]
and Fraigniaud, Heinrich and Kosowski
[
13
]
.

We should point out that in
[
6
,
13
]
, the authors present slightly different (but equivalent) representations of what we call the conflict colouring problem. For instance, in
[
6
]
the authors consider
correspondence colouring
. In this variant we are given a simple graph where each edge
e=uv
corresponds to a partial matching,
M_{e}
, between the candidate colours
L(u)
and
L(v)
. A pair
f\in M_{e}
forbids
u
and
v
from both receiving the colours corresponding to the entries of
f
. This is of course the same problem we consider here, the only important difference being that in our case the pairs in
M_{e}
are instead included in our graph as parallel edges between
u
and
v
, and therefore they contribute to the maximum degree. Our setting most closely resembles that of Dvořák, Esperet, Kang and Ozeki
[
5
]
, and we use the same notion of conflict degree (we define this in a moment) seen in
[
13
]
.

For a multigraph
G=(V,E)
, Dvořák et al.
[
5
]
defined the
conflict choosability
of
G
, denoted
\mathrm{ch}_{\nleftrightarrow}(G)
, as the smallest integer
k
such that any edge labelling
\tau:E\to\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and any assignment of lists
L(v)\subset\mathbb{N}
to every
v\in G
, each of size
k
, permits a vertex colouring
\sigma:V\to L(v)
such that there is no edge
e=uv\in G
where
\tau(e)=(\sigma(u),\sigma(v))
. We call
\sigma
a
proper conflict colouring
.

The reader may notice some ambiguity here, since for a label
\tau(e)=(c,c^{\prime})
it is not clear which of the endpoints of
e
should be forbidden from receiving
c
and which should be forbidden from receiving
c^{\prime}
. We will define some convenient notation to overcome this (see the comment at the beginning of section 3). For now, what we mean will be made clear by the context.

Observe that for any edge labelling and list assignments of size
k
, we can rename the colours in
L(v)
as well as the colours assigned by
\tau
accordingly while preserving the existence of a proper conflict colouring. So in fact we may assume
L(v)=[k]
for each
v\in G
. I.e. conflict colouring and conflict list colouring are one and the same.

It turns out that it is not possible to obtain the same bound for conflict choosability as in Theorem
1.1
, which will be made clear by the following example.

Example 1.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a natural number and
L
be a set of colours with size
\ell\leq\sqrt{\Delta}
. Consider the multigraph
G
with only two vertices,
u
and
v
, each assigned the same set
L
of colours and connected by
\ell^{2}
edges. Note the maximum degree of
G
is
\ell^{2}\leq\Delta
, so if needed we can increase the maximum degree to be exactly
\Delta
by adding dummy vertices. Define
\tau
such that each edge between
u
and
v
is given a unique label from one of the
\ell^{2}
possible pairs of colours, and observe that
G
does not have a proper conflict colouring. I.e.
G
is a multigraph with no cycles of length 3 or 4, but
\mathrm{ch}_{\nleftrightarrow}(G)>\sqrt{\Delta}
.

To investigate what went wrong in the previous example we need to first define a parameter called the conflict degree.

Definition 1.3
.

Given a multigraph
G=(V,E)
, an edge labelling
\tau:E\to\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, a colour
c
, and a pair of vertices
u
and
v
, define
d(\tau,c,u,v)
to be the number of edges
e=uv\in G
such that
\tau(e)=(c,c^{\prime})
for some colour
c^{\prime}
. Define the
conflict degree
of
\tau
, denoted
D(\tau)
, to be the maximum of
d(\tau,c,u,v)
over all colours
c
and pairs
u,v
.

Notice that in Example
1.2
we considered an edge labelling
\tau
with
D(\tau)=\ell
, the size of our lists. In other words if we allow the conflict degree to become too high then we have no hope of finding a proper conflict colouring. In the specific case where we consider lists of size
\ell=(2\sqrt{2}+o(1))\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
, the same problem occurs if
D(\tau)
is as high as
\ell^{15/16}
, which will be made clear by Example
1.7
. Note that Example
1.7
is more involved, and so we include the details at the end of the section. We simply summarize the main point here.

Fact 1.4
.

For any constant
\alpha\geq 1
, there is a multigraph
G
with maximum degree
\Delta
, no cycles of length 3 or 4, an assignment of lists each with size
\ell=\alpha\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
, an edge labelling
\tau
with
D(\tau)=\ell^{f(\alpha)}
, and such that
G
does not have a proper conflict colouring, where
f(\alpha)
is non-negative for
\alpha\geq 1
, non-decreasing, and tending to 1 as
\alpha
approaches infinity. In particular, when
\alpha=2\sqrt{2}+o(1)
we have
f(\alpha)=\frac{15}{16}
.

The function
f
will be defined precisely in Example
1.7
.

Clearly, Fact
1.4
requires us to restrict the types of edge labellings we consider. Moving forward, we restrict the type of edge labelling that is allowed on our graph by imposing an upper bound on the conflict degree. Such an approach has been taken in
[
13
]
. Note that since we consider lists of size
\ell=(2\sqrt{2}+o(1))\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
, Fact
1.4
means we must restrict the conflict degree to be at most
\ell^{15/16}\approx\Delta^{15/32}
. Unfortunately we run into problems even when allowing the conflict degree to be this large. Instead we aim for something closer to
\Delta^{1/4}
, we make no attempt to determine if this is optimal. We arrive at the following generalization of Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.5
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\Delta_{0}
such that if
G
is a multigraph with maximum degree
\Delta>\Delta_{0}
, no cycles of length 3 or 4, an assignment of lists
L(v)
for each vertex
v
such that
|L(v)|\geq(2\sqrt{2}+\epsilon)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
, and an edge labelling
\tau
with
D(\tau)\leq\Delta^{\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+5)^{2}}
, then there exists a proper conflict colouring of
G
from these lists.

The important point here is that there is a tradeoff between the bound on our conflict degree and our list sizes. We can make our list sizes arbitrarily close to
2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
and the conflict degree arbitrarily close to
\Delta^{1/4}
, but not at the same time.

It is easy to see that Theorem
1.5
implies Theorem
1.1
. The key observation is that an instance of an adaptable colouring problem is exactly an instance of a conflict colouring problem with conflict degree 1.

Notice that since Theorem
1.1
is implied by Theorem
1.5
, this means the discussion following the statement of Theorem
1.1
applies to Theorem
1.5
as well. That is, the choice of list sizes in
1.5
is within a factor of 4 of the best possible, even when
D(\tau)\leq 1
.

As mentioned, we cannot quite allow our conflict degree to be as large as
\Delta^{1/4}
, but by allowing the list size to be large we can come close. A direct result of Theorem
1.5
is the following more visually appealing statement, which can be seen by taking
\epsilon=45
.

Corollary 1.6
.

There exists
\Delta_{0}
such that if
G
is a multigraph with maximum degree
\Delta>\Delta_{0}
, no cycles of length 3 or 4, an assignment of lists
L(v)
for each vertex
v
such that
|L(v)|\geq 50\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
, and an edge labelling
\tau
with
D(\tau)\leq\Delta^{1/5}
, then there exists a proper conflict colouring of
G
from these lists.

Actually, if one is more careful with the analysis (in particular the proof of Lemma
3.13
), then Theorem
1.5
can be strengthened slightly and as a result the “50” in Corollary
1.6
can be improved to a “10”. We opt for the weaker statement here to keep the proof cleaner.

In the following sections we give a proof of Theorem
1.5
by colouring
G
using a semi-random procedure. Similar procedures have been used in many places. The procedures in
[
29
,
26
]
are particularly close to the one presented here. Our analysis is heavily based on Kim’s result
[
20
]
, as presented in chapter 12 of
[
23
]
. One of the important differences is Modification
1
, where we remove certain “bad” colours before starting our procedure. This idea has been used in
[
26
]
, but in our case the situation is slightly more delicate, and so we take greater care with how many colours we choose to remove (see remark
3.5
). Another key difference is a new parameter (see
t_{i}(v,u,c)
defined in section
3

[
23

Example 1.7
.

For any
\Delta

\Delta

G
be such a graph.
By Proposition 6 of
[
5

, such a graph satisfies
\mathrm{ch}_{\nleftrightarrow}(G)>\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}=\ell
. This means there is at least one edge labelling of
G
from
[\ell]
which does not permit a proper conflict colouring of the vertices from
[\ell]
. Let
\tau
be this edge labelling (to be clear, each label assigned by
\tau
is a pair of colours). Note that since
G
is simple,
D(\tau)=1
.

Now consider the following graph
G^{\prime}
, constructed by replacing each edge in
G
by
\ell^{\prime}=\ell^{2}

C=[\ell^{\prime}]
of
\ell^{\prime}

\sqrt{\ell^{\prime}}=\ell

\ell
. Let
C_{i}
be the

i,j

P_{ij}=\{(c_{1},c_{2}):c_{1}\in C_{i},c_{2}\in C_{j}\}
. Note that
|P_{ij}|=\ell^{2}
. Now we define an edge labelling
\tau^{\prime}

\tau(e)=(i,j)
, assign a unique pair from
P_{ij}
to each of the corresponding
\ell^{2}

.

Now we show that
\tau^{\prime}
does not permit a proper conflict colouring on

. Suppose to the contrary that
\tau^{\prime}

\sigma^{\prime}
. We define a vertex colouring on

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{\ln\Delta^{\prime}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\ln\Delta\ln\Delta^{\prime}}}

In fact, the same construction can be applied several times. Let

\displaystyle\ell_{0}

\displaystyle=\ell,

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=\Delta,

\displaystyle D(\tau_{0})

\displaystyle=D(\tau).

And by construction we have the following recurrences for

\displaystyle\ell_{i}

\displaystyle=\ell_{i-1}^{2},

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{i-1}\times\ell_{i-1}^{2},

\displaystyle D(\tau_{i})

\displaystyle=D(\tau_{i-1})\times\ell_{i-1}.

\Delta

Probabilistic Tools

Here we describe three probabilistic tools which will be useful in proving our main theorem. The first is the Lovász Local Lemma which is used several times in the proof. There are several versions of the lemma, the version we present here is not the most powerful but nevertheless it is sufficient for our purposes. The original proof can be found in
[
8
]
.

The Lovász Local Lemma
.

Consider a set
\mathcal{E}
of “bad” events in a probability space, such that for each
A\in\mathcal{E}
:

a)

\mathbf{Pr}(A)\leq p<1
, and

b)

A
is mutually independent of a set of all but at most
d
of the other events.

If
4pd\leq 1
then with positive probability, none of the events in
\mathcal{E}
occur.

We use two concentration bounds, the first of which is known as the Chernoff Bound. The original statement can be found in
[
2
]
, we use the version which can be found in chapter 5 of
[
23
]
.

The Chernoff Bound
.

For any
t\geq 0
:

\mathbf{Pr}(|BIN(n,p)-np|>t)\leq 2e^{-((1+\frac{t}{np})\ln(1+\frac{t}{np})-\frac{t}{np})np}

Our second concentration bound is Talagrand’s Inequality. The original statement can be found in
[
30
]
but once again we use the weaker version which appears in
[
24
]
.

Talagrand’s Inequality
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable determined by the independent trials
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{n}
. Suppose that for every set of possible outcomes of the trials, we have:

a)

changing the outcome of any one trial can affect

by at most

, and

b)

for each
s>0
, if
X\geq s
, then there is a set of at most
rs
trials whose outcomes certify that

(we make this precise below).

Then for any
t\geq 0
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}(|X-\mathbf{E}(X)|>t+20q\sqrt{r\mathbf{E}(X)}+64q^{2}r)\leq 4e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{8q^{2}r(\mathbf{E}(X)+t)}}

Let us be more precise with condition (
b
) above. Suppose there is a set
I\subseteq[n]
, and outcomes
\{t_{i}:i\in I\}
such that
X\geq s
whenever
T_{i}=t_{i}
for each
i\in I
. We say that the set of outcomes
\{t_{i}:i\in I\}
for trials
\{T_{i}:i\in I\}

certify
that
X\geq s
.

Proof of Main Theorem

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Let
G=(V,E)
be a multigraph with no cycles of length 3 or 4 and maximum degree
\Delta
. Let
\tau:E\to\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
be an edge labelling on
G
with
D(\tau)\leq\Delta^{\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+5)^{2}}
. Instead of specifying a value for
\Delta_{0}
, throughout the proof we will use the fact that
\Delta
is a sufficiently large constant.

A brief comment on terminology and notation.

For convenience, if
u
and
v

\mathcal{T}(u,v)
as the set of all labels between
u
and
v
. That is,
\mathcal{T}(u,v)=\{\tau(e):e\textit{ is an edge between u and v}\}
. By convention, all pairs of colours in
\mathcal{T}(u,v)
are ordered so that if
(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(u,v)
, then
c
corresponds to the colour disallowed for
u
while
c^{\prime}
corresponds to the colour disallowed for
v
. We write
\mathcal{T}(v,u)
to indicate that the pairs are ordered the opposite way.

Also recall that each label on an edge
e
constrains the colours assigned to the endpoints of
e
. For this reason it is useful to think of a label on an edge as a constraint, and so throughout the proof we use the terms “label” and “constraint” interchangeably. We also speak of the endpoints
u,v
of a constraint
(c,c^{\prime})
, and by this we simply mean that
(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(u,v)
. Similarly for neighbouring vertices
u,v
we write that
u
and
v
are constrained by
(c,c^{\prime})
to mean that
(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(u,v)
. This terminology will simplify our discussion significantly.

Consider the following semi-random procedure for colouring
G
. As mentioned, during the procedure we maintain a list
L(v)
for each vertex
v
, which represents the set of colours available to
v
.

Wasteful Colouring Procedure (
i^{th}
iteration)

1.

Truncate the lists so they all have size
L_{i-1}
(to be defined later)

2.

For each uncoloured vertex
v
, activate
v
with probability
\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}
(
K
is a constant which will be defined precisely later).

3.

For each activated vertex
v
, assign
v
a colour uniformly at random from
L(v)
.

4.

For each activated vertex
v
: If
v
is assigned the colour
c
, for each uncoloured neighbour
u
of
v
and each colour
c^{\prime}
such that
(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(v,u)
, remove
c^{\prime}
from
L(u)
.

5.

For each pair of adjacent vertices
v
and
u
which receive colours
c
and
c^{\prime}
respectively, uncolour both
v
and
u
if
(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(v,u)
.

6.

Conduct an “equalizing” coin flip (to be defined later) for each vertex
v
and colour
c\in L(v)
, removing
c
from
L(v)
if it loses the coin flip.

Now consider the following parameters which will allow us to analyze the procedure. Each parameter refers to the value at the end of iteration
i
, for
i>0
. For
i=0
, each parameter refers to the value before beginning the procedure.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L_{i}(v)&=v^{\prime}\text{s list of colours}\\
\ell_{i}(v)&=|L_{i}(v)|\\
\hfil\displaystyle\begin{split}N_{i}(v,u,c)&=\begin{cases}\{(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(v,u):c^{\prime}\in L_{i}(u)\}&\text{if }u\text{ is an uncoloured neighbour of }v\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{split}\\
N_{i}(v,c)&=\bigsqcup_{u\in N(v)}N_{i}(v,u,c)\\
t_{i}(v,u,c)&=|N_{i}(v,u,c)|\\
t_{i}(v,c)&=|N_{i}(v,c)|=\sum_{u\in N(v)}t_{i}(v,u,c)\end{aligned}

In the definition of
N_{i}(v,c)
, the symbol
\bigsqcup
denotes the disjoint union. Note that
v
could have the constraint (red, red) between two different neighbours. But these two constraints are different because they have different endpoints, so we take the disjoint union to include both of them.

Observation 1
.

For each
v
, neighbour
u\in N(v)
, and
c\in L(v)
, we initially have
t_{0}(v,u,c)\leq D(\tau)
. Since this parameter can only decrease, we have
t_{i}(v,u,c)\leq D(\tau)
for every relevant
i
. Because of our restriction on the conflict degree this also means
t_{i}(v,u,c)\leq\Delta^{\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+5)^{2}}
.

Throughout the proof, many of our results rely on the list sizes being sufficiently large. More specifically, our lemma statements will specify that
L_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
(as we will see, this requirement is fulfilled by Lemma
3.13
).

With list sizes this large, we have:

\displaystyle t_{i}(v,u,c)\leq L_{i}^{1/2-\beta},\text{ where }\beta=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\epsilon+4}{\epsilon+5}\bigg)^{2}.

(2)

We will also be concerned with the value
T_{i}
(to be defined later), which we also require to be sufficiently large. If
T_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
, then again we have:

\displaystyle t_{i}(v,u,c)\leq T_{i}^{1/2-\beta},

(3)

for the same value of
\beta
. The reader should not dwell on the precise value of
\beta
, the only important detail is that
\beta
is in the interval
(0,\frac{1}{2})
.

For our procedure to work we require that certain parameters stay reasonably close to their expected values (i.e. we require our parameters to be strongly concentrated). It turns out that the parameter
t_{i}(v,c)
is not strongly concentrated as it can be affected by a large amount when
c
is assigned to
v
in iteration
i
(in particular it drops to 0). In fact, assigning colours other than
c
to
v
could have the same effect. For this reason, the following closely related parameters will be considered at the end of iteration
i+1
(the purpose of these parameters is to simply ignore the effect of assigning a colour to
v
):

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hfil\displaystyle\begin{split}t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,u,c)=&\text{ }0\text{, if }u\text{ retained a colour during iteration }i+1,\\
&\text{ otherwise the number of labels }(c,c^{\prime})\text{ counted by }t_{i}(v,u,c)\text{ such that in iteration }i+1\text{:}\\
&\qquad\bullet\quad c^{\prime}\text{ was not removed from }u\text{'s list because of a conflict with a neighbour }w\neq v\\
&\qquad\bullet\quad c^{\prime}\text{ was not removed from }u\text{'s list because of an equalizing coin flip}\end{split}\\
t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c)=&\sum_{u\in N(v)}t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,u,c)\end{aligned}

Now it turns out that the parameter
t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c)
is indeed concentrated, as we will show in Lemma
3.10
. Notice that
t_{i+1}(v,c)\leq t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c)
. As we will see, we only need an upper bound on
t_{i+1}(v,c)
. So as long as we have a sufficiently strong bound on
t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c)
we should be able to achieve this.

Now, our goal is to perform several iterations of the procedure so that eventually much of the graph is coloured, and colouring the remaining graph becomes easy. Intuitively, by the end of the procedure we would like that for each vertex
v
, the parameter
\ell_{i}(v)
be large while for each colour
c
in
v
’s list,
t_{i}(v,c)
should be quite small. This way, we have several options when choosing a colour for
v
, and each is unlikely to conflict with any neighbour. This will improve our chances of finding a proper conflict colouring. The next lemma makes this precise.

This result was introduced by Reed
[
28
]
, and has since been seen in modified form in various places (see Proposition 2.1 of
[
29
]
, Lemma 3.1 of
[
26
]
). Likewise, we present a modified version which is suited for our purposes; the proof is nearly identical to the original.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a multigraph with edge labelling
\tau:E\to\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and a list of colours
L(v)
for each vertex
v\in G
. Suppose for each vertex
v\in G
,
|L(v)|\geq\ell
. Also suppose that for each vertex
v
, and each colour
c\in L(v)
, there are at most
\ell/8
edges
e=vu
such that
\tau(e)=(c,c^{\prime})
where
c^{\prime}\in L(u)
. Then there is a proper conflict colouring of
G
from these lists.

Proof.

Arbitrarily truncate the lists to be exactly of size
\ell
, and assign each vertex a colour uniformly at random from its list. Let
\sigma
be the resulting vertex colouring on
G
.

For an edge
e
with endpoints
v
and
u
, let
\tau(e)=(c,c^{\prime})
. We consider the event
A_{e}
where
\sigma(v)=c
and
\sigma(u)=c^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of all such events. If
c\notin L(v)
or
c^{\prime}\notin L(u)
, then
\mathbf{Pr}(A_{e})=0
, and this is mutually independent of any set of events. Otherwise,
\mathbf{Pr}(A_{e})=1/\ell^{2}
.

Consider the following sets of events:

D_{v}=\{A_{f}:f=vv^{\prime}\neq e,(c_{1},c_{2})=\tau(f)\textit{ and }c_{2}\in L(v^{\prime})\}

D_{u}=\{A_{f}:f=uu^{\prime}\neq e,(c_{1},c_{2})=\tau(f)\textit{ and }c_{2}\in L(u^{\prime})\}

(note that these sets could contain events indexed by edges parallel to
e
). Observe that
A_{e}
is mutually independent of all events in
\mathcal{E}-D_{v}-D_{u}
by the Mutual Independence Principle (see chapter 4 of
[
23
]
).

By assumption, for each colour
c_{1}\in L(v)
, there could be at most
\ell/8
edges
e=vv^{\prime}
such that
\tau(e)=(c_{1},c_{2})
and
c_{2}\in L(v^{\prime})
. Hence, summing over all colours in
v
’s list gives a bound of
\ell^{2}/8
on the size of
D_{v}
, and similarly for
D_{u}
. So each bad event occurs with probability at most
p=1/\ell^{2}
and is mutually independent of all but at most
d=\ell^{2}/4
other events. Therefore,
4pd\leq 4(1/\ell^{2})(\ell^{2}/4)\leq 1
. So we can apply the Lovász Local Lemma to obtain a colouring
\sigma
such that there is no edge
e
with endpoints
v
and
u
and
\tau(e)=(c,c^{\prime})
where
\sigma(v)=c
and
\sigma(u)=c^{\prime}
, i.e.
\sigma
is a proper conflict colouring.
∎

The previous lemma permits us to focus on the parameters
\ell_{i}(v)
and
t_{i}(v,c)
, but instead of keeping track of the values
\ell_{i}(v)
and
t_{i}(v,c)
for each
v,c
, we focus on their extreme values. We recursively define appropriate
L_{i}
and
T_{i}
which we would like to serve as a lower bound for
\ell_{i}(v)
and an upper bound for
t_{i}(v,c)
, respectively. Moreover, the value
t_{i}(v,u,c)
will also be of interest to us, as if this value is too large then
\ell_{i}(v)
and
t_{i}(v,c)
will not be sufficiently concentrated.

More precisely, we will show that with positive probability the following property holds at the end of iteration
i
:

Property P(i):
For each uncoloured vertex
v
, uncoloured neighbour
u
of
v
, and each colour
c\in L_{i}(v)
,

\ell_{i}(v)\geq L_{i}

t_{i}(v,c)\leq T_{i}

So if for some iteration,
i
, property
P(i)
holds and
T_{i}\leq\frac{1}{8}L_{i}
then the colouring can be completed by Lemma
3.1
.

As mentioned in step 6 of the procedure, if
c
is in
v
’s list near the end of an iteration (just before step 6), we remove
c
with some probability. We do this in such a way that forces the probability of a vertex retaining a given colour during the entirety of the iteration to be the same across vertices and colours. Now we define
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(v,c)
to be the probability that
c
stays in
v
’s list by the beginning of step 6 of iteration
i+1
, conditional on the event that
c
is in
v
’s list after step 1 of iteration
i+1
. For a neighbour
u
of
v
, there are
t_{i}(v,u,c)
colours in
u
’s list which would cause
c
to be removed from
v
’s list if any one of them is assigned to
u
. Since the size of
u
’s list is precisely
L_{i}
by step 3 in the procedure, and
u
is activated with probability
K/\ln\Delta
, we arrive at the following expression:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(v,c)=\prod_{u\in N(v)}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\times\frac{t_{i}(v,u,c)}{L_{i}}\Big)

(4)

Now we define the following value which we claim is a lower bound on
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(v,c)
and will be easier to work with.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{i}:=\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{T_{i}}

(5)

Claim 3.2
.

If
P(i)
holds,
T_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{i}
, and if
T_{i},L_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
, then
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(v,c)\geq\mathrm{Keep}_{i}
for
\Delta
sufficiently large.

Proof.

It suffices to show the following for
\Delta
sufficiently large:

\displaystyle\Big(1-\frac{Kt_{i}(v,u,c)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)\geq\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{t_{i}(v,u,c)}

(6)

As then we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(v,c)

\displaystyle=\prod_{u\in N(v)}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\times\frac{t_{i}(v,u,c)}{L_{i}}\Big)

by (
4
)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{u\in N(v)}\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{t_{i}(v,u,c)}

by (
6
)

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{\sum_{u\in N(v)}t_{i}(v,u,c)}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{t_{i}(v,c)}

\displaystyle\geq\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{T_{i}}

by property
P(i)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Keep}_{i}

We can show (
6
) by considering two well-known facts:

\displaystyle e^{-x}

\displaystyle\leq 1-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}

for all non-negative
x

(7)

\displaystyle e^{-x}

\displaystyle\geq 1-x

for all
x

(8)

This comes from the fact that for Taylor polynomials of
e^{-x}
with even degree, the remainder is non-positive for all positive
x
. Similarly, the Taylor polynomials of
e^{-x}
with odd degree have a remainder that is non-negative for all
x
.

Now consider

x=\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)t_{i}(v,u,c)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}

and note that by (
2
) and since
L_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
by assumption,
x
tends to
0
as
\Delta
tends to infinity. So we have for
\Delta
sufficiently large:

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)t_{i}(v,u,c)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 1-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}

by (
7
)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{x}{1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30}

since
x
dominates over
\frac{x^{2}}{2}
for large
\Delta

\displaystyle=1-\frac{Kt_{i}(v,u,c)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}

(9)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)t_{i}(v,u,c)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\bigg(-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\bigg)^{t_{i}(v,u,c)}

\displaystyle\geq\bigg(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\bigg)^{t_{i}(v,u,c)}

by (
8
)

which proves the claim.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

We remark that Claim
3.2
remains true even if we replace the expression
\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}
by any positive constant. Although this expression may seem bizarre, we choose it because we require an expression that is quite small for all values of
\epsilon
, even values which are very large.

Equalizing coin flips.

We use Claim
3.2
to define our equalizing coin flips. Define the following probability:

\mathrm{Eq}_{i}(v,c):=1-\mathrm{Keep}_{i}/\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(v,c).

We perform the equalizing coin flip described in step 6 of the procedure by removing any colour
c
from
v
’s list with probability
\mathrm{Eq}_{i}(v,c)
. This way, we ensure that the probability of
c
remaining in
v
’s list by the end of iteration
i+1
is precisely
(1-\mathrm{Eq}_{i}(v,c))\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(v,c)=\mathrm{Keep}_{i}
.

Now initially we have that for each
v
,
\ell_{0}(v)=(2\sqrt{2}+\epsilon)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
. However, for a colour
c
in
v
’s list,
t_{0}(v,c)
could be as high as
\Delta
. Recall that we would like
t_{0}(v,c)
to be quite small, and it turns out that this is not good enough for our purposes, so we remove all colours
c
from
v
’s list for which
t_{0}(v,c)
exceeds some threshold (a similar technique has been used in
[
26
]
). The following claim makes this precise.

Claim 3.4
.

For each vertex
v
, the number of colours
c
for which
t_{0}(v,c)>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\sqrt{\Delta\ln\Delta}
is at most
(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that strictly more than
(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
colours
c
achieve a value of
t_{0}(v,c)>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\sqrt{\Delta\ln\Delta}
. Then the number of edges incident to
v
is is strictly more than
(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\sqrt{\Delta\ln\Delta}=\Delta
, a contradiction.
∎

The previous claim allows us to modify our graph by removing “bad” colours before starting the procedure. In the worst case we are left with at least half of the colours we originally started with.

Modification 1
.

For each vertex
v
and colour
c
in
v
’s list, remove
c
from
v
’s list if
t_{0}(v,c)>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\sqrt{\Delta\ln\Delta}
.

Remark 3.5
.

Eliminating half of
v
’s colours effectively means that we must start with twice the number of colours we really need. In other words, if such “bad” colours posed no problem, and hence there was no need to remove them, we could get away with a leading constant of
\sqrt{2}+\epsilon
in Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.5
. The reader may wonder if imposing a larger threshold in Modification
1
(thus removing less colours) gives a smaller leading constant. Unfortunately, there is an upper limit to what threshold can be used as we must ensure
t_{0}(v,c)
is not too large. Conversely, if the threshold is too relaxed we end up removing too many colours. It turns out that the threshold proposed is optimal in the sense that it strikes the right balance between these two extremes.

Finally, we are ready to give recursive definitions for
L_{i}
and
T_{i}
. Keep in mind that in the following definitions,
\beta
is the constant specified by (
2
). Initially, by Modification
1
and Claim
3.4
we have the following:

L_{0}=(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta},\ \ \ \ \ T_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\sqrt{\Delta\ln\Delta},

and we recursively define:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L_{i+1}&=L_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\\
\\
T_{i+1}&=T_{i}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\Big)\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\end{aligned}

We also give recursive definitions for
L^{\prime}_{i},T^{\prime}_{i}
, which are closely related to
L_{i}
and
T_{i}
. Initially we have the following:

L^{\prime}_{0}=L_{0},\ \ \ \ \ T^{\prime}_{0}=T_{0},

and we recursively define:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L^{\prime}_{i+1}&=L^{\prime}_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\\
\\
T^{\prime}_{i+1}&=T^{\prime}_{i}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\Big)\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\end{aligned}

L^{\prime}_{i}
and
T^{\prime}_{i}
are much simpler to analyze, and we can show that they do not stray too far from values
L_{i}
and
T_{i}
, respectively (see Lemma
3.12
). This allows us to focus our attention on analyzing these simpler equations. Now we move on to proving several lemmas, which will allow us to complete the proof of the main theorem.

Throughout the proof, we use the fact that the ratio
T_{i}/L_{i}
is decreasing. We prove this in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

If for all
j<i,L_{j},T_{j}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
and
T_{j}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{j}
then
T_{i}/L_{i}<T_{i-1}/L_{i-1}

Proof.

The proof is by induction. We assume inductively that:

T_{i}/L_{i}<T_{i-1}/L_{i-1}<\cdots<T_{0}/L_{0}<\ln\Delta

and we shall abbreviate “inductive hypothesis” by
IH
. Note that by
IH
, we have the following useful fact:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{i}=\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{T_{i}}\geq e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)T_{i}}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}}\geq e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)T_{0}}{L_{0}\ln\Delta}}=\Omega(1).

(10)

Now we consider
i+1
. By definition (
3
) we have:

L_{i+1}=L_{i}\times\Big(\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-L_{i}^{-\beta/2}\Big).

Also, we have:

\displaystyle T_{i+1}

\displaystyle=T_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-T_{i}\times\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}^{2}_{i}+T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}

by definition (
3
)

\displaystyle\leq T_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-\mathrm{Keep}_{i}^{2}\times T_{i}^{1-\beta/8}+T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}

since
T_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq T_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-T_{i}^{1-\beta/4}

since
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}=\Omega(1)
by (
10
)

\displaystyle=T_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\times T_{i}^{\beta/4}

\displaystyle\leq T_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\times(T_{i}/L_{i})^{\beta/2}

since
T_{i}/L_{i}<\ln\Delta
by
IH

\displaystyle=T_{i}\times\Big(\mathrm{Keep}_{i}-L_{i}^{-\beta/2}\Big)

so
T_{i+1}/L_{i+1}<T_{i}/L_{i}
.
∎

Notice that since
T_{0}/L_{0}<\ln\Delta
initially, the previous lemma implies
T_{i}/L_{i}<\ln\Delta
for every relevant
i
. It will also be useful to have upper and lower bounds on
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}
, which we will see in the next lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

If for all
j\leq i,L_{j},T_{j}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
and
T_{j}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{j}
then
e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}}\leq\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\leq 1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}

Proof.

For the lower bound, we make use of Lemma
3.6
as well as the same argument of (
10
) to get:

\mathrm{Keep}_{i}=\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{T_{i}}\geq e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)T_{i}}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}}\geq e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)T_{0}}{L_{0}\ln\Delta}}=e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}}.

For the upper bound, we have:

\mathrm{Keep}_{i}=\Big(1-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}\Big)^{T_{i}}\leq e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)T_{i}}{L_{i}\ln\Delta}}\leq e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{8\ln\Delta}}\leq 1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}

using the assumption that
T_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{i}

∎

Remark 3.8
.

We remark that by choice of
K
, the lower bound given by Lemma
3.7
is at least
\frac{1}{2}
. We will see this in Lemma
3.13
when we precisely define
K
(see definition (

The next step is to compute the expected values of our parameters, which we will see in the following lemma.

We consider 3 cases:

•

when

•

when

•

when

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}(A|B_{1})=\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\frac{t_{i}(u,v,c^{\prime})}{L_{i}}\leq\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+L_{i}^{-1/2}

(11)

Therefore, by the law of total probability and (

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{Pr}\big(B_{1}\big)\times\mathbf{Pr}\big(A|B_{1}\big)+\mathbf{Pr}\big(B_{2}\big)\times\mathbf{Pr}\big(A|B_{2}\big)+\mathbf{Pr}\big(B_{3}\big)\times\mathbf{Pr}\big(A|B_{3}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \Bigg(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\Bigg)\times\Bigg(\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+L_{i}^{-1/2}\Bigg)+\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\times\frac{L_{i}-1}{L_{i}}\times\Bigg(\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+L_{i}^{-1/2}\Bigg)\Bigg(1-\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\Bigg)+\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\times\frac{L_{i}^{-1/2}}{L_{i}}

\displaystyle\ \bigg(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\Bigg)\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+L_{i}^{-1/3}.

for

The next step is to show concentration results for our parameters.

Lemma 3.10
.

If
P(i)
holds,
T_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{i}
,and if
T_{i},L_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
, then for any uncoloured vertex
v
and colour
c\in L_{i}(v)
,

a)

\mathbf{Pr}\Big(|\ell_{i+1}(v)-\mathbf{E}(\ell_{i+1}(v))|>L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\Big)<\Delta^{-\ln\Delta}
;

b)

\mathbf{Pr}\Big(|t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c)-\mathbf{E}(t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c))|>\frac{1}{2}T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\Big)<\Delta^{-\ln\Delta}
.

Proof.

To show that our parameters are strongly concentrated, we will make use of Talagrand’s Inequality. Recall that Talagrand’s Inequality requires that a random variable be determined by a set of independent trials.

The random choices made in an iteration are as follows. For each vertex we choose whether or not to activate it. We assign a random colour to each activated vertex. Finally, we conduct an equalizing coin flip for each
v
and
c
in
v
’s list, removing
c
from
v
’s list if it loses the coin flip. A problem arises when applying Talagrand’s Inequality, since these choices are not made independently of one another. For instance, the choice of whether to assign a random colour to a vertex depends on the activation choice for that vertex. Also, the choice of whether to conduct an equalizing coin flip for a given colour and vertex
v
depends on the fact that this colour was not removed from
v
’s list earlier in the iteration, and this is determined by the activation and colour assignments on
v
’s neighbours. We add a set of dummy choices to fix this. We choose a colour for each unactivated vertex and conduct an equalizing coin flip for each
v
and
c
not in
v
’s list. Of course we do not assign the chosen colour to the unactivated vertex and there is no need to remove
c
from
v
’s list if it loses the coin flip, so these dummy choices have no effect on the procedure. Their purpose is to make the set of random choices independent from one another, and this will allow us to apply Talagrand’s Inequality.

a)

We focus on showing that the number of colours removed from
v^{\prime}s
list during iteration
i+1
,
\bar{\ell}
, is highly concentrated.

Changing the activation or colour assignment to any vertex
u\in N(v)
can affect
\bar{\ell}
by at most the maximal value of
t_{i}(u,v,\gamma)
realized by colour
\gamma
. By (
2
) this is at most
L_{i}^{1/2-\beta}
. Changing the colour assignment to any other vertex cannot affect
\bar{\ell}
at all. Changing the result of any equalizing flip can affect
\bar{\ell}
by at most 1.

Suppose
\bar{\ell}\geq s
. We consider the two possible ways a colour
c
could leave
v
’s list. A neighbour
u
of
v
could have caused a conflict, meaning that
u
was assigned a colour
c^{\prime}
such that
(c,c^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(v,u)
. The other way a colour could be removed is via an equalizing flip. Therefore, there must be
s_{1}
neighbours of
v
which were involved in conflicts and caused
v
to lose
s_{1}
distinct colours. There must also be
s_{2}
equalizing flips which resulted in
s_{2}
distinct colours (disjoint from the
s_{1}
previous colours) being removed from
v^{\prime}s
list, and we must have
s_{1}+s_{2}=s
. For the first set of events we require two trials for each colour removed: the activation and colour assigned to the neighbour. For the second set of events we require only one trial, the equalizing flip. Therefore in total we require at most
2s_{1}+s_{2}\leq 2s
trials to certify that
\bar{\ell}\geq s
.

Let
t=L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}
, and note that
\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})
=
L_{i}-\mathbf{E}(\ell_{i+1}(v))=L_{i}-L_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}
. So this expectation is at most
L_{i}
. Therefore since
L_{i}
grows with
\Delta
, we have
t/2\geq 20q\sqrt{\smash[b]{r\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})}}+64q^{2}r
for any
q\leq L_{i}^{1/2-\beta}
and any constant
r
, and for
\Delta
sufficiently large. Using this fact, we have that

\mathbf{Pr}\Big(|\bar{\ell}-\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})|>t\Big)\leq\mathbf{Pr}\Big(|\bar{\ell}-\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})|>\frac{t}{2}+20q\sqrt{r\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})}+64q^{2}r\Big).

So applying Talagrand’s Inequality with
q=L_{i}^{1/2-\beta}
and
r=2
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\Big(|\bar{\ell}-\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})|>L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp\Bigg({-\frac{L_{i}^{2-\beta}}{64q^{2}\Big(\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})+L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\Big)}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp\Bigg({-\frac{L_{i}^{1-\beta}}{128L_{i}^{1-2\beta}}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp\Bigg({-\frac{L_{i}^{\beta}}{128}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp\bigg({-\frac{\ln^{3}\Delta}{128}}\bigg)

since
L_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
by assumption

\displaystyle<\Delta^{-\ln\Delta}

for
\Delta
sufficiently large.

Now by linearity of expectation,
\mathbf{E}(\ell_{i+1}(v))=L_{i}-\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})
. So we have that

\mathbf{Pr}\Big(|\ell_{i+1}(v)-\mathbf{E}(\ell_{i+1}(v))|>L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\Big)=\mathbf{Pr}\Big(|\bar{\ell}-\mathbf{E}(\bar{\ell})|>L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\Big)<\Delta^{-\ln\Delta}

as desired.

u
are constrained by
(c,c^{\prime})\in N_{i}(v,c)
and
u
is not activated then the activation choice for
u
certifies this fact. Otherwise if
u
is uncoloured because of a conflict with a neighbour
w\neq v
, then the activation and colour choices for
u
and
w
certify this. In addition, if
c^{\prime}
is removed from
u
’s list because of a conflict with a neighbour other than
v
then the activation and colour choice for that neighbour certifies this. So in total we require a set of at most
6s
random choices to certify that
Y_{1}\geq s
.

Therefore applying Talagrand’s Inequality, the same calculation from before gives

\mathbf{Pr}\big(|Y_{1}-\mathbf{E}(Y_{1})|>\frac{1}{4}T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\big)<\frac{1}{4}\Delta^{-\ln\Delta}.

Now
Y_{2}
is again bounded above by the random variable
T_{i}^{1/2-\beta}BIN\big(T_{i},\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\times\frac{T_{i}^{1/2-\beta}}{L_{i}}\big)
. So we have

\mathbf{Pr}\big(Y_{2}>\frac{1}{4}T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}\big)<\frac{1}{4}\Delta^{-\ln\Delta},

and this implies
Y
is sufficiently concentrated as before, so the result follows.

∎

Our next step is to show property
P(i)
holds for all appropriate values of
i
.

Lemma 3.11
.

With positive probability,
P(i)
holds for every
i
such that for all
j<i:L_{j},T_{j}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
and
T_{j}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{j}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction. For the base case, we note that the bounds
\ell_{0}(v)\geq L_{0}
and
t_{0}(v,c)\leq T_{0}
clearly hold by Modification
1
. So
P(0)
holds.

For the induction step we assume
P(i)
holds for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and we will show that with positive probability
P(i+1)
holds.

For every
v
, neighbour
u
of
v
, and
c\in L_{i+1}(v)
we define
A_{v}
to be the event that
\ell_{i+1}(v)<L_{i+1}
, and
B_{v,c}
be the event that
t_{i+1}(v,c)>T_{i+1}
. If none of these events hold, then
P(i+1)
holds.

If the event
A_{v}
occurs, by Lemma
3.9
this implies that
|\ell_{i+1}(v)-\mathbf{E}(\ell_{i+1}(v))|>L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}
, and by Lemma
3.10
this happens with probability at most
p=\Delta^{-\ln\Delta}
(notice that we’ve implicitly used the inductive hypothesis here when making use of Lemma
3.9
and Lemma
3.10
).

Also if the event
B_{v,c}
occurs we have:

\displaystyle t_{i+1}(v,c)

\displaystyle>T_{i}(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{i})\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}

\displaystyle\geq t_{i}(v,c)\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\Big)\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}

\displaystyle\geq t_{i}(v,c)\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\Big)\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}+T_{i}^{3/4}+\frac{1}{2}T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{E}(t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c))+\frac{1}{2}T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}

and since
t_{i+1}(v,c)\leq t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c)
we can see this implies
|t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c)-\mathbf{E}(t^{\prime}_{i+1}(v,c))|>\frac{1}{2}T_{i}^{1-\beta/2}
. Again by Lemma
3.10
, this happens with probability at most
p
.

Moreover, each event corresponding to a vertex
v
is determined by equalizing coin flips and colours assigned to vertices of distance at most 2 from
v
. Therefore, by the Mutual Independence Principle (see chapter 4 of
[
23
]
), each event corresponding to a vertex
v
is mutually independent of all events except for those corresponding to vertices of distance at most 4 from
v
. So every event can only be dependent on at most
d=\Delta^{4}\times O\big(\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}\big)<\Delta^{5}
other events. So
pd<\Delta^{-\ln\Delta}\Delta^{5}<1/4
for
\Delta
sufficiently large and the result follows from the Lovász Local Lemma.
∎

Our next lemma shows that the values for
L_{i+1}
and
T_{i+1}
defined in (
3
) do not stray too far from the values
L_{i+1}^{\prime}
and
T_{i+1}^{\prime}
defined in (
3
). As the recursive equations defined in (
3
) have a much nicer structure, this will simplify our analysis significantly moving forward.

Lemma 3.12
.

If for all
j<i
we have
L_{j},T_{j}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
and
T_{j}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{j}
, then

a)

|L_{i}-L^{\prime}_{i}|\leq(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}=o(L^{\prime}_{i})
;

b)

|T_{i}-T^{\prime}_{i}|\leq(T^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}=o(T^{\prime}_{i})
.

Proof.

a)

The proof is by induction. Since
L^{\prime}_{i}>L_{i}
, it suffices to show that
L_{i}^{\prime}\leq L_{i}+(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}
. Initially this is clearly the case. Now suppose that for some
i>0
we have
L_{i}^{\prime}\leq L_{i}+(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}
. It is easy to verify that the function
f(x)=x^{1-\beta/4}-x
has only one critical point in the interval
(0,1)
, namely at
x=(1-\beta/4)^{4/\beta}<e^{-1}
. Therefore
f(x)
is decreasing on the interval
[e^{-1},1]
. By Lemma
3.7
and Remark
3.8
,
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}
is within this interval. Also by Lemma
3.7
we have
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\leq(1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10}\ln\Delta})
and so using the fact that
f
is decreasing we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{i}^{1-\beta/4}-\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\geq\Big(1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}\Big)^{1-\beta/4}-\Big(1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}\Big).

(21)

Now we use the Maclaurin Series expansion of the function
g(x)=(1-x)^{1-\beta/4}=1-(1-\beta/4)x-\frac{\beta}{8}(1-\beta/4)x^{2}-\cdots
to see that
(1-x)^{1-\beta/4}=1-(1-\beta/4)x-O(x^{2})
for
x\in[0,1]
. So for
x={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}
and for
\Delta
sufficiently large,

\Big(1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}\Big)^{1-\beta/4}\geq 1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1-\beta/4)\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}-O\Big(\frac{1}{\ln^{2}\Delta}\Big)\geq 1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1-\beta/8)\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}

So continuing (
21
) we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{i}^{1-\beta/4}-\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\geq 1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1-\beta/8)\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}-\Big(1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{K}{10\ln\Delta}}\Big)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\beta K}{80\ln\Delta}}.

(22)

Now we have:

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Keep}_{i}L^{\prime}_{i}

by definition (
3
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(L_{i}+(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4})

by
IH

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Keep}_{i}L_{i}+\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}

\displaystyle=L_{i+1}+L_{i}^{1-\beta/2}+\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}

using definition (
3
)

\displaystyle\leq L_{i+1}+L^{1-\beta/2}_{i}+\mathrm{Keep}_{i}^{1-\beta/4}(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\beta K}{80\ln\Delta}}(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}

by (
22
)

\displaystyle=L_{i+1}+\mathrm{Keep}_{i}^{1-\beta/4}(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}+L^{1-\beta/2}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\beta K}{80\ln\Delta}}(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}

\displaystyle\leq L_{i+1}+(L^{\prime}_{i+1})^{1-\beta/4}

using definition (
3
)

where in the last bound we use the fact that the negative term dominates over
L^{1-\beta/2}_{i}
for
\Delta
sufficiently large (since
L_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
).

b)

For this part we have
T^{\prime}_{i}<T_{i}
, and so it suffices to show that
T^{\prime}_{i}\geq T_{i}-(T^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}
. The proof uses the same method as before, except this time we show that for
x=\mathrm{Keep}_{i}(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{i})
we have
x^{1-\beta/4}-x=\Omega\big(\frac{1}{\ln\Delta}\big)
, which allows us to complete the proof in the same way.

∎

With the help of Lemma
3.12
we can finally show that our list sizes never become too small, while on the other hand showing that
T_{i}
eventually becomes much smaller than
L_{i}
. This will allow us to apply Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.13
.

There exists
i^{\ast}
such that

a)

For all
i<i^{\ast},T_{i},L_{i}>\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
, and
T_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{i}
;

b)

T_{i^{\ast}}<\frac{1}{8}L_{i^{\ast}}
.

Proof.

a)

For this part we show that
T_{i}
and
L_{i}
are sufficiently large whenever
T_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{i}
. The proof is by induction,
L_{0}
and
T_{0}
are clearly sufficiently large. We show that for some
i
, if
T_{i}
and
L_{i}
are sufficiently large and
T_{i+1}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{i+1}
, then
T_{i+1}
and
L_{i+1}
are sufficiently large as well. If
T_{i+1}<\frac{1}{8}L_{i+1}
then there is no need to continue the procedure for more than
i+1
iterations and this implies the existence of
i^{\ast}
. We show that this indeed happens in part (
b
).

First, recall the definitions of
L^{\prime}_{i+1}
and
T^{\prime}_{i+1}
. Initially, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L^{\prime}_{0}&=L_{0}=(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}\\
\\
T^{\prime}_{0}&=T_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\sqrt{\Delta\ln\Delta}\end{aligned}

and we recursively define:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L^{\prime}_{i+1}&=L^{\prime}_{i}\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\\
\\
T^{\prime}_{i+1}&=T^{\prime}_{i}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\Big)\times\mathrm{Keep}_{i}\end{aligned}

We focus on showing
L_{i+1}
is large first, but rather than finding a lower bound for
L_{i+1}
, Lemma
3.12
allows us to focus on
L^{\prime}_{i+1}
instead. In particular, for each
j\leq i
we focus on bounding
\mathrm{Keep}_{j}
which is closely related to the ratio
r_{j}=T_{j}/L_{j}\approx r^{\prime}_{j}=T^{\prime}_{j}/L^{\prime}_{j}
.

Recall that we gave the following lower bound on
\mathrm{Keep}_{j}
in the proof of Lemma
3.7
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{j}>\exp\bigg(-K\frac{(1+\frac{1}{20}\epsilon e^{-\epsilon})r_{j}}{\ln\Delta}\bigg)>\exp\bigg(-K\frac{(1+\frac{1}{20}\epsilon e^{-\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\bigg)

(23)

Now by (

a
)

) we have

r^{\prime}_{i}=r_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\Bigg(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\mathrm{Keep}_{j}\Bigg).

So by (
23
), and since
r_{0}=\frac{\ln\Delta}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}
by (

a
)

) we get:

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}\times\bigg(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\exp\Big(-K\frac{(1+\frac{1}{20}\epsilon e^{-\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\Big)\bigg)^{i-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln\Delta}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\bigg(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\exp\Big(-K\frac{(1+\frac{1}{20}\epsilon e^{-\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\Big)\bigg)^{i-1}

(24)

Moreover, by Lemma
3.12
we have

\displaystyle r_{i}=\frac{T_{i}}{L_{i}}\leq\frac{T^{\prime}_{i}+(T^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}}{L^{\prime}_{i}-(L^{\prime}_{i})^{1-\beta/4}}=r^{\prime}_{i}\times\frac{1+\frac{1}{(T^{\prime}_{i})^{\beta/4}}}{1-\frac{1}{(L^{\prime}_{i})^{\beta/4}}}=(1+o(1))r^{\prime}_{i}

(25)

where in the last equality we use the fact that
L^{\prime}_{i},T^{\prime}_{i}
are close to
L_{i},T_{i}
respectively by Lemma
3.12
, and the latter values satisfy
L_{i},T_{i}\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
by
IH
. So by (
25
) we get
r_{i}\leq(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)r^{\prime}_{i}
for
\Delta
sufficiently large. So now we have:

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)r^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)\ln\Delta}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\bigg(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}\exp\Big(-K\frac{(1+\frac{1}{20}\epsilon e^{-\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\Big)\bigg)^{i-1}

by (
24
)

which yields a better lower bound on
\mathrm{Keep}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Keep}_{i}

\displaystyle>\exp\Bigg(-K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)r_{i}/\ln\Delta\Bigg)

by (
23
)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Bigg(-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)\ln\Delta}{\ln\Delta(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}}\Big)^{i-1}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg(-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}}\Big)^{i-1}\Bigg)

(26)

It is straightforward to check that for any
\epsilon>0
, we have
(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/30)\leq(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10)
in the above expressions. So now we can proceed with our bound for
L^{\prime}_{i}
. We have:

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=L_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{i}\mathrm{Keep}_{j}

\displaystyle\geq(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\ln\Delta}}\exp\Bigg(-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\Big(1-\frac{K}{\ln\Delta}e^{-\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}}\Big)^{j-1}\Bigg)

by (
26
)

\displaystyle\geq(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\ln\Delta}}\exp\Bigg(-\frac{(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10)\ln\Delta}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}e^{\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}}\Bigg)

by the geometric series

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\ln\Delta}}\times\Delta^{-\frac{(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\exp\Big(\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\Big)}

(27)

Now it suffices to obtain a bound on the exponent of
\Delta
in (
27
). We simply choose a value of
K
which makes the exponent collapse to some value strictly greater than
-\frac{1}{2}
. We take

\displaystyle K=\frac{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}{(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}\ln{(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10)}.

(28)

At this point the reader should verify that this definition is consistent with remark
3.8
. So we have:

\frac{(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\exp\Bigg(\frac{K(1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/20)}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\Bigg)=\Bigg(\frac{1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\Bigg)^{2}

It is straightforward (although tedious) to verify that
\frac{1}{2}-\Big(\frac{1+\epsilon e^{-\epsilon}/10}{\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2}\Big)^{2}>\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon+3}\Big)^{2}
. So continuing (
27
) we obtain:

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{i+1}

\displaystyle\geq(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+3)^{2}}/\sqrt{\ln\Delta}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+3.5)^{2}}

for
\Delta
sufficiently large.

Therefore
L_{i+1}>L^{\prime}_{i+1}-(L^{\prime}_{i+1})^{1-\beta/4}>\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+3.75)^{2}}
for
\Delta
sufficiently large. Since
T_{i+1}\geq\frac{1}{8}L_{i+1}
this means
T_{i+1}>\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
, so both
L_{i+1}
and
T_{i+1}
are at least
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+4)^{2}}
for
\Delta
sufficiently large.

b)

Now we will show that the ratio
r_{i}=T_{i}/L_{i}
eventually becomes smaller than
\frac{1}{8}
.

We know by Lemma
3.7
that
\mathrm{Keep}_{0}\geq c
for some positive constant
c
. We also know by (
25
) that
r_{i}\leq(1+o(1))r^{\prime}_{i}\leq 2r^{\prime}_{i}
for
\Delta
sufficiently large. Now let
\hat{i}=\frac{2}{Kc}\ln\Delta\ln\ln\Delta
. Recalling the value of
r_{0}=\frac{\ln\Delta}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}
we have:

r_{\hat{i}}\leq 2r_{0}\Big(1-\frac{Kc}{\ln\Delta}\Big)^{\frac{2}{Kc}\ln\Delta\ln\ln\Delta}\leq\frac{2}{(\sqrt{2}+\epsilon/2)^{2}}\ln\Delta\times e^{-2\ln\ln\Delta}<\frac{1}{8}.

for sufficiently large
\Delta
.
Let
S=\{i\leq\hat{i}:r_{i}<\frac{1}{8}\}
. Clearly
S
is non-empty, so we choose
i^{\ast}
to be the minimum value in
S
. Now the fact that
r_{i}
is decreasing implies the lemma.

∎

Finally, we put it all together to prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
i^{\ast}
be the natural number guaranteed by Lemma
3.13
. Firstly we can carry out the wasteful colouring procedure for
i^{\ast}
iterations. By Lemma
3.11
and Lemma
3.13
,
P(i)
holds with positive probability for each
i\leq i^{\ast}
so we do in fact perform
i^{\ast}
iterations. Thus by property
P(i^{\ast})
and Lemma
3.13
, we will have that for each
v
and
c\in L(v)
,
t_{i^{\ast}}(v,c)\leq T_{i^{\ast}}\leq\frac{1}{8}L_{i^{\ast}}
, and the colouring can be completed by Lemma
3.1
.

4
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we showed that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\Delta_{0}
such that any multigraph with maximum degree
\Delta>\Delta_{0}
, no cycles of length 3 or 4 and an edge labelling
\tau
with
D(\tau)
at most
\Delta^{\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{2}/(\epsilon+5)^{2}}
can be conflict coloured with at most
\ell=(2\sqrt{2}+\epsilon)\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
colours.

Note that for large
\epsilon
, our result means that a bound on
D(\tau)
of
\Delta^{\frac{1}{4}-o(1)}
is sufficient to complete the colouring with
\ell
colours (here
o(1)
is a function of
\epsilon
). On the other hand, Example
1.7
says that a bound of
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}-o(1)}
is necessary. Closing this gap could be an interesting problem for future work.

For small
\epsilon
the gap is more significant since when
\epsilon
tends to 0, theorem
1.5
gives a bound which tends to
\Delta^{0}
while Example
1.7
says that a bound of
\ell^{15/16}\approx\Delta^{15/32}
is necessary. Closing this gap could be another interesting problem. A step in this direction is to answer the following questions:

Question 1.
Is there a constant
d\in(0,\frac{15}{32})
such that any multigraph with maximum degree
\Delta
, no cycles of length 3 or 4, and an edge labelling with conflict degree at most
\Delta^{d}
can be conflict coloured with lists of size
(2\sqrt{2}+o(1))\sqrt{\Delta/\ln\Delta}
?

Question 2.
If the answer to Question 1 is affirmative, what is the biggest value of
d
possible?

We conclude by pointing out that these questions cannot be resolved in the affirmative with the analysis presented here. Recall Observation
1
and the following discussion. There we showed that if our list sizes are sufficiently large we have:

t_{i}(v,u,c)\leq L_{i}^{1/2-\beta}.

Recall that we required
t_{i}(v,u,c)
to be quite small, and so this bound was important for proving the concentration results (Lemma
3.10
) of our main parameters.

Now when we allow the conflict degree to be as large as
\Delta^{d}
for come absolute constant
d
, then initially
t_{i}(v,u,c)
can also be this large. This becomes a significant problem if
t_{i}(v,u,c)
remains this large throughout the procedure, since towards the end of the procedure our lists are of size
L_{i}\approx\Delta^{\epsilon^{2}}
. In particular, when
\epsilon
is much smaller than
d
it can be the case that
t_{i}(v,u,c)
is as big as
L_{i}
. I.e. assigning
c
to
v
in this case could eliminate
u
’s list entirely.

A natural attempt to overcome this is to show that the parameter
t_{i}(v,u,c)
decreases quickly with each iteration. Unfortunately, there are cases where this does not happen with high probability.

Nevertheless, as far as the authors know Questions 1 and 2 could conceivably be answered in the affirmative via other methods.
