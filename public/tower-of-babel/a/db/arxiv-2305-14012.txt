A Simple Method for Unsupervised Bilingual Lexicon Induction for Data-Imbalanced, Closely Related Language Pairs
Niyati Bafna, Cristina España-Bonet, Josef van Genabith, Benoît Sagot, Rachel Bawden

Abstract
Existing approaches for unsupervised bilingual lexicon induction (BLI) often depend on good quality static or contextual embeddings trained on large monolingual corpora for both languages. In reality, however, unsupervised BLI is most likely to be useful for dialects and languages that do not have abundant amounts of monolingual data. We introduce a simple and fast method for unsupervised BLI for low-resource languages with a related mid-to-high resource language, only requiring inference on the higher-resource language monolingual BERT ( Devlin et al. 2019 ) . We work with two low-resource languages ( < 5 ​ M <5M monolingual tokens), Bhojpuri and Magahi, of the severely under-researched Indic dialect continuum, showing that state-of-the-art methods in the literature show near-zero performance in these settings, and that our simpler method gives much better results. We repeat our experiments on Marathi and Nepali, two higher-resource Indic languages, to compare approach performances by resource range. We release automatically created bilingual lexicons for the first time for five languages of the Indic dialect continuum. 1 1 1 https://github.com/niyatibafna/BLI-for-Indic-languages

Bilingual lexicons are a basic resource with varied uses, both in themselves, for dictionary building and language learning, as well as as seeds for solving other problems in natural language processing (NLP), such as parsing

While there is growing interest in unsupervised or minimally supervised BLI, existing methods often depend on aligning monolingual word embedding spaces, assumed to be of good quality for both languages, and/or bilingual supervision. However, extremely low-resource languages (LRLs) and dialects often do not have good quality word embedding spaces due to lack of large monolingual corpora, leading to very low or near-zero performance of alignment-based methods on these languages
(
Wada et al. 2019
;
Eder et al. 2021
)
. In this work, we look at a realistic application settings of unsupervised BLI, i.e. between a high-resource and low-resource language, aiming to leverage data and tools in the high-resource language to create bilingual resources for low-resource related languages.

Our main contribution is a simple and fast method to collect bilingual lexicons for a low-resource target dialect against the high-resource source dialect, only requiring inference on an already existing HRL mask-filling model such as monolingual BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
.
We work with two extremely low-resourced Indic languages, Bhojpuri (bho) and Magahi (mag), which are closely related to Hindi, our HRL. We release the lexicons we developed for these languages (to our knowledge the first to be publicly released) as well as for three other languages on the Indic dialect continuum, Awadhi, Maithili, and Braj.

Related Work

Although bilingual lexicon induction (BLI) from parallel or comparable corpora has a rich literature, recent years have also seen interest in unsupervised BLI
(
Haghighi et al. 2008
;
Conneau et al. 2018
;
Artetxe et al. 2017a
;
Artetxe et al. 2017b
;
Artetxe et al. 2018b
;
Artetxe et al. 2019
;
Artetxe et al. 2018a
)
, opening up the possibility for BLI for low-resource languages. Most unsupervised approaches, notably
MUSE

(
Conneau et al. 2018
)
and
VecMap

(
Artetxe et al. 2018b
;
Artetxe et al. 2018a
)
are based on training static embeddings from large monolingual corpora
(
Mikolov et al. 2013
;
Bojanowski et al. 2017
)
, and aligning the embeddings using linear or non-linear mappings, relying on a seed to bootstrap the process
(
Xing et al. 2015
;
Artetxe et al. 2016
)
.
There has also been work in trying to adapt these approaches to distant language pairs, notably by relaxing an implicit assumption of embedding space isomorphism
(
Søgaard et al. 2018
;
Patra et al. 2019
;
Glavaš and Vulić 2020
)
. Later works show the failings of these approaches in low-resource settings
(
Adams et al. 2017
;
Kuriyozov et al. 2020
;
Chimalamarri et al. 2020
;
Eder et al. 2021
)
and propose alternative training strategies such as joint training of static embeddings
(
Woller et al. 2021
;
Bafna et al. 2022
)
, and multilingual embeddings from LSTM-based models
(
Wada et al. 2019
)
. However, these works either address a higher resource range (
>15M
tokens), use bilingual lexicons as seeds, or show low scores (
<20
precision@5) for unsupervised BLI for data settings similar to ours. In general, there is a paucity of attention given to the scenario where one language has significantly worse embeddings and no other resources, resulting in failure of these approaches.

Recent works have also looked at using contextual embeddings or BERT-based models
(
Peters et al. 2018
;
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Ruder et al. 2019
)
for BLI.
Gonen et al. 2020
induce word-level translations by directly prompting mBERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
.
Yuan et al. 2020
present a human-in-the-loop system for BLI in four low-resource languages, updating contextual embeddings with the help of annotations provided by a native speaker.
Zhang et al. 2021
present
CSCBLI
, a method that uses a “spring network” to align non-isomorphic contextual embeddings, and interpolates them with static embeddings to estimate word similarities, showing superior results to other methods using contextual embeddings, notably, BLISS
(
Patra et al. 2019
)
. Many of these approaches rely on parallel data or large monolingual corpora for good quality contextual embeddings. However, for low-resource languages, contextual embeddings from both monolingual and multilingual models are known to be unreliable and of poor quality
(
Wu and Dredze 2020
)
.

The existing literature often relies on robust embeddings spaces, static or contextual, for
both
languagues for BLI, whereas, in situations where we need BLI most crucially, i.e. for low-resource languages, we are unlikely to have good embedding spaces for both languages, or the resources available to fund data collection efforts when there are dozens of such languages. Our work aims to introduce a fast and accurate strategy for BLI for low-resource languages that relies on good contextual embeddings for only one of the languages in the pair. We compare our method against representative methods from the above works, namely
VecMap
and
CSCBLI
.

3
Method

Our method is intended for a closely related HRL and LRL pair (e.g. linguistic variants or dialects) written in the same script, given that we can train or already have a good quality monolingual BERT for the HRL. The main idea is that if we mask an unknown word in the low-resource language sentence and ask the HRL BERT to propose candidates, the HRL BERT should have access to enough contextual cues due to shared vocabulary and syntax to propose meaningful
HRL
candidates for the masked token. These candidates can then be reranked by some mechanism, possibly leveraging knowledge of the languages or domain. This gives us translation equivalency between the original target dialect source word and the chosen candidate. These translation equivalents can be reused to replace target dialect words in future sentence with source dialect words, to aid the BERT model to better handle subsequent
[MASK]
ed words. We proceed in an iterative manner over the process of growing the lexicon from equivalents gained from each processed sentence, and using learned equivalents in the lexicon to make the processed sentence more source-dialect-like. We implement two variants of this algorithm:

In the

Rulebook

In this variant, we use a weighted (unigram) edit distance matrix for orthographic similarity scores, updating the probability for any source-target character pair based on newly found translation equivalents. This new weight edit distance function is then applied to the scoring of candidates in the future. We use this idea as described in
(
Bafna et al. 2022

Choosing
(sentence, word)

Intuitively, we want to process unknown words in ‘‘easy’’ sentences first, that is, sentences in which there are very few unknown words and which are therefore already likely to be intelligible to the source BERT.

We mask whole words and accept single token responses (as the default) from the BERT.
This increases the chance that the BERT model gives accurate translation equivalents for the masked word. We maintain a priority list of
(sentence, unk_word)

Specifically, the priority list is created from the

Updating translations

It is possible that in the early stage of this process, a word is assigned an incorrect translation because most of its neighbour words were unknown, or that it was assigned no translation because no candidate passed the similarity threshold. We therefore allow a word in a given sentence to be reprocessed once all other unknown words have been processed an equivalent number of times.

6

that is, we will only process a
(sentence,word)

Baselines

VecMap

Our methods

As the
Basic
and
Rulebook
method are directly dependant on orthographic distance between translation pairs, they are only useful for identifying cognate equivalents or alternate spellings in the source and target language. We observe that these approaches are reasonably successful for Bhojpuri and Magahi on cognate verbs and common nouns (see examples in Table

Basic

Basic

Improvements by
Rulebook

Although
Basic
and
Rulebook
perform roughly at par and give identical predictions for the most part,
Rulebook
has a slight edge when it comes to predicting cognates with common sound correspondences, where
Basic
can be misled by co-incidental orthographic similarities (see row 5).

8
Conclusion

We perform unsupervised BLI for extremely low-resource languages against a related high resource language, introducing a novel method that only requires good quality contextual embeddings for one language in the language pair. We believe that this method addresses an important gap in existing literature for unsupervised BLI, which often relies on good quality embeddings spaces for both languages. We argue that this assumption is unrealistic in true application scenarios of unsupervised BLI. Our method shows superior performance on two truly low-resource languages from the Indic language continuum, against near-zero performances of existing state-of-the-art methods. We also perform control experiments and analyses for two more distantly related Indic languages, and release the resulting bilingual lexicons for five truly low-resource Indic languages.

Limitations

The applicability of our method is restricted to low-resource languages that are related to a high-resource language and show non-trivial lexical overlap with that language. Further, we make it clear that our method is not intended for mid-to-high resourced language pairs (such as Marathi-Hindi), where canonical state-of-the-art methods such as
VecMap
indeed work more robustly, specifically on non-identical word equivalents. Our method therefore has a specific (although important) target scenario, i.e. it is a simple method to quickly build bilingual lexicons for severely under-resourced languages leveraging the resources of a closely related high-resource language, and will perform worse than canonical methods for distant language pairs and high-resource language pairs.
Another limitation of our work is that we were not able to provide true native speaker evaluation for the resulting target language lexicons, instead providing evaluation by a Hindi native speaker relying on knowledge of shared cognates, the morphology of the target language, and inflection tables. Finally, our method is only capable of providing single token matches to the (masked) whole word, we leave it to future work to handle multi-token words and possibly multi-word terms.

Ethics Statement

Our work is driven by the motivation to boost NLP for severly under-resourced language for the Indic language belt, as well as contribute a method that may be relevant to other language families with a similar linguistic and resource setup.
Our method relies on the predictions of large language models for the high-resource language and is therefore fallible to general ethical issues with such models, including caste, religion, and gender biases
(
Malik et al. 2022
)
.
