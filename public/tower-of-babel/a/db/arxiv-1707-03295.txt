Separating regular languages with two quantifier alternations
Thomas Place

Abstract
. We investigate a famous decision problem in automata theory: separation. Given a class of language 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , the separation problem for 𝒞 \mathcal{C} takes as input two regular languages and asks whether there exists a third one which belongs to 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , includes the first one and is disjoint from the second. Typically, obtaining an algorithm for separation yields a deep understanding of the investigated class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . This explains why a lot of effort has been devoted to finding algorithms for the most prominent classes. Here, we are interested in classes within concatenation hierarchies. Such hierarchies are built using a generic construction process: one starts from an initial class called the basis and builds new levels by applying generic operations. The most famous one, the dot-depth hierarchy of Brzozowski and Cohen, classifies the languages definable in first-order logic. Moreover, it was shown by Thomas that it corresponds to the quantifier alternation hierarchy of first-order logic: each level in the dot-depth corresponds to the languages that can be defined with a prescribed number of quantifier blocks. Finding separation algorithms for all levels in this hierarchy is among the most famous open problems in automata theory. Our main theorem is generic: we show that separation is decidable for the level 3 2 \frac{3}{2} of any concatenation hierarchy whose basis is finite. Furthermore, in the special case of the dot-depth, we push this result to the level 5 2 \frac{5}{2} . In logical terms, this solves separation for Σ 3 \Sigma_{3} : first-order sentences having at most three quantifier blocks starting with an existential one.

1.
Introduction

Context.
This paper is part of a research program whose objective is to precisely understand prominent classes of regular languages and in particular those corresponding to descriptive formalisms (such as a logic). Naturally, “understanding” a class
\mathcal{C}
is an informal objective. In the literature, one usually approaches this question by considering a decision problem:
membership
. For a given class
\mathcal{C}
, one looks for procedure deciding whether some input regular language belongs to
\mathcal{C}
. In practice, it turns out that such an algorithm yields a deep understanding of the class
\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, it is an effective description of
all
languages in
\mathcal{C}
.

This approach was originally inspired by a theorem of Schützenberger
[
sfo
]
. From this theorem, one obtains an algorithm which decides whether an input regular language is star-free,
i.e.
, can be expressed with a regular expression using union, complement and concatenation, but not Kleene star. This result was highly influential for several reasons:

•

The star-free languages are among the most prominent sub-classes of the regular languages. In particular, it was later shown by McNaughton and Papert
[
mnpfo
]
they are exactly those which can be defined in first-order logic (
FO
).

•

Being the first result of its kind, Schützenberger theorem cemented membership as the “right” question when aiming to “understand” a given class of languages.

•

Schützenberger developed a general methodology for tackling membership problems (which he applied to the star-free languages).

Such a success motivated researchers to investigate membership for other important classes of languages. This was quite fruitful and the question is now well-understood for several important classes. Prominent examples include the class of piecewise testable languages which was solved by Simon
[
simon75
]
or the two-variables fragment of first-order logic which was solved by Thérien and Wilke
[
twfodeux
]
. However, for some other classes, membership remains wide open, despite a wealth of research work spanning several decades.

Concatenation hierarchies.
In the paper, we investigate one of the most famous among these open questions: the
dot-depth problem
(see
[
jep-dd45
]
for a recent survey on the topic). Schützenberger’s results motivated Brzozowski and Cohen
[
BrzoDot
]
to define a classification of all star-free languages: the
dot-depth hierarchy
. Intuitively, one classifies the star-free languages according to the number of alternations between concatenations and complements required to define them. More precisely, the dot-depth is a particular instance of a generic construction process (which was formalized later) named
concatenation hierarchies
. Such a hierarchy has only one parameter: a “level
0
class” called its
basis
. Once it is fixed, one builds new levels by applying two generic operations: polynomial and Boolean closure. There are two kinds of levels: half levels
\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2}\dots
and full levels
0,1,2,3\dots
. For any full level
n
, the next half and full levels are built as follows:

•

Level
n+\frac{1}{2}
is the
polynomial closure
of level
n
. Given a class
\mathcal{C}
, its polynomial closure
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
is the least class of languages containing
\mathcal{C}
and closed under union, intersection and marked concatenation (
K,L\mapsto KaL
, where
K,L\subseteq A^{*}
and
a\in A
).

•

Level
n+1
is the
Boolean closure
of level
n+\frac{1}{2}
. Given a class
\mathcal{C}
, its Boolean closure
{Bool\/}(\mathcal{C})
is the least class containing
\mathcal{C}
and closed under union and complement.

Thus, a concatenation hierarchy is fully determined by its basis. In the paper, we carry out a generic investigation of hierarchies with a
finite
basis.

There are essentially two prominent hierarchies of this kind: the dot-depth hierarchy
[
BrzoDot
]
and Straubing-Thérien hierarchy
[
StrauConcat
,
TheConcat
]
which are both classifications of the star-free languages. This status is partially explained by their connection with the quantifier alternation hierarchies within first-order logic. One may classify first-order sentences as follows. A sentence is
\Sigma_{n}
either if its prenex normal form has
n
quantifier blocks and starts with an existential one or has strictly less than
n
quantifier blocks. Furthermore, a sentence is
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{n}
if it is a finite Boolean combination of
\Sigma_{n}
sentences. It was shown by Thomas
[
ThomEqu
]
that the dot-depth coincides with quantifier alternation: for any
n\in{\rm Nature}
, dot-depth
n
corresponds to
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{n}
and dot-depth
n+\frac{1}{2}
to
\Sigma_{n+1}
. This result was later lifted to the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy
[
PPOrder
]
: it corresponds to the alternation hierarchy within another variant of first-order logic equipped with a slightly different signature (this does not change the overall expressive power but impacts the languages that one may define at a given level of its alternation hierarchy).

The
dot-depth problem
is to obtain membership algorithms for all levels in these two hierarchies (and therefore, for the alternation hierarchies of first-order logic as well). However, progress has been slow: until recently, only level
\frac{1}{2}

[
arfi87
,
pwdelta
]
, level 1
[
simon75
,
knast83
]
and level
\frac{3}{2}

[
arfi87
,
pwdelta
,
gssig2
]
were solved. See
[
dgk-smallfragments
]
for a survey. Following these results, membership for level 2 remained open for a long time and became famous under the name “dot-depth two problem”.

Separation.
Recently solutions were found for the levels
2
and
\frac{5}{2}
by relying on a new approach
[
pzqalt
]
. The techniques involve considering a new ingredient: a stronger decision problem called
separation
. Rather than asking whether a single input language belongs to the class
\mathcal{C}
under investigation, the
\mathcal{C}
-separation problem takes as input
two
regular languages. It asks whether there exists a third language
belonging to
\mathcal{C}

which contains the first language and is disjoint from the second. The interest in separation is recent. However, it has quickly replaced membership as the central question. This newly acquired status is explained by two main reasons.

First, separation serves as the key ingredient in all recent membership results (see
[
PZ:Siglog15
,
pzcsr17
]
for an overview). A simple but crucial example is the membership algorithm for the level
\frac{5}{2}
in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy
[
pzqalt
]
. It is based on two distinct results:

(1)

A generic reduction from membership for the level
n+1
to separation for the level
n
for any
half
level
n
in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy.

(2)

A separation algorithm for the level
\frac{3}{2}
.

When combined, these two results yield a membership algorithm for the level
\frac{5}{2}
.

However, there is another deeper reason for working with separation instead of membership. Our primary motivation is to thoroughly understand the classes that we investigate: this is why we considered membership in the first place. In this respect, while harder, separation is also far more rewarding than membership. Intuitively, this is easily explained. A membership algorithm for some class
\mathcal{C}
only yields benefits for the languages of
\mathcal{C}
: we are able to detect them and to build a description witnessing this membership. Instead, separation algorithms are universal: their benefits apply to
all
languages. An insightful perspective is to view separation as an approximation problem: given an input pair
(L_{1},L_{2})
, we want to over-approximate
L_{1}
by a language in
\mathcal{C}
, on the other hand
L_{2}
is the specification of what an acceptable approximation is. Altogether, the key idea is that separation yields a more robust understanding of the classes.

Separation is known to be decidable for the lower levels in the Straubing-Thérien and dot-depth hierarchies. This was first shown to be decidable for the levels
\frac{1}{2}
,
1
,
\frac{3}{2}
and
2
in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy
[
martens
,
pvzmfcs13
,
pzqalt
,
pzboolpol
]
. Let us point out that while these results were first formulated independently, it was recently proved in
[
pzboolpol
]
that the four of them are corollaries of only two generic theorems:

•

For any
finite
class
\mathcal{C}
,
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-separation is decidable. The levels
\frac{1}{2}
and
\frac{3}{2}
in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy are of this form (by definition for the former and by a result of
[
pin-straubing:upper
]
for the latter).

•

For any
finite
class
\mathcal{C}
,
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
-separation is decidable. The levels
1
and
2
in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy are of this form (by
[
pin-straubing:upper
]
again for the latter).

This generic approach was introduced in
[
pzboolpol
]
and we shall continue it in the paper. Finally, it is known that any separation solution for a given level in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy may be lifted to the corresponding level in the dot-depth via a generic reduction
[
pzsucc
]
. Thus, separation is also decidable for the levels
\frac{1}{2}
,
1
,
\frac{3}{2}
and
2
in the dot-depth.

Contributions.
Our most important result in the paper is a separation algorithm for the level 5/2 in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy. Note that from a logical point of view, this is the level
\Sigma_{3}
in the quantifier alternation hierarchy of first-order logic: sentences with at most three blocks of quantifiers (i.e. two alternations between quantifiers) and an existential leftmost block. Moreover, we also obtain two important corollaries “for free” from previously known results. First, we are able to lift the result to dot-depth 5/2 using the aforementioned transfer theorem
[
pzsucc
]
. Second, we obtain from the reduction of
[
pzqalt
]
that
membership
is decidable for the level 7/2 in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy (this may be lifted to dot-depth as well using a result of Straubing
[
StrauVD
]
).

A crucial point is that the above result is
not
our main theorem but only its most important corollary. The actual main result is more general for two distinct reasons. First, we actually consider an even stronger problem that separation:
covering
. This third problem was introduced in
[
pzcovering
,
pzcovering2
]
as a generalization of separation. It takes two objects as input, a single regular language
L
and a finite set of regular languages
\mathbf{L}
. Given some class
\mathcal{C}
, the
\mathcal{C}
-covering problem asks whether there exists a
\mathcal{C}
-cover of
L
(i.e. a finite set of languages in
\mathcal{C}
whose union includes
L
) such that no language in this
\mathcal{C}
-cover intersects all languages in
\mathbf{L}
. It is simple to show that separation is the special case of covering when the set
\mathbf{L}
is a singleton. We prove that covering (and therefore separation as well) is decidable for the level 5/2 in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy. Considering covering has benefits: it is arguably even more rewarding than separation and it comes with an elegant framework
[
pzcovering
,
pzcovering2
]
designed for tackling it (which we use to formulate all algorithms presented in the paper). However, a more fundamental motivation for considering covering is that we need to: no “direct” separation algorithm is known for the level 5/2.

Additionally, we follow a generic approach similar to the one used in
[
pzboolpol
]
for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
and
{Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
. Specifically, our main theorem states that covering and separation are decidable for any class of the form
{Pol\/}({Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})))
when
\mathcal{C}
is a
finite
class (it follows from the result of
[
pin-straubing:upper
]
mentioned above that the level 5/2 in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy is such a class). Being generic, this approach yields algorithms for a whole family of classes. Moreover, we are able to pinpoint the key hypotheses which are critical in order to solve separation and covering for the level 5/2.

Finally, we shall also reprove the theorem of
[
pzboolpol
]
for classes of the form
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
when
\mathcal{C}
is finite: the
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering problem is decidable. In fact, we prove a stronger theorem that we require for handling
{Pol\/}({Bool\/}({Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})))
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we define classes of languages as well as the decision problems that we shall consider. Moreover, we introduce some standard terminology that we shall use.

2.1.
Classes of languages

For the whole paper, we fix an arbitrary finite alphabet
A
. We shall denote by
A^{*}
the set of all words over
A
, including the empty word
\varepsilon
. We let
A^{+}=A^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
. If
u,v\in A^{*}
are words, we write

or

A subset of

is called a

. For the sake of avoiding clutter we often denote the singleton language

by

. It is standard to extend the concatenation operation to languages: for

KL

KL=\{uv\mid u\in K\text{ and }v\in L\}
. Moreover, we also consider

, which is less standard. Given

, a
marked concatenation of

with

is a language of the form

for some
a\in A
.

A
class of languages

\mathcal{C}
is simply a set of languages. All classes that we consider in the paper satisfy robust
closure properties
which we define now.

•

A
lattice
is a class of languages
\mathcal{C}
closed under finite union and finite intersection, and such that
\emptyset\in\mathcal{C}
and
A^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
.

•

A
Boolean algebra
is a lattice closed under complement.

•

Finally, we say that a class
\mathcal{C}
is
quotienting
when it is closed under quotients,
i.e.
, when for all
L\in\mathcal{C}
and all
w\in A^{*}
, the following two languages both belong to
\mathcal{C}
,

w^{-1}L\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{u\in A^{*}\mid wu\in L\}\quad\text{and}\quad Lw^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{u\in A^{*}\mid uw\in L\}

In the paper, all classes that we consider are at least
quotienting lattices
. Moreover, we only consider classes which are included in the class of
regular languages
(which is something that we shall implicitly assume from now on). These are the languages that can be equivalently defined by monadic second-order logic, finite automata or finite monoids (we come back to this point in Section
4
).

2.2.
Separation and covering

Our objective in the paper is to study several specific classes of languages (which we present in Section
3
). For this purpose, we shall rely on two decision problems: separation and covering. Both of them are parametrized by an arbitrary class of languages
\mathcal{C}
. Let us start with the definition of separation.

Separation.
Given three languages
K,L_{1},L_{2}
, we say that
K

separates

L_{1}
from
L_{2}
if
L_{1}\subseteq K\text{ and }L_{2}\cap K=\emptyset
. Given a class of languages
\mathcal{C}
, we say that
L_{1}
is
\mathcal{C}
-separable
from
L_{2}
if some language in
\mathcal{C}
separates
L_{1}
from
L_{2}
. Observe that when
\mathcal{C}
is not closed under complement (which is the case for all classes investigated in the paper), the definition is not symmetrical:
L_{1}
could be
\mathcal{C}
-separable from
L_{2}
while
L_{2}
is not
\mathcal{C}
-separable from
L_{1}
. The separation problem associated to a given class
\mathcal{C}
is as follows:

INPUT:

Two regular languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.

OUTPUT:

Is
L_{1}

\mathcal{C}
-separable from
L_{2}
?

We use separation as a mathematical tool whose purpose is to investigate classes of languages: given a fixed class
\mathcal{C}
, obtaining a
\mathcal{C}
-separation algorithm usually requires a solid understanding of
\mathcal{C}
. A typical objective when considering separation is to not only get an algorithm that decides it, but also a generic method for computing a separator, if it exists.

Remark 2.1
.

\mathcal{C}
-separation generalizes another classical decision problem:
\mathcal{C}
-membership which asks whether a single regular language
L
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, it is simple to verify that asking whether
L\in\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to asking whether
L
is
\mathcal{C}
-separable from its complement (in this case, the only candidate for being a separator is
L
itself).

Covering.
Our second problem is more general and was originally defined in
[
pzcovering
,
pzcovering2
]
. Given a language
L
, a
cover of
L
is a
finite
set of languages
\mathbf{K}
such that,

L\subseteq\bigcup_{K\in\mathbf{K}}K

Moreover, given a class
\mathcal{C}
, a
\mathcal{C}
-cover of
L
is a cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
such that all
K\in\mathbf{K}
belong to
\mathcal{C}
.

Covering takes as input a language
L
and a
finite set of languages

\mathbf{L}
. A
separating cover
for the pair
(L,\mathbf{L})
is a cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
such that for every
K\in\mathbf{K}
, there exists
L^{\prime}\in\mathbf{L}
which satisfies
K\cap L^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Finally, given a class
\mathcal{C}
, we say that the pair
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable when there exists a separating
\mathcal{C}
-cover. The
\mathcal{C}
-covering problem is now defined as follows:

INPUT:

A regular language
L
and a finite set of regular languages
\mathbf{L}
.

OUTPUT:

Is
(L,\mathbf{L})

\mathcal{C}
-coverable ?

Remark 2.2
.

This definition is slightly different from the one of
[
pzcovering2
]
in which
\mathbf{L}
is defined as a “multiset” of languages (in the sense that it may contain several instances of the same language). This is natural since each input language in
\mathbf{L}
is actually given by a recognizer (such as an NFA or a monoid morphism) and two distinct recognizers may define the same language. This change is harmless: it is immediate that the two definitions are equivalent.

Let us complete this definition by explaining why covering generalizes separation: the latter is special case of the former when the set
\mathbf{L}
is a singleton.

Fact 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a lattice and
L_{1},L_{2}
two languages. Then
L_{1}
is
\mathcal{C}
-separable from
L_{2}
, if and only if
(L_{1},\{L_{2}\})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable.

Proof.

Assume first that
L_{1}
is
\mathcal{C}
-separable from
L_{2}
and let
K\in\mathcal{C}
be a separator. Then, it is immediate that
\mathbf{K}=\{K\}
is a separating
\mathcal{C}
-cover for
(L_{1},\{L_{2}\})
. Conversely, assume that
(L_{1},\{L_{2}\})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable and let
\mathbf{K}
be a separating
\mathcal{C}
-cover. Moreover, let
K
be the union of all languages in
\mathbf{K}
. We have
K\in\mathcal{C}
since
\mathcal{C}
is a lattice. Clearly,
L_{1}\subseteq K
since
\mathbf{K}
was a cover of
L_{1}
. Moreover, we know that no language in
\mathbf{K}
intersects
L_{2}
since
\mathbf{K}
was separating. Thus,
L_{2}\cap K=\emptyset
which means that
K\in\mathcal{C}
separates
L_{1}
from
L_{2}
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

While covering is a natural generalization of separation, we consider it out of necessity. For the classes that we investigate, no “direct” separation algorithm is known. Our techniques require considering the covering problem.

2.3.
Finite lattices and stratifications

All classes that we consider in the paper are built from an arbitrary finite lattice (i.e. one that contains finitely many languages) using a generic construction process (presented in Section
3
). Consequently, finite lattices will be important in the paper. They have several convenient properties that we present here.

Canonical preorder relations.
Consider a finite lattice
\mathcal{C}
. One may associate a
canonical preorder relation over
A^{*}
to
\mathcal{C}
. The definition is as follows. Given
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
, we write
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
if and only if the following holds:

\text{For all $L\in\mathcal{C}$,}\quad w\in L\ \Rightarrow\ w^{\prime}\in L.

It is immediate from the definition that
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
is transitive and reflexive, making it a preorder.

Example 2.5
.

We let and as the class of languages consisting of all Boolean combinations of languages
A^{*}aA^{*}
, for some
a\in A
. Though this terminology is not standard, “ and ” stands for “alphabet testable”:
L\in\and
if and only if membership of a word
w
in
L
depends only on the set of letters occurring in
w
. Clearly, and is a finite Boolean algebra. In that case,
\leqslant_{\and}
is an equivalence relation which we denote by
\sim_{\and}
: one may verify that
w\sim_{\and}w^{\prime}
if and only if
w
and
w^{\prime}
have the same alphabet (i.e. contain the same set of letters).

The relation
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
has many applications. We start with an important lemma, which relies on the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is finite. We say that a language
L\subseteq A^{*}
is an
upper set
(for
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
) when for any two words
u,v\in A^{*}
, if
u\in L
and
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}v
, then
v\in L
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite lattice. Then, for any
L\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
L\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
L
is an upper set for
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
. In particular,
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
has finitely many upper sets.

Proof.

Assume first that
L\in\mathcal{C}
. Then, for all
w\in L
and all
w^{\prime}
such that
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
, we have
w^{\prime}\in L
by definition of
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence,
L
is an upper set. Assume now that
L
is an upper set. For any word
w
, we write
\uclos w
for the upper set
\uclos w=\{u\mid w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u\}
. By definition of
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}

\uclos w
is the intersection of all
L\in\mathcal{C}
such that
w\in L
. Therefore,
\uclos w\in\mathcal{C}
since
\mathcal{C}
is a finite lattice (and is therefore closed under intersection). Finally, since
L
is an upper set, we have,

L=\bigcup_{w\in L}\uclos w.

Hence, since
\mathcal{C}
is closed under union and is finite,
L
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

While Lemma
2.6
states an equivalence, we mainly use the left to right implication (or rather its contrapositive). One may apply it to show that a language does not belong to
\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, by the lemma, proving that
L\not\in\mathcal{C}
is the same as proving that
L
is not an upper set for
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
. In other words, one needs to exhibit
u,v\in A^{*}
such that
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}v
,

Let us complete these definitions with a few additional useful results. First, as we observed for and in Example

is actually a Boolean algebra, it turns out that

is an equivalence relation, which we shall denote by

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite Boolean algebra. Then for any alphabet
A
, the canonical preorder
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
is an equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
which admits the following direct definition :

w\sim_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
if and only if for all
L\in\mathcal{C}
,
w\in L\ \Leftrightarrow\ w^{\prime}\in L

Thus, for any
L\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
L\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
L
is a union of
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-classes. In particular,
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
has finite index.

Proof.

It is clear that when
w\sim_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
, we have
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
as well. We prove the reverse implication. Let
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
be such that
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
. Let
L\in\mathcal{C}
and observe that by closure under complement, we know that
A^{*}\setminus L\in\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, by definition of
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}w\in L&\Rightarrow&w^{\prime}\in L\\
w\in A^{*}\setminus L&\Rightarrow&w^{\prime}\in A^{*}\setminus L\end{array}

One may now combine the first implication with the contrapositive of the second, which yields
w\in L\ \Leftrightarrow\ w^{\prime}\in L
. We conclude that
w\sim_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
:
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
are the same relation.
∎

Another important and useful property is that when
\mathcal{C}
is quotienting, the canonical preorder
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
is compatible with word concatenation.

Lemma 2.9
.

A finite lattice
\mathcal{C}
is quotienting if and only if its associated canonical preorder
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
is compatible with word concatenation. That is, for any words

,

u\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad v\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}v^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad uv\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}.

Proof.

First assume that
\mathcal{C}
is closed under quotients and let
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}
be four words such that
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{\prime}
and
v\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}v^{\prime}
. We have to prove that
uv\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
. Let
L\in\mathcal{C}
and assume that
uv\in L
. We use closure under left quotients to prove that
uv^{\prime}\in L
and then closure under right quotients to prove that
u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\in L
which terminates the proof of this direction. Since
uv\in L
, we have
v\in u^{-1}\cdot L
. By closure under left quotients, we have
u^{-1}\cdot L\in\mathcal{C}
, hence, since
v\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}v^{\prime}
, we obtain that
v^{\prime}\in u^{-1}\cdot L
and therefore that
uv^{\prime}\in L
. It now follows that
u\in L\cdot(v^{\prime})^{-1}
. Using closure under right quotients, we obtain that
L\cdot(v^{\prime})^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, since
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{\prime}
, we conclude that
u^{\prime}\in L\cdot(v^{\prime})^{-1}
which means that
u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\in L
, as desired.

Conversely, assume that
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
is a precongruence. Let
L\in\mathcal{C}
and
w\in A^{*}
, we prove that
w^{-1}\cdot L\in\mathcal{C}
(the proof for right quotients is symmetrical). By Lemma
2.6
, we have to prove that
w^{-1}\cdot L
is an upper set. Let
u\in w^{-1}\cdot L
and
u^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{\prime}
. Since
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
is a precongruence, we have
wu\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}wu^{\prime}
. Hence, since
L
is an upper set (it belongs to
\mathcal{C}
) and
wu\in L
, we have
wu^{\prime}\in L
. We conclude that
u^{\prime}\in w^{-1}\cdot L
, which terminates the proof.
∎

Stratifications.
While the above notions are useful, the downside is that they only apply to
finite
lattices. However, it is possible to lift their benefits to infinite classes with the notion of
stratification
. Consider an arbitrary
infinite
quotienting lattice
\mathcal{C}
. A

of
\mathcal{C}
is an infinite sequence
\mathcal{C}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k},\dots
of

quotienting lattices such that,

\text{For all $k$,}\quad\mathcal{C}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{k+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{k\in{\rm Nature}}\mathcal{C}_{k}.

The point here is that once we have a stratification of
\mathcal{C}
in hand, we may now associate a canonical preorder
\leqslant_{k}
to each stratum
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Proving that a language

does not belong to
\mathcal{C}
now amounts to proving that it does not belong to any stratum
\mathcal{C}_{k}
: for all

, one needs to exhibit

such that
u\leqslant_{k}v
,
u\in L
and
v\not\in L
.

Concatenation hierarchies and main theorem

In this section, we present the classes that we investigate in the paper. We actually consider a family of classes which are all

We start by defining the family of classes that we consider. Then, we present the main theorem of the paper: covering (and therefore separation as well) is decidable for any class in the family. We illustrate this theorem by discussing its consequences and presenting the most prominent classes to which it applies.

Closure operations

Our generic theorem states that covering is decidable for any class which is built from a

Lemma 3.1

For any quotienting lattice

First-order logic and quantifier alternation

We finish the section by discussing the connection with quantifier alternation hierarchies.

Let us briefly recall the definition of first-order logic over words. One may view a finite word as a logical structure made of a sequence of positions. Each position carries a label in the alphabet

s\in S
,
s^{\omega}
is an idempotent.

A
monoid

M
is a semigroup in which there exists a neutral element denoted
1_{M}
. Observe that
A^{*}
is a monoid with concatenation as the multiplication and
\varepsilon
as the neutral element. Hence, given a monoid
M
, we may consider morphisms
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
(i.e.
\alpha(\varepsilon)=1_{M}
and
\alpha(uv)=\alpha(u)\cdot\alpha(v)
for all
u,v\in A^{*}
). Given a morphism
\alpha
, we say that a language
L\subseteq A^{*}
is
recognized
by
\alpha
if there exists
F\subseteq M
such that
L=\alpha^{-1}(F)
. We call
F
the accepting set of
L
. It is well-known that a language
L\subseteq A^{*}
is regular if and only if it is recognized by a morphism into a
finite
monoid. Moreover, one may compute such a morphism from any representation of
L
(such as an automaton).

Remark 4.1
.

For all morphisms
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
that we consider in the paper,
M
will be finite. Hence, for the sake of avoiding clutter, we make this implicit: whenever we say that “
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
is a morphism”, this implicitly means that
M
is a finite monoid.

\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphisms.
We complete these definitions with an additional notion which is specific to the paper. Our main objective is to prove Theorem
3.7
: we want an algorithm for
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering where
\mathcal{C}
is an arbitrary finite quotienting Boolean algebra. This will require working with morphisms which satisfy a specific property called
\mathcal{C}
-compatibility
. We fix the finite quotienting Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
for the definition.

Recall that since
\mathcal{C}
is a finite Boolean algebra, one may associate a canonical equivalence
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
over
A^{*}
to
\mathcal{C}
. Two words are equivalent when they belong to the same languages in
\mathcal{C}
:

w\sim_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\forall L\in\mathcal{C},\ w\in L\Leftrightarrow w^{\prime}\in L.

Given a word
w
, we denote by
[w]_{\mathcal{C}}
its
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-equivalence class. Recall that by Lemma
2.8
,
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
has finite index and the languages in
\mathcal{C}
are exactly the unions of
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-classes. Moreover, since
\mathcal{C}
is quotienting, we know from Lemma
2.9
that
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
is a congruence for word concatenation. It follows that the quotient set
{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
is a finite monoid and the map
w\mapsto[w]_{\mathcal{C}}
is a morphism from
A^{*}
to
{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
.

We may now define
\mathcal{C}
-compatibility. Consider an arbitrary morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
. We say that
\alpha
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible
when for any
s\in M
, there exists a
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class denoted by
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\alpha^{-1}(s)\subseteq[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
(i.e.
[w]_{\mathcal{C}}=[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
for any
w\in A^{*}
such that
\alpha(w)=s
).

Remark 4.2
.

Given
s\in M
, the
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
is fully determined by
\alpha
when
\alpha^{-1}(s)\neq\emptyset
(
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}=[w]_{\mathcal{C}}
for any
w\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
). If
\alpha^{-1}(s)=\emptyset
, we may choose any
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class as
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
. When we consider a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism, we implicitly assume that the map
s\mapsto[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
is fixed.

We now prove that for any regular language
L
, one may compute a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism which recognizes
L
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given a regular language
L\subseteq A^{*}
, one may compute a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
recognizing
L
.

Proof.

Since
L
is regular, we may compute a finite monoid
N
and a morphism
\beta:A^{*}\to N
(not necessarily
\mathcal{C}
-compatible) recognizing
L
. Since we know that the quotient set
{A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}}
is a finite monoid, the Cartesian product
M=N\times({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{C}}})
is a finite monoid for the componentwise multiplication. It now suffices to define the morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
by
\alpha(w)=(\beta(w),[w]_{\mathcal{C}})
for any
w\in A^{*}
. Clearly,
\alpha
is a morphism which recognizes
L
and one may verify that it is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible.
∎

4.2.
Factorization forests

The factorization theorem of Simon is a combinatorial result which applies to
finite semigroups
. Here, we briefly recall this theorem. We refer the reader to
[
kfacto
,
bfacto
,
cfacto
]
for more details and a proof.

Consider a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\rightarrow M
. An
\alpha
-factorization forest
is an ordered unranked rooted tree whose nodes are labeled by words in
A^{*}
. For any inner node
x
with label
w\in A^{*}
, if
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in A^{*}
are the labels of its children listed from left to right, then
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
. Moreover, all nodes
x
in the forest must be of the three following kinds:

•

Leaves
which are labeled by either a single letter or
the empty word.

•

Binary inner nodes
which have exactly two children.

•

Idempotent inner nodes
which have three or more children. The labels
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
of these children must satisfy
\alpha(w_{1})=\cdots=\alpha(w_{n})=e
where
e
is an idempotent element of
M
.

Given a word
w\in A^{*}
, an
\alpha
-factorization forest for
w
is an
\alpha
-factorization forest whose root is labeled by
w
. The
height
of a factorization forest is the largest
h\in{\rm Nature}
such that it contains a branch with
h
inner nodes (a single leaf has height
0
). We shall also consider the notion of
idempotent height
. The idempotent height of an
\alpha
-factorization forest is the largest number
m\in{\rm Nature}
such that there exists a branch containing
m
idempotent nodes in the forest. We shall use the following notations. Let
s\in M
and
h,m\in{\rm Nature}
:

(1)

F^{\alpha}(s,h,m)\subseteq A^{*}
denotes the language of all words
w\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
admitting an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
.

(2)

F^{\alpha}_{B}(s,h,m)\subseteq A^{*}
denotes the language of all words
w\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
admitting an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
, of idempotent height at most
m
and whose root is a
binary node
.

(3)

F^{\alpha}_{I}(s,h,m)\subseteq A^{*}
denotes the language of all words
w\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
admitting an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
, of idempotent height at most
m
and whose root is a
idempotent node
.

We have the following fact.

Fact 4.4
.

For every morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\rightarrow M
, every
s\in M
and every
h,m\in{\rm Nature}
, we have,

F^{\alpha}(s,h,m)=F^{\alpha}_{B}(s,h,m)\cup F^{\alpha}_{I}(s,h,m)\cup F^{\alpha}(s,0,0)

Proof.

The right to left inclusion is immediate by definition. For the converse inclusion, consider
w\in F^{\alpha}(s,h,m)
. By definition,
w\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
and admits an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
. The root of this forest is either a binary node, an idempotent node or a leaf. In the first case,
w\in F^{\alpha}_{B}(s,h,m)
. In the second one,
w\in F^{\alpha}_{I}(s,h,m)
. Finally, the third case implies that
w\in F^{\alpha}(s,0,0)
. ∎

We turn to the factorization forest theorem of Simon: there exists a bound depending only on
M
such that any word admits an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most this bound.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
simonfacto
,
kfacto
]
)
.

Consider a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\rightarrow M
. For all words
w\in A^{*}
, there exists an
\alpha
-factorization forest for
w
of height at most
3|M|-1
.

Observe that an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.5
is that for any
s\in M
, we have
\alpha^{-1}(s)=F^{\alpha}(s,3|M|-1,3|M|-1)
. We shall use Theorem
4.5
conjointly with a second result on factorization forests which takes the idempotent height into account. Given a word
w\in A^{*}
, an
infix
of
w
is a second word
u\in A^{*}
such that
w=v_{1}uv_{2}
for some
v_{1},v_{2}\in A^{*}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Consider a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and let
h,m\in{\rm Nature}
. Let
w\in A^{*}
admitting an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
. Then any infix of
w
admits an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h+2
and idempotent height at most
m
.

Let us prove Proposition
4.6
. The argument is an induction on the
\alpha
-factorization forest of
w
: we show that it may be “repaired” into another forest for some infix of
w
without adding any idempotent node. We first consider the special case of prefixes and suffixes.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
h,m\in{\rm Nature}
and consider a word
w\in A^{*}
admitting an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
. Then any prefix or suffix of
w
admits an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h+1
and idempotent height at most
m
.

Observe that any infix of a word
w\in A^{*}
is by definition the prefix of a suffix of
w
. Hence, Proposition
4.6
is obtained by applying Lemma
4.7
twice (once for prefixes and once for suffixes). Therefore, we may concentrate on proving Lemma
4.7
. We only treat the case of prefixes (the argument for suffixes is symmetrical).

Let
w\in A^{*}
admitting an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
. We shall denote this forest by
\mathcal{F}
. Consider a prefix
u
of
w
. We construct an
\alpha
-factorization forest for
u
using structural induction on
\mathcal{F}
. There are three cases depending on the root node of
\mathcal{F}
. If the root of
\mathcal{F}
is a leaf node, then
w=a
or
w=\varepsilon
. In particular,
0\leq h
and
0\leq m
. Since
u
is a prefix of
w
, we have
u=a
or
u=\varepsilon
. Hence
u
admits an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height
0\leq h
and idempotent height
0\leq m
.

If the root of
\mathcal{F}
is a binary node, it has two children labeled with words
w_{1},w_{2}\in A^{*}
such that
w=w_{1}w_{2}
. Moreover,
w_{1},w_{2}
admit
\alpha
-factorization forests of heights at most
h-1
and idempotent heights at most
m
. Since
u
is a prefix of
w=w_{1}w_{2}
by hypothesis, there are two possibles cases. First,
u
may be a prefix of
w_{1}
. In this case, it suffices to apply induction to the forest of
w_{1}
to get the desired forest for
u
. Otherwise, there exists a prefix
u^{\prime}
of
w_{2}
such that
u=w_{1}u^{\prime}
. We may apply induction to the forest of
w_{2}
to get a forest of height at most
h-1+1=h
and idempotent height at most
m
for
u^{\prime}
. Using one binary node, one may then combine the forests of
w_{1}
and
u^{\prime}
into a single forest for
u=w_{1}u^{\prime}
. By construction, this new forest has height at most
h+1
and idempotent height at most
m
.

We finish with the case when the root of
\mathcal{F}
is an idempotent node. Its children are labeled with words
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
such that
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
and
\alpha(w_{1})=\cdots=\alpha(w_{n})=e
for some idempotent
e\in M
. Moreover, the words
w_{1},\cdots,w_{n}
admit
\alpha
-factorization forests of heights at most
h-1
and idempotent heights at most
m-1
. Let
i
be the smallest natural such that
u
is a prefix of
w_{1}\cdots w_{i}
. If
i=1
,
u
is a prefix of
w_{1}
and it suffices to apply induction to the forest of
w_{1}
to get the desired forest for
u
. Otherwise, there exists a prefix
u^{\prime}
of
w_{i}
such that
u=w_{1}\cdots w_{i-1}u^{\prime}
. We know that,

•

u^{\prime}
admits an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
(h-1)+1=h
and idempotent height at most
m-1
(this is by induction).

•

w_{1}\cdots w_{i-1}
admit an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
(the root is an idempotent node whose children are labeled with
w_{1},\dots,w_{i-1}
).

These two forests can be combined into a single forest for
u
with one binary node. By definition, this forest has height at most
h+1
and idempotent height at most
m
.

4.3.
A stratification for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})

We turn to the second mathematical tool that we shall need: a stratification for classes of the form
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
when
\mathcal{C}
is a finite quotienting lattice.

Remark 4.8
.

We do not present a stratification for
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
(even if our ultimate objective is to solve
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering). Indeed, it turns out that the stratification of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
that we present here suffices to handle both
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
and
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
.

We fix an arbitrary finite quotienting lattice
\mathcal{C}
and define a finite quotienting lattice
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
for each
k\in{\rm Nature}
. The definition uses induction on
k
and counts the number of marked concatenations that are necessary to define each language in
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
.

•

When
k=0
, we simply define
Pol_{0}(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}
.

•

When
k\geq 1
,
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
is the least lattice such that:

(1)

Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
.

(2)

For any
a\in A
and
L_{1},L_{2}\in Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})
, we have
L_{1}aL_{2}\in Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
.

This concludes the definition. Since
\mathcal{C}
was a finite lattice, it is immediate that all classes
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
are finite lattices as well. Moreover, by Theorem
3.2
, we indeed have,

\text{For all $k\in{\rm Nature}$, }Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq Pol_{k+1}(\mathcal{C})\quad\text{and}\quad{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})=\bigcup_{k\in{\rm Nature}}Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})

Finally, it is straightforward to verify that all classes
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
are quotienting (the argument is identical to the one used for proving that the whole class
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
is quotienting, see Lemma 27 in
[
pzgenconcat
]
for a proof). Thus, we did define a stratification of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. We now prove a few properties of this stratification that we shall need.

Consider the canonical preorder relations associated to the strata. For any
k\in{\rm Nature}
, we denote by
\leqslant_{k}
the preorder associated to
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
. Recall that for any
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
, we have,

w\leqslant_{k}w^{\prime}\quad\text{ if and only if}\quad\text{for all languages $L\in Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})$,\ $w\in L\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\in L$}

We showed in Lemma
2.6
that the languages in
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
are exactly the upper sets for the relation
\leqslant_{k}
. Moreover, since the lattices
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
are quotienting, we know from Lemma
2.9
that the relations
\leqslant_{k}
are compatible with word concatenation.

We present two specific properties of the preorders
\leqslant_{k}
. We start with an alternate definition of
\leqslant_{k}
which is easier to manipulate when proving these two properties. Recall that we write
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
for the canonical preorder associated to the finite quotienting lattice
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
k\in{\rm Nature}
. For any
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
, we have
w\leqslant_{k}w^{\prime}
if and only if the two following properties hold:

(1)

w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}

(2)

If
k\geq 1
, for any decomposition
w=uav
with
u,v\in A^{*}
and
a\in A
, there exist
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
w^{\prime}=u^{\prime}av^{\prime}
,
u\leqslant_{k-1}u^{\prime}
and
v\leqslant_{k-1}v^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
w\leqslant_{k}w^{\prime}
. We have to prove that the two items in the lemma hold. For the first item, observe that by definition,
\mathcal{C}\subseteq Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
. Therefore,
w\leqslant_{k}w^{\prime}\Rightarrow w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
. We turn to the second item. Assume that
k\geq 1
and consider a decomposition
w=uav
of
w
. We have to find an appropriate decomposition of
w^{\prime}
. Let
K_{u}=\{u^{\prime}\in A^{*}\mid u\leqslant_{k-1}u^{\prime}\}
and
K_{v}=\{v^{\prime}\in A^{*}\mid v\leqslant_{k-1}v^{\prime}\}
. By definition
K_{u},K_{v}
are upper sets for
\leqslant_{k-1}
and it follows from Lemma
2.6
that
K_{u},H_{v}\in Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})
. Hence,
K_{u}aK_{v}\in Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
by definition. Moreover, since
w=uav\in K_{u}aK_{v}
and
w\leqslant_{k}w^{\prime}
, it follows that
w^{\prime}\in K_{u}aK_{v}
. Therefore, we obtain
u^{\prime}\in K_{u}
and
v^{\prime}\in K_{v}
such that
w^{\prime}=u^{\prime}av^{\prime}
. It is then immediate by definition of
K_{u}
and
K_{v}
that
u\leqslant_{k-1}u^{\prime}
and
v\leqslant_{k-1}v^{\prime}
.

We turn to the other direction. Let
k\in{\rm Nature}
. Assuming that the two assertions in the lemma hold for
k
, we prove that
w\leqslant_{k}w^{\prime}
. The argument is an induction using induction on
k
. When
k=0
, this is immediate from the first item since
Pol_{0}(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}
(and therefore,
\leqslant_{0}
and
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
are the same relation). We now assume that
k\geq 1
. Let
L\in Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
, we have to prove that
w\in L\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\in L
. By definition,
L
is constructed by applying finitely many unions and intersections to the two following kinds of languages:

(1)

Languages in
Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})
.

(2)

Languages of the form
L_{1}aL_{2}
with
L_{1},L_{2}\in Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})
.

We use a sub-induction on this construction.

•

When
L\in Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})
the implication is immediate. Since
\leqslant_{k}
is finer than
\leqslant_{k-1}
, the assertions in the lemma also hold for
k-1
. We get
w\leqslant_{k-1}w^{\prime}
by induction on
k
.

•

Assume now that
L=L_{1}aL_{2}
with
L_{1},L_{2}\in Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})
. If
w\in L=L_{1}aL_{2}
, then it admits a decomposition
w=uav
with
u\in L_{1}
and
v\in L_{2}
. By the second item, we obtain
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
w^{\prime}=u^{\prime}av^{\prime}
,
u\leqslant_{k-1}u^{\prime}
and
v\leqslant_{k-1}v^{\prime}
. In particular, since
L_{1},L_{2}\in Pol_{k-1}(\mathcal{C})
, it follows by definition of
\leqslant_{k-1}
that
u^{\prime}\in L_{1}
and
v^{\prime}\in L_{2}
, i.e.
w^{\prime}=u^{\prime}av^{\prime}\in L_{1}aL_{2}=L
.

•

Finally, if
L=L_{1}\cup L_{2}
or
L=L_{1}\cap L_{2}
, induction yields that
w\in L_{1}\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\in L_{1}
and
w\in L_{2}\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\in L_{2}
and therefore,
w\in L\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\in L
.

This terminates the proof of Lemma
4.9
.
∎

We now use Lemma
4.9
to present and prove two characteristic properties of the preorders
\leqslant_{k}
which we shall use multiple times. Let us first present the following fact which introduces a characteristic natural number of
\mathcal{C}
that is involved in both properties.

Fact 4.10
.

There exists a natural number
p\geq 1
such that for any word
u\in A^{*}
and natural numbers
m,m^{\prime}\geq 1
, we have
u^{pm}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{pm^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Let
\sim
be the equivalence generated by
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
. That is, for any
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
,
w\sim w^{\prime}
if and only if
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}w
. We know from Lemmas
2.6
and
2.9
that
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
has finitely many upper sets and is compatible with word concatenation. Therefore,
\sim
is a congruence of finite index for word concatenation. It follows that the quotient set
{A^{*}}/{\sim}
is a finite monoid. We let
p=\omega({A^{*}}/{\sim})
(the idempotent power of
{A^{*}}/{\sim}
). The fact is now immediate.
∎

We shall call the natural number
p\geq 1
described in Fact
4.10
the
period of the quotienting lattice
\mathcal{C}

. We are now ready to state the first of our two properties.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
p
be the period of
\mathcal{C}
and
k\in{\rm Nature}
. Then, for any
m,m^{\prime}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
and any word
u\in A^{*}
, we have
u^{pm}\leqslant_{k}u^{pm^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Let
m,m^{\prime}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
and
u
some word, we prove that
u^{pm}\leqslant_{k}u^{pm^{\prime}}
. This amounts to proving that the two items in Lemma
4.9
hold. The argument is an induction on
k
. For the first item, it suffices to prove that
u^{pm}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{pm^{\prime}}
. This is immediate by choice of
p
in Fact
4.10
. This concludes the case
k=0
.

We now consider the second item (which may only happen when
k\geq 1
). Consider a decomposition
u^{pm}=w_{1}aw_{2}
. We have to find a decomposition
u^{pm^{\prime}}=w^{\prime}_{1}aw^{\prime}_{2}
such that
w_{1}\leqslant_{k-1}w^{\prime}_{1}
and
w_{2}\leqslant_{k-1}w^{\prime}_{2}
. By definition, the letter
a
in the decomposition
u^{pm}=w_{1}aw_{2}
falls within some factor
u^{p}
of
u^{pm}
. Let us refine the decomposition to isolate this factor. We have
u^{pm}=u^{pm_{1}}v_{1}av_{2}u^{pm_{2}}
where,

•

m=m_{1}+1+m_{2}

•

v_{1}av_{2}=u^{p}
.

•

u^{pm_{1}}v_{1}=w_{1}
and
v_{2}u^{pm_{2}}=w_{2}
.

Since
m\geq 2^{k+1}-1
by hypothesis and
m=m_{1}+1+m_{2}
, either
m_{1}\geq 2^{k}-1
or
m_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
(possibly both). By symmetry, let us assume that
m_{1}\geq 2^{k}-1
. We use the following claim.

Claim
.

There exist
m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2}\geq 1
such that
m^{\prime}=m^{\prime}_{1}+1+m^{\prime}_{2}
,
u^{pm_{1}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}
and
u^{pm_{2}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
.

Proof.

There are two cases depending on whether
m_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
or not. Assume first that,
m_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
. Since
m^{\prime}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
, we may choose
m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
such that
m^{\prime}=m^{\prime}_{1}+1+m^{\prime}_{2}
. It is now immediate from induction on
k
that
u^{pm_{1}}\leqslant_{k-1}{n}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}
and
u^{pm_{2}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
. Otherwise,
m_{2}<2^{k}-1
. We let
m^{\prime}_{2}=m_{2}
and
m^{\prime}_{1}=m^{\prime}-1-m^{\prime}_{2}
. Clearly,
m^{\prime}_{1}\geq 2^{k}-1
since
m^{\prime}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
. Hence, we get
u^{pm_{1}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}
from induction on
k
. Furthermore,
u^{pm_{2}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
is immediate since
m_{2}=m^{\prime}_{2}
by definition.
∎

We may now finish the proof of Item 2. Let
m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2}\geq 1
be as defined in the claim. We let
w^{\prime}_{1}=u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}v_{1}
and
w^{\prime}_{2}=v_{2}u^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
. Clearly,
w^{\prime}_{1}aw^{\prime}_{2}=u^{pm^{\prime}}
since
v_{1}av_{2}=u^{p}
and
m^{\prime}=m^{\prime}_{1}+1+m^{\prime}_{2}
. Moreover, since
\leqslant_{k-1}
is compatible with multiplication, we have

\begin{array}[]{rll}w_{1}=u^{pm_{1}}v_{1}&\leqslant_{k-1}&u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}v_{1}=w^{\prime}_{1}\\
w_{2}=v_{2}u^{pm_{2}}&\leqslant_{k-1}&v_{2}u^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}=w^{\prime}_{2}\end{array}

This terminates the proof of Item 2.
∎

We turn to the second lemma which states a characteristic property of classes built with polynomial closure.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
p
be the period of
\mathcal{C}
and
k\in{\rm Nature}
. Let
u,v\in A^{*}
such that
u^{p}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}v
. Then, for any
m,m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
, we have
u^{pm}\leqslant_{k}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma
4.11
. Let
k\in{\rm Nature}
,
u,v
satisfying
v\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{p}
, and
m,m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
. We prove that
u^{pm}\leqslant_{k}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
. This amounts to proving that the two items in Lemma
4.9
hold. The argument is an induction on
k
.

For Item 1, we prove that
u^{pm}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
. By hypothesis on
u,v
, we know that
u^{p}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}v
. Therefore, since
\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}
is compatible with concatenation, we get that
u^{p(m^{\prime}_{1}+1+m^{\prime}_{2})}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
. Finally, we obtain by choice of
p
in Fact
4.10
that
u^{pm}\leqslant_{\mathcal{C}}u^{p(m^{\prime}_{1}+1+m^{\prime}_{2})}
. This finishes the proof of Item 1 (and the case
k=0
) by transitivity.

We now consider the second item (which may only happen when
k\geq 1
). Consider a decomposition
u^{pm}=w_{1}aw_{2}
. We have to find a decomposition
u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}=w^{\prime}_{1}aw^{\prime}_{2}
such that
w_{1}\leqslant_{k-1}w^{\prime}_{1}
and
w_{2}\leqslant_{k-1}w^{\prime}_{2}
. By definition, the letter
a
in the decomposition
u^{pm}=w_{1}aw_{2}
falls within some factor
u^{p}
of
u^{pm}
. Let us refine the decomposition to isolate this factor. We have
u^{pm}=u^{pm_{1}}v_{1}av_{2}u^{pm_{2}}
where,

•

m=m_{1}+1+m_{2}

•

v_{1}av_{2}=u^{p}
.

•

u^{pm_{1}}v_{1}=w_{1}
and
v_{2}u^{pm_{2}}=w_{2}
.

Since
m\geq 2^{k+1}-1
by hypothesis and
m=m_{1}+1+m_{2}
, either
m_{1}\geq 2^{k}-1
or
m_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
(possibly both). By symmetry, let us assume that
m_{1}\geq 2^{k}-1
. We use the following claim.

Claim
.

There exist
\ell^{\prime}_{1},\ell^{\prime}_{2}\in{\rm Nature}
such that
m^{\prime}_{2}=\ell^{\prime}_{1}+1+\ell^{\prime}_{2}
,
u^{pm_{1}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{p\ell^{\prime}_{1}}
and
u^{pm_{2}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{p\ell^{\prime}_{2}}
.

Proof.

There are two cases depending on whether
m_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
or not. Assume first that,
m_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
. Since
m^{\prime}_{2}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
, we may choose
\ell^{\prime}_{1},\ell^{\prime}_{2}\geq 2^{k}-1
such that
m^{\prime}_{2}=\ell^{\prime}_{1}+1+\ell^{\prime}_{2}
. That
u^{pm_{1}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{p\ell^{\prime}_{1}}
follows from induction on
k
. Moreover, we know that the inequality
u^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{p\ell^{\prime}_{2}}
holds thanks to Lemma
4.11
.

Otherwise,
m_{2}<2^{k}-1
. We let
\ell^{\prime}_{2}=m_{2}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{1}=m^{\prime}_{2}-1-\ell^{\prime}_{2}
. Clearly,
\ell^{\prime}_{1}\geq 2^{k}-1
since
m^{\prime}_{2}\geq 2^{k+1}-1
. Hence, we get
u^{pm_{1}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{p\ell^{\prime}_{1}}
from induction on
k
. Furthermore,
u^{pm_{2}}\leqslant_{k-1}u^{p\ell^{\prime}_{2}}
is immediate since
m_{2}=\ell^{\prime}_{2}
by definition. ∎

We may now finish the proof of Item 2. Let
\ell^{\prime}_{1},\ell^{\prime}_{2}\geq 1
be as defined in the claim. We let
w^{\prime}_{1}=u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{p\ell^{\prime}_{1}}v_{1}
and
w^{\prime}_{2}=v_{2}u^{p\ell^{\prime}_{2}}
. Clearly,
w^{\prime}_{1}aw^{\prime}_{2}=u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{pm^{\prime}_{2}}
since
v_{1}av_{2}=u^{p}
and
m_{2}^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}_{1}+1+\ell^{\prime}_{2}
. Moreover, since
\leqslant_{k-1}
is compatible with multiplication, we have

\begin{array}[]{rll}w_{1}=u^{pm_{1}}v_{1}&\leqslant_{k-1}&u^{pm^{\prime}_{1}}vu^{p\ell^{\prime}_{1}}v_{1}=w^{\prime}_{1}\\
w_{2}=v_{2}u^{pm_{2}}&\leqslant_{k-1}&v_{2}u^{p\ell^{\prime}_{2}}=w^{\prime}_{2}\end{array}

This terminates the proof of Item 2.
∎

5.
Framework: rating maps and optimal covers

We now present the general framework that we use for formulating our covering algorithms. The notions that we introduce here were originally designed in
[
pzcovering2
]
and we only recall what we need for presenting our results. We refer the reader to
[
pzcovering2
]
for details and background on these notions.

Given a lattice
\mathcal{D}
, our input for the
\mathcal{D}
-covering problem is a pair
(L,\mathbf{L})
where
L
is a regular language and
\mathbf{L}
a finite set of regular languages: we want to know whether there exists a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
which is separating for
\mathbf{L}
. The key idea behind our framework is to replace the set
\mathbf{L}
by a (more general) algebraic object called
multiplicative rating map
. Intuitively, multiplicative rating maps are designed to measure the quality of
\mathcal{D}
-covers. Given a multiplicative rating map
\rho
and a language
L
, we use
\rho
to rank the existing
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
L
. This leads to the definition of “
optimal
”
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
. We are able to reformulate our problem with these notions. The key idea is that instead of deciding whether
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable, we compute an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover for
L
for a multiplicative rating map
\rho
that we build from
\mathbf{L}
. We have two motivations for relying on this approach,

(1)

It yields elegant formulations for covering algorithms. Beyond the two that we present in the paper, we refer the reader to
[
pzcovering2
]
for more examples.

(2)

More importantly, recall that our main goal in the paper is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering. As we already explained, this will require to first prove a preliminary result for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
which is stronger than the decidability of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering (this is why we reprove the result of
[
pzboolpol
]
for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering along the way). The framework presented here is exactly what we need in order to precisely formulate this stronger result.

We start by defining multiplicative rating maps. Then, we explain how they are used to measure the quality of a cover and define optimal covers. Finally, we connect these notions to the covering problem. Let us point out that several statements presented here are without proof, we refer the reader to
[
pzcovering2
]
for these proofs.

5.1.
Multiplicative rating maps

In order to present multiplicative rating maps, we need to introduce a new algebraic structure:
hemirings
. A hemiring is a set
R
equipped with two binary operations called addition (“
+
”) and multiplication (“
\cdot
”) respectively. Moreover, the following axioms have to be satisfied:

•

R
is a commutative monoid for addition. The neutral element is denoted by
0_{R}
.

•

R
is a semigroup for multiplication.

•

Multiplication distributes over addition: for all
r,s,t\in R
we have,

\begin{array}[]{lll}t\cdot(r+s)&=&(t\cdot r)+(t\cdot s)\\
(r+s)\cdot t&=&(r\cdot t)+(s\cdot t)\end{array}

•

0_{R}
is a zero for the multiplication: for any
r\in R
:

0_{R}\cdot r=r\cdot 0_{R}=0_{R}

Remark 5.1
.

Hemirings are a generalization of the more standard notion of semiring which additionally asks for the multiplication to have a neutral element.

Finally, a hemiring
R
is
idempotent
when all elements are idempotents for addition: for all
r\in R
, we have
r+r=r
. In the paper, we only work with idempotent hemirings. Observe that when
R
is such a hemiring, one may define a
canonical partial order
on
R
. Given
r,s\in R
, we shall write
r\leq s
when
s+r=s
. One may verify that this is indeed a partial order (the fact that
R
is idempotent is required here) which is compatible with both addition and multiplication.

Example 5.2
.

The most simple example of an idempotent hemiring (which is crucial here) is the set
2^{A^{*}}
of all languages over
A
. Indeed, it suffices to use union as the addition (the neutral element is
\emptyset
) and language concatenation as the multiplication. Observe that the canonical partial order is inclusion (
L\subseteq H
if and only if
H\cup L=H
). In fact,
2^{A^{*}}
has even more structure: it is a semiring (
\{\varepsilon\}
is neutral for multiplication).

We may now define multiplicative rating maps. We call a hemiring morphism
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
into a
finite
idempotent hemiring
R
a
multiplicative rating map
. Specifically,
\rho
has to satisfy the following axioms:

(1)

\rho(\emptyset)=0_{R}
.

(2)

For any
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
,
\rho(K_{1}\cup K_{2})=\rho(K_{1})+\rho(K_{2})
.

(3)

For any
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
,
\rho(K_{1}K_{2})=\rho(K_{1})\cdot\rho(K_{2})
.

Remark 5.3
.

In
[
pzcovering2
]
, the definition of multiplicative rating maps is slightly more restrictive:
R
must be a finite idempotent semiring and
\rho
must be a semiring morphism. This change is harmless and could actually be avoided: working with semirings suffices to make the connection with covering as seen in
[
pzcovering2
]
. We make it for convenience: the proof of our
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering algorithm (in Section
7
) involves auxiliary multiplicative rating maps and allowing hemirings simplifies their presentation (see Remark
7.3
).

For the sake of improved readability, when applying a multiplicative rating map
\rho
to a singleton language
K=\{w\}
, we shall write
\rho(w)
for
\rho(\{w\})
. Note that
\rho
is increasing for the canonical orders on
2^{A^{*}}
and
R
(this is true for any morphism of idempotent hemirings).

Fact 5.4
.

Consider a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Then, for any two languages
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, the following property holds,
K_{1}\subseteq K_{2}\Rightarrow\rho(K_{1})\leq\rho(K_{2})
.

\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating maps.
We lift the notion of
\mathcal{C}
-compatibility to multiplicative rating maps. Consider a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and a finite quotienting Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
. We say that
\rho
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible when for any
r\in R
, there exists a
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class
[r]_{\mathcal{C}}
such that for any
w\in A^{*}
satisfying
\rho(w)=r
, we have
w\in[r]_{\mathcal{C}}
. When we manipulate
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating maps, we shall implicitly assume that the map
r\mapsto[r]_{\mathcal{C}}
is fixed.

Remark 5.5
.

This definition is distinct from the one of
[
pzcovering2
]
. We make this choice because our definition is simpler and suffices for presenting our results. However, this is harmless: one may show that the two definitions coincide for nice multiplicative rating maps (defined below) and this is the only situation in which we use
\mathcal{C}
-compatibility here.

Nice multiplicative rating maps.
We now define a special class of multiplicative rating maps which is crucial: it is used for the connection with covering. Consider a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. We say that
\rho
is
nice
if for any language
K\subseteq A^{*}
, there exist words
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in K
such that,

\rho(K)=\rho(w_{1})+\cdots+\rho(w_{n})

Remark 5.6
.

There exist multiplicative rating maps which are not nice. For example, let
R=\{0,1,2\}
be the semiring equipped with the following addition and multiplication. For
i,j\in R
, we let
i+j=max(i,j)
,
0\cdot i=i\cdot 0=0
,
1\cdot i=i\cdot 1=i
and
2\cdot 2=2
. Moreover, consider the multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
defined
\rho(\emptyset)=0
,
\rho(K)=1
when
K
is non-empty and finite, and
\rho(K)=2
when
K
is infinite. Clearly,
\rho(A^{*})=2
. However, given finitely many words
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in A^{*}
, we have
\rho(w_{1})+\cdots+\rho(w_{n})=1\neq 2
.

A key point is that nice multiplicative rating maps are finitely representable (which is not the case in general). Let us explain why. Given any multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
we associate a canonical morphism
\beta:A^{*}\to M
(where
M
is a monoid computed from
R
). We then argue than when
\rho
is nice, it is characterized by this canonical morphism
\beta
.

Consider an arbitrary multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. We associate a canonical monoid morphism
\beta
to
\rho
. Essentially,
\beta
is the restriction of
\rho
to
A^{*}
. However, since
R
need not be a monoid for multiplication, we also have to restrict the output set
R
.

We let
R_{A^{*}}\subseteq R
as the set
R_{A^{*}}=\{\rho(w)\mid w\in A^{*}\}
. Clearly, since
\rho
is a multiplicative rating map,
R_{A^{*}}
is a sub-semigroup of
R
for multiplication. Moreover, it is a monoid whose neutral element is
\rho(\varepsilon)
. We now define the canonical morphism
\beta
associated to
\rho
as the following monoid morphism
\beta:A^{*}\to R_{A^{*}}
:

\displaystyle\beta:A^{*}

\displaystyle\to R_{A^{*}}

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\mapsto\rho(w)

When
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
is nice, it is fully determined by the associated canonical morphism
\beta:A^{*}\to R_{A^{*}}
and the finite hemiring
R
. Indeed, the definition of nice multiplicative rating maps exactly states that
\rho(K)=\sum_{w\in K}\rho(w)
. Hence,
\beta
and the addition of
R
determine
\rho(K)
for any language
K
. Consequently, it makes sense to speak of algorithms which take a nice multiplicative rating map as input.

Remark 5.7
.

Observe that when we have a nice multiplicative rating map
\rho
in hand, it is possible to evaluate
\rho(K)
when
K
is a regular language. Indeed,
\rho(K)
is the sum of all elements
\rho(w)
for
w\in K
which is simple to evaluate (see Lemma 5.7 in
[
pzcovering2
]
for details).

Finally, we have the following fact which is immediate from the definitions.

Fact 5.8
.

Consider a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Then the associated canonical morphism
\beta:A^{*}\to R_{A^{*}}
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible.

5.2.
Connection with finite sets of regular languages.

We now explain how one may associate a (computable) multiplicative rating map to a finite set of regular languages. This will later be useful to reformulate the covering problem with our framework.

Consider a finite set of languages
\mathbf{L}
and a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Observe that
2^{\mathbf{L}}
is an idempotent commutative monoid for union. We say that
\rho
extends
\mathbf{L}
when there exists a morphism
\delta:R\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
for the addition of
R
(i.e.
\delta(0_{R})=\emptyset
and
\delta(r_{1}+r_{2})=\delta(r_{1})\cup\delta(r_{2})
for any
r_{1},r_{2}\in R
) such that for every language
K
,

\delta(\rho(K))=\{L\in\mathbf{L}\mid K\cap L\neq\emptyset\}\in 2^{\mathbf{L}}

We call
\delta
an
extending morphism
. Let us point out that
\delta
is finitely representable since both
\mathbf{L}
and
R
are finite. We connect this definition to covering with the following fact.

Fact 5.9
.

Consider a finite set of languages
\mathbf{L}
together with a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
which extends
\mathbf{L}
for the extending morphism
\delta:R\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
. Then, a finite set of languages
\mathbf{K}
is separating for
\mathbf{L}
if and only if
\delta(\rho(K))\neq\mathbf{L}
for every
K\in\mathbf{K}
.

Proof.

Immediate from the definitions. Recall that
\mathbf{K}
is separating for
\mathbf{L}
if for every
K\in\mathbf{K}
, there exits
L\in\mathbf{L}
such that
K\cap L=\emptyset
.
∎

We now show that for any finite set of regular languages
\mathbf{L}
, one may compute an extending nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite quotienting Boolean algebra. Given as input a finite set of regular languages
\mathbf{L}
, one may compute a nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
which extends
\mathbf{L}
and the corresponding extending morphism
\delta:R\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{L}=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\}
. By Lemma
4.3
, for any
i\leq n
, one may compute a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha_{i}:A^{*}\to M_{i}
which recognizes
L_{i}
. We let
F_{i}
as the corresponding accepting set:
L_{i}=\alpha_{i}^{-1}(F_{i})
. One may verify that for any
i\leq n
, the set
2^{M_{i}}
is a finite idempotent hemiring. The addition is union and the multiplication is obtained by lifting the one of
M_{i}
: for
S,T\in 2^{M_{i}}
their multiplication is
S\cdot T=\{st\mid s\in S\text{ and }t\in T\}
. We define,

R=2^{M_{1}}\times\cdots\times 2^{M_{n}}

It is straightforward to verify that
R
is a finite idempotent hemiring for the componentwise addition and multiplication. We define our multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\rho:&2^{A^{*}}&\to&R\\
&K&\mapsto&(\alpha_{1}(K),\dots,\alpha_{n}(K))\end{array}

One may verify that
\rho
is indeed a nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map (
\mathcal{C}
-compatibility comes from the fact that we started from
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphisms).

It remains to explain why
\rho
extends
\mathbf{L}
and how to compute an extending morphism
\delta:R\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
. We define
\delta:R\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
as follows. Consider
r=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})\in R
, for
i\leq n
, we have
L_{i}\in\delta(r)
if and only if
S_{i}\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset
. Clearly, one may compute
\delta
from the morphism
\alpha_{i}
. Let us verify that it is an extending morphism. Let
K\subseteq A^{*}
, we show that
\delta(\rho(K))=\{L\in\mathbf{L}\mid K\cap L\neq\emptyset\}
. Given
L_{i}\in\mathbf{L}
, we have to show that
L_{i}\in\delta(\rho(K))
if and only if
K\cap L_{i}\neq\emptyset
. By definition,
L_{i}\in\delta(\rho(K))
if and only if
\alpha_{i}(K)\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset
. Since
L_{i}=\alpha_{i}^{-1}(F_{i})
, this is equivalent to
K\cap L_{i}\neq\emptyset
, finishing the proof.
∎

5.3.
Imprints and optimal covers

We may now explain how we use multiplicative rating maps to measure the quality of covers. This is based on a new notion called “imprints”. Consider a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
(possibly not nice). For any finite set of languages
\mathbf{K}
, the
\rho
-imprint of
\mathbf{K}
(denoted by
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq R
) is the following set:

\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})=\{r\in R\mid\text{there exists $K\in\mathbf{K}$ such that $r\leq\rho(K)$}\}\subseteq R

When using this notion, we will always have some language
L\subseteq A^{*}
in hand and our objective will be to find the “best possible” cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
. Intuitively,
\rho
-imprints are designed for this purpose: given a candidate cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
, we use the
\rho
-imprint of
\mathbf{K}
to measure its “quality”.

This leads to the notion of optimality. Assume that some arbitrary lattice
\mathcal{D}
is fixed and consider a language
L
. An
optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
which has the smallest possible
\rho
-imprint (with respect to inclusion). That is,
\mathbf{K}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
if and only if,

\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\quad\text{for every $\mathcal{D}$-cover $\mathbf{K}^{\prime}$ of $L$}

In general, there can be infinitely many optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
L
for
\rho
. However, there always exists at least one (we need the fact that
\mathcal{D}
is a lattice for this, see Lemma 4.15 in
[
pzcovering2
]
for the proof).

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a lattice. Then, for any multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and any language
L\subseteq A^{*}
, there exists an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
.

The proof of Lemma
5.11
is non-constructive. In fact, given
L
and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, computing an actual optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
is a difficult problem in general. As seen in Theorem
5.16
below, getting such algorithm yields a procedure for
\mathcal{D}
-covering. Before we can present this theorem, we need a key observation about optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers.

Optimal imprints.
By definition, all optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
L
for
\rho
have the same
\rho
-imprint. Hence, this unique
\rho
-imprint is a
canonical
object for
\mathcal{D}
,
L
and
\rho
. We call it the
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint on
L
and we denote it by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
:

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]=\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq R\quad\text{for every optimal $\mathcal{D}$-cover $\mathbf{K}$ of $L$ for $\rho$}.

We complete this definition with a few properties of optimal imprints. We start with two facts which will be useful (the proofs are available in
[
pzcovering2
]
, see Facts 4.16 and 4.17).

Fact 5.12
.

Consider a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and a language
L
. Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be two lattices such that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. Then,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}[L,\rho]
.

Fact 5.13
.

Consider a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and
\mathcal{D}
a lattice. Let
H,L
be two languages such that
H\subseteq L
. Then,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[H,\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
.

Moreover, we have the following important lemma which connects optimal imprints to multiplication. Again, this lemma is proved in
[
pzcovering2
]
, (see Lemma 5.8)

Lemma 5.14
.

Consider a quotienting lattice
\mathcal{D}
. Let
L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
be two languages and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
a multiplicative rating map. Then, for any
r_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L_{1},\rho]
and
r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L_{2},\rho]
, we have
r_{1}r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L_{1}L_{2},\rho]
.

Finally, we present a useful lemma which gives an alternate definition of
\mathcal{D}
-optimal imprints when
\mathcal{D}
is a finite lattice. We shall use it in proofs together with stratifications. Given a preorder relation
\leqslant
defined on
A^{*}
, a word
w\in A^{*}
and a language
K\subseteq A^{*}
, we write
w\leqslant K
to denote the fact that
w\leqslant u

for all

u\in K
.

Lemma 5.15
.

Assume that
\mathcal{D}
is a
finite
lattice. Let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a multiplicative rating map and
L
a language. Given
r\in R
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
.

(2)

There exist
w\in L
and
K\subseteq A^{*}
such that
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}K
and
r\leq\rho(K)
.

Proof.

We start with preliminary terminology. For every
w\in A^{*}
, we let
K_{w}\subseteq A^{*}
as the least upper set for
\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}
which contains
w
:
K_{w}=\{u\in A^{*}\mid w\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}v\}
. Recall that by Lemma
2.6
, the upper sets of
\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}
are exactly the languages in
\mathcal{D}
. In particular, there are finitely many languages
K_{w}
(even though there are infinitely many words
w\in A^{*}
). It follows that the set
\mathbf{K}=\{K_{u}\mid u\in L\}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
. We may now prove the lemma.

Assume first that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
. Since
\mathbf{K}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
, this implies that
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
by definition of
\mathcal{D}
-optimal imprints. Hence, we have
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
r\leq\rho(K)
. By definition,
K=K_{w}
for some
w\in L
which means that
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}K
and the second assertion in the lemma holds. Conversely, assume that there exist
w\in L
and
K\subseteq A^{*}
such that
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}K
and
r\leq\rho(K)
. Consider an arbitrary
\mathcal{D}
-optimal cover
\mathbf{H}
of
L
. By definition,
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{H})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{H})
. Since
\mathbf{H}
is a cover of
L
and
w\in L
, we have
H\in\mathbf{H}
such that
w\in H
. Moreover, since
H\in\mathcal{D}
, it is a upper set for
\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}
by Lemma
2.6
. Consequently,
w\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}K
and
w\in H
imply that
K\subseteq H
and we get that
r\leq\rho(K)\leq\rho(H)
. We conclude that
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{H})
, finishing the proof.
∎

5.4.
Connection with the covering problem

We may now connect these definitions to the covering problem. We do so with the following theorem.

Theorem 5.16
.

Consider a lattice
\mathcal{D}
. Let
L\subseteq A^{*}
be a language and
\mathbf{L}
a finite set of languages. Moreover, let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a multiplicative rating map extending
\mathbf{L}
for the extending morphism
\delta:R\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
. The following properties are equivalent:

(1)

(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable.

(2)

We have
\mathbf{L}\not\in\delta(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho])
.

(3)

Any optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
is separating for
\mathbf{L}
.

Proof.

We show that
(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(1)
. Let us start with
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Assume that
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable and let
\mathbf{K}
be a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
which is separating for
\mathbf{L}
. We show that
\mathbf{L}\not\in\delta(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho])
. Given
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
, we have to prove that
\mathbf{L}\neq\delta(r)
. Since
\mathbf{K}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
. Hence,
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
which yields
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
r\leq\rho(K)
, i.e.
\rho(K)+r=\rho(K)
. Since
\delta
is a morphism this implies that
\delta(\rho(K))\cup\delta(r)=\delta(\rho(K))
, i.e.
\delta(r)\subseteq\delta(\rho(K))
. Moreover, since
\mathbf{K}
is separating for
\mathbf{L}
, it follows from Fact
5.9
that
\delta(\rho(K))\neq\mathbf{L}
. Consequently,
\delta(r)\subseteq\delta(\rho(K))
yields
\mathbf{L}\neq\delta(r)
, finishing the proof.

We turn to the direction
(2)\Rightarrow(3)
. Assume that
\mathbf{L}\not\in\delta(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho])
. Consider an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
for
\rho
. We show that
\mathbf{K}
is separating for
\mathbf{L}
. By definition of
\mathbf{K}
, for any
K\in\mathbf{K}
, we have
\rho(K)\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
which means that
\delta(\rho(K))\neq\mathbf{L}
by hypothesis. Thus, it follows from Fact
5.9
that
\mathbf{K}
is separating for
\mathbf{L}
.

We finish with the direction
(3)\Rightarrow(1)
. Since we know from Lemma
5.11
that there always exists an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
, Item (3) yields that there exists a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
which is separating for
\mathbf{L}
. Hence,
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable.
∎

Theorem
5.16
formally connects our framework to the covering problem. Let
\mathcal{D}
be some lattice. Given as input a regular language
L
and a finite set of regular languages
\mathbf{L}
, Proposition
5.10
states that we may compute a nice multiplicative rating map
\rho
extending
\mathbf{L}
. By Theorem
5.16
, deciding whether
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable now amounts to computing
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
(we outline the reduction precisely below). Additionally, getting a separating
\mathcal{D}
-cover (if there exists one) reduces to computing an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
.

This is essentially what our algorithms do (for
\mathcal{D}={Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
or
\mathcal{D}=PBPol(\mathcal{C})
). However, rather than directly computing
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
, we will work with some morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
recognizing
L
and compute all sets
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
for
s\in M
simultaneously (this is how we exploit the hypothesis that
L
is regular). As shown in the following proposition, this is sufficient information to compute
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
.

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a lattice. Let
L
be a regular language recognized by a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
for the accepting set
F
(i.e.
L=\alpha^{-1}(F)
). Moreover, let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a multiplicative rating map. For any
s\in M
, let
\mathbf{K}_{s}
be an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for
\rho
. The two following properties hold:

•

We have
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]=\bigcup_{s\in F}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
.

•

The set
\mathbf{K}=\bigcup_{s\in F}\mathbf{K}_{s}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
.

Proof.

We start with the second item. Since
L=\alpha^{-1}(F)
and
\mathbf{K}_{s}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for any
s\in M
, we know that
\mathbf{K}=\bigcup_{s\in F}\mathbf{K}_{s}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
. Let us show that it is optimal. Consider another
\mathcal{D}
-cover
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
of
L
, we show that
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}^{\prime})
. Let
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
. By definition,
r\leq\rho(K)
for some
K\in\mathbf{K}
. Moreover,
K\in\mathbf{K}_{s}
for some
s\in F
. Since
\mathbf{K}_{s}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for
\rho
, it follows that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Finally, since
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
, it is also a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
\alpha^{-1}(s)\subseteq L
(
L=\alpha^{-1}(F)
and
s\in F
). Hence,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}^{\prime})
which yields
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}^{\prime})
, finishing the proof.

We finish with the first item. We just proved that
\mathbf{K}=\bigcup_{s\in F}\mathbf{K}_{s}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
L
. Thus,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]=\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
. Moreover, it is immediate from the definition that
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})=\bigcup_{s\in F}\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{s})
. Hence, since
\mathbf{K}_{s}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for
\rho
which means that
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{s})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
, we obtain
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]=\bigcup_{s\in F}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. ∎

Altogether, this means that we shall be looking for algorithms which take a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a nice multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
as input and compute all
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\rho
-imprints
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
for
s\in M
. It will be convenient to have a single notation which records all these objects.

Given a lattice
\mathcal{D}
, a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
for the following set,

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]=\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]\}\subseteq M\times R

We call
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
the
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\alpha
-pointed
\rho
-imprint
. Clearly, it encodes all sets
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
for
s\in M
. We may now summarize the connection between covering and our framework with the following proposition.

Proposition 5.18
.

Consider a lattice
\mathcal{D}
and some finite quotienting Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
. Assume that there exists an algorithm for the following computational problem:

Input:

A
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and,

a nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
.

Output:

Compute the
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\alpha
-pointed
\rho
-imprint,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
.

Then,
\mathcal{D}
-covering is decidable.

We prove Proposition
5.18
by describing a procedure for
\mathcal{D}
-covering. Our input is a pair
(L,\mathbf{L})
where all languages in
\{L\}\cup\mathbf{L}
are regular: we need to decide whether
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable. The reduction to the problem described in Proposition
5.18
is based on the following steps:

(1)

Compute a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
recognizing
L
. This is possible by Lemma
4.3
. We let
F\subseteq M
as the accepting set:
L=\alpha^{-1}(F)
.

(2)

Compute a nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
which extends
\mathbf{L}
and an extending morphism
\delta:R\to 2^{\mathbf{L}}
. This is possible by Proposition
5.10
.

(3)

Use the procedure given by Proposition
5.18
to compute the
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\alpha
-pointed
\rho
-imprint,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
.

(4)

Compute
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]
from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
. Recall that by Proposition
5.17
, we have,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho]=\bigcup_{s\in F}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]

Moreover, for any
s\in M
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]=\{r\in R\mid(s,r)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]\}
by definition.

(5)

By Theorem
5.16
,
(L,\mathbf{L})
is
\mathcal{D}
-coverable if and only if we have
\mathbf{L}\not\in\delta(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[L,\rho])
. We may now check this condition.

This concludes the proof of Proposition
5.18
.

In view of Proposition
5.18
, when we tackle
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
- and
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering in the next two sections, we shall do so by presenting algorithms which compute
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
and
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
from a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}:\to M
and a nice multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
(which also needs to be
\mathcal{C}
-compatible in the case of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
).

A key point is that we present these two algorithms as elegant characterization theorems. This is a design principle of our framework. Given an arbitrary quotienting lattice
\mathcal{D}
(such as
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
or
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
), the idea is to characterize
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq M\times R
as the least subset of
M\times R
which contains basic elements and is closed under operations within a finite list (which depends on
\mathcal{D}
). In turn, this yields a least fixpoint algorithm for computing
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
: one starts from the set of basic elements and saturates it with the operations.

Remark 5.19
.

There lies the difference between our results for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
and
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
. Our characterization of
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
holds for
any
multiplicative rating map
\rho
(and in particular for those which are not nice). On the other hand, our characterization of
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
only holds for nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating maps. In other words, our results provide more information about
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. Of course, this is useless for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering: considering nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating maps suffices by Proposition
5.18
. However, it turns out that having a characterization of
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
which holds for
all
multiplicative rating maps (even if it is non-effective in general) is crucial for handling
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering.

5.5.
Generic properties of optimal pointed imprints

We finish with an important lemma which presents properties of optimal pointed imprints (i.e. the objects that we now want to compute). These properties are
generic
: they are satisfied by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
for any quotienting lattice
\mathcal{D}
. In particular, they are involved in our characterizations of both
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
and
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
.

Given any multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and any morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
, we shall write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
for the following set,

\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]=\{(\alpha(w),r)\in M\times R\mid w\in A^{*}\text{ and }r\leq\rho(w)\}

It is straightforward to verify that given a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a
nice
multiplicative rating map
\rho
as input, one may compute
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
. Our key lemma is as follows.

Lemma 5.20
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a quotienting lattice,
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
a morphism and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
a multiplicative rating map. Then,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq M\times R
contains the set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
and satisfies the two following closure properties:

(1)

Downset.
For any
(s,r)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
and
r^{\prime}\leq r
,
(s,r^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
.

(2)

Multiplication.
For any
(s_{1},r_{1}),(s_{2},r_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
,
(s_{1}s_{2},r_{1}r_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
.

Proof.

Let us first show that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
contains
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
. Let
(s,r)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
. By definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
, we have to show that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Let
\mathbf{K}
be an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for
\rho
, we have to show that
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
. By definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
, there exists
w\in A^{*}
such that
s=\alpha(w)
and
r\leq\rho(w)
. Consequently
w\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
and there exists
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
w\in K
. Thus,
r\leq\rho(w)\leq\rho(K)
which means that
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
by definition.

It remains to prove that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
is closed under downset and multiplication. We start with downset. Consider
(s,r)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
and
r^{\prime}\leq r
. By definition, we have
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
is a
\rho
-imprint, it is immediate by definition that
r^{\prime}\leq r
implies that
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Therefore,
(s,r^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
.

Closure under multiplication follows from Lemma
5.14
. Let
(s_{1},r_{1}),(s_{2},r_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
. It follows that
r_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}),\rho]
and
r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}),\rho]
. Therefore, since
\mathcal{D}
is a quotienting lattice, Lemma
5.14
yields
r_{1}r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}),\rho]
. Moreover, since
\alpha
is a morphism, it is immediate that
\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}s_{2})
. Hence, Fact
5.13
yields
r_{1}r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}s_{2}),\rho]
which means that

(s_{1}s_{2},r_{1}r_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\alpha,\rho]
by definition.
∎

6.
Covering for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})

In this section, we present the first of our two covering algorithms. Given an arbitrary finite quotienting Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
(which we fix for the section), we show that
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering is decidable. As announced, we use the framework introduced in Section
5
: we present an effective characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints. More precisely, our main theorem is a description of the set
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
for any
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and any multiplicative rating map
\rho
(nice or not). Furthermore, this description yields an algorithm for computing
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
when
\rho
is nice.

Remark 6.1
.

Naturally, the algorithm only applies to nice multiplicative rating maps. It does not even make sense to speak of an algorithm which takes arbitrary multiplicative rating maps as input: we cannot represent them finitely in general. However, the characterization itself applies to
any
multiplicative rating map. While this is useless for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering, having this result will be crucial in Section
7
when we tackle
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering.

We start by presenting an auxiliary result about factorization forests that we shall need for proving our characterization (we choose to isolate it from the main argument as we shall reuse it later when considering
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
). We then present our characterization and devote the remainder of the section to its proof.

6.1.
Covering languages of factorization forests

When proving the difficult direction in our characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints, we shall work with two objects: a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\rightarrow M
and a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Our objective will be to build
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covers of all languages
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for
s\in M
which satisfy specific properties with respect to
\rho
. We shall use the factorization forest theorem: any word admits an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
3|M|-1
(see Section
4
, Theorem
4.5
). Specifically, we use an induction on the height of factorization forests. Our proof is based on a construction which takes as input some height
h
and
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covers for the restrictions of the languages
\alpha^{-1}(s)
to words admitting
\alpha
-factorization forests of height at most
h
. It combines them into larger
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covers which also contain the words admitting forests of height at most
h+1
.

The following lemma formalizes an important part of this construction which we shall later reuse for handling
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
. We assume that the morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\rightarrow M
and the multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
are fixed. Recall that given
s\in M
and
h,m\in{\rm Nature}
,
F^{\alpha}(s,h,m)
(resp.
F^{\alpha}_{I}(s,h,m)
) denotes the language of all
w\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
admitting an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
(resp. whose root is an idempotent node).

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
h,m\geq 1
and an idempotent
e\in M
. Let
\mathbf{U}
be a cover of
F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
and
V\subseteq A^{*}
containing
\alpha^{-1}(e)
. There exists a cover
\mathbf{K}
of
F^{\alpha}_{I}(e,h,m)
such that any
K\in\mathbf{K}
is a concatenation
K=K_{1}\cdots K_{n}
with each
K_{i}
having one of the two following forms:

(1)

K_{i}
is a language in
\mathbf{U}
, or,

(2)

K_{i}=U_{1}\cdots U_{\ell}VU^{\prime}_{1}\cdots U^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}
where
U_{1},\cdots,U_{\ell},U^{\prime}_{1},\dots,U^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathbf{U}
and there exists some
idempotent

f\in R
for multiplication such that
\rho(U_{1}\cdots U_{\ell})=\rho(U^{\prime}_{1}\cdots U^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}})=f
.

We now prove Lemma
6.2
. Consider
h,m\geq 1
and an idempotent
e\in M
. Finally, we let
\mathbf{U}
as a cover of
F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
and
V\subseteq A^{*}
such that
\alpha^{-1}(e)\subseteq V
. We construct a cover
\mathbf{K}
of
F^{\alpha}_{I}(e,h,m)
satisfying the properties described in the lemma.

For the proof, we fix
k=2^{3|R|}
. Given any
n\geq 1
, we define
\mathbf{K}_{n}
as the set of all languages
K\subseteq A^{*}
which are of the form
K=K_{1}\cdots K_{p}
with
p\leq n
where each
K_{i}
is of one of the two following kinds:

(1)

K_{i}\in\mathbf{U}
, or,

(2)

K_{i}=U_{1}\cdots U_{\ell}VU^{\prime}_{1}\cdots U^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}
where
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\leq k
,
U_{1},\cdots,U_{\ell},U^{\prime}_{1},\dots,U^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathbf{U}
and there exists some
idempotent

f\in R
such that
\rho(U_{1}\cdots U_{\ell})=\rho(U^{\prime}_{1}\cdots U^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}})=f
.

Observe that by definition all sets
\mathbf{K}_{n}
are finite (each
K\in\mathbf{K}_{n}
is a concatenation of at most
n\times(2k+1)
languages in the finite set
\mathbf{U}\cup\{V\}
). Moreover all languages in
\mathbf{K}_{n}
are of the form described in Lemma
6.2
. Thus, it suffices to prove that there exists
n\geq 1
such that
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is a cover of
F^{\alpha}_{I}(e,h,m)
. This is what we do.

We start with some terminology. Observe that by definition of
\alpha
-factorization forests, for any word
w\in F^{\alpha}_{I}(e,h,m)
, there exists a sequence of words
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
such that
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{p}
. Given any such sequence
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
, we associate a number that we call its
index
(we shall use this number as an induction parameter). For the definition, we fix an arbitrary linear order on
\mathbf{U}
, the cover of
F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
. Moreover, for any
w\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
, we write
U[w]
for the smallest language in
\mathbf{U}
containing
w
.

Consider a sequence
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
and an idempotent
f\in R
. We say that
f

occurs in the sequence
w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell}
when there exists
i\leq p
and
q\leq k
such that,

\rho(U[w_{i}]\cdots U[w_{i+q-1}])=f

Finally, we define the index of the sequence
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
as the number of
distinct idempotents

f\in R
that occur in
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
. Clearly, the index of
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
is bounded by
|R|
(the key point is that this bound is independent from the length
p
of the sequence). We shall need the following fact which is proved using the factorization forest theorem.

Fact 6.3
.

Consider a sequence
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
of length
p\geq k=2^{3|R|}
. Then the index of
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
is at least one: an idempotent
f\in R
occurs in
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
.

Proof.

For all
i\leq k
, we let
r_{i}=\rho(U[w_{i}])\in R
. Consider the morphism
\beta:R^{*}\to R
which is defined by
\beta(r)=r
for all
r\in R
. It is immediate from Theorem
4.5
that the word
(r_{1})\cdots(r_{k})\in R^{*}
admits a
\beta
-factorization forest of height at most
3|R|-1
. Since
k=2^{3|R|}
, this forest has to contain at least one idempotent node. Thus, we have
i\leq k
and
q\leq k
such that
r_{i}\cdots r_{i+q-1}
is an idempotent
f
of
R
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We may now come back to the proof of Lemma
6.2
and exhibit
n\geq 1
such that
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is a cover of
F^{\alpha}_{I}(e,h,m)
. We use the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
d\in{\rm Nature}
and consider a sequence
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
whose index is at most
d
. There exists a language
K\in\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)(d+1)}
containing
w_{1}\cdots w_{p}
.

Lemma
6.2
is an immediate consequence of Lemma
6.4
: we obtain that
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is a cover of
F^{\alpha}_{I}(e,h,m)
for
n=(k+1)(|R|+1)
. Indeed, by definition, for any
w\in F^{\alpha}_{I}(e,h,m)
there exist
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
such that
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{p}
. Since the index of
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
is at most
|R|
, it follows from Lemma
6.4
that there exist
K\in\mathbf{K}_{n}
which contains
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{p}
.

It remains to prove Lemma
6.4
. Let
d\in{\rm Nature}
and consider a sequence
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
whose index is at most
d
. We construct
K\in\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)(d+1)}
which contains
w_{1}\cdots w_{p}
. The argument is an induction on
d
.

Assume first that
d=0
. In that case, there exists no idempotent
f\in R
occurring in
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
. It is immediate from Fact
6.3
that
p<k
. Thus, we may simply choose,

K=U[w_{1}]\cdots U[w_{p}]

Clearly
K\in\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)(d+1)}
since
p<k
and we have
w_{1}\cdots w_{p}\in K
.

Assume now that
d\geq 1
: some idempotent
f\in R
occurs in
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
. Thus, there exists
i\leq p
and
q\leq k
such that,

\rho(U[w_{i}]\cdots U[w_{i+q-1}])=f

Moreover, it follows from Fact
6.3
, that we may choose
i
and
q
so that
i+q-1\leq k
. Consider the sequence
w_{i+q+1},\dots,w_{p}
. We distinguish two sub-cases depending on whether
f
occurs in
w_{i+q+1},\dots,w_{p}
as well or not.

Case 1: The idempotent
f
does not occur in
w_{i+q+1},\dots,w_{p}
.
In that case, the index of
w_{i+q+1},\dots,w_{p}
is at most
d-1
. Thus, induction yields a language
K^{\prime}\in\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)d}
containing
w_{i+q+1}\cdots w_{p}
. We define our new language
K
as follows:

K=U[w_{1}]\cdots U[w_{i+q}]\cdot K^{\prime}

By definition, we have
w_{1}\cdots w_{p}\in K
. Moreover,
K
is the concatenation of a language in
\mathbf{K}_{k+1}
(i.e.
U[w_{1}]\cdots U[w_{i+q}]
since
i+q-1\leq k
) with a language of
\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)d}
(i.e.
K^{\prime}
). Thus, since
k+1+(k+1)d=(k+1)(d+1)
, we obtain that
K\in\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)(d+1)}
.

Case 2: The idempotent
f
occurs in
w_{i+q+1},\dots,w_{p}
.
By definition, this means that we get
j\geq i+q+1
and
r\leq k
, such that,

\rho(U[w_{j}]\cdots U[w_{j+r-1}])=f

We work with the largest such
j
. In other words, we choose
j
so that
f
does not occur in
w_{j+1},\dots,w_{p}
and, therefore neither in
w_{j+r},\dots,w_{p}
. Hence, the index of
w_{j+r},\dots,w_{p}
is at most
d-1
and induction yields a language
K^{\prime}\in\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)d}
containing
w_{j+r}\cdots w_{p}
. We define our new language
K
as follows:

K=U[w_{1}]\cdots U[w_{i-1}]\cdot(U[w_{i}]\cdots U[w_{i+q-1}]\cdot V\cdot U[w_{j}]\cdots U[w_{j+r-1}])\cdot K^{\prime}

Clearly,
U[w_{1}]\cdots U[w_{i-1}]\cdot(U[w_{i}]\cdots U[w_{i+q-1}]\cdot V\cdot U[w_{j}]\cdots U[w_{j+r-1}])\in\mathbf{K}_{k+1}
since
i\leq k+1
. Thus,
K
is the concatenation of a language in
\mathbf{K}_{k+1}
with another language in
\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)d}
and we get
K\in\mathbf{K}_{(k+1)(d+1)}
.

Finally,
w_{1}\cdots w_{i+q-1}\in U[w_{1}]\cdots U[w_{i+q-1}]
and
w_{j}\cdots w_{p}\in U[w_{j}]\cdots U[w_{j+r-1}]\cdot K^{\prime}
by definition. Moreover, since
w_{i+q},\dots,w_{j-1}\in F^{\alpha}(e,h-1,m-1)
and
e
is idempotent, we have
w_{i+q}\cdots w_{j-1}\in\alpha^{-1}(e)\subseteq V
. Altogether, we get
w_{1}\cdots w_{p}\in K
, concluding the proof.

6.2.
Characterization

We begin with the property characterizing
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints. Consider a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
(note that there is no constraint on
\rho
). In particular, recall that since
\alpha
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible, for any
s\in M
,
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
is well-defined as a
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class containing
\alpha^{-1}(s)
.

We say that a subset
S\subseteq M\times R
is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated
(for
\alpha
and
\rho
) when it contains
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
and is closed under the following operations:

(1)

Downset
: For any
(s,r)\in S
and
r^{\prime}\leq r
, we have
(s,r^{\prime})\in S
.

(2)

Multiplication
: For any
(s_{1},r_{1}),(s_{2},r_{2})\in S
, we have
(s_{1}s_{2},r_{1}r_{2})\in S
.

(3)

{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-closure
: For any pair of multiplicative idempotents
(e,f)\in S
, we have,

(e,f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f)\in S

We may now state the main theorem of the section: the
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal
\alpha
-pointed
\rho
-imprint
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
(for inclusion).

Theorem 6.5

(Characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints)
.

Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
a multiplicative rating map. Then,
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
.

When
\rho
is nice, Theorem
6.5
yields an algorithm for computing
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
from
\alpha
and
\rho
. Indeed, one may compute the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
with a least fixpoint procedure: one starts from
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
and saturates this set with the three operations in the definition (it is clear that all may be implemented). Combined with Proposition
5.18
, this yields the desired corollary.

Corollary 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite quotienting Boolean algebra. The
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covering problem is decidable.

It remains to prove Theorem
6.5
. The remainder of the section is devoted to this argument. Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
as a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
as a multiplicative rating map. We show that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is the least
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
.

We separate the proof in two main steps. Intuitively, they correspond respectively to soundness and completeness of the least fixpoint procedure obtained from the theorem. First, we show that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. This corresponds to soundness (the least fixpoint procedure only computes elements in
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
). Then we show that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is included in any
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset
S\subseteq M\times R
. This corresponds to completeness (the least fixpoint procedure computes all elements in
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
).

6.3.
Soundness

We show that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. Since
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
is a quotienting lattice, we already know from Lemma
5.20
that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
contains
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
and is closed under downset and multiplication. Thus, we may focus on proving that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
satisfies
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-closure. Consider an idempotent
(e,f)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
. We show that,

(e,f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]

By definition of
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
, this means proving that
f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f\in\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]
. We fix an arbitrary optimal
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
\alpha^{-1}(e)
and show that
f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
. We rely on the generic stratification of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
defined in Section
4
(which we may use since
\mathcal{C}
is a finite quotienting Boolean algebra). Recall that the strata are denoted by
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
and the associated canonical preorders by
\leqslant_{k}
. By definition
\mathbf{K}
is a finite set of languages in
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. Therefore, there exists some stratum
k\in{\rm Nature}
such that all languages in
\mathbf{K}
belong to
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
. Consequently, we have
\mathcal{I}_{Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
and it now suffices to show that
f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f\in\mathcal{I}_{Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]
. By Lemma
5.15
, we have to exhibit
w\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
K\subseteq A^{*}
such that
w\leqslant_{k}K
and
f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f\leq\rho(K)
. This is what we do.

We first use our hypothesis that
(e,f)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
to define
w
and
K
. This implies that
f\in\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]
and we get
f\in\mathcal{I}_{Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]
by Fact
5.12
. Therefore, Lemma
5.15
yields
v\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
H\subseteq A^{*}
such that
v\leqslant_{k}H
and
f\leq\rho(H)
. Let
p\in{\rm Nature}
be the period of
\mathcal{C}
(see Fact
4.10
) and
\ell=p2^{k+1}
. We define,

w=v^{\ell}

Clearly,
w\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
since
v\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
e\in M
is idempotent. Finally, we let,

K=H^{\ell}\cdot[e]_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot H^{\ell}

By definition of
K
and since
\rho
is a multiplicative rating map,

(\rho(H))^{\ell}\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot(\rho(H))^{\ell}=\rho(K)

Moreover, since
f\leq\rho(H)
by hypothesis and
f
is idempotent, we obtain,

f\cdot\rho([e]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot f\leq\rho(K)

It remains to show that
w\leqslant_{k}K
. The proof is based on Lemma
4.12
. Consider
u\in K
. We show that
w\leqslant_{k}u
. This will imply that
w\leqslant_{k}K
. By definition, we have
K=H^{\ell}\cdot[e]_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot H^{\ell}
and by hypothesis, we have
v\leqslant_{k}H
. Thus, since
u\in K
and
\leqslant_{k}
is compatible with concatenation (see Lemma
2.9
), we obtain that there exists some
x\in[e]_{\mathcal{C}}
such that,

v^{\ell}\cdot x\cdot v^{\ell}\leqslant_{k}u

Therefore, it now suffices to show that
w\leqslant_{k}v^{\ell}\cdot x\cdot v^{\ell}
. It will then be immediate from transitivity that
w\leqslant_{k}u
as desired. Recall that
p
denotes the period of
\mathcal{C}
and
\ell=p2^{k+1}
. Since
v\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
e
is an idempotent of
M
, we have
v^{p}\in\alpha^{-1}(e)\subseteq[e]_{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus,
x
and
v^{p}
belong to the same
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class:
[e]_{\mathcal{C}}
. We get
v^{p}\sim_{\mathcal{C}}x
and Lemma
4.12
yields that

v^{\ell}\leqslant_{k}v^{\ell}\cdot x\cdot v^{\ell}

Since
w=v^{\ell}
by definition this is exactly the desired property which concludes the proof.

6.4.
Completeness

We turn to completeness. Recall that we have a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Consider
S\subseteq M\times R
which is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. Our objective is to prove that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S
.

We present a generic construction which builds
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covers
\mathbf{K}_{s}
of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for all
s\in M
such that for any
K\in\mathbf{K}_{s}
, we have
(s,\rho(K))\in S
. By definition of
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{s})
and since
S
is closed under downset, (it is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated), this will imply,

\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{s})\subseteq\{r\mid(s,r)\in S\}

Thus, since
\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{s})
by definition of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal
\rho
-imprints, we shall obtain,

\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]\subseteq\{r\mid(s,r)\in S\}

Finally, by definition of
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
, we shall obtain the desired inclusion:
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S
(recall that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]=\{(s,r)\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]\}
).

Remark 6.7
.

This construction yields a generic method for building optimal
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covers of the sets
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for
s\in M
. Indeed, we may apply it in the special case when
S=\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
since we already showed above that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. In this case, we get
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-covers
\mathbf{K}_{s}
of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
for all
s\in M
such that
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}_{s})=\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
.

The construction is based on the factorization forest theorem of Simon which we presented in Section
4
. Recall that for any
s\in M
and
h,m\in{\rm Nature}
, we write
F^{\alpha}(s,h,m)
for the language of all words in
\alpha^{-1}(s)
which admit an
\alpha
-factorization forest of height at most
h
and idempotent height at most
m
. We use the following proposition.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
s\in M
and
h,m\in{\rm Nature}
. There exists a
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
F^{\alpha}(s,h,m)
such that for any
K\in\mathbf{K}
, we have
(s,\rho(K))\in S
.

Before proving the proposition, we use it to finish the proof of Theorem
6.5
. Consider
s\in M
. Our objective is to build a
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-cover
\mathbf{K}_{s}
of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
such that,

(s,\rho(K))\in S\quad\text{for all $K\in\mathbf{K}_{s}$}

Let
h=m=3|M|-1
. We obtain from the factorization forest theorem (i.e. Theorem
4.5
) that
\alpha^{-1}(s)=F^{\alpha}(s,h,m)
. Thus, Proposition
6.8
yields the desired
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-cover
\mathbf{K}_{s}
of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
such that for any

.

It remains to prove Proposition

. Let

-cover

\text{For all $K\in\mathbf{K}$,}\quad(s,\rho(K))\in S

(1)

The construction is an induction on

Remark 6.9

The proof is actually independent from the idempotent height

6.4.1.
Base case: Leaves

Assume that

are either empty or made of a single letter

\mathbf{K}=\{[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}a[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}\mid a\in A\text{ and }\alpha(a)=s\}

Otherwise,

\mathbf{K}=\{[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}a[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}\mid a\in A\text{ and }\alpha(a)=s\}\cup\{[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}\}

It remains to show that

Lemma 6.10
.

We have
(1_{M},\rho([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}))\in S

(\alpha(a),\rho([\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}a[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{C}}))\in S

Proof.

We begin with the first property:

(1_{M},\rho(\varepsilon))=(\alpha(\varepsilon),\rho(\varepsilon))\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]

(1_{M},\rho([1_{M}]_{\mathcal{C}}))=(1_{M},\rho(\varepsilon)\cdot\rho([1_{M}]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot\rho(\varepsilon))\in S

Since

It remains to prove that given

Lemma 6.11

Proof of Lemma

.
Using induction we obtain that for all

, we exhibit

7.
Covering for
PBPol(\mathcal{C})

We now turn to our main result:
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering is decidable for any finite quotienting Boolean algebra
\mathcal{C}
. Again, our approach is based on optimal imprints: we present an effective characterization of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints. For the sake of avoiding clutter, we shall assume that
\mathcal{C}
is fixed for the section.

This characterization is more involved than the one we already obtained for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. First, it applies to a more restricted class of multiplicative rating maps. Specifically, we present a characterization of
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
which holds when
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
is a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism and
\rho
is a
nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible
multiplicative rating map. In other words, we now need
\rho
to be nice and
\mathcal{C}
-compatible (which was not the case for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
).

Remark 7.1
.

Of course, having a characterization restricted to this special case is enough to obtain the desired
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering algorithm by Proposition
5.18
. However, the fact that we now require
\rho
to be nice (which was not the case for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
) is significant. This explains why the arguments of this paper do not extend to higher levels in concatenation hierarchies. The proof of our characterization for
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
relies heavily on the fact that we have a characterization for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
which holds for
all
multiplicative rating maps.

A second point is that our characterization of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints actually involves two distinct objects:

•

As desired, it describes
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
, the
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-optimal
\alpha
-pointed
\rho
-imprint.

•

It also describes a second object:
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
, which is the
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal
\beta
-pointed
\tau
-imprint where
\beta
is the canonical morphism associated to
\rho
(i.e. its restriction to
A^{*}
), and
\tau
is an auxiliary multiplicative rating map built from
\alpha
and
\rho
.

The key idea here is that our descriptions of
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
and
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
are mutually dependent. Reformulated from an algorithmic point of view, this means that we get a least fixpoint procedure which computes
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
and
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
simultaneously (although
\tau
is not nice and can only be used implicitly in the algorithm).

Remark 7.2
.

The presence of this second object
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
explains why we shall need to reuse our characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints (i.e. Theorem
6.5
). In particular, the multiplicative rating map
\tau
will
not
be nice. Hence, it will be important that Theorem
6.5
holds for all multiplicative rating maps.

The section is organized as follows. First, we explain how the auxiliary multiplicative rating map
\tau
is defined from
\alpha
and
\rho
. Then, we present our characterization of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints. Finally, we concentrate on proving our characterization (note that we postpone the difficult direction of this proof to the next section).

7.1.
Auxiliary multiplicative rating map

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a quotienting lattice which is
closed under concatenation
. Consider a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
. We build a new multiplicative rating map
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
(which we shall often simply write “
\tau
” when
\alpha
,
\rho
and
\mathcal{D}
are clear from the context). We shall later use this definition in the special case when
\mathcal{D}=PBPol(\mathcal{C})
. This makes sense since we showed that
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
is a quotienting lattice closed under concatenation in Theorem
3.2
. Let us first explain why the set
2^{M\times R}
is a hemiring.

Since
2^{M\times R}
is a set of subsets, it is an idempotent commutative monoid for union. Thus, we simply use union as our addition (the neutral element is
\emptyset
and the order is inclusion). It remains to define our multiplication. Given
T_{1},T_{2}\in 2^{M\times R}
, we define,

T_{1}\cdot T_{2}=\{(s_{1}s_{2},r)\mid\text{there exists $(s_{1},r_{1})\in T_{1}$ and $(s_{2},r_{2})\in T_{2}$ such that $r\leq r_{1}r_{2}$}\}

One may verify that this is indeed a semigroup multiplication which distributes over the addition (i.e. union) and that
\emptyset
(the neutral element for union) is a zero. It follows that
2^{M\times R}
is an idempotent hemiring.

Remark 7.3
.

Our multiplication is not the most immediate one:
T_{1}\cdot T_{2}
is
not
the set of all multiplications between elements of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. It contains more elements: we make sure that
T_{1}\cdot T_{2}
is closed under downset (if
(s,r)\in T_{1}\cdot T_{2}
and
r^{\prime}\leq r
, then
(s,r^{\prime})\in T_{1}\cdot T_{2}
). We shall need this for proving that
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
is a multiplicative rating map.

Observe that for this multiplication,
2^{M\times R}
is a hemiring but not a semiring. There exists no neutral element for multiplication since a set
T\in 2^{M\times R}
which is not closed under downset cannot be equal to any multiplication.

We are now ready to define our new multiplicative rating map
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
. We use the following definition:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}:&2^{A^{*}}&\to&2^{M\times R}\\
&K&\mapsto&\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid\text{$r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]$}\}\end{array}

Let us verify that
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
is indeed a multiplicative rating map. Note that it is important here that
\mathcal{D}
is a quotienting lattice closed under concatenation.

Lemma 7.4
.

The map
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
is a multiplicative rating map.

Proof.

We have to show that
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
is a hemiring morphism. Let us first consider addition (which is union for both
2^{A^{*}}
and
2^{M\times R}
). Clearly
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(\emptyset)=\emptyset
. Indeed,

\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(\emptyset)=\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\emptyset,\rho]\}=\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid r\in\emptyset\}=\emptyset

We now show that for any
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1}\cup K_{2})=\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cup\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
. We start with the right to left inclusion. Let
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cup\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
and by symmetry, assume that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})
. It follows that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Since
K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)\subseteq(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
, it then follows from Fact
5.13
that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
which exactly says that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
. It remains to treat the left to right inclusion. Let
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
. By definition,
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Consider optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers (for
\rho
)
\mathbf{U}_{1},\mathbf{U}_{2}
of
(K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s))
and
(K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s))
respectively. By definition,
\mathbf{U}_{1}\cup\mathbf{U}_{2}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Thus, we have
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{U}_{1}\cup\mathbf{U}_{2})
and we obtain
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{U}_{1}\cup\mathbf{U}_{2})
. Therefore, there exists
U\in\mathbf{U}_{1}\cup\mathbf{U}_{2}
such that
r\leq\rho(U)
. By symmetry assume that
U\in\mathbf{U}_{1}
, we show that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})
(when
U\in\mathbf{U}_{2}
, one may show that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
). By definition, we have
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{U}_{1})
and since
\mathbf{U}_{1}
is an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
, we know that
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{U}_{1})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Thus,
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
which exactly means that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})
.

This concludes the proof for addition. We turn to multiplication. We show that
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1}K_{2})=\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cdot\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
. We start with the inclusion
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cdot\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})\subseteq\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1}K_{2})
. Let
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cdot\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
. Thus, we have
(s_{1},r_{1})\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})
and
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
such that
s=s_{1}s_{2}
and
r\leq r_{1}r_{2}
. By definition of
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
, we have
r_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}),\rho]
and
r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}),\rho]
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is a quotienting lattice, the following result is immediate from Lemma
5.14
,

r_{1}r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[(K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}))\cdot(K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})),\rho]

Observe that
(K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}))\cdot(K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}))\subseteq K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})
. Moreover, since
\alpha
is a morphism, we have
\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}s_{2})
. Altogether, this means that we have
(K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}))\cdot(K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}))\subseteq K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}s_{2})
. Therefore, we then obtain from Fact
5.13
that
r_{1}r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}s_{2}),\rho]
. Thus, we also have
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}s_{2}),\rho]
by definition of imprints since
r\leq r_{1}r_{2}
. It follows that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1}K_{2})
by definition of
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
.

We finish with the converse inclusion. Let
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1}K_{2})
. By definition, we have
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. For any
t\in M
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, we define
\mathbf{U}_{i,t}
as an optimal
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
K_{i}\cap\alpha^{-1}(t)
. Consider the following finite set of languages
\mathbf{U}
,

\mathbf{U}=\{U_{1}U_{2}\mid\text{there exists $s_{1},s_{2}\in M$ s.t. $s_{1}s_{2}=s$, $U_{1}\in\mathbf{U}_{1,s_{1}}$ and $U_{2}\in\mathbf{U}_{2,s_{2}}$}\}

Observe that
\mathbf{U}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Clearly all languages in
\mathbf{U}
belong to
\mathcal{D}
since
\mathcal{D}
is closed under concatenation by hypothesis. Let us show that
\mathbf{U}
is a cover of
K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Consider
w\in K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
, we exhibit
U\in\mathbf{U}
such that
w\in U
. Since
w\in K_{1}K_{2}
, we have
w=w_{1}w_{2}
with
w_{1}\in K_{1}
and
w_{2}\in K_{2}
. Let
s_{1}=\alpha(w_{1})
and
s_{2}=\alpha(w_{2})
. Altogether, this means that
w_{1}\in K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})
and
w_{2}\in K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})
. Therefore, we have
U_{1}\in\mathbf{U}_{1,s_{1}}
and
U_{2}\in\mathbf{U}_{2,s_{2}}
such that
w_{1}\in U_{1}
and
w_{2}\in U_{2}
. This yields
w\in U_{1}U_{2}
. Finally,
s_{1}s_{2}=\alpha(w)=s
which yields that
U_{1}U_{2}\in\mathbf{U}
by definition.

We may now finish the argument and show that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cdot\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
. Recall that
r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
. Thus, since
\mathbf{U}
is a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
K_{1}K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
, we have
r\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{U})
. It follows that there exists
U\in\mathbf{U}
such that
r\leq\rho(U)
. By definition of
\mathbf{U}
,
U=U_{1}U_{2}
with
U_{1}\in\mathbf{U}_{1,s_{1}}
and
U_{2}\in\mathbf{U}_{2,s_{2}}
where
s_{1},s_{2}\in M
satisfy
s_{1}s_{2}=s
. Let
r_{1}=\rho(U_{1})
and
r_{2}=\rho(U_{2})
. Since
\mathbf{U}_{1,s_{1}}
and
\mathbf{U}_{2,s_{2}}
are optimal
\mathcal{D}
-covers of
K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1})
and
K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2})
respectively, we have
r_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{1}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{1}),\rho]
and
r_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K_{2}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s_{2}),\rho]
. It follows that
(s_{1},r_{1})\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})
and
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
. Since
r\leq\rho(U)=\rho(U_{1})\cdot\rho(U_{2})=r_{1}r_{2}
, it follows by definition of our multiplication that
(s,r)\in\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cdot\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
which concludes the proof (note that we used the hypothesis that
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{1})\cdot\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K_{2})
is closed under downset by definition).
∎

Remark 7.5
.

An important point is that
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
is
not
nice in general even when
\rho
itself is nice. Moreover, given a (regular) language
K\subseteq A^{*}
, computing its image
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K)
is a difficult task: one needs to compute all
\mathcal{D}
-optimal
\rho
-imprints
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
for
s\in M
.

Let us provide an example in which
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
is not nice. Consider the class
\mathcal{D}
which contains all finite languages and
A^{*}
. One may verify that
\mathcal{D}
is a quotienting lattice. Moreover, we let
M=\{1_{M}\}
as the trivial monoid and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
as the only possible morphism. Finally, let
N=\{1_{N},s\}
be the monoid whose multiplication is defined by
ss=1_{N}
(and
1_{N}
is a neutral element). Clearly,
2^{N}
is a finite hemiring: the addition is union and the multiplication is obtained by lifting the one of
N
. We let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{N}
as the nice multiplicative rating map defined by
\rho(a)=\rho(b)=\{s\}
(for every language
K
,
1_{N}\in\rho(K)
if
K
contains a word of even length and
s\in\rho(K)
if
K
contains a word of odd length). We show that in this case the multiplicative rating map
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
is not nice. By definition, for every
K\subseteq A^{*}
, we have,

\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(K)=\{(1_{M},r)\mid\text{$r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[K,\rho]$}\}

Since the only infinite language in
\mathcal{D}
is
A^{*}
, a
\mathcal{D}
-cover of
A^{*}
must contain it. Hence, since
\rho(A^{*})=\{1_{N},s\}
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[A^{*},\rho]=\{\emptyset,\{1_{N}\},\{s\},\{1_{N},s\}\}
. However, given some word
w\in A^{*}
, since
\{w\}\in\mathcal{D}
, it is simple to verify that,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\{w\},\rho]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{\emptyset,\{1_{N}\}\}&\text{if $w$ has even length}\\
\{\emptyset,\{s\}\}&\text{if $w$ has odd length}\end{array}\right.

Altogether, it follows that,

\bigcup_{w\in A^{*}}\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(w)=\{(1_{M},\emptyset),(1_{M},\{1_{N}\}),(1_{M},\{s\})\}\neq\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}(A^{*})

Therefore,
\tau^{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha,\rho}
is not nice.

Remark 7.6
.

We use these definitions in the case when
\mathcal{D}=PBPol(\mathcal{C})
and consider the multiplicative rating map
\tau=\tau^{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}_{\alpha,\rho}
. We intend to consider
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
, the
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal
\beta
-pointed
\tau
-imprint (where
\beta
is the canonical morphism associated to
\rho
but this is unimportant for the moment). Since we proved a characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints (Theorem
6.5
), we already have a lot of information on
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
. However, what we
cannot
do is use this characterization to directly compute
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
from
\alpha
and
\rho
. Indeed, implementing the third operation (

when

is some

-class.

The main consequence of these observations is that we never manipulate

in algorithms. It is only an object that we shall use when proving the correction of our characterization of

-optimal pointed imprints.

7.2.
Characterization

We now turn to our characterization of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints. Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map. We define a notion of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
. Our theorem then states that when
\rho
is nice, the least such subset is exactly
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
, the
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-optimal
\alpha
-pointed imprint on
\rho
.

Remark 7.7
.

We do not need the hypothesis that
\rho
is nice to define
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subsets of
M\times R
: the definition makes sense for any
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map. However, we shall need this hypothesis to prove that the least one is
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
.

Remark 7.8
.

Since
\alpha
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible, for any
s\in M
,
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
, is well-defined as a
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class containing
\alpha^{-1}(s)
. Moreover, since
\rho
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible, for any
r\in R
,
[r]_{\mathcal{C}}
is well-defined as a
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class such that for any
w\in A^{*}
satisfying
r=\rho(w)
, we have
w\in[r]_{\mathcal{C}}
.

As announced, it turns out that our characterization simultaneously describes two sets. The first one is
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq M\times R
: this is the set that we want to compute. The second one is
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
(where
\beta:A^{*}\to R_{A^{*}}
is the canonical morphism associated to
\rho
and
\tau:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
is the multiplicative rating map
\tau^{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}_{\alpha,\rho}
built from
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
,
\rho
and
\alpha
): this is an auxiliary object needed for the computation. This is reflected in the definition of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated objects: it describes the properties satisfied by these two sets. More precisely, the definition applies to pairs
(S,\mathcal{T})
where
S\subseteq M\times R
and
\mathcal{T}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
.

Remark 7.9
.

We use the multiplication on
2^{M\times R}
defined at the beginning of the section. It is not the most natural one. Given
T_{1},T_{2}\in 2^{M\times R}
, their multiplication is,

T_{1}\cdot T_{2}=\{(s_{1}s_{2},r)\mid\text{there exists $(s_{1},r_{1})\in T_{1}$ and $(s_{2},r_{2})\in T_{2}$ such that $r\leq r_{1}r_{2}$}\}

Given a pair
(S,\mathcal{T})
with
S\subseteq M\times R
and
\mathcal{T}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
we say that
(S,\mathcal{T})
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated (for
\alpha
and
\rho
) when the following conditions are satisfied:

•

The set
S
contains
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
and is closed under the following operations:

(1)

Downset:
If
(s,r)\in S
and
r^{\prime}\leq r
, then
(s,r^{\prime})\in S
.

(2)

Multiplication:
for any
(s_{1},r_{1}),(s_{2},r_{2})\in S
, we have
(s_{1}s_{2},r_{1}r_{2})\in S
.

(3)

PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-closure.
For any
(r,T)\in\mathcal{T}
and any idempotent
(e,f)\in T\subseteq M\times R
,

(e,f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f)\in S

•

For any
w\in A^{*}
, if
r=\rho(w)
and
s=\alpha(w)
, then
(r,\{(s,r^{\prime})\mid r^{\prime}\leq r\})\in\mathcal{T}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{T}
is closed under the following operations:

(4)

Downset:
If
(r,T)\in\mathcal{T}
and
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
, then
(r,T^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
.

(5)

Multiplication:
For any
(r_{1},T_{1}),(r_{2},T_{2})\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
(r_{1}r_{2},T_{1}T_{2})\in\mathcal{T}
.

(6)

Nested closure.
For any idempotent
(f,E)\in\mathcal{T}
,

(f,E\cdot T\cdot E)\in\mathcal{T}\quad\text{where $T=\{(s,r)\in S\mid[f]_{\mathcal{C}}=[s]_{\mathcal{C}}\}$}

Remark 7.10
.

Observe that the conditions on
S
and
\mathcal{T}
are mutually dependent. On one hand, the
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-closure operation generates elements of
S
from elements of
\mathcal{T}
. On the other hand, nested closure does the opposite, it builds element of
\mathcal{T}
from those in
S
.

Finally, we say that a subset
S\subseteq M\times R
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated (for
\alpha
and
\rho
), when there exists another subset
\mathcal{T}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
such that the pair
(S,\mathcal{T})
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated.

We may now state the main theorem of this section. It turns out that
when
\rho
is nice
, the least
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
is
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
.

Theorem 7.11

(Characterization of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-optimal imprints)
.

Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
a
nice

\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map. Then,
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is the least
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
.

By definition, of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated sets Theorem
7.11
yields a least fixpoint algorithm for computing the set
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
from a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map
\rho 2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Let us briefly explain how to proceed.

One may compute the least pair
(S,\mathcal{T})
with
S\subseteq M\times R
and
\mathcal{T}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
which is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. We use a least fixpoint procedure which starts from the pair
(\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho],\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
where
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
contains all pairs
(r,\{(s,r^{\prime})\mid r^{\prime}\leq r\})
where
r=\rho(w)
and
s=\alpha(w)
for some
w\in A^{*}
. Then, we saturate this starting pair with the six operations in the definition which can clearly be implemented. Once we have this least
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated pair
(S,\mathcal{T})
in hand, we obtain from Theorem
7.11
that
S
is exactly the desired set
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
.

Remark 7.12
.

Theorem
7.11
does not describe the least subset
\mathcal{T}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
such that the pair
(\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho],\mathcal{T})
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated (which we also compute with our least fixpoint procedure). This is because, we are mainly interested in
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
:
\mathcal{T}
is only an auxiliary object that is required for the computation. However, a byproduct of our proof is that
\mathcal{T}
is exactly
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
where
\beta:A^{*}\to R_{A^{*}}
is the canonical morphism which is associated to
\rho
and
\tau:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
is the multiplicative rating map
\tau^{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}_{\alpha,\rho}
.

Remark 7.13
.

The hypothesis that
\rho
is nice in Theorem
7.11
is mandatory: the statement does not hold for arbitrary multiplicative rating maps. This fact is rather intuitive. One may verify from its definition that the least
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
may only contain pairs
(s,r)
where the element
r\in R
is built by summing and multiplying elements of the form
\rho(w)\in R
(i.e. images of singletons). Thus, considering
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subsets only makes sense when the multiplicative rating map

is characterized by the images of singletons: this is exactly the definition of nice multiplicative rating maps.

We complete the characterization by stating the desired corollary. It is now immediate from Proposition
5.18
that
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering is decidable.

Corollary 7.14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite quotienting Boolean algebra. Then,
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering is decidable.

We turn to the proof of Theorem
7.11
. In this section, we concentrate on the “easy” direction which corresponds to soundness of the least fixpoint procedure: for
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
satisfying the conditions of the theorem, we show that
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq M\times R
is indeed
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. The converse direction (i.e. that
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is the least such subset of
M\times R
) requires more work and we postpone it to the next section.

7.3.
Soundness

We fix a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and a nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
for the proof. We show that
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated.

Remark 7.15
.

We do not use the hypothesis that
\rho
is nice here. This is only needed for the other direction of the proof.

By definition, we first need to choose a subset
\mathcal{T}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
and then prove that the pair
(\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho],\mathcal{T})
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. Let
\beta:A^{*}\to R_{A^{*}}
be the canonical morphism which is associated to
\alpha
(recall that
R_{A^{*}}\subseteq R
is the monoid
R_{A^{*}}=\{\rho(w)\mid w\in A^{*}\}
). Note that
\beta
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible by Fact
5.8
. Moreover, we let
\tau:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
as the multiplicative rating map
\tau^{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}_{\alpha,\rho}
:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\tau:&2^{A^{*}}&\to&2^{M\times R}\\
&K&\mapsto&\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid\text{$r\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[K\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]$}\}\end{array}

Consider the set
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq R_{A^{*}}\times 2^{M\times R}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
. As expected, we prove that,

The pair
(\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho],\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau])
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated

Since
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
and
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
are both quotienting lattices, we already know from Lemma
5.20
that
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
contains
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\alpha,\rho]
and is closed under downset and multiplication. Moreover, we know that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
is closed under downset and multiplication. Therefore, we may concentrate on proving the three following properties:

(1)

For any
w\in A^{*}
, if
r=\rho(w)
and
s=\alpha(w)
, then
(r,\{(s,r^{\prime})\mid r^{\prime}\leq r\})\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
.

(2)

\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
satisfies nested closure (i.e. Operation (
6
)).

(3)

\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
satisfies
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-closure (i.e. Operation (
3
)).

First property

Consider
w\in A^{*}
,
r=\rho(w)
and
s=\alpha(w)
. We have to show that the pair
(r,\{(s,r^{\prime})\mid r^{\prime}\leq r\})
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
.

Since
\beta(w)=\rho(w)
(this is the definition of
\beta
), we have
r=\beta(w)
. We show that,

\{(s,r^{\prime})\mid r^{\prime}\leq r\}\subseteq\tau(w)

By definition, it will follow that
(r,\{(s,r^{\prime})\mid r^{\prime}\leq r\})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\beta,\tau]
which concludes the proof since
\mathcal{P}_{\mathit{triv}}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
by Lemma
5.20
.

Let
r^{\prime}\leq r
, we have to show that
(s,r^{\prime})\in\tau(w)
, i.e.
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\{w\}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
by definition of
\tau
. Since
s=\alpha(w)
, it is immediate that we have
\{w\}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)=\{w\}
. Therefore, we need to show that
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\{w\},\rho]
. This is immediate: any cover
\mathbf{K}
of
\{w\}
must contain a language
K
such that
w\in K
which implies that
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
since
r^{\prime}\leq r=\rho(w)
. This is in particular true for optimal
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covers of
\{w\}
which yields
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\{w\},\rho]
.

Second property

We prove that nested closure is satisfied. Consider an idempotent
(f,E)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
. We have to show that,

(f,E\cdot T\cdot E)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\quad\text{where $T=\{(s,r)\in\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\mid[f]_{\mathcal{C}}=[s]_{\mathcal{C}}\}$}

We use Theorem
6.5
, our characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints. It states that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
is
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-saturated (for
\tau
). In particular, it satisfies
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-closure which yields,

(f,E\cdot\tau([f]_{\mathcal{C}})\cdot E)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]

Therefore, it suffices to show that
T=\tau([f]_{\mathcal{C}})
to conclude the proof. This is what we do. By definition of
\tau
, we know that,

\tau([f]_{\mathcal{C}})=\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid\text{$r\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[[f]_{\mathcal{C}}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]$}\}

Since
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible, we know that any
\alpha^{-1}(s)
is included in the
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
-class
[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
. Therefore, the intersection
[f]_{\mathcal{C}}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s)
is either equal to
\alpha^{-1}(s)
(when
[f]_{\mathcal{C}}=[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
) or empty (when
[f]_{\mathcal{C}}\neq[s]_{\mathcal{C}}
). In the latter case,
\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[[f]_{\mathcal{C}}\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]
is empty. Therefore, we obtain that,

\tau([f]_{\mathcal{C}})=\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid\text{$[f]_{\mathcal{C}}=[s]_{\mathcal{C}}$ and $r\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]$}\}

Moreover, we have
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]=\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid\text{$r\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]$}\}
by definition. Therefore, this can be reformulated as,

\tau([f]_{\mathcal{C}})=\{(s,r)\in\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\mid[f]_{\mathcal{C}}=[s]_{\mathcal{C}}\}=T

This concludes the proof.

Third property

We show that
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-closure is satisfied. Consider a pair
(r,T)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
. Moreover, let
(e,f)\in T\subseteq M\times R
be an idempotent. We have to show that,

(e,f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f)\in\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]

This requires more work. We shall need the following simple fact. Recall that given a quotienting lattice
\mathcal{D}
, we write
{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}
for the quotienting lattice containing all complements of languages in
\mathcal{D}
.

Fact 7.16
.

There exists a
finite
quotienting lattice
\mathcal{D}\subseteq{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
such that,

\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]=\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})}[\alpha,\rho]

Proof.

For all
s\in M
, let
\mathbf{K}_{s}
be an optimal
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-cover of
\alpha^{-1}(s)
(for
\rho
). Recall that we showed in Lemma
3.6
that
PBPol(\mathcal{C})={Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C}))
. Thus, since there are finitely many languages in the sets
\mathbf{K}_{s}
for
s\in M
, there exists some
k\in{\rm Nature}
such that all these languages belong to
{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C}))
. Here,
Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
denotes a stratum in our stratification of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
into finite quotienting lattices (see Section
4
for details). It now suffices to choose
\mathcal{D}=Pol_{k}(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

In view of Fact
7.16
, it now suffices to show that,

(e,f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})}[\alpha,\rho]

By definition, this amounts to proving that
f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f\in\mathcal{I}_{{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]
. We fix an arbitrary optimal
{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
\alpha^{-1}(e)
and show that
f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f\in\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
. Since
{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}
is a finite quotienting lattice, we have a stratification of
{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})
which we introduced in Section
4
. Recall that the strata are denoted by
Pol_{k}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})
and the associated canonical preorder relations by
\leqslant_{k}
. Since
\mathbf{K}
is a finite set of languages in
{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})
, we have a stratum
k\in{\rm Nature}
such that all languages in
\mathbf{K}
belong to
Pol_{k}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})
. Consequently, we have
\mathcal{I}_{Pol_{k}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
and it now suffices to show that
f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f\in\mathcal{I}_{Pol_{k}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})}[\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]
. By Lemma
5.15
, we have to exhibit
w\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
K\subseteq A^{*}
such that
w\leqslant_{k}K
and
f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f\leq\rho(K)
. This is what we do.

We first use our hypotheses that
(r,T)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
and
(e,f)\in T
to introduce a few objects that we need to define
w
and
K
. We do so in the following lemma. Recall that
\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}
denotes the canonical preorder associated to the finite quotienting lattice
\mathcal{D}
(which is compatible with word concatenation by Lemma
2.9
).

Lemma 7.17
.

There exists
u,v\in A^{*}
and
H\subseteq A^{*}
satisfying the following conditions:

•

\rho(u)=r
,
\alpha(v)=e
and
f\leq\rho(H)
.

•

u\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}v
and
v\leqslant_{k}H
.

Proof.

We first define
u,v\in A^{*}
and
H\subseteq A^{*}
. Since
(r,T)\in\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]
and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
it follows from Fact
5.12
that
(r,T)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}[\beta,\tau]
. This implies that
T\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}[\beta^{-1}(r),\tau]
. Therefore, Lemma
5.15
yields
u\in\beta^{-1}(r)
and
L\subseteq A^{*}
such that
T\subseteq\tau(L)
and
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}L
. Moreover, since
(e,f)\in T\subseteq\tau(L)
, we obtain by definition of
\tau
that,

f\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[L\cap\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]

Since
\mathcal{D}\subseteq{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
, we have
Pol_{k}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})\subseteq{Pol\/}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})\subseteq PBPol(\mathcal{C})
. Therefore, it follows from Fact
5.12
that
f\in\mathcal{I}_{Pol_{k}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})}[L\cap\alpha^{-1}(e),\rho]
. Using Lemma
5.15
, this yields
v\in L\cap\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
H\subseteq A^{*}
such that
v\leqslant_{k}H
and
f\leq\rho(H)
.

Let us verify that
u,v\in A^{*}
and
H\subseteq A^{*}
satisfy the conditions described in the lemma. We have
\rho(u)=r
since
u\in\beta^{-1}(r)
which means that
\rho(u)=\beta(u)=r
. Moreover,
\alpha(v)=e
,
f\leq\rho(H)
and
v\leqslant_{k}H
hold by definition of
v
and
H
. Finally,
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}v
holds since
v\in L
and we have
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}L
. ∎

We are now ready to define the appropriate
w\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
K\subseteq A^{*}
such that
w\leqslant_{k}K
and
f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f\leq\rho(K)
. We fix
u,v\in A^{*}
and
H\subseteq A^{*}
as defined in Lemma
7.17
. Let
p\geq 1
be the period of
{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}
(see Fact
4.10
for the definition) and
\ell=p\times 2^{k+1}
. We define,

w=v^{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad K=H^{\ell+p-1}uH^{\ell}\cup H^{\ell}

Clearly,
w\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
since
\alpha(v)=e
and
e
is an idempotent of
M
. It remains to show that
w\leqslant_{k}K
and
f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f\leq\rho(K)
. We start with the latter. By definition and since
\rho
is a multiplicative rating map,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\rho(K)&=&(\rho(H))^{\ell+p-1}\cdot\rho(u)\cdot(\rho(H))^{\ell}+(\rho(H))^{\ell}\\
&=&(\rho(H))^{\ell+p-1}\cdot\rho(u)\cdot(\rho(H))^{\ell}+(\rho(H))^{\ell-1}\cdot\rho(\varepsilon)\cdot\rho(H)\end{array}

By definition in Lemma
7.17
, we have
\rho(u)=r
and
f\leq\rho(H)
. Thus, since
f
is idempotent, we obtain as desired that,

f\cdot(r+\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot f=f\cdot\rho(u)\cdot f+f\cdot\rho(\varepsilon)\cdot f\leq\rho(K)

We finish with the proof that
w\leqslant_{k}K
. Let
x\in K
, we show that
w\leqslant_{k}x
. By definition, we have
K=H^{\ell+p-1}uH^{\ell}\cup H^{\ell}
. Moreover, we know that
v\leqslant_{k}H
by definition in Lemma
7.17
. Thus, since
x\in K
and
\leqslant_{k}
is compatible with concatenation (see Lemma
2.9
), we obtain that one of the two following properties hold:

•

v^{\ell}\leqslant_{k}x
(when
x\in H^{\ell}
) or,

•

v^{\ell+p-1}\cdot u\cdot v^{\ell}\leqslant_{k}x
(when
x\in H^{\ell+p-1}uH^{\ell}
).

Thus, by transitivity, it suffices to show that
w\leqslant_{k}v^{\ell}
and
w\leqslant_{k}v^{\ell+p-1}\cdot u\cdot v^{\ell}
. The former is immediate: we have
w=v^{\ell}
by definition.

It remains to show that
w\leqslant_{k}v^{\ell+p-1}\cdot u\cdot v^{\ell}
. Recall that
\leqslant_{k}
is the canonical preorder of
Pol_{k}({co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D})
. By definition in Lemma
7.17
, we have
u\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}v
. Moreover, since
\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}
is compatible with concatenation, this implies
v^{p-1}u\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}v^{p}
. It follows that
v^{p}\leqslant_{{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}}v^{p-1}u
. Indeed,
v^{p-1}u\leqslant_{\mathcal{D}}v^{p}
means that for any
L\in\mathcal{D}
, we have
v^{p-1}u\in L\Rightarrow v^{p}\in L
. The contrapositive then states that for any
L\in\mathcal{D}
, we have
v^{p}\not\in L\Rightarrow v^{p-1}u\not\in L
. Finally, since the languages of
{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}
are the complements of those in
\mathcal{D}
, it follows that for all
L\in{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}
, we have
v^{p}\in L\Rightarrow v^{p-1}u\in L
, i.e.
v^{p}\leqslant_{{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}}v^{p-1}u
. Therefore, since
w=v^{\ell}
and
\ell=p2^{k+1}
where
p
is the period of
{co\/}\textup{-}\mathcal{D}
, we obtain as desired that
w\leqslant_{k}v^{\ell}\cdot v^{p-1}u\cdot v^{\ell}
from Lemma
4.12
.

8.
Completeness in Theorem
7.11

This last section is devoted to proving the difficult direction in Theorem
7.11
. It corresponds to completeness in the least fixpoint algorithm for
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-covering.

We let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
as a
\mathcal{C}
-compatible morphism and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
as a nice
\mathcal{C}
-compatible multiplicative rating map. Recall that Theorem
7.11
states that
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is the smallest
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset of
M\times R
. We have already showed in the previous section that
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated (this was the soundness proof). Therefore, it remains to show that it is the smallest such subset. This is the purpose of this section.

Thus, we fix some arbitrary
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated subset
S
of
M\times R
and prove that it includes
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
. That is, we show,

\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S

The argument is more involved than what we did for
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
in Section
6
. Indeed, the definition of
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated sets is involved and this is reflected in our argument.

We write
\beta:A^{*}\to R_{A^{*}}
for the canonical morphism associated to
\rho
(which is
\mathcal{C}
-compatible by Fact
5.8
). Moreover, we write
\tau:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
for the multiplicative rating map
\tau^{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}_{\alpha,\rho}
:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\tau:&2^{A^{*}}&\to&2^{M\times R}\\
&K&\mapsto&\{(s,r)\in M\times R\mid\text{$r\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[K\cap\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]$}\}\end{array}

Since
S
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated, the definition yields a subset
\mathcal{T}\subseteq R\times 2^{M\times R}
such that the pair
(S,\mathcal{T})
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated. We actually prove the two following inclusions,

\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq\mathcal{T}

Of course, the difficulty is that these two properties are mutually dependent. This is not surprising since the conditions which make
S
and
\mathcal{T}

PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated are mutually dependent themselves. We cope with this problem by following three steps:

(1)

Step 1:
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
argument:
First, we investigate the set
\mathcal{T}
. Let us point out that we do not yet show that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq\mathcal{T}
: getting this inclusion requires information on the set
S
and we do not have any at this point. Instead, we cope with this problem by introducing a new multiplicative rating map
\gamma:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
which is intuitively a weaker variant of
\tau
parametrized by the set
S
(for any
K
,
\gamma(K)
is a restriction of the set
\tau(K)\subseteq M\times R
which depends on
S
). The main result of this step is as follows:

\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\gamma]\subseteq\mathcal{T}

The key idea is that the definition of
\gamma
depends on
S
. Therefore, later in the proof, whenever we exhibit new elements in
S
, this simultaneously proves the existence of new elements in
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\gamma]
. Consequently, the above inclusion yields that these new elements belong to
\mathcal{T}
as well (and we may use them to exhibit even more elements in
S
). This is based on Theorem
6.5
(our characterization of
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-optimal pointed imprints): we apply it to the multiplicative rating map
\gamma
.

(2)

Step 2: a finite quotienting Boolean algebra
\mathcal{D}\subseteq{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
:
This step reexamines the results obtained in Step 1. We use our new multiplicative rating map
\gamma
to define a key object of the proof: a finite quotienting Boolean algebra
\mathcal{D}\subseteq{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})
. Intuitively,
\mathcal{D}
is designed to contain any language involved in an optimal
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
-cover of
\beta^{-1}(r)
for
\gamma
where
r\in R_{A^{*}}
.

(3)

Step 3:
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-argument:
This step uses all preliminary results that we have obtained in the previous ones to finally prove the desired property:

\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S

8.1.
Step 1:
{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})
argument

We start by defining the auxiliary multiplicative rating map
\gamma:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
that we announced above. Then, we use Theorem
6.5
to prove that,

\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\gamma]\subseteq\mathcal{T}

As we explained,
\gamma
is designed as a weaker variant of
\tau
parametrized by the set
S\subseteq M\times R
. We use this new object to cope with the fact that we do not yet have enough information on
S
to prove directly that
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. The definition is based on the notion of good languages which we present now.

Good languages.
Given some language
G\subseteq A^{*}
, we shall say that
G
is
good
when the two following conditions are satisfied:

•

G
is closed under infixes: for any
u,v_{1},v_{2}\in A^{*}
, if
v_{1}uv_{2}\in G
, then
u\in G
.

•

\tau(G)\subseteq S
.

Note that we already know at least one simple good language: the empty one (
\emptyset
) which trivially satisfies both conditions.

Remark 8.1
.

Our ultimate goal in the proof is to prove that
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S
. Thus, since
\tau(A^{*})=\{(s,r)\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha^{-1}(s),\rho]\}=\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]
by definition, this means that the universal language
A^{*}
is good. However, we
do not know this yet
. While this will be implicit, a key point in later steps of the proof is that it exhibits more and more elements within
S
. Therefore, we shall obtain increasingly large good languages until we prove that
A^{*}
itself is good.

We shall need the following fact which is used for combining two good languages into a single larger one.

Fact 1
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be good languages. Then
G_{1}G_{2}\cup G_{1}\cup G_{2}
is a good language as well.

Proof 8.2
.

Clearly,
G_{1}G_{2}\cup G_{1}\cup G_{2}
is closed under infixes since this was the case for both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Hence, we may concentrate on the second property in the definition of good languages. Since
\tau
is a multiplicative rating map, we have,

\tau(G_{1}G_{2}\cup G_{1}\cup G_{2})=\tau(G_{1})\cdot\tau(G_{2})\cup\tau(G_{1})\cup\tau(G_{2})

We already know that
\tau(G_{1})\subseteq S
and
\tau(G_{2})\subseteq S
since
G_{1},G_{2}
are good. Thus, it suffices to verify that
\tau(G_{1})\cdot\tau(G_{2})\subseteq S
. By definition,
(s,r)\in\tau(G_{1})\cdot\tau(G_{2})
satisfies
s=s_{1}s_{2}
and
r\leq r_{1}r_{2}
for
(s_{1},r_{1})\in\tau(G_{1})\subseteq S
and
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\tau(G_{2})\subseteq S
. Since
S
is
PBPol(\mathcal{C})
-saturated, it is closed under downset and multiplication which yields
(s,r)\in S
.

Definition of
\gamma
.
We now use good languages to introduce our new multiplicative rating map
\gamma:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M\times R}
. As we explained, it is defined from
\tau
. Recall that
2^{M\times R}
is a finite hemiring which we already used as the evaluation set for
\tau
. In particular, recall that it uses a special multiplication which we defined above. We define,

\begin{array}[]{llll}\gamma:&2^{A^{*}}&\to&2^{M\times R}\\
&K&\mapsto&\{(s,r)\mid\text{$(s,r)\in\tau(K\cap G)$ for some good language $G$}\}\end{array}

Note that the definition of
\gamma
depends on
S\subseteq M\times R
since this set constrains which languages are good.

Remark 8.3
.

This new map
\gamma
is strongly related to
\tau
. An apparent connection is that for any
K\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\gamma(K)\subseteq\tau(K)
. Indeed, given
(s,r)\in\gamma(K)
, we have
(s,r)\in\tau(K\cap G)
for some good language
G
. Moreover, we know that
\tau(K\cap G)\subseteq\tau(K)
since
K\cap G\subseteq K
. Thus, we get that
(s,r)\in\tau(K)
. However, the connection between
\gamma
and
\tau
is in fact much stronger. Since we shall later obtain that
A^{*}
is good (see Remark
8.1
), the converse inclusion holds as well:
\gamma(K)\subseteq\tau(K)
. Thus,
\gamma
and
\tau
are actually the same object. Unfortunately, this is an information that we are not able to use. Indeed, we only obtain it at the end of our proof: the fact that
A^{*}
is good follows from our end goal (
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S
).

Another way to explain the situation is as follows. We need to use
\gamma
since we are not able to prove the inclusion
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\tau]\subseteq\mathcal{T}
directly. However, it turns out that when we shall finally obtain
\mathcal{P}_{PBPol(\mathcal{C})}[\alpha,\rho]\subseteq S
(the proof relies on the inclusion
\mathcal{P}_{{Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})}[\beta,\gamma]\subseteq\mathcal{T}
), we will also realize that
\gamma
was
\tau
all along.

Before we can use
\gamma
, we first need to verify that it is indeed a multiplicative rating map. We do so in the next lemma (this is a consequence of the fact that
\tau
is a multiplicative rating map itself).

Lemma 8.4
.

The map
\gamma
is a multiplicative rating map.

Proof 8.5
.

We need to prove that
\gamma
is a hemiring morphism. We start by proving that it is a monoid morphism for addition. Clearly,
\gamma(\emptyset)=\emptyset
. Indeed,

\gamma(\emptyset)=\{(s,r)\mid\text{$(s,r)\in\tau(\emptyset)$}\}=\emptyset

We now show that for any
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\gamma(K_{1}\cup K_{2})=\gamma(K_{1})\cup\gamma(K_{2})
. Assume first that
(s,r)\in\gamma(K_{1})\cup\gamma(K_{2})
. By symmetry assume that
(s,r)\in\gamma(K_{1})
. By definition, we have a good language
G
such that
(s,r)\in\tau(K_{1}\cap G)
. Thus, since
K_{1}\cap G\subseteq(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap G
, we get
(s,r)\in\tau((K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap G)
which means that
(s,r)\in\gamma(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
as desired. Conversely, assume that
(s,r)\in\gamma(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
. By definition, there exists a good language
G
such that
(s,r)\in\tau((K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap G)
. Thus, since
\tau
is a morphism itself, we have
(s,r)\in\tau(K_{1}\cap G)\cup\tau(K_{2}\cap G)
and it follows that
(s,r)\in\gamma(K_{1})\cup\gamma(K_{2})
.

We now show that
\gamma
is a semigroup morphism for multiplication. Let
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
. We show that
\gamma(K_{1}K_{2})=\gamma(K_{1})\cdot\gamma(K_{2})
. Assume first that
(s,r)\in\gamma(K_{1})\cdot\gamma(K_{2})
. Thus, by definition of our multiplication on
2^{M\times R}
, we have
(s_{1},r_{1})\in\gamma(K_{1})
and
(s_{2},r_{2})\in\gamma(K_{2})
such that
s=s_{1}s_{2}
and
r\leq r_{1}r_{2}
. It then follows from the definition of
\gamma
that there exist two good languages
G_{1},G_{2}
such that,

(s_{1},r_{1})\in\tau(K_{1}\cap G_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad(s_{2},r_{2})\in\tau(K_{2}\cap G_{2})

Since we already know that
\tau
is multiplicative, it follows that
(s,r)\in\tau((K_{1}\cap G_{1})(K_{2}\cap G_{2}))
. Moreover, because

\par\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\par Consider the finite quotienting Boolean algebra $\mathcal{D}\subseteq{BPol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ that we defined in Step\penalty\ 2. Consequently, the Cartesian product $P=M\times({A^{*}}/{\sim_{\mathcal{D}}})$ is a finite monoid for the componentwise multiplication and the following map $\eta:A^{*}\to P$ is a morphism:
$$\begin{array}[]{llll}\eta:&A^{*}&\to&P\\

$$F^{\eta}(p,h,m)\subseteq\eta^{-1}(p)\subseteq A^{*}$$for the language of all words in $\eta^{-1}(p)$ which admit an $\eta$-factorization forest of height at most $h$ and idempotent height at most $m$.

We showed that the separation and covering problems are decidable for all classes of the form ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ and $PBPol(\mathcal{C})$ where $\mathcal{C}$ is an arbitrary finite quotienting Boolean algebra. As we explained, these results have important consequences for the quantifier alternation hierarchies within first-order logic overs words: we get that separation and covering are decidable for the level $\Sigma_{3}(<)$. This result may be lifted to the stronger logic $\Sigma_{3}(<,+1,min,max,\varepsilon)$ with a transfer result of\penalty\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{pzsucc}{}{}]}. Finally, one also gets that the membership problem is decidable for $\Sigma_{4}(<)$ and $\Sigma_{4}(<,+1,min,max,\varepsilon)$ using a reduction theorem of\penalty\ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{pzqalt}{}{}]}.
\par Let us point out that while this is not apparent in the above summary, we still know more about ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ than we do about $PBPol(\mathcal{C})$. Indeed, our ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$-covering algorithm is based on a characterization of \emph{${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$-optimal pointed imprints } which holds for {\bf any} multiplicative rating map. This result is stronger than the decidability of ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$-covering (one only needs to consider nice multiplicative rating maps for this). Moreover, the fact that we have this stronger result is precisely why we are able to handle the larger class $PBPol(\mathcal{C})$. On the other hand, while we also have a characterization of $PBPol(\mathcal{C})$-optimal pointed imprints, it only holds for nice multiplicative rating maps. Therefore, a natural next move is trying to generalize this characterization to all \emph{all} multiplicative rating maps. Indeed, such a result could be the key to solving covering for one more level in concatenation hierarchies.
\par Another interesting (and much simpler) objective is analyzing the complexity of the decision problems that we have just solved. Naturally, this depends on the finite quotienting Boolean algebra $\mathcal{C}$ for both ${Pol\/}(\mathcal{C})$ and $PBPol(\mathcal{C})$. We leave this for further work.
