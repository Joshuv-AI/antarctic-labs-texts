Abstract
. We study the free part of the Bernoulli action of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 and the Borel combinatorics of the associated Schreier graphs. We construct orthogonal decompositions of the spaces into marker sets with various additional properties. In general, for Borel graphs Γ \Gamma admitting weakly orthogonal decompositions, we show that χ B ​ ( Γ ) ≤ 2 ​ χ ​ ( Γ ) − 1 \chi_{B}(\Gamma)\leq 2\chi(\Gamma)-1 under some mild assumptions. As a consequence, we deduce that the Borel chromatic number for F ⁡ ( 2 ℤ n ) F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}) is 3 3 for all n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . Weakly orthogonal decompositions also give rise to Borel unlayered toast structures. We also construct orthogonal decompositions of F ⁡ ( 2 ℤ 2 ) F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}) with strong topological regularity, in particular with all atoms homeomorphic to a disk. This allows us to show that there is a Borel perfect matching for F ⁡ ( 2 ℤ n ) F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}) for all n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 and that there is a Borel lining of F ⁡ ( 2 ℤ 2 ) F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}) .

1.
Introduction

This paper is a contribution to the fast developing field of Borel combinatorics, which studies combinatorics of definable graphs and other structures on Polish spaces. For an overview of the entire field we refer the reader to the surveys
[
15
]
and
[
18
]
.

The object of study for Borel combinatorics is a Borel graph
G
on a Polish space
X
. Two main concepts that have been extensively explored are proper colorings and perfect matchings. A
proper coloring
of
G
is a map
c:V(G)\to\kappa
, where
\kappa
is a cardinal, such that for any
x,y\in V(G)
with
(x,y)\in E(G)
,
c(x)\neq c(y)
. A
perfect matching
of
G
is a map
m:V(G)\to V(G)
such that for all
x\in V(G)
,
(x,m(x))\in E(G)
and
m^{2}(x)=x
. Since these concepts are all functions, we may impose definability conditions on them and consider, for instance, continuous or Borel proper colorings (equipping the cardinal
\kappa
with the discrete topology) and continuous or Borel perfect matchings, etc.

The Borel graphs considered in this paper are Schreier graphs of marked group actions. A
marked group
is a pair
(\Gamma,S)
, where
\Gamma
is a group and
S
is a finite generating set of
\Gamma
. Usually we also require
S=S^{-1}
and
1_{\Gamma}\not\in S
. When the generating set is standard or otherwise understood, we omit specifying the set
S
and say that
\Gamma
is a marked group. The
Cayley graph

G(\Gamma,S)
of a marked group
(\Gamma,S)
is defined by

V(G(\Gamma,S))=\Gamma

and

E(G(\Gamma,S))=\{(g,h)\in\Gamma^{2}\,:\,\exists s\in S\ h=sg\}.

When there is a Borel action of a marked group
\Gamma
on a Polish space
X
, the
Schreier graph
of the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
on
X
, denoted
\Sigma(\Gamma,S)
, is defined by

V(\Sigma(\Gamma,S))=X

and

E(\Sigma(\Gamma,S))=\{(x,y)\in X^{2}\,:\,\exists s\in S\ y=s\cdot x\}.

The Schreier graph will be particularly nice when the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
is free; in this case the Schreirer graph on each orbit of the action will be a copy of the Cayley graph
G(\Gamma,S)
.

In this paper the particular marked groups that we consider are
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with their standard sets of generators
\{\pm e_{1},\dots,\pm e_{n}\}
where for
1\leq i\leq n
, the
i
-th coordinate of
e_{i}
is
1
and the other coordinates are
0
. Most of the time we will be considering the
Bernoulli shift
action of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
on
2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}
, where for
g\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
x\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}
,

(g\cdot x)(h)=x(g+h)

for all
h\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. In addition, since we are mostly interested in free actions, we consider the
free part
of the Bernoulli shift action:

F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})=\{x\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\,:\,\forall 0\neq g\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\ g\cdot x\neq x\}.

The systematic study of Borel combinatorics started with
[
16
]
, in which Kechris, Solecki, and Todorcevic introduced and studied the notion of Borel chromatic numbers. When there exists a Borel proper coloring
c:V(G)\to\kappa
for a countable cardinal
\kappa
, we call the least such
\kappa
the
Borel chromatic number
for
G
. A fundamental result proved in
[
16
]
states that, if in a Borel graph
G
every vertex has degree
\leq k
for some finite
k
, then the Borel chromatic number of
G
is
\leq k+1
. Marks
[
17
]
showed that this bound is optimal using Borel determinacy methods. He constructed marked group actions which give rise to Borel acyclic
k
-regular graphs with their Borel chromatic numbers achieving any number from
2
to
k+1
. More recently, Conley, Jackson, Marks, Seward, and Tucker-Drob
[
4
]
improved these examples to hyperfinite Borel graphs, more specifically, Borel graphs which come from marked group actions where the orbit equivalence relation is hyperfinite.

In the case of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
, it is well known by an ergodicity argument that the Borel chromatic number is at least
3
, although the chromatic number of the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is
2
. For
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}})
it is also well known that the Borel chromatic number is exactly
3
. For
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
where
n\geq 2
, Gao and Jackson
[
9
]
constructed a continuous proper coloring with
4
colors. In
[
11
]
the authors of the present paper proved that the continuous chromatic number of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
, where
n\geq 2
, is exactly
4
. We also announced that the Borel chromatic number of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
is
3
. In this paper we present a proof.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
and
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\curvearrowright X
is a free Borel action of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
on a Polish space
X
. Then the Borel chromatic number of the Schreier graph on
X
is
3
.

A classical theorem of König in graph theory (which is a special case of Hall’s marriage theorem) asserts that if
G
is a
k
-regular bipartite graph then
G
has a perfect matching. The Borel version of the statement turns out to be false in general. Marks
[
17
]
constructed, for every
k\geq 2
, a
k
-regular acyclic Borel bipartite graph with no Borel perfect matching. These Borel graphs come from actions of free groups with finitely many generators. In this paper we prove that there is a Borel matching for
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
for any
n\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
and
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\curvearrowright X
is a free Borel action of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
on a Polish space
X
. Then there is a Borel perfect matching for the Schreier graph on
X
.

A related concept in combinatoric is
proper edge-coloring
for a graph
G
, which is a map
k:E(G)\to\kappa
, where
\kappa
is a cardinal, such that for any
e,f\in E(G)
such that
e
and
f
share exactly one vertex,
k(e)\neq k(f)
. The least cardinal
\kappa
that admits a proper edge-coloring
k:E(G)\to\kappa
is called the
edge chromatic number
of
G
. A classical theorem of Vizing states that if in a graph
G
every vertex has degree
\leq k
, then the edge chromatic number of
G
is
\leq k+1
. By a theorem of König, a
k
-regular bipartite graph
G
has edge chromatic number
k
. The Borel versions of these results turn out all to be false in general. Marks
[
17
]
constructed
k
-regular acyclic Borel graphs which all have Borel chromatic number
2
but the Borel edge chromatic numbers vary arbitrarily from
k
to
2k-1
.

Recently, Grebík–Rozhoň
[
14
]
and Weilacher
[
22
]
independently showed that the Borel edge chromatic number for
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
, where
n\geq 2
, is
2n
. This implies our Theorem
1.2
. Bencs–Hrǔsková–Tóth
[
1
]
showed the same theorem for
n=2
, which is a weaker result but sufficient to deduce our theorem. Of course, the methods used in these proofs are all different from the one presented in this paper. In contrast, in
[
11
]
the authors of the present paper showed that the continuous edge chromatic number of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})
is
5
. More recently, Gao–Wang–Wang–Yan
[
12
]
showed that the continuous edge chromatic number of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
for
n\geq 2
is exactly
2n+1
.

In this paper we also consider the combinatorial concepts of line section and lining in a Schreier graph of a marked group action. Following
[
11
]
, a
line section
of
\Sigma(\Gamma,S)
is a subgraph
G
where each vertex in
G
has degree
2
. If
G
is a line section, we call each connected component of
G
a
G
-line
. A line section
G
is
complete
if
G
meets every orbit of the action. A line section
G
is
single
if for each
x\in X
, the intersection of
G
with the orbit of
x
is a nonempty single
G
-line. A
lining

G
is a single line section where the vertex set of
G
is the entire space, and in particular it is a complete line section. A line section
G
on
X
is
Borel
(
clopen
, resp.) if for each generator
e\in S
, the set
\{x\in X\,:\,(x,e\cdot x)\in G\}
is Borel (clopen, resp.).

The authors of the present paper studied the existence of clopen line sections in
[
11
]
. We showed that there do not exist clopen single line sections of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})
; in particular, there are no clopen linings of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})
. In this paper we prove that there exists a Borel lining of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\curvearrowright X
is a free Borel action of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on a Polish space
X
. Then there is a Borel lining for the Schreier graph on
X
.

Recently, Chandgotia–Unger
[
3
]
has proved the existence of Borel linings in higher dimensions with a different method.

The method we use to prove all these combinatorial results can be collectively called the
orthgonal marker method
. The simplest form of this method was first developed by Gao and Jackson in
[
9
]
to tackle the hyperfiniteness problem for countable abelian group actions. Later Schneider and Seward
[
19
]
extended the orthogonal marker method to countable locally nilpotent groups and used it to show the hyperfiniteness of the orbit equivalence relations of their actions. It was already clear in
[
9
]
that the orthognal marker regions we constructed not only can facilitate a proof of hyperfiniteness but also allows us to prove results of combinatorial nature about the Schreier graphs. In fact, it was shown in
[
9
]
that for any
n\geq 2
, there is a continuous proper coloring of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
with
4
colors.

In this paper we further develop the orthogonal marker method and use it to prove our theorems stated above. In Section 2 we first present a review of the orthogonal marker method and prove some improvements. In general, an
orthogonal marker structure
is a sequence
\{\mathcal{R}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of partitions of the phase space
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
such that each element in
\mathcal{R}_{k}
(which we refer to as a
marker region
) is a finite subset of an orbit with certain properties. We regard each
\mathcal{R}_{n}
as a
layer
of the orthogonal marker structure, and
orthogonality
refers to the relationship between marker regions from different layers. The main improvement we explore in Section 2 is that, by controlling the geometric parameters used in the construction of
\mathcal{R}_{k}
, we are able to obtain orthogonal marker regions that are connected. It turns out that connectedness is a key consideration in the construction of combinatorial objects such as proper colorings, perfect matchings, and linings.

Starting from Section 3 we adopt a slightly different point of view when we consider an orthogonal marker structure
\{\mathcal{R}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. Here, for each
k\geq 1
we consider all the partitions
\mathcal{R}_{m}
for
m\geq k
and the coarsest common refinement of all of them, denoted
\mathcal{A}_{k}
. The elements of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
are called
k
-atoms
of the orthogonal marker structure. We also consider the decomposition of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
into finite connected components and define notions of strong and weak orthogonality. In Section 4 we work with weakly orthogonal decompositions with polynomial bound and some additional boundedness condition on the orthogonality constant and prove some results about their Borel chromatic numbers. These results imply Theorem
1.1
. We also show that weak orthogonal decompositions give rise to a combinatorial structure known as
toast
in the literature (defined in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
). In particular, we show that there exists a Borel unlayered toast on
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
for all
n\geq 1
.

In Section 5 we prove the main technical theorem of this paper about orthgonal decompositions. We show that for
n=2
, the orthogonal marker structure
\{\mathcal{R}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
can be constructred so that all
k
-atoms are homeomorphic to disks. This has consequences about the exact structures of
k
-atoms as well as their relationship with the
(k-1)
-atoms, which allow us to perform constructions in Section 6 of a Borel perfect matching (Theorem
1.2
) and in Section 7 of a Borel lining (Theorem
1.3
).

Orthogonal markers and structures for

\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Here we review the construction, summarize the main results, and

The notion of an orthogonal decomposition will be a key component of many of our arguments.

2.1.
Review of clopen rectangular partition construction

A frequent way to construct clopen rectangular partitions is to start with

d
-marker sets. We recall this definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
d>0
be an integer. A
d
-marker set

M_{d}\subseteq F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
is a set satisfying
the following:

(1)

\forall x\neq y\in M_{d}\ \rho(x,y)>d
.

(2)

\forall x\in F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})\ \exists y\in M_{d}\ \rho(x,y)\leq d
.

We often refer to
d
as a
marker distance
. We say the
d
-marker set
M_{d}
is clopen if the set
M_{d}
is a relatively clopen set in
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
.

For each marker distance
d
there is a clopen partition
\mathcal{R}_{d}
of

F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
into rectangles with side lengths in
\{d,d+1\}
. More
precisely,
\mathcal{R}_{d}
is a subequivalence relation of
F_{n}
with the
property that on each
F_{n}
class
[x]
,
\mathcal{R}_{d}\restriction[x]
is a
partition of
[x]
into finite sets, each of which is a rectangle in
the sense that it is of the form
R\cdot y
for some
y\in[x]
where

R\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a rectangle (i.e.,
R=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\prod_{i}[a_{i},b_{i}]
). Also,
\mathcal{R}_{d}
is clopen in the sense that we have the
relative clopenness in
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
of the
set
C
of
x\in F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
such that
[x]_{\mathcal{R}_{d}}=R\cdot x
for some
R

of the form
R=\prod_{i}[0,c_{i}]
. Intuitively, elements of the set
C

are the “bottom-left” corners of all rectangles in the partition
given by
\mathcal{R}_{d}
. We will, with a slight abuse of terminolgy, refer to
\mathcal{R}_{d}

as a “clopen partition” or “clopen finite subequivalence relation” though

\mathcal{R}_{d}
is not relatively clopen as a subset of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})\times F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
.

In
[
9
]
a clopen rectangular partition
\mathcal{R}_{d}
with side lengths in

\{d,d+1\}
is constructed by first starting with a
d^{\prime}
-marker set
M_{d^{\prime}}
with
d^{\prime}\gg d
.
About each point
x\in M_{d^{\prime}}
we consider the “cube”
C=C_{x}
in
[x]
centered at

x
with side lengths
d^{\prime}
. These cubes clearly cover
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
by property (
2
) of Definition
2.1
. We refer to this collection of cubes as a
clopen covering
of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
. For each of these
cubes
C
we “adjust” it by moving each of its faces outward with a distance bounded by a
small fraction of
d^{\prime}
(say no more than
\frac{1}{10}d^{\prime}
). When doing the adjustment we ensure that
each of the faces
f
of
C
stays a certain distance, bounded below by a
fixed fraction
\epsilon d^{\prime}
of
d^{\prime}
(where
\epsilon
is a fixed constant depending only
on
n
), away from any parallel face
f^{\prime}
of a cube
C^{\prime}
within a Hausdorff distance of
5d^{\prime}
from
C
.
To do these adjustments we use a
big-marker-little-marker
procedure. More precisely, we use an auxiliary distance
d^{\prime\prime}\gg d^{\prime}
and a

d^{\prime\prime}
-marker set
M_{d^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq M_{d}
(actually, to make
M_{d^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq M_{d^{\prime}}

we must relax slightly the inequalities in Definition
2.1
by replacing

d
with
(1-\eta)d
for property (
1
) and
(1+\eta)d
for property (
2
) for some small
\eta
). We use
M_{d^{\prime\prime}}

to organize the adjustment process, first adjusting the points of
M_{d^{\prime\prime}}
,
then at step

g_{i}\cdot M_{d^{\prime}}
not already considered, where
\{g_{i}\}
is an enumeration of all

within distance

to the origin. As
d^{\prime\prime}\gg d^{\prime}
, the cubes being adjusted
at any given step do not interfere with each other. After finitely many steps
(roughly
(d^{\prime\prime})^{n}

a clopen partition
\mathcal{R}_{d^{\prime}}
of

into regions each of which is the disjoint union of finitely many rectangles

of side lengths between

. If

is large enough so that

, then we proceed with a

In summary, the key steps of the clopen rectangular partition construction for a marker distance

are the following:

(1)

Find a clopen covering of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
by rectangles of side lengths

;

(2)

Using a big-marker-little-marker procedure, adjust the rectangles in the clopen covering so that any parallel faces of rectangles are at least
\epsilon d^{\prime}
apart, for some constant
\epsilon
depending only on

into rectangular polygonal regions;

(3)

Use a finite subdivision algorithm to divide each of the rectangular polygonal regions in the clopen partition into rectangles of side lengths

or

3.
Orthogonal Decompositions in
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})

In §
2
we recalled the construction of the orthogonal marker regions
\mathcal{R}_{k}=\mathcal{R}^{k}_{1}
for
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})

corresponding to a set of marker distances
d_{1}<d_{2}<\cdots
(which satisfy
d_{k+1}/d_{k}\geq K

for some constant
K=K(n)
depending only on the dimension
n
).
A slightly different point of view results in what
we call an
orthogonal decomposition
for
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
. In §
4
we will present a
more general form of the definition in the context of a Borel graphing of a countable Borel equivalence relation.
The basic idea is to consider the sets
X_{k}=\partial\mathcal{R}_{k}=\bigcup\{\partial R:R\in\mathcal{R}_{k}\}
, the set of boundary
points for the regions in the Borel partition
\mathcal{R}_{k}
. These sets are not pairwise disjoint, but they intersect in
a controlled fashion which we make precise below. We record here the strongest forms of these intersection properties,
while in §
4
we abstract a weaker version on these properties which nonetheless suffices
for some applications (e.g., the existence of toast structures and the Borel proper
3
-coloring of the Schreier graph on
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
).

For each
k
we consider all the partitions
\mathcal{R}_{m}
for
m\geq k
and let
\mathcal{A}_{k}
be the coarsest common refinement of all of them. We call the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
the
k
-atoms
of the orthogonal decomposition. In contrast, we write
X_{k}^{\infty}
for
\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}X_{m}=\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}\partial\mathcal{R}_{m}
and let
\mathcal{R}(k)
denote the collection of connected components of
X-X_{k}^{\infty}
(where
X=F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
).
We call these connected components of
X-X_{k}^{\infty}
the
k
-nuclei
of the orthogonal
decomposition. In §
5
we will show that we may maintain certain topological regularity for the

k
-atoms and the
k
-nuclei, as shown possible in Theorem
2.5
for the marker regions
R\in\mathcal{R}_{k}
themselves. This will guarantee that
the
k
-atoms and
k
-nuclei are in one-one correspondence, and that each
k
-nucleus is exactly the interior of a
k
-atom.
In the rest of this section we describe the basic orthogonality properties of the
k
-nuclei.

The following theorem summarizes the properties of the orthogonal decomposition.

Theorem 3.1
.

Proof.

Let
R
be a connected component of
X-X_{k}^{\infty}
.
We first show (
2
). Let
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots<\ell_{m}

enumerate the
\ell>k
such that
\rho(X_{\ell},R)=1
.
Suppose
m>n
. For each
1\leq j\leq m
let
x_{j}\in X_{\ell_{j}}

be such that
\rho(x_{j},R)=1
. There must be
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
such that

\delta(x_{j_{1}},\ell_{j_{1}},k+1)=\delta(x_{j_{2}},\ell_{j_{2}},k+1)
. However,
we then have that
\rho(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}})>\epsilon d_{k+1}>2d_{k}
(we assume
K
is large enough so that
\epsilon d_{i+1}>2d_{i}
,
that is,
K>\frac{2}{\epsilon}
). However, since

\rho(x_{j_{1}},R)=1
,
\rho(x_{j_{2}},R)=1
, we have that
\rho(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}})\leq d_{k}+2
, a contradiction.

Next we show (
1
). Let
R
be a connected component of
X-X_{k}^{\infty}
,
let
x\in R
, and let

with
\ell\geq k
and

\rho(x,y)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}d_{1}
. Consider the path

from

to
y
such that
\rho(z,y)\leq\rho(x,y)
for all

.
Let

be the first point on the path not in

.
Say
x^{\prime}\in X_{\ell^{\prime}}
. If
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
, then
let
x^{\prime\prime}
be the point of

immediately before

, and we have that

and

\rho(x^{\prime\prime},X_{\ell})=1
.

So, assume
\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell
.
We thus have points
x^{\prime}\in X_{\ell^{\prime}}
,

with
\rho(x^{\prime},R)=1
and
\rho(x^{\prime},y)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}d_{1}
.
Recall

are the coordinate vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Let
x_{0}=x^{\prime}
,
y_{0}=y
, and assume
(\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}e_{n})\cdot x_{0}=y_{0}
for
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We claim that either
\rho(R,X_{\ell})=1
(and thus the corollary is proved) or there are
\beta_{1},\gamma_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}\geq 0
,
\beta_{1}\gamma_{1}\leq 0
, and
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}-\gamma_{1}
such that, letting
x_{1}=(\beta_{1}e_{1})\cdot x_{0}
and

, we have
x_{1}\in X_{\ell^{\prime}}
,

,

, and
\rho(x_{1},y_{1})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}d_{1}
.

If
\alpha_{1}=0
then let
\beta_{1}=\gamma_{1}=0
and we have

and
y_{1}=y_{0}
. Suppose
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
. Let
\beta\geq 0
be the largest such that
\beta\leq|\alpha_{1}|
and
\mbox{sgn}(\alpha_{1})\beta e_{1}\cdot x_{0}
is an element of
X_{\ell^{\prime}}
and is of distance

to

. Also let
\gamma\geq 0
be the largest such that
\gamma\leq|\alpha_{1}|
and
-\mbox{sgn}(\alpha_{1})\gamma e_{1}\cdot y_{0}
is an element of
X_{\ell}
. If
\beta+\gamma\geq|\alpha_{1}|

\beta_{1}=\mbox{sgn}(\alpha_{1})\beta
and
\gamma_{1}=\beta_{1}-\alpha_{1}
. Suppose
\beta+\gamma<|\alpha_{1}|
. Then
-\mbox{sgn}(\alpha_{1})\gamma e_{1}\cdot y_{0}

of

that is perpendicular to

. On the other hand, we have two possible cases for the reason

Applying the claim, we either have verified the corollary, or we will get

∎

4.
Weakly Orthogonal Decompositions

In this section we present some applications of the weakly orthogonal decompositions. One of the applications is to the computation of Borel chromatic numbers. Our results will imply that for all
n\geq 2
, the Schreier graph of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
has Borel chromatic number
3
. This is in contrast with the result in
[
11
]
that their continuous chromatic numbers are
4
. These are the first known results where the Borel characteristics and the continuous characteristics of countabe group actions behave differently. Another application is to the existence of the so-called Borel unlayered toast structures on
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
. The significance of this notion is that it implies hyperfiniteness for the orbit equivalence relation. In contrast with other known results, we have shown in
[
11
]
that continuous toast structures do not exist, and in
[
10
]
that Borel layered toast structures do not exist.

Fix a standard Borel space
X
and a Borel graph
\Gamma\subseteq X\times X
on
X
. Let
\rho_{\Gamma}
denote the shortest-path-length metric on the connected components of
\Gamma
.

Definition 4.1
.

A
weakly orthogonal decomposition of
X
is a collection
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of Borel subsets of
X
satisfying the following two conditions. Below we write
X_{k}^{\infty}
for
\bigcup_{n=k}^{\infty}X_{n}
.

(i)

(decomposition) Every connected component of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty})
is finite, and
\bigcap_{k\geq 1}X_{k}^{\infty}=\varnothing
.

(ii)

(weakly orthogonal) There exists a constant
Q
such that for every
k\geq 1
and every connected component
R
of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty})
we have for all
n\geq k

\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{n}^{\infty})\leq Q\Longrightarrow\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{n}^{\infty})=1.

We furthermore say that
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
has
bounded geometry
if in addition to the above properties it satisfies the following.

(iii)

(bounded geometry) There exists a constant
P
such that for every
k\geq 1
and every connected component
R
of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty})
we have

|\{n\geq k:\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{n})=1\}|\leq P.

We call the smallest
P
satisfying (iii) the
polygonal bound
for
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, and we call the smallest
Q
satisfying (ii) the
orthogonality constant
for
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
.

As we have shown in Corollary
3.2
, in
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})
bounded geomtry weakly orthogonal decompositions exist with polygonal bound
P=n+1
and orthogonality constant
Q=\frac{\epsilon}{2}d_{1}
. Here the constant
\epsilon
depends on
n
but
d_{1}
does not. In particular, we can pick
d_{1}
to be arbitrarily large.

We observe that the existence of a weakly orthogonal decomposition of
X
necessitates hyperfiniteness.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
be a standard Borel space and let
\Gamma\subseteq X\times X
be a Borel graph on
X
. Let
E_{\Gamma}
be the equivalence relation on
X
given by the connected components of
\Gamma
. If there exists a weakly orthogonal decomposition of
X
then
E_{\Gamma}
is hyperfinite.

Proof.

Fix a weakly orthogonal decomposition
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
X
. For
k\geq 1
let
E_{k}
be the equivalence relation which is trivial (consists of singletons) when restricted to
X_{k}^{\infty}
and which when restricted to
X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty}
is given by the connected components of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty})
. The sequence
(E_{k})_{k\geq 1}
witnesses the hyperfiniteness of
E_{\Gamma}
.
∎

We also observe that if a Borel graph
\Gamma
admits a weakly orthogonal decomposition with bounded
geometry, then necessarily
\Gamma
is locally finite. For if
x\in X
, then by bounded geometry, there are only finitely many

n
such that
\rho(x,X_{n})=1
. Let
m
be large enough so that if
\rho(x,X_{n})=1
, then
n<m
, and
x\notin X^{\infty}_{m}
. But then

X\setminus X_{m}^{\infty}
contains
x
and all the neighbors of
x
, and so the connected component of
X\setminus X_{m}^{\infty}

which contains
x
contains all of its neighbors. Since this component is finite,
x
has only finitely many neighbors in
\Gamma
.

For the remainder of this section we fix a bounded geometry weakly orthogonal decomposition
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
X
with polygonal bound
P
and orthogonality constant
Q
. We set
X_{k}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{n=k}^{\infty}X_{n}
.

Definition 4.3
.

A set
R\subseteq X
is a
k
-nucleus
if
R
is a connected component of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty})
. We say that
R
is an
nucleus
if
R
is a
k
-nucleus for some
k\geq 1
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
R
be a nucleus.

•

We define the
stage
of
R
, denoted
s(R)
, to be the maximum
k
such that
R
is a
k
-nucleus.

•

For
m\geq 1
, we define the
m
-boundary index of
R
to be

B_{R}(m)=\{n\geq m:\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{n})=1\}.

•

We define the
amplitude
of
R
to be
a(R)=\max B_{R}(s(R))
.

•

For
d\geq 1
, the
d
-fundamental interior
of
R
is

f_{d}(R)=\{r\in R:\forall n\geq s(R)\ \ \rho_{\Gamma}(r,X_{n}^{\infty})\geq P\cdot d-|B_{R}(n+1)|\cdot d\}.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be nuclei.

(i)

If
R\cap R^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
and
s(R)\leq s(R^{\prime})
then
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
.

(ii)

If
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
and
R\neq R^{\prime}
then
s(R)<s(R^{\prime})
.

(iii)

If
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
then
B_{R}(n)\subseteq B_{R^{\prime}}(n)
for all
n\geq s(R^{\prime})
.

(iv)

|B_{R}(n+1)|\leq P-1
for all
n\geq s(R)
.

(v)

If
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
then
a(R)\leq a(R^{\prime})
.

Proof.

(i). By definition
R
is connected in
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{s(R)}^{\infty})
and therefore connected in the larger graph
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{s(R^{\prime})}^{\infty})
. The set
R^{\prime}
is a connected component of the latter graph, so as
R\cap R^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
we must have
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
.

(ii). For every
k\geq s(R^{\prime})
,
R
is strictly contained in
R^{\prime}
which is connected in the graph
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty})
. So
R
is not a connected component of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{k}^{\infty})
. Therefore
s(R)<s(R^{\prime})
.

(iii). Fix
n\geq s(R^{\prime})
. Suppose that
m\in B_{R}(n)
. Then
\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{m})=1
. Since
R^{\prime}
is a connected component of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{s(R^{\prime})}^{\infty})
and
m\geq n\geq s(R^{\prime})
, we must have
R^{\prime}\cap X_{m}=\varnothing
. Using
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
we obtain
0<\rho_{\Gamma}(R^{\prime},X_{m})\leq\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{m})=1
. Therefore
m\in B_{R^{\prime}}(n)
.

(iv). This follows immediately from the bounded geometry property in Definition
4.1
and the fact that by definition
s(R)\not\in B_{R}(n+1)
for
n\geq s(R)
.

(v). It is immediate from the definitions that
s(R)\leq a(R)
and
s(R^{\prime})\leq a(R^{\prime})
. If
a(R)\leq s(R^{\prime})
then we are done. Otherwise
a(R)\in B_{R}(s(R^{\prime}))\subseteq B_{R^{\prime}}(s(R^{\prime}))
by (iii) and hence

a(R^{\prime})=\max B_{R^{\prime}}(s(R^{\prime}))\geq\max B_{R}(s(R^{\prime}))=\max B_{R}(s(R))=a(R).\qed

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be nuclei and fix
d\geq 1
. Assume that
P\cdot d\leq Q
.

(i)

\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X\setminus R)\geq d
.

(ii)

If
R\cap R^{\prime}=\varnothing
then
\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),f_{d}(R^{\prime}))\geq 2d
.

(iii)

If
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
then
f_{d}(R)\subseteq f_{d}(R^{\prime})
.

(iv)

If
f_{d}(R)\subseteq f_{d}(R^{\prime})
and
a(R)<a(R^{\prime})
then
\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X\setminus f_{d}(R^{\prime}))\geq d
.

Proof.

(i).
R
is a connected component of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{s(R)}^{\infty})
, so from Lemma
4.5
.(iv) we obtain

\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X\setminus R)=\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X_{s(R)}^{\infty})\geq P\cdot d-|B_{R}(s(R)+1)|\cdot d\geq d.

(ii). By swapping
R
and
R^{\prime}
if necessary, we may assume that
s(R)\leq s(R^{\prime})
. Since
R^{\prime}
is a finite connected component of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{s(R^{\prime})}^{\infty})
and
R\cap X_{s(R^{\prime})}^{\infty}=\varnothing
, we have that
\rho_{\Gamma}(R,R^{\prime})\geq 2
. So from (i) we obtain

\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),f_{d}(R^{\prime}))\geq\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X\setminus R)+\rho_{\Gamma}(R,R^{\prime})-1+\rho_{\Gamma}(X\setminus R^{\prime},f_{d}(R^{\prime}))-1\geq 2d.

(iii). By Lemma
4.5
.(iii) we have for all
n\geq s(R^{\prime})

\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X_{n}^{\infty})\geq P\cdot d-|B_{R}(n+1)|\cdot d\geq P\cdot d-|B_{R^{\prime}}(n+1)|\cdot d.

It follows from the definitions that
f_{d}(R)\subseteq f_{d}(R^{\prime})
.

(iv). Fix
y
with
\rho_{\Gamma}(y,f_{d}(R))<d
. We will argue that
y\in f_{d}(R^{\prime})
. By (i) we have that
y\in R\subseteq R^{\prime}
. Consider
s(R^{\prime})\leq n<a(R^{\prime})
. By Lemma
4.5
.(iii) we have
B_{R}(n+1)\subseteq B_{R^{\prime}}(n+1)
. The assumption
a(R)<a(R^{\prime})
further gives
a(R^{\prime})\in B_{R^{\prime}}(n+1)\setminus B_{R}(n+1)
so that
|B_{R}(n+1)|+1\leq|B_{R^{\prime}}(n+1)|
. Therefore for
s(R^{\prime})\leq n<a(R^{\prime})
we have

\rho_{\Gamma}(y,X_{n}^{\infty})>\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X_{n}^{\infty})-d\geq P\cdot d-|B_{R}(n+1)|\cdot d-d\geq P\cdot d-|B_{R^{\prime}}(n+1)|\cdot d.

For
n\geq a(R^{\prime})
we have
n\not\in B_{R}(s(R))
since
a(R)<a(R^{\prime})
. So
\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{n}^{\infty})>1
and thus by the weakly orthogonal property and (i) we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\Gamma}(y,X_{n}^{\infty})

\displaystyle>\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X_{n}^{\infty})-d\geq\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{d}(R),X\setminus R)+\rho_{\Gamma}(R,X_{n}^{\infty})-1-d

\displaystyle\geq Q+1-1=Q\geq P\cdot d=P\cdot d-|B_{R^{\prime}}(n+1)|\cdot d.

We conclude that
y\in f_{d}(R^{\prime})
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The weakly orthogonal property has only been used thus far in proving clause (iv) of Lemma
4.6
.

For a graph
\Gamma
on
X
and
n\geq 1
define

\Gamma^{(n)}=\{(x,y):x\neq y\text{ and }\rho_{\Gamma}(x,y)\leq n\}.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
X
be a standard Borel space and let
\Gamma\subseteq X\times X
be a Borel graph on
X
. Let
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a bounded geometry weakly orthogonal decomposition of
X
with polygonal bound
P
and orthogonality constant
Q
. If
d\geq 1
and
(2d+1)\cdot P\leq Q
, then there is a Borel set
Y\subseteq X
such that both of the graphs
\Gamma^{(d)}\restriction Y
and
\Gamma^{(d)}\restriction(X\setminus Y)
have finite connected components.

Proof.

Call a nucleus
R

maximal
if
a(R)<a(R^{\prime})
for every nucleus
R^{\prime}
with
R^{\prime}\supsetneq R
. For a set
A\subseteq X
set

\partial_{d}A=\{a\in A:\rho_{\Gamma}(a,X\setminus A)\leq d\}.

Define

Y=\bigcup\Big\{\partial_{d}f_{2d+1}(R):R\text{ is a maximal nucleus}\Big\}.

Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be maximal nuclei with

\rho_{\Gamma}(\partial_{d}f_{2d+1}(R),\partial_{d}f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime}))\leq d.

By Lemma
4.6
.(i) we must have that
R\cap R^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
. By swapping
R
and
R^{\prime}
, we may suppose that
s(R)\leq s(R^{\prime})
so that
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
. It follows from Lemma
4.6
.(iii) that
f_{2d+1}(R)\subseteq f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime})
. We have

\rho_{\Gamma}(f_{2d+1}(R),X\setminus f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime}))\leq d+\rho_{\Gamma}(\partial_{d}f_{2d+1}(R),\partial_{d}f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime}))\leq 2d<2d+1,

so from Lemma
4.6
.(iv) we conclude that
a(R)=a(R^{\prime})
. By maximality it follows that
R=R^{\prime}
. We conclude that every connected component of
\Gamma^{(d)}\restriction Y
is contained in
\partial_{d}f_{2d+1}(R)
for some maximal nucleus
R
and hence is finite.

Now fix
x\in X\setminus Y
. By Definition
4.1
.(i) there is
k\geq 1
with
\rho_{\Gamma}(x,X_{k}^{\infty})>P\cdot(2d+1)
. Let
R
be the unique
k
-nucleus containing
x
. Notice that
x\in f_{2d+1}(R)
. Any nucleus
R^{\prime}\supseteq R
with
a(R^{\prime})=a(R)
must be contained in the same connected component of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{a(R)}^{\infty})
as
R
. Since the connected components of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus X_{a(R)}^{\infty})
are finite, there must exist a maximal
R^{\prime}\supseteq R
with
a(R^{\prime})=a(R)
. By Lemma
4.6
.(iii) we have
x\in f_{2d+1}(R)\subseteq f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime})
. Since
\partial_{d}f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime})\subseteq Y
and
x\in f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime})\setminus Y
, the connected component of
x
in
\Gamma^{(d)}\restriction(X\setminus Y)
is contained in
f_{2d+1}(R^{\prime})
and is therefore finite.
∎

The following corollary is based off an argument due to Conley and Miller
[
7
]
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
X
be a standard Borel space and let
\Gamma\subseteq X\times X
be a Borel graph on
X
with
\chi(\Gamma)<\aleph_{0}
. Suppose that there is a bounded geometry weakly orthogonal decomposition of
X
with polygonal bound
P
and orthogonality constant
Q
satisfying
5\cdot P\leq Q
. Then

\chi_{B}(\Gamma)\leq 2\cdot\chi(\Gamma)-1.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.8
, there is a Borel set
Y\subseteq X
such that both of the graphs
\Gamma^{(2)}\restriction Y
and
\Gamma^{(2)}\restriction(X\setminus Y)
have finite connected components. In particular, the connected components of
\Gamma\restriction Y
are finite and therefore we can find a Borel chromatic coloring

c_{Y}:Y\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,\chi(\Gamma)\}

of
\Gamma\restriction Y
. Set
Y^{\prime}=Y\setminus c_{Y}^{-1}(\chi(\Gamma))
and set
c=c_{Y}\restriction Y^{\prime}
. Since we removed a single color from
Y
, we did not remove any pair of adjacent points in
Y
. It follows that for every connected component
C^{\prime}
of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus Y^{\prime})
,
there is a connected component
C
of
\Gamma^{(2)}\restriction(X\setminus Y)
such that
C^{\prime}
is contained in the union of
C
and the set of
neighbors of
C
.
Since
C
is finite and
\Gamma
is locally finite, it follows that
C^{\prime}
is finite.
Therefore there is a Borel chromatic coloring

d\colon X\setminus Y^{\prime}\rightarrow\{\chi(\Gamma),\chi(\Gamma)+1,\ldots,2\cdot\chi(\Gamma)-1\}

of
\Gamma\restriction(X\setminus Y^{\prime})
. The functions
c
and
d
use disjoint colors, so
c\cup d
is a Borel chromatic
(2\cdot\chi(\Gamma)-1)
-coloring of
\Gamma
.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

For every
n\geq 1
,
\chi_{B}(F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}})=3
.

Proof.

We observe that
\chi(F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}))=2
and that Corollary
3.2
holds with

and
Q=\frac{\epsilon}{2}d_{1}
, where we can take

to be arbitrarily large. Then the conclusion holds by Corollary
4.9
.
∎

]

Definition 4.11

Let

be a standard Borel space and

be a Borel graph on

\rho_{\Gamma}
be the shortest-path-length metric on the connected components of

be the equivalence relation on

5.

The orthogonality results of Theorem

these arguments for dimension

\mathbb{R}^{2}

6.1.
Charges, corner functions, and finite Stokes theorems

We first prove a combinatorial result for
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
itself. In fact, this result can be extended to

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which we give, though we only need the case

. The result is a sort of “finite
version” of Stokes theorem. We first present the

For
x=(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let
q(x)=(-1)^{i+j}
and call it the
charge
of
x
.
For
x=(i,j),y=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let
\rho(x,y)=\max\{|i-i^{\prime}|,|j-j^{\prime}|\}

denote the
\ell^{\infty}
distance between
x
and
y
. If
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
let
\partial F=\{x\in F\colon\rho(x,\mathbb{Z}^{2}-F)=1\}
.

For
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
we define a
corner function

c\colon F\to\mathbb{Z}

as follows (the name derives from the fact that for a rectangular polygon in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

the function
c(x)
is
0
except when
x
is a corner point of the boundary).
For
x\in F
we set

c(x)=4-2e(x)+s(x),

where
e(x)
is the number of horizontal and vertical edges that connect
x
to a point of

F
, that is,
e(x)=|\{p\in\{(\pm 1,0),(0,\pm 1)\}\colon x+p\in F\}|
.
Also
s(x)
is the number of points
y
of the form
x+(\pm 1,\pm 1)
which are in
F

and are such that the two points of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which are adjacent to both
x
and
y

are also in
F
.

The following is our “finite Stokes theorem” for finite
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Theorem 6.2

(Finite Stokes)
.

Let
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be finite. Then we have

\sum_{x\in F}q(x)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x\in\partial F}q(x)c(x).

Proof.

For
x\in F-\partial F
, we have that
c(x)=0
, and so

\sum_{x\in\partial F}q(x)c(x)=\sum_{x\in F}q(x)c(x).

So, it suffices to show that
\sum_{x\in F}(4q(x)-q(x)c(x))=0
.
From the definition of
c(x)
we have that

\sum_{x\in F}(4q(x)-q(x)c(x))=\sum_{x\in F}(2q(x)e(x)-q(x)s(x))
.
It suffices to show that each of the sums
\sum_{x\in F}q(x)e(x)

and
\sum_{x\in F}q(x)s(x)
is equal to
0
.

Let
E
be the set of pairs of points (“edges”)
(x,y)
with

x,y\in F
and
x,y
differing by
(\pm 1,0)
or
(0,\pm 1)
.
Then
\sum_{x\in F}q(x)e(x)=\sum_{(x,y)\in E}(q(x)+q(y))=0

as
q(x)+q(y)=0
for all
(x,y)\in E
.

Let
S
be the set of “squares” in
F
, that is,
S
is the set of all

4
-element subsets of
F
of the form
R=\{(i,j),(i+1,j),(i,j+1),(i+1,j+1)\}

for some
i,j
. Then we have

\sum_{x\in F}q(x)s(x)=\sum_{R\in S}\sum_{(a,b)\in R}(-1)^{a+b}=0

as for each square
R
in
F
we have that
\sum_{(a,b)\in R}(-1)^{a+b}=0

6.2.
Currents and extensions of partial matchings

We now discuss some terminolgy and simple results concerning matchings of finite regions in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

The regions we consider will be rectangular polygons that are in between two “parallel” paths. To begin, we consider the simplest kind of such region, which is a region strictly between two parallel (horizontal or vertical) lines in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. For definiteness, let us consider two parallel horizontal lines
y=\alpha
and
y=\beta
(where
\alpha<\beta
are in
\mathbb{Z}
). The region will be defined as

R=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,:\,a\leq x\leq b,\ \alpha<y<\beta\}

for some

in
\mathbb{Z}
. In general
b-a
is much larger than
\beta-\alpha
, and we thus refer to
\beta-\alpha-1
(the number of integers strickly between
\alpha
and
\beta
) as the

of

. For reasons that will become clear, we assume the thickness of
R
is even.

We will be considering matchings of the induced subgraph of the Cayley graph on
R
. To emphasize that these are not necessarily perfect mathchings, we refer to them as
partial matchings
. If
M
is a partial matching of
R
, then
M
is a subgraph of the Cayley graph on
R
such that each vertex has degree 0 or 1, i.e.,
\mbox{deg}_{M}(x)=0\mbox{ or }1
for
x\in R
. We call the set
\{x\in R\,:\,\mbox{deg}_{M}(x)=1\}
the
domain
of
M
, and denote it by
\mathrm{dom}(M)
.

A partial matching of
R
is said to be of the

canonical form
if it is as shown in Figure
11
, i.e., all edges of the matching are horizontal, and the domain of the matching is either the entire
R
(if
b-a+1
is even) or all of
R
except those points with
x
-coordinate
b
(as shown, if
b-a+1
is odd).

.

Consider a more general partial matching
M
of
R
. Let us fix an orientation for this region, say left to right. Consider

S=\{(x,y)\in R\,:\,a<x<b,\ \alpha<y<\beta\}.

Suppose the domain of
M
includes all points of
S
. For all
a<x<b
, we define the
current
of
M
at
x
,
which we denote
C_{M}(x)
or just
C(x)
if
M
is understood.

For
a<x<b
, let
S_{x}=\{(x,y)\,:\,\alpha<y<\beta\}
. For each

,
if
M
matches it with a point to the right of
z
, or with a point below or above
z
, then

contributes
0
to the current. If
M
matches
z
with a point to the
left of
z
, then
z
contributes
q(z)
to the current. Summing these values for the points in

S_{x}
gives the current
C_{M}(x)
. Note that since
R
has even thickness, the canonical matching has current
0
at all

The current is well-defined by the following fact.

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose
M
is a partial
matching of
R
whose domain includes all points of
S
. Then for any
a<x_{0}\leq x_{1}<b
we have that

C_{M}(x_{0})=C_{M}(x_{1})
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the lemma for
x_{0}=a+1
and
x_{1}=b-1
.
Since
R
has even thickness, we have
\sum_{z\in R}q(z)=0
. Also

\sum_{z\in\mathrm{dom}(M)}q(z)=0
, and so,
\sum_{z\in R-\mathrm{dom}(M)}q(z)=0
. Since
S\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(M)
, we get

\sum_{(a,y)\in R-\mathrm{dom}(M)}q(a,y)+\sum_{(b,y)\in R-\mathrm{dom}(M)}q(b,y)=0.

We claim that the first sum equals the current
C_{M}(x_{0})
. This is because, for each
(a,y)\in R-\mathrm{dom}(M)
,
M
matches
(a+1,y)=(x_{0},y)
with a point either to the right, or above and below, and thus
(x_{0},y)
contributes
0
to
C_{M}(x_{0})
; on the other hand, for each
(a,y)\in\mathrm{dom}(M)
,
M
matches
(x_{0},y)
to
(a,y)
, which is to the left of
(x_{0},y)
, and thus
(x_{0},y)
contributes
q(x_{0},y)=-q(a,y)
to
C_{M}(x_{0})
. Therefore

C_{M}(x_{0})=\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathrm{dom}(M)}-q(a,y).

Note again
R
has even thickness, and thus
\sum_{(a,y)\in R}q(a,y)=0
. It follows that

C_{M}(x_{0})=\sum_{(a,y)\in R-\mathrm{dom}(M)}q(a,y).

Now we claim that the second sum equals
-C_{M}(x_{1})
. To see this, again note
\sum_{(x_{1},y)\in R}q(x_{1},y)=0
and write the sum as

\sum_{(x_{1},y)\in A}q(x_{1},y)+\sum_{(x_{1},y)\in B}q(x_{1},y)+\sum_{(x_{1},y)\in C}q(x_{1},y)=0

where
A
is the set of points which are matched by
M
vertically to a point (again in
A
),

B
is the set of points which are matched by
M
to a point to the left, and
C
is the set of points which are matched by
M
to a point to the right. The first sum equals 0, and the second sum equals
C_{M}(x_{1})
. For each
(x_{1},y)\in C
,
(x_{1}+1,y)=(b,y)
is matched by
M
to
(x_{1},y)
, which is to the left of
(b,y)
, and
q(x_{1},y)=-q(b,y)
. Let
D
be the set of all
(b,y)\in R
which are matched by
M
to the point on the left. Thus it suffices to show that

\sum_{(b,y)\in D}q(b,y)+\sum_{(b,y)\in R-\mathrm{dom}(M)}q(b,y)=0.

Finally, let
E
be the set of all points
(b,y)\in R
which are matched by
M
vertically to a point (again in
E
). Since
\sum_{(b,y)\in R}q(b,y)=0
, we get that

\sum_{(b,y)\in R-\mathrm{dom}(M)}q(b,y)+\sum_{(b,y)\in D}q(b,y)+\sum_{(b,y)\in E}q(b,y)=0.

Note that the third term is
0
, thus we get the desired equality. In conclusion, we get

C_{M}(x_{0})=C_{M}(x_{1})
.
∎

In this section we show that there exists a Borel lining of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})
. By a
lining
we mean a subgraph of the Schreier graph of
F(2^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})
which is a Hamiltonian path on each equivalence class. More generally, consider a free action of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
on a Polish space
X
and the associated Schreier graph on
X

[
11
]
, a
line section
is a subgraph
S
of the Schreier graph on
X
where each vertex in
S
has degree
2
. If
S
is a line section, we call each connected component of
S
an
S
-line
. A line section
S
is
complete
if
S\cap[x]\neq\varnothing
for all
x\in X
. A line section
S
is
single
if for each
x\in X
,
S\upharpoonright[x]
is a nonempty single
S
-line. Thus a
lining

S
is a single line section where the vertex set of
S
is the entire space, and in particular it is a complete line section.

A line section
S
on
X
is
Borel
(
clopen
, resp.) if for each generator
e
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
