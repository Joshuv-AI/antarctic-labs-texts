Abstract
This paper introduces and develops Möbius homology , a homology theory for representations of finite posets into abelian categories. Although the connection between poset topology and Möbius functions is classical, we go further by establishing a direct connection between poset topology and Möbius inversions. In particular, we show that Möbius homology categorifies the Möbius inversion, as its Euler characteristic coincides with the Möbius inversion applied to the dimension function of the representation. We also present a homological version of Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem, relating the Möbius homologies of two posets connected by a Galois connection. Our main application concerns persistent homology over general posets. We prove that, under a suitable definition, the persistence diagram arises as an Euler characteristic over a poset of intervals, and thus Möbius homology provides a categorification of the persistence diagram. This furnishes a new invariant for persistent homology over arbitrary finite posets. Finally, leveraging our homological variant of Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem, we establish several results about the persistence diagram.



1
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an abelian group and
P
a finite poset.
For all functions
m:P\to\mathcal{G}
, there exists a unique function
\partial m:P\to\mathcal{G}
such that
for all
b\in P
,

m(b)=\sum_{a:a\leq b}\partial m(a).

The function
\partial m
is called the
Möbius inversion
of
m
.
Often the function
m
is the shadow of a richer algebraic structure.
That is, there is often an abelian category
\mathcal{C}

and a
P
-module, i.e., a functor,

M:P\to\mathcal{C}
whose
dimension function
is
m
.
Here, the dimension function is the assignment to every
b\in P

the representative
\big[M(b)\big]
of the object
M(b)
in the Grothendieck group
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{C}
.
In this case, it is natural to ask for an invariant of
M
that decategorifies to the Möbius inversion
\partial m
.

This paper introduces a homology theory for
P
-modules we call
Möbius homology
.
Every module
M
gives rise to a simplicial cosheaf over the order complex of
P
.
We define the Möbius homology of
M
at an element
b\in P
, denoted
H^{\downarrow}_{\ast}M(b)
, as the homology of the simplicial cosheaf localized to
b
.
Our first result (Theorem
3.13
) states that the Euler characteristic of the Möbius homology is the Möbius inversion.
That is, for all
b\in P
,

\partial m(b)=\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\big[H^{\downarrow}_{d}M(b)\big].

Our second result (Theorem
4.3
) is a homological version of Rota’s Galois connection theorem. Rota’s theorem is an important tool for
studying and computing Möbius inversions, and likewise we use our result to prove several properties of Möbius homology.

Previous Work

Poset topology is the study of the order complex associated with a poset.
This field originates from Rota’s seminal 1964 paper on Möbius functions
[
9
]

and has since evolved into a rich area of study motivated by questions from diverse disciplines
[
20
]
.
The Möbius function is of particular interest due to its connection with the Euler characteristic of the order complex
[
16
]
.
In this work, we explore the topology of
P
-modules and their relationship to Möbius inversion.

The study of
P
-modules as sheaves dates back to at least Deheuvels
[
7
]
.
Building on this perspective, Baclawski introduced Whitney homology for modules over geometric lattices,
providing a categorification of Whitney numbers
[
2
]
.
In a subsequent paper, Baclawski extended this approach by categorifying Rota’s Galois connection theorem
as a Leray spectral sequence
[
3
]
.

Recent work has advanced the categorification of Möbius inversion for posets arising from stratifications.
Petersen
[
14
]
constructed a spectral sequence for stratified spaces
that provides a categorical lift of Möbius inversion. This construction computes the compactly supported cohomology of an open stratum
in terms of the cohomology of closed strata.
More recently, Ayala, Mazel-Gee, and Rozenblyum categorified Möbius inversion in the context of poset modules valued in a stable
\infty
-category
[
1
]
.
Their approach generalizes Petersen’s results.
Both works appear to extend the earlier contributions of Yanagawa in the setting of cellular spaces
[
22
]
.
This broader line of work, including the results presented in this paper, suggests a deeper connection with Euler calculus
[
19
,
17
]
.

Unlike previous approaches, our method avoids spectral sequences and instead provides a homology theory that directly categorifies Möbius inversion.

Application: Persistent Homology

Our discovery of Möbius homology is motivated by questions in persistent homology.
Traditionally, persistent homology starts with a family of topological spaces parameterized by a finite, totally ordered poset
P
.
Apply homology using field coefficients and
the result is a
P
-module of vector spaces

M_{\ast}:P\to{\mathrm{Vec}}
, one for each dimension.
The
persistence diagram
of each
M_{\ast}

is a complete combinatorial invariant that encodes the birth and death of its generators along
P
.

There are many equivalent definitions of the persistence diagram.
The definition proposed by Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer uses an inclusion-exclusion formula
[
6
]
.
From this viewpoint, the persistence diagram of
M_{\ast}
is the Möbius inversion of a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-valued function on the poset of intervals of
P

[
13
]
.
There are now two well developed generalizations of this definition to modules over any finite poset.
The persistence diagram of Kim and Mémoli is a Möbius inversion of the rank invariant of the module
[
11
]
whereas the persistence diagram of Gülem, McCleary, and Patel is the Möbius inversion of the birth-death invariant of the module
[
12
,
10
]
.
When
P
is not totally ordered, neither are complete invariants of the module.
We show how Möbius homology categorifies the birth-death approach and is a stronger invariant than simply the Möbius inversion.
Finally, we demonstrate how the homological version of Rota’s Galois connection theorem helps in computing persistent Möbius homology.

Background

This section provides a brief introduction to Möbius inversions, simplicial cosheaves, the Grothendieck group, and Galois connections.

2.1
Möbius Inversion

We begin with the Möbius inversion formula.
For a more thorough introduction to Möbius inversion, see
[
18
]
.

Let
P
be a finite poset. For
a\leq c
, the (closed)
interval

[a,c]
is the set

[a,c]:=\{b\in P:a\leq b\leq c\}.

Let
\mathcal{I}(P)
denote the set of all intervals in
P
. The
{\mathbb{Z}}
-incidence algebra
on
P
, denoted
{\mathrm{Inc}}(P)
, is the set of all functions
\alpha:\mathcal{I}(P)\to{\mathbb{Z}}
with three operations: scaling, addition, and multiplication.
The first two are clear.
Multiplication of two functions
\alpha
and
\beta
is

(\alpha\ast\beta)[a,c]:=\sum_{b:a\leq b\leq c}\alpha[a,b]\cdot\beta[b,c].

The multiplicative identity is

{\mathbbm{1}}[a,b]=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $a=b$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The
zeta function
is

\zeta[a,b]=1
, for all
a\leq b
.
The zeta function is invertible, and its inverse, denoted
\mu
, is the
Möbius function
.

Definition 2.1
:

Let
P
be a finite poset and
\mathcal{G}
an abelian group.
The
Möbius inversion
of any function
f:P\to\mathcal{G}
is the function

\partial f:P\to\mathcal{G}

defined as

\partial f(c)=\sum_{b:b\leq c}f(b)\cdot\mu[b,c].

(1)

The Möbius inversion is the unique function satisfying the following equation for all
c\in P
:

\displaystyle\sum_{b:b\leq c}\partial f(b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b:b\leq c}\partial f(b)\cdot\zeta[b,c]

\displaystyle=\sum_{b:b\leq c}\left(\sum_{a:a\leq b}f(a)\cdot\mu[a,b]\right)\cdot\zeta[b,c]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a:a\leq c}f(a)\left(\sum_{b:a\leq b\leq c}\mu[a,b]\cdot\zeta[b,c]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a:a\leq c}f(a)\cdot{\mathbbm{1}}[a,c]

\displaystyle=f(c).

That is,
\partial f
is the combinatorial derivative of
f
.

2.2
Simplicial Cosheaves

We introduce simplicial cosheaves as functors from a simplicial complex to an abelian category. The homology of these cosheaves and their Euler characteristic, which lives in the Grothendieck group of the abelian category, will be essential to our main theorems. For more background on cellular (co)sheaves, see
[
8
]
, and for the Grothendieck group, see
[

]

Let

We write

We also think of

\tau\to\sigma
whenever
\tau\geq\sigma

For the moment, let
\mathcal{C}

A

The

For

\partial_{d}:=\sum_{\tau:\dim\tau=d}\partial_{d}|_{\tau}.

of a simplicial cosheaf

\mathcal{C}

Assume
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to a small category.
Denote by
[A]

Definition 2.5
:

The

of

Example 2.6
:

If
{\mathrm{Vec}}
is the category of

-vector spaces,

.

.

Example 2.7
:

Let
\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{C})
be the category of

That is, an object

\phi:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}

Example 2.8

2.3
Galois Connections

We now review some fundamental properties of Galois connections. A Galois connection between posets
P
and
Q
can be understood as a pair of adjoint functors.
It is well known that such adjoint functors induce a homotopy equivalence between the order complexes
\Delta P
and
\Delta Q
; see Quillen
[
15
, §1.3]
.
In this section, we construct an explicit chain homotopy equivalence between
\Delta P
and
\Delta Q
, which will be a key component in the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Definition 2.13
:

A
(monotone) Galois connection
from a poset
P
to a poset
Q
, written

f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
, are monotone functions
f:P\to Q
and
g:Q\to P

such that for all
a\in P
and for all
x\in Q
,
f(a)\leq x
if and only if
a\leq g(x).

The function
f
is called the
left adjoint
of
g
and
g
is called the
right adjoint
of
f

Example 2.14
:

Consider the following Galois connections

f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
where
f

is given by the red dashed arrows and
g
by the blue dashed arrows:

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}P}
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Q}
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Q}
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}P}

Lemma 2.15
:

Every Galois connection
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

For all
a\in P
,
a\leq g\circ f(a)
.

(ii)

For all
x\in Q
,
f\circ g(x)\leq x
.

(iii)

If
f(a)=x
, then
f\circ g(x)=x
.

(iv)

f
is surjective if and only if
g
is injective.

(v)

f
is injective if and only if
g
is surjective.

Proof.

(i)

In the axiom of a Galois connection above, let
x=f(a)
.
Then the inequality
f(a)\leq f(a)
implies
a\leq g\circ f(a)
.

(ii)

In the axiom of a Galois
connection above, let
a=g(x)
.
Then the inequality
g(x)\leq g(x)
implies
f\circ g(x)\leq x
.

(iii)

The inequality
f(a)\leq x
implies
a\leq g(x)
. Since
f
is monotone,
x=f(a)\leq f\circ g(x)
.
Combined with (ii),
f\circ g(x)=x
.

(iv)

Assume
f
is surjective.
Fix distinct elements
x,y\in Q
, and suppose, for the sake of contradiction,

g(x)=g(y)
.
Since
f
is surjective,
f(a)=x
and
f(b)=y
for some
a,b\in P
.
By (iii),
f\circ g(x)=x
and
f\circ g(y)=y
.
Thus
x=y
contradicting
x\neq y
.

Assume
f
is not surjective.
Then there is an
x\in Q
such that
f^{-1}(x)=\emptyset
.
Let
a=g(x)
and let
y=f(a)
.
By (iii),
x\neq y
.
The inequality
f(a)\leq y
implies
a\leq g(y)
.
By (ii),
f\circ g(x)=y\leq x
.
Apply
g
to get
g(y)\leq g(x)=a
.
Thus
g
is not injective because
g(x)=g(y)=a
.

(v)

Assume
f
is injective.
If
a\in P
and
x=f(a)
, then, by (iii),

f\circ g(x)=x
.
Thus
g(x)=a
making
g
surjective.

Assume
f
is not injective.
Then there are are distinct elements

a,b\in P
such that
f(a)=f(b)=x
.
By (iii),
g(x)
is an element of

, which contains both

and

.
Thus

cannot be surjective.

∎

Möbius Inversions and Euler Characteristics

We define two invariants associated to a
P
-module valued in an abelian category.
The first is the Möbius inversion of the module’s
dimension function
.
The second is
Möbius homology
.
Our main theorem (Theorem
3.13
) shows that the Möbius inversion is the Euler characteristic of the Möbius homology.

Definition 3.1
:

Let
P
be a finite poset and
\mathcal{C}
any small abelian category.
A
P
-module
is a functor
M:P\to\mathcal{C}
.

3.1
Dimension Function

The first invariant considers only the objects of
M
while forgetting all its morphisms.

Definition 3.2
:

The
dimension function
of
M
is the function
m:P\to K(\mathcal{C})

that assigns to every
a\in P
the element
\big[M(a)\big]\in K(\mathcal{C})
.

We now look at two similar examples of the dimension function

Order cosheaf

For our second invariant, we start by building a simplicial complex.
A chain of
length

n
in
P
is a sequence

n+1

Definition 3.5
:

The

of

denoted

A simplex

b_{0}<\cdots<b_{i}

if the later is a subchain of the former.

Let

be the functions that assign to each simplex its minimal and maximal elements, respectively, in its corresponding chain. Note that if
\tau\geq\sigma
in
\Delta P
, then
\min(\tau)\leq\min(\sigma)
and
\max(\sigma)\leq\max(\tau)
in
P
.

:

of

:

3.3
Main Theorem

The following theorem relates Möbius inversions to Möbius homology.

Theorem 3.13
:

Let
m:P\to K(\mathcal{C})
be the dimension
function of a
P
-module
M:P\to\mathcal{C}
.
Then for all
b\in P
,

\partial m(b)=\chi\big(\Delta P_{\leq b},\Delta P_{<b};\underline{{M}}\big)=\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\big[H^{\downarrow}_{\ast}M(b)\big].

The proof is based on the following observation.

Lemma 3.14

(Philip Hall’s Theorem, Prop 3.8.5
[
18
]
)
:

For
a\leq b
in
P
, let
n_{d}(a,b)
be the number of chains of length
d
starting at
a
and ending at
b
.
Then,

\mu[a,b]=\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\cdot n_{d}(a,b).

Note that
n_{d}(a,b)
is the number of
d
-simplices in
\Delta P
with minimal element
a
and maximal element
b
.
Further, the sum of
n_{d}(a,b)
, over all
a\leq b
, is the total number of
d
-simplices in
\Delta P
.

Proof of Theorem
3.13
.

We have

\displaystyle\partial m(b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a:a\leq b}m(a)\cdot\mu[a,b]

by Equation (
1
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a:a\leq b}\big[M(a)\big]\cdot\mu[a,b]

by Definition
3.2

\displaystyle=\sum_{a:a\leq b}\big[M(a)\big]\cdot\left(\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\cdot n_{d}(a,b)\right)

by Lemma
3.14

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Delta P\\
\max(\sigma)=b\\
\dim(\sigma)=d\end{subarray}}(-1)^{d}\big[M\left(\min(\sigma)\right)\big]

\displaystyle=\chi\big(\Delta P_{\leq b},\Delta P_{<b};\underline{{M}}\big)

by Definition
2.11

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\big[H^{\downarrow}_{\ast}M(b)\big]

by Proposition
2.12

∎

In the Möbius chain complex
C^{\downarrow}_{\bullet}M(b)
, only simplices in
\Delta P_{\leq b}\setminus\Delta P_{<b}
are relevant. For distinct elements
a
and
b
, we have:

(\Delta P_{\leq a}\setminus\Delta P_{<a})\cap(\Delta P_{\leq b}\setminus\Delta P_{<b})=\emptyset

(3)

Thus, each simplex
\sigma\in\Delta P
appears in the Möbius chain complex for exactly one element of
P
: its maximum element
\max(\sigma)
. This observation leads to the following corollary of our main theorem.

Corollary 3.15
:

Let
m:P\to K(\mathcal{C})
be the dimension function of a
P
-module
M:P\to\mathcal{C}
.
Then,

\sum_{a\in P}\partial m(a)=\chi\big(\Delta P;\underline{{M}}\big)=\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\big[H_{d}(\Delta P;\underline{{M}})\big].

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in P}\partial m(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in P}\chi(\Delta P_{\leq a},\Delta P_{<a};\underline{{M}})

by Theorem
3.13

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in P}\sum_{d\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Delta P\\
\max(\sigma)=a\\
\dim(\sigma)=d\end{subarray}}(-1)^{d}\big[M\left(\min(\sigma)\right)\big]

by Definition
2.11

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Delta P\\
\dim(\sigma)=d\end{subarray}}(-1)^{d}\big[M\left(\min(\sigma)\right)\big]

by Equation (
3
)

\displaystyle=\chi(\Delta P;\underline{{M}})

by Definition
2.9

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\big[H_{d}(\Delta P;\underline{{M}})\big]

by Proposition
2.10

∎

We have seen how Möbius homology is a richer invariant than the Möbius inversion.
However, Möbius homology is not a complete invariant of a

-module.

4
Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem

This section establishes a homological version of Rota’s Galois connection theorem (Theorem
4.3
)
and explores two immediate consequences.
We start with Rota’s original statement
relating the two Möbius functions involved in a Galois connection.

Theorem 4.1

(Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem
[
9
]
)
:

Let
P
and
Q
be finite posets and
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
a Galois connection.
Then for any
a\in P
and
x\in Q
,

\sum_{b:f(b)=x}\mu_{P}(a,b)=\sum_{y:g(y)=a}\mu_{Q}(x,y).

The following statement by Gülem and McCleary
relates the two Möbius inversions involved in a Galois connection and is equivalent to
Theorem
4.1

when the functions involved are valued in any abelian group.

Theorem 4.2

(Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem
[
10
]
)
:

Let
m:P\to{\mathbb{Z}}
and
n:Q\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be two functions and
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g

a Galois connection.
If
n=g\circ m
, then for all
y\in Q
,

\partial n(y)=\sum_{b\in f^{-1}(y)}\partial m(b).

Given a Galois connection
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
and an element
y\in Q
, consider the following two subcomplexes of
\Delta P
:

\displaystyle\Delta f^{-1}_{\leq y}:=\big\{\sigma\in\Delta P:f(\max(\sigma))\leq y\big\}

\displaystyle\Delta f^{-1}_{<y}:=\big\{\sigma\in\Delta P:f(\max(\sigma))<y\big\}.

We now state our theorem and argue how it categorifies Rota’s Galois connection theorem.
The proof of the theorem is presented in the following subsection.

Theorem 4.3

(Homological Rota’s Galois Connection Theorem)
:

For a module
M:P\to\mathcal{C}
and a Galois connection
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
, let
the module
N:=M\circ g:Q\to\mathcal{C}
.
For every
y\in Q
, there is a canonical isomorphism

H^{\downarrow}_{\ast}N(y)\cong H_{\ast}\big(\Delta f^{-1}_{\leq y},\Delta f^{-1}_{<y};\underline{{M}}\big).

(4)

Theorem
4.3
categorifies Theorem
4.2
as follows.
Let
m,n:\to K(\mathcal{C})
be the dimension functions of the modules
M
and
N
, respectively.
By Theorem
3.13
,

\partial n(y)=\sum_{d\geq 0}\big[H^{\downarrow}_{d}N(y)\big].

Note that the preimage on the
right side of Equation (
4
)
is the following disjoint union:

\Delta f^{-1}_{\leq y}\setminus\Delta f^{-1}_{<y}=\bigsqcup_{b\in f^{-1}(y)}\Delta P_{\leq b}\setminus\Delta P_{<b}.

This combined with Definition
2.11
and Lemma
2.12
implies

\sum_{b\in f^{-1}(y)}\partial m(b)=\sum_{d\geq 0}(-1)^{d}\Big[H_{\ast}\big(\Delta f^{-1}_{\leq y},\Delta f^{-1}_{<y};\underline{{M}}\big)\Big].

Remark 4.4
:

The right side of Equation (
4
) can be seen as the limiting term of a Leray-type spectral sequence.
This spectral sequence accounts for the differentials between the Möbius homology objects
\big\{H^{\downarrow}_{d}M(b)\big\}
over all
b\in f^{-1}(y)
and dimensions
d
.

Refinements

An immediate consequence of Theorem
4.3

is that Möbius homology is invariant to refinements of the underlying poset as follows.
A
refinement
of a poset
P
is a Galois connection
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g

such that
f
is injective or, equivalently,

g
is surjective.
In this case, we think of
P
as a subposet of
Q
.

Corollary 4.5
:

Given a refinement
f:P\leftrightarrows Q:g
and a
P
-module
M
, let
the
Q
-module
N:=M\circ g
.
For every
b\in P
,

H^{\downarrow}_{\ast}M(b)\cong H^{\downarrow}_{\ast}N(b)
.
For every
y\in Q\setminus P
,
H^{\downarrow}_{\ast}N(y)=0
because
\Delta f^{-1}_{\leq y}\setminus\Delta f^{-1}_{<y}=\emptyset
.

Bounding Homological Dimension

When the underlying poset of a
P
-module
M
is a finite distributive lattice,
we show in Corollary
4.6
that there exists a bound on the highest non-trivial dimension of its Möbius homology.

Recall that a
lattice
is a poset in which every pair of elements has a meet (greatest lower bound), denoted
\wedge
,
and a join (least upper bound), denoted
\vee
.
A lattice is
distributive
if, for all
a,b,c\in P
,

a\vee(b\wedge c)=(a\vee b)\wedge(a\vee c).

If
a,c\in P
with
a<c
, we say that
c
covers
a
, denoted
a\lessdot c
,
if there is no
b
such that
a<b<c
.
An element
a\in P
is
meet-irreducible
if it is not the maximum element and
there are no distinct elements
b,c\neq a
such that
a=b\wedge c
.

Now, fix an element
b\in P
, and let
C_{b}
be the set of all elements of
P
covered by
b
:

C_{b}:=\{b^{\prime}\in P:b^{\prime}\lessdot b\}.

The set
C_{b}
generates a sublattice of
P
, called the

meet-generated sublattice
:

\mathcal{M}(C_{b}):=\{b\}\cup\{a\in P:a=b_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge b_{k}\;\text{for some}\;b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\in C_{b}\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{M}(C_{b})
has a minimum element, denoted
0_{b}
, which is the meet of all elements in
C_{b}
, and its maximum element is
b
.
Additionally, the set of meet-irreducible elements of
\mathcal{M}(C_{b})
is precisely
C_{b}
.

For every
b\in P
, there is a canonical Galois connection

f:P_{\leq b}\leftrightarrows\mathcal{M}(C_{b}):g

defined as follows: for
a\in P_{\leq b}
, let
A=\{b\}\cup\{b^{\prime}\in C_{b}:a\leq b^{\prime}\}
.
Define
f(a)=\wedge A
, and let
g
be the inclusion map.
It is straightforward to verify that we indeed have a Galois connection.
See Figure
6
for an illustration of this Galois connection.

Before stating and proving the corollary, we need one additional observation.
In a distributive lattice, every maximal chain has the same length,
and that length is given by the number of meet-irreducible elements
[
4
, Lemma 2, p. 59]
.
Consequently, the length of every maximal chain between
0_{b}
and
b
in
\mathcal{M}(C_{b})
equals the cardinality of

5
Persistent Homology

We are now ready to study the concept of
persistence
for a
P
-module using Möbius homology.
The goal of persistence is to capture information that persists across subsets of
P
.
The simplest subsets to consider are (half-open) intervals of
P
.
Our primary construction is the
birth-death module
associated with any given
P
-module.
When
P
is totally ordered, the Möbius homology of this module categorifies the traditional persistence diagram.
To match classical persistence, we assume that the poset
P
has a maximum element, denoted
\infty
.
This ensures we can discuss features that persist indefinitely—a central theme in persistent homology.

For
a\in P
and an object
X\in\mathrm{ob}\,\mathcal{C}
, let

X^{\uparrow a}:P\to\mathcal{C}
be the following module.
For every
b\in P
,

X^{\uparrow a}(b):=\begin{cases}X&\text{ if $a\leq b$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

and for every
b\leq c
the morphism
X^{\uparrow a}(b\leq c)
is the identity when
a\leq b
and zero otherwise.
A
P
-module is
free
if it is the direct sum of modules of the form
X^{\uparrow a}
.

Definition 5.1
:

A
free presentation
of a
P
-module
M:P\to\mathcal{C}
is a natural transformation
\phi:F\Rightarrow M
from a free
P
-module
F
such that for all
a\in P
the morphism
\phi(a):F(a)\to M(a)
is an epimorphism.

The construction of a free presentation below appeared for
{\mathrm{Vec}}
in
[
10
]
. Here we present the same construction in an abelian category for completeness.

Proposition 5.2
:

Every
P
-module
M
has a free presentation.

Proof.

Let
F
be the free
P
-module
\bigoplus_{a\in P}M(a)^{\uparrow a}
.
Define the natural transformation
\phi:F\Rightarrow M

summand-wise as follows.
For
a\in P
, let
\phi_{a}:M(a)^{\uparrow a}\Rightarrow M
be the following natural transformation:

\phi_{a}(b):=\begin{cases}M(a\leq b)&\text{if $a\leq b$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
\phi_{a}(a)
is an isomorphism.
Then
\phi
is the direct
sum
\oplus_{a\in P}\phi_{a}
.
∎

Every module
M
has a free presentation, but it is not unique.
For now, we develop persistence for a fixed free presentation and show it is
well defined in the next subsection.

5.2
Galois Connections and Persistence

The homological version of Rota’s Galois connection theorem applies to persistent Möbius homology.
This follows immediately from the observation that a Galois connection between posets induces a Galois connection between their intervals posets.

A monotone function
f:P\to Q
induces a monotone function
{\mathrm{Int}}\,f:{\mathrm{Int}}\,P\to{\mathrm{Int}}\,Q
defined as
{\mathrm{Int}}\,f[a,b)=\big[f(a),f(b)\big]
.
Similarly, a monotone function
g:Q\to P
induces a monotone function
{\mathrm{Int}}\,g:{\mathrm{Int}}\,Q\to{\mathrm{Int}}\,P
.

Lemma 5.15
:

If

is a Galois connection, then
{\mathrm{Int}}\,f:{\mathrm{Int}}\,P\leftrightarrows{\mathrm{Int}}\,Q:{\mathrm{Int}}\,g

is a Galois connection.

Proof.

The following statement is to be verified: for all
[a,b)\in{\mathrm{Int}}\,P
and
[x,y)\in{\mathrm{Int}}\,Q
,
{\mathrm{Int}}\,f[a,b)\leq[x,y)
iff
[a,b)\leq{\mathrm{Int}}\,g[x,y)
.
Unwind the definition of
\preceq
to get

f(a)\leq x\text{ and }f(b)\leq y\Leftrightarrow a\leq g(x)\text{ and }b\leq g(y).

In other words,
f
and
g
must form a Galois connection which is true by assumption.
∎

Lemma A.2
:

The composition
\Delta f\circ\Delta g:C_{\bullet}(\Delta Q;{\mathbb{Z}})\to C_{\bullet}(\Delta Q;{\mathbb{Z}})
is chain homotopic to the identity.

Proof.

For any
n
-simplex
x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n}
in
\Delta Q
and
y_{i}=f\circ g(x_{i})
as above,
we show

(x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n})-(y_{0}\leq\cdots\leq y_{n})=\partial\circ\psi(x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n})+\psi\circ\partial(x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n}).

Expand the first term to

\displaystyle\partial\circ\psi(x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n})=

\displaystyle\;\partial\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}y_{0}\leq\cdots\leq y_{i}\leq x_{i}<\cdots<x_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\Bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{j}y_{0}\leq\cdots\leq\widehat{y}_{j}\leq\cdots\leq y_{i}\leq x_{i}<\cdots<x_{n}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=i}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}y_{0}\leq\cdots\leq y_{i}\leq x_{i}<\cdots<\widehat{x}_{j}<\cdots<x_{n}\Bigg).

Expand the second term to

\displaystyle\psi\circ\partial(x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n})=

\displaystyle\;\psi\Bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}x_{0}<\cdots<\widehat{x}_{j}<\cdots<x_{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\Bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{i}y_{0}\leq\cdots\leq y_{i}\leq x_{i}<\cdots<\widehat{x}_{j}<\cdots<x_{n}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}(-1)^{i-1}y_{0}\leq\cdots\leq\widehat{y}_{j}\leq\cdots\leq y_{i}\leq x_{i}<\cdots<x_{n}\Bigg).

Note that every term in the second expansion has a corresponding term in the first expansion but with an opposite sign.
This leaves just
(x_{0}<\cdots<x_{n})-(y_{0}\leq\cdots\leq y_{n})
as desired.
∎
