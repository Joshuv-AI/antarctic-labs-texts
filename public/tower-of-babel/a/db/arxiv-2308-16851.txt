Stress-linked pairs of vertices and the generic stress matroid
Dániel Garamvölgyi

Abstract
Given a graph G G and a mapping p : V ⁡ ( G ) → ℝ d p:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d} , we say that the pair ( G , p ) (G,p) is a ( d d -dimensional) realization of G G . Two realizations ( G , p ) (G,p) and ( G , q ) (G,q) are equivalent if each of the point pairs corresponding to the edges of G G have the same distance under the embeddings p p and q q . A pair of vertices { u , v } \{u,v\} is globally linked in G G in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} if for every generic realization ( G , p ) (G,p) and every equivalent realization ( G , q ) (G,q) , ( G + u ​ v , p ) (G+uv,p) and ( G + u ​ v , q ) (G+uv,q) are also equivalent. In this paper we introduce the notion of d {d} -stress-linked vertex pairs. Roughly speaking, a pair of vertices { u , v } \{u,v\} is d {d} -stress-linked in G G if the edge u ​ v uv is generically stressed in G + u ​ v G+uv and for every generic d d -dimensional realization ( G , p ) (G,p) , every configuration q q that satisfies all of the equilibrium stresses of ( G , p ) (G,p) also satisfies the equilibrium stresses of ( G + u ​ v , p ) (G+uv,p) . Among other results, we show that d {d} -stress-linked vertex pairs are globally linked in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , and we give a combinatorial characterization of 2 {2} -stress-linked vertex pairs that matches the conjecture of Jackson et al. about the characterization of globally linked pairs in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . As a key tool, we introduce and study the “algebraic dual” of the d d -dimensional generic rigidity matroid of a graph, which we call the d d -dimensional generic stress matroid of the graph. We believe that our results about this matroid, which describes the global behaviour of equilibrium stresses of generic realizations of G G , may be of independent interest. We use our results to give positive answers to conjectures of Jordán, Connelly, and Grasegger et al.

Introduction

One of the central unsolved problems in combinatorial rigidity theory is finding a combinatorial characterization of globally
d
-rigid graphs for
d\geq 3
. Roughly speaking, a graph
G
is
globally
d
-rigid
if for every generic embedding
p:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
of its vertices into Euclidean space, the (labeled) collection of edge lengths
||p(u)-p(v)||,uv\in E(G)
uniquely determine all of the pairwise distances
||p(u)-p(v)||,u,v\in V(G)
. (We shall give precise definitions and references in the next section.)
It is folklore that a graph is globally
1
-rigid if and only if it is
2
-connected, and Jackson and Jordán
[
19
]
gave a combinatorial characterization of globally
2
-rigid graphs, but the
d\geq 3
case is still wide open.

A major breakthrough in the study of global rigidity is due to Gortler, Healy and Thurston
[
12
]
who, by proving a conjecture of Connelly
[
4
]
, characterized globally rigid graphs in terms of equilibrium stresses. A
d
-dimensional
realization
of a graph
G
is a pair
(G,p)
with
p:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A vector
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}
is an
equilibrium stress
, or simply a
stress
, of
(G,p)
if it satisfies a certain system of equilibrium conditions determined by
(G,p)
. The main result of
[
12
]
states, roughly, that a graph on at least
d+2
vertices is globally
d
-rigid if and only if for any generic
d
-dimensional realization
(G,p)
and any generic equilibrium stress
\omega
of
(G,p)
, the realizations
(G,q)
that have
\omega
as a stress are precisely the affine images of
(G,p)
.
Although this is not a combinatorial characterization, it has nonetheless proved to be a fundamental tool in the combinatorial approach. Apart from the characterization itself, a major contribution of
[
12
]
is the introduction of tools from algebraic geometry to rigidity theory. In particular, the authors gave a geometric interpretation of stresses as the tangent hyperplanes of the
measurement variety
of
G
, the affine variety of every edge measurement that can be obtained from
d
-dimensional realizations of a graph
G
. This geometric picture is at the heart of our investigations in this paper.

While there has been a number of important advances in the combinatorial understanding of global rigidity in the more than one decade since the publication of
[
12
]
,
a general combinatorial characterization still seems elusive.
One possible avenue for progress
is the investigation of globally linked pairs. A pair of vertices
u,v\in V(G)
is
globally linked
in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if for every generic
d
-dimensional realization
(G,p)
, the distances
||p(u^{\prime})-p(v^{\prime})||,u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\in E(G)
uniquely determine the distance
||p(u)-p(v)||
. It is apparent by comparing the definitions that a graph is globally
d
-rigid if and only if every pair of vertices is globally linked in it. This shows that characterizing globally linked pairs is at least as difficult as characterizing global rigidity, and indeed it seems to be more difficult: a characterization (combinatorial or otherwise) of globally linked pairs is not even available in the
d=2
case. Nonetheless, there are a number of conjectures in the literature about globally linked pairs, many of which we will recall throughout the paper, and we believe that even partial progress on these problems may help in understanding the combinatorial nature of global rigidity.

A natural objective, following
[
12
]
, is to find a characterization of globally linked pairs based on equilibrium stresses. An apparent difficulty for this is the fact that, in contrast with global rigidity, being globally linked is not a “generic property”: it can happen that some generic realizations
(G,p)
satisfy the condition defining globally linked pairs, while other generic realizations do not. This problem can be rectified by extending our setting to include
complex realizations
, that is, pairs
(G,p)
where
p:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
maps the vertices of
G
into complex space. In this way one can define what it means for a pair of vertices to be globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and this does turn out to be a generic property. However, this approach presents another difficulty: it seems that the proof method of
[
12
]
, which is based on degree theory, cannot be adapted to the complex setting.

Nonetheless, we
initiate this line of study
by introducing a stress-based analogue of globally linked vertex pairs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(and in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
). Roughly speaking, we say that a pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked
if the edge
uv
is generically stressed in
G+uv
in
d
dimensions and for every generic
d
-dimensional realizaton
(G,p)
and every realization
(G,q)
that satisfies the equilibrium stresses of
(G,p)
,
(G+uv,q)
satisfies the equilibrium stresses of
(G+uv,p)
. We show that
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs are globally linked in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
(and hence in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).
We conjecture that the reverse implication also holds (
6.1
).
As partial evidence for this, we prove the
{d}
-stress-linked analogue of a number of conjectures about globally linked pairs. For example, we show that the combinatorial characterization conjectured by Jackson, Jordán and Szabadka
[
21
]
for globally linked pairs in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(and later by Jackson and Owen
[
23
]
for globally linked pairs in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
) characterizes
{2}
-stress-linked vertex pairs (
Theorem

4.20
). We also settle a significant special case of a “gluing conjecture” made by Jordán and the author in
[
10
]
about globally linked pairs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by proving that the analogous statement holds for
{d}
-stress-linked pairs (
Theorem

4.15
). Our most significant result that is not based on a conjecture about globally linked pairs is given by
Corollary

4.9
: it states that if a pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
, then it remains so after the deletion of any
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge in
G
, and similarly, if an edge
e
of
G
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge, then it remains an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge after adding an edge between any
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pair.

The study of
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs requires a deeper understanding of the “global” behaviour of stresses of generic frameworks. For example, is it possible that two such stresses (possibly belonging to different realizations) only differ in one coordinate? Or, when can we find such a stress whose values are prescribed on some set of edges? In order to handle such questions we introduce the
dual variety
of the measurement variety, which we call the
(
d
-dimensional generic) stress variety
of the graph. While the generic stress variety already appeared implicitly in
[
12
]
, we believe that its full significance in rigidity theory is yet to be understood. In fact, in
[
12
]
, the dual relationship between the measurement variety and the generic stress variety was only used through its corollary that the generic contact locus of the measurement variety is a linear space.

We explore and use this duality to a deeper extent using the perspective of matroid theory. It is well-known that to each affine variety
X
we can associate its
algebraic matroid

\mathcal{M}(X)
, a matroid defined via properties of the coordinate projections of
X
. We call the matroid associated to the stress variety in this way the
(
d
-dimensional generic) stress matroid
of the graph.
We rely on matroid theory more as a language rather than a tool, and our results on the generic stress matroid could be rephrased as purely geometric statements about the stress variety. However, the perspective of algebraic matroids highlights a connection that turns out to be of central importance in this paper: the fact that the algebraic matroid of a complex affine variety is the same as the linear matroid defined by its tangent space at a generic point. In the case of the rigidity matroid this is a well-known observation, roughly stating that a generic realization
(G,p)
is infinitesimally rigid if and only if there is, up to congruence, a finite number of realizations
(G,q)
equivalent to
(G,p)
. However, in the case of the stress variety this connection leads to a novel insight: it gives a geometric interpretation of the dual matroid of the stress matroid in terms of the generic contact locus of the measurement variety (
Theorem

3.2
). This correspondence illuminates both the combinatorial structure of the stress matroid and the geometric structure of the generic contact locus.

We note that in the recent preprint
[
5
]
, the authors introduce and study the
variety of general position stress matrices
of a graph. This variety coincides with the generic stress variety when
G
is globally
d
-rigid, but in general the two varieties can be different. For example, if
G
is
(d+1)
-connected but generically stress-free in
d
dimensions, then the variety of general position stress matrices of
G
has positive dimension, while the generic stress variety of
G
is zero-dimensional.

In
Section

5
, we give three applications of our results on
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs. The first is related to
minimally globally
d
-rigid
graphs, that is, graphs
G
that are globally
d
-rigid but for which
G-e
is not globally
d
-rigid for every
e\in E(G)
. In
[
25
]
, Jordán gave a conjectured upper bound on the number of edges in a minimally globally
d
-rigid graph that is linear in the number of its vertices. He also posed the stronger conjecture that if
G
is globally
d
-rigid but
G-e
is not, then
e
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d+1}
-bridge in
G
. The latter conjecture implies that minimally globally
d
-rigid graphs are not only sparse, but “everywhere sparse”, that is, they satisfy a certain
(d+1)
-sparsity count. While in
[
9
]
Jordán and the author proved the upper bound on the number of edges, the other conjecture remains open. However, using the notion of
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs we give a quick proof that minimally globally
d
-rigid graphs are indeed everywhere sparse (
Corollary

5.3
). More precisely, we show that if
G
is minimally globally
d
-rigid, then every subset
X\subseteq V(G)
of size at least
d+2
induces at most
(d+1)|X|-\binom{d+2}{2}
edges. We also show that this upper bound is strict unless
X
is a clique of size
d+2
.

We also settle two conjectures about graphs with small separators. In
[
3
]
, Connelly showed that if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are globally
d
-rigid graphs with
d+1
vertices in common, then
G_{1}\cup G_{2}-uv
is globally
d
-rigid for any edge
uv\in E(G_{1})\cap E(G_{2})
. He asked whether the analogous result holds for redundantly
d
-rigid graphs. In
[
15
]
, Grasegger et al. considered a similar question for so-called
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuits. They conjectured that if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuits such that
G_{1}\cap G_{2}
is a complete graph on
t
vertices with
2\leq t\leq d+1
, then
G_{1}\cup G_{2}-uv
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit for any edge
uv\in E(G_{1})\cap E(G_{2})
. We give affirmative answers to both of these problems (
Theorems

5.4
and
5.5
). In fact, the two proofs are almost identical. In both cases our strategy is to use the “gluing” result given by
Theorem

4.15
to show that
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G_{1}\cup G_{2}-uv
, and then use
Corollary

4.9
to deduce that since
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
has no
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridges, neither does
G_{1}\cup G_{2}-uv
.

Preliminaries

In this section we give a fairly comprehensive account of the tools and results from algebraic geometry that we will use.
Section

2.1
deals with basic definitions regarding affine varieties.
Everything in this subsection is standard and can be found in most textbooks on basic algebraic geometry, such as
[
17
]
or
[
33
]
(often in much greater generality).
Section

2.2
describes the notion of a “generic point” that is used in this paper. Its content is somewhat specialized, but is not new in the rigidity theory literature, see
[
8
,
13
]
for example.
Section

2.3
introduces algebraic matroids, which in our context will be matroids defined by coordinate projections of varieties. See
[
29
]
for a good general reference on this topic. Finally, in
Section

2.4
we give the basic definitions of rigidity theory from an algebraic geometric point of view. This approach is not the standard one, but it has been explored and used in many recent papers, including the three cited above. For a general reference on rigidity theory see, for example,
[

]
and
[
31
]
.

To avoid some trivialities, we always implicitly assume that the graphs considered in this paper have a nonempty set of edges. Given a graph
G
, we use
V(G)
and
E(G)
to denote the vertex set and the edge set of
G
, respectively.
We assume familiarity with the basic notions of matroid theory; the standard reference is
[
28
]
. Given a matroid
\mathcal{M}
, we use
\mathcal{M}^{*}
to denote the dual matroid of
\mathcal{M}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
has ground set
E
and rank function
r
. A bipartition
(E_{1},E_{2})
of
E
is a
separation
of
\mathcal{M}
if
r(E)=r(E_{1})+r(E_{2})
. A matroid is
connected
if it has no nontrivial separations, or equivalently, if for every pair of elements of
E
there is a circuit of
\mathcal{M}
containing them. Every matroid can be uniquely written as the direct sum of connected matroids, which are called the
connected components
of the matroid. For two matroids
\mathcal{N},\mathcal{M}
we write
\mathcal{N}\preceq\mathcal{M}
to mean that every independent set in
\mathcal{N}
is also independent in
\mathcal{M}
.

2.1
Affine varieties

A set
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
is an
affine variety
if it is the set of common zeroes of some polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
. The
Zariski topology
on
\mathbb{C}^{k}
is the topology whose closed sets are the affine varieties of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
, and more generally, the Zariski topology on a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
is the subspace topology inherited from the Zariski topology on
\mathbb{C}^{k}
. For an arbitrary set
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
, we use
\overline{X}
to denote the
Zariski closure
of
X
, which is the smallest affine variety containing
X
. We say that a set is Zariski closed/open/dense to mean that it is closed/open/dense in the Zariski topology.

An affine variety is
irreducible
if it is nonempty and cannot be written as the proper union of two affine varieties. We stress that our definition of affine varieties does not include irreducibility, although most of the varieties we shall be dealing with are irreducible. Every nonempty affine variety
X
can be uniquely written as the union of a finite number of irreducible affine varieties, which are called the
components
of
X
. An interesting feature of the Zariski topology is that for an irreducible variety
X
, every nonempty Zariski open subset (that is, every nonempty subset of the form
X\setminus Y
for some affine variety
Y
) is Zariski dense in
X
.

An affine variety
X
is
homogeneous
if it is invariant under scaling, that is,
cx\in X
for every
x\in X
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
.
An important class of homogeneous irreducible affine varieties are
linear spaces
, which are just linear subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
.

The
dimension
of an affine variety
X
, denoted by
\dim(X)
, is the largest number
k
for which there exists a chain of irreducible varieties
X_{0}\subsetneq X_{1}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq X_{k}\subseteq X
. It can be seen from this definition that if
X^{\prime}\subsetneq X
are irreducible varieties, then
\dim(X^{\prime})<\dim(X)
.

A subset
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
is
Zariski constructible
if can be obtained from a finite number of affine varieties by taking intersections, unions and complements.
A Zariski constructible set
X
is
irreducible
if its Zariski closure is irreducible. Every Zariski constructible set contains a dense Zariski open subset of its closure.
Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
be an irreducible Zariski constructible set.
A function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
is a
polynomial map
if there exist polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
such that
f(x)=(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{l}(x))
for all
x\in X
. Polynomial maps are continuous with respect to the Zariski topology, and it follows from topological considerations that if
X
is an irreducible Zariski constructible set and
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
is a polynomial map, then
\overline{f(X)}
is also irreducible.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
be an irreducible affine variety defined by some polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
. Consider the polynomial map
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}):\mathbb{C}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
and let
df
be the
Jacobian
of
f
, which can be thought of as a
k\times l
matrix of polynomials. At each point
x\in X
, we define the
tangent space of
X
at
x
(denoted by
T_{x}(X)
) as the kernel of
df_{x}
, the Jacobian of
f
evaluated at
x
. This is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
, and it is independent of the choice of generating polynomials
f
of
X
. The dimension of
T_{x}(X)
is always at least the dimension of
X
, and if the two quantities are the same, then
x
is said to be a
smooth point
of
X
; otherwise it is a
singular point
. The singular points of
X
form a proper subvariety called the
singular locus
of
X
. If
X^{\prime}
is an irreducible affine variety with
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
and
x\in X^{\prime},
then
T_{x}(X^{\prime})
is a linear subspace of
T_{x}(X)
.

Let
x\in X
be a smooth point and let
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
be a vector. By a slight abuse of terminology we say that
\omega
is a
tangential hyperplane to
X
at
x
if
\omega^{\perp}=\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}y_{i}=0\}
contains
T_{x}(X)
. We denote the set of tangential hyperplanes to
T_{x}(X)
by
T_{x}(X)^{\perp}
, and call this the
orthogonal complement
of
T_{x}(X)
.

Let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
be a polynomial map and suppose that

for some irreducible affine variety

The following is one of the many results called Bertini’s theorem. It is an algebraic analogue of Sard’s lemma from differential topology.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
[
33
, Section 6.2]
)
Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
be an irreducible affine variety,
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
a polynomial map and

. Then for a nonempty Zariski open subset of points

is smooth, then there is a nonempty Zariski open subset of points

such that for every
x\in f^{-1}(y)
the map

is surjective.

Let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
be a polynomial map and
y\in\text{Im}(f)
. We call
f^{-1}(y)
the
fiber of
f
over
y
in

. This is also an affine variety. The first part of the following result is often called the fiber dimension theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

(
[
33

)

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
be an irreducible affine variety,
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
a polynomial map and
Y=\overline{f(X)}
. Then for a nonempty Zariski open subset of points

, every component of the fiber of

over

has dimension
\dim(X)-\dim(Y)
.

2.2
Varieties defined over
\mathbb{Q}

In algebraic geometry a property of points of an irreducible affine variety
X
is sometimes said to be “generic” if it holds on a nonempty Zariski open (and hence dense) subset of
X
. However, in the rigidity theory literature it is common to use a stronger notion of genericity which will be more convenient for us as well. Although the results in this subsection are routine, for future reference we provide proofs in the Appendix whenever references are not available.

An affine variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
is
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
if it is the set of common zeroes of some polynomials with rational coefficients. More generally, a Zariski constructible set
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
is
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
if it can be obtained from a finite number of affine varieties defined over
\mathbb{Q}
by taking intersections, unions and complements. The first part of the following proposition is (a special case of) Chevalley’s theorem.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
be a Zariski constructible set defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
a polynomial map with rational coefficients.

(a)

(
[
1
, Theorem 1.22]
) The image
f(X)
is also Zariski constructible and defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.

(b)

(
[
11
, Theorem A.8]
) The Zariski closure
\overline{X}
is also defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.

(c)

(
[
13
, Lemma A.4]
) There is a subvariety
X^{\prime}
of
\overline{X}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
such that
\overline{X}\setminus X^{\prime}\subseteq X
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
be an irreducible variety defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. We say that a point

-generic point lies in

.

(

\mathbb{Q}

(

2.3
Matroids of varieties

In this subsection we define the algebraic matroid of an irreducible affine variety
X
. Again,
[
29
]
is a good reference. Oxley
[
28
, Section 6.7]
also discusses algebraic matroids, but from the perspective of algebraic independence in field extensions rather than coordinate projections of affine varieties. As discussed in
[
29
]
, the two approaches are equivalent when one is working over an algebraically closed field, as we shall do.

Let
E
be a finite set and let us consider the affine space
\mathbb{C}^{E}
with coordinate axes indexed by
E
. Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be an irreducible affnie variety. For every subset
E_{0}\subseteq E
, we denote by
\pi_{E_{0}}
the restriction of the coordinate projection
\mathbb{C}^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
to
X
. Throughout the paper we will use the notation
X_{E_{0}}=\overline{\pi_{E_{0}}(X)}
.
To every irreducible variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
we associate a matroid
\mathcal{M}(X)
on ground set
E
defined by

E_{0}\subseteq E\text{ is independent in }\mathcal{M}(X)\Longleftrightarrow X_{E_{0}}=\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}.

We call
\mathcal{M}(X)
the
algebraic matroid
associated to
X
, and we say that
X
is an
algebraic representation
of
\mathcal{M}(X)
. The rank of
\mathcal{M}(X)
is
\dim(X)
, and the restriction of
\mathcal{M}(X)
to a subset
E_{0}\subseteq E
is the algebraic matroid associated to the projection
X_{E_{0}}
. The following characterization of spanning subsets in algebraic matroids follows from
Theorem

2.2
and its “generic” version
Lemma

2.6
(b).

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be an irreducible affine variety,
E_{0}\subseteq E
a subset and
\pi_{E_{0}}:X\rightarrow X_{E_{0}}
the projection of
X
onto
\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
.

(a)

E_{0}
is spanning in
\mathcal{M}(X)
if and only if for a nonempty Zariski open subset of points
x\in X
, the fiber of
\pi_{E_{0}}
over
\pi_{E_{0}}(x)
is finite.

(b)

If
X
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and
x
is an
X
-generic point, then
E_{0}
is spanning in
\mathcal{M}(X)
if and only if the fiber of
\pi_{E_{0}}
over
\pi_{E_{0}}(x)
is finite.

The next lemma relates the separations of
\mathcal{M}(X)
to the geometry of
X
. It is an easy consequence of the fact that the Cartesian product of irreducible affine varieties is an irreducible affine variety.

Lemma 2.8
.

(
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
)
Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be an irreducible affine variety. A bipartition
(E_{1},E_{2})
of
E
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X)
if and only if
X=X_{E_{1}}\times X_{E_{2}}
, where
X_{E_{i}}=\overline{\pi_{E_{i}}(X)}
. In particular,
e\in E
is a bridge of
\mathcal{M}(X)
if and only if
X=X_{E-e}\times\mathbb{C}
.

If
X^{\prime}\subseteq X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
are irreducible varieties, then clearly
\overline{\pi_{E_{0}}(X^{\prime})}=\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
also implies
\overline{\pi_{E_{0}}(X)}=\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
for any subset
E_{0}\subseteq E
. Thus in this case every independent set of
\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})
is also independent in
\mathcal{M}(X)
, or more concisely,
\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})\preceq\mathcal{M}(X)
.

The following folklore result relates the algebraic matroid of
X
to the algebraic matroid of its tangent space at a suitable point. This connection will be of central importance in this paper.

Theorem 2.9
.

If
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
is an irreducible affine variety, then
\mathcal{M}(X)=\mathcal{M}(T_{x}(X))
holds for a nonempty Zariski open subset of points
x\in X
. If
X
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, then
\mathcal{M}(X)=\mathcal{M}(T_{x}(X))
holds for every
X
-generic point
x
.

Proof.

Note that the projection
\pi_{E_{0}}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
is a linear mapping and hence it coincides with its differential at any point. Let
X_{E_{0}}
denote
\overline{\pi_{E_{0}}(X)}
. It follows by
Theorem

2.1
that there is a nonempty Zariski open subset
X^{\prime}
of points
x\in X
for which the projection
\pi_{E_{0}}:T_{x}{X}\rightarrow T_{\pi_{E_{0}}(x)}(X_{E_{0}})
is surjective for every subset
E_{0}\subseteq E
. Since
X_{E_{0}}=\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
if and only if the tangent space of
X_{E_{0}}
at some smooth point is all of
\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
, it follows that for every
x\in X^{\prime}
we have
\mathcal{M}(X)=\mathcal{M}(T_{x}(X))
. The statement for generic points is proved in the same way by using
Lemma

2.6
(d) instead of
Theorem

2.1
.
∎

We shall often consider algebraic matroids of linear spaces. Unsurprisingly, such matroids can also be viewed as linear matroids, as described by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be a linear space.

(a)

If
M
is any
|E|\times k
matrix whose columns span
X
, then
\mathcal{M}(X)
is the linear matroid (over
\mathbb{C}
) defined by the rows of
M
.

(b)

We have
\mathcal{M}(X^{\perp})=\mathcal{M}(X)^{*}
, where
X^{\perp}=\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{E}:\sum_{e\in E}x_{e}y_{e}=0\,\,\forall x\in X\}
is the orthogonal complement of
X
.

Proof.

(a)
Since
X
is linear, a subset
E_{0}\subseteq E
is independent in
\mathcal{M}(X)
if and only if the projection of
X
to
\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
is surjective. This projection is the same as the image of the matrix
M_{E_{0}}
that consists of the rows of
M
corresponding to
E_{0}
. Thus the projection is surjective if and only if
M_{E_{0}}
has full row rank, which is equivalent to
E_{0}
being independent in the linear matroid defined by the rows of
M
.
(b)
This now follows from the well-known fact that the linear matroid of a vector space is dual to the linear matroid of the orthogonal complement of the vector space, see, e.g.,
[
28
, Section 2.2]
.
∎

The following lemma strengthens
Lemma

2.7
for algebraic matroids of linear spaces.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be a linear space, and
E_{0}\subseteq E
. The following are equivalent.

(a)

The fiber of
\pi_{E_{0}}
over
\pi_{E_{0}}(x)
is a singleton for every
x\in X
.

(b)

E_{0}
is spanning in
\mathcal{M}(X)
.

(c)

The fiber of
\pi_{E_{0}}
over
\pi_{E_{0}}(x)
is a singleton for some

.

Proof.

This follows from
Lemma

2.7
and the observation that
\pi_{E_{0}}:X\rightarrow\pi_{E_{0}}(X)
is a linear transformation and hence its fibers are pairwise isomorphic affine subspaces.
∎

2.4
The rigidity matroid

In this subsection we recall the basic notions of combinatorial rigidity theory. This exposition is nonstandard for two (related) reasons: we work over the complex field
\mathbb{C}
instead of
\mathbb{R}
, and we mainly work with the algebraic representation of the rigidity matroid given by the measurement variety, rather than the linear representation given by the rigidity matrix of a generic realization.

2.4.1
Basic notions

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
n
vertices and let
d\geq 1
be an integer. Given a function
p:V\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
we say that the pair
(G,p)
is a
realization
of
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, or that
(G,p)
is a
framework
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We identify the set of functions
V\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\mathbb{C}^{nd}
and call the elements of this space
configurations
of
V
. If
p\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{nd}
, then we say that
(G,p)
is a realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is
generic
if
p
is
\mathbb{C}^{nd}
-generic. This means that the coordinates of
p
do not satisfy any nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients.

For a pair of vertices
u,v\in V
we let the “distance map”
m_{uv}:\mathbb{C}^{nd}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be

m_{uv}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(p(u)_{i}-p(v)_{i})^{2},

and we define the
edge measurement map

m_{d,G}:\mathbb{C}^{nd}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{E}
as

m_{d,G}(p)=(m_{uv}(p))_{uv\in E}.

Two realizations
(G,p)
and
(G,q)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
are
equivalent
if
m_{d,G}(p)=m_{d,G}(q)
.

The
d
-dimensional measurement variety
of
G
, denoted by
M_{d,G}
, is the Zariski closure of
m_{d,G}(\mathbb{C}^{nd})
. This is an irreducible affine variety, and by
Proposition

2.3
it is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since the coordinate functions of
m_{d,G}
are homogeneous polynomials,
M_{d,G}
is also homogeneous. The
(
d
-dimensional generic) rigidity matroid
of
G
, which we denote by
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)
, is the algebraic matroid
\mathcal{M}(M_{d,G})
associated to
M_{d,G}
.
We use
r_{d}(G)
to denote the rank of
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)
, which is equal to the dimension of
M_{d,G}
. We say that
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent
if
r_{d}(G)=|E(G)|
, and that it is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit
if it is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent but every proper subgraph of
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent. The graph
G
is
d
-rigid
if
E
is spanning in
\mathcal{R}_{d}(K_{V})
, the rigidity matroid of the complete graph on
V
. When
n\geq d+1
, this is equivalent to the condition that
r_{d}(G)=nd-\binom{d+1}{2}
. (If
n\leq d+1
, then
G
is
d
-rigid if and only if it is a complete graph.) An edge
e
of
G
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge
if
r_{d}(G)=r_{d}(G-e)+1
, or equivalently if
e
is not contained in any subgraph of
G
that is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit. A pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked
in
G
if
r_{d}(G+uv)=r_{d}(G)
. When
u
and
v
are nonadjacent in
G
, this means that there is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit in
G+uv
that contains the edge
uv
, or equivalently that
uv
is not an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge in
G+uv
. We say that
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected
if
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)
is connected. The
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected components
of
G
are the subgraphs induced by the connected components of
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)
.

2.4.3
Equilibrium stresses

Let
(G,p)
be a framework in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The
rigidity matrix
of
(G,p)
, denoted by
R(G,p)\in\mathbb{C}^{|E|\times nd}
, is the Jacobian of
m_{d,G}
evaluated at
p
. By combining
Lemma

2.6
(d),
Theorem

2.9
and
Lemma

2.10
(a) we obtain that for any generic
(G,p)
, the row matroid of
R(G,p)
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)
. (This is commonly taken as the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)
.)

A vector
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
is an
equilibrium stress
, or simply a
stress
of
(G,p)
if
\omega^{T}R(G,p)=0
, that is,
\omega
lies in the cokernel of
R(G,p)
. The stresses of
(G,p)
form a linear space
S(G,p)
called the
space of stresses
of
(G,p)
. The next lemma gives a geometric interpretation of
S(G,p)
for generic frameworks.

Lemma 2.15
.

(E.g.,
[
12
, Lemma 2.21]
)
If
(G,p)
be a generic framework in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
x=m_{d,G}(p)
, then
S(G,p)=T_{x}(M_{d,G})^{\perp}
. That is,
S(G,p)
is the set of hyperplanes tangent to
x
at
M_{d,G}
.

Proof.

The space of stresses is just the cokernel of
R(G,p)
, which by elementary linear algebra is the orthogonal complement of the column space of
R(G,p)
. Since
(G,p)
is generic, by
Lemma

2.6
(d) this column space is precisely the tangent space
T_{x}(M_{d,G})
.
∎

When expanded, the condition
\omega R(G,p)=0
yields the
equilibrium conditions
stating that for every vertex

\sum_{uv\in E}\omega(uv)(p(u)-p(v))=0.

This formulation highlights the important fact that given a vector
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
, the set

K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)=\{p\in\mathbb{C}^{nd}:\omega\text{ is a stress of }(G,p)\}

is a linear space. We call
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)
the
(
d
-dimensional) stress kernel
of
\omega
.
2
2

2

We note that in
[

, the name “stress kernel” was reserved for what we call the one-dimensional stress kernel, and the

This space will play an important role throughout this paper. Since every equilibrium condition gives an independent condition for each of the

coordinate vectors of

, we have

as linear spaces. If
(G,p)
is in affine general position, then the coordinate vectors of

and the all ones vector form a linearly independent set contained in

, so in this case we have

It is also clear that the equilibrium conditions are preserved under affine transformations, and hence we have

, where

denotes the set of configurations arising as affine images of

2.4.4
Globally linked pairs and global rigidity

A pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
is
globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
if whenever
(G,p)
is a generic realization and
(G,q)
is equivalent to
(G,p)
, we have
m_{uv}(p)=m_{uv}(q)
, or in other words,
(G+uv,p)
and
(G+uv,q)
are also equivalent.
Although we shall not use it, we note that being globally linked in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
has another natural interpretation: it means that there is a rational function
F:\mathbb{C}^{E(G)}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}
with rational coefficients such that
m_{uv}(p)=F(m_{d,G}(p))
for any generic realization
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Similarly, a pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
is
globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if whenever
(G,p)
is a generic realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(G,q)
is another realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equivalent to
(G,p)
, we have
m_{uv}(p)=m_{uv}(q)
. Being globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
clearly implies being globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and it is unclear whether the two notions are in fact equivalent. We note that (in contrast with the complex case) it can happen that the condition in the definition of being globally linked in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
only holds for some, but not all generic realizations of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If this is the case, then
\{u,v\}
is said to be
weakly globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; see
[
26
]
for recent results regarding this notion.

Interestingly, the three notions of “being globally linked” are known to be equivalent when we require them to hold for all pairs of vertices.

Theorem 2.18
.

(
[
26
, Lemma 3.2]
and
[
14
, Theorem 1]
)
The following are equivalent for a graph
G
.

(a)

Every pair of vertices is weakly globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(b)

Every pair of vertices is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(c)

Every pair of vertices is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

If any of the above three equivalent conditions holds, then we say that
G
is
globally
d
-rigid
. By unraveling condition
(c)
we arrive at the definition that for every generic realization
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, if
(G,q)
is an equivalent framework in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, then
m_{d,K_{V}}(p)=m_{d,K_{V}}(q)
, where
K_{V}
is the complete graph on vertex set

.

As we noted in the Introduction, it is a folklore result that a graph is globally

-rigid if and only if it is
2
-connected. The combinatorial characterization of globally

-rigid graphs is due to Jackson and Jordán. We will need the following form of their result.

Theorem 2.19
.

(
[
19
]
, see also
[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
)
Let
G
be a graph on at least four vertices. The following are equivalent.

(a)

G
is globally
2
-rigid.

(b)

G
is
3
-connected and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected.

(c)

G
is
3
-connected and does not contain
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-bridges.

One of the main results of
[
12
]
is the following characterization of globally
d
-rigid graphs. The original result was stated for real frameworks, and subsequently in
[
14
]
it was extended to the complex setting. It is often formulated in terms of the maximum rank of stress matrices, but the following equivalent form will be more convenient for us. Recall that we use
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)
to denote the
d
-dimensional stress kernel of
\omega
, while
A(G,p)
denotes the set of affine images of
(G,p)
.

Theorem 2.20
.

(
[
12
]
, see also
[
14
, Theorem 1]
)
Let
G
be a graph and
(G,p)
a generic realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then
G
is globally
d
-rigid if and only if there is a stress
\omega\in S(G,p)
for which
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)=A(G,p)
.

We note that in
[
12
]
, another characterization of globally
d
-rigid graphs was given in terms of the “shared stress kernel” instead of the stress kernel. We postpone the introduction of shared stress kernels and this result until
Section

4
, where they shall play an important role.

3
The generic stress matroid

In this section we introduce and study the
d
-dimensional generic stress variety of
G
, which is the dual variety of the measurement variety of
G
. As the name suggests, the generic stress variety describes the global geometry of equilibrium stresses of generic realizations. We shall be particularly interested in the coordinate projections of this variety, and hence we will study its algebraic matroid, which we call the
d
-dimensional generic stress matroid of
G
.

Motivated by possible applications in other areas, in
Section

3.1
we recall the notion of the dual variety and consider the associated “algebraic dual matroid” in a general setting. While the duality theory of varieties is a well-studied topic, we are not aware of other works that consider duality from the perspective of algebraic matroids. In
Section

3.3
we specialize to the setting of rigidity theory and consider the algebraic dual matroid of the rigidity matroid (as represented by the measurement variety). In particular, we obtain some results about the independent sets and the spanning sets of this matroid which we will use in our investigation of
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs in the next section. Before this, in
Section

3.2
we briefly consider another matroid which we call the affine edge measurement matroid of
G
. The significance of this matroid will become apparent in
Section

3.3
where we show, essentially by rephrasing
Theorem

2.20
, that whenever
G
is globally
d
-rigid, the affine edge measurement matroid of
G
is the dual of the stress matroid of
G
.

3.1
The algebraic dual matroid

We start by defining the dual variety and stating the reflexivity theorem along with some of its consequences. A good reference on this topic is
[
7
, Chapter 2]
, with the caveat that the authors use the language of projective varieties, while we are working with the affine cones of such varieties.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be an irreducible homogeneous affine variety. The
conormal bundle

C_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}\times\mathbb{C}^{E}
of
X
is the Zariski closure
of

\{(x,\omega):x\in X\text{ is smooth and }\omega\text{ is a tangent hyperplane of }X\text{ at }x\}.

This is an irreducible affine variety of dimension
|E|
. Let
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
denote the projection of
\mathbb{C}^{E}\times\mathbb{C}^{E}
onto the first and second factor, respectively. It is known that for every smooth point
x\in X
,

\pi_{2}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(x))=T_{x}(X)^{\perp},

(1)

see
[
7
, Section 2.1.4]
.
In other words, taking the Zariski closure in the definition of
C_{X}
does not add new points over smooth points of
X
. The
dual variety
of
X
is defined as
X^{*}=\overline{\pi_{2}(C_{X})}.
This is a homogeneous irreducible affine variety in
\mathbb{C}^{E}
. It can be more concretely described as the Zariski closure of

\bigcup\left\{T_{x}(X)^{\perp}:x\in X\text{ is a smooth point}\right\}.

(2)

In general, we have the inequalities

|E|-\dim(X)\leq\dim X^{*}\leq|E|-1,

where the first inequality is strict whenever
X
does not lie in a linear hyperplane of
\mathbb{C}^{E}
.

The
reflexivity theorem
states that
C_{X^{*}}
, the conormal bundle of
X^{*}
, is the same as
C_{X}
with the two factors switched around:

C_{X^{*}}=\overline{\{(\omega,x):x\in X\text{ is smooth and }\omega\text{ is a tangent hyperplane of }X\text{ at }x\}.}

This implies in particular that
(X^{*})^{*}=X
.
For a smooth point
\omega\in X^{*}
, the
contact locus
of
\omega
in
X
is

L_{X}(\omega)=\overline{\{x\in X:x\text{ is smooth and }\omega\text{ is a tangent hyperplane of }X\text{ at }x\}}.

It follows from the reflexivity theorem and
Equation

1
that
L_{X}(\omega)=T_{\omega}(X^{*})^{\perp}
. In particular,
L_{X}(\omega)
is a linear space of dimension
|E|-\dim(X^{*})
; this is sometimes called the
linearity theorem
.

The following lemma shows that if an affine variety is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, then so is its dual. Its importance in our case will be that it lets us work with generic points of the dual variety. We give a proof sketch in the Appendix.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
X
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, then so are
C_{X}
and
X^{*}
. In this case
X^{*}
can be written as the closure of

\bigcup\left\{T_{x}(X)^{\perp}:x\in X\text{ is }X\text{-generic}\right\}.

(3)

Whenever
\omega\in X^{*}
is
X^{*}
-generic, there is a
C_{X}
-generic pair
(x,\omega)
. For every such pair,
x
is
X
-generic and
\omega\in T_{x}(X)^{\perp}
.

We define the
algebraic dual matroid
of
\mathcal{M}(X)
to be
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
. Note that the algebraic dual is determined not by the matroid
\mathcal{M}(X)
, but rather its algebraic representation
X
. The rank of the algebraic dual matroid is
\dim(X^{*})
. If
X
does not lie in a linear hyperplane, then
\dim(X^{*})\geq|E|-\dim(X)+1=\rk(\mathcal{M}(X)^{*})+1
, which shows that in general
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
is not the dual matroid of
\mathcal{M}(X)
. On the other hand, since
(X^{*})^{*}=X
, the algebraic dual of
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
is
\mathcal{M}(X)
, which is why we use the term “dual” for this construction. We note that if
X
is a linear space, then
X^{*}=X^{\perp}
and hence by
Lemma

2.10
we have
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})=\mathcal{M}(X^{\perp})=\mathcal{M}(X)^{*}
. That is, for linear spaces the algebraic dual matroid does coincide with the dual matroid.

The following theorem highlights another connection between
\mathcal{M}(X)
and its algebraic dual. It follows almost immediately from
Theorem

2.9
. Nonetheless, it turns out to be of central importance in this paper.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be an irreducible homogeneous affine variety defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, and let
\omega\in X^{*}
be a generic point of the dual variety. Then
\mathcal{M}(L_{X}(\omega))=\mathcal{M}(X^{*})^{*}
. That is, the algebraic matroid of the contact locus of
\omega
is the dual matroid of
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
.

Proof.

Recall that
L_{X}(\omega)=T_{\omega}(X^{*})^{\perp}
.
Lemma

2.10
(b) implies that
\mathcal{M}(L_{X}(\omega))
is the dual matroid of
\mathcal{M}(T_{\omega}(X^{*}))
, and it follows from
Theorem

2.9
that the latter is just
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{E}
be an irreducible homogeneous affine variety. We have
\mathcal{M}(X)^{*}\preceq\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
, and similarly
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})^{*}\preceq\mathcal{M}(X)
.

Proof.

We repeat the previous proof for
X
instead of
X^{*}
. By
Theorem

2.9
there is some smooth point
x\in X
such that
\mathcal{M}(X)=\mathcal{M}(T_{x}(X)).
By
Lemma

2.10
we thus have
\mathcal{M}(X)^{*}=\mathcal{M}(T_{x}(X)^{\perp})
. But from
Equation

2
we have
T_{x}(X)^{\perp}\subseteq X^{*},
and hence
\mathcal{M}(T_{x}(X)^{\perp})\preceq\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
. The other statement follows from duality.
∎

It is a standard result that for a matroid
\mathcal{M}
on ground set
E
, a bipartition
(E_{1},E_{2})
of
E
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}
if and only if it is a separation of the dual matroid
\mathcal{M}^{*}
. The following proposition is the analogous statement for the algebraic dual matroid.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X
be an irreducible homogeneous affine variety in
\mathbb{C}^{E}
and
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2}
a bipartition of
E
. Then
(E_{1},E_{2})
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X)
if and only if
(E_{1},E_{2})
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
. Moreover, if
(E_{1},E_{2})
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X)
, then
X^{*}=(X_{E_{1}})^{*}\times(X_{E_{2}})^{*}
, where
X_{E_{i}}=\overline{\pi_{E_{i}}(X)}
is the projection of
X
onto
\mathbb{C}^{E_{i}}
.

Note that by
Lemma

2.8
, if
(E_{1},E_{2})
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
, then
X^{*}=(X^{*})_{E_{1}}\times(X^{*})_{E_{2}}
, where the subscripts again mean that we take the Zariski closure of the projection. However, it is a priori not clear whether
(X^{*})_{E_{i}}=(X_{E_{i}})^{*}
holds, and indeed, this is not true in general. We shall show that when
(E_{1},E_{2})
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X)
, then this equality does hold.

Proof of
Proposition

3.4
.

By symmetry it is enough to show the “only if” direction. Let us suppose that
(E_{1},E_{2})
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X)
. From
Lemma

2.8
we have
X=X_{E_{1}}\times X_{E_{2}}
. This means that if
X_{E_{1}}
is defined by the polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
in variables
x_{e},e\in E_{1}
and
X_{E_{2}}
is defined by the polynomials
g_{1},\ldots,g_{l}
in variables
y_{e},e\in E_{2}
, then
X
is defined by the polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k},g_{1},\ldots,g_{l}
. It follows from the definitions now that for every smooth point
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in X
we have
T_{x}(X)=T_{x_{1}}(X_{E_{1}})\times T_{x_{2}}(X_{E_{2}})
and thus
T_{x}(X)^{\perp}=T_{x_{1}}(X_{E_{1}})^{\perp}\times T_{x_{2}}(X_{E_{2}})^{\perp}
. Combined with the fact that
\dim(X)=\dim(X_{E_{1}})+\dim(X_{E_{2}})
this also shows that
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
in
X
is smooth if and only if
x_{i}
is smooth in
X_{E_{i}}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Let us denote the set of smooth points in
X_{i}
by
\text{Sm}(X_{i})
.
From
Equation

2
it follows that
X^{*}
is the Zariski closure of
\bigcup\{T_{x}(X)^{\perp}:x\in X\text{ is a smooth point}\}.
By the discussion in the preceding paragraph, this set can be also written as

\displaystyle\bigcup\left\{T_{x_{1}}(X_{E_{1}})^{\perp}\times T_{x_{2}}(X_{E_{2}})^{\perp}:x_{i}\in\text{Sm}(X_{i}),i\in\{1,2\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\left\{T_{x_{1}}(X_{E_{1}})^{\perp}:x_{1}\in\text{Sm}(X_{1})\right\}\times\bigcup\left\{T_{x_{2}}(X_{E_{2}})^{\perp}:x_{2}\in\text{Sm}(X_{2})\right\}.

Using
Equation

2
and the well-known fact that the Zariski closure of a Cartesian product of two sets is the Cartesian product of the respective Zariski closures, we obtain
X^{*}=(X_{E_{1}})^{*}\times(X_{E_{2}})^{*}
. This implies that
(X^{*})_{E_{i}}=(X_{E_{i}})^{*}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and now
Lemma

2.8
shows that
(E_{1},E_{2})
is a separation of
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
. This proves the “only if” direction, while the other direction follows by symmetry.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

(
[
7
, Section 2.2.6]
)
Let
X
be an irreducible homogeneous affine variety in
\mathbb{C}^{E}
and
e\in E
. Then
e
is a bridge in
\mathcal{M}(X)
if and only if it is a loop in
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.8
we have
X=X_{E-e}\times\mathbb{C}
. Since the dual of
\mathbb{C}
(as a subvariety of
\mathbb{C}
) is the single point
\{0\}
,
Proposition

3.4
implies that
X^{*}=X^{*}_{E-e}\times\{0\}
, so
e
is a loop in
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
. Conversely, if
e
is a loop in
\mathcal{M}(X^{*})
, then the projection of
X^{*}
onto the coordinate axis of
e
is a zero-dimensional homogeneous variety, so it must be the singleton
\{0\}
. Hence we have
X^{*}=X^{*}_{E-e}\times\{0\}
, and it follows from
Lemmas

2.8
and
3.4
again
e
is a bridge in
\mathcal{M}(X)
.
∎

3.2
The affine edge measurement matroid

Our goal is to investigate the algebraic dual matroid of the generic rigidity matroid
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)=\mathcal{M}(M_{d,G})
. Before that, we take a brief detour to examine another matroid that we shall call the
d
-dimensional affine edge measurement matroid of
G
. This matroid will play an important role in the next subsection.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
(G,p)
a realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that the configuration
p
is in affine general position.
Recall that we use
A(G,p)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{nd}
to denote the linear space of (possibly singular) affine images of
p
. Since
p
is in affine general position and an affine transformation is uniquely determined by its image on
d+1
points in general position, we have
\dim(A(G,p))=d\cdot\min(d+1,n)
.

Let
B(G,p)=m_{d,G}(A(G,p))
be the space of edge measurements of affine images of
(G,p)
. It turns out that
B(G,p)
is also a linear space, which we can describe concretely. Let us define the edge vectors
e_{uv}=p(u)-p(v)
for each
uv\in E
. Note that
e_{uv}
is only defined up to sign; to be more precise, we could take an arbitrary orientation of
G
and define
e_{uv}
using this orientation. Finally, let us denote the space of symmetric
d\times d
matrices over
\mathbb{C}
by
\text{Sym}(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

[
13
, Lemma 4.6]

Let
(G,p)
and
e_{uv},uv\in E
be as in the preceding discussion. Then

B(G,p)=\{(e_{uv}^{T}Qe_{uv})_{uv\in E}:Q\in\text{Sym}(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d})\}.

In particular,
B(G,p)
is a linear space of dimension at most
\binom{d+1}{2}
.

We say that
the edge directions of
(G,p)
lie on a conic at infinity
if there is a nonzero symmetric matrix
Q\in\text{Sym}(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d})
such that
e_{uv}^{T}Qe_{uv}=0
for every
uv\in E
. This means that the linear mapping
Q\mapsto(e_{uv}^{T}Qe_{uv})_{uv\in E}
is singular, which by
Lemma

3.6
is equivalent to the condition that
\dim(B(G,p))<\binom{d+1}{2}.
The following is a classic result of Connelly. The original statement was for frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but the same proof works for frameworks in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

(
[
4
, Proposition 4.3]
)
Let
G
be a graph. Suppose that
G
has a subgraph
G_{0}
in which the degree of every vertex is at least
d
. Let
(G,p)
be a realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. If
(G,p)
is generic, then the edge directions of
(G,p)
do not lie on a conic at infinity.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
G
be a graph. The matroid
\mathcal{M}(B(G,p))
is the same for every generic realization
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Its rank is at most
\binom{d+1}{2}
, and is equal to
\binom{d+1}{2}
if
G
is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent.

Proof.

Recall that by
Lemma

2.10
, the algebraic matroid of a linear space is the same as the row matroid of any matrix whose columns span the space. By fixing a basis of
\text{Sym}(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d})
consisting of integer-valued matrices and considering the linear transformation
\text{Sym}(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d})\rightarrow B(G,p)
described in
Lemma

3.6
in this basis, we obtain a matrix
M(G,p)
whose column space is
B(G,p)
. Since the minors of
M(G,p)
are polynomials with rational coefficients in the coordinates of
p(v),v\in V(G)
, the rank of each submatrix of
M(G,p)
is the same for every generic choice of
p
, and thus so is the row matroid of
M(G,p)
.

Recall that the rank of
\mathcal{M}(B(G,p))
is just
\dim(B(G,p)
, which is at most
\binom{d+1}{2}
by
Lemma

3.6
. If
G
is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent, then it contains an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit
C
as a subgraph. It is well-known (and can be deduced from
Lemma

2.12
(c)) that such a graph has minimum degree at least
d+1
. Hence by
Proposition

3.7
, the edge directions of
(G,p)
do not lie on a conic at infinity for any generic realization
(G,p)
. This means by
Lemma

3.6
that
B(G,p)
is linearly isomorphic to the space of symmetric
d\times d
matrices, which has dimension
\binom{d+1}{2}
.
∎

Following
Proposition

3.8
, we define the
(
d
-dimensional generic) affine edge measurement matroid
of
G
, denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
, to be
\mathcal{M}(B(G,p))
, where
(G,p)
is any generic realization of
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For us, the most important property of this matroid will be the following.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
G
be a graph and
E_{0}\subseteq E(G)
. If
E_{0}
spans at most
d+1
vertices, then it is independent in
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
G_{0}=G[E_{0}]
and
k=|V(G_{0})|
. Consider a generic realization
(G,p)
of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
(G_{0},p_{0})
be the subframework corresponding to
G_{0}
. Since
k\leq d+1
and
(G_{0},p_{0})
is generic, we can map it to any configuration using affine transformations, so
A(G_{0},p_{0})=\mathbb{C}^{kd}
. It is well-known (and can be deduced from
Lemma

2.12
) that graphs on at most
d+1
vertices are
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent, so

\overline{m_{d,G_{0}}(A(G_{0},p_{0}))}=\overline{m_{d,G_{0}}(\mathbb{C}^{kd})}=M_{d,G_{0}}=\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}.

But clearly

\pi_{E_{0}}(B(G,p))=\pi_{E_{0}}(m_{d,G}(A(G,p)))=m_{d,G_{0}}(A(G_{0},p_{0})),

so we have
\overline{\pi_{E_{0}}(B(G,p))}=\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
, which shows that
E_{0}
is independent in
\mathcal{M}(B(G,p))=\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
.
∎

When
d=1
,
Propositions

3.8
and
3.9
give a complete description of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
.

Corollary 3.10
.

For any graph
G
,
\mathcal{A}_{1}(G)
is the rank
1
uniform matroid on
E(G)
.

A computer-assisted computation shows that for
d\leq 4
,
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
is the uniform matroid of rank
\binom{d+1}{2}
for any graph with at least
\binom{d+1}{2}
edges. It is unclear if this holds for all
d
.

Conjecture 3.11
.

If
G
is a graph with at least
\binom{d+1}{2}
edges, then
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
is the uniform matroid of rank
\binom{d+1}{2}.

Recall that the rank of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
is
\binom{d+1}{2}
if and only if for generic realizations
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the edge directions of
(G,p)
do not lie on a conic at infinity. Hence
3.11
would imply a strengthening of
Proposition

3.7
, namely that the edge directions of a generic realization
(G,p)
do not lie on a conic at infinity provided that
G
has at least
\binom{d+1}{2}
edges.

3.3
The generic stress matroid

In this subsection we specialize the discussion of
Section

3.1
to the measurement variety
M_{d,G}
. We refer the reader to
Figure

1
for a summary of the different spaces and matroids considered throughout this subsection.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices,
d\geq 1
a fixed integer, and
(G,p)
a framework in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Recall that
S(G,p)
denotes the linear space of stresses of
(G,p)
, and that for a stress
\omega\in S(G,p)
, we use
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{nd}
to denote the stress kernel of
\omega
, which is the linear space of configurations
q
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
\omega\in S(G,q)
. As we noted before,
A(G,p)\subseteq K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)
holds for any stress
\omega
.

Now let us consider the measurement variety.
Recall that for a generic realization
(G,p)
with
x=m_{d,G}(p)
, we have
S(G,p)=T_{x}(M_{d,G})^{\perp}
by
Lemma

2.15
. Also, it follows from
Lemma

2.5
that the
M_{d,G}
-generic points are precisely the images of generic configurations under
m_{d,G}
. Thus by specializing
Equation

3
we can write the dual variety
M_{d,G}^{*}
as the Zariski closure of

\bigcup\left\{S(G,p):(G,p)\text{ is a generic framework in }\mathbb{C}^{d}\right\}.

(4)

Based on
Equation

4
, we shall call
M_{d,G}^{*}
the
(
d
-dimensional generic) stress variety
of
G
, and we shall call
\mathcal{M}(M_{d,G}^{*})
the
(
d
-dimensional generic) stress matroid
of
G
. That is, the
d
-dimensional generic stress matroid, which we will denote by
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
, is the algebraic dual matroid of
\mathcal{R}_{d}(G)=\mathcal{M}(M_{d,G})
.

Let us say that a vector
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
is a
generic
d
-stress of
G
if
\omega
is
M_{d,G}^{*}
-generic. By specializing
Lemma

3.1
to the measurement variety, we obtain the following basic lemma, which we shall use without reference in the rest of the paper.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
be a generic
d
-stress of the graph
G
. Then
\omega\in S(G,p)
for some generic framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Conversely, every generic framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
has a generic stress
\omega
.

As a weaker notion, we say that the vector
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
is an
almost generic
d
-stress of
G
if
\omega\in S(G,p)
for some generic framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. When the dimension
d
is clear from the context, we shall sometimes wimply write generic stress and almost generic stress, respectively. The description of the stress variety given by
Equation

4
shows that every almost generic stress is contained in
M_{d,G}^{*}
. Note that if
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent, then
M_{d,G}^{*}=\{0\}
and in this case the only almost generic stress is the zero vector.

Next, we consider the
contact locus

L_{d,G}(\omega)=\overline{\{x\in X:x\text{ is smooth and }\omega\in T_{x}(M_{d,G})^{\perp}\}}.

By the general results of
Section

, whenever
\omega
is a generic stress,
L_{d,G}(\omega)
is a linear space, and thus it is irreducible. The following proposition describes how the contact locus of
\omega
relates to the stress kernel of
\omega
.

Lemma 3.13
.

If
\omega
is a generic
d
-stress of the graph
G
, then
L_{d,G}(\omega)=\overline{m_{d,G}(K_{{d},{G}}(\omega))}
.

Proof.

Let
(G,p)
be a generic framework in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\omega\in S(G,p)
. By
Lemma

2.5

x=m_{d,G}(p)
is an
M_{d,G}
-generic point, and consequently it is a smooth point by
Lemma

2.6
(a). It follows from
Lemma

2.15
that
x\in L_{d,G}(\omega)\subseteq M_{d,G}
. In particular,
L_{d,G}(\omega)
contains an
M_{d,G}
-generic point. Now
Lemma

2.4
implies that the
M_{d,G}
-generic points are Zariski dense in
L_{d,G}(\omega)
, and hence we can write

L_{d,G}(\omega)=\overline{\{m_{d,G}(p):(G,p)\text{ is generic and }p\in K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)\}}.

Let us denote the set of generic configurations in
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)
by
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)^{g}
. Since
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)
is a linear space that contains at least one generic configuration (namely,

),
Lemma

2.4
implies that
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)=\overline{K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)^{g}}
. It follows that

L_{d,G}(\omega)=\overline{m_{d,G}(K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)^{g})}=\overline{m_{d,G}(\overline{K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)^{g}})}=\overline{m_{d,G}(K_{{d},{G}}(\omega))},

where the second equality uses the continuity of
m_{d,G}
.
∎

For convenience, we state the specialization of
Theorem

3.2
to the measurement variety as a separate theorem.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
be a generic
d
-stress of the graph
G
. Then
\mathcal{M}(L_{d,G}(\omega))=\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)^{*}
.

The following result reveals the connection between the affine edge measurement matroid and the generic stress matroid. The second statement is essentially a restatement of
Theorem

2.20
based on the stress matroid.

Theorem 3.15
.

We have
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)\preceq\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)^{*}
for any graph
G
. If
G
is
d
-rigid, then
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)=\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)^{*}
if and only if

is globally
d
-rigid.

Proof.

Let
\omega
be a generic
d
-stress of
G
and
(G,p)
a generic framework in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\omega\in S(G,p)
. Since

, we have (using
Lemma

3.13
)

B(G,p)=m_{d,G}(A(G,p))\subseteq\overline{m_{d,G}(K_{{d},{G}}(\omega))}=L_{d,G}(\omega).

Combined with
Theorem

3.14

\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)=\mathcal{M}(B(G,p))\preceq\mathcal{M}(L_{d,G}(\omega))=\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)^{*},

as claimed.

Now suppose that

is
d
-rigid. If

d
-rigid, then we have

by

2.20
and hence

, so
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)=\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)^{*}
. Suppose now that

-rigid. By

. (Note that since

, and hence the rank of

is larger than the rank of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(G)
.

To this end, let

and consider the set

of configurations

is equivalent to

. This is the fiber of
m_{d,G}:\mathbb{C}^{nd}\rightarrow M_{d,G}
over

(b) and the assumption that

\dim(R_{p})=\dim(\mathbb{C}^{nd})-\dim(M_{d,G})=nd-nd+\binom{d+1}{2}=\binom{d+1}{2}.

Now we make the same computation for the restriction

, and hence the fiber of this restricted map over

is still

∎

over

is finite whenever

spans at most

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
n
vertices and let
(G,p)
be a generic realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with a generic stress
\omega
. Let
(G,q)
be another realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with a stress
\omega^{\prime}\neq\omega
. If the subset of edges
E_{0}=\{e\in E:\omega(e)\neq\omega^{\prime}(e)\}
spans at most
d+1
vertices, then the subframework
(G[E_{0}],q_{0})
is not affinely independent. In particular,
\omega^{\prime}
is not an almost generic
d
-stress of
G
.

Proof.

Let us suppose, for a contradiction, that
(G[E_{0}],q_{0})
is affinely independent and let us put
V_{0}=V(G[E_{0}])
. By applying an affine transformation to
(G,q)
we may suppose that
q_{0}=p|_{V_{0}}
. By
Corollary

3.16
,
E-E_{0}
is spanning in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
, which in particular means that there are only a finite number of almost generic
d
-stresses that agree with
\omega
on
E-E_{0}
(see
Lemma

2.7
). We shall contradict this by explicitly finding an infinite number of such stresses.

To this end, let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
be scalars with
\alpha+\beta\neq 0
and let us consider the framework
(G,\alpha p+\beta q)
. We claim that the vector
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
defined by

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(uv)=\frac{\alpha\omega(uv)+\beta\omega^{\prime}(uv)}{\alpha+\beta}

is a stress of
(G,\alpha p+\beta q)
. It suffices to check the equilibrium condition at each vertex
v\in V
. Note that
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
agrees with
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
on
E-E_{0}
. Consequently if
v\notin V_{0}
, then
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
and
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
agree on each edge incident to
v
and the equilibrium condition clearly holds. Thus let us suppose that
v\in V_{0}
and let us consider partition the neighbours of
v
into the sets
A=\{u\in V:uv\in E-E_{0}\}
and
B=\{u\in V:uv\in E_{0}\}
. Using the equilibrium condition on
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
and the observation that if
u\in A,
then
\omega(uv)=\omega^{\prime}(uv)=\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(uv)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in A}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(uv)

\displaystyle\left((\alpha p+\beta q)(u)-(\alpha p+\beta q)(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{u\in A}\omega(uv)(p(u)-p(v))+\beta\sum_{u\in A}\omega^{\prime}(uv)(q(u)-q(v))

\displaystyle=-\alpha\sum_{u\in B}\omega(uv)(p(u)-p(v))-\beta\sum_{u\in B}\omega^{\prime}(uv)(q(u)-q(v))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{u\in B}\left(\alpha\omega(uv)+\beta\omega^{\prime}(uv)\right)(p(u)-p(v))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{u\in B}\frac{\alpha\omega(uv)+\beta\omega^{\prime}(uv)}{\alpha+\beta}(\alpha+\beta)(p(u)-p(v))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{u\in B}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(uv)\left((\alpha p+\beta q)(u)-(\alpha p+\beta q)(v)\right),

where the third and the last equalities used our assumption that
q(u)=p(u)
for
u\in V_{0}
. This shows that the equilibrium condition is satisfied at
v
, and hence
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
is indeed a stress of
(G,\alpha p+\beta q)
. Since
\{\alpha p+\beta q:\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{nd}
is a linear space containing the generic configuration
p
, the generic configurations are dense in it by
Lemma

2.4
. It follows by a simple computation (looking at the values of
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(e)
for some fixed
e\in E_{0}
) that among
\omega_{\alpha,\beta},\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
there is an infinite number of pairwise different almost generic
d
-stresses. But as we noted before, this contradicts the fact that
E-E_{0}
is spanning in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
.
∎

Corollary 3.18
.

Let
G
be a graph,
\omega
a generic
d
-stress of
G
and
\omega^{\prime}
an almost generic
d
-stress of
G
. If the subset of edges
E_{0}=\{e\in E:\omega(e)\neq\omega^{\prime}(e)\}
spans at most
d+1
vertices, then
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
.

So far we have considered the spanning sets of
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
. Now we turn our attention to the independent sets.
For the next proof, we recall the definition of a projective transformation and we introduce some terminology.

Let
(G,p)
be a realization of the graph
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and let
\tilde{p}
denote the configuration in
\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
obtained by augmenting each vector
p(v),v\in V(G)
with the number
1
in the last coordinate. Let us say that a nonsingular matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
is
feasible
(for
(G,p)
) if
\lambda_{v}=(A\tilde{p}(v))_{d+1}\neq 0
for every
v\in V(G)
. Given a feasible matrix
A
, let us consider the configuration
q
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
obtained by deleting the last coordinate of
A\tilde{p}(v)/\lambda_{v}
, for each
v\in V(G)
. We call the configuration
q
obtained in this way the
projective image of
p
under
A
. The key fact, first observed in
[
30
]
, is that if
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
is a stress of
(G,p)
, then the vector
\omega^{\prime}
defined by
\omega^{\prime}(uv)=\lambda_{u}\lambda_{v}\omega(uv)
(where
\lambda_{v},v\in V(G)
are defined as above) is a stress of
(G,q)
. For convenience, we will say that the pair
(q,\omega^{\prime})
is the
projective image of the pair
(p,\omega)
under
A
. Let
P(G,p,\omega)
denote the set of projective images of
(p,\omega)
. We shall need the following technical lemma, whose proof we leave to the Appendix.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices,
(G,p)
a generic realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\omega
a generic
d
-stress with
\omega\in S(G,p)
. Consider the set
P(G,p,\omega)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{nd}\times\mathbb{C}^{E}
defined as above and let
\pi_{2}:\mathbb{C}^{nd}\times\mathbb{C}^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{E}
denote the projection onto the second factor. Then the almost generic
d
-stresses are Zariski dense in
\pi_{2}(P(G,p,\omega))
, and consequently
\pi_{2}(P(G,p,\omega))\subseteq M_{d,G}^{*}
.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
E_{0}\subseteq E
be a set of edges. Suppose that either

(a)

E_{0}
is the edge set of a forest in
G
on at most
d+1
vertices, or

(b)

d=2
and
E_{0}
consists of two disjoint edges.

If none of the edges in
E_{0}
are
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridges in
G
, then
E_{0}
is independent in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
\omega
be a generic
d
-stress of
G
and let
(G,p)
be a generic realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\omega\in S(G,p)
. Note that
\omega(uv)\neq 0
for every
uv\in E_{0}
. Indeed, since
\omega
is
M_{d,G}^{*}
-generic, if it satisfied the equation
x_{uv}=0
, then every element of
M_{d,G}^{*}
would satisfy it. But since
uv
is not an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge of
G
, we know that there exist almost generic stresses of
G
that are nonzero on
uv
.

Let
P(G,p,\omega)
be defined as above. By
Lemma

3.19
, the projection of
P(G,p,\omega)
onto
\mathbb{C}^{E}
is contained in
M_{d,G}^{*}
. Hence to show that
E_{0}
is independent in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)=\mathcal{M}(M_{d,G}^{*})
, it is enough to show that the projection of
P(G,p,\omega)
onto
\mathbb{C}^{E_{0}}
is full-dimensional. In other words, it suffices to show that for almost all collection of values
\sigma_{e}\in\mathbb{C},e\in E_{0}
there is a projective image
(q,\omega^{\prime})
of
(p,\omega)
such that
\omega^{\prime}(e)=\sigma_{e}
for all
e\in E_{0}
.

Recall the notation for projective transformations introduced in the paragraph before
Lemma

3.19
. Let
V_{0}=V(E_{0})
and let us first consider case
(a)
. In this case
|V_{0}|\leq d+1
and hence the “lifted” points
\tilde{p}(v),v\in V_{0}
are linearly independent (otherwise the points
p(v),v\in V_{0}
would not be in affine general position). This means that we can freely map these points to any collection of
d+1
points in
\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
using a linear transformation, and in particular we can freely choose the values
\lambda_{v},v\in V_{0}
(provided that they are nonzero).
Thus all we need to do is to solve the system of equations

\lambda_{u}\lambda_{v}\omega(uv)=\sigma_{uv},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall uv\in E_{0}

(5)

in the variables
\lambda_{v},v\in V_{0}
. Since
E_{0}
is the edge set of a forest, we can do this whenever
\sigma_{uv}\neq 0
for every
uv\in E_{0}
by taking a leaf vertex
v
of this forest with incident edge
uv\in E_{0}
, solving the equations for
E_{0}-uv
recursively and then choosing the value of
\lambda_{v}
suitably.

A similar approach works in case
(b)
, but we have to be a bit more careful. Let
E_{0}=\{v_{1}v_{2},v_{3}v_{4}\}
. The vectors
\tilde{p}(v_{1}),\tilde{p}(v_{2}),\tilde{p}(v_{3})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
are linearly independent and we have
\tilde{p}(v_{4})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}\tilde{p}(v_{i})
for some nonzero scalars
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
. As before, we have to solve
Equation

5
; now we can freely choose the values
\lambda_{v_{1}},\lambda_{v_{2}},\lambda_{v_{3}}
, and by linearity we have
\lambda_{v_{4}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\alpha_{i}\lambda_{v_{i}}
. If we now set
\lambda_{v_{1}}=1,\lambda_{v_{2}}=\sigma_{v_{1}v_{2}}\omega(v_{1}v_{2})^{-1}
and
\lambda_{v_{3}}=c\in\mathbb{C}
, then the value
\lambda_{v_{3}}\lambda_{v_{4}}\omega(v_{3}v_{4})
is a quadratic function of
c
, and hence it is surjective on
\mathbb{C}
. It follows that by suitably choosing the value of
c
we can solve the system of equations
Equation

5
for any choice of
\sigma_{v_{1}v_{2}},\sigma_{v_{3}v_{4}}
, as desired.
∎

The proof technique of
Theorem

3.20
seems quite weak in that it only uses projective images of
(p,\omega)
, and these form a small-dimensional subset of all pairs
(q,\omega^{\prime})
with
\omega^{\prime}\in S(G,q)
. The problem is that even though we know that there are many almost generic stresses
\omega^{\prime}
, it is not clear how to control precisely the values of
\omega^{\prime}
on a given set of edges. It seems likely that the proof method of
Theorem

3.20
can be taken a bit further to show that if
E_{0}
forms a forest on
d+2
vertices, or a pseudoforest on
d+1
vertices, then it is independent in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
. However, we will not pursue this direction. In fact, the only result we will need is the following corollary.

Corollary 3.21
.

The stress matroid
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
does not contain circuits of size two for any graph
G=(V,E)
.

Proof.

If
d=1
, then the statement follows from
Proposition

3.23
below, so let us assume that
d\geq 2
and let
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}\subseteq E
. If
e_{i}
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge in
G
, then by
Corollary

3.5
it is a loop in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
and hence
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is not a circuit. Otherwise
Theorem

3.20
implies that
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is independent in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)
and hence, again, it is not a circuit.
∎

We close this section by describing the one-dimensional generic stress matroid. We will need the specialization of
Proposition

3.4
to the measurement variety.

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
G_{i},i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
denote the
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-components of
G
. Then
\mathcal{S}_{d}(G)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{d}(G_{i})
, and moreover
M_{d,G}^{*}=M_{d,G_{1}}^{*}\times\ldots\times M_{d,G_{k}}^{*}
.

The
\mathcal{R}_{1}
-components of a graph are just its
2
-connected components, which are globally
1
-rigid. Using this observation and the preceding results on the stress matroid we can determine the one-dimensional generic stress matroid of any graph
G
.

Proposition 3.23
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
be the edge sets of its
2
-connected components. Let
m_{i}=|E_{i}|
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.
Then
\mathcal{S}_{1}(G)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}U_{m_{i}-1}(E_{i})
, where
U_{k}(X)
denotes the rank
k
uniform matroid on ground set
X
.

Proof.

The
\mathcal{R}_{1}
-components of a graph are just its
2
-connected components. By
Proposition

3.22
we may assume that
G
is
2
-connected (i.e., globally
1
-rigid). From
Theorem

3.15
we have that
\mathcal{S}_{1}(G)
is the dual of
\mathcal{A}_{1}(G)
. By
Corollary

3.10
,
\mathcal{A}_{1}(G)=U_{1}(E)
, so
\mathcal{S}_{1}(G)=U_{|E|-1}(E)
, as required.
∎

4
Stress-linked vertex pairs

In this section we introduce and investigate
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs, one of the central new ideas in this paper. While the definition of being
{d}
-stress-linked does not involve the generic stress matroid, we will make use of the structural results of the previous section in order to prove our main results about this notion.
Before we start, we recall another pair of definitions from
[
12
]
: that of the shared stress kernel and the shared stress nullity.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices. For a framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
let

denote the set of configurations

for which

, or in other words, for which the framework
(G,q)
satisfies every stress of
(G,p)

[

(

of
(G,p)

to be the set of one-dimensional configurations

for which

; this is the

of
(G,p)
. Both
K(G,p)
and

are linear spaces, and we have
K(G,p)=(K_{1}(G,p))^{d}
as linear spaces. We also have the description

K(G,p)=\bigcap_{\omega\in S(G,p)}K_{{d},{G}}(\omega),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptK_{1}(G,p)=\bigcap_{\omega\in S(G,p)}K_{{1},{G}}(\omega).

Since
K_{1}(G,p)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we trivially have
\dim(K_{1}(G,p))\leq n
. On the other hand, the

(G,p)

, as well as the all ones vector. If
(G,p)
has full affine span, then these

\dim K_{1}(G,p)\geq d+1
.

By general principles the dimension of

is the same for every generic framework
(G,p)
in a given dimension, see
[
12
, Remark 4.2]
.

for generic

is the same in both settings.

[

.

(

If

If

We now arrive to one of the main new notions of this paper.
Let
G
be a graph and

a pair of vertices. We say that

{d}
-stress-linked in

is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in

and

. Note that if

, then

trivially satisfies these conditions. The following proposition rephrases the definition of being
{d}
-stress-linked in a number of convenient forms.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
G
be a graph and
u,v\in V(G)
a nonadjacent pair of vertices such that
\{u,v\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G
. Let
(G,p)
be a generic realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and let
\omega\in S(G+uv,p)
be a stress that is nonzero on
uv
. The following are equivalent.

(a)

\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
;

(b)

K(G+uv,p)=K(G,p)
;

(c)

\omega
is a stress of
(G+uv,q)
for every configuration
q\in K(G,p)
;

(d)

\omega
is a stress of
(G+uv,q)
for every generic configuration
q\in K(G,p)
.

Proof.

Since the pair
\{u,v\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked, it is
{d}
-stress-linked if and only if
k_{\text{sh}}(G+uv,d)=k_{\text{sh}}(G,d)
. This is equivalent to
\dim(K_{1}(G+uv,p))=\dim(K_{1}(G,p))
. We clearly have
K_{1}(G+uv,p)\subseteq K_{1}(G,p)
, and hence we must also have
K_{1}(G+uv,p)=K_{1}(G,p)
. Using the observation that
K(G,p)=(K_{1}(G,p))^{d}
(and similarly for
(G+uv,p)
), this is further equivalent to condition
(b)
. On the other hand, a dimension count shows that
S(G+uv,p)
is generated by
S(G,p)\cup\{\omega\}
, and hence
K(G+uv,p)=K(G,p)\cap K_{{d},{G+uv}}(\omega)
. In other words,
K(G+uv,p)=K(G,p)
holds if and only if
K(G,p)\subseteq K_{{d},{G+uv}}(\omega)
, which is the content of
(c)
. This shows the equivalence of the first three conditions. Condition
(c)
clearly implies
(d)
. For the reverse direction, note that since
(G,p)
is generic, the linear space
K(G,p)
contains a generic configuration and hence by
Lemma

2.4
the generic configurations are Zariski dense in it. The condition that
\omega
is a stress of
(G+uv,q)
is described by polynomial equations, so if it holds for a Zariski dense subset of
K(G,p)
, then it holds for all of it.
∎

We can also give a characterization of
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs in terms of Gauss fibers. Given a generic framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, its

is the set
L(G,p)=\overline{m_{d,G}(K(G,p)})
. It can be shown that the Gauss fiber of
(G,p)
is the intersection of the contact loci
L_{d,G}(\omega)
for
\omega\in S(G,p)
. In particular, it is a linear space. The following statement can be shown by a dimension counting argument similar to the one we saw in the proof of
Theorem

. Since we shall not use this result, we leave its proof to the Appendix.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let

be a graph on
n
vertices and
u,v\in V(G)
a pair of vertices. Let
(G,p)
be a generic framework in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The vertex pair

is
{d}
-stress-linked if and only if
\dim(L(G+uv,p))=\dim(L(G,p))
, where

is the Gauss fiber of
(G,p)
.

4.1
Basic properties of stress-linked pairs

In this subsection we establish some basic results about

\mathbb{C}^{d}

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
G
be a graph on

(a)

We have

if and only if

is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent.

(b)

-bridges and isolated vertices.

(a)
We have

\mathcal{R}_{d}

(b)
For

Proof of
Theorem

4.15
.

Let
S=V_{1}\cap V_{2}
,
G^{\prime}=G+E(K_{S})
and
G_{i}^{\prime}=G_{i}+E(K_{S})
for

. We may suppose that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are induced subgraphs, so that

(G,p)
be a generic realization of

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We start by describing the space of stresses of
(G,p)
. By a slight abuse of notation we shall let
S(G_{i},p_{i})
denote the subspace of
S(G,p)

S(G_{i}^{\prime},p_{i})

Consider

\omega_{i,e}

(G,p)

\omega_{i,e}(e)=1

\{u,v\}

\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{i}

\mathcal{B}_{i}\cup\{\omega_{i,e}:e\in E_{0}\}
is a basis of

, and since we have
S(G^{\prime},p)=S(G_{1}^{\prime},p_{1})\oplus S(G_{2}^{\prime},p_{2})

2.17
(b),

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}\cup\{\omega_{i,e}:i\in\{1,2\},e\in E_{0}\}

\mathcal{B}\cup\{\omega_{1,e}:e\in E_{0}\}

\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G
. Our goal is to show that we also have

. Note that since
(G,q)
is generic, we have
\dim(S(G,p))=\dim(S(G,q))

S(G,p)=S(G,q)
.
Again, for

S(G_{i},q_{i})

for
(G^{\prime},q)

\omega_{i,e}
for
(G,q)
for each

and

, and let us also define the basis
\mathcal{B}^{q}
of

by

\mathcal{B}^{q}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}\cup\{\omega_{1,e}^{q}-\omega_{2,e}^{q}:e\in E_{0}\}.

Our aim is to show that

. By

2.17
(b) we have
S(G^{\prime},q)=S(G_{1}^{\prime},q_{1})\oplus S(G_{2}^{\prime},q_{2})

G_{1}^{\prime}

\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}

\omega_{1}^{\prime}

\omega_{1}+\sum_{e\in E_{0}}\alpha_{e}\omega_{1,e}
for some
\omega_{1}\in S(G_{1},p_{1})

\alpha_{e}\in\mathbb{C},e\in E_{0}

Let us consider the stress

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
G
be the union of the graphs
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1}),G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
with
|V_{1}\cap V_{2}|\leq d+1
and suppose that for every pair of vertices
u,v\in V_{1}\cap V_{2}
,
\{u,v\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked both in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Then for every pair of vertices
u,v\in V_{1}\cap V_{2}
,
\{u,v\}
is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
(and hence in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

We close this subsection by considering the converse question: supposing that for some vertices
u,v\in V(G_{1})
the pair
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
, when can we deduce that it is also
{d}
-stress-linked in
G_{1}
?
Lemma

4.13
shows that this is the case when
G_{1}\cap G_{2}
is a complete graph. Now we consider the case when
V_{1}\cap V_{2}
has size two, and show that if the vertex pair in the intersection is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in at least one of
G_{1}
and

, then
\{u,v\}
is also
{d}
-stress-linked in
G_{1}
.

It is interesting to consider the analogous question for globally linked pairs. If
V_{1}\cap V_{2}=\{x,y\}
and
\{x,y\}
is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{2}
, then it is not difficult to see that
\{u,v\}
is globally linked in
G_{1}
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
if and only if it is globally linked in
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. If we replace
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by
\mathbb{R}^{d}
then it is unclear whether the statement remains true; we posed a special case as a conjecture in
[
10
, Conjecture 5.7]
. The case when
\{x,y\}
is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{1}
seems unclear even in the complex case.

Theorem 4.18
.

Let
G
the union of the induced subgraphs
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
with
V_{1}\cap V_{2}=\{x,y\}
, and let
u,v\in V_{1}
be a pair of vertices. Suppose that at least one of the following holds:

(a)

\{x,y\}
is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{2}
, or

(b)

\{u,v\}\neq\{x,y\}
and
\{x,y\}
is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{1}
.

Then the pair
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
if and only if
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G_{1}
.

Proof.

We start by noting that under our assumption there is no
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit in
G
that intersects both
G_{1}-G_{2}
and
G_{2}-G_{1}
. To the contrary, if there is such an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit
C
, then by
Lemma

2.13
we have
\{x,y\}\subseteq V(C)
. But then
Lemma

2.14
implies that
\{x,y\}
is contained in an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit of
G_{i}+xy
, so the pair is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{i}
, for both
i\in\{1,2\}
, contradicting our assumption.

Now let us prove the statement. If
u
and
v
are adjacent in
G
, then the statement is trivial, so let us assume that
u
and
v
are nonadjacent. If
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G_{1}
, then it is also
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
by
Lemma

4.12
. Let us thus assume that
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
. In particular,
\{u,v\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G
, so there is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit
C
in
G+uv
that contains
uv
. By the discussion in the first paragraph we must have
V(C)\subseteq V(G_{1})
. (Otherwise
V(C)\subseteq V(G_{2})
, which would mean that
\{u,v\}=\{x,y\}
and
\{x,y\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{2}
, contradicting our assumption.) It follows that
\{u,v\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-linked in
G_{1}
.

Let
(G,p)
be a generic realization in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and let
(G_{i},p_{i})
be the subframework corresponding to
G_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
. By
Lemma

2.17
(c) we have
S(G,p)=S(G_{1},p_{1})\oplus S(G_{2},p_{2})
, where by a slight abuse of notation we use
S(G_{i},p_{i})
to denote the stresses of
(G,p)
supported on
E(G_{i})
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Let
\omega
be a stress of
(G_{1}+uv,p_{1})
that is supported on the edges of
C
. By
Proposition

4.3
(d) it suffices to show that
\omega
is also a stress of
(G_{1}+uv,q_{1})
for every generic configuration
q_{1}\in K(G_{1},p_{1})
.
Since
(G_{1},q_{1})
is generic, we have
S(G_{1},p_{1})=S(G_{1},q_{1})
. Let
(G,q)
be the realization obtained from
(G_{1},q_{1})
by gluing a suitably scaled and rotated copy of
(G_{2},p_{2})
along
\{u,v\}
. Then by
Lemma

2.17
(c) again we have

S(G,q)=S(G_{1},q_{1})\oplus S(G_{2},p_{2})=S(G_{1},p_{1})\oplus S(G_{2},p_{2})=S(G,p),

and hence
q\in K(G,p)
. Since
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
, it follows that
\omega
is a stress of
(G+uv,q)
. But since
\omega
is supported on the edges of
C
and

, this implies that
\omega
is also a stress of
(G_{1}+uv,q_{1})
, as desired.
∎

4.3
Stress-linked pairs in low dimensions

In this subsection we give a combinatorial characterization of
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs in the
d=1
and
d=2
cases. Both characterizations involve the notion of
local connectivity
.
Given a graph
G
and a pair of nonadjacent vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, let
\kappa_{G}(u,v)
denote the maximum number of pairwise internally disjoint
u,v
-paths in
G
. By Menger’s theorem this is the same as the minimum size of a vertex set
S
such that
u
and
v
are disconnected in
G-S
.

The following (easy) theorem characterizes
{1}
-stress-linked pairs.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
G
be a graph and
u,v\in V(G)
a nonadjacent pair of vertices. The following are equivalent.

(a)

\{u,v\}
is contained in a globally
1
-rigid subgraph of
G
.

(b)

\{u,v\}
is
{1}
-stress-linked in
G
.

(c)

\{u,v\}
is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{C}^{1}
.

(d)

\{u,v\}
is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
.

(e)

\kappa_{G}(u,v)\geq 2
.

Proof.

The first implication follows from
Propositions

4.6
and
4.12
, and the second from
Theorem

4.5
. The third and four implications are immediate. Finally, if
\kappa_{G}(u,v)\geq 2
, then there are two internally disjoint
u,v
-paths
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
in
G
. Now
P_{1}\cup P_{2}
is a
2
-connected (and hence globally
1
-rigid) subgraph that contains
u
and
v
.
∎

Theorem

4.20
below characterizes
{2}
-stress-linked vertex pairs. As we noted in the Introduction, Jackson, Jordán and Szabadka conjectured in
[
21
, Conjecture 5.9]
that the same condition characterizes globally linked pairs in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and in
[
23
, Conjecture 5.4]
, Jackson and Owen conjectured the same for globally linked pairs in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Theorem 4.20
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
u,v\in V
be a pair of vertices. The pair
\{u,v\}
is
{2}
-stress-linked if and only if either
uv\in E
or there is some
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected subgraph
H
of
G
with
u,v\in V(H)
and
\kappa_{H}(u,v)\geq 3
.

Proof.

We first prove sufficiency by induction on
|V|
. Our proof is essentially the same as that of
[
21
, Theorem 5.7]
(which is the analogous statement for globally linked pairs in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
). The
|V|=2
case is vacuous and the case when
uv\in E
is trivial, so let us suppose that
|V|\geq 3
and
u
and
v
are nonadjacent in
G
. We need to show that
\{u,v\}
is
{2}
-stress-linked in
G
. By
Lemma

4.12
it suffices to show that
\{u,v\}
is
{2}
-stress-linked in
H
, so we may suppose that
H=G
. If
G
is globally
2
-rigid, then we are done by
Proposition

4.6
. Hence let us assume that this is not the case; since
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected,
Theorem

2.19
(b) now implies that
G
is not
3
-connected. On the other hand, it can be deduced from
Lemma

2.13
that
G
is
2
-connected. It follows that there is a separating vertex pair
\{x,y\}
in
G
. We now have two cases: either
\{x,y\}=\{u,v\}
, or
\{x,y\}\neq\{u,v\}
.

Let us first consider the case when
\{x,y\}=\{u,v\}
. Let
uw_{1}
and
vw_{2}
be edges of
G
such that
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are in different components of
G-\{u,v\}
. Since
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected, there is an
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-circuit
C
in
G
that contains both
uw_{1}
and
vw_{2}
. In particular,
\{u,v\}
is a separating vertex pair of
C
. Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be obtained by a
2
-separation of
C
along
\{u,v\}
.
Lemma

2.14
implies that
C_{i}
is an
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-circuit, and thus
\{u,v\}
is
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-linked in
C_{i}-uv
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
. It follows from
Theorem

4.15
that
\{u,v\}
is
{2}
-stress-linked in
C
, and consequently in
G
as well.

Now let us consider the case when
\{x,y\}\neq\{u,v\}
. By the previous reasoning we still get that
\{x,y\}
is
{2}
-stress-linked in
G
, so we may assume that
xy\in E
. Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be obtained by a
2
-separation of
G-xy
along
\{x,y\}
with
\{u,v\}\subseteq V(G_{1})
.
Lemma

2.14
implies that
G_{1}
is also
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected, and since
xy\in E(G_{1})
we also have
\kappa_{G_{1}}(u,v)\geq 3
. Thus by induction
\{u,v\}
is
{2}
-stress-linked in
G_{1}
, and hence by
Lemma

4.12
it is also
{2}
-stress-linked in
G
.

Next, we prove necessity by induction on
|E|
. The case when
|E|=1
or
uv\in E
is immediate, and by
Theorem

2.19
so is the case when
G
is globally
2
-rigid. Note that since
\{u,v\}
is
{2}
-stress-linked, it is also globally linked in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
Theorem

4.5
, which implies that
\kappa_{G}(u,v)\geq 3
. If there is an
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-bridge
e
in
G
, then
\{u,v\}
is also
{2}
-stress-linked in
G-e
by
Theorem

4.8
, and thus we are done by induction. Hence we may assume that
G
is not globally
2
-rigid and that is does not contain
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-bridges. From
Theorem

2.19
(c) it follows that
G
is not
3
-connected. If
G

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper we introduced (among others) the notion of
{d}
-stress-linked vertex pairs. We showed that
{d}
-stress-linked pairs are also globally linked in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and we proved the “
{d}
-stress-linked” analogue of a number of conjectures about globally linked pairs. The main open question that remains is whether being
{d}
-stress-linked is actually equivalent to being globally linked in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or
\mathbb{C}^{d}
). We pose the stronger version as a conjecture.

Conjecture 6.1
.

Let
G
be a graph. A pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
.

We note that checking whether a pair of vertices is
{d}
-stress-linked can be done in randomized polynomial time with an approach similar to the one given in
[
12
, Section 5]
for checking global
d
-rigidity. In the
d=1
and
d=2
cases
Theorems

4.19
and
4.20
also lead to efficient deterministic recognition algorithms.
6.1
would imply the analogous algorithmic results for globally linked vertex pairs.

One natural approach to
6.1
is to try to adapt the degree theory-based proof of
Theorem

2.20
in
[
12
]
. The fact that being globally linked in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is not a generic property is a clear sign of difficulty for this approach. The following conjecture may remedy this problem to some extent. It states that if
\{u,v\}
is not globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
K(G,p)
contains a “witness” of this, for every generic realization
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Conjecture 6.2
.

Let
G
be a graph and
u,v\in V(G)
a pair of vertices, and suppose that
\{u,v\}
is not globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for any generic realization
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exist real configurations
q,q^{\prime}\in K(G,p)
such that
(G,q)
is generic and
(G,q)
and
(G,q^{\prime})
are equivalent, but
(G+uv,q)
and
(G+uv,q^{\prime})
are not equivalent.

Even if
6.1
turns out to be false, the notion of
{d}
-stress-linked pairs seems to be a useful tool in studying rigidity and global rigidity. In particular,
Corollary

4.9
was a key ingredient in some of our applications. The following conjecture generalizes the statement of
Corollary

4.9
from
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridges to
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-components.

Conjecture 6.3
.

Let
G
be a graph and
u,v\in V(G)
a pair of vertices. If
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
, then there is some
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-component
G_{0}
of
G
such that
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G_{0}
.

In particular,
6.3
implies that if
\{u,v\}
is
{d}
-stress-linked in
G
, then the
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-components of
G
and

are essentially the same: the addition of
uv
does not “merge” any two of these components. Similarly to how
Corollary

4.9
can be seen as a strengthening of Hendrickson’s theorem,
6.3
would strengthen
Theorem

5.1
. Note that
6.3
holds in the
d=1
and
d=2
cases by
Theorems

4.19
and
4.20
.

In closing, we would like to briefly consider the applications of the ideas presented in this paper in a wider context. Rigidity theory is mainly concerned with the study of the homogeneous polynomial map
m_{d,G}:\mathbb{C}^{nd}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{E}
. A key feature of this map is that it is quadratic, and hence the entries of its Jacobian matrix are linear polynomials. This implies that for a stress
\omega
,
the stress kernel
K_{{d},{G}}(\omega)
is linear, and in particular it is an irreducible affine variety. Similarly, the shared stress kernel
K(G,p)
of any configuration
p\in\mathbb{C}^{nd}
is linear and hence irreducible.

There are a number of other problems which can be phrased as questions about an algebraic matroid given by the image of some homogeneous polynomial map
f
. Often
f
maps some configuration space into
\mathbb{R}^{E}
or
\mathbb{C}^{E}
, where
E
is the edge set of a (hyper-)graph, see
[
6
,
29
]
. For each such problem the analogue of the shared stress kernel can be defined, and whenever this space is irreducible, at least for generic points of the configuration space, we can also define an analogue of stress-linked vertex pairs (or more generally stress-linked hyperedges). This is the case, for example, in low-rank matrix completion problems, where the associated polynomial map is given by the standard dot product and hence is quadratic, see
[
27
,
34
]
. Applying our methods (in particular, the analogues of
Theorems

4.5
and
5.2
) to this problem leads to a linear upper bound on the number of known entries in a minimally generically uniquely completable partial matrix over
\mathbb{R}
. Note that, in contrast with graph rigidity, in the case of matrix completion “being uniquely completable over
\mathbb{R}
” is not a generic property (and thus is not the same as “being uniquely completable over
\mathbb{C}
”), see
[
22
]
. It would be interesting to understand the structure of the associated “generic stress matroid” in this case.

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
of feasible matrices is a Zariski open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
. It is clear by the definition of a projective image that
P(G,p,\omega)
is the image of this open subset under a regular map, i.e., it can be written as
\Phi(\mathcal{F}),
where
\Phi(x)=(f_{1}(x)/g_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{k}(x)/g_{k}(x))
with
f_{i},g_{i}
polynomials such that
g_{i}
is nonvanishing over
\mathcal{F}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. It is not difficult to see that
\Phi(\mathcal{F})
can be defined by a formula in the language of fields and hence Chevalley’s theorem implies that
P(G,p,\omega)
is a constructible set. Irreducibility follows from topological considerations and the well-known fact that regular maps are continuous with respect to the Zariski topology.

Let
\pi_{1}
denote the projection of
P(G,p,\omega)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{nd}\times\mathbb{C}^{E}
onto the first factor. Then by Chevalley’s theorem
\pi_{1}(G,p,\omega)
is a constructible set, and it contains the generic configuration
p
. It follows from
Lemma

2.4
that the generic configurations are Zariski dense in
\overline{\pi_{1}(P(G,p,\omega))}
, and since a constructible set contains a dense open set of its closure, the generic configurations are also dense in
\pi_{1}(P(G,p,\omega))
. This imples that for a Zariski dense subset of points
(q,\omega^{\prime})
in
P(G,p,\omega)
,
q
is generic, and hence
\omega^{\prime}
is almost generic. This shows that the almost generic
d
-stresses are Zariski dense in
\pi_{2}(P(G,p,\omega))
, and thus they are contained in
M_{d,G}^{*}
. By taking closures we deduce that
\pi_{2}(P(G,p,\omega))
is also contained in
M_{d,G}^{*}
, as claimed.
∎
