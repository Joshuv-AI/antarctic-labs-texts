A posteriori error estimates via equilibrated stress reconstructions for contact problems approximated by Nitsche’s method
Daniele A. Di Pietro, Ilaria Fontana, Kyrylo Kazymyrenko

Abstract
We present an a posteriori error estimate based on equilibrated stress reconstructions for the finite element approximation of a unilateral contact problem with weak enforcement of the contact conditions. We start by proving a guaranteed upper bound for the dual norm of the residual. This norm is shown to control the natural energy norm up to a boundary term, which can be removed under a saturation assumption. The basic estimate is then refined to distinguish the different components of the error, and is used as a starting point to design an algorithm including adaptive stopping criteria for the nonlinear solver and automatic tuning of a regularization parameter. We then discuss an actual way of computing the stress reconstruction based on the Arnold–Falk–Winther finite elements. Finally, after briefly discussing the efficiency of our estimators, we showcase their performance on a panel of numerical tests.



Keywords:
unilateral contact problem, weakly enforced contact conditions, a posteriori error estimate, equilibrated stress reconstruction, Arnold–Falk–Winther mixed finite element, adaptivity

74M15, 74S05, 65N15, 65N30, 65N50

Introduction

In various fields of solid mechanics and engineering it is essential to describe contact and friction between two bodies.
This is the case, e.g., when modelling foundations and joints in buildings or when considering impact problems.
In this paper, we focus on a simplified unilateral contact problem without friction, for which contact is mathematically expressed by some inequalities and complementarity conditions.
These constraints translate non-penetration as well as the absence of cohesive forces and friction between the two bodies.
In order to deal with the above constraints from a numerical point of view, different strategies have been proposed in the literature, including penalized formulations, mixed formulations, and weak enforcement à la Nitsche.
We focus on the latter, which does not require the introduction of Lagrange multipliers and results in a coercive formulation that is easy to implement.
The literature on the numerical approximation of contact problems is vast, and a comprehensive state-of-the-art lies outside of the scope of the present papers.
We refer to the review articles
[
33
,
9
]
and references therein for a broader discussion.

Nitsche’s method was originally introduced in
[
27
]
to weakly enforce Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Its application to the unilateral contact problem considered in this paper was originally proposed in
[
11
]
, where the well-posedness and convergence of a conforming finite element scheme are studied.
Further extensions to problems involving friction or multiple bodies can be found in
[
12
,
7
,
29
,
23
]
;
we also mention
[
8
]
concerning the adaptation of these ideas to Hybrid High-Order discretizations
[
18
,
17
,
15
]
.
A residual-based a posteriori error analysis can be found in
[
10
]
based on a saturation assumption.

In this paper we follow a different path and carry out an a posteriori error analysis based on equilibrated tractions in the spirit of the Prager–Synge equality
[
28
]
(see also
[
21
]
and
[
32
, Chapter 7]
).
This approach, which does not require the saturation assumption when the dual norm of the residual is considered as an error measure, has also the advantage of avoiding unknown constants in the upper bound.
The corresponding error estimate can additionally be refined in order to distinguish the various components of the error (discretization, linearization, regularization).
This decomposition is leveraged here to design a fully adaptive resolution algorithm including an a posteriori-based stopping criterion for the nonlinear solver and the automatic tuning of the regularization parameter.

A crucial ingredient of our a posteriori analysis is a novel
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}(\textbf{div})
-conforming stress reconstruction obtained from the numerical solution by solving small problems on patches around mesh vertices.
In the spirit of
[
30
,
5
]
, we use the Arnold–Falk–Winther mixed finite element spaces with weakly enforced symmetry
[
1
]
; strong symmetry could be enforced using the Arnold–Winther finite element spaces
[
2
]
as in
[
30
]
, but would come at a significantly higher computational cost.
The stress reconstruction is built so that its divergence and its normal component on the contact and Neumann portions of the domain boundary are locally in equilibrium with the volume and surface source terms, respectively (such equilibrium properties are not satisfied by stress fields resulting from
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}
-conforming finite element approximations).
In order to distinguish the various error components, the stress reconstruction is additionally split so as to identify the contributions to the error resulting from linearization and regularization.

The main results of the paper are briefly summarized in what follows.
The basic error estimate of Theorem
4
establishes a guaranteed upper bound for the dual norm of the residual.
Such norm is shown in Theorem
7
to control the energy norm of the error up to a boundary term on the contact region (this term can be eliminated when a saturation assumption similar to the one used in
[
10
]
holds).
A refined error estimate distinguishing the error components is derived in Theorem
11
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we describe the unilateral contact problem and its finite element approximation à la Nitsche.
In Section
3
we derive a basic estimate for the dual norm of the residual and compare this norm with the energy norm.
Section
4
contains a refined version of the estimate distinguishing the error components which serves as a starting point for the development of a fully adaptive algorithm.
An equilibrated stress reconstruction based on the Arnold–Falk–Winther finite element is then proposed in Section
5
.
Section
6
briefly addresses the efficiency of the error estimators.
Finally, some numerical results performed with the open source software FreeFem++ are presented in Section
7
.

2
Setting

In this section we discuss the contact problem and its finite element discretization with weakly enforced contact conditions.

2.1
Unilateral contact problem

2.1.1
Strong formulation

Let
d\in\{2,3\}
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
representing an elastic body. We suppose that
\Omega
is a polygon (if
d=2
) or a polyhedron (if
d=3
), and that its boundary
\partial\Omega
is partitioned into three non-overlapping parts
\Gamma_{D}
,
\Gamma_{N}
, and
\Gamma_{C}
such that
\lvert\Gamma_{D}\rvert>0
and
\lvert\Gamma_{C}\rvert>0
(
\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert
denotes here the Hausdorff measure).
In its reference configuration, the elastic body is in contact through
\Gamma_{C}
with a rigid foundation, and we assume that the (unknown) contact zone in the deformed configuration will be included in
\Gamma_{C}
. The body is clamped at
\Gamma_{D}
and it is subjected to a volume force
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and to a surface load
\boldsymbol{g}_{N}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{N})
on
\Gamma_{N}
; see Figure
5
for an example.

Denoting by
\boldsymbol{n}
the unit normal vector on
\partial\Omega
pointing out of
\Omega
, for any displacement field
\boldsymbol{v}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for any density of surface forces
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{n}
defined on
\partial\Omega
, we have the following (unique) decomposition into normal and tangential components:

\boldsymbol{v}=v^{n}\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{v}^{\boldsymbol{t}}\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{n}=\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{v}).

(2.1)

We consider the following problem:
Find the displacement field
\boldsymbol{u}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})+\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(2.2b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{D}$},

(2.2c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{g}_{N}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{N}$},

(2.2d)

\displaystyle u^{n}\leq 0,\ \sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq 0,\ \sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})u^{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{C}$},

(2.2e)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{C}$},

(2.2f)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nabla v}+\boldsymbol{\nabla v}^{\top})
is the strain tensor field,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}
is the Cauchy stress tensor,
\boldsymbol{\rm div}
is the divergence operator acting row-wise on tensor valued functions, and
\boldsymbol{A}
is the fourth order symmetric elasticity tensor such that, for all second-order tensor
\boldsymbol{\tau}
,
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\lambda\tr(\boldsymbol{\tau})\boldsymbol{I}_{d}+2\mu\boldsymbol{\tau}
, with
\lambda
and
\mu
denoting the Lamé parameters.

Remark 1

(Contact conditions)
.

The first contact condition (
2.2e
) is a complementarity condition:
if, at a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma_{C}
, there is no contact in the deformed configuration (i.e.,
u^{n}<0
), then the normal stress vanishes at that point (i.e.,
\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})=0
);
on the other hand, if at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma_{C}
the normal stress is nonzero (i.e.,
\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})<0
), then in the deformed configuration we still have contact in
\boldsymbol{x}
(i.e.,
u^{n}=0
).
These conditions also account for the absence of
normal cohesive forces between the elastic body and the rigid foundation.
The second contact condition (
2.2f
) simply establishes the absence of friction on
\Gamma_{C}
.

The incorporation of standard friction models to the following a posteriori theory seems possible, but lies outside of the scope of the present paper.
This subject will be addressed in future works.

2.1.2
Weak formulation

Let
D
denote a measurable set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In what follows,
D
will be typically either equal to
\Omega
or to the union of a finite subset of mesh elements.
We denote by
H^{s}(D)
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the usual Sobolev space of index
s
on
D
, with the convention that
H^{0}(D)\coloneqq L^{2}(D)
, the space of square-integrable functions on
D
.
Its vector and tensor versions are denoted respectively by
\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(D)\coloneqq[H^{s}(D)]^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}^{s}(D)\coloneqq[H^{s}(D)]^{d\times d}
.
We let, similarly,
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(D)\coloneq[L^{2}(D)]^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{L}}^{2}(D)\coloneq[L^{2}(D)]^{d\times d}
.
Moreover,
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{s,D}
denotes the norm of
H^{s}(D)
or
\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(D)
according to its argument.
The first subscript is omitted when
s=0
, i.e.,
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{D}
is the standard norm of
L^{2}(D)
,
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(D)
, or
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{L}}^{2}(D)
according to its argument.
The usual inner products of these spaces are denoted by
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{D}
, with the convention that the subscript is omitted when
D=\Omega
.
In what follows, we will also need the space
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}(\textbf{div},D)
spanned by functions of
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{L}}^{2}(D)
with weak (row-wise) divergence in
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(D)
.

Denote by
\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
the subspace of
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
incorporating the Dirichlet boundary condition on
\Gamma_{D}
, and by
\boldsymbol{K}
its subset spanned by admissible displacements, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\ :\ \boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\right\},\qquad\boldsymbol{K}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)\ :\ v^{n}\leq 0\ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{C}\right\}.

The weak formulation of problem (
2.2
) corresponds to the following variational inequality (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
):
Find
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{K}
such that

a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u})\geq L(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{K},

(2.3)

where the bilinear form
a\colon\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
and the linear form
L\colon\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
are defined as follows:
For all
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,

a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})),\qquad L(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})+(\boldsymbol{g}_{N},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Gamma_{N}}.

(2.4)

Problem (
2.3
) admits a unique solution by the Stampacchia theorem.

3
Basic a posteriori error estimate

In this section we derive a basic a posteriori error estimate based on the notion of equilibrated stress reconstruction.

3.1
Error measure

In the framework of a posteriori error estimation, the dual norm of a residual functional can be used as a measure of the error between the exact solution
\boldsymbol{u}
of the problem and the solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
obtained with the finite element method.
Denoting by
(\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega))^{*}
the dual space of
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
, for any
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
the
residual

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\in(\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega))^{*}
is defined by

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle&\coloneqq L(\boldsymbol{v})-a(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\boldsymbol{v})+\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}},v^{n}\right)_{\Gamma_{C}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega),\end{split}

(3.1)

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
denotes the duality pairing between
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
and
(\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega))^{*}
.
We equip
\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
with the following mesh-dependent norm:

|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\boldsymbol{v}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|^{2}\coloneqq\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^{2}+\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,h}^{2}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega),

(3.2)

where

\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,h}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{C}}\frac{1}{h_{F}}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{F}^{2}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega).

(3.3)

It is easy to show that
\left\lvert\,\cdot\,\right\rvert_{C,h}
is subadditive and absolutely homogeneous, i.e., it is a seminorm. As a consequence, also
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\,\cdot\,|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|
is subadditive and absolutely homogeneous. Moreover, if we suppose
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\boldsymbol{v}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|=0
, then both
\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert
and
\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,h}
are zero, and this implies
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}
by the Friedrichs inequality in
\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
, showing that
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\,\cdot\,|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|
is indeed a norm on
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
.

The dual norm of the residual of a function
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
on the normed space
(\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega),|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\,\cdot\,|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|)
is given by

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{*}}\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega),\,|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|\boldsymbol{v}|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|=1\end{subarray}}\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle.

(3.4)

In what follows, the quantity
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{*}}
will be used as a measure of the error committed approximating the exact solution
\boldsymbol{u}
with
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
.

3.2
A posteriori error estimate

We start this section by introducing the concept of equilibrated stress reconstruction and the definition of five error estimators.

Definition 3

(Equilibrated stress reconstruction)
.

We will call
equilibrated stress reconstruction
any second-order tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
such that:

1.

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}(\textbf{div},\Omega)
,

2.

\left(\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}+\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{T}=0
for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{0}(T)
and every
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,

3.

(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n})|_{F}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(F)
for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{C}
, and
\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{F}=\left(\boldsymbol{g}_{N},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{F}
for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{0}(F)
and every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}
,

4.

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{t}}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\Gamma_{C}
.

Given an equilibrated stress reconstruction
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
, for every element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we define the following local error estimators:

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{osc},T}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{h_{T}}{\pi}\lVert\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\rVert_{T},

\displaystyle\text{(oscillation)}

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{str},T}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rVert_{T},

\displaystyle\text{(stress)}

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{Neu},T}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{N}}C_{t,T,F}h_{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\boldsymbol{g}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{F},

\displaystyle\text{(Neumann)}

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{cnt},T}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{C}}h_{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left\lVert\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\sigma^{n}_{h}\right\rVert_{F}.

\displaystyle\text{(contact)}

Here,
C_{t,T,F}
is the constant of the trace inequality
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{F}\rVert_{F}\leq C_{t,T,F}h_{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}
with
\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{F}\coloneq\frac{1}{|F|}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{v}
valid for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}(T)
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
; see
[
32
, Theorem 4.6.3]
or
[
16
, Section 1.4]

Theorem 4

(A posteriori error estimate for the dual norm of the residual)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
be the solution of (
2.7
),
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
the residual defined by (
3.1
), and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
an equilibrated stress reconstruction in the sense of Definition
3
. Then,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{*}}\leq\Biggl(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Bigl((\eta_{\emph{osc},T}+\eta_{\emph{str},T}+\eta_{\emph{Neu},T})^{2}+(\eta_{\emph{cnt},T})^{2}\Bigr)\Biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Proof.

Thanks to the regularity of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
and of its normal trace (see Properties 1. and 3. in Definition
3
), the following Green formula holds:

(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\nabla v})=-\left(\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}\right)+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Gamma_{N}}+(\sigma_{h}^{n},v^{n})_{\Gamma_{C}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega),

(3.5)

where we have also used the decomposition (
2.1
) of the normal stress reconstruction
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}
into normal and tangential components on the contact boundary
\Gamma_{C}
, and the fact that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{t}}|_{\Gamma_{C}}=\boldsymbol{0}
thanks to Property 4. in Definition
3
.
Now, fix
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
such that
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\boldsymbol{v}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|^{2}=\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^{2}+\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,h}^{2}=1
and consider the argument of the supremum in the definition (
3.4
) of the dual norm of the residual.
Expanding
L(\cdot)
and
a(\cdot,\cdot)
according to their definition (
2.4
), adding
and subtracting the term
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v})
, using the symmetry of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
, and applying Green’s formula (
3.5
), we obtain

\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}\rangle&=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})+(\boldsymbol{g}_{N},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Gamma_{N}}-\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)+\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}},v^{n}\right)_{\Gamma_{C}}\\
&\quad+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v})-(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v})\\
&=(\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{v})+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v})+(\boldsymbol{g}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Gamma_{N}}\\
&\quad+\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\sigma^{n}_{h},v^{n}\right)_{\Gamma_{C}}\\
&\eqcolon\mathfrak{T}_{1}+\cdots+\mathfrak{T}_{4}.\end{split}

We estimate each term separately.
Denoting by
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{T}
the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{0}(T)
, and
using Property 2. of Definition
3
with test function
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{T}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{0}(T)
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the Poincaré inequality
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{T}^{0}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{T}\leq h_{T}\pi^{-1}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}
, the first term becomes

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{T}^{0}\boldsymbol{v})_{T}\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\rVert_{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{T}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{h_{T}}{\pi}\lVert\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\rVert_{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{\text{osc},T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}.

For the second term, we simply use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\begin{split}\mathfrak{T}_{2}\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\rVert_{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{\text{str},T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}.\end{split}

Denoting by
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{F}
the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{0}(F)
, and
using Property 3. of Definition
3
with
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{F}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{0}(F)
as a test function, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the trace inequality
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{F}^{0}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{F}\leq C_{t,T,F}h_{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}
,
F\subset\partial T
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{N}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{F}^{0}\boldsymbol{v})_{F}\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{N}}\lVert\boldsymbol{g}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{F}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0}_{F}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{N}}C_{t,T,F}h_{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\boldsymbol{g}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{F}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{\text{Neu},T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla v}\rVert_{T},

where we recall that, for any

\mathcal{F}_{T}^{N}
collects the Neumann faces of
T
contained in
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}
.
Finally, we consider the term on

We define, for all

, the local counterpart of the seminorm (

\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,T}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{C}}\frac{1}{h_{F}}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{F}^{2},

where
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{C}
is the (possibly empty) set collecting the contact faces of
T
contained in
\partial T\cap\Gamma_{C}
.
Then, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{4}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{C}}\left\lVert\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\sigma^{n}_{h}\right\rVert_{F}\lVert v^{n}\rVert_{F}\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{C}}h_{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left\lVert\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\sigma^{n}_{h}\right\rVert_{F}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{\text{cnt},T}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,T}.

Let, for the sake of brevity,
\eta_{a,T}\coloneqq\eta_{\text{osc},T}+\eta_{\text{str},T}+\eta_{\text{Neu},T}
for any element

.
Combining the above results and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega),\,|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|\boldsymbol{v}|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|=1\end{subarray}}\Biggl\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Bigl(\eta_{a,T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{T}+\eta_{\rm cnt,T}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,T}\Bigr)\Biggr\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega),\,|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|\boldsymbol{v}|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|=1\end{subarray}}\Biggl\{\Biggl(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left((\eta_{a,T})^{2}+(\eta_{\rm cnt,T})^{2}\right)\Biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Biggl(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{T}^{2}+\left\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rvert_{C,T}^{2}\right)\Biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Biggr\}

\displaystyle=\Biggl(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Bigl((\eta_{\text{osc},T}+\eta_{\text{str},T}+\eta_{\text{Neu},T})^{2}+(\eta_{\text{cnt},T})^{2}\Bigr)\Biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.\qed

3.3
Comparison between the residual dual norm and the energy norm

The goal of this section is to compare the dual norm
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{*}}
with the energy norm
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\rm en}
of the error, where

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\rm en}^{2}\coloneqq a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega).

(3.6)

Remark 6

(Coercivity of the bilinear form

)
.

The bilinear form
a(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
on the space
(\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega),\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{1,\Omega})
is elliptic with a constant
\alpha
which depends on the Lamé parameter

and on the Korn constant

:

\alpha\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}^{2}\leq a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\rm en}^{2}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega).

(3.7)

Throughout the rest of this section, we adopt the following shorthand notation:
For every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
a\lesssim b
for
a\leq Cb
with
C>0
independent of the mesh size
h
and of the Nitsche parameter
\gamma_{0}
.

, we have

\begin{split}\mathfrak{T}_{2}&=\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}},u^{n}-u^{n}_{h}\right)_{\Gamma_{C}}\\
&=\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}},\frac{1}{\gamma}\bigl[\gamma(u^{n}-u^{n}_{h})-\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\bigr]\right)_{\Gamma_{C}}\\
&=-\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}},\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})-P_{1,\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right)\right)_{\Gamma_{C}}\\
&\quad+\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}},\frac{1}{\gamma}\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right)_{\Gamma_{C}}.\end{split}

(3.20)

Due to the fact that
a[a]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}=([a]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}})^{2}
and
a[b]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\leq[a]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}[b]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}
, it follows that

(a-b)([a]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-[b]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}})=a[a]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}+b[b]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-a[b]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-b[a]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\geq([a]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-[b]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}})^{2}

for every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using the latter inequality with
(a,b)=(P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}),P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))
for the first term in (
3.20
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the second one, we have

\begin{split}\mathfrak{T}_{2}&\leq-\left\lVert\gamma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\right)\right\rVert_{\Gamma_{C}}^{2}\\
&\quad+\left\lVert\gamma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left(\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}-\left[P_{1,\gamma}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right]_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\right)\right\rVert_{\Gamma_{C}}\left\lVert\gamma^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\right\rVert_{\Gamma_{C}}.\end{split}

We continue using the generalized Young inequality
ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}/4
for the second term followed by the saturation assumption (
3.9
) to write:

\mathfrak{T}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma_{0}}\left\lVert{\left(\frac{\gamma_{0}}{\gamma}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})}\right\rVert_{\Gamma_{C}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{4\gamma_{0}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma_{0}\alpha}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\rm en}^{2}.

(3.21)

Combining (
3.14
), (
3.18
), and (
3.21
) we finally get, for a suitable real number

,

\left(\alpha^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-\frac{C}{4\gamma_{0}\alpha}\right)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\rm en}^{2}\leq C\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\rm en}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{*}}

and, taking
\gamma_{0}
sufficiently large,

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\rm en}\lesssim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{*}},

thus concluding the proof of (
3.10
).

Lemma 15

1) By definition,

2) First, we check that, for any

5.3b
) holds for every

(with orthogonal taken with respect to the

) of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{a}}

5.4
), and (

and let

by zero outside of
T

Here, we have used the fact that

3,4) We only detail the proof of 3) as that of 4) is similar.
