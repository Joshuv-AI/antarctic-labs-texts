Abstract
. Physical models often contain unknown functions and relations. The goal of our work is to answer the question of how one should excite or control a system under consideration in an appropriate way to be able to reconstruct an unknown nonlinear relation. To answer this question, we propose a greedy reconstruction algorithm within an offline-online strategy. We apply this strategy to a two-dimensional semilinear elliptic model. Our identification is based on the application of several space-dependent excitations (also called controls). These specific controls are designed by the algorithm in order to obtain a deeper insight into the underlying physical problem and a more precise reconstruction of the unknown relation. We perform numerical simulations that demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach which is not limited to the current type of equation. Since our algorithm provides not only a way to determine unknown operators by existing data but also protocols for new experiments, it is a holistic concept to tackle the problem of improving physical models.

1.
Introduction

The identification of unknown operators in semilinear systems plays a fundamental role in various scientific disciplines, including physics, chemistry, and biology
[
5
,
16
,
36
]
.
Accurately characterizing these operators is crucial for understanding and predicting complex system behavior.
However, they often remain unknown or only partially known, necessitating efficient and reliable reconstruction methods.

Many techniques exist for such identification, drawing from the fields of inverse problems and optimization.
Traditional approaches involve inverse problem methods, inferring the operator from measured outputs under controlled inputs.
Techniques like gradient descent, least squares minimization, or Bayesian approaches are commonly employed
[
20
,
28
]
.
Optimization methods formulate the identification problem as an optimization task, minimizing the discrepancy between model outputs and data.

With the recent surge of machine learning, neural networks are increasingly applied to identification tasks
[
26
,
33
]
.
While these methods have achieved significant progress, a common challenge lies in data quality. Real-world data often suffers from noise and uncertainties, leading to inaccuracies in the reconstructed operator.
Additionally, limited data availability due to cost or time constraints can hinder accurate identification.
Furthermore, biased data can lead to biased estimates of the operator, necessitating careful data collection and analysis procedures.

This paper extends the approach introduced in
[
27
]
and optimized in
[
8
]
to reconstruct unknown nonlinear operators in semilinear elliptic models, addressing some of the challenges mentioned above.
We leverage the power of optimal control techniques, formulating the reconstruction problem as an optimization problem where the objective function measures the discrepancy between the model output and observed data.
By strategically designing control functions and employing optimization algorithms, we aim to drive the model output toward observed data that provides significant insights into the underlying physical system, thereby revealing the characteristics of the unknown operator.
Our method develops optimal protocols to generate data that yields as much insight into the system as possible.
In other words, the goal of the algorithm is to convexify the objective of the identification problem, i.e., increasing the region of convexity around the minimum.
In particular, the basin of attraction of the minimum is extended and the conditioning of the problem in this region is improved.
This capability of the algorithm is further explained and visualized in the numerical section below and in
[
8
]
.
The optimized algorithm was used to identify different linear and nonlinear unknown operators and distributions
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
We extend the existing results by considering a system of elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs) and reconstructing infinite-dimensional objects.

The proposed approach offers several advantages. Unlike traditional methods that require large amounts of high-quality data, our framework incorporates an active learning strategy.
The algorithm selects new data points to collect, focusing on those that provide the most information about the unknown operator, leading to reduced data dependency.
Additionally, the framework provides theoretical guarantees for convergence to the true operator under certain conditions.

Building upon this foundation, we present a computationally efficient numerical scheme to solve the formulated optimization problem, enabling implementation for various semilinear elliptic models.
Our semilinear PDE can be motivated as the steady-state solution to PDEs describing models from epidemics, biochemical systems, and nuclear reactor models or general reaction-diffusion models
[
29
,
32
]
.
Similar models are also used to describe membrane-resonators; see, e.g.,
[
39
]
.
We refer to
[
2
,
23
,
24
,
35
]
and the references therein for existing investigations on the identification of unknown nonlinearities in semilinear PDEs.
Furthermore, we refer the reader to the work
[
22
]
on general inverse problems with PDEs.

2.
Formulation of the problem

We consider a convex two-dimensional spatial domain
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with Lipschitz-continuous boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
.
For
x=(x_{1},x_{2})^{\top}\in\Omega
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}(x)=(y_{1}(x),y_{2}(x))^{\top}
, we consider the following system of semilinear PDEs with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

(2.1a)

\displaystyle-\Delta{\boldsymbol{y}}(x)+{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{y}}(x))

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{y}}(x)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

where
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a control input and
{\boldsymbol{g}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
an unknown nonlinearity.
This structure of the problem is motivated by the stationary Lotka-Volterra system, that we consider in the numerical example below.
In this setting, the variables
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
have the significance of the distribution of two different species that interact with the nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
For
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})^{\top}
, we define the set of admissible controls as

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\coloneqq\big\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\;\big|\;{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathsf{a}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x)\leq{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{b}}\;\text{ for almost all (f.a.a.) }\;x\in\Omega\big\},

with given bounds
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathsf{a}},{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathsf{a}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathsf{b}}
.
Notice that the elements in
{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\subset\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
are uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
by
{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{max}}}\coloneqq\max\{\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathsf{a}}\|_{\infty},\|{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\mathsf{b}}\|_{\infty}\}
.

The nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}
is assumed to lie in a
K
-dimensional space
\mathfrak{G}
.
Thus, given a basis
\mathcal{G}=\{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{K}\}
of
\mathfrak{G}
with functions
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we can write

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{g}}(y)={\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(y)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha_{j}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(y)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(y){\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

for some coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(y)=({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{1}(y),\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{K}(y))\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times K}
.
Our true unknown nonlinearity is now assumed to be given by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\star}}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha^{\star}_{j}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(y)

for an unknown coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\star}=(\alpha_{j}^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
Notice that
{\boldsymbol{g}}
is locally Lipschitz continuous if all elements in
\mathcal{G}
are polynomials or piecewise linear and continuous functions.

For the coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, we assume that they are elements of

\displaystyle{\mathscr{A}_{K}}\coloneqq\big\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\;\big|\;0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq{\alpha_{\mathsf{max}}}\text{ for }i\in[K]\big\},

with a scalar
{\alpha_{\mathsf{max}}}\geq 0
.
Notice that
{\mathscr{A}_{K}}
is nonempty, compact and convex.
Further, we denote by

{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}

the solution to (
2.1
) using the nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and applying controls (inputs)
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}
(cf.
Section

3
).
To identify the unknown true nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\star}}
, we generate a
K
-dimensional set of control functions
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{K}\subset{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}
to perform
K
(laboratory) experiments and obtain the data
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}}_{\star}
for
m\in[K]
.
Then the nonconvex parameter identification problem in order to estimate the nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\star}}
is given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathscr{A}_{K}}}\;\sum_{m=1}^{K}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}}_{\star}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathscr{A}_{K}}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, we did not add an additional regularisation term depending on
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
The controls
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{K}\subset{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}
will be chosen properly by the iterative
Algorithm

4.1
presented in
Section

4
.

3.1.
Existence of solutions and wellposedness

We apply the results of

For the basis functions and the nonlinearity

Assumption 3.1

1)

The basis elements

are continuously differentiable.
This implies in particular that they are
(locally) Lipschitz continuous

\displaystyle{\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(y)-{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(\widetilde{y})\|}_{2}\leq L_{\varphi_{j}}\,{\|y-\widetilde{y}\|}_{2}.

We set
L_{\varphi}\coloneqq\max_{j\in[K]}L_{\varphi_{j}}
.

2)

The basis elements are
bounded
from below and above, i.e., there exist vectors

\displaystyle c_{\varphi_{j}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(y)\leq C_{\varphi_{j}}.

We set

and
C_{\varphi}\coloneqq\max_{j\in[K]}\|C_{\varphi_{j}}\|_{\infty}

Notice that this in particular implies that

\displaystyle\forall M>0\;\exists\phi_{M}>0:\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(y)\|_{2}\leq\phi_{M}\;\forall\|y\|_{2}\leq M.

3)

The basis elements are monotone non-decreasing functions, i.e. we have
\left({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(y)-{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(\widetilde{y})\right)\cdot\left(y-\widetilde{y}\right)\geq 0

For the nonlinearity

It holds that

These hypotheses on the nonlinearity and the definition of

2)

The nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}

\displaystyle\forall M>0\;\exists\varrho_{M}>0:\|{\boldsymbol{g}}(y)\|_{2}\leq\varrho_{M}\;\forall\|y\|_{2}\leq M.

3)

The nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}
is monotone non-decreasing.

Proof.

Part
1)
follows directly from
Assumption

3.1
-
1)
and

3.2

Part

3.2.
Stability with respect to the parameters

In this section, we investigate the wellposedness and Lipschitz continuity (stability) of the parameter-to-state map defined below.
In the following analysis, we take account of the iterative structure of the algorithm defined in
Section

4
. More specifically, we consider a

that indicates the
k

We introduce for

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{A}_{k}}\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathscr{A}_{K}}\,|\,\alpha_{j}=0,\;j=k+1,\ldots,K\}.

From
Theorem

3.6
and
Assumption

, we deduce the following proposition.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
Assumptions

3.1
and
3.2
hold and
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}

Introduce the parameter-to-state map
{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}

We recall the weak formulation (

\displaystyle a[{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{v}}]+\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{v}}(x)\cdot\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(x))\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x)\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

\displaystyle a[\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}]+\int_{\Omega}\bar{{\boldsymbol{y}}}(x)\cdot\,\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\Big(\alpha^{1}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}\big({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}}(x)\big)-\alpha^{2}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}\big({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}(x)\big)\Big)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

We derive the following energy estimate for

Lemma 3.10

Let

Proof.

The claim is true if

\displaystyle\|\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:ellipticity_bilinearity}}}{{=}}a[\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}]=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\int_{\Omega}\bar{{\boldsymbol{y}}}(x)\cdot\left(-\alpha^{1}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}\big({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}}(x)\big)+\alpha^{2}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}\big({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}(x)\big)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

3.3.
Wellposedness of inverse-map

This section is devoted to the stability analysis of the parameters with respect to small perturbations in the solution.
Let us again fix the control
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}
.
We are interested in the stability estimate of the form

\displaystyle{\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}\|}_{\infty}\leq C_{\mathrm{inv}}\,{\|{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}\|}_{{\mathscr{Y}}}.

This inequality implies the Lipschitz continuity of the inverse parameter-to-state map
{\mathscr{P}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
, i.e., the state-to-parameter map given for fixed controls

\displaystyle{\mathscr{P}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}:{\mathscr{B}}\to{\mathscr{A}_{K}},\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}={\mathscr{P}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),

where we introduced the compact set

Therefore, if the difference of two solutions is small in the
{\mathscr{Y}}
norm, then the corresponding parameters will be close with respect to the maximum norm (which is equivalent to all other norms in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
).
This is the content of
Theorem

3.12

Notice that an essential assumption is that the basis elements are bounded away from zero.

Theorem 3.12
.

Suppose that

3.1
and
k\in[K]
hold.
Furthermore, let

\displaystyle{\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}\|}_{\infty}\leq C_{\mathrm{inv}}\,{\|{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}\|}_{{\mathscr{Y}}}.

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{inv}}\coloneqq\sqrt{2\widetilde{c}}\,(1+\,k\,L_{\varphi}\,{\alpha_{\mathsf{max}}}\,C_{\Omega})c_{\varphi}^{-1}

for constants

Proof.

With
\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}
and

(3.10a)

\displaystyle{\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|}_{\infty}^{2}\leq\widetilde{c}\,{\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\|}_{2}^{2}=\widetilde{c}\,c_{\varphi}^{-2}\,{\|c_{\varphi}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|}_{2}^{2}\leq\widetilde{c}\,c_{\varphi}^{-2}\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}})\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}){\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}){\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\,\widetilde{c}\,c_{\varphi}^{-2}\left({\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}){\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}){\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}\|}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+{\|\left({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}})-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}})\right){\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}\|}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

(3.10c)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\,\widetilde{c}\,c_{\varphi}^{-2}\left(\|\Delta({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}})\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+k^{2}\,L_{\varphi}^{2}\,\alpha_{\max}^{2}\,\|{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

3.1
-
2)

4.
Greedy-reconstruction algorithm

In this section, we present our strategy to tackle the identification problem (
2.4
).
For this, we recall some notation.
We denote by
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}
the solution of (
2.1
) with the control
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
and nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.
We have for
1\leq k\leq K-1
the set

{\mathscr{A}_{k}}\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathscr{A}_{K}}\,|\,\alpha_{j}=0,\;j=k+1,\ldots K\}
(cf. (
3.4
)).
Further, we set
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
as the
k
-th canonical vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
Hence,
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}}^{k},{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}
denotes the solution of (
2.1
) with the control
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
and nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{k}
.

We show now how to construct a set of optimized controls and basis elements, in particular with the goal of improving local convexity properties of (
2.4
).
We follow the idea of
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
27
]
, where linear and bilinear dynamical systems have been investigated.
It is the aim of this work to extend this strategy to the general semilinear PDE case (
2.1
).

The main idea is to split the reconstruction process of
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\star}}
into offline and online phases.
In the offline phase, a greedy algorithm computes a set of optimized controls
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{K}
by exploiting only simulations of the semilinear model (
2.1
) and without using any laboratory data.
In the online phase, the computed controls
\{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{K}
are used experimentally to produce the laboratory data
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\star}^{{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{m}}
for
m\in[L]
and to define the nonlinear problem (
2.1
).
While the online phase consists (mathematically) of solving a classical parameter-identification inverse problem, the offline phase requires the greedy algorithm that was first introduced in
[
27
]
and analyzed and improved in
[
9
]
.
The goal of this offline/online framework is to find a good approximation of the unknown operator
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\star}}
for which the difference between observed experimental data and numerically computed data is the smallest for any control.
To do so, the algorithm attempts to distinguish numerical data for any two
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{1}}
,
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}\in\text{span }\mathcal{G}

[
27
]
.
This is achieved by an iterative procedure that performs a sweep over the basis
\mathcal{G}
and computes a new control at each iteration.
Suppose that at iteration
k
the control fields
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{k}
are already computed; the new control function
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{k+1}
is obtained by two substeps, the so-called fitting and splitting step.
One first solves the identification problem (
4.1
) which gives the coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{k}=(\beta^{k}_{j})_{j=1,\dots,k}
.
Then one computes the new control as the solution of the splitting step (
4.2
).

This general procedure can be optimized using some extensions.
More specifically, in each iteration, all elements of
\mathcal{G}
are considered in parallel and the ‘best’ one is chosen in a greedy way; see
[
8
, Section 6.1]
for further explanation of the original and optimized greedy reconstruction algorithm in a linear case.

This offline procedure for our nonlinear case is summarized in
Algorithm

4.1
.
We refer to
[
9
]
for a general idea to prove that this algorithm is able to make problem (
2.4
) (locally) uniquely solvable.

Notice, that during the Algorithm, the basis
\mathcal{G}
is reordered. Since only
k
controls are generated through the execution of the algorithm, this has the effect that actually only the
k
most sensible basis elements are selected, in the sense that they lead to a high difference in the splitting step, in particular higher than the tolerance
\mathrm{tol}_{1}
(cf. (
4.2
)).
In the final identification, only the first
k
basis elements are taken into account. Hence, the reordering has an influence if less than
K
controls are selected.
In general, the value of
\mathrm{tol}_{1}
allows the algorithm to stop with a smaller amount of optimized controls, if any further controls do not provide additional information about the nonlinearity within the given tolerance.
In this work, we focus on the best possible reconstruction ability of our algorithm given a certain set
\mathcal{G}
.
Hence, we keep
\mathrm{tol}_{1}
fixed at machine precision and, therefore, generate the maximum amount of optimized controls.

k
reads

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\widehat{J}_{f}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right\rangle_{2}\geq 0\qquad\qquad\forall{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathscr{A}_{k},\qquad

where
\nabla\widehat{J}_{f}=(\nabla_{\beta_{1}}\widehat{J}_{f},\ldots,\nabla_{\beta_{k}}\widehat{J}_{f})^{\top}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\beta_{j}}\widehat{J}_{f}=\nu\,\beta_{j}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{q}}^{m}(x)\cdot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{m}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\qquad j\in[k].

Now we fix
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and turn to the splitting step in which the new control
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{k+1}
is computed.
The splitting step at iteration
k<K
is given by
(the subscript
s
stands for
“splitting step”
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min J_{s}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k+1})\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k+1}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{subject to}\quad\begin{cases}-\Delta{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}+{\boldsymbol{g}}_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}})={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{|\Gamma}=0,\\
-\Delta{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k+1}+{\boldsymbol{g}}_{k+1}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{k+1})={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k+1}_{|\Gamma}=0,\end{cases}\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and }{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}.\end{split}

Exploiting the control-to-state map
{\mathscr{S}}
defined in
Theorem

3.8
, we can introduce the reduced cost

\displaystyle\widehat{J}_{s}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})\coloneqq J_{s}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}},{\mathscr{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}),{\mathscr{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}}^{k+1}}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})),

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
denotes again the
k
-th unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
We also introduce the reduced problem

(4.5)

\displaystyle\min\widehat{J}_{s}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}.

We can state the following theorem on the existence of solutions to (
4.5
).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
Assumptions

3.1
and
3.2
hold.
Then the optimization problem (
4.5
) has at least one solution
\bar{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}
.

Proof.

Since our
\widehat{J}_{s}
is bounded from below by the virtue of
Theorem

3.6
and the boundedness of the set of admissible controls
{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}}
, we can apply
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
.
∎

The adjoint equations for the fitting step read as follows

\displaystyle-\Delta{\boldsymbol{q}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}+{\boldsymbol{g}}^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{q}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}J_{s}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}};{\boldsymbol{y}}),

\displaystyle-\Delta{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k+1}+{\boldsymbol{g}}^{\prime}_{k+1}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{k+1})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k+1}}J_{s}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}};{\boldsymbol{y}}),

completed with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Since
\nabla{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(y)
and
\nabla{\boldsymbol{g}}_{k+1}(y)
are positive semidefinite, we can ensure a constrained qualification for (
4.4
), so that first-order necessary optimality conditions can be formulated; cf.
[
38
, Section 6.1.2]
.

Suppose that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
solves (
4.5
), then an optimality condition of the splitting step reads

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\widehat{J}_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\geq 0\qquad\qquad\forall{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{\mathrm{ad}}},

where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\widehat{J}_{s}
is given
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\widehat{J}_{s}({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})=\nu\,{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}-({\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k+1})
.

We solve all optimization problems in
Algorithm

4.1
using the MATLAB built-in function
fmincon
which uses the interior-point method; see, e.g.,
[
31
]
. However, any other suitable subroutine could be considered.

5.
Numerical approximation

In this section, we present the numerical approximation of the governing equation.
First, we describe our method to solve the governing model (
2.1
).
We choose now
\Omega=(-x_{\max},x_{\max})^{2}
for a fixed
x_{\max}>0
. We set a numerical grid that provides a partitioning of
\Omega
in
N\times N
,
N>1
, equally-spaced non-overlapping square cells of side length
h=2x_{\max}/N
.
We define the nodal points

\displaystyle x_{1}^{i}=i\,h-x_{\max},\qquad x_{2}^{j}=j\,h-x_{\max}.

Our discrete domain is then given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{h}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i,j=1}^{N}\omega_{ij},

where an elementary cell is defined as

\displaystyle\omega_{ij}\coloneqq\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega\;\big|\;(x_{1},x_{2})\in\left(x_{1}^{i-1},x_{1}^{i}\right)\times\left(x_{2}^{j-1},x_{2}^{j}\right)\right\},\quad i,j\in[N].

In our numerical examples, we use
x_{\max}=1
.

We define the discrete vectors
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{N},{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2(N+1)^{2}}
as
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{N}=(y^{0}_{1},\ldots,y^{N^{2}}_{1},y_{2}^{0},\ldots y^{N^{2}}_{2})^{\top}
and
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{N}=(\varepsilon^{0}_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{1}^{N^{2}},\varepsilon^{0}_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{2}^{N^{2}})^{\top}
.

After discretization of (
2.1
), we end up with

(5.1)

\displaystyle-A{\boldsymbol{y}}^{N}+{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{N})={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{N},

where
A
is the discrete Laplace operator using finite differences and zero boundary conditions
[
25
, Section 2.2]
.

We consider a fixed-point method for solving (
5.1
) numerically
[
4
,
15
]
.
The procedure with relaxation parameter
\lambda_{\mathrm{a}}\in[0,1]
is summarized in
Algorithm

5.1
.

Algorithm 5.1

Fixed-point method

1:

Parameter
0\leq\lambda_{\mathsf{a}}\leq 1
,
tolerance
\mathrm{tol}_{2}>0
, maximum iteration depth
\ell_{\max}\gg 1

2:

Set
\ell=0
and
E\gg\mathrm{tol}_{2}

3:

Generate initial guess
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}^{N}
that satisfies
-A{\boldsymbol{y}}^{N}_{0}={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{N}

4:

while

E<tol

and

\ell<\ell_{\max}

do

5:

Calculate
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{N}
as solution to

-A\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{N}={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{N}-{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{N}_{\ell})

6:

Set
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{N}_{\ell+1}=\lambda_{\mathsf{a}}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{N}_{\ell}+(1-\lambda_{\mathsf{a}})\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{N}
and

7:

Set

8:

end

return

{\boldsymbol{y}}^{N}_{\ell}

Next, we explain the ingredients for
Algorithm

4.1
that we use in our numerical experiment. We choose the following nonlinearity, motivated by coupled Lotka-Volterra equations in the stationary regime (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
21
,
30
]
).
In this setting, the variables
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
have the significance of the distribution of two different species that interact with the nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
Furthermore, usually this interaction is up to constants equal in both components.
Hence, we choose

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(y)\coloneqq(\gamma_{1}\,G_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(y),-\gamma_{2}\,G_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(y))^{\top},

where
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}>0
are given constants and

(5.2)

\displaystyle G_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(y)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}(y),

with basis functions
\varphi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
j\in[K]
.

We use the two-dimensional polynomials as basis functions to reconstruct the unknown nonlinearity
G_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
More precisely, we approximate the nonlinearity using monomials up to entrywise degree
P
, i.e. the basis

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\big\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{K}\big\}\coloneqq\big\{1,y_{1},y_{2},y_{1}y_{2},y_{1}^{2},y_{2}^{2},y_{1}^{2}y_{2},\ldots,y_{1}^{P}y_{2}^{P}\big\}.

The cardinality of
\mathcal{G}
, which is the maximum number of iterations
K
in the algorithm, is then given by
K=|\mathcal{G}|=\binom{2+P}{P}
.
With this setting, the support of the solution
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
of (
2.1
) does not need to be known in advance.
Furthermore, it is possible to make predictions of the nonlinearity outside of the support of the solutions due to the non-locality of the monomials.

We now discuss
Assumption

3.1
for our choice of the basis elements:
1) All basis elements
\varphi_{j}
are smooth and in particular (locally) Lipschitz continuous since they are monomials;
2) The basis elements
\varphi_{j}
are not bounded away from zero and in general not bounded from above.
However, on every bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
they are also bounded and strictly greater than zero.
3) All monomials
\varphi_{j}
are monotonically increasing for nonnegative entries.
However, by our special structure (
5
), the nonlinearity
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is not monotonically increasing on the full space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
but on a (non-empty) subspace.

Hence our choice of monomials does not fit perfectly with our assumptions.
However, they can be fulfilled after restricting ourselves to a non-empty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Furthermore, even though the theoretical assumptions are not completely fulfilled, we observe that our strategy works very well in our numerical experiments.

Error in Taylor coefficients

To round up our numerical experiments, we discuss a different way of measuring the error of the final reconstruction: the absolute value of the difference of the coefficient of each monomial in the Taylor expansion of the true nonlinearity and the reconstructed nonlinearity, respectively.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we presented a greedy reconstruction algorithm to identify unknown operators in nonlinear elliptic models.
We proved the Lipschitz continuity of the parameter-to-state map and its inverse.
We performed several numerical experiments that successfully validate our proposed scheme.

This paper represents a significant step towards untangling the mysteries of unknown nonlinear operators within semilinear elliptic models.
By harnessing the power of optimal control and active learning, we pave the way for a deeper understanding and more accurate predictions of complex physical phenomena governed by these models.
