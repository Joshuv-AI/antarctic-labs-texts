Caustics in turbulent aerosols form along the Vieillefosse line at weak particle inertia
Jan Meibohm, Kristian Gustavsson, Bernhard Mehlig

Abstract
Caustic singularities of the spatial distribution of particles in turbulent aerosols enhance collision rates and accelerate coagulation. Here we investigate how and where caustics form at weak particle inertia, by analysing a three-dimensional Gaussian statistical model for turbulent aerosols in the persistent limit, where the flow varies slowly compared with the particle relaxation time. In this case, correlations between particle- and fluid-velocity gradients are strong, and caustics are induced by large, strain-dominated excursions of the fluid-velocity gradients. These excursions must cross a characteristic threshold in the plane spanned by the invariants Q Q and R R of the fluid-velocity gradients. Our method predicts that the most likely way to reach this threshold is by a unique “optimal fluctuation” that propagates along the Vieillefosse line, 27 ​ R 2 / 4 + Q 3 = 0 27R^{2}/4+Q^{3}=0 . We determine the shape of the optimal fluctuation as a function of time and show that it is dominant in numerical statistical-model simulations even for moderate particle inertia.



I
Introduction

In turbulent aerosols, particle inertia allows heavy particles to detach from the flow and generate folds over configuration space, so-called caustics
Crisanti et al. 1992
;
Falkovich et al. 2002
;
Wilkinson and Mehlig 2005
;
Meibohm et al. 2020
. Near such caustic folds, phase-space neighbourhoods partially focus onto configuration space, leading to transient divergencies of the spatial particle-number density, analogous to caustics in geometrical optics
Berry and Upstill 1980
.
At the same time, caustics delineate regions in configuration space where the particle velocities are multi valued, a phenomenon known as the sling effect
Falkovich et al. 2002
;
Falkovich and Pumir 2007
. It leads to anomalously large relative particle velocities
Falkovich et al. 2002
;
Bec et al. 2010
;
Salazar and Collins 2012
;
Gustavsson and Mehlig 2014
, increased collision rates
Völk et al. 1980
;
Sundaram and Collins 1997
;
Falkovich et al. 2002
;
Voßkuhle et al. 2014
;
Pumir and Wilkinson 2016
, and large collision velocities
Bec et al. 2005
;
Bec et al. 2010
;
Salazar and Collins 2012
;
Gustavsson and Mehlig 2014
. The latter can significantly affect collision outcomes
Wilkinson et al. 2008
;
Windmark et al. 2012
.

The process of caustic formation depends sensitively on the characteristic, dynamical time scales of the fluid and of the particles
Gustavsson and Mehlig 2016
. For a dilute, monodisperse suspension of dense, identical particles, there are three time scales: the Eulerian correlation time
\tau_{\text{c}}
of the flow, its Lagrangian correlation time
\tau_{\text{K}}
, measured along the paths of fluid elements, and the particle relaxation time
\tau_{\text{p}}
, the time it takes the inertial-particle velocity to relax back to the fluid velocity. The particle dynamics is characterised by two dimensionless numbers: The Stokes number
\text{St}\equiv\tau_{\text{p}}/\tau_{\text{K}}
is a dimensionless measure of particle inertia, while the Kubo number
\text{Ku}\equiv\tau_{\text{c}}/\tau_{\text{K}}
determines the degree of persistence of the flow.

The rate of caustic formation has been measured using direct numerical simulation of turbulence
Falkovich and Pumir 2007
;
Bhatnagar et al. 2022
and kinematic turbulence models
Ducasse and Pumir 2009
;
Meneguz and Reeks 2011
. Yet the mechanisms of caustic formation are understood only in certain idealised models and limiting cases. In the so-called white-noise limit
Gustavsson and Mehlig 2016
, the fluid velocities seen by the particles are assumed to fluctuate rapidly (
\text{Ku}\ll 1
), and particle inertia is assumed to be large (
\text{St}\gg 1
). For Gaussian random velocity fields in this limit, caustic formation can be described within a diffusion approximation which predicts that the particle dynamics depends sensitively on the (small) value of the Kubo number
Gustavsson and Mehlig 2016
.

Aerosol models with Gaussian random velocity fields that realistically mimic homogeneous and isotropic turbulence, by contrast, require Kubo numbers of order
10
, and are thus in a parameter regime where the particle dynamics depends only weakly upon
Ku

Gustavsson and Mehlig 2016
. This is a motivation for analysing the opposite, so-called persistent limit of large Kubo numbers and small Stokes numbers. It corresponds to slowly varying fluid velocities and short particle relaxation times.
Model calculations in one
Derevyanko et al. 2007
;
Meibohm et al. 2017
;
Meibohm and Mehlig 2019
and two
Meibohm et al. 2021
spatial dimensions show that caustics form in the persistent limit when the matrix of fluid-velocity gradients reaches a large threshold. Consequently, the most likely way for caustics to form in this limit is by rare, large fluctuations of the fluid-velocity gradients that exceed this threshold. For small
St
, these fluctuations are dominated by a single optimal fluctuation that can be calculated using methods from large deviation theory
Varadhan 1984
;
Freidlin and Wentzell 1984
;
Touchette 2009
, recently reviewed in, e.g.,
Grafke et al. 2015
;
Assaf and Meerson 2017
.

In this paper, we compute the optimal fluctuation of the fluid-velocity gradient matrix
\mathbb{A}
, and the corresponding most likely (optimal) path to caustic formation for an incompressible turbulent flow, by analysing a three-dimensional statistical model in the persistent limit. We first characterise
\mathbb{A}
in terms of its invariants
Q=-\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}^{2})/2
and
R=-\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}^{3})/3

Chong et al. 1990
;
Soria et al. 1994
and show that it must cross a characteristic threshold line in the
Q
-
R
plane for a caustic to form. We then employ large-deviation techniques
Grafke et al. 2015
;
Assaf and Meerson 2017
to determine how this threshold is reached. Our method predicts that the optimal fluctuation, given by the most likely way to reach the threshold, moves along the positive branch (
R>0
) of the so-called Vieillefosse line
Vieillefosse 1982
;
Vieillefosse 1984
;
Cantwell 1992
;
Meneveau 2011
,

\displaystyle\frac{27}{4}R^{2}+Q^{3}=0\,,

(1)

which forms the boundary between extensional and rotational flow configurations. The optimal fluctuation is vorticity-free and unique up to similarity transformations, but exact only for
\text{St}\ll 1
. Nevertheless, numerical simulations of a Gaussian statistical model show that it remains dominant even at
St
numbers as large as
\text{St}\approx 0.3
, suggesting a distinct way of caustic formation in three spatial dimensions that can be tested in experiments
Bewley et al. 2013
. In high-Reynolds-number turbulence, the probability distribution of Lagrangian fluid-velocity gradients is strongly skewed along the positive branch of the Vieillefosse line
Cantwell 1993
;
Chertkov et al. 1999
;
Chevillard and Meneveau 2006
. This means that caustic formation is expected to be significantly higher in actual turbulent flows compared with models that neglect this skewness.

II
Problem formulation

The dynamics of a spherical particle in a fluid-velocity field
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
is well approximated by Stokes’s law
Batchelor 2000

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bm{x}(t)=\bm{v}(t)\,,\qquad\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bm{v}(t)=\tau_{\text{p}}^{-1}\{\bm{u}[\bm{x}(t),t]-\bm{v}(t)\}\,,

(2)

given that the particle is small enough and much denser than the fluid. Here,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
denote particle position and velocity. The particle relaxation time
\tau_{\text{p}}=2a^{2}\rho_{\text{p}}/(9\rho_{\text{f}}\nu)
is a function of the particle size
a
, the kinematic viscosity
\nu
of the fluid, and of the particle and fluid densities,
\rho_{\text{p}}
and
\rho_{\text{f}}
, respectively.

In
d
spatial dimensions, a caustic occurs when the separation vectors between
d+1
nearby particles partially align, so that the spatial volume
\mathscr{\hat{V}}(t)=|\det\mathbb{J}(t)|
, occupied by the particles, momentarily collapses to zero. Here,
\mathbb{J}
denotes the spatial deformation tensor
J_{ij}[\bm{x}(t_{0}),t]=\partial x_{i}(t)/\partial x_{j}(t_{0})
. Caustic formation is closely related to the dynamics of the particle-velocity gradients
Falkovich et al. 2002
;
Gustavsson and Mehlig 2016
, written in dimensionless form as

\displaystyle\text{St}\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\mathbb{Z}}(t)=-\mathbb{Z}(t)-\mathbb{Z}(t)^{2}+\mathbb{A}(t)\,,

(3)

with
\mathbb{Z}(t_{0})=\mathbb{A}(t_{0})
initially. Here,
Z_{ij}(t)=\tau_{\text{p}}\partial v_{i}(t)/\partial x_{j}(t)
and
A_{ij}(t)=\tau_{\text{p}}\partial u_{i}(t)/\partial x_{j}(t)
, are dimensionless matrices of Lagrangian particle-velocity gradients and fluid-velocity gradients, respectively, evaluated at the particle position, i.e.,
\mathbb{Z}(t)=\mathbb{Z}[\bm{x}(t),t]
and
\mathbb{A}(t)=\mathbb{A}[\bm{x}(t),t]
. Time
t
in Eq. (
3
) is measured in units of the Kolmogorov time,
\tau_{\text{K}}=\tau_{\text{p}}\langle\text{Tr}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}^{\sf T}\rangle_{f}^{-1/2}
, where
\langle\ldots\rangle_{f}
denotes a Lagrangian average along the paths of fluid elements. The particle-velocity gradients
\mathbb{Z}
are related to the spatial volume
\mathscr{\hat{V}}(t)
through

\displaystyle\mathscr{\hat{V}}(t)=\mathscr{\hat{V}}(t_{0})\exp\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\!\!{\rm d}s\,\text{Tr}\mathbb{Z}(s)\,.

(4)

Consequently, a necessary condition for caustic formation,
\mathscr{\hat{V}}(t)\to 0
, is that
\text{Tr}\mathbb{Z}(t)
escapes to negative infinity.

In the small-
St
limit, the particle dynamics, Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), occurs on much shorter time scales than the dynamics of
\mathbb{A}
, so the problem can be treated using the persistent limit
Derevyanko et al. 2007
;
Meibohm and Mehlig 2019
;
Meibohm et al. 2021
. In this limit, changes of
\mathbb{A}
in Eq. (
2
) are adiabatic, i.e.,
\mathbb{A}
remains effectively constant
Meibohm and Mehlig 2019
;
Meibohm et al. 2021
as
\mathbb{Z}
varies. Whenever Eq. (
3
) has a globally attracting stable fixed point
\mathbb{Z}_{*}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{Z}_{*},\mathbb{A})\equiv-\mathbb{Z}_{*}-\mathbb{Z}_{*}^{2}+\mathbb{A}=0\,,

(5)

\mathbb{Z}
rapidly relaxes to
\mathbb{Z}_{*}
within a short transient time of order
St
. In this case,
\text{Tr}\mathbb{Z}
remains finite and no caustics form. In the absence of stable fixed points, by contrast, the dynamics drives the particle gradient matrix
\mathbb{Z}
to infinity, so
\text{Tr}\mathbb{Z}\to-\infty
, implying the formation of a caustic.

In summary, at small
St
caustics form through bifurcations of the fixed-point equation (
5
), induced by large but adiabatically slow excursions of
\mathbb{A}
. The probability and shape of these excursions are determined by optimal fluctuation theory
Grafke et al. 2015
;
Assaf and Meerson 2017
, which predicts the most likely excursion that leads to a bifurcation and thus to a caustic.

III
Fixed-point analysis

To find the bifurcations of the fixed points
\mathbb{Z}_{*}(\mathbb{A})
as
\mathbb{A}
changes adiabatically, we note that Eq. (
5
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{P}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{-1}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{P}^{-1}\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{A})\mathbb{P}\,,

(6)

where
\mathbb{P}
is an arbitrary invertible matrix. As a consequence, if
\mathbb{Z}_{*}(\mathbb{A})
is a fixed point, then so is
\mathbb{P}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}_{*}(\mathbb{P}^{-1}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{P})\mathbb{P}
. Due to the symmetry (
6
), the set of fixed points and their stability are invariant under similarity transformations
Golubitsky et al. 1988
, and thus only depend on the invariants of
\mathbb{A}
. For incompressible flow,
\mathbb{A}
is traceless and obeys the characteristic equation
Cayley 1858
;
Hamilton 1853

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{3}-\tfrac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\mathbb{A}-\det(\mathbb{A})\mathbb{1}=0

(7)

with invariants
Chong et al. 1990

\displaystyle Q(\mathbb{A})=-\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\,,\qquad R(\mathbb{A})=-\det(\mathbb{A})=-\frac{1}{3}\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}^{3})\,.

(8)

An analogous characteristic equation holds for
\mathbb{Z}_{*}
, but with a non-vanishing trace term. We characterise the fixed points
\mathbb{Z}_{*}
by evaluating
\cal Z_{*}\equiv\text{Tr}\mathbb{Z}_{*}
. To this end, we raise the fixed-point equation
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{Z}_{*}+\mathbb{Z}_{*}^{2}
to the second and third powers and take the trace. This gives
Q
and
R
in terms of
\text{Tr}(\mathbb{Z}_{*}^{n})
, with
n\leq 6
. From the characteristic equation of
\mathbb{Z}_{*}
we obtain, by multiplication with
\mathbb{Z}_{*}
and taking the trace, expressions for
\text{Tr}(\mathbb{Z}_{*}^{n})
that we substitute into the equations for
Q
and
R
. In this way, we end up with one single equation for
\mathcal{Z}_{*}
:

\displaystyle-16[2\mathcal{Z}_{*}+3]^{2}R=

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{Z}_{*}[\mathcal{Z}_{*}+1]^{2}[\mathcal{Z}_{*}+2]-4Q\right\}\left\{[\mathcal{Z}_{*}+1][\mathcal{Z}_{*}+2]^{2}[\mathcal{Z}_{*}+3]-4Q\right\}\,.

(9)

From Eq. (
9
) we conclude that possible fixed-point values
\mathcal{Z}_{*}
depend only on the invariants
Q
, and
R
of
\mathbb{A}
. Equation (
3
) is unstable in regions in the
Q
-
R
plane where Eq. (
9
) has no solution, allowing
\text{Tr}\mathbb{Z}
to escape to
-\infty
, so that a caustic forms.

In general, regions with different numbers of solutions of Eq. (
9
) (ranging from zero to eight, see discussion below) are separated by saddle-node bifurcations of
\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{Z}_{*},\mathbb{A})=0
, where fixed points are created or destroyed
Strogatz 2018
. Exceptions are bifurcations at isolated points or on symmetry lines where other, e.g. higher-order, bifurcations may occur
Strogatz 2018
. To find the locations of the saddle-node bifurcations in the
Q
-
R
plane, we introduce the coordinate
z_{*}=(\mathcal{Z}_{*}+3/2)^{2}
, because it turns Eq. (
9
) into a quartic polynomial equation in
z_{*}
. Saddle-node bifurcations occur at the simultaneous positive roots of this polynomial and of its derivative with respect to
z_{*}
. Solving for these roots, we find two bifurcation lines, shown as solid lines in Fig.
1
. The first one (orange line in Fig.
1
) is the Vieillefosse line (
1
) for
R\leq 1/256
which distinguishes rotational and extensional regions in the flow.
We note that its positive branch (
R>0
) plays an important role for the Lagrangian dynamics of fluid-velocity gradients. Vieillefosse
Vieillefosse 1982
;
Vieillefosse 1984
showed that Lagrangian fluid-velocity gradients described by the inviscid Navier-Stokes equations self-amplify along the positive branch of Eq. (
1
). When the anisotropic portion of the pressure Hessian is neglected, this self-amplification leads to a finite-time divergence of Lagrangian fluid-velocity gradients along this branch
Vieillefosse 1982
;
Vieillefosse 1984
;
Cantwell 1992
. Although the divergence does not occur in more realistic approximations
Girimaji and Pope 1990
;
Chertkov et al. 1999
;
Chevillard and Meneveau 2006
, the self-amplification effect explains why the distribution of Lagrangian fluid-velocity gradients in Navier-Stokes turbulence is strongly skewed towards the positive branch of Eq. (
1
)
Meneveau 2011
.

The second bifurcation curve (red line in Fig.
1
) is given by

\displaystyle Q=4R-1/16\,\quad\text{for}\quad R\geq-1/32\,.

(10)

The two bifurcation lines meet at
R=-1/32
(not shown) and at
R=1/256
(red square in Fig.
1
). They divide the
Q
-
R
plane into regions where Eq. (
9
) has different numbers of solutions.

Figure 1:
Phase diagram with regions of different numbers of solutions of Eq. (
9
) as a function of
Q
and
R
. The lines show the bifurcations described in the text. The green square
(R,Q)=(0,0)
shows the most likely gradient configuration at steady state, while for infinitesimal particle inertia the threshold (
10
) is reached at the red square
(R,Q)=(1/256,-3/64)
, see Sec.
IV
.

In addition, transcritical bifurcations, which conserve the number of fixed points
Strogatz 2018
, occur on the symmetry line (broken lines in Fig.
1
):

\displaystyle Q=-3/64\,,

(11)

where the
z_{*}
derivative of Eq. (
9
) vanishes. Importantly, the bifurcation line (
10
) separates the region without any fixed points (yellow region) from the rest of the
Q
-
R
plane (blue and crosshatched regions). The latter regions contain a finite number of fixed points and we find numerically that one of them is always stable and smoothly connected to
\mathbb{Z}_{*}\approx\mathbb{A}
close to the origin.

In summary, at small
St
we can invoke time-scale separation. While
\mathbb{Z}
relaxes rapidly,
\mathbb{A}
changes slowly. As soon as
\mathbb{A}
crosses the threshold line (
10
) and enters the yellow region in Fig.
1
, the
\mathbb{Z}
dynamics becomes unstable. As a result, a caustic forms since
\text{Tr}\mathbb{Z}(t)\to-\infty
in a short time.

IV
Optimal fluctuation

At small particle inertia caustics are rare, because typical fluctuations of
\mathbb{A}
along particle paths are of order
\text{St}\ll 1
, much smaller than the threshold (
10
) which is of order unity. While there are typically many ways of similar probability to realise a typical fluctuation (here of order
\text{St}\ll 1
), this is not the case for rare, large fluctuations. Instead, it is a general theme in large-deviation theory
Varadhan 1984
;
Freidlin and Wentzell 1984
;
Touchette 2009
that rare events (here: fluctuations of order unity) are often dominated by unique, optimal fluctuations, that correspond to the most likely realisations of these events. Here we use large-deviation techniques developed to characterise such rare events
Grafke et al. 2015
;
Assaf and Meerson 2017
to determine the optimal fluctuation for
\mathbb{A}
to reach the threshold, starting from the most likely initial value
Q=R=0
.

IV.1
Matrix basis

In homogeneous and isotropic turbulence, the elements
A_{ij}
of
\mathbb{A}
are correlated
Falkovich et al. 2001
which makes calculations cumbersome. It is therefore convenient to represent
\mathbb{A}
in a basis of matrices
\mathbb{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{e}_{8}
,
\mathbb{A}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{8}A_{i}(t)\mathbb{e}_{i}
, chosen in such a way that the random processes
A_{i}(t)
are uncorrelated. The basis we use in the following is orthonormal with respect to the inner product
\langle\mathbb{M},\mathbb{N}\rangle\equiv\text{Tr}(\mathbb{MN})/2
such that
\langle\mathbb{e}_{i},\mathbb{e}_{j}\rangle=g_{ij}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&&&&&&&\\
&-1&&&&&&\\
&&-1&&&&&\\
&&&\phantom{-}1&&&&\\
&&&&\phantom{-}1&&&\\
&&&&&\phantom{-}1&&\\
&&&&&&\phantom{-}1&\\
&&&&&&&\phantom{-}1\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

The basis elements
\mathbb{e}_{1}
,
\mathbb{e}_{2}
, and
\mathbb{e}_{3}
span the space of traceless antisymmetric matrices and can be written

\displaystyle\mathbb{e}_{1}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{e}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{e}_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(13)

The remaining
\mathbb{e}_{i},i=4,\ldots,8
, spanning the space of traceless symmetric matrices, read

\displaystyle\mathbb{e}_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{e}_{5}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{e}_{6}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{e}_{7}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{e}_{8}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-2\end{pmatrix}\,.

(14)

Consequently, the antisymmetric (vorticity) and symmetric (strain) parts of
\mathbb{A}
are expanded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}O_{i}(t)\mathbb{e}_{i}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{S}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{5}S_{i}(t)\mathbb{e}_{i+3}\,,

(15)

respectively, where
O_{i}=A_{i}
and
S_{i}=A_{i+3}
. Note that since
\mathbb{e}_{i}^{\sf T}=\sum_{j=1}^{8}g_{ij}\mathbb{e}_{j}
, one has
\langle\mathbb{e}_{i},\mathbb{e}_{i}^{\sf T}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
. In addition to
\mathbb{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{e}_{8}
a complete matrix basis also includes the unit matrix
\mathbb{e}_{0}=\mathbb{1}
, which is, however, not needed in the following, because
\mathbb{A}
is traceless.

IV.2
Correlation functions of fluid-velocity gradients

For homogeneous and isotropic turbulence, the entries of
\mathbb{O}
and
\mathbb{S}
have zero mean and correlation functions
Brunk et al. 1997
;
Zaichik and Alipchenkov 2003

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{O}_{ik}(t)\mathbb{O}_{jl}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{p}=\sigma_{O}^{2}C^{O}_{ijkl}f_{O}(t-t^{\prime})\,,\qquad\langle\mathbb{S}_{ik}(t)\mathbb{S}_{jl}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{p}=\sigma_{S}^{2}C^{S}_{ijkl}f_{S}(t-t^{\prime})\,,\qquad\langle\mathbb{O}_{ik}(t)\mathbb{S}_{jl}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{p}=0\,,

(16)

where the angular brackets
\langle\cdot\rangle_{p}
denote a steady-state average along inertial-particle trajectories. The correlation functions
f_{O}
and
f_{S}
are normalised such that
f_{O}(0)=f_{S}(0)=1
, and are well approximated by exponential functions with different correlation times of order
\tau_{\text{K}}

Girimaji and Pope 1990
;
Vincenzi et al. 2007
. The structure of the rotationally covariant tensors
C^{S}
and
C^{O}
in Eqs. (
16
) follows from isotropy and incompressibility
Girimaji and Pope 1990
;
Brunk et al. 1997
:

\displaystyle C^{O}_{ijkl}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\,,\qquad C^{S}_{ijkl}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\,.

(17)

Along the paths of fluid elements, the Lagrangian flow is homogeneous, which implies that
Girimaji and Pope 1990
;
Brunk et al. 1997

\displaystyle\langle\text{Tr}[\mathbb{O}^{\!\sf T}\!\!(t)\mathbb{O}(t)]\rangle_{f}=\langle\text{Tr}[\mathbb{S}^{\!\sf T}\!\!(t)\mathbb{S}(t)]\rangle_{f}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\text{Tr}[\mathbb{A}^{\!\sf T}\!\!(t)\mathbb{A}(t)]\rangle_{f}=\frac{\text{St}^{2}}{2}\,.

(18)

Along inertial particle trajectories, however, preferential concentration
Squires and Eaton 1991
;
Eaton and Fessler 1994
breaks homogeneity
Zaichik and Alipchenkov 2009
, so that
\langle\text{Tr}[\mathbb{O}^{\!\sf T}\!\!(t)\mathbb{O}(t)]\rangle_{p}<\langle\text{Tr}[\mathbb{S}^{\!\sf T}\!\!(t)\mathbb{S}(t)]\rangle_{p}

Maxey 1987
. We therefore write the variances
\sigma_{O}
and
\sigma_{S}
in Eq. (
15
) as

\displaystyle\sigma_{O}^{2}=\frac{1}{6}\text{St}^{2}C_{O}(\text{St})\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}^{2}=\frac{1}{10}\text{St}^{2}C_{S}(\text{St})\,,

(19)

where
C_{O}(\text{St})\leq 1/2
and
C_{S}(\text{St})\geq 1/2
are
St
-dependent functions with
\lim_{\text{St}\to 0}C_{O}(\text{St})=\lim_{\text{St}\to 0}C_{S}(\text{St})=1/2
in the inertialess limit. The prefactors in Eq. (
19
) account for the three and five independent components of
\mathbb{O}
and
\mathbb{S}
, respectively. In terms of the orthonormal basis introduced in Sec.
IV.1
, the correlation functions (
16
) are conveniently expressed as

\displaystyle\langle O_{i}(t)O_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{p}=\delta_{ij}\sigma^{2}_{O}f_{\text{O}}(t-t^{\prime})\,,\qquad\langle S_{i}(t)S_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{p}=\delta_{ij}\sigma^{2}_{S}f_{\text{S}}(t-t^{\prime})\,,\qquad\langle O_{i}(t)S_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{p}=0\,.

(20)

Equation (
20
) makes explicit that the elements
A_{i}(t)
are mutually uncorrelated processes with variances
\sigma_{O}
and
\sigma_{S}
.

IV.3
Gaussian approximation

The complete statistical information about
\mathbb{A}(t)
is contained in the cumulant-generating functional
Monin and Yaglom 1971
of the process

\displaystyle\Lambda(\mathbb{G},t)=

\displaystyle\ln\left\langle\exp\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\!\text{d}s\langle\mathbb{A}(s),\mathbb{G}(s)\rangle\right\}\right\rangle_{p}\,.

(21)

Here
\mathbb{G}
is a traceless matrix-valued test function. An expansion in powers of
\mathbb{G}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{8}G_{i}(s)\mathbb{e}_{i}
generates the cumulants of
\mathbb{A}(t)
, which we write in terms of
A_{i}(t)
. Since the correlation functions for the processes
A_{i}(t)
decouple in the orthonormal matrix basis
\mathbb{e}_{i}
[see Eq. (
20
)] we find to second order in
\mathbb{G}

\displaystyle\Lambda(\mathbb{G},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{8}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\!\text{d}s\,\text{d}s^{\prime}\sigma_{i}^{2}f_{i}(s-s^{\prime})G_{i}(s)G_{i}(s^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{G}^{3})\,,

(22)

with
\sigma^{2}_{i}f_{i}(t-t^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{O}f_{O}(t-t^{\prime})
for
i=1,\ldots,3
and
\sigma^{2}_{i}f_{i}(t-t^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{S}f_{S}(t-t^{\prime})
for
i=4,\ldots,8
. The orders
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{G}^{3})
in Eq. (
22
) contain terms of the form
\langle A_{i}(s)A_{j}(t)A_{k}(u)\rangle_{p}
,
\langle A_{i}(s)A_{j}(t)A_{k}(u)A_{l}(v)\rangle_{p}
, and so on. In a mean-field (or Gaussian) approximation, we decompose the higher order correlation functions in terms of the second and first-order ones. This gives
\langle A_{i}(s)A_{j}(t)A_{k}(u)\rangle_{p}\approx\langle A_{i}(s)A_{j}(t)\rangle_{p}\langle A_{k}(u)\rangle_{p}+\ldots=0
and

\displaystyle\langle A_{i}(s)A_{j}(t)A_{k}(u)A_{l}(v)\rangle_{p}\approx\langle A_{i}(s)A_{j}(t)\rangle_{p}\langle A_{k}(u)A_{l}(v)\rangle_{p}+\ldots\,,

(23)

where the ellipsis denotes all permutations of the arguments. Applied to all orders, this approximation makes the terms
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{G}^{3})
in Eq. (
22
) vanish so that the individual processes
A_{i}(t)
become Gaussian. Although the fluid-velocity gradients in the dissipation range of isotropic and homogeneous turbulence are well known not to be Gaussian
Monin and Yaglom 1975
;
Frisch 1997
;
Pope 2000
, Gaussian models for turbulence have proven to provide valuable information about the behaviour of turbulent aerosols
Zaichik and Alipchenkov 2009
;
Gustavsson and Mehlig 2016
;
Pumir and Wilkinson 2016
. Uncorrelated Gaussian processes are independent, as can be seen from the fact that
\Lambda(\mathbb{G},t)
decomposes completely when the
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{G}^{3})
term in Eq. (
22
) is absent. The mutual independence of the processes
A_{i}(t)
is a crucial ingredient for the calculations that follow.

IV.4
Threshold probability

For the stationary Gaussian process
A_{i}(t)
, the probability for reaching a given value
A_{i}
at a given time
t
, reads

\displaystyle P[A_{i}(t)=A_{i}]\propto\text{e}^{-S(A_{i})/\sigma_{i}^{2}}\,,

(24)

where we omitted the normalisation prefactor. Here,
S(A_{i})=A_{i}^{2}/2
denotes the quadratic action associated with reaching
A_{i}
. Due to the mutual independence of the
A_{i}(t)
, we obtain the probability for
\mathbb{A}
to reach
\mathbb{A}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{8}A_{i}\mathbb{e}_{i}
as the product of Eq. (
24
) over
i
,

\displaystyle P[\mathbb{A}(t)=\mathbb{A}]=\prod_{i=1}^{8}P[A_{i}(t)=A_{i}]\propto\text{e}^{-S(\mathbb{A})/\sigma_{S}^{2}}\,,

(25)

characterised by the weighed sum
S(\mathbb{A})
of actions
S(A_{i})
over
i
:

\displaystyle S(\mathbb{A})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{S}}{\sigma_{O}}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}A_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=4}^{8}A_{i}^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{S}}{\sigma_{O}}\right)^{2}\!\left\langle\mathbb{O},\!\mathbb{O}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathbb{S},\mathbb{S}\right\rangle\,.

(26)

Since
\sigma_{S},\sigma_{O}\sim\text{St}
, Eq. (
25
) and (
26
) imply that the exponent in Eq. (
25
) is proportional to
\text{St}^{-2}
for small values of
St
. Also note that
S(\mathbb{A})
is quadratic in
A_{i}
. Both observations are a consequence of approximating
A_{i}(t)
as Gaussian processes.

IV.5
Optimal threshold configuration

To obtain the most likely way for the fluid-velocity gradients to reach the threshold (
10
) and induce a caustic, we must minimise the action (
26
) under the constraint (
10
). To this end, we multiply the constraint with a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
, and
add the product to
S(\mathbb{A})
to form the Lagrange function

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A})=

\displaystyle S(\mathbb{A})-\lambda\left[Q(\mathbb{A})-4R(\mathbb{A})+\frac{1}{16}\right]\,.

(27)

Minimising
\mathscr{L}
over
\mathbb{A}
gives the minimiser
\mathbb{A}^{*}
, the most likely configuration of fluid-velocity gradients at the threshold (
10
).
The symmetry of
\mathbb{F}
under similarity transformations [see Eq. (
6
)] constrains this minimiser. In order to deduce how,
we consider infinitesimal similarity transformations
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A^{\prime}}=\mathbb{P}^{-1}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{P}
with
\det\mathbb{P}=1
, generated by traceless matrices

\displaystyle\mathbb{A^{\prime}}\approx\mathbb{A}+\delta\mathbb{A}\,,\qquad\delta\mathbb{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{8}[\mathbb{A},\mathbb{e}_{i}]\theta_{i}\,,

(28)

where
[\mathbb{M},\mathbb{N}]=\mathbb{MN}-\mathbb{NM}
denotes the commutator between two matrices, and the factors
|\theta_{i}|\ll 1
parametrise the infinitesimal transformation. For the symmetric and antisymmetric parts of
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
, the transformation (
28
) implies
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}=\mathbb{S}+\delta\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{O}^{\prime}=\mathbb{O}+\delta\mathbb{O}
with

\displaystyle\delta\mathbb{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}[\mathbb{S},\mathbb{e}_{i}]\theta_{i}+\sum_{i=4}^{8}[\mathbb{O},\mathbb{e}_{i}]\theta_{i}\,,\qquad\delta\mathbb{O}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}[\mathbb{O},\mathbb{e}_{i}]\theta_{i}+\sum_{i=4}^{8}[\mathbb{S},\mathbb{e}_{i}]\theta_{i}\,.

(29)

The Lagrange function
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A})
in Eq. (27) must be invariant under similarity transformations of the minimizer
\mathbb{A}^{*}
, i.e.,
\delta\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A})=\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A^{\prime}})-\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{A})=0
at
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}^{*}
, which requires that
\delta S(\mathbb{A})=S(\mathbb{A}^{\prime})-S(\mathbb{A})=0
at the minimiser. Applying the infinitesimal transformation (
28
) to
S(\mathbb{A})
, however, we find

\displaystyle\delta S(\mathbb{A})=\left[\left(\frac{\sigma_{S}}{\sigma_{O}}\right)^{2}+1\right]\sum_{i=4}^{8}\left\langle[\mathbb{S},\mathbb{O}],\mathbb{e}_{i}\right\rangle\theta_{i}\,.

(30)

This expression vanishes, i.e.,
S(\mathbb{A})
is invariant, only in two cases: First,
\theta_{i}=0
for
i=4,\ldots,8
which corresponds to the case when
\mathbb{P}
is a rotation
\mathbb{P}^{\sf T}=\mathbb{P}^{-1}
. The second case is
[\mathbb{S},\mathbb{O}]=0
which occurs when either
\mathbb{O}=0
or
\mathbb{S}=0
. It can be checked that the constraint (
10
) is invariant under all similarity transformations, not only rotations, because
\delta Q(\mathbb{A})=\delta R(\mathbb{A})=0
for all
\theta_{i}
. Hence, the constraint requires that
\delta S(\mathbb{A})=0
also for finite
\theta_{i}
,
i=4,\ldots,8
, meaning that either
\mathbb{O}=0
or
\mathbb{S}=0
. However,
\mathbb{S}=0
, and thus
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{O}
, leads to
R(\mathbb{O})=0
and
Q(\mathbb{O})\geq 0
, so the constraint (
10
) cannot be satisfied for any real
\mathbb{O}
. This in turn, shows that the vorticity contribution
\mathbb{O}^{*}
to the optimal threshold configuration
\mathbb{A}^{*}
must vanish,
\mathbb{O}^{*}=0
, and that
\mathbb{A}^{*}
must be a pure strain
\mathbb{A}^{*}=\mathbb{S}^{*}
.

In order to determine the components of
\mathbb{S}^{*}
, we express
\mathbb{A}
in Eq. (
27
) in terms of
A_{i}
, and minimise Eq. (
27
) over
A_{4},\ldots,A_{8}
, while
A^{*}_{1}=A_{2}^{*}=A^{*}_{3}=0
. This leads to two solutions with equal actions
S(\mathbb{A}^{*})=3/64
, which fix the minimising configuration
A^{*}_{i}
up to equivalence under similarity transformations. As a consequence of this symmetry, the minimisers are not isolated points but higher-dimensional manifolds. All minimisers found in this way are related by similarity transformations, so that we may choose particular representatives to gain insight into the most likely threshold configuration
\mathbb{A}^{*}
. The simplest representatives correspond to diagonal
\mathbb{A}^{*}
, and they show that
\mathbb{A}^{*}
takes two positive eigenvalues equal to
1/8
, and one negative eigenvalue equal to
-1/4
of twice the magnitude. Similarity transformations only affect the order of eigenvalues, so we may put all minimisers into the ordered diagonal form

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{*}=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1/2&&\\
&1/2&\\
&&-1\end{pmatrix}

(31)

by a suitable transformation. Expressed in the matrix basis, this corresponds to the simple configuration
A^{*}_{8}=(1/4)\sqrt{3}/2
, and
A^{*}_{1}=A^{*}_{2}=\ldots=A^{*}_{7}=0
.

Up to this point, our analysis provides the most likely threshold configuration that induces a caustic in the small-
St
limit. The strict limit, however, is difficult to approach in numerical simulations, since the threshold probability (
25
) is exponentially suppressed in
St
.

IV.6
Small-
St
correction

We now discuss how to adjust the theory to incorporate the main next-to-leading order effects in
\text{St}\ll 1
, in an approach similar to that used in two spatial dimensions in Ref.
Meibohm et al. 2021
.
Our starting point is Eq. (
3
). Since caustics form on time scales of order
St
, we require the threshold (
10
) to be exceeded for a finite time, such as to leave the dynamics (
3
) sufficient time to form a caustic
Meibohm et al. 2021
. To account for this, we introduce a
St
-dependent threshold
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})=1/4+o(\text{St}^{0})

Meibohm et al. 2021
that consists of the threshold determined above plus a small positive correction
o(\text{St}^{0})
that vanishes as
\text{St}\to 0
. Although this
o(\text{St}^{0})
correction slightly enhances the threshold
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})
, the rate at which trajectories exceed
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})
nevertheless increases as
St
increases, because
\sigma_{S}^{2}\sim\text{St}^{2}
in Eq. (
25
).
Recently, Bätge
et al.

Bätge et al. 2022
described a one-dimensional model for caustic formation where the time needed for caustics to form is accounted for by a
St
-dependent pre-exponential factor, instead of a
St
-correction to the action
S(\mathbb{A}^{*})
as introduced here.

We make the ansatz

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{*}=A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})\begin{pmatrix}1/2&&\\
&1/2&\\
&&-1\end{pmatrix}

(32)

for the gradient matrix, i.e.,
A^{*}_{8}=\sqrt{3}A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})/2
,
A^{*}_{1}=\dots=A^{*}_{7}=0
, leading to the
St
-dependent threshold line

\displaystyle Q=4R-A_{\text{th}}^{2}(\text{St})\left[A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})+3/4\right]\,,

(33)

which reduces to Eq. (
10
) as
\text{St}\to 0
. The magnitude of the difference
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})-1/4
determines by how much the gradient threshold
1/4
is exceeded at the time
t=t_{\text{th}}
when the gradient excursion peaks, and through the dynamics (
3
), it also fixes the time
t_{\text{c}}>t_{\text{th}}
at which the caustic is generated. Using this information and input from numerical simulations, we model the optimal fluctuation to next-to-leading order in
St
.

IV.7
Optimal path to caustic formation

In order to find the optimal path to caustic formation, we must understand how the optimal fluctuation connects typical values of
\mathbb{A}\sim\text{St}
to the optimal threshold configuration
\mathbb{A}^{*}\sim 1
as a function of time. To make this connection, we use that the most likely way
A^{*}_{i}(t)
for a Gaussian process
A_{i}(t)
to reach a large value
A_{i}\gg\sigma_{i}
at a given time
t
is equal to the correlation function (
20
) of the process, normalised to the threshold value
Meibohm et al. 2021
, i.e.,

\displaystyle A^{*}_{i}(t)=A_{i}f_{i}(t-t_{\text{th}})\,.

(34)

We give a derivation of this formula in the Appendix. Since
A^{*}_{8}\gg\sigma_{S}
for the optimal threshold configuration (
32
), we may use Eq. (
34
) to determine the optimal fluctuation
A_{i}^{*}(t)
of the fluid velocity gradients to reach the threshold. This leads to
A^{*}_{8}(t)=\sqrt{3}A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})f_{S}(t-t_{\text{th}})/2
and
A^{*}_{1}(t)=\ldots=A^{*}_{7}(t)=0
. Denoting by
\lambda_{1}(t)>\lambda_{2}(t)>\lambda_{3}(t)
the ordered eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}^{*}(t)
along the optimal fluctuation, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{*}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}(t)&&\\
&\lambda_{2}(t)&\\
&&\lambda_{3}(t)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(35)

we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(t)=\lambda_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{2}A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})f_{S}(t-t_{\text{th}})\,,\qquad\lambda_{3}(t)=-A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})f_{S}(t-t_{\text{th}})

(36)

so that
\mathbb{A}^{*}(t_{\text{th}})=\mathbb{A}^{*}
at the time the threshold is reached [c.f. Eq. (
32
)]. The optimal path to caustic formation
\mathbb{Z}^{*}(t)
for the particle-velocity gradients is now obtained by substituting
\mathbb{A}^{*}(t)
into Eq. (
3
). Since
\mathbb{A}^{*}(t)
is vorticity-free and diagonal in the chosen coordinates,
\mathbb{Z}^{*}(t)
is initially diagonal,
\mathbb{Z}^{*}(t_{0})=\mathbb{A}^{*}(t_{0})
. The initial time
t_{0}
is chosen such that
|t_{\text{c}}-t_{0}|
is of the order of the expected time for caustic formation, which implies
|t_{\text{c}}-t_{0}|\sim\exp[S(\mathbb{A}^{*})/\text{St}^{2}]\gg 1
for the model considered here. Since Eq. (
3
) decouples for diagonal matrices,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{*}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta_{1}(t)&&\\
&\zeta_{2}(t)&\\
&&\zeta_{3}(t)\end{pmatrix}

(37)

remains diagonal along the optimal path to caustic formation. The dynamics of the eigenvalues
\zeta_{i}(t)
is then governed by the equations

\displaystyle\text{St}\,\dot{\zeta}_{i}(t)=-\zeta_{i}(t)-\zeta_{i}^{2}(t)+\lambda_{i}(t)\,,

(38)

with
i=1,2,3
. In words, along the optimal fluctuation, the nine-dimensional matrix equation (
3
) reduces to three uncoupled equations for the eigenvalues
\zeta_{i}
, akin to the one-dimensional equation studied in Refs.
Wilkinson and Mehlig 2003
;
Derevyanko et al. 2007
.
The theory summarised here demonstrates that the process of caustic formation in the persistent limit is essentially one dimensional, as shown for two dimensions in Ref.
Meibohm et al. 2021
and as argued in Ref.
Bätge et al. 2022
. We also see that the leading negative eigenvalue of
\mathbb{A}^{*}
must be smaller than
-1/4
(in our dimensionless formulation) when
\text{St}\to 0
.

We solve Eqs. (
38
) numerically for a given normalised strain correlation function
f_{S}(t)
[see Eq. (
36
)] that we extract from numerical simulations (Sec.
V
). The threshold value
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})
is then obtained from (
38
) by tuning
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})
to match the time difference
t_{\text{c}}-t_{\text{th}}(\text{St})
for the optimal fluctuation with the numerical value. The optimal fluctuation in the
Q
-
R
plane reads

\displaystyle Q(t)=-\frac{3}{4}A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})^{2}f_{S}^{2}[t-t_{\text{th}}(\text{St})]\,,\qquad R(t)=\frac{1}{4}A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})^{3}f_{S}^{3}[t-t_{\text{th}}(\text{St})]\,.

(39)

For
\text{St}\to 0
, we have
|t_{\text{c}}-t_{0}|\to\infty
and
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})\to 1/4
so that Eqs. (
39
) in this case represent an infinite-time trajectory that connects the green and red squares in Fig.
1
, along the right branch of the Vieillefosse line. For finite
St
, the optimal fluctuation remains on this line, but it now penetrates the yellow region in Fig.
1
for a short time, because
A_{\text{th}}(\text{St})>1/4
, and relaxes back to the origin for
t>t_{\text{th}}
.

Note that the Vieillefosse line separates regions in the
Q
-
R
plane where
\mathbb{A}
has real eigenvalues, from regions where two eigenvalues form a complex conjugate pair
Chong et al. 1990
. Thus, on the Vieillefosse line, all eigenvalues are real, and two eigenvalues must be degenerate. Our theory predicts that caustics form when a single eigenvalue of
\mathbb{A}
becomes large and negative, while the other two remain degenerate and positive, with half the magnitude of the negative eigenvalue. For this reason and because the large negative eigenvalue implies
R>0
, this entails that the optimal fluctuation must propagate along the positive branch of the Vieillefosse line for small
St
.

VI
Conclusions

We explained caustic formation in turbulent aerosols for small particle inertia (
\text{St}\ll 1
) in three spatial dimensions by means of optimal fluctuation theory. We found that caustics are formed by an instability of spatial particle neighbourhoods that occurs when the fluid-velocity gradients exceed a large threshold in the
Q
-
R
plane. The most likely way to reach this threshold is by an optimal fluctuation of the fluid-velocity gradients consisting of a large excursion of the strain
\mathbb{S}
, while vorticity
\mathbb{O}
remains small. We determined the shape of the excursion explicitly within a mean-field (Gaussian) approximation. The theory predicts that the optimal fluctuation propagates along the positive branch of the Vieillefosse line
Vieillefosse 1982
;
Vieillefosse 1984
;
Cantwell 1992
to reach the threshold, before it relaxes back to the origin. Using numerical simulations of a Gaussian statistical model, we showed that the optimal fluctuation is dominant not only at infinitesimal
St
, but also at values of order
\text{St}\sim 10^{-1}
, where we observed qualitative agreement with the theory.

The predominance of the strain part in the optimal fluctuation qualitatively explains why collisions are preceded by strong strains and low vorticity in simulations
Perrin and Jonker 2014
. More generally, our fixed-point analysis shows that at small
St
, the particle dynamics enters caustic formation only by posing a threshold for the fluid-velocity gradients. This implies that the rate of caustic formation may be estimated solely from the threshold probability (
25
), without explicitly referring to the particle dynamics, rather than following local particle neighbourhoods over long times, as in Refs.
Falkovich and Pumir 2007
;
Bhatnagar et al. 2022
;
Bewley et al. 2013
.

Finally, our analysis indicates that the tear-shaped elongation of the probability distribution of turbulent fluid-velocity gradients along the positive branch of the Vieillefosse line
Cantwell 1993
;
Chertkov et al. 1999
;
Chevillard and Meneveau 2006
;
Meneveau 2011
may facilitate caustic formation in homogeneous and isotropic turbulence, as it leads to an increased probability of reaching the threshold, perhaps even more so if one takes into account preferential concentration
Johnson and Meneveau 2017
.

24
),

\displaystyle P[A_{i}(t_{\text{th}})=A_{i}]=\langle\delta[A_{i}(t_{\text{th}})-A_{i}]\rangle_{p}=\int\!\!\!\mathscr{D}A_{i}(t)\exp\left\{-\frac{\mathscr{S}[A_{i}(t)]}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\}\delta[A_{i}(t_{\text{th}})-A_{i}]\,,

(40)

where
\delta(x)
denotes the Dirac delta function. For Gaussian processes, the action
\mathscr{S}[A_{i}(t)]
is explicitly known
Van Kampen 2007

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}[A_{i}(t)]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}s\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}\,tA_{i}(s)f_{i}^{-1}(s-t)A_{i}(t)\,.

(41)

Here,
f^{-1}(x)
is the inverse of the normalised correlation function
f_{i}(x)
of
A_{i}(t)
, defined as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}s^{\prime}f_{i}^{-1}(s-s^{\prime})f_{i}(s^{\prime}-t)=\delta(s-t)\,.

(42)

For
A_{i}\gg\sigma_{i}
, the path integral in Eq. (
40
) can be approximated by the saddle point of the action
\mathscr{S}[A_{i}(t)]
under the constraint that
A_{i}(t_{\text{th}})=A_{i}
[enforced by the delta function in Eq. (
40
)]. We perform the constrained minimisation by adding a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
to
\mathscr{S}[A_{i}(t)]
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{\lambda}[A_{i}(t)]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}s\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}\,tA(s)f_{i}^{-1}(s-t)A(t)-\lambda\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\text{d}s\,\delta(t_{\text{th}}-s)A(s)-A_{i}\right]\,.

(43)

For computational convenience, we have expressed the constraint
A_{i}(t_{\text{th}})=A_{i}
as an integral involving a Dirac delta function, as was done in a different context in Ref.
Meerson 2022
. We now compute the optimal fluctuation
A^{*}_{i}(t)
as the constrained saddle point
\delta\mathscr{S}_{\lambda}[A^{*}_{i}(t)]=0
of the path integral in Eq. (
40
). The variation of Eq. (
43
) over
A_{i}(t)
gives

\displaystyle\delta\mathscr{S}_{\lambda}[A^{*}_{i}(t)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}s\,\delta A_{i}(s)\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}t\,f_{i}^{-1}(s-t)A_{i}^{*}(t)-\lambda\delta(t_{\text{th}}-s)\right]\,,

(44)

which leads to the saddle point equation

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}t\,f_{i}^{-1}(s-t)A_{i}^{*}(t)=\lambda\delta(t_{\text{th}}-s)\,.

(45)

Comparison with Eq. (
42
) immediately gives

\displaystyle A_{i}^{*}(t)=\lambda f_{i}(t-t_{\text{th}})\,.

(46)

The Lagrange parameter
\lambda
is now simply obtained by using the constraint
A_{i}^{*}(t_{\text{th}})=A_{i}
and the normalisation condition
f_{i}(0)=1
. This gives
\lambda=A_{i}
and thus the optimal fluctuation (
34
). We evaluate the action
\mathscr{S}[A_{i}(t)]
at the saddle point by using the optimal fluctuation (
34
) and obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}[A^{*}_{i}(t)]=\frac{A_{i}^{2}}{2}\,.

(47)

Hence, we recover the quadratic action in Eq. (
24
) from Eq. (
40
), evaluated at the optimal fluctuation (
34
).
