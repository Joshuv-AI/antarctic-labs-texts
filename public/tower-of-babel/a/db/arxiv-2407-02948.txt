Information Greenhouse: Optimal Persuasion for Medical Test-Avoiders
Zhuo Chen

Abstract
Patients often delay or reject medical tests due to information avoidance, which hinders timely reception of necessary treatments. This paper studies the optimal information policy to persuade an information-avoidant patient to undergo the test and make the best choice that maximizes his health. The patient sequentially decides whether to take the test and the optimal treatment plan. The information provided is about the background knowledge of the disease, and disclosure can take place both before and after the test decision. The optimal information policy depends on whether the patient is willing to be tested when he is completely pessimistic. If so, the optimal policy features warning-in-advance : the disclosure only takes place before the test, and the bad news guarantees the patient to be tested and be treated even without further information. If not, the optimal policy constructs an information greenhouse : an information structure that provides high anticipatory utility is committed when the patient is tested and the test result is bad. Extensions to ex ante participation constraint and general information preference are also considered. JEL classification : C72, D82, D91. Keywords : Dynamic information design; information incentives; information avoidance; anticipatory utility; doctor-patient relationship.

Introduction

Evidence from medicine, psychology, and economics shows that
information avoidance
, the tendency that people deliberately delay or refuse to acquire (free but) potentially useful information,
1
1

1

Information avoidance is also called
information aversion
by other papers. In a decision-theoretic perspective, it indicates a preference for late resolution of uncertainty. I select information avoidance as the name because in some other studies information aversion is used to represent a preference for one-shot resolution of uncertainty, e.g.
Andries and
Haddad 2020
.
is a significant reason preventing people to participate in necessary medical tests on time (See
Golman
et al. 2017
,
Sweeny
et al. 2010
and
Hertwig and
Engel 2016
for reviews of empirical evidence).
For instance, while the early detection of breast cancer is widely recognized to have a deterministic impact on a patient’s five-year survival rate, evidence still suggests that information avoidance leads many women to delay or resist routine mammography
(
Caplan 1995
,
Meechan
et al. 2002
,
Melnyk and
Shepperd 2012
,
Zanella and
Banerjee 2016
, for example)
.
Undoubtedly, it results in significant health and economic losses.

This paper studies how to motivate a medical test-avoider to accept a medical test through information disclosure, thereby maximizing his survival rate.
For two reasons I focus on the information incentive rather than other material incentives or behavioral nudges.
First, in the real-life doctor-patient relationship, communication of information per se is indeed the most common way in reality utilized to influence the patients’ decisions.
Even without deliberate design, the “inartificial” information disclosure rules are still motivating the patients’ decisions every day in a potentially suboptimal way, and therefore it deserves a primary attention.
Second, information avoidance is commonly understood as a mean to manage
anticipatory emotions

(
Loewenstein 1987
,
Caplin and
Leahy 2001
)
, i.e., the emotional experiences generated by the belief about the future outcomes, such as hope, anxiety and suspense, and people avoid information because they anticipate that the information will cause a painful emotional experience.
Based on this concern, information incentive has a more nuanced and profound implication on the hedonic level: what information can change is not only the patient’s objective belief, but also the direct emotional feeling attached to the belief.
Hence, information disclosure in this context will result in several new patterns.

This paper provides a stylized model that describes a typical doctor-patient interaction.
The patient observes some preliminary signs of a disease, and seeks help from a doctor.
The patient’s health outcome is jointly determined by the patient’s current health state, which is either sick or not sick, and whether a costly treatment is taken.
The only way to reveal the current health status is through a medical test, but the patient is reluctant to take the test due to information avoidance.
Therefore, the doctor needs some additional information as an incentive for the patient to debias.

To persuade the patient to accepts the test and choose the optimal treatment, the doctor provides information about the health prospect if the patient is actually sick but forgoes the treatment.
This information, as background knowledge about the disease, is independent of, but complementary with, the test result.
Hence, the doctor’s information disclosure policy and the patient’s voluntary information acquisition behavior collectively determine the patient’s information environment, and the doctor influences the latter by controlling the former.
In addition, I adopt the framework of Bayesian persuasion
(
Kamenica and
Gentzkow 2011
)
, which helps me to identify theoretically the optimal strategy for information disclosure.

The doctor has two time windows to provide information: the
ex ante disclosure
tells the patient something about the untreated prospect before the test decision, while the
interim disclosure
sends another piece of information about the untreated prospect after the test decision but before the treatment decision.
Therefore, the optimization problem is categorized into a two-period dynamic Bayesian persuasion program
(
Ely and
Szydlowski 2020
,
Habibi 2020
,
D’Antoni
et al. 2024
)
.
The two-period setting allows me to solve the persuasion problem in a standard way, which solves first the optimal interim disclosure as constrained Bayesian persuasion problem
(
Doval and
Skreta 2024
)
and then solves the optimal ex ante disclosure by backward induction.

The patient’s preference is depicted by the psychological expected utility model by
Caplin and
Leahy 2001
, which is able to describe information-avoidance preference while preserving the minimal linearity required by information design.

1.1
Main Findings

The main contribution of this paper is the characterization of the optimal information policy.

I begin with characterizing the optimal interim disclosure after the test.
The result is jointly determined by two key tradeoffs.
First, as articulated by
Ball 2023
, the optimal interim disclosure must balances the tradeoff between
guiding the future
, the motive of influencing the patient’s subsequent treatment decision, and
rewarding the past
, the motive to use the information disclosure as a prize for the past decision.
Second, when the doctor rewards the past, she also faces the tradeoff between the instrumental usefulness and the psychological pain brought by the information, which is more common in the literature of information avoidance
(
Kőszegi 2003
,
Kőszegi 2006a
,
Kőszegi 2006b
,
Barigozzi and
Levaggi 2010
,
Schweizer and
Szech 2018
)
.

Taking the tradeoffs into account, the optimal interim disclosure can be solved by maximizing the doctor’s continuation payoff after the test under the constraint of voluntary participation.
I recognize that the optimal interim signal must be either with
perfect bad news
or with
perfect good news
.
Formally, let
\underline{p}\in\{\underline{p}_{H},\underline{p}_{L}\}
be the untreated prospect, and then a binary signal is with perfect good (bad) news if the good news can (cannot) confirm that
\underline{p}=\underline{p}_{H}
, but the bad news cannot (can) confirm
\underline{p}=\underline{p}_{L}
.
2
2

2

In other words, let
\mu
be the belief of
\underline{p}_{H}
. Then the higher belief of a signal with perfect good news must be
1
, and the lower belief of a signal with perfect bad news must be
0
.

In the current context, I interpret signals with perfect good news as a mean of
warning
, because by this signal the probability that the patient becomes pessimistic is larger than the full disclosure benchmark.
Conversely, signals with perfect good news is interpreted as a mean of
comforting
, because by this signal the probability that the patient becomes optimistic is larger than full disclosure.

Thus, the optimal interim signal can be solved by the following algorithm.
First, if the optimal signal for guiding the future, which is with perfect good news, can ensure voluntary participation, it is optimal.
If not, I show that the optimal interim disclosure adheres to a simple “best-good-news” rule, such that the optimal signal must maximize the most optimistic belief among all the signals capable to ensure voluntary participation.
Consequently, the participation constraint must be binding, and the doctor has to compromise to the motive of rewarding the past by making the signal more attractive.
Consequently, the optimal signal can be solved by combining the participation constraint, the revelation principle and the best-good-news rule.
In particular, if the payoff deficit of the optimal signal for guiding the future is small, the doctor attracts the patient by implementing the classical wisdom of
information as carrot
, such that she increases the informativeness of the signal within the signals with perfect bad news.
If the gap is so large that even full disclosure is still insufficient to provide the incentive, the optimal signal is with perfect bad news, which indicates that the doctor, in order to incentivize the patient, makes a commitment to comfort the patient after the bad test result.
The latter is new in the settings with the anticipatory utility.

Then I turn to the ex ante disclosure, and formally solve the optimal information policy.
The main research question here is that when will the doctor benefits from constructing an
information greenhouse
, such that the doctor utilizes the information to “warm up the patient” after receiving a cold message from the test.
Proposition
2
shows that the answer to this question depends on whether the patient
reacts to fear
; that is, whether he is willing to be tested when he completely believe that
\underline{p}=\underline{p}_{L}
.
If so, the optimal policy is
warning-in-advance
, such that the doctor discloses a signal about
\underline{p}
only before the test, and the signal is with perfect good news.
Also, if the patient is tested and the result is bad, the bad news in the ex ante disclosure is sufficient to guarantee the patient to accept the treatment even without any further interim information.
The warning-in-advance policy is standard because it is still optimal in the setting where information avoidance is absent and the test incurs a fixed physical cost (Proposition
3
).
However, if the patient does not react to fear and would rather act as an ostrich burying his head in the sand, the optimal information policy is
comforting reassurance
, by which the doctor adjust the patient’s belief to a zone that he feels comfortable to be tested, and commits to provide comfort (signals with perfect bad news) if the test result is bad.
The latter strategy is new in the presence of anticipatory feelings and information avoidance.
This result manifests as a consequence tradeoff between the patient’s
willingness to be tested
and his
willingness to treated
: although using the interim disclosure to provide comfort can help to make the test more attractive, as a cost the patient may become inappropriately optimistic about the untreated prospect and then forgo the necessary treatment.

So far, the results rely on the assumption that the reception of the doctor’s information is mandatory.
I further relax this assumption, and extend the model to the case that the patient can choose not to hear anything from the doctor.
Thus, an additional
ex ante participation constraint
is added to the problem, which makes policies such like warning-in-advance undesirable.
I fully characterize the optimal policy in Proposition
4
.
Similar with warning-in-advance, I show that by the optimal policy, the doctor complete information disclosure before the test.
However, in this case the doctor has to solve the tradeoff between guiding the future and rewarding the past within the ex ante disclosure.
Consequently, the optimal signal is completely analogous to the optimal interim disclosure derived in Proposition
1
.
That is, if the deficit is small, the warning-in-advance strategy is still optimal, but if the deficit is small, the optimal policy is
precautionary comfort
.
By the latter strategy, the doctor commits to comfort the patient by sending a signal with perfect bad news before the test decision, and keeps silent afterward.

The model is also extended to allow the patient’s information attitude to be arbitrary, which helps the model to fit better with the experiment data.
In Proposition
5
, I show that the solution of the optimal interim disclosure can be extended to this case by the method of
local concavification
.
That is, the persuasion problem can be solved by construction a hypothetical payoff function for the patient, which concavifies the original payoff function within every belief region where each patient’s behavior is optimal, and applying the same method in Proposition
1
.
The only difference is that the number of posterior beliefs may be more than the number of states, which has been observed by a series of papers on constrained Bayesian persuasion
(
Le Treust and
Tomala 2019
,
Boleslavsky and
Kim 2020
,
Doval and
Skreta 2024
)
.
In my model, the additional posterior belief comes from the possibility that the patient is intrinsic information loving, and doctor thus provides an additional piece of information to reward the past.
Also, Proposition
6
solves the optimal information policy when the patient’s payoff function is
inverse S-shaped
, and some new patterns that are not observed in Proposition
2
is identified.

1.2
Literature

The concept of anticipatory emotions is introduced to the field of economics by
Akerlof and
Dickens 1982
and
Loewenstein 1987
, and is extended to the environments with risk and uncertainty by
Wu 1999
,
Caplin and
Leahy 2001
and
Epstein 2008
.
Their works link the concept of anticipatory emotions, especially anxiety and hope, with the decision-theoretic models on temporal resolution of uncertainty
(
Kreps and
Porteus 1978
, for example)
.
Using the decision models, several papers study the choice of whether to accept an informative test, where the basic tradeoff is between the instrumental value of and the pain from information
(
Caplin and
Eliaz 2003
,
Kőszegi 2003
,
Kőszegi 2006a
,
Barigozzi and
Levaggi 2010
)
.

Early game-theoretic works concentrate on the impact of anticipatory emotions and information avoidance in different settings of information communication other than Bayesian persuasion, including verifiable disclosure
(
Caplin and
Leahy 2004
)
and cheap talk
(
Kőszegi 2006b
)
.
The most important insight there is that even if the preferences of the sender and receiver are perfectly aligned, the “good intention” of the sender to protect the receiver’s emotional feeling will destroy the credibility of the information she sends, thereby reducing the receiver’s payoff.
The current paper adopts a different perspective that emphasizes the principal-agent conflict between the two parties.
Also, it focuses on the Bayesian persuasion framework, which helps to better identify the optimal method of information communication without considering the implications posed by the requirement of sequential rationality.

The study of Bayesian persuasion and information design begins with the path-breaking work by
Kamenica and
Gentzkow 2011
.
Among this literature, there has been two persuasion papers that explicitly assume that receiver to have anticipatory feelings and information avoidance.

Schweizer and
Szech 2018
study the problem of optimal test design in a doctor-patient interaction, where the conflict of interest is still absent.
My model shares the spirit that the sender improve the receiver’s anticipatory feeling by tailoring the information structure.
However, in my model the principal-agent conflict is present and the reception of test result is voluntary.
Thus, the tradeoff between guiding the future and rewarding the past in the current paper is absent in theirs.
Also, the model by
Lipnowski and
Mathevet 2018
allows the receiver to have a non-standard information attitude, and there is still no conflict of interest.
They realize that the revelation principle may fail for such a psychological receiver, but show that it will still hold when he is information-avoidant.
3
3

3

Moreover, two papers study the optimal dynamic information policy to maximize the utility of a “psychological Bayesian audience”
(
Ely
et al. 2015
,
Duraj and
He 2019
)
. However, in their models, the receiver has no choice to make, and hence there is no problem of incentive provision.

There are two distinct but deeply connected branches of Bayesian persuasion papers that are closely related to this paper.
The first category is Bayesian persuasion under constraints.
In this category, the paper most closely related to the mine is
Rosar 2017
, who also explicitly studies voluntary participation of a test.
In her model, after the test it is the doctor that makes a decision, and therefore the reason that the receiver is reluctant to be tested are extrinsic.
Also, the model is similar with
Boleslavsky and
Kim 2020
, who consider a moral hazard problem with three players.
Between the agent’s action choice and the contract execution, a sender can transform the agent’s action into messages for the principal based on the committed rule.
In my paper, if we regard the two selves of the patient making the two choices as two distinct players, there is no moral hazard problem between them.

The second category is dynamic information design when the receiver is forward-looking.
In this category, my paper is especially related to
Ely and
Szydlowski 2020
,
Habibi 2020
and
D’Antoni
et al. 2024
, all of which characterize the optimal information policy in the two period setting.
Their papers explicitly point out that when the sender has the power to make a credible commitment about future information policies, information can be used both as a carrot to reward the past effort and as a guide to the future decisions.
In both my model and theirs, the principal can extract the rent by the interim disclosure and the ex ante disclosure can be derived by backward induction.
However, there is a subtle difference that their optimal policies, due to the linearity in preference, satisfies the ex ante participation constraint automatically, but in my model since information could make the patient worse off than the no-information benchmark, the optimal policy violates the ex ante constraint.

Equilibrium concept.

2.2
The Patient’s Preference

The patient’s preference is modeled as in the psychological expected utility model by
Caplin and
Leahy 2001
, which is common in the existing literature.
In this model, the patient’s choices can be understood as a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium in an
intrapersonal game
between two successive selves.

Formally, in the end of period 1 (point A in Figure
1
), the health probability is given by

\displaystyle\eta(a,e^{*},\mu_{1})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha+(1-\alpha)\underline{p}(\mu_{1})&a=0\\
\bar{p}e^{*}+\underline{p}(\mu_{1})(1-e^{*})&a=1\text{ and }\theta=0\\
1&a=1\text{ and }\theta=1\end{array}\right.,

where
e^{*}
is his (rational) expectation of his action at date 2 and
\underline{p}(\mu)\equiv\mu\underline{p}_{H}+(1-\mu)\underline{p}_{L}
for simplicity.
Then at date 2, I normalize the physical prize of being eventually healthy to
1
, and assume that there is no anticipatory emotions within a stage.
Thus, the patient’s payoff in date 2 is given by
u_{2}(a,e^{*},\mu_{1})=\eta(a,e^{*},\mu_{1})-ce
.
Denote by
v_{2}(\alpha,\mu_{1})
the indirect utility at date 2 that optimizes
u_{2}(a,\cdot,\mu_{1})
.

The key assumption of the psychological expected utility model is the dynamic inconsistency arising from the temporal distance between decision making and uncertainty resolution.
Based on this idea, the payoff at the end of date 1 is given by

\displaystyle u_{1}(a,\mu_{1})=\phi\circ v_{2}(a,\mu_{1}).

Function
\phi
measures the distortion of the patient’s preference by the anticipatory emotions in period 1, which shrinks to the standard model if it is linear.
Assume that
\phi
is continuous, smooth, and strictly increasing.
Then the patient, at any date-1 decision node of her, maximizes the expected value of
u_{1}(a,\mu_{1})
based upon the current belief (as well as a correct anticipation of her future behavior) whenever possible.

Information attitude is modeled by the curvature of function
\phi
, and the patient is information-avoidant (resp. loving, neutral) if and only if
\phi
is concave (resp. convex, linear) in probabilities.
6
6

6

The classical interpretation of this result is based on the relationship between risk attitude and time. Suppose the probability of being eventually healthy is
\eta
. Then if the uncertainty is resolved completely at date 1, the utility is
\eta\phi(1)
, while if the uncertainty is resolved completely at date 2, the utility is
\phi(\eta)
. The former is larger if and only if “immediate risk” is more desirable than its corresponding “remote risk”.

To model information avoidance, the concavity of
\phi
is assumed throughout this paper unless explicitly stated.
The concavity of
\phi
can be interpreted as a result of
diminishing marginal utility of hope
.
Hope preservation is widely believed as the
as-if psychology
of information avoidance
(
Chew and Ho 1994
,
Ahlbrecht and
Weber 1996
,
Kocher
et al. 2014
,
Chen 2022
,
Masatlioglu
et al. 2023
)
.
If we think of
\phi(v)-v
as the positive emotional feeling of hope from date-2 payoff
v
, then
\phi
being concave indicates that the marginal increase in utility from an additional increase in the hopefulness (i.e., the belief of the desired outcome) is decreasing.

3.6
Value of Dynamic Commitment

So far, the results relies on the assumption of dynamic commitment.
That is, at date 1 the doctor can commit to implement not only the disclosure rule at the current date, but also the disclosure rule at date 2.
Of course, this assumption is strong.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose the power to make dynamic commitment is absent.
Then if the patient reacts to fear, the warning-in-advance policy is the doctor’s equilibrium strategy, but if not, it is unable to persuade him to be tested with any positive probability.

In fact, in the perspective of sequential rationality, the interim disclosure without dynamic commitment must be optimal for guiding the future.
That is, when
a=1
, the doctor uses
\{\mu_{1}\}

\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{e}
and use
\{\mu_{e},1\}
otherwise.
Thus the warning-in-advance policy can be implemented when without any power of dynamic commitment since the interim disclosure (non-disclosure) is always optimal for guiding the future.
Meanwhile, comforting reassurance is founded upon the capacity to commit because it requires the doctor to use signals with perfect bad news in the interim disclosure, which can never emerge as an equilibrium strategy.

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}V_{1}(\nu)\tau_{1}^{1}(d\nu|\mu)\right)\tau_{0}(d\mu)+\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{C}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}V_{0}(\nu)\tau_{1}^{0}(d\nu|\mu)\right)\tau_{0}(d\mu)\geq V_{0}(\mu_{0}).

(7)

The main technical difficulty here is that backward induction no longer holds.
This is because even in the ex ante disclosure, the doctor also needs to balance the motives of guiding the future and rewarding the past.
Hence, the two instances of disclosure are both responsible to the ex ante participation constraint.

To solve the problem, I introduce the following notations.
First, define function

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\mu)\equiv\max\left\{V_{0}(\mu),\alpha+(1-\alpha)\phi(\bar{p}-c)\right\}

as the doctor’s payoff function when she commits to disclose no information in the interim disclosure.
That is, the patient either takes the test and accepts the treatment, or refuses to see the doctor in the very beginning.
Second, belief
\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
is the solution to equation
\phi(\alpha+(1-\alpha)\underline{p}(\mu_{\mathcal{N}}))=\alpha+(1-\alpha)\phi(\bar{p}-c)
; that is, given there is no information in the interim disclosure,
\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
is the threshold that taking and not taking the test are indifferent.
Third, belief
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
is the solution to

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{V}(1)+(1-\mu_{\mathcal{T}})\mathcal{V}(0)=\mathcal{V}(\mu_{\mathcal{T}}),

and therefore full disclosure in the ex ante disclosure is sufficient to motivate voluntary reception of information if and only if
\mu_{0}\geq\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Finally, belief
\mu_{\mathcal{V}}
is identified by

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{V}}=\sup\left\{\mu\bigg|\,\text{cav}\circ\mathcal{V}(\mu)>\mathcal{V}(\mu)\right\},

and hence by concavification, if there is no interim disclosure, the patient can benefit from persuasion if and only if
\mu_{0}<\mu_{\mathcal{V}}
.

(

Conclusion

The main contribution of this paper is the characterization of the optimal information policy to promote a medical test-avoider to undergo a necessary medical test.
The feature of the problem is that the patient has the decision-making power to acquire some of the information (test result), but is a passive recipient of the other information.
Thus, the sender of the latter information, namely the doctor, can influence the patient’s information environment to create the motivation for the patient to take the test and make the best treatment decision.

Compared with the physical cost benchmark, this paper emphasizes the question that whether the information greenhouse policy, which rewards the voluntary information acquisition by committing to a patient-desired information structure, is useful to motivate the voluntary participation of the test.
The positive answer is
partially
confirmed: It is an efficient tool either if the patient does not react to fear, or if the patient faces an ex ante participation constraint, and otherwise it is inferior to warning-in-advance.

On the technical level, this paper makes two minor contribution on the constrained Bayesian persuasion problems with two states.
The first is the best-good-news rule, a simple rule of thumb to quickly identify the structure of the optimal signal.
The second is the local concavification method used in Section
5
, which helps to characterize the optimal signal when the requirement of the “generalized information design environment” identified by
Doval and
Skreta 2024
are not satisfied.

In practice, I recognize that the doctor or other senders of medical information face more constraints and practical considerations than those considered in this paper.
These additional concerns may render the optimal policy solved by this paper to be non-optimal.
For example, in many cases, the standard operating procedures or the ethical guidelines of medical consultation do not allow the doctor to conceal the information she possesses.
However, even so, the model provides a theoretical framework for evaluating the impact of any information disclosure policy on information-avoidant patients, and the optimal policy can be regarded as a reference point for evaluating the use of these policies.
By comparing the actual diagnostic and treatment process with the optimal policy that is theoretically derived, we can assess to what extent it deters information-avoidant patients and thereby estimate the welfare loss incurred in this regard.

Although I interpret the model in a doctor-patient relationship, there may be some alternative real-life applications.

Appendix A
The Best-Good-News Rule

In this appendix I formally state and prove the best-good-news rule in the traditional two-action-two-state Bayesian persuasion problems with a linear constraint.

Consider a game played between a
sender
and a
receiver
.
The sender designs an information structure in the test, and then the receiver decides whether to accept the test.
The state space of binary, and the test updates the prior belief
\mu_{0}\in(0,1)
to a posterior belief
\mu\in[0,1]
, which are the probabilities assigned to one of the two states.
Based on the posterior belief, the receiver chooses an action
e\in\{0,1\}
, and the game ends.
The Bayesian persuasion problem can be written as

\displaystyle\max_{\tau\in\mathcal{R}(\mu_{0})}:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P(\mu)\tau(d\mu)

\displaystyle\text{subject to: }\int_{0}^{1}V(\mu)\tau(d\mu)\geq\bar{V}_{0}(\mu_{0}),\quad\text{(PC)}

where
P
and
V
are the indirect utility functions of posterior belief
\mu
for the sender and the receiver, respectively, and
\bar{V}_{0}(\mu_{0})
is the payoff that the receiver rejects the test.

Suppose that there exists
\mu_{e}\in(0,1)
, such that the sets of beliefs that
e=1
and
e=0
are optimal are
[0,\mu_{e}]
and
[\mu_{e},1]
, respectively.
Assume functions
P
and
V
are both concave in both
[0,\mu_{e}]
and
[\mu_{e},1]
, and therefore we can focus on signals in set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mu_{0})=\bigg\{\{x,y\}\bigg|1\geq x\geq\max\{\mu_{0},\mu_{e}\}\geq\min\{\mu_{0},\mu_{e}\}\geq y\geq 0\bigg\}.

without loss of generality.

Note that by concavification, the unconstrained optimum is also at-most-binary.
That is, for any
\mu_{0}
, the exists
\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\}\in\mathcal{S}(\mu_{0})
, such that

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}=\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\}\in\arg\max_{\tau\in\mathcal{R}(\mu_{0})}:\int_{0}^{1}P(\mu)\tau(d\mu).

I focus on the case that the (PC) constraint is binding in the constrained optimum, which indicates that signal
\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\}
cannot meet the requirement of (PC).
Thus, the optimization problem can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\max_{x,y:\,\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{S}(\mu_{0})}:

\displaystyle\,\frac{x-\mu_{0}}{x-y}\cdot P(y)+\frac{\mu_{0}-y}{x-y}\cdot P(x)

\displaystyle\text{subject to: }\frac{x-\mu_{0}}{x-y}\cdot V(y)+\frac{\mu_{0}-y}{x-y}\cdot V(x)=\bar{V}_{0}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle\text{(PCB)}.

Here,
(x-\mu_{0})/(x-y)
and
(\mu_{0}-y)/(x-y)
are respectively the probabilities that posterior
y
and
x
realize, which are derived from Bayesian plausibility.

To formally state the best-good-news rule, I introduce the following notations.
First, for any
x\in[\max\{\mu_{0},\mu_{e}\},1]
, define

\displaystyle D(x)=\left\{y\in[0,\min\{\mu_{0},\mu_{e}\}]\bigg|\,\frac{x-\mu_{0}}{x-y}V(y)+\frac{\mu_{0}-y}{x-y}V(x)=\bar{V}_{0}(\mu_{0})\right\}.

That is,
D(x)
is the set of lower beliefs that makes constraint (PCB) binds.
If
D(x)
is non-empty, define
d(x)
is the solution to the following maximization problem:

\displaystyle d(x)=\sup\bigg\{\arg\max_{y\in D(x)}:\,\frac{x-\mu_{0}}{x-y}\cdot P(y)+\frac{\mu_{0}-y}{x-y}\cdot P(x)\bigg\}.

Thus, given
x
,
d(x)
is the “best lower belief” that makes the (PCB) constraint binds.
Second, for any function
G
, define

\displaystyle S_{G}(x)=\frac{G(x)-G(d(x))}{x-d(x)},

as the slope of the segment connecting
(x,G(x))
and
(d(x),G(d(x)))
.
Third, for any
x\geq\max\{\mu_{e},\mu_{0}\}
, define

\mu_{1}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}V(0)&\mu_{0}<\min\{\mu_{e},\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\},\\
\frac{(\mu-\min\{\mu_{e},\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\})V_{0}(1)+(1-\mu)V(0)}{1-\min\{\mu_{e},\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\}}&\mu_{e}\leq\mu\leq\mu_{\mathcal{F}},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu_{H},\mu_{L}\in[0,1]\\
\tau\in\mathcal{R}(\mu_{L})\end{subarray}}:\,\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P(\mu)\tau(d\mu)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H})

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}V(\mu)\tau(d\mu)\geq\bar{V}_{0}(\mu_{L})

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}V(\mu)\tau(d\mu)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot V_{0}(\mu_{H})=V_{0}(\mu_{0}).

\displaystyle\,\int_{0}^{1}P(\mu)\tau(d\mu)

\displaystyle\text{subject to: }\int_{0}^{1}V(\mu)\tau(d\mu)=\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}\cdot V(\mu_{0})-\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}\cdot V(\mu_{H}).

Similar with Proposition

Also, it can be solved by the best-good-news rule, and thus it is either with perfect good news or with perfect bad news.

If there is no interim disclosure, the optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle P(\mu)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H})

By the concavity of

Step 5:

The optimal disclosure when
\mu_{0}\in(\mu_{\mathcal{N}},\mu_{\mathcal{T}})

(15)

Then I show that it cannot satisfy the ex ante participation constraint.

By the best-good-news rule, there is no ex ante signal in

that can dominate the proposed policy without the interim disclosure.
So I only focus on the case that
\tau
is informative.
First, suppose

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P(\mu)\tau(d\mu)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\bar{p}+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H})\leq\frac{1-\mu_{0}}{1-\mu^{*}_{L}}\cdot\bar{p}+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}^{*}}{1-\mu^{*}_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}_{H}.

That is, even though the policy is implementable, the health probability of such a triple

Third, if

is larger than
\mu_{L}^{*}
and
\tau
is informative, then by Step 3,
\tau
is with perfect news.
If it is with perfect bad news, i.e.,

\displaystyle=

Thus, if the policy that dominates the proposed policy exists, then
\mu_{L}
must be larger than
\mu_{L}^{*}
and
\tau
must be with perfect good news.
I denote

as the lower belief, and by this signal the health probability is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\bigg(\frac{1-\mu_{L}}{1-\mu_{l}}\cdot\bar{p}+\frac{\mu_{L}-\mu_{l}}{1-\mu_{l}}\cdot\underline{p}_{H}\bigg)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H}).

If this expression exceeds (

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\bigg[\frac{(\mu_{H}-\mu_{L})(1-\mu_{0})}{(1-\mu_{L}^{*})(\mu_{H}-\mu_{0})}\cdot V(0)+\left(1-\frac{(\mu_{H}-\mu_{L})(1-\mu_{0})}{(1-\mu_{L}^{*})(\mu_{H}-\mu_{0})}\right)\cdot V(1)\bigg]+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\mu_{0}}{1-\mu_{L}^{*}}\cdot V(0)+\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\left(1-\frac{(\mu_{H}-\mu_{L})(1-\mu_{0})}{(1-\mu_{L}^{*})(\mu_{H}-\mu_{0})}\right)\cdot V(1)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H}).

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1-\mu_{0}}{1-\mu_{L}^{*}}\cdot V(0)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}^{*}}{1-\mu_{L}^{*}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(1)

Step 6:
The optimal disclosure when
\mu_{0}\in(\mu_{\mathcal{T}},\mu_{\mathcal{V}})

When
\mu_{0}\in(\mu_{\mathcal{T}},\mu_{\mathcal{V}})
, the proposed policy discloses no information in the interim disclosure and the ex ante signal is
\{0,\mu_{H}^{*}\}
for some

Thus, the patient’s ex ante payoff is

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu^{*}_{H}}\right)\cdot V(0)+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}^{*}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu^{*}_{H})=\mathcal{V}(\mu_{0}),

and the corresponding health probability is

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu^{*}_{H}}\right)\cdot\bar{p}+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}^{*}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H}^{*}).

I prove by contradiction that there exists no policy that satisfies the ex ante participation constraint and yields a higher health probability.
Suppose not, and then denote the optimal signal by triple
\langle\mu_{H},\mu_{L},\tau\rangle
.
Here, if
\tau
is non-disclosure, it cannot be a better policy by the best-good-news rule, and hence I only consider the case that
\tau
is informative.

Then by Step 3,
\tau
is either with perfect goods news or with perfect bad news.
Suppose first it is with perfect bad news.
Then the interim signal is
\{0,\mu_{h}\}
for some
\mu_{h}>\mu_{e}
.
Therefore, the health probability is

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{h})+\left(1-\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\right)\cdot\bar{p}\right)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\right)}_{\equiv\pi}\cdot\bar{p}+\left[\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\cdot\mu_{h}+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\mu_{H}\right]\cdot\underline{p}_{H}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\cdot(1-\mu_{h})+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot(1-\mu_{H})\right]\cdot\underline{p}_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\cdot\bar{p}+(1-\pi)p\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{1-\pi}\right).

Thus, this is also the health probability if the doctor discloses no information in the interim disclosure and sending ex ante signal
\{0,\mu_{0}/(1-\pi)\}
.
Compare the two policies with an identical health probability, the ex ante payoff of the patient under the latter policy is

\displaystyle\pi\cdot\mathcal{V}(0)+(1-\pi)\cdot\mathcal{V}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{1-\pi}\right)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu_{h})+\left(1-\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\right)\cdot V(0)\right)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\cdot V(\mu_{h})+\left(1-\frac{\mu_{L}}{\mu_{h}}\right)\cdot V(0)\right)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H}).

That is, policy
\{0,\mu_{0}/(1-\pi_{H})\}
is strictly better than policy
\langle\mu_{H},\mu_{L},\{0,\mu_{h}\}\rangle
.
Since
\{0,\mu_{0}/(1-\pi_{H})\}
cannot be better than
\{0,\mu_{H}^{*}\}
if it is implementable,
\langle\mu_{H},\mu_{L},\tau\rangle
cannot, either.

Finally, if
\tau
is informative and
\tau
is with perfect good news, then the maximization problem becomes:

\displaystyle\max_{\mu_{H},\mu_{L},\mu_{l}}:\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-\mu_{L}}{1-\mu_{l}}\cdot\bar{p}+\frac{\mu_{L}-\mu_{l}}{1-\mu_{l}}\cdot\underline{p}_{H}\right)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\underline{p}(\mu_{H})

subject to:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\left(\frac{1-\mu_{L}}{1-\mu_{l}}\cdot V(0)+\frac{\mu_{L}-\mu_{l}}{1-\mu_{l}}\cdot V(1)\right)+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H})=\mathcal{V}(\mu_{0}),

Solving the constraint directly, we get

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\frac{V(1)\bar{p}-V(0)\underline{p}_{H}}{V(1)-V(0)}+\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu_{L}}{\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}}\cdot\left(\frac{(\bar{p}-\underline{p}_{H})\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H})}{V(1)-V(0)}+\underline{p}(\mu_{H})\right)-\frac{\bar{p}-\underline{p}_{H}}{V(1)-V(0)}\cdot\mathcal{V}(\mu_{0}).

The first derivative with respect to
\mu_{L}

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{H}-\mu_{0}}{(\mu_{H}-\mu_{L})^{2}(V(1)-V(0))}\cdot\big((\bar{p}-\underline{p}(\mu_{H}))\cdot V(1)-(\bar{p}-\underline{p}_{H})\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H})-(\underline{p}_{H}-\underline{p}(\mu_{H}))V(0)\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\mu_{H})<\frac{\bar{p}-\underline{p}(\mu_{H})}{\bar{p}-\underline{p}_{H}}\cdot V(1)-\frac{\underline{p}_{H}-\underline{p}(\mu_{H})}{\bar{p}-\underline{p}_{H}}\cdot V(0).

Note that the criterion is irrelevant with

is either

\mu_{L}

V(d(x))+(1-d(x))V^{\prime}(d(x))=V(d(x))
.
Combine the two points,

\displaystyle 1=V(l(1))+(1-l(1))V^{\prime}(l(1))>V(d(x))+(1-d(x))V^{\prime}(d(x)).

Simultaneously, by the concavity of
V
on
[\alpha_{e},1]
, the mean value theorem implies

\displaystyle V(x)+(1-x)V^{\prime}(x)\geq V(1)=1,

which verifies the best-good-news rule and completes the proof.

Proof of Corollary
C.2

For any
\alpha_{0}
, by definition,

\displaystyle l(\alpha_{0})=\max_{\alpha<\alpha_{e}}\,\frac{V(\alpha_{0})-V(\alpha)}{\alpha_{0}-\alpha}.

I first show that function
l(\cdot)
is non-increasing.
If
\frac{V(\alpha_{0})-V(0)}{\alpha_{0}}>V^{\prime}(0)
, then
l(\alpha_{0})=0
, and therefore there always exists
\varepsilon
that is sufficiently small, such that
l(\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon)=0
still holds.
If
\frac{V(\alpha_{0})-V(0)}{\alpha_{0}}\leq V^{\prime}(0)
, we must have

\displaystyle V(\alpha_{0})-V(l(\alpha_{0}))=V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))(\alpha_{0}-l(\alpha_{0})).

By the rule of implicit function differentiation, take the first-order derivative for both sides, and we have

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})-V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))\frac{dl(\alpha_{0})}{d\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle=V^{\prime\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))\frac{dl(\alpha_{0})}{d\alpha_{0}}(\alpha_{0}-l(\alpha_{0}))+V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))\left(1-\frac{dl(\alpha_{0})}{d\alpha_{0}}\right).

Rearrange, and then

\displaystyle\frac{dl(\alpha_{0})}{d\alpha_{0}}=\frac{V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})-V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))}{V^{\prime\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))(\alpha_{0}-l(\alpha_{0}))}.

The denominator is obviously negative, and thus it suffices to show that the numerator is positive.

I show this by contradiction.
Suppose
V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})<V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))
, and there must be a left neighborhood
(\alpha_{0}-\varepsilon,\alpha_{0})
, such that for any
\alpha^{\prime}
in this interval, we must have
V(\alpha^{\prime})>L(\alpha^{\prime}|\alpha_{0})
, where

\displaystyle L(\alpha|\alpha_{0})=V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))(\alpha-\alpha_{0})+V(\alpha_{0})

is exactly the straight line connecting points
(\alpha_{0},V(\alpha_{0}))
and
(l(\alpha_{0}),V(l(\alpha_{0})))
.
Also, since
V
is concave in
(0,\alpha_{e})
, we also have
V(\alpha_{e})<L(\alpha_{e}|\alpha_{0})
.
By the intermediate value theorem, there exists
\alpha_{1}\in(\alpha_{e},\alpha^{\prime})\subsetneq(\alpha_{e},\alpha_{0})
, such that
L(\alpha_{1})=V(\alpha_{1}|\alpha_{0})
.
Then by definition, we have
l(\alpha_{0})=l(\alpha_{1})
.
Here, we must have
V^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})>V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{1}))=V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))
.
This is because otherwise we can replicate the construction above to find
\alpha_{2}\in(\alpha_{e},\alpha_{1})
, such that
L(\alpha_{2}|\alpha_{0})=V^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})
and
l(\alpha_{2})=l(\alpha_{1})
.
However, there are at most two intersections for a concave function and a linear function, which indicates the non-existence of
\alpha_{2}
.
Since
V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})-V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))<0
and
V^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})-V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{1}))>0
, by the intermediate value theorem there exists
\alpha\in(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{0})
such that
V^{\prime}(\alpha)-V^{\prime}(l(\alpha))=0
.
By construction, the solution here is exactly
\alpha_{v}
; in other words,
\alpha_{v}\in(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{0})
, which contradicts with the assumption that
\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{v}
.

Since
V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})-V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))<0
, by the envelope theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha_{0}}\left(\frac{V(\alpha_{0})-V(l(\alpha_{0}))}{\alpha_{0}-l(\alpha_{0})}\right)

\displaystyle\propto V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})(\alpha_{0}-l(\alpha_{0}))-(V(\alpha_{0})-V(l(\alpha_{0})))

\displaystyle\propto V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})-\frac{V(\alpha_{0})-V(l(\alpha_{0}))}{\alpha_{0}-l(\alpha_{0})}=V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})-V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))<0.

That is, the slope of function
L(\cdot|\alpha_{0})
increases with
\alpha_{0}
.

Pick any
\alpha_{0}\in(\alpha_{g},\alpha_{v})
and a sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, and then for any
\alpha>\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon
, by
V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))<V^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})
,

\displaystyle L(\alpha|\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))(\alpha-\alpha_{0})+V(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle\leq V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}))(\alpha-\alpha_{0}-\varepsilon)+V(\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq V^{\prime}(l(\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon))(\alpha-\alpha_{0}-\varepsilon)+V(\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon)=L(\alpha|\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon)

Denote by
h(\alpha_{0})
as the upper belief corresponding to
\alpha_{0}
, and then
V(h(\alpha_{0}))=L(h(\alpha_{0})|\alpha_{0})
.
Again, by the rule of implicit function differentiation, we have

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{0}))\cdot\frac{dh(\alpha_{0})}{d\alpha_{0}}=\frac{\partial L(h(\alpha_{0})|\alpha_{0})}{\partial h(\alpha_{0})}\cdot\frac{dh(\alpha_{0})}{d\alpha_{0}}+\frac{\partial L(h(\alpha_{0})|\alpha_{0})}{\partial\alpha_{0}}.

That is,

\displaystyle\frac{dh(\alpha_{0})}{d\alpha_{0}}=\frac{\frac{\partial L(h(\alpha_{0})|\alpha_{0})}{\partial\alpha_{0}}}{V^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{0}))-\frac{\partial L(h(\alpha_{0})|\alpha_{0})}{\partial h(\alpha_{0})}}.

We have shown that the numerator is positive, and the denominator is negative since
h(\alpha_{0})>\alpha_{v}
.
The upper belief is thus decreasing with
\alpha_{0}
.

Finally, denote by
\pi
the probability of the bad news.
Note that

\displaystyle\pi V(l(\alpha_{0}))+(1-\pi)V(h(\alpha_{0}))=V(\alpha_{0}).

When
\alpha
increases, both
V(l(\alpha_{0}))
and
V(h(\alpha_{0}))
decrease but
V(\alpha_{0})
.
To balance the equation,
\pi
must decrease correspondingly, which completes the proof.
