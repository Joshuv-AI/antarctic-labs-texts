Ari Cruz, Pamela E. Harris, Kimberly J. Harry, Jan Kretschmann, Matt McClinton, Alex Moon, John O. Museus, Eric Redmon

Abstract
. A parking function is a sequence α = ( a 1 , a 2 , … , a n ) ∈ [ n ] n \alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in[n]^{n} where its nondecreasing rearrangement β = ( b 1 , b 2 , … , b n ) \beta=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}) satisfies b i ≤ i b_{i}\leq i for all 1 ≤ i ≤ n 1\leq i\leq n . In this article, we study parking functions based on their ascents (indices at which a i < a i + 1 a_{i}<a_{i+1} ), descents (indices at which a i > a i + 1 a_{i}>a_{i+1} ), and ties (indices at which a i = a i + 1 a_{i}=a_{i+1} ). By using multiset Eulerian polynomials, we give a generating function for the number of parking functions of length n n with i i descents. We present a recursive formula for the number of parking functions of length n n with descents at a specified subset of [ n − 1 ] [n-1] . We establish the set of parking functions with descent set I I and the set of parking functions with descent set J = { n − i : i ∈ I } J=\{n-i:i\in I\} are in bijection, and hence these sets have the same cardinality. As a special case, we show that the number of parking functions of length n n with descents at the first k k indices is given by 1 n ​ ( n k ) ​ ( 2 ​ n − k n − k − 1 ) \frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{2n-k}{n-k-1} . We prove this by bijecting to the set of standard Young tableaux of shape ( ( n − k ) 2 , 1 k ) ((n-k)^{2},1^{k}) , which are enumerated by the same formula. We also study peaks and valleys of parking functions, which are indices at which a i − 1 < a i > a i + 1 a_{i-1}<a_{i}>a_{i+1} and a i − 1 > a i < a i + 1 a_{i-1}>a_{i}<a_{i+1} , respectively. We show that the set of parking functions with no peaks and no ties is enumerated by the Catalan numbers, and the set of parking functions with no valleys and no ties is enumerated by the Fine numbers. We conclude our study by characterizing when a parking function is uniquely determined by its statistic encoding; a word indicating what indices in the parking function are ascents, descents, and ties. We provide open problems throughout.

Another permutation statistic of interest is peaks and valleys.
Recall that
when
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}

Billey, Burdzy, and Sagan

showed that the number of elements in

, where

The polynomial

is referred to as the

, and it was conjectured that

has a positive integer expansion in a binomial basis centered at the maximum of

This was proven in the affirmative by Diaz-Lopez, Harris, Insko, and Omar in
[
4
]
.
Motivated by these results, we study the set of peakless-tieless parking functions of length

In Section

•

Corollary
4.5
: If

, then
|\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}|=C_{n}
, the

•

Corollary
4.6

If

, then

|\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}(i)|=C_{n,i}\;,

where
C_{n,i}
denotes the
i
th entry in the

th row of the Catalan triangle
[
11
,
A009766
]
.

We also prove that the number of parking functions of length

with no valleys
and no ties is the Fine numbers (Theorem

[
11
,
A000957
]

It remains an open problem to enumerate all parking functions with a particular peak set.

In Section

Calling the statistic encoding of a parking function, we establish the existence of parking functions for every statistic encoding and fully characterize the parking functions which are uniquely identified by their statistic encodings.

Permutations of Multisets

Recall that a multiset
M
is denoted as
M=\left\{1^{m_{1}},\ldots,n^{m_{n}}\right\}
where
m_{i}
denotes the multiplicity of
i
for all
i\in[n]

A

is a word
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{m}
where
m=m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{n}
and
\pi
contains
i

m_{i}
times for all

We let
\mathfrak{S}_{M}
denote the set of multipermutations on

.
As expected, a

is an index

such that
\pi_{j}>\pi_{j+1}

As before, we let

be the number of descents of
\pi

\displaystyle A_{M}(t)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{M}}t^{\mathrm{des}(\pi)}

(6)

, where the sum is over the set of all multipermutations

The coefficients of

Due to MacMahon
[
10

\displaystyle\frac{A_{M}(t)}{(1-t)^{m+1}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{m_{i}+\ell}{\ell}t^{\ell}.

(7)

Equation (

\displaystyle[t^{k}]A_{M}(t)=\left|\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{M}:\mathrm{des}(\pi)=k\right\}\right|=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{m+1}{\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{m_{i}+k-\ell}{k-\ell},

(8)

where
[t^{k}]A_{M}(t)
denotes the coefficient of
t^{k}
in
A_{M}(t)
. Using (
8
), we enumerate descents in parking functions, a superset of permutations, and generalize the results of

[
13
]
.

\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
.
By its construction, each

However, each

, the value

Now consider the generating function

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}d(n,j)x^{n}y^{j},

Next, we prove some preliminary results which will help us establish that the number of parking functions with descents at the indices in
I\subseteq[n-1]
is the same as the number of parking functions with descents at indices in

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
I\subseteq[n-1]
and
J=\{n-i:i\in I\}
. Then
d(I;n)=a(J;n)

Proof.

Given
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
with
\mathrm{Des}(\alpha)=I
, then
(\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n-1},\ldots,\alpha_{1})
has
\mathrm{Asc}(\alpha)=J
. This defines a bijection between the sets
D(I;n)
and
A(J;n)
. Thus,
d(I;n)=a(J;n)
, as claimed.
∎

Next we define a function used in our subsequent results.
To simplify notation, we denote tuples in one-line notation and refer to them as words, i.e., the tuple
(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})
will be denoted by the word

Definition 3.2
.

Let

denote the orbit of

Given

\mathbf{x}
and
\argmax(\mathbf{x})

\mathbf{x}
.

Define the map

For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{y})

Case 0:

If the length of
\mathbf{x}

.

\mathbf{x}

and

,

and

.

\mathbf{x}
is larger than 1 and there is some
a<b
with

and
b\in\argmax(\mathbf{x})
, then

be the greatest element of
\argmin(\mathbf{x})

\argmax(\mathbf{x})

be the smallest element of
\argmax(\mathbf{x})

Remark 3

In Definition
3.2
,

\mathbf{x}

to

Hence, applying

\nu(\mathbf{x})
having a minimal value to the left of a maximal value.

Lemma 3.6
.

The map
\nu
of Definition
3.2
is injective.

Proof.

We now left-invert
\nu
by inducting on length.
In the base case, when the length is zero or 1, then
\nu
acts as the identity
causing the left-inverse to also be the identity, hence injective.
Assume for strong induction, that
\nu
is left-invertible for all inputs of length less than
n
(i.e. of length
1<k<n
).
Now consider
\nu(\mathbf{x})
of length
n
.
We will left invert
\nu(\mathbf{x})
by uniquely identifying
\mathbf{x}
.
Let
m=\min(\nu(\mathbf{x}))
and
M=\max(\nu(\mathbf{x}))
.
By Definition
3.2
,
\nu
does not change the content of the input.
Hence,
m=\min(\nu(\mathbf{x}))=\min(\mathbf{x})
and
M=\max(\nu(\mathbf{x}))=\max(\mathbf{x})
.
Given that
n>1
, then either:

(1)

\mathbf{x}
has the property of either Case
1
of Definition
3.2
and

\nu(\mathbf{x})=\nu(x_{1}~x_{2}~\cdots~x_{j-1})\,~\underbrace{m}_{j}\,~\nu(x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{i-1})\,~\underbrace{M}_{i}\,~\nu(x_{i+1}~\cdots~x_{n}),

or

(2)

\mathbf{x}
is of the from in Case
2
of Definition
3.2
and

\nu(\mathbf{x})=\nu(x_{1}~x_{2}~\cdots~x_{i-1})\,~\underbrace{M}_{i}~\,\nu(x_{i+1}~\cdots~x_{j-1})\,~\underbrace{m}_{j}\,~\nu(x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{n}),

Thus,
i
and
j
are indices determined using Definition
3.2
.

Given
\nu(\mathbf{x})\in\nu(\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{y}))
, if the indices
i
and
j
can be uniquely identified by the structure of the word
\nu(\mathbf{x})
, then we can inductively construct
\nu^{-1}
by swapping the
i
th and
j
th entries of
\nu(\mathbf{x})
and then applying
\nu^{-1}
to each of the subwords of
\nu(\mathbf{x})
with smaller length.
This then would uniquely identify the input
\mathbf{x}
, and establish injectivity.

The key to this creating the inverse relies on determining whether
\nu(\mathbf{x})
satisfies either:

(a)

all minimal elements are to the left of all maximal elements, or

(b)

there exists a minimal element to the right of a maximal element.

We consider each of the above cases independently.

For Case (a) where all minimal elements are to the left of all maximal elements in
\nu(\mathbf{x})
, the first step in applying
\nu
to
\mathbf{x}
can not have arisen from Case 2 in Definition
3.2
, because Case 2 explicitly places a minimal element to the right of a maximal element. Therefore,
the first step in applying
\nu
to
\mathbf{x}
arose from Case 1 in Definition
3.2
.
This implies that
\mathbf{x}
has all maximal elements to the left of all minimal elements and in the subword
x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{i-1}
, all instances of
M
are to the left of all instances of
m
.
Therefore, if
x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{i-1}
has instances of both
M
and
m
,
\nu
acts on
x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{i-1}
as in Case 1 of Definition
3.2
.
Then

\nu(\mathbf{x})=\nu(x_{1}~x_{2}~\cdots~x_{j-1})\,~\underbrace{m}_{j}\,~\nu(x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{i-1})\,~\underbrace{M}_{i}\,~\nu(x_{i+1}~\cdots~x_{n})

where index
j
is the smallest index that contains a minimal element and index

is the largest index that contains a maximal element.
Therefore,
i=\max(\argmax(\nu(\mathbf{x})))
and

.
Then by induction hypothesis, we have uniquely identified
\mathbf{x}
, which gave rise to
\nu(\mathbf{x})
.

Now consider Case (b) where there exists a minimal element to the right of a maximal element in
\nu(\mathbf{x})
.
Recall that
\nu(\mathbf{x})\in\nu(\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{y}))

By induction on the minimum of the number of minimal and maximal elements in
\mathbf{y}

\nu(\mathbf{x})
was the output from Case 1 in Definition

would have all minimal elements to the left of all maximal elements.

\nu
to
\mathbf{x}
arose from Case 2 in Definition

.
Then

\mathbf{x}
containing the rightmost minimal element that has a maximal element to its right in
\mathbf{x}
.
Thus,

\mathbf{x}=x_{1}~x_{2}~\cdots~x_{i-1}~\underbrace{m}_{i}~x_{i+1}~\cdots~x_{j-1}~\underbrace{M}_{j}~x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{n}.

If a minimal element and a maximal element both appear in the subword

, as otherwise

\nu(\mathbf{x})=\nu(x_{1}~x_{2}~\cdots~x_{i-1})\,~\underbrace{M}_{i}~\,\nu(x_{i+1}~\cdots~x_{j-1})\,~\underbrace{m}_{j}\,~\nu(x_{j+1}~\cdots~x_{n}),

Theorem 3.8

In what follows, we say the sets
I\subseteq[n-1]
and
J=\{n-i:i\in I\}
are
self-dual
if
I=J
. We now give a formula for the number of self-dual sets.

Lemma 3.9
.

The number of sets
I\subseteq[n-1]
such that
I=\{n-i:i\in I\}
is
2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

Proof.

If
n=1
, then
I=J=\emptyset
and there is
2^{0}=1
such set. Fix
n\geq 1
.
For
i\in[n-1]
consider the mapping
i\rightarrow n-i
.
Notice that the set
\{i,n-i\}
is self-dual.
If
n
is even, then the set
\left\{\frac{n}{2}\right\}
is the only self-dual set of one element.
By symmetry, we need only consider
1\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
.
This gives
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
distinct self-dual subsets from which we can construct larger self-dual sets.
To produce such a set, we take any union of our two element (and possibly the one element set for even
n
) self-dual sets to produce a larger self-dual set.
Thus, the total number of self-dual sets of
[n]
is
2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}
.
∎

Our next objective is to give a recursive formula for the number of parking functions of length

To make our approach precise, we begin with some general definitions and notation.

\displaystyle D_{X}(I;n)

Lemma 3.10

Given
\beta\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}

be the multiset of entries of

This follows from definitions of

Recall that

Theorem 3.11

Although we have a recursive formula for the number of parking functions of length
n
with descent set
I\subseteq[n-1]
(Theorem
3.11
), we pose the following.

Problem 2
.

Can one give non-recursive formulas, such as that presented in Proposition
3.13
, for the number of parking functions with other interesting descent sets
I\subseteq[n-1]

4.
Peaks of Parking Functions

The Catalan numbers are one of the most well-studied integer sequences, with
214
different combinatorial explanations in the book
[
15
]
, and many more in entry
[
11
,
A000108
]
.
We now establish that the set of parking functions of length
n
that have no peaks and no ties, referred to as peakless-tieless parking functions, are a new set of Catalan objects.

We begin by setting some needed definitions and notations.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in[n]^{n}
.

•

Define the

tie set
of
\alpha
as

\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)=\{j+1\in[n]:a_{j}=a_{j+1}\}

and order the elements

\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)=\{t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{j}\}
, where
j=|\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)|
.

•

Define the
value set
of
\alpha
as

\mathrm{Val}(\alpha)=\{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{k}\},

the set of elements of
\alpha
in increasing order
1=b^{\prime}_{1}<b^{\prime}_{2}<\dots<b^{\prime}_{k}
, where
k
is the number of elements of
\alpha
.

Definition 4.2
.

Define the function
\varphi:\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}\to\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}
by

\displaystyle\varphi(\alpha)

\displaystyle=(t_{j},t_{j-1},\ldots,t_{2},t_{1},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}),

(16)

where
\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)=\{t_{j}>t_{j-1}>\cdots>t_{1}>1\}
and
\mathrm{Val}(\alpha)=\{1=b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{k}\}
are as in Definition
4.1
.

We illustrate these definitions next.

Example 4.3
.

Consider the nondecreasing parking function

\alpha=(1,1,2,3,4,4)\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{6}
.
Then
\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)=\{2,6\}
and
\mathrm{Val}(\alpha)=\{1,2,3,4\}
.
Now
\varphi(\alpha)
returns the a tuple whose first
|\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)|
elements are the integers in
\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)
arranged in decreasing order, while the remaining values are the elements of
\mathrm{Val}(\alpha)
listed in increasing order. Namely
\varphi(\alpha)=(6,2,1,2,3,4)
.
The result is a peakless-tieless parking function, as claimed.

Theorem 4.4
.

The function
\varphi:\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}\to\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}
in Definition
4.2
is a bijection.

Proof.

Well-defined.
Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
be as given in Definition
4.2
.
In this construction,
|\mathrm{Val}(\alpha)|=k
is the number of values appearing in
\alpha
and
|\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)|=j
is the number of repeated values appearing in
\alpha
.
A value in
\alpha
either repeats or appears exactly once; this implies that
k+j=n
.
Moreover, the construction in (
16
) ensures
b_{1}=1
since the letter
1
must appear in
\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
.
Also, the letters before
1
(if any) decrease down to
1
, and the letters after
1
(if any) increase.
Hence,
\varphi(\alpha)
has no peaks and no ties.
Additionally, note that all cars will still be able to park with preferences
\varphi(\alpha)
.
To see this, note that
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
.
Cars will park in the order they arrive: car
1
parks in spot
1
, car
2
in spot
2
, and so on.
To show that
\varphi(\alpha)
parks, we define
\gamma=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
from
\alpha
by replacing each
a_{i}
where
a_{i}=a_{i-1}
with
i
.
Because
\alpha
parks, each unchanged preference will satisfy
g_{i}\leq i
, and if
g_{j}
is a changed preference,
g_{j}=j\leq j
.
Thus, we conclude that
\gamma
parks.
Now, since
\gamma
and
\varphi(\alpha)
may be permuted into each other,
\varphi(\alpha)
must also park.
Therefore, the nondecreasing rearrangement of
\varphi(\alpha)=(t_{j},t_{j-1},\ldots,t_{1},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})
denoted
(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n}^{\prime})
will satisfy
a_{i}^{\prime}\leq i
for all
i
.
Thus,
\varphi(\alpha)\in\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}
.

Injective.
Let
\alpha,~\beta\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
, and let
\varphi(\alpha)=\varphi(\beta)
.
This implies that
\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Tie}(\beta)
and
\mathrm{Val}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Val}(\beta).

Notice that nondecreasing parking functions
x\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
are completely determined by their tie sets,
\mathrm{Tie}(x)
, and their value sets,
\mathrm{Val}(x)
.
When reconstructing

\alpha
and
\beta
from the value set and the tie set, the repeated values in
x
will be the same for both
\varphi(\alpha)
and
\varphi(\beta)
.
Thus,
\alpha=\beta
.

Surjective.
Let
\beta=(t_{j},t_{j-1},\ldots,t_{2},t_{1},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})\in\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}
, where

T=\{1<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{j-1}<t_{j}\}

and
B=\{1=b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{k}\}
.
It is sufficient to find
\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
with
\mathrm{Tie}(\alpha)=T
and
\mathrm{Val}(\alpha)=B
.
Order the elements of
X=[n]\setminus T=\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n-j}\}
.
Construct

\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
as follows.

(1)

In
\alpha
, place the elements of
B
in order
1=b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{k}
at the indices indexed by
X
in order
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n-j}=x_{k}
. Namely, let
a_{x_{i}}=b_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

(2)

For any
j\in T
, set
a_{j}=a_{j-1}
.

Since
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
is a nondecreasing tuple, it parks if and only if
a_{i}\leq i
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
If
a_{i}=a_{i-1}
and
a_{i-1}\leq i-1
, then
a_{i}\leq i
.
Therefore, it is sufficient to check that
a_{x_{i}}=b_{i}\leq x_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n-j
.

Let
P=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})
be
\beta
in nondecreasing order.
This is a parking function, so
p_{i}\leq i
.

(1)

If there is no duplicate in
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i})
:
t_{1}>i
.
Then in
P
,
t_{1}
has index at lest
i+1
, implying
\mathrm{Val}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i})\subset\mathrm{Val}(p_{1},\ldots p_{i}).

(2)

If there is a duplicate: without loss of generality, we assume it is
a_{i-1}=a_{i}
.

Then
\mathrm{Val}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1})\subset\mathrm{Val}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1})
.
Notice then, that since
a_{i-1}=a_{i}
, we have

\mathrm{Val}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i})=\mathrm{Val}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1})\subset\mathrm{Val}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1})\subseteq\mathrm{Val}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}).

Because of this, we satisfy the claim.
If we take
a_{i}\in\alpha
, we can find some
\ell_{i}\leq i
such that
a_{i}=p_{\ell_{i}}\leq\ell_{i}\leq i
.
Then
a_{i}\leq i
and
\alpha\in\mathrm{PF}^{\uparrow}_{n}
.∎

Corollary 4.6
.

If
\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}(i)=\{\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}:a_{n}=i\}
, then

|\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}(i)|=C_{n,i}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
and
i\in[n]
.
Note that when
n=1
, the set
|\mathrm{PTPF}_{1}(1)|=|\{(1)\}|=1=C_{1,1}
. When
n=2
, the set
|\mathrm{PTPF}_{2}(1)|=|\{(2,1)\}|=1=C_{2,1}
and
|\mathrm{PTPF}_{2}(2)|=|\{(1,2)\}|=1=C_{2,2}
.

Assume for induction that for any
n\leq k
and
i\leq n
,
|\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}(i)|=C_{n,i}
.
Let
\beta=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1},i)\in\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}(i)
.
Prepending
n+1
to
\beta
yields
(n+1,b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1},i)\in\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}(i)
, and by induction we have constructed
C_{n,i}
many elements of
\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}(i)
.
For any element
\gamma=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1},j)\in\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}(j)
with
1\leq j\leq i-1
, appending
i
to
\gamma
yields

(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1},j,i)\in\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}(i)
,
and we construct
\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}C_{n,j}
elements.
These constructions yield distinct elements of
\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}(i)
since the construction creates tuples beginning with
n+1
, while the second begins with
b_{1}\in[n]
.
As these are the only possible values with which the tuple may begin, applying the induction hypothesis yields

|\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}(i)|=C_{n,i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}C_{n,j}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}C_{n,j}=C_{n+1,i}

where the last equality holds by (
17
).
∎

4.1.
Valleys of Parking Functions

In permutations, the map
i\to n-i+1
gives a bijection on permutations, which sends peaks to valleys.
This establishes that the number of permutations with
k
peaks is the same as the number of permutations with
k
valleys.
However, this map is not well-defined for parking functions.
For example,
(1,1,1)
goes to
(3,3,3)
which is not even a parking function.
Moreover, the number of parking functions with
k
peaks is not the same as the number with
k
valleys.
For example when
n=4
, there are five peakless-tieless parking functions:

\mathrm{PTPF}_{n}=\{(1,2,3),(3,2,1),(2,1,2),(3,1,2),(2,1,3)\}.

Whereas, there are six parking functions with no ties and no valleys:

(1,2,3),(3,2,1),(1,2,1),(1,3,1),(1,3,2),(2,3,1).

We now consider the set of valleyless-tieless parking functions; parking functions for which there is no index
1\leq i\leq n-1
such that
a_{i}=a_{i+1}
, nor index
2\leq i\leq n-1
such that
a_{i-1}>a_{i}<a_{i+1}
. We let
\mathrm{VTPF}_{n}
denote the set of valleyless-tieless parking functions of length
n
. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 4.7
.

Valleyless-tieless parking functions of length
n
are enumerated by
|\mathrm{VTPF}_{n}|=F_{n+2}
, where

F_{n}
is the
n
th Fine number (as defined in
[
3
]
) with the first ten values of the sequence being
(1\leq n\leq 10):

1,2,6,18,57,186,622,2120,7338,25724.

To prove Theorem
4.7
, in Subsection
4.2
,
we establish a bijection between the set of valleyless-tieless parking functions and certain types of peakless-tieless parking functions. In Subsection
4.3
, we show that the special set of peakless-tieless parking functions are counted by the Fine numbers. This result will then imply Theorem
4.7
.

4.2.
Bijective Maps

We let
\mathrm{VTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}=1,a_{n+1}>1)
be the set of valleyless-tieless parking functions of length
n+1
in which the first value is one and the last is larger than one.
Similarly,
\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}

is the set of parking functions of length
n+1
for which there is no index
i\in[n]
such that
a_{i}=a_{i+1}
or
a_{i-1}<a_{i}>a_{i+1}
.
We refer to
\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}
as the set of peakless-tieless parking functions of length
n+1
.
We let

\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}>a_{n+1})
be the set of peakless-tieless parking functions of length
n+1
in which the first value is larger than the last.

Lemma 4.8
.

The function
\psi:\mathrm{VTPF}_{n}\to\mathrm{VTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}=1,a_{n+1}>1)
defined by

(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\to(1,a_{1}+1,a_{2}+1,\ldots,a_{n}+1)

is a bijection.

Proof.

First we show injectivity. If
\alpha,\beta\in\mathrm{VTPF}_{n}
with
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
,
\beta=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
and
\psi(\alpha)=\psi(\beta)
, then
a_{i}+1=b_{i}+1
for all
i\in[n]
. Then
a_{i}=b_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, so
\alpha=\beta
.

For surjectivity, suppose
\alpha=(1,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{VTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}=1,a_{n+1}>1)
.
We wish to show that
\beta=(a_{1}-1,a_{2}-1,\ldots,a_{n}-1)\in\mathrm{VTPF}_{n}
.
Note that for
i\in[n]
,
a_{i}>1
and in general an element of
\mathrm{VTPF}_{n}
can only have a 1 in the first and/or last position;
if an instance of 1 has a neighbor both to its left and right and there are no ties, then both of those neighbors are greater than 1, creating a valley and giving rise to a contradiction.
Recall
\alpha
satisfies
a_{n}>1
, so,
a_{i}>1
for all
i\in[n]
.
Moreover,
a_{i}-1\leq n
for all
i\in[n]
.
Hence,
\beta\in[n]^{n}
is a valid parking preference.

Next, we show that
\beta
is a parking function. Let
\alpha^{\uparrow}=(1,x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
be the nondecreasing rearrangement of
\alpha
.
Since
\alpha
is a parking function, we know that
1<x_{i}\leq i+1
for all
i\in[n]
. Then the nondecreasing rearrangement
\beta^{\uparrow}
of
\beta
is
(x_{1}-1,x_{2}-1,\ldots,x_{n}-1)
, which satisfies

0<x_{n}-1\leq i
, so
\beta
parks.
Removing the first element of any parking function, in particular of
\alpha
, will never create a valley or a tie that was not there before.
This ensures that
\beta
also does not have any ties nor valleys.
This then establishes that
\beta\in\mathrm{VTPF}_{n}
and satisfies
\psi(\beta)=\alpha
.
∎

Remark 5
.

Note that in a valleyless-tieless parking function, there is a unique maximal entry.
If this were not the case and the maximum value
k
appeared twice, then either they are consecutive entries in the tuple, creating a tie (a contradiction), or they are nonadjacent entries in the tuple and thus the value(s) between them would either be larger or create a valley, a contradiction.

In the following, we will need to reverse parts of a tuple.
To this end, we define the following.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in[n]^{n}
.
For any
i\in[n]
let

\displaystyle\mathrm{flick}_{i}(x)=(x_{i},x_{i-1},\ldots,x_{1},x_{n},x_{n-1},\ldots,x_{i+1})

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{flick}_{n}(x)=(x_{n},x_{n-1},\ldots,x_{2},x_{1}).

For example, if
\textbf{x}=(1,2,1,3,5,6,3,2)
, then
\mathrm{flick}_{4}(\textbf{x})=(3,1,2,1,2,3,6,5)
and
\mathrm{flick}_{6}(\textbf{x})=(6,5,3,1,2,1,2,3)
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Define the map
\varphi:\mathrm{VTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}=1,a_{n+1}>1)\to\mathrm{PTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}>a_{n+1})
as follows:
If
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n+1})\in\mathrm{VTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}=1,a_{i+1}>1)
and
i\in[n+1]
is the unique index containing the maximal entry of
v
, then

\varphi(v)=\mathrm{flick}_{i}(v).

The map
\varphi
is a bijection.

Proof.

Injective.
Let
v=(1,v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}),w=(1,w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathrm{VTPF}_{n+1}(a_{1}=1,a_{n}>1)
and assume that
\varphi(v)=\varphi(w)
.
Further, assume that the maximal entry of
v
occurs at index
i
, while the maximal entry of
w
occurs at index
j
.
There are two cases to consider.

(1)

If
i=j
, then

\varphi(v)=\varphi(w)
implies that

\mathrm{flick}_{i}(v)=(v_{i-1},v_{i-2},\ldots,v_{1},1,v_{n},v_{n-1},\ldots,v_{i})=(w_{j-1},w_{j-2},\ldots,w_{1},1,w_{n},w_{n-1},\ldots,w_{j})=\mathrm{flick}_{j}(w).

Thus,

We now give a set partition which will prove useful to prove Theorem

Definition 4.11

Remark 6
.

Let

be a peakless-tieless parking function of length

Lemma 4.12

If

Proof.

For the first equality, we establish a bijection between

.

In the reverse direction, if

, then

Theorem 4.13
.

If
