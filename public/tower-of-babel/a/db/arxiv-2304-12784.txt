Time periodic solutions and Nekhoroshev stability to non-linear massive Klein-Gordon equations in Anti-de Sitter
Athanasios Chatzikaleas, Jacques Smulevici

Abstract
. We prove the existence of time-periodic solutions to non-linear massive Klein-Gordon equations in Anti-de Sitter as well as their orbital stability over exponentially long times for certain values of the mass corresponding to completely resonant spectrum. We analyse the resonant system in the Fourier space by relying in particular on Zeilberger’s algorithm which allows for a systematic way to derive recurrence formulae for the Fourier coefficients. We also show that the derivation and analysis of the Fourier systems easily extends to other semi-linear wave equations such as the co-rotational wave map equation.



1.
Introduction

This work concerns the construction of time-periodic solutions to non-linear massive Klein-Gordon equations in the Anti-de Sitter spacetime as well as their orbital stability over exponentially long times, also called
Nekhoroshev stability
. Our main motivation is to develop heuristics, intuition and analytical methods for the rigorous study of small data dynamics to non-linear wave equations, including the Einstein equations, in the vicinity of the Anti-de Sitter spacetime, a general problem whose context we briefly recall here.

1.1.
(In-)stability of AdS

The Anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetime is the unique (up to quotients by isometry subgroups) maximally symmetric solution to the Einstein equations with a negative cosmological constant. In
[

On top of their numerical analysis, they also proposed resonant mode mixing as a mechanism responsible for the instability giving rise to transfer of energy from low to high frequencies. In the setting of the Einstein equations, this energy transfer should eventually lead to the formation of a small black hole. This important work then triggered a substantial amount of numerical and heuristic studies, such as the analysis of the resonant interactions outside from spherical symmetry in
[

In addition, Moschidis has further announced similar rigorous proofs of the AdS instability conjecture in the setting of the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system
[

Most relevant for the present work, Rostworowski-Maliborski

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathsf{L}\phi=-\partial_{x}^{2}\phi-\frac{2-2\delta\sin^{2}(x)}{\sin(x)\cos(x)}\partial_{x}\phi+\delta^{2}\phi,\quad\text{for the KG}\\
\mathfrak{L}\phi=-\partial_{x}^{2}\phi-\frac{2(\delta+2)\cos^{2}(x)-3}{\sin(x)\cos(x)}\partial_{x}\phi+(\delta+2)^{2}\phi,\quad\text{for the WM}.\end{cases}

In both cases, the

\displaystyle L\phi

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{w}\partial_{x}\left(w\partial_{x}\phi\right)+\begin{cases}\delta^{2}\phi,\quad\text{for the KG},\\
(\delta+2)^{2}\phi,\quad\text{for the WM},\\
\end{cases}

where

is a spatial weight,

\displaystyle w(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathsf{w}(x)=\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{2\delta-2}(x),\quad\text{for the KG}\\
\mathfrak{w}(x)=\sin^{2\delta+1}(x)\cos^{3}(x),\quad\text{for the WM}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Finally, we note that, for all
\delta
satisfying Assumption

and for all

, standard weighted Sobolev space arguments (see for example

endowed with the norm

3.1.
Self-adjointness

We consider the Hilbert space

\displaystyle L^{2}\left(\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right);d\mu\right),\quad d\mu(x)=w(x)dx,

(3.2)

associated with the inner product

\displaystyle(f|g)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}f(x)g(x)d\mu(x).

(3.3)

The linearized operator
L
is generated by a closed sesquilinear form

\displaystyle\alpha:H^{1}\left(\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right);d\mu\right)\times H^{1}\left(\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right);d\mu\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

defined by

\displaystyle\alpha(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\partial_{x}\phi(x)\partial_{x}\psi(x)d\mu(x)+\begin{cases}\delta^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\phi(x)\psi(x)d\mu(x),\quad\text{for the KG},\\
(\delta+2)^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\phi(x)\psi(x)d\mu(x),\quad\text{for the WM},\end{cases}

Clearly there exists positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, depending only on
\delta
, such that

\displaystyle c_{1}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}^{2}\leq\alpha(\phi,\phi)\leq c_{2}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}^{2},

for all

, hence the sesquilinear form is elliptic and strictly positive. In particular,
L
is self-adjoint on its domain

. Therefore, all the eigenvalues of

are real and any two eigenfunctions corresponding to different eigenvalues are orthogonal to each other. Moreover, by compactness, the set of eigenfunctions forms a complete basis for

.

3.2.
The linear eigenvalue problem

Next, we study the linear eigenvalue problems

\displaystyle L\phi=\lambda\phi,\quad\phi\in\mathcal{D}(L).

Setting

\displaystyle\left(1-y^{2}\right)u^{\prime\prime}(y)+((1-\delta)-(\delta+3)y)u^{\prime}(y)+\frac{1}{4}\left(\lambda-(2+\delta)^{2}\right)u(y)=0,

4.3.1.
General strategy

In the following lines, we develop a general method that yields linear recurrence relations for the Fourier coefficients from which both their asymptotic behaviour and closed formulas follow easily (Section
1.2
). In order to establish Proposition
4.2
, we study the monotonicity of the sequences

\displaystyle\left\{f(m)=\frac{C_{00mm}}{\omega_{m}^{2}}:m\geq 1\right\}

and proceed as follows. To begin with, we use several identities of orthogonal polynomials from

,
48
,
49
]
to remove the integrals in (

\displaystyle f(m)=\frac{C_{00mm}}{\omega_{m}^{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}F(m,k),

for some integer

(possibly depending on

, both given in

formulas depending on the model we consider. Most importantly,

turns out to be a hypergeometric function with respect to both arguments

and

, meaning that both

and

are rational functions of

and

. Next, we are interested in finding linear recurrence relations for the sums
f(m)
in (

with respect to

. In other words, given a hypergeometric function

and an integer

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\alpha_{j}(m)F(m+j,k)=G(m,k+1)-G(m,k).

Then, if (

, given by (

yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{J}\alpha_{j}(m)F(m+j,k)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\left(G(m,k+1)-G(m,k)\right)\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\alpha_{j}(m)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}F(m+j,k)\right)

\displaystyle=G(m,K+1)-G(m,0)\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\alpha_{j}(m)f(m+j)

\displaystyle=G(m,K+1)-G(m,0),

which is the desired recurrence relation for

. The coefficients

]
and Chapter 6 in
[

be the rescaled initial data defined by (
5.2
). Also, let
h\in H^{4}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
such that
d\mathcal{M}(\xi)[h]=0
and pick an integer
m\geq 0
. Then, according to Lemma
5.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{C_{0000}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}-2\frac{C_{00mm}}{\omega_{m}^{2}}\right)h^{(m)}=0,

(5.3)

for all integers
m\geq 1
, and

\displaystyle\omega_{0}^{2}h^{(0)}

\displaystyle=0,

(5.4)

for
m=0
. On the one hand, using the fact that
\omega_{0}\neq 0
, (
5.4
) yields
h^{(0)}=0
. On the other hand, using Proposition
4.2
, (
5.3
) yields
h^{(m)}=0
, for all integers
m\geq 1
. Consequently, the condition
\ker\left(d\mathcal{M}(\xi)\right)=0
implies

and completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we conclude this section by establishing the regularity conditions required in Theorem

for both models. To begin with, we consider the KG model given by (

Lemma 5.4

(Regularity conditions in Theorem
2.6
for the KG model)
.

Fix
\delta
satisfying Assumption
2.1
and let

\displaystyle f^{(3)}(\phi)=-\mathsf{W}\phi^{3},\quad f^{(4)}(\phi)=0,

for all
\phi\in H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
, with

, where
\mathsf{w}
is given by (
3.1
) and

is given by (

s\geq 2
, we have that

•

f^{(3)}:H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)\rightarrow H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
is a bounded homogeneous polynomial of degree three,

•

f^{(3)}
leaves invariant the domain
H^{s+2}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
.

Proof.

Recall that
H^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
is an algebra, for
s\geq 2>3/2
and that
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}
, for all
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
, by the Sobolev inequality. First, we consider the conformal case
\delta=2
. In this case, the original KG model on AdS for a scalar field
\psi
is conformally equivalent to a KG equation on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
for the scalar field
u=\cos^{-1}(x)\psi
, with the conformal map preserving the
H^{s}
regularity. The KG equation (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) is obtained by rescaling the unknown
\phi
related to

by
\phi=\cos^{-1}(x)u
. Then, for all
s\geq 2
, we use the algebra property to estimate

\displaystyle\|f^{(3)}(\phi)\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}

\displaystyle=\|\mathsf{W}\phi^{3}\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}\simeq\|\cos(x)\mathsf{W}\phi^{3}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\|u^{3}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\lesssim\|u\|^{3}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}.

Similarly, we can transfer the well-known bounds for
H^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
to their AdS counterparts which completes the proof for the case
\delta=2
. Finally, for all
\delta\geq 2
, the

norms are stronger than in the conformal case
\delta=2
, so the estimate above still holds true. We shall omit the details here since we explain both the conformal transformation and the comparison of the norms more precisely below in the proof of Hardy-Sobolev inequality for the KG model, see Lemma
6.1
.
∎

Next, we consider the WM model given by (
2.11
).

Lemma 5.5

(Regularity conditions in Theorem
2.6
for the WM model)
.

Fix
\delta
satisfying Assumption
2.1
and let

\displaystyle f^{(3)}(\phi)=-\mathfrak{W}\phi^{3},\quad f^{(4)}(\phi)=\mathfrak{E}(\cdot,\phi)

for all
\phi\in H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
, with
d\mu=\mathfrak{w}dx
, where
\mathfrak{w}
is given by (
3.1
) and both
\mathfrak{W}
and
\mathfrak{E}
are given by (
2.12
). Then, for all
s>\delta+1
, we have that

•

f^{(3)}:H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)\rightarrow H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
is a bounded homogeneous polynomial of degree three,

•

f^{(3)}
leaves invariant the domain
H^{s+2}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
,

•

f^{(\geq 4)}(\phi)
is differentiable in
H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
, its differentiable is a Lipschitz map and satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\|df^{(\geq 4)}(\phi_{1})-df^{(\geq 4)}(\phi_{2})\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}\lesssim\epsilon^{3}\|\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)},

for all
\|\phi_{1}\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}\lesssim\epsilon
,
\|\phi_{2}\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}\lesssim\epsilon
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta
satisfying Assumption
2.1
and let
s>\delta+1
. All claims as stated above follow directly from the algebra property

\displaystyle\|\phi\psi\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}\lesssim\|\phi\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}\|\psi\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)},

(5.5)

for all
\phi,\psi\in H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
with
d\mu=\mathfrak{w}dx
where
\mathfrak{w}
is given by (
3.1
). To prove the latter, recall that
\mathfrak{w}(x)=\sin^{2\delta+1}(x)\cos^{3}(x)
as well as, for any
\phi
,

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}=\begin{cases}\|L^{k}\phi\|_{L^{2}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)},&\text{ for }s=2k,,\quad k\in\mathbb{N},\\
\|L^{k}\phi\|_{H^{1}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)},&\text{ for }s=2k+1,\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

Let
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
be two smooth cut-off functions such that

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{4}\right],\\
0,&x\in\left[\frac{3\pi}{8},\frac{\pi}{2}\right],\end{cases}\quad\chi_{2}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{8}\right],\\
1,&x\in\left[\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2}\right].\end{cases}

Since only local operators are involved in the definition of the
H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
norm, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}\lesssim\|\chi_{1}\phi\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}+\|\chi_{2}\phi\|_{H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)},

where now
\chi_{i}\phi
vanish in neighbourhoods of the two endpoints of
(0,\pi/2)
. We only focus on a neighbourhood of zero, since the other case can be treated similarly. For this, consider the warped product manifold
(0,\pi/2)\times\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}
endowed with the metric

\displaystyle g(x,\omega)=dx^{2}+\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{\frac{6}{2\delta+1}}(x)\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}}(\omega),

where
\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}}
is the standard round metric on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}
of dimension
2\delta+1
. Its volume form is given by
d\text{vol}=\sqrt{|g|}dx=\sqrt{\det(g)}dx=\sin^{2\delta+1}\cos^{3}(x)dx=\mathfrak{w}dx=d\mu
. Let
\Delta_{g}
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator associated to the metric
g
. Formally, when restricted to radial functions
\phi=\phi(x)
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{|g|}g^{xx}\partial_{x}\phi\right)=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{w}}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{w}\partial_{x}\phi\right)=-\mathfrak{L}\phi+(\delta+2)^{2}\phi,

where the definition of
\mathfrak{L}
is given in Section
3
. Moreover, we claim that the warped product manifold above is regular at
x=0
. Indeed, introduce Cartesian coordinates
(x^{1},\dots,x^{2\delta+2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\delta+2}\simeq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x^{1}&=x\cos(\omega_{1}),\\
x^{2}&=x\sin(\omega_{1})\cos(\omega_{2}),\\
x^{3}&=x\sin(\omega_{1})\sin(\omega_{2})\cos(\omega_{3}),\\
\vdots&\quad\quad\quad\vdots\quad\quad\quad\vdots\quad\quad\quad\vdots\\
x^{2\delta+1}&=x\sin(\omega_{1})\sin(\omega_{2})\sin(\omega_{3})\dots\sin(\omega_{2\delta+1})\cos(\omega_{2\delta+1}),\\
x^{2\delta+2}&=x\sin(\omega_{1})\sin(\omega_{2})\sin(\omega_{3})\dots\sin(\omega_{2\delta+1})\sin(\omega_{2\delta+1}).\end{cases}

We know that the standard Euclidean metric
\delta_{\mathbb{E}}
is given by
\delta_{\mathbb{E}}(x,\omega)=dx^{2}+x^{2}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}}(\omega)
, thus we have

\displaystyle g(x,\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathbb{E}}(x,\omega)+\left(\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{\frac{6}{2\delta+1}}(x)-x^{2}\right)\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathbb{E}}(x,\omega)+x^{4}f(x^{2})\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathbb{E}}(x,\omega)+f(x^{2})\left(x^{2}\delta_{\mathbb{E}}(x,\omega)-x^{2}dx^{2}\right),

for some smooth function
f
. Obviously,
x^{2}
is a smooth function with respect to the Cartesian coordinates
x=(x^{i})
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle dx=\frac{x_{i}}{x}dx^{i},\quad dx^{2}=\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{x^{2}}dx^{i}dx^{j},

hence
x^{2}dx^{2}=x_{i}x_{j}dx^{i}dx^{j}
is also smooth at
x=0
with respect to the Cartesian coordinates
x=(x^{i})
. In conclusion, the warped product manifold
(0,\pi/2)\times\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1}
is smooth and the weighted Sobolev space
H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
we consider coincides with the Sobolev space
H_{\text{rad}}^{s}((0,\pi/2)\times\mathbb{S}^{2\delta+1})
of radial functions on the manifold. Then, by standard Sobolev regularity, the algebra property (
5.5
) holds for any
\phi,\psi\in H^{s}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
with compact support in
[0,3\pi/8]
provided that
s>(2\delta+2)/2=\delta+1
, that completes the proof.
∎

6.
Stability of the solutions to the linear wave equation

In this section, we establish the non-linear stability over exponentially long times to solutions to the KG equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\phi+L\phi=-\epsilon^{2}W\phi^{3},

(6.1)

for a scalar field
\phi:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi/2)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
L
and
W
are given by (
2.4
). To do so, we will apply Theorem
2.7
to the same rescaled first 1-mode given by (
5.2
) and verify the conditions (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) of Theorem
2.7
. We follow the setting in
[
5
]
and rewrite (
6.1
) in the phase space

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathcal{P}=H^{1}\left((0,\pi/2);d\mu\right)\times L^{2}\left((0,\pi/2);d\mu\right)

using symbols in bold to denote its elements. In particular, for any
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi,\partial_{t}\phi)\in\mathcal{P}
, the non-linear wave equation (
6.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\phi}=A\boldsymbol{\phi}+\epsilon^{2}f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),

(6.2)

where
A
is a linear operator defined by

\displaystyle A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{P},\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-L&0\end{pmatrix}

and

\displaystyle f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-W\phi^{3}\end{pmatrix}.

(6.3)

Furthermore, for any
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
, let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{t}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}):=e^{At}\boldsymbol{\zeta}

be the solution to the initial value problem consisting of the linearized equation in (
6.2
) coupled to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
as initial data,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{linear}}(t)=A\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{linear}}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{linear}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\zeta}\end{cases}

(6.4)

and denote by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{linear}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\left\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{t}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}):t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

its trajectory in the phase space
\mathcal{P}
. In addition, let
\xi_{1}
be the same rescale of the first 1-mode as in (
5.2
), and set

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),\quad\xi_{1}=\kappa_{0}e_{0},\quad\xi_{2}=0,\quad\kappa_{0}=\pm\omega_{0}\sqrt{\frac{8}{3C_{0000}}}.

(6.5)

To begin with, we endow the phase space
\mathcal{P}
with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{p},\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle p_{1},\tilde{p}_{1}\rangle_{H^{1}(0,\pi/2)}+\langle p_{2},\tilde{p}_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\pi/2)}=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\partial_{x}p_{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{p}_{1}+p_{1}\tilde{p}_{1}\right)d\mu+\int_{0}^{\pi/2}p_{2}\tilde{p}_{2}d\mu,

for all
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=(\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
, and a symplectic form
\Omega:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\Omega(\mathbf{p},\tilde{\mathbf{p}})=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(p_{1}\tilde{p}_{2}-p_{2}\tilde{p}_{1}\right)d\mu,

for all
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=(\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
. Notice that, since
L
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
(Section
3
), the operator
A
is skew-symmetric with respect to
\Omega
, meaning that

\displaystyle\Omega(A\mathbf{p},\tilde{\mathbf{p}})=-\Omega(\mathbf{p},A\tilde{\mathbf{p}}),

for all
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=(\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
with
p_{1},\tilde{p}_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(L)
.
Moreover, we note that

\displaystyle\Omega(A\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\Omega\left((p_{2},-Lp_{1}),(p_{1},p_{2})\right)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(p_{2}^{2}+p_{1}Lp_{1}\right)d\mu=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(p_{2}^{2}+\left(L^{1/2}p_{1}\right)^{2}\right)d\mu.

The Hamiltonian function

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\phi})+\epsilon^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\phi}),

where

\displaystyle h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\frac{1}{2}\Omega(A\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\quad f(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}W\phi_{1}^{4}d\mu.

(6.6)

We call
h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
the harmonic energy of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. Then, the partial differential equation (
6.1
) is Hamiltonian and formally one has

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(\phi,\partial_{t}\phi)=0.

In fact, Theorem
2.7
is based a perturbation analysis on the surface of constant harmonic energy

\displaystyle\Sigma=\left\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathcal{P}:~h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\kappa_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}\right\}.

As usual in perturbation theory, one also needs an analyticity assumption for the perturbation. In the case of the KG model, this is a consequence of the following Hardy-Sobolev inequality. The following lemma guarantees the analyticity conditions of Theorem
2.7
.

Lemma 6.1

(Hardy-Sobolev inequality)
.

Fix
\delta
satisfying Assumption
2.1
and define
f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
and
f(\boldsymbol{\phi})
by (
6.3
) and (
6.6
) respectively. Then, there exists a positive constant
c
so that

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq c\left(h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right)^{2},\quad\|f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{\mathcal{P}}\leq c\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{P}}^{3},

for all
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta
satisfying Assumption
2.1
and define
f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
and
f(\boldsymbol{\phi})
by (
6.3
) and (
6.6
) respectively for all
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi,\partial_{t}\phi)\in\mathcal{P}
. By density (Section
3.1
), we can assume that
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in C^{\infty}(0,\pi/2)
. Firstly, we focus on
f(\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq c\left(h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right)^{2}
and prove that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathsf{W}\phi^{4}d\mu\right)^{1/4}\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\phi\mathsf{L}\phi d\mu\right)^{1/2},

(6.7)

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}(x)

\displaystyle=\cos^{2(\delta-1)}(x),\quad\mathsf{w}(x)=\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{2(\delta-1)}(x),

\displaystyle d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{w}(x)dx,\quad\mathsf{L}\phi=-\frac{1}{\mathsf{w}}\partial_{x}\left(\mathsf{w}\partial_{x}\phi\right)+\delta^{2}\phi.

We set
\psi=\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\phi
so that
\psi(\pi/2)=0
and compute

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}\partial_{x}\phi

\displaystyle=\mathsf{w}\partial_{x}(\cos^{-(\delta-1)}(x)\psi)

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{2\delta-2}(x)[(\delta-1)\cos^{-\delta}(x)\sin(x)\psi+\cos^{-\delta+1}(x)\partial_{x}\psi]

\displaystyle=(\delta-1)\sin^{3}(x)\cos^{\delta-2}(x)\psi+\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\partial_{x}\psi,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\mathsf{w}\partial_{x}\phi\right)

\displaystyle=(\delta-1)\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{3}(x)\cos^{\delta-2}(x)\psi\right)

\displaystyle+\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right)+\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\partial_{x}\left(\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=3(\delta-1)\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\psi-(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\sin^{4}(x)\cos^{\delta-3}(x)\psi

\displaystyle+(\delta-1)\sin^{3}(x)\cos^{\delta-2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi+\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right)

\displaystyle-(\delta-1)\sin^{3}(x)\cos^{\delta-2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi

\displaystyle=3(\delta-1)\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\psi-(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\sin^{4}(x)\cos^{\delta-3}(x)\psi

\displaystyle+\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathsf{w}}\partial_{x}\left(\mathsf{w}\partial_{x}\phi\right)

\displaystyle=3(\delta-1)\cos^{-\delta+1}(x)\psi-(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\cos^{-\delta-1}(x)\sin^{2}(x)\psi

\displaystyle+\cos^{-\delta+1}(x)\sin^{-2}(x)\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right),

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}\phi

\displaystyle=-3(\delta-1)\cos^{-\delta+1}(x)\psi+(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\cos^{-\delta-1}(x)\sin^{2}(x)\psi

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\cos^{-\delta+1}(x)\psi-\cos^{-\delta+1}(x)\sin^{-2}(x)\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right),

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}\mathsf{L}\phi

\displaystyle=-3(\delta-1)\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\psi+(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\sin^{4}(x)\cos^{\delta-3}(x)\psi

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\sin^{2}(x)\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\psi-\cos^{\delta-1}(x)\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right)

and hence

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}\phi\mathsf{L}\phi

\displaystyle=-3(\delta-1)\sin^{2}(x)\psi^{2}+(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\sin^{4}(x)\cos^{-2}(x)\psi^{2}

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\sin^{2}(x)\psi^{2}-\psi\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right).

Now, using the Dirichlet boundary condition
\psi(\pi/2)=0
, integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\psi\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right)dx=-\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\partial_{x}\psi\right)^{2}\sin^{2}(x)dx

and hence we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\phi\mathsf{L}\phi d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathsf{w}\phi\mathsf{L}\phi dx

\displaystyle=-3(\delta-1)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{2}(x)\psi^{2}dx+(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{4}(x)\cos^{-2}(x)\psi^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{2}(x)\psi^{2}dx-\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\psi\partial_{x}\left(\sin^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\psi\right)dx

\displaystyle=\left[\delta^{2}-3(\delta-1)\right]\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\psi^{2}\sin^{2}(x)dx+(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\tan^{2}(x)\psi^{2}\sin^{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\partial_{x}\psi\right)^{2}\sin^{2}(x)dx.

Notice that

\displaystyle\delta^{2}-3(\delta-1)\geq 1,\quad(\delta-1)(\delta-2)\geq 0,

for all integers
\delta\geq 2
, and consequently we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\phi\mathsf{L}\phi d\mu

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\psi^{2}\sin^{2}(x)dx+\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\partial_{x}\psi\right)^{2}\sin^{2}(x)dx.

We use once again the Dirichlet boundary condition
\psi(\pi/2)=0
to extend
\psi:(0,\pi/2)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to a smooth function
\psi:(0,\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
imposing the reflection symmetry
\psi(x)=-\psi(\pi-x)
, for all
x\in(0,\pi)
. Furthermore, let
f:\mathbb{S}^{3}=(0,\pi)\times(0,\pi)\times(0,2\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x,\vartheta,\varphi)=\psi(x)
and note that
d\text{vol}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}(x,\vartheta,\varphi)=\sin^{2}(x)\sin(\vartheta)dxd\vartheta d\varphi
is the volume form on the entire
3
-sphere. On the one hand, we have that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\phi\mathsf{L}\phi d\mu\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left[\psi^{2}+\left(\partial_{x}\psi\right)^{2}\right]\sin^{2}(x)dx\right)^{1/2}\simeq\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[\psi^{2}+\left(\partial_{x}\psi\right)^{2}\right]\sin^{2}(x)dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\simeq\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}\left[f^{2}+\left(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}f\right)^{2}\right]d\text{vol}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}\right)^{1/2}=\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}.

(6.8)

On the other hand, we also have that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathsf{W}\phi^{4}d\mu\right)^{1/4}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\psi^{4}\sin^{2}(x)dx\right)^{1/4}\simeq\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\psi^{4}\sin^{2}(x)dx\right)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\simeq\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}f^{4}d\text{vol}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}\right)^{1/4}\simeq\|f\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}.

(6.9)

In view of (
6
) and (
6
), in order to prove (
6.7
), it suffices to show that there exists a positive constant so that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}.

The latter is a direct consequence of the standard Sobolev inequality on the 3-sphere (Theorem 5.1, page 121 in
[
31
]
),

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})},

together with the embedding

since
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is compact. Finally, we focus on the second estimate and prove that

. To this end, we proceed as above and obtain

\displaystyle\|f^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathsf{W}\phi^{3}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)}=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathsf{W}^{2}(x)\phi^{6}(x)d\mu(x)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\psi^{6}(x)\sin^{2}(x)dx

Next, we compute the harmonic energy in the Fourier space.

Lemma 6.2

(Harmonic energy)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
be expanded in terms of the eigenfunctions
\{e_{m}:m\geq 0\}
to the linearized operator
L
,

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi_{\alpha}^{(m)}(t)e_{m},\quad\alpha\in\{1,2\}.

Then,

\displaystyle h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(\phi_{2}^{(m)})^{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\omega_{m}^{2}(\phi_{1}^{(m)})^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
and expand its coefficients in terms of the eigenfunctions
\{e_{m}:m\geq 0\}
to the linearized operator
L
,

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi_{\alpha}^{(m)}(t)e_{m},\quad\alpha\in\{1,2\}.

Then, we use the fact that the set of eigenfunctions
\{e_{m}:m\geq 0\}
is orthonormal with respect to the inner product in
L^{2}((0,\pi/2);d\mu)
, to compute that

\displaystyle h_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Omega(A\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\phi_{2}^{2}+\left(L^{1/2}\phi_{1}\right)^{2}\right)d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\phi_{2}^{(i)}\phi_{2}^{(j)}e_{i}(x)e_{j}(x)+\phi_{1}^{(i)}\phi_{1}^{(j)}L^{1/2}e_{i}(x)L^{1/2}e_{j}(x)\right)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\left(\phi_{2}^{(i)}\phi_{2}^{(j)}+\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\phi_{1}^{(i)}\phi_{1}^{(j)}\right)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}e_{i}(x)e_{j}(x)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\left(\phi_{2}^{(i)}\phi_{2}^{(j)}+\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\phi_{1}^{(i)}\phi_{1}^{(j)}\right)\delta_{ij}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(\phi_{2}^{(m)})^{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\omega_{m}^{2}(\phi_{1}^{(m)})^{2},

that completes the proof.
