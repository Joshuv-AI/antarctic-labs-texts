Nodal gaps from local interactions in Sr 2 RuO 4
Aline Ramires

Abstract
Sr 2 RuO 4 has been under intensive scrutiny over the past years after new NMR measurements unveiled that the superconducting state might be a spin singlet. One of the best order parameter candidates in light of these new experiments is a chiral d-wave state with d x ​ z + i ​ d y ​ z d_{xz}+id_{yz} symmetry. This order parameter has been overlooked given the strong two-dimensional character of the normal state electronic structure Recently, a phenomenological proposal based on local interactions with a three-dimensional electronic structure showed that a chiral d-wave state can be stable in Sr 2 RuO 4 once momentum-dependent spin-orbit coupling is properly taken into account. Here we discuss the origin of the nodes and dips in this order parameter as inherited from the normal state Hamiltonian, showing that a nodal gap can emerge out of purely local interactions and connect the presence of nodes with the superconducting fitness measure.

Introduction

Sr
2
RuO
4
was for long thought to be the best candidate material to host a chiral p-wave superconducting state
[
1
,
2
]
. Recent Knight shift experiments, displaying a significant reduction of the spin susceptibility across the superconducting transition usually associated with spin singlet states, challenged this proposal
[
3
,
4
]
. Complementary experiments such as Kerr effect
[
5
]
and muon spin resonance
[
6
,
7
]
indicate time-reversal symmetry breaking in the superconducting state, and ultrasound attenuation experiments
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
suggest a multi-component order parameter. Together, these experiments suggest an order parameter in the
E_{g}
irreducible representation of the
D_{4h}
point group. This order parameter has two components, one with
d_{xz}
and another with
d_{yz}
symmetry. In its chiral form, usually referred to as chiral d-wave, or
d_{xz}+id_{yz}
state, the order parameter breaks time-reversal symmetry. This order parameter candidate was for a long time overlooked within the assumption that a superconducting state with horizontal line nodes requires strong inter-layer interactions, what is at a first sight in contradiction with the strongly two-dimensional Fermi surfaces of Sr
2
RuO
4

[
11
]
.

Recently, the author and collaborators have proposed a picture with local pairing in which a chiral d-wave order parameter is stable
[
12
]
. The main ingredients in this phenomenological theory are strong Hund’s coupling and a faithful description of the normal state in three dimensions, including momentum dependent spin-orbit coupling. The projected gap at the Fermi surfaces have horizontal line nodes at the
k_{z}=\{0,\pm 2\pi\}
planes, and minima along the diagonals
k_{x}=\pm k_{y}
, reminiscent of a
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
gap. The aim of this proceeding article is to discuss how these qualitative features of the gap come about. The main aspect to be highlighted is the construction of the normal state and superconducting order parameters from a microscopic perspective, within the orbital basis. Once the analysis is translated to the band basis, these unexpected features emerge.

2
The normal state Hamiltonian

Following the notation introduced in
[
12
]
, we can describe the normal state of Sr
2
RuO
4
by a three-dimensional three-orbital model. We define the spinor operator
\Phi^{\dagger}_{{\mathbb{k}}}=(c_{{\mathbb{k}},yz{\uparrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},yz{\downarrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},xz{\uparrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},xz{\downarrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},xy{\uparrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},xy{\downarrow}}^{\dagger})
, where
c^{\dagger}_{{\mathbb{k}},\gamma s}
creates an electron with momentum
{\mathbb{k}}
, orbital
\gamma
, and spin
s
, in terms of which we can write the most general single-particle Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\sum_{{\mathbb{k}}}\Phi^{\dagger}_{\mathbb{k}}\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\Phi_{\mathbb{k}}
,
with

\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})=\sum_{a=0}^{8}\sum_{b=0}^{3}h_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})\,\hat{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{b}
.
Here
\hat{\lambda}_{a}
are Gell-Mann matrices encoding the orbital degrees of freedom (DOF),
\hat{\sigma}_{b}
are Pauli matrices encoding the spin DOF
(
\hat{\lambda}_{0}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
are unit matrices), and
h_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})

are real and even functions of momentum. In presence of spacial inversion and time-reversal symmetries, out of the 36 pairs of indexes
(a,b)
, only 15 are symmetry allowed. These are described in detail in the supplementary material of Suh et al.
[
12
]
. With parameters obtained from first principle calculations, this model generates three almost cylindrical Fermi surfaces with a non-trivial orbital and spin texture (see for example Veenstra et al.
[
13
]
).

For the analytical discussion below, it is useful to focus on the normal state Hamiltonian along specific planes. If we look along the
XZ
(
YZ
) plane we find only the
\beta
and
\gamma
bands, the former composed of
d_{xz}
(
d_{yz}
) and the later of
d_{xy}
orbitals. This allows us to construct simpler two-orbital models along these planes,

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{XZ(YZ)}=\sum_{{\mathbb{k}}}\Psi^{\dagger}_{\mathbb{k}}\hat{H}_{0}^{XZ(YZ)}({\mathbb{k}})\Psi_{\mathbb{k}},

in the basis
\Psi^{\dagger}_{\mathbb{k}}=(c_{{\mathbb{k}},xz(yz){\uparrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},xz(yz){\downarrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},xy{\uparrow}}^{\dagger},\allowbreak c_{{\mathbb{k}},xy{\downarrow}}^{\dagger})
, with

\hat{H}_{0}^{XZ(YZ)}({\mathbb{k}})=\sum_{a,b=0}^{3}\tilde{h}_{ab}^{XZ(YZ)}({\mathbb{k}})\,\hat{\tau}_{a}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{b},

where the
\tilde{h}_{ab}^{XZ(YZ)}({\mathbb{k}})
are real even functions of momentum, and both
\hat{\tau}_{a}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{b}
are Pauli matrices encoding the orbital and the spin DOF, respectively (
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
and
\hat{\tau}_{0}
are identity matrices).
There are, in principle, 16 pairs of indexes
(a,b)
but in the presence of time-reversal and inversion symmetries these are constrained to only six. The symmetry-allowed terms are listed in Table
1
, accompanied by the explicit form of the dominant terms for each plane, as well as the associated physical process.

3
Local order parameters

In analogy to the normal state Hamiltonian, we parametrize the superconducting order parameters for the three orbital model as

\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})=\sum_{a,b}d_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})\,\hat{\lambda}_{a}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{b}\,(i\hat{\sigma}_{2}),

with momentum-dependent functions
d_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})
carrying information about its magnitude and phase. Each order parameter component is labelled by a pair of indexes
[a,b]
(note the different brackets used for the parametrization of the terms in the normal state Hamiltonian and for the order parameter). In the three orbital model, the proposed chiral d-wave state is dominated by
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{E_{g}}({\mathbb{k}})=d_{0}[\hat{\lambda}_{5}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{3}+i\hat{\lambda}_{6}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{3}](i\hat{\sigma}_{2}).

(1)

In the microscopic basis this order parameter is momentum independent (or s-wave) with magnitude
d_{0}
, antisymmetric in the orbital DOF (given the explicit form of the Gell-Mann matrices
\lambda_{5}
and
\lambda_{6}
), and a spin triplet state, with the d-vector along the z-axis.

Along the
XZ(YZ)
plane we can write the local gap matrices as

\hat{\Delta}^{XZ(YZ)}({\mathbb{k}})=\sum_{ab}d_{ab}^{XZ(YZ)}({\mathbb{k}})\,\hat{\tau}_{a}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{b}\,(i\hat{\sigma}_{2})
.
Each order parameter component is labelled by a pair of indexes with a label concerning the plane
[a,b]_{p}
, with
p=\{XZ,YZ\}
. Here we focus on local, s-wave, order parameters, which should satisfy the condition imposed by fermionic anti-symmetry,
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})=-\hat{\Delta}^{T}(-{\mathbb{k}})\Rightarrow\hat{\Delta}=-\hat{\Delta}^{T}
,
so we consider only order parameters associated with anti-symmetric matrices. These are listed in Tab.
2
.

The orbitally antisymmetric order parameter, when projected into the XZ (YZ) plane takes the form
[2,3]_{XZ(YZ)}
, as
\hat{\tau}_{2}
is the only anti-symmetric 2
\times
2 matrix. More specifically, the
[6,3]
component corresponding to an antisymmetric pair of
d_{xz}
and
d_{xy}
orbitals is associated with
[2,3]_{XZ}
along the
XZ
plane. Conversely, the
[5,3]
component corresponding to an antisymmetric pair of
d_{yz}
and
d_{xy}
orbitals is associated with
[2,3]_{YZ}
along the
YZ
plane.

4
Origin of line nodes

For this discussion, the simple 2
\times
2 models along the planes
p=\{XZ,YZ\}
are going to be useful so we can proceed analytically. As the matrix structures are the same for both planes, we drop the
p
superscript from here on. In order to determine the gap in the band basis, it is useful to look directly at the eigenvalaues of the BdG Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{BdG}({\mathbb{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})&\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\\
\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})&-\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where
\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
is the normal-state Hamiltonian and
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})=\Delta_{0}\hat{\tau}_{c}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{d}(i\hat{\sigma}_{2})
, with different gaps associated with distinct subscripts
[c,d]
, as listed in Table
2
. Here we already assume the gap to be momentum-independent with magnitude
\Delta_{0}
. The eigenvalues can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle E^{\pm,\pm}({\mathbb{k}})=\pm\sqrt{\tilde{h}_{00}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})+\tilde{{\mathbb{h}}}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})+\Delta_{0}^{2}\pm 2\sqrt{\tilde{h}_{00}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})\tilde{{\mathbb{h}}}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})+\Delta_{0}^{2}f^{2}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})}},

(3)

where
\tilde{{\mathbb{h}}}({\mathbb{k}})=(\tilde{h}_{10}({\mathbb{k}}),\tilde{h}_{30}({\mathbb{k}}),\tilde{h}_{21}({\mathbb{k}}),\tilde{h}_{22}({\mathbb{k}}),\tilde{h}_{23}({\mathbb{k}}))
is a five-dimensional vector. The function

f^{2}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})=\frac{1}{16}\text{Tr}[\tilde{F}_{C}^{0\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\tilde{F}_{C}^{0}({\mathbb{k}})],

is written in terms of the normalized superconducting fitness function
\tilde{F}_{C}^{0}({\mathbb{k}})=\hat{F}_{C}^{0}({\mathbb{k}})/\Delta_{0}
, with

\hat{F}_{C}^{0}({\mathbb{k}})=\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})-\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})
. Note that the normal-state eigenenergies are given by

E_{0}^{\pm}({\mathbb{k}})=\tilde{h}_{00}({\mathbb{k}})\pm|\tilde{{\mathbb{h}}}({\mathbb{k}})|
,
such that the Fermi surface is defined by
\tilde{h}_{00}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})=\tilde{{\mathbb{h}}}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})
. Note also that if
f^{2}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})=0
, the eigenenergies read
E^{\pm,\pm}({\mathbb{k}})=\pm\sqrt{(|\tilde{h}_{00}({\mathbb{k}})|\pm|\tilde{{\mathbb{h}}}({\mathbb{k}})|)^{2}+\Delta_{0}^{2}}
, and the superconducting state is fully gapped.

In case
f^{2}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})\neq 0
, the energy spectra in the superconducting state at the momenta belonging to the normal-state Fermi surface can then be written as

\displaystyle E^{\pm,\pm}({\mathbb{k}})\Big|_{FS}=\pm\sqrt{2\tilde{h}_{00}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})+\Delta_{0}^{2}\pm 2\sqrt{\tilde{h}_{00}^{4}({\mathbb{k}})+\Delta_{0}^{2}f^{2}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})}}.

(4)

If we now expand for small
\Delta_{0}
, we find for the low lying bands:

\displaystyle E^{\pm,-}({\mathbb{k}})\Big|_{FS}\approx\pm\Delta_{0}\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{f_{C}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})}{\tilde{h}_{00}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})}\right)},

(5)

and we can
define the gap structure in the band basis as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{B}({\mathbb{k}})=\left[E^{+,-}({\mathbb{k}})\Big|_{FS}-E^{-,-}({\mathbb{k}})\Big|_{FS}\right]/2\approx\frac{\Delta_{0}}{|\tilde{h}_{00}({\mathbb{k}})|}\sqrt{\tilde{h}_{00}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})-f_{C}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})}.

(6)

This definition is in direct analogy to the single band problem with normal state dispersion
\epsilon({\mathbb{k}})
. The dispersion in the superconducting state is
E^{\pm}({\mathbb{k}})=\pm\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}({\mathbb{k}})+\Delta^{2}}
, so we can identify the gap at the Fermi surface by taking
\epsilon({\mathbb{k}})=0
, and defining
[E^{+}({\mathbb{k}})|_{FS}-E^{-}({\mathbb{k}})|_{FS}]/2=|\Delta|
.

From Eq.
6
, it is clear that the condition for the presence of nodes at the normal state Fermi surface is:

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{00}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})-f_{C}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})=0\hskip 28.45274pt\text{or}\hskip 28.45274pt\tilde{{\mathbb{h}}}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})-f_{C}^{2}({\mathbb{k}})=0.

(7)

These equations indicate that there can be nodes in the superconducting spectrum of Sr
2
RuO
4
given the nontrivial form factors originated from the normal-state Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
, even though the gap in the orbital basis is isotropic (s-wave). Note that the fitness function
\hat{F}^{0}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})
appears in this context as a key parameter defining the nodes, reinforcing its importance for the understanding of the phenomenology of superconducting states in complex materials
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
12
]
. As a final note, keeping higher order terms in
\Delta_{0}^{2}
in the expansion above, we find structures associated with Bogoliubov Fermi surfaces, which can be understood as inflated nodes
[
19
,
20
]
. We are not going to discuss these further here.

5
Origin of gap minima along the diagonals

A second feature of the chiral d-wave order parameter dominated by the
\{[5,3],[6,3]\}
components is the presence of gap minima along the diagonals, reminiscent of a
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
structure. This can be understood by the fact that the two-component aspect of the order parameter in this case is not carried by the basis functions
\sin(k_{x})\sin(k_{z})
and
\sin(k_{y})\sin(k_{z})
, but is carried by the orbital content of the gap structure. The orbital content is encoded by the Gell-Mann matrices. These matrices encode specific types of pairing:
\lambda_{5}
corresponds to antisymmetric pairing between
d_{yz}
and
d_{xy}
orbitals, while
\lambda_{6}
corresponds to antisymmetric pairing between
d_{xz}
and
d_{xy}
orbitals. In this case, the orbital content on the Fermi surfaces is going to limit the magnitude of the gap along specific directions. In Fig.
1
we compare the gap structure expected for a
d_{xz}+id_{yz}
order parameter (accompanied by the
[0,0]
matrix structure), displaying gap maxima along the diagonals, with the gap structure associated with the
[5,3]+i[6,3]
structure, with gap minima along the diagonals.
