From the Sigma-type to the Grothendieck construction
Iosif Petrakis

Abstract
We translate properties of the Sigma-type in Martin-Löf Type Theory ( MLTT ) (\mathrm{MLTT}) to properties of the Grothendieck construction in category theory. Namely, equivalences in MLTT \mathrm{MLTT} that involve the Sigma-type motivate isomorphisms between corresponding categories that involve the Grothendieck construction. The type-theoretic axiom of choice and the “associativity” of the Sigma-type are the main examples of this phenomenon that are treated here. Keywords : Martin-Löf type theory, type-theoretic axiom of choice, category theory, Grothendieck construction



1
Introduction

In category theory the Grothendieck construction is a general method of generating fibrations that
generalises the semidirect product of monoids (see
[
2
]
, section 12.2). There are various instances
in which the Grothendieck construction “appears” in connection to Martin-Löf Type Theory
(\mathrm{MLTT})
.
For example, Hofmann in
[
6
]
, p. 140, defined the comprehension of a family over a groupoid as
a special case
of the Grothendieck construction. In book-HoTT
[
16
]
, section 6.12, it is mentioned that the
Sigma-type
\sum_{x:W}P(x)
, where

W
is a higher inductive type and
P
is a type-family over
W
, is, from a category-theoretic point
of view, the
“Grothendieck construction” of
P
. In
[
12
]
Palmgren used the Grothendieck construction and
the fact that
this construction can appropriately be iterated, in order to naturally model dependent type theory in
the form
of contextual category. Quite earlier, in
[
11
]
Obtułowicz had also applied an iterated version
of the
Grothendieck construction to a hierarchy of indexed categories.
The Grothendieck construction was also used by Hyland and Pitts in
[
7
]
, pp. 182-184, in their
development of categorical models of the calculus of constructions. For the relation of the Grothendieck
construction to the Chu construction see
[
15
]
.

Here we are concerned with two variants of the Grothendieck construction: the
Grothendieck construction on
\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
-valued presheaves and the Grothendieck construction on
\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
-valued
presheaves.
The first, also known as the category of elements, was “first done by Yoneda
and developed by Mac Lane well before Grothendieck” (see
[
9
]
, p. 44), and it is used e.g.,
in the proof of the fact that every
\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
-valued presheaf is a colimit of representable presheaves
(see
[
9
]
, pp. 42-43). The second, is the original construction
of Grothendieck (see
[
3
]
).

Our main observation is that certain equivalences in
\mathrm{MLTT}
that involve the Sigma-type
motivate isomorphisms between corresponding categories that involve the Grothendieck construction.
The type-theoretic axiom of choice (Theorem
3.6
) and
the “associativity” of the Sigma-type (Theorem
4.1
) are the main examples of
this phenomenon that are treated here.
The proof of Theorem
3.6
can also be seen as the translation the proof of the type-theoretic
axiom of choice.
Actually, our proof is closer to the translation of the type-theoretic proof in Bishop Set Theory
(\mathrm{BST})

(see
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
), and it requires the use both of
\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
-valued presheaves
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
-valued presheaves.

The aforementioned phenomenon is rooted to the fact that quite often the Grothendieck construction
has a behavior in category theory analogous to that of the Sigma-type in
\mathrm{MLTT}
, or to that of the
disjoint union
of a set-indexed family of (Bishop) sets in (
\mathrm{BST}
) set theory (see
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
for
the Bishop case).
This is clear for the definition of the
objects of the constructed category.
The “interpretation” of the Grothendieck construction as the categorical version of the disjoint union
of sets is
justified, for example, by the role of the Grothendieck construction in the proof of equivalence between
the two ways of describing
families of categories; the
pointwise indexing
and the
display indexing
given by a
fibration.
It is exactly the role played by the disjoint union of a family of sets in the proof of equivalence
between the
two corresponding ways of describing families of sets. Both, the disjoint union and the Grothendieck
construction,
are used in the proof of getting a display indexing from a pointwise indexing (see
[
8
]
,
pp. 20-21 and p. 111). In
[
8
]
, p. 29, Jacobs mentions that this switching between the two
representations
of families of categories through the Grothendieck construction “is an extension of what we have
for sets”. What
our analysis here shows is that this is not all an accident.

\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}

\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}

\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
),
R\colon(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D})^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}

is a
\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
-valued presheaf on the (small) category
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}
, and
\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
is the category of

,

.
We do not include the proofs of facts that are straightforward to show.

Definition 2.1

(The Yoneda-Mac Lane construction)
.

If
P=(P_{0},P_{1})\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}\in\mathrm{PSh}(\mathcal{C})
, the category of elements

\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)
of
P
has objects pairs
(a,u)
, where
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
u\in P_{0}(a)
.
We denote the
disjoint union of the sets
P_{0}(a)
, where
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
, by

\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}P_{0}(a).

A morphism
f^{*}\colon(a,u)\to(b,v)
is a morphism
f\colon a\to b
such that
[P_{1}(f)](v)=u
.
If
g^{*}\colon(b,v)\to(c,w)
, then
g^{*}\circ f^{*}=g\circ f
, and
1_{(a,u)}=1_{a}
.

More standard notations for the category of elements are

\int(\mathcal{C},P),\ \ \ \int_{\mathcal{C}}P,

but here we follow Palmgren’s notation used in
[
12
]
for it. Since
\mathcal{C}
is small, the
category
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)

is also small. An implementation of the category of elements in
\mathrm{MLTT}

would treat
\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
as a type in some universe of types

\mathcal{U}
,
P_{0}
as a type family
P_{0}\colon\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathcal{U}
over
\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
, and the objects of

\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)
as the type

\sum_{a:\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}P_{0}(a).

The connection of the category of elements with the
\Sigma
-type of
\mathrm{MLTT}
fully justifies
Palmgren’s notation.
It is immediate to see that
\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{P}\colon\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)\to\mathcal{C}
, where

\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{P}=\big((\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{P})_{0},(\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{P})_{1}\big)
with
(\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{P})_{0}(a,u)=a

and
(\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{P})_{1}(f^{*})=f
, is a functor. Actually,
\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{P}
is a discrete fibration.
If
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
, the functor
\mathcal{Y}^{a}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
is defined by
\mathcal{Y}^{a}_{0}(b)=\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{C}}(b,a)

and if
f\colon b\to c
in
\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{Y}^{a}_{1}(f)\colon\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{C}}(c,a)\to\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{C}}(b,a)

is defined by
the rule
h\mapsto h\circ f
, for every
h\in\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{C}}(c,a)
.
It is immediate to see that
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{Y}^{a})
is the slice category
\mathcal{C}/a
, and
it is straightforward to show
that the slice category
\mathrm{PSh}(\mathcal{C})/P
is equivalent to
\mathrm{PSh}(\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P))
.

Proposition 2.2
.

We have that
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)=(\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{0},\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{1})\colon\mathrm{PSh}(\mathcal{C})\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
, where

\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)=(\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{0},\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{1}),

\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{0}(P)=\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P);\ \ \ \ P\in\mathrm{PSh}(\mathcal{C}),

\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{1}(\eta\colon P\Rightarrow Q)\colon\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)\to\Sigma(\mathcal{C},Q)

[\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{1}(\eta)]_{0}\colon\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}P_{0}(a)\to\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}Q_{0}(a)

[\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{1}(\eta)]_{0}(a,u)=(a,\eta_{a}(u));\ \ \ \ (a,u)\in\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}P_{0}(a).

Moreover, if
f^{*}\colon(a,u)\to(b,v)
, then

[\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{1}(\eta)]_{1}(f^{*})=f
.

Proof.

We only show that
[\Sigma(\mathcal{C},-)_{1}(\eta)]_{1}(f^{*})\colon(a,\eta_{a}(u))\to(b,\eta_{b}(v))
, as
by the commutativity
of the corresponding diagram we get
[Q_{1}(f)](\eta_{b}(v))=\eta_{a}\big([P_{1}(f)](v)=\eta_{a}(u)
.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

If
F\in\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
, then
R^{F}=(R^{F}_{0},R^{F}_{1})\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
, where

R^{F}_{0}(a)=R_{0}(a,F_{0}(a));\ \ \ \ a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}},

R^{F}_{1}(f\colon a\to b)\colon R_{0}(b,F_{0}(b))\to R_{0}(a,F_{0}(a))

R^{F}_{1}(f)=R_{1}(f,F_{1}(f)),

as
(f,F_{1}(f))\colon(a,F_{0}(a))\to(b,F_{0}(b))
in
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 2.4
.

If
P\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
is a
\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
-valued presheaf on
\mathcal{C}
, the product set

\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}P_{0}(a)
of
P
has elements families
\Phi=(\Phi_{a})_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}
, where

\Phi_{a}\in P_{0}(a)
, for every
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
, such that

\forall_{a,b\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}\forall_{f\in\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{C}}(a,b)}\big([P_{1}(f)](\Phi_{b})=\Phi_{a}\big).

Proposition 2.5
.

We have that
\Pi R=((\Pi R)_{0},(\Pi R)_{1})\colon\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
, where

\big(\Pi R\big)_{0}(F)=\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R^{F}_{0}(a)=\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,F_{0}(a));\ \ \ \ F\in\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}),

\big(\Pi R\big)_{1}(\eta\colon F\Rightarrow G)\colon\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,G_{0}(a))\to\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,F_{0}(a))

\bigg[\big[\big(\Pi R\big)_{1}(\eta)\big](\Phi)\bigg]_{a}=[R_{1}(1_{a},\eta_{a})](\Phi_{a});\ \ \ \ \Phi\in\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,G_{0}(a)),\ a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}.

Proof.

We show that
\big(\Pi R\big)_{1}(\eta)
is well-defined i.e.,
\big(\Pi R\big)_{1}(\eta)\big](\Phi)\in\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,F_{0}(a))
, if
\Phi\in\prod_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,G_{0}(a))
. If

f\colon a\to b
in
\mathcal{C}
, then by the definition of
R^{F}
in Proposition
2.3
, and by
Definition
2.4
we have that

[R^{G}_{1}(f)](\Phi_{b})=[R_{1}(f,G_{1}(f))](\Phi_{b})=\Phi_{a}.

We need to show that

[R^{F}_{1}(f)]\bigg(\bigg[\big(\Pi R\big)_{1}(\eta)\big](\Phi)\bigg]_{b}\bigg)=\bigg[\big(\Pi R\big)_{1}(\eta)\big](\Phi)\bigg]_{a}\ \ \ \ \ \mbox{i.e.,}

[R_{1}(f,F_{1}(f))]\bigg([R_{1}(1_{b},\eta_{b})](\Phi_{b})\bigg)=[R_{1}(1_{a},\eta_{a})](\Phi_{a}).

Since
\eta
is a natural transformation we have that

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ [R_{1}(f,F_{1}(f))]\bigg([R_{1}(1_{b},\eta_{b})](\Phi_{b})\bigg)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(f,F_{1}(f))]\circ R_{1}(1_{b},\eta_{b})](\Phi_{b})

\displaystyle=\big[R_{1}\big((1_{b},\eta_{b})\circ(f,F_{1}(f))\big)\big](\Phi_{b})

\displaystyle=\big[R_{1}\big((f,\eta_{b}\circ F_{1}(f)\big)\big](\Phi_{b})

\displaystyle=\big[R_{1}\big((f,G_{1}(f)\circ\eta_{a}\big)\big](\Phi_{b})

\displaystyle=\big[R_{1}\big((f\circ 1_{a},G_{1}(f)\circ\eta_{a}\big)\big](\Phi_{b})

\displaystyle=\big[R_{1}\big((f,G_{1}f))\circ(1_{a},\eta_{a})\big)\big](\Phi_{b})

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(1_{a},\eta_{a})]\bigg(\big[R_{1}(f,G_{1}(f))\big](\Phi_{b})\bigg)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(1_{a},\eta_{a})](\Phi_{a}).

The rest of the proof is straightforward.
∎

Next we describe the second projection associated to the Grothendieck construction similarly to
the definition
of the second projection associated to the
{\sum}
-type.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
P\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
.

(i)
P^{\Sigma}=(P^{\Sigma}_{0},P^{\Sigma}_{1})\colon\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)^{\mathrm{op}}\to Set
, where

P^{\Sigma}_{0}(a,u)=P_{0}(a)
, for every

(a,u)\in\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)
, and
P^{\Sigma}_{1}(f^{*}\colon(a,u)\to(b,v))=P_{1}(f)\colon P_{0}(b)\to P(a)
,
for every morphism
f^{*}\colon(a,u)\to(b,v)
.

(ii) The family
\textnormal{{pr}}_{2}^{P}=(\textnormal{{pr}}_{2}^{P}(a,u))_{(a,u)\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P}}
, where
\textnormal{{pr}}_{2}^{P}(a,u)=u
,
for every

(a,u)\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)}
, belongs to the product set

\prod_{(a,u)\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)}}P^{\Sigma}_{0}(a,u)=\prod_{(a,u)\in\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{D}}}P_{0}(a)}P_{0}(a).

Proof.

The proof of (i) is immediate, while for (ii) it suffices to show the condition of
Definition
2.4
.
Let
f^{*}\colon(a,u)\to(b,v)
in
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)
i.e.,
f\colon a\to b
such that

[P_{1}(f)](v)=u
. Thus

\big[P_{1}^{\Sigma}(f^{*})\big]\big(\textnormal{{pr}}_{2}^{P}(b,v)\big)=[P_{1}(f)](v)=u=\textnormal{{pr}}_{2}^{P}(a,u)
.
∎

3
The distributivity of
\Pi
over
\Sigma
and the Grothendieck construction

We shall use calligraphic letters for
\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
-valued presheaves.
For a covariant version of the following construction see
[
2
]
, pp. 337-338.

Definition 3.1

(The Grothendieck construction)
.

If
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1})\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
,
the category
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P})

has objects pairs
(a,x)
, where
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
x\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(a)}
.
We denote
\mathrm{Ob}_{\Sigma(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P})}
, the disjoint union of the sets
\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(a)}
,
where
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
, by

\sum_{a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(a)}.

A morphism from
(a,x)
to

(b,y)
is a pair
(f,\phi)
, where
f\colon a\to b
in
\mathcal{C}
and
\phi\colon x\to[\mathcal{P}_{1}(f)]_{0}(y)
in
\mathcal{P}_{0}(a)
.
If
(g,\theta)\colon(b,y)\to(c,z)
, the composition

(g,\theta)\circ(f,\phi)\colon(a,x)\to(c,z)
is the pair
\big(g\circ f,[\mathcal{P}_{1}(f)]_{1}(\theta)\circ\phi\big)
,
where

[\mathcal{P}_{1}(f)]_{1}(\theta)\circ\phi\colon x\to[\mathcal{P}_{1}(g\circ f)]_{0}(z)=[\mathcal{P}_{1}(f)]_{0}\big([\mathcal{P}_{1}(g)]_{0}(z)\big)
.
Finally,
1_{(a,x)}=(1_{a},1_{x})
.

If we consider a set as a discrete category, then Definition
3.1
is a generalisation of
Definition
2.1
.
Let
\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{D}}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
the constant presheaf
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{C}
i.e.,

\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(a)=\mathcal{D}
,
for every
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(f\colon a\to b)=1_{\mathcal{D}}
, for every

f\in\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{C}}(a,b)
.
It is immediate to see that

\Sigma(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{D}})=\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}.

This is the translation of the type-theoretic equality

\sum_{x:A}B\equiv A\times B.

Clearly,
\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{\mathcal{P}}\colon\Sigma(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P})\to\mathcal{C}
, where

\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{\mathcal{P}}=\big((\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{\mathcal{P}})_{0},(\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{\mathcal{P}})_{1}\big)
with
(\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{\mathcal{P}})_{0}(a,x)=a

and
(\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{\mathcal{P}})_{1}\big((f,\phi)\colon(a,x)\to(b,y)\big)=f
, is a functor. Actually,

\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}^{\mathcal{P}}
is
a split fibration. Next we translate accordingly the type-theoretic equivalence

\sum_{x:A}\sum_{y:B}R(x,y)\simeq\sum_{y:B}\sum_{x:A}R(x,y).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

(i)
R^{a}=(R^{a}_{0},R^{a}_{1})\colon\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
, where
R^{a}_{0}(x)=R_{0}(a,x)
,
for every
x\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{D}}
, and
R^{a}_{1}(\phi\colon x\to y)=R_{1}(1_{a},\phi)\colon R_{0}(a,y)\to R_{0}(a,x)
,
for every
\phi\colon x\to y
in
\mathcal{D}
.

(ii)
{}^{x}R=(^{x}R_{0},^{x}R_{1})\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Set}}
, where
{}^{x}R_{0}(a)=R_{0}(a,x)
, for every

a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}
,
and
{}^{x}R_{1}(f\colon a\to b)=R_{1}(f,1_{x})\colon R_{0}(b,x)\to R_{0}(a,x)
, for every
f\colon a\to b
in
\mathcal{C}
.

(iii)
\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}=(\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{0},\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{1})\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
, where

\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{0}(a)=\Sigma(\mathcal{D},R^{a});\ \ \ \ a\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}},

\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{1}(f)\colon\Sigma(\mathcal{D},R^{b})\to\Sigma(\mathcal{D},R^{a});\ \ \ \ f\in\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{C}}(a,b),

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{1}(f)\big]_{0}\colon\sum_{x\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{D}}}R_{0}(b,x)\to\sum_{x\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{D}}}R_{0}(a,x)

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{1}(f)\big]_{0}(x,u)=\big(x,[R_{1}(f,1_{x})](u)\big),

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{1}(f)\big]_{1}\big(\phi^{*}\colon(x,u)\to(y,v)\big)\colon\big(x,[R_{1}(f,1_{x})](u)\big)\to\big(y,[R_{1}(f,1_{y})](v)\big),

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{1}(f)\big]_{1}\big(\phi^{*}\big)=\phi.

(iv)
\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}=(\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{0},\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{1})\colon\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
, where

\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{0}(x)=\Sigma(\mathcal{C},^{x}R);\ \ \ \ x\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{D}},

\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{1}(\phi)\colon\Sigma(\mathcal{C},^{y}R)\to\Sigma(\mathcal{C},^{x}R);\ \ \ \ \phi\in\mathrm{Mor}^{\mathcal{D}}(x,y),

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{1}(\phi)\big]_{0}\colon\sum_{x\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,y)\to\sum_{x\in\mathrm{Ob}_{\mathcal{C}}}R_{0}(a,x)

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{1}(\phi)\big]_{0}(a,u)=\big(a,[R_{1}(1_{a},\phi)](u)\big),

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{1}(\phi)\big]_{1}\big(f^{*}\colon(a,u)\to(b,v)\big)\colon\big(a,[R_{1}(1_{a},\phi)](u)\big)\to\big(b,[R_{1}(1_{b},\phi)](v)\big),

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R}_{1}(\phi)\big]_{1}\big(f^{*}\big)=f.

(v) The categories
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R})
and
\Sigma(\mathcal{D},\Sigma^{\mathcal{C},R})
are isomorphic.

Proof.

The proofs of (i) and (ii) are immediate. For (iii) we only show that

\big[\Sigma^{\mathcal{D},R}_{1}(f)\big]_{1}
is well-defined.
If
\phi^{*}\colon(x,u)\to(y,v)
in
\Sigma(\mathcal{D},R^{b})
i.e.,
\phi\colon x\to y

such that
[R_{1}(1_{b},\phi)](v)=u
,
we show that
\phi\colon\big(x,[R_{1}(f,1_{x})](u)\big)\to\big(y,[R_{1}(f,1_{y})](v)\big)

in
\Sigma(\mathcal{D},R^{a})
, as

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ [R_{1}(1_{a},\phi)]\big([R_{1}(f,1_{y})](v)\big)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(1_{a},\phi)\circ R_{1}(f,1_{y})](v)

\displaystyle=\big[R_{1}\big((f,1_{y})\circ(1_{a},\phi)\big)\big](v)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(f\circ 1_{a},1_{y}\circ f)](v)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(f,\phi)](v)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(1_{b}\circ f,\phi\circ 1_{x})](v)

\displaystyle=\big[R_{1}\big((1_{b},\phi)\circ(f,1_{x})\big)\big](v)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(f,1_{x})]\big([R_{1}(1_{b},\phi)](v)\big)

\displaystyle=[R_{1}(f,1_{x})](u).

The proof of (iv) is similar to the proof of (iii), and the proof of
(v) is straightforward.
∎

Next we extend Definition
2.4
to
\mathrm{\mathbf{Cat}}
-valued presheaves.

Definition 3.3

5
Concluding remarks

Here we presented two non-trivial examples of translating equivalences in
\mathrm{MLTT}
that involve
the
\Sigma
-type to
isomorphisms of categories that involve the Grothendieck construction. There are also many simple
examples
of this phenomenon, like the isomorphism of the categories
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},P)\times\Sigma(\mathcal{D},Q)

and
\Sigma(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D},P\times Q)

(or the categories
\Sigma(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P})\times\Sigma(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Q})
and
\Sigma(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D},\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Q})
), that
are not developed here. We expect to include other interesting instances
of this phenomenon in future work. E.g., type-theoretic equivalences involving the
\Sigma
-type and
the fiber of a function,
like
\textnormal{{pr}}_{1}
(see e.g., section 4.8 in
[
16
]
), are expected to be translated into the language
of the
Grothendieck construction, since the fiber of a functor can be constructed as a pullback
(see e.g.,
[
8
]
, pp. 26-27).
As the Grothendieck construction can be generalised to higher category theory (see e.g.,
[
10
]
), the
extension of the aforementioned phenomenon to higher category theory is also expected.
