An extension of the Geometric Modulus Principle to holomorphic and harmonic functions
Matt Hohertz

Abstract
Kalantari’s Geometric Modulus Principle describes the local behavior of the modulus of a polynomial. Specifically, if p ⁡ ( z ) = a 0 + ∑ j = k n a j ​ ( z − z 0 ) j , a 0 ​ a k ​ a n ≠ 0 p(z)=a_{0}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{n}a_{j}\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{j},\;a_{0}a_{k}a_{n}\neq 0 , then the complex plane near z = z 0 z=z_{0} comprises 2 ​ k 2k sectors of angle π k \frac{\pi}{k} , alternating between arguments of ascent (angles θ \theta where | p ⁡ ( z 0 + t ​ e i ​ θ ) | > | p ⁡ ( z 0 ) | \left|p(z_{0}+te^{i\theta})\right|>\left|p(z_{0})\right| for small t t ) and arguments of descent (where the opposite strict inequality holds). In this paper, we generalize the Geometric Modulus Principle to holomorphic and harmonic functions. As in Kalantari’s original paper, we use these extensions to give succinct, elegant new proofs of some classical theorems from analysis.

Extension to harmonic functions

In this section we extend the Geometric Modulus Principle to harmonic functions, using the fact that all harmonic functions are locally the real part of some holomorphic function. Define an
argument of ascent
(resp.,
argument of descent
) of a harmonic function
u
at
z_{0}
to be an angle
\theta
such that
{u\left(z_{0}+te^{i\theta}\right)}>{u\left(z_{0}\right)}
[resp.,
{u\left(z_{0}+te^{i\theta}\right)}<{u\left(z_{0}\right)}

C_{a}(u,z_{0})
and
C_{d}(u,z_{0})

In what follows, assume

Let

Note that

\displaystyle\left|e^{f(z)}\right|

Theorem III
(Geometric Modulus Principle for harmonic functions)

Let
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the real part of a holomorphic function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that
k:=\displaystyle\min_{\ell\geq 1}\{\ell:f^{(\ell)}(z_{0})\neq 0\}

C_{a}(u,z_{0})
and

then
\theta
is an argument of ascent if

\frac{-2\beta-\pi}{2k}<\theta<\frac{-2\beta+\pi}{2k}\quad\left(\mbox{mod }\frac{2\pi}{k}\right).

(22)

The interiors of the remaining sectors are arguments of descent.

Proof

By Lemma
3

\displaystyle=\left|g(z_{0})\right|^{2}\cdot f^{(k)}(z_{0}).

3
Corollaries

In his original paper, Kalantari uses the Geometric Modulus Principle to give a two-line proof of the Maximum Modulus Principle for polynomials. Having expanded his theorem to apply to all holomorphic functions, we generalize his proof
2
2

2

[
Kalantari 2011
, Theorem 2]
to a proof of the general Maximum Modulus Principle.

Theorem IV
(Maximum Modulus Principle)

Let
\Omega
be a connected, open subset of
\mathbb{C}
; let
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be holomorphic. If

\left|f(z_{0})\right|\geq\left|f(z)\right|

for all
z
in a neighborhood of some
z_{0}\in\Omega
, then
f
is constant on
\Omega
.

Proof

By Theorem
II
, if
f
is non-constant then it has at least one direction of ascent at each
z_{0}\in\Omega
; in particular,
\left|f\right|
cannot attain a local maximum in
\Omega
.

■

■

By analogous reasoning, we prove the Maximum Principle for harmonic functions.

Theorem V
(Maximum Principle)

If the harmonic function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains a local extremum at
z_{0}\in\Omega
, then
u
is constant.

Proof

If
u
is non-constant, then it is locally the real part of a non-constant holomorphic function. Thus, by Theorem
III
, it has at least one argument of ascent and at least one argument of descent.

■

■

Another straightforward corollary of Theorem
III
is that the zeros of non-constant harmonic functions are never isolated, in contrast to the holomorphic case.

Theorem VI

If the non-constant harmonic function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
has a zero at
z=z_{0}
, then it also has a zero in every punctured disc
\{z:0<\left|z-z_{0}\right|<t\}
.

Proof

By Theorem
III
, the cones of ascent and descent of
u
at
z_{0}
partition the unit circle into at least two sectors. By continuity, these sectors are bounded by the level curve
\{z\in\Omega:u(z)=0\}
.

4
Conclusion

By expanding the Geometric Modulus Principle, we obtain stronger versions of two of the most powerful theorems in analysis: the Maximum Modulus Principle for holomorphic functions and the Maximum Principle for harmonic functions. Given the implications of these theorems, of which Liouville’s Theorem and the Schwarz lemma stand out in particular, we expect that careful application of the Geometric Modulus Principle will yield more results like those of Section
3
, or allow for briefer or more constructive proofs of existing results.
