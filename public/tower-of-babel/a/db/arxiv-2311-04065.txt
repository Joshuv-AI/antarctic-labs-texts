A study of the One-Dimensional Heat-Conduction Equation with Radiation
Mihai Halic

Abstract
. We consider a boundary value problem (BVP) modelling one-dimensional heat-conduction with radiation, which is derived from the Stefan-Boltzmann law. The problem strongly depends on the parameters, making difficult to estimate the solution. In here we apply an analytical approach to determine upper and lower bounds to the exact solution of the BVP, which allows estimating the latter. Finally, we support our theoretical arguments with numerical data, by implementing them into the MAPLE computer program.

Primary 34B15; Secondary 34L30, 34B60

1.
Introduction

Firnett-Troesch
[
4
]
applied the so-called ‘shooting method’ to numerically analyse two second order BVPs, which depend in a sensitive way on the parameters. The author devised
[
5
]
an analytic approach for determining explicit approximate solutions to such BVPs, and compared the outcome with existing numerical results for the ‘first Troesch-equation’
y^{\prime\prime}=L\sinh(Ly)
. The analytical method turned out to be several orders of magnitude preciser than previous numerical methods, especially for large values of
L
.
Here we develop this approach, and consider the ‘second Troesch-equation’ that is,

\hskip 34.5021ptu^{\prime\prime}_{\rm heat}=b^{2}(u^{4}_{\rm heat}-t^{4}),\quad u_{\rm heat}(0)=1,\;u_{\rm heat}(1)=t\in(0,1),\hskip 34.5021pt\text{(HCR)}

which describes the steady-state, one-dimensional heat conduction with radiation. The equation depends on two parameters:
b
is related to the radiative properties of materials, while
t
is roughly the ratio between the initial and final temperatures.

Our goal here is to approximately solve (HCR), understand the dependence of its solution on the parameters, elucidate the law governing the boundary layer, etc. These objectives turn out to be challenging. There seems to be a rather restricted literature dedicated to the analysis of this very equation, in spite of being interesting:

–

On the mathematics side, the ODE is far from being classically integrable. Existence results are proved in
[
8
,
7
,
10
]
. Power series expansions are obtained in
[
11
,
12
]
; their terms are complicated and, for good approximations, the order of the development quickly increases with the parameters.

From a numerical perspective, (HCR) often has the so-called ‘boundary layer property’,
u_{\rm heat}
has huge derivative near
x=0
, which is a major obstacle.
For running the shooting method, one needs a very good estimate of either
u^{\prime}_{\rm heat}(0)\ll 0
or
u^{\prime}_{\rm heat}(1)\approx 0
. Unfortunately, for larger
b
—physically realistic situations involve
b\approx 30\dots 40
— these values are hard to estimate. Indeed, Firnett-Troesch consider only a few values (
\,0.8,0.4,0\,
) of
t
, and deal with values of
b
up to
20
.

[
4
, p.427]
:

[
4
, Figure 6.1, p.426]
:

“If the differential equation is integrated with a guess […] which is even slightly in error, then a strongly diverging solution is obtained.”

Thus one faces a chaotic situation —tiny changes lead to major variations—, which raises the issue how to deal with a non-integrable ODE, without completely relying on computer power.
Our approach is to determine explicit upper and lower bounds —so-called envelopes— and deduce the dependence on the parameters. Quite remarkably, the conclusions follow naturally.

–

On the physics side, the equation is based on the Stefan-Boltzmann law for the heat radiation; it’s a rare physical law involving
4^{th}
-power dependence on a parameter (the temperature). Numerous textbooks
[

,

,
3
,

,

]
, written from both theoretical and engineering standpoints, show the importance of heat conduction and radiation.

Outline

At the core of our approach, there is a careful analysis of the heat conduction equation. The large variations of the output, generated by insignificant changes in the input, can’t be controlled without taming the mechanism which yields solution. The computer work supplements and enhances the presentation, also reinforces the (possibly confusing) theoretical arguments with numerical evidence.

Background matter

The following calculations are tedious, but essential.

2.1.
A transcendental equation I

We consider real numbers
\zeta>1
,
{\Lambda}>0
, and the equation in the unknown
c\geqslant 0
:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{r}\displaystyle\frac{\zeta-c}{\zeta+c}=\frac{1-c}{1+c}\cdot e^{2{\Lambda}c}\;\Leftrightarrow\;(\zeta-1)\cdot\frac{c}{\zeta-c^{2}}=\tanh({\Lambda}c)\;\Leftrightarrow\;\zeta=\underbrace{c\cdot\frac{1-c\cdot\tanh({\Lambda}c)}{c-\tanh({\Lambda}c)}}_{rhs(c)}.\end{array}

(2.1)

First we analyse the poles of the right-hand side. The equation
c=\tanh({\Lambda}c)
has the solution
c_{0}({\Lambda})=0
, for
{\Lambda}\in(0,1]
. For
{\Lambda}>1
, it admits a (unique) solution
c_{0}({\Lambda})\in(0,1)
. In all cases,
c_{0}({\Lambda})
can be expressed as an infinite iteration of the ‘tanh’ function:

c_{0}({\Lambda})=\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot\dots))).

This formula allows computing approximate values for
c_{0}({\Lambda})
:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{|l|l|}\hline\cr{\Lambda}&c_{0}({\Lambda})=\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot\dots)))\\
\hline\cr{\Lambda}=1&c_{0}(1)=0\\
\hline\cr{\Lambda}=1.10&c_{0}(1.10)\approx 0.5\\
\hline\cr{\Lambda}=1.50&c_{0}(1.50)\approx 0.85\\
\hline\cr{\Lambda}=1.67&c_{0}(1.67)\approx 0.9\\
\hline\cr{\Lambda}=2&c_{0}(2)\approx 0.95\\
\hline\cr{\Lambda}\geqslant 5&c_{0}({\Lambda})>0.999\approx 1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(2.2)

A numerical experiment shows that
20
iterations of the function
\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot)
are necessary to obtain a two digit approximation —an upper bound— of
c_{0}({\Lambda})
, for
{\Lambda}\geqslant 1.1
.

Given
\zeta>1
and
{\Lambda}>0
, the equation (
2.1
) admits a unique solution
c=c(\zeta,{\Lambda})
, as follows:

(i)

for
{\Lambda}>1
, one has
c\in(c_{0}({\Lambda}),1)
.

(ii)

for
{\Lambda}\in(0,1]
,
\zeta<\frac{1}{1-{\Lambda}}
, one has
c\in(0,1)
.
(For
{\Lambda}=1
, we let
1/(1-1)=+\infty
.)

Proof.

In the first case, we have
\mathop{\lim}\limits_{c\to c_{0}({\Lambda})^{+}}rhs(c)=+\infty
and
rhs(1)=1<\zeta
. In the second case, the denominator doesn’t vanish, and we have
\mathop{\lim}\limits_{c\to 0^{+}}rhs(c)=\frac{1}{1-{\Lambda}}>\zeta
,
rhs(1)=1<\zeta
. Since
c\mapsto rhs(c)
is decreasing, the uniqueness follows.
∎

2.2.
Bounding the solution
c

We determine explicit upper and lower bounds for the exact solution of (
2.1
), depending on the parameters
\zeta,{\Lambda}
. The previous Lemma shows that we should distinguish between two cases:
{\Lambda}>1
(actually, we take
{\Lambda}\geqslant 1.1
) and
{\Lambda}<1.1
. The approaches to estimating the solutions are different.

2.2.1.
Case
{\Lambda}\geqslant 1.5

Polynomial approximations fail in this situation because the
\tanh
-function approaches exponentially fast the value
1
. Since
c
belongs to
(0,1)
, we write:

\;c=\tanh(Y)=\tanh({\Lambda}X)>c_{0}({\Lambda})=\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot c_{0}({\Lambda})),\;X>c_{0}({\Lambda}).

The addition formula for the
\tanh
-function yields:

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{c}{\tanh(Y-{\Lambda}\tanh(Y))}=\frac{\tanh({\Lambda}X)}{\tanh({\Lambda}(X-\tanh({\Lambda}X)))},\quad X=c_{0}({\Lambda})+\gamma,\;\gamma>0.

(2.3)

To determine explicit approximations, we consider the inequalities ‘
\leqslant
’ and ‘
\geqslant
’.

‘
\leqslant
’

Since
c>c_{0}({\Lambda})
, it’s enough to have
\tanh\big({\Lambda}(X-\tanh({\Lambda}X))\big)\leqslant\zeta^{-1}c_{0}({\Lambda})
, so

X-\tanh({\Lambda}X)\leqslant{\Lambda}^{-1}\cdot\tanh^{-1}(\zeta^{-1}\cdot c_{0}({\Lambda})).

By using (
2.2.1
), the left-hand side is less than
\gamma+c_{0}({\Lambda})-\tanh({\Lambda}c_{0}({\Lambda}))=\gamma
. Overall, we deduce that the following value is convenient:

\displaystyle c_{-}(\zeta,{\Lambda})=\tanh\biggl({\Lambda}\cdot c_{0}({\Lambda})+\tanh^{-1}(\zeta^{-1}\cdot c_{0}({\Lambda}))\biggr).

(2.4)

‘
\geqslant
’

As
c<1
, it’s enough to have:

\begin{array}[]{l}\tanh\big({\Lambda}(X-\tanh({\Lambda}X))\big)\geqslant\zeta^{-1}\geqslant\zeta^{-1}c,\\[8.61108pt]
\gamma+\tanh({\Lambda}c_{0}({\Lambda}))-\tanh({\Lambda}X)=X-\tanh({\Lambda}X)\geqslant{\Lambda}^{-1}\tanh^{-1}(\zeta^{-1}).\end{array}

A concavity argument yields
\tanh({\Lambda}X)-\tanh({\Lambda}c_{0}({\Lambda}))\leqslant\frac{{\Lambda}\gamma}{\cosh^{2}({\Lambda}c_{0}({\Lambda}))}
, which implies that the following value is convenient:

\displaystyle c_{+}(\zeta,{\Lambda})=\tanh\biggl({\Lambda}\cdot c_{0}({\Lambda})+\frac{\tanh^{-1}(\zeta^{-1})}{1-\frac{{\Lambda}}{\cosh^{2}({\Lambda}\cdot c_{0}({\Lambda}))}}\biggr).

(2.5)

Note that, by differentiating
c_{0}({\Lambda})=\tanh({\Lambda}c_{0}({\Lambda}))
for
{\Lambda}
, one finds that the denominator above is positive.

2.2.2.
Case
{\Lambda}\geqslant 5,\;\zeta\leqslant e^{{\Lambda}}

In this situation, the previous formulae can be simplified, by eliminating
c_{0}({\Lambda})\approx 1
: we replace
X=1+\gamma
in (
2.2.1
), with
\gamma\geqslant 0
. The very same approach yields:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{`$\leqslant$' is satisfied by}&\displaystyle c_{-}(\zeta,{\Lambda}):=\tanh\Big({\Lambda}\cdot\tanh({\Lambda})+\tanh^{-1}\big(\zeta^{-1}\cdot\tanh({\Lambda})\big)\Big);\\[8.61108pt]
\text{`$\geqslant$' is satisfied by}&\displaystyle c_{+}(\zeta,{\Lambda}):=\tanh\Biggl(\frac{\tanh^{-1}(\zeta^{-1})+\frac{{\Lambda}(\sinh(2{\Lambda})-2{\Lambda})}{2\cosh^{2}({\Lambda})}}{1-\frac{{\Lambda}}{\cosh^{2}({\Lambda})}}\Biggr).\end{array}

(2.6)

The condition
\zeta\leqslant e^{\Lambda}
is necessary to ensure that
\gamma
is positive.

2.2.3.
Case
{\Lambda}\in(0,1.5)

In this case, the bounds
c_{0}({\Lambda})<c<1
are imprecise, we need better ones. The series development of
\frac{\tanh(x)}{x}
yields the estimate:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\Bigl|\frac{\tanh(x)}{x}-(1-0.2x^{2})\Bigr|\leqslant 0.15x^{2},\quad\forall\,x\in[0,1.5].\end{array}

(2.7)

Thus the solutions of the bi-quadratic equations

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{\zeta-1}{{\Lambda}}=(\zeta-c^{2})\cdot\Bigl(1-0.35\cdot{\Lambda}^{2}c^{2}\Bigr),\quad\frac{\zeta-1}{{\Lambda}}=(\zeta-c^{2})\cdot\Bigl(1-0.05\cdot{\Lambda}^{2}c^{2}\Bigr),\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle c_{\rho}(\zeta,{\Lambda})={\Biggl[\frac{2\frac{1-\zeta({\Lambda}-1)}{{\Lambda}}}{(1+\rho\cdot\zeta{\Lambda}^{2})+\sqrt{(1-\rho\cdot\zeta{\Lambda}^{2})^{2}+4\rho{\Lambda}(\zeta-1)}}\Biggr]}^{1/2},\;\rho=0.35\;and\;0.05,\end{array}

(2.8)

are bounds
c_{0.35}(\zeta,{\Lambda})<c<c_{0.05}(\zeta,{\Lambda})
to the exact solution of (
2.1
). We use these values in (
2.2.1
); for shorthand, we drop the variables
(\zeta,{\Lambda})
.

‘
\leqslant
’

It’s enough to have
\tanh\big({\Lambda}(X-\tanh({\Lambda}X))\big)\approx\zeta^{-1}c_{0.35}
, so we set:

c_{-}(\zeta,{\Lambda}):=\tanh\Big({\Lambda}\cdot c_{0.35}+\tanh^{-1}\big(\zeta^{-1}\cdot c_{0.35}\big)\Big).

‘
\geqslant
’

It’s enough to have
\tanh\big({\Lambda}(X-\tanh({\Lambda}X))\big)\approx\zeta^{-1}c_{0.05}
, so we set:

c_{+}(\zeta,{\Lambda}):=\tanh\Biggl(\frac{\tanh^{-1}(\zeta^{-1}c_{0.05})+\frac{{\Lambda}\big(\sinh(2{\Lambda}c_{0.05})-2{\Lambda}c_{0.05}\big)}{2\cosh^{2}({\Lambda}c_{0.05})}}{1-\frac{{\Lambda}c_{0.05}}{\cosh^{2}({\Lambda}c_{0.05})}}\Biggr).

(i) One should keep in mind that
c_{+}(\zeta,{\Lambda})\leqslant c\leqslant c_{-}(\zeta,{\Lambda})
. The notation is chosen in such a way that the
-
(resp.
+
) indices determines lower (resp. upper) bounds for the exact solution of the heat equation.

(ii) The equations (
2.2.1
), (
‘

⩾

’
‣
2.2.1
) involve
c_{0}({\Lambda})
, which is implicitly defined. For numerical computations, we replace it by
30
iterations of
\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot)
that is:

c_{0}({\Lambda})\approx\underbrace{\tanh({\Lambda}\cdot\tanh(\dots(\tanh({\Lambda})\dots)))}_{30\;\text{times}},\quad{\Lambda}\in[1.5,5].

For large values of
{\Lambda}
, one may use the estimates (
2.2.2
).

2.3.
A transcendental equation II

For real numbers
z_{1}>z_{0}>0
,
L>0
,
\beta\geqslant\alpha>0
, we consider the equation in
C\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{z_{1}-\alpha C}{z_{1}+\beta C}=\frac{z_{0}-\alpha C}{z_{0}+\beta C}\cdot e^{2LC}.

(2.9)

(For our purposes, we’ll need
\beta=z_{0}=1
, only.) The substitutions

\zeta_{i}:=z_{i}+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}C\;(i=0,1),\quad c:=\frac{\beta+\alpha}{2\zeta_{0}}C,\quad{\Lambda}:=\frac{2\zeta_{0}L}{\beta+\alpha},\quad\zeta:=\frac{\zeta_{1}}{\zeta_{0}},

reduce this equation to (
2.1
), but extra complications occur. The unknown
C
is given by the equation

\displaystyle C=\frac{2z_{0}c}{(\beta+\alpha)-(\beta-\alpha)c},

(2.10)

so
\zeta,c
are related. (Before,
c
and
\zeta
were independent.) Fortunately, we are interested in solving the inequalities ‘
\leqslant
’ and ‘
\geqslant
’ in (
2.3
), which lead to

\zeta=\frac{z_{1}+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}C}{z_{0}+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}C}=c\cdot\frac{1-c\cdot\tanh({\Lambda}c)}{c-\tanh({\Lambda}c)}.

with inequalities in the same direction, respectively.

–

The left-hand side is decreasing in the variable
C
(see (
2.3
)), and we have

\displaystyle\frac{z_{0}c_{0}({\Lambda})}{\beta}\leqslant\frac{2z_{0}c_{0}({\Lambda})}{(\beta+\alpha)-(\beta-\alpha)c_{0}({\Lambda})}<C<\frac{z_{0}}{\alpha}.

(2.11)

–

The right-hand side is increasing in
{\Lambda}
, and we have
\frac{z_{0}}{\beta}L\leqslant{\Lambda}\leqslant\frac{z_{0}}{\alpha}L
.
Thus we obtain approximate solutions of the inequalities:

‘
\leqslant
’

It requires solving

\zeta_{-}:=\frac{z_{1}+\frac{(\beta-\alpha)z_{0}}{2\beta}c_{0}(\frac{z_{0}}{\beta}L)}{z_{0}+\frac{(\beta-\alpha)z_{0}}{2\beta}c_{0}(\frac{z_{0}}{\beta}L)}\leqslant c\cdot\frac{1-c\cdot\tanh(\frac{z_{0}}{\beta}Lc)}{c-\tanh(\frac{z_{0}}{\beta}Lc)},

which is satisfied by
c=c_{-}(\zeta_{-},\frac{z_{0}}{\beta}L)
.

‘
\geqslant
’

It requires solving

\zeta_{+}:=\frac{z_{1}+\frac{(\beta-\alpha)z_{0}}{2\alpha}}{z_{0}+\frac{(\beta-\alpha)z_{0}}{2\alpha}}\geqslant c\cdot\frac{1-c\cdot\tanh(\frac{z_{0}}{\alpha}Lc)}{c-\tanh(\frac{z_{0}}{\alpha}Lc)},

which is satisfied by
c=c_{+}(\zeta_{+},\frac{z_{0}}{\alpha}L)
.

The corresponding values
C_{\pm}
are obtained by inserting
c_{\pm}
into (
2.3
).

2.4.
Solution to an ODE

Some calculations yield the following:

Given
0<\varepsilon<1
and
0<C<\varepsilon^{-1}
. The solution to the ODE

\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{2{\tilde{B}}}{1+\varepsilon}\bigl(y^{2.5}+(1-\varepsilon)Cy-\varepsilon C^{2}y^{-0.5}\bigr),\quad y(1)=T,

is the function

\displaystyle y(x)=y_{{\tilde{B}},C,\varepsilon}(x):=\biggl[C\cdot\frac{T^{1.5}\cdot(1+\varepsilon\cdot e^{3\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}C(1-x)})+\varepsilon C(e^{3\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}C(1-x)}-1)}{T^{1.5}(e^{3\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}C(1-x)}-1)+C(e^{3\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}C(1-x)}+\varepsilon)}\biggr]^{2/3}.

(2.12)

This function has the boundary value
y(0)=1
precisely when
{\tilde{B}},C,\varepsilon
satisfy the equation (
2.3
), with
z_{0}=1,z_{1}=T,\alpha=\varepsilon,\beta=1
.

2.5.
Criteria for upper/lower envelopes

We will determine global, as well as partial envelopes for
u_{\rm heat},y_{\rm heat}
. For deciding that a function is indeed a global envelope, we’ll use the following criterion:

Let
f,g
be continuous, positive on
[m,M]
, such that one of them is strictly increasing. We consider the solutions to the following boundary-value problems:

\begin{array}[]{llr}&y_{0}^{\prime\prime}=f(y_{0}),&y_{0}(0),y_{0}(1)\;given\;in\;[m,M],\\
&y^{\prime\prime}=g(y),&y(0),y(1)\;given\;in\;[m,M].\\
\end{array}

If one has the following three inequalities

g-f,\;y_{0}(0)-y(0),\;y_{0}(1)-y(1)\geqslant 0,\quad\text{(resp. $\leqslant 0$)}

then we have
y_{0}-y\geqslant 0
(resp.
\leqslant 0
).

In this article, we’ll consider
f(y)=B^{2}(5y^{4}-5)
. The proposition can be reformulated in terms of the so-called ‘residue functional’ corresponding to it, which associates the function

{\rm resd}(y):=y^{\prime\prime}-B^{2}(5y^{4}-5),

to any (differentiable) function
y
. The residue measures the deviation of
y
from being a solution to the given ODE. Our criterion reads as follows:

–

If
y(0)\geqslant 1,y(1)\geqslant T
, and
{\rm resd}(y)\leqslant 0
, then
y
is greater than
y_{\rm heat}
.

–

If
y(0)\leqslant 1,y(1)\leqslant T
, and
{\rm resd}(y)\geqslant 0
, then
y
is smaller than
y_{\rm heat}
.

This is a criterion for global envelopes. However, we’ll be also interested in partial envelopes, along the interesting boundary layer.

Let
F,G
be positive, differentiable on
[1,T]
, and consider the IVPs

\begin{array}[]{llll}y_{0}^{\prime}=\sqrt{2}\cdot F(y_{0}),&\;y^{\prime}=\sqrt{2}\cdot G(y),&&y_{0}(1)=y_{0}(1)=T\;\text{given}.\end{array}

If the following inequality is satisfied (where
r\in[1,T]
):

G-F\geqslant 0,\;\text{for}\;y\in[r,T],\quad\text{(resp. $\leqslant 0$)},

then
y_{0}-y\geqslant 0
(resp.
\leqslant 0
), whenever
y\in[r,T]
.

The function of interest will be
F(y)=B^{2}\cdot\sqrt{y^{5}-5y+4+\delta}
, with
\delta\geqslant 0
. Let us explain how this will be used to determine partial envelopes in Propositions
5.1.2
and
5.2.1
.
The IVPs are obtained by respectively integrating the BVPs, so

F(y)=\sqrt{\delta+\int_{0}^{y}f(\xi){\rm d}\xi},\;\text{ and similarly for}\;G.

Suppose we are in the following situation:
G(r)\geqslant F(r)
and there are solutions to the IVPs above, such that
{\rm resd}(y)=g(y)-f(y)\geqslant 0,\;y\in[r,T]
; this condition holds, in particular, if
{\rm resd}(y=r)\geqslant 0
and
{\rm resd}(y)
in increasing on
[r,T]
). Then Proposition
2.5
applies.

3.
The heat conduction equation: I

3.1.
Estimates

We are interested in bounding the solution of the BVP:

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}_{\rm heat}=b^{2}(u^{4}_{\rm heat}-t^{4}),\quad u_{\rm heat}(0)=1,\;u_{\rm heat}(1)=t\in(0,1).

(3.1)

We perform several changes of variables, to bring the problem into a more suitable form. The substitution
x\mapsto 1-x
yields

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}=b^{2}(u^{4}-t^{4}),\quad u(0)=t\in(0,1),\;u(1)=1.

(3.2)

By integrating, we obtain

\frac{(u^{\prime})^{2}}{2t^{2}}=\frac{b^{2}t^{3}}{5}\Bigl(\frac{u^{5}}{t^{5}}-5\frac{u}{t}+A\Bigr).

The derivative at the left-end of the interval
[0,1]
is expected to be close to zero. We set
A=4+\delta
and
y:=u/t
, so the equation becomes:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{y^{\prime}_{\rm heat}}{\sqrt{2}}=B\cdot\sqrt{y^{5}_{\rm heat}-5y_{\rm heat}+4+\delta},&\displaystyle y_{\rm heat}(0)=1,y_{\rm heat}(1)=t^{-1},\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}_{\rm heat}=5B^{2}(y^{4}_{\rm heat}-1),&\displaystyle B:=\frac{bt^{3/2}}{\sqrt{5}}.\end{array}

(3.3)

Clearly, the equation can’t be integrated exactly, so we need to approximate it. At the left-end of
[0,1]
, we have
y_{\rm heat}(0)=1
. However, at the right-end, the function
y_{\rm heat}
take large values, for
t
close to zero, so we need to take into account the large-
y
behaviour. Therefore, we consider the boundary value problem

{\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}=B\cdot G(y),\quad y(0)=1,y(1)=\frac{1}{t},}

with the right-hand side satisfying the following requirements:

–

The difference
\sqrt{y^{5}-5y+4+\delta}-G(y)
is small, for all
y\geqslant 1
;

–

The equation
y^{\prime}=B\cdot G(y)
is explicitly integrable.

For large
y
, we have the expansion

\sqrt{y^{5}-5y+4+\delta}=y^{\frac{5}{2}}-\frac{5}{2}y^{-\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{4+\delta}{2}y^{-\frac{5}{2}}+\dots=y^{\frac{5}{2}}\cdot\biggl(1-\frac{5}{2}y^{-4}+\frac{4+\delta}{2}y^{-5}+\cdots\biggr),

which hints to considering
G(y)=y^{5/2}\cdot c(y)
, such that the approximation is still good for
y\approx 1
. Thus the function
c(y)
must satisfy
c(1)\approx 0
and
\mathop{\lim}\limits_{y\to\infty}c(y)=1
. The asymptotic expansion hints to considering

G(y)=y^{2.5}\cdot(1-\text{const.}\cdot y^{-2.5-r})=y^{2.5}-\text{const.}\cdot y^{-r},

with
\text{const.}\approx 1
.
It remains to determine a value of
r
which is appropriate for both requirements. The numerator of the difference

\sqrt{y^{5}-5y+4}-(y^{5/2}-y^{-r})=\frac{-5y+4+2y^{2.5-r}-y^{-2r}}{\sqrt{y^{5}-5y+4}+(y^{5/2}-y^{-r})},

vanishes of second order at
y=1
for
r_{0}=\sqrt{10}-2.5\approx 0.6623
; for approximation purposes, a convenient value for
r
should be chosen nearby. However, the resulting ODE must be explicitly integrable, too, and this determines
r=0.5
.

3.2.
A boundary value problem I

The value
r=0.5
leads to the following BVP (where we set
T:=t^{-1}
):

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l|l}\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}=B\cdot(y^{2.5}-C^{2}y^{-0.5}),&y^{\prime\prime}=B^{2}(5y^{4}-4C^{2}y-C^{4}y^{-2}),\\
y(0)=1,y(1)=T,&y(0)=1,y(1)=T.\end{array}

(3.4)

In the first formulation, the constant
C>0
is determined by the boundary conditions. For approximating the exact solution, we change our point of view. We drop the boundary value at
x=0
, and consider the initial value problem (IVP):

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}={\tilde{B}}\cdot(y^{2.5}-C^{2}\cdot y^{-0.5}),\quad y\geqslant 1,\;C\in(0,1],\\[4.30554pt]
y(1)=T,\end{array}

(3.5)

whose general solution is:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{T^{1.5}-C}{T^{1.5}+C}=\frac{y^{1.5}-C}{y^{1.5}+C}\cdot e^{3\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}C\cdot(1-x)},\quad\frac{T^{1.5}-C}{T^{1.5}+C}=\frac{1-C}{1+C}\cdot e^{3\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}C},\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle y={\Biggl[\frac{C}{\tanh\Big(\tanh^{-1}(Ct^{1.5})+1.5\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}C(1-x)\Big)}\Biggr]}^{2/3}\kern-4.30554pt.\end{array}

(3.6)

(The parameter
{\tilde{B}}
should be thought off as an ‘improved value’ for
B
, which increases the accuracy of the estimates.) The equation defining
C
was analysed in Section
2
, with the parameter
{\Lambda}=1.5\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}}
; by our discussion, we have
C\leqslant 1
. To obtain an upper bound for (

), the second derivative should satisfy Proposition
2.5
:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}y^{\prime\prime}={\tilde{B}}^{2}(5y^{4}-4C^{2}y-C^{4}y^{-2})\leqslant B^{2}(5y^{4}-5),\quad\forall\,y\in[1,T],\quad y(0)\geqslant 1.\end{array}

(3.7)

The left-hand side is decreasing in
C
, so it’s enough to ensure that the inequality is satisfied for
C=1
. Let

\displaystyle{\tilde{B}_{+}}:=(\tilde{q}_{+})^{-1}\cdot B,\;\text{with}\;\tilde{q}_{+}:=\Bigl[\frac{(1-t^{3})(5+t^{3})}{5(1-t^{4})}\Bigr]^{1/2}\in[\sqrt{0.9},1].

(3.8)

The function

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{+}(x):={\Big[\tanh\Bigl(\tanh^{-1}(t^{1.5})+1.5\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}_{+}}(1-x)\Bigr)\Big]}^{-\frac{2}{3}},

satisfies

\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{y}_{+}^{\prime\prime}={({\tilde{B}_{+}})}^{2}(5\tilde{y}_{+}^{4}-4\tilde{y}_{+}-\tilde{y}_{+}^{-2}),\\[8.61108pt]
\tilde{y}_{+}(1)=T=t^{-1},\;\tilde{y}_{+}(0)=\biggl(1+\frac{2}{\exp\bigl(2\tanh^{-1}(t^{1.5})+3\sqrt{2}{\tilde{B}_{+}}\bigr)-1}\biggr)^{\frac{2}{3}},\end{array}

and it is greater than the exact solution
y_{\rm heat}
of the BVP (
3.1
).

The function
\tilde{y}_{+}
satisfies the conditions of Proposition

.

\Box

We briefly pause the analysis to analyse the behaviour of the function

3.3.
Estimating the derivative

The major issue for the shooting method
[
4
]
is the difficulty to estimate the initial derivative
y^{\prime}_{\rm heat}(0)=\sqrt{\delta}
. A crucial by-product of Lemma
3.2
is an upper bound for
\delta
and, a posteriori, for the derivative at
x=1
.

The following estimates hold for the derivative of
y_{\rm heat}
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\delta\leqslant\Delta:={\Bigg[\frac{\tilde{y}_{+}(0.25)-1}{0.25\sqrt{2}B}\Bigg]}^{2}\leqslant\frac{15}{B^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(e^{3B}-1)^{2}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{e^{3B}-1}\Big)^{2};\\
\displaystyle 0\leqslant y^{\prime}_{\rm heat}(1)-\sqrt{2}B\sqrt{T^{5}-5T+4}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}B\delta}{2\sqrt{T^{5}-5T+4}}\leqslant\frac{11\Big(1+\frac{1}{e^{3B}-1}\Big)^{2}}{B(e^{3B}-1)^{2}\cdot\sqrt{T^{5}-5T+4}}.\end{array}

(3.9)

The moral is that, already for
B\geqslant 2
, the derivative
y_{\rm heat}^{\prime}(1)
differs from the (universal) lower bound
\sqrt{2}B\sqrt{T^{5}-5T+4}
by a tiny amount, of size
o(B^{-1}e^{-6B}T^{-2.5})
. Indeed, for
B\geqslant\frac{1}{3}
, one has
\delta\leqslant 100B^{-2}e^{-6B}.

Note that
y_{\rm heat}
is convex —that is,
y_{\rm heat}^{\prime\prime}>0
—, so its derivative at
x=0
is less than the slope of the secant line between
x=0
and
x=h=0.25
. Since
y_{\rm heat}
is less than
\tilde{y}_{+}
, we deduce the following estimate:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\sqrt{2}B\sqrt{\delta}=y_{\rm heat}^{\prime}(0)&\leqslant\frac{\tilde{y}_{+}(h)-1}{h}\leqslant\frac{\tilde{y}_{+}(h)^{3}-1}{3h}=\frac{2\big(\tilde{y}_{+}(h)^{3/2}-1\big)+\big(\tilde{y}_{+}(h)^{3/2}-1\big)^{2}}{3h}.\end{array}

By inserting
x=h
into the defining formula, we obtain
\tilde{y}_{+}(h)^{3/2}-1\leqslant\frac{2}{e^{3.1B}-1}
, thus

\sqrt{\delta}\leqslant\frac{4}{h\cdot 3\sqrt{2}\cdot B}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{3.1B}-1}\Big(1+\frac{1}{e^{3B}-1}\Big).

It remains to use this estimate, to bound the derivative of
y_{\rm heat}
at
x=1
.

\Box

3.4.
Application to the ‘shooting’ method

The content of this section should probably belong to Section
7
, which is devoted to numerical simulations. However, we believe that this short digression is useful to present some applications following from theoretical considerations.

The shooting method is doomed to fail without an accurate knowledge of the initial derivative of
y_{\rm heat}
. According to Proposition
3.3
, we have

0\leqslant\delta\leqslant\Delta={\Bigg[\frac{\tilde{y}_{+}(0.25)-1}{0.25\sqrt{2}B}\Bigg]}^{2}.

This bare inequality already yields three possible ways of approximating
y_{\rm heat}
(the abbreviation ‘RK’ stands for Runge-Kutta):

(i)

Let
\delta=0
, and consider the IVP
y^{\prime}/\sqrt{2}=B\sqrt{y^{5}-5y+4},\;y(1)=T
, whose solution is denoted by
y_{RK+}
. Its second derivative satisfies the same equation
y^{\prime\prime}=5B^{2}(y^{4}-1)
as
y_{\rm heat}
, and
y_{RK+}^{\prime}(1)<y_{\rm heat}^{\prime}(1)
, hence
y_{RK+}>y_{\rm heat}
.

(ii)

Let
\delta=\Delta
, and consider
y^{\prime}/\sqrt{2}=B\sqrt{y^{5}-5y+4+\Delta},\;y(1)=T
, whose solution is denoted by
y_{RK-}
. Its second derivative satisfies
y^{\prime\prime}=5B^{2}(y^{4}-1)
, same as
y_{\rm heat}
, and
y_{RK-}^{\prime}(1)>y_{\rm heat}^{\prime}(1)
, hence
y_{RK-}<y_{\rm heat}
.

(iii)

Let
\delta=d:={\bigg[\frac{\tilde{y}_{+}(0.25)-\tilde{y}_{+}(0)}{0.25\sqrt{2}B}\bigg]}^{2}
, which is between
0
and
\Delta
, and consider the corresponding IVP. Its solution
y_{RK}
will approximate
y_{\rm heat}
the best.

One is led to asking what is the precision of these approximations.

The error of the approximation
y_{\rm heat}(x)\approx y_{RK}(x)
is at most
err_{y}(x)=y_{RK+}(x)-y_{RK-}(x)
. The size of the maximal error equals
O(e^{-3B})
.

The error for
u_{\rm heat}(1-x)\approx t\cdot y_{RK}(x)
has the order
O(t\cdot e^{-3B})
.

The error function is decreasing, has negative derivative, so the maximum is attained at
x=0
, and is bounded above by
const\cdot\sqrt{\Delta}
.

The estimates (

The heat conduction equation: II

Now we refine the previous methods.

4.1.
A boundary value problem II

The change of variables
z=y^{1.5}
in (
3.2
) yields the ODE
2z^{\prime}=3\sqrt{2}B(z^{2}-C^{2})
, which requires integrating

\frac{2{\rm d}z}{z^{2}-C^{2}}=\frac{{\rm d}z}{C}\Big[\frac{1}{z-C}-\frac{1}{z+C}\Big].

Here we consider a one-parameter deformation of this
1
-form, namely:

\frac{{\rm d}z}{C}\Big[\frac{1}{z-C\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{z+C}\Big]=\frac{(1+\varepsilon){\rm d}z}{z^{2}+(1-\varepsilon)Cz-\varepsilon C^{2}},

where
\varepsilon<1
is introduced to shift the pole of the initial
1
-form away from
z=C\approx 1
, since poles yield major computational errors. The function
z
solves

\frac{{\rm d}z}{C}\Big[\frac{1}{z-C\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{z+C}\Big]=3\sqrt{2}B{\rm d}x,\quad z(0)=z_{0}\approx 1,\;z(1)=T^{1.5},

so it satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\frac{z-\varepsilon C}{z+C}=\frac{T^{1.5}-\varepsilon C}{T^{1.5}+C}\cdot e^{-3\sqrt{2}BC(1-x)},

(4.1)

with
C
determined by the equality:

\displaystyle\frac{T^{1.5}-\varepsilon C}{T^{1.5}+C}=\frac{z_{0}-\varepsilon C}{z_{0}+C}\cdot e^{2LC},\quad L:=1.5\sqrt{2}B.

(4.2)

We recover the situation described in Remark
2.2.3
.

For the initial function
y=z^{2/3}
, we are considering the solution to the new BVP,

\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{2{\tilde{B}}}{1+\varepsilon}\bigl(y^{2.5}+(1-\varepsilon)Cy-\varepsilon C^{2}y^{-0.5}\bigr),\quad y(0)=1,\;y(1)=T,

(4.3)

whose second derivative satisfies:

y^{\prime\prime}=\frac{4{\tilde{B}}^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}\Bigl(5y^{4}+7(1-\varepsilon)Cy^{2.5}-2(4\varepsilon-1-\varepsilon^{2})C^{2}y-\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)C^{3}y^{-0.5}-\varepsilon^{2}C^{4}y^{-2}\Bigr).

(4.4)

The discussion in Section
3.2
corresponds to
\varepsilon=C=1
. We introduce the notation:

B=q\cdot{\tilde{B}}.

The residue function of
y
satisfies the formula:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{{\rm resd}(y)}{B^{2}}&=R(y,q,C,\varepsilon)=\bigl(1-q^{2}\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}\bigr)\cdot 5y^{4}+7(1-\varepsilon)Cy^{2.5}-2(4\varepsilon-1-\varepsilon^{2})C^{2}y\\[8.61108pt]
&-\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)C^{3}y^{-0.5}-\varepsilon^{2}C^{4}y^{-2}+5q^{2}\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4},\end{array}

involving the parameters
q,\varepsilon,C
, which must be finely tuned so that Proposition
2.5
will be eventually satisfied: the solution to the BVP (
4.1
) is a lower (resp. upper) bound for the solution of (
3.1
) if
R(y,q,C,\varepsilon)
is positive (resp. negative) for
y\in[0,1]
.

(i)

The value of
q
is determined by the derivative condition

\frac{y^{\prime}(1)}{\sqrt{2}}\approx B\sqrt{T^{5}-4T+(4+\delta)},

and by ensuring the correct inequality; the bounds (
3.3
) for
\delta
are essential.

–

for
\leqslant
, we impose
y^{\prime}(1)/\sqrt{2}\leqslant q{\tilde{B}}\cdot\sqrt{T^{5}-4T+4}
.

–

for
\geqslant
, we impose
y^{\prime}(y)/\sqrt{2}\geqslant q{\tilde{B}}\cdot\sqrt{y^{5}-4y+(4+\Delta)}
, for
y=T,\sqrt{T}
;

(ii)

The deformation parameter
\varepsilon
will be typically close to
1
, and it’s determined by the curvature/residue condition
R(y,\dots)=rB^{2},
where

(iii)

Finally,

is determined by the boundary condition

(cf. Section
2

4.2.
Analysis of the residue

The sign of the residue function is a decisive matter. In explicit situations, the simplest way to settle the issue is by plotting the function. Here we analyse the sign of this expression (dependence on parameters) by relying on analytical tools. The polynomial in four variables above has the following properties:

–

R(1,1,1,1)=0,\;\frac{{\rm d}R}{{\rm d}C}(1,1,1,1)<0,\;\frac{{\rm d}R}{{\rm d}\varepsilon}(1,1,1,1)<0;

–

R(1,1,C,\varepsilon)=C^{2}(2+C-C^{2})\varepsilon^{2}-C(7+8C+C^{2})\varepsilon+5+7C+2C^{2};

–

The quadratic equation
R(1,1,1,\varepsilon)=0
admits two roots:
1
and
7
. For small variations of the parameters, these roots will persist, and we denote by
\varepsilon(y,q,C,r)
the root of
R(y,q,C,\varepsilon)=r
which is close to
1
. For shorthand, we abusively denote it by
\varepsilon(y)
, since we are mainly interested in the dependence in
y
.

We denote
s:=y^{-1}\in[0,1]
, and let:

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{-}(s):=1-\frac{4}{5}s^{3}+\frac{3}{5}s^{4},\qquad\tilde{\varepsilon}_{+}(s):=1-\frac{4}{5}s^{3}+\frac{8-3s}{5}s^{4}.

Then the following statements hold:

–

R(s^{-1},1,1,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{-}(s)),R(s^{-1},1,1,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{+}(s))
are continuous, for
s\in[0,1]
.

–

They satisfy the inequalities:

\forall\,s\in[0,1],\qquad R(s^{-1},1,1,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{-}(s))\geqslant 2,\quad R(s^{-1},1,1,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{+}(s))\leqslant-\frac{2}{3}.

Thus, for small values of the parameters
q,C,r
, the root
\varepsilon(s)
of
R(s^{-1},q,C,\varepsilon)=r
belongs to the interval
[\tilde{\varepsilon}_{-}(s),\tilde{\varepsilon}_{+}(s)]
.

One should still clarify what’s the meaning of ‘small’ in the statement. The series expansion of the root of
R(s^{-1},1,C,r)=0
is
\varepsilon(y)=1-\frac{4}{5}C^{2}s^{3}+\Big(1-\frac{r}{5}\Big)s^{4}+O(s^{5})
. One may check that the Lemma still holds for
1-C\leqslant t^{3}/10
and
|r|\leqslant 0.5
.

The continuity reduces to the fact that the series development of
R
contains only positive powers of
s
. The indicated inequalities are obtained by plotting the corresponding graphs.

For the second statement, a computation shows that the power series expansion of the root of
R(s^{-1},1,C,r)=0
is
\varepsilon(y)=1-\frac{4}{5}C^{2}s^{3}+\Big(1-\frac{r}{5}\Big)s^{4}+O(s^{5})
.

\Box

Lemma
4.2
is important to understand the monotonicity of the residue function. Below we compute the components of the gradient of
R
; the abbreviation
{l.o.t.}
stands for ‘lower order terms’. Also, we think off
C
as function of
q
and
\varepsilon
, since
C
is determined by the boundary condition at

(see Procedure
4.1
); a

.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}R}{{\rm d}y}&=\Big(1-q^{2}\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}\Big)\cdot 20y^{3}+17.5(1-\varepsilon)Cy^{1.5}-2(4\varepsilon-1-\varepsilon^{2})C^{2}+{l.o.t.};\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle{\Bigl.\frac{{\rm d}R}{{\rm d}q}\Bigr|}_{y=T}&=-10q\frac{(1+ee)^{2}}{4}T^{4}+7(1-\varepsilon)\frac{{\rm d}C}{{\rm d}q}T^{2.5}+{l.o.t.}\approx-10T^{4}-14(1-\varepsilon)BT^{2.5};\\[8.61108pt]

For

is mostly positive, so

Furthermore, the dominant component of the gradient is

5.1.2.
A partial minorant

When attempting to improve the precision of the lower bound, one inevitably faces the strong increasing tendency of
y\mapsto R(y,\dots)

The way out is to determine a partial lower bound along the boundary layer

. They yield the values of
\varepsilon

–

Our relevant functions

8.
Conclusion

The article investigates the equation of heat conduction with radiation:

u^{\prime\prime}_{\rm heat}=b^{2}(u^{4}_{\rm heat}-t^{4}),\quad u_{\rm heat}(0)=1,\;u_{\rm heat}(1)=t\in(0,1).

It combines analytical with numerical techniques.

On the analytic side, we determined global and partial, upper and lower bounds for the exact solution
u_{\rm heat}
. Also, we clarified the law, the value, governing the boundary layer property of
u_{\rm heat}
. Moreover, we estimated the initial derivative
u^{\prime}_{\rm heat}(1)
of the solution to the heat equation, which is essential for running the Runge-Kutta algorithm. The precision of the estimate for
u_{\rm heat}
is
O(t\cdot e^{-3B}).

On the numerical side, we implemented the analytical arguments into the MAPLE computer program and produced numerical data for various parameters. The precision in estimating
u_{\rm heat}
—the number of exact decimals— has the same order of magnitude as the boundary-value
bt^{0.25}
.
