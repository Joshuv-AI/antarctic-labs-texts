The error term in counting prime pairs
Leon Chou, Summer Haag, Jake Huryn, Andrew Ledoan

Abstract
. We relate the size of the error term in the Hardy–Littlewood conjectured formula for the number of prime pairs to the L 1 L^{1} norm of an exponential sum over the primes formed with the von Mangoldt function.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 11N05; Secondary 11P32, 11N36

1.
Introduction

Let
S(\alpha)
denote the exponential sum

S(\alpha)=\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)e(n\alpha),

where
e(u)=e^{2\pi iu}
and
\Lambda(n)
is von Mangoldt’s function defined by

\Lambda(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\log p&\mbox{if $n=p^{m}$ for some prime $p$ and integer $m\geq 1$,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The usual generating function for prime pairs is

(1)

\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leq N}\psi_{2}(N,k)e(k\alpha),

where

(2)

\psi_{2}(N,k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\leq N\\
n^{\prime}-n=k\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)\Lambda(n^{\prime}).

If
k
is zero, then the prime number theorem gives

(3)

\psi_{2}(N,0)=\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)^{2}=N\log N+O(N)\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$.}

If
k
is nonzero and even, then the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture
[
9
]
for prime pairs states that

\psi_{2}(N,k)\sim\mathfrak{S}(k)(N-\lvert k\rvert)\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$,}

where

\mathfrak{S}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle 2C_{2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid k\\
p>2\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{p-1}{p-2}\right)}&\mbox{if $k$ is nonzero and even,}\\
0&\mbox{if $k$ is odd,}\end{array}\right.

and

C_{2}=\prod_{p>2}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^{2}}\right)=0.66016\ldots.

If
k
is odd, then
\psi_{2}(N,k)
has nonzero terms only when
n
or
n^{\prime}
is a power of two. Hence,

\psi_{2}(N,k)\ll(\log N)^{2}.

From the recent works of Zhang
[
21
]
, Maynard
[
13
,
14
]
, and Tao
[
18
,
19
]
we now know that there exist infinitely many integers
k
and a constant
c_{1}
for which

\psi_{2}(N,k)\gg\frac{N}{(\log N)^{c_{1}}},

but the method used is unlikely to lead to asymptotic formulas or to prove this result for all even
k
. In this paper, we are interested with the size of the error in the Hardy–Littlewood conjectured formula for
\psi_{2}(N,k)
. We show, in particular, that it cannot always be small. This problem has previously been dealt with by Montgomery and Vaughan
[
16
]
for sums of primes. We will see that their method is less successful here. In the first place, for a given fixed even value of
k
, nothing that is nontrivial is currently known. Heath-Brown
[
10
]
proposed the following problem for twin primes at an Oberwolfach meeting in 1994:

If
p_{n}
denotes the
n
th twin prime, then

p_{n+1}-p_{n}=\Omega((\log p_{n})^{2}).

Improve on this result. Hence, or otherwise, improve on the trivial relation

\pi_{2}(x)-c_{2}\int_{2}^{x}\frac{dt}{(\log t)^{2}}=\Omega(1),

where
c_{2}
is a positive constant.

This is equivalent to proving

\psi_{2}(N,2)\neq\mathfrak{S}(2)N+O((\log N)^{2}),

but there has been no progress on this problem. Thus, instead of individual
k
, we will consider the total error, or variance, over all
k
and define

E(N)=\sum_{1\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(\psi_{2}(N,k)-\mathfrak{S}(k)(N-\lvert k\rvert))^{2}.

give a clear indication of the close agreement between
\psi_{2}(N,k)
and
\mathfrak{S}(k)(N-\lvert k\rvert)
. Based on this and more extensive numerical evidence supplied by Funkhouser et al.
[
2
]
, we propose the following conjecture concerning
E(N)
.

Prime Pair Error Conjecture
.

There exists a positive constant
c_{3}
such that

E(N)\sim c_{3}N^{2}(\log N)^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$.}

We show in Theorem
1
that the Prime Pair Error Conjecture implies the Riemann Hypothesis. Obtaining upper bounds for
E(N)
is a very hard problem. Assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet’s
L
-functions, we have the bound

E(N)\ll N^{5/2}(\log N)^{c_{4}}

for some positive constant
c_{4}
. This falls short of the bound in the Prime Pair Error Conjecture by a factor of
N^{1/2}
.

Our main results connect
E(N)
to the
L^{1}
norm of
S(\alpha)
. The latter was first studied by Vaughan
[
20
]
. He proved the lower bound

(4)

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\gg N^{1/2}.

It is easy to obtain an upper bound not much larger than this. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Parseval’s equation, together with (
1
) through (
3
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\right)^{1/2}=\psi_{2}(N,0)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))N^{1/2}(\log N)^{1/2}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$.}

This upper bound has been improved slightly by Goldston
[
6
]
, who proved, for any positive
\epsilon
and
N\geq N_{0}(\epsilon)
,

(5)

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\leq\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)N\log N\right]^{1/2}.

Equations (
4
) and (
5
) are respectively the current best upper and lower bounds known for the
L^{1}
norm of
S(\alpha)
. As we will see below, it appears to be a difficult problem to improve on either of these bounds. “It seems quite likely,” Vaughan
[
20
]
wrote, that there exists a positive constant
c_{5}
such that the
L^{1}
norm of
S(\alpha)
is asymptotically
c_{5}(N\log N)^{1/2}
as
N\to\infty
, “but if true this must lie very deep.” This problem is still unsolved. We will formulate it as the following conjecture.

Vaughan’s Conjecture
.

There exists a positive constant
c_{6}
satisfying
0<c_{6}\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
such that

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\sim c_{6}N^{1/2}(\log N)^{1/2}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$.}

We emphasize that both the Prime Pair Error Conjecture and Vaughan’s Conjecture are extremely difficult problems and there is little hope at present for making progress on proving them. What seems like a much easier problem is the following conjecture.

Prime Pair Error Lower Bound Conjecture
.

We have

E(N)\gg N^{2}(\log N)^{2}.

[
16
]
proved essentially this conjecture for the corresponding problem for the error in the Goldbach problem. Moreover, their method which we partly employ here almost works. Many of our results hold when we replace Vaughan’s
L^{1}
Conjecture with the following lower bound conjecture.

Vaughan Lower Bound Conjecture
.

We have

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\gg N^{1/2}(\log N)^{1/2}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$.}

In Section
2
, we will state all of our results in detail. Our first theorem collects together a number of simple results from Corollaries
1
,
2
, and
3
.

Theorem 1
.

(i) The Prime Pair Error Conjecture implies Vaughan’s Lower Bound Conjecture. (ii) If Vaughan’s Conjecture holds with
c_{7}<1/\sqrt{2}
, then the Prime Pair Error Lower Bound Conjecture is true. (iii) Moreover, if

E(N)=o(N^{2}(\log N)^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$,}

so that the Prime Pair Error Lower Bound Conjecture is false for all sufficiently large
N
, then Vaughan’s Conjecture is true with
c_{7}=1/\sqrt{2}
.

2.
Statement of results

It is not hard to relate
E(N)
to the classical results on the error in the prime number theorem. This follows Montgomery and Vaughan’s work
[
16
]
, but here it is particularly simple. We easily obtain the following result.

Theorem 2
.

We have

E(N)=\Omega(N^{2}(\log\log\log N)^{2}).

If
\Theta
is the supremum of the real parts of the zeros of Riemann’s zeta function, then

E(N)=\Omega(N^{1+2\Theta-\epsilon})

for every positive
\epsilon
.

If the Riemann Hypothesis is false, then the latter estimate in Theorem
2
is stronger than the former estimate. Moreover, we see that the Prime Pair Error Conjecture implies that
\Theta=1/2
and thus the Riemann Hypothesis. Theorem
2
uses the variability in the number of primes up to
N
to directly generate the growth in
E(N)
. However, we expect that the growth of
E(N)
is more strongly influenced by the additive generating function for prime pairs.

Theorem 3
.

If, for sufficiently large
N
and a computable large positive constant
c_{8}
,

(6)

\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}N\log N-c_{8}N,

then

(7)

E(N)\gg\left[\frac{1}{2}N\log N-\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right)^{2}\right]^{2}.

In particular, (
6
) implies

E(N)\gg N^{2}.

Unlike the lower bound in Theorem 2, Theorem 3 shows that, subject to (
6
),
E(N)\gg N^{2}
is never small. We immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

If, for sufficiently large
N
and a fixed constant
\delta
satisfying
0<\delta<1/2
,

\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta\right)N\log N,

then

E(N)\gg\delta^{2}N^{2}(\log N)^{2}.

Hence, if the Vaughan
L^{1}
Conjecture holds for any constant
c_{5}<1/\sqrt{2}
, then the Prime Pair Error Lower Bound Conjecture holds.

Corollary 2
.

If

E(N)=o(N^{2}(\log N)^{2}),

then

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}N^{1/2}(\log N)^{1/2}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$.}

To see this, we first note that, by Theorem 3, this follows from (
7
) subject to (
6
). On the other hand, if (
6
) is false for some set of values of
N
, then by Theorem 5 below, for those values of
N
,

\left(\frac{1}{2}N\log N-c_{9}N\right)^{1/2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}N\log N\right)^{1/2}+c_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\log N}\right)^{1/2}\log\log N.

The conclusion of Corollary 2 follows independent of the hypothesis that

E(N)=o(N^{2}(\log N)^{2}.

Our second main result shows that
E(N)
and the
L^{1}
norm of
S(\alpha)
cannot both be too small.

Theorem 4
.

We have

(E(N)+N^{2}(\log N)^{2})\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha+O(N^{1/2}(\log\log N)^{1/2})\right)^{2}\gg N^{3}(\log N)^{3}.

The following corollaries are immediate.

Corollary 3
.

If

E(N)\ll N^{2}(\log N)^{c_{11}}

for some positive constant
c_{11}
satisfying
2\leq c_{11}<3
, then

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\gg N^{2}(\log N)^{(3-c_{11})/2}.

In particular, if
c_{11}=2
and if

E(N)\ll N^{2}(\log N)^{2},

then

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\gg N^{1/2}(\log N)^{1/2}.

Consequently, the Prime Pair Error Conjecture implies Vaughan’s Lower Bound Conjecture.

Corollary 4
.

If

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\ll N^{1/2}(\log N)^{c_{12}}

for some positive constant
c_{12}
satisfying
0<c_{12}\leq 1/2
, then

E(N)\gg N^{2}(\log N)^{3-2c_{12}}.

Corollary 5
.

If

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\ll N^{1/2}(\log\log N)^{1/2},

then

E(N)\gg\frac{N^{2}(\log N)^{3}}{\log\log N}.

Many of our results depend on improving the upper bound in (
5
). Turning to this question, we prove the following result.

Theorem 5
.

We have

\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}N\log N+\frac{3}{2}N\log\log N+O(N).

Alternatively, for some positive constant
c_{10}
and
N
sufficiently large,

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}N\log N\right)^{1/2}+c_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\log N}\right)^{1/2}\log\log N.

From Theorem 3, even small improvements in Theorem 5 would have important consequences for
E(N)
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
2

For positive
N
, using

\psi(N)=\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n),

we obtain

\psi(N)^{2}=\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\leq N}\Lambda(n)\Lambda(n^{\prime})=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leq N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\leq N\\
n^{\prime}-n=k\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)\Lambda(n^{\prime})=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leq N}\psi_{2}(N,k).

Let

\psi(N)=N+R(N).

The prime number theorem is equivalent to

R(N)=o(N)\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as $N\to\infty$.}

Since

\psi(N)^{2}=N^{2}+2NR(N)+R(N)^{2}

and

\sum_{1\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(N-\lvert k\rvert)\mathfrak{S}(k)=N^{2}+O(N\log N)

(see
[
3
,
15
]
), we deduce that

\sum_{1\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(\psi_{2}(N,k)-(N-\lvert k\rvert)\mathfrak{S}(k))=R(N)(2N+R(N))+O(N\log N).

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\left\lvert\sum_{1\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(\psi_{2}(N,k)-(N-\lvert k\rvert)\mathfrak{S}(k))\right\rvert^{2}\leq 2NE(N).

We conclude that

E(N)\geq 2N\left[R(N)\left(1+\frac{R(N)}{2N}\right)+O(\log N)\right]^{2}.

By Littlewood’s theorem,

R(N)=\Omega_{\pm}(N^{1/2}\log\log\log N)

(see
[
17
]
), and the first assertion of Theorem
2
follows. If the Riemann Hypothesis is false, we use

R(N)=\Omega_{\pm}(N^{\Theta-\epsilon})

for every positive
\epsilon
(see
[
12
,
17
]
). In fact, Littlewood proved that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\pm R(N)}{N^{1/2}\log\log\log N}\geq\frac{1}{2},

and so it follows that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{E(N)}{N^{2}(\log\log\log N)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

4.
Proof of Theorem
3

Our main results depend on approximating
\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}
by

V_{y}(\alpha)=\sum_{q\leq y}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left\lvert I\left(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right)\right\rvert^{2},

where

I(\alpha)=\sum_{n\leq N}e(n\alpha),

where
\mu(q)
is the Möbius function and
\phi(q)
is the Euler totient function. Here,
V_{y}(\alpha)
is obtained by taking the diagonal terms from squaring the absolute value of the major arc approximation
J_{Q}(\alpha)
of
S(\alpha)
used by Goldston
[
6
]
and removing the restricted support for each arc. (This is explained in Section
7
. Our use of
V_{y}(\alpha)
is independent of these heuristics.) Obviously,
V_{y}(\alpha)
is real-valued and nonnegative. Using the relation

\lvert I(\alpha)\rvert^{2}=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(N-\lvert k\rvert)e(k\alpha),

we obtain the Fourier series

\displaystyle V_{y}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(N-\lvert k\rvert)\left(\sum_{q\leq y}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(-\frac{ka}{q}\right)\right)e(k\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(N-\lvert k\rvert)\mathfrak{S}_{y}(k)e(k\alpha),

where

\mathfrak{S}_{y}(k)=\sum_{q\leq y}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}c_{q}(-k)

and

c_{q}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{an}{q}\right).

The Ramanujan sum
c_{q}(n)
is an even function in
n
and a multiplicative function of
q
for a fixed value of
n
, and satisfies

(8)

c_{q}(n)=\sum_{d\mid(q,n)}d\mu\left(\frac{q}{d}\right)=\mu\left(\frac{q}{(q,n)}\right)\frac{\phi(q)}{\displaystyle\phi\left(\frac{q}{(q,n)}\right)}

(see
[
17
, Theorem 4.1, (4.7)]
).

It is now easy to verify that
\mathfrak{S}(k)
in the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture for prime pairs has the series representation

\mathfrak{S}(k)=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)^{2}}c_{q}(-k)

for
k\neq 0
. While we do not define
\mathfrak{S}(k)
for
k=0
, we have from
[
17
, 2.1.1, Ex. 17]
that

(9)

\mathfrak{S}_{y}(0)=\sum_{q\leq y}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)}=\log y+O(1).

Let

(10)

a_{0}(N,y)=\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)^{2}-N\mathfrak{S}_{y}(0).

By (
3
) and (
9
), for
1\leq y\leq N
,

(11)

a_{0}(N,y)=N\log\left(\frac{N}{y}\right)+O(N).

Let

V(\alpha)=\sum_{1\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(N-\lvert k\rvert)\mathfrak{S}(k)e(k\alpha).

By Parseval’s identity,

\displaystyle E(N)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\lvert\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}-V(\alpha)-\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)^{2}\right\rvert^{2}d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert(\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}-V_{y}(\alpha)-a_{0}(N,y))-(V(\alpha)-V_{y}(\alpha)+N\mathfrak{S}_{y}(0))\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha.

Let now

J=J(N,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}-V_{y}(\alpha)-a_{0}(N,y)\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha

and

W=W(N,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert V(\alpha)-V_{y}(\alpha)+N\mathfrak{S}_{y}(0)\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

(12)

E(N)\geq J-2(JW)^{1/2}+W=(J^{1/2}-W^{1/2})^{2}.

We obtain a lower bound for
E(N)
from a lower bound for
J
and an asymptotic formula for
W
. From Parseval’s identity and
[
7
, Theorem 2]
, for
1\leq y\leq N^{1/2}
,

(13)

\begin{split}W&=\sum_{1\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq N}(N-\lvert k\rvert)^{2}(\mathfrak{S}(k)-\mathfrak{S}_{y}(k))^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{3}N^{3}H(y)-\frac{1}{4}N^{2}\left[\log\left(\frac{N}{y^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]+MN^{2}\log\left(\frac{N}{y^{2}}\right)\\
&\hskip 10.00002pt+O(N^{2})+O\left(\frac{N^{2}}{y^{1/2}}\log(2N)\right),\end{split}

where

H(y)=\frac{L}{y^{2}}(1+o(1)),\hskip 10.00002ptL=\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{2p-1}{p(p-1)^{2}}\right),

and

M=\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p}\frac{(p-2)\log p}{p(p-1)^{2}}.

Hence, for
1\leq y\leq N^{1/2}
,

(14)

W\asymp\frac{N^{3}}{y^{2}}.

The implied upper and lower bound constants are computable since, as mentioned in
[
7
]
, the term
O(N^{2})
in (
13
) can be replaced by
c_{13}N^{2}+o(N^{2})
for a computable constant
c_{13}
.

Montgomery and Vaughan
[
16
]
obtained a lower bound for what corresponds to
J
for the Goldbach problem. Their argument is that, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

(15)

J\geq T^{2},

where

T=T(N,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}-V_{y}(\alpha)-a_{0}(N,y)\rvert\,d\alpha.

But, by (
10
),

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}(\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}-V_{y}(\alpha)-a_{0}(N,y))\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha-\int_{0}^{1}V_{y}(\alpha)\,d\alpha-a_{0}(N,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)^{2}-N\mathfrak{S}_{y}(0)-a_{0}(N,y)\equiv 0,

and this argument fails. In the Goldbach problem, the term
a_{0}(N,y)=0
, which is why it succeeds there.
We now modify this argument to obtain a lower bound for
J
in terms of the
L^{1}
norm of
S(\alpha)
.

Lemma 1
.

If, for
y_{0}\leq y\leq N^{1/2}
for some
y_{0}
satisfying
N^{1/2}/\log N\leq y_{0}\leq N^{1/2}
and a computable positive constant
c_{14}
,

\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right)^{2}\leq a_{0}(N,y)-c_{14}\frac{N^{3/2}}{y},

then

E(N)\geq\frac{1}{4}\left[a_{0}(N,y)-\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right)^{2}\right]^{2}\gg\frac{N^{3}}{y^{2}}.

Theorem 3 is the special case of Lemma
1
where we take
y_{0}=N^{1/2}
.

Proof.

We utilize the inequality, for any two real numbers
a
and
b
,

\lvert a^{2}-b^{2}\rvert\geq(\lvert a\rvert-\lvert b\rvert)^{2}.

This inequality holds true because of the identity

\lvert a^{2}-b^{2}\rvert=(\lvert a\rvert-\lvert b\rvert)^{2}+2\min(\lvert a\rvert,\lvert b\rvert)\lvert\lvert a\rvert-\lvert b\rvert\rvert,

which in turn holds true since we can assume without loss of generality that
a\geq b\geq 0
, in which case it is obvious. Since
V_{y}(\alpha)\geq 0
and
a_{0}(N,y)>0
for
1\leq y\leq N^{1/2}
, we have

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert^{2}-a_{0}(N,y)\rvert\,d\alpha-\int_{0}^{1}V_{y}(\alpha)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert-a_{0}(N,y)^{\frac{1}{2}}\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha-N\mathfrak{S}_{y}(0)

First Proof of Theorem 5

If

n=\prod_{k=1}^{r}p_{k}^{a_{k}},

then

\log n=\sum_{k=1}^{r}a_{k}\log p_{k}=\sum_{d\mid n}\Lambda(d),

and so by Möbius inversion

\Lambda(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\log(n/d).

Following
[
4
]
, we approximate
\Lambda(n)
by

\Lambda_{R}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\leq R\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right),

which truncates the number of divisors
d
of
n
and smooths the sum. If
1<n\leq R
, then the condition
d\leq R
can be dropped, so that
\Lambda_{R}(n)=\Lambda(n)
. Therefore, we can interpret
\Lambda_{R}(n)
as an approximation of
\Lambda(n)
which gets better as
R
increases up to
n
where it becomes
\Lambda(n)
.

We thus introduce the approximation for
S(\alpha)
,

S_{R}(\alpha)=\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda_{R}(n)e(n\alpha).

We make use of the formula, for
1\leq R\leq N
,

\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda_{R}(n)^{2}=N\log R+O(N)

due to Graham
[
8
]
. In the range
1\leq R\leq N^{1/2}/\log N
, this can be obtained by an elementary calculation. By the prime number theorem, we also obtain, for
1\leq R\leq N
and any positive constant
c_{13}
,

\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda_{R}(n)\Lambda(n)=N\log R+O(R)+O(N(\log N)^{-c_{13}})

(see
[
4
]
).
By Parseval’s identity, we have by (
3
) and the above estimates, for
1\leq R\leq N
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)-S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\lvert\sum_{n\leq N}(\Lambda(n)-\Lambda_{R}(n))e(n\alpha)\right\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq N}(\Lambda(n)-\Lambda_{R}(n))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)^{2}-2\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)\Lambda_{R}(n)+\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda_{R}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle=N\log N-2N\log R+N\log R+O(N)

\displaystyle=N\log\left(\frac{N}{R}\right)+O(N).

Hence, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, for
1\leq R\leq N
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)-S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)-S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert^{2}\,d\alpha\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(N\log\left(\frac{N}{R}\right)+O(N)\right)^{1/2}.

By the reverse triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha-\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)-S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\left(N\log\left(\frac{N}{R}\right)+O(N)\right)^{1/2}.

We now make use of the following result that we will prove below.

Lemma 2
.

We have

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\ll R\log N.

Thus, we conclude

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\leq\left(N\log\left(\frac{N}{R}\right)+O(N)\right)^{1/2}+O(R\log N).

We take
R=N^{1/2}/(\log N)^{3/2}
and on squaring obtain

\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}N\log N+\frac{3}{2}N\log\log N+O(N),

from which

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}N\log N\right)^{1/2}+c_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\log N}\right)^{1/2}\log\log N

for a positive constant
c_{10}
. This proves Theorem 5.

It remains to prove Lemma
2
. We use

\sum_{n\leq N}e(n\alpha)\ll\min\left(N,\frac{1}{\|\alpha\|}\right)

to obtain

\displaystyle S_{R}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\leq R\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)e(n\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\leq R}\mu(d)\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)\sum_{m\leq N/d}e(dm\alpha)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{d\leq R}\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)\min\left(\frac{N}{d},\frac{1}{\|d\alpha\|}\right).

Thus,

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\ll\sum_{d\leq R}\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\min\left(\frac{N}{d},\frac{1}{\|d\alpha\|}\right)\,d\alpha.

As a function of
\alpha
, the integrand on the right-hand side is a function with period
1/d
. Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\min\left(\frac{N}{d},\frac{1}{\|d\alpha\|}\right)d\alpha

\displaystyle\ll d\int_{0}^{1/d}\min\left(\frac{N}{d},\frac{1}{\|d\alpha\|}\right)d\alpha

\displaystyle\ll d\left(\int_{0}^{1/N}\frac{N}{d}\,d\alpha+\int_{1/N}^{1/(2d)}\frac{1}{d\alpha}\,d\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\ll 1+\log\left(\frac{N}{2d}\right)\ll\log N.

Hence,

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert S_{R}(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha\ll\sum_{d\leq R}\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)\log N\ll R\log N.

This completes the proof of Lemma
2
.

The idea here is that
S_{R}(\alpha)
contributes
N\log R
in the
L^{2}
norm but nothing in the
L^{1}
norm until
R
is about size
N^{1/2}
. Thus, for example, with
R=N^{1/2}/\log N
, we see
S_{R}(\alpha)
removes half of the
L^{2}
norm of
S(\alpha)
, but leaves the
L^{1}
norm of
S(\alpha)
unchanged. Hence, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives an improved upper bound for the
L^{1}
norm of
S(\alpha)
.

and directly pull out the major arc contribution. This provides a slightly better approximation for

than

Let

is a fraction with

. Hence, we obtain a spike function in each interval which is largest when

of order

\mu(q/d)=\mu(m)=\mu(q)\mu(d)
. Hence,

\lambda_{R}(n)=\sum_{q\leq R}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)}\sum_{d\mid(q,n)}d\mu(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leq R\\
d\mid n\end{subarray}}d\mu(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq R\\
d\mid q\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)}.

Since

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq R\\
d\mid q\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(q)^{2}}{\phi(q)}=\sum_{dm\leq R}\frac{\mu(dm)^{2}}{\phi(dm)}=\frac{\mu(d)^{2}}{\phi(d)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq R/d\\
(m,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(m)^{2}}{\phi(m)},

we have

(22)

\lambda_{R}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leq R\\
d\mid n\end{subarray}}\frac{d\mu(d)}{\phi(d)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq R/d\\
(n,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n)^{2}}{\phi(n)}.

Thus, from (
21
),

\displaystyle\lambda_{R}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leq R\\
d\mid n\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)+\textrm{smaller terms}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{R}(n)+\textrm{smaller terms}.

This shows that
\Lambda_{R}(n)
is the first term in the more refined approximation
\lambda_{R}(n)
.

As in the proof for
S_{R}(\alpha)
in Lemma
2
, by (
19
) and (
22
),

\mathscr{S}_{R}(\alpha)=\sum_{n\leq N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\leq R\end{subarray}}a(d,R)e(n\alpha)=\sum_{d\leq R}a(d,R)\sum_{m\leq N/d}e(md\alpha),

where

a(d,R)=\frac{d\mu(d)}{\phi(d)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq R/d\\
(n,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(m)^{2}}{\phi(m)}.

Thus, as before,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\mathscr{S}_{R}(\alpha)\rvert\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d\leq R}\lvert a(d,R)\rvert\int_{0}^{1}\min\left(\frac{N}{d},\frac{1}{\|\alpha d\|}\right)d\alpha

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{d\leq R}\lvert a(d,R)\rvert\left[1+\log\left(\frac{N}{2d}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ll\log N\sum_{d\leq R}\log\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)\ll R\log N.

This completes the proof of Lemma
3
.
