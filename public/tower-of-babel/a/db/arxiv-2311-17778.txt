Unified Binary and Multiclass Margin-Based Classification
Yutong Wang yutongw@umich.edu, Clayton Scott clayscot@umich.edu

Abstract
The notion of margin loss has been central to the development and analysis of algorithms for binary classification. To date, however, there remains no consensus as to the analogue of the margin loss for multiclass classification. In this work, we show that a broad range of multiclass loss functions, including many popular ones, can be expressed in the relative margin form , a generalization of the margin form of binary losses. The relative margin form is broadly useful for understanding and analyzing multiclass losses as shown by our prior work [ Wang and Scott 2020 , Wang and Scott 2021 ] . To further demonstrate the utility of this way of expressing multiclass losses, we use it to extend the seminal result of Bartlett et al. 2006 on classification-calibration of binary margin losses to multiclass. We then analyze the class of Fenchel-Young losses, and expand the set of these losses that are known to be classification-calibrated.

Introduction

Classification into
k\geq 2
categories is the learning task of selecting a
classifier
, i.e., a function from the feature space
\mathcal{X}
to the set of labels
[k]:=\{1,\dots,k\}
, given training data. Many of the most popular and successful classification methods aim to find a classifier with minimum risk, where the risk of a classifier is the expected value of a loss function that measures the quality of predictions. Indeed, logistic regression, support vector machines, boosting, and neural network methods can all be viewed as algorithms to minimize the risk associated with a certain loss. In practice, “loss-based” approaches to binary (
k=2
) and multiclass (
k\geq 3
) cases are formulated differently. Because of this discrepancy, the theory and practice of multiclass methods often lag behind their binary counterparts. The purpose of this work is to bridge this gap by introducing a framework that unifies binary and multiclass loss-based classification.

The standard approach to binary classification is to learn a
discriminant
function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that maps an instance
x
to a
discriminant

g(x)
, e.g.,
g(x)=w^{T}x+b
for linear classification. Identifying
\{1,2\}
with
\{-1,+1\}
, a label
y
is predicted by
\mathrm{sign}(g(x))
. The loss ascribed to a discriminant
g
and a pair
(x,y)
is defined in terms of a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, with the loss being equal to
\psi((-1)^{y}g(x))
. Loss functions of this form are referred to as binary
margin loss
functions. Examples include the logistic loss
\psi(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})
(logistic regression), the hinge loss
\psi(t)=\max(0,1-t)
(support vector machines), the exponential loss
\psi(t)=e^{-t}
(AdaBoost), and the sigmoid loss
\psi(t)=1/(1+e^{t})
(some neural networks).

The conventional approach to multiclass classification seeks to learn a
class-score function

f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k}):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, e.g., a feed-forward neural network. The label for an input
x
is predicted by taking the argmax of the
class-score vectors

\mathbf{v}:=f(x)
. Multiclass loss functions, e.g., cross entropy, may be viewed as functions
\mathcal{L}:\{1,\dots,k\}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
. The loss incurred by a class-score function
f
on a pair
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times[k]
is then
\mathcal{L}(y,f(x))
. When a multiclass loss function is expressed in terms of a class-score function output, we say that it is in
class-score form
.

We show that a large family of multiclass loss functions in class-score form, including cross-entropy, multiclass exponential loss
[
Mukherjee and Schapire 2013
]
, multiclass hinge losses
[
Crammer and Singer 2001
,
Weston and Watkins 1998
]
,
Gamma-Phi losses
[
Beijbom et al. 2014
]
,
and Fenchel-Young losses
[
Blondel et al. 2020
]
, can be expressed in what we call the
relative-margin form
. Instead of being defined in terms of a class-score function output
f(x)
, in the relative margin form a loss is expressed
\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}g(x))
, which involves the following elements:

1.
a symmetric
1
1

1

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}
,

2.

3.
a discriminant function of the form

When

Background

In this section, we define the probabilistic setting assumed throughout this work.
Moreover, we first review the background on classification-calibration.

Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be drawn from a joint distribution
P
over
\mathcal{X}\times[k]
.
Let
\mathbb{I}
be the indicator function and
\mathbb{I}\{y\neq j\}
be the
01-loss
for
y,j\in[k]

The goal of classification is to select a classifier

minimizing the
01-risk

R_{01,P}(h):=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y\neq h(X)\}\right]

A fundamental approach for learning a classifier is
empirical risk minimization
(ERM), which selects an

minimizing the empirical 01-risk

\hat{R}_{01}^{n}(h):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{y_{i}\neq h(x_{i})\}
over a class of functions
\mathcal{H}
.

Directly minimizing the empirical 01-risk objective is often intractable
4
4

4

See
Bhattacharyya et al. 2018
and the references therein
due to the discreteness of the objective.
The surrogate-based approach addresses this issue as follows.
Define
the
\mathcal{L}
-risk

for a fixed
continuous loss function
\mathcal{L}

by
R_{\mathcal{L}}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}[\mathcal{L}(Y,f(X))]

and
the empirical
\mathcal{L}
-risk by
\hat{R}_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(f):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}(y_{i},f(x_{i}))
. Minimizing
\hat{R}_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(f)
over a family of
f

A class-score function
f
induces a discrete-valued classifier

\argmax\circ f(x):=\argmax_{j=1,\dots,k}f_{j}(x)
with ties broken arbitrarily.
An important question is whether good performance with respect to the surrogate
\mathcal{L}
-risk “transfers” back to the 01-risk, the original objective.

Definition 1.1
.

A surrogate loss
\mathcal{L}
has the

5
5

5

This property appears in many works, e.g.,

For binary margin-based losses

1.3
Related work

\bullet

Sufficient conditions for CC of multiclass convex margin-based losses
.
For binary convex loss in margin form, the sufficient conditions for CC are easy to verify and apply to common losses of interest. Indeed,
Bartlett et al. 2006

Multiclass margins & margin-based loss
.
A line of work has considered the problem of extending the binary margin

\psi^{\mathsf{CE}}(\mathbf{z})=\log(1+\exp(-z_{1})+\exp(-z_{2}))

Example 2.5
.

The

[

The cross entropy

The

[

\phi(t)=\exp(-t)
and

Example 2.6
.

A notable case of the Gamma-Phi loss is when

The

2.1
Matrix label code and the relative margin form

This section introduces the multiclass generalization of
\{\pm 1\}
: the
matrix label code

\{\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\}_{y=1}^{k}
.

Definition 2.8

(Matrix label code)
.

For
k\geq 2
and
y\in[k]
, define the
(k-1)\times(k-1)
matrix
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
as follows:
For
y=k
,
\bm{\Upsilon}_{k}:=\mathbf{I}_{k-1}
.
For
y\in[k-1]
, define
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
column-wise by

[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}]_{:j}:=\begin{cases}\mathbb{e}^{(k-1)}_{j}&:j\neq y\\
-\mathbb{1}^{(k-1)}&:j=y,\end{cases}\quad\mbox{for each $j\in[k-1]$.}

Equivalently, to construct
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
for each
y\in[k-1]
, first take the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}_{k-1}
, then replace the
y
-th column by all

-1
’s.

Note that
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{2}=\mathbf{I}_{k-1}
for all
y\in[k-1]
, i.e.,
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
is an
involution
.
Moreover, in the binary case where
k=2
, we have
\bm{\Upsilon}_{1}=-1
and
\bm{\Upsilon}_{2}=1
, i.e., the matrix label code reduces to the label encoding
\{\pm 1\}
.

Next, recall that a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

is
symmetric
if
f(\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}(\cdot))=f(\cdot)
for all
\sigma\in\mathtt{Sym}(n)
.
We now state the main property of the PERM loss and the matrix label code:

Theorem 2.9

(Relative-margin form)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a PERM loss with template
\psi
,
and let

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
y\in[k]
be arbitrary.
Then

\psi
is a symmetric function.
Moreover,

\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathbf{v})=\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}).

(2)

Conversely, let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric function. Define a multiclass loss function
\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}):\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
according to
eq.

2
. Then
\mathcal{L}
is a PERM loss with template
\psi
.

Equation

2
is referred to as the
relative margin form
of
\mathcal{L}
.
Theorem
2.9
and other results in this section are proved in Section
C
in the appendix.

Remark 2.10
.

The relative margin form can be interpreted as a one-to-one correspondence between PERM losses
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
and symmetric functions
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Thus, we can refer to a PERM loss by either
\mathcal{L}
or
\psi
without ambiguity.

Remark 2.11

(Uniqueness of the matrix label code)
.

In Section
J
of the appendix, we
prove a unique-ness result: any set of matrices
\{{\bm{\Upsilon}}_{y}^{\prime}\}_{y=1}^{k}
satisfying the conclusion of Theorem
2.9
is equal to the matrix label code
(Definition
2.8
) up to row permutations of the
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
’s.

Remark 2.12
.

Observe
that
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}=(v_{y}-v_{1},v_{y}-v_{2},\dots,v_{y}-v_{k})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
where the
v_{y}-v_{y}
entry is omitted.
This is Lemma
B.2
in the appendix.
Now, note that the right hand side is exactly the relative margin as defined in
Rosset et al. 2003
.
The advantage of the left hand expression
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}
is that the label
y
acts via its label encoding
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
by left matrix multplication.
This “disentanglement” of the label encoding and the loss can facilitate calculations and will be crucial in our key
Lemma
G.6
.

Remark 2.13

(Prior work)
.

The matrices in the definition of the matrix label code have been used by
Wang and Scott 2020
,
Wang and Scott 2021
for
analyzing the Weston-Watkins (WW) SVMs
[
Weston and Watkins 1998
]
.

Wang and Scott 2020
show that the hinge loss from the WW-SVM is calibrated with respect to the ordered partition loss, and uses this theory to explain the empirical observation made by
Dogan et al. 2016
that WW-SVM performs well even under significant label noise.

Wang and Scott 2021

derive a reparametrization of the Weston-Watkins dual problem that decomposes into subproblems that can be solved exactly in
O(k\log(k))
time, leading to faster performance for the linear WW-SVMs with many classes.
The “disentanglement” discussed in the previous remark is the key ingredient for deriving this reparametrization.

This work extends
Wang and Scott 2020
,
Wang and Scott 2021
beyond the multiclass SVM setting, and provides a framework for using these matrices for
margin-based losses.

2.2
Relationship between class-score functions and discriminant functions

As mentioned in the introduction, the “sign” function
converts a real-valued discriminant function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
to a discrete-valued classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\to\{1,2\}

by choosing
h(x)
to be
y\in\{1,2\}
such that
(-1)^{y}g(x)\geq 0
with ties broken arbitrarily.
To generalize this to the multiclass case, first recall that when
k=2
, the matrix label code is equal to
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}=(-1)^{y}
.
Thus,
(-1)^{y}g(x)\geq 0
iff
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}g(x)\geq 0
.
More simply put,
y
is the predicted class if and only if
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
is the sign of
g(x)
.

Next, we define the multiclass “sign” function analogously.
Given
a vector-valued discriminant function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
we define a discrete-valued classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\to[k]
by taking
y\in[k]
such that
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}g(x)\succeq\mathbb{0}
. Similar to the binary case, ties can be broken arbitrarily.
Thus,
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
can be viewed as the sign of
g(x)
.
To relate this multiclass “sign” function method for producing a classifier to the conventional “
\argmax
” method, we have

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\mathbf{z}:=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}
, and
y\in[k]
. Then

y\in\argmax_{j}v_{j}
iff

\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z}\succeq\mathbb{0}
.
Furthermore, let
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathbf{z}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. Then

y\in\argmax_{j}v_{j}

iff

y\in\argmax_{j}v_{j}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For the first part, recall from
Remark
2.12
that

\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}=(v_{y}-v_{1},v_{y}-v_{2},\dots,v_{y}-v_{k})^{\top}
where the
v_{y}-v_{y}
entry is omitted.
Therefore,
the assertion
“
y\in\argmax_{j}v_{j}
iff

\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z}\succeq 0
” follows immediately from the definition of
\succeq
and
\mathbf{z}
.
For the “Furthermore” part, note that by construction we have

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=(0,v_{2}-v_{1},v_{3}-v_{1},\dots,v_{k}-v_{1})^{\top}
.
By adding the constant
v_{1}
to all entries of
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
, we recover
\mathbf{v}
. Thus, the indices maximizing
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
are the same as those of
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k})^{\top}
.
∎

The first part of Proposition
2.14
gives an intuitive explanation for our earlier definition of a multiclass “sign”.
The second part of
Proposition
2.14
gives a simple formula for computing the “
\argmax
” from the discriminant function.

Remark 2.15
.

Proposition
2.14
can be seen as an equivalence between class-score functions and discriminant functions.
Given a class-score function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
we can derive
a discriminant function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{k-1}

by defining
g(x):=\mathbf{D}f(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Conversely, we can view the discriminant function
g
as the “given”,
and derive a class-score function
f
by defining
f(x):=\begin{bmatrix}0&-g(x)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
In both cases,
\argmax f(x)=\argmax\begin{bmatrix}0&-g(x)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.

3
Classification-calibration and Consistency

This section is a brief review of the core definitions and theorem from

in preparation for our results on sufficient conditions for classification-calibration.

Below, we assume the probability setting introduced earlier in Section
1.2
.

Definition 3.1

(Range and its convex hull)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a function.
Denote by
\mathsf{ran}(f):=\{f(x):x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}

Definition 3.2

(

)
.

A set

if there exists a

11

11

The function
\theta
is called a
calibrated link
for

.

\theta:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to[k]

\textstyle\inf\{\langle\mathbf{p},\bm{\zeta}\rangle:\bm{\zeta}\in S\,:\,p_{\theta(\bm{\zeta})}<\max\mathbf{p}\}>\inf_{\bm{\zeta}\in S}\langle\mathbf{p},\bm{\zeta}\rangle

(3)

\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{k}
.

Intuitively, Definition

says that the lowest achievable conditional risk when predicting the wrong label (Eqn. (
3

conditional Bayes risk (Eqn. (
3

Define

\underline{\smash{\argmax}}(v)=\min\{i\in[k]:v_{i}=\max_{j\in[k]}v_{j}\}.

When

is permutation equivariant and classification-calibrated, we can take

to be simply

. See

.
As alluded to earlier, the significance of Definition

Theorem 3.3

(

)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a PERM loss. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of
all
Borel functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}

If

\mathcal{L}

has the

For all sequence of function classes

\textstyle R_{\mathcal{L},P}(\hat{f}_{n})\overset{P}{\to}\inf_{f}R_{\mathcal{L},P}(f)\quad\mbox{implies}\quad R_{01,P}(\underline{\smash{\argmax}}\circ\hat{f}_{n})\overset{P}{\to}\inf_{h}R_{01,P}(h)

Remark 3.4
.

In applications,

is often taken to be an

-risk empirical minimizer over a training dataset of cardinality

any
sequence of functions
\hat{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Checking that a multiclass loss

is classification-calibration is a non-trivial task, requiring delicate analysis of the loss function

]
.
Recently,

established a sufficient condition of classification-calibration for Gamma-Phi losses (Example

Below, we derive a sufficient conditions for general PERM losses.

4
Regular PERM losses

To demonstrate the utility of the relative margin form and Theorem
2.9
, we prove
in this section
Theorem
4.7
,
a sufficient condition for classification-calibration (CC) of multiclass loss functions.
To this end, we begin with the necessary definitions.

Definition 4.1
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is

1.

coercive

if for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the
set

\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(\mathbf{v})\leq c\}

(i.e., the
c
-sublevel set
)
is bounded,

2.

semi-coercive

if for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
there exists
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\textstyle\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(\mathbf{v})\leq c\}\subseteq\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:b\leq\min_{j\in[n]}v_{j}\}.

The definition of a coercive function is well-studied
[
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
]
.
However, semi-coercivity appears to be new.
Intuitively, a function is semi-coercive if, for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
, its
c
-sublevel set is contained in a translation of the positive orthant.

Definition 4.2

(Regular PERM loss)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a PERM loss with template
\psi
.
We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
regular
if

\psi
is nonnegative, twice differentiable, strictly convex, semi-coercive,
and
the gradient
\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\prec\mathbb{0}
is entrywise negative for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.

Below, we give a sufficient condition for a Gamma-Phi loss (Example
2.5
) to be a regular PERM loss. But first, we discuss the intuition behind the condition
\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\prec\mathbb{0}
.
The condition
\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\prec\mathbb{0}
is reminiscent of a condition in
Bartlett et al. 2006
, which shows that in the binary case a convex margin loss
\psi
is classification-calibrated if and only if
\psi
is differentiable at
0
and
\dot{\psi}(0)<0
where the “overdot” denotes taking derivative of a univariate function.
If we view
-\dot{\psi}(\cdot)
as a “vector field” on
\mathbb{R}
, then this condition on the derivative can be stated as “the vector field
-\dot{\psi}(\cdot)
near
0
points toward the positive half of the real line”.

Figure 2:
Vector field

where

is the template of the cross entropy (Example
2.4
).
The contour lines are shaded according to the value of the function
\psi
(darker is larger). See Figure
1
.

The condition
\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\prec\mathbb{0}
can be thought of in a similar fashion.
See Figure

where

Note that the vectors point toward the shaded region whose interior is the positive orthant in

However, Definition

requires the strict negativity of the gradient for all of

Next, we define the “truncation” of a PERM loss

Proposition 4.3

(Truncation)
.

Assume

Let

Define

is a well-defined symmetric function

Proof.

Let

The condition that

.
∎

By Theorem

, the symmetric function

.
Note that if

6
Discussion and Future Directions

We proposed the relative margin form as a multiclass extension to the popular (binary) margin loss framework.
We characterized the set of losses that can be expressed in the relative margin form — the PERM losses.
Our Theorem
4.7
generalizes to the multiclass case the seminal result of
[
Bartlett et al. 2006
, Theorem 6]
.
Central to our analysis is the matrix label code (Definition
2.8
) which we expect to be useful beyond the scope here.
Utilizing our framework, we extended the existing set of known sufficient conditions for the classification-calibration of Fenchel-Young losses.
We expect the framework to be useful for research on multiclass classification, e.g., for
\mathcal{H}
-consistency

[
Long and Servedio 2013
,
Zhang and Agarwal 2020
,
Awasthi et al. 2022
]
.

\bullet\,\,
Characterization of classification-calibration
.
One weakness of our work is that, compared to the characterization of CC in the binary case
[
Bartlett et al. 2006
, Theorem 6]
, we are only able to prove an analogous result (Theorem
4.7
) in the multiclass case under additional assumptions.
Future work will investigate whether this can be improved.

\bullet\,\,
Cost-weighted classification
.
Our results are limited to the traditional notion of classification-calibration under the assumption that the cost of misclassifying all classes are the same.
The more general notion of
\mathbf{c}
-classification-calibration described in
Williamson et al. 2016
allows the cost of misclassification to be class-dependent according to some class-specific weight vector
\mathbf{c}
.
An interesting future direction is to consider
a setting where a general
\mathbf{c}
-classification-calibration sufficient condition similar to our
Theorem
4.7
can be derived for non-symmetric losses.
More specifically, given a classification-calibrated PERM loss, is there a principled way to modify the loss to make it
\mathbf{c}
-classification calibrated?

\bullet\,\,
Combining losses
.
Hui et al. 2023
demonstrate the effectiveness of summing existing losses (the cross entropy and the square loss) to design new losses for classification.
Unfortunately, our result in Proposition
4.8

does not apply to their new loss since the square loss is not totally regular.
One approach may be to connect our technique with that of
Williamson and Cranko 2023
for studying the geometric properties of sums of losses.

\bullet\,\,
Beyond classification
.
While this work is concerned with classification-calibration, there are many works on calibration for more general discrete supervised learning tasks.

Ramaswamy and Agarwal 2016
developed theory for multiclass classification with abstain option and, more generally, losses defined over finite sets i.e., discrete losses.

Finocchiaro et al. 2019
showed that there exists polyhedral losses that are calibrated with respect to arbitrary discrete losses.
An interesting question is if the PERM loss framework can useful for analyzing such surrogate losses.
Beyond classification-calibration, a potentially fruit direction is to employ the PERM loss framework to study optimization and learning theory.

Appendix B
Properties of the matrix label code

As mentioned in the main text, the definition of matrix label code has already been introduced in
Wang and Scott 2020
.
However, that work, the matrix label code is used to analyze a particular optimization problem resulting from the Weston-Watkins SVM
[
Weston and Watkins 1998
]
.
Here, we develop a comprehensive theory for using matrix label code with PERM loss.

First, we note that
for
y\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
, the matrix
\bm{\Upsilon}^{(k)}_{y}
acts on
a vector
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{k-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
by

\forall j\in[k-1],\,[\bm{\Upsilon}^{(k)}_{y}\mathbf{z}]_{j}:=\begin{cases}z_{j}-z_{y}&:j\neq y\\
-z_{y}&:j=y.\end{cases}

(6)

Remark B.1
.

Throughout this section, we consider
k
fixed and write
\bm{\Upsilon}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\Upsilon}_{k}
instead of
\bm{\Upsilon}_{1}^{(k)},\cdots,\bm{\Upsilon}_{k}^{(k)}
.
Moreover, whenever
\mathbf{T}_{y}
does not have a superscript, we implicitly assume that
\mathbf{T}_{y}=\mathbf{T}_{y}^{(k)}
.
In some results, we will work with
(k-1)\times(k-1)
permutation matrices in which case we will write, for example,

\mathbf{T}_{(y,y^{\prime})}^{(k-1)}
where
y,y^{\prime}\in[k-1]
.

Lemma B.2
.

For all
y\in[k]
, we have

\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}.

In particular, for all
i>1
and
j\in[k-1]
, we have

[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{j}=\begin{cases}v_{y}-v_{j}&:y\neq j\\
v_{y}-v_{k}&:y=j.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
y=k
, then
\mathbf{T}_{y}
and
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
are both identity matrices and there is nothing to show. Otherwise, suppose that
y<k
.
Consider
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
We first calculate
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}\mathbf{v}
.
For each
j\in[k-1]
, we have

[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}\mathbf{v}]_{j}=[\mathbf{T}_{y}\mathbf{v}]_{k}-[\mathbf{T}_{y}\mathbf{v}]_{j}=v_{y}-v_{\tau_{y}(j)}=\begin{cases}v_{y}-v_{j}&:y\neq j\\
v_{y}-v_{k}&:y=j.\end{cases}

(7)

Note that the first equality follows from the definition of
\mathbf{D}
(Definition
2.1
).
Next, we compute

\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}
.
Using Eqn. (
6
), we have for each
j\in[k-1]
that

[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{j}=\begin{cases}[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{j}-[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{y}&:j\neq y\\
-[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{y}&:j=y.\end{cases}

For the
j\neq y
case, we have

[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{j}-[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{y}=(v_{k}-v_{j})-(v_{k}-v_{y})=v_{y}-v_{j}
.
For the
y=j
case, we have

-[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{y}=-(v_{k}-v_{y})=v_{y}-v_{k}
.
Thus,

[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}\mathbf{v}]_{j}=[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}]_{j}

for all
j
, which implies that

\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}\mathbf{v}=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}
. Since
\mathbf{v}
was arbitrary, we have
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}
.
∎

Remark B.3
.

Let
\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}
denote the Moore-Penrose inverse of
\mathbf{D}
.
Since
\mathbf{D}
contains a copy of the identity matrix,
\mathbf{D}
has full column rank. Hence,
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}
is the identity.

Definition B.4
.

For each
\sigma\in\mathtt{Sym}(k)
, define the
(k-1)\times(k-1)
matrix
\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}:=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}
.

Lemma B.5
.

For all
y\in[k]
, we have
\mathbf{M}_{\tau_{y}}=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
B.2
, we have
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}
which implies that

\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{T}_{y}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{M}_{\tau_{y}}
.
∎

Lemma B.6
.

If
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathtt{Sym}(k)
, then
\mathbf{S}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbf{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
y\in[k]
be arbitrary.
By definition, we have
[\mathbf{S}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{v}]_{y}=v_{\sigma(\sigma^{\prime}(y))}
.
On the other hand,

[\mathbf{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\mathbf{v})]_{y}=[\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\mathbf{v}]_{\sigma^{\prime}(y)}=[\mathbf{v}]_{\sigma(\sigma^{\prime}(y))}=v_{\sigma(\sigma^{\prime}(y))}
. Thus, we are done.
∎

Lemma B.7
.

For all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathtt{Sym}(k)
, we have
\mathbf{M}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbf{M}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbf{M}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}

by Lemma
B.6
.
Thus, it suffices to show

\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\mathbf{D}^{\dagger}
.
First, by
the definition of
\mathbf{D}

the nullspace of
\mathbf{D}
is

\ker(\mathbf{D})=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:v_{1}=v_{2}=\cdots=v_{k}\}
.
Next, let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the subspace

\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}=0\}

Appendix D
Well-incentivized losses

This section discusses the “well-incentivized” property of a loss that should be satisfied for a multiclass classification loss to be useful.
This will be made clear after
Remark
D.2
.

Definition D.1

(Well-incentivized losses)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a multiclass loss function (Definition
2.2
).
We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
well-incentivized
(resp.
strictly well-incentivized

for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and distinct

(resp.
v_{j}<v_{y}

\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{v})\geq\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathbf{v})

(resp.
\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{v})>\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathbf{v})
).

Remark D.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}
is well-incentivized. Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
y\in\argmax_{j\in[k]}v_{j}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathbf{v})=\min_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{v})
.

Remark
D.2
implies that for well-incentivized losses, the
\argmax
predictor is correct, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathbf{v})
is minimized when the class score
[\mathbf{v}]_{y}
is the highest.
Next, we define a condition on the template
\psi
such that the corresponding PERM loss is well-incentivized:

Definition D.3
.

A function

is

non-increasing
(resp.
decreasing
)
if for all
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\mathbf{v}\succeq\mathbf{w}
(resp.
\mathbf{v}\succ\mathbf{w}
)
we have

f(\mathbf{v})\leq f(\mathbf{w})
(resp.
f(\mathbf{v})<f(\mathbf{w})
).

Proposition D.4

Let

If
\nabla_{f}(\cdot)\preceq\mathbb{0}
(resp.
\nabla_{f}(\cdot)\prec\mathbb{0}

is non-increasing (resp. decreasing).

.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a PERM loss whose template
\psi
is non-increasing (resp. decreasing).
Then
\mathcal{L}
is (resp. strictly) well-incentivized.

Let

53

Appendix E
Proof of Proposition
4.6

For notational simplicity, we write
\dot{\phi}
and
\ddot{\phi}
for the first and second derivative of
\phi
and likewise for
\gamma
.
Let
\psi
be the template of
\mathcal{L}^{\gamma,\phi}
, which is given explicitly in Example
2.5
.
We first show that
\nabla\psi(\mathbf{z})\prec\mathbb{0}
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.
Equivalently, we show that

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z_{i}}(\mathbf{z})<0

for each
i\in[k-1]
.
Using the chain rule, we get

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z_{i}}(\mathbf{z})=\dot{\gamma}\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)\dot{\phi}(z_{i})

which is negative by the assumptions of Proposition
4.6
.

Next, we show that
\psi
is strictly convex. To this end, it suffices to show that the Hessian is positive definite for all input
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}

[
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
, §3.1.4]
.
We being by calculating the mixed partial derivative for
i,l\in[k-1]
:

\textstyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial z_{l}\partial z_{i}}(z)=\ddot{\gamma}\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)\dot{\phi}(z_{l})\dot{\phi}(z_{i})+\delta_{il}\dot{\gamma}\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)\ddot{\phi}(z_{i})

where

\delta_{il}=1

if
i=l
and

\delta_{il}=0

otherwise.
Abusing notation, we write

\dot{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
to denote the (column) vector

\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\phi}(z_{1})&\cdots&\dot{\phi}(z_{k-1})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and define

\ddot{\phi}(\mathbf{z})
likewise.
Thus, the Hessian of
\psi
at
\mathbf{z}
is given by

\textstyle\ddot{\gamma}\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)\dot{\phi}(\mathbf{z})\dot{\phi}(\mathbf{z})^{\top}+\dot{\gamma}\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)\mathrm{diag}(\ddot{\phi}(\mathbf{z})).

The assumption on
{\phi}
implies that

\mathrm{diag}(\ddot{\phi}(\mathbf{z}))

is a positive definite matrix.
Moreover,
\dot{\phi}(\mathbf{z})\dot{\phi}(\mathbf{z})^{\top}
is a positive definite matrix since it is the outer product of a vector with itself.
By the assumption on
\gamma
, the scalar-valued coefficients

\ddot{\gamma}\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)

and

\dot{\gamma}\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)

are nonnegative and positive, respectively.
Therefore, the Hessian of
\psi
is positive definite.

Next, we check that
\psi
is semi-coercive. Winding Definition
D.3
, this means that for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}:\psi(\mathbf{z})\leq c\}\subseteq\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}:\min_{j\in[k-1]}z_{j}\geq b\}.

(16)

Note that
\gamma
and
\phi
are injective by our assumptions in Proposition
4.6
.
Next, note that

\displaystyle\textstyle\psi(\mathbf{z})=\gamma\left(\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\right)\leq c\iff

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[k-1]}\phi(z_{j})\leq\gamma^{-1}(c)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\max\left\{\phi(z_{j}):j\in[k-1]\right\}\leq\gamma^{-1}(c)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\phi\left(\min\left\{z_{j}:j\in[k-1]\right\}\right)\leq\gamma^{-1}(c)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\phi(\min z)\leq\gamma^{-1}(c)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\min z\geq\phi^{-1}(\gamma^{-1}(c)).

If we let
\phi^{-1}(\gamma^{-1}(c))=:b
, then we get
eq.

16
.
Thus, we have proven that
\mathcal{L}^{\gamma,\phi}
is regular.
Now, by the assumption that
\phi(t)\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
, we immediately get that

\mathcal{L}^{\gamma,\phi}
is totally regular since all truncations of
\mathcal{L}^{\gamma,\phi}

are also Gamma-Phi losses with the same
\gamma,\phi
.

Semi-coercive functions

In this section, we study the properties of a PERM loss

Lemma G.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
be a PERM loss whose template
\psi
is semi-coercive. Let
\ell
be the reduced form of
\mathcal{L}
.
Then, for all
\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the set

\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}:\ell(\mathbf{z})\preceq\bm{\zeta}\}
is bounded.

Proof.

Below, let
\mathbf{z}
denote an arbitrary element of
\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.
In set-builder notations, we write
\{\mathbf{z}:\mathtt{condition}\}
to mean

\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}:\mathtt{condition}\}
.
Now, by
eq.

14
, we have that

\ell(\mathbf{z})\preceq\bm{\zeta}

if and only if

\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})\leq\zeta_{y}

for all

.
Thus, we get

\displaystyle\textstyle\{\mathbf{z}:\ell(\mathbf{z})\preceq\bm{\zeta}\}=\bigcap_{y\in[k]}\{\mathbf{z}:\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})\leq\zeta_{y}\}=\bigcap_{y\in[k]}\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\left(\{\mathbf{z}:\psi(\mathbf{z})\leq\zeta_{y}\}\right)

For the last equality above, we used the fact that
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{-1}

(Lemma

)
and that

\{\mathbf{z}:\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})\leq\zeta_{y}\}=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{-1}\left(\{\mathbf{z}:\psi(\mathbf{z})\leq\zeta_{y}\}\right)

.
Putting it all together, we have

\textstyle\{\mathbf{z}:\ell(\mathbf{z})\preceq\bm{\zeta}\}\subseteq\bigcap_{y\in[k]}\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}(\{\mathbf{z}:\min\mathbf{z}\geq b_{y}\})=:B

Thus, it suffices to show that

is bounded.
Below, we prove this.

Since the empty set is bounded, we assume below that

is nonempty.
Let
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k-1}

First, recall the infinity-norm:
\|\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}:=\max\{|z^{\prime}_{i}|:i\in[k-1]\}

To prove that

\|\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq M

Note that we can express
\|\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
alternatively as

\textstyle\|\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=\max\left\{|\max\mathbf{z}^{\prime}|,|\min\mathbf{z}^{\prime}|\right\},

(19)

where
\max\mathbf{z}^{\prime}:=\max_{j\in[k-1]}z^{\prime}_{j}
and

\min\mathbf{z}^{\prime}:=\min_{j\in[k-1]}z^{\prime}_{j}

Finally, define

We claim that

\|\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq M

First, we note that
\min\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\geq b_{k}

\bm{\Upsilon}_{k}
is the identity (Definition

From

18

Next, let

From

18
, we have
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}(\{\mathbf{z}:\min\mathbf{z}\geq b_{y}\})

.

First consider the case when

Then
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}

Next, consider the case when

Furthermore, if
\psi
is strictly convex, then
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is strictly convex for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
.

Proof.

Recall that
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{y\in[k]}p_{y}\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})
where
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
is an invertible matrix by Lemma
B.8
.
Thus, if
\psi
is (strictly) convex, then
\mathbf{z}\mapsto\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})
is (strictly) convex for each
y\in[k]
.
For each
\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{k}
,
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is a convex combination of convex function and is thus convex.
Furthermore, if
\mathbf{p}\in\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
, then
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is a convex combination of strictly convex function and is thus strictly convex.
See
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
for instance.
∎

An easy consequence of the above result is the following:

Corollary G.4
.

Let
\mathbf{p}\in\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
be arbitrary and
\mathcal{L}
be a nonnegative PERM loss whose
\psi
is semi-coercive.
If
\psi
is convex, then the infimum
\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{z})
is attained.
Furthermore, if
\psi
is strictly convex, then the infimum is attained by
a
unique
minimizer, i.e.,
\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{z})
is a singleton set.

Proof.

By Proposition
G.2
,
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is coercive.
By Proposition
G.3
,
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is strictly convex.
By the Extreme Value Theorem, a continuous and coercive function has at least one global minimum.
Furthermore, a strictly convex functions have at most one global minimum.
For a reference of these result standards, see
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
.
∎

In view of Corollary
G.4
, we define:

Definition G.5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a PERM loss whose template
\psi
is nonnegative, strictly convex and semi-coercive.
Define the
link function

\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}:\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})\to\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
by letting
\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{p})
be the unique element of
\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{z})
.

In this section, we give a sufficient condition on
\mathcal{L}
for
\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}
of Definition
G.5
to be a
bijection
.
We will need the concept of an
M-matrix
, which is reviewed in Section
K.1
.

Lemma G.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
be a regular PERM loss (Definition
4.2
) with reduced form
\ell
and template
\psi
.
For all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
, the
(k-1)\times(k-1)
matrix

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}):=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\ell_{1}}(\mathbf{z})&\cdots&\nabla_{\ell_{k-1}}(\mathbf{z})\end{bmatrix}

is a non-singular M-matrix with strictly positive diagonal elements.
Furthermore, both
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
and
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})^{\top}
are strictly monotone.

See Section
K.2

for our notations on the derivative operator
\nabla
.

The definition of a
(strictly) monotone
matrix is discussed in Section
K.1
.

Proof.

First, we compute the gradient of
\ell=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}):\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
For each
y\in[k]
, we have by definition that
\ell_{y}(\mathbf{z})=\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})
(
eq.

14
).
Thus, by the chain rule, we have

\nabla_{\ell_{y}}(\mathbf{z})=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\nabla_{\psi}(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z}).

(20)

Next, fix
y\in[k-1]
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.
Let
\mathbf{w}:=\nabla_{\psi}(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})
.
Then by the assumption that
\nabla_{\psi}(\cdot)\prec\mathbb{0}
, we have
\mathbf{w}
is a entrywise negative column vector, i.e.,
\mathbf{w}\prec\mathbb{0}
.
Moreover, by

eq.

20
we have

\nabla_{\ell_{y}}(\mathbf{z})=\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}
.
Thus, for each
j\in[k-1]
, we have
[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}]_{j}=([\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}]_{:j})^{\top}\mathbf{w}
.
Recall from
Definition
2.8
that

[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}]_{:j}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{e}_{j}^{(k-1)}&:j\neq y\\
-\mathbb{1}^{(k-1)}&:j=y\end{cases},\quad\mbox{ which implies that }\quad[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}]_{j}=\begin{cases}w_{j}&:j\neq y\\
-\sum_{l\in[k-1]}w_{l}&:j=y.\end{cases}

In particular,
[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}]_{j}\leq 0
for all
j\neq y
which proves that
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is a Z-matrix.
Furthermore, note that the fact
\mathbf{w}<0
and
[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}]_{y}=-\sum_{l\in[k-1]}w_{l}
implies that the diagonals of
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
are positive.
Observe that
\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}
has the property that

\textstyle|[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}]_{y}|=-\sum_{l\in[k-1]}w_{l}>-\sum_{l\in[k-1]:l\neq y}w_{l}=\sum_{l\in[k-1]:l\neq y}|[\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{w}]_{l}|.

Note that the strict inequality above follows from the fact that
\mathbf{w}\prec\mathbb{0}
and so in particular
-w_{y}>0
.
This proves that
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is strictly diagonally dominant.
By Corollary
K.3
, we have that
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is a non-singular M-matrix.
For the “Furthermore” part, we can apply Lemma
K.5
since the diagonal elements of
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
are positive.
By Corollary
K.6
,
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})^{\top}
is also strictly monotone.
∎

Lemma G.7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
be a regular PERM loss.
Let
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{k}
be arbitrary. Then
\mathbf{z}
minimizes
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
if and only if

-p_{k}\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&\cdots&p_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

(21)

where
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is as in Lemma
G.6
.
Furthermore,
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
has a minimizer, then
\mathbf{p}\in\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
.

Proof.

Proposition
G.3
asserts that
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
is convex.
For a differentiable convex function, recall that the gradient-vanishing condition is necessary and sufficient for optimality of unconstrained optimization
[
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
]
.
Thus,
\mathbf{z}
minimizes
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}

iff

\textstyle\mathbb{0}=\nabla_{C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}}(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{j\in[k]}p_{j}\nabla_{\ell_{j}}(\mathbf{z})=p_{k}\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&\cdots&p_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

(22)

which is equivalent to

eq.

21
.

Next, for the “Furthermore” part, first note that
Lemma
G.6
says
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is a non-singular M-matrix.
Now, we first show that
p_{k}\neq 0
. If
p_{k}=0
, then Equation (
21
)
reduces to
\mathbb{0}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&\cdots&p_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Since
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is non-singular, we must have
p_{1}=\cdots=p_{k-1}=0
as well which implies that
\mathbf{p}=\mathbb{0}
. But this contradicts that
\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{k}
.
Thus,
p_{k}>0
and so
-p_{k}\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\succ 0
.
From Lemma
G.6
, we have that
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is strictly monotone.
Combined with

eq.

21
, we can conclude that
p_{y}>0
for each
y\in[k-1]
as well.
∎

The “Furthermore” part of
Lemma
G.7

immediately implies the following.

Corollary G.8
.

If
\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{k}\setminus\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
, then
\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{z})=\emptyset
.

Proposition G.9
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular PERM loss.
Then
\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}
(Definition
G.5
) is a bijection.

Proof.

First, we prove that
\mathsf{link}
is injective. Suppose that
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
are such that
\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{q})=:\mathbf{z}
.
Then by

eq.

21
, we have that

-\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})^{-1}=p_{k}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&\cdots&p_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}=q_{k}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}&\cdots&q_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

(23)

Thus,
(1-p_{k})/p_{k}=(p_{1}+\cdots+p_{k-1})/p_{k}=(q_{1}+\cdots+q_{k-1})/q_{k}=(1-q_{k})/q_{k}
implies that
p_{k}=q_{k}
.
Therefore,
eq.

23
implies that
p_{y}=q_{y}
for each
y\in[k-1]
as well.
Thus,
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
which proves that
\mathsf{link}
is injective.

Next, we prove that
\mathsf{link}
is surjective. Pick
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.
From Lemma
G.6
, we have that
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is non-singular and strictly monotone.
By non-singular-ness, there exists
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
such that
-\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\mathbf{v}
.
Furthermore, since
-\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\succ 0
and
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is strictly monotone, we have
\mathbf{v}\succ 0
.
Define
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
by
p_{k}:=(v_{1}+\dots+v_{k-1}+1)^{-1}
and

p_{y}:=v_{y}p_{k}

for each
y\in[k-1]
.
Clearly, we have
\mathbf{p}\succ 0
. Furthermore,

p_{1}+p_{2}+\dots+p_{k}=p_{k}(v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k-1}+1)=1.

Thus, we have
\mathbf{p}\in\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
.
By construction,
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{p}
satisfy
eq.

21
. Thus
\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{z}
follows from the definition of
\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}
.
∎

Remark G.10
.

Before proceeding, we remark that Proposition
G.9
gives theoretical support to the conjectural observation in
Nowak-Vila et al. 2019
regarding the injectivity of the
link function
.

G.3
Geometry of the loss surface

Recall from Definition
3.1
and
Theorem
3.3
that
the classification-calibration of the
set

\mathsf{ran}_{\mathtt{c}}(\mathcal{L})
implies the
classification-calibration of the
loss

\mathcal{L}
.
In general, the set
\mathsf{ran}_{\mathtt{c}}(\mathcal{L})
may be difficult to compute.
In this section, we study the geometry of the set
\mathsf{ran}(\mathcal{L})
when
\mathcal{L}
is a regular PERM loss which enables us to compute the convex hull
\mathsf{ran}_{\mathtt{c}}(\mathcal{L})
of
\mathsf{ran}(\mathcal{L})
.
One of the main tools is the mapping defined below:

Corollary G.11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular PERM loss with reduced form
\ell
.
Then
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is injective.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
are such that
\ell(\mathbf{z})=\ell(\mathbf{w})
.
By Proposition
G.9
, there exists
\mathbf{p}\in\mathsf{int}(\Delta^{k})
such that
\mathsf{link}^{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{z}
.
Now,
\langle\mathbf{p},\ell(\mathbf{z})\rangle=\langle\mathbf{p},\ell(\mathbf{w})\rangle
implies that both
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}
minimize
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
.
By Corollary
G.4
, we have
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}
and so
\ell
is injective.
∎

Definition G.12
.

Given a PERM loss
\mathcal{L}
with reduced form
\ell
, we define two functions
F
and
G
mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
by

F(\mathbf{z},\lambda)=\ell(\mathbf{z})+\lambda\mathbb{1}
and
G(\mathbf{z},t)=\ell(\mathbf{z})+t\mathbb{e}^{(k)}_{k}
, where
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
For computing
\nabla_{F}
,
we view the tuple
\mathbf{z},\lambda
as a (column) vector

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}^{\top}&\lambda\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Hence,

\nabla_{F}(\mathbf{z},\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})^{\top}&\mathbb{1}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Likewise for the tuple
\mathbf{z},t
and
\nabla_{G}
.

Remark G.13
.

In the context of Definition
G.12
, it is perhaps more precise to write
F(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}^{\top}&\lambda\end{bmatrix}^{\top})
. However, this notation is cumbersome. Below, we will always use the tuple notation.
The reason we discuss the vector notation is so that
\nabla F
and
\nabla G
can be more simply treated as gradients of vector input-valued functions (rather than matrix input-valued).

Below, we will study the properties of the two functions from Definition
G.12
.

G.3.1
Properties of the
F
function

The main result of this section is to prove that
F
is a homeomorphism
from
\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
(Corollary
G.19
).

Lemma G.14
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular PERM loss with reduced form
\ell
and
F
be as in Definition
G.12
. Then
F
is injective. In other words,
if
\ell(\mathbf{z})+\lambda\mathbb{1}=\ell(\mathbf{w})+\mu\mathbb{1}
for some
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, then both
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}
and
\lambda=\mu
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
be as in the statement of the lemma.
Our goal is to show that both
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}
and
\lambda=\mu
.
First, consider the case that
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}
. Then
\ell(\mathbf{z})=\ell(\mathbf{w})
and so
\lambda=\mu
.
Thus,
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}
implies
\lambda=\mu
.

Next, consider the case that
\lambda=\mu
. Then we have
\ell(\mathbf{z})=\ell(\mathbf{w})
.
By Corollary
G.11
, we have
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}
.
Therefore,
\lambda=\mu
implies
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}
.

Thus, it only remains to show that if both
\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{w}
and
\lambda\neq\mu
, then we have a contradiction.
Without loss of generality, suppose that
\lambda>\mu
.
Then we have
\ell(\mathbf{z})+(\lambda-\mu)\mathbb{1}=\ell(\mathbf{w})
.
Thus, for all
\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{k}
, we have

\langle\mathbf{p},\ell(\mathbf{w})\rangle=\langle\mathbf{p},\ell(\mathbf{z})+(\lambda-\mu)\mathbb{1}\rangle>\langle\mathbf{p},\ell(\mathbf{z})\rangle.

Thus, there does not exist
\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{k}
such that
\mathbf{w}
is the minimizer of
C^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
.
But this contradicts since
Proposition
G.9
implies that

\mathsf{link}
is surjective.
∎

Lemma G.15
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular PERM loss with reduced form
\ell
. Let
F
be as in Definition
G.12
.
Then for all
(\mathbf{z},\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\times\mathbb{R}
,
\nabla_{F}(\mathbf{z},\lambda)
is non-singular.

Proof.

Recall that

\nabla_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{(k-1)\times k}

and

\mathbb{1}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Thus,

\nabla_{F}(\mathbf{z},\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})^{\top}&(\mathbb{1}^{(k)})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
is a
k\times k
square matrix.
To show that it is non-singular, first pick
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
arbitrary.
It suffices to check that if
\nabla_{F}(\mathbf{z},\lambda)\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{0}
then
\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{0}
.

Towards this, first note that
\nabla_{F}(\mathbf{z},\lambda)\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{0}
can be equivalently stated as
both

\nabla_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{v}=0
and

(\mathbb{1}^{(k)})^{\top}\mathbf{v}=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}=0
.
Replacing
\mathbf{v}
by
-\mathbf{v}
if necessary, we assume that
v_{k}\geq 0
.
Recall
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
from
Lemma
G.6
.
Then the identity
\nabla_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{0}
can be rewritten as

-v_{k}\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&\cdots&v_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Since
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z})
is monotone and
-v_{k}\nabla_{\psi}(\mathbf{z})\succeq 0
, we get that
v_{y}\geq 0
for each
y\in[k-1]
.
Combined with the fact that

\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbb{1}=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}=0
, we have that
\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{0}
, as desired.
∎

Now, by applying the inverse function theorem (Theorem
K.8
), we immediately have the following.

Corollary G.16
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular PERM loss with reduced form
\ell
. Let
F
be as in Definition
G.12
.
For all
(\mathbf{z},\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, there exist open neighborhoods
U\ni(\mathbf{z},\lambda)
and
V\ni F(\mathbf{z},\lambda)
such that
F|_{U}:U\to V
is a diffeomorphism.

Proposition G.17
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular PERM loss with reduced form
\ell
. Let
F
be as in Definition
G.12
.
The map
F
is a bijection.

Proof.

Lemma
G.14
shows that
F
is injective.
To show that
F
is surjective, we prove that
\mathsf{ran}(F)
is both open and closed as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
This would imply that
\mathsf{ran}(F)=\mathbb{R}^{k}

since the only subets of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
that are both open and closed are
\emptyset
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Now, Lemma
G.16
shows that
\mathsf{ran}(F)
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
It remains to prove that
\mathsf{ran}(F)
is closed.

To this end, consider a sequence
\{(\mathbf{z}^{(i)},\lambda^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that
F(\mathbf{z}^{(i)},\lambda^{(i)})=\ell(\mathbf{z}^{(i)})+\lambda^{(i)}\mathbb{1}
converges to
\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Our goal is to show that
\bm{\zeta}\in\mathsf{ran}(F)
.

We begin by first picking
\epsilon>0
.
Let
\mathbb{1}:=\mathbb{1}^{(k)}
(without the superscript
(k)
) denotes the
k
-dimensional vector of all ones. Since the sequence converges, there exists
M
such that

\bm{\zeta}-\epsilon\mathbb{1}\preceq\ell(\mathbf{z}^{(i)})+\lambda^{(i)}\mathbb{1}\preceq\bm{\zeta}+\epsilon\mathbb{1}

for all
i\geq M
. Before proceeding, we prove a helper lemma.

Lemma G.18

(Helper lemma)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a PERM loss with reduced form
\ell
and template
\psi
such that
\nabla_{\psi}(\cdot)\preceq 0
. Then for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
, we have that

\min_{y\in[k-1]}\ell_{y}(\mathbf{z})\leq\psi(\mathbb{0}^{(k-1)})=:C
, where
\mathbb{0}^{(k-1)}
is the
(k-1)
-dimensional all-zeros vector.

Proof of helper lemma.

Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be such that
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{z}
, where we recall from Definition
2.1
that
\mathbf{D}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{I}_{k-1}&\mathbb{1}^{(k-1)}\end{bmatrix}
. As in Remark
C.4
, we can take
\mathbf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{z}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Next, let
y\in\argmax_{j\in[k-1]}v_{j}
.
Recall from Section
A
that
\min(\cdot)
over vector-valued inputs denotes entrywise minimum.
Then by Remark
D.2
and Proposition
D.5
,
we have that
\min(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{z}))=\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathbf{v})
.
Next by
eq.

14
from Remark
C.4
, we have

[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})]_{y}=\ell_{y}(\mathbf{z}).

Let
\mathbf{w}:=\sigma_{y}(\mathbf{v})
.
(Recall from Section
A
that
\sigma_{y}\in\mathtt{Sym}(k)
is the transposition that swaps
k
and
y
.)
Note that by construction we have
k\in\argmax\mathbf{w}
.
By permutation-equivariance, we have

[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})]_{y}=[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v})]_{\sigma_{y}(k)}=[\mathcal{L}(\sigma_{y}(\mathbf{v}))]_{k}=[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w})]_{k}.

Again by
eq.

14
from Remark
C.4
, we have

[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w})]_{k}=\psi(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w})
.

Since
k\in\argmax\mathbf{w}
, we have that
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}_{\geq 0}
belongs to the non-negative orthtant.
In other words,
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbb{0}^{(k-1)}
.
Finally, since
\nabla_{\psi}(\cdot)\preceq 0
, we have that
\psi(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w})\leq\psi(\mathbb{0}^{(k-1)})
.
∎

We now return to the proof of the proposition.
Let
C
be as in the helper lemma.
Then

\min(\ell(\mathbf{z}^{(i)})+\lambda^{(i)}\mathbb{1})=\min(\ell(\mathbf{z}^{(i)}))+\lambda^{(i)}\leq C+\lambda^{(i)}.

Thus, we have

\min(\bm{\zeta})-\epsilon\leq C+\lambda^{(i)}

and which implies that
-\lambda^{(i)}\leq C+\epsilon-\min(\bm{\zeta})=:D
.
From this, we get that

\ell(\mathbf{z}^{(i)})\preceq\bm{\zeta}+\epsilon\mathbb{1}-\lambda^{(i)}\mathbb{1}\preceq\bm{\zeta}+(\epsilon+D)\mathbb{1}

Thus, for all
i\geq M
we have
\mathbf{z}^{(i)}\in\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}:\ell(\mathbf{z})\preceq\bm{\zeta}+(\epsilon+D)\mathbb{1}\}

which is a bounded set by
Lemma
G.1
.
By passing to a subsequence, we may assume that
\mathbf{z}^{(i)}
converges to some
\mathbf{z}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.
Thus, we have

\lambda^{(i)}\mathbb{1}
converges to
\ell(\mathbf{z}^{*})+\bm{\zeta}
, which implies in particular that

Proposition I.6
.

Let
f:D\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be of Legendre type and
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex, differentiable and strictly increasing.
Let
C=\mathrm{int}(D)
.
Suppose that
D
is compact and there exists
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in C
such that
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in D}f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}^{*})
.
Then
g\circ f:D\to\mathbb{R}
is of Legendre type.

Proof.

We check that the items of Definition
5.6
hold.
Item
1
clearly holds since
f
and
g\circ f
have the same domain.

Now for Item
2
, note that
g\circ f
is differentiable by the Chain Rule. Thus it remains to show that
g\circ f
is strictly convex.
For all
\mathbf{x},y\in D
such that
\mathbf{x}\neq y
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

f(\lambda\mathbf{x}+(1-\lambda)y)<\lambda f(\mathbf{x})+(1-\lambda)f(y).

This is due to
f
being strictly convex.
Next, since
g
is strictly increasing, we have

g(f(\lambda\mathbf{x}+(1-\lambda)y))<g(\lambda f(\mathbf{x})+(1-\lambda)f(y))

By the convexity of
g
, we have

g(\lambda f(\mathbf{x})+(1-\lambda)f(y))\leq\lambda g(f(\mathbf{x}))+(1-\lambda)g(f(y)))

which shows that
g\circ f
is strictly convex.

For Item
3
, we check that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\|\nabla_{g\circ f}(\mathbf{x}^{i})\|=+\infty
for all sequences
\{\mathbf{x}^{i}\}\subseteq C
such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\partial D
.
To this end, we first prove the claim that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for all sequences
\{\mathbf{x}^{i}\}\subseteq C
with
\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\partial D
we have
\lim_{i\to\infty}f(\mathbf{x}^{i})\geq\epsilon
.
Since
f
is convex on
D
, we know that
f
is continuous on
D
. This is
Rockafellar 1970
.
In particular,
f
is continuous on
\partial D=D\setminus C
as well. Since
\partial D
is compact, we have
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\partial D}f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}^{\dagger})
for some
\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}\in\partial D
.
Since
\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}\neq\mathbf{x}^{*}
, we must have
f(\mathbf{x}^{\dagger})\neq f(\mathbf{x}^{*})
by the strict convexity of
f
.
In particular,
f(\mathbf{x}^{\dagger})>f(\mathbf{x}^{*})
. Now, letting
\epsilon=f(\mathbf{x}^{\dagger})
, the claim follows.

Next we prove that
g^{\prime}(\epsilon)>0
.
Since
g
is increasing, we have
g\geq 0
.
We proceed by considering the two cases
g^{\prime}(0)>0
and
g^{\prime}(0)=0
separately.
In the first case, the convexity of
g
implies that
g^{\prime}
is non-decreasing and so
g^{\prime}(\epsilon)>0
holds.
In the second case, if
g^{\prime}(\epsilon)=0
, then we must have
g^{\prime}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,\epsilon]
.
But this implies that
g
is constant on
[0,\epsilon]
which contradicts that
g
is strictly increasing.
Thus,
g^{\prime}(\epsilon)>0
.

Finally, by the Chain Rule, we have
\nabla_{g\circ f}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{g}(f(\mathbf{x}))\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})=g^{\prime}(f(\mathbf{x}))\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})
.
Thus,

\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla_{g\circ f}(\mathbf{x}^{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}g^{\prime}(f(\mathbf{x}^{i}))\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{i})\geq g^{\prime}(\epsilon)\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{i}).

Since
g^{\prime}(\epsilon)>0
and does not depend on
i
, we have
\lim_{i\to\infty}\|\nabla_{g\circ f}(\mathbf{x}^{i})\|=+\infty
, as desired.
∎

Lemma J.2

\psi

\psi(t\mathbf{a})=\psi(t\mathbb{e}_{1})

\psi(\mathbf{a})=\psi(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}a_{j}\mathbb{e}_{j})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}a_{j}\psi(\mathbb{e}_{j})=\psi(\mathbb{e}_{1})

To prove the claim, let
\mathbf{b}:=\mathbf{A}\mathbb{1}
and suppose that
\mathbf{b}
has a zero entry at
j\in[k-1]
, i.e.,
b_{j}=0
.
Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary number and
let
\mathbf{z}:=t\mathbb{1}
in the assumption of Lemma
J.3
.
Then we have by this assumption that

\psi(t\mathbb{1})=\psi(t\mathbf{A}\mathbb{1})=\psi(t\mathbf{b})
.
Note that

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\psi(t\mathbb{1})=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\log(1+\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\exp(-t)})=\log(1)=0.

On the other hand

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\psi(t\mathbf{b})\geq\lim_{t\to+\infty}\log(1+\exp(-tb_{j}))=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\log(1+1)>0.

This is a contradiction and proves that
\mathbf{A}\mathbb{1}=\mathbb{1}
.
∎

Lemma J.4
.

Let
\psi
be the template of the multinomial logistic loss (Example
2.4
).
Let
j\in[k]
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k-1)\times(k-1)}
be arbitrary. Suppose that

\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\mathbf{z})=\psi(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.
Then
\mathbf{A}
is equal to the
\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}
up to row permutation.
In other words, there exists
\sigma\in\mathtt{Sym}(k)
such that
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}
.

Proof of Lemma
J.4
.

We first prove that the assumption of Lemma
J.4

implies that of the previous Lemma
J.3
:

\psi(\mathbf{z})=\psi(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
.
Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
be arbitrary and let
\mathbf{z}:=\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\mathbf{u}
.
Since
\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}^{2}=\mathbf{I}
, we have

\psi(\mathbf{u})=\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\mathbf{u})=\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\mathbf{z})=\psi(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})=\psi(\mathbf{A}\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\mathbf{u})

where for the third equality from the left we used the assumption of Lemma
J.4
.

Now, by the previous Lemma
J.3
, we have that
\mathbf{A}\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}=\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\in\mathtt{Sym}(k)
.
Using
\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}^{2}=\mathbf{I}
, we get

\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}=\mathbf{A}\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}=\mathbf{A}
. That is to say
\mathbf{A}
is equal to
\bm{\Upsilon}_{j}
up to permutations of the rows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
J.1
.

Let
y\in[k]
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be fixed. Let
\mathbf{z}:=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}
.
By Theorem
2.9
, we have

\psi(\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}\mathbf{z})=\psi(\mathbf{A}_{y}\mathbf{z})
. Since
\mathbf{D}
is surjective, the preceding identity holds for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
. Thus
by Lemma
J.4
,
\mathbf{A}_{y}=\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}\bm{\Upsilon}_{y}
for some
\sigma\in\mathtt{Sym}(k)
.
∎

Appendix K
Mathematical Background

We review mathematical background on fundamental topics that are used throughout this work.

K.1
Non-singular M-matrix

We recall some definitions from linear algebra.

Definition K.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a matrix.
We say that
\mathbf{A}
is a

1.

Z-matrix
if
a_{ij}\leq 0
whenever
i\neq j
.

2.

M-matrix
if
\mathbf{A}
is a Z-matrix and all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
have nonnegative real parts.

3.

strictly diagonally dominant matrix
if

|a_{ii}|>\sum_{j\in[n]:j\neq i}|a_{ij}|
for all
i\in[n]
.

4.

monotone matrix
if for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\succeq 0
implies
\mathbf{x}\succeq 0
.
If, in addition,
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\succ 0
implies
\mathbf{x}\succ 0
, then
\mathbf{A}
is said to be
strictly monotone
.

Theorem K.2

(Levy-Desplanques/Gershgorin circle)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a strictly diagonally dominant matrix. Then

\mathbf{A}
is non-singular whose
eigenvalues all have nonnegative real parts.

The above result immediately implies the following:

Corollary K.3
.

A strictly diagonally dominant Z-matrix is a non-singular M-matrix.

Non-singular M-matrices have many equivalent characterizations. The one relevant to us is the following:

Theorem K.4

(
Plemmons 1977
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Z-matrix.
Then
\mathbf{A}
is a non-singular M-matrix if and only
\mathbf{A}
is a monotone matrix.

Lemma K.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a non-singular M-matrix. If the diagonals of
\mathbf{A}
are positive, then
\mathbf{A}
is strictly monotone.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be arbitrary such that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbb{0}
.
Our goal is to show that
\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbb{0}
.
First, from Theorem
K.4
, we have that
\mathbf{A}
is monotone.
Thus,
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbb{0}
implies
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbb{0}
.
We only have to check additionally that
\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbb{0}
.
Since
\mathbf{A}
is a Z-matrix, the off-diagonals are non-positive, i.e.,
a_{ij}\leq 0
for all
i,j\in[n]
such that
i\neq j
.
Now, let
i\in[n]
. We need to check that
x_{i}>0
.
To this end, note that

0<[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}]_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}x_{j}=a_{ii}x_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}x_{j}\leq a_{ii}x_{i}.

Note that
\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}x_{j}\leq 0
because
x\succeq\mathbb{0}
and
a_{ij}\leq 0
.
Finally,
x_{i}>0
since
a_{ii}>0
.
∎

Corollary K.6
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a non-singular M-matrix. Then
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
is also a non-singular M-matrix. Moreover, if the diagonals of
\mathbf{A}
are positive, then
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
is strictly monotone.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
are both non-singular and have the same set of eigenvalues. Moreover,
\mathbf{A}
being a Z-matrix implies that
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
is a Z-matrix.
Thus,
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
is a non-singular M-matrix.
Finally, Lemma
K.5
implies that
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
is a strictly monotone matrix.
∎

K.2
Vector calculus

Let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a differentiable function.
Below, let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote a generic input variable of
f
. Let
i\in[n]
denote the index of the dimensions of
\mathbf{x}
, and let
j\in[m]
denote the index of the component functions of
f
.
The
gradient
of
f
at
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted
\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})
, is the
n\times m
matrix
whose
(i,j)
-th entry

[\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})]_{ij}:=\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})
.
Note that we can write the above as

\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{f_{1}}(\mathbf{x})&\cdots&\nabla_{f_{m}}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}.

If
f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
for a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, then
\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}
.
The transpose of the gradient (matrix) is referred to as the
Jacobian matrix
.
Below, we cite results from
Munkres 2018
regarding the Jacobian matrix.
The two results below are Ch2.-Theorem 7.1 and Ch.2-Theorem 8.2 from
Munkres 2018
, respectively, restated in terms of the gradient.

Theorem K.7

(Chain rule)
.

If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
are differentiable, then
we have
\nabla_{g\circ f}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{g}(f(\mathbf{x}))
.

A special case of the chain rule is that
if
f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
for a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
then

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}g(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\nabla_{g}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})
.

Theorem K.8

(Inverse function theorem)
.

Let
U
be open in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be
r
-times continuously differentiable and
V=f(U)
.
If
f
is one-to-one on
U
and if
\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})
is non-singular for all
\mathbf{x}\in U
, then
V
is open in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the inverse function
g:V\to U
is
r
-times continuously differentiable.

The following is an immediate consequence of Theorems
K.8
and
K.7
:

Corollary K.9
.

In the setting of Theorem
K.8
, we have

\nabla_{f^{-1}}(f(\mathbf{x}))=\nabla_{f}(\mathbf{x})^{-1}.

Remark K.10

(Hessians)
.

If
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a scalar-valued functions of a vector-valued input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the gradient
\nabla_{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector-valued function.
In this case, it makes sense to write
\nabla_{\nabla_{\varphi}}(\mathbf{x})
which is a
n\times n
matrix. Indeed, this is simply the
Hessian
of
\varphi
at
\mathbf{x}
which we denote by
\nabla^{2}_{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})
, following the standard convention.

Remark K.11

(Time derivatives)
.

For
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
differentiable functions of a univariate “time” variable
t\in\mathbb{R}
, note that
\nabla_{f}(t)
is a
1\times m
matrix, i.e., a
row
vector of the time derivatives.
It is convenient to adopt the notation
\frac{df}{dt}(t)=f^{\prime}(t):=\nabla_{f}(t)^{\top}
to denote the time derivatives as a
column
vector.
In this notation, the
product rule
for the time derivative of the inner product of two functions
f
and
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
can be stated as

\tfrac{d}{dt}(f(t)^{\top}g(t))=f^{\prime}(t)^{\top}g(t)+f(t)^{\top}g^{\prime}(t).

(52)

Equation

52
follows immediately from the ordinary product rule from elementary calculus.
If
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a differentiable function,
then the
chain rule
for computing the time derivative of the composition
h\circ g
can be stated as

\tfrac{d}{dt}(h(g(t)))=\nabla_{h}(g(t))^{\top}g^{\prime}(t).

(53)

To see this, note that by definition, we have

\tfrac{d}{dt}(h(g(t)))=\nabla_{h\circ g}(t)^{\top}
. Now, by Theorem
K.7

\nabla_{h\circ g}(t)=\nabla_{g}(t)\nabla_{h}(g(t))=g^{\prime}(t)^{\top}\nabla_{h}(g(t))
.
Taking transpose, we get

eq.

53
.
