Proof of Hofer-Wysocki-Zehnder’s two or infinity conjecture
Dan Cristofaro-Gardiner, Umberto Hryniewicz, Michael Hutchings, Hui Liu

Abstract
. We prove that if a Reeb flow on a closed connected three-manifold has more than two simple periodic orbits, then it has infinitely many, as long as the associated contact structure has torsion first Chern class. As a special case, we prove a conjecture of Hofer-Wysocki-Zehnder published in 2003 asserting that a smooth and autonomous Hamiltonian flow on ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} has either two or infinitely many simple periodic orbits on any regular compact connected energy level that is transverse to the radial vector field. Other corollaries settle some old problems about Finsler metrics: we show that every Finsler metric on S 2 S^{2} has either two or infinitely many prime closed geodesics; and we show that a Finsler metric on S 2 S^{2} with at least one closed geodesic that is not irrationally elliptic must have infinitely many prime closed geodesics. The novelty of our work is that we do not make any nondegeneracy hypotheses.

Introduction

1.1.
The main result and corollaries

Let
Y
be a closed oriented three-manifold. A

on

is a
1

\lambda\wedge d\lambda>0

d\lambda(R,\cdot)=0,\quad\quad\lambda(R)=1.

The flow of

A periodic orbit of the Reeb flow, which we call a

\gamma^{\prime}(t)=R(\gamma(t))

\gamma

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

be a contact form on

be the associated contact structure. Assume that the first Chern class

has either two or infinitely many simple Reeb orbits.

1.2.
Some history and related results

The three-dimensional case of the Weinstein conjecture
[
52
]
asserts that every contact form on a closed three-manifold has at least one Reeb orbit. This was proved for star-shaped hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
using variational methods by Rabinowitz
[
45
]
, who also proved a higher dimensional analogue in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. The Weinstein Conjecture was proved for all contact forms on
S^{3}
, and for all overtwisted contact forms on any closed three-manifold, in work of Hofer
[
20
]
where the foundations of holomorphic curve theory in symplectizations were first introduced and developed. The Weinstein conjecture was established for all closed three-manifolds by Taubes
[
49
]
using Seiberg-Witten theory; see
[
32
]
for a survey. Taubes subsequently generalized this work to prove an isomorphism between embedded contact homology (ECH) and Seiberg-Witten Floer cohomology reviewed in §
2.3
below. This isomorphism, together with a “Weyl law” for the ECH spectrum reviewed in §
2.5
below, was used in
[
9
]
to show that in fact, every contact form on a closed three-manifold has at least two simple Reeb orbits. It was proved in
[
17
]
that the symplectic field theory of Eliashberg-Givental-Hofer
[
13
]
can be combined with arguments of Hingston
[
19
]
and Ginzburg
[
16
]
to prove that the Reeb flow has at least two simple closed orbits in the special case of star-shaped hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Prior to these works, it was shown by Bangert and Long
[
4
]
that there are at least two prime closed geodesics for any Finsler metric on
S^{2}
. A detailed description of the dynamics when there are exactly two simple Reeb orbits was given in
[
8
]
, using a result from
[
37
]
on the nondegenerate case. Such examples are quite special; among other things, the three-manifold must be diffeomorphic to
S^{3}
or a lens space. In fact it was shown by Irie
[
39
]
that for a
C^{\infty}
generic contact form on a closed three-manifold
Y
, not only are there infinitely many simple Reeb orbits, but moreover the union of the images of the Reeb orbits is dense in
Y
.

The two or infinity dichotomy in Corollary
1.3
was proved for the special case of convex hypersurfaces by Hofer-Wysocki-Zehnder
[
24
]
. The main ingredient in the proof is to show that in this case there is always a disk-like global surface of section. This perspective is in fact central to our work, as the notion of a global surface of section, or more precisely the slightly more general notion of a Birkhoff section (see §
2.8
for the definition), plays a key role in our arguments. As mentioned above, in
[
25
]
, Hofer-Wysocki-Zehnder proved the conclusion of Corollary
1.3
for star-shaped hypersurfaces under the additional hypothesis that the contact form is nondegenerate and the stable and unstable manifolds of all hyperbolic Reeb orbits intersect transversely. This work introduced the theory of finite energy foliations, which generalizes Birkhoff sections.

As mentioned in Remark
1.2
,
Colin-Dehornoy-Rechtman
[
7
]
proved that there are two or infinitely many simple Reeb orbits for any nondegenerate contact form on a closed connected three-manifold. This is a consequence of a general existence result for “broken book decompositions”. The latter are analogues of finite-energy foliations, and were used in
[
7
]
to establish deep structural results for Reeb flows of nondegenerate contact forms on closed three-manifolds.

1.3.
Idea of the proof and organization of the paper

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is as follows. Suppose that there are only finitely many simple Reeb orbits. Following the strategy in
[
10
]
, we would like to use holomorphic curves to find a genus zero Birkhoff section for the Reeb flow. Roughly speaking, this is an open surface
\Sigma\subset Y
such that the simple Reeb orbits correspond to
ends
of
\Sigma
, together with simple periodic orbits of an area-preserving return map
\varphi:\Sigma\to\Sigma
. If such a genus zero Birkhoff section can be found, then one can apply a theorem of Franks
[
14
]
to deduce that there are either two or infinitely many simple Reeb orbits.

The difficulty with this approach is to deal with the case when the contact form
\lambda
is degenerate. Much of the theory of holomorphic curves that we would want to use, including embedded contact homology (ECH), is only defined for nondegenerate contact forms. To get started, we can find a sequence of nondegenerate contact forms
\lambda_{k}
converging to
\lambda
. The idea is then to find
Birkhoff sections for
\lambda_{k}
, via projections of holomorphic curves
C_{k}
, that can be taken to converge to a
Birkhoff section for
\lambda
. A priori, there are two significant challenges with this: i) in general Birkhoff sections need not converge; ii) the arguments in
[
10
]
used the assumption of finitely many orbits, and one can not assume this for the
\lambda_{k}
.

To attempt to get around the first point, we develop some criteria in terms of the
C_{k}
in order to guarantee convergence; a crucial role is played by the speed of convergence of the ends of the
C_{k}
. The difficulty, then, is finding such
C_{k}
. Fortuitously, it turns out if the
C_{k}
are cylinders of low enough action, detected by the ECH “
U
-map”, then the desired conditions hold as a consequence of the ECH “partition conditions”. Thus, we return to point ii), with an additional constraint that the Birkhoff section should be an annulus; we also need a uniform upper bound on the periods of the boundary Reeb orbits.
All of this requires lengthy
arguments involving details of the holomorphic curves arising from ECH, and a new invariant called “the score” of a curve is introduced. These arguments appear in Section 3, and the arguments for taking the
C_{k}
to the limit appear in Sections 4, 5 and 6; the arguments Section 4 - 6 also require considerable
technical
work to deal with holomorphic curves asymptotic to possibly degenerate Reeb orbits.

In fact, in the end, we do not quite prove that the annular Birkhoff sections for
\lambda_{k}
can be taken to converge to an annular Birkhoff section for
\lambda
. This may be true, but we prove a slightly weaker statement which is sufficient for our purposes. Namely, the limits of the boundary Reeb orbits of the Birkhoff sections give a two-component link
\mathcal{L}
in
Y
whose components are simple Reeb orbits of
\lambda
. In addition, there is a moduli space
\mathcal{M}
consisting of holomorphic cylinders with one marked point, and a covering map
\mathcal{M}\to Y\setminus\mathcal{L}
. Finally, the Reeb flow on
Y\setminus\mathcal{L}
lifts to a flow on
\mathcal{M}
to which Franks’s theorem can be applied to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.4.
Some open questions and a small step

It remains an open question whether the
torsion hypothesis can be removed from Theorem
1.1
. By
[
8
, Cor. 1.3]
, this is equivalent to the question of whether every contact form on a closed three-manifold that is not a lens space must have infinitely many simple Reeb orbits.

In the cases where there are countably infinitely many simple Reeb orbits, one can ask for lower bounds on the growth rate of the number of simple Reeb orbits with period
\leq T
as
T\to\infty
. Our methods allow a small step in this direction. Enumerate the simple Reeb orbits as
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots
such that the period of
\gamma_{i}
is a nondecreasing function of
i
. Then it follows from Proposition
3.4
below that for a torsion contact form on a three-manifold other than
S^{3}
or a lens space, we obtain an upper bound (possibly very large) on the period of
\gamma_{n+1}
in terms of data associated to
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
.

When

2.2.
Holomorphic currents and the index inequality

Definition 2.3
.

An almost complex structure

•

Given a

, §3.4]
:

Proposition 2.5
.

If

is somewhere injective, then

(2.5)

\operatorname{ind}(C)\leq I(C),

with equality only if

is embedded in
\mathbb{R}\times Y
.

Here

denotes the

of

[

, §3.2]
for details.

Using the index inequality, one can prove the following key result:

Proposition 2.6
.

[
34
, Prop. 3.7]

Suppose
J
is a generic
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure. Let
\alpha
and

(0)

2.3.
The definition of ECH

Let
\Gamma\in H_{1}(Y)
, and let
\lambda
be a nondegenerate contact form on
Y
. The
embedded contact homology

ECH(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
is a vector space over
\mathbb{Z}/2
defined as follows.
It is also possible to define ECH with integer coefficients, as explained in
[
36
, §9]
, but we will not need this.

Definition 2.7
.

We define the chain complex
ECC(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
to be the
\mathbb{Z}/2
vector space generated by ECH generators
\alpha
with
[\alpha]=\Gamma
. Next, choose a generic
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure
J
. We define a differential

\partial_{J}:ECC(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)\longrightarrow ECC(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)

as follows. If
\alpha
is an ECH generator with
[\alpha]=\Gamma
, then

\partial_{J}\alpha=\sum_{\beta}\langle\partial_{J}\alpha,\beta\rangle\beta

where

\langle\partial_{J}\alpha,\beta\rangle=\#\{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)/\mathbb{R}\mid I(\mathcal{C})=1\}.

Here the sum is over ECH generators
\beta
with
[\beta]=\Gamma
, and
\#
denotes the mod 2 count.

A compactness argument in
[
34
, §5.3]
shows that
\partial_{J}
is well defined. It is shown in
[
35
,
36
]
that
\partial_{J}^{2}=0
. We then define
ECH(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
to be the homology of the chain complex
(ECC(Y,\lambda,\Gamma),\partial_{J})
.

It follows from the index ambiguity formula (
2.3
) that the ECH index defines a relative
\mathbb{Z}/d
grading on
ECH(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
, where
d
is the divisibility of
c_{1}(\xi)+2\operatorname{PD}
in
H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
mod torsion, which is always an even integer. Here the index difference between two ECH generators
\alpha
and
\beta
with
[\alpha]=[\beta]=\Gamma
is defined to be
I(\alpha,\beta,Z)\mod d
for any
Z\in H_{2}(Y,\alpha,\beta)
. We say that a class in
ECH(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
is
homogeneous
if it can be represented by a sum of ECH generators each of which have the same relative
\mathbb{Z}/d
-grading. In the special case
\Gamma=0
, there is a canonical
\mathbb{Z}/d
grading, where the grading of an ECH generator
\alpha
with
[\alpha]=0
is defined to be

(2.6)

I(\alpha)=I(\alpha,\emptyset,Z)

for any
Z\in H_{2}(Y,\alpha,\emptyset)
. By
[
30
, Prop. 1.6(c)]
, there is also a canonical
\mathbb{Z}/2
grading on
ECH(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma
; an ECH generator
\alpha=\{(\alpha_{i},m_{i})\}
has even grading if and only if an even number of the Reeb orbits
\alpha_{i}
are positive hyperbolic.

Taubes
[
50
]
proved that if
Y
is connected, then there is a canonical isomorphism of relatively
\mathbb{Z}/d
-graded
\mathbb{Z}/2
-vector spaces

(2.7)

ECH_{*}(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)\simeq\widehat{HM}^{-*}(Y,\mathfrak{s}_{\xi}+\operatorname{PD}(\Gamma);\mathbb{Z}/2).

Here the right hand side is a version of Seiberg-Witten Floer cohomology, as defined by Kronheimer-Mrowka
[
41
]
, with
\mathbb{Z}/2
-coefficients. Also
\mathfrak{s}_{\xi}
denotes the spin-c structure on
Y
determined by the oriented
2
-plane field
\xi
; the spin-c structure
\mathfrak{s}_{\xi}+\operatorname{PD}(\Gamma)
has first Chern class
c_{1}(\xi)+2\operatorname{PD}(\Gamma)
. In particular, it follows from the canonical isomorphism (
2.7
) that
ECH(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
does not depend on the choice of generic
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure
J
, and so we can denote it by
ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma)
.

2.4.
The
U
map

Suppose now that the three-manifold
Y
is connected. There is then a “
U
-map”

(2.8)

U:ECH_{*}(Y,\xi,\Gamma)\longrightarrow ECH_{*-2}(Y,\xi,\Gamma)

which will play a key role in our arguments.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
y
be a point on
Y
which is not on the image of any Reeb orbit, and let
J
be a generic
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure. We then define a map

U_{J,y}:ECC_{*}(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)\longrightarrow ECC_{*-2}(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)

as follows. If
\alpha
is an ECH generator with
[\alpha]=\Gamma
, then

U_{J,y}\alpha=\sum_{\beta}\langle U_{J,y}\alpha,\beta\rangle\beta

where

(2.9)

\langle U_{J,y}\alpha,\beta\rangle=\#\left\{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)\mid I(\mathcal{C})=2,\;(0,y)\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

As explained in
[
37
, §2.5]
,
U_{J,y}
is a chain map:

\partial_{J}\circ U_{J,y}=U_{J,y}\circ\partial_{J}.

Consequently
U_{J,y}
induces a map on
ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma)
. As shown in
[
37
, §2.5]
, this map does not depend on the choice of point
y
(here one uses the assumption that
Y
is connected), so we can denote it by
U_{J}
. Taubes
[
51
]
further showed that under the isomorphism (
2.7
), the map
U_{J}
agrees with a “U-map” on Seiberg-Witten Floer cohomology, so in particular we have a well-defined map as in (
2.8
).

We will need the following fact about the nontriviality of the
U
-map, which is a consequence of
[
10
, Lem. A.1(a)]
.

Definition 2.9
.

A
U
-sequence
in
ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma)
is a sequence of nonzero classes
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\geq k_{0}}
in
ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma)
such that
U\sigma_{k+1}=\sigma_{k}
for each
k\geq k_{0}
.

Proposition 2.10
.

If
Y
is connected and
c_{1}(\xi)+2\operatorname{PD}(\Gamma)\in H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
is torsion, then there exists a
U
-sequence
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\geq k_{0}}
in
ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma)
such that each class
\sigma_{k}
is homogeneous and has even grading.

2.5.
Spectral invariants and the volume property

It follows from the definition of “
\lambda
-compatible” almost complex structure that the differential
\partial_{J}
, and the U-map
U_{J,y}
, decrease the symplectic action: If
\alpha
and
\beta
are ECH generators with
\langle\partial_{J}\alpha,\beta\rangle
or
\langle U_{J,y}\alpha,\beta\rangle
nonzero, then
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)>\mathcal{A}(\beta)
. In particular, for each real number
L
, we can define the
filtered embedded contact homology
, denoted by
ECH^{L}(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)
, to be the homology of the subcomplex of
(ECC(Y,\lambda,\Gamma),\partial_{J})
generated by ECH generators with symplectic action less than
L
. There is also an inclusion-induced map

(2.10)

ECH^{L}(Y,\lambda,\Gamma)\longrightarrow ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma).

It is shown in
[
38
, Thm. 1.3]
that the filtered ECH and the inclusion-induced map (
2.10
) do not depend on the choice of generic
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure
J
. However the filtered ECH, unlike the regular ECH, does depend on the contact form
\lambda
and not just on the contact structure
\xi
, as detected for example by the following spectral invariants.

Definition 2.11
.

Given a nonzero class
\sigma\in ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma)
, we define the
spectral invariant

c_{\sigma}(Y,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}

to be the infimum over
L
such that
\sigma
is in the image of the inclusion-induced map (
2.10
).

Equivalently, if one makes a choice of generic
\lambda
-compatible
J
, then
c_{\sigma}(Y,\lambda)
is the minimum
L
such that the class
\sigma
can be represented by a cycle in the chain complex
(ECC(Y,\lambda,\Gamma),\partial_{J})
which is a sum of ECH generators each having symplectic action less than or equal to
L
. As shown in
[
33
]
, each spectral invariant
c_{\sigma}
is a
C^{0}
-continuous function of the contact form
\lambda
, and as such extends by continuity to degenerate contact forms.

The following “Weyl law” for the ECH spectrum will play a key role in the proof of the main theorem. This is a special case of
[
11
, Thm. 1.3]
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Suppose
Y
is connected and
c_{1}(\xi)+2\operatorname{PD}(\Gamma)\in H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
is torsion. Let
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\geq k_{0}}
be a
U
-sequence in
ECH(Y,\xi,\Gamma)
. Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{c_{\sigma_{k}}(Y,\lambda)^{2}}{k}=2\int_{Y}\lambda\wedge d\lambda.

2.6.
Partition conditions

Let
\alpha=\{(\alpha_{i},m_{i})\}
and
\beta=\{(\beta_{j},n_{j})\}
be orbit sets, and suppose that
C\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is a somewhere injective curve. Then
C
has positive ends at covers of
\alpha_{i}
with total multiplicity
m_{i}
; the multiplicities of these covers determine a partition of
m_{i}
, which we denote by
p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(C)
. Likewise, the multiplicities of the covers of
\beta_{j}
at which
C
has negative ends determine a partition of
n_{j}
, which we denote by
p_{\beta_{j}}^{-}(C)
. It turns out that if equality holds in the index inequality (
2.5
), then these partitions are uniquely determined as follows.

Definition 2.13
.

If
\theta
is a real number and
m
is a positive integer, we define two partitions of
m
, the
positive partition

p_{\theta}^{+}(m)
and the
negative partition

p_{\theta}^{-}(m)
, as follows. We define the positive partition
p^{+}_{\theta}(m)
to be the set of horizontal displacements of the maximal polygonal path that starts at
(0,0)
, ends at
(m,\lfloor m\theta\rfloor)
, is the graph of a piecewise linear concave function with vertices at integer lattice points, and does not go above the line
y=m\theta
.
Here “horizontal displacement” means the horizontal displacement between two consecutive integer lattice points on the path. Similarly, we define the negative partition
p^{-}_{\theta}(m)
to be the set of horizontal displacements of the edges of the minimal polygonal path that starts at
(0,0)
, ends at
(m,\lceil m\theta\rceil)
, is the graph of a piecewise linear convex function with vertices at integer lattice points, and does not go below the line
y=m\theta
.

Note that
p_{\theta}^{-}(m)=p_{-\theta}^{+}(m)
. Also note that for a given
m
, the partitions
p_{\theta}^{+}(m)
and
p_{\theta}^{-}(m)
depend only on the equivalence class of
\theta
in
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. The following is a basic example:

Example 2.14
.

If
0\leq\theta<1/m
, then
p_{\theta}^{+}(m)=(1,\ldots,1)
. This is because the maximal polygonal path described above is the horizontal line from
(0,0)
to
(m,0)
.

We will also need the following generalization:

Example 2.15
.

If
\theta
is a rational number
a/b
in lowest terms, and if
m
is a multiple of
b
, then for
\epsilon\geq 0
sufficiently small with respect to
m
and
\theta
, we have
p_{a/b+\varepsilon}^{+}(m)=(m/b,\ldots,m/b)
. This is because the maximal polygonal path described above is the line from
(0,0)
to
(m,ma/b)
.

If
\gamma
is a simple Reeb orbit, then the linearized Reeb flow has a well-defined rotation number
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
independent of a choice of trivialization. Given a positive integer
m
, we define

p_{\gamma}^{+}(m)=p_{\theta}^{+}(m),\quad\quad\quad p_{\gamma}^{-}(m)=p_{\theta}^{-}(m).

Now let
J
be a generic
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure, suppose that
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)
has ECH index
1
or
2
, and in the latter case suppose that
\alpha
and
\beta
are ECH generators. Write
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}
as in Proposition
2.6
. For each
i
, let
m_{i}^{\prime}
denote the total covering multiplicity of the covers of
\alpha_{i}
at which
C_{1}
has positive ends. Thus
m_{i}^{\prime}\leq m_{i}
, and
m_{i}-m_{i}^{\prime}
is the multiplicity of the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\alpha_{i}
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. Likewise, let
n_{j}^{\prime}
denote the total covering multiplicity of the covers of
\beta_{j}
at which
C_{1}
has negative ends.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
J
be generic, let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)
be a
J
-holomorphic current with ECH index
1
or
2
, assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
are ECH generators if
I(\mathcal{C})=2
, and write
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}
as in Proposition
2.6
. Then:

•

For each
i
with
m_{i}^{\prime}>0
, and for each
n
with
0\leq n\leq m_{i}-m_{i}^{\prime}
, we have

(2.11)

p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(m_{i}^{\prime}+n)=p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(C_{1})\cup p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(n).

•

Similarly, for each
j
with
n_{j}^{\prime}>0
, and for each
n
with
0\leq n\leq n_{j}-n_{j}^{\prime}
, we have

(2.12)

p_{\beta_{j}}^{-}(n_{j}^{\prime}+n)=p_{\beta_{j}}^{-}(C_{1})\cup p_{\beta_{j}}^{-}(n).

Here
\cup
denotes the union of multisets, and we interpret
p_{\gamma}^{\pm}(0)=\emptyset
.

In particular, (
2.11
) implies that if
C_{1}
has positive ends at covers of
\alpha_{i}
, then the multiplicities of these covers are determined by
p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(C_{1})=p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(m_{i}^{\prime})
.

Remark 2.17
.

In the case when
\mathcal{C}_{0}
includes the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\alpha_{i}
and
C_{1}
has a positive end asymptotic to a cover of
\alpha_{i}
, so that
m_{i}>m_{i}^{\prime}>0
, it follows from (
2.11
) with
n=m_{i}-m_{i}^{\prime}
that if
1\in p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(m_{i}-m_{i}^{\prime})
, then
1\in p_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}(m_{i})
. The converse is also true; this follows from
[
35
, Lem. 7.28]
. Likewise, if
n_{j}>n_{j}^{\prime}>0
, then
1\in p_{\beta_{j}}^{-}(n_{j}-n_{j}^{\prime})
if and only if
1\in p_{\beta_{j}}^{-}(n_{j})
.

Here are some additional facts about the partitions that we will need.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
be irrational, and let
m
be a positive integer. Then:

(a)

1\in p_{\theta}^{\pm}(m)\Longleftrightarrow 1\notin p_{\theta}^{\mp}(m)
.

(b)

If
m>1
, then the partitions
p_{\theta}^{+}(m)
and
p_{\theta}^{-}(m)
are disjoint.

(c)

If
|p_{\theta}^{+}(m)|+|p_{\theta}^{-}(m)|\leq 3
, then
m\{\theta\}<2
or
m(1-\{\theta\})<2
, where
\{\theta\}\in[0,1)
denotes the fractional part of
\theta
.

Proof.

Statements (a) and (b) are proved in
[
30
, Rem. 4.4]
; see also
[
34
, Ex. 3.13(c)]
for a stronger version of (b). Statement (c) is shown in the proof of
[
10
, Lem. 4.9]
.

2.7.
The
J_{0}
index and topological complexity bounds

Recall from the index inequality (
2.5
) that the ECH index bounds the Fredholm index of a somewhere injective curve. There is a variant of the ECH index, called the
J_{0}
-index
, which instead bounds the topological complexity of the curve.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
\alpha=\{(\alpha_{i},m_{i})\}
and
\beta=\{(\beta_{j},n_{j})\}
be orbit sets with
[\alpha]=[\beta]
, and let
Z\in H_{2}(Y,\alpha,\beta)
. We define

(2.13)

J_{0}(\alpha,\beta,Z)=I(\alpha,\beta,Z)-\left(2c_{\tau}(Z)+{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha)-{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\beta)\right).

Here
{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha)
adds up the Conley-Zehnder indices of the “top” iterates of the orbits
\alpha_{i}
, namely

(2.14)

{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha)=\sum_{i}{\rm CZ}_{\tau}(\alpha_{i}^{m_{i}}).

If
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, then similarly to (
2.4
), we write
J_{0}(\mathcal{C})=J_{0}(\alpha,\beta,[\mathcal{C}])
.

In the situation relevant to us,
J_{0}
bounds topological complexity as follows. Suppose that the almost complex structure
J
is generic, let
\alpha
and
\beta
be ECH generators, suppose
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)
has ECH index
2
, and write
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}
as in Proposition
2.6
. For each simple Reeb orbit
\gamma
, define
e_{\gamma}^{+}(\mathcal{C})
to be twice the number of positive ends of
C_{1}
at covers of
\gamma
, minus
1
if
C_{1}
has positive ends at covers of
\gamma
but
\mathcal{C}_{0}
does not include the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
. Likewise, define
e_{\gamma}^{-}(\mathcal{C})
to be the twice the number of negative ends of
C_{1}
at covers of
\gamma
, minus
1
if
C_{1}
has negative ends at covers of
\gamma
but
\mathcal{C}_{0}
does not include the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
. Write
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=e_{\gamma}^{+}(\mathcal{C})+e_{\gamma}^{-}(\mathcal{C})
. We then have:

Proposition 2.20
.

In the above situation we have

(2.15)

J_{0}(\mathcal{C})=2g(C_{1})-2+\sum_{\gamma}e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C}).

Here
g(C_{1})
denotes the genus of
C_{1}
, and the sum is over simple Reeb orbits
\gamma
.

Proof.

The fact that the left hand side is greater than or equal to the right hand side is a restatement of
[
37
, Lem. 3.5]
. Although this inequality is all that we actually need, one can further obtain equality as explained in the footnote on
[
37
, page 919]
.

2.8.
Criterion for a Birkhoff section

We now recall a criterion for when the nontrivial component of a holomorphic curve counted by the
U
map gives rise to a Birkhoff section for the Reeb flow.

Definition 2.21
.

Let
(Y,\lambda)
be a contact three-manifold. A
Birkhoff section
for the Reeb flow is an embedded open surface
\Sigma\subset Y
such that:

•

The Reeb vector field
R
is transverse to
\Sigma
.

•

There is a compact surface with boundary
\overline{\Sigma}
, such that
\operatorname{int}(\overline{\Sigma})=\Sigma
, and the inclusion
\Sigma\to Y
extends to an immersion
g:\overline{\Sigma}\to Y
such that each boundary circle of
\overline{\Sigma}
is mapped to the image of a Reeb orbit, and
\Sigma\cap g\left(\partial\overline{\Sigma}\right)=\emptyset
.

•

For every
y\in Y\setminus g\left(\partial\overline{\Sigma}\right)
, the Reeb trajectory starting at
y
intersects
\Sigma
in both forward and backward time.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
J
be a generic
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure, let
\alpha
and
\beta
be ECH generators, let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, and suppose that
I(\mathcal{C})=2
. Write
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}
as in Proposition
2.6
. Suppose that:

(a)

C_{1}
has at least two ends.

(b)

C_{1}
has genus zero.

(c)

C_{1}
does not have two positive ends, or two negative ends, at covers of the same simple Reeb orbit.

(d)

C_{1}
has no ends at positive hyperbolic orbits.

(e)

The component of the moduli space
\mathcal{M}^{J}(\alpha,\beta)/\mathbb{R}
containing
C_{1}
is compact.

Then the restriction of the projection
\mathbb{R}\times Y\to Y
to
C_{1}
is an embedding, and its image is a Birkhoff section for the Reeb flow.

Proof.

This follows from
[
10
, Prop. 3.2]
, as explained in
[
10
, §5.2]
.
∎

3.
Birkhoff sections for nondegenerate perturbations

3.1.
Introduction

The aim of this section is to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
Y
be a closed connected three-manifold, let
\lambda
be a contact form on
Y
with finitely many simple Reeb orbits, and assume that
c_{1}(\xi)\in H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
is torsion, where
\xi=\ker\lambda
. Let
\eta>0
be given. Then there exists a sequence
(\lambda_{k},J_{k},\alpha_{k},\beta_{k},C_{k})_{k=1,2,\dots}
where:

•

\lambda_{k}=f_{k}\lambda
is a nondegenerate contact form,
f_{k}\to 1
in
C^{\infty}
, and for every simple Reeb orbit
\gamma
of
\lambda
, we have
f_{k}|_{\gamma}=1
and
df_{k}|_{\gamma}=0
.

•

J_{k}
is a
\lambda_{k}
-compatible almost complex structure, and
J_{k}\to J
in
C^{\infty}
as
k\to\infty
, where
J
is a
\lambda
-compatible almost complex structure,

•

\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are (not necessarily simple) Reeb orbits for
\lambda_{k}
such that
\alpha_{k}\to\alpha_{\infty}
and
\beta_{k}\to\beta_{\infty}
in
C^{\infty}
, where
\alpha_{\infty}
and
\beta_{\infty}
are Reeb orbits for
\lambda
,

•

C_{k}
is a
J_{k}
-holomorphic cylinder in
\mathbb{R}\times Y
with positive end asymptotic to
\alpha_{k}
and negative end asymptotic to
\beta_{k}
for each
k
,

such that for each
k
:

(a)

\operatorname{ind}(C_{k})=I(C_{k})=2
, see §
2.2
.

(b)

\mathcal{A}(\alpha_{k})-\mathcal{A}(\beta_{k})<\eta
.

(c)

If
\alpha_{\infty}
is degenerate, then the rotation number of
\alpha_{k}
is in the interval
(-1/2,0)

from below as

\beta_{\infty}
is degenerate, then the rotation number of
\beta_{k}
is in the interval
(0,1/2)

(d)

C_{k}
projects to a Birkhoff section for the Reeb flow for

, see §
2.8
.

Remark 3.2
.

In
[
10
, Prop. 4.2]
, it was shown that for a single nondegenerate contact form with only finitely many simple Reeb orbits, there is always a genus zero Birkhoff section with at most three punctures. For our purposes it is important to obtain a sequence of cylinders with uniform bounds on the action as
k\to\infty
, for nondegenerate contact forms that do not necessarily have finitely many simple Reeb orbits; new ideas are required for this. We discuss this further in the outline of the proof below.

Remark 3.3
.

As explained in
[
37
, §4.6]
, the existence of a Birkhoff section given by holomorphic cylinders implies that the three-manifold
Y
is a lens space or
S^{3}
. Hence Proposition
3.1
already implies Theorem
1.1
when
Y
is not
S^{3}
or a lens space; in this case
\lambda
must have infinitely many simple Reeb orbits.

Outline of the proof of Proposition
3.1

For any given
\lambda_{k}
, one can use the non-triviality of the
U
-map in ECH to produce a “chain” of a large number of
J_{k}
-holomorphic curves with ECH index
2
. The first challenge is to show that some of these are cylinders. Building on ideas from
[
37
,
10
]
, we accomplish this by a kind of probabilistic argument, showing that in fact “most” of the curves must be cylinders. As in
[
37
,
10
]
, the starting point behind this is to show that the curves on average have
J_{0}
approximately
2
; this is the one place where we use the assumption that
c_{1}(\xi)
is torsion.
A
J_{0}=2
curve need not be a cylinder, however (see §
2.7
), and so a deeper analysis is needed; moreover, the arguments in
[
10
]
are geared towards finding curves with at most three punctures, under the assumption of finitely many simple Reeb orbits, and so are far from sufficing for this.
The main new ingredient we introduce for this purpose is to define a “score” for each curve; roughly speaking, the idea is that cylinders should be characterized by having score
0
, and on the other hand the score is nonnegative, so, one can deduce the desired existence result by arguing that the total score of the chain of curves is not too large.

Since we must consider a sequence of contact forms
\lambda_{k}
, we must also keep careful track of the various parameters in these probabilistic arguments. In particular, we will define a
k
-independent constant
T_{q}
such that all holomorphic curves under consideration go between orbit sets for
\lambda_{k}
with action
\leq T_{q}
.

As a result, the proof of Proposition
3.1
shows the following more general statement, which is also applicable to contact forms with infinitely many simple Reeb orbits; as mentioned previously, it gives, for example, information about the growth rate of the orbits when the manifold is not a lens space and the contact structure is torsion. (For simplicity we will not write a separate proof of this as it is not needed for the main theorem.)

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
Y
be a closed connected three-manifold, let
\lambda
be a contact form on
Y
, and assume that
c_{1}(\xi)\in H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
is torsion, where
\xi=\operatorname{Ker}(\lambda)
. Suppose that
\lambda
has at least
n
simple Reeb orbits. Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
denote the first
n
simple Reeb orbits in terms of action
3
3

3

That is,
\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{1})\leq\cdots\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{n})
, and any other simple Reeb orbit has action at least that of
\gamma_{n}
.
. Let

be given. Then there is a constant

3.2.1.
The perturbation lemma

The sequence of contact forms in Proposition
3.1
will come from the following lemma, the idea of which is due to Bangert

]
:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(Y,\lambda)
be a closed contact three-manifold with finitely many simple Reeb orbits. Let

Then there is a sequence of nondegenerate contact forms

converging in

to
\lambda
, such that:

(a)

For every

and every simple Reeb orbit
\gamma
of
\lambda

f_{k}|_{\gamma}=1
and
df_{k}|_{\gamma}=0
. In particular,
\gamma
is also a simple Reeb orbit of
\lambda_{k}
.

(b)

There exists a compact neighborhood

\lambda

.

(c)

For every

\lambda_{k}

for

(1)

If
\gamma^{\prime}

.

(2)

If
\gamma^{\prime}
has rational rotation number, written in lowest terms with respect to some trivialization as

, or

Consider a sequence of nondegenerate contact forms
\lambda_{k}=f_{k}\lambda
such that

in

, and such that on each of the finitely many simple Reeb orbits

∎

3.2.3.
Basic estimates

The following estimate gives a useful lower bound on the difference of actions between certain orbit sets.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
M>1
. Then there is a constant
\epsilon_{M}>0
such that for any two orbit sets
\alpha
,
\beta
for
\lambda
with distinct actions, if either orbit set has complexity
\leq M
, or if both orbit sets have exactly one simple Reeb orbit with multiplicity
>M
and these simple orbits are the same, then

(3.1)

\left|\mathcal{A}(\alpha)-\mathcal{A}(\beta)\right|\geq\epsilon_{M}\,.

Proof.

Let
S_{M}
denote the set of actions of orbit sets of complexity
\leq M
. Since
the set
\mathcal{P}
of simple Reeb orbits of
\lambda
is finite, it follows that the set
S_{M}
is finite, and the set
S_{\infty}
of all actions of orbit sets is discrete. Thus there is a positive lower bound
\epsilon^{\prime}_{M}
on the set of distances between two distinct actions of orbit sets with at least one in
S_{M}
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be as in the statement of the lemma.
If either orbit set has complexity
\leq M
, then their actions will differ by at least
\epsilon^{\prime}_{M}>0
.

If on the other hand both
\alpha
and
\beta
have exactly one simple Reeb orbit with multiplicity
>M
, which is the same for both orbit sets, then denote this simple Reeb orbit by
\gamma
. Consider the orbit sets
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
obtained from
\alpha
and
\beta
by changing the multiplicity of
\gamma
to zero and keeping the multiplicities of all other simple Reeb orbits the same. Then
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
are orbit sets with complexity
\leq M
. Since the set
S_{M}
is finite, it follows that the set
S_{M}^{\prime}
of differences between elements of
S_{M}
is also finite. Now we have

\left|\mathcal{A}(\alpha)-\mathcal{A}(\beta)\right|=\left|\mathcal{A}(\alpha_{0})-\mathcal{A}(\beta_{0})+z\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\right|

for some integer
z
. By finiteness of the set

, the right hand side of the above equality, when nonzero, is bounded from below by some
\epsilon_{M}^{\prime\prime}>0
.
We then take
\epsilon_{M}=\min(\epsilon_{M}^{\prime},\epsilon_{M}^{\prime\prime})
.
∎

There is also the following useful bound on the relative Chern class. If

There is a constant

U
-curves

We now use the
U
-map on ECH to obtain a “chain” of
U
-curves. The following is loosely related to
[
10
, Lemma 4.4]
:

Lemma 3.12
.

If
k
is sufficiently large, then there exist:

•

ECH generators
\alpha(p_{0}),\ldots,\alpha(q)
for
\lambda_{k}
, with
[\alpha(i)]=0\in H_{1}(Y)
and
\mathcal{A}(\alpha(q))\leq T_{q}
,

•

U
-curves
\mathcal{C}(i)\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha(i),\alpha(i-1))
for
p_{0}<i\leq q
,

such that:

(a)

There are at most
T_{q}/\epsilon^{\prime}
indices
i\in\{p_{0}+1,\ldots,q\}
such that

\mathcal{A}(\alpha(i))-\mathcal{A}(\alpha(i-1))\geq\epsilon^{\prime}.

(b)

We have

(3.15)

\sum^{q}_{i={p_{0}+1}}J_{0}(\mathcal{C}(i))\leq 2(q-p_{0})+2\delta_{1}T_{q}.

Proof.

Since the ECH spectral invariants are
C^{0}
-continuous functions of the contact form, see §
2.5
, it follows from the bound (
3.4
) and equation (
3.9
) that if
k
is sufficiently large then

(3.16)

c_{\sigma_{q}}(Y,\lambda_{k})\leq T_{q}.

By the definition of the spectral invariant
c_{\sigma_{q}}
, we can find a cycle
x
in the ECH chain complex
(ECC(Y,\lambda,0),\partial_{J_{k}})
representing the class
\sigma_{q}
, such that each ECH generator that is a summand in
x
has action
\leq T_{q}
. By the definition of
U
-sequence, we have
U_{J_{k},y}^{q-p_{0}}x\neq 0
. There is then some summand of
x
, which we can call
\alpha(q)
, such that
U_{J_{k},y}^{q-p_{0}}x\neq 0
. This means that there are ECH generators
\alpha(p_{0}),\ldots,\alpha(q-1)
such that

\langle U_{J_{k},y}\alpha(i),\alpha(i-1)\rangle\neq 0

for
p_{0}<i\leq q
. By the definition (
2.9
) of the chain map
U_{J_{k},y}
, it follows that for each
i
with
p_{0}<i\leq q
there exists a
U
-curve
\mathcal{C}(i)\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha(i),\alpha(i-1))
.

Assertion (a) follows immediately from the fact that
\mathcal{A}(\alpha(q))\leq T_{q}
and the nonnegativity of
\mathcal{A}(\alpha(i))
and
\mathcal{A}(\alpha(i))-\mathcal{A}(\alpha(i-1))
for each
i
. It remains to prove assertion (b).

By equation (
2.13
) and the fact that
I(\mathcal{C}(i))=2
, we have a telescoping sum

\begin{split}\sum_{i=p_{0}+1}^{q}J_{0}(\mathcal{C}(i))-2(q-p_{0})&=\sum_{i=p_{0}+1}^{q}\left(J_{0}(\mathcal{C}(i))-I(\mathcal{C}(i))\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=p_{0}+1}^{q}\left(\left[2c_{\tau}(\alpha(i-1))+{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha(i-1))\right]-\left[2c_{\tau}(\alpha(i))+{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha(i))\right]\right)\\
&=\left[2c_{\tau}(\alpha(p_{0}))+{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha(p_{0}))\right]-\left[2c_{\tau}(\alpha(q))+{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha(q)\right].\end{split}

So to prove part (b), it is enough to show that if
k
is sufficiently large, then

(3.17)

\left|2c_{\tau}(\alpha)+{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha)\right|\leq\delta_{1}T_{q}

for every ECH generator
\alpha
for
\lambda_{k}
with
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\leq T_{q}
.

Suppose to start that
k\geq k_{T_{q}}
, where the constant
k_{T_{q}}
is provided by Lemma
3.5
(c). Recall from §
3.2.2
that for each orbit set
\alpha
for
\lambda_{k}
with
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\leq T_{q}
, there is a unique orbit set
\underline{\alpha}
for
\lambda
such that
\alpha
is homologous to
\underline{\alpha}
in
N
. Since the trivialization
\tau
of
\xi
over the Reeb orbits in
\alpha
and
\underline{\alpha}
extends to a trivialization of
\xi
over all of
N
, it follows from the definition of the relative first Chern class in §
2.1
that

c_{\tau}(\alpha)=c_{\tau}(\underline{\alpha}).

By this equation and Lemma
3.8
, to prove (
3.17
), it is enough to show that

\left|{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}(\alpha)-{\rm CZ}_{\tau}^{\operatorname{top}}\left(\underline{\alpha}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\mathcal{A}(\alpha)}{\ell}.

By our choice of
\ell
in §
3.2.5
, there are at most
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)/\ell
Reeb orbits in
\alpha
. So to complete the proof, it is enough to show that if
k\geq k_{T_{q}}
is sufficiently large, then for every (not necessarily simple) Reeb orbit
\gamma
of
\lambda_{k}
with
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq T_{q}
, if
\underline{\gamma}
is the corresponding Reeb orbit of
\lambda
(in the notation of Lemma
3.5
(c),
\underline{\gamma}
is either
\gamma^{\prime}
or
(\gamma^{\prime})^{b}
), then

(3.18)

\left|{\rm CZ}_{\tau}(\gamma)-{\rm CZ}_{\tau}\left(\underline{\gamma}\right)\right|\leq 1.

By Lemma
3.5
(c) and equation (
2.1
), the bound (
3.18
) holds when
k
is sufficiently large.
∎

3.3.
The score

To analyze the chain of
U
-curves produced in §
3.2.7
, we now define a “score” associated to each
U
-curve and analyze it in detail.

3.3.1.
Definition of the score

Definition 3.13
.

Let
\alpha
be an orbit set for
\lambda_{k}
, and let
\gamma
be a simple Reeb orbit of
\lambda_{k}
.

•

We say that
\gamma
is a
special component
of
\alpha
if
m(\gamma,\alpha)>1
and
1\notin p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))
. Define
s(\gamma,\alpha)
to be
1
if
\gamma
is a special component of
\alpha
, and
0
otherwise. Denote the number of special components of
\alpha
by

s(\alpha)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}s(\gamma,\alpha).

•

We say that
\gamma
is a
p^{+}
-component
of
\alpha
if
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))=(m(\gamma,\alpha))
and
m(\gamma,\alpha)\geq M
, where
M
is the constant provided by Lemma
3.9
. Define
p^{+}(\gamma,\alpha)
to be
1
if
\gamma
is a
p^{+}
-component of
\alpha
, and
0
otherwise. Denote the number of
p^{+}
-components of
\alpha
by

p^{+}(\alpha)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}p^{+}(\gamma,\alpha).

•

Similarly, we say that
\gamma
is a
p^{-}
-component
of
\alpha
if
p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\alpha))=(m(\gamma,\alpha))
and
m(\gamma,\alpha)\geq M
. Define
p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha)
to be
1
if
\gamma
is a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
, and
0
otherwise. Denote the number of
p^{-}
-components of
\alpha
by

p^{-}(\alpha)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha).

Definition 3.14
.

If
\alpha
is an orbit set for
\lambda_{k}
, its
orbit score
is the quantity

S(\alpha):=p^{+}(\alpha)+s(\alpha)-p^{-}(\alpha).

Definition 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
be a
U
-curve. The
orbit score
of
\mathcal{C}
is

S(\mathcal{C})=S(\alpha)-S(\beta).

The
total score
of
\mathcal{C}
(also called the “score” when there is no danger of confusion) is

T(\mathcal{C})=3y(\mathcal{C})+S(\mathcal{C})

where we define

y(\mathcal{C})=J_{0}(\mathcal{C})-2.

Definition 3.16
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a
U
-curve as above, and if
\gamma
is a simple Reeb orbit of
\lambda_{k}
, we define the contribution to the orbit score of
\mathcal{C}
associated to
\gamma
by

(3.19)

S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=\left(p^{+}(\gamma,\alpha)+s(\gamma,\alpha)+p^{-}(\gamma,\beta)\right)-\left(p^{+}(\gamma,\beta)+s(\gamma,\beta)+p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha)\right).

We define the contribution to the total score of
\mathcal{C}
associated to
\gamma
by

(3.20)

T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})+3e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C}),

where
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})
was defined in §
2.7
.

Remark 3.17
.

It follows from equation (
2.15
) and the above definitions that if we write
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}
as in Proposition
2.6
, then

(3.21)

T(\mathcal{C})=6g(C_{1})-12+\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C}).

Moreover, in the sum on the right, the contribution
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})
is nonzero only if
C_{1}
has an end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
.

3.3.2.
How orbits contribute to the score

We now collect some lemmas about how orbits contribute to the score of a
U
-curve.

Lemma 3.18
.

If
k
is sufficiently large then the following holds. Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
be a
U
-curve such that
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\leq T_{q}
. Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
and suppose that
C_{1}
has at least one end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
. Let
m_{0}
denote the multiplicity of the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. Then:

(a)

T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 3
.

(b)

If
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=3
, then
m_{0}=0
, the curve
C_{1}
has a unique end at a cover of
\gamma
, and if this end is positive then the covering multiplicity must be one.

Proof.

By equation (
3.19
) we have
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-3
. If
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 3
then it follows from equation (
3.20
) that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 6
. It therefore remains to consider the cases where
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\in\{1,2\}
.

Case 1:
Suppose that
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=1
. In this case, it follows from the definition of
e_{\gamma}
in §
2.7
that
m_{0}=0
, and the curve
C_{1}
has just one end at a cover of
\gamma
.

Subcase 1a:
Suppose that the end of
C_{1}
at a cover of
\gamma
is a positive end.
Then
p^{+}(\gamma,\beta)=s(\gamma,\beta)=0
.

If
m(\gamma,\alpha)=1
, then
p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha)=0
(since
M>1
), so
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
, so
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 3
, and the conditions for equality in (b) hold.

If
m(\gamma,\alpha)>1
, then it follows from Proposition
2.16
that
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))=(m(\gamma,\alpha))
, so
s(\gamma,\alpha)=1
. Since
\alpha
is an ECH generator,
\gamma
has irrational rotational number, so it follows from Lemma
2.18
(b) that
p^{-}_{\gamma}(m(\gamma,\alpha))\neq(m(\gamma,\alpha))
. This implies that
p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha)=0
. We conclude that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})>0
, so
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})>3
.

Subcase 1b:
Suppose that the end of
C_{1}
at a cover of
\gamma
is a negative end.

Then
p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha)=0
. Similarly to the above paragraph, it follows from Proposition
2.16
that
p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\beta))=(m(\gamma,\beta))
and then from Lemma
2.18
(b) that
p^{+}(\gamma,\beta)=0
. By Lemma
2.18
(a), we have
1\in p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\beta))
, so
s(\gamma,\beta)=0
. Thus
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
, so
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 3
, and the conditions for equality in (b) hold.

Case 2:
Suppose that
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=2
. It will suffice to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-2
, so that
T_{\gamma}(C)>3
.

Subcase 2a:
Suppose that
m_{0}=0
. Then by the definition of
e_{\gamma}
, the curve
C_{1}
has a positive end and a negative end asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
. Then
s(\gamma,\beta)=0
by the same argument as in Subcase 1b, so
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-2
.

Subcase 2b:
Suppose that
m_{0}>0
. Then
C_{1}
has a unique end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
. To prove that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-2
, suppose to get a contradiction that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=-3
. Then
\gamma
is both a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
and a
p^{+}
-component of
\beta
.

Consider the case where the end of
C_{1}
asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
is a positive end, and let
d
denote the covering multiplicity. Then
m(\gamma,\beta)=m_{0}
, and
m(\gamma,\alpha)=m(\gamma,\beta)+d
. By Proposition
2.16
,

\begin{split}p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))&=p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\beta))\cup(d)\\
&=(m(\gamma,\beta),d)\end{split}

since
\gamma
is a
p^{+}
-component of
\beta
. In particular
|p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))|=2
.

Since
\gamma
is a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
, we also have
|p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\alpha))|=1
. We conclude that the pair
(\gamma,m(\gamma,\alpha))
is exceptional. Since
m(\gamma,\alpha)\geq M
by the definition of “
p^{+}
-component”, this contradicts
Lemma
3.9
if
k\geq k_{T_{q}}^{\prime}
.

The case when the end of
C_{1}
asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
is a negative end is handled by a symmetric argument.
∎

The next lemma gives a refinement of the previous one when we assume in addition that
C_{1}
has a “high multiplicity” positive end.

Definition 3.19
.

A positive or negative end of a
J_{k}
-holomorphic curve asymptotic to the
d
-fold cover of an orbit
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
is
high multiplicity
if
d\geq M
.

Lemma 3.20
.

If
k
is sufficiently large then the following holds. Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
be a
U
-curve such that
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\leq T_{q}
. Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
and suppose that
C_{1}
has at least one high multiplicity positive end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
. Let
m_{0}
denote the multiplicity of the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. Then:

(a)

If
C_{1}
has a unique end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
, then

T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 5
, with equality only if
m_{0}\neq 1
.

(b)

If
C_{1}
has at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
, then
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 8
, with equality only if
C_{1}
has exactly one positive and one negative end asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
and
m_{0}=0
.

Proof.

(a) Suppose that
C_{1}
has a unique end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
.

Case 0:
Suppose that
m_{0}=0
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 5
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=1
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 2
. From Proposition
2.16
and Lemma
2.18
(b), we learn that
\gamma
is a
p^{+}
-component and a special component but not a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
. Hence
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=2
.

Case 1:

Suppose that
m_{0}=1
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 6
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=2
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
. Since
C_{1}
has no negative ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
, it follows that
p^{+}(\gamma,\beta)=s(\gamma,\beta)=0
. In addition, if
k\geq k_{T_{q}}^{\prime\prime}
, then
p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha)=0
, because otherwise the pair
(\gamma,m(\gamma,\alpha))
would be exceptional by Proposition
2.16
, and this would contradict Lemma
3.9
because
m(\gamma,\alpha)\geq M+1
. It then follows from equation (
3.19
) that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
.

Case 2:

Suppose that
m_{0}\geq 2
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 5
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=2
as above, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-1
. By Proposition
2.16
and Remark
2.17
, we have
1\in p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))
if and only if
1\in p_{\gamma}^{+}(m_{0})
. Since
m_{0}=m(\gamma,\beta)
, it follows that
s(\gamma,\alpha)-s(\gamma,\beta)=0
.

To finish showing that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-1
, we observe that if
k\geq k_{T_{q}}^{\prime}
then
\gamma
cannot be both a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
and a
p^{+}
-component of
\beta
. Otherwise the pair
(\gamma,m(\gamma,\alpha))
would be exceptional by Proposition
2.16
, contradicting Lemma
3.9
since
m(\gamma,\alpha)\geq M+2
.

(b) Suppose that
C_{1}
has at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
.

Note first that if
m_{0}>0
, or if
C_{1}
has at least three ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
, then
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 4
, and so
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 9
. Thus we can assume that
m_{0}=0
and that
C_{1}
has exactly two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
.

Case 1:
Suppose that
C_{1}
has two positive ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 9
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=3
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
. Since
m(\gamma,\beta)=0
, it is enough to show that
\gamma
is not a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
. But if
\gamma
were a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
, then the pair
(\gamma,m(\gamma,\alpha))
would be exceptional by Proposition
2.16
, contradicting Lemma
3.9
if
k\geq k_{T_{q}}^{\prime}
.

Case 2:
Suppose that
C_{1}
has a positive end and a negative end asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 8
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=2
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 2
. By Proposition
2.16
,
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))=(m(\gamma,\alpha))
, so
\gamma
is a
p^{+}
-component and special component of
\alpha
. By Lemma
2.18
(b),
\gamma
cannot be a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
. By Proposition
2.16
again,
p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\beta))=(m(\gamma,\beta))
. So by Lemma
2.18
(b),
\gamma
cannot be a
p^{+}
-component of
\beta
, and by Lemma
2.18
(a),
\gamma
cannot be a special component of
\beta
. Thus
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 2
by equation (
3.19
).
∎

The next lemma gives a similar refinement of Lemma
3.18
when we assume in addition that
C_{1}
has a high multiplicity negative end.

Lemma 3.21
.

If
k
is sufficiently large then the following holds. Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
be a
U
-curve such that
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\leq T_{q}
. Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
and suppose that
C_{1}
has at least one high multiplicity negative end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
. Let
m_{0}
denote the multiplicity of the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. Then:

(a)

If
C_{1}
has a unique end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
, then

T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 4
, with equality only if
m_{0}=0
.

(b)

If
C_{1}
has at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
, then
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 7
, with equality only if
C_{1}
has exactly one positive and one negative end asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
, the covering multiplicity for the positive end is one, and
m_{0}=0
.

Proof.

(a) Suppose that
C_{1}
has exactly one end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
.

Case 0:
Suppose that
m_{0}=0
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 4
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=1
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 1
. By Proposition
2.16
, we have
p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\beta))=(m(\gamma,\beta))
, so
p^{-}(\gamma,\beta)=1
. By Lemma
2.18
(b), we have
p^{+}(\gamma,\beta)=0
. By Lemma
2.18
(a), we have
s(\gamma,\beta)=0
. Hence
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=1
.

Case 1:
Suppose that
m_{0}=1
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 5
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=2
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-1
. We have
p^{-}(\gamma,\alpha)=0
by definition, because
m(\gamma,\alpha)=1
. By Proposition
2.16
, we have
p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\beta))=(m(\gamma,\beta)-1,1)
. If
k\geq k_{T_{q}}^{\prime\prime}
then it follows that
p^{+}(\gamma,\beta)=0
, or else the pair
(\beta,m(\gamma,\beta))
would be exceptional, contradicting Lemma
3.9
. Hence there is only one possible negative term in equation (
3.19
).

Case 2:
Suppose that
m_{0}>1
. Again we need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 5
. This is proved by a symmetric argument to Case 2 of the proof of Lemma
3.20
(a).

(b) Suppose that
C_{1}
has at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.20
(b), we can assume that
m_{0}=0
and that
C_{1}
has exactly two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
.

Case 1:
Suppose that
C_{1}
has two negative ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 8
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=3
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-1
. This holds if
k\geq k_{T_{q}}^{\prime\prime}
, because
p^{+}(\gamma,\beta)=0
, because otherwise the pair
(\gamma,m(\gamma,\beta))
would be exceptional, contradicting Lemma
3.9
.

Case 2:
Suppose that
C_{1}
has exactly one positive and one negative end asymptotic to covers of
\gamma
. We need to show that
T_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 7
, with equality only if
m(\gamma,\alpha)=1
.

In this case
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=2
, so we need to show that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 1
, with equality only if
m(\gamma,\alpha)=1
. By Proposition
2.16
,
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m(\gamma,\alpha))=(m(\gamma,\alpha))
and
p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\beta))=(m(\gamma,\beta))
. Thus
\gamma
is a
p^{-}
-component of
\beta
, and
\gamma
is a special component of
\alpha
if
m(\gamma,\alpha)>1
. By Lemma
2.18
(b),
\gamma
is not a
p^{+}
-component of
\beta
or a special component of
\beta
or a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
. We are now done by equation (
3.19
).
∎

3.3.3.
Nonnegativity of the score

The following proposition is our main interest in the score: it asserts that low energy curves in our
U
-sequence have nonnegative score, and in fact positive score except in a few cases that can be classified.

Lemma 3.22
.

If
k
is sufficiently large then the following holds. Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
be a
U
-curve such that
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\leq T_{q}
. Assume that:

•

\mathcal{A}(\alpha)-\mathcal{A}(\beta)<\epsilon^{\prime}
, where the constant
\epsilon^{\prime}
was specified in §
3.2.5
.

•

C_{1}
is not a cylinder.

Then:

(a)

The total score satisfies
T(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
.

(b)

If
T(\mathcal{C})=0
, then:

(1)

C_{1}
has genus zero and exactly three ends.

(2)

There are unique, distinct simple Reeb orbits
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
such that
C_{1}
has a high multiplicity positive end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{1}
and a high multiplicity negative end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{2}
.

(3)

Either
C_{1}
has an additional positive end asymptotic to a simple Reeb orbit distinct from
\gamma_{1}
, or
C_{1}
has an additional negative end asymptotic to a cover of a simple Reeb orbit distinct from
\gamma_{2}
.

(4)

If a trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
appears in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, and if
C_{1}
has an end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
, then
\gamma=\gamma_{1}
, and the multiplicity of
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{1}
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is at least
2
.

(5)

J_{0}(\mathcal{C})=2
if
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{1}
appears in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, and
J_{0}(\mathcal{C})=1
otherwise.

Proof.

Let
\alpha(1)
denote the positive orbit set of
C_{1}
, and let
\beta(1)
denote the negative orbit set of
C_{1}
. Note that
C_{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha(1),\beta(1))
is also a
U
-curve.

Suppose first that
C_{1}
has ends asymptotic to covers of at least
5
distinct simple Reeb orbits of
\lambda_{k}
. Then it follows from Remark
3.17
and Lemma
3.18
(a) that
T(\mathcal{C})\geq 3
, so the proposition is true in this case.

It remains to consider the case where
C_{1}
has ends asymptotic to covers of at most
4
distinct simple Reeb orbits, i.e.
|\alpha(1)|+|\beta(1)|\leq 4
. By Lemma
3.10
, if
k
is sufficiently large then the inequality (
3.10
) holds. Assume that
k
is sufficiently large in this sense and also sufficiently large so that Lemma
3.11
applies.
Then by Lemma
3.11
, there are distinct simple Reeb orbits
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
such that
C_{1}
has a high multiplicity positive end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{1}
and a high multiplicity negative end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{2}
. We now consider two cases, depending on whether or not
C_{1}
has ends asymptotic to covers of any additional simple Reeb orbits.

Case 1:
Suppose that
C_{1}
has an end asymptotic to a cover of a simple Reeb orbit
\gamma_{3}
distinct from
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
. Let
m_{i}
denote the multiplicity of the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{i}
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
for
i=1,2,3
. Then by Remark
3.17
and Lemmas
3.18
(a),
3.20
, and
3.21
, we have

(3.22)

\begin{split}T(\mathcal{C})&\geq 6g(C_{1})-12+T_{\gamma_{1}}(\mathcal{C})+T_{\gamma_{2}}(\mathcal{C})+T_{\gamma_{3}}(\mathcal{C})\\
&\geq 6g(C_{1})-12+5+4+3\\
&\geq 0.\end{split}

This proves (a). To prove (b), suppose that equality holds in (
3.22
). Then
g(C_{1})=0
, the curve
C_{1}
does not have ends asymptotic to covers of any orbits other than
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, or
\gamma_{3}
, and we have
T_{\gamma_{1}}(\mathcal{C})=5
,
T_{\gamma_{2}}(\mathcal{C})=4
, and
T_{\gamma_{3}}(\mathcal{C})=3
. In particular, the end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{3}
is not high multiplicity. By Lemma
3.20
,
C_{1}
has no other ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{1}
, and
m_{1}\neq 1
. By Lemma
3.21
,
C_{1}
has no other ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{2}
, and
m_{2}=0
. By Lemma
3.18
,
m_{3}=0
, and
C_{1}
has only one end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{3}
. Moreover, if this end is a positive end, then the covering multiplicity is
1
. Thus assertions (1)–(4) in the proposition hold. Assertion (5) follows from equation (
2.15
).

Case 2:
Suppose that
C_{1}
does not have ends asymptotic to covers of any simple orbits other than
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
. In this case, similarly to (
3.22
), we have

T(\mathcal{C})\geq 6g(C_{1})-3.

We can assume that
C_{1}
has genus
0
, or else
T(\mathcal{C})>0
and we are done. Thus by Remark
3.17
we have

(3.23)

T(\mathcal{C})=-12+T_{\gamma_{1}}(\mathcal{C})+T_{\gamma_{2}}(\mathcal{C}).

Since we assumed that
C_{1}
is not a cylinder, it must have at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{1}
or at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{2}
.

Suppose first that
C_{1}
has at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{1}
. Then by equation (
3.22
) and Lemmas
3.20
and
3.21
, we have
T(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
, with equality only if
T_{\gamma_{1}}(\mathcal{C})=8
and
T_{\gamma_{2}}(\mathcal{C})=4
. If the latter equalities hold, then
C_{1}
has exactly one positive end and one negative end asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{1}
, the curve
C_{1}
has only one end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{2}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{0}
does not include the trivial cylinders
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{1}
or
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{2}
. Thus
J_{0}(\mathcal{C})=1
by equation (
2.15
).

Finally suppose that
C_{1}
has at least two ends asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{2}
. Then by equation (
3.22
) and Lemmas
3.20
and
3.21
, we have
T(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
, with equality only if
T_{\gamma_{1}}(\mathcal{C})=5
and
T_{\gamma_{2}}(\mathcal{C})=7
. If the latter equalities hold, then
C_{1}
has only one end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{1}
, and
m_{1}\neq 1
; moreover
C_{1}
has exactly one positive and one negative end asymptotic to covers of
\gamma_{2}
, the covering multiplicity for the positive end is
1
, and
m_{2}=0
. Thus assertions (1)–(4) hold, and assertion (5) follows from (
2.15
) as before.
∎

3.4.
Putting it all together

We can now complete the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

3.4.1.
Finding a low energy cylinder

We first prove a lemma which provides the cylinders claimed by Proposition
3.1
. Assume that
k
is sufficiently large so that Lemma
3.12
is applicable, and let
\mathcal{C}(i)\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha(i),\alpha(i-1))
for
p_{0}<i\leq q
be a chain of
U
-curves provided by Lemma
3.12
. Below we say that
\mathcal{C}(i)
is
low energy
if

\mathcal{A}(\alpha(i))-\mathcal{A}(\alpha(i-1))<\epsilon^{\prime};

otherwise we say that
\mathcal{C}(i)
is
high energy
. Also, if we write a
U
-curve
\mathcal{C}
in the usual manner as
\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}
, then we refer to
C_{1}
as the
nontrivial component
of
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 3.23
.

If
k
is sufficiently large, then there exists
i\in\{p_{0}+1,\ldots,q\}
such that the
U
-curve
\mathcal{C}(i)
is low energy, and the nontrivial component of
\mathcal{C}(i)
is a cylinder.

Proof.

Suppose to get a contradiction that there is no
i
with
p_{0}<i\leq q
such that
\mathcal{C}(i)
is low energy and the nontrivial component of
\mathcal{C}(i)
is a cylinder. By this hypothesis and Lemma
3.22
, we can decompose

(3.24)

\{p_{0}+1,\ldots,q\}=G_{0}\sqcup G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}\sqcup G_{3}

where

\begin{split}G_{0}&=\{i\mid\text{$\mathcal{C}(i)$ is high energy}\},\\
G_{1}&=\{i\mid\text{$\mathcal{C}(i)$ is low energy, $T(\mathcal{C}(i))=0$, $J_{0}(\mathcal{C}(i))=1$}\},\\
G_{2}&=\{i\mid\text{$\mathcal{C}(i)$ is low energy, $T(\mathcal{C}(i))=0$, $J_{0}(\mathcal{C}(i))=2$}\},\\
G_{3}&=\{i\mid\text{$\mathcal{C}(i)$ is low energy, $T(\mathcal{C}(i))>0$}\}.\end{split}

The strategy will be to put bounds on the cardinalities of the above four subsets, and to contradict (
3.24
) by showing that the total cardinality is too small. We proceed in five steps.

Step 1.
We first put bounds on
|G_{0}|
.

Note that since
\mathcal{A}(\alpha(q))\leq\delta_{2}q^{1/2}
by (
3.16
), we have

(3.25)

|G_{0}|\leq\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\epsilon^{\prime}\,.

Note also that for each
i
we have the coarse bound

(3.26)

T(\mathcal{C}(i))\geq-9,

by Remark
3.17
and Lemma
3.18
.
It follows from (
3.26
) and Lemma
3.12
(a) that

(3.27)

\sum_{i\in G_{0}}T(\mathcal{C}(i))\geq-9\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\epsilon^{\prime}\,.

Step 2.
We now show that
|G_{3}|<q^{2/3}
.

To start, observe that

\displaystyle\sum^{q}_{i=p_{0}+1}T(\mathcal{C}(i))

\displaystyle=S(\alpha(q))-S(\alpha(p_{0}))+3\sum^{q}_{i=p_{0}+1}y(\mathcal{C}(i))

\displaystyle=[S(\alpha(q))-S(\alpha(p_{0}))]+3\left(-2(q-p_{0})+\sum^{q}_{i=p_{0}+1}J_{0}(\mathcal{C}(i))\right)

\displaystyle\leq[S(\alpha(q))-S(\alpha(p_{0}))]+6\delta_{1}\delta_{2}q^{1/2}

(3.28)

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\ell+6\delta_{1}\delta_{2}q^{1/2}.

Here the first line uses a telescoping sum, the second line uses the fact that
y=-2+J_{0}
, and the the third line uses the inequality (
3.15
). The fourth line holds because if
\alpha=\alpha(q)
or
\alpha=\alpha(p_{0})
then we have
|S(\alpha))|\leq 2|\alpha|
by definition, and
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\geq|\alpha|\ell
; on the other hand,
\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\leq\delta_{2}q^{1/2}
by (
3.16
).

Now by definition, if
i\in G_{3}
then
T(\mathcal{C}(i))\geq 1
, and if
i\in G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}
then
T(\mathcal{C}(i))=0
. Thus

\begin{split}|G_{3}|&\leq\sum_{i\in G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}\sqcup G_{3}}T(\mathcal{C}(i))\\
&=\sum_{i=p_{0}+1}^{q}T(\mathcal{C}(i))-\sum_{i\in G_{0}}T(\mathcal{C}(i))\\
&\leq 4\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\ell+6\delta_{1}\delta_{2}q^{1/2}+9\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\epsilon^{\prime}\\
&<q^{2/3}\end{split}

Here the second to last line uses (
3.28
) and (
3.27
), while the last line uses (
3.5
).

Step 3.
We now show that
|G_{1}|<q^{4/5}
.

To do so, we will use a slight variant of the total score. If
\mathcal{C}
is a
U
-curve, define

T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C})=2y(\mathcal{C})+S(\mathcal{C})=T(\mathcal{C})-y(\mathcal{C}).

If
i\in G_{1}
, then
T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}(i))=1
. The reason is that by the definition of
G_{1}
, we have
T(\mathcal{C}(i))=0
and
y(\mathcal{C}(i))=J_{0}(\mathcal{C}(i))-2=-1
.

To bound
T^{\prime}
of other
U
-curves, if
\gamma
is a simple Reeb orbit of
\lambda_{k}
, and if
\mathcal{C}
is a
U
-curve, then analogously to (
3.20
), define

T^{\prime}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})+2e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C}).

As in Remark
3.17
, we have

(3.29)

T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C})=4g(C_{1})-8+\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}T^{\prime}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C}).

Moreover in the sum on the right, the contribution
T^{\prime}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})
is nonzero only if
C_{1}
has an end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
. We now have the following variant of Lemma
3.18
(a):

Lemma 3.24
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}+C_{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
be a
U
-curve, and let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
. Suppose that
C_{1}
has at least one end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
. Then
T_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\mathcal{C})\geq 1
.

Proof.

By hypothesis,
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})>0
. If
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 2
, then the lemma is true automatically since
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-3
by equation (
3.19
). It therefore remains to consider the case where
e_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=1
. In this case
\mathcal{C}_{0}
does not include the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
, and
C_{1}
has exactly one end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
.

If this end is a positive end, then
\gamma
is not a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
. If
m(\gamma,\alpha)=1
, this is true by definition since
M>1
. If
m(\gamma,\alpha)>1
, then by Proposition
2.16
,
p^{+}_{\gamma}(m(\gamma,\alpha))=(m(\gamma,\alpha))
, and also
\gamma
has irrational rotation number since
\alpha
is an ECH generator. Then by Lemma
2.18
(b),
p_{\gamma}^{-}(m(\gamma,\alpha))\neq(m(\gamma,\alpha))
, so
\gamma
is not a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
. Since
\gamma
is not a
p^{-}
-component of
\alpha
, it follows from equation (
3.19
) that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
, so
T^{\prime}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})=S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})+2\geq 2
.

Suppose now that the end of
C_{1}
asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{i}
is negative. As in the previous paragraph,
\gamma
is not a
p^{+}
-component of
\beta
. It then follows from equation (
3.19
) that
S_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq-1
, so
T^{\prime}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{C})\geq 1
.
∎

It follows from equation (
3.29
) and the above lemma that every
U
-curve
\mathcal{C}
satisfies
T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C})\geq-7
.

We can now finish the proof that
|G_{1}|<q^{4/5}
. Note that similarly to (
3.28
), we have

(3.30)

\sum_{i=p_{0}+1}^{q}T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}(i))\leq 4\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\ell+4\delta_{1}\delta_{2}q^{1/2}.

Now for
i\in G_{2}
, we have
T(\mathcal{C}(i))=y(\mathcal{C}(i))=0
by definition, so
T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}(i))=0
. Thus

\begin{split}|G_{1}|&\leq\sum_{i\in G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}}T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}(i))\\
&=\sum^{q}_{i=p_{0}+1}T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}(i))-\sum_{i\in G_{0}}T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}(i))-\sum_{i\in G_{3}}T^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}(i))\\
&\leq 4\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\ell+4\delta_{1}\delta_{2}q^{1/2}+7\left(\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\epsilon^{\prime}+q^{2/3}\right)\\
&<q^{4/5}.\end{split}

Here the second to last line uses (
3.25
) and Step 2, while the last line uses the inequality (
3.6
).

Step 4.
We now show that
|G_{2}|<q^{5/6}
.

If
\alpha
is an orbit set, define
K(\alpha)\leq 0
to be minus the number of simple Reeb orbits
\gamma
such that
m(\gamma,\alpha)>1
. If
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
is a
U
-curve, define

K(\mathcal{C})=K(\alpha)-K(\beta)+2y(\mathcal{C}).

Lemma 3.25
.

If
i\in G_{2}
, then
K(\mathcal{C}(i))\geq 1
.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(i)\in\mathcal{M}^{J_{k}}(\alpha,\beta)
where
\alpha=\alpha(i)
and
\beta=\alpha(i-1)
. Since
y(\mathcal{C})=0
, we need to show that
K(\alpha)>K(\beta)
. We will adopt the notation from Lemma
3.22
. By Lemma
3.22
(b), the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{1}
appears with multiplicity at least
2
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, while there are no other trivial cylinders
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
such that
C_{1}
has an end asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma
. Thus we can remove any trivial cylinders other than
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{1}
from
\mathcal{C}_{0}
without changing
K(\mathcal{C})
, and we will assume that this has been done. There are now two cases to consider.

The first case is where
C_{1}
has two positive ends and one negative end. Then
m(\gamma_{1},\alpha)>1
, while the other positive end is asymptotic to a simple Reeb orbit by Lemma
3.22
(b)(3), so
K(\alpha)=-1
. On the other hand, since the trivial cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times\gamma_{1}
appears with multiplicity at least
2
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, and the negative end of
C_{1}
asymptotic to a cover of
\gamma_{2}\neq\gamma_{1}
is high multiplicity, we have
K(\beta)=-2
.

The second case is where
C_{1}
has one positive end and two negative ends. Then
\gamma_{1}
is the only simple Reeb orbit that appears in
\alpha
, so
K(\alpha)=-1
. On the other hand the distinct simple Reeb orbits
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
both appear in
\beta
with multiplicity greater than one, so
K(\beta)\in\{-2,-3\}
.
∎

We can now complete the proof that
|G_{2}|\leq q^{5/6}
. To start, similarly to (
3.28
), we have

(3.31)

\sum_{i=p_{0}+1}^{q}K(\mathcal{C}(i))\leq 2\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\ell+4\delta_{1}\delta_{2}q^{1/2}.

Also observe that for every
i
, we have
K(\mathcal{C}(i))\geq-8
by equation (
2.15
). Thus we have

\begin{split}|G_{2}|&\leq\sum_{i\in G_{2}}K(\mathcal{C}(i))\\
&=\sum_{i=p_{0}+1}^{q}K(\mathcal{C}(i))-\sum_{i\in G_{0}\sqcup G_{1}\sqcup G_{3}}K(\mathcal{C}(i))\\
&\leq 2\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\ell+4\delta_{1}\delta_{2}q^{1/2}+8\left(\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\epsilon^{\prime}+q^{4/5}+q^{2/3}\right)\\
&<q^{5/6}.\end{split}

Here the second to last line uses Steps 1, 2, and 3, while the last line uses the inequality (
3.7
).

Step 5.
We now complete the proof of Lemma
3.23
. By the decomposition (
3.24
) and Steps 1–4, we have

q-p_{0}<\delta_{2}q^{1/2}/\epsilon^{\prime}+q^{4/5}+q^{5/6}+q^{2/3}.

This contradicts the inequality (
3.8
).
∎

3.4.2.
Conclusion

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Continue with the setup from §
3.2.2
.

By Lemma
3.23
, if
k
is sufficiently large (we pass to a subsequence so that this is true for all
k
), there exists a
U
-curve for
(\lambda_{k},J_{k})
such that the positive orbit set has action
\leq T_{q}
, and the nontrivial component is a low energy cylinder. Choose such a
U
-curve for each
k
, and denote the nontrivial component by
C_{k}
. Let
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
denote the Reeb orbits for
\lambda_{k}
to which the positive and negative ends of
C_{k}
respectively are asymptotic.

Similarly to §
3.2.2
, it follows from Lemma
3.5
that for each
k
, there are unique Reeb orbits
\underline{\alpha_{k}}
and
\underline{\beta_{k}}
for
\lambda
that are homotopic to
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
respectively in
N
. Since there is a uniform upper bound on the action of
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
, there are only finitely many options for
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
. We can then pass to a subsequence so that there are Reeb orbits
\alpha_{\infty}
and
\beta_{\infty}
for
\lambda
such that
\underline{\alpha_{k}}=\alpha_{\infty}
and
\underline{\beta_{k}}=\beta_{\infty}
for each
k
. Then the sequence
((\lambda_{k},J_{k},\alpha_{k},\beta_{k},C_{k}))
satisfies all of the bullet points in Proposition
3.1
, as well as assertions (a) and (b). To complete the proof of Proposition
3.1
, we now prove assertions (c) and (d).

To prove assertion (c) in Proposition
3.1
, note that by Lemma
3.10
, if
k
is sufficiently large (we pass to a subsequence so that this is true for all
k
), then

(3.32)

\mathcal{A}(\alpha_{k})-\mathcal{A}(\beta_{k})>\overline{h}.

It then follows from Lemma
3.11
that the Reeb orbits
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
have multiplicity
\geq M\geq 3
. In particular, since
C_{k}
together with some trivial cylinders goes between ECH generators, the orbits
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are elliptic and so their rotation numbers are irrational.

Let
m
denote the covering multiplicity of the Reeb orbit
\alpha_{k}
, let
\gamma
denote the underlying simple Reeb orbit, and let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
denote the rotation number of
\gamma
, so that the rotation number of
\alpha_{k}
is
m\theta
. By Proposition
2.16
, we have
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m)=(m)
. Now suppose that
\alpha_{\infty}
is degenerate; then its rotation number must be
0\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Thus the rotation number of the simple orbit underlying
\alpha_{\infty}
is a rational number, which we write in lowest terms as
a/b
, and
m
is an integer multiple of
b
. Choose
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small that Example
2.15
is applicable, and also sufficiently small so that
m\varepsilon<1/2
. By Lemma
3.5
, if
k
is sufficiently large (we pass to a subsequence so that this is true for all
k
), then
|\theta-a/b|<\varepsilon
. By Example
2.15
, if
\theta-a/b\in(0,\varepsilon)
, then
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m)=(m/b,\ldots,m/b)
. Since
m\geq M
and we assumed in §
3.2.5
that
M>B_{0}
, we have
m>b
, so the partition
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m)
has at least two elements. This contradicts the fact that
p_{\gamma}^{+}(m)=(m)
. Thus we must have
\theta-a/b\in(-\varepsilon,0)
, and it follows that the rotation number of
\alpha_{k}
is
m\theta\in(-1/2,0)
.

A symmetric argument shows that if
\beta_{\infty}
is degenerate, then if
k
is sufficiently large (we pass to a subsequence so that this is true for all
k
), then the rotation number of
\beta_{k}
is in the interval
(0,1/2)

Finally, to prove assertion (d) in Proposition

, we will invoke Proposition

satisfies all of the criteria in Proposition

except possibly for (e), which we still need to show. That is, we need to show that the component of the moduli space

containing

is compact. To do so, we use a variant of the proof of
[
10

.

Suppose to get a contradiction that this moduli space is not compact. Then there must exist a sequence of elements of the moduli space that converges to a

-holomorphic building from

to
\beta_{k}
with at least two levels. No level of the building can include a holomorphic plane, because the minimum action of a Reeb orbit is greater than

which is greater than

. Hence every level of the building is a cylinder. By Proposition
2.6
, there are exactly two levels, and each level has ECH index

. Let
\gamma
denote the intermediate Reeb orbit. Since

and
\beta_{k}
are elliptic, it follows from the ECH index parity conditions discussed in §

that

is a cover of a positive hyperbolic orbit. By the partition conditions in Proposition

and Example

must be simple. By the inequality (

), at least one of the two cylinders in the building must have the property that the action difference between the Reeb orbits at the positive and negative ends is greater than

is low energy.
∎

4.
Preliminaries about holomorphic curves in symplectizations

Equipped with our Birkhoff sections for the nondegenerate perturbations
\lambda_{n}
, we now turn to the rest of the proof.
Before proceeding, we first explain the general idea behind our argument.

Outline of the argument

We have a sequence of holomorphic cylinders
C_{n}
that give a Birkhoff section for
\lambda_{n}
and we would like to prove a two or infinity result in the limit. Our basic idea for this is to obtain a compact moduli space of holomorphic cylinders in the limit. To prove this, we first prove a general automatic transversality result that holds even for degenerate contact forms as long as the curves under consideration are asymptotic to
Reeb orbits at their punctures and converge exponentially fast to them; we leverage a weighted Fredholm theory for this. Next, we need to show that the
C_{n}
give rise to curves with the required exponential convergence; this is where condition (c) in Proposition
3.1
is used.
We also obtain a kind of weak version of the usual SFT compactness theorem that suffices for our purposes. Thus, we are able to obtain a well-defined nonempty transverse moduli space of cylinders in the limit. To show that it is compact, the basic idea is as follows: a nonconvergent sequence of cylinders would give rise to an orbit as an asymptotic limit with positive intersection number with the relative homology class of the projection of the
C_{n}
; thus, close to the limit we would obtain a loop in the projection of the image of
C_{n}
with positive intersection number, and this contradicts the fact that each
C_{n}
has an embedded projection to the 3-manifold. Equipped with the necessary moduli space of cylinders in the limit, we then lift the Reeb flow to the moduli space via an evaluation map; we show that the lifted flow has an annular global cross-section, and so we are justified in applying a theorem of Franks to conclude that if the original Reeb flow had only finitely many orbits, this lifted flow would have none, hence the original flow has precisely two.

Executing the above sketch will take up the remainder of our paper.
To proceed, let

4.2.
Weighted Fredholm theory and automatic transversality

In this section, we prove what we will need to know about the Fredholm theory for a pseudo-holomorphic curve with exponential convergence to Reeb orbits at its punctures; we do not assume that the Reeb orbits at these punctures are nondegenerate.

We assume in this section that
{\widetilde{u}}
is a pseudo-holomorphic map as in (
4.2
) with an asymptotic formula at every puncture.
Assume also that
\pi_{\lambda}\circ du
does not vanish identically on
\dot{S}
.
By connectedness of
S
, the latter is equivalent to saying that
{\widetilde{u}}
is not a (possibly branched) cover of a trivial cylinder.
It follows from Carleman’s similarity principle, and the fact that
\pi_{\lambda}\circ du
satisfies a Cauchy-Riemann type equation in suitable local representations, that every zero of
\pi_{\lambda}\circ du
is isolated and contributes positively to the total algebraic count.
By Theorem
4.8
and Definition

, it follows that
{\widetilde{u}}
has a nontrivial asymptotic formula at every puncture, and that there are only finitely many zeros of
\pi_{\lambda}\circ du
.
Denote by

the asymptotic limit of
{\widetilde{u}}
at

, and by
\mu^{\pm}_{i}\in\sigma(A_{\gamma^{\pm}_{i}})
the asymptotic eigenvalue of
{\widetilde{u}}
at

.
Note that
\mu^{+}_{i}<0
and
\mu^{-}_{i}>0
.

The invariants

and

were introduced in
[

]
.
The invariant
{\mathrm{wind}}_{\pi}({\widetilde{u}})
is defined as the total algebraic count of zeros of

.
In particular

{\mathrm{wind}}_{\pi}({\widetilde{u}})=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\text{$u$ is an immersion transverse to $R$.}

A global symplectic trivialization

The invariant

Lemma 4.9
.

The inequality

2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\pi}({\widetilde{u}})\leq{\mathrm{ind}}_{\delta}({\widetilde{u}})-2+2g+\#\Gamma-\#\Gamma^{\rm odd}

In particular, if

,

and

{\mathrm{wind}}_{\pi}({\widetilde{u}})=0
and

is an immersion transverse to the Reeb vector field.
Moreover, if
g=0
,
{\mathrm{ind}}_{\delta}({\widetilde{u}})=2
and

then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\mu_{i}^{+})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma^{+}_{i}))

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\mu_{i}^{-})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\nu^{\geq\delta}(\gamma^{-}_{i}))

Proof.

Note that the identities

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\mu^{-}_{i})\geq 2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\nu^{\geq\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{-}))={\rm CZ}^{\delta}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\gamma_{i}^{-})+p^{\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{-})

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\mu^{+}_{i})\leq 2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{+}))={\rm CZ}^{-\delta}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\gamma_{i}^{+})-p^{-\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{+})

follow from the definitions of the quantities involved, and from the basic properties of spectra of asymptotic operators described before.
It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\mu^{+}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq-\#\Gamma^{\rm odd}_{+}+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\rm CZ}^{-\delta}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\gamma_{i}^{+})\,,

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\mu_{i}^{-})

\displaystyle\leq-\#\Gamma^{\rm odd}_{-}-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\rm CZ}^{\delta}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\gamma_{i}^{-})\,.

Using these identities we estimate:

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\pi}({\widetilde{u}})=2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\infty}({\widetilde{u}})-4+4g+2\#\Gamma

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\mu^{+}_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\mu^{-}_{i})\right]-4-4g+2\#\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\rm CZ}^{-\delta}_{\tau_{i}^{+}}(\gamma_{i}^{+})-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\rm CZ}^{\delta}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\gamma_{i}^{-})\right]-\#\Gamma^{\rm odd}-4+4g+2\#\Gamma

\displaystyle={\mathrm{ind}}_{\delta}({\widetilde{u}})+2-2g-\#\Gamma-\#\Gamma^{\rm odd}-4+4g+2\#\Gamma

\displaystyle={\mathrm{ind}}_{\delta}({\widetilde{u}})-2+2g+\#\Gamma-\#\Gamma^{\rm odd}

as desired.

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\mu^{-}_{i})-{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{i}^{-}}(\nu^{\geq\delta}(\gamma^{-}_{i}))

Next we discuss Fredholm theory.
Let

Lemma 4.11
.

Under the above assumptions, the identity

.

\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{+}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{E},\tau_{T})-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{-}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{E},\tau_{T})=2-2g-\#\Gamma\,,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{+}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{E},\tau_{T})+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{+}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{\xi},\tau_{N})\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{-}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{E},\tau_{T})+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{-}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{\xi},\tau_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=2-2g-\#\Gamma+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{+}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{\xi},\tau_{N})-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{-}}{\mathrm{wind}}_{\partial^{-}_{i}K_{r}}(\tau_{\xi},\tau_{N})\right)\,.

To construct a weighted Fredholm theory, Hofer, Wysocki and Zehnder consider in
[
23
]
a certain Banach space of sections of

.

\epsilon

as above, as a

A smooth Fredholm map is then defined on a neighborhood of zero of this Banach space, in such a way that exponentiating sections in its zero locus gives rise to images of curves in
\mathcal{M}_{J}
near

The linearization at zero is called the linearized Cauchy-Riemann operator at

Lemma 4.12

(Automatic transversality)
.

Assume that
g=0
,
{\mathrm{ind}}_{\delta}({\widetilde{u}})=2
and
p^{\mp\delta}(\gamma^{\pm}_{i})=1
for all
\gamma_{i}^{\pm}
.
Then
C
is immersed and
D_{\widetilde{u}}
is surjective.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
4.9
that
C
is immersed.
Hence, we can consider the functional analytic set-up from
[
23
]
as outlined above.
Let
\zeta\in\ker D_{\widetilde{u}}
be a section that does not vanish identically.
In
[
23
]
it is shown that
D_{\widetilde{u}}
is a Cauchy-Riemann type operator.
Moreover, it can be represented near a puncture
z^{\pm}_{i}
using holomorphic polar coordinates
(s,t)
as above, and the trivialization
\tau_{N}
, by
\partial_{s}+J(s,t)\partial_{t}+A(s,t)
where
-J(s,t)\partial_{t}-A(s,t)
converges as
\epsilon s\to+\infty
to a representation of the asymptotic operator

in the frame
\tau_{N}(\gamma^{\pm}_{i})
.
Hence, if we represent
\zeta
as a
\mathbb{C}
-valued function

near

using

and

[

, Theorem A.1]
or
[

to obtain an asymptotic formula

Here

satisfies
\epsilon\kappa<0
, and

is the representation in

of a corresponding eigensection.
A crucial, but simple, remark at this point is that the inequalities below follow from the definition of
\delta

\displaystyle\epsilon=+1\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{N}(\gamma^{+}_{i})}(\kappa)\leq{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{N}(\gamma^{+}_{i})}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{+}))

\displaystyle\epsilon=-1\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{N}(\gamma^{-}_{i})}(\kappa)\geq{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{N}(\gamma^{-}_{i})}(\nu^{\geq\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{-}))\,.

Standard degree theory and Lemma

together imply that the total algebraic count

of zeros of
\zeta
satisfies

\displaystyle 2\#\zeta^{-1}(0)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{N}(\gamma^{+}_{i})}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{+}))-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{N}(\gamma^{-}_{i})}(\nu^{\geq\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{-}))

\displaystyle=-4+2\#\Gamma+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{\xi}(\gamma^{+}_{i})}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{+}))-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}2{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau_{\xi}(\gamma^{-}_{i})}(\nu^{\geq\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{-}))

\displaystyle=-4+2\#\Gamma-\#\Gamma^{\rm odd}+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\rm CZ}_{\tau_{\xi}(\gamma^{+}_{i})}^{-\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{+})-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\rm CZ}_{\tau_{\xi}(\gamma^{-}_{i})}^{\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{-})

\displaystyle=-2-2+\#\Gamma+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\rm CZ}_{\tau_{\xi}(\gamma^{+}_{i})}^{-\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{+})-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}{\rm CZ}_{\tau_{\xi}(\gamma^{-}_{i})}^{\delta}(\gamma_{i}^{-})

\displaystyle=-2+{\mathrm{ind}}_{\delta}({\widetilde{u}})=0\,.

Hence, sections in
\ker D_{\widetilde{u}}
never vanish, or vanish identically.
Assume, by contradiction, that the cokernel of
D_{\widetilde{u}}
is not zero.
Then there are at least three linearly independent sections in
\ker D_{\widetilde{u}}
because the index of
D_{\widetilde{u}}
is two.
Since
N_{\widetilde{u}}
has rank two, this implies that a linear combination of them would vanish at some point.
But above we proved that the only way in which a section can vanish at some point is by vanishing identically.
This contradiction shows that
D_{\widetilde{u}}
has a trivial cokernel or, in other words, that
D_{\widetilde{u}}
is surjective.
∎

5.1.
Localizing the ends

Lemma 5.3

For every
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
-invariant open

[{\widetilde{u}},z]
follows since
T_{[{\widetilde{u}}]}\mathcal{Y}
is spanned by
\zeta_{1}+\mathbb{R}\zeta_{0}
, and
u
is an immersion.
The proof that
{\rm ev}
is a local diffeomorphism is complete.

To prove that
{\rm ev}|_{F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})}
is a proper map into
Y\setminus(x^{+}(\mathbb{R})\cup x^{-}(\mathbb{R}))
, we need to consider a sequence
[{\widetilde{u}}_{n}=(a_{n},u_{n}),z_{n}]\in\mathcal{Y}
satisfying
u_{n}(z_{n})\to p
,
p\not\in x^{+}(\mathbb{R})\cup x^{-}(\mathbb{R})
, and prove that some subsequence of
[{\widetilde{u}}_{n},z_{n}]
converges in
\mathcal{Y}
.
From the compactness of
\mathcal{Y}
given by Lemma
6.3
we can assume, up to selection of a subsequence, that
[{\widetilde{u}}_{n}]\to[{\widetilde{u}}]\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Up to reparametrizing and translating in the
\mathbb{R}
-direction, we can further assume
{\widetilde{u}}_{n}\to{\widetilde{u}}
in
C^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
.
Let
V
be a closed neighborhood of
x^{+}(\mathbb{R})\cup x^{-}(\mathbb{R})
such that
p\not\in V
.
From Lemma
6.2
we find
\underline{s}>0
such that
|s|>\underline{s}\Rightarrow u_{n}(s,t)\in V\ \forall(n,t)
.
Hence
z_{n}=(s_{n},t_{n})
satisfies
|s_{n}|\leq\underline{s}
when
n\gg 1
and, up to a subsequence, we may further assume that
z_{n}\to z_{*}
for some
z_{*}\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.
Hence
[{\widetilde{u}}_{n},z_{n}]\to[{\widetilde{u}},z_{*}]
.
∎

We can now put all of this together to prove the main theorem of our paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Recall that we are assuming that the Reeb flow of
\lambda
has finitely many simple Reeb orbits. We will now conclude that there are exactly two simple Reeb orbits.

Recall that to each Reeb orbit
\gamma=(x,T)
of
\lambda
, and each (homotopy class of) symplectic trivialization
\tau
of
\xi_{\gamma}
, there is an associated rotation number
{\rm rot}_{\tau}(\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}
uniquely characterized by

{\rm rot}_{\tau}(\gamma)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{{\rm CZ}_{\tau^{k}}(\gamma^{k})}{2k}

where
\tau^{k}
denotes the trivialization of
\xi_{\gamma^{k}}
induced from
\tau
.
It is not assumed here that
\tau
comes from a trivialization over the simple Reeb orbit underlying
\gamma
.

Denote
L=x^{+}(\mathbb{R})\cup x^{-}(\mathbb{R})
.
Denote the primitive period of
x^{+}
by
\underline{T}^{+}
, and the multiplicity of
\gamma^{+}
by
k_{+}
.
The simple Reeb orbit underlying
\gamma^{+}
is
\underline{\gamma}^{+}=(x^{+},\underline{T}^{+})
, and we have
\gamma^{+}=(\underline{\gamma}^{+})^{k_{+}}=(x^{+},T^{+}=k_{+}\underline{T}^{+})
.

Consider a Martinet tube
\Psi:U^{+}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\times B
around
\gamma^{+}
, with coordinates
(\theta,x_{1}+ix_{2})
.
If the argument of
x_{1}+ix_{2}
is denoted by
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
then, by (iv) in Lemma
6.3
, the slope of
{\widetilde{u}}
at the positive puncture with respect to coordinates
(\theta,\frac{\varphi}{2\pi})\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is
(k_{+},{\mathrm{wind}}_{\tau^{k_{+}}}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma^{+})))
, where
\tau
is the trivialization of
\xi_{\underline{\gamma}^{+}}
induced by the frame
\{\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}}\}
, and
\tau^{k_{+}}
is the trivialization of
\xi_{\gamma^{+}}
induced by
\tau
.

The Reeb vector field
R
of
\lambda
is complete on
Y\setminus L
.
For each
{\widetilde{u}}=(a,u)
that represents a curve in
\mathcal{Y}
, the
Y
-component
u
is transverse to
R
.
Hence, a trajectory of
R
has non-negative intersection number with
u
, and any intersection with
u(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
counts positively to the intersection number.
We split the remaining arguments into a few claims.

We want to lift the Reeb flow via the evaluation map, and find a global cross-section for the lifted flow. Our first claim collects what we need to know away from the boundary:

(C1)

For each open neighborhood
W
of
L
there exists
\ell_{W}>0
such that if
b-a>\ell_{W}
then for every
[{\widetilde{u}}=(a,u)]\in\mathcal{Y}
and every piece of trajectory
c:[a,b]\to Y\setminus W
we have
c([a,b])\cap u(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\neq\emptyset
.

To prove (C1), consider the map
{\rm ev}:F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})\to Y\setminus L
.
This map is a proper local diffeomorphism, by Lemma
6.4
.
Denote by
\tilde{R}
the lift of
R
by
{\rm ev}
.
Hence
\tilde{R}
is a complete vector field.
Moreover,
F:F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{Y}
is a fibration over
\mathcal{Y}
and
\tilde{R}
is transverse to the fibers.
Therefore we can find a global parameter
s\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
on
\mathcal{Y}
satisfying
\iota_{\tilde{R}}d(s\circ F)>0
.
Since
Y\setminus W
is compact in
Y\setminus L
,
{\rm ev}^{-1}(Y\setminus W)
is a compact subset of
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})
.
Let
\Delta
be the infimum of
\iota_{\tilde{R}}d(s\circ F)
on
{\rm ev}^{-1}(Y\setminus W)
.
By compactness, this infimum is a minimum.
Hence,
\Delta>0
.
Let
c:[a,b]\to Y\setminus W
be a piece of a trajectory of
R
.
Consider a lift
\tilde{c}:[a,b]\to{\rm ev}^{-1}(Y\setminus W)
of
c
by
{\rm ev}
.
Then
\tilde{c}
is a piece of trajectory
\tilde{R}
.
Moreover, a lift
[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
of
s\circ F\circ\tilde{c}
oscillates more than
\Delta(b-a)
.
This oscillation is strictly larger than
1
if
b-a>\Delta^{-1}
.
Hence,
\tilde{c}
intersects every fiber of
F
when
b-a>\Delta^{-1}
.
Claim (C1) is proved if we set
\ell_{W}=\Delta^{-1}
.

Our next claim collects what we need to know near the boundary.

(C2)

There is a neighborhood
W_{*}
of
L
and
\ell_{*}>0
such that if
b-a>\ell_{*}
then for every
[{\widetilde{u}}=(a,u)]\in\mathcal{Y}
and every piece of Reeb trajectory
c:[a,b]\to W_{*}
we have
c([a,b])\cap u(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\neq\emptyset
.

We prove (C2).
For every
T>0
there is an open neighborhood
W_{*}^{+}=W_{*}^{+}(T)
of
x^{+}(\mathbb{R})
with coordinates
(\theta,z=x_{1}+ix_{2}=|z|e^{i\varphi})\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{C}
such that:

•

x^{+}(t)=(t/\underline{T}^{+},0)
.

•

d\theta\wedge dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}
is positive relatively to
\lambda\wedge d\lambda
.

•

For every piece Reeb trajectory of time-length
T
contained in
W_{*}^{+}
, a lift to
\mathbb{R}
of
\varphi/2\pi
along the trajectory oscillates not less than
{\rm rot}(\underline{\gamma}^{+})\lfloor T/\underline{T}^{+}\rfloor-1
.

Here
{\rm rot}(\underline{\gamma}^{+})
is computed with respect to the frame
\{\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}}\}
.
As remarked above, for an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{Y}
represented by a pseudo-holomorphic map
{\widetilde{u}}=(a,u)
, if
\rho\gg 1
the loop
t\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\mapsto u(\rho,t)\in W_{*}^{+}\setminus x^{+}(\mathbb{R})
has slope
(k_{+},{\mathrm{wind}}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{+}))
in the basis
\{d\theta,\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\}
.
Here the winding is computed in a trivialization aligned with the frame
\{\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}}\}
.
Hence, the argument of a Reeb trajectory of time-length
T
contained in
W_{*}^{+}
relative to
u([\rho,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
has a lift to
\mathbb{R}
that oscillates

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=2\pi\left({\rm rot}(\underline{\gamma}^{+})-\frac{{\mathrm{wind}}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{+}))}{k_{+}}\right)\lfloor T/\underline{T}^{+}\rfloor-O(1)

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(\frac{{\rm rot}(\gamma^{+})-{\mathrm{wind}}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{+}))}{k_{+}}\right)\lfloor T/\underline{T}^{+}\rfloor-O(1)\ \,.

Here
O(1)
denotes a positive constant independent of
T
and of
{\widetilde{u}}
.
We know that

{\rm rot}(\gamma^{+})>{\mathrm{wind}}(\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{+}))

since
\nu^{<-\delta}(\gamma_{+})
is a negative eigenvalue of the asymptotic operator at
\gamma_{+}
.
Hence, we can fix
T
such that
D>3\pi
, and conclude that any piece of Reeb trajectory contained in
W_{*}^{+}=W_{*}^{+}(T)
of time-length
T
intersects the image of
u
.
An analogous argument at the negative puncture will produce a neighborhood
W_{*}^{-}
of
x^{-}(\mathbb{R})
with similar properties.
Take
W_{*}=W_{*}^{+}\cup W_{*}^{-}
and (C2) is proved.

We now put (C1) and (C2) together to prove the following:

(C3)

There exists
\ell>0
such that if
b-a>\ell
then for every piece of Reeb trajectory
c:[a,b]\to Y\setminus L
and every
[{\widetilde{u}}=(a,u)]\in\mathcal{Y}
we have
c([a,b])\cap u(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\neq\emptyset
.

Let
\phi^{t}
denote the Reeb flow of
\lambda
.
To prove (C3), consider a neighborhood
W_{*}
of
L
and
\ell_{*}>0
given by (C2). Let
W\subset W_{*}
be a neighborhood of
L
such that if
p\in W
then
\phi^{[0,\ell_{*}+1]}(p)\subset W_{*}
.
Let
\ell_{W}>0
be given by (C1).
Consider
p\in Y\setminus L
arbitrary.
If
\phi^{[0,\ell_{W}+1]}(p)\cap W=\emptyset
then, by (C1),
\phi^{[0,\ell_{W}+1]}(p)
intersects the projection to
Y
of every curve in
\mathcal{Y}
.
If
\phi^{[0,\ell_{W}+1]}(p)\cap W\neq\emptyset
then we have a piece of trajectory inside
\phi^{[0,\ell_{W}+\ell_{*}+2]}(p)
of time-length
\ell_{*}+1
that is contained in
W_{*}
.
By (C2) it must intersect the projection to
Y
of every curve in
\mathcal{Y}
.
We proved that one can take
\ell=\ell_{W}+\ell_{*}+2
for (C3).

Let us now complete the proof.
Lift the Reeb flow
\phi^{t}
of
\lambda
from
Y\setminus L
to a flow
\psi^{t}
on
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})
via the proper local diffeomorphism
{\rm ev}|_{F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})}
.
By Lemma
6.3
, the flow
\psi^{t}
is transverse to the fibers of the fibration
F:F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{Y}
.
By (C3) each fiber is a global cross-section for
\psi^{t}
.
Lemma
6.3
(i) also tells us that the
\phi^{t}
-invariant
2
-form
d\lambda
lifts to a
\psi^{t}
-invariant
2
-form on
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})
that defines an area-form of finite total area on each fiber of the map
F
.
Franks’ results from
[
14
]
guarantee that
\psi^{t}
has either infinitely many simple periodic orbits, or no simple periodic orbits at all.

Periodic orbits of
\psi^{t}
are mapped onto periodic orbit of
\phi^{t}
by
F
.
Conversely, properness of the local diffeomorphism
{\rm ev}|_{F^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})}
implies that a periodic orbit of
\phi^{t}
in
Y\setminus L
lifts to finitely many periodic orbits of
\psi^{t}
.
It follows that
\psi^{t}
has finitely many simple periodic orbits, hence no periodic orbits at all.
This implies that
\phi^{t}
has no periodic orbits on
Y\setminus L
.
∎
