Static Hovering Realization for Multirotor Aerial Vehicles with Tiltable Propellers
Mahmoud Hamandi, Lakmal Seneviratne, Yahya Zweiri

Abstract
This paper presents a theoretical study on the ability of multi-rotor aerial vehicles (MRAVs) with tiltable propellers to achieve and sustain static hovering at different orientations. To analyze the ability of MRAVs with tiltable propellers to achieve static hovering, a novel linear map between the platform’s control inputs and applied forces and moments is introduced. The relation between the introduced map and the platform’s ability to hover at different orientations is developed. Correspondingly, the conditions for MRAVs with tiltable propellers to realize and sustain static hovering are detailed. A numerical metric is then introduced, which reflects the ability of MRAVs to sustain static hovering at different orientations. A subclass of MRAVs with tiltable propellers is defined as the Critically Statically Hoverable platforms (CSH), where CSH platforms are MRAVs that cannot sustain static hovering with fixed propellers, but can achieve static hovering with tilting propellers. Finally, extensive simulations are conducted to test and validate the above findings, and to demonstrate the effect of the proposed numerical metric on the platform’s dynamics.

Introduction

Multi-Rotor Aerial Vehicles (MRAVs) have been widely studied in the literature in the last decade. These platforms have seen an increase in their actuation abilities from the first presented designs
[
1
]
demonstrating the ability to achieve static hovering, to modern platforms able to apply independent forces and moments
[
2
]
in all directions, or achieve omnidirectinal flight
[
3
,
4
]
.
This increase in abilities allowed new applications to emerge that were previously not possible with fixed wing Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAV)s
[
5
]
, most notably, Aerial Physical Interaction
[
6
,
7
]
and Human Robot Interaction
[
8
]
.

The majority of MRAV solutions rely on designs with fixed propellers (
i.e.
, the direction of thrust generated by each propeller is constant with respect to the body frame). Platforms with fixed propellers require less moving parts, and are thus easier to design, build and maintain. However, and as shown in
[
9
]
, platforms with tiltable propellers could achieve desired actuation properties with fewer propellers. Therefore, they provide an interesting solution to many UAV applications
[
10
]
. In addition, since these platforms can achieve desired actuation properties with fewer propellers, they could withstand propeller failures, and maintain hover after the failure of one or more propellers.

In this perspective, there has been multiple prototype platforms in the literature that demonstrate the actuation abilities of MRAVs with tiltable propellers, with two
[
11
]
, three
[
12
]
, four
[
13
]
and more propellers
[
14
]
. However, it is noted that each presented prototype proposes different strategies for actuation allocation,
i.e.
, for mapping and allocating the platform’s controls to achieve the desired forces and moments.
For example, the authors in
[
15
]
study the effect of changes in propellers’ orientations and thrusts
on each force or moment axis, while operating near hover.
While such a method allowed earlier platforms with actuated propellers to achieve static hovering and follow desired trajectories, they do not easily generalize to different platforms.
On the other hand, the authors in
[
16
]
propose a direct allocation method, where a linear map is proposed between the platform’s wrench (
i.e.
, forces and moments) and the three-dimensional thrust generated by each propeller.
The authors in
[
17
]
propose to control platforms with tiltable propellers at a higher differential level, where they propose a linear map between the wrench derivative and the derivative of the propellers’ thrusts and orientations. It is noted that the linear map in
[
17
]
is variable, as it depends on the current control inputs, while the one from
[
16
]
is fixed. On the other hand, the map from
[
17
]
can be derived for any MRAV with tiltable propellers, while the one from
[
16
]
can only encompass platforms with propellers tilting in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Multiple papers from the literature analyzed thoroughly the ability of platforms with fixed propellers to hover at different orientations
[
18
]
, achieve omnidirectional flight
[
9
]
, and hover following the failure of some of their propellers
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
The authors in
[
22
]
analyze the hovering efficiency of different platforms, and show the ability of their proposed tiltrotor platform to achieve omnidirectional flight, with a higher efficiency than other fixed omnidirectional platforms from the literature.
While the advantage of MRAVs with tilting propellers is clear from the corresponding designs in the literature (example
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
), the analysis of the ability of generic MRAV with tiltable propellers to achieve static hovering, and sustain static hovering is still missing in the literature.
The work in
[
9
]
and
[
25
]
discuss such abilities for both fixed and actuated propellers; however, their formulation is generic to both types of platforms, and as such, does not cover comprehensively the effect of propeller tilting on the platform’s ability to hover.

This paper addresses this gap, and presents a comprehensive formulation that reflects the static hovering ability of any MRAV with tiltable propellers at different orientations. Then at each possible orientation, we analyze the stability of the corresponding hover,
i.e.
, the ability to sustain achieved hovering in the presence of external disturbances. The main novel contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

Introduce a generic linear map between the wrench and controls for any tiltable MRAV that reflects all hovering orientations.

2.

Analyze the ability of tiltable MRAVs to achieve static hovering at different orientations.

3.

Analyze the ability of tiltable MRAVs to sustain static hovering at different orientations,
i.e.
, the ability of tiltable MRAV to sustain static hovering while tilting between different propeller configurations. Introduce a numerical metric that reflects the corresponding ability.

4.

Introduce a subclass of MRAVs with tiltable propellers
that cannot sustain static hovering with fixed propellers, but achieve static hovering with tilting propellers. We refer to platforms in this class as Critically Statically Hoverable (CSH) platforms.

5.

Demonstrate the static hovering ability and fail-safe robustness of the proposed CSH platforms in extensive simulation.

6.

Demonstrate in simulation the effect of the proposed numerical metric on the platform’s dynamic response at different hovering orientations.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section
2
summarizes the required definitions for the discussion of this paper, and introduces the allocation map for generic MRAVs with tiltable propellers. Section
3
discusses static hovering of generic MRAVs and the corresponding actuation properties. Section
4
introduces Critically Statically Hoverable platforms, and demonstrates with a few examples how such platforms can achieve different actuation properties. Section
5
tests and validates the theoretical findings with extensive simulations, and finally, Section
6
concludes the paper.

,

2.1
Allocation Strategies

In this paper, we refer to an allocation strategy, or allocation map, as any mapping between the platform’s control inputs and the applied wrench. Since we are introducing two control inputs, such mapping could be in one of the following forms, which will be discussed below:

1.

\bm{H}\rightarrow\bm{w}
; or

2.

\bm{V}\rightarrow\bm{w}.

First, to illustrate the effect of the allocated wrench
\bm{w}
on the platform’s dynamics, let us write the dynamic equation of motion of a multirotor platform following the Newton-Euler formalism as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}m\dot{\bm{v}}_{B}\\
\bm{J}_{B}\dot{\bm{\omega}}_{B}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}mg\bm{z}_{W}\\
\bm{\omega}_{B}\times\bm{J}_{B}\bm{\omega}_{B}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{G}\bm{w}(\bm{u},\bm{\Omega})

(5)

where
m\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\bm{J}_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{>0}
are the platform mass and inertia tensor with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{B}
,
g
is the gravitational constant,
\dot{\bm{v}}_{B},\dot{\bm{\omega}}_{B},\bm{\omega}_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are respectively the platform linear acceleration in
\mathcal{F}_{W}
and the angular acceleration and velocity in
\mathcal{F}_{B}
, and
\bm{G}
is a transformation matrix as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{G}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{3}&\bm{0}_{3\times 3}\\
\bm{0}_{3\times 3}&\bm{R}_{B}\end{bmatrix}

(6)

where
\bm{R}_{B}
is the rotation matrix that brings a vector in
\mathcal{F}_{B}
to
\mathcal{F}_{W}
.

Let us first look at the mapping from
\bm{H}
to the platform wrench
\bm{w}

(7)

where

is the fixed full allocation matrix; this allocation matrix is fixed for any control inputs, however, it is only valid for platforms with fixed propellers. The authors in

]
also show that the allocation matrix in Eq. (

\bm{F}(\bm{H})
for a platform with tiltable propellers. However, the generic allocation matrix maps the derivative of the control input to the derivative of the wrench as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{w}}(\bm{u},\bm{\Omega},\dot{\bm{u}},\dot{\bm{\Omega}})=\bm{F}(\bm{H})\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\bm{u}}\\
\dot{\bm{\Omega}}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\frac{\delta\bm{w}}{\delta\bm{u}}\;\frac{\delta\bm{w}}{\delta\bm{\Omega}}\right]_{\begin{pmatrix}{\scriptstyle\bm{u}}\\
{\scriptstyle\bm{\Omega}}\end{pmatrix}}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\bm{u}}\\
\dot{\bm{\Omega}}\end{bmatrix}

where

is referred to as the vector allocation matrix.
Note that the allocation strategy in Eq. (

. Other formulations presented in the literature can reflect partial control of the propellers’ orientation, such as the one presented for the hexarotor with tilting propellers in

Let us modify the matrix

depending on whether each of its propellers can be tilted about two axes, one axis or no axes. In the latter, the corresponding vector

is fixed, and thus each matrix

Finally, let us define the control input corresponding to

Similarly, we define the force set at hover

as the set of forces that can be applied by the platform while applying zero-moment:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{\bm{f}=\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}\;|\;\forall\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\;s.t.\;\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}=\bm{0}\}

and

Finally, we define the moment set at hover
\mathcal{M}
as the set of moments that can be applied by the platform while applying a force that counteracts gravity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\{\bm{m}=\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}\;|\;\forall\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\;s.t.\;\lVert\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}\rVert\geq mg\}

While the authors in

]

to understand the properties and abilities of any MRAV, it is easy to see that the full allocation matrix changes depending on the operating point, and as such, in the case of tiltable propellers

, where

is the set of feasible wrench. In fact, the set of feasible wrench can be described as follows:
{\mathcal{W}}:=\{\Image(\bm{F}(\bm{H})\;\forall\bm{H}\in\mathcal{C}\}
, where

is the full allocation matrix from Eq. (

. On the other hand, it is easy to see that for any platform (fixed or actuated)

3
STATIC HOVERING

In what follows we will focus on the hovering ability of a tiltable MRAV.
Let us first define the static hovering ability, as the ability of the platform to stabilize its position at a certain altitude, with negligible linear and angular velocities as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{B}\rightarrow\bm{p}_{B}^{d},\;\bm{R}_{B}\rightarrow\bm{R}_{B}^{h};\;{\bm{v}}_{B}\rightarrow\bm{0};\;\bm{\omega}_{B}\rightarrow\bm{0}

(14)

where
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}
is a constant hovering orientation such that
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\in\mathcal{R}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is the set of orientations for which static hovering is possible.

Static hovering should be differentiated from dynamic hovering, where the platform can reach a desired position while varying its orientation, and with a non negligible linear and angular velocity. An example of a platform that can achieve dynamic hovering was presented in
[
27
]
.

Conditions for static hovering were presented in
[
18
]
for platforms with fixed propellers. Since
\Image(\bm{A}^{\prime}):=\mathcal{W}
, conditions similar to
[
18
]
can be derived while relying on the vector allocation matrix
\bm{A}^{\prime}
as follows:

\displaystyle\rank(\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime})=3

(15)

\displaystyle\exists\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\interior(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})\;s.t.\;\;\bigg\{\begin{matrix}\lVert\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}\rVert&\geq&mg\\
\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}&=&\bm{0}\end{matrix}

(16)

where following Eq. (
16
), if the platform is able to apply a force magnitude larger than its weight, the platform will be able to achieve static hovering at any orientation
\mathcal{R}
such that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\{\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\;|\;\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}{\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}}\bm{V}^{\prime}\geq\bm{R}_{B}^{h}{\bm{\bm{z}_{W}}}mg\}

(17)

where
\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}
is the basis of the null space of
\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime}
.
Let us note that
\forall\bm{R}_{B}^{T}\in\mathcal{R}
,
\bm{R}_{B}^{T}\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}\propto\bm{z}_{W}
, and define the set of hovering orientations as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\;\text{s.t.}\;\bm{d}=\bm{R}_{B}\bm{z}_{W}\propto\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}\;\;\forall\bm{R}_{B}\in\mathcal{R}\}

(18)

Remark 2
.

Note that multiple platform orientations
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\in\mathcal{R}
could correspond to the same hovering orientation
\bm{d}\in\mathcal{Z}
. For example, for a classical quadrotor,
\bm{d}=\bm{z}_{B}
corresponds to the set of rotation matrices
\bm{R}_{z}(\gamma_{i})\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\forall\gamma_{i}\;\in[-\pi,\pi]
.

The conditions (
15
), (
16
) are equivalent to the condition below
[
19
]
:

Property 1
.

A platform is capable of static hovering if and only if
\bm{0}\in\interior(\mathcal{M})

While intuitively for a platform to be able to hover it should only be able to lift its weight while applying zero moment, the condition of
\bm{0}\in\interior(\mathcal{M})
is necessary so that the platform can reorient itself and counteract any disturbances.

As seen above, the
\bm{A}^{\prime}
matrix allows the analysis of all the forces and moments the platform could reach, and correspondingly, allows the analysis of the hovering ability of the platform.
However, with platforms with tiltable propellers, it is important to take into consideration the propeller re-orientation dynamics. As such, it is important to understand, at each hovering orientation
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}
the ability of the platform to reorient itself and counteract disturbances, which will be discussed in subsection
3.2
.

3.1
Static Hovering Realization

In this subsection, we aim to characterize the possible static hovering orientations,
i.e.
, the dimensionality of
\mathcal{Z}
. The authors in
[
9
]
claimed that for any platform,
\mathcal{Z}
can be fully characterized from the full allocation matrix
\bm{F}
, and the set of allowable control inputs
\mathcal{C}
. As discussed in section
2.1
, while
\Image(\bm{F}_{fixed}):=\mathcal{W}
in the case of fixed propellers,
\Image(\bm{F}):\neq\mathcal{W}
in the case of tiltable propellers. Hence, in what follows we will develop the conditions for static hovering realization for any MRAV while relying on the vector allocation matrix
\bm{A}^{\prime}
.

First, let us group platforms’ based on the dimensionality of
\mathcal{Z}
as follows:

1.

Unidirectional Thrust (UDT)

2.

Multidirectional Thrust (MDT)

3.

Fully Actuated (FA)

4.

Omnidirectionl (OD)

Let us note that each platform is assumed to be able to apply moments in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to balance its attitude. A necessary condition for this assumption is as follows:

Assumption 1
.

Any MRAV studied in this paper can change its attitude in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with its vector allocation matrix satisfying:

\displaystyle\rank(\bm{A_{m}}^{\prime})=3

(19)

Proposition 1
.

\dim{(\mathcal{Z})}\leq\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

The proof of proposition
1
follows from the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
as follows:

{\dim{(\mathcal{Z})}=\dim(\Image(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}))\leq\dim(\Image(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}))=\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})}
.
∎

Following proposition
1
, we can define the properties of the platform groups as follows:

Definition 2
.

A platform is said to be a unidirectional thrust platform (UDT) if its thrust can be varied along one direction while hovering as follows:

\displaystyle\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})=1\;\text{and}\;\rank(\bm{A}^{\prime})=4

(20)

Correspondingly, this platform has a unique hovering orientation direction.

Definition 3
.

A platform is said to be a multidirectional thrust platform (MDT) if its thrust can be varied in a plane while hovering as follows:

\displaystyle 2\leq\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})\leq 3\;\text{and}\;5\leq\rank(\bm{A}^{\prime})\leq 6

(21)

Correspondingly, this platform can achieve static hovering in a plane.

Definition 4
.

A platform is said to be a fully actuated platform (FA) if its thrust can be varied in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
while hovering as follows:

\displaystyle\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})=3\;\text{and}\;\rank(\bm{A}^{\prime})=6

(22)

Correspondingly, this platform can achieve static hovering in a variety of orientations in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 5
.

A platform is said to be an omnidirectional platform (OD) if it can hover in all directions in a sphere around its CoM as follows:

\displaystyle\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})=3\;\text{and}\;\rank(\bm{A}^{\prime})=6

\displaystyle and\;\forall\bar{\bm{f}}\in\text{ball}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\bm{0},mg),\;\bar{\bm{f}}\in\interior(\mathcal{F})

(23)

where
\text{ball}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\bm{0},mg)
refers to a topological ball centered about the origin
\bm{0}
with radius
mg
.

As proposed in
[
25
]
, to understand numerically if a platform is OD (
i.e.
, satisfies condition (
23
)), it is enough to compare the platform’s Omnidirectional Lift (ODL) to its weight.
The ODL was defined as the radius of the maximum inscribed sphere inside of the platform’s set of feasible forces
\mathcal{F}
. As such, condition (
23
) can be modified as follows:

Definition 6
.

A platform is said to be an omnidirectional platform (OD) iff:

\displaystyle\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})=3\;

\displaystyle\text{and}\;\rank(\bm{A}^{\prime})=6

ODL

\displaystyle\geq mg

(24)

As seen above, to understand the dimensionality of
\mathcal{Z}
for a MRAV, it is enough to analyze it’s vector allocation matrix
\bm{A}^{\prime}
. On the other hand, the OD property requires analysis of the feasible force set in addition to the analysis of the vector allocation matrix.
However, note that the analysis of the dimensionality of
\mathcal{Z}
assumed that the corresponding platform can achieve static hovering; as shown in Eqs. (
15
), (
16
), one of the conditions for static hovering is the platform’s ability to apply forces larger than its weight. Consequently, while the definitions (
3
)-(
4
) do not explicitly analyze the platform’s set of feasible controls
\mathcal{C}
, the necessary hovering ability inherently requires the analysis of the platform’s actuation limits.

3.2
Static Hovering Sustainability

In the case of platforms with tiltable propellers, we refer to static hovering sustainability as the platform’s ability to maintain already achieved static hovering. In this section we will analyze the ability of the platform to hover in the neighborhood of a hovering orientation, the ability of the platform to maintain hovering with fixed propellers’ orientations, and the ability of the platform to change from one hovering orientation to another.

First, let us assume that for any hovering orientation
\bm{d}_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}
, there exists a control input
{\bm{H}_{0}=(\bm{u}_{0},\bm{\Omega}_{0})\in\interior(\mathcal{C})}
that achieves the hovering conditions (
15
), (
16
). Note that
\bm{H}_{0}\in\interior(\mathcal{C})
can be uniquely mapped to a
\bm{V}_{0}^{\prime}\in\interior(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
.
Then, let us define the set
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
as the set of hovering orientations in the neighborhood of
\bm{d}_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{0}=\{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\;\text{s.t}\;\bm{d}\in\text{ball}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\bm{d}_{0},\eta)\}

(25)

where
\eta
represents a small orientation change.

Proposition 2
.

If the platform is FA, then
\exists\eta
such that static hovering can be achieved
\forall\bm{d}\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
.

Proof.

First, let us note that for any control input
\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
,
for a FA platform,
\Image(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then
\forall\bm{d}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,

{\exists\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\;\text{s.t.}\;\bm{d}\propto\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}}
.
Then, let us decompose
\bm{d}\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
as
\bm{d}=\bm{d}_{0}+\delta\bm{d}
. Static hovering can be achieved for any
\eta
such that:

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}(\bm{V}^{\prime}+\delta\bm{V}^{\prime})\geq mg(\bm{d}+\delta\bm{d})

(26)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\bm{V}^{\prime}+\delta\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\delta\bm{d}\in\text{ball}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\bm{0},\eta)

where
\delta\bm{V}^{\prime}
is a change in the control input
\bm{V}^{\prime}
required to make the change in hovering orientation.

Let us assume
{\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}=mg\bm{d}}
, and rewrite Eq. (
26
) as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}^{\prime}\delta\bm{V}^{\prime}\geq mg\delta\bm{d}

(27)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\bm{V}^{\prime}+\delta\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\delta\bm{d}\in\text{ball}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\bm{0},\eta)

Since the platform if FA, and from the continuity of
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, there exists
\eta
that satisfied Eq. (
27
).
∎

While proposition
2
holds for any FA platform, such guarantees do not exist for UDT or MDT platforms.

While FA platforms can sustain static hovering for any orientation in
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
, it is important to analyze the ability of MRAV with tiltable propellers to sustain static hovering after the failure of their servomorotrs,
i.e.
, if the propellers’ orientations are fixed at
\bm{\Omega}_{0}
.

Proposition 3
.

Any MRAV platform with
N\leq 3
cannot sustain static hovering with fixed propellers’ orientations
\bm{\Omega}=\bm{\Omega}_{0}
.

Proof.

With fixed propellers, the matrix
\bm{A}^{\prime}
is reduced such that
{\bm{A}_{fixed}^{\prime}=\bm{F}_{fixed}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times N}}
with a corresponding controls input
\bm{u}
; as such
\rank(\bm{A}_{fixed}^{\prime})\leq N
.
With a control input
\bm{H}_{0}
, the platform is capable of applying a force that can counteract gravity, while applying zero-moment.
It is easy to see that any control input
\bm{u}=k\bm{u}_{0}
, with
k\in\mathbb{R}
being a random scalar, can still achieve the hovering conditions (
16
) necessary to achieve hovering.
Accordingly, at a fixed
\bm{\Omega}_{0}
,
\rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m})\geq 1
.

Rewriting the above inequality,

\displaystyle rank(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m})=N-rank(\bm{A}_{m})^{\prime}-dim\left(ker(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})\cap ker(\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime})\right)\geq 1

(28)

\displaystyle rank(\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime})\leq N-1-dim\left(ker(\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime})\cap ker(\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime})\right)

(29)

In the case where
N\leq 3
,
\rank(\bm{A}_{m}^{\prime})\leq 2
, and as such, the platform cannot sustain static hovering with fixed propellers’ orientations
\bm{\Omega}=\bm{\Omega}_{0}
as it cannot achieve the conditions from Eqs. (
15
), (
16
) at the same time.
∎

Following proposition
2
and the consequent proof, it is important to analyze for any MRAV with tiltable propellers the effect of changes in its control input
\bm{V}^{\prime}
on the applied wrench. As such, let us define the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{0}^{\prime}=\{\forall\bm{V}^{\prime}|\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\;and\;\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\text{ball}(\bm{V}_{0}^{\prime},\epsilon)\}

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}=\{\forall\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\;s.t.\;\bm{w}=\bm{A}^{\prime}\bm{V}^{\prime}\;\forall\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}^{\prime}\}

(31)

where
\text{ball}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\bm{V}_{0}^{\prime},\epsilon)
refers to a topological ball centered about the origin
\bm{V}_{0}^{\prime}
with radius
\epsilon
and where
\epsilon
is a small tolerance. For convenience, let us denote by
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{0}
as the set of moments and forces corresponding to
\mathcal{W}_{0}
.

To characterize the stability of the platform’s static hovering at a hovering orientation
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\in\mathcal{R}
, it is required to understand the force and moment components of the set
\mathcal{W}_{0}
; for simplicity, let us assume that the produced force inside
\mathcal{W}_{0}
to be relatively close to the hovering force as assumed in
[
19
]
. However, the analysis of the feasible moments inside the corresponding set is still required to understand if the platform can change its hovering orientation and to counteract external disturbances.
Let us denote the moment set corresponding to
\mathcal{W}_{0}
by
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

To be able to numerically characterize the stability of each hovering orientation
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\in\mathcal{R}
, let us introduce the Local Hoverability Index (LHI) as the maximum moment the platform can apply in any direction at any hovering orientation
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\in\mathcal{R}
:

\displaystyle\text{LHI}=\argmax_{d}\;\text{ball}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\bm{0},d)\in\mathcal{M}_{0}

(32)

To ease the computation of the LHI, in what follows we will discuss how to compute it from the full allocation matrix
\bm{F}
and the derivative of the control inputs
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proposition 4
.

\forall\bm{V}^{\prime}\in\interior(\mathcal{V}_{0}^{\prime})
, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
,
\rank(\bm{F})=\rank(\bm{A}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

As
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we can linearize the applied wrench with respect to
\bm{H}_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{w}

\displaystyle=\bm{w}_{0}+\left.\frac{d\bm{w}}{d\bm{H}}\right|_{\bm{H}_{0}}(\bm{H}-\bm{H}_{0})

(33)

\displaystyle\bm{w}

\displaystyle=\bm{w}_{0}+\bm{F}(\bm{H}_{0})\;\dot{\bm{H}}dt

where
\bm{w}_{0}
is the wrench applied by the control input
\bm{V}_{0}^{\prime}
.
Correspondingly,
\mathcal{W}_{0}
can be expressed as a codomain of
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}=\{\bm{w}|\bm{w}={\bm{w}_{0}}+\bm{F}(\bm{H}_{0})\dot{\bm{H}}dt\;\forall\dot{\bm{H}}\in\dot{\mathcal{C}}\}

(34)

where
dt\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a time constant used for calculation purposes.
Consequently, we can see from Eq. (
31
) and Eq. (
34
) that

\mathcal{W}_{0}:=\Image(\bm{F}(\bm{H}_{0}))=\Image(\bm{A}^{\prime})\;\forall\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\in\mathcal{R}
, and as such, proposition
4
holds.
∎

Following the above proposition, and the corresponding proof, we can see that computing the LHI from
\bm{F}
provides the same
\mathcal{M}_{0}
that could have been computed from
\bm{A}^{\prime}
.

Finally, we assume
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
to be fixed for each actuator type. While this assumption and the introduction of Eq. (
34
) allow for ease of computation of
\mathcal{W}_{0}
, we do note that in reality
\dot{\mathcal{C}}
changes depending on
\bm{H}_{0}
. Consequently, the corresponding LHI can only be used as a relative metric that compares the hovering stability of one platform with respect to another, or the hovering stability of different hovering orientations
\bm{R}_{B}^{h}\in\mathcal{R}
for the same platform.

4
Critically Statically Hoverable Platforms

In what follows, we refer to Critically Statically Hoverable (CSH) platforms as any platform that can statically hover, while achieving one of the actuation properties UDT to OD as long as it can actuate its propellers, while it cannot statically hover with its propellers fixed.
While this condition can occur with
N\geq 4
, it can easily be avoided (for example, the classical quadrotor has
N=4
and can hover without any propeller tilting). On the other hand, any MRAV with
N\leq 3
, and as demonstrated in proposition
3
, cannot hover unless its propellers are actuated. As such, and following the previous propositions, we can state the following Lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Any multirotor aerial vehicle, with
N\leq 3
that can achieve static hovering is a Critically Statically Hoverable platform.

In what follows we will analyze some example CSH platforms with
N\leq 3
, to demonstrate how such platforms can achieve actuation properties UDT to OD; note that in the following examples we will focus on the minimal configuration that can achieve each corresponding actuation property.

4.1
UDT CSH platforms

This class describes platforms that can achieve static hovering while applying forces along one direction. This class is similar in actuation properties to the classical quadrotor platform.
This class can be achieved with
N=3
and
N=2
, where the former requires one of the propellers to be actuated along a single orientation, while the later requires both propellers to be actuated, each along a single orientation.
With such platforms,
\bm{A}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times 4}
; as such, the basis of the null space of the moment set
\bm{B}_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4\times 1}

\bm{z}_{B}^{0}=\bm{A}_{f}^{\prime}\bm{B}_{m}
.

An example platform with

It is easy to see that such a platform can hover about a single orientation, such that:

Similarly, such platforms with

MDT CSH platforms

This class describes platforms that can achieve static hovering while applying forces along a plane.
Such platforms are rarely seen in the literature, however, they can be achieved with

4.3
FA and OD CSH platforms

In this subsection we discuss both FA and OD CSH platforms. First let us note that to achieve FA a platform with

4.4
Weight Concern

While adding servomotors to the platform allows the increase of its
DoF
, and allow a platform with
N=2
or
3
to achieve UDT, MDT, FA and OD, we have to note that adding such servomotors adds additional weight to the platform.
The added weight comes from the servomotor weight, the platform design that allows the incorporation of the servomotor, and any additional mechanism necessary to transfer the rotation motion from the servomotor to the thruster.

In contrast with adding extra thrusters, usually each thruster adds additional lift to the platform. Consequently, while adding thrusters to the platform adds weight, it can also increase the platform’s total lift. On the other hand, adding servomotors allows an increase in the platform’s
DoF
s without contributing to the platform’s lift capacity.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we present a thorough analysis of the static hovering ability of MRAV platforms with tiltable propellers, and present the algebraic conditions to achieve and sustain desired static hovering properties. To do so, we formally introduce and model such platforms, and present an allocation map for any MRAV with tiltable propellers. Then we present the conditions for a platform to hover at a single orientation, in a plane, or in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Furthermore, we present the conditions for a platform to sustain achieved hovering, and present a numerical metric that reflects the stability of each hovering orientation. Based on the presented conditions, we introduce a class of MRAVs referred to as Critically Statically Hoverable (CSH) platforms, which are platforms that cannot sustain static hovering without active propeller tilting. In addition, we present a thorough simulation study to test and validate the presented conditions. These simulations show clearly the effect of the presented numerical metric (LHI) on the platform’s dynamic response and ability to apply different forces and moments.
Moreover, these simulations show clearly that a platform with
N\leq 3
is a CSH platform, where the dimension of the moment set with fixed propellers is less than
N
, even if the platform is OD when all of its propellers are fully functional.

Based on the fundamental conditions and properties discussed in this paper, future studies could include the optimization of minimal CSH platform design to maximize the LHI metric at different orientations. Alternatively, future studies could use the LHI metric in the control loop, where a controller that advantages orientations with higher LHI is expected to achieve more stable static hovering.

Moreover, in the case of MRAV with
N\geq 4
, CSH conditions could occur after propeller failure. Accordingly, it would be interesting to extend the conditions from this work to understand the fail-safe robustness of generic MRAVs with tiltable propellers.
