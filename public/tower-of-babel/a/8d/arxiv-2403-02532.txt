Superposition detection and QMA with non-collapsing measurements
Roozbeh Bassirian, Kunal Marwaha

Abstract
We prove that 𝖰𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{QMA}} where the verifier may also make a single non-collapsing measurement [ ABFL16 ] is equal to 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯 {\mathsf{NEXP}} , resolving an open question of Aaronson [ Aar23 ] . We show this is a corollary to a modified proof of 𝖰𝖬𝖠 + = 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯 {\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}} [ BFM24 ] . At the core of many results inspired by Blier and Tapp [ BT12 ] is an unphysical property testing problem deciding whether a quantum state is close to an element of a fixed basis.



1
Introduction

Hidden-variable theories attempt to explain the probabilistic nature of quantum mechanics as downstream effects of parameters beyond the quantum state. If these parameters are accessible to a quantum computer, unusually strong computation is possible. Aaronson and collaborators
[
Aar05b
,
ABFL16
]
proposed
and studied enhancements to
{\mathsf{BQP}}

inspired by these theories.

One such upgrade is the power to make “non-collapsing” measurements, exemplified through the class
{\mathsf{CQP}}
(Collapse-free Quantum Polynomial-time)
[
ABFL16
]
.
After applying each gate or (collapsing) intermediate measurement, a
{\mathsf{CQP}}
machine receives a measurement of the current quantum state in the computational basis
without perturbing the state
.
[
ABFL16
]
proved that such a machine can solve problems in
{\mathsf{SZK}}
, and in a black-box setting, solve unstructured search faster than Grover’s algorithm (but not in polynomial time). This power is dramatically increased with quantum
advice
: Aaronson showed that
{\mathsf{CQP}}/{\mathsf{qpoly}}={\mathsf{ALL}}

[
Aar18
]
, which contrasts with

{\mathsf{BQP}}/{\mathsf{qpoly}}\subseteq{\mathsf{QMA}}/{\mathsf{poly}}

[
Aar05a
,
AD14
]
.

A related concept to quantum advice is quantum
verification
with an untrusted witness; see
[
Gha23
]
for a recent survey. One particularly mysterious version of such a complexity class is
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)

[
KMY03
]
: quantum verification where the witness is guaranteed to be
separable
. Even after much effort
[
BT12
,
ABDFS09
,
CD10
,
Per12
,
HM13
,
GSSSY18
,
SY23
]
, little is known about the power of
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
besides the trivial bounds
{\mathsf{QMA}}\subseteq{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)\subseteq{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

Recently,
[
JW23
]
proposed a new approach to show
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
by introducing the class
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(2)
, where the witness is also guaranteed to have
non-negative amplitudes
in the computational basis.
They proved that
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
, and suggested the possibility of
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(2)
.
However, any such result must rely on the separability guarantee of
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
, since it was later shown that
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}}

[
BFM24
]
.
1
1

1

{\mathsf{QMA}}={\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
would imply, for example, that
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.
In particular, quantum verification with only a
non-negative amplitudes
guarantee is maximally powerful.

Very recently, Aaronson asked the following question:

Question 1

(
[
Aar23
]
)
.

What is the power of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
where the verifier can make non-collapsing measurements?

This computational model is trivially upper-bounded by
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
, yet Aaronson conjectured it to be
equal
to
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
. In this work, we show this conjecture to be true, even with a
single
non-collapsing measurement!
Notably, our proof is inspired by the proof of
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}}

[
BFM24
]
.
To unify these results, we identify a property testing problem we call
superposition detection
: can one distinguish the set of computational basis states
\mathcal{B}
from states
\epsilon
-far from
\mathcal{B}
?
The latter states are in
superposition
over computational basis states.
We show that a quantum verifier has the power of
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
if it can test this property on arbitrary states — more specifically, states that are obtained after a collapsing measurement on a quantum witness.

A note for the cautious reader: with a single copy, this property testing task is physically unrealistic — it even results in distinguishability of statistically equal ensembles (see
Fact

23
).
Any successful detector must access information beyond the quantum state.
We show that a quantum computer equipped with a “non-collapsing” measurement can always detect superposition.
On the other hand,
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
only considers a subset of quantum witnesses, all of which admit efficient superposition detection.
As a result, both computational models have the power of
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

With multiple copies, superposition detection is possible — but generating the copies seems to require
post-selection

[
Aar05c
,
Kin18
]
, inverse exponential precision
[
Per12
]
, or simply too many quantum witnesses
[
ABDFS09
,
CD10
,
CF11
]
. These are exactly the issues that have plagued approaches inspired by Blier and Tapp
[
BT12
]
to prove
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

1.1
Techniques

Our techniques primarily follow that of
[
JW23
,
BFM24
]
, which are inspired by the work of Blier and Tapp
[
BT12
]
. We describe the general approach and then note the differences in this work.

The central challenge is to place an
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-complete problem in a variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
. Following
[
JW23
,
BFM24
]
, we use a
succinct
constraint satisfaction problem with
constant
gap. In
(1,\delta)
-
GapCSP
, either all constraints can be satisfied, or at most a
\delta
fraction of constraints can be satisfied. This problem is
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-hard due to the PCP theorem
[
AS92
,
ALMSS98
,
Har04
]
.
2
2

2

As we do not consider
{\mathsf{NP}}
-completeness in this work, we often omit the word
succinct
when describing
(1,\delta)
-
GapCSP
.

Consider a witness over two registers: the
constraint index
register with a polynomial number of qubits, and a constant-sized
value
register.
The protocol chooses one of two tests to run with some probability. The first test checks the
rigidity
of a quantum state, i.e. accepting if the witness is close to the form
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{j\in[R]}\ket{j}\ket{\sigma(j)}
. The second test verifies the
constraints
of the CSP by measuring the state in the computational basis and verifying the constraint indexed by the outcome of the first register.
Using several tricks from the PCP literature
[
Din07
,
JW23
]
, the second test is able to decide
(1,\delta)
-
GapCSP
whenever the witness is
rigid
.

Surprisingly, the
non-negative amplitudes
guarantee is only used in the
first
test, i.e. checking the
rigidity
of the witness.
With some probability, the verifier encourages a large
\ell_{1}
-norm by measuring the overlap with the uniform superposition state
\ket{+}
.
It must now enforce that the witness sends at most
one
basis element in the
value
register per basis element in the
constraint index
register. For example, after measuring the
constraint index
register in the computational basis, the verifier must reject any superposition in the remaining quantum state.
[
JW23
,
BFM24
]
can enforce this property assuming the witness has non-negative amplitudes, thus deciding the
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-complete problem.

In this work, we consider any algorithm that detects
superposition
over constant-sized computational basis states:

Definition 2

(Superposition detector)
.

A
superposition detector

\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\Delta}
is an algorithm that inputs a quantum state
\ket{\psi}
on
k
qubits and outputs
1
or
0
, such that:

•

If
\ket{\psi}
is a computational basis state on
k
qubits, then
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\Delta}(\ket{\psi})=1
with probability
1
.

•

If
|\innerproduct{e}{\psi}|^{2}\leq 1-\epsilon
for every computational basis state
\ket{e}
on
k
qubits, then
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\Delta}(\ket{\psi})=1
with probability at most
1-\Delta<1
.

We show that if a quantum computer can implement a superposition detector over the
value
register of the quantum witness, then it can check
rigidity
of the witness, and thus decide the
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-complete problem. Since the
value
register is constant-sized, we only need a superposition detector where
k
is some constant.

We use this generalization in two places.
First, we show that a
single
non-collapsing measurement allows a quantum computer to detect superposition on
any
quantum state, and thus decide any problem in
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
. This resolves
Question

1
.
Curiously, in both the verification and advice setting
[
Aar18
]
, the power of non-collapsing measurements is magnified when the verifier is given a state it cannot prepare.

Second, we show that if the witness has non-negative amplitudes, a quantum computer can efficiently detect superposition; this reproves the statement
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}}

[
BFM24
]
.
The
superposition detection
viewpoint gives new intuition for this result:

{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
and
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}(2)

pre-select
quantum witnesses where the verifier can detect superposition.
However, the verifier cannot detect superposition in
all
quantum witnesses unless
{\mathsf{QMA}}\neq{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.
As a consequence, any proof of
{\mathsf{NEXP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
must handle
some
witnesses in a fundamentally new way.

1.2
Our results

We first prove the following statement:

Theorem 3

(Main theorem)
.

Consider a variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
where given any constant
k
and
\epsilon>0
,
the verifier is also allowed to partially measure the quantum witness in the computational basis, and then apply
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\Delta}
for some
\Delta=\Omega(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)})
.
Then this variant contains
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

This has two direct consequences.
First, since a superposition detector can be efficiently implemented on all quantum states if the verifier can make non-collapsing measurements, we resolve
Question

1
:

Corollary 4
.

Consider the variant of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
where the verifier is allowed to make
one
non-collapsing measurement at any point in the computation. Then this variant equals
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
, even if the measurement outcome can only be used at the end of the computation.

Second, since a superposition detector can be implemented for all quantum witnesses with
non-negative amplitudes
, we recover the main result of
[
BFM24
]
:

Corollary 5

(
[
BFM24
]
)
.

{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}}
.

Preliminaries

We first define
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{c,s}
. When

Definition 6

(
{\mathsf{QMA}}_{c,s}
)
.

Let
c,s:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be polynomial-time computable functions. A promise problem
\mathcal{L}_{\text{yes}},\mathcal{L}_{\text{no}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}

{\mathsf{QMA}}_{c,s}

{\mathsf{BQP}}

n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}

•

Completeness:
if
x\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{yes}}
, then there exists a state
\ket{\psi}
on
\mathrm{poly}(n)

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[V(x,\ket{\psi})\text{ accepts}]\geq c(n)\,.

•

Soundness:
If
x\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{no}}
, then for every state
\ket{\psi}
on
\mathrm{poly}(n)
qubits, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[V(x,\ket{\psi})\text{ accepts}]\leq s(n)\,.

We prove
Theorem

3
using the
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
-complete problem proposed by
[

]

Definition 7

.

A

The

\sigma:[N]\to\Sigma

.

Definition 9

(

)

The

-

takes as input a

-CSP system
\mathcal{C}

\mathop{\bf val\/}(\mathcal{C})\leq\delta

Theorem 10

(
[
JW23
]
)
.

There exist constants
q
and
\delta<1
and a family of succinct
(N_{i},R_{i},q,\Sigma=\{0,1\})
-CSP systems
\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}

-

]

.

Definition 11

(Rigid and quasirigid states,
[

]
)

Given a

-CSP system

, consider

Fact 13

(

Proof of main theorem

Our protocol depends on
R,q,\Sigma
that determine the
(N,R,q,\Sigma)
-CSP system
\mathcal{C}
. It considers quantum witnesses over two registers: a
constraint index
register of dimension
R
and a
value
register of dimension
\kappa:=|\Sigma|^{q}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{j\in[R],x\in\Sigma^{q}}a_{j,x}\ket{j}\ket{x}\,,

for some
a_{j,x}\in\mathbb{C}
so that
\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}=1
. Before describing the protocol, we define two tests,
\mathsf{Density}
and
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}
, that act on the quantum witness. We use them in tandem to ensure the witness is
rigid
.

Definition 14
.

\mathsf{Density}
takes as input a quantum state, measures it in the Fourier basis, and accepts iff the observed basis element is
\ket{+}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R\cdot\kappa}}\sum_{j\in[R],x\in\Sigma^{q}}\ket{j,x}
.

Definition 15
.

\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta}
(with
\epsilon,\Delta>0
) takes as input a quantum state, measures the
constraint index
register in the computational basis, then sends the remaining quantum state to a superposition detector
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{\log\kappa,\epsilon,\Delta}
, and accepts iff the output of
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{\log\kappa,\epsilon,\Delta}
is
1
.

\mathsf{Density}
is straightforward: it checks the overlap of a quantum state with the uniform superposition state. Let
A(\mathsf{V},\ket{\psi})
be the acceptance probability of test
\mathsf{V}
when input the quantum state
\ket{\psi}
.

Fact 16
.

A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})=|\innerproduct{+}{\psi}|^{2}
. Moreover, if
\ket{\psi}
is
rigid
, then
A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})=\frac{1}{\kappa}
.

\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta}
collapses the
constraint index
register of the input, and ensures that there is no superposition over the
value
register. A quasirigid state perfectly satisfies this test.

Fact 17
.

If
\ket{\psi}
is
quasirigid
, then
A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})=1
. For arbitrary
\ket{\psi}
,

\displaystyle A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})\leq\sum_{j\in[R]}\left(\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{q}}|a_{j,x}|^{2}\right)\cdot c_{j,\epsilon}=(1-\Delta)+\Delta\cdot\hskip-11.38109pt\sum_{j\in[R];c_{j,\epsilon}=1}\left(\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{q}}|a_{j,x}|^{2}\right)\,,

where
c_{j,\epsilon}=1-\Delta
if
\max_{x\in\Sigma^{q}}|a_{j,x}|^{2}\leq(1-\epsilon)\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{q}}|a_{j,x}|^{2}
, and
1
otherwise.

In a formal sense,
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta}
ensures that the input is close to a quasirigid state:

Lemma 18
.

Suppose
A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})=w
. Then there exists a
quasirigid
state
\ket{\phi}
such that
|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}|^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)\frac{w-(1-\Delta)}{\Delta}
.

Proof.

Choose a map
f:[R]\to\Sigma^{q}
so that for every clause
j\in[R]
,
f(j)
is an element of
\Sigma^{q}
that has the largest weight; i.e.
|a_{j,f(j)}|^{2}=\max_{x\in\Sigma^{q}}|a_{j,x}|^{2}
. Consider the
quasirigid
state
\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\sum_{j\in[R]}a_{j,f(j)}\ket{j}\ket{f(j)}
, where
\gamma=\sum_{j\in[R]}|a_{j,f(j)}|^{2}
is chosen so that
\innerproduct{\phi}{\phi}=1
. Then
|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}|^{2}=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\sum_{j\in[R]}|a_{j,f(j)}|^{2}\right|^{2}=\gamma
.
Finally, notice that

\displaystyle\gamma\geq\sum_{j\in[R];c_{j,\epsilon}=1}|a_{j,f(j)}|^{2}\geq\sum_{j\in[R];c_{j,\epsilon}=1}(1-\epsilon)\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{q}}|a_{j,x}|^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)\cdot\frac{w-(1-\Delta)}{\Delta}\,.

∎

Since quasirigid states only use a
\frac{1}{\kappa}
fraction of basis elements, they will not test well on
\mathsf{Density}
. In fact, no quantum state can do well on both
\mathsf{Density}
and
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta}
:

Lemma 19
.

Suppose
A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})=w_{\mathsf{D}}\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}
and
A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})=w_{\mathsf{Q}}
. Then
(w_{\mathsf{D}}-\frac{1}{\kappa})^{2}+(1-\epsilon)\frac{w_{\mathsf{Q}}-(1-\Delta)}{\Delta}\leq 1
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

18
, there exists a quasirigid state
\ket{\phi}
such that
|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}|^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)\frac{w_{\mathsf{Q}}-(1-\Delta)}{\Delta}
.
Let
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{j\in[R]}b_{j}\ket{j}\ket{\sigma(j)}
for some function
\sigma
and amplitudes
\{b_{j}\}_{j\in[R]}
.
Using
Fact

13
, we see that

\left||\innerproduct{+}{\psi}|^{2}-|\innerproduct{+}{\phi}|^{2}\right|
is at most
\sqrt{1-(1-\epsilon)\frac{w_{\mathsf{Q}}-(1-\Delta)}{\Delta}}
. Note that
|\innerproduct{+}{\psi}|^{2}=w_{\mathsf{D}}
, and by Cauchy-Schwarz,
|\innerproduct{+}{\phi}|^{2}=\frac{1}{R\cdot\kappa}\left|\sum_{j\in[R]}b_{j}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{j\in[R]}|b_{j}|^{2}=\frac{1}{\kappa}
. Since
w_{\mathsf{D}}\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}
, we have
w_{\mathsf{D}}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\leq|\innerproduct{+}{\psi}|^{2}-|\innerproduct{+}{\phi}|^{2}
, which is at most
\sqrt{1-(1-\epsilon)\frac{w_{\mathsf{Q}}-(1-\Delta)}{\Delta}}
. The statement follows after rearranging terms.
∎

We show that the
best
quantum states for this pair of tests are close to
rigid
states. Intuitively, the quasirigid states that test well on
\mathsf{Density}
have nearly uniform amplitudes, and so are close to rigid.

Claim 20
.

Suppose
A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})\geq 1-\Delta\cdot d_{\mathsf{Q}}
. Then there exists a rigid state
\ket{\chi}
such that
|\innerproduct{\chi}{\psi}|^{2}\geq\kappa\cdot A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})-(\kappa+1)\sqrt{\epsilon+d_{\mathsf{Q}}}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

18
, there exists a quasirigid state
\ket{\phi}
such that
|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}|^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)(1-d_{\mathsf{Q}})
. By
Fact

13
, we have

\left||\innerproduct{\mu}{\psi}|^{2}-|\innerproduct{\mu}{\phi}|^{2}\right|\leq\sqrt{1-(1-\epsilon)(1-d_{\mathsf{Q}})}\leq\sqrt{\epsilon+d_{\mathsf{Q}}}
for any unit vector
\ket{\mu}
. We use this in two places. First, since
|\innerproduct{+}{\psi}|^{2}=A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})
, applying
Fact

13
with
\ket{\mu}=\ket{+}
implies
|\innerproduct{+}{\phi}|^{2}\geq A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})-\sqrt{\epsilon+d_{\mathsf{Q}}}
. Now let
\ket{\chi}
be the
rigid
state with the same computational basis elements as
\ket{\phi}
. Then

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{\chi}{\phi}|^{2}=\kappa|\innerproduct{+}{\phi}|^{2}\geq\kappa\cdot A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})-\kappa\sqrt{\epsilon+d_{\mathsf{Q}}}\,.

Applying
Fact

13
using
\ket{\mu}=\ket{\chi}
implies
|\innerproduct{\chi}{\psi}|^{2}\geq\kappa\cdot A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})-\kappa\sqrt{\epsilon+d_{\mathsf{Q}}}-\sqrt{\epsilon+d_{\mathsf{Q}}}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Suppose for any constant
k
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists some
\Delta
at least inverse polynomial in input size such that
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\Delta}
can be efficiently implemented.
We construct the following
{\mathsf{QMA}}
verifier that decides every
GapCSP
instance guaranteed by
Theorem

10
, choosing
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\epsilon
later on:

•

With probability
p_{1}
, run
\mathsf{Density}
.

•

With probability
p_{2}
, run
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta}
.

•

With probability
p_{3}
, run the protocol
\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}
guaranteed by
Lemma

12
.

Lemma

12
implies the existence of absolute constants
\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}},\xi,\kappa
. We choose
\epsilon
and
distance thresholds

\nu_{\text{low}},\nu_{\text{high}}
to be small positive constants that satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle\epsilon<\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}\leq\nu_{\text{low}}^{2}\leq 1\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{\nu_{\text{low}}}\leq\frac{\xi}{6(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\kappa\nu_{\text{low}}+(\kappa+1)\sqrt{\epsilon+\nu_{\text{low}}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{\xi}{2}\,.

For example, this can be done by first making all constants equal and small enough to satisfy the right-most inequality, then reducing
\nu_{\text{high}}
to satisfy the middle inequality, then reducing
\epsilon
to satisfy the first inequality.

Now we choose the probabilities
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
. Let

\displaystyle p_{1}=\frac{1}{Z}\,,

\displaystyle p_{2}=\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})(1-\epsilon)}{\Delta(\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}-\epsilon)Z}\,,

\displaystyle p_{3}=\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})Z}\,,

where
Z:=1+\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})(1-\epsilon)}{\Delta(\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}-\epsilon)}+\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})}
, so that the probabilities sum to
1
.

Given a quantum witness
\ket{\psi}
, the verifier accepts with probability

\displaystyle p_{1}\cdot A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})+p_{2}\cdot A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})+p_{3}\cdot A(\mathsf{ConstraintCheck},\ket{\psi})\,.

In completeness, we consider the quantum witness guaranteed by
Lemma

12
. Since this state is rigid, by
Fact

16
and
Fact

17
, the verifier accepts with probability at least
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}:=p_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{\kappa}+p_{2}\cdot 1+p_{3}\cdot\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}}
.

We split soundness into four cases, depending on the acceptance probabilities of
\mathsf{Density}
and
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}
. We give a graphical picture in
Figure

1
.

Figure 1:
Plot of possible acceptance probabilities for the tests
\mathsf{Density}
and
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta}
when
\Delta=1
. The numbers
1
to
4
correspond to the four cases in soundness in the proof of
Theorem

3
. The upper right corner is forbidden by
Lemma

19
. Rigid states live at the black “
\times
” icon by
Fact

16
and
Fact

17
.

1.

In the first case, the witness doesn’t test well enough on
\mathsf{Density}
. If
A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})=\frac{1}{\kappa}-d
for
d\geq\nu_{\text{low}}
, then the verifier accepts with probability at most

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}\cdot(\frac{1}{\kappa}-d)+p_{2}\cdot 1+p_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2Z}\,.

2.

In the second case, the witness tests too well on
\mathsf{Density}
, and so cannot test well enough on
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}
. If
A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})=\frac{1}{\kappa}+d
for
d\geq\nu_{\text{high}}
, then by
Lemma

19
,
A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})\leq 1-\Delta+\Delta\cdot\frac{1-d^{2}}{1-\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+d\right)+p_{2}\cdot\left(1-\Delta+\Delta\cdot\frac{1-d^{2}}{1-\epsilon}\right)+p_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\Delta\cdot\left(\frac{d^{2}-\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\right)+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})\,.

The right-most expression depends on
d
through the term

p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\Delta\cdot\left(\frac{d^{2}-\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\right)=p_{1}\left(d-\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})(d^{2}-\epsilon)}{\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}-\epsilon}\right)\,.

This term is decreasing with
d
when
d\geq\nu_{\text{high}}
; one can verify this by taking a partial derivative with respect to
d
. So the expression is maximized at
d=\nu_{\text{high}}
, and so

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\left(\nu_{\text{high}}-\frac{(\nu_{\text{low}}+\nu_{\text{high}})(\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}-\epsilon)}{\nu_{\text{high}}^{2}-\epsilon}\right)+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2Z}\,.

3.

In the third case, the witness doesn’t test well enough on
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}
, and the acceptance probability of
\mathsf{Density}
is not too high. If
A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})=\frac{1}{\kappa}+d
for
-\nu_{\text{low}}\leq d\leq\nu_{\text{high}}
and
A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})\leq 1-\Delta\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}
, then

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+d\right)+p_{2}\cdot\left(1-\Delta\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}\right)+p_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\Delta\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})\,.

By our choice of constants,
d\leq\nu_{\text{high}}\leq\nu_{\text{low}}
. Moreover, one can verify that
p_{2}\Delta\geq 2\cdot p_{1}
whenever
\nu_{\text{high}}\leq 1
. So
p_{1}\cdot d-p_{2}\Delta\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}\leq-p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}
, and
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}+p_{3}\cdot(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}}{2Z}
.

4.

In the fourth case, the witness tests well on
\mathsf{QuasiCheck}
, and the acceptance probability of
\mathsf{Density}
is not too low. So the witness must be close to
rigid
, and do worse on
\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}
. If
A(\mathsf{Density},\ket{\psi})=\frac{1}{\kappa}+d
for
-\nu_{\text{low}}\leq d\leq\nu_{\text{high}}
and
A(\mathsf{QuasiCheck}_{\epsilon,\Delta},\ket{\psi})\geq 1-\Delta\cdot\nu_{\text{low}}
, then by
Claim

20
, there is a rigid state
\ket{\chi}
such that
|\innerproduct{\chi}{\psi}|^{2}\geq 1+\kappa d-(\kappa+1)\sqrt{\epsilon+\nu_{\text{low}}}\geq 1-\kappa\nu_{\text{low}}-(\kappa+1)\sqrt{\epsilon+\nu_{\text{low}}}
. We use
Fact

13
with
\Pi
equal to the accepting projector of
\mathsf{ConstraintCheck}
; then the difference
|A(\mathsf{ConstraintCheck},\ket{\psi})-A(\mathsf{ConstraintCheck},\ket{\chi})|\leq\left(\kappa\nu_{\text{low}}+(\kappa+1)\sqrt{\epsilon+\nu_{\text{low}}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{\xi}{2}
. Putting this together,

\displaystyle\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{NO}}

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+d\right)+p_{2}\cdot 1+p_{3}\cdot\left(\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\xi+\frac{\xi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+p_{1}\cdot\nu_{\text{high}}-p_{3}\cdot\frac{\xi}{2}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}+\frac{1}{Z}\left(\nu_{\text{high}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{low}}\cdot\xi}{4(1-\textnormal{C}_{\textnormal{YES}})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{2Z}\,.

So, this protocol succeeds with completeness
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}
and soundness at most
\textnormal{P}_{\textnormal{YES}}-\frac{\nu_{\text{high}}}{2Z}
.
∎

4
Implications

4.1
{\mathsf{QMA}}
with non-collapsing measurements equals
{\mathsf{NEXP}}

The ability to make non-collapsing measurements implies efficient superposition detection.

Claim 21
.

Consider any positive integer
k
and constant
0<\epsilon\leq 1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}
. Then a quantum computer with the ability to make
just one
non-collapsing measurement can efficiently implement
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,2(x-x^{2})}
, where
x=\min(\epsilon,\frac{1}{2^{k}})
.

Proof.

Suppose the quantum computer is given a
k
-qubit state
\ket{\psi}
, and choose any
\epsilon>0
. The computer first makes one non-collapsing measurement of
\ket{\psi}
. The computer then makes a (collapsing) measurement of
\ket{\psi}
in the computational basis, and outputs
1
if and only if the measurements agree.

If
\ket{\psi}
is truly a computational basis state, then the computer always outputs
1
. Now suppose
|\innerproduct{e}{\psi}|^{2}\leq 1-\epsilon
for all
k
-qubit computational basis states
\ket{e}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Let
\ket{e_{*}}
be a state in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
with the largest squared overlap with
\ket{\psi}
, and let
t=|\innerproduct{e_{*}}{\psi}|^{2}
. Then the probability of outputting
1
can be upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\sum_{\ket{e}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(|\innerproduct{e}{\psi}|^{2}\right)^{2}=|\innerproduct{e_*}{\psi}|^{4}+\sum_{\ket{e}\in\mathcal{B}_{k};\ket{e}\neq\ket{e_*}}|\innerproduct{e}{\psi}|^{4}\leq t^{2}+\Bigg(\sum_{\ket{e}\in\mathcal{B}_{k};\ket{e}\neq\ket{e_*}}|\innerproduct{e}{\psi}|^{2}\Bigg)^{2}=t^{2}+(1-t)^{2}\,,

which equals
1-2(t-t^{2})
.
This is maximized at the largest and smallest valid values of
t
.
Observe that
t\geq\frac{1}{2^{k}}
since
\ket{e_{*}}
has the largest squared overlap with
\ket{\psi}
, and
t\leq 1-\epsilon
by supposition.
∎

The lower bound of
Corollary

4
is then a direct consequence of
Theorem

3
and
Claim

21
.
For the upper bound, note that any efficient quantum circuit that includes collapsing or non-collapsing measurements can be classically simulated in exponential time, so
{\mathsf{QMA}}
with non-collapsing measurements is at most
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
. This resolves
Question

1
.

[
ABFL16
]
defined two complexity classes with non-collapsing measurement:
{\mathsf{CQP}}
and
{\mathsf{naCQP}}
(non-adaptive
{\mathsf{CQP}}
).
In
{\mathsf{naCQP}}
, the non-collapsing measurement results can only be used at the
end
of the quantum computation (i.e. in classical post-processing).
We remark that both
Claim

21
and the proof of
Theorem

3
satisfy this non-adaptive condition.

The original framing of
Question

1
in
[
Aar23
]
directly considers the power of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
with a
{\mathsf{naCQP}}
(or
{\mathsf{CQP}}
) verifier. This has a technical issue which we discuss here.
[
ABFL16
]
considered a
classical
oracle
\mathcal{O}
that receives a quantum circuit and outputs non-collapsing measurement results.
{\mathsf{naCQP}}
(
{\mathsf{CQP}}
) is then defined as
{\mathsf{P}}
with
1
(
\mathrm{poly}(n)
) query to
\mathcal{O}
. If we use such a machine as a verifier, it equals
{\mathsf{NEXP}}
only if
\mathcal{O}
can also receive the
quantum
witness. To handle this issue, we consider
{\mathsf{QMA}}
where the verifier can make a non-collapsing measurement on an arbitrary quantum state at
any
point in the computation.
Here,
Claim

21
uses one non-collapsing measurement, which is immediately followed by one collapsing measurement.

[
AGIMR24
]
instead defines the class
{\mathsf{PDQMA}}
, where the verifier first runs a quantum circuit (with collapsing measurements), then makes
all
non-collapsing measurements, and finally runs a classical circuit given the non-collapsing measurement results. In this model,
Claim

21
is not valid as written, since non-collapsing measurements must occur after all collapsing measurements. However, by replacing the final collapsing measurement with a non-collapsing measurement, we recover
{\mathsf{NEXP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{PDQMA}}
using
two
non-collapsing measurements. We remark that this is optimal:
{\mathsf{PDQMA}}
with one non-collapsing measurement is equal to
{\mathsf{QMA}}
, since the verifier’s final measurement can always be collapsing.
[
AGIMR24
]
uses
O(n\log n)
non-collapsing measurements to prove
{\mathsf{NEXP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{PDQMA}}
.

4.2
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}={\mathsf{NEXP}}

If a quantum witness has
non-negative amplitudes
, a partial measurement in the computational basis will preserve this property.
A
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machine can implement superposition detection on any such quantum state:

Claim 22
.

Consider the algorithm
\mathsf{Verify}^{+}_{k}
, which on input a
k
-qubit quantum state
\ket{\psi}
, measures
\ket{\psi}
in the Fourier basis and outputs
0
if the observed basis component is
\frac{1}{2^{k/2}}\sum_{i\in[2^{k}]}\ket{i}
, and
1
otherwise. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, there is a superposition detector
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\sqrt{\epsilon/2^{k}}}
such that for every state
\ket{\psi}
with non-negative computational basis amplitudes,
A(\mathsf{Verify}^{+}_{k},\ket{\psi})=A(\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\sqrt{\epsilon/2^{k}}},\ket{\psi})-\frac{1}{2^{k}}
.

Proof.

Let the input be
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i\in[2^{k}]}a_{i}\ket{i}
. Then
A(\mathsf{Verify}^{+}_{k},\ket{\psi})=1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left|\sum_{i\in[2^{k}]}a_{i}\right|^{2}
. Since all
a_{i}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle A(\mathsf{Verify}^{+}_{k},\ket{\psi})=1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\Big(\sum_{i\in[2^{k}]}a_{i}\Big)^{2}=1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(1+\sum_{i,j\in[2^{k}];i\neq j}a_{i}a_{j}\right)\leq 1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\,,

and this is achieved if
\ket{\psi}
is a computational basis state.
Now, let
i^{*}=\text{argmax}_{i\in[2^{k}]}a_{i}^{2}
. Since
\sum_{i\in[2^{k}]}a_{i}^{2}=1
,
a_{i^{*}}\geq\frac{1}{2^{k/2}}
. If
a_{i^{*}}^{2}\leq 1-\epsilon
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in[2^{k}];i\neq j}a_{i}a_{j}\geq\frac{1}{2^{k/2}}\sum_{j\in[2^{k}],j\neq i^{*}}a_{j}\geq\frac{1}{2^{k/2}}\sqrt{\sum_{j\in[2^{k}],j\neq i^{*}}a_{j}^{2}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2^{k/2}}\,.

∎

Corollary

5
is then a direct consequence of
Theorem

3
and
Claim

22
. Note that
\mathsf{Verify}^{+}_{k}
acts as a superposition detector, since the additive error
\frac{1}{2^{k}}
in
Claim

22
does not change the promise gap of the protocol in
Theorem

3
.

In
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
, one
only
considers quantum witnesses which admit efficient superposition detection by
Claim

22
.
By contrast,
Theorem

3
implies that this property cannot be tested on
all
quantum witnesses, assuming
{\mathsf{QMA}}\neq{\mathsf{NEXP}}
. In fact, it is information-theoretically
impossible
, assuming the density matrix is “all there is” in quantum mechanics:

Fact 23
.

Fix
k
. Consider the set
S_{1}
of
k
-qubit computational basis states and the set
S_{2}
of
k
-qubit Fourier basis states. Then a uniformly random choice from
S_{1}
has the same density matrix as a uniformly random choice from
S_{2}
. Despite this,
\mathsf{SupDetect}_{k,\epsilon,\Delta}
distinguishes these two options for all
\epsilon\leq 1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}
and
\Delta>0
.

Does it help to have more quantum witnesses?
Note that having
two
copies of a quantum state allows one to detect superposition: measure each copy in the computational basis, and output
1
iff the outcomes agree. However, in
Theorem

3
, the superposition detector is used on a quantum witness
after a partial measurement
.
In completeness, even with
\mathrm{poly}(n)
quantum witnesses, there is an inverse exponential chance of receiving two copies of the same post-measurement state.
So as is, we cannot use the additional quantum witnesses to detect superposition unless we allow an inverse exponential promise gap, or
post-selection

[
Aar05c
]
.
We observe that
Theorem

3
neatly recovers the results
\text{precise}{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}

[
Per12
]
and
\text{post}{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)={\mathsf{NEXP}}

[
Kin18
]
, as well as the
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
protocol with logarithmic-sized proofs for
{\mathsf{NP}}

[
BT12
]
.

In light of this, we suggest that lower bounds to
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
must go beyond the
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{+}
framework, as the verifier must be able to handle quantum witnesses without directly detecting superposition.

Open problems

{\mathsf{QCMA}}

?

4.2
.

{\mathsf{BQP}}
(or
