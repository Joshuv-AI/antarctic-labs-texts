The Geometry of Reachability in Continuous Vector Addition Systems with States
Shaull Almagor, Arka Ghosh, Tim Leys, Guillermo A. Pérez

Abstract
We study the geometry of reachability sets of continuous vector addition systems with states (VASS). In particular we establish that they are “almost” Minkowski sums of convex cones and zonotopes generated by the vectors labelling the transitions of the VASS. We use the latter to prove that short so-called linear path schemes suffice as witnesses of reachability in continuous VASS of fixed dimension. Then, we give new polynomial-time algorithms for the reachability problem for linear path schemes. Finally, we also establish that enriching the model with zero tests makes the reachability problem intractable already for linear path schemes of dimension two.

Introduction

Vector Addition Systems with States (VASS, for short)
are rich mathematical models for the description of distributed systems, as well as chemical and biological processes, and more
[
21
]
. They comprise a finite control graph with natural-valued counters, which updates the counters by adding to them integer-valued vectors that label the edges of the graph.
VASS arise naturally as an arguably-cleaner model than Petri nets, due to their reachability problem being polytime-interreducible with that of Petri nets.

While VASS
are a very expressive model of
concurrency that admits algorithmic analysis, the complexity of several
associated decision problems is — in the worst case — prohibitively high.
For instance, the
rechability problem
, which asks “is a given
target configuration reachable from a given initial configuration?” was recently proved to be Ackermann-complete
[
8
]
.

Continuous VASS
were introduced by Blondin and
Haase
[
3
]
as an alternative to
continuous Petri
nets

[
9
]
which
trade off the ability to encode discrete information in favor of
computational and practical benefits. A VASS essentially consists of a finite-state machine whose transitions are labelled with integer vectors. Besides the current state, a configuration of the VASS also comprises the current values of a set of counters. When a transition is taken, the state of the machine changes and the values of the counters are updated by adding to them the vector that labels the transition. A continuous VASS additionally allows to scale the update vector by some scalar
0<\alpha\leq 1

In contrast to the

Despite the relatively low computational complexity,

considered as tractable.
The only subcase previously known to be in
\mathbf{P}

are allowed to hold negative values. It is also worth noting that the aforementioned
\mathbf{NP}
upper bound is obtained by encoding the reachability problem into
the existential fragment of the first-order theory of the reals with addition
and order. It is natural to ask whether more efficient algorithms or encodings
into “simpler” logics exist, e.g. linear programming, even if only for
particular subcases.

Reachability in fixed-dimension VASS.

Recall that the reachability problem for discrete VASS is now known to be
Ackermann-complete. The upper bound is due to Leroux and Schmitz
[
17
]
,
while the lower bound was independently proven by Leroux
[
16
]
and
by Czerwiński and Orlikowski
[
8
]
. The latter, relatively new, lower
bound has renewed interest in what could be named the
Bordeaux-Warsaw
program
— that is, the study of the computational complexity of the
reachability problem for low-dimensional VASS and extensions thereof (see,
e.g.,
[
6

5
,
7
,
10
]
). Indeed, one hopes that in such
cases, there may be efficient algorithms for the problem. Additionally, to
quote Czerwiński and Orlikowski
[
8

: “it is easier to [design]
sophisticated techniques working in a simpler setting [that might] result in
finding new techniques useful in much broader generality.” For dimensions
1

and
2
(and counter updates encoded in binary) the problem is known to be

\mathbf{NP}
-complete
[
14
]
and
\mathbf{PSPACE}
-complete
[
2
]
,
respectively.

An important structural restriction on VASS which is often used as an
intermediate step in establishing upper bounds is that of
flatness
,
i.e. disallowing nested cycles. In fact, the upper bounds for dimensions
1

and
2
mentioned above can be seen as a consequence of such VASS being
effectively flattable
[
18
]
. A further restriction consists of asking
that the set of all runs of the VASS can be represented by a single regular
expression
\pi_{0}\chi_{1}^{*}\dots\pi_{n-1}\chi_{n}^{*}\pi_{n}
over the
transitions. Such VASS are called

(LPS, for short).

Preliminaries

In this work,
\mathbb{Q}
denotes the set of all rational numbers;
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
, all strictly positive rational numbers; and
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, all nonnegative ones — including
0
. Similarly,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of all natural numbers including
0
.

Topology and geometry.

Let
d
be a positive integer.
For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the
open ball of radius
r
around
x
as usual:

B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x})=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}:\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\rVert_{2}<r\}.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. Then, we define the
interior
of
X
as

\mathrm{int}(X)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in X\mid\exists\varepsilon>0,B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\subseteq X\}.

We define the
closure
of
X
as

\mathrm{cl}(X)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}\mid\forall\varepsilon>0,\ B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\cap X\neq\emptyset\}

and the
boundary
of
X
as

\mathrm{bd}(X)=\mathrm{cl}(X)\setminus\mathrm{int}(X).

Let
G\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a set of (generating) vectors. We write
\mathrm{cone}(G)

to denote the
(rational convex) cone

\{\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\boldsymbol{g_{i}}\mid k\in\mathbb{N},\boldsymbol{g_{i}}\in G,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\}
. The
(linear) span
of

G
is defined as follows:
\mathrm{span}(G)=\{\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\boldsymbol{g_{i}}\mid k\in\mathbb{N},\boldsymbol{g_{i}}\in G,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\}
. Finally, the
affine
hull

\mathrm{aff}(G)
of
G
is the set
\{\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\boldsymbol{g_{i}}\mid k\in\mathbb{N},\boldsymbol{g_{i}}\in G,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q},\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}=1\}
. (In
particular, note that if
\boldsymbol{0}\in G
then
\mathrm{aff}(G)=\mathrm{span}(G)=\mathrm{span}(H)
, for some
H\subseteq G
with cardinality at most
d
.)

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. Then, we define the
relative interior of
X
,

\mathrm{relint}(X)
, as its interior with respect to its embedding into its own affine hull — where it
has full dimension
— as follows:

\mathrm{relint}(X)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in X\mid\exists\varepsilon>0,B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\cap\mathrm{aff}(X)\subseteq X\}.

2.1
Continuous VASS

Let
d
be a positive integer.
A
continuous VASS

\mathcal{V}
of dimension
d
is a tuple
(Q,T,\ell)
where
Q
is a
finite set of states,
T\subseteq Q\times Q
is a finite set of transitions,
and
\ell:T\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{d}
assigns an
update label
to

Paths and runs.

A
configuration

c\in Q\times\mathbb{Q}^{d}
is a tuple consisting of
a state and the concrete values of the
d

counters
associated with the
VASS.
We typically denote the configuration
(p,\boldsymbol{x})
by
p(\boldsymbol{x})
.

A
path

\pi
is a sequence
(p_{1},p_{2})(p_{2},p_{3})\dots(p_{n-1},p_{n})\in T^{*}

|\pi|
to denote the length of the path,

|\pi|=n-1
. A

•

(q_{i},q_{i+1})\in T
and

•

Often, we refer to the

as the

.
We say

As a more concrete example, consider the path
(q_{0},q_{1}),(q_{1},q_{2}),(q_{2},q_{3})

Classes of continuous VASS.

Classical VASS semantics only allows nonnegative counter values. In contrast, we consider continuous VASS both in a setting where only nonnegative counter values are allowed, denoted
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}

\mathbb{Q}

3
The Geometry of
\mathbb{Q}
VASS Reachability Sets

In this section we discuss the geometry of the reachability sets in continuous VASS of dimension
d
. We first discuss paths and cycles separately. Then, we show that for solving the
reachability problem, we only need to take short
linear path schemes
into consideration.

3.1
The geometry of reachability sets for cycles

For this section, we fix a cycle
\chi=(p_{1},p_{2})\dots(p_{m},p_{m+1})
, with
p_{1}=p_{m+1}
. We study the geometry of the set
\mathrm{Reach}(\chi^{*})
, where
\chi^{*}
stands for
\{\chi^{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Intuitively, following
\chi
allows us to add a scaled version of each transition vector along
\chi
arbitrarily many times, with the proviso that the scaling is strictly positive (the restriction to scale up to
1
disappears since we can repeatedly take the cycle). Thus, we can intuitively reach the interior of a cone, i.e. a positive linear combination of the vectors along
\chi
. For example, a cycle with vectors
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
will allow us to reach
\{(x,y)\mid x>0,y>0\}\cup\{(0,0)\}
.
However, this intuition needs to be formalized carefully to account for linear dependencies between the vectors. This may render the cone not full-dimensional, i.e. its linear span may be a strict subspace of the vector space it is in. That would mean that the interior of the cone is empty. However, in such cases, the reachability set still is a “flattened” version of the interior, namely the relative interior of the cone.

From cycles to cones.

We formalize our intuition by proving that
\mathrm{Reach}(\chi^{*})
is the relative interior of the cone generated by

G=\{\ell(p_{i},p_{i+1})\mid 1\leq i\leq m\}
.

Indeed, all points
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{Reach}(\chi^{*})
can be obtained as positive linear combinations of generators. To any such
\boldsymbol{x}
, we can add or subtract any generating vector and stay within
\mathrm{cone}(G)
, as long as it is sufficiently scaled down. Conversely, if one can add and subtract suitably scaled versions of all generating vectors to a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{cone}(G)
, and remain within
\mathrm{cone}(G)
, then it must be in the (relative) interior of
\mathrm{cone}(G)
.

Below, we write
\mathrm{relint}(G)

for
\mathrm{relint}(\mathrm{cone}(G))
.

Theorem 3.1

Let
G
be as defined above. Then,

\mathrm{Reach}(\chi^{*})=\mathrm{relint}(G)\cup\{\boldsymbol{0}\}.

Note that we consider the set
G
of all labels of transitions from
\chi
,
ignoring the fact that multiple transitions can have the same label. This is justified by the following lemma.

Lemma 1

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. Then, there exists
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^{n}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\boldsymbol{g_{i}}
if and
only if
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{Reach}(\chi^{k})
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 1
.

3.2
The geometry of reachability sets for paths

For this section, we fix a path

\pi=(p_{1},p_{2})\dots(p_{m},p_{m+1})
.
Similarly to the case of cycles, we establish a connection
between
\mathrm{Reach}(\pi)
and a particular type of polytope known as a
zonotope.

Intuitively, since we now have a path that is taken once (rather than a cycle), the restriction that the scaling is at most
1
comes into play, and limits us. Moreover, multiplicities of linearly dependent vectors along the path also matter. This renders the reachability set somewhat more complicated.

Zonotopes.

Let
G=\{\boldsymbol{g_{1}},\dots,\boldsymbol{g_{k}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a finite set of
(generating) vectors. We write
\mathrm{zono}(G)
to denote the
zonotope
2
2

2

Zonotope is the standard term, but since we do not use any of its special properties, the reader may view this as a standard polytope.

\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{i}\boldsymbol{g_{i}}\mid a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q},0\leq a_{i}\leq 1\}
.

Following our interior-based approach for cycles, we study the reachable part of the boundary of a zonotope. We define
\sigma_{G}
as the sum of all vectors in
G
, or
\sigma_{G}\coloneqq\sum_{\boldsymbol{g}\in G}\boldsymbol{g}
.
Additionally, we define the set
\mathrm{adj}(Z)
of
faces
of
\mathrm{zono}(G)
that are
adjacent to
\sigma_{G}
below. A face of
\mathrm{zono}(G)
is any non-empty intersection of
\mathrm{zono}(G)
with a half-space
H
such that none of the interior points of
\mathrm{zono}(G)
lies on the boundary of
H
.

Definition 1
(Adjacent Sets)

We define
\mathrm{adj}(G)
as the union of
\{\sigma_{G}\}
and all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
on the relative interior of a face of
\mathrm{zono}(G)
that contains
\sigma_{G}
.

Observe that
\mathrm{adj}(G)=\emptyset
whenever
\sigma_{G}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{zono}(G))
. In
Figure 2

From paths to zonotopes.

We show
\mathrm{Reach}(\pi)
has a close relation with a zonotope whose generator is derived from the
multiset

M\coloneqq[\ell(p_{i},p_{i+1})\mid 1\leq i\leq m]
.
Intuitively, we obtain from
M
a generator
G_{M}
by summing together co-linear vectors that are in the same “orientation”. For example, the vectors
(1,0)
and
(2,0)
along a single path have the same effect as
(3,0)
, but
(1,0)
and
(-1,0)
have distinct effects. Technically, this is captured by grouping together vectors that are in the cones of each other. More formally,
choose
M^{\prime}\subseteq M\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
such that for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in M\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
there is a unique vector
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in M^{\prime}
such that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{cone}{(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})}
. Then,
define
G_{M}
as follows:

G_{M}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in M\cap\mathrm{cone}{(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}})}\boldsymbol{u}\>\middle|\>\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in M^{\prime}\right\}.

We show that the reachability set of
\pi
can be computed by taking
\mathrm{zono}(G_{M})
, and removing its boundary except for faces adjacent to
\sigma_{G}
. The intuition behind this is similar to that of cycles: one can add strictly positive-scaled versions of the generating vectors, and therefore boundary elements that are obtained using 0-scales are unreachable. However, in zonotopes there are additional boundary faces that are obtained by capping the scale at
1
on some elements, and these are the faces adjacent to
\sigma_{G}

3.3
The geometry of reachability sets for linear path schemes

We now turn to combine our geometrical understanding of cycles (

A linear path scheme (LPS, for short) is a regular expression
\pi_{0}\chi_{1}^{*}\pi_{1}\dots\chi_{n}^{*}\pi_{n}
over the transitions. Importantly, the
\pi_{i}
are
(possibly empty) paths; the

\{\pi_{0}\chi_{1}^{k_{1}}\dots\chi_{n}^{k_{n}}\pi_{n}\mid k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\}
of paths that follows each of the paths exactly once and each of
the cycles an arbitrary number of times.

For the rest of this section, we fix a

\sigma=\pi_{0}\chi_{1}^{*}\pi_{1}\dots\chi_{n}^{*}\pi_{n}
.

From previous developments in this work, the reader might already believe that the reachability set of an LPS can be shown to be the Minkowski sum of suitable subsets of convex cones and zonotopes. It transpires that one can further simplify this and obtain a characterization which involves a single zonotope and a polynomial number of cones.

Below, we write
G(\pi)

as defined in

; for each

to denote the generator of the convex cone for the cycle

\mathrm{Reach}(\sigma)
is:

\textstyle\mathrm{relint}(\mathrm{zono}(G(\pi)))\cup\mathrm{adj}(G(\pi))+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{relint}\left(\mathrm{cone}\left(\bigcup_{i\in I_{j}}G(\chi_{i})\right)\right)\cup\{\boldsymbol{0}\}.

4
The Complexity of
\mathbb{Q}
VASS Reachability

In this section, we use our results concerning the geometry of reachability sets to give an
\mathbf{NP}

Theorem 4.1

For every

, given a
\mathbb{Q}

and

First, without loss of generality, we assume

if and only if

In the following we prove
that if

then there is a linear path scheme
\sigma
such that

for some

and

and exponential on the dimension

4.1
Short linear path schemes suffice

Presently, we argue that for any path we can find an LPS with a number of cycles that is polynomial in the number of transitions of the
\mathbb{Q}
VASS and exponential on the dimension such that all paths and cycles are simple, the set of transitions in the LPS is the same as that in the path, and the reachability set of the LPS includes that of the path.

For convenience, we define the
support
of a path
\pi=t_{1}\dots t_{n}
as the set of all transitions that are present in the path:
\llbracket\pi\rrbracket\coloneqq\{t_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
.
For an LPS
\sigma=\pi_{0}\chi_{1}^{*}\dots\chi^{*}_{n}\pi_{n}
, its support is
\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket\coloneqq\llbracket\pi_{0}\rrbracket\cup\bigcup_{i=1}\llbracket\chi_{i}\rrbracket\cup\llbracket\pi_{i}\rrbracket
.

Theorem 4.2

Let
\pi
be an arbitrary path. Then, there exists a linear path scheme
\sigma=\pi_{0}\chi_{1}^{*}\pi_{1}\dots\chi_{n}^{*}\pi_{n}
such that:

•

n\leq d|T|^{d+1}
,

•

\pi_{i}
is a simple path for all
0\leq i\leq n
,

•

\chi_{i}
is a simple cycle for all
1\leq i\leq n
,

•

\llbracket\pi\rrbracket=\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket
, and

•

\mathrm{Reach}(\pi)\subseteq\mathrm{Reach}(\sigma)
.

Most properties of the LPS in the result above follow from considering an LPS with minimal length, with the length of an LPS defined as
|\sigma|\coloneqq|\pi_{0}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\chi_{i}|+|\pi_{i}|
. The main technical hurdle is thus the upper bound on the number of cycles. Our approach is to remove cycles whose support is covered by other cycles. The result below, which follows directly from
Theorem 3.1
and
4
, gives us that flexibility. As in
subsection 3.3
, we write
G(\chi)
to denote the generator of the convex cone for
\chi
, i.e.
G(\chi)=\{\ell(t)\mid t\in\llbracket\chi\rrbracket\}
.

Lemma 5

Let
\chi
be a cycle and
C
be a set of cycles. If
\llbracket\chi\rrbracket\subseteq\bigcup_{\theta\in C}\llbracket\theta\rrbracket
and
\mathrm{span}(G(\theta_{1}))=\mathrm{span}(G(\theta_{2}))
for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in C\cup\{\chi\}
then:

\textstyle\mathrm{Reach}(\chi^{*})+\sum_{\theta\in C}\mathrm{Reach}(\theta^{*})=\sum_{\theta\in C}\mathrm{Reach}(\theta^{*}).

Proof
(of
Theorem 4.2
)

By the pigeonhole principle, if
D
is a set of cycles with
|D|>|T|
then
there is some
\chi\in D
such that
\llbracket\chi\rrbracket\subseteq\bigcup_{\theta\in C}\llbracket\theta\rrbracket
, with
C\coloneqq D\setminus\{\chi\}
. It thus follows from
5

that to bound the number of cycles from the LPS in

it
suffices to bound the size of the set of possible spans. Since the labels of

\mathbb{Q}^{d}

From the results above, it follows that to check whether a configuration is

4.2
Reachability for linear path schemes is tractable

In this section, we show that determining whether a configuration is reachable via a linear path scheme is decidable in polynomial time.

Theorem 4.3

Given a linear path scheme
\sigma
and
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}

Based on
3

It is known that the feasibility problem for linear programs is decidable in polynomial time even in the presence of strict inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
20

\textstyle(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\prec b)\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}\bigwedge_{j\in J_{i}}x_{j}>0,

\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d},b\in\mathbb{Q}

[
3

1

7
Conclusion

In this work, we give geometrical characterizations for the reachability sets of continuous VASS as well their flat and linear path scheme restrictions. Using these characterizations, we show that for any fixed dimension, polynomial-sized linear path schemes suffice as witnesses of reachability and that reachability in linear path schemes is tractable (even if the dimension is not fixed). In addition, sharpen hardness results for reachability for continuous VASS in the presence of zero tests: it is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard already for dimension two.

Two problems that remain open are the following. First, we do not know whether reachability is decidable for continuous VASS with zero tests of dimension
d=2,3
. In dimension
d=1
, the problem is known to be solvable in polynomial time
[
4
]
. Second, we do not know what the exact complexity of the reachability problem is for fixed-dimension LPS with zero tests.
