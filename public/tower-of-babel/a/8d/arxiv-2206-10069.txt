Moments and asymptotics for a class of SPDEs with space-time white noise
Le Chen, Yuhui Guo, Jian Song

Abstract
. In this article, we consider the nonlinear stochastic partial differential equation of fractional order in both space and time variables with constant initial condition: ( ∂ t β + ν 2 ( − Δ ) α / 2 ) u ( t , x ) = I t γ [ λ u ( t , x ) W ˙ ( t , x ) ] t > 0 , x ∈ ℝ d , \left(\partial^{\beta}_{t}+\dfrac{\nu}{2}\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}\right)u(t,x)=\>I_{t}^{\gamma}\left[\lambda u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x)\right]\quad t>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, where W ˙ \dot{W} is space–time white noise, α > 0 \alpha>0 , β ∈ ( 0 , 2 ] \beta\in(0,2] , γ ≥ 0 \gamma\geq 0 , λ ≠ 0 \lambda\neq 0 and ν > 0 \nu>0 . The existence and uniqueness of solution in the Itô-Skorohod sense is obtained under Dalang’s condition. We obtain explicit formulas for both the second moment and the second moment Lyapunov exponent. We derive the p p -th moment upper bounds and find the matching lower bounds. Our results solve a large class of conjectures regarding the order of the p p -th moment Lyapunov exponents. In particular, by letting β = 2 \beta=2 , α = 2 \alpha=2 , γ = 0 \gamma=0 , and d = 1 d=1 , we confirm the following standing conjecture for the stochastic wave equation: t − 1 ​ log ⁡ 𝔼 ⁡ [ u ​ ( t , x ) p ] ≍ p 3 / 2 , for p ≥ 2 as t → ∞ . \displaystyle t^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)^{p}]\asymp p^{3/2},\quad\text{for $p\geq 2$ as $t\to\infty$.} The method for the lower bounds is inspired by a recent work by Hu and Wang [ HW21 ] , where the authors focus on the space-time colored Gaussian noise.

1.
Introduction

Let
\dot{W}
be a
space-time white noise
, namely, a centered Gaussian noise with covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)]=\delta(t-s)\delta(x-y),

(1.1)

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function. The following
stochastic heat equation

\text{(SHE)}\quad\begin{cases}\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}-\dfrac{\nu}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\right)u(t,x)=\>\lambda u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},&\end{cases}

(1.2)

and
stochastic wave equation

\text{(SWE)}\quad\begin{cases}\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\dfrac{\nu}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\right)u(t,x)=\>\lambda u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},\quad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}u(0,\cdot)=u_{1},\end{cases}

(1.3)

with
\lambda\neq 0
,
\nu>0
,
u_{0},u_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
are two canonical stochastic partial differential equations. SHE
(
1.2
) has been widely and extensively studied with many fine properties, among which the probability moments
enjoy the following explicit asymptotics:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}p^{-3}

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{p}\right]=\frac{\lambda^{4}}{24},

\displaystyle\text{for all $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and},

(1.4a)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{p}\right]=\frac{1}{24}p(p^{2}-1)\lambda^{4},

\displaystyle\text{for all $p\geq 2$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$};

(1.4b)

see
[
Che15
]
and references therein. One major tool in studying this parabolic
equation is the
Feynman-Kac representation
of the moments as being used in
ibid
. Note
that the quantity on the right-hand side of (
1.4b
) is called the
p
-th moment
Lyapunov exponent
, which characterizes the intermittency property of the solution; see

[
CM94
]
.

In contrast, much less is known for the hyperbolic counterpart — SWE (
1.3
). The lack of
Feynman-Kac representation for the moments poses a major difficulty in this study. To our best
knowledge, only the following upper bound of the
p
-th moment Lyapunov exponent is known: for some
constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{p}\right]\leq Cp^{3/2}\quad\text{for all $p\geq 2$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$};

(1.5)

see, e.g.,
[
CD15
]
. It has been long conjectured that the exponent
3/2
in
(
1.5
) is sharp; yet there lacks of a rigorous proof. Dalang and Mueller

[
DM09
]
studied the three-dimensional SWE with a Gaussian noise that
is white-in-time and colored-in-space with a bounded correlation function, namely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)\right]=\delta(t-s)f(x-y),

(1.6)

where
f
is a nonnegative, nonnegative definite and bounded function. Using an earlier developed
Feynman-Kac-type formula for moments in
[
DMT08
]
, they
established the following large-time asymptotics:

\displaystyle C_{1}p^{4/3}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}\left[|u(t,x)|^{p}\right]}{t}\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}\left[|u(t,x)|^{p}\right]}{t}\leq C_{2}p^{4/3},

(1.7)

for all
p\geq 2
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
; see Theorem 1.1 (
ibid.
). To obtain the lower bound in
(
1.7
), their arguments crucially depend on the property that one can find a small
indicator function below
f(x)
near the origin, i.e.,
c1_{\{|x|\leq r\}}\leq f(x)
for all

x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This requirement prevents the application to the space-time white noise case. Recently,
Hu and Wang
[
HW21
]
obtained the matching lower and upper
p
-th moment
Lyapunov exponents for a wide range of SPDEs with space-time colored Gaussian noise

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)\right]=\gamma(t-s)\Lambda(x-y).

(1.8)

Certain choices or limits of the parameters of the noise in (
1.8
) may suggest the
correct moment asymptotics for SWE (
1.3
). However, just as the SHE case, the SWE with space-time white noise needs a separate treatment (see Remark
5.9
for more details). One
of the major contributions of this paper is to carry out such arguments and confirm the conjecture
about the moment asymptotics of SWE (
1.3
) by showing that if
u_{0}>0
and
u_{1}\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle C_{1}p^{3/2}\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)^{p}]}{t}\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)^{p}]}{t}\leq C_{2}p^{3/2},

\displaystyle p\geq 2,

(1.9a)

\displaystyle C_{3}\>t\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{p\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)^{p}]}{p^{3/2}}\leq\limsup_{p\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}[u(t,x)^{p}]}{p^{3/2}}\leq C_{4}\>t,

\displaystyle t>0,

(1.9b)

where
C_{1},\cdots,C_{4}
are some nonnegative constants that do not depend on
t
and
p
.

It turns out that the method that we use to resolve the above conjecture can be applied to a much
wider class of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). Indeed, in this paper, we will
study the following stochastic fractional diffusion equation with both SHE (
1.2
) and SWE
(
1.3
) as two special cases:

\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{t}^{\beta}+\dfrac{\nu}{2}\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}\right)u(t,x)=\>I_{t}^{\gamma}\left[\lambda u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x)\right],&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},&\text{ if }\beta\in(0,1],\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},\quad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}u(0,\cdot)=u_{1},&\text{ if }\beta\in(1,2],\end{cases}

(1.10)

where
\dot{W}
is space-time white noise,
\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}
is the fractional
Laplacian and

\displaystyle\alpha>0,\quad\beta\in(0,2],\quad\gamma\geq 0,\quad\lambda\neq 0,\quad\nu>0,\quad u_{0},u_{1}\in\mathbb{R}.

The symbol
\partial_{t}^{\beta}
denotes the
Caputo fractional differential
operator of order

\beta>0
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\beta}f(t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(n-\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f^{(n)}(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{\beta+1-n}}\mathrm{d}\tau,&\mbox{if }\beta\neq n,\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}t^{n}}f(t),&\mbox{if }\beta=n,\end{cases}

where
n=\left\lceil\beta\right\rceil
is the smallest integer that is not smaller than
\beta
(i.e.,

\left\lceil\cdot\right\rceil
is the ceiling function), and
\Gamma(x)
is the
gamma function
. We use

I_{t}^{\gamma}
to refer to the
Riemann-Liouville integral
in the time variable to the right of
zero
I_{0+}^{\gamma}
; see Definition
A.1
.

The SPDE (
1.10
) is interpreted as the following integral equation:

u(t,x)=J_{0}(t,x)+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x-y)u(s,y)W(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y),

(1.11)

where
J_{0}(t,x)
is the solution to the homogeneous equation (see (
3.3
) and
(
3.4
) below),
p(t,x)
is the underlying fundamental solution (see (
3.2
)), and the
stochastic integral refers to the
Walsh
or
Skorohod integral
. Set the following four
constants:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lcl}\theta:=2(\beta+\gamma)-2-\beta d/\alpha,&&t_{p}:=p^{1+1/(1+\theta)}t,\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\Theta:=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E^{2}_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}(-2^{-1}\nu|\xi|^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\xi,&&\widehat{t}:=\Theta\>\Gamma\left(\theta+1\right)t^{\theta+1},\end{array}

where the function
E_{a,b}(z)
is the
two parameter Mittag-Leffler function
of two parameters
(see, e.g.,
[
KST06
, Section 1.8]
), i.e., for
a,b>0
,

\displaystyle E_{a,b}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(ak+b)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

(1.14)

We use the convention
E_{a}(\cdot):=E_{a,1}(\cdot)
.

We will prove in Theorem
3.3
below that under
Dalang’s condition

\begin{cases}\displaystyle d<2\alpha+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\min\{2\gamma-1,0\},&\text{ if }\beta\in(0,2),\\
d<\alpha\min\{2,1+\gamma\},&\text{ if }\beta=2,\end{cases}

(1.15)

there exists an unique random field solution
u(t,x)
with finite
p
-th moment for all
p\geq 2
,
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is an easy exercise to check that Dalang’s condition (
1.15
)
implies that
\theta>-1
and
\Theta<\infty
, so that all constants in (
1
) are
well-defined. The aim of this paper is to establish the following theorem, which gives the exact
formula for the second moment and the sharp moment asymptotics both in terms of
t
and
p\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that Dalang’s condition (
1.15
) is satisfied and let
u(t,x)
be the solution to
(
1.10
). Recall that the quantities
\theta
,
\Theta
,
t_{p}
and
\widehat{t}
are defined
in (
1
). The
p
-th moment satisfies the following properties:

(a) When
p=2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[u^{2}(t,x)\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rc}u_{0}^{2}\>E_{\theta+1}\left(\lambda^{2}\widehat{t}\>\right)&\qquad\text{if $\beta\in(0,1]$,}\\

(1.16)

for all

and

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{2}\right]=\left(\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1)\right)^{1/(\theta+1)},\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

(1.17)

(b) For any

\displaystyle\left|\left|u(t,x)\right|\right|_{p}^{2}\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ rc }2u_{0}^{2}\>E_{\theta+1}\left(8p\lambda^{2}\widehat{t}\>\right)&\qquad\text{if $\beta\in(0,1]$,}\\

\end{array}\right.

for all

and

(1.22)

(1.23b)

(c) If in addition we have

(1)

either

and the fundamental function

is nonnegative or

\alpha=\beta=2
and
\gamma=0
; and

(2)

then by treating

\mathbb{R}_{+}

\displaystyle C:=\liminf_{t_{p}\rightarrow\infty}t_{p}^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{p}\right]>0.

(1.24)

In particular, by freezing an even integer
p\geq 2
or
t>0
, with the same constant
C

(

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{p}\right]\geq Cp^{1+1/(\theta+1)},

(1.25a)

\displaystyle\liminf_{p\rightarrow\infty}p^{-\left(1+1/(\theta+1)\right)}\log\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{p}\right]\geq Ct.

(1.25b)

\displaystyle J_{0}(t,x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)\mathrm{d}y,&\text{if $\beta\in(0,1]$},\\
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}^{*}(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}(t,x)u_{1}(y)\mathrm{d}y,&\text{if $\beta\in(1,2]$}.\end{cases}

(3.3)

When the initial conditions
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
are two constants, then by (
C.4
) and
(
C.6
),
J_{0}(t,x)
does not depend on
x
and hence is denoted by
J_{0}(t)
later on:

J_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}u_{0}\mathcal{F}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}(t,\cdot)(0)=u_{0},&\text{if $\beta\in(0,1]$},\\
u_{0}\mathcal{F}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}^{*}(t,\cdot)(0)+u_{1}\mathcal{F}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}(t,\cdot)(0)=u_{0}+u_{1}t,&\text{if $\beta\in(1,2]$},\end{cases}

(3.4)

where
\mathcal{F}g=\widehat{g}
is the Fourier transform of
g
in spatial variable, i.e., if

g(t,\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}g(t,\xi)=\widehat{g}(t,\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\mathrm{d}x.

Let
W=\left\{W_{t}(A):A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),t\geq 0\right\}
be a space–time white noise defined on a
complete probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the
collection of Borel sets with finite Lebesgue measure. Let

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(W_{s}(A):0\leq s\leq t,A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\vee\mathcal{N},\quad t\geq 0,

be the natural filtration augmented by the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{N}
generated by all
\mathbb{P}
-null
sets in
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A process
u=\left\{u(t,x):t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}
is called a
random field solution
to
(
3.1
) if it is adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, jointly
measurable with respect to
\mathcal{B}\left((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\times\mathcal{F}
,
square integrable in the sense that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}y\>p(t-s,x-y)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho\left(u(s,y)\right)^{2}\right]<\infty,\quad\text{for all $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,}

and satisfies the following integral equation a.s.

u(t,x)=J_{0}(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x-y)\rho\left(u(s,y)\right)W(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y),

(3.5)

for all
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
J_{0}(t,x)
is given by (
3.3
) and the stochastic
integral on the right-hand side is the
Walsh integral

[
Wal86
]
.

The existence and uniqueness of the mild solution to (
3.1
) with the bounded initial
conditions are well covered by the classical Dalang-Walsh theory; see

[
Wal86
,
Dal99
,
Dal+09
]
. In that
theory,
Dalang’s condition
usually refers to some simplified, but still equivalent, conditions
to

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(s,y)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y<\infty,\quad\text{for all $t>0$}.

(3.6)

Note that condition (
3.6
) is the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence and
uniqueness of a global solution for the corresponding linear equation, i.e., the case when

\rho(u)\equiv 1
in (
3.1
). The following lemma finds out the explicit form of Dalang’s
condition for (
1.10
) and (
3.1
), which extends Lemma 5.3 of

[
CHN19
]
from the case
\alpha\in(0,2]
and
\beta\in(0,2)
to the case

\alpha>0
and
\beta\in(0,2]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Dalang’s condition)
.

For the SPDE (
3.1
), Dalang’s condition (
3.6
) is equivalent to
(
1.15
).

Proof.

By (
C.5
) and the Parseval–Plancherel identity, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|p(s,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{p}(s,\xi)|^{2}d\xi=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}s^{2(\beta+\gamma)-2}E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}^{2}(-2^{-1}\nu|\xi|^{\alpha}s^{\beta})\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{2(\beta+\gamma)-2-\beta d/\alpha}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}(2^{-1}\nu)^{-d/\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}^{2}(-|\eta|^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\eta,

(3.7)

where in the last step we have used the change of variable
\xi=(2^{-1}\nu s^{\beta})^{-1/\alpha}\eta
. Then clearly, Dalang’s condition (
3.6
) is equivalent to

\begin{cases}&2(\beta+\gamma)-2-\beta d/\alpha>-1,\\
&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}^{2}(-|\xi|^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\xi<\infty.\end{cases}

(3.8)

To characterize the second condition in (
3.8
), noting that

E_{\beta,\beta+\nu}^{2}\left(-|\cdot|\right)
is locally integrable, it suffices to know the
asymptotic behavior of
E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}^{2}(-|\xi|^{\alpha})
as
|\xi|\to\infty
. By Lemma

A.6
, as
|\xi|\to\infty
,

E_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}\left(-|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)|\xi|^{\alpha}}+O(|\xi|^{-2\alpha}),&\beta\in(0,2),\\
\displaystyle\frac{\cos\left(\sqrt{|\xi|^{\alpha}}-\pi(\gamma+1)/2\right)}{|\xi|^{\alpha(1+\gamma)/2}}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)|\xi|^{\alpha}}+O\left(|\xi|^{-2\alpha}\right),&\beta=2,\end{cases}

(3.9)

Then, for
\beta\in(0,2)
, clearly (
3.8
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle 2(\beta+\gamma)-2-\frac{\beta d}{\alpha}>-1~\text{ and }~2\alpha>d,

which can be also expressed as
d<2\alpha+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\min\{2\gamma-1,0\}
. For the case

\beta=2
, by (
3.9
), we have as
|\xi|\to\infty
,

\displaystyle E^{2}_{\beta,\beta+\gamma}\left(-|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\cos^{2}\left(\sqrt{|\xi|^{\alpha}}-\pi(\gamma+1)/2\right)}{|\xi|^{\alpha(1+\gamma)}}+\frac{1}{\Gamma^{2}(\gamma)|\xi|^{2\alpha}}

\displaystyle+2\frac{\cos\left(\sqrt{|\xi|^{\alpha}}-\pi(\gamma+1)/2\right)}{\Gamma(\gamma)|\xi|^{\alpha(3+\gamma)/2}}+O\left(|\xi|^{-5\alpha/2}\right).

Thus, the second condition in (
3.8
) is equivalent to, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}\frac{\cos^{2}\left(\sqrt{|\xi|^{\alpha}}-\pi(\gamma+1)/2\right)}{|\xi|^{\alpha(1+\gamma)}}\mathrm{d}\xi<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{2\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\xi<\infty,

where the first condition is equivalent to
\alpha(1+\gamma)>d
by Lemma
B.1
and
the second one is
2\alpha>d
. Therefore, when
\beta=2
, we have that (
3.8
) is
equivalent to

\begin{cases}&d<\alpha(\gamma+\frac{3}{2}),\\
&d<\alpha\min\{2,1+\gamma\},\end{cases}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d<\alpha\min\left\{2,1+\gamma\right\}.

This completes the proof of Lemma
3.2
.
∎

(3.10)

,

]
.

Remark 3.5

In
[

]
and

Remark 3.6

(Second moment comparison for nonlinear SPDEs)
.

Let
u(t,x)
be the solution to (
3.1
) as stated in Theorem
3.3
. Suppose that

\sigma
is Lipschitz continuous and satisfies the following cone condition with some constants

0\leq\underline{\lambda}\leq\overline{\lambda}
:

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}|x|\leq\left|\sigma(x)\right|\leq\overline{\lambda}|x|,\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Then by denoting the right-hand side of (
1.16
) by
f_{\lambda}(t)
, the moment formula in
Theorem
1.1
can be extended directly to this case by the following
moment comparison principle for the second moment:

\displaystyle f_{\underline{\lambda}}(t)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[u(t,x)^{2}\right]\leq f_{\overline{\lambda}}(t).

(3.11)

When the noise is white in time but colored in space (see (
1.6
)),
the moment comparison principle (for
p\geq 2
) or more generally the stochastic comparison
principle becomes much more involved and the parabolic nature of the equation will play an
important role. Hence, one can in principle only handle the case when
\beta=1
. One may check
the work along this line in
[
CK19
,
CH19
,
CK20
]
. However, for the
space-time white noise case, the second moment comparison as in (

) comes for
free. Note that when the noise is colored in time (see (
1.8
)), to the best our
knowledge, one can only handle the linear case, namely,
\sigma(u)=\lambda u
. In this case, the
moment comparison principle can be easily established by comparing the movements chaos by chaos.

Remark 3.7

(Wiener chaos expansion)
.

When
\rho(u)=\lambda u
, instead of using Dalang-Walsh theory, one can equivalently establish the
solution to (
1.10
) using the Wiener chaos expansion specified as follows: Set
u_{0}(t,x)=J_{0}(t)
and for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle u_{n}(t,x)=J_{0}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}\int_{[0,t]^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{kd}}g_{k}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{k};t,x)W(\mathrm{d}s_{1},\mathrm{d}x_{1})\cdots W(\mathrm{d}s_{k},\mathrm{d}x_{k}),

where

\displaystyle g_{k}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{k};t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(t-s_{k},x-x_{k})p(s_{k}-s_{k-1},x_{k}-x_{k-1})\cdots p(s_{2}-s_{1},x_{2}-x_{1})J_{0}(s_{1}){{\rm 1\mkern-1.5mu}\!{\rm I}}_{\{0<s_{1}<\dots<s_{k}<t\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{r=1}^{k}p(s_{r+1}-s_{r},x_{r+1}-x_{r})J_{0}(s_{1}){{\rm 1\mkern-1.5mu}\!{\rm I}}_{\{0<s_{1}<\dots<s_{k}<t\}},

(3.12)

where we use the convention
s_{k+1}=t
and
x_{k+1}=x
. Then, the mild solution has the
following so-called Wiener chaos representation:

u(t,x)=J_{0}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot;t,x)),

(3.13)

where
f_{k}(\cdot;t,x)
is the symmetrization of
g_{k}(\cdot;t,x)
given by, denoting by
\mathcal{P}_{k}
the set of all permutations of
\{1,\dots,k\}
,

f_{k}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{k};t,x)=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}g_{k}(s_{\sigma(1)},\dots,s_{\sigma(k)},x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(k)};t,x)

(3.14)

and
I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot;t,x))
denotes the
k
-th multiple Wiener-Itô integral. We refer the
interested readers to
[
Hu17
]
for more details.

4.
Second moment formula and upper bounds for the
p
-th moments

In this section, we shall prove parts (a) and (b) of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of part (a) of Theorem
1.1
.

By the Itô-Walsh isometry, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[u^{2}(t,x)\right]=J_{0}^{2}(t)+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{2}(t-s,x-y)\mathbb{E}\left[u^{2}(s,y)\right]\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y,

where
J_{0}(t)
is given by (
3.4
). Note that due to the choice of the constant initial
conditions, the solution to the homogeneous equation does not depend on
x
, i.e.,

J_{0}(t,x)=J_{0}(t)
. Hence, through a standard Picard iteration, one can show that the second moment

\mathbb{E}\left(u(t,x)^{2}\right)
does not depend on
x
. Let
\eta(t)=\mathbb{E}\left(u(t,x)^{2}\right)
. Invoking
(
3
), the above equation can be written as

\eta(t)=J_{0}^{2}(t)+\lambda^{2}\Theta\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\theta}\eta(s)\mathrm{d}s,

(4.1)

where
\theta
and
\Theta
are given in (
1
). Now we solve the fractional integral
equation (
4.1
) for
\beta\in(0,1]
and for
\beta\in(1,2]
separately.

Case 1.
When
\beta\in(0,1]
, we have
J_{0}(t)=u_{0}
by (
C.3
) and thus
(
4.1
) is equivalent to

\begin{cases}\left(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}\eta\right)(t)=\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1)\eta(t)+(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}u_{0}^{2})(t),\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\eta(0)=u_{0}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta^{(k)}(0)=0,\text{ for }k=1,2,...,\left\lceil\theta\right\rceil,\end{cases}

where
D^{\theta+1}
is Riemann-Liouville derivative given in Definition
A.2
. Using the
Caputo fractional derivative given in Definition
A.5
, it can written as

\begin{cases}\left({}^{C}D_{0+}^{\theta+1}\eta\right)(t)=\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1)\eta(t).\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\eta(0)=u_{0}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta^{(k)}(0)=0,\text{ for }k=1,2,...,\left\lceil\theta\right\rceil,\end{cases}

of which the solution is directly given by (
A.4
):

\eta(t)=u_{0}^{2}E_{\theta+1}\left(\lambda^{2}\widehat{t}\>\right).

This proves the first part of (
1.16
).

Case 2.
When
\beta\in(1,2]
, we have
J_{0}(t)=u_{0}+u_{1}t
by (
C.3
) and
(
4.1
) now is

\eta(t)=u_{0}^{2}+2u_{0}u_{1}t+u_{1}^{2}t^{2}+\lambda^{2}\Theta\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\theta}\eta(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.2)

Let
f(t):=2u_{0}u_{1}t+u^{2}_{1}t^{2}
then (
4.1
) can be written as

\begin{cases}(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}\eta)(t)=\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1)\eta(t)+(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}[u_{0}^{2}+f(\cdot)])(t),\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\eta(0)=u_{0}^{2},~\eta^{(1)}(0)=2u_{0}u_{1},~\eta^{(2)}(0)=2u_{1}^{2},\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\eta^{(k)}(0)=0,\text{ for $k=3,\dots,\left\lceil\theta\right\rceil$.}\end{cases}

(4.3)

In order to apply the formula (
A.4
), we will transform (
4.3
) into a Caputo
fractional differential equation. When
\theta+1\in(0,1)
, by (
A.2
), we can write
(
4.3
) as

\begin{cases}{}^{C}D_{0+}^{\theta+1}\eta(t)=\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1)\eta(t)+(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}f)(t).\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\eta(0)=u_{0}^{2}.\end{cases}

The solution now follows directly from (
A.4
):

\displaystyle\eta(t)=

\displaystyle~u_{0}^{2}E_{\theta+1}\left(\lambda^{2}\widehat{t}\>\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\theta}E_{\theta+1,\theta+1}\left(\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1)(t-s)^{\theta+1}\right)(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}f)(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.4)

For the integral on the right-hand side, by (
1.14
) and Lemma
A.3
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\theta}E_{\theta+1,\theta+1}\left(\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1)(t-s)^{\theta+1}\right)(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}f)(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\theta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1))^{k}}{\Gamma((k+1)(\theta+1))}(t-s)^{k(\theta+1)}(D_{0+}^{\theta+1}f)(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1))^{k}\left(I_{0+}^{(\theta+1)(k+1)}D_{0+}^{\theta+1}f\right)(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\lambda^{2}\Theta\Gamma(\theta+1))^{k}\left(I_{0+}^{k(\theta+1)}f\right)(t).

The term
\left(I_{0+}^{k(\theta+1)}f\right)(t)=\left(I_{0+}^{k(\theta+1)}(2u_{0}u_{1}s+u_{1}^{2}s^{2})\right)(t)
can be computed explicitly noting that Lemma
A.4
yields

\left(I_{0+}^{k(\theta+1)}s\right)(t)=\frac{t^{k(\theta+1)+1}}{\Gamma(k(\theta+1)+2)}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(I_{0+}^{k(\theta+1)}s^{2}\right)(t)=\frac{2t^{k(\theta+1)+2}}{\Gamma(k(\theta+1)+3)}.

(4.5)

Combining (
4
)–(

) and applying (

), we arrive at

\eta(t)=u_{0}^{2}E_{\theta+1}\left(\lambda^{2}\widehat{t}\>\right)+2u_{0}u_{1}tE_{\theta+1,2}\left(\lambda^{2}\widehat{t}\>\right)+2u_{1}^{2}t^{2}E_{\theta+1,3}\left(\lambda^{2}\widehat{t}\>\right).

This proves the second part of (
1.16
) for
\theta+1\in(0,1)
. For the other two cases

\theta+1\in[1,2)
and
\theta+1\geq 2
, one can calculate in a similar way and prove the desired
result. Finally, (
1.17
) is a direct consequence of Lemma
A.6
. This
completes the proof of part (a) of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Remark 4.1

Proof of part (b) of Theorem
1.1
.

Fix an arbitrary

. By (

) we have

\displaystyle\|u(t,x)\|_{p}\leq|J_{0}(t)|+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(t-s,x-y)\lambda u(s,y)W(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y)\right|^{p}\right]\right)^{1/p}.

Applying the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality , we have

\|u(t,x)\|_{p}\leq|J_{0}(t)|+C_{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{2}(t-s,x-y)\lambda^{2}u^{2}(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y\right)^{p/2}\right]\right)^{1/p},

where
C_{p}
is the universal constant in the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality satisfying
C_{p}\in(0,2\sqrt{p})
and
C_{p}=(2+o(1))\sqrt{p}
as

(see

[
CK91

CK12
]
). By Minkowski’s inequality, we get

\|u(t,x)\|_{p}\leq|J_{0}(t)|+2\sqrt{p}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda^{2}p^{2}(t-s,x-y)\|u(s,y)\|_{p}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/2}.

Denote
\psi(t)=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|u(t,x)\|_{p}^{2}
and recall the definition
(
1
) of
\theta
and
\Theta
. We have

\psi(t)\leq 2\left(J_{0}^{2}(t)+4p\lambda^{2}\Theta\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\theta}\psi(s)\mathrm{d}s\right).

Applying Lemma
B.2
, we have

Proposition 5.1

For all

and

Let

Definition A.1

The

\displaystyle g(x)=\frac{\pi}{4\Gamma\left(2(1-1/\alpha)\right)\sin\left(\pi/\alpha\right)}\left[\left(x+b\right)^{1-2/\alpha}+|b-x|^{1-2/\alpha}\text{sgn}(b-x)\right].

Now we compute the Fourier transforms for these two functions. It is clear that

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\xi)=\frac{2\sin(b\>\xi)}{\xi}.

Let
h(\xi)=|\xi|^{-2(1-1/\alpha)}\sin\left(b\>|\xi|\right)
. By (2) on p. 19 of
[
Erd+54
]
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}h(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{ix\xi}h(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(\xi)\cos(x\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{1}{\pi}g(x),

under the following condition:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2}{\alpha}-1\right|<1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha>1.

Hence, when
\alpha>1
, we have
\widehat{g}(\xi)=\pi h(\xi)
. Then by the Plancherel theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{g}(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2\sin(b\>\xi)}{\xi}\pi\xi^{-2(1-1/\alpha)}\sin\left(b\>\xi\right)\mathrm{d}\xi=\alpha I.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{b}g(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2\Gamma\left(2(1-1/\alpha)\right)\sin\left(\pi/\alpha\right)}\int_{0}^{b}\left[\left(x+b\right)^{1-2/\alpha}+(b-x)^{1-2/\alpha}\right]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi 2^{1-2/\alpha}b^{2-2/\alpha}}{\Gamma\left(2-2/\alpha\right)\left(2-2/\alpha\right)\sin\left(\pi/\alpha\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi 2^{1-2/\alpha}b^{2-2/\alpha}}{\Gamma\left(3-2/\alpha\right)\sin\left(\pi/\alpha\right)}.

Hence, if
\alpha=2
, the above expression becomes
b\pi
. This proves the lemma for the case
\alpha=2
. Now if
\alpha\neq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi 2^{1-2/\alpha}b^{2-2/\alpha}}{\Gamma\left(3-2/\alpha\right)\sin\left(\pi/\alpha\right)}\times\frac{\Gamma\left(2(1/\alpha-1)\right)}{\Gamma\left(2(1/\alpha-1)\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-2/\alpha}b^{2-2/\alpha}\Gamma\left(2(1/\alpha-1)\right)}{\sin\left(\pi/\alpha\right)}\sin\left(2\pi/\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{2-2/\alpha}b^{2-2/\alpha}\Gamma\left(2(1/\alpha-1)\right)\cos\left(\pi/\alpha\right).

where we have applied the reflection formula for Gamma function (
A.7
). This proves
the lemma.
∎

The following lemma will be used to prove the lower bound of moment estimates in Theorem

5.8
. For two sequence of positive numbers
a_{n},b_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote

a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=1
.

Lemma B.4
.

(1) As
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{n}^{\infty}t^{n}e^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\sim\frac{n^{n}}{e^{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}{2}}\quad\text{and}

(B.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\frac{n^{m}}{m!}\sim\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\frac{n^{m}}{m!}\sim\frac{1}{2}e^{n}.

(B.5)

(2) Given
\alpha>0
, for
n
sufficiently large, there exist two positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending only on
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}\left(\frac{n^{m}}{m!}\right)^{\alpha}\geq c_{1}\exp(c_{2}n).

Proof.

(1) Denote the integral in (
B.4
) by
I
. By change of variable

x=t/\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n}
, we see that

I=\frac{n^{n}\sqrt{n}}{e^{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sqrt{n}x}\left(1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n}\mathrm{d}x.

Notice that
\left(1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n}e^{-\sqrt{n}x}\leq(1+x)e^{-x}
for all
t>0

and
n\geq 1
with the upper bound being integrable. Hence, by the dominated convergence theorem
and L’Hospital’s rule, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}I\frac{e^{n}}{n^{n}\sqrt{n}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}e^{-\sqrt{n}x}\left(1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}},

which proves (
B.4
).

To prove (
B.5
), it suffices to show
R_{n}(n)\sim\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\>e^{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}e^{-n}R_{2n}(n)=0
, where
R_{k}(x)
is the remainder function for the Taylor expansion of

e^{x}
:

\displaystyle R_{k}(x)=\sum_{m=k}^{\infty}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-t)^{k}}{k!}e^{t}\mathrm{d}t.

For
R_{n}(n)
, by change of variable we get

\displaystyle R_{n}(n)=\int_{0}^{n}\frac{(n-t)^{n}}{n!}e^{t}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{e^{n}}{n!}\int_{0}^{n}x^{n}e^{-x}\mathrm{d}x.

By Stirling’s formula
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}e^{-n}n^{n}
and (
B.4
), we can show

\int_{0}^{n}x^{n}e^{-x}\mathrm{d}x=\left(\Gamma(n+1)-\int_{n}^{\infty}x^{n}e^{-x}\mathrm{d}x\right)\sim\frac{1}{2}n!.

(B.6)

For
R_{2n}(n)
, we have

\displaystyle R_{2n}(n)=\int_{0}^{n}\frac{(n-t)^{2n}}{(2n)!}e^{t}\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{n^{n}}{(2n)!}\int_{0}^{n}(n-t)^{n}e^{t}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{e^{n}n^{n}}{(2n)!}\int_{0}^{n}x^{n}e^{-x}\mathrm{d}x.

Thus, by (
B.6
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{R_{2n}(n)}{e^{n}}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\frac{n^{n}(n!)}{(2n)!}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\frac{n^{2n}e^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n}}{(2n)^{2n}e^{-2n}\sqrt{4\pi n}}=0,

which proves (
B.5
).

(2) The desired result follows directly from the fact

\displaystyle\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}\left(\frac{n^{m}}{m!}\right)^{\alpha}\geq\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left(\frac{n^{m}}{m!}\right)^{\alpha}\geq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\frac{n^{m}}{m!}\right)^{\alpha},&\text{if $\alpha\in(0,1]$},\\
\displaystyle n^{1-\alpha}\left(\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\frac{n^{m}}{m!}\right)^{\alpha},&\text{if $\alpha>1$}.\end{cases}

Then an application of (
B.5
) proves (2).
∎

is the solution to the homogeneous equation and

\displaystyle Y(t,x):=Y_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,d}(t,x)={}_{t}D_{0+}^{\left\lceil\beta\right\rceil-\beta-\gamma}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}(t,x),

\displaystyle Z^{*}(t,x):=Z^{*}_{\alpha,\beta,d}(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z_{\alpha,\beta,d}(t,x),\quad\text{if $\beta\in(1,2]$,}
