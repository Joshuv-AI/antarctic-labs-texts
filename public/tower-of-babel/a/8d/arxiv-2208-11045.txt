Fusion Frame Homotopy and Tightening Fusion Frames by Gradient Descent
Tom Needham, Clayton Shonkwiler

Abstract
Finite frames, or spanning sets for finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, are a ubiquitous tool in signal processing. There has been much recent work on understanding the global structure of collections of finite frames with prescribed properties, such as spaces of unit norm tight frames. We extend some of these results to the more general setting of fusion frames—a fusion frame is a collection of subspaces of a finite-dimensional Hilbert space with the property that any vector can be recovered from its list of projections. The notion of tightness extends to fusion frames, and we consider the following basic question: is the collection of tight fusion frames with prescribed subspace dimensions path connected? We answer (a generalization of) this question in the affirmative, extending the analogous result for unit norm tight frames proved by Cahill, Mixon and Strawn. We also extend a result of Benedetto and Fickus, who defined a natural functional on the space of unit norm frames (the frame potential), showed that its global minimizers are tight, and showed that it has no spurious local minimizers, meaning that gradient descent can be used to construct unit-norm tight frames. We prove the analogous result for the fusion frame potential of Casazza and Fickus, implying that, when tight fusion frames exist for a given choice of dimensions, they can be constructed via gradient descent. Our proofs use techniques from symplectic geometry and Mumford’s geometric invariant theory.

Introduction

A
frame
is a spanning collection of vectors in a Hilbert space which satisfies a certain Parseval-like condition
[
25

. Frames are important in the context of signal processing, where a signal is modeled as a vector in a Hilbert space and is encoded by the list of its inner products with the vectors in the frame
[

For practical reasons, there has been increased interest in finite frames, that is, spanning sets of vectors for finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces; see

Typically, one requires a frame to satisfy certain norm or spectral constraints. For example, a frame

The goal of this paper is to extend results on spaces of frames to the setting of a more general signal processing tool: a
fusion frame

20

As in the case of (classical) frames, there is typically a focus on fusion frames with extra structure. For instance, the notion of a tight frame generalizes to that of a

•

In

[

1.1
Fusion Frame Homotopy

Recall that a
tight fusion frame
(TFF) is a fusion frame
(\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{N})
such that the frame operator
\sum_{i}P_{i}
is a multiple of the identity. If
k_{i}=\dim(\mathcal{S}_{i})=\operatorname{rk}(P_{i})
then, since all nonzero eigenvalues of an orthogonal projector are equal to 1,

\operatorname{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}(P_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}=:n,

so it must be the case that a TFF has frame operator equal to
\frac{n}{d}\mathbb{I}_{d}
, where
\mathbb{I}_{d}
is the identity operator on
\mathbb{K}^{d}
. Since the
P_{i}
uniquely determine and are uniquely determined by the
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, we will also call
(P_{1},\dots,P_{N})
a (tight) fusion frame when the corresponding
(\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{N})
is.

With the frame homotopy conjecture
[
26
]
(resolved by Cahill, Mixon, and Strawn in 2017
[
11
]
) in mind, the following is a very natural question:

Question 1.1
.

Is the space of TFFs in
\mathbb{K}^{d}
with given ranks
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
path-connected?

The first goal of this paper is to show that the answer to
1.1
is always “yes” for complex fusion frames (i.e., when
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
). In fact, we will prove a much more general theorem about spaces of operator-valued frames with fixed spectral data.

To motivate the definition given below, notice that, for a fusion frame
(P_{1},\dots,P_{N})
, each projector
P_{i}
has a full-rank square root
A_{i}:\mathbb{K}^{d}\to\mathbb{K}^{k_{i}}
so that
A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}=P_{i}
. This square root is unique up to composing with a unitary transformation of the codomain:

Proposition 1.2

(​​
[
33
, Theorem 7.3.11]
)
.

Suppose
A:\mathbb{K}^{d}\to\mathbb{K}^{k}
and
B:\mathbb{K}^{d}\to\mathbb{K}^{k}
are linear maps. Then
A^{\ast}A=B^{\ast}B
if and only if there exists a unitary transformation
U\in\operatorname{U}(k)
so that
B=UA
.

Hence, up to this indeterminacy, we can also think of a fusion frame as a collection of operators
(A_{1},\dots,A_{N})
so that
A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
is an orthogonal projector for each
i=1,\dots,N
and so that
A_{1}^{\ast}A_{1}+\dots+A_{N}^{\ast}A_{N}
is positive definite. This is the definition we will generalize by relaxing the condition on the individual
A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
:

Definition 1.3
.

Let
d,N,k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
be positive integers. Let
\bm{k}:=(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
. Then an
operator-valued frame
of type
(d,\bm{k})
is an
N
-tuple
\bm{A}:=(A_{1},\dots,A_{N})
of linear maps
A_{i}:\mathbb{K}^{d}\to\mathbb{K}^{k_{i}}
so that the
frame operator

S_{\bm{A}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}

is positive definite. The space of operator-valued frames of type
(d,\bm{k})
will be denoted
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
.

This definition is essentially the specialization to finite dimensions of Kaftal, Larson, and Zhang’s definition
[
34
]
of an operator-valued frame. As with fusion frames, we will define
P_{i}:=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
, so that the frame operator is
\sum_{i}P_{i}
. In practice, we will make the simplifying assumption
\operatorname{rk}(A_{i})=k_{i}
, by restricting the codomain of
A_{i}
to its image if necessary.

The feature that distinguishes fusion frames among the operator-valued frames is that the
P_{i}
are orthogonal projectors of rank
k_{i}
, which means precisely that the
k_{i}
nonzero eigenvalues of
P_{i}
are all equal to 1. More generally, we can consider spaces of operator-valued frames with fixed spectral data:

Definition 1.4
.

Let
d,N,k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
be positive integers and
\bm{k}:=(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
. For each
i
, let
\bm{r}_{i}=(r_{i1},\dots,r_{ik_{i}})
with
r_{i1}\geq\dots\geq r_{ik_{i}}>0
,
1
1

1

Remember our simplifying assumption that the
A_{i}
should be full rank.
and let
\bm{r}=(\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\bm{r}_{N})
.
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
will denote the space of operator-valued frames
(A_{1},\dots,A_{N})
of type
(d,\bm{k})
for which
P_{i}=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
has nonzero eigenvalues equal to
\bm{r}_{i}
. Equivalently, each
r_{ij}=\sigma_{ij}^{2}
, where the
\sigma_{ij}
are the singular values of
A_{i}
.

Example 1.5
.

Let
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{N})
and let
\bm{r}=(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N})
such that every
\bm{r}_{i}
is a list of
k_{i}
ones. Then
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
is equivalent to the space of fusion frames with prescribed ranks
\bm{k}
. Indeed, it is the space of
\bm{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{N})
so that
P_{i}=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
is a rank-
k_{i}
orthogonal projector and the frame operator
S_{\bm{A}}=\sum_{i}P_{i}
is positive-definite. Since this space is of particular interest, we use the specialized notation
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}:=\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
. As a special case, if
k_{i}=1
for all
i
then
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
is equivalent to the space of unit-norm frames of length
N
in
\mathbb{K}^{d}
.

Tight fusion frames are also distinguished among fusion frames by spectral data since multiples of the identity are uniquely determined by their spectra:
\lambda\mathbb{I}_{d}
is the only operator with spectrum
(\lambda,\dots,\lambda)
. Fixing the spectral data of the frame operator is more natural than fixing the frame operator itself, since we can always diagonalize the frame operator by precomposing the
A_{i}:\mathbb{K}^{d}\to\mathbb{K}^{k_{i}}
by a common unitary transformation of the domain. Hence, a natural generalization of tight frames is the collection of operator-valued frames with fixed spectrum of their frame operator:

Definition 1.6
.

Let
d,N,k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
be positive integers and
\bm{k}:=(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
. Let
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d}>0
.
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
will denote the space of operator-valued frames
(A_{1},\dots,A_{N})
whose frame operator
S_{\bm{A}}=\sum_{i}A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
has spectrum
\bm{\lambda}
.

Example 1.7
.

Let
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{N})
and let
\bm{\lambda}=(1,\ldots,1)
be the list of
d
ones. Then
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
could reasonably be called the space of Parseval operator-valued frames of type
(d,\bm{k})
, by analogy with the case
k_{i}=1
for all
i
, when
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
is equivalent to the space of Parseval frames of length
N
in
\mathbb{K}^{d}
(that is, frames whose frame operator is the identity).

Of course, the definition of a tight fusion frame includes both fixed spectral data of the
P_{i}
and fixed spectral data of the frame operator, so it involves intersecting two of the spaces defined above. In that spirit, define

\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r}):=\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}\cap\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r}).

Hence, the operator-valued generalization of
1.1
is the following:

Question 1.8
.

For given
d,N,\bm{k},\bm{r},\bm{\lambda}
, is the space
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
path-connected?

In the case of complex classical frames (i.e.,
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
and
\bm{k}=(1,\dots,1)
, but general
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{r}
) we answered this question in the affirmative using symplectic geometry
[
49
]
. Here, we give an affirmative answer for general complex operator-valued frames:

Theorem 1.9
.

The space
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is always path-connected.

Since the empty set is trivially path-connected, the substantive content of this theorem is that
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is path-connected whenever it is non-empty. For discussion of when it is empty, see
Remark

1.16
.

1.2
Benedetto–Fickus Theorem for Fusion Frames

We now specialize back to fusion frames, but again work over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Recall from
Example

1.5
that, for
d
and
N
positive and
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
,
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
denotes the space of (square roots of) fusion frames with prescribed ranks
\bm{k}
.

It is easy to generate random elements of
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
: for each
i
, let
B_{i}
be a
k_{i}\times d
matrix with standard Gaussian entries, and let
A_{i}
be the result of orthogonalizing the rows of
B_{i}
. Given their usefulness in applications, it is desirable to generate not just fusion frames, but
tight
fusion frames. Following the lead of Benedetto and Fickus
[
6
]
, a plausible strategy for doing so is to define a potential function on
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
whose global minima are exactly the set of TFFs, and then flow along the negative gradient direction of this potential. A natural candidate for such a potential is the fusion frame potential defined by Casazza and Fickus
[
15
]
, generalizing Benedetto and Fickus’ frame potential. In the definition below, and at times throughout the rest of the paper, we will abuse notation and also use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\|\cdot\|
to denote, respectively, the Frobenius inner product and norm on a space of operators; the meaning should always be clear from context.

Definition 1.10
.

Let
d,N,k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
be positive integers. The
fusion frame potential

\operatorname{FFP}:\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A}):=\left\|S_{\bm{A}}\right\|^{2}.

Note that the fusion frame potential could be generalized to arbitrary spaces of operator-valued frames, though we will not do so here. As Casazza and Fickus showed (see also
4.1
), the fusion frame potential satisfies a Welch-type lower bound which is achieved exactly at the TFFs. Hence, when they exist, TFFs are exactly the global minima of
\operatorname{FFP}
, and it is natural to ask whether we can get to these global minima by negative gradient flow:

Question 1.11
.

When they exist, can we construct TFFs by flowing along the negative gradient of
\operatorname{FFP}
? That is, are all local minima of
\operatorname{FFP}
also global in this setting?

An affirmative answer to this question is essentially a conjecture of Massey, Ruiz, and Stojanoff
[
44
, Conjecture 4.3.3]
. For unit-norm tight frames, Benedetto and Fickus showed that there are no spurious local minima of the frame potential, completely resolving
1.11
in this case:

Theorem 1.12

(Benedetto–Fickus theorem
[
6
]
)
.

Let
d
and
N
be positive and
\bm{k}=(1,\dots,1)
. Then
\operatorname{FFP}:\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}\to\mathbb{R}
has no spurious local minimizers.

In
[
46
]
we gave three new proofs of this result, one of which we now intend to generalize to fusion frames. The exposition here is self-contained, but parallels that in
[
46
, §4]
.

To state our theorem, we need to define a suitable notion of genericity for fusion frames.

Definition 1.13
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
and recall that, for each
i=1,\dots,N
, the image of the orthogonal projector
P_{i}=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
is a
k_{i}
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subset\mathbb{K}^{d}
. We say that
\bm{A}

has property

\mathscr{S}
if, for all proper linear subspaces
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{K}^{d}
,

\frac{1}{\dim\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dim(\mathcal{S}_{i}\cap\mathcal{Q})\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}=\frac{n}{d}.

Roughly speaking, this condition says that no subspace of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
intersects too many of the
\mathcal{S}_{i}
. For example, in the classical frames case
\bm{k}=(1,\dots,1)
, property
\mathscr{S}
says the fraction of frame vectors lying in any given
r
-dimensional subspace is no more than
\frac{r}{d}
. In particular, this is a much weaker condition than being full spark.

More generally, in the case of equal-rank fusion frames—i.e., each
P_{i}
is rank
k
—property
\mathscr{S}
is weaker than the condition
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dim(\mathcal{S}_{i}\cap\mathcal{Q})\leq\dim\mathcal{Q}
for all proper subspaces
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{K}^{d}
. Fusion frames satisfying this latter condition are relevant to the problem of compressed sensing with block sparsity
[
27
,
8
]
since they provide unique reconstructions for the largest possible class of block-sparse signals, much as classical full spark frames are optimal for traditional compressed sensing
[
2
,
24
]
.

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
0<d,N,k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
. Consider the negative gradient flow
\Gamma:\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
defined by the differential equation

\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},0)=\bm{A}_{0},\quad\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t)=-\operatorname{grad}\operatorname{FFP}(\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t))

where
\operatorname{grad}
is the Riemannian gradient and
\bm{A}_{0}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
is an initial frame.

If
\bm{A}_{0}
has property
\mathscr{S}
, then
\bm{A}_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t)\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
is a global minimum of
\operatorname{FFP}
.

As mentioned above, for parameters
d
and
\bm{k}
which admit TFFs, the TFFs are exactly the global minima of
\operatorname{FFP}
, so for those parameters this theorem implies that fusion frames with property
\mathscr{S}
always flow to tight fusion frames. Moreover, these are exactly the parameters for which
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
contains fusion frames with property
\mathscr{S}
(
4.14
), which turn out to be dense in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
(
4.7
). This implies that there are fusion frames arbitrarily close to any non-minimal critical point of
\operatorname{FFP}
which flow to a global minimum, so
\operatorname{FFP}
cannot have any local minima that are not global minima.

Corollary 1.15
.

When
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
contains TFFs, all local minima of
\operatorname{FFP}
are global minima.

This generalizes Benedetto and Fickus’ result to fusion frames and completely answers
1.11
. See also work of Heineken, Llarena, and Morillas
[
29
]
, which gives a similar answer for a restriction of
\operatorname{FFP}
to a subset of
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
, and of Massey, Ruiz, and Stojanoff
[
44
]
, who proved an analogous result with respect to a different notion of distance on fusion frames.

Even in some situations where there cannot be any TFFs—for example, when
d=N=3
and
\bm{k}=(1,1,2)
—the negative gradient flow of
\operatorname{FFP}
empirically seems to avoid spurious local minima, so there is hope that the conclusion of
1.15
follows from weaker hypotheses.

Remark 1.16

(Admissibility)
.

In light of
1.15
, it would be useful to know when the space of TFFs is non-empty. More generally, we can ask whether
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is non-empty, which is really prior to the question of whether it is path-connected. In the context of classical frames (i.e.,
\bm{k}=(1,\dots,1)
), this is sometimes called the
admissibility problem
, and its resolution follows easily from the Schur–Horn theorem
[
53
,
33
]
(see also
[
3
,
22
]
and Cahill, Fickus, Mixon, Poteet, and Strawn’s contructive solution
[
10
]
): in this setting,
\bm{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{N})
is a single list of positive numbers, and
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is nonempty precisely when
\bm{\lambda}

majorizes

\bm{r}

[
43
]
, meaning that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\quad\text{for all }k=1,\dots,d,

where we assume
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{r}
are sorted in decreasing order. In particular, the space
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\left(\frac{N}{d},\dots,\frac{N}{d}\right)}(1,\dots,1)
of unit-norm tight frames is always nonempty when
N\geq d
.

More generally, admissibility has been completely resolved for all spaces of tight fusion frames (therefore precisely determining the scope of
1.14
and
1.15
): by Casazza, Fickus, Mixon, Wang, and Zhou
[
16
]
when all
P_{i}
have the same rank, and in general by Bownik, Luoto, and Richmond
[
9
]
. Even for tight fusion frames, the conditions on

and
\bm{k}
which ensure non-emptiness are quite complicated, boiling down to non-vanishing of certain Littlewood–Richardson coefficients.

However, this is not really surprising, given that in general admissibility for operator-valued frames is equivalent to the following question: what
\bm{\lambda},\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\bm{r}_{N}
can be the eigenvalues of
d\times d
Hermitian matrices
M,P_{1},\dots,P_{N}
so that
M=P_{1}+\dots+P_{N}
? In 1962, Horn conjectured necessary and sufficient conditions involving a complicated system of inequalities between
\bm{\lambda}
and the
\bm{r}_{i}

[
32
]
. This became known as the Horn conjecture, which was eventually proved in the late 1990s by Klyachko
[
38
]
and Knutson and Tao
[
39
,
40
]
; see Fulton’s survey
[
28
]
for more, and Berenstein and Sjamaar’s paper
[
7
]
for a generalization. The answer does not depend on the base field: if
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is non-empty, then so is
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
(see, e.g.,
[
28
, Theorem 20]
).

Thus, the admissibility problem for operator-valued frames is at least implicitly resolved by the proof of the Horn conjecture (see also
[
44
]
), and more explicit solutions exist in the most common cases of interest, namely frames and tight fusion frames.

2
Symplectic Machinery

As in our earlier work on frames
[
49
]
, our strategy for proving
1.9
involves symplectic geometry. First, we will review some standard concepts from symplectic geometry, which will help both to provide a quick reference and to establish our notation and sign conventions. Our main references for symplectic geometry are McDuff and Salamon
[
45
]
and Cannas da Silva
[
14
]
.

2.1
Definitions

A
symplectic manifold
is a pair
(M,\omega)
, where
M
is a (smooth, real) manifold and
\omega
is a closed, nondegenerate 2-form on
M
. For each point
p\in M
and for each pair of tangent vectors
X,Y\in T_{p}M
, we write
\omega_{p}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}
for the evaluation of
\omega
at the point on the pair. Being closed means that
d\omega
is identically zero, where
d
is the exterior derivative on
M
, and being nondegenerate means that, for each
p\in M
and each
X\in T_{p}M
, there exists
Y\in T_{p}M
so that
\omega_{p}(X,Y)\neq 0
. Nondegeneracy implies that a symplectic manifold must be even-dimensional over
\mathbb{R}
.

Example 2.1
.

The simplest example of a symplectic manifold is
\mathbb{C}^{n}\approx\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. For
p\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
there is a natural isomorphism
T_{p}\mathbb{C}^{n}\approx\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and complex coordinates
(x_{1}+\sqrt{-1}y_{1},\dots,x_{n}+\sqrt{-1}y_{n})
for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
correspond to real coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
, with respect to which the
standard symplectic form
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is given by

\omega=dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}+\dots+dx_{n}\wedge dy_{n}.

We can rewrite this in complex coordinates as follows: given
p\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
Z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n}),W=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in T_{p}\mathbb{C}^{n}\approx\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\omega_{p}(Z,W)=-\operatorname{Im}(\overline{w}_{1}z_{1}+\dots+\overline{w}_{n}z_{n})=-\operatorname{Im}(W^{\ast}Z)=-\operatorname{Im}\langle Z,W\rangle.

In fact, Darboux’s theorem
[
45
, Theorem 3.2.2]
implies that every point in a symplectic manifold has a neighborhood on which the symplectic structure looks like the standard one on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Symplectic geometry grew out of Hamiltonian mechanics, and a big emphasis both historically and currently is on the interactions between symplectic structures and symmetries; that is, group actions on symplectic manifolds. In general, if
G
is a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and
G
acts on a manifold
M
, then each
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
determines a vector field
Y_{\xi}
on
M
as follows. For
p\in M
and
g\in G
, let
g\cdot p
denote the action of
g
on
p
. Then we define

\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{p}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\exp(\varepsilon\xi)\cdot p,

where
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to F
is the exponential map of
G
.

Now, suppose
G
acts on a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
. If
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is the dual of
\mathfrak{g}
, a map
\Phi:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is called a
momentum map
for the
G
-action if it satisfies the following conditions.

First, let
D\Phi(p):T_{p}M\to T_{\Phi(p)}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
be the derivative of
\Phi
at
p\in M
. Since
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is a vector space, there is a natural isomorphism
T_{\Phi(p)}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\approx\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, so we can interpret
D\Phi(p)
as a map to
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. Hence, for each
X\in T_{p}M
,
D\Phi(p)(X)
is an element of the dual space of
\mathfrak{g}
; that is,
D\Phi(p)(X):\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
\Phi
to be a momentum map we require this map to satisfy the compatibility condition

D\Phi(p)(X)(\xi)=\omega_{p}(\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{p},X)

for all
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Also, we require a momentum map
\Phi
to be equivariant with respect to the given action of
G
on
M
and the natural coadjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. More explicitly, the
adjoint action
of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}
is the linearization at the identity of the conjugation action of
G
on itself; that is, for each
g\in G
the map
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is the derivative at the identity of the map
h\mapsto ghg^{-1}
. In turn, the
coadjoint action
of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
gives a map
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}:\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\to\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
for each
g\in G
which is defined by
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}(\chi)(\xi):=\chi(\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}(\xi))
. When
G
is a matrix group, both
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}
and
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}
can be interpreted as conjugation by
g
. Now, the equivariance condition on momentum maps is that, for each
g\in G
and each
p\in M
,

\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}(\Phi(p))=\Phi(g\cdot p).

When a
G
-action admits a momentum map, the action is called
Hamiltonian
and the tuple
(M,\omega,G,\Phi)
is a
Hamiltonian
G
-space
.

Proposition 2.2

(cf.
[
45
, Exercise 5.3.16]
)
.

Suppose
G
is a Lie group and
(M_{i},\omega_{i},G,\Phi_{i})
are Hamiltonian
G
-spaces for
i=1,\dots,n
. Then the diagonal action of
G
on
M_{1}\times\dots\times M_{n}
is Hamiltonian with momentum map

\Phi(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})=\Phi_{1}(p_{1})+\dots+\Phi_{n}(p_{n}).

Proof.

The standard symplectic form on a product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}
of symplectic manifolds is
\pi_{1}^{\ast}\omega_{1}+\dots+\pi_{n}^{\ast}\omega_{n}
, where
{\pi_{k}:\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}\to M_{k}}
is projection onto the
k
th factor and
\pi_{k}^{\ast}
is the induced pullback operator on forms.

Any tangent vector
X\in T_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}
is of the form
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
for
X_{i}\in T_{p_{i}}M_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. In particular, if
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, then the associated vector field

\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})}=(\left.(Y_{1})_{\xi}\right|_{p_{1}},\dots,\left.(Y_{n})_{\xi}\right|_{p_{n}}),

where each
\left.(Y_{i})_{\xi}\right|_{p_{i}}=(d\pi_{i})_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})}\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})}
is the vector field on
M_{i}
associated to
\xi
.

Let
\Phi:\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}\to\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
be defined as in the statement of the proposition and let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in T_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle D\Phi(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D\Phi_{i}(p_{i})(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i})_{p_{i}}(\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{p_{i}},X_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{1})_{p_{i}}((d\pi_{i})_{(p_{i},\dots,p_{i})}\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})},X_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}^{\ast}(\omega_{i})_{p_{i}}(\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})},X_{i})

\displaystyle=\omega_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})}(\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})},(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})),

where we’ve used linearity in various place. So
\Phi
satisfies the appropriate compatibility condition with
\omega
.

Moreover, if
g\in G
and
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}
, then

\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}(\Phi(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}))=\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}(p_{i})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}(\Phi_{i}(p_{i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}(g\cdot p_{i})=\Phi(g\cdot p_{1},\dots,g\cdot p_{n})=\Phi(g\cdot(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}))

using linearity of
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}
and
G
-equivariance of the
\Phi_{i}
, so
\Phi
is
G
-equivariant, and hence is a momentum map for the
G
-action.
∎

2.2
Coadjoint Orbits

An important class of Hamiltonian spaces are coadjoint orbits, which we now describe in some detail, loosely following
[
45
, Example 5.3.11]
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and dual Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. Let
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and let
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
be the
coadjoint orbit
through
\chi
; that is,

\mathcal{O}_{\chi}:=\left\{\operatorname{Ad}_{g}^{\ast}(\chi)\,|\,g\in G\right\}.

It is a standard fact that
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
has a natural symplectic form called the
Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau (KKS) form

[
4
, II.3.c]
, denoted
\omega^{\text{KKS}}
, defined as follows. The tangent space to
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
at
\chi
consists of vectors of the form
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{\ast}\chi
, where
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{\ast}
is the coadjoint representation of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
; that is, the derivative of the coadjoint representation
\operatorname{Ad}^{\ast}:G\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{g}^{\ast})
at the identity. Then

\omega^{\text{KKS}}_{\chi}(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{\ast}(\chi),\operatorname{ad}_{\zeta}^{\ast}(\chi)):=\chi([\xi,\zeta]),

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{g}
.

The action of
G
on
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
is Hamiltonian, with momentum map
\Phi:\mathcal{O}_{\chi}\to\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
simply being the inclusion map. To see this, first notice that the vector field
Y_{\xi}
on
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
induced by
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
is

\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{\chi}=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp\varepsilon\xi}^{\ast}(\chi)=\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{\ast}\chi.

If
\Phi
is the inclusion of
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
into
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, then its derivative
D\Phi(\chi):T_{\chi}\mathcal{O}_{\chi}\to\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is also an inclusion, so

D\Phi(\chi)(\operatorname{ad}_{\zeta}^{\ast}(\chi))(\xi)=\operatorname{ad}_{\zeta}^{\ast}(\chi)(\xi)=\chi(-\operatorname{ad}_{\zeta}(\xi))=\chi(-[\zeta,\xi])=\chi([\xi,\zeta])\\
=\omega^{\text{KKS}}(\operatorname{ad}_{\xi}^{\ast}(\chi),\operatorname{ad}_{\zeta}^{\ast}(\chi))=\omega^{\text{KKS}}(\left.Y_{\xi}\right|_{\chi},\operatorname{ad}_{\zeta}^{\ast}(\chi)).

Since
\Phi
is obviously equivariant, this shows that it is a momentum map for the
G
-action on
\mathcal{O}_{\chi}
.

Combining the above discussion with
2.2
yields the following corollary:

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with dual Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. Let
\mathcal{O}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{O}_{n}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
be coadjoint orbits with their KKS forms
\omega_{i}^{\text{KKS}}
. Then
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}^{\ast}\omega_{i}^{\text{KKS}}\right)
is symplectic and the diagonal coadjoint action of
G
is Hamiltonian with momentum map

\Phi(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n})=\chi_{1}+\dots+\chi_{n},

where on the right hand side we have identified each
\chi_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{i}
with its inclusion into
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
.

2.3
Level Sets of Momentum Maps

We are shortly going to associate
P_{i}
with fixed spectra with points on a coadjoint orbit of
\operatorname{U}(d)
, so fixing the spectra of
P_{1},\dots,P_{N}
corresponds to choosing coadjoint orbits of
\operatorname{U}(d)
and taking their Cartesian product, and the associated momentum map of the diagonal
\operatorname{U}(d)
action will be the frame operator by
2.3
. This will all be explained in the next section, but the point is that the
tight
operator-valued frames with fixed spectra of the
P_{i}
will be precisely a level set of the momentum map, and the frame homotopy problem in this case boils down to showing connectedness of this level set. Fortunately for us, there are powerful results showing that level sets of momentum maps are usually connected.

While one can prove somewhat more general results, we will only consider Hamiltonian
G
-spaces
(M,\omega,G,\Phi)
where both
M
and
G
are compact. Since we intend to apply these results to the diagonal action of
\operatorname{U}(d)
on a product of its coadjoint orbits, this will suffice for our purposes.

Fix a
G
-invariant inner product on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. This induces a vector space isomorphism of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
with
\mathfrak{g}
, and hence determines an inner product and norm on the codomain
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
of the momentum map
\Phi
. Kirwan
[
36
]
showed that, while it is not quite a Morse–Bott function, the norm-squared map
\|\Phi\|^{2}
has many of the desirable properties of such functions.

Theorem 2.4

(Kirwan
[
36
, Theorem 4.16]
)
.

The set of critical points for
\|\Phi\|^{2}
is a finite disjoint union of closed subsets
\{C_{\beta}:\beta\in\mathcal{B}\}
on each of which
\|\Phi\|^{2}
takes a constant value (here the indexing set
\mathcal{B}
is a finite subset of the positive Weyl chamber
\mathfrak{t}_{+}
of
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
).

Moreover, there is a smooth stratification
\{S_{\beta}:\beta\in\mathcal{B}\}
of
M
, where
p\in S_{\beta}
if and only if the limit set of its image under the flow of
-\operatorname{grad}\|\Phi\|^{2}
(for an appropriate choice of
G
-invariant Riemannian metric on
M
) is contained in
C_{\beta}
.

Finally, for each
\beta
the inclusion
C_{\beta}\hookrightarrow S_{\beta}
is an equivalence of Cech cohomology and also
G
-invariant cohomology.

Moreover, Kirwan showed
[
36
, 4.18]
that the
S_{\beta}
are all locally closed, even-dimensional submanifolds of
M
. Since it is impossible to disconnect a manifold by removing submanifolds of codimension
\geq 2
, each stratum must be connected. In particular, the stratum
S_{0}
of points which flow to
\Phi^{-1}(0)
is connected; by the equivalence of Cech cohomology, the level set
\Phi^{-1}(0)=C_{0}
is also connected:

Theorem 2.5

(Kirwan
[
37
, (3.1)]
; see also
[
54
, Remark 5.8]
)
.

Let
(M,\omega,G,\Phi)
be a Hamiltonian
G
-space with
M
and
G
compact. Then
\Phi^{-1}(0)
is connected.

In general, we will be interested not just in
\Phi^{-1}(0)
, but also in
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O})
, where
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is a coadjoint orbit. Fortunately, the “shifting trick” will allow us to easily translate
2.5
to this more general setting:

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
(M,\omega,G,\Phi)
be a Hamiltonian
G
-space with
M
and
G
compact and let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
be a coadjoint orbit. Then
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O})
is connected.

Proof.

The goal is to build a new Hamiltonian
G
-space
(\overline{M},\overline{\omega},G,\overline{\Phi})
so that
\overline{\Phi}^{-1}(0)\approx\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O})
. This is exactly what the shifting trick (see
[
14
, §24.4]
or
[
45
, Proof of Proposition 5.4.15]
) was designed to do.

Specifically, we know that
(\mathcal{O},\omega^{\text{KKS}})
is symplectic, and hence so is
(\mathcal{O},-\omega^{\text{KKS}})
. Let
\overline{M}=M\times\mathcal{O}
with symplectic form
\overline{\omega}=\pi_{1}^{\ast}\omega+\pi_{2}^{\ast}(-\omega^{\text{KKS}})
, where as usual
\pi_{i}
is projection onto the
i
th factor. Let the
G
action on
\overline{M}
be the diagonal action. Then
2.2
and the discussion in
Section

2.2
imply that

\overline{\Phi}(p,\chi):=\Phi(p)-\chi

is a momentum map for this action.

Now,

\overline{\Phi}^{-1}(0)=\{(p,\Phi(p))\,|\,p\in M,\Phi(p)\in\mathcal{O}\}

is connected by
2.5
. Since this space is certainly homeomorphic to
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O})
, we conclude that
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O})
is also connected.
∎

3
The Symplectic Geometry of Spaces of Operator-Valued Frames

We now relate the general machinery of the previous section to operator-valued frames. Throughout this section we will fix positive integers
d,N,k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
. We will also fix
\bm{r}=(\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\bm{r}_{N})
, where
\bm{r}_{i}=(r_{i1},\dots,r_{ik_{i}})
with
r_{i1}\geq\dots\geq r_{ik_{i}}>0
.

Given this data, consider the space
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
of all operator-valued frames
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
, where
A_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{k_{i}}
is linear and
P_{i}:=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
has spectrum
\bm{r}_{i}
. The space
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
is not obviously symplectic, but our first goal is to show that the quotient
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})/(\operatorname{U}(k_{1})\times\dots\times\operatorname{U}(k_{N}))
is symplectic, and in fact is essentially a product of coadjoint orbits of
\operatorname{U}(d)
.

To start, we recall that the space
\mathcal{H}(d)
of
d\times d
Hermitian matrices can be identified with the dual
\mathfrak{u}(d)^{\ast}
of the Lie algebra of
\operatorname{U}(d)
by the isomorphism

\displaystyle\alpha:\mathcal{H}(d)

\displaystyle\to\mathfrak{u}(d)^{\ast}

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\eta\mapsto\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\eta^{\ast}\xi)=\left\langle\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\xi,\eta\right\rangle=:\alpha_{\xi}(\eta)\right).

We collect relevant lemmas from our previous paper
[
49
, §2.2.1 and §2.2.2]
in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.1
.

Under this identification, the coadjoint action of
\operatorname{U}(d)
on
\mathfrak{u}(d)^{\ast}
corresponds to the conjugation action of
\operatorname{U}(d)
on
\mathcal{H}(d)
, and hence coadjoint orbits of
\operatorname{U}(d)
can be identified with collections of Hermitian matrices with fixed spectrum
\bm{\mu}
, which we will denote
\mathcal{O}_{\bm{\mu}}\subset\mathcal{H}(d)
.

For each
i=1,\dots,N
, the collection of
P_{i}\in\mathcal{H}(d)
with spectrum
\bm{r}_{i}
is exactly
\mathcal{O}_{\bm{r}_{i}}
, and so

\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r}):=\{(P_{1},\dots,P_{N})\in\mathcal{H}(d)^{N}\,|\,\operatorname{spec}(P_{1})=\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{spec}(P_{N})=\bm{r}_{N}\}=\mathcal{O}_{\bm{r}_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathcal{O}_{\bm{r}_{N}}

(1)

is a product of coadjoint orbits.
2.3
now has the following immediate consequence:

Corollary 3.2
.

The momentum map
\Phi
for the diagonal conjugation action of
\operatorname{U}(d)
on
\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
is precisely the frame operator:

\Phi(P_{1},\dots,P_{N})=P_{1}+\dots+P_{N}.

Fix
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d}>0
. Define

\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r}):=\{(P_{1},\dots,P_{N})\in\mathcal{H}(d)^{N}\,|\,\operatorname{spec}(P_{1})=\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{spec}(P_{N})=\bm{r}_{N},\operatorname{spec}(P_{1}+\dots+P_{N})=\bm{\lambda}\}.

(2)

In other words, this is the collection of
P_{i}
with both the right individual spectra and the right spectrum of the frame operator. Then
3.2
and
2.6
imply:

Proposition 3.3
.

\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})=\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{\bm{\lambda}})\subset\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
is connected.

Note that, if we think of fusion frames in the usual way as a collection of subspaces or, equivalently, orthogonal projectors, this already gives an affirmative answer to
1.1
in the case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
.

In the more general operator-valued frame case, the question is how
\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
relates to
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
, which is the space we want to show is connected. As previously discussed, while
A_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{k_{i}}
uniquely determines
P_{i}=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
, the operator
A_{i}
cannot be uniquely determined from
P_{i}
. Indeed, if
U\in\operatorname{U}(k_{i})
, then

(UA_{i})^{\ast}(UA_{i})=A_{i}^{\ast}U^{\ast}UA_{i}=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}=P_{i},

so composing with a unitary transformation of the codomain leaves
P_{i}
invariant.
1.2
says that this is the only indeterminacy, and hence the set of
P_{i}
with given spectrum is precisely the collection of cosets of the (left) unitary action on the set of
A_{i}
with given singular values:

\{P_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}\,|\,\operatorname{spec}{P_{i}}=\bm{r}_{i}\}\approx\{A_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{k_{i}}\,|\,\operatorname{spec}(A^{\ast}A)=\bm{r}_{i}\}/\operatorname{U}(k_{i}).

In turn, this implies that

\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\approx\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})/(\operatorname{U}(k_{1})\times\dots\times\operatorname{U}(k_{N})),

(3)

and hence that

\mathcal{PF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})\approx\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})/(\operatorname{U}(k_{1})\times\dots\times\operatorname{U}(k_{N})).

Here, we are using the fact that the operations of taking a level set and taking a quotient commute with one another. That is, suppose that
X
is a topological space with an action by a group
G
and
\Psi:X\to Y
is a
G
-invariant map, so that the induced quotient map
\widetilde{\Psi}:X/G\to Y
taking an equivalence class
[x]
to
\Psi(x)
is well defined; then
\widetilde{\Psi}^{-1}(y)=\Psi^{-1}(y)/G
for any
y\in Y
. Indeed,
[x]\in\widetilde{\Psi}^{-1}(y)
if and only if
x\in\Psi^{-1}(y)
, or equivalently
[x]\in\Psi^{-1}(y)/G
.

Therefore, the following lemma (with
X=\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
and
G=\operatorname{U}(k_{1})\times\dots\times\operatorname{U}(k_{N})
) combined with
3.3
implies that
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is connected. Since
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is a real algebraic set in
\mathbb{C}^{k_{1}\times d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{C}^{k_{N}\times d}\approx\mathbb{R}^{2(k_{1}+\dots+k_{N})d}
, it is locally path-connected, so that connectivity implies path-connectivity, completing the proof of
1.9
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a topological space and let
G
be a connected topological group acting continuously on
X
. If
X/G
is connected, then
X
is connected.

The lemma follows from standard point-set topology arguments (see, e.g.,
[
42
, Exercise 5.5]
), since connectedness of
G
implies the fibers of the quotient map are connected.

4
Tightening Fusion Frames

We now specialize to fusion frames, but relax our assumption on the base field, so that
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Recall the definition of the fusion frame potential
\operatorname{FFP}:\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A}):=\left\|S_{\bm{A}}\right\|^{2}.

One of Casazza and Fickus’ first results about the fusion frame potential was a Welch-type lower bound:

Proposition 4.1

(
[
15
, Proposition 1]
)
.

Let
d,N,k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
be positive integers and define
n:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{N}
. Then for
\bm{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{N})\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
,

\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A})\geq\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}\right)^{2}=\frac{n^{2}}{d}

with equality if and only if
\bm{A}
is a tight fusion frame.

Let
\Lambda=\frac{n}{d}
and define
\bm{\Lambda}=(\Lambda,\dots,\Lambda)
. If they exist, the TFFs must have frame operator
\Lambda\mathbb{I}_{d}
, so that
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\subset\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
is exactly the collection of TFFs. Hence,
4.1
says that if this collection of TFFs is nonempty then it is exactly the set of global minimizers of
\operatorname{FFP}
in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
.

The hard work of proving
1.14
is in proving the
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
case. The real case then follows immediately since
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{k}}\subset\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
is invariant under the gradient flow of
\operatorname{FFP}
. We record this observation in the following proposition:

Proposition 4.2
.

For given
d
and
\bm{k}
, if
1.14
is true for
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
, then it is also true for
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{k}}
.

Except where explicitly pointed out below, we will assume
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
in what follows. The strategy for proving the complex case of
1.14
is to show that property
\mathscr{S}
(
Definition

1.13
) satisfies the following conditions:

1.

gradient flow (and its limit) preserves
\mathscr{S}
, but

2.

no non-minimizing critical point of the operator-valued frame potential satisfies
\mathscr{S}
.

Our argument has roots in Mumford’s
geometric invariant theory (GIT)

[
48
]
(see
[
55
]
for a nice introduction), which we introduce in some generality before specializing.

Let
G
be a reductive algebraic group that acts linearly on a finite-dimensional complex vector space
V
. For example,
G
might be
GL(V)
or
SL(V)
. A nonzero vector
v\in V
is
unstable
under the action of
G
if the closure
\overline{G\cdot v}
of the
G
-orbit of
v
contains the origin; otherwise
v
is
semi-stable
. Notice that the unstable points are precisely those in the vanishing locus of every
G
-invariant homogeneous polynomial on
V
, and hence the semi-stable points are those on which some
G
-invariant homogeneous polynomial does not vanish.

As one might expect, semi-stability is a feature of the orbit of
v
: either the entire orbit consists of semi-stable points, or the entire orbit consists of unstable points.

Proposition 4.3

(see, e.g.,
[
46
, Proposition 6]
)
.

Given a nonzero
v\in V
that is semi-stable, every point in
\overline{G\cdot v}
is also semi-stable.

4.1
V
and the
\operatorname{SL}(d)
Action

In our application of GIT, we will have
G=\operatorname{SL}(d)
. To determine the appropriate vector space
V
, we first recall the
Plücker embedding

\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{C}^{d})
, defined on the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
of
k
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We can represent a
k
-plane by any basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
for it. Then the Plücker embedding is defined to be the projectivization of the map
\tau:(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{k}\to\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{C}^{d}
defined by

\tau(v_{1},\dots,v_{k}):=v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}.

When
(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
and
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
span the same
k
-dimensional subspace, then
\tau(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})=\det(h)\tau(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
where
h\in\operatorname{GL}(k)
is the change-of-basis matrix, so both map to the same point in projective space, and the Plücker embedding is well-defined on the Grassmannian. Of course, the standard action of
\operatorname{SL}(d)
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
induces an action on
\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{C}^{d}
by

g\cdot(v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}):=(gv_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge(gv_{k})

and extending linearly.

How do we get from fusion frames to Grassmannians, and hence to such a representation of
\operatorname{SL}(d)
?

For
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
, each
P_{i}=A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
is a rank-
k_{i}
orthogonal projector and the rows
a_{i1},\dots,a_{ik_{i}}
of
A_{i}
give an orthonormal basis for the
k_{i}
-dimensional subspace which is the image of
P_{i}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{O}_{\bm{r}_{i}}
is the collection of all rank-
k_{i}
orthogonal projectors, which is symplectomorphic to the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}_{k_{i}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
.

Define
\tau_{i}:A_{i}\mapsto a_{i1}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{ik_{i}}^{\ast}
, the projectivization of which is exactly the Plücker embedding of
\operatorname{Gr}_{k_{i}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, and
\tau_{i}
is equivariant with respect to the right
\operatorname{SL}(d)
action
g\cdot A_{i}:=A_{i}g^{\ast}
on the domain and the
\operatorname{SL}(d)
action described above on the codomain:

\tau(g\cdot A_{i})=\tau(A_{i}g^{\ast})=(ga_{i1}^{\ast})\wedge\dots\wedge(ga_{ik_{i}}^{\ast})=g\cdot\tau(A_{i})

for any
g\in\operatorname{SL}(d)
.

Next, define
\tau:\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}\to\bigwedge^{k_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\dots\otimes\bigwedge^{k_{N}}\mathbb{C}^{d}
by
\tau:=\tau_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\tau_{N}
, so that

\tau(\bm{A})=\tau_{1}(A_{1})\otimes\dots\otimes\tau_{N}(A_{N})=\left(a_{11}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{1k_{1}}^{\ast}\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(a_{N1}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{Nk_{N}}^{\ast}\right).

In other words, the projectivization of
\tau
is the Segre embedding of the product of Plücker embeddings of the individual factors.

Finally, then, our vector space
V=\bigwedge^{k_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\dots\otimes\bigwedge^{k_{N}}\mathbb{C}^{d}
, on which
G=\operatorname{SL}(d)
acts by

g\cdot\left((v_{11}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{1k_{1}})\otimes\dots\otimes(v_{N1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{Nk_{N}})\right):=\left((gv_{11})\wedge\dots\wedge(g_{1k_{1}}))\otimes\dots\otimes((gv_{N1})\wedge\dots\wedge(gv_{Nk_{N}}))\right)

and extending linearly.

The point of defining property
\mathscr{S}
as we have is the following theorem of Mumford (stated originally in terms of Grassmannians):

Theorem 4.4

(Mumford
[
48
, Proposition 4.3]
; see also
[
47
]
and
[
36
, §16.3]
)
.

\bm{A}
has property
\mathscr{S}
if and only if
\tau(\bm{A})
is semi-stable with respect to the
\operatorname{SL}(d)
action.

As pointed out just before
4.3
, the semi-stable points in
V
are those on which some
G
-invariant homogeneous polynomial does not vanish. Hence,
4.4
implies that if
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
has property
\mathscr{S}
, then there is some
G
-invariant homogeneous polynomial which does not vanish at
\tau(\bm{A})
. Since the coordinates of
\tau(\bm{A})
are precisely the determinants of all the
k_{i}\times k_{i}
minors of the
A_{i}
, and since these determinants are themselves polynomials in the entries of
\bm{A}
, this means that there is some polynomial expression in the coordinates of
\bm{A}
which does not vanish. Therefore, the collection of
\bm{A}
with property
\mathscr{S}
is Zariski-open in the smooth, connected, real algebraic variety
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
, and hence is either empty or dense (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Proposition 5.11]
). Moreover, the same reasoning applies in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{k}}
. Therefore, we have:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. When it is non-empty, the collection of all fusion frames in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
with property
\mathscr{S}
is dense.

We also take this opportunity to show that TFFs always have property
\mathscr{S}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and suppose
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
is a TFF. Then
\bm{A}
has property
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Since
\bm{A}
is tight, its frame operator
S_{\bm{A}}=\frac{n}{d}\mathbb{I}_{d}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{K}^{d}
be a proper subspace and let
P_{\mathcal{Q}}
be orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{Q}
.

For each
i=1,\dots,N
, any nonzero vector in
\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{S}_{i}
is fixed by the product
P_{\mathcal{Q}}P_{i}
, and hence
\operatorname{tr}(P_{\mathcal{Q}}P_{i})\geq\dim(\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{S}_{i})
, since all eigenvalues of
P_{\mathcal{Q}}P_{i}
are real and non-negative. Therefore, since
P_{\mathcal{Q}}S_{\bm{A}}=\frac{n}{d}P_{\mathcal{Q}}
,

\frac{n}{d}\dim(\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{tr}(P_{\mathcal{Q}}S_{\bm{A}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}(P_{\mathcal{Q}}P_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dim(\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{S}_{i}),

so
\bm{A}
has property
\mathscr{S}
.
∎

Combining
4.5
and
4.6
yields the following immediate corollary:

Corollary 4.7
.

Whenever there are TFFs in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
, the fusion frames with property
\mathscr{S}
are dense in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
.

4.2
Property
\mathscr{S}
Satisfies
1
and
2

4.2.1
Property
\mathscr{S}
Satisfies
1

The goal in this subsection is to show that the gradient flow of
\operatorname{FFP}
preserves property
\mathscr{S}
.

Notice that, if
\bm{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{N})
is a fusion frame, then the rows of each
A_{i}
form an orthonormal set, and hence each
a_{i1}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{ik_{i}}^{\ast}\in\bigwedge^{k_{i}}\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a unit vector with respect to the standard inner product on
\bigwedge^{k_{i}}\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In turn, this implies that
\tau(\bm{A})\in\left(\bigwedge^{k_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\bigwedge^{k_{N}}\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)
is also a unit vector. In other words:

Lemma 4.8
.

\tau\left(\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}\right)
is contained in the unit sphere, and in particular is bounded away from the origin.

Now we compute the gradient of
\operatorname{FFP}
, first by computing the extrinsic gradient of its extension to the entire vector space containing
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
:

Lemma 4.9
.

Define
\operatorname{EFP}:\mathbb{C}^{k_{1}\times d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{C}^{k_{N}\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
to be the extension of
\operatorname{FFP}
to all of
\mathbb{C}^{k_{1}\times d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{C}^{k_{N}\times d}
given by
\operatorname{EFP}(A_{1},\dots,A_{N}):=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}\right\|^{2}
. Its gradient is

\nabla\operatorname{EFP}(\bm{A})=(4A_{1}S_{\bm{A}},\ldots,4A_{N}S_{\bm{A}}).

(4)

Proof.

Let
\bm{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{N})\in T_{\bm{A}}(\mathbb{C}^{k_{1}\times d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{C}^{k_{N}\times d})\approx\mathbb{C}^{k_{1}\times d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{C}^{k_{N}\times d}
and consider the directional derivative of
\operatorname{EFP}
at
\bm{A}
in the direction
\bm{B}
. In the following, we slightly abuse notation and generically use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the Frobenius inner product on matrix spaces of various dimensions. We have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}\operatorname{EFP}(\bm{A}+\epsilon\bm{B})

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}(A_{i}+\epsilon B_{i})^{\ast}(A_{i}+\epsilon B_{i})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}\left<\sum_{i=1}^{N}(A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}+\epsilon(A_{i}^{\ast}B_{i}+B_{i}^{\ast}A_{i})+\epsilon^{2}B_{i}^{\ast}B_{i}),\sum_{i=1}^{N}(A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}+\epsilon(A_{i}^{\ast}B_{i}+B_{i}^{\ast}A_{i})+\epsilon^{2}B_{i}^{\ast}B_{i})\right>

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}\left<\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{N}(A_{i}^{\ast}B_{i}+B_{i}^{\ast}A_{i})\right>

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{Re}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left<S_{\bm{A}},A_{i}^{\ast}B_{i}\right>

(5)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left<4A_{i}S_{\bm{A}},B_{i}\right>=\mathrm{Re}\left<(4A_{1}S_{\bm{A}},\ldots,4A_{N}S_{\bm{A}}),(B_{1},\ldots,B_{N})\right>.

(6)

The equality (
5
) makes the replacement
S_{\bm{A}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
, uses linearity in the second coordinate to move the summation out of the inner product and uses properties of the (real part of the) inner product to equate
\mathrm{Re}\langle\cdot,A_{i}^{\ast}B_{i}+B_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}\rangle=2\mathrm{Re}\langle\cdot,A_{i}^{\ast}B_{i}\rangle
. The quantity (
6
) is the (real part of the) standard inner product for
\mathbb{C}^{k_{1}\times d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{C}^{k_{N}\times d}
applied to
(4A_{1}S_{\bm{A}},\ldots,4A_{N}S_{\bm{A}})
(the claimed formula for the gradient) and
\bm{B}
. This implies (
4
).
∎

And now the intrinsic gradient:

Lemma 4.10
.

The Riemannian gradient of
\operatorname{FFP}:\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}\to\mathbb{R}
, is

\operatorname{grad}\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A})=\left(4(A_{1}S_{\bm{A}}-(A_{1}S_{\bm{A}}A_{1}^{\ast})A_{1}),\ldots,4(A_{1}S_{\bm{A}}-(A_{1}S_{\bm{A}}A_{1}^{\ast})A_{1})\right).

Proof.

The Riemannian gradient
\operatorname{grad}\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A})
is the projection of the extrinsic gradient
\nabla\operatorname{EFP}(\bm{A})
onto the tangent space to
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
at
\bm{A}
. This means that on the
i
th block we need to project
4A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}
onto the orthogonal complement of the row span of
A_{i}
. This orthogonal projection is accomplished by right-multiplying by
(\mathbb{I}_{d}-A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i})
, so the projection is
4A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}(\mathbb{I}_{d}-A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i})=4\left(A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}-\left(A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}A_{i}^{\ast}\right)A_{i}\right)
. The result follows.
∎

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose
\bm{A}_{0}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
has property
\mathscr{S}
and that
\Gamma:\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
is the gradient flow defined in
1.12
. Then
\bm{A}_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t)
has property
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Using
4.10
, we have that the
i
th block of
\operatorname{grad}\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A})
is

4\left(A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}-\left(A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}A_{i}^{\ast}\right)A_{i}\right)=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}4\exp(-\epsilon A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}A_{i}^{\ast})A_{i}\exp(\epsilon S_{\bm{A}}).

(7)

Exponentiating a matrix always yields an invertible matrix, so (
7
) tells us that
\operatorname{grad}\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A})
is tangent to the orbit
\left(\operatorname{GL}(d)\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{GL}(k_{i})\right)\cdot\bm{A}
, where
(g,(h_{1},\dots,h_{N}))\in\operatorname{GL}(d)\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{GL}(k_{i})
acts on
\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{C}^{k_{i}\times d}
by

(g,(h_{1},\dots,h_{N}))\cdot\bm{A}=(h_{1}A_{1}g^{\ast},\dots,h_{N}A_{n}g^{\ast}).

For
(g,(h_{1},\dots,h_{N}))\in\operatorname{GL}(d)\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{GL}(k_{i})
, we normalize
g
to get something in
\operatorname{SL}(d)
without changing the action by moving a scalar to the other factor:
\left((\det g)^{-1/d}g,\left((\overline{\det g})^{-1/d}h_{1},\dots,(\overline{\det g})^{-1/d},h_{N}\right)\right)\in\operatorname{SL}(d)\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{GL}(k_{i})
and

(g,(h_{1},\dots,h_{N}))\cdot\bm{A}=\left((\det g)^{-1/d}g,\left((\overline{\det g})^{-1/d}h_{1},\dots,(\overline{\det g})^{-1/d},h_{N}\right)\right)\cdot\bm{A},

so
\operatorname{grad}\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A})
is actually in the tangent space to
\left(\operatorname{SL}(d)\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{GL}(k_{i})\right)\cdot\bm{A}
at
\bm{A}
. Therefore,

\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t)\in\left(\operatorname{SL}(d)\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{GL}(k_{i})\right)\cdot\bm{A}_{0}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

If
(g,(h_{1},\dots,h_{N})\in\operatorname{SL}(d)\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{GL}(k_{i})
, then

\displaystyle\tau((g,(h_{1},\dots,h_{N}))\cdot\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\left(\left(ga_{11}^{\ast}h_{1}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(ga_{1k_{1}}^{\ast}h_{1}^{\ast}\right)\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\left(ga_{N1}^{\ast}h_{N}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(ga_{Nk_{N}}^{\ast}h_{N}^{\ast}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\det(h_{1}^{\ast})\left(ga_{11}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(ga_{1k_{1}}^{\ast}\right)\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\det(h_{N}^{\ast})\left(ga_{N1}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(ga_{Nk_{N}}^{\ast}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\det(h_{i}^{\ast})\left[\left(\left(ga_{11}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(ga_{1k_{1}}^{\ast}\right)\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\left(ga_{N1}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(ga_{Nk_{N}}^{\ast}\right)\right)\right]\in\left(\operatorname{SL}(d)\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}\right)\cdot\tau(\bm{A}),

where
(g,a)\in\operatorname{SL}(d)\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
acts on
\left(\bigwedge^{k_{1}}\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\bigwedge^{k_{N}}\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)
by

(g,a)\cdot\left[\left(v_{11}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{1k_{1}}\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(v_{N1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{Nk_{N}}\right)\right]:=a\left[\left(\left(gv_{11}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(gv_{1k_{1}}\right)\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\left(gv_{N1}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(gv_{Nk_{N}}\right)\right)\right].

This implies that
\tau(\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t))\in\left(\operatorname{SL}(d)\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}\right)\cdot\tau(\bm{A}_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
. Since
\tau(\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t))
is a unit vector for all
t
by
4.8
, so is the limit
\tau(\bm{A}_{\infty})
.

Since everything is bounded away from the origin and since rescaling a vector by a nonzero scalar does not affect its semistability with respect to the
\operatorname{SL}(d)
-action,
4.3
implies that the entire gradient flow line, including
\tau(\bm{A}_{\infty})
, is semi-stable, and hence
\bm{A}_{\infty}
has property
\mathscr{S}
by
4.4
.
∎

4.2.2
Property
\mathscr{S}
Satisfies
2

Finally, we need to show that critical points which are not global minima do not satisfy property
\mathscr{S}
. We do this by showing that, if
\bm{A}
is a non-minimizing critical point, then
\tau(\bm{A})
is not semi-stable with respect to the
\operatorname{SL}(d)
action. Semi-stability is defined in terms of the full group orbit, but this is typically much too big to be tractable. Instead, it is preferable to work with one-parameter subgroups, which remarkably turn out to be sufficient.

We briefly return to discussing a general reductive group
G
acting linearly on a vector space
V
. A
one-parameter subgroup
of
G
is a homomorphism of algebraic groups
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to G
. Any such homomorphism induces a decomposition
V=\bigoplus_{i\in I}V_{i}
and integer weights
w:I\to\mathbb{Z}
so that, for every
i\in I
,
v\in V_{i}
, and
t\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,

\lambda(t)\cdot v=t^{w(i)}v.

It follows immediately from the definition that a nonzero vector
v\in V
is unstable under the action of
G
if there exists a one-parameter subgroup
\lambda
so that

\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot v=0.

Much less obvious is that the converse holds:

Theorem 4.12

(Hilbert–Mumford criterion
[
48
,
30
]
)
.

v\in V\backslash\{0\}
is unstable under the action of
G
if and only if there exists a one-parameter subgroup
\lambda
of
G
so that

\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot v=0.

This will be our key tool in proving that property
\mathscr{S}
satisfies
2
.

Proposition 4.13
.

Suppose
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
is a critical point of
\operatorname{FFP}
which is not tight. Then
\bm{A}
does not have property
\mathscr{S}
. In particular, if
\bm{A}
is a critical point which is not a global minimum, then it does not have property
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
be a critical point of
\operatorname{FFP}
. Then
\operatorname{grad}\operatorname{FFP}(\bm{A})=0
; by
4.10
, this implies that, for each
i=1,\dots,N
,

0=A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}-A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}=A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}(\mathbb{I}_{d}-A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}).

The operator
\mathbb{I}_{d}-A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}
is orthogonal projection onto the orthogonal complement of
\operatorname{row}(A_{i})
, the row space of
A_{i}
(since
\bm{A}
is a fusion frame, the rows of
A_{i}
are orthonormal). The above equation then says that the rows of
A_{i}S_{\bm{A}}
lie in
\operatorname{row}(A_{i})
. In other words,
\operatorname{row}(A_{i})
is an invariant subspace for the frame operator
S_{\bm{A}}
, and hence has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
\left.S_{\bm{A}}\right|_{\operatorname{row}(A_{i})}
, so there exists
U_{i}\in\operatorname{U}(k_{i})
so that the rows of
\widetilde{A}_{i}:=U_{i}A_{i}
are eigenvectors of
\left.S_{\bm{A}}\right|_{\operatorname{row}(A_{i})}
, and hence also of
S_{\bm{A}}=S_{\widetilde{\bm{A}}}
. So far, this is not new: the conclusion of the previous sentence is exactly Casazza and Fickus’ characterization of the critical points of the fusion frame potential
[
15
, Theorem 4]
.

If
\bm{A}
is not tight, then the frame operator
S_{\bm{A}}
has at least two distinct eigenvalues. Let
\lambda
be the largest eigenvalue, with corresponding eigenspace
E_{\lambda}
of dimension
\ell
and orthogonal complement
E_{\lambda}^{\bot}
of dimension
d-\ell
. Since the average of the eigenvalues of
S_{\bm{A}}
is

\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(S_{\bm{A}})=\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}=\frac{n}{d}

and the eigenvalues aren’t all equal, we know that the largest eigenvalue
\lambda>\frac{n}{d}
.

Up to conjugating
S_{\bm{A}}
by
U\in\operatorname{U}(d)
(corresponding to right-multiplying each
A_{i}
by
U^{\ast}
), we can make the simplifying assumption that the frame operator is diagonal:
S_{\bm{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\mathbb{I}_{\ell}&0\\
0&S^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}
, where
S^{\prime}
is a diagonal (but not necessarily scalar) matrix. Hence,
E_{\lambda}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell}\}
and
E_{\lambda}^{\bot}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{\ell+1},\dots,e_{d}\}
.

If, for each
i=1,\dots,N
,
\tilde{a}_{i1},\dots,\tilde{a}_{ik_{i}}
are the rows of
\widetilde{A}_{i}
, then, since each
\tilde{a}_{ij}
is an eigenvector of
S_{\bm{A}}
and distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal, the
\tilde{a}_{ij}
split into two perpendicular groups: those in
E_{\lambda}
and those in
E_{\lambda}^{\bot}
. Let
\tilde{b}_{1},\dots,\tilde{b}_{m}
be the collection contained in
E_{\lambda}
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{b}_{i}^{\ast}\tilde{b}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\mathbb{I}_{\ell}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

so that
\tilde{b}_{1}^{\ast},\dots,\tilde{b}_{m}^{\ast}
are a
\lambda
-tight frame for
E_{\lambda}
. Since the
\tilde{b}_{i}
are unit vectors,

\ell\lambda=\operatorname{tr}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\mathbb{I}_{\ell}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}=\operatorname{tr}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\ast}\tilde{b}_{m})=\operatorname{tr}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{b}_{i}\tilde{b}_{i}^{\ast})=m,

it follows that
\lambda=\frac{m}{\ell}
and hence that
\frac{m}{\ell}>\frac{n}{d}
; equivalently,
md-n\ell>0
.

We’re now ready to show that
\bm{A}
does not have property
\mathscr{S}
. To see this, consider the 1-parameter subgroup
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to\operatorname{SL}(d)
given as a block matrix by

\lambda(t)=\begin{bmatrix}t^{d-\ell}\mathbb{I}_{\ell}&0\\
0&t^{-\ell}\mathbb{I}_{d-\ell}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\tilde{a}_{i1}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge\tilde{a}_{ik_{i}}^{\ast}=\det(U_{i}^{\ast})a_{i1}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{ik_{i}}^{\ast}
, it follows that
\tau(\bm{A})=\rho\tau(\widetilde{\bm{A}})
for unimodular
\rho=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\det(U_{i})
, and hence

\lambda(t)\cdot\tau(\bm{A})=\rho\lambda(t)\cdot\tau(\widetilde{\bm{A}})=\rho\left(\left(\lambda(t)\tilde{a}_{11}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(\lambda(t)\tilde{a}_{1k_{1}}^{\ast}\right)\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\left(\lambda(t)\tilde{a}_{N1}^{\ast}\right)\wedge\dots\wedge\left(\lambda(t)\tilde{a}_{Nk_{N}}^{\ast}\right)\right)\\
=t^{m(d-\ell)-(n-m)\ell}\rho\left(\tilde{a}_{11}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge\tilde{a}_{1k_{1}}^{\ast}\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\left(\tilde{a}_{N1}^{\ast}\wedge\dots\wedge\tilde{a}_{Nk_{N}}^{\ast}\right)=t^{md-n\ell}\rho\tau(\widetilde{\bm{A}}),

which goes to zero as
t\to 0
since
md-n\ell>0
.

Therefore,
\tau(\bm{A})
is not semi-stable, and hence, by
4.4
,
\bm{A}
does not have property
\mathscr{S}
.

Finally, if
\bm{A}
is not a global minimum, then it cannot be tight by
4.1
, so we see that non-minimum critical points cannot have property
\mathscr{S}
.
∎

If
d,N
, and
\bm{k}
are such that
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
contains no TFFs (see
Remark

1.16
for conditions on when this occurs), then any global minimum
\bm{A}
of
\operatorname{FFP}
cannot be tight, so
4.13
shows that
\bm{A}
cannot have property
\mathscr{S}
. By the contrapositive of
4.11
, then, nothing in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
that flows to
\bm{A}
under the negative gradient flow of
\operatorname{FFP}
can have property
\mathscr{S}
, either. Since this is true for all global minima, there is some open set containing the global minima which completely avoids the fusion frames with property
\mathscr{S}
. Therefore, the set of fusion frames in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
with property
\mathscr{S}
cannot be dense, and hence, by
4.5
, must be empty. In other words:

Corollary 4.14
.

\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}
contains fusion frames with property
\mathscr{S}
if and only if it contains TFFs.

4.2.3
Completing the Proof of
1.14

We now have all the tools we need to prove that gradient descent limits to a global minimizer.

Proof of
1.14
.

If
\bm{A}_{0}\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{C}^{d},\bm{k}}
has property
\mathscr{S}
, the limit
\bm{A}_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma(\bm{A}_{0},t)
has property
\mathscr{S}
by
4.11
. Since
\bm{A}_{\infty}
is a limit point of the gradient flow, it must be a critical point of
\operatorname{FFP}
. Since it has property
\mathscr{S}
,
4.13
implies that
\bm{A}_{\infty}
is a global minimizer of
\operatorname{FFP}
.

This proves
1.14
when
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
. The real case then follows immediately by
4.2
.
∎

5
Discussion

There are choices of
d
,
N
, and
\bm{k}
for which there are no fusion frames with property
\mathscr{S}
: for example,
d=N=3
and
\bm{k}=(1,1,2)
. Elements
(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})\in\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{3},(1,1,2)}
will determine two lines
\ell_{1}=\operatorname{row}(A_{1})
and
\ell_{2}=\operatorname{row}(A_{2})
and a plane
\mathcal{S}=\operatorname{row}(A_{3})
. If
\mathcal{Q}
is a plane containing
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
, then it must intersect
\mathcal{S}
at least in a line, so

\frac{1}{\dim\mathcal{Q}}(\dim(\ell_{1}\cap\mathcal{Q})+\dim(\ell_{2}\cap\mathcal{Q})+\dim(\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{Q}))\geq\frac{3}{2}>\frac{4}{3}=\frac{\dim\ell_{1}+\dim\ell_{2}+\dim\mathcal{S}}{3}.

Hence, nothing in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{3},(1,1,2)}
has property
\mathscr{S}
, so
1.14
tells us nothing. Moreover, by
4.6
, there are no TFFs in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{3},(1,1,2)}
.

Figure 1:
Left: Values of
\operatorname{FFP}
at each step in a simple gradient descent with fixed step sizes starting from a random point in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},(1,1,2)}
. Right: The limiting fusion frame of this gradient descent which achieves the minimum possible value of
\frac{11}{2}
.

Nonetheless, running gradient descent from random starting points in
\mathcal{FF}^{\mathbb{R}^{3},(1,1,2)}
seems to always find minimizers of
\operatorname{FFP}
in practice.
Figure

1
shows the value of
\operatorname{FFP}
rapidly decreasing to the global minimum value
\frac{11}{2}
, which is greater than the value
\frac{16}{3}
that a TFF would have. As observed by Casazza and Fickus
[
15
, p. 17]
, the minimum is achieved by fusion frames of the form shown on the right of
Figure

1
, where
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
lie in a plane
\mathcal{Q}
perpendicular to
\mathcal{S}
and the lines
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
, and
\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{S}
correspond to a tight Mercedes–Benz frame for
\mathcal{Q}
.

This suggests that the fusion frame Benedetto–Fickus theorem may hold even for parameters where there are no fusion frames with property
\mathscr{S}
. We also expect our approach to proving
1.14
will extend to more general spaces
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
of operator-valued frames with fixed spectra of the
P_{i}
, though the details seem more complicated. Hence, we pose the following conjecture:

Conjecture 5.1
.

Let
d,N,k_{1},\dots k_{N}
be positive integers and fix
\bm{r}
. Let
\operatorname{OFP}:\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{K}^{d},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\to\mathbb{R}
be the obvious generalization of
\operatorname{FFP}
to operator-valued frames. Then all local minima of
\operatorname{FFP}
are global minima.

We expect that, as in the case of classical frames
[
50
]
,
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{H}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
is path-connected, where
\mathbb{H}
is the skew-field of quaternions. However,
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
cannot always be connected. For example, translating a result of Kapovich and Millson
[
35
, Theorem 1]
to our setting and notation implies that
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{R}^{2},(1,1,1,1)}_{(5,5)}(3,3,3,1)
is not connected. On the other hand, Cahill, Mixon, and Strawn
[
12
]
proved that the space
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},(1,\dots,1)}_{\left(\frac{N}{d},\dots,\frac{N}{d}\right)}(1,\dots,1)
of real unit-norm tight frames is connected for all
d\geq 2
and
N\geq d+2
, so there is some interesting characterization of when the
\mathcal{OF}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{k}}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r})
are connected still waiting to be discovered.

Cahill, Mixon, and Strawn’s proof of the Frame Homotopy Theorem relied heavily on the use of
eigensteps
, which are the eigenvalues of the partial sums of the
P_{i}
. While eigensteps can be similarly defined for fusion frames and even operator-valued frames, it is not clear whether they would be a useful tool for studying connectedness in the real case. Eigensteps give good coordinates for classical frame spaces because they are
action coordinates
—that is, they are the coordinates of a momentum map for a Hamiltonian action of a half-dimensional torus
[
51
]
. This means that not only is the image a convex polytope, but the fibers of the eigenstep map are reasonably simple and well-understood. For dimension-counting reasons it seems unlikely that eigensteps could give action coordinates for fusion frames or operator-valued frames, but it is desirable to find similarly useful coordinates in this more general setting.
