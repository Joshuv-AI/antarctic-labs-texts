The walled Brauer category and stable cohomology of IA n \IA_{n}
Erik Lindell, With an appendix by Mai Katada

Abstract
. The IA \IA -automorphism group is the group of automorphisms of the free group F n F_{n} that act trivially on the abelianization F n ab F_{n}^{\mathrm{ab}} . This group is in many ways analoguous to Torelli groups of surfaces and their higher dimensional analogues. In recent work, the stable rational cohomology of such groups was studied by Kupers and Randal-Williams, using the machinery of so-called Brauer categories. In this paper, we adapt their methods to study the stable rational cohomology of the IA \IA -automorphism group. We obtain a conjectural description of the algebraic part of the stable rational cohomology and prove that it holds up to degree Q + 1 Q+1 , given the assumption that the stable cohomology groups are stably finite dimensional in degrees up to Q Q . In particular, this allows us to compute the algebraic part of the stable cohomology in degree 2, which we show agrees with the part generated by the first cohomology group via the cup product map and which has previously been computed by Pettet. In the appendix, written by Mai Katada, it is shown how the results of the paper can be applied to compute the stable Albanese (co)homology of the IA \IA -automorphism group.



1.
Introduction

Let
F_{n}:=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
denote the free group on
n
generators and
\Aut(F_{n})
its automorphism group. The action on
F_{n}^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
defines a surjective homomorphism
\Aut(F_{n})\to\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and we define
\IA_{n}
, the
\IA
-automorphism group of
F_{n}
, as the kernel of this action. The subject of this paper is the rational cohomology of this group. Similarly to the groups
\Aut(F_{n})
, the
\IA
-automorphism groups fit into a sequence

(1)

\IA_{1}\to\IA_{2}\to\cdots\to\IA_{n}\to\IA_{n+1}\to\cdots

given by extending an automorphism by acting trivially on the new generator. However, unlike for the corresponding sequence of the groups
\Aut(F_{n})
, the induced sequence in (co)homology of
\IA_{n}
does not stabilize. On the other hand, by definition
\IA_{n}
sits in a short exact sequence

(2)

1\to\IA_{n}\to\Aut(F_{n})\to\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\to 1,

making
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
into a
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation. When taking this extra structure into account, the cohomology groups still turn out to be the most tractable in a range where
n
is sufficiently large compared to the cohomological degree. For example, it was proven by Kawazumi
[
Kaw05
]
, and independently by Cohen-Pakianathan and Farb (both unpublished), that we have

H^{1}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n),

where
H(n):=H_{1}(F_{n},\mathbb{Q})
and
H^{\vee}(n):=H^{1}(F_{n},\mathbb{Q})
. Based on this, it was conjectured by Church and Farb
[
CF13
]
that the sequence induced in (co)homology by (
1
) satisfies
representation stability
. Since we will not consider representation stability in any detail in this paper, we refer to
[
CF13
]
for the precise definition, and only say that in this situation it essentially says that for
n
sufficiently large compared to the degree
*
,
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
is the restriction of a
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-representation (i.e. an
algebraic
representation of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
) and that its decomposition into irreducible
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-representations is independent of
n
.

The stable rational cohomology of
\IA_{n}
has also recently been studied by Katada
[
Kat22
]
and Habiro-Katada
[
HK23
]
. In
[
Kat22
]
, Katada studied that so called
Albanese cohomology
of
\IA_{n}
, which is defined as the image of the cup product map
\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{1}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\to H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
and denoted
H_{A}^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
. Based on her calculations, she conjectured a complete description of the stable Albanese cohomology, which can be slightly reformulated as follows:

Conjecture 1.1
.

[
Kat22
, Conjecture 8.2]

For
n\gg*
, we have

H^{*}_{A}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}(\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n))/\mathsf{IH},

where
\mathsf{IH}
is the ideal generated by elements of the form

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\otimes e_{i})\wedge(e_{i}^{\#}\wedge f_{3}\otimes v)-(f_{3}\wedge f_{1}\otimes e_{i})\wedge(e_{i}^{\#}\wedge f_{2}\otimes v)\in\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}(\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n)),

where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is the standard basis of
H(n)
, defined by
e_{i}:=[x_{i}]
, and
\{e_{1}^{\#},\ldots,e_{1}^{\#}\}
is the dual basis.

Remark 1.2
.

We use the notation
\mathsf{IH}
for this ideal as the relation (
3
) corresponds to a “directed IH-relation” among
(2,1)
-valent directed graphs, as explained in Section
6
below.

In the same paper, Katada introduced a graded
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation
W_{*}(n)
(see Appendix
A
for a definition of this representation) and proved that for
n\gg*
,
W_{*}(n)^{\vee}\subseteq H_{A}^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})

[
Kat22
, Theorem 6.1]
. Based on low degree cases, she conjectured that in fact

W_{*}(n)^{\vee}\cong H^{*}_{A}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}(\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n))/\mathsf{IH}

for
n\gg*
(
[
Kat22
, Conjecture 1.7]
).

In
[
HK23
]
, the authors study the entire stable rational cohomology of
\IA_{n}
and conjecture a complete description, based on Katada’s conjecture and a weaker version of the conjecture by Church and Farb that
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
satisfies representation stably, namely that it is stably a finite dimensional and algebraic representation of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

One of the main inputs for their calculation is the stable cohomology of
\Aut(F_{n})
with the coefficients
H(n)^{\otimes p}\otimes H^{\vee}(n)^{\otimes q}
, which was recently computed by the author in
[
Lin22
]
. By studying the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence associated to the short exact sequence (
2
), with these representations as coefficients, they proved the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

cf.
[
HK23
, Conjecture 7.5, Theorem 7.6]

Assuming the conjecture that stably
H_{A}^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\cong W_{*}(n)^{\vee}
and that stably
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
is an algebraic
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation in each degree, it follows that in a range
n\gg*
we have a
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivariant ring isomorphism

H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}(\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n))/\mathsf{IH}\otimes\mathbb{Q}[y_{4},y_{8},\ldots],

where
\mathbb{Q}[y_{4},y_{8},\ldots]
is a trivial graded
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation with
|y_{4i}|=4i
.

Remark 1.4
.

We recall that the stable rational cohomology of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
was computed by Borel in
[
Bor74
]
and is isomorphic to the exterior algebra
\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}\{x_{5},x_{9},\ldots\}
, where
|x_{4i+1}|=4i+1
. The trivial graded representation
\mathbb{Q}[y_{4},y_{8},\ldots]
in the theorem above appears when studying so-called “anti-transgression maps” in the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence associated to the short exact sequence (
2
), which gives rise to a degree
-1
map
\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}\{x_{5},x_{9},\ldots\}\to H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})}
. We explain this in more detail in Section
5.1
(cf.
[
HK23
, Section 6]
).

The idea of studying the stable cohomology of
\IA_{n}
using this spectral sequence is not unique to the work of Habiro and Katada. For example, in
[
KRW20
]
and
[
RW24
]
, Kupers-Randal-Williams and Randal-Williams used this kind of approach to study the stable cohomology of Torelli groups of surfaces and their higher dimensional analogues. Furthermore, by utilizing a category theoretical approach using so-called
Brauer categories
, Kupers and Randal-Williams were able to effectively study the structure of these cohomology groups simoultaneously as representations of arithmetic groups as well as rings. The goal of this paper is to apply the methods of
[
KRW20
]
to the study of
\IA_{n}
. This will allow us to retrieve the results of Habiro and Katada in a somewhat different way and also to push them a bit further.

To state our main result, we must first recall a little bit more background. Although not phrased directly like this in their paper, the crucial part in the assumption of Habiro and Katada that
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
is stably finite dimensional and algebraic is the implication that
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
satisfies
Borel vanishing
:

Definition 1.5
.

A
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation
V
is said to satisfy
Borel vanishing
if in some range
n\gg*
, the cup product map

H^{*}(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Q})\otimes V^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})}\to H^{*}(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),V)

is an isomorphism.

Remark 1.6
.

Most “reasonable” representations of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
satisfy this. For example, Borel proved in
[
Bor74
]
that any finite dimensional algebraic representation does and in Section
2
we show that this actually implies Borel vanishing for any finite dimensional
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation.

This means that we do not need to make any assumptions about the algebraicity of the representation
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
. However, the method we employ is still only able to detect the “algebraic part” of the cohomology. For
V
a
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation we define its
algebraic part

V^{\textrm{alg}}
as

V^{\textrm{alg}}:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\subseteq V\\
W\text{ algebraic}\end{subarray}}W\subseteq V.

With these definitions, our main theorem can be stated as follows:

Theorem A
.

There is a
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivariant graded ring homomorphism

\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}\left(\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n)\right)\otimes\mathbb{Q}[y_{4},y_{8},\ldots]\to H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\textrm{alg}}

where
\mathbb{Q}[y_{4},y_{8},\ldots]
is a trivial graded
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation. If there is a
Q\geq 0
such that
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
satisfies Borel vanishing in each degree
*<Q
, then in a range
n\gg*
, this ring homomorphism is surjective in degrees
*\leq Q
and the kernel is the ideal
\mathsf{IH}
.

We note that, as expected, we obtain the same conjectural description of
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\textrm{alg}}
as Habiro and Katada (since in their case they assume that the entire stable cohomology is algebraic). However, we remark that in this theorem, there is no assumption about the Albanese cohomology of
\IA_{n}
.

Let us also remark on the degree shift appearing in the theorem and its corollary, which is obtained by pushing the spectral sequence argument of Habiro and Katada a bit further (see Section
5
below). Since we have seen that
H^{1}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n)
is in fact a finite dimensional (even algebraic) representation of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and thus satisfies Borel vanishing, we get in particular the following corollary:

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
\mathsf{IH}^{2}
denote the degree 2 subspace of the ideal
\mathsf{IH}
. For
n\gg 2
, we have

H^{2}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\textrm{alg}}=H_{A}^{2}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}\left(\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n)\right)/\mathsf{IH}^{2}.

Remark 1.8
.

The stable second rational Albanese cohomology of
\IA_{n}
has previously been computed by Pettet
[
Pet05
]
and this corollary shows that this is actually all of the stable rational algebraic cohomology of
\IA_{n}
in degree 2.

1.1.
Categorical version of the main theorem

Theorem
A
is derived from a categorical description, which we derive using the methods of
[
KRW20
]
, applied to the so-called
downward walled Brauer category
. Let us briefly summarize the definition of this category, so that we can state the categorical version of the main result in these terms. To make the notation a bit less cluttered, let us write
G:=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
for the rest of this subsection.

Figure 1.
A morphism in
\dwBr
.

Definition 1.9
.

The downward walled Brauer category, which we denote by
\dwBr
, is the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear category whose objects are pairs of finite sets
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
and where the space of morphisms
\Hom_{\dwBr}(S,T)
is spanned by diagrams such as those in Figure
1
(where the order of crossings is not part of the data, but only included in the image to make it easier to read). Composition is given by concatenation of such diagrams.

We define a functor
K:\dwBr\to\Gr(\Rep(G))
(the category of graded
G
-representations) by
(S_{1},S_{2})=H(n)^{\otimes S_{1}}\otimes H^{\vee}(n)^{\otimes S_{2}}
, which we think of as a graded representation concentrated in degree 0, and which on morphisms is given by applying the duality pairing
H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n)\to\mathbb{Q}
to the tensor factors that are matched via horizontal strands in a diagram like the one in Figure
1
, and permuting the remaining ones according to the vertical strands.

By taking linear duals, we get a functor
K^{\vee}:\dwBr^{\mathrm{op}}\to\Gr(\Rep(G))
, which we use to construct a functor
K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}(-)
from the category of functors
\dwBr\to\Gr(\Rep(G))
to
\Gr(\Rep(G))
, using the coend

K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}M:=\int^{x\in\dwBr}K^{\vee}(x)\otimes M(x).

The category
\dwBr
has a symmetric monoidality given by disjoint union and this allows us to define a symmetric monoidality on the category of functors
\dwBr\to\Gr(\Rep(G))
by Day convolution. It follows from the general results of
[
KRW20
, Section 2]
that the functor
K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}(-)
is strongly symmetric monoidal.

We will use this functor to describe
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\textrm{alg}}
, so let us briefly describe the functor which will play the role of
M
. We define a functor
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}:\dwBr\to\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})
, where
\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})
is the category of graded
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces (which we may also consider as the subcategory of
\Gr(\Rep(G))
consisting of trivial graded representations), by letting
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(S)
be the graded vector space with a basis given by partitions of the set
S_{1}
with

(1)

at least
|S_{2}|
parts,

(2)

|S_{2}|
of the parts are labeled by the elements of
S_{2}
and the remaining parts are unlabeled,

(3)

and where there are no labeled parts of size 1.

We consider such a labeled partition as having degree
|S_{1}|-|S_{2}|
, so in particular
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(S)
is concentrated in a single degree.

For a morphism described by a diagram such as that in Figure
1
, the functor acts by permuting and relabeling according to the vertical strands, and merging parts according to the horizontal strands. We describe this in detail in Section
4.3
below.

Tensoring pointwise with the sign representation
\det(S):=\det\left(\mathbb{Q}^{S_{1}}\right)\otimes\det\left(\mathbb{Q}^{S_{1}}\right)
of the product of the symmetric groups on
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, which we consider as a graded representation concentrated in degree 0, we obtain a functor
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det:\dwBr\to\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})
.
We prove in Section
4
that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det
is a commutative ring object in the category of functors from
\dwBr
to
\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
, so
K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det)
is a commutative ring object in
\Gr(\Rep(G))
. With this, we can now state the categorical version of the main theorem:

Theorem B
.

There is a graded ring homomorphism

(4)

K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det)\otimes\mathbb{Q}[y_{4},y_{8},\ldots]\to H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\textrm{alg}}

If there is a
Q\geq 0
such that
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})
satisfies Borel vanishing in each degree
*<Q
, then in a range
n\gg*
, the ring homomorphism (
4
) is an isomorphism in degrees
*\leq Q
.

Theorem
A
is proven from Theorem
B
by showing that the graded commutative ring
K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det)
is in fact isomorphic to
\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}(\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}2}\kern-0.5pt}}H^{\vee}(n)\otimes H(n))/\mathsf{IH}
, in a stable range of degrees. This is proven in Section
6
below.

Remark 1.10
.

In correspondence with the author, regarding an earlier draft of this paper, it was explained by Mai Katada that in a stable range of degrees we have that
K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det)\cong W_{*}(n)^{\vee}
, as graded
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-representations, and that combining this fact with certain results from
[
Kat22
]
, it is possible to prove her conjecture about the stable Albanese (co)homology. She kindly agreed to add her proof in Appendix
A
below.

1.2.
Overview of the paper

Let us give a brief overview of the structure of the paper.

In Section
2
, we recall some background on representation theory, including Schur-Weyl duality and the invariant theory of the general linear group. After this we cover Borel vanishing in some more detail and show that every representation of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
which is finite dimensional satisfies Borel vanishing.

In Section
3
we start by recalling the general categorical setup from
[
KRW20
]
and some of their general results. We then apply this to the walled Brauer category (a category which has
\dwBr
as a subcategory) and prove the main categorical lemmas concerning this category needed to prove Theorem
B
. This section closely follows
[
KRW20
, Sections 2.2-2.3]
.

In Section
4
, we recall the main result of
[
Lin22
]
, which is a description of the stable cohomology of
\Aut(F_{n})
with coefficients in the representations
H(n)^{\otimes p}\otimes H^{\vee}(n)^{\otimes q}
, in terms of a space spanned by partitions of
\{1,\ldots,p\}
labeled by the set
\{1,\ldots,q\}
, for any
p,q\geq 0
. However, in
[
Lin22
]
only the existence of an isomorphism between these spaces is demonstrated, so in this section we also construct such an isomorphism explicitly, using the results of Kawazumi
[
Kaw05
]
and Kawazumi-Vespa
[
KV21
]
. After this, we prove that this actually defines an isomorphism of symmetric monoidal functors on the walled Brauer category.

In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
B
, by studying the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence associated to the short exact sequence (
2
) and then combining this with the categorical tools from the previous sections. The analysis of the spectral sequence closely follows the methods of
[
HK23
]

2.
Background on representation theory

In this section, we recall some background on representation theory needed to prove the main theorem. In order to study Torelli groups of surfaces and their higher dimensional analogues in
[
KRW20
]
, Kupers and Randal-Williams use representation theory of orthogonal and symplectic groups. In our setting, we instead need to consider representations of the general linear group. In this section we therefore retread much of what Kupers and Randal-Williams cover in Section 2 of their paper, but in the case of the general linear group.

2.1.
Representation theory of the general linear group

Let
H(n):=H_{1}(F_{n},\mathbb{Q})
. Let
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
denote the group of
\mathbb{Q}
-linear automorphisms of
H(n)
. The group
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
is the
\mathbb{Q}
-points of the algebraic group
\GGL_{n}
, which is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.

2.1.1.
Irreducible representations of
\GGL_{n}
and of symmetric groups

We start by recalling the definition of the irreducible representations of the algebraic group
\GGL_{n}
, as well as those of symmetric groups, as these are intimately connected. In particular, the irreducible representations of both groups are classified using
partitions
.

Definition 2.1
.

A parition is a decreasing sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{l}\geq 0)
of non-negative integers that eventually reaches zero. We call
|\lambda|:=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{l}
the
weight
of the partition, and if
|\lambda|=k
for some non-negative integer
k
, we say that
\lambda
is a partition of
k
. The largest
l
such that
\lambda_{l}>0
is called the
length
of the partition and we denote it by
l(\lambda)
.

The irreducible representations of the symmetric groups
\Sigma_{k}
are called
Specht modules
and are indexed by partitions of
k
. We denote the Specht module associated to a partition
\lambda
of weight
k
by
S^{\lambda}
. This is the image of the
Young symmetrizer

c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Q}[\Sigma_{k}]
acting on
\mathbb{Q}[\Sigma_{k}]
(see for example
[
FH91
]
for a definition of
c_{\lambda}
).

We can also associate a representation of
\GGL_{n}
to any partition
\lambda
, of any weight
k
, by applying the
Schur functor
associated to
\lambda
:

(5)

V_{\lambda}:=S_{\lambda}(H(n))=S^{\lambda}\otimes_{\Sigma_{k}}H(n)^{\otimes k}.

This representation is always irreducible, and non-zero as long as the length of
\lambda
is at most
n
. For example, for
\lambda=(1)
, we simply get back
V_{1}=H(n)
. However, not all irreducible representations of
\GGL_{n}
are of this kind, for example the dual representation of
H(n)
. For notational convenience, let us write
H^{\vee}(n):=H(n)^{\vee}
for the linear dual representation.

To describe all of the irreducible representations of
\GGL_{n}
, we need to consider the mixed tensor powers of
H(n)
and
H^{\vee}(n)
. For
p,q\geq 0
, we let

(6)

K_{p,q}(n):=H(n)^{\otimes p}\otimes H^{\vee}(n)^{\otimes q}.

Note that by applying the duality pairing
\lambda:H(n)\otimes H^{\vee}(n)\to\mathbb{Q}
, we get a
\GGL_{n}
-equivariant map
\lambda_{i,j}:K_{p,q}(n)\to K_{p-1,q-1}(n)
for all
p,q\geq 1
,
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq j\leq q
. We define

(7)

K_{p,q}^{\circ}(n):=\bigcap_{i,j}\ker(\lambda_{i,j}).

Definition 2.2
.

If
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions, we call the pair
(\lambda,\mu)
a
bipartition
and if
|\lambda|=p
and
|\mu|=q
, we say it is a bipartition of
(p,q)
and write
|(\lambda,\mu)|=(p,q)
. We define the
length
of a bipartition
(\lambda,\mu)
as
l(\lambda,\mu)=l(\lambda)+\lambda(\mu)
.

For a bipartition
(\lambda,\mu)
, we define representations

(8)

V_{\lambda,\mu}:=(S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu})\otimes_{\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}}K_{p,q}^{\circ}(n).

Similarly to above, we have that
V_{\lambda,\mu}
is non-zero as long as
l(\lambda,\mu)\leq n
and irreducible in this case. Furthermore, these are all the irreducible representations of
\GGL_{n}
, up to isomorphism.

The following important theorem tells us how
K^{\circ}_{p,q}(n)
decomposes into irreducibles:

Theorem 2.3

(Schur-Weyl duality)
.

The
\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}\times\GGL_{n}
-representation
K_{p,q}^{\circ}(n)
decomposes into irreducibles as

K_{p,q}^{\circ}(n)\cong\bigoplus_{|(\lambda,\mu)|=(p,q)}S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu}\otimes V_{\lambda,\mu}.

Proof.

See for example
[
Koi89
, Theorem 1.1]
.
∎

In order to use the categorical framework which we will describe in Section
3
below, we will need to work with tensor powers of
H(n)
and
H^{\vee}(n)
over arbitrary finite sets. We therefore note that for any pair
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
of finite sets, we can define
K_{S_{1},S_{2}}(n)
and
K_{S_{1},S_{2}}^{\circ}(n)
analogously to the definitions above. For brevity, we will often write
K_{S}(n)=K_{S_{1},S_{2}}(n)
and
K_{S}(n)^{\circ}=K_{S_{1},S_{2}}^{\circ}(n)

For a pair of finite sets
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
, we can also describe a decomposition of
K_{S}(n)
in terms of the representations
K^{\circ}_{I}(n)
, for
I\subseteq S
. In order to do this, note that we have a form
\omega:\mathbb{Q}\to H(n)\otimes H^{\vee}(n)
, which is dual to the pairing
\lambda
. We can describe it by sending 1 to the element corresponding to
\mathrm{id}_{H(n)}
under the isomorphism
H(n)\otimes H^{\vee}(n)\cong\Hom_{\mathbb{Q}}(H(n),H(n))
. If
e_{i}:=[x_{i}]\in H(n)
are the standard basis elements, and we write
e^{\#}_{i}
for the dual basis elements in
H^{\vee}(n)
, we thus have

\omega(1)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\otimes e^{\#}_{i}.

We remark that this means that
\lambda(\omega(1))=n
.

For any
s_{1}\in S_{1}
and
s_{2}\in S_{2}
we can apply the form to get a
\GGL_{n}
-equivariant map

\omega_{s_{1},s_{2}}:K_{S\setminus(\{s_{1}\},\{s_{2}\})}(n)\to K_{S}(n).

We call this map an
insertion
. The representation
K_{S}(n)
satisfies the following:

Proposition 2.4
.

For any pair
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
of finite sets we have

(9)

K_{S}(n)=K^{\circ}_{S}(n)\oplus\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1}\in S_{1}\\
s_{2}\in S_{2}\end{subarray}}\omega_{s_{1},s_{2}}(K_{S\setminus(\{s_{1}\},\{s_{2}\})}(n))

Proof.

We prove this similarly to
[
FH91
, Equation 17.12]
. Letting
e_{i}:=[x_{i}]\in H(n)
denote the standard basis, we have a standard inner product on
H(n)
, which we will denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, given by
\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
. This induces a dual metric on
H^{\vee}(n)
and these two metrics together induce a metric on
K_{S}(n)=H(n)^{\otimes S_{1}}\otimes H^{\vee}(n)^{\otimes S_{2}}
, for any pair
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
of finite sets , which we will also denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, for simplicty.

Now let
a\in H(n)
and
\beta\in H^{\vee}(n)
. Writing these as
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}
and
\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}e^{\#}_{i}
, for
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
, we get

\displaystyle\langle\omega(1),a\otimes\beta\rangle=\sum_{i,j,k}a_{j}b_{k}\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle\langle e^{\#}_{i},e^{\#}_{k}\rangle=\sum_{i,j,k}a_{j}b_{k}\delta_{ij}\delta_{ik}=\sum_{i}a_{i}b_{i}=\lambda(a,\beta).

It follows from this that for any
s_{1}\in S_{1}
,
s_{2}\in S_{2}
,
\ker(\lambda_{s_{1},s_{2}})=\mathrm{im}(\omega_{s_{1},s_{2}})^{\perp}
, which implies (
9
).
∎

We may now apply this proposition inductively to
K_{S\setminus(\{s_{1}\},\{s_{2}\})}
and decompose further, which corresponds to applying several insertions at once. To make this more precise, let us first make the following definition:

Definition 2.5
.

If
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
is a pair of finite sets, a
bipartite matching
of
S
is a partition of
S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}
into disjoint ordered pairs
(a,b)
such that
a\in S_{1}
and
b\in S_{2}
. Let
\mathrm{M}(S)
denote the set of bipartite matchings of
S
.

Remark 2.6
.

Note that the a bipartite matching of
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
is equivalent to a bijection between the two sets. However, we use this terminology in analogy with the one used in
[
KRW20
]
.

If
I\subseteq S
with
|I_{1}|=|I_{2}|
and
m\in\mathrm{M}(I)
, we thus get a
\GGL_{n}
-equivariant map

\omega_{m}:K_{S\setminus I}(n)\to K_{S}(n)

and we can decompose
K_{S}(n)
as follows:

Proposition 2.7
.

For
S
a pair of finite sets, the representation
K_{S}(n)
decomposes as

K_{S}(n)\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{\min(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq S\\
|I_{1}|=|I_{2}|=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathrm{M}(I)\end{subarray}}\omega_{m}(K_{S\setminus I}^{\circ}(n)).

For
n\geq|S|
, this is a direct sum

K_{S}(n)\cong\bigoplus_{I\subseteq S}\bigoplus_{m\in\mathrm{M}(I)}\omega_{m}(K_{S\setminus I}^{\circ}(n)).

Proof.

Let
s=\min(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)
. We start introducing the notation that for any
0\leq i\leq s
,

K^{(i)}_{S}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq S\\
|I_{1}|=|I_{2}|=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathrm{M}(I)\end{subarray}}\omega_{m}(K_{S\setminus I}^{\circ}(n))\subseteq K_{S}(n).

Thus we first want to show that

K_{S}(n)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{s}K_{S}^{{(i)}}(n).

We prove this by induction on
s
. If
s=0
, we have
K_{S}^{(s)}(n)=K_{S}^{\circ}(n)=K_{S}(n)
, so the statement is trivially true. Now suppose that statement holds for some
s\geq 0
and suppose that
\min(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)=s+1
. By first applying Proposition
2.4
and then the inductive assumption, we get

\displaystyle K_{S}(n)

\displaystyle=K_{S}^{(0)}(n)\oplus\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1}\in S_{1}\\
s_{2}\in S_{2}\end{subarray}}\omega_{s_{1},s_{2}}(K_{S\setminus(\{s_{1}\},\{s_{2}\})}(n))

\displaystyle=K_{S}^{(0)}(n)\oplus\sum_{s_{1},s_{2}}\omega_{s_{1},s_{2}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{s}K_{S\setminus(\{s_{1}\},\{s_{2}\})}^{(i)}(n)\right)

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{s+1}K_{S}^{(i)}(n).

Finally, let us assume that
n\geq|S|
. In this case, it follows by
[
Ben+94
, Theorem 2.7, 2.11]
that the sum

K^{(i)}_{S}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq S\\
|I_{1}|=|I_{2}|=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathrm{M}(I)\end{subarray}}\omega_{m}(K_{S\setminus I}^{\circ}(n))\subseteq K_{S}(n)

is in fact an internal direct sum. Thus the second part of the proposition follows.
∎

In anticipation of the next section, we also note the following basic observation about insertions:

Lemma 2.8
.

If
v\in H(n)
, then
\lambda_{1,1}(v\otimes\omega(1))=v
. Similarly, if
\alpha\in H^{\vee}(n)
, then
\lambda_{1,2}(\omega(1)\otimes\alpha)=\alpha
.

Proof.

Via the isomorphism
H(n)\otimes H^{\vee}(n)\cong\Hom_{\mathbb{Q}}(H(n),H(n))
we have
K_{2,1}(n)\cong H(n)\otimes\Hom_{\mathbb{Q}}(H(n),H(n))
and under this identification, the map
\lambda_{1,1}:K_{2,1}(n)\to H(n)
corresponds to the evaluation map. Since the isomorphism sends
\omega(1)
to
\mathrm{id}_{H(n)}
by definition, the first statement follows. The second statement is obtained in the corresponding way, using the isomorphism
H(n)\otimes H^{\vee}(n)\cong\Hom_{\mathbb{Q}}(H^{\vee}(n),H^{\vee}(n))
.
∎

2.1.2.
The arithmetic subgroup
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})

If
\mathbf{G}
is an algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, recall that an
arithmetic subgroup
is a subgroup
G\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
which is commensurable to
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
. Our main example will be
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
which is an arithmetic subgroup of
\GGL_{n}
. Note that the standard representation
\Aut(F_{n})\to\GL(H(n))
factors through
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Remark 2.9
.

Sometimes it will also be useful to consider the algebraic subgroup
\mathbf{SL}_{n}^{\pm}\subset\GGL_{n}
, defined by the polynomial function
\det(X)^{2}-1
. Note that
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is an arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbf{SL}_{n}^{\pm}
as well. Furthermore, since
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is Zariski dense in
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
, it follows that
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is Zariski dense in
\SL_{n}^{\pm}(\mathbb{Q})
. In contrast
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is
not
Zariski dense in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
(consider again the polynomial function
\det(X)^{2}-1
).

2.1.3.
Invariant theory of
\GGL_{n}
and
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})

As above, we write
\lambda:H(n)\otimes H(n)^{*}\to\mathbb{Q}
and
\omega:\mathbb{Q}\to H(n)\otimes H(n)^{*}
for the natural pairing and form, respectively. Since these maps are
\GGL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-equivariant, the image of
\omega
is in particular a
\GGL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-invariant tensor and we can use the form to construct tensors of this kind, by applying several insertions, as in the previous subsection. In particular, we get a linear map:

(10)

\mathbb{Q}\{\text{Bipartite matchings of }S\}\to K_{S}(n)^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})},

defined by
m\mapsto\omega_{m}(1)
. The map satisfies the following proposition:

Proposition 2.10
.

For any
p,q\geq 0
, the map (
10
) is a surjection. Furthermore, it is an isomorphism as long as
n\geq|S|+3
.

Proof.

For surjectivity, we use the first fundamental theorem of invariant theory for
\mathbf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
(see for example
[
Pro07
, Chapter 11.1.2, Theorem 3]
1
1

1

Here, the theorem is stated for the field
\mathbb{C}
, but by the reasoning at the beginning of the same chapter, the results are also valid over
\mathbb{Q}
.
), which can be stated as saying that the the
\mathbf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-invariants are generated by elements of the form
\omega_{m}(1)
, and possibly by determinant representations in

or

. However, since the determinant representation of

is the sign representation, it follows that the elements

2.2.
Borel vanishing.

As we have already noted, the first main input we will use to compute stable cohomology of
\IA_{n}
is the stable cohomology of
\Aut(F_{n})
with the twisted coefficients
K_{S}(n)
. The second main input is the stable cohomology of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with twisted coefficients, which is known for a large class of coefficients due to work of Borel.

Definition 2.11
.

If
\mathbf{G}
is an algebraic group,
G
is an arithmetic subgroup and
V
is a finite dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-representation of
G
, then
V
is
algebraic
if there is a morphism of algebraic groups
\mathbf{G}\to\GGL(V)
such that taking
\mathbb{Q}
-points and restricting to
G
yields the representation
G\to\GGL(V)
. We say that a representation
V
of
G
is
almost algebraic
if there is a finite index subgroup
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
such that the restriction
G^{\prime}\to\GGL(V)
is algebraic.

Remark 2.12
.

If
\rho:\mathbf{SL}_{n}^{\pm}\to\GGL(V)
is an irreducible algebraic representation, it lifts to an algebraic representation of
\GGL_{n}
in a unique way (see
[
FH91
, §15.5]
). For
V
a finite dimensional of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, it thus follows that the notion of
V
being almost algebraic does not depend on whether we consider
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
as an arithmetic subgroup of
\GGL_{n}
of of
\mathbf{SL}_{n}^{\pm}
.

The main reason to consider almost algebraic representations is the following theorem:

Theorem 2.13

(
[
Ser79
, Page 109]
)
.

If
\mathbf{G}
is a simple algebraic group of
\mathbb{Q}
-rank
\geq 2
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
,
G
is an arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
and
V
is a finite-dimensional representation of
G
, then
V
is almost algebraic.

As
\mathbf{SL}_{n}^{\pm}
contains
\mathbf{SL}_{n}
as a finite index subgroup and
\mathbf{SL}_{n}
is a simple algebraic group, the theorem applies to almost algebraic representations of arithmetic subgroups of
\mathbf{SL}_{n}^{\pm}
. In particular finite-dimensional representations of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
are almost algebraic. Considering almost algebraic representations will be useful in the proof of the following theorem, which is a slightly stronger version of the Borel vanishing theorem for
\GGL_{n}
(cf.
[
KRW20
, Theorem 2.3]
):

Theorem 2.14

(Borel vanishing)
.

Let
G
be an arithmetic subgroup of
\GGL_{n}
and
V
be a finite dimensional representation of
G
. For
n\geq*+2
, the maps

H^{*}(\GGL_{\infty},\mathbb{Q})\otimes V^{G}\to H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Q})\otimes V^{G}\to H^{*}(G,V)

are isomorphisms, where
H^{*}(\GGL_{\infty},\mathbb{Q})=\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}\{x_{5},x_{9},\ldots\}
.

Proof.

First, for
V
a finite dimensional and algebraic
G
-representation, this is due to Borel
[
Bor74
]
[
Bor81
]
, but with a different range. The improved range is due to Li and Sun
[
LS19
, Example 1.10]
. For
V
finite dimensional and thus almost algebraic, we may apply the same transfer argument as in
[
KRW20
]
, but let us spell it out in detail for completeness. Let
G^{\prime}\leq G
be a finite index subgroup of
G
such that the restriction of
G\to\GGL(V)
is algebraic. We may assume that
G^{\prime}
is a normal subgroup of
G
by taking the normal core. We then have a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Q})\otimes V^{G}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(G,V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(G^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})^{G/G^{\prime}}\otimes(V^{G^{\prime}})^{G/G^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(H^{*}(G^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})\otimes V^{G^{\prime}})^{G/G^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(G^{\prime},V)^{G/G^{\prime}},}

where the top horizontal arrow is given by cup product, the first bottom horizontal arrow is the natural inclusion, the second bottom horizontal arrow is induced by the cup product and the vertical arrows are given by transer maps. By
[
Bro94
, Proposition 10.4]
, the vertical arrows are isomorphisms and by the first part of the proof, the second bottom horizontal arrow is as well, in a range of degrees. Furthermore, by the first part of the theorem
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{*}(G^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{*}(\GGL_{\infty},\mathbb{Q})
, so it follows that
G
acts trivially on
H^{*}(G^{\prime},\mathbb{Q})
in the given range of degrees, and thus the first lower horizontal arrows is also an isomorphism in this range. It follows that the top horizontal arrows is also an isomorphism in the given range of degrees.
∎

As in
[
KRW20
, Section 2.1.2]
, let us point out that in particular, if
V
is finite dimensional, the theorem implies that for
n\geq 3
, we have
H^{1}(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),V)=0
, so by the long exact sequence in group cohomology we get that
[-]^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})}
is an exact functor on the category of finite dimensional representations. Furthermore, we have that if
V
and
W
are finite dimensional then

\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})}(V,W)\cong H^{1}(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),V^{\vee}\otimes W)=0,

so all extensions of finite dimensional representations split.

3.1.
Categorical background

We start by briefly summarizing the setup and results of
[
KRW20
, Section 2.2]
. This can be safely skipped by the reader familiar with that paper.

We start from two categories. First, we let
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,1_{\mathcal{A}})
denote a symmetric monoidal
\mathbb{Q}
-linear abelian category which we assume has all finite enriched colimits. In our applications, this will generally be the category
\Rep(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}))
.

Secondly, we let
(\Lambda,\oplus,0_{\Lambda})
be a symmetric monoidal
\mathbb{Q}
-linear category, where we have a partial order
\leq
on the isomorphism classes of objects, defined by saying that
[x]\leq[y]
whenever
\Hom_{\Lambda}(x,y)\neq 0
. Furthermore, we will assume that any object
x\in\Lambda
only has nonzero morphisms to a finite number of objects, up to isomorphism. In our applications,
\Lambda
will be the
downward walled Brauer category
, which we introduce in the next subsection.

We will also consider the abelian categories
\mathcal{A}^{\Lambda}
and
(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{\Lambda}
, of functors from
\Lambda
to the respective categories.

There are two subclasses of objects of these categories that will be of particular interest, but which we can define in any abelian (resp. symmetric monoidal) category:

Definition 3.1
.

We say that an object
B
of an abelian category
\mathcal{B}
is of
finite length
if there is a finite sequence

0\hookrightarrow B_{1}\hookrightarrow B_{2}\hookrightarrow\cdots\hookrightarrow B_{k}=B

of monomorphisms, such that at each step, the only quotients of the cokernel
\coker(B_{i+1}\hookrightarrow B_{i})
are 0 and the cokernel itself. Note that in
\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
this just corresponds to being a finite dimensional vector space. We write
\mathcal{B}^{f}
for the full subcategory of objects of finite length.

Remark 3.2
.

The category
\mathcal{A}
is tensored over
(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{f}
, which means that for
V\in(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{f}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
there is an object
A\tensoredover V\in\mathcal{A}
. The object
A\tensoredover V
is characterized by a natural isomorphism
\Hom_{\mathcal{A}}(A\tensoredover V,-)\cong\Hom_{\mathbb{Q}}(V,\Hom_{\mathcal{A}}(A,-))
. In particular, there is a functor

1_{\mathcal{A}}\tensoredover-:(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{f}\to\mathcal{A}^{f},

which has a right adjoint
\Hom_{\mathcal{A}}(1_{\mathcal{A}},-):\mathcal{A}^{f}\to(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{f}
. Note that for
\mathcal{A}=\Rep(G)
, where
G
is a group, the functor
1_{\mathcal{A}}\tensoredover-
is simply given by considering a vector space as a trivial representation, and the right adjoint is given by taking the
G
-invariants of a
G
-representation.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that an object
C
of a symmetric monoidal category

is

if there is another object

, which we call a

3.2.
The walled Brauer category

Let
G=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathcal{A}:=\Rep(G)
be the category of representations of
G
. We shall assume that
n\geq 3
, so that
[-]^{G}
is an exact functor and and all extensions split.

Definition 3.6
.

The
walled Brauer category

(\wBr_{d},\oplus,0)
of charge
d\in\mathbb{Q}
is the symmetric monoidal
\mathbb{Q}
-linear category defined as follows:

•

The objects of
\wBr_{d}
are pairs
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
of finite sets.

•

The space of morphisms
\wBr_{d}(S,T)
is the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space with basis given by quadruples
(f,g,m_{S},m_{T})
, where
f
is a bijection from a subset
S^{\prime}_{1}\subset S
to a subset
T^{\prime}_{1}\subset T_{1}
,
g
is a bijection from a subset
S^{\prime}_{2}\subset S_{2}
to a subset
T^{\prime}_{2}\subset T_{2}
,
m_{S}
is a bipartite matching of
(S_{1}\setminus S^{\prime}_{1},S_{2}\setminus S^{\prime}_{2})

and
M_{T}
is a bipartite matching of
(T_{1}\setminus T^{\prime}_{1},T_{2}\setminus T^{\prime}_{2})
. Note that this means that a morphism in
\wBr_{d}(S,T)
corresponds precisely to a bipartite matching of
(S_{1}\sqcup T_{2},T_{1}\sqcup S_{2})
.
The data of a morphism can be recorded graphically as illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
Illustratation of a morphism in the walled Brauer category. Note that the order of crossings is not part of the data and does not matter for the morphism, but is only included to make the picture easier to read.

•

The composition
\wBr_{d}(S,T)\otimes\wBr_{d}(T,U)\to\wBr_{d}(S,U)
is by defined concatenating pictures like that in Figure
2
vertically, removing closed components and multiplying by
d
, raised to the power of the number of closed components removed.

Figure 3.
The monoidal product of two morphisms in
\wBr_{n}
.

•

The monoidal product is given on objects by
(S_{1},S_{2})\oplus(T_{1},T_{2}):=(S_{1}\sqcup T_{1},S_{2}\sqcup T_{2})
and on morphisms by concatenating diagrams like that in Figure
2
horizontally, with the obvious symmetry. The monoidal unit
0
is the pair
(\varnothing,\varnothing)
.

As in
[
KRW20
]
, will be particularly interested in the following subcategory of
\wBr_{d}
:

Definition 3.7
.

The
downward walled Brauer category

\dwBr\subset\wBr_{d}
is the subcategory with the same objects as
\wBr_{d}
, but only with

\Hom_{\dwBr}(S,T)=\mathbb{Q}\{(f,g,m_{S},\varnothing)\in\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(S,T)\}.

Note that this means that no closed components can appear when composing morhpisms, so this subcategory is independent of the charge
d
. We will write
i:\dwBr\to\wBr_{d}
for the inclusion functor.

3.2.1.
Left Kan extensions along the inclusion functor

For
\mathcal{C}
a symmetric monoidal
\mathbb{Q}
-linear abelian category (either
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
), we will several times consider the functor
i_{*}:\mathcal{C}^{\dwBr}\to\mathcal{C}^{\wBr_{n}}
given by left Kan extension along the inclusion functor
i
, so let us described this functor in some detail. Let
A:\dwBr\to\mathcal{C}
be a functor. The functor
i_{*}A:\wBr_{n}\to\mathcal{C}
may then be expressed using the coend

(13)

i_{*}A:=\int^{T\in\dwBr}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(T,-)\tensoredover A(T)\in\mathcal{C}^{\wBr_{n}},

which may also be expressed as the coequalizer of the diagram

\bigoplus_{(U\to T)\in\dwBr}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(T,-)\tensoredover A(U)\rightrightarrows\bigoplus_{T\in\dwBr}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(T,-)\tensoredover A(T).

We note that any morphism
(f,g,m_{T},m_{S})\in\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(T,S)
can be written as a composition

(\mathrm{id}_{S^{\prime}_{1}},\mathrm{id}_{S^{\prime}_{2}},\varnothing,m_{S})\circ(f,g,m_{T},\varnothing),

and since the first map in the composition is in
\dwBr(T,S^{\prime})
, it follows that for
S\in\wBr_{n}
, we have

(14)

i_{*}(A)(S)\cong\bigoplus_{I\subseteq S}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)\tensoredover A(I)

Note that
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)=\mathbb{Q}M(I)
, so for brevity, we denote a basis element of
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)
by
m_{S\setminus I}
instead of
(\varnothing,\varnothing,\varnothing,m_{S\setminus I})
. To describe the functor
i_{*}(A)
on morphisms, we consider two main cases:

(1)

First, consider a morphism of the form
F=(f,g,\varnothing,m_{T}):S\to T
, i.e. where
f:S_{1}\to T_{1}
and
g:S_{2}\to T_{2}
are injections and
m_{T}
is a bipartite matching of
(T_{1}\setminus f(S_{1}),T_{2}\setminus g(S_{2}))
. For
I\subseteq S
, let
J:=(f(I_{1}),g(I_{2}))\subseteq T
. Since
m_{T}
is a (possibly empty) bipartite matching of
T\setminus J
, the morphism
F
restricts to morphisms
F_{I}=(f|_{I_{1}},g|_{I_{2}},\varnothing,\varnothing)\in\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(I,J)
and
F_{S\setminus I}=(f|_{S_{1}\setminus I_{1}},g|_{S_{2}\setminus I_{2}},\varnothing,m_{T})\in\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(S\setminus I,T\setminus J)
. Note that
F_{I}
is a morphism in
\Hom_{\dwBr}(I,J)
and we have

i_{*}(A)(f,g,\varnothing,m_{T})=\bigoplus_{I\subseteq S}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,F_{S\setminus I})\otimes A(F_{I}).

(2)

Secondly, for
S^{\prime}=(\{s_{1}\},\{s_{2}\})\subseteq S
, let
S^{\prime\prime}:=S\setminus S^{\prime}
and let us consider a morphism of the form

F=(\mathrm{id}_{S_{1}^{\prime\prime}},\mathrm{id}_{S_{2}^{\prime\prime}},(s_{1},s_{2}),\varnothing)\in\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(S,S^{\prime\prime}).

Now consider
I\subseteq S
. Here we must consider four different cases:

(a)

First, suppose
S^{\prime}\subseteq I
. In this case, the morphism
F
restricts to the identity on
S\setminus I
and on
I
to the morphism
F^{\prime}:=(\mathrm{id}_{I_{1}\setminus\{s_{1}\}},\mathrm{id}_{I_{2}\setminus\{s_{2}\}},(s_{1},s_{2}),\varnothing)
, which is a morphism in
\Hom_{\dwBr}(I,I\setminus S^{\prime})
, so on the summand of
i_{*}(A)(S)
corresponding to
I
,
i_{*}(A)(F)
is given by

\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,\mathrm{id}_{S\setminus I})\otimes A(F^{\prime}).

(b)

Next, suppose
S^{\prime}\subset S\setminus I
. Similarly as in the previous case, the morphism
F
restricts to the identity on
I
an on
S\setminus I
to the morphism
F^{\prime}:=(\mathrm{id}_{S_{1}\setminus\{s_{1}\}},\mathrm{id}_{S_{2}\setminus\{s_{2}\}},(s_{1},s_{2}),\varnothing)
. On the summand of
i_{*}(A)(S)
corresponding to
I
,
i_{*}(A)(F)
is thus given by

\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,F^{\prime})\otimes A(\mathrm{id}_{I}).

In this case, we can note that since
S^{\prime}\subset S\setminus I
, the morphism
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,F^{\prime})
is explicitly given by removing
(s_{1},s_{2})
from the matching of the morphism in
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)
and multiplying by
n
.

(c)

Next, suppose
s_{1}\in I_{1}
and
s_{2}\in S_{2}\setminus I_{2}
. This case is a bit more complicated and we cannot use the functoriality of the tensor factors of each summand directly. Instead, we describe the morphism for each basis element of
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)
separately. For
m_{S\setminus I}\in\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)
, let
s_{1}^{\prime}\in S_{1}\setminus I_{1}
be such that
(s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2})\in m_{S\setminus I}
. We define
I^{\prime}=(I_{1}^{\prime},I_{2}^{\prime})\subseteq S
by
I_{1}^{\prime}:=(I_{1}\setminus\{s_{1}\})\sqcup\{s_{1}^{\prime}\}
and
I_{2}^{\prime}:=I_{2}
. We let
F^{\prime}=(f^{\prime},\mathrm{id}_{I_{2}},\varnothing,\varnothing):I\to I^{\prime}
be the morphism where

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(s)=\begin{cases}s_{1}^{\prime}&\text{ if }s=s_{1},\\
s&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Similarly, we have a linear map
T:\mathbb{Q}\{m_{S\setminus I}\}\to\mathbb{Q}\{m_{S^{\prime\prime}\setminus I^{\prime}}\}
given by
m_{S\setminus I}\mapsto m_{S\setminus I}\setminus(s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2})
. On the summand
\mathbb{Q}\{m_{S\setminus I}\}\tensoredover A(I)
the morphism
i_{*}(A)(F)
is then given by

i_{*}(A)(F)=T\otimes A(F^{\prime})

(d)

Finally, the case
s_{1}\in S_{1}\setminus I_{1}
and
s_{2}\in I_{2}
is determined in the same way as the previous case.

Any morphism in
\wBr_{n}
is a composition of a number of morphisms of the forms (1) and (2), so this completely describes the functor.

Example 3.8
.

Now let us apply this description to an explicit example, which will be of particular interest. For
n\geq 2
, we define a functor
K:\wBr_{n}\to\Rep(G)
, given on objects by
K(S)=K_{S}(n)
and for
(f,g,m_{S},m_{T}):S\to T
,
K(f,g,m_{S},m_{T})
is the composition

\displaystyle K_{S}(n)\xrightarrow{\lambda_{m_{S}}}K_{S^{\prime}}(n)\xrightarrow{f\otimes g}K_{T^{\prime}}(n)\xrightarrow{\omega_{m_{T}}}K_{T}(n),

where we by abuse of notation also use
f
and
g
to denote the maps given by permuting the tensor factors according to these bijections.

We also define a functor
K^{\circ}:\dwBr\to\Rep(G)
which on objects is given by
K(S)=K_{S}^{\circ}(n)
and on morphisms is defined just as
K
. We then have the following proposition, which is essentially a direct consequence of Proposition
2.7
:

Proposition 3.9
.

There is a natural transformation
\psi:i_{*}K^{\circ}\to K
, of functors
\wBr_{n}\to\Rep(G)
, where
\psi_{S}
is surjective for all
S\in\wBr_{n}
and an isomorphism if
n\geq|S|
.

Proof.

First, we note that for
S\in\wBr_{n}
and any
I\subseteq S
, we can define a
G
-equivariant map
\omega_{(-)}(1):\Hom(\varnothing,S\setminus I)\to K(S\setminus I)^{G}
by
m\mapsto\omega_{m}(1)
. Using (
14
) for
i_{*}K^{\circ}
, we can thus for any
S\in\wBr_{n}
define a
G
-equivariant map

\psi_{S}:\bigoplus_{I\subseteq S}\omega_{(-)}(1)\otimes\mathrm{inc}_{I}:i_{*}(K^{\circ})(S)\to K(S),

where
\mathrm{inc}_{I}:K^{\circ}(I)\to K(I)
denotes the inclusion, for any
I\subseteq S
. Using the above description of the functor
i_{*}K^{\circ}
on morphisms, it is a straightforward verification that the maps
\psi_{S}
define a natural transformation. For the morphisms in (2b), one uses the fact that
\lambda(\omega(1))=n
and for (2c) and (2d) one uses Lemma
2.8
. It follows by Proposition
2.7
that
\psi_{S}
is surjective for any
S
and an isomorphism as long as
n\geq|S|
.
∎

By dualizing the values on objects and on morphisms, we also get a functor
K^{\vee}:\wBr_{n}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\Rep(G)
. Our main results will be proven using the following proposition, which is analogous to
[
KRW20
, Proposition 2.16]
. In what follows,
i^{*}:\mathcal{A}^{\wBr_{n}}\to\mathcal{A}^{\dwBr}
denotes the restriction functor, i.e. the functor given by precomposing a functor with the inclusion functor
i
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
A\in(\mathbb{Q}\text{-}\mathrm{mod})^{\dwBr}
be a functor of finite length, and
B
be a representation of
G
(not necessarily finite dimensional). Given a natural isomorphism

\phi^{\wBr_{n}}:i_{*}(A)\to[K\otimes B]^{G},

of functors in
(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{\wBr_{n}}
, there is an induced map

\phi:i^{*}(K^{\vee})\otimes^{\dwBr}(1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover A)\to B,

of
G
-representations, which is an isomorphism onto the maximal algebraic subrepresentation of
B
. Furthermore, for any bipartition
(\lambda,\mu)
of
(p,q)
and for
n\gg p+q
, the multiplicity of the irreducible
G
-representation
V_{\lambda,\mu}
in
B
is the same as that of the irreducible
\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}
-representation
S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu}
in
A(p,q)
.

Proof.

First, let us explain why we do not need to add any assumption on the dimension of the representation
B
, following
[
KRW23
]
. For any
S\in\wBr_{n}
, we have
[K(S)\otimes B]^{G}\cong\Hom_{G}(K(S)^{\vee},B)
and since
K(S)^{\vee}
is algebraic, any map in
\Hom_{G}(K(S)^{\vee},B)
factors through the inclusion
B^{\textrm{alg}}\subseteq B
. Thus the natural transformation

\phi^{\wBr_{n}}:i_{*}(A)\to[K\otimes B]^{G}
factors through the functor
[K\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}
. Since the inclusion map
[K(S)\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}\to[K(S)\otimes B]^{G}
is injective, for any
S\in\wBr_{n}
and
\phi^{\wBr_{n}}
is assumed to be a natural isomorphism, the natural transformation
i_{*}(A)\to[K\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}
, through which it factors, is thus necessarily an isomorphism as well. For the rest of the proof, we may thus replace
B
by
B^{\textrm{alg}}
and we will by abuse of notation denote the map
i_{*}(A)\to[K\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}
by
\phi^{\wBr_{n}}
as well.

Next, we note that since
A
is assumed to have finite length, the assumption that
\phi^{\wBr_{n}}
is an isomorphism implies that
[K(S)\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}\cong\Hom_{G}(K(S)^{\vee},B^{\textrm{alg}})
is finite dimensional for every
S\in\wBr_{n}
. Furthermore, since
A
is of finite length, we have that there exists some
N\geq 0
such that
[K(S)\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}=0
for all
S
with
|S|>N
. Since every irreducible algebraic
G
-representation
V_{\lambda,\mu}
appears in
K(S)^{\vee}
for some choice of
S
, it follows that
\Hom_{G}(V_{\lambda,\mu},B^{\textrm{alg}})
is also finite dimensional for every bipartition
(\lambda,\mu)
, and zero if
|\lambda|+|\mu|>N
. For each bipartition
(\lambda,\mu)
we have an evaluation map
V_{\lambda,\mu}\otimes\Hom_{G}(V_{\lambda,\mu},B^{\textrm{alg}})\to B^{\textrm{alg}}
, and by adding these together we get a surjective map

\bigoplus_{(\lambda,\mu)}V_{\lambda,\mu}\otimes\Hom_{G}(V_{\lambda,\mu},B^{\textrm{alg}})\to B^{\textrm{alg}}.

Since
V_{\lambda,\mu}
is non-zero only if
l(\lambda,\nu)\leq n
, and
\Hom_{G}(V_{\lambda,\mu},B^{\textrm{alg}})
is only nonzero for
|\lambda|+|\mu|\leq N
, it follows that there is only a finite number of non-zero summands in this direct sum, and so the domain of this map is finite dimensional as well. Thus
B^{\textrm{alg}}
is finite dimensional.

Now, let us move on to prove the first statement of the proposition. This proof is essentially the same as that of
[
KRW20
, Proposition 2.16]
, but we include the details for completeness. We start by showing the existence of the map
\phi
. By the adjunction between the functors
i_{*}
and
i^{*}
, the natural transformation
\phi^{\wBr_{n}}
defines a natural transformation

\phi^{\dwBr}:A\to i^{*}[K\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}=[i^{*}(K\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}})]^{G}.

However, the functor
[-]^{G}:\Rep(G)^{f}\to(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{f}
is the same as
\Hom_{\Rep(G)}(1_{\Rep(G)},-)
, which by Remark
3.2
is right adjoint to the functor

1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover-:(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{f}\to\Rep(G)^{f},

giving us a new natural transformation

1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover A\to i^{*}(K\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}})

For any
S\in\dwBr
, we thus get a
G
-equivariant map

(1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover A)(S)\to i^{*}K(S)\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}

and thus by adjunction a
G
-equivariant map

i^{*}K^{\vee}(S)\otimes(1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover A)(S)\to B^{\textrm{alg}}.

This induces the
G
-equivariant map

\phi:i^{*}K^{\vee}\otimes^{\dwBr}(1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover A)\to B^{\textrm{alg}}.

To prove that this is an isomorphism, we will use Lemma
3.5
. In this setting, we note that
\Delta:\Rep(G)\to(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{\dwBr}
is the functor
[i^{*}K\otimes-]^{G}
, so we will start by proving that

\Delta(\phi):[i^{*}K\otimes(i^{*}(K^{\vee})\otimes^{\dwBr}(1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover A))]^{G}\to[i^{*}K\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}

is a natural isomorphism, by using the assumption that
\phi^{\wBr_{n}}
is. In order to do this, will show that for any
S\in\wBr_{n}
, the map
\phi^{\wBr_{n}}_{S}
factors as a surjection followed by the map
\Delta(\phi)
.

We start by unraveling the definition of
\Delta(\phi)
. For any
S\in\dwBr
, we can write the source of this natural transformation evaluated at
S
as

\left[\int^{T\in\dwBr}K(S)\otimes K(T)^{\vee}\tensoredover A(T)\right]^{G}

and since
[-]^{G}
is exact, this is the same as

\int^{T\in\dwBr}[K(S)\otimes K(T)^{\vee}]^{G}\otimes A(T).

Noting that a basis morphism
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(S,T)
is given by a bipartite matching of
(S_{1}\sqcup T_{2},S_{2}\sqcup T_{1})
, Proposition
2.10
tells us that there is a surjection

\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(S,T)\twoheadrightarrow[K(S)\otimes K(T)^{\vee}]^{G},

for any
S,T\in\wBr_{n}
. Furthermore, these are the components of a natural transformation of bifunctors
\wBr_{n}\times\wBr_{n}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
from
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(-,-)
to
[K\otimes K^{\vee}]^{G}
. Thus we have a surjection

\displaystyle\kappa:i_{*}(A)(S)=\int^{T\in\dwBr}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(S,T)\otimes A(T)\to\int^{T\in\dwBr_{n}}[K(S)\otimes K(T)^{\vee}]^{G}\otimes A(T).

Unraveling the adjunctions used to define
\phi
shows that
\phi^{\wBr_{n}}_{S}
is precisely the composition

i_{*}(A)(S)\overset{\kappa}{\longrightarrow}\int^{T\in\dwBr}[K(S)\otimes K(T)^{\vee}]^{G}\otimes A(T)\overset{\Delta(\phi)}{\longrightarrow}[i^{*}K(S)\otimes B^{\textrm{alg}}]^{G}.

Since this composition is an isomorphism by assumption, it follows that
\kappa
must be injective and thus an isomorphism. If this is the case, so must
\Delta(\phi)
.

Since
\Delta(\phi)
is an isomorphism, it follows by Lemma
3.5
that both the kernel and cokernel of
\phi
are in
\Rep(G)_{K}^{\circ}
. By definition, this is the category of representations
V
such for any
S\in\dwBr
, we have
[V\otimes K(S)]^{G}=\Hom_{\Rep(G)}(1_{\Rep(G)},V\otimes K(S))=0
. If
V
contains an algebraic subrepresentation, it follows by Proposition
2.10
and the fact that every irreducible algebraic representation of
G
is a summand of
K(S)
, for some
S\in\dwBr
, that
V\in\Rep(G)_{K}^{\circ}
precisely when it has no algebraic subrepresentation. The kernel of
\phi
is a subrepresentation of
i^{*}(K^{\vee})\otimes^{\dwBr}(1_{\Rep(G)}\tensoredover A)
, which is algebraic, since it is a quotient of a direct sum of representations of the form
K(S)\otimes A(S)
, where
K(S)
is algebraic and
A(S)
is trivial. Since
\ker(\phi)\in\Rep(G)_{K}^{\circ}
, it thus follows that it must be trivial, so
\phi
is injective. Since the image of
\phi
is also an algebraic representation and the cokernel lies in
\Rep(G)_{K}^{\circ}
, meaning that every algebraic subrepresentation of
B
is in the image of
\phi
, it follows that
\mathrm{im}(\phi)=B^{\textrm{alg}}
.

To prove the final part of the proposition, we use that by Proposition
3.9
, we have a natural transformation
\psi:i_{*}K^{\circ}\to K
, such that
\psi_{S}
is surjective and is an isomorphism for

. Since
[-]^{G}
is exact, it follows that we get a natural transformation

such that
\psi^{\prime}_{S}
is also surjective and an isomorphism for

. If
S
is such that

we thus have an isomorphism

\phi^{\wBr_{n}}_{S}:i_{*}(A)(S)\to i_{*}[K^{\circ}(S)\otimes B]^{G}
, and thus an isomorphism
A(S)\to[K^{\circ}(S)\otimes B]^{G}
. For
S=([p],[q])
, with

, we thus have

as
\Sigma_{p}\otimes\Sigma_{q}
-representations and so it follows by Theorem

that

(15)

\displaystyle A(p,q)\cong\bigoplus_{|(\lambda,\mu)|=(p,q)}S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu}\otimes[V_{\lambda,\mu}\otimes B]^{G}.

Applying the functor
\Hom_{\Sigma_{p}\otimes\Sigma_{q}}(S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu},-)
to both sides now implies that

\Hom_{\Sigma_{p}\otimes\Sigma_{q}}(S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu},A(p,q))\cong[V_{\lambda,\mu}\otimes B]^{G}\cong\Hom_{G}(V_{\lambda,\mu}^{\vee},B)\cong\Hom_{G}(V_{\mu,\lambda},B)

Since the dimension of the first term is the multiplicity of

in
A(p,q)
and the dimension of the final term is the multiplicity of

in
B
.
∎

We also have the following proposition, analogous to

For

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[K(S)\otimes K^{\vee}(S)\right]^{G}\otimes A(S)}

Stable cohomology with twisted coefficients

The main input for the proof of Theorem 1 is the stable cohomology of

with coefficients in the representations

]
. In order to state the result, let us define the following graded representations of

Definition 4.1
.

For
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
a pair of finite sets, we make the following definitions:

•

Let

be the graded vector space, concentrated in degree

|S_{2}|

|S_{2}|
of the parts labeled by different elements of
S_{2}
and the remaining parts are unlabeled (we can think of these as being labeled by zero). The
\Sigma_{S_{1}}\times\Sigma_{S_{2}}
-action is given by permuting elements and labels. If
\{P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{l}\}
is a partition of

for the labeled partition where

is labeled by

, which is either zero or an element of
S_{2}
.

•

Let
\det(S):=\det(\mathbb{Q}^{S_{1}})\otimes\det(\mathbb{Q}^{S_{2}})
, where
\det(\mathbb{Q}^{S_{i}}):=\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}S_{i}}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}S_{i}}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}S_{i}}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}S_{i}}\kern-0.5pt}}\mathbb{Q}^{S_{i}}

With this definition we can state the main theorem of
[

]
as follows:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let

. In degrees

, we have

(16)

\mathcal{P}(p,q)\otimes\det(p,q)\cong H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{p,q}(n))

as

We describe the isomorphism explicitly in the following sections. After this, we show how both source and target naturally define the components of functors

and how the isomorphism in Theorem

defines the components of a natural isomorphism between these functors.

4.1.
Twisted cohomology classes

To explicitly describe the isomorphism (
16
), we start by recalling the definition of certain cohomology classes introduced by Kawazumi in
[
Kaw05
]
.

If we let
A_{n}^{2}:=\Aut(F_{n})\ltimes F_{n}
, we have a crossed group homomorphism
k_{0}:A_{n}^{2}\to H(n)
given by
(\phi,\gamma)\mapsto[\gamma]
, which thus defines a cohomology class
[k_{0}]\in H^{1}(A_{n}^{2},H(n))
. For any
p\geq 1
, the short exact sequence

1\to F_{n}\to A_{n}^{2}\to\Aut(F_{n})\to 1

gives us a Gysin map
\pi_{!}:H^{p}(A_{n}^{2},H(n)^{\otimes p})\to H^{p-1}(\Aut(F_{n}),H(n)^{\otimes p}\otimes H^{\vee}(n))
, so for each
p\geq 1
we can define a class

h_{p,1}:=\pi_{!}([k_{0}]^{\otimes p})\in H^{p-1}(\Aut(F_{n}),H(n)^{\otimes p}\otimes H^{\vee}(n)).

Here we note that
h_{1,1}=[\mathrm{id}_{H(n)}]\in H^{0}(\Aut(F_{n}),\Hom_{\mathbb{Q}}(H(n),H(n)))
.

Furthermore, with
\lambda_{1,1}:H(n)^{\otimes(p+1)}\otimes H^{\vee}(n)\to H(n)^{\otimes p}
being the contraction map, we define for each
p\geq 0
a class

h_{p,0}:=(\lambda_{1,1})_{*}(h_{p+1,1})\in H^{p}(\Aut(F_{n}),H(n)^{\otimes p}).

We can note that since
h_{1,1}=[\mathrm{id}_{H(n)}]
, it follows that
h_{0,0}=n\in H^{0}(\Aut(F_{n}),\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Q}
.

Remark 4.3
.

In
[
Kaw05
]
, the class
h_{p,1}
is denoted
h_{p-1}
and the class
h_{p,0}
is denoted by
\bar{h}_{p}
. In Kawazumi’s notation, the index corresponds to the cohomological degree, whereas in our case,
h_{p,q}\in H^{p-q}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{p,q}(n))
, for
p-q\geq 0
and
q=0,1
. We use this different notation since it will make our indexing easier going forward.

4.2.
The wheeled PROP-structure

Kawazumi and Vespa
[
KV21
]
recently showed that the stable cohomology groups of
\Aut(F_{n})
with the coefficients
K_{p,q}(n)
assemble to something called a
wheeled PROP
. The definition of this structure is a bit involved, so we refer the reader to
[
MMS09
]
for details and only give a partial definition.

Definition 4.4
.

A wheeled PROP
\mathcal{C}
in
\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})
is a collection
\{\mathcal{C}(p,q)\}_{p,q\geq 0}
of graded vector spaces, with a commuting left
\Sigma_{p}
-action and right
\Sigma_{q}
-action, together with equivariant structure maps

(1)

\otimes:\mathcal{C}(p_{1},q_{1})\otimes\mathcal{C}(p_{2},q_{2})\to\mathcal{C}(p_{1}+p_{2},q_{1}+q_{2})
, called
horizontal composition
,

(2)

\circ:\mathcal{C}(p,q)\otimes\mathcal{C}(q,r)\to\mathcal{C}(p,r)
called
vertical composition
and

(3)

for
p,q\geq 1
and each
1\leq i\leq p
,
1\leq j\leq q
a map
\xi_{i,j}:\mathcal{C}(p,q)\to\mathcal{C}(p-1,q-1)
called
contraction
,

together with a number of compatibility conditions, detailed in
[
MMS09
]
. We refer to the space
\mathcal{C}(p,q)
as the
space of operations of

biarity

(p,q)
.

Remark 4.5
.

One of the compatibility conditions of wheeled PROPs is that the vertical composition maps are in fact determined by the horizontal composition maps and the contraction maps, in the sense that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}(p,q)\otimes\mathcal{C}(q,r)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}(p+q,q+r)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}(p,r)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\otimes}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\circ}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\xi_{1,p+1}^{\circ q}}

is commutative, where
\xi^{\circ q}_{p+1,1}
denotes the
q
-fold composition.

The collection
\{K_{p,q}(n)\}_{p,q\geq 0}
naturally forms a wheeled PROP, with the composition maps induced by the literal composition maps on the spaces
\Hom_{\mathbb{Q}}(H(n)^{\otimes q},H(n)^{\otimes p})
and the contraction maps induced by the duality pairing
\lambda:H(n)\otimes H^{\vee}(n)\to\mathbb{Q}
. By using the cup product, this induces corresponding maps on the collection
\{H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{p,q}(n))\}_{p,q\geq 0}
. If we let

, it was proven by Kawazumi and Vespa in
[

]
that this makes the collection

into a wheeled PROP.

In

]

((f^{\prime})^{-1}\times(g^{\prime})^{-1})(P)=P_{\lambda_{P},k_{P}}
, it follows that
(f^{\prime}\times g^{\prime})\otimes(f^{-1}\times g^{-1}):[p]\times[q]\to[p]\times[q]
preserves the labeled partition
P_{\lambda_{P},k_{P}}
. The subgroup of
\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}
preserving
P_{\lambda_{P},k_{P}}
is generated by permutations of the individual parts
Q_{i}
of this partition, permutations of different unlabeled parts of the same size and permutations of labeled parts of the same size, together with a corresponding permutation of the labels. A permutation
\sigma
of
Q_{i}=\{p_{1}+\cdots+p_{i-1}+1,\cdots,p_{1}+\cdots p_{i}\}
acts on
h_{p_{i},k_{i}^{\prime}}
by permuting the factors of
[k_{0}]^{\otimes p_{i}}
and since
[k_{0}]
is of degree one, this has the effect of changing the sign by
\textrm{sgn}(\sigma)
. But this is also the effect on
\det(p)
, so
\sigma
acts trivially on
\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})
.

The group of permutations of the set
\{Q_{i}\in P_{\lambda_{P},k_{P}}\mid|Q_{i}|=j,\text{ }Q_{i}\text{ unlabeled}\}
is generated by transpositions of pairs of unlabeled parts. Such a permutation
\sigma
acts on the first factor of
\mathcal{K}(p,q)\otimes\det(p,q)
by transposing the first and last factor in a product of the form
h_{j,0}ch_{j,0}
, where
c
is some cohomology class, i.e. by the sign
(-1)^{(|c|+j)j+|c|j}=(-1)^{j^{2}}=(-1)^{j}
. On
\det(p,q)
it acts by the sign of
j
transpositions, i.e. by
(-1)^{j}
. Thus
\sigma
acts trivially on
\kappa(\lambda_{p},k_{P})
.

Similarly, the subgroup of
\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}
which permutes labeled parts of the same size and their labels accordingly is generated by transpositions of labeled parts. Such a product
\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2}
of permutations acts on the first factor of
\mathcal{K}(p,q)\otimes\det(p,q)
by transposing the first and last factor in a product
h_{j,1}ch_{j,1}
and thus acts by the sign
(-1)^{(j-1)(|c|+j-1)+(j-1)|c|}

=(-1)^{(j-1)^{2}}=(-1)^{j-1}
. Since
\sigma_{1}
consists of
j
transpositions and
\sigma_{2}
by one transposition,
\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2}
acts on
\det(p,q)
by
(-1)^{j+1}
, so it acts trivially on
\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})
.

The map is equivariant essentially by construction, but let us show it for completeness. For brevity, let us for any
k
write
v_{k}:=e_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{k}
for the generator in
\det(\mathbb{Q}^{k})
. Let
\sigma_{1}\in\Sigma_{S_{1}}
and
\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma_{2}
. If
f,g
are as in the construction of
\kappa_{S}
, it follows that
(f\circ\sigma_{1}^{-1})\times(g\circ\sigma_{2}^{-1})
sends the labeled partition
(\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2})\cdot P
to
P_{\lambda_{P},k_{P}}
. We thus have

\displaystyle\kappa_{S}((\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2})\cdot P)

\displaystyle=\left(f\circ\sigma_{1}^{-1})^{-1}\times(g\circ\sigma_{2}^{-1})^{-1}\right)_{*}(\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})\otimes v_{p}\otimes v_{q})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{1}\circ f^{-1})\times(\sigma_{2}\circ g^{-1})_{*}(\kappa_{\lambda_{P},k_{P}}\otimes v_{p}\otimes v_{q})

\displaystyle=\left((\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2})\circ(f^{-1}\times g^{-1})\right)_{*}(\kappa_{\lambda_{P},\kappa_{P}}\otimes v_{p}\otimes v_{q})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2})_{*}\cdot\kappa_{S}(P).

To show injectivity in the stable range, we start by noting that choosing any pair of bijections
f:S_{1}\to[p]
and
g:S_{2}\to[q]
, gives us an isomorphism
H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{S}(n))\to\mathcal{H}(p,q)
and it is sufficient to show that the precomposition of this isomorphism with
\kappa_{S}
is injective. This composition lands in
\mathcal{K}(p,q)
by construction. Furthermore, every class of the form
\kappa(\lambda,k)
is in the image and these generate
\mathcal{K}(p,q)
as a
\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}
-representation. The composite map is
\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}
-equivariant, by considering the source as a representation via the isomorphism
\Sigma_{S_{1}}\times\Sigma_{S_{2}}\to\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}
induced by
f\times g
. Thus it follows that the map surjects onto
\mathcal{K}(p,q)
. However, a simple dimension counting argument using that the only relations in
\mathcal{K}
are (
17
) and (
18
) shows that

\dim(\mathcal{P}(S))=\dim(\mathcal{P}(p,q))\leq\dim(\mathcal{K}(p,q)),

so the map is injective.
∎

By adjunction, we get an equivariant map

\Phi_{S}:\mathcal{P}(S)\otimes\det(S)\to H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{S}(n)),

which is also injective in the stable range. Since these representations are abstractly isomorphic in a stable range, by Theorem
4.2
, we can conclude the following:

Corollary 4.9
.

For any pair
S=(S_{1},S_{2})
of finite sets, the map

\Phi_{S}:\mathcal{P}(S)\otimes\det(S)\to H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{S}(n))

is an isomorphism in the range
2*\leq n-|S_{1}|-|S_{2}|-3
.

4.3.
Functoriality

Our next goal is to understand
H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{S}(n))
as the components of a functor on the category
\wBr_{n}
. The functor
K:\wBr_{n}\to\Rep(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}))
, which was introduced in Section
3
, factors through
\Rep(\Aut(F_{n}))
, so let us by abuse of notation also write
K
for the functor to this category. By postcomposition with the functor
H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),-)
we thus get a functor

H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K):\wBr_{n}\to\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}).

Let us describe this functor in terms of
\mathcal{P}(S)\otimes\det(S)
.

We will define the functor
\mathcal{P}\otimes\det:\wBr_{n}\to\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
by first describing the tensor factors separately. We have already defined
\mathcal{P}(S)
for
S\in\wBr_{n}
, so let us describe what is does on morphisms:

(1)

A morphism of the form
(f,g,\varnothing,\varnothing)
, where
f:S_{1}\to T_{1}
and
g:S_{2}\to T_{2}
are bijections, is sent to the obvious map
\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathcal{P}(T)
given by permuting partitions and labels.

(2)

A morphism of the form
(\mathrm{id}_{S_{1}},\mathrm{id}_{S_{2}},\varnothing,(x,y)):(S_{1},S_{2})\to(S_{1}\sqcup\{x\},S_{2}\sqcup\{y\})
is sent to the map
\mathcal{P}(S_{1},S_{2}\{y\})\to\mathcal{P}(S_{1}\sqcup\{x\},S_{2}\sqcup\{y\})
which ads the labeled part
(x;y)
,

(3)

A morphism of the form
(\mathrm{id}_{S_{1}\setminus\{x\}},\mathrm{id}_{S_{2}\setminus\{y\}},(x,y),\varnothing):(S_{1},S_{2})\to(S_{1}\setminus\{x\},S_{2}\setminus\{y\})
is sent to the map
\mathcal{P}(S_{1},S_{2})\to\mathcal{P}(S_{1}\setminus\{x\},S_{2}\setminus\{y\})
defined as follows:

(a)

If
(\{P_{i},y_{i}\})
is a labeled partition such that
P_{i}=\{x\}
and
y_{i}=y
for some
i
, we remove this part and multiply the labeled partition by
n
.

(b)

If
(\{P_{i},y_{i}\})
is a labeled partition such that
x\in P_{i}
and
y_{i}=y
for some
i
, but
P_{i}\neq\{x\}
, we change this part to the unlabeled part
\{P_{i}\setminus\{x\},0\}
and

(c)

If
(\{P_{i},y_{i}\})
is a labeled partition such that
x\in P_{i}
and
y_{j}=y
, for
i\neq j
we merge the parts into the labeled part
\{(P_{i}\setminus\{x\})\sqcup P_{j},y_{i}\}
.

Now let us define the second tensor factor: we let
\det(S)=\det\left(\mathbb{Q}^{S_{1}}\right)\otimes\det\left(\mathbb{Q}^{S_{2}}\right)
as above and for a morphism
(f,g,m_{S},m_{T})
we define
\det(f,g,m_{S},m_{T})
as follows (cf.
[
KRW20
, Sections 3.5-3.6]
):

(1)

A morphism of the form
(f,g,\varnothing,\varnothing)
, where
f:S_{1}\to T_{1}
and
g:S_{2}\to T_{2}
are bijections, is send to the obvious map
\det(S)\to\det(T)
by permuting tensor factors.

(2)

A morphism of the form
(\mathrm{id}_{S_{1}},\mathrm{id}_{S_{2}},\varnothing,(x,y)):(S_{1},S_{2})\to(S_{1}\sqcup\{x\},S_{2}\sqcup\{y\})
is sent to the map
(-\wedge x)\otimes(-\wedge y)
.

(3)

A morphism of the form
(\mathrm{id}_{S_{1}\setminus\{x\}},\mathrm{id}_{S_{2}\setminus\{y\}},(x,y),\varnothing):(S_{1},S_{2})\to(S_{1}\setminus\{x\},S_{2}\setminus\{y\})
is sent to the inverse of the map described in the previous case.

Since any morphism in
\wBr_{n}
can be written as a composition of morphisms of the kinds considered above, this describes the functor
\mathcal{P}\otimes\det
completely.

Proposition 4.10
.

The maps
\Phi_{S}:\mathcal{P}(S)\otimes\det(S)\to H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{S}(n))
define the components of a natural transformation of functors in
(\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}))^{\wBr_{n}}
.

Proof.

We will show that the maps
\kappa_{S}:\mathcal{P}(S)\to H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K_{S}(n))\otimes\det(S)
define a natural transformation. The statement for
\Phi_{S}
then follows by adjunction.

It is sufficient to show that for any morphism
F\in\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(S,T)
of the three kinds described in the cases (1)-(3) above, we have a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}(S)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}(T)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K(S))\otimes\det(S)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K(T))\otimes\det(T)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{S}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{T}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{*}}

where we write
F_{*}
for the morphism given by functoriality, for brevity. For
F=(f,g,\varnothing,\varnothing)
, this is proven in the same way as the equivariance of the map
\Phi_{S}
.

Suppose
T=(S_{1}\sqcup\{x\},S_{2}\sqcup\{y\})
and
F=(\mathrm{id}_{S_{1}},\mathrm{id}_{S_{2}},\varnothing,(x,y))
, where
|S_{1}|=p
and
|S_{2}|=q
. For any
f:S_{1}\to[p]
and
g:S_{2}\to[q]
, we can extend these to
\bar{f}:S_{1}\sqcup\{x\}\to[p+1]
and
\bar{g}:S_{2}\sqcup\{y\}\to[q+1]
by sending
x\mapsto p+1
and
y\mapsto q+1
. As before, let us for brevity write
v_{k}:=e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{k}
for the generator of
\det(\mathbb{Q}^{k})
, for any
k\geq 0
. If
P\in\mathcal{P}(S)
, let
f\times g:S_{1}\times S_{2}\to[p]\times[q]
be bijections sending
P
to a standard labeled partition. Then

\displaystyle F_{*}(\kappa_{S}(P))

\displaystyle=F_{*}((f^{-1}\times g^{-1})_{*}(\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})\otimes v_{p}\otimes v_{q}))

\displaystyle=\omega_{x,y}\left((f^{-1}\times g^{-1})_{*}\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})\right)\otimes(f^{-1}_{*}(v_{p})\wedge x\otimes g^{-1}_{*}(v_{q})\wedge y)

\displaystyle=(\bar{f}^{-1}\times\bar{g}^{-1})_{*}(\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})h_{1,1}\otimes v_{p+1}\otimes v_{q+1})

\displaystyle=(\bar{f}^{-1}\times\bar{g}^{-1})_{*}(\kappa(\lambda_{P\sqcup(x;y)},k_{P\sqcup(x;y)})\otimes v_{p+1}\otimes v_{q+1})

\displaystyle=\kappa_{T}((P\sqcup(x;y)))

\displaystyle=\kappa_{T}(F_{*}(P)).

Next suppose
F
is as in (3). If
f:S_{1}\to[p]
and
g:S_{2}\to[q]
are bijections, this once again induces in a unique way bijections
\bar{f}:S_{1}\setminus\{x\}\to[p-1]
and
\bar{g}:S_{2}\setminus\{y\}\to[q-1]
, by preserving the order of the elements in the image.

First suppose
P\in\mathcal{P}(S)
is a labeled partition as in (a). Let
f
and
g
be bijections as in the definition of
\kappa_{S}
. Using the first contraction relation in Proposition
4.6
we get

\displaystyle F_{*}(\kappa_{S}(P))

\displaystyle=F_{*}((f^{-1}\times g^{-1})_{*}(\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})\otimes v_{p}\otimes v_{q}))

\displaystyle=\lambda_{x,y}\left((f^{-1}\times g^{-1})_{*}\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})\right)\otimes(\bar{f}^{-1}_{*}v_{p-1}\otimes\bar{g}^{-1}_{*}v_{q-1})

\displaystyle=(\bar{f}^{-1}\times\bar{g}^{-1})_{*}(\lambda_{f(x),g(y)}\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P}))\otimes(\bar{f}^{-1}_{*}v_{p-1}\otimes\bar{g}^{-1}_{*}v_{q-1})

\displaystyle=(\bar{f}^{-1}\times\bar{g}^{-1})_{*}(n\cdot\kappa(\lambda_{P\setminus(x;y)},k_{P\setminus(x;y)})\otimes(\bar{f}^{-1}_{*}v_{p-1}\otimes\bar{g}^{-1}_{*}v_{q-1})

\displaystyle=\kappa_{S}(n\cdot P\setminus(x;y))

\displaystyle=\kappa_{S}(F_{*}(P)).

The case (b) is almost by an almost identical calculation using the same contraction relation, whereas the case (c) follows from the second contraction relation in Proposition
4.6
by a similar calculation.

∎

Next, we define a functor
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}:\dwBr\to\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
by letting
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(S)\subset\mathcal{P}(S)
be generated by the set of labeled partitions in
\mathcal{P}(S)
with no labeled parts of size one. On morphisms, the functor is defined as
\mathcal{P}
. These two functors are related as follows:

Proposition 4.11
.

There is a natural isomorphism
i_{*}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\to\mathcal{P}
of functors in
(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{\wBr_{n}}
.

Proof.

We will define the components of this natural transformation by defining it on each summand of the decomposition of
i_{*}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
from (
14
), so recall that this states that

i_{*}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})(S)\cong\bigoplus_{I\subseteq S}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)\otimes\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(I).

For any
S\in\wBr_{n}
and any
I\subseteq S
, there is a linear map
\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)\to\mathcal{P}(S\setminus I)
, which in the case where
|S_{1}\setminus I_{1}|\neq|S_{2}\setminus I_{2}|
is just the zero map, since the source is zero in this case, and otherwise is given by

\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},y_{k})\}\mapsto\{(x_{1};y_{1}),(x_{2};y_{2}),\ldots,(x_{k};y_{k})\}.

There is also a linear map
\mathcal{P}(S\setminus I)\otimes\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(I)\to\mathcal{P}(S)
, given by merging labeled partitions. We thus get a composite map

i_{*}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})(S)=\bigoplus_{I\subseteq S}\Hom_{\wBr_{n}}(\varnothing,S\setminus I)\otimes\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(I)\to\bigoplus_{I\subseteq S}\mathcal{P}(S\setminus I)\otimes\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(I)\to\mathcal{P}(S).

Since the left Kan extension precisely adds all those labeled partitions with some labeled parts of size one, this is an isomorphism.

Showing that this is indeed a natural transformation is a straightforward verification, done by comparing the description of the functor
i_{*}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
on the “generating” morphisms in
\wBr_{n}
in the general description in Section
3.2.1
and the similar description in the definition of
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

4.4.
Multiplicativity

The functor
H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K)
is lax symmetric monoidal (i.e. a commutative ring object in the category
(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})^{\wBr_{n}}
), with the monoidality given by the cup product

H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K(S))\otimes H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K(T))\to H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K(S\sqcup T)),

for
S,T\in\wBr_{n}
. The functor
\mathcal{P}\otimes\det:\wBr_{n}\to\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
is also lax symmetric monoidal, with the monoidality

(\mathcal{P}(S)\otimes\det(S))\otimes(\mathcal{P}(T)\otimes\det(T))\to\mathcal{P}(S\sqcup T)\otimes\det(S\sqcup T)

given by disjoint union of labeled partitions and exterior product. This defines a lax symmetric monoidality for the functor
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det:\dwBr\to\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod}
in the same way.

Proposition 4.12
.

The natural isomorphism

\Phi:\mathcal{P}\otimes\det\to H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K)

is compatible with the monoidalities of these functors.

Proof.

Once again, it is sufficient, and somewhat simpler, to verify this statement for the natural isomorphism
\kappa:\mathcal{P}\to H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K)\otimes\det
. Let
S,T\in\wBr_{n}
be such that
|S_{1}|=p_{1}
,
|S_{2}|=p_{2}
,
|T_{1}|=q_{1}
,
|T_{2}|=q_{2}
. For
P\in\mathcal{P}(S)
and
Q\in\mathcal{P}(T)
, let
f_{i}:S_{i}\to[p_{i}]
and
g_{i}:T_{i}\to[q_{i}]
be bijections such that
f_{1}\times f_{2}
sends
P
to a standard labeled partition
P_{\lambda_{P},k_{P}}\in\mathcal{P}([p_{1}],[p_{2}])
, and
g_{1}\times g_{2}
sends
Q
to a standard labeled partition
P_{\lambda_{Q},k_{Q}}\in\mathcal{P}([q_{1}],[q_{2}])
. Let us write
\lambda_{P}=(\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{l})
,
k_{P}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{l})
,
\lambda_{Q}=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{\prime}_{r})
and
k_{Q}=(k_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,k_{r}^{\prime})
.

Now let
\bar{g}_{i}:T_{i}\to\{p_{i}+1,p_{i}+2,\ldots,p_{i}+q_{i}\}
be defined by
\bar{g}_{i}(t)=g_{i}(t)+p_{i}
. This means that
(f_{1}\sqcup\bar{g}_{1})\times(f_{2}\sqcup\bar{g}_{2})
sends

to a labeled partition
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}([p_{1}+q_{1}],[p_{2}+q_{2}])
. There then exist permutations
\sigma_{1}\in\Sigma_{p_{1}+q_{1}}
and
\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma_{p_{2}+q_{2}}
such that
P^{\prime\prime}:=(\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2})\cdot P^{\prime}
is a standard labeled partition.

Let
\sigma\in\Sigma_{l+r}
be a permutation such that
\sigma(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l},\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\lambda_{r}^{\prime})
is a partition (i.e. weakly decreasing). Let us write
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
for the resulting partition and
k^{\prime\prime}:=\sigma(k_{1},\ldots,k_{l},k_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,k_{r}^{\prime})
. We then have
P^{\prime\prime}=P_{\lambda^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime}}
, so it follows that

\displaystyle\kappa_{S\sqcup T}(P\sqcup P^{\prime})

\displaystyle=((\sigma_{1}\circ(f_{1}\sqcup\bar{g}_{1}))^{-1}\times(\sigma_{2}\circ(f_{2}\sqcup\bar{g}_{2}))^{-1})_{*}(\kappa(\lambda^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})\otimes v_{p_{1}+q_{1}}\otimes v_{p_{2}+q_{2}})

\displaystyle=((\sigma_{1}\circ(f_{1}\sqcup\bar{g}_{1}))^{-1}\times(\sigma_{2}\circ(f_{2}\sqcup\bar{g}_{2}))^{-1})_{*}(h_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime}_{1}}\cdots h_{\lambda_{l+r}^{\prime\prime},k_{l+r}^{\prime\prime}}\otimes v_{p_{1}+q_{1}}\otimes v_{p_{2}+q_{2}})

\displaystyle=((f_{1}\sqcup\bar{g}_{1})^{-1}\times(f_{2}\sqcup\bar{g}_{2})^{-1})_{*}\big(\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})\kappa(\lambda_{P^{\prime}},k_{P^{\prime}})\otimes v_{p_{1}+q_{1}}\otimes v_{p_{2}+q_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle=(f_{1}^{-1}\times f_{2}^{-1})_{*}(\kappa(\lambda_{P},k_{P})\otimes v_{p_{1}}\otimes v_{p_{2}})(g_{1}^{-1}\times g_{2}^{-1})_{*}(\kappa(\lambda_{P^{\prime}},k_{P^{\prime}})\otimes v_{q_{1}}\otimes v_{q_{2}})

\displaystyle=\kappa_{S}(P)\kappa_{T}(P^{\prime}).

The fact that there is no sign change in the third step is explained by a similar argument as in the proof of Proposition
4.8
, as
\sigma_{1}^{-1}\times\sigma_{2}^{-1}
acts by permuting the classes

, the sign change of which is cancelled by the action on the sign representations.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem B

Having described the functor
H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),K)\cong\mathcal{P}\otimes\det:\wBr_{n}\to\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})
, we want to relate this functor to the functor
[H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\otimes K]^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})}:\wBr_{n}\to\Gr(\mathbb{Q}\text{-mod})
, in order to apply Proposition
3.10
. Similarly to in
[
KRW20
]
, this will be done using the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence associated to the short exact sequence defining
\IA_{n}
. However, this spectral sequence has already been analysed thoroughly in
[
HK23
]
, so we will recall their work and expand some of their results slightly, after which we reframe the results in the categorical perspective.

5.1.
The
\GL_{n}
-invariants of
H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})

In
[
HK23
, Section 6]
, Habiro and Katada define classes which we will denote by
y_{4k}\in H^{4k}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})}
for all
k\geq 1
, using what they call “anti-transgression maps”. Let us start by recalling the general definition of these maps.

Suppose that we have a first quadrant cohomological spactral sequence
E
such that for some
N>0
,
E_{\infty}^{p,q}=0
whenever
0<p+q<N
. Thus it follows that for
0\leq k<N
we have
E_{k+2}^{0,k}=E_{k+2}^{k+1,0}=0
, since there can be no non-zero differentials to or from these terms on the
E_{k+2}
-page. Thus the differential

d_{k+1}:E_{k+1}^{0,k}\to E_{k+1}^{k+1,0}

is an isomorphism, so we can define a linear map

\varphi_{k}:=\iota_{k}\circ(d_{k+1})^{-1}\circ\pi_{k+1}:E_{2}^{k+1,0}\to E_{2}^{0,k}

where
\pi_{k+1}:E_{2}^{k+1,0}\twoheadrightarrow E_{k+1}^{k+1,0}
and
\iota_{k}:E_{k+1}^{0,k}\hookrightarrow E_{2}^{0,k}
are the edge homomorphisms. We call
\varphi_{k}
an
anti-transgression
map.

We will apply this notion to the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence associated to the short exact sequence

(21)

1\to\IA_{n}\to\Aut(F_{n})\to\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\to 1.

Recall that for
n\gg*>0
, we have
H^{*}(\Aut(F_{n}),\mathbb{Q})=0
, so for
n\gg k
, we get a well-defined anti-transgression map

\varphi_{k}:H^{k+1}(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Q})\to H^{k}(\IA_{n})^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Since
H^{*}(\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathop{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigwedge$}^{\mspace{-2mu}*}\kern-0.5pt}}\{x_{4i+1}\mid i\geq 1\}
in a range

, where the generators are indexed by their degrees, we can define elements

We will start by proving a series of lemmas. The first is surely a standard result in homological algebra, but we include an elementary proof for completeness:

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose

is a map of cochain complexes such that for some

f^{i}:A^{i}\to V^{i}
is surjective and

is injective. Then

is surjective and
H(f)^{i+1}
is injective.

Proof.

First, we introduce the notation

for the differentials in the chain complexes, for all

(22)

\displaystyle f^{j}(\ker(d_{A}^{j}))\subseteq\ker(d_{B}^{j}),

(23)

\displaystyle f^{j}(\mathrm{im}(d_{A}^{j}))\subseteq\mathrm{im}(d_{B}^{j}),

for all
j
.

Let us start by proving the surjectivity of
H(f)^{i}
. Let
w\in\ker(d_{B}^{i})\subset B^{i}
and
v\in A^{i}
such that
f^{i}(v)=w
. Since
0=d_{B}^{i}(w)=d_{B}^{i}(f^{i}(v))=f^{i+1}(d_{A}^{i}(v))
and
f^{i+1}
is injective, we have
v\in\ker(d_{A}^{i})
. Thus the restriction of
f^{i}
to
\ker(f^{i})
is a surjection onto
\ker(d_{B}^{i})
. By (
23
) with
j=i
, it follows that
H(f)^{i}
is surjective.

Next, let us prove injectivity of
H(f)^{i+1}
. By (
22
) for
j=i+1
and the assumption, we have that the restriction of
f_{2}
to
\ker(d_{A}^{i+1})
is an injection into
\ker(d_{B}^{i+1})
. Suppose
v\in A^{i+1}
is such that

, i.e.
f^{i+1}(v)=d_{B}^{i}(w)
for some

. By surjectivity of

, we have some

such that

is injective, we have

, so

. Thus

is injective.
∎

denote the operadic suspension of the operad
\mathcal{C}om
of non-unital commutative algebras.
Thus, we have
\mathcal{P}_{0}(0)=0
and
\mathcal{P}_{0}(k)=\det(\mathbb{Q}^{k})[k-1]
in cohomological dimension
k-1
for
k\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\circlearrowright}
denote the wheeled completion of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\circlearrowright}}
the wheeled PROP freely generated by
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\circlearrowright}
, which was introduced by
[
KV21
]
.
Let
\mathcal{O}=\bigoplus_{k\geq 2}\mathcal{P}_{0}(k)
denote the non-unital suboperad of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
,
\mathcal{O}^{\circlearrowright}
the non-unital wheeled sub-operad of
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\circlearrowright}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}^{\circlearrowright}}
the non-unital wheeled sub-PROP of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\circlearrowright}}
.
By these constructions, the wheeled PROP
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\circlearrowright}}
corresponds to the
\wBr_{n}
-module
\mathcal{P}\otimes\det
and the non-unital wheeled PROP
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}^{\circlearrowright}}
corresponds to the
\dwBr
-module
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det
.

Lemma A.3

(
[
Kat22
, Proposition 12.3]
)
.

We have an isomorphism of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-representations

\displaystyle W_{i}(n)\cong\bigoplus_{p-q=i}K^{\circ}_{p,q}(n)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}[\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}]}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}^{\circlearrowright}}(p,q)

for sufficiently large
n
with respect to
i
.

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

By Proposition 6.3, we stably have an isomorphism

\displaystyle R\cong R_{\text{pres}},

of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-representations. By Remark 6.2, we have

\displaystyle R_{\text{pres}}\cong H^{*}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})/{\langle R_{2}\rangle},

where
\langle R_{2}\rangle
denotes the two-sided ideal of
H^{*}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})
generated by

R_{2}=\ker(H^{2}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})\cong{\bigwedge}^{2}H^{1}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\xrightarrow{\cup}H^{2}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})).

Since the Albanese cohomology of
\IA_{n}
is defined to be the image of the cup product map

\bigwedge^{*}H^{1}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q})\xrightarrow{\cup}H^{*}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q}),

we have a surjective map of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-representations

\displaystyle H^{*}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})/{\langle R_{2}\rangle}\twoheadrightarrow H^{*}_{A}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q}).

Therefore, we stably have a surjective map of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-representations

\displaystyle R\cong R_{\text{pres}}\cong H^{*}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})/{\langle R_{2}\rangle}\twoheadrightarrow H^{*}_{A}(\IA_{n},\mathbb{Q}).

We stably have

R_{i}\cong W_{i}(n)^{\vee}

by Lemma
A.3

since we stably have an isomorphism of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
-representations

\displaystyle\begin{split}R_{i}=(i^{*}(K^{\vee})\otimes^{\dwBr}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det))_{i}&\cong\bigoplus_{p-q=i}K^{\circ}_{q,p}(n)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}[\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}]}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\otimes\det)(p,q)\\
&\cong\bigoplus_{p-q=i}K^{\circ}_{q,p}(n)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}[\Sigma_{p}\times\Sigma_{q}]}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}^{\circlearrowright}}(p,q),\end{split}

where the first isomorphism is verified by Proposition 2.7.
Therefore, the statement follows from Theorem
A.2
.
