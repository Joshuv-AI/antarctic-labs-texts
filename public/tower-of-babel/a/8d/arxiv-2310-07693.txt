Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to give natural examples of 𝚺 1 1 \analytic -complete and 𝚷 1 1 \coanalytic -complete sets. In the first part, we consider ideals on ω \omega . In particular, we show that the Hindman ideal ℋ \mathcal{H} is 𝚷 1 1 \coanalytic -complete and consider a number of ideals generated in the similar fashion. Moreover, we show that the ideal 𝒟 \mathcal{D} is also 𝚷 1 1 \coanalytic -complete. In the second part, we focus on families of trees (on ω \omega and 2 2 ) containing a specific tree type. We show the connection between two topics and explore some classical tree types (like Sacks and Miller).

For this part assume that

Definition 1
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,

. We say that

to

satisfying

Definition 2
.

We say that

is
\analytic
-complete
if
A
is analytic and for every Polish space

every analytic set

is Borel reducible to

.

Fact 1.1
.

If an analytic set

is Borel reducible to
A

\analytic
-complete, then

is
\analytic
-complete.

Note that existence of analytic non-Borel sets and closure of Borel sets under Borel maps implies that all
\analytic
-complete sets are not Borel. Moreover, in order to apply
1.1
, we need an example of
\analytic
-complete set. Such an example can be found among trees over
\omega
.

Definition 3
.

A set
T\subseteq\seqN
is a
tree over
\omega
if

\left(\forall\sigma\in\seqN\right)\left(\forall\tau\in\seqN\right)\left(\sigma\in T\land\tau\subseteq\sigma\implies\tau\in T\right).

Set of all trees over
\omega
will be denoted by
\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}
. A
body
of a tree

[T]=\left\{\sigma\in\baire:\left(\forall n\in\omega\right)\left(\sigma\upharpoonright n\in T\right)\right\}.

Using above definition set

can be seen as a

subset of

. Therefore,

is a Polish space. By

let us denote the collection of all ill-founded trees over

, i.e. all trees with non-empty body. It occurs that

is an example of
\analytic
-complete set we were looking for (see e.g.

Definition 4
.

We say that
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
\coanalytic
-complete
if

is
\analytic
-complete.

In the first part of this paper we will consider ideals on natural numbers.

Definition 5
.

A family
\mathcal{I}\subseteq P(\omega)
is an

if
\mathcal{I}
is closed

•

under taking subsets, i.e

•

. A space

is naturally (via characteristic function

) homeomorphic to the Cantor space

. In this context, questions about Borel complexity, being

2.
Ideals on
\omega

In this section we will follow notation introduced in
[
4
]
. Let
\Lambda
and
\Omega
be countable infinite sets. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a nonempty family of infinite subsets of
\Omega
such that
F\backslash K\in\mathcal{F}
for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
and a finite set
K\subseteq\Omega
. For a function
\rho:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow[\Lambda]^{\omega}
we define a family

\mathcal{I}_{\rho}=\left\{A\subseteq\Lambda:\forall F\in\mathcal{F}(\rho(F)\nsubseteq A\right)\}.

Definition 6
.

Function
\rho:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow[\Lambda]^{\omega}
is
partition regular
if

•

(\forall E,F\in\mathcal{F})(E\subseteq F\implies\rho(E)\subseteq\rho(F))

•

{(\forall F\in\mathcal{F})(\forall A,B\in\Lambda)(\rho(F)=A\cup B}\implies\\
{(\exists E\in\mathcal{F})(\rho(E)\subseteq A\lor\rho(E)\subseteq B))}

•

\forall F\in\mathcal{F}\exists E\in\mathcal{F}(E\subseteq F\land\forall a\in\rho(E)\exists K\in[\Omega]^{<\omega}(a\notin\rho(E\backslash K)))

As proven in
[
4
, Proposition 3.3]
, if
\rho:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow[\Lambda]^{\omega}
is partition regular, then family
\mathcal{I}_{\rho}
is an ideal on
\Lambda
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{I}
is an ideal on
\Lambda
, the function
\rho_{\mathcal{I}}:\mathcal{I}^{+}\rightarrow[\Lambda]^{\omega}
given by

\rho_{\mathcal{I}}(B)=B

is partition regular and
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\mathcal{I}}}
, so every ideal is representable in this way.

We will also need an option to extend
\rho
on finite sets. Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a nonempty family of finite subsets of
\Omega
and there exists a function
\lambda:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\Lambda
satisfying condition

(1)

(\forall F\in\mathcal{F})(\forall a\in\rho(F))(\exists G\in\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{P}(F))(\lambda(G)=a).

Notice that it allows us to define
\rho^{\prime}:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)

\rho^{\prime}(B)=\left\{\lambda(G):G\subseteq B\land G\in\mathcal{G}\right\},

which is an extension of
\rho
on entire
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. In considered ideals existence of function
\lambda
(and the extension) will be clear, so we will identify
\rho
with this extension.

2.1.
Unified approach to reduction

In a few following subsections we will give examples of families, defined as above, which are
\coanalytic
-complete. In order to do that we construct reductions of
\textrm{IF}_{\omega}
. Since they are very similar to each other, it makes sense to define the reduction here and only later prove that they work for specific examples.

Fix function
\rho
as earlier. We would like to construct a Borel function
\varphi:\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
such that
\varphi^{-1}[\mathcal{I}_{\rho}^{c}]=\textrm{IF}_{\omega}
.
Let us first fix an injection
\alpha:\seqN\rightarrow\omega
satisfying

(2)

\sigma\subseteq\tau\Rightarrow\alpha(\sigma)\leq\alpha(\tau).

Now define

\varphi(T)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in T}\rho(\{\alpha(\sigma\upharpoonright k):\;\;k<|\sigma|\}).

From condition (
1
), if
\tau\in[T]
, then
\rho(\{\alpha(\tau\upharpoonright k):k\in\omega\})\subseteq\varphi(T)
, so image of an ill-founded tree is in
\mathcal{I}_{\rho}^{c}
. Therefore, the only thing left is to prove that if
\rho(B)\subseteq\varphi(T)
for some
B\in\mathcal{F}
, then
[T]\neq\emptyset
.

2.2.
Ramsey ideal

Definition 7
.

Define function
r:[\omega]^{\omega}\rightarrow[[\omega]^{2}]^{\omega}
by

r(B)=[B]^{2}.

Ideal
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{I}_{r}
is called the
Ramsey ideal
.

In
[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
the following result was already proven. However, we would like to give a proof based on the unified approach.

Theorem 2.1
.

Set
\mathcal{R}
is
\coanalytic
-complete.

Proof.

Suppose that
[B]^{2}\subseteq\varphi(T)
for some
B\in[\omega]^{\omega}
. Take any
a,b\in B
,
a<b
.
\{a,b\}\in\varphi(T)
, hence (from definition of
\varphi
), there is
\sigma\in T
such that
\alpha^{-1}(a)=\sigma\upharpoonright k
and
\alpha^{-1}(b)=\sigma\upharpoonright l
for some
k,l<|\sigma|
,
k\neq l
. Thus, because of condition (
2
),
\alpha^{-1}(a)\subset\alpha^{-1}(b)
and
\alpha^{-1}(a),\alpha^{-1}(b)\in T
. Using this argument we can find a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\omega}
of elements of
B
such that

i<j\implies\alpha^{-1}(a_{i})\subset\alpha^{-1}(a_{j})

and
\alpha^{-1}(a_{i})\in T
for every
i\in\omega
. That generates an infinite branch in tree
T
.
∎

2.3.
Hindman ideal

Definition 8
.

Define function
\textrm{FS}:[\omega]^{\omega}\rightarrow[\omega]^{\omega}
by

\textrm{FS}(B)=\left\{\sum_{n\in F}n:\;\;F\subseteq B\text{ is nonempty and finite}\right\}.

A set
A\subseteq\omega
is called an
IP-set
if there is an infinite
B\subseteq A
satisfying
\textrm{FS}(B)\subseteq A
.

Hindman ideal
is a family
\mathcal{H}
of all non-IP-sets, i.e.
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{FS}}
. As proven in
[
4
, Proposition 5.2.(5)]
, Hindman ideal is coanalytic. Below theorem is a strengthening of that result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Set
\mathcal{H}
is
\coanalytic
-complete.

Proof.

First, we need an additional condition on function
\alpha
, namely that
\alpha:\seqN\rightarrow\left\{2^{2n}:n\in\omega\right\}
.

Suppose that
\varphi(T)
is an IP-set, i.e.
\textrm{FS}(B)\subseteq\varphi(T)
for some
B\in\infomega
. Note that
B\subseteq\textrm{FS}(B)
and, because of the formula for
\varphi
,
\varphi(T)
contains only numbers, which in binary expansion have
1
at even positions. From now on we will identify natural numbers with their binary expansions. Take any
a_{0}\in B
and consider a set
B_{0}=B\setminus\{b\in B:b>a_{0}\}
. Take any
b_{0}\in B_{0}
. From above remark
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
have
1
s at even positions. Moreover, positions of
1
s in
a_{0}
are disjoint from positions of
1
s in
b_{0}
(otherwise
a_{0}+b_{0}\in\textrm{FS}(B)
has
1
at an odd position). Put
a_{1}=a_{0}+b_{0}
, consider a set
B_{1}=B\setminus\{b\in B:b>a_{1}\}
and take any
b_{1}\in B_{1}
. With the same argument as before,
b_{1}
and
a_{1}
have
1
s at disjoint positions. So we put
a_{2}=a_{1}+b_{1}
and go on with the construction.

As a result we obtain a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\omega}
of natural numbers satisfying

a_{n}\text{ has 1 at position $k$}\Rightarrow a_{n+1}\text{ has 1 at position $k$}

for every
n\in\omega
. This sequence induces a sequence
(2^{c_{n}})_{n\in\omega}
of increasing powers of
2
(by decomposing elements of
(a_{n})_{n\in\omega}
into sums of powers of
2
) contained in
\varphi(T)
. Now note that, by definition of
\varphi
and condition (
2
),

\alpha^{-1}(2^{c_{n}})\in T\;\land\;\alpha^{-1}(2^{c_{n}})\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(2^{c_{n+1}})

holds for every
n\in\omega
. Hence,
(\alpha^{-1}(2^{c_{n}}))_{n\in\omega}
is an increasing sequence of elements of
T
, so
[T]\neq\emptyset
.
∎

2.4.
Modifications of Hindman ideal

Definition 9
.

For
k\geq 2
define function
\textrm{FS}_{k}:[\omega]^{\omega}\rightarrow[\omega]^{\omega}
by

\textrm{FS}_{k}(B)=\left\{\sum_{n\in F}n:\;\;|F|=k\right\}.

A set
A\subseteq\omega
is called an
IP_{k}
-set
if there is an infinite
B\subseteq A
satisfying
\textrm{FS}_{k}(B)\subseteq A
.

From now on let us fix a natural number
k\geq 2
.

Analogously as before,
\mathcal{H}_{k}
will denote a family of all non-
\IP_{k}
-sets, i.e.
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{FS}_{k}}

Fact 2.3
.

Family
\mathcal{H}_{k}
forms an ideal.

Proof.

Closure under taking subsets is clear. Let
A,B\subseteq\omega
be non-
\IP_{k}
-sets (without loss of generality disjoint). Suppose
A\cup B
is an
\IP_{k}
-set, i.e. there is
C\in\infomega
such that
\textrm{FS}_{k}(C)\subseteq A\cup B
. Consider a function
c:[C]^{k}\rightarrow\{A,B\}
defined with formula

c(E)=\begin{cases}A,&\mbox{if }\sum_{n\in E}n\in A\\
B,&\mbox{if }\sum_{n\in E}n\in B\\
\end{cases}.

Using Ramsey’s Theorem, there is an infinite
D\subseteq C
such that all
k
-element subsets of
D
are "colored" by
c
with the same color, without loss of generality
A
. Thus,
\textrm{FS}_{k}(D)\subseteq A
, what gives us a contradiction.
∎

Obviously, every IP-set is
\IP_{k}
set, so
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is a subideal of
\mathcal{H}
. This way we obtain an infinite collection of ideals over
\omega
. We shall first focus on the structure of this collection.

Fact 2.4
.

Let
n,m\in\omega
. Then

n\mid m\iff\mathcal{H}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{m}.

Proof.

Assume
n\mid m
. Take
k\in\omega
such that
m=nk
. Suppose
A
is an
\IP_{m}
-set, i.e. there is an infinite set
B\subseteq\omega
satisfying
\textrm{FS}_{m}(B)\subseteq A
. Since
B
is infinite, there is
p\in\omega
such that a set

B_{p}=\{a\in B:a\equiv p\mod n\}

is infinite. Fix
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n(k-1)-1}\in B_{p}
. Note that
n\mid\sum_{i<n(k-1)}a_{i}
, so

b=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i<n(k-1)}a_{i}
is a natural number.
Now, consider sets
B^{\prime}=B\backslash\{a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n(k-1)-1}\}
and
B^{\prime\prime}=B^{\prime}+b=\{c+b:c\in B^{\prime}\}
. Take any
b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}\in B^{\prime\prime}
. There are
c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}\in B^{\prime}
such that
b_{i}=c_{i}+b
for every
i<n
. Then

\sum_{i<n}b_{i}=\sum_{i<n}(c_{i}+b)=bn+\sum_{i<n}c_{i}=\sum_{j<n(k-1)}a_{j}+\sum_{i<n}c_{i}\in FS_{m}(B)

as a sum of
n(k-1)+n=nk=m
elements from the set
B
. Therefore,
\textrm{FS}_{n}(B^{\prime\prime})\subseteq\textrm{FS}_{m}(B)\subseteq A
, so
A
is an
\IP_{n}
-set.

Assume now that
n\nmid m
. Consider set
A=\{k\in\omega:n\nmid k\}
. Observe that

\textrm{FS}_{m}(\{kn+1:k\in\omega\})\subseteq A,

so
A
is an
\IP_{m}
-set. However, it is not an
\IP_{n}
-set. Suppose it is. There is an infinite
B\subseteq\omega
such that
\textrm{FS}_{n}(B)\subseteq A
. Since
B
is infinite, we can (like before) find
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}
such that
a_{i}\equiv a_{j}\mod n
for each
i,j<n
. But then
\sum_{i<n}a_{i}\in FS_{n}(B)\subseteq A
is divisible by
n
, what yields a contradiction.
∎

.

For each

[

is a proper subideal of

. Moreover, it is coanalytic (again due to

.

Set
\mathcal{D}
is
\coanalytic
-complete.

Proof.

This time we assume that
\alpha:\seqN\rightarrow\{2^{n}:n\in\omega\}
.

Suppose now that
\Delta(B)\subseteq\varphi(T)
for some infinite
B\subseteq\omega
. Take
n_{0}=\min\{k:(\exists n\in\omega)(2^{n}-2^{k}\in\Delta(B))\}
. Without loss of generality we can assume that

(otherwise if we consider

). Observe that now all elements of the set

(because only differences of powers of

). Take any

and
\alpha^{-1}(2^{n_{1}})\in T

3.
Trees

As noted in the introduction, a tree over a countable set
A
can be seen as a point in space
\mathcal{P}(A^{<\omega})
, which is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
. Therefore, families of trees, as subsets of this space, have similar topological structure to ideals on
\omega
. In the second part of this work we will investigate descriptive complexity of classical families of trees, like Miller or Laver. Let us start with a theorem connecting this topic with earlier results.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider function

M(B)=\{\sigma\in B^{<\omega}:\sigma\text{ is increasing}\}.

A family
\mathcal{I}_{M}
is
\coanalytic
-complete.

Proof.

In fact we can prove something even stronger, namely that
\varphi^{-1}[\textrm{IF}_{\omega}]=\textrm{IF}_{\omega}
. This, supported with
\varphi[\textrm{IF}_{\omega}]\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{M}^{c}
, will give us thesis.

Suppose that
\tau\in[\varphi(T)]
. Take any
k\in\omega
. By definition of body,
\tau\upharpoonright(k+2)\in\varphi(T)
. Hence, there is
\sigma\in T
such that
\alpha(\sigma\upharpoonright k_{1})=\tau(k)
and
\alpha(\sigma\upharpoonright k_{2})=\tau(k+1)
for some
k_{1},k_{2}\in\omega
. But
\tau(k+1)>\tau(k)
(since
\tau\upharpoonright(k+2)\in\varphi(T)
is increasing), so because of condition (
2
)
k_{1}<k_{2}
. Therefore,
\alpha^{-1}(\tau(k))\subset\alpha^{-1}(\tau(k+1))
, so
(\alpha^{-1}(\tau(k)))_{k\in\omega}
is an increasing sequence of elements of
T
, so
[T]\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Clearly,
\mathcal{I}_{M}
is not a family of trees (it does not necessarily have to be closed under taking subsequences). However, consider a function

\psi(A)=\left\{\sigma\upharpoonright k:\sigma\in A,k<|\sigma|\right\},

which "treefies" every subset of
\seqN
. Let us recall that
\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}
is a Polish space with subbase sets of the form
\{T\in\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}:\tau\in T\}
(inherited from the space
\mathcal{P}(\seqN)
) for some fixed
\tau\in\seqN
. Now see that

\psi^{-1}(\{T\in\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}:\tau\in T\})=\bigcup_{\sigma\supseteq\tau}\{A\in\mathcal{P}(\seqN):\sigma\in A\},

so
\psi
is continuous. Since
\analytic
-completeness is preserved by continuous maps, we can reformulate
Theorem

3.1
in terms of trees.

Corollary 3.2
.

The family of all trees containing
M(B)
for some
B\in[\omega]^{\omega}
is
\analytic
-complete.

Definition 11
.

We call a tree of the form
M(B)
for some
B\in[\omega]^{\omega}
a
Mathias bush
.

Moreover, if
T_{\sigma}=\{\tau\in\seqN:\sigma\concat\tau\in T\}
is a Mathias bush for some
\sigma\in T
, we call tree
T
a
Mathias tree
.

The idea of above definitions comes from Mathias forcing. Mathias forcing consists of pairs
(s,S)
, where
s\in\seqN
is increasing and
S\in[\omega]^{\omega}
such that
\max(\textrm{rng}(s))<\min(S)
. The ordering of pairs is defined as follows:

(s,S)\leq(t,T)\iff s\subseteq t\land S\supseteq T\land\textrm{rng}(t)\backslash\textrm{rng}(s)\subseteq S\backslash T.

Hence, if we fix a pair
(s,S)
, we can think of Mathias tree as a tree with stem
s
and all possible
t\in\seqN
such that
(s,S)\leq(t,T)
for some
T\in[\omega]^{\omega}
.

Now note that in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
having stem or not does not make any difference. Therefore we can conclude the following result.

Corollary 3.3
.

The family of all trees containing a Mathias tree is
\analytic
-complete.

Definition 12
.

A tree
T\in\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}
is called
Miller
if

(\forall\sigma\in T)(\exists\tau\supseteq\sigma)(\exists^{\infty}n\in\omega)(\tau\concat n\in T).

A tree
T\in\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}
is called
Laver
if

(\exists\sigma\in T)(\forall\tau\supseteq\sigma)(\exists^{\infty}n\in\omega)(\tau\concat n\in T).

Clearly, all Mathias trees are both Miller and Laver. This remark together with proof of
Theorem

3.1
and earlier remarks give us

Corollary 3.4
.

The family of all trees containing a Miller tree is
\analytic
-complete.

The family of all trees containing a Laver tree is
\analytic
-complete.

We would also like to explore some known families of trees over set
\{0,1\}
. Like before, such trees can be seen as points in space
\mathcal{P}(\seq 2)
, homeomorphic to
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
(from which we can derive a Polish space
\textrm{Tree}_{2}
). However, unlike in case of
\omega
,
\textrm{IF}_{2}
is a
G_{\delta}
subset of
\textrm{Tree}_{2}
.

Definition 13
.

A tree
T\in\textrm{Tree}_{2}
is called a
Sacks tree
(or
perfect tree
) if

(\forall\sigma\in T)(\exists\tau\in T)(\tau\supseteq\sigma\land\tau\concat 0\in T\land\tau\concat 1\in T).

A tree
T\in\textrm{Tree}_{2}
is called a
Silver tree
if there exist a coinfinite set
A\subseteq\omega
and a function
x:\omega\rightarrow 2
such that

T=\left\{\sigma\in\seq 2:(\forall n\in|\sigma|\cap A)(\sigma(n)=x(n)\right\}.

Then we call
A
the
domain
of
T
(and
\omega\backslash A
the
co-domain
) and
x
the
Silver function
of
T
.

Theorem 3.5
.

The family
\mathcal{S}
of all trees containing a Sacks tree is
\analytic
-complete.

Proof.

For the proof we will need following lemma, which proof can be found in
[
8
, Proposition 4.2.5]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
be an uncountable Polish space. Then

U(X)=\left\{K\in\ K(X):|K|>\omega\right\}

is
\analytic
-complete, where
K(X)
is the space of all compact subsets of
X
equipped with Vietoris topology.

Define
\varphi:K(2^{\omega})\rightarrow\textrm{Tree}_{2}
by

\varphi(K)=\left\{\sigma\upharpoonright k:\sigma\in K,k\in\omega\right\}.

We need to show that
\varphi
is Borel and
\varphi^{-1}[\mathcal{S}]=U(2^{\omega})
. Let us start with the second condition. By
\mathbb{S}a
denote the family of all Sacks trees.

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}[\mathcal{S}]

\displaystyle=\left\{K\in K(2^{\omega}):\varphi(K)\in\mathcal{S}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{K\in K(2^{\omega}):(\exists T\in\mathbb{S}a)(T\subseteq\varphi(K))\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{K\in K(2^{\omega}):(\exists T\in\mathbb{S}a)([T]\subseteq[\varphi(K)])\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{K\in K(2^{\omega}):(\exists T\in\mathbb{S}a)([T]\subseteq K)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{K\in K(2^{\omega}):|K|>\omega\right\}=U(2^{\omega}),

where second to last equality comes from Sacks dichotomy for closed subsets of
2^{\omega}
.

Now, fix
s\in\seq 2
. With
[s]_{\textrm{Tree}_{2}}
and
[s]_{2^{\omega}}
denote the subbase clopen sets generated by
s
in spaces
\textrm{Tree}_{2}
and
2^{\omega}
, respectively, i.e.

[s]_{\textrm{Tree}_{2}}=\left\{T\in\textrm{Tree}_{2}:s\in T\right\},[s]_{2^{\omega}}=\left\{\sigma\in 2^{\omega}:\sigma\supseteq s\right\}.

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}[[s]_{\textrm{Tree}_{2}}]

\displaystyle=\left\{K\in K(2^{\omega}):(\exists\sigma\in K)(\exists k\in\omega)(s=\sigma\upharpoonright k)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{K\in K(2^{\omega}):K\cap[s]_{2^{\omega}}\neq\emptyset\right\},

which is an open set in Vietoris topology. Therefore, function
\varphi
is continuous.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

The family
\mathcal{V}
of all trees containing a Silver tree is
\analytic
-complete.

Proof.

We construct a Borel function
\varphi:\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}\rightarrow\textrm{Tree}_{2}
such that
\varphi^{-1}[\mathcal{V}]=\textrm{IF}_{\omega}
. First, define some auxiliary functions. Let
\alpha:\omega\rightarrow\seq 2
be a binary expansion of natural numbers,
\beta:\seq 2\rightarrow\seq 2
be defined by formula

\beta((a_{0}a_{1}\ldots a_{k}))=(a_{0}a_{0}a_{1}a_{1}\ldots a_{k}a_{k})

and
\gamma:\seqN\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\seq 2)
be defined by

\gamma((n_{0}n_{1}\ldots n_{k}))=\{\beta(\alpha(n_{0}))\concat 01i_{0}01\concat\ldots\concat\beta(\alpha(n_{k}))\concat 01i_{k}01:\\
i_{0},i_{1}\ldots,i_{k}\in\{0,1\}\}.

Now we can define function
\varphi^{\prime}:\textrm{Tree}_{\omega}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\seq 2)
:

\varphi^{\prime}(T)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in T}\gamma(\sigma).

As we can see,
\varphi^{\prime}(T)
are not trees, because they are not closed under taking subsequences. However, we can one again fix it by "treefying" a result, i.e.

\varphi=\psi\circ\varphi^{\prime}.

Clearly, if
\tau=(\tau_{0}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\ldots)\in[T]
then
\varphi(T)
contains a Silver tree with Silver function
x=\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 01\underline{0}01\concat\beta(\alpha(\tau_{1}))\concat 01\underline{0}01\concat\ldots
and co-domain being a set of indices of underlined
0
s.

Suppose now that
\varphi(T)
contains a Silver tree
S
. Silver function of
S
has to be of the form
x_{S}=\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 01i_{0}01\concat\beta(\alpha(\tau_{1}))\concat 01i_{1}01\concat\ldots
for some
\tau=(\tau_{0}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\ldots)
and
i_{0},i_{1},\ldots\in\{0,1\}
(because no other sequences are produced by function
\varphi
). Note that if the co-domain of
S
is a subset of positions of
i_{k}
s in
x_{S}
,
\tau\in[T]
(and
\tau
can be uniquely identified from
x_{S}
). Assume then that there is such
m
in co-domain of
S
(denote the co-domain of
S
as
A_{S}
). Without loss of generality
m\leq|\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 01i_{0}01|
(argument can be repeated inside any block of the form
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{k}))\concat 01i_{k}01
).

First, note that
m\neq|\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))|+4
and
m\neq|\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))|+5
(i.e. it can not be any of the last two positions), since function
\varphi
fixes values at two positions after block of the form
\beta(n)\concat 01i
. Consider the case
m=|\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))|+2
. As
m\in A_{S}
,
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 01,\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 00\in S
. Since in Silver tree every sequence can be extended to the one, which follows Silver function,
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 00i_{0}01\concat\beta(\alpha(\tau_{1}))\concat 0\in S
. But then in
S
there is a sequence of the form
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat(00)^{k}\concat(11)^{l}\concat 10
(for some
k,l\in\omega
), which is not possible by the form of function
\varphi
.

Now consider the case
m=|\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))|+1
. Because
m\in A_{S}
,
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 0,\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 1\in S
. But then, similarly as in previous case,
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 11i_{0}01\beta(\alpha(\tau_{1}))0\in S
. Therefore, a sequence of the form
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))\concat 11\concat(00)^{k}\concat(11)^{l}\concat 10
(for some
k,l\in\omega
) is in
S
, which is again impossible.

There are two more possibilities for
m\leq|\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))|
: either
2\mid m
or
2\nmid m
. Let us focus on the second one (the first one can be analyzed in the similar way). If
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))
has
1
at the position
m
, it has
1
also at the position
m-1
. But then sequence of the form
\beta(n)\concat 10
(for some
n\in\omega)
is in
S
, which leads to contradiction. If
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))
has
0
at positions
m
and
m-1
,
\beta(n)\concat 00,\beta(n)\concat 01\in S
for some
n\in\omega
and
\beta(n)\concat 00\subseteq\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))
. There is
\upsilon\in\seq 2
, consisting only of blocks
00
and
11
, such that
\beta(\alpha(\tau_{0}))=\beta(n)\concat 00\concat\upsilon
. Then
\beta(n)\concat 01\concat\upsilon\concat 01\in S
, so either
\beta(n)\concat 0101\in S
,
\beta(n)\concat 0111\in S
,
\beta(n)\concat 010001\in S
(which all lead to immediate contradiction) or
\beta(n)\concat 010011\concat\mu\concat 01\in S
, where
\upsilon=0011\concat\mu
. Hence, there is
a\in\omega
such that either
1\concat\mu\concat 0\subseteq\beta(a)
or
\beta(a)\subseteq 1\concat\mu\concat 0
. The first case leads to the situation that
\beta(a)
contains block
10
with
1
at even position, which is not possible. However, in the second one
\beta(a)\concat 10\subseteq 1\concat\mu\concat 0
, which is again impossible, because then
\beta(n)\concat 01001\concat\beta(a)\concat 10\in S
.

∎
