Parametric Continued Fractions for π 2 \pi^{2} , ζ ⁡ ( 3 ) \zeta(3) , and other Constants
Henri Cohen

Abstract
We give an extensive list of parametrized families of polynomial continued fractions of smallest possible degrees for π 2 \pi^{2} and ζ ⁡ ( 3 ) \zeta(3) , and mention similar results for other constants.



1
Introduction

One can find in the literature a huge number of continued fractions
(abbreviated CFs) for interesting constants and functions. The purpose of
this paper is to give possibly
experimental
results showing that there
often exist
families
of such CFs, usually depending on two, three,
and even four parameters. In many cases these experimental results can be

proved
, usually because either
p_{n}/q_{n}
or
p_{n}/q_{n}-p_{n-1}/q_{n-1}

have an explicit expression. In other cases, I have not bothered to try
to prove the experimental results, although it may not be difficult.
Thus, when I speak of results, they should be considered experimental, but
with considerable evidence. I would appreciate receiving any additional
data of the same kind.

Notation: a CF will be written as a pair of sequences
(a_{n},b_{n})_{n\geq 0}

representing the CF
a_{0}+b_{0}/(a_{1}+b_{1}/(a_{2}+b_{2}/(a_{3}+\cdots)))
(warning:
note this precise convention, other authors have different notation).
All the CFs that we will encounter will be
convergent
: if as usual

p_{n}/q_{n}=a_{0}+b_{0}/(a_{1}+\cdots+b_{n-1}/a_{n})
, this means that

z=\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}/q_{n}
exists, and when we speak of the speed of
convergence, we mean the behavior of
z-p_{n}/q_{n}
.

Note that we always assume that
b_{n}\neq 0
for all
n
, but we allow a
finite number of
a_{n}
to vanish.

Definition 1.1

(1)

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence (always assumed to be of rational numbers,
and usually of integers). We say that it is ultimately polynomial if
there exists a polynomial denoted
a(x)
(by abuse of notation) with
rational coefficients such that
a_{n}=a(n)
and
a_{n}\neq 0
for
n

sufficiently large.

(2)

We say that the CF
(a_{n},b_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is of
polynomial type

if the sequences
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are ultimately polynomial.

As a practical notation, if
a_{n}=a(n)
for
n\geq n_{0}
, we will denote the
sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by the
(n_{0}+1)
-component vector

A=(a_{0},a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n_{0}-1},a(n))
, where the last entry is a polynomial.
Of course this is not unique: we also have for instance

A=(a_{0},a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n_{0}-1},a_{n_{0}},a(n))
with
a_{n_{0}}=a(n_{0})
.

Thus a CF will be denoted by a pair of such vectors
A
and
B
representing

(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
, and if the CF converges to some number
z
, we will
write
z=(a_{n},b_{n})
.

Definition 1.2

We recall that two CFs
(a_{n},b_{n})
and
(a^{\prime}_{n},b^{\prime}_{n})
are
said to be
equivalent
if there exists an integer
k
such that

(a^{\prime}_{n},b^{\prime}_{n})=(a_{n+k},b_{n+k})
for
n
sufficiently large, i.e., if
the
tails
of the CFs are ultimately the same.

Since we assume everything rational, if we set
z=(a_{n},b_{n})
and

z^{\prime}=(a^{\prime}_{n},b^{\prime}_{n})
, the CFs for
z
and
z^{\prime}
are equivalent if and only if
there exists a Möbius transformation with integer coefficients sending
z

to
z^{\prime}
, in other words
z^{\prime}=(Az+B)/(Cz+D)
for integers
A
,
B
,
C
,
D

with
AD-BC\neq 0
.

We can now give our final definition:

Definition 1.3

We say that a pair of polynomials
(A(x),B(x))
with
rational coefficients
represents
a constant
z
if there exists
a CF
(a_{n},b_{n})
converging to
z
such that
a_{n}=A(n)
and
b_{n}=B(n)

for
n
sufficiently large, and in that case we simply write by abuse
of notation
z=(A(n),B(n))
.

Because of what we have said above, this is almost, but not quite, equivalent
to the fact that the CF
(A(n),B(n))_{n\geq 0}
converges to a Möbius
transform of
z
(always assumed with integer coefficients): indeed, some
values of
A(n)
and/or
B(n)
may vanish, which kills this property. But
if we avoid these vanishing values by changing the corresponding coefficients
of the CF to any nonzero value, this is indeed true.

Our goal is, given some interesting constant
z
, to find all (or at least
many) pairs
(A,B)
representing
z
, subject to suitable restrictions.
In particular, recall that if
(a_{n},b_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a CF converging to
z

and
r_{n}
is any sequence with
r_{0}=1
and
r_{n}\neq 0
for all
n
, the CF

(a_{n}r_{n},b_{n}r_{n}r_{n+1})_{n\geq 0}
also converges to
z
. We will thus
restrict to polynomials
(A,B)
of smallest possible degrees.

To make this more precise, we introduce the following definitions:

Definition 1.4

We say that
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
is a
rational period of
degree
k
if it is equal to the sum of a convergent series of the form

\sum_{n\geq 1}\chi(n)f(n)
, where
\chi
is a periodic arithmetic function
taking
rational
values, and
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}(x)
is a rational function with
rational coefficients whose denominator is of degree
k
.

Note that this definition is
not
compatible with the definition
of periods as given by Kontsevitch–Zagier
[
3
]
. Note also that
a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linear combination of rational periods of degree
\leq k
is again
a rational period of degree
\leq k
.

Examples of periods of degree 1:
\pi
,
\pi/\sqrt{d}
,
\log(2)
,

\sqrt{2}\log(1+\sqrt{2})
,
\sqrt{3}\log(2+\sqrt{3})
,

\sqrt{5}\log((1+\sqrt{5})/2)
.

Examples of periods of degree 2:
\pi^{2}
,
\pi^{2}/\sqrt{d}
,

G=L(\chi_{-4},2)
(Catalan’s constant),
G_{3}=L(\chi_{-3},2)
.

Examples of periods of degree 3:
\pi^{3}
,
\pi^{3}/\sqrt{d}
,
\zeta(3)
.

By using Euler’s transformation of series it is clear that a period has a
CF expansion where
a(n)
and
b(n)
are polynomials with
rational coefficients for
n
large. However this is usually uninteresting,
or at least not any more interesting than the series itself. Thus, really
interesting CFs are those for which the degrees of the polynomials are
reasonably small, specifically
\deg(a(n))\leq k
and
\deg(b(n))\leq 2k
for

n
sufficiently large (we will say that it has
bidegree

\leq(k,2k)
).
Trivial examples:
S^{+}=\sum_{n\geq 1}1/P(n)
and

S^{-}=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n-1}/P(n)
with
\deg(P)=k\geq 2
have the CFs

S^{+}=((0,P(n)+P(n-1)),(1,-P(n)^{2}))
and
S^{-}=((0,P(n)-P(n-1)),(1,P(n)^{2}))

by Euler, of the required degrees.

Note that if we apply Euler’s transformation to

S=\sum_{n\geq 1}(n+1)/n^{3}=\zeta(3)+\zeta(2)
which is a period of degree
3
,
we obtain a CF of bidegree
(4,8)
. However, there does exist a CF of
bidegree
(3,6)
, for instance we have the (easily proved) CF:

\zeta(3)+\zeta(2)=((3,2n^{3}+2n^{2}+3n+1),(-1,-n(n+1)^{5}))\;.

For most of this paper, we will focus on the cases
z=\pi^{2}
(or
equivalently
z=\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6
) and
z=\zeta(3)
,
and attempt to classify
all
representations of
\pi^{2}
with bidegree

\leq(2,4)
, and of
\zeta(3)
with bidegree
\leq(3,6)
.
A completely analogous study can be made for other periods such
as those mentioned above, we will briefly mention this in the last section.

Note that, contrary to
[
1
]
, we are interested in CFs for “pure”
periods such as those mentioned above, and not in linear combinations,
which is considerably more restrictive since one needs to get rid of
“parasitic” terms (for instance
\log(2)
for
\pi^{2}
, or
\log(2)

and
\zeta(2)
for
\zeta(3)
, see below).

2
CFs for
\pi^{2}
: Convergence in
1/n^{P}
,
P\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}

As far as the author is aware, up to equivalence there exist exactly nine
types of convergence of polynomial continued fractions for
\pi^{2}
with
bidegree
\leq(2,4)
: two families with convergence in
1/n^{P}
with
P\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
one with convergence in
1/n^{P}
with
P\in 1/2+{\mathbb{Z}}
, one with convergence
in
(-1)^{n}/n^{P}
, one with convergence
1/(E^{n}n^{P})
for each of
E=4/3
,
2
,

4
, and
-8
, and finally the unique CF of Apéry with
convergence in
1/E^{n}
for
E=-((1+\sqrt{5})/2)^{10}
. We consider
each of these families in turn.

2.1
First Family

Using Euler’s transformation of series into CFs applied to

\pi^{2}/6=\sum_{n\geq 1}1/n^{2}
, we see that
\pi^{2}/6
is given by the CF

((0,2n^{2}-2n+1),(1,-n^{4}))
, with speed of convergence identical to that of
the series, in
1/n
, which we write in abbreviated form as explained above
where we do not give the initial values of
a_{n}
and
b_{n}

\pi^{2}=(2n^{2}-2n+1,-n^{4})\;.

Remark: even though this notation means that there can be a number of
initial values of
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
which are not given by the general
polynomial formulas, in most of the examples that we will see in this
paper, only
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
would need to be specified (for instance
here
a_{0}=0
and
b_{0}=1
), the other
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
for
n\geq 1
are given
by the polynomials.

If we apply the Bauer–Muir transformation, we check that for any nonnegative
integer
k\geq 0
we also have
\pi^{2}=(2n^{2}-2n+k^{2}+k+1,-n^{4})
.

This naturally leads us to
search numerically
for other representations
of the form
\pi^{2}=(A(n),B(n))
, with
A(n)=2n^{2}+a_{1}n+a_{0}
and
B(n)
of
degree
4

with leading coefficient
-1
. We speed up the search by assuming that
B

is a product of linear factors with integral coefficients (you are welcome to
try other types of polynomials, but I am pretty sure that you will not find
others), and since
(A(n+j),B(n+j))
is equivalent to
(A(n),B(n))
, we can
assume that
B(n)=-n(n+u_{1})(n+u_{2})(n+u_{3})
with
0\leq u_{1}\leq u_{2}\leq u_{3}
.

We find a
very large
number of such representations, from which one
can easily extract one and two-parameter families, less easily three-parameter
families, and finally with considerable difficulty, a single four-parameter
family to rule them all:

\pi^{2}=(A(n),B(n))
with

\displaystyle A(n)

\displaystyle=2n^{2}+(3u+v+w-2)n

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+k^{2}+u^{2}+uk+vk+wk+uv+uw+vw+k-u+1

\displaystyle B(n)

\displaystyle=-n(n+u)(n+u+v)(n+u+w)\;,

where
u
,
v
,
w
, and
k
are nonnegative integer parameters, with
speed of convergence in
C/n^{u+v+w+2k+1}
for some constant
C
.

An extensive additional search seems to show that there are no additional
sporadic representations. However, we will see in Section
3
that
it is also possible to choose
v
or
w
(but not both) in
1/2+{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof:
in the present case, it is possible, not only to prove that
the four-parameter family above does represent
\pi^{2}
, but also to
explain where it originates.

Consider the series
S=\sum_{n\geq 1}1/\prod_{0\leq i\leq m-1}(n+i)^{e_{i}}
,
where
e_{i}=1
if
0\leq i\leq m_{1}-1
or
m_{1}+m_{2}\leq i\leq m-1
,
and
e_{i}=2
if
m_{1}\leq i\leq m_{1}+m_{2}-1
, where
m_{1}\geq 0
,
m_{2}\geq 1
,
and
m\geq m_{1}+m_{2}
. By expanding into partial fractions it is immediate
to show that the sum
S
satisfies

S=a\pi^{2}+b
with
a
,
b
rational and
a\neq 0
. On the other hand,
applying Euler’s transformation of
S
into a CF, and simplifying by
suitable factors, it is not difficult to show that
S
(hence
\pi^{2}
) is
represented by the CF
(A(n),B(n))
, with

\displaystyle A(n)

\displaystyle=(n-1)(n+m_{1}-1)+(n+m_{1}+m_{2}-1)(n+m-1)\text{\quad and\quad}

\displaystyle B(n)

\displaystyle=-n(n+m_{1})(n+m_{1}+m_{2}-1)(n+m-1)\;.

This gives the three-parameter family above when
k=0
, and the case
of general
k
is obtained by successive Bauer–Muir accelerations.

2.2
Second Family

We also have the modified series obtained by removing the Euler factor
at
2
:
\pi^{2}/8=\sum_{n\geq 1}1/(2n-1)^{2}
, giving the
CF
((0,1,8n^{2}-16n+10),(1,-(2n-1)^{4}))
for
\pi^{2}/8
, that we write in
abbreviated form

\pi^{2}=(8n^{2}-16n+10,-(2n-1)^{4})\;.

All the work has been done for us in the first family: we simply replace

n
by
n-1/2
and multiply by suitable powers of
2
to simplify the
formulas, giving the single four-parameter family
\pi^{2}=(A(n),B(n))
with

\displaystyle A(n)

\displaystyle=8n^{2}+4(3u+v+w-4)n

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+4(k^{2}+u^{2}+uk+vk+wk+uv+uw+vw)+4k-10u-2v-2w+10

\displaystyle B(n)

\displaystyle=-(2n-1)(2n-1+2u)(2n-1+2u+2v)(2n-1+2u+2w)\;,

with the same speed of convergence in
C/n^{u+v+w+2k+1}
.

However, inspection shows that while
v
,
w
, and
k
must still be
nonnegative, we can have
u
negative as long as
u\geq-k-\min(v,w)
.
Note that here we
cannot
choose
v
or
w
in
1/2+{\mathbb{Z}}
.

3
CFs for
\pi^{2}
: Convergence in
1/n^{P}
,
P\in 1/2+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}

We recall the following formula due to Euler:

\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{(2z)^{2n}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}=2\asin^{2}(z)\;,

which we will use several times. Here, we chooose
z=1
to obtain
the series

\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{4^{n}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}=\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{2}\;,

and using Euler’s transformation of series into CFs we see that
\pi^{2}/2

is given by the CF
\pi^{2}/2=((0,4n^{2}-5n+2),(2,-2n^{3}(2n-1)))
with convergence
identical to that of the series in
\sqrt{\pi}/n^{1/2}
, which we write
in abbreviated form

\pi^{2}=(4n^{2}-5n+2,-2n^{3}(2n-1))\;.

If we apply the Bauer–Muir transformation, we check that for any nonnegative
integer
k\geq 0
we also have
\pi^{2}=(4n^{2}-5n+2k^{2}+k+2,-2n^{3}(2n-1))
.

Here, we could do a new search, but luckily we have already done the work:
it is simply the first four-parameter family found above, but now with the
parameter
w
being a half integer instead of an integer. We thus replace

w
by
w-1/2
and simplify, so we obtain the four-parameter family

\pi^{2}=(A(n),B(n))
with

\displaystyle A(n)

\displaystyle=4n^{2}+(6u+2v+2w-5)n+2k^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+(2u+2v+2w+1)k+2u^{2}+(2v+2w-3)u+v(2w-1)+2

\displaystyle B(n)

\displaystyle=-2n(n+u)(n+u+v)(2n+2u+2w-1)\;,

where
u
,
v
, and
k
are nonnegative integer parameters and
w\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
speed of convergence in
C/n^{u+v+w+2k+1/2}
for some constant
C
.

The
proof
of the validity of this family follows from the one
given in Section
2.1
.

4
CFs for
\pi^{2}
: Convergence in
(-1)^{n}/n^{P}

Using Euler’s transformation of series into CFs but now applied to

\pi^{2}/12=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n-1}/n^{2}
, we see that
\pi^{2}/12
is given by the
CF
((0,2n-1),(1,n^{4}))
, with speed of convergence identical to that of the
series, in
(-1)^{n}/(2n^{2})
, which we write in abbreviated form

\pi^{2}=(2n-1,n^{4})\;.

If we apply the Bauer–Muir transformation, we immediately see that for
any
odd
integer
k\geq 1
we also have
\pi^{2}=(k(2n-1),n^{4})
.
We easily check that this is also true for even integers, so we have found
a one-parameter family. Now we search numerically for other
representations of the form
\pi^{2}=(A(n),B(n))
, with
A(n)
of degree
1
and

B(n)
monic of degree
4
, as before assumed to be a product of linear
factors with integral coefficients. Since
(-A(n),B(n))

is easily seen to be equivalent to
(A(n),B(n))
, we can also
assume that the leading coefficient of
A(n)
is positive, so we set

A(n)=a_{1}n+a_{0}
with
a_{1}\geq 1
and
B(n)=n(n+u_{1})(n+u_{2})(n+u_{3})
with

0\leq u_{1}\leq u_{2}\leq u_{3}
. We again find a large number of representations,
and after some cleaning up, we obtain a three-parameter family
and four one-parameter families, and no additional sporadic representations:

\displaystyle\pi^{2}

\displaystyle=((k+1)(2n+2u+2v-1),n(n+u)(n+u+2v)(n+2u+2v))

\displaystyle=((2u^{2}+8u+7)n+(2u+3)(u^{2}+3u+1),n(n+u)(n+u+1)(n+2u+4))

\displaystyle=((2u^{2}+8u+7)n+(2u+3)(u+2)(u+3),n(n+u+3)(n+u+4)(n+2u+4))

\displaystyle=((8u+7)n+(24u^{2}+24u+3),n(n+2u)(n+4u+1)(n+6u+4))

\displaystyle=((8u+7)n+6(u+1)(4u+3),n(n+2u+3)(n+4u+4)(n+6u+4))\;,

where
u
,
v
, and
k
are nonnegative integer parameters, with
speeds of convergence
(-1)^{n}C/n^{2k+2}
,
(-1)^{n}C/n^{2u^{2}+8u+7}
,

(-1)^{n}C/n^{2u^{2}+8u+7}
,
(-1)^{n}C/n^{8u+7}
and
(-1)^{n}C/n^{8u+7}

respectively, for some constants
C
.

Proof:
Although similar to the proof given in Section
2.1

the proof of the above is both more complicated and more
interesting since we will need to generalize the construction. Here we
consider the series

S=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n-1}/\prod_{0\leq i\leq m-1}(n+i)^{e_{i}}\;,

with the same
e_{i}
as above. Once again we can simplify the result
given by Euler’s transformation of
S
into a CF, and we obtain that

S
is represented by the CF
(A(n),B(n))
with

\displaystyle A(n)

\displaystyle=-(n-1)(n+m_{1}-1)+(n+m_{1}+m_{2}-1)(n+m-1)\text{\quad and\quad}

\displaystyle B(n)

\displaystyle=n(n+m_{1})(n+m_{1}+m_{2}-1)(n+m-1)\;.

The problem is that the sum
S
is not anymore of the form
a\pi^{2}+b
, but
of the form
a\pi^{2}+c\log(2)+b
. Thus, if we want a CF representing
\pi^{2}

itself, we need
c=0
. It is not difficult to show that this is indeed the
case when
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}):=(m_{1},m_{2},m-m_{1}-m_{2})
is of one of the following
three shapes:
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(m_{1},2u+1,m_{1})
or
(2u,2u+2,2u+3)
, or

(2u+3,2u+2,2u)
. It should not be difficult to prove that there are no
other cases, but I have not done so. In any event, the first shape
followed by Bauer–Muir accelerations leads to the three-parameter family,
and the other two shapes lead to the last two one-parameter families.

The first two one-parameter families do
not
come from this
construction, but from a more general one. We first note the following
result, whose easy proof is left to the reader

Proposition 4.1

Let
c(x)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
be a polynomial with
\deg(c)\geq 2
,
let
z=\pm 1
, let
P\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
be an irreducible polynomial which divides

c(x)P(x+1)+zc(x-1)P(x-1)
, let
R(x)=(c(x)P(x+1)+zc(x-1)P(x-1))/P(x)

and
g(x)=\gcd(c(x),c(x-1))
, and set

S=\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{z^{n}}{c(n)P(n)P(n+1)}\;.

Assume that
c(x)
,
P(x)
, and
P(x+1)
are pairwise coprime.
We have the continued fraction

S=[[0,R(n)/g(n)],[r_{1},-zc(n)^{2}/(g(n)g(n+1))]]
and the identity

S=r_{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{n}d(n)/c(n)
for some rational numbers
r_{1}
and

r_{2}
and polynomial
d
with
\deg(d)<\deg(c)
.

We also need the following conjecture:

Conjecture 4.2

Let
u
be a nonnegative integers. Up to a multiplicative constant there
exists a unique polynomial
P
such that

\displaystyle(x+2u+4)(x+u+1)

\displaystyle P(x+1)-(x-1)(x+u-1)P(x-1)

\displaystyle=((2u^{2}+8u+7)x+(2u+3)(u^{2}+3u+1))P(x)\;,

and we have
\deg(P)=u(u+3)
. The same is true if we require

\displaystyle(x+2u+4)(x+u+4)

\displaystyle P(x+1)-(x-1)(x+u+2)P(x-1)

\displaystyle=((2u^{2}+8u+7)x+(2u+3)(u+2)(u+3))P(x)\;.

Assuming this conjecture, in Proposition
4.1
we choose
z=-1
,

c(x)=\prod_{0\leq i\leq 2u+4}(n+i)^{e_{i}}
as before, with
m_{1}=u
,
m_{2}=2
, and

m_{3}=u+3
, so
m=2u+5
, and
P
the polynomial satisfying the first identity
above. We check that
g(x)=(x+u)\prod_{0\leq i\leq 2u+3}(x+i)
, hence

-zc(n)^{2}/(g(n)g(n+1))=n(n+u)(n+u+1)(n+2u+4)\;,

and because of the identity for
P
,

R(n)/g(n)=((2u^{2}+8u+7)n+(2u+3)(u^{2}+3u+1)\;.

Finally, the identity for

S
given by Proposition
4.1
implies that
S
is a linear combination
of
\pi^{2}
,

with the
same speed of convergence as the series in
\sqrt{\pi}/2/((-4)^{n}n^{5/2})
,
which we write as

\zeta(3)=(3n^{3}+n^{2}-3n+1,2n^{5}(2n-1))\;.

Here, a variant of Bauer–Muir gives the family

\zeta(3)=(3n^{3}+(11k+1)n^{2}-(11k+3)n+3k+1,n^{4}(2n-k)(2n-k-1))

with convergence in
C/((-4)^{n}n^{5k+5/2})
. A search apparently does not
give any other CF.

15
CFs for
\zeta(3)
: Convergence in
1/(1+\sqrt{2})^{4n}

Let

C_{n}=\sum_{n/2\leq k\leq n}\binom{n}{k}^{2}\binom{2k}{n}^{2}

(these numbers do not seem to have a name).
From the recursion that one can find
for these numbers and the computation of Apéry limits
(see for instance
[
2
]
), we have the CF

\zeta(3)=((0,(2n-1)(3n^{2}-3n+1)),(8/7,-n^{6}))
with speed of convergence

4\pi^{3}/7/(1+\sqrt{2})^{4n+2}
, whose
n
th denominator

q(n)
is given by
q(n)=n!^{3}C_{n}/4^{n}
, giving the formula

\dfrac{7\zeta(3)}{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{16^{n}}{n^{3}C_{n}C_{n-1}}\;.

As usual we write the CF in abbreviated form

\zeta(3)=((2n-1)(3n^{2}-3n+1),-n^{6})\;.

Since the exponent of the speed of convergence is irrational, Bauer–Muir
acceleration would give irrational coefficients so is not applicable,
and I have not been able to find other CFs of this type.

16
CFs for
\zeta(3)
: Convergence in
1/(1+\sqrt{2})^{8n}

This is the famous CF of Apéry:

\zeta(3)=((0,(2n-1)(17n^{2}-17n+5)),(6,-n^{6}))

with convergence in
4\pi^{3}/(1+\sqrt{2})^{8n+4}
.

This CF seems to be unique of its kind, i.e. of the form

\zeta(3)=(34n^{3}+a_{2}n^{2}+a_{1}n+a_{0},-n(n+u_{1})(n+u_{2})(n+u_{3})(n+u_{4})(n+u_{5})(n+u_{6}))
.

17
CFs for
\zeta(3)
: Other Speeds of Convergence

I have not found any convergent CF of the form

\zeta(3)=(A(n),B(n))
with
\deg(A)\leq 3
,
\deg(B)\leq 6
and
A
and
B

with integral coefficients other than the
families mentioned above, up to trivial equivalence such as changing

n
into
n+j
or multiplying
(A,B)
by
(c,c^{2})
for a nonzero
constant
c
. Of course, there exist infinitely many other
CFs for
\zeta(3)
, but of larger bidegrees.

18
Other Periods

All the preceding sections dealt with CFs for
\pi^{2}
and
\zeta(3)
.
A similar study can be done for other periods. I briefly summarize what I
have found, restricting as usual to CFs of bidegrees
\leq(k,2k)
for periods
of degree
k
.

18.1
Periods of Degree
1

As just mentioned, we restrict to bidegrees
\leq(1,2)
.

\bullet

z=\pi
: one finds parametric families of CFs with convergence in

(-1)^{n}/n^{P}
,
1/(2^{n}n^{P})
, and
(-1)^{n}/(1+\sqrt{2})^{2n}
.

\bullet

z=\pi/\sqrt{3}
: one finds parametric families of CFs with
convergence in
1/((4/3)^{n}n^{P})
,
(-1)^{n}/(3^{n}n^{P})
,
1/(4^{n}n^{P})
, and

(-1)^{n}/(2+\sqrt{3})^{2n}
.

\bullet

z=\log(2)
: one finds parametric families of CFs with convergence
in
(-1)^{n}/n^{P}
,
1/(2^{n}n^{P})
,
(-1)^{n}/(8^{n}n^{P})
,
1/(9^{n}n^{P})
, and

1/(1+\sqrt{2})^{4n}
.

Note that one can also study CFs for
z=\log(m)
for other rational
values of
m
.

\bullet

z=\sqrt{2}\log(1+\sqrt{2})
: one finds parametric families of
CFs with convergence in
1/(1+\sqrt{2})^{2n}
.

18.2
Periods of Degree
2

We restrict to bidegrees
\leq(2,4)
.

\bullet

z=\pi^{2}
: see the extensive lists given in the preceding sections.

\bullet

z=L(\chi_{-3},2)
: one finds parametric families of CFs with
convergence
1/((9/8)^{n}n^{P})
and
1/(9^{n}n^{P})
.

\bullet

z=L(\chi_{-4},2)=G
, Catalan’s constant: one finds parametric
families of CFs with convergence
1/n^{P}
,
(-1)^{n}/n^{P}
, and
1/(2^{n}n^{P})
.

18.3
Periods of Degree
3

We restrict to bidegrees
\leq(3,6)
.

\bullet

z=\zeta(3)
: see the extensive lists given in the preceding
sections.

\bullet
For other periods of degree
3
such as
\pi^{3}
and
\pi^{3}/\sqrt{3}
,
either the CFs are isolated, or are one-parameter families coming from
Bauer–Muir acceleration.

I would evidently welcome any additional data and comments
on the subject of this paper.
