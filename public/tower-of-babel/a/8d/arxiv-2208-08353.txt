Thermoviscous Hydrodynamics in Non-Degenerate Dipolar Bose Gases Preprint: APS/123-QED
Reuben R.W. Wang, John L. Bohn

Abstract
We present a hydrodynamic model of ultracold, but not yet quantum condensed, dipolar Bosonic gases. Such systems present both s s -wave and dipolar scattering, the latter of which results in anisotropic transport tensors of thermal conductivity and viscosity. This work presents an analytic derivation of the viscosity tensor coefficients, utilizing the methods established in [Wang et al., arXiv:2205.10465], where the thermal conductivities were derived. Taken together, these transport tensors then permit a comprehensive description of hydrodynamics that is now embellished with dipolar anisotropy. An analysis of attenuation in linear waves illustrates the effect of this anisotropy in dipolar fluids, where we find a clear dependence on the dipole orientation relative to the direction of wave propagation.

Introduction

The field of ultracold dipolar physics has seen exciting progress in the last 2 decades
Bohn et al. 2017
;
Moses et al. 2017
;
Liu and Luo 2021
, brought about by technological advances in the cooling and trapping of magnetic lanthanide atoms
Griesmaier et al. 2005
;
Lu et al. 2011
;
Aikawa et al. 2012
;
Phelps et al. 2020
;
Chomaz et al. 2022
, and heteronuclear polar molecules

;
Ni et al. 2008
;
Anderegg et al. 2017
;
Voges et al. 2020
;
Valtolina et al. 2020
. Of note are recent experiments that have realized evaporative cooling in 3-dimensional polar molecular gases, made possible by electric field
Li et al. 2021
or microwave shielding
Anderegg et al. 2021
;
Schindewolf et al. 2022
. The observed high ratio of elastic to inelastic collision rates permit long lived samples even at high densities, motivating study of dipolar induced anisotropic phenomena deep in the hydrodynamic regime.
Ref.
Wang and Bohn 2022
takes the first step in formulating a hydrodynamic model of ultracold dipolar Bose gases, by deriving the transport tensor of thermal conductivity using the Chapman-Enskog procedure
Uehling 1934
;
Taxman 1958
;
Monchick and Mason 1961
;
Chapman and Cowling 1990
. Here we extend this formulation by deriving the viscosity tensor for gases consisting of dipolar constituents.

To construct these tensors, we assume molecular scattering cross sections due to ideal point dipoles aligned in a particular direction in space, as described in Ref.
Bohn and Jin 2014
. Fortunate for our analysis, the effective long-ranged molecular interaction potential between microwave shielded molecules is equivalent to the classical dipole potential
Karman et al. 2022
;
Schindewolf et al. 2022
, permitting use of the collision cross sections derived in Ref.
Bohn and Jin 2014
. The same is apparently not strictly true for electric field shielded molecules
Lassablière and Quéméner 2022
, but use of the same cross section has previously proven adequate in describing cross-dimensional thermalization experiments
Li et al. 2021
;
Wang and Bohn 2021
.

These transport tensors then permit us to study the propagation of waves through the dipolar gas via the dispersion relation
Sommerfeld and Brillouin 1960
, derived from the equations of conservation and constitution. Although extensively studied in quantum degenerate dipolar gases
Wenzel et al. 2018
;
Ticknor et al. 2011
;
Block et al. 2012
;
Andreev 2013
;
Ronen and Bohn 2010
, wave phenomena in their still thermal counterparts remain less treated. A goal of this work is, therefore, also to motivate deeper investigations of normal phase dipolar gases in the ultracold community. These systems promise a vast variety of dynamical phenomena still unexplored, for example anisotropies in flow behavior or in turbulence.

II

II
Anisotropic Viscosity

II.1
General

Whereas a dilute gas is described in terms of the distribution of molecular velocities, a fluid in the hydrodynamic limit is described in terms of macroscopic observables such as the density
\rho
, velocity
\bm{U}
, and temperature
T
, all of which may vary in time and space. The governing equations for these quantities, including in ultracold systems
Nikuni and Griffin 1998a
, are the equations of conservation
Uehling and Uhlenbeck 1933
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\partial_{j}\left({\rho U_{j}}\right)=0,

(1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho U_{i}\right)+\partial_{j}\left(\rho U_{j}U_{i}\right)=\partial_{j}\sigma_{ij},

(1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho T)+\partial_{j}\left(\rho TU_{j}\right)=\frac{2m}{3k_{B}}\left(\sigma_{ij}\partial_{j}U_{i}-\partial_{j}J_{j}\right),

(1c)

where
\partial_{i}
denotes the partial derivative with respect to coordinate
r_{i}
, and we invoke the summation conventions.
The left-hand side of each of these equations denotes the rate of change of the relevant quantity at a given location in the fluid, including convective transport due to the fact that the fluid is in motion. In the first, this derivative of the mass density
\rho
is zero; this is the equation of continuity. In the second equation, the change of the momentum density
\rho\bm{U}
is given by forces acting in the fluid, thus giving the Navier-Stokes equation
Navier 1823
;
Stokes 2007
. These forces are in turn given as gradients of the stress tensor
\sigma
, denoting the force on a surface with normal vector
\hat{\bm{r}}_{i}
, in direction
\hat{\bm{r}}_{j}
. These forces are illustrated schematically in Figure
1
, where all vectors appear to have the same length, although this is not generally true in the fluid. In the third equation of motion, for the mass-weighted temperature distribution, the rate of change depends on both the stress tensor and on the heat conduction vector
\bm{J}
.

The equations (
1
) are general in the absence of long-ranged forces between the molecules, and are described in Refs.
Bond et al. 1965
;
Chapman and Cowling 1990
;
De Groot and Mazur 2013
. Additional external forces, such as the trap confining the atoms, can be added as necessary. To apply the equations of change to a particular fluid, such as the dipolar gas we have in mind, requires some constitutive relations describing the fluid. For example, the stress tensor is written

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=-P\delta_{ij}+\tau_{ij}.

(2)

Here
P
is the thermodynamic pressure, related to the density and temperature by the fluid’s equation of state, which we will take as the ideal gas law in what follows. The remaining part,
\bm{\tau}
, is the viscous stress tensor, i.e., the part arising from viscosity. In a Newtonian fluid the viscous shear is assumed to be a linear function of the velocity gradients, so that generally,

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}=\mu_{ijk\ell}\;\partial_{\ell}U_{k},

(3)

where
\bm{\mu}
is in general a fourth-rank viscosity tensor and the flow velocity gradients
\partial_{\ell}U_{k}
, characterize the rate of strain on differential fluid volumes.
We will see, however, that ultracold inelastic collisions leave only a symmeterized portion of
\partial_{\ell}U_{k}
relevant.
Meanwhile, the heat conduction vector is assumed to be linearly related to temperature gradients in accordance with Fourier’s law,

\displaystyle J_{i}=-\kappa_{ij}\partial_{i}T,

(4)

where
\bm{\kappa}
is the thermal conductivity tensor,

The hydrodynamic relations of the dilute dipolar gas are therefore specified once the tensors
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\kappa}
are determined. The thermal conductivity
\bm{\kappa}
was previously derived in Ref.
Wang and Bohn 2022
. Here we turn our attention to the viscosity tensor
\bm{\mu}
.

II.2
Microscopic Theory

The hydrodynamic variables are given by the velocity-averaged quantities

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t)=mn(\bm{r},t)=\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)m,

(5a)

\displaystyle\bm{U}(\bm{r},t)=\frac{1}{n(\bm{r},t)}\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)\bm{v},

(5b)

\displaystyle T(\bm{r},t)=\frac{2}{3n(\bm{r},t)k_{B}}\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)\frac{1}{2}m\bm{u}^{2}.

(5c)

where
f(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)
denotes the phase space distribution of the molecules. The so-called peculiar velocity
\bm{u}(\bm{r})=\bm{v}-\bm{U}(\bm{r})
, is the velocity of molecules relative to the local flow velocity.

Similarly, the stress tensor is defined microscopically by the
integral

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}

\displaystyle=-m\int d^{3}uf(\bm{u})u_{i}u_{j}.

(6)

This integral computes the mean flux of momentum
mu_{i}
through a surface with normal unit vector
{\hat{r}}_{j}
, thus describing a force per area on that surface.

In the spirit of the perturbative method of Chapman and Enskog, the phase space density is assumed to differ but little from its equilibrium value,

\displaystyle f\approx f_{0}\left[1+\Phi\right],

(7)

where
\Phi\ll 1
and the equilibrium distribution of peculiar velocities is

\displaystyle f_{0}(\bm{u},\beta)

\displaystyle=n_{0}(\beta)c_{0}(\bm{u},\beta)

\displaystyle=n_{0}(\beta)\left(\frac{m\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\frac{m\beta}{2}\bm{u}^{2}\right),

(8)

n_{0}
is the gas equilibrium number density,
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
and
\bm{u}^{2}=u_{k}u_{k}
.

Reference
Wang and Bohn 2022
argued, after a lengthy derivation based on
Bond et al. 1965
, that a suitable variational
ansatz
for
\Phi
is given in terms of gradients of temperature and velocity as

\displaystyle\Phi=V_{l}b_{lk}\partial_{k}(\ln T)+2m\beta W_{ij}a_{ijkl}D_{kl}.

(9)

This expression is written in terms of the vector

\displaystyle V_{i}(\bm{u})\equiv\left(\frac{\beta m\bm{u}^{2}}{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)u_{i},

(10)

and symmetrized quantities

\displaystyle W_{ij}(\bm{u})\equiv u_{i}u_{j}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\bm{u}^{2},

(11a)

\displaystyle D_{ij}(\bm{U})\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{j}U_{i}+\partial_{i}U_{j}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\partial_{k}U_{k},

(11b)

where the coefficients
\bm{b}
and
\bm{a}
are to be determined variationally.

The term in
\Phi
involving
\ln T
contributes to the thermal conductivity and was evaluated in Ref.
Wang and Bohn 2022
. Here we focus on the other term, in terms of which the stress tensor becomes

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}

\displaystyle=-m\int d^{3}uf_{0}(\bm{u})\left[1+\Phi(\bm{u})\right]u_{i}u_{j}

(12)

\displaystyle=-\frac{n_{0}}{\beta}\delta_{ij}-2\left(m^{2}\beta\int d^{3}uf_{0}(\bm{u})u_{i}u_{j}W_{mn}a_{mnk\ell}\right)D_{k\ell}.

This expression identifies the thermodynamic pressure as
P=n_{0}/\beta
, and the quantity in parentheses as related to the shear viscosity.

II.3
A Note on Symmetry

The viscosity tensor as defined by Eq. (
3
) gives the stresses as linear combinations of the unsymmetrized second-rank tensor
\partial_{l}U_{k}
giving the gradients of the fluid’s velocity components. By contrast, the microscopic evaluation of stresses (
II.2
) from the Boltzmann equation, relates these stresses only to the symmetrized tensor
\bm{D}
. The difference is telling: generally this tensor can be reduced in the usual way into the traceless, second-rank
\bm{D}
, along with an antisymmetric tensor
\bm{R}
and a scalar,

\displaystyle\partial_{\ell}U_{k}=D_{k\ell}+R_{k\ell}+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{k\ell}\gradient\cdot\bm{U},

(13)

where

\displaystyle R_{k\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\ell}U_{k}-\partial_{k}U_{\ell}\right).

(14)

The absence of the antisymmetric tensor and the scalar from the expression connotes that there are no rotational viscosities, nor bulk viscosities in a dilute gas of particles with no internal degrees of freedom
Chapman and Cowling 1990
;
Gad-el Hak 1995
;
Graves and Argrow 1999
.
Without loss of generality, the viscous stress tensor can now be written in terms of just the traceless symmetric flow-velocity gradient tensor (a.k.a. strain rate tensor)

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}=2\mu_{ijk\ell}D_{k\ell}.

(15)

Note that this conclusion is independent of the form of the collision cross section of the molecules.

The relation between the two forms of the symmetrized tensors is conveniently handled via a contraction,

\displaystyle W_{ij}(\bm{u})={\cal I}_{ijk\ell}u_{k}u_{\ell}=u_{k}u_{\ell}{\cal I}_{k\ell ij},

(16a)

\displaystyle D_{ij}(\bm{U})={\cal I}_{ijk\ell}\partial_{\ell}U_{k}=\partial_{\ell}U_{k}{\cal I}_{k\ell ij},

(16b)

with the traceless symmetric tensor

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{ijmn}=\frac{\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+\delta_{in}\delta_{jm}}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{mn}.

(17)

Written in these terms, the expression for the shear stress tensor is

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}=-2m^{2}\beta\int d^{3}uf_{0}u_{i}u_{j}u_{m}u_{n}{\cal I}_{mnop}a_{opk\ell}D_{k\ell}.

(18)

The integrand now consists of products of components of the peculiar velocity
\bm{u}
, multiplied by the known equilibrium velocity distribution. All such integrals are readily evaluated (many leading to Kronecker delta functions), whereby the viscosity tensor ultimately becomes

\displaystyle\mu_{ijk\ell}=-\frac{2n_{0}}{\beta}{\cal I}_{ijmn}a_{mnk\ell},

(19)

in terms of the variational parameters
\bm{a}
. These parameters must be determined by an approximate solution of the Boltzmann equation.

II.4
Approximate Solution to the Boltzmann Equation

To this end, we start with the Boltzmann equation for the local phase space distribution

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{i}\partial_{i}\right)f(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)={\cal C}[f(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)],

(20)

where
{\cal C}[f]

is the two-body collision integral
Reif 2009
. We ignore molecular finite-size effects required at higher densities, which would modify the collision integral above
Chapman and Cowling 1990
;
Hoffman and Curtiss 1965
. At the current experimental regime of interest (detailed in Sec.
III
), we find that such effects only contribute
<5\%
changes to the transport coefficients.

Assuming the close-to-equilibrium phase space distribution of Eq. (
7
), we arrive at the
ansatz
in Eq. (
9
) and the approximate Boltzmann equation

\displaystyle f_{0}\>W_{k\ell}D_{k\ell}\approx 2C[f_{0}W_{ij}]a_{ijk\ell}D_{k\ell}.

(21)

Note that this refers to the portion of the simplified equation that pertains to viscosity, i.e., it does not include terms with temperature gradients.
Further details of this approximation are provided in Ref.
Wang and Bohn 2022
.

A visualization of the stresses (black arrows) on a differential fluid volume element (red cube) due to thermodynamic pressure and gradients in the velocity field.

To obtain explicit forms for the variational coefficients
a_{ijk\ell}
and hence the viscosities by Eq. (
19
), we rewrite the right-hand side of Eq. (
21
) as

\displaystyle 2C[f_{0}W_{mn}]a_{mnk\ell}D_{k\ell}

\displaystyle\quad\quad=2C[f_{0}W_{mn}]\left({\cal I}_{mnrs}a_{rsk\ell}\right)D_{k\ell}

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-\frac{\beta}{n_{0}}C[f_{0}W_{mn}]\mu_{mnk\ell}D_{k\ell}.

(22)

Multiplying both sides of Eq. (
21
) by
W_{ij}
and integrating over
\bm{u}
then gives

\displaystyle T_{ijk\ell}=M_{ijmn}\mu_{mnk\ell}.

(23)

where

\displaystyle T_{ijk\ell}=\int d^{3}uf_{0}(\bm{u})W_{ij}W_{k\ell}=\frac{2n_{0}}{(m\beta)^{2}}{\cal I}_{ijk\ell},

(24a)

\displaystyle M_{ijmn}=-\frac{\beta}{n_{0}}\int d^{3}u\>W_{ij}{\cal C}[f_{0}W_{mn}].

(24b)

The
M_{ijmn}
integrals are particularly involved due to the highly anisotropic differential cross section of dipolar Bosons, for which the appropriately symmeterized scattering amplitude from Ref.
Bohn and Jin 2014
is given as

\displaystyle f_{B}\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{\prime}_{r},\hat{\bm{u}_{r}}\right)=\frac{a_{d}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{4}{3}-\frac{2a_{s}}{a_{d}}-\frac{2(\hat{\bm{u}_{r}}\cdot\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}})^{2}+2(\hat{\bm{u}}^{\prime}_{r}\cdot\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}})^{2}-4(\hat{\bm{u}_{r}}\cdot\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}})(\hat{\bm{u}}^{\prime}_{r}\cdot\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}})(\hat{\bm{u}_{r}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{\prime}_{r})}{1-(\hat{\bm{u}_{r}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{\prime}_{r})^{2}}\right),

(25)

where
a_{s}
is the
s
-wave scattering length,
\bm{u}_{\mathrm{r}}=\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{1}
is the relative peculiar velocity between colliding molecules, primes denote post-collision velocities and
\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}}
is the dipole alignment axis. This provides us the differential cross section via
{d\sigma/d\Omega^{\prime}}=\absolutevalue{ f_B \left( \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\prime}_r, \hat{\boldsymbol{u}_r} \right) }^{2}
, with which to compute
\bm{M}
.

After evaluating the integrals in Eq. (
24b
), as detailed in App.
A
, obtaining the
\bm{\mu}
tensor now requires us to invert
\bm{M}
, which is most easily performed by converting
\bm{M}
into its matrix representation denoted by an overhead circle,
\mathring{\bm{M}}
. This is done by mapping index pairs to single indices
(i,j)\rightarrow(i^{\prime})
, via the relation

\displaystyle i^{\prime}=3(j-1)+i,

(26)

rendering
M_{ijk\ell}\rightarrow\mathring{M}_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
. In its
9\times 9
matrix representation, the inherent symmetries of
\bm{M}
reduces its matrix rank from 9 to 5. This prevents us from inverting the matrix in its current representation, so we are now required to perform a change of basis transformation which decomposes the
9\times 9
matrix into a block-diagonal matrix with a
5\times 5
irreducible block. The desired change of basis matrix
\mathring{\bm{C}}
, is obtained by diagonalizing the isotropic tensor
\bm{{\cal I}}
in its matrix representation,

\displaystyle\mathring{\bm{{\cal I}}}=\mathring{\bm{C}}\left(\mathbb{I}_{5\times 5}\oplus\bm{0}_{4\times 4}\right)\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1},

(27)

where
\mathbb{I}
and
\bm{0}
are the identity and zero matrices respectively, with dimensions specified by their subscripts, and
\oplus
denotes a direct sum. Applying the transformation
\mathring{\bm{C}}
to Eq. (
23
) gives

\displaystyle\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{T}}\mathring{\bm{C}}

\displaystyle=\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\left(\mathring{\bm{M}}\mathring{\bm{\mu}}\right)\mathring{\bm{C}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{M}}\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\right)\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}\mathring{\bm{\mu}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right),

(28)

which leaves both sides of the equation above to only have a
5\times 5
non-trivial matrix block. The structure of these matrices are shown more explicitly by writing

\displaystyle\left[\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{T}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right]_{5\times 5}\oplus\bm{0}_{4\times 4}=

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\left[\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{M}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right)\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{\mu}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right)\right]_{5\times 5}\oplus\bm{0}_{4\times 4}.

(29)

The direct sum with
\bm{0}_{4\times 4}
is trivial, so we can just consider the
5\times 5
irreducible subspace. This now allows us to effectively invert
\mathring{\bm{M}}
by

\displaystyle\left[\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{\mu}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right]_{5\times 5}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{M}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right)\right]_{5\times 5}^{-1}\left[\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{T}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right)\right]_{5\times 5},

(30)

and taking the direct sum of the expression above with
\bm{0}_{4\times 4}
, to give

\displaystyle\mathring{\bm{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\mathring{\bm{C}}\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{M}}^{+}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right)\left(\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}\mathring{\bm{T}}\mathring{\bm{C}}\right)\mathring{\bm{C}}^{-1}=\mathring{\bm{M}}^{+}\mathring{\bm{T}},

(31)

where
\mathring{\bm{M}}^{+}
is a pseudo-inverse of
\mathring{\bm{M}}
defined by the procedure above, which satisfies
\mathring{\bm{M}}^{+}\mathring{\bm{M}}\mathring{\bm{\mu}}=\mathring{\bm{\mu}}
. Finally, we apply the inverse mapping of Eq. (
26
) to attain the rank-4 tensor form of
\bm{\mu}
. The explicit expressions for the viscosity coefficients are highly complicated, but tabulated in App.
A
as a function of the dipole angle
\Theta
, defined to be the angle between
\hat{\bm{{\cal E}}}
, and
\hat{\bm{z}}
in the
x,z
-plane.

II.5
Viscosities for Upright Dipoles

In the case where the dipole and
\hat{\bm{z}}
axes coincide (i.e.
\Theta=0
), we find that the stress tensor simplifies greatly and can be written in a form more familiar in the usual theory of viscosity.
That is, the stress tensor can be decomposed into a “
normal
” part which includes a proportionality with the symmetrized velocity gradients,

\displaystyle\tau^{0}_{ij}=2\mu^{0}_{ij}\circ D_{ij},

(32)

and 2 additional “
anomalous
” stress terms which modify the radial diagonal stresses

1
1

1

Such an anomalous stress also arises in nematic liquid crystals, where it is phenomenologically grouped together with the thermodynamic pressure to give an effective pressure
De Gennes and Prost 1993
.

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}_{11}=\tau^{\prime}_{22}=2\mu^{\prime}D_{33}.

(33)

The symbol
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product (the element-wise product in which repeated indices remain unsummed). The total stress is then written as the sum of the above two parts
\tau_{ij}=\tau^{0}_{ij}+\tau^{\prime}_{ij}
, with the corresponding viscosity coefficients in this representation given explicitly as

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{13}=\mu^{0}_{23}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2(\mathscr{A}_{2}-3\mathscr{A}_{1}-{4\tilde{a}_{d}^{2}/63})},

(34a)

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{12}=\mu^{0}_{11}=\mu^{0}_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2\mathscr{A}_{2}},

(34b)

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{33}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2\mathscr{A}_{2}}+\frac{2\mu_{0}\mathscr{A}_{1}}{\mathscr{A}_{2}(\mathscr{A}_{2}-4\mathscr{A}_{1})},

(34c)

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mu^{0}_{11}-\mu^{0}_{33}\right).

(34d)

Above, the
\bm{\mathscr{A}}
’s are polynomial functions of the reduced dipole length
\tilde{a}_{d}\equiv a_{d}/a_{s}
, in units of the scattering length

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{1}=\frac{4}{63}\tilde{a}_{d}^{2}-\frac{16}{21}\tilde{a}_{d},

(35a)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{2}=\frac{32}{63}\tilde{a}_{d}^{2}-\frac{32}{21}\tilde{a}_{d}+4.

(35b)

The dipole length is defined as
a_{d}=md^{2}/(8\pi\epsilon_{0}\hbar^{2})
where

is the electric dipole moment,
m
is the molecular mass and
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity. The viscosity coefficients are given in units of the Chapman-Enskog result

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\frac{5}{16a_{s}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{m}{\pi\beta}}.

(36)

corresponding to the viscosity of a gas of hard spheres with diameter
a_{s}

Chapman and Cowling 1990
.

We remark in passing that the stress tensor in this representation remains traceless, therefore the gas should not possess a bulk viscosity. This is a feature expected of monatomic gases in general. It applies here since, at ultralow temperatures, only the ground state of the molecule is accessible.

In the limit where the scattering length remains finite and the dipole length goes to zero, we get
\mu^{\prime}=0
and all the other coefficients reduce to the same value,

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{ij}(a_{d}=0)=\frac{\mu_{0}}{8}=\frac{5}{128a_{s}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{m}{\pi\beta}},

(37)

which is 8 times smaller than
\mu_{0}
since the
s
-wave scattering cross section in Bose gases is
8\pi a_{s}^{2}
(instead of
\pi a_{s}^{2}
for classical hard spheres), consistent with Ref.
Kavoulakis et al. 1998
.

\displaystyle\eta^{0}_{ij}(a_{d})\equiv\frac{\mu^{0}_{ij}(a_{d})}{\mu^{0}_{ij}(a_{d}=0)},\quad\eta^{\prime}(a_{d})\equiv\frac{\mu^{\prime}(a_{d})}{\mu^{0}_{ij}(a_{d}=0)},

\tilde{a}_{d}

On the microscopic level, the differential scattering of dipolar molecules exhibits a large anisotropy due to the dipole interaction itself, compounded by the interference of this scattering with the
s
-wave part characterized by a scattering length. The way in which this interference plays out is via a tremendous variation of the relative viscosity coefficients as
\tilde{a}_{d}
is varied. Indeed, the ratio of
\mu^{0}_{33}
to
\mu^{0}_{11}
can vary from 0.36 to 9.3 based on the value and sign of
a_{s}
. Relative viscosities are therefore highly tunable in laboratory experiments via the scattering length with blue-detuned circularly polarized microwaves
Lassablière and Quéméner 2018
(also tunable in Lanthanide atoms via the multitude of Fano-Feshbach resonances
Tang et al. 2015
;
Patscheider et al. 2022
), leading to phenomena yet to be explored.

III
Attenuation in Dipolar Gases

Having both the thermal conductivity and viscosity tensors now allows fluid dynamical studies in ultracold dipolar systems. As a first application, we analyze linear wave propagation through an initially uniform density gas. To do so, the density, flow velocity, and temperature variations are written in terms of fluctuations about their equilibrium distributions

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t)=\rho_{0}[1+\chi(\bm{r},t)],

(39a)

\displaystyle U_{i}(\bm{r},t)=v_{s}\xi_{i}(\bm{r},t),

(39b)

\displaystyle T(\bm{r},t)=T_{0}[1+\epsilon(\bm{r},t)].

(39c)

where
v_{s}=\sqrt{{5/(3m\beta_{0})}}
is the ideal gas speed of sound.
Plugging the form of Eq. (
39
) into Eq. (
1
) and assuming
\chi,\>\xi_{i},\>\epsilon\ll 1
, allows a linearization to first-order in the fluctuations which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}\approx-v_{s}{\partial_{j}\xi_{j}},

(40a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\xi_{i}}{\partial t}\approx-\frac{3}{5}v_{s}\partial_{i}(\epsilon+\chi)+\frac{3\beta_{0}v_{s}}{5n_{0}}\partial_{j}\tau_{ij},

(40b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial t}\approx-\frac{2}{3}v_{s}\partial_{j}\xi_{j}+\frac{2}{3n_{0}k_{B}}\kappa_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\epsilon,

(40c)

as detailed in App.
B
.

Solutions to Eqs. (
40
) are obtained by utilizing the planewave ansatz for each dynamical variable,
resulting in the system of equations

\displaystyle\omega\chi\approx v_{s}K_{j}\xi_{j},

(41a)

\displaystyle\omega\xi_{i}\approx\frac{3}{5}v_{s}K_{i}(\epsilon+\chi)-\frac{i}{\rho_{0}}\mu_{ijk\ell}K_{j}K_{\ell}\xi_{k},

(41b)

\displaystyle\omega\epsilon\approx\frac{2}{3}v_{s}K_{j}\xi_{j}-\frac{2i}{3n_{0}k_{B}}\kappa_{ij}K_{i}K_{j}\epsilon,

(41c)

Defining
thermal conductivity and viscosity associated rates

\displaystyle\Gamma=-\frac{2\kappa_{ij}}{3n_{0}k_{B}}K_{i}K_{j},

(42a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ik}=-\frac{\mu_{ijk\ell}}{\rho_{0}}K_{j}K_{\ell},

(42b)

we get the linear system above written as the eigenvalue matrix equation

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&v_{s}\bm{K}^{T}&0\\
\frac{3}{5}v_{s}\bm{K}&i\bm{\Lambda}&\frac{3}{5}v_{s}\bm{K}\\
0&\frac{2}{3}v_{s}\bm{K}^{T}&i\Gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\chi\\
\bm{\xi}\\
\epsilon\end{pmatrix}=\omega\begin{pmatrix}\chi\\
\bm{\xi}\\

(43)

A dispersion relation is then obtained via the characteristic polynomial of Eq. (
43

\lambda

\displaystyle\omega^{3}-i\omega^{2}[\Gamma+\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})]-\omega[v_{s}K]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{i}{5}v_{s}^{2}\big[K_{x}^{2}(3\Gamma+5(\Lambda_{22}+\Lambda_{33}))

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\>\>\>+K_{y}^{2}(3\Gamma+5(\Lambda_{11}+\Lambda_{33}))

IV
Conclusions and Outlooks

Recent experiments have demonstrated the ability to shield ultracold molecules from inelastic collisional losses, allowing long-lived dense samples of highly dipolar gases. As these molecular gases enter the hydrodynamic regime, a continuum model which includes the transport tensors of thermal conductivity and viscosity is warranted to describe the fluid dynamics. The thermal conductivities have been derived in Ref.
Wang and Bohn 2022
, and viscosities in this work, which now permit comprehensive phenomenological studies of Bosonic fluid systems that are enriched by dipolar anisotropy.

As a first analysis, we looked at how the dipole orientation dependence of our derived transport tensors carry over into the attenuation of linear waves generated by a constant frequency source. We find that attenuation is most pronounced when the directions of wave propagation and dipole alignment coincide (i.e.
\Theta=0
), whereas least attenuation occurs in the orthogonal configuration (i.e.
\Theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
). These results are illustrated with plots of the attenuation length and percentage extinction, that show a significant variation of these quantities with
\Theta
. Experiments which measure the attenuation length as a function of the dipole orientation could therefore provide an experimental means to extracting the anisotropic transport tensor coefficients.

In the future,
higher density corrections to the derived transport tensors can be included using the generalized Chapman-Enskog method
Chapman and Cowling 1990
;
Hoffman and Curtiss 1965
. This modification would result in the emergence of a bulk viscosity and explorations of hydrodynamic phenomena not present in the current theory.
At lower temperatures, the inclusion of quantum statistical effects
Uehling and Uhlenbeck 1933
to our derived transport tensors could permit a normal-superfluid phase coupled hydrodynamic model for dipolar systems, extending the formalism established by Zaremba, Griffin and Nikuni
Nikuni and Griffin 1998b
;
Zaremba et al. 1999
;
Nikuni et al. 2000
.
The mechanism to which dipolar gases crossover from the dilute to hydrodynamic regimes might also be of interest to the ultracold community, such as in the context of evaporative cooling. Such a theory would interpolate the formulation presented in this work with that in Refs.
Wang et al. 2020
;
Wang and Bohn 2021
.
Finally, the results of this work can also be extended to systems of Fermionic polar molecules and lanthanide atoms, by utilizing the scattering cross section for identical Fermions found in Ref.
Bohn and Jin 2014
.

Appendix B
Linearizing the Balance Equations

This section of the appendix details the linearization of the balance equations with the variational forms in Eq. (
39
). First, we shall take that the thermodynamic pressure to be given by the ideal gas law

\displaystyle P=\frac{n}{\beta}.

(61)

Then starting with the continuity equation, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\partial_{j}\left({\rho U_{j}}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}+\rho_{0}v_{s}\left({\partial_{j}\xi_{j}}+{\xi_{j}\partial_{j}\chi}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}+\sqrt{\frac{5}{3m\beta_{0}}}{\partial_{j}\xi_{j}}\approx 0.

(62)

The Navier-Stokes equation is then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho U_{i}\right)+\partial_{j}\left(\rho U_{j}U_{i}\right)=-\partial_{i}P+\partial_{j}\tau_{ij},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle v_{s}\rho_{0}\left((1+\chi)\frac{\partial\xi_{i}}{\partial t}+\xi_{i}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+v_{s}^{2}\rho_{0}\Big(\partial_{j}(\xi_{j}\xi_{i})+\xi_{j}\xi_{i}\partial_{j}\chi\Big)

\displaystyle\quad=-\frac{n_{0}}{\beta_{0}}(1+\epsilon)\partial_{i}\chi-\frac{n_{0}}{\beta_{0}}(1+\chi)\partial_{i}\epsilon+\partial_{j}\tau_{ij},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\xi_{i}}{\partial t}\approx-\sqrt{\frac{3}{5m\beta_{0}}}\partial_{i}(\epsilon+\chi)+\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{3\beta_{0}}{5m}}\partial_{j}\tau_{ij}.r_{i},

(63)

Finally, we have the energy balance equation as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho T)+\partial_{j}\left(\rho TU_{j}\right)=\frac{2m}{3k_{B}}(\sigma_{ij}\partial_{j}U_{i}-\partial_{j}J_{j}),

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\rho_{0}T_{0}\left((1+\chi)\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial t}+(1+\epsilon)\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+v_{s}\rho_{0}T_{0}\Big[(1+\chi)(1+\epsilon)\partial_{j}\xi_{j}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+(1+\chi)\xi_{j}\partial_{j}\epsilon+(1+\epsilon)\xi_{j}\partial_{j}\chi\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{2m}{3k_{B}}(v_{s}\sigma_{ij}\partial_{j}\xi_{i}-\partial_{j}J_{j}),

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\epsilon+\chi\right)+\frac{5}{3}\sqrt{\frac{5}{3m\beta_{0}}}\partial_{j}\xi_{j}\approx-\frac{2}{3}\frac{\beta_{0}}{n_{0}}\partial_{j}J_{j},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{5}{3m\beta_{0}}}\partial_{j}\xi_{j}\approx-\frac{2}{3}\frac{\beta_{0}}{n_{0}}\partial_{j}J_{j}.

(64)

In summary these grant us the closed set of equations in Eq. (
40
) of the main text.
