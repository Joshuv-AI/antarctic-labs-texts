Improved Rates of Bootstrap Approximation for the Operator Norm: A Coordinate-Free Approach
Miles E. Lopes

Abstract
Let Σ ^ = 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n X i ⊗ X i \widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\otimes X_{i} denote the sample covariance operator of centered i.i.d. observations X 1 , … , X n X_{1},\dots,X_{n} in a real separable Hilbert space, and let Σ = E ​ ( X 1 ⊗ X 1 ) \Sigma=\textup{{E}}(X_{1}\otimes X_{1}) . The focus of this paper is to understand how well the bootstrap can approximate the distribution of the operator norm error n ​ ‖ Σ ^ − Σ ‖ op \sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}} , in settings where the eigenvalues of Σ \Sigma decay as λ j ​ ( Σ ) ≍ j − 2 ​ β \lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\asymp j^{-2\beta} for some fixed parameter β > 1 / 2 \beta>1/2 . Our main result shows that the bootstrap can approximate the distribution of n ​ ‖ Σ ^ − Σ ‖ op \sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}} at a rate of order n − β − 1 / 2 2 ​ β + 4 + ϵ n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}} with respect to the Kolmogorov metric, for any fixed ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 . In particular, this shows that the bootstrap can achieve near n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} rates in the regime of large β \beta —which substantially improves on previous near n − 1 / 6 n^{-1/6} rates in the same regime. In addition to obtaining faster rates, our analysis leverages a fundamentally different perspective based on coordinate-free techniques. Moreover, our result holds in greater generality, and we propose a model that is compatible with both elliptical and Marčenko-Pastur models in high-dimensional Euclidean spaces, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be centered i.i.d. observations in a real separable Hilbert space
(\mathbb{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, and let the sample and population covariance operators be denoted respectively as
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\otimes X_{i}
and
\Sigma=\textup{{E}}(X_{1}\otimes X_{1})
. In the fields of high-dimensional statistics and functional data analysis, many strands of research are connected to the study of the operator norm error, denoted

T_{n}=\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}.

Indeed, the operator norm is a standard measure of error in the extensive literature on covariance estimation
(
Rudelson 1999
;
Bickel and Levina 2008
;
Cai, Zhang and Zhou 2010
;
Adamczak et al. 2011
;
Lounici 2014
;
Bunea and Xiao 2015
;
Koltchinskii and
Lounici 2017a
;
Minsker 2017
;
Zhivotovskiy 2023
, e.g.)
.
Furthermore, the statistic
T_{n}
plays an important role in principal components analysis, where it appears frequently in various error bounds for sample eigenvalues and eigenvectors
(e.g.
(
Bosq 2000
, §4.2)
,
(
Yu, Wang and Samworth 2014
)
). More broadly still, the statistic
T_{n}
is closely related to errors that arise in the analysis of randomized sketching algorithms for large-scale matrix computations
(
Martinsson and Tropp 2020
;
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
;
Yao, Erichson and Lopes 2023
, e.g.)
.

Despite the prevalence of the statistic
T_{n}
, the standard tools for approximating its distribution have some essential limitations.
At a high level, the bulk of existing results describing
T_{n}
can be organized into two types. In the direction of high-dimensional statistics, the large body of work on covariance estimation has developed non-asymptotic tail bounds for
T_{n}
, as cited earlier. Alternatively, in the direction of functional data analysis, the relevant theory has focused mainly on the limiting distribution for the operator
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)
as
n\to\infty
, which implicitly describes
T_{n}
via the continuous mapping theorem
(
Dauxois, Pousse and
Romain 1982
;
Bosq 2000
;
Mas 2002
;
Mas 2006
;
Hsing and Eubank 2015
;
Paparoditis and
Sapatinas 2016
, e.g.)
.

While both of these types of results yield valuable insights, they do not provide a complete understanding of the fluctuations of
T_{n}
. For instance, asymptotic distributional approximations generally do not provide much guidance about how quickly accuracy improves as
n\to\infty
. Meanwhile, non-asymptotic tail bounds are often impractical for approximating the numerical values of tail probabilities, because such bounds are usually stated in terms of unspecified or conservative constants. On this point, it is worth clarifying that non-asymptotic distributional approximations often also involve unspecified constants, but they are less of a practical concern. To illustrate the difference, a tail bound for
T_{n}
typically has the form
\textup{{P}}(T_{n}>t)\leq Cb(t,n,\Sigma)
, where
b
is a function and
C
is a constant. By contrast, non-asymptotic distributional approximation results often show that an event of the form
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\textup{{P}}(T_{n}\leq t)-\widehat{F}_{n}(t)|\leq Cb(n,\Sigma)
holds with high probability, where
\widehat{F}_{n}
is an estimate for the distribution function of
T_{n}
. Hence, in the latter context, the constant
C
plays an essentially theoretical role since it does not interfere with the practical use of
\widehat{F}_{n}(t)
, whereas the tail bound
Cb(t,n,\Sigma)
cannot be applied numerically without specifying a particular value of
C
.

In light of these issues, there is substantial interest in establishing
rates
of distributional approximation. Recently, this interest has stimulated a line of work on rates of bootstrap and Gaussian approximations for the distributions of
T_{n}
and other statistics related to
\widehat{\Sigma}

(
Koltchinskii and
Lounici 2017b
;
Han, Xu and Zhou 2018
;
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
;
Naumov, Spokoiny and
Ulyanov 2019
;
Silin and Fan 2020
;
Yao and Lopes 2023
;
Jirak and Wahl 2022
)
. Here, we only discuss the papers
(
Han, Xu and Zhou 2018
;
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
from this list, since they deal specifically with
T_{n}
, whereas the others deal with qualitatively different statistics arising from
\widehat{\Sigma}
. When the data are
p
-dimensional Euclidean vectors, i.e.
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the papers
(
Han, Xu and Zhou 2018
;
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
establish non-asymptotic high-probability upper bounds on
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\textup{{P}}(T_{n}\leq t)-\textup{{P}}(T_{n}^{*}\leq t|X)|
, where
T_{n}^{*}
is a bootstrap version of
T_{n}
, and
\textup{{P}}(\cdot\,|X)
refers to probability that is conditional on
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. In a setting where the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
are not assumed to have any decay structure, the paper
(
Han, Xu and Zhou 2018
, Theorem 2.3)
obtains a bound of order
p^{9/8}/n^{1/8}
, up to factors of
\log(n)
. On the other hand, in the distinct setting where the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
decay according to
\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\asymp j^{-2\beta}
for some fixed parameter
\beta>1/2
, the paper
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
, Theorem 2.1)
obtains a bound of order
n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{6\beta+4}}
, up to factors of
\log(n)
.

Although the result in
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
has the virtue of being applicable to very high-dimensional data with
p\gg n
, it still has several key drawbacks—which the current paper seeks to overcome. Most notably, the rate
n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{6\beta+4}}
can
never be faster than
n^{-1/6}
, for any value of
\beta>1/2
. At a heuristic level, such a rate seems to leave substantial room for improvement, because if
\beta
is large, then the data will be concentrated near a low-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{H}
, which should seemingly enable the bootstrap to achieve rates of approximation that are near
n^{-1/2}
. Another drawback is that the result in
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
requires the data to take values in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, which is incongruous with the fact that the result is dimension-free. In particular, the result cannot be applied to standard types of functional data that reside in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Contributions.
In relation to the points just discussed, the main contributions of this paper are briefly summarized as follows:

1.

Under conditions more general than those in
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
, our main result gives a high-probability upper bound on
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\textup{{P}}(T_{n}\leq t)-\textup{{P}}(T_{n}^{*}\leq t|X)|
that is of order
n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
. In particular, this rate is near
n^{-1/2}
in the regime of large
\beta
, and in fact, it is faster than
n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{6\beta+4}}
for every value of
\beta>1/2
.

2.

We allow the observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
to take values in an abstract separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. Our setup also allows
\mathbb{H}
to vary with
n
, which makes it possible to handle both functional data and high-dimensional data, with
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
p\gg n
, in a unified way.

3.

We develop a data-generating model that is compatible with both Marčenko-Pastur models and elliptical models in high dimensions (see Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
). This model has the favorable property that it can be defined by a concise set of assumptions within the setting of an abstract separable Hilbert space. Likewise, this model may be of independent interest in both high-dimensional statistics and functional data analysis.

4.

Whereas the papers
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
and
(
Han, Xu and Zhou 2018
)
represent
T_{n}
as
\sup_{\|u\|=1}|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle u,X_{i}\rangle^{2}-\textup{{E}}\langle u,X_{i}\rangle^{2}|
and analyze it as a supremum of an empirical process,
our work takes a fundamentally different perspective based on
coordinate-free techniques
, as explained below.

Proof techniques.
Our approach begins with a preliminary dimension-reduction step, based on an approximation of the form
\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}\approx\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top})\|_{\textup{op}}
, where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
k
may increase with
n
. Once this has been done, the first key idea is to view
Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top},\dots,Y_{n}Y_{n}^{\top}
as
vectors
of dimension
\frac{1}{2}k(k+1)
, rather than as symmetric matrices of size
k\times k
. The immediate benefit of this viewpoint is that it enables us to leverage well-developed Berry-Esseen bounds that can handle general norms of sums of Euclidean vectors
(
Fang and Koike 2020
;
Bentkus 2003
, e.g.)
.

However, the price to pay for this benefit is that we need to deal with the covariance matrix of the “vector”
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top})
. Here, it is crucial to notice that when this sum is viewed as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{k(k+1)/2}
, its covariance matrix is a complicated object. Indeed, if we let
\mathsf{K}
denote the covariance matrix of
VV^{\top}
for a generic random vector
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, then the matrix
\mathsf{K}
is of size
\frac{1}{2}k(k+1)\times\frac{1}{2}k(k+1)
and has entries that are indexed by 4-tuples
(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})\in\{1,\dots,k\}^{4}
. To make matters even more challenging, we will sometimes need to work with the inverse
\mathsf{K}^{-1}
, which is prohibitive when using an entrywise representation of
\mathsf{K}
.

The second key idea is to recognize the advantages of coordinate-free techniques. That is, rather than thinking of
\mathsf{K}
as a matrix, we think of it as an abstract linear operator.
To explain this more precisely, first let
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
denote the Hilbert space of symmetric matrices of size
k\times k
, equipped with the inner product
\llangle A,B\rrangle=\operatorname{tr}(AB)
. Then, we can define
\mathsf{K}
in a coordinate-free manner as the linear operator from
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
to
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
that satisfies

\llangle A,\mathsf{K}(B)\rrangle\ =\ \textup{cov}(\llangle A,VV^{\top}\rrangle,\llangle B,VV^{\top}\rrangle)

(1.1)

for all
A,B\in\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
, where
\textup{cov}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the usual covariance for scalar random variables
(
Eaton 1983
)
.
The main advantage of this viewpoint is that it avoids the complexity of
\mathsf{K}
at an entrywise level. In addition, there are two other further advantages. First, it turns out that much of the information we need to extract from the inverse
\mathsf{K}^{-1}
can be obtained by minimizing and maximizing the function
A\mapsto\llangle A,\mathsf{K}(A)\rrangle
over certain subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. Second, the inner product
\llangle A,\mathsf{K}(A)\rrangle
is often amenable to direct calculation, since it is the same as the variance of a quadratic form,
\operatorname{var}(V^{\top}AV)
. This being said, we should also clarify that our use of coordinate-free techniques is more utilitarian than aesthetic, and we do not hesitate to use coordinates in other aspects of our work.

One more item to expand on is the dimension-reduction step mentioned earlier. This involves projecting
\mathbb{H}
-valued data into
k
-dimensional Euclidean spaces with
k\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, which is an essential technique in many aspects of functional data analysis
(
Ramsay and Silverman 1997
;
Bosq 2000
;
Cai and Hall 2006
;
Horváth and Kokoszka 2012
;
Fremdt et al. 2014
, e.g.)
. In our work, this technique allows us to analyze
\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top})\|_{\textup{op}}
as a convenient surrogate for
\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}
(see Lemma
5.1
).
Perhaps the main point of contrast between our use this technique and more common uses in functional data analysis is that we focus on the operator norm. This is because the Frobenius (Hilbert-Schmidt) norm tends to play a more dominant role in the theory of functional data. Another notable feature of our work is that we account for the projected dimension
k
in a quantitative manner, which is made possible by results from high-dimensional probability. In this respect, our main tool is Lemma
9.2
(established in
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
, Theorem 2.2)
), which provides a bound on the
q
th moment
\textup{{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}
in the general setting of separable Hilbert spaces and allows
q\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. At the same time, it should be emphasized that this bound on
\textup{{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}
is but one of many related results
(
Mendelson and Pajor 2005
;
Mendelson and Pajor 2006
;
Oliveira 2010
;
Tropp 2012
;
Koltchinskii and
Lounici 2017a
, e.g.)
that developed from the seminal work
(
Rudelson 1999
)
based on non-commutative Khintchine inequalities.

Notation and terminology.
The Hilbert norm on
\mathbb{H}
is denoted
\|\cdot\|
, and the direct sum of
\mathbb{H}
with itself is denoted
\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}
. The tensor product
x\otimes y
of any two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{H}
is defined as the linear operator that satisfies
(x\otimes y)z=\langle y,z\rangle x
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
. All Euclidean vectors are regarded as column vectors, except when they are appended with a
\top
.
When
x
and
y
are Euclidean vectors, we will use
x\otimes y
and
xy^{\top}
interchangeably, depending on notational convenience. As an important clarification, the expression
A\otimes B
does
not
denote the Kronecker product of
A,B\in\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. Rather, we use
A\otimes B
to denote the tensor product of
A
and
B
, viewed as elements of the Hilbert space
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. When referring to the Kronecker product of
A
and
B
, we will write
A\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}B
, which is the linear operator on
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
that satisfies
(A\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}B)C=ACB
for all
C\in\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. Additional background on coordinate-free definitions of tensor and Kronecker products in the context of multivariate statistics can be found in
(
Eaton 1983
)
.

If
\mathsf{L}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is a linear operator, then its operator norm is defined by
\|\mathsf{L}\|_{\textup{op}}=\sup_{\|u\|=1}\|\mathsf{L}u\|
, and its Frobenius norm is defined by
\|\mathsf{L}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j}\|\mathsf{L}\phi_{j}\|^{2}
, where
\{\phi_{j}\}
is any orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{H}
. If
\mathsf{L}
is compact and positive-semidefinite, or if
\mathsf{L}
is self-adjoint and
{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})<\infty
, then we write
\lambda_{1}(\mathsf{L})\geq\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{L})\geq\cdots
for the sorted eigenvalues, with
\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{L})
and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{L})
denoting the maximum and minimum eigenvalues in the latter case. In the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the standard basis vectors are
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
, and the ordinary Euclidean norm is
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. For real numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
, we define
\textsf{Diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
as the
k\times k
diagonal matrix whose
j
th diagonal entry is
a_{j}
. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
, we define
\textsf{Diag}(A)
as the
k\times k
diagonal matrix obtained by setting the off-diagonal entries of
A
to 0. The identity matrix is denoted by
I
, with its size being understood from context.

If
U
is a scalar random variable, then its
L^{q}
norm is defined by
\|U\|_{L^{q}}=(\textup{{E}}|U|^{q})^{1/q}
for any
q\geq 1
. The basic inequality
\textup{{P}}(|U|\geq e\|U\|_{L^{q}})\leq e^{-q}
will often be referred to as Chebyshev’s inequality. The expression
\mathcal{L}(U)
refers to the distribution of
U
. If
V
is another scalar random variable, then
U\overset{{\scriptsize\mathcal{L}}}{=}V
means that
U
and
V
are equal in distribution. As a more compact notation for the Kolmogorov metric, we write
d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(U),\mathcal{L}(V))=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\textup{{P}}(U\leq t)-\textup{{P}}(V\leq t)|
. A random vector is said to be isotropic if its covariance matrix is equal to the identity matrix.

If
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
are sequences of non-negative real numbers, the relation
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
means that there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that
a_{n}\leq c\,b_{n}
holds for all large
n
. If both of the relations
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
hold, then we write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
. When introducing constants that do not depend on
n
, we will often re-use the symbol
c
, so that its value may change at each appearance. For the maximum and minimum of a pair of real numbers
a
and
b
, we write
a\vee b=\max\{a,b\}
and
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
.

2
Main result

Our work will be done with a sequence of models implicitly embedded in a triangular array, whose rows are indexed by
n
. The space
\mathbb{H}
and all model parameters are allowed to vary freely with
n
, except when stated otherwise. Note that in the case when
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, this means
p
is a function of
n
. Hence, if a constant
c
does not depend on
n
, then
c
does not depend on
p
either. To handle the finite and infinite-dimensional settings in a streamlined way, we let
\mathcal{J}=\{1,\dots,{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})\}
when
{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})<\infty
, and we let
\mathcal{J}=\{1,2,\dots\}
when
{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})=\infty
. Within this basic setup, our main model assumptions are stated below.

Assumption 2.1

(Data generating model)
.

The random vectors
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{H}
are centered and i.i.d., with a compact covariance operator
\Sigma=\textup{{E}}(X_{1}\otimes X_{1})
whose spectral decomposition is
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\phi_{j}\otimes\phi_{j}
, with
\|\phi_{j}\|=1
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
.

(a).

There is a parameter
\nu>0
, fixed with respect to
n
, such that the random variables
\{\zeta_{1j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
defined by
\zeta_{1j}=\langle X_{1},\phi_{j}\rangle/\|\langle X_{1},\phi_{j}\rangle\|_{L^{2}}
satisfy

\sup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\,\sup_{q\geq 2}\,q^{-\nu}\|\zeta_{1j}\|_{L^{q}}\ \lesssim\ 1.

(b).

There is a parameter
\beta>1/2
, fixed with respect to
n
, such that the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
satisfy

1\ \lesssim\ \inf_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)j^{2\beta}\ \leq\ \displaystyle\sup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\textstyle\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)j^{2\beta}\ \lesssim\ 1.

(c).

For each
d\in\mathcal{J}
,
let
{\mathsf{J}}_{d}:\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
be the covariance operator of
(\zeta_{11},\dots,\zeta_{1d})\otimes(\zeta_{11},\dots,\zeta_{1d})
, viewed as a random element of
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
.
Then,

1\ \lesssim\ \inf_{d\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})\ \leq\ \sup_{d\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{\max}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})\ \lesssim\ 1.

Remarks.
The random variables
\{\zeta_{1j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
are often referred to as the standardized principal component scores of
X_{1}
, which appear in the Karhunen-Loève expansion
X_{1}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)}\zeta_{1j}\phi_{j}

(
Hsing and Eubank 2015
;
Bosq 2000
;
Horváth and Kokoszka 2012
)
. More generally, this expansion serves as a framework for formulating model assumptions in functional data analysis
(
Jacques and Preda 2014
;
Yao, Müller and Wang 2005
;
Jirak and Wahl 2023
, e.g.)
.
With regard to the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
, decay constraints akin to condition (b) are especially common
(
Hall and Horowitz 2007
;
Cai and Hall 2006
, e.g.)
, and the condition
\beta>1/2
is a natural enhancement of the basic inequality
\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\leq\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)j^{-1}
that holds for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
. The main novelty of Assumption
2.1
is condition (c), which allows for a variety of high-dimensional and infinite-dimensional models to be handled in a unified way, as will be explained in Section
3
.

As one more preliminary item, we must define the bootstrap version of the sample covariance operator. For this purpose, we will focus on the non-parametric (or empirical) bootstrap
(
Efron 1979
)
, which generates
X_{1}^{*},\dots,X_{n}^{*}
by sampling with replacement from
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, and then uses the matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{*}\otimes X_{i}^{*}
to produce
\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{\textup{op}}
as a bootstrap sample of the original statistic
\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}
.

The following theorem is our main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds. Then, there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the event

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\textup{{P}}\Big(\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}\leq t\Big)-\textup{{P}}\Big(\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{\textup{op}}\leq t\,\Big|X\Big)\bigg|\ \leq\ c\,n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}

(2.1)

holds with probability at least
1-c/n
.

Remarks.
Although the constant
c
does not depend on
n
, it should be noted that
c
may depend on
\epsilon
,
\beta
,
\nu
, as well as constants that are implicit in the expression
\lesssim 1
in Assumption
2.1
. The proof is outlined in Section
4
, and the detailed arguments are given in subsequent sections. Regarding the quantity
\beta-1/2
in the exponent of the bound, the numerical experiments in
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
provide strong evidence that this quantity is essential. Those experiments demonstrate that if
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and if both
p
and
n
are large, then there is a sharp transition in the performance of the bootstrap, from accurate to inaccurate, depending on whether
\beta>1/2
or
\beta<1/2
. Hence, this suggests that the assumption
\beta>1/2
is unavoidable for ensuring bootstrap consistency in high dimensions, and that the rate in Theorem
2.1
behaves correctly in the extreme case when
\beta\approx 1/2
. Meanwhile, for the other extreme case
\beta\approx\infty
, the rate is near
n^{-1/2}
, which appears ideal considering that a
n^{-1/2}
rate is generally unimprovable in the univariate Berry-Esseen theorem. In the intermediate regime
1/2\ll\beta\ll\infty
, we leave the problem of determining the optimal rate of bootstrap approximation for future work. Another related open question is whether the rate can be formulated in a way that does not depend on the decay profile
\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\asymp j^{-2\beta}
. That is, it would be of interest to express the rate in terms of spectral parameters that can be associated with
\Sigma
in greater generality. A prototypical example of such a parameter is the effective rank
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)/\|\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}
, which naturally respects the scale-invariance of the Kolmogorov metric, and is known to be a key determinant of the magnitude of
\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}

(
Koltchinskii and
Lounici 2017a
)
. Nevertheless, in the context of bootstrap approximation, it is unclear to what extent this or other parameters may be needed.

Looking towards other possible extensions of Theorem
2.1
, it would be of interest to determine the extent to which similar rates of approximation can be developed for resampling methods other than the non-parametric bootstrap.
Some examples include “weighted” or “multiplier” bootstraps, which are often defined so that
\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{\textup{op}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}X_{i}\otimes X_{i}\|_{\textup{op}}
for certain types of multiplier random variables
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}

(
Han, Xu and Zhou 2018
;
Naumov, Spokoiny and
Ulyanov 2019
;
Jirak and Wahl 2022
;
Yu, Zhao and Zhou 2022
;
Ding et al. 2023
)
. This reduces to the non-parametric bootstrap when
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=(M_{1}-1,\dots,M_{n}-1)
, with
(M_{1},\dots,M_{n})
being drawn from a Multinomial
(n,\frac{1}{n},\dots,\frac{1}{n})
distribution, independently of the data. Among other possible choices for the multipliers, some well-known options are to generate
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
as i.i.d.
N(0,1)
or Rademacher random variables, independently of the data. Beyond these examples, parametric bootstrap methods can also be considered,
which generate
X_{1}^{*},\dots,X_{n}^{*}
from a parametric distribution that has been fitted to the data, and then use
\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{\textup{op}}
with
\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{*}\otimes X_{i}^{*}
. In recent years, parametric bootstrap methods have shown promise in solving inference problems related to high-dimensional covariance matrices, as these methods are able to directly account for certain model structures
(
Lopes, Blandino and
Aue 2019
;
Wang and Lopes 2023
;
Yu, Xie and Zhou 2023
)
.

3
Examples of conforming models

This section presents a variety of models that satisfy Assumption
2.1
, in both the infinite and high-dimensional settings.

3.1
Infinite dimensions

Gaussian models.
In the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on the unit interval,
\mathbb{H}=L^{2}[0,1]
, there are several fundamental Gaussian distributions that satisfy all aspects of Assumption
2.1
. In fact, conditions
2.1
(a) and (c) hold for any centered Gaussian distribution on
L^{2}[0,1]
, regardless of the covariance structure. To see this, first note that if
X_{1}
is a centered Gaussian random element, then the sequence
\{\zeta_{1j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
consists of i.i.d.
N(0,1)
random variables, which implies that the moment condition
2.1
(a) holds with
\nu=1/2

(
Vershynin 2018
, p. 25)
. Also, this property of
\{\zeta_{1j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
implies that

\mathsf{J}_{d}
is equal to twice the identity operator for each
d\in\mathcal{J}
, and hence condition
2.1
(c) holds with
\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{J}_{d})=\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{J}_{d})=2
. (The calculation for verifying this is a special case of equation (
3.6
) in the proof of Proposition
3.2
below.) Lastly, the spectrum decay condition
2.1
(b) is satisfied by several familiar Gaussian processes on [0,1], such as standard versions of Brownian motion, the Brownian bridge, and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which all correspond to
\beta=1
. Moreoever, by considering integrated Gaussian processes, it is possible to develop examples corresponding to any integer
\beta>1
. For further details about the eigenvalues associated with these processes, we refer to
(
Ritter 2000
, Ch. 4 pp. 70-71, and Prop. 10)
.

Non-Gaussian models.
Let us return to the general situation where
\mathbb{H}
is only assumed to be separable. Here, a model can be specified by defining the observations to be of the form
X_{1}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)}\zeta_{1j}\phi_{j}
for a sequence of uncorrelated and standardized random variables
\{\zeta_{1j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
, while allowing
\{\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
to be any sequence that satisfies condition
2.1
(b), and allowing
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
to be any orthonormal basis. For condition
2.1
(a) to hold, it is sufficient for the random variables
\{\zeta_{1j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
to be i.i.d. copies of
\zeta_{11}
, where the latter random variable is centered with unit variance and satisfies
\sup_{q\geq 2}q^{-\nu}\|\zeta_{11}\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim 1
for some
\nu>0
that is fixed with respect to
n
. Notably, this moment condition is weaker than
\zeta_{11}
having a moment generating function, because if
\zeta_{11}
does have a moment generating function, then the condition holds with
\nu=1

(
Vershynin 2018
, Prop. 2.7.1)
.
Lastly, condition
2.1
(c) holds if the random variable
\zeta_{11}
has one additional property, which is that
\operatorname{var}(\zeta_{11}^{2})\gtrsim 1
. This conclusion is reached in two steps, by first considering the formula
\llangle A,\mathsf{J}_{d}(A)\rrangle=2\|A\|_{F}^{2}+(\operatorname{var}(\zeta_{11}^{2})-2)\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{jj}^{2}
, which holds for all
A\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
and all
d\in\mathcal{J}
when
\{\zeta_{1j}\}
are i.i.d. copies of
\zeta_{11}

(
Bai and
Silverstein 2010
, eqn. 9.8.6)
. In turn, by maximizing and minimizing this formula for
\llangle A,\mathsf{J}_{d}(A)\rrangle
over the set of symmetric matrices with
\|A\|_{F}=1
, the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{J}_{d}
can be bounded away from zero and infinity via the calculations (
3.6
) through (
3.8
) in the proof of Proposition
3.2
below (while taking
d=p
and
\Phi=I
in the notation used there).

3.2
High dimensions

For data that reside in a Euclidean space
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, two of the most popular types of models in high-dimensional statistics and random matrix theory are elliptical models and Marčenko-Pastur models. Since both types allow for the spectral decay condition
2.1
(b), it will only be necessary to show that they are compatible with conditions
2.1
(a) and (c). For simplicity, we only define these models in the case of centered random vectors. It is also important to note that our high-dimensional examples do not constrain the size of
p
with respect to
n
.

Elliptical models.
A random vector
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to follow an elliptical model if there exists a non-random positive semidefinite matrix
M\in\mathbb{S}^{p\times p}
and a random vector
Z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

X_{1}\ \overset{}{=}\ MZ_{1}
,
where
Z_{1}
has the properties that
\textup{{E}}(Z_{1}Z_{1}^{\top})=I
, and
QZ_{1}\overset{{\scriptsize\mathcal{L}}}{=}Z_{1}
for any non-random orthogonal matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. More extensive background on elliptical models can be found in
(
Anderson 2003
;
Muirhead 2009
)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose the random vector
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
follows an elliptical model with a positive definite covariance matrix. Then, condition
2.1
(a) holds when
\sup_{q\geq 2}q^{-\nu}\|Z_{11}\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim 1
for some constant
\nu>0
not depending on
n
, and condition
2.1
(c) holds when
\textup{{P}}(Z_{1}=0)=0
and
\operatorname{var}(\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{2})\asymp\textup{{E}}(\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{2})
.

Remarks.
The proof is given in Section
3.3
. The condition
\operatorname{var}(\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{2})\asymp\textup{{E}}(\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{2})
appears elsewhere in connection with high-dimensional sample covariance matrices, such as in
(
Hu et al. 2019
;
Wang and Lopes 2023
)
, where it is used to analyze the limit laws of linear spectral statistics in elliptical models. To provide some concrete examples for which the conditions of Proposition
3.1
hold, consider a random vector of the form
Z_{1}=\eta_{1}U_{1}
, where
U_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere, and
\eta_{1}
is a positive random variable that is independent of
U_{1}
. If the square of
\eta_{1}
can be represented as
\eta_{1}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\eta_{1j}^{2}
for some positive i.i.d. random variables
\eta_{11},\dots,\eta_{1p}
satisfying
\textup{{E}}(\eta_{11}^{2})=1
,
\operatorname{var}(\eta_{11}^{2})\gtrsim 1
, and
\sup_{q\geq 2}q^{-\tilde{\nu}}\|\eta_{11}^{2}\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim 1
for some fixed
\tilde{\nu}>0
that does not depend on
n
, then
Z_{1}
satisfies all the conditions in the proposition with
\nu=(\tilde{\nu}+1)/2
. This class of distributions for
\eta_{1}^{2}
includes familiar parametric distributions such as Chi-Squared
(p)
, Gamma
(p,1)
, and
\tau\cdot\,
Negative-Binomial
(p,\tau)
for any fixed
\tau\in(0,1)
.

More generally, it is natural to look for examples of conforming elliptical models when
\mathbb{H}
is infinite-dimensional. (In the context of an abstract separable Hilbert space, a random element is said to be elliptical if all of its one-dimensional projections are elliptical random variables, as defined above for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
p=1

(
Boente, Barrera and Tyler 2014
, Definition 2.2)
.) However, it turns out that when
\mathbb{H}
is infinite-dimensional, the class elliptical distributions is much more restricted in a certain sense as compared to the finite-dimensional case. In particular, if
X_{1}
is elliptical and has a covariance operator with infinite rank, then
X_{1}
must arise from a scale mixture of Gaussian distributions
(
Boente, Barrera and Tyler 2014
, Prop. 2.1)
. That is,
X_{1}
must be representable in the form
X_{1}=\eta_{1}V_{1}
, where
V_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
is a centered Gaussian random element, and
\eta_{1}
is a non-negative random variable that is independent of
V_{1}
. This contrasts sharply with the finite-dimensional setting, where many elliptical distributions are not of this type, such as the uniform distribution on the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Also, it is possible to check that if
\mathbb{H}
is infinite-dimensional, then a scale mixture of Gaussian distributions can only satisfy Assumption
2.1
(c) when the mixing variable
\eta_{1}
is a constant, resulting in a Gaussian distribution. (See the remark following the proof of Proposition
3.3
below for details.)

Marčenko-Pastur models.
A random vector
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to follow a Marčenko-Pastur model if the following holds: There is a non-random positive semidefinite matrix
M\in\mathbb{S}^{p\times p}
and a random vector
Z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
having i.i.d. entries with
\textup{{E}}(Z_{11})=0
and
\operatorname{var}(Z_{11})=1
, such that

X_{1}\ \overset{}{=}\ MZ_{1}
.
We refer to
(
Bai and
Silverstein 2010
;
Couillet and Debbah 2011
)
for additional details on these models, which are sometimes referred to as “separable” or “independent component” models.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose the random vector
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
follows a Marčenko-Pastur model with a positive definite covariance matrix. Then, condition
2.1
(a) holds when
\sup_{q\geq 2}q^{-\nu}\|Z_{11}\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim 1
for some constant
\nu>0
not depending on
n
, and condition
2.1
(c) holds when
\operatorname{var}(Z_{11}^{2})\asymp 1
.

Remarks.
The proof is given in Section
3.4
. To see that the condition
\operatorname{var}(Z_{11}^{2})\asymp 1
is quite mild, note that the lower bound
\operatorname{var}(Z_{11}^{2})\gtrsim 1
serves to avoid the extreme case when
Z_{11}
is a Rademacher variable. That is, if
Z_{11}
is standardized and
\operatorname{var}(Z_{11}^{2})=0
, then
Z_{11}
must take the values
\pm 1
with equal probability.

3.3
Proof of Proposition
3.1

To start, it is helpful to recognize that the matrix
M
is the same as
\Sigma^{1/2}
. Also, for any
d\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, let
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
be the matrix whose columns are the leading
d
eigenvectors
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d}
of
\Sigma
, and let
D=\textsf{Diag}(\lambda_{1}(\Sigma),\dots,\lambda_{d}(\Sigma))
. By the definition of
(\zeta_{11},\dots,\zeta_{1d})
in Assumption
2.1
, multiplying both sides of the equation
X_{1}=MZ_{1}
with the matrix
D^{-1/2}\Phi^{\top}
implies

(\zeta_{11},\dots,\zeta_{1d})=\Phi^{\top}Z_{1}.

(3.1)

Furthermore, due to the orthogonal invariance of
Z_{1}
, it follows that
\Phi^{\top}Z_{1}\overset{{\scriptsize\mathcal{L}}}{=}(Z_{11},\dots,Z_{1d})
, and that all the coordinates of
Z_{1}
have the same distribution. Therefore, condition
2.1
(a) is implied by
\sup_{q\geq 2}q^{-\nu}\|Z_{11}\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim 1
.

To address condition
2.1
(c), the relation (
3.1
) implies that
{\mathsf{J}}_{d}
is also the covariance operator of
(\Phi^{\top}Z_{1})(\Phi^{\top}Z_{1})^{\top}
, and so the formula

\begin{split}\llangle A,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle&\ =\ \ \operatorname{var}(\langle\Phi^{\top}Z_{1},A\Phi^{\top}Z_{1}\rangle)\end{split}

(3.2)

holds for any
A\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
. Next, the orthogonal invariance of
Z_{1}
with
\textup{{P}}(Z_{1}=0)=0
implies that the random variable
\|Z_{1}\|_{2}
and the random vector
Z_{1}/\|Z_{1}\|_{2}
are independent, with
Z_{1}/\|Z_{1}\|_{2}
being uniformly distributed on the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{p}

(
Muirhead 2009
, Theorem 1.5.6)
. Based on this, if we let
r_{p}=\frac{1}{p(p+2)}\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{4}
, and calculate the variance in (
3.2
) using
(
Hu et al. 2019
, Lemma A.1)
, then it follows that

\begin{split}\llangle A,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle&\ =\ (r_{p}-1)\operatorname{tr}(\Phi A\Phi^{\top})^{2}\ +\ 2r_{p}\|\Phi A\Phi^{\top}\|_{F}^{2},\\
&\ =\ (r_{p}-1)\operatorname{tr}(A)^{2}\ +\ 2r_{p}\|A\|_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(3.3)

We will now establish 2.1(
c
) by using the formula above to separately show that
\lambda_{\max}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})\lesssim 1
and
\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})\gtrsim 1
(with these bounds holding uniformly with respect to
d
). For the largest eigenvalue, we have

\begin{split}\lambda_{\max}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})&\ =\ \sup_{\|A\|_{F}=1}\llangle A,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ [(r_{p}-1)\vee 0]\,d\,+\,2r_{p},\end{split}

where the last step follows from the general inequality
\operatorname{tr}(A)^{2}\leq\|A\|_{F}^{2}d
.
In the case when
r_{p}<1
, we have
\lambda_{\max}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})<2
, and so we may consider the case
r_{p}\geq 1
, where it is enough to bound
(r_{p}-1)d+2r_{p}
.
For this purpose, observe that the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
ensure there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
p
such that

\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{4}\ =\ (\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}\ +\ \operatorname{var}(\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{2})\ \leq\ p^{2}\ +\ cp,

(3.4)

where we have used
\textup{{E}}(Z_{1}Z_{1}^{\top})=I
. Hence,

\begin{split}(r_{p}-1)d+2r_{p}&\ =\ \Big(\textstyle\frac{\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{4}-p(p+2)}{p(p+2)}\Big)d\ +\ \frac{2\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{4}}{p(p+2)}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ \Big(\textstyle\frac{(c-2)p}{p(p+2)}\Big)d\ +\ 2\frac{(p^{2}+cp)}{p(p+2)}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ 1.\end{split}

Therefore, we conclude that
\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{J}_{d})\lesssim 1
.

To lower bound
\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})
, we proceed along similar lines,

\begin{split}\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})&\ =\ \inf_{\|A\|_{F}=1}\llangle A,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ \inf_{\|A\|_{F}=1}(r_{p}-1)\operatorname{tr}(A)^{2}+2r_{p}.\end{split}

In the case when
r_{p}\geq 1
, this implies
\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})\geq 2
, and so it remains to consider the case when
r_{p}<1
. This leads to

\begin{split}\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})&\ =\ -\sup_{\|A\|_{F}=1}\Big((1-r_{p})\operatorname{tr}(A)^{2}\Big)\ +\ 2r_{p}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ -(1-r_{p})d\ +\ 2r_{p}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ \Big(\textstyle\frac{\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{4}-p(p+2)}{p(p+2)}\Big)d\ +\ 2\textstyle\frac{\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{4}}{p(p+2)}.\end{split}

Similarly to (
3.4
), we have
\textup{{E}}\|Z_{1}\|_{2}^{4}\geq p^{2}+c^{\prime}p
for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
not depending on
p
. Noting that
d\leq p
, and that the condition
r_{p}<1
implies
c^{\prime}-2<0
, we have

\begin{split}\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})&\ \geq\ \textstyle\frac{(c^{\prime}-2)p}{p(p+2)}d\ +\ \textstyle\frac{2(p^{2}+c^{\prime}p)}{p(p+2)}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \geq\ \textstyle\frac{c^{\prime}p}{p+2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \gtrsim\ 1,\end{split}

which completes the proof.∎

Remark.
Some steps in the previous proof shed light on Assumption
2.1
(c) in the case when
X_{1}
is has a centered elliptical distribution and
\textup{dim}(\mathbb{H})=\infty
. Here, we show that if the covariance operator
\Sigma
of
X_{1}
has infinite rank in this context, then
X_{1}
can only satisfy Assumption
2.1
(c) when it is Gaussian. To begin, recall from our discussion in Section
3.2
that the infinite rank of
\Sigma
requires
X_{1}
to be a scale mixture of Gaussian distributions
(
Boente, Barrera and Tyler 2014
, Prop. 2.1)
. That is,
X_{1}=\eta_{1}V_{1}
, where
V_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
is a centered Gaussian random element with covariance operator
\Sigma
, and
\eta_{1}
is a non-negative random variable that is independent of
V_{1}
with
\textup{{E}}(\eta_{1}^{2})=1
. From this representation of
X_{1}
, it follows that for any integer
d\geq 1
, we have
(\zeta_{11},\dots,\zeta_{1d})=\eta_{1}W_{1}
, where
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a standard Gaussian vector that is independent of
\eta_{1}
.
Then, for any symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, a direct calculation gives

\begin{split}\llangle A,\mathsf{J}_{d}(A)\rrangle&\ =\ \textup{var}(\eta_{1}^{2}\,\langle W_{1},AW_{1}\rangle)\\
&\ =\ \operatorname{tr}(A)^{2}\operatorname{var}(\eta_{1}^{2})+2\|A\|_{F}^{2}(\operatorname{var}(\eta_{1}^{2})+1),\end{split}

and so

\begin{split}\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{J}_{d})&\ =\ \sup_{\|A\|_{F}=1}\llangle A,\mathsf{J}_{d}(A)\rrangle\ =\ (d+2)\operatorname{var}(\eta_{1}^{2})+2.\end{split}

Thus, the only way
\sup_{d\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{\max}({\mathsf{J}}_{d})
can be finite in Assumption
2.1
(c) is if
\operatorname{var}(\eta_{1}^{2})=0
, which means that
\eta=1
almost surely, and so
X_{1}
must be Gaussian.

3.4
Proof of Proposition
3.2

As in the previous proof, note that the matrix
M
is equal to
\Sigma^{1/2}
, and for any
d\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, let
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
be the matrix whose columns are the leading
d
eigenvectors
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d}
of
\Sigma
. It follows that

(\zeta_{11},\dots,\zeta_{1d})=\Phi^{\top}Z_{1},

(3.5)

with the entries on the left side being as defined in Assumption
2.1
. Due to Rosenthal’s inequality (Lemma
9.3
in Section
9
), we have the following bound for any
q\geq 2
and
j=1,\dots,d
,

\begin{split}\|\zeta_{1j}\|_{L^{q}}&\ =\ \big\|\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{p}\phi_{jl}Z_{1l}\big\|_{L^{q}}\\
&\ \lesssim\ q\cdot\max\bigg\{\big\|\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{p}\phi_{jl}Z_{1l}\big\|_{L^{2}}\,,\,\big(\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{p}\big\|\phi_{jl}Z_{1l}\|_{L^{q}}^{q}\big)^{1/q}\bigg\}\\
&\lesssim\ q\cdot\max\big\{1\,,\ q^{\nu}\big(\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{p}|\phi_{jl}|^{q}\big)^{1/q}\Big\}\\
&\lesssim\ q^{\nu+1},\end{split}

where we have used the fact that
\phi_{j}
is a unit vector. This handles the condition
2.1
(a), since the exponent
\nu+1
here does not need to match the exponent
\nu
appearing in condition
2.1
(a).

Turning our attention to condition
2.1
(c), fix any
A\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
and observe that (
3.5
) gives

\begin{split}\llangle A,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle&\ =\ \operatorname{var}(\langle\Phi^{\top}Z_{1},A\,\Phi^{\top}Z_{1}\rangle).\end{split}

Letting
\kappa=\textup{{E}}(Z_{11}^{4})
and noting that
\Phi
has orthogonal columns, we may evaluate the last expression using a known formula for the variance of a quadratic form involving centered i.i.d. random variables with unit variance
(
Bai and
Silverstein 2010
, eqn. 9.8.6)
,

\begin{split}\llangle A,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle&\ =\ 2\|A\|_{F}^{2}+\ (\kappa-3)\sum_{j=1}^{p}[\Phi A\Phi^{\top}]_{jj}^{2}.\end{split}

(3.6)

Now, we can compute the smallest eigenvalue of
{\mathsf{J}}_{d}
as

\begin{split}\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{J}_{d})&\ =\ \inf_{\|A\|_{F}=1}\llangle A\,,\,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ 2\ +\ \inf_{\|A\|_{F}=1}(\kappa-3)\Big\llangle\Phi A\Phi^{\top},\textsf{Diag}(\Phi A\Phi^{\top})\Big\rrangle.\end{split}

In the case when
\kappa\geq 3
, the last expression gives
\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{J}_{d})\geq 2
. To handle the case when
\kappa<3
, note that if
A,B\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
, then
\llangle B,\textsf{Diag}(B)\rrangle\leq\|B\|_{F}^{2}
, and
\|\Phi A\Phi^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}=\|A\|_{F}^{2}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{J}_{d})&\ \geq\ 2\,+\,(\kappa-3)\ =\ \operatorname{var}(Z_{11}^{2})\ \gtrsim\ 1.\end{split}

(3.7)

Similarly, for the largest eigenvalue, we have

\begin{split}\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{J}_{d})&\ =\ \sup_{\|A\|_{F}=1}\llangle A,{\mathsf{J}}_{d}(A)\rrangle\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ 2\ +\ \sup_{\|A\|_{F}=1}(\kappa-3)\Big\llangle\Phi A\Phi^{\top}\,,\textsf{Diag}(\Phi A\Phi^{\top})\Big\rrangle\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ 2\,+\,|\kappa-3|\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ 1,\end{split}

(3.8)

as needed.∎

4
Outline of the proof of Theorem
2.1

Before organizing the main parts of the proof, we need to define a collection of objects that will appear frequently in the arguments.

Special indices.
First, let
\epsilon
be as chosen in the statement of Theorem
2.1
,
and define the associated integer

m=\Big\lceil n^{\frac{\epsilon(\beta-1/2)}{12\beta(2\beta+5)^{2}}}\wedge{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})\Big\rceil.

(4.1)

The exponent on
n
in this definition serves only as a technical expedient, and a smaller choice of the exponent could also be used.
Second, let
C>2\beta/(2\beta-1)

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then,

\text{{I}}_{n}\ \lesssim\ n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}.

The Kolmogorov distance between the distributions of any two random variables
U
and
V
on the same probability space can be bounded as

d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(V),\mathcal{L}(U))\ \leq\ \textup{{P}}(|V-U|\geq\delta)\ +\ \sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\textup{{P}}\big(|U-t|\leq\delta\big),

(5.1)

for any
\delta>0
.
For the remainder of the current proof, we will take
V=\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}
and
U=\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}
so that
\text{{I}}_{n}=d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(V),\mathcal{L}(U))
. Also note that we may assume without loss of generality that
k<{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})
, for otherwise
V=U
, and then
\textup{I}_{n}=0
.

The coupling probability
\textup{{P}}(|V-U|\geq\delta)
is handled by Lemma
5.1
below, which ensures that there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the following event holds with probability at most
c/n
,

\bigg|\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}-\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\bigg|\ \geq\ c\,n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4}}\log(n)^{\nu+1/2}.

Accordingly, we will take
\delta=c\,n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4}}\log(n)^{\nu+1/2}
in (
5.1
).

To handle the anti-concentration probability in (
5.1
), we will use the fact that if two random variables are close in the Kolmogorov metric, then they have similar anti-concentration behavior. More precisely, if
W
is any other random variable, then

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\textup{{P}}\big(|U-t|\leq\delta\big)\ \leq\ \sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\textup{{P}}\big(|W-t|\leq\delta\big)\ +\ 2d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(U),\mathcal{L}(W)).

(5.2)

In the current context, we will take
W=\|G\|_{{}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{-41944}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.4861pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}}}
, and Lemma
5.2
will show that
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\textup{{P}}\big(|\|G\|_{{}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{-41944}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.4861pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}}}-t|\leq\delta\big)
is at most of order
n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}
based on the choice of
\delta
mentioned earlier. Hence, it remains to bound
d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(U),\mathcal{L}(W))
, which will be based on a comparison with
\|G\|_{\textup{op}}
, namely

d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(U),\mathcal{L}(W))\ \leq\ d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(U),\mathcal{L}(\|G\|_{\textup{op}}))\ +\ d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(\|G\|_{\textup{op}}),\mathcal{L}(W)).

The first term on the right is the same as
\text{{II}}_{n}
, and Proposition
6.1
will show that it is at most of order
n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}
. Lastly, Proposition
5.2
will show that
d_{\textup{K}}(\mathcal{L}(\|G\|_{\textup{op}}),\mathcal{L}(W))
is at most of order
1/n
, which completes the proof.∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then, there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the following event holds with probability at most
c/n
,

\bigg|\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}-\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\bigg|\ \geq\ cn^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4}}\log(n)^{\nu+1/2}.

Remark.
Strictly speaking, the difference
\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}-\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}
is always non-negative, but we have retained the absolute value, since it makes the statement of the lemma appear more natural when it is applied in the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Define the projection operator
\mathsf{\Pi}_{k}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
by

\mathsf{\Pi}_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle x,\phi_{j}\rangle\phi_{j},

and let
\mathsf{I}
denote the identity operator on
\mathbb{H}
.
Also, recall the notations
\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}
and
\Sigma_{k}=\textup{{E}}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{k})
, where
Y_{i}=(\langle X_{i},\phi_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle X_{i},\phi_{k}\rangle)
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Since the map
x\mapsto(\langle x,\phi_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle x,\phi_{k}\rangle)
is an isometry from
\textup{span}\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k}\}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the variational representation for the operator norm of a compact operator
(
Hsing and Eubank 2015
, Theorem 4.3.3)
implies

\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\ =\ \|\mathsf{\Pi}_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\mathsf{\Pi}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}.

Therefore,

\begin{split}\bigg|\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}-\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\bigg|&\ \leq\ \Big\|(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\,-\,\mathsf{\Pi}_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\mathsf{\Pi}_{k}\Big\|_{\textup{op}}\\
&\ =\ \Big\|(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{\Pi}_{k})(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)+\mathsf{\Pi}_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{\Pi}_{k})\Big\|_{\textup{op}}\\
&\ \leq\ 2\Big\|(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{\Pi}_{k})(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\Big\|_{\textup{op}}\\
&\ =\ 2\Bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{\Pi}_{k})X_{i}]\otimes X_{i}\ -\ \textup{{E}}\big([(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{\Pi}_{k})X_{i}]\otimes X_{i}\big)\Bigg\|_{\textup{op}}.\end{split}

(5.3)

Next, let
\mathbb{B}
be the unit ball of
\mathbb{H}
, and define
\mathbb{B}_{-k}\subset\mathbb{B}
as the subset of vectors
v\in\mathbb{B}
satisfying
\langle v,\phi_{j}\rangle=0
for all
j=1,\dots,k
. Hence, for any
u\in\mathbb{B}
, there exists some
v\in\mathbb{B}_{-k}
such that
\langle u,(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{\Pi}_{k})X_{i}\rangle=\langle v,X_{i}\rangle
. So, applying the variational representation of the operator norm to the last expression in (
5.3
), we have

\begin{split}\bigg|\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}-\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\bigg|&\ \ \leq\ \displaystyle\ \sup_{(v,w)\in\mathbb{B}_{-k}\times\mathbb{B}}2\Bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle v,X_{i}\rangle\langle X_{i},w\rangle\,-\,\textup{{E}}(\langle v,X_{i}\rangle\langle X_{i},w\rangle)\Bigg|.\end{split}

(5.4)

Let
t>0
be a free parameter to be chosen later, and define the vectors

\omega(t)=\textstyle\frac{tv}{2}+\textstyle\frac{w}{2t}\text{ \ \ \ \ \ and \ \ \ \ \ }\tilde{\omega}(t)=\frac{tv}{2}-\frac{w}{2t}.

Because the bilinear form
(v,w)\mapsto\langle v,X_{i}\rangle\langle X_{i},w\rangle
takes the same value when acting on
(v,w)
and
(tv,\frac{w}{t})
, the polarization identity implies

\langle v,X_{i}\rangle\langle X_{i},w\rangle\ =\ \langle\omega(t),X_{i}\rangle^{2}\ -\ \langle\tilde{\omega}(t),X_{i}\rangle^{2}.

To control the squares on the right side, it is helpful to define the linear operator
{\mathsf{A}}(t):\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}
according to

{\mathsf{A}}(t)x\ =\ \Big(\textstyle t(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{\Pi}_{k})x\,,\,\frac{x}{t}\Big).

Based on this definition, the following relations hold simultaneously for all
t>0
when the vectors
v
and
w
satisfy
(v,w)\in\mathbb{B}_{-k}\times\mathbb{B}
,

\langle\omega(t),X_{i}\rangle\ =\ \Big\langle(\textstyle\frac{v}{2},\textstyle\frac{w}{2})\,,\,{\mathsf{A}}(t)X_{i}\Big\rangle\text{ \ \ \ and \ \ \ }\langle\tilde{\omega}(t),X_{i}\rangle\ =\ \Big\langle(\textstyle\frac{v}{2},-\textstyle\frac{w}{2})\,,\,{\mathsf{A}}(t)X_{i}\Big\rangle.

Furthermore, by letting
\xi_{i}(t)={\mathsf{A}}(t)X_{i}
, and noting that
(\textstyle\frac{v}{2},\textstyle\frac{w}{2})
and
(\textstyle\frac{v}{2},-\textstyle\frac{w}{2})
lie in the unit ball
\bar{\mathbb{B}}
of
\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}
, it follows from (
5.4
) that

\begin{split}\bigg|\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}-\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\bigg|&\ \leq\ \displaystyle\ \sup_{u\in\bar{\mathbb{B}}}\,\,4\Bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle u,\xi_{i}(t)\rangle^{2}-\,\textup{{E}}\langle u,\xi_{i}(t)\rangle^{2}\Bigg|\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ 4\Bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(t)\otimes\xi_{i}(t)-\textup{{E}}\,\xi_{i}(t)\otimes\xi_{i}(t)\Bigg\|_{\textup{op}}.\end{split}

(5.5)

The norm in the last line can be handled using Lemma
9.2
, which provides a general bound on the operator norm error of sample covariance matrices in separable Hilbert spaces, developed previously in
(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
)
. Specifically, if we let
q=\log(n)\vee 3
, then this result gives

\begin{split}\Bigg(\textup{{E}}\bigg|\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}&-\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\bigg|^{q}\Bigg)^{1/q}\ \lesssim\ \ \bigg(\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\|\textup{{E}}\xi_{1}(t)\otimes\xi_{1}(t)\|_{\textup{op}}^{1/2}\cdot(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}(t)\|^{2q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}\bigg)\bigvee\bigg(\textstyle\frac{q}{n}\cdot(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}(t)\|^{2q})^{\frac{1}{q}}\bigg).\end{split}

(5.6)

To bound the right side, first observe that

\begin{split}\|\textup{{E}}\xi_{1}(t)\otimes\xi_{1}(t)\|_{\textup{op}}^{1/2}&\ =\ \|{\mathsf{A}}(t)\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ t\lambda_{k}(\Sigma)^{1/2}\ +\ \textstyle\frac{1}{t}\lambda_{1}(\Sigma)^{1/2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ tk^{-\beta}\ +\ \textstyle\frac{1}{t}.\end{split}

(5.7)

Next, we have

\begin{split}(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}(t)\|^{2q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}&\ \leq\ t\Big\|\textstyle\sum_{j\geq k+1}\langle X_{1},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big\|_{L^{q}}^{1/2}\ +\ \textstyle\frac{1}{t}\Big\|\textstyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\langle X_{1},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big\|_{L^{q}}^{1/2}\\
&\ \leq\ t\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j\geq k+1}\|\langle X_{1},\phi_{j}\rangle\|_{L^{2q}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\ +\ \frac{1}{t}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\|\langle X_{1},\phi_{j}\rangle\|_{L^{2q}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ q^{\nu}t\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j\geq k+1}j^{-2\beta}\Big)^{1/2}\ +\ \textstyle\frac{q^{\nu}}{t}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}j^{-2\beta}\Big)^{1/2}\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(Assumptions~\ref{A:model}(a) and (b))}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ q^{\nu}\Big(tk^{-\beta+1/2}\ +\ \textstyle\frac{1}{t}\Big).\end{split}

(5.8)

Taking
t=k^{\beta/2}
in (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) gives

\|\textup{{E}}\,\xi_{1}(t)\otimes\xi_{1}(t)\|_{\textup{op}}^{1/2}\ \lesssim\ k^{-\beta/2},

(5.9)

and

(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}(t)\|^{2q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}\ \lesssim\ q^{\nu}\,k^{-\beta/2+1/2}.

(5.10)

Combining these bounds with (
5.6
), we have

\begin{split}\Bigg(\textup{{E}}\bigg|\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}-\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\bigg|^{q}\Bigg)^{1/q}&\ \lesssim\ \Big(\textstyle\frac{q^{\nu+1/2}}{\sqrt{n}}k^{-\beta+1/2}\Big)\vee\Big(\textstyle\frac{q^{2\nu+1}}{n}k^{-\beta+1}\Big)\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ \textstyle\frac{q^{\nu+1/2}\,k^{-\beta+1/2}}{\sqrt{n}}.\end{split}

(5.11)

To finish the proof, note that we may assume that
k<{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})
, for otherwise
\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\textup{op}}
and
\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}
are equal. Consequently, the definition of
k
in (
4.3
) implies
k\geq n^{\frac{1}{2\beta+4}}
. Finally, rescaling both sides of (
5.11
) by
\sqrt{n}
and applying Chebyshev’s inequality leads to the stated result.∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1

where the second step uses the norm inequalities
\|\cdot\|_{{}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{-41944}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.4861pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{F}

\begin{split}(\textup{{E}}\|G\|_{F})^{2}&\ \leq\ \textup{{E}}\|G\|_{F}^{2}\\[5.69046pt]

.

hold, and let

and

(5.17)

Remark.

For the arguments used to prove Proposition
5.2
in this section, we may assume without loss of generality that
m<{\textup{dim}}(\mathbb{H})
, due to the following reasoning: Based on the definitions of
m
and
k
in (
4.1
) and (
4.3

\mathbb{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}}^{k}

Here, we only explain the proof at a high level, deferring the substantial steps to lemmas that are given later in this section.
For any

, define the events

\mathcal{A}(t)=\big\{\|G\|_{{}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{-41944}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.4861pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}}}\leq t\big\}\text{ \ \ \ and \ \ \ }\mathcal{B}(t)=\big\{\|G\|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}>t\big\}.

(5.18)

In terms of this notation, it is straightforward to check that the following relation holds for any

,

\bigg|\textup{{P}}\big(\|G\|_{\textup{op}}\leq t\big)\ -\ \textup{{P}}\big(\|G\|_{{}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{272637}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{-41944}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.4861pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangle$}}}}}}}\leq t\big)\bigg|\ =\ \textup{{P}}\Big(\mathcal{A}(t)\cap\mathcal{B}(t)\Big).

For any given pair of real numbers

and

This can be checked by separately considering the cases

5.2.1

To upper bound the probability

(
Lopes and Yao 2022
)
. For this purpose, we define the

of any non-zero positive semidefinite matrix

as

{\tt{r}}(A)\ =\ \textstyle\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A)^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}.

To allow the lower-tail bound to be understood more clearly, we have stated it below in terms of an index

that is distinct from the sample size

in the current paper.

Lemma 5.4

(
Lopes and Yao 2022

.

Let

be a Gaussian random vector with a correlation matrix

, and entries satisfying
\textup{{E}}(\gamma_{j})=0
and
\operatorname{var}(\gamma_{j})=1
for all

.
Also let

be any constants that are fixed with respect to

. Then, there is a constant

depending only on

and

such that the following inequality holds for any integer

(5.20)

We now state the main result of this subsection, which yields the desired upper bound on
\textup{{P}}(\mathcal{A}(t_{2}))
.

.

Lemma 5.6

2.1
hold. Then,

\min_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\operatorname{var}\Big(\llangle G,e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\rrangle\Big)\ \gtrsim\ \lambda_{\ell}(\Sigma)^{2}.

(5.26)

In addition, if
R
denotes the correlation matrix of the random vector
(\llangle G,e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}\rrangle,\dots,\llangle G,e_{\ell}e_{\ell}^{\top}\rrangle)
, then

{\tt{r}}(R)\ \gtrsim\ \ell.

(5.27)

First we establish (
5.26
). Recall the notation
\zeta_{1}=(\zeta_{11},\dots,\zeta_{1k})
, as well as the facts that
e_{j}^{\top}Y_{1}=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)}e_{j}^{\top}\zeta_{1}
, and that
G
has the same covariance operator as
Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}
. So, for any
j=1,\dots,\ell
, we have

\begin{split}\operatorname{var}(\llangle G,e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\rrangle)&\ =\ \operatorname{var}(\llangle Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top},e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\rrangle)\\
&\ =\ \lambda_{j}(\Sigma)^{2}\operatorname{var}\big(\llangle\zeta_{1}\zeta_{1}^{\top}\,,\,e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\rrangle\big)\\
&\ =\ \lambda_{j}(\Sigma)^{2}\big\llangle e_{j}e_{j}^{\top},{\mathsf{J}}_{k}(e_{j}e_{j}^{\top})\big\rrangle\\
&\ \gtrsim\ \lambda_{j}(\Sigma)^{2},\end{split}

(5.28)

where the last step follows from Assumption
2.1
(c). This establishes (
5.26
).

Regarding (
5.27
), it suffices to show that
\|R\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\ell
, since
\operatorname{tr}(R)=\ell
.
To proceed, recall the notation
g_{j}=\llangle G,e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\rrangle
, and note that the squared entries of
R
are given by

R_{jj^{\prime}}^{2}\ =\ \frac{\textup{cov}(g_{j},g_{j^{\prime}})^{2}}{\operatorname{var}(g_{j})\operatorname{var}(g_{j^{\prime}})}.

(5.29)

By essentially repeating the first several steps of (
5.28
), we have

\begin{split}\textup{cov}(g_{j},g_{j^{\prime}})&\ =\ \lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\lambda_{j^{\prime}}(\Sigma)\big\llangle e_{j}e_{j}^{\top},{\mathsf{J}}_{k}(e_{j^{\prime}}e_{j^{\prime}}^{\top})\big\rrangle.\end{split}

If we let
K\in\mathbb{S}^{\ell\times\ell}
have entries
K_{jj^{\prime}}=\big\llangle e_{j}e_{j}^{\top},{\mathsf{J}}_{k}(e_{j^{\prime}}e_{j^{\prime}}^{\top})\big\rrangle
, then (
5.28
) and (
5.29
) imply that the squared entries of
R
satisfy the bound

R_{jj^{\prime}}^{2}\ \lesssim\ K_{jj^{\prime}}^{2}
.
This yields

\begin{split}\|R\|_{F}^{2}&\ \lesssim\ \|K\|_{F}^{2}\ \leq\ \ell\,\|K\|_{\textup{op}}^{2}.\end{split}

(5.30)

Next, observe that if the operator
{\mathsf{J}}_{k}
is represented as a symmetric matrix of size
\frac{1}{2}k(k+1)\times\frac{1}{2}k(k+1)
with entries
\{\llangle e_{i}e_{j}^{\top},{\mathsf{J}}_{k}(e_{i^{\prime}}e_{j^{\prime}}^{\top})\rrangle\}
for
i\leq j
and
i^{\prime}\leq j^{\prime}
, then
K
corresponds to a principal submatrix of
\mathsf{J}_{k}
. Therefore,
\|K\|_{\textup{op}}\leq\|\mathsf{J}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\lesssim 1
by Assumption
2.1
(c), and so
\|R\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\ell
.∎

5.2.2
Upper bound for
\textup{{P}}(\mathcal{B}(t_{1}))

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then, there is a constant
c_{1}>0
not depending on
n
, such that the choice

t_{1}=c_{1}m^{-2\beta+1}\sqrt{\log(n)}

(5.31)

implies

\textup{{P}}(\mathcal{B}(t_{1}))\ \lesssim\ \textstyle\frac{1}{n}.

(5.32)

The overall idea for proving (
5.32
) is to develop an upper bound on the
L^{q}
norm of the random variable
\|G\|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}
and apply Chebyshev’s inequality.
To begin with the details, let
\eta=m^{-\beta+1/2}
, and define the diagonal linear operator
D:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
according to

Dx\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{m}2\eta\langle x,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}\ +\ \sum_{l=m+1}^{k}2\langle x,e_{l}\rangle e_{l}.

(5.33)

In a moment, we will need to use the fact that the image of
\mathbb{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}^{k}
under the inverse
D^{-1}
is contained in the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{k}
. This can be seen by noting that for any
u\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}^{k}
we have

\begin{split}\|D^{-1}u\|_{2}&\ =\ \bigg\|\textstyle\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle u,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}+\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=m+1}^{k}\langle u,e_{l}\rangle e_{l}\bigg\|_{2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ \textstyle\frac{1}{2\eta}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle u,e_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big)^{1/2}+\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ 1,\end{split}

where the last step uses the definitions of
\mathbb{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}^{k}
and
\eta
.
Hence, for any
u\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}^{k}
, there is a vector
w\in\mathbb{B}^{k}
such that
Dw=u
, which yields

\begin{split}\|G\|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}&\ \leq\ \sup_{w\in\mathbb{B}^{k}}\Big|\Big\langle Dw,GDw\Big\rangle\Big|\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ \|DGD\|_{F},\end{split}

where for ease of notation we have identified
D
with its matrix representation in the standard basis.

We are now in position to bound the
L^{q}
norm of
\|G\|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}
, taking
q=\log(n)\vee 3
. Since the
(i,j)
entry of
DGD
is equal to
G_{ij}D_{ii}D_{jj}
, we have

\begin{split}\big\|\|G\|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}\big\|_{L^{q}}&\ \leq\ \bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|G_{ij}\|_{L^{q}}^{2}D_{ii}^{2}D_{jj}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.\end{split}

(5.34)

Using a standard moment bound for centered Gaussian random variables
(
Vershynin 2018
, p.25)
, as well as the fact that
G
has the same covariance operator as
Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}
, it follows that

\begin{split}\|G_{ij}\|_{L^{q}}^{2}&\ \lesssim\ q\operatorname{var}(G_{ij})\\
&\ =\ q\operatorname{var}(Y_{1i}Y_{1j})\\
&\ \leq\ q\,\lambda_{i}(\Sigma)\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)\textup{{E}}(\zeta_{1i}^{2}\zeta_{1j}^{2})\\
&\ \lesssim\ q\,\lambda_{i}(\Sigma)\lambda_{j}(\Sigma),\end{split}

where we recall that
Y_{1}=(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(\Sigma)}\zeta_{11},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_{k}(\Sigma)}\zeta_{1k})
. Combining with (
5.34
), we conclude that

\begin{split}\big\|\|G\|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}\big\|_{L^{q}}&\ \lesssim\ \sqrt{q}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}(\Sigma)D_{jj}^{2}\\
&\ \lesssim\ \sqrt{q}\bigg(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\eta^{2}j^{-2\beta}+\sum_{j=m+1}^{k}j^{-2\beta}\bigg)\\
&\ \lesssim\ \sqrt{q}\big(\eta^{2}\ +\ m^{-2\beta+1}\big)\\
&\ \lesssim\sqrt{\log(n)}m^{-2\beta+1}.\end{split}

Under the choice of
q=\log(n)\vee 3
, this bound on
\big\|\|G\|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{248663}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.12pt}{\resizebox{142949}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.11998pt}{\resizebox{65911}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\!\triangledown$}}}}}}\big\|_{L^{q}}
leads to the stated result via Chebyshev’s inequality for a suitable choice of
c_{1}
.∎

6
The terms
\text{{II}}_{n}
and
\widehat{\text{{II}}}_{n}
: Gaussian and bootstrap approximation

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then,

\text{{II}}_{n}\ \lesssim\ n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}.

(i)

In addition, there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the event

\widehat{\text{{II}}}_{n}\ \leq\ c\,n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}

(ii)

holds with probability at least
1-c/n
.

We begin with part (i). Let
\mathscr{C}
denote the class of all convex Borel subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. Since the function
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}
is convex, we have

\begin{split}\text{{II}}_{n}&\ =\ \sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\textup{{P}}\Big(\sqrt{n}\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\leq t\Big)\ -\ \textup{{P}}\big(\|G\|_{\textup{op}}\leq t\big)\bigg|\\[8.5359pt]
&\ \leq\ \sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{C}}\bigg|\textup{{P}}\Big(\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k})\in\mathcal{C}\Big)\ -\ \textup{{P}}\big(G\in\mathcal{C}\big)\bigg|.\end{split}

(6.1)

Next, recall that
\widehat{\Sigma}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}=\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top})
, and let
{\mathsf{C}}:\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}\to\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
denote the covariance operator of
Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}
, so that for any
A,B\in\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
we have

\llangle A,{\mathsf{C}}(B)\rrangle\ =\ \textup{cov}\Big(\big\llangle A,Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}\big\rrangle,\big\llangle B,Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}\big\rrangle\Big).

It is possible to write the operator
\mathsf{C}
more explicitly as

\mathsf{C}=\big(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\big)\,{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\,\big(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\big),

(6.2)

which can be verified by observing that the identity
Y_{k}=\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\zeta_{1}
and the definition of
\mathsf{J}_{k}
yield

\begin{split}\Big\llangle A\,,\,\big(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\big)\,{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\,\big(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\big)(B)\Big\rrangle\ &=\Big\llangle\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{k}^{1/2},\mathsf{J}_{k}\big(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}B\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\big)\Big\rrangle\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ \textup{cov}\Big(\big\llangle\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{k}^{1/2},\zeta_{1}\zeta_{1}^{\top}\big\rrangle\ ,\ \big\llangle\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}B\Sigma_{k}^{1/2},\zeta_{1}\zeta_{1}^{\top}\big\rrangle\Big)\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ \textup{cov}\Big(\big\llangle A,Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}\big\rrangle,\big\llangle B,Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}\big\rrangle\Big).\end{split}

The representation of
\mathsf{C}
in (
6.2
) shows that it is a composition of invertible operators, and hence invertible.
So, for each
i=1,\dots,n
, we may define

M_{i}={\mathsf{C}}^{-1/2}\Big(Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top})\Big),

(6.3)

which we will regard as a centered and isotropic random vector in
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. (Note that
M_{i}
takes values in
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
since
\mathsf{C}^{-1/2}
is an operator from
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
to itself.) Using this definition, the bound (
6.1
) leads to

\begin{split}\text{{II}}_{n}\ \leq\ \sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{C}}\bigg|\textup{{P}}\Big(\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(M_{1}+\dots+M_{n})\in\mathcal{C}\Big)\ -\ \gamma(\mathcal{C})\bigg|,\end{split}

(6.4)

where
\gamma
denotes the standard Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. To bound the last expression, we will apply a version of the multivariate Berry-Esseen Theorem due to
(
Fang and Koike 2020
)
, recorded later in Lemma
9.1
, which yields

\begin{split}\text{{II}}_{n}&\ \lesssim\ \displaystyle\textstyle\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\cdot\Big(\textstyle\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\Big)^{1/4}\cdot(\textup{{E}}\|M_{1}\|_{F}^{4})^{1/2}\cdot\big(|\log(\textup{{E}}\|M_{1}\|_{F}^{4}/n)|\vee 1\big).\end{split}

(6.5)

Finally, in Lemma
6.1
, it is shown that

\textup{{E}}\|M_{1}\|_{F}^{4}\ \lesssim\ k^{4}
, and combining this with
k\lesssim n^{\frac{1}{2\beta+4}}
leads to the bound (i).

Turning to part (ii), recall that
Y_{1}^{*},\dots,Y_{n}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
are sampled with replacement from
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
. In addition, let
\widehat{{\mathsf{C}}}:\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}\to\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
denote the covariance operator of the conditional distribution of
Y_{1}^{*}(Y_{1}^{*})^{\top}
given
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.
It follows from Lemma
6.3
that
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}
is invertible with probability at least
1-c/n
, and on this high probability event we may define the bootstrap counterpart of
M_{i}
as

M_{i}^{*}=\widehat{{\mathsf{C}}}^{-1/2}\Big(Y_{i}^{*}(Y_{i}^{*})^{\top}\,-\,\textup{{E}}\big(Y_{i}^{*}(Y_{i}^{*})^{\top}\big|X\big)\Big).

(6.6)

Next, by repeating the steps in the proof of part (i), there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the following bound holds with probability at least
1-c/n
,

\begin{split}\widehat{\text{{II}}}_{n}&\ \ \leq\ \ \displaystyle\textstyle\frac{c}{n^{1/2}}\cdot\Big(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\Big)^{1/4}\cdot(\textup{{E}}(\|M_{1}^{*}\|_{F}^{4}|X))^{1/2}\cdot\big(|\log(\textup{{E}}(\|M_{1}^{*}\|_{F}^{4}|X)/n)|\vee 1\big).\end{split}

Lastly, it is shown in Lemma
6.2
that the bound

\textup{{E}}(\|M_{1}^{*}\|_{F}^{4}|X)\ \leq\ c\,k^{4}\log(n)^{8\nu}

holds with probability at least
1-c/n
, which leads to the statement (ii).
∎

Lemma 6.1
.

If the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold and
M_{1}
is defined as in (
6.3
), then

Due to (
6.2
), the inverse of

\mathsf{C}^{-1}=\big(\Sigma_{k}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{-1/2}\big)\,{\mathsf{J}}_{k}^{-1}\,\big(\Sigma_{k}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{-1/2}\big).

Recall the notation
\zeta_{i}=(\zeta_{i1},\dots,\zeta_{ik})
for each

, and that this random vector satisfies
\zeta_{i}=\Sigma_{k}^{-1/2}Y_{i}
.
Using Assumption
2.1
(c), the following bounds hold with probability 1,

\begin{split}\|M_{1}\|_{F}^{4}&\ =\ \Big\llangle Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}),{\mathsf{C}}^{-1}\Big(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top})\Big)\Big\rrangle^{2}\\[5.69046pt]

(6.7)

for some constant
c>0
not depending on
n
. To complete the proof, it remains to bound
\textup{{E}}\|\zeta_{1}\|_{2}^{8}
, for which we have

\begin{split}\Big(\textup{{E}}\|\zeta_{1}\|_{2}^{8}\Big)^{1/4}&\ \leq\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\zeta_{1j}^{2}\|_{L^{4}}\ \lesssim\ k,\end{split}

as needed. ∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold, and let
M_{1}^{*}
be as defined in (
6.6
). Then, there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the following event holds with probability at least
1-c/n
,

\textup{{E}}\big(\|M_{1}^{*}\|_{F}^{4}\,\big|\,X\big)\ \leq\ c\,k^{4}\log(n)^{8\nu}.

The proof proceeds by analogy with that of Lemma
6.1
, but involves some extra technical hurdles. First, let
\zeta_{1}^{*}=\Sigma_{k}^{-1/2}Y_{1}^{*}
, and let
\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}
denote the conditional covariance operator of
\zeta_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{1}^{*})^{\top}
given
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, which satisfies

\llangle A\,,\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}(B)\rrangle\ =\ \ \textup{cov}\Big(\big\llangle A,\zeta_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{1}^{*})^{\top}\big\rrangle\,,\,\big\llangle B,\zeta_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{1}^{*})^{\top}\big\rrangle\,\Big|\,X\Big)

(6.8)

for any
A,B\in\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
. Also, recall that
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}
denotes the covariance operator of
Y_{1}^{*}(Y_{1}^{*})^{\top}
conditional on
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, which is related to
\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}
through the formula

\widehat{\mathsf{C}}=(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{1/2})\ \widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\otimes}}\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}).

(6.9)

Hence,
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}
is invertible whenever
\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}
is, and it will be shown in Lemma
6.3
that there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the event
\{\lambda_{\min}(\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,)\geq 1/c\}
holds with probability at least
1-c/n
. When this event occurs, we may bound
\|M_{1}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}
according to

\begin{split}\|M_{1}^{*}\|_{F}^{4}&\ =\ \bigg\llangle Y_{1}^{*}(Y_{1}^{*})^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{1}^{*}(Y_{1}^{*})^{\top}|X)\,,\,\widehat{\mathsf{C}}^{-1}\Big((Y_{1}^{*}(Y_{1}^{*})^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(Y_{1}^{*}(Y_{1}^{*})^{\top}|X)\Big)\bigg\rrangle^{2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ \bigg\llangle\zeta_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{1}^{*})^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(\zeta_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{1}^{*})^{\top}|X)\,,\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}^{\ -1}\Big(\zeta_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{1}^{*})^{\top}-\textup{{E}}(\zeta_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{1}^{*})^{\top}|X)\Big)\bigg\rrangle^{2}\\[5.69046pt]

&\ \leq\ c\|\zeta_{1}^{*}\|_{2}^{8}\ +\ c\textup{{E}}\big(\|\zeta_{1}^{*}\|_{2}^{8}\,\big|X\big).\end{split}

(6.10)

Therefore, the following event also occurs with probability at least

,

\begin{split}\textup{{E}}\big(\|M_{1}^{*}\|_{F}^{4}\big|X\big)&\ \leq\ c\,\textup{{E}}\big(\|\zeta_{1}^{*}\|_{2}^{8}\,\big|X\big)\\[5.69046pt]

\begin{split}\Big\|\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\zeta_{i}\|_{2}^{8}\Big\|_{L^{q}}&\ \leq\ \big\|\|\zeta_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\big\|_{L^{4q}}^{4}\\
&\ \leq\ \bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\zeta_{1j}\|_{L^{8q}}^{2}\bigg)^{4}\\
&\ \lesssim\ q^{8\nu}k^{4},\end{split}

where the last step uses Assumption
2.1
(a). Hence, by Chebyshev’s inequality, the event

occurs with probability at most
c/n
. Applying this to (
6.11
) completes the proof.∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then, there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the following events each hold with probability at least
1-c/n
,

\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}-{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\ \leq\ \frac{ck\log(n)^{2\nu+1/2}}{n^{1/2}},

(6.12)

and

\lambda_{\min}(\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k})\ \geq\ 1/c.

(6.13)

In addition, if the event (
6.13
) holds, then the operator
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}
given by the formula (
6.9
) is invertible.

Due to Weyl’s inequality
(
Horn and Johnson 2012
, Theorem 4.3.1)
and Assumption
2.1
(c), the statement (
6.13
) follows from (
6.12
). Also, the assertion that (
6.13
) implies the invertibility of
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}
is immediate from the formula (
6.9
).

It remains to prove (
6.12
). Let
\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}\ =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\zeta_{i}^{\top}
, and note from (
6.8
) that the operator
\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}
can be expressed as

\begin{split}\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}&\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\zeta_{i}\zeta_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}\ )\otimes(\zeta_{i}\zeta_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}\ ).\end{split}

To decompose
\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}
, define

\begin{split}\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime}\ =\ \textstyle\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\zeta_{i}\zeta_{i}^{\top}-I)\otimes(\zeta_{i}\zeta_{i}^{\top}-I)\ \ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime\prime}&\ =\ (\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}-I)\otimes(\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}-I)\end{split}

so that
\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,=\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime}\,-\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime\prime}
, which yields the bound

\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}-{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\ \leq\ \|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\!\!^{\prime}-{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\ +\ \|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime\prime}\|_{\textup{op}}.

(6.14)

For the first term on the right side,
note that if we put
\xi_{i}=\zeta_{i}\zeta_{i}^{\top}-I
, then
\textup{{E}}(\xi_{i}\otimes\xi_{i})={\mathsf{J}}_{k}
, and so we may apply Lemma
9.2
to obtain the following bound with
q=\log(n)\vee 3
,

\begin{split}\Big(\textup{{E}}\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime}-{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}\Big)^{1/q}&\ =\ \bigg(\textup{{E}}\Big\|\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\otimes\xi_{i}-\textup{{E}}(\xi_{i}\otimes\xi_{i})\Big\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}\bigg)^{1/q}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ \bigg(\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}^{1/2}\,(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}\|_{F}^{2q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}\bigg)\bigvee\bigg(\textstyle\frac{q}{n}\,(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}\|_{F}^{2q})^{\frac{1}{q}}\bigg).\end{split}

Furthermore, we have

\begin{split}(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}\|_{F}^{2q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}&\ \leq\ (\textup{{E}}\|\zeta_{1}\|_{2}^{4q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}\ +\ \sqrt{k}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\zeta_{1j}\|_{L^{4q}}^{2}\ +\ \sqrt{k}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ q^{2\nu}k+\ \sqrt{k},\end{split}

(6.15)

where we have used Assumption
2.1
(a) in the last step. Combining this with
\|{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}\lesssim 1
from Assumption
2.1
(c) yields

\begin{split}\Big(\textup{{E}}\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime}-{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}\Big)^{1/q}&\ \lesssim\ \Big(\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{n}}(q^{2\nu}k+\sqrt{k})\Big)\bigvee\Big(\textstyle\frac{q}{n}(q^{2\nu}k+\sqrt{k})^{2}\Big)\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\textstyle\frac{q^{2\nu+1/2}k}{n^{1/2}}.\end{split}

To bound the second term on the right side of (
6.14
), observe that

\begin{split}\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime\prime}\|_{\textup{op}}&\ =\ \Big\|(\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}-I)\otimes(\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}-I)\Big\|_{\textup{op}}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ =\ \|\overline{\zeta\zeta^{\top}\!\!}-I\|_{F}^{2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ k\|\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\zeta_{i}^{\top}-I\|_{\textup{op}}^{2},\end{split}

where we have used the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{F}^{2}\leq k\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}^{2}
on
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
.
Since
\textup{{E}}(\zeta_{1}\zeta_{1}^{\top})=I
, we may apply Lemma
9.2
to the last line, which yields

\begin{split}\big(\textup{{E}}\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,\!\!^{\prime\prime}\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}\big)^{1/q}&\ \lesssim\ k\,\bigg[\Big(\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{2q}}{\sqrt{n}}\,(\textup{{E}}\|\zeta_{1}\|_{2}^{4q})^{\frac{1}{4q}}\Big)\bigvee\Big(\textstyle\frac{2q}{n}\,(\textup{{E}}\|\zeta_{1}\|_{2}^{4q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}\Big)\bigg]^{2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ k\Big[\Big(\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{q^{2\nu}k}\Big)\vee\Big(\textstyle\frac{q}{n}q^{2\nu}k\Big)\Big]^{2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ \textstyle\frac{q^{2\nu+1}k^{2}}{n},\end{split}

where we have re-used part of (
6.15
) in the second step.
Combining the last several steps and noting that
q^{2\nu+1}k^{2}/n\lesssim q^{2\nu+1/2}k/n^{1/2}
gives

\Big(\textup{{E}}\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}-{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\ \lesssim\ \textstyle\frac{q^{2\nu+1/2}k}{n^{1/2}}.

Therefore, the stated bound (
6.12
) follows from Chebyshev’s inequality. ∎

The term
\widehat{\text{{III}}}_{n}
: Gaussian comparison

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then, there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the event

\widehat{\text{{III}}}_{n}\ \leq\ c\,n^{-\frac{\beta-1/2}{2\beta+4+\epsilon}}

holds with probability at least
1-c/n

Define the operator
\widehat{{\mathsf{D}}}:\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}\to\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
, according to
\widehat{\mathsf{D}}={\mathsf{J}}_{k}^{-1/2}\,\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,{\mathsf{J}}_{k}^{-1/2}-\mathsf{I}
, where
{\mathsf{I}}
is the identity operator.
As an initial step, we claim that the following bound holds with probability 1,

\widehat{\text{{III}}}_{n}\ \leq\ 2k\,\|\widehat{{\mathsf{D}}}\|_{\textup{op}}.

(7.2)

In verifying the claim, we may restrict our attention to the case when the event
\{\|\widehat{{\mathsf{D}}}\|_{\textup{op}}\leq 1/2\}
holds, since
\widehat{\text{{III}}}_{n}
cannot be greater than 1.
To proceed, we will adapt an argument from the proof of Lemma A.7 in
(
Spokoiny and Zhilova 2015
)
. Let
\mathscr{B}
denote the collection of all Borel subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{k\times k}
, and let
d_{\text{KL}}
denote the KL-divergence. Pinsker’s inequality implies

\begin{split}\widehat{\text{{III}}}_{n}&\ =\ \sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\textup{{P}}\big(\|G\|_{\textup{op}}\leq t\big)-\textup{{P}}\big(\|G^{*}\|_{\textup{op}}\leq t\big|X\big)\bigg|\\[8.5359pt]

&\ \leq\ \textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{d_{\text{KL}}\big(\mathcal{L}(G^{*}|X)\big\|\mathcal{L}(G)\big)}.\end{split}

Since the distributions
\mathcal{L}(G^{*}|X)
and
\mathcal{L}(G)
are centered and Gaussian, the KL-divergence between them can be calculated explicitly
(
Pardo 2006
, p.33)
in terms of the their respective covariance operators
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}
and
\mathsf{C}
,

\begin{split}d_{\text{KL}}\big(\mathcal{L}(G^{*}|X)\big\|\mathcal{L}(G)\big)&\ =\ \textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf{C}^{-1}\widehat{\mathsf{C}})\,-\,\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf{I})\,-\,\log\det(\mathsf{C}^{-1}\widehat{\mathsf{C}})\Big).\end{split}

(7.3)

To modify the formula for the KL divergence, recall the formulas for
\mathsf{C}
and
\widehat{\mathsf{C}}
given in (
6.2
) and (
6.9
), and note that the following algebraic relations hold

\begin{split}\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf{C}^{-1}\widehat{\mathsf{C}})&\ =\ \operatorname{tr}(\widehat{\mathsf{D}}\,+\,\mathsf{I})\\[5.69046pt]
\log\det(\mathsf{C}^{-1}\widehat{\mathsf{C}})&\ =\ \log\det(\widehat{\mathsf{D}}\,+\,\mathsf{I}).\end{split}

Applying these relations to (
7.3
) gives

\begin{split}d_{\text{KL}}\big(\mathcal{L}(G^{*}|X)\big\|\mathcal{L}(G)\big)&\ =\ \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k(k+1)/2}\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\lambda_{j}(\widehat{{\mathsf{D}}})-\log(\lambda_{j}(\widehat{{\mathsf{D}}})+1)\Big).\end{split}

Noting that
|\lambda_{j}(\widehat{{\mathsf{D}}})|\leq\|\widehat{{\mathsf{D}}}\|_{\textup{op}}\leq 1/2
, the claim (
7.2
) follows from the basic inequality
x-\log(x+1)\leq x^{2}
for
|x|\leq 1/2
.

Finally, to complete the proof of the proposition, it remains to develop a high-probability upper bound on
\|\widehat{\mathsf{D}}\|_{\textup{op}}
. By Lemma
6.3
and Assumption
2.1
(c), there is a constant
c>0
not depending on
n
such that the following bounds hold with probability at least
1-c/n
,

\begin{split}\|\widehat{\mathsf{D}}\|_{\textup{op}}&\ =\ \big\|{\mathsf{J}}_{k}^{-1/2}\big(\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,-\,{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\big)\,{\mathsf{J}}_{k}^{-1/2}\big\|_{\textup{op}}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ \textstyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}({\mathsf{J}}_{k})}\,\big\|\,\widehat{\!{\mathsf{J}}}_{k}\,-\,{\mathsf{J}}_{k}\big\|_{\textup{op}}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \leq\ \frac{c\,k\log(n)^{2\nu+1/2}}{n^{1/2}}.\end{split}

(7.4)

Applying the bound
k\lesssim n^{\frac{1}{2\beta+4}}
to (
7.4
) and (
7.2
) implies the stated result. (It is worth noting that (
7.4
) actually implies a bound on
\widehat{\text{{III}}}_{n}
that is somewhat stronger than the one stated (
7.1
). Nevertheless, (
7.1
) is easier to connect to the rest of the proof of Theorem
2.1
and is all that is needed.)∎

9
Background results

To clarify a bit of terminology in the following result, we say that a random matrix
M\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
is isotropic if
\textup{cov}(\llangle A,M\rrangle,\llangle B,M\rrangle)=\llangle A,B\rrangle
holds for all
A,B\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
.

Lemma 9.1

(
Fang and Koike 2020
, Theorem 2.1)
.

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{n}
be i.i.d. random matrices in
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
that are centered and isotropic. In addition, let
\gamma
denote the standard Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
, and let
\mathscr{C}
denote the class of all convex Borel subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
.
Then, there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\sup_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{C}}\bigg|\textup{{P}}\Big(\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(M_{1}+\dots+M_{n})\in\mathcal{C}\Big)\ -\ \gamma(\mathcal{C})\bigg|\ \leq\ \displaystyle\textstyle\frac{c}{n^{1/2}}\cdot\Big(\textstyle\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\Big)^{1/4}\cdot(\textup{{E}}\|M_{1}\|_{F}^{4})^{1/2}\cdot\big(|\log(\textup{{E}}\|M_{1}\|_{F}^{4}/n)|\vee 1\big).

(9.1)

Remark.
In the paper
Fang and Koike 2020
, this result is stated in terms of i.i.d. random vectors
V_{1},\dots,V_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are centered and isotropic. Letting
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a standard Gaussian vector, and letting
\mathscr{K}
denote the class of all convex Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the result states that there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\sup_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathscr{K}}\Big|\textup{{P}}\big(\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\in\mathcal{K}\big)-\textup{{P}}(Z\in\mathcal{K})\Big|\ \leq\ \textstyle\frac{c\,d^{1/4}}{n^{1/2}}\cdot(\textup{{E}}\|V_{1}\|_{2}^{4})^{1/2}\,\big(|\log(\textup{{E}}\|V_{1}\|_{2}^{4}/n)|\vee 1\big).

(9.2)

This bound directly implies (
9.1
) due to the fact that
(\mathbb{R}^{d(d+1)/2},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
) and
(\mathbb{S}^{d\times d},\llangle\cdot,\cdot\rrangle)
are isometric. In particular, for any isometry
\psi:\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d(d+1)/2}
, it can be checked that if
M_{1},\dots,M_{n}
are i.i.d. centered isotropic random matrices in
\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
, then
\psi(M_{1}),\dots,\psi(M_{n})
are centered isotropic random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d(d+1)/2}
. Furthermore,
\psi
preserves convexity, and the relation
\|M_{1}\|_{F}=\|\psi(M_{1})\|_{2}
holds almost surely.

Lemma 9.2

(
Lopes, Erichson and
Mahoney 2023
, Theorem 2.2)
.

Let
q\geq 3
, and let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be i.i.d. random elements of a real separable Hilbert space with norm
\|\cdot\|
. Also, define the quantity

r(q)\ =\ \frac{q\,\Big(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}\|^{2q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}}{\big\|\textup{{E}}\,\xi_{1}\otimes\xi_{1}\big\|_{\textup{op}}}.

Then, there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\begin{split}\bigg(\textup{{E}}\bigg\|\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\otimes\xi_{i}-\textup{{E}}\xi_{i}\otimes\xi_{i}\bigg\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}\bigg)^{1/q}&\ \leq\ c\,\big\|\textup{{E}}\xi_{1}\otimes\xi_{1}\big\|_{\textup{op}}\Big(\sqrt{\textstyle\frac{r(q)}{n^{1-3/q}}}\,\vee\,\textstyle\frac{r(q)}{n^{1-3/q}}\Big).\end{split}

In particular, when
q\geq\log(n)\vee 3
,

\bigg(\textup{{E}}\bigg\|\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\otimes\xi_{i}-\textup{{E}}\xi_{i}\otimes\xi_{i}\bigg\|_{\textup{op}}^{q}\bigg)^{1/q}\ \leq\ c\bigg(\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|\textup{{E}}\xi_{1}\otimes\xi_{1}\|_{\textup{op}}^{1/2}\,(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}\|^{2q})^{\frac{1}{2q}}\bigg)\bigvee\bigg(\textstyle\frac{q}{n}\,(\textup{{E}}\|\xi_{1}\|^{2q})^{\frac{1}{q}}\bigg).

The following result is a version of Rosenthal’s inequality, which is a consequence of Theorem 1 in
Talagrand 1989
.

Lemma 9.3
.

Fix
q\geq 2
, and let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be independent centered random variables. Then, there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\bigg\|\tsum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\bigg\|_{L^{q}}\ \leq\ c\,q\,\bigg(\,\bigg\|\tsum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\bigg\|_{L^{2}}\!\textstyle\bigvee\,\displaystyle\Big(\tsum_{i=1}^{n}\|\xi_{i}\|_{L^{q}}^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\bigg).
