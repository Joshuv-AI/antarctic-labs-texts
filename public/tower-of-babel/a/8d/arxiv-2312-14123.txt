The interacting vacuum and tensions: a comparison of theoretical models Journal: Physics of the Dark Universe
Marco Sebastianutti, Natalie B. Hogg, Marco Bruni

Abstract
We analyse three interacting vacuum dark energy models with the aim of exploring whether the H 0 H_{0} and σ 8 \sigma_{8} tensions can be simultaneously resolved in such models. We present the first ever derivation of the covariant gauge-invariant perturbation formalism for the interacting vacuum scenario, and, for the sub-class of geodesic cold dark matter models, connect the evolution of perturbation variables in this approach to the familiar cosmological observables. We show how H 0 H_{0} and σ 8 \sigma_{8} evolve in three interacting vacuum models: firstly, a simple linear coupling between the vacuum and cold dark matter; secondly, a coupling which mimics the behaviour of a Chaplygin gas; and finally a coupling which mimics the Shan–Chen fluid dark energy model. We identify, if any, the regions of parameter space which would correspond to a simultaneous resolution of both tensions in these models. When constraints from observational data are added, we show how all the models described are constrained to be close to their Λ \Lambda CDM limits.

Interacting vacuum and its covariant and gauge-invariant perturbations

Perturbation theory is an essential part of cosmology, allowing us to understand the formation and growth of the observed structure on the largest scales. The common way to deal with perturbations is to specify a background spacetime and decompose physical quantities into a homogeneous background and inhomogeneous perturbations. Although GR is covariant, i.e. manifestly independent from the coordinate choice, splitting variables into a background part and a perturbation is not a covariant procedure, and therefore introduces a coordinate or gauge dependence, as first geometrically elucidated by
[

In order to deal with this problem, Bardeen-type variables can be used
[

. These are linear combinations of gauge-dependent quantities specifically constructed to be gauge-invariant variables
at first order
. Although this procedure can be extended to higher order
[
52
]
, the use of the Bardeen formalism can make it difficult to interpret the results of a given calculation, since the physical and geometrical meaning of the variables is often obscure unless a gauge is specified.

An alternative approach is to use covariantly-defined spatial gradients of physical quantities, such as the energy density, the expansion scalar or the 3-Ricci curvature. In this approach no approximation is used in defining the variables, which are then exactly defined in any spacetime.

2.1
Covariant description of the interacting vacuum

Following
[
31
]
, let us now consider a covariant description of both the perfect fluid and vacuum components, as well as the interaction between the two.

The energy–momentum tensor of a perfect fluid is:

T_{\mu\nu}=(\rho+p)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+p\,g_{\mu\nu},

(1)

where
\rho
is the fluid energy density and
p
its pressure, which vanishes in the case of a CDM (or baryonic) component;
u^{\mu}
is the 4-velocity of the fluid and the eigenvector of
T_{\mu\nu}
which defines the fluid rest frame.
The energy–momentum tensor of a vacuum component,
\check{T}_{\mu\nu}
, is proportional to the metric

\check{T}_{\mu\nu}=-Vg_{\mu\nu},

(2)

and by comparison with the perfect fluid energy–momentum tensor in (
1
) we can identify the vacuum energy density and pressure as
\rho=V=-p
; its equation of state is thus
w\equiv p/\rho=-1
.

The interaction between the perfect fluid and the vacuum energy is introduced in the following way:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\nu}

\displaystyle=-Q_{\nu},

(3)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\check{T}^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\nu}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\nu}V=Q_{\nu},

(4)

where the interaction 4-vector
Q^{\mu}
stands for the energy–momentum flow between the two components.
This scenario reduces to the standard
\Lambda
CDM non-interacting limit when the interaction vanishes, i.e.
Q^{\mu}=0
and
V=\Lambda/\kappa=
constant. Adding (
3
) to (
4
) we immediately recover the covariant energy–momentum conservation relation

\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}_{\,\,\text{(tot)}}=\nabla_{\mu}(T^{\mu\nu}+\check{T}^{\mu\nu})=0,

(5)

as implied by the Einstein equations and the Bianchi identities.

In general, for any given 4-velocity
n^{\mu}
(a timelike vector), one can project a tensorial quantity into a part parallel to
n^{\mu}
and a part orthogonal to it by the use of a projector tensor
P^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\nu}\equiv\delta^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\nu}+n^{\mu}n_{\nu}
. Using specifically the fluid 4-velocity
u^{\mu}
, we can then define the projector

h_{\mu\nu}\equiv g_{\mu\nu}+u_{\mu}u_{\nu},

(6)

and then the energy–momentum exchange
Q^{\mu}
can be decomposed into two parts,

Q^{\mu}=Qu^{\mu}+f^{\mu}.

(7)

For observers comoving with the perfect fluid,
Q=-Q_{\mu}u^{\mu}
represents the energy exchange between the two components and
f^{\mu}=h^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\nu}Q^{\nu}
represents the momentum transfer; from the above definition we have
f_{\mu}u^{\mu}=0
. In this paper we shall focus on the case of a pure energy exchange in the frame of the fluid
u^{\mu}
,
f^{\mu}=0
.

It is clear from (
2
) that any timelike 4-vector is an eigenvector of
\check{T}_{\mu\nu}
with eigenvalue
V
. This means that
V
is the energy density of vacuum for any observer; in other words, the vacuum energy does not have a unique 4-velocity, or else, absolute motion with respect to vacuum could be detected, as observed by
[
58
]
. However, one can use
Q^{\mu}=-\nabla^{\mu}V
to define a preferred 4-velocity for the vacuum,

\check{u}^{\mu}=\frac{-\nabla^{\mu}V}{\sqrt{\lvert\nabla_{\nu}V\nabla^{\nu}V\rvert}},

(8)

so that in the
\check{u}^{\mu}
frame there is no momentum transfer, as shown in Eq. (
15
).

2.2
Covariant gauge-invariant formalism

Having defined the covariant vacuum, we can proceed to the CGI formalism for perturbations. Central to the CGI approach is the Stewart and Walker lemma
[
44
]
for the first order perturbation
\delta\textbf{T}
of a tensorial quantity
T
about a given background spacetime, where
T
acquires value
\textbf{T}_{0}
;
the lemma states that:

The linear perturbation
\delta\textbf{T}
of a tensorial quantity
T
with value
\textbf{T}_{0}
on the background spacetime is gauge-invariant if and only if one of the following holds:

1.

\textbf{T}_{0}
vanishes;

2.

\textbf{T}_{0}
is a constant scalar; or

3.

\textbf{T}_{0}
is a constant linear combination of Kronecker deltas.

This simply follows from the fact that if
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the generator of the gauge transformation
\tilde{x}^{\mu}=x^{\mu}+\lambda\xi^{\mu}
on the background spacetime, where
\lambda
is an expansion parameter, then in general
\delta\tilde{\textbf{T}}=\delta\textbf{T}+\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\textbf{T}_{0}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\textbf{T}_{0}
is the Lie derivative of
\textbf{T}_{0}
along
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, see
[
50
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
for more details and the extension to higher orders.

In the following, after introducing the covariant spacetime splitting, we will specialise to the FLRW background. In order to define CGI perturbations of the latter, according to the first condition of the lemma we simply need to find covariantly defined quantities which vanish in FLRW.
3
3

3

We do not consider the second and third conditions of the lemma, as in an FLRW Universe the only invariantly defined constant is the cosmological constant and no tensors that are constant products of Kronecker deltas occur naturally.
In this formalism, therefore, one starts from exact nonlinear equations for these quantities, then, the linearisation scheme simply consists of linearising these equations with respect to the CGI variables. Quantities which do not vanish in the FLRW background spacetime, such as the fluid density
\rho
and pressure
p

Finally, assuming

, we can also write down the exact Hamiltonian constraint relating the spatial Ricci scalar of the hypersurfaces orthogonal to the perfect fluid flow

with the shear and energy densities:

{}^{(3)}R=2\left[\sigma^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\Theta^{2}+\kappa\left(\rho+V\right)\right].

(20)

This shows that at first order, when we can neglect the CGI
\sigma^{2}
term, there are only three degrees of freedom, e.g. the two energy densities and the 3-curvature, with the fourth variable given by the Hamiltonian constraint.

2.2.3
Spatially flat FLRW background

We examine our results thus far in a spatially flat (
{}^{(3)}R=0
) FLRW background, in which shear and vorticity do not contribute and
a_{\mu}=0
. In such a background the expansion scalar is
\Theta=3H
and the Raychaudhuri equation (

\displaystyle\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}=\dot{H}+H^{2}=-\frac{\kappa}{6}(\rho-2V),

(21)

where
a
is the cosmic scale factor,
H\equiv\dot{a}/a
is the Hubble expansion rate and we consider a perfect fluid with vanishing pressure,
p=0
, which corresponds to non-relativistic matter or dust.

In both the non-interacting (
V=\Lambda/\kappa
) and the interacting (
V=V(t)
) case, the vacuum energy accelerates the expansion of spacetime, working against the action of matter. However, in the latter case, the acceleration can be enhanced or suppressed compared to the acceleration in the former case; this depends on the direction of the energy transfer between vacuum and matter and the specific form of the coupling function, as we will demonstrate below.

The Friedmann constraint equation in this FLRW background, obtained from (
20
), simply reads

\displaystyle H^{2}=\frac{\kappa}{3}\left(\rho+V\right).

(22)

2.3
Geodesic CDM and a pure energy exchange

From now on, instead of referring to the perfect fluid energy density as
\rho
we use
\rho_{\rm m}=\rho_{\rm b}+\rho_{\rm c}
the total matter energy density, namely the sum of the baryonic and CDM components. As for CDM, the baryonic component is modelled by a pressureless fluid (
p_{\rm b}=0
). We therefore study a 3-components Universe two of which, CDM and vacuum, are interacting.

We also wish to consider a scenario where the clustering of CDM is only due to gravity, i.e. we need to make sure that the CDM particles do not feel a fifth force due to the interaction.
This means that there must be no acceleration acting on the CDM particles; in other words, CDM must remain geodesic.
By examining (
18
), we can see that for the acceleration
a^{\mu}_{\,\rm c}=0
, the CGI variables on the right-hand side must vanish.

While
P^{\mu}=0
for CDM, to ensure
V^{\mu}
vanishes we set
f^{\mu}=0
in (
14
). This implies that the vacuum itself remains homogeneous in the matter rest frame and any interaction between the vacuum and CDM will be a pure energy exchange, also homogeneous in the matter rest frame, with no momentum transfer. By virtue of (
8
), this also means that the vacuum 4-velocity equals the CDM one, i.e.
\check{u}^{\mu}=u^{\mu}_{\,\rm c}
.

The geodesic CDM assumption implies that the sound speed,
c_{s}^{2}
, of vacuum plus matter density perturbations is zero, meaning that the Jeans length vanishes and matter, affected by the vacuum–CDM interaction, clusters on all scales as in standard
\Lambda
CDM
[
33
]
.

It is important to stress that this geodesic interaction is an approximation valid only at early times and on large scales. Via the imposition of the geodesic condition, the CDM 4-velocity defines a potential flow, i.e.
u^{\mu}_{\rm\,c}\propto\nabla^{\mu}V
, which gives rise to irrotational CDM. At late times, when not only scalar but also vector perturbations are relevant for structure formation, it is expected that non-linear structure growth leads to vorticity
[
64
,
65
]
. Therefore, the assumption of a pure energy exchange, which allows CDM to remain geodesic, must fail below some length scale. If irrotational CDM were present at all scales, distinctive observational features, such as the overproduction of supermassive black holes, would have arisen
[
66
]
.

While the spatially flat FLRW interacting background remains unaffected by the geodesic CDM condition, this is not the case for linear perturbations. In the CGI framework, the fact that the momentum transfer
f^{\mu}=0
forces the vacuum to remain unperturbed, i.e.
V^{\mu}=0
. This simplifies considerably the way vacuum–CDM interaction enters into the first order differential equations for the CGI variables
\Delta^{\mu}
,
Z^{\mu}
and
R^{\mu}
. In particular, we show that some care must be taken when choosing the explicit form of the
exact
(and not background) energy exchange
Q
.

2.3.1
Evolution of first order perturbations

In the following and for the rest of this paper we will write the comoving fractional total matter density spatial gradient as
\Delta_{\rm m}^{\mu}
. In addition, we make the assumption that the baryonic component is comoving with CDM at all times. In the CGI language this results in
\Theta_{\rm b}=\Theta_{\rm c}=\Theta
, meaning that the 4-velocities of baryons and CDM coincide with the one of the perfect fluid, which is now the total matter 4-velocity, i.e.
u^{\mu}_{\rm m}=u^{\mu}
.

As a consequence of the geodesic condition
V^{\mu}=0
, in turn this implies that

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla^{\mu}Q=HV^{\mu}-\frac{Q}{\rho_{\rm m}}V^{\mu}+\dot{V}^{\mu}=0,

(28)

and the dynamical first order differential equations (
23
), (
25
) and (
27
) reduce to the system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs), cf.
[
34
,
67
]

\displaystyle\dot{\Delta}^{\mu}_{\rm m}

\displaystyle=-Z^{\mu}+\frac{Q}{\rho_{\rm m}}\Delta^{\mu}_{\rm m},

(29)

\displaystyle\dot{Z}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-2HZ^{\mu}-\frac{\kappa\rho_{\rm m}}{2}\Delta^{\mu}_{\rm m},

(30)

\displaystyle\dot{R}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0.

(31)

If we express the linearised Hamiltonian constraint in (
26
) as a function of
Z^{\mu}
,

Z^{\mu}=-\frac{1}{4a^{2}H}R^{\mu}+\frac{\kappa\rho_{\rm m}}{2H}\Delta^{\mu}_{\rm m},

(32)

we can use the above to write a first order inhomogeneous differential equation for
\Delta_{\rm m}^{\mu}

\displaystyle\dot{\Delta}^{\mu}_{\rm m}=\left(\frac{Q}{\rho_{\rm m}}-\frac{\kappa\rho_{\rm m}}{2H}\right)\Delta^{\mu}_{\rm m}+\frac{1}{4a^{2}H}R^{\mu},

(33)

or alternatively, by differentiating with respect to cosmic time, a more standard second order homogeneous differential equation,

\displaystyle\ddot{\Delta}^{\mu}_{\rm m}

\displaystyle+\left(2H-\frac{Q}{\rho_{\rm m}}\right)\dot{\Delta}^{\mu}_{\rm m}

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{\rho_{\rm m}\left(\dot{Q}+5HQ\right)+Q^{2}}{\rho_{\rm m}^{2}}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\rho_{\rm m}\right]\Delta^{\mu}_{\rm m}=0.

(34)

The growth of matter perturbations and hence the formation of large-scale structures in the (geodesic CDM) interacting vacuum scenario is enclosed in the growing mode of the above second order ODE. Such a solution is crucially connected with familiar observable quantities in cosmology such as the growth rate
f
and
\sigma_{8}
, the amplitude of the matter power spectrum on a scale of
8\mathrm{h}^{-1}
Mpc.

2.3.2
A specific type of energy transfer

If, in addition to the geodesic condition for which
V_{\mu}=0
, we assume an interaction of the type

a generic function of the vacuum energy density, we find that

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla_{\mu}Q\propto\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla_{\mu}\left[\Theta f(V)\right]=\frac{f(V)}{a}Z_{\mu}+3Hf^{\prime}(V)V_{\mu},

where the prime denotes a derivative w.r.t.

However, this does not imply that in the geodesic CDM framework models with a background energy exchange of the form

are unphysical. In fact, such a background form could come from a covariant

and

\displaystyle Q=\frac{q}{3}\Theta V+F(X),

which in the background reduces to

We do not intend to speculate further on possible choices for the function

, our intention here being only to underline the physical consistency of this class of geodesic CDM models at first perturbative order.
In the next sections, after re-expressing the second order ODE (

the CGI matter perturbation variable
with standard observational probes of structure growth.

2.3.3
Aggregation of matter

Following

40

\displaystyle a\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla^{\nu}\Delta^{\mu}_{\text{m}}\equiv\Delta^{\mu\nu}_{\text{m}}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}_{\text{m}}+\Sigma^{\mu\nu}_{\text{m}}+\frac{1}{3}\Delta_{\text{m}}h^{\mu\nu}.

(37)

In analogy with the
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla_{\nu}u_{\mu}

for the symmetric traceless part which encodes anisotropy and

for the trace part, which is directly related to the spherically symmetric spatial variation of density where matter accumulates. This latter quantity is associated with the spatial aggregation of matter which we might expect to reflect the existence of high density structures in the Universe.

General inhomogeneities will have all three of these components – for example, there could exist aggregation of matter (
\Delta_{\rm m}>0
) in a deformed structure (

Using the linear identities detailed in

\displaystyle\frac{\rm{d}^{2}}{\rm{d}a^{2}}\Delta_{\rm m}

\displaystyle+\left[\left(a\mathcal{H}\right)^{-1}\frac{\rm{d}}{\rm{d}a}\left(g\mathcal{H}\right)+a^{-2}\left(g-\frac{3}{2}\Omega_{\rm m}\right)\right]\Delta_{\rm m}=0,

2.3.4
Effect of perturbations on observable quantities

The above discussion takes on a more familiar form with the imposition of the comoving-orthogonal gauge which, for a geodesic CDM interaction, automatically implies a synchronous frame (we show this explicitly in

). This leads to the CGI quantity
\Delta_{\rm m}
reducing to the matter density contrast

f\sigma_{8}(a)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{8}}{\mathrm{d}\ln a},

(39)

where the growth rate of cosmic structures

f(a)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln D^{+}_{\rm m}}{\mathrm{d}\ln a}.

(40)

The parameter

\sigma_{8}
is the root mean square amplitude of matter density fluctuations on a comoving scale of

Mpc,

\sigma_{R}^{2}(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{3}P_{\rm linear}(k,a)}{2\pi^{2}}\,W^{2}(kR)\,\mathrm{d}\ln k,

(41)

where
P_{\rm linear}(k,a)
is the linear matter power spectrum and

is the window function.
The scale factor dependence of the
\sigma_{8}
parameter
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{8}(a)=\sigma_{8}(1)\frac{D^{+}_{\rm m}(a)}{D^{+}_{\rm m}(1)},

(42)

which is a common choice to describe the normalisation of matter density fluctuations.
Due to the contribution of the interaction to the evolution of the matter density contrast (
2.3.3
), the original growth rate which we can call

(as defined in (
40
)) picks up an additional term
[
33
]
,

(43)

where

was defined above. This means that observational probes such as redshift space distortions (RSDs; the distortions of galaxy clustering when observed in redshift space due to their peculiar velocities) measure

rather than
f_{\rm bare}\sigma_{8}
in interacting vacuum cosmologies. For notational simplicity, we will refer to
f_{\rm i}
as

from now on. This is relevant due to our later use of constraints obtained on interacting models using RSD data.

3
Model choice and analysis method

Up to this point, we have derived the dynamical equations for both background and linear perturbations and introduced some relevant quantities for the study of the growth of cosmic structures in the interacting vacuum scenario, while remaining agnostic about the exact form that the interaction takes.

We now describe the specific models for the interaction that we choose to study in this paper. With the specific interaction models chosen, we can solve the equations for the background and first order perturbations in each model, and make plots of observable quantities such as
h
and
f\sigma_{8}
. With the cosmological behaviour of these models made clear in these plots, we will show situations in which cosmological tensions can or cannot be resolved in the interacting vacuum picture. We remind the reader that from now on we set
\kappa=1
.

3.1
Model choice

In a geodesic CDM framework, we consider three interacting vacuum models: the first with a background interaction linear in the vacuum energy density
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
, the second with a background interaction mimicking a generalised Chaplygin gas
[
36
,
37
]
and the third with a background interaction inspired by a Shan–Chen fluid
[
38
]
. While the generalised Chaplygin gas and Shan–Chen dark energy models were originally proposed as fluid dark energy models
[
27
,
29
]
, for the purposes of the comparisons in this work, we express them as interacting vacuum models.

3.1.1
Interacting linear vacuum model

In the interacting linear vacuum (LV) model
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
, the background coupling function is given by

Q=qHV,

(44)

where the magnitude of the constant dimensionless coupling parameter
q
controls the strength of the vacuum–CDM interaction and its sign dictates the direction of the energy transfer.
Since both the vacuum energy density,
V
, and the Hubble function,
H
, are positive quantities, we can see that for
q>0
the vacuum energy density grows and, due to total energy conservation, CDM decays; in other words, we have an energy transfer from CDM to vacuum. Conversely, for
q<0
the vacuum energy density decays and CDM grows; the non-interacting or
\Lambda
CDM limit occurs for
q=0
.

Integrating the continuity equations (
16
) and (
17
) with today initial conditions,
a=1
, we obtain the following analytical solutions for the CDM and vacuum energy densities:

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{c}}(a)

\displaystyle=3H^{2}_{0}\left[\Omega_{\rm c,0}a^{-3}+\Omega_{\rm V,0}\frac{q}{3+q}\left(a^{-3}-a^{q}\right)\right],

(45)

\displaystyle V(a)

\displaystyle=3H^{2}_{0}\Omega_{\rm V,0}a^{q},

(46)

where
H_{0}
is the Hubble parameter and
\Omega_{\rm c,0}
and
\Omega_{\rm V,0}
are the current (i.e.
z=0
) values of the dimensionless density parameters for CDM and vacuum.

From the Friedmann constraint equation (
22
), we derive analytical expressions for the reduced dimensionless Hubble function
h(a)
, the dimensionless total matter density
\Omega_{\rm m}(a)
and the dimensionless interaction function
g(a)
:

\displaystyle h(a)

\displaystyle=h_{0}\left[\frac{3\left(1-\Omega_{\rm m,0}\right)a^{3+q}+3\Omega_{\rm m,0}+q}{\left(3+q\right)a^{3}}\right]^{1/2},

(47)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm m}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\Omega_{\rm m,0}+q-q(1-\Omega_{\rm m,0})a^{3+q}}{3\Omega_{\rm m,0}+q+3(1-\Omega_{\rm m,0})a^{3+q}},

(48)

\displaystyle g(a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(1-\Omega_{\rm m}(a)\right)}{\Omega_{\rm m}(a)}q,

(49)

where
\Omega_{\rm m,0}=\Omega_{\rm b,0}+\Omega_{\rm c,0}
and

and

[

Moving now to first order perturbations, we numerically integrate (

3.1.2
Interacting generalised Chaplygin gas model

The second model we consider is derived from the generalised Chaplygin gas (gCg)
[
26
,
27
]
, a unified dark matter fluid characterised by the equation of state

\displaystyle p_{{}_{\rm gCg}}=-A\rho_{{}_{\rm gCg}}^{-\alpha}.

(50)

Following
[
35
]
, we think of the interacting CDM and vacuum so that their total energy density,
\rho_{\rm tot}
, is the same as that of the gCg:

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm tot}

\displaystyle\equiv\rho_{{}_{\rm gCg}}=\rho_{\rm c}+V,

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=-p_{{}_{\rm gCg}}.

(51)

Using (
50
) and (
51
) we find

A=V(\rho_{\rm c}+V)^{\alpha}
and, inserting this expression into (
17
), we obtain the background energy transfer function for the interacting generalised Chaplygin gas model
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
:

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=3\alpha H\frac{V\rho_{\rm c}}{V+\rho_{\rm c}}

(52)

The sign and magnitude of the constant dimensionless coupling parameter
\alpha
denotes the direction and strength of the CDM–vacuum interaction: if
\alpha>0
the energy is transferred from CDM to vacuum, vice versa for
\alpha<0
.

From (
50
) and (
51
) and integrating the continuity equation for the single component generalised Chaplygin gas with initial conditions set at
a=1
, we obtain the following analytical solution for the total energy density:

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm tot}(a)=

\displaystyle\;3H_{0}^{2}\left(\Omega_{\rm c,0}+\Omega_{\rm V,0}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\Omega_{\rm V,0}+\Omega_{\rm c,0}a^{-3(1+\alpha)}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}.

(53)

From the Friedmann constraint equation (
22
), we get

\displaystyle h(a)

\displaystyle=h_{0}\left[\Omega_{\rm b,0}a^{-3}+\left(\Omega_{\rm c,0}+\Omega_{\rm V,0}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\left(\Omega_{\rm V,0}+\Omega_{\rm c,0}a^{-3(1+\alpha)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

(54)

and, substituting (
52
) in the dimensionless interaction parameter
g
, we find

\displaystyle g(a)=-3\alpha\frac{\Omega_{\rm c}(a)}{\Omega_{\rm m}(a)}\frac{\Omega_{\rm V}(a)}{\Omega_{\rm c}(a)+\Omega_{\rm V}(a)}.

(55)

By looking at
\Omega_{\rm V}(a)
explicitly,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm V}(a)

\displaystyle=a^{3(1+\alpha)}\Omega_{\rm V,0}\left(\Omega_{\rm c,0}+\Omega_{\rm V,0}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\Omega_{\rm c,0}+a^{3(1+\alpha)}\Omega_{\rm V,0}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\left(\Omega_{\rm c,0}+\Omega_{\rm V,0}\right)+\left(\Omega_{\rm c,0}+\Omega_{\rm V,0}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\left(\Omega_{\rm c,0}+a^{3(1+\alpha)}\Omega_{\rm V,0}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\right]^{-1},

(56)

it is easy to see that a standard matter dominated era is again recovered for
a\ll 1
, when
\Omega_{\rm V}\simeq 0
and
g\simeq 0
. We deal with linear matter density perturbations in the same way as in the interacting linear model.

3.1.3
Interacting Shan–Chen model

The third interacting vacuum model we analyse originates from the non-linear Shan–Chen (SC) equation of state which was originally used to describe matter in lattice kinetic theory
[
28
]
. This equation of state was then repurposed to describe a dark energy fluid
[
29
,
30
]
and subsequently an interacting vacuum dark energy
[
38
]
. The non-linear Shan–Chen equation of state is

\displaystyle p_{{}_{\rm SC}}

\displaystyle=\beta\rho_{*}\left[\frac{\rho_{{}_{\rm SC}}}{\rho_{*}}+\frac{G}{2}\psi^{2}\right],

(57)

with

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha\frac{\rho_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{411758}{163840}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\rm SC$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{411758}{163840}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\rm SC$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{306367}{163840}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\rm SC$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{306368}{163840}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\rm SC$}}}}}}}{\rho_{*}}}.

(58)

The parameters
\beta
,
G
and
\alpha
are all dimensionless and control various aspects of the behaviour of the fluid (see
[
38
]
for an overview of the behaviour of the interacting vacuum Shan–Chen model). The parameter
\rho_{*}
is a characteristic energy density scale which we keep fixed to the value of the critical density today,
\rho_{*}=3H_{0}^{2}
. This is to ensure that the interaction has maximum effect at late times, but such a model could be adapted to create an early dark energy model by setting this energy scale to, for example, the critical density at recombination.
We build the corresponding interacting vacuum model by treating CDM as pressureless dust and vacuum energy as a SC dark energy, i.e.
V=\rho_{{}_{\rm SC}}
. From the SC continuity equation

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{{}_{\rm SC}}=-3H\left(\rho_{{}_{\rm SC}}+p_{{}_{\rm SC}}\right),

(59)

substituting (
57
) and (
58
), we obtain the background energy transfer parameterisation that enters in (
16
) and (
17
),

Q=3qH\left[\left(1+\beta\right)V+\frac{\beta G}{2}\rho_{*}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha\frac{V}{\rho_{*}}}\right)^{2}\right],

(60)

where, as in the previous models we have described, the dimensionless coupling strength parameter,
q
, controls the overall strength and direction of the interaction.
5
5

5

Note that in (
60
) we have absorbed a minus sign in the
q
parameter. In this manner, when
\beta=0
, the SC model reduces to an interacting linear vacuum.

Due to the complexity of this parameterisation, it is not possible to obtain analytical solutions for this model. Instead, we numerically solve the dynamical equations for both the background and first order perturbations, setting the initial conditions as in the other two discussed models.

3.2
Analysis method

Having solved the equations for the background and first order perturbations in each of the models described above, we plot various observables to understand the different cosmologies produced by each interacting model. To do this, we need to choose some reference values for the parameters, such as
q
, so that a wide range of possible cosmological effects can be seen in each model. We then use results from the literature to select values of the model parameters which are favoured by observable data, and plot the same cosmological quantities again, in order to make a qualitative comparison. The theoretical behaviour of the models allows us to identify which parts of parameter space may or may not lead to resolutions of the cosmological tensions in each model, whereas the plots using parameter values informed by data will show us how these models actually fare when confronted by observations.

3.2.1
Initial conditions

For what we will refer to as the theory plots, the initial conditions of (
16
), (
17
) and (
2.3.3
) are fixed during matter domination, at
a=\frac{1}{1+1000}
, when the vacuum–CDM interaction is negligible for the models we consider. We do this by evolving a reference
\Lambda
CDM cosmology backwards in time and fixing the initial conditions at the aforementioned redshift. This amounts to turning the interaction on starting from a common
\Lambda
CDM cosmology in the past, characterised by the parameters

h_{0}=0.70
,
\Omega_{\rm b,0}=0.05
,
\Omega_{\rm c,0}=0.25
and
\sigma_{8}=0.80
. The interaction will then affect the future evolution in each model depending on the sign and magnitude of the corresponding coupling strengths.

For what we will refer to as the data plots, the initial conditions are fixed to the current i.e.
a=1
values as constrained by observational data. We specifically use the values from
[
34
]
for the linear vacuum,
[
37
]
for the generalised Chaplygin gas, and
[
38
]
for the Shan–Chen model. We list the numerical values for the parameters in tables in each subsection.

5
Conclusions

Our work had two distinct aims: on the one hand, to provide a complete description of the interacting vacuum scenario using the covariant gauge-invariant approach; and on the other, to explore three specific models of interaction with the goal of identifying why these models may or may not be able to resolve the well-known cosmological tensions.

In section
2
, we began by developing the covariant theory of an interacting vacuum, before introducing the gauge-invariant approach and the relevant covariant and gauge-invariant quantities. We provided a physical interpretation of these quantities, and, specialising to a spatially flat FLRW background, imposed the geodesic condition for the CDM particles by mandating that the interaction between CDM and vacuum is a pure energy exchange. In the last part of the section, we developed the evolution of first order perturbations in the CGI approach and, after a brief aside on a specific choice of energy exchange in the geodesic CDM framework, we connected them with
structure growth in cosmology and the familiar observable quantity
\sigma_{8}
. In section
3
we then introduced the specific models that were studied in this work: the interacting linear vacuum model
[
31
,
33
]
, the interacting generalised Chaplygin gas model
[
36
,
37
]
and the interacting Shan–Chen model
[
38
]
.

In section
4
we presented the results from the analysis of the above-described models. In the case of the interacting linear vacuum, we found that, due to a minimum in the function
\sigma_{8}(q)
around
q=0
, the two cosmological tensions can never be simultaneously resolved. In fact, for values of the coupling strength sufficiently close to zero,
\sigma_{8}
will always remain too close to
\Lambda
CDM in this model, regardless of the direction of the coupling (CDM to vacuum or vice versa). The sign of the coupling does alter the effect of the interaction on the background expansion, with a positive coupling (growing vacuum) leading to an increase in
H_{0}
.

The interacting generalised Chaplygin gas model had a similar behaviour at the background level, but there is no minimum in the
\sigma_{8}(\alpha)
function close to the
\Lambda
CDM limit of
\alpha=0
. This means that this model has the potential to resolve both tensions simultaneously. This would require a positive value for the coupling strength i.e. a growing vacuum. However, when confronted with observational data, the opposite occurs: both tensions are simultaneously worsened, and a decaying vacuum is favoured. This result was obtained with relatively minimal data: just CMB TT and low-
\ell
polarisation data. The interacting gCg model was also recently analysed with a larger dataset consisting of Planck 2018 CMB TTTEEE spectra, RSD and JLA SNIa
[
81
]
. In that case, a coupling between dark matter and vacuum was indicated by the data, but the cosmological tensions were still not fully resolved.

Lastly, we examined the interacting Shan–Chen model. In this case, we found that there is also a region of parameter space in which both tensions can be resolved simultaneously, but that observational data constrains the model parameters to be close to the
\Lambda
CDM limit, ending up with a worsening of the
\sigma_{8}
tension. Furthermore, we saw that, due to the two additional parameters in this model with respect to
\Lambda
CDM, the parameter space contains complex bimodal features, making it challenging for a Metropolis–Hastings MCMC analysis to sample from. It would then be interesting for such models to be analysed with, for example, a nested sampling method, to ensure that all the features of the parameter space are fully explored.

From these results we conclude that simple interacting vacuum dark energy models like the linear vacuum model analysed here are not able to resolve the
H_{0}
and
\sigma_{8}
tensions simultaneously; or one tension may be slightly relaxed, always at the expense of exacerbating the other. In more complicated models like the interacting Chaplygin gas and the interacting Shan–Chen model, there exist regions of parameter space where both tensions may simultaneously be resolved, but observational data tends to constrain the model parameters to be extremely close to their
\Lambda
CDM limits, irrespective of the existence of such regions. Since all of these models have additional parameters with respect to
\Lambda
CDM, they will likely be disfavoured from a statistical standpoint. This can be seen in the
\chi^{2}
values for the Shan–Chen model presented in
[
38
]
(all larger than
\Lambda
CDM) and the Bayes factors for a time-dependent linear vacuum found by
[
82
]
(no evidence that the model should be preferred over
\Lambda
CDM).

Our results are in agreement with recent works which studied dark energy models which have small deviations from a
\Lambda
CDM background expansion, or small deviations from Newton’s constant represented by an effective gravitational constant,
G_{\rm eff}

[
83
,
84
]
. In these cases, it was shown that a simultaneous resolution of the
H_{0}
and
\sigma_{8}
tensions is very difficult, with the evolving dark energy equation of state
w(z)=p/\rho
being required to cross the phantom divide i.e.
w=-1
at some point in its history, or else
G_{\rm eff}
needing to be significantly reduced. For the latter criterion, interacting models with momentum transfer may provide a way forward
[
85
,
86
]
.

In conclusion, if statistically convincing resolutions to both cosmological tensions are simultaneously sought, other avenues besides the simple interacting vacuum scenarios which only have energy exchange studied here should be prioritised for exploration. However, in light of the recent reduction of the
\sigma_{8}
tension with the
Hillipop
Planck likelihood
[
19
]
, it may only be necessary to search for models in which the
H_{0}
tension alone is resolved. Furthermore, exploring models with spatial curvature may be a promising avenue for investigation
[
87
]
.

Appendix A
Equivalence of the CGI and Bardeen variables at first order

The standard way to deal with perturbations in cosmology is to use the Bardeen variables
[
45
]
. These are linear combinations of gauge-dependent quantities built such that they are gauge-invariant at first order in perturbations.
The Bardeen approach, unlike the CGI one, is based on coordinates; therefore its variables acquire a physical meaning only once a specific gauge is chosen.

By expanding the CGI quantities into a background and a first order part, we recover the corresponding gauge-invariant Bardeen variables. In this appendix we show the equivalence,
at first order
, of the two methods by decomposing a particularly relevant CGI quantity, the comoving fractional density spatial gradient
\Delta^{\mu}
. We refer the reader to
[
39
]
for a complete list of identities.

We start from the linearly perturbed spatially flat FLRW metric in the form:

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=-(1+2\phi){\rm d}t^{2}+2a\partial_{i}B\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}x^{i}

\displaystyle+a^{2}[(1-2\psi)\delta_{ij}+2\partial_{i}\partial_{j}E]{\rm d}x^{i}{\rm d}x^{j},

(61)

where we take into account scalar perturbations only.
In such a spacetime, an exact variable, for instance a scalar
f
, gets split into a homogeneous background part, denoted with a bar, and an inhomogeneous linear perturbation, i.e.
f(t,x^{i})=\bar{f}(t)+\delta f(t,x^{i})
. In general, this splitting is not a covariant procedure and leads to a gauge (or coordinate) dependence as can be noticed by looking at (
A
).
The perturbed 4-velocity of a perfect fluid,
u^{\mu}=\bar{u}^{\mu}+\delta u^{\mu}
, is given by:

\displaystyle u_{\mu}=[-1-\phi,\partial_{i}\theta],\quad\quad u^{\mu}=[1-\phi,a^{-1}\partial^{i}v],

(62)

where
\theta=a(v+B)
.

The time derivative of the scalar
f
reads:

\displaystyle\dot{f}

\displaystyle\equiv u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}f,

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{u}^{\mu}+\delta u^{\mu}\right)\nabla_{\mu}\left(\bar{f}+\delta f\right),

\displaystyle=\dot{\bar{f}}+\left(\delta f\right)^{\cdot}-\phi\dot{\bar{f}}.

(63)

Projecting with the spatial metric
h_{\mu\nu}
at linear order

\displaystyle h_{\mu}^{\,\,\,\nu}

\displaystyle=\bar{h}_{\mu}^{\,\,\,\nu}+\delta h_{\mu}^{\nu},

(64)

\displaystyle=\bar{h}_{\mu}^{\,\,\,\nu}+\bar{u}_{\mu}\delta u^{\nu}+\delta u_{\mu}\bar{u}^{\nu},

(65)

we obtain the spatial gradient of
f
:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla_{\mu}f

\displaystyle\equiv h_{\mu}^{\,\,\,\nu}\nabla_{\nu}f,

\displaystyle=\bar{h}_{\mu}^{\,\,\,\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\left(\bar{f}+\delta f\right)+\bar{u}_{\mu}\delta u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\bar{f}+\delta u_{\mu}\bar{u}^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\bar{f},

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla_{\mu}\delta f+\bar{u}_{\mu}\delta u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\bar{f}+\delta u_{\mu}\dot{\bar{f}},

\displaystyle=\left[-\delta u^{0}\dot{\bar{f}}+\delta u_{0}\dot{\bar{f}},\partial_{i}\delta f+\delta u_{i}\dot{\bar{f}}\right],

\displaystyle=\left[0,\partial_{i}\left(\delta f+\theta\dot{\bar{f}}\right)\right].

(66)

From the above expression, the linearised comoving fractional density spatial gradient is easily derived

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mu}\equiv\frac{a}{\rho}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla_{\mu}\,\rho=\left[0,a\,\partial_{i}\left(\delta+\theta\dot{\bar{\rho}}/\bar{\rho}\right)\right]=\left[0,a\,\partial_{i}\Delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rm B}}\right],

(67)

where
\delta=\delta\rho/\bar{\rho}
is the gauge-dependent density contrast and
\Delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rm B}}\equiv\delta+\theta\dot{\bar{\rho}}/\bar{\rho}
the corresponding first order gauge-invariant Bardeen variable. From (
37
) we can expand the trace of
\Delta_{\mu\nu}
,

\displaystyle\Delta\equiv a\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 10.73616pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 12.21767pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 13.43997pt\mathopen{\vphantom{\nabla}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{(3)}}}}\nabla_{\mu}\Delta^{\mu}=\left[0,\partial_{i}\partial^{i}\left(\delta+\theta\dot{\bar{\rho}}/\bar{\rho}\right)\right]=\left[0,\nabla^{2}\Delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rm B}}\right]

(68)

and obtain an explicitly-comoving expression, where
\nabla^{2}\equiv\partial_{i}\partial^{i}
. This proves the equivalence of the two approaches to cosmological perturbation theory at linear order.
