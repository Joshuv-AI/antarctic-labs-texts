Percolation in Random Graphs of Unbounded Rank
Nils Detering, Jimin Lin

Abstract
Bootstrap percolation in (random) graphs is a contagion dynamics among a set of vertices with certain threshold levels. The process is started by a set of initially infected vertices, and an initially uninfected vertex with threshold k k gets infected as soon as the number of its infected neighbors exceeds k k . This process has been studied extensively in so called rank one models. These models can generate random graphs with heavy-tailed degree sequences but they are not capable of clustering. In this paper, we treat a class of random graphs of unbounded rank which allow for extensive clustering. Our main result determines the final fraction of infected vertices as the fixed point of a non-linear operator defined on a suitable function space. We propose an algorithm that facilitates neural networks to calculate this fixed point efficiently. We further derive criteria based on the Fréchet derivative of the operator that allows one to determine whether small infections spread through the entire graph or rather stay local.



1
Introduction

Bootstrap percolation
models the spread of some activation or infection among a set of vertices. It has been studied on structures such as trees, (random) graphs, and lattices, among others. Common to all of these studies is the specification of a local rule according to which the infection spreads locally between neighbors, and one is then interested in understanding the process on a global level. The most classical setting is possibly that of
k
-threshold percolation, where the process starts with a set of initially infected vertices and in the following a vertex gets infected as soon as
k
of its neighbors are infected. Bootstrap percolation has been used to study the demagnetization in magnetic materials (
Chalupa
et al. 1979
), impulses in brain neuronal networks (
Tlusty and
Eckmann 2009
,
Goltsev et al. 2010
), storage failure in computer networks (
Kirkpatrick et al. 2002
), contagion in financial networks (
Amini
et al. 2016
,
Detering et al. 2019
), and the spreading of disease (
Hurd 2021
). In the following, we universally use the term
infection
as a synonym for activation, default, or other vertex states that change during the course of the process.

This paper focuses on bootstrap percolation in random graphs. It was first investigated in
Balogh and
Pittel 2007
and
Fontes and
Schonmann 2008
for the configuration model where a phase transition of the
k
-threshold percolation in
d
-regular graph was derived. More general results for graphs with arbitrary degree distributions were obtained in
Baxter et al. 2010
and
Amini 2010
. Bootstrap percolation in the Erdös Rényi
G_{n,p}
model was explored comprehensively by
Janson
et al. 2012
.
Turova and
Vallier 2015
found a narrower critical window for phase transition when a one-dimensional lattice was added to the
G_{n,p}
graph. A variant of bootstrap percolation in the
G_{n,p}
model which considered the synchronous and asynchronous percolation processes with inhibitory and excitatory vertices was studied in the recent work of
Einarsson et al. 2019
. For bootstrap percolation in the inhomogeneous Chung-Lu random graph model with a power-law degree distribution,
Amini and
Fountoulakis 2014
derived a threshold function for the number of initially infected vertices that ensures that a positive fraction becomes infected at the end. The size of the final cluster of infected vertices triggered by a fixed proportion of initially infected vertices is determined in
Amini
et al. 2014
. In a directed Chung-Lu model with heterogeneous thresholds and initial infections,
Detering et al. 2019
study the size of the final cluster and, in some cases, lower bounds for the final cluster that do not depend on the size of the initial infection. In random geometric graphs,
Bradonjić and
Saniee 2014
determined the critical point of the first order phase transition of bootstrap percolation.
Janson
et al. 2019
presented a modified non-monotonous bootstrap percolation which allows infected vertices to recover. The influence of the underlying geometry of inhomogenous geometric graph on the percolation speed was studied in the recent work by
Koch and
Lengler 2016
.

A majority of the random graph models mentioned above, including the Erdös Rényi model, the Chung-Lu model, and certain geometric models can be categorized as
rank one
random graph. The rank one random graphs are a popular class that can generate heterogeneous degrees but properties related to the graph can in many cases still be described by low dimensional objects. They are a subset of a more general model introduced in
Bollobás
et al. 2007
where every vertex has a type
x\in\mathcal{S}
from some type space
\mathcal{S}
. The connection probability between two vertices of type
x
and
y
is given by
\kappa(x,y)/n
, where
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called the kernel function. The rank one models are now those where
\kappa
is of the special form
\kappa(x,y)=\phi(x)\phi(y)
for some function
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and variants thereof. The name originates from the fact that in this case, for a graph with
n
vertices and types
x_{i},i\in[n]
, the matrix of connection probabilities is of rank one and given by
\Phi\cdot\Phi^{T}
where
\Phi=(\phi(x_{1}),\dots,\phi(x_{n}))^{T}
. Here
A^{T}
denotes the transpose of the vector (matrix) of
A
. For the random graph models of rank one, it turns out that the final set of infected vertices can actually be determined by solving a one-dimensional fixed point equation (
Amini
et al. 2014
,
Detering et al. 2019
). In
Detering et al. 2020
a model with fixed rank
k
has been proposed that in some way maintains the multiplicative structure of the connection probabilities. In such case, the resulting fixed point equations are multidimensional.

However, despite the capacity to capture inhomogeneity, a shortcoming of the finite rank models is their inability to build flexible clusters. Clusters are however very pronounced in most real networks where often the geographical location determines the belonging to a certain cluster. One example is the worldwide interbank lending network. The intra-country connection between core banks and local banks can be large in some countries, but the inter-country connection between the core banks and foreign local banks is usually much smaller. Another example is social networks where the probability of connections between individuals strongly depends on the location of residence. To alleviate this neglect of the clustering phenomena, one straightforward approach is to adopt a more flexible kernel function for generating the connection probability, which, in the matrix analogy, is to adopt a matrix with unbounded rank for the connection probabilities.

In this paper, we therefore study a bootstrap percolation process in inhomogeneous random graphs with connection probabilities described by a general kernel function
\kappa
. We derive a space
\mathcal{F}
of real valued functions defined on
\mathcal{S}
and a non-linear operator
\Psi_{\kappa}
defined on this function space that fully describes the bootstrap percolation process. More precisely, if
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
is the point-wise smallest fixed point of the operator
\Psi_{\kappa}
(i.e.
\Psi_{\kappa}(\hat{f})=\hat{f}
) and
\hat{f}
is continuous, then, for large
n
, the final number of infected vertices is given by
n\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}(x)d\mu
, where
\mu
is a measure on
\mathcal{S}
that describes the type distribution of the vertices. In most cases, when
\kappa
satisfies some Lipchitz condition, we can actually show that a smallest fixed point exists and that it is continuous. In the next step, we then find that the Fréchet derivative of
\Psi_{\kappa}
at the origin (the function that is constantly equal to zero) determines whether small infections spread to a large part of the graph or stay local. We provide an algorithm that effectively determines the fixed point
\hat{f}
and we provide an extensive numerical case study.

The content of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the random graph, outline our main results and explain our proof strategy. In Section
3
we present our algorithm to determine the first fixed point of the operator
\Psi_{\kappa}
. We also perform an extensive case study that shows that our asymptotic results hold already for fairly small networks. In Appendix
A
, we study systems with only finitely many types. These systems serve as the tool in our proof of the general setting. Finally, in Appendix
B
, we provide the proofs of our main results as well as most auxiliary results.

2
Model, Results, and Strategy of Proof

Random Graph model:

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider a vertex set
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Each vertex
i\in[n]
is assigned a deterministic parameter
s_{i}(n)\in\mathcal{S}
. This parameter is called the vertex type and it takes value in a compact metric space
\mathcal{S}
. The type of a vertex will determine its local connectivity properties. Let
\bm{s}(n)=(s_{1}(n),\dots,s_{n}(n))
be the vector of the types of all vertices. We shall often drop the dependency on
n
and just write
\bm{s}
and
s_{i}
for
i\in[n]
. We consider directed random graphs without self-loops. The connection probability
p_{ij}=p_{ij}(n)
between two vertices
i,j\in[n]
depends now on their types
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
and is specified by some non-negative and Borel measurable kernel function:
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. In particular, a directed edge between vertex
i
and
j
(
i\neq j
) is present with probability

p_{ij}=\min\{1,\kappa(s_{i},s_{j})/n\}.

Further, let the event that an edge is present be independent of the presence of all other edges.

Bootstrap percolation:

In addition to the parameter
s_{i}
we assign to each vertex
i\in[n]
a second deterministic parameter
k_{i}(n)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The parameter
k_{i}(n)
represents a threshold level of vertex
i
, and determines in the percolation process how many neighbors of the vertex
i
need to be infected for the vertex
i
to become infected itself. Denote by
\bm{k}(n)=(k_{1}(n),\dots,k_{n}(n))
the vector of thresholds.

More precisely, let
D_{0}
be the set of initially infected vertices, i.e.
D_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{i\in[n]|k_{i}=0\}
, which starts the process. Let further
N(i)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{j\in[n]|E_{ji}=1\}
be the neighbors with a directed edge towards vertex
i
. The variable
E_{ji}
is the indicator that
i
receives a edge from
j
. Then, in the first generation those vertices get infected whose number of edges from vertices in
D_{0}
is larger than their threshold, i.e.
D_{1}=\{i\in[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|D_{0}\cap N(i)|\geq k_{i}\}
.
As the infection continues to spread, the infected vertices in the
m
-th generation, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
, are given by

\displaystyle D_{m}=\{i\in[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|D_{m-1}\cap N(i)|\geq k_{i}(n)\}.

(2.1)

Since there are only
n
vertices in total, the spread certainly comes to an end after at most
n
steps and
D_{M}=D_{M+1}=\dots
for some
M\leq n
. We denote by
D_{n}
the final set of infected vertices. Note that given the realization of the random graph, the bootstrap percolation process is deterministic. In Figure
1
we exemplify the bootstrap percolation process for a graph with
n=10
vertices. In this example graph,
2
vertices are initially infected and all remaining vertices have a threshold equal to
2
. The graph is a random sample arising from a kernel that we specify in our case study in Section
3
.

In this paper we investigate the fraction
n^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert D_{n}\right\rvert}
of infected vertices as
n\rightarrow\infty
, in the random graph described above. This requires that we have a sequence of random graphs parameterized by
n
. Recall that for fixed
n
, all relevant information about the probabilistic structure of the skeleton of the graph is encoded in
\kappa
and
\bm{s}(n)
while the additional information required for the bootstrap percolation process is encoded in
\bm{k}(n)
. A sequence of random graphs, together with their percolation parameters, can then be derived by specifying
\kappa
and a sequence of vectors
(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We shall pose a regularity condition that ensures that the proportion of vertices of each type and threshold converges as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption 2.1
.

For each
n
and for given
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and Borel set
A\subset\mathcal{S}
, let
U_{k}^{(n)}(A)=\{i\in[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}k_{i}=k,s_{i}\in A\}
be the set of vertices with threshold equal to
k
and type in
A
. Let further
\nu^{(n)}(k,A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|U_{k}^{(n)}(A)|/n
, or equivalently,
\nu^{(n)}(k,A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i\in[n]}I_{\{k\}}(k_{i})I_{A}(s_{i})/n
be their proportion, where the function
I_{B}
is the indicator function for a set
B
, i.e.
I_{B}(b)=1
when
b\in B
and zero otherwise. We shall assume that there exists a measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathcal{S}
such that for each fixed
k
, the measure
\nu(k,\cdot)
is a Borel measure on
\mathcal{S}
and that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and Borel set
A
it holds that:

\displaystyle\nu^{(n)}(k,A)\mathrel{{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}}\nu(k,A).

(2.2)

We denote by
\mu
the marginal measure of
\nu
on
\mathcal{S}
, and by
\eta_{k}
the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu(k,\cdot)
against
\mu
on
\mathcal{S}
, that is, for every Borel set
A
,

\displaystyle\mu(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\nu(k,A),

\displaystyle\nu(k,A)=\int_{A}\eta_{k}(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s).

(2.3)

Figure 1:

One instance of the bootstrap percolation process with
n=10
vertices. This percolation process lasts for six rounds until no more additional infections take place. The rounds are ordered from left to right and from top to bottom. The green points are vertices with threshold
2
that have not received any edge from an infected vertex yet, the darker green points are vertices that have received an edge from exactly one infected vertex, and the violet points are vertices that are infected, either initially or during the process. Note that this graph is directed.

We collect all information about the vertex sequence in the vector
\mathcal{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa,\nu,(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and denote the random graph with
n
vertices derived from this sequence by
G(n,\mathcal{V})
. The random graph model used here is an adapted version of the model proposed in
Bollobás
et al. 2007
, enriched by the threshold values
\bm{k}(n)
which are crucial parameters for the process we study. While the actual
final set of infected vertices

\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))
depends on the random graph
G(n,\mathcal{V})
, we will see that under Assumption
2.1
for large
n
, a law or large numbers holds, and we can describe
n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|
purely in terms of
\kappa
,
\nu
and
\mathcal{S}
.

2.1
Results

To ignite the bootstrap percolation process, we shall first assume that
\nu(0,\mathcal{S})>0
, which ensures that as
n\rightarrow\infty
there exists a positive fraction of initially infected vertices, and we then study the final number of infected vertices. Later in Section
2.1.2
, we will start with a graph without initial infections, and then study the impact of imposing a small fraction of ex-post infections. This will lead to the idea of the resilience of the random graph.

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that the kernel
\kappa
is continuous on the space
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
, equipped with the product topology.

In light of Assumption
2.2
note that by the Tychonoff theorem, it follows from compactness of
\mathcal{S}
that
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
is compact with respect to the product topology, and then in particular that
\kappa
is bounded on
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
. We let in the following
M_{\kappa}
be the bound of
\kappa
. Our main result will show that under Assumption
2.1
and
2.2
the final proportion of infected vertices
n^{-1}|D_{n}|
can be determined by solving a fixed point equation in some function space.

Remark 2.3
.

It would be interesting to allow for discontinuous
\kappa
or a state space
\mathcal{S}
that is not compact. This more general model setup has the advantage that
\kappa
is not necessarily bounded and that one may generate random graphs in which vertices of some type have very large degrees. However, without Assumption
2.2
it appears that even the existence of a first fixed point of the related operators can not be guaranteed, except for some special cases. From a practical perspective, a bounded
\kappa
is not very restrictive as one may always choose a sufficiently large cutoff. Moreover, to determine the end of the contagion process, it will be necessary to explicitly solve the fixed point equation, which will be challenging numerically if
\kappa
is unbounded. For the question of resilience that we address in Section
2.1.2
, considering unbounded
\kappa
is however particularly interesting. We leave the analysis in this more general model for future research. Since allowing for bounded but possibly discontinuous
\kappa
does not significantly enrich the theoretical treatment of the percolation process that we consider, we restrict to a setup that keeps the presentation simpler.

We now introduce the operators which will be important for the analysis of the bootstrap percolation process.

Related operators:

Let

\mathcal{F}_{b}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+},\text{bounded, Borel}\}

the set of bounded positive and Borel measurable functions on
\mathcal{S}
. We equip
\mathcal{F}_{b}
with the supremum norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{\infty}
defined by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f(s)\right\rvert}
which turns
\mathcal{F}_{b}
into a metric space with metric given by
d(f_{1},f_{2})=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f_{1}(s)-f_{2}(s)\right\rVert}_{\infty}
. We stress that
\mathcal{F}_{b}
is actually not a vector space but only a convex cone because it contains only the non-negative functions. Although
\mathcal{F}_{b}
is not a vector space we will still make use the norm symbol
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{\infty}
in many cases which causes no problem since
\mathcal{F}_{b}\subset\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\text{bounded, Borel}\}
. We further define

\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1],\text{Borel}\}\subset\mathcal{F}_{b},

the subset of those functions that are bounded by
1
.

For convenience, also define the two constant functions
\mathcal{F}_{b}\ni\mathbf{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\{0\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{b}\ni\mathbf{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\{1\}
. Define the operators
\Lambda_{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{b},P_{\kappa}^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\Psi_{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{1}
by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\kappa}[f](\cdot)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa(s,\cdot)f(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s),

(2.4)

\displaystyle P_{\kappa}^{k}[f](\cdot)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}[f](\cdot)\right)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda_{\kappa}[f](\cdot)},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\kappa}[f](\cdot)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}(\cdot)\left(1-\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}P_{\kappa}^{k^{\prime}}[f](\cdot)\right),

(2.6)

where
\eta_{k}(\cdot)
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative defined in (
2.3
). We use center-dot notation with bracket
[\cdot]
for arguments that are function valued
f\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
, and center-dot notation with parenthesis
(\cdot)
for arguments of type
s\in\mathcal{S}
. For example, for
f,h\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
,
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, we evaluate
\Lambda_{\kappa}[f+h](s^{\prime})=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa(s,s^{\prime})[f+h](s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s)
. To keep the notation neat, we will sometimes omit the bracket or parenthesis argument as long as this does not cause any confusion. In the following two lemmas, we state some important properties of the operators defined above. For
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
, we say that
f\leq g
if only if
f(s)\leq g(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 2.4

(Monotonicity)
.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
with
f\leq g
it holds that
\Psi_{\kappa}f\leq\Psi_{\kappa}g
.

Lemma 2.5

(Continuous image of
\Psi_{\kappa}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\cdot}^{c}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\cdot}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f\text{ is continuous}\}
, then for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
,
\Lambda_{\kappa}[f]\in\mathcal{F}_{b}^{c}
,
P_{\kappa}^{k}[f]\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{c}
and
\Psi_{\kappa}[f]\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{c}
. Moreover, if we further assume
\kappa
to be Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{\kappa}
, then for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
,
\Lambda_{\kappa}[f]\in\mathcal{F}_{b}^{Lip}
,
P_{\kappa}^{k}[f]\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{Lip}
and
\Psi_{\kappa}[f]\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{Lip}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{\cdot}^{Lip}\subset\mathcal{F}_{b}
is defined by
\mathcal{F}_{\cdot}^{Lip}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\cdot}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f\text{ is Lipschitz with constant }L_{\kappa}(1+M_{\kappa})M_{\kappa}e^{M_{\kappa}}\}
.

We will see that if a minimal fixed point of the operator
\Psi_{\kappa}
exists, then it allows us to determine the final proportion of infected vertices. By fixed point, we mean a function
f
in the convex cone
\mathcal{F}_{b}
such that
f=\Psi_{\kappa}f
. With a Lipschitz condition on
\kappa
, we can prove the existence of a minimal fixed point of
\Psi_{\kappa}
:

Lemma 2.6

(Existence of the minimal fixed point)
.

Let
\kappa
be Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{\kappa}
and
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{Lip}
as defined in Lemma
2.5
. Let further
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{F}_{1}^{Lip}
be the set defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{Lip}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f=\Psi_{\kappa}f\}.

(2.7)

Then
\mathcal{H}
is not empty and includes all the fixed points. Each
h\in\mathcal{H}
is continuous and there exists a function
\eta_{0}\leq\hat{f}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\hat{f}\leq h
for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
. We call
\hat{f}
the minimal fixed point. Clearly, it then also holds that
\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}\mathrm{d}\mu=\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big\{\int_{\mathcal{S}}f\mathrm{d}\mu\big\}
.

The proof of Lemma
2.5
relies on an argument that requires us to find a subset
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subset\mathcal{F}_{b}
of measurable functions that contains all fixed points of
\Psi_{\kappa}
(if any) and which is such that for any subset
\mathcal{H}_{2}\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}
the pointwise supremum (respectively infimum) of the functions in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is a function in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Because the supremum of measurable functions is not measurable in general, we need to choose a continuity property that is stable under taking the supremum. Without the assumption that
\kappa
is Lipschitz continuous, it is therefore in general not possible to show the existence of a minimal fixed point, except for some special cases where
\kappa
is either of a particular structural form, or the thresholds of the vertices are all either
0
or
1
.

Next, we shall specify the "Fréchet derivative" on a convex cone. Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{b}
is a convex cone such that
af_{1}+bf_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
for
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
and any non-negative scalar
a,b\geq 0
. Though the Fréchet derivative is usually defined on a vector space, it can be defined in the same spirit on
\mathcal{F}_{b}
. Let
L^{+}(\mathcal{F}_{b})\subset C(\mathcal{F}_{b},\mathcal{F}_{b})
be a subset of continuous functions which are non-negative and closed under linear combinations with non-negative scalar. For an operator
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{b}
, we call
D\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow L^{+}(\mathcal{F}_{b})
the Fréchet derivative of
\Phi
in
L^{+}(\mathcal{F}_{b})
if for all
f,h\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
it holds

\lim_{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi(f+h)-\Phi(f)-D\Phi f[h]\right\rVert}_{\infty}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}}=0.

(2.8)

Lemma 2.7

(Fréchet derivative)
.

The operator
\Psi_{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{b}
is Fréchet differentiable at every point
f\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
and the derivative
D\Psi_{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow L^{+}(\mathcal{F}_{b})
, is given by

D\Psi_{\kappa}f[\cdot](\cdot)=\Lambda_{\kappa}[\cdot](\cdot)V[f](\cdot)

(2.9)

where
V[f](\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}(\cdot)P_{\kappa}^{k-1}f(\cdot)
.

2.1.1
Final Set of Infected Vertices

We are now ready to state our first main result about the final number of infected vertices.

Theorem 2.8

(Final fraction of infected vertices)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a vertex sequence with resulting random graph
G(n,\mathcal{V})
and assume that a minimal fixed point
\hat{f}
of
\Psi_{\kappa}
exists. Then the following holds:

1.

For every
\varepsilon>0
it holds that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathds{P}\left(n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|\geq\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}\mathrm{d}\mu-\varepsilon\right)=1.

(2.10)

2.

If, in addition, there exists some non-negative and continuous function
h\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
and small
\epsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle D\Psi_{\kappa}\hat{f}[h]-h<-\epsilon\mathbf{1},

(2.11)

then

\displaystyle n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{p}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}\mathrm{d}\mu.

(2.12)

In particular for the case of
\kappa
Lipschitz, we know by Lemma
2.6
that a minimal fixed point exists. Except for some pathological cases, then also the derivative condition holds and Theorem
2.8
allows us to determine the final fraction of infected vertices at the end of the bootstrap percolation process.

To gain some intuition for this result, let us give some heuristic interpretation. Note that a vertex of type
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
has a degree that is approximately Poisson distributed with parameter

\Lambda_{\kappa}[\mathbf{1}](s^{\prime})=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa(s,s^{\prime})d\mu(s).

At the end of the process, some fraction
\hat{f}(s)
of all vertices of type
s\in\mathcal{S}
will be infected. If we would now add a new vertex of type
s^{\prime}
with a threshold value equal to
k
to the graph and connect this vertex to the existing vertices with probability described by the kernel and all edges independent, then that vertex has a degree to the set of infected vertices that is Poisson distributed with parameter given by

\Lambda_{\kappa}[\hat{f}](s^{\prime})=\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa(s,s^{\prime})\hat{f}(s)d\mu(s),

and the probability of it being infected is given by

1-\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}P_{\kappa}^{k^{\prime}}[\hat{f}](s^{\prime}),

(2.13)

which is the probability that a Poisson distributed random variable with parameter
\Lambda_{\kappa}[\hat{f}](s^{\prime})
is larger or equal to
k
. Surprisingly, it turns out that despite the complicated dependency of the degree of a vertex in the graph and its state of infection, an existing uniformly selected vertex of the graph, conditioned on its threshold being
k
and its type being
s^{\prime}
, has a probability of being infected given by (
2.13
). Summing up over the proportion of all vertices with type
s^{\prime}
but possibly different threshold leads to the fixed point condition. The derivative condition (
2.11
) is required to exclude an unstable equilibrium in which the additional default of a few vertices could restart the process, leading to many additional infections. The condition ensures that when almost
\hat{f}(s)
of type
s
vertices are infected for every
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the exploration of another infected vertex leads on average to less than one additional infected vertex.

2.1.2
Resilience

In the previous section, we studied the final set of infected vertices for a random graph with a positive fraction of initially infected vertices, i.e.
\nu_{0}(\mathcal{S})>0
. We now look at an initially uninfected graph and want to make statements about the resilience of the graph to ex-post infections.
In order to keep our notation consistent, we adapt the previous notation of the random graph and related operators for the ex-post infected graph and denote the quantities related to the uninfected graph with a tilde instead, i.e. for the uninfected graph we have
\widetilde{\nu}_{0}(\mathcal{S})=0
. Assume that ex-post we infect some vertices by setting their threshold value to
0
while we keep all other thresholds unchanged. A question that arises naturally is then whether there exists a lower bound
\Delta
for the fraction of infected vertices such that any ex-post infection leads to at least
\Delta n
infected vertices at the end of the process. If that is the case, the graph is considered
non-resilient
. On the contrary, we call it
resilient
if a reduction of the fraction of initially infected vertices to
0
ensures that also the final fraction of infected vertices goes to
0
.

To formalize the setup, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa,\widetilde{\nu},(\bm{s}(n),\widetilde{\bm{k}}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a vertex sequence that fulfils Assumption
2.1
and that is such that
\widetilde{\nu}(0,\mathcal{S})=0
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we now define a new threshold sequence
\bm{k}(n)=(k_{1}(n),\dots,k_{n}(n))
which is such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq n
either
k_{i}(n)=\widetilde{k}_{i}(n)
or
k_{i}(n)=0
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and Borel set
A\subset\mathcal{S}
, define

\nu^{(n)}(k,A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i\in[n]}I_{\{k\}}(k_{i})I_{A}(s_{i}).

We further assume that for the new sequence there exists again a measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathcal{S}
such that for each fixed
k
, the measure
\nu(k,\cdot)
is a Borel measure on
\mathcal{S}
and that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and Borel set
A
it holds that:

\displaystyle\nu^{(n)}(k,A)\mathrel{{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}}\nu(k,A).

(2.14)

Moreover, we assume that
\nu(0,\mathcal{S})>0
, so as a result of the threshold changes a fraction of vertices is infected as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and these infected vertices start the percolation process. Let as before
\mathcal{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa,\nu,(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

By construction of
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{V}
we have the following relations for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}(k,A)-\nu(k,A)\geq 0\text{ for }k\geq 1

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}(0,A)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\nu}(k,A)-\nu(k,A).

(2.15)

In the same fashion of (
2.3
), for subset
A\subset\mathcal{S}
, we denote the marginal distribution and the Radon-Nikodym derivative by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}(A)\equiv\mu(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\nu(k,A),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}(k,A)=\int_{A}\widetilde{\eta}_{k}(s)\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mu}(s),

\displaystyle\nu(k,A)=\int_{A}\eta_{k}(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s).

(2.16)

We now define resilience and non-resilience as follows:

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be the initial uninfected vertex sequence and
\mathcal{V}
the vertex sequence with ex-post infections. As before denote by
G(n,\widetilde{\mathcal{V}})
and
G(n,\mathcal{V})
the corresponding random graphs and note that they both have exactly the same skeleton distribution and differ only in terms of their threshold values.

We call the random graph
G(n,\widetilde{\mathcal{V}})

1.

non-resilient
, if there exists an
\Delta>0
only depending on
\widetilde{\nu}
such that
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|/n\geq\Delta
, for
n
large, independent of the precise specification of
\nu
.

2.

resilient
, if for all
\alpha>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|/n<\alpha
for
0<\nu(0,\mathcal{S})<\delta
.

In other words, for a
non-resilient
graph, no matter how small the initial infection is, the final fraction of infected vertices will always exceed a certain level, while, for a
resilient
graph, the final fraction of infected vertices is small as long as the initial infection is small. In the following, we provide a condition that allows us to determine whether a random graph is resilient or non-resilient.
Introduce the two operators
\widetilde{\Psi}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{b}
and
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{b}
corresponding to the uninfected
G(n,\widetilde{\mathcal{V}})
and the infected graph
G(n,\mathcal{V})
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}[f](\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\eta}_{k}(\cdot)\left(1-\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}P_{\kappa}^{k^{\prime}}[f](\cdot)\right),

\displaystyle\Psi[f](\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}(\cdot)\left(1-\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}P_{\kappa}^{k^{\prime}}[f](\cdot)\right),

(2.17)

where
P_{\kappa}^{k}
is as defined in equation (
2.4
). Note that the summation of
\widetilde{\Psi}
starts from
k=1
as
\widetilde{\nu}(0,\mathcal{S})=0
.
Also, for
\widetilde{\Psi}f
we can calculate the Fréchet derivative
D\widetilde{\Psi}f
as in equation (
2.9
). The following theorem provides the resilience condition.

Theorem 2.10

(Resilience)
.

If there exists a continuous and non-negative function
h\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
such that

1.

D\widetilde{\Psi}\mathbf{0}[h]>h
. Then, the graph is non-resilient.

2.

D\widetilde{\Psi}\mathbf{0}[h]<h
. Then, the graph is resilient.

Moreover, the graph cannot be both non-resilient and resilient, i.e. there do not exit functions
h,g\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
such that
D\widetilde{\Psi}\mathbf{0}[h]>h
and
D\widetilde{\Psi}\mathbf{0}[g]<g
hold at the same time. Note that
D\widetilde{\Psi}\mathbf{0}[h]
is the Fréchet derivative of the operator
\widetilde{\Psi}
at the point
\mathbf{0}
, applied to
h
.

Remark 2.11
.

It is worth noting that our notion of resilience is in some cases related to the existence of a giant component.
Bollobás
et al. 2007
proved in their Theorem 3.1 that a giant component exists if the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
norm of the operator
\Lambda_{\kappa}
is larger than
1
. In our model, if all the vertices have a threshold equal to one, then our condition 1. implies that the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
norm of
\Lambda_{\kappa}
is larger than
1
and the network must have a giant component.

2.2
Proof Strategy

2.2.1
Final Fraction of Infected Vertices

We provide a sketch of the proof ideas which are carried out in detail in Appendix
A
and
B
.

We first derive a sequence of partitions of the space
\mathcal{S}
. Based on this sequence of partitions we can then define coupling kernels
\kappa_{L}^{+}
and
\kappa_{L}^{-}
for
L\in\mathbb{N}
, which are step functions that dominate the original kernel from above and below, and which, in addition to some other properties, are such that the first fixed points of
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}^{-}}
and
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}^{+}}
converge to the first fixed point of
\Psi_{\kappa}
. Collapsing the types in each subset into a single type, these coupling kernels then lead to two sequences of random graphs, one which has more edges than the original graph, and another one with fewer edges. This leads to upper and lower bounds of
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|
in terms of the final fraction of infected vertices in this finite-type graph. We can then show that the final number of infected vertices in the finite-type random graphs can be obtained as a solution to a finite-dimensional fixed point equation. However, in this approximation, the dimension of the related fixed point equation is actually finite and increasing in
L
and the connection to the operator equation
\Psi[\hat{f}]=\hat{f}
is not straightforward at first. However, we can embed the finite-dimensional system into the original system, which then lets us show that not only the operators
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}^{\pm}}
are converging but also the fraction of infected vertices. In this section, we describe the required auxiliary results and the embedding in more detail. In Section
2.2.2
we explain the proof strategy for our resilience result. All proofs are then carried out in detail in Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
.

The following lemma provides the basis for the construction of our approximating graph sequence:

Lemma 2.12
.

There exists a sequence, indexed by
L\in\mathbb{N}
, of measurable partitions
\{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}\}_{l\in[N(L)]}
of
\mathcal{S}
, where
N(L)
is the finite number of subsets, such that

1.

\{\mathcal{S}_{L^{\prime}}^{l}\}_{l\in[L^{\prime}]}
for each
L^{\prime}>L
is a refinement of
\{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}\}_{l\in[N(L)]}
, i.e. each
\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}
is a union
\cup_{j\in J}\mathcal{S}_{L^{\prime}}^{j}
for some set
J\subset N(L^{\prime})
.

2.

As
L\rightarrow\infty
,
\mu(S_{L}^{l})\rightarrow 0

\forall l\in[N(L)]
.

Based on the partition in the previous lemma we can now define kernels that are step functions and are the basis for defining our coupling graphs.

Definition 2.13

(Coupling graphs)
.

For
G(n,\mathcal{V})
, we construct two sequences of coupling graphs indexed by
N\in\mathbb{N}
as follows. Partition the type space
\mathcal{S}
into
N(L)
subsets according to Lemma
2.12
. Define two stepwise kernel functions by

\displaystyle\kappa_{L}^{+}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}\in[N(L)]}I_{\mathcal{S}^{l}}(s)I_{\mathcal{S}^{l^{\prime}}}(s^{\prime})\sup_{x\in\mathcal{S}^{l},y\in\mathcal{S}^{l^{\prime}}}\kappa(x,y),

(2.18)

\displaystyle\kappa_{L}^{-}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}\in[N(L)]}I_{\mathcal{S}^{l}}(s)I_{\mathcal{S}^{l^{\prime}}}(s^{\prime})\inf_{x\in\mathcal{S}^{l},y\in\mathcal{S}^{l^{\prime}}}\kappa(x,y),

(2.19)

where
I_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}(s)
is the indicator function taking value
1
if
s\in\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}
, otherwise
0
.
With these stepwise kernels
\kappa_{L}^{-}
and
\kappa_{L}^{+}
which are such that
\kappa_{L}^{-}\leq\kappa\leq\kappa_{L}^{+}
, we can now specify two vertex sequences
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa_{L}^{-},\nu,(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))
and
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa_{L}^{+},\nu,(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))
, that define random graphs
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{-})
and
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{+})
. These graphs now couple the original graph
G(n,\mathcal{V})
in the following sense: For any pair of vertices
i,j\in[n]
in a graph
G
, let
E_{i,j}(G)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=I_{\{i\text{ connects to }j\text{ in graph }G\}}
be the indicator of an edge from
i
to
j
. By construction we can now ensure that
E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{-}))\leq E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))\leq E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{+}))
. One way to generate such coupling systems, for example, is by uniform random variables to generate edges as follows. For each pair of vertices
i,j\in[n]
, generate
U\sim\text{Uniform([0,1])}
, and set the edge by
E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{-})=I_{\{U\leq\kappa_{L}^{-}(s_{i},s_{j})\}}
,
E_{\kappa_{L}}(i,j)=I_{\{U\leq\kappa_{L}(s_{i},s_{j})\}}
, and
E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{+})=I_{\{U\leq\kappa_{L}^{+}(s_{i},s_{j})\}}
. As a result, we have that
E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{-}))\leq E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))\leq E_{i,j}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{+}))
as desired, and for the total number of infected vertices after the percolation it follows that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{-}))|\leq|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|\leq|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{+}))|.

(2.20)

In this sense, we call
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{-})
a
lower coupling graph
of
G(n,\mathcal{V})
and
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{+})
an
upper coupling graph
of the original
G(n,\mathcal{V})
.

By the construction of the coupling graphs, we hope that we can determine
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{\pm}))|
, and that
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{\pm}))|
will converge to
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|
as the partitions for
\mathcal{S}
get finer as
L\rightarrow\infty
. Because the kernels
\kappa_{L}^{\pm}
are step functions it holds that
\kappa_{L}^{\pm}(s,s^{\prime})=\kappa_{L}^{\pm}(r,r^{\prime})
for any
s,r\in\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}
and
s^{\prime},r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l^{\prime}}
, i.e. the connection probability
\kappa_{L}^{\pm}(s,s^{\prime})
equals to
\kappa_{L}^{\pm}(r,r^{\prime})
for any pair of types in the same type intervals. Hence, for all the vertices we only need to know the index
l
of the set
\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}
that they belong to in order to determine their connection probabilities, and therefore we can just assign the integer
l\in[N(L)]
as a type and define a discrete kernel
\kappa^{\pm d}_{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[N(L)]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
accordingly. This leads to a random graph with the same skeleton distribution as the one defined by
\kappa_{L}^{\pm}
. By this grouping of the vertex types, we can turn the coupling graphs into graphs of finitely many vertex types with a discrete kernel function. Denote this random graph by
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d,\pm})
. It follows obviously that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{\pm}))|=|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d,\pm}))|.

(2.21)

We then first investigate the final fraction of infected vertices in the graph
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d,\pm})
.

Definition 2.14

(Graph with finite vertex type)
.

For fixed
N(L)
we consider a random graph with vertices having types in the set
[N(L)]
and thresholds
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The proportion of vertices with certain threshold and type is now described by the numbers
(\mathbf{\nu}^{l,(n)}_{k})_{l\in[N(L)],\ k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where

\mathbf{\nu}^{l,(n)}_{k}(A)=n^{-1}\sum_{i\in[n]}I_{\{k\}}(k_{i})I_{\{l\}}(s_{i})

(2.22)

is their proportion. As before, we assume that there exists
\{\mathbf{\nu}^{l}_{k}\}_{l\in[N(L)],\ k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that

\mathbf{\nu}^{l,(n)}_{k}\mathrel{{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}}\mathbf{\nu}^{l}_{k},

(2.23)

i.e. the proportion of vertices with a certain type and threshold converges.
For the connection probability, we define a discrete kernel
\kappa^{d}_{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[N(L)]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Note that we use a superscript
d
to distinguish the kernel defined on
[N(L)]^{2}
from the one defined on
\mathcal{S}^{2}
. Then, for
i,j\in[n]
, the connection probability is given by
\min\{1,\kappa^{d}_{L}(l_{i},l_{j})/n\}
. Again we define the vertex sequence
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa^{d}_{L},\nu,(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))
for this graph and denote by
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d})
the resulting random graph.

For this finite-type vertex sequence, it turns out that the percolation process can be fully described by the solution of a system of ordinary differential equations of dimension
N(L)
. The final fraction of infected vertices
n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d}))|
is then determined from the first joint zero of this system. While we postpone the formal derivations and discussions to Appendix
A
, we state and explain the solution functions here. For
l\in[N(L)]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we define the functions
\nu_{k}^{l}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]^{N(L)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=-z^{l}+\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{0})+\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}\nu_{k^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{0})\left(1-\sum_{k^{\prime\prime}=0}^{k^{\prime}-1}p(k^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{l}(\mathbf{z})\right)

(2.24)

\displaystyle\nu_{k}^{l}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}=k}^{\infty}\nu_{k^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{0})p(k^{\prime}-k,\lambda^{l}(\mathbf{z})),\ \forall k\geq 1,

(2.25)

where
\nu_{k}^{l}(\mathbf{0})
is the initial proportion of vertices with type
l
and threshold
k
,
p(k,\lambda)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\tfrac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}
is a Poisson probability function,
\mathbf{z}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{z^{l}\}_{l\in[N(L)]}\in[0,1]^{N(L)}
is an
N(L)
-dimensional vector, and
\lambda^{l}(\mathbf{z})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[N(L)]}\kappa(l^{\prime},l)z^{l^{\prime}}
. The quantities
\mathbf{z}
,
\lambda
, and

are the discrete counterparts of the operators
f
,
\Lambda
and
P
defined in (
2.4
). In a sequential formulation of the process that we introduce in Appendix
A
, the function
\nu_{k}^{l}
tracks the number of vertices of type
l
that need another
k
infected neighbors to become infected themselves. The argument vector contains for each type
l\in[N(L)]
, the fraction of vertices of this type that are infected and whose impact on the system has already been explored.

In Appendix
A
, we show that if we find

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{z}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\mathbf{z}}\left\{\sum_{l\in[N(L)]}z^{l}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\left\{\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z})\right\}_{l\in[N(L)]}=\mathbf{0}\right\},

(2.26)

and a condition on the partial derivative of the multivariate function
\{\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z})\}_{l\in[N(L)]}
in
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}
holds, then
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}))|/n
, the total proportion of infected vertices in
G(n,\mathcal{V}^{d}_{L})
, will asymptotically converge to
\sum_{l\in[N(L)]}\widehat{z}^{l}
.

Now, having the formulas introduced above, we are able to formalize how we can embed the finite-dimensional description of the process for the random graph
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d})
into the operator
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}}
which is based on a step function kernel
\kappa_{L}
.

Definition 2.15

(Embedded finite-dimensional functions)
.

Divide the space
\mathcal{S}
into
L
subsets
(\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l})_{l\in[N(L)]}
the same way as in Definition
2.13
, then define the step functions
\kappa_{L}(s,s^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and for
\mathbf{z}\in[0,1]^{N(L)}
the function
f_{L}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1]
as

\displaystyle\kappa_{L}(s,s^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}\in[N(L)]}I_{\mathcal{S}^{l}}(s)I_{\mathcal{S}^{l^{\prime}}}(s^{\prime})\kappa^{d}_{L}(l,l^{\prime}),

\displaystyle f_{L}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{l\in[N(L)]}\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l})}z_{L}^{l}I_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}(s),

(2.27)

The following equality holds:

\displaystyle\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z}_{L})

\displaystyle=-z_{L}^{l}+\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\nu_{k^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{0})\left(1-\sum_{k^{\prime\prime}=0}^{k^{\prime}-1}p(k^{\prime\prime},\lambda^{j}(\mathbf{z}_{L}))\right)

(2.28)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}f_{L}(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s)+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}\Psi_{\kappa_{L}}[f_{L}](s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s),

(2.29)

where
\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z}_{L})
and
\nu_{k}^{l}(\mathbf{0})
are defined in equation (
2.24
).
Because all fixed points of
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}}
are step functions, this will then allow us to conclude that the first zero
\widehat{z}_{L}^{l}
of
\{\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z})\}_{l\in[N(L)]}
and the first fixed point
\hat{f}_{L}
of
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}}
are related by

\hat{f}_{L}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{l\in[N(L)]}\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l})}\widehat{z}_{L}^{l}I_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}(s)

(2.30)

and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in[N(L)]}\widehat{z}_{L}^{l}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}_{L}(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s).

(2.31)

Moreover, for
0<w_{l}<1,\ \sum_{l\in[N(L)]}w_{j}=1
and
h_{L}\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
defined by

\displaystyle h_{L}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{l\in[N(L)]}\tfrac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l})}w^{l}I_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}(s),

(2.32)

it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[N(L)]}w^{l^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z}_{L})}{\partial z^{l^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=-w^{l}+\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[N(L)]}\kappa_{L}^{d}(l^{\prime},l)w^{l^{\prime}}\right)\nu_{1}^{l}(\mathbf{z}_{L})

(2.33)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}h_{L}(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s)+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{L}^{l}}D\Psi_{\kappa_{L}}f_{L}[h_{L}](s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s).

(2.34)

On the left hand side we have the directional derivative of the multivariate function
\{\nu_{0}^{l}(\mathbf{z})\}_{l\in[N(L)]}
while on the right hand side we have an expression that involves the Fréchet derivative in the direction
h_{L}
. The identity will allow us to derive stopping criteria for the percolation process in
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d})
from the Fréchet derivative of
\Psi_{\kappa}
. Because of the above equalities, we say that
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d})
can be embedded into
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L})
. Because
|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{\pm}))|=|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L}^{d,\pm}))|
, these equalities allow us to show that the set of infected vertices is related to the fixed point of
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}}
.

In the following simple lemma, we state some convergence properties of the operators

2.2.2
Resilience

For the proof of non-resilience, one first notes that the existence of a function
h\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
such that
D\widetilde{\Psi}\mathbf{0}[h]>h
implies that by continuity of the derivative, there exists
\alpha>0
such that
\widetilde{\Psi}[ah]>0
for
a\in(0,\alpha]
. We will then see that for the operator
\Psi
of the infected network it actually holds that
\Psi[ah]\geq\widetilde{\Psi}[ah]>0
. The lemma below then ensures that a fixed point
\hat{f}
of
\Psi
(if any) is such that
\hat{f}\geq\alpha h
. We can then show that the monotonically increasing sequence of fixed points
\hat{f}^{-}_{L}
for the approximating random graphs is such that
\hat{f}^{-}_{L}\geq\alpha h
for
L
large, which will lead to the lower bound.

Lemma 2.19
.

If there exists a continuous and positive function
h\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{c}
and
a_{0}\in[0,1]
such

\displaystyle\Psi[ah]>ah,

(2.38)

for all
a\in[0,a_{0}]
.
Then, for any
g\in\mathcal{F}_{b}
such that

\displaystyle\Psi[g]=g,

(2.39)

it holds that
g\geq a_{0}h
.

To show resilience, the situation is reversed. Using the condition
D\widetilde{\Psi}\mathbf{0}[h]<h
one can show that for sufficiently small infections there exists
\alpha>0
such that
\Psi[ah]-ah<\mathbf{0}
for
a\in(0,\alpha]
. By Lemma
2.16
together with the embedding (
2.33
) we can then use the approximating kernels
\kappa_{L}^{+}
to show that there exists a constant
C>0
such that for small enough initial infection
\nu_{0}(\mathcal{S})>0
, the final fraction of infected vertices is bounded by
C\nu_{0}(\mathcal{S})
.

Fixed Point Algorithm and Case Study

In Section

of

Thereupon, we first propose an algorithm to approximate the minimal fixed point

3.1
Approximating the Fixed Point with a Neural Network

Here we give a brief description of a neural network, and then state a proposition that ensures the viability of our method to approximate the fixed point
\hat{f}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A single layer neural network

maps

d_{y}

\displaystyle f^{NN}=W_{2}\circ\sigma_{1}\circ W_{1},

(3.1)

where

W_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{W_{1}}}
,

are composable affine maps such that

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\bm{\theta}_{1}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1},

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mathbf{x})=\bm{\theta}_{2}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{2},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W_{1}}\times d_{x}},

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{W_{1}}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W_{1}}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W_{2}}}.

(3.3)

and
\sigma_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
is some activation function.

We collect in
\bm{\theta}=\{\bm{\theta}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2},\mathbf{b}_{2}\}
the parameter set of the neural network. The dimension

is the hyperparameter specified when implementing the neural network. Additionally, a multiple-layer neural network can be defined by the compositions

\displaystyle f^{NN}=\cdots W_{3}\circ\sigma_{2}\circ W_{2}\circ\sigma_{1}\circ W_{1}.

(3.4)

We represent a neural network, with either a single or multiple layers, in the form of

.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Assume that the minimal fixed point
\hat{f}
of
\Psi_{\kappa}
exists. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a neural network
f^{NN}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1]
, represented by
f^{NN}(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
where
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is its parameter set, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f^{NN}(s;\bm{\theta})-\hat{f}(s)\right\rVert}<\epsilon,

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f^{NN}(s;\bm{\theta})-\Psi_{\kappa}f^{NN}(s;\bm{\theta})\right\rVert}<\epsilon.

(3.5)

Proof.

Recall that the functions
f\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{c}
are continues and that in particular
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{c}
. Then the existence of
f^{NN}
such that the first inequality holds is a direct consequence of the universal approximation theorem by
Cybenko 1989
. The second inequality can be ensured by possibly reducing
\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f^{NN}(s;\bm{\theta})-\hat{f}(s)\right\rVert}
further and applying Lemma
2.16
.
∎

We propose to approximate the minimal fixed point by the following procedure. The corresponding pseudocode is provided in Algorithm
1
.

1.

Initialize an neural network
f^{NN}
with parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,

2.

Evaluate the objective function with mean absolute error for the minimization:

\displaystyle J(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f^{NN}(x_{i};\bm{\theta}_{0})-\Psi_{\kappa}^{NN}f_{j}^{NN}(x_{i};\bm{\theta}_{0})\right\rvert}+\gamma\int_{\mathcal{S}}f^{NN}(s;\bm{\theta})\mathrm{d}\mu(s),

(3.6)

where
0<\gamma<1
is a parameter and
\{x_{0},\dots,x_{N}\}
are data points sampled from
\mathcal{S}

and its operator

3.

Evaluate the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J(\bm{\theta}_{0})
and obtain the new parameter

with gradient descent

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{1}=\bm{\theta}_{0}-\alpha_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J(\bm{\theta}_{0}),

(3.7)

is the learning rate, and

4.

Repeat step 2 and step 3 until the stopping criterion

\hat{f}

Remark 3.3
.

In the above proposition and algorithm, we explained how a single-layer neural network approximates the minimal fixed point with a simple gradient descent algorithm. In practice, many specifications can be adapted to speed up the learning process, such as using neural networks with more layers rather than one layer, and updating the parameters with more advanced methods such as the Adam algorithm (

Remark 3.4

Appendix A
Graph with A Finite Number of Vertex Types

In this section, we study the graph with finite vertex type introduced in Definition
2.14
. We start with a setting with bounded thresholds. For this let
K\in\mathbb{N}
and we assume that
k_{i}(n)\in[K]\cup\{0\}
for all
i\in[n]
. We will later extend the model to allow for thresholds in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For this reason we will refer to the vertex sequence by
\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa^{d}_{L},\nu,(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))
with
K
explicit in the subscript and to the corresponding graph by
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d})
.

Instead of exploring the final set of infected vertices
\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d}))
by generations as described in Section
2
, we use a
sequential process
that results in the same set of infected vertices. The idea is that in each step we only explore the effect on the system triggered by one infected vertex.

For this, at the beginning of each iteration, we uniformly select an infected vertex from the set of unexplored infected vertices. Denote this vertex by
i
. We then explore all edges that vertex
i
sends to uninfected vertices. We then reduce the threshold value of each receiving vertex by
1
. If the threshold of a receiving vertex reaches
0
, then this vertex is infected and we include it in the set of unexplored infected vertices. Then, after we have updated the threshold of all vertices that received an edge from vertex
i
, we remove the vertex
i
from the set of unexplored infected vertices. The effect of this vertex has now been explored. We repeat the iteration until there are no more unexplored infected vertices left.

Sequential Exploration Process:
To formulate the sequential process, we introduce a "time" index
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
to denote the
t
-th iteration. We first define for
l\in[L]
and

the sets

\displaystyle U_{k}^{l}(0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{i\in\left[n\right]|l_{i}=l,k_{i}(0)=k\},

\displaystyle u_{k}^{l}(0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|U_{k}^{l}(0)|,

where

is the (initial) set of type

. We further denote by

their size. Moreover, similar to the regularity condition (

), we shall assume the initial proportion

will converge to a limit

\displaystyle u_{k}^{l}(0)/n\mathrel{{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}}\nu_{k}^{l,*},

where

.

Then we keep track of the evolution of the thresholds of the vertices throughout the iterations. For any vertex

at round

, its threshold
0\leq k_{i}(t)\leq k_{i}(0)
equals to
k_{i}(0)
minus the number of edges it has received from explored infected vertices. For each

we therefore group vertices by their types and (current) thresholds:

\displaystyle U_{k}^{l}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{i\in\left[n\right]|l_{i}=l,k_{i}(t)=k\},

\displaystyle u_{k}^{l}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|U_{k}^{l}(t)|,

We also use

to denote all the vertices with threshold

at time

to denote their size:

\displaystyle U_{k}(t)=\cup_{l\in[L]}U_{k}^{l}(t),

\displaystyle u_{k}(t)=\sum_{l\in[L]}u_{k}^{l}(t).

(A.4)

Note that after each iteration, we drop the selected and explored infected vertex from the set

, i.e. if

is selected, then

for all

. It holds that

The last sets to be tracked are

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(t,G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d}))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\text{infected vertices explored up to time }t\},

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d}))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\text{infected vertices at the end of the process}\}.

(A.6)

By the nature of the sequential process we know that
u_{0}(0)\leq|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d}))|\leq n
.

Therefore, the sequential process is explored in the following manner. At time
t
, we uniformly select an unexplored infected vertex from set
U_{0}(t)
, say vertex
i
, whose threshold is
k_{i}(t)=0
. The probability that the type
l_{i}
of the uniformly selected vertex

is equal to
l
is given by
\tfrac{u_{0}^{l}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}
. Then, we explore all the vertices that receive an edge from
i
. If a vertex
j
with type
l_{j}
and threshold
k_{j}(t)>0
receives an edge from
i
, which happens with probability
\kappa(l_{i},l_{j})/n
, then we set
k_{j}(t+1)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=k_{j}(t)-1
. Henceforth in the next time step

,
j
moves to the set
U_{k_{j}(t)-1}^{l_{j}}(t+1)
or, equivalently,
U_{k_{j}(t+1)}^{l_{j}}(t+1)
. After all other vertices receiving an edge from
i
are examined, we consider the vertex
i
as explored and add it to the set
\mathbb{D}(t,G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d}))=\mathbb{D}(t-1,G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{d}))\cup\{i\}
and remove it for future explorations:
i\notin U_{0}(t+1)
.

Let

, we can write down the expected change of the sizes of the vertex sets by the following equations:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[u_{0}^{l}(t+1)-u_{0}^{l}(t)|h(t)]

\displaystyle=\left\{-\frac{u_{0}^{l}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}+\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}\frac{u_{0}^{l^{\prime}}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}\right)u_{1}^{l}(t)\right\},

(A.7)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[u_{k}^{l}(t+1)-u_{k}^{l}(t)|h(t)]

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}\frac{u_{0}^{l^{\prime}}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}\right)\left(u_{k+1}^{l}(t)-u_{k}^{l}(t)\right)\right\},\ \forall 1\leq k\leq K-1,

(A.8)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[u_{K}^{l}(t+1)-u_{K}^{l}(t)|h(t)]

\displaystyle=\left\{-\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}\frac{u_{0}^{l^{\prime}}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}\right)u_{K}^{l}(t)\right\},

(A.9)

and, consequently, the expected change of the sizes of the entire vertex sets across each threshold can be summarized by

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[u_{0}(t+1)-u_{0}(t)|h(t)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[L]}\mathds{E}[u_{0}^{l}(t+1)-u_{0}^{l}(t)|h(t)],

(A.10)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[u_{k}(t+1)-u_{k}(t)|h(t)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[L]}\mathds{E}[u_{k}^{l}(t+1)-u_{k}^{l}(t)|h(t)],\ \forall 1\leq k\leq K-1,

(A.11)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[u_{K}(t+1)-u_{K}(t)|h(t)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[L]}\mathds{E}[u_{K}^{l}(t+1)-u_{K}^{l}(t)|h(t)].

(A.12)

To understand the system of equations (
A.7
), let’s focus on vertices of type
l\in[L]
and the step from time
t
to
t+1
. For the first equation, the expected change of the number of infected vertices of type
l
consists of two parts. First, one already infected vertex is picked up for the sequential exploration with probability
\frac{u_{0}^{l}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}
. This vertex will then drop out and not be in the set
U_{0}^{l}(t+1)
, it will be "explored" in this step, which explains the term
-\frac{u_{0}^{l}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}
. The second, positive term accounts for the newly infected vertices in
U_{1}^{l}(t)
with a threshold equal to
1
at the beginning of the step and which get infected because they receive an edge from the currently explored vertex. Because our sequential process can only reduce thresholds by at most one in each time step, only the set
U_{1}^{l}(t)
contributes to new infections. The probability to select a vertex of type
l^{\prime}
for exploration in step
t+1
is
\frac{u_{0}^{l^{\prime}}(t)}{u_{0}(t)}
. Conditioning on the type of the vertex selected being of type
l^{\prime}
, the probability for it to connect to a vertex in the set
U_{1}^{l}(t)
is
\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}
. Hence, by summarizing all vertices in
U_{1}^{l}(t)
we obtain the second term. For threshold
1\leq k\leq K-1
, the change of the set
U_{k}^{l}(t)
results from vertices in
U_{k+1}^{l}(t)
which receive an edge from the explored infected vertex and are therefore added to
U_{k}^{l}(t+1)
. The negative term comes from those vertices in
U_{k}^{l}(t)
that are added to
U_{k-1}^{l}(t+1)
because they receive an edge from the currently explored vertex. Last, the number of vertices in
U_{K}(t)
in expectation decreases by the number of vertices in
U_{K}(t)
that receive an edge from the vertex that is currently being explored.

A heuristic interpretation of the solution (

\tau
of the percolation, for a vertex of type

is exactly defined in the form of the probability mass function of a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda

at time

\tau

We are interested in the time

for

and thus stops to hold before the sequential exploration comes to the end, we shall first study the time when the number of unexplored infected vertices reach a small proportion

and show that this time converges as

goes to

. Then, in the next step, we derive a condition that ensures that when the fraction of unexplored infected vertices reduces to zero, the actually process ends and it can not rebound and trigger new infections. That is, let
\hat{t}^{\delta}
be the first time when

, then we need to show that
\hat{t}/n
converges and that

Remaining Process:

The approximation system (
A.35
) works in the domain
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
before the process reaches
\widehat{\tau}^{\delta}
at which point there is still a proportion
\delta
of infected vertices left that is not yet explored. We want to know whether the percolation arising from these remaining
\delta n
vertices comes to the end soon or whether they still trigger a large number of additional infections. Thus, we study now the process triggered by the remaining small proportion
\delta
of infected vertices, which we call the
remaining process
. We explore the remaining process starting from the step
\lfloor\widehat{\tau}^{\delta}n\rfloor
by generations instead of the sequential procedure used so far. Then, we show that, under an appropriate condition, the proportion of infected vertices in the remaining process will converge to zero.

For this we group the remaining uninfected vertices at step

into

Let

\displaystyle W^{l}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=U_{1}^{l}(\lfloor\widehat{\tau}^{\delta}n\rfloor),

denote sets of weak type
l
vertices which have threshold equal to

, and their union set of all weak vertices; and

\displaystyle S^{l}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{k=2}^{K}U_{k}^{l}(\lfloor\widehat{\tau}^{\delta}n\rfloor),

\displaystyle S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{l\in[L]}S^{l}

(A.45)

denote sets of strong type
l
vertices which have threshold greater or equal to
2
, and their union set of all "strong" vertices.
To track the iterative exploration, we use subscript
j\in\mathbb{N}
for the
j
-th round of iteration of our exploration process. Let
U_{0}^{l}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=U_{0}^{l}(\lfloor\widehat{\tau}^{\delta}n\rfloor)
denote the "initial" set of infected vertices of type
l\in[L]
in the remaining process;
W_{j}^{l}
and

denote the type

"weak" vertices and "strong" vertices in the

Our remaining process is explored as follows: in the first round, we examine all the "weak" vertices and "strong" vertices that are infected by vertices in

,

which leads to the Proposition

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}
as defined in (
A.40
). If for some
\epsilon>0
and some some direction
\{w^{l}\}_{1\leq l\leq L}\in\{w^{l}>0,\sum_{l\in[L]}w^{l}\leq 1\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\nu_{0}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})}{\partial z^{l^{\prime}}}<-\epsilon,\ \forall 1\leq l\leq L,

(A.46)

then there exists some
\delta^{*}>0
and
0<\epsilon^{*}<\epsilon
such that for all
0<\delta<\delta^{*}
it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\nu_{0}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{\delta})}{\partial z^{l^{\prime}}}<-\epsilon^{*},\ \forall 1\leq l\leq L.

(A.47)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{\delta}
is as defined in (
A.40
).

Proof.

Note that
\mathbf{z}^{\delta}\leq\widehat{\mathbf{z}}
. For some positive
\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
, let
\mathbf{z}^{\delta}=\widehat{\mathbf{z}}-\bm{\delta}
, plug in the Derivative (
A.39
) into the l.h.s of the inequality (
A.47
), and insert
\lambda
and
p(k,\lambda)
defined in equation (
A.17
) and (
A.35
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\nu_{0}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})}{\partial z^{l^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\kappa(l^{\prime},l)\nu_{1}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}-\bm{\delta})

(A.48)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\kappa(l^{\prime},l)\sum_{k^{\prime}=k}^{K}\nu_{k^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{0})p(k^{\prime}-k,\lambda^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}-\bm{\delta}))

(A.49)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\kappa(l^{\prime},l)\sum_{k^{\prime}=k}^{K}\nu_{k^{\prime}}^{l}(\mathbf{0})p(k^{\prime}-k,\lambda^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}))\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{l}(\bm{\delta})}

(A.50)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\nu_{0}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})}{\partial z^{l^{\prime}}}\min_{l\in[L]}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{l}(\bm{\delta})}

(A.51)

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon\min_{l\in[L]}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{l}(\bm{\delta})}

(A.52)

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon\min_{l\in[L]}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{l}(\bm{\delta}^{*})},

(A.53)

where
\mathbf{0}<\bm{\delta}<\bm{\delta}^{*}
. Let
\epsilon^{*}=-\epsilon\min_{l\in[L]}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{l}(\bm{\delta}^{*})}
and the result follows.
∎

Proposition A.5

(Convergence of remaining process)
.

If condition
A.46
of Lemma
A.4
holds,
then

\displaystyle n^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}W_{j}\cup S_{j}\right\rvert}\mathrel{{\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits}}0.

(A.54)

Proof.

Apply Lemma
A.4
, and choose a small
\delta^{*}>0
, then for any
0<\delta<\delta^{*}
, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\nu_{0}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{\delta})}{\partial z^{l^{\prime}}}<-\epsilon^{*},\ \forall 1\leq l\leq L,

(A.55)

for some small
0<\epsilon^{*}<\epsilon
. Then,
for the fixed
\{w^{l}\}_{1\leq l\leq L}
, plugging the derivative (
A.39
) into the above inequality yields

\displaystyle\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\kappa(l^{\prime},l)\nu_{1}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{\delta})<c_{1}^{l}w^{l}<w^{l}-\epsilon^{*},

(A.56)

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in[L]}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}w^{l^{\prime}}\kappa(l^{\prime},l)\nu_{1}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{\delta})<\sum_{l\in[L]}c_{1}^{l}w^{l}<c_{1}\sum_{l\in[L]}w^{l}\leq c_{1},

(A.57)

where
0<c_{1}^{l}<1
and
c_{1}=\max_{l}c_{1}^{l}
are constants not depending on
\delta
.

Next, for any other
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta^{*}
and the fixed direction
\mathbf{w}
, as
\nu_{0}^{l}
decreases with
\delta
we are able to choose
0<\delta<\delta^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\nu_{1}^{l}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{\delta})<w^{l}\delta^{\prime},\ \forall l\in[L].

(A.58)

Then, we use induction to show that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|W_{j}|\right]\leq c_{1}c^{j-1}\delta^{\prime}n,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|S_{j}|\right]\leq c_{2}c^{j-1}\delta^{\prime}n,

where

,
c_{2}=c-c_{1}
. First, for
j=1
, fix an infected vertex

and a "weak" vertex

, we write the connection probability as

\displaystyle p_{l^{\prime}l}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathds{P}\left\{E_{xy}=1|x\in U_{0}^{l^{\prime}},y\in W^{l}\right\}=\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}.

(A.60)

Then, the probability that the "weak" vertex

is infected is

\displaystyle p_{l}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathds{P}\left\{\cup_{l^{\prime}=1}^{L}\cup_{x\in U_{0}^{l^{\prime}}}\{E_{xy}=1\}|x\in U_{0},y\in W^{l}\right\}\leq\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}|U_{0}^{l^{\prime}}|.

and
|W^{l}|=|U_{1}^{l}(\lfloor\widehat{\tau}^{\delta}n\rfloor)|
, we obtain the following inequality for the expected size of "weak" vertices infected in the first round:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|W_{1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[\sum_{x,y}I_{\{E_{xy}=1\}}|x\in U_{0},y\in W\right]

(A.62)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\in[L]}|W^{l}|p_{l}

(A.63)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[L]}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}|W^{l}||U_{0}^{l^{\prime}}|

(A.64)

\displaystyle\leq n\delta^{\prime}\sum_{l\in[L]}c_{1}^{l}w^{l}

(A.65)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\delta^{\prime}n,

(A.66)

where we use inequalities (
A.56
) and (
A.58
).

For "strong" vertices, we first calculate the probability that a fixed vertex
y\in S
receives edges from at least two infected vertices

and
x_{2}\in U_{0}^{l^{\prime\prime}}
which is

\displaystyle p(x_{1},x_{2},y,l^{\prime},l^{\prime\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left\{\{E_{x_{1}y}=1,E_{x_{2}y}=1\}|x_{1}\in U_{0}^{l^{\prime}},x_{2}\in U_{0}^{l^{\prime\prime}},y\in S\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[L]}\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)\kappa(l^{\prime\prime},l)}{n^{2}}|S^{l}|.

(A.68)

The expected number of "strong" vertices infected in the first round can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|S_{1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[I_{\{E_{x_{1}y}=1,E_{x_{2}y}=1\}}|x_{1}\in U_{0},x_{2}\in U_{0},y\in S\right]

(A.69)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}|U_{0}^{l^{\prime}}|\sum_{l^{\prime\prime}\in[L]}|U_{0}^{l^{\prime\prime}}|p(x_{1},x_{2},y,l^{\prime},l^{\prime\prime})

(A.70)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M^{2}}{2n^{2}}|U_{0}|^{2}|S|

(A.71)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\delta^{\prime 2}n

(A.72)

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\delta^{\prime}n,

(A.73)

where
M=\max_{l^{\prime}}\{\sum_{l\in[L]}\kappa(l^{\prime},l)\}
by Assumption
2.2
.

Next, by induction, assume inequality (
A.59
) holds for
j=i,i\geq 1
, then for
j=i+1
we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|W_{i+1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[I_{\{E_{xy}=1\}}|x\in W_{i}\cup S_{i},y\in W\backslash\cup_{j=1}^{i}W_{i}\right]

(A.74)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[I_{\{E_{xy}=1\}}|x\in W_{i}\cup S_{i},y\in W\right]

(A.75)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\in[N(L)]}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[L]}\frac{\kappa(l^{\prime},l)}{n}|W^{l}|\mathds{E}\left[|(W_{i}\cup S_{i})^{l^{\prime}}|\right]

(A.76)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}c^{i}m\delta^{\prime}n,

(A.77)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|S_{i+1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left[I_{\{E_{x_{1}y}=1,E_{x_{2}y}=1\}}|x_{1}\in W_{i}\cup S_{i},x_{2}\in W_{i}\cup S_{i},y\in\backslash\cup_{j=1}^{i}S_{i}\right]

(A.78)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2n^{2}}\mathds{E}\left[|(W_{i}\cup S_{i})|\right]^{2}|S|

(A.79)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{2}c^{2i}\delta^{\prime 2}n

(A.80)

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}c^{i}\delta^{\prime}n.

(A.81)

Applying inequalities (
A.74
) and recalling that

, for any
a>0
we can use the Markov inequality which leads to the result (
A.54

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left\{\frac{|\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}W_{j}\cup S_{j}|}{n}>a\right\}

\displaystyle\leq a^{-1}\mathds{E}\left[\frac{|\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}W_{j}\cup S_{j}|}{n}\right]

(A.82)

\displaystyle\leq a^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathds{E}\left[\frac{|W_{j}\cup S_{j}|}{n}\right]

(A.83)

\displaystyle\leq a^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(c_{1}+c_{2})c^{j}\delta^{\prime}

(A.84)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c\delta^{\prime}}{(1-c)a}.

(A.85)

∎

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

The statement becomes obvious once we rewrite (

) as

\Psi_{\kappa}[\cdot](\cdot)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}(\cdot)\mathds{P}\left\{\text{Poi}(\Lambda_{\kappa}[\cdot](\cdot))\geq k)\right\},

\Lambda_{\kappa}[f](\cdot)\leq\Lambda_{\kappa}[g](\cdot)

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

We prove the second case where
\kappa
is Lipschitz. If
\kappa
is only continuous, then by compactness of
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
, it follows from the Heine–Cantor theorem that
\kappa
is uniformly continuous on
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
. It is then easy to see that one can adapt the following proof by replacing the Lipschitz constant
L_{\kappa}
by an
\epsilon
-
\delta
argument. Note that
\Lambda_{\kappa}[f]\leq M_{\kappa}
as
f(s)\leq 1
, and that
\mathbf{0}\leq P_{\kappa}^{k}[f]\leq\mathbf{1}
,
\mathbf{0}\leq\Psi_{\kappa}[f]\leq\mathbf{1}
as they are in the form of a Poisson probability. For the Lipschitz continuity, we examine each of the operators as follows:

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa(s,x)f(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s)-\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa(s,y)f(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\right|

(B.1)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|\kappa(s,x)-\kappa(s,y)|f(s)\mathrm{d}\mu(s)

(B.2)

\displaystyle\leq L_{\kappa}|x-y|.

(B.3)

Next, for the
P_{\kappa}^{k}
operators and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it follows that

\displaystyle|P_{\kappa}^{k}f(x)-P_{\kappa}^{k}f(y)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)\right)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)}-\frac{\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)\right)^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)}\right|

(B.5)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)}}{k!}\left|\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)\right)^{k}-\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)\right)^{k}\right|+\frac{\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)\right)^{k}}{k!}\left|\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)}-\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)}\right|

(B.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)\right)^{i}\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)\right)^{k-1-i}}{k!}\left|\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)\right|+\frac{\left(\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)\right)^{k}}{k!}\left|\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)-\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)\right|.

(B.7)

In the last line, the first term applies the equality
1-a^{k}=(1-a)(1+a+\dots+a^{k-1})
with
a=\Lambda_{\kappa}f(y)/\Lambda_{\kappa}f(x)
, and the second term uses that
|\mathrm{e}^{-x}-\mathrm{e}^{-y}|\leq|x-y|
. Then, recalling the Lipchitz continuity of
\Lambda_{\kappa}
and the upper bound of
\kappa

\displaystyle|P_{\kappa}^{k}f(x)-P_{\kappa}^{k}f(y)|

\{\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L}}\}_{L\geq 1}
is increasing, we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L+1}}g_{L+1}(s)-g_{L+1}(s)\geq\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L}}\underline{g}(s)-\underline{g}(s)>0\text{ for }s\in\mathcal{S}_{L}^{j^{*}}.

(B.49)

Hence,
g_{L+1}
can only be a fixed point of
\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L+1}}
if
\hat{f}^{-}_{L}\leq g_{L+1}
.

We conclude that
\{\hat{f}^{-}_{L}\}_{L\geq 1}
is a bounded and increasing sequence. By the monotone convergence theorem, for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
there exists the point-wise limit
\hat{f}^{-}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\hat{f}^{-}_{L}(s)
. Then by Lemma
2.16
, we have that
\Psi_{\kappa_{L}^{\pm}}\hat{f}_{L}^{-}\rightarrow\Psi_{\kappa}\hat{f}^{-}
uniformly.

Collecting what we have shown so far we get that

\hat{f}^{-}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\hat{f}_{L}^{-}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\Psi_{\kappa_{L}^{\pm}}\hat{f}_{L}^{-}=\Psi_{\kappa}\hat{f}^{-}

and hence
\hat{f}^{-}
is a fixed point of
\Psi_{\kappa}
. Moreover,
\hat{f}^{-}
is indeed the minimal fixed point,
\hat{f}^{-}\equiv\hat{f}
. It follows from the fact that the sequence
\{\hat{f}_{L}^{-}\}
is bounded by
\hat{f}
. Too see this, assume there exists an
L^{*}
such that
\hat{f}_{L^{*}}^{-}>\hat{f}
. Then it follows in particular that
\hat{f}_{L}^{-}\geq\hat{f}_{L^{*}}^{-}>\hat{f}
for
L\geq L^{*}
. Let
g=\hat{f}+\varepsilon h<\hat{f}_{L^{*}}^{-}
for a small enough
\varepsilon
. From the condition on the Fréchet derivative of
\Psi_{\kappa}
it follows that
\Psi_{\kappa}g-g<0
. As
\kappa^{-}_{L}\leq\kappa
, we obtain that
\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L}}g-g<0
. Approximating
g
with step functions
g_{L}
on
\{\mathcal{S}^{l}_{L}\}_{l\in N(L)}
, it follows for
L
large that also
\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L}}g_{L}-g_{L}<0
. This implies that
\hat{f}_{L}^{-}
cannot be the minimal fixed point of
\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L}}
(compare again
[
8
, Lem 3.5]
).

Revisiting the uniform convergence of the operator, we see that
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\hat{f}_{L}^{-}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\Psi_{\kappa_{L}^{-}}\hat{f}_{L}^{-}=\Psi_{\kappa}\hat{f}=\hat{f}
, we obtain the uniform convergence of the minimal fixed points. Finally, It follows directly that
\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}^{-}_{L}\mathrm{d}\mu\rightarrow\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}\mathrm{d}\mu
and again by Lemma
2.16
that
D\Psi_{\kappa^{-}_{L}}\hat{f}_{L}^{-}\rightarrow D\Psi_{\kappa}\hat{f}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.19
.

Assume there exist such a fixed point
g
with

. Let
\hat{a}=\sup_{a\in[0,a_{0}]}\{ah(s)\leq g(s)\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}\}

. We obtain that

. The order preserving property of
\Psi
(Lemma
2.4
) and the inequility (
2.38
) shows that
\Psi[g](\hat{s})-g(\hat{s})\geq\Psi[\hat{a}h](\hat{s})-\hat{a}h(\hat{s})>0

at the point

.

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

This proof follows the similar idea of building coupling graphs
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{L,K}^{\pm})
for
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{K})
as in the proof of Theorem
A.8
. For
G(n,\mathcal{V})
, we will build coupling graphs by clipping the thresholds and reduce to the random graphs
G(n,\mathcal{V}_{K})
that we have already studied.

The regularity condition
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(k,\mathcal{S})=1
implies that the proportion of vertices with large thresholds is small, i.e.

\forall\epsilon>0
,
\exists K_{0}\in\mathcal{N}
s.t.
\forall K\geq K_{0}
,
\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\nu(k,\mathcal{S})<\epsilon
.
Define
\epsilon_{K}(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1]
by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{K}(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\nu(k,\cdot),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{K}\equiv\epsilon_{K}(\mathcal{S}).

(B.50)

We build an
upper coupling graph

G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
and a
lower coupling graph

G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
with vertex sequence
\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa,\overline{\nu}^{K},(\bm{s}(n),\bm{k}(n))
and
\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\kappa,\underline{\nu}^{K},(\bm{s}((1-\epsilon_{K})n),\bm{k}((1-\epsilon_{K})n))
. Their vertex distributions are

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}^{K}(k,\cdot)=\begin{cases}\nu(k,\cdot),&0\leq k\leq K-1\\
\sum_{k^{\prime}=K}^{\infty}\nu(k^{\prime},\cdot),&k=K\end{cases},

\displaystyle\underline{\nu}^{K}(k,\cdot)=\frac{\nu(k,\cdot)}{1-\epsilon_{K}},\quad

\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq K.

(B.51)

For
G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
, we "clip" the large thresholds to
K
by mapping all vertices in
G(n,\mathcal{V})
with threshold
k\geq K
into vertices with only threshold
K
in
G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
. Hence,
G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
is more vulnerable to infection than
G(n,\mathcal{V})
. For the
G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
, we exclude all the vertices in
G(n,\mathcal{V})
with thresholds larger than
K
. Their proportion is
\epsilon_{K}
. Hence
G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
only has
(1-\epsilon_{K})n
vertices. However, we consider the fraction
n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}))|
instead of
((1-\epsilon_{K})n)^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}))|
. It is equivalent to considering graph with
n
vertices in which the vertices with large threshold values are considered non-infectable. In this sense
G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
is less vulnerable to infection than
G(n,\mathcal{V})
. Consequently, by such a construction, we have the following inequality:

\displaystyle n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}))|\leq n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|\leq n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}))|.

(B.52)

The operators for
G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
and
G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
are given by

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}_{K}[f](\cdot)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\nu}^{K}(k,\cdot)}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\mu}^{K}(\cdot)}\left(1-\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}P^{k^{\prime}}[f](\cdot)\right),

(B.53)

\displaystyle\underline{\Psi}_{K}[f](\cdot)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{\mathrm{d}\underline{\nu}^{K}(k,\cdot)}{\mathrm{d}\underline{\mu}^{K}(\cdot)}\left(1-\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}P^{k^{\prime}}[f](\cdot)\right).

(B.54)

Applying again the coupling graph method explained in Section
2
to the graph
G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
(resp.
G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
), we build sequences of coupling graphs, denoted by
G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K,L}^{\pm})
(reps.
G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K,L}^{\pm})
) with
L\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\{\overline{\Psi}_{K,L}^{\pm}\}_{L\in\mathbb{N}}
(resp.
\{\underline{\Psi}_{K,L}^{\pm}\}_{L\in\mathbb{N}}
) be the corresponding sequence of operators. Working through essentially the same techniques as used in Lemma
2.16
and Proposition
2.17
, we can easily show the existence of the minimal fixed points
\overline{f}_{K}
(resp.
\underline{f}_{K}
) for
\overline{\Psi}_{K}
(resp.
\underline{\Psi}_{K}
), and further prove the following:

\displaystyle\overline{f}_{K},\underline{f}_{K}\rightarrow\hat{f}\text{ uniformly},

\displaystyle D\overline{\Psi}_{K}\overline{f}_{K},D\underline{\Psi}_{K}\underline{f}_{K}\rightarrow D\Psi_{\kappa}\hat{f}\text{ uniformly},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}}\overline{f}_{K},\int_{\mathcal{S}}\underline{f}_{K}\rightarrow\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}.

(B.55)

Finally, due to the condition (
2.11
) on the derivative

B.55

D\underline{\Psi}_{K}

A.8
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathbb{D}(G((1-\epsilon_{K})n,\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}))|}{(1-\epsilon_{K})n}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{p}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\underline{f}_{K}\xrightarrow[K\rightarrow\infty]{\text{unif}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}\xleftarrow[K\rightarrow\infty]{\text{unif}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\overline{f}_{K}\xleftarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{p}\frac{|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{K}))|}{n}.

(B.56)

Then, squeezing the inequality (
B.52
), we have

\displaystyle n^{-1}|\mathbb{D}(G(n,\mathcal{V}))|\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{p}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{f}.

(B.57)

Proof of Theorem

.

Note that
\mathbf{0}
is a fixed point of
\widetilde{\Psi}
