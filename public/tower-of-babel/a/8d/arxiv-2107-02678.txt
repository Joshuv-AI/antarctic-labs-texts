Deriving Kepler's Laws Using Quaternions
Christopher J. Abel

Abstract
In the past, Kepler painstakingly derived laws of planetary motion using difficult to understand and hard to follow techniques. In 1843 William Hamilton created and described the quaternions, which extend the complex numbers and can easily describe rotations in three dimensional space. In this article, we will harness this system to provide a new and intuitive way to derive Kepler’s laws. This will include using a quaternionic version of the spatial Kepler problem differential equation, and using the general solution to describe the motion of planets orbiting a central body. We use the standard method for regularizing celestial mechanics, but this article will be solely focused on showing the validity of Kepler’s laws.



I
The Star Conjugate

In order to describe the KS transformation in the form of quaternions we require a new operation that will help remove the fourth dimension for any general quaternion. This setup was suggested by
Waldvogel 2006
. We define the
star conjugate
of a quaternion
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{H}
to be

\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=q_{0}+q_{1}i+q_{2}j-q_{3}k.

(1)

Note that all the star conjugate does is negate the
k
term. We can easily represent this in terms of the quaternion conjugate,

\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=(-k)\overline{\boldsymbol{q}}k.

(2)

One may easily show that for any
\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}
,

(\boldsymbol{q}^{*})^{*}=\boldsymbol{q},\qquad|\boldsymbol{q}^{*}|=|\boldsymbol{q}|,\qquad(\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{v})^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\boldsymbol{q}^{*}.

(3)

We now define a mapping between the quaternion algebra to take advantage of the star conjugate. Consider the mapping,

\boldsymbol{q}\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}^{*}.

(4)

Immediately we notice,

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}^{*})^{*}=(\boldsymbol{q}^{*})^{*}\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=\boldsymbol{x},

(5)

which implies
\boldsymbol{x}
has the form

\boldsymbol{x}=x_{0}+x_{1}i+x_{2}j.

(6)

To find the values of
\{x_{i}\}_{0}^{2}
, we do the quaternion multiplication. The result yields

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=q_{0}^{2}-q_{1}^{2}-q_{2}^{2}+q_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=2(q_{0}q_{1}-q_{2}q_{3})

(7)

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=2(q_{0}q_{2}+q_{1}q_{3}).

This is the KS transformation in its classical form. Thus the KS transformation
q=(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\mapsto x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is given by the quaternion relation

\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}^{*},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=x_{0}+x_{1}i+x_{2}j
,
\boldsymbol{q}=q_{0}+q_{1}i+q_{2}j+q_{3}k
, and
\boldsymbol{q}^{*}
is defined as (
1
).

II
Differentiation

Since the mapping (
4
) involving the star conjugate maps from four dimensions to three, we are left with one degree of freedom. We take advantage of this by simplifying differentiation. To do this we impose a bilinear relation between a quaternion
\boldsymbol{q}
and its differential,
d\boldsymbol{q}
, being

\boldsymbol{q}\,d\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=d\boldsymbol{q}\,\boldsymbol{q}^{*}.

(9)

Note (
9
) has the form of a commutator. The differential of the star conjugate mapping (
4
) then becomes

d\boldsymbol{x}=d\boldsymbol{q}\,\boldsymbol{q}^{*}+\boldsymbol{q}\,d\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=2d\boldsymbol{q}\,\boldsymbol{q}^{*}.

(10)

Using these techniques,
Waldvogel 2008
reduces the spatial Kepler problem to

2\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime\prime}+h\boldsymbol{u}=0,

(11)

with the parameter

dt=r\cdot d\tau,\quad\frac{d}{d\tau}()=()^{\prime},

(12)

where
t
is time,
r
is the radius, and
u=q^{*}
. For later importance, we note the energy integral:

-2\left|\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\right|^{2}=-\mu+rh.

(13)

III
Deriving Kepler’s laws

In this section, we will derive Kepler’s first law in the standard method for regularizing celestial mechanics, then after which we will prove Kepler’s second and third laws using new techniques based upon differentiation and integration.

III.1
Kepler’s first law

We first solve (
11
) to find the general solution,

\boldsymbol{u}=A\cos(\omega\tau)+B\sin(\omega\tau),\ \omega=\sqrt{\frac{h}{2}},\ A,B\in\mathbb{H}.

(14)

There are two equations we note before continuing. The first is the
\cos
and
\sin
functions are linear in
\mathbb{H}
, and thus are two dimensional. The second is since the equation for motion of
\boldsymbol{u}
is two dimensional, we may rotate it into the complex plane without loss of generality, to make solving the fine details a bit easier. So after rotating to the complex plane, we rotate once more, such that the apocenter is a real number greater than zero.

Note that we are not scaling, translating, or making any changes to the equation at all, but merely changing its direction. Once we have solutions to the equation of motion in this form, we may rotate it back to the original equation of motion as needed.

After doing our rotations, (
14
) becomes,

\boldsymbol{u}=A\cos(\omega\tau)+iB\sin(\omega\tau),\ A,B\in\mathbb{R}.

(15)

Earlier we made the substitution
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{u}
, but with our rotations we have
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}
, not
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{H}
. Thus for our substitution, we need not include the star conjugate, so we have
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{u}^{2}
. Therefore our equation for motion of
\boldsymbol{x}
is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\left(A\cos(\omega\tau)+iB\sin(\omega\tau)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=A^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega\tau)-B^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega\tau)+2AB\cos(\omega\tau)\sin(\omega\tau)

=\frac{A^{2}-B^{2}}{2}+\frac{A^{2}+B^{2}}{2}\cos(2\omega\tau)+iAB\sin(2\omega\tau).

(16)

Since
2\omega\tau
defines the angle of the planet from the center, it must be the eccentric anomaly. Thus the eccentric anomaly, which we denote
E
, must be,

E=2\omega\tau=\sqrt{2h}\tau.

(17)

We substitute (
17
) into (
16
) to get,

\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{A^{2}-B^{2}}{2}+\frac{A^{2}+B^{2}}{2}\cos(E)+iAB\sin(E).

(18)

From equation (
18
) we see the center, which we denote
c
, must be,

c=\frac{A^{2}-B^{2}}{2}.

(19)

Since we know
(A^{2}+B^{2})/2\geq AB
for all
A
and
B
, we have,

a=\frac{A^{2}+B^{2}}{2},

(20)

where
a
is the semi-major axis, and,

b=AB,

(21)

where
b
is the semi-minor axis. We now may prove Kepler’s first law.

Theorem 1

(The Law of Orbits)
.

All planets move in elliptical orbits, with the sun at one focus.

Proof.

Note that the equation for the planets motion has the sun placed at the origin. Also note that the equation for motion is obviously either an ellipse, or a perfect circle. When using the semi-major axis
a
, the semi-minor axis
b
, and the center
c
, we find,

a^{2}-b^{2}-c^{2}=0.

(22)

This is the definition of a focus of an ellipse, thus the planet orbits the origin. Also if the orbit is a perfect circle, the center is at zero, and thus orbits the sun. Therefore since the sun is at the origin, the planet orbits the sun at one focus.
∎

III.2
Kepler’s second law

Since we have derived Kepler’s first law, we know that

e=\frac{c}{a},

(23)

where
e
is the eccentricity of the orbit. We then use (
19

A=\sqrt{a(1+e)},\qquad B=\sqrt{a(1-e)}.

Plugging (

\boldsymbol{x}=a(e+\cos(E))+ia\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\sin(E).

Using (

r=a(1+e\cos(E)).

(26)

We are now prepared to derive Kepler’s second law.

Theorem 2

(The Law of Areas)
.

A line that connects a planet to the sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{x}=a(e+\cos(E))+ia\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\sin(E)
, we have,

\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=a(e+\cos(E)),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=a\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\sin(E).

(27)

We then let,

\varphi=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\right),

(28)

and let the area swept out by the line segment be
\Sigma

We then take the derivative, cancel one

We now reduce the fraction. First we look at the numerator,

\frac{e\cos(E)+1}{(\cos(E)+e)^{2}}.

We then multiply the

\frac{(\cos(E)+e)^{2}-(e^{2}-1)\sin^{2}(E)}{(\cos(E)+e)^{2}}.

\frac{e\cos(E)+1}{(\cos(E)+e)^{2}-(e^{2}-1)\sin^{2}(E)}.

Note that the numerator is identical to

\frac{(e\cos(E)+1)^{2}}{(\cos(E)+e)^{2}-(e^{2}-1)\sin^{2}(E)}.

We then expand the numerator and denominator to get,

\frac{e^{2}\cos^{2}(E)+2e\cos(E)+1}{\cos^{2}(E)\!+\!2e\cos(E)+e^{2}-e^{2}\sin^{2}(E)\!+\!\sin^{2}(E)}.

(39)

Note we can now reduce the numerator and denominator to be the same value, so

\frac{e^{2}(1-\sin^{2}(E))+2e\cos(E)+1}{e^{2}(1-\sin^{2}(E))+2e\cos(E)+1}=1.

Thus substituting (

\frac{d\Sigma}{dt}=a\sqrt{\frac{h}{2}(1-e^{2})}.

(41)

We now have

III.3
Kepler’s third law

We now work to represent the energy
h

13

{2\left|\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\right|^{2}=2\left|\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\right|^{2}}

We set (

h=\frac{\mu}{2a}.

(43)

We now are prepared to derive Kepler’s third law.

Theorem 3

(The Law of Periods)
.

The square of the period of any planet is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit.

Proof.

Firstly we define,

n:=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{a^{3}}}.

(44)

We square and divide (
44

\frac{n^{2}}{2}=\frac{\mu}{2a^{3}}.

(45)

We substitute (

\frac{n^{2}}{2}=\frac{h}{a^{2}}.

We then multiply by two, take the square root, and substitute (

n=\frac{1}{a}\frac{dE}{d\tau}=\frac{1}{a}\frac{dE}{dt}\frac{dt}{d\tau}.

From (

We then substitute (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}ndt
