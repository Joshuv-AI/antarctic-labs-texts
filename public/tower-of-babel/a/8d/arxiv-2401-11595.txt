Local Harmonic Approximation to Quantum Mean Force Gibbs State
Prem Kumar

Abstract
When the strength of interaction between a quantum system and bath is non-negligible, the equilibrium state can deviate from the Gibbs state. Here, we obtain an approximate expression for such a mean force Gibbs state for a particle in an arbitrary one dimensional potential, interacting with a bosonic bath. This approximate state is accurate when either the system-bath coupling or the temperature is large, or when the third and higher derivatives of the potential are small compared to certain system-bath specific parameters. We show that our result recovers the ultra strong coupling and high temperature results recently derived in literature. We then apply this method to study some systems like a quartic oscillator and a particle in a quartic double-well potential. We also use our method to analyze the proton tunneling problem in a DNA recently studied in literature [Slocombe et al., Comm. Phys., vol. 5, no. 1, p. 109, 2022], where our results suggest the equilibrium value of the probability of mutation to be orders of magnitude lower than the steady state value obtained there ( 10 − 8 10^{-8} vs 10 − 4 10^{-4} ).

II
Main results

We consider a particle of mass
m
in an arbitrary potential
V(q)
, linearly coupled to a bath of harmonic oscillators. This system is described by the Hamiltonian,

H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+V(q)\\
+\sum_{k}\left[\frac{p_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}\left(q_{k}-\frac{c_{k}q}{m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right],

(2)

where
\omega_{k}
,
m_{k}
, and
c_{k}
are the frequency, mass, and coupling strength of the
k
th oscillator, respectively. For simplicity, we assume a one dimensional system, though our results are easily generalized to higher dimensions. Here and throughout, we assume
\hbar=1
.
Throughout this article we write operator matrix elements in position basis as
O(q,\eta)=\bra{q+\eta}\hat{O}\ket{q-\eta}
. Diagonal elements are obtained when
\eta=0
and will be denoted by
O(q)=O(q,0)
.

Upon integrating out the bath degrees of freedom, the MFGS for the particle can be expressed as a Euclidean path integral
weiss2012quantum
,

\rho(q,\eta)=Z_{R}^{-1}\int_{q+\eta}^{q-\eta}\mathcal{D}q(\tau)e^{-S_{0}[q(\tau)]-\Phi[q(\tau)]}

(3)

where,
\int_{x}^{y}\mathcal{D}q(\tau)
signifies
q(0)=x
and
q(\beta)=y
in the functional integral. The reduced partition function is the ratio of bath and system+bath partition functions,
Z_{R}=Z/Z_{B}
, and

\displaystyle S_{0}[q(\tau)]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\left(\frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}(\tau)^{2}+V\left(q(\tau)\right)\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\Phi[q(t)]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}K\left(\tau-\tau^{\prime}\right)\left(q(\tau)-q(\tau^{\prime})\right)^{2}

(5)

are the action for the free particle and the influence action, respectively. The bath correlation function is defined as,

\displaystyle K(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\frac{\cosh{[\omega(\beta/2-\tau)]}}{\sinh{[\omega\beta/2]}}

(6)

in terms of the spectral density

J(\omega)=\alpha\sum_{k}\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}\omega_{k}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k}),

(7)

where we have introduced the dimensionless number
\alpha
to characterize the strength of the coupling.

At large system-bath coupling the influence action [
Eq. 5
] dominates over the free action, such that amplitudes for paths with
q(\tau)\neq q(\tau^{\prime})
are exponentially small in the path integral
Eq. 3
. In the limit
\alpha\to\infty
, the ultra-strong coupling (USC) limit, the amplitudes for these path vanish and only the constant path contributes,
q(\tau)=q(\tau^{\prime})=q
with
\dot{q}(\tau)=0
. Similarly, at high temperature (but arbitrary coupling) the path length
\beta=1/k_{B}T
becomes small, so the kinetic term in the free action,
\propto\dot{q}(\tau)^{2}
, increasingly suppresses amplitudes for paths that deviate significantly from
q(\tau)=q
. In the limit
T\to\infty
(zero path length) we once again find that the only relevant path is the constant path with
\dot{q}(\tau)=0
. Thus, in either the USC limit or infinite temperature limit we have,

\displaystyle\rho(q,\eta)=\delta(\eta)Z^{-1}e^{-\beta V(q)},

(8)

where now
Z=\int dqe^{-\beta V(q)}
. This is the same USC result obtained in
cresser2021weak
and is CV equivalent of the high temperature result given in
timofeev2022hamiltonian
. Note that the argument used above to obtain the USC-MFGS is equally applicable for discrete systems. See Appendix.
D
for more details.

Given the discussion above, when either the coupling or temperature is large, but not infinite, we expect that only paths with small deviation around the constant path will contribute significantly to the path integral. In other words, reparameterising the path variable as
q(\tau)=q+\delta(\tau)
, we expect that only small values of
\delta(\tau)
will be relevant to the final result. Therefore, we propose a local harmonic approximation (LHA) for
\rho(q,\eta)
, where for large coupling or temperature, the effect of the potential locally around
q
is effectively harmonic. Then, to second order in
\delta(\tau)
, we can write

V(q+\delta(\tau))\\
\approx V(q)+\delta(\tau)V_{1}(q)+\frac{1}{2}\delta(\tau)^{2}V_{2}(q)\\
=V(q)-\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{q}^{2}Q_{q}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{q}^{2}\left(\delta(\tau)+Q_{q}\right)^{2}

(9)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{q}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{V_{2}(q)/m}

(10)

\displaystyle Q_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{1}(q)}{V_{2}(q)}

(11)

are the frequency and the displacement of the effective harmonic potential, respectively, and
V_{n}(q)
is the
n
th derivative of
V(q)
. In Fig.
1
we show the effective harmonic potentials found for different values of
q
using a double well potential for
V(q)
as an example.

With
Eq. 9
, the path integral in
Eq. 3
becomes Gaussian and can be computed exactly to obtain

\rho(q,\eta)=Z_{R}^{-1}\frac{\exp\left(-\beta V(q)\right)}{\exp\left(-\beta\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{q}^{2}Q_{q}^{2}\right)}\tilde{Z}_{q}\tilde{\rho}_{q}(Q_{q},\eta)

(12)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{q}(Q_{q},\eta)
is the MFGS for a HO with frequency
\omega_{q}
, coupled to the same bath as the original system, and
\tilde{Z}_{q}
is the corresponding partition function for the HO-bath system which depends on
V(q)
only through the effective frequency,
\omega_{q}
. We highlight that both
\tilde{\rho}_{q}
and
\tilde{Z}_{q}
are straightforward to compute since the HO-bath system is Gaussian and thus exactly solvable
grabert1988quantum
;
weiss2012quantum
. Although we have expressed
\rho(q,\eta)
in terms of the Gaussian state of a HO, we stress that
Eq. 12
is generally not Gaussian with respect to
q
. This is because the potential,
V(q)
, remains completely arbitrary and all quantities on the right hand side of
Eq. 12
are explicit or implicit functions of this potential.

While we have derived the LHA [
Eq. 12
] under the assumption of either strong coupling or large temperature, we note that it is exact for arbitrary coupling and temperature when the potential is actually harmonic, i.e. when
V(q)=aq+bq^{2}
for some
a
and
b
, no matter how large
\delta(\tau)
becomes. We therefore expect
Eq. 12
to also be accurate even at weak coupling and low temperature, where
\delta(\tau)
may not be small, as long as the third and higher derivatives of the potential are relatively small. This gives our result great flexibility in its range of validity.

Fitting a harmonic potential at each point
q
of a general potential
V(q)

One possible problem with the LHA is that when
V_{2}(q)<0
, e.g., near the maxima of the potential
V(q)
, the effective frequency of the HO defined in
Eq. 9
becomes imaginary, so the HO is inverted
barton1986quantum
;
subramanyan2021physics
;
baskoutas1993quantum
(see the orange curve in Fig.
1
). For an inverted HO, the potential is unbounded from below as
q\to\pm\infty
, so the MFGS for the HO,
\tilde{\rho}_{q}
in
Eq. 12
, is unphysical, unnormalisable, and gives divergent observables, e.g.
\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}=\infty
.
However, we show in Appendix.
2
that as long as
V_{2}(q)>-V_{P}
, for some
V_{P}>0
, the combination
\tilde{Z}_{q}\tilde{\rho}_{q}
can still be finite, where the quantity
\tilde{Z}_{q}\tilde{\rho}_{q}
amounts to an unnormalised path integral which has its important contribution still coming from a finite deviation from the constant path
q(\tau)=q
, as required for LHA to work for general potentials.

This number,
V_{P}
, is given by the smallest solution of the following equation in
x
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x}{y_{n}-x}=\frac{1}{2},

(13)

where

y_{n}=m\nu_{n}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}\frac{2\nu_{n}^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\nu_{n}^{2}},

(14)

and
\nu_{n}=2\pi nk_{B}T
are the Matsubara frequencies. Thus, provided that
V_{2}(q)>-V_{P}
, the LHA remains finite even for
V_{2}(q)<0
and then can be applied to nontrivial potentials, like the one depicted in Fig.
1
. We note that in the large coupling or temperature limits we have assumed in deriving the LHA, for
x<y_{1}
, the
n=1
term in
Eq. 13
dominates and we obtain the approximate solution
V_{P}\approx y_{1}/3
. Since
y_{n}
diverges with both coupling and temperature, we find the condition
V_{2}(q)>-V_{P}
is always satisfied in this limit, consistent with LHA becoming exact there.

In order to estimate the error associated with LHA, let us first define two length scales. For the effective quadratic potential approximated at point
q
, let
q_{cl}(\tau)
denote the classical path starting and ending at the point
q+\eta
and
q-\eta
. Then, let
\Delta(q,\eta)
denote the maximum deviation of the classical path from the constant path
q^{\prime}(\tau)=q
and let
\mathcal{E}(q)
denote the typical quantum deviation of all the paths about
q_{cl}(\tau)
that have significant contribution to the action. An estimate for
\Delta(q,\eta)
and
\mathcal{E}(q)
is derived in Appendix.
1
.

Then, the leading order correction to LHA is given by
\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)\rho(q,\eta)
, where we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)

\displaystyle\equiv-\beta\frac{V_{3}(q)}{3!}\left(\Delta(q,\eta)^{3}+3\mathcal{E}(q)^{2}\Delta(q,\eta)\right)

(15)

The relative error associated with the expectation value of an observable
\hat{O}
is given as [Appendix.
C
],

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\hat{O}}=\frac{1}{\braket{\hat{O}}}\int dq\int d\eta\;\hat{O}(q,\eta)\epsilon(q,\eta)|\rho(q,\eta)|

(16)

where, we have,

\displaystyle\epsilon(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)|+\epsilon_{T}

(17)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\int dq\rho(q)|\mathcal{T}(q)|

(18)

For LHA to be valid for the matrix element
\rho(q,\eta)
, we need
\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)\ll 1
. We also need
\epsilon_{T}\ll 1
, which translates into,

|V_{3}(q)|<<\frac{3!}{\beta\rho(q)\left|\Delta(q)^{3}+3\mathcal{E}(q)^{2}\Delta(q)\right|}

(19)

If, for a system,
Eq. 19
is invalid in some region, this does not make LHA totally inapplicable. As an example, if it is valid in two regions of space, A and B, then LHA can still predict the ratio of populations
{\rho_{A}}/{\rho_{B}}
in these two regions.

Finally, for an off-diagonal density matrix element
\rho(q,\eta)
, the value of the classical deviation,
\Delta(q,\eta)
, is at least as large as
\eta
, and hence as the value of
\eta
is increased, a stronger constraint on
V_{3}(q)
is imposed in
Eq. 19
. This sets a limit on the length scale on which LHA can accurately capture spatial coherences.

We summarize by commenting that each term in the action in
Eq. 3
gives rise to a regime in which LHA is accurate, i.e. the kinetic, dissipative and potential terms each give the high temperature, large coupling and the ‘slowly varying potential’ limit, respectively.

We are now going to use LHA to study various CV systems. Let us first study a quartic oscillator whose potential is given by,

V(q)=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}q^{2}+aq^{4}

(20)

A quartic potential is the simplest bounded potential that provides a deviation from the quadratic HO potential, latter being the case for which LHA works exactly. A quartic oscillator has been studied in the context of quantum to classical transition
Oliveira2006Quantum-classical
, quantum chaos
B.C.Bag1998A
;
Jt1992Semiclassical
;
Chakrabarty2019Out
and as a model of bath oscillators interacting with a system
Montenegro2014Nonlinearity
. Here, we study how the non-Gaussianity that naturally emerges in the MFGS of a quartic oscillator changes as the system bath coupling is increased.

We use an Ohmic spectral density of exponential cutoff form (as some integrals required to do the TEMPO calculations done in this section are analytically known in this cutoff),

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{2m\gamma}{\pi}\omega\exp\left(-\frac{\omega}{\Omega}\right)

(21)

Using Hartree atomic units, we set
\omega=1E_{h}
,
\Omega=5E_{h}
,
m=1m_{e}
and
k_{B}T=0.5E_{h}
.

To characterize the MFGS, we calculate the second and fourth order cumulants of position, i.e.
\kappa_{2}=\langle q^{2}\rangle
and
\kappa_{4}=\langle q^{4}\rangle-3\langle q^{2}\rangle^{2}
, respectively. The former of these quantifies the width of the spatial probability density while the latter quantifies how non-Gaussian the state is, since all cumulants of order three and higher vanish for Gaussian states. The system is symmetric around
q=0
so the odd-order cumulants of
q
vanish identically, regardless of whether the state is Gaussian.

To benchmark our results we use the analytically known weak and USC results, as well as the numerically exact method, TEMPO
strathearn2018efficient
;
strathearn2020modelling
;
chiu2022numerical
, applicable in weak and intermediate coupling regime.

The TEMPO is a tensor network technique to efficiently evaluate the path integral given in
Eq. 3
. It has been previously used to calculate the MFGS for discrete systems

. Here we use it to study a CV system like a particle in a generic 1D potential
V(q)
. Although the system in question is infinite dimensional, at any given temperature and coupling, it can be approximated as an effectively finite dimensional system by truncating the system Hilbert space in the energy eigenbasis
topaler1993system
. The value of this effective dimension can be determined through a convergence test. In this case for example, for
\gamma=0.5\;E_{h}
, we needed to treat the system as an effectively 9 dimensional discrete system to get sufficient convergence.

Asymmetric double well potential

Let us now discuss an asymmetric quartic DW potential of the form,

V(q)=a_{4}q^{4}-a_{2}q^{2}+a_{1}q

where we set
a_{4}=1/2=a_{2}
. In this and following sections, we use an ohmic spectral density of Drude-Lorentz form,

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{2m\gamma}{\pi}\omega\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\Omega^{2}}

IV
Conclusion

For a particle in a 1D potential
V(q)
, we derived an approximate expression for the MFGS [
Eq. 1
]. This method is accurate when either the system-bath coupling or the temperature is large, or when the third and higher derivatives of the potential are small compared to certain system-bath specific parameters. This result thus has applications in a wide variety of problems in quantum thermodynamics, technology, chemistry and quantum biology.

We have found that for a CV system, the USC
cresser2021weak
and high temperature
timofeev2022hamiltonian
results obtained recently are a special case of LHA [
Eq. 12
]. As a future direction, it would be interesting to apply a similar approach to use the analytically known exact expression for the master equation for a harmonic oscillator
hu1992quantum
to derive an approximate master equation for a particle in generic potential
V(q)
. Moreover, determining the origin of mismatch between MFGS and the steady state expression for the mutated population
\mathcal{M}
for the proton tunneling problem considered in
Sec. III.3
will be of interest
slocombe2022open
.

Finally, different ways to improve on LHA and get better error bounds for it will enhance its applicability to physical problems.

Appendix A
Summary of main results

In this section, we will summarize the basic results of this paper. For details, see the sections mentioned here.

The path integral representation for the MFGS,
\rho(q,\eta)=\braket{q+\eta|\hat{\rho}|q-\eta}
, is given as,

\rho(q,\eta)=Z_{R}^{-1}\int_{q+\eta}^{q-\eta}\mathcal{D}q(\tau)e^{-S[q(\tau)]}

(32)

Parameterising the path variable in terms of its deviation from the constant path,
q(\tau)=q+\delta(\tau)
, we expand the potential to second order in
\delta
[
Eq. 9
], resulting in
Eq. 32
becoming,

\displaystyle\rho(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=Z_{R}^{-1}\exp\left(-\beta V(q)+\beta\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{q}^{2}Q_{q}^{2}\right)\int_{\eta}^{-\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)e^{-S_{q}[\delta(\tau)+Q_{q}]}

(33)

where
S_{q}[\delta(\tau)+Q_{q}]
is the action for a HO with frequency
\omega_{q}
[
Eq. 10
]. Reparametrize
\delta(\tau)+Q_{q}\to\delta(\tau)
to get,

\displaystyle\int_{\eta}^{-\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)e^{-S_{q}[\delta(\tau)+Q_{q}]}

\displaystyle=\int_{Q_{q}+\eta}^{Q_{q}-\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)e^{-S_{q}[\delta(\tau)]}

(34)

\displaystyle=\tilde{Z}_{q}\tilde{\rho}_{q}(Q_{q},\eta)

(35)

where,
\tilde{\rho}_{q}(q,\eta)
is the MFGS for a HO with frequency
\omega_{q}
. That is, the functional integral in this equation is identical to that of a HO but for the reduced HO partition function
\tilde{Z}_{q}
.
This leads us to [
Eq. 12
],

\rho(q,\eta)=Z_{R}^{-1}\frac{\exp\left(-\beta V(q)\right)}{\exp\left(-\beta\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{q}^{2}Q_{q}^{2}\right)}\tilde{Z}_{q}\tilde{\rho}_{q}(Q_{q},\eta)

(36)

We can write the expression for LHA more explicitly as,

\displaystyle\rho(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\mathcal{G}(q)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}}\left(\frac{V_{1}(q)}{V_{2}(q)}\right)^{2}-2\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}\eta^{2}-\beta\left(V(q)-\frac{V_{1}(q)^{2}}{2V_{2}(q)}\right)\right)

(37)

Here,
\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}
and
\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}
are the corresponding effective
\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}
and
\braket{\hat{p}^{2}}
values of the harmonic potential approximated at point
q
, whose value is given as

;
grabert1988quantum
,

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\beta}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}_{n}(q)}

(38)

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\beta}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\omega_{q}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}{\mathcal{A}_{n}(q)}

(39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}(q)

\displaystyle=\omega_{q}^{2}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}

(40)

\displaystyle\nu_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\pi nk_{B}T

(41)

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{J(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}}\frac{2\nu_{n}^{2}}{\omega^{\prime 2}+\nu_{n}^{2}}

(42)

To arrive at
Eq. 37
, we did the following replacement in
Eq. 36
.
The expression for the reduced partition function,
\tilde{Z}_{q}
, is given as [Appendix.
1

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{q}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(q)\sqrt{2\pi\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}}

where
\mathcal{I}(q)
is defined as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(q)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{0}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)e^{-S_{q}[\delta(\tau)]}

(45)

Here,

q

can depend upon

\Omega
and
\gamma
but not on

because the latter explicitly depends upon

by definition [

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=J\left(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{n}\right)\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{0}\left(2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}\right)^{2}-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{n}\left(x_{n}^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\right)\right\}

Here,
J
is the Jacobian coming from the linear transformation given in

.
We will now analytically evaluate this integral. But we should first note that this integral will diverge if for
n\geq 1
,
\alpha_{n}\leq 0
, which would break down LHA.

Before proceeding, note that we have

\mathcal{\alpha}_{|n+1|}>\mathcal{\alpha}_{|n|}
.
This is true because
\nu_{|n+1|}>\nu_{|n|}
follows from definition and immediately gives
\zeta_{|n+1|}>\zeta_{|n|}
since
x/(a+x)
monotonically increases for positive
x
and
a
. Hence, as a necessary condition for

to be convergent, we just need to impose that
\alpha_{1}>0
. We will hence restrict ourselves to this constraint. (As a side remark, note that this assumption automatically ensures the convergence of
\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}
[
Eq. 39
].)
But it is still unclear whether
\alpha_{0}\leq 0
will lead to divergence in
Eq. 78
or not.
In this section, we will see that
\mathcal{I}
can be convergent even if
\alpha_{0}\leq 0
.

After doing the
y_{n}
integral in
Eq. 78
, we get,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=J\left(\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha_{m}}}\right)\left(\prod_{n=1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{n}\right)\exp\left\{-\left(2\alpha_{0}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}\right]^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}x_{n}^{2}\right)\right\}

Let us define the negative of the exponent above as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\equiv\left(2\alpha_{0}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}\right]^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}x_{n}^{2}\right)

If
\min_{x_{n}}\mathcal{F}>0
, then
\mathcal{I}
will converge. We can think of
\mathcal{F}
as a diagonal component of a matrix
M
, i.e.

\mathcal{F}\equiv\langle v|M|v\rangle

where,

\langle v|=\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},...|
and

M=D+2\alpha_{0}J

where, for
i,j\geq 1
,
J_{ij}=1
and
D_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\alpha_{i}
. Let
\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of matrix
M
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=J\left(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}\lambda_{n}}}\right)

Before proceeding, let us first simplify an expression we will need immediately. From the definition of
\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}
[
Eq. 38
] and
\alpha_{n}
[
Eq. 75
], we have,

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle\implies 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}

(83)

Proceeding with the derivation of
\mathcal{I}
again, we next need to calculate the value of the product
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}
. We note that this is just given by the determinant of the matrix
M
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle=|M|

(84)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\right)+2\alpha_{0}\left(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\right)\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\right)

(85)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\right)\left(1+\alpha_{0}\left(\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\right)\qquad\text{from \autoref{eqn:sum_as_q2}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=J\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\pi}{\alpha_{n}}\right)

scales as

. But to get a reasonable result for LHA, we can proceed as follows. In the expression for LHA [

], we effectively have a factor of,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{I}(q)}{Z_{R}}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{G}(q)}{Z}

\displaystyle Z=Z_{R}\left(\frac{1}{J}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\frac{m}{\beta}(\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n})}{\pi}\right)

We hence have, for
\mathcal{G}(q)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z}{Z_{R}}\mathcal{I}=\left(\frac{1}{J}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\frac{m}{\beta}(\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n})}{\pi}\right)\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n}-\frac{m\omega^{2}}{\beta}}{\alpha_{n}}

This quantity can be shown to converge. On the other hand,

\Omega

\gamma

\omega

Now, we are going to investigate the condition for

\alpha_{n\geq 1}>0

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\implies\alpha_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}\right)

\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle\implies\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{n}}

Appendix C
Error estimation

In this section, we are going to derive the expression for the estimation of the error associated with LHA [
Eq. 16
].
We start with
Eq. 3
. Parameterising the path variable as
q(\tau)=q+\delta(\tau)
, where
q(\tau)
starts and ends at points
q+\eta
and
q-\eta
, respectively, we can write,

V(q+\delta)=V(q)+V_{1}(q)\delta+\frac{1}{2!}V_{2}(q)\delta^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}V_{3}(q)\delta^{3}+O(\delta^{4})

(99)

Let us write the full action in terms of the quadratic part and higher order terms in the potential, as,

\displaystyle S[q+\delta(\tau)]=S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]+\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\sum_{n=3}\frac{1}{n!}V_{n}(q)\delta(\tau)^{n}

(100)

Then, writing the exact state
\sigma(q,\eta)
in terms of harmonic action
S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]
and higher order corrections over it, we get,

\displaystyle\sigma(q,\eta)=Z^{\prime-1}\left\{\int_{-\eta}^{\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\sum_{n=3}\frac{1}{n!}V_{n}(q)\delta(\tau)^{n}\right)e^{-S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]}\right\}

(101)

Here,
Z^{\prime}
is the normalization factor defined as,

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dq\left\{\int_{0}^{0}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\sum_{n=3}\frac{1}{n!}V_{n}(q)\delta(\tau)^{n}\right)e^{-S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]}\right\}

(102)

Let us approximate
Eq. 101
as,

\displaystyle\sigma(q,\eta)

\displaystyle\approx\rho(q,\eta)-\sum_{n=3}\frac{V_{n}(q)}{n!}\left\{\int_{-\eta}^{\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\delta(\tau)^{n}\right)e^{-S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]}\right\}

(103)

\displaystyle\approx\rho(q,\eta)-\frac{V_{3}(q)}{3!}\left\{\int_{-\eta}^{\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\delta(\tau)^{3}\right)e^{-S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]}\right\}

(104)

Here,
\rho(q,\eta)
is the LHA state and in the last step, we have made a first order approximation. We now need to calculate the quantity,

\displaystyle I_{3}=\int_{-\eta}^{\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\delta(\tau)^{3}\right)e^{-S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]}

(105)

Here, we are going to estimate this path integral using the following argument. Reparametrize
q+\delta(\tau)\to q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)+\delta(\tau)
. Then using
S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]\to S_{q}[q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)+\delta(\tau)]=S_{q}[q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)]+S_{q}[\delta(\tau)]
, we get,

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=e^{-S_{q}[q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)]}\int_{0}^{0}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\left(q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)-q+\delta(\tau)\right)^{3}\right)e^{-S_{q}[\delta(\tau)]}

(106)

Now, if
n
is an odd number, then we have
(-\delta(\tau))^{n}=-\delta(\tau)^{n}
. But since
S_{q}[-\delta(\tau)]=S_{q}[\delta(\tau)]
, when we take the full path integral, we get,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{0}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau(q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)-q)^{m}\delta(\tau)^{n}\right)e^{-S_{q}[\delta(\tau)]}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{for odd}\;n

(107)

\displaystyle\implies I_{3}

\displaystyle=e^{-S_{q}[q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)]}\int_{0}^{0}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\left((q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)-q)^{3}+3(q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)-q)\delta(\tau)^{2}\right)\right)e^{-S_{q}[\delta(\tau)]}

(108)

Now, we are going to replace
q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)-q
with its maximum value, given by
\Delta(q,\eta)
, and
\delta(\tau)
with its typical value, given by
\mathcal{E}(q)
, to obtain,

\displaystyle I_{3}\approx\beta\left(\Delta(q,\eta)^{3}+3\mathcal{E}(q)^{2}\Delta(q,\eta)\right)\int_{-\eta}^{\eta}\mathcal{D}\delta(\tau)e^{-S_{q}[q+\delta(\tau)]}

(109)

We will estimate
\Delta(q,\eta)
and
\mathcal{E}(q)
in
Sec. 1
.

Let’s define a quantity,
\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)
, as a measure of the relative error corresponding to the matrix element
\rho(q,\eta)
as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)

\displaystyle\equiv-\beta\frac{V_{3}}{3!}\left(\Delta(q,\eta)^{3}+3\mathcal{E}(q)^{2}\Delta(q,\eta)\right)

(110)

Also, for convenience, let us define,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\int dq\rho(q)|\mathcal{T}(q)|

(111)

\displaystyle=\braket{|\mathcal{T}(q)|}

(112)

Then,
Eq. 101
becomes,

\displaystyle\sigma(q,\eta)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\rho(q,\eta)\pm\rho(q,\eta)|\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)|}{Z^{\prime}}

(113)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho(q,\eta)\pm\rho(q,\eta)|\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)|}{1\pm\epsilon_{T}}

(114)

\displaystyle\approx\rho(q,\eta)\pm\epsilon(q,\eta)\rho(q,\eta)

(115)

where,

\displaystyle\epsilon(q,\eta)=|\mathcal{T}(q,\eta)|+\epsilon_{T}

(116)

gives a measure of the total relative error for the matrix element
\rho(q,\eta)
. To get an expression for the relative error associated with an observable
\hat{O}
, we define,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\hat{O}}=\frac{1}{\braket{\hat{O}}}\int dq\int d\eta\;\hat{O}(q,\eta)\epsilon(q,\eta)|\rho(q,\eta)|

(117)

1
Length Scale estimation

In the following subsections, we will estimate
\Delta(q,\eta)
and
\mathcal{E}(q)
.
Let
q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)
be the classical path for an HO, starting and ending at points
q+\eta
and
q-\eta
, respectively, given by,

q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}e^{i\nu_{n}\tau}

(118)

Then,
a_{n}
is given as
weiss2012quantum
,

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\frac{i\nu_{n}}{\omega^{2}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}\right)2\eta+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}\frac{q}{m\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}}\right\}

(119)

We conjecture that for
\eta=0
,
|q_{\textbf{cl}}(\tau)-q|
will have its global maxima at
\tau=\beta/2
. This intuition rests on the fact that in the general path integral equation [
Eq. 3
], although the potential term will push the extremum classical path towards the potential minima, the kinetic and dissipative terms will help remove any oscillations from it. Hence, the curve will have a single extrema, and since for
\eta=0
, the classical path can be seen to be made up of only cosine Fourier components and hence is symmetric about
\tau=\beta/2
, therefore this extrema will be at
\tau=\beta/2
. We have also verified this numerically. When
\eta\neq 0
, we note that although the classical path still does not have any oscillations,
\tau=\beta/2
does not give the extremum point anymore, but we assume that the maximum among
|q_{\textbf{cl}}(\beta/2)-q|
and
|\eta|
still gives a rough estimate for the maximum deviation of the classical path from the constant path
q^{\prime}(\tau)=q
.

We will hence use the quantity
q_{\textbf{cl}}(\beta/2)-q
to estimate
\Delta(q,\eta)
. We simplify this expression as,

\displaystyle q_{\textbf{cl}}(\beta/2)-q

\displaystyle=-q+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}a_{n}

(120)

\displaystyle=q\left(\frac{1}{m\beta\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\nu_{n}^{2}+\omega^{2}+\zeta_{n}}-1\right)

(121)

Hence, the estimate for the maximum deviation of the classical path is then given by,

\displaystyle\Delta_{HO}(q,\eta)=\mathop{\mathrm{max}}\left\{\left|q\left(-1+\frac{1}{m\beta\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\nu_{n}^{2}+\omega^{2}+\zeta_{n}}\right)\right|,|\eta|\right\}

(122)

Relating
q\equiv V_{1}(q)/V_{2}(q)
[
Eq. 11
], we directly recover the expression for the maximum deviation of the classical path claimed earlier [
Eq. 50
].

Next, in order to estimate
\mathcal{E}(q)
, rename
r_{n}\equiv|b_{n}|
[
Eq. 74
] and write the path integral for
\mathcal{I}
[
Eq. 78
] in polar coordinate as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=J\left(\prod_{n=1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr_{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r_{n}d\theta_{n}\right)e^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{0}b_{0}^{2}+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{n}r_{n}^{2}\right)}

(123)

Note that
\theta_{n}
dependence in the integrand is in
b_{0}
and comes through the following constraint [
Eq. 76
],

\displaystyle b_{0}=-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Re(b_{n})

(124)

We are now in the position to immediately do all the
d\theta_{n}
integrals in the
Eq. 123
. For this, we note that for a fixed value of
b_{n}
’s, where
n=0,1,2,...
, the
d\theta_{n}
integrals in
Eq. 123
give us a measure of all the configurations that satisfy the constraint in
Eq. 124
.

Now consider a 2D random walk starting from origin, where the nth step size for the walk is given by the function
\mathcal{S}(n)=2|b_{n}|=2r_{n}
. Then after
N\to\infty
steps, the probability distribution
P(x)
for this walker to have the x-axis position value of
-b_{0}
gives a measure that is equivalent to doing all the
d\theta_{n}
integrals in
Eq. 123
. Note that there is a factor of 2 in the definition for
\mathcal{S}(n)
because of the corresponding factor of 2 in
Eq. 124
.

This probability distribution is given as
sherman1972summation
,

P(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

(125)

where, the variance
\sigma^{2}
is given by,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathcal{S}(n))^{2}

(126)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}^{2}

(127)

Note that the Gaussian probability distribution for the random walk is true only if a few sets of steps,
\mathcal{S}(n)

sherman1972summation

We hence replace all the
d_{\theta_{n}}

b_{0}
, and
Eq. 123

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=J\left(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}r_{n}dr_{n}\right)\int_{-R}^{R}db_{0}\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}e^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_{0}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)b_{0}^{2}+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{n}r_{n}^{2}\right)}

(128)

where

Eq. 124

Note that this equation gives a typical length-scale for the value for

and
b_{0}
as following,

\displaystyle r_{n}^{2}=|b_{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle\implies\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\approx 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}\qquad\text{from \autoref{eqn:sigma squared}}

(130)

\displaystyle\implies b_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}+\frac{1}{2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}}}

Using the typical value of
|b_{n}|
from here, we can go back in the position space representation of
\delta(\tau)
[

\displaystyle\delta(\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{0}+\frac{1}{2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}}}}+\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}e^{i\theta_{n}}e^{i\nu_{n}\tau}\right)

\theta_{-n}=-\theta_{n}

become truly random and this again reduces to the random walk problem with fixed step size
sherman1972summation

\phi_{n}
,

\displaystyle h(x)=c_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\sin{\left(2\pi nx+\phi_{n}\right)}

(133)

What is the probability density,
P(y)
, that
h(x)=y

Using similar random walk arguments as used before,

\displaystyle P(y)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}e^{\frac{-(y-c_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}^{2}}

An estimate for

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{HO}(q)=c_{0}+\sigma

For our case, from

, we have,

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle c_{n\geq 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}+\frac{1}{2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}}}

Hence, from
Eq. 135
,
Eq. 136
,
Eq. 137
and
Eq. 138
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{HO}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}}{\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}}}+\sqrt{\beta^{2}\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}}\right)\qquad\text{from \autoref{eqn:sum_as_q2}}

Similarly, from definition of
\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}
[
Eq. 39
], we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\beta}+2\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\lim_{\zeta_{n}\to\infty}\frac{\frac{V_{2}(q)}{m}+\zeta_{n}}{\frac{V_{2}(q)}{m}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}

(147)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\beta}+2\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}1

(148)

\displaystyle=\infty

(149)

Finally, from definition of
\mathcal{G}(q)
[
Eq. 46
], we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\mathcal{G}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_{2}(q)\beta\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}{\mathcal{A}_{n}(q)}

(150)

\displaystyle=\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_{2}(q)\beta\frac{1}{\beta V_{2}(q)}}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}1

(151)

\displaystyle=1

(152)

Hence expression for USC-LHA [
Eq. 37
] becomes,

\displaystyle\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\rho(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=\delta(\eta)Z^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{(V_{1}(q)/V_{2}(q))^{2}}{2\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}}-\beta\left(V(q)-\frac{V_{1}(q)^{2}}{2V_{2}(q)}\right)\right)

(153)

\displaystyle\implies\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\rho(q)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\exp\left(-\beta V(q)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\beta V_{1}(q)^{2}}{2V_{2}(q)}+\frac{\beta V_{1}(q)^{2}}{2V_{2}(q)}\right)

(154)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\exp\left(-\beta V(q)\right)

(155)

which is the CV version of the USC result
cresser2021weak
. To see this equivalence, note that the USC result is usually stated as,

\rho=Z_{USC}^{-1}\exp{\left(-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{s}P_{n}\right)}

(156)

where
P_{n}=\ket{x_{n}}\bra{x_{n}}
are projection operators on the non-degenerate eigenstates
\ket{x_{n}}
of the system coupling operators
X
and
Z_{USC}
is a normalization factor.

We note that in the CV limit,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{s}P_{n}

\displaystyle=\int dq\left(\frac{\bra{q}\hat{P}^{2}\ket{q}}{2m}+V(q)\right)\ket{q}\bra{q}

(157)

Now,
\sum_{q}\bra{q}\hat{P}^{2}\ket{q}\ket{q}\bra{q}
is proportional to the identity operator, and hence will commute with the potential operator. Therefore, in the expression for the USC-MFGS, the contribution of the momentum operator can be absorbed in the trace and we recover
Eq. 155
as the USC result.

Now, let us calculate
\Delta(q,\eta)
in this limit. Let us first evaluate
W(q)
[
Eq. 52
] that comes up in the definition of
\Delta(q,\eta)
as,

\displaystyle\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}W(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta V_{2}(q)}+\frac{1}{m\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\lim_{\zeta_{n}\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\frac{V_{2}(q)}{m}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}

(158)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta V_{2}(q)}

(159)

Hence, from the definition of
\Delta(q,\eta)
[
Eq. 50
], we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\Delta(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\left|\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\frac{V_{1}(q_{0})}{V_{2}(q_{0})}\left(1-\frac{W(q_{0})}{\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q_{0})}}\right)\right|,\left|\eta\right|\right\}

(160)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\left|\frac{V_{1}(q_{0})}{V_{2}(q_{0})}\left(1-\frac{\frac{1}{\beta V_{2}(q)}}{\frac{1}{\beta V_{2}(q)}}\right)\right|,\left|\eta\right|\right\}

(161)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,\left|\eta\right|\right\}

(162)

Similarly, let us calculate
\mathcal{E}(q)
in this limit. From the definition of
\mathcal{E}(q)
[
Eq. 51
], we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{J(\omega)\to\infty}\sqrt{\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}-\frac{1}{V_{2}(q)\beta}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{V_{2}(q)\beta\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}}}+1\right)

(164)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{V_{2}(q)\beta}-\frac{1}{V_{2}(q)\beta}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{V_{2}(q)\frac{1}{V_{2}(q)\beta}}}+1\right)

(165)

\displaystyle=0

(166)

Hence, we note that both
\Delta(q,\eta)
and
\mathcal{E}(q)
have expected behavior in the USC limit.

2
High temperature limit

To understand why LHA becomes accurate in the high temperature limit, consider an arbitrary path
q(\tau)
starting and ending at the points
q+\eta
and
q-\eta
, respectively. Now, for
a>1
, let us rescale
\beta\to\beta/a
such that we have
q(\tau)\to q_{a}(\tau)\equiv q(a\tau)
, where we have identified the paths
q(\tau)
and
q_{a}(\tau)
with each other through a one-to-one and onto map. Note that we have
q(0)=q+\eta=q_{a}(0)
and
q(\beta)=q-\eta=q_{a}(\beta/a)
. Now, define the new action,
S_{a}[q_{a}(\tau)]
as,

\displaystyle S_{a}[q_{a}(\tau)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau\left(\frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}_{a}(\tau)^{2}+V\left(q_{a}(\tau)\right)\right)+\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau^{\prime}K_{a}\left(\tau-\tau^{\prime}\right)\left(q_{a}(\tau)-q_{a}(\tau^{\prime})\right)^{2}

(167)

where,

K_{a}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\frac{\cosh{[\omega(\beta/2a-\tau)]}}{\sinh{[\omega\beta/2a]}}

(168)

Then, we have,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta/a}\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}q_{a}(\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=a^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta/a}\frac{d^{2}}{d(a\tau)^{2}}q(a\tau)d\tau

(169)

\displaystyle=a\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}q(\tau)d\tau

(170)

Similarly, we have,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta/a}V(q_{a}(\tau))d\tau=\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{\beta}V(q(\tau))d\tau

(171)

Before moving to the dissipative term, we write the hyperbolic factor coming in the correlation function [
Eq. 168
] in large
a
limit for
0\leq\tau\leq\beta/a
as,

\displaystyle\lim_{a>>1}\frac{\cosh{[\omega(\beta/2a-\tau)]}}{\sinh{[\omega\beta/2a]}}=\frac{2a}{\omega\beta}

(172)

For the dissipative term,
T_{D}
, in
Eq. 167
, we then have,

\displaystyle T_{D}

\displaystyle=\lim_{a>>1}\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau K_{a}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})(q_{a}(\tau)-q_{a}(\tau^{\prime}))^{2}

(173)

\displaystyle=\lim_{a>>1}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau\frac{\cosh{[\omega(\beta/2a-(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))]}}{\sinh{[\omega\beta/2a]}}(q(a\tau)-q(a\tau^{\prime}))^{2}

(174)

\displaystyle\approx\lim_{a>>1}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta/a}d\tau\frac{2a}{\omega\beta}(q(a\tau)-q(a\tau^{\prime}))^{2}

(175)

\displaystyle\approx\lim_{a>>1}\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\frac{2}{\omega\beta}(q(\tau)-q(\tau^{\prime}))^{2}

(176)

Hence, for large
a
, while the potential and dissipative terms scale as
1/a
, the kinetic term scales as
a
, dominating in high temperature limit and causing the paths with large variation to contribute less.

Analogous to the USC limit [Appendix.
1
], the high temperature limit gives us the following values for the relevant quantities,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta V_{2}(q)}

(177)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\beta}

(178)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\mathcal{G}(q)

\displaystyle=1

(179)

Hence the high temperature limit for the expression of LHA [
Eq. 37
] is given as,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\rho(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to 0}Z^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta V_{2}(q)}{2}\left(\frac{V_{1}(q)}{V_{2}(q)}\right)^{2}-\frac{m}{2\beta}\eta^{2}-\beta\left(V(q)-\frac{V_{1}(q)^{2}}{2V_{2}(q)}\right)\right)

(180)

\displaystyle\implies\lim_{\beta\to 0}\rho(q)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\exp\left(-\beta V(q)\right)

(181)

We will now show that this high temperature MFGS is equivalent to the ones recently derived in literature
cresser2021weak
;
gelzinis2020analytical
;
timofeev2022hamiltonian
.
Timofeev et al.
timofeev2022hamiltonian
have shown that in the high temperature limit, at arbitrary coupling, the general Hamiltonian of mean force,
H_{MF}
, is given as,

\displaystyle H_{MF}=\sum_{n}\langle n|H_{S}|n\rangle+\sum_{n\neq m}\bra{n}H_{S}\ket{m}\exp\{-\beta\Lambda(q_{n}-q_{m})^{2}/6\}\ket{n}\bra{m}

(182)

where
\Lambda\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)/\omega
and
q_{n}
are the eigenvalues of the system coupling operator
\hat{q}
. Note that
Eq. 182
differs from the one obtained by the Timofeev et al. by a term of
-\Lambda\hat{q}^{2}
because their definition of the free Hamiltonian includes the counterterm. For a CV system,
Eq. 182
translates into,

\displaystyle H_{MF}=\left(\int dxV(x)\ket{x}\bra{x}\right)+\left(\int\int dxdy\frac{\bra{x}\hat{P}^{2}\ket{y}}{2m}\exp\left\{-\beta\Lambda\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{6}\right\}\ket{y}\bra{x}\right)

(183)

Let us first set
\Lambda=0
for simplicity. Then, we have,

\displaystyle\implies\exp\{-\beta\hat{H}_{MF}\}

\displaystyle\approx\exp\{-\beta V(x)\}\times\exp\left\{-\beta\frac{\hat{P}^{2}}{2m}\right\}+O(\beta^{2})

(184)

Let us focus on the second term,

\displaystyle\bra{x}\exp(-\beta\hat{P}^{2}/2m)\ket{y}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\beta}}\exp\left\{-\frac{m}{2\beta}(x-y)^{2}\right\}

(185)

\displaystyle\approx\delta(x-y)\qquad\text{in high temperature limit}

(186)

We hence recover the high temperature MFGS result [
Eq. 181
]. Note that when
\Lambda\neq 0
, then the off-diagonal elements of
H_{MF}
[
Eq. 183
] get further suppressed and it can be formally shown that the exponential of the second term in
Eq. 183
gets closer to
\delta(x-y)
as compared with the
\Lambda=0
case, and hence
Eq. 181
remains the high temperature MFGS.

We will now calculate
\Delta(q,\eta)
[
Eq. 50
] and
\mathcal{E}(q)
[
Eq. 51
] in the high temperature limit. For
\Delta(q,\eta)
, we first evaluate,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{V_{1}(q)}{V_{2}(q)}\left(1-\frac{W(q)}{\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{V_{1}(q)}{V_{2}(q)}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\frac{V_{2}(q)}{M}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}}{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\frac{V_{2}(q)}{M}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}}\right)

(187)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{V_{1}(q)}{V_{2}(q)}\left(1-\frac{\frac{M}{V_{2}(q)}}{\frac{M}{V_{2}(q)}}\right)

(188)

\displaystyle=0

(189)

We then have,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\Delta(q,\eta)=\left|\eta\right|

(190)

Similarly, for
\mathcal{E}(q)
, we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to 0}\sqrt{\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}-\frac{1}{m\omega^{2}\beta}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{m\omega^{2}\beta\braket{\hat{q}^{2}}}}+1\right)

(191)

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\beta\to 0}\sqrt{\frac{2}{m\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}}

(192)

\displaystyle=0

(193)

Since these length scales become smaller as the temperature is increased, we infer that LHA becomes more accurate in the process.

3
High bath cutoff limit

Let
\Omega
denote the bath cutoff frequency. Then in the limit
\Omega\to\infty
, we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Omega\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}d\omega=\infty

(194)

From the definition of
\zeta_{n}
[
Eq. 42
], we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\zeta_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}}d\omega^{\prime}

(195)

\displaystyle=\infty

(196)

\displaystyle\implies\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\omega^{2}+\zeta_{n}}{\omega^{2}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}

\displaystyle=1

(197)

\displaystyle\implies\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega^{2}+\zeta_{n}}{\omega^{2}+\nu_{n}^{2}+\zeta_{n}}

\displaystyle=\infty

(198)

\displaystyle\implies\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}

\displaystyle=\infty

(199)

Here we have used the definition of
\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}
[
Eq. 39
]. Since
\braket{\hat{P}^{2}(q)}
diverges, in this limit, LHA [
Eq. 37
] will not have coherence in the position basis, and therefore will be given by,

\rho(q,\eta)=\delta(\eta)Z^{-1}\mathcal{G}(q)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\braket{\hat{X}^{2}(q)}}\left(\frac{V_{1}(q)}{V_{2}(q)}\right)^{2}-\beta\left(V{(q)}-\frac{V_{1}{(q)}^{2}}{2V_{2}{(q)}}\right)\right)

(200)
