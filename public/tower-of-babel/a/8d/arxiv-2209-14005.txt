A cone-theoretic barycenter existence theorem
Jean Goubault-Larrecq ORCID:0000-0001-5879-3304, Xiaodong Jia ORCID:0000-0001-9310-6143

Abstract
. We show that every continuous valuation on a locally convex, locally convex-compact, sober topological cone ℭ \mathfrak{C} has a barycenter. This barycenter is unique, and the barycenter map β \beta is continuous, hence is the structure map of a 𝐕 w \mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}} -algebra, i.e., an Eilenberg-Moore algebra of the extended valuation monad on the category of T 0 T_{0} topological spaces; it is, in fact, the unique 𝐕 w \mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}} -algebra that induces the cone structure on ℭ \mathfrak{C} .

1.
Introduction

It is well-known that every probability measure
\mu
defined on a
compact convex subset of a Hausdorff topological vector space has a
unique barycenter
\beta(\mu)
, and that the barycenter map
\beta

is continuous
[
Cho69
, Chapter 6, 26.3]
. Our purpose
is to establish a similar theorem in the setting of topological cones,
in the sense of Reinhold Heckmann
[
Hec96
]
and Klaus Keimel

[
Kei08b
]
; those are definitely not limited to cones
that one can embed in vector spaces.

Applications.

Such a barycenter existence theorem is strongly linked to the question
of the elucidation of the algebras of the monad
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
of
continuous valuations on the category
\mathbf{Top}_{0}
of
T_{0}
topological
spaces, as well as of the monads of probability and subprobability
valuations. (See
[
CEK06
,
GJ19
]
. We will introduce

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
below.) As a result, we will be able to show that a rich
collection of topological cones give rise to such algebras.

Investigating the structure of algebras of monads is not just an
interesting, and important, mathematical question, but also has
application in semantics. For example, knowing that the category of
algebras of the subprobability valuation monad on the category of
continuous dcpos is isomorphic to the category of so-called continuous
Kegelspitzen is crucial to give an adequate semantics to the
variational quantum programming language of
[
JKL+22
]
,
where barycenter maps are used to link the category of some special
Von-Neumann algebras to the Kleisli category of the so-called minimal
valuations monad.

Related work.

Ben Cohen, Martín Escardó and Klaus Keimel

[
CEK06
]
first asked the question of characterizing the
algebras of
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
on
\mathbf{Top}_{0}
, and then on the subcategory

\mathbf{SComp}
of stably compact spaces and continuous maps. They also
observed how the question was deeply linked to a notion of barycenter
adapted from Choquet’s work. There seems to be a gap in their
Proposition 2, however (see Proposition
1
below), and

[
GJ19
]
is probably now a better reference. Both papers only
give
examples
of spaces where such barycenters exist, as well
as of algebras of
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
, but no general existence theorem.

Keimel also fully characterized the algebras of the monad of
probability measures over compact ordered spaces

[
Kei08a
]
. This can be seen as a close result, because
of the tight relationship between compact ordered spaces and stably
compact spaces. But the category of compact ordered spaces and
continuous maps is equivalent to the category of stably compact spaces
and
perfect
maps, not continuous maps. Keimel’s result itself
extends Świrszcz’s characterization of the algebras of the monad of
probability measures on compact Hausdorff spaces

[
Świ74
]
.

Returning to categories of spaces that are not necessarily Hausdorff,
with continuous, not necessarily perfect maps, we do have some results
for certain submonads of
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
: this includes the monads of
so-called simple valuations and of point-continuous valuations

[
GJ19
,
Hec96
]
, for which we have general barycenter
existence theorems, and complete characterizations of the
corresponding algebras. Since point-continuous valuations and
continuous valuations coincide on continuous dcpos, those results
encompass the case of continuous Kegelspitzen mentioned earlier.

Our purpose here is to give a barycenter existence theorem that would
need assumptions that are as weak as possible. We initially looked
for variants of arguments due to Choquet
[
Cho60
]
, Edwards

[
Edw63
]
, and Roberts
[
Rob78
]
, among
others. These arguments work as follows. Define a partial ordering
between probability distributions by
\mu_{1}\preceq\mu_{2}
if and only
if
\int h\;d\mu_{1}\geq\int h\;d\mu_{2}
for every convex map
h
in some
class (typically, bounded, continuous maps); this formalizes the idea
that
\mu_{2}
has a more concentrated distribution of mass than

\mu_{1}
, while keeping the same center of mass. Then, build a
continuous map
F
such that
\mu\preceq F(\mu)
for all probability
distributions
\mu
, and such that
F(\mu)=\mu
if and only if
\mu

is a Dirac measure
\delta_{x}
. Finally, using a compactness argument,
extract a convergent subsequence from the sequence

{(F^{n}(\mu))}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
: this must converge to some Dirac
measure
\delta_{x}
, and by construction
x
will be a barycenter of

\mu
. Details may vary. While there are immediate obstructions to
generalizing that form of argument to a non-Hausdorff setting (e.g.,
limits are not unique), it is possible to do so, but the result we
obtained by following this path was disappointing: a long, complicated
proof, which required very strong assumptions in the end.

In comparison, we will use a much simpler strategy: we reduce the
question to the already known construction of barycenters of
point-continuous valuations, by embedding our cone into a larger one,
the upper powercone introduced by Keimel

[
Kei08b
, Section 11]
. We only need two additional
lemmata: one to actually show that there is such a cone embedding,
Lemma
2
, and one to transfer any barycenter
in the larger cone back to the smaller cone, Lemma
4
.

2.
Cones, continuous valuations, barycenters

We refer the reader to
[
GHK+03
,
Gou13
]
for more
information on domain theory and topology, especially non-Hausdorff
topology.

A
dcpo
(short for directed-complete partial order) is a poset

(X,\leq)
such that every directed family
D
has a supremum

\sup D
; we write
\sup\nolimits^{\scriptstyle\uparrow}D
to stress the fact that
D
is directed.

\textcolor
black A subset
A
of
D
is
upwards-closed
if and only if

x\in A
and
x\leq y
imply
y\in A
. We will also write

\mathop{\uparrow}A
for the upward closure

\{y\in X\mid x\leq y\text{ for some }x\in A\}
, and
\mathop{\uparrow}x

instead of
\mathop{\uparrow}\{x\}
,
\textcolor
blackand similarly for the downward closure

\mathop{\downarrow}A
, and
\mathop{\downarrow}x
. The
Scott topology
on
(X,\leq)

consists of Scott-open subsets of
X
, which are defined to be
upwards-closed and inaccessible by directed suprema. That is, an
upwards-closed subset
U
is
Scott-open
if and only if every
directed family
D
intersects
U
if
\sup\nolimits^{\scriptstyle\uparrow}D\in U
. A
Scott-continuous map between dcpos is a monotonic map that preserves
directed suprema; equivalently, a continuous map, provided the given
dcpos are given the Scott topology.

We write
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for the set of non-negative real numbers, and
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

for
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
, where
\infty
is larger than any element
of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
\textcolor
black
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a dcpo in the natural ordering
\leq
on real numbers,

\mathbb{R}_{+}
is not. We equip the dcpo
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with the Scott topology; its
non-empty open subsets are the half-open intervals
]a,\infty]
,

a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. (And we give
\mathbb{R}_{+}
the subspace topology.) With that
topology, the continuous maps from a topological space to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
are
usually called the
lower semicontinuous maps
.

The way-below relation
\ll
on a dcpo
X
is defined by
x\ll y
if
and only if for every directed family
D
such that
y\leq\sup\nolimits^{\scriptstyle\uparrow}D
,
there is a
z\in D
such that
x\leq z
. We write
\hbox to0.0pt{$\downarrow$\hss}\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\downarrow$}y
for

\{x\in X\mid x\ll y\}
. A
continuous dcpo
is a dcpo in
which
\hbox to0.0pt{$\downarrow$\hss}\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\downarrow$}y
is directed and
\sup\nolimits^{\scriptstyle\uparrow}\hbox to0.0pt{$\downarrow$\hss}\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\downarrow$}y=y
for every
point
y
.

\textcolor

blackThe
specialization preordering

\leq
of a topological
space
X
is defined by
x\leq y
if and only if every open
neighborhood of
x
contains
y
. The space
X
is
T_{0}
if and
only if
\leq
is antisymmetric. The
saturation
of a subset

A
of
X
is the intersection of its open neighborhoods, and
coincides with
\mathop{\uparrow}A
. The closure of a singleton
\{x\}
is
equal to
\mathop{\downarrow}x
, where
\mathop{\downarrow}
is relative to the specialization
preordering. Any continuous map between topological spaces is
monotonic with respect to the underlying specialization preorderings.

\textcolor

blackA subset
K
of a topological space
X
is
compact
if and
only if every open cover of
K
contains a finite subcover; no
separation axiom is intended here. If
K
is compact, then
\mathop{\uparrow}K

is both compact and saturated. A topological space is
locally
compact
if every point in it has a neighborhood base of compact
subsets, namely if for every point
x
and every open neighborhood

U
of
x
, there is a compact set
Q
such that

x\in int({Q})\subseteq Q\subseteq U
. (We write

int({K})
for the
interior
of
K
.) We may equivalently
require
Q
to be compact
saturated
, by replacing
Q
by

\mathop{\uparrow}Q
. A space is
sober
if every nonempty irreducible
closed subset in it is equal to
\mathop{\downarrow}x
for some unique point
x
.
Every continuous dcpo is locally compact and sober in its Scott
topology
\textcolor
black
[
GHK+03
, Corollary II-1.13]
.

Following Heckmann
[
Hec96
]
and Keimel

[
Kei08b
]
, a
cone

\mathfrak{C}
is an additive commutative
monoid with an action
a,x\mapsto a\cdot x
of the semi-ring
\mathbb{R}_{+}

on
\mathfrak{C}
(
scalar multiplication
). The latter means that the
following laws are satisfied:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}0\cdot x=0&(ab)\cdot x=a\cdot(b\cdot x)&1\cdot x=x\\
a\cdot 0=0&a\cdot(x+y)=a\cdot x+a\cdot y&(a+b)\cdot x=a\cdot x+b\cdot x.\end{array}

We will often write
ax
instead of
a\cdot x
. In other words, a
cone satisfies the same axioms as a vector space, barring those
mentioning subtraction.
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a cone, and embeds (
\textcolor
blackas a
cone) into the real vector space
\mathbb{R}
.
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a cone, but
embeds in no real vector space, because it fails the cancellation law

x+y=x+z\mathrel{\Rightarrow}y=z
. None of the other cones we will consider embed
in any real vector space, for the same reason.

\textcolor

blackIn this paper, a
topological cone
is a cone with a
T_{0}
topology
that makes both addition and scalar multiplication jointly continuous—where we
recall that the topology on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is induced by the Scott topology on

\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, which is not Hausdorff. A
semitopological cone
is
defined similarly, except that addition is only required to be
separately continuous.
\textcolor
blackSince continuous maps are monotonic with
respect to the specialization preordering
\leq
, in a
semitopological cone,
ax\leq bx
whenever
a\leq b
; in particular,

0
is the least element. Hence a non-trivial semitopological cone

\textcolor
black
\mathfrak{C}
is never
T_{1}
, but is always compact.
\textcolor
blackCompactness
comes from the fact that every open cover
{(U_{i})}_{i\in I}
will
be such that
0\in U_{i}
for some
i\in I
, and then
U_{i}=\mathfrak{C}
,
since
U_{i}
is upwards-closed and
0
is the least element of
\mathfrak{C}
.

A
d-cone
is a cone with a partial ordering that turns it into a
dcpo, and such that addition and scalar multiplication are both
Scott-continuous.
\textcolor
blackA continuous d-cone is a d-cone that is also
a continuous dcpo. Every continuous d-cone is a topological cone
in the Scott topology. For example,
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a continuous d-cone.

A subset
A
of a cone
\mathfrak{C}
is
convex
if and only if

ax+(1-a)y\in A
for all
x,y\in A
and
a\in[0,1]
. A
semitopological cone is
weakly locally convex
, resp.
locally convex
, resp.
locally convex-compact
, if and
only if every point has a base of convex, resp. convex open, resp. convex compact, neighborhoods. Every continuous d-cone is not only
topological, but also locally convex and locally convex-compact

[
Kei08b
, Lemma 6.12]
.

{exa}

Here are a few examples to bear in mind. We will give a few
others below. The point of
(2)
and

(3)
is twofold: to show that cones can be
very different from real vector spaces; and to show that there are
sober, locally convex, locally convex-compact topological cones
that are not continuous d-cones.

(1)

For every space
X
, let us consider the dcpo
{\mathcal{L}}X
of
all lower semicontinuous maps from
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, namely,
those continuous maps from
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
equipped with the
Scott topology. With the obvious addition and scalar
multiplication, and the Scott topology of the pointwise
ordering,
{\mathcal{L}}X
is a semitopological cone.

If
X
is core-compact, namely, if its lattice of open sets is a
continuous dcpo (every locally compact space is core-compact,
and the two notions are equivalent for sober spaces), then

{\mathcal{L}}X
is a continuous d-cone
[
GJ19
, Example 3.5]
.
In particular, it is topological, sober, locally convex, and
locally convex-compact.

(2)

This one is due to Heckmann

[
Hec96
, Section 6.1]
, see also

[
GJ19
, Example 3.6]
. Every sup-semilattice
L
with a
least element gives rise to a semitopological cone in its Scott
topology by defining
x+y
as
x\vee y
,
0
as the least
element
\bot
, and
a\cdot x
as
\bot
if
a=0
, and as
x

otherwise; it is a continuous d-cone (hence topological, locally
convex, and locally convex-compact) if
L
is a continuous
complete lattice.

For general
L
, it remains that every upwards-closed subset is
convex. Hence each such sup-semilattice
L
is a locally convex
semitopological cone in its Scott topology. It is locally
convex-compact if
L
is also locally compact in the Scott
topology. In that case,
L
is also a topological cone. This is
because on a core-compact poset
L
, the Scott topology on

L\times L
is the same as the product topology of the Scott
topology on
L

[
GHK+03
, Theorem II-4.13]
.

In particular, consider any locally compact sup-semilattice

L
with bottom that is not a continuous dcpo. Then
L
is
topological, locally convex, and locally convex-compact, but
not a continuous d-cone. The simplest example is given by
putting two copies of
\mathbb{N}
side by side, with extra
bottom and top elements, as shown on the right. Its nonempty
proper Scott open subsets are those of the form

\mathop{\uparrow}\{m,n^{\prime}\}
where
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and they are all
compact, so
L
is locally compact. One can even check that
it is a quasi-continuous dcpo

[
GHK+03
, Definition III-3.2]
, which not only
implies that it is locally compact, but also sober

[
GHK+03
, Proposition III-3.7]
. However,
L
is
not a continuous dcpo, since the way-below relation is
characterized by
x\ll y
if and only if
x=\bot
.

(3)

Here is a variant on the
previous example. We consider a complete lattice
L
with its

upper topology
, which is the coarsest topology that makes
the sets
\mathop{\downarrow}x
closed. The specialization ordering is the
ordering
\leq
of
L
. By a result of Schalk

[
Sch93
, Proposition 1.7]
,
L
is sober. We equip
L

with the same cone structure as in
(2)
. Then

L
is topological, since

{(\_+\_)}^{-1}(\mathop{\downarrow}x)=\mathop{\downarrow}x\times\mathop{\downarrow}x
is closed in the
product topology for every
x\in L
, and similarly for

{(\_\cdot\_)}^{-1}(\mathop{\downarrow}x)=(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathop{\downarrow}x)\cup(\{0\}\times L)
. As in
(2)
, every upwards-closed
subset of

is convex, so
L
is locally convex, and
L
is
locally convex-compact if and only if it is locally compact.

In particular, the lattice
\Gamma(X)
of closed subsets of any
topological space
X
, with the upper topology of inclusion
(also known as the
lower Vietoris topology
), union as
addition, and empty set as zero, is a sober, locally convex
topological cone. It is locally compact, hence locally
convex-compact, if
X
is itself locally compact

[
Sch93
, Proposition 6.11]
. But, say,

\Gamma(\mathbb{R})
, where
\mathbb{R}
is taken with its usual metric
topology (a locally compact space), is not a continuous dcpo (or
a quasi-continuous dcpo) with its Scott topology; the point is
that the upper topology differs from the Scott topology: by a
result of Chen, Kou and Lyu

[
CKL22
, Proposition 3.15]
, the only
T_{1}
spaces

X
such that the upper and Scott topologies coincide on

\Gamma(X)
are discrete.

We write
\mathfrak{C}^{*}
for the cone of linear lower semicontinuous maps

\Lambda\colon\mathfrak{C}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, where linear means preserving sums and
scalar products. Every locally convex
T_{0}
semitopological cone is

linearly separated
, in the sense that for every pair of points

x
,
y
such that
x\not\geq y
, there is a map
\Lambda\in\mathfrak{C}^{*}

such that
\Lambda(x)<\Lambda(y)

[
Kei08b
, Corollary 9.3]
. By multiplying by an
appropriate constant, we may require
\Lambda(x)\leq 1
and

\Lambda(y)>1
.

A
continuous valuation

\nu
on a space
X
is a map from the
lattice of open subsets of
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\nu(\emptyset)=0
,

\nu(U\cup V)+\nu(U\cap V)=\nu(U)+\nu(V)
for all open
sets
U
and
V
, and such that
\nu
is Scott-continuous

[
Jon90
]
. This is a close cousin of the notion of measure:
every continuous valuation extends to a
\tau
-smooth measure
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
X
provided that
X
is LCS-complete

[
dBGJL19
, Theorem 1]
, and that includes the case of all
locally compact sober spaces. Additionally, there is a notion of
integral of maps

with respect to continuous
valuations
\nu
on
X
, with the usual properties. We will use the
following four:

•

\int_{x\in X}h(x)\;d\delta_{x_{0}}=h(x_{0})
, where

\delta_{x_{0}}
is the
Dirac valuation
at
x_{0}
, defined by

\delta_{x_{0}}(U)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}1
if
x_{0}\in U
,
0
otherwise;

\textcolor
black

•

\int_{x\in X}\chi_{U}(x)\;d\nu=\nu(U)
, where
\chi_{U}

is the characteristic function of an open set
U
, defined as

\chi_{U}(x)=1
if
x\in U
,
0
otherwise;

•

the
change of variable
formula: for every continuous map

f\colon X\to Y
,

\int_{y\in Y}h(y)\;df[\nu]=\int_{x\in X}h(f(x))\;d\nu
; here

f[\nu]
is the
image valuation
defined by

f[\nu](V)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\nu(f^{-1}(V))
;

•

integration is linear in the integrated function
h
.

We write
\mathbf{V}X
for the set of all continuous valuations on
X
.
The
weak topology
on
\mathbf{V}X
is the coarsest one that makes

\nu\mapsto\nu(U)
continuous for every open set
U
. In other
words, a subbase of the weak topology is given by the sets

[U>r]\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\{\nu\in\mathbf{V}X\mid\nu(U)>r\}
, where

ranges
over the open subsets of

. We write
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X

for
\mathbf{V}X

: for all

and
\nu
,
\mu\leq\nu
if and only if

\mu(U)\leq\nu(U)

.
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}

extends to an endofunctor on the category
\mathbf{Top}_{0}
of
T_{0}

topological spaces, and its action on morphisms

is
given by
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}f(\nu)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}f[\nu]
.

3.
The barycenter existence theorem

We start with a topological cone
\mathfrak{C}
. We use a construction due to
Keimel
[
Kei08b
, Section 11]
: the
upper powercone

{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
of
\mathfrak{C}
consists of all non-empty convex, compact saturated
subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
. (A
saturated
set is one that is the
intersection of its open neighborhoods, or equivalently an
upwards-closed set, with respect to the specialization preordering.)
Addition is defined by

Q_{1}\underline{+}Q_{2}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\mathop{\uparrow}\{x_{1}+x_{2}\mid x_{1}\in Q_{1},x_{2}\in Q_{2}\}
, scalar multiplication by

a\underline{\cdot}Q\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\mathop{\uparrow}\{a\cdot x\mid x\in Q\}
, and the
zero is
\underline{0}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\mathfrak{C}
. The cone
{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
is given the

upper Vietoris topology
, which is generated by the basic open
sets
\Box U\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}\mid Q\subseteq U\}
, where
U

ranges over the open subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
. Its specialization ordering is

reverse
inclusion
\supseteq
, and every open set is Scott-open
(with respect to
\supseteq
) when
\mathfrak{C}
is sober. If additionally

\mathfrak{C}
is locally convex and locally convex-compact, then
{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
is a
continuous d-cone, and the Scott and upper Vietoris topologies
coincide
[
Kei08b
, Theorem 12.6]
.

The construction
{\mathcal{S}}
is the functor part of a monad on the category
of
sober topological cones and linear continuous maps as homomorphisms.
This is a consequence of the results in Sections 11 and 12
of
[
Kei08b
]
. We will use its unit, which we write as

\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}\colon\mathfrak{C}\to{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}

\mathop{\uparrow}x

For every
\Lambda\in\mathfrak{C}^{*}
, for every

,
we let
\overline{\Lambda}(Q)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\min_{x\in Q}\Lambda(x)

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a topological cone. For every
\Lambda\in\mathfrak{C}^{*}

is an element of
({\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C})^{*}

\overline{\Lambda}\circ\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}=\Lambda

Proof 3.1
.

\overline{\Lambda}
is continuous because

{\overline{\Lambda}}^{-1}(]r,\infty])=\Box{\Lambda^{-1}(]r,\infty])}
for every

. For every

, for
every
Q\in{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
,

\overline{\Lambda}(a\underline{\cdot}Q)=\min_{x\in Q}\Lambda(a\cdot x)

\textcolor

\Lambda
is continuous hence monotonic, and taking the minimum of a monotonic map over the
upward-closure of a set is the same as taking the minimum on that

\overline{\Lambda}(a\underline{\cdot}Q)=a\cdot\min_{x\in Q}\Lambda(x)=a\overline{\Lambda}(Q)
. Similarly, for all

\overline{\Lambda}(Q_{1}\underline{+}Q_{2})=\min_{x_{1}\in Q_{1},x_{2}\in Q_{2}}\Lambda(x_{1}+x_{2})=\overline{\Lambda}(Q_{1})+\overline{\Lambda}(Q_{2})

\overline{\Lambda}\circ\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}
maps every

to
\min_{y\in\mathop{\uparrow}x}\Lambda(y)=\Lambda(x)
,
because
\Lambda
is monotonic.

There is a subspace

of
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X

consisting of so-called

\textcolor

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a linearly separated
T_{0}
semitopological cone and

non-empty compact saturated subset of
\mathfrak{C}
. If the map

\varphi\colon\Lambda\mapsto\min_{x\in Q}\Lambda(x)
is

to

for some

.

Proof 3.2
.

Let us assume that
Q
cannot be written as
\mathop{\uparrow}x
for any

, or equivalently, that
\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
is not reduced to just
one point. (Also,
\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
is non-empty, since
Q
is non-empty.)
For every
x\in\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
,
\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
contains a distinct, hence
incomparable, point
\overline{x}
.

Since
\mathfrak{C}
is linearly separated, we can find a
\Lambda_{x}\in\mathfrak{C}^{*}

such that
\Lambda_{x}(x)>1
and
\Lambda_{x}(\overline{x})\leq 1
,
for each
x\in\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
. Let

H_{x}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\Lambda_{x}^{-1}(]1,\infty])
: when
x
varies over

\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
, those open sets cover
\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
, hence
\textcolor

upward closure, which is
Q
. Since
Q
is compact, there is a
finite subset
E
of
\mathop{\text{Min}}Q
such that

Q\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in E}H_{x}
.

Let
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\sum_{x\in E}\Lambda_{x}
, another element of

\mathfrak{C}^{*}
. Since
\varphi
is linear by assumption,

\varphi(\Lambda)=\sum_{x\in E}\varphi(\Lambda_{x})
.

Relying on the compactness of

,

\varphi(\Lambda)=\min_{z\in Q}\Lambda(z)
is equal to

\Lambda(x_{0})
for some

. Since

, there is an

, namely such that
\Lambda_{x}(x_{0})>1

\textcolor

\Lambda_{x}(\overline{x})\leq 1
, and

\varphi(\Lambda_{x})=\min_{z\in Q}\Lambda_{x}(z)\leq 1<\Lambda_{x}(x_{0})

\varphi(\Lambda_{y})=\min_{z\in Q}\Lambda_{y}(z)\leq\Lambda_{y}(x_{0})

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a locally convex, locally convex-compact, sober
topological cone
\mathfrak{C}
. Every continuous valuation
\nu
on
\mathfrak{C}
has
a unique barycenter
\beta(\nu)
, and the barycenter map
\beta
is
continuous; hence
\beta
is the structure map of a

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebra on
\mathfrak{C}
, and the unique one that induces the
given cone structure on
\mathfrak{C}
.

Proof 3.3
.

Under the given assumptions on
\mathfrak{C}
,
{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
is a continuous d-cone,
so Proposition
3
applies: there is a unique

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebra structure

\alpha\colon\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}{({\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C})}\to{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
that induces the
given cone structure on
{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
.

Let us consider any continuous valuation
\nu
on
\mathfrak{C}
. Since the
unit
\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}
of the
{\mathcal{S}}
monad is, in particular,
continuous, we can form the image valuation
\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}[\nu]
on

{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}
. We let
Q\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\alpha(\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}[\nu])
.

For every
\Lambda\in\mathfrak{C}^{*}
, by Lemma
2
,

\overline{\Lambda}
is in
({\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C})^{*}
. Since
\alpha
is a
barycenter map, we must have

\overline{\Lambda}(Q)=\int_{Q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{C}}\overline{\Lambda}(Q^{\prime})\;d\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}[\nu]
. By the change of variable formula, the
latter is equal to

\int_{x\in\mathfrak{C}}\overline{\Lambda}(\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}(x))\;d\nu
\textcolor
black, which is equal to

\int_{x\in\mathfrak{C}}\Lambda(x)\;d\nu

\textcolor
blackby
Lemma
2
. Also,

\overline{\Lambda}(Q)=\min_{x\in Q}\Lambda(x)=\varphi(\Lambda)
, taking the notation
\varphi
from
Lemma
4
. Since
\Lambda
is arbitrary in
\mathfrak{C}^{*}
, this
shows that
\varphi
is equal to the map

\Lambda\in\mathfrak{C}^{*}\mapsto\int_{x\in\mathfrak{C}}\Lambda(x)\;d\nu
,
which is linear. Hence, by Lemma
4
(which applies
since
\mathfrak{C}
is locally convex
T_{0}
hence linearly separated; it is

T_{0}
since sober),
Q=\mathop{\uparrow}x_{\nu}
for some
x_{\nu}\in\mathfrak{C}
.

For every
\Lambda\in\mathfrak{C}^{*}
, the equation

\overline{\Lambda}(Q)=\int_{x\in\mathfrak{C}}\Lambda(x)\;d\nu
then
simplifies to

\Lambda(x_{\nu})=\int_{x\in\mathfrak{C}}\Lambda(x)\;d\nu
, showing
that

is a barycenter of
\nu
. Since
\mathfrak{C}
is linearly
separated and
T_{0}
, it is unique
[
CEK06
,
GJ19
]
.

Let us define
\beta
by
\beta(\nu)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}x_{\nu}
, for every

\nu\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}\mathfrak{C}
. We claim that
\beta
is continuous. For
every open subset

of
\mathfrak{C}
, for every
\nu\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}\mathfrak{C}
,

\alpha(\eta^{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mathfrak{C}}[\nu])\in\Box U

\textcolor

and since

is a functor.

We conclude by Proposition
1

. We will see in Example
4

that not all continuous valuations
\nu

\mathfrak{C}^{*}
have
barycenters, hence not all
\mathfrak{C}^{*}

(5)

For every topological space
X
,

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
is topological, sober, and locally convex, see
Example
2

. Hence
Theorem
5
also gives us a
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebra
structure and barycenters if
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
is locally
convex-compact. We will see below that this holds true when
X

is stably locally compact, notably
(Proposition
6

; examining this gap is
the purpose of Section

.

4.
Are the assumptions necessary?

One would like
\textcolor
blackto know whether the assumptions of
Theorem
5
are needed. If a semitopological cone
\mathfrak{C}

has a
\mathbf{V}_{w}
-algebra structure that induces the given cone
structure, then
\mathfrak{C}
has to be sober, topological, and weakly
locally convex
[
GJ19
, Proposition 4.9]
. We do not know
whether it has to be locally convex.
But does it have to be locally convex-compact?
Example
3

(5)

suggests that the answer is no. However, we will give an example
illustrating that Theorem
5
may fail if
\mathfrak{C}
is not
locally convex-compact.

\textcolor

blackBut first let us look at the special case
of
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebras in the category of stably compact spaces, as
Cohen, Escardó and Keimel did
[
CEK06
]
. In that setting,
we will show that it is indeed required that

\mathfrak{C}
be locally convex-compact. We write
\mathbf{SComp}
for the category
of stably compact spaces and continuous maps. A space is
stably
locally compact
if and only if it is sober, locally compact, and
the intersection of any two compact saturated subsets is compact; it
is
stably compact
if and only if it is also compact.
\textcolor
blackOn
a stably compact space
Y
, the complements of compact saturated
subsets form a topology called the
cocompact topology
. The
set
Y
with the cocompact topology is then also stably compact

[
Gou13
, Section 9.1.2]
.

\textcolor

blackIn the proof of the following, we will use some results due to
Gordon Plotkin
[
Plo06
]
. Plotkin uses the
weak
∗
-Scott topology on
\mathbf{V}X
, equating it with
\mathfrak{C}^{*}
where

\mathfrak{C}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}{\mathcal{L}}X
; that is the same thing as the weak topology on

\mathbf{V}X
, by
[
AMJK04
, Proposition 34]
. Let us write

[Q\geq r]
for the collection of continuous valuations
\nu
such
that
\nu(U)\geq r
for every open neighborhood
U
of
Q
. Then
Corollary 1 of
[
Plo06
]
, together with subsequent
comments, states that, for every stably locally compact space
X
,

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
is stably compact, and a subbase of closed subsets of
the cocompact topology is given by the sets
[Q\geq r]
, where
Q

ranges over the compact saturated subsets of
X
and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
In particular, all such sets
[Q\geq r]
are compact in

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
. They are clearly saturated.

A
linear retract
of a semitopological cone is a retract whose
retraction (not the section) is linear

[
Hec96
, Proposition 6.6]
.

Proposition 6
.

For every stably locally compact space
X
,
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
is a
locally convex-compact topological cone. Each of those properties
is preserved by linear retracts. Hence, for every

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebra
\alpha\colon\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X\to X
in
\mathbf{SComp}
,
X

is a locally convex-compact topological cone in the induced cone
structure.

X
is also sober, since stably locally compact. As a topological
cone, it will even be stably compact. Hence only local convexity is
missing.

Proof 4.1
.

We already know that
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
is a topological cone.
\textcolor
blackWe
use the following fact about locally compact spaces such as
X
:
for every compact saturated subset
Q_{0}
of
X
and every open
subset
U
of
X

\textcolor
blackthat contains
Q_{0}
, there is a compact saturated subset
Q
of
X

such that
Q_{0}\subseteq int({Q})\subseteq Q\subseteq U

[
Gou13
, Proposition 4.8.14]
. As a consequence, the
family
\mathcal{F}_{U}
of interiors
int({Q})
, where
Q
ranges
over the compact saturated subsets of
U
, is directed: it is
nonempty by the previous observation with
Q_{0}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}\emptyset
,
and for any two elements
int({Q_{1}})
and
int({Q_{2}})

of
\mathcal{F}_{U}
, the same observation with

Q_{0}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\text{def}}}}Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}
gives a larger element
int({Q})
of

\mathcal{F}_{U}
. Additionally,
U
is the union of that family,
since every point
x\in U
is in some element of
\mathcal{F}_{U}
,
by definition of local compactness.

Given
\nu\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
and an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of

\nu

\textcolor
blackin the weak topology,
\mathcal{U}
contains an open neighborhood of
\nu
of the
form
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}[U_{i}>r_{i}]
, where each
U_{i}
is open in
X

and
r_{i}>0
. Since
X
is locally compact,
\textcolor
blackeach
U_{i}
is
the union of the directed family
\mathcal{F}_{U_{i}}
. Since
\nu

is Scott-continuous,
\nu(int({Q_{i}}))>r_{i}
for some

\textcolor
blackcompact saturated subset
Q_{i}
of
U_{i}
. Let
\epsilon>0

be such that
\nu(int({Q_{i}}))>r_{i}+\epsilon
for every
i
.
Then
\nu
is in
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}[int({Q_{i}})>r_{i}+\epsilon]
.
The latter is open and included in

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}[Q_{i}\geq r_{i}+\epsilon]
, which is compact
saturated
\textcolor
black, being a finite intersection of compact saturated
sets in a stably compact space. Then,

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}[Q_{i}\geq r_{i}+\epsilon]
is included in

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}[U_{i}>r_{i}]\subseteq\mathcal{U}
. Finally,

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}[Q_{i}\geq r_{i}+\epsilon]
is convex,
\textcolor
blackas an
intersection of convex sets; any set of the form
[Q\geq r]
is
convex, since for all
\mu,\nu\in[Q\geq r]
, for every

a\in[0,1]
, for every open neighborhood
U
of
Q
,

(a\cdot\mu+(1-a)\cdot\nu)(U)=a\mu(U)+(1-a)\nu(U)\geq ar+(1-a)r=r
. Therefore
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X
is locally
convex-compact.

Let us consider a linear retraction
r\colon\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{D}
, with
associated section
s\colon\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{C}
. If
\mathfrak{C}
is locally
convex-compact, then for every
y\in\mathfrak{D}
and every open
neighborhood
V
of
\textcolor
black
y
,
s(y)
has a convex compact neighborhood

Q
included in
r^{-1}(V)
. Let
U
be the interior of
Q
. The
image of
Q
under
r
is convex, compact, contains the open
neighborhood
s^{-1}(U)
of
y
, and is included in
V
. Hence

\mathfrak{D}
is locally convex-compact. (This argument is the same as

[
Hec96
, Proposition 6.6]
.) Also, if
\mathfrak{C}
is topological,
then addition and scalar multiplication on
\mathfrak{D}
are such that

x+y=r(s(x)+s(y))
and
a\cdot x=r(a\cdot s(x))
, hence are
jointly continuous, so
\mathfrak{D}
is topological.

The final part
\textcolor
blackfollows from the fact that any
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebra is a
linear retraction, with the unit
\eta_{X}
as section.

We now give an example to show that Theorem
5
may fail
if local convex-compactness of
\mathfrak{C}
is not assumed, even when
\mathfrak{C}
is a locally
convex, sober topological cone.

{exa}

Let
\mathbb{R}
be the set of reals with the usual metric topology and
\mathfrak{C}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
,
the set of point-continuous valuations on
\mathbb{R}
. Similar to
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}\mathbb{R}
,
the space
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
, with the relative weak topology from
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}\mathbb{R}
, also is a
locally convex sober topological cone
[
Hec96
]
.

The cone
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
is actually strictly contained
in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}\mathbb{R}
. The Lebesgue valuation
\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}
, obtained by restricting the
Lebesgue measure on opens of
\mathbb{R}
is a continuous valuation that is not in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
; see for example
[
Hec96
, Section 4.1]
.
The map
\eta_{\mathrm{p}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}\colon x\mapsto\delta_{x}
is a well-defined continuous map, as
\delta_{x}

is point-continuous for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence
\eta_{\mathrm{p}}[\lambda]
, the image valuation defined as
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}(\eta_{\mathrm{p}})(\lambda)
, is a
continuous valuation in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
. However, we claim that
\eta_{\mathrm{p}}[\lambda]
does not have
a barycenter in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
.

Assume that
\eta_{\mathrm{p}}[\lambda]
does have a barycenter in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
and we denote it by
\nu
. Then
by definition of barycenters,

\Lambda(\nu)=\int_{\mu\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}}\Lambda(\mu)~d\eta_{\mathrm{p}}[\lambda]
for each continuous linear map

\Lambda\colon\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. In particular, each open subset
U
in
\mathbb{R}
determines
such a continuous linear map
\Lambda_{U}\colon\mu\mapsto\mu(U)
. So for each open
U
in
\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\nu(U)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{U}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mu\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}}\Lambda_{U}(\mu)~d\eta_{\mathrm{p}}[\lambda]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mu\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}}\mu(U)~d\eta_{\mathrm{p}}[\lambda]

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\delta_{x}(U)~d\lambda

(by the change of variable formula)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\chi_{U}(x)~d\lambda

\displaystyle=\lambda(U).

So
\nu=\lambda
. But this would contradict to the fact that
\lambda
is not point-continuous, and hence
\eta_{\mathrm{p}}[\lambda]
does not
have any barycenters in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
. As a byproduct, we know that
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
cannot be
locally convex-compact by Theorem
5
.

We also realize that
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbb{R}
is isomorphic to the dual cone
({\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{R})^{*}
, where
{\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{R}
consists
of all lower semi-continuous maps on
\mathbb{R}
, and both
{\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{R}
and
({\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{R})^{*}
are endowed with the
topology of pointwise convergence
[
Hec96
, Theorem 8.2]
.
Hence, continuous valuations on a dual cone may fail to have barycenters.

5.
Conclusion

\textcolor

blackTheorem
5
is a pretty general barycenter existence
theorem, which also serves to show that a large collection of
topological cones has a (unique)
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebra structure
inducing its cone structure. Through the examples we have given, we
hope to have demonstrated that this theorem encompasses most of the
cases where we already knew that barycenters existed, at the very least.

We sum up the main remaining questions as follows.

•

\textcolor

blackWhich dual cones
\mathfrak{C}^{*}
are
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebras? We
mentioned this in Example
2

(4)
, and we
said that those that are locally convex-compact fit the bill in
Example
3
, and not all dual cones are locally
convex-compact in Example
4
.

•

Given a
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}
-algebra
\alpha\colon\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{w}}X\to X
,

\textcolor
blackwe know that
X
has an induced cone structure that is weakly
locally convex. Need it be locally convex? Both local convexity
and local convex-compactness are stronger properties than weak
local convexity, and none is known to imply the other. One should
probably focus on the case of stably locally compact
X
, where we
know at least that local convex-compactness is needed, by
Proposition
6
.

•

\textcolor

blackIn the converse direction, what weaker conditions
\textcolor
blackthan
local convexity and local convex-compactness would be enough to
deduce the same conclusions as those of
Theorem
5
? Those conditions need to be at least as
strong as weak local convexity.
