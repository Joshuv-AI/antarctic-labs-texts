Average diffusion rate of Ehrenfest Wind-tree billiards
Simon BARAZER

Abstract
. One of the versions of the wind-tree model of Boltzmann gas, suggested by Paul and Tatiana Ehrenfest more than a century ago, can be seen as a billiard in the plane endowed with ℤ ⊕ ℤ \mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} -periodic rectangular obstacles. In the breakthrough paper by V. Delecroix, P. Hubert and S. Lelievre the authors proved, that the diffusion rate of trajectories in such a billiard is equal to 2 3 \frac{2}{3} , that is the maximal distance from the origin achieved by a point of a typical trajectory on a segment of time [ 0 , t ] [0,t] grows roughly as t 2 3 t^{\frac{2}{3}} for large t t . Here 2 3 \frac{2}{3} is the Lyapunov exponent of the associated renormalizing dynamical system. This pioneering result does not tell, however, whether trajectories spend most of the time close or far from the initial point. In the current paper, we prove that the average distance from the origin grows with the same rate t 2 3 t^{\frac{2}{3}} . In plain terms, it means that trajectories mostly stay as far as possible from the initial point (though, it is known that the wind-tree billiard is recurrent, so trajectories occasionally pass close to the initial point). More generally, fundamental rigidity results by A. Eskin and M. Mirzakhani completed by certain genericity results by J. Chaika and A. Eskin imply that the diffusion rate of almost all flat geodesic rays on any ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} -cover of a closed translation surface S S is given by certain Lyapunov exponent of the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle on the SL 2 ​ ( ℝ ) \text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) -orbit closure of S S . In this paper we prove that in this most general setting, the average and maximum diffusion rates coincide.

1.
Introduction

The wind-tree model is an infinite
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-periodic billiard in the
plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where identical rectangular obstacles are placed in
such way that the corresponding sides are aligned with the horizontal
and vertical axes and the centers of rectangles are located at the
lattice points, see figure
1
). More formally, for every
couples of real parameters
(a,b)\in(0,1)^{2}
the wind-tree table

T(a,b)
is defined as

T(a,b)=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigsqcup_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left(m-\frac{a}{2},m+\frac{a}{2}\right)\times\left(n-\frac{b}{2},n+\frac{b}{2}\right).

It’s assumed that reflections on the sides of
the rectangles are regular and the speed of all trajectories is
constant equal to one (figure
1
). A version of this
model was introduced by Tatiana and Paul Erhenfest more than a
century ago, see
[
EE90
]
. It have been studied
more recently by using the tools of translation surfaces theory.
Athur Avila and Pascal Hubert proved in
[
AH20
]

that the trajectories in the
wind-tree are recurrent. Later Vincent Delecroix, Pascal Hubert and
Samuel Lelièvre proved in
[
DHL14
]

that the diffusion rate of a typical
trajectory is equal to
\frac{2}{3}
. If
\gamma_{t}(x,\theta)
is the
wind-tree flow in the direction
\theta
starting from
x\in T(a,b)
,
they proved the following theorem.

Theorem

(Delcroix, Hubert, Lelievre, 2014)
.

For any
(a,b)\in(0,1)^{2}
,
for almost every
\theta
(depending on
(a,b)
) and for all
x\in T(a,b)
having an infinite future orbit, the following relation holds:

\limsup_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\log d(x,\gamma_{t}(x,\theta))}{\log t}=\frac{2}{3}\,.

Anton Zorich and Vincent Delecroix generalized this result to a large
class of wind-tree billiards with more complicated obstacles

[
DZ20
]
. Charles Fougeron studied
in
[
Fou20
]
orbit closures of flat surfaces
associated to even more general wind-tree billiards. In particular,
he identified the Lyapunov exponent of the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle
that governs the associated diffusion rate. In a different direction,
Krzysztof Fraczek and Corinna Ulcigrai showed
in
[
FU14
]
that the directional flow for almost all
wind-tree billiard is non-ergodic.

Note that the diffusion rate
\frac{2}{3}
is defined in the original

[
DHL14
]
of
Delecroix–Hubert–Lelièvre as the

the origin achieved by a point of a typical trajectory on a large

. In particular, we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

For any
(a,b)\in(0,1)^{2}
,
for almost every
\theta
(depending on
(a,b)
) and for all

having an infinite future orbit, the following relation holds:

\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\log\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}d(x,\gamma_{t}(x,\theta))dt}{\log T}=\frac{2}{3}\,.

The strategy of the proof is similar to the proof of the Theorem of
Delecroix–Hubert–Lelièvre presented above. The first step is
standard: following the Katok–Zemliakov construction
(
[
ZK75
]
, see also
[
MT02
]
), the authors of
[

]

construct a translation surface

without boundary from

. By construction, the

is conjugate to the geodesic flow on the

. The fact that the wind-tree

\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-periodic implies that the surface

is a

\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-cover over a compact translation surface

. In this way,

on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-covers of translation surfaces.

We call
\tilde{S}\to S
a

if
\tilde{S}
is
a connected
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-cover of a compact translation surface

.
We will see that such a cover is defined by a
cohomology class

in the first cohomology

As in
[
DHL14
]
,
the diffusion of a billiard trajectory can be

with geodesic segments on
S
completed to closed paths
in a natural way. We denote by

By

We prove the following theorem.

The proof of this proposition uses tools from dynamics on the moduli

Zor99
]
, by G. Forni

[

]
and applied in

]
. Let

\pi:\tilde{S}\to S

be the

\tilde{S}

To study the drift of a trajectory starting from

(1.1)

\langle f,C_{N}(S,x)\rangle\,.

. The growth of

If we fix a cross section for the Teichmüller flow in the moduli space

in the cross section such that

Background facts and constructions

The present paper uses tools from the theory of compact flat
surfaces. We refer to the surveys of A. Zorich
[
Zor06
]

and of J.C. Yoccoz
[
Yoc07
]
for an elementary
introduction to the subject and a survey
[
FM14
]

of G. Forni and C. Matheus for more details. In this section, we
introduce the material which will be used in the proof of
Theorems
1
and
2
announced in the introduction. We
start by presenting some basic facts on flat surfaces and lattice
coverings. We also define the Teichmüller and moduli spaces of flat
surfaces and give recent results related to the dynamics of the

\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

Compact flat surfaces

A compact translation surface
S
is commonly defined as a pair

(S,\omega)
where
S
is a compact Riemann surface and
\omega
is a
non-zero holomorphic differential on
S
. The one-form
\omega

defines a flat metric
ds^{2}=\text{Re}(\omega\otimes\bar{\omega})
. Compactness of the surface
S
implies that the induced
area of
S
is necessarily finite.

The metric as above has singularities
at the finite set
\Sigma_{\omega}
of zeros of
\omega
;
at a point
x\in\Sigma_{\omega}
the metric has a conical
singularity of angle
2\pi(k_{x}+1)
, where
k_{x}
is the order of the
zero. If
g
is the genus of the surface, the Gauss–Bonnet formula
gives

\sum_{x}k_{x}=2g-2\,.

By taking local primitives of
\omega
in simply-connected domains in
the complement to
\Sigma_{\omega}
, we get an atlas of charts on

S\backslash\Sigma_{\omega}
with values in
\mathbb{C}
and such that the
transition functions are translations.

The geodesic flow is defined on the tangent bundle to
S\backslash\Sigma_{\omega}
. A trajectory may land to
a singularity in a finite time in onward or in a backward direction.
Such trajectories are called
singular trajectories
.

The translation structure allows
to trivialize the tangent
bundle over
S\backslash\Sigma_{\omega}
. Thus,
a geodesic on a translation surface
preserves the initial direction, which allows to
consider a
directional flow

in any fixed direction
\theta
.
We mainly consider the vertical (pointing to the North) and the
horizontal (pointing to the Est) directional flows. The geodesic flow on
a flat surface has been studied extensively in the past decades.
The following fundamental result concerning the
directional flows is due to S. Kerckhoff, H. Masur and
P. Smillie
[
KMS86
]

Theorem

(Kerckoff, Masur, Smillie)
.

For every translation surface
S
,
the directional flow in direction
\theta

is uniquely ergodic for almost all directions
\theta
.

A saddle connection on a flat surface is a singular trajectory which
connects two singularities (possibly the same). We are particularly
interested in the vertical and horizontal saddle connections. The
following result from
[
Kea75
]

is often called
Keane’s criterion
.

Theorem

(Keane
[
Kea75
]
)
.

If a flat surface has no saddle connections in direction
\theta
,
then the geodesic flow in this direction is minimal.

Lattice coverings

A
lattice cover of a translation surface
is a triple

(\tilde{S},S,\pi)
. Where
S
is a compact translation surface,

\tilde{S}
is a connected translation surface, and

\pi:\tilde{S}\rightarrow S
is a holomorphic
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-covering map. Moreover, we assume
that the holomorphic one-form
\tilde{\omega}
,
defining the translation structure
on
\tilde{S}
, is the pullback of the holomorphic one-form
\omega

defining the
translation structure on
S
. That is
\pi^{*}\omega=\tilde{\omega}
.
Lattice
\mathbb{Z}
-covers were studied in
[
HW12
]

and
[
HHW13
]
. For instance, we can think to a surface embedded in an highter dimentionnal torus and periodic in d-directions.

By definition, the monodromy group
of a lattice cover is
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
We denote his action
on
\tilde{S}
by
\mathbf{n}\cdot x
. If we
fix a base point
x
on
S
, an isomorphism of the monodromy
group with
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
defines a morphism

f:\pi_{1}(S,x)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

This morphism factorizes thought the Abelianised group and defines a
class
f
in the first cohomology of
S
:

f\in\text{Hom}(H_{1}(S,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Z}^{d})=H^{1}(S,\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

The class
f
characterizes the cover up to an isomorphism. We
will use the cohomology class
f
to measure the deviation of a
trajectory.

Teichmüller spaces

The Teichmüller spaces of flat surfaces are spaces of isotopy classes of
flat surfaces with prescribed singularities (we refer to
[
Yoc07
]
,
[
FM14
]
and
[
Zor06
]
for an
introduction to the subject). We fix a compact topological surface

M
of genus
g
and a set
\Sigma=\{x_{1},...,x_{r}\}
of distinct
points in
M
. Let
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},...,\kappa_{r})
a set of non
negative integers such that

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\kappa_{l}=2g-2,

The Teichmüller space
\mathcal{TH}(\kappa)
associated to
(M,\Sigma,\kappa)
is the set of isotopy classes of compact marked translation surfaces
\phi:S\rightarrow M
such that, for all
i
the surface
S
have a conical singularity of angle
2\pi(\kappa_{i}+1)
at
\phi^{-1}(x_{i})
(a zeros of order
\kappa_{i}
) and no singularities elsewhere.

The Teichmüller spaces have a nice structure of complex linear
manifolds, the local charts are given by taking period coordinates in
the relative cohomology

(\phi,S)\rightarrow[\phi_{*}\omega]\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma,\mathbb{C}).

The period map is a local homeomorphism (see

[
Yoc07
]
).

The mapping class group
\text{Mod}(M,\Sigma)
is the group of isotopy classes of diffeomorphisms on
(M,\Sigma)
, it acts naturally on the Teichmüller spaces. The moduli space
\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
is the quotient space of

\mathcal{TH}(\kappa)
by
\text{Mod}(M,\Sigma)
. Flat surfaces can have nontrivial automorphism groups;
\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
is a complex orbifold in
general, but we can find a finite cover which is a manifold. The area of a surface define a function on the moduli and Teichmüller space
and we denote
\mathcal{TH}^{1}(\kappa),\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
the level set of surfaces
with an area equal to one.

Remark 1

(Pointed surfaces)
.

We can add marked points that are regular to surfaces in Teichmüller spaces; regular means that they do not correspond to conical singularities of the underlying surface. We call such surfaces: pointed surfaces. For a given
\kappa
we denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{TH}}(\kappa)=\mathcal{TH}((0,\kappa))
the Teichmüller space of flat surfaces in
\mathcal{TH}(\kappa)
with a marked regular point (it’s the universal curve over
\mathcal{TH}(\kappa)
). There is a natural projection
\widetilde{\mathcal{TH}}(\kappa)\to\mathcal{TH}(\kappa)
that is invariant under the action of the mapping class group. An automorphism of a flat surface is locally given by a translation, if an automorphism stabilise a regular point then it’s locally constant near this point and then
it’s globally trivial when the surface is connected. Using this we
see that the moduli spaces
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\kappa)
of pointed surfaces are
manifolds.

Dynamics in the moduli space of translation surfaces

In this paper we use the left action of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the moduli spaces of translation surfaces
\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
(see
[
Zor06
]
or
[
FM14
]
for a survey).
The group
acts by linear deformations of the flat structure and preserves the
area of flat surfaces then it also preserves the hypersuface
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
. The action
of the group
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
in period coordinates can be seen as
the diagonal action on the coefficients
\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Two
one-parameter subgroups of
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
are important in the
study of translation surfaces: the diagonal subgroup and the subgroup

\text{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The flow induced by the diagonal subgroup is
denoted by

(2.1)

g_{s}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{s}&0\\
0&e^{-s}\end{pmatrix}

and called the
Teichmüller flow
.
This flow shrinks the flat metric in the vertical
direction and dilates the metric in the horizontal direction. The flow

r_{\theta}
is defined by the action of
\text{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
:

r_{\theta}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}\cos(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{array}\right).

It does not change the flat metric, but it changes
the distinguished direction (“direction of the North”).

The vector space
H^{1}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{C})
serving for period
coordinates admits a natural integer lattice

H^{1}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{Z}\oplus i\mathbb{Z})
, which endows it with a
canonical volume element: the one, for which a fundamental domain of
the lattice has a unit volume. This volume element defined in period
coordinates induces a well-defined volume element in every stratum

\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
of translation surfaces called the

Masur–Veech
volume element. By construction the group

\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
preserves the Masur–Veech volume element and the flat area of the surfaces. Thus, the Masur–Veech volume element in
\mathcal{H}(\kappa)

induces a natural volume element on the hypersurface

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
. The following Theorem
proved independently by H. Masur
[
Mas82
]
and by
W. Veech
[
Vee78
]
is the keystone result in dynamics in the
moduli space of translation surfaces.

Theorem

(Masur, Veech)
.

The total volume of any stratum
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
in the moduli space
of compact translation surfaces is finite. The Teichmüller flow acts
ergodically on every connected component of every stratum.

Orbit closures and affine invariant submanifolds

Let
S
be a translation surface of area
1
in some stratum

\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
,
S\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
. Detecting
the orbit closure

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{S}=\overline{\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cdot S}
(resp
\mathcal{H}_{S}=\overline{\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cdot S}
) of the orbit of
S
in

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
is one of the most important questions in the theory of
translation surfaces.
The Magic Wand theorem of
A. Eskin–M. Mirzakhani–A. Mohammadi
[
eskin2013invariant
]
,

[
EMM15
]

(and an important complement by S. Filip
[
Fil16
]
)
provide the general structure of

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{S}
.

Theorem

(Eskin–Mirzakhani–Mohammadi)
.

For each translation surface
S
the orbit closure

\mathcal{H}_{S}

is locally a linear subspace in period coordinates
(possibly a finite collection of linear subspaces).

The orbit closure
\mathcal{H}_{S}

admits a measure
\tilde{\mu}_{S}
such that

•

Locally, the measure
\tilde{\mu}_{S}
is proportional to the Lebesgue measure in the period coordinates.

•

The measure
\mu_{S}
induced on
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{S}
is an invariant probability measure for the Teichmüller flow.

•

The measure
\mu_{S}
is ergodic for the Teichmüller flow.

Hodge bundle and Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle

Every stratum
\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
admits two natural vector bundles
\hat{H}^{1}\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
and
H^{1}\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
with
fibers
H^{1}(S,\Sigma_{S},\mathbb{R})
and
H^{1}(S,\mathbb{R})
over a point in

\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
represented by a translation surface
S
. Both bundles
carry a natural flat connection called the
Gauss–Manin
connection
uniquely determined by the property of equivariance of
lattices
H^{1}(S,\Sigma_{S},\mathbb{Z})
and
H^{1}(S,\mathbb{Z})
respectively. The

Kontevich–Zorich cocycles
correspond to the monodrony of
these connections along the
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action. These
cocycles, which we denote by
G_{s}
, serve for an efficient
renormalization procedure.

The bundle
H^{1}\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
is endowed with a natural norm
\|.\|_{h}

called the
Hodge norm
; this norm is induced from the
classical Hodge norm on
H^{1,0}(S,\mathbb{C})
by the canonical isomormhism

H^{1}(S,\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow H^{1,0}(S,\mathbb{C})\,.

G. Forni proved in
[
FM14
]
the following uniform
upper bound for the growth rate of the Hodge norm along the
Teichmüller flow:

Theorem

(Forni,
[
FM14
]
)
.

For every
u\in H^{1}\mathcal{H}(\kappa)
such that
\|u\|_{h}=1
the following
bound holds:

\frac{d\|G_{s}u\|_{h}}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\leq 1\,.

This bound implies, in particular,
integrability of the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle for any affine
invariant probability measure on
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
.

Oseledets and Birkhoff ergodic theorems

A. Eskin and J. Chaika have proved in
[
CE15
]

the following
important versions of the Birkhoff and
Oseldets ergodic theorems for the measure
\mu_{S}
and the
Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle. These theorems are particularly efficient
in the study of translations surfaces (see
also the survey of J. Chaika
[
CE15
]
).

Theorem

(Chaika–Eskin)
.

For any translation surface
S

in any stratum
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
,
for almost any angle
\theta
and for any
continuous function
\phi
with compact support in
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{S}

the following equality holds:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi(g_{s}r_{\theta}S)ds=\int_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(S)}\phi~d\mu_{S}\,.

Theorem

(Chaika–Eskin)
.

For any translation surface
S
in any stratum
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\kappa)
, and for
almost any angle
\theta
, the translation surface
r_{\theta}S
is
Oseledets-generic for the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle along the
Teichmüller flow with respect to the measure
\mu_{S}
.

Remark 2

(cocycles with multiple components)
.

Recall that a cohomology class
f\in H^{1}(S,\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}^{d}

Veech’s zippered rectangles construction is a particularly convenient
way to represent a vertical flow on a compact translation surface,

Large excursions

In this section, we prove the main theorems of the paper. We first show briefly how we can reduce the problem from lattice coverings of translation surfaces to compact translation surfaces. This was previously discussed in the paper
[
DHL14
]
. In this same section, we rephrase the theorem
2
by using the formalism of zippered rectangle construction

. In a second, by using Veech zippered rectangles construction, we choose a convenient cross section for the Teichmüller flow and then consider the first return map on this section. To define the cross section, we use the lemma
3
which is given in the appendix. In the third section, by using the normalization procedure, we produce a sequence of trajectories with a good diffusion rate, and we show that these excursions occur at exponential times. In fourth, from the results of A. Zorich

By using the excursions, we can show that the trajectory goes far from the initial point; with the upper bound, we can control the diffusion near these excursions and show that a typical trajectory spends a large part of its time far from the starting point.

Uniform upper bound:

For the last section, we need a uniform upper bound, which can be obtained using the same techniques as in the Zorich paper’s
[
Zor99
]
. We do not give proof of this result here.

Proposition 2
.

For almost all
\theta
, for all cocycle
f
such that
\Lambda f>0
, and for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N_{0}
such that

\frac{\log|\langle f,C_{\textsc{n}}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|}{\log n}\leq\Lambda(f)+\epsilon,~~~\forall\textsc{n}\geq N_{0}.

The bound is true for all
x\in I^{*}(S_{\theta})
(i.e. points with an infinite future orbit).

The important point in this proposition is the fact that the bound is uniform on the segment
I^{*}(S_{\theta})
, we will use it in the next section. We can obtain the following corollary by performing a summation of the last inequality.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\theta
and
f
that satisfy the statement of the last proposition. Then, for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N_{0}
such that

\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|\leq \exp\left(\left(\Lambda(f)+1+\epsilon\right)\log\textsc{n}\right),~~~\forall\textsc{n}\geq N_{0},

and the bound is true for all
x\in I^{*}(S_{\theta})
.

Proof.

Let
\theta
satisfying the assumption of the last proposition. For all
\epsilon>0
, according to the last proposition, there is
N_{0}\geq 0
such as: for all
\textsc{n}\geq N_{0}
and all
x\in I^{*}(S_{\theta})
we have

|\langle f,C_{\textsc{n}}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|\leq\textsc{n}^{\Lambda f+\frac{\epsilon}{2}}

Moreover, by using the fact that
C_{\textsc{n}}
is an additive cocycle we have the trivial bound:

|\langle f,C_{\textsc{n}}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|\leq\textsc{n}\max_{\alpha}|\langle f,h_{\alpha}\rangle|.

We can write, for all
\textsc{n}\geq N_{0}

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k<N_{0}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|+\sum_{N_{0}\leq k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N^{2}_{0}\max_{\alpha}|\langle f,h_{\alpha}\rangle|+\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}k^{\Lambda f+\frac{\epsilon}{2}}

Using comparison series vs integral, we have

\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}k^{\Lambda f+\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\leq\int_{0}^{\textsc{n}+1}x^{\Lambda f+\frac{\epsilon}{2}}dx\leq\frac{(\textsc{n}+1)^{\Lambda f+\frac{\epsilon}{2}+1}}{\Lambda f+\frac{\epsilon}{2}+1}.

Then we can find two constants
A,B
(depending of
\epsilon
only ) such as, for all
\textsc{n}\geq N_{0}
, we have

\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|\leq A+B~\textsc{n}^{\Lambda f+1+\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\leq(A~\textsc{n}^{-\Lambda f-1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}+B)~\textsc{n}^{\Lambda f+1+\frac{\epsilon}{2}}.

If
n
goes to
\infty
, the factor
A~\textsc{n}^{-\Lambda f-1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}+B
is bounded, then its smaller than
\textsc{n}^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}
for
n
large enought. Then, by increasing
N_{0}
, we show that: for all
\epsilon
, there is
N_{0}
such that, for all
\textsc{n}\geq N_{0}
we have

\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|\geq\textsc{n}^{\Lambda f+1+\epsilon}.

As before, the bound is uniform on
I^{*}(S_{\theta})
.
∎

Lower bound:

Now, we need to establish a lower bound for the second statement of proposition
2
.

Proof.

First, we exhibit intervals of discrete times on which the sum of
|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|
is large enough. To do this, we use large excursions (see proposition
1
) and the fact that the diffusion is bounded around such excursions (see corollary
3
). The trajectory goes far and stays far enough. In a second, we prove that these intervals can be used to minorate
\frac{\log\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}C_{k}(S_{\theta})}{\log\textsc{n}}
where
n
belongs to some intervals of discrete times. Finally, we show that the union of these intervals contains
N_{1}+\mathbb{N}
for
N_{1}
large enought.

1)
For all
p\geq 0
, we can write

\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}_{n}+p}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|\geq\sum_{0\leq k\leq p}|\langle f,C_{\textsc{n}_{n}+k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|.

Using the fact that
C_{\textsc{n}}(S_{\theta},x)
is an additive cocycle for the interval exchange
T_{S_{\theta}}
, we have:

|\langle f,C_{\textsc{n}_{n}+k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|\geq|\langle f,C_{\textsc{n}_{n}}(S_{\theta})\rangle|-|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},T_{S_{\theta}}^{\textsc{n}_{n}}x)\rangle|.

Of course, if
x
has an infinite future orbit, then it is also true for
T_{S_{\theta}}^{\textsc{n}_{n}}x
. We can apply proposition
3
for an
\epsilon
such that
0<2\epsilon<\text{min}\{\beta,\Lambda f\}
, we can find
p_{\epsilon}
which does not depend on
x,n
and such that, for all
p\geq p_{\epsilon}

\sum_{0\leq k\leq p} |\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},T_{S_{\theta}}^{\textsc{n}_{n}}x)\rangle|\leq\exp\left((\Lambda f+1+\epsilon)\log p\right).

Using the lower bound of proposition
1
and the lower bound on
\textsc{n}_{n}
, there is
n_{\epsilon}
such that, for all
n\geq n_{\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle \sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}_{n}+p}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta},x)\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p\exp\left((\Lambda f-\epsilon)\textsc{n}_{n}\right)-\exp\left((\Lambda f+1+\epsilon)\log p\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p\exp\left((\Lambda f-\epsilon)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)-\exp\left((\Lambda f+1+\epsilon)\log p\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p\left(\exp\left((\Lambda f-\epsilon)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)-\exp\left((\Lambda f+\epsilon)\log p\right)\right).

If
p\leq 2^{-\frac{1}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}}\exp\left(\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)
, we then have

\exp\left((\Lambda f-\epsilon)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)-\exp\left((\Lambda f+\epsilon)\log p\right)\geq\frac{\exp\left((\Lambda f-\epsilon)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)}{2}

Let
p_{n}
be the integer part of
\exp(-\frac{\log(2)}{\Lambda f+\epsilon})\exp\left(\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)
. As
p_{n}
goes to
\infty
when
n
grows up, there is
n_{\epsilon}^{\prime}\geq n_{\epsilon}
and
A_{2}>0
such that if
n\geq n^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
, we have
p_{n}\geq n_{\epsilon}
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle \sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}_{n}+p_{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle \frac{p_{n}}{2}\exp\left((\Lambda f-\epsilon)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle \left(2^{-\frac{1}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}}\exp\left(\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)-1\right) \exp\left( (\Lambda f-\epsilon)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(2^{-\frac{1}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}}-\exp\left(-\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)\right)\exp\left(\left(\Lambda f+\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}-\epsilon\right)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right).

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle A_{2}\exp\left(\left(\Lambda f+\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}-\epsilon\right)(\beta-\epsilon)n\right).

Finally obtain

\displaystyle \log\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}_{n}+p_{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\log\sum_{\textsc{n}_{n}<k\leq\textsc{n}_{n}+p_{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\Lambda f+\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}-\epsilon\right)(\beta-\epsilon)n +\log A_{2}.

2)
Using the expression of
p_{n}
, there exist constants
A_{3}
independent of
\epsilon,n
such that

p_{n}\leq A_{3}\exp\left(\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}(\beta-\epsilon)n\right)\leq A_{3}\exp((\beta+\epsilon)n).

If
n
is large enough, we have obviously

\frac{\log(A_{3}+1)}{n+1}\leq\epsilon,~~~\text{and}~~~\frac{\beta-\epsilon}{n+1}\leq\epsilon.

And then

\displaystyle \frac{(\beta-\epsilon)n}{\log(\textsc{n}_{n+1}+p_{n+1})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(\beta-\epsilon)n}{\log(A_{3}\exp((\beta-\epsilon)(n+1))+\textsc{n}_{n+1})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(\beta-\epsilon)n}{\log(A_{3}+1)+(\beta+\epsilon)(n+1)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta-2\epsilon}{2\epsilon+ \beta}.

Then, for all

\textsc{n}_{n}+p_{n}\leq\textsc{n}
and
\textsc{n}\leq\textsc{n}_{n+1}+p_{n+1}

\displaystyle \frac{\log\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|}{\log\textsc{n}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle \frac{\log\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}_{n}+p_{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|}{\log(\textsc{n}_{n+1}+p_{n+1})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{n(\beta-\epsilon)}{\log(\textsc{n}_{n+1}+p_{n+1})}\left(\Lambda f+\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}-\epsilon\right) +\frac{\log A_{2}}{\log(\textsc{n}_{n+1}+p_{n+1})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle \frac{\beta-2\epsilon}{2\epsilon+ \beta} \left(\Lambda(f)+\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}-\epsilon\right) +\frac{\log A_{2}}{\log(\textsc{n}_{n+1}+p_{n+1})}.

As it goes to zeros when

is large, the last term is useless; we can find

such that, for all

and for all

\textsc{n}_{n}+p_{n}\leq\textsc{n}~\text{and}~\textsc{n}\leq\textsc{n}_{n+1}+p_{n+1},

we have

\frac{\log\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|}{\log\textsc{n}}\geq \frac{\beta-2\epsilon}{2\epsilon+\beta} \left(\Lambda f+\frac{\Lambda f-\epsilon}{\Lambda f+\epsilon}-2\epsilon\right).

3)
It remains to prove that the bound is true for all

big enough. Let

To conclude, the bound is valid for all

\liminf_{\textsc{n}} \frac{\log\sum_{k\leq\textsc{n}}|\langle f,C_{k}(S_{\theta})\rangle|}{\log\textsc{n}} \geq \Lambda f+1.

Because the LHS tends to

when
