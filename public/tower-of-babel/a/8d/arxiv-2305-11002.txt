Exact tunneling splittings from symmetrized path integrals
George Trenins, Lars Meuser, Hannah Bertschi, Odysseas Vavourakis, Reto Flütsch, Jeremy O. Richardson

Abstract
We develop a new simulation technique based on path-integral molecular dynamics for calculating ground-state tunneling splitting patterns from ratios of symmetrized partition functions. In particular, molecular systems are rigorously projected onto their J = 0 J=0 rotational state by an “Eckart spring” that connects two adjacent beads in a ring polymer. Using this procedure, the tunneling splitting can be obtained from thermodynamic integration at just one (sufficiently low) temperature. Converged results are formally identical to the values that would have been obtained by solving the full rovibrational Schrödinger equation on a given Born–Oppenheimer potential energy surface. The new approach is showcased with simulations of hydronium and methanol, which are in good agreement with wavefunction-based calculations and experimental measurements. The method will be of particular use for the study of low-barrier methyl rotations and other floppy modes, where instanton theory is not valid.

III.1
Path-Integral Molecular Dynamics

For Eq. (
11
) to be of practical use, we need a means of
calculating partition functions without having to solve the
Schrödinger equation. The path-integral
formulation of quantum mechanics is particularly suited to this purpose
and is easily extended to obtain the symmetrized
partition function,
Z_{P}
.
First, we apply the symmetric Trotter product
formula,
Suzuki 1985

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\hat{H}}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\hat{V}/2}\mkern 1.5mu\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\hat{T}}\mkern 1.5mu\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\hat{V}/2}\right)^{N},

(21)

where
\beta_{N}=\beta/N
,
\hat{V}
is the potential energy operator and
\hat{T}
is the kinetic energy operator. Denoting the bracketed product of operators as

\hat{O}_{N}
, in the limit as
N\to\infty
we have

\displaystyle Z_{P}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}\,\braket{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}|(\hat{O}_{N})^{N}\hat{P}|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,\braket{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{N}|\hat{O}_{N}\hat{P}|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}}\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\braket{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}|\hat{O}_{N}|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}}.

(22)

To arrive at the right-hand side of Eq. (
22
),
we inserted
N-1
position resolutions of the identity. The bold
cursive
\bm{x}

refers to the set of
N
vectors
\{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\,|\,i=1\ldots N\}
, where
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}=(x^{\smash{(i)}}_{1},\,x^{\smash{(i)}}_{2},\,\ldots,\,x^{\smash{(i)}}_{f})
and
f
is the number of configurational
degrees of freedom (DoF). For the
integration, we use the shorthand notation

\int\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[\int\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\right].

(23)

Upon evaluating
the matrix elements in Eq. (
22
), we can express the partition function as

\displaystyle Z_{P}

\displaystyle=(2\pi\hbar)^{-Nf}\int\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}H^{P}_{\mkern-1.0muN}(\bm{p},\bm{x})}

(24a)

\displaystyle H^{P}_{\mkern-1.0muN}

\displaystyle=H^{(\text{open})}_{\mkern-1.0muN}+\sum_{k=1}^{f}\frac{m_{k}\omega_{N}^{2}}{2}\left[x^{(N)}_{k}\!-\hat{P}x^{(1)}_{k}\right]^{2}

(24b)

\displaystyle\hskip-15.00002pt\begin{multlined}H^{(\text{open})}_{\mkern-1.0muN}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{{k}=1}^{f}\frac{(p^{(i)}_{k})^{2}}{2m_{k}}+V(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})\right]\\
{}+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=1}^{f}\frac{m_{k}\omega_{N}^{2}}{2}\left[x^{(i+1)}_{k}\!-x^{(i)}_{k}\right]^{2},\end{multlined}

where
m_{k}
is the mass of the
k
-th DoF
and
\bm{p}
refers to the set of momenta
\{\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{i}\,|\,i=1\ldots N\}
.
The Hamiltonian in this expression describes
an extended
classical
system
comprised of
N
replicas of the original
set of particles that reside on the potential
energy surface
V(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. Replicas with adjacent indices
(
i
and
i\pm 1
) are connected by harmonic springs
of frequency
\omega_{N}=1/\beta_{N}\hbar
,
so that the term
H^{\smash{(\text{open})}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}

corresponds to a linear polymer or an open necklace
in which replicas are the “beads.”

The second term in Eq. (
24b
) introduces a spring between
the ends of the necklace (beads 1 and
N
). When the symmetry
operator
\hat{P}
is taken to be the identity
\hat{E}
,

\hat{E}\bm{\mathrm{x}}=\bm{\mathrm{x}}
, the additional spring connects
the extended classical system into a “ring polymer.”
In this case,
Z_{E}
refers to the standard quantum partition function,

Z
, and Eq. (
24a
) represents the discretized version of a
Feynman path
integral expression for the trace of the Boltzmann
operator.
Feynman and Hibbs 1965
Crucially, Eq. (
24a
)
is in the form of a classical partition function
(for the ring polymer at a temperature
T\times N
). As such, it can be evaluated using
well-established techniques from classical statistical mechanics.
Because of the connection to the path-integral
formalism, simulations that sample Eq. (
24a
)
using classical dynamics generated by
H^{\smash{P}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}

(in combination with a suitable thermostat to ensure
canonical sampling) are known as path-integral molecular
dynamics
(PIMD).
Tuckerman 2010
Many PIMD techniques are easily
compatible with other choices of
\hat{P}
, for which the cyclic
symmetry of the ring polymer
is broken.
As we shall see in Secs.
IV.2
and
IV.3
,
the ends of the necklace,
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}
and

\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{N}
, tend to explore
degenerate minima related by the symmetry operation
\hat{P}
, and the
intermediate
beads sample the potential energy barrier connecting the two low-energy
regions.

We note in passing that the symmetrized partition functions used here
also arise in the context of bosonic and fermionic
quantum statistics.
Feynman 1972

For example, Eq. (
10
) evaluated for the
totally symmetric irrep of the complete nuclear

is the contribution to the
bosonic partition function from the states that have a totally symmetric nuclear spin part;
evaluated for the antisymmetric irrep, it gives the contribution from the states with an antisymmetric nuclear spin part.
The contributions to the fermionic partition function can be obtained in an equivalent way.
6
6

6

Here, we are
not required to subtract terms of a similar
magnitude, and so do not suffer from the fermionic sign problem.

Other irreps could be
paired with nuclear spin states of different symmetries
or ascribed to hypothetical particles
with more exotic quantum statistics.
Due to this formal connection, we could in principle
make use of PIMC
Ceperley 1995
and PIMD methods

Runeson, Nava, and Parrinello 2018
;
Hirshberg, Rizzi, and Parrinello 2019
developed for simulating
the quantum thermodynamics of bosonic and fermionic systems.
For now, however, it seems more pragmatic to use the PIMD
formulation presented in this section, combined with
thermodynamic integration (TI) as described in Sec.
III.3
.

III.2
Eckart Spring

The distinguishing feature of the
J=0
partition function in
Eq. (
19
) is the additional integral over orientations,
\bm{\Omega}
.
Repeating the manipulations from Sec.
III.1
results in the following
“polymer” partition function:

\displaystyle Z^{J=0}=(2\pi\hbar)^{-Nf}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\,\left\{\vphantom{\sum_{M}^{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}H_{N}(\bm{p},\bm{r})}\times\right.

\displaystyle\!\!\left.\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\Omega}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\beta_{N}\omega_{N}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathclap{a=1}}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\left\lVert\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}_{a}\!-\hat{R}(\bm{\Omega})\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}_{a}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\!\right\},

(25)

where
n_{\mathrm{at}}
is the number of atoms in the molecule,
f=3n_{\mathrm{at}}
,
and

\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{\smash{(i)}}
is a three-dimensional coordinate vector
\big(x_{a}^{\smash{(i)}}\!,\,y_{a}^{\smash{(i)}}\!,\,z_{a}^{\smash{(i)}}\big)

describing the
a
-th atom of mass
m_{a}
in the
i
-th
system replica (bead).
The integral over
\bm{\Omega}
can be expressed
as

I(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}\bm{\Omega}\,\exp\left[-\frac{Nf\big(\bm{\Omega};\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}\big)}{2\beta\hbar^{2}}\right],

(26)

where

f\big(\bm{\Omega};\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}\big)=\sum_{\mathclap{a=1}}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\left\lVert\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}_{a}\!-\hat{R}(\bm{\Omega})\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}_{a}\right\rVert^{2}.

(27)

For the purposes of evaluating Eq. (
26
),
we can approximate
f\big(\bm{\Omega};\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}\big)
by a Taylor series expansion in
\bm{\Omega}

about its minimum, truncated at second order. In doing so, we replace
the (rather non-trivial)
integral over
\bm{\Omega}
with a three-dimensional Gaussian integral, which can be
evaluated analytically. It can be shown that the relative error
introduced by this approximation vanishes in the many-bead (
N\to\infty
) limit, which is the same limit
as we generally aim for in PIMD simulations. This way of approximating
integrals is known as Laplace’s method
or, more generally, the method of steepest descent.
Bender and Orszag 1978
It
is a standard technique of
asymptotic analysis and forms the foundation of instanton
theory.
Richardson 2018b

However, unlike in instanton theory, the approximation here can be made
exact by increasing
N
. The error
scales as
O(N^{-1})
, in principle decaying more slowly
than the
O(N^{-2})
error from ring-polymer
discretization.
Schmidt and Lee 1995

Despite the formally slower scaling, we observe that the
Eckart spring does not negatively impact the rate at which
tunneling splitting calculations converge with respect
to the number of beads.

For the steepest-descent approach to be practical, we need an efficient
way of locating the minimum of
f(\bm{\Omega})
(from here on, the
parametric dependence on
\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!
and
\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}\!
is
dropped for brevity). The problem amounts to rotating replica 1 about
its CoM such that
the mass-weighted square distance to replica
N
is minimized. This
concept is
closely tied to the Eckart frame, which is central to
the construction of the rovibrational Hamiltonian
Eckart 1935
;
Bright Wilson, Decius, and Cross 1955
and has previously
featured in another PIMD method developed by one of the
authors.
Trenins, Willatt, and Althorpe 2019
Note, however, that the Eckart
frame is not used to define a set of curvilinear coordinates
in the present work; all our expressions are in a Cartesian frame.

The algorithm for finding
the Eckart rotation is comprehensively laid out
in Ref.
Krasnoshchekov, Isayeva, and Stepanov 2014
. Here, we summarize the
key details of the procedure.
First, the rotation is represented by a unit quaternion
\bm{\mathrm{q}}=(q_{0},\,q_{1},\,q_{2},\,q_{3})

with a real part
q_{0}
. This is related to the rotation matrix via

\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\Omega})=\begin{pmatrix}1-2q_{2}^{2}-2q_{3}^{2}&2q_{1}q_{2}+2q_{0}q_{3}&2q_{1}q_{3}-2q_{0}q_{2}\\
2q_{1}q_{2}-2q_{0}q_{3}&1-2q_{1}^{2}-2q_{3}^{2}&2q_{2}q_{3}+2q_{0}q_{1}\\
2q_{1}q_{3}+2q_{0}q_{2}&2q_{2}q_{3}-2q_{0}q_{1}&1-2q_{1}^{2}-2q_{2}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(28)

such that

\hat{R}(\bm{\Omega})\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(1)}=\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\Omega})\left[\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(1)}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{com}}\right]+\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{com}}

(29)

with

\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{com}}=\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(1)}\Big/\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}

(30)

denoting the CoM of replica 1. For the rest of this section it is assumed that
we are working in the CoM frame of replica 1, such that

\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\smash{(1)}}_{\mathrm{com}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
.
With the definitions in Eqs. (
28
)–(
30
),
it can be shown that the function in Eq. (
27
) is a quadratic form
in
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
,

f(\bm{\Omega})=\bm{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{C}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{q}},

(31)

with
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
a
4\times 4
real symmetric matrix that is a simple
function of
m_{a}
,
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{\smash{(1)}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{\smash{(N)}}

(
a=1,\dotsc,n_{\mathrm{at}}
). The explicit form of
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
is given in
Eq. (24) of Ref.
Krasnoshchekov, Isayeva, and Stepanov 2014
where, in our
case,

\displaystyle x_{+a}

\displaystyle=x_{a}^{(N)}+x_{a}^{(1)},

\displaystyle x_{-a}

\displaystyle=x_{a}^{(N)}-x_{a}^{(1)},

(32)

and similarly for
y
and
z
coordinates. The
quadratic form can be diagonalized by finding the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
. The smallest eigenvalue

\lambda_{\smash{0}}

corresponds to the minimum of
f(\bm{\Omega})
, and the associated
normalized
eigenvector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\smash{(0)}}
is the quaternion that accomplishes
the rotation onto the minimum. In our case, the
ring-polymer potential ensures that
\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!
and
\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}\!
have a strong structural similarity and can be
rotated onto each other such that they are nearly coincident. The similarity of the structures means
that the optimal rotation is unique, unless
the molecules fluctuate about a (nearly) linear
geometry.
Jørgensen 1978

Construction and diagonalization of

\bm{\mathrm{C}}
are numerically inexpensive relative to the calculation
of the PES, so the outlined procedure can be readily incorporated
into a PIMD simulation.

To proceed with the steepest-descent approximation, we denote the optimal rotation
with
\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}})
, and the corresponding rotated structure
with
\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}{}^{\smash{(1)}}_{a}=\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}})\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\smash{(1)}}_{a}
.
Using
\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\Omega})=\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\delta\bm{\Omega})\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}})
,
the function
f(\bm{\Omega})
is rewritten as

\begin{multlined}f(\bm{\Omega})=\sum_{\mathclap{a=1}}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\left\lVert\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}_{a}\!-\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}^{(1)}_{a}\right\rVert^{2}\\
{}+2\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(N)}\cdot[\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\delta\bm{\Omega})]\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)},\end{multlined}

(33)

where
\bm{\mathrm{I}}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix. Next we express
the rotation

\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\delta\bm{\Omega})

about the minimum in terms of Tait–Bryan angles

\begin{pmatrix}\phi&\theta&\chi\end{pmatrix}
,

Goldstein, Poole, and Safko 2002

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\delta\bm{\Omega})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}c_{\theta}c_{\chi}&-c_{\theta}s_{\chi}&s_{\theta}\\
s_{\phi}s_{\theta}c_{\chi}+c_{\phi}s_{\chi}&-s_{\phi}s_{\theta}s_{\chi}+c_{\phi}c_{\chi}&-s_{\phi}c_{\theta}\\
-c_{\phi}s_{\theta}c_{\chi}+s_{\phi}s_{\chi}&c_{\phi}s_{\theta}s_{\chi}+s_{\phi}c_{\chi}&c_{\phi}c_{\theta}\end{pmatrix},

(34)

where
c_{\phi}=\cos\phi
,
s_{\phi}=\sin\phi
, etc. The
corresponding integral over
orientations is

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\bm{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\chi

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\chi,

(35)

with
z=\cos\theta
. Now we expand
\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\delta\bm{\Omega})
in a Taylor
series about
\left(\phi,\,z,\,\chi\right)=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
.
Up to second order,

\begin{multlined}\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}(\delta\bm{\Omega})\sim\begin{pmatrix}0&\chi&-z\\
-\chi&0&\phi\\
z&-\phi&0\end{pmatrix}\\
\rule{0.0pt}{25.00003pt}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}z^{2}+\chi^{2}&0&0\\
-2z\phi&\phi^{2}+\chi^{2}&0\\
-2\phi\chi&-2z\chi&z^{2}+\phi^{2}\end{pmatrix}.\end{multlined}

(36)

The first (linear) term of Eq. (
36
) contributes

2\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(N)}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&\chi&-z\\
-\chi&0&\phi\\
z&-\phi&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)}

(37)

to
f(\bm{\Omega})
. This evaluates to zero, because
\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)}

satisfies the rotational Eckart conditions.
Bright Wilson, Decius, and Cross 1955

Hence,
only the second- (and higher-) order terms make a non-zero contribution
around the stationary point

f(\bm{\Omega})\sim\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\big\lVert\mathbf{r}_{a}^{(N)}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)}\big\rVert^{2}+\delta\bm{\Omega}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}\cdot\delta\bm{\Omega},

(38)

where
\delta\bm{\Omega}
stands for

\left(\phi,\,z,\,\chi\right)
and
\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}=\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}[\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}]
is a
3\times 3

matrix with elements

\displaystyle\Theta_{ij}[\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\big[(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(N)}\!\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)})\delta_{ij}-r_{a,i}^{(N)}\widetilde{r}_{a,j}^{(1)}\big],

(39)

which is symmetric because of the rotational Eckart conditions.
This finally allows us to approximate the integrand in
Eq. (
26
) by a
Gaussian, so that

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\Omega}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\beta_{N}\omega_{N}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathclap{a=1}}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\left\lVert\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}_{a}\!-\hat{R}(\bm{\Omega})\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}_{a}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\sim{}

(40)

\displaystyle\mkern-10.0mu\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta_{N}\omega_{N}^{2}}\right)^{\mathrlap{\!3/2}}\frac{1}{\det\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}^{1/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\beta_{N}\omega_{N}^{2}}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{n}m_{a}\left\lVert\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(N)}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)}\right\rVert^{2}\right].

Under this steepest-descent approximation (which becomes exact as
N\to\infty
),

\displaystyle Z^{J=0}

\displaystyle=(2\pi\hbar)^{-Nf}\mkern-6.0mu\int\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\,u(\bm{r})\mkern 2.0mu\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\widetilde{H}_{N}(\bm{p},\bm{r})},

(41a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{N}

\displaystyle=H^{\text{(open)}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}+\sum_{a=1}^{n}\frac{m_{a}\omega_{N}^{2}}{2}\left\lVert\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(N)}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)}\right\rVert^{2},

(41b)

\displaystyle u(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta_{N}\omega_{N}^{2}}\right)^{\!3/2}\mkern-14.0mu\frac{1}{\det\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}[\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}]^{1/2}},

(41c)

giving the final form of the rotationally projected partition function.

The projection introduces into the integrand a prefactor
u
,
proportional to
\det\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}^{-1/2}
. The matrix

\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}
is
approximately equal to the
molecule’s inertia tensor, and its determinant will fluctuate with a relatively
small amplitude over the course of a simulation. The thermal
expectation value of

can, therefore, be
calculated efficiently using standard PIMD techniques.

The other change is the replacement of the harmonic spring between replica 1 and replica

N
with an “Eckart spring,” as defined by the sum in Eq. (
41b
). This
modified spring does not specifically favor
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{N}

that are close
to each other. Instead, low energies are achieved for replica pairs whose CoMs
and
shapes
are near coincident, regardless of relative
orientation. The two end beads will tend to explore the same potential
energy well, but at a range of relative orientations. This stems from the rotational
averaging that lead us to
Z^{J=0}
in the first place. The forces due to the Eckart spring, required to simulate such trajectories, can be readily obtained from the

\bm{\mathrm{C}}
-matrix and the normalized eigenvector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{(0)}
associated with
its smallest eigenvalue:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{b}^{(i)}}\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{at}}}m_{a}\left\lVert\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(N)}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)}\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{\mathclap{\mu,\nu=0}}^{3}\mkern 2.0muq^{(0)}_{\mu}\frac{\partial C_{\mu\nu}}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{b}^{(i)}}q^{(0)}_{\nu}.

(42)

The same line of reasoning can be applied to the symmetrized partition
function in Eq. (
20b
). Going through the derivation we find that
Eq. (
41
) still applies, but with a Hamiltonian

\widetilde{H}^{P}_{\mkern-1.0muN}=\widetilde{H}^{\text{(open)}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}+\sum_{a=1}^{n}\frac{m_{a}\omega_{N}^{2}}{2}\left\lVert\hat{P}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{a}^{(N)}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}_{a}^{(1)}\right\rVert^{2},

(43)

where
\hat{P}^{-1}
is the inverse of
\hat{P}
. The prefactor is
similarly modified, now depending on
\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}[\hat{P}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}\!,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{(1)}]
defined as in
Eq. (
39
). The inclusion of an Eckart spring with a
permutation–inversion
operation similarly allows the end beads to assume a range of
relative orientations, except now
they tend to explore
different
potential energy wells,
with minima related by the operation
\hat{P}
.

III.3
Thermodynamic Integration

The general expression for the tunneling splitting, Eq. (
11
),
and
its particular realizations for the double [Eq. (
3
)] and
triple well
[Eq. (
14
)] are (or can be) written in terms of ratios of
symmetrized partition
functions. These functions are of the form

Z_{P}^{J=0}=(2\pi\hbar)^{-Nf}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\ u(\bm{r})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\widetilde{H}^{P}_{\mkern-1.0muN}(\bm{p},\bm{r})}.

(44)

The ratio of two partition functions that are symmetrized by the
operators
\hat{P}_{1}
and
\hat{P}_{0}
, respectively, can be expressed as
Z^{\smash{J=0}}_{P_{1}}/Z^{\smash{J=0}}_{P_{0}}=A\mkern 2.0mu\mathrm{e}^{\smash{-\beta_{N}\Delta F}}
,
where

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\Delta F}=\frac{\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\widetilde{H}^{P_{1}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}(\bm{p},\bm{r})}}{\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\widetilde{H}^{P_{0}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}(\bm{p},\bm{r})}}

(45)

and

. Here,

refers to Eq. (
41c
) evaluated with

\bm{\mathrm{\Theta}}[\hat{P}_{i}^{\smash{-1}}\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\smash{(N)}}\!,\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\smash{(1)}}]
,
and

\displaystyle\left\langle u(\bm{r})\right\rangle_{P}=\frac{\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\ u(\bm{r})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\widetilde{H}^{P}_{\mkern-1.0muN}(\bm{p},\bm{r})}}{\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\widetilde{H}^{P}_{\mkern-1.0muN}(\bm{p},\bm{r})}}

we define

\displaystyle Z_{\xi}

\displaystyle=(2\pi\hbar)^{-Nf}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{p}\int\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\widetilde{H}_{\xi}}

(47a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{\xi}

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\widetilde{H}^{P_{0}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}+\xi\widetilde{H}^{P_{1}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}

(47b)

and use the relation

\displaystyle\Delta F

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}}\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln Z_{\xi}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\int_{0}^{1}\!\left\langle\widetilde{H}^{P_{1}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}-\widetilde{H}^{P_{0}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}\right\rangle_{\!\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(48)

In practice, the integral over
\xi
is approximated using a numerical quadrature
scheme, which prescribes a set of grid points at which to
calculate the integrand. As in previous work,
Mátyus, Wales, and Althorpe 2016
;
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Vaillant, Wales, and Althorpe 2019
;
Vaillant, Wales, and Althorpe 2018

we find that Clenshaw–Curtis (CC) and Gauss–Legendre (GL)
quadrature
Press et al. 2007
both
offer a substantial improvement over simpler schemes, e.g., Simpson’s
rule.
At every grid point, the integrand is in the form of a thermal average
for
a system described by the mixed Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{\xi}
. Most of
the terms in the
average cancel exactly: the only remaining contribution
is the difference of potential energies in

\widetilde{H}^{\smash{P_{1}}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}

and
\widetilde{H}^{\smash{P_{0}}}_{\mkern-1.0muN}
due to the springs
connecting replica 1 and replica
N
.

Having obtained all the necessary averages, we calculate their weighted sum with
the weights prescribed by the quadrature scheme. This yields a
numerical
approximation to the
\xi
-integral in Eq. (
48
) and, hence, a value for

\Delta F
. PIMD simulations at
\xi=0
and
1

produce the prefactor
A
, which combines with
\Delta F

to give us the target ratio
Z^{\smash{J=0}}_{P_{1}}/Z^{\smash{J=0}}_{P_{0}}=A\mkern 2.0mu\mathrm{e}^{\smash{-\beta_{N}\Delta F}}
.
This can be substituted
into Eq. (
11
), to give the desired tunneling splitting
constant.

directly employing these in our calculations,

The entire process consists in running a set of PIMD simulations
(no wavefunction calculations involved). To propagate the dynamics and ensure proper
sampling, we use a multiple-time-step (MTS) integrator combined with an Andersen thermostat.
Tuckerman 2010
;

at intervals of

reported tunneling splittings is
described in Appendix

V
Conclusions

We have presented an exact path-integral method for calculating
ground-state rovibrational tunneling splittings from ratios of
symmetrized partition functions. By construction, splitting values
are the only unknown parameters in our approach, and a rigorous
projection onto the
J=0
state is achieved by means of an Eckart
spring (avoiding the need to integrate over orientations explicitly).
The first feature makes it possible to extract tunneling splittings
from just a single thermodynamic integration, offering
a simplification over the procedure used by Mátyus
and co-workers.
Mátyus, Wales, and Althorpe 2016
;
Mátyus and Althorpe 2016

The second feature means that rotational projection is
performed in an exact and computationally efficient manner,
unlike in previous PIMD studies,
Mátyus, Wales, and Althorpe 2016
;
Vaillant, Wales, and Althorpe 2018
;
Vaillant, Wales, and Althorpe 2019
;
Zhu et al. 2022

such that we can fully account for rovibrational coupling effects.

A perturbatively corrected instanton (RPI+PC)
calculation
Lawrence, Dušek, and Richardson 2023
would likely approach the
accuracy of our PIMD method for the molecular systems
in this study. However, whereas the perturbative correction
will eventually fail, the PIMD calculation should remain robust even
in extremely low-barrier systems, for which large-amplitude motions
correspond to above-barrier
states.
Sarka and Császár 2016
;
Wang, Carter, and Bowman 2015

Additionally, unlike the current formulation of RPI+PC, our method
can be readily extended to
J>0
states, so that in the future
we hope to use Eckart-spring PIMD to study the dependence of
tunneling splitting on the rotational state.
Verhoeve et al. 1989

We should also be able to calculate the energy levels of fluxional
