On the dynamics of grounded shallow ice sheets: Modelling and analysis.
Paolo Piersanti, Roger Temam

Abstract
. In this article we formulate a model describing the evolution of thickness of a grounded shallow ice sheet. The thickness of the ice sheet is constrained to be nonnegative. This renders the problem under consideration an obstacle problem. A rigorous analysis shows that the model is thus governed by a set of variational inequalities that involve nonlinearities in the time derivative and in the elliptic term, and that it admits solutions, whose existence is established by means of a semi-discrete scheme and the penalty method.

1.
Introduction

The study of ice sheets melting and its correlation with the global warming problem has been attracting the interest of experts from all over the branches of science.

In this article we propose and study a mathematical model describing the evolution of the thickness of a grounded shallow ice sheet (from now on, simply
shallow ice sheet
). The ice thickness of a shallow ice sheet evolves as a consequence of many factors like, for instance, the rate at which snow deposits, the rate at which melting occurs, as well as the velocity at which the glacier slides along the lithosphere. Since the ice thickness level is constrained to remain on or above the lithosphere at all times, the problem under consideration can be regarded as a time-dependent obstacle problem.

Grounded ice sheets and sea ice are two related but physically different problems. The mathematical literature related to ice sheets and glaciers and which deals with sea ice and glaciers is vast and abundant; in this direction we mention, for instance, the articles
[
32
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
41
,
51
,
52
]
, and the celebrated article by W.D. Hibler
[
30
]
to which we refer later on, and which deals with sea ice. The local-in-time well-posedness of Hibler’s model has recently been established in the article by Liu, Thomas and Titi
[
40
]
. The authors designed a regularizing scheme based on physical observations for deriving the sought local-in-time well-posedness.

Brandt et al. discussed in
[
8
]
the local-in-time well-posedness of Hibler’s model by means of a different regularization approach. The authors constructed the local strong solutions of the corresponding regularized system by investigating the analytic semi-group property of the parabolic operator of the regularized system in a bounded domain, and they also established the global well-posedness in time for initial data close to constant equilibria.

In this direction, we also mention the recently appeared pre-prints
[
2
,
3
,
7
]
.

In the recent pre-print
[
24
]
, Figalli, Ros-Oton and Serra studied the phase transition of ice melting to water as a Stefan problem. The results established in this article provide a refined understanding of the Stefan problem’s singularities and answer some long-standing open questions in the field of free-boundary problems.

In 2002 Calvo, Díaz, Durany, Schiavi and Vázquez
[
13
]
published a paper discussing the evolution of the thickness of a shallow ice sheet as an obstacle problem. In their article, the authors only considered one spatial direction, they assumed the basal velocity to be smooth, and assumed the lithosphere to be flat. This seminal paper is, to our best knowledge, the very first record in the literature where a sound model describing the evolution of ice sheets thickness in the context of obstacle problems is discussed. This formulation was also exploited to justify the generation of fast ice streams in ice sheets flowing along soft and deformable beds
[
18
]
. In this direction, we also cite the related previous paper
[
17
]
.

Ten years later, in 2012, Jouvet and Bueler
[
34
]
studied the steady (i.e., time-independent) version of the problem considered in
[
13
]
where, this time, two spatial directions and a more general lithosphere topography were taken into account.

It appears that the ice thickness evolution as a time-dependent obstacle problem over a two-dimensional spatial domain has not been addressed in the literature yet.
The purpose of this article is exactly to address this problem under suitable assumptions.

This article is divided into four Sections (including this one). In Section
2
we present the main notations we shall be using throughout the manuscript, and we formally derive the governing equations for our model.

In Section
3
we formulate the “penalized” version corresponding to the obstacle problem introduced in Section
2
, and we establish the existence of solutions to this model by resorting to a series of new preparatory results as well as the Dubinskii’s compactness theorem.

Finally, in Section
4
, we pass to the limit in the penalty parameter and we recover the actual model corresponding to the model we formally derived in Section
2
. This model, for which we also define the rigorous concept of solution, will take the form of a set of variational inequalities. The presence of the constraint, which adds a further nonlinearity to the two already considered (the first nonlinearity appears in the evolutionary term, while the second nonlinearity is the
p
-Laplacian), requires new strategies to be adopted in the limit passage in order to overcome the arising mathematical difficulties. In particular, we will see that vector-valued measures will play a critical role in the analysis.

A

Let

and let

be a generic point in
\overline{\Omega}
. Let
\bm{\nu}
denote the outer unit vector field along the boundary of

. Let

\nabla=\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\right),

\bm{v}=(v_{1},v_{2}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

\nabla\cdot\bm{v}:=\dfrac{\partial v_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+\dfrac{\partial v_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}.

Let

be given and let us consider the time interval

The lithosphere elevation is described by the function
b:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
. Positive values of

are associated with altitudes above the sea level, whereas negative values of
b
are associated with altitudes below the sea level.
We also assume, without loss of generality, that the lithosphere topography does not change throughout the observation time.

The elevation of the upper ice surface is described by the function
h:[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
. It is immediate to observe that

(2.1)

h\geq b\quad\textup{ in }[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}.

The ice thickness
H:=h-b
is thus nonnegative in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
. This consideration implies that the problem of studying the evolution of the sea ice thickness can be regarded as an obstacle problem, where the
obstacle
is represented by the lithosphere.

This constraint implies the existence of a
free boundary

[
9
,
12
,
23
]
. For all, or possibly almost every (a.e. in what follows)
t\in(0,T)
, we define the set
\Omega_{t}^{+}
by:

(2.2)

\Omega_{t}^{+}:=\{x\in\Omega;h(t,x)>b(x)\}=\{x\in\Omega;H(t,x)>0\}.

The set
\Omega_{t}^{+}
denotes the region of
\Omega
which is covered with ice at the time instant
t
. The corresponding free boundary is the set

(2.3)

\Gamma_{f,t}:=\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{t}^{+}.

The variation of the ice thickness
H
is influenced by two source terms: the surface-mass balance
a_{s}
, which is associated with ice accumulation and ablation rate, and the basal melting rate
a_{b}
. The function
a_{b}
is equal to zero when the basal temperature is smaller than the ice melting point; otherwise, it is greater than zero.
The functions
a_{s}
and
a_{b}
, in general, solely depend on the horizontal location
x
and the surface elevation
h=h(t,x)
at time
t\in[0,T]
. The terms
a_{s}
and
a_{b}
can thus be regarded as functions

\displaystyle a_{s}

\displaystyle:[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle a_{b}

\displaystyle:[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}.

In what follows we assume that
a_{b}\equiv 0
.
We define the function
a:[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by:

a:=a_{s},

and we recall that this function is
assumed
to be continuous in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
, strictly positive in a subset of
\Omega_{t}^{+}
(where accumulation occurs), strictly negative in
\Omega_{t}^{-}:=\Omega\setminus(\Omega_{t}^{+}\cup\Gamma_{f,t})
(where ablation occurs), and nonnegative in the complementary region of
\Omega_{t}^{+}
characterised by ablation
[
29
,
34
,
53
]
. Ice sheets are incompressible, non-Newtonian, gravity driven flows
[
25
,
29
]
. Ice flows from areas of
\Omega_{t}^{+}
characterised by accumulation (i.e., regions where
a_{s}>0
) to areas of
\Omega_{t}^{+}
characterised by ablation (i.e., regions where
a_{s}\leq 0
).

Ice thickness is also influenced by the basal sliding velocity
\bm{U}_{b}
, which can be regarded as a given vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
solely depending on the horizontal position, i.e.,

\bm{U}_{b}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}.

and we recall that (cf., e.g.,
[
29
]
), when the ice base is frozen, we have
\bm{U}_{b}=\bm{0}
.

The vector field
\bm{U}:[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the horizontal ice flow velocity; the vector field
\bm{Q}
denotes the
volume flux
, defined as the integral of the horizontal ice flow velocity
\bm{U}
with respect to the vertical direction (cf., e.g., equation (5.47) of
[
29
]
), namely:

(2.4)

\bm{Q}:[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\qquad\textup{ and }\quad\bm{Q}:=\int_{b}^{h}\bm{U}\,\mathrm{d}z.

In the same spirit as Jouvet & Bueler
[
34
]
, we
assume
that for each
t\in(0,T)
there is no volume ice flow towards
\Omega_{t}^{-}
, i.e.,

(2.5)

\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{\nu}=0\quad\textup{ on }\Gamma_{f,t}.

In view of (
2.5
), we can naturally extend the volume flux
\bm{Q}
by zero outside
\Omega_{t}^{+}
, i.e.,

(2.6)

\bm{Q}=\bm{0}\quad\textup{ in }\Omega_{t}^{-}.

Besides, once again in the spirit of
[
34
]
, we have that

(2.7)

H=0\textup{ on }\Gamma_{f,t}\qquad\textup{ and }\quad H=0\textup{ on }\partial\Omega.

The evolution of the ice thickness is governed by the
ice thickness equation
(cf., e.g., equation (5.55) in
[
29
]
), that we recall here below:

(2.8)

\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}+a.

The free boundary
\Gamma_{f,t}
and the region
\Omega_{t}^{+}
are correctly described
only
through a weak formulation of the problem under consideration. Prior to rigorously stating the weak formulation of the problem under consideration, we have to
formally
recover the boundary value problem associated with (
2.8
).
To this aim we
first
fix a
smooth enough
test function
v
such that
v\geq b
in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
and,
second
, we multiply (
2.8
) by
(v-h)
and integrate over
\Omega
. As a result of this manipulation of (
2.8
), the following identity holds for all
t\in(0,T)
:

(2.9)

\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}a(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x.

By virtue of the fact that
h\geq b
in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
, we can specialise
v=h
. The definition of
\Omega_{t}^{-}
in turn implies that
\Omega=\Omega_{t}^{+}\sqcup\Omega_{t}^{-}\cup\Gamma_{f,t}
, where the symbol
\sqcup
denotes the union of two disjoint sets.
An application of (
2.6
) and of the Gauss-Green theorem to (
2.9
) gives

(2.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}a(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}a(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\right)(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}^{-}}\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{\nu}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma-\int_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}\bm{Q}\cdot\nabla(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=0\textup{ by \eqref{outer-flux} and the fact that }\partial\Omega_{t}^{+}=\partial\Omega_{t}^{-}}-\int_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}a(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x.

In order to work out the following step of the boundary value problem recovery, let us recall that
\Omega_{t}^{-}
denotes the region in
\Omega
where the lithosphere is
not
covered with ice, i.e., we have
H=0
in
\Omega_{t}^{-}
. Moreover, the ice thickness
H
in
\Omega_{t}^{-}

either
does not change (
\partial H/\partial t=0
)
or
increases (
\partial H/\partial t>0
); equivalently, the ice thickness
H

cannot

\Omega_{t}^{-}
.

On the one hand, in the region
\Omega_{t}^{+}
the ice thickness evolution is governed by (
2.8
), so that we have

(2.11)

\int_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\right)(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T).

On the other hand, specialising
v=h+\varphi
in (
2.10
), where
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}((0,T)\times\Omega)
,
\varphi\geq 0
in
[0,T]\times\Omega
, recalling that
a\leq 0
in
\Omega_{t}^{-}
, and recalling the remark made above about the nonnegativeness of
\partial H/\partial t

\Omega_{t}^{-}
, we obtain

(2.12)

\int_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}-a\right)\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0,\quad\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T).

Putting together (
2.9
)–(
2.12
) gives

(2.13)

\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\right)\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0,\quad\textup{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{D}((0,T)\times\Omega),\varphi\geq 0\textup{ in }[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}.

The latter can be straightforwardly changed into:

(2.14)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a=0,\quad\textup{ in }\Omega_{t}^{+}=\{x\in\Omega;H(t,x)>0\}\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\geq 0,\quad\textup{ in }\Omega_{t}^{-}=\Omega\setminus(\Omega_{t}^{+}\cup\Gamma_{f,t})\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T).

Define
H_{0}:=h(0)-b
, and observe that
H_{0}\geq 0
by virtue of the observation made at the beginning of this Section (Section
2
). Putting together (
2.7
) and (
2.14
) gives the sought free boundary value problem:
Find

H\geq 0

satisfying:

(2.15)

\begin{cases}&\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a=0,\quad\textup{ in }\Omega_{t}^{+}=\{x\in\Omega;H(t,x)>0\}\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T),\\
\\
&\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\geq 0,\quad\textup{ in }\Omega_{t}^{-}=\Omega\setminus(\Omega_{t}^{+}\cup(\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{t}^{+}))\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T),\\
\\
&H(t,\cdot)=0,\quad\textup{ on }\partial\Omega\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T),\\
\\
&H(0,\cdot)=H_{0}\geq 0.\end{cases}

Multiplying the equation in the boundary value problem (
2.15
) by

, integrating over
(0,T)\times\Omega
, and observing that

in
\Omega_{t}^{-}
gives:

(2.16)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\right)(h-b)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

Let
v=v(t,x)
be any test function such that
v(t,x)\geq b(x)
for a.e.
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega
. Multiplying the equations in the boundary value problem (
2.15
) by
(v-b)
, and integrating over
(0,T)\times\Omega
gives:

(2.17)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\right)(v-b)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0.

Combining (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) gives:

(2.18)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{Q}-a\right)(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0.

The arbitrariness of the test function
v
taken as above finally allows us to write down the weak variational formulation associated with the boundary value problem (
2.15
) (recall that
H:=h-b
):

Find

H\geq 0

satisfying the variational inequalities:

(2.19)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bm{Q}\cdot\nabla(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}a(v-h)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

for all test functions

v

such that

v\geq b

for a.e.

(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega
, a
nd satisfying the following boundary conditions along

\partial\Omega

H=0\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,

and satisfying the following initial condition

H(0,x)=H_{0}(x),\quad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega,

where

H_{0}=h(0)-b

is given, nonnegative and nonzero
(see (

)).

Note that, at least for the time being, the rigorous concept of solution has not been defined yet as we did not specify the regularity of
H
and of the data. This task will be postponed to forthcoming Sections.

Ice is viscous too; its viscosity is described in terms of the Glen power law (cf., e.g., equation (4.16) in
[
29
]
) with ice softness coefficient
A(x,z)
and exponent
2.8\leq p\leq 5
. The attainable values for
p
are suggested by laboratory experiments
[
28
]
. Regarding the ice softness as a function is motivated by the idea of coupling the ice thickness equation with a thermodynamical model
[
29
,
31
]
.

By equation (5.84) in
[
29
]
, the horizontal ice flow velocity
\bm{U}
can be expressed in terms of the elevation of the upper ice surface
h
via the following formula:

(2.20)

\bm{U}(\cdot,\cdot,z)=-2(\rho g)^{p-1}\left(\int_{b}^{z}A(s)(h-s)^{p-1}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)|\nabla h|^{p-2}\nabla h+\bm{U}_{b}.

Observe that, not only does the formula (
2.20
) use Glen’s power law, but also that it is only valid in a certain shallow limit derived from the Stokes model (cf., e.g.,
[
53
]
).

Plugging formula (
2.20
) into (
2.4
) gives:

(2.21)

\displaystyle\bm{Q}

\displaystyle=-2(\rho g)^{p-1}\left(\int_{b}^{h}\int_{b}^{z}A(s)(h-s)^{p-1}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}z\right)|\nabla h|^{p-2}\nabla h+(h-b)\bm{U}_{b}

\displaystyle=-2(\rho g)^{p-1}\left(\int_{b}^{h}A(s)(h-s)^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)|\nabla h|^{p-2}\nabla h+(h-b)\bm{U}_{b},

where the latter equality is obtained by an integration by parts with respect to the variable

.

The weak form (
2.19
) is a degenerate extension of the

-Laplacian obstacle problem since, at each time

, the thickness

.
This is the reason why the solution of (

, viz.

]
and
[

]
.
A further reformulation that recovers nondegenerate

-Laplacian form is proposed next, cf. (

While this formulation involves only a flat obstacle, on the one hand, it acquires a “tilt” which does not preserve monotonicity of the variational form on the other hand.

As already mentioned by Jouvet & Bueler
[
34
]
, the expression of
\bm{Q}
in (

) exhibits a degenerate behaviour at the free boundary, in the sense that the gradient norm power blows up as

gets close to the free boundary of
\Omega
. In this direction, we refer the reader to the article
[
50
]
for a laboratory verification of the boundary degeneracy.

We also observe that the model we are considering follows the formulation originally proposed by J.W. Glen
[
27
]
, according to which ice was regarded as a viscous fluid.
The celebrated model proposed by Hibler, itself inspired by the article of M.D. Coon
[
16
]
, is on the one hand
less precise
than Glen’s model as the ice velocity
\bm{U}
has a simplified expression. On the other hand, it achieves the goal of describing in the same instance the behaviours of ice as a solid and as a fluid.

The operation of averaging considerably simplifies the expression of the ice volume flux
\bm{Q}
, while the coupling of the shallow ice equation for the averaged ice thickness with the mechanical equation spares the effort of expressing the ice flow velocity
\bm{U}
in terms of the ice thickness.

In order to overcome the difficulty arising as a result of the gradient degeneracy in the vicinity of the free boundary, we introduce the following transformation, originally suggested in
[
45
]
(see also
[
13
]
):

(2.22)

H:=u^{(p-1)/2p}.

Observe that
H>0
in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
if and only if
u>0
in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
, and that
H(t,x)=0
if and only if
u(t,x)=0
,
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and
t\in[0,T]
. As a result of the transformation (
2.22
), we
first
obtain, formally,

(2.23)

\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(|u|^{\frac{3p-1}{2p}-2}\,u),

secondly
, we obtain

(2.24)

\int_{b}^{h}A(s)(h-s)^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s=u^{\frac{(p+1)(p-1)}{2p}}\int_{0}^{1}A(x,b+u^{\frac{p-1}{2p}}\,s^{\prime})(1-s^{\prime})^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime},

and,
third
, we obtain:

(2.25)

|\nabla h|^{p-2}\nabla h=\left(\dfrac{p-1}{2p}\right)^{p-1}u^{\frac{(-p-1)(p-2)}{2p}}\left|\nabla u+u^{(p+1)/2p}\nabla b\right|^{p-2}u^{\frac{-p-1}{2p}}\left(\nabla u+u^{(p+1)/2p}\nabla b\right).

For all
x\in\Omega
and all
u=u(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the vector field
\bm{\Phi}:\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by:

(2.26)

\bm{\Phi}=\bm{\Phi}(x,u):=-\dfrac{2p}{p-1}u^{(p+1)/2p}\nabla b.

Plugging (
2.26

(2.27)

|\nabla h|^{p-2}\nabla h=\left(\dfrac{p-1}{2p}\right)^{p-1}u^{\frac{(-p-1)(p-2)}{2p}}\left|\nabla u-\bm{\Phi}\right|^{p-2}u^{\frac{-p-1}{2p}}\left(\nabla u-\bm{\Phi}\right).

Finally, for all
x\in\Omega
and all

, we define the vector field
\bm{\Psi}:\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

(2.28)

\bm{\Psi}=\bm{\Psi}(x,u):=(h-b)\bm{U}_{b},

and the function
\tilde{a}:[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by:

(2.29)

\tilde{a}(t,x,u):=a(t,x,b+u^{(p-1)/2p}).

Inserting (

)–(

(2.30)

-\bm{Q}=2\left(\rho g\dfrac{p-1}{2p}\right)^{p-1}\left[\int_{0}^{1}A(x,b+u^{(p-1)/2p}s^{\prime})(1-s^{\prime})^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right]|\nabla u-\bm{\Phi}|^{p-2}(\nabla u-\bm{\Phi})-\bm{\Psi}.

For the sake of brevity, we define the function
\mu:\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as a function of

(2.31)

\mu(x,u):=2\left(\rho g\dfrac{p-1}{2p}\right)^{p-1}\left[\int_{0}^{1}A(x,b+u^{(p-1)/2p}s^{\prime})(1-s^{\prime})^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right].

Observe that plugging (
2.31

2.30

(2.32)

-\bm{Q}=\mu(x,u)|\nabla u-\bm{\Phi}|^{p-2}(\nabla u-\bm{\Phi})-\bm{\Psi}.

Plugging (

2.29

Find

(2.33)

\begin{cases}&\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(|u|^{\frac{3p-1}{2p}-2}\,u)-\nabla\cdot\left(\mu(x,u)|\nabla u-\bm{\Phi}|^{p-2}(\nabla u-\bm{\Phi})-\bm{\Psi}\right)=\tilde{a},\textup{ in }\Omega_{t}^{+}\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T),\\
\\
&\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(|u|^{\frac{3p-1}{2p}-2}\,u)-\nabla\cdot\left(\mu(x,u)|\nabla u-\bm{\Phi}|^{p-2}(\nabla u-\bm{\Phi})-\bm{\Psi}\right)\geq\tilde{a},\textup{ in }\Omega_{t}^{-}\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T),\\
\\
&u(t,\cdot)=0,\quad\textup{ on }\partial\Omega\textup{ for all }t\in(0,T),\\
\\
&u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}:=H_{0}^{(2p/(p-1))}\geq 0.\end{cases}

Similarly to (

Find

u\geq 0

(2.34)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(|u|^{\frac{3p-1}{2p}-2}\,u)\right)(v-u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mu(x,u)|\nabla u-\bm{\Phi}|^{p-2}(\nabla u-\bm{\Phi})\cdot\nabla(v-u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{a}(v-u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bm{\Psi}\cdot\nabla(v-u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

for all test functions

v\geq b

for a.e.

u=0\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,

and satisfying the following initial condition

Let us now establish some preparatory results. The first result collects some properties of the negative part operator.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
m\geq 1
an integer, be an open set. The operator
-\{\cdot\}^{-}:L^{2}(\mathscr{O})\to L^{2}(\mathscr{O})
defined by

f\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O})\mapsto-\{f\}^{-}:=\min\{f,0\}\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}),

is monotone, bounded and Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant equal to
1
.

Proof.

Let
f
and
g
be arbitrarily given in
L^{2}(\mathscr{O})
. In what follows, sets of the form
\{f\geq 0\}
read

\{x\in\mathscr{O};f(x)\geq 0\}.

Recall that the negative part of a function is also given by

f^{-}=\dfrac{|f|-f}{2}.

We have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{O}}\left\{(-\{f\}^{-})-(-\{g\}^{-})\right\}(f-g)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathscr{O}}|-\{f\}^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathscr{O}}|-\{g\}^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{\{f\leq 0\}\cap\{g\leq 0\}}(-\{f\}^{-})(-\{g\}^{-})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\{f\leq 0\}\cap\{g\leq 0\}}\left|(-\{f\}^{-})-(-\{g\}^{-})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0,

and the monotonicity is thus established.

Let us show that the operator under consideration is bounded, in the sense that it maps bounded sets onto bounded sets. Let
\mathscr{F}\subset L^{2}(\mathscr{O})
be bounded. By Hölder’s inequality, we have that for each
f\in\mathscr{F}
,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O})\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left|\int_{\mathscr{O}}(-\{f\}^{-})v\,\mathrm{d}x\right|}{\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})},

and the boundedness of
\mathscr{F}
implies the boundedness of the supremum. The boundedness property is thus established.

Finally, to establish the Lipschitz continuity property, evaluate

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathscr{O}}|(-\{f\}^{-})-(-\{g\}^{-})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}=\left(\int_{\mathscr{O}}\left|\dfrac{f-|f|}{2}-\dfrac{g-|g|}{2}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathscr{O}}|(f-g)-(|f|-|g|)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\{\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})}+\||f|-|g|\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})}\}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathscr{O}}\left||f|-|g|\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathscr{O}}|f-g|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})},

where the inequality holds thanks to the Minkowski inequality. In conclusion, we have shown that

\|(-\{f\}^{-})-(-\{g\}^{-})\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})}\leq\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{O})},

and the arbitrariness of
f
and
g
gives the desired Lipschitz continuity property. This completes the proof.
∎

The following lemma presents an inequality that was originally proved in 1973 by Pedersen in the context of
C^{\ast}
-algebras (cf., e.g., Proposition 1.3.8 of
[
43
]
) to show that the mapping
t\mapsto t^{\beta}
, with
0<\beta<1
, is monotone, where
t
here denotes a generic operator. This inequality plays a crucial role in the forthcoming analysis. We hereby provide an alternate elementary proof proof which solely makes use of convex analysis in the special case where
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
0<\beta\leq 1
. Then

\left||x|^{\beta}-|y|^{\beta}\right|\leq|x-y|^{\beta},\quad\textup{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

If
\beta=1
or at least one between
x
and
y
is equal to zero then the conclusion is trivially true.
So, let us consider the case where
0<\beta<1
, and assume, without loss of generality, that
|x|\geq|y|>0
, and let

t:=\dfrac{|y|}{|x|}\in[0,1].

Let us first show that

1-t^{\beta}\leq(1-t)^{\beta},\quad\textup{ for all }t\in[0,1].

Since
0<\beta\leq 1
and since
t\in[0,1]
, we have that
t^{\beta}\geq t
. Therefore, we have that two consecutive applications of this estimate lead to

(1-t)^{\beta}\geq 1-t\geq 1-t^{\beta},

thus proving the inequality:

\left||x|^{\beta}-|y|^{\beta}\right|\leq\left||x|-|y|\right|^{\beta},\quad\textup{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

An application of the triangle inequality to the right-hand side of the estimate above and the monotonicity of the mapping
\xi\geq 0\mapsto\xi^{\beta}\geq 0
lead to the sought inequality and completes the proof.
∎

We hereby provide a complementary result to Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\sigma>1
. Then:

|x-y|^{\sigma}\leq\left|x^{\sigma}-y^{\sigma}\right|,\quad\textup{ for all }x,y\geq 0.

Proof.

Assume, without loss of generality, that
x\geq y>0
, and let

t:=\dfrac{x}{y}\geq 1.

Therefore, the sought inequality is equivalent to proving that

(t-1)^{\sigma}\leq t^{\sigma}-1,\quad\textup{ for all }t\geq 1.

Consider the function
f:[1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(t):=(t-1)^{\sigma}-t^{\sigma}+1.

Observe that

f^{\prime}(t)=\sigma(t-1)^{\sigma-1}-\sigma t^{\sigma-1}=\sigma((t-1)^{\sigma-1}-t^{\sigma-1}).

Since
\sigma-1>0
and since
0\leq t-1<t
, the monotonicity of the power operator gives that
(t-1)^{\sigma-1}\leq t^{\sigma-1}
, so that
f^{\prime}(t)\leq 0
for all
t>1
. Since
f(1)=0
, we infer that
f(t)\leq 0
for all
t\geq 1
and the proof is complete.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
3.3
, we can establish the following result, thanks to which we will be able to define a sound initial condition for the variational formulation we will be considering.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
T>0
be given, let
1<\sigma<2
, and let
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
be such that:

\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(|u|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u\right)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Then, we have that
\left(|u|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u\right)\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Observe that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left||u|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since it was assumed that
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
, then the latter integral is finite. Hence, combining the latter with the assumption on the distributional derivative in time gives

\left(|u|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u\right)\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)).

To show that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
, consider the function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

h(x)=|x|^{\frac{2-\sigma}{\sigma}}x,\quad\textup{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Clearly, the fact that
1<\sigma<2
implies that
h(0)=0
, or equivalently, that
x=0
is a removable discontinuity for
h
.
The Nemytskii’s operator associated with the function
h
clearly satisfies the assumptions of Theorem 1.27 in
[
47
]
and we thus have that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
as it was to be proved.
∎

It is actually possible to establish that
|u|\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
by resorting to a technique independent of the critical continuity theorem for Nemytskii’s operators.
Such a technique will be exploited to establish the uniform convergence in Lemma
3.6
.
We have to show that for each
t_{0}\in[0,T]
,

\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{\Omega}||u(t)|-|u(t_{0})||^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

By Lemma
3.3
, we have that the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the triangle inequality give

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}||u(t)|-|u(t_{0})||^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\left||u(t)|^{\sigma}-|u(t_{0})|^{\sigma}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\left|\left||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)\right|^{2/\sigma}\right|^{\sigma}-\left|\left||u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right|^{2/\sigma}\right|^{\sigma}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\left||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)\right|^{2}-\left||u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right|^{2}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\left(\left||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)\right|-\left||u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right|\right)\cdot\left(\left||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)\right|+\left||u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right|\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)\right|-\left||u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right|\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\left||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)\right|+\left||u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right|\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)-|u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\left||u(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t)\right|+\left||u(t_{0})|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u(t_{0})\right|\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Observe that the continuity in
[0,T]
of
\left(|u|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u\right)
implies the uniform boundedness of the second factor, as well as that the first factor tends to zero as
t\to t_{0}
.

The next lemma establishes that the supremum can be interchanged with a monotonically increasing continuous extended-real-valued function.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a monotonically increasing and continuous function. Then, given any
S\subset\mathbb{R}
,

\sup_{x\in S}f(x)=f(\sup S).

Proof.

By the definition of supremum, we can find a maximizing sequence
\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset S
for which

s_{k}\to\sup S,\quad\textup{ as }k\to\infty.

By the assumed continuity of
f
, we have that

\lim_{k\to\infty}f(s_{k})=f(\sup S).

Since
f(s_{k})\leq\sup_{x\in S}f(x)
then the inequality
f(\sup S)\leq\sup_{x\in S}f(x)
is obviously true, on the one hand. On the other hand, since
f
is monotone, we have that

f(x)\leq f(\sup S),\quad\textup{ for all }x\in S.

Therefore, passing to the supremum on the left-hand side, we obtain that

\sup_{x\in S}f(x)\leq\sup_{x\in S}f(\sup S)=f(\sup S),

and the proof is complete.
∎

The next lemma establishes a convergence property for sequences of functions enjoying the regularities announced in Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
T>0
be given, let
1<\sigma<2
.
Let
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset L^{\infty}(0,T;W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))
be such that:

\left\{|u_{k}|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\textup{ strongly converges in }\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)).

Then
\{|u_{k}|\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
strongly converges in
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Since the space
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
is complete, it suffices to show that the sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\sigma}(\Omega))
, i.e., we have to show that:

(3.4)

\lim_{k,s\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{\Omega}||u_{k}(t)|-|u_{s}(t)||^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\sigma}=0.

Using Lemma
3.3
, Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
3.5
, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{\Omega}||u_{k}(t)|-|u_{s}(t)||^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\sigma}\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left||u_{k}(t)|^{\sigma}-|u_{s}(t)|^{\sigma}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|\left||u_{k}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}(t)\right|^{2}-\left||u_{s}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{s}(t)\right|^{2}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg\{\left(\int_{\Omega}\left||u_{k}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}(t)-|u_{s}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{s}(t)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega}\left||u_{k}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}(t)+|u_{s}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{s}(t)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\bigg\}^{1/\sigma}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left[\left\||u_{k}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}(t)-|u_{s}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{s}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\||u_{k}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}(t)+|u_{s}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{s}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]\right\}^{1/\sigma}.

The assumed strong convergence implies that the second factor is uniformly bounded with respect to
t\in[0,T]
. Hence, the latter term is less or equal than:

\displaystyle\bigg\{\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\||u_{k}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}(t)-|u_{s}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{s}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\||u_{k}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{k}(t)+|u_{s}(t)|^{\frac{\sigma-2}{2}}u_{s}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]\bigg\}^{1/\sigma}.

Since the second factor is uniformly bounded with respect to
t\in[0,T]
and since the first factor tends to zero as
k,s\to\infty
by the assumed strong convergence, we finally obtain the sought convergence (
3.4
). This completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma establishes an immersion that will be used in the proof of the existence of the solution.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
T>0
be given and let
X
be a normed vector space with the Radon-Nikodym property (cf., e.g.,
[
49
]
).
Let
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle
denote the duality between
\left(\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)\right)^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)
, and let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the duality between
X^{\ast}
and
X
.

The mapping

\Upsilon:L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})\to\left(\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)\right)^{\ast}

defined by

\langle\langle\Upsilon u,v\rangle\rangle:=\int_{0}^{T}\langle u,v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\textup{ where }u\in L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})\textup{ and }v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X),

is linear, continuous and injective. Besides, if
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is bounded in
L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})
then
\{\Upsilon u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is bounded in
\left(\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)\right)^{\ast}
.

Proof.

Let us check that
\Upsilon
is linear. For each
u_{1},u_{2}\in L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})
we have that

\displaystyle\langle\langle\Upsilon(u_{1}+u_{2}),v\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{1}+u_{2},v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{1},v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{2},v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\langle\langle\Upsilon u_{1},v\rangle\rangle+\langle\langle\Upsilon u_{2},v\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle\Upsilon u_{1}+\Upsilon u_{2},v\rangle\rangle,

for all
v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)
.

Similarly, for each
\alpha\neq 0
(for
\alpha=0
the conclusion is immediate) and each
u\in L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})
, we have that

\langle\langle\Upsilon(\alpha u),v\rangle\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\alpha\langle u,v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=\langle\langle\Upsilon u,\alpha v\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle\alpha\Upsilon u,v\rangle\rangle,

for all
v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)
, and the sought linearity property is thus proved.

To show the continuity of the mapping
\Upsilon
, let
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be such that
u_{k}\to u
in
L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})
. By Hölder’s inequality in Lebesgue-Bochner spaces (cf., e.g.,
[
56
]
), and the fact that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;X)}
coincides with
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)}
along the elements of the space
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)
, we have that:

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left|\langle\langle\Upsilon u_{k}-\Upsilon u,v\rangle\rangle\right|}{\|v\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)}}\leq\dfrac{\int_{0}^{T}|\langle u_{k}-u,v\rangle|\,\mathrm{d}t}{\|v\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)}}\leq\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})}\to 0\textup{ as }k\to\infty.

The boundedness of the mapping
\Upsilon
is thus a direct consequence of the linearity and the continuity.

To prove that
\Upsilon
is injective, assume that
\Upsilon u_{1}=\Upsilon u_{2}
in
\left(\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)\right)^{\ast}
then, for each
v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X)
, we have that

\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{1}-u_{2},v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

In particular, consider functions of the form
v(t):=\varphi(t)w
, with
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(0,T)
and
w\in X
. Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(0,T)
and let
w\in X
vary arbitrarily. Since
X
has the Radon-Nikodym property, we have that (cf., e.g., Theorem 8.13 of
[
37
]
) the latter becomes

0=\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{1}-u_{2},v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=\left\langle\int_{0}^{T}(u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t))\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,w\right\rangle,\quad\textup{ for all }w\in X.

This in turn implies that

\int_{0}^{T}(u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t))\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0\textup{ in }X^{\ast}.

Since this conclusion is independent of the choice of
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(0,T)
, an application of the Fundamental Lemma of the Calculus of Variations (cf., e.g.,
[
56
]
) gives that
u_{1}=u_{2}
in
L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})
and the injectivity is thus proved.

∎

The conclusion of Lemma
3.7
is that the space
L^{1}(0,T;X^{\ast})
can be identified with a subspace of
(\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];X))^{\ast}
.

Problem

4.
Existence of weak solutions and variational formulation for Problem (

)

The recovery of the variational formulation for Problem (
2.33

We first establish the existence of solutions for Problem

ℓ

by means of a semi-discrete scheme. For each

, consider the semi-discrete problem:

Problem

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{k}\{|u_{\ell,k}^{n+1}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell,k}^{n+1}-|u_{\ell,k}^{n}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell,k}^{n}\}-\nabla\cdot\left(\mu|\nabla u_{\ell,k}^{n+1}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell,k}^{n+1}\right)-\dfrac{\{u_{\ell,k}^{n+1}\}^{-}}{\ell}

is the prescribed element appearing in Problem

\blacksquare

The following existence-and-uniqueness result can be established.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T>0
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
p

\alpha

Proof.

For the sake of brevity, define

(4.2)

\tilde{a}_{k}^{n}:=\dfrac{1}{k}\int_{nk}^{(n+1)k}\tilde{a}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Consider the operator
A_{\ell}:W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\to W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)
defined by

(4.3)

Consider the operator

The operators

By Lemma

and Lemma

Finally, an application of the Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality and the monotonicity of the negative part operator (Lemma

\dfrac{\langle A_{\ell}v,v\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}}{\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}}\geq\dfrac{\|v\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}+\mu_{1}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}}{\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}}\geq\mu_{1}c_{0}\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{p-1},

which finally implies that
u_{\ell}
is a solution of Problem

P
ℓ

. This completes the proof.
∎

The next step, which constitutes the final step of the existence result we want to prove, is the passage to the limit as
\ell\to 0^{+}
as well as the recovery of the actual model governing the variation of shallow ice sheets in time.

In all what follows, we exploit the
p
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, namely,

|x|:=\left(|x_{1}|^{p}+|x_{2}|^{p}\right)^{1/p},\quad\textup{ for all }x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The estimates (
4.6
), (
4.9
), (
4.11
), (
4.12
) and Theorem
4.3
imply that there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\ell
such that

(4.50)

\displaystyle\|u_{\ell}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

\displaystyle\left\||u_{\ell}|^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}u_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

\displaystyle\left\|\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(|u_{\ell}|^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}u_{\ell})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

\displaystyle\left\||u_{\ell}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

\displaystyle\left\|\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(|u_{\ell}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\ell}\right).

By the Banach-Alaoglu-Bourbaki theorem (cf., e.g., Theorem 3.6 of
[
10
]
) we infer that, up to passing to a subsequence still denoted by
\{u_{\ell}\}_{\ell>0}
, the following convergences hold:

(4.51)

\displaystyle u_{\ell}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}u,

\displaystyle\textup{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle|u_{\ell}|^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}u_{\ell}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v,

\displaystyle\textup{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(|u_{\ell}|^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}u_{\ell}\right)\rightharpoonup\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}v}{\,\mathrm{d}t},

\displaystyle\textup{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle|u_{\ell}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}w,

\displaystyle\textup{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Omega)).

Observe that the first convergence of (
4.51
), namely
u_{\ell}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}u
in

, implies that

u_{\ell}\rightharpoonup u,\quad\textup{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\simeq L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)\textup{ as }\ell\to 0^{+}.

The third estimate in (
4.13
) imply that
-\{u_{\ell}\}^{-}\to 0
in

.
Therefore, the continuity and the monotonicity of the negative part established in Lemma

allow us to apply Theorem 9.13-2 of
[

, getting:

(4.52)

\{u\}^{-}=0\quad\textup{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

which means that
u(t)\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
, for a.e.

.

Using the same compactness argument as in Theorem
4.3
, an application of Dubinskii’s theorem (Lemma

) with
A_{0}:=L^{\frac{2p}{\alpha}}(\Omega)
,
A_{1}:=L^{2}(\Omega)
,
q_{0}=\infty
and

gives that:

\left\{|u_{\ell}|^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}u_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell>0}\textup{ strongly converges in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\frac{2p}{\alpha}}(\Omega)).

Moreover, by Lemma
3.10
(a), it can be established that:

(4.53)

\left\{|u_{\ell}|^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}u_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell>0}\textup{ strongly converges in }\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)).

The same monotonicity argument as in Theorem
4.3
and the third convergence in (

) in turn imply that the limit
v
in (

) takes the following form:

v=|u|^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}u\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

The first convergence in (
4.51
) gives that
u_{\ell}\rightharpoonup u
in
L^{2}(0,T;W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))
. Let us now show that:

(4.54)

|u_{\ell}|\rightharpoonup u,\quad\textup{ in }L^{2}(0,T;W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)).

Note that, thanks to Stampacchia’s theorem
[

makes sense.
To prove that this convergence holds, fix any

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle v,|u_{\ell}|-u\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle v,\{u_{\ell}\}^{+}+\{u_{\ell}\}^{-}-u\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle v,u_{\ell}-u\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle v,\{u_{\ell}\}^{-}\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\to 0,\quad\textup{ as }\ell\to 0^{+},

since the first term converges to zero by the first convergence in (
4.51
) and the second term converges to zero thanks to (

). The convergence (
4.54
) is thus established.
An application of Lemma

\left\{|u_{\ell}|\right\}_{\ell>0}\textup{ strongly converges in }\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\alpha}(\Omega)),

. Combining this with the convergences (

) and (

|u_{\ell}|\to u,\quad\textup{ in }\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\alpha}(\Omega)).

Define the linear and continuous operator

by:

L_{0}(v):=v(0),\quad\textup{ for all }v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];L^{\alpha}(\Omega)).

By (

, we have that

in

. However, since

, we immediately deduce that

and so that the weak-star limit

Note that the variational equation in Problem

ℓ

takes the following equivalent form:

(4.56)

\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(|u_{\ell}(t)|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}(t))-\nabla\cdot\left(\mu|\nabla u_{\ell}(t)|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell}(t)\right)-\dfrac{1}{\ell}\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}=\tilde{a}(t),\quad\textup{ in }W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega).

Integrating (

) in

gives:

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{T}\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{a}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu|\nabla u_{\ell}(t)|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell}(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(|u_{\ell}(t)|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{a}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu|\nabla u_{\ell}(t)|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell}(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t-\left[|u_{\ell}(T)|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}(T)-|u_{0}|^{\alpha-2}u_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{a}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu|\nabla u_{\ell}(t)|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell}(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t-\left[|u_{\ell}(T)|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}(T)-u_{0}^{\alpha-1}\right],\quad\textup{ in }W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),

where the last equality holds since

by assumption.

Let
v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
be arbitrarily chosen satisfying

. By Stampacchia’s theorem (cf. the seminal article
[
55
]
) we have that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\ell}\left|\left\langle\int_{0}^{T}\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}\,\mathrm{d}t,v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\leq\left|\left\langle\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{a}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\left\langle\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu|\nabla u_{\ell}(t)|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell}(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|

\displaystyle+\left\||u_{\ell}(T)|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}(T)\right\|_{L^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}+\|u_{0}^{\alpha-1}\|_{L^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{a}\|_{W^{1,p}(0,T;\mathcal{C}^{0}(\overline{\Omega}))}+T\left\|\nabla\cdot\left(\mu|\nabla u_{\ell}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega))}+\|u_{\ell}(T)\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha-1}+\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha-1}.

Thanks to Lemma
3.6
, the boundedness of
\mu
, and the boundedness of the

\ell
. By the arbitrariness of

with

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\dfrac{1}{\ell}\left|\left\langle\int_{0}^{T}\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}\,\mathrm{d}t,v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\leq C,\quad\textup{ for all }\ell>0,

for some
C>0
independent of
\ell
or, equivalently,

(4.57)

\left\|\dfrac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{T}\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq C,\quad\textup{ for all }\ell>0,

for some
C>0
independent of
\ell
. By Bochner’s theorem (cf., e.g., Theorem 8.9 of
[
37
]
), we have that the following inequality is always true:

\left\|\dfrac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{T}\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq\dfrac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{T}\|\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}\|_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

We now show that the inverse inequality holds true too. Observe that, by Stampacchia’s theorem, we have that

, so that
\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}\geq 0
in
\overline{\Omega}
, for a.e.

. Therefore, we have:

for a.e.

. Therefore, for a.e.

\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\left|\langle\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-},v\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|

is attained for functions

\overline{\Omega}
, or less or equal than zero in
\overline{\Omega}
. In view of this remark, we have that:

(4.58)

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\\
v\geq 0\textup{ in }\overline{\Omega}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t,

so that the last two inequalities in (

Let us now show that:

(4.59)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Let
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
,
\|v_{k}\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1
be such that the following convergence in
\mathbb{R}
holds

(4.60)

\lim_{j\to\infty}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v_{j}\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|,

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Observe that, for each

, we always have:

(4.61)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v_{j}\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since the convergence in (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v_{j}\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
\|v\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left\langle\dfrac{\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}}{\ell},v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(|u_{\ell}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}\right)\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\tilde{w}_{t},\quad\textup{ in }\mathcal{M}([0,T];W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega))\textup{ as }\ell\to 0^{+}.

It can be observed that the vector-valued measure

appearing in (

\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(|u|^{\alpha-2}u\right).

To see this, let

(4.67)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\tilde{w}_{t},\varphi v\rangle\rangle_{\mathcal{M}([0,T];W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)),\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(|u_{\ell}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}\right),v\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\ell\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(|u_{\ell}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}\right)v\,\mathrm{d}x\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(|u|^{\alpha-2}u\right)v\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Keeping the convergence (

|u_{\ell}|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}\to w=|u|^{\alpha-2}u,\quad\textup{ in }L^{(\alpha^{\prime}-1)p}(0,T;L^{(\alpha^{\prime}-1)p}(\Omega)),

and

Thanks to Lemma

and the Dinculeanu-Zinger theorem (Lemma

Thanks to Lemma

(4.69)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(|u_{\ell}(t)|^{\alpha-2}u_{\ell}(t)\right),u_{\ell}(t)\right\rangle_{W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega),W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t=\dfrac{\|u_{\ell}(T)\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}}{\alpha^{\prime}}-\dfrac{\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}}{\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\to\dfrac{\|u(T)\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}}{\alpha^{\prime}}-\dfrac{\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega)}^{\alpha}}{\alpha^{\prime}},\quad\textup{ as }\ell\to 0^{+}.

Specializing

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mu(x)|\nabla u_{\ell}(t)|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\ell}(t)\cdot\nabla(u_{\ell}(t)-u(t))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\{u_{\ell}(t)\}^{-}(u_{\ell}(t)-u(t))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{a}(t)(u_{\ell}(t)-u(t))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

4.51

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u(t))|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{i}(u(t))\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|(\widetilde{\partial_{i}u})(t)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\ell\to 0^{+}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|(\widetilde{\partial_{i}u_{\ell}})(t)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)=\liminf_{\ell\to 0^{+}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{i}(u_{\ell}(t))\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)

where the last equality is due to the fact that the assumption

Conclusions

In this article we formulated a time-dependent model governing the evolution of the thickness of a shallow grounded ice sheet undergoing regimes of melting and ablation. The shallow ice sheet varying height, that is the unknown the model is described in terms of, is subjected to obey the constraint of being nonnegative. For this reason, the problem under consideration can be regarded as an obstacle problem.

First, we recovered the formal model, based on Glen’s power law. This passage was inspired by the modelling for the static case of Jouvet & Bueler
[
34
]
.

Second, we incorporated the constraint, in the form of a monotone term, in the model. By so doing it was possible to write down the variational formulation of the model under consideration in terms of a variational equation posed over a vector space.
The existence of solutions for the penalized model was established thanks to the Dubinskii compactness theorem and a series of preliminary results, that we stated in the form of Lemmas.

Third, we let the penalty parameter approach zero and we recovered the actual model, which takes the form of a variational inequality tested over a nonempty, closed, and convex subset of the space
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))
.

It is worth mentioning that the concept of solution we are considering in this paper is different from the one discussed in Definition 3.1 on page 688 of
[
13
]
. In our context, the modelling did not lead us to consider the analogue of the function
j
appearing in Definition 3.1 on page 688 of
[
13
]
, and we did not require our weak solutions to satisfy the identity indicated in Definition 3.1 on page 688 of
[
13
]
. Therefore, differently from
[
13
]
, the proof of the existence result for the concept of solution we considered in this paper required the exploitation of a compactness argument based on the Dubinskii’s compactness lemma as well as several other brand new preparatory inequalities.
