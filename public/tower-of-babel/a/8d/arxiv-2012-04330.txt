New classes of minimal knot diagrams Thanks: This work was supported by the Russian Science Foundation (project no. 19-11-00151)
Ilya Alekseev

Abstract
. We describe a new class of minimal link diagrams. This class includes certain alternating diagrams, the standard diagrams of all torus links, and numerous homogeneous diagrams whose minimality has not been proven before. Besides, we describe a new larger class of link diagrams with the least number of Seifert circles among all diagrams of a given link. Our approach refers to the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality.



1.
Introduction

The central concepts of the present paper are minimal link diagrams and the crossing number of links.
A diagram
\mathcal{D}
of a link
\mathcal{L}
is said to be
minimal
if
\mathcal{D}
has the least number of crossings among all diagrams of
\mathcal{L}
. The number of crossings of a minimal diagram of
\mathcal{L}
is called the
crossing number
of
\mathcal{L}
.

In this paper, we assume that all link diagrams are oriented, and for standard definitions, we mostly use the conventions of
[
Cr04
]
.

At this moment, several remarkable phenomena were discovered concerning minimal diagrams. In particular, certain easy to verify visual properties of link diagrams guarantee the minimality. The examples include reduced alternating diagrams, adequate diagrams, reduced Montesinos diagrams, and closures of certain positive braid diagrams due to J. Gonzàlez-Meneses and P. M. G. Manchòn.

In the present paper, we introduce
locally twisted
link diagrams, which extend the above list. To state our main result, we require an additional notion. In
[
Cr89
]
, P. R. Cromwell introduces the class of
homogeneous
link diagrams, which contains both alternating and positive ones, and proposes studying the crossing number of the corresponding links. We provide a sufficient condition of minimality for homogeneous diagrams.

Theorem 1.1
.

All locally twisted homogeneous link diagrams are minimal.

To define locally twisted diagrams, we introduce link templates. To be precise, these diagrams involve specific templates, referred to as
knitted
.
The idea of the templates is that one can obtain each link diagram by arranging several braid diagrams on the plane and connecting them appropriately.
In this paper, we consider homogeneous braid diagrams.
One divides each such diagram into
uniform layers
.
Roughly speaking, a link diagram is locally twisted if it admits a knitted template such that each of the uniform layers of the corresponding braid diagrams is twisted enough in a certain sense.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we establish
a more general result (Theorem
1.2
), which refers to the notion of Seifert circles.

We say that a diagram
\mathcal{D}
of a link
\mathcal{L}
is
optimal
if
\mathcal{D}
has the least number of Seifert circles among all diagrams of
\mathcal{L}
. The number of Seifert circles of an optimal diagram of
\mathcal{L}
equals the braid index of
\mathcal{L}
(see
[
Yam87
, Theorem 3]
).

A well-known inequality referred to as the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality relates the breadth of the skein polynomial of the link given by a diagram
\mathcal{D}
with the number of Seifert circles of
\mathcal{D}
. If the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality is sharp for
\mathcal{D}
, then
\mathcal{D}
is optimal.

Theorem 1.2
.

The Morton–Franks–Williams inequality is sharp for all locally twisted link diagrams.

Corollary 1.3
.

All locally twisted link diagrams are optimal.

We deduce several results related to Theorem
1.1
. We say that a link is
locally twisted
(resp.
locally twisted homogeneous
) if it admits a locally twisted (resp. locally twisted homogeneous) diagram.

A well-known conjecture states that the crossing number of all connected sums of two links equals the sum of the crossing numbers of these links (see
[
Kir97
, Problem 1.65]
,
[
La09
]
, and
[
Ad94
, p. 69]
). Equivalently, all connected sums of minimal link diagrams are minimal. This conjecture is still open. Since all connected sums of adequate diagrams are adequate, the conjecture holds for adequate links. It is easy to check that all connected sums of locally twisted homogeneous links admit locally twisted homogeneous diagrams. Therefore, Theorem
1.1
implies the following result.

Corollary 1.4
.

The conjecture that the crossing number is additive under connected sums holds for all locally twisted homogeneous links in the sense that all connected sums of locally twisted homogeneous diagrams are minimal.

Following
[
Mal18
]
, we say that a minimal diagram
\mathcal{D}
is

1
-regular
if for any diagram
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, the crossing numbers of links determined by connected sums of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
are bounded from below by the number of crossings of
\mathcal{D}
.
If the conjecture on the additivity of crossing number holds,
then any minimal link diagram is
1
-regular. By using properties of the Kauffman polynomial, one can show that all adequate diagrams are
1
-regular (see
[
B+20
, Theorem 1]
). Also, all minimal diagrams of torus links are
1
-regular (see
[
Di04
, Theorem 3.8]
). In
[
Di04
, Theorem 3.8]
, the author gives a sufficient condition on a link diagram to be
1
-regular. It follows from
[
Cr89
, Corollary 4.1]
that all optimal homogeneous diagrams satisfy this condition. Therefore, Corollary
1.3
implies the following result.

Corollary 1.5
.

All locally twisted homogeneous diagrams are
1
-regular.

A braid diagram is said to be
minimal
if it has the least number of crossings among all braid diagrams that represent the same braid. A problem due to J. Stallings (see
[
Kir97
, Problem 1.8]
) asks whether words in the standard Artin generators (and their inverses) corresponding to minimal braid diagrams are closed under end extension (replacing a final letter
s
by
ss
). We propose a natural analog of this conjecture for minimal link diagrams.

The link diagram transformations of the form

\mapsto

and

\mapsto

are referred to as the
doubling of a crossing
.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an oriented link diagram. Let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be a diagram obtained from
\mathcal{D}
by the doubling of a crossing. If
\mathcal{D}
is minimal, then
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is minimal too.

It follows by definition that all diagrams obtained from adequate ones by the doubling of a crossing are adequate. Therefore, Conjecture
1.6
holds for them. It is easy to check that all diagrams obtained from locally twisted homogeneous ones by the doubling of a crossing are locally twisted homogeneous. Therefore, Theorem
1.1
implies the following result.

Corollary 1.7
.

Conjecture
1.6
holds for all locally twisted homogeneous link diagrams in the sense that all link diagrams obtained from locally twisted homogeneous ones by the doubling of a crossing are minimal.

1.1.
Related results

We list several results related to Theorem
1.2
.

If a link diagram
\mathcal{D}
has two Seifert circles that share precisely one crossing, then
\mathcal{D}
is not optimal. Namely, one can apply a transformation that reduces the number of Seifert circles. This transformation is referred to as the
Murasugi–Przytycki move
(see
[
St13
, Definition 2.1]
). In
[
DHL19
, Theorem 1.1]
, the authors show that
if an alternating diagram
\mathcal{D}
has no pair of Seifert circles that share precisely one crossing,
then the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality is sharp for
\mathcal{D}
. Thus, they characterize those alternating diagrams
that are optimal. Many, but not all, optimal alternating diagrams admit flypes (see below) that transform them into locally twisted ones.

All prime knots up to
10
crossings except
9_{42}
,
9_{49}
,
10_{132}
,
10_{150}
, and
10_{156}
admit diagrams for which the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality is sharp
(see
[
Ad94
, p. 174]
).
The same is true for the closures of all positive braids with
at most
3
strands (see
[
Nak04
, Proposition 3.1]
), fibered alternating links (see
[
Mur91
, Theorem A]
), and rational links (see
[
Mur91
, Theorem B]
).

Another class of link diagrams for which the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality is sharp is that of the closures of all positive braid diagrams with the full twist
(see
[
FW87
, Corollary 2.4]
,
[
Cr04
, Theorem 10.5.1]
,
[
GM14
, Corollary 4.5]
,
[
Kal09
, Theorem 1.3]
, and
[
FK17
, Corollary 1.4]
).
For example, it follows by definition that standard diagrams of all torus links lie within this class.
In
[
Nak20
]
, the author generalizes the braid closure construction and introduces
knitted diagrams
, which are similar to the knitted templates defined below.
It follows from
[
Nak20
, Theorem 2.4]
that
the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality is sharp for all positive knitted diagrams with the full twists.
It turns out that a positive link diagram is knitted if and only if it is locally twisted.

We recall the definition of certain braid diagrams introduced by J. Gonzàlez-Meneses and P. M. G. Manchòn in
[
GM14
]
.
We refer to these braid diagrams as
GMM
ones.
By definition,
a braid diagram is a GMM diagram if and only if the corresponding word in the alphabet of the standard Artin generators
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots\}
is obtained from the empty word by a finite sequence of transformations of the following types:

(1)

for some
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
, inserting
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i}
;

(2)

for some
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
, doubling a letter
\sigma_{i}
;

(3)

applying either the braid relation or the far commutativity one.

In
[
GM14
, Corollary 4.3]
, the authors prove that given a positive braid diagram
\mathcal{V}
, the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality is sharp for
the closure of
\mathcal{V}
if and only if
\mathcal{V}
is GMM. In particular, the closures of all GMM braid diagrams are both optimal and minimal (see Proposition
3.1
).

In a forthcoming paper, we will extend the class of the closures of GMM braid diagrams to other link templates, and we will study
the sharpness of the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality for the corresponding link diagrams.

1.2.
Discussion

At the end of the 19th century, P. G. Tait made three conjectures, called the
Tait conjectures
, concerning alternating knots and links (see an expository note
[
Men19
]
):

(1)

any reduced alternating diagram is minimal;

(2)

any two reduced alternating diagrams that represent the same link have the same writhe;

(3)

any two reduced alternating diagrams that represent the same link are related through a finite sequence of diagram transformations referred to as
flypes
.

All of the Tait conjectures hold.
In
[
MT93
, Main Theorem]
, W. Menasco and M. B. Thistlethwaite prove the third Tait conjecture, called the
Tait flyping conjecture
.
The second Tait conjecture follows from the third one.
The first Tait conjecture
was proved
by L. Kauffman, M. B. Thistlethwaite, and K. Murasugi independently by using the Jones polynomial (see
[
Kau87
, Theorem 2.10]
,
[
Thi87
, Theorem 2]
, and
[
Mur87
, Theorem A]
).
Namely, they showed that the number of crossings of any diagram of a link
\mathcal{L}
is bounded from below by
the breadth of
the Jones polynomial
of
\mathcal{L}
. Furthermore, for any reduced alternating link diagram, this inequality is sharp.

In
[
Thi88
, Corollary 3.4]
, M. B. Thistlethwaite uses a generalization of the Jones polynomial referred to as the Kauffman polynomial and obtains similar minimality results
for adequate link diagrams.
All reduced alternating diagrams are adequate. The simplest adequate non-alternating prime knots have
10
crossings, and there are
3
such:
10_{152}
,
10_{153}
, and
10_{154}
.

It is noteworthy that if a prime diagram is not alternating, the inequality concerning the breadth of the Jones polynomial is strict. This implies that any minimal diagram of each prime alternating link is alternating.
In
[
LT88
, Theorem 10]
, W. B. R. Lickorish and M. B. Thistlethwaite use this fact to show that some specific link diagrams referred to as reduced Montesinos ones are minimal, and thus they calculate the crossing number of all Montesinos links.

Traditionally, one indexes tables of (non-oriented) prime knots by the crossing number and one counts mirror images as a single knot type.
It follows from the definitions that if a link diagram is locally twisted homogeneous, then its mirror image too.
A similar result holds for the reverse of a link diagram.

Besides, no analog of the Tait flyping conjecture has been found for homogeneous links so far.

A natural question is whether one can read off topological
properties of the link given by a diagram
\mathcal{D}

(such as knottedness, splitness, primeness, and hyperbolicity)
from
\mathcal{D}
.
In some sense, homogeneous links are visually knotted. Namely, a homogeneous link
\mathcal{L}
is trivial if and only if for some (and hence any) homogeneous diagram
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{L}
,
the Seifert graph of
\mathcal{D}

has no cycles (see
[
Cr89
, Theorem 3]
and
[
Cr04
, Corollary 7.6.3]
).
A link
\mathcal{L}
is said to be
split
if
\mathcal{L}
admits a disconnected diagram.
Homogeneous links are visually split in the sense that a homogeneous link
\mathcal{L}
is split if and only if
some (and hence any) homogeneous diagram
of
\mathcal{L}

is disconnected (see
[
Cr89
, Corollary 3.1]
and
[
Cr04
, Corollary 7.6.4]
).
In
[
Cr93
]
, the author conjectures that any link given by a prime homogeneous diagram is prime.
Equivalently, the conjecture states that
homogeneous links are visually prime in the sense that a homogeneous link
\mathcal{L}
is prime if and only if some (and hence any) homogeneous diagram of
\mathcal{L}
is prime. This conjecture holds for both alternating (see
[
Men84
, Theorem 1]
and
[
Lick97
, Theorem 4.4]
) and positive links (see
[
Oz02
, Theorem 1.4]
and
[
Cr93
, Theorem 1.2]
).
Finally, in
[
Men84
, Corollary 2]
, the author proves that if a non-torus alternating link
\mathcal{L}
is both prime and non-split, then
\mathcal{L}
is hyperbolic.
However, the problem of visual determining whether a link given by a homogeneous diagram is hyperbolic remains unsolved.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we give a detailed definition of both locally twisted diagrams and locally twisted homogeneous ones.
In Section 3, we state the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality explicitly,
reduce Theorem
1.1
to Corollary
1.3
,
and
give a definition of
resolution trees for the skein polynomial.
The remaining part of the paper aims to prove Theorem
1.2
. The basis of our proof is the techniques developed in
[
DHL19
]
.
In Section 4, we introduce resolution trees that we call coherent.
In Section 5, we introduce castle structures for link diagrams to describe specific resolution trees that we call special coherent.
In Section 6, we apply these resolution trees to prove Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Locally twisted homogeneous diagrams

This section aims to define locally twisted homogeneous link diagrams.
First, we introduce locally twisted braid words. Then, we define link templates, which generalize the Alexander closure of braids construction, and we give a complete definition of locally twisted link diagrams. Finally, we define homogeneous link diagrams introduced in
[
Cr89
]
.

2.1.
Locally twisted braid words

Given a set of symbols
S
, a finite sequence of elements of
S
is called a
word in an alphabet
S
. We write words without commas and we denote by
S^{\ast}
the set of all words in the alphabet
S
. In this paper, we consider alphabets of the form
S\subseteq\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1}^{-1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{2}^{-1},\ldots\}
.

Figure 1.

Examples of braid diagrams.

Given
n\geq 2,
an element of
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1},\sigma_{n-1}^{-1}\}^{\ast}

is called a
braid word
.
We visualize braid words by their
n

1
for the braid diagram with
4

\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{n}
the braid group with
n
strands. Recall that
\mathcal{B}_{n}
admits the following presentation with the standard Artin generators:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n}\simeq\langle\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}\mid\sigma_{k}\sigma_{k+1}\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k+1}\sigma_{k}\sigma_{k+1},\ 1\leq k\leq n-1;\ \sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i},\ |i-j|\geq 2\rangle.

The relation
\sigma_{k}\sigma_{k+1}\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k+1}\sigma_{k}\sigma_{k+1}
is called the
braid relation
, and the relation
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}
is called the
far commutativity
relation. An element of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is called a
braid
with
n
strands.

Given
i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
such that
i<j
, let

(2.1)

\displaystyle\delta_{i,j}:=(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\ldots\sigma_{j-1}\sigma_{j})(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\ldots\sigma_{j-1})\ldots(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1})\sigma_{i},

and let
\Delta_{i,j}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
be the braid corresponding to
\delta_{i,j}
. The braid
\Delta_{1,n}
is referred to as the
half twist
, the
fundamental braid
, and the

in
\mathcal{B}_{n}
. The braid
\Delta_{1,n}^{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is referred to as the
full twist
.

In
[
St78
]
, J. Stallings introduces the concept of homogeneous braid words. Namely, given
n\geq 2
and given
r=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n-1})\in\{1,-1\}^{n-1}
, a braid word
w\in\{\sigma_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}^{\pm 1}\}^{\ast}
is said to be

contains none of the letters
\sigma_{1}^{-r_{1}},\sigma_{2}^{-r_{2}},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}^{-r_{n-1}}
.

Let
r\in\{1,-1\}^{n-1}
. Let
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
be such that

1~=~i_{1}~<~i_{2}~<~\ldots~<~i_{m}~=~n

and for all
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
, one has
r_{i_{k}}=r_{i_{k}+1}=\ldots=r_{i_{k+1}-1}
and
r_{i_{k+1}-1}\neq r_{i_{k+1}}
.

Given an
r
-homogeneous braid word
w
and

, we say that the braid word obtained from

by deleting all letters except
\sigma_{i_{k}}^{\pm 1},\sigma_{i_{k}+1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,\sigma_{i_{k+1}-1}^{\pm 1}

.

2.3.
Homogeneous link diagrams

Recall that we assume all link diagrams to be oriented.
A crossing is said to be positive (resp. negative) if it has the form on the left (resp. right) picture in Figure
6
.

Figure 6.
Smoothing of a crossing.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a link diagram. Simple closed curves obtained by smoothing all crossings of
\mathcal{D}
are called
Seifert circles
of
\mathcal{D}
.

Let
\Gamma(\mathcal{D})
be the undirected multigraph whose vertex set is the set of all Seifert circles of
\mathcal{D}
and whose
edge set is the set of all crossings of
\mathcal{D}
. Each edge of
\Gamma(\mathcal{D})
endowed with a sign according to the type of the corresponding crossing.
The graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{D})
is referred to as the
Seifert graph
of
\mathcal{D}
. Note that
\Gamma(\mathcal{D})
is bipartite, that is, it contains no odd-length cycles.

To define homogeneous link diagrams, we follow
[
Cr89
]
.
Let
\Gamma
be a Seifert graph.
A vertex
v
of
\Gamma
is said to be a
cut vertex
if the number of components of
\Gamma-v
is greater than that of
\Gamma
. Suppose that
\Gamma
contains a cut vertex
v
, and let
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\ldots,\Gamma_{n}
be the components of
\Gamma-v
. We say that the
n
subgraphs
\Gamma_{1}\cup v,\Gamma_{2}\cup v,\ldots,\Gamma_{n}\cup v
are obtained from
\Gamma
by
cutting

\Gamma
at
v
. Cutting
\Gamma
at each of its cut vertices produces a set of components, each one being a subgraph of
\Gamma
containing no cut vertices. Such a component is called a
block
of
\Gamma
. A block
H
of
\Gamma
is said to be
homogeneous
if all edges of
H
have the same sign.

A diagram
\mathcal{D}
is said to be
homogeneous
if each block of
\Gamma(\mathcal{D})
is homogeneous.
A link is said to be
homogeneous

if it admits a homogeneous
diagram.

We list several diagrammatic properties
related to
homogeneity.
First, if a diagram
\mathcal{D}
is positive, that is, all crossings of
\mathcal{D}
are positive, then
\mathcal{D}
is homogeneous. Second, if
\mathcal{D}
is alternating, then
\mathcal{D}
is homogeneous. Third, the Seifert circles of a link diagram
\mathcal{D}
divide the plane into regions. Suppose that each of these regions contains crossings of the same type. Then
\mathcal{D}
is homogeneous. Fourth, let
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
be a template, and let
\pi
be a braid placement. If the link diagram determined by
\pi
is homogeneous, then for each
\alpha~\in~\mathcal{A}
, the braid word
\pi(\alpha)
is
r
-homogeneous for some
r\in\{1,-1\}^{\left\lVert\alpha\right\rVert-1}
. In particular, if the link diagram determined by
\pi
is alternating, then for each
\alpha~\in~\mathcal{A}
, the braid word
\pi(\alpha)
is either
r
-homogeneous or
(-r)
-homogeneous for
r=(-1,1,-1,1,\ldots,(-1)^{\left\lVert\alpha\right\rVert})\in\{1,-1\}^{\left\lVert\alpha\right\rVert-1}
whenever for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,\left\lVert\alpha\right\rVert-1\}
, the word
\pi(\alpha)
contains either
\sigma_{i}
or
\sigma_{i}^{-1}
.

Definition 2.3
.

A link diagram
\mathcal{D}
is said to be
locally twisted homogeneous
if
\mathcal{D}
is both locally twisted and homogeneous.

3.
The skein polynomial

This section aims to state the Morton–Franks–Williams inequality, reduce Theorem
1.1
to Corollary
1.3
, and introduce resolution trees for the skein polynomial.

3.1.
The Morton–Franks–Williams inequality

In
[
F+85
,
PT87
]
, the authors prove that there is a unique function that maps each link diagram
\mathcal{D}
to a two-variable Laurent polynomial
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)\in\mathbb{Z}[a^{\pm 1},z^{\pm 1}]
such that:

(1)

if two link diagrams
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
represent the same link, then
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime};a,z)
;

(2)

one has

\displaystyle a\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{+};a,z)-a^{-1}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{-};a,z)=z\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{0},a,z)

whenever
\mathcal{D}_{+}
,
\mathcal{D}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{-}
are link diagrams that coincide except at a small region where the diagrams are presented as in Figure
6
, respectively;

(3)

if
\mathcal{D}
is a knot diagram
with zero crossings,
then
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)=1
.

The polynomial
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)
is referred to as the
skein polynomial
, the
HOMFLY polynomial
, the
HOMFLY-PT polynomial
, the
generalized Jones polynomial
, and the
twisted Alexander polynomial
of
\mathcal{D}
. The second condition is referred to as the
skein relation
.

Given a link
\mathcal{L}
, let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L};a,z):=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)
for some (and hence any) diagram
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{L}
. Denote by
s(\mathcal{D})
the number of Seifert circles of
\mathcal{D}
and by

\omega(\mathcal{D})
the
writhe
of
\mathcal{D}
, that is, the difference between the number of positive and the number of negative crossings of
\mathcal{D}
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\mathcal{L};z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z^{\pm 1}]
be polynomials such that
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L};a,z)=\sum_{i=e}^{E}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\mathcal{L};z)a^{i}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{e}(\mathcal{L};z)~\neq~0
, and
\mathcal{Q}_{E}(\mathcal{L};z)~\neq~0
. In
[
Mor86
, Theorem 1]
, the author proves that for any diagram
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{L}
, one has

(3.1)

\displaystyle-\omega(\mathcal{D})-(s(\mathcal{D})-1)\leq e\leq E\leq-\omega(\mathcal{D})+(s(\mathcal{D})-1).

In particular,

(3.2)

\displaystyle(E-e)/2+1\leq s(\mathcal{D}).

The latter is referred to as the
Morton–Franks–Williams inequality
.
Given a link diagram
\mathcal{D}
, we say that (
3.2
) is
sharp
for
\mathcal{D}
if
(E-e)/2+1=s(\mathcal{D})
. In this case,
\mathcal{D}
is optimal.
Note that (
3.2
) is sharp if and only if both outer inequalities in (
3.1
) are sharp.

3.2.
Reduction of Theorem
1.1
to Corollary
1.3

Given a link diagram
\mathcal{D}
, denote by
|\mathcal{D}|
the number of crossings of
\mathcal{D}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{i}(\mathcal{L};a)\in\mathbb{Z}[a^{\pm 1}]
be polynomials such that
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L};a,z)=\sum_{i=m}^{M}\mathcal{P}_{i}(\mathcal{L};a)z^{i}
,
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{L};a)~\neq~0
, and
\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mathcal{L};a)~\neq~0
. In
[
Mor86
, Theorem 2]
, the author proves that for any diagram
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{L}
, one has

(3.3)

\displaystyle M\leq|\mathcal{D}|-s(\mathcal{D})+1.

By combining (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), one has

\displaystyle M+(E-e)/2\leq|\mathcal{D}|.

It turns out that for all homogeneous diagrams, (
3.3
) is sharp (see
[
Cr89
, Theorem 4]
,
[
Cr04
, Theorem 7.6.2]
, and
[
Man12
, Theorem 6]
). It is worth noting that the sharpness of (
3.3
) does not characterize homogeneous links since (
3.3
) is sharp for an almost positive diagram of
12n_{149}
, which is not homogeneous.

By using the sharpness mentioned above, we prove Proposition
3.1
below.
Assertion (i) was firstly observed in
[
Mur91
, Proposition 7.4]
.
This assertion implies that Theorem
1.1
is a special case of Corollary
1.3
.
For the sake of completeness, we provide a proof.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose a homogeneous diagram
\mathcal{D}
of a link
\mathcal{L}
is optimal.
Then

(i)

the diagram
\mathcal{D}
is minimal;

(ii)

any minimal diagram of
\mathcal{L}
is optimal.

Proof.

Let us prove assertion (i). Let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be an arbitrary link diagram of
\mathcal{L}
.
Since
\mathcal{D}
is optimal, the inequality
s(\mathcal{D})\leq s(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
holds. One has

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DegCrSeif}}}{{=}}M+s(\mathcal{D})-1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DegCrSeif}}}{{\leq}}(|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|-s(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})+1)+s(\mathcal{D})-1\leq|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|.

Therefore,
\mathcal{D}
is minimal.

Let us prove assertion (ii). Let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be a minimal diagram of
\mathcal{L}
. In particular,
|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|=|\mathcal{D}|
. One has

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DegCrSeif}}}{{\leq}}|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|-M+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DegCrSeif}}}{{=}}|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|-(|\mathcal{D}|-s(\mathcal{D})+1)+1=s(\mathcal{D}).

Therefore, since

\mathcal{D}
is optimal,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is optimal too.
∎

3.3.
Resolution trees

The process of evaluating the skein polynomial of a link diagram by repeated application of the skein relation is referred to as
resolution
. One records this process schematically in a binary structure,
referred to as
a
resolution tree
(see
[
GM14
, p. 18]
,
[
Cr89
, p. 538]
, and
[
Nak04
, p. 15]
),
a
resolving tree
(see
[
LDH19
, p. 193]
,
[
DHL19
, p. 4]
,
[
Cr04
, p. 183]
,
[
F+85
, p. 4]
, and
[
Ad94
, p. 167]
), and
a
computation tree
(see
[
FW87
, p. 99]
and
[
Kal09
, p. 653]
).
Namely, given a link diagram
\mathcal{D}
,
a resolution tree for
\mathcal{D}
is
a weighted
binary tree
\mathcal{T}
such that:

(1)

each node of
\mathcal{T}
is a link diagram;

(2)

the root node of
\mathcal{T}
is
\mathcal{D}
;

(3)

each leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
represents an unlink;

(4)

each internal node has exactly two children. The corresponding three link diagrams are identical except at one crossing, and they relate by one of the two relations at that crossing, as shown in Figure
7
.

Figure 7.
The weight assignment for a resolution tree. The picture was taken
from
[
LDH19
]
.

We refer to the lines connecting nodes of a binary tree as
branches
.

Any resolution tree
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{D}
gives rise to a decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)
as a sum, which is indexed by leaf nodes of
\mathcal{T}
, as follows. Calculations show that if
\mathcal{D}
is the link diagram with zero crossings and with precisely
n
link components, one has

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)=((a-a^{-1})z^{-1})^{n-1}.

Note that the skein relation admits the following equivalent forms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{+};a,z)

\displaystyle=a^{-2}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{-};a,z)+a^{-1}z\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{0},a,z),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{-};a,z)

\displaystyle=a^{2}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{+};a,z)-az\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_{0},a,z).

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
. Denote by
\gamma(\mathcal{U})
the number of link components of
\mathcal{U}
. Let
P
be a unique path on
\mathcal{T}
from the root node
\mathcal{D}
to the leaf node
\mathcal{U}
. It is easy to see that the contribution of
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)
is
((a-a^{-1})z^{-1})^{\gamma(\mathcal{U})-1}
multiplied by the weights of the branches in
P
. Let
t(\mathcal{U})
be the number of crossings of
\mathcal{D}
that one smoothed in obtaining
\mathcal{U}
, and
t^{-}(\mathcal{U})
be the number of negative crossings among the smoothed ones. As Figure
7
shows, the degree of
a
in the weight of a branch is equal to the change of writhe from
the parent to the child.
Besides, a
z
term in the weight of the branch indicates that the
child is obtained from the
parent
by a crossing smoothing and a negative sign in the weight indicates that the smoothed crossing is negative. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)=\sum\limits_{\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{T}_{\circ}}(-1)^{t^{-}(\mathcal{U})}z^{t(\mathcal{U})}a^{\omega(\mathcal{U})-\omega(\mathcal{D})}((a-a^{-1})z^{-1})^{\gamma(\mathcal{U})-1},

where
\mathcal{T}_{\circ}
is the set of leaf nodes of
\mathcal{T}
.

Following
[
DHL19
]
,
we think of a resolution tree as a graph of a branching process.
Namely, at each internal node, one takes a crossing of the current link diagram and branch on smoothing and flipping the crossing. Hence, to specify a resolution tree, we can describe a rule that determines the corresponding crossings of intermediate link diagrams. The rule determines the evolution of the branching process.

With these ideas in mind, we describe resolution trees introduced in
[
DHL19
]
.

4.
Coherent resolution trees

This section aims to
describe two classes of resolution trees introduced in
[
DHL19
]
. We call the corresponding trees
\mathrm{X}
-coherent
and
\mathrm{Y}
-coherent
. The description is rather complex, so we start with a simpler construction of
descending resolution trees
.

4.1.
Preliminaries on link diagrams

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a link diagram.
Let
x\in\mathcal{D}
, and let
C
be a Seifert circle of
\mathcal{D}
. We say that
x
is a
point on
C
if
x
lies in the intersection of
C
and
\mathcal{D}
. We say that
x
is a
point on
\mathcal{D}
if
x
is a point on a Seifert circle of
\mathcal{D}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a link diagram and
m=\gamma(\mathcal{D})
. Let
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}
be a sequence of points on
\mathcal{D}
such that for
i\neq j
, the points
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
lie on distinct link components of
\mathcal{D}
.
We refer to
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}
as
base points
.

We travel through
\mathcal{D}
by moving along each link component of
\mathcal{D}
as follows. We start at the first base point
p_{1}
and move according to the orientation. As we reach
p_{1}
again, we proceeding to the second marked point
p_{2}
and start moving according to the orientation. We continue in the same way until one visits the link component containing
p_{m}
entirely. This process is called the
natural travel
determined by
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}
.

Let
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}
be a sequence of base points on
\mathcal{D}
.
During
the corresponding
natural travel, we visit each crossing of
\mathcal{D}
exactly twice. A crossing of
\mathcal{D}
is said to be
descending
(resp.
ascending
) if one travels along the overpassing (resp. underpassing) strand first. The diagram
\mathcal{D}
is said to be
descending
(resp.
ascending
) if each crossing of
\mathcal{D}
is descending (resp. ascending).
We emphasize that these diagrammatic properties depend on the base points.
If
\mathcal{D}
is descending (resp. ascending), then the components of
\mathcal{D}
are both layered from top to bottom (resp. from bottom to top) and represent the unknots. In this case, the diagram
\mathcal{D}
represents the unlink with precisely
m
link components.

Let
x
be a point on
\mathcal{D}
.
We travel through
\mathcal{D}
according to the orientation. Denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D};x)
the longest path starting at
x
one traveled before meeting either
x
or
an ascending crossing, that is, a crossing of
\mathcal{D}
such that one meets its underpassing strand first. We refer to the path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D};x)
as a
maximal descending path
in
\mathcal{D}
starting at
x
.

We define a
maximal ascending path
in
\mathcal{D}
starting at
x
similarly.

4.2.
Definition of descending resolution trees

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a link diagram. Let us describe all descending resolution trees for
\mathcal{D}
at once. The construction of each descending resolution tree
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{D}

consists of several phases. The resulting tree
\mathcal{T}
depends on sequences of base points on
\mathcal{D}
.

We start with the one node tree
\mathcal{T}_{0}
. At the end of phase
k
, we obtain a rooted subtree
\mathcal{T}_{k}
of
\mathcal{T}
. The resulting subtrees satisfy

\{\mathcal{D}\}=\mathcal{T}_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{T}_{m}=\mathcal{T}.

Thus, one obtains each of the trees from the previous one by extensions shown in Figure
7
.

In the first phase, we choose an arbitrary base point
x_{1}
on
\mathcal{D}
. We find the maximal descending path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D};x_{1})
on
\mathcal{D}
starting at
x_{1}
. If
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D};x_{1})
is closed, that is,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D};x_{1})
is the whole link component of
\mathcal{D}
containing
x_{1}
, then the phase ends. Assume
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D};x_{1})
is not closed. In this case, we extend the current tree at a crossing of
\mathcal{D}
that is the end of the path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D};x_{1})
. At this moment, the tree
\mathcal{T}_{1}
consists of three nodes: a parent
\mathcal{D}
and its children
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}
such that
|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|=|\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}|+1
. Then, we find the maximal descending path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime};x_{1})
on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
starting at
x_{1}
. If
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime};x_{1})
is not closed, then we extend the current tree
\mathcal{T}_{1}
similarly by adding children of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. We repeat the same procedure for all leaf nodes
\mathcal{U}
of the current tree
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. At the end of the first phase, for each leaf node
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the maximal descending path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{U};x_{1})
on
\mathcal{U}
starting at
x_{1}
is closed. In this case,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{U};x_{1})
is the whole link component of
\mathcal{U}
containing
x_{1}
. If each leaf node of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is a knot diagram, then the construction of
\mathcal{T}
ends. Otherwise, we move to the next phase.

a point on the top left arc.
The picture was taken
from
[
LDH19
]
.

In the second phase, we extend
\mathcal{T}_{1}
as follows. For each leaf node
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, we choose an arbitrary base point
x_{2}
on a link component of
\mathcal{V}
distinct from that of containing
x_{1}
.
We emphasize that the point
x_{2}
depends on
\mathcal{V}
.
We find the maximal descending path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{V};x_{2})
on
\mathcal{U}
starting at
x_{2}
. If
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{V};x_{2})
is not closed, then we extend the current tree at a crossing of
\mathcal{V}
that is the end of the path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{V};x_{2})
. We proceed similarly. The second phase continues until, for each leaf node
\mathcal{V}
of the current tree
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, the path
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{V};x_{2})
is closed. In this case,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{V};x_{2})
is the whole link component of
\mathcal{V}
containing
x_{2}
.

We repeat the same procedure until all leaf nodes of the current tree are descending diagrams. Since any descending diagram represents an unlink, the resulting tree
\mathcal{T}
is a resolution tree.

By varying the base points on the leaf nodes of the trees
\mathcal{T}_{0},\mathcal{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{m-1}
, we obtain a few descending resolution trees for
\mathcal{D}
.

See Figure

for a descending resolution tree example of the Alexander closure of
\sigma_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}
. For the sake of simplicity, one presents the nodes as braid diagrams.

The class of descending resolution trees was introduced in
[
Cr89
, Theorem 2]
. This class gives an idea of coherent resolution trees.

4.3.
Definition of coherent resolution trees

We are in the position of describing
\mathrm{X}
-coherent and
\mathrm{Y}
-coherent resolution trees.
Recall that all link diagrams lie on the oriented plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Thus, one may distinguish clockwise and counterclockwise Seifert circles of
\mathcal{D}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a link diagram. Let us describe all
\mathrm{X}
-coherent resolution trees for
\mathcal{D}
at once. The construction of each
\mathrm{X}
-coherent resolution tree
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{D}
consists of several phases. The resulting tree
\mathcal{T}
depends on sequences of base points on
\mathcal{D}
.

We start with the one node tree
\mathcal{T}_{0}
. At the end of phase
k
, we obtain a rooted subtree
\mathcal{T}_{k}
of
\mathcal{T}
. The resulting subtrees satisfy

\{\mathcal{D}\}=\mathcal{T}_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{T}_{m}=\mathcal{T}.

Thus, one obtains each of the trees from the previous one by extensions shown in Figure
7
.

Following
[
DHL19
]
, we define two rules called
descending
and
ascending
ones. In the descending rule, one keeps a descending crossing unchanged and branches on flipping and smoothing an ascending one.
In the ascending rule, one keeps an ascending crossing currently visited and branches on flipping and smoothing a descending one.

At each phase, we follow either the descending or ascending rule.
By definition, we follow the descending rule if and only if the Seifert circle containing the current base point is clockwise.

More precisely, in the first phase, we choose an arbitrary base point
x_{1}
on
\mathcal{D}
. We find the maximal descending (resp. ascending) path in
\mathcal{D}
starting at
x_{1}
whenever the corresponding Seifert circle is clockwise (resp. counterclockwise). If the path is not closed, then we extend the current tree at a crossing of
\mathcal{D}
that is the end of the path. We repeat the same procedure for all leaf nodes
\mathcal{U}
of the current tree
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. At the end of the first phase, for each leaf node
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, the maximal descending (resp. ascending) path in
\mathcal{U}
starting at
x_{1}
is closed. If each leaf node
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is a knot diagram, then the construction ends. Otherwise, we move to the next phase.

In the second phase, we extend
\mathcal{T}_{1}
as follows. For each leaf node
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, we choose an arbitrary base point
x_{2}
on a link component of
\mathcal{V}
distinct from that of containing
x_{1}
.
We emphasize that the point
x_{2}
depends on
\mathcal{V}
.
We find the maximal descending (resp. ascending) path in
\mathcal{V}
starting at
x_{2}
whenever the corresponding Seifert circle is clockwise (resp. counterclockwise). If the path is not closed, then we extend the current tree at a crossing of
\mathcal{V}
that is the end of the path. We continue similarly. The second phase ends when for each leaf node
\mathcal{V}
of the current tree
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, the maximal descending (resp. ascending) path in
\mathcal{V}
starting at
x_{2}
is closed.

We repeat the same procedure until, for each leaf node
\mathcal{U}
of the current tree, one visits all link components of
\mathcal{U}
. This completes the construction of
\mathcal{T}
.

It is easy to see that for any leaf node
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{T}
, each link component of
\mathcal{U}
is either descending or ascending. Also, the link components of
\mathcal{U}
are stacked over each other. Thus,
\mathcal{U}
represents an unlink. Therefore,
\mathcal{T}
is a resolution tree.

By varying the base points on the leaf nodes of the trees
\mathcal{T}_{0},\mathcal{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{m-1}
, we obtain a few
\mathrm{X}
-coherent resolution trees for
\mathcal{D}
.

We define
\mathrm{Y}
-coherent resolution trees similarly. Namely, we follow the descending rule if and only if the Seifert circle containing a current base point is counterclockwise.

5.
Special coherent resolution trees

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we use specific
\mathrm{X}
-coherent and
\mathrm{Y}
-coherent resolution trees referred to as
special
.
In our approach, a class of special coherent resolution trees corresponds to the ordered pair consisting of a link template and a braid placement.
To define these resolution trees, we refer to castle structures for link diagrams introduced in
[
DHL19
]
. The definition of special coherent resolution trees requires specific types of castles that we call
appropriate

[
DHL19
, Lemma 4.3]
, the authors claim that any link diagram admits an appropriate castle. There are several inaccuracies in their proof. Thus, we fix it.

5.1.

Following

We endow each floor

Lemma 6.6
.

For each
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
with
r_{i_{k}}=-1
, one neither smoothed nor flipped at least
2|v_{k,1}|
negative crossings of
[\pi(\alpha)]
in obtaining
[\pi_{d}(\alpha)]
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, one has
r_{1}=-1
. First, suppose
k=1
.

Let
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
be the index such that
\mathrm{LC}(\mathcal{U};x_{i})
contains the starting point of the first Seifert segment of
[\pi(\alpha)]
.

The link component
\mathrm{LC}(\mathcal{U};x_{i})

determines a curve
t
on the braid diagram
[\pi(\alpha)]
.
It follows from Lemma
6.1
that
t
contains crossings of
[v_{1,1}]
of the form
\sigma_{1}^{-1},\sigma_{2}^{-1},\ldots,\sigma_{i_{2}-1}^{-1}
.
Lemma
6.5
implies that
t
exits
[\pi(\alpha)]
through the ending point of the first Seifert segment of
[\pi(\alpha)]
. Therefore,
t
contains at least
i_{2}-1
additional crossings of
[\pi(\alpha)]
of the form
\sigma_{i_{2}-1}^{-1},\sigma_{i_{2}-2}^{-1},\ldots,\sigma_{1}^{-1}
. Therefore, one neither smoothed nor flipped at least
2(i_{2}-1)
negative crossings of
[\pi(\alpha)]
in obtaining
[\pi_{d}(\alpha)]
.

For each
j\in\{2,3,\ldots,i_{2}-1\}
, we apply similar arguments concerning the link component of
\mathcal{U}
that contains the starting point of the index
j
Seifert segment of
[\pi(\alpha)]
.
Therefore,
one neither smoothed nor flipped at least

\displaystyle 2(i_{2}-1)+2(i_{2}-2)+\ldots+2=2i_{2}(i_{2}-1)=2|v_{1,1}|

negative crossings of
[\pi(\alpha)]
in obtaining
[\pi_{d}(\alpha)]
.

For
k>1

One has

(6.2)

\displaystyle t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast};\alpha)+\sum\limits_{k\in J}2|v_{k,1}|=n(\pi(\alpha)).

Lemma
6.6
implies

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k\in J}2|v_{k,1}|\leq n(\pi(\alpha))-t^{-}(\mathcal{U};\alpha).

By combining (
6.2
) and (
6.3
), one has
t^{-}(\mathcal{U};\alpha)\leq t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast};\alpha)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle t^{-}(\mathcal{U})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}t^{-}(\mathcal{U};\alpha)\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast};\alpha)=t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast}).

The second step is complete.

6.3.
Third step

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
. Recall that the contribution of
\mathcal{U}
to the skein polynomial
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)
is

\displaystyle(-1)^{t^{-}(\mathcal{U})}z^{t(\mathcal{U})}a^{\omega(\mathcal{U})-\omega(\mathcal{D})}((a-a^{-1})z^{-1})^{\gamma(\mathcal{U})-1}.

Therefore, the highest
a
-degree term that
\mathcal{U}
contributes to
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D};a,z)
is

\displaystyle(-1)^{t^{-}(\mathcal{U})}z^{t(\mathcal{U})-\gamma(\mathcal{U})+1}a^{\omega(\mathcal{U})-\omega(\mathcal{D})+\gamma(\mathcal{U})-1}.

Suppose that
\mathcal{U}
contributes to the the highest
a
-degree term. Recall that in the first step, we showed that there exits a leaf node
\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
of
\mathcal{T}
that contributes to the the highest
a
-degree term.
Besides,
in the second step, we showed that
\gamma(\mathcal{U})=s(\mathcal{D})
,
\omega(\mathcal{U})=0
, and
t^{-}(\mathcal{U})\leq t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})
. Therefore,
\mathcal{U}
contributes to the term

(6.4)

\displaystyle(-1)^{t^{-}(\mathcal{U})}z^{t(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})-s(\mathcal{D})+1}a^{-\omega(\mathcal{D})+s(\mathcal{D})-1}

if and only if
t(\mathcal{U})=t(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})
.

Suppose that
t(\mathcal{U})=t(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})
. Denote by
p(\mathcal{D})
the number of positive crossings of
\mathcal{D}
. Given a leaf node
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{T}
, denote by
t^{+}(\mathcal{V})
the number of positive crossings of
\mathcal{D}
that one smoothed in obtaining
\mathcal{V}
. In the first step, we showed that
t^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})=p(\mathcal{D})
. One has

\displaystyle t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})=t(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})-t^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})=t(\mathcal{U})-p(\mathcal{D})\leq t(\mathcal{U})-t^{+}(\mathcal{U})=t^{-}(\mathcal{U}).

Hence,

t^{-}(\mathcal{U})=t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})
. Therefore, the contribution of
\mathcal{U}
to the term (
6.4
) has the same sign
(-1)^{t^{-}(\mathcal{U}^{\ast})}
as of
\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
. Thus, this term does not cancel. This shows that

E=-\omega(\mathcal{D})+s(\mathcal{D})-1.

The third step is complete. Theorem
1.2
is proved.
