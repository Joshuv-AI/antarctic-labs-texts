Construction of Algorithms for Parallel Addition
Jan Legerský, Milena Svobodová

Abstract
An algebraic number β ∈ ℂ \beta\in\mathbb{C} with no conjugate of modulus 1 1 can serve as the base of a numeration system ( β , 𝒜 ) (\beta,\mathcal{A}) with parallel addition, i.e., the sum of two operands represented in base β \beta with digits from 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is calculated in constant time, irrespective of the length of the operands. In order to allow parallel addition, sufficient level of redundancy must be given to the alphabet 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . The complexity of parallel addition algorithm depends heavily on the size # ​ 𝒜 \#\mathcal{A} of the alphabet: the bigger alphabet is considered, the lower complexity of the parallel addition algorithm may be reached, and vice versa. Here we aim to find parallel addition algorithms on alphabets of the minimal possible size, for a given base. As the complexity of these algorithms becomes quite huge in general, we introduce a so-called Extending Window Method (EWM) – in fact an algorithm to construct parallel addition algorithms. This method can be applied on bases β \beta which are expanding algebraic integers, i.e., β \beta whose all conjugates are greater than 1 1 in modulus. Convergence of the EWM is not guaranteed, nevertheless, we have developed tools for revealing non-convergence, and there is a number of successful applications. Firstly, the EWM provides the same parallel addition algorithms as were previously introduced by A. Avizienis, C.Y. Chow & J.E. Robertson or B. Parhami for integer bases and alphabets. Then, by applying the EWM on selected complex bases β \beta with non-integer alphabets 𝒜 ⊂ ℤ ⁡ [ β ] \mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta] , we obtain new results – parallel addition algorithms on alphabets of minimal possible size, which could not be found so far (manually). The EWM is helpful also in the case of block parallel addition.

Introduction

Numeration systems
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
are defined by a base
\beta
(sometimes also called a radix) and an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
– the set of digits. Usually, the base is greater than
1
in modulus:
|\beta|>1
. A number
x
is said to have a representation in the numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
, if there exist digits
x_{j}
from the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
such that
x=\sum_{j=r}^{l}x_{j}\beta^{j}
, with
l\in\mathbb{Z}
and
r\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\})
. The
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation of
x
is then denoted by the string
(x_{l}\cdots x_{0}\raisebox{0.43057pt}{{.}}x_{-1}\cdots)_{\beta}
, and it may not be unique.

Our goal is to find those numeration systems
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
that allow parallel addition, i.e., where the operation of adding two numbers having
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representations has constant time complexity, assuming that arbitrary number of operations can be run in parallel. This concept was introduced by A. Avizienis in
[
1
]
, and then was intensively elaborated and brought into practice – not just for the sake of the addition operation itself, but also as part of other calculations (e.g. fast algorithms for multiplication or division). In practice, we actually consider only summands with finite
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representations as the operands of addition algorithms.

When approaching the problem of parallel addition, we start by identifying the bases
\beta
for which parallel addition is possible at all, in a broad scope – so not only integer, but also real and complex bases. The requirement to allow parallel addition forces the base to be an algebraic number (when considering
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
). Existence of parallel addition algorithm for a given base depends on the fact whether any of its algebraic conjugates is of modulus equal to
1
– if not, then parallel addition algorithms exist for that base (with a suitably chosen alphabet
\mathcal{A}
). But if
\beta
has any algebraic conjugate equal to
1
in modulus, then parallel addition in base
\beta
is impossible.

In the second step, given a base
\beta
suitable for parallel addition, we focus on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. In principle, the more elements in the alphabet, the better chances for the numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
to allow parallel addition – as the redundancy increases, and it is the redundancy that makes parallel addition possible. In
[
4
]
, a constructive method is provided, describing how to choose the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
(of consecutive integers, containing
0
, and symmetric), and then how to perform parallel addition in thus obtained numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
. The approach used there consists in finding a
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation of zero with one dominant digit, i.e., strictly greater than the sum of moduli of all the other digits. Such representation of zero then serves as the core rewriting rule within the parallel addition algorithm, and also determines the size of the alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}
.

The drawback of this approach is that the alphabets
\mathcal{A}
are very large, and so is often also the carry propagation (due to the length of the rewriting rule). Too much redundancy may be inconvenient for other operations – e.g. division or comparison. Therefore, our goal is to find alphabets of minimal size allowing parallel addition. For
\beta
being an algebraic integer, lower bounds on the cardinality of integer alphabets
\mathcal{A}
for parallel addition were specified in
[
5
]
; and, for several classes of the bases
\beta
, the parallel addition algorithms using (integer) alphabets of the minimal size were actually found (
[
5
,
3
]
).

Although the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is most often selected as a subset of integers, the problem is relevant also in a more general setting, namely considering
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. Some of the results and conditions on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
to enable parallel addition, obtained earlier for integer alphabets, were generalized in
[
14
]
also to
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. Especially the lower bound on minimal cardinality of
\mathcal{A}
in the case of
\mathcal{A}[\beta]=\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. It is proven that the alphabet must contain all congruence classes modulo
\beta
in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
, and also all congruence classes modulo
\beta-1
in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. This is already quite an important requirement to fulfil, but still leaving freedom when selecting the digits to compose the set
\mathcal{A}
. For various reasons, such as decreasing the computing demands for operations in
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
, or to enable (on-line) division, we may propose the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
to be symmetric, or to contain the smallest possible digits in modulus.

In the sequel, we first recall the basic terminology and known results about parallel addition, in Section
2
. Then, in Section
3
, we stress the difference between standard and parallel addition, and fix the notation to be used within the algorithms later. Once having a hypothesis, namely a set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
as the candidate for
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
to become a numeration system with parallel addition, we use a so-called Extending Window Method (EWM), which tries, in an automated and systematic way, to derive an algorithm of parallel addition for this numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
. This idea of the EWM (a new result), together with schemes of its main algorithms are presented in Section
4
.

Usage of the Extending Window Method is limited to bases
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
being algebraic integers, and at the same time also
expanding
, i.e., their conjugates must be all greater than
1
in modulus. Convergence of the EWM is guaranteed only for its first phase (generation of the weight coefficients set); whereas for the second phase (assignment of the weight coefficients to all necessary combinations of input digits), we developed tools for revealing non-convergence. This is explained in Section
5
. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, the EWM is a valuable tool in the search for parallel addition algorithms, because the attempts to derive the algorithms manually are very laborious – in fact close to impossible – already for quite small alphabets (e.g. with less than 10 digits).

Section
6
discusses generalization of EWM to bases and alphabets in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
for an algebraic integer
\omega
. It also summarizes the basic information about the actual implementation of the EWM done in SageMath.

Although the EWM does not guarantee finding the parallel addition algorithms in all cases where they exist, it brings a significant number of successful results; especially when considering non-integer alphabets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
, and also for block parallel addition. In Section
7
, we first show that in many (but not all) cases, the EWM provides the same parallel addition algorithms as derived earlier manually. Then, we give examples of new results – parallel addition algorithms obtained only via EWM.

2
Preliminaries and Known Results

2.1
Concept of Parallel Addition

The idea of parallel addition has been formalized using the notion of so-called
p
-local function
:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be alphabets.
A
function

\varphi:\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is said to be
p
-local

if there exist
r,t\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
p=r+t+1
and a function
\phi:\mathcal{B}^{p}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
such that,
for any
w=(w_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and its image
z=\varphi(w)=(z_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
we have
z_{j}=\phi(w_{j+t},\dots,w_{j-r})
for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The parameters
t
and
r
are called
anticipation
and
memory
, respectively.

This means that a window of length
p
computes the digit
z_{j}
of the image
\varphi(w)
from
(w_{j+t},\dots,w_{j-r})
, the digit
z_{j-1}
from digits
(w_{j+t-1},\dots,w_{j-r-1})
etc.

Since two
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representations may be easily summed up digit-wise in parallel, the crucial point of parallel addition is conversion of a
(\beta,\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A})
-representation of the sum to a
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation. The notion of
p
-local function is applied to this conversion.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\beta
be a base and let
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
be alphabets containing
0
. A function
\varphi:\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

[i)]

1.

for any
w=(w_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}
with finitely many non-zero digits,
z=\varphi(w)=(z_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
has only finite number of non-zero digits, and

2.

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}w_{j}\beta^{j}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}z_{j}\beta^{j}

is called
digit set conversion
in the base
\beta
from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{A}
. Such a conversion
\varphi
is said to be
computable in parallel
if
\varphi
is a
p
-local function for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Parallel addition
in a numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
is a digit set conversion in base
\beta
from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}
which is computable in parallel.

2.2
Parallel Addition Algorithms in Minimal Alphabets - Known Results

Naturally, the first class of bases where the concept of parallel addition was introduced and studied are integers. In
[
1
]
, A. Avizienis gave an algorithm for parallel addition in numeration systems with positive integer bases
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
,
\beta\geq 3
and with (symmetric) integer alphabets
\mathcal{A}=\{-a,\ldots,0,\ldots,a\}
, such that
\beta/2<a\leq\beta-1
. The size of thus prescribed alphabets for parallel addition equals
\#\mathcal{A}=2a+1
, which is at least
\beta+2
for
\beta
odd and
\beta+3
for
\beta
even. So the redundancy is a bit higher here (+2 and +3 digits for odd and even bases, respectively) than the minimum needed for parallel addition (just +1 digit on top of the canonical alphabet); but, in turn, the algorithm performing parallel addition is very simple, and the carry propagates by just one position.

The level of redundancy in
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
was decreased to the minimum for even positive integer bases
\beta=2a
(including also
\beta=2
) in the parallel addition algorithm provided in
[
2
]
; using again symmetric alphabets, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}=\{-a,\ldots,0,\ldots,a\}
with
\#\mathcal{A}=2a+1=\beta+1
. And in
[
17
]
, B. Parhami gave the algorithms for all positive integer bases
\beta\geq 2
, both even and odd, on any (generally non-symmetric) alphabets of consecutive integers (containing zero) of the minimal size
\#\mathcal{A}=\beta+1
.

Parallel addition algorithms on (integer) alphabets of the minimal size are provided in
[
5
]
and
[
3
]
for several classes of the bases:

•

positive and negative integers
\beta=\pm b
, with
b\in\mathbb{N},b\geq 2
: on
\#\mathcal{A}=b+1
;

•

positive and negative rational numbers
\beta=\pm a/b
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
co-prime: on
\#\mathcal{A}=a+b
;

•

quadratic Pisot numbers
\beta>1
fulfilling
\beta^{2}=a\beta-b
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
,
a-2\geq b\geq 1
: on
\#\mathcal{A}=a+b-1
;

•

quadratic Pisot numbers
\beta>1
fulfilling
\beta^{2}=a\beta+b
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, and
a-1\geq b\geq 2
or
a\geq b=1
: on
\#\mathcal{A}=a+b+1
;

•

roots of
\beta=\sqrt[\ell]{b}
, with
\ell,b\in\mathbb{N}
,
b\geq 2
: on
\#\mathcal{A}=b+1
(where
\beta
cannot be written as
\beta=\sqrt[\ell^{\prime\prime}]{c}
, with
\ell=\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}
,
b=c^{\ell^{\prime}}
, and
\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime},c\in\mathbb{N}
).

As for the bases being roots of
\beta=\sqrt[\ell]{-b}
, with
b\in\mathbb{N}
,
b\geq 2
, no general formula of parallel addition algorithm on alphabets of minimal size has yet been provided for this class as a whole, but just for selected individual cases (studied in
[
16
,
5
]
), again on integer alphabets:

•

Penney base
\beta=\imath-1
: on
\#\mathcal{A}=5
;

•

Knuth base
\beta=2\imath
: on
\#\mathcal{A}=5
;

•

base
\beta=\imath\sqrt{2}
: on
\#\mathcal{A}=3
.

2.3
Calculating in Blocks

In
[
8
]
, an alternative approach was introduced to perform calculations on
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representations, namely calculating in so-called
k
-blocks. It means that, for a given positive integer
k\geq 1
, we divide the
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation into blocks of
k
digits, and then treat it in fact as a
(\beta^{k},\mathcal{B})
-representation, where the alphabet
\mathcal{B}=\{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}\beta^{j}\,|\,a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}\}
contains new digits with respect to new base
\beta^{k}
.

As to the problem of parallel addition, we explain in Section
2.4
that the
k
-block concept does not broaden the set of eligible bases. Nevertheless, it may help to decrease the size of alphabets (level of redundancy) needed for parallel addition. This is the case e.g. of the
d
-bonacci bases – roots of the minimal polynomials
X^{d}=X^{d-1}+X^{d-2}+\cdots+X+1
for
d\geq 2
: here the block concept decreases the size of the alphabet for parallel addition to just
3
digits, instead of the
d+1
digits necessary for
1
-block parallel addition.

For the so-called Canonical Number Systems (CNS, introduced by B. Kovács in
[
9
]
, and then extensively studied by others), with a complex base
\beta
and alphabet
\{0,1,\ldots,|N(\beta)|-1\}
, where
|N(\beta)|
denotes the norm of
\beta
over
\mathbb{Q}
, it is proved in
[
3
]
that block parallel addition is possible on the alphabets
\{0,1,\ldots,2|N(\beta)|-2\}
or
\{-|N(\beta)|+1,\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,|N(\beta)|-1\}
. The size of these alphabets (
\#\mathcal{A}=2|N(\beta)|-1
) may be substantially smaller than the minimal size of alphabet needed for
1
-block parallel addition in the same base
\beta
. For instance, see Section
7
:

•

Penney base
\beta=\imath-1
requires a
5
-digit alphabet for (
1
-block) parallel addition, but only
3
digits for
k
-block parallel addition: an algorithm using
k=4
is provided in
[
6
]
, and we further diminish the block size to
k=2
in this work;

•

Eisenstein base
\beta=\exp{(2\pi\imath/3)}
requires a
7
-digit alphabet for (
1
-block) parallel addition, but only
5
digits for
k
-block parallel addition: we find an algorithm using
k=3
.

2.4
Necessary Conditions on Bases and Alphabets for Parallel Addition

Let us recall that we still consider just finite
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representations as the operands (summands to be added):

\text{Fin}_{\mathcal{A}}(\beta)=\left\{x=\sum_{j=R}^{L}x_{j}\beta^{j}\,|\,x_{j}\in\mathcal{A},L,R\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}\,,

(1)

and let us denote

\mathcal{A}[\beta]=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{i}\beta^{i}\colon a_{i}\in\mathcal{A},\,N\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathbb{Z}[\beta]=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{i}\beta^{i}\colon a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\,N\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\,.

(2)

For the case of alphabets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}
of consecutive integers, the condition on base
\beta
to allow parallel addition was proved in
[
3
]
, and later in
[
14
]
it was generalized to alphabets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\beta
be a complex number such that
|\beta|>1
. There exists an alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
with
0\in\mathcal{A}
and
1\in\text{Fin}_{\mathcal{A}}(\beta)
which allows
k
-block parallel addition in
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, if and only if
\beta
is an algebraic number with no conjugate of modulus
1
. If this is the case, then there also exists an alphabet of consecutive integers containing
0
which enables
1
-block parallel addition in base
\beta
.

The problem of the minimal alphabet size for parallel addition was again studied first for the case of alphabets of consecutive integers, e.g. in
[
5
]
, and then extended in
[
14
]
to the more general case of
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. Those results provide not just the lower bound on the alphabet size, but also the form of digits that have to be contained in the alphabet, based on congruence classes modulo
\beta
and
\beta-1
.
We recall that
\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
are congruent modulo
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
if and only if
\gamma-\delta\in\alpha\cdot\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If a numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
with
\mathcal{A}[\beta]=\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
allows parallel addition, then the alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
contains at least one representative of each congruence class modulo
\beta
and modulo
\beta-1
in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.

In the case of bases
\beta
being algebraic integers, it is convenient that the lower bound on the size of alphabets for parallel addition can be expressed by means of the minimal polynomial of
\beta
:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
be a numeration system such that
\beta\in\mathbb{C},|\beta|>1
is an algebraic integer with minimal polynomial
m_{\beta}
, and let
\mathcal{A}[\beta]=\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. If
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
allows parallel addition, then
\beta
is expanding and

\#\mathcal{A}\geq\max\{|m_{\beta}(0)|,|m_{\beta}(1)|\}\,.

Moreover, if
\beta
has a positive real conjugate, then

\#\mathcal{A}\geq\max\{|m_{\beta}(0)|,|m_{\beta}(1)|+2\}\,.

For completeness, let us state also the earlier result from
[
5
]
, which is a bit stronger than Theorem
2.5
for the alphabets of consecutive integers, as it does not require that
\mathcal{A}[\beta]=\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
:

Theorem 2.6
.

Consider a base
\beta\in\mathbb{C},|\beta|>1
, being an algebraic integer with minimal polynomial
m_{\beta}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an alphabet of consecutive integers containing
0
and
1
. If addition in
{\rm Fin}_{\mathcal{A}}(\beta)
is computable in parallel, then
\#\mathcal{A}\geq|m_{\beta}(1)|
. If, moreover, the base has a positive real conjugate, then
\#\mathcal{A}\geq|m_{\beta}(1)|+2
.

3
Addition – Standard vs. Parallel

The general idea of addition (standard or parallel) in any numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
is the following: we sum up two numbers digit-wise, and then convert the result with digits in
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
into the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. Obviously, digit-wise addition is computable in parallel, so the problematic part is the digit set conversion of thus obtained result. It can be easily done in a standard way (from right to left), but parallel digit set conversion is non-trivial. Parallel conversion may be based on the same formulas as the standard one, but the choice of so-called
weight coefficients
differs in general.

Let
w_{n^{\prime}}\cdots w_{1}w_{0}\bullet w_{-1}\cdots w_{-m^{\prime}}
be a
(\beta,\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A})
-representation of
w\in\text{Fin}_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}(\beta)
obtained by digit-wise addition of
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representations of summands. We search for a
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation of
w
, i.e., a sequence
z_{n}\cdots z_{1}z_{0}z_{-1}\cdots z_{-m}
such that
z_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
and
z_{n}\cdots z_{1}z_{0}\bullet z_{-1}\cdots z_{-m}=(w)_{\beta,\mathcal{A}}
. Note that the indices
n
and
-m
of the first and the last non-zero digits of the converted representation
(w)_{\beta,\mathcal{A}}=z_{n}\cdots z_{1}z_{0}\bullet z_{-1}\cdots z_{-m}
generally differ from the indices
n^{\prime}
and
-m^{\prime}
of the original representation
(w)_{\beta,{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}}=w_{n^{\prime}}\cdots w_{1}w_{0}\bullet w_{-1}\cdots w_{-m^{\prime}}
. We have
n\geq n^{\prime}
and
m\geq m^{\prime}
; if not, the converted representation is padded by zeros. For easier notation, such representations can be multiplied by
\beta^{m^{\prime}}
. Hence, without loss of generality, we can consider only conversion of elements of
(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A})[\beta]
, i.e., numbers from
\text{Fin}_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}(\beta)
, whose representations have all digits with negative indices equal to zero.

Digits
w_{j}
are converted from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
into the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
by digit-wise addition of suitable representations of zero. Any polynomial
R(x)=r_{s}x^{s}+\dots+r_{1}x+r_{0}
with coefficients
r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
such that
R(\beta)=0
gives a representation of zero in the base
\beta
. Such polynomial
R
is called a
rewriting rule
for
\beta
. One of the coefficients of
R
which is greatest in modulus (so-called
dominant coefficient
) may be used for conversion of a digit from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{A}
. Nevertheless, the Extending Window Method proposed later in Section
4
to generate parallel addition algorithms is strongly dependent on the rewriting rule. Therefore, usage of an arbitrary rewriting rule
R
is not within the scope of this work, and we focus only on the simplest possible representation of zero – rewriting rule deduced from the polynomial

R(x)=x-\beta\in\left(\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\right)[x]\,.

(3)

Since
R(\beta)=0=\beta^{j}\cdot R(\beta)=1\cdot\beta^{j+1}-\beta\cdot\beta^{j}
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a representation of zero in the form
1(-\beta)0\cdots 0\bullet=(0)_{\beta}
, with
-\beta
on the
j
-th position. We multiply this representation by a so-called
weight coefficient

q_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
, in order to obtain another representation of zero in the form

q_{j}(-q_{j}\beta)\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{j}\bullet=(0)_{\beta}\,.

This is digit-wise added to
w_{n}\cdots w_{1}w_{0}\bullet
in order to convert the digit
w_{j}
from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
into the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. Such conversion of the
j
-th digit causes a
carry

q_{j}
onto the
(j+1)
-th position, i.e., to the left neighbour. This is clear from the column notation of digit-wise addition.

w_{n^{\prime}}

\cdots

w_{j+1}

w_{j}

w_{j-1}

\cdots

w_{1}

w_{0}

\bullet

=

(w)_{\beta,{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}}

q_{j-1}

-\beta q_{j-1}

\iddots

=

(0)_{\beta}

\iddots

q_{j}

-\beta q_{j}

=

(0)_{\beta}

z_{n}\cdots z_{n^{\prime}}

\cdots

z_{j+1}

z_{j}

z_{j-1}

\cdots

z_{1}

z_{0}

\bullet

=

(w)_{\beta,{\mathcal{A}}}

Hence, the desired formula for conversion on the
j
-th position is
z_{j}:=w_{j}+q_{j-1}-q_{j}\beta
. The coefficient
q_{-1}
is zero, since there is no carry from the right onto the
0
-th position. The terms
carry
and
weight coefficient
are related to a specific position: a weight coefficient
q_{j-1}
is a carry from the right neighbour
(j-1)
onto the
j
-th position,
q_{j}
is a weight coefficient chosen on the
j
-th position, and thus
q_{j}
is a carry from the
j
-the position onto the
(j+1)
-th position, etc. Therefore, the conversion using the rewriting rule
x-\beta
prolongs the part of non-zero digits only to the left, as there is no carry to the right. So all positions with negative indices remain with zero digits in the converted representation
z_{n}\cdots z_{1}z_{0}\bullet=(w)_{\beta,{\mathcal{A}}}
.

The conversion preserves the value of
w
, since only representations of zero are added, formally

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 0}z_{j}\beta^{j}

\displaystyle=w_{0}-\beta q_{0}+\sum_{j>0}(w_{j}+q_{j-1}-q_{j}\beta)\beta^{j}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 0}w_{j}\beta^{j}+\sum_{j>0}q_{j-1}\beta^{j}-\sum_{j\geq 0}q_{j}\cdot\beta^{j+1}=

(4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 0}w_{j}\beta^{j}+\sum_{j>0}q_{j-1}\beta^{j}-\sum_{j>0}q_{j-1}\cdot\beta^{j}=\sum_{j\geq 0}w_{j}\beta^{j}=w\,.

The weight coefficients
q_{j}
must be chosen so that the converted digits
z_{j}
are in the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, namely,

z_{j}=w_{j}+q_{j-1}-q_{j}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\mathrm{\ for\ any\ }j\in\mathbb{N}\,.

(5)

For standard addition algorithm, the digit set conversion runs from the right (
j=0
) to the left (
j=n
), until all non-zero digits and carries are converted into the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. In this way, determination of the weight coefficients is a trivial task since the carry is known when a weight coefficient is determined.

But when designing parallel addition algorithms, choosing the weight coefficient is the crucial and difficult task, because there might be more possible carries from the right neighboring
(j-1)
-th position that arrive to the
j
-th position. This is done via the Extending Window Method, as described in Section
4
. We require that the digit set conversion from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{A}
is computable in parallel, i.e. there exist constants
r,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that for all
j\geq 0
it holds that
z_{j}=z_{j}(w_{j+t},\dots,w_{j-r})
. In our case, the anticipation
t
equals zero, since we use the rewriting rule
x-\beta
. To avoid dependency on all less significant digits (on the right from the processed position), we need some variety in the choice of the weight coefficient
q_{j}
. This implies that the used numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
must be redundant.

The core difference between standard and parallel addition can be expressed, for conversion of
(\beta,\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A})
-representation
w_{n^{\prime}}\ldots w_{1}w_{0}\bullet=(w)_{\beta,\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}
into
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation
z_{n}\ldots z_{1}z_{0}\bullet=(w)_{\beta,\mathcal{A}}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{standard\ addition:}\qquad z_{j}

\displaystyle=z_{j}(w_{j},\dots,w_{j-r},\dots,w_{0})\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{parallel\ addition:}\qquad z_{j}

\displaystyle=z_{j}(w_{j+t},\dots,w_{j-r})\,.

Remark 3.1
.

Consider a base
\beta\in\mathbb{C},|\beta|>1
, and alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
with sets
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
satisfying:

0\in\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{A}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{B}\,,

(6)

and let
l\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

(\forall x\in\mathcal{A})(\exists d^{(1)},\ldots,d^{(l)}\in\mathcal{D})(x=d^{(1)}+\cdots+d^{(l)})\,.

(7)

Then existence of parallel conversion in base
\beta
from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{A}
implies existence of parallel conversion in base
\beta
from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}
, and thus also existence of parallel addition in the numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
.

Clearly, when summing up two elements
x,y\in\text{Fin}_{\mathcal{A}}(\beta)
expressed as
x=\sum x_{j}\beta^{j},y=\sum y_{j}\beta^{j}
with
x_{j},y_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
, we can use the form (
7
) of each
x_{j}=d_{j}^{(1)}+\cdots+d_{j}^{(l)}
, and denote by
d^{(k)}=\sum d_{j}^{(k)}\beta^{j}
for each
k=1,\ldots,l
. Then, we split the operation
x+y
into
l
operations, gradually producing
z^{(k)}:=d^{(k)}+z^{(k-1)}
for
k=1,\ldots,l
, with
z^{(0)}=y
. Each
k
-th step provides a
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation of
z^{(k)}
via parallel conversion of
d^{(k)}+z^{(k-1)}
from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{A}
. After
l
iterations, we get the desired
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
-representation of
z^{(l)}
in the form

\displaystyle z^{(l)}

\displaystyle=d^{(l)}+z^{(l-1)}=d^{(l)}+(d^{(l-1)}+z^{(l-2)})=\cdots

\displaystyle=d^{(l)}+(d^{(l-1)}+(\cdots+(d^{(1)}+y)))=\sum_{k=1}^{l}d^{(k)}+y=x+y\,.

The number
l\in\mathbb{N}
of iterations is fixed (not depending on
x,y\in\text{Fin}_{\mathcal{A}}(\beta)
), and composition of a fixed number of parallel conversions is still a parallel conversion; so we have the conversion from
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}
done in parallel.

This approach can be easily used for alphabets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}
of consecutive integers, e.g.:

•

\mathcal{A}=\{m,\ldots,0,\ldots,M\}
with
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1\}
,
\mathcal{B}=\{m-1,\ldots,M+1\}
and
l=\max\{-m,M\}
; or

•

\mathcal{A}=\{0,\ldots,M\}
with
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{B}=\{0,\ldots,M+1\}
and
l=M
.

In the case of complex alphabets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
, the application may be useful especially when bigger (non-minimal) alphabets are considered for the parallel addition algorithm.

4
Extending Window Method to Construct Parallel Addition Algorithms

For a numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
such that
\beta
is an algebraic integer and
{\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]}
, and let
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
be an input alphabet with
\mathcal{A}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
, according to Remark
3.1
. The Extending Window Method (EWM) is a newly proposed approach, attempting to construct algorithms for digit set conversion in the base
\beta
from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{A}
computable in parallel. Due to our choice (
3
) of the representation of zero used within the EWM, the resulting parallel addition algorithms always have zero anticipation
t=0
, so carries only from the right.

As mentioned above, the key problem is to find appropriate weight coefficients
q_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
such that

z_{j}=\underbrace{w_{j}}_{\in\mathcal{B}}+\,q_{j-1}-q_{j}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\mathrm{\quad for\ all\quad}j\geq 0\,,

for any input
w\in\text{Fin}_{\mathcal{B}}(\beta)
with
(\beta,\mathcal{B})
-representation
w_{n^{\prime}}\dots w_{1}w_{0}\bullet=(w)_{\beta,\mathcal{B}}
.

The digits
z_{j}
of the result have to satisfy
z_{j}=z_{j}(w_{j},\dots,w_{j-r})
for some fixed memory
r\in\mathbb{N}
, so the carries
q_{j}
have to fulfil
q_{j}=q_{j}(w_{j},\ldots,w_{j-(r-1)})
for the same
r
. But the fact that the converted digit is on the
j
-th position is not important – the conversion must proceed in the same way on every position. Therefore, we simplify the notation by omitting the index
j
from the subscripts. From now on,
w_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
is the converted digit,
w_{-1}w_{-2}\dots\in\mathcal{B}
are its neighboring digits on the right,
q_{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
is the carry from the right, and we search for a weight coefficient
q_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
such that

z_{0}=w_{0}+q_{-1}-q_{0}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\,.

Before describing the Extending Window Method, let us introduce two definitions:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
be a numeration system, and let
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
be a digit set such that
\mathcal{A}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
. Any finite set
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
containing
0
such that

\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{A}+\beta\mathcal{Q}

is called a
weight coefficients set
for the given numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
and input digit set
\mathcal{B}
.

A weight coefficients set
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
satisfies

(\forall w_{0}\in\mathcal{B})(\forall q_{-1}\in\mathcal{Q})(\exists q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q})(\underbrace{w_{0}+q_{-1}-q_{0}\beta}_{z_{0}}\in\mathcal{A})\,.

(8)

In other words, there is a weight coefficient
q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
for any carry
q_{-1}\in\mathcal{Q}
from the right and for any digit
w_{0}
from the input alphabet
\mathcal{B}
, such that
z_{0}=w_{0}+q_{-1}-q_{0}\beta
is in the target alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
be a weight coefficients set for numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
and input digit set
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
q:\mathcal{B}^{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
be a mapping such that

\displaystyle w_{0}+q(w_{-1},\dots,w_{-r})-\beta q(w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)})

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{A}\text{ for\ any }w_{0},w_{-1},\dots,w_{-r}\in\mathcal{B}\,,

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad q(0,\dots,0)

\displaystyle=0\,.

Such mapping
q
is called
weight function of length

r
for
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
and input digit set
\mathcal{B}
.

Having a weight function
q:\mathcal{B}^{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
, we define a function
\phi:\mathcal{B}^{r+1}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
by the formula

\phi(w_{0},\dots,w_{-r})=w_{0}+\underbrace{q(w_{-1},\dots,w_{-r})}_{=q_{-1}}-\beta\underbrace{q(w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)})}_{=q_{0}}=:z_{0}\in\mathcal{A}\,,

(9)

and thus obtain the digit set conversion from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{A}
in base
\beta
as
(r+1)
-local function, with anticipation
0
and memory
r
. The requirement that
q(0,\ldots,0)=0
, i.e., zero output of the weight function
q
for the input of
r
zeros, guarantees that
\phi(0,\dots,0)=0
. Thus, the first condition of Definition
2.2
is satisfied. The second condition follows from the equation (
3
).

Let us recall the principle of the digit set conversion algorithms based on the rewriting rule
x-\beta
. Assume existence of the weight coefficients set
\mathcal{Q}
and the weight function
q:\mathcal{B}^{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
for the given numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
and the input digit set
\mathcal{B}
. To convert
w_{n^{\prime}}\ldots w_{1}w_{0}\bullet=(w)_{\beta,\mathcal{B}}
into
z_{n}\ldots z_{1}z_{0}\bullet=(w)_{\beta,\mathcal{A}}
, first assign the weight coefficients
q\in\mathcal{Q}
for each position – independently, so all at once (in parallel). Then multiply the rewriting rule by the weight coefficients
q
, and add them digit-wise to the input sequence. In fact, it means that the equation (
5
) is applied on each position independently. The digit set conversion is computable in parallel thanks to the fact that the weight coefficients are determined as outputs of the weight function
q
of a fixed length
r
.

In order to enable this way of computation, we introduce the so-called
Extending Window Method
. It works in two phases, for a given numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
and an input digit set
\mathcal{B}
. First, it finds some weight coefficients set
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
, as described in Definition
4.1
. This set
\mathcal{Q}
then serves as the starting point for the second phase, in which we gradually increment the expected length
r
, until the weight function
q:\mathcal{B}^{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
is uniquely defined for each
(w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)})\in\mathcal{B}^{r}
, as required in Definition
4.2
. If both these phases are successful, the local conversion function is finally determined – we use the weight function outputs
q
as the weight coefficients in the formula (
9
).

Further in this section, we describe construction of the weight coefficients sets and the weight functions, in the two phases respectively. Convergence of both phases is then discussed in Section
5
.

4.1
Phase 1 – Weight Coefficients Set

The goal of the first phase is to compute a weight coefficients set
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
, i.e., find a set
\mathcal{Q}\ni 0
such that

\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{A}+\beta\mathcal{Q}\,.

We build a sequence
\mathcal{Q}_{0},\mathcal{Q}_{1},\mathcal{Q}_{2},\dots
of sets

iteratively, by extending
\mathcal{Q}_{k}
to

This procedure is repeated until the extended weight coefficients set
\mathcal{Q}_{k+1}
is the same as the previous set
\mathcal{Q}_{k}

We start with

to cover all sums

and digits

to

with such suitable coefficients, and increase

. And so on, until there is no need to add more elements into

4.2
Phase 2 – Weight Function

In the second phase, we want to find a length
r\in\mathbb{N}
and a weight function
q:\mathcal{B}^{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
. We start with the weight coefficients set
\mathcal{Q}
obtained in Phase 1. The idea is to reduce the number of necessary weight coefficients which are used to convert a given input digit up to just one single value. This is done by increasing gradually the number
r
of considered input digits (to the right). When we know more digits (right neighbours of the processed input digit) that cause the carry from the right, then we may be able to decrease the set of possible carries from the right, and consequently need less weight coefficients to convert the input digit into
\mathcal{A}
.

We introduce a notation for the sets of possible weight coefficients for given input digits. If
w_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
, then
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0}]}
denotes a subset of
\mathcal{Q}
such that

(\forall q_{-1}\in\mathcal{Q})(\exists q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0}]})(w_{0}+q_{-1}-q_{0}\beta\in\mathcal{A})\,.

It means that, since the input digits on the right side from the processed input digit can be arbitrary, any carry
q_{-1}
from the set
\mathcal{Q}
is possible. However, we may be able to limit the set
\mathcal{Q}
to its subset
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0}]}
of weight coefficients which allow the conversion of
w_{0}
to
\mathcal{A}
, due to knowledge of the input digit
w_{0}
itself.

By induction with respect to
k\in\mathbb{N},k\geq 1
, for all
(w_{0},\dots,w_{-k})\in\mathcal{B}^{k+1}
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
denote a subset of
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
such that

(\forall q_{-1}\in\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]})(\exists q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]})(w_{0}+q_{-1}-q_{0}\beta\in\mathcal{A})\,.

The sets
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
of possible weight coefficients, and consequently a weight function
q
, are constructed by Algorithm
3
. The idea is to check all possible right carries
q_{-1}\in\mathcal{Q}
and determine a minimal subset of values
q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
such that

z_{0}=w_{0}+q_{-1}-q_{0}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\,.

So we obtain a subset
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0}]}\subset\mathcal{Q}
of weight coefficients which are necessary to cover the sum of the digit
w_{0}
with any carry
q_{-1}\in\mathcal{Q}
. This is done separately for every
w_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
, so that we obtain such a subset
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0}]}\subset\mathcal{Q}
for all
w_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
. Then, assuming that we know the input digit
w_{-1}
, the set of possible carries from the right is also reduced to
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1}]}
. Thus we may consider the pair
(w_{0},w_{-1})
of input digits, and reduce the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0}]}
to a set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},w_{-1}]}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0}]}
, which is again a minimal subset necessary to cover all elements of
w_{0}+\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1}]}
.

In the
k
-th step, we search for a minimal subset
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
such that

w_{0}+\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]}\subset\mathcal{A}+\beta\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}\,.

Apart from the processed digit itself, we consider here also
k
digits on the right. To construct the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
, we select from
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
such weight coefficients which are necessary to cover the sums of digits
w_{0}+q_{-1}
, with all possible carries
q_{-1}
from the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]}
.

Proceeding in this manner may lead to a unique weight coefficient
q_{0}
for long enough
r
-tuple
of considered input digits
(w_{0},\ldots,w_{-(r-1)})
. If there is
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)}]}=1\mathrm{\quad for\ all\quad}(w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)})\in\mathcal{B}^{r}\,,

then the output
q(w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)})
is defined as the only element of
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)}]}
.
To verify that

z_{0}=\phi(w_{0},\dots,w_{-r})=w_{0}+\underbrace{q(w_{-1},\dots,w_{-r})}_{=q_{-1}}-\beta\underbrace{q(w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)})}_{=q_{0}}

is an element of the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, just recall that
q_{0}=q(w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)})
is the only element of the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(r-1)}]}
,
which was constructed so that

At the same time,

is the only element of
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-r}]}
, which is a subset of
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-(r-1)}]}
.

Unfortunately, finiteness of Phase 2 is not guaranteed. Some ways that might reveal non-convergence are discussed in Section

5.1
Convergence of Phase 1

In this section, we show that, if the Extending Window Method converges, then the base

must be

, i.e., all its conjugates are greater than
1
in modulus. Then we prove that this property – expanding base – is also a sufficient condition for convergence of Phase 1, provided that the alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
contains at least one representative of each congruence class modulo
\beta
in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. We see from Theorem
2.4
that the requirements put on the alphabet

are in line with the necessary conditions on

for parallel addition.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
be an alphabet such that
1\in\mathcal{A}[\beta]
. If the Extending Window Method with the rewriting rule
x-\beta
converges for numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
, then the base
\beta
is expanding.

Proof.

By Corollary 3.6 in
[
14
]
, if the numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
allows parallel addition without anticipation (i.e.,
t=0
in Definition
2.1
), then
\beta
is expanding. Parallel addition produced by the EWM is indeed without anticipation, since there is no carry to the right when using the rewriting rule
x-\beta
.
∎

We need to define a norm in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
, in order to prove Lemma
5.6
, which provides a finite set of weight coefficients
\mathcal{Q}
. Finiteness of
\mathcal{Q}
is crucial for the proof of convergence of Phase 1. We exploit the fact that
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]=\{\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}u_{i}\beta^{i}\colon u_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
d
is the degree of
\beta
, if and only if
\beta
is an algebraic integer. Hence, there is an obvious bijection
\pi:\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{d}
given by

\pi(u)=({u}_{0},{u}_{1},\cdots,{u}_{d-1})^{T}\quad\text{ for every }u=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}u_{i}\beta^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\,.

Using the concept of companion matrix, the additive group
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
can be equipped with multiplication such that the mapping
\pi
is a ring isomorphism (e.g. see
[
11
]
). For our purpose, the following lemma is sufficient.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
be an algebraic integer with the minimal polynomial
m_{\beta}(x)=x^{d}+p_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+p_{1}x+p_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. If
S_{\beta}
is the companion matrix of
m_{\beta}
, i.e.,

S_{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&-p_{0}\\
1&0&\cdots&0&-p_{1}\\
0&1&\cdots&0&-p_{2}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&-p_{d-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}\,,

then
\pi(\beta u)=S_{\beta}\cdot\pi(u)
for any
u=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}u_{i}\beta^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.

Proof.

If
\pi(u)=({u}_{0},{u}_{1},\cdots,{u}_{d-1})^{T}
, then

\displaystyle\pi(\beta u)

\displaystyle=\pi\left(\beta\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}u_{i}\beta^{i}\right)=\pi\left(u_{d-1}\underbrace{(-p_{d-1}\beta^{d-1}-\dots-p_{1}\beta-p_{0})}_{=\beta^{d}}+\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}u_{i}\beta^{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\left(-p_{0}u_{d-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}(u_{i-1}-u_{d-1}p_{i})\beta^{i}\right)

\displaystyle=(-p_{0}u_{d-1},u_{0}-u_{d-1}p_{1},\dots,u_{d-2}-u_{d-1}p_{d-1})=S_{\beta}\cdot\pi(u)\,.

∎

We define a vector norm and a matrix norm induced by a given diagonalizable matrix, and the following Lemma
5.4
shows selected properties of the norm given by the companion matrix
S_{\beta}
and
S_{\beta}^{-1}
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a diagonalizable matrix, and let
P\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a nonsingular matrix which diagonalizes

, i.e.,

for some diagonal matrix

. We define a

\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{M}
by

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{M}:=\left\lVert Px\right\rVert_{2}\quad\mathrm{for\ all}\ x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\,,

where

is the Euclidean norm. A
matrix norm

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{M}
is induced by the vector norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{M}
by

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|A\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{M}:=\sup_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{M}=1}\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{M}\quad\mathrm{for\ all}\ A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\,.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic integer of degree
d
. If
S_{\beta}
is the companion matrix of the minimal monic polynomial
m_{\beta}
of
\beta
, then

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|S_{\beta}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\max\{|\beta^{\prime}|\colon\beta^{\prime}\text{ is conjugate of }\beta\},\text{ and }

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|S_{\beta}^{-1}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{1}{|\beta^{\prime}|}\colon\beta^{\prime}\text{ is a conjugate of }\beta\right\}\,.

Proof.

It is well known that the characteristic polynomial of the companion matrix
S_{\beta}
is
m_{\beta}
(see e.g.
[
7
]
). Since the minimal polynomial
m_{\beta}
has no multiple roots,
S_{\beta}
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{C}
. Namely, there is a nonsingular complex matrix
P
such that
S_{\beta}=P^{-1}DP
, where
D
is diagonal matrix with the conjugates of
\beta
on the diagonal. Therefore, the norms
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{S_{\beta}}
and
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}
are well-defined. Since the matrix
S_{\beta}^{-1}
is also diagonalized by
P
, the vector norms
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{S_{\beta}}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{S_{\beta}^{-1}}
are the same, and so are the induced matrix norms
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}
and
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}^{-1}}
.

Now, we use a known result from matrix theory
[
7
]
: if

is a diagonalizable matrix, then the spectral radius

of the matrix

equals
\rho(M)={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|M\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{M}
. Since the eigenvalues of

are the conjugates of

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|S_{\beta}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}=\rho(S_{\beta})=\max\{|\beta^{\prime}|\colon\beta^{\prime}\text{ is conjugate of }\beta\}\,.

Similarly,

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|S_{\beta}^{-1}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|S_{\beta}^{-1}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}^{-1}}=\rho(S_{\beta}^{-1})=\max\{\frac{1}{|\beta^{\prime}|}\colon\beta^{\prime}\text{ is conjugate of }\beta\}\,,

where we use the fact that the eigenvalues of
S_{\beta}^{-1}
are reciprocal of the eigenvalues of
S_{\beta}
.
∎

Finally, we may define a norm in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\pi
be the isomorphism between
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
and
(\mathbb{Z}^{d},+,\odot_{\beta})
. Using notation from the previous Lemma

, we define
\beta
-norm

\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\beta}:\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
by

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\beta}=\left\lVert\pi(x)\right\rVert_{S_{\beta}}\quad\mathrm{for\ all}\ x\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\,.

An important property of the
\beta
-norm is that, for a given constant
K>0
, there are only finitely many elements of
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
bounded by
K
in this norm. The explanation is as follows: images of elements of
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
under the isomorphism

are integer vectors, and there are only finitely many integer vectors in any finite-dimensional vector space bounded by any norm. It is a consequence of equivalence of all norms on a finite-dimensional vector space.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
be an expanding algebraic integer of degree
d
. If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
such that
\mathcal{A}
contains at least one representative of each congruence class modulo

\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
such that
\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{A}+\beta\mathcal{Q}

Proof.

We use the mapping
\pi:\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and the
\beta
-norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\beta}
to give a bound on elements of
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. Let
\gamma
be the smallest conjugate of
\beta
in modulus. Denote

. Consequently, set

R:=\frac{C}{|\gamma|-1}\mathrm{\quad and\quad}\mathcal{Q}:=\{q\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\colon\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\beta}\leq R\}\,.

(10)

By Lemma
5.4
, we have

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|S_{\beta}^{-1}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}=\max\left\{\frac{1}{|\beta^{\prime}|}\colon\beta^{\prime}\text{ is conjugate of }\beta\right\}=\frac{1}{|\gamma|}\,.

Also, we have
|\gamma|>1
, as
\beta
is an expanding algebraic integer. Since

, the set

is non-empty. Any element

and

can be written as

for some

and

due to the presence of at least one representative of each congruence class modulo

in

. Using the isomorphism

, we may write
\pi(q^{\prime})=S^{-1}_{\beta}\cdot\pi(b-a+q)
. We prove that

\displaystyle\left\lVert q^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\pi\left(q^{\prime}\right)\right\rVert_{S_{\beta}}=\left\lVert S^{-1}_{\beta}\cdot\pi\left(b-a+q\right)\right\rVert_{S_{\beta}}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|S^{-1}_{\beta}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{S_{\beta}}\left\lVert b-a+q\right\rVert_{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\gamma|}\left(\left\lVert b-a\right\rVert_{\beta}+\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\beta}\right)\leq\frac{1}{|\gamma|}\left(C+R\right)=\frac{C}{|\gamma|}\left(1+\frac{1}{|\gamma|-1}\right)=R\,.

Hence

, and thus

. Since there are only finitely many elements of

bounded by the constant

must be finite.
∎

. We prove by induction that all intermediate weight coefficient sets

. Let us start with

, whose elements are bounded by any positive constant. Suppose that the intermediate weight coefficients set

has elements bounded by the constant

5.2
Convergence of Phase 2

We do not have any straightforward conditions, sufficient or necessary, for convergence of Phase 2 of the EWM, based on properties of the base
\beta
or alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. Nevertheless, the non-convergence can be controlled during the course of the algorithm. An easy check of non-convergence can be done by finding the weight coefficient sets
\mathcal{Q}_{[b,\dots,b]}
for each
b\in\mathcal{B}
. For that purpose, we introduce a notion of so-called
stable Phase 2
,
which is then used also in the main result of this section: the control of non-convergence during Phase 2 is transformed into searching for a cycle in a directed graph.

Firstly, we mention several equivalent conditions of non-convergence of Phase 2:

Lemma 5.8
.

The following statements are equivalent for the EWM applied on a numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
and an input alphabet
\mathcal{B}
:

[i)]

1.

Phase 2 of the EWM does not converge;

2.

(\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N})\,(\exists\,(w_{0},\dots,w_{-k})\in\mathcal{B}^{k+1})\,(\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}\geq 2)
;

3.

(\exists\,(w_{-j})_{j\geq 0}\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{N}})(\exists\,k_{0}\in\mathbb{N})(\forall k\geq k_{0})(\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}=\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}\geq 2)
.

Proof.

i)
\iff
ii):
The
while
loop in Algorithm
3
ends if and only if there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}=1
for all
(w_{0},\dots,w_{-k})\in\mathcal{B}^{k+1}
.

ii)
\iff
iii):
For
\Rightarrow
, there is an infinite sequence
(w_{-j})_{j\geq 0}
such that
\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}\geq 2
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, since
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}\supset\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k+1)}]}
. Hence, the sequence of integers
(\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]})_{k\geq 0}
is eventually constant. The opposite implication is trivial.
∎

We need to ensure that the choice of a possible weight coefficients set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
is determined by the input digits
(w_{0},\ldots,w_{-k})\in\mathcal{B}^{k+1}
and the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]}
, while the influence of the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
is limited. This is formalized in the following definition:

Definition 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an alphabet of input digits. We say that
Phase 2
of the EWM is
stable
if

\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]}=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}\implies\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}

for all
k\in\mathbb{N},k\geq 2
and for all
(w_{0},\dots,w_{-k})\in\mathcal{B}^{k+1}
.

Although this definition may seem restrictive, it is actually a natural way how an algorithm should be designed. The set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
is constructed so that

\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]}\subset\mathcal{A}+\beta\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}\,,

i.e., there is no reason to choose the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
as a proper subset of
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
, as we know that

\mathcal{B}+\underbrace{\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}}_{=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]}}\subset\mathcal{A}+\beta\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}\,,

and
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
was chosen as sufficient. In other words, if
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
is a proper subset of
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
, then the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
could have been chosen as
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
in the previous iteration step already.

We may guarantee that Phase 2 is stable by wrapping any way of choice of the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}
into a simple

[
12
]
for details.

Now we use the fact that finiteness of Phase 2 implies existence of a length

such that, for every

, the set

contains only one element; where
\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m}
is a shorter notation for
\mathcal{Q}_{[\underbrace{\scriptstyle b,\dots,b}_{m}]}\,.

The following theorem shows that

must decrease every time we increase the length

Theorem 5.10
.

If
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
are such that the sets
\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}-1}
produced by a stable Phase 2 of the EWM have the same size, then

\#\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m}=\#\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}\qquad\mathrm{\quad for\ every\quad}m\geq m_{0}-1\,.

Particularly, if
\#\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}\geq 2
, then the Phase 2 of the EWM does not converge.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}-1}
, the assumption of the same size implies
\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}=\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}-1}\,.
By the assumption that Phase 2 is stable, we have

\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}=\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}-1}\quad\implies\quad\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}+1}=\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}\quad\implies\quad\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}+2}=\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}+1}\quad\implies\quad\cdots

This implies the statement. If
\#\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle m_{0}}\geq 2
, then statement
iii)
in Lemma
5.8
holds for the sequence
(b)_{j\geq 0}
.
∎

We use this result as follows: For all input digits
b\in\mathcal{B}
, we run Algorithm
3
limited only to
k
-tuples
(b,\ldots,b)\in\mathcal{B}^{k}
. In every iteration
k
, we check whether
\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle k}
is smaller than
\mathcal{Q}_{[\scriptstyle b]}^{\scriptstyle k-1}
. If not, then it does not converge for the input
b\cdots b
, and hence the original EWM with input alphabet
\mathcal{B}
does not converge either. This check is linear in the length of the window, and thus it is fast.

In general, it happens that
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}
for some combination of input digits
(w_{0},\dots,w_{-k})\in\mathcal{B}^{k+1}
, and Phase 2 of the EWM still does converge. Thus, a condition which signifies non-convergence during Phase 2 is more complicated. It can be formulated as searching for an infinite path in a so-called
Rauzy graph
:

Definition 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an alphabet of input digits, and let Phase 2 of the EWM be stable.
Consider
k\in\mathbb{N},k\geq 2
. We set

\displaystyle V_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k})\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\colon\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k}]}=\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-(k-1)}]}\right\}\,\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle E_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k})\rightarrow(w^{\prime}_{-1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{-k})\in V_{k}\times V_{k}\colon\vphantom{(w^{\prime}_{-1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{-(k-1)})}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.(w_{-2},\dots,w_{-k})=(w^{\prime}_{-1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{-(k-1)})\right\}\,.

The directed graph
G_{k}=(V_{k},E_{k})
is called
Rauzy graph of Phase 2 (for the length
k
)
.

This term comes from combinatorics on words. The vertices of the Rauzy graph
G_{k}
are combinations of input digits for which the size of their possible weight coefficients sets did not decrease with an increment of the length
k
; whereas in combinatorics on words, the vertices are given as factors of some language. But the directed edges are placed in the same manner – if some combination of digits without the first digit equals another combination without the last digit.

The structure of the Rauzy graph
G_{k}
signifies whether the non-decreasing combinations are such that they cause non-convergence of Phase 2. Existence of an infinite walk in
G_{k}
implies that Phase 2 does not converge:

Theorem 5.12
.

Let Phase 2 of the EWM be stable. If there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N},k_{0}\geq 2
, and
(w_{0},\dots,w_{-k_{0}})\in\mathcal{B}^{k_{0}+1}
such that

[i)]

1.

\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}-1)}]}>1
and

2.

there is an infinite walk
((w^{(i)}_{-1},\dots,w^{(i)}_{-k_{0}}))_{i\geq 1}
in
G_{k_{0}}
starting in the vertex
(w^{(1)}_{-1},\dots,w^{(1)}_{-k_{0}})=(w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k_{0}})\,,

then Phase 2 does not converge.

Proof.

Let us set
(w_{k})_{k\geq 0}:=w_{0},w_{1}^{(1)},\dots,w^{(1)}_{k_{0}-1},w^{(1)}_{k_{0}},w_{k_{0}}^{(2)},w_{k_{0}}^{(3)},w_{k_{0}}^{(4)},\dots
; and we prove that

\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}=\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}-1)}]}>1\mathrm{\quad for\ all\quad}k\geq k_{0}-1\,,

so the condition
iii)
in Lemma
5.8
is satisfied. Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
(w_{-(1+\ell)},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell)})
is a vertex of
G_{k_{0}}
, the set
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-\ell},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell)}]}
equals
\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-\ell},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell-1)}]}
. As Phase 2 is stable, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-\ell},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell)}]}=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-\ell},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell-1)}]}

\displaystyle\implies\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-(\ell-1)},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell)}]}=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-(\ell-1)},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell-1)}]}

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

\displaystyle\implies\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell)}]}=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{-1},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell-1)}]}

\displaystyle\implies\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell)}]}=\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}+\ell-1)}]}\,.

Hence,
\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-k}]}=\#\mathcal{Q}_{[w_{0},\dots,w_{-(k_{0}-1)}]}>1
for all
k\geq k_{0}-1
.
∎

Let us remark that existence of an infinite walk in a finite graph is equivalent to existence of a cycle in the graph. Thus, if there is an infinite walk, we may find another one whose sequence of vertices is eventually periodic. This fact can be used for revealing non-convergence of Phase 2 during its run. Namely, in
k
-th iteration, we construct the Rauzy graph
G_{k+1}
and check whether there is an infinite walk in
G_{k}
starting in
(w_{-1},\dots,w_{-k_{0}})
. This modification of Phase 2 is elaborated in
[
12
]
.

6
Implementation of Extending Window Method

We implement the Extending Window Method (EWM) in a more general setting than explained above. Let
\omega
be an algebraic integer. Parallel addition is searched for a numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
such that
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
and
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
. All elements of
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
are algebraic integers, since
\omega
is an algebraic integer, and clearly
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\subset\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
. We run the EWM just as described above, but all steps being now computed in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, instead of
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.

Let us remark that, for instance, the necessary condition from Theorem
2.4
, that
\mathcal{A}
must contain all representatives modulo
\beta-1
, is not valid in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
anymore, since congruence classes in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
are different if
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
– see the discussion in conclusion of
[
14
]
. Nevertheless, the statements in Theorem
5.1
(
\beta
must be expanding), Theorem
5.7
(convergence of Phase 1),
and Theorems
5.10
and
5.12
(control of convergence of Phase 2) can be proven also for
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, see
[
12
]
.

Our implementation of the EWM in SageMath can be downloaded from
[
13
]
. User information is provided in
readme.md
, and more details about the implementation can be found also in
[
12
]
. We remark that the program allows to choose an algebraic integer
\omega
by means of its minimal polynomial
m_{\omega}
, then the base
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
and the alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
. An input alphabet
\mathcal{B}\subsetneq\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
or a block length
k\in\mathbb{N}
for
k
-block approach may be specified.

One can also select various methods of choice in Phase 1 and in Phase 2. The Algorithm
2
above describes methods encoded by
’1b’
(absolute value) and
’1d’
(
\beta
-norm) for Phase 1. The ways of choice in the Algorithm
4
for Phase 2 can be selected by
’2b’
(center of gravity) and
’2d’
(
\beta
-norm). Description of other methods available in the program implementation can be found in
[
12
]
or directly in the source code.

Before starting Phase 2, the program checks whether it converges for inputs consisting of repetition of a single digit
(b,\ldots,b)
, and stops if not, according to Theorem
5.10
.
During the computation of Phase 2, Rauzy graphs are constructed, and the computation stops if the conditions of Theorem
5.12
are satisfied. In other words, non-finiteness of the EWM can be revealed by these checks.

Results of the program for selected examples from Section
7
are available at
[
15
]
. Various combinations of methods in Phase 1 and Phase 2 are included. Log-files provide information about the course of the computations, and the resulting weight functions
q
are stored in CSV format when the EWM is successful.

7
Results Obtained by Extending Window Method

This chapter shows selected results of the Extending Window Method (EWM). Firstly, we focus on numeration systems with parallel addition algorithms known from previous works of various authors – we investigate whether the EWM delivers the same algorithms as they have developed manually. Then, we describe a set of new results – parallel addition algorithms not known so far.

.

7.4.1
Penney Base with Integer Alphabet:
1
-block

We consider
5
-digit integer alphabets, e.g. non-negative
\mathcal{A}=\{0,\dots,4\}
or symmetric
\mathcal{A}=\{-2,\dots,2\}
. The EWM does not work here: although Phase 1 finds successfully the weight coefficient sets
\mathcal{Q}
, there are several digits
b\in\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
not passing the one-letter-input part of Phase 2 in the EWM algorithm.

But we can obtain the parallel addition algorithms due to the fact that
\beta_{P}^{4}=-4
, and using the results obtained earlier for the negative integer base
\gamma=-4
. The modification of algorithm from base
\gamma=-4
to
\beta_{P}=-1+\imath=\sqrt[4]{-4}
, with the same alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, is quite simple. We perform conversion of digits from
w_{j}\in\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}
to

, by means of the same weight coefficients
q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{for}\ (\gamma,\mathcal{A}):

\displaystyle\qquad z_{j}:=w_{j}+q_{j-1}-q_{j}\gamma=w_{j}+q_{j-1}+4q_{j}\qquad\ \mathrm{where}\quad q_{j}=q_{j}(w_{j},w_{j-1})\,;

\displaystyle\mathrm{for}\ (\beta_{P},\mathcal{A}):

\displaystyle\qquad z_{j}:=w_{j}+q_{j-4}-q_{j}\beta_{P}^{4}=w_{j}+q_{j-4}+4q_{j}\qquad\mathrm{where}\quad q_{j}=q_{j}(w_{j},w_{j-4})\,.

While in
(-4,\mathcal{A})
, we had
z_{j}=z_{j}(w_{j},q_{j},q_{j-1})=z_{j}(w_{j},w_{j-1},w_{j-2})
, and so parallel addition was a
3
-local function, now in
(-1+\imath,\mathcal{A})
we obtain

, and thus
9
-local function of parallel addition, with memory
r=8
and anticipation
t=0
.

For the example of symmetric alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2\}
, the weight function
q_{j}:(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A})^{2}\rightarrow\{0,\pm 1\}=\mathcal{Q}
for parallel addition in
(\beta_{P},\mathcal{A})
has the following form:

\displaystyle q_{j}=+1

\displaystyle\mathrm{for}

\displaystyle(w_{j}\leq-3)\quad\mathrm{or}\quad(w_{j}=-2\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ w_{j-4}\geq+2)

\displaystyle q_{j}=-1

\displaystyle\mathrm{for}

\displaystyle(w_{j}\geq+3)\quad\mathrm{or}\quad(w_{j}=+2\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ w_{j-4}\leq-2)

\displaystyle q_{j}=0\ \

\displaystyle\mathrm{otherwise}\,.

So here we have example of numeration systems
(\beta_{P},\mathcal{A})
where parallel addition algorithms exist, but the EWM it not able to find them, although its prerequisites are fulfilled.

7.4.2
Penney Base with Complex Alphabet:
1
-block

Using the fact that
\pm\imath\equiv_{-2+\imath}\pm 2
and the Penney base
\beta_{P}
fulfils

, we can move from the integer alphabet

to the complex alphabet

, still containing representatives of all
5

, i.e.,

and anticipation

, so

Having an alphabet

. The weight function has six arguments, so the maximum size of the look-up table could be
\#(\mathcal{A}_{P}+\mathcal{A}_{P})^{6}=13^{6}=4\,826\,809
. The algorithm obtained by EWM results in the look-up table for

of somewhat smaller size
2\,165\,713
– but still, rather disadvantageous.

7.5

Let us denote by

\beta_{E}
generates the set

(11)

Again, we derive the minimum size of alphabets
\mathcal{A}_{E}
(integer or complex) allowing
1
-block parallel addition in this base, due to Theorem
2.5
, as
\#\mathcal{A}_{E}\geq\max\{|m_{\beta_{E}}(0)|,|m_{\beta_{E}-1}(0)|\}=\max\{3,7\}=7\,.
For
k
-block parallel addition, the alphabet size of
5
is suggested in Remark
7.1
.

7.5.1
Eisenstein Base with Integer Alphabet:
1
-block

We consider
7
-digit integer alphabets, non-negative
\mathcal{A}=\{0,\ldots,6\}
or symmetric
\mathcal{A}=\{-3,\ldots,3\}
, and apply the EWM on such numeration systems – but unsuccessfully. Phase 1 does find the weight coefficient sets
\mathcal{Q}
, but there are several digits

not passing the one-letter-input part of Phase 2 in the EWM.

Parallel addition algorithms for the Eisenstein base
\beta_{E}
with the mentioned integer alphabets

could be found manually, using the rewriting rule

, but with quite complex weight function; and the resulting parallel addition function would be

7.5.2

1
-block

We have applied the EWM successfully on the following alphabet

(12)

The EWM provides parallel addition algorithm in numeration system

, as follows:

•

the weight coefficients set is, by coincidence, equal to

of size

;

•

the memory parameter

, i.e., each weight coefficient

depends on (at most)

•

thus

, i.e., the parallel addition is a

r=3
means that description of the weight coefficient function
q_{j}:(\mathcal{A}_{E}+\mathcal{A}_{E})^{3}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{E}
requires to provide the values

. This look-up table can be economized by making use of the

\mathcal{A}_{E}
,
\mathcal{A}_{E}+\mathcal{A}_{E}

8
Conclusions

The (automated) Extending Window Method (EWM) for construction of parallel addition algorithms, as proposed and elaborated in this work, can be regarded in fact as generalization of the intuitive (manual) methods used by other authors earlier. For instance, the EWM delivers the same parallel addition algorithms for integer bases as found previously by
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
17
]
, or
[
5
]
, as illustrated in Table
1
.

When considering certain classes of real or complex bases (quadratic, or
\ell
-th roots of integers), the EWM does provide parallel addition algorithms on (integer or non-integer) alphabets of minimal size, however, some of the underlying local functions are rather complex, even more than in some of the algorithms found manually earlier. See selected examples of real bases in Tables
2
and
3
, and of complex bases in Tables
4
and
5
.

In the case of complex bases and non-integer alphabets, manual search for the parallel addition algorithms is extremely laborious (if possible at all); but the EWM could do that successfully (including block parallel addition as well), e.g. for the Penney and Eisenstein bases, samples of the Canonical Numeration Systems, as summarized in Table
6
.

Open problems remaining for future analysis regarding the Extending Window Method for construction of parallel addition algorithms are mainly the following:

•

Generalization of the Extending Window Method implementation using an arbitrary rewriting rule, instead of just
(X-\beta)
. This may be useful especially for numeration systems
(\beta,\mathcal{A})
with non-integer base
\beta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
and integer alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}
.

•

Answering the open question of Phase 2 convergence within the Extending Window Method. This would require deeper analysis of underlying mathematical structures for the various methods considered to select the weight coefficients while iterating within Phase 2.
