A lower bound for essential covers of the cube
Gal Yehuda, Amir Yehudayoff

Abstract
. Essential covers were introduced by Linial and Radhakrishnan as a model that captures two complementary properties: (1) all variables must be included and (2) no element is redundant. In their seminal paper, they proved that every essential cover of the n n -dimensional hypercube must be of size at least Ω ⁡ ( n 0.5 ) \Omega(n^{0.5}) . Later on, this notion found several applications in complexity theory. We improve the lower bound to Ω ⁡ ( n 0.52 ) \Omega(n^{0.52}) , and describe two applications.



1.
Introduction

The vertices of the hypercube can be embedded in Euclidean space
as
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
A hyperplane
h=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle z,v\rangle=\mu\}
covers the vertex
x
if
x\in h
.

What is the minimum number of hyperplanes that are needed
to cover all vertices?

This question is motivated by problems in combinatorial geometry
[
8
]
,
combinatorics
[
1
,
7
]
,
proof complexity
[
4
,
5
]
and more.
For an introduction to this and related topics, see the survey by Saks
[
11
]
.

The two hyperplanes
\{z_{1}=1\}
and
\{z_{1}=-1\}
cover all vertices.
This solution is optimal but also degenerate and not so interesting.
There are two standard ways for defining non-degenerate covers:
skew and essential covers.
In a skew cover, all normal vectors have full support (see e.g.
[
11
,
13
]

and references within).
In an essential cover, all variables must appear
and no hyperplane is redundant
[
7
]
.
Our focus is on the latter.

Definition 1

(Linial and Radhakrishnan
[
7
]
)
.

The hyperplanes
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
form an essential cover of the
n
-cube if

(E1)

for every vertex
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, there is
i\in[k]
so that
x\in h_{i}
.

(E2)

for every
j\in[n]
, there is
i\in[k]
such that the normal
v_{i}
of
h_{i}
satisfies
v_{i,j}\neq 0
.

(E3)

for every
i\in[k]
, there is a vertex
x
so that
x
is covered only by
h_{i}
.

Condition (E
1
) means that
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}

form a cover.
Condition (E
2
) says that every variable
“appears” in at least one of the hyperplanes.
Condition (E
3
) means that no strict subset
of
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
form a cover (“criticality” or “minimality”).

Linial and Radhkrishnan proved that the smallest size
e(n)
of an essential cover of the
n
-cube satisfies

\tfrac{1}{2}(\sqrt{4n+1}+1)\leq e(n)\leq\lceil\tfrac{n}{2}\rceil.

When the hyperplanes are assumed to be
positive
,
a sharp
n+1
lower bound was proved by Saxton
[
12
]
.
Saxton also reduced the general case to a problem
on permanents of matrices.
We provide the first improvement over the lower bound from
[
7
]
.

Theorem
.

e(n)\geq\Omega(n^{0.52})
.

Our proof-plan is straightforward.
Show that few hyperplanes must miss a vertex of the cube.
But this is false; two hyperplanes can cover the whole cube.
We must use the essential condition.

How?

Our high-level approach is inspired by ideas from
[
13
]

where a lower bound for the slicing problem was proved.
Specifically,
we rely on the connection to convex
geometry that was established in
[
13
]
.
The lower bound is based on Bang’s solution
of Tarski’s plank problem
[
3
,
2
]
.

Proving lower bounds for the slicing problem is in general harder than for covering problems.
For example, any lower bound for the slicing problem
immediately implies a lower bound for the skew covering problem
[
13
]
.
In the slicing problem, however, we can assume that the normal vectors
are generic. This assumption is powerful, e.g., when proving anti-concentration results.
In essential covers, a major difficulty is that the hyperplanes are not generic;
a small perturbation of a cover is not a cover.
This leads to several new challenges,
and requires developing a better structural understanding
of the few but unknown hyperplanes.

1.1.
Applications

The theorem immediately implies two new lower bounds in proof complexity,
as we briefly describe next.

The first lower bound is for
resolution over linear equations
\text{Res}(\text{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}})
.
This proof system was introduced by Raz and Tzameret
[
10
]
as an extension of resolution with “counting capabilities”.
Part and Tzameret
[
9
]
studied
tree-like proofs in this system.
They proved the following lower bound.
Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}
be linear functions in
n
variables over
\mathbb{Q}
where each depends on at least
\frac{n}{2}
variables.
Then, any tree-like
\text{Res}(\text{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}})
derivation of
a tautology of the form
\vee_{j\in[N]}g_{j}=0
is of size
2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
.
The
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
in the exponent is in fact
\Omega(e(n))

and follows from Linial and Radhkrishnan’s
lower bound.
Our result immediately improves the lower bound
to
2^{\Omega(n^{0.52})}
.

The second application is in the stabbing planes proof system.
This proof system was introduced by Beame et al. as an extension of cutting planes that is more similar to
algorithms that are used in practice
[
4
]
.
Dantchev, Galesi, Ghani, and Martin proved a lower bound of
\Omega(n)
on the refutation-size of Tseitin formulas over the
n\times n
grid
[
5
]
.
Their lower bound is in fact of the form
\Omega(e(n^{2}))
.
Our result thus yields the better lower bound
\Omega(n^{1.04})
.

2.
Finding the uncovered vertex

Assume towards a contradiction that the
k\leq\tfrac{n^{0.52}}{10}
hyperplanes defined by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
form an essential cover of the hypercube for large
n
.
Our goal is to locate a vertex
u
that is not covered.
This is done in three phases. In each phase we “discover” some more coordinates in
u
.

2.1.
Finding structure

Let
V
be the
k\times n
matrix whose rows are
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
.
The analysis is based on a structural lemma for
V
that is stated below.
Figure
1
may help in understanding the lemma.
The proof of the lemma is deferred to Section
3
.
To state the lemma, we need the following definitions.
Denote by
\|v\|_{0}
the sparsity of
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;
i.e.,
\|v\|_{0}
is the number of non-zero entries in
v
.

Definition 2

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
C_{0}>1
be the constant from Claim 10 in
[
13
]
.
A vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has many scales if
v
can be partitioned into
S:=\lfloor n^{0.001}\rfloor
vectors
v^{(1)},v^{(2)},\ldots,v^{(S)}
such that

\|v^{(s)}\|_{2}\geq C_{0}\|v^{(s+1)}\|_{2}

for all
s<S
.
The smallest scale of
v
is defined to be
\|v^{(S)}\|_{2}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
be the matrix defined above
(its rows are
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
and
k\leq\frac{n^{0.52}}{10}
).
Then, there is a partition of the rows of
V
to four parts
[k]=K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cup K_{3}\cup K_{4}
and a partition of the columns to three parts
[n]=N_{1}\cup N_{2}\cup N_{3}
with
|N_{1}|\geq\tfrac{n}{2}
such that the following hold:

(1)

Every column in
N_{1}\cup N_{2}
has sparsity at most
n^{0.04}
.

(2)

For every row
i\in K_{1}
, the
N_{1}\cup N_{2}
coordinates of
v_{i}
are zero.

(3)

For every row
i\in K_{2}
, the
N_{1}
coordinates of
v_{i}
are zero, and there are at least
4|K_{2}|^{2}
non-zero elements in the
N_{2}
coordinates of
v_{i}
.

(4)

For
i\in K_{3}
, let
v^{\prime}_{i}
be the projection of
v_{i}

to the
N_{1}
coordinates.
The vector
v^{\prime}_{i}
is non-zero.
Let
\phi_{i}>0
be so that
\|\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{i}\|_{2}=1
.
For every column
j\in N_{1}
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in K_{3}}\phi_{i}^{2}{v^{\prime}}^{2}_{ij}<n^{-0.196}.

In particular, item (
1
) implies

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in K_{3}}|\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{ij}|<\sqrt{n^{0.04}n^{-0.196}}\leq n^{-0.078}.

(5)

For every row
i\in K_{4}
, the projection of
v_{i}
to the
N_{1}\cup N_{2}
coordinates has many scales, its smallest scale is non-zero, and the position of the smallest scale contains
N_{1}
.

V
.

2.2.
Phase I

In this phase, we fix the
N_{3}
coordinates in
u
.
We know that
|N_{3}|<n
.
Condition (E
2
) implies that
K_{1}\neq[k]
.
Condition (E
3
) implies
that there is a vertex
x
that is not covered by the hyperplanes in
K_{1}
.
For every
j\in N_{3}
set
u_{j}=x_{j}
.
The rest of the entries in
u
shall be determined in later phases.
Denote the part of
u
that was just fixed by
u^{(I)}
.
The
u^{(I)}
part of
u
deals with all hyperplanes in
K_{1}
.

2.3.
Phase II

In this phase, we fix the
N_{2}
coordinates in
u
.
This is done with the aid of randomness.
The randomness simultaneously deals with rows in
K_{2}
and in
K_{4}
.
We denote by
v^{\prime}_{i}
the projection of
v_{i}
to the
N_{1}
coordinates,
by
v^{\prime\prime}_{i}
the projection to the
N_{2}
coordinates,
and by
v^{(I)}_{i}
the projection to coordinates in
N_{3}
.

Claim 4
.

There is
x^{\prime\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{2}}
so that

(1)

For every
i\in K_{2}
,

\langle x^{\prime\prime},v_{i}^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\langle u^{(I)},v_{i}^{(I)}\rangle\neq\mu_{i}.

(2)

For every
i\in K_{4}
and
x^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{1}}
,

|\langle x^{\prime\prime},v_{i}^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\langle u^{(I)},v_{i}^{(I)}\rangle-\mu_{i}|>|\langle x^{\prime},v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle|.

Choose the
N_{2}
coordinates of
u
to be
x^{\prime\prime}
.
Denote this part of
u
by
u^{(II)}
.
The first condition implies that
u^{(II)}
deals with all rows in
K_{2}
.
The second condition implies that it deals with all rows in
K_{4}
,
regardless of the future choice of the
N_{1}
coordinates in
u
.

In order to prove the claim, we need the following two anti-concentration results.
The first is known as the Littlewood-Offord lemma
[
8
]

and was proved by Erdős
[
6
]
.
The second appears in
[
13
]
.
We denote by
x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
a uniformly random vertex
of the cube.

Claim 5

(
[
8
,
6
]
)
.

For all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\|v\|_{0}>0
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
,

\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[\langle x,v\rangle=a]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\|v\|_{0}}}.

Lemma 6

(
[
13
]
)
.

There is a constant
C_{1}>1
so that the following holds.
If
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has many scales and its smallest scale is
\delta>0
, then for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\geq 2
we have

\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[|\langle x,v\rangle-a|\leq b\delta]<C_{1}\exp\Big(-\frac{S}{C_{1}}+C_{1}\log(b)\Big).

Proof of Claim
4
.

Pick
x^{\prime\prime}
uniformly at random.
First, consider
i\in K_{2}
.
By Claim
5
,
because
\|v^{\prime\prime}_{i}\|_{0}\geq 4|K_{2}|^{2}
,

\Pr_{x^{\prime\prime}}[\langle x^{\prime\prime},v_{i}^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\langle u^{(I)},v_{i}^{(I)}\rangle=\mu_{i}]\leq\frac{1}{2|K_{2}|}.

Second, consider
i\in K_{4}
.
Row
v_{i}
has many scales and
v^{\prime}_{i}
is part of its smallest scale.
Let
\alpha
be the vector consisting of the largest
S-1
scales of
v_{i}
.
The vector of the smallest scale in
v_{i}
has two parts:
v^{\prime}_{i}
and the part outside the
N_{1}
columns which we denote by
\beta
.
Partition
x^{\prime\prime}
to
x_{\alpha}
,
x_{\beta}
accordingly.
Let
\delta>0
be the smallest scale of
\alpha
.
By Cauchy-Schwartz, for all
x^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{1}}
,

|\langle x^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{i}\rangle|\leq\sqrt{n}\|v^{\prime}_{i}\|_{2}<\sqrt{n}\delta.

By Lemma
6
, conditioned on the value of
x_{\beta}
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{x_{\alpha}}[|\langle x^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}_{i}\rangle+\langle u^{(I)},v_{i}^{(I)}

\displaystyle\rangle-\mu_{i}|\leq\sqrt{n}\delta]\leq C_{1}\exp\Big(-\frac{S-1}{C_{1}}+C_{1}\log n\Big).

The same bound holds when we average over
x_{\beta}
as well.

Finally, the union bound over
K_{2}\cup K_{4}
completes the proof.
∎

2.4.
Phase III

It remains to choose the
N_{1}
coordinates in
u
.
Let
\tilde{u}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{2}\cup N_{3}}
denote the part of
u
that was already fixed
(it is comprised of
u^{(I)}
and
u^{(II)}
).
Let
\tilde{v}_{i}
be projection of
v_{i}

to the
N_{2}\cup N_{3}
coordinates.

Claim 7
.

There is
x^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{1}}
so that for all
i\in K_{3}
,

\langle x^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{i}\rangle+\langle\tilde{u},\tilde{v}_{i}\rangle\neq\mu_{i}.

Before proving the final claim, let us see how it completes the proof
of the theorem.
The final part
u^{(III)}
of
u
is
x^{\prime}
.
The vertex
u
is indeed not covered by any of the hyperplanes:
All hyperplanes in
K_{1}
are avoided due to
u^{(I)}
,
because the rest of their entries are zero.
All hyperplanes in
K_{2}
are avoided due to
u^{(II)}
,
because the rest of their entries are zero.
All hyperplanes in
K_{3}
are avoided due to
u^{(III)}
.
All hyperplanes in
K_{4}
are avoided due to
u^{(II)}
as well,
because
u^{(III)}
has no real affect.

A key piece of the argument is a lemma that Ball isolated
from Bang’s solution
of Tarski’s plank problem
[
3
,
2
]
.

Lemma 8

(
[
3
,
2
]
)
.

Let
M
be a
k\times k
real symmetric matrix with ones on the diagonal.
Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\geq 0
.
Then, there is
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
so that for every
i\in[k]
,

|\theta(M\epsilon)_{i}-\gamma_{i}|\geq\theta.

Proof of Claim
7
.

For
i\in K_{3}
,
let
\phi_{i}>0
be so that
\|\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{i}\|_{2}=1

and let

\gamma_{i}=\phi_{i}(\mu_{i}-\langle\tilde{u},\tilde{v}_{i}\rangle).

Let
V^{\prime}
be the matrix with rows
\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}

for
i\in K_{3}
.
Consider the
K_{3}\times K_{3}
matrix
M=V^{\prime}V^{\prime T}
.
Let

\theta=n^{0.078}.

By Bang’s lemma, there is
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{K_{3}}
so that for each
i\in K_{3}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\langle\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{i},z\rangle-\gamma_{i}|\geq\theta,

where

z=\theta V^{\prime T}\epsilon.

By (
2.2
),

\|z\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

We round
z
to a vertex of the cube
\{\pm 1\}^{N_{1}}
in two steps.
The first step uses linear algebra as in
[
13
]
.

Claim 9

(
[
13
]
)
.

There is
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}
so that the following hold:

(1)

For each
i\in K_{3}
, we have
\langle w,v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle z,v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle
.

(2)

\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

(3)

Let
N_{0}
be the set of coordinates in
w
that are not
\pm 1
.
Then
|N_{0}|\leq k
.

If
N_{0}
is empty then we are done, in light of (
2.3
)
and because
w\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{1}}

is so that

\langle w,v^{\prime}_{i}\rangle+\langle\tilde{u},\tilde{v}_{i}\rangle=\mu_{i}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\langle w,\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{i}\rangle=\gamma_{i}.

Otherwise, we need to round the coordinates in
N_{0}
to
\pm 1
.
This is done using extra randomness.
Let
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}
be a random vector distributed as follows.
Its coordinates are independent so that
\delta_{j}+w_{j}
takes values in
\{\pm 1\}
and
\mathbb{E}\delta_{j}=0
.
Its marginals can be computed by

w_{j}=\mathbb{E}[\delta_{j}+w_{j}]=2\Pr[\delta_{j}=1-w_{j}]-1

so that

\Pr[\delta_{j}=1-w_{j}]=\frac{1+w_{j}}{2}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta_{j}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1+w_{j}}{2}(1-w_{j})^{2}+\frac{1-w_{j}}{2}(-1-w_{j})^{2}=1-w_{j}^{2}.

Consider the random vertex

x^{\prime}=\delta+w\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{1}}.

The rest of the analysis is split between two cases.
For each
i\in K_{3}
, let

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sum_{j\in N_{1}}(1-w_{j}^{2})\phi_{i}^{2}{v^{\prime}_{ij}}^{2}.

First, consider some
i
so that
\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq n^{0.151}
; if there are no such
i
’s then continue to case two.
We use the following concentration of measure.

Theorem 10

(Bernstein)
.

Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}
be independent random variables with mean zero that are almost surely at most two in absolute value. Let
\sigma^{2}=\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}[z_{j}^{2}]
. Then, for all
t>0
, we have

\Pr\big[\sum_{j}z_{j}\geq t\big]\leq\exp\big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+2t}\big)
.

By (
2.3
), by the choice of
\theta

and by Claim
9
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\langle x^{\prime},\phi_{i}v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle=\gamma_{i}]=\Pr[\langle\delta,\phi_{i}v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle=\gamma_{i}-\langle z,\phi_{i}v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle]\leq\Pr[|\langle\delta,\phi_{i}v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle|\geq n^{0.078}].

By Bernstein’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Pr[|\langle\delta,\phi_{i}v_{i}^{\prime}\rangle|\geq n^{0.078}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{n^{0.154}}{2n^{0.151}+2n^{0.078}}\Big).

This probability is small enough so that we can safely apply the union bound
over all such
i
’s.

We now move to the second and final case.
We harness anti-concentration one more time
(for the proof see Section
4
).

Claim 11
.

There is a constant
C>0
so that the following holds.
Let
P
be a product distribution on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
Let
\sigma^{2}
be the variance of
\sum_{j}z_{j}
for
z\sim P
.
Assume that
\sigma^{2}>0
.
For every vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\|v\|_{0}=n
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
we have
\Pr_{z\sim P}[\langle v,z\rangle=\gamma]\leq\frac{C}{\sigma}
.

Fix some
i
so that
\sigma_{i}^{2}>n^{0.151}
.
Let
J
be the set of non-zero coordinates in
v^{\prime}_{i}
.
The variance of
\sum_{j\in J}x^{\prime}_{j}
is

\sigma_{J}^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\tfrac{1-w_{j}^{2}}{4}
.
Using (
2.1
),

(2.4)

\displaystyle 4n^{-0.196}\sigma_{J}^{2}\geq\sum_{j\in J}(1-w_{j}^{2})\phi^{2}_{i}{v^{\prime}_{ij}}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}>n^{0.151}.

By Claim
11
, the probability that

\langle x^{\prime},\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{i}\rangle=\gamma_{i}
is at most
\frac{2C}{n^{0.1735}}
.

We need to justify the final union bound.
By (
2.1
) again,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in K_{3}}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sum_{j\in N_{1}}(1-w_{j}^{2})\sum_{i\in K_{3}}\phi_{i}^{2}{v^{\prime}_{ij}}^{2}<n^{-0.196}|N_{0}|\leq n^{0.324}.

It follows that the number of
i
’s with
\sigma_{i}^{2}>n^{0.151}
is at most

n^{0.324-0.151}\leq n^{0.173}
.
The probability that
there exists
i
with
\sigma_{i}^{2}>n^{0.151}
so that

\langle x^{\prime},\phi_{i}v^{\prime}_{i}\rangle=\gamma_{i}
is at most

n^{0.173}\cdot\tfrac{2C}{n^{0.1735}}\leq n^{-0.0004}.\qed

3.
Decomposing the matrix

For the proof of Lemma
3
,
we need a version of Lemma 13 in
[
13
]
.
For completeness, we include its proof below.

Lemma 12

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
be a matrix so that
k\leq n^{0.52}
.
Then, there is a partition of the rows of
V
to two parts
[k]=L_{1}\cup L_{2}
and a partition of the columns to two parts
[n]=M_{1}\cup M_{2}
with
|M_{2}|\leq n^{0.7171}
such that the following hold:

(1)

Let
V^{\prime}=(v^{\prime}_{ij})
be the submatrix of
V
defined by rows in
L_{1}
and columns in
M_{1}
; normalize the non-zero rows in
V^{\prime}
to have
\ell_{2}
-norm one.
For every column
j\in M_{1}
, we have
\sum_{i\in L_{1}}{v^{\prime}}^{2}_{ij}<n^{-0.196}
.

(2)

Every row
i\in L_{2}
has many scales, and the position of its smallest scale contains the
M_{1}
columns.

Proof.

We use the following terminology.
The mass of a vector
w
is
\|w\|_{2}^{2}
.
Initialize
L_{1}=[k],L_{2}=\emptyset,M_{1}=[n]
and
M_{2}=\emptyset
.
If there is no column
j\in M_{1}
with mass

\sum_{i}{v_{ij}}^{2}\geq n^{-0.1961},

then we are done.
Otherwise, there are such columns.
Start moving them one-by-one from
M_{1}
to
M_{2}
.
When we move a column, the norm inside
M_{1}
of each row changes, but we do not immediately renormalize it.

Let
\tau>0
be so that

\tfrac{1-\tau}{\tau}=C^{2}_{0}
, where
C_{0}
is from Definition
2
.
If for a given row
i\in L_{1}
, after the removal of a column, the total current mass inside
