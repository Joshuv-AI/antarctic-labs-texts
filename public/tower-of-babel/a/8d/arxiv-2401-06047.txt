Computing Data Distribution from Query Selectivities
Pankaj K. Agarwal, Rahul Raychaudhury, Stavros Sintos, Jun Yang

Abstract
We are given a set 𝒵 = { ( R 1 , s 1 ) , … , ( R n , s n ) } \mathcal{Z}=\{(R_{1},s_{1}),\ldots,(R_{n},s_{n})\} , where each R i R_{i} is a range in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , such as rectangle or ball, and s i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] s_{i}\in[0,1] denotes its selectivity . The goal is to compute a small-size discrete data distribution 𝒟 = { ( q 1 , w 1 ) , … , ( q m , w m ) } \mathcal{D}=\{(q_{1},w_{1}),\ldots,(q_{m},w_{m})\} , where q j ∈ ℝ d q_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and w j ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] w_{j}\in[0,1] for each 1 ≤ j ≤ m 1\leq j\leq m , and ∑ 1 ≤ j ≤ m w j = 1 \sum_{1\leq j\leq m}w_{j}=1 , such that 𝒟 \mathcal{D} is the most consistent with 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} , i.e., err p ​ ( 𝒟 , 𝒵 ) = 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n | s i − ∑ j = 1 m w j ⋅ 𝟙 ​ ( q j ∈ R i ) | p \mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\!\lvert{s_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(q_{j}\in R_{i})}\rvert^{p} is minimized. In a database setting, 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} corresponds to a workload of range queries over some table, together with their observed selectivities (i.e., fraction of tuples returned), and 𝒟 \mathcal{D} can be used as compact model for approximating the data distribution within the table without accessing the underlying contents. In this paper, we obtain both upper and lower bounds for this problem. In particular, we show that the problem of finding the best data distribution from selectivity queries is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete. On the positive side, we describe a Monte Carlo algorithm that constructs, in time O ⁡ ( ( n + δ − d ) ​ δ − 2 ​ polylog n ) O((n+\delta^{-d})\delta^{-2}\mathop{\mathrm{polylog}}n) , a discrete distribution 𝒟 ~ \tilde{\mathcal{D}} of size O ⁡ ( δ − 2 ) O(\delta^{-2}) , such that err p ​ ( 𝒟 ~ , 𝒵 ) ≤ min 𝒟 ⁡ err p ​ ( 𝒟 , 𝒵 ) + δ \mathrm{err}_{p}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{Z})\leq\min_{\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})+\delta (for p = 1 , 2 , ∞ p=1,2,\infty ) where the minimum is taken over all discrete distributions. We also establish conditional lower bounds, which strongly indicate the infeasibility of relative approximations as well as removal of the exponential dependency on the dimension for additive approximations. This suggests that significant improvements to our algorithm are unlikely.

Introduction

The
selectivity
of a selection query on a set of objects in a database is the probability of a random object in the database satisfying the query predicate.
A key step in query optimization, selectivity estimation is used by databases for
estimating costs of alternative query processing plans and picking the best one.
Consequently, selectivity estimation has been studied extensively in the last few decades
[
30
,
35
,
42
,
43
,
45
]
.

Historically, selectivity estimation has been data-driven.
These approaches construct, or dynamically maintain, a small-size synopsis of the data distribution using histograms or random samples that minimize estimation error.
While these methods work well in low dimensions, they suffer from the curse of dimensionality.
As a result, interest in learning-based methods for selectivity estimation has been growing over the years
[
23
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
37
,
39
]
.
Many different methods have been proposed that work with the data distribution, observed selectivities from query workloads, or a combination of both.
At a high-level, many of these techniques build a model of the underlying data distribution and use it to answer queries. While they work very well in practice, often outperforming their traditional counterparts, a theoretical understanding of this line of work is missing. This leads to the natural question, whether selectivity can be learned efficiently from a small sample of query selectivities alone, without access to the data distribution.

Hu et al.
[
26
]
formalize the learnability of the selectivity estimation problem in this setting.
They use the agnostic-learning framework
[
24
]
, an extension of the classical PAC learning framework for real-valued functions,
where one is given a set of sample queries from a fixed query distribution and their respective selectivities (the
training set
),
and the goal is to efficiently construct a data distribution so that
the selectivity of a new query from the same query distribution can be answered with high accuracy.
They show that for a wide class of range queries,
the selectivity query can be learned within error
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
with probability at least
1-\delta

using a training set of size
\varepsilon^{-O(1)}\log\delta^{-1}
,
where the exponent of
\varepsilon
depends on the query type; see
[
26
]
for a precise statement of their results.
Informally, learnability implies that performance of a model on the training set generalizes to unseen queries from the same distribution. This reduces the task of learning to finding a (model of the) data distribution that best fits the training data i.e.
Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)
.

Although Hu et al.
[
26
]
prove a sharp bound on the sample complexity, their algorithm for ERM takes prohibitively long and produces a data distribution of large size.
They also present fast heuristics to construct small-size data distributions, but they do not provide any guarantee on the performance with respect to the best data distribution fitting the training set.

This raises the question of how to develop a provably efficient and effective algorithm
for constructing the best data distribution (in a given family) from a training set.

Note that the size of the distribution computed by
[
26
]
,
can further be reduced to
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
by choosing an
\varepsilon
-approximation, with an increase of
\varepsilon
in the error;
see
[
22
]
and
Section
3
below.
However, the paper aims at computing a small-size distribution
(whose performance is comparable to the best data distribution)
directly and efficiently, without constructing a large-size distribution first.
For this problem, we obtain hardness results as well as efficient algorithms.

Basic Algorithm

In this section we present our basic algorithm for computing a small-size discrete distribution that (approximately) minimizes the
\ell_{1}
empirical error.
For simplicity, let
\mathrm{err}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\alpha^{*}
denote
\mathrm{err}_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\alpha^{*}_{1}
, respectively.
Given a training set
\mathcal{Z}
and a parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, it computes a discrete distribution
\mathcal{D}^{*}
of size
O(\delta^{-2}\log n)

such that
\mathrm{err}(\mathcal{D}^{*},\mathcal{Z})=\alpha^{*}+\delta
.
At the heart, there is a decision procedure
IsFeasible
that, given
\mathcal{Z}
and parameters
\alpha,\delta\in(0,1)
,
returns a distribution
\mathcal{D}^{*}
with
\mathrm{err}(\mathcal{D}^{*},\mathcal{Z})\leq\alpha+\delta/2
if
\alpha\geq\alpha^{*}
and returns
No
if
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
.

We do not know the value of
\alpha^{*}
, so we perform a binary search on the value of
\alpha
.
In particular, let
E=\{\frac{\delta}{2},(1+\frac{\delta}{2})\frac{\delta}{2},(1+\frac{\delta}{2})^{2}\frac{\delta}{2},\ldots,1\}
be a discretization of the range
[0,1]
, and let
\alpha_{j}
be the
j
-th value of
E
.
Suppose the binary search currently guesses
\alpha_{i}
to be the current guess of
\alpha^{*}
.
If
\textsc{IsFeasible}(\mathcal{Z},\delta,\alpha_{i})
returns a distribution
\mathcal{D}
,
we continue the binary search for values less than
\alpha_{i}
in
E
.
Otherwise, we continue the binary search for values greater than
\alpha_{i}
in
E
.
At the end of the binary search, we return the last distribution
\mathcal{D}^{*}
that
IsFeasible
found.
Assuming the correctness of the decision procedure, the algorithm returns the desired distribution in
\log\frac{1}{\delta}
iterations.
If
\alpha^{*}\leq\delta/2
, then
\textsc{IsFeasible}(\mathcal{Z},\delta,\alpha_{1})
returns a distribution
\mathcal{D}^{*}
such that

\mathrm{err}(\mathcal{D}^{*},\mathcal{Z})\leq\alpha_{1}+\delta/2=\delta\leq\alpha^{*}+\delta
.
In any other case, without loss of generality assuming that
\alpha_{i-1}<\alpha^{*}\leq\alpha_{i}

\alpha_{i}\leq(1+\delta/2)\alpha^{*}
.
By definition,

\textsc{IsFeasible}(\mathcal{Z},\delta,\alpha_{i})
returns a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
such that
\mathrm{err}(\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{Z})\leq\alpha_{i}+\delta/2
.
Hence,

\mathrm{err}(\mathcal{D}^{*},\mathcal{Z})\leq\mathrm{err}(\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{Z})\leq\alpha_{i}+\delta/2\leq(1+\delta/2)\alpha^{*}+\delta/2\leq\alpha^{*}+\delta.

0\leq\kappa\leq 2n
, let
\mathsf{A}_{\kappa}\mathsf{x}\geq\mathsf{b}_{\kappa}
denote the
\kappa
-th constraint of (
4
), (
5
), (
6
). Namely,
\mathsf{A}_{0}\mathsf{x}\geq\mathsf{b}_{0}
denotes (
4
), and for
1\leq i\leq n
,
\mathsf{A}_{2i-1}\mathsf{x}\geq\mathsf{b}_{2i-1}
and
\mathsf{A}_{2i}\mathsf{x}\geq\mathsf{b}_{2i}
denote the constraints (
5
) and (
6
), respectively, for the rectangle
R_{i}
. Then (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
) asks whether there exists an
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{X}
such that
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}\geq\mathsf{b}
.

We use a Multiplicative-Weight-Update (MWU) method to solve this linear program, following the general approach described by Arora et al.
[
4
]
, though the exact implementation depends on the specific
\mathsf{LP}
; see also
[
41
]
. We describe how this approach is implemented in our setting. We will need this algorithm for the faster implementation described in
Section
3
.

2.2
MWU algorithm

We describe an algorithm that either returns a
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}
such that
\mathsf{A}\tilde{\mathsf{x}}\geq\mathsf{b}-\delta/4
or returns that there is no feasible solution for (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
). We set two parameters
\eta=\frac{\delta}{c_{1}}
and
T=\left\lceil{c_{2}\delta^{-2}\ln(2n+1)}\right\rceil
, where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are sufficiently large constants to be chosen later.
The algorithm works in
T
rounds.
At the beginning of round
t
, it has a
(2n+1)
-dimensional probability vector
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=(w_{0}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}},\ldots,w_{2n}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})
.
Initially,
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}=(\frac{1}{2n+1},\ldots,\frac{1}{2n+1})
.
In the
t
-th round, the algorithm solves the decision problem consisting of one constraint

{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}\geq{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{b},\quad\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{X}

(8)

which we refer to as the
expected constraint
.
The algorithm computes, as described below,

\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=\argmax_{x\in\mathbb{X}}{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}
.

If
{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}<{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{b}
, we conclude that (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
) is infeasible because, by definition, any feasible solution of (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
) satisfies (
8
), and we return
No
. Otherwise, for
i=0\ldots 2n
, we set

w_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t+1)}}=w_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\frac{1-\eta(\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i})}{\mu^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t+1)}}}

(9)

where
\mu^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t+1)}}
is a normalization factor so that
\lVert{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t+1)}}}\rVert_{1}=1
.

If the algorithm does not return
No
within the first
T
rounds,
then after completing
T
rounds, it returns
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
.
Suppose
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}=(\tilde{\mathsf{u}},\tilde{\mathsf{v}})
. We return the distribution
\tilde{D}=\{(p_{j},\tilde{v}_{j})\mid\tilde{v}_{j}>0\}
.

Computing
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}
.
We now describe the computation of
\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=(u_{1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}},\ldots,u_{n}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}},v_{1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}},\ldots,v_{m}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})
at each step. We can express the LHS of the expected constraint (
8
) as

{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\psi_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}v_{j}

(10)

\displaystyle\text{where }\quad\varphi_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}

\displaystyle=w_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}+w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\frac{w_{0}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}{n}\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq n,

(11)

\displaystyle\psi_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:p_{j}\in R_{i}}w_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\quad\text{for }1\leq j\leq m.

(12)

Note that the two terms in (
10
) do not share variables and (
7
) does not involve
u_{i}
’s, so we can maximize each of them independently. Recall that
u_{i}\in[0,1]
, so to maximize (
10
), we choose for
1\leq i\leq n
,

u_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\varphi_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}>0,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(13)

Next, we choose
v_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
as follows.
Let
j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=\argmax_{1\leq j\leq m}\psi_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
. Since
\mathsf{v}\in[0,1]^{m}
and
\lVert{\mathsf{v}}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
for
(\mathsf{u},\mathsf{v})\in\mathbb{X}
, we choose for
1\leq j\leq m
,

v_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }j=j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\text{ and }\psi_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}>0,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(14)

Note that although many
u_{i}
’s could be set to
1
, at most one
v_{j}
is set to
1
. This property will be crucial for our faster implementation in the next section. Since at most
T

v_{j}
’s are non-zero,
|\tilde{\mathcal{D}}|\leq T=O(\delta^{-2}\log n)
.

This completes the description of our basic algorithm. We now analyze its running time and correctness.

2.3
Analysis

Lemma 2
.

Assume that the MWU algorithm returns a solution
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}
after
T
rounds.
Then
\mathsf{A}_{i}\tilde{\mathsf{x}}\geq\mathsf{b}_{i}-\delta/4
, for every
0\leq i\leq 2n
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that for any round
1\leq t\leq T
,
|\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i}|\leq 2
.
Let
\mathcal{T}^{-}_{i}=\{t\leq T\mid\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i}<0\}
be the subset of rounds where
\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i}<0
.
Using the analysis in Arora et al.
[
4
]
(see the proof of Theorem 3.3), for every
0\leq i\leq 2n
, we obtain,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i})+\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}|\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i}|+\frac{\ln(2n+1)}{\eta}

\displaystyle=(1+\eta)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i})+2\eta\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}^{-}_{i}}\frac{1}{2}|\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i}|+\frac{\ln(2n+1)}{\eta}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\eta)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i})+2\eta T+\frac{\ln(2n+1)}{\eta}.

The last inequality follows because
|\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\mathsf{b}_{i}|\leq 2
.
Noting that
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
, we get

0\leq(1+\eta)(\mathsf{A}_{i}\tilde{\mathsf{x}}-\mathsf{b}_{i})+4\eta+\frac{2\ln(2n+1)}{\eta T}
.
By choosing
c_{1}=32
and
c_{2}=512
, we obtain
\eta=\frac{\delta}{32}
and
T=\left\lceil{512\delta^{-2}\ln(2n+1)}\right\rceil
. Therefore

(1+\eta)(\mathsf{A}_{i}\tilde{\mathsf{x}}-\mathsf{b}_{i})+\delta/4\geq 0\Leftrightarrow\mathsf{A}_{i}\tilde{\mathsf{x}}\geq\mathsf{b}_{i}-\delta/4.

∎

Lemma 3
.

Given a training set
\mathcal{Z}
and parameters
\alpha,\delta
,
the algorithm
IsFeasible
either returns a discrete distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
of size
O(\delta^{-2}\log n)
such that
\mathrm{err}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{Z})\leq\alpha+\delta/2
, or returns No for
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
.

Proof.

First, by definition, our algorithm always solves the expected constraint optimally.
Hence, if the algorithm stops in iteration
t\leq T
(because we cannot satisfy the expected constraint
{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}\geq{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{b}
for
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{X}
) then it is also true that there is no
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{X}
such that
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}\geq\mathsf{b}
. So the algorithm correctly returns that the
\mathsf{LP}
is infeasible and
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
.

Next, assume that the algorithm returns
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
.
Recall that
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}=(\tilde{\mathsf{u}},\tilde{\mathsf{v}})=(\tilde{u}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{u}_{n},\tilde{v}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{v}_{n})\in\mathbb{X}
.
Since
\mathbb{X}
is convex and
\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\in\mathbb{X}
for each
1\leq t\leq T
, it also holds that
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}
and
\lVert{\tilde{\mathsf{v}}}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
.
Hence, it follows that
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is indeed a distribution of size at most
T=O(\delta^{-2}\log n)
.
From Lemma
2
and the equivalence of (
\mathsf{FP}
2.1
) and (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
), we get

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{u}_{i}\leq\alpha+\delta/4\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\sum_{j:p_{j}\in R_{i}}\tilde{v}_{j}-s_{i}\right|\leq u_{i}+\delta/4

for
i=1\ldots n
.
Recall that
s_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(R_{i})=\sum_{j}\mathbbm{1}(p_{j}\in R_{i})\tilde{v}_{j}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|s_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(R_{i})-s_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{u}_{i}+\delta/4\leq\alpha+\delta/4+\delta/4=\alpha+\delta/2.\qed

As for the running time, the binary search executes
O(\log\frac{1}{\delta})
iterations, each of which runs the
IsFeasible
algorithm. We need
O(m\log n)=O(n^{d}\log n)
time to construct (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
). The
IsFeasible
algorithm runs for
T=O(\delta^{-2}\log n)
rounds. In each round
t
, we need
O(n)
time to compute the values of
\mathsf{u}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
by (
11
) and (
13
). We need
O(m\log n)=O(n^{d}\log n)
time to find all the values
\psi_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
by (
12
).
Putting everything together, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

The algorithm runs in

O(\delta^{-2}n^{d}\log^{2}n\log\delta^{-1})
time.

3
The Improved Algorithm

We present an implementation of a small variant of the algorithm in the last section that computes the desired distribution with high probability in
n(\delta^{-1}\log n)^{O(1)}
time (see Theorem
9
below for a more precise characterization).
There are two challenges in implementing the
IsFeasible
procedure efficiently.
First, (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
) has
O(n^{d})
variables—although
\mathsf{u}
has dimension
n
,
\mathsf{v}
has dimension
m=O(n^{d})
—so we cannot afford to represent (
\mathsf{LP}
2.1
) explicitly.
Second, in each round
t
, computing
\mathsf{u}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
is easy, but computing
\mathsf{v}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
quickly seems challenging even though only one of the values in
\mathsf{v}
is non-zero.
We exploit underlying geometry to address both challenges.
We first describe how to implement
IsFeasible
without writing the
\mathsf{LP}
explicitly and then describe how to compute
\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
quickly.

Implicit representation of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{LP}}
.
We maintain the probability vector
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
as before. We also maintain a multi-set
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of points. Initially,
\mathcal{F}=\emptyset
.
Each round adds one point to
\mathcal{F}
, so
\left|{\mathcal{F}}\right|\leq T=O(\delta^{-2}\log n)
. Since we have
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
at our disposal, we can compute
u_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
, using (
11
) and (
13
) as before. The main question is how to compute
j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=\argmax_{1\leq j\leq m}\psi_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
without maintaining all the variables explicitly. For each rectangle
R_{i}\in{\mathcal{R}}
, let
\omega^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}(R_{i})=w_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
.
For each point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define its
depth
with respect to
({\mathcal{R}},\omega^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})
, denoted by
0pt(x)
, to be

0pt(x)=\sum_{R\in{\mathcal{R}}}\mathbbm{1}(x\in R)\omega^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}(R).

It is easily checked that the depth of all points lying in the same cell of
\mathop{\mathrm{\EuScript{A}}}({\mathcal{R}})
is the same, and that many cells may have the same depth. By the definition of the depth,
\psi_{j}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=0pt(p_{j})
. Thus the problem of computing
j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
is equivalent to choosing a point
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the maximum depth, i.e.,
0pt(p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})=\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}0pt(p)
. Chan
[
8
]
has described an
O(n^{d/2})
time algorithm to compute a point of maximum depth in a set of
n
weighted rectangles in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We refer to this algorithm as
\textsc{ DeepestPt}({\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{\omega})
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is a set of
n
rectangles in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathsf{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the weight vector.

We thus proceed in the
t
-th round of
IsFeasible
as follows.
First, compute the vector
\mathsf{u}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
as before.
Next, we compute the deepest point
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
by calling
\textsc{ DeepestPt}({\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{\omega}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})
. If
0pt(p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})>0
, then we add
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
to the set
F
; otherwise we ignore it.
Intuitively, this is equivalent to setting
v_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}=1
if
\psi_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}>0
and
0
otherwise.
We do not know how to compute
j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
efficiently and thus cannot compute which
v_{j}
needs to be set to
1
.
But what comes to our rescue is that we only need to compute
w_{q}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\mathsf{A}_{q}\mathsf{x}
, for all
0\leq q\leq 2n
, to check whether the expected constraint holds and to update the weight factors.
Fortunately, we can accomplish this using only
\mathsf{u}
and
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
, without an explicit representation of
\mathsf{x}
, as follows.
We set
w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\mathsf{A}_{2i-1}\mathsf{x}=w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}u_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
and
w_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\mathsf{A}_{2i}\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{w}_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}u_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}

if
0pt(p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})\leq 0
or
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\notin R_{i}
,
and we set
w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\mathsf{A}_{2i-1}\mathsf{x}=w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}(u_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-1)
and
w_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\mathsf{A}_{2i}\mathsf{x}=w_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}(u_{i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}+1)

if
0pt(p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})>0
and
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\in R_{i}
.
It can be checked that these values are the same as when we set
v_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}
as above.

After having computed
w_{q}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\mathsf{A}_{q}\mathsf{x}
for all
0\leq q\leq 2n
, we check whether
{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}<{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{b}
. If so, we stop and return
No
. Otherwise, we compute
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t+1)}}
using (
9
) as before. If the algorithm is not aborted within the first
T
rounds, we return
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\{(p,\frac{1}{T})\mid p\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}
is a multi-set. If there are
s
copies of a point
p
, we keep only one copy of
p
and set its weight to
\frac{s}{T}
. The following lemma establishes the correctness of the algorithm.

Lemma 5
.

Given
\mathcal{Z}
and
\delta\in[0,1]
,
\mathrm{err}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{Z})=\mathrm{err}(\hat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{Z})
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we assume that for any
\mathsf{\omega}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
,
1\leq t\leq T
, the maximum-depth cell in
\mathop{\mathrm{\EuScript{A}}}({\mathcal{R}})
with respect to
\mathsf{\omega}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
is unique and that
j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
are distinct for each
t\leq T
. Then by the definition of depth,
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
and
p_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}
lie in the same cell of
\mathop{\mathrm{\EuScript{A}}}({\mathcal{R}})
,
so the value of
{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}
computed by the implicit algorithm is the same as
{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
computed by the basic algorithm.
Hence, assuming
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
computed by the two algorithms is the same,
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t+1)}}
computed by them is also the same.
Recall that
v_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
is set to
1
if and only if
\psi_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}>0
, which is the same condition when
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
is added to
\mathcal{F}
by the implicit algorithm.
v_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=1
if and only if
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\in F
. Hence,
p_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
if and only if
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\mathcal{D}})
and
p_{j^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}
and
p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
lie in the same cell of
\mathop{\mathrm{\EuScript{A}}}({\mathcal{R}})
. The same argument as in
Lemma
1
now implies that
\mathrm{err}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{Z})=\mathrm{err}(\hat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{Z})
.
∎

Fast computation of
\boldsymbol{p^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}
.
The main observation is that it is not crucial to compute
\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}=\argmax_{\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{X}}{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}
in the
t
-th round of the MWU algorithm. Instead, it suffices to compute
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
such that
{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\tilde{\mathsf{x}}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}\geq{\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}}^{\top}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{x}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}-\delta/12
.
In the terminology of the implicit
\mathsf{LP}
algorithm, this is equivalent to saying that it suffices to compute a point
\tilde{p}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
with
0pt(\tilde{p}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})\geq\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}0pt(p)-\delta/12
.
We use
\varepsilon
-approximations and random sampling
[
22
,
10
]
to compute
\tilde{p}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
quickly. The notion of
\varepsilon
-approximation is defined for general range spaces but we define
\varepsilon
-approximation in our setting.

Given a set
{\mathcal{R}}
of rectangles and a weight function
\omega:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow[0,1]
a multi-subset
\mathcal{N}\subset{\mathcal{R}}
is called an
\varepsilon
-approximation
of
({\mathcal{R}},\omega)
if for any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left|{\frac{0pt(x,{\mathcal{R}},\omega)}{\omega({\mathcal{R}})}-\frac{0pt(x,\mathcal{N})}{\left|{\mathcal{N}}\right|}}\right|\leq\varepsilon,

(15)

where
\Delta(x,{\mathcal{R}},\omega)
is the weighted depth of point
x
in the set of rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
with weights
\omega
, and
\Delta(x,\mathcal{N})
is the depth of point
x
in the set of rectangles
\mathcal{N}
assuming that the weight of each rectangle in
\mathcal{N}
is
1
.

Let
A
be a random (multi-)subset of
{\mathcal{R}}
of size
O(\varepsilon^{-2}\ln\phi^{-1})
where at each step each rectangle of
{\mathcal{R}}
is chosen with probability proportional to its weight (with repetition). It is well known
[
22
,
10
]
that
A
is an
\varepsilon
-approximation with probability at least
1-\phi
. With this result at our disposal, we compute
\tilde{p}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
, an approximately deepest point with respect to
({\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}})
, as follows. Let
\mathsf{w}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
be as defined above.
Let
\mathsf{w}^{+}=(w^{+}_{1},\ldots,w^{+}_{n})
and
\mathsf{w}^{-}=(w^{-}_{1},\ldots,w^{-}_{n})
be two vectors such that
w^{+}_{i}=w_{2i}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
and
w^{-}_{i}=w_{2i-1}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}
for every
1\leq i\leq n
.
We set
r=c_{3}\delta^{-2}
, where
c_{3}>0
is a sufficiently large constant.
We repeat the following for
\mu=O(\log n)
times: For each
h\leq\mu
,
we choose a random sample
\mathcal{N}^{+}_{h}
of
({\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{w}^{+})
of size
r
and another random sample
\mathcal{N}^{-}_{h}
of
({\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{w}^{-})
of size
r
. Let
\mathcal{N}_{h}=\mathcal{N}^{+}_{h}\cup\mathcal{N}^{-}_{h}
. We define the weight function
\bar{\omega}_{h}:\mathcal{N}_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\bar{\omega}_{h}(R)=\begin{cases}\frac{\lVert{\mathsf{w}^{+}}\rVert_{1}}{r}&\text{if }R\in\mathcal{N}^{+}_{h},\\
-\frac{\lVert{\mathsf{w}^{-}}\rVert_{1}}{r}&\text{if }R\in\mathcal{N}^{-}_{h}.\end{cases}

(16)

We compute a deepest point
\tilde{p}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}}_{h}
with respect to
(\mathcal{N}_{h},\bar{\omega}_{h})
along with
0pt(\tilde{p}_{h}^{\smash{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}},\mathcal{N}_{h},\bar{\mathsf{\omega}}_{h})
by calling
\textsc{ DeepestPt}(\mathcal{N}_{h},\bar{\omega}_{h})
.
After repeating
\mu
times, we choose as

5.1
NP-Completeness

Let
Selectivity
be the decision problem of constructing a distribution with small size and minimum error. More specifically,
let
\mathcal{Z}=\{(R_{1},s_{1}),...,(R_{n},s_{n})\}
be the input training set consisting of

\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}

\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})\leq\varepsilon
.
We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 12
.

The

problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-Complete, even for
d=2
.

The
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness proof is based on a gadget used by
[
36
]
to prove the NP-hardness of the
Square Cover
problem: given a set
\mathcal{R}
of
n
squares, and a parameter
k
, decides if there are
k
points
S\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
R\cap S\neq\emptyset
for each
R\in\mathcal{R}
.
We prove Theorem
12
by reducing
3
-SAT to the
Selectivity
problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
(X,C)
be the
3
-SAT formula consisting of variables
x_{1},...,x_{n}
and clauses
C_{1},...,C_{m}
. We construct a set of weighted axis-aligned rectangles
\mathcal{Z}
and a number
k
such that
(X,C)
is satisfiable if and only if there exists a discrete distribution
\mathcal{D}
of size
k
such that
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})=0
.

In the previous reduction, we created a training set
\mathcal{Z}
such that there exists a
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}
with
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})=0
if and only if the formula is satisfiable. This implies something stronger. Given
\mathcal{Z}
and
k
, let
\alpha^{*}_{p,k}=\min_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\mathrm{err}_{p}({\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z}})
.

Corollary 13

Let

be a function. Given a training set

and an input parameter

.

5.2
Conditional lower bounds

A natural question that arises now is, given

, whether we can find a discrete distribution of size at most
g(n)k
such that the error is at most
f(n)\alpha^{*}_{p,k}
, where
f(n),g(n)
are non-negative functions.
As it turns out, if we are interested in near-linear algorithms, then it is unlikely.
The same technique shows that removing the exponential dependency on

from additive approximations is unlikely.

Formally, we define the

problem. We are given a training set

of

.

,

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 14
.

The

problem cannot be solved in
h(d)n^{O(1)}
time, where
h
is any computable function
h:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, under the
\mathsf{FPT}\neq W[1]
conjecture.

of

\mathbb{R}^{d}

The requirements for

For the coverage problem, we are given a set

of

In

Related Work

Selectivity Estimation.
Selectivity estimation techniques can be broadly classified into three regimes: query-driven, data-driven, and hybrid. Most literature focuses on orthogonal range queries.

Query-driven methods: These methods derive selectivity estimates based on previous queries and their results. They do not require access to the underlying data distribution. Methods falling under this category include DQM
[
23
]
and the query-driven histogram techniques such as STHoles
[
6
]
, Isomer
[
44
]
, and QuickSel
[
39
]
. They construct models or histograms using results from previous queries and apply these models to estimate selectivity for new queries.

Data-driven methods: These methods, on the other hand, derive selectivity estimates from the underlying data distribution. They sample data and build statistical models that capture the data distribution, which are then used for selectivity estimation. There is long history along this direction, but some recent examples in this category include Naru
[
48
]
, DQM-D
[
34
]
, and DeepDB
[
25
]
.

Hybrid methods: These methods take into account both the query workload and the underlying data distribution. They not only model the data distribution but also incorporate query feedback to refine the model over time. Examples of such methods include MSCN
[
27
]
and LW
[
14
]
, which utilize both data and query features in a regression-based framework for selectivity estimation.
See
[
46
]
for a detailed literature review.

Our method is query-driven, but is unique because none of query-driven and data-driven models above offered theoretical guarantees on the learnability of the functions or their learning procedures.

Multiplicative-Weights-Update (MWU) method.
The MWU method has been independently rediscovered multiple times and has found applications in several areas including machine learning
[
16
]
, game theory
[
17
,
19
]
, online algorithms
[
15
]
, computational geometry
[
11
]
, etc. In the context of optimization problems, there is a rich body of work on using MWU and related techniques to efficiently solve special classes of LPs and SDPs
[
29
,
41
,
49
,
3
]
. The technique we use in this paper is an adaption of the one used by
[
41
]
to solve packing and covering LPs. See also
[
4
]
for a detailed overview. The MWU method has also been used to implicitly solve linear programs. A classical example is the multicommodity flow problem where the describing the LP explicitly requires exponential number constraints
[
18
]
. Furthermore, there has been a lot of work related to implicitly solving special classes of LPs in computational geometry or combinatorial optimization using the MWU method
[
9
,
11
,
12
]
, the primal dual method
[
47
]
, or the ellipsoid method
[
21
,
20
]
.

Despite the geometric nature of our problem, to the best of our knowledge, all previous (geometric) techniques that implicitly solve an
\mathsf{LP}
do not extend to our problem.
In particular, in
[
11
]
the authors study various geometric packing and covering problems, such as the weighted set cover of points by disks, and the maximum weight independent set of disks, using the MWU method. The main difference from our setting is that for all problems they study, the matrix
\mathsf{A}
and the vector
\mathsf{b}
are non-negative. One of the main challenges in our setting is that we have to deal with both positive and negative values in
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{b}

2

d
with both positive and negative weights.

7
Conclusion and future work

In this work, we studied the problem of finding the data distribution to fit a query training set consisting of axis-aligned rectangles (representing orthogonal range selectivity queries) with the smallest error. While the problem has been studied in the past,
only an expensive
\Omega(n^{d})
algorithm was known for constructing a distribution of size
O(n^{d})
.
We showed that the decision problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete even for
d=2
.

Based on a standard complexity conjecture, we also gave conditional lower bounds showing that the exponential dependency on
d
is inevitable for additive or relative approximations.

On the positive side, for the
\ell_{1}
empirical error, we gave a
O((n+\delta^{-d})\delta^{-2}\mathop{\mathrm{polylog}}n)
time algorithm that returns a data distribution of size
O(\delta^{-2})
with additive error
\delta
. Furthermore, we showed that our algorithm for
\ell_{1}
error can be extended to
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{\infty}
, as well as any type of ranges as long as they are algebraic sets of constant complexity.
In view of our hardness results, significant improvements to our upper bounds are unlikely.

There are some interesting directions following from this work.
What properties should the data and query distribution satisfy so that the running time is polynomial in
d
as well?

In addition to selectivity queries, another interesting line of work is to study the construction of distributions for other types of database queries such as aggregation and joins.

Proof of

In the coverage problem, we are given a set
{\mathcal{R}}
of
n-1
rectangles in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a box
B
. The coverage problem asks if the union of rectangles in
{\mathcal{R}}
covers
B
completely. The coverage problem is known to be
W[1]
-Hard. Under the
\mathsf{FPT}\neq W[1]
conjecture, this implies that there cannot be an algorithm for coverage with running time
h(d)n^{O(1)}
, where
h:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm for
Selectivity+
.
We are going to prove by contradiction that
\mathcal{A}
cannot run in
h(d)n^{O(1)}
time, under the
\mathsf{FPT}\neq W[1]
conjecture.
Let assume that
\mathcal{A}
runs in
h(d)n^{O(1)}
time.

For each rectangle
R_{i}\in{\mathcal{R}}
, we construct
z_{i}=(R_{i},0)
, i.e., rectangle
R_{i}
with selectivity
0
. Finally, we add
z_{n}=(B,1)
. We set
k=1
.
The running time to construct the instance of the
Selectivity+
problem is
O(d\cdot n)
. We then run
\mathcal{A}
on this instance.

We first consider the case when the input is a
No
instance of the coverage problem. This implies that there exists a point
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
q\cap B\neq\emptyset
and
q\bigcap(\bigcup_{R_{i}\in{\mathcal{R}}}R_{i})=\emptyset
. Consider the distribution
\mathcal{D}=\{(q,1)\}
. Notice that
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})=0=\alpha^{*}_{p,1}=\alpha^{*}_{p,k}=f(n)\alpha^{*}_{p,k}
because
q
lies only inside
B
, where
B
has selectivity
1
and all other rectangles
R_{i}
have selectivity
0
.
By definition of
Selectivity+
,
\mathcal{A}
in this instance must return a distribution
\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})=0
.

In the
Yes
instance of the coverage problem, if
q\in B
then
q\in\bigcup_{R_{i}\in{\mathcal{R}}}R_{i}
. Hence, if we add any point
q\in B
in distribution
\mathcal{D}
with positive probability then
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})>0
because the selectivity of every rectangle in
{\mathcal{R}}
is
0
. On the other hand, the selectivity of
B
is
1
so if we do not add any point
q\in B
in
\mathcal{D}
with positive probability then
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})>0
. In any case,
f(n)\alpha^{*}_{p,k}\geq\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})>0
. Actually,

,
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})>\frac{1}{2n^{2}}

. Thus,

.

Overall, let

be the distribution returned by

in

\mathcal{D}
, we can find
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})
in

time, because
|\mathcal{D}|\leq g(n)k\leq n
.
If
\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})=0
then the solution to the coverage problem in the original instance is

\mathrm{err}_{p}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{Z})>0
, then the solution to the coverage problem in the original instance is

.
This implies a
h(d)n^{O(1)}
time algorithm for the coverage problem, which is a contradiction, under the
\mathsf{FPT}\neq W[1]
conjecture.
Hence,
\mathcal{A}
cannot run in
h(d)n^{O(1)}
time.
