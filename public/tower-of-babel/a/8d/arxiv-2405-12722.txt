The DKP Equation in Presence of a Cusp Potential: Transmission Resonances and Bound States
Vicente A. Arévalo, Sebastián Valladares, Clara Rojas

Abstract
In this article, we solve the Duffin–Kemmer–Petiau (DKP) equation in the presence of the cusp potential for spin–one particles. We derived the scattering solutions and calculated the bound states in terms of the Whittaker functions. We show that transmission resonances are present, as well as the particle–anti-particle bound states.

Introduction

In the literature, there is a relativistic equation called Duffi–Kemmer–Petiau (DKP) equation, which describes the dynamics of scalar (spin–0) and vector (spin–1) particles in
analogy with the Dirac equation for spin–half fields
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Besides, it is essential to mention that the equation was not taken into account due to its equivalence with the Klein–Gordon and Proca equations. Despite this, some aspects cannot be explained without using the DKP equation.

The DKP formalism with different types of couplings is used in a wide area of physics, including applications in Quantum Chromodynamics at large and short distances
[
4
]
, studies of covariant Hamiltonian dynamics
[
5
]
, symmetries of very special relativity, thermal properties of bosons
[
6
]
, among others
[
7
,
8
]
. Therefore, it represents a promising area of research.

Additionally, the DKP equation has been used to model several interactions, including, but not limited to, those involved in many scattering processes in nuclear physics
[
9
]
. Furthermore, one–dimensional potentials have been much more prevalent in DKP formalism in recent decades due to the substantial assistance that the resultant simple equations offer for understanding physical systems in higher dimensions
[
10
]
. Among the potentials used, we can mention the double–step potential
[
11
,
12
]
, the Varshni potential model
[
13
]
, the DKP oscillator
[
14
,
15
]
, hyperbolic tangent potential
[
16
]
, also to study the transmission resonances in an asymmetric cusp potential
[
17
]
. In this sense, the purpose of this article is to address the behavior of a spinor particle for a cusp potential, including scattering and bound states of the wave equations, which are of great interest in such quantum mechanical systems and it is a well–understood problem for the Schrödinger equation
[
18
]
.

The one–dimensional symmetric cusp potential barrier is given by

V(x)=V_{0}\exp\left(-\dfrac{|x|}{a}\right),

(1)

it is an asymptotically vanishing potential for large values of the space variable
x
. The parameter
V_{0}
shows the height of the barrier (see Fig.
1
) or the depth of the well, depending on its sign. The positive constant a determines the shape of the potential
[
19
]
. Moreover, the cusp potential can be regarded as a screened one–dimensional Coulomb potential if
V_{0}\leq 0
[
20
]
.

2
Scattering states

As stated above, the Duffin–Kemmer–Petiau (DKP) equation can be used to describe spin–zero and one particles, and with the introduction of an electromagnetic field interaction
[
7
]
, it takes the following form

\left[i\beta^{\mu}\left(\partial_{\mu}+ieA_{\mu}\right)-1\right]\Psi(\vec{r},t)=0,

(2)

where
\Psi(\vec{r},t)
represents the DKP spinor of the particle in
(3+1)
dimensions, and the matrices
\beta^{\mu}
satisfy the following condition
\beta^{\mu}\beta^{\nu}\beta^{\lambda}+\beta^{\lambda}\beta^{\nu}\beta^{\mu}=\eta^{\mu\nu}\beta^{\lambda}+\eta^{\lambda\nu}\beta^{\mu}

[
1
,
21
]
,

It is important to mention that in
(3+1)
dimensions, the
\beta
matrices are five-dimensional and ten-dimensional for spin–zero and spin–one particles, respectively
[
22
]
. Nevertheless, in
(1+1)
dimensions, the scalar (spin–zero) and vector (spin–one) sectors are unitarily equivalent
[
1
,
23
]
. It means that the spin–one case has to be treated as a spin–zero case.

As the potential involved is time-independent, the solution of Eq. (
2
) can be obtained by
\Psi(x,t)=e^{iEt}\phi(x)
. Moreover, the DKP equation can be then rewritten in
(1+1)
dimensional representation as follows

\left\{\beta^{0}\left[E-V(x)\right]+i\beta^{1}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}-1\right\}\phi(x)=0,

(3)

where
\phi(x)
is the spinor,
E
is the energy, and
V(x)
represents the coupled potential to the DKP equation. For
(1+1)
dimension, we consider the following expressions for
\beta^{0}
, and
\beta^{1}
matrices
[
23
,
24
]
, with the metric tensor
\eta^{\mu\nu}=\textnormal{diag}\left(1,-1\right)
.

\beta^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&i\\
0&0&0\\
-i&0&0\end{array}\right),\quad\beta^{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&i&0\\
i&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right).

(4)

Since,
\beta^{0}
, and
\beta^{1}
are
3\times 3
matrices,
\phi(x)
must be decomposed in three components, such that

\phi(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Psi_{(}x)\\
\Phi_{(}x)\\
\Theta(x)\end{array}\right),

(5)

generally,
\Psi(x)
,
\Phi(x)
,
\Theta(x)
are functions that describe particles in the DKP theory.

From here, it is not difficult to verify that by using Eqs. (
5
), and (
4
), on Eq. (
3
) lead to a system of three equations
[
16
]
.

\begin{cases}\left\{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\left[E-V(x)\right]^{2}-1\right\}\Psi(x)=0,\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\Phi(x)=-\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Psi(x)\\
\Theta(x)=-i\left[E-V(x)\right]\Psi(x)\vskip 2.84526pt.\end{cases}

(6)

where it is clear that
\Phi(x)
and
\Theta(x)
depend on the Klein–Gordon equation solution (i.e.,
\Psi(x)
).

2.1
Transmission Resonances

To calculate the transmission resonances we solve Eq. (
6
) with the cusp potential barrier, which is given by
[
20
,
25
]

V(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hskip-5.69046ptV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}&\text{ for }\quad x<0,\\
\hskip 2.84544ptV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}&\text{ for }\quad x>0.\end{array}\right.

(7)

The form of this barrier is represented in Fig.
1
.

To solve the problem, it is important to consider the Klein–Gordon equation for both regions, the positive and negative. For
x<0
. We solve the differential equation

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\phi_{L}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\left[\left(E-V_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)^{2}-1\right]\phi_{L}(x)=0.

(8)

Making the change of variable
y=2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}
,

y\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\left(y\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{L}}{\mathrm{d}y}\right)-\left[\left(iaE-\dfrac{y}{2}\right)^{2}+a^{2}\right]\phi_{L}=0.

(9)

Setting
\phi_{L}=y^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}f(y)
, the previous equation reduces to the Whittaker differential equation.

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}f(y)}{\mathrm{d}y^{2}}+\left[-\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{iaE}{y}+\dfrac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}-\mu^{2}}{y^{2}}\right]f(y)=0.

(10)

Eq. (
10
) is the Whittaker differential equation
[
26
]
whose general solution can be written as

\phi_{L}(y)=c_{1}y^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M_{\kappa,\mu}(y)+c_{2}y^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M_{\kappa,-\mu}(y),

(11)

where
M_{\kappa,\mu}(y)
is the Whittaker function, and

\kappa=iaE,\quad\mu=ia\sqrt{E^{2}-1}.

(12)

Notice that the general solution given in Eq. (
11
) is made by the incident and the reflected part of the wave. Moreover, the goal is to solve the system of equations Eq. (
6
). Therefore, let’s analyze both cases separately.

2.2
Incident Part

The solution of the system of the system Eq. (
6
) for the incident part is composed by

\displaystyle\Psi_{inc}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\dfrac{M_{\kappa,\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}},

(13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{inc}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\dfrac{1}{2a\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\left[\left(2\kappa+1-2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)M_{\kappa,\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\left(2\kappa+2\mu+1\right)M_{\kappa+1,\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right],

(14)

\displaystyle\Theta_{inc}(x)

\displaystyle=i~c_{1}\dfrac{\left(e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}-E\right)M_{\kappa,\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}.

(15)

For simplicity, we can group these solutions in a vector representation

G_{inc}=\begin{pmatrix}\quad c_{1}\dfrac{M_{\kappa,\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\\
c_{1}\dfrac{1}{2a\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\left[\left(2\kappa+1-2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)M_{\kappa,\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right.\\
\hskip 59.75095pt-\left.\left(2\kappa+2\mu+1\right)M_{\kappa+1,\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right]\\
i~c_{1}\dfrac{\left(e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}-E\right)M_{\kappa,\mu}(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}})}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\par\end{pmatrix},

(16)

where
G_{inc}
represents the vector that contains the incident solutions.

2.3
Reflected Part

Similar to before, the solution for the reflected part is composed by:

\displaystyle\Psi_{ref}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}\dfrac{M_{\kappa,-\mu}(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}})}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}},

(17)

\displaystyle\Phi_{ref}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}\dfrac{1}{2a\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\left[\left(2\kappa+1-2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right.

(18)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\left(2\mu-1-2\kappa\right)M_{\kappa+1,-\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\Theta_{ref}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i~c_{2}\dfrac{(e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}-E)M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}},

(19)

Or in vector representation:

G_{ref}=\begin{pmatrix}\quad c_{2}\dfrac{M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\\
c_{2}\dfrac{1}{2a\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\left[\left(2\kappa+1-2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right.\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+\left.\left(2\mu-1-2\kappa\right)M_{\kappa+1,-\mu}\left(2iae^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}\right)\right]\\
i~c_{2}\dfrac{\left(e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}-E\right)M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\end{pmatrix},

(20)

where
G_{ref}
is the vector that contains the reflected solutions.

Now we consider the solution for
x>0
. In this case, the differential equation to solve is

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\phi_{R}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\left[\left(E-V_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)^{2}-1\right]\phi_{R}(x)=0.

(21)

Making the variable change
z=2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}
, Eq. (
21
) can be written as

z\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left[z\dfrac{\phi_{R}(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}\right]-\left[\left(iaE-\dfrac{z}{2}\right)^{2}+a^{2}\right]\phi_{R}(z)=0.

(22)

Putting
\phi_{R}(z)=z^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}g(z)
we obtain the Whittaker differential equation
[
26
]

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}g(z)}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}+\left[-\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{iaE}{z}+\dfrac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}-\mu^{2}}{z^{2}}\right]g(z)=0,

(23)

which solution is given by
[
26
]

\phi_{R}(z)=c_{3}z^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M_{\kappa,-\mu}(z).

(24)

The general solution given in Eq. (
24
) corresponds to the transmitted part of the wave.

2.4
Transmitted part

The solutions for the right side are,

\displaystyle\Psi_{trans}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{3}\dfrac{M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}},

(25)

\displaystyle\Phi_{trans}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{3}\dfrac{\sqrt{iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}{\sqrt{2}a^{2}V_{0}}\left[\left(ie^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\left(1+2\kappa\right)+2aV_{0}\right)M_{\kappa,-\mu}(2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}})\right.

(26)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.i~e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\left(1+2\kappa-2\mu\right)M_{\kappa+1,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\Theta_{trans}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i~c_{3}\dfrac{\left(e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}-E\right)M_{\kappa,-\mu}(2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}})}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}},

(27)

In vector representation,

G_{trans}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{c_{3}M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\\
c_{3}\dfrac{\sqrt{iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}{\sqrt{2}a^{2}V_{0}}\left[\left(i~e^{\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\left(1+2\kappa\right)+2aV_{0}\right)M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)\right.\\

\dfrac{i~c_{3}\left(e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}V_{0}-E\right)M_{\kappa,-\mu}\left(2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}\right)}{\sqrt{2iaV_{0}e^{-\nicefrac{{x}}{{a}}}}}\end{pmatrix},

(28)

where
G_{trans}
is the vector that contains the transmitted solutions.

M_{\kappa,\mu}(y)=e^{\nicefrac{{-y}}{{2}}}y^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu},

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\left|\dfrac{c_{2}\left(2iaV_{0}\right)^{-\mu}}{c_{1}\left(2iaV_{0}\right)^{\mu}}\right|^{2},

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\left|\dfrac{c_{3}\left(2iaV_{0}\right)^{-\mu}}{c_{1}\left(2iaV_{0}\right)^{\mu}}\right|^{2}.

3
Bound States

The quantum mechanical nature of the bound states arises from the possibility that a wave state can be confined in a potential
[
27
,
28
]
.
Additionally, a potential will support at least a bound state if the following condition is satisfied
[
29
]
,

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}~V\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x~<0.

(41)

Now, using condition (
41
) for the potential (
1
) with negative
V_{0}
it is possible to find that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}~V\left(x\right)dx=~-2aV_{0}~<0,

(42)

which implies that independently of the potential parameters

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we have solved the relativistic DKP equation for the cusp potential barrier within the framework of spin–one taking into account the equivalence with spin–zero particles in the DKP theory for
(1+1)
dimensions. Our calculations involved determining the wave functions associated with both scattering and bound–states of the potential through the use of Whittaker functions. Furthermore, we have computed the transmission and reflection coefficients of the potential in great detail by analyzing the continuity and asymptotic properties of the wave functions. Moreover, we have shown that the energy equation related to the bound states in the DKP equation is the same result of a Klein–Gordon equation in a cusp potential well
[
25
]
