Towards an Elementary Formulation of the Riemann Hypothesis in Terms of Permutation Groups
Will Cavendish, Jacob Tsimerman

Abstract
This paper investigates the relationship between the Riemann hypothesis and the statement ∀ n , g ⁡ ( n ) ≤ e p n \forall n,~g(n)\leq e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}} , where g ⁡ ( n ) g(n) is the maximum order of an element of S n S_{n} , the symmetric group on n n elements, and p n p_{n} is the n n -th prime. We show this inequality holds under the Riemann Hypothesis. We also make progress towards establishing the converse by proving ∃ n , g ⁡ ( n ) > e p n \exists n,~g(n)>e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}} if the Riemann Hypothesis is false and the supremum of the set of the real parts of the Riemann zeta function’s zeros sup { ℜ ⁡ ( ρ ) | ζ ⁡ ( ρ ) = 0 } \sup\{\Re(\rho)~|~\zeta(\rho)=0\} is not equal to 1.



In a series of papers starting in the late 1960s (e.g.,
[
6
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
2
]
), Nicolas and his collaborators established an intriguing relationship between the Riemann Hypothesis and the theory of permutation groups. One of the most striking, the central result of
[
2
]
, is that the Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to the statement

g(n)\leq e^{\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}}\textrm{~ for all }n.

Here
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
denote Landau’s function, the function that takes
n\in\mathbb{N}
to the maximum order of an element of
S_{n}
, the symmetric group on
n
elements.

Note that by the prime number theorem,
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
is approximately equal to the
n
-th prime number
p_{n}
. This brings us to the central question of this paper: Can
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
be replaced by
p_{n}
in the above equivalence? In what follows, we give a partial answer to this question.

Theorem 1
.

If the Riemann Hypothesis is true, then

g(n)\leq e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}}

for all
n\geq 1
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\zeta
denote the Riemann zeta function. If the Riemann Hypothesis is false and
\sup\{\Re(\rho)~|~\zeta(\rho)=0\}\neq 1
, then there exists
n
such that
g(n)>e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}}
.

These results show that question of whether the Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to
\forall n,~g(n)\leq e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}}
comes down to the following unsolved problem: Does
g(n)
take values greater than
e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}}
in the case that the Riemann Hypothesis is “as false as possible,” i.e., when there exists a sequence
\{\rho_{i}\}
of zeros of
\zeta(s)
such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\Re(\rho_{i})=1
?

1
Bounding
g(n)
under the Riemann Hypothesis

We begin by establishing Theorem
1
. Following
[
2
]
, we define

a_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\log g(n)}{(n~\log n)^{1/4}}

(1)

The central ingredient in our proof is the following result from
[
2
]
:

Theorem 3

(Theorem 1.1(ii) in
[
2
]
)
.

Under the Riemann Hypothesis,

a_{n}\geq\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{3}-c-\frac{0.43\log\log n}{\log n}~\textrm{for all }n\geq 2

where
c=\sum_{\rho}\frac{1}{|\rho(\rho+1)|}\approx 0.046117644421509...
and the sum is taken over the set of non-trivial zeros of the Riemann
\zeta
function.

The second estimate we will need is the following:

Lemma 1.0.1
.

Under the Riemann Hypothesis,

\left|\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)-p_{n}\right|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8\pi}\log^{2}(2n\log n)\sqrt{n\log n}

for all
n\geq 2657
.

Proof.

Under the Riemann Hypothesis, a well-known result of Schoenfeld
[
9
, Corollary 1]
gives

|\pi(x)-\mathrm{li}(x)|<\frac{\sqrt{x}\log x}{8\pi}

for all
x\geq 2657
where
\pi(x)
denotes the prime-counting function. Plugging in the
n
-th prime
p_{n}
and observing that
\pi(p_{n})=n
we get:

|n-\mathrm{li}(p_{n})|<\frac{\sqrt{p_{n}}\log p_{n}}{8\pi}.

(2)

Applying the mean value theorem to
\mathrm{li}(x)
at the points
p_{n}
and
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{li}(\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n))-\mathrm{li}(p_{n})}{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)-p_{n}}=\mathrm{li}^{\prime}(x_{n})=\frac{1}{\log x_{n}}

for some
x_{n}
between
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
and
p_{n}
. Taking the absolute value of both sides and multiplying through by denominators, we have

|\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)-p_{n}|=(\log x_{n})|n-\mathrm{li}(p_{n}))|

(3)

Combining this with equation (
2
), we have

|\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)-p_{n}|<(\log x_{n})\frac{\sqrt{p_{n}}}{8\pi}\log p_{n}

(4)

for
n\geq 2657
. An elementary argument shows that
t\mapsto\mathrm{li}(2t\log t)-t
is positive for
t\geq 3
. Since
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(t)
is strictly increasing on
(1,\infty)
, this implies that
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)<2n\log n
for
n\geq 3
.

From
[
8
, (3.13)]
, for
n\geq 6
,

p_{n}<n(\log n+\log\log n)<2n\log n

Since
x_{n}
lies between
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
and
p_{n}
,
x_{n}<2n\log n
for all
n\geq 3
as well. Substituting these inequalities into inequality (
4
) above, we get

|\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)-p_{n}|<\log(2n\log n)\left|\frac{1}{8\pi}\sqrt{2n\log n}\log(2n\log n))\right|

=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8\pi}\log^{2}(2n\log n)\sqrt{n\log n}

for all
n\geq 2657
.

∎

Lemma 1.0.2
.

Under the Riemann Hypothesis,

\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\sqrt{p_{n}}<a_{n}\cdot(n\log n)^{1/4}

for all
n>10^{10}
.

Proof.

By the mean value theorem applied to the function
x\mapsto\sqrt{x}
at the points
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
and
p_{n}
,

\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\sqrt{p_{n}}=\frac{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)-p_{n}}{2\sqrt{x_{n}}}

(5)

for some
x_{n}
lying between
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
and
p_{n}
. By Rosser’s theorem
[
7
]
,
p_{n}>n\log n
for all
n
. A simple calculation shows that the mapping
t\mapsto t-\mathrm{li}(t\log t)
is increasing for
t>e^{e}
and positive for
t>40.5
and therefore
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)>n\log n
for all integers
n>40
. Since
x_{n}
lies between
p_{n}
and
\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
, it follows that
x_{n}>n\log n
, so

\sqrt{n\log n}<\sqrt{x_{n}}

for
n>40
. We therefore have

\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\sqrt{p_{n}}<\frac{|\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)-p_{n}|}{2\sqrt{n\log n}}

for
n>40
by equation (
5
).

Applying Lemma
1.0.1
to the numerator of the right-hand side of the above, we get

\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\sqrt{p_{n}}<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{16\pi}\log^{2}(2n\log n)

(6)

for all
n\geq 2657
.

Using Theorem
3
, we have

0.14-\frac{0.43\log\log n}{\log n}\leq\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{3}-c-\frac{0.43\log\log n}{\log n}\leq a_{n}

for all
n\geq 2
. A simple calculation also shows that the left-hand side of the above equation is always larger than 0.08 for
n>10^{10}
, so

0.08\cdot(n\log n)^{1/4}<a_{n}\cdot(n\log n)^{1/4}

(7)

for all
n>10^{10}
.

Direct calculation also gives

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{16\pi}\log^{2}(2n\log n)<0.08\cdot(n\log n)^{1/4}

for all
n>10^{10}
, so combining the above with equation (
7
) we get

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{16\pi}\log^{2}(2n\log n)<a_{n}\cdot(n\log n)^{1/4}

for all
n>10^{10}
. Putting this together with equation (
6
) above, we get

\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\sqrt{p_{n}}<a_{n}\cdot(n\log n)^{1/4}

for all
n>10^{10}
.

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

By taking the logarithm of both sides of the inequality
g(n)\leq e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}}
and rearranging terms, we obtain the inequality

\sqrt{p_{n}}-\log(g(n))>0.

It therefore suffices to show that, under the Riemann Hypothesis, this inequality holds for all
n\geq 1
. We proceed by showing this in two cases, one for
n>10^{10}
, and one for
n\leq 10^{10}
.

For
n>10^{10}
, Lemma
1.0.2
together with the definition of
a_{n}
gives us

\sqrt{p_{n}}-\log(g(n))=\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\log(g(n))-(\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\sqrt{p_{n}})

>\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\log(g(n))-a_{n}\cdot(n\log n)^{1/4}=0

We now consider the case where
n\leq 10^{10}
. For
n=1
or 2, one can easily check that
g(n)\leq e^{\sqrt{p_{n}}}
. For
3\leq n\leq 10^{10}
,
p_{n}\leq 2n\log n\leq 2\times 10^{10}\log(10^{10})<10^{14}
holds. If
m\leq 10^{14}
,
\pi(m)<\mathrm{li}(m)
by a result of Kotnik
[
4
]
(that has subsequently been proven for all
m
up to
10^{19}
by Büthe
[
1
]
.) Therefore

n=\pi(p_{n})<\mathrm{li}(p_{n})
, from which it follows that
p_{n}>\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)
.

Applying the square roots to both sides of this inequality, we have

\sqrt{p_{n}}>\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}
, so

\sqrt{p_{n}}-\log(g(n))>\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}-\log(g(n))

for all
n<10^{10}
.

Since
\sqrt{\mathrm{li}^{-1}(n)}>\log(g(n))
under the Riemann Hypothesis by the central result of
[
2
]
, the righthand side of the above is always positive, so

\sqrt{p_{n}}-\log(g(n))>0

as required.

∎

2
Finding large values of
g(n)
when the Riemann Hypothesis is false

We now turn to the proof of the second part of Theorem
1
. Throughout this section, we let

\Theta=\sup\{\Re(s)~|~\zeta(s)=0\}

and we assume
\Theta>\frac{1}{2}
.

Our approach follows the work of Massias, Nicolas, and Robin in
[
5
]
. We begin by recalling some terminology and results from
[
5
]
. Building on the work of Nicolas in
[
6
]
, the authors of
[
5
]
define a non-decreasing function
\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{N}
mapping
\eta\mapsto N_{\eta}
with the property
N_{\eta}\in g(\mathbb{N})
for all
\eta
. Given any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, they define
\rho=\rho(n)
to be such that

N_{\rho}=\max\{N_{\eta}~|~N_{\eta}\leq g(n)\}.

They also define
x_{1}=x_{1}(n)
be such that

x_{1}/\log x_{1}=\rho(n).

We note that
x_{1}(n)
and
\rho(n)
are non-decreasing functions of
n
. Following Nicolas et al., we will leave the dependence of
x_{1}
and
\rho
on
n
implicit in many of the expressions that follow.

The proof of Theorem
2
will require several bounds related to
\rho
,
x_{1}
and
N_{\rho}
established in
[
5
]
. The first is given by the following lemma, which appears as equation (6) in
[
5
]
. Let
\theta
and
\psi
denote Chebyshev’s functions

\theta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p

and

{\psi(x)=\sum_{p^{k}\leq x}\log p}.

Lemma 2.0.1
.

For
x_{1}
and
N_{\rho}
as above, we have

\theta(x_{1})\leq\log N_{\rho}\leq\psi(x_{1}).

Given a real-valued function
f
and a positive function
h
, let
f(x)=\Omega_{+}(h(x))
denote
\displaystyle{\limsup_{x\to\infty}}\frac{f(x)}{h(x)}>0
. The following lemma follows directly by combining results of
[
6
]
.

Lemma 2.0.2
.

If
\frac{1}{2}<\Theta<1
,

\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n))-n=\Omega_{+}\left(x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1}\right)

Proof.

Equation (28) in
[
5
]
gives

\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n=-\left(\Pi_{1}(x_{1})-\mathrm{li}(\psi^{2}(x_{1}))\right)+O(x_{1}^{3/2}/\log x_{1})

(8)

where
\Pi_{1}(x)=\sum_{p^{k}\leq x}\frac{p^{k}}{k}
and
\psi
is Chebyshev’s function
\psi(x)=\sum_{p^{k}\leq x}\log p
. As is pointed out in
[
5
]
, the convexity of the function
t\mapsto\mathrm{li}(t^{2})
for
t\geq e
implies

\mathrm{li}(\psi^{2}(x_{1}))\geq\mathrm{li}(x_{1}^{2})+\frac{x_{1}}{\log x_{1}}(\psi(x_{1})-x_{1})

for all sufficiently large
x_{1}
. Substituting this into equation (
8
) and rearranging terms, we have

\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n))-n\geq\mathrm{li}(x_{1}^{2})-\Pi_{1}(x_{1})+\frac{x_{1}}{\log x_{1}}(\psi(x_{1})-x_{1})+O\left(x_{1}^{3/2}/\log x_{1}\right).

(9)

From Lemma C part (iii) of
[
5
]
, if
\Theta<1
,

\mathrm{li}(x_{1}^{2})-\Pi_{1}(x_{1})+\frac{x_{1}}{\log x_{1}}(\psi(x_{1})-x_{1})=\Omega_{+}(x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1}).

Substituting this into equation (
9
), we have

\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n))-n\geq\Omega_{+}(x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1})+O\left(x_{1}^{3/2}/\log x_{1}\right).

Since
\Theta+1>3/2
by assumption,
x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1}
dominates the
O\left(x_{1}^{3/2}/\log x_{1}\right)
term, so we obtain

\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n))-n=\Omega_{+}(x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1})

as required.
∎

A third result we will need is that any element of the image
g(\mathbb{N})
of Landau’s function is close to an element of the image of
\rho\mapsto N_{\rho}
as given by the following lemma (equation (11) from
[
5
]
):

Lemma 2.0.3
.

With
N_{\rho}
as above,

\log(g(n))=\log N_{\rho}+O(\log x_{1}).

The final ingredients we will need are bounds on the error term in the prime number theorem. To this end, we consider the function

R(x)=\sup_{e\leq s\leq x}|\pi(s)-\mathrm{li}(s)|,

which, as we will show, satisfies the following bound.

Lemma 2.0.4
.

For all
x\in[e,\infty)
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

R(x+b)\leq R(x)+2(b+e+1)

The proof of Lemma
2.0.4
requires the following elementary lemma about the growth of the absolute value of the difference between two positive, monotone increasing functions that satisfy a sublinearity condition.

Lemma 2.0.5
.

Let
L\geq 0
and let
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
be positive, monotone increasing functions such that for all
x\in[L,\infty)
and
i\in\{1,2\}

•

f_{i}(x)\leq x

•

there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

f_{i}(x+b)-f_{i}(x)\leq b+C

Then
h(x)=\displaystyle\sup_{L\leq s\leq x}|f_{1}(s)-f_{2}(s)|
satisfies

h(x+b)\leq h(x)+2(b+C+L)

for all
x\in[L,\infty)
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
k(s)=|f_{1}(s)-f_{2}(s)|
, so
h(x)=\displaystyle\sup_{L\leq s\leq x}k(s)
. Since

h(x+b)=\sup_{L\leq s\leq x+b}k(s)=\max\left\{\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}k(s),\sup_{L+b\leq s\leq x+b}k(s)\right\},

it suffices to prove

\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}k(s)\leq h(x)+2(b+C+L)

(10)

and

\sup_{L+b\leq s\leq x+b}k(s)\leq h(x)+2(b+C+L).

(11)

Inequality (
10
) follows from the sublinearity assumptions on
f_{i}
, since

\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}k(s)=\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}|f_{1}(s)-f_{2}(s)|\leq\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}|f_{1}(s)|+\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}|f_{2}(s)|

\leq\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}s+\sup_{L\leq s\leq L+b}s=2(L+b)\leq h(x)+2(b+C+L).

For inequality (
11
), the positivity and monotonicity of
f_{i}
together with the bound
f_{i}(x+b)-f_{i}(x)\leq b+C
give us

k(s+b)=|f_{1}(s+b)-f_{2}(s+b)|

=|f_{1}(s+b)-f_{1}(s)-(f_{2}(s+b)-f_{2}(s))+f_{1}(s)-f_{2}(s)|

\leq|f_{1}(s+b)-f_{1}(s)|+|f_{2}(s+b)-f_{2}(s)|+|f_{1}(s)-f_{2}(s)|

=f_{1}(s+b)-f_{1}(s)~+~f_{2}(s+b)-f_{2}(s)~+~k(s)

\leq 2(b+C)+k(s).

Taking the supremum over
s\in[L,x]
of both sides of this inequality, we have

\sup_{L\leq s\leq x}k(s+b)\leq\sup_{L\leq s\leq x}k(s)+2(b+C)=h(x)+2(b+C)\newline
\leq h(x)+2(b+C+L)

Since

\sup_{L\leq s\leq x}k(s+b)=\sup_{L+b\leq s\leq x+b}k(s)

this establishes inequality (
11
).
∎

2.0.5
to prove Lemma
2.0.4

Proof of Lemma
2.0.4
.

By Lemma
2.0.5
, it suffices to check that
\mathrm{li}(x)
and
\pi(x)
satisfy the assumptions of the lemma on
[e,\infty)
with constants
C=1
and
L=e
. Monotonicity and positivity follow easily from the definition, as does
\pi(x)\leq x
and
\mathrm{li}(x)\leq x
for
x\geq 2
.

To see that
\pi(x+b)\leq\pi(x)+b+1
, note that
\pi(x+b)-\pi(x)
counts the number of primes in the interval
(x,x+b]
. Since this interval contains at most
b+1
integers, the result follows.

For
\mathrm{li}(x)
, we have that for all
x\geq e
and
b\geq 0
,

\mathrm{li}(x+b)=\mathrm{li}(x)+\int_{x}^{x+b}\frac{ds}{\log s}

\leq\mathrm{li}(x)+\int_{x}^{x+b}1\cdot ds=\mathrm{li}(x)+b\leq\mathrm{li}(x)+b+1

since
1/\log s<1
on
[e,\infty)
.

∎

Lemma 2.0.6
.

If
1/2<\Theta<1
, then

R(\log^{2}(g(n)))=O(x_{1}^{2\Theta}\log x_{1}).

Proof.

Let
N_{\rho}
be the largest element of the image of the map
\rho\mapsto N_{\rho}
less than
g(n)
as above. By Lemma
2.0.3
,

\log(g(n))\leq\log N_{\rho}+C\log x_{1}

for some
C>0
, so

\log^{2}(g(n))\leq\log^{2}(N_{\rho})+2C(\log N_{\rho})(\log x_{1})+C^{2}\log^{2}(x_{1}).

By the monotonicity of
R
and Lemma
2.0.4
, we have

R(\log^{2}(g(n)))\leq R(\log^{2}(N_{\rho})+2C(\log N_{\rho})(\log x_{1})+C^{2}\log^{2}(x_{1}))

\leq R(\log^{2}(N_{\rho}))+2(2C(\log N_{\rho})(\log x_{1})+C^{2}\log^{2}(x_{1})+e+1).

By Lemma
2.0.1
, we have that the above is bounded by

R(\psi^{2}(x_{1}))+4C\psi(x_{1})\log x_{1}+2C^{2}\log^{2}(x_{1})+2e+2=R(\psi^{2}(x_{1}))+O(x_{1}\log x_{1})

since
\psi(x_{1})=O(x_{1})
by the prime number theorem. Since
x_{1}\log x_{1}
is negligible relative to
x_{1}^{2\Theta}\log x_{1}
when
\Theta>1/2
, it therefore suffices to show that
R(\psi^{2}(x_{1}))=O(x_{1}^{2\Theta}\log x_{1})
.

Since
\Theta<1
by assumption, we have that
R(x_{1})=O(x_{1}^{\Theta}\log x_{1})
(cf
[
3
]
Theorem 30). Using
\psi(x_{1})=O(x_{1})
once again,

R(\psi^{2}(x_{1}))=O(R(x_{1}^{2}))=O(x_{1}^{2\Theta}\log x_{1})

as required.

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
, i.e., if
\Theta>\frac{1}{2}
, there exists an integer

such that

.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Since the exponential, square-root, and prime counting function
\pi
are all monotone, the conclusion of Theorem
2
is equivalent to the statement

\exists n,~\pi(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n>0.

Let
N_{\rho}
be the largest element of the image of the map
\rho\mapsto N_{\rho}
less than
g(n)
. Then

\pi(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n=\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n+\pi(\log^{2}(g(n)))-\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n)))

\geq\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n-R(\log^{2}(g(n))).

(12)

By Lemma
2.0.2
, we have

\mathrm{li}(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n=\Omega_{+}(x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1})

and by Lemma
2.0.6
, we have

R(\log^{2}(g(n)))=O(x_{1}^{2\Theta}\log x_{1}).

Applying this to equation (
12
) we have

\pi(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n\geq\Omega_{+}(x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1})-O(x_{1}^{2\Theta}\log x_{1}).

Since
\Theta<1
,
2\Theta<\Theta+1
, so
R(\psi^{2}(x_{1}))
is negligible relative to
x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1}
and therefore

\pi(\log^{2}(g(n)))-n\geq\Omega_{+}(x_{1}^{\Theta+1}/\log x_{1}).

It follows that
\pi(\log^{2}(g(n))-n
must take a positive value for some
n
.

∎
