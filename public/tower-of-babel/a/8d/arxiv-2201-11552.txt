The gauge invariant cosmological Jacobi map from weak lensing at leading order
and Giovanni Marozzi

Abstract
We compute the weak lensing Jacobi map at first order in perturbation theory and show that it is both, gauge invariant and symmetric. Linear perturbations therefore do not induce any rotation. However, vector and tensor perturbations do induce B B -modes in the shear. We show that contrary to what is often claimed in the literature, the shear B B -mode power spectrum is not fully determined by the rotation power spectrum. Also the E E -mode shear power spectrum is not determined by the convergence power spectrum. While this difference is small for scalar perturbations, it becomes very significant for tensor perturbations, i.e. gravitational waves.

Introduction

In this paper we show that the treatment of weak lensing typically found in the cosmology literature is not complete and actually it is not gauge invariant, since often one part of
the Jacobi map is neglected. This is unproblematic in a nearly
Newtonian situation but becomes relevant on very large
scales and, especially, when we include vector and tensor modes.
Working
at first order in cosmological perturbation theory
, we obtain the following results when we correctly consider the Jacobi map for lensing:

•

The Jacobi map is gauge invariant when expressed in terms of
z
and
{\bf n}
. Here
z
is the observed redshift and
{\bf n}
is the observation direction.

•

The Jacobi map is symmetric, hence there is no rotation at first order. We have
\omega\equiv 0
for scalar, vector and tensor perturbations.

•

The
B
-mode of the shear does not vanish for vector and tensor perturbations and, especially, its angular power spectrum is not given by the one of the rotation,
\omega
, as claimed in the literature, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

•

The power spectrum of the convergence
\kappa

-mode of the shear are not simply related as claimed in the literature, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. We find that while for scalar perturbations this difference is relevant only at large scales,

C^{E}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})=\langle a^{E}_{\ell m}(z_{1}){a^{E}_{\ell m}}^{*}(z_{2})\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad C^{B}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})=\langle a^{B}_{\ell m}(z_{1}){a^{B}_{\ell m}}^{*}(z_{2})\rangle\,.

(3.16)

Here
\langle\dots\rangle
stands for an
ensemble average
over different realizations of the perturbations. Contrary to
\gamma_{\pm}
the shear
E
- and
B
-mode spectra are independent of the chosen coordinate system. Since they have opposite helicity,
E
- and
B
-modes are uncorrelated.

3.1
Power spectra

In this section we present the power spectrum for
\gamma_{\pm}(z,{\bf n})
. Inserting (
2.19
) in (
3.12
) we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{\pm}(z,{\bf n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{\pm}^{2}\int_{0}^{r_{s}}dr\frac{r_{s}-r}{r\,r_{s}}\left(\sigma_{r}^{(v)}+H^{(t)}_{rr}-\Phi-\Psi\right)-\partial_{\pm}\,\int_{0}^{r_{s}}\frac{dr}{r}\left(\sigma^{(v)}_{\pm}+2H^{(t)}_{r+}\right)+H^{(t)}_{\pm\pm}\,.

We focus our investigation on tensor perturbations. The scalar part is simple and leads to the well known standard result, see, e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, and, as already mentioned, vector perturbations are absent in simple inflationary models.

In Appendix
B.1
we give the detailed derivation of the shear tensor power spectrum using the total angular momentum method. Here we just report the results for the
E
- and
B
-mode spectra. We can decompose each of them in the form

\displaystyle C^{X}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv

\,{}_{0}C^{X}(z_{1},z_{2})+\,_{1}C^{X}(z_{1},z_{2})+\,_{2}C^{X}(z_{1},z_{2})

(3.18)

\displaystyle+\,_{01}C^{X}(z_{1},z_{2})+\,_{02}C^{X}(z_{1},z_{2})+\,_{12}C^{X}(z_{1},z_{2})\,,

where
0,~1,~2
respectively label helicity 0, helicity 1 and helicity 2 projections of the tensor modes onto the screen. A single subscript stands for auto-correlation of helicity projections whereas double subscripts indicate cross-correlations between different helicity projections. We obtain the following non-vanishing contributions for the auto-correlations

\,{}_{0}C^{E}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{32\pi}\left[\frac{(\ell+2)!}{(\ell-2)!}\right]^{2}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}dr\int_{0}^{r_{2}}dr^{\prime}\frac{r_{1}-r}{rr_{1}}\frac{r_{2}-r^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}r_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z,z^{\prime})\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{(kr)^{2}}\frac{j_{\ell}(kr^{\prime})}{(kr^{\prime})^{2}}

\,{}_{1}C^{E}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell+2)^{2}}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}\frac{dr}{r}\int_{0}^{r_{2}}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{j_{\ell+1}(kr)}{kr}-(\ell+1)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{(kr)^{2}}\right]\left[\frac{j_{\ell+1}(kr^{\prime})}{kr^{\prime}}-(\ell+1)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr^{\prime})}{(kr^{\prime})^{2}}\right]

\,{}_{2}C^{E}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z_{1},z_{2})\left[\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}\frac{j_{\ell}(kr_{1})}{(kr_{1})^{2}}-j_{\ell}(kr_{1})+\frac{j_{\ell-1}(kr_{1})}{kr_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}\frac{j_{\ell}(kr_{2})}{(kr_{2})^{2}}-j_{\ell}(kr_{2})+\frac{j_{\ell-1}(kr_{2})}{kr_{2}}\right]

\,{}_{1}C^{B}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}\frac{dr}{r}\int_{0}^{r_{2}}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\left[(\ell^{2}-\ell-3)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{kr}+j_{\ell-1}(kr)+j_{\ell+1}(kr)\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[(\ell^{2}-\ell-3)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr^{\prime})}{kr^{\prime}}+j_{\ell-1}(kr^{\prime})+j_{\ell+1}(kr^{\prime})\right]

\,{}_{2}C^{B}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z_{1},z_{2})

(3.19)

\displaystyle\times\left[(\ell+2)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr_{1})}{kr_{1}}-j_{\ell+1}(kr_{1})\right]\left[(\ell+2)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr_{2})}{kr_{2}}-j_{\ell+1}(kr_{2})\right]\,.

The non-vanishing cross-correlations are

\,{}_{01}C^{E}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell+2)}{16\pi}\frac{(\ell+2)!}{(\ell-2)!}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}dr\frac{r_{1}-r}{rr_{1}}\int_{0}^{r_{2}}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{(kr)^{2}}\left[\frac{j_{\ell+1}(kr^{\prime})}{kr^{\prime}}-(\ell+1)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr^{\prime})}{(kr^{\prime})^{2}}\right]+\left(z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2}\right)

\,{}_{02}C^{E}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16\pi}\frac{(\ell+2)!}{(\ell-2)!}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}dr\frac{r_{1}-r}{rr_{1}}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z,z_{2})

\displaystyle\times\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{(kr)^{2}}\left[\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}\frac{j_{\ell}(kr_{2})}{(kr_{2})^{2}}-j_{\ell}(kr_{2})+\frac{j_{\ell-1}(kr_{2})}{kr_{2}}\right]+\left(z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2}\right)

\,{}_{12}C^{E}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell+2)}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}\frac{dr}{r}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z,z_{2})\left[\frac{j_{\ell+1}(kr)}{kr}-(\ell+1)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{(kr)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}\frac{j_{\ell}(kr_{2})}{(kr_{2})^{2}}-j_{\ell}(kr_{2})+\frac{j_{\ell-1}(kr_{2})}{kr_{2}}\right]+\left(z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2}\right)

\,{}_{12}C^{B}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}\frac{dr}{r}\int k^{2}dkP_{T}(k,z,z_{2})\left[(\ell^{2}-\ell-3)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{kr}+j_{\ell-1}(kr)+j_{\ell+1}(kr)\right]

(3.20)

\displaystyle\times\left[(\ell+2)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr_{2})}{kr_{2}}-j_{\ell+1}(kr_{2})\right]+\left(z_{1}\leftrightarrow z_{2}\right)\,.

Here
P_{T}(k,z_{1},z_{2})
is the unequal redshift tensor power spectrum. It is obtained from the inital power spectrum
P_{T}(k)
and the tensor transfer function
T_{T}(k,z)
by
P_{T}(k,z_{1},z_{2})=P_{T}(k)T_{T}(k,z_{1})T_{T}(k,z_{2})
.
The relations between the comoving distance and redshift are obvious,
r_{1}=r(z_{1})
,
r_{2}=r(z_{2})
,
z=z(r)
and
z^{\prime}=z(r^{\prime})
.
The
E
- and
B
-mode power spectra (
3.18
), determined through Eqs. (
3.19
) and (
3.20
)), agree with the results derived in Refs.
[
20
,
3
]
, see Appendix
B.2
for a detailed comparison.

5
, we will present numerical results of our helicity decomposition for a tensor power spectrum from inflation.

4
Convergence

As already stressed before, our results show that in general
\kappa
and
\gamma_{E}
are different. In this section, we explicitly evaluate the angular power spectrum of the convergence in order to quantify this difference for scalar and tensor perturbations. To this aim, we start from the Jacobi map given in Eq. (
2.22
) and evaluate the convergence according to Eq. (
3.9
). We find

\displaystyle\kappa(z,{\bf n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi-\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\right)\delta z_{GI}+\frac{\delta w_{GI}}{r}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{a}\delta\theta^{a}_{GI}-\frac{1}{2}H^{(t)}_{ab}\hat{\gamma}^{ab}\,,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\kappa^{(T)}(z,{\bf n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\right)\delta w^{\prime}_{GI}+\frac{\delta w_{GI}}{r}

(4.2)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r_{s}}\frac{dr}{r^{2}}\left(\Delta_{2}\delta w_{GI}-2\,r\,\hat{\gamma}^{ab}\nabla_{a}H^{(t)}_{rb}\right)-\frac{1}{2}H^{(t)}_{ab}\hat{\gamma}^{ab}\,.

While the first line is the full
\kappa
, the second relation is valid only for tensor perturbations.

For comparison with the literature we also
evaluate (
2.15
)
for scalar perturbations at fixed time,
t
, in longitudinal gauge. After some integration by part we find

\displaystyle\kappa^{(S)}(t,{\bf n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi+\int_{0}^{{r_{z}}}\frac{dr}{{r_{z}}}\left[\frac{{r_{z}}-r}{2\,r}\Delta_{2}(\Phi+\Psi)-(\Phi+\Psi)\right]\,,

(4.3)

where
r_{z}
is the comoving distance out to redshift
z(t)
.
This result agrees with Ref.
[
27
]
, but note that it is not gauge invariant. Nevertheless, in addition to the traditional integral of
\Delta_{2}(\Phi+\Psi)
which comes from
\nabla_{a}\delta\theta^{a}_{GI}
and is also present in the scalar shear, there are additional relativistic terms.
The observable
\kappa(z,{\bf n})
contains additional corrections of similar amplitude as the relativistic terms.
For scalar perturbations Eq. (
4.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\kappa^{(S)}(z,{\bf n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi-\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\right)\delta z_{GI}+\frac{\delta w_{GI}}{r}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{a}\delta\theta^{a}_{GI}\,,

(4.4)

where for scalar perturbations we have

\displaystyle\delta w_{GI}=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}\left(\Phi+\Psi\right)\,,

\displaystyle\nabla_{a}\delta\theta^{a}_{GI}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}\frac{r-r^{\prime}}{rr^{\prime}}\Delta_{2}\left(\Phi+\Psi\right)\,,

\displaystyle\delta z_{GI}=

\displaystyle-\Phi-\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}\left(\Phi^{\prime}+\Psi^{\prime}\right)-\partial_{r}\int_{0}^{t_{s}}dt\,\frac{a(t^{\prime})}{a(t)}\,\Phi(t^{\prime},r)\,,

(4.5)

so that finally

\displaystyle\kappa^{(S)}(z,{\bf n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi+\Phi+\int_{0}^{{r_{z}}}\frac{dr}{{r_{z}}}\left[\frac{{r_{z}}-r}{2\,r}\Delta_{2}(\Phi+\Psi)-(\Phi+\Psi)\right]

(4.6)

\displaystyle+\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\right)\left[\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}\left(\Phi^{\prime}+\Psi^{\prime}\right)+\partial_{r}\int_{0}^{r_{s}}dr^{\prime}\,\frac{a(t^{\prime})}{a(t_{s})}\,\Phi(t^{\prime},r^{\prime})\right]-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\Phi\,.\qquad

In
\Lambda
CDM cosmology the term
\partial_{r}\int_{0}^{r_{s}}dr^{\prime}\,\frac{a(t^{\prime})}{a(t_{s})}\,\Phi(t^{\prime},r^{\prime})
can be replaced by the radial velocity,
V_{r}
. For this one has to assume that sources and observer move on geodesics which is not necessarily true for the velocity field of the cosmic fluid which obeys the Euler equation. However, for pressureless matter in
\Lambda
CDM the fluid velocity is geodesic. Comparing (
4.6
) with (
4.3
), we notice that several additional terms of the order of the gravitational potentials
\Psi
appear as well as one term proportional to the velocity,
\partial_{r}\Psi
which is parametrically a factor
k/\mathcal{H}
larger than potential terms.
Our result actually agrees up to the sign with the perturbation of the area distance which can be found e.g. in Refs.
[
28
,
29
,
26
]
.
The expression for its power spectrum, the
C_{\ell}^{(s)\,\kappa}(z_{1},z_{2})
is somewhat cumbersome and not very illuminating. It is given explicitly in the Appendix, Eq. (
C.9
) and plotted in the figures of Section
5
.

Let us now concentrate on tensor perturbations.
We recall that for tensor perturbations

\delta w_{GI}=\int_{0}^{r_{s}}dr\,H^{(t)}_{rr}\,.

(4.7)

At this point, we make use of the fact that
H^{(t)}_{ij}
is transverse and traceless, so that we can write
r\nabla^{a}H^{(t)}_{ra}=-\left(\partial_{r}H^{(t)}_{rr}+\frac{3}{r}H^{(t)}_{rr}\right)
and
\hat{\gamma}^{ab}H^{(t)}_{ab}=-H^{(t)}_{rr}
. This leads to the following expressions for the convergence from tensor perturbations

\displaystyle\kappa(z,{\bf n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\right)\int_{0}^{r_{z}}dr\,\partial_{t}H^{(t)}_{rr}+\frac{1}{r_{z}}\int_{0}^{r_{z}}dr\,H^{(t)}_{rr}

(4.8)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r_{z}}dr\left(\frac{r_{z}-r}{r_{z}r}\Delta_{2}\,H^{(t)}_{rr}+2\,\partial_{r}H^{(t)}_{rr}+6\,\frac{H^{(t)}_{rr}}{r}\right)+\frac{1}{2}H^{(t)}_{rr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r_{z}}dr\,\left[\frac{H^{(t)}_{rr}}{r_{z}}-3\frac{H^{(t)}_{rr}}{r}-\left(2-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r_{z}}\right)\partial_{r}H^{(t)}_{rr}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{r_{z}-r}{r_{z}r}\Delta_{2}\,H^{(t)}_{rr}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r_{z}}\right)H^{(t)}_{rr}\,.

Eq. (
4.8
) has been further manipulated in order to write all the integrated terms without time derivative. To this aim, we made use of the fact that all the integrals in Eq. (
4.8
) are done along the past light-cone, where
\frac{d}{dr}=-\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}
. This result agrees (up to a sign) with the perturbation of the area distance from tensor perturbations, see
[
30
]
.
In Appendix
B.3
we calculate the convergence power spectrum from (
4.8
). Here we just report the final result.

Introducing the kernels

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(k,r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\right)\frac{j_{\ell+1}(kr^{\prime})}{kr^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{2-\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}-\frac{4(\ell-2)-\ell(\ell+1)}{2}+\frac{\ell-2}{\mathcal{H}r}-3\right)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr^{\prime})}{(kr^{\prime})^{2}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(k,r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}r}\right)\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{(kr)^{2}}\,,

(4.9)

the convergence power spectrum from tensor perturbations is given by

\displaystyle C^{\kappa}_{\ell}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\frac{(\ell+2)!}{(\ell-2)!}\int k^{2}dk\left\{\int^{r_{1}}_{0}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\int^{r_{2}}_{0}\frac{dr^{\prime\prime}}{r^{\prime\prime}}P_{T}(k,z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime})\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(k,r_{1},r^{\prime})\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(k,r_{2},r^{\prime\prime})\right.

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\left.P_{T}(k,z_{1},z_{2})\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(k,r_{1})\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(k,r_{2})+\int_{0}^{r_{1}}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}P_{T}(k,z^{\prime},z_{2})\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(k,r_{1},r^{\prime})\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(k,r_{2})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{0}^{r_{2}}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}P_{T}(k,z_{1},z^{\prime})\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(k,r_{2},r^{\prime})\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(k,r_{1})\right\}\,.

The corresponding kernels
\mathcal{I}^{E}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{L}^{E}_{\ell}
for the shear
E
-modes are

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{E}_{\ell}(k,r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\frac{r-r^{\prime}}{r}-(\ell-2)-3\right]\frac{j_{\ell}(kr^{\prime})}{(kr^{\prime})^{2}}+\frac{j_{\ell+1}(kr^{\prime})}{kr^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{E}_{\ell}(k,r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{j_{\ell}(kr)}{(kr)^{2}}\,,

(4.11)

which are clearly different. They are the kernels one obtains when considering only the term
\Delta_{2}\delta w_{GI}
in (
4.1
).

While the difference between
\kappa
and
E
-mode shear power spectra for scalar modes are terms which are parametrically smaller, by at least one factor
(k/\mathcal{H})
than the equal term which dominates on small scales,
k\gg\mathcal{H}
, for tensor perturbations
the terms are all of the same order and we can at best say that
\kappa
and
E
-shear power spectra are of the same order of magnitude.

k>\mathcal{H}
, the
E
and
B

In Ref.
[
3
]
it has been shown that this tensor auto-correlation spectrum is much smaller than intrinsic alignment and will therefore be very hard to measure. For this reason we also show the cross-correlations for different redshifts which are not plagued by intrinsic alignment.

In fig.
4
we consider the correlations between different redshifts. The coefficients of the expansion in spherical harmonics of the
E
- and
B
- modes oscillate with a frequency determined by the comoving distance to the source. By correlating different redshifts we therefore multiply spherical Bessel functions with different phases which lead to oscillations in the cross power spectrum. Moreover, the conversion from the angular multipole
\ell
to the physical separation between the two sources is redshift dependent. In particular, a given multipole
\ell
corresponds to a larger physical distance when increasing the redshift difference
\Delta z=\left|z_{1}-z_{2}\right|
, leading to a suppression of the angular power spectrum. For the largest redshift difference,
z_{2}=1.25
(orange lines) the rapid oscillations of the spherical Bessel functions are challenging our accuracy. The wiggles at
\ell>60
are not physical but an accuracy problem.

In fig.
4
, lower panel, we also show the tensor spectrum of the convergence
\kappa

E
-mode spectrum (up to an
\ell
-factor which rapidly converges to
1

\ell
), we see a very different behavior. At equal redshift (blue line) the
\kappa

E
-mode power spectrum. This remains roughly true for the unequal redshift spectra even though the shapes, e.g., the number and positions of the zero-crossings, are similar.

Contrary to that, the
E

B

\ell>6

E
- and
B
-part of the Weyl tensor of the gravitational waves. In Appendix

B

In Fig.

Our results can be summarized as follows: For linear tensor perturbations, the

- and
B
-mode spectra of the shear are identical, the
\kappa
-spectrum is more than an order of magnitude smaller for

E
-mode and the
\kappa

B
-mode and the rotation vanish.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have computed the Jacobi map to first order in cosmological perturbation theory. This is the correct gauge invariant and fully relativistic lensing map. We have shown that contrary to the often used ’amplification matrix’
\partial_{a}\theta_{b}
, the Jacobi matrix has in general four degrees of freedom and is symmetric at first order in perturbation theory also when vector and tensor perturbations are included. Therefore lensing rotation vanishes at first order also for vector and tensor perturbations. Furthermore, the simple relations between the convergence
\kappa
and the shear
E
-mode
\gamma_{E}
are not valid. While for scalar perturbations the difference is relatively small (up to 20%) and only relevant on large scales,
\ell\lesssim 20
, it is very significant for tensor perturbations, for which the corresponding power spectra differ by more than an order of magnitude. More precisely, the tensor convergence power spectrum is on average a factor 10 to 50 times smaller then the tensor shear
E
-mode power spectrum. This factor depends on the redshift and is largest at
z_{*}\simeq 1.6
which maximizes the angular diameter distance. Even though, at first, this is surprising, it is certainly not inconceivable. While for scalar perturbations there is a hierarchy between time derivatives,
\propto\mathcal{H}
and spatial derivatives
\propto k
, this is no longer the case for tensor perturbations which oscillate with frequency
k
. Therefore, the additional terms which appear in our relativistic treatment, which contain radial derivatives that can be converted into time derivatives on the lightcone, are expected to be of the same order of magnitude as the original terms, and therefore they can change the result very substantially.

This equivalence of spatial and temporal derivatives for free, subhorizon tensor perturbations also explains the fact that the
E
- and
B
-mode shear power spectra are identical well inside the horizon, for
z=2
this is above
\ell\sim 6
.

Furthermore, the simple relation between the
\gamma_{B}
and rotation power spectrum is not valid as the latter vanishes at first order in perturbation theory while the former is equal to the
E
-mode spectrum for tensor perturbations.

While lensing shear spectra at equal redshift are plagued by intrinsic alignment and therefore difficult to measure, cross spectra at different redshift might be a way out of this systematic problem.

Evolution of shear E and B modes

Let us start from the evolution equation for the Jacobi map (see

\displaystyle 2\bar{d}\left[(1-\kappa)\text{Re}\Psi_{0}+\gamma_{1}\,\Phi_{00}\right]\,,

Defining

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\kappa)\text{Re}\Psi_{0}+\gamma_{1}\,\Phi_{00}\,,

But

- and
B
-modes.
The

- and

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}+\Delta\right)H_{ij}\simeq-k^{2}H_{ij}

\displaystyle B_{ij}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{nmi}\partial_{t}\left(\partial_{m}H_{jn}-\partial_{n}H_{jm}\right)\simeq-kk_{m}H_{jn}\epsilon_{nmi}\,.
