Nearly-Optimal Hierarchical Clustering for Well-Clustered Graphs A preliminary version of the work appeared at the 40th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML ’23).
Steinar Laenen, Bogdan-Adrian Manghiuc, He Sun

Abstract
This paper presents two efficient hierarchical clustering ( HC ) algorithms with respect to Dasgupta’s cost function. For any input graph G G with a clear cluster-structure, our designed algorithms run in nearly-linear time in the input size of G G , and return an O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -approximate HC tree with respect to Dasgupta’s cost function. We compare the performance of our algorithm against the previous state-of-the-art on synthetic and real-world datasets and show that our designed algorithm produces comparable or better HC trees with much lower running time.

Introduction

Hierarchical clustering (
HC
) is the recursive partitioning of a dataset into increasingly smaller clusters via an effective binary tree representation, and has been employed as a standard package in data analysis with widespread applications in practice.
Traditional
HC
algorithms are typically based on agglomerative heuristics and, due to the lack of a clear objective function, there was limited work on their analysis.
Dasgupta
[
Das16
]
introduced a simple cost function for hierarchical clustering, and this work has inspired a number of algorithmic studies on hierarchical clustering.

In this paper we study efficient hierarchical clustering for graphs with a clear structure of clusters. We prove that, under two different conditions of an input graph
G
that characterise its cluster-structure, one can construct in nearly-linear time

2
Related Work

Addressing the lack of an objective function for hierarchical clustering, Dasgupta
[
Das16
]
introduced a simple cost function to measure the quality of an
HC
tree, and proved several properties of the cost function. Dasgupta further showed that a recursive sparsest cut algorithm can be applied to construct an
O(\log^{3/2}n)
-approximate
HC
tree.
Charikar and Chatziafratis
[
CC17
]
improved the analysis of constructing
HC
trees based on the sparsest cut problem, and proved that an
\alpha
-approximate algorithm for the sparsest cut problem can be employed to construct an
O(\alpha)
-approximate
HC
tree. This implies that, by applying the state-of-the-art for the sparsest cut problem
[
ARV09
]
, an
O(\sqrt{\log n})
-approximate
HC
tree can be constructed in polynomial-time.

It is known that, assuming the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis, it is
NP
-hard to construct an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree for general graphs
[
CC17
]
. Hence, it is natural to examine the conditions of input graphs under which an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree can be constructed in polynomial-time. Cohen-Addad et al.
[
CAKMT17
]
studied a hierarchical extension of the classical stochastic block model (
SBM
) and showed that, for graphs randomly generated from this model, there is an
SVD
projection-based algorithm
[
McS01
]
that, together with linkage heuristics, constructs a
(1+o(1))
-approximate
HC
tree with high probability.
Manghiuc and Sun
[
MS21
]
studied hierarchical clustering for well-clustered graphs and proved that, when there is a cluster-structure of an input graph
G
, an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree of
G
can be constructed in polynomial-time; their designed algorithm is based on the graph decomposition algorithm by Oveis Gharan and Trevisan
[
OGT14
]
, and has high time complexity.

There are recent studies of
hierarchical clustering in different models of computation. For instance, Kapralov et al.
[
KKLM22
]
studied the problem of learning the hierarchical cluster structure of graphs in a semi-supervised setting.
Their presented algorithm runs in sub-linear time and, under some clusterability conditions of an input graph
G
with
k
clusters, their algorithm
O(\sqrt{\log k})
-approximates Dasgupta’s cost of an optimal
HC
tree.
This work is incomparable to ours: the objective of their work is to approximate Dasgupta’s cost of an
HC
tree, while the output of our algorithms is a complete
HC
tree.

Finally, there are studies of hierarchical clustering under different objective functions.
Moseley and Wang
[
MW17
]
studied the dual of Dasgupta’s cost function.
This objective, and a dissimilarity objective by Cohen-Addad et al.
[
CAKMTM19
]
, have
received considerable attention
[
AAV20
,
CCN19
,
CYL+20
]
. It is important to notice that an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree can be constructed efficiently for general graphs under these objectives, suggesting the fundamental difference in the computational complexity of constructing an
HC
tree under different objectives. This is the main reason for us to entirely focus on Dasgupta’s cost function in this work.

3.2
Hierarchical Clustering

A
hierarchical clustering (
HC
) tree
of a given graph
G
is a binary tree
\mathcal{T}
with
n
leaf nodes such that each leaf corresponds to exactly one vertex
v\in V(G)
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be an
HC
tree of a graph
G=(V,E,w)
, and
N\in\mathcal{T}
be an arbitrary internal node
2
2

2

We consider any non-leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
an
internal node
. We always use the term
node(s)
for the nodes of
\mathcal{T}
and the term
vertices
for the elements of the vertex set
V
.
of
\mathcal{T}
.

We denote
\mathcal{T}[N]
to be the subtree of
\mathcal{T}
rooted at
N
,
\mathsf{leaves}\left(\mathcal{T}[N]\right)
to be the set of leaf nodes of
\mathcal{T}[N]
,
and
\mathrm{parent}_{\mathcal{T}}(N)
to be the parent of node
N
in
\mathcal{T}
.

In addition, each internal node
N\in\mathcal{T}
induces a unique vertex set
C\subseteq V
formed by the vertices corresponding to
\mathsf{leaves}(\mathcal{T}[N])
.
For ease of presentation, we sometimes abuse the notation and write
N\in\mathcal{T}
for both the internal node of
\mathcal{T}
and the corresponding subset of vertices in
V
.

To measure the quality of an
HC
tree
\mathcal{T}
with similarity weights, Dasgupta
[
Das16
]
introduced the cost function defined by

\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T})\triangleq\sum_{e=\{u,v\}\in E}w_{e}\cdot\lvert\mathsf{leaves}\left(\mathcal{T}[u\vee v]\right)\rvert,

where
u\vee v
is the lowest common ancestor of
u
and
v
in
\mathcal{T}
. Sometimes, it is convenient to consider the cost of an edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E
in
\mathcal{T}
as
\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)\triangleq w_{e}\cdot\lvert\mathsf{leaves}(\mathcal{T}[u\vee v])\rvert
.
Trees that achieve a better hierarchical clustering have a lower cost, and the objective of
HC
is to construct trees with the lowest cost based on the following consideration: for any pair of vertices
u,v\in V
that corresponds to an edge of high weight
w_{uv}
(i.e.,
u
and
v
are highly similar), a “good”
HC
tree would separate
u
and
v
lower in the tree, thus reflected in a small size of
|\mathsf{leaves}(\mathcal{T}[u\vee v])|
.
We denote by
\mathsf{OPT}_{G}
the minimum cost of any
HC
tree of
G
, i.e.,
\mathsf{OPT}_{G}=\min_{\mathcal{T}}\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T})
, and use the notation
\mathcal{T}^{*}
to refer to an
optimal
tree achieving the minimum cost. We say that an
HC
tree
\mathcal{T}
is an
\alpha
-approximate
tree if
\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T})\leqslant\alpha\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}
for some
\alpha\geqslant 1
.

3.3
Contracted Graphs

Our work is based on
contracted

[
KKLM22
]
in the context of hierarchical clustering.

Definition 3.1

(Contracted Graph,
[
KKLM22
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph, and
\mathcal{A}=\{A\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a partition of
V
.
We say that the vertex and edge-weighted graph
H=([k],\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{2}{[k]}{2},W^{*},w^{*})
is a contraction of
G
with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if for every
i,j\in[k]
we have that
W^{*}(i,j)=w(A_{i},A_{j})
and for every
i\in[k]
we have
w^{*}(i)=|A_{i}|
.
We denote the contraction of
G
with respect to
\mathcal{A}
as
G/\mathcal{A}
.

Note that contracted graphs are
vertex-weighted
, i.e., every vertex
u\in V(H)
has a corresponding weight. To measure the quality of an
HC
tree
\mathcal{T}
on a vertex-weighted graph
H=(V,E,W,w)
, we define the
weighted Dasgupta’s cost
of
\mathcal{T}
on
H
as

\mathsf{WCOST}_{H}(\mathcal{T})\triangleq\sum_{e=\{u,v\}\in E}W(u,v)\sum_{z\in\mathsf{leaves}\left(\mathcal{T}[u\vee v]\right)}w(z).

We denote by
\mathsf{WOPT}_{H}
the minimum cost of any
HC
tree of
H
, i.e.,
\mathsf{WOPT}_{H}=\min_{\mathcal{T}}\mathsf{WCOST}_{H}(\mathcal{T})
.

For any set
S\subset V(H)
we define the sparsity of the cut
(S,V\setminus S)
in
H
as

\mathsf{sparsity}_{H}(S)\triangleq\frac{W(S,V\setminus S)}{w(S)\cdot w(V\setminus S)},

(3.2)

where
w(S)\triangleq\sum_{v\in S}w(v)
. The vertex-weighted sparsest cut of
G
is the cut with the minimum sparsity.

We call the vertex-weighted variant of the recursive sparsest cut algorithm the
WRSC
algorithm, which is described as follows:
Let
\alpha\geqslant 1
, and
H=(V,E,W,w)
be a vertex and edge-weighted graph. Let
(S,V\setminus S)
be a vertex-weighted sparsest cut of
H
. The
WRSC
algorithm on
H
is a recursive algorithm that finds a cut
(T,V\setminus T)
satisfying
\mathsf{sparsity}_{H}(T)\leqslant\alpha\cdot\mathsf{sparsity}_{H}(S)
, and recurs on the induced subgraphs
H[T]
and
H[V\setminus T]
. Kapralov et al.
[
KKLM22
]
showed that the approximation guarantee of this algorithm for constructing
HC
trees follows from the one for non-vertex-weighted graphs
[
CC17
]
, and their result is summarised as follows:

Lemma 3.2

(
[
KKLM22
]
)
.

Let
H=(V,E,W,w)
be a vertex and edge-weighted graph. Then, the
WRSC
algorithm achieves an
O(\alpha)
-approximation for the weighted Dasgupta’s cost of
H
, where
\alpha
is the approximation ratio of the sparsest cut algorithm used in
WRSC
.

We emphasise that we only use the
combinatorial
properties of vertex-weighted graphs. As such we don’t consider their Laplacian matrices and the corresponding spectra.

3.4
Spectral Clustering

Another key component used in our analysis is spectral clustering, which is one of the most popular clustering algorithms used in practice
[
NJW01
,
ST96
]
.
For any input graph
G=(V,E,w)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, spectral clustering consists of the following three steps: (1) compute the eigenvectors
f_{1}\ldots f_{k}
of
\mathcal{L}_{G}
, and embed each
u\in V(G)
to the point
F(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
based on
f_{1}\ldots f_{k}
;
(2) apply
k
-means on the embedded points
\{F(u)\}_{u\in V(G)}
; (3) partition
V
into
k
clusters
P_{1}\ldots P_{k}
based on the output of
k
-means.

To analyse the performance of spectral clustering, one can examine the scenario in which there is a large gap between
\lambda_{k+1}
and
\rho(k)
. By the higher-order Cheeger inequality (
3.1
), we know that a low value of
\rho(k)
ensures that the vertex set
V
of
G
can be partitioned into
k
subsets (clusters), each of which has conductance upper bounded by
\rho(k)
; on the other hand, a high value of
\lambda_{k+1}
implies that any
(k+1)
-way partition of
V
would introduce some
A\subset V
with conductance
\Phi_{G}(A)\geqslant\rho(k+1)\geqslant\lambda_{k+1}/2
.

Based on this observation, a sequence of works
[
Miz21
,
MS22
,
PSZ17
]
showed that, assuming the presence of a large gap between
\lambda_{k+1}
and
\rho(k)
,
spectral clustering returns clusters
P_{1},\ldots P_{k}
of low outer conductance
\Phi_{G}(P_{i})
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
. We remark that spectral clustering can be implemented in nearly-linear time
[
PSZ17
]
.

4
Hierarchical Clustering for Well-Clustered Graphs: Previous Approach

Our presented new algorithms are based on the work of Manghiuc and Sun
[
MS21
]
on the same problem, and this section gives a brief overview of their approach. We consider a graph
G=(V,E,w)
to have
k
well-defined clusters if
V(G)
can be partitioned into disjoint subsets
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
such that (i) there’s a sparse cut between
A_{i}
and
V\setminus A_{i}
, formulated as
\Phi_{G}(A_{i})\leqslant\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
, and (ii) each
G[A_{i}]
has high inner conductance
\Phi_{G[A_{i}]}\geqslant\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}
. Then, for an input graph
G
with a clear cluster-structure, the algorithm by
[
MS21
]
, which we call the
MS
algorithm in the following, constructs an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree of
G
in polynomial-time. At a very high level, the
MS
algorithm is based on the following two components:

•

They first show that, when an input graph
G
of
n
vertices and
m
edges has high conductance, an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree of
G
can be constructed based on the degree sequence of
G
, and the algorithm runs in
O(m+n\log n)
time. We use
\mathbb{T}_{\mathrm{deg}}
to express such trees constructed from the degree sequence of
G
.

•

They combine the first result with the algorithm proposed in
[
OGT14
]
, which decomposes an input graph into a set of clusters
A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}
, where every
A_{i}
has low outer-conductance
\Phi_{G}(A_{i})
and high inner-conductance
\Phi_{G[A_{i}]}
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
.

With these two components, one might intuitively think that an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree of
G
can be easily constructed by (i) finding clusters
\{A_{i}\}
of high conductance, (ii) constructing an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree
\mathbb{T}_{i}=\mathbb{T}_{\mathrm{deg}}(G[A_{i}])
for every
G[A_{i}]
, and (iii) merging the constructed
\{\mathbb{T}_{i}\}
in an “optimal” way. However, Manghiuc and Sun
[
MS21
]
show by counterexample that this is not sufficient and, in order to achieve an
O(1)
-approximation, further decomposition of every
\{A_{i}\}
would be necessary. Specifically, they adjust the algorithm of
[
OGT14
]
,
and further decompose every vertex set
A_{i}
of high-conductance into smaller subsets, which they call
critical nodes
. They show that these critical nodes can be
carefully merged to construct an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree for well-clustered graphs. On the downside, as the
MS
algorithm is heavily based
[
OGT14
]
,
the time complexity of their algorithm is
\widetilde{O}\left(k^{3}m^{2}n\cdot\left(w_{\max}/w_{\min}\right)\right)
, which limits the application of their algorithm on large-scale datasets.

5
Algorithms

This section presents our hierarchical clustering algorithms for well-clustered graphs. It consists of two subsections, each of which corresponds to one algorithm.

5.1
The First Algorithm

In this subsection we present a nearly-linear time algorithm that, given a well-clustered graph
G
with a constant number of clusters as input, constructs an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree of
G
with respect to Dasgupta’s cost. Our result is as follows:

Theorem 5.1
.

Given a connected graph
G=(V,E,w)
and some constant
k
as input such that
\lambda_{k+1}=\Omega(1)
,
\lambda_{k}=O(k^{-12})
, and

w_{\mathrm{max}}/w_{\mathrm{min}}=O(n^{\gamma})
for a constant
\gamma>1
, there is a nearly-linear time algorithm that constructs an
HC
tree
\mathcal{T}
of

G
satisfying
\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T})=O\left(1\right)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}
.

In comparison to the previous algorithms for hierarchical clustering on well-structured graphs
[
CAKMT17
,
MS21
]
, the advantages of our algorithm are its simplicity and nearly-linear time complexity, which is optimal up to a poly-logarithmic factor.

5.1.1
Overview of the Algorithm

We first describe the algorithm behind Theorem
5.1
.
Our algorithm consists of four steps. For any input graph
G=(V,E,w)
and parameter
k
, our algorithm first runs spectral clustering and obtains clusters
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
.

The second step of our algorithm is a degree-based bucketing procedure. We set
\beta\triangleq 2^{k(\gamma+1)}
, and define for any
P_{i}
returned from spectral clustering and
u\in P_{i}
the set

B\left(u\right)\triangleq\left\{v\in P_{i}:d_{u}\leqslant d_{v}<\beta\cdot d_{u}\right\}.

(5.1)

Moreover, as
B(u)
consists of all the vertices
v\in P_{i}
with
d_{u}\leqslant d_{v}<\beta\cdot d_{u}
, we generalise the definition of
B(u)
and define for any
j\in\mathbb{Z}
that

B^{j}(u)=\left\{v\in P_{i}:\beta^{j}\cdot d_{u}\leqslant d_{v}<\beta^{j+1}\cdot d_{u}\right\}.

By definition, it holds that
B(u)=B^{0}(u)
, and
\{B^{j}(u)\}_{j}
forms a partition of

5.1.2
Proof Sketch of Theorem
5.1

We first analyse the approximation ratio of our constructed tree, and show why these simple four steps suffice to construct an
O(1)
-approximate
HC
tree for well-clustered graphs. By the algorithm description, our constructed
\mathcal{T}
is based on merging different
\mathcal{T}_{B}
, and it holds that

\displaystyle\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathsf{COST}_{G\left[B\right]}\left(\mathcal{T}_{B}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}\\
B\neq B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e=\{u,v\}\\
u\in B,v\in B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e).

(5.2)

The first step of our analysis is to upper bound the total contribution of the internal edges of all the buckets, and
our result is as follows:

Lemma 5.2
.

It holds that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathsf{COST}_{G[B]}\left(\mathcal{T}_{B}\right)=O\left(\beta\cdot k^{23}/\lambda_{k+1}^{10}\right)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

By Lemma
5.2
, the total cost induced from the edges inside every bucket can be
directly
used when constructing the final tree
\mathcal{T}
. This is crucial for our analysis since our defined buckets can be constructed in nearly-linear time. In comparison to this step, the
MS
algorithm relies on finding critical nodes, which is computationally much more expensive.

Next, we analyse the second term of (
5.2
). For ease of presentation, let
\widetilde{E}
be the set of edges crossing different buckets, and
\mathcal{T}_{G/\mathcal{B}}
the tree returned from the
WRSC
algorithm; remember that every leaf of
\mathcal{T}_{G/\mathcal{B}}
corresponds to a vertex set in
\mathcal{B}
. By construction, we know that the weight of
every edge
e\in\widetilde{E}
contributes to the edge weight in
G/\mathcal{B}
, and it holds that

\sum_{e\in\widetilde{E}}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)=\mathsf{WCOST}_{G/\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{T}_{G/\mathcal{B}}).

(5.3)

Moreover, since
\mathcal{T}_{G\setminus\mathcal{B}}
is constructed by performing the
WRSC
algorithm on
G\setminus\mathcal{B}
, we have by Lemma
3.2
that

\mathsf{WCOST}_{G/\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{T}_{G/\mathcal{B}})=O\left(\alpha\right)\cdot\mathsf{WOPT}_{G/\mathcal{B}},

(5.4)

where
\alpha
is the approximation ratio achieved by the
WRSC
algorithm.

Our next lemma is the key to the overall analysis, and upper bounds
\mathsf{WOPT}_{G/\mathcal{B}}
with respect to
\mathsf{OPT}_{G}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

It holds that

\mathsf{WOPT}_{G/\mathcal{B}}=O\left(\beta\cdot k^{23}/\lambda_{k+1}^{10}\right)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

Combining (
5.3
), (
5.4
) with Lemma
5.3
, we have

\sum_{e\in\widetilde{E}}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)=O\left(\alpha\cdot\beta\cdot k^{23}/\lambda_{k+1}^{10}\right)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

(5.5)

Next, we study
\alpha
, which is the only term in the approximation ratio of (
5.5
) that is not necessarily a constant. Recall that our choice of
\gamma
and
\beta
satisfies

\frac{w_{\max}}{w_{\min}}=O(n^{\gamma})

and
\beta=2^{k(\gamma+1)}
. Hence, it holds that

\frac{\Delta_{G}}{\delta_{G}}=O\left(n^{\gamma+1}\right),

and the total number of buckets in
\mathcal{B}
is upper bounded by

k\cdot\max\{1,\log_{\beta}n^{\gamma+1}\}\leqslant k+\frac{k(\gamma+1)}{\log\beta}\cdot\log n=k+\log n.

Thanks to this, there are at most
k+\log n
vertices in
G/\mathcal{B}
, and a sparsest cut of
G/\mathcal{B}
can be found in
O(n)
time by enumerating all of its possible subsets; as such we can set
\alpha=1
in the analysis. We highlight that this is another advantage of our bucketing step: with careful choice of parameters, we only need to study a contracted graph with
O(\log n)
vertices, whose sparsest cut can be found in linear time.
Since the
WRSC
algorithm only computes the vertex-weighted sparsest cut
O(\log n)
times, the overall running time of
WRSC
is
O(n\cdot\log n)
.
We remark that
we don’t need to deal with the general sparsest cut problem,
for which most approximation algorithms are based on complicated optimisation techniques (e.g.,
[
ARV09
]
).

Combining (
5.2
) with Lemmas
5.2
,
5.3
as well as the fact that
\alpha=1
proves the approximation guarantee of Theorem
5.1
.

Next, we sketch the running time analysis of Algorithm
1
. The first step (Line
1
) applies spectral clustering, and takes
\widetilde{O}(m)
time
[
PSZ17
]
. The second step (Lines

–

\sum_{i=1}^{k}O\left(|P_{i}|\log|P_{i}|\right)=O(n\log n).

The third step (Lines
1
–
1

constructs an arbitrary binary tree
\mathcal{T}_{B}
for every bucket
B\in\mathcal{B}

The actual construction of the trees in this step isn’t difficult, but we need to store several attributes for each internal node as we build the tree, such that we can compute Dasgupta’s cost in nearly-linear time as well. We refer the reader to the appendix for the detailed discussion of this step. In the last step (Lines

5.1.3
Proof Sketch of Lemma
5.2
and Lemma
5.3

Our key approach to breaking the running time barrier of
[
MS21
]
is that, instead of approximating the optimal tree
\mathcal{T}^{*}
, we approximate the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\textsf{MS}}
constructed by the algorithm of
[
MS21
]
. We know from their result that one can upper bound the cost of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}
with respect to
\mathsf{OPT}_{G}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}})=O\left(k^{22}/\lambda_{k+1}^{10}\right)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

We take the existence of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}
for granted, and perform two transformations to construct a tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime}
, while ensuring that the total introduced cost from the two transformations can be upper bounded. In the first step, we identify how every bucket
B\in\mathcal{B}
is spread throughout
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}
, and make sure that all the separate components that make up
B
are isolated in our new tree. We call the resulting tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime}
, and prove that

\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime}\right)\leqslant\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}\right)+O(k^{21}/\lambda_{k+1}^{10})\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

For the second transformation, we carefully adjust the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime}
, such that the currently isolated components that make up every bucket
B\in\mathcal{B}
get grouped together into the same subtree. We call the resulting tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime}
, and bound its cost by

\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\leqslant\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime}\right)+O(\beta\cdot k^{23}/\lambda_{k+1}^{10})\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

Taking these transformations together, we get that

\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\leqslant O(\beta\cdot k^{23}/\lambda_{k+1}^{10})\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

(5.6)

Notice that we can perform these two transformations without an explicit construction of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}
, but we still end up with a tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime}
with bounded Dasgupta cost. Moreover, since every bucket
B\in\mathcal{B}
in
\mathcal{T}_{\textsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime}
is separated,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime}
is also a tree on the contracted graph
G/\mathcal{B}
; this allows us to upper bound
\mathsf{WOPT}_{G/\mathcal{B}}
with
\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{MS}}^{\prime\prime})
. Combining this with (
5.6
) proves Lemma
5.3
.
With the same approach, we prove that
Lemma
5.2

holds as well.

We remark that this is another conceptual contribution of our paper: although
[
MS21
]
shows that a similar proof technique can be generalised to construct
O(1)
-approximate
HC
trees for
expander
graphs, we show that such proof technique can be used to obtain
O(1)
-approximate
HC
trees for
well-clustered
graphs.

5.2
The Second Algorithm

In this subsection, we present another nearly-linear time hierarchical clustering algorithm for well-clustered graphs. Here, we assume that the degrees of vertices inside every cluster are
almost balanced
, i.e., it holds for an optimal partitioning
S_{1},\ldots S_{k}
corresponding to
\rho(k)
that the degrees inside each
S_{i}
are upper bounded by a parameter
\eta_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. With this condition, our second algorithm achieves the same approximation guarantee as Algorithm
1
even with
k=O(\log^{c}n)
for some constant
c
. This result is summarised as follows:

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a graph with
k
clusters
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
such that
\lambda_{k+1}=\Omega(1)
,
\max_{i}(\Delta_{G}(S_{i})/\delta_{G}(S_{i}))\leqslant\eta_{S}
,
\rho(k)\leqslant k^{-4}
, and
\Phi_{G[S_{i}]}\geqslant\Omega(1)
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
. Then, there is a nearly-linear time algorithm that constructs an
HC
tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
of

G
satisfying
\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}})=O\left(1\right)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}
.

The algorithm behind Theorem
5.4
is similar with Algorithm
1
, and is described in Algorithm
2

1
.

1.

We adjust the bucketing step by setting the bucketing parameter

\eta_{S}
is an upper bound for
\max_{i}\left(\Delta_{G}(S_{i})/\delta_{G}(S_{i})\right)

u^{(1)}
of minimum degree inside

We first examine the upper and lower bounds of the cost of

Fact A.1
.

It holds for any

\mathcal{T}
of

\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T})\leqslant\frac{n\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,G\,\!\right)}{2}.

Secondly, it is known that
\mathsf{OPT}_{G}
can be lower bounded with respect to the degree distribution of

Lemma A.1

(
[

]
)

It holds for any graph

\mathsf{OPT}_{G}\geqslant\frac{2\Phi_{G}}{9}\cdot\max\left\{\frac{\mathrm{vol}(G)^{2}}{\Delta_{G}},\delta_{G}\cdot n^{2}\right\}=\frac{2\Phi_{G}}{9}\cdot n\cdot\mathrm{vol}(G)\cdot\max\left\{\frac{d_{G}}{\Delta_{G}},\frac{\delta_{G}}{d_{G}}\right\}.

Next, we introduce the notion of a

Definition A.2

(Dense branch,
[
MS21
]
)

Given a graph

It is important to note that the dense branch of

\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T})\geqslant\frac{\Phi_{G}}{2}\cdot|A_{k}|\cdot\mathrm{vol}(A_{k})

A.2
Spectral Clustering

Another component used in our analysis is spectral clustering. To analyse the theoretical performance of spectral clustering, one can examine the scenario in which there is a large gap between
\lambda_{k+1}
and
\rho(k)
. By the higher-order Cheeger inequality (
3.1
), we know that a low value of
\rho(k)
ensures that the vertex set
V
of
G
can be partitioned into
k
subsets (clusters), each of which has conductance upper bounded by
\rho(k)
; on the other hand, a large value of
\lambda_{k+1}
implies that any
(k+1)
-th partition of

would introduce some

with conductance
\Phi_{G}(A)\geqslant\rho(k+1)\geqslant\lambda_{k+1}/2
.
Based on this, Peng et al.
[
PSZ17
]
introduced the parameter

\Upsilon(k)\triangleq\frac{\lambda_{k+1}}{\rho(k)}

(A.1)

and showed that a large value of
\Upsilon(k)
is sufficient to guarantee a good performance of spectral clustering. The result of
[
PSZ17
]
has been improved by a sequence of works, and the following result, which can be shown easily by combining the proof technique of
[
PSZ17
]
and
[
MS22
]
, will be used in our analysis.

Lemma A.9
.

There is an absolute constant
C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, such that the following holds:
Let
G
be a graph with
k
optimal clusters
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, and
\Upsilon(k)\geqslant C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}\cdot k
. Let
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be the output of spectral clustering and, without loss of generality, the optimal correspondence of
P_{i}
is
S_{i}
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
. Then, it holds for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
that

\mathrm{vol}(P_{i}\triangle S_{i})\leqslant\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}(S_{i}),

where
A\triangle B
for any sets
A
and
B
is defined by
A\triangle B\triangleq(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.
Moreover, these
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
can be computed in nearly-linear time.

A.3
Strong Decomposition Lemma

The objective of this subsection is to prove the following decomposition lemma. The lemma shows that, under a certain eigen-gap condition, an input graph

can be partitioned into clusters with bounded inner and outer conductance, and certain constraints on cut values. We remark that, while obtaining the partition promised by the lemma below requires high time complexity, we only need the existence of such partition in our analysis.

Lemma A.10

(Improved Strong Decomposition Lemma)
.

Let

(A2)

e=\{u,v\}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)=n\cdot w(S_{1},V\setminus S_{1})\leqslant n\cdot\mathrm{vol}(S_{1})\cdot\rho(k)\leqslant k\cdot|S_{1}|\cdot\rho(k)\cdot\mathrm{vol}(S_{1}).

We continue with looking at the edges between
S_{2}
and
V\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
. As
S_{2}
is the largest cluster among
S_{2},\ldots,S_{k}
, it holds that
|S_{2}|\geqslant\frac{n-|S_{1}|}{k-1}
, which implies that
n-|S_{1}|<k\cdot|S_{2}|
. Notice that any edge
e=\{u,v\}
with
u\in S_{2}
and
v\in V\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
satisfies that
\lvert\mathsf{leaves}\left(\mathcal{T}_{S}[u\vee v]\right)\rvert=n-|S_{1}|
, and as such we have that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S_{2},v\not\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}\\
e=\{u,v\}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)\leqslant(n-|S_{1}|)\cdot w(S_{2},V\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2}))\leqslant k\cdot|S_{2}|\cdot\rho(k)\cdot\mathrm{vol}(S_{2}).

Generalising the analysis above, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\widetilde{E}}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S_{i},v\in S_{j}\\
e=\{u,v\}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)

\displaystyle\leqslant k\cdot\rho(k)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\cdot\mathrm{vol}(S_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{18\cdot\eta_{S}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{OPT}_{G[S_{i}]},

(C.3)

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
A.8
.
Combining (
C.1
), (
C.2
) with (
C.3
), we have that

\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{S})\leqslant\frac{36\cdot\eta_{S}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G},

which proves the statement.
∎

C.2
Algorithm Description

Now informally describe our algorithm and refer the reader to Algorithm
2
for the formal presentation.
The algorithm takes as input a graph
G=(V,E,w)
with
k
optimal clusters
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
.
For simplicity, we assume that the algorithm knows the target number of clusters
k
and an upper bound
\eta_{S}
such that

\eta_{S}\geqslant\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\frac{\Delta(S_{i})}{\delta(S_{i})},

and we carefully deal with this assumption at the end of the section.
As the first step, the algorithm runs the
\mathsf{SpectralClustering}
subroutine and obtains the approximate clusters
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
.
Following our previous discussion, it is unclear whether the algorithm can or cannot use the clusters
P_{i}
directly in order to construct an
O(1)
-approximate tree.
To overcome this difficulty, we further partition every
P_{i}
into degree-based buckets, but rather than setting
\beta=2^{k(\gamma+1)}

1

\beta=\eta_{S}
. Recall then for any set
P_{i}
and every vertex
u\in P_{i}
, the
bucket
of
P_{i}
starting at
u
is defined

B\left(u\right)=\left\{v\in P_{i}:d_{u}\leqslant d_{v}<\eta_{S}\cdot d_{u}\right\}.

Again, it is important to note that the bucket
B(u)
contains
every
vertex
v\in P_{i}
whose degree satisfies
d_{u}\leqslant d_{v}<\eta_{S}\cdot d_{u}
.

6
(a) for an illustration of the bucketing procedure.

).
To motivate this choice, notice that every cluster
P_{i}

S_{i}

\mathcal{T}_{B_{j}}

, and adds the tree
\mathcal{T}_{B_{j}}
to a global set of trees
\mathbb{T}
.
This process is repeated for every cluster
P_{i}
.
As a final step, the algorithm merges the trees inside
\mathbb{T}
in a caterpillar fashion in decreasing order of the sizes, placing the larger sized trees closer to the root (Lines
2
-
2

2
).
The resulting tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
is the output of the algorithm.

C.3
Algorithm Analysis

This subsection analyses the performance of Algorithm
2
, and proves Theorem
5.4
. It is organised as follows: in the first part we prove several properties of our carefully constructed bucketing procedure.
In the second part, we prove that the cost of our constructed tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
achieves the claimed approximation guarantee.
Thirdly, we give a detailed description of how we can efficiently construct the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
and compute its cost in nearly-linear time.
The proof of Theorem
5.4
follows by combining the three main results.

We first prove that our bucketing procedure induces at most
2k
buckets inside every cluster
P_{i}
, and the total number of buckets throughout all clusters
P_{i}
is at most
2k^{2}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
P_{i}
be an arbitrary cluster, and
u^{*}_{i}=\operatorname{argmax}_{u\in P_{i}}\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u\right)\,\!\right)
be a vertex inducing the bucketing
\mathcal{B}_{u_{i}^{*}}
. The following statements hold:

1.

B\left(u_{i}^{*}\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{u_{i}^{*}}
;

2.

For every optimal cluster
S_{j}
, the vertices in
S_{j}\cap P_{i}
belong to at most two buckets, i.e.,

\left\lvert\left\{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{u_{i}^{*}}:S_{j}\cap B_{t}\neq\emptyset\right\}\right\rvert\leqslant 2;

3.

The bucketing induced by
u_{i}^{*}
contains at most
2k
buckets

\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{u_{i}^{*}}\right\rvert\leqslant 2k
.

Proof.

The first property follows trivially from the definition. The second property follows from our assumption that
\Delta(S_{i})\leqslant\eta_{S}\cdot\delta(S_{i})
for every optimal cluster
S_{i}
.
Therefore, the vertices inside
S_{i}
belong to at most
2
buckets.
The third property follows from the second one and summing over all clusters.
∎

Our second result presents a very important property of our bucketing procedure.
We show that, in every cluster
P_{i}
, exactly one bucket
B_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{u_{i}^{*}}
has large overlap with the optimal cluster
S_{i}
, and all the other buckets have low volume.
We emphasise that this result is only possible due to our calibration technique of choosing the bucketing induced by a vertex
u_{i}^{*}\in P_{i}
, whose bucket
B\left(u_{i}^{*}\right)
has the highest volume.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
P_{i}
be an arbitrary cluster, and
u^{*}_{i}=\operatorname{argmax}_{u\in P_{i}}\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u\right)\,\!\right)
be a vertex inducing the bucketing
\mathcal{B}_{u_{i}^{*}}
.
The following two statements hold:

1.

There exists a unique
heavy
bucket
B_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{u^{*}_{i}}
such that
\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\geqslant\left(1-\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)
;

2.

For every other bucket
B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{u^{*}_{i}}\setminus\{B_{j}\}
it holds that
\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\,\!\right)\leqslant\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)
.

Proof.

For the first statement, we show that the bucket
B(u^{*}_{i})
satisfies the claim.
Let
u_{\min}
be a vertex in
P_{i}\cap S_{i}
of the lowest degree.
Since the degrees of all vertices in
S_{i}
(and hence in
P_{i}\cap S_{i}
) are within a factor of
\eta_{S}
from each other, we know that all vertices in
P_{i}\cap S_{i}
must be in the bucket
B\left(u_{\min}\right)
, i.e.,
P_{i}\cap S_{i}\subseteq B\left(u_{\min}\right)
.
Moreover, by the choice of
u^{*}_{i}
we have that

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u^{*}_{i}\right)\,\!\right)\geqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u_{\min}\right)\,\!\right)\geqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\geqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)-\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\triangle S_{i}\,\!\right)\geqslant\left(1-\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{vol}(S_{i}),

(C.4)

where the last inequality follows by Lemma
A.9
.
We now write

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u_{i}^{*}\right)\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u^{*}_{i}\right)\,\!\right)-\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u^{*}_{i}\right)\setminus S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)-\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\setminus S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right),

where the first inequality uses (
C.4
).
The uniqueness of the heavy bucket follows by noticing that
\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B(u_{i}^{*})\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)>\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)/2
.

The second statement essentially follows from the fact that
S_{i}
has a large volume overlap with
B\left(u_{i}^{*}\right)
.
Concretely, let
B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{u_{i}^{*}}\setminus\left\{B\left(u_{i}^{*}\right)\right\}
be an arbitrary bucket.
We have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)+\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\setminus S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)-\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\setminus B_{t}\,\!\right)+\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\setminus S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)-\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\cap B\left(u_{i}^{*}\right)\,\!\right)+\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\triangle S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)+\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

In order to analyse the final constructed tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
, we analyse the properties of the buckets in the context of the entire graph
G
, and not just on every set
P_{i}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}
the set containing all the buckets obtained throughout all sets
P_{i}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{B}\triangleq\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{B}_{u^{*}_{i}},

where
u^{*}_{i}=\operatorname{argmax}_{u\in P_{i}}\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B\left(u\right)\,\!\right)
is a vertex inducing the corresponding bucketing in set
P_{i}
.
We remark that
\mathcal{B}
is a partition of
V
.
We use

\ell\triangleq|\mathcal{B}|

to denote the total number of buckets, and we know by Lemma
C.2
that
\ell\leqslant 2k^{2}
.
For convenience, we label the buckets
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}\}
in decreasing order of the sizes breaking ties arbitrarily, i.e.,
|B_{i}|\geqslant|B_{i+1}|
, for all
i<\ell
.

Based on Lemma
C.3
, exactly
k
buckets in
\mathcal{B}
are heavy, meaning that they have large overlap with the optimal clusters
S_{i}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}
the set of those
k
buckets; we emphasise once more that each heavy bucket corresponds to a unique cluster
S_{i}
and vice-versa.
Additionally, we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}\triangleq\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}
the set of the remaining
light
buckets.

The next two results summarise the main properties of our constructed buckets.
We first present three properties of the heavy buckets which bound their size, volume and weight of the crossing edges.

Lemma C.4
.

For every heavy bucket
B_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}
corresponding to
S_{i}
, the following statements hold:

(B1)

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\,\!\right)\leqslant\left(1+\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)
;

(B2)

|B_{j}|\leqslant\left(1+\eta_{S}\right)\cdot|S_{i}|
.

(B3)

w(B_{j},\overline{B_{j}})\leqslant\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}+\lambda_{k+1}}{\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)
.

Proof.

Let
B_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}
be a heavy bucket corresponding to
S_{i}
.
For (B1), we have that

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\,\!\right)=\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)+\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\setminus S_{i}\,\!\right)\leqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)+\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\triangle S_{i}\,\!\right)\leqslant\left(1+\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right).

To prove (B2), suppose for contradiction that
|B_{j}|>\left(1+\eta_{S}\right)\cdot|S_{i}|
, which implies that
|B_{j}\setminus S_{i}|=|B_{j}|-|B_{j}\cap S_{i}|>\eta_{S}\cdot|B_{j}\cap S_{i}|
.
Based on this, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\setminus S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant|B_{j}\setminus S_{i}|\cdot\delta(B_{j}\setminus S_{i})>\eta_{S}\cdot|B_{j}\cap S_{i}|\cdot\frac{\Delta(B_{j}\cap S_{i})}{\eta_{S}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\geqslant\left(1-\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right),

where the second inequality follows from the fact that, inside bucket
B_{j}
all the degrees are within a factor of
\eta_{S}
of each other and the last inequality follows by Lemma
C.3
.
This implies that

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)<\frac{1}{1-\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\triangle S_{i}\,\!\right)\leqslant\frac{\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)}{\frac{3\Upsilon(k)}{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}-2}\leqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right),

where the second inequality follows by Lemma
A.9
, and last inequality by our assumption on
\Upsilon(k)
.

Finally, we prove (B3). We have that

\displaystyle w(B_{j},\overline{B_{j}})

\displaystyle\leqslant w(B_{j}\cap S_{i},\overline{B_{j}})+w(B_{j}\setminus S_{i},\overline{B_{j}})

\displaystyle\leqslant w(B_{j}\cap S_{i},\overline{B_{j}}\cap S_{i})+w(B_{j}\cap S_{i},\overline{B_{j}}\setminus S_{i})+\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\setminus S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,\overline{B_{j}}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)+w(S_{i},\overline{S_{i}})+\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\triangle S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)+\rho(k)\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)+\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)

(C.5)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}+\lambda_{k+1}}{\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right),

(C.6)

where (
C.5
) follows from Lemmas
C.3
and
A.9
, and (
C.6
) uses the fact that
\rho(k)=\frac{\lambda_{k+1}}{\Upsilon(k)}
.
∎

The final technical result is similar with Lemma
C.4
, and presents several useful upper bounds of the volume and size of the light buckets.

Lemma C.5
.

For every
light
bucket
B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}
, the following statements hold:

(B4)

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)\leqslant\eta_{S}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)
, for all clusters
S_{i},S_{j}
satisfying
|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\leqslant|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|
;

(B5)

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\leqslant\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)
, for all clusters
S_{i}
;

(B6)

\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}|B_{t}|\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\,\!\right)\leqslant\frac{4k^{2}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}\cdot\eta_{S}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)
.

Proof.

Let
B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}
be a light bucket, and let
S_{i},S_{j}
be optimal clusters so that
|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\leqslant|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|
. It holds that

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)\leqslant|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\cdot\Delta(B_{t}\cap S_{j})\leqslant|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\cdot\eta_{S}\cdot\delta(B_{t}\cap S_{i})\leqslant\eta_{S}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right),

where the second inequality uses the fact that inside bucket
B_{t}
all the degrees are within a factor of
\eta_{S}
. This proves (B4).

Next, we prove (B5). Suppose that
B_{t}
is a bucket from some cluster
P_{r}
, and let
S_{i}
be an arbitrary cluster. The proof continues by a case distinction:

Case
1
:
r=i
.

In this case, since
B_{t}
is a light bucket in
P_{r}
, by Lemma
C.3
we have that

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)=\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{r}\,\!\right)\leqslant\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{r}\,\!\right).

Case
2
:
r\neq i
.

In this case we simply have that

\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\leqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{r}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\leqslant\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,P_{i}\triangle S_{i}\,\!\right)\leqslant\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right).

Combining the two cases proves (B5).

We finally prove (B6). We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}|B_{t}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1:\\
|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\leqslant|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\end{subarray}}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1:\\
|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|>|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\end{subarray}}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)\right)

(C.7)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1:\\
|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\leqslant|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\end{subarray}}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1:\\
|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|>|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\end{subarray}}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\cdot\eta_{S}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\right)

(C.8)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(k\sum_{j=1}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{j}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)+k\sum_{i=1}^{k}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\cdot\eta_{S}\cdot\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2k\cdot\eta_{S}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2k\cdot\eta_{S}\cdot\frac{2k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)

(C.9)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4k^{2}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}\cdot\eta_{S}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right),

where (
C.7
) follows by grouping the terms
|B_{t}\cap S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\cap S_{j}\,\!\right)
into two categories, (
C.8
) follows by (B4), and (
C.9
) follows by (B5).
∎

Now we combine the properties shown earlier, and upper bound the cost of
our constructed tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
through the following lemma.

Lemma C.6
.

It holds that

\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}\right)\leqslant\left(1+\frac{5k^{4}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\frac{36\cdot\eta_{S}^{2}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}.

Proof.

By construction, we know that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
is the caterpillar tree formed by merging the trees
\mathcal{T}_{B_{j}}
, for all
B_{j}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Therefore, in order to bound the overall cost it suffices to bound the cost within each induced subtree
\mathcal{T}_{B_{j}}
as well as the cost of the edges crossing different buckets.
Formally, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathsf{COST}_{G[B_{j}]}\left(\mathcal{T}_{B_{j}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{t=j+1}^{\ell}\sum_{e\in E(B_{j},B_{t})}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}|B_{j}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\,\!\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{t=j+1}^{\ell}\sum_{e\in E(B_{j},B_{t})}\mathsf{cost}_{G}(e).

(C.10)

W study the first term of (
C.10
), and have that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}|B_{j}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{B_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}}|B_{j}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{j}\,\!\right)+\sum_{B_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{L}}}|B_{t}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,B_{t}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1+\eta_{S}\right)\left(1+\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)\right)+\frac{4k^{2}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}\cdot\eta_{S}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)\right)

(C.11)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(2\eta_{S}\cdot\left(1+\frac{k\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)+\frac{4k^{2}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}\cdot\eta_{S}}{3\Upsilon(k)}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\mathrm{vol}\!\left(\!\,S_{i}\,\!\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(2\eta_{S}+\frac{2k^{2}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}\cdot\eta_{S}}{\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\frac{18\cdot\eta_{S}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}

(C.12)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{k^{2}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\frac{36\cdot\eta_{S}^{2}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}

where (
C.11
) follows from (B1), (B2), (B6), and (

) follows by Lemma
A.8
.

Next we look at the second term of (

).
Let
B_{j}\in\mathcal{B}

B_{j}

.

Theorem C.7

(Formal Statement of Theorem
5.4
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
graph of
n
vertices,
m
edges, and optimal clusters
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
corresponding to
\rho(k)
satisfying

,
\Phi_{G[S_{i}]}\geqslant\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}

1\leqslant i\leqslant k
, and

there is an
\widetilde{O}(m)
time algorithm that returns both an
HC

tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
of
G
, and
\mathsf{COST}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}\right)
.
Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathsf{COST}_{G}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}\right)\leqslant\left(1+\frac{5k^{4}\cdot C_{\ref{lem:MS22+}}}{\Upsilon(k)}\right)\cdot\frac{36\cdot\eta_{S}^{2}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{G}

\eta_{S}

\max_{i}\left(\Delta(S_{i})/\delta(S_{i})\right)

Proof.

The approximation guarantee of the returned tree
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}
from Algorithm
2
follows from Lemma
C.6
. By a similar proof as the proof of Lemma
B.9
, it holds that the time complexity of Algorithm
2
is
\widetilde{O}(m)

\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}}

O(\operatorname{polylog}n)
. Therefore, by Lemma
B.8
, we can compute
\mathsf{COST}_{G}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{SCB}})

\widetilde{O}(m)
.

It remains to deal with our assumption that the algorithm knows the number of clusters
k
and an upper bound

\eta_{S}\geqslant\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\frac{\Delta(S_{i})}{\delta(S_{i})}.

If the number of clusters

2

O(\operatorname{polylog}(n))

By adding an extra

As a result, we return the tree with the minimum cost among all different runs of our algorithm.
