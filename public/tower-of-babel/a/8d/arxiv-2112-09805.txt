m m th roots of the identity operator and the geometry conjecture
Stephen Simons

Abstract
In this paper, we give three different new proofs of the validity of the geometry conjecture about cycles of projections onto nonempty closed, convex subsets of a Hilbert space. The first uses a simple minimaxtheorem, which depends on the finite dimensional Hahn-Banach theorem. The second uses Fan’s inequality, which has found many applications inoptimization and mathematical economics. The third uses three results on maximally monotone operators on a Hilbert space.

Introduction

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
m\geq 2
. For
j=1,\dots,m
, let
C_{j}
be a nonempty closed convex subset of
H
and
P_{j}
be the projection of
H
onto
C_{j}
. The element
z=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{m})
of
H^{m}
is said to be a
cycle
if

z_{1}=P_{1}z_{m},\ z_{2}=P_{2}z_{1},\ \dots,\ z_{m}=P_{m}z_{m-1}.

The
geometry conjecture
, which was first formulated in
[
4
, Conjecture 5.1.6]
in 1997, stated that
there exist
v_{1},\dots v_{m}\in H
such that
v_{1}+\cdots v_{m}=0
and

\{z_{m}:(z_{1},\dots z_{m})\hbox{ is a cycle}\}=C_{m}\cap(C_{m-1}+v_{m-1})\cap(C_{1}+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}v_{i}).

(1)

This conjecture was finally solved in the affirmative in
[
3
, Theorem 9, pp. 7–8]
. In this paper, we give a proof of this conjecture which is simpler than that in
[
3
]
, and extends the result to a more general situation. (See Theorem
7
.)

Section
2
contains the algebraic results on which our analysis is based. It discusses certain linear operators on a vector space,
X
, which has no additional structure.

In Section
3
, we assume that
X
is endowed with a Hilbert space structure, and we assume that the operator
R
introduced in Section
2
is an isometry. All the subsequent analysis depends on the disarmingly simple Equations (
5
) and (
6
), which will be used 6 times in what follows. It is important to realize that the Hilbert space
X
is not necessarily the same as the Hilbert space,
H
, discussed at the beginning of this introduction — for the material in Section
4
,
X
will be
H^{m}
.

The first main result in Section
3
is the somewhat technical Lemma
6
, which is about the support function of a nonempty closed, convex subset of
X
. Our proof of this goes by way of a simple minimax theorem, which depends on nothing more complicated than the Hahn-Banach theorem in finite dimensional spaces. We state the relevant minimax theorem in Theorem
5
. We do not work in the Hilbert space
X
, but the Hilbert subspace
Y
introduced in Section
2
. Theorem
7
is the main result of the paper, and gives a generalization to this more abstract situation of the solution to the geometry conjecture.

In Section
4
, we apply Theorem
7
to obtain a solution to the geometry conjecture. (See Theorems
9
and
10
.)

In Section
5
we give two other ways of obtaining Theorem
7
, the
Fan’s inequality approach
and the
maximally monotone operator approach
.

In Theorem
12
, we state Fan’s inequality, and show how this can be used instead of Theorem
5
to obtain Lemma
6
. This is the
Fan’s inequality approach
.

In Lemma

using three (non–trivial) results on maximally monotone operators on a Hilbert space. This corresponds more closely with the method used in
[

The average operator

Let
X
be a linear space,
R\colon\ X\to X
be linear and
R^{m}=I_{X}
. Let

A:=\textstyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}R^{i}.\hbox{ ($A$ stands for {average}.)}

Let
Y
be the subspace
\{y\in X\colon\ Ay=0\}
of
X
. We now define
S\colon\ X\to X
by
S:=R-I_{X}
and
Q\colon\ Y\to X
by
Q:=\frac{1}{m}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}iR^{i}
. (Compare with
[
3
, Equation (28), p. 4]
.)

Lemma 1
.

We have

S(X)\subset Y,

(2)

\hbox{for all}\ y\in Y,\ Qy\in Y\hbox{ and }S(Qy)=y.

(3)

Proof.

We have

mAS=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}(R^{i+1}-R^{i})=R^{m+1}-R=0

and (
2
) follows immediately.

Note that

mAR=mRA=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}R^{i+1}=\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m}R^{i}+R^{m+1}=\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m}R^{i}+R=mA.

Thus
AR=RA=A
. It is easy to deduce from this that, if
1\leq k\leq m
then

AR^{k}=A\hbox{ and }R^{k}A=A.

(4)

Let
y\in Y
. Then
A(Qy)=(AQ)y=\frac{1}{m}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}iAR^{i}y
. From (
4
),
A(Qy)=\frac{1}{m}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}iAy=0
, and so
Qy\in Y
. Now

mRQy=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}iR^{i+1}y=\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m}(i-1)R^{i}y=\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}(i-1)R^{i}y+my-y

and

mQy=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}iR^{i}y=Ry+\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}iR^{i}y.

Thus

\displaystyle mS(Qy)

\displaystyle=mRQy-mQy=\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}(i-1)R^{i}y+my-y-Ry-\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}iR^{i}y

\displaystyle=my-y-Ry-\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}R^{i}y=my-mAy=my.

The proof of (

∎

Results for Hilbert spaces

We now suppose that
X
is a Hilbert space. We have the following Theorem with two parts and a one–line proof. Theorem
3
(a) is due to Xianfu Wang.

Theorem 3
.

Let
{\cal R}\colon X\to X
be a linear map and
{\cal S}:={\cal R}-I_{X}
. Then:

(a)

{\cal R}
is nonexpansive if, and only if, for all
x\in X
,
\|{\cal S}x\|^{2}\leq-2\langle{\cal S}x,x\rangle
.

(b)

{\cal R}
is an isometry if, and only if, for all
x\in X
,
\|{\cal S}x\|^{2}=-2\langle{\cal S}x,x\rangle
.

Proof.

Here is the one line: for all
x\in X
,

\|{\cal S}x\|^{2}+2\langle{\cal S}x,x\rangle=\langle{\cal S}x,{\cal S}x+2x\rangle=\langle{\cal R}x-x,{\cal R}x+x\rangle=\|{\cal R}x\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}.

That completes the proof.
∎

\hbox{for all}\ x\in X,\ \|Sx\|^{2}=-2\langle Sx,x\rangle.

There exist

Define

Cycles of projections

Let
X:=H^{m}
,
C:=C_{1}\times\cdots\times C_{m}\subset H^{m}
,

R(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}):=(x_{m},x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1}),

Y

R

(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{m})

\hbox{for all}\ i=1,\dots,m,\ z_{i}\in C_{i}\hbox{ and, }\hbox{for all}\ i=1,\dots,m-1,\ z_{i}-z_{i+1}=d_{i+1}.

Since
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}=0
, it follows from (

and the result is immediate from Theorem

((
12
)
\iff
(
14
)) and (

).
∎

It is now convenient to make a notational change. Let

, from which
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}=0
and

. With this substitution, we get:

.

(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{m})
is a cycle if, and only if,

\hbox{for all}\ i=1,\dots,m,z_{i}\in C_{i}\hbox{ and, }\hbox{for all}\ i=1,\dots,m-1,\ z_{i+1}=z_{i}+v_{i}.

We now prove the geometry conjecture:

We have

\{z_{m}:(z_{1},\dots z_{m})\hbox{ is a cycle}\}=C_{m}\cap(C_{m-1}+v_{m-1})\cap(C_{1}+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}v_{i}).

) If

Thus we have established the inclusion (

) in (

(

\zeta\in C_{m}\cap(C_{m-1}+v_{m-1})\cap(C_{1}+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}v_{i})

5
Other approaches leading to Theorem

Theorem

[

. It is closely related to fixed point theorems, and has found many applications in optimization and mathematical economics. It first appeared in

]

Theorem 12
.

Let
D
be a compact, convex subset of a topological vector space, and
f\colon\ D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
f
is quasiconcave with respect to its first variable and lower semicontinuous with respect to its second variable. Then

\min\nolimits_{d\in D}\sup\nolimits_{b\in D}f(b,d)\leq\sup\nolimits_{b\in D}f(b,b).

It was actually proved in (

) and (

) in the proof of Lemma

,

. Thus, from Theorem

,
\min\nolimits_{d\in D}\sup\nolimits_{b\in D}f(b,d)\leq 0
, and we obtain (
9
) as before. This is the
Fan’s inequality approach
.

The statement of Lemma
16
below is identical with that of Lemma
6
, except that (

) and (

) are in the reverse order to that of (

) and (
9
), and the quantifier “

” in (

” in (

). So Lemma

. Thus we obtain the

Let
