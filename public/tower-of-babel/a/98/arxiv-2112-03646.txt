Formal ternary laws and Buchstaber’s 2-groups
David Coulette, Frédéric Déglise, Jean Fasel, Jens Hornbostel

Abstract
. We compare formal ternary laws to Buchstaber’s 2-valued formal group laws.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Formal group laws (or FGL for short) are fundamental in many areas of mathematics.
In algebraic topology, since Quillen’s work on complex cobordism,
one dimensional commutative formal group laws have been an important tool
when studying Chern classes for complex oriented cohomology theories
E^{*}
.
In
[
Qui69
]
, Quillen discovered that the first Chern class of a tensor product
of line bundles is computed by a formal group law canonically associated with
the
E^{*}
. Moreover, he showed that the formal group law associated with complex
cobordism is the universal one, introduced by Lazard in
[
Laz55
]
.
The work of Quillen has been hugely influential in algebraic topology.

Among many of his pursuers, Buchstaber has intiatied a program for studying symplectic cobordism,
via the realization of Pontryagin classes of sympectic bundles in complex cobordism
(
[
BN71
,
Buh75
,
Buh76
,
Buh78
]
, see also
[
AL75
]
).
Most notably, he coined down the notion of (commutative one-dimensional)
2-valued formal group

over a base ring
R
,
formed by certain pairs
(F_{1}(x,y),F_{2}(x,y))
of formal power series in two variables with coefficients in
R

(see Example
2.2.2
for more details).
In fact, he showed that the Pontryagin polynomial of a tensor product of the
two copies of the universal symplectic bundle gives rise (after taking a “square root”)
to a 2-valued formal group. Moreover, the 2-valued group obtained in this way
satisfies a special condition, that Buchstaber calls “type I” (see
2.3.1
),
and he finally shows that one obtains the universal 2-valued formal group of type I, at least after
inverting
2
. This is therefore an analogue of Quillen’s identification of the
formal group law of complex cobordism with the universal one.

More recently, Voevodsky opened a door between algebraic topology and algebraic geometry, introducing among other things algebraic cobordism,
algebraic Morava K-theory and the motivic Steenrod algebra.
After a formalization of
(algebraic) oriented theories
in algebraic geometry by Panin and Smirnov,
Levine and Morel
[
LM07
]
have extended the work of Quillen on cobordism and formal group laws,
within the setting of oriented theories.
In particular, they show that the algebraic cobordism of any characteristic
0
field is isomorphic to the Lazard ring,
and the formal group law derived from Chern classes in algebraic cobordism
agrees with the universal formal group law:
op. cit.
Th. 1.2.6.
1
1

1

This was later
generalized by Marc Hoyois in
[
Hoy15
, Proposition 8.2]
for characteristic
p>0
fields after inverting
p
,
and by Markus Spitzweck in
[
Spi20
, Theorem 6.7]
for
a local regular ring after inverting the characteristic exponent of its residue field.

The path opened by these mathematicians has become particularly fecund and inspired
many new results and computations among which:
Riemann-Roch formulas (
[
Pan04
]
,
[
Smi06
]
,
[
Dég18b
]
),
motivic Landweber existence theorem (
[
NSØ09
]
),
Schubert calculus (
[
HK11
]
,
[
CPZ13
]
,
[
CZZ15
]
),
operations on oriented theories (
[
Vis19
]
,
[
Mer21
]
),
bivariant formalism (
[
SY14
]
,
[
Dég18a
]
).

However, there are important theories in motivic homotopy,
such as Chow-Witt groups (
[
BM00
]
,
[
Fas20
]
) and higher Grothendieck-Witt groups
(encompassing both orthogonal and hermitian K-theories, see e.g.
[
Hor05
]
,
[
ST15
]
or
[
Sch17
]
)
which are not oriented, while they possess a well-developed theory of characteristic classes
(Euler and Thom classes).
Motivated by these examples, Panin and Walter introduced a refinement of orientation theory in
motivic homotopy theory in a series of papers
[
PW10
,
PW18
,
PW19b
,
PW19a
]
.
The idea of their theory is to restrict the existence of Thom classes to particular
kind of vector bundles, the most important examples being the symplectic bundles
(
\mathrm{Sp}
-orientations) and oriented bundles (
\mathrm{SL}
-orientations).
The first case seems the most successful analogue of usual orientation (now
\mathrm{GL}
-orientation),
as it allows to get a theory of
Borel classes
, completely analogous to that
of Chern classes.
Borel classes are the motivic counter-part of Pontryagin classes in topology.

In the same spirit as Buchstaber’s 2-valued groups,
the second and third authors have developed in
[
DF21
]
an initial
idea of Walter which proposes to define an analogue for Borel classes of
the formal group laws associated with Chern classes. This is the theory
of
formal ternary laws
. The ternary comes from the basic fact
that a double tensor products of symplectic bundles is not symplectic, whereas
a triple tensor product is. Therefore, formal ternary laws arise by looking at the
Borel classes of triple tensor products of symplectic bundles of (minimal) ranks
2
.
As such a triple tensor product is of rank
8
, there are
4
relevant Borel classes
which lead in fact to four power series in three variables
(see §
3.1
).

One interesting features of the theory is that one gets series with coefficients
in the Grothendieck-Witt ring
\GW(\mathbb{Z})
, rather than just
\mathbb{Z}
in the classical case.
Formal ternary laws are therefore more complicated than formal group laws,
but still seem to play a classification role for motivic ring spectra analogous
to the latter for usual ring spectra. In fact, the second and third authors
successfully in
[
DF21
]
computed the formal ternary law
associated with Chow-Witt groups
(and its higher analogue, the MW-motivic ring spectrum,
[
BCD+20
]
)
while that of Grothendieck-Witt groups was recently computed in
[
FH20
]

– see Example
3.2
for both cases.
According to the nature of these theories, the corresponding formal ternary laws
are the analogue of the additive and multiplicative formal group laws respectively.
As in topology, these results are useful for computing stable operations.
In particular the second and third authors built the so-called Borel character,
analogue of the Chern character, which corresponds to the exponential isomorphism
from the multiplicative to the additive formal group laws with rational coefficients.

1.2.
Main results

The aim of this paper is first to develop the algebraic part of the theory
of formal ternary laws with the aim to relate it with Buchstaber’s
2-valued formal groups.

In order to formulate this comparison, we introduce a generalization of Buchstaber’s theory,
the category
\mathscr{G}_{n,d}(R)
of
n
-valued
d
-ary formal group laws
over a ring
R
,
or
(n,d)
-groups for short (see
2.2.1
).
The justification for this extension is
that while 2-groups are
2
-valued secondary formal group laws,
formal ternary laws not only are
4
-valued but also depends on three formal variables.
In this general framework we introduce a natural notion of degree
(
2.1.5
), based on the analogy with homology.
The minimal and maximal degrees of the coefficients of an
(n,d)
-group roughly measure its complexity.
As an example, we could mention that there is only one formal group law with minimal and maximal degrees both
0
, the
additive
one,
formal group law of degree
1
are exactly the
multuplicative ones
, and there are no
formal group of finite degree distinct from
0
and
1
.

We also introduce the analogue of the Lazard ring (
2.2.5
)
for
(n,d)
-groups.
In particular, we let
\mathcal{B}
be the ring which classifies the 2-groups, and
\mathcal{B}^{0}
the quotient ring
of
2
-groups whose coefficients have degree
0
. Then a basic but fundamental observation of Buchstaber
can be restated (
2.2.9
) by saying that:

\mathcal{B}^{0}\simeq\mathbb{Z}[\gamma]/\big((\gamma+2)(\gamma-2)^{2}\big).

This is a fundamental difference with the structure of the Lazard ring, as this ring is in particular
non-reduced.

Next we introduce after Walter the notion of
4
-valued formal ternary laws
(
i.e.
FTL,
see
3.1.5
),
which is an algebraization of the notion appearing in
[
DF21
, 2.3.2]
. Comparing
to
loc. cit.
and the original axioms of Walter, the novelty here is the so-called

\epsilon
-linearity axiom.
We refer the reader to
Section

5
for the fact that these FTL
are naturally attached to
\mathrm{Sp}
-oriented cohomology theories.
2
2

2

This improves
the results of
loc. cit.
which treated only the case
of
\mathrm{SL}
-oriented theories.

Note that FTL are not exactly
(4,3)
-groups: in fact, they have a
quadratic
nature
which is reflected by the fact that we work over the Grothendieck-Witt ring of
\mathbb{Z}
,

\GW(\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{Z}[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^{2}-1)
rather than just
\mathbb{Z}
(see
3.1.6
).
However we can introduce the classifying ring
\mathcal{W}
of FTL, that we call
the
Walter ring
. Our first theorem is a classification result:

Theorem 1
.

(see
3.1.12
)
After inverting
2
, there are only two FTLs of degree
\leq 0
.
One of this law is the FTL associated with Chow-Witt groups (see
[
DF21
]
).

Therefore, the FTL associated with Chow-Witt groups is the analogue of the additive FTL.
See
3.1.12
for a more precise version in terms of the Walter ring
and
3.1.13
for further explanations.

Formal ternary laws have an extremely high computational complexity,
due to the associativity axiom. More precisely, the Walter ring is naturally defined
as a polynomial algebra with an infinite number of variables modulo an ideal
\mathcal{R}_{FTL}
,
corresponding to the axioms (
3.1.9
).
Nonetheless, this ideal is very difficult to compute.
Therefore the preceding theorem could only be proved with the help of a computer.
More generally, we developed a sage implementation which computes
the Grobner basis of the ideal
\mathcal{R}_{FTL}
, for a possible range of degrees.
See
Appendix

A
for more details.

The main result of this paper is the following comparison statement below between the following three categories over an arbitrary
commutative ring
R
:

•

\mathcal{FGL}(R)=\mathscr{G}_{1,2}(R)
the category of formal group laws.

•

(
2-\mathcal{FGL})_{I}(R)\subset\mathscr{G}_{2,2}(R)
Buchstaber’s category of 2-groups of the first type.

•

\mathcal{FTL}(R)=
the category of formal ternary laws.

Theorem 2
.

There is a functor
C:(2-\mathcal{FGL})_{I}\rightarrow\mathcal{FTL}
which has a concrete interpretation in terms of characteristic classes. Composing it with the functor
N:\mathcal{FGL}\rightarrow(2-\mathcal{FGL})_{I}
from Proposition
2.3.12
yields a functor which maps the FGL for Chow groups to the oriented part (set
\epsilon=-1
) of the FTL for Chow-Witt groups.

As a consequence of this theorem, it is in principle possible to determine all the FTL associated to oriented theories, including algebraic cobordism. It also gives an answer to the obvious question to know if FTL can be described in terms of more basic ingredients such as 2-valued formal groups à la Buchstaber. The answer is negative in general (e.g.
4.0.3
and the subsequent discussion). Concretely, this means that in general the Borel classes of a triple product of symplectic bundles can’t be computed using hypothetic characteristic classes (satisfying Buchstaber’s axioms) attached to a product of two symplectic bundles.

To conclude, let us stress some consequences of the universality of the Walter ring
\mathcal{W}
. This discussion can be taken as a philosophical explanation of the results of the paper. If
\mathbb{E}
is an Sp-oriented motivic ring spectrum over a field
k
, then there is a ring homomorphism

\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{E}^{2*,*}(\Spec k)

defined by mapping the generators of the left-hand side to the corresponding coefficient in the formal ternary law attached to
\mathbb{E}
. In the particular case of
\mathbf{MSp}
, recall that there is at present no complete computation of
\mathbf{MSp}^{2*,*}(\Spec k)
, as well as of the homotopy groups of its topological counterpart
MSp
. Using complex realization, we obtain a string of ring homomorphisms

\mathcal{W}\to\mathbf{MSp}^{2*,*}(\Spec{\mathbb{C}})\to\pi_{-2*}(MSp)

which factors through a string of ring homomorphisms

(1.2.0.a)

\mathcal{W}/(\epsilon+1)\to\mathbf{MSp}^{2*,*}(\Spec{\mathbb{C}})\to\pi_{-2*}(MSp)

(see Remark
5.0.1
for more details). Using real realization instead, we obtain (using e.g.
[
BH20
, Corollary 4.7]
) a sequence

(1.2.0.b)

\mathcal{W}/(\epsilon-1)\to\mathbf{MSp}^{2*,*}(\Spec{\mathbb{R}})\to\pi_{-*}(MU)

and the ring
\mathcal{W}
can then be thought of as providing a (possibly partial) set of generators and relations for both
\pi_{-2*}(MSp)
and
\pi_{-*}(MU)
. At the moment, we are not able to perform integral computations in the Walter ring, and we leave this question to further work. After inverting
2
, the computations become much easier. For instance, Section
A.5
shows that

(\mathcal{W}^{\leq 2}/\langle\epsilon-1\rangle)\otimes\mathbb{Z}[1/2]\simeq\mathbb{Z}[1/2][a^{3}_{111},a^{1}_{111}]/\langle(a^{3}_{111}-8)(a^{3}_{111}+8)\rangle

which fits well with the computations of
\pi_{-*}(MU)\otimes\mathbb{Z}[1/2]
(see Remark
3.1.13
for a tentative explanation of the relation
(a^{3}_{111}-8)(a^{3}_{111}+8)=0
). At present, we do not know if our system of relations in
\mathcal{W}
is complete, i.e. that the ring homomorphism
\mathcal{W}\to\mathbf{MSp}^{2*,*}(\Spec k)
is an isomorphism for a reasonable field
k
. We hope to explore this question in future work.

2.
On Buchstaber’s multi-valued series and formal group laws

2.1.
Multivalued series

The following definition is a variation on Buchstaber’s algebraic theory
of multi-valued power series, compare
[
Buh75
, section 1]
.

Definition 2.1.1
.

Let
R
be a ring, and
n,d>0
be integers.
We let
\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
be formal variables.
An
n
-valued
d
-dimensional (power) series with coefficients in
R
, or
(n,d)
-series over
R
for short,
will be a polynomial

F_{t}(\underline{x})
in
R[[\underline{x}]][t]
of degree
n
and constant term
1
. We use the generic notation:

(2.1.1.a)

F_{t}(\underline{x})=1+F_{1}(\underline{x})t+\ldots+F_{n}(\underline{x})t^{n}.

We will also consider a different way of writing this type of polynomials, that we call the

Buchstaber form
:

\tilde{F}_{t}(\underline{x}):=t^{n}F_{(-t^{-1})}(\underline{x})=t^{n}-F_{1}(\underline{x})t^{n-1}+\ldots+(-1)^{n}F_{n}(\underline{x}).

We write
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(R)
the corresponding
R
-module.

Let
\varphi:R\rightarrow R^{\prime}
be a morphism of rings.
The obvious corestriction of scalars functor
\varphi_{*}:R[[\underline{x}]][t]\rightarrow R^{\prime}[[\underline{x}]][t]
induces a map:

\varphi_{*}:\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(R)\rightarrow\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(R^{\prime}),

which we will still call the
corestriction
(along
R^{\prime}/R
).

Remark 2.1.2
.

(1)

Obviously,
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(R)
is isomorphic to the
R
-module
R[[\underline{x}]]^{n}

and the
(n,d)
-series
F_{t}(\underline{x})
is determined by the
n
-tuple of power series in the
variables
\underline{x}
:
F_{1}(\underline{x}),\ldots,F_{n}(\underline{x})
.

In particular,
\mathscr{F}_{1,d}
is isomorphic to the
R
-module of power series in
\underline{x}
.
Given such a power series
\varphi(\underline{x})
, we will write
\varphi_{t}(\underline{x})=1+\varphi(\underline{x})t

the corresponding object of
\mathscr{F}_{1,n}
.

(2)

The notation (
2.1.1.a
) is motivated by the theory of characteristic classes,
and especially total classes (Chern, Pontryagin, Borel, etc.).
The Buchstaber form
\tilde{F}_{t}(\underline{x})
, taken from the convention of
[
Buh75
]
,
is useful when reasoning in terms of roots. We keep the two possible conventions to help the reader
compare our notations with that of Buchstaber, but we believe that the notation (
2.1.1.a
)
is better suited when dealing with examples from motivic homotopy theory which are our motivation.

Example 2.1.3
.

Let
\mathbb{E}
be an oriented motivic ring spectrum over a scheme
S
and let
\mathbb{P}_{S}^{\infty}
be the infinite projective space (which is the colimit of
\mathbb{P}_{S}^{n}
for
n
increasing). Let
\mathbb{E}^{**}=\mathbb{E}^{**}(S)
be the ring of coefficients.

Then it is well-known that
\mathbb{E}^{**}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty}_{S})=\mathbb{E}^{**}[[x]]
,
where
x
is the first Chern class of the universal line bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{\infty}_{S}
. Note further that
x
is of cohomoloical degree
(2,1)
. More generally, we have
\mathbb{E}^{*,*}((\mathbb{P}^{\infty}_{S})^{\times d})=\mathbb{E}^{**}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
for any
d\geq 1
, where
x_{i}
are the first Chern classes of the corresponding line bundles, sitting in degree
(2,1)
. Let now
G_{d,\infty}
be the Grassmannian of
d
-dimensional sub-vector bundles in an infinite dimensional vector bundle over
S
,
and let
V_{d,\infty}
be the universal
d
-dimensional vector bundle over
\mathrm{Gr}_{d,\infty}
. The sum of the universal line bundles yields a morphism

(\mathbb{P}^{\infty}_{S})^{\times d}\to G_{d,\infty}

and we may consider the pull-back of the total Chern class (with coefficients in
\mathbb{E}^{*,*}(G_{d,\infty})
)

c_{t}(V):=1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(V)t^{i},

along this morphism. Each
c_{i}(V)
is a homogeneous power series of degree
i
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
and we obtain an element of
\mathscr{F}_{d,d}(\mathbb{E}^{*,*})
. More precisely, we have
c_{i}(V)=e_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree
i
in the variables
\underline{x}
. Further, consider the map

(\mathbb{P}^{\infty}_{S})^{\times 2}\to\mathbb{P}_{S}^{\infty}

classifying the tensor product of the two universal line bundles. Pulling-back the Chern class
1+xt
to
(\mathbb{P}^{\infty}_{S})^{\times 2}
, we obtain a polynomial in
\mathscr{F}_{1,2}(\mathbb{E}^{*,*})
of the form

1+F(x_{1},x_{2})t

where
F(x_{1},x_{2})
is the first Chern class of the product. This is the so-called formal group law of
\mathbb{E}^{*,*}
.

2.1.4
.

We can generically write an
(n,d)
-series
F_{t}(\underline{x})
with coefficients in
R
as

(2.1.4.a)

F_{t}(\underline{x})=1+\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\\
l=1,\ldots,n}a_{\underline{\alpha}}^{l}\underline{x}^{\underline{\alpha}}t^{l},

for coefficients
a_{\underline{\alpha}}^{l}\in R
. The preceding example leads us to consider a grading on
(n,d)
-series,
defined by the convention that the formal variables
x_{1},...,x_{d}
have degree
+1
and the indeterminate

has degree
-1
.

Definition 2.1.5
.

Consider the above notation. We define the degree (resp. valuation) of the
(n,d)
-series
F_{t}(\underline{x})
as the integer
\delta
(resp.
\nu
)
such that:

\displaystyle\delta=\sup(A),\text{resp. }\nu=\inf(A),

\displaystyle A=\{|\underline{\alpha}|-l\mid\underline{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},1\leq l\leq n,a_{\underline{\alpha}}^{l}\neq 0\}.

In particular, we view
\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(R)
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
R
-module.

By definition,
\delta\geq\nu\geq-n
. The interval
[\nu,\delta]
measures the
complexity of the
(n,d)
-series
F_{t}(\underline{x})
.

Example 2.1.6
.

In Example
2.1.3
, one notes that the Chern classes
c_{l}(V)
are of bidegree
(2l,l)=l(2,1)
.
In particular, if one writes:

c_{l}(V)=\sum_{\underline{\alpha}}a_{\underline{\alpha}}^{l}\underline{c}^{\underline{\alpha}},

the (cohomological) bidegree of

is
(l-|\underline{\alpha}|)(2,1)
, and this legitimates the choice of grading

Remark 2.1.7
.

There is an obvious multiplication map:

\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(R)\times\mathscr{F}_{m,e}(R)\rightarrow\mathscr{F}_{n+m,d+e},F_{t}(\underline{x}),G_{t}(\underline{y})\mapsto F_{t}(\underline{x}).F_{t}(\underline{y})

where the product is taken in the ring

. Whereas this product is quite natural,
we will rather use the substitution product of

Definition 2.1.8
.

Consider an
(n,d)
-series
F_{t}(\underline{x})\in\mathscr{F}_{n,d}(R)
.
A
root
of
F_{t}(\underline{x})
will be a root of
\tilde{F}_{t}(\underline{x})
, as a polynomial in

.
We say that
F_{t}(\underline{x})
is
split
if
\tilde{F}_{t}(\underline{x})
is split, as a polynomial in
t
.
In the latter case, we will generically denote by
F^{[i]}
,
i=1,...,n
the corresponding roots.
It follows that

F_{t}(\underline{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+F^{[i]}t).

Example 2.1.9
.

The previous definition is motivated by the splitting principle. As in Example
2.1.3
, we have

c_{t}(V):=1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(V)t^{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+x_{i}t)

In other words, the
n
-dimensional
d
-valued series
c_{t}(V)
is split in
\mathbb{E}^{*,*}[[\underline{x}]][t]
–
i.e.

the polynomial
\tilde{c}_{t}(V)\in\mathbb{E}^{**}[[\underline{c}]][t]
splits.

Remark 2.1.10
.

Beware that if
R
is not integral, the roots of a split series
F_{t}(\underline{x})
are not unique
in general.

2.1.11
.

The algebraic splitting principle
.
Consider an
(n,d)
-series
F_{t}(\underline{x})
with coefficients in
R
.
Note that there always exists a finite ring extension
R[[\underline{x}]]\rightarrow R^{\prime}

such that
\tilde{F}_{x}(\underline{x})
splits in
R^{\prime}
. In fact, there is a universal one given by the formula

R^{\prime}=R[[\underline{x}]][\underline{t}]/(e_{i}(\underline{t})-F_{i}(\underline{x}),i=1,\ldots,n)

where
\underline{t}=(t_{1},...,t_{d})
,
e_{i}(\underline{t})
is the
i
-th elementary symmetric polynomial in the variables

\underline{t}
. Given a finite extension
A/R[[\underline{x}]]
in which
\tilde{F}_{x}(\underline{x})
splits, we denote by
F^{[i]}
(or sometimes
F_{A}^{[i]}
if
A
is important) its roots so that we have:

F_{t}(\underline{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+F^{[i]}t),

and call them the
formal roots
of
F_{t}(\underline{x})
in
A
. There is a bijection between the set of pairs
(A,F^{[i]}_{A})
(with
A
finite over
R[[\underline{x}]]
) and the set of finite homomorphisms of
R[[\underline{x}]]
-algebras
R^{\prime}\to A
.

2.1.12
.

Substitutions
.
We will say that a power series
\varphi(x)
in one variable is
composable
if
\varphi(0)=0
. Then for any power series
\psi(x)
, the composite

Then the coefficient of
x^{i}y^{j}t^{l}
in this expression gives the image of the element
a_{ij}^{l}\in\mathcal{B}

under
\mathfrak{S}
. When
l=1
, this rule is simple:
\mathfrak{S}(a_{ij}^{1})=2a_{ij}
.
When
l=2
, one gets relations like:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(a_{1i}^{2})=2a_{1,i-1},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(a_{22}^{2})=a_{11}^{2}+4a_{21},\ \mathfrak{S}(a_{23}^{2})=2a_{11}a_{12}+2a_{13}+2a_{22},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(a_{22}^{2})=2a_{11}a_{22}+2a_{12}^{2}+4a_{23}.

2.3.4
.

We now start with preparations which will lead to another more subtle functor
\mathcal{FGL}\to 2-\mathcal{FGL}
which produces 2-FGL of the first type and is more closely related to characteristic classes.

Given a formal group law
F(x,y)
, we will denote by

\bar{x}=-x+a_{11}x^{2}+(a_{12}-a_{11}-a_{11}^{2})x^{3}+\ldots

the unique power series in
x
such that
F(x,\bar{x})=0
—

i.e.
the formal inverse, see
[
Str
, Lem. 2.7]
. For any composable
f\in R[[x]]
we denote more generally by
\overline{f}
the series

\bar{f}:=\bar{x}(f)=-f+a_{11}f^{2}+(a_{12}-a_{11}-a_{11}^{2})f^{3}+\ldots

Lemma 2.3.5
.

For any composable power series
f\in R[[x]]
, the series
\overline{f}
is the unique series such that

F(f,\overline{f})=0.

Proof.

As
F(x,\bar{x})=0
, it follows immediately, replacing
x
by
f
that
F(f,\overline{f})=0
. Unicity is obvious: If
g\in R[[x]]
is such that
F(f,g)=0
, we have

g=F(g,0)=F(g,F(f,\overline{f}))=F(F(g,f),\overline{f})=\bar{f}.

∎

For further use, it is worth noting that
\bar{x}
induces an automorphism of the formal group law
F
, in the sense that

\overline{F(x,y)}:=\bar{x}(F(x,y))=F(\bar{x},\bar{y}).

The following lemma is probably well-known, but we include it for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.3.6
.

Let
F\in R[[x,y]]
be a (commutative) formal group law. Then, there exists a unique power series
G\in R[[x]]
such that
x+\bar{x}=G(x\bar{x})
.

Proof.

We start with a few preliminary observations. We have a ring homomorphism

R[[x,y]]\to R[[x]]

such that
x\mapsto x
and
y\mapsto\bar{x}
. As
\bar{x}=-x+\sum_{i\geq 2}a_{i}x^{i}
, we get
x+\bar{x}=x\bar{x}=0\pmod{x^{2}}
and then the ideal
\langle x+y,xy\rangle^{n}
is mapped under this homomorphism into
\langle x^{2n}\rangle
. The power series
F
being symmetric, we may write it as

F(x,y)=x+y+uxy+\sum_{i+j\geq 2}a_{ij}(x+y)^{i}(xy)^{j}.

We now construct for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
polynomials
G_{n}\in R[t]
of degree
n
by induction. We set
G_{0}=0,G_{1}=-ut
and

G_{n}=-ut-\sum_{i+j=2}^{n}a_{ij}G_{n-1}(t)^{i}t^{j}\pmod{t^{n+1}}

for
n\geq 2
. We now check by induction that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

x+\bar{x}=G_{n}(x\bar{x})
modulo
\langle x^{2n+2}\rangle
.

(2)

G_{n}=G_{n-1}\pmod{t^{n}}
.

For
n=0
, we already know that
x+\bar{x}=0\pmod{x^{2}}
and the second relation is empty. For
n=1
, we observe that

F(x,y)=x+y+uxy\pmod{\langle x+y,xy\rangle^{2}}

and it follows that

0=F(x,\bar{x})=x+\bar{x}+ux\bar{x}\pmod{x^{4}}.

As
G_{1}=-ut
, both properties are satisfied. Let then
n\geq 2
be such that properties 1. and 2. are satisfied for
n-1
.
Modulo
\langle x+y,xy\rangle^{n+1}
, we have

F(x,y)=x+y+uxy+\sum_{i+j=2}^{n}a_{ij}(x+y)^{i}(xy)^{j}

and consequently

0=F(x,\bar{x})=x+\bar{x}+ux\bar{x}+\sum_{i+j=2}^{n}a_{ij}(x+\bar{x})^{i}(x\bar{x})^{j}\pmod{x^{2n+2}}.

By construction
x+\bar{x}=G_{n-1}(x\bar{x})
modulo
\langle x^{2n}\rangle
, i.e.
x+\bar{x}=G_{n-1}(x\bar{x})+Hx^{2n}
for some polynomial
H
. Modulo
\langle x^{2n+2}\rangle
, we have

(x+\bar{x})^{i}=G_{n-1}(x\bar{x})^{i}+iHG_{n-1}(x\bar{x})^{i-1}x^{2n}

and we can use the fact that
G_{n-1}(t)=0\pmod{t}
and
x\bar{x}=0
modulo
\langle x^{2}\rangle
to get
(x+\bar{x})^{i}=G_{n-1}(x\bar{x})^{i}\pmod{\langle x^{2n+2}\rangle}
. Altogether, the above expression reads as

0=x+\bar{x}+ux\bar{x}+\sum_{i+j=2}^{n}a_{ij}G_{n-1}(x\bar{x})^{i}(x\bar{x})^{j}\pmod{x^{2n+2}}.

This means that
x+\bar{x}=G_{n}(x\bar{x})
modulo
x^{2n+2}
, and the first point is satisfied. We are left with proving that
G_{n}=G_{n-1}
modulo
t^{n}
. By definition, we may write

G_{n}=-ut-\sum_{i+j=2}^{n}a_{ij}G_{n-1}(t)^{i}t^{j}\pmod{t^{n+1}}.

We know by induction that
G_{n-1}=G_{n-2}\pmod{t^{n-1}}
. It follows that
G_{n-1}t=G_{n-2}t
modulo
t^{n}
. On the other hand,
G_{n-2}=0\pmod{t}
and we obtain for
i\geq 2
that

G_{n-1}^{i}=(G_{n-2}+\alpha t^{n-1})^{i}=G_{n-2}^{i}.

Modulo
t^{n}
, we then obtain

G_{n}=-ut-\sum_{i+j=2}^{n}a_{ij}G_{n-1}(t)^{i}t^{j}=-ut-\sum_{i+j=2}^{n}a_{ij}G_{n-2}(t)^{i}t^{j}=G_{n-1}.

∎

Lemma 2.3.9

Let

∎

Remark 2.3.13
.

The functor
N_{R}
is not faithful. Consider the additive formal group law
F(x,y)=x+y
and the morphism
\Theta:F\to F
given by the series
\Theta(t)=-t
(i.e.
\Theta=\bar{x}
). Then, we see that the series
\Theta^{\prime}(t)=t
and
\Theta
have the same image under the functor. Indeed,

-\Theta(x)\Theta(\bar{x})=--x(-\bar{x})=x^{2},

showing that
\varphi(\Theta)=t
. The same applies to
\Theta^{\prime}
.

More generally, let
F(x,y)
be an arbitrary formal group law. Then, we know that the series
\bar{x}^{F}=\bar{x}
is an endomorphism of
F
. As
\bar{x}(\bar{x})=x
, it follows that
\varphi(\bar{t})=t=\varphi(t)
.

Remark 2.3.14
.

The functor
N
is not full either. Consider the elementary
2
-formal group of the first type

. Now, consider the series
\varphi(x)=-x

and

3.1.
Algebraic definitions

3.1.1
.

Our base ring will be the ring

\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}:=\GW(\mathbb{Z}),

i.e.
the Grothendieck-Witt ring associated with the category of finitely generated projective
\mathbb{Z}
-modules equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form.
Let
<-1>
be the class of the bilinear form

\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}

given by
(x,y)\mapsto-xy
.
Following Morel’s conventions in motivic homotopy theory, we set
\epsilon:=-<-1>
.
Then the canonical map:

\mathbb{Z}[t]\rightarrow\GW(\mathbb{Z}),t\mapsto\epsilon

is an epimorphism whose kernel is the ideal
(t^{2}-1)

(see
[
MH73
, chap. 2]
). One writes suggestively
\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{Z}[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^{2}-1)
.
In particular,
\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
2
:
any element can uniquely be written as
a+b.\epsilon
for a couple of integers
(a,b)
.

One defines the plus-part (resp. minus-part) of
\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}
as the quotient ring:

\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}^{+}=\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}/(\epsilon+1)\text{ resp. }\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}^{-}=\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}/(\epsilon-1).

We have an obvious identification
\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}^{+}\simeq\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}^{-}\simeq\mathbb{Z}
)
obtained by mapping the class of
a+b\epsilon
to
a-b
(resp.
a+b
).
The following lemma is now obvious.

Lemma 3.1.2
.

The canonical morphism of rings

\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}^{+}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}^{-}\simeq\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}

is injective and its image is given by the set of pairs
(n,m)

such that
n\equiv m\mod 2
. It is an isomorphism after inverting
2

on both sides.

Geometrically,
\Spec(\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon})
is the union of two closed subschemes whose reductions are

\Spec(\mathbb{Z})
, and which intersects in a single point,
\Spec(\mathbb{F}_{2}[t]/(t+1)^{2})
.

Corollary 3.1.3
.

For any
\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}
-algebra
A
, one puts
A^{\pm}=A\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}}\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}^{\pm}
.

There is a canonical map:
A\rightarrow A^{+}\times A^{-}
which is a monomorphism
if
A
is flat over
\mathbb{Z}_{\epsilon}
, and an isomorphism if
2
is invertible in
A
.

We constructed a functor

The motivation of the construction of
C
is of geometric nature (compare also section
5
below). Consider the universal bundles
U_{1},U_{2}
and
U_{3}
on
(\mathrm{H}{\mathbb{P}}^{\infty})^{\times 3}=\mathrm{BSp}_{2}^{\times 3}
. We write as usual
x
for the first Borel class of
U_{1}
,
y
for the first Borel class of
U_{2}
and
z
for the first Borel class of
U_{3}
. Suppose that we can associate characteristic classes to
U_{1}U_{2}
under the form of a reasonable series

F_{t}(x,y)=1+F_{1}(x,y)t+F_{2}(x,y)t^{2},

for instance for
F_{t}
a two-valued formal group law. The roots
F^{[1]}
and
F^{[2]}
represent characteristic classes of bundles
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
having the property that

U_{1}U_{2}=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}

over a suitable extension of
(\mathrm{H}{\mathbb{P}}^{\infty})^{\times 2}
. We could then write

U_{1}U_{2}U_{3}=(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})\cdot U_{3}=V_{1}U_{3}\oplus V_{2}U_{3}

The characteristic classes of the first bundle are given by the polynomial

1+F_{1}(F^{[1]},z)t+F_{2}(F^{[1]},z)t^{2}

and the ones of the second bundle by the polynomial

1+F_{1}(F^{[2]},z)t+F_{2}(F^{[2]},z)t^{2}

The product of the two polynomials is the well-defined series
C_{t}(F):=F_{t}(F_{t}(x,y),z)
. Explicitly, we have

C_{i}(F)(x,y,z)=\begin{cases}F_{1}(F^{[1]},z)+F_{1}(F^{[2]},z)&\text{ if $i=1$,}\\
F_{2}(F^{[1]},z)+F_{2}(F^{[2]},z)+F_{1}(F^{[1]},z)F_{1}(F^{[2]},z)&\text{ if $i=2$,}\\
F_{1}(F^{[1]},z)F_{2}(F^{[2]},z)+F_{2}(F^{[1]},z)F_{1}(F^{[2]},z)&\text{ if $i=3$,}\\
F_{2}(F^{[1]},z)F_{2}(F^{[2]},z)&\text{ if $i=4$.}\end{cases}

Lemma 4.0.1
.

Let
F_{t}
be a 2-valued formal group law of the first type over a ring
R
. Then, the series
C_{t}(F)(x,y,z):=F_{t}(F_{t}(x,y),z)
is a FTL over the ring
R
.

Proof.

We start with the symmetry axiom. As
F_{t}(x,y)=F_{t}(y,x)
, we obviously have
C_{t}(F)(x,y,z)=C_{t}(F)(y,x,z)
. To prove that
C_{t}(F)(x,y,z)=C_{t}(F)(x,z,y)
, we use the associativity and commutativity axioms for
F

F_{t}(F_{t}(x,y),z)=F_{t}(x,F_{t}(y,z))=F_{t}(x,F_{t}(z,y))=F_{t}(F_{t}(x,z),y).

For the neutral element, we note that
\epsilon
acts as
-1
on
R
, which implies that we have to prove that

C_{t}(F)(x,0,0)=1+4xt+6x^{2}t^{2}+4x^{3}t^{3}+x^{4}t^{4}.

This is a direct computation using
F_{t}(x,0)=1+2xt+x^{2}t^{2}
. For the weak neutral element axiom, we observe that
F_{2}(x,x)=0
implies that
F_{t}(x,x)=1+F_{1}(x,x)t
so that

C_{t}(F)(x,x,0)=F_{t}(F_{t}(x,x),0)=F_{t}(F_{1}(x,x),0)=(1+F_{1}(x,x)t)^{2}

yielding obviously
C_{3}(F)(x,x,0)=C_{4}(F)(x,x,0)=0
.
Finally, we have

\displaystyle C_{t}(F)(C_{t}(F)(x,y,z),v,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{t}(F)(F_{t}(F_{t}(x,y),z),v,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{t}(F_{t}(F_{t}(F_{t}(x,y),z),v),w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{t}(F_{t}(F_{t}(x,F_{t}(y,z)),v),w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{t}(F_{t}(x,F_{t}(F_{t}(y,z),v)),w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{t}(F)(x,C_{t}(F)(y,z,v),w).

∎

Appendix A
Sage implementation of FTL computations

We describe here the algorithm used to compute a standard (Groebner) basis of the ideal
\mathcal{R}_{FTL}
(see Proposition
3.1.9
) of and its implementation using the Sagemath
[
The21
]
software together with the libsingular
[
DGPS21
]
back-end for multivariate polynomial computations.

A.1.
Main problem characteristics and design principle

The inputs of the algorithm are a base numerical ring
R
(among
\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
), and a degree range
(d_{min},d_{max})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},-4\leq d_{min}\leq d_{max}
. With those restrictions, the unknown FTL coefficients span a finite set
a=\{a^{l}_{ijk},d_{min}\leq deg(a^{l}_{ijk})\leq d_{max},1,\leq l\leq 4\}
. The five axioms stated in Definition
3.1.5
induce relations between the FTL coefficients in the polynomial ring
R[\epsilon,a,\alpha]
, where
\alpha
stands for one or more optional formal variables. For technical reasons this is more convenient than to work explicitly in
R_{\epsilon}[a]
. Additional constraints can be appended to the relation set in order to obtain the desired quotient ring, (for instance
\epsilon^{2}-1
for
R_{\epsilon}[a]
or
2\alpha-1=0
for
R[\epsilon,a][\frac{1}{2}]
).
The overall purpose of the algorithm is first to compute the finite set
S_{FTL}=\cup S_{axiom}=\{r_{p},1\leq p\leq N,r_{p}\in R[\epsilon,a]\}
of relations stemming from the five FTL axioms and then compute a standard basis
g_{FTL}
of the ideal generated by
S_{FTL}
. Noting
Gb
the algorithm used for Groebner basis computation, a direct resolution scheme would simply read
g_{FTL}\leftarrow Gb(\mathcal{I}(S_{FTL}))
.

Unlike symmetry, (weak) neutral element, and
\epsilon
-linearity axioms, the set of relations induced by the associativity axiom tends to be quite large (hundreds to hundreds of thousands), with total degrees up to a few tens even for relatively small values (
1
or
2
) of
d_{max}
. Even using a sparse storage scheme, the memory footprint of the full relations set can easily grow to tens of gigabytes. A second issue is the Groebner basis computation, which can generate numerous intermediate
S
-polynomials with huge coefficients. Either of memory and processing time costs can easily saturate available resources, even on ”fat” computing nodes with hundreds of gigabytes of memory.

A.2.
Description of the implemented scheme

The resolution scheme presented hereafter is designed to mitigate those technical difficulties and allow to solve the problem for a limited range of degrees (up to
d_{max}=2
for some special cases).
Two optimization strategies are used to limit resources consumption. The first is to express the problem in the most compact manner to avoid representation overhead costs. The second is divide-and-conquer, i.e. split the whole problem into a sequence of smaller ones.

A first representation optimization stems from the symmetry axiom, which is the easiest to deal with. The symmetry relation set is fully generated by simple relations of the form
a^{l}_{ijk}-a^{l}_{\sigma(ijk)}=0
, with
\sigma\in S_{3}
. For any fixed
l
, FTL coefficients with the same
l
value and whose
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime})
indexes share a common orbit under
S_{3}
can be replaced by a single coefficient with indices
(i_{0},j_{0},k_{0})
, which amounts to simply removing them and replacing the monomial
X^{i}_{0}Y^{j}_{0}Z^{k}_{0}
by the corresponding monomial symmetric polynomial in the expression of
F_{t}
. We note
\overline{a}
this reduced set of FTL coefficients. Working with relations in
R[\epsilon,\overline{a}]
with this form for
F
, the symmetry axiom is structurally enforced, with a significant reduction in the number of variables.

Problem splitting is obtained by partitioning
S_{FTL}
into
K
small subsets
S_{FTL}=\cup_{p\leq K}s_{p}
. In order to avoid storing the full set of relations, the scheme operates in an iterative manner on an ideal sequence
\mathcal{I}_{p}
, defined by its generating set
g_{p}
, with initial value
g_{0}=\emptyset
. A each step
p\leq K
of the algorithm, the small subset
s_{p}
of the relations is generated on the fly. In the current implementation,
s_{p}
is the full set of relations for all axioms except associativity, and a single relation for each of the associativity axiom relations.

A reduced Groebner basis of
\mathcal{I}(g_{p}\cup s_{p})
is then computed and used to replace
g
. A single iteration of the scheme at step
p
reads thus
g_{p+1}\leftarrow Gb(\mathcal{I}(g_{p}\cup s_{p}))
. Obviously, this scheme is more efficient memory-wise than the naive scheme provided that the intermediate generators sets
g
size and complexity stay lower than the one of the full set of relations. While this is true for the final generating set
g_{K}
(
S_{FTL}
has a lot of redundancy), this is not guaranteed for the intermediate ones, whose complexity depend both on the way
S_{FTL}
is partitioned, and the underlying Groebner basis algorithm. From a computation cost point of view, the scheme is obviously not optimally efficient : indeed the GB algorithm is only providing partial information about the ideal at each step, preventing it from operating optimally and leading to quite a lot of redundant computations. In practice, in many cases the intermediate
g_{p}
complexity stays rather low, is quite often invariant
g_{p+1}=g_{p}
due to the large redundancy of associativity relations.

Based on this observation, a slight variation of the iteration scheme has also been implemented : after generation of
s_{p}
,
s_{p}
is reduced by
\mathcal{I}
before attempting computation of the Groebner basis. The scheme thus reads
g_{p+1}\leftarrow Gb(\mathcal{I}(g_{p}\cup s_{p}/\mathcal{I}(g_{p})))
. In cases when
g_{p+1}=g_{p}
,
s_{p}
actually reduces to
0
so that there is no need for an additional Groebner basis computation. Though this pre-reduction step is formally redundant, it appears empirically to ease the work of the Groebner algorithm when working over
\mathbb{Z}
and limit coefficient swelling.

A the end of each step, a second use of the representation sparsity optimization is then performed. Once the new set of generators
g_{p+1}
is computed, trivial relations of the form
r_{u}=a^{l}_{ijk}-c=0,c\in R[\epsilon]
are searched for. Whenever such a relation is found, the corresponding FTL coefficient is replaced by the unique solution
c
, and
a^{l}_{ijk}
removed from the pool of active variables of the working ring. While this is formally completely equivalent to keeping
r_{u}
in the generating set
g_{p}
, it has a non-negligible impact on memory consumption and computation complexity. A first guaranteed reduction is due to the fact that the number of active variables drives the size of all multiindexes representing monomial exponents in the polynomial representation of elements of
R[\epsilon,\overline{a}]
). This implies memory savings proportional to the number of variables that can be removed that way, at the risk of a slight increase in the complexity of the coefficients. Second, in practice, the solutions
c
are usually fairly simple and often zero, leading in a drastic reduction of the complexity of generating relations in the restricted polynomial ring. Most such substitutions stem from the neutral, weak neutral element and linearity axioms, which are treated first in the resolution scheme, and occur early in the computation.

A.3.
Short description of the code repository

The the core algorithm and data structures are regrouped in a single Python module (ftl_comp.py) meant to be imported and used from Sage. Basic usage examples are provided in the form of Jupyter notebooks for light computations and post-computation work using saved results, and batch python scripts for non-interactive heavier computations. A selection of computations results saved in json format is also provided.
