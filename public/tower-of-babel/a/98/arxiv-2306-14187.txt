1.
Introduction

Understanding the sharp constants and extremals of Sobolev Inequalities is an important problem due to its applicability in the study of many partial differential equations. One of the important example in this direction is the well known Yamabe problem from differential geometry where the knowledge of extremals of the Euclidean Sobolev inequality or more generally the classification of positive solutions of the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation is used in a very crucial way. In the case of Euclidean space extremals of Sobolev inequalities have been classified by now and one of the main development in the process has been establishing the radial symmetry of solutions.

In this article we study the Hyperbolic symmetry of solutions of the following quasilinear PDE in the
\displaystyle n
dimensional real Hyperbolic space
\displaystyle(\mathbb{H}^{n},g)
of constant sectional curvature
\displaystyle-1
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u-\lambda|u|^{p-2}u=|u|^{q-2}u\text{ in }\mathbb{H}^{n}\\
u\geq 0,\;u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).\end{cases}

(1.1)

The equation (
1.1
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation satisfied by the extremals of the Poincaré-Sobolev inequality in
\displaystyle(\mathbb{H}^{n},g)
given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}-\lambda|u|^{p}\,dV_{g}\geq S_{\lambda,q}\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}\right)^{p/q},\forall\,u\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

(1.2)

where,
\displaystyle\lambda\in\left[0,\lambda_{\text{max}}\right)
and
\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{max}}:=\inf_{u\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\setminus 0}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}}{\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}}=\left(\frac{n-1}{p}\right)^{p}
denotes the best constant in the Poincaré inequality in Hyperbolic space,
\displaystyle\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u:=\operatorname{div}_{g}(|\nabla_{g}u|^{p-2}\nabla_{g}u)
is the corresponding
\displaystyle p
-Laplace operator,
\displaystyle 1<p<n
,
\displaystyle p<q\leq p^{\ast}
,
\displaystyle p^{\ast}=\frac{np}{n-p}
denotes the critical Sobolev exponent and
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is the completion of
\displaystyle C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with the norm
\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}\right)^{1/p}
. Note that when
\displaystyle n=2
we are only considering
\displaystyle p<2
and
\displaystyle q\in(2,p^{\ast}]
where,
\displaystyle p^{\ast}=\frac{2p}{2-p}
. A solution of (
1.1
) is a
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left[|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-2}g(\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}\phi)-\lambda|u|^{p-2}u\phi\right]\,dV_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u|^{q-2}u\phi\,dV_{g},\;\forall\,\phi\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

By writing this equation in either the Poincaré ball model or in the upper half-space model, it can be easily seen from the standard regularity theory (see
[
35
]
,
[
18
]
) that the solutions of (
1.1
) are indeed
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
for some
\displaystyle\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Our main result in this article is the sharp asymptotics and Hyperbolic symmetry of solutions of (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let,
\displaystyle u
be a solution of (
1.1
), then there is a point
\displaystyle O\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and a positive strictly monotone decreasing function
\displaystyle\Phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\displaystyle u(x)=\Phi(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(O,x))
. The function
\displaystyle\Phi(t)
is
\displaystyle C^{1}
at
\displaystyle t=0
with
\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(0)=0
and smooth for all
\displaystyle t>0
. It satisfies the pointwise bounds
\displaystyle ce^{-\alpha_{\lambda}t}\leq\Phi(t)\leq Ce^{-\alpha_{\lambda}t}
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 0
,
\displaystyle ce^{-\alpha_{\lambda}t}\leq-\Phi^{\prime}(t)\leq Ce^{-\alpha_{\lambda}t}
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
, where,
\displaystyle c,C>0
are some positive constants (which depend on
\displaystyle\Phi
) and
\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}=-\alpha_{\lambda}
, where,
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}
is the unique positive root of
\displaystyle|\alpha|^{p-2}\alpha(n-1-(p-1)\alpha)=\lambda
such that
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}\in\left(\frac{n-1}{p},\frac{n-1}{p-1}\right]
.

In the case of Euclidean Sobolev inequality, the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}u=|u|^{p^{\ast}-2}u\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},u\geq 0,u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(1.3)

has been studied in detail. When
\displaystyle p=2
, the symmetry of solutions has been established in the seminal work of Gidas-Ni-Nirenberg
[
20
]
using the moving plane method. However when
\displaystyle p\not=2
there are many obstructions for proving the symmetry of solutions for the
\displaystyle p
-Laplace equations using the moving plane method mainly due to the lack of strong comparison principle, lack of tools to get the moving plane argument started from infinity (in the case of problems in unbounded domain) etc. Note that the
\displaystyle p
-Laplace operator is elliptic on points where
\displaystyle\nabla u\not=0
and in the complement it is either degenerate or singular depending on
\displaystyle p>2
or
\displaystyle p<2
respectively. The first significant progress in applying the moving plane method for the
\displaystyle p
-Laplace case was made by Damascelli in
[
10
]
where
he proved the radial symmetry for positive solutions of equations of the form
\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}u=f(u)
with Dirichlet boundary condition in bounded domains in the Euclidean space. The results in the entire space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
was established in
[
12
]
for solutions of the same PDE with proper decay/integrability, where
\displaystyle f
was non increasing in some interval
\displaystyle(0,s_{0})
. See also
[
32
]
for related developments.

In the case of Sobolev extremals, the nonlinear term is strictly increasing and hence the results of
[
12
]
can not be applied to obtain radial symmetry of Sobolev extremals. It was shown in

[

\displaystyle 1<p<2

\displaystyle u(x)\leq C|x|^{-m}

\displaystyle|\nabla u|\leq C|x|^{-(m+1)}

\displaystyle|x|

\displaystyle u(x)\geq c|x|^{-m}
for
\displaystyle|x|
large when
\displaystyle p^{\ast}<2

\displaystyle m>\frac{n-p}{p}

[
11

without any additional decay assumption when
\displaystyle\frac{2n}{n+2}\leq p<2
(i.e.,
\displaystyle p<2
and
\displaystyle p^{\ast}\geq 2

Tools for handling the issue of strong comparison in the general case
\displaystyle p>2
was developed by Damascelli and Sciunzi in
[

]
,
[
15
]
. Again these informations are not enough to obtain symmetry results for (

\displaystyle u(x)\approx|x|^{-\frac{n-p}{p-1}}
and
\displaystyle|\nabla u|\leq C|x|^{-\frac{n-1}{p-1}}
for
\displaystyle|x|
large. With this and the results of
[

the symmetry results follows when
\displaystyle 1<p<2
. The symmetry result for the case
\displaystyle p>2
was completed by Sciunzi
[
30

by establishing a precise pointwise gradient bound

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x)|\approx|x|^{-\frac{n-1}{p-1}}
for
\displaystyle|x|
large. More recently the classification result for (

) has also been established by a technique independent of the moving plane method in conical domains as well as the full Euclidean space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
in
[
9

. We also refer to
[

,
[

and
[

for the classification result of (

) where the finite energy assumption
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
has been relaxed in certain range of
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle n
.

Coming to the equation (
1.1
), when
\displaystyle p=2
the symmetry of solution has been studied in
[
1
]
for
\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4}n(n-2)
and subsequently extended to the full range of
\displaystyle\lambda
in
[

using the method of moving plane. Consequently in
[

the solutions have been studied thoroughly by analysing the corresponding ordinary differential equation. In this situation the moving plane method can be applied as we are in the elliptic regime and we have appropriate Sobolev inequalities. Thus the Euclidean method can be adopted to this geometric situation by reflecting over foliations of the Hyperbolic space.

Our main focus in this article is the case
\displaystyle p\not=2
. Like in the Euclidean case here also the issues are lack of strong comparison principles and also moving the plane from infinity. The issue of strong comparison principle in bounded subsets of
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n}
can be established by suitably adapting the ideas of
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
to the Hyperbolic space, the main challenge in this case is to establish the precise asymptotic estimates at infinity.

The most important contribution of this article is to establish sharp asymptotic estimates to the positive solutions of (
1.1
) and its gradient, so that the moving plane method can be made to work in this case by suitably adapting the Euclidean tools. In the Euclidean case the precise
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
-estimates were obtained by crucially rescaling the equations. However in our case we do not have any substitute for scaling. In Theorem-
3.6
we will establish the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
-estimates by finding appropriate sub and super solutions combined with weak comparison tools using the Picone identity. The estimates we obtained in Theorem-

\displaystyle p=2
, which in fact improves the estimates obtained in
[
24
]
except in cases where explicit solutions are known. To prove the estimates on the gradient, the upper bound follows from the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
-estimate and a use of Harnack inequality, however the lower bound on the gradient at infinity is the difficult estimate to prove. In the Euclidean case (
1.3
) this is achieved in
[
30
]
by a blow up analysis via rescaling which leads to the limiting equation
\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}u=0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle u(x)\approx|x|^{-\frac{n-p}{p-1}}

[

to be constant multiples of the fundamental solution
\displaystyle u(x)=c|x|^{-\frac{n-p}{p-1}}
(see also
[

[
26

for a similar limiting equation with a Hardy potential with singularity at origin in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The main difference of our work when comparing with the Euclidean references mentioned above is in the nature of the blow up and the limiting problem. In our case the limiting problem is in fact an eigenvalue problem with a pole at ‘infinity’. We need to establish a classification result for positive eigenfunctions of the following eigenvalue problem

-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u=\lambda u^{p-1}

(1.4)

which are comparable to certain eigenfunctions with desired behavior at infinity. Here by an eigenfunction we means a
\displaystyle C^{1}
function which satisfy the relation

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-2}g(\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}\phi)\,dV_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\lambda u^{p-1}\phi\,dV_{g}

for all
\displaystyle\phi\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.

Fix a point
\displaystyle O\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\displaystyle(r,\theta)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be the geodesic normal coordinates centred at
\displaystyle O
. For
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
define a function
\displaystyle E_{\xi}
by

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{\xi}(x)=\left(\cosh r-(\xi\cdot\theta)\sinh r\right)^{-\alpha_{\lambda}}

(1.5)

where,
\displaystyle(r,\theta)
is the geodesic normal coordinate of
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}
is as in Theorem-
1.1
. Note that
\displaystyle E_{\xi}
extends as a
\displaystyle C^{1}
function to
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n}
. With this definition we can state the classification result as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
\displaystyle E_{\xi}
be as in (
1.5
). If
\displaystyle E
satisfies (
1.4
) and has the bounds
\displaystyle E_{\xi}(x)\leq E(x)\leq CE_{\xi}(x)
for some
\displaystyle C>1
and all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
then
\displaystyle E\equiv cE_{\xi}
for some
\displaystyle c\in[1,C]
.

We prove Theorem-
1.2
in Section-
3

3.8
formulated in the equivalent Poincaré ball model
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
of the Hyperbolic space.

The problem of classification of positive eigenfunctions in general may have independent interest from a potential theoretic perspective, particularly in the study of Martin boundary. We recall some relevant definitions. Let,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
be a homogeneous elliptic operator, meaning if
\displaystyle u
solves
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u=0
then so does
\displaystyle cu
for any constant
\displaystyle c
. Suppose,
\displaystyle u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
be a positive solution to the equation
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u=0
such that
\displaystyle u
has vanishing ‘trace’ on
\displaystyle\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{\xi\}
i.e., it vanishes in some generalized trace sense on the the ideal boundary (or sphere at infinity) except at
\displaystyle\xi
(see the definition of ideal boundary or the sphere at infinity in Section-
2
). We call such a solution
\displaystyle u
to be a minimal function relative to the point
\displaystyle\xi\in\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}
(identified with
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
) if and only if any other solution
\displaystyle\tilde{u}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\tilde{u}=0
satisfying
\displaystyle 0\leq\tilde{u}\leq Cu
for some positive constant
\displaystyle C
, must be a positive constant multiple of
\displaystyle u
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\equiv cu
for some
\displaystyle c>0
. We say that the
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
-Martin boundary can be identified with
\displaystyle\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}
if each point
\displaystyle\xi\in\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n}
has a unique (up to constant multiple) positive minimal function relative to it.

In the linear case when
\displaystyle p=2
, the conclusion of Theorem-
1.2
can be obtained by only assuming the upper bound (see Remark-
3.10
). The minimality of the eigenfunctions
\displaystyle E_{\xi}
relative to
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

[

and Minemura
[

. The representation of eigenfunctions on non-compact symmetric space through a hyperfunction on its minimal boundary, known as the Helgason conjecture, was established in full generality in
[
22

. It would be interesting to know if the eigenfunctions
\displaystyle E_{\xi}
are minimal relative to
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
for a general
\displaystyle 1<p<n

1.2
can be relaxed.

3.
Decay estimate for solutions and its gradient

We begin by showing that the solutions to equation (
1.1
) have higher integrability and are bounded by proceeding along the arguments in
[
37
]
,
[
33
]
.

Let us consider the auxiliary function

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(\alpha):=|\alpha|^{p-2}\alpha(n-1-(p-1)\alpha)

(3.1)

then,
\displaystyle\displaystyle f(0)=f\left(\frac{n-1}{p-1}\right)=0
and
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\alpha)=(p-1)|\alpha|^{p-2}(n-1-p\alpha)
. Thus
\displaystyle f
has a unique maximum at
\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{c}=\frac{n-1}{p}
and is concave and increasing in
\displaystyle[0,\alpha_{c})
and decreasing in
\displaystyle\left(\alpha_{c},\dfrac{n-1}{p-1}\right]

\displaystyle f(\alpha)=\lambda
has two roots
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda},\beta_{\lambda}
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0\leq\beta_{\lambda}<\frac{n-1}{p}<\alpha_{\lambda}\leq\frac{n-1}{p-1}

(3.2)

for any
\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda\in\left[0,\left(\frac{n-1}{p}\right)^{p}\right)
.

3.1.
Boundedness of solution

In what follows we will use the notation
\displaystyle\Omega_{m}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}:|u|>m\right\}
, its characteristic function
\displaystyle\chi_{m}:=\chi_{\Omega_{m}}
and the characteristic function of complement will be denoted by
\displaystyle\chi_{m}^{c}:=\chi_{\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\Omega_{m}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle u
be a solution to (
1.1
) when
\displaystyle q=p^{\ast}
. Then
\displaystyle u\in L^{k}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
for
\displaystyle p\leq k<p^{\ast}\left(\frac{n-1}{p\beta_{\lambda}}\right)
where,
\displaystyle\beta_{\lambda}
is as in (
3.2
). Hence,
\displaystyle u
has higher integrability than
\displaystyle p^{\ast}
.

Proof.

Note that since
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
by the Poincaré-Sobolev inequality in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
we know that
\displaystyle u\in L^{k}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
for
\displaystyle p\leq k\leq p^{\ast}
. Let us denote by
\displaystyle u_{m}:=\min(|u|,m)
for
\displaystyle m>0
. Testing equation (
1.1
) with
\displaystyle\varphi=u_{m}^{p\beta-p}u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
where
\displaystyle\beta>1
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-2}g(\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}\varphi)\,dV_{g}-\lambda\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|u|^{p-2}u\varphi\,dV_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|u|^{q-2}u\varphi\,dV_{g}.

(3.3)

Then on the LHS of (
3.3
) we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-2}g(\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}\varphi)\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}\left(\chi_{m}^{c}(p\beta-p+1)|u|^{p\beta-p}+\chi_{m}m^{p\beta-p}\right)\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{(p\beta-p+1)}{\beta^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}\left(\chi_{m}^{c}\beta^{p}|u|^{p\beta-p}+\chi_{m}m^{p\beta-p}\right)\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{(p\beta-p+1)}{\beta^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(u_{m}^{\beta-1}u\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}

(3.4)

where, we used the Bernoulli’s inequality
\displaystyle\beta^{p}=(1+(\beta-1))^{p}\geq 1+p(\beta-1)
. Again using Poincaré inequality in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{p\beta-p+1}{\beta^{p}}-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(u_{m}^{\beta-1}u\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}\leq\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|u|^{q}u_{m}^{p\beta-p}\,dV_{g}.

(3.5)

Therefore, on the RHS of (
3.5
) for any
\displaystyle m>m_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|u|^{q}u_{m}^{p\beta-p}\,dV_{g}\leq m_{0}^{p\beta-p}\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}^{c}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}+\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}}|u|^{q}u_{m}^{p\beta-p}\,dV_{g}.

(3.6)

Using Holder’s inequality followed by Sobolev Inequality on the second term on RHS of (
3.6
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}}|u|^{q}u_{m}^{p\beta-p}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}\right)^{1-\frac{p}{q}}\left(\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}}\left(u_{m}^{\beta-1}|u|\right)^{q}\,dV_{g}\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq S\left(\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}\right)^{1-\frac{p}{q}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(u_{m}^{\beta-1}u\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{p\beta-p+1}{\beta^{p}}-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(u_{m}^{\beta-1}u\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}

(3.7)

by choosing
\displaystyle m_{0}
sufficiently large depending on
\displaystyle\beta
. Therefore, combining (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.1
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{p\beta-p+1}{\beta^{p}}-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(u_{m}^{\beta-1}u\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}\leq 2m_{0}^{p\beta-p}\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}^{c}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}

(3.8)

followed by an application of Sobolev inequality and letting
\displaystyle m\to\infty
we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{p\beta-p+1}{\beta^{p}}-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}|u|^{\beta q}\,dV_{g}\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}\leq Cm_{0}^{p\beta-p}\int_{\Omega_{m_{0}}^{c}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}.

(3.9)

Now, note that making the change of variable
\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta=\frac{n-1}{p\alpha}
we have

\frac{p\beta-p+1}{\beta^{p}}=\frac{\alpha^{p-1}(n-1-(p-1)\alpha)}{\lambda_{\text{max}}}>\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{\text{max}}}

provided
\displaystyle\alpha\in(\beta_{\lambda},\alpha_{\lambda})
as in (
3.2
) i.e., if
\displaystyle\beta\in\left(\frac{n-1}{p\alpha_{\lambda}},\frac{n-1}{p\beta_{\lambda}}\right)
. This concludes the proof of higher integrability. ∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle u
be a solution of (
1.1
) then
\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
. Furthermore,
\displaystyle u(x)\to 0
and
\displaystyle|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}(x)\to 0
as
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)\to\infty
.

Proof.

The proof in the sub-critical case
\displaystyle q<p^{\ast}
follows in a similar way to the critical case. Note that in the critical case
\displaystyle q=p^{\ast}
we may rewrite equation (
1.1
) as

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}u=(\lambda+|u|^{p^{\ast}-p})|u|^{p-1}u

(3.10)

where,
\displaystyle\lambda+|u|^{p^{\ast}-p}\in L^{k}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
for some
\displaystyle\displaystyle k>\frac{n}{p}
by the higher integrability proved in Lemma-
3.1
.

Therefore, by usual Moser’s iteration (see
[
31
]
,
[
36
]
) we have the estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{B_{1}(0)}|u|\leq C\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p^{\ast}}(B_{2}(0))}

(3.11)

where, the constant
\displaystyle C
only depends on
\displaystyle\lVert\lambda+|u|^{p^{\ast}-p}\rVert_{L^{k}(B_{2})}\leq C_{1}\left(\lambda+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p^{\ast}\beta_{0}}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}^{p^{\ast}-p}\right)
for
\displaystyle\displaystyle 1<\beta_{0}<\frac{n-1}{p\beta_{\lambda}}
. Since, the equation (
3.10
) is invariant under isometries of the Hyperbolic space
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
then for any
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
consider
\displaystyle\tau_{x}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
such that
\displaystyle\tau_{x}(0)=x
. Then using (
3.11
) on
\displaystyle v:=(u\circ\tau_{x})
we have
\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{B_{1}(0)}|v|\leq C\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p^{\ast}}(B_{2}(0))}
or equivalently
\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{B_{1}(x)}|u|\leq C\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p^{\ast}}(B_{2}(x))}
for any
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Combining this with standard
\displaystyle C^{1,\sigma}
estimates (see
[
18
]
,
[
35
]
) we have
\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{B_{1/2}(x)}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}\leq C^{\prime}\sup_{B_{1}(x)}|u|\leq C^{\prime\prime}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p^{\ast}}(B_{2}(x))}
for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and the later goes to
\displaystyle 0
as
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)\to\infty
since
\displaystyle u\in L^{p^{\ast}}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
. ∎

3.2.
Super-solution and sub-solutions to the equation

In the following lemma we identify the candidates for sub(super)-solutions to our equation which will be used to establish the decay estimates.

Lemma 3.3
.

The function
\displaystyle\displaystyle v(x):=\left[\cosh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{m}\right)\right]^{-m\alpha_{\lambda}}
for
\displaystyle m\geq 2
is a super-solution to the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v-\lambda v^{p-1}\geq c_{\lambda}e^{-\frac{2}{m}\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}v^{p-1}

(3.12)

in
\displaystyle\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)>R_{\lambda}
for some
\displaystyle R_{\lambda}>0
and some positive constant
\displaystyle c_{\lambda}>0
.

Also,
\displaystyle\displaystyle v(x):=\left[\sinh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{2}\right)\right]^{-2\alpha_{\lambda}}
is a sub-solution to the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v-\lambda v^{p-1}\leq 0

(3.13)

for
\displaystyle\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)>R_{\lambda}
for some
\displaystyle R_{\lambda}>0
.

Proof.

Recall that in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
we have
\displaystyle\displaystyle t_{x}:=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)=\log\left(\frac{1+|x|}{1-|x|}\right)
or equivalently
\displaystyle r_{x}:=|x|=\tanh(t_{x}/2)
. Therefore we may express the Riemannian metric
\displaystyle g
in geodesic polar coordinates at the origin as

\displaystyle\displaystyle g=\frac{4dx^{2}}{(1-|x|^{2})^{2}}=\left(\frac{2}{1-r^{2}}\right)^{2}(dr^{2}+r^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}})=dt^{2}+(\sinh t)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}.

(3.14)

Then the
\displaystyle\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}
of a radial function
\displaystyle v(x)
, which we continue to denote as
\displaystyle v(t)
with
\displaystyle t=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)
, is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\sinh t)^{n-1}}\left((\sinh t)^{n-1}|v^{\prime}(t)|^{p-2}v^{\prime}(t)\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|v^{\prime}(t)|^{p-2}\left[(p-1)v^{\prime\prime}(t)+(n-1)\coth(t)v^{\prime}(t)\right].

(3.15)

We consider the function
\displaystyle\displaystyle v(t):=\left[\cosh\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)\right]^{-m\alpha_{\lambda}}
where,
\displaystyle m\geq 2
. Then by a direct computation of the derivatives we have

v^{\prime}(t)=-\alpha_{\lambda}\left[\cosh\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)\right]^{-m\alpha_{\lambda}}\tanh\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)=-\alpha_{\lambda}\tanh\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)v(t)

and

v^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\frac{\alpha_{\lambda}}{m}\left[\cosh\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)\right]^{-2}v(t)+\alpha_{\lambda}^{2}\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)v(t).

Substituting in the expression (
3.2
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-2}\tanh^{p}\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)\left[(p-1)\frac{\alpha_{\lambda}}{m}\left[\sinh\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)\right]^{-2}-(p-1)\alpha_{\lambda}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad\left.+(n-1)\alpha_{\lambda}\coth t\coth\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)\right]v^{p-1}(t).

(3.16)

Now we have the asymptotic expansions of the following functions and writing
\displaystyle e^{-t/m}=s
for brevity

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[\sinh\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)\right]^{-2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=4e^{-2t/m}\left(1+2e^{-2t/m}+O(e^{-4t/m})\right)=4s^{2}+O(s^{4})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\coth\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+2e^{-2t/m}+O(e^{-4t/m})=1+2s^{2}+O(s^{4})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\coth t

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+2e^{-2t}+O(e^{-4t})=1+2s^{2m}+O(s^{4m})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tanh^{p}\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1-2e^{-2t/m}+O(e^{-4t/m})\right)^{p}=1-2pe^{-2t/m}+O(e^{-4t/m})=1-2ps^{2}+O(s^{4}).

(3.17)

Substituting the asymptotic expressions in (
3.2
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-2}(1-2ps^{2}+O(s^{4}))\left[(n-1)\alpha_{\lambda}(1+2s^{2}+O(s^{4}))(1+2s^{2m}+O(s^{4m}))\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad\left.-(p-1)\alpha_{\lambda}^{2}+4(p-1)\frac{\alpha_{\lambda}}{m}(s^{2}+O(s^{4}))\right]v^{p-1}(t)

(3.18)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1-2ps^{2}+O(s^{4}))\left[\underbrace{\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}(n-1-(p-1)\alpha_{\lambda})}_{=:f(\alpha_{\lambda})=\lambda}+2\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}(n-1)s^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{4\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}}{m}(p-1)s^{2}+O(s^{4})\right]v^{p-1}(t)

(3.19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left[\lambda+2\left(\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}(n-1)-p\lambda\right)s^{2}+\frac{4\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}}{m}(p-1)s^{2}+O(s^{4})\right]v^{p-1}(t)

(3.20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lambda v^{p-1}(t)+2c_{\lambda}e^{-2t/m}\left(1+O(e^{-2t/m})\right)v^{p-1}(t)

(3.21)

where,
\displaystyle f(\alpha)
is as in (
3.1
), the constant
\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{\lambda}:=\left(\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}(n-1)-p\lambda\right)+\frac{2\alpha_{\lambda}^{p-1}}{m}(p-1)
is positive since,
\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}>\frac{n-1}{p}
and
\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda<\left(\frac{n-1}{p}\right)^{p}
. Therefore the function
\displaystyle\displaystyle v(x):=\left[\cosh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{m}\right)\right]^{-m\alpha_{\lambda}}
for
\displaystyle m\geq 2
is a super-solution to the equation (
3.12
) whenever
\displaystyle t=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)>R_{\lambda}
is chosen large enough.

By a similar calculation we also have
\displaystyle\displaystyle v(x):=\left[\sinh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{2}\right)\right]^{-2\alpha_{\lambda}}
is a sub-solution to (
3.13
) in
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)>R_{\lambda}
. ∎

3.3.
Proof of sharp decay

We first prove a weaker decay estimate for solutions to (
1.1
) which we will subsequently reuse to prove the sharp decay estimate. Before proceeding with the proof of decay estimates we recall the following Picone-inequality:

Lemma 3.4
.

(Picone Inequality) Let
\displaystyle p\in(1,n)
and
\displaystyle u,v\in W^{1,p}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
be two positive functions then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(u,v):=

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-2}g\left(\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}\left(u-\frac{v^{p}}{u^{p}}u\right)\right)+|\nabla_{g}v|_{g}^{p-2}g\left(\nabla_{g}v,\nabla_{g}\left(v-\frac{u^{p}}{v^{p}}v\right)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq C_{p}\min\left\{u^{p},v^{p}\right\}\left(|\nabla_{g}\log u|_{g}+|\nabla_{g}\log v|_{g}\right)^{p-2}|\nabla_{g}(\log u-\log v)|_{g}^{2}.

(3.22)

Proof.

Directly follows from the
\displaystyle p
-homogeneity and multiplying by the conformal factor in the Euclidean inequality from Lemma-3.1 in
[
39
]
when
\displaystyle 1<p<2
and Lemma-3.1 of
[
26
]
when
\displaystyle p>2
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let,
\displaystyle u
be a solution to (
1.1
). Then for each
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
such that
\displaystyle\lambda+\varepsilon<\lambda_{\text{max}}
there is a constant
\displaystyle C_{\lambda+\varepsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(x)\leq C_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\left[\cosh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{2}\right)\right]^{-2\alpha_{\lambda+\varepsilon}},\,\forall\,x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}.

(3.23)

Proof.

Recall from Lemma-
3.3
, the function
\displaystyle\displaystyle v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x):=C_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\left[\cosh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{2}\right)\right]^{-2\alpha_{\lambda+\varepsilon}}\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
is a super-solution to the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}-\lambda v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}^{p-1}\geq\varepsilon v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}^{p-1}.

(3.24)

Using Lemma-
3.2
we may choose
\displaystyle R_{\lambda+\varepsilon}>0
large enough such that
\displaystyle u^{q-p}(x)\leq\varepsilon
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{\lambda+\varepsilon}}
. Then
\displaystyle u
is a subsolution to the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}u-\lambda u^{p-1}\leq\varepsilon u^{p-1}.

(3.25)

Finally we may fix
\displaystyle C_{\lambda+\varepsilon}>0
large enough such that
\displaystyle u\leq v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}
in
\displaystyle B_{R_{\lambda+\varepsilon}}
. Let
\displaystyle\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
be a non-negative test function such that
\displaystyle\eta\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle B_{R}
and
\displaystyle\eta\equiv 0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus B_{R+1}
where,
\displaystyle R>R_{\lambda+\varepsilon}
. Then testing (
3.25
) with
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}:=\eta u^{1-p}(u^{p}-v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}^{p})_{+}
and (
3.24
) with
\displaystyle\varphi_{2}:=\eta v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}^{1-p}(u^{p}-v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}^{p})_{+}
which are admissible test functions supported in
\displaystyle B_{R+1}\setminus B_{R_{\lambda+\varepsilon}}
and subtracting we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}\cap\left\{u\geq v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\right\}}\eta\mathscr{B}\left(u,v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\right)\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}\cap\left\{u\geq v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\right\}}\eta(u^{q-p}-\varepsilon)(u^{p}-v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}^{p})_{+}\,dV_{g}+\int_{B_{R+1}\setminus B_{R}}|\nabla_{g}\eta|_{g}\left(|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-1}u+|\nabla_{g}\log v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}|_{g}^{p-1}u^{p}\right)\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R+1}\setminus B_{R}}|\nabla_{g}\eta|_{g}\left(|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-1}u+|\nabla_{g}\log v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}|_{g}^{p-1}u^{p}\right)\,dV_{g}

(3.26)

where,
\displaystyle\mathscr{B}\left(u,v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\right)
is as in (
3.4
). On the RHS of (
3.3
) we use the estimates
\displaystyle|\nabla_{g}\eta|_{g}\leq c
(for all
\displaystyle R>0
),

\int_{B_{R+1}\setminus B_{R}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-1}u\,dV_{g}\leq\lVert\nabla_{g}u\rVert_{L^{p}(B_{R+1}\setminus B_{R})}^{p-1}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}(B_{R+1}\setminus B_{R})}\to 0

as
\displaystyle R\to\infty
and
\displaystyle|\nabla_{g}\log v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}|_{g}\leq A_{\lambda+\epsilon}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
.
Using the Picone inequality (
3.4
) on the LHS of (
3.3
) and letting
\displaystyle R\to\infty
on the RHS of (
3.3
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{p}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{\lambda+\varepsilon}}\cap\left\{u\geq v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\right\}}v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}^{p}\left(|\nabla_{g}\log u|_{g}+|\nabla_{g}\log v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}|_{g}\right)^{p-2}|\nabla_{g}(\log u-\log v_{\lambda+\varepsilon})|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{g}\leq 0.

Since,
\displaystyle\nabla_{g}v_{\lambda+\varepsilon}\neq 0

Now we proceed with the proof of sharp decay estimate and gradient estimate for a solution of (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Let,
\displaystyle u
be a solution of (
1.1
). Then there are constants
\displaystyle C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
\displaystyle m\geq 2
(chosen large enough depending on the exponent
\displaystyle q
) such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{1}\left[\sinh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{2}\right)\right]^{-2\alpha_{\lambda}}\leq u(x)\leq C_{2}\left[\cosh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{m}\right)\right]^{-m\alpha_{\lambda}},\,\forall\,x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus B_{\overline{R}}

(3.28)

for some
\displaystyle\overline{R}>0
.

Proof.

We prove the upper bound. The proof of lower bound is much simpler and follows similarly.

Recall from Lemma-
3.3
that
\displaystyle\displaystyle v(x):=C_{2}\left[\cosh\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}{m}\right)\right]^{-m\alpha_{\lambda}}
is a super-solution to the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v-\lambda v^{p-1}\geq c_{\lambda}e^{-\frac{2}{m}\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x)}v^{p-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{\lambda}}

(3.29)

for
\displaystyle C_{2}>0
and
\displaystyle m\geq 2
.

Let
\displaystyle\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
be a non-negative test function such that
\displaystyle\eta\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle B_{R}
and
\displaystyle\eta\equiv 0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus B_{R+1}
where,
\displaystyle R>\overline{R}>R_{\lambda}
(
\displaystyle\overline{R}
to be determined later).

Then testing equation (
1.1
) with
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}:=\eta u^{1-p}(u^{p}-v^{p})_{+}
and equation (
3.29
) with
\displaystyle\varphi_{2}:=\eta v^{1-p}(u^{p}-v^{p})_{+}
and subtracting we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}\cap\left\{u\geq v\right\}}\eta\mathscr{B}(u,v)\,dV_{g}

where,
\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(u,v)
is as in (
3.4

3.5

\displaystyle\overline{R}>0

\displaystyle u\leq v
in
\displaystyle B_{\overline{R}}

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq 0

\displaystyle I_{2}\to 0
as
\displaystyle R\to\infty

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{p}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus B_{\overline{R}}\,\cap\,\left\{u\geq v\right\}}v^{p}\left(|\nabla_{g}\log u|_{g}+|\nabla_{g}\log v|_{g}\right)^{p-2}|\nabla_{g}(\log u-\log v)|_{g}^{2}\,dV_{g}\leq 0.

\displaystyle\nabla_{g}v\neq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}

Theorem 3.8
.

Let,
\displaystyle\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\text{max}})
and
\displaystyle v
be a
\displaystyle C^{1}
solution of the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}v=\lambda v^{p-1}

(3.44)

in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
such that there exists positive constant
\displaystyle C>0
and a point
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{\xi}\leq v\leq CE_{\xi}

(3.45)

where,
\displaystyle E_{\xi}(x):=\left(\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{|x-\xi|^{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{\lambda}}
. Then
\displaystyle\exists\,c_{0}\in[1,C]
such that
\displaystyle v\equiv c_{0}E_{\xi}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
. This in particular implies
\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla_{g}v|_{g}}{v}=\frac{|\nabla_{g}E_{\xi}|_{g}}{E_{\xi}}\equiv\alpha_{\lambda}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

Proof.

It is more convenient to prove the result in the upper half-space model of the Hyperbolic space. Using an orthogonal transformation in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
about origin without loss of generality we may assume
\displaystyle\xi=-e_{n}
. Recall that the extended Mobius map
\displaystyle\Phi:\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\cup\{\infty\}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{B}^{n}}
defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi(x):=\left(\frac{2x_{1}}{|x+e_{n}|^{2}},\cdots,\frac{2x_{n-1}}{|x+e_{n}|^{2}},\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{|x+e_{n}|^{2}}\right)\text{ and }\Phi(\infty):=-e_{n}

(3.46)

defines an isometry between the Poincaré ball model
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
and the upper half-space model
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n}
of the Hyperbolic space. In fact
\displaystyle\Phi
is the inversion map w.r.t. the sphere centered at
\displaystyle-e_{n}
and radius
\displaystyle\sqrt{2}
given by
\displaystyle\Phi(x)=-e_{n}+\frac{2(x+e_{n})}{|x+e_{n}|^{2}}
and it satisfies
\displaystyle\Phi\circ\Phi=\operatorname{Id}
.

Let,
\displaystyle u:=v\circ\Phi
. Then the problem can be equivalently posed in upper half-space model as

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u=\lambda u^{p-1}

(3.47)

and satisfies the bounds

\displaystyle\displaystyle x_{n}^{\alpha_{\lambda}}\leq u(x)\leq Cx_{n}^{\alpha_{\lambda}}.

(3.48)

We claim that
\displaystyle u(x)=c_{0}x_{n}^{\alpha_{\lambda}}
for some
\displaystyle c_{0}\in[1,C]
, from which our claim about the eigenfunction in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
model will follow. We start by showing that
\displaystyle u
has one dimensional symmetry in the
\displaystyle e_{n}
-direction. This then reduces the problem to showing uniqueness (up to constant multiples) of solutions to an ordinary differential equation.

Step-I:
Note that due to the Hyperbolic isometry invariance and homogeneity of the equation (
3.44
) by applying the standard
\displaystyle C^{1,\sigma}
estimate and the Harnack inequality we have the global gradient estimate

|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}(x)\leq Au(x)

for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}

For
\displaystyle x_{0}=(x_{0}^{\prime},0)\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
\displaystyle R>0
, let us denote the Hyperbolic half-spaces

H_{R}(x_{0}):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:|x-x_{0}|<R,\,x_{n}>0\right\}

and its boundary the orthogonal sphere (orthogonal to the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}

We start by noting that

Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{\tau}=\lambda u_{\tau}^{p-1}

along with the bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{R^{2}x_{n}}{|x-x_{0}|^{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{\lambda}}\leq u_{\tau}(x)\leq C\left(\frac{R^{2}x_{n}}{|x-x_{0}|^{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{\lambda}}.

(3.50)

Comparing (
3.48

\displaystyle u(x)<u_{\tau}(x)
in the set
\displaystyle H_{R^{\prime}}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

We test equations (
3.47

. From the strong minimum-principle (see Lemma-
5.7
) we conclude
\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{\infty}\equiv 0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
i.e.,
\displaystyle w_{\infty}(t)=ct^{\alpha}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
for some
\displaystyle c>0
(recall
\displaystyle\alpha>0
from (
3.55
) so that
\displaystyle w_{\infty}
has no critical points in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
). But comparing with the global bounds (
3.53

\displaystyle w_{\infty}
we must have
\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{\lambda}
. By a similar consideration for the supremum
\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\alpha=\beta=\alpha_{\lambda}

Remark 3.10
.

For
\displaystyle\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\text{max}})

\displaystyle\xi\in\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{B}^{n}(=\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
has at least two distinct classes (upto constant multiple) of corresponding positive eigenfunctions solving (
3.44

\displaystyle\displaystyle E(x,\xi)=\left(\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{|x-\xi|^{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{\lambda}}\text{ and }F(x,\xi)=\left(\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{|x-\xi|^{2}}\right)^{\beta_{\lambda}}

(3.59)

where,
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}
and
\displaystyle\beta_{\lambda}
are as in (

). The important distinction between them is that
\displaystyle\lVert\nabla_{g}E(.,\xi)\rVert_{L^{p}(\overline{H})}<+\infty
for every Hyperbolic closed half-space
\displaystyle\overline{H}

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}

\displaystyle\overline{H}

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla_{g}F(.,\xi)\rVert_{L^{p}(\overline{H})}=+\infty

\displaystyle\overline{H}

\displaystyle E(.,\xi)
in each half-space
\displaystyle\overline{H}
away from the ‘singularity’ at
\displaystyle\xi

We only state an extract of the representation theorem in the linear case
\displaystyle p=2
due to Helgason
[
21
]
and Minemura
[
25
]
relevant to our particular case. We denote the normalized measure on
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with
\displaystyle d\sigma
, the space of all analytic functions on
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
by
\displaystyle C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
and its dual the space of analytic functionals (hyperfunctions) by
\displaystyle C^{-\omega}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. Let us denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}:=-\Delta^{\mathbb{B}^{n}}-\lambda
.

Theorem 3.11
.

The Poisson-Helgason transform
\displaystyle P_{\lambda}:C^{-\omega}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\to C^{\omega}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(T)(x):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}E(x,\xi)T(\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi),\text{ for }T\in C^{-\omega}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})

(3.60)

is an isomorphism between
\displaystyle C^{-\omega}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
, with inverse
\displaystyle B_{\lambda}
given by a generalized boundary value. For
\displaystyle v\in\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
the hyperfunction
\displaystyle B_{\lambda}(v)
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left<B_{\lambda}(v),\varphi\right>:=c_{n,\lambda}\lim\limits_{r\to 1^{-}}(1-r^{2})^{\alpha_{\lambda}-(n-1)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}v(r\xi)\varphi(\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi),\text{ for }\varphi\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})

(3.61)

where,
\displaystyle c_{n,\lambda}=\frac{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\alpha_{\lambda}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\alpha_{\lambda}-\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}
.

For the sake of completeness we include a short proof of the minimality of
\displaystyle E(.,\xi)
relative to
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
in the linear case
\displaystyle p=2
. Let us consider the following weighted analogue of a Hardy Space

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{B}^{n}):=\left\{f\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{B}^{n}):\sup_{r\in[0,1)}(1-r^{2})^{\alpha_{\lambda}-(n-1)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}|f(r\xi)|\,d\sigma(\xi)<+\infty\right\}.

(3.62)

We note that if
\displaystyle v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{B}^{n})\cap\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
, then the set of Borel measures

d\mu_{r}(\xi):=c_{n,\lambda}(1-r^{2})^{\alpha_{\lambda}-(n-1)}v(r\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi)\in M(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})

for
\displaystyle r\in[0,1)
has uniformly bounded total variation norm. Extracting a
\displaystyle w^{\ast}
-convergent subsequence
\displaystyle\mu_{r_{j}}\overset{w^{\ast}}{\rightharpoonup}\mu
as
\displaystyle r_{j}\to 1^{-}
we note that in (
3.61
) we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left<B_{\lambda}(v),\varphi\right>=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\varphi\,d\mu,\,\forall\,\varphi\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}).

(3.63)

Thus (
3.63
) extends by density to all
\displaystyle\varphi\in C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
and hence
\displaystyle B_{\lambda}(v)=\mu\in M(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
is given by a Borel measure. This establishes an isomorphism of
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{B}^{n})\cap\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
and
\displaystyle M(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. In particular we note that positive functions in
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{B}^{n})\cap\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
corresponds to non-negative measures in
\displaystyle M(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. However we note that if
\displaystyle v\in\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
is a positive eigenfunction then by the mean-value equality in Hyperbolic space (see
[
34
]
, Theorem-5.5.5) we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}v(r\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi)=g_{\alpha_{\lambda}}(r\eta)v(0)

(3.64)

where,
\displaystyle r\in[0,1),\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\displaystyle g_{\alpha_{\lambda}}\in\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
is the unique bounded radial eigenfunction with
\displaystyle g_{\alpha_{\lambda}}(0)=1
given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{\alpha_{\lambda}}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}E(x,\eta^{\prime})\,d\sigma(\eta^{\prime})

(3.65)

with asymptotic boundary behavior (see
[
34
]
, Theorem-5.5.7 and Corollary-5.5.8) given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{\alpha_{\lambda}}(r\eta)\approx(1-r^{2})^{n-1-\alpha_{\lambda}}.

(3.66)

This in particular implies that
\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}=\sup_{r\in[0,1)}(1-r^{2})^{\alpha_{\lambda}-(n-1)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}v(r\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi)\leq C^{\prime}v(0)
, i.e., positive eigenfunctions are in
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
and hence have non-negative measure for a generalized boundary value. As a consequence if
\displaystyle v\in\operatorname{Ker}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\right)
is a positive eigenfunction satisfying the estimate from above

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0\leq v(x)\leq CE(x,\xi)

(3.67)

for some
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, then

\left<B_{\lambda}(v),\varphi\right>=c_{n,\lambda}\lim\limits_{r\to 1^{-}}(1-r^{2})^{\alpha_{\lambda}-(n-1)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}v(r\eta)\varphi(\eta)\,d\sigma(\eta)=0

for all
\displaystyle\varphi\in C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
with
\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)\cap\{\xi\}=\emptyset
. Thus
\displaystyle B_{\lambda}(v)
is a non-negative measure supported at
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and hence must be a dirac mass,
\displaystyle B_{\lambda}(v)=c_{0}\delta_{\xi}
for some
\displaystyle c_{0}\geq 0
. Therefore,
\displaystyle v=P_{\lambda}(c_{0}\delta_{\xi})=c_{0}E(.,\xi).

4.
Proof of symmetry

We will use the Alexandrov Moving Plane method to prove symmetry of positive energy solutions to (
1.1
) in the Poincaré ball model
\displaystyle(\mathbb{B}^{n},g)
of the Hyperbolic space. Recall that the Hyperbolic reflections are isometries of the Hyperbolic space
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
given by spherical inversions (or reflections) with respect to generalized Euclidean spheres that are orthogonal to
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. In particular these are either orthogonal Euclidean spheres
\displaystyle S(a,r):=\{x:|x-a|=r\}
where
\displaystyle|a|>1
and
\displaystyle r^{2}=|a|^{2}-1
or are Euclidean hyperplanes passing through the origin of
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

Given a direction
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
we perform the moving plane method in direction
\displaystyle\xi
by reflecting along the family of orthogonal spheres parametrized by
\displaystyle S\left(\mu^{-1}\xi,\sqrt{|\mu|^{-2}-1}\right)
for
\displaystyle\mu\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}
. The limiting case
\displaystyle\mu=0
corresponds to reflecting along the Euclidean hyperplane passing through
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and orthogonal to
\displaystyle\xi
. When
\displaystyle\mu\neq 0
we denote the reflection across
\displaystyle S\left(\mu^{-1}\xi,\sqrt{|\mu|^{-2}-1}\right)
by
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu,\xi}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu,\xi}(x):=\mu^{-1}\xi+\frac{\left(|\mu|^{-2}-1\right)\left(x-\mu^{-1}\xi\right)}{|x-\mu^{-1}\xi|^{2}}

(4.1)

and when
\displaystyle\mu=0
we denote

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{0,\xi}(x):=x-2(x,\xi)\xi.

(4.2)

Thus
\displaystyle S\left(\mu^{-1}\xi,\sqrt{|\mu|^{-2}-1}\right)
separates
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
into two isometric connected components (half-spaces). We denote by
\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mu}(\xi)
the half-space in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
containing
\displaystyle\xi
in its boundary shared with
\displaystyle\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{B}^{n}
(
\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
). We also denote the part of the sphere
\displaystyle S\left(\mu^{-1}\xi,\sqrt{|\mu|^{-2}-1}\right)
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
as
\displaystyle T_{\mu}(\xi):=S\left(\mu^{-1}\xi,\sqrt{|\mu|^{-2}-1}\right)\cap\mathbb{B}^{n}
. In this notation we have
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu,\xi}\left(\Sigma_{\mu}(\xi)\right)=\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{\mu}(\xi)}=\Sigma_{-\mu}(-\xi)
.

Before proceeding with the proof of symmetry we need the following simple lemma comparing the image and object distances of a reflection from an observer in Hyperbolic space. The proof is similar to that of the same fact in the Euclidean space.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle T
be a generalized orthogonal sphere in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
which splits the space into two isometric components (half-spaces)
\displaystyle\Sigma
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Sigma}
. Let
\displaystyle E
(observer) be a fixed point in
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
. Let
\displaystyle P
(object) be a point in
\displaystyle\Sigma
and we denote its reflection in
\displaystyle T
as
\displaystyle\sigma_{T}(P)=P^{\prime}
(image). Then
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,E)\leq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},E)
with equality if and only if
\displaystyle E
lies on
\displaystyle T
.

Proof.

The proof can be seen as a corollary of the Pythagoras’ theorem (or more generally the law of cosines in Hyperbolic space, see Theorem-3.5.3 in
[
28
]
). Let
\displaystyle\ell
denote the Hyperbolic line passing through
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
which intersects
\displaystyle T
at
\displaystyle O
. Then clearly
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,O)=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},O)
i.e.,
\displaystyle O
is the mid-point of
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
on the line
\displaystyle\ell
. It is easier to visualize it after applying a Hyperbolic isometry to bring
\displaystyle O
to the origin of
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Then in this new frame,
\displaystyle T
is a Euclidean hyperplane passing through
\displaystyle O
splitting
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
into two half-spaces
\displaystyle\Sigma
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Sigma}
.
\displaystyle\ell
is an Euclidean line passing through
\displaystyle O
and orthogonal to
\displaystyle T
. Let
\displaystyle F
be the orthogonal projection of
\displaystyle E
on the line
\displaystyle\ell
(i.e.,
\displaystyle F
is intersection of
\displaystyle\ell
with the unique Hyperbolic line
\displaystyle\ell_{1}
through
\displaystyle E
which is orthogonal to
\displaystyle\ell
). Then applying Hyperbolic Pythagoras’ theorem to the triangles
\displaystyle\Delta PFE
and
\displaystyle\Delta P^{\prime}FE
respectively we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\cosh(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,E))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\cosh(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,F))\cosh(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(E,F))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\cosh(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},E))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\cosh(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},F))\cosh(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(E,F))

respectively. Therefore the inequality
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,E)\leq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},E)
will follow if we see that
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,F)\leq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},F)
. Note that the orthogonal projection
\displaystyle F
of the point
\displaystyle E
on the line
\displaystyle\ell
must lie on the same half-space
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
as
\displaystyle E
(the unique Hyperbolic line
\displaystyle\ell_{1}
passing through
\displaystyle E\in\overline{\Sigma}
which is orthogonal to
\displaystyle\ell
must be entirely contained in
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
). Since,
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},F)=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},O)+\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(O,F)
(as
\displaystyle O=T\cap\ell
lies in between the points
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Sigma}
and
\displaystyle F\in\overline{\Sigma}
on the line
\displaystyle\ell
) and
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},O)=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,O)
, using triangle inequality we get

\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},F)=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,O)+\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(O,F)\geq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,F)

and consequently
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P,E)\leq\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(P^{\prime},E)
. Equality holds if and only if
\displaystyle F=O
i.e.,
\displaystyle E
lies on
\displaystyle T
. This completes the proof of the claim.
∎

Step-III:
Finally applying another Hyperbolic translation

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=0

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}(e_{1})

\displaystyle e_{j}
-directions for
\displaystyle j=2,\cdots,n

\displaystyle u\equiv(u\circ\sigma_{0,e_{j}}
) in
\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}(e_{j})

\displaystyle u
is non-decreasing in direction of the moving plane we must have
\displaystyle u(0)=\sup_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}u=M
(say). Since,
\displaystyle u
is symmetric about the
\displaystyle n
orthogonal planes passing through
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{B}^{n}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle u(x)=u(-x)
by successively reflecting along these
\displaystyle n
planes. We claim that for any direction
\displaystyle\overline{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, the moving plane in the direction of
\displaystyle\overline{e}
must stop only after it reaches the origin
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Otherwise, if we have
\displaystyle u\equiv(u\circ\sigma_{\mu,\overline{e}})
in
\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mu}(\overline{e})
for some
\displaystyle\mu\neq 0

\displaystyle T_{\mu}(\overline{e})
stops at an optimal position before reaching
\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle 0
about
\displaystyle T_{\mu}(\overline{e})

\displaystyle u\equiv M
along the line segment joining
\displaystyle 0
and its reflection
\displaystyle O_{1}=\sigma_{\mu,\overline{e}}(0)
. Again due to the point symmetry about
\displaystyle 0
we must have
\displaystyle u(-O_{1})=M
. By reflecting
\displaystyle-O_{1}
about
\displaystyle T_{\mu}(\overline{e})
we have
\displaystyle u\equiv M
on the line segment joining
\displaystyle-O_{1}
and its reflection
\displaystyle O_{2}=\sigma_{\mu,\overline{e}}(-O_{1})
. If we denote
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,O_{1})=2\ell

\displaystyle O_{k}:=\sigma_{\mu,\overline{e}}(-O_{k-1})
we get
\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,O_{k})=2k\ell
. Therefore,
\displaystyle u\equiv M
along the entire line passing through
\displaystyle 0
in direction
\displaystyle\overline{e}

\displaystyle u
. Therefore,
\displaystyle u\equiv(u\circ\sigma_{0,\overline{e}})
for any
\displaystyle\overline{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\displaystyle u
is a radially symmetric function in
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{n}
with respect to the origin
\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle u
is strictly decreasing in radial direction (i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{u}=\{0\}

\displaystyle u
or argue as in beginning of Step-II to conclude that

cannot have a bounded connected component since, level sets of
\displaystyle u
are spheres. This completes the proof of Hyperbolic symmetry of
\displaystyle u
. Finally we note that if we denote
\displaystyle u(x)=\Phi(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(0,x))
then
\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(t)<0

\displaystyle t>0

\displaystyle\Phi(t)
is smooth for
\displaystyle t>0

\displaystyle\infty

Lemma 5.5

Let,
\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}

(1)

(2)

6.
Existence and nonexistence

In this section we will briefly look in to the question of existence and non existence of solution for the problem (
1.1
). In view of the symmetry result Theorem-
1.1
it is enough to work in the subspace of
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
consisting of radial functions.

Fix a point
\displaystyle O\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{rad}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
be the closure of
\displaystyle C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
functions which are radial with respect to
\displaystyle O
. Let
\displaystyle u(x)=v(\operatorname{dist}_{g}(O,x))
be a radial
\displaystyle C^{1}_{c}
function, then for
\displaystyle t>0

|v(t)|=\left|-\int_{t}^{\infty}v^{\prime}(s)\,ds\right|\leq\left(\int_{t}^{\infty}|v^{\prime}(s)|^{p}(\sinh s)^{n-1}\,ds\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(\sinh s\right)^{-\frac{n-1}{p-1}}\,ds\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}.

Hence for
\displaystyle R>0
there exists a dimensional constant
\displaystyle C_{R}>0
such that

|u(x)|\leq C_{R}\|\nabla_{g}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus B_{R}(O))}\;e^{-\frac{n-1}{p}\operatorname{dist}_{g}(O,x)},\;x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus B_{R}(O)

(6.1)

holds for all
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{rad}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. As an immediate corollary we get

Lemma 6.1
.

The embedding
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{rad}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is compact for any
\displaystyle p<q<p^{\ast}
.

Proof follows using standard arguments (see
[
5
]
, Theorem-3.1).

Using the above Compactness lemma we have the existence in the subcritical range
\displaystyle p<q<p^{\ast}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle p<q<p^{\ast}
, then the problem (
1.1
) admits a solution.

Proof.

By standard arguments it is enough to show that
\displaystyle S_{\lambda,q}
is achieved in (
1.2
) by a function
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. Note that by symmetrization arguments
[
4
, Chapter 7]

S_{\lambda,q}=\inf\limits_{u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),u\not=0}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}-\lambda|u|^{p}\,dV_{g}}{\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}\right)^{p/q}}=\inf\limits_{u\in\mathcal{D}_{rad}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),u\not=0}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}-\lambda|u|^{p}\,dV_{g}}{\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}\right)^{p/q}}.

Let,
\displaystyle u_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{rad}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
be a minimizing sequence for the above infimum. With out loss of generality we may assume
\displaystyle u_{n}\geq 0
and using the Ekeland variational principle,
\displaystyle u_{n}
is a Palais-Smale sequence and
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p}-\lambda u_{n}^{p}\,dV_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}^{q}\,dV_{g}
. Thus,
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p}-\lambda u_{n}^{p}\,dV_{g}\rightarrow\left(S_{\lambda,q}\right)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}
. Since
\displaystyle\lambda<\left(\frac{n-1}{p}\right)^{p}
, this implies
\displaystyle u_{n}
is bounded in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{rad}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. Passing to a subsequence if necessary we may assume
\displaystyle u_{n}
converges weakly to some
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{D}_{rad}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.
Passing to a further subsequence we may assume
\displaystyle u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
\displaystyle L^{q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
(using the previous Lemma-
6.1
), pointwise a.e.,
\displaystyle u_{n}^{p-1}\rightharpoonup u^{p-1}
in
\displaystyle L^{\frac{p}{p-1}}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and

\displaystyle|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p-2}\nabla_{g}u_{n}\rightharpoonup V
for some vector field
\displaystyle V\in L^{\frac{p}{p-1}}
. Since
\displaystyle u_{n}
is a Palais-Smale sequence we have for for all

\displaystyle\phi\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
,

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left[|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p-2}g(\nabla_{g}u_{n},\nabla_{g}\phi)-\lambda u_{n}^{p-1}\phi\right]\,dV_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}^{q-1}\phi\,dV_{g}+o(1),\;{\text{a}s}\;n\rightarrow\infty.

Passing to the limit as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
we see that

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left[g(V,\nabla_{g}\phi)-\lambda u^{p-1}\phi\right]\,dV_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u^{q-1}\phi\,dV_{g}.

It follows from
[
6
]
that
\displaystyle V=|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p-2}\nabla_{g}u
and hence
\displaystyle u
solves
\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u-\lambda u^{p-1}=u^{q-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{H}^{n}
and in particular
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}-\lambda u^{p}\,dV_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u^{q}\,dV_{g}
. Moreover
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u|^{q}\,dV_{g}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u_{n}|^{q}\,dV_{g}=\left(S_{\lambda,q}\right)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}.
Thus
\displaystyle u
is a non-negative extremal.
∎

Next we consider the critical case
\displaystyle q=p^{\ast}
. The crucial step is to obtain higher integrability, see for example

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\displaystyle S_{p}
be the best constant in the Euclidean Sobolev inequality defined by

\displaystyle S_{p}=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{p}dx:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|u|^{p^{\ast}}dx=1,u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right\}

We have the following Sobolev inequality (see

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{p}\geq S_{p}\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|u|^{p^{\ast}}\,dV_{g}\right)^{p/p^{\ast}},\forall\,u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

(6.2)

Hence we have
\displaystyle S_{\lambda,p^{\ast}}\leq S_{p}
. Choose
\displaystyle q_{n}\in(p,p^{\ast})
such that
\displaystyle q_{n}\rightarrow p^{\ast}
as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}-\lambda u_{n}^{p-1}=S_{\lambda,q_{n}}u_{n}^{q-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

It is easy to see that
\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}S_{\lambda,q_{n}}\leq S_{\lambda,p^{\ast}}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}S_{\lambda,q_{n}}:=\Lambda_{0}\leq S_{\lambda,p^{\ast}}

\displaystyle u_{n}
is bounded in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{rad}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

\displaystyle u_{n}
, and a
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{rad}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

\displaystyle u_{n}\rightarrow u
pointwise a.e.,
\displaystyle u_{n}\rightharpoonup u

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1,p}_{rad}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u-\lambda u^{p-2}=\Lambda_{0}u^{q-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{H}^{n}

\displaystyle u\not=0
and hence a constant multiple of
\displaystyle u

Using the pointwise bound (

as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
for any
\displaystyle R>0.
Thus,
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}^{q_{n}}\,dV_{g}\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u^{p^{\ast}}\,dV_{g}
as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
if we have for some
\displaystyle q>p^{\ast}
and a
\displaystyle R>0
such that
\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(O)}u_{n}^{q}\,dV_{g}\;\leq C,\forall\,n
. We will show that this is indeed true if we assume
\displaystyle S_{\lambda,p^{\ast}}<S_{p}
.

Let us assume
\displaystyle S_{\lambda,p^{\ast}}<S_{p}
. Choose
\displaystyle\Lambda\in(\Lambda_{0},S_{p})
. Fix
\displaystyle R>0
and a radial function
\displaystyle\phi\in C_{c}^{1}(B_{R}(O))
such that
\displaystyle\phi\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle B_{R/2}(O)
. Let
\displaystyle k>1
to be chosen later. Testing the PDE for
\displaystyle u_{n}
with
\displaystyle\phi^{p}u_{n}^{k}
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left[|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p-2}g(\nabla_{g}u_{n},\nabla_{g}(\phi^{p}u_{n}^{k}))-\lambda u_{n}^{p-1+k}\phi^{p}\right]\,dV_{g}=S_{\lambda,q_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}^{q_{n}-1+k}\phi^{p}\,dV_{g}.

Since
\displaystyle u_{n}
is bounded in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, the second term on the LHS is uniformly bounded if
\displaystyle p-1+k<p^{\ast}
.
Expanding the first term on LHS, we get

k\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p}\phi^{p}u_{n}^{k-1}\,dV_{g}+\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p-2}g(\nabla_{g}u_{n},\nabla_{g}\phi)(p\phi^{p-1}u_{n}^{k})\,dV_{g}.

Since the integrand of the second term is supported in the annulus with radius
\displaystyle R/2
and
\displaystyle R
, the radial bound (
6.1
) implies that
\displaystyle u_{n}
is uniformly bounded in this annulus. Thus the second term is bounded by
\displaystyle C\int_{B_{R}(O)}|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p-1}\,dV_{g}
which is uniformly bounded. Now using Young’s inequality we see that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{kp^{p}}{(k+p-1)^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(\phi u_{n}^{\frac{k+p-1}{p}}\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq k\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p}\phi^{p}u_{n}^{k-1}\,dV_{g}+C\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left(|\nabla_{g}\phi|_{g}^{p}u_{n}^{p+k-1}+|\nabla_{g}u_{n}|_{g}^{p-1}u_{n}^{k}|\nabla_{g}\phi|_{g}\right)\,dV_{g}.

The last integral is uniformly bounded as argued before if
\displaystyle k-1+p\leq p^{\ast}
, thus substituting back these informations we get the existence of a constant
\displaystyle C>0
such that

\frac{kp^{p}}{(k+p-1)^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(\phi u_{n}^{\frac{k+p-1}{p}}\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}\leq C+\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}^{q_{n}-1+k}\phi^{p}\,dV_{g}.

Now using (
6.2
) the last term can be estimated as follows,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}^{q_{n}-1+k}\phi^{p}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left(u_{n}^{\frac{p-1+k}{p}}\phi\right)^{p}(u_{n}^{q_{n}-p})\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\Lambda\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left(u_{n}^{\frac{p-1+k}{p}}\phi\right)^{q_{n}}\,dV_{g}\right)^{\frac{p}{q_{n}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}u_{n}^{q_{n}}\,dV_{g}\right)^{\frac{q_{n}-p}{q_{n}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq|V_{g}(B(O,R))|^{\frac{p^{\ast}-q_{n}}{p^{\ast}}}\Lambda\left(\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left(u_{n}^{\frac{p-1+k}{p}}\phi\right)^{p^{\ast}}\,dV_{g}\right)^{\frac{p}{p^{\ast}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq|V_{g}(B(O,R))|^{\frac{p^{\ast}-q_{n}}{p^{\ast}}}\frac{\Lambda}{S_{p}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(\phi u_{n}^{\frac{k+p-1}{p}}\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(\phi u_{n}^{\frac{k+p-1}{p}}\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}

as
\displaystyle q_{n}\to p^{\ast}
for all
\displaystyle n
large and some
\displaystyle\alpha\in(0,1)
. Choosing
\displaystyle k
close to 1 such that
\displaystyle\frac{kp^{p}}{(k+p-1)^{p}}>1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
we get
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{g}\left(\phi u_{n}^{\frac{k+p-1}{p}}\right)\right|_{g}^{p}\,dV_{g}\leq C
for all
\displaystyle n
large and hence the higher integrability follows by Sobolev inequality (
6.2
). This completes the proof.
∎

When
\displaystyle p=2
the precise existence and nonexistence result was proved in
[
24
]
. When
\displaystyle p\not=2
we have the following existence theorem in the critical case.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\displaystyle 1<p<n
such that
\displaystyle n>p^{2}
. When
\displaystyle p<2
,
\displaystyle n>3p-2
,
\displaystyle\lambda>0
and
\displaystyle q=p^{\ast}
then the problem (
1.1
) has a non zero solution.

Using standard test functions we can see that in this case
\displaystyle S_{\lambda,p^{\ast}}<S_{p}
, see the proof of Theorem-1.5 in
[
19
]
for details.

We end the section with a Pohozhaev non-existence result for (
1.1
) when
\displaystyle q=p^{\ast}
and
\displaystyle\lambda=0
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\lambda=0
and
\displaystyle q=p^{\ast}
then the problem (
1.1
) does not have any non zero solution.

Proof.

Assume the problem has a nontrivial solution. Then by our symmetry result Theorem-
1.1
the initial value problem

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left((\sinh t)^{n-1}|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+(\sinh t)^{n-1}u^{p^{\ast}-1}=0\\
u(0)=\alpha,u^{\prime}(0)=0,u(t)>0,u^{\prime}(t)<0,\forall\,t>0\end{cases}

(6.3)

has a solution for some
\displaystyle\alpha>0
satisfying the asymptotic estimates stated in Theorem-
1.1
namely
\displaystyle u(t)\sim u^{\prime}(t)\sim e^{-\frac{n-1}{p-1}t}
for
\displaystyle t
large.

Multiplying the equation (
6.3
) with
\displaystyle(\sinh t)u^{\prime}(t)
and integrating from
\displaystyle 0
to some
\displaystyle R>0
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}\left[\left((\sinh t)^{n-1}|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+u^{p^{\ast}-1}(\sinh t)^{n-1}\right](\sinh t)u^{\prime}(t)\;dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{R}\left((\sinh t)^{n-1}|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)((\sinh t)u^{\prime}(t))^{\prime}\,dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{p^{\ast}}\int_{0}^{R}\left(u^{p^{\ast}}\right)^{\prime}(\sinh t)^{n}\;dt+(\sinh R)^{n}|u^{\prime}(R)|^{p}.

(6.4)

Again integrating by parts and rearranging the terms, we get

\frac{p-n}{p}\int_{0}^{R}\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p}-u^{p^{\ast}}\right)(\sinh t)^{n-1}\cosh t\;dt=\left[\frac{p-1}{p}|u^{\prime}(R)|^{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\ast}}u^{p^{\ast}}(R)\right](\sinh R)^{n}

(6.5)

Similarly multiplying the equation (
6.3
) by
\displaystyle(\cosh t)u(t)
and simplifying as before we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\left(|u(t)|^{p^{\ast}}-|u^{\prime}(t)|^{p}\right)(\sinh t)^{n-1}\cosh t\;dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}|u^{\prime}(t)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(t)u(t)(\sinh t)^{n}\;dt

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+|u^{\prime}(R)|^{p-1}u(R)(\sinh R)^{n-1}\cosh R.

(6.6)

Substituting (
6
) in (
6.5
) and simplifying using the asymptotic estimates of
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle u^{\prime}
we get,

\frac{n-p}{p}\int_{0}^{R}|u^{\prime}(t)|^{p-2}u(t)u^{\prime}(t)(\sinh t)^{n}\;dt=O\left(e^{\left(n-\frac{n-1}{p-1}p\right)R}\right)+O\left(e^{\left(n-\frac{n-1}{p-1}p^{\ast}\right)R}\right)\to 0

as
\displaystyle R\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus we get
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|u^{\prime}(t)|^{p-1}u(t)(\sinh t)^{n}\;dt=0
, which is a contradiction.
∎
