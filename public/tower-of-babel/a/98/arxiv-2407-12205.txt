One-dimensional coefficient inverse problems by transformation operators


Abstract
We prove the uniqueness for an inverse problem of determining a matrix coefficient P ⁡ ( x ) P(x) of a system of evolution equations σ ​ ∂ t u = ∂ x 2 u ⁡ ( t , x ) − P ⁡ ( x ) ​ u ​ ( t , x ) \sigma\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)-P(x)u(t,x) for 0 < x < ℓ 0<x<\ell and 0 < t < T 0<t<T , where ℓ > 0 \ell>0 and T > 0 T>0 are arbitrarily given. The uniqueness results assert that two solutions have the same Cauchy data at x = 0 x=0 over ( 0 , T ) (0,T) and the same initial value or the final value which is positive on [ 0 , ℓ ] [0,\ell] , then the zeroth-order coefficient is uniquely determined on [ 0 , ℓ ] [0,\ell] . The uniqueness for inverse coefficient problem for a system of evolution equations without boundary conditions over the whole boundary is an open problem even in the one-dimension in the case where only initial value is given as spatial data. Moreover, in the case of the zero initial condition, we prove the uniqueness in the half of the spatial interval.

.

Since

R(0)z(t,x)+\int^{t}_{0}R^{\prime}(t-\tau)z(\tau,x)ds=\partial_{t}\left(\int^{t}_{0}R(t-\tau)z(\tau,x)d\tau\right),

in view of (3.4), we have

\partial_{t}\left(\widetilde{w}(t,x)-\int^{t}_{0}R(t-\tau)z(\tau,x)d\tau\right)=0.

Hence, by
\widetilde{w}(0,\cdot)=0
in
(0,\ell)
, we obtain

\widetilde{w}(t,x)=\int^{t}_{0}R(t-\tau)z(\tau,x)d\tau,\quad 0<t<T,\,x\in(0,\ell),

that is,

\widetilde{w}(t,x)=\int^{t}_{0}R(\tau)z(t-\tau,x)d\tau,\quad 0<t<T,\,x\in(0,\ell).

(3.5)

We will prove that
z\in H^{1,2}((0,T)\times(0,\ell))
satisfies

\sigma\partial_{t}z(t,x)-\partial_{xx}z(t,x)+Q(x)z(t,x)=0\quad\mbox{in}\,\,(0,T)\times(0,\ell)

(3.6)

and

z(t,0)=\partial_{x}z(t,0)=0\quad\mbox{in $(0,T)$}.

(3.7)

First we will verify (
3.7
). Indeed,
\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}(t,0)=\partial_{x}\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}(t,0)=0
in
(0,T)
by (3.3), and so

(Mz)(\cdot,0)=(M\partial_{x}z)(\cdot,0))=0
in
(0,T)
.
Consequently, the injectivity of
K_{1}
directly yields (
3.7
).

Next, we will prove (
3.6
).
First, using
\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}\in H^{1,2}((0,T)\times(0,\ell))\subset C([0,T];L^{2}(0,\ell))
,
by (3.4) we have

\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}(0,x)=R(0)z(0,x),\quad x\in(0,\ell).

On the other hand, substituting
t=0
in (3.3), we obtain

\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}(0,x)=-\frac{1}{\sigma}R(0)K(x,0),\quad x\in(0,\ell).

Hence
R(0)z(0,x)=-\frac{1}{\sigma}R(0)K(x,0)
for
x\in(0,\ell)
.
By
R(0)\neq 0
, we reach

z(0,x)=-\frac{1}{\sigma}K(x,0),\quad x\in(0,\ell).

(3.8)

We proceed to the completion of the proof of (
3.6
).
In terms of (
3.5
) and (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\sigma\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}(t,x)=\sigma R(t)z(0,x)+\int^{t}_{0}R(\tau)\sigma\partial_{t}z(t-\tau,x)d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-R(t)K(x,0)+\int^{t}_{0}R(\tau)\sigma\partial_{t}z(t-\tau,x)d\tau

and

(\partial_{x}^{2}-Q(x))\widetilde{w}(t,x)=\int^{t}_{0}R(\tau)(\partial_{x}^{2}-Q(x))z(t-\tau,x)d\tau,\quad 0<t<T,\,x\in(0,\ell).

Consequently (3.3) implies

\begin{split}&-R(t)K(x,0)=\sigma\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}-\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{w}(t,x)+Q\widetilde{w}(t,x)\\
=&-R(t)K(x,0)+\int^{t}_{0}R(\tau)(\sigma\partial_{t}z-\partial_{x}^{2}z+Qz)(t-\tau,x)d\tau,\end{split}

that is,

\int^{t}_{0}R(\tau)(\sigma\partial_{t}z-\partial_{x}^{2}z+Qz)(t-\tau,x)d\tau=0,\quad 0<t<T,\,x\in(0,\ell).

We set
Z(\sigma^{*})(\tau):=((\sigma\partial_{\tau}z-\partial_{x}^{2}z+Qz)(\tau,\cdot),\,\sigma^{*})_{L^{2}(0,\ell)}
for any
\sigma^{*}\in L^{2}(0,\ell)
. Then

\int^{t}_{0}R(\tau)Z(\sigma^{*})(t-\tau)d\tau=0,\quad 0<t<T.

By the Titchmarsh convolution theorem (e.g., Titchmarsh
[
24
]
),
there exists
t_{*}(\sigma^{*})\in[0,T]
such that

R(\tau)=0\quad\mbox{for $0<\tau<T-t_{*}(\sigma^{*})$},\quad Z(\sigma^{*})(\tau)=0\quad\mbox{for $0<\tau<t_{*}(\sigma^{*})$}.

If
t_{*}(\sigma^{*})<T
, then
R(\tau)=0
for
0<\tau<T-t_{*}(\sigma^{*})

with
T-t_{*}(\sigma^{*})>0
, which implies
R(0)=0
. This is impossible
because we assume (
1.14
), by which we have
R(0)\neq 0
.
Therefore
t_{*}(\sigma^{*})=T
.
Hence,
((\sigma\partial_{\tau}z-\partial_{x}^{2}z+Qz)(\tau,\cdot),\,\sigma^{*})_{L^{2}(0,\ell)}=0

for any
\tau\in(0,T)
and any
\sigma^{*}\in L^{2}(0,\ell)
.
Thus the verification of (
3.6
) is complete.

Now we can complete the proof of Theorem
4
.
Indeed, from (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), we can apply
the unique continuation for the parabolic equation, so that
we reach
z=0
in
(0,T)\times(0,\ell)
. In particular,

z(0,\cdot)=0
for
0<x<\ell
.
Then equality (
3.8
) implies
K(x,0)=0
on
(0,\ell)
.
By the uniqueness of solution to an initial value problem
of the hyperbolic equation in (2.1) with zero initial values

K(x,0)=\partial_{y}K(x,0)=0
on
\{(x,0);0<x<\ell\}
, we
have
K(x,y)=0
if
(x,y)
is in the interior of the triangle
with vertices
(0,0)
,
(\ell,0)
and
(\frac{\ell}{2},\,\frac{\ell}{2})
.
In particular
K(x,x)=0
on
(0,\frac{\ell}{2})
. This implies

P=Q
in
\left(0,\,\frac{\ell}{2}\right)
. Thus the proof of
Theorem
4
is complete.

\blacksquare

Proof of Corollary
5
.

We set

R(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\int^{x}_{0}r(y)dy,\quad\widetilde{R}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\int^{x}_{0}\widetilde{r}(y)dy,\quad 0<x<\ell

and

W(t,x):=e^{R(x)}u(t,x),\quad\widetilde{W}(t,x):=e^{\widetilde{R}(x)}\widetilde{u}.

Then,

\sigma\partial_{t}W=\partial_{x}^{2}W-\left(\frac{1}{4}r^{2}-\frac{1}{2}r^{\prime}+P(x)\right)W,\quad W(0,x)=0,\quad 0<x<\ell,\,0<t<T,

and

\sigma\partial_{t}\widetilde{W}=\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{W}-\left(\frac{1}{4}\widetilde{r}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}{\widetilde{r}}^{\prime}+Q(x)\right)\widetilde{W},\quad\widetilde{W}(0,x)=0,\quad 0<x<\ell,\,0<t<T.

By (
1.19
), we have

W(t,0)=\widetilde{W}(t,0)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\partial_{x}W(t,0)=\partial_{x}\widetilde{W}(t,0)\quad\mbox{in}\,\,(0,T).

We can readily check the conditions on
W
and
\widetilde{W}
in Theorem
4
,
so that the application of Theorem
4
completes the proof of
Corollary
5
.

\blacksquare

4
Proof of Theorem
6

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\ell]
be the maximal number satisfying

P(x)=Q(x)\quad\mbox{in}\quad(0,\varepsilon).

(4.1)

By the assumption of the theorem, such
\varepsilon>0
exists.
We can assume
\varepsilon<\ell
. Otherwise, the theorem is already proved.
We choose
x_{0}>0
sufficiently small such that
0<x_{0}<\varepsilon
.

We introduce a transformation of the variables:
x\mapsto\eta
by

\eta=x-\varepsilon+x_{0}
for
\varepsilon-x_{0}<x<\ell
.
We note that
\varepsilon-x_{0}<x<\ell
if and only if

0<\eta<\ell+x_{0}-\varepsilon
, and in particular,

\varepsilon-x_{0}<x<\varepsilon
if and only
0<\eta<x_{0}
.
We set
P(\eta;x_{0}):=P(x)
,
Q(\eta;x_{0}):=Q(x)
,

u^{k}(t,\eta;x_{0}):=u^{k}(t,x)
and
\widetilde{u}^{k}(t,\eta;x_{0}):=\widetilde{u}^{k}(t,x)
for

1\leq k\leq N
.

Then, (
4.1
) implies

P(\eta;x_{0})=Q(\eta;x_{0})\quad\mbox{for $0<\eta<x_{0}$}.

(4.2)

We set
\Omega_{0}:=\{(\eta,y);\,0<y<x<\ell-\varepsilon+x_{0}\}
.
In terms of Lemma 1, there exists a unique solution

K(\eta,y;x_{0})\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{0}})
to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{\eta}^{2}K(\eta,y;x_{0})-\partial_{y}^{2}K(\eta,y:x_{0})=Q(\eta;x_{0})K(\eta,y;x_{0})-K(\eta,y;x_{0})P(y;x_{0})\quad\mbox{in $\Omega_{0}$},\\
&\partial_{y}K(\eta,0;x_{0})=0,\quad 0<\eta<\ell-\varepsilon+x_{0},\\
&2\frac{d}{d\eta}K(\eta,\eta;x_{0})=Q(\eta;x_{0})-P(\eta;x_{0}),\quad 0<\eta<\ell-\varepsilon+x_{0},\quad K(0,0;x_{0})=0.\end{array}\right.

(4.3)

Setting

V^{k}(t,\eta):=u^{k}(t,\eta;x_{0})+\int^{\eta}_{0}K(\eta,y;x_{0})u^{k}(t,y;x_{0})dy,\quad 1\leq k\leq N,

by Lemma
2
we obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\sigma\partial_{t}V^{k}-\partial_{\eta}^{2}V^{k}+Q(\eta;x_{0})V^{k}=-K(\eta,0;x_{0})\partial_{\eta}u^{k}(t,0;x_{0})\quad\mbox{in $(0,T)\times(0,\ell-\varepsilon+x_{0})$},\\
&\partial_{\eta}V^{k}(t,0)=\partial_{\eta}{u}^{k}(t,0;x_{0})\quad\mbox{and}\quad V^{k}(t,0;x_{0})=u^{k}(t,0;x_{0})\quad\mbox{in $(0,T)$}.\end{array}\right.

(4.4)

By (4.2), we see

K(\eta,\eta;x_{0})=0\quad\mbox{for $0<\eta<x_{0}$}.

Therefore, the uniqueness of solution to problem (4.3) yields

K(\eta,y;x_{0})=0,\quad 0<y<\eta<x_{0}.

(4.5)

Hence, substituting
y=0
, we reach

K(\eta,0;x_{0})=0\quad\mbox{for $0<\eta<x_{0}$}.

With (4.3), we obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{\eta}^{2}K(\eta,y;x_{0})-\partial_{y}^{2}K(\eta,y;x_{0})=Q(\eta;x_{0})K(\eta,y;x_{0})-K(\eta,y;x_{0})P(y;x_{0}),\\
&\quad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mbox{for $0<y<\eta<x_{0}$},\\
&K(\eta,0;x_{0})=\partial_{y}K(\eta,0;x_{0})=0,\quad 0<\eta<x_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(4.6)

Moreover, we make the even extension with respect to
y=0
to

\Omega_{\pm}:=\{(\eta,y);\,-\eta<y<\eta,\,0<\eta<x_{0}+\ell-\varepsilon\}
:

K(\eta,y:x_{0})=K(\eta,-y;x_{0})\quad\mbox{for $-\eta<y<0,\,0<\eta<x_{0}+\ell-\varepsilon$}.

In view of
\partial_{y}K(\eta,0;x_{0})=0
for
0<\eta<x_{0}+\ell-\varepsilon
, we can
readily verify that the extended
K(\eta,y;x_{0})
is in
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{\pm}})
,
and satisfies the hyperbolic equation in (4.3) in
\Omega_{\pm}
.
Therefore, setting
\delta:=\min\{x_{0},\,\ell-\varepsilon\}
, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{\eta}^{2}K(\eta,y;x_{0})-\partial_{y}^{2}K(\eta,y;x_{0})=Q(\eta;x_{0})K(\eta,y;x_{0})-K(\eta,y;x_{0})P(y;x_{0}),\\
&K(x_{0},y:x_{0})=\partial_{\eta}K(x_{0},y;x_{0})=0\quad\mbox{for $-x_{0}<y<x_{0}$.}\end{array}\right.

(4.7)

The uniqueness of solution to the hyperbolic equation with
initial condition (4.7) implies that

K(\eta,y;x_{0})=0
if
x_{0}<\eta<x_{0}+\delta
and

\eta-2x_{0}<y<-\eta+2x_{0}
, from which we see

K(\eta,0;x_{0})=0
for
x_{0}<\eta<x_{0}+\delta
with
\delta>0
.
Then

K(\eta,0;x_{0})\partial_{x}u^{k}(t,0)=0\quad\mbox{for $k\in\{1,2,...,N\}$, $0<\eta<x_{0}+\delta$,
$0<t<T$}

and
P(\eta;x_{0})=Q(\eta;x_{0})
for
0<\eta<x_{0}
.
In view of (1.3) - (1.4) - (1.5), we can apply Lemma 3 and we can find
a constant
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that
P(\eta;x_{0})=Q(\eta;x_{0})
for
0<\eta<x_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}
, that is,
P(x)=Q(x)
for
0<x<\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon
.
This is a contradiction against the choice of
\varepsilon
in (4.1).
Thus the proof of Theorem 6 is complete.

\blacksquare

5
Concluding remarks

•

We can describe our main achievements:

(i) Uniqueness without full boundary conditions in spatially one-dimensional
cases.

(ii) Wide applicability of our method which is based on
the transformation operator but does not depend on any results on
inverse spectral problems.

•

In this article, we mainly consider equations of the form

\sigma\partial_{t}u-\partial_{x}^{2}u+P(x)u=0,

[formula]

but by means of the classical Liouville
transform, we can reduce an inverse problem of determining

p(x)
in

\sigma\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\partial_{x}(p(x)\partial_{x}u(t,x))

to the inverse problem for (5.1). We omit the details.

•

Inverse parabolic problems with initial values are
difficult and the uniqueness is not known in general.
In order to solve it, one way is to change the inverse
parabolic problem to an inverse hyperbolic problem.

One traditional way is that
by considering an integral transform in time of solutions to
parabolic equations, we can reduce the inverse parabolic
problem to an inverse hyperbolic problem where the
uniqueness is classical with initial values.
Such an integral transform is similar to the Laplace transform and
called Reznitskaya’s transform in the context of the inverse problem
(e.g., Romanov
[
20
]
),
but we need data of the solutions over the time interval

(0,\infty)
. In the case where the solution data are time analytic,
we can reduce data over
(0,\infty)
to data over a finite time interval and
the analyticity is valid if the boundary values are time analytic and
the coefficients of the parabolic equation are independent of the time
or analytic in time. As for this approach, see
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
.
However, in our case, since we do not assume the boundary
values on the whole boundary
x=0
and
x=\ell
, we can not expect
the time analyticity of
u
and
\widetilde{u}
.
