Mean-Field Description of Cooperative Scattering by Atomic Clouds
Nicola Piovella

Abstract
We present analytic expressions for the scattering of light by an extended atomic cloud. We obtain the solution for the mean-field excitation of different atomic spherical distributions driven by an uniform laser, including the initial build-up, the steady-state and the decay after the laser is switched off. We show that the mean-field model does not describe subradiant scattering, due to negative interference of the photons scattered by N N discrete atoms.



I
Introduction

The cooperative emission from a system of
N
two-level excited atoms has been the object of an intense investigation is the past, starting from the pioneering studies on Dicke superradiance
Dicke 1954
. On the other hand, the diffusive regime of cooperative scattering in dense medium has been study extensively in the past by a diagramatic approach
Hayden et al. 2001
, where light travels over a distance much larger than the mean free path. More recently, optical properties of atomic clouds beyond the single-atom level have been studied theoretically
Cherroret et al. 2016
;
Kwong et al. 2019
and experimentally
Saint-Jalm et al. 2018
, where the connections between the perturbative diffusive theory and the coupled-dipole description have been investigated. Also, cooperative scattering from dense cold atomic clouds has been the object of intense investigation
Jennewein et al. 2016
.
These studies are complementary to a different regime where the light scattering induces a dipole-dipole interaction between the atom pairs, leading to the cooperative processes of superradiance and subradiance. This regime is characterized to be dominated by single-scattering of photons by many atoms, whereas to the diffusive regime is dominated by multiple scattering. The transition between single and multiple scattering is controlled by the optical thickness parameter
b(\Delta)=b_{0}/(1+4\Delta^{2}/\Gamma^{2})
Labeyrie et al. 2003
;
Guerin et al. 2017
, where
b_{0}
is the resonant optical thickness,
\Delta
is the detuning of the laser frequency from the atomic resonance frequency and
\Gamma
is the transition linewidth. In this contest, a new kind of single-photon superradiance has been proposed by Scully and coworkers
Scully et al. 2006
;
Svidzinsky et al. 2008
;
Svidzinsky et al. 2010
, from an extended ensemble of
N
atoms prepared by absorption of a single photon and exhibiting superradiant decay. A bridge between this single-photon superradiance and the more classical process of cooperative scattering of an incident laser by
N
atoms
Lehmberg 1970
has been proposed by a series of theoretical and experimental papers
Courteille et al. 2010
;
Bienaimé et al. 2010
;
Bienaimé et al. 2013
;
Chabé et al. 2014
;
Bachelard et al. 2016
. A more intriguing effect in such systems is subradiance, also initially proposed by Dicke in 1954
Dicke 1954
, i.e. destructive interference effect leading to the partial trapping of light in the system. This effect has been predicted
Bienaimé et al. 2012
and then observed
Guerin et al. 2016
in a system of driven cold atoms, after that the laser is abruptly switched off and the emitted photons detected in a given direction. From a theoretical point of view, subradiance has been investigated mostly studying the eigenvalues of the system and identified in the most long-lived modes, surviving after the more fast superradiant modes have been extinguished
Bellando et al. 2014
;
Guerin and Kaiser 2017
;
Cottier et al. 2018
. The analysis was based on the numerical solution of the coupled-dipole model of
N
two-level atoms driven by an uniform laser field. A continuous-distribution version of this model allows for an analytical treatment of the problem. This has been done extensively in a series of papers by Svidzinsky et al.
Svidzinsky et al. 2008
;
Svidzinsky et al. 2010
, considering the temporal decay of the system initially prepared in some given excited state. The stationary problem of the system driven by an uniform laser has been studied in terms of the collective modes in ref.
Bachelard et al. 2011
;
Bachelard et al. 2012
. However, the cooperative decay after that the laser has been switched off has not yet been studied by using the continuous-distribution model (or the so-called mean-field (MF) model), except in ref.
Cottier et al. 2018
where however the solution has been obtained numerically.

The aim of this paper is to provide analytical expressions for the excitation of the driven system and for the scattered light intensity. This will encompass both the analytical work by Svidzinsky et al.
Svidzinsky et al. 2008
;
Svidzinsky et al. 2010
, who did not consider a driven system, and the numerical results of ref.
Cottier et al. 2018
.

We outline that the MF model assumes a coherent interaction between the scatterers, neglecting granularity and fluctuations in the atomic distribution. These ingredients are necessary in order to describe the random walk of the scattered photons, leading to the diffusive regime for sufficiently dense samples
Labeyrie et al. 2003
;
Guerin et al. 2017
. Hence, the MF model is not able to describe the diffusive regime, where the atom scatters a photon many times within a mean-free path. As already mentioned, multiple-scattering regime is characterized by a large optical thickness
b=L/\ell_{sc}
, where
L
is the size of the medium,
\ell_{sc}=1/n\sigma_{sc}
is the mean-free pass,
n
is the atomic density,
\sigma_{sc}=(6\pi/k^{2})/(1+4\Delta^{2}/\Gamma^{2})
is the scattering cross-section and
k
is the laser wavenumber. Hence, the MF model is valid for small optical thickness
b(\Delta)=b_{0}/(1+4\Delta^{2}/\Gamma)
, i.e. for large resonant optical thickness
b_{0}
and large detuning, such that
b(\Delta)\ll 1
.

The paper is organized as follows. In sec.
II
we present the general MF equations for a continuous atomic distribution. In sec.
III
we derive the expression for the average quantities and the scattered light intensity and power. The particular cases of uniform, parabolic and Gaussian radial distribution are discussed in sec.
IV
and compared with the numerical solution of the discrete model. Conclusions are summarized in sec.
V
.

II
General equations

From a microscopic point of view and using a dipole approximation, our medium is composed of an ensemble of
N

two-level atoms with position
\mathbf{r}_{j}
, whose atomic transition has frequency
\omega_{a}
, linewidth
\Gamma
and dipole
d
(polarization effects are neglected). The system is driven by a monochromatic plane wave with electric field
E_{0}
, frequency
\omega_{0}
and wave vector
\mathbf{k}_{0}
, detuned from the atomic transition by
\Delta_{0}=\omega_{0}-\omega_{a}
. In the linear regime and in the Markov approximation (valid if the decay time is larger than the photon time-of-flight through the atomic cloud), the problem reduces to the following differential equation for the atomic dipole amplitudes
\beta_{j}

Bienaimé et al. 2011
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\beta_{j}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(i\Delta_{0}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\right)\beta_{j}-\frac{i\Omega_{0}}{2}e^{i\mathbf{k}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\sum_{m\neq j}G_{jm}\beta_{m}(t).

(1)

where
\Omega_{0}=dE_{0}/\hbar
is the Rabi frequency and

G_{jm}=\frac{\exp(ik_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|)}{ik_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|}=\frac{\sin(k_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|)}{k_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|}-i\frac{\cos(k_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|)}{k_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|}

(2)

The kernel
G_{jm}
describes the coupling between the dipoles, mediated by the photons exchanged between the dipoles. It has a real component (sine term), describing the cooperative atomic decay, and an imaginary component (cosine term) describing the cooperative Lamb shift
O.Scully 2009
. The latter
becomes significant when the number of atoms in a cubic optical wavelength,
n\lambda^{3}
, is larger than unity, such that
the contribution from the virtual photons becomes relevant.

In light scattering experiments, disorder plays a role when the number of atoms projected onto a cross section perpendicular to the incident beam is small enough so that a light mode focused down to the diffraction limit (that is
\lambda^{2}
) would be able to resolve and count the atoms. In
other words, the stochastic fluctuations induced by the random positions of the atoms can be neglected when the total number of atoms
N
is larger than the number of modes
\sigma^{2}
(where
\sigma=k_{0}R
and
R
is the transverse size of the system) that fit
into the cloud’s cross section, i.e. when the optical density is
b_{0}=3N/\sigma^{2}\gg 1
. Under this hypothesis, the particles can be
described by a smooth density
n(\mathbf{r})
and their probability to be excited by a field
\beta(\mathbf{r},t)
.
By approximating the sum over
j
by an integral over the smooth density, i.e.
\sum_{j}\rightarrow\int d\mathbf{r}n(\mathbf{r})
, Eq.(
1
) turns into

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\beta(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}=\left(i\Delta_{0}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\right)\beta(\mathbf{r},t)-\frac{i}{2}\Omega_{0}e^{i\mathbf{k}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\int d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}n(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\exp(ik_{0}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)}{ik_{0}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}\beta(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t).

(3)

Using

\frac{\exp(ik_{0}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)}{ik_{0}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}j_{n}(k_{0}r_{<})Y_{n,m}(\theta,\phi)Y^{*}_{n,m}(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})h^{(1)}_{n}(k_{0}r_{>})

(4)

where
Y_{n,m}(\theta,\phi)
are the spherical harmonics,
j_{n}(r)
and
h^{(1)}_{n}(r)=j_{n}(r)+iy_{n}(r)
are the spherical Bessel and Hankel functions of first kind, respectively, and

r_{<}
(
r_{>}
) is the smaller (larger) between
r
and
r^{\prime}
. Taking
\theta
as the polar angle with respect the direction of the wave vector
\mathbf{k}_{0}
,
we can expand

\beta(\mathbf{r},t)=\sum_{n,m}\alpha_{n,m}(t)\beta_{n}(r)Y_{n,m}(\theta,\phi).

(5)

By substituting it in Eq.(
3
) and assuming a radial distribution,
n(r)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\left\{\dot{\alpha}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}-\left(i\Delta_{0}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\right)\alpha_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\right\}\beta_{n^{\prime}}(r)Y_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)=-\frac{i}{2}\Omega_{0}e^{ik_{0}r\cos\theta}

(6)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma}{2}(4\pi)\int_{0}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})\int d\Omega^{\prime}\sum_{n^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime}}j_{n^{\prime\prime}}(k_{0}r_{<})Y_{n^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime}}(\theta,\phi)Y^{*}_{n^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime}}(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})h^{(1)}_{n^{\prime\prime}}(k_{0}r_{>})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\alpha_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\beta_{n^{\prime}}(r^{\prime})Y_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}),

where
d\Omega^{\prime}=d\phi^{\prime}\sin\theta^{\prime}d\theta^{\prime}
.
Since

\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\,Y^{*}_{n,m}(\theta,\phi)Y_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)=\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}

(7)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\,Y^{*}_{n,m}(\theta,\phi)e^{ik_{0}r\cos\theta}=2\delta_{m,0}\sqrt{\pi(2n+1)}i^{n}j_{n}(k_{0}r),

(8)

multiplying Eq.(
6
) by
Y_{n,m}^{*}(\theta,\phi)
and integrating over the angles, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\{\dot{\alpha}_{n,m}-\left(i\Delta_{0}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\right)\alpha_{n,m}\right\}\beta_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Omega_{0}\delta_{m,0}\sqrt{\pi(2n+1)}i^{n}j_{n}(k_{0}r)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma}{2}(4\pi)\alpha_{n,m}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})j_{n}(k_{0}r_{<})h^{(1)}_{n}(k_{0}r_{>})\beta_{n}(r^{\prime}).

If
\alpha_{n,m}(0)=0
, the only components different from zero are those for
m=0
. So, defining
\alpha_{n}=\sqrt{(2n+1)/4\pi}\,\alpha_{n,0}
and since
Y_{n,0}(\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{(2n+1)/4\pi}\,P_{n}(\cos\theta)
where
P_{n}(x)
are the Legendre polynomial, we write

\beta(r,\theta,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(t)j_{n}(k_{0}r)P_{n}(\cos\theta)

(10)

where
\alpha_{n}(t)
is the solution of the following differential equation

\displaystyle\left\{\dot{\alpha}_{n}-\left(i\Delta_{0}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\right)\alpha_{n}\right\}j_{n}(k_{0}r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\frac{\Omega_{0}}{2}(2n+1)i^{n}j_{n}(k_{0}r)-\frac{\Gamma}{2}F_{n}(r)\alpha_{n}.

(11)

where

\displaystyle F_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi\left\{h^{(1)}_{n}(k_{0}r)\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})j^{2}_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})+j_{n}(k_{0}r)\int_{r}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})j_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})h^{(1)}_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})\right\}

We observe that
F_{n}(r)
has a real part and an imaginary part. The real part is
\mathrm{Re}\{F_{n}(r)\}=\lambda_{n}j_{n}(k_{0}r)
where

\lambda_{n}=4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}drr^{2}n(r)j^{2}_{n}(k_{0}r)

(13)

is the collective decay rate of the mode
n

and it corresponds to the contribution of the sine term of the kernel of Eq.(
3
). The imaginary part is

\mathrm{Im}\{F_{n}(r)\}=4\pi\left\{y_{n}(k_{0}r)\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})j^{2}_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})+j_{n}(k_{0}r)\int_{r}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})j_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})y_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})\right\}

(14)

and contributes to the cooperative Lamb shift, arising from the cosine term of the kernel of Eq.(
3
).
When the detuning
\Delta_{0}
is much larger than the collective Lamb shift, the sine-kernel provides a good approximation to the solution.

III
Average quantities

Using the expansion (
10
), we can calculate the average:

\displaystyle\langle|\beta(t)|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{N}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}n(r)|\beta(r,\theta,t)|^{2}dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{m}^{*}(t)\alpha_{n}(t)\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}n(r)j_{m}(k_{0}r)j_{n}(k_{0}r)dr\int_{-1}^{1}P_{m}(x)P_{n}(x)dx.

Using

\int_{-1}^{1}dx\,P_{m}(x)P_{n}(x)=\frac{2}{2n+1}\delta_{m,n},

(16)

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle|\beta|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\alpha_{n}(t)|^{2}\lambda_{n}}{2n+1}.

(17)

The far-field amplitude of the radiation scattered by
N
atoms along the direction of the
wave-vector
\mathbf{k}=k_{0}(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)
is

\displaystyle E_{s}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta_{j}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}

(18)

where
E_{1}=(dk_{0}^{2}/4\pi\epsilon_{0}r)\exp(ik_{0}r)
. For a continuous distribution,

\displaystyle E_{s}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\theta^{\prime}d\theta^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})\beta(r^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})e^{-ik_{0}r^{\prime}[\sin\theta\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos(\phi-\phi^{\prime})+\cos\theta\cos\theta^{\prime}]}dr^{\prime}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi E_{1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\theta^{\prime}d\theta^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\prime 2}n(r^{\prime})\beta(r^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})J_{0}(k_{0}r^{\prime}\sin\theta\sin\theta^{\prime})e^{-ik_{0}r^{\prime}\cos\theta\cos\theta^{\prime}}dr^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}i^{-n}\lambda_{n}P_{n}(\cos\theta)

where
J_{0}(x)
is the zero-order Bessel function and we used the integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\theta^{\prime}P_{n}(\cos\theta^{\prime})J_{0}(k_{0}r^{\prime}\sin\theta\sin\theta^{\prime})e^{-ik_{0}r^{\prime}\cos\theta\cos\theta^{\prime}}d\theta^{\prime}=2i^{-n}j_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})P_{n}(\cos\theta).

(20)

The angular distribution of the power scattered by
N
atoms is

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{d\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c\epsilon_{0}}{2}|E_{s}(\mathbf{k})|r^{2}=\frac{P_{1}}{4\pi}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta_{j}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}\right|^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{1}}{4\pi}\left\{\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}|^{2}+\sum_{j}\sum_{m\neq j}\beta_{j}\beta_{m}^{*}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m})}\right\}

where
P_{1}=ck_{0}^{4}d^{2}/(32\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0})
. The total scattered power is obtained by integrating over the solid angle, giving

P=P_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\beta_{j}\beta_{m}^{*}\frac{\sin(k_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|)}{k_{0}|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{m}|}.

(22)

For a continuous distribution,

\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{P_{1}}{4\pi}\left\{N\langle|\beta|^{2}\rangle+\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}i^{-n}\lambda_{n}P_{n}(\cos\theta)\right|^{2}\right\}

(23)

By integrating over the solid angle
4\pi
, the total scattered power is

P=P_{1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\alpha_{n}|^{2}\lambda_{n}(1+\lambda_{n})}{2n+1}.

(24)

IV
Specific radial distribution

We consider three different spherical distributions for which exact analytic expressions can be obtained. These include a sphere with uniform, parabolic and Gaussian profile.

IV.1
Uniform sphere
Svidzinsky et al. 2008
;
Svidzinsky et al. 2010

For an uniform sphere of radius
R
and density
n(r)=N/V
where
V=(4\pi/3)R^{3}
and
0<r<R
,

F_{n}(r)=\frac{3N}{R^{3}}\left\{h^{(1)}_{n}(k_{0}r)\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}j^{2}_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})+j_{n}(k_{0}r)\int_{r}^{R}dr^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}j_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})h^{(1)}_{n}(k_{0}r^{\prime})\right\}

(25)

Taking
r=R
and defining
k_{0}R=\sigma
, we obtain

F_{n}(\sigma)=\frac{3N}{\sigma^{3}}h^{(1)}_{n}(\sigma)\int_{0}^{\sigma}dxx^{2}j^{2}_{n}(x).

(26)

Since

\displaystyle\int x^{2}j^{2}_{n}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x^{3}}{2}\left\{j_{n}^{2}(x)-j_{n-1}(x)j_{n+1}(x)\right\}

(27)

we obtain

F_{n}(\sigma)=h^{(1)}_{n}(\sigma)\lambda_{n}

(28)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3N}{2}\left\{j^{2}_{n}(\sigma)-j_{n-1}(\sigma)j_{n+1}(\sigma)\right\}

(29)

is the collective decay rate of the mode
n
.
By inserting these expressions in Eq.(
11
) with
r=R
we obtain, for
j_{n}(\sigma)\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}_{n}-\Gamma\left\{i(\delta-\omega_{n})-\frac{1}{2}(1+\lambda_{n})\right\}\alpha_{n}=-i\frac{\Omega_{0}}{2}(2n+1)i^{n}

(30)

where
\delta=\Delta_{0}/\Gamma
and
\omega_{n}=[y_{n}(\sigma)/j_{n}(\sigma)]\lambda_{n}/2
is the collective Lamb shift of the mode
n
.
Equation (
30
) can be straightforwardly integrated and, once inserted in
Eq. (
10
), leads to the following expression for the excitation amplitude

\beta(r,\theta,t)=\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Gamma}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{n}(2n+1)j_{n}(k_{0}r)P_{n}(\cos\theta)}{2(\delta-\omega_{n})+i(1+\lambda_{n})}\left[1-e^{i(\delta-\omega_{n})\Gamma t-(1+\lambda_{n})\Gamma t/2}\right]

(31)

If the pump is switched off after the steady-state is reached (taken as the time
t=0
),

\displaystyle\beta^{\mathrm{(free)}}(r,\theta,t)=\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Gamma}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{n}(2n+1)j_{n}(k_{0}r)P_{n}(\cos\theta)}{2(\delta-\omega_{n})+i(1+\lambda_{n})}e^{i(\delta-\omega_{n})\Gamma t-(1+\lambda_{n})\Gamma t/2}.

(32)

Then

\langle|\beta^{\mathrm{(free)}}|^{2}\rangle=\frac{\Omega_{0}^{2}}{N\Gamma^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+1)\lambda_{n}}{4(\delta-\omega_{n})^{2}+(1+\lambda_{n})^{2}}e^{-(1+\lambda_{n})\Gamma t},

(33)

\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{P_{1}}{4\pi}\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+1)\lambda_{n}e^{-(1+\lambda_{n})\Gamma t}}{4(\delta-\omega_{n})^{2}+(1+\lambda_{n})^{2}}+\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+1)\lambda_{n}P_{n}(\cos\theta)}{2(\delta-\omega_{n})^{2}+i(1+\lambda_{n})}e^{-i\omega_{n}\Gamma t-(1+\lambda_{n})\Gamma t/2}\right|^{2}\right\}

(34)

and

P(t)=P_{1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+1)\lambda_{n}(1+\lambda_{n})e^{-(1+\lambda_{n})\Gamma t}}{4(\delta-\omega_{n})^{2}+(1+\lambda_{n})^{2}}.

(35)

We observe that this solution does not describe the subradiant decay after the laser is cut off, since every mode has a decay rate
(1+\lambda_{n})\Gamma>\Gamma
i.e. larger than the single-atom decay. The MF model is unable to describe subradiance, experimentally observed in
Araújo et al. 2016
and theoretically discussed in
Bienaimé et al. 2012
: single photon subradiance arises from the anti-symmetric states of
N
atoms, in which only a single excitation among
N
is present
(
Dicke 1954
;
Scully 2015
)
. Hence, it can be described only by the discrete model of Eq.(
1
). Conversely, single-photon superradiance can be well described by the MF model, as it will discussed in the following.

For a small cloud, with
\sigma\ll 1
, only the term
n=0
, with
\lambda_{0}\approx N
, decays fast (Dicke superradiance
Dicke 1954
), while all the other terms with
n\geq 1
are suppressed by a factor
\sigma^{2n}
. The collective shift is
\omega_{0}\sim-N/2\sigma
.
The case of a large cloud is illustrated by
Fig.
1
and
2
, showing
\lambda_{n}/N
and
\omega_{n}/N

\sigma=20
, as obtained from Eq.(

for
\sigma\gg 1

n

and drops to zero for

, approximately as

\lambda_{n}\approx\frac{3\pi N}{4n^{2}n!^{2}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\right)^{n}.

(36)

The collective Lamb shift
\omega_{n}
in the limit
\sigma\gg 1
and
n<\sigma
is approximately

\omega_{n}\sim-(\lambda_{N}/2)\cot(\sigma-n\pi/2)\sim(3N/4\sigma^{2})\{\tan\sigma,-\cot\sigma\}
, where the first value is for
n
odd and the second for
n
even (dashed blue line and dash-dotted red line in Fig.

, respectively).
We observe that
\omega_{n}
changes sign with
n
and, with the exception for the values of
\sigma

\tan\sigma
or
\cot\sigma
are large, it averages to zero and gives a negligible contribution. For large detuning,
\delta\gg 1
, it can be neglected.

shows the average excitation probability
\langle|\beta(t)|^{2}\rangle

\Gamma t
for
\delta=10
,
\sigma=20

1

;

;

\langle|\beta(t)|^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{N\Gamma^{2}}\frac{\Omega_{0}^{2}}{4\delta^{2}+(1+\lambda_{N})^{2}}\left|1-e^{i\delta\Gamma t-(1+\lambda_{N})\Gamma t/2}\right|^{2}.

When the laser is cut off, at short times the decay is superradiant, with
\lambda_{n}\approx\lambda_{N}
and

V
Conclusions

The aim of this paper has been to provide an analytical description of the cooperative light scattering by an ensemble of atoms driven by an uniform laser beam. We have compared the mean-field (MF) model, where a continuous atomic distribution is assumed, to the numerical results from the discrete coupled dipoles model. The MF model describes a coherent interaction between the atoms, neglecting multiple scattering and diffusion effects due to the random walk of the photon within a mean-free pass distance. For these reasons, the validity of the MF model is limited to a regime with small optical thickness
b=b_{0}/(1+4\delta^{2})\ll 1
, but still cooperative when
b_{0}\gg 1
and
\delta\gg 1
. In this regime the MF model gives a rather accurate description of the atomic excitation and of the scattered light intensity when the laser is on, but is unable to describe the subradiant decay after the laser is cut off. This suggests that subradiance is intrinsically related to the discreetness of the system and to the anti-symmetric properties of the single-excitation
N
-atomic states. Contrarily to previous works, we do not assume an initial preparation of the atoms in a superposition of states with a single excitation (the so-called Dicke states), but the excitation is provided by a classical uniform laser. The atomic system reaches a stationary state which is dominated by the Timed-Dicke symmetric state. When the laser is cut, the early decay is superradiant, with a rate
\Gamma_{sr}\sim N\Gamma/(k_{0}R)^{2}
, where
R
is the size of the atomic cloud. The MF solution can be expressed in terms of collective modes whose features depend on the atomic distribution. We discussed the cases of uniform, parabolic and Gaussian spherical distribution. When the cloud’s size is smaller than an optical wavelength, a single mode with decay rate
N\Gamma
will dominate, whereas for an extended cloud many modes are present, up to a number
n\sim k_{0}R
: the fastest modes are those with a decay rate proportional to the resonant optical thickness
b_{0}
, down to the slower ones with decay rate
\Gamma
. So, the last surviving modes when the laser is off are those with a single-atom decay rate. In this sense, the subradiant component of the excited state is lost in a MF description.
