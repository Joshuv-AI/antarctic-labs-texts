More stochastic expansions for the pricing of vanilla options with cash dividends
Fabien Le Floc’h

Abstract
There is no exact closed form formula for pricing of European options with discrete cash dividends under the model where the underlying asset price follows a piecewise lognormal process with jumps at dividend ex-dates. This paper presents alternative expansions based on the technique of Etore and Gobet, leading to more robust first, second and third-order expansions across the range of strikes and the range of dividend dates.

Introduction

The Black-Scholes-Merton framework supposes a continuous dividend yield. In practice however, dividends are better modelled as fixed cash amounts, especially for short maturities (the first two or three years) where the uncertainty related to the dividend amount is low. For very short maturities (a quarter), the dividend amount is known exactly. For longer maturities, proportional dividends are more appropriate. From the arbitrage-free hypothesis and ignoring transaction costs, a discrete dividend implies that the stock jumps from the dividend amount at the dividend ex-date.

In order to stay within the Black-Scholes-Merton framework, practitioners often compute the equivalent dividend yield, that is the dividend yield which leads to the same forward price as with pure discrete dividends for a given maturity. One issue however, is that the Black-Scholes-Merton model is then inconsistent around a dividend date. Indeed, around an ex-dividend date
t_{i}
, the option price must obey the following continuity relation
[
2
]

V_{\textsf{call}}(S_{0},K,t_{i}^{-})=V_{\textsf{call}}(S_{0},K-\delta_{i},t_{i}^{+})\,,

(1)

for a cash dividend amount
\delta_{i}
, where
V_{\textsf{call}}(S_{0},K,t)
is the price of a European call option of strike
K
and maturity
t
and initial underlying price
S_{0}
.

As an example, let us consider the parameters
S_{0}=100,K=100,\delta_{i}=1,t_{i}=0.5
, volatility
\sigma=0.30
, interest rate
r=0
, and apply the Black-Scholes formula using the constant dividend yield
\mu_{T}=r-\frac{1}{T}\ln\frac{S_{0}-\delta_{i}}{S_{0}}
, which preserves the forward price to maturity. We obtain

\displaystyle V_{\textsf{call}}(100,100,0.4999)=8.446

\displaystyle\textmd{,}\quad V_{\textsf{call}}(100,99,0.5001)=8.363\textmd{.}

The discontinuity is more obvious in the plot of the option price against the time to maturity (Figure
1
).

Figure 1:
Discontinuity of the option price under the forward model at a dividend date.
S_{0}=100,K=100,\delta_{i}=1,t_{i}=0.5,\sigma=30\%,r=q=0
.

As a consequence, under the Black-Scholes model, the volatility
\sigma
must jump just after the dividend date in order for the prices to obey the continuity relationship. In our example, the call prices are equals if the volatility jumps to
\sigma(t_{i}^{+})=30.3\%
. Thus, in practice, the discontinuity may be solved by using a specific interpolation in time of the implied volatility surface around the dividend dates. It will also impact directly any implementation of the Dupire local volatility model, as the local volatility is computed from the time-derivative of the call option prices
[
3
]
. The impact on path-dependent options is more subtle, and there, the models can not be reconciled. In particular, American options are very sensitive to the dividend modelling: a dividend yield, a proportional dividend and a cash dividend lead to significantly different exercise boundaries, even when those are calibrated to give the same European option price
[
7
,
5
,
9
]
.
Other issues of the equivalent dividend yield approach are: an exploding forward dividend yield which will impact numerical accuracy when solving the Black-Scholes PDE, wrong delta and gamma hedging greeks.

In order to better take into account the discontinuity at dividend dates, and obtain a more meaningful hedging for European and American options, a natural model is to represent the equity or equity index as a piecewise lognormal process, which jumps from the dividend amount at each exercise date. There is however no exact analytical formula for this problem. Standard numerical techniques like Monte-Carlo, or better, finite difference or quadrature methods can be applied without much difficulty. The pricing of European options requires however fast evaluation methods in practice. While a quadrature might be fine for a single dividend
[
6
]
, it is much slower for many dividends. As a consequence many have tried to develop corrections to the Black-Scholes formula to take into account the discrete dividends.
Bos and Vandermark 2002
proposed a simple adjustment to the spot and the strike. More recently more precise approximations were discovered
[
8
,
10
]
. Of particularly interest is the stochastic Taylor expansion technique around a shifted lognormal model of
Etore and Gobet 2012
leading to first-, second- and third-order (with regards to the dividend amount) formulas. Here, we will apply their Taylor expansion technique, but on a different proxy, aiming for more accurate approximations of European options prices under the piecewise lognormal model.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the notation that will be used across the paper. In sections
3
,
4
, we apply the Etore-Gobet expansion technique using respectively the forward model and the Lehman model of
Bos and Vandermark 2002
as a proxy to obtain European option prices expansions of up to third-order. Finally, in section
5
, we compare the accuracy and performance against of our new expansions against other approximations for the price of European options under the piecewise-lognormal model.

2
Notation

We borrow the notation of
Etore and Gobet 2012
as we reuse their technique. The asset price with proportional dividends
y_{i}\in[0,1)
and cash dividends
\delta_{i}\geq 0
is represented by the process
S^{(y,\delta)}
. The piecewise lognormal dynamics of the stock price under the risk-neutral measure
\mathbb{Q}
between two dividend dates is:

dS_{t}^{(y,\delta)}=\sigma_{t}S_{t}^{(y,\delta)}dW_{t}+(r_{t}-q_{t})S_{t}^{(y,\delta)}dt

(2)

where
r_{t}
is the risk free deterministic interest rate and
q_{t}
a deterministic repo spread. And it jumps at the dividend dates
t_{i}
:

S_{t_{i}}^{(y,\delta)}=S_{t_{i}^{-}}^{(y,\delta)}-(\delta_{i}+y_{i}S_{t_{i}^{-}}^{(y,\delta)})=S_{t_{i}^{-}}^{(y,\delta)}(1-y_{i})-\delta_{i}\textmd{.}

(3)

The fictitious asset without dividends
S
follows

dS_{t}=\sigma_{t}S_{t}dW_{t}+(r_{t}-q_{t})S_{t}dt

(4)

with initial value
S_{0}=S_{0}^{(y,\delta)}
.
The zero discount factor
B_{t}
, the discount factor
D_{t}
and the lognormal martingale
M_{t}
are defined as

B_{t}=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}r_{s}ds}\textmd{, }D_{t}=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}(r_{s}-q_{s})ds}\textmd{, }M_{t}=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}\textmd{.}

(5)

Thus, we have
S_{t}=S_{0}\frac{M_{t}}{D_{t}}
.
We set
\pi_{i,n}=\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}(1-y_{j})
and will make use of the simplified notation

\hat{\delta}_{i}=\delta_{i}\pi_{i,n}\frac{D_{t_{i}}}{D_{T}}

(6)

where
T
will typically be the option maturity.

Finally we recall the Lemma 1.1 of
Etore and Gobet 2012
:

Lemma 2.1
.

We have
S_{t}^{(y,\delta)}=\pi_{0,n}S_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\pi_{i,n}\frac{S_{t}}{S_{t_{i}}}
.

3
Expansion around the forward

Etore and Gobet 2012
consider an expansion around a shifted lognormal spot model where the asset price
\bar{S}_{t}^{(y,\delta)}
follows

\bar{S}_{t}^{(y,\delta)}=S_{t}-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\pi_{i,n}\frac{S_{t}}{S_{t_{i}}}\right]=\pi_{0,n}S_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}

(7)

We will instead do an expansion on the forward model :

F_{t}=\left(\pi_{0,n}S_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\pi_{i,n}D_{t_{i}}\right)\frac{M_{t}}{D_{t}}

(8)

We recall that the degree
k
of smoothness of
h
is defined as
H_{k}
: the function
h
is
(k-1)
-times continuously differentiable and the
(k-1)
-th derivative is almost everywhere differentiable. Moreover, the derivatives are polynomially bounded: for some positive constant C and p, one has
|h(x)|+\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\partial_{x}^{j}h(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{p})
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.1
First-order expansion around the forward

Theorem 3.1
.

For a smooth function
h
satisfying
H_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}-K)\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)\right]

(9)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\left(\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]\right.

(10)

\displaystyle\left.-\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]\right)

(11)

\displaystyle+\Error_{2}(h)

(12)

where
|\Error_{2}(h)|\leq c(1+S_{0}^{p})\sup_{i}\left(\delta_{i}\sigma\sqrt{t_{i}}\right)^{2}
.

Proof 3.2
.

Let us first rewrite Lemma
2.1
in terms of the forward
F_{t}
:

\displaystyle S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}

\displaystyle=\pi_{0,n}S_{0}\frac{M_{T}}{D_{T}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i,n}\delta_{i}\frac{M_{T}}{D_{T}}\frac{D_{t_{i}}}{M_{t_{i}}}

\displaystyle=F_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i,n}\delta_{i}\frac{M_{T}}{D_{T}}\left(D_{t_{i}}-\frac{D_{t_{i}}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=F_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\left(M_{T}-\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right)\textmd{.}

(13)

We then apply a simple Taylor expansion of order-1 on
h
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}-K)\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime}(F_{T}-K)\left(M_{T}-\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right)\right]+\Error_{2}(h)

(14)

where
|\Error_{2}(h)|\leq(1+S_{0}^{p})\sup_{i}\left(\delta_{i}\|\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}-M_{T}\|\right)^{2}
.
Reusing Lemma 3.3 of
Etore and Gobet 2012
, we have

\|\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}-M_{T}\|\leq\bar{\sigma}\sqrt{t_{i}}\textmd{.}

(15)

The assumptions on
h
allow us to interchange derivation and expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime}(F_{T}-K)\left(M_{T}-\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right)\right]=

\displaystyle-\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)M_{T}\right]+\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right]

(16)

We now proceed to a change of measure defined by
\frac{M_{T}}{M_{\tau}}
where
\tau=0
and
\tau=t_{i}
for the two expectations of the right on side. Under the new measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}
,
\bar{W}_{t}=W_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}1_{\tau\leq s\leq T}ds
is a Brownian motion.
F_{T}
under
\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}
has the same law as
F_{T}e^{\int_{\tau}^{T}\sigma^{2}ds}
under
\mathbb{Q}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)M_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}hF_{T}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]

(18)

.

Second-order expansion around the forward

Theorem 3.3
.

For a smooth function
h
satisfying
H_{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}-K)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\left(\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]\right.\left.-\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j}\partial^{2}_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds+\int_{t_{j}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]e^{\int_{\max(t_{i},t_{j})}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{j}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\partial^{2}_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds+\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{j}\right)^{2}\partial^{2}_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{2\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]e^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}

\displaystyle+\Error_{3}(h)

(19)

where
|\Error_{3}(h)|\leq c(1+S_{0}^{p})\sup_{i}\left(\delta_{i}\sigma\sqrt{t_{i}}\right)^{3}
.

Proof 3.4
.

We start with a Taylor expansion of order-2 on
h
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}-K)\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime}(F_{T}-K)\left(M_{T}-\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}-K)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\left(M_{T}-\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\Error_{3}(h)

(20)

where
|\Error_{3}(h)|\leq(1+S_{0}^{p})\sup_{i}\left(\delta_{i}\|\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}-M_{T}\|\right)^{3}
.

The first two terms stem from the first-order expansion of Theorem
3.1
. The remaining term can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}-K)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\left(M_{T}-\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right)\right)^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}-K)\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{j}}}\right]

\displaystyle-2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{j}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}-K)\frac{M_{T}^{2}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}-K)M_{T}^{2}\right]\textmd{.}

(21)

Similarly to equality
17

\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{j}}}=e^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}(1_{t_{i}\leq s\leq T}+1_{t_{i}\leq s\leq T})dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}(1_{t_{i}\leq s\leq T}+1_{t_{i}\leq s\leq T})^{2}ds}e^{\int_{\max(t_{i},t_{j})}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}\textmd{.}

(22)

This means that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}-K)\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{i}}}\frac{M_{T}}{M_{t_{j}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds+\int_{t_{j}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]e^{\int_{\max(t_{i},t_{j})}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}-K)\frac{M_{T}^{2}}{M_{t_{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h^{\prime\prime}(F_{T}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds+\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}

(25)

Under the forward model, the option price of strike

, forward

and maturity

[

]
:

\displaystyle V_{B}(f,k)=\eta B_{T}\left[x\Phi(\eta d_{1}(f,k))-k\Phi(\eta d_{2}(f,k))\right]

(26)

where

is the cumulative normal distribution function,

The derivatives towards the strike are:

\displaystyle\partial_{k}V_{B}(f,k)

\displaystyle=-\eta B_{T}\Phi(\eta d_{2}(f,k))\textmd{ ,}

(28)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{k}V_{B}(f,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{T}}{k\sqrt{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds}}\phi(d_{2}(f,k))

where

is the normal density function. The following relations will be useful to simplify the expansions of the vanilla option price:

\displaystyle\partial_{f}V_{B}(f,k)=\eta B_{T}\Phi(\eta d_{1}(f,k))\textmd{ ,}

(30)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{f}V_{B}(f,k)=\eta\frac{B_{T}}{f\sqrt{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds}}\phi(d_{1}(f,k))\textmd{ ,}

(31)

\displaystyle d_{2}\left(fe^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds},k\right)=d_{1}\textmd{ ,}

(32)

\displaystyle\phi(d_{2}(f,k))=\frac{f}{k}\phi(d_{1}(f,k))\textmd{ ,}

(33)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\phi\left(d_{2}\left(fe^{2\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds},k\right)\right)e^{\int_{t}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds}=\frac{1}{f}\phi\left(d_{1}(f,k)-\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds}}\right)\textmd{ .}

Let

to the vanilla option payoff leads to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}|\eta S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}-\eta K|^{+}\right]=

\displaystyle\eta B_{T}\left[f\Phi(\eta d_{1})-K\Phi(\eta d_{2})\right]

\displaystyle-\eta B_{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\left(\Phi(\eta d_{1}-\eta\frac{v_{t_{i}}^{2}}{v_{T}})-\Phi(\eta d_{1})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}B_{T}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j}\frac{\phi(d_{1}+v_{T}-\frac{v_{t_{i}}^{2}+v_{t_{j}}^{2}}{v_{T}})}{kv_{T}}e^{v_{T}^{2}-v_{\max(t_{i},t_{j})}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}B_{T}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{j}\right)^{2}\frac{\phi(d_{1})}{fv_{T}}

\displaystyle+\Error_{3}(h)

(35)

where

, with

Note that it could also be expressed purely with derivatives of the vanilla option towards the forward (i.e. through the usual option Delta and Gamma greeks):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}|\eta S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}-\eta K|^{+}\right]=

\displaystyle V_{B}\left(f,K\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\alpha_{i}}\left[\frac{\partial V_{B}}{\partial f}\left(fe^{-\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sigma^{2}(s)ds},K\right)-\frac{\partial V_{B}}{\partial f}\left(f,K\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\hat{\alpha_{i}}\hat{\alpha_{j}}e^{\int_{0}^{\min(t_{i},t_{j})}\sigma^{2}(s)ds}\frac{\partial^{2}V_{B}}{\partial f^{2}}\left(fe^{-\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sigma^{2}(s)ds-\int_{0}^{t_{j}}\sigma^{2}(s)ds},K\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\sum_{j}^{n}\hat{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}V_{B}}{\partial f^{2}}\left(f,K\right)\right]

(36)

where we used the equation (
34

.

This is not too surprising. We performed the Taylor expansion on the strike variable as it is simpler to derive, but there is an equivalent formulation in terms of the forward variable.

3.3
Third-order expansion around the forward

Theorem 3.5
.

For a smooth function
h
satisfying
H_{4}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(S_{T}^{(y,\delta)}-K)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}-K)\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\delta}_{i}\left(\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]\right.\left.-\partial_{K}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{T}h(F_{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds}-K)\right]\right)

4
Expansion around the Lehman model

The Lehman model consists in simple adjustment of the spot and the strike in the Black-Scholes formula. The spot is adjusted by the present value of near-term dividends, just as in the forward model, while the strike is adjusted by far-away dividends

2

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{t_{i}}{T}\delta_{i}\pi_{i,n}\frac{D_{t_{i}}}{D_{T}}\textmd{ .}

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\pi_{0,n}\frac{S_{0}}{D_{t}}-X^{n}_{t}\right)M_{t}\textmd{ ,}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{0}}{D_{T}}\pi_{0,n}-X^{n}_{T}\textmd{ ,}

In theory, we can expect the expansion around the displaced strike to be more accurate when the dividends are near the maturity date of the option: in the extreme case where the dividends happen at maturity, the zero-th order price stemming from the shifted Black formula is exact. Similarly, we can expect the expansion around the forward to be more accurate when the dividends are near the valuation date. In this regard, being a mix of the two, the expansion around the Lehman model should provide a more stable approximation over the full range of dividend dates.

With a single dividend, the second-order approximations can be 5000 times faster than a finite difference method, with 100 dividends, they are still around forty times faster than a finite difference method. With around 1000 dividends they become of similar performance. For third-order approximations, the threshold is around 100 dividends. 6
Conclusion

Among the first-order expansions, our expansion with the Lehman proxy is as robust as Zhang first-order expansion, but purely analytical, while the latter expansion requires a (simple) numerical solving.

Among the second-order methods, we found the Zhang method to be the most accurate across a wide range of strikes, dividend dates and dividend amounts. Our second-order expansion using the Lehman model as a proxy is as robust as Zhang second-order formula across strikes and expiries, even with unrealistically large dividends, but it has a larger error. It is however possible to compute the Greeks through analytical formulas even in the second and third-order expansions, and the formulas for the Greeks are not more complex than the formula for the price. The second-order Zhang method does not allow this as easily.

Finally, our third-order expansion using the Lehman model as proxy is the most accurate in practical use cases. Its accuracy is not as good for very long term options where dividends would be modelled as cash. Furthermore, it will be significantly slower for a very large number of dividends. In reality however, the practice is to model long term dividends as proportional and those drawbacks may not be relevant.

A classical approach to price American option under the piecewise-lognormal model consists in applying a finite difference method to the corresponding partial differential equation. The accurate and fast expansions for European options prices under the piecewise-lognormal model allow for an interesting alternative method to price American options. Indeed,
Vellekoop and
Nieuwenhuis 2011
show that exercise boundary of an American option, and thus its price, may be computed by solving a non-linear integral equation. The one-dimensional integral involves the cumulative probability density of the piecewise-lognormal model, which is generally unknown, but may easily be approximated using the expansions presented in this paper.
