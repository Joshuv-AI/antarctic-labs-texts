2.1.
The
j
-invariant of a lattice

A
lattice
is defined to be an additive subgroup
L
of
\mathbb{C}
which is generated by two complex numbers
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
that are linearly independent over
\mathbb{R}
. We express this by writing
L=[\omega_{1},\omega_{2}].

The
j
-invariant
j(L)
of a lattice
L
is defined to be the complex number

j\left(L\right)=1728\dfrac{g_{2}\left(L\right)^{3}}{g_{2}\left(L\right)^{3}-27g_{3}\left(L\right)^{2}},

where

g_{2}\left(L\right)=60\sum_{\omega\in L\backslash\{0\}}\dfrac{1}{\omega^{4}}=60\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n=-\infty\\
\left(m,n\right)\neq\left(0,0\right)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{\left(m\tau+n\right)^{4}},

and

g_{3}\left(L\right)=140\sum_{\omega\in L\backslash\{0\}}\dfrac{1}{\omega^{6}}=140\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n=-\infty\\
\left(m,n\right)\neq\left(0,0\right)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{\left(m\tau+n\right)^{6}},

where
L=[1,\tau]
with
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
, the upper-half plane.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
L
be a lattice, and let
\overline{L}
denote the lattice obtained by complex conjugation. Then
g_{2}(\overline{L})=\overline{g_{2}(L)},g_{3}(\overline{L})=\overline{g_{3}(L)}
and
j(\overline{L})=\overline{j(L)}
.

Proof.

From the definition of
g_{2}(L)
, we have

\displaystyle g_{2}(\overline{L})

\displaystyle=60\sum_{\omega\in\overline{L}\backslash\{0\}}\dfrac{1}{\omega^{4}}=60\sum_{\omega\in L\backslash\{0\}}\dfrac{1}{\overline{\omega^{4}}}=\overline{60\sum_{\omega\in L\backslash\{0\}}\dfrac{1}{\omega^{4}}}=\overline{g_{2}(L)}.

A similar argument can be implemented to show that
g_{3}(\overline{L})=\overline{g_{3}(L)}
.

Finally, putting all things together produces

j\left(\overline{L}\right)=1728\dfrac{g_{2}\left(\overline{L}\right)^{3}}{g_{2}\left(\overline{L}\right)^{3}-27g_{3}\left(\overline{L}\right)^{2}}=1728\dfrac{\overline{g_{2}\left(L\right)^{3}}}{\overline{g_{2}\left(L\right)^{3}}-27\overline{g_{3}\left(L\right)^{2}}}=\overline{j(L)},

which is the desired result.
∎

We say that two lattices
L
and
L^{\prime}
are
homothetic
if there is a nonzero complex number
\lambda
such that
L^{\prime}=\lambda L
. Note that homothetic lattices have the same
j
-invariant.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
5
, Theorem 10.9]
)
.

If
L
and
L^{\prime}
are lattices in
\mathbb{C}
, then
j(L)=j(L^{\prime})
if and only if both the lattices
L
and
L^{\prime}
are homothetic.

Next, we prove an important result that will play a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}
be a proper fractional
\mathcal{O}
-ideal, where
\mathcal{O}
is an order in an imaginary quadratic number field. Then
j(\mathfrak{a})
is a real number if and only if the class of
\mathfrak{a}
has order at most 2 in the ideal class group
\Cl\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
.

Proof.

From Proposition
2.1
, we know that
j(\mathfrak{a})
is a real number if and only if
j(\mathfrak{a})=j(\overline{\mathfrak{a}})
. Now Lemma
2.2
tells us that this is only possible when
\mathfrak{a}
and
\overline{\mathfrak{a}}
are homothetic. Or equivalently, when they represent the same ideal in the ideal class group
\Cl\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
. By computing
\mathfrak{a}\overline{\mathfrak{a}}
and comparing the
j
-invariants by a similar argument that we just sketched above, it is easy to see that

\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}^{-1}=\overline{\mathfrak{a}}.

Note that this is not true as a statement about ideals, here we are explicitly referring to ideal classes, that is, elements of
\Cl\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
. Putting all things together gives us the desired result.
∎

As a consequence of the above Proposition, we have the following important corollary:

Corollary 2.4
.

The
j
-invariant
j(\mathcal{O})
is a real number for any order
\mathcal{O}

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Since
t_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]
and the constant term of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is always
\pm 1
, which follows from
[
11
, Section 3]
where the authors state that
t_{n}
is a unit, it follows that
t_{n}^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]
. From Proposition
1.2
, we have

\mathfrak{j}_{n}=\left(t_{n}^{6}-27t_{n}^{-6}-6\right)^{3}.

Therefore, we deduce that
\mathfrak{j}_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]
and thus
\mathbb{Z}\left[\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right]\subseteq\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]
.
Now plugging in
\mathfrak{a}=\mathbb{Z}\left[\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right]
and
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}=\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]
in Proposition
2.5
produces

\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{Z}\left[\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right]\right)=\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]\right)\left[\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]:\mathbb{Z}\left[\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right]\right]^{2},

or equivalently,

\Delta\left(H_{n}\right)=\Delta\left(\mathcal{P}_{n}\right)\left[\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]:\mathbb{Z}\left[\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right]\right]^{2},

which is the desired result.
\blacksquare

1.5

Simply notice that
\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}\left(t_{n}\right)}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right)}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\left(\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right)
, where
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}\left(t_{n}\right)}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right)}
follows from the fact that
\mathbb{Q}\left(\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right)=\mathbb{Q}\left(t_{n}\right)
, as shown in Proposition

. Now, as we did earlier, plugging in
\mathfrak{a}=\mathbb{Z}\left[t_{n}\right]
and
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\mathfrak{j}_{n}\right)}

2.5

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

From the definition of Hilbert class polynomial, recall that its roots are given by

\{j(\mathfrak{a})\mid\mathfrak{a}\in\Cl\left(\mathcal{O}\right)\}.

From Proposition
2.3
, we deduce that the real roots of Hilbert class polynomial
H_{n}
are given by

\{j(\mathfrak{a})\mid\mathfrak{a}\in\Cl\left(\mathcal{O}\right),\textrm{order of }\mathfrak{a}\textrm{ at most }2\}=\Cl\left(n\right)[2].

Therefore, the number of non-real roots of
H_{n}
is given by
\left|\Cl\left(n\right)\right|-\left|\Cl\left(n\right)\left[2\right]\right|=h_{n}-\left|\Cl\left(n\right)\left[2\right]\right|
. Recall that the discriminant of a polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}
is positive if and only if the number of non-real roots of the polynomial is divisible by 4. Putting all things together gives us the desired result.
\blacksquare

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.11

Assume that
n
is a positive squarefree integer. Ye
[
19
, Corollary 1.2]
very recently explicitly computed the discriminant of Hilbert class polynomials
H_{n}
as

\displaystyle\log\left|\Delta\left(H_{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h_{K}}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[\mathfrak{a}]\in\Cl_{K}\\
[\mathfrak{a}]\neq[\mathcal{O}_{K}]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\sum_{X,Y=-\infty}^{\infty}\kappa\left(1-\dfrac{n\left(2AX+BY\right)^{2}+\left(nY-2A\ell\right)^{2}}{4An},\dfrac{B\ell}{n}f_{1}^{(\mathfrak{a})}-\dfrac{2A\ell}{n}f_{2}^{(\mathfrak{a})}+L_{-}^{(\mathfrak{a})}\right)

where
\mathfrak{a}=\left[A,\dfrac{B+\sqrt{-n}}{2}\right],f_{1}^{(\mathfrak{a})}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&B\\
0&A\end{pmatrix},f_{2}^{(\mathfrak{a})}=\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},h_{K}
is the class number of
K=\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{-n}\right)
,
L_{-}^{(\mathfrak{a})}
denotes the lattice
\mathbb{Z}f_{1}^{(\mathfrak{a})}+\mathbb{Z}f_{2}^{(\mathfrak{a})}
, and

\displaystyle\kappa\left(m,\mu\right)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{h_{K}}\left(\sum_{q\,\textrm{inert}}\xi_{q}\left(m,\mu\right)\left(\ord_{q}(m)+1\right)\rho_{K}\left(mn/q\right)\log q+\rho_{K}\left(mn\right)\sum_{q\mid n}\xi_{q}\left(m,\mu\right)\ord_{q}(mn)\log q\right)

where
\xi_{q}
and
\rho_{K}
are as defined in
[
19
, Corollary 1.2]
.

To prove Theorem
1.11
, we will show that
\log 3
never appears in
\kappa\left(m,\mu\right)
for any choice of parameters
m
and
\mu
.
From the definition of
\kappa\left(m,\mu\right)
, it suffices to prove that 3 always splits in
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{-n}\right)
and does not divide
n

n\equiv 11\,(\textup{mod}\,24)

Recall that a prime number
\mathfrak{p}
splits in the imaginary quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{-n}\right)
if and only if
-n
is a nonzero quadratic residue mod
\mathfrak{p}
. The quadratic residues mod 3 are 0 and 1, and it is easy to see that when
n\equiv 11\,(\textup{mod}\,24)

-n\equiv 1\,(\textup{mod}\,3)

-n
is a nonzero quadratic residue mod 3 for all positive squarefree integers
n\equiv 11\,(\textup{mod}\,24)
. This completes the proof.
\blacksquare

We can now replace the Hilbert class polynomials

and their discriminants
\Delta\left(H_{n}\right)
with the Ramanujan polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and their discriminants
\Delta\left(\mathcal{P}_{n}\right)
to simplify computations wherever needed.
Ramanujan polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{n}
can be used in the generation of special curves, such as MNT curves
[
13
,
16
]
, and in the generation of elliptic curves that do not necessarily have prime order
[
1
]
.

Moreover, problems such as primality testing and proving
[
1
]
, the generation of elliptic curve parameters
[
11
]
and the
representability of primes by quadratic forms
[

]
could be considerably improved once we know more about Ramanujan polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and the discriminant and traces.
