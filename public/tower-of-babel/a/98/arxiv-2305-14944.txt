A note on the computational complexity of the moment-SOS hierarchy for polynomial optimization Conference: International Symposium on Symbolic and Algebraic Computation 2023; July 24–27, 2023; Tromsø, Norway International Symposium on Symbolic and Algebraic Computation 2023 (ISSAC 2023), July 24–27, 2023, Tromsø, Norway DOI: 10.1145/3597066.3597075 ISBN: 979-8-4007-0039-2/23/07
Sander Gribling, Sven Polak, Lucas Slot

Abstract
. The moment-sum-of-squares (moment-SOS) hierarchy is one of the most celebrated and widely applied methods for approximating the minimum of an n n -variate polynomial over a feasible region defined by polynomial (in)equalities. A key feature of the hierarchy is that, at a fixed level, it can be formulated as a semidefinite program of size polynomial in the number of variables n n . Although this suggests that it may therefore be computed in polynomial time, this is not necessarily the case. Indeed, as O’Donnell ( ODonnell:bitcomplexity ) and later Raghavendra & Weitz ( RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity ) show, there exist examples where the sos-representations used in the hierarchy have exponential bit-complexity. We study the computational complexity of the moment-SOS hierarchy, complementing and expanding upon earlier work of Raghavendra & Weitz ( RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity ) . In particular, we establish algebraic and geometric conditions under which polynomial-time computation is guaranteed to be possible.



1.
Introduction

Consider the polynomial optimization problem:

(POP)

\begin{split}f_{\min}:=\min\penalty\ &f(\mathbf{x})\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\penalty\ &g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\quad(1\leq i\leq m),\\
&h_{j}(\mathbf{x})=0\quad(1\leq j\leq\ell),\\
&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{split}

where
f,g_{i},h_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
are given
n
-variate polynomials.
The feasible region of
Eq.

POP
is a basic
semialgebraic
set, which we denote by:

S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\penalty\ h_{j}(\mathbf{x})=0\}.

Problems of the form
Eq.

POP
are generally hard and non-convex. They naturally capture several classical combinatorial problems, and have applications in finance, energy optimization, machine learning, optimal control and quantum computing. As they are often intractable, several techniques have been proposed to approximate them. Perhaps the most well-known and studied among these techniques is the so-called
moment-SOS hierarchy
, due to Lasserre
(
Lasserre:seminal
)
and Parillo
(
Parillo:sos
)
. The main idea behind the hierarchy is that one can certify the nonnegativity of a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
on
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
by representing it as a
weighted sum of squares
:

(1)

p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}h_{j}(\mathbf{x})p_{j}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]
are sums of squares,
p_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
and we set
{g_{0}(\mathbf{x})=1}
for convenience. We say that a representation
Eq.

1
is of degree
t
if
\mathrm{deg}(g_{i}\sigma_{i})\leq t
and
\mathrm{deg}(h_{j}p_{j})\leq t
for all
i,j
.
For
t\in\mathbb{N}
, one then obtains a lower bound
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}\leq f_{\min}
on the minimum of
f
by:

(SOS)

\begin{split}\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}:=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{\lambda:\,&f-\lambda\penalty\ \text{has a representation\penalty\ \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{EQ:SOSRepresentation}}\\[-5.0pt]
&\text{ of degree }2t\big\}.\end{split}

For fixed level
t
, this lower bound may be computed by solving a semidefinite program (SDP) involving matrices of size polynomial in
n
.
It is often claimed that one may therefore (approximately) compute
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
in polynomial time, for instance by applying the ellipsoid algorithm. As was noted by O’Donnell
(
ODonnell:bitcomplexity
)
and later by Raghavendra & Weitz
(
RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity
)
, this is not necessarily the case. Indeed, polynomial runtime of the ellipsoid algorithm is only guaranteed when the feasible region of the SDP contains an
inner ball
which is not too small, and is contained in an
outer ball
which is not too large. Informally, these two balls ensure that it is possible to choose the coefficients of the multipliers
\sigma_{i},p_{j}
in the representation
Eq.

1
so that their
bit-complexity
is polynomial in
n
. We call such a representation
compact
.
Roughly speaking,
the following mild algebraic boundedness assumption on the feasible region
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
guarantees the existence of the inner ball for the SDP-formulation of
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
.

Definition 1.

We say that the problem
Eq.

POP
is
explicitly bounded

if
g_{1}(\mathbf{x})=R^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}
for some
R\geq 0
.

Remark 2.

If
Eq.

POP
is explicitly bounded, then
Eq.

SOS
is feasible for all
t\geq\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil
.

Remark 3.

Explicit boundedness is a slightly stronger assumption than the usual Archimedean condition, which merely requires
{R^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}}
to have a representation of the form
Eq.

1
. For example, the semialgebraic set defined by
x^{2}(1-x^{2})\geq 0
is Archimedean
1
1

1

Technically, both the Archimedean and explicit boundedness condition are properties of the description of a semialgebraic set and not of the set itself. However since we always fix the description, we often write ‘the set
S(\bm{g},\bm{h})
is explicitly bounded’.
since we have the identity
1-x^{2}=(1-x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}(1-x^{2})
, but it clearly does not have a constraint of the form
R^{2}-x^{2}
in its description. Likewise, we point out that a compact semialgebraic set is not necessarily Archimedean (see, e.g.
(
Prestel:polopt
, Ex. 6.3.1)
,
(
Laurent:polopt
, Ex. 3.19)
).

The remaining question, then, is whether an outer ball always exists. O’Donnell
(
ODonnell:bitcomplexity
)
shows that in fact, it does not; he constructs an example where every representation
Eq.

1
of
f(\mathbf{x})-\mathrm{sos}(f)_{2}
necessarily involves multipliers
\sigma_{i},h_{j}
whose coefficients are doubly-exponentially large in
n
. Raghavendra & Weitz
(
RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity
)
subsequently show that it is possible to construct such an example even when the equalities
\mathbf{h}
include the boolean constraints
{\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}=0}
, negatively answering a question posed by O’Donnell
(
ODonnell:bitcomplexity
)
. On the positive side, they show conditions under which existence of a compact representation
Eq.

1
is guaranteed. These conditions are met for the reformulation of several well-known combinatorial problems as a
Eq.

POP
, as well as for optimization over the unit hypersphere. To state our and their results, we make the natural assumption that the coefficients of the objective function
f
and the polynomials
g_{i},h_{j}
defining the feasible region
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
of
Eq.

POP
have polynomial bit-complexity.

Assumption 4.

Throughout, we assume that the coefficients of the polynomials
f,g_{i},h_{j}
in
Eq.

POP
have polynomial bit-complexity in
n
and that their degree is independent of
n
. We also assume that the number of constraints (
m+\ell
) is polynomial in
n
.

Theorem 5 (Main positive result of
(
RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity
)
, paraphrased).

Let
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
be a semialgebraic set and let
t\geq\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil
be fixed. Assume that
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is explicitly bounded:
g_{1}(\mathbf{x})=R^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}
for some
1\leq R\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
. Suppose furthermore that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

For any
p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{t}
with
p(\mathbf{x})=0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
, there are
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
such that:

p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}p_{j}(\mathbf{x})h_{j}(\mathbf{x}),

and
\mathrm{deg}(p_{j}h_{j})=O(t)
.

(2)

Let
\mu
be the uniform probability measure on
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
. The
moment matrix

M_{t}(\mu)
defined by:

M_{t}(\mu)_{\alpha,\beta}:=\int_{S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha+\beta}d\mu(\mathbf{x})\quad(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{t})

has smallest non-zero eigenvalue
\geq 2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}
.

(3)

There exists an
\eta\geq 2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}
such that
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq\eta
for all
\mathbf{x}\in S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
and
1\leq i\leq m
.

Then the program
Eq.

SOS
has an (approximately) optimal solution involving only multipliers
\sigma_{i},p_{j}
whose coefficients are at most
2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
.

The conditions of
Theorem

5
have a natural interpretation in the dual formulation of
Eq.

SOS
, the
moment formulation
, which reads (see, e.g.,
(
deKlerkLaurent:survey
)
):

\begin{split}\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}:=\inf_{L\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2t}^{*}}\big\{L(f):\,&L(1)=1,L(p)\geq 0\text{ for }p\\[-5.0pt]
&\text{with a representation\penalty\ \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{EQ:SOSRepresentation}}\\
&\text{of degree }2t\big\}.\end{split}

We refer to
Section

3.3
for a detailed discussion of
Section

1
and the duality between it and
Eq.

SOS
. For now let us introduce two types of matrices associated to linear functionals
L\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2t}^{*}
: the
moment matrix

M_{t}(L)
and, for a polynomial
g
, the localizing matrix
M_{t}(gL)
. Their entries are given by

(2)

\displaystyle M_{t}(L)_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=L(\mathbf{x}^{\alpha+\beta})\quad

\displaystyle(|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq t),

(3)

\displaystyle M_{t}(gL)_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=L(g(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\alpha+\beta})\quad

\displaystyle(|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq t-\lceil\deg(g)/2\rceil).

For ease of notation, we use a subscript
t
for the localizing matrix
M_{t}(gL)
even though it is indexed by monomials of degree at most
t-\lceil\deg(g)/2\rceil
.
Here we use terminology associated to measures since the linear functionals
L
in
Section

1
can be viewed as relaxations of probability measures: any probability measure
\mu
whose support is contained in
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
gives rise to a feasible
L_{\mu}
defined by

(4)

L_{\mu}(p)=\int_{S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})}p(\mathbf{x})d\mu(\mathbf{x})\text{ for }p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2t}.

We can then interpret the conditions in
Theorem

5
as follows: (
2
) says that
M_{t}(L_{\mu})
has smallest non-zero eigenvalue
\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
and (
3
) implies that the same holds for all localizing matrices
M_{t}(gL_{\mu})
with
g\in\mathbf{g}
.

1.1.
Our contributions

The main goal of this paper is to carefully map under what circumstances computation of the bounds
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
(and/or
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}
) and the corresponding representation
Eq.

SOS
is guaranteed to be possible in polynomial time.

Our starting point will be the following proposition which concerns the bounds
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}
. The proof of this proposition consists of a straightforward reformulation of the conditions of strict feasibility for the SDP formulation of
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}
for explicitly bounded polynomial optimization problems, see
Section

3.4
. As we will see there, the conditions (1) and (2) in
Proposition

6
below will guarantee the existence of a ball in the feasible region of (
MOM
), whose radius depends on the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the (localizing) moment matrices corresponding to a feasible solution
L
.

Proposition 6.

Let
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
be a semialgebraic set and let
t\geq\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil
be fixed. Assume that
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
is explicitly bounded:
g_{1}(\mathbf{x})=R^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}
for some
1\leq R\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
. Suppose furthermore that there exists an
L\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2t}^{*}
with
L(1)=1
and the following properties:

(1)

For any
g\in\mathbf{g}
and any
p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{t-\lceil\deg(g)/2\rceil}
, if
L(gp^{2})=0
, then there are
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
such that:

gp^{2}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}p_{j}(\mathbf{x})h_{j}(\mathbf{x}),

and
\deg(p_{j})\leq 2t-\deg(h_{j})
for each
j\in[\ell]
. We recall that
1\in\mathbf{g}
by convention.

(2)

The matrices
M_{t}(L)
and
M_{t}(gL)
(
g\in\mathbf{g}
) have smallest non-zero eigenvalue at least
2^{-\poly(n)}
.

Then for
\varepsilon\geq 2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}
, the bound
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}
(which equals
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
) may be computed in polynomial time in
n
up to an additive error of at most
\varepsilon
.

The statement of
Proposition

6
is stronger than the result of Raghavendra & Weitz in the sense that it guarantees polynomial-time computation of the bound
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
, whereas Theorem
5
only guarantees existence of a compact representation
Eq.

1
.
Furthermore, its conditions do not require
strict positivity
of the inequality constraints
\mathbf{g}
on
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
. As we see below, it therefore applies to several natural settings where
Theorem

5
may not be applied. On the other hand, the first condition of
Proposition

6
is more restrictive than the first condition of
Theorem

5
. We note that it is satisfied for example when
L
is the linear operator associated to a positive Borel measure supported on
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
, and the constraints
\mathbf{h}
form a Gröbner basis of a real radical ideal (cf.
(
Laurent:polopt
, Sec. 2)
).

Our first contribution is a sufficient condition for the second requirement of
Proposition

6
.

Theorem 7.

Let
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
be an explicitly bounded semialgebraic set with
R\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
and let
L\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2t}^{*}
be a feasible solution to
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
fixed. Assume that
L(\mathbf{x}^{\alpha})\in\mathbb{Q}
has polynomial bit-complexity for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2t}
. Then the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
M_{t}(L)
is at least
2^{-\poly(n)}
and the same holds for the localizing matrices
M_{t}(gL)
for
g\in\mathbf{g}
.

Our second contribution is an alternative,
geometric
condition on the feasible region
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
of
Eq.

POP
which guarantees polynomial-time computation of
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
in the special case where the formulation does not contain any equality constraints. We write
S(\mathbf{g})
for
S(\mathbf{g},\emptyset)
.

Theorem 8.

Let
S(\mathbf{g})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a semialgebraic set defined only by inequalities. Assume that the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

S(\mathbf{g})
is explicitly bounded:
g_{1}(\mathbf{x})=R^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}
with constant
1\leq R\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
.

(2)

S(\mathbf{g})
contains a ball of radius

, i.e.,
B(z,r)\subseteq S(\mathbf{g})
for some

.

Then, for fixed

and
\varepsilon\geq 2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}
, the bound
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
may be computed in polynomial time in
n
up to an additive error of at most
\varepsilon
.

The inclusions
B(z,r)\subseteq S(\mathbf{g})\subseteq B(z,R)
for
\frac{1}{r},R\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
are a natural way to ensure that
f
has an approximate minimizer over

whose bit-complexity is
\poly(n)
. Furthermore, they are very reminiscent of the sufficient conditions for solving semidefinite programs in polynomial time, see

1
below.

As we will see in Proposition
5
, it is possible to choose constraints
\mathbf{g}=(g_{i})
, each with

bit-complexity, such that the second condition of Theorem
8
is not satisfied. Notably, the resulting semialgebraic set

does not satisfy the conditions of Theorem
5
or Theorem
6
either.

Finally, as a third contribution, we make explicit the connection between computational aspects of the primal formulation
Eq.

SOS
of the sos-hierarchy, and its dual formulation
Eq.

in terms of moments. This connection is implicitly present in the proof of Theorem
5
in
(
RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity
)
.

Theorem 9.

Let
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
be a semialgebraic set and suppose that the conditions of Theorem
6
or Theorem
8
are satisfied.
Then, for a fixed
t\geq\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a sum-of-squares representation

1
proving nonnegativity of

2.
On the geometric condition

Before we move on to the proofs of our results, let us give some examples of natural settings where they may be applied. In general, Theorem
8
is better equipped to deal with non-discrete semialgebraic sets
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
than Theorem
5
. The third condition of Theorem
5
, which demands in particular that
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0
for each
\mathbf{x}\in S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
, is rather hard to satisfy:

Example 1.

The unit hypercube
[-1,1]^{n}
, the unit ball
B^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the standard simplex
\Delta^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are semialgebraic sets, defined by:

\displaystyle[-1,1]^{n}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:1-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}\geq 0,\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n\},

\displaystyle B^{n}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:1-\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0\},

\displaystyle\Delta^{n}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{x}_{i}\geq 0,\penalty\ 1-\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\geq 0\}.

It is straightforward to see that they each satisfy the conditions of Theorem
8
(after adding a ball constraint
g_{1}(\mathbf{x})=R^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}
if needed). They do not, however, satisfy the third condition of Theorem
5
.

The following proposition gives an alternative sufficient condition related to strict feasibility of
Eq.

POP
, which implies that the conditions for Theorem
8
are satisfied and thus guarantees polynomial-time computability of the bound
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}
.

Proposition 1.

Let
S(\mathbf{g})
be a full-dimensional semialgebraic set contained in a ball of radius
R\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
. Assume that there exists a rational point
\mathbf{x}\in S(\mathbf{g})
with
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0
for all
i
, whose bit-complexity is polynomial in
n
. Then
S(\mathbf{g})
contains a ball of radius
r\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
.

Proof.

As the polynomials
g_{i}
are of fixed degree and have bounded coefficients, their Lipschitz constants on
B(0,R)
can be bounded by
2^{\poly(n)}
. Furthermore, as
\mathbf{x}
has polynomial bit-complexity in
n
, we know that
{g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{Q}}
has polynomial bit-complexity as well, and therefore
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0
implies that
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
. Together, this implies that there exists an
r\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
such that
g_{i}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{y}
with
{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\leq r}
, i.e., such that
S(\mathbf{g})
contains the ball of radius
r\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
centered at
\mathbf{x}
.
∎

2.1.
Semialgebraic sets with large volume contain a large ball

Another class of semialgebraic sets satisfying the conditions of
Theorem

8
are those of sufficiently large volume. In the case that
S(\mathbf{g})
is convex, we have the following consequence of John’s theorem.

Lemma 2.

Let
S(\mathbf{g})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
convex
semialgebraic set. Assume that
S(\mathbf{g})
is contained in a ball of radius
R\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
. Then there exists a constant

r\geq\sqrt{\vol(S(\mathbf{g}))}\cdot 2^{-\poly(n)}

such that
S(\mathbf{g})
contains a (translated) ball of radius
r
.

Proof.

By John’s theorem
(
John:ellipsoid
)
there exists an ellipsoid
E
with center
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

E\subseteq S(\mathbf{g})\subseteq c+n(E-c).

These inclusions show that
n^{n}\vol(E)=\vol(c+n(E-c))\geq\vol(S(\mathbf{g}))
and thus
\vol(E)\geq\vol(S(\mathbf{g}))n^{-n}
. Let
E=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(x-c)^{T}A^{-1}(x-c)\leq 1\}
for a positive definite matrix
A
. Let
v
be an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda
. Then
c\pm\sqrt{\lambda}v\in E\subseteq B(0,R)
and hence
\lambda\leq R^{2}
. Moreover,
\vol(E)=\vol(B(0,1))\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}
where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
A
. Combined with the lower bound on
\vol(E)
, this shows for each
i\in[n]
that

\lambda_{i}\geq\vol(S(\mathbf{g}))n^{-n}(R^{2})^{-(n-1)}\vol(B(0,1))^{-1}.

We thus have
B(z,r)\subseteq E\subseteq S(\mathbf{g})
for
z=c
and

r=\left(\frac{\vol(S(\mathbf{g}))}{n^{n}R^{2(n-1)}\vol(B(0,1))}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\sqrt{\vol(S(\mathbf{g}))}\cdot 2^{-\poly(n)}.\qed

Lemma

2
can be used to show that full-dimensional, rational polytopes contain a large enough ball (which is well known, see, e.g.,
(
schrijveretal:ellipsoidpaper
, Thm. 3.6)
,
(
GacsLovasz:polytope
, Lem. 1-2)
).

Corollary 3.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a full-dimensional polytope defined by rational linear inequalities with polynomial bit-complexity. Then
P
is contained in a ball of radius
R\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
and
{\vol P\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}}
. Using
Lemma

2
,
P
thus contains a ball of radius
r\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
.

Proof.

Let
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}\}
be the vertices of
P
, each of which has polynomial bit-complexity in
n
, as a consequence of Cramer’s rule. As
P
is full-dimensional, we may assume w.l.o.g. that
0\in P
and that
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots p_{n}
are linearly independent. Therefore,

0<\vol\big(\mathrm{span}\{p_{1},\ldots p_{n}\}\big)=\frac{1}{n!}|\det\big(p_{1}\penalty\ p_{2}\penalty\ \ldots\penalty\ p_{n}\big)|.

But since the
p_{i}
’s have polynomial bit-complexity in
n
, we may conclude that

\vol P\geq\frac{1}{n!}|\det\big(p_{1}\penalty\ p_{2}\penalty\ \ldots\penalty\ p_{n}\big)|\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}.\qed

In fact, a result similar to
Lemma

2
can be shown
without
the assumption that
S(\mathbf{g})
is convex. That is,
any
bounded semialgebraic set
S(\mathbf{g})\in B(0,R)
with
R\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
and
{\vol(S(\mathbf{g}))\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}}
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

8
.
To show this, we use an upper bound on the volume of neighborhoods of algebraic varieties from
(
Basu:hausdorff
)
. Obtaining such volume bounds is a problem with a long history, see, e.g.,
(
Wongkew:tubular
;
Lotz:tubular
;
Basu:hausdorff
)
for a discussion.

Proposition 4.

Let
S(\mathbf{g})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

2.2.
A counterexample

To end this section, we show that the second condition of Theorem

Proposition 5.

There exists a full-dimensional semialgebraic set

, defined only by polynomial inequalities

\displaystyle L_{\mu_{z}}(\mathbf{x}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int_{[-r,r]^{n}+z}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}d\mu_{z}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{[-r,r]^{n}}(\mathbf{x}-z)^{\alpha}d\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=L_{\mu_{0}}((\mathbf{x}-z)^{\alpha}).

Second, note that the coefficients
c_{\gamma}
in the expansion of
(\mathbf{x}-z)^{\alpha}
in the monomial basis all have polynomial bit-complexity. The nonzero coefficients are those for which
\gamma
is entrywise less than or equal to
\alpha
, which we denote
\gamma\leq\alpha
. Note that there are at most
h(n,t):={n+t\choose t}
such coefficients. We may thus use
Eq.

11
to find that

(12)

L_{\mu_{z}}(\mathbf{x}^{\alpha})=L_{\mu_{0}}((\mathbf{x}-z)^{\alpha})=\sum_{\gamma\leq\alpha}c_{\gamma}\cdot L_{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{x}^{\gamma})

has polynomial bit-complexity for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq 2t
(as
t
is fixed). It follows that
L_{\mu_{z}}
satisfies the condition of
Theorem

7
.

6.
From moments to sums of squares: proof of Theorem
9

Propositions

6
and
8
show that, under their respective conditions, we can find an additive
\varepsilon
-approximation to
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}=\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
in time
\poly(n,\log(1/\varepsilon))
by solving
Eq.

MOM-SDP
. We now show that, under these conditions, we have a compact sum-of-squares proof for this bound as well.

Theorem 1 (Detailed version of
Theorem

9
).

Let
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
be a semialgebraic set and suppose that the conditions of Theorem
6
or Theorem
8
are satisfied for some fixed
t\geq\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil
. Suppose that
f-\lambda
has a sum-of-squares decomposition:

(13)

f(\mathbf{x})-\lambda=\sigma_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}h_{j}(\mathbf{x})p_{j}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\deg(h_{j}p_{j})\leq 2t
and the sum-of-squares are of the form
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_{i,k}^{2}
for
s_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
with
\deg(g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2})\leq 2t
.
Then, for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a polynomial
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
of degree
2t
such that

(14)

f(\mathbf{x})-\lambda+\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\sigma}_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\tilde{\sigma}_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}h_{j}(\mathbf{x})\tilde{p}_{j}(\mathbf{x})

where, for
i=0,\ldots,m
, we have
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{s}_{i,k}^{2}
for polynomials
\tilde{s}_{i,k}
with bit-complexity
\poly(n,\log(1/\varepsilon))
and
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon R^{2t}2^{\poly(n)}
. In particular, this proves nonnegativity of
f(\mathbf{x})-\lambda+R^{2t}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}
on
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
.

From the SDP-formulation of
Eq.

SOS
it follows that one can bound

by the rank of the matrices involved, i.e., we have
K\leq\binom{n+t}{t}
and thus
K\leq\poly(n)
.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Let
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_{i,k}^{2}
and
p_{j}
as in
Eq.

13
be given. We first show that the coefficients of the
s_{i,k}
are upper bounded.
To do so, let
L
be a linear functional that satisfies the conditions of
Proposition

6
(in the proof of
Theorem

8
we also construct such an
L
).
We then have

L(f-\lambda)=L(\sigma_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}L(g_{i}\sigma_{i})
, and since all terms on the right-hand side are nonnegative, this implies in particular that (using Lemma
3
):

\displaystyle L(g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq L(f-\lambda)\leq R^{2t}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\mathrm{bitcomplexity}(f)),

for all
0\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq k\leq K
,
where we set
g_{0}=1
for convenience. We can now distinguish two cases: (i)
L(g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2})\neq 0
, or (ii)
L(g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2})=0
. In the first case, if
L(g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2})\neq 0
, then we also have

L(g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2})\geq\lambda_{\min}(M_{t}(g_{i}L))\cdot\|s_{i,k}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\lambda_{\min}(M_{t}(g_{i}L))
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
M_{t}(g_{i}L)
,
and so

\|s_{i,k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq R^{2t}\cdot\lambda_{\min}(M_{t}(g_{i}L))^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\mathrm{bitcomplexity}(f)).

For the second case, if on the other hand
L(g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2})=0
, then by condition (1) of
Proposition

6
we have
g_{i}s_{i,k}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}h_{j}q_{j}
for some polynomials
q_{j}
. We may thus remove such
s_{i,k}
from the sum-of-squares part of
Eq.

13
and add them to the ideal part of the certificate.

Using the above bound on the coefficients of the
s_{i,k}
, we now show a bound on the size of the coefficients of the
p_{j}
. The polynomial identity
Eq.

13
allows us to view the coefficients of the
p_{j}
as the solution of a linear system
A\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{b}
where
\mathbf{p}
is a vector that contains the coefficients of the
p_{j}
(
\mathbf{p}
is at most
\ell\binom{n+2t}{2t}
-dimensional),
A
contains coefficients of the
h_{j}
, and
\mathbf{b}
is the
\binom{n+2t}{2t}
-dimensional vector that contains the coefficients of
f(\mathbf{x})-\lambda-\sum_{i=0}^{m}g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. The system
A\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{b}
is feasible,
\mathbf{b}\neq 0
, and therefore
r=\rank(A)
is strictly positive. Let
\overline{A}
be an invertible
r
-by-
r
submatrix of
A
and write
\overline{\mathbf{p}}
and
\overline{\mathbf{b}}
for the restrictions of
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{b}
to the corresponding rows/columns. Cramer’s rule then shows that the
i
th coordinate of
\overline{\mathbf{p}}
can be written as

\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{i}=\frac{\det(\overline{A}_{i})}{\det(\overline{A})}

where
\overline{A}_{i}
is the matrix formed by replacing the
i
th column of
\overline{A}
with the vector
\mathbf{\overline{}}b
. To upper bound
|\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{i}|
we must give a lower bound on
|\det(\overline{A})|
and an upper bound on
|\det(\overline{A}_{i})|
.
Let us first observe that
|\det(\overline{A})|\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
.
Indeed,
\overline{A}
is an invertible
r
-by-
r
matrix with
r\in\poly(n)
, its entries have bit-complexity
\poly(n)
since they correspond to coefficients of the
h_{j}
, and therefore applying
Lemma

1
to a suitable integer multiple of
\overline{A}
shows that
|\det(\overline{A})|\geq 2^{-\poly(n)}
. To upper bound
|\det(\overline{A}_{i})|
it suffices to observe that all entries of
\overline{A}_{i}
are upper bounded in absolute value by
2^{\poly(n)}
: for the
i
th column this follows from the above-derived bound on the
\sigma_{i}
, for the other columns, as before, we observe that they contain coefficients of the
h_{j}
.
By setting all remaining coordinates to zero, we can extend
\overline{\mathbf{p}}
to a feasible solution
\mathbf{p}
of
A\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{b}
.
To summarize, this shows that there exists a sum-of-squares decomposition of
f-\lambda
as in
Eq.

13
where
\|s_{i,k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq R^{2t}\lambda_{\min}(M_{t}(g_{i}L))^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)
for all
i,k
and
\|p_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
for all
j
.

We finally show that rounding each coefficient of this certificate to few bits introduces a small error. For each
i\in[m],j\in[K]
, let
\tilde{s}_{i,k}
be the polynomial
s_{i,k}
with each of the coefficients rounded to the nearest integer multiple of
\varepsilon
. Therefore, for
|\alpha|\leq t
we have
|(s_{i,k})_{\alpha}-(\tilde{s}_{i,k})_{\alpha}|\leq\varepsilon
. Using the identity

\tilde{s}_{i,k}^{2}-s_{i,k}^{2}={(\tilde{s}_{i,k}+s_{i,k})(\tilde{s}_{i,k}-s_{i,k})}
,
this shows
|(\tilde{s}_{i,k}^{2}-s_{i,k}^{2})_{\alpha}|\leq\varepsilon(2(s_{i,k})_{\alpha}+\varepsilon)
and thus
{\|\tilde{s}_{i,k}^{2}-s_{i,k}^{2}\|_{1}}\leq\varepsilon(2\|s_{i,k}\|_{1}+\varepsilon\binom{n+2t}{2t})
. Similarly, for each
j\in[\ell]
, let
\tilde{p}_{j}
be the polynomial
p_{j}
with each of the coefficients rounded to the nearest integer multiple of
\varepsilon
. Then we have
Eq.

14
for the polynomial
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
defined as

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i=0}^{m}g_{i}(\tilde{\sigma}_{i}-\sigma_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}h_{j}(\tilde{p}_{j}-p_{j})

and hence

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}\|g_{i}\|_{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\tilde{s}_{i,k}^{2}-s_{i,k}^{2}\|_{1}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\|h_{j}\|_{1}\|\tilde{p}_{j}-p_{j}\|_{\infty}.

As shown above, we have
K\in\poly(n)
,
\|\tilde{p}_{j}-p_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
, and

\|\tilde{s}_{i,k}^{2}-s_{i,k}^{2}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\left(R^{2t}\lambda_{\min}(M_{t}(g_{i}L))^{-1}\poly(n)+\varepsilon\binom{n+2t}{2t}\right).

Using

we moreover have
\lambda_{\min}(M_{t}(g_{i}L))^{-1}\leq 2^{\poly(n)}
. Combining these estimates shows
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon R^{2t}2^{\poly(n)}
.

The statement
f(\mathbf{x})-\lambda+R^{2t}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}\geq 0
on
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
follows from
Eq.

14
by adding
R^{2t}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}-\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
to both sides of the equation, using the fact that

R^{2t}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{1}-\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2t},

which follows from
Lemma

2
below.
∎

Lemma 2.

Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
be such that for
R>0
we have
R^{2}-x_{i}^{2}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2}
for each
i\in[n]
. For
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq 2t
, we have

R^{|\alpha|}-x^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2t}.

Proof.

We first consider the case of coordinatewise even exponents, i.e.,
R^{2|\alpha|}-x^{2\alpha}
. For this we use induction on
|\alpha|
. The case
|\alpha|=1
holds by assumption and for
|\alpha|>1
we use the identity

R^{2|\alpha|}-x^{2\alpha}=R^{2|\alpha|-2}(R^{2}-x_{j}^{2})+x_{j}^{2}(R^{2|\alpha|-2}-x^{2(\alpha-e_{j})})\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2|\alpha|}

where
j\in[n]
is an index for which
\alpha_{j}>0
and
e_{j}
is the
j
-th unit vector. Here we use that the first term on the right hand side belongs to
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2}
and the second term belongs to
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2|\alpha|}
as
R^{2|\alpha|-2}-x^{2(\alpha-e_{j})}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2|\alpha|-2}
by the induction hypothesis.

Now let
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\gamma|\leq 2t
. We distinguish two cases:
|\gamma|
is odd or even. When
|\gamma|
is odd we write
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq t
and
|\alpha|+1=|\beta|=\frac{|\gamma|+1}{2}
. We then observe that we have the following identity

\displaystyle\frac{(Rx^{\alpha}-x^{\beta})^{2}+R^{2}(R^{2|\alpha|}-x^{2\alpha})+(R^{2|\beta|}-x^{2\beta})}{2R}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2+2|\alpha|}+R^{2|\beta|}-2Rx^{\alpha+\beta}}{2R}=R^{|\gamma|}-x^{\gamma}

where in the last equality we use the identities
R^{|\gamma|}=R^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}=R^{1+2|\alpha|}=R^{2|\beta|-1}
. In the first part of the proof we have shown that
R^{2|\alpha|}-x^{2\alpha},R^{2|\beta|}-x^{2\beta}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2t}
and since moreover
(Rx^{\alpha}-x^{\beta})^{2}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2t}
, the above identity thus shows that
R^{|\gamma|}-x^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{g})_{2t}
.
Finally, for the case where
|\gamma|
is even we use a similar argument. We write
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\alpha|=|\beta|
and use the identity

R^{|\gamma|}-x^{\gamma}=\frac{(x^{\alpha}-x^{\beta})^{2}+(R^{2\alpha}-x^{2\alpha})+(R^{2\beta}-x^{2\beta})}{2}.\qed

7.
Discussion

We have given algebraic and geometric conditions that guarantee polynomial-time computability of the moment-SOS hierarchy for polynomial optimization problems
Eq.

POP
. In the general, explicitly bounded setting, our conditions are similar to the ones considered by Raghavendra & Weitz
(
RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity
)
to show existence of compact sum-of-squares certificates. For full-dimensional feasible regions
S(\mathbf{g})
, we give explicit, geometric conditions, which include for instance that
S(\mathbf{g})
either contains a small ball, a strictly feasible point of low bit-complexity, or has sufficient volume. Furthermore, we make explicit the connection between polynomial-time computability of the bound
\mathrm{mom}(f)_{t}
and the existence of compact feasible solutions to the sum-of-squares formulation
\mathrm{sos}(f)_{t}
, which is only implicitly present in
(
RaghavendraWeitz:bitcomplexity
)
.

A general geometric condition

Theorem

8
applies only when the feasible region
S(\mathbf{g})
of
Eq.

POP
is a full-dimensional semialgebraic set. It would be very interesting to formulate a similar, geometric condition that guarantees polynomial-time computability of the moment-SOS hierarchy in the general case. This requires finding an appropriate analog of the second condition of
Theorem

8
in the setting where
S(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
might not be full-dimensional.

Relation to the complexity of SDP

Our present discussion relates closely to the more general study of the computational complexity of semidefinite programming. It is an open question whether SDPs can be solved to (near-)optimality in polynomial-time. Even the exact complexity of testing feasibility of SDPs is not known. We do know that in the bit-model, membership of the feasibility problem in NP and Co-NP is simultaneous
(
Ramana:sdp
)
. (In the real number model of Blum-Shub-Smale it lies in
{\text{NP}\cap\text{Co-NP}}

(
Ramana:sdp
)
.)

On the positive side, polynomial-time solvability of SDPs is guaranteed when the feasible region contains an ‘inner ball’ and is contained in an ‘outer ball’ of appropriate size
(
schrijveretal:ellipsoid
;
deKlerkVallentin:SDPcomplexity
)
. On the negative side, there are several classes of relatively simple SDPs whose feasible solutions nonetheless have exponential bit-complexity, see, e.g.,
(
Pataki:exponentialsize
)
and the discussion therein.

In principle, these positive and negative results on SDPs provide conditions on
Eq.

SOS
and
Eq.

MOM
that (partly) show when polynomial-time computation can and cannot be guaranteed.
The key difference with our results is that we only impose conditions on the original polynomial optimization problem
Eq.

POP
, rather than on the semidefinite programs resulting from the moment/sum-of-squares relaxations.

Finding exact SOS-decompositions

In the setting of polynomial optimization, it usually suffices to find
approximate
SOS-decompositions, for which one can use (standard) SDP-solvers. The problem of finding exact SOS-decompositions is more complicated. In the general case one could in principle use, for example, quantifier-elimination algorithms
(
Basu:algorithms
;
Renegar:quantifier
)
. In the univariate case specialized algorithms have been developed
(
Schweighofer:univariatealgorithm
;
Chevillardetal:univariateapproximation
)
, see also
(
Magronetal:univariatesos
)
. We note however that none of these methods come with polynomial runtime guarantees.

Appendix A
A quasipolynomial runtime guarantee when
t=O(\log n)

Throughout, we have assumed that the level
t
of the hierarchy is
fixed
. As the SDPs (
SOS
) and (
MOM
) involve matrices of size
h(n,t)={n+t\choose t}\lesssim n^{t}
, this is a natural assumption if one is interested in polynomial-time computation. In the regime

, one obtains instead SDPs of

polynomial size

[
12
]

Our main results carry over to this setting in a natural way, showing quasipolynomial runtime guarantees under the same assumptions on

and

The arguments of
