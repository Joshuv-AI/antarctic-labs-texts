Domain Decomposition Method for Poisson–Boltzmann Equations based on Solvent Excluded Surface
Abhinav Jha, Benjamin Stamm

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a domain decomposition method for the nonlinear Poisson-Boltzmann equation based on a solvent-excluded surface widely used in computational chemistry. The model relies on a nonlinear equation defined in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with a space-dependent dielectric permittivity and an ion-exclusion function that accounts for steric effects. Potential theory arguments transform the nonlinear equation into two coupled equations defined in a bounded domain. Then, the Schwarz decomposition method is used to formulate local problems by decomposing the cavity into overlapping balls and only solving a set of coupled sub-equations in each ball. The main novelty of the proposed method is the introduction of a hybrid linear-nonlinear solver used to solve the equation. A series of numerical experiments are presented to test the method and show the importance of the nonlinear model.



Keywords:
Implicit Solvation Model, Poisson-Boltzmann Equation, Domain Decomposition Method, Solvent Excluded Surface, Stern Layer

1
Introduction

In computational chemistry, the Poisson-Boltzmann (PB) equation is a widely used model for modeling the ionic effects on molecular systems. It belongs to the class of implicit solvation models where the solute is treated microscopically, and the solvent is treated on its macroscopic physical properties, such as dielectric permittivity and ionic strength. Because of this treatment, implicit solvation models are computationally efficient, require fewer parameters, and implicitly consider the sampling over degrees of freedom of the solvent. For this reason, they are widely used in practice and are a popular computational approach to characterize solvent effects in the simulation of properties and processes of molecular systems
[
60
,
52
,
41
,
59
]
.

The history of the PB model can be traced back to 1910 when Gouy
[
19
]
and Chapman
[
8
]
independently used it to equate the chemical potential and relative forces acting on a small adjacent volume in an ionic solution between two plates having different voltages. Later, in 1923, Debye and Hückel generalized the concept by applying it to the theory of ionic solutions, leading to a successful interpretation of thermodynamic data
[
14
]
. A combination of both approaches, including a rigid layer close to the charged surface called the Stern layer and the Gouy-Chapman type diffusive layer, was introduced in 1924 by Stern
[
58
]
. The PB equation we consider in this paper is the realization of the Gouy-Chapman model with the possibility of including Stern layer correction
[
51
]
.

In this paper, we consider the nonlinear Poisson-Boltzmann (NPB) equation, which is used to describe the dimensionless electrostatic potential
\psi(\boldsymbol{x})
and is given by

-\nabla\cdot\left[\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{2}\varepsilon_{s}\sinh\left(\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\frac{1}{\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}}\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad\mathrm{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{3},

for the case of
1:1
electrolyte solvents. Here
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})
represents the relative space-dependent dielectric permittivity,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}
is the absolute dielectric permittivity of vacuum,
\varepsilon_{s}
is the relative dielectric permittivity of the solvent,
\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the charge distribution of the solute,
\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})
is the ion-exclusion function that ensures that the ion concentration tends to zero inside the solute cavity, one in the bulk solvent region, and also accounts for the Stern layer,
\kappa
is the Debye Hückel screening constant, and
\beta=K_{\mathrm{B}}T/e
, where
K_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the temperature in Kelvins (K), and
e
is the elementary charge.

With the assumption that
\psi
satisfies the low-potential condition, i.e.,
|\psi|\ll 1
, the NPB equation can be linearized to get the linear Poisson-Boltzmann (LPB) equation

-\nabla\cdot\left[\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{2}\varepsilon_{s}\psi(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}}\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad\mathrm{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Some standard numerical methods for solving the PB equations include the finite difference method (FDM), the boundary element method (BEM), and the finite element method (FEM). We briefly overview them and mention some ongoing work in these areas. We refer to
[
33
]
for a detailed comparison of the methods.

The FDM is one of the most popular methods for solving the LPB or the NPB equation. It follows the standard finite difference approach, where a grid covers the region of interest, and then different boundary conditions are chosen. Some of the popular software packages using the FDM include UHBD
[
34
]
, Delphi
[
27
]
, MIBPB
[
9
]
, and APBS
[
2
,
23
]
. One drawback of this approach is the increased computational cost with respect to the grid dimension, making it challenging to achieve high accuracy. Some recent developments in this area can be found in
[
36
,
37
,
11
]
.

The BEM is another approach where the LPB equation is recast as an integral equation defined on a two-dimensional solute-solvent interface. This method can be optimized using the fast multipole or hierarchical treecode technique. The AFMPB solver
[
32
,
67
]
uses the former acceleration technique, whereas the TABI-PB
[
18
,
61
]
uses the latter one.
The PB-SAM solver developed by Head-Gordon et al.
[
31
,
64
,
65
]
discretizes the solute-solvent interface (such as the van der Waals (vdW) surface) with grid points on atomic spheres like a collocation method and solves the associated linear system by use of the fast multipole method. However, one drawback is that integral equation-based methods cannot be generalized to NPB. Hybrid approaches combining the FDM and BEM exist; see
[
5
]
.

The FEM approach is one of the most flexible approaches for solving the PB equation. It can solve both the linear and the nonlinear PB, providing more flexible mesh refinement and proper convergence analysis
[
10
]
. A posteriori error estimation also exists for this method
[
24
,
38
]
. The SDPBS and SMPBS offer fast and efficient approximations of the size-modified PB equation
[
62
,
66
,
22
,
63
]
. Recently a hybrid approach combining the FEM and BEM has been proposed in
[
6
]
.

Now, we briefly overview the domain decomposition methods recently proposed in the context of implicit solvation models. Recently, in
[
46
]
, a domain decomposition algorithm for the LPB equation (ddLPB), which uses a particular Schwarz domain decomposition method, has been developed. The ddLPB method has been developed for the LPB equation defined on the vdW-cavity with discontinuous
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})
and didn’t include a Stern layer correction, i.e.,
\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})
. A further linear scaling approach for computing the first derivatives and eventually the forces has been presented in
[
21
]
following the ideas from
[
35
]
. The ideas of the ddLPB method can be traced back to the domain decomposition methods proposed for the conductor-like screening model (COSMO), (ddCOSMO)
[
7
,
28
,
30
]
and the polarizable continuum model (PCM), (ddPCM)
[
56
,
17
,
40
]
. These methods do not require any mesh or grid of the molecular surface, are easy to implement, and ddCOSMO is about two orders of magnitude faster than the state-of-the-art
[
29
]
.
An open-source software
ddX
has been released, which encompasses all these methods
[
39
]
.
In general, domain decomposition methods for diffusion problems are known not to be scalable unless a coarse correction is used. However, for non-globular structures such as proteins, recent investigations
[
12
,
13
,
49
]
have shown the scalability of ddCOSMO without coarse correction.

An essential feature of the implicit solvation model is the choice of the solute-solvent boundary. Most methods use the vdW-cavity or the solvent-accessible surface (SAS),
[
26
]
as they are topologically simple, but they do not describe the solute-solvent interaction well. The solvent-excluded surface (SES) first developed in
[
50
]
is one of the few surfaces that captures the interaction quite well. In fact, it has also been observed that in some chemical calculations, the SES cavity yields better results
[
48
,
43
]
. In
[
44
]
, a mathematical framework was provided for computing the SES. As mentioned, the ddLPB method was developed with the vdW-cavity and can be extended to the SAS cavity. The ddPCM method with the SES cavity (ddPCM-SES) was proposed and studied in
[
45
]
.

This work proposes a new domain decomposition method for the NPB equation based on the SES (ddNPB-SES). We present a Schwarz domain decomposition method for the NPB equation that includes steric effects, i.e., the presence of a Stern layer, which was missing from the previous work,
[
46
]
. A further development from the previously mentioned work is using the SES for the solute-solvent boundary. With the help of the SES, we introduce a continuous relative dielectric permittivity function
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})
and an ion-exclusion function
\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})
. Based on the definition of
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})
, we develop a hybrid linear-nonlinear solver that uses local spectral methods for the discretization, using spherical harmonics and Legendre polynomials as basis functions. This contrasts with the traditional numerical techniques for solving the NPB equation, where a nonlinear solver is used in the whole domain. The development of a hybrid linear-nonlinear solver becomes important as the low-potential condition is usually not satisfied near the surface of the molecules. Hence, we use a nonlinear model near the surface of a molecule and a linear model in the bulk solvent region. The nonlinearity of the model also plays a critical role in the computation of the electrostatic solvation energy
E_{s}
, as under the low-potential condition, the solvent’s contribution is not considered. The proposed method can also solve the LPB equation defined on the SES, i.e., ddLPB-SES; hence, this work encompasses both models. Therefore, this work is an extension of the ddLPB method,
[
46
]
, and introduces a new method, ddNPB-SES, for treating the important nonlinear effects. The major contribution of this work can be summarized as:

\bullet

Introduction of a domain decomposition method for the NPB equation.

\bullet

Use of a Solvent-Excluded Surface for the solute-solvent boundary.

\bullet

Inclusion of steric effects.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that deals with all three points mentioned above. This work can also be regarded as a PDE-IE (Integral Equation) coupling of the NPB equation.
In this regard, the recent work
[
6
]
is related as it deals with such a coupling, but it considers the LPB equation only.

Finally, let us mention that the method’s efficiency is based on the scalability of Poisson and screened Poisson problems in the cavity of the molecule.
Therefore, the scalability discussed above also applies in this setting.

The breakdown of the paper is as follows: In Sec.
2
, we derive the NPB equation and introduce different solute-solvent boundaries. We also present a continuous relative dielectric permittivity function and an ion-exclusion function based on the SES. In Sec.
3
, we transform the problem into different domains, introduce a global strategy for solving them, and lay out the domain decomposition method. In Sec.
4
, we derive single-domain solvers for the homogeneous screened Poisson (HSP) and the generalized screened Poisson (GSP) equation in a unit ball. Next, in Sec.
5
, we present a comprehensive numerical study for the ddNPB-SES method for molecules ranging from one to twenty-four atoms. Lastly, in Sec.
6
, we present a summary and an outlook.

2
Problem Statement

We represent the solvent by a polarizable and ionic continuum. The freedom of the movement of ions is modeled by Boltzmann statistics, i.e., the Boltzmann equation is used to calculate the local ion density
c_{i}
of the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
type of ion as follows

c_{i}=c_{i}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{-W_{i}}{K_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right),

(1)

where
c_{i}^{\infty}
is the bulk ion concentration at an infinite distance from the solute molecule, and
W_{i}
is the work required to move the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
type of ion to a given position from an infinite distance.

The electrostatic potential
\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})
of a general implicit solvation model is described by the Poisson equation as follows

-\nabla\cdot\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]=\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho^{\mathrm{ions}}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\mathrm{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{3},

(2)

where
\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{O}\left(1/|\boldsymbol{x}|\right)
as
|\boldsymbol{x}|\rightarrow\infty
. Here
\rho^{\mathrm{ions}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the charge distribution of the solvation system.

We derive the PB equation using Eq. (
1
) and Eq. (
2
) as

-\nabla\cdot\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]=\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{ions}}}c_{i}^{\infty}z_{i}e\exp\left(\frac{-z_{i}e\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})}{K_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right)\quad\mathrm{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{3},

(3)

where
z_{i}
is the (partial) charge of the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
type of ion and
N_{\mathrm{ions}}
are the number of ion types.

In the case of a
1:1
electrolyte, there are two opposite charge ions
(+e
and
-e)
and we then get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}c_{i}^{\infty}z_{i}e\exp\left(\frac{-z_{i}e\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})}{K_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right)

\displaystyle=ce\exp\left(\frac{-e\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})}{K_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right)-ce\exp\left(\frac{e\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})}{K_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right)

\displaystyle=-2ce\sinh\left(\frac{e\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})}{K_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right).

(4)

Then substituting Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
3
) we obtain

-\nabla\cdot\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})2ce\sinh\left(\frac{e\tilde{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})}{K_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right)=\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\mathrm{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{3},

(5)

the nonlinear Poisson-Boltzmann equation (NPB).

2.1
Solute Probe

One of the important properties of the implicit solvation model is the choice of the solute probe and, accordingly, the solute-solvent boundary. A straightforward choice is using the van der Waals (vdW) surface, i.e., the topological boundary of the union of solute’s vdW-atoms with experimentally fitted radii. Another choice is the solvent-accessible surface (SAS), which is defined by tracing the center of an idealized (spherical) solvent probe (representing a solvent molecule) when rolling over the solute molecule. The region enclosed by the SAS is called the SAS-cavity, which we denote by
\Omega_{\mathrm{SAS}}
and its boundary by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{SAS}}
.

The vdW and the SAS are topologically not the correct answers to the cavity problem as they poorly describe the region where the solvent can touch. However, as they are topologically simple, they are widely used in numerical computations. Another solute-solvent boundary is the solvent-excluded surface (SES), which represents the boundary of the region where the probe has no access due to the presence of the solute. In other words, SES is the boundary of the union of all spherical probes that do not intersect the vdW balls of the solute molecule. The region enclosed by the SES is the SES cavity, which we denote by
\Omega_{\mathrm{SES}}
and the boundary by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{SES}}
. The mathematical characterization of the surface can be found in
[
44
]
. From the geometrical viewpoint, the SES is smoother than the vdW and SAS cavities.

The NPB equation does not consider the absorbing ions’ finite size; hence, the ionic concentration can exceed the maximally allowed coverage near the surface. These are referred to as steric effects. The NPB model is modified to include a Stern layer,
[
4
]
to account for these steric effects.

Now, we set certain notations. We assume that the molecule comprises
M
atoms and the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
atom has center
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and vdW radii
r_{i}
. The solvent probe radius is denoted by
r_{p}
, and the Stern layer length is denoted by
a
. In practice, the SAS cavity is defined by rolling a probe of radius
r_{p}
; to incorporate the Stern layer instead, we use a probe of radius
r_{p}+a
. Including the Stern layer means the solute touches the solvent after distance
a
. If we use a probe of radius
r_{p}
, we have a non-smooth surface near the solute-solvent interaction (see Fig.
1
(left)). Therefore, we use a probe of radius
r_{p}+a
to restore smoothness near the interaction (see Fig.
1
(right)). The boundary
\Gamma_{\lambda^{-}}
denotes the boundary where the ion concentration starts. More details will be given in the following subsection. Furthermore, for each atom, we define an “enlarged” ball
\Omega_{i}
with center
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and radius
R_{i}=r_{i}+r_{p}+a+r_{0}
, where
r_{0}
is a non-negative constant used to control the nonlinear regime. Outside of the enlarged balls, one can use the LPB equation as the low potential condition is satisfied. The parameter
r_{0}
gives a good idea for the cutoff where we switch from the nonlinear model to the linear model.

The SES cavity is entirely covered by the union of
\Omega_{0}
of enlarged balls, i.e.,

\Omega_{\mathrm{SES}}\subset\Omega_{0}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\Omega_{i},\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\Omega_{i}=B_{R_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),

and
B_{R_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
denotes a ball with center
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and radius
R_{i}
. We also denote the bulk solvent region by
\Omega_{\infty}:=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{0}^{\mathrm{C}}
and the boundary of
\Omega_{0}
by
\Gamma_{0}
.

Let
f_{\mathrm{SAS}}
denote the signed distance function to
\Omega_{\mathrm{SAS}}
(i.e., negative inside the SAS cavity and positive outside the SAS cavity). We then have a mathematical characterization of the two cavities

\Omega_{\mathrm{SES}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\ f_{\mathrm{SAS}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq-r_{p}-a\right\}\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \Omega_{0}=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\ f_{\mathrm{SAS}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq r_{0}\right\}.

Also, we can characterize their boundary surfaces by

\Gamma_{\mathrm{SES}}=f^{-1}_{\mathrm{SAS}}(-r_{p}-a)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\Gamma_{0}=f^{-1}_{\mathrm{SAS}}(r_{0}).

3
Formulation of the Problem

In this section, we reduce our problem to different domains by introducing a new hybrid linear-nonlinear PB model and the domain decomposition strategy used to solve the resulting equation.

In the previous section, we introduced the dielectric permittivity and ion-exclusion functions. Let
\psi=e\tilde{\psi}/K_{\mathrm{B}}T
denote the dimensionless electrostatic potential, then the NPB equation reduces to

-\nabla\cdot\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\beta\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})2ce\sinh\left(\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\mathrm{in}\ \ \mathbb{R}^{3}.

(8)

Since the support of
\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is contained in
\Omega_{0}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}(\rho^{\mathrm{sol}})\subset\Omega_{0}
, by using the definition of
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})
, Eq. (
8
) reduces to

-\varepsilon_{s}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}\beta\Delta\psi(\boldsymbol{x})+2ce\sinh\left(\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=0\quad\mathrm{in}\ \ \Omega_{\infty}.

In the bulk solvent region
\Omega_{\infty}
, we can assume that the potential
\psi
satisfies the low potential condition, i.e.,
|\psi|\ll 1
, and hence we can linearise the above equation to get a homogeneous screened Poisson (HSP) equation as

-\Delta\psi(\boldsymbol{x})+\kappa^{2}\psi(\boldsymbol{x})=0\quad\ \ \mathrm{in}\ \ \Omega_{\infty},

(9)

where
\kappa^{2}=2ce/\beta\varepsilon_{s}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}
is the square of the Debye Hückel screening constant.

Next, we note that inside the cavity
\Omega_{0}
, we still keep the NPB equation of the form

-\nabla\cdot\left[\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{2}\varepsilon_{s}\sinh\left(\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\frac{1}{\beta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}}\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\mathrm{in}\ \ \Omega_{0}.

(10)

Along the solute-solvent boundary
\Gamma_{0}
, equations (
9
)–(
10
) satisfy the jump conditions

3.1
Transformation of the Problem

We notice that we have two equations defined in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, one inside the cavity,
\Omega_{0}
, and one in the bulk solvent region
\Omega_{\infty}
in combination with interface conditions. In this subsection, we transform our problem and define them as two coupled PDEs in
\Omega_{0}
.

Let us denote

as the Dirichlet trace from

to

can be represented by a single-layer potential,

. From the continuity of the single layer operator, we can extend

to

4.1
HSP Solver

In
[
46

, an HSP solver was developed for the ddLPB meth-od. One can use the same solver for ddNPB as well. For completeness, we outline the main ideas in this subsection.

The HSP equation in the unit ball is given by

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{\mathrm{e}}+\kappa^{2}u_{\mathrm{e}}

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\ \ \ B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0}),

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{e}}

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{e}}\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on}\ \ \ \mathbb{S}^{2},

(26)

where
B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0})
denotes the unit ball at center
\boldsymbol{0}
and for
j=1,\dots,M
,
\phi_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{x})=h_{\mathrm{e},j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}+R_{j}\boldsymbol{x})
for the HSP equation in the sub-domain
\Omega_{j}
.

The solution of Eq. (
4.1
) in
H^{1}(B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0}))
can be written as

u_{\mathrm{e}}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\left[\phi_{\mathrm{e}}\right]_{\ell}^{m}\frac{i_{\ell}(r)}{i_{\ell}(1)}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),\quad 0\leq r\leq 1,\ \ 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\quad 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi,

where
i_{\ell}
is the modified spherical Bessel function of the first kind, see
[
1
, Chapter 14]
,
Y_{\ell}^{m}
denotes the (real orthonormal) spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
and order
m
defined on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and

\left[\phi_{\mathrm{e}}\right]_{\ell}^{m}=\int_{\partial B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0})}\phi_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{s})Y_{\ell}^{m}(\boldsymbol{s})d\boldsymbol{s},

is the real coefficient of
u_{\mathrm{e}}
corresponding to the mode
Y_{\ell}^{m}
. Now
u_{\mathrm{e}}
can be numerically approximated by
\overline{u}_{\mathrm{e}}
in the discretization space spanned by truncated basis of spherical harmonics
\left\{Y_{\ell}^{m}\right\}_{0\leq\ell\leq\ell_{\max},-\ell\leq m\leq\ell}
, defined by

\overline{u}_{\mathrm{e}}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\ell,m}\left[\tilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{e}}\right]_{\ell}^{m}\dfrac{i_{\ell}(r)}{i_{\ell}(1)}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),\quad 0\leq r\leq 1,\quad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\quad 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi,

(27)

where

\sum_{\ell,m}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\max}}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell},

\ell_{\max}
denotes the maximum degree of spherical harmonics and

\left[\tilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{e}}\right]_{\ell}^{m}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{leb}}}\omega_{n}^{\mathrm{leb}}\phi_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n})Y_{\ell}^{m}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n}).

To approximate the integration, we use the Lebedev quadrature formula
[
20
]
where
\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
are the Lebedev quadrature points
[
20
]
,
\omega_{n}^{\mathrm{leb}}
are the corresponding weights, and
N_{\mathrm{leb}}
are the number of Lebedev quadrature points. The reason we use Lebedev quadrature over other quadrature is that it gives exact integration for spherical harmonics up to a given order (depending on the quadrature points), achieving thus good accuracy for few point evaluations, see
[
7
]
.

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\ \ \ B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0}),

\displaystyle u(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\mathrm{on}\ \ \ \mathbb{S}^{2},

where for

For a Laplace equation (see

\displaystyle=0\quad

(29)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left[\tilde{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\left(w+\hat{u}_{1}\right)(\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\left(\overline{w+\hat{u}_{1}}\right)\right)\left(w+\hat{u}_{1}\right)(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x})\ \

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\ \ B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0}),

\displaystyle w(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=0\ \

\displaystyle\mathrm{on}\ \ \mathbb{S}^{2},

which can be further reduced to

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left[\tilde{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla w(\boldsymbol{x})\right]+\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\left(\overline{w+\hat{u}_{1}}\right)\right)w(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\ \ B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0}),

\displaystyle w(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on}\ \ \mathbb{S}^{2},

(30)

where
\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x})+\nabla\cdot\left[\tilde{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\hat{u}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]-\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\left(\overline{w+\hat{u}_{1}}\right)(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\hat{u}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Throughout this section we use
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\overline{w}^{\hat{u}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)
to denote
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\left(\overline{w+\hat{u}_{1}}\right)(\boldsymbol{x})\right)
.

From the definition of
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x}),\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and the fact that the vdW-ball,
B_{r_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\subset\Omega_{j}
, the reaction potential
\psi_{\mathrm{r}}
defined in Eq. (
3.3
) is harmonic in the smaller ball
B_{r_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
and consequently
u(\boldsymbol{x})
in Eq. (
4.2
) and
w(\boldsymbol{x})
in Eq. (
4.2
) are harmonic in
B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
, where

\delta=\frac{r_{j}}{r_{j}+r_{p}+r_{0}+a}\in(0,1).

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}=B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0})\setminus B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
be the region between
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
and we define the subspace
H_{0,\delta}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})\subset H^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})
as follows

H_{0,\delta}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})=\left\{w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta}):w|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}=0\right\}.

We seek a weak solution restricted to
H_{0,\delta}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})
, for that we write the variational formulation as: Find
w\in H_{0,\delta}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})
such that:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla w(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\overline{w}^{\hat{u}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)w(\boldsymbol{x})\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}\left(\mathcal{T}w\right)\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad

\displaystyle\forall\ \ \tilde{w}\in H_{0,\delta}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta}),

(31)

where we used
\tilde{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
on
\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
and
\mathcal{T}
is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator of the harmonic extension in
B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
.

Assume that we have an expansion of the Dirichlet boundary condition, then
w
is restricted to
\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
as follows

w|_{\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}(\delta,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\ell,m}\gamma_{\ell m}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),\quad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi;\quad 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi.

(32)

Then, we can extend
w
harmonically by

w|_{B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\ell,m}\gamma_{\ell m}\left(\frac{r}{\delta}\right)^{\ell}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),\quad 0\leq r\leq\delta;\quad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi;\quad 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi.

(33)

Let
\boldsymbol{n}_{\delta}
be the unit normal vector pointing outward on the ball
\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
with respect to the ball
B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
. As a consequence we compute the normal derivative
\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{\delta}}w=\nabla w\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\delta}
on
\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
as

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{T}w\right)|_{B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}(\delta,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{\delta}}w(\delta,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m}\gamma_{\ell m}\left(\frac{\ell}{\delta}\right)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),\qquad

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta\leq\pi;\quad 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi.

(34)

Remark 10

The bilinear form on the left side of Eq. (
4.2
),

\displaystyle a(w,\tilde{w})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla w(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x})+\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\overline{w}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)w(\boldsymbol{x})\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x})

(35)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}\left(\mathcal{T}w(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x}),

is positive definite and symmetric by the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
and the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{T}
for given
w(\boldsymbol{x})
.

4.2.1
Galerkin Solution in Unit Ball

For finding the functions belonging to
H_{0,\delta}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})
; we introduce the radial functions

\varrho_{i}(r)=(1-r)L_{i}^{\prime}\left(\frac{2(r-\delta)}{1-\delta}-1\right),

i.e.,
\varrho_{i}(1)=0
. Here
L_{i}
denotes the Legendre polynomial of the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
degree. We then discretize both the radial part and the spherical part of the unknown
w
by linear combination of the basis element
\{\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\}
with
1\leq i\leq N;0\leq\ell\leq\ell_{\max};
and
-\ell\leq m\leq\ell
, where
N
denotes the maximum degree of Legendre polynomials and
\ell_{\max}
denotes the maximum number of spherical harmonics. We denote the space spanned by these elements by
\mathcal{B}_{N,\ell_{\max}}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})
which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{N,\ell_{\max}}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{span}\left\{\varrho_{i}(r)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi):1\leq i\leq N,\quad 0\leq\ell\leq\ell_{\max},\quad-\ell\leq m\leq\ell\right\}

\displaystyle\subset H_{0,\delta}^{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta}).

Then, the Galerkin discretisation of the variational formulation (
4.2
) reads: Find
w_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}_{N,\ell_{\max}}(\mathcal{D)}
, such that

a\left(w_{\mathcal{B}},\varrho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{f}\varrho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},\quad\forall\ \ \varrho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}\in\mathcal{B}_{N,\ell_{\max}}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta}),

(36)

where
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is given by Eq. (
35
). Since
w_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}_{N,\ell_{\max}}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})
, we can write
w_{\mathcal{B}}
in the form

w_{\mathcal{B}}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell,m}\left[\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\right]_{i\ell}^{m}\varrho_{i}(r)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),\quad\forall\ \ \delta\leq r\leq 1;\ 0\leq\theta\leq\pi;\ \ 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi,

(37)

and consequently

\left(\mathcal{T}w_{\mathcal{B}}\right)|_{B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}(\delta,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell,m}\left[\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\right]_{i\ell}^{m}\left(\frac{\ell}{\delta}\right)\varrho_{i}(\delta)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),\ \forall\ 0\leq\theta\leq\pi;\ 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi,

(38)

where
\left[\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\right]_{i\ell}^{m}
is the real coefficient of
w_{\mathcal{B}}
corresponding to the node
\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}
.

Substituting Eq. (
37
) and Eq. (
38
) in Eq. (
36
) and taking the test function
\varrho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}=\varrho_{j}(r)Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(\theta,\varphi)
, we then obtain a system of equations for all
1\leq j\leq N,0\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell_{\max},
and
-\ell^{\prime}\leq m^{\prime}\leq\ell^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell,m}\left[\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\right]_{i\ell}^{m}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\Big(\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\Big)\cdot\nabla\Big(\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\Big)+\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\overline{w}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\hat{u}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\ell}{\delta}\int_{\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\Bigg)=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{f}\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}.

(39)

In order to write a system of equations, we define the index

k=N\left(\ell^{2}+m+1\right)+i\in\{1,2,\dots,N\left(\ell_{\max}+1\right)^{2}\},

which corresponds to the triple
(i,\ell,m)
. Let
k
corresponds to
(i,\ell,m)
and
k^{\prime}
corresponds to
(j,\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime})
. Then Eq. (
4.2.1
) can be recast as

\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{A}}}\ \overline{X}_{\mathrm{r}}=\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{F}}},

(40)

where
\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{A}}}
is a matrix of dimension
N(\ell_{\max}+1)^{2}\times N(\ell_{\max}+1)^{2}
with elements
\left(\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{A}}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}
for all
1\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq N(\ell_{\max}+1)^{2}
, defined by

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{A}}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\left(\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\right)+\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\left(\overline{w}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\hat{u}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\ell}{\delta}\int_{\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}},

(41)

\overline{X}_{\mathrm{r}}
is the column vector of
N\left(\ell_{\max}+1\right)^{2}
unknowns
\left[\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\right]_{i\ell}^{m}
, i.e.,

(\overline{X}_{\mathrm{r}})_{k}=\left[\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\right]_{i\ell}^{m},\quad\forall\ \ k\in\{1,\dots,N(\ell_{\max}+1)^{2}\},

(42)

and
\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{F}}}
is a column vector with
N(\ell_{\max}+1)^{2}
entries defined by

(\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{F}}})_{k^{\prime}}=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\tilde{f}\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}},\quad\forall\ \ k^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N(\ell_{\max}+1)^{2}\}.

(43)

To summarize, in order to solve Eq. (
4.2
) we need to solve Eq. (
40
) to obtain
\left[\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\right]_{i\ell}^{m}
and then obtain an approximate solution
w_{\mathcal{B}}(r,\theta,\varphi)\in\mathcal{B}_{N,\ell_{\max}}(\mathcal{D}_{\delta})
according to Eq. (
37
). Since,
w_{\mathcal{B}}
is harmonic in
B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
,
w_{\mathcal{B}}
can be extended harmonically in the ball
B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
following Eq. (
33
) and hence we obtain an approximate solution defined in
B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0})
to Eq. (
4.2
).

Remark 11

The final thing remaining in the discretization process is the evaluation of the integrals in
\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{A}}}
and
\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{F}}}
. We have integrals over the annulus
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
and
\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
. Here, we follow the ideas from
[
45
]
; for completeness, we present a brief overview. The integral over
\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{\delta}\int_{\partial B_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{0})}\varrho_{i}Y_{\ell}^{m}\varrho_{j}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\ell\delta\varrho_{i}(\delta)\varrho_{j}(\delta)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{\ell}^{m}Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\ell\delta\varrho_{i}(\delta)\varrho_{j}(\delta)\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}},

where
\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
and
\delta_{mm^{\prime}}
are the Kronecker delta functions.

The integral over
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
can be divided into radial and spherical parts. Let
h\in L^{1}\left(B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0})\right)
then the integral of
h
over
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
can be written separately as

\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}h(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\delta}^{1}r^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}h(r,\boldsymbol{s})d\boldsymbol{s}dr,\quad\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

and
\boldsymbol{x}=r\boldsymbol{s}
. The spherical part can be computed using the Lebedev quadrature
[
20
]
. For the radial part we use the Legendre-Gauß-Lobatto (LGL) quadrature rule
[
42
]
defined by quadrature points
x_{m}\in[-1,1]
and the quadrature weights
\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{lgl}}
,
1\leq m\leq N_{\mathrm{lgl}}
for
N_{\mathrm{lgl}}
quadrature points. Using the change of variable

r=\tfrac{1-\delta}{2}\left(x+1\right)+\delta,\quad x\in[-1,1],

we approximate the integral by the following quadrature rule

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}h(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}\approx

\displaystyle\tfrac{1-\delta}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{lgl}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{leb}}}\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{lgl}}\omega_{n}^{\mathrm{leb}}\left(\tfrac{1-\delta}{2}(x_{m}+1)+\delta\right)^{2}h\left(\tfrac{1-\delta}{2}(x_{m}+1)+\delta,\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\right).

5
Numerical Simulations

This section presents some numerical studies for the ddNPB-SES method and compares them with the ddLPB-SES method.

We first introduce the electrostatic solvation energy for the NPB equation. For the NPB, the solvation energy becomes more involved than the LPB. We follow the definition of the energy from
[
55
]
, which is given by

\displaystyle E_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\rho^{\mathrm{sol}}(\boldsymbol{x})\psi_{\mathrm{r}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{\beta^{2}\kappa^{2}\varepsilon_{s}}{8\pi}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\Big[\psi_{\mathrm{r}}(\boldsymbol{x})\sinh\left(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)-2\left\{\cosh\left(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)-1\right\}\Big].

Remark 12

The second term in
E_{s}
is referred to as the electrostatic stress, and the last term is the osmotic pressure, see
[
55
]
. In the case of the LPB equation, we can linearize the last two terms and get
E_{s}
corresponding to the ddLPB method (see
[
46
]
).

Now, we provide more details about solving the system of nonlinear equations. We notice that in matrix
\overline{\mathrm{\textbf{A}}}
, the first and third parts in Eq.(
4.2.1
) are constant throughout the iterative process, and hence they need to be computed only once and can be used later. For computing the solution at the
(\nu)^{\mathrm{th}}
iterative step, we follow a damping approach in which

X^{(\nu)}=X^{(\nu-1)}+\omega\left(X^{\mathrm{aux}}-X^{(\nu-1)}\right),\qquad\nu\geq 1

where
X^{\mathrm{aux}}
is obtained by solving Eq. (
40
) and
\omega\in(0,1]
is a damping parameter. One can choose a constant or an adaptive damping parameter. For nonlinear iterations, an adaptive choice of
\omega
yields better results (see
[
54
]
), but in this work, the constant damping gave satisfactory results. Hence, we fix
\omega=0.5
for the simulations, which is obtained empirically.

We have three different iteration loops in our method. We refer to the global iteration process as outer iterations (indexed by
\mathtt{k}
), and the convergence is reached when the relative increment satisfies

\mathtt{inc}_{\mathtt{k}}:=\frac{|E_{s}^{\mathtt{k}}-E_{s}^{\mathtt{k}-1}|}{|E_{s}^{\mathtt{k}}|}\leq\mathtt{tol},\qquad\mathrm{for}\ \ \mathtt{k}\geq 1,

and given tolerance,
tol
. The second is the dd-iterative loop for the GSP solver (Eq. (
3.3
)–(
21
)); here, the stopping criteria is the relative reduction of the reaction potential but with a relaxed stopping criteria of
10\times\mathtt{tol}
. Lastly is the nonlinear loop for solving Eq. (
40
). Here we again use the relative reduction of the solution vector with the stopping criteria of
100\times\mathtt{tol}
. Along with this stopping criteria, we also have a maximal number of iterative loops for each iteration. To solve the outer iterative loop, we start with the zero solution as the initial iterate, but we use the solution from the previous iterative step for subsequent iterations. We follow the same procedure for the other iterative loops as well. Unless specified by default, we set
\mathtt{tol}=10^{-6}
and solve the system of equations using the LU decomposition method.

Lastly, we would like to mention the constants arising in the method. By default, we assume the solute cavity in a vacuum and the solvent to be water. Hence, the relative dielectric permittivity of the solute cavity is one and
\varepsilon_{s}=78.54
at room temperature,
T=298.15
K. Further, we set the Debye Hückel constant,
\kappa=0.104
Å
-1
for an ionic strength of
0.1
molar. We use the Hartree energy unit system and thus
4\pi\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}=1
. We read the input files in Å units, but then we internally convert them to the Hartree units, and hence, the distance is represented by atomic units (a.u.). The atomic centers, charges, and the vdW radii are obtained from the
PDB
files
[
3
]
and the
PDB2PQR
package
[
15
,
16
]
. Finally, we set the probe radius
r_{p}=1.4
Å. All the simulations were performed on the in-house
MATLAB
.

6
Summary and Outlook

This paper proposes a new method for solving the nonlinear Poisson-Boltzmann equation using the domain decomposition paradigm for the solvent-excluded surface.

The original problem defined in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is transformed into two coupled equations described in the bounded solute cavity based on potential theory arguments. An enlarged cavity was defined for each atom encompassing the Stern layer and a nonlinear regime. Then, the Schwarz domain decomposition method was used to solve these two problems by decomposing them into balls. We develop two single-domain solvers for solving the HSP and GSP equations in a unit ball. The GSP solver was a nonlinear solver that used spherical harmonics for angular direction and Legendre polynomials for radial direction. An SES-based continuous dielectric permittivity function and an ion-exclusion function were proposed and were encompassed in the GSP solver. The novelty of the method is that the nonlinearity is incorporated only in the proximity of the molecule, whereas in the bulk solvent region, the linear model was used. A series of numerical results have been presented to show the performance of the ddNPB-SES method, which shows the importance of using the nonlinear regime near the surface.

In the future, we would like to implement this method in our open-source software
ddX
,
[
39
]
, to simulate bigger molecules. We would also like to study the nonlinear solver better using Newton’s methods and incorporating acceleration techniques such as dynamic damping and Anderson acceleration.
