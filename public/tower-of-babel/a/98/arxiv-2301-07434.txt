Integral representation of superoscillations via complex Borel measures and their convergence
Jussi Behrndt, Fabrizio Colombo, Peter Schlosser, Daniele C. Struppa

Abstract
. In the last decade there has been a growing interest in superoscillations in various fields of mathematics, physics and engineering. However, while in applications as optics the local oscillatory behaviour is the important property, some convergence to a plane wave is the standard characterizing feature of a superoscillating function in mathematics and quantum mechanics. Also there exists a certain discrepancy between the representation of superoscillations either as generalized Fourier series, as certain integrals or via special functions. The aim of this work is to close these gaps and give a general definition of superoscillations, covering the well-known examples in the existing literature. Superoscillations will be defined as sequences of holomorphic functions, which admit integral representations with respect to complex Borel measures and converge to a plane wave in the space 𝒜 1 ​ ( ℂ ) \mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}) of exponentially bounded entire functions.



AMS Classification 35A20, 35A08.

Keywords: Superoscillations, Borel measures, infinite order differential operators.

The above examples show that there exist many variants of superoscillations, defined in various ways and having different types of oscillatory behaviour. One of the main purposes of this paper is to propose the general Definition

Definition 1.1
.

The space of entire functions with exponential growth is defined as

(1.9)

\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})\coloneqq\Set{F:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\text{ entire}}{\exists A,B\geq 0\text{ such that }|F(z)|\leq Ae^{B|z|}\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{C}}.

For any
F_{0},(F_{n})_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
we say that
F_{n}\rightarrow F_{0}
converges in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
if and only if there exists some
B\geq 0
, such that

(1.10)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup\limits_{z\in\mathbb{C}}|F_{n}(z)-F_{0}(z)|e^{-B|z|}=0.

The idea is now to consider superoscillations as certain superpositions of plane waves with frequencies in a bounded range
[-k_{0},k_{0}]
, but converging to a plane wave with frequency
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]
exceeding this range.

Definition 1.2
.

A sequence of functions of the form

(1.11)

F_{n}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

with a common maximal frequency
k_{0}>0
and complex Borel measures
\mu_{n}
on
[-k_{0},k_{0}]
, is called superoscillating, if there exists some
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]
, such that

(1.12)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(z)=e^{iaz}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}).

Note, that any function of the form (
1.11
) is an element in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
. In fact, since a complex measure has finite total variation, the exponential boundedness follows immediately from the estimate

|F_{n}(z)|\leq\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{|k||z|}d|\mu_{n}|(z)\leq|\mu_{n}|([-k_{0},k_{0}])e^{k_{0}|z|},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, the holomorphicity follows from a version of the dominated convergence theorem which allows to carry the derivatives inside the integral.

Also note that the functions (
1.1
) are superoscillation in the sense of Definition
1.2
since the representation (
1.11

with the complex Borel measures

(1.13)

\mu_{n}(B)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{j=0,k_{j}(n)\in B}^{n}C_{j}(n),\quad\text{for every Borel set }B\subseteq[-1,1],

and the convergence (
1.12

[

The considerations in this section are inspired by M.V. Berry’s paper

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt+\frac{\|g^{\prime}\|_{\Delta}+\|gk^{\prime}\|_{\Delta}}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{a}\Big|\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{s(1-i)}{\delta\sqrt{2}}\Big)\Big|ds\bigg),

where in the second inequality we performed the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
and used the estimate

|z|\leq e^{|z|}
to get rid of the
z
-dependency inside the brackets. Using the representation
\operatorname{erfc}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(t+z)^{2}}dt
of the complementary error function, see
[
1
, Eq. (7.1.2)]
, it can be estimated by

\Big|\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{s(1-i)}{\delta\sqrt{2}}\Big)\Big|=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(t+\frac{s(1-i)}{\delta\sqrt{2}})^{2}}dt\Big|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}-\frac{\sqrt{2}\,ts}{\delta}}dt\leq\min\Big\{\frac{\delta\sqrt{2}}{s\sqrt{\pi}},1\Big\},

where in the last inequality we estimated either
e^{-t^{2}}\leq 1
or
e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}\,ts}{\delta}}\leq 1
to get the minimum as an upper bound. This inequality on the one hand gives an
\delta
-independent bound of the last integral in (
2.8
) as well as for every
s\in(0,a]
the pointwise convergence as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. With the dominated convergence theorem this then leads to

\displaystyle\Big|F_{\delta}^{(3)}(z)-\frac{g(ia)}{2}e^{ik(ia)z}\Big|e^{-(\|k\|_{\Delta}+1)|z|}\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{\|g\|_{\Delta}}{2}\Big|\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{a(1-i)}{\delta\sqrt{2}}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle+\frac{\|g^{\prime}\|_{\Delta}+\|gk^{\prime}\|_{\Delta}}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{a}\Big|\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{s(1-i)}{\delta\sqrt{2}}\Big)\Big|ds\overset{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Since the right hand side is independent of
|z|
we conclude the convergence

\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{\delta}^{(3)}(z)=\frac{g(ia)}{2}e^{ik(ia)z}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}).

For the same reason also

\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{\delta}^{(2)}(z)=\frac{g(ia)}{2}e^{ik(ia)z}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}).

Summing up, we have proved that all the terms in (
2.6
) converge in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})

and hence (
2.4
) follows.
∎

The next corollary puts the result of Theorem
2.1
into the perspective of superoscillations and proves that under certain assumptions on the frequency function
k
, the resulting functions
F_{\delta}
satisfy Definition
1.2
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
b\geq a>0
and
k,g
be as in Theorem
2.1
. Assume, in addition, that there exists some
k_{0}>0

such that

(2.9)

g(ia)=1,\quad k(ia)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]\quad\text{and}\quad k(u)\in[-k_{0},k_{0}]\text{ for every }u\in\mathbb{R}.

Then the functions
F_{\delta}
in (
2.3
) are superoscillating with limit

(2.10)

\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{\delta}(z)=e^{ik(ia)z}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}).

Proof.

Since
g(ia)=1
by assumption, the convergence (
2.10
) follows from Theorem
2.1
. Hence it remains to verify the integral representation (
1.11
). For this we first define the complex Borel measure

\sigma_{\delta}(A)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{A}g(u)e^{-\frac{(u-ia)^{2}}{2\delta^{2}}}du

for Borel sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
With this measure the function
F_{\delta}
admits the representation

F_{\delta}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{ik(u)z}d\sigma_{\delta}(u),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

In a second step we consider the Borel measure

\mu_{\delta}(B)\coloneqq\sigma_{\delta}\big(\Set{u\in\mathbb{R}}{k(u)\in B}\big)

for Borel sets
B\subseteq[-k_{0},k_{0}]
and rewrite

F_{\delta}
as

F_{\delta}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{\delta}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

which is exactly the form (
1.11
). Since by assumption
k(u)\in[-k_{0},k_{0}]
for every
u\in\mathbb{R}
and since the frequency
k(ia)
of the limit function in (
2.10
) lies in
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]
, the functions
F_{\delta}
are indeed superoscillating in the sense of Definition
1.2
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Note that in Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
we may modify the functions
k,g
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-b,b]
as long as (
2.2
) and (
2.9
) remain valid. Since this only changes the functions
F_{\delta}
but not the limit
g(ia)e^{ik(ia)z}
in (
2.4
), any such modification leads to a new family of superoscillating functions.

To illustrate our result we provide some possible choices of functions
k
and
g
which lead to superoscillations in Corollary
2.2
. In particular, we will use the functions
k
from the original paper
[
17
]
.

Example 2.4
.

Possible choices of the frequency function are

k_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{u^{2}}{2}},\quad k_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{\cosh(u)},\quad k_{3}(u)=e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}},\quad k_{4}(u)=\cos(u).

All these functions satisfy
k_{j}(u)\in[-1,1]
for
j=1,2,3,4
and for every
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Evaluated for complex arguments these functions admit the values

k_{1}(ia)=\frac{1}{1-\frac{a^{2}}{2}},\quad k_{2}(ia)=\frac{1}{\cos(a)},\quad k_{3}(ia)=e^{\frac{a^{2}}{2}},\quad k_{4}(ia)=\cosh(a),

and hence
k_{1}(ia)>1
for every
a\in(0,\sqrt{2})
,
k_{2}(ia)>1
for every
a\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
k_{3,4}(ia)>1
for every
a>0
. With the constant
g_{j}\equiv 1
for
j=1,2,3,4
, the assumptions of Corollary
2.2
are satisfied and we end up with superoscillating functions
F_{\delta}
. Another possible choice considered in
[
17
]
is for any
0<a\leq 2
the functions

g_{5}(u)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }u\in\overline{\Delta},\\
0,&\text{if }u\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-a,a],\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad k_{5}(u)=\begin{cases}1-\frac{u^{2}}{2},&\text{if }u\in\overline{\Delta}\\
0,&\text{if }u\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-a,a].\end{cases}

Here,
\Delta
is the triangle from Theorem
2.1
with
b=a
. Also these functions satisfy the assumtions of Corollary
2.2
and lead to superoscillating functions
F_{\delta}
.

3.
Superoscillating sinc-functions

In
[
18
]
M.V. Berry considered another type of superoscillating functions

(3.1)

F_{\delta}(z)=\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\operatorname{sinc}\bigg(\sqrt{z^{2}-\frac{2iaz}{\delta}-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}}\bigg),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

for some fixed
a>1
and every
\delta>0
; here
\operatorname{sinc}(z)\coloneqq\frac{\sin(z)}{z}
denotes
the Sinus cardinalis and the complex square root is fixed by

(3.2)

0\leq\operatorname{Arg}(\sqrt{\,\cdot\,}\,)<\pi.

The aim of this section is to verify that (
3.1
) is indeed superoscillatory in the sense of Definition
1.2
. This will be done in two steps. In Theorem
3.2
we prove the convergence (
1.12
) and with the help of Lemma
A.2
we conclude the integral representation (
1.11
).

We start with a technical estimate of the complex square root, which will be important for the
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-convergence of the functions (
3.1
) in Theorem
3.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
a>1
there exists some
C\geq 0
, such that

(3.3)

\big|\sqrt{z^{2}-2iaz-1}+az-i\big|\leq C\min\{|z|,|z|^{2}\},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

In the first step we consider the function
f(z)\coloneqq\sqrt{z^{2}-2iaz-1}-az+i
. Assume that
f(z_{0})=0
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
, that is,

\sqrt{z_{0}^{2}-2iaz_{0}-1}=az_{0}-i.

Squaring both sides leads to
z_{0}^{2}=a^{2}z_{0}^{2}
and since obviously
z_{0}\neq 0
by the choice of the square root in (
3.2
), this is a contradiction to
a>1
. Hence
f
does not have any zeros. Since
a>1
by assumption
we also conclude

\lim\limits_{|z|\rightarrow\infty}|f(z)|=\infty,

and consequently there exists
c>0
such that

(3.4)

|f(z)|\geq c,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

In the second step we consider
g(z)\coloneqq\sqrt{z^{2}-2iaz-1}+az-i
. Using (
3.4
), this function can be estimated by

(3.5)

|g(z)|=\Big|\frac{g(z)f(z)}{f(z)}\Big|=\Big|\frac{(1-a^{2})z^{2}}{f(z)}\Big|\leq\frac{(a^{2}-1)|z|^{2}}{c},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Moreover,
g
can also be estimated by

(3.6)

|g(z)|\leq\sqrt{(|z|+a)^{2}}+a|z|+1=(1+a)(1+|z|),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Using now (
3.5
) for
|z|\leq 1
and (
3.6
) for
|z|\geq 1
this implies the estimate (
3.3
).
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

For every
a>1
and
\delta>0
the functions
F_{\delta}
in (
3.1
) belong to the space

\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
and converge as

(3.7)

\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{\delta}(z)=e^{iaz}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}).

Proof.

For this proof it will be convenient to use the notation

(3.8)

R_{\delta}(z)\coloneqq\sqrt{z^{2}-\frac{2iaz}{\delta}-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

so that we can write
F_{\delta}(z)=\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\operatorname{sinc}(R_{\delta}(z))
. Although the complex square root in (
3.8
) is not entire, in the series expansion of the
\operatorname{sinc}
-function only even powers appear and we still end up with the entire function

F_{\delta}(z)=\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)!}R_{\delta}(z)^{2n}=\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)!}\Big(z^{2}-\frac{2iaz}{\delta}-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\Big)^{n}.

For
\delta>0
fixed and
z\in\mathbb{C}
sufficiently large we have
1\leq|R_{\delta}(z)|\leq 2|z|
and hence the exponential bound

|F_{\delta}(z)|=\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\frac{|\sin(R_{\delta}(z))|}{|R_{\delta}(z)|}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\big|e^{iR_{\delta}(z)}-e^{-iR_{\delta}(z)}\big|\leq\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}e^{2|z|}

holds for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
sufficiently large. It follows that
F_{\delta}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.

In order to show that
F_{\delta}
converges as in (
3.7
) we first estimate the difference between
F_{\delta}(z)
and
G_{\delta}(z)\coloneqq\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\operatorname{sinc}(\frac{i}{\delta}-az)
. Using the identity
\operatorname{sinc}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{it\xi}dt
,
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
, we find

F_{\delta}(z)-G_{\delta}(z)=\frac{1}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}\big(e^{itR_{\delta}(z)}-e^{it(\frac{i}{\delta}-az)}\big)dt,

and with the help of (
A.1
) we obtain

(3.9)

\begin{split}|F_{\delta}(z)-G_{\delta}(z)|&\leq\frac{1}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}|t|\Big|R_{\delta}(z)+az-\frac{i}{\delta}\Big|e^{|t|\max\{|R_{\delta}(z)|,|\frac{i}{\delta}-az|\}}dt\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}\Big|R_{\delta}(z)+az-\frac{i}{\delta}\Big|e^{|t|(|R_{\delta}(z)+az-\frac{i}{\delta}|+|az-\frac{i}{\delta}|)}dt.\end{split}

Since the estimate (
3.3
) translates into

\Big|R_{\delta}(z)+az-\frac{i}{\delta}\Big|=\frac{1}{\delta}\big|\sqrt{\delta^{2}z^{2}-2ia\delta z-1}+a\delta z-i\big|\leq C\min\{|z|,\delta|z|^{2}\},

we can further estimate (
3.9
) in the form

(3.10)

\begin{split}|F_{\delta}(z)-G_{\delta}(z)|&\leq C|z|^{2}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{|t|(C|z|+a|z|+\frac{1}{\delta})}dt\\
&\leq C|z|^{2}e^{(C+a)|z|}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\frac{|t|}{\delta}}dt\\
&=2C\delta|z|^{2}e^{(C+a)|z|}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}(e^{\frac{1}{\delta}}-1)\\
&\leq 2C\delta|z|^{2}e^{(C+a)|z|};\end{split}

in the last inequality we used
|z|^{2}\leq 2e^{|z|}
, which follows from the power series representation of the exponential.

In a second step we estimate the difference between
G_{\delta}(z)
and
e^{iaz}
. For this, we again use
\operatorname{sinc}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{it\xi}dt
,
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
, as well as integration by parts to rewrite
G_{\delta}
as

\displaystyle G_{\delta}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{it(\frac{i}{\delta}-az)}dt

\displaystyle=-e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}\Big(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-\frac{t}{\delta}}\Big)e^{-iatz}dt

\displaystyle=e^{iaz}-e^{-\frac{2}{\delta}}e^{-iaz}-iaze^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-\frac{t}{\delta}}e^{-iatz}dt.

This representation allows the estimate

(3.11)

\begin{split}|G_{\delta}(z)-e^{iaz}|&\leq\Big(e^{-\frac{2}{\delta}}+a|z|e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-\frac{t}{\delta}}dt\Big)e^{a|z|}\\
&=\big(e^{-\frac{2}{\delta}}+a\delta|z|(1-e^{-\frac{2}{\delta}})\big)e^{a|z|}\\
&\leq(e^{-\frac{2}{\delta}}+a\delta|z|)e^{a|z|}\\
&\leq(e^{-\frac{2}{\delta}}+a\delta)e^{(a+1)|z|},\end{split}

where in the last inequality we used
|z|\leq e^{|z|}
, which follows from the power series representation of the exponential.

Combining now (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) leads to the estimate

|F_{\delta}(z)-e^{iaz}|e^{-(C+a+1)|z|}\leq 4C\delta+e^{-\frac{2}{\delta}}+a\delta,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

and since the right hand side is
z
-independent and converges to zero as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, this shows

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{\delta}(z)=e^{iaz}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.
∎

The convergence result of Theorem
3.2
together with the integral representation of the

\operatorname{sinc}
-function
in Lemma
A.2
imply the superoscillatory property
of the functions
F_{\delta}
in (
3.1
).

Corollary 3.3
.

For every
a>1
the functions
F_{\delta}
in (
3.1
) are superoscillating with limit

(3.12)

\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{\delta}(z)=e^{iaz}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}).

Proof.

The convergence (
3.12
) was shown in Theorem
3.2
and it remains the integral representation (
1.11
). From (
A.3
) with
z
and
b
replaced by
z-\frac{ia}{\delta}
and

\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}-1}}{\delta}
, respectively, we obtain

F_{\delta}(z)=\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\frac{1}{\delta}}\operatorname{sinc}\bigg(\sqrt{z^{2}-\frac{2iaz}{\delta}-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}}\bigg)=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ikz}e^{\frac{ak-1}{\delta}}J_{0}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(a^{2}-1)(1-k^{2})}}{\delta}\bigg)dk.

Using the complex Borel measure

\mu_{\delta}(B)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{B}e^{\frac{ak-1}{\delta}}J_{0}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(a^{2}-1)(1-k^{2})}}{\delta}\bigg)dk

for Borel sets
B\subseteq[-1,1]
we conclude that
F_{\delta}
admits the integral representation (
1.11
) with
k_{0}=1
and hence the functions
F_{\delta}
are superoscillating according to Definition
1.2
.
∎

4.
Superoscillations with prescribed frequencies

The standard example (
1.1
) of a superoscillating function is a linear combination of plane waves

(4.1)

F_{n}(z)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)e^{ik_{j}(n)z},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

with frequencies
k_{j}(n)=1-\frac{2j}{n}
and coefficients
C_{j}(n)=\binom{n}{j}(\frac{1+k}{2})^{n-j}(\frac{1-k}{2})^{j}
. For a long time it was not clear how many of such superoscillating functions exist.
In the recent paper
[
6
]
for any set of given frequencies
k_{j}(n)\in[-1,1]
and any target frequency
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]
coefficients
C_{j}(n)
were constructed, such that the corresponding sequence (
4.1
) approximates the exponential
e^{iax}
in the sense that the Taylor series
of
F_{n}(x)
and
e^{iax}
coincide up to order
n
. This approximation suggests a certain
superoscillatory behaviour, but is not sufficient to conclude the convergence
F_{n}(z)\rightarrow e^{iaz}

in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.
The analysis in this section is partly inspired by these earlier considerations in
[
6
]
.
In particular, in Corollary
4.2
we conclude the
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-convergence
under sufficient conditions on the frequencies
k_{j}(n)
.

Starting from a more general perspective we shall study functions of the form

(4.2)

F_{n}(z)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)f_{j,n}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

with frequency functions

(4.3)

f_{j,n}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{j,n}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

The idea is now to choose the coefficients
C_{j}(n)
in such a way that the Taylor coefficients of
F_{n}(z)
and
e^{iaz}
coincide up to order
n
, that is,

(4.4)

F_{n}^{(l)}(0)=(ia)^{l},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,l\in\{0,\dots,n\},

or, more explicitly using (
4.3
) and (
4.2
)

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}(ik)^{l}d\mu_{j,n}(k)=(ia)^{l},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,l\in\{0,\dots,n\}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
this is a linear system of the form

(4.5)

S_{n}\mathbf{c}_{n}=\mathbf{a}_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where the matrix
S_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
and the vectors

\mathbf{c}_{n},\mathbf{a}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
are given by

(4.6)

S_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}k^{0}d\mu_{0,n}(k)&\dots&\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}k^{0}d\mu_{n,n}(k)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}k^{n}d\mu_{0,n}(k)&\dots&\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}k^{n}d\mu_{n,n}(k)\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{c}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{0}(n)\\
\vdots\\
C_{n}(n)\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{a}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{0}\\
\vdots\\
a^{n}\end{pmatrix}.

The following Theorem
4.1
is of abstract nature and provides conditions such that
the functions
F_{n}
in (
4.2
) are superoscillating. Using point measures
\mu_{j,n}
in Corollary
4.2
we complete the earlier considerations in
[
6
]
.
A different situation based on the choice of absolutely continuous measures is treated in Corollary
4.4
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
k_{0}>0
,
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]
,
\mu_{j,n}
be complex Borel measures on
[-k_{0},k_{0}]
,
and let
\mathbf{c}_{n}
be a solution of the system (
4.5
) for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If there exist constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\geq 0
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}

(4.7)

|\mu_{j,n}|([-k_{0},k_{0}])\leq\kappa_{1}^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad|C_{j}(n)|\leq\kappa_{2}^{n}

hold, then the functions
F_{n}
in (
4.2
) are superoscillating with limit

(4.8)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(z)=e^{iaz}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}).

Proof.

First of all, it is clear that
f_{j,n}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
for every
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
and hence
F_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
by the arguments given below Definition
1.2
. The bounds of the measures
\mu_{j,n}
and the coefficients
C_{j}(n)
in (
4.7
) immediately lead to the estimate

(4.9)

|F_{n}(z)|\leq\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}|C_{j}(n)|\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{|k||z|}d|\mu_{j,n}|(k)\leq(n+1)(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}e^{k_{0}|z|},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Next, we write the difference between
F_{n}(z)
and
e^{iaz}
as the Taylor series

(4.10)

F_{n}(z)-e^{iaz}=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{F_{n}^{(l)}(0)-(ia)^{l}}{l!}z^{l}=\sum\limits_{l=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{n}^{(l)}(0)-(ia)^{l}}{l!}z^{l},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

where in the second equality we used (
4.4
). For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
the Cauchy integral formula along a circle of radius
(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|
leads to the following bounds for the coefficients in
(
4.10
)

\displaystyle\frac{|F_{n}^{(l)}(0)-(ia)^{l}|}{l!}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\xi|=(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|}\frac{F_{n}(\xi)-e^{ia\xi}}{\xi^{l+1}}d\xi\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{l}|z|^{l}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big|F_{n}\big((1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|e^{i\varphi}\big)-e^{ia(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|e^{i\varphi}}\Big|d\varphi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{l}|z|^{l}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big((n+1)(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}e^{k_{0}(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|}+e^{|a|(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|}\Big)d\varphi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(n+1)(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}+1}{(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{l}|z|^{l}}e^{|a|(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|},

where we used (
4.9
) and
k_{0}<|a|
in the estimate of the integrand. Plugging this into the Taylor series (
4.10
) gives

(4.11)

\begin{split}|F_{n}(z)-e^{iaz}|&\leq\big((n+1)(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}+1\big)e^{|a|(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|}\sum\limits_{l=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{l}}\\
&=\frac{(n+1)(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}+1}{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}}e^{|a|(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.\end{split}

Due to (
4.10
) this inequality obviously holds for
z=0
and hence we conclude
the
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-convergence

\sup\limits_{z\in\mathbb{C}}|F_{n}(z)-e^{iaz}|e^{-|a|(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})|z|}\leq\frac{(n+1)(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}+1}{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}(1+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})^{n}}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Finally, the integral representation (
1.11
) of the functions
F_{n}
is satisfied by

F_{n}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)\mu_{j,n}(k)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

using the Borel measure
\mu_{n}(B)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)\mu_{j,n}(B)
for Borel sets
B\subseteq[-k_{0},k_{0}]
.
We have shown that the functions
F_{n}
are superoscillating with limit (
4.8
).
∎

In the next corollary we return to the initial problem of this section. The aim is to find for given
k_{j}(n)
coefficients
C_{j}(n)
in (
4.1
) such that the resulting functions
F_{n}
are superoscillating. Here the measures
\mu_{j,n}
in Theorem
4.1

are chosen as point measures and an additional condition on the frequencies
k_{j}(n)
is imposed.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
k_{0}>0
,
k_{j}(n)\in[-k_{0},k_{0}]
, and assume that

(4.12)

\prod\limits_{l=0,l\neq j}^{n}|k_{l}(n)-k_{j}(n)|\geq\kappa^{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n\},

holds for some
\kappa>0
. For
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]
define the coefficients

(4.13)

C_{j}(n)=\prod\limits_{l=0,l\neq j}^{n}\frac{k_{l}(n)-a}{k_{l}(n)-k_{j}(n)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n\}.

Then the functions

(4.14)

F_{n}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)e^{ik_{j}(n)z},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

are superoscillating with limit
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(z)=e^{iaz}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Observe first that the functions (
4.14
) can be written in the form (
4.2
)
using the point measures

\mu_{j,n}(B)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }k_{j}(n)\in B,\\
0,&\text{if }k_{j}(n)\notin B,\end{cases}

for Borel sets
B\subseteq[-k_{0},k_{0}]
.
Since the first bound in (
4.7
) is trivially satisfied, it remains to check that
the (unique) solution
C_{j}(n)
of (
4.5
) satisfies the second bound
in (
4.7
). The explicit form of the matrix
S_{n}
in this particular setting is

S_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}(n)^{0}&\dots&k_{n}(n)^{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k_{0}(n)^{n}&\dots&k_{n}(n)^{n}\end{pmatrix}.

This is a Vandermonde matrix, which is known to be invertible whenever the
(k_{j}(n))_{j=0}^{n}
are pairwise disjoint. But this is obviously the case due to the assumption (
4.12
). Moreover, using Cramer’s rule and the known determinant formula for Vandermonde matrices, one can easily derive the explicit representation
(
4.13
) for the coefficients
C_{j}(n)

of the solution, see also
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
. The assumption (
4.12
) now allows to estimate these coefficients as

|C_{j}(n)|=\prod\limits_{l=0,l\neq j}^{n}\frac{|k_{l}(n)-a|}{|k_{l}(n)-k_{j}(n)|}\leq\prod\limits_{l=0,l\neq j}^{n}\frac{k_{0}+|a|}{|k_{l}(n)-k_{j}(n)|}\leq\Big(\frac{k_{0}+|a|}{\kappa}\Big)^{n},

which shows the second bound in (
4.7
). It follows from
Theorem
4.1
that the functions
F_{n}
are superoscillating.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that the frequencies
k_{j}(n)=1-\frac{2j}{n}
in (
1.2
) can be estimated by

\prod\limits_{l=0,l\neq j}^{n}|k_{l}(n)-k_{j}(n)|=\prod\limits_{l=0,l\neq j}^{n}\frac{2|l-j|}{n}=\frac{2^{n}j!(n-j)!}{n^{n}}=\frac{2^{n}n!}{n^{n}{n\choose j}}\geq\frac{1}{e^{n}},

where in the last inequality we used
{n\choose j}\leq 2^{n}
as well as
\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\leq e^{n}
, which is a consequence of the Stirling formula. Hence, Corollary
4.2
applies and the coefficients

C_{j}(n)
in (
4.13
) lead to superoscillating functions
F_{n}
in (
4.14
).
Note that these coefficients do not coincide with those in (
1.2
).

Now we turn to a different situation, where the measures
\mu_{j,n}
in Theorem
4.1
are absolutely continuous. Here the initial idea is to replace the exponentials
e^{ik_{j}(n)z}
in (
4.1
) by the derivatives of a bandlimited function

(4.15)

f(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}h(k)dk,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

with
h\in L^{1}([-k_{0},k_{0}])
;
note that
h
is the compactly supported Fourier transform of
f
.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
k_{0}>0
and let
h\in L^{1}([-k_{0},k_{0}])

be a nonnegative function from the Szegö class, i.e. for some
\alpha,\beta\in[-k_{0},k_{0}]
with
\alpha<\beta
one has

(4.16)

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{\ln(h(k))}{\sqrt{(\beta-k)(k-\alpha)}}dk>-\infty,

and let
f
be as in (
4.15
).
Then, for every
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]
there exist coefficients
C_{j}(n)\in\mathbb{C}
such that the functions

(4.17)

F_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)f^{(j)}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

are superoscillating with limit
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(z)=e^{iaz}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Note that the functions
F_{n}
in (
4.17
) are of the form (
4.2
) if we use the measures

(4.18)

\mu_{j,n}(B)=\int_{B}(ik)^{j}h(k)dk

for Borel sets
B\subseteq[-k_{0},k_{0}]
.
Hence we are in the situation to apply Theorem
4.1
but have to check the two estimates (
4.7
). The bound of the absolute variations of the measures is clearly satisfied by

|\mu_{j,n}|([-k_{0},k_{0}])=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}|k|^{j}|h(k)|dk\leq k_{0}^{j}\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}|h(k)|dk\leq\max\{k_{0},1\}^{n}\|h\|_{L^{1}}.

To calculate the coefficients
C_{j}(n)
we have to solve the system (
4.5
) or, equivalently,
the system
M_{n}\mathbf{d}_{n}=\mathbf{a}_{n}
with the matrix and the vectors

(M_{n})_{j,l}=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}k^{j+l}h(k)dk,\quad(\mathbf{d}_{n})_{j}=i^{j}C_{j}(n),\quad(\mathbf{a}_{n})_{l}=a^{l},

where we shifted the powers
i^{j}
of the imaginary unit from the coefficient matrix into the solution vector. Now consider the matrix

(\widetilde{M}_{n})_{j,l}\coloneqq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}k^{j+l}h(k)dk,\quad j,l\in\{0,\dots,n\}.

It is shown in
[
32
]
that its lowest eigenvalue
\widetilde{\lambda}_{n}=\inf_{0\neq x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\frac{\langle\widetilde{M}_{n}x,x\rangle}{|x|^{2}}
is bounded from below by

\widetilde{\lambda}_{n}\geq\kappa^{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

for some
\kappa>0
. Hence
\widetilde{M}_{n}
is elliptic with bound
\widetilde{M}_{n}\geq\kappa^{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
the estimate

\langle M_{n}x,x\rangle=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}\Big|\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}k^{j}x_{j}\Big|^{2}h(k)dk\geq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\Big|\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}k^{j}x_{j}\Big|^{2}h(k)dk=\langle\widetilde{M}_{n}x,x\rangle\geq\kappa^{n}|x|^{2},

shows that the same is true for the matrix
M_{n}
. Consequently, the inverse matrix exists and is bounded by
\|M_{n}^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa^{n}}
. Finally we can estimate the elements
C_{j}(n)
of the solution by

|C_{j}(n)|\leq|\mathbf{d}_{n}|=|M_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa^{n}}|\mathbf{a}_{n}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{n+1}\max\{a^{n},1\}}{\kappa^{n}}.

Hence, also the second bound in (
4.7
) is satisfied and the assertions follow from
Theorem
4.1
.
∎

In the following example we make a special choice of the function
h
in (
4.15
) in order to find a close connection between the functions (
4.17
) and the method presented in the paragraph below equation (
1.8
).

Example 4.5
.

In this example we apply Corollary
4.4
with
k_{0}=1
and the constant function
h(k)=\frac{1}{2}
. The exact integral

\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\ln(\frac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{(1-k)(k+1)}}dk=-\pi\ln(2)>-\infty

shows that the condition (
4.16
) is satisfied in this setting. Moreover, the function
f
in (
4.15
) is given by

f(z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ikz}dk=\operatorname{sinc}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

which means that the functions
F_{n}
in (
4.17
) become the sum of derivatives of the
\operatorname{sinc}
-function

(4.19)

F_{n}(z)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}(n)\operatorname{sinc}^{(j)}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

This example is of particular importance, since these functions are closely related to the method of P. Ferreira, A. Kempf and D. Lee
in the series of papers
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
28
,
29
]
.
There functions of the form (
1.8
) were considered, with coefficients
(c_{l})_{l}
chosen in such a way that at prescribed points
(x_{l})_{l}
the function
F
admits the prescribed values
F(x_{l})=a_{l}
,
l\in\{0,\dots,m\}
. Furthermore, in the paper
[
27
]
this construction is extended in the sense that also the values of the derivative
F^{\prime}
can be prescribed and it is then straight forward to also prescribe the derivatives up to any order
n
, i.e.
F^{(j)}(x_{j,l})=a_{j,l}
,
l\in\{0,\dots,m\}
,
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. This then leads to a function
F
of the form

F(x)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{m}c_{0,l}\operatorname{sinc}(x-x_{0,l})+\sum\limits_{l=0}^{m}c_{1,l}\operatorname{sinc}^{\prime}(x-x_{1,l})+\dots+\sum\limits_{l=0}^{m}c_{n,l}\operatorname{sinc}^{(n)}(x-x_{n,l}).

In the special case that we choose
m=0
, the points
x_{0,0}=x_{1,0}=\dots=x_{n,0}=0
as the origin and the values
a_{j,0}=(ia)^{j}
, we end up with exactly the function (
4.19
), namely

F(x)=c_{0,0}\operatorname{sinc}(x)+c_{1,0}\operatorname{sinc}^{\prime}(x)+\dots+c_{n,0}\operatorname{sinc}^{(n)}(x).

5.
Superoscillating solutions of the generalized Schrödinger equation

In this final section we derive two methods to construct new families of superoscillating functions out of given ones. The underlying technique has its origin in
[
7
]
and was revisited in
[
8
,
9
,
14
]
; it is based on the generalized force free Schrödinger equation

(5.1)

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(t,z)=-H\Big(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big)\Psi(t,z),\quad t,z\in\mathbb{C},

where for some entire
H(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}h_{l}z^{l}
the operator
H(-i\frac{d}{dz})\coloneqq\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}h_{l}(-i)^{l}\frac{d^{l}}{dz^{l}}
is defined as the corresponding infinite order differential expression. If we now consider superoscillating initial conditions
\Psi_{n}(0,z)=F_{n}(z)
of the form (
1.11
), then
the equation (
5.1
) admits the explicit solution

(5.2)

\Psi_{n}(t,z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{iH(k)t}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k),\quad t,z\in\mathbb{C};

cf. Lemma
5.1
.
One may expect that the solutions
\Psi_{n}(t,\,\cdot\,)
are superoscillatory also for
t\not=0
. This method is specified in Theorem
5.2
(i). Moreover, it turns out that in the point
z=0
the functions
\Psi_{n}(\,\cdot\,,0)
are superoscillatory in the time variable
t
as well, see Theorem
5.2
(ii).

In the next lemma we collect some properties of the propagator of the generalized Schrödinger equation (
5.1
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
H:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be an entire function. For every
t\in\mathbb{C}
the operator

(5.3)

U_{t}\coloneqq\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(it)^{m}}{m!}H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)^{m},

is continuous as an operator
U_{t}:\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
and it acts on plane waves
e^{iaz}
and functions
F_{n}(z)
of the form (
1.11
) as

(5.4)

U_{t}e^{iaz}=e^{iH(a)t}e^{iaz}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{t}F_{n}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{iH(k)t}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k),

respectively. Moreover, for every
F\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
, the function
\Psi(t,z)=U_{t}F(z)
,
t,z\in\mathbb{C}
, is a solution of the generalized Schrödinger equation (
5.1
) with initial condition
\Psi(0,z)=F(z)
.

Proof.

The fact that the operator
U_{t}
is continuous in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
was already shown
in
[
15
, Theorem 2.7]
. To investigate the action (
5.4
) of
U_{t}
on plane waves
e^{iaz}
, we start with the power series representation
H(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}h_{l}z^{l}
. Then the operator
H(-i\frac{d}{dz})
acts on plane waves
e^{iaz}
as multiplication

H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)e^{iaz}=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}h_{l}(-i)^{l}\frac{d^{l}}{dz^{l}}e^{iaz}=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}h_{l}a^{l}e^{iaz}=H(a)e^{iaz}

and hence the operator
U_{t}
acts as

(5.5)

U_{t}e^{iaz}=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(it)^{m}}{m!}H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)^{m}e^{iaz}=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(iH(a)t)^{m}}{m!}e^{iaz}=e^{iH(a)t}e^{iaz}.

To determine the action of
U_{t}
on functions
F_{n}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k)
we
have to interchange the complex derivatives with the integral. In the first step we use a version of the dominated convergence theorem and obtain

\frac{d^{l}}{dz^{l}}\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}\frac{d^{l}}{dz^{l}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k).

Applying the dominated convergence theorem once more, we are also allowed to carry the infinite sum
\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}
of the operator
H(-i\frac{d}{dz})
inside the integral. This gives

H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k).

Using the dominated convergence a third time for the series
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}
of the operator
U_{t}
finally gives

U_{t}\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}U_{t}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{iH(k)t}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k),

where in the second equation we used (
5.5
).

In the last part of the proof let
F\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
and consider
\Psi(t,z)\coloneqq U_{t}F(z)
,
t,z\in\mathbb{C}
. Interchanging derivatives and sums for similar reasons as above, we obtain that

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(t,z)

\displaystyle=i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(it)^{m}}{m!}H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)^{m}F(z)

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(it)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)^{m}F(z)

\displaystyle=-H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(it)^{m}}{m!}H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)^{m}F(z)

\displaystyle=-H\Big(-i\frac{d}{dz}\Big)\Psi(t,z).

Hence
\Psi(t,z)
in indeed a solution of the generalized Schrödinger equation (
5.1
). Since
U_{0}=\text{id}
reduces to the identity operator, the solution also satisfies the initial condition
\Psi(0,z)=F(z)
.
∎

The novelty of Theorem
5.2
below is that
arbitrary superoscillating functions of the form (
1.11
) are allowed, while in
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
14
]
only the standard example (
1.1
) was considered. Furthermore,
it is shown that the resulting functions
F_{n}^{(1)}
and
F_{n}^{(2)}
in (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) converge in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
, while only uniform convergence on compact sets was proven in
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
14
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
k_{0}>0
,
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-k_{0},k_{0}]
, and
\mu_{n}
be complex Borel measures on
[-k_{0},k_{0}]
.
Assume that the functions

(5.6)

F_{n}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

are superoscillating with limit

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(z)=e^{iaz}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.
Then one can construct the following two new families of superoscillating functions:

(i)

For every entire function
H:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
the functions

(5.7)

F_{n}^{(1)}(z)\coloneqq e^{-H(a)}\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{H(k)}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

are superoscillating with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}^{(1)}(z)=e^{iaz}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.

(ii)

For every entire function
H:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with satisfies
\operatorname{ran}\big(H|_{[-k_{0},k_{0}]}\big)\subseteq[-h_{0},h_{0}]
and
H(a)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-h_{0},h_{0}]
for some
h_{0}>0
the sequence

(5.8)

F_{n}^{(2)}(z)\coloneqq\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{iH(k)z}d\mu_{n}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

is superoscillating with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}^{(2)}(z)=e^{iH(a)z}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

For the proof of (i) we consider the operator (
5.3
) with
t=-i
. From the action of this operator in (
5.4
) we obtain

U_{-i}F_{n}(z)=\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{H(k)}e^{ikz}d\mu_{n}(k)=e^{H(a)}F_{n}^{(1)}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Since
U_{-i}
is continuous in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
due to Lemma
5.1
we conclude the convergence

(5.9)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}^{(1)}(z)=e^{-H(a)}\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{-i}F_{n}(z)=e^{-H(a)}U_{-i}e^{iaz}=e^{iaz}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C}),

where the first identity in (
5.4
) was used in the last step.
The fact that
F_{n}^{(1)}
is of the form (
1.11
) is clear using the complex Borel measure

\sigma_{n}(B)=e^{-H(a)}\int_{B}H(k)d\mu_{n}(k)

for Borel sets
B\subseteq[-k_{0},k_{0}]
.
Hence, the functions
F_{n}^{(1)}
are superoscillating with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}^{(1)}(z)=e^{iaz}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
.

(ii)  According to the
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-convergence (
1.12
), there exists some
B\geq 0
, such that

A_{n}\coloneqq\sup\limits_{z\in\mathbb{C}}|F_{n}(z)-e^{iaz}|e^{-B|z|}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

By the choice of the constants
A_{n}
, we obtain the estimate

|F_{n}(z)-e^{iaz}|\leq A_{n}e^{B|z|},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

In the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 2.7]
the authors obtain the estimate

|U_{t}(F_{n}(z)-e^{iaz})|\leq A_{n}e^{\widetilde{D}|t|}e^{\widetilde{B}|z|},\quad t,z\in\mathbb{C},

for some
\widetilde{B},\widetilde{D}\geq 0
. For
z=0
this estimate shows the convergence

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{C}}\big|U_{t}(F_{n}(z)-e^{iaz})|_{z=0}\big|e^{-\widetilde{D}|t|}\leq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}=0.

In other words, we conclude the
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-convergence

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{t}F_{n}(z)|_{z=0}=U_{t}e^{iaz}|_{z=0}

in the variable
t
. Using the identities (
5.4
) this convergence can be written as

(5.10)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}^{(2)}(t)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}e^{iH(k)t}d\mu_{n}(k)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{t}F_{n}(z)|_{z=0}=U_{t}e^{iaz}|_{z=0}=e^{iH(a)t}.

Since
\operatorname{ran}\big(H|_{[-k_{0},k_{0}]}\big)\subseteq[-h_{0},h_{0}]
by assumption, we can choose the complex Borel measures

\sigma_{n}(B)\coloneqq\mu_{n}\big(\Set{k\in[-k_{0},k_{0}]}{H(k)\in B}\big)

for Borel sets
B\subseteq[-h_{0},h_{0}]
and
transform
F_{n}^{(2)}
into the form

(5.11)

F_{n}^{(2)}(t)=\int_{-h_{0}}^{h_{0}}e^{ikt}d\sigma_{n}(k),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

which is the
representation (
1.11
). Together with the convergence (
5.10
) to a plane wave with frequency
H(a)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-h_{0},h_{0}]
this shows that the functions
F_{n}^{(2)}
are superoscillating.
∎

This appendix contains two technical results which are used in Section
2

\operatorname{sinc}
-function.

Lemma A.1
.

For every
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
one has

(A.1)

|e^{z_{1}}-e^{z_{2}}|\leq|z_{1}-z_{2}|e^{\max\{|z_{1}|,|z_{2}|\}}.

Proof.

First we write for every
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}

|1-e^{z}|^{2}=(1-\cos(y)e^{x})^{2}+\sin^{2}(y)e^{2x}=1-2\cos(y)e^{x}+e^{2x}.

Using
\cos(y)\geq 1-\frac{y^{2}}{2}
we can estimate this by

|1-e^{z}|^{2}\leq(1-e^{x})^{2}+y^{2}e^{x}.

Since
|1-e^{x}|\leq|x|e^{|x|}
, which follows directly from the power series representation of the exponential, the above inequality becomes

(A.2)

|1-e^{z}|^{2}\leq x^{2}e^{2|x|}+y^{2}e^{x}\leq(x^{2}+y^{2})e^{2|x|}=|z|^{2}e^{2|\operatorname{Re}(z)|},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Next consider
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and assume
\operatorname{Re}(z_{1})\leq\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})
. Then
(
A.2
) implies

|e^{z_{1}}-e^{z_{2}}|=e^{\operatorname{Re}(z_{1})}|1-e^{z_{2}-z_{1}}|\leq e^{\operatorname{Re}(z_{1})}|z_{2}-z_{1}|e^{\operatorname{Re}(z_{2}-z_{1})}=|z_{2}-z_{1}|e^{\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})}\leq|z_{2}-z_{1}|e^{|z_{2}|},

and in the same way for
\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})\leq\operatorname{Re}(z_{1})
one obtains
|e^{z_{1}}-e^{z_{2}}|\leq|z_{1}-z_{2}|e^{|z_{1}|}
.
These estimates immediately lead to (
A.1
).
∎

To verify the integral representation (
1.11
) of the function (
3.1
) in the proof of Corollary
2.2
we need the following integral representation of the
\operatorname{sinc}
-function. A sketch of this proof is already given in
[
18
, Appendix C]
.

Lemma A.2
.

For any
b>0
one has

(A.3)

\operatorname{sinc}\big(\sqrt{z^{2}+b^{2}}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ikz}J_{0}(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}})dk,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

where
J_{0}
is the Bessel function of order zero.

Proof.

We start by deriving the so called Mehler-Sonine integral representation of the Bessel function

(A.4)

J_{0}(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\sin(x\cosh(t))dt,\quad x>0.

Using the Cauchy theorem we can transform for every
R>0
the integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}e^{ix\cosh(t)}dt

\displaystyle=i\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{ix\cosh(it)}dt+\int_{0}^{R}e^{ix\cosh(t+i\frac{\pi}{2})}dt-i\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{ix\cosh(R+it)}dt

\displaystyle=i\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{ix\cos(t)}dt+\int_{0}^{R}e^{-x\sinh(t)}dt-i\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{ix\cosh(R)\cos(t)}e^{-x\sinh(R)\sin(t)}dt.

Performing the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
, the last integral vanishes due to the dominated convergence theorem and we get

\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{R}e^{ix\cosh(t)}dt=i\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{ix\cos(t)}dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x\sinh(t)}dt.

Using the classical integral representation of the Bessel function
[
1
, Eq. (9.1.18)]
we get

\displaystyle J_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(x\cos(t))dt=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos(x\cos(t))dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{Im}\Big(\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{R}e^{ix\cosh(t)}dt\Big)=\frac{2}{\pi}\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\sin\big(x\cosh(t)\big)dt,

which is exactly the stated integral representation (
A.4
).

For the main part of the proof let us consider the function

S(z)\coloneqq\operatorname{sinc}\big(\sqrt{z^{2}+b^{2}}\big),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Since the restriction
S|_{\mathbb{R}}
is square integrable, its Fourier transform is given by the improper Riemann integral

\mathcal{F}[S|_{\mathbb{R}}](k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{R_{1},R_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-R_{1}}^{R_{2}}e^{-ikx}S(x)dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{R_{1},R_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\cos(kx)S(x)dx,\quad k\in\mathbb{R},

where the imaginary part of the integral vanishes in the limit due to the symmetry of
S
. Starting with
|k|<1
, we substitute
x=b\sinh(t)
, to write this integral as

(A.5)

\mathcal{F}[S|_{\mathbb{R}}](k)=\frac{b}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{\widetilde{R}_{1},\widetilde{R}_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\widetilde{R}_{1}}^{\widetilde{R}_{2}}\cos\big(kb\sinh(t)\big)S\big(b\sinh(t)\big)\cosh(t)dt,

where we used
\widetilde{R}_{i}\coloneqq\text{arsinh}(\frac{R_{i}}{b})
,
i=1,2
. Using the trigonometric identity
2\sin(u)\cos(v)=\sin(u+v)+\sin(u-v)
we can write the integrand as

\displaystyle 2b\cos\big(kb\sinh(t)\big)S\big(b\sinh(t)\big)\cosh(t)

\displaystyle=2\cos\big(kb\sinh(t)\big)\sin\big(b\cosh(t)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=\sin\big(b\cosh(t)+kb\sinh(t)\big)+\sin\big(b\cosh(t)-kb\sinh(t)\big).

Since
|k|<1
there exists some
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
e^{2t_{0}}=\frac{1+k}{1-k}
. Then, it is easy to verify that
\cosh(t_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}}}
and
\sinh(t_{0})=\frac{k}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}}}
and consequently

\cosh(t)\pm k\sinh(t)=\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,\cosh(t\pm t_{0}).

Using this identity we can further rewrite the integrand as

\displaystyle 2b\cos\big(kb\sinh(t)\big)S\big(b\sinh(t)\big)\cosh(t)=

\displaystyle\sin\big(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,\cosh(t+t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle+\sin\big(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,\cosh(t-t_{0})\big).

Plugging this representation in the integral (
A.5
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[S|_{\mathbb{R}}](k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{\widetilde{R}_{1},\widetilde{R}_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\widetilde{R}_{1}}^{\widetilde{R}_{2}}\Big(\sin\big(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,\cosh(t+t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+\sin\big(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,\cosh(t-t_{0})\big)\Big)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{\widehat{R}_{1},\widehat{R}_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\widehat{R}_{1}}^{\widehat{R}_{2}}\sin\big(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,\cosh(t)\big)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\lim\limits_{\widehat{R}_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{\widehat{R}_{2}}\sin\big(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\,\cosh(t)\big)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}}J_{0}\big(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\big),

where in the second line we substituted
t\rightarrow t\mp t_{0}
, used
\widehat{R}_{1}\coloneqq\widetilde{R}_{1}\mp t_{0}
,
\widehat{R}_{2}\coloneqq\widetilde{R}_{2}\pm t_{0}
, in the two respective integrals and added them together. Moreover, in the last equation we used the representation (
A.4
).

For
k>1
we use the Cauchy theorem to change the integration path to a semicircle in the lower half space

(A.6)

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}[S|_{\mathbb{R}}](k)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{R_{1},R_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-R_{1}}^{R_{2}}e^{-ikx}S(x)dx\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}e^{-ikx}S(x)dx\\
&=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}R\,e^{-ikRe^{i\varphi}}S(Re^{i\varphi})e^{i\varphi}d\varphi.\end{split}

Choosing
R>b
and using that

(A.7)

R^{2}-b^{2}\leq|R^{2}e^{2i\varphi}+b^{2}|\leq R^{2}+b^{2},\quad\varphi\in[\pi,2\pi],

the function
S
in the integrand can be estimated by

(A.8)

|S(Re^{i\varphi})|=\bigg|\frac{\sin(\sqrt{R^{2}e^{2i\varphi}+b^{2}})}{\sqrt{R^{2}e^{2i\varphi}+b^{2}}}\bigg|\leq\frac{e^{|\operatorname{Im}(\sqrt{R^{2}e^{2i\varphi}+b^{2}})|}}{\sqrt{R^{2}-b^{2}}},\quad\varphi\in[\pi,2\pi].

Writing any
w\in\mathbb{C}
as
w=|w|e^{i\operatorname{Arg}(w)}
with
\operatorname{Arg}(w)\in[0,2\pi)
, our choice (
3.2
) of the complex square root shows
\sqrt{w}=\sqrt{|w|}\,e^{i\frac{\operatorname{Arg}(w)}{2}}
. Using this, we rewrite the imaginary part

\operatorname{Im}(\sqrt{w})=\sqrt{|w|}\,\sin\Big(\frac{\operatorname{Arg}(w)}{2}\Big)=\frac{\sqrt{|w|}}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{1-\cos(\operatorname{Arg}(w))}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{|w|-\operatorname{Re}(w)},\quad w\in\mathbb{C}.

This representation and the estimate (
A.7
) can now be used to estimate the exponent in (
A.8
) by

2\big|\operatorname{Im}\big(\sqrt{R^{2}e^{2i\varphi}+b^{2}}\big)\big|^{2}=|R^{2}e^{2i\varphi}+b^{2}|-\operatorname{Re}(R^{2}e^{2i\varphi}+b^{2})\leq R^{2}-R^{2}\cos(2\varphi)=2R^{2}\sin^{2}(\varphi).

Hence we can further estimate (
A.8
) by

|S(Re^{i\varphi})|\leq\frac{e^{-R\sin(\varphi)}}{\sqrt{R^{2}-b^{2}}},\quad\varphi\in[\pi,2\pi].

Using this inequality, the Fourier transform (
A.6
) can be estimated by

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}[S|_{\mathbb{R}}](k)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\frac{R}{\sqrt{R^{2}-b^{2}}}e^{(k-1)R\sin(\varphi)}d\varphi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{R}{\sqrt{R^{2}-b^{2}}}e^{(k-1)R\sin(\varphi)}d\varphi

\displaystyle=0.

For
k<-1
we also conclude
\mathcal{F}[S|_{\mathbb{R}}](k)=0
by the symmetry of
S
.

Summing up, we have now shown that

(A.9)

\mathcal{F}[S|_{\mathbb{R}}](k)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}}J_{0}(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}}),&\text{if }|k|<1,\\
0,&\text{if }|k|>1.\end{cases}

Applying the inverse Fourier transform to (
A.9
) we obtain

S(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ikx}J_{0}(b\sqrt{1-k^{2}})dk,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
J_{0}
is a bounded function, the right hand side extends to an entire function when
x\in\mathbb{R}
is replaced by
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Since the holomorphic extension is unique it coincides with
S
and we conclude the stated integral representation (
A.3
).
∎
