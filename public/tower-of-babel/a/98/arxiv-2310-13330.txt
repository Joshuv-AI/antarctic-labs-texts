Flat to nonflat: Calculating nonlinear power spectra of biased tracers for nonflat Λ \Lambda CDM model
Ryo Terasawa, Ryuichi Takahashi, Takahiro Nishimichi, Masahiro Takada

Abstract
The growth of large-scale structure, together with the geometrical information of cosmic expansion history and cosmological distances, can be used to obtain constraints on the spatial curvature of the universe that probes the early universe physics, whereas modeling the nonlinear growth in a nonflat universe is still challenging due to computational expense of simulations in a high-dimensional cosmological parameter space. In this paper, we develop an approximate method to compute the halo-matter and halo-auto power spectra for nonflat Λ \Lambda CDM model, from quantities representing the nonlinear evolution of the corresponding flat Λ \Lambda CDM model, based on the separate universe (SU) method. By utilizing the fact that the growth response to long-wavelength fluctuations (equivalently the curvature), T δ b ​ ( k ) T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k) , is approximated by the response to the Hubble parameter, T h ​ ( k ) T_{h}(k) , our method allows one to estimate the nonlinear power spectra in a nonflat universe efficiently from the power spectra of the flat universe. We use N N -body simulations to show that the estimator can provide the halo-matter (halo-auto) power spectrum at ∼ 1 % \sim 1\% ( ∼ 2 % \sim 2\% ) accuracy up to k ≃ 3 ​ ( 1 ) ​ h ​ Mpc − 1 k\simeq 3(1)\,h{\rm Mpc}^{-1} even for a model with large curvature Ω K = ± 0.1 \Omega_{K}=\pm 0.1 . Using the estimator we can extend the prediction of the existing emulators such as Dark Emulator to nonflat models without degrading their accuracy. Since the response to long-wavelength fluctuations is also a key quantity for estimating the super sample covariance (SSC), we discuss that the approximate identity T δ b ​ ( k ) ≈ T h ​ ( k ) T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)\approx T_{h}(k) can be used to calculate the SSC terms analytically.



I
Introduction

The spatial curvature of the universe (
\Omega_{K}
) is an important quantity that characterizes the geometry of the universe and probes the physics of the early universe including the mechanism of inflation.
The curvature of the universe influences various observables, including the spatial distribution of galaxies, through both its geometric properties and its impact on the growth of large-scale structures (LSS). The geometrical constraint, inferred from
the primary CMB anisotropy information of the
Planck
data
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
,
is given as

\Omega_{K}=-0.044^{+0.018}_{-0.015}
(68% CL,
Planck
TT, TE, EE+lowE), implying a
2\sigma
hint of the close geometry, although most of the constraints are consistent with flat universe
(
Alam et al. 2017
;
Kumar et al. 2021
;
Vagnozzi et al. 2021a
;
Dhawan et al. 2021
;
Alam et al. 2021
;
Vagnozzi et al. 2021b
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
DES Collaboration et al. 2022
;
Tristram et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.
This indicates the importance of constraining the curvature from LSS as an independent probe.
Furthermore, to obtain more precise constraints on the curvature close to the amplitudes of primordial fluctuations,
|\Omega_{K}|\sim 10^{-4}
, it is crucial to combine observations of these two effects
(
Leonard et al. 2016
, e.g.)
.
However, achieving this requires development of a theoretical model that adequately incorporates mode coupling effects. Mode coupling refers to the phenomenon where the non-linear nature of gravity causes fluctuations of different wavelengths to interact with each other.
Constructing such a model is generally challenging. Despite its inherent difficulty, incorporating small-scale information is vital for obtaining stronger constraints on cosmological parameters.
For flat cosmological models in particular, fitting formulae or emulators based on the predictions of
N
-body simulations have been developed
(
Peacock and Dodds 1996
;
Smith et al. 2003
;
Heitmann et al. 2010
;
Heitmann et al. 2009
;
Takahashi et al. 2012
;
Heitmann et al. 2016
;
Lawrence et al. 2017
;
Nishimichi et al. 2019
;
Reimberg et al. 2020
;
Mead et al. 2021
;
Smith and Angulo 2019
;
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2021
, e.g.)
.
For example,
Dark Emulator

(
Nishimichi et al. 2019
)
predicts the basic statistical quantities of dark matter halos (halos in what follows for simplicity)
such as their abundance as a function of mass and the halo power spectrum down to the nonlinear scale. With these nonlinear predictions of the halo statistics, together with the halo occupation distribution (hereafter, HOD) description, Ref.
Miyatake et al. 2023
analyzed the two-point correlation function of galaxy clustering, galaxy-galaxy lensing and cosmic shear (so-called
3\times 2
pt analysis), and obtained tighter
constraints on the cosmological parameters compared to the traditional analysis based on the linear bias
model
(
Sugiyama et al. 2023
)
.

Theoretical models for the power spectrum in a nonflat universe are still in the development stage compared to models in flat universe due to the computational expense of covering a wider range in a multi-dimensional cosmological parameter space.
Because of this limitation of knowledge on
nonlinear scales, in the
3\times 2
pt analysis of the DES Y3
(
DES Collaboration et al. 2022
)
data they used only the linear scales. In this work, we provide a method to model the
power spectrum down to nonlinear scales so that one can gain information on the curvature from small scales.

The effect of curvature on structure formation can be identified with the very long-wavelength density fluctuations in the
\Lambda
CDM
model based on the separate universe (SU) approach

Baldauf et al. 2011
;
Takada and Hu 2013
;
Li et al. 2014a
;
Li et al. 2014b
;
Wagner et al. 2015a
.
In
Terasawa et al. 2022
, we developed a method to compute the nonlinear matter power spectrum for nonflat cosmologies utilizing the SU approach.
We utilized the approximate identity that states the effect of these long-wavelength density fluctuations on structure formation is well reproduced by the response to the Hubble parameter
h

(
Schaan et al. 2014
;
Terasawa et al. 2022
)
.
Using the response to long-wavelength fluctuations modeled in this way, we have shown that the model of the matter power spectrum for flat universes,whose accurate model calculation is already available from the fitting formula or emulator, can be extended to calculate the nonlinear matter power spectrum for a universe with non-zero curvature.

In this work, we apply the SU approach to predict the halo-matter and halo-auto power spectra down to the nonlinear scales for nonflat cosmologies.
We will verify that the approximate identity holds for the halo-matter and halo-auto power spectrum responses and construct the estimators of these power spectra using the response to the Hubble parameter. The nonlinear prediction of these spectra
together with the HOD prescription provides a way to predict the galaxy-galaxy lensing or galaxy clustering data down to small scales
(
Miyatake et al. 2022a
;
Miyatake et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.

Another application of the modeling of the response to long-wavelength fluctuations is to compute
the super sample covariance (SSC)
(
Takada and Hu 2013
)
, which is the sample variance contribution
caused by mode coupling with long-wavelength fluctuations.
SSC for the power spectrum can be computed using its response to the super survey modes
(
Schaan et al. 2014
, e.g.)
.
We also provide a way to compute the response to the super survey modes using the power spectrum response to
h
, which enables us to utilize a fitting formula or an emulator validated only for flat geometry.

In the literature, there are analytical methods to compute the response, with the perturbation theory
(
Baldauf et al. 2016
, e.g.)
or halo model
(
Krause and Eifler 2017
, e.g.)
.
Using these analytical methods, we can calculate the response quickly.
However, these models suffer from the limitation of scales or inaccuracy.
On the other hand, measuring the SSC from
the scatter among an ensemble of simulations
(
Bayer et al. 2023
, e.g.)
is expected to provide an accurate covariance matrix with a relatively high computational cost.
The size of the data vector is expected to become larger for future surveys, especially the ones using multi-tracer, for which the number of simulations needed could be
\mathcal{O}(10^{2})-\mathcal{O}(10^{3})
.
In this paper, our model is compared with simulations, and shown to be more accurate than the analytical methods used in the literature, while the computational cost is much cheaper than a simulation ensemble.
Our method for computing the total response would be implemented into the Core Cosmology Library
(
Chisari et al. 2019
)
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we first review the SU approach for the nonlinear matter power spectrum in Ref.
Terasawa et al. 2022
and generalize it to predict the halo-matter or halo power spectrum. In Sec.
III
we introduce our method to compute the total response to the super survey modes using the response to
h
. In Sec.
IV
we describe details of
N
-body simulations for flat and nonflat
\Lambda
CDM
models used in this paper. In Sec.
V
we present the main results of this paper and show a numerical validation of our methods.
Sec.
VI
is devoted to discussion and conclusion.
In Appendix
A
we describe the details of how to compute the power spectrum response to the long-wavelength fluctuations.
Throughout the paper, we assume
\Lambda
CDM
model and flat geometry for the fiducial cosmology. We also assume that the SSC is calculated in this fiducial cosmology.

II
SU approach for
P(k;\Omega_{K})

II.1
SU approach for
P_{\rm mm}(k;\Omega_{K})

In this section we briefly review
the SU approach to predict the nonlinear matter power spectrum
P_{\rm mm}(k)
for
a nonflat universe following Ref.
Terasawa et al. 2022
.

First, since the effect of the curvature or the long-wavelength density fluctuations on structure formation appears only in the late universe, throughout this paper we consider a model where structure formation in the early universe is identical to that of the fiducial flat universe. Specifically, we keep the parameters

\displaystyle\left\{\omega_{\rm c},\omega_{\rm b},A_{s},n_{s}\right\},

(1)

fixed, where
\omega_{\rm c}(\equiv\Omega_{\rm c}h^{2})
and
\omega_{\rm b}(\equiv\Omega_{\rm b}h^{2})
are the physical density parameters of CDM and baryon, respectively, and

A_{s}
and
n_{s}
are the amplitude (at the pivot scale
k_{\rm pivot}=0.05\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
)
and the spectral tilt of the power spectrum of primordial curvature perturbations.
Note that we fix
the sum of neutrino masses
so that the early universe physics remains unchanged, and treat its impact only through the linear transfer function of
total matter fluctuations at
z=0

(see Refs.
Nishimichi et al. 2019
;
Bayer et al. 2021
, for more details)
.
These models with the four fixed parameters (Eq.
1
) share the same
linear power spectrum
P_{L}(k)

at sufficiently high redshifts
z_{i}\gg 1
.
The remaining parameters that affect
structure formation
are
\Omega_{K}
and
h
within the nonflat
\Lambda
CDM
model. Note that the density parameter for the cosmological constant,
\Omega_{\Lambda}
, is automatically determined once the parameters above are all fixed.

In the SU approach, the effects of the background density modulation
\delta_{\rm b}(t)
in a flat universe are interpreted as the local effective cosmology with modified background density

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{\rm m}(t)=\bar{\rho}_{{\rm m}f}(t)\left[1+\delta_{\rm b}(t)\right],

(2)

and non-zero curvature corresponding to
\delta_{\rm b}
(see below).
Hence it gives a mapping between nonflat
and flat
\Lambda
CDM
models. Hereafter we call the two models

\Omega_{K}
-
\Lambda
CDM
and
f\Lambda
CDM
, respectively, and we denote quantities in the
f\Lambda
CDM
model by sub/superscript “
f
”. We assume
\delta_{\rm b}(t)
evolves according to the linear growth factor
D_{f}(t)

as
\delta_{\rm b}(t)\propto D_{f}(t)
.

The curvature and overdensity are related to each other via

\displaystyle\Omega_{K}=-\frac{5\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{3}\frac{{\delta_{\rm b}(t)}}{D_{f}(t)}.

(3)

The remaining parameter,
h
, is mapped as

\displaystyle h_{f}=h(1-\Omega_{K})^{1/2}.

(4)

Note that the redshifts in these two cosmologies
at a given cosmic time
t
are related as

\displaystyle(1+z_{f})[1+\delta_{\rm b}(z_{f})]^{1/3}=1+z.

(5)

Inversely, we can treat the nonflat universe as an overdense/underdense region in the corresponding flat universe, and the power spectrum in the target nonflat universe can be approximated by Taylor expansion around the flat universe as
discussed in Ref.
Terasawa et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle{P}(k,z;\Omega_{K})

\displaystyle\simeq P^{f}(k,z_{f};\delta_{\rm b})

\displaystyle\hskip-30.00005pt\simeq\left.P^{f}(k,z_{f})\right|_{\delta_{\rm b}=0}\left[1+\left.\frac{\partial\ln P^{f}(k,z_{f};\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},\delta_{\rm b}=0}\delta_{\rm b}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-30.00005pt\equiv\tilde{P}(k,z;\Omega_{K}),

(6)

where
\delta_{\rm b}\equiv\delta_{\rm b}(z_{f})
.
In the last equality on the r.h.s.,
we have put the tilde symbol
\tilde{\hskip 10.00002pt}
on
\tilde{P}(k,z;\Omega_{K})
to explicitly denote
that
\tilde{P}
is an “estimator” of the nonlinear
matter power spectrum for the
\Omega_{K}
-
\Lambda
CDM
model.
We introduced subscript “G” to
\partial P^{f}(k)/\partial\delta_{\rm b}
to emphasize that it is the growth response
(
Li et al. 2014a
)
.
The growth response can be computed as the difference between the power spectra at a fixed comoving wavenumber
k
in the two SU cosmologies, omitting the dilation effect that originates from the difference in the scale factors (Sec.
III
). Throughout the paper, the wavenumber
k
refers to a given comoving wavenumber even if the corresponding physical scales differ among the cosmologies.
Note that the expansion of Eq. (
6
) is applicable to not only the matter power spectrum but also the halo-matter and halo-auto power spectra as we will show below.

Furthermore, we define a normalized growth response
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm mm}(k)
as

\displaystyle T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm mm}(k)\equiv\left[2\frac{\partial\ln D(\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right]^{-1}\left.\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm mm}(k;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},\delta_{\rm b}=0}.

(7)

The normalized response has an asymptotic behavior of
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm mm}\rightarrow 1
at the linear limit

k\rightarrow 0
, because
P_{\rm mm}(k,z)\propto D(z)^{2}P_{L}(k,z_{i})
in such linear regime.
The linear limit of the matter power spectrum growth response is given in
Refs.
Baldauf et al. 2011
;
Takada and Hu 2013
as

\displaystyle 2\frac{\partial\ln D(\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\approx\frac{26}{21}.

(8)

Since we have a prediction for the linear power spectrum for the
\Omega_{K}
-
\Lambda
CDM
model, we only need to expand the nonlinear correction
B(k,z)
defined as
P(k,z)\equiv P_{L}(k,z)B(k,z)
.
Using the normalized response
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)
, we can write the expansion as

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(k;\Omega_{K})=\left(\frac{D(z)}{D_{f}(z_{f})}\right)^{2}P^{f}(k)\left[1+\frac{26}{21}(T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)-1)\delta_{\rm b}(z_{f})+\left(\frac{G_{2}(k)}{2}-\frac{1501}{1323}-\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)^{2}(T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)-1)\right)\delta_{\rm b}(z_{f})^{2}\right],

(9)

where

\displaystyle G_{2}(k)\equiv\frac{1}{P^{f}(k)}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}P(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}^{2}}\right|_{{\rm G},\delta_{\rm b}=0}

(10)

and
1501/1323
is the linear limit of
G_{2}(k)/2
for the matter power spectrum
(
Wagner et al. 2015b
)
, derived in an Einstein-de Sitter cosmology.
We explicitly wrote down the expansion up to
the second order in
\delta_{\rm b}
and show below that
the second order term is negligible for moderate values of
|\Omega_{K}|
that are consistent with the current bounds,
|\Omega_{K}|\lesssim\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
.
For the matter power spectrum, the normalized growth response
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)
ranges about
[0.3,1.6]
at redshift
z\simeq[0,1.5]

in the range of
k\simeq[10^{-2},6]\,h{\rm Mpc}^{-1}

(see Fig. 2 of Ref.
(
Terasawa et al. 2022
)
) and
G_{2}(k)/2
ranges about
[0.5,2]
at
z=0

in the range of
k\simeq[10^{-2},2]\,h{\rm Mpc}^{-1}

(Fig. 2 of
(
Wagner et al. 2015a
)
). Since the prefactors of the first and second order terms before
\delta_{\rm b}
and
\delta_{\rm b}^{2}
are at most
\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
, the contributions from the first and second order terms are at percent and sub-percent level, respectively, even for a large curvature case of
|\Omega_{K}|\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})

(corresponding to
\delta_{\rm b}(t)\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
). As we will show below, the second or higher order terms are also negligible for the halo-matter or halo-auto power spectrum. Hence we consider the expansion up to the first order of
\delta_{\rm b}
throughout this paper.

We also define the normalized growth response to
h
within the flat model as:

\displaystyle T_{h}^{\rm mm}(k)\equiv\left[2\frac{\partial\ln D}{\partial h}\right]^{-1}\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm mm}(k;h)}{\partial h}.

(11)

As shown numerically in Refs.
(
Li et al. 2014b
;
Terasawa et al. 2022
)
(also as will be shown in Fig.
1
), these two responses agree well even in the nonlinear regime:
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm mm}(k)\simeq T_{h}^{\rm mm}(k)
. Finally, using this approximate identity
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)\simeq T_{h}(k)
and ignoring the terms of second or higher order in
\delta_{\rm b}(t)
, we can approximate the estimator as

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(k,z;\Omega_{K})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D(z)}{D_{f}(z_{f})}\right)^{2}P^{f}(k,z_{f})

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{26}{21}(T_{h}(k)-1)\delta_{\rm b}(z_{f})\right].

(12)

By construction, the estimator reproduces the linear prediction at
k\rightarrow 0
, where the first order term vanishes because
T_{h}^{\rm mm}(k\rightarrow 0)=1
.
The novel feature of the above estimator (Eq.
12
) is that it allows one to compute the nonlinear matter power spectrum
for nonflat universe from the quantities in flat universe.
With this estimator we can extend emulators available in the community, which are applicable only for flat universes, to predict the power spectrum in a nonflat universe.

\delta_{\rm b}
and
h

N
-body simulations (see Section
IV
for details of the simulations).
The circle, triangle, and square symbols show
the responses of the matter power spectrum (
T^{\rm mm}(k)
), halo-matter power spectrum (
T^{\rm hm}(k)
), and halo power spectrum (
T^{\rm hh}(k)
), respectively.

T^{\rm hh}(k)
, we only plot the scales where halo power spectra after subtracting the shot noise have positive values. We slightly shift the symbols of

along the

In this paper, we extend the SU approach mentioned above to the halo-matter and halo-auto power spectra.
Similarly to the above discussion,
we define
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm XY}(k)
and
T_{h}^{\rm XY}(k)
as

\displaystyle T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm XY}(k)

\displaystyle\equiv\left[2\frac{\partial\ln D(\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right]^{-1}\left.\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm XY}(k;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},\delta_{\rm b}=0},

\displaystyle T_{h}^{\rm XY}(k)

\displaystyle\equiv\left[2\frac{\partial\ln D}{\partial h}\right]^{-1}\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm XY}(k;h)}{\partial h},

(13)

where
\rm{XY}=\{\rm mm,hm,hh\}
. When considering the response of
P_{\rm hm}(k)
or
P_{\rm hh}(k)
, we perform the derivative keeping the comoving halo number density fixed.
As we will specify the latter in Sec.
A.3
, these responses are not the same as the growth response in the SU approach where the derivative is performed keeping the halo mass threshold fixed.

As a highlight of our approach, in Fig.
1
we show the responses of power spectra of matter-matter, halo-matter, and halo-halo
to
\delta_{\rm b}
or
h
,
T^{\rm mm}
,
T^{\rm hm}
and
T^{\rm hh}
, which are computed using Eq. (
13
).
At the large-scale limit
k\rightarrow 0
, the matter-matter response
T^{\rm mm}_{\delta_{\rm b}}=1
and
T^{\rm mm}_{h}=1
by definition. Given that the
abundance-matched halo samples correspond to the same initial density peaks, which is true as long as mergers do not severely affect this correspondence,
the clustering amplitudes of the abundance-matched halos on large scales barely change. This is why
T^{\rm hh}(k\rightarrow 0)\simeq 0
and

T^{\rm hm}
is smaller than
T^{\rm mm}
.
The figure also shows that the responses for “mm”, “hm” and “hh”
have quite different

k
-dependence. Hence accurate calibration is important to capture the scale dependence.
The responses for
\delta_{\rm b}
and
h
are in good agreement with each other, for all the “mm”, “hm” and “hh” power spectra, which validates our method to approximate the response to
\delta_{\rm b}
by the response to
h
.

Finally, using the approximate identity
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)\simeq T_{h}(k)
, we can obtain the estimator as

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{\rm XY}(k,z;\Omega_{K})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D(z)}{D_{f}(z_{f})}\right)^{2}P_{\rm XY}^{f}(k,z_{f})

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{26}{21}(T^{\rm XY}_{h}(k)-1)\delta_{\rm b}(z_{f})\right].

(14)

III
Total response estimator using flat
\Lambda
CDM
model

In this section, we discuss that our approximation using the response of the power spectrum to the Hubble parameter can be used to calibrate the SSC of a cosmological observable. Here the SSC is the sampling variance error of the observable in a finite-volume
survey, which arises from the mode coupling of density fluctuations in the survey with density fluctuations on scales greater than the survey window, i.e. super-survey modes
(
Takada and Hu 2013
;
Li et al. 2014a
;
Takahashi et al. 2019
)
.
For instance,
the SSC gives a dominant source of the sample variance on scales larger than the scales where the shot noise dominates, e.g., for the two-point correlation function of cosmic shear, which is given by the
weighted line-of-sight integration of the matter power spectrum.

SSC for the X- and Y-observables in the
i
-th and
j
-th bins, respectively, is generally expressed, as proposed by Ref.
Takada and Hu 2013
, as

\displaystyle C^{({\rm XY}){\rm SSC}}_{ij}\approx\sigma_{\rm b}^{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{O}_{{\rm X}i}}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{O}_{{\rm Y}j}}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}},

(15)

where
\sigma_{\rm b}^{2}
is the variance in the linear density filtered by
a window function assuming a sufficiently large survey volume in that the super-survey modes (
\delta_{\rm b}
)
are in the linear regime,
and
\partial{\cal O}/\partial\delta_{\rm b}
is the
total
response of the observable to
\delta_{\rm b}
including both the growth and dilation
responses (see below).
The observables can be any statistical cosmological quantities such as power spectrum, bispectrum, and cluster mass function.
In this paper, we consider, as
\mathcal{O}_{\rm X}
and/or
\mathcal{O}_{\rm Y}
, either of the matter, halo-matter, or halo-halo power spectum and the
i
-th index in the above equation corresponds to the
i
-th

k
bin. Otherwise, the above equation is a general expression of SSC. We also note that, once the total response for the 3D observable (e.g.
the matter power spectrum) is given, the SSC
to the corresponding angular observable (e.g., cosmic power spectrum) can be obtained by a weighted line-of-sight integral of the 3D SSC term
(
Takahashi et al. 2019
)
.

In the following, we provide an approximate way to compute the total response using the power spectrum response to
h
.

III.1
Preliminary

First, we summarize analytical methods of computing the total response proposed in the literature.

\bullet
Perturbation theory
–
The total response can be computed using the perturbation theory (PT)
Baldauf et al. 2016
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm mm,PT}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{68}{21}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial\ln k^{3}P_{L}(k)}{\partial\ln k},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm gm,PT}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{68}{21}+\frac{b_{g,2}}{b_{g}}-b_{g}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial\ln k^{3}P_{L}(k)}{\partial\ln k},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm gg,PT}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{68}{21}+2\frac{b_{g,2}}{b_{g}}-2b_{g}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial\ln k^{3}P_{L}(k)}{\partial\ln k},

(16)

where
b_{g}
and
b_{g,2}
denote the first- and second-order Eulerian galaxy biases.
Using the halo occupation distribution
(HOD) prescription
(
Zheng et al. 2005
, e.g.)
,
b_{g}
is given as

\displaystyle b_{g}=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}}\int~\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)b_{{\rm h},1}(M)\langle N_{\mathrm{g}}(M)\rangle,

(17)

with the mean number density of galaxies

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{g}=\int~\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)\langle N_{\mathrm{g}}(M)\rangle,

(18)

where
\langle N_{\mathrm{g}}(M)\rangle
is the mean number of galaxies in a halo,

\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}M
is the halo mass function in the mass range

[M,M+\mathrm{d}M]
, and
b_{{\rm h},1}(M)
is the linear bias of halos of mass
M
.
Throughout the paper, we use the fitting function by Ref.
Tinker et al. 2010
for the linear halo bias
b_{{\rm h},1}(M)
.

Similarly,
b_{g,2}
is given as

\displaystyle b_{g,2}=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}}\int\!\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)b_{{\rm h},2}(M)\langle N_{\mathrm{g}}(M)\rangle,

(19)

where
b_{{\rm h},2}(M)
is the 2nd-order halo bias of halos of mass
M
. Throughout this paper, we use the fitting formula of
b_{{\rm h},2}
proposed in Ref.
Hoffmann et al. 2017
, which gives
b_{{\rm h},2}(M)

in terms of the linear halo bias, i.e.

b_{{\rm h},2}=b_{{\rm h},2}(b_{{\rm h},1})
.

As we will see in Figs.
6
and
7
, the perturbation theory well describes the response on large scale, but it fails to reproduce
the small-scale behavior,
where perturbation theory itself starts to break down.

\bullet
Halo Model
–
The halo model
(
Sheth and Tormen 2002
, e.g.)
is commonly used to calculate the response of the matter power spectrum in the literature
(
Takada and Hu 2013
;
Li et al. 2014a
;
Chiang et al. 2014
)
.
Ref.
(
Krause and Eifler 2017
)
derived the responses for the multiprobe power spectrum, denoted as

P_{\rm XY}
, based on the halo model. Following the notations in Ref.
(
Krause and Eifler 2017
)
, the
responses are written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P_{\rm XY}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{47}{21}+\frac{I_{\rm X}^{2}(k)}{I_{\rm X}^{1}(k)}+\frac{I_{\rm Y}^{2}(k)}{I_{\rm Y}^{1}(k)}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial\ln P_{L}(k)}{\partial\ln k}\right)

\displaystyle\times I_{\rm X}^{1}(k)I_{\rm Y}^{1}(k)P_{L}(k)

\displaystyle+I_{\rm XY}^{1}(k,k)-\left[b_{\rm X,X=g}+b_{\rm Y,Y=g}\right]P_{\rm XY}(k),

(20)

where
b_{{\rm X,X}={\rm g}}=b_{\rm g}
for
{\rm X}={\rm g}
and otherwise
b_{{\rm X,X}={\rm g}}=0

and the functions
I_{\rm X}^{\alpha}
and
I_{\rm XY}^{\alpha}
are defined as

\displaystyle I_{\rm X}^{\alpha}(k)

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)b_{\rm{h},\alpha}(M)\tilde{u}_{\rm X}(k;M),

\displaystyle I_{\rm XY}^{\alpha}(k,k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)b_{\rm{h},\alpha}(M)\tilde{u}_{\rm X}(k;M)\tilde{u}_{\rm Y}(k^{\prime};M),

(21)

where
\tilde{u}_{\rm X}(k;M)
is the Fourier transform of the radial profile of tracers X in host halos, multiplied by the number density normalization and the HOD function for galaxies following the notations in Ref.
Krause and Eifler 2017
. Note that
\tilde{u}_{\rm X}(k;M)
has a dimension of volume.

We stress that we included the terms proportional to
I_{\rm X}^{2}(k)
in the parentheses of Eq. (
20
), which were missing
in Ref.
(
Krause and Eifler 2017
)
. These terms depend on the 2nd-order halo bias,
b_{{\rm h},2}
, which arises from the response of the linear halo bias

b_{{\rm h},1}
to
\delta_{\rm b}
. For the matter power spectrum (for the case
{\rm X}={\rm Y}={\rm m}
),
the terms proportional to
I_{\rm m}^{2}(k)
are negligible due to the halo model consistency relation
(
Wagner et al. 2015b
)
.
Since
I_{g}^{1}(k)\rightarrow b_{g}
and
I_{g}^{2}(k)\rightarrow b_{g,2}
at the
k\rightarrow 0
limit,
I_{g}^{2}(k)/I_{g}^{1}(k)\rightarrow b_{g,2}/b_{g}
at
the limit, which reproduces the PT response (Eq.
16
).
Thus we believe that the above response formula (Eq.
20
) is more accurate in the sense that it includes the PT theory at the limit
of
k\rightarrow 0
.

We will below assess the accuracy and limitation of these analytical formulae of the power spectrum responses by comparing the model predictions with
the simulation results.

III.2
h
response method

The long-wavelength modes (super-survey modes) whose
wavelengths are larger than the survey volume/simulation box affect the growth of LSS via mode coupling. The effects of the long-wavelength modes can be considered as the background density (mean density) modulation,
\delta_{\rm b}
, which in turn can be interpreted as the local effective cosmology with non-zero curvature corresponding to
\delta_{\rm b}
(the SU approach
(
Baldauf et al. 2011
;
Takada and Hu 2013
;
Li et al. 2014a
;
Li et al. 2014b
;
Wagner et al. 2015a
, e.g.)
).

Different large-scale structure tracers are measured with respect to either the
global
or
local
mean density of the tracers, where
the
local
mean is the average density of the tracers in a finite-volume survey. Depending on this difference,
the response of power spectrum of X and Y tracers,

P_{\rm XY}
, to the super survey modes can be decomposed into three contributions
(
Li et al. 2014a
;
Takahashi et al. 2019
, e.g.)
:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm XY}(k;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm total}

\displaystyle=n+\left.\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm XY}(k;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial\ln k^{3}P_{\rm XY}(k)}{\partial\ln k}.

(22)

The first term on the r.h.s. accounts for the change in the mean density of the tracers by
\delta_{\rm b}
, used in the definition of the density fluctuation field;
for example, for the matter field (XY=mm),
\delta_{\mathrm{m}}=\rho_{\mathrm{m}}/\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{m}}-1
.
The cases of
n=2,1
and
0
correspond to
“
{\rm XY}
”
=
“
{\rm mm}
”, “
{\rm gm}
” and “gg”, respectively, which are
relevant to cosmic shear, galaxy-galaxy weak lensing and
galaxy-galaxy clustering, respectively
(
Takahashi et al. 2019
)
.
The second term is the growth response, which describes the fractional change in the power spectrum amplitude by
the presence of
\delta_{\rm b}
.
The last term is the dilation response, which originates from the change in the physical scale corresponding to a given comoving scale due to the change in cosmic expansion.

Using this decomposition, we propose the matter power spectrum response estimator as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm mm}(k;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm total}

\displaystyle=2+\frac{26}{21}T_{h}^{\rm mm}(k)-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial\ln k^{3}P_{\rm mm}(k)}{\partial\ln k},

where we have used the fact that the normalized growth response to
\delta_{\rm b}
is approximated by that to
h
:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm mm}(k;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{G}=\frac{26}{21}T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm mm}(k)\simeq\frac{26}{21}T_{h}^{\rm mm}(k).

(24)

Similarly, as we will show in Appendix
A.3
, we can use the approximate identity of the responses of halo-matter and halo-auto power spectra to predict the growth responses of galaxy-matter and galaxy-auto power spectra with the HOD description:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial P_{\rm gm}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}}\int\mathrm{d}M\left\{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}n(>M)\right.\left[b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M)P_{\rm hm}(k;M)+\frac{26}{21}T_{h}^{\rm hm}(k;>M)P_{\rm hm}(k;>M)\right]

\displaystyle~~~~~\left.+\frac{1}{3}P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right\}-b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gm}(k),

(25)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial P_{\rm gg}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\iint\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M^{\prime})}{\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}}\right.n(>M)n(>M^{\prime})\left[2b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M^{\prime})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,M^{\prime})\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~+\left.\left.\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)T_{h}^{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})+\frac{2}{3}{P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle~~~~~+\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)\left[b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(M)\langle\tilde{U}_{g}^{2}(k;M)\rangle+\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial\langle\tilde{U}_{g}^{2}(k;M)\rangle}{\partial\ln k}\right]-2b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gg}(k),

(26)

where the super-script
L
denotes the Lagrangian bias and
\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)
describes the quantity arising from
the galaxy-halo connection (see Appendix
A
for the definition).

Since the
h
response is defined within flat cosmologies, we can predict the power spectrum response from flat cosmology predictions or simulations without
performing a pair of nonflat universe simulations following the SU framework.
As we will show below, using the simulation-based emulator we can predict the response accurately down to the nonlinear scale.

Finally, to ensure the estimator is correct at large scales, we stitch the estimator with perturbation theory prediction
(
Baldauf et al. 2016
)
described above in Eq. (
16
).
Specifically, we smoothly stitch the estimator with perturbation theory prediction as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm XY}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm XY,PT}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}e^{-(k/k_{\rm switch})}

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial\ln P_{\rm XY,est}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\left[1-e^{-(k/k_{\rm switch)}}\right].

(27)

Throughout the paper, we adopt the switching scale
k_{\rm switch}=0.08~h~{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
for XY = mm, gm and gg.

IV.3
Measurements of power spectra and the responses

To calculate the power spectrum from each simulation output, we assign the
N
-body particles, halos, or galaxies on
2048^{3}
grids using
the cloud-in-cells (CIC) method
(
Hockney and Eastwood 1981
)
to obtain the density fields of matter, halo or galaxy. After performing the Fourier transform,
we correct for the window function of CIC following the method described in Ref.
Jing 2005
.
We will show the results at wavenumbers smaller than the Nyquist frequency,
k=3.2~h_{f}{\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}}
.
Furthermore, to evaluate the power spectrum at
k\geq 1.6~h_{f}{\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}}
accurately, we fold the particle positions
into a smaller box by replacing
\mathbb{x}\rightarrow\mathbb{x}\%(L/2)
, where the operation
a\%b
stands for
the remainder of the division of
a
by
b
. This procedure leads to effectively
2
times higher resolution.
For the halo-auto and galaxy-auto power spectrum, we subtract the shot noise from the measurements,
where we simply assume the shot noise to be given by the number density of the tracers.

Since we use the fixed box size and the same particle number, we
use the same
k
binning to estimate the average of
|\delta_{\bf k}|^{2}
in each
k
bin to estimate the band power.
We then use the two-side numerical derivative method to compute the power spectrum responses.
We compute the growth response as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\ln P(k,z)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},\rm sim}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln P_{+}(k;\delta_{\rm{b}0}=+\epsilon)-\ln P_{-}(k;\delta_{\rm{b}0}=-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon\times D(z)}

(30)

and compute the total response as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial P(k,z)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm total,sim}=(n-1)P(k,\delta_{\rm b0}=0)

\displaystyle+\frac{P_{+}((1-\delta_{\rm b}/3)k;\delta_{\rm{b}0}=+\epsilon)-P_{-}((1+\delta_{\rm b}/3)k;\delta_{\rm{b}0}=-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon\times D(z)},

(31)

where
\delta_{\rm{b}0}\equiv\delta_{\rm b}(z_{f}=0)
and
\epsilon=0.01
for the
\Omega_{K}
-
\Lambda
CDM
1 simulations.
To reduce statistical stochasticity (or sample variance), we employ the same initial seeds as those for the “fiducial” model. The column “
N_{\rm real}
”
in Table
1
denotes the number of realizations for paired simulations, where each pair uses the same initial seeds.
For
\Omega_{K}
-
\Lambda
CDM
1 and
h
-
\Lambda
CDM models, we run 8 paired simulations to estimate the statistical scatters.

Note that halos are selected using the abundance matching method, where the same number of halos, selected in the ascending order of masses, are identified
in the two simulations when computing the responses.

In Fig.

Again it is clear that the approximate identities

Since the pairs of SU simulations (“

We showed the results for only one case of the number density selected halo sample,

As discussed in Ref.
Terasawa et al. 2022

T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)
and

If we assume the universal halo mass function, which depends on cosmology only through

P_{L}(k)

Hence, we can expect the responses
T(k)

expected to be constant.
On the other hand, the responses in the nonlinear regime could be different due to the difference in the growth history of the structure
(
Terasawa et al. 2022

(
Li et al. 2014b

For
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)
and
T_{h}(k)

VI
conclusion

In this paper, we have developed an approximate method to model the halo-matter and halo-auto power spectra for the nonflat
\Lambda
CDM
model, from quantities representing the nonlinear evolution of the corresponding flat
\Lambda
CDM
model, based on the SU method.
The key points to build the estimator are the correspondence between the nonflat and flat universes through the SU picture and the equivalence of the growth responses to long-wavelength modes and the Hubble parameter.
This work is a sequel of our previous research
Terasawa et al. 2022
, in which we proposed the approximate method for predicting the nonlinear matter power spectrum for nonflat
\Lambda
CDM
model.

The estimator of the halo-matter (halo-auto) power spectrum has
\sim 1\%
(
\sim 2\%
) accuracy even for large curvature model with
\Omega_{K}=\pm 0.1
.
Using the estimator we can extend the existing emulators to predict the nonlinear power spectra for
nonflat universe without degrading its accuracy. In particular, we showed we can extend the
Dark Emulator
to predict the galaxy-matter and galaxy-auto power spectra for a
non-zero curvature model.

The response
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)
is also a key quantity for estimating SSC. We utilized the approximate identity
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}(k)\approx T_{h}(k)
and proposed the calculation of the total response (SSC terms) using

Halofit
or
Dark Emulator
.
We showed that our method can predict the total response at an accuracy
better than the analytical methods used in the literature such as the perturbation theory and the halo model, thanks to capturing the nonlinear response through
T_{h}(k)
.
Our method for computing the total response would be implemented into the Core Cosmology Library
(
Chisari et al. 2019
)
.

Although we assume
\Lambda
CDM
model in this paper, we expect our approach to approximate the response to
\delta_{\rm b}
is also applicable to
w
CDM model, where dark energy is not the cosmological constant. Once validated with numerical simulations, we can ease the computational cost of the SU simulations by substituting it with the response to another parameter which can be evaluated fast.
In this paper, we focused on the responses to
\delta_{\rm b}
and
h
, but we can think of other parameters that share the almost same response in wider cosmological parameter space, which can be used to extend the simulation-based theory predictions and to ease the computational cost for building such models.

Appendix A
Calculation methods of the response functions of halo
and galaxy power spectra

A.1
The response of galaxy-matter power spectrum

We define the cumulative number density of halos as

\displaystyle n(>M_{\rm th};\delta_{\rm b})\equiv\int_{\rm M_{th}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{M}n_{\ln M}(M;\delta_{\rm b}),

(32)

where
n_{\ln M}\equiv\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}\ln M
.
We define the following dimensionless quantities:

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M_{\mathrm{th}})\equiv n(>M_{\mathrm{th}})P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M_{\mathrm{th}}),

(33)

for mass-threshold samples (above mass
M_{\mathrm{th}}
) and

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;M_{-},M_{+})\equiv n(M_{-},M_{+})P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;M_{-},M_{+}),

(34)

for mass bin samples in the mass range of
[M_{-},M_{+}]
, respectively.
Using these quantities, all the equations for
P_{\mathrm{gm}}
and its response can be
simplified. The idea behind this is as follows. First, mass-threshold halo samples are easier to analyze in simulations. Once the halo-matter power spectrum for various different mass thresholds is known, one can convert them to that for mass-bin samples by taking a derivative w.r.t. the mass threshold. Numerically, this can be done by taking the finite difference:

P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;M)\simeq\dfrac{n(>M_{-})P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M_{-})-n(>M_{+})P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M_{+})}{n(>M_{-})-n(>M_{+})},

(35)

where
M_{\pm}=M\pm\epsilon_{M}
with some small

\epsilon_{M}
compared to
M_{\pm}
.
In the above, note that the halo-matter power spectrum is a halo number-weighted quantity, and thus
n
must be considered appropriately. This equation is actually implemented in
Dark Emulator
. Now, using
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}
, this relation is simplified as

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;M_{-},M_{+})=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M_{-})-\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M_{+}).

(36)

We can use a similar trick for the galaxy-matter power spectrum. Assuming a halo occupation distribution model, we can have

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{gm}}(k)=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{\mathrm{g}}}\int\mathrm{d}M\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;M),

(37)

where
\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)
describes the Fourier transform of the average radial profile of galaxy number density
in host halos
with mass
M
, which can be computed by taking the product of
the halo occupation
distribution
N_{\mathrm{g}}(M)
and the radial profile of each galaxy.

This can be evaluated by substituting Eq. (
35
). Now, multiplying both sides of Eq. (
37
) by
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{g}}
and defining
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{gm}}=\bar{n}_{\mathrm{g}}P_{\mathrm{gm}}
, one can compute

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{gm}}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}M\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}M}\Bigl(-\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)\Bigr)

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)\Bigr|_{0}^{\infty}+\int\mathrm{d}M\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}M\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M).

In the above, we have performed an integration by parts to obtain the second line and then use the fact that
N_{\mathrm{g}}(M)
tends to zero at the low mass end and
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(M)
tends to zero at the high mass end to reach the final line.
This is how we can avoid estimating the power spectrum for the mass-bin samples and directly compute the galaxy-matter power spectrum from the mass-threshold halo samples.

The responses are also related by a similar equation as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{gm}}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\mathrm{b}}}\right|_{\rm G}=\int\mathrm{d}M\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)}{\partial\delta_{\mathrm{b}}}\right|_{{\rm G},M}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{\mathrm{b}}}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right|_{\rm G}\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)\right],

(39)

where the subscript
M
denotes the derivative with fixed mass threshold. Assuming that the cosmological dependence is fully encoded in
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{gm}}
and that the galaxy profile around halos
in physical scale and the mean HOD are given irrespective of the cosmological model, which is partly validated in Ref
(
Voivodic and Barreira 2021
)
, one can trivially derive

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{\mathrm{b}}}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right|_{\rm G}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}.

(40)

Finally, one can convert this to the standard response function by

\displaystyle\left.\dfrac{\partial P_{\mathrm{gm}}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\mathrm{b}}}\right|_{\rm G}=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{\mathrm{g}}}\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{gm}}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\mathrm{b}}}\right|_{\rm G}-b_{\mathrm{g}}^{\mathrm{L}}P_{\mathrm{gm}}(k),

(41)

where
b_{g}^{L}
is the Lagrangian galaxy bias, related to the Eulerian bias as
b_{g}=b_{g}^{L}+1
.
We can give a physical interpretation of the bias factor as follows. The Lagrangian galaxy bias,
b_{g}^{L}
, describes the change in the number density of galaxies at a fixed Lagrangian, or, equivalently,
local
comoving
volume in the SU picture, and the difference between the Eulerian and the Lagrangian bias,
b_{g}-b_{g}^{L}=1
represents the contribution from the change in the physical volume, i.e., equivalent to the dilation effect. Note that the second term in Eq.
41
,
-b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gm}(k)
comes from the response of
\bar{n}_{g}
and since the growth response should be evaluated at a fixed comoving volume, the response of
\bar{n}_{g}
gives
b_{g}^{L}
instead of
b_{g}
.

A.2
The response of galaxy auto-power spectrum

Similarly, we can also evaluate the galaxy-auto power spectrum using mass-threshold quantities.
First, the halo-auto power spectrum of the mass-bin samples can be calculated by taking the finite difference:

\displaystyle P_{\rm hh}(k;M_{1},M_{2})=\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial M\partial M^{\prime}}\left.\left[n(>M)n(>M^{\prime})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})\right]\right|_{M=M_{1},M^{\prime}=M_{2}}}{\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M_{1})\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M_{2})}.

(42)

Following the halo model description, we split the galaxy power spectrum into the 2-halo and 1-halo terms as
P_{\rm gg}(k)=P_{\rm gg}^{2h}(k)+P_{\rm gg}^{1h}(k)
.
Using Eq.
42
, we can write the 2-halo term of the galaxy power spectrum as

\displaystyle P_{\rm gg}^{2h}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle\int\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}}(M^{\prime})\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M^{\prime})\rangle P_{\rm hh}(k;M,M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}n(>M)\int\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M^{\prime})\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}}n(>M^{\prime})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\iint\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M^{\prime})}{\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime}),

(43)

where we did integral by parts and ignored the surface terms, and
we define

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})

\displaystyle\equiv n(>M_{\rm th})n(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th}).

(44)

The SU growth responses of the 2-halo term of the galaxy-auto power spectrum can be calculated as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial P_{\rm gg}^{2h}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\iint\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M^{\prime})}{\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M,M^{\prime}}\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~+\left.\left.2\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right|_{\rm G}{\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})}\right]-2b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gg}^{2h}(k).

(45)

For the 1-halo term, we can compute the SU growth response as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial P_{\rm gg}^{1h}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}M}(M)\left[b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(M)\langle\tilde{U}_{g}^{2}(k;M)\rangle+\left.\frac{\partial\langle\tilde{U}_{g}^{2}(k;M)\rangle}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}\right]-2b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gg}^{1h}(k).

(46)

Note that the response of the 1-halo term in our approach is identical to that of the halo model description
(
Krause and Eifler 2017
;
Nicola et al. 2020
, e.g.)
.

A.3
The response calibration of halo and galaxy power spectra
using the abundance matching method

In this subsection, we describe the relation between the SU growth response and the number density fixed or abundance matched response which we clarify later.
We denote the derivative performed keeping the comoving halo number density fixed
as “AM” response, which stands for Abundance Matching method.
When evaluating the AM response,
we change the threshold
M_{\rm th}(\delta_{\rm b})
so that the cumulative halo number density in the comoving volume is kept fixed when varying
\delta_{\rm b}

(
Li et al. 2016
)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}n(>M_{\rm th};\delta_{\rm b})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}=0.

(47)

We define the mass threshold shift
s(M)
to keep the number density as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ln M_{\rm th}\equiv s(M_{\rm th})\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}.

(48)

We can calculate Eq. (
47
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}n(>M_{\rm th};\delta_{\rm b})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\int_{\rm M_{th}(\delta_{\rm b})}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{M}n_{\ln M}(M;\delta_{\rm b})

\displaystyle=\int_{\rm M_{th}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{M}\frac{\partial n_{\ln M}(M;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\left[F_{1}(M=\infty)-F_{1}(M=M_{\rm th})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\rm M_{th}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{M}\frac{\partial n_{\ln M}(M;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}-n_{\ln M}(M_{\rm th})s(M_{\rm th})

\displaystyle=0,

(49)

where we defined
F_{1}(M)
as the function satisfying
\partial F_{1}(M)/\partial\ln M=n_{\ln M}(M)

and we can calculate its response as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{1}(M)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F_{1}(M)}{\partial\ln M}\frac{\partial\ln M}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=n_{\ln M}(M)s(M).

(50)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\rm M_{th}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{M}\frac{\partial n_{\ln M}(M;\delta_{\rm b})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}=n_{\ln M}(M_{\rm th})s(M_{\rm th}).

(51)

Using this, the Lagrangian halo bias above the mass threshold is given as
(
Li et al. 2016
)

\displaystyle b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M_{\rm th})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{n(>M_{\rm th})}\int_{M_{\rm th}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{M}b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}n_{\ln M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(>M_{\rm th})}\int_{M_{\rm th}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{M}\frac{\partial\ln n_{\ln M}}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}n_{\ln M}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{\ln M}(M_{\rm th})s(M_{\rm th})}{n(>M_{\rm th})}.

(52)

Likewise, the AM response of

\mathscr{P}_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\int_{\rm M_{th}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ln Mn_{\ln M}(M)P_{\rm hm}(k;M)

\displaystyle=\int_{\rm M_{th}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ln M\frac{\partial~}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\left[n_{\ln M}(M)P_{\rm hm}(k;M)\right]+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\left[F_{2}(M=\infty)-F_{2}(M=M_{\rm th})\right]

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M_{\rm th}}-n_{\ln M}(M_{\rm th})P_{\rm hm}(k;M_{\rm th})s(M_{\rm th})

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M_{\rm th}}-b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M_{\rm th})n(>M_{\rm th})P_{\rm hm}(k;M_{\rm th}),

(53)

where
F_{2}(M)
is a function satisfying
\partial F_{2}(M)/\partial\ln M=n_{\ln M}(M)P_{\rm hm}(k;M)
.
On the other hand, the l.h.s. of Eq. (
53
) can be written as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=n(>M_{\rm th})\left.\frac{\partial P_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=n(>M_{\rm th})\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)P_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})\,T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})

\displaystyle\simeq n(>M_{\rm th})\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)P_{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})\,T_{h}^{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th}),

(54)

where we used the approximate relation
T_{h}^{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})\approx T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm hm}(k;>M_{\rm th})
in the last equation.
Hence, we can calculate the

P_{\rm gm}(k)
SU-growth response using
T_{h}(k;>M_{\rm th})
as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial P_{\rm gm}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{gm}}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}-b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gm}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}}\int\mathrm{d}M\left\{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M}+\frac{1}{3}\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right\}-b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gm}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}}\int\mathrm{d}M\left\{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}n(>M)\left[b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M)P_{\rm hm}(k;M)+\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)T_{h}^{\rm hm}(k;>M)P_{\rm hm}(k;>M)\right]\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~+\left.\frac{1}{3}P_{\mathrm{hm}}(k;>M)\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right\}-b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gm}(k).

(55)

The AM response of
\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})
is calculated as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M_{\rm th},M^{\prime}_{\rm th}}+\int_{M_{\rm th}}\mathrm{d}\ln Mn_{\ln M}(M)\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\left[F_{3}(M^{\prime}=\infty)-F_{3}(M^{\prime}=M^{\prime}_{\rm th})\right]\right\}

\displaystyle~~~~~+\int_{M^{\prime}_{\rm th}}\mathrm{d}\ln M^{\prime}n_{\ln M}(M^{\prime})\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\left[F_{3}(M=\infty)-F_{3}(M=M_{\rm th})\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M_{\rm th},M^{\prime}_{\rm th}}-b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})n(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})\int_{M_{\rm th}}\mathrm{d}\ln Mn_{\ln M}(M)P_{\rm hh}(k;M,M^{\prime}_{\rm th})

\displaystyle~~~~~-b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M_{\rm th})n(>M_{\rm th})\int_{M^{\prime}_{\rm th}}\mathrm{d}\ln M^{\prime}n_{\ln M}(M^{\prime})P_{\rm hh}(k;M^{\prime},M_{\rm th})

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M_{\rm th},M^{\prime}_{\rm th}}-n(>M_{\rm th})n(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})

\displaystyle~~~~~\times\left[b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},M^{\prime}_{\rm th})+b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M_{\rm th})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M^{\prime}_{\rm th},M_{\rm th})\right],

(56)

where
F_{3}(M)
satisfying

\partial F_{3}(M)/\partial\ln M=n_{\ln M}(M)P_{\rm hh}(k;M,M^{\prime})
.
Since the l.h.s. of Eq. (
56
) can be written using the approximate relation
T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})\approx T_{h}^{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})
as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=n(>M_{\rm th})n(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})}{\mathrm{d}\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=n(>M_{\rm th})n(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)T_{\delta_{\rm b}}^{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})

\displaystyle\simeq n(>M_{\rm th})n(>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)T_{h}^{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th}).

(57)

The SU-growth response of

P_{\rm gg}^{2h}
can be related to
T_{h}^{\rm hh}(k;>M_{\rm th},>M^{\prime}_{\rm th})
as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial P_{\rm gg}^{2h}(k)}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{\rm G}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\iint\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M^{\prime})}{\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})}{\partial\delta_{\rm b}}\right|_{{\rm G},M,M^{\prime}}\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~\left.+\frac{2}{3}{\mathscr{P}_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right]-2b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gg}^{2h}(k)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{g}^{2}}\iint\mathrm{d}M\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M^{\prime})}{\mathrm{d}M^{\prime}}\right.n(>M)n(>M^{\prime})\left[2b_{\mathrm{h},1}^{L}(>M^{\prime})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,M^{\prime})\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~+\left.\left.\left(\frac{26}{21}\right)T_{h}^{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})+\frac{2}{3}{P_{\rm hh}(k;>M,>M^{\prime})}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ln k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\langle\tilde{U}_{g}(k;M)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}M}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle~~~~~-2b_{g}^{L}P_{\rm gg}^{2h}(k).

(58)
