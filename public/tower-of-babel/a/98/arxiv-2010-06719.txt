Convergent series of integers with missing digits
Melvyn B. Nathanson

Abstract
. A classical theorem of Kempner states that the sum of the reciprocals of positive integers with missing decimal digits converges. This result is extended to much larger families of “missing digits” sets of positive integers with convergent harmonic series.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
11A63, 11B05, 11B75, 11K16.

1.
Kempner’s theorem for
g
-adic repesentations

A
harmonic series
is a series of the form
\sum_{a\in A}1/a
,
where
A
is a set of positive integers.
For example, let
A_{10,9}
be the set of positive integers
in which the digit
9
does not occur in the usual decimal representation.
Kempner
[
6
]
proved in 1914 that the harmonic series

\sum_{a\in A_{10,9}}1/a
converges.
He called this “a curious convergent series.”
More generally, for every integer
g\geq 2
, every positive integer
n

has a unique
g
-adic representation
of the form
n=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}g^{i}
,
with digits
c_{i}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,g-1\}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,m-1
and
c_{m-1}\neq 0
.
If
A_{g,c}
is the set of integers whose
g
-adic representation contains no digit
c
, then
the infinite series
\sum_{a\in A_{g,c}}1/a
converges.
This is Theorem 144 in Hardy and Wright
[
4
]
.

Kempner’s theorem has been studied and extended by
Baillie
[
1
]
, Farhi
[
2
]
, Gordon
[
3
]
, Irwin
[
5
]
,
Nathanson
[
10
]
,
Schmelzer and Baillie
[
12
]
, and Wadhwa
[
13
,
14
]
.

Lubeck and Ponomarenko
[
7
]
used a probability argument to obtain
a remarkably strong form of Kempner’s theorem.

Theorem 1

(Lubeck and Ponomarenko)
.

Let
0<\lambda<1
.
Let
A^{(\lambda)}_{m}
be the set of integers
n\in[10^{m-1},10^{m}-1]

such that at most
\lambda m

digits of
n
are equal to 9, and let
A_{10,9}^{(\lambda)}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}A^{(\lambda)}_{m}
.
If
\lambda<1/10
, then the harmonic series
\sum_{a\in A_{10,9}^{(\lambda)}}1/a
converges.
If
\lambda\geq 1/10
, then the harmonic series
\sum_{a\in A_{10,9}^{(\lambda)}}1/a
diverges.

This result extends to
g
-adic representations for all
g\geq 2
.

The
g
-adic representation is a special case of a more general method to represent the positive integers.
A
\mathcal{G}
-adic sequence
is a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers
\mathcal{G}=(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}

such that
g_{0}=1
and
g_{i}
divides
g_{i+1}
for all
i\geq 0
.
The integer

d_{i}=\frac{g_{i+1}}{g_{i}}

satisfies
d_{i}\geq 2
and

(1)

g_{k+1}=g_{k}d_{k}=d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}\cdots d_{k-1}d_{k}

for all
k\geq 0
.
Every positive integer
n
has a unique representation in the form

(2)

n=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}g_{i}

where
c_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,d_{i}-1\}
for all
i=0,1,\ldots,m-1
and
c_{m-1}\neq 0
.
We call (
2
) the
\mathcal{G}
-adic representation
of
n
.
This is equivalent to deBruijn’s additive system (Nathanson
[
8
,
9
]
).

Harmonic series constructed from sets of positive integers
with missing
\mathcal{G}
-adic digits do not necessarily converge.
The following result constructs sets of integers
with missing
\mathcal{G}
-adic digits whose harmonic series do converge,
and also sets of integers
with missing
\mathcal{G}
-adic digits whose harmonic series do not diverge.

The
\mathcal{G}
-adic sequence
\mathcal{G}=(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
has

quotients bounded by
d
if

(3)

d_{i}=\frac{g_{i+1}}{g_{i}}\leq d\qquad\text{for all $i=0,1,2,\ldots$.}

Nathanson
[
11
]
obtained the following criterion for the convergence and divergence
of harmonic series of positive integers with missing
\mathcal{G}
-adic digits.

Theorem 2

(Nathanson)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a
\mathcal{G}
-adic sequence with quotients bounded by
d
.
Let
I
be a set of nonnegative integers, and
let
I(k)
be the number of
i\in I
with
i\leq k
.
For all
i\in I
, let
U_{i}
be a nonempty proper subset of
[0,d_{i}-1]
.

Let
n=\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}g_{i}
be the
\mathcal{G}
-adic representation of the positive integer
n
,
and let
A
be the set of positive integers
n
such that

c_{i}\in[0,d_{i}-1]\setminus U_{i}
for all
i\in I
.
If

(4)

I(k)\geq\frac{(1+\delta)\log k}{\log(d/(d-1))}

for some
\delta>0
and all
k\geq k_{0}(\delta)
,
then the harmonic series
\sum_{a\in A}1/a
converges.
If

(5)

I(k)\leq\frac{(1-\delta)\log k}{\log d}

for some
\delta>0
and all
k\geq k_{1}(\delta)
,
then the harmonic series
\sum_{a\in A}1/a
diverges.

In this paper we show that the Lubeck-Ponomarenko theorem extends to
\mathcal{G}
-adic representations.
Moreover, it can also be significantly strengthened, even for the usual
g
-adic representations.

2.
Harmonic series for
\mathcal{G}
-adic repesentations

We use a simple form of Chernoff’s inequality in probability theory.

Theorem 3

(Chernoff)
.

Let
p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{m-1}\in[0,1]
and let
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}

be independent Bernoulli random variables with

\Prob(X_{i}=1)=p_{i}\qqand\Prob(X_{i}=0)=1-p_{i}

for
i=0,1,\ldots,m-1
. Let

X=X_{0}+X_{1}+\cdots+X_{m-1}

and

\mu=\mathbf{E}(X)=p_{0}+p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m-1}.

For all
0<\delta<1
,

\Prob(X\leq(1-\delta)\mu)<e^{-\mu\delta^{2}/2}.

The short proof is in the Appendix.

We denote the cardinality of the set
S
by
\card(S)
or by
|S|
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a
\mathcal{G}
-adic sequence
with quotients bounded by
d
, and let

(6)

0\leq\lambda<\frac{1}{d}.

Let
U_{i}
be a proper nonempty subset of
[0,d_{i}-1]
for all
i=0,1,2,\ldots
,
and let
\mathcal{U}=(U_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
.
Let
A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}
be the set of positive integers
n
such that,
if
n\in[g_{m-1},g_{m}-1]
has the
\mathcal{G}
-adic representation

n=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}g_{i}

then

\card\{i\in[0,m-1]:c_{i}\in U_{i}\}\leq\lambda m.

There exists
\sigma<1
such that

\sum_{a\in A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}}\frac{1}{a^{\sigma}}<\infty.

Proof.

Let

I_{m}=[g_{m-1},g_{m}-1]\cap\mathbf{Z}\qqand J_{m}=[0,g_{m}-1]\cap\mathbf{Z}.

We have
|I_{m}|=g_{m}-g_{m-1}
and
|J_{m}|=g_{m}
.
Every integer
n\in J_{m}
has a unique
\mathcal{G}
-adic representation

n=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}g_{i}

with
c_{i}\in[0,d_{i}-1]
for all
i=0,1,\ldots,m-1
.
Note that
I_{m}\subseteq J_{m}
and
n\in J_{m}\setminus I_{m}
if and only if
c_{m-1}=0
.

Define the uniform probability measure on the set
J_{m}
:

\Pr(n)=\frac{1}{|J_{m}|}=\frac{1}{g_{m}}\qquad\text{for all $n\in J_{m}$.}

Define the random variable
X:J_{m}\rightarrow[0,m]
as follows.
For
n=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}g_{i}\in J_{m}
, let

(7)

X(n)=\card\{i\in[0,m-1]:c_{i}\in U_{i}\}.

We have

\Pr\left(X\leq\lambda m\right)=\frac{\left|A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}\cap J_{m}\right|}{|J_{m}|}

and so

(8)

\left|A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}\cap I_{m}\right|\leq\left|A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}\cap J_{m}\right|=\Pr\left(X\leq\lambda m\right)g_{m}.

For all
i=0,1,2,\ldots,m-1
, define the Bernoulli random variable
X_{i}:J_{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
by

(9)

X_{i}(n)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $c_{i}\in U_{i}$}\\
0&\text{if $c_{i}\notin U_{i}$.}\end{cases}

We have

\Pr(X_{i}=1)=\frac{|U_{i}|}{d_{i}}

and

\Pr(X_{i}=0)=1-\frac{|U_{i}|}{d_{i}}

and so
X_{i}
has the expected value

\mathbf{E}(X_{i})=\frac{|U_{i}|}{d_{i}}.

Choose
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
.
Recall that

|J_{m}|=g_{m}=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}d_{i}.

We have

\displaystyle\Pr(X_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\text{ for all }i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|J_{m}|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
\varepsilon_{i}=1\end{subarray}}^{m-1}|U_{i}|\ \prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
\varepsilon_{i}=0\end{subarray}}^{m-1}\left(d_{i}-|U_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
\varepsilon_{i}=1\end{subarray}}^{m-1}\frac{|U_{i}|}{d_{i}}\ \prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
\varepsilon_{i}=0\end{subarray}}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{|U_{i}|}{d_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{{i=0}}^{m-1}\Pr\left(X_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\right)

and so
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}
are independent random variables.

For
m=1,2,3,\ldots
, define
d_{m}^{*}
by

\frac{1}{d_{m}^{*}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{|U_{i}|}{d_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{d}=\frac{1}{d}.

It follows that
0<d^{*}_{m}\leq d
.
From (
7
) and (
9
) we obtain

X=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}X_{i}

and so

\mu=\mathbf{E}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{E}(X_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{|U_{i}|}{d_{i}}=\frac{m}{d^{*}_{m}}\geq\frac{m}{d}.

Because
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}
are independent Bernoulli random variables,
Chernoff’s inequality will give an upper bound for
\Pr(X\leq\lambda m)
.
From (
6
), we have

0<\lambda d_{m}^{*}\leq\lambda d<1

and so

0<1-\lambda d_{m}^{*}=\delta<1.

Thus,

\lambda m=\lambda d_{m}^{*}\mu=(1-\delta)\mu

and

\displaystyle\Pr(X\leq\lambda m)

\displaystyle=\Pr(X\leq(1-\delta)\mu)

\displaystyle<e^{-\mu\delta^{2}/2}=e^{-(1-\lambda d_{m}^{*})^{2}m/2d_{m}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(1-\lambda d)^{2}m/2d}.

Inequality (
8
) gives

(10)

\left|A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}\cap I_{m}\right|\leq\Pr\left(X\leq\lambda m\right)g_{m}\leq e^{-(1-\lambda d)^{2}m/2d}g_{m}.

Choose
\sigma\in(0,1)
such that

(11)

1<d^{1-\sigma}<e^{(1-\lambda d)^{2}/2d}.

Because
\mathcal{G}
has quotients bounded by
d
, we have

\frac{g_{m}}{d}\leq\frac{g_{m}}{d_{m-1}}=g_{m-1}=\prod_{i=0}^{m-2}d_{i}\leq d^{m-1}.

Also,
a\geq g_{m-1}
for all
a\in I_{m}
.
Applying (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}}\frac{1}{a^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{a\in A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}\cap I_{m}}\frac{1}{a^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{|A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}\cap I_{m}|}{g_{m-1}^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-(1-\lambda d)^{2}m/2d}g_{m}}{g_{m-1}^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-(1-\lambda d)^{2}m/2d}g_{m-1}}{g_{m-1}^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=d\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{-(1-\lambda d)^{2}m/2d}g_{m-1}^{1-\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{-(1-\lambda d)^{2}m/2d}d^{(m-1)(1-\sigma)}

\displaystyle=d^{\sigma}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{d^{1-\sigma}}{e^{(1-\lambda d)^{2}/2d}}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle<\infty

by inequality (
11
). This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}
be the set of positive integers constructed
in Theorem
4
. The Dirichlet series

F_{A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}}(s)=\sum_{a\in A_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U}}^{(\lambda)}}\frac{1}{a^{s}}

has abscissa of convergence

\sigma_{c}\leq 1-\frac{(1-\lambda d)^{2}}{2d\log d}.

Note that, in the
g
-adic case, Theorem
1

asserts only that
\sigma_{c}\leq 1
.

Appendix: Proof of Chernoff’s inequality

Let
X
be a nonnegative random variable on a countable probability space
\Omega
.
For all
b>0
, we have Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}X(\omega)\Prob(\omega)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\Omega\\
X(\omega)\geq b\end{subarray}}X(\omega)\Prob(\omega)

\displaystyle\geq b\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\Omega\\
X(\omega)\geq b\end{subarray}}\Prob(\omega)=b\Prob(X\geq b)

and so

(12)

\Prob(X\geq b)\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}(X)}{b}.

For
s>0
, the function
y=e^{-sx}
is decreasing and positive, and so

X(\omega)\leq a
if and only if
e^{-sX(\omega)}\geq e^{-sa}
.
Applying (
12
) to the random variable
e^{-sX}

with
b=e^{-sa}
, we obtain

(13)

\Prob(X\leq a)=\Prob\left(e^{-sX}\geq e^{-sa}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-sX}\right]}{e^{-sa}}.

Let
X_{i}
be a Bernoulli random variable with

\Prob(X_{i}=1)=p_{i}\qqand\Prob(X_{i}=0)=1-p_{i}

Using the inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
, we have

\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-sX_{i}}\right]=e^{-s}p_{i}+e^{0}(1-p_{i})=1+\left(e^{-s}-1\right)p_{i}\leq e^{\left(e^{-s}-1\right)p_{i}}.

Let

X=X_{0}+X_{1}+\cdots+X_{m-1}

and

\mu=\mathbf{E}(X)=p_{0}+p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m-1}.

Recall that if
Y_{0},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m-1}
are independent random variables, then

\mathbf{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}Y_{i}\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\mathbf{E}\left[Y_{i}\right].

The random variables
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}
are independent,
and so the random variables
e^{-sX_{0}},e^{-sX_{1}},\ldots,e^{-sX_{m-1}}
are independent. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-sX}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-s\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}X_{i}}\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}e^{-sX_{i}}\right]=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-sX_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}e^{\left(e^{-s}-1\right)p_{i}}=e^{\left(e^{-s}-1\right)\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}p_{i}}=e^{\left(e^{-s}-1\right)\mu}.

Consider the function

h(\delta)=(1-\delta)\log(1-\delta)+\delta-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.

We have
h(0)=0
and, for
0<\delta<1
,

h^{\prime}(\delta)=-\log(1-\delta)-\delta>0.

It follows that
h(\delta)>0
. Equivalently,

(14)

-\delta-(1-\delta)\log(1-\delta)<-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}

Let
s>0
and
0<\delta<1
.
Inequality (
13
) with
a=(1-\delta)\mu
gives

\displaystyle\Prob(X\leq(1-\delta)\mu)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-sX}\right]}{e^{-s(1-\delta)\mu}}\leq\frac{e^{\left(e^{-s}-1\right)\mu}}{e^{-s(1-\delta)\mu}}

\displaystyle=e^{\left(e^{-s}-1+s(1-\delta)\right)\mu}.

We minimize
e^{-s}-1+s(1-\delta)
by choosing
s=-\log(1-\delta)
.
Applying inequality (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-s}-1+s(1-\delta)

\displaystyle=-\delta-(1-\delta)\log(1-\delta)<-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}

and so

\Prob(X\leq(1-\delta)\mu)<e^{-\mu\delta^{2}/2}.

This completes the proof.
