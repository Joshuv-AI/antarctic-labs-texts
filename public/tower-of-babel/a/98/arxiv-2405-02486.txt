Concurrent Stochastic Games with Stateful-discounted and Parity Objectives: Complexity and Algorithms
Ali Asadi, Krishnendu Chatterjee, Raimundo Saona, Jakub Svoboda

Abstract
We study two-player zero-sum concurrent stochastic games with finite state and action space played for an infinite number of steps. In every step, the two players simultaneously and independently choose an action. Given the current state and the chosen actions, the next state is obtained according to a stochastic transition function. An objective is a measurable function on plays (or infinite trajectories) of the game, and the value for an objective is the maximal expectation that the player can guarantee against the adversarial player. We consider: (a) stateful-discounted objectives, which are similar to the classic discounted-sum objectives, but states are associated with different discount factors rather than a single discount factor; and (b) parity objectives, which are a canonical representation for ω \omega -regular objectives. For stateful-discounted objectives, given an ordering of the discount factors, the limit value is the limit of the value of the stateful-discounted objectives, as the discount factors approach zero according to the given order. The computational problem we consider is the approximation of the value within an arbitrary additive error. The above problem is known to be in EXPSPACE for the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives and in PSPACE for parity objectives. The best-known algorithms for both the above problems are at least exponential time, with an exponential dependence on the number of states and actions. Our main results for the value approximation problem for the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives and parity objectives are as follows: (a) we establish TFNP[NP] complexity; and (b) we present algorithms that improve the dependency on the number of actions in the exponent from linear to logarithmic. In particular, if the number of states is constant, our algorithms run in polynomial time.

Introduction

In this work, we present improved complexity results and algorithms
for the value approximation of concurrent stochastic games with two
classic objectives.
Below we present the model of concurrent stochastic games, the relevant objectives,
the computational problems, previous results, and finally our contributions.

Concurrent stochastic games.

Concurrent stochastic games are two-player zero-sum games played on finite-state graphs
for an infinite number of steps.
These games were introduced in the seminal work of Shapley
[
31
]
and are a fundamental model in game theory.
In each step, both players simultaneously and independently of the other player choose an action.
Given the current state and the chosen actions, the next state is obtained according to a stochastic
transition function.
An infinite number of such steps results in a play which is an infinite sequence of states and actions.
Concurrent stochastic games have been widely studied in the literature from the mathematical perspective
[
31
,
17
,
18
,
28
]
, and from the algorithmic and computational complexity perspective, including: complexity for reachability objectives
[
11
,
16
,
19
,
25
]
, algorithms for limit-average objectives
[
24
,
29
]
, complexity for qualitative solutions for omega-regular objectives
[
10
]
, complexity for quantitative solutions for omega-regular objectives
[
8
,
15
]
, and in the context of temporal logic
[
1
]
.
In particular, in the analysis of reactive systems, concurrent games provide the appropriate model for reactive systems with components that interact synchronously
[
1
,
13
,
14
]
.

Objectives.

An objective is a measurable function that assigns to every play a real-valued reward.
The classic discounted-sum objective is as follows: every transition is assigned
a reward and the objective assigns to a play the discounted-sum of the rewards.
While the classic objective has a single discount factor, the stateful-discounted objective has multiple discount factors.
In the stateful-discounted objective, each state is associated with a discount factor, and, in the objective, the discount at a step depends on the current state.
We also consider the boolean parity objectives, which are a canonical form to express all
\omega
-regular objectives
[
34
]
, which provide a robust specification for all properties that arise in verification.
For example, all LTL formulas can be converted to deterministic parity automata.
In parity objectives, every state is associated with an integer priority, and a play is winning for (or satisfies) the objective if the minimum priority visited infinitely often is even.

Strategies and values.

Strategies are recipes that define the choice of actions of the players.
They are functions that, given a game history, return a distribution over actions.
Given a concurrent stochastic game and an objective, the value of Player 1 at a state is the maximal expectation that the player can guarantee for the objective against all strategies of Player 2.
For stateful-discounted objectives, given an ordering of the discount factors, the limit value at a state is the limit of the value function for the discounted objective as the discount factors approach zero in the given order.

Computational problems.

Given a concurrent stochastic game, the main computational problems are:
(a) the
value-decision
problem, given a state and a threshold
\alpha
, asks whether the value at the state is at least
\alpha
;
and (b) the
value-approximation
problem, given a state and an error
\varepsilon>0
, asks to compute an approximation of the value for the state within an additive error of
\varepsilon
.
We consider the above problems for the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives and the value for parity objectives.

Motivation.

The motivation to study the limit of the stateful-discounted objective is as follows.
First, this limit generalizes the classic limit-average objectives.
Second, it characterizes the value for the parity objectives in concurrent stochastic games
[
21
,
12
]
, where the order of limit corresponds to the order of importance of priorities in parity objectives.
Third, the limit value has been shown to correspond to the value for other objectives such as priority mean-payoff for various subclasses of concurrent stochastic games
[
22
]
.
The motivation to study the value-approximation problem as opposed to the value-decision is that for concurrent games, even for special classes of objectives such as reachability and safety, values can be irrational, and the decision problem related to exact value is SQRT-SUM hard
[
16
]
as explained below.
Hence, approximation of values is a natural problem to study from an algorithmic and computational complexity perspective.

Previous results.

For a single discount factor, the limit value corresponds to the value of the well-studied mean-payoff or long-run average objectives
[
28
]
, and, for parity objectives, the computational problems admit a linear reduction to the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives
[
21
,
12
]
.
The value-decision problem for concurrent stochastic games is SQRT-SUM hard
[
16
]
:
this result holds for reachability objectives, and hence also for parity objectives and the limit value for even a single discount factor.
The SQRT-SUM problem is a classic problem in computational geometry, and whether SQRT-SUM belongs to NP has been a long-standing open problem.
The complexity upper bounds for the value-approximation problem of concurrent stochastic games are as follows:
(a) EXPSPACE for the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives; and
(b) PSPACE for parity objectives
[
7
,
8
]
.
The above result for the limit value follows from a reduction to the theory of reals,
where the number of discount factors corresponds to the number of quantifier alternation.
For the special class of reachability objectives, the complexity upper bound of TFNP[NP] for
the value-approximation problem has been established in
[
19
]
, where TFNP[NP] is the total functional form of the second level of the polynomial hierarchy.
The result of
[
19
]
has been recently extended to limit-average objectives (which correspond to the limit-value of single discount factor)
[
6
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, the above complexity upper bounds are the best bounds for
limit value of general stateful-discounted objectives and parity objectives.
The best-known algorithms for the value-approximation problem are as follows:
(a) double exponential time for the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives;
(b) exponential time for parity objectives, where the exponent is a product that depends at least linearly on the number of states and actions
[
8
,
7
]
(see
Section

3
for further details).
While iterative approaches are desirable, they neither exist for parity objectives nor guarantee efficiency even in special cases.
For example, for reachability and safety objectives, iterative approaches like value-iteration or strategy-iteration have a double-exponential lower bound
[
23
]
; and, for parity objectives, iterative approaches like strategy iteration are not known as strategies require infinite-memory
[
10
]
.

Our contributions.

In this work, our main contributions are as follows:
(a) we establish TFNP[NP] upper bounds for the value-approximation problem for concurrent stochastic games, both for the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives and the parity objectives; and (b) we present algorithms which are exponential time and improve the dependency on the number of actions in the exponent from linear to logarithmic.
In particular, if the number of states is constant, our algorithms run in polynomial time.

Preliminaries

We present standard definitions related to concurrent stochastic games.

Basic Notations.
Given a finite set
\mathcal{X}
, a probability distribution over
\mathcal{X}
is a function
\mu\colon\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu(x)=1
.
The set of all probability distributions over
\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
\Delta(\mathcal{X})
.
For
\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
, the support of
\mu
is defined as
\text{supp}(\mu)\coloneqq\{x\mid\mu(x)>0\}
.
For a positive integer
k
, the set of positive integers smaller than or equal to
k
is defined as
[k]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Given a real
x
, we denote
2^{x}
by
\exp(x)
.

Concurrent stochastic games.

A concurrent stochastic game (CSG) is a two-player finite game

G=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\delta)
consisting of

•

the set of states
\mathcal{S}
, of size
n
;

•

the sets of actions for each player
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, with at most
m
actions; and

•

the stochastic transition function
\delta\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
.

Steps.

Given an initial state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the game proceeds as follows.
In each step, both players choose an action simultaneously,
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
.
Based on both actions
(a,b)
and current state
s
, the next state is drawn
according to the probability distribution
\delta(s,a,b)
.

Histories and plays.

At step
k
of CSGs, each player possesses information in the form of the finite sequence of the states visited and the actions chosen by both players. A
k
-history
\omega^{(k)}=\langle s_{0},a_{0},b_{0},s_{1},a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle
is a finite sequence of states and actions such that, for all steps
0\leq t<k
,
we have
s_{t+1}\in\text{supp}(\delta(s_{t},a_{t},b_{t}))
.
The set of all
k
-histories is denoted by
\Omega^{(k)}
.
Similarly, a play
\omega=\langle s_{0},a_{0},b_{0},s_{1},a_{1},b_{1},\ldots\rangle
is an infinite sequence of states and actions
such that, for all steps
t\geq 0
,
we have
s_{t+1}\in\text{supp}(\delta(s_{t},a_{t},b_{t}))
.
The set of all plays is denoted by
\Omega
.
For any state
s
, the set of all plays starting at
s
, i.e.,
\omega=\langle s_{0},a_{0},b_{0},\ldots\rangle
where
s_{0}=s
, is denoted by
\Omega_{s}
.

Objectives.

An objective is a measurable function that assigns a real number to all plays. Player 1 aims to maximize the expectation of the objective, while Player 2 minimizes it.

•

Parity objective.

Given a priority function
p\colon\mathcal{S}\to\{0,\dots,d\}
with
d
as its index, the
parity
objective is an indicator of the even parity condition on minimal priority visited infinitely often in plays. More formally, we define
\parity_{p}\colon\Omega\to\{0,1\}
as

\parity_{p}(\omega)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\min\{p(s)\mid\forall i\geq 0\;\exists j\geq i\;s_{j}=s\}\text{ is even}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

•

Stateful-discounted objective.
Consider
d
discount factors
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}\in(0,1]
. Given an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
, we define the discount function
{\Lambda}\colon\mathcal{S}\to\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}\}
as
{\Lambda}(s)\coloneqq\lambda_{\chi(s)}
for all states
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Given a reward function
r\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to[0,1]
that assigns a reward value
r(s,a,b)
for all
(s,a,b)
, the stateful-discounted objective
\discounted_{\Lambda}\colon\Omega\to[0,1]
is defined as, for all
\omega=\langle s_{0},a_{0},b_{0},\cdots\rangle
,

\discounted_{\Lambda}(\omega)\coloneqq\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(r(s_{i},a_{i},b_{i}){\Lambda}(s_{i})\prod_{j<i}1-{\Lambda}(s_{j})\right)\,.

Strategies.

A strategy is a function that assigns a probability distribution over actions to every finite history and is denoted by
\sigma\colon\bigcup_{k}\Omega^{(k)}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
for Player 1 (resp.
\tau\colon\bigcup_{k}\Omega^{(k)}\to\Delta(\mathcal{B})
for Player 2).
Given strategies
\sigma
and
\tau
, the game proceeds as follows.
At step
k
, the current history is some
\omega^{(k)}\in\Omega^{(k)}
.
Player 1 (resp. Player 2) chooses an action according to the distribution
\sigma\left(\omega^{(k)}\right)
(resp.
\tau\left(\omega^{(k)}\right)
).
The set of all strategies for Player 1 and Player 2 is denoted by
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
respectively.
A
stationary
strategy depends on the past
observations only through the current state.
A stationary strategy for Player 1 (resp. Player 2) is denoted by
\sigma\colon\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
(resp.
\tau\colon\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{B})
).
The set of all stationary strategies for Player 1 and Player 2 is denoted by
\Sigma^{S}
and
\Gamma^{S}
respectively.
A
pure stationary
strategy
\sigma\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
(resp.
\tau\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{B}
) for Player 1 (resp. Player 2) is a stationary strategy that maps to Dirac distributions only. The set of all pure stationary strategies for Player 1 and Player 2 is denoted by
\Sigma^{PS}
and
\Gamma^{PS}
respectively.

Probability space.

An initial state
s
and a pair of strategies (
\sigma
,
\tau
) induce a unique probability over
\Omega_{s}
, endowed with the sigma-algebra generated by the cylinders corresponding to finite histories. We denote by
\mathbb{P}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}
and
\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}
the probability and the expectation respectively.

We state the determinacy for CSGs with stateful-discounted and parity objectives.

Theorem 1

(Parity determinacy
[
27
]
)
.

For all CSGs, states
s
, and priority functions
p
,

\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\inf_{\tau\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}[\parity_{p}]=\inf_{\tau\in\Gamma}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}[\parity_{p}]\,.

Theorem 2

(Stateful-discounted determinacy
[
31
]
)
.

For all CSGs, states
s
, reward functions, and discount functions
{\Lambda}
, we have

\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{S}}\inf_{\tau\in\Gamma^{S}}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}[\discounted_{\Lambda}]=\inf_{\tau\in\Gamma^{S}}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{S}}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}[\discounted_{\Lambda}]\,.

Values.

The above determinacy results imply that switching the quantification order of strategies do not make
a difference and leads to the unique notion of value.
The stateful-discounted value for a state
s
is defined as

\val_{\Lambda}(s)\coloneqq\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{S}}\inf_{\tau\in\Gamma^{S}}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}[\discounted_{\Lambda}]\,.

We define the parity value
\val_{p}(s)
for a state
s
analogously. The limit value for a state
s
is defined as

\val_{\chi}(s)\coloneqq\lim_{\lambda_{1}\to 0^{+}}\cdots\lim_{\lambda_{d}\to 0^{+}}\val_{\Lambda}(s)\,.

\varepsilon
-optimal strategies.

Given
\varepsilon\geq 0
, a strategy
\sigma
for Player
1
is
\varepsilon
-optimal
for the stateful-discounted objective if, for all states
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\inf_{\tau\in\Gamma^{S}}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}[\discounted_{\Lambda}]\geq\val_{\Lambda}(s)-\varepsilon\,.

We say the strategy is
optimal
if
\varepsilon=0
.
The notion of
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies for Player
2
is defined analogously. Similarly, we define
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies for the parity objectives.

Approximate value problems.

We consider two value problems stated as follows.

LimitValue
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function
r
, an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
, and an additive error
\varepsilon
.
The transition function
\delta
and the reward function
r
are represented by rational numbers using at most
\mathrm{B}
bits.
Compute an approximation
v
of the limit value at state
s
such that

|v-\val_{\chi}(s)|\leq\varepsilon\,.

ParityValue
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a priority function
p
with index
d
, and an additive error
\varepsilon
.
The transition function
\delta
is represented by rational numbers using at most
\mathrm{B}
bits.
Compute an approximation
v
of the parity value at state
s
such that

|v-\val_{p}(s)|\leq\varepsilon\,.

Overview of Results

We first discuss the previous results in the literature, and then, we show our contributions.

Previous results.

We discuss the previous works on computing the approximation of limit and parity values in CSGs. A natural approach for these computational problems is via the theory of reals. We first recall the main computational result of the theory of reals, which is a specialization of Theorem 1 of
[
4
]
.

Theorem 3

(
[
4
]
)
.

Consider
\ell
variables
x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell}
and the set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\cdots,P_{k}\}
, where, for all
i\in[k]
, we have
P_{i}
is a polynomial in
x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell}
of degree at most
D
with integer coefficients of bit-size at most
\mathrm{B}
.
Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{d}
be a partition of
x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell}
into
d
subsets such that
X_{i}
has size
\ell_{i}
. Let

\Phi=(Q_{d}X_{d})\;\cdots\;(Q_{1}X_{1})\quad\phi(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k})

be a sentence with
d
alternating quantifiers
Q_{i}\in\{\exists,\forall\}
such that
Q_{i+1}\neq Q_{i}
, and
\phi(P_{1},\cdots,P_{k})
is a quantifier-free formula with atomic formulas of the form
P_{i}\bowtie 0
where
\bowtie\,\in\{<,>,=\}
. Then, there exists an algorithm to decide the truth of
\Phi
in time

k^{\prod_{i}\mathcal{O}(\ell_{i}+1)}\cdot D^{\prod_{i}\mathcal{O}(\ell_{i})}\cdot\mathcal{O}(\len(\phi)\mathrm{B}^{2})\,,

where
\len(\phi)
is the length of the quantifier-free formula
\phi
.

Along with the above algorithmic result, the following complexity result also follows from
[
4
]
: if there is constant number of quantifier alternations, then complexity is PSPACE, and in general the complexity is EXPSPACE.
We now discuss the algorithms and complexity results from the literature for the limit value of stateful discounted-sum objectives.
The basic computational approach is via the theory of reals.
For a single discount factor, the reduction to the theory of reals and dealing with its limit (which corresponds to limit-average objectives) was presented in
[
9
]

In the general case (
d

\exists\varepsilon_{i^{\prime}}\;\forall\varepsilon_{i}\leq\varepsilon_{i^{\prime}}
in the theory of reals.
Thus, concurrent stochastic games with the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives can be reduced to the theory of reals with quantifier alternation.
This reduction gives a theory of reals sentence with the following parameters:

\ell=\mathcal{O}(m^{2}n),\quad k=\mathcal{O}(m^{2}n),\quad D=4,\quad\prod_{i}(\ell_{i}+1)=\mathcal{O}(2^{d}m^{2}n)\,,

Applying
Theorem

3
to the reduction we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4

(
LimitValue

.

For the

1.

The problem is in

; and

2.

the problem can be solved in time
\exp\left(\mathcal{O}\left(2^{d}m^{2}n+\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(\mathrm{B})\right)\right)
.

For parity objectives, the result of
[
21
,

]
reduces CSGs with parity objectives to CSGs with the limit value of stateful-discounted objectives. The reduction is achieved as follows. Consider the formula
R(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{2n-1})
from

states that the limit of
R(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{2n-1})
corresponds to the value for parity objectives. The Pre operator of the formula corresponds to the Bellman-operator for stateful-discounted objectives, which establish the connection to stateful-discounted games. This connection is made more explicit in the construction provided in
[

. This linear reduction and the above theorem lead to similar results for parity objectives.
Besides this reduction to the theory of reals, there are two other approaches for the
ParityValue
problem.
First, we can consider the nested fixpoint characterization as provided in

]
and a reduction to the theory of reals with quantifier alternation.
However, this does not lead to a better complexity.
Second, a different approach is presented in

7

This approach has the following components:
(a) it enumerates over all possible subsets of actions for every state;
(b) for each of the enumeration, it requires a solution of a qualitative value problem (or limit-sure winning) in concurrent stochastic games with parity objectives, and the value-approximation for concurrent stochastic games with reachability objectives.
This approach gives PSPACE complexity and the algorithmic complexity is
\exp\left(\mathcal{O}\left(mn+d\log(n)+\log(\log(1/\varepsilon))+\log(\mathrm{B})\right)\right)
.

4.1
Notations and Selected Results from Literature

We first present some basic notations and definitions.

Basic notations.

Given a positive integer
k
, we define
\bit(k)\coloneqq\lceil\log_{2}(k+1)\rceil
.
For a rational
k_{1}/k_{2}
, we define
\bit(k_{1}/k_{2})\coloneqq\bit(k_{1})+\bit(k_{2})
.
Given a real
x
, the sign function is

\sign(x)=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }x<0,\\
0&\text{if }x=0,\\
1&\text{if }x>0.\end{cases}

Moreover, we use the classic arithmetic with infinity, i.e.,
x+\infty=\infty
and
x-\infty=-\infty
.

Polynomials.

A
uni-variate
polynomial
P
of degree
D
with integer coefficients of bit-size
\mathrm{B}
is defined as
P(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{D}c_{i}x^{i}
where
|c_{i}|<2^{\mathrm{B}}
. We define
\lVert P\rVert_{\infty}\coloneqq\max(|c_{0}|,\cdots,|c_{D}|)
.
A
k
-variate polynomial
P
in
x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}
of degree
D_{1},\cdots,D_{k}
with integer coefficients of bit-size
\mathrm{B}
is defined as

P(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k})\coloneqq\sum_{0\leq i_{1}\leq D_{1}}\cdots\sum_{0\leq i_{k}\leq D_{k}}c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}^{i_{j}}\,,

where
|c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}|\leq 2^{\mathrm{B}}
.
Polynomial
P
is nonzero if
c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}\neq 0
for some
i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}
.
We say
\alpha
is a root of
P
if
P(\alpha)=0
.
In this work, we only consider real roots.

Matrix notations.

Given a square matrix
M
, we denote the determinant of
M
by
\det(M)
and denote the signed sum of all minors of
M
by
S(M)
.
Given two
k\times\ell
matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, we denote the Hadamard product of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
by
M_{1}\odot M_{2}
.
Given a positive integer
k
, often implicitly clear from context, we denote by
\mathbf{1}
(resp.
\mathbf{0}
) the
k
-dimensional vector with all elements equal to
1
(resp.
0
) and denote by
Id
the
k\times k
identity matrix.

Matrix games.

A matrix
M
defines a game played between two opponents, where rows (resp. columns) correspond to possible actions for the row- (resp.
column-)
player, and the entry
(M)_{i,j}
is the reward the column-player pays the row-player when the pair of actions
(i,j)
is chosen.
The value of a matrix game, denoted
\val M
, is the maximum amount the row-player can guarantee, i.e., the amount they can obtain regardless of the column-player’s strategy.

Bellow, we recall some useful results from the literature regarding the value of a matrix game (
Lemma

7
) and the determinant of a polynomial matrix (
Lemma

8
).

Lemma 7

(
[
32
, Thm. 2]
)
.

Consider a matrix game
M
. Then, there exists a square sub-matrix
M_{0}
such that

\val(M)=\frac{\det(M_{0})}{S(M_{0})}
.

Lemma 8

(
[

, Prop. 8.12]
)

Consider a

matrix

Consider a

matrix

whose entries are polynomials in

of degrees

with integer coefficients of bit-size

. Then,

is a polynomial in

4.2
Bounds on Roots of Polynomials with Integer Coefficients

In this subsection, we present a bound on the roots of multi-variate polynomials
P
with integer coefficients (
Lemma

10
). This result shows that there exists a region close to
\mathbf{0}
within which
P
does not have a root. We use this technical result to establish a connection between the stateful-discounted value and limit value.

Lemma 10
.

Consider a nonzero polynomial
P
in
x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell}
of degrees
D_{1},\cdots,D_{\ell}
with integer coefficients of bit-size
\mathrm{B}
.
Let
D\coloneqq\max(D_{1},\cdots,D_{\ell})
and
\mathrm{B}_{1}\coloneqq 4\ell\bit(D)+\mathrm{B}+1
. Then,

\displaystyle\forall\,x_{1}\in\left(0,\exp(-\mathrm{B}_{1})\right]\quad\forall\,x_{2}\in\left(0,(x_{1})^{D+1}\right]\quad\cdots\quad\forall\,x_{\ell}\in\left(0,(x_{\ell-1})^{D+1}\right]

\displaystyle|P(x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell})|\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-\ell)\cdot(x_{\ell})^{D+1}\,.

Proof.

We proceed with the proof by induction on
\ell
.

Base case
\ell=1
.

Consider
P(x_{1})=c_{0}+c_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+c_{D_{1}}x_{1}^{D_{1}}
. Note that
P
is nonzero.
Let
k
be the smallest index where
c_{k}\neq 0
. Therefore, for all
x_{1}\leq\exp(-\mathrm{B}_{1})
, we have

\displaystyle|P(x_{1})|

\displaystyle\geq|c_{k}|x_{1}^{k}-\sum_{j>k}|c_{j}|x_{1}^{j}

\displaystyle(\text{triangle inequality})

\displaystyle\geq|c_{k}|x_{1}^{k}-x_{1}^{k+1}\sum_{j>k}|c_{j}|

\displaystyle(x_{1}^{j}\leq x_{1}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq x_{1}^{k}-x_{1}^{k+1}\sum_{j>k}|c_{j}|

\displaystyle(|c_{k}|\geq 1)

\displaystyle\geq x_{1}^{k}-D\exp(\mathrm{B})x_{1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle(|c_{j}|\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}))

\displaystyle\geq x_{1}^{k}\left(1-D\exp(\mathrm{B})x_{1}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{rearrange})

\displaystyle\geq x_{1}^{k}\left(\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1})x_{1}-D\exp(\mathrm{B})x_{1}\right)

\displaystyle(x_{1}\leq\exp(-B_{1}))

\displaystyle\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-1)x_{1}^{D+1}\,,

\displaystyle(k\leq D\text{ and }D\exp(\mathrm{B})\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-1))

which completes the case.

Induction case
\ell>1
.

We partition
P
into

P(x)=P_{0}+P_{1}(x_{1})+\cdots+P_{\ell}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell})\,.

Note that
P
is nonzero.
Therefore, let
i
be the smallest index where
P_{i}\neq 0
. By the choice of
i
, for all
j<i
, we have that
P_{j}=0
.
Fix
x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell}
such that
x_{1}\leq\exp(-\mathrm{B}_{1})
, and
x_{i}\leq x_{i-1}^{D+1}
for
i>1
.
We show that

\left\lvert P_{i}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i})\right\rvert-\sum_{j>i}\left\lvert P_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{j})\right\rvert\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-\ell)x_{\ell}^{D+1}\,,

(1)

which concludes the case.

To bound the term
\left\lvert P_{i}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i})\right\rvert
of LHS of
Eq.

1
from below, we define

Q(y)\coloneqq P_{i}(x_{1}\cdots,x_{i-1},y)=\sum_{j=1}^{D_{i}}C_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1})y^{j}\,,

where for all
1\leq j\leq D_{i}
, we have

C_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1})\coloneqq\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{D_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{i-1}=0}^{D_{i-1}}c_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{i-1},j,0,\cdots,0}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}x_{k}^{j_{k}}\,.

Note that
Q
is a univariate polynomial of degree at most
D_{i}
, where the absolute value of coefficients are at most

\displaystyle|C_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1})|

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\mathrm{B})(D+1)^{i-1}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}+(i-1)\bit(D+1))

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}+2\ell\bit(D))

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-\bit(D)-(i+1))\,

(2)

where in the first inequality we use
c_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{i-1},j,0,\cdots,0}\leq\exp(\mathrm{B})
, in the second inequality we use
(D+1)^{i-1}\leq\exp((i-1)\bit(D+1))
, in the third inequality we use
i-1\leq\ell
and
\bit(D+1)\leq 2\bit(D)
, and in the fourth inequality we use
\mathrm{B}+2\ell\bit(D)+\bit(D)+(i+1)\leq\mathrm{B}_{1}
. For all
1\leq j\leq D_{i}
, note that
C_{j}
is a polynomial in
x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1}
of degrees
D_{1},\cdots,D_{i-1}
with integer coefficients of bit-size
\mathrm{B}
. Therefore, if
C_{j}
is nonzero, then by induction, we have

C_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1})\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i-1))x_{i-1}^{D+1}\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i-1))x_{i}\,.

(3)

Consider
Q(y)=c_{0}+c_{1}y+\cdots+c_{d_{i}}y^{D_{i}}
, where for all
j
, we have
c_{j}=C_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1})
.
Since
P_{i}
is nonzero, by the definition of
Q
, there exists
j
such that
C_{j}\neq 0
.
Therefore, by
Eq.

3
, we have that
Q
is also nonzero.
Let
k
be the smallest index where
c_{k}\neq 0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|P_{i}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i})|

\displaystyle\quad=|Q(x_{1})|

\displaystyle(\text{def. }Q)

\displaystyle\quad\geq|c_{k}x_{i}^{k}|-\sum_{j>k}|c_{j}x_{i}^{j}|

\displaystyle(\text{triangle inequality})

\displaystyle\quad\geq|c_{k}x_{i}^{k}|-x_{i}^{k+1}\sum_{j>k}|c_{j}|

\displaystyle\left(x_{i}^{j}\leq x_{i}^{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=x_{i}^{k}\left(|c_{k}|-x_{i}\sum_{j>k}|c_{j}|\right)

\displaystyle(\text{rearrange})

\displaystyle\quad\geq x_{i}^{k}\left(|c_{k}|-\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i+1))x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{\lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{Eq: Upper bound of coeffs of G}})

\displaystyle\quad\geq x_{i}^{k}\left(\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i-1))x_{i}-\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i+1))x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{\lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{Eq: Lower bound of coeffs of G}})

\displaystyle\quad\geq x_{i}^{D+1}\left(\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i-1))-\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i+1))\right)\,.

\displaystyle(k\leq D)

(4)

To bound the term
\sum_{j>i}\left\lvert P_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{j})\right\rvert
of the LHS of
Eq.

1
from above, consider
P_{j}
for all
j>i
. Recall that
P_{j}
is a polynomial in
x_{1},\cdots,x_{j}
of degrees at most
D_{1},\cdots,D_{j}
with integer coefficients of bit-size
\mathrm{B}
. Therefore, the number of terms in
P_{j}
is at most
(D+1)^{j}
, and for each term, the degree of
x_{j}
is at least 1. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\lvert P_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{j})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left(D+1\right)^{j}\cdot\exp(\mathrm{B})\cdot x_{j}

\displaystyle(c_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{j},0,\cdots,0}\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}))

\displaystyle\leq\left(D+1\right)^{\ell}\cdot\exp(\mathrm{B})\cdot x_{j}

\displaystyle\left((D+1)^{j}\leq(D+1)^{\ell}\right)\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{j>i}\left\lvert P_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{j})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(D+1)^{\ell}\exp(\mathrm{B})\sum_{j>i}x_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\ell(D+1)^{\ell}\exp(\mathrm{B})\cdot x_{i+1}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\bit(\ell)+\ell\bit(D+1)+\mathrm{B}\right)\cdot x_{i+1}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\bit(\ell)+2\ell\bit(D)+\mathrm{B}\right)\cdot x_{i+1}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i+1))x_{i+1}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-(i+1))x_{i}^{D+1}\,,

(5)

where in the second inequality we use
x_{j}\leq x_{i+1}
for all
j\geq i+1
, in the third inequality we use
\ell\cdot(D+1)^{\ell}\leq\exp(\bit(\ell)+\ell\bit(D+1))
, in the fourth inequality we use
\bit(D+1)\leq 2\bit(D)
, in the fifth inequality we use
\bit(\ell)+2\ell\bit(D)+\mathrm{B}+(i+1)\leq\mathrm{B}_{1}
, and in the sixth inequality we use
x_{i+1}\leq x_{i}^{D+1}
.
By combining
Eqs.

4
and
5
, we get

\displaystyle\left\lvert P_{i}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i})\right\rvert-\sum_{j>i}\left\lvert P_{j}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{j})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-i)x_{i}^{D+1}

\displaystyle\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-\ell)x_{i}^{D+1}

\displaystyle(i\leq\ell)

\displaystyle\geq\exp(\mathrm{B}_{1}-\ell)x_{\ell}^{D+1}\,,

\displaystyle(x_{\ell}\leq x_{i})

which concludes the claim, completes the case, and yields the result.
∎

.

4.3
Characterization of Stateful-discounted Value

In this subsection, we introduce a new characterization of the stateful-discounted value in CSGs (
Corollaries

12
and
11
), which generalizes the result presented in
[
2
]
from a single discount factor to multiple discount factors. In particular,
Corollary

12
generalizes Theorem 1 of
[
2
]
.

Stateful-discounted payoff.
Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function
r
, and a discount function
{\Lambda}
. Given a pair of stationary strategies
(\sigma,\tau)
, we define the stateful-discounted payoff as

\nu^{\sigma,\tau}(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}[\discounted_{\Lambda}]\,.

By fixing
\sigma
and
\tau
, we obtain a transition function

\delta^{\sigma,\tau}(s,s^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{A}\\
b\in\mathcal{B}\end{subarray}}\sigma(s)(a)\cdot\tau(s)(b)\cdot\delta(s,a,b)(s^{\prime})\,,

which is described as a matrix, i.e.,
\delta^{\sigma,\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. The stage reward function is defined as

r^{\sigma,\tau}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{A}\\
b\in\mathcal{B}\end{subarray}}\sigma(s)(a)\cdot\tau(s)(b)\cdot r(s,a,b)\,,

which is described as a vector, i.e.,
r^{\sigma,\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, the Bellman operator defined in
[
31
]
can be written as a recursive expression:

\nu^{\sigma,\tau}={\Lambda}\odot r^{\sigma,\tau}+(\mathbf{1}-{\Lambda})\odot\left(\delta^{\sigma,\tau}\nu^{\sigma,\tau}\right)\,.

The matrix
\text{Id}-\left(\,(\mathbf{1}-{\Lambda})\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,\right)\odot\delta^{\sigma,\tau}
is strictly diagonally dominant, and therefore, is invertible. By Cramer’s rule, we have

\nu^{\sigma,\tau}(s)=\frac{\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\sigma,\tau)}{\nabla_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\tau)}\,,

(6)

where
\nabla_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\tau)\coloneqq\det\left(\text{Id}-\left(\,(\mathbf{1}-{\Lambda})\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,\right)\odot\delta^{\sigma,\tau}\right)
and
\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\sigma,\tau)
is the determinant of an
n\times n
matrix derived by substituting the
s
-th column of the matrix
\text{Id}-\left((\mathbf{1}-{\Lambda})\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right)\odot\delta^{\sigma,\tau}
with
{\Lambda}\odot r^{\sigma,\tau}
.

Auxiliary matrix game
W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)
.
We define a matrix game where the actions of each player are the pure stationary strategies in the stochastic game. The payoff of the game is obtained by the linearization of the quotient in
Eq.

6
. More formally,
for all parameters
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}
, and
\widehat{\tau}\in\Gamma^{PS}
, we define the payoff of the matrix game as

W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)[\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau}]\coloneqq\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})-z\cdot\nabla_{\Lambda}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})\,.

The value of
W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)
is denoted by
\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)\right)
.

The following statements (
Lemma

11
and
Corollary

12
) connect the stateful-discounted value with the value of the matrix game.

Lemma 11
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function, and an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
. Then, the following assertions hold.

1.

The map
(z,\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})\mapsto\val(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z))
is continuous;

2.

for all discount factors
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
z_{1}\leq z_{2}
, we have that

\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z_{1})\right)\geq\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z_{2})\right)+(z_{2}-z_{1})\left(\min_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)^{n}
, in particular,
z\mapsto\val(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z))
is strictly decreasing; and

3.

for all discount factors
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
, we have
\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(\val_{\Lambda}(s))\right)=0
.

Corollary 12
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function, and a discount function
{\Lambda}
. Then,
\val_{\Lambda}(s)
is the unique
z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z^{*})\right)=0\,.

Below we define randomized strategies in the matrix game derived from stationary strategies in concurrent stochastic games.

Strategies for the matrix game.

Given a Player-1 (resp. Player 2) stationary strategy
\sigma
(resp.
\tau
), we denote by
\bm{\sigma}
(resp.
\bm{\tau}
) a randomized strategy for the matrix game
W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)
defined as

\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\coloneqq\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sigma(s)(\widehat{\sigma}(s))\quad\forall\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}\,.

Also, we define

W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)[\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau}]\coloneqq\sum_{\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}}\sum_{\widehat{\tau}\in\Gamma^{PS}}\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\cdot\bm{\tau}(\widehat{\tau})\cdot W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)[\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau}]\,.

The following result is instrumental to prove that the value of the matrix game is strictly decreasing (
Lemma

11
-
Item

2
).

Lemma 13
.

Consider a
k\times k
stochastic matrix
M
and a discount function
{\Lambda}\colon[k]\to\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}\}
. Then, we have

\det\left(\text{Id}-\left((\mathbf{1}-{\Lambda})\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right)\odot M\right)\geq\left(\min_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)^{k}\,.

Proof.

Fix
\widehat{M}\coloneqq\text{Id}-\left((\mathbf{1}-{\Lambda})\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right)\odot M
.
We claim that the matrix
\widehat{M}
is a strictly diagonally dominant matrix. Indeed,
M
is a stochastic matrix. Therefore, for the
i
-th row of
\widehat{M}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{i,i}-\sum_{j\neq i}|\widehat{M}_{i,j}|

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-{\Lambda}(i)\right)\sum_{j}M_{i,j}

\displaystyle(\text{def. }\widehat{M})

\displaystyle={\Lambda}(i)\,.

\displaystyle(M\text{ is a stochastic matrix})

Consider the (possibly complex) eigenvalues
\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{k}
. By Gershgorin circle theorem
[
20
]
, for all
i
, we have that
|\xi_{i}-1|\leq 1-\min_{i}\lambda_{i}
.
Therefore, we have

\det(\widehat{M})=\prod_{i}\xi_{i}\geq\left(\min_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)^{k}\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

The following result is instrumental to prove that the stateful-discounted value forces the matrix game to have value zero (
Lemma

11
-
Item

3
).

Lemma 14
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function, a discount function
{\Lambda}
, and a Player-1 stationary strategy
\sigma
. Then, for all
\widehat{\tau}\in\Gamma^{PS}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)[\bm{\sigma},\widehat{\tau}]=\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})-z\cdot\nabla_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})\,,

where
\bm{\sigma}
is the strategy for
W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)
derived by the strategy
\sigma
.

Proof.

For all
\sigma\in\Sigma^{S}
and
\tau\in\Gamma^{S}
, we define
M(\sigma,\tau)\coloneqq\text{Id}-\left((1-{\Lambda})1^{\top}\right)\odot\delta^{\sigma,\tau}
. We also denote by
\sigma_{s\to a}
the stationary strategy derived from
\sigma
where Player 1 chooses the action
a
at the state
s
. By definition,
\nabla_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\tau)=\det(M(\sigma,\tau))
.
We now show that

\nabla_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})=\sum_{\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}}\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\cdot\nabla_{\Lambda}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})\,.

Indeed, the
s
-th row of
M(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})
only depends on
\sigma(s)
. Therefore, by multi-linearity of the determinant, we have

\displaystyle\det(M(\sigma,\widehat{\tau}))

\displaystyle=\det\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sigma(s)(a)M(\sigma_{s\to a},\widehat{\tau})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sigma(s)(a)\cdot\det(M(\sigma_{s\to a},\widehat{\tau}))\,.

Hence, by induction, we have

\nabla_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})=\det(M(\sigma,\widehat{\tau}))=\sum_{\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}}\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\cdot\det(M(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau}))=\sum_{\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}}\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\cdot\nabla_{\Lambda}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})\,.

By similar arguments, we can show that

\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})=\sum_{\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}}\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\cdot\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)[\bm{\sigma},\widehat{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}}\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\cdot W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)[\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widehat{\sigma}\in\Sigma^{PS}}\bm{\sigma}(\widehat{\sigma})\left[\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})-z\cdot\nabla_{\Lambda}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})\right]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})-z\cdot\nabla_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\widehat{\tau})\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

4.4
Characterization of Limit Value

In this subsection, we introduce a new characterization of the limit value in CSGs (
Corollaries

16
and
15
), which generalizes the result presented in
[
2
]
from a single discount factor to multiple discount factors. In particular,
Corollary

16
generalizes Theorem 2 of
[
2
]
.

Limit function.
Given a CSG
G
, a reward function, and an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
, we define the limit function as

F_{\chi}^{s}(z)\coloneqq\lim_{\lambda_{1}\to 0^{+}}\cdots\lim_{\lambda_{d}\to 0^{+}}\frac{\val\left(W^{s}_{\Lambda}(z)\right)}{(\lambda_{d})^{n}}\,.

Lemma 15
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function, and an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
. Then, the following assertions hold.

1.

For all
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the limit
F_{\chi}^{s}(z)
exists in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
; and

2.

There exists
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
F_{\chi}^{s}(z_{2})\leq 0\leq F_{\chi}^{s}(z_{1})
.

Corollary 16
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function, and an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
. Then,
\val_{\chi}(s)
is the unique
z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall z>z^{*}\quad F_{\chi}^{s}(z)<0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall z<z^{*}\quad F_{\chi}^{s}(z)>0\,.

Below we first present
Lemma

17
, which shows that, given a fixed parameter
z
, the value of the matrix game is a rational function when discount factors are small enough, which implies the existence of the limit function. We then prove
Lemma

15
and
Corollary

16
.

Lemma 17
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function, an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
, and a real number
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, there exist two polynomials
P
and
Q
in
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
such that

\displaystyle\exists\lambda^{0}_{1}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{1}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{1})\quad\cdots\quad\exists\lambda^{0}_{d}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{d}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{d})

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\quad Q(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)\right)=\frac{P(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}{Q(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}\,.

Proof.

By
Lemma

7
, there exists a sub-matrix
M
in
W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)
such that

\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)\right)=\frac{\det(M)}{S(M)}\,.

Since
z
is fixed, the matrix

is a matrix with polynomial entries in
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
. Therefore, by
Lemmas

8
and
9
, there exist two polynomials
P
and
Q
in
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
such that

\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)\right)=\frac{P(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}{Q(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}\,.

Since the matrix
W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)
is finite, the set of all sub-matrices is finite. Therefore, there exist two finite sets of polynomials
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
such that for all
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
, there exists

and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
such that

\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)\right)=\frac{P(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}{Q(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}\,.

By

11
-
Item

1
, we have

is continuous in

. Therefore, as

varies in

, the value of the matrix game can only jump from one rational function to another if the graphs of two rationals intersect. More formally,

can jump from

We now claim that for every

and

, either

and

are congruent, or

\displaystyle\exists\lambda^{0}_{1}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{1}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{1})\quad\cdots\quad\exists\lambda^{0}_{d}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{d}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{d})

.

.
If

, then

and
\frac{P_{2}}{Q_{2}}
are congruent, which complete the case.

Case

.
If

, then by

10
, we have that

\displaystyle\exists\lambda^{0}_{1}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{1}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{1})\quad\cdots\quad\exists\lambda^{0}_{d}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{d}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{d})

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\quad C(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})\neq 0\,.

, then
\frac{P_{1}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}{Q_{1}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}\neq\frac{P_{2}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}{Q_{2}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}
, which completes the case and concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

.

We prove the two items as follows.

1.

It is a direct implication of
Lemma

17
.

2.

We define

and

. For all
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
, we have

and taking

which yields the result.

∎

Proof of

.

Consider the function
F_{\chi}^{s}
.
By
Lemma

15
-
Item

2
, it is not constant.
By
Lemma

11
-

, it is decreasing.
Let

be the point where

changes sign.

.
Hence,

\displaystyle\exists\lambda^{0}_{1}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{1}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{1})\quad\cdots\quad\exists\lambda^{0}_{d}>0\quad\forall\lambda_{d}\in(0,\lambda^{0}_{d})

4.5
Approximation of Limit Value

In this subsection, we introduce an approach for the approximation of the limit value via the stateful-discounted value (
Theorem

18
).
The rest of the subsection is dedicated to its proof.

Theorem 18
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function
r
, an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
, and an additive error
\varepsilon>0
. The transition function
\delta
and the reward function
r
are represented by rational numbers of bit-size
\mathrm{B}
.
Fix

D\coloneqq\max(|\Sigma^{PS}|,|\Gamma^{PS}|),\quad\mathrm{B}_{1}\coloneqq 11Dn(\mathrm{B}+\bit(n)+\bit(D)+\bit(\varepsilon))\,,

, we set
\lambda^{0}_{i}\coloneqq\exp\left(-\mathrm{B}_{1}(nD+1)^{i-1}\right)
.
Then, we have

|\val_{{\Lambda}^{0}}(s)-\val_{\chi}(s)|\leq\varepsilon\,.

Remark 19

(Novelty)
.

As mentioned previously, our result is a generalization of
[
2
]
. The key non-trivial aspect of the generalization relies on the fact that
[
2
]
considers uni-variate polynomials, whereas our result requires analysis of multi-variate polynomials.

10
is the key mathematical foundation, and the complete proofs require significant technical generalization.

Below we first show the algebraic properties of

(

20
) to derive some insights on the asymptotic behavior of the sign of the map

as
\lambda_{d},\cdots,\lambda_{1}
go to 0 respectively (
Lemma

21
). We then use
Lemma

21
to establish a connection between the stateful-discounted value and the limit value in
Theorem

18
.

Lemma 20
.

Consider a CSG
G
, a state
s
, a reward function
r
, and an assignment function
\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to[d]
. The transition function
\delta
and the reward function

are represented by rational numbers of bit-size
\mathrm{B}
.

D\coloneqq\max(|\Sigma^{PS}|,|\Gamma^{PS}|)

Proof.

By definition,

for all pure stationary strategies
\widehat{\sigma}
and
\widehat{\tau}
.
Note that
\nabla_{\Lambda}^{s}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})
and
\nabla_{\Lambda}(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\tau})
are the determinant of two matrices whose entries are polynomial in
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
of degrees

with coefficients from the set
\{0,2^{-B},2\cdot 2^{-\mathrm{B}},\cdots,1\}
.
Therefore, by
Lemma

8
, the entries of

are polynomials in
z
,
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
of degrees

with integer coefficients of bit-size

We now define two sets of nonzero polynomials
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
as

\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\left\{\det(M)\mid M\text{ is a sub-matrix of }2^{n\mathrm{B}}W_{\Lambda}^{s}(z)\right\}\,,

By

, there exists a sub-matrix

in the matrix game

for the

matrix
M
. Note that

is a polynomial matrix. Hence, there exist
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
such that for all
z
and
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}
, we have

Q(z,\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})\neq 0,\quad\val\left(W_{\Lambda}^{s}\right)=\frac{P(z,\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}{Q(z,\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})}\,.

We now show that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
satisfy the requirements of the lemma.
Let

and

, where

11
-
Item

are polynomials in

\displaystyle k\mathrm{B}^{\prime}+k\bit(k)+(d+1)\bit(kn+1)

\displaystyle=D(n\mathrm{B}+3n\bit(n)+\bit(D)+4n\bit(nD))

where in the first inequality we use

6.1
Definitions and Selected Results from Literature

We present some basic notations and definitions related to Markov Chains, Markov Decision Processes, and the classic symbolic representation for numbers and probability distributions, called floating-point.

Markov decision processes and Markov chains.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, a Player-
i
Markov decision process (Player-
i
MDP) is a special class of CSGs where the other player has only one action and is denoted by
\mathscr{P}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\delta\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S}))
.
A Markov chain (MC) is a special class of MDPs where both players have only one action and is denoted by
\mathscr{C}=(\mathcal{S},\delta\colon\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S}))
. In Markov chains we write
\delta(s,s^{\prime})
to denote
\delta(s)(s^{\prime})
.

Absorbing MCs.

We say an MC
\mathscr{C}
is
absorbing
if there exists a subset of absorbing states
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that

•

For all
s\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
, we have
\delta(s,s)=1
; and

•

For all
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{0}
, there exist states
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}
such that
\delta(s_{i},s_{i+1})>0
and
s_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
.

States in
\mathcal{S}_{0}
are called absorbing.

MDPs and MCs given stationary strategies in CSGs.

Given a stationary strategy
\sigma
for Player 1 in a game
G
, by fixing the strategy
\sigma
, we obtain a Player-2 MDP
G_{\sigma}=(S,\mathcal{B},\delta_{\sigma})

where the transition function
\delta_{\sigma}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{B}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is given by

\delta_{\sigma}(s,b)(s^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\delta(s,a,b)(s^{\prime})\cdot\sigma(s)(a)\,,

for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
. Analogously, we obtain Player-1 MDP
G_{\tau}
by fixing a stationary strategy
\tau
for Player 2.
Moreover, by fixing stationary strategies
\sigma
and
\tau
for both players, we obtain an MC
G_{\sigma,\tau}=(\mathcal{S},\delta_{\sigma,\tau})
, where the transition function
\delta_{\sigma,\tau}\colon\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is given by

\delta_{\sigma,\tau}(s)(s^{\prime})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\delta(s,a,b)(s^{\prime})\cdot\sigma(s)(a)\cdot\tau(s)(b)\,,

for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Reachability objectives in MCs.
Given an MC
\mathscr{C}
and a target set
T\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, the reachability objective is the indicator function of plays eventually reaching
T
. More formally, for a play
\omega=\langle s_{0},s_{1},\cdots\rangle
, we define
\reach_{T}\colon\Omega\to\{0,1\}
as

\reach_{T}(\omega)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\exists i\geq 0\;s_{i}\in T\\
0\,.&\end{cases}

We define the probability of reaching the target set
T
from state
s
as
\val_{T}(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{s}[\reach_{T}]
.

Floating-point number representation.
We define the set of floating-point numbers with precision
\ell
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\ell)\coloneqq\left\{m\cdot 2^{e}\quad\mid\quad m\in\{0,\cdots,2^{\ell}-1\},\quad e\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}\,.

The floating-point representation of an element
x=m\cdot 2^{e}\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)
uses
\bit(m)+\bit(|e|)
bits. We define the relative distance of two positive real numbers
x,\widetilde{x}
as

\rel(x,\widetilde{x})\coloneqq\max\left\{\frac{x}{\widetilde{x}},\frac{\widetilde{x}}{x}\right\}-1=\inf\{\alpha>0\kern 5.0pt:\kern 5.0ptx\leq(1+\alpha)\widetilde{x},\quad\widetilde{x}\leq(1+\alpha)x\}\,.

We say
x
is
(\ell,i)
-close to
\widetilde{x}
if

\rel(x,\widetilde{x})\leq(1-2^{1-\ell})^{-i}-1
,
where
\ell
is a positive integer and
i
is a non-negative integer.

Arithmetic operations.
We define
\oplus^{\ell},\ominus^{\ell},\otimes^{\ell},\oslash^{\ell}
as finite precision arithmetic operations
+,-,*,/
respectively by truncating the result of the exact arithmetic operation to
\ell
bits. We drop the superscript
\ell
if context is clear.

Floating-point probability distribution representation.

We denote by
\mathcal{D}(\ell)
the set of all floating-point probability distributions with precision
\ell
.
A probability distribution
\mu\in\Delta([t])
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(\ell)
if there exists
w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{t}\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)
such that

•

For all
i\in[t]
, we have
\mu(i)=\frac{w_{i}}{\sum_{j\in[t]}w_{j}}
; and

•

\sum_{j\in[t]}w_{j}
and 1 are
(\ell,t)
-close.

We define the relative distance
\rel
for probability distributions as

\rel(\mu,\widetilde{\mu})\coloneqq\max\{\rel(\mu(i),\widetilde{\mu}(i)):i\in[t]\}
.
We say
\mu
is
(\ell,i)
-close to
\widetilde{\mu}
if

\rel(\mu,\widetilde{\mu})\leq(1-2^{1-\ell})^{-i}-1
,
where
\ell
is a positive integer and
i
is a non-negative integer.

6.3
Stateful-discounted Value Approximation in MDPs

In this subsection, we present an NP procedure for the approximate decision problem of the stateful-discounted values in MDPs.
This is accomplished by guessing a pure stationary strategy and verifying if the strategy achieves the given threshold.
The verification procedure uses the algorithm for computing approximate stateful-discounted values in MCs.

Lemma 34
.

The problem of deciding if the stateful-discounted value for Player-1 MDPs is below a threshold up to an additive error is in NP where the input is a Player-1 MDP
\mathscr{P}
, a reward function
r
, a discount function
{\Lambda}
, a state
s
, a threshold
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, an additive error
\varepsilon=2^{-\kappa}
and a positive integer
\ell
such that, for all
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\delta(s^{\prime},a)\in D(\ell),\quad r(s^{\prime},a)\in\mathcal{F}(\ell),\quad{\Lambda}(s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}(\ell),\quad\ell\geq 1000n^{2}+\kappa\,.

Note that, the numbers
\alpha
and
\varepsilon
are represented in fixed-point binary and the NP procedure is such that

•

If
\alpha\leq\val_{\Lambda}(s)-\varepsilon
, then it outputs YES; and

•

If
\alpha\geq\val_{\Lambda}(s)+\varepsilon
, then it outputs NO.

Proof.

We first present the NP procedure and then prove its soundness and completeness.

Procedure.

The procedure guesses a pure stationary strategy
\sigma
for Player 1.
Note that the size of the representation of a pure stationary strategy is polynomial with respect to the size of the representation of
\mathscr{P}
.
By fixing
\sigma
, we obtain an MC
\mathscr{P}_{\sigma}
.
We denote by
v_{\sigma}
its stateful-discounted value.
By
Lemma

28
, there exists a polynomial time algorithm that computes an
\varepsilon
-approximation
\widetilde{v}_{\sigma}
of
v_{\sigma}
.
Our procedure outputs YES if
\alpha\leq\widetilde{v}_{\sigma}(s)
.
If there exists no such pure stationary strategy, the procedure outputs NO.

Soundness.

If
\alpha\leq\val_{\Lambda}(s)-\varepsilon
, then, by
[
18
]
, there exists a pure stationary strategy
\sigma
such that
\val_{\Lambda}=v_{\sigma}
.
The procedure non-deterministically guesses
\sigma
.
By
Lemma

33
, we have
\widetilde{v}_{\sigma}(s)+\varepsilon\geq v_{\sigma}(s)
.
Therefore, we have
\alpha\leq\widetilde{v}_{\sigma}(s)
, and the procedure outputs YES.

Completeness.

If
\alpha\geq\val_{\Lambda}(s)+\varepsilon
, then for all pure stationary strategies
\sigma
, we have
\alpha\geq v_{\sigma}(s)+\varepsilon
.
By
Lemma

33
, we have
\widetilde{v}_{\sigma}(s)-\varepsilon\leq v_{\sigma}(s)
.
Therefore,
\alpha\geq\widetilde{v}_{\sigma}(s)
which implies that the procedure outputs NO and yields the result.
∎

Lemma

More formally, we have the following result.

\mathscr{P}
, a reward function
r
, a discount function
{\Lambda}
, a state
s
, a threshold
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, an additive error
\varepsilon=2^{-\kappa}
and a positive integer
\ell
such that, for all
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and

, we have

\delta(s^{\prime},a)\in D(\ell),\quad r(s^{\prime},a)\in\mathcal{F}(\ell),\quad{\Lambda}(s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}(\ell),\quad\ell\geq 1000n^{2}+\kappa\,.

Lemma 36
.

Consider
i\in\{1,2\}
and two Player-
i
MDPs
\mathscr{P}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}
with identical state and action sets, a reward function
r
, and a discount function
{\Lambda}
.
We denote by
\val_{\Lambda}
and
\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}
the stateful-discounted value of
\mathscr{P}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}

Then, for all

, we have

\left|\val_{\Lambda}(s)-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}(s)\right|\leq\frac{\|\delta-\widetilde{\delta}\|_{\infty}}{\min_{s}{\Lambda}(s)}\,.

Proof.

We only prove for Player-1 MDPs. The proof for Player-2 MDPs is symmetric. By the Bellman equation defined in
[
31
]
, for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\val_{\Lambda}(s)-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\max_{a}\Bigg|{\Lambda}(s)r(s,a)+(1-{\Lambda}(s))\sum_{s^{\prime}}\delta(s,a)(s^{\prime})\val_{\Lambda}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\phantom{\leq\max_{a}\Bigg|}\qquad-{\Lambda}(s)r(s,a)-(1-{\Lambda}(s))\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{\delta}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}(s^{\prime})\Bigg|

\displaystyle\quad=(1-{\Lambda}(s))\max_{a}\Bigg|\sum_{s^{\prime}}\delta(s,a)(s^{\prime})(\val_{\Lambda}(s^{\prime})-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}(s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad\phantom{=(1-{\Lambda}(s))\max_{a}\Bigg|}\qquad+\sum_{s^{\prime}}(\delta(s,a)(s^{\prime})-\widetilde{\delta}(s,a)(s^{\prime}))\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}(s^{\prime})\Bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1-{\Lambda}(s))\left(\|\val_{\Lambda}-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}+\|\delta-\widetilde{\delta}\|_{\infty}\,\|\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1-{\Lambda}(s))\left(\|\val_{\Lambda}-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}+\|\delta-\widetilde{\delta}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(1-\min_{s}{\Lambda}(s)\right)\left(\|\val_{\Lambda}-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}+\|\delta-\widetilde{\delta}\|_{\infty}\right)\,,

(14)

where in the first inequality we use the Bellman equation, in the first equality we use algebraic manipulation, in the second inequality we use the definition of the norm and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, in the third inequality we use
\|\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}\leq\|r\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, and in the fourth inequality we use
\min_{s}{\Lambda}(s)\leq{\Lambda}(s)
.
Since
Eq.

14
holds for all states
s

\|\val_{\Lambda}-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}\leq\left(1-\min_{s}{\Lambda}(s)\right)\left(\|\val_{\Lambda}-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}+\|\delta-\widetilde{\delta}\|_{\infty}\right)\,,

or equivalently

\|\val_{\Lambda}-\widetilde{\val}_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{1-\min_{s}{\Lambda}(s)}{\min_{s}{\Lambda}(s)}\right)\|\delta-\widetilde{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\|\delta-\widetilde{\delta}\|_{\infty}}{\min_{s}{\Lambda}(s)}\,,

which yields the result.

Concluding remarks.

In this work, we present improved complexity upper bounds and algorithms for the value approximation problem for concurrent stochastic games
with two classic objectives.
There are several interesting directions for future work.
First, whether the complexity can be further improved from
TFNP[NP] to TFNP is a major open question, even for reachability objectives.
Second, whether for parity objectives, the dependency on
d
can be improved from linear to logarithmic, retaining the logarithmic dependence on
m
, is another interesting open question.
Finally, the study of priority mean-payoff objectives for concurrent stochastic games and their connection to stateful-discounted objectives is another interesting direction for future work.
