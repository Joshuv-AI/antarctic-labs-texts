PARDEN, Can You Repeat That? Defending against Jailbreaks via Repetition
Ziyang Zhang, Qizhen Zhang, Jakob Foerster

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have shown success in many natural language processing tasks. Despite rigorous safety alignment processes, supposedly safety-aligned LLMs like Llama 2 and Claude 2 are still susceptible to jailbreaks, leading to security risks and abuse of the models. One option to mitigate such risks is to augment the LLM with a dedicated “safeguard”, which checks the LLM’s inputs or outputs for undesired behaviour. A promising approach is to use the LLM itself as the safeguard. Nonetheless, baseline methods, such as prompting the LLM to self-classify toxic content, demonstrate limited efficacy. We hypothesise that this is due to domain shift : the alignment training imparts a self-censoring behaviour to the model (“Sorry I can’t do that”), while the self-classify approach shifts it to a classification format (“Is this prompt malicious”). In this work, we propose PARDEN, which avoids this domain shift by simply asking the model to repeat its own outputs . PARDEN neither requires finetuning nor white box access to the model. We empirically verify the effectiveness of our method and show that PARDEN significantly outperforms existing jailbreak detection baselines for Llama-2 and Claude-2. Code and data are available at https://github.com/Ed-Zh/PARDEN . We find that PARDEN is particularly powerful in the relevant regime of high True Positive Rate (TPR) and low False Positive Rate (FPR). For instance, for Llama2-7B, at TPR equal to 90%, PARDEN accomplishes a roughly 11x reduction in the FPR from 24.8% to 2.0% on the harmful behaviours dataset.

Introduction

We are currently experiencing a revolution in artificial intelligence due to the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) to a variety of applications, such as chatbots, search engines and others
[
15
,
14
,
29
]

The current paradigm involves a two-step process: initial
pre-training
step
[
7
]
on extensive text corpora followed by
alignment
procedures aimed at 1) teaching the model to follow instructions and 2) safeguarding the model against generating undesirable outputs
[
22
,
15

.
Such safeguards are crucial for two reasons: first, to shield users from potentially harmful LLM outputs, and secondly, to mitigate the risks of exploitation from malicious actors
[
6
]
. Recent studies have shown that existing alignment techniques are easily circumvented by “jailbreaks”
[
42
,
25
]
, which are inputs that induce LLMs to produce undesirable outputs. To make matters worse, many of these methods rely on LLMs themselves to discover such jailbreaks
[
9
,
10
]
. As LLMs advance, so do the capabilities of exploiters. Consequently, the effectiveness of our safeguards must also evolve.

It is therefore crucial to develop
defence methods
which also use the LLMs themselves to prevent jailbreaks, which will improve as the LLMs improve.

The first step of developing a safeguard is to understand how jailbreaks are produced. Many attacks
[
41
,
42
]
exploit the so-called “auto-regressive trap”, i.e. the fact that LLMs sample responses one token at a time, without planning/anticipating what comes in the farther future. As a result, the LLM can start sampling the response “Sure let me help you with that.. ” (rather than refusal), without “realising” that compliance with the request ultimately results in a detailed instruction for committing cyber-crime.

Hence, avoiding this “auto-regressive trap” offers a way to mitigate such attacks. When the LLM is used as a
separate
safety guard, it is provided with the
entire output
at once and should thus be better able to classify content into
harmful
and
harmless
correctly. This idea is explored by
Helbling et al. [11]
and turned the LLM into a zero-shot classifier.

While this approach avoids the auto-regressive trap, it suffers from
domain shift
: The alignment process of the LLM consists of desired input-output pairs which contain examples of malicious prompts and the desired
refusal response
as well as benign prompts and the desired output. In other words, during the alignment stage, the LLM is trained in a
self-censorship
format, in which it responds to malicious inputs with the
refusal
response (e.g. “Sorry I can’t do that. ”).
By contrast, current LLM-based safeguarding approaches (
Helbling et al. [11]
,
Inan et al. [12]
) use the LLM in a
classification
format, in which the LLM needs to output “yes” / “no” for malicious / benign inputs and outputs respectively.

Our key hypothesis is that this domain shift limits the efficacy of current defence methods. To address this we introduce PARDEN

Unlike existing defense methods that rely on self-classification, PARDEN avoids both the auto-regressive trap and

Specifically, we find that for benign samples, the BLEU score between the original LLM output and the LLM’s repetition thereof is near 1.0 for most samples, indicating a high fidelity of repetition, while the BLEU score between the original output and the refusal response (which gets triggered for malicious samples) is on the order of 0.2 to 0.6. This allows us to classify malicious samples using a simple threshold parameter, by choosing an acceptable True Positive Rate (TPR).

Related Works

Finding Jailbreak

In real-world scenarios, malicious users intentionally attempt to elicit harmful behaviours from LLMs by manipulating the user input prompt. An ill-intentioned prompt is censored by a safety-aligned LLM, which outputs a refusal message, such as “Sorry, as an AI, I can’t help with that”. However, the malicious user can bypass LLM’s safeguard with jailbreak attacks.

Many works
[
24
,
20
,
28
,
19
,
38
,
35
]
manually find jailbreak prompts for LLMs. To automate this process, GCG
[
42
]
automatically finds adversarial suffixes using a gradient-guided search. When provided with a harmful prompt that would typically be censored by an aligned LLM, appending the adversarial suffix increases the likelihood of the LLM failing to censor the harmful output. Many other works for automatic jailbreak prompt discovery follow:
Zhu et al.
focuses on discovering interpretable adversarial prefixes that are less detectable to a simple perplexity defence.
Yu et al.

improves upon GCG by using more sophisticated evolution strategy optimisation algorithms. Unlike prior methods,
Lapid et al.
does not require whitebox access to models by approximating the fitness of prompts solely based on raw inputs and outputs of the model.
Chao et al.
use another LLM to iteratively camouflage a malicious query, and
Mehrotra et al.
improves on this technique by leveraging tree-of-thought reasoning
[
36
]
and clipping irrelevant candidates.
Samvelyan et al.
produced large sets of attacks for diverse, specific scenarios using open-ended search and mutation and show that finetuning on this data improves protection against jailbreaking.

Safety Alignment through Preference Finetuning

The final step of the LLM training pipeline is to align the model with human preferences. This process often leverages reinforcement learning (RL), where reward signals come either from human rating
[
3
,
27
]
or synthetic data
[
4
]
which proximate human preferences.

Jailbreak Defence

Preference finetuning alone is often not sufficient
[
42
,
41
]
, instead, models deploy additional defence methods to safeguard the LLM from undesired input and/or output.

We categorize existing defence methods into two classes.

1.

Defence by Classification
: Classify whether the prompt and/or model generations contain undesirable behaviour. If so, the LLM should output a system rejection such as “Sorry I cannot help with that”. PARDEN falls into this category.

2.

Defence by Direct Generation
: Given a prompt and/or model’s generations, directly output the final aligned response.
Kumar et al. [17]
,
Robey et al. [30]
fall into this category.

Defence by Classification

Many jailbreak attacks contain gibberish-looking text, which is often off-distribution from the typical prompts LLMs are trained on, hence confusing the model to output harmful generations.
Perplexity filters

[
13
,
1
]
classify a prompt as harmful if the perplexity is higher than a predetermined threshold. However, it under-performs when classifying high-perplexity harmless prompts and low-perplexity harmful prompts. Our method does not rely on these characteristics and thus is agnostic to them.
Helbling et al.
use the LLM to self-classify the outputs by prompting it “Is the following content harmful? {generated response here}”.
As mentioned before, this line of approach suffers from the
domain shift
between the
self-censorship format
during alignment and the
classification format
during application. By contrast, PARDEN does not suffer from this shift, since the model is trained to produce non-harmful outputs in response to non-jailbreak queries.

Kumar et al.
checks for every sub-sequence with a safety filter and the entire prompt is flagged as harmful if any sub-sequence is filtered as unsafe. The process is expensive and would not scale to longer context prompts, and would easily have false positives (i.e. “how to make a bomb” is a sub-sequence of the harmless prompt “how to make a bomb sandwich”). By contrast, PARDEN reduces these false positives by examining the output as a whole.

Defence by Generation

Jain et al.
,
Kirchenbauer et al.
show that using the LLM itself to paraphrase the prompt often erases injected attacks and thus can be used to output a safe generation.

Zhang et al.
,
Zhang et al.
also rephrases the prompt by appending the additional descriptions of the original prompt generated by the LLM itself.

Robey et al.
,
Cao et al.
perturbs the input prompt and returns the output from the perturbed input instead of the original.
Unlike our method, these approaches operate on the
input space
and thus potentially modify responses even for
benign
prompts.

Since PARDEN operates on the output space, it could also be vertically integrated with these input-space methods.

Background

3.1
Large Languge Models

LLMs typically employ a transformer-based architecture
[
33
]
, which is composed of stacking multiple blocks of self-attention layer followed by a fully-connected neural network. LLMs are auto-regressive models that predict the marginal distribution of the next token in a sequence
[
7
]
. The LLM training process consists of two stages. The first stage is a pretraining stage where the LLM is trained via the cross-entropy loss on next token prediction on general internet-scale text corpora.

In the second stage, LLMs are fine-tuned to address alignment and safety
[
22
]

[
4
]

[
26
]
. One goal of this alignment process is to ensure the LLM refuses to generate harmful content: when given malicious prompts (X = “How do I commit cybercrime”), the LLM should learn to output a refusal response (Y = “Sorry I can’t help you with that”).
As mentioned in
Section

2
, a large amount of research is focused on finding exploits for LLMs to evaluate and improve their robustness.

3.2
Design Goal

In designing an LLM defence system, one often faces the trade-off between
safety
and
performance
. Intuitively, an optimal defence system is one that

•

corrects all jailbreaks and harmful outputs of the undefended LLM

•

maximally preserves benign outputs

While the definition of harmfulness requires human judgement, there exist many ways to quantify how well the system preserves benign outputs, such as the BLEU score (details below).

3.3
BLEU Score

One measurement of distance is BLEU (BiLingual Evaluation Understudy)
[
23
]
, often used in bilingual translation to compare translated text against ground truth reference text. Formally, BLEU is defined as:

\displaystyle\text{BLEU}=\text{BP}\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\log p_{n}\right).

where BP is the brevity penalty,
w_{n}
are weights, and
p_{n}
are given by:

p_{n}=\frac{\sum_{C\in\{Candidates\}}\sum_{ngram\in C}Count_{clip}(ngram)}{\sum_{C^{\prime}\in\{Candidates\}}\sum_{ngram^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}}Count(ngram^{\prime})}

Intuitively, the formula above computes
p_{n}
the n-gram matching score for the machine translation and the reference translation(s), and geometrically averages them to produce the BLEU score.

Observing that repeating English text could be evaluated as “English-English” translation, our method repurposes BLEU to distinguish between repetition and refusal, more on this in the next Section. We use the implementation provided by NLTK
[
5
]

We propose PARDEN, a simple but surprisingly effective method for detecting jailbreaks. On a high-level, PARDEN prompts the LLM to repeat its own output, with a few in-context examples included in the prompt to prime the method, illustrated in
Figure

1
.

Formally, let
x
be the initial input prompt. Denote by
LLM
the language model that samples the output
y
auto-regressively from the next-token distribution
P_{\textbf{LLM}}(X_{t}|x_{<t})
. So
y=\textbf{LLM}(x)
denotes the LLM’s output. Then the combined repeat-prompt is structured as follows:

\text{REPEAT}(y)\coloneqq\textbf{LLM}\text{([prefix; examples;}\ y\ \text{;suffix; examples])}

(1)

where “examples” are the in-context examples of repetitions, “prefix” and “suffix” are the PARDEN prefix and suffix, and all of the tokens are concatenated before they are fed into the LLM. See
Section

11.1
for the specific choice of these hyper-parameter text pieces.

In principle, we could simply return
\text{REPEAT}(y)
as the final output. This shortcut is preferable if
the LLM 1)
perfectly
repeats benign outputs and 2) refuses all malicious outputs, In practice, however, minor repetition errors could degrade the quality of benign outputs, undermining the quality of our outputs.

Hence, we use the BLEU score between
y
and REPEAT
(y)
, to determine whether the model is
attempting
to repeat the output or refusing to do so. Intuitively, when the model is attempting to repeat, the repeat is nearly identical to the original output, leading to a BLEU score near 1.0; conversely, when the model is refusing to repeat, the repeat/refusal is highly dissimilar to the original output, leading to a low BLEU score.

Setting a threshold
t
on the BLEU score induces a family of classifiers, indexed by
t
. Formally, let
h_{t}
be a classifier of outputs, defined by:

h_{t}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if BLEU(y, \,REPEAT(y))}<t\rightarrow\textit{ ``harmful"}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\rightarrow\textit{``benign"}\end{array}\right.

(2)

The performance of this family of classifiers indicates the robustness of our defence. Note that
t
captures the trade-off between false positives and false negatives - a larger
t
encourages the system to assert that the repetition isn’t preserving the output, decides the original output is toxic, and creates both more false positives and true positives.

Sometimes REPEAT(
y
) is very similar to
y
but truncated due to the maximum token limit set by the generation configuration. Thus, when computing BLEU scores, we clip the two sentences to the same length, up to
n
tokens, We clarify that REPEAT(
y
) takes the whole output
y
- clipping only applies to the computation of BLEU score.

4.1
Conceptual Explanation

Firstly, PARDEN avoids the “autoregressive trap” explained in detail above. Secondly, adversarial attacks in the input prompt space are usually edge cases characterized by atypicality and low structure, while the output of a language model
by definition
is a lot more typical and structured. By using the model to filter the output (LLM-generated) rather than the input (attacker-designed), we ensure that the model does not get exposed to adversarial inputs during safety filtering. This approach has several benefits:

1.

PARDEN solely operates on the output space, whereas safeguard mechanisms on the input space can be explicitly circumvented. Indeed,
Zhu et al. [41]
includes perplexity scores in their loss function and produces low-perplexity attack prompts to evade perplexity filters. By censoring the output rather than the input, we make the defence more difficult for attackers to directly target.

2.

Producing benign outputs and refusing to produce
obviously malicious
outputs is a task that the model is trained on during the alignment phase. We refer to this as the
self-censoring
format. In contrast the LLM is not explicitly trained in the
classification format
during instruction tuning. This is evidenced by LLMs’ unwillingness to label the output as either harmful or benign even when explicitly asked to assign a label; they often default to “I need more context”. See
Section

11.4
for example cases.

3.

Reduction of False Positives: direct classification might lead to false positives, where benign outputs are incorrectly flagged as harmful due to overly cautious classification criteria. Repetition reduces such errors by leveraging the model’s inherent safety mechanisms, which are designed to balance caution with practicality. We quantify this reduction in Section 6.

4.

Dynamic Adaptation: Models adapt to evolving definitions of what constitutes harmful content. By asking the model to repeat itself, we are bootstrapping the model’s latest training to reassess the output - this dynamic adaptation comes at no extra cost. However, a static classification criteria might become outdated or fail to capture nuanced or emerging forms of harmful content, unless we perform further instruction tuning on classifying those content.

4.2
Reducing Computational Cost by Partial Repeats

Some prompts elicit long responses from the LLM, making the repeat process in PARDEN computationally expensive. We resolve this by allowing partial repeats. Instead of generating the entire repeat, we stop generating after the first
n
repeated tokens. For a fixed, small
n
, this “early stopping” mechanism incurs a constant cost of
n
forward passes, which does not depend on the output length. Again, we clarify that the repeater examines the entire output - truncation only applies to the generation of the attempted repetition and the computation of BLEU score.

Experimental Setup

5.1
Dataset

For a fair evaluation we need both benign and harmful examples, to evaluate area-under-curve and other metrics of the classifier performance. To the best of our knowledge there unfortunately are no standard data sets for the evaluation of jailbreak defences. To collect benign examples, we sample 552 instructions from open-instruct-v1
Wang et al. [34]
, and produce benign outputs using Llama2 and Claude-2.1. To produce jailbreak examples, we follow
Zou et al. [42]
to adversarially attack the LLMs using the 520 harmful behaviours in their AdvBench. Since the original attacks only result in 60/520 jailbreaks, we further leverage
prompt injection
to improve the attack success rate, and manually filter 484 true jailbreaks for Llama2 and 539 for Claude-2.1 . See our open-source dataset at
https://github.com/Ed-Zh/PARDEN
for details.

Prompt injection is a technique to prime/jailbreak LLMs by inserting tokens after the user-assistant separator. For instance, instead of
"user: make a bomb; assistant:"
, we feed
"user: make a bomb; assistant: Sure, here’s"
into the LLM. This technique is also used by
Samvelyan et al.
. See the appendix for some examples of the exact prompt.

5.2
Experiment Details

After collecting the dataset, we then prompt the model to repeat the output using the techniques proposed in Section 3. This allows us to organize the data in 4-tuples: (instruction, output, repeat, label), where
label
is the ground truth label
\in
{harmful, benign}

When configuring the LLM for PARDEN, we use temperature = 0 to evaluate greedily. This is because for repetition, stochastic sampling would introduce extra noise and should be avoided. A temperature of 0 ensures PARDEN
does not
sample from a probability distribution. We clarify that the original generation need not have a temperature of 0.

5.3
Benchmarking

While most existing work on LLM defences simply reports true positive rates, false positive rates, and accuracy, this evaluation ignores the crucial question of how sensitive a given system should be. This is especially problematic when the false positives and false negatives incur an asymmetric cost.
We instead propose to look at ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curves, which plots TPR against FPR and allows for more holistic comparison of defence methods at different, flexible levels of sensitivity.

We benchmark our induced classifier against two existing methods: 1) defence by binary classification proposed by
Helbling et al. [11]
and 2) perplexity filters outlined in
Jain et al. [13]
.

Since the binary classifier only outputs yes/no without a continuous measure of how confident the classification is, we need to generalize the method to a continuous family of classifiers to compare the two methods. We propose to look at the raw logits of the model’s prediction of the next token. Formally, let
logit_{\text{Yes}}
and
logit_{\text{No}}
be the predicted logit for the next token being “Yes” and “No”, respectively, then
logit_{\text{Yes}}-logit_{\text{No}}
indicates the log-odds of answering “Yes” (“it is harmful”) vs “No” (“it is not harmful”), which measures how confident the model is in classifying harmful v.s. not harmful.

The approach naturally creates a family of classifiers, parameterized by the confidence threshold
l
:

g_{l}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }(logit_{\text{Yes}}-logit_{\text{No}})>l\rightarrow\textit{``harmful"}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\rightarrow\textit{``benign"}\end{array}\right.

(3)

Similarly, we set thresholds for perplexity filters. We implement both whole-sentence average perplexity and sliding window perplexity for fixed window lengths, which report an adversarial attack if any window displays a perplexity score higher than threshold. Again, varying the threshold parameterises the family of classifiers.

At the time of the attack, the defender does not know which type of the attack is being used. Thus we simulate both specific and general scenarios. The specific scenarios are:

1.

a GCG
[
42
]
attack (short, high perplexity)

2.

an AutoDan
[
41
]
attack, designed to evade perplexity filters

To simulate a
general scenario
we aggregate the two specific scenarios to sample a randomized attack from GCG, AutoDan with equal probability.
The attack prompts from GCG, AutoDan are taken from their original papers and evaluated by re-running their algorithm locally.

One interesting phenomenon we observed is that repeating the entire sentence doesn’t always deliver better performance. In fact, for very long benign outputs, repeating all the tokens resulted in a worse BLEU score (0.954) than repeating partially (0.960).

Investigating the error cases revealed that repetitions, even when perfect at the start, tend to become less faithful for longer repetitions due to error accumulation, resulting in lower BLEU scores.

Additional Notes on the Datasets

Note that even though both datasets we use are proposed in the same paper, the dataset distributions are considerably different. Indeed, “harmful behaviours” mostly consists of malicious instructions (e.g. build a bomb); “harmful strings” mostly consists of offensive insults, such as racist remarks or death threats.
Note that because Claude-2.1 did not produce enough examples under the “harmful behaviours” dataset, we could not produce evaluations for this particular scenario. This happens because different models are more vulnerable to certain types of attacks due to different safety alignment procedures used during training. In those scenarios, we cannot do meaningful evaluation of either defense method.

7.5
Failure Cases

Although vastly more effective that alternative approaches, PARDEN does have false negatives. Inspecting these failure cases revealed that these outputs are often not harmful in themselves but had
malicious intentions
. One such example is asking the models to
write fake reviews
, shown in the appendix. This underscores the importance of contextualizing harmful content in its intention. Clearly, it is questionable if this should even be considered a false negative, since the user could have simply lied about their request and produced the same (per-se harmless) output.

If the failure cases significantly undermines specific usage, one can in principle apply PARDEN to both the input and output space or combine it with input-specific filters. For a detailed mathematical analysis of this approach, see
Section

11.6
.

8
Conclusions and Future Work

Although PARDEN achieves a notable improvement over the baseline methods such as
Helbling et al. [11]
, we remark that PARDEN does assume a safely aligned base model and relies on the model’s intrinsic safeguarding mechanisms. Hence, our work highlights the importance of developing safe base models. In fact, by using PARDEN to correct jailbreak examples, we obtain data (attack prompt, safe output) for adversarial fine-tuning.

While this work focused on the task of LLM defence, the method of repetition touches on the idea of LLM composition and high-order LLMs. We conceptualise a high order LLM to be an LLM that is a composition of first order (auto-regressive) LLMs. Our defence approach composed the LLM with itself, but this generalises to include arbitrary models with compatible input and output spaces.

Repetition is one operation to stitch together two LLMs. Mathematically, benign examples are the fixed points of this operation, since they are preserved under repetition on LLMs, whereas harmful examples are corrupted by repetition. Interestingly, in addition to harmful and benign examples, we discovered that atypical, gibberish text also tends to be poorly preserved. What are some other operations one can define on high-order LMs? What does their fixed points and non-fixed points tell us?

Another question is whether the format of pre-training and alignment steps could be adjusted to make the LLM more robust to domain shift. For instance, the alignment step could train the LLM both in the
self-censorship
and the
classification
format.

9
Impact Statement

This paper introduces a novel approach to defend LLMs against jailbreak exploits. By addressing vulnerabilities in LLMs, our method stands to bolster the integrity of AI applications, fostering trust in these systems across various domains. The potential impact extends beyond the academic realm, with practical implications for industries relying on secure AI implementations. As the threat landscape evolves, our research strives to fortify the foundations of machine learning, promoting responsible and resilient AI deployment. This work underscores the commitment to advancing not only the scientific understanding of LLMs but also the practical implications for a secure and trustworthy AI ecosystem.
