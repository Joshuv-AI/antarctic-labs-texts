Some constructions of Weinstein manifolds with chaotic Reeb dynamics
Laurent Côté

Abstract
. The goal of this note is to describe some constructions of Weinstein manifolds with chaotic Reeb dynamics, and to explain how this property can sometimes be detected directly from the skeleton.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Some problems and questions

Let
X
be a Liouville manifold. For the purpose of studying Reeb dynamics on
\partial_{\infty}X
, it is often useful to consider the
growth rate
of Floer theoretic invariants (such as symplectic cohomology, wrapped Floer cohomology, contact homology…) as a function of the action. This growth rate a priori depends on some choices, but one can show that it is well-defined up to a natural notion of scaling equivalence.

When the growth rate is exponential, this tends to force the Reeb dynamics to be chaotic. This heuristic is made precise e.g. in work of Macarini–Schlenk
[
21
]
, Alves
[
4
]
, Alves–Meiwes
[
3
]
and others. McLean
[
23
,
24
]
(building on work of Seidel
[
29
]
) also showed that
SH(X)
(and
HW(K,L)
) cannot grow super-polynomially if
X
is symplectomorphic to an affine variety; this ultimately comes from the fact that affine varieties can be shown to admit a nice contact form at infinity having tame Reeb dynamics.

These phenomena are now well-understood. Nevertheless, various questions remain which are (to the author’s taste) rather natural. Here are some closely related problems and questions which will be taken up in this note.

(1)

Describe conditions on the skeleton of a Weinstein manifold
X
which guarantee that
X
is not symplectomorphic to an affine variety.

(2)

Suppose that
X
is obtained by attaching a
critical
handle to
T^{*}M
, where
M
is a closed hyperbolic manifold. Describe conditions on the attaching sphere which ensure that
X
is not symplectomorphic to an affine variety.

(3)

Construct a Weinstein manifold which is not symplectomorphic to an affine variety and which does not contain a closed exact Lagrangian.

(4)

Does there exist a contact structure
\xi_{\operatorname{exotic}}
on
S^{5}
with the property that for every contact form
\operatorname{ker}\alpha=\xi_{\operatorname{exotic}}
, the associated Reeb flow has positive topological entropy?

(5)

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
be a Lefschetz fibration on a Weinstein manifold. Consider the partially wrapped Fukaya category
\mathcal{W}(X,f^{-1}(\infty))
. This is a smooth and proper category whose Serre functor
\mathbf{W}[-1]
was described by Sylvan
[
30
]
. Describe conditions on
X
(independent of
f
) which guarantee that the Serre functor has positive categorical entropy.

1.2.
What is known?

Concerning (
1
), it is known that
\operatorname{skel}(X,\lambda)=M
implies that
(X,\lambda)=(T^{*}M,\lambda_{\operatorname{std}})
. When
M
is a hyperbolic (or rationally hyperbolic) manifold, it follows from work of McLean
[
23
]
that
X
is not symplectomorphic to an affine variety. On the opposite end of the spectrum, it is not difficult to show that
any closed manifold
of real dimension
n
can be embedded in the skeleton of a smooth affine variety of complex dimension
n
(see
Proposition

2.9
). To the best of our knowledge, nothing is known beyond these two extreme cases.

Concerning (
2
) and (
3
), McLean
[
23
]
has constructed many examples of Weinstein manifolds which are not symplectomorphic to affine varieties. To the best of our knowledge, all of these examples are built as subcritical handle attachments on cotangent bundles (or products of these).

Concerning (
4
), Alves and Meiwes
[
3
]
proved the analogous result on
S^{2n+1}
for all
n>2
. However, the case
n=2
remained open. (The analogous statement when
n=1
is expected to be false.)

Concerning (
5
), it was observed in
[
8
]
(see Ex. 8.8 and Sec. 8.2 in
loc. cit.
) that this property holds for cotangent bundles of hyperbolic and rationally hyperbolic manifolds.

1.3.
The contribution of this note

The goal of this note is to study the above questions. The main reason why we are able to say anything new is mostly thanks to the recent work of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende
[
16
]
on the foundations of wrapped Fukaya categories, which we combine with ideas of other authors including McLean
[
24
]
and Alves–Meiwes
[
3
]
.

As mentioned above, the standard approach for studying questions of this type is to consider an action filtration on either symplectic cohomology or wrapped Floer cohomology. One then studies the growth of the hom-spaces with respect to this filtration. When
X=T^{*}M
, the action filtrations on
SH/HW
match up with the action filtrations on
\mathcal{L}M/\Omega M
induced by a choice of metric on
M
, which allows one to perform many computations. However, to go beyond cotangent bundles, one needs to understand how the action filtration behaves under various operations. This is actually quite hard to do. For symplectic cohomology, McLean
[
22
]
described the effect of subcritical handle attachments and products on the filtered growth, and this was already non-trivial. Trying to redo the entire package of
[
16
]
in the filtered setting seems like a rather daunting undertaking.

Instead of doing this, we use a basic but important observation due to Alves and Meiwes
[
3
]
, namely that one can bound the action-filtered growth of wrapped Floer cohomology from below in terms of the internal multiplicative growth. This is a direct consequence of the fact that the action filtration is sublinear with respect to multiplication. However, the multiplicative growth is a purely algebraic invariant which does not need a filtration, so it is very well behaved under the package of operations in
[
16
]
.

1.4.
Context and acknowledgements

This note replaces a previous paper which was roughly three times longer, so let us explain what changed. In the previous version, the aim was to define a categorical notion of “algebraic hyperbolicity” which could be used to detect chaotic Reeb dynamics and would be well-behaved under various natural operations. However, we subsequently realized that essentially all of the applications can be obtained (and in some cases improved) by much simpler arguments. We also found various errors in the previous version, some of which seem serious. Since the categorical approach appears to be a distraction, we decided to abandon it entirely.

2.
Main definitions

2.1.
Topological and categorical entropy

The topological entropy is one of the simplest measures of complexity of a dynamical system. Intuitively, a system with positive topological entropy can be viewed as chaotic.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space. Let
\Phi=\{\phi_{t}\}
be a one-parameter family of homeomorphisms and consider the family of metrics

(2.1)

d^{\Phi}_{T}(x,y)=\max_{0\leq t\leq T}d(\phi_{t}x,\phi_{t}y)

depending on
T>0
. Let
B_{\Phi}(x,\epsilon,T)=\{y\in X\mid d^{\Phi}_{T}(x,y)\}<\epsilon
be the ball of radius
\epsilon
centered at
x
. Now let
S_{d}(\Phi,\epsilon,T)\in\mathbb{N}
be defined as the smallest number of balls of radius
\epsilon
needed to cover
X
. Set
h_{d}(\Phi,\epsilon):=\operatorname{limsup}_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\operatorname{log}S_{d}(\Phi,\epsilon,T)
.

The
topological entropy
of the flow
\Phi=\{\phi_{t}\}
on the compact metric space
(X,d)
is

(2.2)

h(\Phi)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}h_{d}(\Phi,\epsilon).

It can be shown
[
19
, Sec. 3.1]
that this quantity only depends on
\Phi
and the topology induced by
d
.

Example 2.1
.

It follows from the definition that
h(\Phi)=0
if
\phi_{t}
is an isometry for all
t
, or if it is periodic. More surprisingly, it can be shown
[
19
, Sec. 3.3]
that the topological entropy of any gradient flow on a compact manifold vanishes. In contrast, the geodesic flow on a connected, orientable surface
\Sigma
endowed with a metric of constant curvature has positive topological entropy if
g\geq 2
and vanishing topological entropy otherwise
[
27
, Sec. 3.2.5]
.

There is a categorical analog of the topological entropy which has introduced by Dimitrov–Haiden–Kontsevich–Katzarkov
[
10
]
. Given a smooth an proper
A_{\infty}
category
\mathcal{C}
and an endofunctor
F
, the
(categorical) entropy
of
F
is the quantity:

(2.3)

h(F):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\log\operatorname{dim}\hom_{H(\mathcal{C})}(G,F^{n}G)\in[-\infty,\infty)

where
G
is any (split)-generator of
\mathcal{C}
. This quantity can be shown to be independent of the choice of
G
. (More generally, the categorical entropy can be defined for any endofunctor acting on a triangulated category.)

2.2.
Liouville manifolds

We assume that the reader is familiear with the definition of Liouville manifolds/sectors and their wrapped Fukaya category; see
[
14
]
for the relevant background. Unless otherwise indicated, we will always consider the wrapped Fukaya category with
\mathbb{Z}/2
-gradings and
\mathbb{Q}
-coefficients. In particular, all Liouville manifolds/sectors and all Lagrangians in the Fukaya category are equipped with appropriate orientation/grading data. (The reason for taking
\mathbb{Q}
-coefficients is that we sometimes want to appeal to computations from rational homotopy theory.)

2.3.
Multiplicative growth

Let
A
be an associative
k
-algebra. Given a finite set of morphisms
\Sigma\subseteq\operatorname{Mor}(\mathcal{C})
and an integer
n\geq 1
, let
W_{\Sigma}(n)
be the span of all composable words of length at most
n
in the elements of
\Sigma
, that is

(2.4)

W_{\Sigma}(n)=\operatorname{span}_{k}\{a_{1}\dots a_{l}\mid a_{i}\in\Sigma,l\leq n\}\subseteq A.

Definition 2.2
.

An associative algebra
A
is said to have
exponential (multiplicative) growth
if

(2.5)

\operatorname{limsup}_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log\dim_{k}W_{\Sigma}(n)>0

for some finite set of elements
\Sigma\subseteq A
. An object
K
in an
A_{\infty}
category
\mathcal{C}
is said to have exponential algebraic growth if
H^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C})(K,K)
has exponential growth.

Example 2.3
.

A group
G
has exponential growth (in the usual sense of e.g.
[
18
, Sec. 1]
) iff the group ring
\mathbb{Z}[G]
has exponential multiplicative growth.

We now introduce the following terminology:

Definition 2.4
.

A Liouville sector is said to be
big
is there exists a Lagrangian disk
K\in\mathcal{W}(X)
(equipped with appropriate orientation/grading data) such that
HW^{\bullet}(K,K)
has exponential multiplicative growth.

The terminology “big” is intended to be purely auxiliary (i.e. the reason for introducing this adjective is to make certain statements in this paper less wordy, and we trust that it will not cause confusion with unrelated notions of big-ness in other areas of mathematics.)

The easiest examples of big Liouville sectors are the following cotangent bundles.

Example 2.5
.

Let
M
be an aspherical compact manifold with boundary. If
\pi_{1}(M)
has exponential growth, then
T^{*}M
is big. Examples include: any closed manifold admitting a metric with all sectional curvatures strictly less than zero (see
[
25
, Thm. 2]
).

Example 2.6
.

Let
M
be a (say simply-connected, spin) manifold and let
F\subseteq T^{*}M
be a cotangent fiber. Then
CW^{\bullet}(F,F)\simeq C_{-\bullet}(\Omega M;\mathbb{Q})
. Hence
T^{*}M
is big if
H_{\bullet}(\Omega M;\mathbb{Q})
has exponential multiplicative growth. Morally, one expects this property to hold whenever
M
is rationally hyperbolic; this would in particular follow from a positive solution to the celebrated Avramov–Félix conjecture
[
12
, Sec. 39.4]
. Here are some additional assumptions on
M
which ensure that
H_{\bullet}(\Omega M;\mathbb{Q})
has exponential multiplicative growth (and hence
T^{*}M
is big):

(i)

M
has the rational homotopy type of a wedge of
m\geq 2
spheres (see
[
12
, Sec. 33(c); Ex. 1]
);

(ii)

H_{2i}(M;k)=0
for all
i>0
and
M
does not have the rational homotopy type of a sphere (see
[
12
, Sec. 24(c)]
);

(iii)

M
is a connected sum of spin manifolds, each of whose rational cohomology algebra requires at least two generators (see
[
13
, Cor. 3.4 and Rmk. 3.5]
);

(iv)

M
is rationally hyperbolic and
H_{\bullet}(\Omega X;k)
is finitely generated as an algebra.

2.4.
Consequences of exponential multiplicative growth

The following theorem is a restatement of work of McLean
[
24
]
, Alves–Meiwes
[
3
]
and the author and Kartal
[
8
]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose that
(X,\lambda)
is big. Then:

(1)

X
is not symplectomorphic to an affine variety;

(2)

\partial_{\infty}X
is not contactomorphic to the link of an isolated complex singularity;

(3)

for any contact form on
(\partial_{\infty}X,\operatorname{ker}\lambda)
, the Reeb flow has positive topological entropy;

(4)

if
X
is Weinstein and
\mathfrak{f}\subseteq\partial_{\infty}X
is the page of a Weinstein open book, then
\mathbf{W}^{-1},\mathbf{M}^{-1}
have positive categorical entropy.

The functors
\mathbf{W}^{-1}:\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{W}(X,\mathfrak{f})
and
\mathbf{M}^{-1}:\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{W}(\hat{\mathfrak{f}})\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{W}(\hat{\mathfrak{f}})
were described by Sylvan
[
30
]
and are, respectively, the “wrap-once negatively” and “inverse monodromy” auto-equivalences. Modulo some folkloric compatibility statements,
\mathbf{W}^{-1}
is the Serre functor of the Fukaya–Seidel category
\mathcal{W}(X,\mathfrak{f})
(as a consequence of
\mathbf{W}^{-1}
being a Serre functor,
\mathbf{W}
has positive entropy iff
\mathbf{W}^{-1}
does; see the discussion in
[
8
, Sec. 8.2]
).

Proof.

(
3
) was proved by Alves–Meiwes in
[
3
, Thm. 1.7]
. Let us consider (
4
). First, we endow
\mathcal{W}(X)
with an integral action filtration according to
[
8
, Def. 5.1]
. (The idea is to present the wrapped Fukaya category as the localization of some (homologically smooth and proper) partially wrapped Fukaya category, and to use the filtration induced by this presentation). Concretely, this means that for every
K,L\in\mathcal{W}(X)
, there is a filtration
F^{p}HW^{\bullet}(K,L)
which is subadditive with respect to multiplication. The assumption that
X
is big immediately implies that
F^{p}HW^{\bullet}(K,K)
grows exponentially for some Lagrangian disk
K
(by subadditivity). This implies (
4
) by
[
8
, (8.9), (8.9)]
(note that the growth of
F^{p}HW^{\bullet}(K,K)
is denoted by
\gamma_{K,L}
in
[
8
]
).

Let
H
be a (say time-independent) Hamiltonian which is cylindrical at infinity with positive slope and let
\phi_{nH}
denote the flow of
nH
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then it is proved in
[
8
, Thm. 7.2]
that the growth of the sequence
\gamma^{ham}_{K,L}:=\operatorname{dim}(HF^{\bullet}(\phi_{nH}K,L)\to HW^{\bullet}(K,L))
agrees (up to shift and scaling) with the growth of
F^{p}HW^{\bullet}(K,L)
. Now
\gamma^{ham}_{K,L}
is precisely the quantity considered by
[
24
, Def. 2.4]
, so (
1
) and (
2
) follow from
[
24
, Thm. 1.2]
.
∎

2.5.
Miscellaneous facts

The following proposition follows from
[
2
, Thm. 3.27]
(note however that this particular statement certainly does not need
[
2
]
; it is for instance already contained in Sec. 12.3 in
[
7
]
).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(X,\lambda)
be a Weinstein manifold. Let
M
be a compact manifold (possibly with boundary) and suppose that
i:M\to\operatorname{Skel}(X,\lambda)
is a smooth embedding into the Lagrangian stratum. Then after possibly deforming
\lambda
in a compact set,
i
is covered by an inclusion of Liouville sectors
\tilde{i}(T^{*}M,\lambda_{\operatorname{std}})\hookrightarrow(X,\lambda)
.
∎

In particular, it follows from
Proposition

2.8
that any point in the Lagrangian stratum is contained the image of some sectorial embedding
(T^{*}M,\lambda_{\operatorname{std}})\to(X,\lambda)
after possibly deforming
\lambda
. Note that the proof of
Proposition

2.8
uses the assumption that
(X,\lambda)
is Weinstein in an essential way. It is unknown whether a similar result holds for arbitrary Liouville manifolds.

The next proposition, which was promised earlier, shows that
any
closed
n
-manifold embeds into the skeleton of a smooth affine variety of complex dimension
n
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
M
be a closed manifold of real dimension
n
. For any
N\geq 2n+1
, there exists a smooth affine variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{N}
of complex dimension
n
with the following properties:

•

(X,\lambda|_{X})
is a Weinstein manifold, where
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}dy_{i}-y_{i}dx_{i})
is the standard radial Liouville form;

•

M
is diffeomorphic to a connected component of the skeleton
\operatorname{Skel}(X,\lambda|_{X})
.

Proof.

According to a classical theorem of Nash (see
[
20
, Thms. 2 and 3]
), there exists a collection
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
of real polynomials in
N
variables such that
M
is a connected component of the zero set
V_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Viewing the
f_{i}
as complex polynomials, we set
X:=V_{\mathbb{C}}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
. After possibly perturbing the
f_{i}
, we may assume that
X
is a smooth complex algebraic variety and that the function
\phi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=|z_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|z_{n}|^{2}
is Morse and exhausting. One can check that
\lambda=d^{\mathbb{C}}\phi
, from which it follows that
(X,\lambda|_{X},\phi)
is a Weinstein structure (see
[
7
, Sec. 1.1]
).

Observe that
X
is stable under complex conjugation, which sends
\lambda\mapsto-\lambda
and
d\lambda\mapsto-d\lambda
. It follows that the Liouville vector field
v_{X}
on
(X,\lambda|_{X})
is preserved by complex conjugation. In particular,
v_{X}
is tangent to the real locus
X_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq X
. Since
M
is a connected component of
X_{\mathbb{R}}
, it follows immediately that
M
is contained in the skeleton of
(X,\lambda|_{X})
. It is similarly straightforward to check that
X
has complex dimension
n
: indeed, since
T_{m}X
is also fixed by complex-conjugation for any
x\in M
, it follows that
\operatorname{dim}T_{m}X=2\operatorname{dim}T_{m}M
.
∎

3.
Operations which preserve multiplicative growth

3.1.
Subcritical and flexible handle attachments

Given a Liouville sector
(X,\lambda)
of dimension
2n
, recall that a Liouville hypersurface
F\subseteq\partial X
is said to be
subcritical
if its its skeleton is contained in the smooth image of a second countable manifold of dimension
<n-1
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(X_{1},\lambda_{1})
and
(X_{2},\lambda_{2})
be Liouville manifolds. Suppose that
(X,\lambda)
is a Liouville manifold obtained by gluing
X_{1},X_{2}
along a subcritical Weinstein hypersurface
F
. Then

(3.1)

\mathcal{W}(X_{1})\sqcup\mathcal{W}(X_{2})\to\mathcal{W}(X)

is a pre-triangulated equivalence. Hence
\mathcal{W}(X)
is big if
\mathcal{W}(X_{1})
or
\mathcal{W}(X_{2})
is big.

Proof.

Since
F
is subcritical we have
\mathcal{W}(F)=0
. We also have
\mathcal{W}(X_{i},\mathfrak{c}_{F})=\mathcal{W}(X_{i})
by stop removal (see
[
16
, Thm. 1.16]
). Now apply
[
16
, Thm. 1.20]
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,\lambda)
be a Weinstein manifold and let
F\subseteq\partial_{\infty}X
be a Liouville hypesurface. Suppose that
\operatorname{Skel}(F)\subseteq\partial_{\infty}X
is a (smooth) sphere and let
D\subseteq\mathcal{W}(X,\Lambda)
be the linking disk. If
\Lambda
is a loose Legendrian sphere, then
CW^{\bullet}(D,D)_{X,\Lambda}
is acyclic.

Proof.

This following from work of Ekholm–Lekili
[
11
]
who prove (see Thm. 2 and the surrounding discussion) that
CW^{\bullet}(D,D)_{X,\Lambda}
is isomorphic to the Chekanov–Eliashberg dg algebra of
\Lambda
with loop-space coefficients, which is acyclic since
\Lambda
is loose. (An alternative argument, which has the advantage of remaining entirely in the framework of
[
16
]
, will appear in forthcoming work of O. Lazarev.)
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(X_{1},\lambda_{1})
and
(X_{2},\lambda_{2})
be Liouville manifolds. Suppose that
(X,\lambda)
is obtained by gluing
X_{1},X_{2}
along the a Weinstein hypersurface
F
. If
\operatorname{Skel}(F)\subseteq\partial X_{1}
is a loose Legendrian sphere
\Lambda
and
(X_{1},\lambda_{1})
is Weinstein up to deformation, then there exists a fully faithful embedding
\mathcal{W}(X_{1})\to\mathcal{W}(X)
. Hence
\mathcal{W}(X)
is big if
\mathcal{W}(X_{1})
is big.

Proof.

Since
F
is Weinstein,
F=T^{*}\Lambda
up to deformation. The map
\mathcal{W}(F)\to\mathcal{W}(\check{X}_{1})=\mathcal{W}(X_{1},F)=\mathcal{W}(X_{1},\Lambda)
sends the cotangent fiber to the linking disk of
\Lambda
. In particular
\mathcal{W}(F)\to\mathcal{W}(\check{X}_{1})
has acyclic image according to
Lemma

3.2
. The proposition follows. ∎

3.2.
Products

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X_{0},X_{1}
be Weinstein manifolds and let
X=X_{0}\times X_{1}
. Suppose that the following two conditions hold:

•

X_{0}
or
X_{1}
is big;

•

\mathcal{W}(X_{0})
and
\mathcal{W}(X_{1})
are both nonzero (or equivalently,
SH(X_{0}),SH(X_{1})
are nonzero by
[
17
]
).

Then
X
is big.

Proof.

According to
[
16
, Thm. 1.5]
, there is a fully faithful embedding

(3.2)

\mathcal{W}(X)\otimes\mathcal{W}(X^{\prime})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{W}(X\times X^{\prime}).

If
X_{0}
is big, then there exists a disk
K\in\mathcal{W}(X)
with exponential multiplicative growth. By assumption (ii),
\mathcal{W}(X_{1})
is non-zero and Weinstein. Hence, since Weinstein manifolds are generated by cocores (by deep work of Chantraine–Dimitroglou Rizell–Ghiggini–Golovko
[
6
]
and independently Ganatra–Pardon–Shende
[
16
]
), it follows that there exists a cocore disk
L\in\mathcal{W}(X_{1})
such that
HW(L_{1},L_{1})\neq 0
. Now
HW^{\bullet}(K\times L,K\times L)
contains
HW^{\bullet}(K,K)\otimes HW^{\bullet}(L,L)
as a subalgebra by (
3.2
), and hence contains
HW^{\bullet}(K,K)=HW^{\bullet}(K,K)\otimes e_{L}
as a subalgebra. This proves the claim.
∎

3.3.
Covariant functoriality

Recall that a Liouvile domain is just the truncation of a Liouville manifold. Similarly, the truncation of a Liouville sector shall be called a (sectorial)
Liouville domain with corners
. More precisely, a (sectorial) Liouville domain with corners is an exact symplectic manifold with corners
(V,\lambda)
whose boundary admits a decomposition
\partial V=\partial_{h}V\cup\partial_{v}V
. We require that
(\partial_{h}V,\lambda)
is a contact manifold with convex boundary. We also require that the Liouville flow preserve
V
in a neighborhood of
\partial_{h}V
, so that there is a natural decomposition
([-\epsilon,0]\times\partial_{h}V)
.

Let
j:X_{0}^{\operatorname{in}}\hookrightarrow X_{0}
be an inclusion of (honest) Liouville domains (i.e. a proper embedding which strictly pulls back the Liouville form). Let
X^{\operatorname{in}}
(resp.
X
) denote their completions. Note that
X^{\operatorname{in}}
does not in general admit a proper embedding into
X
.

Suppose that
i^{\operatorname{in}}:V\hookrightarrow X_{0}^{\operatorname{in}}
and
i:V\hookrightarrow X_{0}
are inclusions of Liouville domains with corners with the following properties: (i)
i=j\circ i^{\operatorname{in}}
; (ii)
i^{\operatorname{in}}
and
i
induce inclusions of Liouville sectors
\hat{V}\hookrightarrow X^{\operatorname{in}}
(resp.
\hat{V}\hookrightarrow X
).

Proposition 3.5
.

Under the above hypotheses, the following diagram commutes

(3.3)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{W}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{W}(\hat{V})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{W}(X^{\operatorname{in}})}

Proof.

The wrapped Fukaya category is functorial under inclusions of stopped Liouville sectors. But the Viterbo restriction functor is constructed in
[
16
, Sec. 8.2]
as a composition of such inclusions.
∎

Corollary 3.6

(of
Proposition

3.5
)
.

If there exists a Lagrangian disk
L\in\mathcal{W}(\hat{V})
such that the image of
HW_{\mathcal{W}(\hat{V})}(L,L)\to HW_{\mathcal{W}(X_{0})}(L,L)
has exponential multiplicative growth, then
(X,\lambda)
is big.
∎

4.
Applications

4.1.
Recognizing big Weinstein manifolds from their skeleton

The following proposition gives a criterion to check that a Weinstein manifold is big in terms of the stratified topology of its skeleton.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(X,\lambda)
be a Weinstein manifold of dimension
2n
. Let
M
be a closed, connected manifold of dimension
n
, possibly with boundary. Suppose that there exists a smooth embedding
f:M\to X
which satisfies the following two conditions:

(i)

the image of
f
is contained in the (open!) Lagrangian stratum of
\operatorname{Skel}(X,\lambda)
;

(ii)

there is an extension

(4.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{f}}

where
\tilde{f}:K\to X
is an exact Lagrangian embedding.

If the image of the induced map
H_{\bullet}(\Omega M;\mathbb{Q})\to H_{\bullet}(\Omega K;\mathbb{Q})
has exponential multiplicative growth, then
X
is big.

Figure 4.1.
This a priori complicated arboreal skeleton admits an obvious embedding of the genus
2
surface with the shaded region removed. Its Weinstein thickening (which exists in light of
[
2
]
) can thus be recognized as big by
Proposition

4.1
.

Proof.

Applying
Proposition

2.8
, we may assume (after possibly deforming
\lambda
while keeping
\operatorname{skel}(X,\lambda)
fixed) that
f
extends to a
strict
Liouville sector embedding
\sigma:T^{*}M\hookrightarrow X
. By the relative version of Moser’s theorem (and after possibly slightly shrinking
M
inwards and further deforming
\lambda
),
\tilde{f}
extends to a strict Liouville embedding
\tilde{\sigma}:X_{0}:=T^{*}_{\leq 1}K\hookrightarrow X
which agrees with
\sigma
on their common domain of definition. We are now in the setting of
Proposition

3.5
with
V=T^{*}M
. The result thus follows from
Corollary

3.6
and the standard isomorphism between the wrapped Floer theory of a cotangent fiber and the homology of the based loopspace (see e.g.
[
15
, Cor. 6.1]
for the most general version, where it is also shown that this isomorphism is compatible with codimension zero inclusions).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Both assumptions in the statement of
Proposition

4.1
are needed. Indeed,
Proposition

2.9
implies that any manifold whatsoever can be embedded in the skeleton of an algebraic variety (which is therefore not big): this explains why one must impose that the image of
f
is contained in the Lagrangian stratum of
\operatorname{Skel}(X,\lambda)
. To see that (ii) is also needed, let
N
be a closed hyperbolic manifold of dimension
>2
and let
g
be a Morse function with a single maximum. Consider the induced handle decomposition on
X=T^{*}N
. Now let
X^{\prime}
be obtained by “loosifying” the top handle attachment, that is, by attaching the top handle along a stabilization of the original Legendrian knot. Then the skeleton of
X^{\prime}
admits an embedding of
N-B
into the Lagrangian stratum. However,
X^{\prime}
is a flexible Weinstein manifold, so its wrapped Fukaya category is zero. In particular, it is not big.

Example 4.3
.

Suppose
M
is a compact hyperbolic manifold (more generally,
M
admits a metric with all sectional curvatures strictly less than zero). Then
H^{\bullet}(\Omega_{*}(M-D))\to H^{\bullet}(\Omega_{*}M)=k[\pi_{1}(M)]
is surjective. Now
k[\pi_{1}(M)]
has exponential multiplicative growth by
Example

2.5
. Hence
X
is big.

Example 4.4
.

Suppose
M
is a rationally hyperbolic compact spin manifold whose rational cohomology algebra requires at least
2
generators, and such that
H_{*}(\Omega M;\mathbb{Q})
has exponential multiplicative growth. (Morally, “most” spin manifolds satisfy this condition; to get an explicit example, take (i) or (iii) in
Example

2.6
).

According to
[
13
, Thm. 3.3]
, the inclusion
M-D\to M
induces a surjection
\pi_{*}(\Omega(M-D))\otimes\mathbb{Q}\to\pi_{*}(\Omega M)\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. Passing to the universal enveloping algebra, we get a surjection of associative algebras
H_{*}(\Omega(M-D);\mathbb{Q})\to H_{*}(\Omega M;\mathbb{Q})
(the universal enveloping algebra functor is right exact). Hence
X
is big.

Example 4.5

(Critical handle attachments)
.

Proposition

4.1
immediately allows one to detect critical handle attachments which do not destroy “bigness”. For example, suppose that
M
is hyperbolic and let
X
be obtained by attaching a critical handle to
T^{*}M
. If the front projection of the attaching sphere lands inside a disk, then it follows from
Example

4.3
that
X
is big. (More generally, the same conclusion holds if the front projection is contained in an open set
U\subseteq M
such that
\pi_{1}(M-U)
has exponential growth).

4.2.
Plumbings and immersions

As an illustration of
Proposition

4.1
, we consider skeleta with “simple normal crossing” singularities. It will be convenient to let
\mathscr{E}
denote the set of all closed manifolds which satisfy the assumptions of
Example

4.3
or
Example

4.4
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose that
(X,\lambda)
is Weinstein and that
\operatorname{Skel}(X,\lambda)
is a union of closed Lagrangian submanifolds
L_{1},\dots,L_{l}
with the property that
L_{i}\bigcap(\bigcup_{j\neq i}L_{i})\subseteq L_{i}
is contained in a ball. If
L_{i}\in\mathscr{E}
for some
i
, then
(X,\lambda)
is big.

Proof.

Up to reordering the
L_{i}
, we may assume that
L_{1}\in\mathscr{E}
. By assumption, there exists a ball
B\subseteq L_{1}
such that
B
contains
L_{1}\bigcap(\bigcup_{i>1}L_{i})\subseteq L_{1}
. Then the conditions of
Proposition

4.1
are satisfied (take
f
to be the identity map and let
K=L_{1}
).
∎

Example 4.7

(Plumbings)
.

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{l}
be closed manifolds and let
X
be the plumbing of the cotangent bundles of the
M_{i}
according to any graph. Then
(X,\lambda)
satisfies the assumptions of
Proposition

4.6
. In particular,
(X,\lambda)
is big if
M_{i}\in\mathscr{E}
for some
i
.

In particular, a plumbing of cotangent bundles, one of which is (say) hyperbolic, is not symplectomorphic to an affine variety.

Another rational natural class of skeleta arise from immersions. Here also,
Proposition

4.1
can be applied rather easily. For example:

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that
(X,\lambda)
is Weinstein and that
\operatorname{Skel}(X,\lambda)
is the image of a Lagrangian immersion
f:\Sigma_{g}\to(X,\lambda)
with only simple double points, for
g\geq 2
. Then
(X,\lambda)
is big.

Proof.

Let
B\subseteq\Sigma_{g}
be a ball which contains the preimages of all the double points. Let
K
be obtained by performing Polterovich surgery on the double points. Then
K
is a (possibly non-orientable) Riemann surface which contains
\Sigma_{g}-B
. By van Kampen, the image of the inclusion
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g}-B)\to\pi_{1}(K)
has exponential growth. Now apply
Proposition

4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

The skeleton of the affine variety
\mathbb{C}^{2}-\{xy=1\}
is the
Whitney immersion
, which is an immersion of the
2
-sphere with a single double point (see e.g.
[
9
]
). In contrast, it follows from
Proposition

4.8
that the skeleton of an algebraic variety cannot be the image of an immersion of a surface of genus greater than
2
. We do not know whether there exists an algebraic variety whose skeleton is an immersed torus with at least one double point (of course, both
T^{*}S^{2}
and
T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{2}
are symplectomorphic to affine varieties). We remark that given any immersion
M\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with only simple double points, it is straightforward to construct a Weinstein manifold whose skeleton is the image of
f
.

4.3.
A big Weinstein manifold having no exact Lagrangians

For any
n\geq 6
, Abouzaid and Seidel
[
1
, Thm. 1.3]
constructed infinitely many examples of Weinstein manifolds of dimension
2n
whose symplectic cohomology with
\mathbb{Z}/2
-coefficients vanishes but whose symplectic cohomology with
\mathbb{Z}
-coefficients (and hence also with
k
-coefficients) is nonzero.

Let
X
be such a Weinstein manifold and let
Y
be a big Weinstein manifold. Then
X\times Y
is big by
Proposition

3.4
. On the other hand, the Künneth theorem for symplectic cohomology (see
[
26
]
) implies that
SH^{\bullet}(X\times Y;\mathbb{Z}/2)=SH^{\bullet}(X;\mathbb{Z}/2)\otimes SH^{\bullet}(Y;\mathbb{Z}/2)=0.
It follows by Viterbo restriction for symplectic cohomology with
\mathbb{Z}/2
-coefficients that
X\times Y
does not contain a closed exact Lagrangian.

4.4.
A big Weinstein manifold with boundary
S^{5}

Most of the arguments below were explained to the author by Marcelo Alves, and are drawn from
[
3
, Sec. 7.3]
. Consider the Brieskorn manifold
\Sigma(2,3,7)
. This is an aspherical integral homology
3
-sphere;
\pi_{1}(\Sigma(2,3,7))
can be generated by two elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
, and this group has exponential growth (see
[
3
, Sec. 7.3]
).

We consider
M:=S^{*}\Sigma(2,3,7)=\Sigma(2,3,7)\times S^{2}
(recall that all orientable
3
-manifolds are parallelizable). Let
Q=D^{*}\Sigma(2,3,7)
. Choose isotropics representing
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
. Now attach handles along
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
. Let
Q^{\prime}
be the resulting Liouville domain, let
M^{\prime}=\partial Q^{\prime}
be the surgered contact manifold, and let
W
be the trace of the surgery (i.e.
W
is a cobordism with boundary
M^{\prime}\sqcup\overline{M}
and
Q^{\prime}=Q\cup W
). By choosing the framing appropriately, we can assume that the first Chern class of
Q^{\prime}
is trivial (indeed, note that
c_{1}(Q)=0
because
\Sigma(2,3,7)
is parallelizable; now the restriction of a trivialization of
\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}TQ
to the
\gamma_{i}
determines the correct framing).

Lemma 4.10
.

We have
\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})=0
and
\pi_{2}(M^{\prime})=H_{2}(M^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The effect of surgery on homotopy and homology is well understood. To see that
\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})=0
, simply apply
[
28
, Prop. 4.19(ii)]
(put
m=5,n=1
since we are dealing with
1
-surgery on a
5
manifold; hence the condition
2n+2\leq m
is verified). By Hurewicz,
\pi_{2}(M^{\prime})=H_{2}(M^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})
. Now
H_{2}(M^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})
can be computed by a routine diagram chase using
[
28
, Prop. 4.19(iii)]
(again with
m=5,n=1
).
∎

By transversality, we can represent each element of
\pi_{2}(M^{\prime})=H_{2}(M^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by an embedded sphere. Let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}:S^{2}\to M^{\prime}
be such embeddings which correspond to generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. By
[
22
, Lem. 2.6]
and the fact that
c_{1}(Q^{\prime})=0
, we can in fact assume that the
\sigma_{1}
are (mutually disjoint)
embedded Legendrians
. Now let
\tilde{\sigma}_{1},\tilde{\sigma}_{2}
be the loosifications of
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
(that is, the
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}
are loose Legendrians which are embedded, disjoint, and
C^{0}
close to the
\sigma_{i}
; their existence follows from Murphy’s h-principle
[
7
, Thm. 7.25]
).

Now attach handles along the
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}
; let
Q^{\prime\prime}
be the resulting Liouville domain, let
M^{\prime\prime}=\partial Q^{\prime\prime}
be the surgered contact boundary. One can verify that (use e.g.
[
31
, Lem. 1]
) this handle attachment has the effect of killing
H_{2}(M^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})
; it also does not affect
\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})=H_{1}(M^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})=0
(use again e.g.
[
28
, Prop. 4.19(ii)]
). Hence
\pi_{1}(M^{\prime\prime})=H_{1}(M^{\prime\prime};\mathbb{Z})=H_{2}(M^{\prime\prime};\mathbb{Z})=0
. We conclude by appealing to the classical fact that a simply-connected closed
5
-manifold is entirely determined by its second homology (see
[
5
, Thm. 2.2]
). In particular, it follows that
M^{\prime\prime}
is the
5
-sphere.

Corollary 4.11
.

There exists a big Weinstein manifold whose ideal contact boundary is diffeomorphic to
S^{5}
.

Proof.

Indeed,
\widehat{Q^{\prime\prime}}
is such a Weinstein manifold: it was constructed by first attaching subcritical handles to
Q
, and then attaching flexible handles. Since
Q=D^{*}\Sigma(2,3,7)
and we saw that
\pi_{1}(\Sigma(2,3,7))
has exponential growth, it follows (
Example

2.5
) that
\widehat{Q}
is big. But the property of being big is preserved under subcritical and flexible handle attachments (by
Proposition

3.1
and
Proposition

3.3
), so the corollary follows.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Any closed manifold of dimension
5
which admits a Stein fillable contact structure admits a (possibly different) contact structure
\xi_{pos}
with positive entropy (i.e. with the property that for every contact form inducing
\xi_{pos}
, the Reeb flow has positive topological entropy).

Proof.

Let
V
be closed contact manifold with Stein filling
W
. Then
W\sqcup\widehat{Q^{\prime\prime}}
is certainly big. Now attach an appropriate (subcritical)
1
-handle whose effect on the contact boundary is to perform a connected sum of
V=\partial_{\infty}W
and
S^{5}=\partial_{\infty}\hat{Q^{\prime\prime}}
. The resulting Weinstein manifold is still big, and
V\#S^{5}
is of course diffeomorphic to
V
.
∎
