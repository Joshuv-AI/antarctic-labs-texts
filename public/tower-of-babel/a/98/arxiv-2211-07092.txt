Offline Estimation of Controlled Markov Chains: Minimaxity and Sample Complexity
Imon Banerjee, Harsha Honnappa, Vinayak Rao

Abstract
In this work, we study a natural nonparametric estimator of the transition probability matrices of a finite controlled Markov chain. We consider an offline setting with a fixed dataset, collected using a so-called logging policy. We develop sample complexity bounds for the estimator and establish conditions for minimaxity. Our statistical bounds depend on the logging policy through its mixing properties. We show that achieving a particular statistical risk bound involves a subtle and interesting trade-off between the strength of the mixing properties and the number of samples. We demonstrate the validity of our results under various examples, such as ergodic Markov chains, weakly ergodic inhomogeneous Markov chains, and controlled Markov chains with non-stationary Markov, episodic, and greedy controls. Lastly, we use these sample complexity bounds to establish concomitant ones for offline evaluation of stationary Markov control policies.

Introduction

This paper presents probably approximately correct (PAC)-style minimax sample complexity results for statistical estimation of transition matrices of discrete-time, finite-state controlled Markov chains (CMCs)
[
8
]
.
We model a controlled Markov chain as a discrete-time pair process
\{X_{i},a_{i}\}
where
\{a_{i}\}
is a sequence of controls and
\{X_{i}\}
is the state sequence that, conditioned on

, follows a Markov transition kernel.
In this paper, we answer the following question:

What is the minimum number of samples required to estimate the transition matrices of a discrete-time finite-state controlled Markov chain to any given degree of precision?

We answer this question by showing that a particular nonparametric estimator (see below) of the transition matrix is minimax optimal. The control sequence can be viewed as generated by a ‘logging’ or ‘behavior’ policy. Assuming the policy is stationary Markovian,
\{X_{i},a_{i}\}
is jointly Markovian
[
23
, Chapter 2.3]
. This simplifies the problem to one of estimating the transition kernel of a Markov chain, and opens the door to a number of results under suitable ergodicity and mixing assumptions. Some very recent ones include frequentist
[
63
]
and Bayesian
[
4
]
PAC bounds. On the other hand, if the logging policy is non-Markovian, finite sample statistical inference results are quite sparse. Nevertheless, this is an important setting as argued in
[
29
,
30
,
57
,
46
]
. While
[
57
,
46
]
focus on non-Markovian policies in the online setting,
[

,
30

Consequently, an understanding of the sample complexity of estimating the transition kernel for a non-Markovian controls is an important open problem. In this work, all we assume is that the
a_{i}

and

.

Although this particular non-parametric estimator of transition matrices has been previously used in the context of model-based RL studies

1.

Our main result (

As we argue in

2.

We prove in

that the transition probabilities can be estimated even under a weaker mixing assumption than is required for minimaxity (
Theorem

3.2
). However, this involves a trade-off, requiring more samples (roughly
\Omega(d^{2}k^{2})
in place of
\Omega(d^{2}k)
) to achieve the same level of estimation error.

3.

A useful implication of our sample complexity results is that they yield error bounds for offline policy evaluation (OPE).
Theorem
5.1
evaluates stationary Markov policies from data logged using non Markovian controls.
The resulting sample complexity recovers minimax optimal rates in the literature, which typically assume Markovian logging policies.
Furthermore, Theorem
5.2
demonstrates a sample complexity of estimating the optimal policy under sufficient regularity conditions on the model class.

From a methodological perspective, our analysis reveals two principles that are broadly useful in establishing sample efficiency results for learning CMCs and other controlled stochastic models. First, is a ‘Goldilocks principle’ that no state-control pair must be visited too many or too few times in a single observed sample path. This can be achieved by ensuring that the control sequence is such that the time-to-return to a particular pair is uniformly bounded over the state-control space (see Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
).

Second, the effect of history on the probability of under- or over-visiting any part of the state-control space is controlled by the mixing properties of the control sequence, as defined in Assumption
4.1
and Assumption
4.3
.
Roughly speaking, weaker mixing properties imply looser bounds on these probabilities, in turn implying that estimators are possibly sample inefficient.
The bulk of the existing literature on offline estimation of CMCs focuses on the setting where the control sequence forms a stationary ergodic Markov sequence and, under this condition, the nonparametric estimator is minimax optimal, as implied by
Theorem

3.2
and
Proposition

4.3
.
However, if the control sequence mixes comparatively slowly (say, polynomially), then
Theorem

3.1
yields a loose sample complexity bound.

As we prove in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
, it is relatively straightforward to verify the geometric mixing properties of the control sequences when the controls are Markovian.
However, when the controls are non-Markovian, there is no general result to demonstrate geometric mixing. Thus, a practitioner must be cautious of erroneously assuming the logging policy to be Markovian when it is not.
If the controls are not Markovian, then one needs
\Omega(d^{2}k^{2})
samples instead of
\Omega(d^{2}k)
to control the probability of large estimation errors on the transition probabilities.

As a final note on the methodological implications, while we focus on finite state-control spaces, we believe that these principles, and our analysis, yield a broad framework for proving sample efficiency results for offline estimation of CMCs, and potentially other controlled stochastic models, under more general model assumptions. For instance, if one uses a histogram or a density estimator of a transition kernel on continuous state spaces and compact control spaces, then our results are directly applicable, although the optimal sample complexity would depend upon the smoothness properties of the transition function.

1.

Question of Aperiodicity.
Consider the following three transition probability matrices

\displaystyle M^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
1/3&1/3&1/3\\
1/3&1/3&1/3\end{bmatrix}\ M^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}1/3&1/3&1/3\\

[

3.

Question of Mixing.
An ergodic Markov chain on a finite state space is uniformly mixing. However, no equivalent result exists for controlled Markov chains. It is not clear if geometrically fast mixing is sufficient or necessary for the accurate estimation of transition probability matrices.

Below, we state some assumptions that allow us to address these challenges. Our first two assumptions address the notions of aperiodicity and irreducibility. Informally, we need to ensure that no part of the chain is deterministic in nature, and every state-control pair

3.3
Sketch of Proof of Theorem
3.2

The proof of the Theorem is divided into two parts: (1) the sample complexity, and (2) the minimaxity. The proof of sample complexity proceeds similarly to the proof of
Theorem

3.1
. The key difference is in Step 4, where instead of using Proposition
3.2
, we use Proposition
3.3
. The key intuition in this difference is to use a tighter Chernoff concentration inequality that is available for exponentially mixing random variables, instead of a weaker Hoeffding’s inequality. This produces a tighter sample complexity that is provably minimax. The details of the first part can be found in
Section

F.3
.
For this sketch, we focus on the minimaxity. Let
\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}
be the class of all controlled Markov chain on state space
\chi
with control space
\mathbb{I}
.
For two stochastic matrices
\mathbb{M}_{1},\mathbb{M}_{2}
, define

\left\|\mathbb{M}_{1}-\mathbb{M}_{2}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}:=\sup_{l\in\mathbb{I}}\left\|M_{1}^{(l)}-M_{2}^{(l)}\right\|_{\infty}
.
Observe the minimax risk satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{m}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\right)\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\right),

for any subclass of controlled Markov chains
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}
and any estimation procedure,
\hat{\mathbb{M}}
. The rest of the proof proceeds through 2 cases by constructing appropriate subclasses
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. The motivation behind these choices are based on the fact that the uniform distribution is the least favourable choice for estimation
[
10
,
16
,
31
,
17
]
. We make these examples explicit in Section
D
.

CASE I:

\left(m<\frac{8d}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(1+\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}\right)}\right)

Here, we choose a class of controlled Markov chains with controls distributed uniformly over
\mathbb{I}
and transition matrices, for vectors
\sigma=\left(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\frac{d}{2}}\right)\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{\frac{d}{2}}
, given by

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&\ldots&\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&p_{\star}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&\ldots&\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&p_{\star}\\
\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon}{d}&\ldots&\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{\frac{d}{2}}\varepsilon}{d}&p_{\star}\end{pmatrix}.

We then use Tsybakov’s reduction method to lower bound
\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\right)
for our chosen subclass of controlled Markov chains.

CASE II:

m<\left(2\ \mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2},2\ \mathbb{C}_{\zeta,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{\zeta,\delta}\right)^{2}\right)

STEP 1.
For this case, we set
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
to be a class of controlled Markov chains with controls and transition probability matrices described in Section
D
.

STEP 2.
We then use Tsybakov’s reduction method
[
59
, Chapter 2.2]
to observe that for any random variable
\mathbb{T}
,

\mathcal{R}_{m}\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\,|\,\mathbb{T}>m\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\mathbb{T}>m}\right).

\mathbb{T}
is chosen to be an appropriate “touring time” (the time to visit sufficiently many state-control pairs).

STEP 3.
We then prove that
\mathbb{P}\left({\mathbb{T}>m}\right)
is bounded away from zero as long as

m<2\ \mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)
.

STEP 4.
We then argue that whenever
\mathbb{T}>m
, there exists a state-control pair
s_{0},l_{0}
such that
N_{s_{0}}^{(l_{0})}=0
.

STEP 5.
If
N_{s_{0}}^{(l_{0})}=0
, so is
N_{s_{0},t}^{(l_{0})}=0
for all
t\in\chi
. This proves that there is an uniform error to estimate

4.1
Reduction of Assumptions

Reduction of Return Times.

First consider the assumption on return times introduced in Assumption
3.2
.

We call a sequence of random variables
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
a
j
th-order Markov chain if for all
i
, the conditional distribution of
Z_{\infty},\dots,Z_{i}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\infty},\dots,Z_{i}|Z_{i-1},\dots,Z_{0}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\infty},\dots,Z_{i}|Z_{i-1},\dots,Z_{i-j}\right).

Observe that if
a_{i}
is
j
th-order Markovian then so is the paired process
(X_{i},a_{i})
. For convenience of notation, define
\tau^{\dagger}:=\sum_{p=0}^{i-1}\tau_{s,l}^{(p)}
and observe that
\tau_{s,l}^{(i)}
is a function of only
X_{\tau^{\dagger}+1},a_{\tau^{\dagger}+1},\dots,X_{\infty},a_{\infty}
. It now follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{s,l,i}\mathrm{E}[\tau^{(i)}_{s,l}|{\mathcal{F}_{\tau^{\dagger}}}]=\sup_{s,l,i}\mathrm{E}[\tau^{(i)}_{s,l}|\mathcal{H}_{\tau^{\dagger}-j}^{\tau^{\dagger}}]\text{ almost everywhere.}

Moreover, if
a_{i}
is independent of time point
i
(also called “stationary”), then we further have

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sup_{s,l,i}\mathrm{E}[\tau^{(i)}_{s,l}|\mathcal{H}_{\tau^{\dagger}-k}^{\tau^{\dagger}}]=\sup_{s,l}\mathrm{E}[\tau^{(1)}_{s,l}|X_{0}=s,a_{0}=l]\text{ almost everywhere.}

(15)

Reduction of Mixing Coefficients.

Next, we decompose the
\bar{\eta}
-mixing coefficients of the paired process
\{X_{i},a_{i}\}
into mixing coefficients over states and controls.
We motivate this decomposition using two facts:

1.

The controls of a controlled Markov chain are often chosen by the user and is well behaved.

2.

The mixing coefficients of the individual processes can be analysed more directly.

We begin by defining the
\gamma
-mixing coefficients
\gamma_{p,j,i}
for controls as the following total variation distance

\displaystyle\gamma_{p,j,i}:=\sup_{s_{p},\hbar_{i+j}^{p-1},\hbar_{0}^{i}}\left\|\mathbb{P}\left(a_{p}|X_{p}=s_{p},\mathcal{H}_{i+j}^{p-1}=\hbar_{i+j}^{p-1},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{i}=\hbar_{0}^{i}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(a_{p}|X_{p}=s_{p},\mathcal{H}_{i+j}^{p-1}=\hbar_{i+j}^{p-1}\right)\right\|_{TV},

(16)

where
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{p}=s_{p},\mathcal{H}_{i+j}^{p-1}=\hbar_{i+j}^{p-1},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{i}=\hbar_{0}^{i}\right)>0
.

Assumption 4.1

(Mixing of controls)
.

There exists a constant
\mathbb{C}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq i\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\infty}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\infty}}\sum_{p=i+j+1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\infty}}\gamma_{p,j,i}\leq\frac{\mathbb{C}}{2}.

Remark 4.1
.

In the Markovian settings, when the sequence of controls

depend only upon
X_{i}
,

\gamma_{p,j,i}=0

for all

. In such case, Assumption
4.1
is satisfied with

. This extends to the case where
a_{i}
depends upon

many past time points. If

depend only upon

, then Assumption

is satisfied with

\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{m},a_{m})\right\}
be a collected sample.
For all integers

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}_{i,j}:=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},s_{2}\in\chi,l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathbb{I},\\

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=s_{2},a_{i}=l_{2})>0\end{subarray}}{\sup}\|\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}|X_{i}=s_{1},a_{i}=l_{1}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}|X_{i}=s_{2},a_{i}=l_{2}\right)\|_{TV},

Controlled Markov chains with non-stationary Markov controls

A controlled Markov chain is said to have non-stationary Markov controls if for any time period
i
, state
s
, control
l
, and sample history
\hbar_{0}^{i-1}

\mathbb{P}\left(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{i-1}=\hbar_{0}^{i-1}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(a_{i}=l|X_{i}=s\right).

For convenience, we refer to it as simply Markov controls.
Observe that we allow the law of the control sequence to depend upon the time step

We can write the transition probability of the state-control pair as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=t,a_{i}=l^{\prime}|X_{i-1}=s,a_{i-1}=l\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=t|X_{i-1}=s,a_{i-1}=l\right)\mathbb{P}(a_{i}=l^{\prime}|X_{i}=t)

\displaystyle=M_{s,t}^{(l)}\times P_{t,l^{\prime}}^{(i)}.

It is straightforward to see that the state-control pair is a time inhomogeneous Markov chain with transition probabilities given by
M_{s,t}^{(l)}\times P_{s,l^{\prime}}^{(i)}

Our goal is to estimate the transition probabilities
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=t|X_{i-1}=s,a_{i-1}=l^{\prime})

Definition 4.1
.

Define

\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,j)}
is recursively defined as

\tau_{s,l}^{(i,\star,j)}:=\min\{n:(a_{\tau_{\star}+n}=l),a_{j}\neq l\kern 4.62497pt\forall\kern 4.62497pt\tau_{\star}<j<\tau_{\star}+n\}.

and

.

For some constant

For all

Under the conditions of Assumption

Sample Complexity of Policy Evaluation and Optimal Policy Recovery

Offline policy recovery (OPR) is a key problem in offline RL settings, wherein one wishes to identify the policy that maximises the
value function
given a sequence of states and controls generated by an unknown logging policy and unknown transition probabilities. An allied problem is offline policy evaluation (OPE) where we use the state-control sequence to estimate the value function of an arbitrary policy. In this section we demonstrate how our previous results can be used to provide a high probability bound for recovering the optimal policy. We first use Theorem
3.1
to provide a high probability bound for OPE, and then extend that to a corresponding result for OPR.

To fix these ideas, we introduce some notation. Let
\Delta(\mathbb{I})
denote the probability simplex on the control space, and suppose
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathbb{I})
is a given stationary stochastic policy.
Let the matrices
M
and
\Pi
represent the probabilities of the next state and action respectively, given the current state and action. Thus
M=[M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(k)}]^{T}
, and
\Pi=\left[\pi,\pi,\dots,\pi\right]
are
dk\times d
dimensional matrices.
Let
\tilde{g}:=(\tilde{g}(x,a):(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{I})
be a
dk\times 1
vector where element
s+k(l-1)
denotes the cost associated with the state
s
and control
l
. Then, the per-stage
expected
cost function
g:=(g(x):x\in\chi)
is a
d\times 1
vector where
g(x)=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{I}}\pi(x,a)\tilde{g}(x,a))
.
For a known discount factor
0<\alpha_{dis}<1
, the value function
V:=(V(x):x\in\mathcal{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
obtained by solving the Bellman equation
[
6
]
, is given by

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\Pi^{T}M\right)^{-1}g.

Substituting
M
by
\hat{M}
, we obtain the plug-in estimate
\hat{V}=\left(I-\alpha_{dis}\Pi^{T}\hat{M}\right)^{-1}g
.

The next theorem provides a sample complexity bound on estimating the value function

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{m},a_{m})\right\}
be a sample from a controlled Markov chain with stationary controls. Assume that for some

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=s,a_{i}=l\right)>T^{-1}
for all

c>1
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\hat{V}-V\right\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\right)<\delta
if

m>c\;\max\left\{\frac{T_{\alpha}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{dkT_{\alpha}}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}\right),\mathbb{C}_{\theta}^{2}\max\left\{{T}^{2},\frac{1}{\left(1-\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}\right)^{2}}\right\}\log\left(\frac{dk}{\delta}\right)\right\},

\text{ where }T_{\alpha}={\left\|g\right\|_{1}^{2}d\alpha_{dis}^{2}T}/{(1-\alpha_{dis})^{4}},\zeta_{1}=\zeta_{2}=T^{-1},\text{ and }\mathbb{C}_{\theta}=T/dk.

The proof of this theorem can be found in
Section

X_{i},a_{i}
.

Conclusions

In this paper, we derive exact rates of convergence for the empirical estimator of the transition probability matrices of a controlled Markov chain, and used these to derive the sample complexity of achieving a desired estimation risk.
We tease out the exact effect of the mixing coefficients of the states and controls on the sample complexity, and provide conditions under which the empirical estimator is minimax optimal.
We use our sample complexity results in a number of examples, including error bounds for the value function and the optimal policy estimated from data. Below, we highlight three possible topics for future research.

Countable state spaces.

As an obvious extension to our work, consider the problem of countably infinite state and control spaces. Some work in this regard can be found in
[
63
]
where state spaces are countably infinite, but there are no reasonable extensions to the setting with countably infinite control space.

Uncountably infinite state space and finite controls

We have also found no result which derives the minimax sample complexity of estimating the transition probability distribution of a controlled Markov chain on an uncountably infinite state space. The histogram estimator is the obvious counterpart to this work. Indeed, recent studies
[
54
,
39
]
demonstrates promising properties of the former. But to the best of our knowledge, the techniques do not translate to uncountable control spaces, and optimally estimating the transition probability distribution remains an open question.

Learning in presence of weaker mixing or adversarial controls.

Although strong mixing properties of the controls is a sufficient condition for the ‘estimability’ of the transition matrices, it may not be a realistic assumption when the system dynamics are weakly mixing or adversarial
[
47
]
. System identification under the presence of an adversary remains an interesting question was addressed in a recent paper
[
56
]
using strong linearization assumptions and exponential computation times. However, this is well beyond the scope of the current work and is a direction for future study.

Instance-dependent and data-dependent learning.

Our bounds are derived over an entire class of CMC models. Indeed, so long as a specific model conforms to the assumptions we have imposed over this class, our bounds are applicable. This reflects extant analyses of offline RL
[
33
,
34
]
. On the other hand recent work in online RL seeks to establish
instance-dependent
bounds for specific models (for example
[
74
,
44
,
25
]
). Establishing such bounds for offline CMC identification is also an interesting open problem.

[
25
]
also emphasize that inferential theory for RL should be data-dependent, for instance allowing for the computation of data-dependent confidence intervals. This is an important future direction for our work as well.

B.1
Concentration Inequalities

Hoeffding’s concentration inequality for mixing sequences.

For two sequences of real numbers
\tilde{x}=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{m}\}
and
\tilde{y}=\{y_{0},\dots,y_{m}\}
, define the Hamming metric
d
between them as
d(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}):=\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}[x_{i}\neq y_{i}]
.
Observe that for any two sequences
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}
we have,

\displaystyle|N_{s}(\tilde{x})-N_{s}(\tilde{y})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}[x_{i}=s]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}[y_{i}=s]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\mathbbm{1}[x_{i}=s]-\mathbbm{1}[y_{i}=s]\right|

(50)

∎

F.2
Proof of Sample Complexity in Theorem
3.2

Proof.

The first part of this proof follows similarly to that of
Theorem

3.1
.
The key difference is to use a tighter Chernoff concentration inequality that is available for exponentially mixing random variables, instead of a weaker Hoeffding’s inequality. This produces a tighter sample complexity that we can proceed to prove is minimax.

We proceed until
eq.

42
, and analyse Term 1 similarly as before to get

\sum_{n=n_{low,s}}^{n_{high,s}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\hat{M}^{(l)}(s,\cdot)-{M}^{(l)}(s,\cdot)\right\|_{1}>\varepsilon,N_{s}^{(l)}=n\right)\leq m\exp\left({-\frac{n_{low,s}}{2}\max\left\{0,\varepsilon-\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{high,s}}}\right\}^{2}}\right).

(51)

The difference arises in the analysis of Term 2, where, instead of using Proposition
3.2
, we use Proposition
3.3
to obtain

Term 2

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(N_{s}^{(l)}\notin[n_{low,s},n_{high,s}]\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\;\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}\left(n_{low,s}-\frac{m}{2T}\right)^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{s}^{(l)}+1+\left(\frac{m}{2T}-n_{low,s}\right)\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+2\exp\left(-\;\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}\left(n_{high,s}-m\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}\right)^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{s}^{(l)}+1+\left(n_{high,s}-m\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}\right)\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right).

Observe that the right-hand side of the previous equation is increasing in
\rho_{s}^{(l)}
.
Furthermore, we also have
\rho_{s}^{(l)}\leq\rho_{\star}
.
Thus, we can replace
\rho_{s}^{(l)}
by
\rho_{\star}
in the upper bound to get

Term 2

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\;\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}\left(n_{low,s}-\frac{m}{2T}\right)^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}+1+\left(\frac{m}{2T}-n_{low,s}\right)\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\exp\left(-\;\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}\left(n_{high,s}-m\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}\right)^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}+1+\left(n_{high,s}-m\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}\right)\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right).

Next, we substitute the values
n_{high,s}=m(1+\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\})/2
and
n_{low,s}=m/4T
into the previous term to get

Term 2

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\;\frac{1}{16T^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}m^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}+1+\frac{m}{4T}\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\exp\left(-\;\ \frac{1}{4}\frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}m^{2}\left(1-\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}\right)^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}+1+m\left(\frac{1-\max\{\zeta_{1},1-\zeta_{2}\}}{2}\right)\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right).

(52)

We only analyse the first term. The calculations for the second term follow in a similar way. Recall from hypothesis that
m>\mathbb{C}_{T}
. Obviously,
m>4T
. In other words,
1<m/T
.
Substituting
1
for
m/T
into the denominator, we get

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\;\frac{1}{16T^{2}}\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}m^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}+1+\frac{m}{4T}\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\exp\left(-\;\frac{1}{16T^{2}}\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}m^{2}}{4m\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}+\frac{m}{4T}+\frac{m}{4T}\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=2\exp\left(-\;\frac{1}{4}\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}m}{16\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}T^{2}+T+T\left(\log m\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\exp\left(-\;\frac{1}{64}\ \frac{\mathbb{C}_{pel}\frac{m}{\left(\log m\right)^{2}}}{\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}T^{2}+2T}\right).

The last inequality follows by taking
16(\log m)^{2}
common from the denominator and trivially upper bounding
(\log m)^{-2}
by
1
.
Observe that
\mathbb{C}_{pel}/64\left(\mathbb{C}_{\Delta}\rho_{\star}T^{2}+2T\right)
is less than
1
and constant in
m
.
Recall from
eq.

13
that the
inverse of
this term was defined to be
\mathbb{C}_{T}
.
It is clear that

2dk\exp\left(-\;\frac{m}{\mathbb{C}_{T}(\log m)^{2}}\right)

is decreasing in
m
. Our objective is to find an
m
such that

\displaystyle 2dk\exp\left(-\;\frac{m}{\mathbb{C}_{T}(\log m)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{3},

which is equivalent to finding an
m
such that

\displaystyle\frac{m}{(\log m)^{2}}

\displaystyle>\mathbb{C}_{T}\log\left(\frac{6dk}{\delta}\right)=\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}.

(53)

Let
m=2\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2}
.
The denominator of the term on the left-hand side of the previous equation decomposes into

\displaystyle(\log m)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\log\left(2\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\log 2+\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}+2\log\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2}\left(1+\frac{\log 2}{\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}}+2\frac{\log\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}}{\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}}\right)^{2}.

Consider the function,

\displaystyle f(x)=\left(1+\frac{\log 2}{\log x}+2\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}\right)^{2}.

This is obviously decreasing in
x
. It can be easily verified that there exists a universal constant ‘
c
’ such that
f(x)<2
if
x>c
.
Using this fact and substituting
m
in
eq.

53
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{m}{(\log m)^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\frac{\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2}}{\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2}f(\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta})}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}.

Therefore, the first term on the left-hand side of
eq.

52
is upper bounded by
\delta/3
.
We can similarly show that, whenever
m>2\mathbb{C}_{\zeta,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{\zeta,\delta}\right)^{2}
the second term on the right-hand side of
eq.

52
can be upper bounded by
\delta/3
.
We now proceed similarly to
eq.

48
.
This gives us that, under our current hypothesis,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{l\in\mathbb{I}}{\sup}\|\hat{M}^{(l)}-M^{(l)}\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\right)<\delta.

This completes the proof of the sample complexity. We can now proceed to the proof of Minimaxity.
∎

F.3
Proof of Minimaxity in Theorem
3.2

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}
be the class of all controlled Markov chains on state space
\chi
with control space
\mathbb{I}
.
We can view an element
\mathcal{P}
of
\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}
as a doublet
(\mathbb{M},P)
, where

\mathbb{M}:=(M^{(1)},\dots,M^{(k)})
is a collection of distinct
d
-state Markov transition matrix, and
P:=\left(P_{1},P_{2},\dots,\right)
is the distribution of control sequences, with each
P_{i}
being a probability measure on
\mathbb{I}
that depends on the history until time point
i
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{\chi}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{I}}
be the set of all
\mathbb{M}
and
P
, respectively.
As before, for
\mathbb{M}_{1},\mathbb{M}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\chi}
let

\left\|\mathbb{M}_{1}-\mathbb{M}_{2}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}=\sup_{l\in\mathbb{I}}\left\|M_{1}^{(l)}-M_{2}^{(l)}\right\|_{\infty}.

For
\left\{(X_{0},a_{0}),\dots,(X_{m},a_{m})\right\}\in(\chi\times\mathbb{I})^{m}
, a sample of length
m
from some CMC belonging to
\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}
, define an estimation procedure
\hat{\mathbb{M}}

as the mapping
\hat{\mathbb{M}}:(\chi\times\mathbb{I})^{m}\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{\chi}
.
We seek to provide a lower bound for the minimax risk over all estimation procedures:

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{m}&=\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{(\mathbb{M},P)\in\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\right).\end{split}

(54)

We note that if
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\chi,\mathbb{I}}
is a subclass of CMC’s, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{m}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{(\mathbb{M},P)\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\right).

(55)

The rest of the proof proceeds by constructing an appropriate subclass
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Part 1 (
m<cT/\varepsilon^{2}
):

Our example below considers a CMC with stationary Markovian controls. Any such CMC with
k
transition matrices and
d^{\prime}
states
can be viewed as a single Markov chain with
d=d^{\prime}k
states.
For convenience, we use the latter representation. By
{M}
denote its transition matrix, and by
\hat{M}
denote the estimate.
Without losing generality, let there be
d+1
states where
d
is even; the odd case is handled similarly.
Let
0<p_{\star}<\frac{1}{d+1}
and for a vector
\sigma=\left(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\frac{d}{2}}\right)\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{\frac{d}{2}}
, define
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\sigma)
as the following perturbation of
\left(\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d},\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d},\dots,p_{\star}\right)
:

\boldsymbol{\eta}(\sigma)=\left(\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon}{d},\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon}{d},\dots,\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{\frac{d}{2}}\varepsilon}{d},\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{\frac{d}{2}}\varepsilon}{d},p_{\star}\right).

Since
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{32}
and
d>2
by hypothesis, it follows that
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\sigma)
is a valid probability mass function on
\{1,\dots,d+1\}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
be a class of transition matrices indexed by
\sigma
, taking the form

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&\ldots&\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&p_{\star}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&\ldots&\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}&p_{\star}\\
\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon}{d}&\ldots&\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{\frac{d}{2}}\varepsilon}{d}&p_{\star}\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

From the Varshamov-Gilbert lemma
[
60
, Theorem 5.1.7]
, there exists

\Sigma\subset\left\{-1,1\right\}^{d/2}
,
\left|\Sigma\right|=2^{d/16}
, such that
for
(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime})\in\Sigma
with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\neq\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}
,
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\mathbbm{1}[\boldsymbol{\sigma_{i}}\neq\boldsymbol{\sigma_{i}^{\prime}}]\geq\frac{d}{16},

Define the subclass
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
as

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:=\left\{M_{\sigma}:\sigma\in\Sigma\right\}

Recall that by
eq.

55
, that it is enough to find a lower bound on

\inf_{\hat{M}}\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|M-\hat{M}\right\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon}\right).

By applying Tsybakov’s reduction method
[
59
, Theorem 2.5]
to our problem, we obtain the following lower bound,

\inf_{\hat{M}}\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|M-\hat{M}\right\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cfrac{2^{2-\frac{d}{16}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},m}}{\log{2^{\frac{d}{16}}}}\right),

(58)

where
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},m}
is the KL-divergence between

M_{0}
and
M_{\sigma}
(for some
\sigma\in\Sigma
), both viewed as distributions over sequences of length
m
.
Recall the following chain rule for KL-divergence from
[
63
, Lemma 6.2]

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},m}\leq p_{\star}m\mathcal{D_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}},

where
\mathcal{D_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}
is the KL-divergence between
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\sigma)
and
\left(\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d},\dots,\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d},p_{\star}\right)
. A direct computation of
\mathcal{D_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{D_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{i}\varepsilon}{d}\log\left(\frac{\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{i}\varepsilon}{d}}{\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}}\right)+\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{i}\varepsilon}{d}\log\left(\frac{\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{i}\varepsilon}{d}}{\frac{1-p_{\star}}{d}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\varepsilon}{d}\log\left(\frac{1-p_{\star}+16\varepsilon}{1-p_{\star}}\right)+\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\varepsilon}{d}\log\left(\frac{1-p_{\star}-16\varepsilon}{1-p_{\star}}\right)\right).

Denoting
1-p_{\star}
by
A
and
16\varepsilon
by
B
allows us to rewrite the previous equation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left((A+B)\log\left(1+\frac{B}{A}\right)+(A-B)\log\left(1-\frac{B}{A}\right)\right).

Observe that
B=16\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
and
A=1-p_{\star}>1-\frac{1}{d+1}>\frac{1}{2}
. This implies that
\frac{B}{A}<1
. Since
\log\left(1+x\right)\leq x
whenever
x>-1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(A+B)\frac{B}{A}-(A-B)\frac{B}{A}=\frac{B^{2}}{A}=\frac{256\varepsilon^{2}}{1-p_{\star}}\leq 512\varepsilon^{2}.

Substituting this value in
section

F.3
, and further substituting in that value into
eq.

58
, we obtain

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{M}}\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|M-\hat{M}\right\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cfrac{2^{2-\frac{d}{16}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma}512p_{\star}m\varepsilon^{2}}{\log{2^{\frac{d}{16}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cfrac{2^{2-\frac{d}{16}}|\Sigma|512p_{\star}m\varepsilon^{2}}{\log{2^{\frac{d}{16}}}}\right).

Recall that our choice of
\Sigma
satisfies
|\Sigma|=2^{\frac{d}{16}}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cfrac{2^{2-\frac{d}{16}}|\Sigma|512p_{\star}m\varepsilon^{2}}{\log{2^{\frac{d}{16}}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cfrac{32768p_{\star}m\varepsilon^{2}}{d\log{2}}\right).

Therefore, whenever

m\leq\frac{d(1-2\delta)\log{2}}{32768p_{\star}\varepsilon^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{(\mathbb{M},P)\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\right)>\delta.

To complete the proof, we need to relate the quantities
d
and
p_{*}
to the expected return time
T
of the process. From the statement of the theorem, we need to show that there exists an universal constant
c_{1}
such that
T
satisfies

T\leq c_{1}\frac{d}{p_{\star}},

where
T
is the return time as defined in Assumption
3.2
.
It is easily verifiable that for any
\sigma\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{d/2}
the vector

\left(\frac{(1-p_{\star})^{2}+\left(1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon\right)p_{\star}}{d},\dots,\frac{(1-p_{\star})^{2}+\left(1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{d/2}\varepsilon\right)p_{\star}}{d},p_{\star}\right)

represents the stationary distribution corresponding to the transition probability Matrix
M_{\sigma}
as defined in
56
. We know from Kac’s theorem
[
43
, Theorem 10.2.2]
that the expected return time to any state is the inverse of its stationary probability. It follows from
section

F.3
that the expected return times are

\left(\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}+\left(1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon\right)p_{\star}},\dots,\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}+\left(1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{d/2}\varepsilon\right)p_{\star}},\frac{1}{p_{\star}}\right).

Since

\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}+\left(1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon\right)p_{\star}}\leq\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}}

for any value of
\sigma_{1}\in\left\{1,-1\right\}
it follows that

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}+\left(1-p_{\star}+16\sigma_{1}\varepsilon\right)p_{\star}},\dots,\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}+\left(1-p_{\star}-16\sigma_{d/2}\varepsilon\right)p_{\star}},\frac{1}{p_{\star}}\right\}

\displaystyle\ \leq\max\left\{\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}},\frac{1}{p_{\star}}\right\}.

Since
p_{\star}<1/(d+1)
, it follows that

\max\left\{\frac{d}{(1-p_{\star})^{2}},\frac{1}{p_{\star}}\right\}<\frac{d}{p_{\star}}.

Therefore, setting

, it follows that
whenever,

m\leq\frac{T(1-2\delta)\log{2}}{32768\varepsilon^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{(\mathbb{M},P)\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\right)>\delta.

This completes the proof of the first part.

Part 2
\left(m<\left\{2\ \mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{T,\delta}\right)^{2},2\ \mathbb{C}_{\zeta,\delta}\left(\log\mathbb{C}_{\zeta,\delta}\right)^{2}\right\}\right)

Case I:

to be the first time all of the states

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{m}&\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{(\mathbb{M},P)\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\,|\,\mathbb{T}>m\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\mathbb{T}>m}\right).\end{split}

H.13

Lemma F.1
.

Let
\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbb{M}}}\sup_{(\mathbb{M},P)\in\mathbb{H}_{\iota}\times\{P^{(0)}\}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{\mathbb{M}}-\mathbb{M}\right\|_{\infty}^{*}>\varepsilon}\,|\,\mathbb{T}>m\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\mathbb{T}>m}\right).

Next, let

be any control.
By definition of

\displaystyle\ \geq\inf_{\hat{M}^{(l_{0})}}\sup_{\xi^{(l_{0})}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{(d/3)}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left\|\hat{M}^{(l_{0})}-M_{\iota,\xi^{(l_{0})}}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon}\,|\,\mathbb{T}>m\right).

Then for

Therefore,

\left\{l_{0}:\xi^{\star}\neq\xi^{(l_{0})}\right\}\subset\left\{l_{0}:\left\|M_{\iota,\xi^{(l_{0})}}^{(l_{0})}-\hat{M}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon\right\}
