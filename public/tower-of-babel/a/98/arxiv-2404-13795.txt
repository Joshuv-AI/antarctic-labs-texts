Abstract
In this work we study symmetric random matrices with variance profile satisfying certain conditions. We establish the convergence of the operator norm of these matrices to the largest element of the support of the limiting empirical spectral distribution. We prove that it is sufficient for the entries of the matrix to have finite only the 4 4 -th moment or the 4 + ϵ 4+\epsilon moment in order for the convergence to hold in probability or almost surely respectively. Our approach determines the behaviour of the operator norm for random symmetric or non-symmetric matrices whose variance profile is given by a step or a continuous function, random band matrices whose bandwidth is proportional to their dimension, random Gram matrices, triangular matrices and more.

Introduction

The problem of understanding the operator norm of a large random matrix with independent entries is multidisciplinary, occupying mathematicians, statisticians, physicists. On the mathematical side, tools from classical probability, geometric analysis, combinatorics, free probability and more have been used. The problem dates back to 1981, where in
[
20
]
the convergence of the largest eigenvalue of renormalized Wigner matrices (symmetric, i.i.d. entries) to the edge of the limiting distribution was established when the entries of the matrix are bounded. Next, in
[
3
]
, the authors gave necessary and sufficient conditions for the entries of a Wigner matrix to converge. The crucial condition was that the entries should have finite 4-th moment. Similar bounds have been given to non-symmetric matrices with i.i.d. entries. Then, the difference of the largest eigenvalue and its limit, after re-normalization, was proven to converge to the Tracy-Widow law in
[
28
]
. Later, universality results were established for sparse random matrix models, for example in
[
23
]
for random graphs and in
[
26
]
for random banded matrices. Moreover, sharp non-asymptotic results for a general class of matrices were established in
[
5
]
and in
[
4
]
.

All the models mentioned above can be considered as random matrices with general variance profile, i.e., random matrices whose entries’ variances can depend on the dimension of the matrix and the location of the element in the matrix. These models have also drawn a lot of attention lately, see for example
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
, where non-Hermitian models are considered. More specifically, assume that
A_{N}=(a_{i,j}^{(N)}),N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},
is a sequence of symmetric random matrices, with
a_{i,j}^{(N)}
real valued having mean zero and variance
s_{i,j}^{(N)}
bounded by a fixed number, say 1. Classically, the first question is whether the empirical spectral distribution of an appropriate normalization of
A_{N}
(e.g.,
A_{N}/\sqrt{N}
) converges to a nontrivial probability measure, as in Wigner’s theorem. Nothing guarantees that, and one can construct examples where the sequence of the empirical spectral distributions does not converge. The work
[
30
]
, using the notion of graphons, gave conditions on the variance profile
s_{i,j}^{(N)},i,j\in[N],N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
so that convergence takes place.

The next, natural, question concerns the convergence of the largest eigenvalue to the largest element of the support of the limiting distribution. Again, this in not automatic but requires additional assumptions. It was established in the recent works
[
1
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
17
]
(whose focus however is not this question) for some class of random matrices with a general variance profile under the assumption that the entries of the matrices have finite all moments (the first two works assume that each
a_{i,j}^{(N)}
is sharp sub-Gaussian, the last two assume that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
there is a constant bounding the
2k
moment of each
a_{i,j}^{(N)}
). In this paper, we generalize these results, i.e., we establish the convergence of the largest eigenvalue of general variance profile random matrices to the largest element of the support of the limiting empirical spectral distribution under general assumptions for the variance profile of the matrices. Regarding finiteness of moments, we assume only that
\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]}\mathbf{E}|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{4}<\infty
.

Notation
.
For any
N\times N
matrix
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in[N]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
with eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}(A)\}_{i\in[N]}
, the measure

\mu_{A}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(A)}

will be called the Empirical Spectral Distribution (E.S.D.) of
A
. When the eigenvalues are real, write
\lambda_{\max}(A)
for the maximum among them. We will use the following two norms on square matrices. For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,

\displaystyle|A|_{\operatorname{op}}

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:||x||_{2}=1}||Ax||_{2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(AA^{T})}

(1.1)

\displaystyle||A||_{\max}

\displaystyle:=\max_{i,j\in[N]}|a_{i,j}|.

(1.2)

It is easy to see that
|A|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq N||A||_{\max}
and if the matrix
A
is symmetric, then

|A|_{\operatorname{op}}:=\max_{i\in[N]}|\lambda_{i}(A)|.

(1.3)

Statement of the results

Throughout this section,
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
is a sequence of symmetric random matrices with independent entries (up to symmetry),
A_{N}=(a_{i,j}^{(N)})_{i,j\in[N]}
is an
N\times N
matrix, and all
\{a_{i,j}^{(N)}:N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]\}
are defined on the same probability space and take real values.

A standard assumption for the sequence is the following (see relation (2.2.1) in
[
2
]
).

Assumption 2.1
.

1.

\mathbf{E}a_{i,j}^{(N)}=0
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]
, and
\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]}\mathbf{E}|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{2}<\infty
.

2.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[N]}\mathbf{E}\Big\{|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|\geq\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\Big\}=0.

(2.1)

This is satisfied in the case that
\{a_{i,j}^{(N)}:N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N],i\leq j\}
are i.i.d. with mean 0 and finite variance. But it is not enough to guarantee that the ESD of the appropriately normalized
A_{N}
converges to a nontrivial limit. To state a sufficient condition for this, we introduce some notation that will be used throughout the work.
We let

s_{i,j}^{(N)}:=\mathbf{E}\{|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{2}\}

(2.2)

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]
and
V_{0}:=\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]}s_{i,j}^{(N)}\in[0,\infty)
.

Also, let
\mathbf{C}_{k}
be the set of ordered rooted trees with
k
edges (where
k\in\mathbb{N}
) of all non-isomorphic plane rooted trees with
k+1
vertices, i.e. all trees with
k+1
vertices, a vertex distinguished as a root and an ordering amongst the children of any vertex. The number of such trees is the
k-
th Catalan number, i.e.,

|\mathbf{C}_{k}|=\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k},

(2.3)

and a trivial bound that we will use is
|\mathbf{C}_{k}|\leq 2^{2k}
. For each such tree, we consider its vertices ordered
v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k}
so that
v_{0}
is the root, each parent is smaller than its children, and the children keep the order they have as vertices of an ordered tree. A labeling of such a tree is an ordered
k+1
-tuple
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{k})
of different objects, the object
\ell_{i}
is the label of vertex
v_{i}
.

A quantity of fundamental importance for the sequel is the following sum

M_{N}(k):=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{C}_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{k+1}\\
\text{ labeling of }T\end{subarray}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(T)}s^{(N)}_{i,j}.

(2.4)

E(T)
denotes the set of edges of the tree
T
. Note that
M_{N}(0)=N
since by convention the product over an empty index set equals 1.

Assumption 2.2
.

There is a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{M_{N}(k)}{N^{k+1}}=\int x^{2k}\,d\mu(x).

(2.5)

A tool for checking this assumption is explained in Remark
2.13
below.

If the sequence
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
satisfies both Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, then
\mu_{A_{N}/\sqrt{N}}\Rightarrow\mu
with probability one (see the proof of Theorem 3.2 of
[
30
]
). The measure
\mu
is symmetric with compact support contained in
[-2\sqrt{V_{0}},2\sqrt{V}_{0}]
. The compactness of the support follows from (
2.5
),
M_{N}(k)\leq|\mathbf{C}_{k}|N^{k+1}V_{0}^{k}
, and
|\mathbf{C}_{k}|\leq 2^{2k}
.
Let

\mu_{\infty}:=\sup\text{suppt}\,\mu.

(2.6)

We seek conditions under which the maximum eigenvalue of
A_{N}/\sqrt{N}
converges to
\mu_{\infty}
in probability. An easy argument will give us the lower bound, and since
\lambda_{\max}(A)\leq|A|_{\operatorname{op}}
for any symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, it will be enough to prove the upper bound for the operator norm of
A_{N}/\sqrt{N}
.

For this purpose, we need stronger assumptions. The following is stronger than Assumption
2.1
.

Assumption 2.3
.

(a)

\mathbf{E}a_{i,j}^{(N)}=0
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N],\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]}\mathbf{E}|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{2}\leq 1
, and
\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]}\mathbf{E}|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{4}<\infty
.

(b)

For any
{\varepsilon}>0
it is true that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{P}(|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|\geq{\varepsilon}\sqrt{N})=0.

(2.7)

Note that condition (
2.7
) is satisfied if we assume that all
\{a_{i,j}^{(N)}:N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]\}
have the same distribution with finite 4-th moment.

We gain control over
|A_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}
through the traces of high moments of
A_{N}
, and the main difficulty, which the next conditions (Assumption
2.4
and Assumption
2.6
) try to address, is how to connect these traces with
\mu_{\infty}
, which emerges out of
\{\lambda_{i}(A_{N}):i\in[N]\}
only after we take
N\to\infty
.

Assumption 2.4
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
i,j\in[N]
it is true that

s_{i,j}^{(N)}\leq\min\{s_{2i,2j}^{(2N)},s_{2i-1,2j}^{(2N)},s_{2i-1,2j-1}^{(2N)}\}.

(2.8)

For example, this assumption is satisfied if
s_{i,j}^{(N)}=h(i/N,j/N)
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]
, where
h:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,\infty)
is a function decreasing separately in each variable.

In order to give the next sufficient condition, we first give some definitions.

Definition 2.5
.

(i) We call graphon any Borel measurable function
W:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is symmetric and integrable.

(ii) For any bounded graphon
W
and any multigraph
G=(V,E)
, we call isomorphism density from
G
to
W
the quantity

t(G,W):=\int_{[0,1]^{|V|}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E}W(x_{i,}x_{j})\prod_{i\in V}dx_{i}.

(2.9)

Now, let
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
be a sequence of random matrices with elements having finite second moment. Each
A_{N}
defines a graphon,
W_{N}
, through the relation

W_{N}(x,y):=s^{(N)}_{\lceil Nx\rceil,\lceil Ny\rceil}

(2.10)

for each
(x,y)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]
. For this relation,
\lceil 0\rceil
denotes 1.

Assumption 2.6
.

There exists a graphon
W
such that the
W_{N}
of (
2.10
) satisfies

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}t(T,W_{N})=t(T,W)

(2.11)

for any finite tree
T
. Moreover, for any
D>0
there exists some
C=C(D)\in(0,\infty)
and
N_{0}=N_{0}(D)\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that for any
N\geq N_{0}
it holds

\int_{[0,1]^{2}}|W_{N}(x,y)-W(x,y)|\,dx\,dy\leq CN^{-D}.

(2.12)

This assumption together with Assumption
2.3
implies Assumption
2.2
(This will be explained in Lemma
4.2
). Again, we denote by
\mu_{\infty}
the maximum of the support of
\mu
.

The assumptions we made so far will lead to convergence in probability of the largest eigenvalue. Next we give some extra condition, which will lead to the almost sure convergence of the largest eigenvalue.

Assumption 2.7
.

(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
is a sequence of symmetric random matrices, the entries of each
A_{N}
are independent (up to symmetry), and there exists a random variable
X
with mean
0
, variance
1
, and finite
4+\delta
moment for some
\delta>0
, which stochastically dominates the entries of
A_{N}
in the following sense

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|\{A_{N}\}_{i,j}|\geq t)\leq\mathbf{P}(|X|\geq t),\text{ for all }t\in[0,\infty),N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N].

(2.13)

We are now ready to present our first main result.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
be a sequence of matrices satisfying Assumption
2.3
. Then if either Assumptions
2.2
and
2.4
hold or Assumption
2.6
holds, it is true that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|A_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}=\mu_{\infty}\text{ in probability }

(2.14)

where
\mu_{\infty}
is defined in (
2.6
).
Moreover, if the sequence
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
satisfies Assumption
2.7
, the convergence in (
2.14
) holds in the almost sure sense.

Note that Assumption
2.4
is restrictive and does not cover several of the well-known and studied models. Thus, in what follows, we try to extend the domain of validity of Theorem
2.8
. We first give two definitions.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
U\subset\mathbb{[}N]^{2}
:

\bullet
We call a
(x,y)\in U

internal point
of
U
if
\{(x+d_{1},y+d_{2}):d_{1},d_{2}\in\{-1,0,1\}\}\subset U
. We denote by
U^{o}
the set of internal points of
U
.

\bullet
We say that
U
is
axially convex
if
(i,j)\in U,(i,j^{\prime})\in U,r\in[N],(r-j)(r-j^{\prime})<0
imply
(i,r)\in U
and
(i,j)\in U,(i^{\prime},j)\in U,r\in[N],(r-i)(r-i^{\prime})<0
imply
(r,j)\in U
.

Definition 2.9

(Generalized step function variance profile)
.

Let
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
be a sequence of symmetric random matrices,
A_{N}
of dimension

(a)

If

then
R(A):=\{(i,j):(j,i)\in A\}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}
.

(b)

For any
m\in[d_{N}]

such that

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{B}^{(N)}_{m}\subset\mathcal{B}^{(2N)}_{f}.

(2.15)

(c)

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

intersects

Then if for all

(i,j)
-entry of

is given by

\displaystyle s_{i,j}^{(N)}:=\sum_{m\in[d_{N}]}s^{(N)}_{m}1_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{B}^{(N)}_{m}}

for some set of numbers

(A_{N})_{N\geq 1}

\bullet

A_{N}

\bullet

Next, we study the operator norm of two widespread random matrix models.

Definition 2.11

(Step function variance profile)
.

Consider

a)

m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and numbers
\{\sigma_{p,q}\}_{p,q\in[m]}\in[0,1]^{m\times m}
with
\sigma_{p,q}=\sigma_{q,p}
for all
p,q\in[m]
.

b)

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, a partition of
[N]
into
m

\{I_{p}^{(N)}\}_{p\in[m]}
. The numbering of the intervals is such that
x<y
whenever
x\in I_{p}^{(N)},y\in I_{q}^{(N)}
and
p<q
. Let
L_{p}^{(N)}
and

be the left and right endpoint respectively of
I_{p}^{(N)}
.

c)

Numbers
0=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{m-1}<\alpha_{m}:=1
. We assume that
\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{p}^{(N)}/N=\alpha_{p}
for each

.

d)

A random variable
X_{0}
with
\mathbf{E}(X_{0})=0,\mathbf{E}(X_{0}^{2})=1
.

For each

, define the matrix
\Sigma_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

(\Sigma_{N})_{i,j}=\sigma_{p,q}

, and let

.

Remark 2.13

(Checking Assumption
2.2
)
.

A sufficient condition for the validity of Assumption
2.2
is that
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
and there is a graphon
W
such that
W_{N}\to W
almost everywhere in
[0,1]\times[0,1]
.

Indeed, the bounded convergence theorem gives that
t(T,W_{N})\to t(T,W)
for all trees. Then Theorem 3.2 (a) of
[
30
]
shows that the ESD of
A_{N}/\sqrt{N}
converges almost surely weakly to a probability measure
\mu^{\sqrt{W}}
whose
2k
moment equals

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{T\in\mathbf{C}_{k}}t(T,W_{N})

(2.25)

while the moments of odd order are 0. Then, for each
T\in\mathbf{C}_{k}
,

0\leq t(T,W_{N})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{K+1}\\
\text{ labeling of }T\end{subarray}}N^{-k-1}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(T)}s^{(N)}_{i,j}=O(1/N),

(2.26)

because
t(T,W_{N})
is simply the same as the sum in the previous relation with the only difference that
i
is not required to be a labeling, i.e., it can have repetitions. It follows that Assumption
2.2
holds. As we remarked after (
2.5
),
\mu^{\sqrt{W}}
is symmetric and has bounded support. Denote by
\mu^{\sqrt{W}}_{\infty}
the largest element of the support.

If, in the two models above,
X_{0}
has finite
4+\delta
moment for some small
\delta>0
, then it is easy to see that the sequence
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
satisfies Assumptions
2.3
. It also satisfies Assumption
2.2
because it satisfies Assumption
2.1
and, in both cases,
W_{N}(x,y)
converges to
\sigma^{2}(x,y)
for almost all

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}

has mean value 0, variance 1, and finite

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|A_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}=\mu^{\sigma}_{\infty}\ \ \ \ \text{a.s.}

(2.27)

Let

(2.28)

3
Analysis of high order moments

Assume at the moment that the entries of
A_{N}
have finite moments of all orders.

We will relate the largest eigenvalue with a high moment of the measure
\mu_{N}
and at the same time this moment will be controlled by
\mu_{\infty}
. In general, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it is true that

\mathbf{E}\tr(A_{N}^{2k})=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},........,i_{2k}\in[N]}\mathbf{E}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{2k}a_{i_{l},i_{l+1}}^{(N)}\right)

(3.1)

with the conventions that
i_{2k+1}=i_{1}
, when
k=0
the sum is only over
i_{1}\in[N]
, and the product over an empty set equals 1.

Now, for a term with indices
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2k}
, we let
\textbf{i}:=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2k})
and
X(\textbf{i}):=\prod_{l=1}^{2k}a_{i_{l},i_{l+1}}^{(N)}
. For such an
i
we also use the term cycle. Then consider the graph
G(\textbf{i})
with vertex set

V(\textbf{i})=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2k}\}

and set of edges

\{\{i_{r},i_{r+1}\}:r=1,2,\ldots,2k\}.

(3.2)

As explained in
[
2
]
(in the proof of relation (3.1.6) there,
pages 49, 50 or in Theorem 3.2 of
[
30
]
), the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{k+1}}\mathbf{E}\tr(A^{2k})

remains the same if in the sum of (
3.1
) we keep only the summands such that

the graph
G(\textbf{i})
is a tree with
k+1
vertices

(3.3)

Then, necessarily, the path
i_{1}\to i_{2}\to\cdots\to i_{2k}\to i_{1}
traverses each edge of the tree exactly twice, in opposite
directions of course.
Such a
G(\textbf{i})
becomes an ordered rooted tree if we mark
i_{1}
as the root and order children of the same vertex according to the order they appear in the cycle.

Cycles
i
that don’t satisfy (
3.3
) we call bad cycles. So, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the sum in (
3.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\tr(A^{2k})

\displaystyle=M_{N}(k)+B_{N}(k),

(3.4)

where

\displaystyle M_{N}(k)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{C}_{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{(2k)\vee 1}:G(\mathbf{i})\sim T}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(G(\mathbf{i}))}s^{(N)}_{i,j},

(3.5)

\displaystyle B_{N}(k)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{2k}:\text{bad cycle}}\mathbf{E}X(\textbf{i}).

(3.6)

Recall that
\mathbf{C}_{k}
are the ordered rooted trees with
k
edges and
G(\mathbf{i})\sim T
means that the graphs are isomorphic as ordered rooted trees. Note also that
M_{N}(k)
has already been defined in (
2.4
) but the two definitions for it agree. Also,
M_{N}(0)=N,B_{N}(0)=0
.

The plan is to control the expectation of the trace in (
3.4
) through an appropriate bound involving various
M_{N}(j)^{\prime}s
. To control the term
B_{N}(k)
, we adopt the analysis of Section 2.3 of
[
27
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A_{N}
be an
N\times N
symmetric random matrix with independent entries (up to symmetry) and with
\mathbf{E}(a_{i,j}^{(N)})=0,s_{i,j}^{(N)}\leq 1
for all
N\in\mathbb{N},i,j\in[N]
. Assume additionally that the absolute value of the entries of the matrix are all supported in
[0,CN^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}]
for some
\epsilon>0
. Then for all
N
large enough and all integers
1\leq k<N
it is true that

|B_{N}(k)|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}(4k^{5})^{2k-2s}\left(CN^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}\right)^{2k-2s}\sum_{t=1}^{(s+1)\wedge k}(4k^{4})^{4(s+1-t)}M_{N}(t-1).

(3.7)

Proof.

We bound each term of the sum defining
B_{N}(k)
. Take a bad cycle
i
and let

•

t: the number of vertices of
G(\textbf{i})
,

•

s
: the number of the edges of
G(\textbf{i})
,

•

e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{s}
: the edges of
G(\textbf{i})
in order of appearance in the cycle,

•

a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}
: the multiplicities of
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{s}
in the cycle.

That is,
a_{q}
is the number of times the (undirected) edge
e_{q}
appears in the cycle. Note that
t\leq s+1
(true for all graphs) and
t\leq k
because the cycle is bad.

Additionally, in case
t\geq 2
, we let
T(\textbf{i})
be the rooted ordered tree obtained from
G(\textbf{i})
by keeping only edges that lead to a new vertex at the time of their appearance in the cycle. The root is
i_{1}
and we declare a child of a vertex smaller than another if it appears earlier in the cycle. In case
t=1
,
T(\textbf{i})
is the graph with one vertex,
i_{1}
, and one edge (loop) with end vertices
i_{1},i_{1}
. Thus,
T(\textbf{i})
has
t
vertices and
1\vee(t-1)
edges.

To bound
|\mathbf{E}X(\textbf{i})|
, notice that if any of
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}
is 1, we have
\mathbf{E}X(\textbf{i})=0
by the independence of the elements of
A_{N}
and the zero
mean assumption. We assume therefore that all multiplicities are at least 2. Using the information about the mean, variance, and support of
|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|
, we get that for any integer
a\geq 2
it holds

\mathbf{E}(|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{a})\leq(C_{1}N^{1/2-\epsilon})^{a-2}s_{i,j}^{(N)}.
Thus

\mathbf{E}|X(\textbf{i})|=\prod_{q=1}^{s}\mathbf{E}|X_{e_{q}}|^{a_{q}}\leq(CN^{1/2-{\varepsilon}})^{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{s}-2s}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(G(\textbf{i}))}s_{i,j}^{(N)}\leq(CN^{1/2-{\varepsilon}})^{2k-2s}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(T(\textbf{i}))}s_{i,j}^{(N)}.

(3.8)

In the second inequality, we used the fact that
s_{i,j}^{(N)}\in[0,1]
for all
i,j,N
.
For integers
s,t\geq 1,a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\geq 2
and
T\in\mathbf{C}_{t-1}
let

N_{T,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}}=\begin{array}[]{l}\text{the number of bad cycles with $T(\textbf{i})\sim T$, indices $1,2,\ldots,t$, appearing in this order,}\\
\text{and edge multiplicities }a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}.\end{array}

(3.9)

Using the bound on
N_{T,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}}
provided by Lemma
9.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle|B_{N}(k)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k\wedge(s+1)}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}}(CN^{1/2-{\varepsilon}})^{2k-2s}\left\{\mathbf{1}_{t=1}\sum_{i\in[N]}s_{i,i}^{(N)}+\mathbf{1}_{t\geq 2}\sum_{T\in\textbf{C}_{t-1}}N_{T,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}}\sum_{\textbf{i}\in[N]^{2k}:T(\textbf{i})\sim T}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(T(\textbf{i}))}s_{i,j}^{(N)}\right\}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k\wedge(s+1)}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}}(CN^{1/2-{\varepsilon}})^{2k-2s}\left\{\mathbf{1}_{t=1}\sum_{i\in[N]}s_{i,i}^{(N)}+\mathbf{1}_{t\geq 2}\sum_{T\in\textbf{C}_{t-1}}N_{T,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}}\sum_{\textbf{i}\in[N]^{2(t-1)}:T(\textbf{i})\sim T}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(T(\textbf{i}))}s_{i,j}^{(N)}\right\}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k\wedge(s+1)}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}}(CN^{1/2-{\varepsilon}})^{2k-2s}(4k^{4})^{4(s+1-t)+2(k-s)}M_{N}(t-1).

(3.12)

We used here the fact that
s_{i,i}^{(N)}\leq 1
, so that
\sum_{i\in[N]}s_{i,i}^{(N)}\leq N=M_{N}(0)
.
The inside sum in (
3.12
) is over all
s
-tuples of integers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}
greater than or equal to 2 with sum
2k
. By subtracting 2 from each
a_{i}
, we get an
s
-tuple of non-negative integers with sum
2k-2s
. The number of such
s
-tuples is
\left(\kern-1.99997pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{s}{2k-2s}\right)\kern-1.99997pt\right)
(combinations with repetition), which is at most
s^{2(k-s)}\leq k^{2(k-s)}
. Thus the above sum is bounded by

\sum_{s=1}^{k}(4k^{5})^{2(k-s)}(CN^{1/2-{\varepsilon}})^{2k-2s}\sum_{t=1}^{k\wedge(s+1)}(4k^{4})^{4(s+1-t)}M_{N}(t-1).

(3.13)

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
be a sequence of symmetric matrices and
R>0
so that the sequence satisfies Assumption
2.3
and the following condition
\Sigma(R)
:

For each
C_{1}>0
there are
C_{2}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that

M_{N}(k)\leq C_{2}N^{k+1}R^{2k}

(3.14)

for all
N,k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
with
N\geq N_{0}
and
1\leq k\leq C_{1}\log N
.

Then for each
\epsilon>0
, it holds

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|A_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}\geq R(1+\epsilon)\right)=0.

(3.15)

Proof.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1/8)
and define the
N\times N
matrices
A_{N}^{\leq},A_{N}^{>}
by

\displaystyle(A_{N}^{\leq})_{i,j}:=a^{(N)}_{i,j}1_{|a_{i,j}|\leq N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}},

(3.16)

\displaystyle(A_{N}^{>})_{i,j}:=a^{(N)}_{i,j}1_{|a_{i,j}|>N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}

(3.17)

for all
i,j\in[N]
. For a random matrix
H:=(h_{i,j})
,
\mathbf{E}H
denotes the matrix whose
(i,j)
element is
\mathbf{E}h_{i,j}
provided that the mean value of
h_{i,j}
can be defined. Note that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}|A_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(|A_{N}^{\leq}-\mathbf{E}A_{N}^{\leq}|_{\operatorname{op}}+|\mathbf{E}A_{N}^{\leq}|_{\operatorname{op}}+|A_{N}^{>}|_{\operatorname{op}}\right).

(3.18)

We will bound the three terms in the right hand side of the last inequality. For the first two, we use only Assumption
2.3
and the arguments in the proof of Theorem 2.3.23 in
[
27
]
.

1) The term
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\mathbf{E}(A^{\leq}_{N})|_{\operatorname{op}}
is a deterministic sequence that converges to 0 because, since
a_{i,j}^{(N)}
is centered, we have

|(\mathbf{E}A^{\leq}_{N})_{i,j}|=|(\mathbf{E}A^{>}_{N})_{i.j}|\leq N^{-3(\frac{1}{2}-\eta)}\sup_{N}\max_{i,j}\mathbf{E}|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{4}.

(3.19)

And using the inequality
|C|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq N||C||_{\max}
, we get that

|\mathbf{E}(A^{\leq}_{N})|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq N^{3\eta-\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{N}\max_{i,j}\mathbf{E}|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{4}\overset{N\to\infty}{\to}0.

2) The term
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|A^{>}_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}
converges to
0
in probability. Indeed, for any
\delta_{1}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|A^{>}_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}>\delta_{1}\sqrt{N})\leq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|>\delta_{1}\sqrt{N}\text{ for some }i,j\in[N])

(3.20)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|A^{>}_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}>\delta_{1}\sqrt{N}\text{ and }|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|\leq\delta_{1}\sqrt{N}\text{ for all }i,j\in[N]).

(3.21)

The first quantity goes to zero as
N\to\infty
because of (
2.7
). For the second, it is an easy exercise to show that if each entry of a matrix
M
has absolute value at most
a
and each row and column of
M
has at most one non-zero element then
|M|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq a
(use the expression
|M|_{\operatorname{op}}=\sup_{x:||x||_{2}=1}||Mx||_{2}
). Consequently, the probability in (
3.21
) is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq N}\mathbf{P}(|a_{i,j_{1}}|>N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta},|a_{i,j_{2}}|>N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq N}\mathbf{P}(|a_{i_{1},j}|>N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta},|a_{i_{2},j}|>N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta})

(3.22)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2N\binom{N}{2}\frac{\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]}(\mathbf{E}\{|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{4}\})^{2}}{N^{4-8\eta}}\leq\frac{1}{N^{1-8\eta}}\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+},i,j\in[N]}(\mathbf{E}\{|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|^{4}\})^{2}\overset{N\to\infty}{\to}0.

(3.23)

We used the independence of the entries in each row or column and Markov’s inequality.

3) To deal with
|A_{N}^{\leq}-\mathbf{E}A_{N}^{\leq}|_{\operatorname{op}}
, we will use Proposition
3.1
. Let

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{N}

\displaystyle:=A_{N}^{\leq}-\mathbf{E}A_{N}^{\leq},

(3.24)

\displaystyle s_{i,j}^{(N),\leq}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{E}\{(\tilde{A}_{N})_{i,j}^{2}\}.

(3.25)

Proposition
3.1
applies to
\tilde{A}_{N}
because any element of the matrix, say
(\tilde{A}_{N})_{i,j}
, has zero mean and variance
s_{i,j}^{(N),\leq}\leq\mathbf{E}\{(A_{N}^{\leq})^{2}\}\leq\mathbf{E}(A_{N}^{2})=s_{i,j}^{(N)}\leq 1
. Thus, if we denote by
\tilde{M}_{N}(m)
the terms (
2.4
) for
m\in[N]
and for the matrix
\tilde{A}_{N}
, we will have
\tilde{M}_{N}(m)\leq{M}_{N}(m)
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and Proposition
3.1
, gives that for any
1\leq k<N
,

\mathbf{E}\tr(\tilde{A}_{N}^{2k})\leq M_{N}(k)+\sum_{s=1}^{k}(4k^{5})^{2k-2s}\left(2N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}\right)^{2k-2s}\sum_{t=1}^{(s+1)\wedge k}(4k^{4})^{4(s+1-t)}M_{N}(t-1)

(3.26)

Now fix
C_{1}>0
, its value will be determined in (
3.32
) below. For
1\leq k\leq C_{1}\log N
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\tr(\tilde{A}_{N}^{2k})\leq C_{2}N^{k+1}R^{2k}+C_{2}\sum_{s=1}^{k}(4k^{5})^{2k-2s}\left(2N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}\right)^{2k-2s}\sum_{t=1}^{(s+1)\wedge k}(4k^{4})^{4(s+1-t)}N^{t}R^{2(t-1)}.

(3.27)

Next, we focus on the second summand in the right hand side of the previous inequality for
N
large enough. In the sum in

\displaystyle C_{2}\frac{k}{R^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{k}(4k^{5})^{2k-2s}\left(2N^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}\right)^{2k-2s}(4k^{4})^{4(s+1)}\left(\frac{NR^{2}}{(4k^{4})^{4}}\right)^{(s+1)\wedge k}

[in summing the geometric series in (

\mathbf{E}\tr(\tilde{A}_{N}^{2k})\leq N^{k+1}R^{2k}\{1+o(1)\}^{2k}.

with

.

, pick

(3.32)

and apply the above for

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|\tilde{A}_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}\geq R(1+\epsilon)\right)

A tool for proving almost sure convergence of the sequence
|A_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}/\sqrt{N}
is the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(A_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
be a sequence of matrices,
A_{N}
is
N\times N
, and
R>0
so that the sequence satisfies Assumption
2.3
(a), condition
\Sigma(R)
, and Assumption
2.7
. Then

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A_{N}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}\leq R\,\text{ a.s.}

(3.34)

Proof.

Pick
\eta\in(0,1/8)
, its exact value will be determined below, and define the matrices
A_{N}^{\leq},\mathbf{E}A_{N}^{\leq}
as in the proof of Proposition
3.2
. The proof will be accomplished once we show that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N}\frac{|A_{N}^{\leq}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}\leq R,\ \ \text{ a.s., and}

(3.35)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(A_{N}\neq A^{\leq}_{N}\text{ for infinitely many }N\right)=0.

(3.36)

Proof of (
3.35
).
Since
\Sigma(R)
holds for the sequence
(A_{N})_{N\geq 1}
, the proof of Proposition
3.2
(the part with heading 3) shows that

\limsup_{N}\frac{|A^{\leq}_{N}-\mathbf{E}A_{N}^{\leq}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}\leq\mu_{\infty}\ \text{ a.s. }

(3.37)

because the upper bound in (
3.33
) is summable with respect to
N
.
In the same proof it is shown that

\limsup_{N}\frac{|\mathbf{E}A_{N}^{\leq}|_{\operatorname{op}}}{\sqrt{N}}=0\text{ a.s. }

Using these two facts and the triangle inequality we get (
3.35
).

Proof of (
3.36
).
Let
X
be the random variable that stochastically dominates the entries of
A_{N}
in the sense of (
2.13
). Let
X_{N}
be a sequence of symmetric random matrices after an appropriate coupling such that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
i,j\in[N]
it is true that

\displaystyle|a^{(N)}_{i,j}|\leq|(X_{N})_{i,j}|

(3.38)

and the entries of
X_{N}
are independent up to symmetry and all following the same law as
X.
It is an easy exercise to show that for any
a,c>1
and
Y

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a^{k}\mathbf{P}(|Y|\geq c^{k})\leq\frac{1}{a-1}\mathbf{E}\big\{|Y|^{\frac{\log a}{logc}}\big\}.

(3.39)

Using this inequality and the fact that the random variable
X
has finite

moment, we get that all

satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{2m}\mathbf{P}(|X|\geq 2^{\frac{m}{2}(1-\eta)})<\infty.

(3.40)

Thus, picking in the beginning of the proof an arbitrary
\eta
with
0<\eta<(1/8)\wedge(\delta/(4+\delta))
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(A_{N}\neq A^{\leq}_{N}\text{ for infinitely many }N\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(\text{ for infinitely many $N$ there are }i,j\in[N]:|a_{i,j}^{(N)}|>CN^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}\right)

(3.41)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\text{ for infinitely many $N$ there are }i,j\in[N]:|X_{i,j}^{(N)}|>CN^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(X_{N}\neq X^{\leq}_{N}\text{ for infinitely many }N\right).

(3.42)

In the second line, the inequality is a consequence of (
3.38
), and the matrix
X^{\leq}_{N}
is the matrix whose
(i,j)
element is
(X_{N})_{i,j}\mathbf{1}_{|(X_{N})_{i,j}|\leq CN^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}
. The convergence of the series in (
3.40
). implies that the probability in the right hand side of (
3.42
) is
0
(see
[
2
]
, pages 94 and 95) and finishes the proof of (

).

\displaystyle\frac{M^{(1),\leq}_{N}(k)}{N^{k+1}}=\frac{1}{N^{k+1}}\mathbf{E}\tr\{(A^{(1),\leq}_{N})^{2k}\}+o(1)

(9.12)

as
N\to\infty
. We will prove that the right hand side converges to
\int x^{2k}d\mu
as
N\to\infty
. It will be convenient to let
B_{N}:=A_{N}^{(1),\leq}/\sqrt{N}
and
\{\lambda_{i}(B_{N}):i\in[N]\}
its eigenvalues.

Pick some
C>\mu_{\infty}
and consider the function
g_{C}(x)=(|x|\wedge C)^{2k}
, which is bounded and continuous. Then,

\frac{1}{N^{k+1}}\mathbf{E}\tr\{(A^{(1),\leq}_{N})^{2k}\}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}\{(\lambda_{i}\{B_{N}\})^{2k}\}

(9.13)

and the right hand side can be estimated as follows.

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}\{(\lambda_{i}\{B_{N}\})^{2k}\}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}g_{C}(\lambda_{i}\{B_{N}\})\right|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}\{\lambda^{2k}_{i}(B_{N})\mathbf{1}_{|\lambda_{i}(B_{N})|\geq C}\}

(9.14)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\lambda^{4k}_{i}(B_{N})}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(|\lambda_{i}(B_{N})|\geq C)}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathbf{E}\lambda_{i}^{4k}(B_{N})}{N}}\sqrt{\ \frac{\mathbf{E}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\textbf{1}_{|\lambda_{i}(B_{N})|\geq C}}{N}}\overset{N\to\infty}{\to}0.

To justify the convergence to zero, note that the quantity in the second square root converges to zero by our choice of
C>\mu_{\infty}
and the in probability weak convergence of the E.S.D. of
B_{N}
to
\mu
. The quantity in the first square root is bounded in
N
because, due to (
9.12
), its difference from
M_{N}^{(1),\leq}(2k)/N^{2k+1}
is bounded and the latter is less than
M_{N}(2k)/N^{2k+1}
which is bounded in
N
since it converges to
\int x^{4k}\,d\mu
.

The in probability weak convergence (
9.7
) implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{C}(\lambda_{i}\{B_{N}\})\to\int x^{2k}d\mu\text{ in probability, }

(9.15)

and the boundedness of
g_{C}
allows to conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}g_{C}(\lambda_{i}\{B_{N}\})=\int x^{2k}d\mu.

(9.16)

Thus, relations (
9.12
), (
9.13
), (
9.14
),(
9.16
) show that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{M^{(1),\leq}_{N}(k)}{N^{k+1}}=\int x^{2k}d\mu.

(9.17)

And this combined with (
9.11
) concludes the proof.
∎
