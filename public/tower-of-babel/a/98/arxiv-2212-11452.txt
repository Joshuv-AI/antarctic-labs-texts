Concentration of Equilibria and Relative Instability in Disordered Non-Relaxational Dynamics
Pax Kivimae

Abstract
We consider a system of random autonomous ODEs introduced by Cugliandolo et al. [ 1 ] , which serves as a non-relaxational analog of the gradient flow for the spherical p p -spin model. The asymptotics for the expected number of equilibria in this model was recently computed by Fyodorov [ 2 ] in the high-dimensional limit, followed a similar computation for the expected number of stable equilibria by Garcia [ 3 ] . We show that for p > 9 p>9 , the number of equilibria, as well as the number of stable equilibria, concentrate around their respective averages, generalizing recent results of Subag and Zeitouni [ 4 , 5 ] in the relaxational case. In particular, we confirm that this model undergoes a transition from relative to absolute instability, in the sense of Ben Arous, Fyodorov, and Khoruzhenko [ 6 ] .

Introduction

Understanding the typical behavior of dynamic equilibria (stationary points) in large disordered complex systems is a far-reaching problem motivated by numerous applications in fields such as machine learning, ecology, and economics, among others. The time evolution of such systems may often be modeled by a set of coupled first-order nonlinear autonomous ordinary differential equations (ODEs)

\frac{dx_{t}}{dt}=\textbf{F}(x_{t}),

(1.1)

where
F
is a random vector field on some smooth high-dimensional manifold where
x_{t}
takes its values.

Much of the interest in this question began with the celebrated paper of May
[
7
]
, who modeled the behavior of large ecological systems around an equilibrium point as a random linear system, which May then analyzed by employing tools developed in random matrix theory. They observed that as one varies the model parameters, the unique equilibrium transitions from being typically stable to typically unstable at a fixed value, a result which becomes deterministic in the high-dimensional limit. This result, known as the May-Wigner transition, has since been verified rigorously in a wide variety of models and formed the basis of the local study of equilibria in random systems (see
[
8
]
for a modern review).

To study global questions about the dynamics however, one must instead work with non-linear systems, which may have many competing equilibria of varying stability. The simplest statistic available in this case is the number of equilibria, as well as the number of equilibria which are additionally stable. The computation of these expected counts has seen extensive development in recent years through application of the Kac-Rice formula (see
[
9
]
and the results therein).

Foundational to recent work in the non-relaxational (i.e. where
F
is not the gradient vector field of some potential function) case has been works of Ben Arous, Fyodorov and Khoruzhenko
[
10
,
6
]
, who considered a non-linear analogue of May’s model and found the model undergoes two distinct transitions. More specifically, the expected number of equilibria was computed in
[
10
]
, who found that for suitably strong noise, this count would become exponentially large in the system dimension, whereas for weak noise the system would typically have only a single equilibrium point. This transition is expected to indicate the onset of complex behavior in the dynamics.

The behavior within the complex phase was further studied in
[
6
]
by considering the expected number of stable equilibria. They found a further transition occurs for this quantity, which is specific to the non-relaxational case. In particular, they found that if the relative strength of the non-relaxational terms was brought above a certain threshold, the system appeared to transition from having an exponentially large number of stable equilibria, to simply having none at all. They designated these as the regimes of ”relative” and ”absolute” instability, respectively. Moreover, by computing the ratio of these expected counts in the relative instability phase, they predicted that while the number of stable equilibria typically would grow exponentially large, the fraction of equilibria which are stable would be exponentially small.

Similar results results for such expected counts have been obtained in other models
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
, and in particular, the recent works of Fyodorov and Garcia
[
2
,
3
]
found analogous transitions in the non-relaxational spherical
p
-spin model. This model was introduced by Cugliandolo et al.
[
1
]
as a generalization of the spherical pure
p
-spin model, which has served as one of the paradigmatic models in complex systems. In
[
2
]
, asymptotics for the expected number of equilibria were given, which were found to grow exponentially, independent of the relative strength of the non-relaxational term. The expected number of stable equilibria however, were found in
[
3
]
to not only depend on this strength, but would transition from growing exponentially large to exponentially small below a certain threshold, indicating a transition from relative to absolute instability should occur at this point.

The purpose of this paper is to confirm that such a transition occurs in this model. This is, to the knowledge of the author, the first rigorous confirmation that the transition from relative to absolute instability occurs in any of the models predicted above. To do this, we must understand the typical (quenched) behavior of these counts, which in general may deviate drastically from what one would predict from their expected (annealed) value (see
[
14
]
and the discussion in
[
6
]
).

While powerful heuristics for computing the typical behavior exist in the physics literature
[
15
,
16
]
, rigorously computing them, and understanding when they coincide with their annealed counterparts, has only seen progress quite recently. In particular, in the relaxational case, major progress was made by Subag
[
4
]
, who showed that the number of local minima for the spherical
p
-spin model concentrates around its average. This result was then extended by Subag and Zeitouni
[
5
]
to the number of critical points. These results have proven important in establishing properties of the dynamics
[
17
]
as well as properties of corresponding potential landscape
[
18
,
19
]
. Additionally, these works have since been extended to other models in the relaxational case
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. The main goal of this paper is an extension of these methods, and in particular the required framework and results in random matrix theory, the non-relaxational setting.

We now recall the model, introduced in
[
1
]
and based on the earlier model introduction in the
p=2

[
23
]
. Fix a choice of
p\geq 3
and
\tau\in[-(p-1)^{-1},1]
. Let
\textbf{f}(x)=(f_{k}(x))_{k=1}^{N}
be the smooth centered Gaussian vector-valued function on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
whose components satisfy

\mathbb{E}[f_{k}(x)f_{l}(y)]=\delta_{kl}p\left(x,y\right)_{N}^{p-1}+\tau p(p-1)\left(\frac{y_{l}x_{k}}{N}\right)\left(x,y\right)_{N}^{p-2},\;\;\;1\leq k,l\leq N,

(1.2)

where
(x,y)_{N}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}
denotes the Euclidean inner product. We then define
\textbf{F}(x)
for

by

\textbf{F}(x):=-\lambda(x)x+\textbf{f}(x),

(1.3)

where
S_{N}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\|x\|^{2}=N\}
denotes the
(N-1)
-sphere of radius
\sqrt{N}
and
\lambda(x)=(x,\textbf{f}(x))_{N}

\textbf{F}(x)\in T_{x}S_{N}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(v,x)_{N}=0\}.

(1.4)

One may alternatively define
F
by specifying the terms in the following decomposition of

f_{i}(x)=c_{s}\nabla_{i}^{euc}H(x)+\frac{c_{a}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}A_{ij}(x),

(1.5)

where the matrix
A_{ij}(x)
is anti-symmetric (i.e.
A_{ij}=-A_{ji}
),
\nabla_{i}^{euc}
denotes the
i

are normalization constants. When
p=2
, this coincides with the decomposition of a matrix in symmetric and anti-symmetric parts. In addition, it is similar in form to the gradient-solenodial (Hodge) decomposition used to define the models in
[

,

to be a spherical
p
-spin model, and for each of

to be an independent
(p-2)
-spin model. Then taking
c_{s}=\sqrt{p^{-1}(1+(p-1)\tau)}
and

, the components of (
1.5
) satisfy (
1.2
), as checked in Lemma

We note that in the case of
\tau=1
we have that

and that
c_{s}=1
so that
\textbf{F}(x)=\nabla H(x)
. That is to say in this case,

is not only relaxational, but given by the gradient vector field of the spherical
p
-spin model. This contrasts the case where
\tau=-(p-1)^{-1}
, where we have that
c_{s}=0
so that

is purely anti-symmetric.

Another case of interest is when
\tau=0
, where one sees that (
1.2
) becomes
\mathbb{E}[f_{k}(x)f_{l}(y)]=\delta_{kl}p\left(x,y\right)_{N}^{p-1}
, so that each of the
f_{i}
are independent. Moreover, each coincides with a spherical
(p-1)
-spin model up to a normalization factor. In particular, in this case equilibria correspond to the zeros of a set of i.i.d. random functions.

We will now prepare to precisely state our results, for which we will need notation. Let us denote the number of equilibria of the vector field
F
as
\mathcal{N}_{N,tot}
, and similarly for denote the number of stable equilibria as
\mathcal{N}_{N,st}
. Asymptotics for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,tot}]
were obtained by Fyodorov
[
2
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
2
]

We have that for
|\tau|<1
and
p\geq 3
that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,tot}])=\frac{1}{2}\log(p-1).

(1.6)

Note that if one lets
\tau=1
, this coincides with the results of Auffinger, Ben Arous, and Černý
[
24
]
for the spherical
p
-spin model. Building upon this, Garcia
[
3
]
computed asymptotics for
\mathbb{E}]\mathcal{N}_{N,st}]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
3
]

We have that for
|\tau|<1
and
p\geq 3
that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,st}])=\frac{1}{2}\log(p-1)-\frac{(p-2)(1+\tau)}{2(1+(p-1)\tau)}=:\theta_{p,\tau}.

(1.7)

With some mild algebraic manipulation, this shows that for each
p\geq 3
, there is a critical value

\tau_{c}(p):=\frac{(p-2)-\log(p-1)}{(p-1)\log(p-1)-(p-2)}>0,

(1.8)

such that for
\tau<\tau_{c}(p)
it is exponentially rare that the system has even a single stationary equilibrium, while for
\tau>\tau_{c}(p)
the expected number of stationary equilibria is exponentially large, but growing at a rate slower than the expected total number. Note this coincides exactly with the transition from relative to absolute instability found in
[
6
]
. Our main result will be the following concentration result, which confirms that this transition occurs.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume
p>9
and
\tau\in(0,1)
. Then in probability as
N\to\infty

N^{-1}\log(\mathcal{N}_{N,tot})\Rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\log(p-1).

(1.9)

If in addition
\tau\in(\tau_{c}(p),1)
, then in probability as
N\to\infty

N^{-1}\log(\mathcal{N}_{N,st})\Rightarrow\theta_{p,\tau},

(1.10)

so that in particular we have also in probability as
N\to\infty

N^{-1}\log(\mathcal{N}_{N,st}/\mathcal{N}_{N,tot})\Rightarrow-\frac{(p-2)(1+\tau)}{2(1+(p-1)\tau)}.

(1.11)

We prove this by applying the second moment method. Before doing this, it will be useful to introduce the counts for equilibrium points with a given Lagrange multiplier. A key motivation for this is that the value taken by the Lagrange multiplier at an equilibrium point will essentially determine the equilibrium’s stability. Explicitly, let us define the threshold

E_{\infty;p,\tau}=(1+\tau)\sqrt{p(p-1)}.

(1.12)

Then a fixed equilibrium point

satisfying

is typically stable, and similarly a equilibrium point satisfying
\lambda(x)<E_{\infty;p,\tau}
is typically unstable. In particular, we will find it useful to denote for

, the total number of equilibria

, such that

by
\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)
, and similarly for
\mathcal{N}_{N,st}(u)
. We now recall the following more general form of Theorem

, also derived in
[

]
.

Theorem 1.4
.

[
2
]

Assume
|\tau|<1
and
p\geq 3
. For
u\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)])=\sup_{v>u}\Sigma^{p,\tau}(v),

(1.13)

where
\Sigma^{p,\tau}
is a fixed function recalled in (
1.29
) below.

An important threshold for the function
\Sigma^{p,\tau}
will be the unique value
E_{0;p,\tau}>0
, for which
\Sigma^{p,\tau}(v)<0
for

. Note by Markov’s inequality, it is exponentially rare for there to exist a single equilibrium such that the associated Lagrange multiplier is greater than

\epsilon>0
. We also observe that
\Sigma^{p,\tau}(E_{\infty;p,\tau})=\theta_{p,\tau}
, so that

With these thresholds defined, our primary result then is the following second moment estimates.

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume
\tau\in(0,1)
and
p>9

u\in[-\infty,E_{0;p,\tau})

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)]^{2}}=1.

If in addition
\tau\in(\tau_{c}(p),1)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,st}(u)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,st}(u)]^{2}}=1.

Remark 1.6

Note then when

By a standard argument, (

\log(\mathcal{N}_{N}(u))-\log(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)])\Rightarrow 0.

In particular, we observe that (

Theorem 1.7
.

For any
|\tau|<1

1.1
Methods

We will now discuss the methods we use to show our main result, with a more complete outline being the focus of Section
2
. The starting point will be the Kac-Rice formula, which will give us an explicit representation of
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)^{2}]
(see Lemma
2.1
). The primary difficulty in analyzing this expression is a term involving the product of absolute determinants of a pair of conditional Jacobians.

The corresponding expression for the first moment in
[
2
]
expresses
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)]
as a fixed multiple of the expected absolute determinant of a matrix sampled from the elliptic ensemble (recalled below in Definition
1.8
), up to a random multiple of the identity. Fixing this multiple, the problem of computing the first moment is reduced to producing asymptotics for the expected absolute characteristic polynomial of the elliptic ensemble.

To compute the second moment however, we must deal with both the effect the conditioning has on the Jacobian, as well as the effect of the correlations between the Jacobians themselves. The first effectively augments the law of the Jacobians by a low-rank perturbation, so we are reduced to the problem of computing the expected characteristic polynomial for perturbations of a pair of correlated matrices both sampled from the elliptic ensemble.

On the exponential scale, we do this by approximating the characteristic polynomial by the exponential of a more well-behaved linear statistic. Not only does this make the characteristic polynomial more stable under perturbation, but using suitable concentration estimates, we may show that this statistic may be replaced by its expectation, which is insensitive to correlations. In the symmetric case this method was explored quite fully in the work of Ben Arous, Bourgade and McKenna
[
25
]
, who showed how one may employ this method under a fairly mild set of conditions. We follow this method, which entails establishing and modifying a variety of results from symmetric matrix theory to the non-symmetric setting, where the eigenvalues are less stable under perturbation. This will be the focus of Section
7
. Many of our results are given specifically for the elliptic ensemble, but essentially only rely on concentration estimates for the matrix and rates of convergence for the expected empirical measures of shifted singular values. As such, we expect our general set-up should readily apply to most non-symmetric matrices for which the Kac-Rice formula has currently been applied.

To obtain estimates on the determinant term to vanishing order, as needed for the proof of Theorem
1.5
, will be a more difficult task. The key problem is that in this case the correlations between the Jacobians are no longer negligible, as indeed the fluctuations of the determinant are typically on the order of a polynomial factor. Thus the primary difficulty is to control these fluctuations, for which our main tool will be a moment bound for the determinant term which is tight up to a constant. An important technical input for this will be the computation of the higher moments for the absolute characteristic polynomial of the elliptic ensemble up to constant order, which is obtained in our companion paper
[
26
]
.

Finally we comment on our restrictions on
(p,\tau)
. We believe one could relax the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
in sight of Theorem
1.7
. The restriction on
p>9
is needed only in the proof of Lemma
2.12
and is similar in nature to the restriction to
p\geq 32
in
[
5
]
, though removing it is beyond the scope of our methods. The primary reason our proof holds for this lower value of
p
is that the characteristic polynomial when
\tau<1
has weaker fluctuations then in the symmetric case, as well as the related increase in the typical spacing of eigenvalues from
N^{-1}
in the symmetric case to
N^{-1/2}
.

While we believe our methods could be easily generalized to the case
\tau=0
, significant changes would be needed to include
\tau<0
. The first problem is that while the largest real eigenvalue of the elliptic ensemble is typically
1+\tau
, the largest singular value is typically
1+|\tau|
. This renders a large portion of Section
7
useless, as the largest singular value no longer accurately detects when a shift by the identity is positive definite. Moreover, we are unsure of how to obtain any constant order asymptotics for the higher moments of the absolute characteristic polynomial of the elliptic ensemble in this case.

1.2
Structure

We begin the paper with Section
2
, which outlines the general argument and reduces the proofs of our main results to a series of more technical results proven in the later sections.

In Section
3
we will provide our application of the Kac-Rice formula to the second moment, and give results which describe the structure of the conditional Jacobian at two points. In Section

We then refine our results to the constant order in Section

Section

contains the results we need to treat the characteristic polynomial on the exponential scale, as well as outside of the bulk. The methods are quite general, and we expect them to apply to many non-symmetric matrix ensembles which are suitably well behaved (as in the sense of

. The treatment of the characteristic polynomial in the bulk is more complicated, and is obtained in the companion paper

Finally, we provide two Appendices. Appendix

(1.26)

I_{\tau}(u)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2(1+\tau)}u^{2}-\frac{|u|(|u|-\sqrt{u^{2}-4\tau})}{4\tau}-\log(\frac{|u|+\sqrt{u^{2}-4\tau}}{2});\;\;\tau\neq 0\\
-\log u+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-\frac{1}{2};\;\;\tau=0\end{cases}.

(1.27)

It will be convenient to define a rescaling of this as well

\phi_{\tau,p}(u)=\phi_{\tau}\left(\frac{u}{\sqrt{p(p-1)}}\right).

(1.28)

With this notation the annealed complexity function is given as

\Sigma^{p,\tau}(u)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log(p-1)-\frac{u^{2}}{2p\alpha}+\phi_{\tau,p}\left(u\right)

(1.29)

where here and elsewhere
\alpha=1+\tau(p-1)
. For the two-point complexity function, we first define an auxiliary function

h(r):=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-r^{2p-2}}\right),

(1.30)

and define

\Sigma_{2}^{p,\tau}(r,u_{1},u_{2})=1+\log(p-1)+h(r)-\frac{1}{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\Sigma_{U}(r)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\phi_{\tau,p}\left(u_{1}\right)+\phi_{\tau,p}\left(u_{2}\right),

(1.31)

where
\Sigma_{U}(r)=\Sigma_{U,p,\tau}(r)
is a certain 2-by-2 matrix-valued function defined in (
A.62
) below.

Finally, we define the key classical ensemble occurring in this model, namely the (Gaussian) real elliptic ensemble.

Definition 1.8
.

For
-1\leq\tau\leq 1
we will say a
N
-by-
N
random matrix
A_{N}
belongs to the real elliptic ensemble
GEE(\tau,N)
if, when considered as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}}
,
A_{N}
is a centered Gaussian random vector whose covariance is given by

\mathbb{E}[A_{ij}A_{kl}]=\frac{1}{N}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\tau\delta_{il}\delta_{jl}).

(1.32)

The limiting law for the empirical measure (when
|\tau|<1
) for the elliptic ensemble was first identified by Girko
[
30
,
31
]
as the uniform measure on the ellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
defined above. More specifically, it was shown in
[
32
]
(see as well
[
33
]
) that for
A_{N}
sampled from
GEE(\tau,N)
, we have as
N\to\infty

\mu_{A_{N}}\Rightarrow\frac{1}{(1-\tau^{2})\pi}I_{\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}

(1.33)

in probability, where
I_{\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}
denotes the indicator function of the ellipse defined in (
1.23
). When
\tau=0
this recovers the circular law, and when
\tau\to 1
we recover the semi-circular law.

Outline of The Proofs for Theorems
1.5
and
1.7

In this section, we outline the general structure of the proof of our main results. We begin by giving the exact expressions for the first two moments of
\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)
and then introduce Theorem
2.3
, which concerns the computation of
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)^{2}]
up to the exponential scale. We then provide some analytic results about the resulting complexity functions, from which we derive Theorem
1.7
.

After this we will proceed to the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Our first step will be to reduce this proof to Proposition
2.6
, which concerns the contribution of points which are roughly orthogonal. This makes the computation at this scale possible, as the components appearing in the Kac-Rice formula become roughly independent. In particular, we are able to prove Theorem
1.5
through a series of estimates for the absolute determinant term in the Kac-Rice formula.

Our exact formula for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)]
which will follow from a standard application of the Kac-Rice formula. This expresses
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)]
as an integral over
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\in S_{N}\times S_{N}
, though as the law of
F
is isotropic, we will reduce this to an integral depending only on
r=(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})_{N}
by the co-area formula.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(M_{N}^{k}(r))_{k=1,2}
and
(U^{k}_{N}(r))_{k=1,2}
denote the random variables given in Lemma
3.3
. These are, respectively, a pair of
(N-1)
-by-
(N-1)
random matrices and a pair of random scalars with marginal law given by
(U^{1}_{N}(r),U^{2}_{N}(r))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(0,N^{-1}\Sigma_{U}(r))
where
\Sigma_{U}(r)
is given by (
A.62
). Then for nice
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and nice
I\subseteq(-1,1)

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)]=

(2.1)

C_{N}\int_{I}\left(\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-r^{2p-2}}\right)^{\frac{N-2}{2}}\mathcal{G}(r)\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(M^{k}_{N}(r))|I(U^{k}_{N}(r)\in B)\right]dr.

(2.2)

where here
\mathcal{G}(r):=(1-r^{2})^{-1/2}(1-(r^{p}-\tau(p-1)r^{p-2}(1-r^{2}))^{2})^{-1/2}
and

C_{N}=\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-1})\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-2})\left((N-1)(2\pi)^{-1}(p-1)\right)^{(N-1)},

(2.3)

where
\mathrm{vol}(S^{\ell-1})
denotes the surface area of the
(\ell-1)
-dimensional unit sphere

\mathrm{vol}(S^{\ell-1})=\frac{2\pi^{\ell/2}}{\Gamma(\ell/2)}.

(2.4)

This will be proven in Section
3
, based on the form of the Kac-Rice formula derived in Appendix
B
. From this expression, we see that the primary obstacle for obtaining asymptotics for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)]
is understanding the term

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(M^{k}_{N}(r))|I(U^{k}_{N}(r)\in B)\right].

(2.5)

To express how this is done, it will be convenient to recall the corresponding exact expression for the annealed complexity, which occurs as Theorem 3.1 of
[
2
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A_{N-1}
be sampled from
GEE(\tau,N-1)
and let
X_{N}
be an independent centered Gaussian random variable with variance
p\alpha/N
. Then for nice
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
we have that

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)]=\bar{C}_{N}\mathbb{E}\left[|\det\left(A_{N-1}-\hat{X}_{N}I\right)|I(X_{N}\in B)\right],

(2.6)

where here

\hat{X}_{N}=\left(\frac{N}{(N-1)p(p-1)}\right)^{1/2}X_{N},

(2.7)

\bar{C}_{N}=\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-1})\left((N-1)(2\pi)^{-1}(p-1)\right)^{(N-1)/2}.

(2.8)

Conditioning on the value of
X_{N}
, we see that the computation of
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)]
is similarly reduced to the computation of the expected value of the absolute characteristic polynomial of a matrix sampled from
GEE(\tau,N-1)
. To relate this to the term relevant to the second moment, we define
(\bar{M}^{k}_{N}(r,u_{1},u_{2}))_{k=1,2}
, to be
M^{k}_{N}(r)
conditioned on the event
(U^{1}_{N}(r)=u_{1},U^{2}_{N}(r)=u_{2})
. We will show in Section
5
that there are rank-
2
matrices
E_{N}^{k}(r,u_{1},u_{2})
such that we may write

(\bar{M}_{N}^{k}(r,u_{1},u_{2}))_{k=1,2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r)-\hat{u}_{k,N}I+E_{N}^{k}(r,u_{1},u_{2}))_{k=1,2}

(2.9)

where
(A_{N-1}^{1}(r),A_{N-1}^{2}(r))
are a pair of correlated
GEE(\tau,N-1)
matrices and

\hat{u}_{k,N}:=\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{(N-1)p(p-1)}}\right)u_{k}.

(2.10)

Thus to compare the first and second moments of
\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)
, we want to show that the contribution to the determinant coming from the perturbation term
E_{N}^{k}(r,u_{1},u_{2})
, as well as the effect of the correlations between the matrices
(A_{N-1}^{1}(r),A_{N-1}^{2}(r))
, are both negligible.

On the exponential scale, which is sufficient to obtain the leading order term of
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)]
, this will follow by replacing the determinant with a suitably regularized analog. For brevity, we will only obtain an upper bound for the leading order behavior, as it is all we will need to demonstrate concentration. The corresponding lower bound may be obtained by applying the methods developed in
[
25
]
. The following computation will be our main result on this scale, whose proof will be the focus of Section
4
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For nice
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and nice
I\subseteq(-1,1)
, we have that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)])\leq\sup_{u_{1},u_{2}\in B,r\in I}\Sigma_{2}^{p,\tau}(r,u_{1},u_{2}).

(2.11)

We note that with Theorems
1.4
and
2.3

1.7

Lemma 2.4

For nice
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
that intersects
(-E_{0;p,\tau},E_{0;p,\tau})

\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)^{2}])\leq\sup_{u\in B,r\in(-1,1)}\Sigma_{2}^{p,\tau}(r,u,u).

(2.12)

This result will be proven in Section

Lemma 2.5

Fix a nice subset

(-E_{0;p,\tau},E_{0;p,\tau})

if and only if

\sup_{v\in B,r\in I}\Sigma_{2}^{p,\tau}(r,v,v)=2\sup_{v\in B}\Sigma^{p,\tau}(v),

(2.13)

with equality replaced by

Proof of Theorem
1.7

By Jensen’s inequality we only need to show that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(u)]^{2}}\right)\leq 0.

and

∎

2.5

\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N}-uI)|^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N}-uI)|]^{2}}.

In the case where

and

To deal with this, we first remove a number of negligible subsets of points from our counts. We show below (see Remark

Thus to show the desired claim for

Lemma 5.1

Fix a Gaussian pair

(5.1)

We now proceed to the proof of Lemma

In the symmetric case, rotation invariance is quite strong, and this gives a direct recurrence in terms of the characteristic polynomials of smaller size. In our case however, we instead rely on direct comparison of the terms in this expansion for both

Proof of Lemma
2.10
.

We will fix and omit the choice of

(5.3)

By Lemma

(5.5)

+\mathbb{E}[|\det(\bar{H}_{N}-vI)|^{\ell}|\bar{V}_{N}^{t}\bar{G}_{N}\bar{W}_{N}|^{\ell}].

(5.6)

The first term is bounded by

\bar{R}_{N}=(\bar{V}_{N},\bar{W}_{N}),\;\;\;\bar{Q}_{N}=\sqrt{\|\bar{V}_{N}\|^{2}\|\bar{W}_{N}\|^{2}-(\bar{V}_{N},\bar{W}_{N})^{2}}.

s_{N-1}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)\geq s_{N-1}(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)

(5.31)

-\left(1-(1-|r_{N}|^{p-2})^{1/2}\right)s_{1}(A_{N-1}^{k})-|r_{N}|^{(p-2)/2}s_{1}(A_{N-1}),

(5.32)

we see that on
\mathcal{E}_{\text{large}}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},k}^{c}
, and for large enough
N
, that

s_{N-1}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)>c|r_{N}|^{(p-3)/2-c}.

(5.33)

By Corollary
7.2
we have that for
k=1,2

|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|\leq|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|\left(1+\frac{\|E_{N}^{k}\|}{s_{N-1}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r)-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)}\right)^{2},

(5.34)

Thus conditional on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\text{large}}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},2}^{c}
we have that

|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|\leq|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|\left(1+C|r_{N}|^{c/2}\right)^{2}=

(5.35)

|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|(1+o(1)).

(5.36)

To further simplify the term on the right, we will introduce our final event. Let us denote
K=[2/c]^{-1}
and
\epsilon=K^{-1}
so that
\epsilon<c
. We define

\mathcal{E}_{\text{medium}}=\bigcup_{k=1,2}\bigcup_{m=0}^{K-1}\{s_{N-[N^{\epsilon m}]+1}(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)>cN^{\epsilon m-1}\}.

(5.37)

We observe that for
1\leq m\leq K
, the number of
i
such that
N-[N^{\epsilon(m-1)}]<i\leq N-[N^{\epsilon m}]
is less than
N^{\epsilon m}
. Then applying Corollary
7.2
again we see that

\frac{|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|}{|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\left(1+\frac{s_{i}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-A_{N-1}^{k})}{s_{N-i}(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)}\right)

(5.38)

\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\left(1+\frac{s_{1}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-A_{N-1}^{k})}{s_{N-i}(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)}\right)\leq

(5.39)

\prod_{m=0}^{K-1}\left(1+\frac{s_{1}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-A_{N-1}^{k})}{s_{N-[N^{\epsilon m}]+1}(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)}\right)^{N^{\epsilon(m+1)}}.

(5.40)

We note that on
\mathcal{E}_{\text{large}}^{c}
we have that

s_{1}(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-A_{N-1}^{k})\leq\left(1-(1-|r_{N}|^{p-2}_{N})^{1/2}\right)s_{1}(A_{N-1}^{k})

(5.41)

+|r_{N}|^{(p-2)/2}s_{1}(A_{N-1})\leq C|r_{N}|^{(p-2)/2}.

(5.42)

Thus conditional on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\text{large}}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{medium}}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},k}^{c}
we have that

\frac{|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}(r_{N})-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|}{|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|}\leq

(5.43)

C\left(1+C|r_{N}|^{1/2+c}\right)^{N^{\epsilon}}\times\prod_{m=1}^{K-1}(1+C|r_{N}|^{(p-2)/2}N^{1-\epsilon m})^{N^{\epsilon(m+1)}}.

(5.44)

As
|r_{N}|<C\sqrt{\log(N)/N}
, we see that the first term is
1+o(1)
. Similarly, as
(p-2)/4>1+\epsilon
, we see that
|r_{N}|^{(p-2)/2}N^{1+\epsilon}\to 0
, so that each term in the product on the right is
1+o(1)
. Combining these claims we see that for
k=1,2
and on
\mathcal{E}_{\text{large}}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{medium}}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},2}^{c}

|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|\leq|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|(1+o(1)).

(5.45)

In particular, we see that to prove both of our desired claims it is sufficient to bound the contribution to the determinant coming from each of these events.

To begin we will recall some bounds on the probabilities for these events coming from Section
7
. By Lemma
7.6
we see that
\mathbb{P}(s_{1}(A_{N-1})>C)\leq Ce^{-Nc}
, and similarly for
A_{N-1}^{k}
. Combining this with the final claim of Lemma
5.2
we conclude that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\text{large}})\leq Ce^{-cN^{1/2}}
. By applying Lemma
7.10
we see as well that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\text{medium}})\leq Ce^{-cN^{\epsilon}}
. Finally by Lemma
7.8
, for
\ell=1,2
we have that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},\ell})\leq C\left(Nr^{(p-3)/2-c}_{N}+e^{-cN}\right).

(5.46)

Now to first let us bound the contribution from an arbitrary event
\mathcal{A}
. We see from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|I_{\mathcal{A}}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|^{2}]^{1/2}\leq

(5.47)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})^{1/2}\prod_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}\left[|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|^{4}\right]^{1/4}.

(5.48)

Employing Lemma
2.10
we have that

\prod_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}[|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|^{4}]^{1/4}\leq CN^{3/2}\prod_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|].

(5.49)

In particular, if
N^{3/2}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})^{1/2}\to 0
we have that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|I_{\mathcal{A}}]}{\prod_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|]}\right)=0.

(5.50)

This holds for both
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{E}_{\text{large}}
and
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{E}_{\text{medium}}
in either case, so all that remains is to treat the case of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},\ell}
for
\ell=1,2
. For this note that for an event
\mathcal{A}
, which is only dependant on
A_{N-1}^{1}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|I_{\mathcal{A}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{1})|^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{2})|^{2}I_{\mathcal{A}}]^{1/2}=

(5.51)

\left(\prod_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}[|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k})|^{2}]^{1/2}\right)\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})^{1/2}\leq

(5.52)

CN^{1/4}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})^{1/2}\prod_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}^{k}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|]

(5.53)

where in the second to last equality we have employed the independence of
A_{N-1}^{1}
from
\bar{M}_{N}^{2}
. A similar inequality holds for events only involving
A_{N-1}^{2}
. In particular, when the event only depends on a single
A_{N-1}^{k}
, it suffices to show that
N^{1/2}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})\to 0
. In view of (
5.46
) this holds for
\mathcal{E}_{\text{small},k}
as
\frac{1}{2}+1-\frac{(p-3)}{4}<0
for
p>9
.

With this done, we will now proceed to the proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
2.9
.

By employing the expression for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)]
given in Lemma
2.1
and the expression for
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)]
given in Lemma
2.2
we see that

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,2}(B,I)]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)]^{2}}=\bar{C}_{N}^{-2}C_{N}\int_{I}\left(\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-r^{2p-2}}\right)^{\frac{N-2}{2}}\mathcal{G}(r)R_{N}(r)dr,,

(5.54)

R_{N}(r):=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(M_{N}^{k}(r))|I(U_{N}^{k}(r)\in B)]}{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{X}_{N}I)|I(X_{N}\in B)]^{2}}.

(5.55)

We note

\bar{C}_{N}^{-2}C_{N}=\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-2})/\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-1}).

(5.56)

By Stirling’s approximation we see that

\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-2})/\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-1})=(1+O(N^{-1}))\sqrt{N/2\pi}.

(5.57)

We also note that as
p\geq 3
,
\mathcal{G}(r)=1+O(r^{2})
. Thus (
5.54
) can be rewritten as

(1+O(N^{-1}))\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi}}\int_{(-\rho_{N},\rho_{N})}e^{-N(\frac{r^{2}}{2}+O(r^{3}))+O(r^{2})}R_{N}(r)dr.

(5.58)

To compute
R_{N}(r)
we recall that
\sqrt{N}(U_{N}^{1}(r),U_{N}^{2}(r))
is centered Gaussian vector with covariance matrix
\Sigma_{U}(r)
, whose inverse is computed in (
A.62
). We note that
k_{2}(r)=O(r^{p})
,
k_{1}(r)-b(r)=O(r^{2p-2})
, and
b(r)=1+O(r^{2p-2})
so that

\Sigma_{U}(r)^{-1}=\frac{1}{p\alpha b(r)}\begin{bmatrix}k_{1}(r)&k_{2}(r)\\
k_{2}(r)&k_{1}(r)\end{bmatrix}=(p\alpha)^{-1}I+O(r^{p}).

(5.59)

In particular the density of the event
(U_{N}^{1}(r)=u_{1},U_{N}^{2}(r)=u_{2})
is given by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2}\det(\Sigma_{U}(r))}}\exp\left(-\frac{N}{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\Sigma_{U}(r)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=

(5.60)

\frac{1+O(r^{p})}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2}(p\alpha)^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{N}{2p\alpha}(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2})(1+O(r^{p}))\right).

(5.61)

So we see conditioning on the energy value in both the numerator and the denominator in
R_{N}(r)
that

R_{N}(r)=e^{-NO(r^{3})}\frac{\int_{B\times B}\mathbb{E}\big[\prod_{k=1,2}|\det(\bar{M}_{N}^{k}(r,u_{1},u_{2}))|]e^{-\frac{N}{2p\alpha}u_{k}^{2}}du_{1}du_{2}}{\int_{B\times B}\prod_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{u}_{N,k}I)|]e^{-\frac{N}{2p\alpha}u_{k}^{2}}du_{1}du_{2}}.

(5.62)

We recall the fact that for two arbitrary positive functions
f
and
g
on some Euclidean subset
B
, we have that

\frac{\int_{B}f(u)du}{\int_{B}g(u)du}\leq\sup_{u\in B}\frac{f(u)}{g(u)}.

(5.63)

Thus inserting (
5.62
) into (
5.58
) and maximizing over the values of
(u_{1},u_{2})
using (
5.63
) completes the proof.
∎

Now give a proof of Lemma

, which is relatively straightforward given Theorem

.

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

For the duration of this proof we will adopt the notation

\mathcal{N}_{N,unst}(B)=\mathcal{N}_{N}(B)-\mathcal{N}_{N,st}(B)

(5.64)

for the total number of unstable points, so that we must show that for
u>E_{\infty;p,\tau}

\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,unst}((u,\infty))]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}((u,\infty))]}\right)<0.

(5.65)

We first observe that

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,unst}((u,\infty))]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}((u,\infty))]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,unst}((u,\infty))]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}((u,u+1))]}\leq

(5.66)

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}([u+1,\infty))]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,unst}((u,u+1))]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}((u,u+1))]}.

(5.67)

To study the first term in the right-most expression, we note that as
\Sigma^{p,\tau}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
(see Remark
6.2
) we have by Theorem
1.4
that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}((u,u+1))]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}([u+1,\infty)]}\right)>0.

(5.68)

This shows the first term is exponentially small, so in particular it suffices to show that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,unst}((u,u+1))]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}((u,u+1))]}\right)<0.

(5.69)

By conditioning on
X_{N}
in Lemma
2.2
we have that

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N}((u,u+1))]=\bar{C}_{N}\sqrt{\frac{N}{p\alpha}}\int_{u}^{u+1}\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I)|]e^{-N\frac{v^{2}}{2p\alpha}}dv.

(5.70)

A similar application of the Kac-Rice formula for stable points gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,st}((u,u+1))]=

(5.71)

\bar{C}_{N}\sqrt{\frac{N}{p\alpha}}\int_{u}^{u+1}\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I)|I(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1}))\leq\hat{v}_{N})]e^{-N\frac{v^{2}}{2p\alpha}}dv,

(5.72)

where as above,
\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1})
is the eigenvalue with the largest real part. In particular, we may write

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{N,unst}((u,u+1))]=

(5.73)

\bar{C}_{N}\sqrt{\frac{N}{p\alpha}}\int_{u}^{u+1}\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I)|I(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1}))>\hat{v}_{N})]e^{-N\frac{v^{2}}{2p\alpha}}dv.

(5.74)

The event on the right hand side may equivalently be stated as saying that at least one eigenvalue of
A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I
has positive real part.

Taking the ratio these integrands we see that the left hand side of (
5.69
) is bounded by

\sup_{u<v<u+1}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I)|I(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1}))>\hat{v}_{N})]}{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I)|]}\leq

(5.75)

\sup_{u<v<u+1}\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1}))>\hat{v}_{N})^{1/2}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I)|^{2}]^{1/2}}{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N-1}-\hat{v}_{N}I)|]}\right).

(5.76)

The second term on the right is polynomial, so we focus on bounding the first. We note that for
u<v<u+1

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1}))>\hat{v}_{N})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1}))>\hat{u}_{N}).

(5.77)

Now using the inequalities

\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N}-1))\leq|\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1})|\leq\sigma_{1}(A_{N-1})

(5.78)

(recall that
\sigma_{1}(A_{N-1})
is the largest singular value) we have that

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{N-1}(A_{N-1}))>\hat{u}_{N})\leq\mathbb{P}(s_{1}(A_{N-1})>\hat{u}_{N}).

(5.79)

Now as
u>E_{\infty;p,\tau}
we see that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\hat{u}_{N}>1+\tau
. In particular, by Lemma
7.6
we see that there are
C,c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(s_{1}(A_{N-1}))>\hat{u}_{N})\leq Ce^{-cN}.

(5.80)

Combining this with Theorem
2.11
completes the proof.

We now give a proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

We note that

\mathbb{E}[(X_{N},Y_{N})^{2}]=N^{-1}(N-1)\Sigma_{12}^{2}+N^{-1}(\Sigma_{12}^{2}+2\Sigma_{11}\Sigma_{22})=\Sigma_{12}^{2}+O(N^{-1}),

(5.81)

and similarly

\mathrm{Var}(\|X_{N}\|^{2}),\mathrm{Var}(\|Y_{N}\|^{2})=O(N^{-1})

\mathcal{A}_{N}=\left\{\min(|(X_{N},Y_{N})^{2}-\Sigma_{12}^{2}|,|\|X_{N}\|^{2}-\Sigma_{11}^{2}|,|\|Y_{N}\|^{2}-\Sigma_{22}^{2}|)>N^{-1/4}\right\}

(5.82)

Chebysev’s inequality shows that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{N})\to 0

N\to\infty

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[f(\|X_{N}\|^{2},\|Y_{N}\|^{2},(X_{N},Y_{N})^{2})I_{\mathcal{A}^{c}_{N}}]=f(\Sigma_{11}^{2},\Sigma_{22}^{2},\Sigma_{12}^{2}).

\mathbb{E}[f(\|X_{N}\|^{2},\|Y_{N}\|^{2},(X_{N},Y_{N})^{2})I_{\mathcal{A}_{N}}]\leq

(5.84)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{N})^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[f(\|X_{N}\|^{2},\|Y_{N}\|^{2},(X_{N},Y_{N})^{2})^{2}]^{1/2},

(5.85)

\Sigma_{Z}^{1,2}(r)=\Sigma_{Z}^{2,1}(r)^{t}
verifies the first claim. The claim for

is similar, which establishes (
5.26
).

To complete the proof of Lemma

, we need to prove the statements about

. The claim involving the rank is obvious, so we focus on the latter. The primary tool is the following result, proven in Appendix

, which quantifies the rate at which

vanishes as

Lemma 5.3
.

For
\epsilon>0
there is
C>0
such that for
|r|<1-\epsilon
,
k,l=1,2
, and
1\leq i\leq N-2

\mathbb{E}[(Z_{N-1,i}^{k}(r))^{2}]\leq CN^{-1}r^{p-3},\;\;\mathbb{E}[(Z_{i,N-1}^{k}(r))^{2}]\leq CN^{-1}r^{p-3},

(5.97)

\mathbb{E}[(Z_{N-1,N-1}^{k}(r))^{2}]\leq CN^{-1}r^{p-4},\;\;|m^{k}_{l}(r)|\leq Cr^{p-3}.

(5.98)

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

As noted, we only need to show (
5.27
). We note that

\|E^{k}_{N}(r,u_{1},u_{2})\|^{2}_{F}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\left(Z_{i,N-1}^{k}(r)^{2}+Z_{N-1,i}^{k}(r)^{2}\right)+

(5.99)

(m_{1}^{k}(r)\hat{u}_{N,1}+m_{2}^{k}(r)\hat{u}_{N,2}+Z^{k}_{N-1,N-1}(r))^{2}.

(5.100)

By Lemma
5.3
and the independence of these elements, we see that for large enough

,

is stochastically dominated by

(5.101)

where

is a

and

is a standard Gaussian random variable. By appealing to Cramér’s Theorem, we see that for

. More simply note that there are

such that

Lemma 6.1

Proof.

This will follow from studying the behavior of the function

In the case where

\bar{\Sigma}_{2}^{p,\tau}(1,u)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log(p-1)+\frac{u^{2}}{u_{th;p,\tau}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\log(p-1)}{2}-\frac{\log(p-1)(p-1)(1+\tau)}{(p-2)}+

(6.55)

\frac{\log(p-1)\alpha}{(p-2)}\bigg)=\frac{\log(p-1)}{2}\left(1-\frac{u^{2}}{u_{th;p,\tau}^{2}}\right),

(6.56)

which verifies (
6.45

∎

Random Matrix Theory and Elliptic Ensembles

In this section we will prove the majority of the random matrix results we need concerning the elliptic ensembles. Our first goal will be to give a proof of Lemma
4.2
, which gives the behavior of the characteristic polynomial on the exponential scale. Our other main result will be Proposition
7.9
, which gives our bounds on the characteristic polynomial outside of the bulk. In the course of proving these, we will also need an overcrowding result to control the behavior of the smallest singular value. This is given by Lemma
7.8
. The majority of the results in this section will follow from applying standard Gaussian concentration inequalities to the forms of the elliptic law given in
[

To begin we recall some general facts from linear algebra. The first of these is the Hoffman-Wielandt inequality (see Lemma 2.1.19 of
[
38
]

n

symmetric matrices

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{i}(B)|^{2}\leq\text{\rm Tr}(A-B)^{2}=\|A-B\|^{2}.

(7.1)

We also recall that for an
n
-by-
n
matrix

\bar{M}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&M\\
M^{t}&0\end{bmatrix}

-s_{1}(M),\dots,-s_{n}(M),s_{n}(M),\dots,s_{1}(M)

-by-
n
matrices

and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|s_{i}(A)-s_{i}(B)|^{2}\leq\|A-B\|^{2}.

(7.3)

From this statement, we conclude the following result, which may be compared to Lemma 2.3.1 of
[
38
]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

[
38
]

Let
f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function of norm less than
C
. Then for any

the function on
N
-by-
N
matrices given by

A\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\nu_{A,z}(dx)

has Lipschitz norm bounded by
C/\sqrt{N}
(with respect to the Frobenius norm)

Proof.

If
A
and
B
are two

N
matrices then

|\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\nu_{A,z}(dx)-\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\nu_{B,z}(dx)|\leq\frac{C}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|s_{i}(A-zI)-s_{i}(B-zI)|\leq

(7.5)

\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|s_{i}(A-zI)-s_{i}(B-zI)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\|A-B\|^{2}.

(7.6)

∎

We will also need a bound on the determinant of the sum of two matrices. In the case of symmetric matrices this follows from the main result of
[

]
, and for non-symmetric case it follows by applying the symmetric case to (

Corollary 7.2

In particular, if

We will now give a concentration estimate for

To make use of this, we will need some tail estimates for the largest singular value. By Corollary 2.3 of

Lemma 7.6

For

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[s_{1}(A_{N})]=1+|\tau|,

(7.20)

(7.21)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(s_{1}(A_{N})>1+|\tau|+\epsilon)e^{N\epsilon^{2}/32}=0.

Proof.

To begin we note that

s_{1}(A_{N})=\sup_{x,y\in S^{N-1}}|x^{t}A_{N}y|.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{|z|<K}\bigg|(N\ell)^{-1}\log(\mathbb{E}[\exp(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(x)\nu_{A_{N},z}(dx))])

Lemma 7.8

For any

\mathbb{P}\left(s_{N}(A_{N}-uI)\leq\epsilon\right)\leq C(\epsilon N+e^{-cN}).

We first deal with the case of

\mathbb{P}(s_{N}(A_{N}-uI)\leq 1)\leq\mathbb{P}(|u|-s_{1}(A_{N})\leq 1)\leq\mathbb{P}(3\leq s_{1}(A_{N}))\leq Ce^{-cN}

\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)\leq

\mathbb{P}(\nu_{A_{N},x}([0,\epsilon])\neq 0)=\mathbb{P}(s_{N}(A_{N}-xI)\leq\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}(|x|-\epsilon\leq s_{1}(A_{N})).

7.6
there is
C,c>0
such that

\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}]([0,\epsilon])\leq\mathbb{P}(\nu_{A_{N},x}([0,\epsilon])\neq 0)\leq\mathbb{P}(|x|-\epsilon\leq s_{1}(A_{N}))\leq Ce^{-cN}.

(7.64)

In particular, for the limiting measure we see that

\nu_{x}([0,\epsilon])=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}]([0,\epsilon])=0.

(7.65)

Thus by the above results we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(y)\nu_{x}(dy)=\phi_{\tau}(x).

(7.66)

In particular, this shows that on the exponential scale, making the replacement of
\log
by
\log_{\epsilon}
without taking
\epsilon\to 0
should yield a tight upper-bound, which makes sense as no singular values should greatly exceed
1+\tau
. However to push this to the constant scale will be more technical, and we first must replace
\epsilon
with a much smaller value.

For this first note that for
\delta\in(0,\epsilon)
, we have the crude bound

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)-\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right|\leq\log(\epsilon/\delta)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}]([0,\epsilon]).

(7.67)

In particular, if we take
\delta_{N}:=e^{-N^{10}}
, then we have (potentially for larger
C
) that

\log(\epsilon/\delta_{N})\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}]([0,\epsilon])\leq CN^{10}e^{-cN}.

(7.68)

In particular, adjusting
C,c>0
, we have that

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)-\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right|\leq Ce^{-cN},

(7.69)

so enlarging
C>0
again we have that

\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)\leq\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)\leq

(7.70)

C\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right).

(7.71)

In particular for larger
C>0
we have that

\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N}-xI)|^{\ell}]\leq C\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right).

(7.72)

We will show that we also have that

\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N}-xI)|^{\ell}]\geq C^{-1}\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right).

(7.73)

For this we define the event

\mathcal{E}_{N}=\{s_{N}(A_{N}-uI)>\delta_{N}\}.

(7.74)

By Lemma
7.8
there is
C,c>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c})\leq Ce^{-cN}
. We note that by excluding the event
\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}
, we get the bound

\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N}-xI)|^{\ell}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)I_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}].

(7.75)

We may rewrite the right hand side as

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)I_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\right].

(7.76)

We show that the second term is negligible compared to the first. Indeed, by Jensen’s inequality we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)\right]\geq\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right),

(7.77)

and by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)I_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\right]\leq

(7.78)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N2\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c})^{1/2}\leq

(7.79)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N2\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c})^{1/2}.

(7.80)

Proceeding as in the above work, we see that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N2\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)\right]^{1/2}=\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(y)\nu_{x}(dy),

(7.81)

and similarly that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)=\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(y)\nu_{x}(dy).

(7.82)

In particular (
7.77
), (
7.80
), and the bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c})\leq Ce^{-cN}
together show that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\nu_{A_{N},x}(dy)\right)I_{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{c}}\right]}{\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)}=0.

(7.83)

Thus with (
7.76
) we have shown (
7.73
), so altogether,

C^{-1}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N}-xI)|^{\ell}]}{\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)}\leq C.

(7.84)

Note this suffices to establish (
7.58
) as employing (
7.84
) in the case of
\ell=1
we obtain that

C^{-\ell}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\det(A_{N}-xI)|]^{\ell}}{\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)}\leq C^{\ell},

(7.85)

so using (
7.84
) again in the case of our chosen
\ell
gives (
7.58
).

Next, we show how the second claim, (
7.59
), follows if we show that for fixed
0<\epsilon<1-|\tau|
,

|N\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)-(N-k)\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},x}](dy)|\leq C.

(7.86)

Note that by using (
7.84
) twice, we see that (
7.59
) is equivalent to the claim that

C^{-1}\leq\frac{\exp\left(N\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)\right)}{\exp\left((N-k)\ell\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N-k}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},x}](dy)\right)}\leq C,

(7.87)

or equivalently that

|N\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)-(N-k)\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N-k}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},x}](dy)|\leq C.

(7.88)

However, by using (
7.69
) twice, we see that

|N\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)-(N-k)\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\delta_{N-k}}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},x}](dy)|\leq

(7.89)

2C+|N\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)-(N-k)\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},x}](dy)|.

(7.90)

Thus (
7.59
) follows immediately from this if we show (
7.86
).

To prepare to show (
7.86
), note that by employing the tails estimates of Lemma
7.6
, we see that for
K>0
sufficiently large

\lim_{N\to\infty}|N\int_{K}^{\infty}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)|=0.

(7.91)

Let us denote the principal
(N-k)
-by-
(N-k)
submatrix of
A_{N}
by
\bar{A}_{N}
. We note by the interlacing property for the singular values of a submatrix, we have for any
a\in\mathbb{R}

|N\nu_{A_{N},x}([0,a])-(N-k)\nu_{\bar{A}_{N},x}([0,a])|\leq k.

(7.92)

In particular, by bounding
\log_{\epsilon}(y)
by its maximum, we see that

\left|N\int_{0}^{K}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N},x}](dy)-(N-k)\int_{0}^{K}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{\bar{A}_{N},x}](dy)\right|\leq

(7.93)

2k\left|\log_{\epsilon}(0)+\log_{\epsilon}(K)\right|.

(7.94)

Now we note the distributional equality

\eta_{N,k}\bar{A}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}A_{N-k},\;\;\;\eta_{N,k}=\sqrt{\frac{N}{N-k}}.

(7.95)

In particular, we see that

\int_{0}^{K}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{\bar{A}_{N},x}](dy)=\int_{0}^{\eta_{N,k}K}\log_{\epsilon}(\eta_{N,k}^{-1}y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},\eta_{N,k}x}](dy).

(7.96)

Using (
7.12
) and noting that
\eta_{N,k}=1+O(N^{-1})
one may check that for large
C

N\left|\int_{0}^{\eta_{N,k}K}\log_{\epsilon}(\eta_{N,k}^{-1}y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},\eta_{N,k}x}](dy)-\int_{0}^{K}\log_{\epsilon}(y)\mathbb{E}[\nu_{A_{N-k},x}](dy)\right|\leq C.

(7.97)

Combining these observations completes the proof.
∎

.

For any

In this section we derive the exact form of the Kac-Rice formula used in Lemma

[

]
to a general Riemannian manifold. The method to adapt such a result from the Euclidean case to a general Riemannian manifold is also discussed in (1.3.2) of Chapter 6 in
[
28
]

In preparation, we denote for a smooth manifold
M
, a vector field
f
on
M
, a function

B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}

N_{f,h}(B)=\#\{x\in M:f(x)=0,h(x)\in B\}.

(B.1)

Lemma B.1

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold of dimension
d
,
f
a random vector field on

and

a random function such that

is a smooth Gaussian random field. We assume that for each
x\in M
, the Gaussian vector

is non-degenerate. Then for any choice of a (piecewise) smooth orthonormal frame field
E=(E_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
on
M
, and open
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[N_{f,h}(B)]=\int_{M}\mathbb{E}[|\det(\nabla_{E}f(x))|I(h(x)\in B)|f_{E}(x)=0]\varphi_{f_{E}(x)}(0)\omega_{M}(dx),

(B.2)

where
\omega_{M}
denotes the Riemannian volume form on
M
,
\varphi_{f_{E}(x)}(0)
denotes the density of the random vector
f_{i,E}(x)=(f(x),E_{i}(x))_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
(*,*)_{M}
denotes the Riemannian metric on
M
, and lastly
[\nabla_{E}f(x)]_{ij}=E_{i}f_{j,E}(x)
.

Proof.

We will begin with the case where
M
is identified with an open subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the standard Euclidean metric, and where
E
is the standard Euclidean frame field. We first verify the conditions of Theorem 6.2 of
[
28
]
with
Z=f_{E}
. The only one which we have not taken as an assumption is that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\text{there is }x\in U\text{ with }f(x)=0\text{ and }\det(\nabla_{E}f(x))=0\bigg)=0.

(B.3)

On the other hand employing their Proposition 6.5 (and an exhaustion of
U
by compact subsets) we see that (
B.3
) follows from the non-degeneracy assumption on
f(x)
for all
x\in U
. Thus by Theorem 6.4 of
[
28
]
we see that for any bounded continuous function
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[\sum_{x\in M:\nabla_{E}f(x)=0}g(h(x))]=\int_{U}\mathbb{E}[|\det(\nabla_{E}f(x))|g(h(x))|f_{E}(x)=0]\varphi_{f_{E}(x)}(0)\prod_{i=1}^{d}dx_{i}.

(B.4)

On the other hand, as
B
is open, we may write the indicator function of
B
as the pointwise limit of an monotone increasing sequence of positive continuous functions (take for example
\epsilon^{-1}d(x,U^{c})/(1+\epsilon^{-1}d(x,U^{c}))
where here
d(x,A)=\inf_{y\in A}\|x-y\|
). By applying the monotone convergence theorem to (
B.4
) for these functions we obtain (
B.2
) in our current case.

To proceed, we now relax the assumption on the metric and frame field. We will denote by
\nabla^{euc}
the standard Euclidean frame field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and denote by
f^{euc}
and
\nabla^{euc}f
the quantities with respect to this basis. We observe that for
1\leq i\leq d
we have that
E_{i}(x)=[g(x)^{-1/2}\nabla^{euc}]_{i}
where
[g(x)]_{ij}=(E_{i}(x),E_{j}(x))
is the metric tensor. We also have that
f_{E}(x)=g(x)^{-1/2}f^{euc}(x)
and that

\nabla_{E}f(x)=g(x)^{-1}\nabla^{euc}f(x)+g^{-1/2}(x)(\nabla^{euc}g^{-1/2}(x))f^{euc}(x).

(B.5)

From these relations we derive that
\varphi_{f_{E}(x)}(0)=\det(g(x))^{1/2}\varphi_{f^{euc}(x)}(0)
and

\mathbb{E}[|\det(\nabla_{E}f(x))|I(f(x)\in B)|f_{E}(x)=0]=

(B.6)

\det(g(x))^{-1}\mathbb{E}[|\det(\nabla^{euc}f(x))|I(f(x)\in B)|f^{euc}(x)=0].

(B.7)

As we have that
\omega_{U}(dx)=\det(g(x))^{1/2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}dx_{i}
, we see thus that the integral on the right-hand side of (
B.4
) is independent of both the choice of metric on
M
, and of the choice of orthonormal frame. Thus we have established the case where
M
may be embedded as an open subset of Euclidean space.

Now finally for the case of a general manifold
M
, we take a countable cover of
M
by Euclidean neighborhoods
(U_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
and define
V_{m}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}U_{i}
. We observe that all non-empty finite intersections of the
U_{i}
may be realized as open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, so by the inclusion-exclusion principle, we see that (
B.2
) holds when both sides are restricted to
V_{m}
. Again applying the monotone convergence theorem completes the proof.
∎
