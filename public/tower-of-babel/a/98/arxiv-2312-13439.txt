Active particle in one dimension subjected to resetting with memory
Denis Boyer, Satya N. Majumdar

Abstract
The study of diffusion with preferential returns to places visited in the past has attracted an increased attention in recent years. In these highly non-Markov processes, a standard diffusive particle intermittently resets at a given rate to previously visited positions. At each reset, a position to be revisited is randomly chosen with a probability proportional to the accumulated amount of time spent by the particle at that position. These preferential revisits typically generate a very slow diffusion, logarithmic in time, but still with a Gaussian position distribution at late times. Here we consider an active version of this model, where between resets the particle is self-propelled with constant speed and switches direction in one dimension according to a telegraphic noise. Hence there are two sources of non-Markovianity in the problem. We exactly derive the position distribution in Fourier space, as well as the variance of the position at all times. The crossover from the short-time ballistic regime, dominated by activity, to the large-time anomalous logarithmic growth induced by memory is studied. We also analytically derive a large deviation principle for the position, which exhibits a logarithmic time-scaling instead of the usual algebraic form. Interestingly, at large distances, the large deviations become independent of time and match the non-equilibrium steady state of a particle under resetting to its starting position only.



I
Introduction

Consider a single particle diffusing on a line with diffusion constant

D
. In Ref.
BS_2014
, this diffusive dynamics was studied in the
presence of an additional resetting move that is history dependent. In
this model, in addition to diffusion, the particle also undergoes
resetting with rate
r
to a previously visited position according to
the following stochastic rule. At any given time
t
, the particle
chooses at random (
i.e.
, with probability density
1/t
) a
preceding time
t^{\prime}\in[0,t)
, and resets to the position at which it was
located at
t^{\prime}
. Thus the resetting move tends to dynamically localize
the particle near the positions that are most often visited, as the
probability to choose a particular site for revisit is proportional to
the accumulated occupation time at that site. The position distribution
of the particle was found to approach a Gaussian form at late times
t
,
with the variance growing in a slow, anomalous way as
(2D/r)\,\ln(rt)
asymptotically

. This very slow dynamics emerges
from resetting induced memory effects: the particle becomes sluggish and
“reluctant” to move away from its familiar territory that it has
already visited. Moreover, the position distribution is always
time-dependent and does not approach a stationary state at late times,
in contrast to diffusion with stochastic resetting to a single site

EM_2011
;
EMS_review
. This simple model was able to fit quantitatively several statistical properties of the movements of capuchin monkeys in the wild
BS_2014
, bringing further evidence that many animal species use sophisticated cognitive skills to explore their environment and that memory should be incorporated in biological random walk models
BDF_2008
;
F_2008
;
VM_2009
;
F_2013
;
MFM_2014
;
FC_2021
;

;

;
FBGM_2017

.
Moreover, central and local limit

In this paper, we consider an active version of this model where the resetting dynamics is similar as above,
except that between resets the particle undergoes a self-propelled active dynamics in one spatial dimension with velocities
\pm v_{0}
, instead
of ordinary diffusion. Correlated random walks have been widely used to model the directional persistence which characterizes the movements of many animals and cells (see, e.g.,

The state of the particle at time
t
is now specified by two degrees of freedom: the
position and the velocity.
More precisely, in a small time
dt
, with probability
1-r\,dt
,
the position
x(t)
of the particle gets
incremented by
v_{0}\,\sigma(t)\,dt

\sigma(t)=\pm 1

\langle v_{0}\,\sigma(t)\,v_{0}\,\sigma(t^{\prime})\rangle=v_{0}^{2}\,e^{-2\,\gamma\,|t-t^{\prime}|}\,.

(1)

With the complementary probability
r\,dt
, the particle undergoes resetting. If this happens, the particle chooses a previous time interval
[t^{\prime},t^{\prime}+dt^{\prime}]
with uniform probability
dt^{\prime}/t
, where
0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t
. It then changes the
current position
x
to the position
x^{\prime}
it occupied at time
t^{\prime}
, and also takes the velocity it had at
t^{\prime}
. Thus the transition
from the state
(x,\sigma,t)
to
(x^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},t^{\prime})
occurs with probability

1

Figure 1:
An active particle (dark blue arrow) moves at constant speed

r

t^{\prime}\in[0,t)

In this model, there are clearly two sources of non-Markovianity. The first coming from the driving noise which has
a memory encoded in the autocorrelation function above (which for finite
\gamma
is not delta correlated
as in the white noise case). Whereas the complete state of the particle
(x(t),\sigma(t))
has a Markovian evolution when the motion is driven by the telegraphic noise, the marginal process
x(t)
is non-Markovian. The second source of non-Markovianity comes from the resetting moves which depends
on the past history of the trajectory. In this case, even the process
(x(t),\sigma(t))
is non-Markovian.
Thus the model is characterized by two time scales:

(i)

t_{1}^{*}\sim 1/\gamma
that denotes the typical time between two consecutive switches of the driving noise
and
(ii)

t_{2}^{*}\sim 1/r
denoting the typical time between two consecutive resetting events. The interplay
between these two time-scales leads to a position distribution
p_{r}(x,t)
that is rather rich
and nontrivial, as we will illustrate below. We note that a model with the same active dynamics on a
line, but subjected to a resetting only to the fixed initial position (with velocity randomized after each reset),
was solved exactly in Ref.
EM_2018
.
The resetting protocol in our model here is thus quite different from that of Ref.
EM_2018
. Here,
under resetting, both the position and velocity of the particle get reset to the values they had at a previous time, chosen uniformly at random. Consequently, the positions that have been often visited are more likely to be chosen as a resetting point in the future. Actually, under these rules, the probability that the particle chooses a given visited location
x^{\prime}
is proportional to the total time it spent at that location.

(2)

By taking derivatives with respect to

. For example,

in the problem. Indeed, the

\displaystyle V_{0}(t)\simeq\begin{cases}v_{0}^{2}\,t^{2}\quad\,\quad{\rm for}\quad t\ll 1/\gamma\\
\\

Interestingly, in this

;

(5)

where

In the limit

III
The exact position distribution

We consider an active self-propelled particle on the line whose position, between resetting events, evolves
via
dx/dt=v_{0}\sigma(t)
, where
\sigma(t)=\pm 1
is the telegraphic noise that
switches between the two states with rate
\gamma
. The state of the particle
at time
t
is specified by two degrees of freedom
(x,\sigma)
, namely the
position and the velocity (in units of
v_{0}
).
Let us recall the resetting dynamics. At time
t
, with probability
rdt^{\prime}/t
the particle chooses any previous
time
t^{\prime}\leq t
and resets,
i.e.
the position and velocity get reset to the values taken at
t^{\prime}
.
Let
p_{r}(x,\sigma,t)
denotes the probability density
that the particle is at position
x
with velocity
\sigma
at time
t
.
To take into account the memory effect, we also need to define the
two-point function
p_{r}(x,\sigma,t\,;\,x^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, which denotes the joint probability density for the particle
to be at
(x^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
at time
t^{\prime}
and
and at
(x,\sigma)
at
t
, with
t^{\prime}\leq t
. Clearly, if we integrate the two-point functions
over
(x^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
(or alternately
over
(x,\sigma)
), we recover the
marginal one-point probability density

\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}=\pm 1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{r}(x,\sigma,t\,;\,x^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}=p_{r}(x,\sigma,t)\,,\quad{\rm and}\,\,{\rm similarly}\quad\sum_{\sigma=\pm 1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{r}(x,\sigma,t\,;\,x^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,dx=p_{r}(x^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,.

(18)

With these ingredients at hand, we can now write down a Fokker-Planck equation for the evolution of the one-point
position distributions
p_{r}(x,\sigma=1,t)\equiv p_{r}^{+}(x,t)
and
p_{r}(x,\sigma=-1,t)\equiv p_{r}^{-}(x,t)
as follows.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{r}^{+}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-v_{0}\,\partial_{x}p_{r}^{+}(x,t)-\gamma\,p_{r}^{+}(x,t)+\gamma\,p_{r}^{-}(x,t)-r\,p_{r}^{+}(x,t)+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\sum_{\sigma=\pm 1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,p_{r}(x^{\prime},\sigma,t;\,x,+1,t^{\prime})\,

(19)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{r}^{-}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{0}\,\partial_{x}p_{r}^{-}(x,t)-\gamma\,p_{r}^{-}(x,t)+\gamma\,p_{r}^{+}(x,t)-r\,p_{r}^{-}(x,t)+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\sum_{\sigma=\pm 1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,p_{r}(x^{\prime},\sigma,t;\,x,-1,t^{\prime})\,.

(20)

The first three terms on the right hand side (r.h.s.) of this pair of equations describe
the standard evolution under the self-propelled dynamics. The third term
in the first line (respectively the second line) describes the loss of probability density from position

(x,+1)
(respectively
(x,-1)
) at time
t
due to resetting to other coordinates.
The last term on the first line (respectively the second line) describes the gain in the probability density
at
(x,+1)
(respectively at
(x,-1)
) due to resetting
from other positions occupied at time
t
just before resetting and labeled by
(x^{\prime},\sigma)
. If the particle has to reset to
(x,+1)
(respectively

(x,-1)
) from
(x^{\prime},\sigma)

at time
t
, it must have been
at
(x,+1)
(respectively
(x,-1)
) at a previous time
t^{\prime}\leq t
and the probability density of this event
is simply the
two-point function
p_{r}(x^{\prime},\sigma,t;x,+1,t^{\prime})
. Finally, we need to integrate over all
(x^{\prime},\sigma)
from
which the particle may arrive at
(x,+1)
(respectively
(x,-1)
) by resetting. The reason for the solvability
for the one-point
position distribution in this model can then traced back to the second relation in Eq. (
18
), which
allows us to write a closed pair of equations for the one-point function only, without involving higher-point functions,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{r}^{+}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-v_{0}\,\partial_{x}p_{r}^{+}(x,t)-\gamma\,p_{r}^{+}(x,t)+\gamma\,p_{r}^{-}(x,t)-r\,p_{r}^{+}(x,t)+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}p_{r}^{+}(x,t^{\prime})

(21)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{r}^{-}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{0}\,\partial_{x}p_{r}^{-}(x,t)-\gamma\,p_{r}^{-}(x,t)+\gamma\,p_{r}^{+}(x,t)-r\,p_{r}^{-}(x,t)+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}p_{r}^{-}(x,t^{\prime})\,.

(22)

These two equations start from the initial conditions

p_{r}^{+}(x,0)=p_{r}^{-}(x,0)=\frac{1}{2}\delta(x)\,,

(23)

which corresponds to a particle starting at the origin with equally likely velocities

\pm v_{0}
. The boundary conditions are
p_{r}^{\pm}(x\to\pm\infty,t)=0
.
The full position distribution at time
t
is then obtained by summing the two solutions

p_{r}(x,t)=p_{r}^{+}(x,t)+p_{r}^{-}(x,t)\,.

(24)

By integrating Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
) over
x
and adding, one can easily check
that the total probability
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{r}(x,t)\,dx=1
is conserved at all times.
Evidently, for
r=0
, these equations reduce to the standard pair of Fokker-Planck equations
studied extensively in the literature
Weiss_2002
;

IV
Exact computation of the variance

From the exact Fourier transform of the position distribution in Eq. (
57
), one can,
in principle, compute all the moments by making a small
k
expansion. By symmetry,
the mean position is identically zero,
\langle x\rangle(t)=0
at all times. Thus the variance is
given by

V_{r}(t)=\langle x^{2}\rangle(t)-[{\langle x\rangle}(t)]^{2}=-\frac{d^{2}q_{r}(k,t)}{dk^{2}}\Big|_{k=0}\,.

(62)

To perform the small
k
expansion of
q_{r}(k,t)
, it turns out to be convenient to
work with time integrated Fourier transform in Eq. (
60
).
For small
k
, keeping terms up to
O(k^{2})
, one gets

1-\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-v_{0}^{2}\,k^{2}}}\simeq-\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\quad{\rm and}\quad 1+\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-v_{0}^{2}\,k^{2}}}\simeq 2+\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\,.

(63)

Expanding the eigenvalues in Eq. (
36
) up to
O(k^{2})
we get

\lambda_{1}(k)\simeq 2\gamma-\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma}\quad{\rm and}\quad\lambda_{2}(k)\simeq\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma}\,.

(64)

Using these expansions in Eq. (
60
), we get after some steps (see Appendix
B
for details),

Q_{r}(k,t)=t\,\left[1-\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{4\gamma^{2}}\,\left(-1+M\left(\frac{2\gamma}{r+2\gamma},2,-(r+2\gamma)\,t\right)-\gamma\,t\,{}_{2}F_{2}\left[\{1,1\},\{2,3\},-rt\right]\right)\right]+O(k^{4})\,.

(65)

Furthermore, it turns out that the function
{}_{2}F_{2}\left[\{1,1\},\{2,3\},-rt\right]
can be
expressed in terms of elementary functions as

{}_{2}F_{2}\left[\{1,1\},\{2,3\},z\right]=-\frac{2}{z^{2}}\,\left[e^{z}-1-z+\gamma_{E}\,z+z\,\Gamma(0,-z)+z\ln(-z)\right]\,,

(66)

where
\Gamma(0,z)=\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-x}dx/x
for
z>0
and
\gamma_{E}
is the Euler’s constant.

Deriving Eq. (
65
) twice with respect to
k
and setting
k=0
gives the time integrated variance

\int_{0}^{t}V_{r}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}=\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\,\left[-t+t\,M\left(\frac{2\gamma}{r+2\gamma},2,-(r+2\gamma)\,t\right)+\frac{2\gamma}{r^{2}}\,g(rt)\right]\,,

(67)

where

g(y)=1-e^{-y}+y\ln(y)+y\,\int_{y}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\,dx-(1-\gamma_{E})\,y\,.

(68)

Finally, taking a derivative with respect to
t
gives our final exact expression for the variance,
valid for all
t
and given by Eq. (
13
) in the Introduction. In the limit
r\to 0
, it is easy to show,
using
M(1,2,z)=(e^{z}-1)/z
, that Eq. (
13
) reduces to the known result in Eq. (
3
).
One can also derive the asymptotic behaviors of
V_{r}(t)
for small
and large
t
, as shown in Eq. (

).

While we can not invert the Fourier transform

For large

), one finds that for

), one gets

p_{r}(x,t)\sim\exp\left[-\frac{\gamma\,r\,x^{2}}{2\,v_{0}^{2}\,\ln(rt)}\right]\,\quad{\rm for}\quad|x|\ll\ln(rt)\,,

(83)

which shows that the typical fluctuations of
O\left(\sqrt{\ln(rt)}\right)
around the mean are Gaussian
distributed. Moreover, the variance
v_{0}^{2}\ln(rt)/(\gamma\,r)
matches perfectly with
the leading asymptotic growth of the variance in Eq. (
14
).
Furthermore, using
D_{\rm eff}=v_{0}^{2}/(2\gamma)
, one recovers, at late times,
the Gaussian behavior with variance
(2D_{\rm eff}/r)\,\ln(rt)
as
in ordinary diffusion with the same memory-dependent resetting
BS_2014
;
BEM_2017
.
This is expected since at very late times the active dynamics is known to effectively behave
as a standard diffusion with an effective diffusion constant
D_{\rm eff}=v_{0}^{2}/(2\gamma)
.

In the opposite limt
|z|\to\infty
, we get by substituing the linear behavior (
82
)
into the large deviation form (
74
)

p_{r}(x,t)\sim\exp\left[-\frac{\sqrt{r(r+2\gamma)}}{v_{0}}\,|x|\right]\quad{\rm for}\quad|x|\gg\ln(rt)\,.

(84)

This result may, at first glance, look surprising because it says that very far away from the center,
i.e.
,
for
|x|\gg\ln(rt)
, the distribution actually becomes time-independent at late times. However,
this is what one would expect in hindsight. At late times, the particle is essentially concentrated
in a core region around the origin of width
\sqrt{\ln(rt)}
. Seen from very far away from the core,
the dynamics essentially reduces to an active particle that is reset effectively to the origin.
This later problem was studied in Ref.
EM_2018
and the authors demonstrated that the
position distribution approaches a stationary form at late times
which happens to exactly coincide with Eq. (
84
). Thus it is quite natural
that
p_{r}(x,t)
in our problem, for
|x|\gg\ln(rt)
, becomes stationary as shown by Eq. (
84
). In the Appendix
A
, we show that the results of this Section are consistent with the recent findings of Ref.
BM_2023
.

VI
Summary and Conclusion

We have studied an active diffusion model with memory in one spatial
dimension, in which a self-propelled particle moves at constant speed

v_{0}
and switches direction stochastically at rate
\gamma
. Memory
effects are implemented through a protocol where the particle interrupts
its active motion and resets at rate
r
to positions and velocities
that were occupied at
previous times. Namely, during a time interval
[t,t+dt]
, with
probability
rdt
the particle chooses a time
t^{\prime}
in the past,
uniformly distributed in
[0,t]
, and takes the position and velocity it
had at time
t^{\prime}
, before continuing its active motion from there. This
model extends previous studies that considered random walks or Brownian
particles with similar resetting protocols

BS_2014
;
BR_2014
;
BP_2016
;
BEM_2017
. The case studied here also
differs significantly from the same active process in which resetting
occurs to a unique position (
e.g.
, the starting position)

EM_2018
, due to the fact that it lacks a steady state. The memory
rule makes the particle return more often to positions frequently
occupied (typically near its starting position), generating a diffusive
process in which the variance of the position grows very slowly with
time, but without being arrested asymptotically.

We have calculated exactly the distribution of the position of the
particle in Fourier space, extending the results of Ref.
BEM_2017

for Brownian motion. While we were not able to invert this Fourier
transform, we used
it to calculate exactly the full time dependence of the variance of the
position. The interplay between activity and memory leads to a variance
growing as
t^{2}
at short times and as
\ln(rt)
as large times, this
latter regime being a fingerprint of preferential visit models. The
knowledge of the position distribution, whose central part becomes
Gaussian at large times, allowed us to derive a large deviation
principle and to study the rate function explicitly for the first time
for this type of processes. At distances much larger than a typical
scale
\ln(rt)
, the distribution of the position becomes exponential,
independent of time and coincides with the non-equilibrium steady state
produced by resetting an active particle to its starting position only.

This problem could be generalized in several directions in the future, for instance, to other protocols such as those involving periodic resetting. The results recently derived in Ref.
BM_2023
for large deviations could be applied to this case and many others. Systems of many independent particles become correlated over time when subject to simultaneous resetting
BHMS_2023
;
BKMS_2023
. How correlations are built though resetting in preferential revisit models is also a question that deserves further study.

Appendix A
A quenched large deviation principle for Brownian and active particles with memory

We show that our results on large deviations, in particular the
expression (
75
) of the rate function, can be derived
by an alternative method
using a recent theorem of Boci and Mailler valid for a more general class of
processes with preferential revisits
BM_2023
. We first recall
briefly their main results. Let
z(t)
be a Markov process and

\bm{L}=\{L_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
an infinite set of
independent and identically distributed
(i.i.d.) positive random
variables each distributed via
\phi(L)
. The
L_{i}
’s represent the time
intervals between successive resetting events.
During each such interval the position of the walker
x(t)
evolves freely
according to the process
z(t)
. When the
n
-th
resetting occurs (at time
t_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}
), the walker randomly
chooses a time in the past
t^{\prime}\in[0,t_{n}]
with a normalized probability density

\mu(t^{\prime})/\int_{0}^{t_{n}}dt\,\mu(t)
where
\mu
is a given function, and relocates to the position it occupied at that time,
i.e.
,
x(t_{n})=x(t^{\prime})
. From there,
x
continues to evolve as
z
until
the next resetting at time
t_{n+1}
. While we took a uniform kernel
(
\mu(t^{\prime})=1
), Boci and Mailler considered a more general form

\mu(t^{\prime})=\frac{\alpha}{t^{\prime}}(\ln t^{\prime})^{\alpha-1}e^{\beta(\ln t^{\prime})^{\alpha}},

(85)

which reduces to the uniform case when
\alpha=\beta=1
.

The next goal is to find a large deviation principle for
the position
x(t)
, given that a large deviation principle
exists for the underlying free Markov process
z(t)
between
two resettings. The prescriptions of Boci and Mailler for
computing this large deviation principle proceeds via
three steps (here we simplify the notations of Ref.
BM_2023

to adapt to the language of physicists).

•

One first assumes that the free Markov process
z(t)
admits a large
deviation principle,
i.e.
, there exists a function

\Lambda(\zeta)
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\langle e^{\zeta z(t)}\rangle=\Lambda(\zeta),

(86)

for all
\zeta
and with
z(t=0)
fixed. This simply means that to
leading order for large
t
,

\langle e^{\zeta z(t)}\rangle\sim\exp\left[t\,\Lambda(\zeta)\right]\,.

(87)

•

The next step is to compute the following generating function
g(\xi)

for all
\xi

g(\xi)\equiv\frac{\langle e^{\xi L}-1-\xi L\rangle}{\xi\,\langle L\rangle}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{\xi L}-1-\xi\,L\right)\,\phi(L)\,dL}{\xi\,\int_{0}^{\infty}L\,\phi(L)\,dL}\,,

(88)

where we recall that
\phi(L)
is the distribution of the interval
L
between
two successive resets.

•

Knowing the two functions

\Lambda(\zeta)
and
g(\xi)
from above, the final step
consists in computing the following Legendre transform

\Psi(w)=\sup_{y}\{w\,y-g\left(\Lambda(y)\right)\}\,.

(89)

Once
\Psi(w)
is computed, the Boci-Mailler theorem states
that under rather mild conditions the position
x(t)
satisfies
a
quenched
large deviation principle,
i.e.
,
conditioned on the
L_{i}
’s. For all
w>0
, this principle can be written as

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\ln\left[{\rm Prob}(|x(t)|\geq ws(t)|\bm{L})\right]}{s(t)}=-\inf_{y\geq w}\Psi(y)=-\Psi(w),

(90)

where the last equality assumes that
\Psi(y)
is a monotonously
increasing function of
y
. The scaling factor
s(t)
is given by

s(t)=(\ln t)^{\alpha}\quad{\rm if}\ \beta\neq 0\,.

(91)

In the language of physicists, the statement in Eq. (
90
) simply
means that to leading order in large
t
, the cumulative position
distribution behaves as

{\rm Prob}\left(|x(t)|\geq X\right)\sim\exp\left[-(\ln t)^{\alpha}\,\Psi\left(\frac{X}{(\ln t)^{\alpha}}\right)\right]\,,

(92)

where the rate function
\Psi(w)
is computed from Eq. (
89
). For
the uniform memory kernel where
\alpha=1
and
\beta=1
, Eq. (
92
)
takes precisely our large deviation form in Eq. (
74
).

The results in Eqs. (
92
) and (
89
)
were derived in Ref.
BM_2023
by a very different probabilistic
method, namely, via a
mapping of the memory induced resetting random walk to a weighted random recursive tree.
This is a quenched principle in the sense that the averages are not
taken over
\bm{L}
. The authors of Ref
BM_2023

nevertheless believe that the
same principle should hold in the more difficult annealed case, provided
that
\phi(L)
decays to 0 fast enough at large
L
.
Below, we apply this general prescription
to compute the large deviation principle
in two simple cases. In both cases, we assume

\phi(L)=r\,e^{-r\,L}\quad{\rm for}\quad L\geq 0\,,

(93)

where
r
represents the resetting rate.

When the underlying free process
z(t)
is a
standard Brownian motion in one dimension.
We first compute
\Lambda(\zeta)
in
Eq. (
86
). This is easy since

\langle e^{\zeta\,z(t)}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{4\,\pi\,D\,t}}\,e^{\zeta\,z-z^{2}/{4Dt}}=e^{D\,t\,\zeta^{2}}\,,

(94)

implying from Eq. (
86
) that

\Lambda(\zeta)=D\,\zeta^{2}\,.

(95)

Next, substituting
\phi(L)
from Eq. (
93
) in (
88
), it
is easy to see that

\displaystyle g(\xi)=\begin{cases}\frac{\xi}{r-\xi}\quad\quad\quad\,\,{\rm if}\quad\xi<r\\
\\
\infty\quad\quad\quad\quad{\rm if}\quad\xi\geq r.\end{cases}

(96)

We use Eq. (
89
) with the functions
\Lambda(\zeta)
and
g(\xi)
explicitly given by Eqs. (
95
) and (
96
), respectively, and obtain for the Brownian
motion with memory-induced resetting

\Psi_{\rm BM}(w)=\sup_{0<y<\sqrt{\frac{r}{D}}}\left\{w\,y-\frac{y^{2}}{\frac{r}{D}-y^{2}}\right\}\quad{\rm for}\quad w\geq 0\,.

(97)

The rate function
\Psi_{\rm BM}(w)
is symmetric in
w
, hence
it suffices just to consider
w\geq 0
.
We note that the function inside the supremum in (
97
)
diverges at
y=\sqrt{r/D}
, thus making the allowed
interval for
y
bounded.
This is similar to the situation we encountered
in Eq. (
75
) before. It is a consequence of
Eq. (
96
) and of the fact that
\phi(L)
does not
decay faster than an exponential at large
L
.

The above result for the Brownian case can also be derived from our
active particle
calculation by setting
r\ll\gamma
,
i.e.
,
by expanding Eq. (
75
) at first order in
R
.
Then, recalling that
D_{eff}=v_{0}^{2}/(2\gamma)
and

z=\sqrt{r/(2D_{eff})}(x/\ln t)
in the notation of
Section
V
, we make the change of variables

x/\ln t\to w
and
\sqrt{r/(2D_{eff})}q\to y
in
Eq. (
75
), and actually
recover Eq. (
97
) with
D=D_{eff}
.

Although the maximization of Eq. (
97
) cannot be carried out
explicitly, one can study the behavior of
\Psi_{\rm BM}(w)

at small and large
w
, in the same way as in Section
V
.
It is easy to show that
\Psi_{\rm BM}(w)
has the following
asymptotic behaviors

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm BM}(w)\simeq\begin{cases}\frac{r}{4D}\,w^{2}\quad\quad{\rm as}\quad|w|\to 0\\
\\
\sqrt{\frac{r}{D}}\,|w|\quad\,\,{\rm as}\quad|w|\to\infty\end{cases}

(98)

Consequently, Eq. (
92
) gives

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(|x(t)|>X)\propto\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{X^{2}}{\frac{4D}{r}(\ln t)^{\alpha}}\right)\quad\,\,\,\,{\rm as}\quad X\ll(\ln t)^{\alpha}\\
\\
\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{r}{D}}\,X\right)\,\quad\quad\,\,{\rm as}\quad X\gg(\ln t)^{\alpha}.\end{cases}

(99)

This result agrees with Eq. (
83
)
with
D
in (
99
)
replaced by
D_{\rm eff}=v_{0}^{2}/(2\gamma)

and
\alpha=1
, while the large deviations are again independent of
time at large
X
and coincide with the non-equilibrium steady state of
a Brownian particle under stochastic resetting to the origin only
EM_2011
.

When the underlying process
z(t)
corresponds
to a free run-and-tumble particle in one dimension.

Here
z(t)
is the position of the
active particle driven by the telegraphic noise.
Although this process is non-Markov, we may still try to
apply the general
prescriptions in Eqs. (
87
)-(
90
)
to this case
note
. To compute
\Lambda(\zeta)
for the active particle,
we start from the general relation

\langle e^{\zeta\,z(t)}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\ e^{\zeta\,z}\,P(z,t)\sim e^{\Lambda(\zeta)\,t},

(100)

where
P(z,t)
is the position distribution of the active particle at
time
t
. As the exact expression for
P(z,t)
is not so simple (see Eq. (
5
)), we rather take the Laplace transform of Eq. (
100
) with
respect to
t
and get

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\ e^{\zeta\,z}\,\widetilde{P}(z,s)\sim\frac{1}{s-\Lambda(\zeta)},\quad{\rm with}\ s>\Lambda(\zeta),

(101)

where
\widetilde{P}(z,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-st}\,P(z,t)
.
Therefore
\Lambda(\zeta)
is the largest real pole of the integral
in the left hand side of Eq. (
101
). This integral can be
calculated from the known expression for
\widetilde{P}(z,s)

(see,
e.g.
,
EM_2018
), given explicitly by

\widetilde{P}(z,s)=\frac{\lambda(s)}{2s}\ e^{-\lambda(s)|z|},\quad{\rm with}\quad\lambda(s)=\sqrt{s(s+2\gamma)}/v_{0}.

(102)

One then obtains,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\ e^{\zeta\,z}\widetilde{P}(z,s)=\frac{\lambda(s)}{2s}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda(s)-\zeta}+\frac{1}{\lambda(s)+\zeta}\right)

(103)

Assuming
\zeta>0
without loosing generality (since the active particle process is symmetric), a pole is contained in the first term of the r.h.s. of Eq. (
103
),

\frac{1}{\lambda(s)-\zeta}\simeq\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}(s^{*})(s-s^{*})},

(104)

where
s^{*}
is the largest root of the equation
\lambda(s)-\zeta=0
.
Identifying
s^{*}
with
\Lambda(\zeta)
, one deduces from Eq. (
102
)

\Lambda(\zeta)=-\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+v_{0}^{2}\zeta^{2}}.

(105)

Inserting this expression in Eq. (
89
) with
g(\xi)
given by
Eq. (
96
), the rate function becomes

\Psi_{\rm active}(w)=\sup_{0<y<y_{max}}\left\{w\,y-\frac{\sqrt{1+\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}y^{2}}-1}{1+R-\sqrt{1+\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}y^{2}}}\right\}\quad{\rm with}\quad R=\frac{r}{\gamma}.

(106)

By making the same changes of variable as in the BM case above,
we see that Eq. (
106
) is equivalent to
Eq. (
75
).

Appendix B
Small
k
expansion of
Q_{r}(k,t)
up to order 2

Substituting the small
k
expansions (
63
) and (
64
)
in the expression (
60
) of
Q_{r}(k,t)
, we get

Q_{r}(k,t)\simeq\frac{t}{2}\left[-\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\,\,M\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}(k)}{r+\lambda_{1}(k)},2,-(r+\lambda_{1}(k))\,t\right)+\left(2+\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right)\,M\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(k)}{r+\lambda_{2}(k)},2,-(r+\lambda_{2}(k))\,t\right)\right]\,.

(107)

Since we are interested in the expansion only up to
O(k^{2})
for small
k
,
we can now put
k=0
in the arguments of the function
M
in the first term
on the r.h.s. of Eq. (

),

,

M\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}(k)}{r+\lambda_{1}(k)},2,-(r+\lambda_{1}(k))\,t\right)\simeq M\left(\frac{2\gamma}{r+2\gamma},2,-(r+2\gamma)\,t\right)\,.

(108)

For the second term on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
107
), we need to expand
the arguments of
M
for small
k
. Using Eq. (
64
) we get

\frac{\lambda_{2}(k)}{r+\lambda_{2}(k)}\simeq\frac{v_{0}^{2}\,k^{2}}{2\gamma r}\quad{\rm and}\quad r+\lambda_{2}(k)\simeq r+\frac{v_{0}^{2}\,k^{2}}{2\gamma}\,.

(109)

Thus the
M
function in the second term on the r.h.s. in Eq. (
107
) reduces to

M\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(k)}{r+\lambda_{2}(k)},2,-(r+\lambda_{2}(k))\,t\right)\simeq M\left(\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma r},2,-\left(r+\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma}\right)\,t\right)\,.

(110)

We now need to expand this function for small
k
up to

. This is a bit tricky.
We first expand the third argument of
M
in Eq. (
110
) and get

M\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(k)}{r+\lambda_{2}(k)},2,-(r+\lambda_{2}(k))\,t\right)\simeq M\left(\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma r},2,-rt\right)-\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma}\,t\,\partial_{z}M\left(\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma r},2,z\right)\Big|_{z=-rt}\,.

(111)

Using the identity,
\partial_{z}M(a,b,z)=(a/b)M(a+1,b+1,z)

for small

M\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(k)}{r+\lambda_{2}(k)},2,-(r+\lambda_{2}(k))\,t\right)\simeq M\left(\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{2\gamma r},2,-rt\right)\,.

M\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(k)}{r+\lambda_{2}(k)},2,-(r+\lambda_{2}(k))\,t\right)\simeq 1-\frac{v_{0}^{2}k^{2}}{4\gamma}\,t\,{}_{2}F_{2}\left[\{1,1\},\{2,3\},-rt\right]\,.

Collecting all these expansions together into Eq. (

given by Eq. (
