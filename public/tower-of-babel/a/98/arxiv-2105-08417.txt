Approximate solutions of convex semi-infinite optimization problems in finitely many iterations
Jochen Schmid and Miltiadis Poursanidis Fraunhofer Institute for Industrial Mathematics (ITWM), 67663 Kaiserslautern, Germany jochen.schmid@itwm.fraunhofer.de

Abstract
We develop two adaptive discretization algorithms for convex semi-infinite optimization, which terminate after finitely many iterations at approximate solutions of arbitrary precision. In particular, they terminate at a feasible point of the considered optimization problem. Compared to the existing finitely feasible algorithms for general semi-infinite optimization problems, our algorithms work with considerably smaller discretizations and are thus computationally favorable. Also, our algorithms terminate at approximate solutions of arbitrary precision, while for general semi-infinite optimization problems the best possible approximate-solution precision can be arbitrarily bad. All occurring finite optimization subproblems in our algorithms have to be solved only approximately, and continuity is the only regularity assumption on our objective and constraint functions. Applications to parametric and non-parametric regression problems under shape constraints are discussed.



Index terms: convex semi-infinite optimization, finitely feasible adaptive discretization algorithm, regression under shape constraints

Introduction

In this paper, we are concerned with convex semi-infinite optimization
[

,

,

,

,

that is, optimzation problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}f(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g_{i}(x,y)\leq 0\quad\text{for all }y\in Y\text{ and }i\in I

(1.1)

with a (strictly) convex objective function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
on some compact convex subset

of a normed vector space and with infinitely many convex constraint functions
g_{i}(\cdot,y):X\to\mathbb{R}
indexed by indices

and

from a finite set

and, respectively, an infinite compact metric space

A simple but important application of semi-infinite optimization is shape-constrained regression
[
7
,
3
,
12
]
, that is, the problem to find a model for an unknown functional relationship of interest such that, on the one hand, the model optimally fits the available experimental data and such that, on the other hand, the model strictly respects the available theoretical prior knowledge about the shape of the functional relationship to be modeled. Common examples of such shape knowledge are given by monotonicity or convexity knowledge which, expressed in terms of partial derivatives, takes the form of semi-infinite constraints as in (

).

What we contribute in this paper is two adaptive discretization algorithms for convex semi-infinite optimization problems of the form (
1.1
), which compute an approximate solution of (
1.1
) of arbitrary precision in finitely many iterations and then terminate. In other words, for every given approximation error tolerance
\delta>0

\delta

1.1
) after finitely many iterations (the total number of iterations depending on
\delta
, of course). As usual, a

-approximate solution of (
1.1
) is a point

which is feasible for (
1.1
) and for which

exceeds the optimal value of (
1.1
) at most by

, for short:

\displaystyle x^{*}\in F_{0}(Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x^{*})\leq\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x)+\delta,

(1.2)

where
F_{0}(Y)
stands for the feasibility set of (
1.1
). In virtue of the feasibility of approximate solutions, our algorithms in particular – and in sharp contrast to the algorithm from
[
21
]
– belong to the class of finitely feasible algorithms. In recent years, a lot of such finitely feasible algorithms have been developed for general non-convex semi-infinite optimization problems
[
14
,
20
,
4
,
10
]
– including generalized, mixed-integer, and coupling-equality- and existence-constrained semi-infinite optimization problems
[
14
,
15
,
5
,
6
]
.

Compared to these algorithms, the two algorithms developed here require considerably smaller discretizations and are thus computationally favorable. Indeed, in updating their discretizations from iteration to iteration, our algorithms do not only add new points but also drop old points from the previous discretization. Also, at least our first algorithm is conceptually simpler than the algorithms from
[
14
,
4
]
. And finally, our algorithms for convex semi-infinite optimization problems terminate at approximate solutions of arbitrary precision. In contrast, for general semi-infinite problems as treated in
[
14
,
20
,
4

, the best possible approximate-solution precision can be arbitrarily bad. In fact, this can happen already for just quasi-convex constraint functions.

We now describe our two algorithms in a bit more detail. As in
[
14
,
15
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
the restrictions

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}f(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g_{i}(x,y)\leq-\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }y\in Y\text{ and }i\in I

of (

play an important role here. At the heart of our algorithms is an adaptive discretization algorithm in the spirit of

, which for a given restriction parameter

1.3
) up to small approximation errors. We will refer to this algorithm as the core algorithm in the following. As we will see, this core algorithm for given

which, on the one hand, is feasible for (
1.1
) and for which, on the other hand,
f(x_{-\varepsilon}^{*})
exceeds the optimal value of the restriction (
1.3
) only slightly. In short,

\displaystyle x_{-\varepsilon}^{*}\in F_{0}(Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x_{-\varepsilon}^{*})\leq\min_{x\in F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)}f(x)+\delta^{*}

(1.4)

for some small
\delta^{*}>0
, where
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)
denotes the feasibility set of the restricted optimization problem (
1.3
). We will also see that the optimal value of the restricted problem (
1.3

1.1
), that is,

\displaystyle\min_{x\in F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)}f(x)\searrow\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x)\qquad(\varepsilon\searrow 0).

(1.5)

In view of (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), it is not surprising that by applying the core algorithm in a suitable way with suitably decreased restriction parameters
\varepsilon
, one can construct algorithms that finitely terminate at approximate solutions of the original problem (
1.1
) of arbitrary precision.

In our first algorithm – termed the sequential algorithm – we sequentially apply the core algorithm again and again, each time with a smaller restriction parameter
\varepsilon>0
. In our second algorithm – termed the simultaneous algorithm – we follow
[
14
,
4
]
, namely, we simultaneously apply the core algorithm for
\varepsilon>0
and the core algorithm for
\varepsilon=0
. Compared to the sequential algorithm, the decreasing mechanism for

Similarly, the termination index in the simultaneous algorithm depends on such a posteriori quantities, while in the sequential algorithm the termination index depends only on input quantities and thus is known in advance.

All finite optimization subproblems occurring in our two algorithms, that is, all discretized versions of (
1.3
) and all feasibility test problems

\displaystyle\max_{y\in Y}g_{i}(x^{*},y)\qquad(i\in I)

(1.6)

for given points

, have to be solved only approximately – with decreasing approximation error tolerances. Concerning the regularity of the objective and constraint functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
and

, no differentiability has to be assumed – mere continuity is sufficient. And finally, the domains
X
and
Y
do not have to be embedded in finite-dimensional vector spaces – it suffices when they are a compact convex subset of any normed vector space and, respectively, a compact metric space. In particular, our algorithms can be applied in the same way both to parametric and to non-parametric shape-constrained regression problems. In the particularly important case of polynomial regression under shape constraints, the feasibility test problems (
1.6

[
8
,
9
]
can be used to approximately solve these problems.

In the entire paper, we will adopt the following notation and terminology. We will often write
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon}(Y^{*})
for the relaxation

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}f(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g_{i}(x,y)\leq-\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }y\in Y^{*}\text{ and }i\in I

of (

) with

for the auxiliary, feasibility test problems (

) for the point

. As above,

\displaystyle F_{-\varepsilon}(Y^{*}):=\{x\in X:g_{i}(x,y)\leq-\varepsilon\text{ for all }y\in Y\text{ and }i\in I\}

(1.8)

denotes the feasibility set of (
1.7
). A
\delta
-approximate solution of (
1.7
) is defined analogously to (
1.2
) and a
\delta
-approximate solution of the maximization problem (
1.6
) is defined as a
\delta
-approximate solution of the corresponding minimization problem with objective function
-g_{i}(x^{*},\cdot)
, of course. Also,

denotes the modulus of continuity of the function

, that is,

\displaystyle\omega_{f}(r):=\sup\{|f(a)-f(b)|:a,b\in X\text{ with }\left\|a-b\right\|\leq r\}\qquad(r\in[0,\infty))

We begin with an elementary solvability result, which will continually – and mostly tacitly – be used later on.

Condition 2.1

f\in C(X,\mathbb{R})

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that Condition

is satisfied. As soon as the optimization problem (

) with some arbitrary subset

of

and some arbitrary

is feasible, it has a solution. If even Condition

Since the feasibility set

) is a non-empty compact set by assumption, the optimization problem (

) has a solution by the assumed continuity of

. In case even Condition

We continue with a fundamental convergence result. It states that in the case of strict feasibility and of convex constraint functions, the optimal values of the restricted optimization problems (
1.3
) converge to the optimal value of the original optimization problem (

) at a rate determined by the modulus of continuity of

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
f\in C(X,\mathbb{R})
and
g_{i}\in C(X\times Y,\mathbb{R})
for every
i\in I
, where
X\neq\emptyset
is a convex compact subset of a normed vector space,

is a compact metric space and

is a finite index set. Suppose further that (
1.1
) is strictly feasible, that is,

\displaystyle F_{0-}(Y):=\{x\in X:g_{i}(x,y)<0\text{ for all }y\in Y\text{ and }i\in I\}\neq\emptyset

and that

. Then

\displaystyle\min_{x\in F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)}f(x)\leq\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x)+\omega_{f}(\operatorname{diam}X\cdot\varepsilon/\varepsilon^{*})\qquad(\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon^{*}])

holds true, where

is the modulus of continuity of

as defined in (

). In particular,

\displaystyle\min_{x\in F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)}f(x)\searrow\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x)\qquad(\varepsilon\searrow 0).

(2.3)

Proof.

As a first step, we observe that
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)\nearrow F_{0-}(Y)
as

, that is,

\displaystyle F_{-\varepsilon_{1}}(Y)\subset F_{-\varepsilon_{2}}(Y)\qquad(\varepsilon_{1}\geq\varepsilon_{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{0-}(Y)=\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0}F_{-\varepsilon}(Y).

(2.4)

Indeed, the relation (
2.4
.a) is trivial and the relation (
2.4
.b) immediately follows from the continuity of the functions

for

and the compactness of

.

As a second step, we show that for every
\varepsilon^{*}>0
with

) holds true.
So, let
\varepsilon^{*}>0
be such that

. Choose and fix an arbitrary element

be an arbitrary solution of (

) (Proposition

), that is,

\displaystyle x_{0}\in F_{0}(Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x_{0})=\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x).

Also, let

Additionally, we trivially see from the definition of

\displaystyle\left\|x_{-\varepsilon}-x_{0}\right\|=(\varepsilon/\varepsilon^{*})\,\left\|x_{-\varepsilon^{*}}-x_{0}\right\|\leq\operatorname{diam}F_{0}(Y)\cdot\varepsilon/\varepsilon^{*}\qquad(\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon^{*}]).

Combining now (

\displaystyle=f(x_{-\varepsilon})-(f(x_{-\varepsilon})-f(x_{0}))

An immediate consequence of (

\displaystyle g(x,y):=g_{0}(x):=\begin{cases}x^{2}-1,\quad x\in[-1,1]\\
0,\quad x\in X\setminus[-1,1]\end{cases}\qquad((x,y)\in X\times Y).

It is then clear that Condition

3
Convergence and termination of the core algorithm

In this section, we introduce the core algorithm and establish corresponding convergence and termination results that are at the heart of our finite-termination results for both the sequential and the simultaneous algorithm.

3.1
Core algorithm

As has already been mentioned in the introduction, the core algorithm (Algorithm
3.1
) is inspired by the well-known and classic algorithms from
[
2
]
. In contrast to
[
2
]
however, the core algorithm requires only approximate solutions of all arising finite optimization subproblems. And, moreover, just like
[
14
]
the core algorithm features discretized versions of the restrictions (

) of (

) with general restriction parameters

, whereas

Algorithm 3.1
.

Input: sequences
(\overline{\delta}_{k})
,
(\underline{\delta}_{k,i})
in
[0,\infty)
for

,
\rho\in[0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
, a finite subset

of

. With these inputs at hand, perform the following steps:

1.

Set

2.

-approximate solution

3.

Compute a

-approximate solution of

4.

Check the sign of

•

If

and return to Step 2 with

. In this updating rule, the component

\displaystyle Y^{k+1}_{1}:=\{y\in Y^{k}:g_{i}(x^{k},y)\geq-\varepsilon-\rho\text{ for some }i\in I\}

and the component

is chosen as a finite subset of

that contains a strongest violator

\displaystyle\{y^{k,i_{k}}\}\subset Y^{k+1}_{2}\subset Y\qquad\text{and}\qquad|Y^{k+1}_{2}|<\infty,

where

.

•

\lambda_{k}^{*}\in[1/2,1)
, the estimate

\displaystyle\lambda g_{i}(x^{k},y)+(1-\lambda)g_{i}(x^{k+1},y)\leq-\varepsilon\qquad(y\in Y^{k},i\in I\text{ and }\lambda\in[\lambda_{k}^{*},1])

(3.9)

holds true. We have to define
\lambda_{k}^{*}
in different ways depending on the value of
\rho
. Write

\displaystyle\eta:=-\varepsilon-\rho\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{g}(x,y):=\max_{i\in I}g_{i}(x,y)

for brevity. In case
\rho=\infty
we take
\lambda_{k}^{*}:=\lambda^{*}:=1/2\in[1/2,1)
. In case
\rho\in(0,\infty)
we take
\lambda_{k}^{*}:=\lambda^{*}\in[1/2,1)
to be so close to
1
that

\displaystyle(1-\lambda^{*})(|\eta|+\left\|\overline{g}\right\|_{\infty})\leq\rho.

(3.10)

And in case
\rho=0
we take
\lambda_{k}^{*}\in[1/2,1)
to be so close to
1
that

\displaystyle(1-\lambda_{k}^{*})(|\eta|+\left\|\overline{g}\right\|_{\infty})\leq\min\{1/2\,(\eta-g_{i}(x^{k},y)):y\in Y^{k}\setminus Y^{k+1}_{1}\text{ and }i\in I\}

(3.11)

(which is possible because the minimum on the right-hand side is strictly positive by the definition of
Y^{k+1}_{1}
and the finiteness of
Y^{k}
and
I
).
With these definitions of
\lambda_{k}^{*}
, we can now establish (
3.9
).
In the case where
\rho=\infty
, we have
Y^{k}=Y^{k+1}_{1}\subset Y^{k+1}
. Consequently,
g_{i}(x^{k},y)\leq-\varepsilon
and
g_{i}(x^{k+1},y)\leq-\varepsilon
for every
y\in Y^{k}
and
i\in I
by the approximate solution properties of
x^{k}
and
x^{k+1}
, respectively, and therefore

\displaystyle\lambda g_{i}(x^{k},y)+(1-\lambda)g_{i}(x^{k+1},y)\leq-\varepsilon\qquad(y\in Y^{k},i\in I\text{ and }\lambda\in[0,1]).

(3.12)

In particular, this proves (
3.9
) in the case
\rho=\infty
.
In the case where
\rho\in[0,\infty)
, we prove (
3.9
) first for
y\in Y^{k}\cap Y^{k+1}_{1}
and then for
y\in Y^{k}\setminus Y^{k+1}_{1}
.
If
y\in Y^{k}\cap Y^{k+1}_{1}
, then
y\in Y^{k}\cap Y^{k+1}
and therefore

\displaystyle\lambda g_{i}(x^{k},y)+(1-\lambda)g_{i}(x^{k+1},y)\leq-\varepsilon\qquad(i\in I\text{ and }\lambda\in[0,1])

(3.13)

by the same reasoning as for (
3.12
).
If
y\in Y^{k}\setminus Y^{k+1}_{1}
, then

\displaystyle\lambda g_{i}(x^{k},y)+(1-\lambda)g_{i}(x^{k+1},y)

\displaystyle=\eta+(1-\lambda)(|\eta|+g_{i}(x^{k+1},y))-\lambda(\eta-g_{i}(x^{k},y))

\displaystyle\leq-\varepsilon-\rho+(1-\lambda_{k}^{*})(|\eta|+\left\|\overline{g}\right\|_{\infty})-1/2\,(\eta-g_{i}(x^{k},y))

\displaystyle\leq-\varepsilon\qquad(i\in I\text{ and }\lambda\in[\lambda_{k}^{*},1])

(3.14)

by virtue of (
3.10
) or (
3.11
), respectively. In particular, (
3.13
) and (
3.2
) together prove (
3.9
) in the case
\rho\in[0,\infty)
, as desired.

As a second step, we show that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the estimate

\displaystyle f(x^{k})\leq f(\lambda x^{k}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1})+\frac{\overline{\delta}_{k}}{1-\lambda_{k}^{*}}\qquad(\lambda\in[0,1])

(3.15)

holds true.
In order to do so, we consider the function
\varphi_{k}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\varphi_{k}(\lambda):=f(\lambda x^{k}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1})
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Since
x^{k}
is a
\overline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution of
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon}(Y^{k})
, it follows by the first step (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(1)=f(x^{k})\leq\min_{x\in F_{-\varepsilon}(Y^{k})}f(x)+\overline{\delta}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq f(\lambda x^{k}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1})+\overline{\delta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{k}(\lambda)+\overline{\delta}_{k}\qquad(\lambda\in[\lambda_{k}^{*},1]).

(3.16)

Since, moreover,
\varphi_{k}
is convex by the convexity of
f
, it further follows by the secant-slope inequality for convex functions on the reals (Remark IV.2.11 (b) and (c) of
[
1
]
) and by (
3.2
) that

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(1)-\varphi_{k}(\lambda)\leq\frac{\varphi_{k}(1)-\varphi_{k}(\lambda)}{1-\lambda}\leq\frac{\varphi_{k}(1)-\varphi_{k}(\lambda_{k}^{*})}{1-\lambda_{k}^{*}}\leq\frac{\overline{\delta}_{k}}{1-\lambda_{k}^{*}}\qquad(\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{k}^{*}]).

(3.17)

Combining now (
3.2
) and (
3.17
), we obtain (
3.15
), as desired.

As a third step, we show that the estimate (
3.7
) holds true for some
C\in[0,\infty)
.
If Condition
3.2
(i) is satisfied, then there is a
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\overline{\delta}_{k}=0
for all
k\geq k_{0}
. So, by the second step (
3.15
) the asserted estimate (
3.7
) holds true with

\displaystyle C:=\max\{1/(1-\lambda_{k}^{*}):k\in\{0,\dots,k_{0}\}\}\in[0,\infty).

(3.18)

If Condition
3.2
(ii) is satisfied, then
\rho\in(0,\infty)
and thus, by our definition,
\lambda_{k}^{*}=\lambda^{*}
is independent of
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. So, by the second step (
3.15
) the asserted estimate (
3.7
) holds true with

\displaystyle C:=1/(1-\lambda^{*})\in[0,\infty).

(3.19)

As a fourth step, we finally show that there is a null sequence
(\nu_{k})
such that
(f(x^{k})+\nu_{k})
is monotonically increasing.
Indeed, from the estimate (
3.7
) (with
\lambda:=0
), one immediately verifies that the sequence
(f(x^{k})+\nu_{k})
with

\displaystyle\nu_{k}:=C\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\overline{\delta}_{l}-C\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\overline{\delta}_{l}\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N}_{0})

(3.20)

is monotonically increasing and that
(\nu_{k})
is a null sequence, as desired.
∎

As a second important lemma, we show that the accumulation points of the (non-terminating) sequences generated by the core algorithm with a certain restriction parameter
\varepsilon
belong to the feasibility set
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)
. In proving this, also the strict convexity of the objective function
f
is crucial. It should be noticed that
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)
does not have to be assumed to be non-empty – the non-emptiness follows by the non-termination assumption.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that Condition
2.2
and
3.2
are satisfied. Suppose further that
(x^{k})
is generated by Algorithm
3.1
with some
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
and that
(x^{k})
is non-terminating. Then every accumulation point of
(x^{k})
belongs to
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)
.

Proof.

We use the abbreviations
\overline{g}(x,y):=\max_{i\in I}g_{i}(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
and
\overline{y}^{k}:=y^{k,i_{k}}
(strongest violator) throughout the proof. As a first step, we observe that

\displaystyle g_{i}(x^{k},y)\leq\overline{g}(x^{k},\overline{y}^{k})+\underline{\delta}_{k,i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{g}(x^{k+1},\overline{y}^{k})\leq-\varepsilon

(3.21)

for all
y\in Y
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Indeed, since
y^{k,i}
is a
\underline{\delta}_{k,i}
-approximate solution of
\mathrm{Aux}_{i}(x^{k})
, we see that for every
y\in Y
and every
i\in I
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle g_{i}(x^{k},y)\leq g_{i}(x^{k},y^{k,i})+\underline{\delta}_{k,i}\leq g_{i_{k}}(x^{k},y^{k,i_{k}})+\underline{\delta}_{k,i}\leq\overline{g}(x^{k},\overline{y}^{k})+\underline{\delta}_{k,i},

which is (
3.21
.a). Since moreover
\overline{y}^{k}\in Y^{k+1}
by the definition of
Y^{k+1}
and since
x^{k+1}
by its approximate solution property belongs to
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y^{k+1})
, we further see that (
3.21
.b) is satisfied, as desired.

As a second step, we show that for every convergent subsequence
(x^{k_{l}})
of
(x^{k})
there is another subsequence
(k_{l_{j}})
such that

\displaystyle x^{k_{l_{j}}+1}\longrightarrow\lim_{l\to\infty}x^{k_{l}}\qquad(j\to\infty).

(3.22)

So, let
(x^{k_{l}})
be a convergent subsequence of
(x^{k})
. Since
X
is compact, the sequence
(x^{k_{l}+1})
has a convergent subsequence
(x^{k_{l_{j}}+1})
. We denote the limits of these convergent subsequences by

\displaystyle x^{*}:=\lim_{l\to\infty}x^{k_{l}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^{**}:=\lim_{j\to\infty}x^{k_{l_{j}}+1}

(3.23)

and will show that
x^{*}=x^{**}
. It follows from Lemma
3.3
with
\lambda:=1/2
that

\displaystyle f(x^{*})=\lim_{j\to\infty}f(x^{k_{l_{j}}})\leq\lim_{j\to\infty}\bigg(f\bigg(\frac{x^{k_{l_{j}}}+x^{k_{l_{j}}+1}}{2}\bigg)+C\overline{\delta}_{k_{l_{j}}}\bigg)=f\bigg(\frac{x^{*}+x^{**}}{2}\bigg).

(3.24)

It further follows from Lemma
3.3
that the sequence
(f(x^{k})+\nu_{k})
is monotonically increasing for some null sequence
(\nu_{k})
and therefore

\displaystyle f(x^{*})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\big(f(x^{k_{l_{j}}})+\nu_{k_{l_{j}}}\big)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(f(x^{k})+\nu_{k})

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\big(f(x^{k_{l_{j}}+1})+\nu_{k_{l_{j}}+1}\big)

\displaystyle=f(x^{**}).

(3.25)

Assuming now that
x^{*}\neq x^{**}
, we obtain from (
3.24
), the strict convexity of
f
, and (
3.2
) the following strict inequality:

\displaystyle f(x^{*})\leq f\bigg(\frac{x^{*}+x^{**}}{2}\bigg)<\frac{f(x^{*})+f(x^{**})}{2}=f(x^{*}).

(3.26)

Contradiction! So, we must have
x^{*}=x^{**}
, as desired.

As a third step, we show that every accumulation point of
(x^{k})
belongs to
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)
.
So, let
x^{*}
be an accumulation point of
(x^{k})
. Since
Y
is compact, there is a subsequence
(k_{l})
such that
x^{k_{l}}\longrightarrow x^{*}
as
l\to\infty
and such that
(\overline{y}^{k_{l}})
converges to some
y^{*}\in Y
. Also, by the second step, there exists yet another subsequence
(k_{l_{j}})
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}x^{k_{l_{j}}+1}=x^{*}=\lim_{l\to\infty}x^{k_{l}}.

(3.27)

Combining now (
3.21
.a), (
3.27
), (
3.21
.b), we obtain the following estimate for every
y\in Y
and every
i\in I
:

\displaystyle g_{i}(x^{*},y)=\lim_{l\to\infty}g_{i}(x^{k_{l}},y)\leq\lim_{l\to\infty}\overline{g}(x^{k_{l}},\overline{y}^{k_{l}})=\overline{g}(x^{*},y^{*})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\overline{g}(x^{k_{l_{j}}+1},\overline{y}^{k_{l_{j}}})\leq-\varepsilon.

In other words,
x^{*}
belongs to
F_{-\varepsilon}(Y)
, as desired.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose that Condition
2.2
and
3.2
are satisfied and that
F_{0}(Y)\neq\emptyset
. Suppose further that
(x^{k})
is generated by Algorithm
3.1
with
\varepsilon:=0
and that
(x^{k})
is non-terminating. Then
(x^{k})
converges to the unique solution of (
1.1
).

Proof.

We show that
(x^{k})
has only one accumulation point, namely the unique solution
x^{*}
of (
1.1
) (Proposition
2.3
). It is then clear that
(x^{k})
must converge to
x^{*}
.
So, let
x^{**}
be any accumulation point of
(x^{k})
and let
(x^{k_{l}})
be a subsequence with
x^{k_{l}}\longrightarrow x^{**}
as
l\to\infty
. Since
x^{**}\in F_{0}(Y)
by Lemma
3.4
with
\varepsilon:=0
, we have on the one hand that

\displaystyle\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x)\leq f(x^{**}).

(3.28)

Since
x^{k}
is a
\overline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution of
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon}(Y^{k})
with
\varepsilon:=0
and

for every

, we have on the other hand that

(3.29)

Combining now (
3.28
) and (
3.29
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x)\leq f(x^{**})=\lim_{l\to\infty}f(x^{k_{l}})\leq\min_{x\in F_{0}(Y)}f(x).

(3.30)

In conjunction with the aforementioned feasibility relation

, the relation (

) proves that
x^{**}
is a solution of (
1.1
). And therefore
x^{**}=x^{*}
, as desired.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Suppose that Condition
2.2
and
3.2
are satisfied. Suppose further that
(x^{k})
is generated by Algorithm
3.1
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
. Then
(x^{k})
is terminating.

Proof.

Assume that
(x^{k})
is non-terminating. Since
X
and
Y
are compact, we can then choose convergent subsequences
(x^{k_{l}})
and
(\overline{y}^{k_{l}})
of
(x^{k})
and
(\overline{y}^{k}):=(y^{k,i_{k}})
(strongest violators), respectively. We denote their limits by
x^{*}
and

and write

for brevity. It further follows by the non-termination assumption that

because otherwise the algorithm would terminate for some

. And finally it follows by the non-termination assumption and Lemma

Combining now (

) and (

), we see that

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{l\to\infty}\overline{g}(x^{k_{l}},\overline{y}^{k_{l}})=\overline{g}(x^{*},y^{*})\leq-\varepsilon<0.

Contradiction!
∎

4
Approximate solutions with the sequential algorithm

In this section, we apply the core algorithm (Algorithm
3.1
) with smaller and smaller restriction parameters
\varepsilon>0
in sequence in order to obtain our sequential finitely terminating algorithm (Algorithm
4.2
).

4.1
Sequential algorithm

Algorithm 4.1
.

Input: sequences
(\overline{\delta}_{k})
,
(\underline{\delta}_{k,i})
in
[0,\infty)
for
i\in I
,
\rho\in[0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
,
r\in(1,\infty)
, a number
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and a fnite subset
Y^{0}
of
Y
. With these inputs at hand, perform the following steps:

1.

Set
k=0

2.

Check whether or not the restricted discretized problem
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y^{k})
is feasible

•

If
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y^{k})
is infeasible, then set

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\varepsilon_{k}/r\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y^{k+1}:=Y^{k}

and return to Step 2 with
k
replaced by
k+1

•

If
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y^{k})
is feasible, then do the following:

2.1

Compute a
\overline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution
x^{k}
of
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y^{k})

2.2

Compute a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution
y^{k,i}
of
\mathrm{Aux}_{i}(x^{k})
for every
i\in I

2.3

Check the sign of
g_{i}(x^{k},y^{k,i})+\underline{\delta}_{k,i}
for
i\in I

–

If
g_{i}(x^{k},y^{k,i})>-\underline{\delta}_{k,i}
for some
i\in I
, then set

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\varepsilon_{k},

\displaystyle Y^{k+1}:=\{y\in Y^{k}:g_{i}(x^{k},y)\geq-\varepsilon_{k}-\rho\text{ for some }i\in I\}\cup\{y^{k,i_{k}}\}

and return to Step 2 with
k
replaced by
k+1

–

If
g_{i}(x^{k},y^{k,i})\leq-\underline{\delta}_{k,i}
for every
i\in I
, then terminate.

Just like in the core algorithm, the points
y^{k,i_{k}}
in the above algorithm denote strongest violators.

Algorithm 4.2
.

Input: sequences
(\overline{\delta}_{k})
,
(\underline{\delta}_{k,i})
in
[0,\infty)
for
i\in I
,
\rho\in[0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
,
r\in(1,\infty)
, a number
\varepsilon_{0,0}>0
, a fnite subset
Y^{0,0}
of
Y
, and a termination index
m^{*}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
. With these inputs at hand, perform the following steps:

1.

Set
m=0

2.

Apply Algorithm
4.1
with
(\overline{\delta}_{m,k}):=(\overline{\delta}_{m+k})
,
(\underline{\delta}_{k,i})
,
\rho
,
r
,
\varepsilon_{m,0}
,
Y^{m,0}
as inputs in order to compute in finitely many steps an
x^{m,k_{m}}\in X
and an
\varepsilon_{m,k_{m}}\in(0,\varepsilon_{m,0}]
such that

\displaystyle x^{m,k_{m}}\in F_{0}(Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x^{m,k_{m}})\leq\min_{x\in F_{-\varepsilon_{m,k_{m}}}(Y)}f(x)+\overline{\delta}_{m,k_{m}}

(4.1)

3.

If
m<m^{*}
, then set
\varepsilon_{m+1,0}:=\varepsilon_{m,k_{m}}/r
, choose a finite subset
Y^{m+1,0}
of
Y
, and return to Step 2 with
m
replaced by
m+1

4.

If

, then terminate.

4.2
Approximate solutions in finitely many iterations

With the help of the termination result for the core algorithm (Corollary
3.6
) and the convergence rate (
2.2
), we can now establish our main finite-termination result for the sequential algorithm. It says that for every given
\delta>0
, the sequential algorithm with termination index
m^{*}
chosen as in (
4.10
) below terminates at a
\delta
-approximate solution of (
1.1
) after finitely many, namely precisely
m^{*}
, iterations.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that Condition
2.2
and
3.2
are satisfied and that (
1.1
) is strictly feasible. Suppose further that
(x^{k})
and
(\varepsilon_{k})
are generated by Algorithm
4.1
. Then
(x^{k})
terminates at some
k^{*}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

\displaystyle x^{k^{*}}\in F_{0}(Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x^{k^{*}})\leq\min_{x\in F_{-\varepsilon_{k^{*}}}(Y)}f(x)+\overline{\delta}_{k^{*}}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k^{*}}\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]

(4.3)

Proof.

In the entire proof, we denote the set of iteration indices by
K
.
As a first step, we show that there exists a
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y^{k})
is feasible for every iteration index
k\in K
with
k\geq k_{0}
. Assume the contrary, then

\displaystyle F_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y^{k})=\emptyset

(4.4)

for infinitely many
k\in K
and, in particular,
K=\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Consequently, by the definition of Algorithm
4.1
, the restriction parameter is decreased infinitely often by the factor

and therefore

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}\longrightarrow 0\qquad(k\to\infty).

(4.5)

Since now (
1.1
) is strictly feasible by assumption, there exists an
\varepsilon^{*}>0
with
F_{-\varepsilon^{*}}(Y)\neq\emptyset
by virtue of (

.b). And thus, by (
4.5
) and (

.a), there exists a
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle F_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y^{k})\supset F_{-\varepsilon_{k}}(Y)\supset F_{-\varepsilon^{*}}(Y)\neq\emptyset\qquad(k\geq k_{0}).

(4.6)

Contradiction to our assumption (
4.4
)!

As a second step, we show that
(x^{k})
terminates at some
k^{*}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and that (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) are satisfied.
Indeed, by the first step and the definition of Algorithm
4.1
, the restriction parameter is not decreased anymore from iteration

onwards, that is,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}=\dotsb=\varepsilon_{k_{0}}=:\varepsilon_{*}\qquad(K\ni k\geq k_{0})

We therefore see that the sequence
(x^{k_{0}+k})_{k\in K-k_{0}}
can be generated by Algorithm

with
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon_{*}\in(0,\infty)
and with input
(\overline{\delta}_{k_{0}+k})
,
(\underline{\delta}_{k_{0}+k,i})
,
\rho
. So, by virtue of Corollary

, the sequence
(x^{k_{0}+k})_{k\in K-k_{0}}
and, by extension,
(x^{k})_{k\in K}
must be terminating. We denote the terminal iteration index of the latter sequence by
k^{*}:=\max K<\infty
. It then follows by the definition of Algorithm
4.1
that

\displaystyle g_{i}(x^{k^{*}},y^{k^{*},i})\leq-\underline{\delta}_{k^{*},i}\qquad(i\in I).

(4.8)

Since
y^{k^{*},i}
is a
\underline{\delta}_{k^{*},i}
-approximate solution of
\mathrm{Aux}_{i}(x^{k^{*}})
, we therefore see that

\displaystyle g_{i}(x^{k^{*}},y)\leq g_{i}(x^{k^{*}},y^{k^{*},i})+\underline{\delta}_{k^{*},i}\leq 0\qquad(y\in Y\text{ and }i\in I)

(4.9)

and thus (
4.2
.a) is satisfied. Since

is a
\overline{\delta}_{k^{*}}
-approximate solution of
\mathrm{SIP}_{-\varepsilon_{k^{*}}}(Y^{k^{*}})
and

, we further see that (
4.2
.b) is satisfied.
And finally, (
4.3
) is satisfied because the sequence
(\varepsilon_{k})
is obviously monotonically decreasing.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that Condition
2.2
and
3.2
are satisfied and that (

) is strictly feasible. Suppose further that
\delta>0
,
\varepsilon_{0,0}>0
,

and
\varepsilon^{*}>0
with

are given and that

6
Shape-constrained regression

We now show how the algorithms and results developed above can be applied to solve shape-constrained regression problems to arbitrary precision. Shape-constrained regression is about finding a model
u\mapsto v(u)
for some true functional relationship of interest which, on the one hand, optimally fits given data points
(u_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(u_{N},t_{N})
and which, on the other hand, satisfies certain shape constraints known about the relationship to be modeled. In the following, we will express the deviation of the model
u\mapsto v(u)
from the data in terms of the mean-squared error and, moreover, we will confine ourselves to shape constraints that can be expressed in terms of the partial derivatives of
u\mapsto v(u)
, like monotonicity constraints or convexity constraints, for instance. So, the shape-constrained regression problems considered here, take the following form:

\begin{gathered}\min_{v\in V}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\big(v(u_{l})-t_{l}\big)^{2}+\sigma(v)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\psi_{i}\big(u,(\partial^{\alpha}v(u))_{|\alpha|\leq m}\big)\leq 0\\
\quad\text{for all }u\in U\text{ and }i\in I,\end{gathered}

(6.1)

where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
V\subset C^{m}(U,\mathbb{R})
is a suitable set of model ansatz functions
v
and where
\sigma(v)
is a regularization term. Clearly, this is a semi-infinite optimization problem of the general form (
1.1
) considered above. Simple sufficient conditions for the continuity and convexity assumptions (Condtion
2.1
and
2.2
) to be satisfied are as follows.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that
U
is compact in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
V
is a compact subset of
C^{m}(U,\mathbb{R})
and that
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{\overline{m}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz continuous function for every
i\in I
, where
I
is some finite index set and
\overline{m}:=\binom{m+d}{d}
is the number of multi-indices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with degree
|\alpha|\leq m
. Suppose further that
\sigma:V\to[0,\infty)
is continuous and that, for some given data points
(u_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(u_{N},t_{N})\in U\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle f(v):=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\big(v(u_{l})-t_{l}\big)^{2}+\sigma(v)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{i}(v,u):=\psi_{i}\big(u,(\partial^{\alpha}v(u))_{|\alpha|\leq m}\big)

(6.2)

for
u\in U
and
v\in V
. Then
f\in C(V,\mathbb{R})
and
g_{i}\in C(V\times U,\mathbb{R})
for every
i\in I
.
If, in addition,
V
is convex,
\sigma
is strictly convex and the functions
\psi_{i}(u,\cdot)
are affine, then
f
is strictly convex and
g_{i}(\cdot,u)
is convex for every
u\in U
and
i\in I
.

Proof.

An elementary verification.
∎

Very roughly, shape-constrained regression problems can be categorized according to whether the chosen ansatz function set
V
is finite-dimensionally parametrized or not:

\displaystyle\dim\operatorname{span}V<\infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad\dim\operatorname{span}V=\infty,

respectively. Accordingly, we will speak of parametric or, respectively, non-parametric shape-constrained regression problems.

Example 6.2
.

A simple example of a finite-dimensionally parametrized set
V
as above is given by

\displaystyle V:=\{\text{polynomials on $U$ of degree at most $n$ with coefficients contained in $W$}\}

\displaystyle=\{v_{w}:w\in W\},

(6.3)

where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact and
v_{w}(u):=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq n}w_{\alpha}u^{\alpha}
for
u\in U
and where
W
is a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\overline{n}}
with
\overline{n}:=\binom{n+d}{d}
. As a strictly convex regularization term, we can choose
\sigma(v_{w}):=\varsigma|w|^{2}
with some arbitrary
\varsigma>0
. If the functions
\psi_{i}(u,\cdot)
are affine, then the lower-level problems

\displaystyle\max_{u\in U}g_{i}(u,v_{w})=\max_{u\in U}\psi_{i}\big(u,(\partial^{\alpha}v_{w}(u))_{|\alpha|\leq m}\big)

(6.4)

are multivariate polynomial optimization problems and thus tailor-made solvers like
[
8
]
(based on
[
13
]
) or
[
9
]
can be used to approximately solve them.
\blacktriangleleft

Example 6.3
.

A simple example of a not finite-dimensionally parametrized set
V
as above is given by

\displaystyle V:=\{v\in C^{m+1}(U,\mathbb{R}):\left\|v\right\|_{C^{m+1}}\leq R\},

(6.5)

where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact and convex with non-empty interior and
R\in(0,\infty)
.
Indeed, this set is a compact subset of
C^{m}(U,\mathbb{R})
by the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. In order to see that
\operatorname{span}V
is indeed infinite-dimensional, notice that it contains all the monomials
U\ni u\mapsto u^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and that these monomials make up an infinite linearly independent set. As a strictly convex regularization term, we can choose
\sigma(v):=\varsigma\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(U,\mathbb{R})}^{2}
with some arbitrary
\varsigma>0
.

\blacktriangleleft
