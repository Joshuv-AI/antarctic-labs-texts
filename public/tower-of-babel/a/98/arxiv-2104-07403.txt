Evidence of Random Matrix Corrections for the Large Deviations of Selberg’s Central Limit Theorem
E. Amzallag, L.-P. Arguin, E. Bailey, K. Hui, R. Rao

Abstract
Selberg’s central limit theorem states that the values of log ⁡ | ζ ⁡ ( 1 / 2 + i ​ τ ) | \log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}\tau)| , where τ \tau is a uniform random variable on [ T , 2 ​ T ] [T,2T] , is distributed like a Gaussian random variable of mean 0 0 and standard deviation 1 2 ​ log ⁡ log ​ T \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\log\log T} . It was conjectured by Radziwiłł that this breaks down for values of order log ⁡ log ⁡ T \log\log T , where a multiplicative correction C k C_{k} would be present at level k ​ log ⁡ log ​ T k\log\log T , k > 0 k>0 . This constant should be equal to the leading asymptotic for the 2 ​ k t ​ h 2k^{th} moment of ζ \zeta , as first conjectured by Keating and Snaith using random matrix theory. In this paper, we provide numerical and theoretical evidence for this conjecture. We propose that this correction has a significant effect on the distribution of the maximum of log ⁡ | ζ | \log|\zeta| in intervals of size ( log ⁡ T ) θ (\log T)^{\theta} , θ > 0 \theta>0 . The precision of the prediction enables the numerical detection of C k C_{k} even for low T T ’s of order T = 10 8 T=10^{8} . A similar correction appears in the large deviations of the Keating-Snaith central limit theorem for the logarithm of the characteristic polynomial of a random unitary matrix, as first proved by Féray, Méliot and Nikeghbali.

Introduction and Main Results

1.1
Introduction

The large values of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
on the critical line
{\rm Re}(s)=1/2
play an important role in number theory.
There are several conjectures describing its purported behavior.
For example, the
moment conjecture
gives the precise asymptotics of the moments of the function on the interval
[T,2T]
(see for example
[
keasna00a
,
ivic85
,
titchmarsh86
]
):

Conjecture 1

(Moment Conjecture)
.

For
k\geq 0
, as
T\rightarrow\infty
,

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}t)|^{2k}{\rm d}t\sim C_{k}(\log T)^{k^{2}}.

(1)

Lower bounds agreeing with the predicted leading exponent are known unconditionally (see
[
radsou13
,
heasou20
]
and the earlier works of
[
heabro81
,
ram94
]
). Consistent upper bounds on the level of the leading exponent are known unconditionally for
0\leq k\leq 2
(from the work of
[
hearadsou19
]
), and for all
k\geq 0
conditionally on the Riemann hypothesis
[
har13b
,
sou09
]
. This paper is predominantly about the constants
C_{k}
.

At the level of the constant, Conjecture
1
is only proved in the case
k=1
, by Hardy and Littlewood with
C_{1}=1
, and the case
k=2
by Ingham with
C_{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}

[
harlit18
,

]
. The constants
C_{k}
,
k>0
, have been conjectured in
[
keasna00a
]
using random matrix theory to be of the following form

C_{k}=a_{k}\cdot f_{k},

(2)

where

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\text{ primes}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{k^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\Gamma(k+m)}{m!\Gamma(k)}\right)^{2}p^{-m},

(3)

and

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{G}^{2}(1+k)}{\mathcal{G}(1+2k)}.

(4)

Here,
\mathcal{G}
denotes the Barnes
\mathcal{G}-
function. An alternative approach using Dirichlet series yields the same conjecture
[
diagolhof03
]
.

As pointed out in
[

]
, the constants

should also appear in the large deviations of Selberg’s central limit theorem.

(5)

However, for
V
of the order of the variance, the proposed correction is:

Conjecture 2

(Radziwiłł’s Conjecture

1.2
Results

The main objective of this paper is to provide more evidence that the moment correction
C_{k}
, predicted by random matrix theory, should be present in (
6
).
On the theoretical side, it was proved by Féray, Méliot, and Nikeghbali that a similar correction naturally appears in the large deviations of the Keating-Snaith central limit theorem for the characteristic polynomial of the circular unitary ensemble (CUE):

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 7.5.1
[
FerMelNik2016
]
)
.

Let
P_{N}(\theta)=\det(I-e^{{\rm i}\theta}U)
be the characteristic polynomial of an
N\times N
random matrix
U
sampled under the Haar measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}(N)}
on the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(N)
.
Write
Q_{2}(N)
for the second cumulant of
\log|P_{N}(\theta)|
.
Then we have for
V\sim k\sqrt{2\log N}
,
k\geq 0
, and any
\theta\in[0,2\pi)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}(N)}\Big(\log|P_{N}(\theta)|>\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}\cdot V\Big)\sim f_{k}\int_{V}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\rm d}x,\quad\text{ where }f_{k}=\dfrac{\mathcal{G}^{2}(1+k)}{\mathcal{G}(1+2k)}.

The statement of Theorem
1.1
is precisely the random matrix analogue of (
6
). Using the usual dictionary (see, e.g.,
[
keasna00a
,
katsar99b
,
cfkrs05
]
), one compares the unitary characteristic polynomial
P_{N}(\theta)
with
\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}t)
, and by comparing densities of eigenvalues and zeros of zeta, the matrix size
N
corresponds to a height
\log(t/2\pi)
.

Large deviations in various ranges were also considered by Hughes et al.
[
hugkeaoco01
]
. In that paper, they show that
\log|P_{N}(\theta)|/A(N)
satisfies a large deviation principle, for various ranges of
A(N)
. The appropriate range in the context of Theorem
1.1
is
moderate
deviations:
\sqrt{\log{N}}\ll A(N)\ll N
, and it is shown in
[
hugkeaoco01
]
that the rate function is either quadratic or linear, depending on the precise growth of
A(N)
.
Similar results have also been proved in the general context of
\beta
-ensembles and Wigner matrices in
[
DorEic2013
,
DorEic2013Cumu
]
.
Theorem
1.1
differs in that it examines a very particular form of
A(N)
and derives the resulting precise constant multiple of the Gaussian.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
was done in
[
FerMelNik2016
]
in the general context for mod-
\phi
convergence.
For completeness, we provide the detailed computation following the result of Keating & Snaith in Section
2
.

The statement of Theorem
1.1
equally holds for the imaginary part of
\log P_{N}(\theta)
, with the appropriate change to the leading order correction
2

In this case, one would instead find that
f_{k}=\mathcal{G}(1-k)\mathcal{G}(1+k)

2k^{th}
moment of

.
. Additionally, the statement of Theorem
1.1
can be generalized to other classical compact groups, where the distribution should again be Gaussian but with a different correction
g_{k}
(corresponding to the relevant matrix group moment), see for example
[

]
. From such calculations one could deduce conjectures akin to Conjecture

for symplectic and orthogonal families of
L

[
cfkrs05
]
).

Beyond the theoretical evidence from Theorem
1.1
, we also provide numerical evidence of the presence of the correction
C_{k}
.
We choose to investigate its effect on the maximum of the real part of the logarithm of the zeta function in short intervals, instead of directly testing (
6
).
The reason for this is that the presence of the correction leads to a very precise refinement of the Fyodorov-Hiary-Keating conjecture for the maximum of
\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}\tau+{\rm i}h)|
for
h
in a short interval,
[
fyohiakea12
,
fyokea14
]
. The conjecture was originally stated for
mesoscopic
intervals, that is, intervals of size
2\pi(\log T)^{\theta}
for
\theta\leq 0
.
The statement was adapted in
[
argouirad19
]
to
macroscopic
intervals of size
2\pi(\log T)^{\theta}
, for
\theta>0
, but fell short of capturing the order one terms.
It turns out that the correction
C_{k}
has a measurable effect on the recentering of the maximum. More precisely, we propose the following refinement:

Conjecture 3
.

Consider a fixed
\theta>0
.
If
\tau
is uniformly distributed on
[T,2T]
, then we have

\max_{|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta}}\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|=\sqrt{1+\theta}\log\log T-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\theta}}\log\log\log T+\mathcal{G}_{\theta,T},

(8)

where
(\mathcal{G}_{\theta,T},T\geq 1)
is a sequence of random variables converging in distribution to a Gumbel random variable
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
with
\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\leq x)=\exp(-e^{-\frac{1}{\beta}(x-m)})
and parameters

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\beta(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\theta}}

(9)

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=m(\theta)=(0.06537\dots)+\beta^{2}\log C_{\sqrt{1+\theta}}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\Big(\log(1+\theta)-\log(4\pi)\Big).

As can be seen from the leading order of (
8
), the relevant regime of large deviation at a given
\theta
is
\sqrt{1+\theta}\log\log T
.
Together with Conjecture
2
, this leads naturally to the choice
k=\sqrt{1+\theta}
for
C_{k}
in (
9
).
The precise numerical constant appearing in the definition of
m
in (
9
) is the Meissel-Mertens constant divided by
4

).

The upshot of Conjecture

is a very precise prediction to order one for the maximum of
\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|
, including very good control of the finite-size effects, that can be compared to the numerical data. The high precision of the conjecture to order one is the saving grace here, as the factors
\log\log T
,
\log\log\log T
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\theta,T}

T

In particular, this spares us some of the difficulty of testing the moment conjecture, see
[

We chose to test Equations (

) and (

The case
\theta=0
is special as Conjecture

that the subleading order should instead be

In addition, the fluctuations should not be exactly Gumbel but a randomly shifted Gumbel. The effect of the random shift is such that the right tail of the distribution of the recentered maximum is not exponential, as for a pure Gumbel, but should be heavier: of the form
ye^{-\beta y}
. This would in effect

The two above corrections seem hard to observe numerically. One problem is that they are competing effects, which may mutually cancel. Secondly, there is the systematic problem that the standard deviation of the maximum is fairly large at
\theta=0
, as can be seen for example in Figure

Theoretical progress to settle the Fyodorov-Hiary-Keating conjecture has recently been made in
[

abbrs19

argbourad20
]

A continuous smoothing of the subleading order between
-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\theta}}\log\log T
to
-\frac{3}{4}\log\log T
as
\theta\downarrow 0
has been proposed in
[
argdubhar21
]
by taking
\theta\sim(\log\log T)^{-\alpha}
,

. This gives a subleading order of
-\frac{(1+2\alpha)}{4}\log\log T
.
Again, this interpolation seems hard to capture numerically as the standard deviation of the maximum is large for small
\theta
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

For completeness, we provide the proof of Theorem
1.1
following Keating & Snaith
[
keasna00a
]
.
Recall that we write
P_{N}(\theta)=\det(I-e^{{\rm i}\theta}U)
for the characteristic polynomial of a random
N\times N
unitary matrix. Let
V\sim k\sqrt{2\log N}
. We will show that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}(N)}\left(\frac{\log|P_{N}(\theta)|}{\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}}>V\right)\sim f_{k}\int_{V}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\rm d}x,

(10)

where

f_{k}=\frac{\mathcal{G}^{2}(1+k)}{\mathcal{G}(1+2k)},

(11)

and
Q_{2}(N)
is the second cumulant of
\log|P_{N}(\theta)|
. Keating and Snaith calculated the asymptotic form of the cumulants
Q_{j}(N)
,

\displaystyle Q_{1}(N)

\displaystyle=0

(12)

\displaystyle Q_{2}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log N+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma+1)+\frac{1}{24N^{2}}-\frac{1}{80N^{4}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{6}}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle Q_{m}(N)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\frac{2^{m-1}-1}{2^{m-1}}\left(\Gamma(m)\zeta(m-1)-\frac{(m-3)!}{N^{m-2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{m-1}}\right),

(14)

for
m\geq 3
.

Let the probability density function of
\log|P_{N}(\theta)|
be

\rho_{N}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}(N)}[\mathbbm{1}\{\log|P_{N}(\theta)|=x\}],

(15)

where the average is taken over the unitary group with respect to Haar measure. Additionally define the moment generating function for
\log|P_{N}(\theta)|
to be

M_{N}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}(N)}\left[(\log|P_{N}(\theta)|)^{j}\right]}{j!}s^{j}.

(16)

Using the Selberg integral, Keating and Snaith
[
keasna00a
]
determined a finite
N
formula for
M_{N}(s)
, valid for all real
\theta
and
{\rm Re}(s)>-1

M_{N}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}(N)}[|P_{N}(\theta)|^{s}]=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(j)\Gamma(j+s)}{\Gamma^{2}(j+s/2)}.

(17)

Due to the rotational invariance of the Haar measure on
\mathcal{U}(N)
, the right hand side of (
17
) is independent of
\theta
. From (
17
), one deduces that, as
N\rightarrow\infty
,
M_{N}(s)\sim f_{k}N^{k^{2}}
, where
f_{k}
is given by (
11
).

Define

\tilde{\rho}_{N}(x)=\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}\rho_{N}(\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}x),

(18)

the probability density function of
\log|P_{N}(\theta)|
rescaled.
Keating and Snaith determined (cf.
[
keasna00a
]
, Equation (53)) that

\tilde{\rho}_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\left(1+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}(N)}{Q_{2}(N)^{\frac{m}{2}}}\sum_{p=0}^{m}\binom{m}{p}x^{p}\mathcal{E}(m,p)\right),

(19)

where

\mathcal{E}(m,p)=\begin{cases}{\rm i}^{m-p}(m-p-1)!!,&m-p\text{ even},\\
0,&m-p\text{ odd}\end{cases},

(20)

and where the terms
A_{m}(N)
are determined combinatorially from the cumulants and Equation (
18
). For example,

\displaystyle A_{3}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{3}(N)}{3!}

\displaystyle A_{4}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{4}(N)}{4!}

\displaystyle A_{5}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{5}(N)}{5!}

\displaystyle A_{6}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{6}(N)}{6!}+\frac{1}{2!}\frac{Q_{3}(N)^{2}}{(3!)^{2}}.

As
N\rightarrow\infty
,
A_{m}(N)
approaches a constant, see
[
keasna00a
]
.
Evaluating Equation (
19
) at
x=\tfrac{k\log N}{\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}}
then gives

\tilde{\rho}_{N}\left(\frac{k\log N}{\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}\log^{2}N}{Q_{2}(N)}}\left(1+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}(N)}{Q_{2}(N)^{\frac{m}{2}}}\sum_{p=0}^{m}\binom{m}{p}\left(\frac{k\log N}{\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}}\right)^{p}\mathcal{E}(m,p)\right).

(21)

Note that
x\sim k\sqrt{2\log N}
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
Also, for large
N
, the sum over
p
is dominated at
p=m
, hence

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{N}\left(\frac{k\log N}{\sqrt{Q_{2}(N)}}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}\log^{2}N}{Q_{2}(N)}}\left(1+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}A_{m}(N)\left(\frac{k\log N}{Q_{2}(N)}\right)^{m}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-k^{2}\log N}e^{k^{2}(\gamma+1)}\left(1+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}A_{m}(N)(2k)^{m}\right),

(23)

using Equation (
13
).

Finally, recall from the comment following Equation (
17
) that

f_{k}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{M_{N}(2k)}{N^{k^{2}}}.

(24)

Then, since the cumulants
Q_{m}(N)
are related to the Taylor coefficients of
\log M_{N}(s)
(cf. Equation (
16
)),

M_{N}(s)=\exp\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{m}(N)}{m!}s^{m}\right),

(25)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{M_{N}(2k)}{N^{k^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(2k^{2}Q_{2}(N)-k^{2}\log N+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{m}(N)}{m!}(2k)^{m}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle\sim\exp\left(k^{2}(\gamma+1)+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{m}(N)}{m!}(2k)^{m}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(k^{2}(\gamma+1)\right)\left(1+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}A_{m}(N)(2k)^{m}\right).

(28)

The final line follows by expanding the the exponential with the infinite sum in the exponent in the Taylor series, and then grouping terms according to power of
2k
. The fact that this results in the weights
A_{m}(N)
follows immediately from the combinatorial definition, see the discussion following (
20
) and
[
keasna00a
]
. Hence
f_{k}
is given by Equation (
28
), and the result follows by substituting the expression into (
23
).

Derivation of Prediction

3.1
Derivation of Conjecture
3

In this section, we derive Conjecture
3
based on the following assumptions:

Assumption
.

For
\tau
a uniform random variable on
[T,2T]
, the stochastic process

(|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|,|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta})

satisfies the following:

1.

Discretization: the maximum over the interval
[-\pi(\log T)^{\theta},\pi(\log T)^{\theta}]
can be reduced to the maximum over a discrete set
\mathcal{H}_{\theta,T}
corresponding to the midpoints between the zeros of
|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|
on the interval.

2.

Independence: The variables
|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|
,
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\theta,T}
, are independent.

3.

Large deviations of Selberg’s central limit theorem: Equation (
6
) holds with
k=\sqrt{1+\theta}
.

Assumption
1
is reasonable as the maximum should be achieved between two zeros. This can be rigorously established, see
[
argouirad19
]
.
Assumption
2
cannot be exact, but it is likely a very good approximation. Indeed, the correlation between the values at
h
and
h^{\prime}
decays very fast with the distance: as
|h-h^{\prime}|^{-1}
,
[
argouirad19
]
. Assumption
3
is the one to be tested. The choice of
k
comes from the expected leading order of the maximum being
\sqrt{1+\theta}\log\log T
.

From these assumptions, the derivation of the distribution of the maximum is a standard computation in extreme value theory. However, we shall need very good control of the finite-size effects to compare with numerics, so we include the details. The finite-size effects are discussed in the next section.

The number of zeros
\mathcal{N}(t)
on
[0,t]
is known to a very good level of precision thanks to the Riemann-von Mangoldt formula, see for example
[
tsang84
,
ivic85
]
,

\mathcal{N}(t)=\frac{t}{2\pi}\log\frac{t}{2\pi e}+\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm Im}\log\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}t)+\mathcal{O}(1).

(29)

This implies that the number of zeros in the interval
[\tau-\pi(\log T)^{\theta},\tau+\pi(\log T)^{\theta}]
is

\displaystyle N_{\theta,T}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\tau+\pi(\log T)^{\theta})-\mathcal{N}(\tau-\pi(\log T)^{\theta})

(30)

\displaystyle=(\log T)^{\theta}\log\frac{\tau}{2\pi e}+\mathcal{O}((\log T)^{\theta})

\displaystyle=(\log T)^{1+\theta}+\mathcal{O}((\log T)^{\theta}).

This will be the approximation for the cardinality of the discrete set
\mathcal{H}_{\theta,T}
.
The above implies

\log N_{\theta,T}=(1+\theta)\log\log T+\mathcal{O}((\log T)^{-1}).

(31)

Focussing on Assumption
3
, the right tail of the distribution of
\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}\tau)|
is expected to be Gaussian with multiplicative correction
C_{k}
and variance

\sigma^{2}_{T}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\leq T}\frac{1}{p}.

(32)

In
[
rad11
]
, Conjecture
2
is stated for
\sigma_{T}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\log\log T
. Whereas it is true that
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\leq T}\frac{1}{p}=(1+{\rm o}(1))\log\log T
, the
{\rm o}(1)
-term is needed for precise numerics. Mertens’ second theorem asserts that, see for example
[
Van17
]
,

\sum_{p\leq T}\frac{1}{p}=\log\log T+B+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{e^{-\sqrt{0.175\log T}}}{(\log T)^{3/4}}\right),

(33)

where
B=0.26149\dots
is the Meissel-Mertens contant. Hence, from (
32
), the standard deviation is asymptotically

\sigma_{T}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log T}+\frac{B}{2\sqrt{2\log\log T}}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-\sqrt{0.1\log T}}).

(34)

We are now ready to derive Conjecture
3
. We use the shorthand notation
N=N_{\theta,T}
,
\sigma=\sigma_{T}
and
C=C_{\sqrt{1+\theta}}
(the moment coefficient).
Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
, we have for any
Y>0
with
\sigma Y\sim\sqrt{1+\theta}\log\log T
,

\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta}}\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|>\sigma\ Y\right)=\left(1-\frac{NC\int_{Y}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\rm d}x}{N}\right)^{N}.

(35)

First, note that

\left(1-\frac{a}{n}\right)^{n}=e^{-a}\cdot\left(1-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{n}\right)\right),

(36)

so that

\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta}}\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|>\sigma\ Y\right)\sim\exp\left(-NC\int_{Y}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\rm d}x\right).

(37)

The correct level of the maximum is obtained by choosing
Y
for which the numerator in Equation (
35
) is of order one. With this mind, consider
Y^{\star}
the solution to the equation

NC\int_{Y^{\star}}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\rm d}x=1.

(38)

Write
\sigma Y=\sigma\ Y^{\star}+y
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
.
With this notation, Equation (
37
) becomes

\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta}}\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|>\sigma\ Y^{\star}+y\right)\sim\exp\left(-G(y)\right),

(39)

where

G(y)=\dfrac{\int_{Y^{\star}+y/\sigma}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}{\rm d}x}{\int_{Y^{\star}}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}{\rm d}x}.

(40)

The quantity
\sigma Y^{\star}
is the deterministic shift in Equation (
9
). To see this, recall that standard Gaussian estimates give the asymptotics

\left(1-\frac{1}{Y^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{Y}\frac{e^{-\frac{Y^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq\int_{Y}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\rm d}y\leq\frac{1}{Y}\frac{e^{-\frac{Y^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\quad Y\geq 1.

(41)

In other words, it is possible to write

\int_{Y}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\rm d}y=\left(1-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{Y^{2}}\right)\right)\frac{1}{Y}\frac{e^{-\frac{Y^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

(42)

Combining (
38
) and (
42
) gives the following equation for
Y^{\star}
:

\frac{NC}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{e^{-\frac{(Y^{\star})^{2}}{2}}}{Y^{\star}}=1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{(Y^{\star})^{2}}\right).

(43)

The solution can be approximated recursively.
A first approximation omitting
\frac{1}{Y^{\star}}=e^{-\log Y^{\star}}
yields
Y^{\star}\approx\sqrt{2\log N}
.
Writing
Y^{\star}=\sqrt{2\log N}+\delta
in (
43
) gives an equation for
\delta
:

\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}+\delta\sqrt{2\log N}+\frac{1}{2}\log\log N+\frac{1}{2}\log 2+\log\left(1+\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2\log N}}\right)=\log C-\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi.

(44)

It is straightforward to solve this by expanding the logarithm to get

\displaystyle Y^{\star}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\log N}+\delta

(45)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\log N}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\log N}}\Big(\log\log N+\log{4\pi}-2\log C\Big)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}\right).

(46)

It remains to note that Equation (
30
) and Equation (
34
) imply

\displaystyle\sigma\sqrt{2\log N}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\theta}\ \log\log T+\sqrt{1+\theta}\ \frac{B}{2}+{\rm o}(1),

(47)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{2\log N}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\theta}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}((\log\log T)^{-1})\right).

(48)

After multiplication by
\sigma
, the first two terms in Equation (
46
) give the leading correction for the maximum of
\log|\zeta|
:

\sqrt{1+\theta}\log\log T-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\theta}}\log\log\log T.

The remaining terms with
\log C
,
B
,
\log(1+\theta)
and
\log 4\pi
amount to the deterministic shift
m
in Equation (
9
).

It remains to study the fluctuations around
Y^{\star}
.
This is done by computing the asymptotics of the function
G
, defined by (
40
).
Using the Gaussian estimate (
41
) again yields

G(y)\sim e^{-\frac{Y^{\star}}{\sigma}y}\cdot\frac{e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{(2\sigma^{2})}}}{1+\frac{y}{\sigma Y^{\star}}}.

(49)

Equation (
9
) follows by noting that
e^{-y^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\sim 1
by (
34
), and
1+\frac{y}{\sigma Y^{\star}}\sim 1
for any fixed
y
by (
46
).
Moreover, Equations (
30
), (
34
) and (
46
) imply

\frac{Y^{\star}}{\sigma}\sim 2\sqrt{1+\theta}.

(50)

Putting this back in (
49
) gives the parameter
\beta
of the Gumbel distribution in Equation (
9
).

3.2
Discussion of the Finite-Size Effects

In this section, we explain how the numerical predictions of Conjecture
3
are obtained, including the computations of the coefficients
C_{k}
.

According to Conjecture
3
, the mean of
\max_{|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta}}\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|
consists of two terms: the deterministic recentering

\sqrt{1+\theta}\log\log T-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\theta}}\log\log\log T,

(51)

and the expectation of the Gumbel random variable
\mathcal{G}_{\theta,T}
, which in the limit is

\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{G}_{\theta}]=m+\beta\gamma,

(52)

where
\gamma
is the Euler constant
\gamma=0.577\dots
, and with the parameters
m
and
\beta
given in Equation (
9
).

To give an idea of the orders of magnitude in the problem considered, note that for
T=10^{8}
, we have

\log\log 10^{8}=2.78\dots,\quad\log\log\log 10^{8}=1.07\dots\ .

(53)

Scaling the simulations up to to
T=10^{23}
wouldn’t result in much additional precision, since there

\log\log 10^{23}=3.97\dots,\quad\log\log\log 10^{23}=1.39\dots\ .

(54)

This modest gain would come at a substantial computational time cost.
Despite the curse of iterated logarithms, it is possible to derive accurate numerical predictions thanks to a precise control of the lower order terms and their finite-size corrections.

The computation of the deterministic shift
\sigma Y^{\star}
follows the treatment in the last section.
For the number of zeros
N_{\theta,T}
, we rely on Equation (
30
) and take

N_{\theta,T}\approx(\log T)^{\theta}\log\frac{T}{2\pi e}.

(55)

The error term in (
30
) is of order
(\log T)^{-1}
, which is comparatively small.
For the variance
\sigma_{T}^{2}
, note that the constant
B=0.26149\dots
is fairly close to the value of
\log\log T
in view of (
53
).
For this reason, we use Equation
34
in the computation. One might expect a quadratic correction to
\sum_{p\leq T}\frac{1}{p}
of the form
\sum_{p\leq T}\frac{1}{8p^{2}}\approx\frac{1}{32}
, due to the expansion of the Euler product. However, this is negligible for our purpose.
The approximation of the product by the exponential in Equation
36
also comes at a low cost, since the multiplicative error
(1-\mathcal{O}(a^{2}/n))
is evaluated at
a=1
, by design in (
38
), and at
n=N_{\theta,T}\approx(\log T)^{\theta}\geq 18.4
. This multiplicative error becomes of small shift in the exponential, allowing us to discard it.
The Gaussian estimate (
43
) is not quite precise enough for these fine numerics. Indeed, this leads to Equation (
46
), with an error
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\log N_{\theta,T}}{\log N_{\theta,T}}\right)
.
This error remains substantial at any
T
’s that are computationally reachable. For this reason, we approximate
Y^{\star}
directly by numerically solving Equation (
38
).
This takes care of all finite-size corrections to
\sigma Y^{\star}
.

It remains to evaluate the mean of the Gumbel random variable
\mathcal{G}_{\theta,T}
.
As can be seen from Equation (
49
), the function
G
converges to
e^{-2\sqrt{1+\theta}y}
, albeit very slowly. The term
e^{-y^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}
is particularly problematic since
\sigma
is of the order
\sqrt{\log\log T}
.
To take care of this, instead of using
\beta\gamma
in the mean of a Gumbel (cf. Equation (
52
)), we simply evaluate the mean by working directly with the function
G(y)
in Equation (
40
).
The mean of the recentered random variable
\max_{|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta}}\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|-\sigma Y^{\star}
can then be evaluated using the cumulative distribution function
1-\exp(-G(y))
. In Equation (
50
), we also use
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma Y^{\star}}
for
\beta
instead of the limiting value
(2\sqrt{1+\theta})^{-1}
. For
\theta=3
, these considerations yield a mean of
0.17\dots
compared to
0.14\dots
for the limiting function
e^{-2\sqrt{1+\theta}y}
. In the same way, the variance of
\max_{|h|\leq\pi(\log T)^{\theta}}\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}(\tau+h))|
in the limit should be the one of a Gumbel with parameter
\beta
, i.e.,

\text{Var}(\mathcal{G}_{\theta})=\frac{\beta^{2}\pi^{2}}{6}.

(56)

The finite-size effects of computing the variance with the CDF
1-\exp(-G(y))
are tiny compared to using (
56
), so we use Equation (
56
) for simplicity.

C_{k}
