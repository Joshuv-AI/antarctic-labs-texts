DIISC-V: Variations in H α \alpha -to-FUV Star Formation Rate Ratios Across Star-forming Regions in Nearby Galaxies Facilities: GALEX, VATT, Spitzer, WISE, VLA
Mansi Padave, Sanchayeeta Borthakur, Rolf A. Jansen, David A. Thilker, Jacqueline Monkiewicz, Rogier A. Windhorst

Abstract
We present the variations in far-ultraviolet (FUV) and H α \alpha star formation rates (SFR), SFR UV and SFR Hα , respectively, at sub-kpc scales in 11 galaxies as part of the Deciphering the Interplay between the Interstellar Medium, Stars, and the Circumgalactic medium (DIISC) survey. Using archival GALEX FUV imagery and H α \alpha +[N II] narrowband images obtained with the Vatican Advanced Technology Telescope, we detect a total of 1335 (FUV-selected) and 1474 (H α \alpha -selected) regions of recent high-mass star formation, respectively. We find the H α \alpha -to-FUV SFR ratios tend to be lower primarily for FUV-selected regions, where SFR H ​ α {}_{\textrm{H}\alpha} generally underestimates the SFR by an average factor of 2–3, for SFR < < 10 -4 M ⊙ yr -1 . In contrast, the SFRs are generally observed to be consistent for H α \alpha -selected regions. This discrepancy arises from morphological differences between the two indicators. Extended FUV morphologies and larger areas covered by FUV-only regions, along with decreasing overlap between FUV clumps and compact H II regions with R / R 25 R/R_{25} suggest that stochastic sampling of the initial mass function may be more pronounced in the outer regions of galaxies. Our observed H α \alpha -to-FUV SFR ratios are also consistent with stochastic star formation model predictions. However, using larger apertures that include diffuse FUV emission results in an offset of 1 dex between SFR Hα and SFR UV . This suggests that the observed low H α \alpha -to-FUV SFR ratios in galaxies are likely caused by diffuse FUV emission, which can contribute ∼ \sim 60–90% to the total FUV flux.

I
Introduction

The H
\alpha
nebular line and far-ultraviolet (FUV) continuum are two of the most widely used tracers of star formation in galaxies
[
33
,
37
]
. The FUV continuum is produced by non-ionizing UV photons from O-stars as well as B- and A-stars and probes timescales of
\sim
100 Myr.
In contrast, H
\alpha
emission is a byproduct of gas ionized by UV photons emanating from the photospheres of short-lived massive O-type and early B-type stars, tracing a shorter star formation timescale of
\lesssim
10 Myr. Despite their power to probe recent and current high-mass star formation, respectively, these indicators have long been scrutinized due to the discrepancy between the star formation rates (SFR) of galaxies inferred from them.

Following the standard calibrations from
[
34
]
, consistent values for the SFR, invariant with time and environment, are expected from the two indicators. Nonetheless, several observations have revealed a systematic decline in the H
\alpha
-to-FUV ratios with decreasing luminosities and at low levels of star formation in galaxies
[
12
,
6
,
30
,
53
,
45
,
10
]
.
It is found that while the average ratio remains constant at SFR
\gtrsim 0.1
M
⊙
yr
-1
, it varies by a factor of 2 to an order of magnitude for SFR
\lesssim 10^{-3}
M
⊙
yr
-1
, also observed for low-mass dwarf galaxies
[
45
,
44
]
. These observations highlight the nuances in the production of H
\alpha
and FUV photons, due to deviations from the assumptions that are foundational to SFR prescriptions: a constant star formation history (SFH) over
\sim
1 Gyr, a well-populated initial mass function (IMF) for solar metallicity, and no loss of Lyman continuum photons or dust extinction.

Many independent studies have analyzed the variations in H
\alpha
-to-FUV ratios in galaxies to explore the deviations from the model assumptions. Non-constant or bursty SFH have been found to have a considerable impact on the ratios
[
30
,
75
,
19
,
31
]
.
Lower H
\alpha
emission in galaxies, especially for the low mass systems (
M<10^{8}M_{\odot}
), can be explained by a lull in star formation, during which the H
\alpha
luminosities can drop quickly compared to the FUV luminosities
[
51
]
.
Moreover, stochastic sampling of stellar and cluster IMFs means lower-mass systems are likely to have fewer high-mass clusters and, consequently, fewer high-mass stars
[
21
,
39
]
.
Additionally, the escape of ionizing photons from HII regions lowers photoionization rates, and excluding the diffuse component of H
\alpha
emission in the warm ionized medium can lead to underestimating H
\alpha
luminosities
[
28
,
18
,
29
,
61
,
44
,
74
]
. Given the abundance of evidence supporting these factors, they all likely play a role in the observed variations in H
\alpha
-to-FUV ratios.

On spatially-resolved scales, the H
\alpha
-to-FUV ratios in galaxies provide interesting insights into star formation in galactic outskirts. In search of a star formation threshold,
[
49
]
identified truncated H
\alpha
radial profiles in nearby spiral galaxies. However, such sharp declines were not observed in the UV profiles, and many nearby galaxies showed UV knots at large galactocentric distances or emitted faint UV, termed Type-I and Type-II extended ultraviolet (XUV) disks
[
69
]
. These differences in the radial profiles have been associated both with the relative numbers of the detected UV and H
\alpha
knots as well as the declining fluxes in the outer disks of galaxies
[
79
,
23
]
.
H
II
regions have also been discovered well beyond the optical edge (R
25
) of the galaxy disks
[
26
,
20
,
72
,
46
,
64
,
76
]
, sometimes as far out as 4R
25
, as is the case with M83
[
68
]
.

So far, H
\alpha
-to-FUV ratios in galaxies have been extensively investigated with integrated luminosity and SFR measurements.
On spatially-resolved scales, the ratios have been explored mainly for XUV disk galaxies or individual galaxies
[
5
,
13
,
57
]
. As part of the Deciphering the Interplay between Star Formation, Interstellar Medium, and the Circumgalactic Medium (DIISC) survey,
[
57
]
investigated H
\alpha
emission for FUV-selected stellar associations in NGC 3344. They found a considerable scatter in the H
\alpha
-to-FUV SFR ratios in the XUV region, with SFR
UV
\gtrsim
SFR
Hα
for the stellar associations.
In this paper, we delve deeper into the variations between the FUV and H
\alpha
SFR indicators on sub-kpc scales for a larger sample and include star-forming galaxies showing signatures of stellar disk growth
[see
58
, and §
II
for details]
from the DIISC survey. Our primary goal in this work is to examine the morphology and spatial distribution of FUV and H
\alpha
emission and investigate aperture effects on the connection between SFR
UV
and SFR
Hα
at sub-kpc scales. The structure of the paper is as follows. In §
II
, we present our sample, observations, and data reduction and calibration procedures. The source detection on the H
\alpha
and FUV maps is described in §
III
. We present our results on the H
\alpha
-to-FUV SFR ratios of the detected sources and their spatial overlaps in §
IV
, followed by a discussion on the relation between SFR
Hα
and SFR
UV
. Finally, we present our conclusions in §
VI
.

II.1
Data Reduction & Calibration of H
\alpha
Images

The VATT H
\alpha
+[N II] and
r
images were reduced as described in
[
57
]
and
[
58
]
. In brief, we use standard IRAF procedures to perform overscan correction, bias subtraction, and flat-field correction.
Removal of cosmic rays was carried out using the
L.A.COSMIC
routine
[
70
]
.
Science exposure frames were aligned using
Astroalign

[
7
]
before co-adding. The World Coordinate System of the final stacked images was refined with the
http://astormetry.net
software
[
43
]
.
The background was estimated using Astropy Photutils
[
11
]
and subsequently removed. Flux calibration of the
r
maps was carried out using the Aperture Photometry Tool
[
42
]
by matching the instrumental magnitudes to the SDSS DR12 magnitudes of field stars.
For the broad-band
r
images, we reach 1
\sigma
depths of surface brightness of
\sim
25–27 mag arcsec
-2
.

The H
\alpha
+[N II] images were processed further to remove the stellar continuum. The
r
image was appropriately scaled and then subtracted from the H
\alpha
image, to produce an emission-line only H
\alpha
+[N II] image.
The scale factor,
F
, defined as the ratio of narrow-to-broad band counts, was estimated empirically using the field stars under the assumption that they do not emit H
\alpha
.
We iterated the continuum subtraction process for a range of scale factors until the residual flux of non-saturated foreground stars was lowest, and the surface brightness of the continuum-dominated regions reached the the background level.
This implicitly assumes the galaxy disk spectral energy distribution matches the foreground stars and does not vary with location. For the selected galaxies in our sample, the nominal scale factors were found to be between 0.031–0.047.

The absolute flux measurements of the emission-only H
\alpha
+[N II] image were determined using the method outlined in
[
35
]
. We employed the photometric zero point (ZP) of the
r
-band image, the bandwidth (FWHM
{}_{\text{NB}}
) of the corresponding narrowband H
\alpha
filter, count rates (CR
{}_{\text{H}\alpha}
) in counts s
-1
from the continuum-subtracted H
\alpha
+[N II] map, and
F
to compute the total emission-line flux,
f_{\text{tot}}
:

f_{\text{tot}}=U\cdot\text{FWHM}_{\text{NB}}\cdot\text{CR}_{\text{H}\alpha}\cdot T_{\lambda}^{-1}

Here,
U
and
T_{\lambda}
are calculated as follows:

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}10^{-0.4[\text{ZP}+2.397-\kappa\sec(x)]}

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=T_{NB}(\lambda)-T_{r}(\lambda)\frac{t_{r}}{t_{NB}}F

We assumed a standard atmospheric extinction coefficient,
\kappa=0.08
mag airmass
-1
for airmass
x
. The term
T_{\lambda}
adjusts for the effective filter transmission at the redshifted H
\alpha
line, ensuring the recovery of the H
\alpha
flux from the
r
image lost during continuum subtraction.

T_{NB}(\lambda)
and
T_{r}(\lambda)
represent the normalized filter transmission, while
t_{NB}
and
t_{r}
denote the exposure times of the H
\alpha
and
r
maps, respectively. A Galactic (foreground) extinction correction is also applied based on
[
67
]
, with A
{}_{H\alpha}=2.5\times E(\bv)
.
We note that these calculations contain the contribution from the
[N II]
\lambda\lambda 6548,6584
forbidden lines. All narrowband H
\alpha
observations of our sample include [N II] emission and the process of subtracting the [N II] flux and its implications are discussed in §
III.4
.

III.1
Source Detection

We created a catalog of both UV- and H
\alpha
-bright objects in our galaxies using
Source Extractor

[
8
]
and its Python library,
sep

[
4
]
. UV-bright stellar complexes or star-forming clumps are detected using the GALEX maps and are referred to as FUV-selected regions. H
II
regions, H
\alpha
knots or H
\alpha
-bright objects detected using the VATT H
\alpha
+[N II] maps are termed as H
\alpha
-selected regions.

The FUV-selected regions were detected by extracting sources 3
\sigma
above the background level of the FUV maps. To optimize source extraction in various environments, such as the densely populated central regions of galaxies and the sparser galactic outskirts, we execute separate runs of
sep
on cutouts of different regions within each galaxy. Adjustments to the background box and filter sizes, as well as the deblending threshold and contrast parameter, were made for each run to ensure efficient object detection and minimize overlap between detected objects. After a preliminary catalog of objects is created for a galaxy, the catalog is cleaned for background and foreground contaminants and overlapping apertures. Apertures at the positions of foreground stars seen in the optical images were removed. Additionally, manual adjustments were made to redefine certain apertures to encompass detected regions effectively.

A similar process is employed for extracting H
\alpha
-selected regions from the H
\alpha
+[N II] maps. Prior to source extraction, the H
\alpha
+[N II] maps, with a resolution of
\sim
1
\farcs
5 FWHM, are convolved to match the resolution of the GALEX FUV map (4
\farcs
2 FWHM). To achieve this, the H
\alpha
+[N II] maps, which have a pixel scale of 0
\farcs
375/pixel, were first resampled to 1
\farcs
5/pixel to match the FUV maps and then smoothed. This process ensures consistent source detection and spatial matching, facilitating a direct comparison between the FUV-selected and H
\alpha
-selected regions.

For the final catalog, we also ensure that only regions reliably detected in both the H
\alpha
and FUV datasets are considered. For this, a region is included in the final catalog only if it is detected above the sensitivity limits of both maps. The final catalog comprises 1335 regions detected in the FUV and 1474 regions detected in H
\alpha
across our sample of 11 DIISC galaxies. In Figure
3
, we show histograms depicting the distributions of various properties of the FUV- and H
\alpha
-selected regions, along with their radial distribution. Properties of the regions detected in each galaxy are described in the following sections and summarised in Table
2
.

III.2
Source Sizes & Morphologies

The physical sizes of the detected regions vary between 60 pc to 3.6 kpc. These range from compact, isolated stellar associations or H
II
-regions to UV-bright star-forming clumps or conglomerations of H
II

\alpha

Consequently, the morphology of the FUV-selected regions tends to be more elongated than the H
\alpha
-selected regions, as observed in the top left panel of Figure

\alpha
-selected regions are also smaller than those of the FUV-selected regions, as depicted in the top right panel of Figure
3

\gtrsim
1 kpc
2
in the catalog are predominantly from the more distant galaxies in our sample: NGC 99, NGC 3433, and NGC 3485, as a direct consequence of degradation of resolution.

III.4
H
\alpha
-to-FUV Ratios of Sources

The H
\alpha
-to-FUV SFR ratios, or SFR
Hα
/SFR
UV
are estimated for all FUV- and H
\alpha
-selected regions. Before estimating the SFRs, we attempt to retrieve the true H
\alpha

\alpha
+[N II] maps. Corrections for the contamination from [N II]
\lambda\lambda 6548,6584
forbidden lines are implemented using [N II]/H
\alpha

2
and described below.

Olvera et al. (2024) investigated the optical spectra of H
II
regions in NGC 99, estimating the metallicity gradient in the galaxy using several emission lines-selected indicators, including [N II]/H
\alpha

\sim
25% of H
\alpha
in the center, decreasing to
\sim
6% in the outer zones of the galaxy.
Similarly, in NGC 3344, the [N II]/H
\alpha
ratios range from 0.6 in the inner regions to 0.2 in the outer regions
[
55
]
. We make use of the [N II]/H
\alpha
gradient from
[
55
]
to recover the H
\alpha
flux in NGC 3344 in this study instead of the integrated [N II]/H
\alpha
value of 0.52 used in
[
57

For NGC 3351, NGC 4303, and NGC 4321, gas-phase metallicity gradients from
[

[
16

\alpha

\alpha

[

R_{25}
compared to those beyond and reduce systematic uncertainties.
For most galaxies, the [N II]/H

R\leq R_{25}

\alpha

We also account for internal dust extinction of the FUV and H
\alpha

For extinction correction, a combination of FUV and H

We use the following calibrations computed in

[

\displaystyle SFR_{\rm UV}

\displaystyle=0.68\times 10^{-28}~{\rm L}_{\nu,\rm{FUV}}+2.14\times 10^{-42}~{\rm L}_{\rm{24}\mu{\rm m}}

\displaystyle SFR_{\rm{H}\alpha}

\displaystyle=5.3\times 10^{-42}(\rm{L}_{\rm{H}\alpha}+3.1\times 10^{-2}~\rm{L}_{\rm{24}\mu{\rm m}})

where SFR

⊙

\alpha

-1
Hz

-1

-1

[

[
40

Where 24

We only apply a first-order extinction correction from the IR maps to a detected region when estimating SFRs instead of estimating 22
\mu
m or 24

Secondly, the 24

Instead, we make use of the 24

\alpha

Our results so far have highlighted the effect of FUV- and H
\alpha
-selected apertures on H
\alpha
-to-FUV SFR ratios of star-forming regions. While in general, FUV and H
\alpha
closely follow each other, discrepancies in the FUV-to-H
\alpha

\alpha
-to-FUV ratios in this section.

We present the H

We do not observe a severe decline in the ratios as is seen for integrated SFRs from

\sim

\sim

[

\alpha
-selected regions contribute 10–50%, and the clump fraction is on average
\sim

\times

Moreover, it is also probable that regions with high SFR

VI
Conclusion

In this study, we investigate the distribution and variations in the H
\alpha
and FUV emission and examine H
\alpha
-to-FUV flux ratios across star-forming regions in 11 nearby star-forming galaxies. We utilize archival GALEX FUV imaging and our narrowband H
\alpha
+[N II] imaging from the VATT to extract FUV and H
\alpha
sources from the respective maps. We note these sources as FUV-selected and H
\alpha
-selected regions. We investigate the demographics of these regions, morphologies, and radial distributions and also explore SFR
UV
and SFR
Hα
for these regions. We summarize our key findings below:

1.

We detect 1335 FUV-selected and 1474 H
\alpha
-selected regions throughout our sample of 11 galaxies. The morphology of the FUV-selected regions tends to be extended and these regions generally occupy 55–85% of the total detected area in a galaxy. H
\alpha
-selected regions, on the other hand, are generally compact compared to the FUV-selected regions.

2.

The spatial distribution of the FUV-selected and H
\alpha
-selected regions are identical, and regions are detected even beyond
R_{25}
. However, the areal spread of FUV-selected regions is significantly larger than H
\alpha
-selected regions and increases with
R/R_{25}
. The overlap between FUV- and H
\alpha
-selected regions also decreases with
R/R_{25}
pointing towards a radial increase in the stochastic effects in the production of massive stars responsible for producing H
II
regions. Our observed distribution of H
\alpha
-to-FUV SFR ratios agrees with stochastic star formation models that simultaneously consider the SFR, CMF, and IMF.

3.

Lower H
\alpha
-to-FUV SFR ratios at low levels of SFRs are primarily observed for FUV-selected regions. At SFR
\lesssim 10^{-3}
M
⊙
yr
-1
, SFR
Hα
is underestimated by a factor of 2–3 for the FUV-selected regions. However, when larger grids are used to estimate SFRs, this factor consistently increases, and can be as high as
\sim
10. This discrepancy is due to the increasing contribution from diffuse UV flux not fully captured by FUV-selected regions, which only account for 8–36% of the total UV flux in our sample. On the other hand, H
\alpha
-to-FUV SFR ratios for H
\alpha
-selected regions do not show any dependence on
R/R_{25}
and SFR
Hα
, and are only underestimated by a factor of 1.2–1.5.

The scatter in the SFR
Hα
and SFR
UV
also suggests that other factors, such as dust, LyC leakage, and varying star formation histories are also playing a role in governing the H
\alpha
-to-FUV ratio. In the future, further modeling of star formation history, metallicities, and dust effects may improve our understanding of observed offsets and how best to evaluate SFRs in low-density media.
