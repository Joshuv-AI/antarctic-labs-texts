1
Introduction

Let
\xi
be a non-zero real number and let
n
be a positive integer. Dirichlet’s theorem (1842) is one of the most basic results of Diophantine approximation. It shows that for any real number
H>1
, there exists a non-zero integer point
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
such that

\displaystyle\max\big\{|x_{1}|,\dots,|x_{n}|\big\}\leq H\and|x_{0}+x_{1}\xi+\cdots+x_{n}\xi^{n}|\leq H^{-n}.

(1.1)

It is natural to ask if we can improve the exponent
n
of
H^{-n}
, and this question gives rise to two Diophantine exponents. The so-called
uniform exponent of approximation

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
(resp. the
ordinary
exponent
\omega_{n}(\xi)
), is the supremum of the real numbers
\omega>0
such that the system

\displaystyle\|P\|\leq H\and 0<|P(\xi)|\leq H^{-\omega}

admits a non-zero solution
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
of degree at most
n
for each sufficiently large
H
(resp. for arbitrarily large
H
). Here,
\|P\|
denotes the (naive)
height
of
P
, defined as the largest absolute value of its coefficients. These quantities have been extensively studied over the past half-century, see for example
[
5
]
for a nice overview of the subject. By Dirichlet’s theorem, if
\xi
is not an algebraic number of degree
\leq n
, then we have

\omega_{n}(\xi)\geq\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\geq n,

and it is well known that those inequalities are equalities for almost all real numbers
\xi
(w.r.t. Lebesgue measure). Note that if
\xi
is an algebraic number of degree
d
, then
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
and
\omega_{n}(\xi)
are both equal to
\min\{n,d-1\}
(it is a consequence of Schmidt’s subspace theorem, see
[
5
, Theorem 2.10]
). We can therefore restrict our study to the set of transcendental real numbers. The initial question “can we improve the exponent
n
in Dirichlet’s Theorem?” may be rephrased as follows: “does there exist a transcendental real number
\xi
satisfying
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>n
?”. For
n=1
the answer is negative and rather elementary to prove, so the first non-trivial case is
n=2
. Before the early
2000
s, it was conjectured that no such number existed. This belief was swept away by Roy’s extremal numbers
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
1
]
, whose exponent
\widehat{\omega}_{2}
is equal to the maximal possible value
(3+\sqrt{5})/2=2.618\cdots
. Since then, several families of transcendental real numbers whose uniform exponent
\widehat{\omega}_{2}
is greater than
2
have been discovered (see for example
[
22
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
15
,
18
]
). However, for
n\geq 3
the mystery remains, and it is still an open question wether or not there exists
\xi\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
with
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>n
.

In this paper, we are interested in finding an upper bound for the uniform exponent
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
, as this could provide clues to solving the initial problem. Brownawell’s version of Gel’fond’s criterion
[
3
]
implies that
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 3n
. In 1969, Davenport and Schmidt
[
10
, Theorem 2b]
showed that for any transcendental real number
\xi
and any integer
n\geq 2
, we have

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-1.

(1.2)

Up to now, few improvements have been made. Bugeaud and Schleischitz
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
first got the upper bound

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq n-\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{n^{2}-2n+1/4}=2n-\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon_{n},

(1.3)

where
\varepsilon_{n}>0
tends to
0
as
n
tends to infinity. Recently, Marnat and Moshchevitin
[
13
]
proved an important conjecture of Schmidt and Summerer on the ratio
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)/\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
(also see
[
19
, Chapter 2]
for an alternative proof based on parametric geometry of numbers). In
[
23
]
, Schleischitz pointed out that we can use the aforementioned inequality in the proof of (
1.3
) to get

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-2,

for each
n\geq 10
. This is currently the best known upper bound. Let us also mention that using parametric geometry of numbers, Schleischitz
[
24
, Theorem 1.1]
was able to replace the estimate (
1.3
) by

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq\frac{3(n-1)+\sqrt{n^{2}-2n+5}}{2}=2n-2+\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}

where
\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}>0
tends to
0
as
n
tends to infinity. For
n=3,\dots,9
, bounds that are better than (
1.2
), but (strictly) greater than
2n-2
, are known. For example, for each transcendental real number
\xi
, we have

\widehat{\omega}_{3}(\xi)\leq 3+\sqrt{2}=4.41\cdots

see
[
8
]
. In this paper, without relying on Marnat-Moshchevitin’s inequality, we show in Section
7
that the upper bounds
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-2
holds for any
n\geq 4
. We also improve the upper bound for
\widehat{\omega}_{3}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
be a transcendental real number. If
n\geq 4
, then

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-2.

For
n=3
, we have the weaker estimate
\widehat{\omega}_{3}(\xi)\leq 2+\sqrt{5}=4.23\cdots
.

We do not think that these upper bounds are optimal. Our main result below is a significant improvement of the previous results as
n
tends to infinity and does not require Marnat and Moshchevitin’s inequality
[
13
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Set
a=1/3
. There exists a computable constant
N\geq 1
such that, for each
n\geq N
and any transcendental real number
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-an^{1/3}.

The constant
a=1/3
is not optimal. Numerical calculations based on the results from Section
11
suggest that we could take
N
rather “small” in Theorem
1.2
(maybe
N\leq 10^{4}
?). However, as we wanted to keep the arguments and calculations as clear and simple as possible, we did not try to provide an explicit value of
N
.

Theorem
1.2
can be compared to
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
, where we study
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(\xi)
, the uniform exponent of rational simultaneous approximation to the successive powers
\Xi=(1,\xi,\xi^{2},\dots,\xi^{n})
(which is known to be, in a sense, dual to
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
), see Section
2
for the precise definition and more details. We were not able to deduce one result from the other, even though some objects from the proofs clearly play similar roles. For example, given a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
of degree at most
n
, which is a good approximation, we can associate the
k+1
polynomials
P,XP,\dots,X^{k}P
of degree at most
n+k
. They provide information on
\widehat{\omega}_{n+k}(\xi)
. On the other hand, if we consider
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
which is a good approximation of
\Xi
(for simultaneous approximation), we can associate the
k+1
blocks of successive
n+1-k
coordinates of
\mathbf{y}
, which are rather good approximations of
(1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{n-k})
. They in turn provide information on
\widehat{\lambda}_{n-k}(\xi)
. Note that the difficulties in the proofs of both theorems are not in the same places. In particular, in this paper we have to work with
irreducible
polynomials, a rather heavy constraint. Also, one of the most delicate parts of our approach is to bound from above the ordinary exponent
\omega_{n}(\xi)
, whereas this is rather “simple” to do that for the ordinary exponent
\lambda_{n}(\xi)
in
[
16
]
.

Before presenting our strategy, let us quickly explain Davenport and Schmidt’s proof of the upper bound (
1.2
). Given a real number
\widehat{\omega}<\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
, they show, using elementary means and Gelfond’s Lemma, that there are infinitely many pairs of coprime polynomials
P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
of degree at most
n
, such that

\|Q\|\leq\|P\|\and\max\{|Q(\xi)|,|P(\xi)|\}\ll\|P\|^{-\widehat{\omega}},

(where the implicit constant only depends on
n
). It implies that the resultant
\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)
, which is a non-zero integer, satisfies

1\leq|\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)|\ll\|P\|^{n-1}\|Q\|^{n-1}\max\big\{\|P\||Q(\xi)|,\|Q\||P(\xi)|\big\}\ll\|P\|^{2n-1-\widehat{\omega}}.

The first upper bound for
|\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)|
is classical, see Lemma
4.1
. Since
\|P\|
can be arbitrarily large, they deduced that the exponent
2n-1-\widehat{\omega}
is non-negative. Estimate (
1.2
) follows by letting
\widehat{\omega}
tend to
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
. Note that the term
2n
in (
1.2
) is directly related to the size of the
2n\times 2n
determinant defining
\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)
(if we suppose that
P
and
Q
have degree exactly
n
).

The key idea in the proof of our main Theorem
1.2
is to work with a large number of “good” linearly independent polynomial approximations
Q_{0},\dots Q_{j+1}
rather than just two polynomials
P
and
Q
as above. By doing this, we can replace
\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)
by a non-zero
(2n-j)\times(2n-j)
determinant (whose non-zero entries are among the coefficients of the polynomials
Q_{k}
). Under the ideal and unlikely assumption that

\displaystyle\|Q_{k}\|\leq\|Q_{0}\|\and|Q_{k}(\xi)|\ll\|Q_{0}\|^{-\widehat{\omega}}\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{(for $k=0,\dots,j$)},

(1.4)

the aforementioned determinant would be bounded from above by
\|Q_{0}\|^{2n-j-1-\widehat{\omega}}
. So, together with an additional non-vanishing assumption, it would lead to
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-j-1
. Several new difficulties arise when trying to make the above arguments work. We introduce the tools for the construction of the generalized resultant in Section
6
. To ensure that this determinant does not vanish, we need the extra assumption that
Q_{0},\dots,Q_{j+1}
are irreducible polynomials. The idea is to first fix a sequence of best approximations, that we called
minimal polynomials
, and then to consider their highest-degree irreducible factors (which also happen to be rather good approximations). We deal with this question in Section
5
. Two obstacles remain. Firstly, note that it may be possible that the best polynomial approximations span a subspace of dimension
3
, even when
\xi
is transcendental and
n
is large, see
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
. Therefore, as soon as
j>1
(we will later choose
j\asymp n^{1/3}
), we have to justify that we can find
j+2
linearly independent polynomials as above. The second major problem is the control of the sequence
Q_{0},\dots,Q_{j+1}
. Estimates (
1.4
) seem out of reach, instead we get upper bounds of the form

\displaystyle\|Q_{k}\|\leq\|Q_{0}\|\and|Q_{k}(\xi)|\ll\|Q_{0}\||^{-\widehat{\omega}\theta}\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{(for $k=0,\dots,j$)},

(1.5)

where
\theta<1
depends only on
n
and
j
, and is “close” to
1
if
j
is not too large compared to
n
. The main ingredients for showing this are related to
twisted heights
, see Sections
8.2
and Appendix
A
, and an important inequality on the height of subspaces due to Schmidt. The parameter
\theta
in (
1.5
) is a function of the exponent of best approximation
\omega_{n}(\xi)
. We show in Section
10
that if the uniform exponent satisfies
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\geq 2n-d
(with
d\ll n^{1/3}
), then the ordinary exponent
\omega_{n}(\xi)
is bounded from above by
2n+2d^{2}
, and the ratio
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)/\omega_{n}(\xi)
is therefore close to
1
. This part, which is essentially independent from the others, is rather delicate, because we work with the polynomials
Q_{i}
. They are certainly irreducible, but not as good approximations as the minimal polynomials. More precisely, there could be large gaps between the height of two successive
Q_{i}
. If we could drop the irreducibility condition and directly work with the sequence of minimal polynomials, we could possibly replace the upper bound
2n-\mathcal{O}(n^{1/3})
with
2n-\mathcal{O}(n^{1/2})
in Theorem
1.2
. Section
11
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

2
Notation

Throughout this paper,
\xi
denotes a transcendental real number.

The floor (resp. ceiling) function is denoted by
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
(resp.
\lceil\cdot\rceil
). If
f,g:I\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
are two functions on a set
I
, we write
f=\mathcal{O}(g)
or
f\ll g
or
g\gg f
to mean that there is a positive constant
c
such that
f(x)\leq cg(x)
for each
x\in I
. We write
f\asymp g
when both
f\ll g
and
g\ll f
hold.

Let
K
be a field. If
\mathcal{A}
is a subset of a
K
-vector space
V
, we denote by
\left\langle\mathcal{A}\right\rangle_{K}\subseteq V
the
K
-vector space spanned by
\mathcal{A}
, with the convention that
\left\langle\emptyset\right\rangle_{K}=\{0\}
.

Given a ring
A
(typically
A=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}
) and an integer
n\geq 0
, we denote by
A[X]
the ring of polynomials in
X
with coefficients in
A
, and by
A[X]_{\leq n}\subseteq A[X]
the subgroup of polynomials of degree at most
n
. We say that
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
is
primitive
if it non-zero and the greatest common divisor of its coefficients is
1
. Given
P=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}X^{k}\in\mathbb{R}[X]
, we set

\displaystyle\|P\|=\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|a_{k}|.

Gelfond’s Lemma can be written as follows (see
e.g.
[
4
, Lemma A.3]
as well as
[
3
]
). For any non-zero polynomials
P_{1},\dots,P_{r}\in\mathbb{R}[X]
with product
P=P_{1}\cdots P_{r}
of degree at most
n
, we have

e^{-n}\|P_{1}\|\cdots\|P_{r}\|<\|P\|<e^{n}\|P_{1}\|\cdots\|P_{r}\|.

(2.1)

In particular, for each non-zero polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
and each factor
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
of
P
, we have
e^{-n}\|Q\|<\|P\|
. We will often use (
2.1
) as follows. If
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
is irreducible and if
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
is a non-zero polynomial which satisfies
\|P\|\leq e^{-n}\|Q\|
, then
Q
cannot divide
P
. They are thus coprime polynomials.

We recall the definition of the resultant, which, as explained in the introduction, is useful for estimating the exponent
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
(also see Section
4
). Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
be non-constant polynomials of degree
p
and
q
respectively, and let
a_{i},b_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
P(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{k}X^{k}
and
Q(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{q}b_{k}X^{k}
.
Their
resultant

\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)
is defined as the
(q+p)
-dimensional determinant

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)=\begin{array}[]{cc}\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{p}&0&\dots\\
a_{p-1}&a_{p}&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
a_{0}&\\
0&a_{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
&&&a_{0}\end{array}\right.&\left.\begin{array}[]{cccc}b_{q}&0&\dots\\
b_{q-1}&b_{q}&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
b_{0}&\\
0&b_{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
&&&b_{p}\end{array}\right|\\
\underbrace{\hskip 76.82234pt}_{q}&\underbrace{\hskip 76.82234pt}_{p}\end{array}.

Besides the exponents of linear approximation
\omega_{n}
and
\widehat{\omega}_{n}
, we will also need the following exponents of simultaneous rational approximation. For each positive integer
n
, the exponent
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(\xi)
(resp.
\lambda_{n}(\xi)
) is the supremum of the real numbers
\lambda\geq 0
such that the system

\displaystyle|y_{0}|\leq Y\and L(\mathbf{y})\leq Y^{-\lambda}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }L(\mathbf{y}):=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|y_{0}\xi^{k}-y_{k}|,

admits a non-zero integer solution
\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
for each sufficiently large
Y\geq 1
(resp. for arbitrarily large
Y
). Dirichlet’s theorem
[
25
, §II.1, Theorem 1A]
implies that
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(\xi)\geq 1/n
. The best upper bounds known to date for
\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(\xi)
when
n\geq 4
are established in a join work with Roy in
[
16
]
. In particular, there is an explicit positive constant
a
such that

\widehat{\lambda}_{n}(\xi)\leq\frac{1}{n/2+an^{1/2}+1/3},

and sharper results are also obtained when
n
is small.

3
Minimal polynomials

A
sequence of minimal polynomials
(associated to
n
and
\xi
) is a sequence
(P_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of non-zero polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
satisfying the following properties

(i)

the sequence
\big(\|P_{i}\|\big)_{i\geq 0}
is strictly increasing,

(ii)

the sequence
\big(|P_{i}(\xi)|\big)_{i\geq 0}
is strictly decreasing,

(iii)

if
|P(\xi)|<|P_{i}(\xi)|
for some index
i\geq 0
and a non-zero
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
, then
\|P\|\geq\|P_{i+1}\|
.

Note that if we require the dominant coefficient of
P_{i}
to be positive (and since
\xi
is transcendental), then the above sequence is uniquely determined (up to the first terms). Let
(P_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be a sequence as above. We have the classical formulas:

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)=\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\log|P_{i}(\xi)|}{\log\|P_{i+1}\|}\and\omega_{n}(\xi)=\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\log|P_{i}(\xi)|}{\log\|P_{i}\|}.

(3.1)

In particular, given a positive real number
\widehat{\omega}
with
\widehat{\omega}<\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
, then we have, for each sufficiently large index
i
,

|P_{i}(\xi)|\leq\|P_{i+1}\|^{-\widehat{\omega}}\and\|P_{i+1}\|^{\tau}\leq\|P_{i}\|,\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\tau:=\frac{\widehat{\omega}}{\omega_{n}(\xi)},

(3.2)

(with the convention
\tau=0
if
\omega_{n}(\xi)=\infty
). The second inequality in (
3.2
) justifies
the need to bound from above
\omega_{n}(\xi)
. Given a non-zero
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
, we set
\omega(P)=0
if
\|P\|=1
. Otherwise, we denote by
\omega(P)
the real number satisfying

|P(\xi)|=\|P\|^{-\omega(P)}.

With this notation, we have

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(\xi)=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|P\|\rightarrow\infty\\
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}\end{subarray}}\omega(P)=\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\omega(P_{i})\and\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}\omega(P_{i})\geq\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi).

(3.3)

The following results are well-known. We prove them for the sake of completion. The first one follows from the arguments of the proof of
[
9
, Lemma 2]
(also see
[
21
, Lemma 4.1]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
i\geq 0
and write
V_{i}=\left\langle P_{i},P_{i+1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}
. Then
\{P_{i},P_{i+1}\}
forms a
\mathbb{Z}
–basis of the lattice
V_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that
\{P_{i},P_{i+1}\}
is not a
\mathbb{Z}
–basis of
V_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
. Then there exists a non-zero
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
which may be written as
Q=rP_{i}+sP_{i+1}
, where
r,s\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfy
|r|,|s|\leq 1/2
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\|Q\|\leq|r|\|P_{i}\|+|s|\|P_{i+1}\|<\|P_{i+1}\|\and|Q(\xi)|\leq|r||P_{i}(\xi)|+|s||P_{i+1}(\xi)|<|P_{i}(\xi)|.

This contradicts the minimality property of
P_{i}
.
∎

The next result is analogous to the second part of
[
21
, Lemma 4.1]
. The construction of
S_{i}
is also somewhat similar to what Davenport and Schmidt do in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
i\geq 0
, define

S_{i}=P_{i}(\xi)P_{i+1}-P_{i+1}(\xi)P_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}.

Then

\frac{1}{2}\|S_{i}\|\leq\|P_{i+1}\||P_{i}(\xi)|\leq 2\|S_{i}\|.

Moreover, if for integers
0\leq i<j
the space spanned by
P_{i},P_{i+1},\cdots,P_{j}
has dimension
2
, then
S_{j-1}=\pm S_{i}
. In particular

\|P_{i+1}\||P_{i}(\xi)|\asymp\|P_{j}\||P_{j-1}(\xi)|.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that the quantity
\|S_{i}\|
satisfies
\|S_{i}\|\asymp\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(V_{i})
, where
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}
is defined in Section
8.2
and
V_{i}=\left\langle P_{i},P_{i+1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. We will study this function more deeply in full generality later.

Proof.

We easily get
\|S_{i}\|\leq 2\|P_{i+1}\||P_{i}(\xi)|
. Define
R_{+},R_{-}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
by

R_{\pm}=P_{i+1}\pm P_{i}.

Suppose that there exists
\varepsilon\in\{+,-\}
such that
|R_{\varepsilon}(\xi)|\leq|P_{i}(\xi)|/2
. Then by minimality of
P_{i}
, we must have
\|R_{\varepsilon}\|\geq\|P_{i+1}\|
. Since
S_{i}=P_{i}(\xi)R_{\varepsilon}-R_{\varepsilon}(\xi)P_{i}
, we find

\displaystyle\|S_{i}\|\geq|P_{i}(\xi)|\|R_{\varepsilon}\|-|R_{\varepsilon}(\xi)|\|P_{i}\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|P_{i+1}\||P_{i}(\xi)|.

Assume that
|R_{+}(\xi)|,|R_{-}(\xi)|\geq|P_{i}(\xi)|/2
. This is equivalent to

|P_{i+1}(\xi)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|P_{i}(\xi)|.

Again, this yields
\|S_{i}\|\geq|P_{i}(\xi)|\|P_{i+1}\|-|P_{i+1}(\xi)|\|P_{i}\|\geq\|P_{i+1}\||P_{i}(\xi)|/2
.

Now, let us write
V_{i}=\left\langle P_{i},\dots,P_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, with
j>i
, and suppose that
V_{i}
has dimension
2
. We need to prove that
S_{j-1}=\pm S_{i}
. If
j=i+1
it is automatic, we may therefore assume that
j\geq i+2
. By Lemma
3.1
, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
P_{i}=aP_{i+1}+bP_{i+2}
. Since
\{P_{i},P_{i+1}\}
is also a
\mathbb{Z}
–basis of
V_{i}
, we have
b=\pm 1
, and we deduce that

S_{i}=\big(aP_{i+1}(\xi)+bP_{i+2}(\xi)\big)P_{i+1}-P_{i+1}(\xi)\big(aP_{i+1}+bP_{i+2}\big)=-bS_{i+1}=\pm S_{i+1}.

By induction, we get
S_{i}=\pm S_{i+1}=\cdots=\pm S_{j-1}
.
∎

The proof of
[
9
, Lemma 3]
(which deals with the case
n=2
) yields the classical following result.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
. Then, there are infinitely many indices
i\geq 1
for which
P_{i-1}
,
P_{i}
and
P_{i+1}
are linearly independent.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that there exists
i\geq 0
such that
V=\left\langle P_{i},P_{i+1},\dots\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
has dimension
2
. By Lemma
3.2
there exists
c>0
such that for each
j>i
we have

\displaystyle 0<\|P_{i+1}\||P_{i}(\xi)|\leq c\|P_{j}\||P_{j-1}|.

This leads to a contradiction since
\|P_{j}\||P_{j-1}|\leq\|P_{j}\|^{1-\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)+o(1)}
tends to
0
as
j
tends to infinity.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

As mentioned in the introduction, it is however possible that all polynomials
P_{i}
with
i
large enough lie in a subspace of dimension
3
, see
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
.

4
Resultant and first estimates

The following useful result can be easily obtained from the proof of
[
10
, §5]
(also see of
[
3
, Lemma 1]
). We recall the arguments since they illustrate (in a simpler situation) how we will deal with generalized determinants.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p,q
be positive integers with
p,q\leq n
. There exists a constant
c>0
depending on
\xi
and
n
only, with the following property. For any polynomials
P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
of degree
p
and
q
respectively, we have

|\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)|\leq c\|P\|^{q-1}\|Q\|^{p-1}\max\big\{\|P\||Q(\xi)|,\|Q\||P(\xi)|\big\}.

Proof.

Let
a_{i},b_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
P(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{k}X^{k}
and
Q(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{q}b_{k}X^{k}
. For
i=1,\dots,p+q-1
, we add to the last row of the determinant (
2
) the
i
-th row multiplied by
\xi^{p+q-i}
. This last row now becomes

\displaystyle\Big(\xi^{q-1}P(\xi),\dots,\xi P(\xi),P(\xi),\xi^{p-1}Q(\xi),\dots,\xi Q(\xi),Q(\xi)\Big).

Using the upper bounds
|a_{i}|\leq\|P\|
and
|b_{j}|\leq\|Q\|
for the other entries of (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)|\ll\|P\|^{q-1}|P(\xi)|\|Q\|^{p}+\|P\|^{q}\|Q\|^{p-1}|Q(\xi)|,

where the implicit constant only depends on
p,q
and
\xi
.
∎

The next result, which is also based on inequalities involving resultants, will be used in Section
10
. It ensures that if
R\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
is a “good” approximation, in the sense that
R(\xi)
is very small compared to
\|R\|
, and if we write
R
as a product of coprime polynomials
B_{1}\cdots B_{k}
, then one of those factors is also a “good” approximation, while the product of the others is not.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
m,k
be positive integers. There exists a constant
c>0
depending on
m
and
\xi
only, with the following property. Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
be non constant, pairwise coprime polynomials, and suppose that
R:=B_{1}\cdots B_{k}
has degree at most
m
. Then, there exists
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
such that

|B_{j}(\xi)|\leq c\|R\|^{m-1}|R(\xi)|\and\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}|B_{i}(\xi)|\geq c^{-1}\|R\|^{-(m-1)}.

Proof.

If
k=1
this is trivial. We now suppose that
k\geq 2
and we write
d_{i}=\deg(B_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,k
. By hypothesis, we have
\deg(R)=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}\leq m
. Note that

\displaystyle|R(\xi)|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}|B_{i}(\xi)|\and\|R\|\asymp\prod_{i=1}^{k}\|B_{i}\|,

(4.1)

the second inequality coming from Gelfond’s (the implicit constants depending only on
m
). Let
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
be such that
|B_{j}(\xi)|
is minimal and fix
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
with
i\neq j
. Since
B_{i}
and
B_{j}
are coprime, their resultant
\mathrm{Res}(B_{i},B_{j})
is a non-zero integer. Using Lemma
4.1
, we find

\displaystyle 1\leq|\mathrm{Res}(B_{i},B_{j})|

\displaystyle\ll\|B_{i}\|^{d_{j}-1}\|B_{j}\|^{d_{i}-1}\big(\|B_{j}\||B_{i}(\xi)|+\|B_{i}\||B_{j}(\xi)|\big)

\displaystyle\ll\|B_{i}\|^{d_{j}}\|B_{j}\|^{d_{i}}|B_{i}(\xi)|,

the implicit constant depending only on
\xi
and
m
, hence

-\log|B_{i}(\xi)|\leq d_{j}\log\|B_{i}\|+d_{i}\log\|B_{j}\|+\mathcal{O}(1).

On the other hand, by summing the above inequalities for
i\neq j
(and since
\max\{m-d_{j},d_{j}\}\leq m-1
), and by using (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}-\log|B_{i}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq d_{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}\log\|B_{i}\|+(m-d_{j})\log\|B_{j}\|+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle\leq(m-1)\log\|R\|+\mathcal{O}(1).

We easily deduce that

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}|B_{i}(\xi)|\gg\|R\|^{-(m-1)}\and|R(\xi)|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}|B_{i}(\xi)|\gg|B_{j}(\xi)|\|R\|^{-(m-1)}.

∎

5
A sequence of irreducible polynomials

As explained in the introduction, to get the upper bound
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-1
, the strategy of Davenport and Schmidt
[
10
]
consists in considering the resultant
\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)
of two “good” polynomial approximations
P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
. To ensure that
\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)
does not vanish, they need a polynomial
P
which is irreducible (for it is then easy to find
Q
so that
P
and
Q
are coprime). The same difficulty appears in
[
8
]
. Similarly, we will not work directly with a sequence of minimal polynomials. Instead, we will considerer the largest irreducible factors of the minimal polynomials. Now, let
n,d
be integers with

2\leq d<1+\frac{n}{2}.

In this section, we assume that the transcendental real number
\xi
satisfies
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>2n-d
and we fix a real number
\widehat{\omega}
(arbitrarily close to
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
) such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>\widehat{\omega}>2n-d.

(5.1)

We denote by
(P_{i})_{i\geq 0}
a sequence of minimal polynomials associated to
n
and
\xi
. Our goal is to prove the existence of a sequence
(Q_{i})_{i\geq 0}
as below.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that (
5.1
) holds. Then, there exist a sequence
(Q_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of pairwise distinct polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
and an index
j_{0}\geq 0
with the following properties. The sequence
(\|Q_{i}\|)_{i\geq 0}
is bounded below by
2
, unbounded and non-decreasing, and for any
i\geq 0

(i)

Q_{i}
is irreducible (over
\mathbb{Z}
) and has degree at least
n-d+2
;

(ii)

Q_{i}
divides
P_{j}
for some index
j\geq j_{0}
(not necessarily unique), and for each
j\geq j_{0}
there exists
k\geq 0
such that
Q_{k}
divides
P_{j}
;

(iii)

|Q_{i}(\xi)|=\|Q_{i}\|^{-\omega(Q_{i})}\leq\|Q_{i}\|^{-\widehat{\omega}}
, and we further have

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(\xi)=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\omega(Q_{k})\and\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\omega(Q_{k})\geq\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi).

(5.2)

(iv)

if
Q_{i}
divides a minimal polynomial
P_{j}
with
j\geq j_{0}
, then

\|P_{j}\|\leq\|Q_{i}\|^{1+\theta_{i}},\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\theta_{i}=\frac{\omega(Q_{i})-2n+d}{n-2d+3};

(5.3)

(v)

we have

\|Q_{i+1}\|^{\tau}\leq\|Q_{i}\|\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\tau=\frac{\widehat{\omega}\big(\widehat{\omega}-n-d+3\big)}{\omega_{n}(\xi)\big(\omega_{n}(\xi)-n-d+3\big)},

(5.4)

with the convention
\tau=0
if
\omega_{n}(\xi)=\infty
;

The above proposition is essentially a consequence of Lemma
5.3
below. Assertion
(iii)
ensures that the polynomials
Q_{i}
are quite good approximations, and they can be used to compute the exponent of best approximation
\omega_{n}(\xi)
. Estimate (
5.4
) is the analog of the second inequality of (
3.2
) but is way more difficult to prove. The main reason behind this difficulty is that there may be many polynomials
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
with
\|Q_{i}\|<\|P\|<\|Q_{i+1}\|
and
|P(\xi)|<Q_{i}(\xi)

In order to prove Proposition
5.1
, we need the two technical lemmas below. Essentially, they will be used to prove that the factors of
P_{i}
of small degree are bad approximations. This will lead to the existence of a factor of large degree which is necessarily a rather good approximation.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that (
5.1
) holds. Then, there exists a constant
c\in(0,1)
depending only on
\xi
and
n
such that for any non-zero polynomial
R\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n-d+1}
we have

|R(\xi)|\geq c\|R\|^{-(n+\deg(R)-1)}\geq c\|R\|^{-(2n-d)}.

(5.5)

In particular (
5.5
) holds for any
R\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq d-2}
.

Proof.

If
R
is constant we have
|R(\xi)|=\|R\|
and the result is trivial. Now, suppose that
R
is irreducible and not constant. We adapt the arguments of Davenport and Schmidt
[
10
, §5–6]
. Set
H=e^{-n}\|R\|
. By definition of
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)
and
\widehat{\omega}
, if
H
is sufficiently large, there exists a non-zero
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
such that

\|P\|\leq H\and|P(\xi)|\leq H^{-\widehat{\omega}}.

By (
2.1
), the (irreducible) polynomial
R
is not a factor of
P
, they are thus coprime polynomials. Their resultant is a non-zero integer, and using Lemma
4.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\ll\|P\|^{\deg(R)-1}\|R\|^{n}|P(\xi)|+\|P\|^{\deg(R)}\|R\|^{n-1}|R(\xi)|

\displaystyle\ll H^{n+\deg(R)-1-\widehat{\omega}}+H^{n+\deg(R)-1}|R(\xi)|.

Since
\widehat{\omega}>2n-d
and
\deg(R)\leq n-d+1
, the first term tends to
0
as
H
tends to infinity. Hence
1\ll H^{n+\deg(R)-1}|R(\xi)|
, which implies (
5.5
).

If
R
is not irreducible, we write
R=\prod_{i=1}^{s}R_{i}
with integer
s\geq 1
and
R_{1},\dots,R_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
irreducible of degree
\leq\deg(R)
(possibly constant). Combining
\|R\|\asymp\prod_{i=1}^{s}\|R_{i}\|
together with (
5.5
) applied with the irreducible polynomials
R_{i}
, we find

\displaystyle|R(\xi)|=\prod_{i=1}^{s}|R_{i}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\gg\prod_{i=1}^{s}\|R_{i}\|^{-(n+\deg(R)-1)}\gg\|R\|^{-(n+\deg(R)-1)}.

Finally, the last assertion comes from the fact that
d-1\leq n+d-1
(since
d\leq 1+n/2
).
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that (
5.1
) holds. There exist
i_{0}\geq 0
and a constant
c>0
such that for each
i\geq i_{0}
the polynomial
P_{i}
has a unique irreducible factor
\widetilde{P}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
of degree
\geq n-d+2
and positive leading coefficient. It satisfies

|P_{i}(\xi)|\|P_{i}\|^{n+d-3}\geq c|\widetilde{P}_{i}(\xi)|\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|^{n+d-3},

(5.6)

moreover
\big(\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|\big)_{i\geq i_{0}}
tends to infinity and as
i
tends to infinity. For each
i
large enough we have
\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|>1
, and writing
|\widetilde{P}_{i}(\xi)|=\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|^{-\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})}
, we furthermore have

\omega_{n}(\xi)=\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})\and\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})\geq\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi).

(5.7)

Proof.

First, note that since
d<1+n/2
, if we decompose
P_{i}
as a product of irreducibles, there is at most one factor of degree
\geq n-d+2
. Fix
i\geq 0
large enough so that
\omega(P_{i})\geq\widehat{\omega}
, and write

P:=P_{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{s}R_{k}

where
R_{1},\dots,R_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
are irreducible polynomials (and
s
is a positive integer). Suppose that
\deg(R_{k})\leq n-d+1
for each
k=1,\dots,s
. Then, by Lemma
5.2
together with
\|P\|\asymp\prod_{k}\|R_{k}\|
, we find

\displaystyle\|P\|^{-\widehat{\omega}}\geq|P(\xi)|=\prod_{k=1}^{s}|R_{k}(\xi)|\gg\prod_{k=1}^{s}\|R_{k}\|^{-(2n-d)}\asymp\|P\|^{-(2n-d)}.

This is impossible if
i
is sufficiently large since
\widehat{\omega}>2n-d
. Therefore, if
i
is large enough, one of the factors
R_{k}
has degree at least
n-d+2
. Without loss of generality, we may suppose that it is
R:=R_{1}
.
Write
S:=\prod_{k=2}^{s}R_{k}
, so that
P=RS
. We have
\deg(S)\leq d-2
, and (
5.5
) of Lemma
5.2
yields

\displaystyle|S(\xi)|\gg\|S\|^{-(n+d-3)}.

Together with
\|P\|\asymp\|R\|\|S\|
, it leads to

\displaystyle|P(\xi)|=|R(\xi)||S(\xi)|\gg|R(\xi)|\|S\|^{-(n+d-3)}\asymp|R(\xi)|\|R\|^{n+d-3}\|P\|^{-(n+d-3)},

and (
5.6
) follows easily by setting
\widetilde{P}_{i}:=R
. The rest of the proof is based solely on (
5.6
) and the inequality
\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|\ll\|P_{i}\|
. Note that
|P_{i}(\xi)|\|P_{i}\|^{n+d-3}\ll\|P_{i}\|^{n+d-3-\widehat{\omega}}
tends to
0
as
i
tends to infinity (using
d<1+n/2
together with
\omega(P_{i})>2n-d
). We deduce that
|\widetilde{P}_{i}(\xi)|\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|^{n+d-3}
also tends to
0
as
i
tends to infinity, which is possible only if
\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|
tends to infinity. In particular, if
i
is large enough we must have
\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|>1
. Writing
|\widetilde{P}_{i}(\xi)|=\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|^{-\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})}
, we also have
\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})>n+d-3
. Now, using
\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|\ll\|P_{i}\|
, and taking the
\log
of the two sides of (
5.6
), we get

\displaystyle\big(\omega(P_{i})-(n+d-3)\big)\log\|P_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq\big(\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})-(n+d-3)\big)\log\|\widetilde{P}_{i}\|+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})-(n+d-3)\big)\big(\log\|P_{i}\|+\mathcal{O}(1)\big)+\mathcal{O}(1).

By dividing by
\log\|P_{i}\|
and by simplifying, we deduce that
\omega(\widetilde{P}_{i})\geq\omega(P_{i})\big(1-o(1)\big)
and (
5.7
) follows easily from (
3.3
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Let
i_{0}\geq 0

6
On the dimension of some polynomials subspaces

We start by introducing some families of vector spaces spanned by polynomials, and we study their dimensions.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
k\geq n
be an integer and let
\mathcal{A}
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}
. We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\big\{Q,XQ,\dots,X^{k-\deg(Q)}Q\,;\,Q\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{0\}\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq k},

\displaystyle V_{k}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{B}_{k}(\mathcal{A})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle g_{\mathcal{A}}(k)

\displaystyle=\dim V_{k}(\mathcal{A}).

The spaces
V_{k}(\mathcal{A})
play the role of the spaces
\mathcal{U}^{k}(\mathcal{A})
in
[
16
, Section 3]
(for simultaneous approximation). We obtain analog properties. Note that if
\mathcal{A}
contains at least one non-zero polynomial, then

V_{n}(\mathcal{A})\varsubsetneq V_{n+1}(\mathcal{A})\varsubsetneq\cdots.

(6.1)

The goal of this section is to prove the following result. We could not find a reference for the proposition below.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
k
be an integer with
0\leq k\leq n
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be a set of
k+1
linearly polynomials of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}
. Suppose that the
\gcd
of the elements of
\mathcal{A}
is
1
(in other words, the ideal spanned by
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathbb{R}[X]
). Then

V_{2n-k}(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-k}.

(6.2)

The case
k=1
is a classical result (it is implied by the fact that the resultant of two coprime polynomials is non-zero). The proof of Proposition
6.2
is given at the end of the section. Recall that a function
f:\{n,n+1,\ldots\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
concave
if for any
i>n
, it satisfies

f(i)-f(i-1)\geq f(i+1)-f(i).

The next result is a dual version of
[
16
, Proposition 3.1]
(where we deal with simultaneous approximation to the successive powers of
\xi
).

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\neq\{0\}
be a non-empty subset of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}
. The function
g_{\mathcal{A}}
is concave and (strictly) increasing on
\{n,n+1,\ldots\}
.

Proof.

The series of inclusion (
6.1
) shows that the function
g_{\mathcal{A}}
is increasing on
\{n,n+1,\dots\}
. For simplicity, we write
V_{i}=V_{i}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}(\mathcal{A})
for each
i\geq n
. Given an integer
i\geq n
we have
XV_{i}\subseteq V_{i+1}
, and we set

h(i):=\dim\big(V_{i+1}/XV_{i}\big)=g_{\mathcal{A}}(i+1)-g_{\mathcal{A}}(i).

We have to prove that
h
is decreasing on
\{n,n+1,\cdots\}
. Fix
i\geq n+1
and consider the linear map
\pi:V_{i}\rightarrow V_{i+1}/XV_{i}
defined by
\pi(P)=P+XV_{i}
. Since
\mathcal{B}_{i}\cup X\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
, we have
V_{i}+XV_{i}=V_{i+1}
. So
\pi
is surjective, and consequently
\textrm{Im }\pi=V_{i+1}/XV_{i}
is isomorphic to
V_{i}/\ker\pi
.
On the other hand,
XV_{i-1}\subseteq V_{i}\cap XV_{i}\subseteq\ker\pi
, so
XV_{i-1}
is subspace of
\ker\pi
. Hence

\displaystyle h(i-1)=\dim\big(V_{i}/XV_{i-1}\big)\geq\dim\big(V_{i}/\ker\pi\big)=\dim\big(V_{i+1}/XV_{i}\big)=h(i).

∎

This section is divided into two parts. We introduce and study a quantity

in Section

8.1
Multilinear algebra and Hodge duality

For each integer
m
, we view
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
as an Euclidean space for the usual scalar product
\left(\cdot\mid\cdot\right)
, and we denote by
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}

, we identify
\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
with
\mathbb{R}^{N}

k

\mathbb{Q}
,
i.e.
such that
\left\langle V\cap\mathbb{Q}^{m+1}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=V

inside

–basis

of
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
(see
[
26
, 26, Chapter I, Lemma 8A]
). In this paper, we need to work with a “twisted” height and the corresponding version of Schmidt’s inequality (which is obtained by following Schmidt’s original arguments).

Let

denote the canonical basis of

\displaystyle*:{\bigwedge}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{\sim}{\bigwedge}^{m+1-k}(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})

\displaystyle*(\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{e}_{i_{k}})=\varepsilon_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\mathbf{e}_{j_{1}}\,\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{e}_{j_{m+1-k}}

for any indices

and

, where

is a system of Plücker coordinates of a
k

is a system of Plücker coordinates of its orthogonal

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\lrcorner\,\mathbf{X}=*\big(\mathbf{y}\wedge(*\mathbf{X})\big)\in{\bigwedge}^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})

of
\mathbf{X}
by

(see

(8.2)

P_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge P_{k}=\alpha Q_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge Q_{k},

and so

\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(V)=\left\|\Xi_{m}\,\lrcorner\,(Q_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge Q_{k})\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\Xi_{m}\,\lrcorner\,(P_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge P_{k})\right\|_{2}.

On the other hand, by (
8.1
) combined with Hadamard’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\Xi_{m}\,\lrcorner\,(P_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge P_{k})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}P_{i}(\xi)\times P_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{P_{i}}\wedge\cdots\wedge P_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{i=1}^{k}|P_{i}(\xi)|\|P_{1}\|\cdots\widehat{\|P_{i}\|}\cdots\|P_{k}\|

(recall that the naive height
\|\cdot\|
is defined in Section
2
).
∎

9
Subfamilies of polynomials: dimension and height

Let
d,n,\xi
and
\widehat{\omega}
be as in Section
5
. In particular we have

2\leq d<1+\frac{n}{2},

and we suppose that (
5.1
) holds, namely

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>\widehat{\omega}>2n-d.

Let us fix a sequence of minimal polynomials
(P_{i})_{i\geq 0}
associated to
n
and
\xi
as in Section
3
. We denote by
(Q_{i})_{i\geq 0}
the sequence of irreducible factors given by Proposition
5.1
. In particular, for each
i\geq 0
we have

|Q_{i}(\xi)|\leq\|Q_{i}\|^{-\widehat{\omega}},

(9.1)

as well as

\displaystyle\|Q_{i+1}\|^{\tau}\leq\|Q_{i}\|,\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\tau=\frac{\widehat{\omega}\big(\widehat{\omega}-n-d+3\big)}{\omega_{n}(\xi)\big(\omega_{n}(\xi)-n-d+3\big)}\in[0,1).

(9.2)

Under the hypothesis that
d
is not too large, we will prove in the next section that
\omega_{n}(\xi)<\infty
, and thus
\tau>0
.
Here, we investigate the following question: can we find “large” subfamilies of
(Q_{i})_{i\geq 0}
which are linearly independent and whose elements have “comparable” height? More precisely, given two indices
k<i
, can we find an exponent
\theta_{j}\in(0,1)
which depends only on
d,n
and the dimension
j+1
of the subspace
\left\langle Q_{k},Q_{k+1},\dots,Q_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
(and not on the indices
i
and
k
), such that
\|Q_{i}\|^{\theta_{j}}\ll\|Q_{k}\|
? We can view it as a generalization of (
9.2
). With this goal in mind, let us introduce some notation.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
m_{n}=m_{n}(\xi)\in[2,n+1]
be the integer

m_{n}:=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\dim(\left\langle Q_{i},Q_{i+1},\dots\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}).

Remark.
Note that we might have
m_{n}<n+1
, since, unlike the dual setting of simultaneous approximation (see
[
16
, Eq. (5.3)]
), it is possible that the sequence
(P_{i})_{i\geq j}
is contained in a proper subspace of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}
, see
e.g.
[
14
]
. However, we will show later that under the hypothesis
d\asymp n^{1/3}
, we have
m_{n}\gg n^{1/3}
. The next definition is a dual version of
[
16
, Definition 5.2]
. Even though it is not important for our purpose, it is interesting to note that in
[
16
, Definition 5.2]
, the spaces
A_{j}[i]
are generated by the points
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i+1},\dots
coming
after
the good approximation
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, whereas in the present setting we need to consider the points
Q_{i},Q_{i-1},\dots
coming
before

Q_{i}
. It does not seem to work well the other way round.

Definition 9.2
.

Let
j_{1}>j_{0}\geq 0
be such that

\dim\left\langle Q_{j_{0}},Q_{j_{0}+1},\dots,Q_{j_{1}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\dim\left\langle Q_{j_{0}},Q_{j_{0}+1},\dots\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=m_{n}.

For each
i\geq j_{1}
and
j=0,\dots,m_{n}-2
, we define

\sigma_{j}(i)=k,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{j}[i]=\left\langle Q_{k},Q_{k+1},\dots,Q_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\and Y_{j}(i)=\|Q_{k-1}\|,

where
k\in\{j_{0}+1,\dots,i\}
is the smallest index such that
\dim\left\langle Q_{k},\dots,Q_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=j+1
.

Proposition
6.2
implies that

V_{2n-j}\big(A_{j}[i]\big)=\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}\hskip 20.00003pt(j=1,\dots,m_{n}-2).

(9.3)

Definition 9.3
.

Let
\tau\in(0,1)
. We associate to
\tau
a sequence
(\tau_{j})_{0\leq j\leq n/2}
by setting
\tau_{0}=\tau
, and for
j=1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor

\displaystyle\tau_{j}=\alpha_{j}\bigg(\tau_{j-1}-\frac{2j-1}{2n-d}\bigg),\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\alpha_{j}=\frac{(2n-d)\tau^{2}}{(n-2j)\tau+n-j+1}.

The main result of this section is the following. The second part of the proposition, which we will use later to get a lower bound for
m_{n}
, will be proved thanks to Corollary
9.7
below.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
\tau\in(0,1)
and let
(\tau_{j})_{0\leq j\leq n/2}
be as in Definition
9.3
. Suppose that

\|Q_{i+1}\|^{\tau}\leq\|Q_{i}\|\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{for each sufficiently large $i$}.

(9.4)

Then for each large enough
i
, we also have

\displaystyle\|Q_{i}\|^{\tau_{j}}\ll Y_{j}(i)\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{for }j=0,\dots,\min\Big\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,m_{n}-2\Big\},

(9.5)

with implicit constants which do not depend on
i
and
j
.

Remark.
We will use the exponent
\tau
given in (
9.2
). We will prove that under suitable conditions, the exponent of best approximation
\omega_{n}(\xi)
is not “too large”, which ensures that
\tau
is “close” to
1
. This question, which is one of the delicate parts of this paper, will be dealt with in Section
10
.

In order to get (
9.5
), we will try to adopt a strategy similar to the one of
[
16
, §5]
in the setting of simultaneous approximation to the successive powers of
\xi
. New difficulties arise however, for example we need to work with
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}
instead of the standard height of subspaces (see Section
8.1
). Schmidt’s inequality (
8.6
) will play a key-role in our proofs. We keep the notation of Definition
6.1
for the sets
\mathcal{B}_{k}(\mathcal{A})
and the subspaces
V_{k}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq k}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may suppose that the index
j_{0}
is large enough for us to have (
9.4
) for each
i\geq j_{0}-1
. Let us fix
i\geq j_{1}
, and for simplicity write
m=m_{n}
and
Y_{k}:=Y_{k}(i)
for
k=0,\dots,m-2
.

We prove (
9.5
) by induction on
j
. If
j=0
, then we have
Y_{0}=\|Q_{i-1}\|
since
\sigma_{0}(i)=i
. By (
9.4
) applied with
i-1
instead of
i
, we get
\|Q_{i}\|^{\tau_{0}}\leq Y_{0}
. Now, let
j\in\{1,\dots,m-2\}
with
j\leq n/2
such that (
9.5
) holds for
j-1
. If
\tau_{j}\leq 0
, then (
9.5
) holds trivially for
j
. We assume that
\tau_{j}>0
. In particular, we also have
\tau_{j-1}>0
. Write
P:=Q_{\sigma_{j}(i)}
and
Q:=Q_{\sigma_{j}(i)+1}
. By (
9.4
), we have

\displaystyle\|Q\|^{\tau^{2}}\leq\|P\|^{\tau}\leq Y_{j}.

(9.6)

Since
P
and
Q
are coprime, Lemma
6.4
implies that
\dim V_{2n-j}(P,Q)\geq 2(n-j+1)
. Therefore, there exists a family of
2n-3j+1
linearly independent polynomials

\mathcal{U}_{j}:=\{U_{0},\dots,U_{2n-3j}\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2n-j}(P,Q)

such that
A_{j}[i]\cap\left\langle\mathcal{U}_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\{0\}
. Note that since
j\leq n/2
, we may choose
\mathcal{U}_{j}
such that it contains at least
n-2j
polynomials whose height is equal to
\|P\|
. The remaining
n-j+1
ones have height
\leq\|Q\|
. By (
9.3
), we have
V_{2n-j}\big(A_{j}[i]\big)=\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}
. Therefore, there exists

\mathcal{V}_{j}:=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{j-1}\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2n-j}(Q_{\sigma_{j}(i)},\dots,Q_{i})

(with the convention
\mathcal{V}_{j}=\emptyset
if
j=1
) such that we have the direct sum

A_{j}[i]\oplus\left\langle\mathcal{U}_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\oplus\left\langle\mathcal{V}_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}.

All the polynomials of
\mathcal{V}_{j}
have height at most
\|Q_{i}\|
. Let
k\in\{\sigma_{j}(i),\dots,i\}
which maximizes
|Q_{k}(\xi)|/\|Q_{k}\|
and define

A:=A_{j}[i]\and B:=\left\langle\mathcal{U}_{j}\cup\mathcal{V}_{j}\cup\{Q_{k}\}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},

so that
A+B=\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}
and
A\cap B=\left\langle Q_{k}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. We will now make a crucial use of the function
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}
introduced in Definition
8.1
(here, the ambient space is
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}
, identified to
\mathbb{R}^{2n-j+1}
via (
8.9
)). Recall that

\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A+B)=\mathcal{D}_{\xi}\big(\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}\big)=\left\|(1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{2n-j})\right\|_{2}\asymp 1,

and that according to (
8.10
) the primitive polynomial
Q_{k}
satisfies

\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A\cap B)=\mathcal{D}_{\xi}\big(\left\langle Q_{k}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\big)=|Q_{k}(\xi)|.

Schmidt’s inequality (
8.6
) applied with the subspaces
A
and
B
yields

\displaystyle|Q_{k}(\xi)|\asymp\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A+B)\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A\cap B)\leq\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A)\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(B),

(9.7)

the implicit constants depending only on
n
and
\xi
(and not on the indices
i,j
). It remains to estimate
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A)
and
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(B)
. The subspace
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}
is generated by the
2n-2j+1
linearly independent polynomials
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}_{j}\cup\mathcal{V}_{j}\cup\{Q_{k}\}
. Moreover (see the remarks after the constructions of
\mathcal{U}_{j}
and
\mathcal{V}_{j}
), we have

\prod_{R\in\mathcal{V}}\|R\|\leq\|P\|^{n-2j}\|Q\|^{n-j+1}\|Q_{i}\|^{j-1}\|Q_{k}\|.

By choice of
k
, for each
R\in\mathcal{V}
we also have
|R(\xi)|/\|R\|\ll|Q_{k}(\xi)|/\|Q_{k}\|
, and Lemma
8.3
combined with the above yields the upper bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(B)\ll|Q_{k}(\xi)|\|P\|^{n-2j}\|Q\|^{n-j+1}\|Q_{i}\|^{j-1}.

The space
A_{j}[i]\subseteq\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq 2n-j}
is spanned by a set
\mathcal{U}
of
j+1
linearly polynomials that may be chosen among
Q_{\sigma_{j-1}(i)-1}
,…,
Q_{i-1}
,
Q_{i}
. For each
R\in\mathcal{U}
, we have
\|R\|\leq\|Q_{i}\|
and
|R(\xi)|\leq\|R\|^{-\widehat{\omega}}\leq Y_{j-1}^{-\widehat{\omega}}
. Combined with Lemma
8.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A)\ll\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}|R(\xi)|\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{U}\\
S\neq R\end{subarray}}\|S\|\ll Y_{j-1}^{-\widehat{\omega}}\|Q_{i}\|^{j}.

Then, combining the above upper bounds for
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(B)
and
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A)
with (
9.7
) and (
9.6
), we get

\displaystyle Y_{j-1}^{\widehat{\omega}}\ll\|P\|^{n-2j}\|Q\|^{n-j+1}\|Q_{i}\|^{2j-1}

\displaystyle\ll Y_{j}^{(n-2j)/\tau+(n-j+1)/\tau^{2}}\|Q_{i}\|^{2j-1},

where the implicit constants depend on
n
and
\xi
only. Using the induction hypothesis, we also have
\|Q_{i}\|^{\widehat{\omega}\,\tau_{j-1}}\ll Y_{j-1}^{\widehat{\omega}}
, hence

\displaystyle\|Q_{i}\|^{\widehat{\omega}\,\tau_{j-1}-2j+1}\ll Y_{j}^{(n-2j)/\tau+(n-j+1)/\tau^{2}}=Y_{j}^{(2n-d)/\alpha_{j}}.

Rising each term to the power
\alpha_{j}/(2n-d)
and using
\widehat{\omega}>2n-d
we easily deduce (
9.5
) for
j
, which concludes our induction step.
∎

Remark 9.5
.

We could get an exponent
\tau_{j}
a little bit greater in the above proposition (by giving a slightly better estimate of
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(A)
is the proof). However, those improvements would only lead to a larger constant
a
in Theorem
1.2
at best; the term
n^{1/3}
would remain the same, whereas we are expecting
n^{1/2}
. We preferred to keep the arguments simple.

Proposition 9.6
.

Let the hypotheses be as in Proposition
9.4
and write
m=m_{n}
. For any
\lambda<\lambda_{n}(\xi)
, there are infinitely many indices
i
such that

Y_{m-2}(i)\leq\|Q_{i}\|^{1/(\widehat{\omega}\lambda\tau)}.

In particular, there are infinitely many indices
i
such that

Y_{m-2}(i)\leq\|Q_{i}\|^{\mu},\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\mu:=\frac{n}{(2n-d)\tau}.

(9.8)

Proof.

By definition of
m
, the subspace

V=\left\langle Q_{\sigma_{m-2}(i)-1},Q_{\sigma_{m-2}(i)},\dots,Q_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

(9.9)

of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}
, does not depend on
i
, for each
i\geq j_{1}
, where
j_{1}
comes from Definition
9.2
. It has dimension
m
since
\dim A_{m-2}[i]=m-1
and
Q_{\sigma_{m-2}(i)-1}\notin A_{m-2}[i]
. Fix two positive real numbers
\alpha,\lambda
with
\lambda<\alpha<\lambda_{n}(\xi)
, and suppose by contradiction that there exists an index
i_{0}\geq j_{1}
such that for each
i\geq i_{0}

Y_{m-2}(i)\geq\|Q_{i}\|^{\theta},\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\theta=\frac{1}{\widehat{\omega}\lambda\tau}.

(9.10)

By hypothesis, we can also assume that
\|Q_{i+1}\|^{\tau}\leq\|Q_{i}\|
for each
i\geq i_{0}
. Identifying
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
via the isomorphism (
8.9
), we claim that the point
\Xi=(1,\xi,\xi^{2},\dots,\xi^{n})
is orthogonal to
V
, with respect to the standard scalar product
\left(\cdot\mid\cdot\right)
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

By definition of
\lambda_{n}(\xi)
there exist infinitely many non-zero
\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
satisfying

L(\mathbf{y})=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|y_{0}\xi^{k}-y_{k}|\leq Y^{-\alpha},\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }Y=\|\mathbf{y}\|=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|y_{k}|.

Let
(\mathbf{y}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be an unbounded sequence of such points ordered by increasing norm. This sequence converges projectively to
\Xi=(1,\xi,\xi^{2},\dots,\xi^{n})
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
(\|\mathbf{y}_{0}\|)^{\alpha}>2(n+1)\|Q_{i_{0}}\|
. Fix an index
j
arbitrarily large. For simplicity, set
\mathbf{y}:=\mathbf{y}_{j}
and
Y=\|\mathbf{y}_{j}\|
. There exists an index
i\geq i_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\|Q_{i}\|<\frac{Y^{\alpha}}{2(n+1)}\leq\|Q_{i+1}\|\leq\|Q_{i}\|^{1/\tau}.

(9.11)

Note that
i
tends to infinity as
j
tends to infinity. Let
k\in\{\sigma_{m-2}(i)-1,\dots,i\}
. The polynomial
Q:=Q_{k}
is identified with an integer point
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
such that
Q(\xi)=\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\Xi\right)
. Since
\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y}\right)=\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y}-y_{0}\Xi\right)+y_{0}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\Xi\right)
, we get

\displaystyle|\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y}\right)|\leq(n+1)\|Q\|L(\mathbf{y})+Y|Q(\xi)|

(this argument is similar to the one used by Laurent in the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 5]
). Our hypothesis (
9.10
) yields

\displaystyle\|Q_{i}\|^{\theta}\leq Y_{m-2}(i)\leq\|Q\|\leq\|Q_{i}\|.

Using (
9.11
) together with
L(\mathbf{y})\leq Y^{-\alpha}
, we get

\displaystyle(n+1)\|Q\|L(\mathbf{y})<\frac{1}{2}.

Moreover, (
9.11
) also yields
Y^{1/\widehat{\omega}}\ll\|Q_{i}\|^{1/(\widehat{\omega}\alpha\tau)}=\|Q_{i}\|^{\theta\lambda/\alpha}
, where the implicit constant only depends on
n
. Since
\lambda<\alpha
, we may choose
j
so large that
(2Y)^{1/\widehat{\omega}}<\|Q_{i}\|^{\theta}
. Combining this with the estimate
|Q(\xi)|\leq\|Q\|^{-\widehat{\omega}}
from (
9.1
), we also get

Y|Q(\xi)|\leq Y\|Q\|^{-\widehat{\omega}}\leq Y\|Q_{i}\|^{-\theta\,\widehat{\omega}}<\frac{1}{2}.

We conclude that the integer
|\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{y}\right)|
is (strictly) less that
1
. It is thus equal to
0
, and so
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
are orthogonal. By letting
\mathbf{z}
vary, this implies that
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{j}
is orthogonal to the subspace
V
. Since this is true for all sufficiently large
j
, it follows that the (projective) limit
\Xi
is also orthogonal to
V
. This proves our claim and provides the required contradiction since no
Q_{i}
vanishes at the transcendental number
\xi
. Thus, (
9.10
) does not hold for arbitrarily large indices
i
. Estimate (
9.8
) follows by noticing that
\lambda_{n}(\xi)\geq 1/n
by Dirichlet’s theorem, and
\widehat{\omega}>2n-d
. We may therefore choose
\lambda<\lambda_{n}(\xi)
so that
\lambda\widehat{\omega}>(2n-d)/n
.
∎

Corollary 9.7
.

Under the same hypotheses, suppose moreover that
m=m_{n}
satisfies
m-2\leq n/2
, and let
(\tau_{j})_{0\leq j\leq n/2}
be as in Definition

. Then, we have

\tau_{m-2}\leq\mu=\frac{n}{(2n-d)\tau}.

By Proposition

combined with Proposition

there are infinitely many indices

for which

. Since
\|Q_{i}\|
tends to with

, we deduce that

.
∎

10
Upper bound on the exponent of best approximation

Our goal is the following result, which we will prove at the end of this section.

Proposition 10.1
.

Suppose that
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>2n-d
, with an integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\displaystyle 2\leq d\leq\sqrt[3]{n/4}
. Then, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n+P(n,d),\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }P(n,d)=\frac{n(4d^{2}-d-5)+8d^{2}-2d-15}{2n-8d^{2}+2d+15}.

If moreover we have
d\leq\Big\lceil\sqrt[3]{n/16}\Big\rceil
and
n>16
, then

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n+2d^{2}.

Let
d,n,\xi
and
\widehat{\omega}
be as in Sections
5
and
9
. We suppose thus that
2\leq d<1+n/2
and that (
5.1
) holds, namely

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>\widehat{\omega}>2n-d.

Fix a sequence of minimal polynomials
(P_{i})_{i\geq 0}
associated to
n
and
\xi
as in Section
3
. We denote by
(Q_{i})_{i\geq 0}
the sequence of irreducible factors given by Proposition
5.1
. Unless otherwise stated, all the constants implicit in the symbols
\ll
,
\gg,\asymp
and
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
only depend on
n
,
d
,
\xi
and
\widehat{\omega}
.

Remark 10.2
.

According to Proposition
5.1
, we have
\omega_{n}(\xi)=\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\omega(Q_{i})
. Also by (
5.3
), we have

\|P_{j}\|\leq\|Q_{i}\|^{1+\theta_{i}},\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\theta_{i}=\frac{\omega(Q_{i})-2n+d}{n-2d+3},

(10.1)

for each
i\geq 0
and each
j
such that
Q_{i}
divides
P_{j}
. Proposition
10.1
implies that if
d^{3}
is small compared to
n
, then
\theta_{i}=\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/n)
is also small, and
Q_{i}
has “almost” the same height as
P_{j}
.

In order to bound from above
\omega_{n}(\xi)
, it suffices to do so for
\omega(Q_{i})
. We could try to use (
10.1
), which implies that any minimal polynomial of height greater than
\|Q_{i}\|^{1+\theta_{i}}
is not divisible by
Q_{i}
. They are thus coprime and we may consider their (non-zero) resultant. However we cannot conclude, as
\theta_{i}
is too large. To solve this problem, we need several lemmas. We first start by a few simple observations. A quick computation yields

(1+\theta_{i})(2n-d)=\omega(Q_{i})+(n+d-3)\theta_{i}.

(10.2)

More generally, for each
\eta\geq 0
,
we have

\big[1+\theta_{i}(1-\eta)\big](2n-d)=\omega(Q_{i})+\big(n+d-3-\eta(2n-d)\big)\theta_{i}.

(10.3)

Under the condition
\eta<(n+d-3)/(2n-d)
, which holds as soon as
\eta<1/2
, it implies that for each
i\geq 0
, we have

|Q_{i}(\xi)|=\|Q_{i}\|^{-\omega(Q_{i})}>\|Q_{i}\|^{-\big(1+\theta_{i}(1-\eta)\big)(2n-d)}.

(10.4)

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
i\geq 0
and
\eta\in[0,1/2)
, and suppose that
R\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq d-2}
is a non-zero polynomial such that
P:=Q_{i}R
has degree at most
n
, and
P
is solution of

\|P\|\leq H:=\|Q_{i}\|^{1+\theta_{i}(1-\eta)}\and|P(\xi)|\leq H^{-2n+d}.

(10.5)

Define

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}=\frac{(2n-d)\eta}{n+d-3}\and\eta^{\prime\prime}=\frac{(2n-2d+3)\eta+d-3}{n+d-3}.

Then, we have the following properties.

(i)

The polynomial
R
is non-constant. We have
d\geq 3
and

\displaystyle\|R\|^{-(n+d-3)}\ll|R(\xi)|\leq\|Q\|^{-(n+d-3)(1-\eta^{\prime})\theta_{i}}.

(10.6)

(ii)

There exist a non-constant irreducible polynomial
A\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq n}
and an integer
e\in[1,d-2]
such that
A^{e}

Proof of Proposition
10.1
.

The condition

implies that

. Set
\eta=1/(2d+5/2)
. Note that the upper bound

is not optimal in Proposition
10.1
(and could be slightly improved by choosing the parameter

closer to

). Suppose that

and define

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}=\frac{(2n-2d+3)\eta+d-3}{n+d-3}\and\nu=\frac{1}{d+1}.

A direct computation yields

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}-\nu=\frac{-n+4d^{3}-11d^{2}-13d+6}{(4d+5)(n+d-3)(d+1)}<0,

so that
\eta^{\prime\prime}<\nu\leq 1/3
. Since

(1-2\eta^{\prime\prime})/(1-\eta^{\prime\prime})\geq(1-2\nu)/(1-\nu)

\displaystyle\frac{1-2\nu}{1-\nu}-\Big(1-\frac{1}{d-2}+\frac{2d}{n}\Big)=\frac{2(n-d^{3}+2d^{2})}{nd(d-2)}\geq 0.

The conditions of Lemma
10.4
are therefore fulfilled for

(they are automatic for d=2). Consequently, for each large enough

there exists a non-zero polynomial

coprime with

Such a polynomial is non-constant, and Lemma
4.1
yields

\displaystyle 1\leq|\mathrm{Res}(Q_{i},P)|

\displaystyle\ll\|Q_{i}\|^{n-1}\|P\|^{n}|Q_{i}(\xi)|+\|Q_{i}\|^{n}\|P\|^{n-1}|P(\xi)|

\displaystyle\ll\|Q_{i}\|^{n-1+n(1+\theta(1-\eta))-\omega(Q_{i})}.

As
\|Q_{i}\|
tends to infinity, it follows that

\displaystyle n-1+n(1+\theta(1-\eta))-\omega(Q_{i})\geq\mathcal{O}\Big(1/\log\|Q_{i}\|\Big).

Using the definition (
10.1
) of

, a direct computation leads us to the estimate

\displaystyle(n\eta-2d+3)\omega(Q_{i})\leq 2\eta n^{2}-(3d+\eta d-5)n+2d-3+\mathcal{O}\Big(1/\log\|Q_{i}\|\Big).

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\eta n^{2}-(3d+\eta d-5)n+2d-3}{n\eta-2d+3}

\displaystyle\;=2n+\frac{n(d-1-\eta d)+2d-3}{n\eta-2d+3}=2n+P(n,d),

is defined as in the statement of Proposition

(and

. A direct computation yields

\displaystyle Q(n,d)=-n(d+5)+16d^{4}-4d^{3}-22d^{2}-2d-15.

If
\displaystyle d\leq\sqrt[3]{n/16}
we have

, and therefore

. We obtain

, and consequently
\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n+2d^{2}

and

, where

we still have

, or equivalently if

and

, we find

\displaystyle=-16\rho^{3}(\rho+t+5)+16\big(\rho^{4}+4t\rho^{3}+6t^{2}\rho^{2}+4t^{3}\rho+t^{4}\big)

\displaystyle=16\rho^{3}(3t-5)+16\big(6t^{2}\rho^{2}+4t^{3}\rho+t^{4}\big)\leq 16R(\rho),

Proof of the main theorem

In this last section we prove our main Theorem
1.2
in the following stronger form.

Theorem 11.1
.

Let
\varepsilon=0.3748\cdots
be the unique (positive) solution of the equation
(1+x)e^{x}=2
and set
a=\big(2\varepsilon(2-e^{\varepsilon})/9\big)^{1/3}=0.3567\cdots
. There exists an explicit constant
C>0
such that, for each

and any transcendental real number

, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-an^{1/3}+C.

Since
1/3<a
, it implies Theorem
1.2
. We first establish a preliminary result which uses the following notation. Let
n,d
be integers with
2\leq d\leq\sqrt{n/4}
. In particular
d\leq 1+n/2
. We define

\omega(d,n):=2n+P(n,d),\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }P(n,d)=\frac{n(4d^{2}-d-5)+8d^{2}-2d-15}{2n-8d^{2}+2d+15},

as well as

\tau(d,n)=\frac{(2n-d)(n-2d+3)}{\omega(d,n)\big(\omega(d,n)-n-d+3\big)}\and\mu(d,n):=\frac{n}{(2n-d)\tau}.

Let
\big(\tau_{i}(d,n)\big)_{0\leq i\leq n/2}
be the sequence associated to

as in Definition
9.3
.

.

Let

be non-negative integers with

and

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(d,n)>\mu(d,n)\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{for $k=0,\dots,j$}.

Then for any transcendental real number

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-\min\big\{d,d_{j}\big\},\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }d_{j}=2j-1-\frac{j-1}{\tau_{j}(d,n)}.

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>2n-d

such that

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)>\widehat{\omega}>2n-d.

Let
(P_{i})_{i\geq 0}

and
\xi

. We denote by

the sequence of irreducible factors given by Proposition

\|Q_{i+1}\|^{\tau(d,n)}\leq\|Q_{i}\|.

For simplicity, we write

and
\tau_{k}=\tau_{k}(d,n)

with

. We claim that
j<m-2
. By contradiction, otherwise we have

. Then Corollary
9.7
yields

11.1
). Hence our claim. Let

. Set

\|P\|\leq e^{-n}\|Q\|=:X\and|P(\xi)|\leq X^{-\widehat{\omega}}.

) the (irreducible) polynomial

\alpha_{j}\leq 1
and
\tau_{j}(d,n)\geq 0
. By definition, we have

\tau_{k-1}(d,n)=\alpha_{k}^{-1}\tau_{k}(d,n)+\frac{2k-1}{2n-d}\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{(for $k=1,\dots,j$).}

Since
\alpha_{k}^{-1}\geq\alpha_{j}^{-1}\geq 1
, this yields
\tau_{k-1}(d,n)>\tau_{k}(d,n)
. This proves the first assertion of our lemma. The second one follows easily.
∎

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
n,d,j
be non-negative integers with
2\leq d\leq\sqrt{n/4}
and
1\leq j\leq n/2
. Define

\alpha=\alpha(d,n):=\frac{(2n-d)\tau(d,n)^{2}}{(n-2)\tau(d,n)+n},

and suppose that

\displaystyle\alpha^{j}>\frac{j(2j-1)\tau(d,n)}{(n-2)\tau(d,n)+n}=\frac{j(2j-1)\alpha}{(2n-d)\tau(d,n)}.

(11.5)

Then,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and for
k=0,\dots,j
, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(d,n)\geq\alpha^{j}\tau(d,n)-\frac{j(2j-1)\tau(d,n)^{2}}{(n-2)\tau(d,n)+n}>0.

Proof.

We have
\alpha\in(0,1)
since
\tau(d,n)<1
and
d\geq 2
. For simplicity, we write
\tau=\tau(d,n)
. Let
(\sigma_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence defined by
\sigma_{0}=\tau
, and

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}=\alpha\bigg(\sigma_{k-1}-\frac{2k-1}{2n-d}\bigg)\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{for }k\geq 1.

Using (
11.5
), we find

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\alpha^{j}}=\frac{\sigma_{j-1}}{\alpha^{j-1}}-\frac{2j-1}{(2n-d)\alpha^{j-1}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}-\frac{1}{2n-d}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{2k-1}{\alpha^{k-1}}\geq\tau-\frac{j(2j-1)}{(2n-d)\alpha^{j-1}}>0.

(11.6)

In particular
\sigma_{j}\geq 0
. Since
\sigma_{k-1}\geq\alpha^{-1}\sigma_{k}
, by induction, we get
\sigma_{j}<\sigma_{j-1}<\cdots<\sigma_{0}
. Moreover,
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{k}
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq k\leq n/2
, where
\alpha_{k}
is as in Definition
9.3
. Another quick induction yields
\sigma_{k}\leq\tau_{k}
for
k=0,\dots,j
. We conclude by combining
\sigma_{j}\leq\sigma_{k}\leq\tau_{k}
with (
11.6
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
11.1
.

Define a function
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
f(x)=x(2-e^{x})
. Let
\varepsilon=0.3748\cdots
be the unique solution of the equation
(1+x)e^{x}=2
. It is the abscissa of the maximum of
f
. Set

\displaystyle a=\sqrt[3]{\frac{2\varepsilon(2-e^{\varepsilon})}{9}}=0.3567\cdots

Let
n\geq 1
and define
d=d(n)
and
j(n)
by

\displaystyle d(n)=\lceil an^{1/3}\rceil\and j:=j(n):=\bigg\lceil\frac{2\varepsilon n}{9d^{2}}\bigg\rceil.

We suppose
n\geq 30
so that
2\leq d\leq 1+n/2
and
1\leq j\leq n/2
. Since
d^{4}/n^{2}\asymp d/n=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2/3})
, we find
\omega(d,n)=2n+2d^{2}+\mathcal{O}(d)
, and then

\displaystyle\tau(d,n)=1-\frac{3d^{2}}{n}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{2/3}}\bigg)\and\alpha(d,n)=1-\frac{9d^{2}}{2n}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{2/3}}\bigg),

(where
\alpha(d,n)
is defined in Lemma
11.2
). In particular, by choice of
j
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha(d,n)^{j}=\exp\big(j\log(\alpha(d,n))\big)=\exp\bigg(-\frac{9jd^{2}}{2n}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}\bigg)\bigg)=e^{-\varepsilon}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}\bigg).

(11.7)

Since

\frac{j(2j-1)\tau(d,n)}{(n-2)\tau(d,n)+n}=\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}\bigg),

there exists
N_{1}\geq 30
such that condition (
11.5
) of Lemma
11.2
is fulfilled for each
n\geq N_{1}
. Thus, for
k=0,\dots,j
, we have

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(d,n)\geq\alpha(d,n)^{j}\tau(d,n)-\frac{j(2j-1)\tau(d,n)^{2}}{2n-2}=e^{-\varepsilon}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}\bigg).

In particular
d_{j}=2j-1-(j-1)/\tau_{j}(d,n)
satisfies

\displaystyle d_{j}\geq j\big(2-e^{\varepsilon}\big)+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\varepsilon(2-e^{\varepsilon})n}{9d^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(1)=\frac{a^{3}n}{d^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(1)=d+\mathcal{O}(1).

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\mu(d,n)=\frac{n}{(2n-d)\tau}=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}\bigg).

Since
e^{-\varepsilon}>1/2
, by (
11.7
) there exists
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
such that condition (
11.1
) of Theorem
11.2
is fulfilled for each
n\geq N_{2}
. We conclude that for any
n\geq N_{2}
and any transcendental real number
\xi
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{n}(\xi)\leq 2n-\min\{d,d_{i}\}=2n-d+\mathcal{O}(1).

∎

Appendix A
Appendix: Twisted heights

The purpose of this appendix is to give another interpretation of the quantity
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(V)
defined in Section
8.2
. Our first approach was actually to work with the heights
\mathcal{H}_{T}
defined below. We are thankful to Damien Roy for pointed out the link with Hodge’s duality.

Fix
A\in\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})
and let
V
be a
k
–dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Its (twisted) height
\mathcal{H}_{A}(V)
is defined as
the covolume of the lattice
A(V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m+1})
inside the subspace
AV
(with the convention that
\mathcal{H}_{A}(V)=1
if
V=\{0\}
). Explicitly, we have

\mathcal{H}_{A}(V):=\left\|A\mathbf{x}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge A\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\|_{2},

(A.1)

where
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{k})
is any
\mathbb{Z}
–basis of the lattice
V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m+1}
. Then Schmidt’s inequality generalizes as follows

\mathcal{H}_{A}(U+V)\mathcal{H}_{A}(U\cap V)\leq\mathcal{H}_{A}(U)\mathcal{H}_{A}(V)

(A.2)

for any subspaces
U,V
of
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. The proof is the same as for rational subspaces (see
[
26
, 26, Chapter I, Lemma 8A]
and
[
13
, §5]
). Similarly to Marnat and Moshchevitin
[
13
, §5]
, we consider twisted heights of the following form. Let
T>1
be a parameter. We define the matrix
A_{m,T}\in\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})
as

A_{m,T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}T^{m}&0&\dots&0\\
0&T^{-1}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
0&&&T^{-1}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&\xi&\cdots&&\xi^{m}\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&&&\\
0&\cdots&&0&1\end{array}\right),

so that for each polynomial
P=a_{0}+\cdots+a_{m}X^{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq m}
(identified to a point of
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
via (
8.9
)), we have

A_{m,T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{0}\\
\vdots\\
a_{m}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}T^{m}P(\xi)\\
T^{-1}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
T^{-1}a_{m}\end{array}\right).

(A.3)

We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{m,T}
(or simply
\mathcal{H}_{T}
if there is no ambiguity about the integer
m
) the twisted height
\mathcal{H}_{A}
associated to the matrix
A=A_{m,T}
. Note that

\mathcal{H}_{T}\big(\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq m}\big)=\mathcal{H}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})=\det(A)=1.

Definition A.1
.

Let
V
be a subspace of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq m}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. We set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(V)=\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}T^{-\mathrm{codim}(V)}\mathcal{H}_{m,T}(V),

where
\mathrm{codim}(V)=m+1-\dim(V)
denotes the codimension of the space
V
inside
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq m}
. In particular,
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq n})=1
, and for any primitive polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]_{\leq m}
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}\big(\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\big)=|P(\xi)|=\mathcal{D}_{\xi}\big(\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\big).

Our goal is now to prove that for any non-zero subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq m}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(V)\asymp\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(V),

where
\mathcal{D}_{\xi}
is as in Definition
8.1
(and the implicit constant depends on
m
and
\xi
only). First, note that since
\dim(U+V)+\dim(U\cap V)=\dim U+\dim V
for any subspaces
U,V
of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq m}
, we deduce from (
A.2
) (with
A=A_{m,T}
) the following version of Schmidt’s inequality, which is the analog of Proposition
8.2

\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(U+V)\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(U\cap V)\leq\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(U)\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(V),

(A.4)

valid for any
U,V
of
\mathbb{R}[X]_{\leq m}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
V
be a
k
–dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, with
1\leq k\leq m+1
, and set
\Xi_{m}=(1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{m})
. We have

\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(V)\ll\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(V)\leq\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(V),

(A.5)

where the implicit constant depends on
\xi
and
m
only. Moreover, for any
\mathbb{Z}
–basis
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{k})
of
V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(V)=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}\left(\Xi_{m}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{+}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{x}_{k}^{+}\right\|_{2},

(A.6)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
denotes the point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
deprived of its first coordinate.

Before to prove this result, we introduce some notation that we will need in the proof. Given two positive integers
p
and
q
, we define
\mathcal{I}(p,q)
as the set of
p
–tuples
(i_{1},\dots,i_{p})
of integers with
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}\leq q
. Let
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{q})
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
. For any
I\in\mathcal{I}(p,q)
as above, set
\mathbf{e}_{I}=\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{e}_{i_{p}}\in\bigwedge^{p}\mathbb{R}^{q}
. For any
\mathbf{X}\in\bigwedge^{p}\mathbb{R}^{q}
, we call
I
–coordinate of
\mathbf{X}
its
\mathbf{e}_{I}
–coordinate in the basis
(\mathbf{e}_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{I}(p,q)}
. For any
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, we denote by
M(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p})
the
q\times p
matrix whose columns are
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p}
written in the basis
\mathbf{e}
, and by
\mathfrak{D}_{I}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p})
the minor formed by the rows of
M(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p})
of index
i
in
I
. Then, writing
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{x}_{k}
, we have the classical formula

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(p,q)}\mathfrak{D}_{I}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p})\mathbf{e}_{I}\and\left\|\mathbf{X}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(p,q)}\mathfrak{D}_{I}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p})^{2}.

(A.7)

Therefore, for each
I\in\mathcal{I}(p,q)
, the
I
–coordinate of
\mathbf{X}
is
\mathfrak{D}_{I}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{p})
.

Proof of Proposition
A.2
.

Fix
T\geq 1
and for
i=1,\dots,k
set

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\mathbf{x}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{x}_{k},\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }p_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}\left(\Xi_{m}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}T^{-m+k-1}\mathbf{y}_{1}(T)\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{y}_{k}(T),\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where }\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i}(T)=A_{m,T}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}.

By (
8.4
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\xi}(V)=\left\|\mathbf{Z}\right\|_{2}\and\mathcal{D}_{\xi}^{\prime}(V)=\left\|\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{2}.

We prove the following properties. For
i=1,\dots,k
we set
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(\left(\Xi_{m}\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}
.

(i)

For each
J=(1,j_{2},\dots,j_{k})\in\mathcal{I}(k,m+2)
, the
J
–coordinate of
\mathbf{z}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{z}_{k}
is equal to the
K
–coordinate of
\mathbf{Z}
, where
K=(j_{2}-1,\dots,j_{k}-1)\in\mathcal{I}(k-1,m+1)
.

Fix
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{I}(k,m+1)
.

(ii)

If
i_{1}\geq 2
, then the
I
–coordinate of
\mathbf{Y}
is equal to
0
.

(iii)

If
i_{1}=1
, then the
I
–coordinate of
\mathbf{Y}
is equal to the
J
–coordinate of
\mathbf{z}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{z}_{k}
, where
J=(1,i_{2}+1,\dots,i_{k}+1)
. It is also equal to the
K
–coordinate of
\mathbf{Z}
, where
K=(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})
.

To prove the first assertion, it suffices to expand the determinant
\mathfrak{D}_{J}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k})
along its first row. Let
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{I}(k,m+1)
. Suppose first that
i_{1}\neq 1
. Then, by Hadamard’s inequality, the
I
–coordinate of
\mathbf{y}_{1}(T)\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{y}_{k}(T)
satisfies

|D_{I}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k})|\ll\prod_{j=1}^{k}T^{-1}\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|=\mathcal{O}(T^{-k}),

and we deduce that the
I
–coordinate of
\mathbf{Y}
is equal to
0
, which proves assertion
(ii)
. Suppose now that
i_{1}=1
and set
J=(1,i_{2}+1,\dots,i_{k}+1)
. Then

\displaystyle D_{I}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k})=T^{m+1-k}\mathfrak{D}_{J}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k}),

hence the first part of
(iii)
. The second part is obtained by combining the above with assertion
(i)
.

We deduce from the last two assertions that all the non-zero coordinates of
\mathbf{Y}
are coordinates of
\mathbf{Z}
, thus
\left\|\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\mathbf{Z}\right\|_{2}
, which proves the second inequality in (
A.5
). For the first estimate, we need to estimate the
K
–coordinates of
\mathbf{Z}
with
K\in\mathcal{I}(k-1,m+1)
of the form
(1,i_{2},\dots,i_{k-1})
. According to assertion
(i)
, they are exactly the determinants
\mathfrak{D}_{J}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k})
with
J=(1,2,j_{3},\dots,j_{k})
in
\mathcal{I}(k,m+2)
.

Fix a
J\in\mathcal{I}(k,m+2)
as above. The second row of the matrix
M(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k})
is a linear combination of the remaining rows (with coefficients in absolute value between
1
and
|\xi|^{m}
). We deduce that
\mathfrak{D}_{J}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k})
can be written as a linear combination of
\mathfrak{D}_{J^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k})
, where
J^{\prime}
belong to the subset of
\mathcal{I}(k,m+2)
consisting in the
k
–tuples whose second element is
\geq 3
. By assertion
(iii)
, they are all coordinates of
\mathbf{Y}
, hence
|\mathfrak{D}_{J}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k})|\ll\left\|\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{2}
. We conclude that
\left\|\mathbf{Z}\right\|_{2}\ll\left\|\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{2}
.

Finally, fix
(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{I}(k-1,m)
and set
K=(i_{2}+1,\dots,i_{k}+1)
. By definition of
\mathbf{Z}
, the
K
–coordinate of
\mathbf{Z}
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\mathfrak{D}_{I}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{\mathbf{x}_{i}},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\mathfrak{D}_{J}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{+},\cdots,\widehat{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{k}^{+}).

By assertion
(iii)
, this is also the
(1,K)
–coordinate of
\mathbf{Y}
. So, the set of non-zero coordinates of
\mathbf{Y}
is exactly equal to the set of non-zero coordinates of the point

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\,\mathbf{x}_{1}^{+}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{x}_{k}^{+}.

Eq. (
A.6
) follows from the second identity of (
A.7
).
∎
