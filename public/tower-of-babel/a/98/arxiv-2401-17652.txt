Keldysh Theory of Thermal Transport in Multiband Hamiltonians Preprint: APS/123-QED
Luqman Saleem, Udo Schwingenschlögl, Aurélien Manchon

Abstract
We establish a comprehensive theoretical framework for systems subjected to a static uniform temperature gradient, employing the non-equilibrium Keldysh-Dyson formalism. This framework interprets the statistical force due to the temperature gradient as a mechanical force, utilizing both Luttinger’s scalar and Moreno-Coleman-Tatara’s vector potentials, which collectively emulate the gauge invariance stemming from the conservation of energy. Our approach has the ability to treat heat current and heat magnetization on an equal footing, thereby extending and generalizing previous formalisms. The derived result for the thermal conductivity is applied to investigate the thermal characteristics of Weyl magnons in a stacked honeycomb ferromagnet featuring a trivial insulator phase, a magnon Chern insulator phase, and three Weyl magnon phases. Against the expectation from the Berry curvature, the magnon Chern insulator phase exhibits the highest transverse thermal conductivity.

The Baym-Kadanoff

Its extension to temperature gradient perturbations will enable the calculation of both heat current and heat magnetization in both fermionic and bosonic systems. A Green’s function formalism is also readily adaptable to multiband Hamiltonians and effects related to the Berry curvature of multiband Bloch states. This will enable a more accurate description of the thermal transport in interacting and disordered systems, and pave the way for the design and optimization of magnonic devices.

This study presents a theoretical framework to calculate linear and nonlinear responses to a steady and uniform temperature gradient. The temperature gradient is described by both Luttinger’s scalar and thermal vector potentials, which together result in thermal electric and magnetic fields. Just as the charge current is proportional to the applied electric field, the heat current is proportional to the thermal electric field; and the heat magnetization is related to the thermal magnetic field. The response function is then computed within the Keldysh-Dyson formalism in the stationary case.

Secondly, the heat currents can be expressed in terms of Fermi or Bose distribution functions, enabling the separation of intrinsic and extrinsic contributions to the thermal transport coefficients. Thirdly, the formalism is readily applicable to multiband systems, providing a versatile tool for investigating thermal transport in magnets with multiple magnetic sublattices as well as in magnetic heterostructures. Fourthly, the formalism can be extended to other transport quantities (e.g., particle transport, nonequilibrium densities) and time-dependent non-uniform problems.

II
Heat Transport and Thermal Vector Potential

The thermal vector potential
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is related to Luttinger’s scalar potential
\phi(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and the temperature gradient as
Tatara 2015a

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})=\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\phi(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})=\frac{\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}T}{T},

(1)

with
\partial_{t}\equiv\partial/\partial t
. Unlike the electromagnetic theory, where the magnetic vector potential possesses U(1) gauge symmetry,
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
does not possess gauge symmetry in the formal sense because the energy conservation arises from the global translation symmetry with respect to time. However, it can be shown that it couples to the energy in minimal form, i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{p}}-\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}\epsilon_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{p}}
is the canonical momentum and
\epsilon_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}
is the energy in momentum space
Tatara 2015a
. In real space-time operator form this relation becomes
\bm{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{p}}-i\hbar\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}\partial_{t}
. The minimal form can be attributed to a “gauge invariance” resulting from the energy conservation law. In fact, one can attribute a part of the temperature gradient to Luttinger’s scalar potential and the other part to the thermal vector potential as

\frac{\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}T}{T}=\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\phi(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})+\partial_{t}\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}}).

(2)

Consequently, similar to the electromagnetic theory, we obtain gauge invariance under the transformations
\phi\to\phi+d\chi/dt
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}\to\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}-\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\chi
, where
\chi
is a scalar field
Tatara 2015a
. To elevate the thermal theory to the level of the electromagnetic theory, we define a thermal electric field

\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})\equiv-\partial_{t}\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})-\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\phi(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})

(3)

and a thermal magnetic field

\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})\equiv\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\times\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}}).

(4)

\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
gives rise to the mechanical force due to the temperature gradient. The heat current density can be defined as quantum expectation value

\bm{\mathbf{J}}_{Q}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})=\pm i\text{Tr}\big(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{J}}}_{Q}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})\hat{G}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T}}^{<}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})\big).

(5)

Here and in the following, the upper sign is for Bosons and the lower sign is for Fermions.
\hat{\bm{\mathbf{J}}}_{Q}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is the heat current density operator defined as product of the velocity operator and time derivative
Shitade 2014
and
\hat{G}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T}}^{<}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is the lesser component of the Green’s function (proportional to
\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T}(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
). The hat sign indicates the matrix of operators in the internal degrees of freedom. Moreover, a thermodynamical definition can be constructed for the heat magnetization
\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{Q}
by noticing that the thermal vector potential couples to the heat current density
Zhang et al. 2020
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{Q}=-\left(\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{T}}\right)_{\mu,T}.

(6)

\Omega=E-TS-\mu N
is the free energy with
E
,
S
,
\mu
, and
N
denoting the internal energy, entropy, chemical potential, and particle number, respectively.

III
Keldysh-Dyson formalism for the thermal vector potential

We start by defining a general unperturbed Hamiltonian for Bosons or Fermions as

\displaystyle{\hat{H}}_{0}=\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\left({\hat{h}}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{p}})+\hat{V}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\right)\Psi(\bm{\mathbf{r}}),

(7)

where
{\hat{h}}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{p}})={\left(-i\hbar\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\right)}^{2}/2m
is the non-interacting kinetic part,
\hat{V}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is a general one-particle potential, and
\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
\Psi(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
are the creation and annihilation field operators, respectively.

Moving forward, we adopt a four-vector notation: Time and space coordinates are represented as
x\equiv x^{\mu}=(t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
and
x_{\mu}=(-t,\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, derivatives are represented as
{\partial}_{x^{\mu}}=({\partial}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}})
and
{\partial}_{x_{\mu}}=(-{\partial}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}})
, and potentials are represented as
A^{\mu}_{T}(x)=(\phi(x),{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}_{T}(x))
and
{{(A_{T})}_{\mu}}(x)=(-\phi(x),{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}_{T}(x))
. We proceed by applying a temperature gradient via the thermal electromagnetic tensor

F^{\mu\nu}_{T}(x)={\partial}_{x_{\mu}}A^{\nu}_{T}(x)-{\partial}_{x_{\nu}}A^{\mu}_{T}(x).

(8)

A “gauge-invariant” Hamiltonian that incorporates both Luttinger’s scalar and thermal vector potentials is formulated as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=

\displaystyle\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\left(\hat{h}_{0}(\bm{\mathbf{p}}-i\hbar\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}\partial_{t})+\hat{V}(\bm{\mathbf{r}})\right)\Psi(x)

\displaystyle+i\hbar\int d\bm{\mathbf{r}}\phi(x)\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\partial_{t}\Psi(x).

(9)

The second term is Luttinger’s Hamiltonian
Luttinger 1964
. While
\hat{H}(t)
does not include interactions such as the particle-particle interaction, the following formalism still applies even when these interactions are present. However, a self-energy correction according to the Feynman rule is necessary.

III.1
Green’s functions

The non-equilibrium Green’s functions are defined on the Keldysh contour

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})=-i\big\langle T_{C}{\left(\Psi(x_{1})\Psi^{\dagger}(x_{2})\right)}\big\rangle.

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})\left(-i\hbar D_{t_{2}}-{\hat{h}}_{0}(i\hbar\bm{\mathbf{D}}_{x_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle-\int dx_{3}\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{3})\Sigma(x_{3},x_{2})=\delta(x_{1}-x_{2}),

(11b)

(12)

In real-time representation both the Green’s function
\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{G}^{R}(x_{1},x_{2})&2\hat{G}^{<}(x_{1},x_{2})\\
0&\hat{G}^{A}(x_{1},x_{2})\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\Sigma(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{\Sigma}^{R}(x_{1},x_{2})&2\hat{\Sigma}^{<}(x_{1},x_{2})\\
0&\hat{\Sigma}^{A}(x_{1},x_{2})\end{array}\right).

The lesser, advanced, and retarded Green’s functions are defined as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hat{G}^{<}(x_{1},x_{2})&=\mp i\left\langle{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x_{2}){\Psi}(x_{1})\right\rangle,\\
\hat{G}^{A}(x_{1},x_{2})&=+i\theta(t_{2}-t_{1})\left\langle{\left[{\Psi}(x_{1}),{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x_{2})\right]}_{\mp}\right\rangle,\\
\hat{G}^{R}(x_{1},x_{2})&=-i\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\left\langle{\left[{\Psi}(x_{1}),{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x_{2})\right]}_{\mp}\right\rangle.\end{aligned}

(14)

The lesser component is directly connected to the density matrix and can be used to calculate expectation values of operators.

In a compact form, the Dyson equations (
11a
) and (
11b
) read

\displaystyle\left(\left((\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{-1}-\Sigma\right)\star\mathcal{G}\right)(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\delta(x_{1}-x_{2}),

(15a)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{G}\star\left((\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{-1}-\Sigma\right)\right)(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\delta(x_{1}-x_{2})

(15b)

with the convolution star product defined as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}\star\mathcal{B})(x_{1},x_{2})=\int dx_{3}\mathcal{A}(x_{1},x_{3})\mathcal{B}(x_{3},x_{2})

(16)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{-1}(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(i\hbar D_{t_{1}}-{\hat{h}}_{0}(-i\hbar\bm{\mathbf{D}}_{x_{1}})\right)\delta(x_{1}-x_{2}),\\
=\left(-i\hbar D_{t_{2}}-{\hat{h}}_{0}(i\hbar\bm{\mathbf{D}}_{x_{2}})\right)\delta(x_{1}-x_{2}).\end{aligned}

(17)

coordinates into the relative microscopic

Expansion of the Dyson equations

The thermal conductivity tensor is defined as

is the contribution from heat current density,

Shitade 2014

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{J}}_{Q}

\displaystyle=\pm i\hbar\text{Tr}\int\frac{d\pi^{0}}{2\pi}\pi^{0}\bm{\mathbf{\hat{v}}}\left(\hat{G}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T},\text{I}}^{<}f_{\mp}+\hat{G}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T},\text{II}}^{<}f^{\prime}_{\mp}\right)\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T}.

Its contribution to

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{i\hbar}{T}\text{Tr}\int\frac{d\pi^{0}}{2\pi}\pi^{0}\hat{v}_{i}\left(\hat{G}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T},\text{I}}^{<}f_{\mp}+\hat{G}_{\bm{\mathbf{E}}_{T},\text{II}}^{<}f^{\prime}_{\mp}\right)_{j},

, and

Owerre 2018b

Su and Wang 2017

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle,n}\bm{\mathbf{S}}_{i,n}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{S}}_{j,n}+\sum_{\llangle i,j\rrangle}\bm{\mathbf{D}}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathbf{S}}_{i,n}\times\bm{\mathbf{S}}_{j,n}\right)

(50)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,n}K_{i}\left(S_{i,n}^{z}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i,\langle n,m\rangle}J_{i}\bm{\mathbf{S}}_{i,n}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{S}}_{i,m},

\langle\cdots\rangle

(with
\nu_{ij}

S_{i,n}^{-}=&S_{i,n}^{x}-iS_{i,n}^{y}=\sqrt{2S_{i,n}}\gamma_{i,n}^{\dagger}\;\sqrt{1-{\frac{\gamma_{i,n}^{\dagger}\gamma_{i,n}}{2S_{i,n}}}}\;,\\
S_{i,n}^{z}=&(S_{i,n}-\gamma_{i,n}^{\dagger}\gamma_{i,n}).\end{aligned}

VI
Summary and Discussion

We presented a theoretical framework for investigating the response of heat currents to a temperature gradient using the Keldysh-Dyson formalism. The core of this approach is an expansion of the Dyson equations through the Moyal product within the Wigner representation. The temperature gradient is described using both Luttinger’s scalar potential (
\phi
) and Moreno-Coleman-Tatara’s vector potential (
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}
). While the thermal field does not arise from a formal gauge symmetry, we can achieve “gauge invariance” under the transformations
\phi\to\phi+\frac{d\chi}{dt}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}\to\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}-\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\chi
by assigning a portion of the temperature gradient to
\phi
and the remainder to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{T}
. This establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the responses induced by an electromagnetic field and by a temperature gradient.

We derived the thermal conductivity tensor encompassing contributions from the heat current and heat magnetization, applicable to general multiband Hamiltonians. Our formalism treats the heat current and heat magnetization on equal footing, mirroring the usage of the method of Ref.
Onoda et al. 2006
for calculating the electric current density and subsequently calculating the orbital magnetization
Zhu et al. 2012
. Our result for clean limit reduces to the well established expression
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011a
;
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011b
;
Murakami 2011
;
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011c
;
Matsumoto et al. 2014
;
Murakami and Okamoto 2017
;
Shitade 2014
;
Qin et al. 2011
.

The introduced approach has several advantages over previous formalisms. Firstly, it readily accounts for diamagnetic currents by replacing the canonical momentum with the kinetic momentum. Secondly, it can be applied to interacting and/or disordered systems. Thirdly, its ability to address non-linear responses extends beyond the scope of formalisms based on the Kubo formula. Fourthly, it can be extended to other transport quantities (e.g., particle transport, nonequilibrium densities) and time-dependent non-uniform problems.

Using the derived formula for the thermal conductivity in the clean limit, the thermal properties of a stacked honeycomb ferromagnet were analyzed for all its phases (trivial insulator, magnon Chern insulator, and three WM phases with WM nodes at different points in the momentum space). Decomposing the three-dimensional Hamiltonian into two-dimensional Hamiltonians indexed by
k_{z}
, we observed that the Chern number remains constant for all values of
k_{z}
for the trivial and magnon Chern insulator phases, while it changes abruptly from 0 to 1 as
k_{z}
passes through WM nodes. Accordingly, in the trivial and magnon Chern insulator phases the
xy
-component of the two-dimensional thermal conductivity exhibits only minor variations as a function of
k_{z}
, while it changes significantly as
k_{z}
passes through WM nodes in the WM phases. The primary contributions to the three-dimensional thermal conductivity in the WM phases are due to the WM nodes (monopoles of the Berry curvature). While one might expect the absolute value of the
xy
-component of the three-dimensional thermal conductivity to be higher in the WM phases than in the other two phases, interestingly, the magnon Chern insulator exhibits the highest absolute values, which can be understood by noting that in the WM phases the Berry curvature changes sign as
k_{z}
passes through WM nodes.
