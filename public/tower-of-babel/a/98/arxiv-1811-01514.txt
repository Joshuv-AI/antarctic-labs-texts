Complex Variables for Fractional-Order Systems
Emmanuel A. Gonzalez

Abstract
This paper discusses and summarizes some results on complex variables that are very useful in fractional-order systems analysis and design, specifically when the system is analyzed in the frequency domain. The author hopes that this document will serve as a handy reference when performing computations with complex variables, especially when working within the Laplace or Fourier domains. The reader can refer to Table I for the summary of these formulas.



1
Basics

A complex variable

s=\sigma+j\omega

has two parts: the real part represented by the variable
\sigma

and an imaginary part represented by the variable
\omega
. The real
part of
z
is denoted by
\mathrm{Re}\left(s\right)
such that

\mathrm{Re}\left(s\right)=\sigma
, while the imaginary part of
s

is denoted by
\mathrm{Im}\left(s\right)
such that
\mathrm{Im}\left(s\right)=\omega
.

Complex variables can also be added and multiplied. Consider two complex
variables
s_{1}=\sigma_{1}+j\omega_{1}
and
s_{2}=\sigma_{2}+j\omega_{2}
.
The following complex variable properties hold:

s_{1}+s_{2}=\left(\sigma_{1}+j\omega_{1}\right)+\left(\sigma_{2}+j\omega_{2}\right)=\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\right)+j\left(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}\right),

and

s_{1}s_{2}=\left(\sigma_{1}+j\omega_{1}\right)\left(\sigma_{2}+j\omega_{2}\right)=\left(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}-\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\right)+j\left(\sigma_{1}\omega_{2}+\sigma_{2}\omega_{1}\right).

A complex variable
s
can be geometrically considered as a vector
with a length (also known as
magnitude
or
modulus
) and
direction (also known as
argument
or
angle
) in either
radians or degrees. The magnitude of a complex variable
s
is obtained
using the formula

\left|s\right|=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2}},

while the argument of the complex variable
s
is obtained using
the formula

\arg s=\tan^{-1}\frac{\omega}{\sigma}

in radians for
\omega,\sigma>0
.

The magnitude of the product of two complex numbers
s_{1}
and
s_{2}

is the product of their magnitudes, i.e.
\left|s_{1}s_{2}\right|=\left|s_{1}\right|\left|s_{2}\right|
.
The argument of the product of two complex numbers is the sum of their
arguments, i.e.
\arg s_{1}s_{2}=\arg s_{1}+\arg s_{2}
.

2
Roots of Complex Variables

One of the most-important formula by de Moivre’s is

\sqrt[n]{s}=\sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\varphi+2k\pi}{n}\right)+j\sin\left(\frac{\varphi+2k\pi}{n}\right)\right],

(2.1)

for
0\leq k\leq n,
and where
r=\left|s\right|=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2}}

and
\varphi=\arg s=\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/\sigma\right)
in radians
are the magnitude and arguments of
s
, respectively. This property
is very important especially when
n>1
because this allow us to
evaluate the complex variable with a fractional exponent, i.e.
s^{1/n}
,
which is predominant in fractional-order systems.

An example of a fractional-order transfer function is

Z\left(s\right)=\frac{V\left(s\right)}{I\left(s\right)}=\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}C}=\frac{10,000}{s^{1/2}},

which is an example of a fractional-order capacitor with pseudo-capacitance

C=1/10,000=100

\mu
F/
\sqrt{\mathrm{sec}}
. The reader may refer
to
[
1
]
on discussions about capacitor theory.

3
Results

3.1
Preliminary Case:
s^{\alpha}=\left(\sigma+j\omega\right)^{\alpha}

where
0<\alpha<1
and
\sigma,\omega>0

Since
0<\alpha<1
, it is assumed that the exponent of the complex
variable
s
is a fraction. For simplicity, let’s assign
n=1/a

so we can easily use de Moivre’s formula in (
2.1
).

The magnitude of
s=\sigma+j\omega
is
r=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2}}

while its argument is
\varphi=\arg\left(s\right)=\arg\left(\sigma+j\omega\right)=\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/\alpha\right)
.

Through de Moivre’s formula, the following equation is obtained

s^{\alpha}=s^{1/n}=\left(\sigma+j\omega\right)^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\varphi+2k\pi}{n}\right)+j\sin\left(\frac{\varphi+2k\pi}{n}\right)\right],

for
0\leq k\leq n
. Using the values of
r
and
\varphi
in the
equation results in

\begin{array}[]{ll}\sqrt[n]{\sigma+j\omega}&=\sqrt[n]{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2}}}\left[\cos\frac{\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/\alpha\right)+2k\pi}{n}+j\sin\frac{\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/\alpha\right)+2k\pi}{n}\right]\\
&=\left(\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)^{1/2n}\left[\cos\frac{\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/\alpha\right)+2k\pi}{n}+j\sin\frac{\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/\alpha\right)+2k\pi}{n}\right],\end{array}

and since
n=1/a
, the equation can be rearragned as

\left(\sigma+j\omega\right)^{\alpha}=\left(\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)^{\alpha/2}\left[\cos\alpha\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{\omega}{\sigma}+2k\pi\right)+j\sin\alpha\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{\omega}{\sigma}+2k\pi\right)\right],

(3.1)

for
0\leq k\leq n
.

By assuming
k=0
, Equation (
3.1
) can be rewritten
as

\left(\sigma+j\omega\right)^{\alpha}=\left(\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)^{\alpha/2}\left[\cos\alpha\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{\omega}{\sigma}\right)+j\sin\alpha\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{\omega}{\sigma}\right)\right]

(3.2)

Although this result may not be usually used for systems analysis
because we only normally use the imaginary part of the complex variable
in both the Laplace and Fourier transforms, this result is still beneficial
to easily derive the results in the next cases.

3.2
Case 1:
s^{\alpha}=\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}
where
0<\alpha<1

and
\omega>0

This is a case where the real part is zero,
\sigma=0
. By using
the previous result in Equation (
3.2
),
and letting
\sigma=0
, we can obtain another relationship.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(0+j\omega\right)^{\alpha}&=\left(0^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)^{\alpha/2}\left[\cos\alpha\left(\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/0\right)\right)+j\sin\alpha\left(\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/0\right)\right)\right]\\
&=\omega^{\alpha}\left[\cos\alpha\left(\pi/2\right)+j\sin\alpha\left(\pi/2\right)\right].\end{array}

The expression can then be rewritten as

\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}=\omega^{\alpha}\left[\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}+j\sin\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right].

(3.3)

It is straightforward to get the magnitude of this expression. Since
the magnitude can be expressed as a product of their individual magnitudes,
we can write this expression to get the magnitude as

\left|\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}\right|=\left|\omega^{\alpha}\right|\left|\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}+j\sin\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right|.

The left multiplicand is a pure real variable therefore
\left|\omega^{\alpha}\right|=\omega^{\alpha}
.
The right multiplicand is a complex variable therefore we use Pythagorean
theorem to get its magnitude which is
\sqrt{\cos^{2}\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)+\sin^{2}\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)}
.
However, a well-known property in trigonometry states that
\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1
,
therefore the entire square root term becomes unity. The magnitude
of Equation (
3.3
) then simply becomes

\left|\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}\right|=\omega^{\alpha}.

(3.4)

To get the argument of
\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}
, we need to
know that the argument of the product of two multiplicands is the
sum of their arguments. We can now write this as

\arg\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}=\arg\omega^{\alpha}+\arg\left(\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}+j\sin\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)

in radians. The argument of
\omega^{\alpha}
which we can rewrite
as
\omega^{\alpha}=\omega^{\alpha}+j0
is
\tan^{-1}\left(0/\omega^{\alpha}\right)=0
.
The argument of
\cos\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)+j\sin\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)

is

\tan^{-1}\frac{\sin\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)}{\cos\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)}

which can also be represented as

\tan^{-1}\tan\frac{\alpha\pi}{2},

or simply

\arg\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}

(3.5)

in radians.

3.3
Case 2:
as^{\alpha}+b=a\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}+b
where
0<\alpha<1
,

\omega>0
, and
a,b>0

This case is usually found in fractional-order transfer functions.
The expression can be obtained by adding
b>0
and multiplying
a>0

in
\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}
in Equation (
3.3
).
The resulting expression becomes

a\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}+b=a\omega^{\alpha}\left[\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}+j\sin\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right]+b.

It is necessarily to have these terms rearranged to cluster the real
and imaginary components separately

a\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}+b=\left(b+a\omega^{\alpha}\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)+ja\omega^{\alpha}\sin\frac{\alpha\pi}{2},

(3.6)

because such arrangement is beneficial when getting the magnitude
and argument of Equation (
3.6
).

The magnitude response of Equation (
3.6
)
is obtained as

\left|a\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}+b\right|=\sqrt{b^{2}+a^{2}\omega^{2\alpha}+2ab\omega^{2}\cos\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}},

(3.7)

while the argument is obtained as

\arg\left(a\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}+b\right)=\tan^{-1}\frac{a\omega^{\alpha}\sin\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)}{b+a\omega^{\alpha}\cos\left(\alpha\pi/2\right)}.

(3.8)

4
Summary

We now summarize the results in Table I for the reader’s convenience.

Case I is a simple case where the magnitude and argument of the term

\left(j\omega\right)^{\alpha}
is easily determined through straightfoward
trigonometry. Case II, on the other hand, is a case mostly found in
many fractional calculus applications especially in engineering. Having

a=1
and
b=0
for Case II make it equivalent to Case I.
