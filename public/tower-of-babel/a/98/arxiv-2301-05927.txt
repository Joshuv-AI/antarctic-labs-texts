Aspects of Supergroup Gauge Theory
Taro Kimura 木村 太郎

Abstract
We provide a survey of recent studies of supergroup gauge theory. We first discuss supermatrix model as a zero-dimensional toy model of supergroup gauge theory and its geometric and algebraic characterization. We then focus on four-dimensional Yang–Mills theory with supergroup gauge symmetry and explore its non-perturbative properties, including instanton calculus, Seiberg–Witten geometry, Bethe/gauge correspondence, and its realization with intersecting defects.

The main object that we discuss in this article is

Regarding the particle statistics, Fierz and Pauli formulated a connection with the particle spins, which is nowadays known as the

Integer-spin particles are bosons, while odd-half-integer–spin particles are fermions.

This theorem is obtained under the following conditions:

•

Lorentz covariance and relativistic causality

•

Positive energies and positive norms in the Hilbert space

Therefore, for example, this theorem is not applied to the ghost particle appearing in the gauge fixing process, which is a spin-0 fermionic particle.
The fundamental degrees of freedom of supergroup gauge theory are spin-1 boson and fermion, and thus it is not compatible with the spin-statistics theorem.
In fact, the spectrum of supergroup gauge theory is not bounded and there appear negative energy states.

In spite of unphysical natures, as discussed in this article, we can discuss various non-perturbative aspects of supergroup gauge theory as a natural extension of the ordinary gauge theory, and it indicates a chance of non-perturbative completion.

Organization of the article

A purpose of this article is to provide a self-contained overview of supergroup gauge theory.
For this purpose, we start in Sec.
2
with basic notions of supermathematics, including the introduction of Grassmann algebras, supervector space, superalgebra, supermatrix, and Lie supergroup, which could be skipped by experienced readers on this subject.

In Sec.
3
, we discuss the supermatrix model, which can be viewed as a zero-dimensional supergroup gauge theory.
In fact, the supermatrix model plays a role of a toy model in the study of supergroup gauge theory, which exhibits various similar properties to higher-dimensional theory discussed in the latter part of this article.
After introducing the eigenvalue integral form of the partition function, in particular we study the asymptotic behavior based on the Coulomb gas method.
We then study the operator formalism that we call the free field realization of the (super)matrix model, and explore the underlying infinite dimensional algebraic structure.

In Sec.
4
, we introduce the supergroup gauge theory and discuss its basic properties.
We discuss realizations of supergroup theory from non-supergroup theory through analytic continuation to unphysical regime, and we show the construction from string/M-theory perspective providing the Seiberg–Witten geometry for supergroup gauge theory.

In Sec.
5
, we explore instantons in supergroup gauge theory, which plays an important role in the study of non-perturbative aspects.
After considering the ADHM construction of instantons, a systematic approach to construct the instanton solutions, for supergroup theory, we study the instanton moduli space and apply the equivariant localization formalism to derive the instanton partition function.
We obtain the instanton partition function in the three-fold way, the equivariant index formula, the contour integral formula, and the combinatorial formula, for supergroup gauge theory.

In Sec.
6
, we explore non-perturbative aspects of supergroup gauge theory based on the instanton partition function obtained in advance.
We demonstrate that the instanton partition function is also obtained in the framework of topological string involving both positive and negative branes.
For this purpose, we introduce the negative brane analog of the topological vertex that we call the anti-veretx to compute the supergroup partition function.
We then study the non-perturbative Schwinger–Dyson equation associated with the instanton partition function, which gives rise to doubly quantum Seiberg–Witten geometry, and discuss the free field realization associated with the underlying algebraic structure.
In this case, we show that the instanton partition function obeys a
q
-analog of the Virasoro constraint.
We also explore a connection with the quantum integrable system, called the Bethe/gauge correspondence, for supergroup gauge theory, and discuss its implications on the quantum integrable system side.
We then discuss realizations of supergroup gauge theory in physical setups.
We in particular study the codimension-two surface defect operators and show that the supergroup structure emerges from intersecting defects.

2.1
Grassmann algebras

The starting point of supermathematics is the Grassmann algebra, which involves anti-commuting variables (Grassmann variables),

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\theta_{j}=-\theta_{j}\theta_{i}\,.

(2.1)

This anti-commutativity immediately leads to nilpotency of the Grassmann variables,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{2}=0\,.

(2.2)

In general, a product of even number of Grassmann variables is commutative (Grassmann even), while a product of odd number of Grassmann variables is anti-commutative (Grassmann odd).
We remark that the Grassmann even variable is commutative, but still nilpotent: For example, we have
(\theta_{1}\theta_{2})\theta_{3}=\theta_{3}(\theta_{1}\theta_{2})
, while
(\theta_{1}\theta_{2})^{2}=0
.

In the definition of the complex conjugation for the Grassmann variables, we need a modification compared with the ordinary variables:
Applying the conjugation operator twice, we have an extra sign factor,

\displaystyle\bar{\bar{\theta}}=-\theta\,.

(2.3)

From this definition, the norm of the Grassmann variable becomes self-conjugate,
3
3

3

Another convention is also applied in the literature:
No sign factor for the double conjugation
\bar{\bar{\theta}}=\theta
, while the conjugation of the product is given by
\overline{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}\cdots\theta_{n}}=\bar{\theta}_{n}\cdots\bar{\theta}_{2}\bar{\theta}_{1}
.
In this convention, we still have the relation
\overline{\bar{\theta}\theta}=\bar{\theta}\theta
.

\displaystyle\overline{\bar{\theta}\theta}=-\theta\bar{\theta}=\bar{\theta}\theta\,,

(2.4)

and the Hermitian scalar product behaves as

\displaystyle\overline{\bar{\theta}_{1}\theta_{2}}=-\theta_{1}\bar{\theta}_{2}=\bar{\theta}_{2}\theta_{1}\,.

(2.5)

Derivative and integral

The derivative and the integral for the Grassmann variable are defined as follows,

\displaystyle\derivative{\theta_i}{\theta_j}=\delta_{ij}\,,\qquad\int\differential{\theta}_{i}\theta_{j}=\delta_{ij}\,,\qquad\int\differential{\theta}_{i}1=0\,.

(2.6)

Hence, these operations are essentially equivalent.
Since the Grassmann variables are anti-commutative, we have to be careful of the ordering of the derivative and the integral operation.
Under the linear transformation
\theta_{i}\to\theta^{\prime}_{i}=(A\theta)_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}\theta_{j}
, the integral measure behaves as follows

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}\differential{\theta}^{\prime}_{i}=\int\prod_{i}\differential{\theta}_{i}(\det A)^{-1}\,,

(2.7)

which is opposite to the measure behavior of the commutative variables involving the determinant factor in the numerator.

2.2
Supervector space

A (complex) supervector is an element of the (complex) supervector space, which is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded vector space of dimension
(N|M)
,

\displaystyle\Psi=(z_{i},\theta_{j})_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\in\mathbb{C}^{N|M}=\mathbb{C}_{0}^{N}\oplus\mathbb{C}_{1}^{M}\,,

(2.8)

where the index of
\mathbb{C}_{\sigma}
,
\sigma=0,1
denotes the Grassmann parity.
In general, a supervector space consists of even and odd subspaces,

\displaystyle V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}\,.

(2.9)

We denote the parity of the element
x
by
|x|=0
if
x\in V_{0}
and
|x|=1
if
x\in V_{1}
.
We define the parity flip operator
\Pi
, such that
(\Pi V)_{0}=V_{1}
and
(\Pi V)_{1}=V_{0}
.
The superdimension of
V
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{sdim}V=\sum_{\sigma=0,1}(-1)^{\sigma}\operatorname{dim}V_{\sigma}=\operatorname{dim}V_{0}-\operatorname{dim}V_{1}\,,

(2.10)

which may take a negative integer value.

Superalgebra

Superalgebra is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded algebra that consists of the even part and the odd part,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{A}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{A}_{1}\,.

(2.11)

We define the supercommutator for
x,y\in\mathfrak{A}
as follows,

\displaystyle[x,y]=xy-(-1)^{|x||y|}yx\,,

(2.12)

which is skew-symmetric,

\displaystyle[x,y]=-(-1)^{|x||y|}[y,x]\,.

(2.13)

Then, we define the Lie superalgebra that obeys the super analog of the Jacobi identity,

\displaystyle[x,[y,z]]=[[x,y],z]+(-1)^{|x||y|}[y,[x,z]]\,.

(2.14)

2.3
Supermatrix

For supervector spaces
V
and
W
, we define a linear map, which is described using a supermatrix,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}M_{00}&M_{01}\\
M_{10}&M_{11}\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)=\bigoplus_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}=0,1}\operatorname{Hom}(V_{\sigma^{\prime}},W_{\sigma})\,,

(2.15)

where each block of the matrix is given by

\displaystyle M_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\in\operatorname{Hom}(V_{\sigma^{\prime}},W_{\sigma})\,.

(2.16)

Since
M_{01}
and
M_{10}
change the Grassmann parity, they consists of the Grassmann odd variables.
In particular, if
V=W
, we have
M\in\operatorname{End}(V)=\bigoplus_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}=0,1}\operatorname{Hom}(V_{\sigma},V_{\sigma^{\prime}})
, which is invertible if both
M_{00}
and
M_{11}
are invertible.
The set of invertible supermatrices defines a general linear supergroup,
\mathrm{GL}(V)=\mathrm{GL}(V_{0}|V_{1})
.

We remark that the definition of supermatrix is not unique to (
2.15
).
For example, denoting even and odd variables by
x_{ij}
and
\xi_{ij}

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&\xi_{13}\\
x_{21}&x_{22}&\xi_{23}\\
\xi_{31}&\xi_{32}&x_{33}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&\xi_{12}&x_{13}\\
\xi_{21}&x_{22}&\xi_{23}\\
x_{31}&\xi_{32}&x_{33}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&\xi_{12}&\xi_{13}\\
\xi_{21}&x_{22}&x_{23}\\
\xi_{31}&x_{32}&x_{33}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.17)

which correspond to the convention of supervector,
\Psi=(z_{1},z_{2},\theta)
,

This is related to the ambiguity of the root system in Lie superalgebra.

Supertrace

We define the supertrace operation for the supermatrix,

4

4

Not to be confused with the symmetrized trace.
We will introduce one-parameter deformation of the supertrace in (

\displaystyle\str M=\sum_{\sigma=0,1}(-1)^{\sigma}\tr_{\sigma}M

(2.18)

where
\tr_{\sigma}

\tr_{\sigma}M=\tr M_{\sigma\sigma}

Compared with the definition of the superdimension (

An important property of the supertrace is the cyclicity,

\displaystyle\str M_{1}M_{2}=\str M_{2}M_{1}\,,

Supertransposition

We define the supertransposition operation for the supermatrix,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}^{\text{st}}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{\text{t}}&C^{\text{t}}\\
-B^{\text{t}}&D^{\text{t}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.20)

where we denote the ordinary transposition by
A^{\text{t}}
, etc.
This supertransposition shows an analogous property to the ordinary case,
(M_{1}M_{2})^{\text{st}}=M_{2}^{\text{st}}M_{1}^{\text{st}}
, while it is not an involution,
(M^{\text{st}})^{\text{st}}\neq M
in general.
The Hermitian conjugation is defined as follows,

\displaystyle M^{\dagger}=\overline{M}^{\text{st}}\,,

(2.21)

which is then an involution,
(M^{\dagger})^{\dagger}=M
.

Superdeterminant

We define the superdeterminant, which is also called the Berezinian,

\displaystyle\sdet\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\det(A - B D^{-1} C)}{\det D}=\frac{\det A}{\det(D - C A^{-1} B)}\,.

(2.22)

This is analogous to the determinant formula for the block matrix,

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\det(A - B D^{-1} C)\det D=\det A\det(D - C A^{-1} B)\,.

(2.23)

We have the multiplicative property for the superdeterminant,
\sdet(M_{1}M_{2})=\sdet M_{1}\sdet M_{2}
.
We remark an identity

\displaystyle\str\log M=\log\str M\,.

(2.24)

Recalling the behavior of the Grassmann variable measure (
2.7
), the integral measure denoted by
\differential{\Psi}=\differential{z}_{1}\cdots\differential{z}_{N}\differential{\theta}_{1}\cdots\differential{\theta}_{M}
behaves under the linear transform for the supervector (
2.8
),
\Psi^{\prime}=M\Psi
, as follows,

\displaystyle\differential{\Psi'}=\differential{\Psi}(\sdet M)\,.

(2.25)

2.4
Supergroup

Unitary supergroup

For the complex supervector space element (
2.8
),
\Psi\in\mathbb{C}^{N|M}
, we consider the squared norm as follows,

\displaystyle|\Psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}|z_{i}|^{2}+\sum_{j\in[M]}\bar{\theta}_{j}\theta_{j}=\sum_{i\in[N]}|z_{i}|^{2}-\sum_{j\in[M]}\theta_{j}\bar{\theta}_{j}=\str(\Psi\Psi^{\dagger})\,.

(2.26)

Then, we define the unitary supergroup
\mathrm{U}(N|M)
as the isometry group with respect to the supervector space
\mathbb{C}^{N|M}
, such that,

\displaystyle|\Psi|^{2}=|U\Psi|^{2}\,,\qquad U^{\dagger}=U^{-1}\,.

(2.27)

We remark that
|U\Psi|^{2}=\str(U\Psi\Psi^{\dagger}U^{\dagger})=\str(\Psi\Psi^{\dagger})
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}(N|M)=\{U\in\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{N|M})\mid U^{\dagger}=U^{-1}\}\,.

(2.28)

The even part of the unitary supergroup
\mathrm{U}(N|M)
is given by
\mathrm{U}(N)\times\mathrm{U}(M)
, and the odd part is given by
N\times\overline{M}
and
\overline{N}\times M
representations of the even part with the dimension
2NM
.

Orthosymplectic supergroup

We consider the real supervector space
\mathbb{R}^{N|2M}
and the squared norm of
\Psi=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{2M})\in\mathbb{R}^{N|2M}
as follows,

\displaystyle|\Psi|^{2}=\sum_{i\in[N]}x_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{j\in[M]}\theta_{2j-1}\theta_{2j}=\Psi^{\text{t}}\Omega\Psi=\str\left(\Omega\Psi\Psi^{\text{t}}\right)\,,

(2.29)

where we define the skew-symmetric form for the Grassmann odd sector,

\displaystyle\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}_{N}&0\\
0&\mathbbm{1}_{M}\otimes\mathbf{j}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\mathbf{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(2.30)

We remark that the standard bilinear form does not work for the Grassmann variables due to its nilpotency,
\sum_{j\in[M]}\theta_{j}\theta_{j}=0
.
The isometry group for the real supervector space
\mathbb{R}^{N|2M}
is given by

\displaystyle|\Psi|^{2}=|U\Psi|^{2}\,,\qquad U^{\text{st}}\Omega U=\Omega\,.

(2.31)

We see that
|U\Psi|^{2}=\str(\Omega U\Psi\Psi^{\text{t}}U^{\text{st}})=\str(\Omega\Psi\Psi^{\text{t}})
.
The orthosymplectic supergroup for general field
\mathbb{K}
is given as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{OSp}(N|2M,\mathbb{K})=\{U\in\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{K}^{N|2M})\mid U^{\text{st}}\Omega U=\Omega\}\,.

(2.32)

Precisely speaking, the orthosymplectic group realized as the isometry group of the real supervector space is a compact supergroup, which is in fact a subgroup of the unitary supergroup, denoted by
\mathrm{UOSp}(N|M)=\mathrm{OSp}(N|2M,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{U}(N|2M)
.
This is analogous to the notation of the compact symplectic group
\mathrm{USp}(2n)=\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{U}(2n)
, which is also understood as the quaternionic unitary group
\mathrm{U}(n,\mathbb{H})
.
In this article, we use the notation,
\mathrm{OSp}(N|M)=\mathrm{UOSp}(N|2M)
and
\mathrm{Sp}(n)=\mathrm{USp}(2n)
unless it causes confusion.
5
5

5

In this notation, we have the isomorphisms at the level of Lie algebra,
\mathfrak{sp}_{1}=\mathfrak{su}_{2}
,
\mathfrak{sp}_{2}=\mathfrak{so}_{5}
.

The even part of
\mathrm{OSp}(N|M)
is thus given by
\mathrm{O}(N)\times\mathrm{Sp}(M)
, and the odd part is given by
N\times 2M
representation of the even part with the dimension
2NM
.

Another situation that leads to the orthosymplectic supergroup is a subsector of the supervector space
\mathbb{C}^{2N|2M}
,

\displaystyle\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}&z_{2}\\
-\bar{z}_{2}&\bar{z}_{1}\\
z_{3}&z_{4}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
-\bar{z}_{2N}&\bar{z}_{2N-1}\\
\theta_{1}&\bar{\theta}_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\theta_{M}&\bar{\theta}_{M}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\Psi^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{z}_{1}&-z_{2}&\bar{z}_{3}&\cdots&-z_{2N}&\bar{\theta}_{1}&\cdots&\bar{\theta}_{M}\\
\bar{z}_{2}&z_{1}&\bar{z}_{4}&\cdots&z_{2N-1}&-\theta_{1}&\cdots&-\theta_{M}\end{pmatrix}

(2.33)

where the two-by-two block in the bosonic part is identified with a quaternion

\displaystyle x_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}z_{2i-1}&z_{2i}\\
-\bar{z}_{2i}&\bar{z}_{2i-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}\,.

(2.34)

Hence, this is an element in
\mathbb{H}^{N}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{2M}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2N|2M}
.
We denote the norm of
x\in\mathbb{H}
by
|x|
, such that
\bar{x}x=|x|^{2}\mathbbm{1}
where
\mathbbm{1}
is the quaternion identity.
The norm of this supervector is given by

\displaystyle|\Psi|^{2}=\tr_{\mathbb{H}}\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi=2\sum_{i\in[N]}|x_{i}|^{2}+2\sum_{j\in[M]}\bar{\theta}_{j}\theta_{j}=\str(\Psi\Psi^{\dagger})\,,

(2.35)

where we denote the trace operation over the quaternion by
\tr_{\mathbb{H}}
, i.e., for the quaternion units,
\mathbbm{1}
,
\mathbf{i}
,
\mathbf{j}
,
\mathbf{k}
, we have
\tr_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbbm{1}=2
,
\tr_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{i}=\tr_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{j}=\tr_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbf{k}=0
.
The isometry group of this supervector is thus given by the orthosymplectic supergroup
\mathrm{U}(2N|2M)\supset\mathrm{OSp}(M|N)\supset\mathrm{Sp}(N)\times\mathrm{O}(M)
.

Analytic continuation

We discuss how to obtain the supergroups considered above through the analytic continuation.
First of all, the dimension of each classical group is given by
6
6

6

The orthogonal group is further classified by the parity of the rank,
\dim\mathrm{O}(2N)=N(2N-1)
,
\dim\mathrm{O}(2N+1)=N(2N+1)
.
We remark that
\dim\mathrm{O}(2N+1)=\dim\mathrm{Sp}(N)
.
This dimension formula is also understood from the construction of the adjoint representations for these classical groups (
5.63
).

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{U}(N)=N^{2}\,,\qquad\dim\mathrm{O}(N)=\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\,,\qquad\dim\mathrm{Sp}(N)=N(2N+1)\,,

(2.36)

from which we observe the following relations,

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{U}(-N)=\dim\mathrm{U}(N)\,,\qquad\dim\mathrm{O}(-2N)=\dim\mathrm{Sp}(N)\,.

(2.37)

Such a relation is discussed also at the level of their irreducible representations
[
Kin71
]
.
From this point of view, these supergroups are obtained from the ordinary classical groups through the analytic continuation,

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}(N+M)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle M\leftrightarrow-M}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\ \mathrm{U}(N|M)\,,

(2.38a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{O}(N+2M)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle M\leftrightarrow-M}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\ \mathrm{OSp}(N|M)\,,\qquad\mathrm{Sp}(N+M)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle M\leftrightarrow-M}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\ \mathrm{OSp}(2M|N)\,.

(2.38b)

In particular, we have the relations between the superdimension and the dimension of the classical groups,

\displaystyle\operatorname{sdim}\mathrm{U}(N|M)

\displaystyle=(N^{2}+M^{2})-2NM=(N-M)^{2}=\operatorname{dim}\mathrm{U}(N+M)\Big|_{M\to-M}\,,

(2.39a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{sdim}\mathrm{OSp}(N|M)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{N(N-1)}{2}+M(2M+1)\right)-2NM=2\left(\frac{N}{2}-M\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{N}{2}-M\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dim}\mathrm{O}(N+2M)\Big|_{M\to-M}=\operatorname{dim}\mathrm{Sp}\left(\frac{N}{2}+M\right)\Bigg|_{N\to-N}\,.

(2.39b)

This interpretation based on the analytic continuation seems also reasonable from the relation between the ordinary trace and the supertrace.

Guh10
]
for details on this subject.

3.1
Hermitian supermatrix model: superunitary ensemble

Let
H
be an
(N|M)
-dimensional Hermitian supermatrix,
H^{\dagger}=H
.
We define the partition function of Hermitian supermatrix model of rank
(N|M)
as follows
[
AGM91
,
Yos92
]
,

\displaystyle Z_{N|M}=\int\differential{H}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{g}\str V(H)}\,,

(3.1)

with a polynomial potential function of degree
(d+1)
,

\displaystyle V(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{d+1}\frac{t_{n}}{n}\,x^{n}\,.

(3.2)

This integral is invariant under superunitary transformation,
H\to UHU^{\dagger}
,
U\in\mathrm{U}(N|M)
:
The potential part is invariant due to the cyclic property of the supertrace (
2.19
),
\str V(UHU^{\dagger})=\str V(H)
, and the measure part is due to the unitarity of the superdeterminant
|\sdet U|=1
.
From this point of view, this model is also called the
superunitary ensemble
as a supermatrix generalization of the unitary ensemble of the ordinary random matrices.
See, e.g.,
[
Meh04
,
For10
,
EKR15
]
.
In fact, this symmetry is interpreted as a remnant of the supergroup gauge symmetry.

Similarly to the ordinary Hermitian matrix, we can diagonalize the Hermitian supermatrix via the superunitary transform,
7
7

7

Eigenvalues of Hermitian supermatrices are ordinary commutative numbers (Recall that the diagonal blocks of supermatrices are ordinary matrices).
The matrix model involving Grassmann odd eigenvalues is known as the super-eigenvalue model
[
AGIMZ92
]
.

\displaystyle H=UZU^{\dagger}\,,\qquad U\in\mathrm{U}(N|M)\,,

(3.3)

where we denote the diagonal supermatrix by

\displaystyle Z=\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&Y\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad X=\operatorname{diag}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\,,\ Y=\operatorname{diag}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{M})\,.

(3.4)

We remark that the choice of
U
is not unique in the process of diagonalization: (i) The eigenvalues can be permuted
(x_{i},y_{j})_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\to(x_{\sigma(i)},y_{\sigma^{\prime}(j)})_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}
where
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
,
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{M}
.
(ii) Since the diagonal superunitary matrix commutes with the diagonal supermatrix,
U_{\text{diag}}ZU_{\text{diag}}^{\dagger}=Z
, where
U_{\text{diag}}\in\mathrm{U}(1)^{N+M}
, the decomposition is invariant under redefinition
U\to UU_{\text{diag}}
.

Taking the derivative of the relation above, we have the following expression,

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}\differential{H}U=\differential{Z}+[U^{\dagger}\differential{U},Z]\,.

(3.5)

We remark that
U^{\dagger}\differential{U}
is the Maurer–Cartan one-form with respect to the supergroup
\mathrm{U}(N|M)
, and the Hermitian supermatrix takes a value in the Lie superalgebra
\operatorname{Lie}\mathrm{U}(N|M)
(up to the imaginary unit).
Then, the right hand side of this equation can be also written using the covariant derivative in the adjoint representation,
D=\differential{}+U^{\dagger}\differential{U}
.
Denoting
I_{0}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
I_{1}=\{N+1,\ldots,N+M\}
, and
\mathsf{i}(i)=i
for
i\in I_{0}
and
\mathsf{i}(i)=i-N
for
i\in I_{1}
, each component of (
3.5
) is given by

\displaystyle(U^{\dagger}\differential{H}U)_{ii}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\differential{x}_{\mathsf{i}(i)}&(i\in I_{0})\\
\differential{y}_{\mathsf{i}(i)}&(i\in I_{1})\end{cases}

(3.6a)

\displaystyle(U^{\dagger}\differential{H}U)_{ij}

\displaystyle=(U^{\dagger}\differential{U})_{ij}\times\begin{cases}x_{\mathsf{i}(j)}-x_{\mathsf{i}(i)}&(i,j\in I_{0})\\
y_{\mathsf{i}(j)}-y_{\mathsf{i}(i)}&(i,j\in I_{1})\\
y_{\mathsf{i}(j)}-x_{\mathsf{i}(i)}&(i\in I_{0},j\in I_{1})\\
x_{\mathsf{i}(j)}-y_{\mathsf{i}(i)}&(i\in I_{1},j\in I_{0})\end{cases}

(3.6b)

Hence, the supermatrix measure is given in terms of the eigenvalues and the eigenvector components as follows,

\displaystyle\differential{H}=|\Delta_{N|M}(X|Y)|^{2}\differential{X}\differential{Y}\differential{U}

(3.7)

where we define

\displaystyle\differential{X}=\prod_{i\in[N]}\differential{x}_{i}\,,\quad\differential{Y}=\prod_{i\in[M]}\differential{y}_{i}\,,\quad\differential{U}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N+M}\operatorname{Re}(U^{\dagger}\differential{U})_{ij}\operatorname{Im}(U^{\dagger}\differential{U})_{ij}\,.

(3.8)

In particular,
\differential{U}
is the Haar measure on the unitary supergroup
\mathrm{U}(N|M)
.
The Jacobian part is given by the Cauchy determinant

\displaystyle\Delta_{N|M}(X|Y)=\prod_{i<j}^{N}(x_{j}-x_{i})\prod_{i<j}^{M}(y_{j}-y_{i})\prod_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}(x_{i}-y_{j})^{-1}\,,

(3.9)

which has a determinantal formula for
N\geq M
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{N|M}(X|Y)=\det_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[N],\,j\in[M]\\
k\in[N-M]\end{subarray}}\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}^{k-1}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{i}-y_{j}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.10)

In the limit
M\to 0
, this is reduced to the Vandermonde determinant,

\displaystyle\Delta_{N|0}(X)=\Delta_{N}(X)=\prod_{i<j}^{N}(x_{j}-x_{i})\,.

(3.11)

Based on these diagonalization process, we obtain the eigenvalue integral form of the partition function,

\displaystyle{Z}_{N|M}=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathrm{U}(N|M))}{N!M!}\int\differential{\mu(X)}\differential{\mu(Y)}|\Delta_{N|M}(X|Y)|^{2}\,,

(3.12)

where the integral measure is given by

\displaystyle\differential{\mu(X)}=\prod_{i\in[N]}\frac{\differential{x}_{i}}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{g}V(x_{i})}\,,\qquad\differential{\mu(Y)}=\prod_{i\in[M]}\frac{\differential{y}_{i}}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{+\frac{1}{g}V(y_{i})}\,.

(3.13)

Constant factors are understood as follows:
The factorial terms are the volumes of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{M}
, which are the Weyl group of
\mathrm{U}(N)
and
\mathrm{U}(M)
, and
(2\pi)^{N+M}=\operatorname{vol}(\mathrm{U}(1)^{N+M})
, the volume of the maximal Cartan torus of
\mathrm{U}(N|M)
.
We have several remarks on this formula:

1.

The signatures of the potential term for the
x
-variables and the
y
-variables are opposite.
Hence, we should consider a complex contour to obtain a converging integral.
For example, for the Gaussian case
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
, the
x
-integral is taken along the real axis,
-\infty\to+\infty
, while the
y
-integral should be taken along the imaginary axis,
-\mathrm{i}\infty\to+\mathrm{i}\infty
, or vice versa.

2.

The denominator contribution in the Cauchy determinant is singular in the limit
x_{i}\to y_{j}
.
If there is an intersection of the
x
-contour and the
y
-contour, such a singularity should be regularized using the principal value prescription.

3.

The volume of the unitary supergroup becomes zero if
NM\neq 0
due to the Grassmann variable integral (Berezin’s theorem. See e.g.,
[
Vor15
]
).
Hence, we consider the partition function formally normalized by this zero-volume,
\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}:=Z_{N|M}/\operatorname{vol}(\mathrm{U}(N|M))
.
8
8

8

It would be possible that the zero-volume factor cancels the diverging behavior of the eigenvalue integral to give a finite value in the end.
We do not discuss details of this issue any further in this article.

Taking into account these points, we obtain the eigenvalue integral form of the supermatrix partition function.

The eigenvalue integral form of the regularized partition function of the Hermitian supermatrix model is given as follows,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}=\frac{1}{N!M!}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\gamma_{x}^{N}\times\gamma_{y}^{M}}\differential{\mu(X)}\differential{\mu(Y)}|\Delta_{N|M}(X|Y)|^{2}\,,

(3.14)

where we denote the principal value integral by
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\differential{x}f(x)
, and
\gamma_{x}
and
\gamma_{y}
are the integration contours on the complex plane that provide a converging integral.

3.2
Real-quaternion supermatrix model: super orthosymplectic ensemble

We consider an
(N|M)
-dimensional real-quaternion self-conjugate supermatrix,

\displaystyle H=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}

(3.15)

where
A
is an
N
-dimensional real symmetric matrix, and
D
is an
M
-dimensional quaternion self-dual matrix (realized as a
2M
-dimensional Hermitian matrix).

B
is a real Grassmann matrix of size
N\times 2M
and, in order that
H
is self-conjugate
H^{\dagger}=H
, we have
C=-B^{\text{t}}
.
In this case, similarly to the Hermitian case (
3.3
), we can diagonalize the supermatrix via the orthosymplectic transformation,

\displaystyle H=UZU^{\dagger}\,,\qquad U\in\mathrm{OSp}(N|M)\,.

(3.16)

The diagonal supermatrix
Z
is given by

\displaystyle Z=\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&Y\otimes\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.17)

where we denote the identity element in quaternion by
\mathbbm{1}
.
Namely, if we use the two-by-two matrix realization of quaternion, it is given by the identity matrix of rank two.
Hence, the supertrace is in this case given by

\displaystyle\str Z=\sum_{i\in[N]}x_{i}-2\sum_{j\in[M]}y_{j}\,.

(3.18)

We should be careful of the multiplicity in the quaternionic sector, which corresponds to the Kramers doublet.
We will define a deformed supertrace operation respecting this multiplicity (see (
3.26
)).

Applying the same argument to the unitary case, we have the relation (
3.5
) where the corresponding components are given as in (
3.6
).
In this case, we should be careful of that the
y
-variables are doubly degenerated.
Hence, we should count twice for the mixing terms
y_{j}-x_{i}
and four (
=2\times 2
) times for the quaternion part
y_{j}-y_{i}
.
Therefore, the real-quaternion supermatrix measure is given as follows,

\displaystyle\differential{H}=|\Delta_{N|M}^{(1|4)}(X|Y)|\differential{X}\differential{Y}\differential{U}

(3.19)

where we define

\displaystyle\differential{X}=\prod_{i\in[N]}\differential{x}_{i}\,,\qquad\differential{Y}=\prod_{i\in[M]}\differential{y}_{i}\,,\qquad

(3.20)

and the corresponding Haar measure
\differential{U}
of supergroup
\mathrm{OSp}(N|M)
.
We use the following notation for the Jacobian part,

\displaystyle\Delta_{N|M}^{(\beta|\beta^{\prime})}(X|Y)=\frac{\Delta_{N}(X)^{\beta}\Delta_{M}(Y)^{\beta^{\prime}}}{\prod_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}(x_{i}-y_{j})^{2}}

(3.21)

In this notation, we have
\Delta_{N|M}(X|Y)^{2}=\Delta_{N|M}^{(2|2)}(X|Y)
.
Collecting all the contributions, we obtain the real-quaternion supermatrix.

The eigenvalue integral form of the regularized partition function of real-quaternion supermatrix model is given as follows,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}:=\frac{Z_{N|M}}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathrm{OSp}(N|M))}=\frac{1}{N!M!}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\gamma_{x}^{N}\times\gamma_{y}^{M}}\differential{\mu(X)}\differential{\mu(Y)}|\Delta_{N|M}^{(1|4)}(X|Y)|\,.

(3.22)

We study several aspects of the supermatrix models in the following part.

3.3
Coulomb gas analysis

In the context of matrix model, we are in particular interested in the asymptotic behavior in the large size limit of the matrix model.
We study such an asymptotic limit of the supermatrix model based on the Coulomb gas analysis.
See also
[
Bab22
]
for details in this part.

We start with the partition function of the
\beta
-deformed supermatrix model,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}=\frac{1}{N!M!}\int\differential{\mu(X)}\differential{\mu(Y)}|\Delta_{N|M}^{(\beta|\beta^{\prime})}(X|Y)|\,,\qquad\beta\beta^{\prime}=4\,,

(3.23)

where the integral measure is given by

\displaystyle\differential{\mu(X)}=\prod_{i\in[N]}\frac{\differential{x}_{i}}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}V(x_{i})}\,,\qquad\differential{\mu(Y)}=\prod_{i\in[M]}\frac{\differential{y}_{i}}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{+\frac{b^{-1}}{g}V(y_{i})}\,,\qquad b=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2}}\,.

(3.24)

In this notation, the measure factor is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{N|M}^{(\beta|\beta^{\prime})}(X|Y)=\frac{\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\Delta_{M}(Y)^{2b^{-2}}}{\prod_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}(x_{i}-y_{j})^{2}}\,.

(3.25)

For the
\beta
-deformed case, it is convenient to define the
b
-deformed supertrace,

\displaystyle\str_{b}\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=b\tr_{0}A-b^{-1}\tr_{1}D\,.

(3.26)

With this operation, the potential factor is concisely written as

\displaystyle\str_{b}V(Z)=b\sum_{i\in[N]}V(x_{i})-b^{-1}\sum_{j\in[M]}V(y_{j})\,,

(3.27)

where the diagonal supermatrix
Z
is given as (
3.4
).
Such a deformation is discussed in the context of symmetric polynomial associated with the Lie superalgebra root system.
See, e.g.,
[
Ser01
,
Ser02
,
SV05
,
HL09
,
Des12
]
, and also discussions in Secs.
6.4
and
6.5
.

3.3.1
Saddle point equation

To study the asymptotic behavior of the supermatrix model, we rewrite the partition function in the following form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}\approx\int\differential{X}\differential{Y}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{g^{2}}S(X|Y)}

(3.28)

where the integral measure is given by

\displaystyle\differential{X}=\prod_{i\in[N]}\differential{x}_{i}\,,\qquad\differential{Y}=\prod_{j\in[M]}\differential{y}_{j}\,,

(3.29)

and we define the effective action

\displaystyle S(X|Y)

\displaystyle=bg\sum_{i\in[N]}V(x_{i})-b^{-1}g\sum_{j\in[M]}V(y_{j})

\displaystyle\quad-2b^{2}g^{2}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\log(x_i - x_j)-2b^{-2}g^{2}\sum_{i<j}^{M}\log(y_i - y_j)+2g^{2}\sum_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\log(x_i - y_j)\,.

(3.30)

Then, introducing two parameters,

\displaystyle\mathsf{t}_{0}=bgN\,,\qquad\mathsf{t}_{1}=b^{-1}gM\,,

(3.31)

we consider the following asymptotic limit (’t Hooft limit) of the supermatrix model,

\displaystyle g\ \longrightarrow\ 0\,,\qquad N,M\ \longrightarrow\ \infty\,,\qquad\mathsf{t}_{0},\mathsf{t}_{1}=O(1)\,.

(3.32)

In the ’t Hooft limit, the eigenvalue integral localizes on the configuration that obeys the following saddle point equations,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial S}{\partial x_{i}}=+bgV^{\prime}(x_{i})-2b^{2}g^{2}\sum_{j\in[N]\backslash\{i\}}\frac{1}{x_{i}-x_{j}}+2g^{2}\sum_{j\in[M]}\frac{1}{x_{i}-y_{j}}\,,

(3.33a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial S}{\partial y_{i}}=-b^{-1}gV^{\prime}(y_{i})-2b^{-2}g^{2}\sum_{j\in[M]\backslash\{i\}}\frac{1}{y_{i}-y_{j}}+2g^{2}\sum_{j\in[N]}\frac{1}{y_{i}-x_{j}}\,.

(3.33b)

We introduce the auxiliary functions,

\displaystyle W_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=bg\sum_{i\in[N]}\frac{1}{x-x_{i}}=bg\tr_{0}\left(\frac{1}{x-X}\right)\,,

(3.34a)

\displaystyle W_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=b^{-1}g\sum_{i\in[M]}\frac{1}{x-y_{i}}=b^{-1}g\tr_{1}\left(\frac{1}{x-Y}\right)\,,

(3.34b)

\displaystyle P_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=bg\sum_{i\in[N]}\frac{V^{\prime}(x)-V^{\prime}(x_{i})}{x-x_{i}}=bg\tr_{0}\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)-V^{\prime}(X)}{x-X}\right)\,,

(3.34c)

\displaystyle P_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=b^{-1}g\sum_{i\in[M]}\frac{V^{\prime}(x)-V^{\prime}(y_{i})}{x-y_{i}}=b^{-1}g\tr_{1}\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)-V^{\prime}(Y)}{x-Y}\right)\,.

(3.34d)

The auxiliary functions
W_{\sigma}(x)
are in particular called the resolvents that involve a pole singularity at
x\in\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
and
x\in\{y_{j}\}_{j\in[M]}
, respectively.
Although the other functions
P_{\sigma}(x)
look a similar form, they are polynomial functions having no pole singularity.
Recalling that the potential function is given as (
3.2
), the asymptotic behaviors of these auxiliary functions are given by

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(x)\ \xrightarrow{x\to\infty}\ \frac{\mathsf{t}_{\sigma}}{x}\,,\qquad P_{\sigma}(x)\ \xrightarrow{x\to\infty}\ \mathsf{t}_{\sigma}t_{d+1}x^{d-1}\,.

(3.35)

Using these auxiliary functions, we may rewrite the saddle point equation (
3.33
) as follows,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-P_{0}(x)+V^{\prime}(x)W_{0}(x)-W_{0}(x)^{2}-bgW_{0}^{\prime}(x)+2g^{2}\sum_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\frac{1}{(x-x_{i})(x_{i}-y_{j})}\,,

(3.36a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=+P_{1}(x)-V^{\prime}(x)W_{1}(x)-W_{1}(x)^{2}-b^{-1}gW_{1}^{\prime}(x)+2g^{2}\sum_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\frac{1}{(x-y_{j})(y_{j}-x_{i})}\,.

(3.36b)

Moreover, we define the supertrace analog of the auxiliary functions,

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}(x)

\displaystyle=W_{0}(x)-W_{1}(x)=g\str_{b}\left(\frac{1}{x-Z}\right)\,,

(3.37a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}(x)

\displaystyle=P_{0}(x)-P_{1}(x)=g\str_{b}\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(x)-V^{\prime}(Z)}{x-Z}\right)\,,

(3.37b)

which show the following asymptotic behavior,

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}(x)\ \xrightarrow{x\to\infty}\ \frac{\mathsf{t}_{0}-\mathsf{t}_{1}}{x}\,,\qquad\mathsf{P}(x)\ \xrightarrow{x\to\infty}\ (\mathsf{t}_{0}-\mathsf{t}_{1})t_{d+1}x^{d-1}\,.

(3.38)

The total resolvent
\mathsf{W}(x)
, that we call the superresolvent, has poles with the residue
+bg
for
x\in\{x_{i}\}
and
-b^{-1}g
for
x\in\{y_{i}\}
, while
\mathsf{P}(x)
is again a polynomial function.
Then, combining the two equations, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}(x)-V^{\prime}(x)\mathsf{W}(x)+\mathsf{W}(x)^{2}+g(bW_{0}^{\prime}(x)+b^{-1}W_{1}^{\prime}(x))\,.

(3.39)

We study this equation in detail in Sec.
3.3.3
.

We remark that the two saddle point equations (
3.33
) are written as a single equation using the superresolvent,

\displaystyle 0=V^{\prime}(x)-2\mathsf{W}_{\text{reg}}(x)\,,\qquad x\in\{x_{i},y_{j}\}\,,

(3.40)

where we define the regularized one by

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}_{\text{reg}}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle bg\sum_{j\in[N]\backslash\{i\}}\frac{1}{x-x_{j}}-W_{1}(x)&(x=x_{i})\\
\displaystyle W_{0}(x)-b^{-1}g\sum_{j\in[M]\backslash\{i\}}\frac{1}{x-y_{j}}-W_{1}(x)&(x=y_{i})\end{cases}

(3.41)

We remark that the expression of the saddle point equation (
3.40
) is identical to the standard matrix model by replacing the superresolvent with the original one.
However, its analytic property should be different since the superresolvent may contain both positive and negative residues, while the original resolvent only involves positive one.

3.3.2
Functional method

Let us discuss an alternative approach based on the functional method.
We define the density functions
\rho_{\sigma}(x)
for
\sigma=0,1
.
Then, we rewrite the effective action (
3.30
) using these density functions,

\displaystyle S[\rho_{0,1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma=0,1}\left[(-1)^{\sigma}\mathsf{t}_{\sigma}\int\differential{x}\rho_{\sigma}(x)V(x)-2\mathsf{t}_{\sigma}^{2}\int_{x<y}\differential{x}\differential{y}\rho_{\sigma}(x)\rho_{\sigma}(y)\log|x-y|\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mathsf{t}_{0}\mathsf{t}_{1}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\differential{x}\differential{y}\rho_{0}(x)\rho_{1}(y)\log|x-y|+\sum_{\sigma=0,1}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\sigma}}\mathsf{t}_{\sigma}\ell_{\sigma,i}\left(\epsilon_{\sigma,i}-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\sigma,i}}\differential{x}\rho_{\sigma}(x)\right)\,,

(3.42)

where we consider the
m_{\sigma}
-cut solution for each sector,
\sigma=0,1
:
We added the Lagrange multiplier that imposes the condition

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\sigma,i}}\differential{x}\rho_{\sigma}(x)=\epsilon_{\sigma,i}\,,

(3.43)

where we denote the cut by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}=\bigsqcup_{i\in[m_{\sigma}]}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma,i}\,,\qquad\sigma=0,1\,,

(3.44)

and the corresponding filling fraction
(\epsilon_{\sigma,i})_{\sigma=0,1,i\in[m_{\sigma}]}
with
\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m_{\sigma}]}\epsilon_{\sigma,i}=1
.
Taking the functional derivative of the effective action, we obtain

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{C}_{\sigma,i}\ :\quad\mathsf{t}_{\sigma}^{-1}\frac{\delta S[\rho_{0,1}]}{\delta\rho_{\sigma}(x)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sigma}\left(V(x)-2\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\differential{y}\bar{\rho}(y)\log|x-y|\right)-\ell_{\sigma,i}

\displaystyle=:(-1)^{\sigma}V_{\text{eff}}(x)-\ell_{\sigma,i}\,,

(3.45)

where we define the effective potential
V_{\text{eff}}(x)
and the effective density functions,

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(x)=\mathsf{t}_{0}\rho_{0}(x)-\mathsf{t}_{1}\rho_{1}(x)\,.

(3.46)

The functional version of the saddle point equation (
3.40
) is obtained by the derivative of the effective potential,

\displaystyle\derivative{V_\text{eff}(x)}{x}=V^{\prime}(x)-2\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\differential{y}\frac{\bar{\rho}(y)}{x-y}\,.

(3.47)

Writing the integral form of the superresolvent,

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}(x)=\int\differential{y}\frac{\bar{\rho}(y)}{x-y}\,,

(3.48)

the regularized one is given by the principal value integral

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}_{\text{reg}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\differential{y}\frac{\bar{\rho}(y)}{x-y}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\mathsf{W}(x\pm\mathrm{i}0):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathsf{W}(x+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)+\mathsf{W}(x-\mathrm{i}\epsilon)}{2}\,.

(3.49)

Hence, we obtain the functional version of (
3.40
) as follows,

\displaystyle\derivative{V_\text{eff}(x)}{x}=0\ \implies\ V^{\prime}(x)-2\operatorname{Re}\mathsf{W}(x\pm\mathrm{i}0)=0\,,\qquad x\in\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}\,.

(3.50)

3.3.3
Spectral curve and quantization

We have seen that the saddle point equation of the supermatrix model gives rise to the relation among the resolvents as shown in (
3.39
).
Further taking the limit
g\to 0
, the relation (
3.39
) is written in a closed form of the superresolvent,

\displaystyle 0=\mathsf{W}(x)^{2}-V^{\prime}(x)\mathsf{W}(x)+\mathsf{P}(x)\,,

(3.51)

which defines the spectral curve of the supermatrix model:

Given the potential function
V^{\prime}(x)
and the polynomial function
\mathsf{P}(x)
, the spectral curve of the supermatrix is given as follows,

\displaystyle\Sigma=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\mid\mathcal{H}(x,y)=0\}\,,\qquad\mathcal{H}(x,y)=y^{2}-V^{\prime}(x)y+\mathsf{P}(x)\,.

(3.52)

This is formally identical to the spectral curve of the standard matrix model (see, e.g.
[
EKR15
]
), but as we mentioned before, we should be careful of its analytic property.

While the spectral curve is based on the closed equation for the superresolvent, the saddle point equation (
3.39
) itself is not written as a closed form.
In order to discuss an alternative form, we rewrite the resolvents as follows,

\displaystyle W_{0}(x)=bg\derivative{x}\log\psi_{0}(x)\,,\qquad W_{1}(x)=b^{-1}g\derivative{x}\log\psi_{1}(x)

(3.53)

where we define the wave functions (characteristic polynomials),

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(x)=\prod_{i\in[N]}(x-x_{i})\,,\qquad\psi_{1}(x)=\prod_{i\in[M]}(x-y_{i})\,.

(3.54)

Then, the superresolvent is given by the logarithmic derivative

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}(x)=g\derivative{x}\log\frac{\psi_{0}(x)^{b}}{\psi_{1}(x)^{b^{-1}}}\,.

(3.55)

Together with these wave functions, we can recast the saddle point equation (
3.39
) in the following form,

\displaystyle\left[D_{x}^{(b)2}-V^{\prime}(x)D_{x}^{(b)}+\mathsf{P}(x)\right]\psi_{0}\cdot\psi_{1}=0\,,

(3.56)

where we define the
b
-deformed Hirota derivative,

\displaystyle D_{x}^{(b)}\psi\cdot\phi=g\left(b\partialderivative{x}-b^{-1}\partialderivative{x'}\right)\psi(x)\phi(x^{\prime})\Big|_{x=x^{\prime}}=gb\psi^{\prime}(x)\phi(x)-gb^{-1}\psi(x)\phi^{\prime}(x)\,.

(3.57)

The standard Hirota derivative corresponds to the case
b=1
(
\beta=2
):
D_{x}=D_{x}^{(1)}
.
In fact, this bilinear equation is interpreted as a quantization of the spectral curve (
3.52
):

Based on the two-variable function
\mathcal{H}(x,y)
that defines the spectral curve (
3.52
), we have the quantum curve for
\beta
-supermatrix model involving the Hirota derivative,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\hat{x},\hat{y})\psi_{0}\cdot\psi_{1}=0\,,\qquad\hat{x}=x\,,\ \hat{y}=gD_{x}^{(b)}\,.

(3.58)

Recalling the definition of the Hirota derivative, the canonical commutation relation is given by

\displaystyle[\hat{y},\hat{x}]=\begin{cases}+gb&(\text{for}\ \psi_{0})\\
-gb^{-1}&(\text{for}\ \psi_{1})\\
\end{cases}

(3.59)

From this point of view, we call the bilinear equation (
3.56
) the quantum curve for the supermatrix model.
We now have the both positive and negative quantum parameters (Planck constants) for quantization of the supermatrix spectral curve, corresponding to that the superresolvent has both positive and negative residues.

Moreover, we now relate the supermatrix parameters to the so-called
\Omega
-background parameters (see Sec.
5
),

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\left(g,-\frac{g}{b^{2}}\right)\,.

(3.60)

In this notation, we have
b^{2}=-\epsilon_{1}/\epsilon_{2}
, and thus the condition
b=1
is equivalent to
\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}=0
.
Then, the
b
-Hirota derivative is rewritten using
\epsilon_{1,2}
as follows,

\displaystyle D_{x}^{(b)}\psi\cdot\phi

\displaystyle=b\epsilon_{1}\psi^{\prime}(x)\phi(x)+b\epsilon_{2}\psi(x)\phi^{\prime}(x)=:bD_{x}^{(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})}\psi\cdot\phi\,,

(3.61)

where
D_{x}^{(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})}
is the
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
-deformed Hirota derivative defined in
[
NY05a
]
.
This operator is reduced to the ordinary derivative in the limit
\epsilon_{1}\to 0
or
\epsilon_{2}\to 0
, which is called the Nekrasov–Shatashvili (NS) limit,

\displaystyle D_{x}^{(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})}\psi\cdot\phi\ \longrightarrow\ \begin{cases}\epsilon_{1}\psi^{\prime}(x)\phi(x)&(\epsilon_{2}\to 0)\\
\epsilon_{2}\psi(x)\phi^{\prime}(x)&(\epsilon_{1}\to 0)\end{cases}

(3.62)

See also Sec.
6.4
for a related discussion.

3.3.4
Gaussian model

We consider the simplest example with the quadratic potential
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
, which is called the Gaussian matrix model.
In this case, the quantum curve is given by

\displaystyle\left[D_{x}^{(b)2}-xD_{x}^{(b)}+(\mathsf{t}_{0}-\mathsf{t}_{1})\right]\psi_{0}\cdot\psi_{1}=0\,.

(3.63)

Hence, in particular for the unitary case
b=1
(
\beta=2
), we have

\displaystyle\left[D_{\xi}^{2}-\xi D_{\xi}+(N-M)\right]\psi_{0}\cdot\psi_{1}=0\,,\qquad x=\sqrt{g}\xi\,.

(3.64)

This bilinear equation is known to be (a part of) the bilinear equations for the
\tau
-functions in the symmetric form of the Painlevé IV equation
[
NY99
]
.
In this case, the polynomial solution is given by the generalized Hermite polynomial, which is given through specialization of Schur functions.
In the NS limit, as mentioned above, the Hirota derivative is reduced to the ordinary derivative, where this bilinear equation is accordingly reduced to the differential equation for the Hermite polynomial.

3.4
Free field realization

We turn to discuss algebraic aspects of supermatrix model.
In particular, we show that the supermatrix partition function has a realization in terms of the chiral boson fields (free field realization).

3.4.1
Operator formalism

In order to discuss the free field formalism, we consider the ordinary
\beta
-deformed matrix model of rank
N
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{N}=\int\differential{X}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}\tr V(X)}\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\,.

(3.65)

In this case, the matrix moment (the power-sum average of the eigenvalues) is given as follows,

\displaystyle\left<\tr X^{n}\right>=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\int\differential{X}\left(\tr X^{n}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}\tr V(X)}\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}=-\frac{b}{g}n\partialderivative{t_n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}\,.

(3.66)

Hence, the derivative with the coupling constant
\{t_{n}\}
plays a similar role to the multiplication of the matrix power
\{\tr X^{n}\}
.
This is because the potential factor
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}\tr V(X)}
plays a role of the plane wave factor
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}px}
in the Fourier transform (FT).
In order to have this correspondence for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the potential with infinitely many coupling constants,
V(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t_{n}}{n}x^{n}
.
For the supermatrix model defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}=\frac{1}{N!M!}\int\differential{X}\differential{Y}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{g}\str_{b}V(Z)}\frac{\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\Delta_{M}(Y)^{2b^{-2}}}{\prod_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}(x_{i}-y_{j})^{2}}\,,

(3.67)

the same argument is applied for
\langle\str_{b}X^{n}\rangle\leftrightarrow-\frac{1}{g}n\partialderivative{t_n}
.

Then, we define oscillator operators,

\displaystyle a_{n}=\sqrt{2}gn\partialderivative{t_n}\left(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{FT}}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}b}\tr X^{n}\right)\,,\qquad a_{-n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}g}t_{n}\,,\qquad(n>0)

(3.68)

which obeys the commutation relation of the Heisenberg algebra,

\displaystyle[a_{n},a_{m}]=n\delta_{n+m,0}\,.

(3.69)

In addition, we also add the zero modes
(a_{0},\bar{a}_{0})
with the commutation relation,

\displaystyle[a_{n},\bar{a}_{0}]=\delta_{n,0}\,,

(3.70)

where we interpret
a_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{FT}}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}-(\tr X^{0})/\sqrt{2}b=-N/\sqrt{2}b
.
In this formalism, there exist infinitely many operators
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
They are independent operators if the matrix size is taken to be infinite
N\to\infty
.
For example, if
N=1
, we have relations among these operators,
\tr X^{n}=(\tr X)^{n}
.

We define the vacuum state, which is annihilated by the positive modes,

\displaystyle a_{n}|0\rangle=0\,,\qquad n\geq 0\,.

(3.71)

In this sense, the positive modes are the annihilation operators, and the negative modes are the creation operators.
We also define the charged vacuum using the zero mode,

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\bar{a}_{0}}|0\rangle\,,\qquad a_{0}|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle\,.

(3.72)

Based on the operators defined above, we define the operators, called the
chiral boson
and the
U(1) current
, as follows,

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}}\frac{a_{n}}{n}x^{-n}+a_{0}\log x+\bar{a}_{0}

\displaystyle\implies\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}b}\left[\tr\log(x - X)-\frac{b}{g}V(x)\right]\,,

(3.73a)

\displaystyle J(x)

\displaystyle=\partial\phi(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{a_{n}}{x^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\implies\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}b}\left[\tr\left(\frac{1}{x-X}\right)-\frac{b}{g}V^{\prime}(x)\right]\,.

(3.73b)

Furthermore, we also define the energy-momentum tensor

\displaystyle T(x)=\frac{1}{2}{:JJ:}(x)+\rho\partial J(x)=:\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{L_{n}}{x^{n+2}}\,,\qquad\rho=\sqrt{2}(b-b^{-1})\,,

(3.74)

where we denote the normal ordering symbol by
{:-:}
, the annihilation operators are placed to right, the creation operators are placed to left in this symbol.
Recalling the operator product expansion (OPE) of the current operators,

\displaystyle J(x)J(x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}+\text{regular}\,,

(3.75)

the normal ordering is given as follows,

\displaystyle{:JJ:}(x)=\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}\left[J(x)J(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}\right]\,.

(3.76)

It turns out that the generators
\{L_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
written in terms of the oscillators,

\displaystyle L_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}{:a_{m}a_{n-m}:}-\rho(n+1)a_{n}\,,

(3.77)

obey the algebraic relation of the Virasoro algebra,

\displaystyle[L_{n},L_{m}]=(n-m)L_{n+m}+\frac{c}{12}n(n^{2}-1)\delta_{n+m,0}\,,

(3.78)

with the central charge

\displaystyle c=1-6(b-b^{-1})^{2}=\begin{cases}1&(\beta=2)\\
-2&(\beta=1,4)\end{cases}\,.

(3.79)

Hence, the energy-momentum tensor plays a role of the generating current of the Virasoro algebra, and the construction of the energy-momentum tensor as a bilinear form of the current operator is called the Sugawara construction.

3.4.2
Vertex operators

We define the vertex operator from the chiral boson,

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\alpha}(x)={:\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\phi(x)}:}\,.

(3.80)

Then, the OPE with the energy-momentum tensor is given by

\displaystyle T(x)\mathsf{V}_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})=\frac{\Delta_{\alpha}}{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}\mathsf{V}_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})+\frac{1}{x-x^{\prime}}\partialderivative{x'}\mathsf{V}_{\alpha}(x^{\prime})+\text{regular}

(3.81)

where the coefficient called the conformal weight
\Delta_{\alpha}
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\alpha+\rho)\,.

(3.82)

From the Virasoro algebra point of view, the conformal weight is given by the eigenvalue of the operator
L_{0}
.
Moreover, the operator annihilated by
L_{n>0}
is called the primary operator, and the vertex operator
\mathsf{V}_{\alpha}(x)
is actually primary.
We remark that there are two possibilities to provide
\Delta_{\alpha}=1
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=-\sqrt{2}b\,,\qquad\alpha_{1}=\sqrt{2}b^{-1}\quad\implies\quad\Delta_{\alpha_{0,1}}=1\,.

(3.83)

Hence, defining the screening current having the conformal weight one,

\displaystyle S_{\sigma}(x)={:\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{\sigma}\phi(x)}:}\,,\qquad\sigma=0,1\,,

(3.84)

the singular part of the OPE with the energy-momentum tensor is written as a total derivative,

\displaystyle T(x)S_{\sigma}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}S_{\sigma}(x^{\prime})+\frac{1}{x-x^{\prime}}\partialderivative{x'}S_{\sigma}(x^{\prime})+\text{regular}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{x'}\left[\frac{1}{x-x^{\prime}}S_{\sigma}(x^{\prime})\right]+\text{regular}\,.

(3.85)

This implies that the screening charge defined by

\displaystyle Q_{\sigma}=\oint\differential{x}S_{\sigma}(x)\,,

(3.86)

does not provide a singular contribution in the OPE, and thus it commutes with the energy-momentum tensor

\displaystyle[T(x),Q_{\sigma}]=0\,.

(3.87)

This is a crucial property that characterizes the Virasoro algebra:
In fact, the Virasoro algebra is defined as a commuting sub-algebra of the Heisenberg algebra, and such a characterization can be applied to more generalized situations (W-algebras).
See, e.g.,
[
BS93
]
.

3.4.3
Construction of matrix model

Let us discuss how to construct the matrix model based on the operator formalism discussed above.
Recalling that the vertex operator product is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{V}_{\alpha}(x)\mathsf{V}_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})}{:\mathsf{V}_{\alpha}(x)\mathsf{V}_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}):}=(x-x^{\prime})^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,,

(3.88)

the screening charge product is given by

\displaystyle Q_{0}^{N}

\displaystyle=\oint_{|x_{1}|<\cdots<|x_{N}|}\differential{X}\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}{:\prod_{i\in[N]}S_{0}(x_{i}):}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\oint\differential{X}\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}{:\prod_{i\in[N]}S_{0}(x_{i}):}\,,

(3.89)

where the integration contour is initially taken in the radial ordering, and then analytically continuated to obtain the second expression.
Hence, defining the
\mathcal{Z}
-state

\displaystyle|\mathcal{Z}_{N}\rangle=Q_{0}^{N}|0\rangle\,,

(3.90)

and the modified dual charged vacuum,
9
9

9

This modified vacuum is realized as a coherent state with respect to the Heisenberg algebra.

\displaystyle\langle\alpha;d+1|a_{-n}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\langle\alpha;d+1|\alpha&(n=0)\\
\displaystyle\langle\alpha;d+1|\frac{\mathfrak{t}_{n}}{\sqrt{2}g}&(n\in[d+1])\\
0&(\text{otherwise})\end{cases}

(3.91)

we obtain the matrix model partition function as a correlation function of the vertex operators,

\displaystyle\langle N\alpha_{0};d+1|\mathcal{Z}_{N}\rangle=\langle N\alpha_{0};d+1|Q_{0}^{N}|0\rangle=\frac{1}{N!}\oint\differential{X}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}\tr V(X)}\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\,,

(3.92)

where the potential function is now given by a finite polynomial function,
V(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{d+1}\frac{\mathfrak{t}_{n}}{n}x^{n}
.
Considering both screening charges
Q_{0,1}
, we instead obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}\rangle

\displaystyle:=Q_{0}^{N}Q_{1}^{M}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!M!}\oint\differential{X}\differential{Y}\frac{\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\Delta_{M}(Y)^{2b^{-2}}}{\prod_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}(x_{i}-y_{j})^{2}}{:\prod_{i\in[N]}S_{0}(x_{i})\prod_{j\in[M]}S_{1}(y_{j}):}\,,

(3.93)

which gives rise to the supermatrix model partition function.

The eigenvalue integral form of supermatrix model partition function is realized using two types of screening charges,

\displaystyle\langle N\alpha_{0}+M\alpha_{1};d+1|\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!M!}\oint\differential{X}\differential{Y}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}\tr V(X)}\mathrm{e}^{+\frac{b^{-1}}{g}\tr V(Y)}\frac{\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\Delta_{M}(Y)^{2b^{-2}}}{\prod_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}(x_{i}-y_{j})^{2}}\,.

(3.94)

We remark that such an integral form of the correlation function is known as the
Dotsenko–Fateev integral formula
in the context of two-dimensional CFT.

3.4.4
Virasoro constraint

Let us consider the action of the energy-momentum tensor on the vacuum state,

\displaystyle T(x)|0\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{L_{n}}{x^{n+2}}|0\rangle\,.

(3.95)

Requiring the regularity at
x=0
, we should have

\displaystyle L_{n}|0\rangle=0\,,\qquad n\geq-1\,.

(3.96)

This condition is rephrased as follows:
The vacuum is interpreted as the primary field with the conformal weight zero, hence
L_{n\geq 0}
annihilates the vacuum.
Furthermore,
L_{-1}
is a generator of the translation realized by the derivative
\partialderivative{x}
.
Hence,
L_{-1}
annihilates the vacuum since it is translation invariant.

Recalling that the
\mathcal{Z}
-state is constructed from the vacuum with the screening charges, that commute with the energy-momentum tensor (and thus the Virasoro generators).
This implies that the
\mathcal{Z}
-state shows the same behavior as in the case of the vacuum (
3.96
).

The
\mathcal{Z}
-state constructed by the screening charges obeys the following relation,

\displaystyle L_{n}|\mathcal{Z}_{N|M}\rangle=0\,,\qquad n\geq-1\,.

(3.97)

This is called the Virasoro constraint for the (super)matrix model.

We see how this Virasoro is obtained in the context of the matrix model.
We start with the following identity,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int\differential{X}\sum_{\ell\in[N]}\partialderivative{x_\ell}\left[x_{\ell}^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}\tr V(X)}\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\differential{X}\sum_{\ell\in[N]}\left[kx_{\ell}^{k-1}-\frac{b}{g}V^{\prime}(x_{\ell})x_{\ell}^{k}+2b^{2}\sum_{j(\neq\ell)}\frac{x_{\ell}^{k}}{x_{\ell}-x_{j}}\right]\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{b}{g}\tr V(X)}\Delta_{N}(X)^{2b^{2}}\,.

(3.98)

Recalling the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j(\neq i)}\frac{2x_{i}^{k}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{x_{i}^{k}-x_{j}^{k}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\tr X^{m}\tr X^{k-m-1}-k\tr X^{k-1}\,,

(3.99)

we obtain the following relation among the expectation values of the matrix moments, called the
loop equation
,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\langle\tr X^{m}\tr X^{k-m-1}\rangle+(b^{-2}-1)k\langle\tr X^{k-1}\rangle-\frac{b^{-1}}{g}\langle\tr X^{k}V^{\prime}(X)\rangle=0\,.

(3.100)

In the operator formalism discussed in Sec.
3.4.1
, we may write this relation as

\displaystyle L_{k-1}\mathcal{Z}_{N}=0\,,\qquad k\geq 0\,,

(3.101)

where we apply the free field realization of the Virasoro generator (
3.77
).
See
[
DE10
]
for the loop equation of the supermatrix model in the case
b=1
.

4
Supergroup gauge theory

In this section, we introduce supergroup gauge theory, gauge theory having supergroup gauge symmetry, and discuss fundamental perspectives.

Differential forms

Let
G
be a Lie supergroup, and the corresponding Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{Lie}G
.
Let
M
be a
d
-dimensional space-time manifold.
The fundamental degrees of freedom of supergroup gauge theory is the one-form connection that takes a value in
\mathfrak{g}
,
A\in\Omega^{1}(M,\mathfrak{g})
.
Then, we define the covariant derivative
D=d+A
and the curvature two-form given by
D^{2}=dA+A^{2}\in\Omega^{2}(M,\mathfrak{g})
.
The
G
-gauge transformation is given by

\displaystyle G\ :\ D\longmapsto gDg^{-1}\,,\qquad F\longmapsto gFg^{-1}\,,\qquad A\longmapsto gAg^{-1}+gdg^{-1}\,,\qquad g\in G\,.

(4.1)

Formally, these expressions are parallel with the ordinary (non-supergroup) gauge theory.
In the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded situation, the connection is then called the
superconnection

[
Qui85
,
MQ86
]
.
Let us discuss the supermatrix realization of these differential forms.
Let
G=\mathrm{U}(n_{0}|n_{1})
for the moment.
In this case, the connection
A
is given by an anti-Hermitian supermatrix,

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}A^{(0)}&\psi\\
\psi^{\dagger}&A^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad A^{(\sigma){\dagger}}=-A^{(\sigma)}\quad\implies\quad A^{\dagger}=-A\,.

(4.2)

We recall that
\bar{\bar{\theta}}=-\theta
for the Grassmann variable (see (
2.3
)).
We remark that
A^{(\sigma)}
is in the adjoint representation of
\mathrm{U}(n_{\sigma})
, and
\psi
is in the bifundamental representation,
\mathrm{U}(n_{0})\times\overline{\mathrm{U}(n_{1})}
.
Moreover, each component is given by

\displaystyle A^{(\sigma)}=A_{\mu}^{(\sigma)}dx^{\mu}\,,\qquad\psi=\psi_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\,.

(4.3)

Hence,
\psi_{\mu}
is a spin-1 fermionic degree of freedom, that is not compatible with the spin-statistics theorem.
The curvature two-form is given by

\displaystyle F=dA+A\wedge A=\begin{pmatrix}dA^{(0)}+A^{(0)}\wedge A^{(0)}+\psi\wedge\psi^{\dagger}&d\psi+A\wedge\psi+\psi\wedge B\\
d\psi^{\dagger}+\psi^{\dagger}\wedge A+B\wedge\psi^{\dagger}&dA^{(1)}+A^{(1)}\wedge A^{(1)}+\psi^{\dagger}\wedge\psi\end{pmatrix}\,.

(4.4)

We may write the wedge product using the anti-symmetrization symbol,

\displaystyle a\wedge b=\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{\mu}b_{\nu}-a_{\nu}b_{\mu}\right)dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=\frac{1}{2}a_{[\mu}b_{\nu]}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}\,.

(4.5)

Therefore, the component of the curvature two-form is given by

\displaystyle F=\frac{1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

(4.6)

where we have

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}F_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}+\psi_{[\mu}\psi^{\dagger}_{\nu]}&\partial_{[\mu}\psi_{\nu]}+A^{(0)}_{[\mu}\psi_{\nu]}+\psi_{[\mu}A^{(1)}_{\nu]}\\
\partial_{[\mu}\psi_{\nu]}^{\dagger}+\psi_{[\mu}^{\dagger}A^{(0)}_{\nu]}+A^{(1)}_{[\mu}\psi^{\dagger}_{\nu]}&F_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}+\psi_{[\mu}^{\dagger}\psi_{\nu]}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(4.7)

We denote the curvature associated with the bosonic subgroup of
G
by
F^{(\sigma)}=dA^{(\sigma)}+A^{(\sigma)}\wedge A^{(\sigma)}=\frac{1}{2}F^{(\sigma)}_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}
, e.g., for
G=\mathrm{U}(n_{0}|n_{1})
,
F^{(\sigma)}\in\Omega^{2}(M,\mathfrak{u}_{n_{\sigma}})
.

In general, we define the commutator for the differential forms as follows:
Let
X=X^{a}\otimes t^{a}\in\Omega^{p}(M,\mathfrak{g})
and
Y=Y^{a}\otimes t_{b}\in\Omega^{q}(M,\mathfrak{g})
with the generators of the Lie superalgebra denoted by
(t^{a})_{a\in[\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}]}
.
Recalling
X^{a}\wedge Y^{b}=(-1)^{pq}(-1)^{|a||b|}Y^{b}\wedge X^{a}
, denoted by
|a|=|X_{a}|
, etc, the commutator for the differential forms is defined by

\displaystyle[X,Y]

\displaystyle=t^{a}t^{b}\otimes X^{a}\wedge Y^{b}-(-1)^{pq}t^{b}t^{a}\otimes Y^{b}\wedge X^{a}

\displaystyle=(t^{a}t^{b}-(-1)^{|a||b|}t^{b}t^{a})\otimes X^{a}\wedge Y^{b}

\displaystyle=[t^{a},t^{b}]\otimes X^{a}\wedge Y^{b}\,.

(4.8)

The commutator in the last line is the supercommutator with respect to the superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
defined in (
2.12
).
For the one-form connection,
A\in\Omega^{1}(M,\mathfrak{g})
, we have

\displaystyle A\wedge A=\frac{1}{2}[A,A]=\frac{1}{2}[t^{a},t^{b}]\otimes A^{a}\wedge A^{b}\,.

(4.9)

Therefore, the component of the curvature two-form is given in the same way as the ordinary case,

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}+[A_{\mu},A_{\nu}]\,,

(4.10)

which takes a value in the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

4.1
Supergroup Yang–Mills theory

We consider the Yang–Mills (YM) action for the supergroup theory,

\displaystyle S_{\text{YM}}=-\frac{1}{g^{2}}\int_{M}\str(F\wedge\star F)\,,

(4.11)

where we define the Hodge star operator,
\star
:
\Omega^{p}(M)\to\Omega^{d-p}(M)
.
Hence, we have the volume form,
F\wedge\star F=\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}d\!\operatorname{vol}(M)
.
In order to impose the invariance under the
G
-gauge transform (
4.1
), we replace the ordinary trace with the supertrace.

From the expression of the two-form curvature (

), we can explicitly write down the YM action in terms of the gauge fields,
(A^{(\sigma)},\psi)
, which would be complicated.
Hence, for the moment, we just write the leading contributions,

\displaystyle S_{\text{YM}}=-\frac{1}{g^{2}}\int_{M}\tr_{0}(F^{(0)}\wedge\star F^{(0)})+\frac{1}{g^{2}}\int_{M}\tr_{1}(F^{(1)}\wedge\star F^{(1)})+\cdots\,,

(4.12)

which are the standard YM actions of the bosonic subgroups
\mathrm{U}(n_{\sigma})
in
G=\mathrm{U}(n_{0}|n_{1})
.
Now we observe that the kinetic term signatures are opposite due to the supertrace property, that means that the spectrum of supergroup YM theory is not bounded.
Such a property was also found in the supermatrix model discussed in Sec.
3
, and thus it seems to be a universal behavior in supergroup theory.
Due to this unboundness, the notion of vacua is not well-defined in this case, and thus we would need a non-perturbative completion for supergroup theory:
10
10

10

Such a situation resembles unstable vacua.
In this case, we should find true (stable) vacua non-perturbatively.

In fact, it has been known that supergroup theory is pertubatively equivalent to the ordinary gauge theory through the analytic continuation, but there would be an essential difference in the non-perturbative regime.

Even though there are no bounded vacua, one can still consider the equation of motion with respect to the YM action, which is given by

\displaystyle D(\star F)=0\,.

(4.13)

This is a second order non-linear PDE on
M
, which is in general difficult to solve.
In the four-dimensional case, we have a special class of solutions, called the (anti-)instanton, given by the solution of the (anti-)self-dual ((A)SD) YM equation,

\displaystyle\star F=\mp F\,.

(4.14)

One can check that the instanton provides a solution of the equation of motion using the Bianchi identity,

\displaystyle D(\star F)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(A)SD}}}{{=}}\mp DF\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Bianchi}}}{{=}}0\,.

(4.15)

We remark that the Bianchi identity still holds for the supergroup case due to the Jacobi identity (
2.14
).
In fact, the instanton plays an essential role in non-perturbative aspects of supergroup gauge theory.
We will discuss the details of instantons in Sec.

4.2
Quiver gauge theory realization

As observed in (
4.12

In fact, supergroup gauge theory has a realization as a quiver gauge theory through analytic continuation.

Quiver gauge theory is a class of gauge theories involving multiple gauge degrees of freedom: The gauge group is given by a product form,
G=\prod_{i}G_{i}
.
In addition to the gauge field, there is another degree of freedom, called the
bifundamental matter

G_{i}\times G_{j}
,
G_{i}\times\overline{G}_{j}

In the case of

\mathrm{U}(n_{\sigma})

\mathrm{U}(n_{0})\times\overline{\mathrm{U}(n_{1})}
and the conjugate.
Hence, these fermionic fields are interpreted as bifundamental matters from this point of view.
Since we have two such fields

\widehat{A}_{1}
quiver gauge theory.

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{g_{0}^{2}}\,,\,\frac{1}{g_{1}^{2}}\right)\ \longrightarrow\ \left(+\frac{1}{g^{2}}\,,\,-\frac{1}{g^{2}}\right)\,.

(4.16)

4.3

String/M-theory provides various insights on non-perturbative aspects of gauge theory.

5
Supergroup instanton counting

As in the case of the ordinary non-supergroup gauge theory, the instanton plays an important role in the study of non-perturbative aspects of supergroup gauge theory.
In this section, we explore several aspects of instantons and their applications to study dynamics of supergroup gauge theory.

5.1
Instanton moduli space

The instanton is a solution of the ASDYM equation (
4.14
).
Writing the SD/ASD part of the curvature two-form as

\displaystyle F_{\pm}=\frac{F\pm\star F}{2}\,,

(5.1)

such that
F=F_{+}+F_{-}
, the ASDYM equation is rewritten as

\displaystyle F_{+}=0\,,

(5.2)

which would be thought of as a “half” flat connection, and hence the corresponding moduli space is expected to have a rich mathematical structure.

Let
M=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
G=\mathrm{U}(n)
.
In order that the YM action becomes finite, we require that the curvature behaves as
F\to 0
as
x\to\infty
.
This implies that the connection approaches to the pure gauge at the boundary,
A\xrightarrow{x\to\infty}gdg^{-1}
, where we obtain a map,
g
:
\partial\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathbb{S}^{3}\to G
.
Knowing that
\pi_{3}(G)=\mathbb{Z}
for any compact simple Lie groups, we can apply a classification on the gauge field with respect to the topological charge

\displaystyle k=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{M}\tr F\wedge F=c_{2}[M]\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

(5.3)

which is called the instanton number, given by the integral of the second Chern class over
M
.
13
13

13

Precisely speaking, the four-manifold
M
should be compact to have
c_{2}[M]\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In this case,
\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
can be mapped to a four-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{4}
through the stereographic projection.
This map does not violate the ASD property since it is a conformal map.
Under this map, the boundary of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is mapped to the north pole (marked point) on
\mathbb{S}^{4}
, where we fix the gauge as
A=gdg^{-1}
(called the framing).

Here, we focus on the ASD instanton solution having a positive charge
k\geq 0
.
The SD anti-instanton solution is similarly obtained by changing the orientation of the manifold.
Hence, the moduli space of instantons has a decomposition

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{G}=\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{M}_{G,k}\,,

(5.4)

where each topological sector of the moduli space is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{G,k}=\{A\in\Omega^{1}(M,\mathfrak{g})\mid F_{+}=0,c_{2}[M]=k\}/\mathcal{G}\,.

(5.5)

We remark that the
\mathcal{G}
-quotient means the quotient with respect to the equivalent class under
G
-gauge transformation.

5.2
ADHM construction of instanton

Although the definition of the moduli space (

In order to discuss such an instanton moduli space, we can apply the ADHM construction, which is a systematic approach to obtain instanton solutions

]

See, e.g.,
[
DHKM02
]
for an extended review on this subject.
For the

\displaystyle N=\mathbb{C}^{n}\,,\qquad K=\mathbb{C}^{k}\,.

(5.6)

The fundamental degrees of freedom in this construction are the linear maps associated with these vector spaces,

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Hom}(K,K)\oplus\operatorname{Hom}(K,K)\oplus\operatorname{Hom}(N,K)\oplus\operatorname{Hom}(K,N)

\displaystyle\ni(B_{1},B_{2},I,J)\,,

(5.7)

where we impose the so-called ADHM equations,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathbb{R}}:=[B_{1},B_{1}^{\dagger}]+[B_{2},B_{2}^{\dagger}]+II^{\dagger}-J^{\dagger}J\,,

(5.8a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathbb{C}}:=[B_{1},B_{2}]+IJ\,.

(5.8b)

We remark that these equations are invariant under the

\displaystyle g\cdot(B_{1},B_{2},I,J)=(gB_{1}g^{-1},gB_{2}g^{-1},gI,Jg^{-1})\,,\qquad g\in\mathrm{U}(k)\,.

(5.9)

We call

the

Based on these degrees of freedom, we have an alternative description of the instanton moduli space,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{G,k}=\{(B_{1},B_{2},I,J)\in X\mid\mu_{\mathbb{R}}=0,\mu_{\mathbb{C}}=0\}/\!\!/\!\!/\mathrm{U}(k)\,.

(5.10)

The triple slash means the hyper-Kähler quotient with respect to three moment maps,

We show that the ASD connection can be constructed from these ADHM variables.
We define the zero-dimensional Dirac operator,

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}-z_{1}&B_{2}-z_{2}&I\\

(5.11)

(5.12)

where we define the quarternion basis,

\displaystyle\sigma=(\mathrm{i}\vec{\sigma},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}})\,,\qquad\bar{\sigma}=(-\mathrm{i}\vec{\sigma},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}})\,.

(5.13)

Their product is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}\bar{\sigma}_{\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}+\mathrm{i}\eta_{\mu\nu}^{(+)}\,,\qquad\bar{\sigma}_{\mu}{\sigma}_{\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}+\mathrm{i}\eta_{\mu\nu}^{(-)}\,,

(5.14)

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}D=\Delta\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,,

(5.15)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=(B_{1}-z_{1})(B_{1}^{\dagger}-\bar{z}_{1})+(B_{2}-z_{2})(B_{2}^{\dagger}-\bar{z}_{2})+II^{\dagger}

(5.16)

Therefore, the asymptotic behavior is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}D^{\dagger}=\{\Psi:N\to K\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus N\mid D^{\dagger}\Psi=0,\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi=\mathbbm{1}_{N}\}\,.

We define the projector from

\displaystyle P:=\Psi\Psi^{\dagger}=\mathbbm{1}_{K\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus N}-D(\Delta^{-1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}})D^{\dagger}\,,

(5.19)

\displaystyle F=dA+A\wedge A=d\Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}_{K\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus N}-\Psi\Psi^{\dagger})d\Psi=\Psi^{\dagger}(dD)(\Delta^{-1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}})(dD^{\dagger})\Psi\,.

(5.21)

(5.22)

In addition, applying Osborn’s formula

5.4
Equivariant localization

Based on the description of the instanton moduli space, we apply the equivariant localization formalism to compute the partition function of supergroup gauge theory.

In the Euclidean path integral formalism, the partition function of
G
-gauge theory is given as follows,

where we consider the case

Writing the one-form connection in the form of

k

In this decomposition, the weight with the action is written by

Noticing that the integral over

\mathfrak{M}_{G,k}

14
14

14

The anti-instanton partition function shall be obtained by the complex conjugate of the instanton partition function:

\displaystyle Z_{k}=\int_{\mathfrak{M}_{G,k}}1=\operatorname{vol}(\mathfrak{M}_{G,k})\,.

As mentioned in Sec.

The instanton partition function corresponds to the trivial insertion

In the presence of the fundamental matter, we add the equivariant Euler class of the matter bundle

For

5.4.2
Equivariant fixed points

We turn to more details on the instanton moduli space.
We denote the fiber of the cotangent bundle at the marked point

by

\displaystyle Q=T_{o}^{\vee}\mathbb{C}^{2}=Q_{1}\oplus Q_{2}

(5.43)

with the character
\operatorname{ch}Q_{i}=q_{i}=\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon_{i}}
(

We use the notation

\displaystyle Q_{12}=Q_{1}\otimes Q_{2}=\det Q\,,\qquad

\displaystyle q_{12}=q_{1}q_{2}\quad(\epsilon_{12}=\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})\,,

(5.44a)

\displaystyle P_{i}=\wedge Q_{i}=1-Q_{i}\quad(i=1,2)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle P_{12}=P_{1}P_{2}\,.

(5.44b)

Then, the equivariant group actions on the moduli space are given by

\displaystyle g\cdot(B_{1},B_{2},I,J)

\displaystyle=(gB_{1}g^{-1},gB_{2}g^{-1},gI,Jg^{-1})\,,

\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{GL}(K)\,,

(5.45a)

\displaystyle h\cdot(B_{1},B_{2},I,J)

\displaystyle=(B_{1},B_{2},Ih^{-1},hJ)\,,

\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{GL}(N)\,,

(5.45b)

\displaystyle(q_{1},q_{2})\cdot(B_{1},B_{2},I,J)

\displaystyle=(q_{1}^{-1}B_{1},q_{2}^{-1}B_{2},I,q_{12}^{-1}J)\,,

\displaystyle(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathsf{T}_{Q}\subset\mathrm{GL}(Q)\,.

(5.45c)

Parametrizing these group elements as
g=\mathrm{e}^{\phi}
,
h=\mathrm{e}^{a}
,
q_{i}=\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon_{i}}
(
i=1,2
), namely

,

,
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\in\operatorname{Lie}\mathsf{T}_{Q}
, the fixed point equations are given as follows,

\displaystyle[\phi,B_{i}]-\epsilon_{i}B_{i}

\displaystyle=0\,,\qquad(i=1,2)

(5.46a)

\displaystyle\phi I-Ia

\displaystyle=0\,,

(5.46b)

\displaystyle-J\phi+(a-\epsilon_{12})J

\displaystyle=0\,.

(5.46c)

We apply the basis diagonalizing

, which corresponds to

, and

.
We have the (left/right) eigenvalue equations,

and

.
Moreover, using (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\phi B_{1}^{i-1}B_{2}^{j-1}I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(a_{\alpha}+(i-1)\epsilon_{1}+(j-1)\epsilon_{2})B_{1}^{i-1}B_{2}^{j-1}I_{\alpha}\,,

(5.47a)

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}B_{1}^{i-1}B_{2}^{j-1}\phi

\displaystyle=J_{\alpha}B_{1}^{i-1}B_{2}^{j-1}(a_{\alpha}-i\epsilon_{1}-j\epsilon_{2})\,.

(5.47b)

Applying the stability condition,

, and recalling

k
eigenvectors in the form of

Applying the co-stability condition, we instead obtain

(5.58)

Another expression is given as a finite sum,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}\mathsf{Y}\Big|_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}}x-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}}xq_{12}&(\text{stability})\\
\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}}xq_{12}-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}}x&(\text{co-stability})\end{cases}

(5.59)

where we define the addable and removable boundary of the partition,

\displaystyle\partial_{+}\lambda=\{(i,\lambda_{\alpha,i}+1)\mid\lambda_{\alpha,i-1}>\lambda_{\alpha,i}\}_{\alpha\in[n],i\in\mathbb{N}}\,,\quad\partial_{-}\lambda=\{(i,\lambda_{\alpha,i})\mid\lambda_{\alpha,i+1}<\lambda_{\alpha,i}\}_{\alpha\in[n],i\in\mathbb{N}}

(5.60)

where we put
\lambda_{\alpha,0}=\infty

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\{\mathrm{e}^{a_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{i-1}q_{2}^{j-1}\mid(i,j)\in\partial_{+}\lambda\}&(\text{stability})\\
\displaystyle\{\mathrm{e}^{a_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{-i}q_{2}^{-j}\mid(i,j)\in\partial_{+}\lambda\}&(\text{co-stability})\end{cases}\,,

(5.61a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\{\mathrm{e}^{a_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{i-1}q_{2}^{j-1}\mid(i,j)\in\partial_{-}\lambda\}&(\text{stability})\\
\displaystyle\{\mathrm{e}^{a_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{-i}q_{2}^{-j}\mid(i,j)\in\partial_{-}\lambda\}&(\text{co-stability})\end{cases}\,.

(5.61b)

The tangent bundle is then obtained from the observable sheaf as follows,

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}:=\frac{\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}\mathsf{Y}}{P_{12}}=-T\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}+\frac{N^{\vee}N}{P_{12}}\,.

(5.62)

The last term

The combination of

corresponds to the adjoint representation of GL group.
In order to construct the SO/Sp gauge theory, we consider the anti-symmetric and symmetric tensor product, which yield the adjoint representation of SO/Sp group

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\text{SO}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathsf{Y}^{2}-\mathsf{Y}^{[2]}}{P_{12}}\,,\qquad\mathsf{V}_{\text{Sp}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathsf{Y}^{2}+\mathsf{Y}^{[2]}}{P_{12}}\,,

(5.63)

where we denote the degree-

Adams operation to

Hence, the tangent bundle at the fixed point is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\lambda}=-T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}+\frac{N^{\vee}N}{P_{12}}=\frac{\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}\mathsf{Y}}{P_{12}}\Bigg|_{\lambda}=\frac{P_{1}^{\vee}}{P_{2}}\mathsf{X}^{\vee}\mathsf{X}\,.

(5.64)

Denoting the total fixed point configuration space by
\mathfrak{M}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}^{\mathsf{T}}
, the full partition function is given as follows.

The full partition function of pure SYM gauge theory is given by summation over the fixed point contributions,

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathsf{T}}}\mathfrak{q}^{|\lambda|}\,\mathbb{I}[\mathsf{V}_{\lambda}]\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle(5d)}}{{=}}\ \sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathsf{T}}}\mathfrak{q}^{|\lambda|}\prod_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}_{\lambda}\times\mathcal{X}_{\lambda}}\frac{(x^{\prime}/x;q_{2}^{-1})_{\infty}}{(q_{1}x^{\prime}/x;q_{2}^{-1})_{\infty}}\,,\qquad|q_{2}|>1\,,

(5.65)

where
(z;q)_{\infty}

For four and six-dimensional cases, we obtain a similar infinite product formula based on the gamma and elliptic gamma functions, respectively.

5.4.4
Combinatorial formula

We have seen that the partition function is obtained by applying the index functor to the tangent bundle.
The full partition function is written in the closed form, whereas it involves infinite product as in (
5.65
).
In fact, the infinite product contribution originates from the perturbative part, and one can subtract the finite rational contribution to be identified with the instanton partition function.
For this purpose, we evaluate the character of the tangent bundle with the fix point configuration (see, e.g.
[
Nak99
]
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in[n]}\mathrm{e}^{a_{\alpha\beta}}\Xi(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1},q_{2})\,,

(5.66)

where we denote
a_{\alpha\beta}=a_{\alpha}-a_{\beta}
.
The combinatorial factor is given by

\displaystyle\Xi(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ch}\left(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\beta}}+Q_{12}^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\alpha}})^{\vee}-P_{12}^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\alpha}})^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\beta}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\lambda_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{\mathbf{l}_{\beta}(s)}q_{2}^{-\mathbf{a}_{\alpha}(s)-1}+\sum_{s\in\lambda_{\beta}}q_{1}^{-\mathbf{l}_{\alpha}(s)-1}q_{2}^{\mathbf{a}_{\beta}(s)}\,,

(5.67)

where we define the arm and leg lengths,

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\alpha}(i,j)=\lambda_{\alpha,i}-j\,,\qquad\mathbf{l}_{\alpha}(i,j)=\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,j}-i\,.

(5.68)

We remark that the same combinatorial factor is obtained starting from the co-stability condition.
We observe the following relation,

\displaystyle\Xi(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})=\Xi(\check{\lambda}_{\alpha},\check{\lambda}_{\beta};q_{2,1})=q_{12}^{-1}\Xi(\lambda_{\beta},\lambda_{\alpha};q_{1,2}^{-1})\,.

(5.69)

Hence, applying the index functor, we obtain the
k
-instanton partition function, a.k.a.,
Nekrasov partition function
.

The instanton partition function has the following combinatorial formula (Nekrasov partition function),

\displaystyle Z_{k}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}^{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbb{I}[-T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}]=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}^{\mathsf{T}}}\prod_{\alpha,\beta\in[n]}\frac{1}{\mathsf{N}_{\lambda_{\alpha}\lambda_{\beta}}(a_{\alpha\beta};\epsilon_{1,2})}\,,

(5.70)

where we define the combinatorial factor,

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}_{\lambda_{\alpha}\lambda_{\beta}}(z;\epsilon_{1,2})

\displaystyle=\prod_{s\in\lambda_{\alpha}}[z+\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{l}_{\beta}(s)-\epsilon_{2}(\mathbf{a}_{\alpha}(s)+1)]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{s\in\lambda_{\beta}}[z-\epsilon_{1}(\mathbf{l}_{\beta}(s)+1)+\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{a}_{\alpha}(s)]\,.

(5.71)

5.4.5
Contour integral formula

It has been known that the instanton partition function can be described in the form of contour integral, a.k.a., LMNS (Losev–Moore–Nekrasov–Shatashvili) formula.
For this purpose, we use the vector bundles on the moduli space without substituting the fixed point value, i.e.,
\operatorname{ch}K=\sum_{I\in[k]}\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{I}}
.
Then, we compute the index functor of the tangent bundle to the
k
-instanton moduli space, which gives rise to the contour integral over the maximal Cartan torus
\mathsf{T}_{K}\subset\mathrm{GL}(K)
.

The instanton partition function has the following contour integral formula (LMNS formula),

\displaystyle Z_{k}=\mathbb{I}[-T\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}]=\frac{1}{k!}\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]^{k}}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]^{k}}\oint_{\mathsf{T}_{K}}\differential{\underline{\phi}}\frac{1}{P(\underline{\phi})\widetilde{P}(\underline{\phi}+\epsilon_{12})}\prod_{I\neq J}^{k}\mathscr{S}(\phi_{IJ})^{-1}\,,

(5.72)

where we define the gauge polynomials

\displaystyle P(\underline{\phi})=\mathbb{I}[N^{\vee}K]=\prod_{I\in[k],\alpha\in[n]}[\phi_{I}-a_{\alpha}]\,,\quad\widetilde{P}(\underline{\phi})=\mathbb{I}[K^{\vee}N]=\prod_{I\in[k],\alpha\in[n]}[-\phi_{I}+a_{\alpha}]

(5.73)

and the
\mathscr{S}
-function,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(z)=\frac{[z-\epsilon_{1,2}]}{[z][z-\epsilon_{12}]}\,.

(5.74)

We use the notation,
[z-\epsilon_{1,2}]=[z-\epsilon_{1}][z-\epsilon_{2}]
.
The integral measure is defined as
\differential{\underline{\phi}}=\prod_{I\in[k]}\differential{\phi}_{I}/2\pi\mathrm{i}
, which is understood as the index of the zero mode appearing from
K^{\vee}K
.
We have the Weyl group volume,
k!=|\mathfrak{S}_{k}|
.
In fact, the poles of the integrand are consistent with the fixed points, such that we take the residues at
\phi_{I}=\phi_{I-1}+\epsilon_{1,2}
or
\phi_{I}=a_{\alpha}
, which yields the map,
\{\phi_{I}\}_{I\in[k]}\to\{a_{\alpha}+(i-1)\epsilon_{1}+(j-1)\epsilon_{2}\}_{\alpha\in[n],(i,j)\in\lambda_{alpha}}
.
This multi-variable contour integral is also understood as the Jeffrey–Kirwan residue prescription
[
JK95
]
.
See, e.g.,
[
BEHT15
,
HKY15
,
HKKP15
,
Nak15
]
for details.

5.4.7

We apply the formalism discussed above to supergroup gauge theory.

|q_{1}|<1
and

\displaystyle Z_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\prod_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}^{0}_{\lambda}\times\mathcal{X}^{0}_{\lambda}}\frac{(x^{\prime}/x;q_{2}^{-1})_{\infty}}{(q_{1}x^{\prime}/x;q_{2}^{-1})_{\infty}}\prod_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\check{\mathcal{X}}^{1}_{\lambda}\times\check{\mathcal{X}}^{1}_{\lambda}}\frac{(q_{12}x^{\prime}/x;q_{1})_{\infty}}{(q_{1}x^{\prime}/x;q_{1})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{(x,x^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{0}_{\lambda}\times\check{\mathcal{X}}_{\lambda}^{1}}\left(1-\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}\right)^{-1}\left(1-q_{12}\frac{x}{x^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\,.

(5.98)

We remark a similarity of this partition function to the supermatrix model discussed in Sec.
3
.
We will see further similarities from the underlying algebraic point of view.
See Sec.
6.3
.

Combinatorial formula

We consider the combinatorial formula, which gives rise to the finite rational expression,

\displaystyle\operatorname{sch}T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}=\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}=0,1}(-1)^{\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\in[n_{\sigma}],\beta\in[n_{\sigma^{\prime}}]}\mathrm{e}^{a^{\sigma}_{\alpha}-a^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\beta}}\Xi_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})\,,

(5.99)

where the diagonal combinatorial factors are given by

\displaystyle\Xi_{00}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})=\Xi(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})\,,\qquad\Xi_{11}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})=\Xi(\lambda_{\beta},\lambda_{\alpha};q_{1,2})\,.

(5.100)

This is clear from the original expression of
\Xi
in terms of the polynomial ideals (
5.50
) and (
5.67
).
See Sec.
5.4.4
.
The off-diagonal factors are given as follows (see
[
KP19
,
Kim21
]
),

\displaystyle\Xi_{01}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ch}\left(Q_{12}^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\beta}})^{\vee}+Q_{12}^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\alpha}})^{\vee}-P_{12}^{\vee}Q_{12}^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\alpha}})^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\beta}})^{\vee}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{\lambda_{\beta,1}}\left[q_{1}^{-\check{\lambda}_{\beta,j^{\prime}}-i}q_{2}^{-\lambda_{\alpha,i}-j^{\prime}}-q_{1}^{-i}q_{2}^{-j^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{(i,j)\in\lambda_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{-i}q_{2}^{-\lambda_{\beta,1}-j}+\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\lambda_{\beta}}q_{1}^{-\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}-i^{\prime}}q_{2}^{-j^{\prime}}\,,

(5.101a)

\displaystyle\Xi_{10}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ch}\left(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\beta}}+\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\alpha}}-P_{12}^{\vee}(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\alpha}})(\mathsf{I}_{\emptyset}/\mathsf{I}_{\lambda_{\beta}})\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{\lambda_{\beta,1}}\left[q_{1}^{\check{\lambda}_{\beta,j^{\prime}}+i-1}q_{2}^{\lambda_{\alpha,i}+j^{\prime}-1}-q_{1}^{i-1}q_{2}^{j^{\prime}-1}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{(i,j)\in\lambda_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{i-1}q_{2}^{\lambda_{\beta,1}+j-1}+\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\lambda_{\beta}}q_{1}^{\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}+i^{\prime}-1}q_{2}^{j^{\prime}-1}\,.

(5.101b)

In contrast to the diagonal part
\Xi_{00(11)}
, further simplification does not occur for these off-diagonal ones.
We remark that these off-diagonal factors are symmetric under
\lambda_{\alpha}\leftrightarrow\lambda_{\beta}
,

\displaystyle\Xi_{01}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})=\Xi_{01}(\lambda_{\beta},\lambda_{\alpha};q_{1,2})\,,\qquad\Xi_{10}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})=\Xi_{10}(\lambda_{\beta},\lambda_{\alpha};q_{1,2})\,,

(5.102)

and we have the relation,

\displaystyle q_{12}\,\Xi_{01}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2})=\Xi_{10}(\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta};q_{1,2}^{-1})\,.

(5.103)

Applying the index functor to this expression, we obtain the instanton partition function.
In addition to the Nekrasov factor for the diagonal part (
5.71
), we also define the off-diagonal combinatorial factor,

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}^{01}_{\lambda_{\alpha}\lambda_{\beta}}(z;\epsilon_{1,2})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}}\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{\lambda_{\beta,1}}\frac{[z-i\epsilon_{1}-j^{\prime}\epsilon_{2}]}{[z-(\check{\lambda}_{\beta,j^{\prime}}+i)\epsilon_{1}-(\lambda_{\alpha,i}+j^{\prime})]}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda_{\alpha}}[z-i\epsilon_{1}-(\lambda_{\beta,1}+j)\epsilon_{2}]\prod_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\lambda_{\alpha}}[z-(\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}+i^{\prime})\epsilon_{1}-j^{\prime}\epsilon_{2}]\,,

(5.104)

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}^{10}_{\lambda_{\alpha}\lambda_{\beta}}(z;\epsilon_{1,2})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}}\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{\lambda_{\beta,1}}\frac{[z+(i-1)\epsilon_{1}+(j^{\prime}-1)\epsilon_{2}]}{[z+(\check{\lambda}_{\beta,j^{\prime}}+i-1)\epsilon_{1}+(\lambda_{\alpha,i}+j^{\prime}-1)]}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda_{\alpha}}[z+(i-1)\epsilon_{1}+(\lambda_{\beta,1}+j-1)\epsilon_{2}]\prod_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\lambda_{\alpha}}[z+(\check{\lambda}_{\alpha,1}+i^{\prime}-1)\epsilon_{1}+(j^{\prime}-1)\epsilon_{2}]\,.

(5.105)

The combinatorial form of the
k
-instanton partition function for
\mathrm{U}(n_{0}|n_{1})
supergroup gauge theory is given as follows.

The instanton partition function of
\mathrm{U}(n_{0}|n_{1})
gauge theory has the following combinatorial expression,

\displaystyle Z_{k}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}^{\mathsf{T}}}\frac{\prod_{\alpha\in[n_{0}],\beta\in[n_{1}]}\mathsf{N}^{01}_{\lambda_{\alpha}^{0}\lambda_{\beta}^{1}}(a_{\alpha}^{0}-a_{\beta}^{1};\epsilon_{1,2})\mathsf{N}^{10}_{\lambda_{\beta}^{1}\lambda_{\alpha}^{0}}(a_{\beta}^{1}-a_{\alpha}^{0};\epsilon_{1,2})}{\prod_{\sigma=0,1}\prod_{\alpha,\beta\in[n_{\sigma}]}\mathsf{N}_{\lambda_{\alpha}^{\sigma}\lambda_{\beta}^{\sigma}}(a_{\alpha}^{\sigma}-a_{\beta}^{\sigma};\epsilon_{1,2})}\,.

(5.106)

We see that the denominator factors are the standard gauge node contributions, whereas the numerator factors are analogous, but not identical to the bifundamental hypermultiplet contributions.

6.2
Non-perturbative Schwinger–Dyson equation

The instanton partition function is given by summation over the fixed point configurations, which can be related to each other by the process to add/remove instantons.
We discuss the behavior of instanton partition function under such a non-perturbative process of adding/removing instantons, which gives rise to functional relations that we call the
non-perturbative Schwinger–Dyson equations

[
KMZ12
,
KMZ13
,
BMZ16
,
Nek16
,
KP18c
]
.

6.2.1
Adding/removing instantons

We consider instanton-adding process by shifting the vector space,
K\to K+V
with
V=\mathbb{C}^{v}
, where
v
is the number of instantons that we add to the configuration.
Under this shift, the tangent bundle at the fixed point
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}^{\mathsf{T}}
behaves as follows,

\displaystyle\delta_{V}T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}V+Q_{12}^{\vee}V^{\vee}\mathsf{Y}-P_{12}^{-1}V^{\vee}V\,.

(6.6)

Hence, this shift defines a map,
\delta_{V}
:
\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}\to\mathfrak{M}_{n,k+v}
.
Applying the index functor, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{V}Z_{\lambda}=\mathbb{I}[-\delta_{V}T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}]=\frac{Z_{\lambda+v}}{Z_{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{v!}\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]^{v}}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]^{v}}\oint\differential{\underline{\phi}}\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(\underline{\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(\underline{\phi}+\epsilon_{12})}\prod_{I\neq J}^{v}\mathscr{S}(\phi_{IJ})^{-1}\,,

(6.7)

where we denote by
Z_{\lambda+v}
the instanton partition function contribution with the charge
k+v
that can be obtained from the configuration
\lambda
by adding
v
instantons, and we define the
\mathscr{Y}
-function with the line bundle
\mathsf{x}
whose Chern root is given by
x
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}\mathbf{x}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:obs_sheaf_def}}}{{=}}\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\lambda}^{[\log]}}\frac{[x-x^{\prime}]}{[x-x^{\prime}-\epsilon_{1}]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:obs_sheaf_comb}}}{{=}}\frac{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}^{[\log]}}[x-x^{\prime}]}{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}^{[\log]}}[x-x^{\prime}-\epsilon_{12}]}\,,

(6.8a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[\mathbf{x}^{\vee}\mathsf{Y}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:obs_sheaf_def}}}{{=}}\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\lambda}^{[\log]}}\frac{[x^{\prime}-x]}{[x^{\prime}-x+\epsilon_{1}]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:obs_sheaf_comb}}}{{=}}\frac{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}^{[\log]}}[x^{\prime}-x]}{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}^{[\log]}}[x^{\prime}-x+\epsilon_{12}]}\,,

(6.8b)

with the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\bullet}^{[\log]}=\{\log x\mid x\in\mathcal{X}_{\bullet}\}\,.

(6.9)

We have the relation for these two
\mathscr{Y}
-functions,

\displaystyle\mathscr{Y}(z)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n}\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}(z)&(4d)\\
(-1)^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-nz}\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{\alpha\in[n]}a_{\alpha}}\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}(z)&(5d\ \&\ 6d)\\
\end{cases}.

(6.10)

We denote
\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(\underline{\phi})=\prod_{I\in[v]}\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(\phi_{I})
and
\differential{\underline{\phi}}=\prod_{I\in[v]}\differential{\phi}_{I}/2\pi\mathrm{i}
.
We focus on the simplest case,
v=1
.
In this case, we evaluate the contour integral surrounding the poles of
1/\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(\phi)
based on the finite product form of the
\mathscr{Y}
-function,

\displaystyle\delta_{V}Z_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}\oint\frac{\differential{\phi}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}({\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}({\phi}+\epsilon_{12})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}^{[\log]}}\frac{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}^{[\log]}}[x-x^{\prime}-\epsilon_{12}][x^{\prime}-x]}{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}^{[\log]}\backslash\{x\}}[x-x^{\prime}][x^{\prime}-x-\epsilon_{12}]}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}^{[\log]}}\frac{-1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(x)\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x+\epsilon_{12})}\,,

(6.11)

where the configuration
\lambda^{\prime}
is obtained by shift (adding an instanton).
The last equality is shown as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}\frac{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}^{[\log]}}[x-x^{\prime}-\epsilon_{12}][x^{\prime}-x]}{\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda}^{[\log]}\backslash\{x\}}[x-x^{\prime}][x^{\prime}-x-\epsilon_{12}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(x)\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(x+\epsilon_{12})}\times\lim_{x^{\prime\prime}\to x}\frac{[x-x^{\prime\prime}][x^{\prime\prime}-x-\epsilon_{12}]}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(x)\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(x+\epsilon_{12})}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\lim_{x^{\prime\prime}\to x}\frac{[x-x^{\prime\prime}][x^{\prime\prime}-x-\epsilon_{12}][x^{\prime\prime}-x-\epsilon_{1,2}]}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}][x^{\prime\prime}-x][x^{\prime\prime}-x-\epsilon_{12}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(x)\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x+\epsilon_{12})}\,.

(6.12)

From this expression, we identify
Z_{\lambda^{\prime}}/Z_{\lambda}=-1/\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(x)\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x+\epsilon_{12})
, from which we obtain

\displaystyle\res_{x^{\prime}\to x}\left[Z_{\lambda^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}+\epsilon_{12})+Z_{\lambda}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(x^{\prime})}\right]=0\,.

(6.13)

Summing up all the configurations, we see that

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}(x):=\left<\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}(x+\epsilon_{12})+\frac{\mathfrak{q}}{\mathscr{Y}(x)}\right>

(6.14)

is a pole-free regular function in
x
, where we define the gauge theory average of the observable by
\left<\mathcal{O}\right>=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathsf{T}}}Z_{\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
.
The combination of the
\mathscr{Y}
-functions yielding a pole-free function is called the (average of)
qq
-character (of
A_{1}
quiver in this case)
[
Nek16
]
.
21
21

21

The character of the fundamental representation of SL(2) is given by
y+y^{-1}
.
In general, the Seiberg–Witten geometry of quiver gauge theory is described using the fundamental representation characters of the Lie group associated with the quiver structure
[
NP12

.
Considering the situation
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=(\hbar,0)

[

of Yangian/quantum affine algebra

The

Physically, the

[

AKKS18

HK22

Indeed, the Seiberg–Witten curve is obtained in the classical limit
\epsilon_{1,2}\to 0

y+\mathfrak{q}/y=\det(x - \phi)
by identifying the characteristic polynomial by
\mathscr{T}(x)
.
22

Precisely speaking, we should convert
\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}
to
\mathscr{Y}
using the relation (

We remark that in this limit, we can apply the saddle point analysis, so that the observable average can be replaced by the on-shell value with the saddle point configuration

We may consider the removing process in a similar way.
In this case, we consider the shift of the form,
K\to K-V
.
The tangent bundle behaves as

\displaystyle\delta_{-V}T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}V-Q_{12}^{\vee}V^{\vee}\mathsf{Y}-P_{12}^{-1}V^{\vee}V\,,

(6.15)

where
\delta_{-V}
:
\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}\to\mathfrak{M}_{n,k-v}
, and the index functor yields

\displaystyle\delta_{-V}Z_{\lambda}=\mathbb{I}[-\delta_{-V}T_{\lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}]=\frac{Z_{\lambda-v}}{Z_{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{v!}\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]^{v}}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]^{v}}\oint\differential{\underline{\phi}}\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(\underline{\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(\underline{\phi}+\epsilon_{12})\prod_{I\neq J}^{v}\mathscr{S}(\phi_{IJ})^{-1}\,.

(6.16)

In the case of
v=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{-V}Z_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}\oint\frac{\differential{\phi}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}({\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}({\phi}+\epsilon_{12})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda}^{[\log]}}-\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(x+\epsilon_{12})\,,

(6.17)

where we denote the configuration obtained by removing an instanton by
\lambda^{\prime}
.
We can similarly obtain the

6.2.2
Supergroup analysis

We consider the adding/removing instanton process for supergroup gauge theory.
Denoting the observable sheaf
\mathsf{Y}=\mathsf{Y}_{0}\oplus\mathsf{Y}_{1}
, the character of even and odd part is given as follows,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}\mathsf{Y}_{0}\Big|_{\lambda}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{0}}}x-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{0}}}xq_{12}\,,\qquad\operatorname{ch}\mathsf{Y}_{1}\Big|_{\lambda}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{1}}}xq_{12}-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{1}}}x

(6.18)

where we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{\pm}\lambda^{0}}=\{\mathrm{e}^{a_{\alpha}^{0}}q_{1}^{i-1}q_{2}^{j-1}\mid(i,j)\in\partial_{\pm}\lambda^{0}\}\,,\qquad\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{\pm}\lambda^{1}}=\{\mathrm{e}^{a_{\alpha}^{1}}q_{1}^{-i}q_{2}^{-j}\mid(i,j)\in\partial_{\pm}\lambda^{1}\}\,.

(6.19)

The
\mathscr{Y}
-function is then defined as follows,

\displaystyle\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{0,\lambda^{0}}(z)}{\mathscr{Y}_{1,\lambda^{1}}(z)}\,,\qquad\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{0,\lambda^{0}}(z)}{\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{1,\lambda^{1}}(z)}\,,

(6.20)

where each factor is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{Y}_{0,\lambda^{0}}(z)=\frac{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{0}}^{[\log]}}[z-x]}{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{0}}^{[\log]}}[z-x-\epsilon_{12}]}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\mathscr{Y}_{1,\lambda^{1}}(z)=\frac{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{1}}^{[\log]}}[z-x-\epsilon_{12}]}{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{1}}^{[\log]}}[z-x]}\,,

(6.21a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{0,\lambda^{0}}(z)=\frac{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{0}}^{[\log]}}[x-z]}{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{0}}^{[\log]}}[x-z+\epsilon_{12}]}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{1,\lambda^{1}}(z)=\frac{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{1}}^{[\log]}}[x-z+\epsilon_{12}]}{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{1}}^{[\log]}}[x-z]}\,.

(6.21b)

Under the shift
K\to K+V
by
V=\mathbb{C}^{v_{0}|v_{1}}

6.6
).
The resulting contour integral is given as follows,

\displaystyle\delta_{V}Z_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{v_{0,1}!}\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]^{v_{01}}}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]^{v_{01}}}\oint\differential{\underline{\phi}}\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}(\underline{\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}(\underline{\phi}+\epsilon_{12})}\frac{\prod^{I\in[v_{0}]}_{J\in[v_{1}]}\mathscr{S}(\phi^{0}_{I}-\phi^{1}_{J})\mathscr{S}(\phi^{1}_{J}-\phi^{0}_{I})}{\prod_{I\neq J}^{v_{0}}\mathscr{S}(\phi^{0}_{IJ})\prod_{I\neq J}^{v_{1}}\mathscr{S}(\phi^{1}_{IJ})}\,.

(6.22)

The one-dimensional cases
v=(1|0)
and
(0|1)
are simultaneously formulated by

\displaystyle\delta_{V}Z_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}\oint\frac{\differential{\phi}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}_{\lambda}({\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\lambda}({\phi}+\epsilon_{12})}=\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}\oint\frac{\differential{\phi}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{1,\lambda^{1}}({\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{1,\lambda^{1}}({\phi}+\epsilon_{12})}{\mathscr{Y}_{0,\lambda^{0}}({\phi})\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{0,\lambda^{0}}({\phi}+\epsilon_{12})}

\displaystyle=\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]}\oint\frac{\differential{\phi}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{0}}^{[\log]}}[\phi-x-\epsilon_{12}][x-\phi]}{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{0}}^{[\log]}}[\phi-x][x-\phi-\epsilon_{12}]}\frac{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{+}\lambda^{1}}^{[\log]}}[\phi-x-\epsilon_{12}][x-\phi]}{\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\partial_{-}\lambda^{1}}^{[\log]}}[\phi-x][x-\phi-\epsilon_{12}]}\,.

(6.23)

From this expression, we observe that adding an instanton to the positive node (positive instanton) is equivalent to removing an instanton from the negative node (negative instanton).
Similarly, removing a positive instanton is equivalent to adding a negative instanton from this point of view.
Therefore, we obtain the same
qq
-character expression in terms of the total
\mathscr{Y}
-functions,

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}(x)=\left<\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}(x+\epsilon_{12})+\frac{\mathfrak{q}}{\mathscr{Y}(x)}\right>=\left<\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{0}(x+\epsilon_{12})}{\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{1}(x+\epsilon_{12})}+\mathfrak{q}\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{1}(x)}{\mathscr{Y}_{0}(x)}\right>\,,

(6.24)

which is again consistent with
\widehat{A}_{1}
quiver realization in Sec.
4.2
by identifying
\mathscr{Y}_{0,1}(x)
with the
\mathscr{Y}
-functions of
\widehat{A}_{1}
quiver.
Identifying the

-function with the supercharacteristic function of the adjoint scalar,

theory discussed in Sec.

6.2.3
Geometry of

-character

Let us comment on geometric representation theoretical perspectives of the

-character.
We reconsider the shift

\displaystyle\delta_{V}\left(\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}W-T\mathfrak{M}_{n,k}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}V-Q_{12}^{\vee}V^{\vee}\mathsf{Y}-P_{12}^{\vee}V^{\vee}W+P_{12}^{\vee}V^{\vee}V\,,

where we define

, such that

The generic

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{w}=\sum_{v=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{q}^{v}\,\mathsf{T}_{w,v}\,,

where each contribution is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{w,v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[\mathsf{Y}^{\vee}(W-(1+Q_{12}^{\vee})V)-P_{12}^{\vee}V^{\vee}W+P_{12}^{\vee}V^{\vee}V]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v!}\frac{[-\epsilon_{12}]^{v}}{[-\epsilon_{1,2}]^{v}}\oint\differential{\underline{\phi}}\frac{\mathscr{Y}(\underline{\xi})}{\mathscr{Y}(\underline{\phi})\mathscr{Y}(\underline{\phi}-\epsilon_{12})}\mathscr{S}(\underline{\xi}-\underline{\phi})\prod_{I\neq J}^{v}\mathscr{S}(\phi_{IJ})^{-1}\,.

We denote
\mathscr{Y}(\underline{\xi})=\prod_{\alpha\in[w]}\mathscr{Y}(\xi_{\alpha})
,
\mathscr{S}(\underline{\xi}-\underline{\phi})=\prod_{\alpha\in[w],I\in[v]}\mathscr{S}(\xi_{\alpha}-\phi_{I})
, etc.
We remark that the dual function
\widetilde{\mathscr{Y}}
is converted to
\mathscr{Y}
for convenience.
Taking the pole at
\phi_{I}=\xi_{\alpha}
in the contour integral, we obtain the result
[

,

]

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{w,v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{I}\bigsqcup\mathsf{J}=[w]\\
|\mathsf{J}|=v\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathsf{I},\beta\in\mathsf{J}}\mathscr{S}(\xi_{\alpha}-\xi_{\beta})\frac{\prod_{\alpha\in\mathsf{I}}\mathscr{Y}(\xi_{\alpha})}{\prod_{\beta\in\mathsf{J}}\mathscr{Y}(\xi_{\beta}-\epsilon_{12})}\,.

(6.28)

There are
{w\choose v}
contributions in

for given

, which correspond to the fixed points in the cotangent bundle of the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(v,w)
given as a quiver variety of type
A_{1}
.
From the representation theoretical point of view, this corresponds to the degree-
w
tensor product of the fundamental representation of quantum affine algebra

associated with
A_{1}
quiver
[
Nak94a
,
Nak98
,
Nak01a
]

In order to obtain the

See also

]

The contribution to the

Applying the same argument to Sec.

Moreover, we may rewrite this formula as follows,

where we define another genus, an analog of

6.3
Free field realization

Similarly to the matrix model as discussed in Sec.
3.4
, the gauge theory partition function has a similar free field realization.
Let us discuss an operator formalism of gauge theory in this part.

6.3.1
Holomorphic deformation

In the context of four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=2
gauge theory, the holomorphic function, called the
prepotential
, plays a central role to characterize the supersymmetric Lagrangian.
The ordinary SYM theory corresponds to the quadratic prepotential
\mathscr{F}=\tr\Phi^{2}
at UV.
We now consider generic holomorphic deformation of the prepotential,
\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{F}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}\tr\Phi^{n}
.
Even after the deformation, we can still apply the localization computation, and the partition function is given as follows
[
NY03
,
MN07
]
,

\displaystyle Z(t)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathsf{T}}}Z_{\lambda}(t)\,,\qquad Z_{\lambda}(t)=Z_{\lambda}Z^{\text{pot}}_{\lambda}(t)

(6.37)

where we define the potential term

\displaystyle Z^{\text{pot}}_{\lambda}(t)=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}\mathcal{O}_{n,\lambda}\right)\,.

(6.38)

We denote the
\lambda
-fixed point contribution of the chiral ring operator by
\mathcal{O}_{n,\lambda}=\tr\Phi^{n}|_{\lambda}
for 4d, and 5d and 6d analogues are obtained by the Wilson loop and the Wilson surface extending on the circle and the torus.

This
t
-deformed partition function is a generating function of the chiral ring operators,

\displaystyle\left<\mathcal{O}_{n}\right>=\partialderivative{t_n}\log Z(t)\Big|_{t\to 0}\,.

(6.39)

From this point of view, the
t
-dependent part plays a similar role to the potential function in the matrix model as discussed in Sec.
3.4
, and we see the Heisenberg algebra,

\displaystyle[\partial_{n},t_{m}]=\delta_{n,m}

(6.40)

on the Fock space,
\mathsf{F}=\mathbb{C}\llbracket\partial_{n},t_{n}\rrbracket\ket{0}
, such that the vacuum state is defined as
\partial_{n}\ket{0}=0
(
t
-constant).
The dual vacuum is then defined by
\bra{0}t_{n}=0
.

6.3.2
Z
-state

In the operator formalism, the deformation parameter behaves as an operator acting on the Fock space.
From this point of view, the
t
-extended partition function is also seen as an operator, and through the operator-state correspondence, we define the
Z
-state as follows,

\displaystyle\ket{Z}=Z(t)\ket{0}\,.

(6.41)

We also define
\ket{Z_\lambda}=Z_{\lambda}(t)\ket{0}
, such that the total
Z
-state is given by
\ket{Z}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathsf{T}}}\ket{Z_\lambda}
.
From this
Z
-state, the undeformed partition function can be obtained as follows,

\displaystyle Z=\innerproduct{0}{Z}=\bra{0}Z(t)\ket{0}\,.

(6.42)

Namely, the partition function is given as a correlator (of vertex operators as we will see soon) in this formalism.
Such a connection between the BPS observables and the formalism of CFT-like theory is called the BPS/CFT correspondence
[
Nek04a
]
, and the realization of the partition function as a correlator is interpreted as a consequence of such a correspondence.

We remark that the expression (
6.42
) implies that the partition function is realized as a correlator on a sphere.
One can similarly consider a torus correlator (a character on the Fock module), and it turns out that such a torus correlator computes the six-dimensional partition function in this context
[
KP18b
]
.
In the formalism of class
\mathcal{S}
theory
[
Gai12
]
, on the other hand, the torus correlator is associated with the loop structure in quiver gauge theory, typically found in affine quiver gauge theory.
These two realizations are related to each other through the duality exchanging base and fiber of Seiberg–Witten geometry, and S-duality in type IIB string theory
[
KMV98
,
AHK98
]
.

We see that this

Having the mode expansion,

\displaystyle[T_{n},T_{m}]=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(T_{n-k}T_{m+k}-T_{m-k}T_{n+k})-\frac{(1-q_{1}^{n})(1-q_{2}^{n})}{1-q_{12}}(q_{12}^{n}-q_{12}^{-n})\delta_{n+m,0}\,,

\displaystyle f(x)=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q_{1})(1-q_{2})}{n(1+q_{12}^{n})}x^{n}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}x^{k}\,.

(6.53)

\mathsf{Y}

\mathsf{Y}(x)\mathsf{Y}(x^{\prime})/{:\mathsf{Y}(x)\mathsf{Y}(x^{\prime}):}=f^{-1}(x^{\prime}/x)

The algebraic relation (
6.52

\mathsf{T}

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}\right)\mathsf{T}(x)\mathsf{T}(x^{\prime})-f\left(\frac{x}{x^{\prime}}\right)\mathsf{T}(x^{\prime})\mathsf{T}(x)=-\frac{(1-q_{1})(1-q_{2})}{1-q_{12}}\left(\delta\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}q_{12}\right)-\delta\left(\frac{x}{x^{\prime}}q_{12}\right)\right)\,,

We have remarks on this relation.

From this point of view, we have a connection with quantum integrable system in this limit.

Another remark is that, interpreting the

From this point of view, the gauge theory average of

6.5
Higgsing and intersecting defects

As we discussed throughout this article, supergroup gauge theory inevitably violates the unitarity, and thus its physical realization seems not to be straightforward.
In this part, we would demonstrate that supergroup theory can be engineered from physical setups by imposing the defect operators.

In the context of supersymmetric gauge theory, there exist two branches in the moduli space of supersymmetric vacua, called the Coulomb and Higgs branches (In general, one can also consider the mixed branch).
Although Seiberg–Witten theory of four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=2
gauge theory describes the Coulomb branch, it has been known that there exists a root of Higgs branch locus, which is reached by tuning the fundamental mass parameter to the Coulomb moduli parameter as
m_{f}=a_{\alpha}

[
Dor98
,
DHT99
]
.
In the presence of the
\Omega
-background, this condition is “quantized” to be
m_{f}=a_{\alpha}+n_{\alpha,1}\epsilon_{1}+n_{\alpha,2}\epsilon_{2}
, and under this condition, the site
(n_{\alpha,1}+1,n_{\alpha,2}+1)
cannot be included by the
\alpha
-th partition,
(n_{\alpha,1}+1,n_{\alpha,2}+1)\not\in\lambda_{\alpha}
(the pit condition
[
BFM18
]
).
26
26

26

This can be seen as follows:
The fundamental hypermultiplet contribution is given by (
5.75

\mathsf{H}_{\text{inst}}=\mathsf{M}^{\vee}\mathsf{K}

At the fixed point
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathsf{T}}

\operatorname{ch}\mathsf{H}_{\text{inst}}=\sum_{\alpha\in[n]}\sum_{(i,j)\in\lambda_{\alpha}}q_{1}^{i-1-n_{\alpha,1}}q_{2}^{j-1-n_{\alpha,2}}

Hence, if
(n_{\alpha,1}+1,n_{\alpha,2}+1)\in\lambda_{\alpha}
, we have a zero mode, and the corresponding contribution to the partition function becomes zero.

Namely, we have a restriction on the instanton configuration.
In fact, such a restriction on a partition is also discussed in the context of representation theory of supergroup:

In particular, for

From physical point of view,

Computing the partition function of this configuration, it is given in the form of (deformation of) supermatrix model, which implies that we have an emerging supergroup structure originated from physical (non-supergroup) theory by considering the intersecting defects.

A similar realization of supergroup theory can be discussed in a higher-dimensional setup.
