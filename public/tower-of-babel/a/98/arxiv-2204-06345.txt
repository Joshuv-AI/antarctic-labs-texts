Abstract




Abstract.
Let
2\leq n\leq 5
.
We establish an apriori
interior Hölder regularity of
C^{2}
-stable solutions to the semilinear equation

-\Delta u=f(u)
in any domain of
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
for any nonlinearity
f\in C^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}})
.
If
f
is nondecreasing and convex in addition,
we obtain an interior Hölder regularity, and hence the local boundedness, of

W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solutions by locally approximating
them via
C^{2}(\Omega)
-stable solutions. In particular, we
do not require any lower bound on
f
.

1
Introduction

Let
n\geq 2
and
\Omega\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
be a bounded
domain.
Consider the semilinear elliptic equation

-\Delta u=f(u)\quad{\rm in}\ \Omega,

(1.1)

where the nonlinearity
f\in C^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}})
, that is,
f
is locally Lipschitz in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
The equation (
1.1
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation for minimizers of the energy functional

\mathcal{E}(u,\Omega):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}F(u)\,dx,

where
F(t)=\int^{t}_{0}f(t)\,dt
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
A function
u:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called as a

L^{1}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
-weak solution
to the equation (
1.1
) if
u\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
,
f(u)\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
and

-\int_{\Omega}u\Delta\xi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}f(u)\xi\,dx\quad\forall\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

(1.2)

If
u
is a
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-weak solution, that is,
u\in W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
apriori and
u
is a
L^{1}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
-weak solution to (
1.1
) in the sense of (
1.2
), then
(
1.2
) reads as

\int_{\Omega}Du\cdot D\xi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}f(u)\xi\,dx\quad\forall\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

(1.3)

Moreover, write

f^{\prime}_{-}(t):=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(t)-f(t-h)}{h}\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

which, when
f\in C^{1}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
, coincide with
f^{\prime}(t)
.
A
L^{1}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
-weak solution
u
to (
1.1
) is called as a stable solution if

f^{\prime}_{-}(u)\in L^{1}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
and

\int_{\Omega}|D\xi|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}_{-}(u)\xi^{2}\,dx\geq 0\quad\forall\xi\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\Omega).

(1.4)

Note that (
1.4
) means the nonnegativity of the second variation of the energy
\mathcal{E}(u,\Omega)
at
u
, and moreover, (
1.4
) coincides with the nonnegativity of
the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the linearized operator
-\Delta-f^{\prime}_{-}(u)
in
\Omega
, for more details see
[
16
]
.

In dimension
n\leq 9
,
Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
3
]
first proved the boundedness of
weak stable solutions
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
to
the equation (
1.1
) with the nonlinearity
f(t)=e^{t}
, that is,
the equation
-\Delta u=e^{u}
. Later, Brezis and Vázquez
[
1
]
obtained an analogue result
for the equation (
1.1
) with
the nonlinearity
f(t)=(1+t)^{p}
for any
p>1
, that is,
-\Delta u=(1+u)^{p}
.
However, in dimension
n\geq 10
, one cannot expect the boundedness of
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)

stable solutions to (
1.1
) in general; indeed,

-2\ln|x|\in W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})\setminus L^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a stable solution to
-\Delta u=2(n-2)e^{u}
in the unit ball
B_{1}
,
see
[
1
,
2
]
for details.

The results above lead to a long-standing conjecture (see
[
2
]
and also
[
10
]
):
in dimension
n\leq 9
, if the nonlinearity
f
is positive, nondecreasing, convex and superlinear at

+\infty
, then stable solutions
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
to the equation (
1.1
) are always bounded.
This conjecture was
also motivated by the Brezis’ boundedness conjecture and Brezis-Vázquez’
W^{1,2}
-regularity conjecture for extremal solutions
(apriori in
L^{1}(\Omega)
) to the equation (
1.1
); for more details see
[
2
]
.
Recently, this conjecture
was completely answered by Cabré, Figalli, Ros-Oton and Serra
[
10
]
; see also
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
22
,
26
]
for earlier contributions towards this conjecture.
Indeed, if
f
is nonnegative, Cabré, Figalli, Ros-Oton and Serra
[
10
]
established
a crucial apriori interior Hölder estimate for
C^{2}
-stable solutions
u
to (
1.1
):

\displaystyle\underset{{B_{r}(x_{0})}}{\rm osc}\ u\leq C(n)\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\|Du\|_{L^{2}(B_{2R}(x_{0}))},\quad\forall x_{0}\in\Omega,\,0<r<R<\frac{1}{4}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x_{0},\partial\Omega),

(1.5)

for some dimensional exponent
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Here

B_{r}(y)
denotes the ball with center
y
and radius

r>0
. Moreover, they showed that
L^{2+\gamma}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
-norm of
Du
for some dimensional exponent
{\gamma}>0

and
C^{\alpha}(\overline{B_{r}(x_{0})})
-norm of
u
are
bounded by the
L^{1}(B_{2r}(x_{0}))
norm of
u
.
Combining with moving plane method, they bounded
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
-norm of
u
by
L^{1}(\Omega)
-norm
when
\Omega
is convex
C^{1}
domain and
u
has zero boundary.
If
f
is nondecreasing and convex in addition,
they bounded

L^{2+\gamma}(\Omega)
-norm of
Du

and
C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
-norm of
u
via
L^{1}(\Omega)
-norm
when
\Omega
is of
C^{3}
and
u
has zero boundary.
Via an approximation argument they further bound the
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
-norm of all
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
-stable solutions via some constant depending only on
\Omega
and rate
f(t)/t
,
where they require
f(t)/t\to+\infty
as
t\to+\infty
.
The above bound via
L^{1}
-norm is very important for them
to show that extremal solutions are indeed
W^{1,2}_{0}
-weak solutions.
Note that their proofs, especially the proof of their Lemma 3.1 which is crucial to get (
1.5
), heavily relies on the nonnegativity of
f
.

It is natural to ask, in dimension
n\leq 9
, whether the nonnegativity of the nonlinearity
f
is necessary to get the boundedness
of
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
-stable solutions to (
1.1
)?
Cabré
[
12
]
further asked
that whether the nonnegativity of the nonlinearity
f
is necessary to get the interior regularity
of stable solutions?
It was already proved by Cabré and Capella
[
4
]
that
the nonnegativity of
f
is unnecessary to get the boundedness of
radial stable solutions
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
to (
1.1
).
Moreover, in dimension
n\leq 4
, the nonnegativity of
f
is shown by Cabré
[
5
]
to be unnecessary to get the apriori boundedness regularity for

C^{2}
-stable solutions to (
1.1
); but in dimension
5\leq n\leq 9
,
his approach does not work. Recently, in dimension
n\leq 4
,
Cabré
[
9
]
also provided a new and simpler proof of this result in
[
5
]
. In dimension
5
, Cabré
[
9
]
established the interior
L^{p}
bounds of
C^{2}
-stable solutions with
p<+\infty
for any
C^{1}
nonlinearity
f
.

In this paper we prove that, in dimension
n\leq 5
,
the nonnegativity of
f
is unnecessary to get the following apriori
interior Hölder regularity of
C^{2}(\Omega)
-stable solutions to (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
2\leq n\leq 5
.
There exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C\geq 1
depending only on
n
such that

C^{2}(\Omega)
-stable
solutions to equation (
1.1
) with
f\in C^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}})
enjoy the interior Hölder
C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
-regularity (
1.5
).

Moreover, under nondecrease and
convexity assumptions on
f
, we show that
the nonnegativity of
f
is unnecessary to get
the interior Hölder regularity of

W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solutions to (
1.1
).
For convenience, we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=\{f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad f\in C^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}})\ {\rm is\ nondecreasing\ and\ convex}\}.

We emphasis again that the nonlinearity in the class
\mathcal{C}
is not
necessarily nonnegative.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
2\leq n\leq 5
.
There exists
\alpha=\alpha(n)\in(0,1)
and
C=C(n)\geq 1
such that

W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable
solutions to the equation (
1.1
) with
f\in\mathcal{C}
enjoy the Hölder
C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
-regularity (
1.5
)
with
0<r<R/2<r_{0}
for some
r_{0}=r_{0}(f,n,x_{0})>0
, and hence
are locally bounded in
\Omega
.

Obviously, we would conclude Theorem
1.2
from
Theorem
1.1
if
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solutions can be approximated via

C^{2}
-stable solutions in certain sense.
Recall that if
f\in\mathcal{C}
is nonnegative, it was proved in
[
10
, Proposition 4.2]
that

W^{1,2}_{0}
-stable solutions to (
1.1
) is globally approximated in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
by

C^{2}(\Omega)
-stable solution
u^{\epsilon}
to the equation
-\Delta v=f_{\epsilon}(v)
in
\Omega
for some
0\leq f_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Below we show that, in any dimension,
the nonnegativity of
f
is unnecessary to get the following local approximation
to
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solutions via
C^{2}
-stable solutions.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
.
Let
u
be a
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solution to the equation (
1.1
), where
f\in\mathcal{C}
.
For any
x_{0}\in\Omega
there exists
r_{0}>0
depending on
f,n
and
x_{0}
so that
we can find a family
\{u^{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})}\subset C^{2}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))
for some sufficiently small
\epsilon_{0}>0
satisfying that

u^{\epsilon}
is a stable solution to
-\Delta v=f_{\epsilon}(v)
in
B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})
for some
f_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}
,
and that
u^{\epsilon}\to u
in
W^{1,2}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

We prove Theorem
1.2
as below.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
u
be a
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solution to the equation (
1.1
), where
f\in\mathcal{C}
.
For any
x_{0}\in\Omega
, let
r_{0}>0
and
u_{\epsilon}\in C^{2}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))
be as in Theorem
1.3
.
Applying Theorem
1.1
to
u_{\epsilon}
, one has

\displaystyle{\rm osc}_{B_{r}(x_{0})}u_{\epsilon}\leq C(n)\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{\alpha}\|Du_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))},\quad\forall r<r_{0}/2.

(1.6)

By Theorem
1.3
,
u_{\epsilon}\to u
in
W^{1,2}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))
as
\epsilon\to 0
, one concludes that
(
1.6
) also holds for
u
. We complete this proof.
∎

The proof of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3
is presented in Section 2 and Section 3 respectively. Below we sketch some ideas of the proofs.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to built up the following crucial inequality for
C^{2}
-stable solutions
u
to (
1.1
):

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}|x|^{-n+2}|Du|^{2}\,dx\leq C(n)\int_{B_{2r}\backslash B_{r}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx,

(1.7)

that is, Lemma 2.1 in Section 2. We write
B_{r}=B_{r}(0)
and assume
0\in\Omega
for simple.
Then a hole-filling together with an iteration argument
leads to Theorem
1.1
, see Section 2 for details.

In order to prove (
1.7
) or Lemma
2.1
, we choose test function

\xi=|Du||x|\eta
in (
1.4
). Equivalently, we
choose test functions
\zeta=|x|\eta
for any
\eta\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\Omega)
in
the reversed
Poincaré type formula in Lemma
2.2
, which was proved by
[
23
]
(see also
[
10
,
24
]
), so to obtain
Lemma
2.3
.
Our test functions are different from test functions
\xi=(x\cdot Du)\eta

chosen in (
1.4
) by
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
, but it is similar to test functions
|Du||x|^{-\beta}
with some
\beta>0
chosen by Cabré
[
9
, Theorem 1.5]
.
Then we have to bound all terms including Hessian (or second order derivatives) in Lemma
2.3
. To this end,
we decompose
D^{2}uDu
along the normal direction and the tangential space of the level set
\{u=c\}
for any
c
, that is,

\frac{D^{2}uDu}{|Du|}=\frac{\Delta_{\infty}u}{|Du|^{3}}Du+[\frac{D^{2}uDu}{|Du|}-\frac{\Delta_{\infty}u}{|Du|^{3}}Du]\quad{\rm on}\quad\{Du\neq 0\};

where
\Delta_{\infty}u=D^{2}uDu\cdot Du
is the
\infty
-Laplacian. In order to bound
\frac{\Delta_{\infty}u}{|Du|^{3}}Du
, we further write

\frac{\Delta_{\infty}u}{|Du|^{3}}Du=\left(\frac{\Delta_{\infty}u}{|Du|^{2}}-\Delta u\right)\frac{Du}{|Du|}+\Delta u\frac{Du}{|Du|}\quad{\rm on}\quad\{Du\neq 0\}.

Then an upper bound for
(\frac{\Delta_{\infty}u}{|Du|^{3}}-\frac{\Delta u}{|Du|})
,
the mean curvature of
\{u=c\}
up
to multiple
(n-1)
, was derived in Lemma
2.5
by using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.
Via integration by parts we also have an upper bound for the integration of
\int_{\Omega}\Delta u(Du\cdot x)\eta^{2}\,dx
as in lemma
2.7
.
Combining all of them we could get an estimate without Hessian terms; see Lemma
2.8
.
Taking
\eta=|x|^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}\phi
we prove Lemma
2.1
via a direct calculation, that is, (
1.7
).

To prove Theorem 1.3, we first built up in Lemma 3.1 the existence of
C^{2}
-solution to the equation

-\Delta v=f^{\prime}_{-}(1)v-[f^{\prime}_{-}(1)-f(1)]
in balls
B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})
with given Dirichlet boundary
u
,
where the range of
r_{0}
is determined by the Sobolev inequality and
{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x_{0},\partial\Omega)
. Thanks to Lemma 3.1, we are able to adapt the arguments for

[
10
, Proposition 4.2]
to
approximate
locally the stable solution via
C^{2}
-stable solutions.
Details of the proof of Theorem 1.3 are given in Section 3 for reader’s convenience.

Finally, we list several remarks.

Remark 1.4
.

(i)
The
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solutions in
Theorem
1.2
can not be relaxed to some
L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
-stable solutions.
Indeed, in dimension
3\leq n\leq 9
,
consider the equation
-\Delta u=\lambda_{s}(1+u)^{p}
in
B_{1}
, where

\frac{n}{n-2}<p\leq\frac{n+2\sqrt{n-1}}{n-4+2\sqrt{n-1}},\quad\lambda_{s}=\frac{2}{p-1}\left(n-\frac{2p}{p-1}\right)>0,

The function
|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}-1\in L^{1}(B_{1})

satisfies (
1.2
) and (
1.4
). But

|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}-1\notin W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(B_{1})

and

|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}-1\notin L^{\infty}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(B_{1})
, see
Brezis and Vázquez
[
1
]
for more details.

(ii) In Theorem
1.1
and also Theorem
1.2
, it is not clear to us whether the
L^{2}(B_{r})
-norm of
Du
can be bounded by
L^{1}(B_{2r})
norm of
u
.
Recall that in the case
f\geq 0
, this is proved in
[
10
]
and
is crucial to get
W^{1,2}
-regularity of extremal
solutions.

(iii) In dimension
n\leq 5
, it remains open whether the nonnegativity of
f
is unnecessary to get
the global aproiori Hölder regularity of
C^{2}(\Omega)
-stable solutions with zero boundary, global approximation to
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
-stable solutions via
C^{2}(\Omega)
-stable solutions
and also global Hölder regularity of
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
-stable solution.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

To prove Theorem
1.1
it suffices to prove the following key lemma. In this section, we always let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be any stable solution to the equation (
1.1
), where
f\in C^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
0\in\Omega
.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2)(6-n)}{4}\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\phi^{2}\,dx+(n-3)\int_{\Omega}(Du\cdot x)^{2}|x|^{-n}\phi^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+4}|D\phi|^{2}\,dx-n\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}(x\cdot D\phi)\phi\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+4\int_{\Omega}(x\cdot Du)(Du\cdot D\phi)\phi|x|^{-n+2}\,dx\quad\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

(2.1)

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We may assume
3\leq n\leq 5
. Indeed, in dimension 2,
one may add an additional artificial variable similarly to
[
10
, Section 1.4]
and
[
11
, Remark 1.7]
.
Let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be a stable solution to (
1.1
).
Below we only need to show that
(
1.5
) holds
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and constant
C
depending only on
n
.
Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
and
0<r<R\leq\frac{1}{4}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x_{0},\partial\Omega)
.
Up to a translation argument, we may
x_{0}=0
. Since
3\leq n\leq 5
, we have the coefficient
\frac{(n-2)(6-n)}{4}>0
in (
2.1
).
Applying Young’s inequality, from (
2.1
) we deduce that

\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\phi^{2}\,dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+4}|D\phi|^{2}\,dx\quad\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

Given any
k\geq-1
, taking the cut-off function

\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{2^{-k}R})
satisfying

\displaystyle\phi=1\quad{\rm in}\ B_{2^{-k-1}R},\quad 0\leq\phi\leq 1\quad{\rm in}\ B_{2^{-k}R},\quad|D\phi|\leq\frac{4}{2^{-k}R}\quad{\rm in}\ B_{2^{-k}R},

we get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2^{-k-1}R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx\leq C(n)\int_{B_{2^{-k}R}\backslash B_{2^{-k-1}R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx.

(2.2)

Adding both sides by
C(n)\int_{B_{2^{-k-1}R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx
one has

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2^{-k-1}R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx\leq\frac{C(n)}{C(n)+1}\int_{B_{2^{-k}R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx\quad\forall k\geq 0.

By iteration, we further have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2^{-k-1}R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx\leq\left[\frac{C(n)}{C(n)+1}\right]^{k}\int_{B_{R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Writing
\alpha=\log_{2}\frac{C(n)}{C(n)+1}
, and applying (
2.2
) with
k=-1
we further have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2^{-k-1}R}}|Du|^{2}|x|^{-n+2}\,dx\leq C(n)2^{k\alpha}\int_{B_{2R}}|Du|^{2}\,dx\quad\forall k\geq 0.

Letting
k\geq 1
such that
2^{-k-2}R\leq r\leq 2^{-k-1}R
, from this we conclude (
1.5
) as desired.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Lemma
2.1
, which consists of a sequence of lemmas.
We begin with the following important reversed Poincaré type formula
due to Sternberg and Zumbrun
[
23
,
24
]
; see also Cabré et al
[
10
, Lemma 2.3]

for an alternative proof which will be stated here
for the convenience of reader.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}[|D^{2}u|^{2}-|D|Du||^{2}]\zeta^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}|D\zeta|^{2}\,dx\quad\forall\zeta\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\Omega).

(2.3)

Proof.

Recall the following divergence structure

|D^{2}v|^{2}-(\Delta v)^{2}={\rm div}(D^{2}vDv-\Delta vDv)\quad\forall v\in C^{3}.

By an approximation argument we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}[|D^{2}u|^{2}-(\Delta u)^{2}]\zeta^{2}\,dx=2\int_{\Omega}[\Delta u(Du\cdot D\zeta)-D^{2}uDu\cdot D\zeta]\zeta\,dx\quad\forall\zeta\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\Omega).

Since
-\Delta u=f(u)
, via integration by parts we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\Delta u)^{2}\zeta^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(\Delta u)f(u)\zeta^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}_{-}(u)|Du|^{2}\zeta^{2}\,dx+2\int_{\Omega}f(u)(Du\cdot D\zeta)\zeta\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}_{-}(u)|Du|^{2}\zeta^{2}\,dx-2\int_{\Omega}\Delta u(Du\cdot D\zeta)\zeta\,dx.

By the definition of the stable solution, one has

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}_{-}(u)|Du|^{2}\zeta^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|D(|Du|\zeta)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|D|Du||^{2}\zeta^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}|D\zeta|^{2}\,dx+2\int_{\Omega}(D^{2}uDu\cdot D\zeta)\zeta\,dx.

Combing all these estimates together we conclude (
2.3
).
∎

Taking
\zeta=|x|\eta

To bound the second order derivatives of

1/\epsilon
, that is,
\{f(1/\epsilon)+f^{\prime}_{-}(1/\epsilon)(t-1/\epsilon)\}_{t\geq 1/\epsilon}
.

Obviously, we have the following properties:

f_{\epsilon}

and
(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}
are nondecreasing, and
(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}\leq f^{\prime}_{-}(1/\epsilon)
,

(3.9)

and

\displaystyle\mbox{
$f\geq f_{\epsilon_{1}}\geq f_{\epsilon_{2}}$ whenever $0<\epsilon_{1}\leq\epsilon_{2}\leq 1$, }\quad f=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}f_{\epsilon}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}\leq f^{\prime}_{-}.

(3.10)

Consequently,
f_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}
is Lipschitz function in
{\mathbb{R}}
, and hence

\displaystyle\quad|f_{\epsilon}(t)|\leq|f(0)|+f^{\prime}_{-}(1/\epsilon)|t|\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(3.11)

Thanks to
(
3.11
),
v\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})
implies

f_{\epsilon}(v)\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})
. Moreover, since
f=f_{\epsilon}
when
t<1/\epsilon
, by the convexity of
f_{\epsilon}
we have

\displaystyle f_{\epsilon}(t)\geq(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}(1)t-[(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}(1)-f_{\epsilon}(1)]=At-K,\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

(3.12)

where and below we write
A=f^{\prime}_{-}(1)
and
K=f^{\prime}_{-}(1)-f(1)
.

Step 2.
By Lemma
3.1
, there exists unique
u^{(0)}\in C^{2}(B_{1})
satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta u^{(0)}=Au^{(0)}-K\quad

\displaystyle{\rm in}\ B_{1},\quad u^{(0)}=u\quad{\rm on}\ \partial B_{1};

(3.13)

moreover,
u^{(0)}\leq u
almost everywhere in
B_{1}
.
Given any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, set
u^{(0)}_{\epsilon}:=u^{(0)}
and
for any
j\geq 1
, since
f_{\epsilon}
is Lipschitz in
{\mathbb{R}}
,
there is a function
u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})\cap C^{2}(B_{1})
satisfying

\displaystyle-\Delta u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}=f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon})\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})\quad{\rm in}\ B_{1};\quad u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}=u\quad{\rm on}\ \partial B_{1}.

(3.14)

We claim that

(i)

u^{(0)}\leq u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\leq u^{(j+1)}_{\epsilon}\leq u\quad\forall\epsilon\in(0,1],\ \forall j\geq 0;

(ii)

u^{(j)}_{\epsilon_{2}}\leq u^{(j)}_{\epsilon_{1}}\quad\mbox{ $\forall\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in(0,1]$ with $\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}$,\ $\forall j\geq 0$; }

(iii)

u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})
uniformly in

\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
j\geq 0
.

Proof of Claim (i)
.
Thanks to (
3.10
) and
u^{(0)}_{\epsilon}=u^{(0)}\leq u
one has

\displaystyle-\Delta(u-u^{(1)}_{\epsilon})=f(u)-f_{\epsilon}(u^{(0)}_{\epsilon})=(f(u)-f_{\epsilon}(u))+(f_{\epsilon}(u)-f_{\epsilon}(u^{(0)}_{\epsilon}))\geq 0

in weak sense, that is,
u-u^{(1)}_{\epsilon}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
is superharmonic.
By the maximum principle, we get
u^{(1)}_{\epsilon}\leq u
in
B_{1}
.
On the other hand, with the aid of (
3.12
) we obtain

\displaystyle-\Delta(u^{(1)}_{\epsilon}-u^{(0)}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=f_{\epsilon}(u^{(0)}_{\epsilon})-(Au^{(0)}_{\epsilon}-K)\geq 0,

and hence, by the maximum principle, we get
u^{(0)}_{\epsilon}\leq u^{(1)}_{\epsilon}
.

For any
j\geq 2
, if
u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon}\leq u
, similarly one has

\displaystyle-\Delta(u-u^{(j)}_{\epsilon})=f(u)-f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=(f(u)-f_{\epsilon}(u))+(f_{\epsilon}(u)-f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon}))\geq 0,

and hence, by the maximum principle, we have
u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\leq u
. On the other hand, by (
3.10
),
if
u^{(j-2)}\leq u^{(j-1)}
, then

\displaystyle-\Delta(u^{j}_{\epsilon}-u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon})=f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon})-f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j-2)}_{\epsilon})\geq 0,

and hence, by the maximum principle again one has
u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon}\leq u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}
.

Proof of Claim (ii)
.
Note that
u^{(0)}_{\epsilon_{1}}=u^{(0)}_{\epsilon_{2}}
.
For
j\geq 1
, if
u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon_{2}}\leq u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon_{1}}
,
by (
3.10
)
one has

\displaystyle-\Delta(u^{(j)}_{\epsilon_{1}}-u^{(j)}_{\epsilon_{2}})=f_{\epsilon_{1}}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon_{1}})-f_{\epsilon_{2}}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon_{2}})\geq 0

and hence by the maximum principle, we obtain

u^{(j)}_{\epsilon_{2}}\leq u^{(j)}_{\epsilon_{1}}
.

Proof of Claim (iii)
. Since (i) implies
|u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}|\leq|u^{(0)}_{\epsilon}|+|u|
,
by
u^{(0)}=u^{(0)}_{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(B)
and
u\in W^{1,2}(B)
, one knows that
|u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}|\in L^{2}(B)
uniformly in
\epsilon>0
and
j\geq 0
.
Moreover, observe that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}|Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{B_{1}}Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\cdot(Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}-Du)\,dx+\int_{B_{1}}|Du|^{2}\,dx.

(3.15)

By integration
by parts,
u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}-u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
and
(
3.14
), one has

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\cdot(Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}-Du)\,dx=-\int_{B_{1}}f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon})(u-u^{(j)}_{\epsilon})\,dx.

Recalling that (i) gives
u-u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\geq 0
in
B_{1}
, and (
3.12
) gives

f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon})\geq Au^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon}-K
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\cdot(Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}-Du)\,dx\leq-\int_{B_{1}}(Au^{(j-1)}_{\epsilon}-K)(u-u^{(j)}_{\epsilon})\,dx.

Since
u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(B_{1})
uniformly in
j,\epsilon
, we conclude

Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(B_{1})
uniformly in
j,\epsilon
.

Step 3.
By (iii) and compactness of Sobolev spaces,
we know that for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,

u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}
converges to some function
u_{\epsilon}
in
L^{2}(B_{1})
and weakly in
W^{1,2}(B)
as
j\to\infty
.
Thus

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}Du_{\epsilon}\cdot D\phi\,dx=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{B_{1}}Du^{(j)}_{\epsilon}\cdot D\phi\,dx=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{B_{1}}f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j)}_{\epsilon})\phi\,dx.

By the continuity of
f_{\epsilon}
,
\lim_{j\to\infty}f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j)}_{\epsilon})=f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})
.
Thanks to
(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}\leq f^{\prime}_{-}(1/\epsilon)
, by (i) one has

\displaystyle|f_{\epsilon}(u^{(j)}_{\epsilon})|\leq f^{\prime}_{-}(1/\epsilon)|u^{(j)}_{\epsilon}|+|f(0)|\leq f^{\prime}_{-}(1/\epsilon)(|u^{(0)}|+|u|)+|f(0)|\in L^{2}(B_{1}).

Thus it follows by the Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}Du_{\epsilon}\cdot D\phi\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})\phi\,dx.

This implies that

u_{\epsilon}
is a weak solution to

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{\epsilon}=f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})\quad{\rm in}\ B_{1};u_{\epsilon}=u\quad{\rm on}\ \partial B_{1}.

(3.16)

Thanks to
(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}\leq f^{\prime}_{-}(1/\epsilon)
, by the standard elliptic theory, we know that
u_{\epsilon}\in C^{2}(B_{1})
(see
[
18
]
).

Since
u_{\epsilon}\leq u
,
(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}\leq f^{\prime}_{-}
and
(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}
is nondecreasing, we have

(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}(u_{\epsilon})\leq(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}(u)\leq f^{\prime}_{-}(u)

and hence

\int_{B_{r_{0}}}(f_{\epsilon})^{\prime}_{-}(u_{\epsilon})\xi^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{B_{1}}f^{\prime}_{-}(u)\xi^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{B_{1}}|D\xi|^{2}\,dx,

which implies that
u_{\epsilon}
is a stable solution.

Step 4.
By (iii) and the definition of
u_{\epsilon}
, we know that
u_{\epsilon}\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})

uniformly in
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Thus
u_{\epsilon}
converges to some function
u^{\star}
in
L^{2}(B_{1})
and
weakly in
L^{2}(B_{1})
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
Since (ii) implies that
u^{(0)}\leq u_{\epsilon_{2}}\leq u_{\epsilon_{1}}\leq u
whenever
0<\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}<1
,
we know that
u^{\star}\leq u
a. e. in
B_{1}
. Note that
u^{\star}-u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
.

Next we show that
u^{\star}
is a weak solution to

\displaystyle-\Delta u^{\star}=f(u^{\star})\quad{\rm in}\ B_{1},\quad u^{\star}=u\quad

\displaystyle{\rm on}\ \partial B_{1}.

(3.17)

Considering (
3.16
) and
Du_{\epsilon}\to Du^{\star}
weakly in
L^{2}(B_{1})
as
\epsilon\to 0
, we only need to prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{1}}f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}f(u^{\star})\,dx.

(3.18)

To see this, write

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}|f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})-f(u^{\star})|\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}\cap\{u<1/\epsilon\}}|f(u_{\epsilon})-f(u^{\star})|\,dx+\int_{B_{1}\cap\{u\geq 1/\epsilon\}}|f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})-f(u^{\star})|\,dx.

(3.19)

By (
3.12
) and (i), we get

\displaystyle|f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})|+|f(u^{\star})|+|f(u_{\epsilon})|

\displaystyle\leq 6[|f(u)|+A|u^{(0)}|+A|u|+K]\in L^{1}(B_{1}),

(3.20)

Thanks to this and
f(u_{\epsilon})\to f(u^{\star})
a. e., the first term in the right-hand side of (
3
) tends to zero.
Moreover due to (
3.20
) again and the absolute continuity of integrals, the second term in right-hand side of (
3
) also tends to zero.
Thus (
3.18
) holds as desired.

Step 5.
To show
u=u^{\star}
a. e. in
B_{1}
, we argue by contradiction. Assume that
u\not=u^{\star}
a. e. in
B_{1}
.
Since
u^{\star}\leq u
a. e. in
B_{1}
and
f
is nondecreasing, we have

-\Delta(u-u^{\star})=f(u)-f(u^{\star})\geq 0\quad\mbox{ in weak sense},

that is,
u-u^{\star}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
is superharmonic in
B_{1}
.
Since
u\not\equiv u^{\star}
, by the strong maximum principle we have
u-u^{\star}>0
a. e. in
B_{1}
.

Since the convexity of
f
gives

f(u^{\star})-f(u)\geq f^{\prime}_{-}(u)(u^{\star}-u),

by
u^{\star}\leq u
a. e. in
B_{1}
one has

\displaystyle(f(u)-f(u^{\star}))(u-u^{\star})\leq f^{\prime}_{-}(u)(u-u^{\star})^{2}\quad\mbox{a.\,e. in $B_{1}$ }.

On the other hand, applying the stability inequality to
u-u^{\star}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
(up to some approximation via functions in
C^{0,1}_{c}(B_{1})
),
recalling

-\Delta(u-u^{\star})=f(u)-f(u^{\star})
in
B_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}f^{\prime}_{-}(u)(u-u^{\star})^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{B_{1}}|D(u-u^{\star})|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}(f(u)-f(u^{\star}))(u-u^{\star})\,dx.

We then conclude that

(f(u)-f(u^{\star}))(u-u^{\star})=f^{\prime}_{-}(u)(u-u^{\star})^{2}\quad{\rm a.e.\ in}\ B_{1},

and hence

\displaystyle f(u)-f(u^{\star})=f^{\prime}_{-}(u)(u-u^{\star})\quad\mbox{a.\,e. in $B_{1}$ }.

By the convexity of
f
, we know that

f
is linear in the interval
[u^{\star}(x),u(x)]
for a. e.
x\in B_{1}
.
Via the same argument as in
[
10
, Proposition 4.2]
and
[
15
, Theorem 16]
, one concludes that
f
is linear on
({\rm\,essinf\,}_{B_{1}}u^{\star},{\rm\,esssup\,}_{B_{1}}u)=({\rm\,essinf\,}_{B_{1}}u^{\star},+\infty)
.
That is,
f^{\prime}_{-}\leq C
for some constant
C<+\infty
.
This contradicts with

{\rm\,esssup\,}_{B_{1}}f^{\prime}_{-}(u)=+\infty
.
Therefore
u=u^{\star}
a.e in
B_{1}
.

Step 6.
We claim that
u_{\epsilon}\to u
in
W^{1,2}(B_{1})
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Indeed,
thanks to Step 5,
by (iii) we know that
u_{\epsilon}\to u
in
L^{2}(B_{1})
and

Du_{\epsilon}\to Du
weakly in
L^{2}(B_{1})
. It remains to show that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{1}}|Du_{\epsilon}|^{2}\leq\int_{B_{1}}|Du|^{2}\,dx.

(3.21)

Testing (
3.16
) with
u-u_{\epsilon}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}Du_{\epsilon}\cdot(Du-Du_{\epsilon})\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})(u-u_{\epsilon})\,dx.

It follows by
u^{(0)}\leq u_{\epsilon}\leq u
, (
3.9
) and (
3.12
) that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}|Du_{\epsilon}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}Du_{\epsilon}\cdot Du\,dx-\int_{B_{1}}f_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon})(u-u_{\epsilon})\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}}Du_{\epsilon}\cdot Du\,dx-\int_{B_{1}}(Au^{(0)}-K)(u-u_{\epsilon})\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}}Du_{\epsilon}\cdot Du\,dx+\left(\int_{B_{1}}(Au^{(0)}-K)^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{B_{1}}(u_{\epsilon}-u)^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

From this, thanks to
u^{(0)},u\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})
, using
u_{\epsilon}\to u
in
L^{2}(B_{1})
and

Du_{\epsilon}\to Du
weakly in
L^{2}(B_{1})
one gets (
3.21
).
This implies that
u_{\epsilon}\to u
in
W^{1,2}(B_{1})
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
