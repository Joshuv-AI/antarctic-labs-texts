Ancilla-free continuous-variable SWAP test
T.J. Volkoff, Yiğit Subaşı

Abstract
We propose a continuous-variable (CV) SWAP test that requires no ancilla register, thereby generalizing the ancilla-free SWAP test for qubits. In this ancilla-free CV SWAP test, the computational basis measurement is replaced by photon number-resolving measurement, and we calculate an upper bound on the error of the overlap estimate obtained from a finite Fock cutoff in the detector. As an example, we show that estimation of the overlap of pure, centered, single-mode Gaussian states of energy E E and squeezed in opposite quadratures can be obtained to error ϵ \epsilon using photon statistics below a Fock basis cutoff O ⁡ ( E ​ ln ⁡ ϵ − 1 ) O(E\ln\epsilon^{-1}) . This cutoff is greatly reduced to E + O ⁡ ( E ​ ln ⁡ ϵ − 1 ) E+O(\sqrt{E}\ln\epsilon^{-1}) when the states have rapidly decaying Fock tails, such as coherent states. We show how the ancilla-free CV SWAP test can be extended to many modes and applied to quantum algorithms such as variational compiling and entanglement spectroscopy in the CV setting. For the latter we also provide a new algorithm which does not have an analog in qubit systems. The ancilla-free CV SWAP test is implemented on Xanadu’s 8-mode photonic processor in order to estimate the vacuum probability of a two-mode squeezed state.

1.1
Primer on multimode continuous-variable systems

Let
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(the set of square integrable measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}
) have orthonormal basis given by the eigenfunctions of a quantum harmonic oscillator of unit frequency and unit mass. We define an
M
-mode continuous-variable system as
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M})\cong\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes M}
, where
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is the set of square summable sequences of complex numbers. This is a representation of the canonical commutation relation algebra
[a_{i},a_{j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{i,j}
,
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, where
a_{j}^{\dagger}={1\over\sqrt{2}}\left(x_{j}-{\partial\over\partial x_{j}}\right)

[
20
]
. The vacuum wavefunction proportional to
\Phi_{0}(x):=e^{-{1\over 2}\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{j}^{2}}
corresponds to the state
|0\rangle_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes|0\rangle_{M}
. More generally, the wavefunction proportional to
\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left(x_{j}-{\partial\over\partial x_{j}}\right)^{n_{j}}\Phi_{0}(x)
corresponds to the Fock state
|n_{1}\rangle_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes|n_{M}\rangle_{M}
.

A distinguished set of CV states are the Gaussian states, characterized by Wigner functions that are Gaussians on phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2M}

[
21
]
. In this work we make use of three unitary operations that map the set of Gaussian states to itself:

\displaystyle D(\alpha)_{j}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha a_{j}^{\dagger}-h.c.}\;\;\text{(displacement)}

\displaystyle S(z)_{j}

\displaystyle=e^{{1\over 2}(\overline{z}a_{j}^{2}-h.c.)}\;\;\text{(single mode squeezing)}

\displaystyle U_{\text{BS}}(\theta,\phi)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=e^{\theta(e^{i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}-h.c.)}\;,\;i\neq j\;\;\text{(beamsplitter)}

(1)

with
\alpha,z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\theta\in[0,\pi]
,
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
. We will also utilize phase space rotations, which take the form
\prod_{j=1}^{M}e^{-i\phi_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}}
. In Section
3
, we utilize two-mode squeezed states, defined by

|\text{TMSS}_{r}\rangle_{1,2}:=U_{\text{BS}}\left({\pi\over 4},\pi\right)_{1,2}S(r)_{1}\otimes S(-r)_{2}|0\rangle_{1}\otimes|0\rangle_{2}

(2)

and in Section
3
we utilize CV coherent states, i.e., states of the form
|\alpha\rangle:=D(\alpha)|0\rangle
.

In this work, we consider CV systems to be read out by photon number-resolving measurement. This is a projective measurement with outcome indexed by
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\times M}
corresponding to a
M
-mode Fock state.

2
Ancilla-free, destructive discrete variable (DV) SWAP test

It is well-known that the expected value of
SWAP
(considered as an observable) in a product state on two registers is given by the trace of the product of the density operators on both registers
[
22
]
, i.e.

\langle\texttt{SWAP}\rangle_{\rho\otimes\sigma}=\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}\rho\otimes\sigma=\text{tr}\rho\sigma\,.

(3)

It suffices to know how to implement
SWAP
between two registers since
SWAP
between multiple registers is defined as the tensor products of the former.

We first discuss the destructive, ancilla-free SWAP test algorithm for qubits for which we know an efficient quantum circuit in terms of elementary gates. For two qubits, the eigenvectors of
SWAP
are given by the Bell basis

\displaystyle|\Phi_{0,0}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}+|1\rangle_{A}|1\rangle_{B}\right)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{0,1}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}-|1\rangle_{A}|1\rangle_{B}\right)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{1,0}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over\sqrt{2}}\left(|1\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}+|0\rangle_{A}|1\rangle_{B}\right)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{1,1}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over\sqrt{2}}\left(|1\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}-|0\rangle_{A}|1\rangle_{B}\right)

(4)

with respective eigenvalues
\lambda_{i,j}=(-1)^{ij}
.
A measurement in the Bell basis can be implemented by a computational basis measurement preceded by a quantum circuit
C^{\dagger}
, where
C
satisfies
C|i\rangle_{A}|j\rangle_{B}=|\Phi_{i,j}\rangle
such as
C=CNOT_{BA}H_{B}
– see Fig.
1
b).

For
\rho
and
\sigma
generally mixed states of
K
qubits, the expectation value in (
3
) can be estimated by preparing the two states, measuring pairs of qubits in each system in the Bell basis, and repeating this
S
times. The following then is an unbiased estimator of the overlap
\text{tr}\rho\sigma
:

\text{tr}\rho\sigma

\textstyle\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\prod_{k=1}^{K}(-1)^{i_{k}(s)j_{k}(s)}

(5)

where
i_{k}(s),j_{k}(s)
are the outcomes of the Bell measurements of the
k
-th pair of qubits in the
s
-th run of the experiment.
The Berry-Esseen theorem implies that the difference between the distribution of the estimator and the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\text{tr}\rho\sigma,O(1/S))
is at most
O(1/\sqrt{S})

[
23
]
.

The appropriate generalization of the Bell basis measurement to the case of qudit registers

is the projective measurement defined by the eigenvectors of
SWAP
. These can be constructed using the fact that for a bipartite system
AB
with
\text{dim}A=\text{dim}B=d
,
\text{{SWAP}}=P_{+}-P_{-}
where
P_{+}
(
P_{-}
) is the projector to the symmetric (antisymmetric) subspace of the Hilbert space

.
Since the spectrum of
SWAP
is highly degenerate there are many unitaries that diagonalize it.
One such unitary is given by

V|i\rangle|j\rangle=\begin{cases}|i\rangle|i\rangle&j=i\\
{1\over\sqrt{2}}\left(|i\rangle|j\rangle+|j\rangle|i\rangle\right)&i<j\\
{1\over\sqrt{2}}\left(|i\rangle|j\rangle-|j\rangle|i\rangle\right)&i>j\end{cases}

(6)

which generates the
SWAP

\lambda_{i,j}=(-1)^{1(i>j)}
where

is the indicator function of the

\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{\times 2}
. Therefore, the circuit in Fig.

3
) for a system of qudits.

Unitary
V
of Eq. (
6

d=2
,

does not correspond to the unitary of Fig.
1

To the best of our knowledge, short-depth gate decompositions of any

|\Phi_{z,x}\rangle
defined by

|\Phi_{z,x}\rangle\equiv\left(X(x)\otimes Z(z)\right){1\over\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|i\rangle_{A}|i\rangle_{B}

(7)

where

3
Ancilla-free, destructive continuous variable (CV) SWAP test

In principle, the DV operator
V
can be extended to a countably infinite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
of two quantum harmonic oscillators (i.e., two CV registers). However, the computational basis states
|j\rangle
correspond to Fock states, so the action of
V^{\dagger}
on, e.g., a tensor product of CV coherent states, would produce optically non-classical, non-Gaussian states. In particular,
V^{\dagger}
would not be implementable on a near-term photonic processor such as Xanadu’s X8, which produces non-classical, Gaussian states
[
24
]
. In this section, we seek an ancilla-free CV SWAP test that requires only linear optical operations and photon number-resolving measurement.

The
SWAP
gate for continuous variables can be written as

\text{{SWAP}}=e^{i\pi\left({a_{1}^{\dagger}-a_{2}^{\dagger}\over\sqrt{2}}\right)\left({a_{1}-a_{2}\over\sqrt{2}}\right)}.

(8)

As in the case of
SWAP
on a tensor product of isomorphic finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, (
8
) is both unitary and self-adjoint. A generalized version of
SWAP
has been experimentally implemented for two-mode CV systems in a circuit quantum electrodynamics framework
[
25
]
. A proposal for implementing the ancilla-full CV SWAP test using a fault-tolerant ancilla prepared in a Kerr cat logical qubit appears in Ref.
[
26
]
.

To prove (
8
), one verifies that the unitary has the correct action on the canonical operators,
\text{{SWAP}}a^{\dagger}_{1(2)}\text{{SWAP}}^{\dagger}=a^{\dagger}_{2(1)}
. One can see that the
SWAP
gate is a linear optical unitary (i.e., its generator is quadratic in the creation and annihilation operators and commutes with the total photon number
\sum_{j=1,2}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}
), and can therefore be written as a composition of 50:50 beamsplitters and local phase shifters according to the rectangular decomposition
[
27
]
. For example, defining the beamsplitter as in (
1
), one gets

\text{{SWAP}}=e^{i{\pi\over 2}(a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{1}+a^{\dagger}_{2}a_{2})}U_{\text{BS}}({\pi\over 2},-{\pi\over 2}).

(9)

Note that
U_{\text{BS}}({\pi\over 4},0)|n\rangle_{1}|m\rangle_{2}
is an eigenvector of
SWAP
with eigenvalue
(-1)^{n}
, and that these form a complete set of eigenvectors.
Thus we can estimate the expectation value of
SWAP
using the circuit in Fig.
1
d, in which only the photon count
n
in the first register contributes to the overlap estimator.
Note that this is inherently different from the particular DV SWAP test algorithm discussed in the previous section, where the eigenvalue of
SWAP
is determined by the measurement outcomes on both registers.
One might try to reverse engineer an ancilla-free DV SWAP test algorithm for qudits based on the present CV algorithm. Alas, this is not possible because the beamsplitter unitary maps some states in the qudit subspace outside this subspace. This demonstrates that calculating some functions of qudit states might be much easier when embedded in a CV system, due to the fact that the state is occasionally allowed to leave the subspace associated with the qudit.

Further,
SWAP
in (
9
) commutes with the projections
Q_{2M}
of subspaces spanned by
\{|n\rangle_{A}|m\rangle_{B}:n+m\leq 2M\}
and, as a consequence, also commutes with its complement
Q_{2M}^{C}=\mathbb{I}-Q_{2M}
. The set of states
\{U_{\text{BS}}({\pi\over 4},0)|n\rangle_{A}|m\rangle_{B}:n+m\leq 2M\}
is therefore an alternative orthonormal basis for the subspace
Q_{2M}\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
associated with
Q_{2M}
.
We define two operators that implement
SWAP
on the
Q_{2M}\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
subspace and its complement via

\displaystyle\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}

\displaystyle\equiv Q_{2M}\,\text{{SWAP}}\,Q_{2M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n+m\leq 2M}\left[(-1)^{n}U_{\text{BS}}\left({\pi\over 4},0\right)|n\rangle_{A}|m\rangle_{B}\langle n|_{A}\langle m|_{B}U_{\text{BS}}\left({\pi\over 4},0\right)^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}^{C}

\displaystyle\equiv Q_{2M}^{C}\,\text{{SWAP}}\,Q_{2M}^{C}

(10)

which satisfy
\text{{SWAP}}=\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}+\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}^{C}
.

Consider states
\rho
and
\sigma
such that
\text{supp }\rho\otimes\sigma
is contained in
Q_{2M}\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. Then

\displaystyle\text{tr}\rho\sigma

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}\rho\otimes\sigma

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}Q_{2M}\rho\otimes\sigma Q_{2M}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}(\rho\otimes\sigma).

(11)

Therefore, photon number-resolving detection with a finite experimental threshold on the total number of photons can be used to estimate the expectation of
SWAP
in
\rho\otimes\sigma
granted all photon numbers above the threshold have zero probability. In an experiment, if the input states have local Fock cutoff
M^{\prime}
, the local photon number-resolving detectors should have a threshold of at least
2M^{\prime}
photons, i.e., (
11
) holds for
M\geq M^{\prime}
. Thus
2M^{\prime}
is the minimal photon detector threshold that allows to apply the ancilla-free, CV SWAP test for input states with local Fock cutoff
M^{\prime}
.

This fact provides an opportunity to concretely illustrate how the ancilla-free CV SWAP test takes into account amplitudes on all
2M^{\prime}
Fock basis states to estimate the overlap. Consider
M^{\prime}=1
and the task of estimating the overlap of single photon states
|\psi\rangle=\alpha_{1}|0\rangle+\beta_{1}|1\rangle
and
|\phi\rangle=\alpha_{2}|0\rangle+\beta_{2}|1\rangle
with our algorithm. The overlap is a homogeneous polynomial of order 4 in
\alpha_{1}
,
\overline{\alpha_{1}}
,
\alpha_{2}
,
\overline{\alpha_{2}}
,
\beta_{1}
,
\overline{\beta_{1}}
,
\beta_{2}
,
\overline{\beta_{2}}
, and this polynomial contains the term
|\beta_{1}|^{2}|\beta_{2}|^{2}
whenever both
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are nonzero. But writing out
U_{\text{BS}}(\pi/4,0)^{\dagger}|\psi\rangle|\phi\rangle
, one sees that there is no way to get this term without registering both the
|0\rangle|2\rangle
and
|2\rangle|0\rangle
photon count outcomes with +1 weight. Physically, these outcomes can be considered as “Hong-Ou-Mandel” contributions to the overlap. More generally, when
\rho
and
\sigma
have support on Fock states up to
|M^{\prime}\rangle
, one needs to take
M\geq M^{\prime}
in the expression (
11
) so that all photon interferences arising from
U_{\text{BS}}({\pi\over 4},0)^{\dagger}
contribute to the overlap estimate.

To rigorously extend the above argument to CV states
\rho\otimes\sigma
with arbitrary support requires some analysis in separable Hilbert space. For instance, the photon number operator needs to have its domain defined, its spectral theorem should be stated, etc. We will forego the formalities, simply noting that
\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a countable orthonormal basis for a CV mode
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
as discussed in Section
1.1
. Consider pure CV states
|\psi\rangle
and
|\phi\rangle
. Their respective amplitudes in the above orthonormal basis are square summable by Parseval identity, so
\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}Q_{2M}|\psi\rangle_{A}|\phi\rangle_{B}=|\psi\rangle_{A}|\phi\rangle_{B}
. Define two sequences of states
\xi_{2M}\equiv{1\over q_{2M}}Q_{2M}|\psi\rangle_{A}|\phi\rangle_{B}\langle\psi|_{A}\langle\phi|_{B}Q_{2M}
and
\zeta_{2M}\equiv{1\over 1-q_{2M}}(\mathbb{I}-Q_{2M})|\psi\rangle_{A}|\phi\rangle_{B}\langle\psi|_{A}\langle\phi|_{B}(\mathbb{I}-Q_{2M})
, where
q_{2M}:=\langle\psi|\langle\phi|Q_{2M}|\psi\rangle|\phi\rangle
allows to define the respective normalization factors. Then

\displaystyle|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{A}\otimes|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|_{B}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}+\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}^{C})|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{A}\otimes|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|_{B}

\displaystyle=q_{2M}\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}\xi_{2M}+(1-q_{2M})\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}^{C}\zeta_{2M}

(12)

where the second line uses the fact that
(\mathbb{I}-Q_{2M})\text{{SWAP}}Q_{2M}=(\mathbb{I}-Q_{2M})Q_{2M}\text{{SWAP}}=0
.
Using (
11
), the result (
12
) is extended by bilinearity (in the tensor factors
A
and
B
) to normal states
[
28
]

\rho_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}
of two CV modes. The error due to Fock cutoff can be bounded as:

\displaystyle\big|\text{tr}\rho\sigma-\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}(\rho\otimes\sigma))\big|

\displaystyle=|\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}^{C}(\rho\otimes\sigma)|

\displaystyle=|\text{tr}\text{{SWAP}}Q_{2M}^{C}(\rho\otimes\sigma)Q_{2M}^{C}|

\displaystyle\leq\text{tr}Q_{2M}^{C}(\rho\otimes\sigma)Q_{2M}^{C}

\displaystyle\leq 1-q_{2M}

(13)

\displaystyle\leq 1-q^{\rho}_{M}q^{\sigma}_{M}

(14)

where
q^{\rho}_{M}=\text{tr}(\rho\sum_{n=0}^{M}|n\rangle\langle n|)
and similarly for
q^{\sigma}_{M}
.
Again from the Parseval identity, it follows that the left hand side of (

M
.
Thus the accuracy of the estimate obtained using a photon number-resolving measurement depends on
M
and the precision depends on the number of shots
S

\text{tr}(\text{{SWAP}}_{2M}\rho\otimes\sigma)

3.1
Experimental demonstration

To provide a proof-of-principle demonstration of the ancilla-free CV SWAP test, it would be ideal to implement an experiment showing convergence of the estimator (
15
) with different states and energy ranges. However, such an experiment is not presently possible using cloud-based CV processors such as Xanadu’s X8 device
[
24
]
, which restricts the input state, decomposition of linear optical circuit, and photon number-resolving measurement cutoff. In particular, one cannot probe different energy ranges. Further, on the optical chip, a linear optical unitary is always precompiled to the rectangular decomposition, and there is no option to change the decomposition (the optical chip is not reconfigurable). Specifications of the optical chip and photon number-resolving measurement for the X8 can be found in Ref.
[
24
]
; for the present purposes we note that the photodetectors have a quantum efficiency above 95% and a local photon number cutoff between 5 and 7.

Putting aside the limitations of the X8 device for doing a full experimental analysis, it is possible to implement the CV SWAP circuit in order to verify that it produces an approximation to the analytical result (recall that the multimode ancilla-free CV SWAP test simply requires applying the beamsplitter

4.1
Variational quantum compiling

The CV SWAP test provides an alternative method for computing cost functions that appear in the task of variational quantum compiling of CV circuits
[
31

32
]

4K

|\psi_{\text{in}}\rangle\equiv\bigotimes_{j=1}^{K}|\psi_{\text{in}}^{(j)}\rangle

(27)

where
A_{j},A_{j}^{\prime}\cong A
,
R_{j},R_{j}^{\prime}\cong R
. The circuit in Fig.
1
d is then applied to all mode pairs
A_{j}A_{j}^{\prime}
and
R_{j}R_{j}^{\prime}
(the CV circuit architecture may require that beamsplitters be applied to adjacent modes, in which case the appropriate

gates should be applied). This procedure works because the states

4.2
Two-copy test

The CV SWAP test immediately gives a CV generalization of the two-copy test
[
14
]
.
The latter is based on the observation that for pure states
|\Psi\rangle
, the squared expectation value
|\langle\Psi|U|\Psi\rangle|^{2}
for some unitary operator
U
is equal to the overlap between states
|\Psi\rangle
and
U|\Psi\rangle
. Note that both real and imaginary parts of
\langle\Psi|U|\Psi\rangle
can be estimated using the Hadamard test which also works for mixed states. However, the two-copy test has some advantages.
Unlike the Hadamard test which requires controlled-
U
, the two-copy test only uses
U
. In general, controlled versions of unitaries require more complicated (larger depth) quantum circuits, which for noisy devices can be detrimental. Moreover, a controlled unitary cannot be enacted if one only has black-box access to the unitary
[
33
]
. The two-copy test is useful whenever one does not know how to measure in the eigenbasis of
U
, but
U
is easy to implement.

We now describe the two-copy test in the CV setting. For a state
|\Psi\rangle
on
n
mode CV register
C
and a CV unitary
U
acting on
C^{\prime}
, one computes
|\langle\Psi|U|\Psi\rangle|^{2}
by preparing
|\Psi\rangle_{C}\otimes U|\Psi\rangle_{C^{\prime}}
with
C\cong C^{\prime}
, and applying the ancilla-free,
2
-mode CV SWAP test (the circuit is Fig.
1
d on each mode pair
C_{j}C_{j}^{\prime}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
). The full circuit has depth 2.

As an example, the CV two-copy test with
U
a cyclic permutation of many CV modes can be employed for computing
(\text{tr}\rho^{n})^{2}
,
n\geq 2
. This was also a primary application of the DV two-copy test
[
14
]
. Specifically, one takes
C
to be a CV register
C=AB=A_{1}B_{1}\cdots A_{n}B_{n}
and
U
to be the CV cyclic permutation
\texttt{PERM}_{A}\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{n}\text{{SWAP}}_{A_{j}A_{j+1}}
(where
n+1
is taken modulo
n
) on the
n
-mode register
A
consisting of isomorphic modes
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
(
\rho
can be considered as a state on
A_{j}
for any

).
We let
|\Psi\rangle_{C}\equiv\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}|\psi\rangle_{A_{j}B_{j}}
,
j=1,\dots,n
, and
|\psi\rangle
is a purification of
\rho
. We prepare a second copy
|\Psi\rangle_{C^{\prime}}
and the state
|\Psi\rangle_{C}\otimes|\Psi\rangle_{C^{\prime}}
is then subjected to
\texttt{PERM}_{A^{\prime}}
, i.e., a cyclic permutation of the subsystem
A^{\prime}
in the second copy.
Finally, the ancilla-free CV SWAP test of Section
3
is carried out on the state
|\Psi\rangle_{C}\otimes\texttt{PERM}_{A^{\prime}}|\Psi\rangle_{C^{\prime}}
. This algorithm works because analogous to (
3
)

\langle\Psi_{C}|\texttt{PERM}_{A}|\Psi_{C}\rangle=\text{tr}\rho^{n}\,.

(30)

One can see that this CV two-copy test produces an estimate of
(\text{tr}\rho^{n})^{2}
.
For
n=2
, the full circuit for computing (
30
) from input
|\psi\rangle_{A_{1}B_{1}}|\psi\rangle_{A_{2}B_{2}}|\psi\rangle_{A_{1}^{\prime}B_{1}^{\prime}}|\psi\rangle_{A_{1}^{\prime}B_{1}^{\prime}}
is as shown in Fig.
4
. More generally, for a pure CV state
|\psi\rangle_{C_{1}\cdots C_{n}}
on
n
CV modes, the CV two-copy test allows to compute the quantities
(\text{tr}\rho_{\Sigma}^{n})^{2}
, where
\Sigma\subset\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\}

It should be noted that
\texttt{PERM}_{A^{\prime}}
does not need to be implemented as a gate as it amounts to a reindexing of the registers. This is the advantage of the two-copy test over the Hadamard test, where
\texttt{PERM}_{A^{\prime}}
is controlled on an ancilla and has to be implemented on the device.
Nevertheless, one may still have to implement some
SWAP
gates for the two-copy test in devices with limited connectivity. We note that a constant-depth version of the Hadamard test has been proposed that makes use of constant-depth preparation of ancilla Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) states and constant-depth implementation of of a multiply controlled
PERM

[
34
]
.

4.3
Extension of SWAP test and entanglement spectroscopy

We now discuss a novel CV algorithm for calculation of
\text{tr}(\rho^{L})
(

is possible in CV systems. To see this, note that similar to the construction of eigenvectors of the CV

such that

(here we are using a

(a_{0}^{\dagger},\ldots,a_{L-1}^{\dagger})
via a circulant matrix, so defining

\prod_{j=0}^{L-1}{b_{j}^{\dagger n_{j}}\over\sqrt{n_{j}!}}|\text{VAC}\rangle

\prod_{j=0}^{L-1}e^{2\pi ijn_{j}\over L}

and the

5
Discussion

The destructive, ancilla-free CV SWAP test presented in this work is envisioned as an equality testing subalgorithm
[
37
]
for CV states that can be embedded in larger CV quantum algorithms. Our discussions of applications such as variational quantum compiling and implementation in hybrid DV-CV systems indicate potential implementations in near-term quantum hardware. Indeed, we were able to carry out a demonstration on the X8 device, thereby showing the feasibility of the proposed CV SWAP test for few CV modes on a noisy photonic chip.

We note that a SWAP test for high-dimensional motional states of trapped ions has recently been implemented
[
38
]
, further suggesting the importance of the SWAP test beyond the qubit context. Advantages of the present proposal include: no ancilla degrees of freedom or controlled gates, fully linear optical depth 1 circuit, and photon number-resolving measurement that can be achieved using transition-edge sensors. These requirements are presently available on photonic chips and free-space optical setups which manipulate low-energy continuous variable states. Technologies that extend these systems to a wider range of frequency modes and energies will allow for practical implementation of the present algorithm in more quantum optical settings.
