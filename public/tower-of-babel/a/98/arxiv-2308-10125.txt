mKdV-Related Flows for Legendrian Curves in the Pseudohermitian 3-Sphere This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Symmetry, Invariants, and their Applications in honor of Peter J. Olver. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Olver.html
Annalisa CALINI a, Thomas IVEY a and Emilio MUSSO b

Abstract
We investigate geometric evolution equations for Legendrian curves in the 3-sphere which are invariant under the action of the unitary group U ⁡ ( 2 ) {\rm U}(2) . We define a natural symplectic structure on the space of Legendrian loops and show that the modified Korteweg–de Vries equation, along with its associated hierarchy, are realized as curvature evolutions induced by a sequence of Hamiltonian flows. For the flow among these that induces the mKdV equation, we investigate the geometry of solutions which evolve by rigid motions in U ⁡ ( 2 ) {\rm U}(2) . Generalizations of our results to higher-order evolutions and curves in similar geometries are also discussed.

Introduction

Broadly speaking, the study of integrable geometric evolution equations for curves has led to fruitful discoveries of the interactions between the structures associated to integrability (e.g., Lax pairs, conservation laws, and Bäcklund transformations) and the geometric and topological features of solution curves. While several authors (including some of us) have extensively studied integrable realizations of the sine-Gordon and nonlinear Schrödinger equations in Euclidean geometry
[
2
,
3
,
16
]
, additional integrable equations arise through investigations of flows in less familiar geometries. For example, the KdV equation appears in many geometric contexts, including flows for curves in the centroaffine plane
[
5
,
27
]
and null curves in Minkowski 3-space
[
23
]
, and investigations of curve evolutions in the projective plane and higher-dimensional centroaffine spaces have uncovered geometric flows that realize the Boussinesq and Kaup–Kuperschmidt hierarchies
[
6
,
22
]
.

In this article, we study flows for curves in 3-dimensional pseudohermitian Cauchy–Riemann (CR) geometry, specializing to the homogeneous geometry of the 3-sphere. Recall that a CR structure of hypersurface type (sometimes referred to as a pseudoconformal structure) on a manifold of real dimension
2n+1
consists of a contact structure together with a compatible almost-complex structure on the
2n
-dimensional contact planes.
(Additional non-degeneracy conditions are usually assumed, but these are vacuous
in the case
n=1
on which we will focus.)

The 3-sphere inherits its standard CR structure via its embedding as the hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
comprised of the set of unit length
of vectors with respect to the Hermitian inner product. The automorphism group of this structure is strictly larger than
{\rm U}(2)
; in fact, the pseudoconformal 3-sphere is preserved by the 8-dimensional group
{\rm SU}(2,1)
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
by preserving a Hermitian inner product of split signature. In that setting, the 3-sphere is identified with the projectivization of the cone of non-zero null vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
and the standard contact structure in
S^{3}
can be expressed in terms of this inner product.

In previous work
[
4
,
21
,
24
,
25
]
, we investigated
geometric invariants and geometric evolution equations for Legendrian curves as well as curves transverse to the contact distribution in the pseudoconformal 3-sphere.
The setting of this article is
pseudohermitian
CR geometry, a sub-geometry of CR geometry in which a contact form is specified, resulting in a compatible
hermitian metric on the contact planes. The canonical connection and curvature for such structures were introduced by Webster
[
30
]
. In dimension three, the simply-connected homogeneous pseudo-Hermitian CR manifolds with constant Webster curvature consist of the 3-sphere

S^{3}=\mathrm{U}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)
, the universal cover of anti-de Sitter space
A^{3}=\mathrm{U}(1,1)/\mathrm{U}(1)
, and the Heisenberg group
H^{3}
. In each case, the group preserves a fibration to a 2-dimensional space form, where the fibers are transverse to the contact planes.
For the case of the 3-sphere, this is the Clifford map

\pi_{C}\colon S^{3}\to S^{2}
,
a geometrical version of the Hopf fibration (see Section
2.1
for details).

In this article, we will focus on closed Legendrian curves in pseudo-Hermitian
S^{3}
, in particular their discrete invariants, how their geometry is related
to that of their images
under
\pi_{C}
, and integrable geometric evolution equations that arise naturally for such curves.
Among other results, we will show that there exists an infinite sequence of geometric evolution equations

\dfrac{\partial\gamma}{\partial t}=\mathbf{Z}_{n}[\gamma],\qquad n\geq 1,

(1.1)

which induce the
n
th flow of the mKdV hierarchy for the curvature of the Legendrian curve
\gamma
.

These evolution equations are related to geometric realizations of the mKdV hierarchy that
have already appeared in the literature.
Indeed, applying the Clifford projection to a Legendrian curve in
S^{3}
produces
a curve in
S^{2}
with the same curvature (up to a factor of 1/2), and curves evolving
by (
1.1
) project to curves evolving by flows previously
identified by Goldstein and Petrich
[
10
,
11
]
as inducing the mKdV hierarchy (see also the works by Doliwa and Santini
[
7
]
and by Langer and Perline
[
17
]
).
However, the flows we define in this work are geometrically distinctive for two reasons.
First, each flow
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
is Hamiltonian with respect to a symplectic structure on the space of periodic Legendrian curves, which is not induced by any corresponding
structure on
S^{2}
. Moreover, while curves in
S^{2}
can be lifted to Legendrian curves in
S^{3}
, the Clifford projection does not induce a surjective map from
the space of closed Legendrian curves to closed curves in
S^{2}
. As we shall see,
a closed curve in
S^{2}
must satisfy a rationality condition on its total curvature in order to have a lift into
S^{3}
as a closed Legendrian curve, where the resulting rational number is related to the discrete invariants of the lift, as a closed Legendrian curve.

We now summarize the contents of the paper:

–

In Section
2
, we review the geometry of Legendrian curves in
S^{3}
– in particular, the moving frame and curvature, and their relation to the projection under
\pi_{C}
. We define the Legendrian lift of a regular curve
in
S^{2}
, and compute the lifts of constant curvature circles as an example. We also review the discrete invariants of closed Legendrian curves, and compute such invariants for the circle lifts.

–

In Section
3
, we define a symplectic structure on the space of periodic Legendrian curves in
S^{3}
(modulo symmetries), and show that Hamiltonian vector field for total length induces mKdV evolution for curvature. More generally, we show that the entire mKdV hierarchy
can be induced by geometric flows for Legendrian curves.

–

In Section
4
, we compute the stationary curves for the mKdV vector field
Z_{1}
,
determining which of these are closed and, possibly, periodic in time. For a selection of representative examples, we use the Heisenberg projection, a contactomorphism of the sphere punctured at a point with
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, to obtain planar projections
that enable us to compute discrete invariants,
such as the Maslov index and Bennequin number (see
[
8
]
and the literature therein for an introduction to Legendrian knots and their contact invariants.)

–

In Section
5
, we discuss some open questions and directions for future research.

2
Legendrian curves in pseudohermitian
\boldsymbol{S^{3}}

2.1
Moving frames and curvature

Let
\langle-,-\rangle
be the standard Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and
S^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
denote the set of unit vectors.
We will think of a regular parametrized curve
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\to S^{3}

as a
\mathbb{C}^{2}
-valued function of parameter
x
.
Differentiating
\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle=1
shows that
\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma\rangle
is pure imaginary. The curve
\gamma
is
Legendrian
if it satisfies the condition

\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma\rangle=0.

(2.1)

We will say that
\gamma
is a unit-speed curve if
\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma_{x}\rangle=1
identically, and we will use
s
in place of
x
whenever we are assuming a unit-speed parametrization. We let
{\mathcal{P}}
denote the set of regular parametrized Legendrian curves in
S^{3}
, and
{\mathcal{P}}^{1}
denote the subset of those with unit speed.
The group
\mathrm{U}(2)
of unitary matrices acts transitively on
S^{3}
,
preserving the Hermitian inner product,
and thus inducing actions on
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}^{1}
.

Differentiating
\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle=1
and using both the Legendrian condition (
2.1
) and the unit-speed condition

\langle\gamma_{s},\gamma_{s}\rangle=1
, shows that the matrix

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma\ \gamma_{s}\end{pmatrix}

takes value in
\mathrm{U}(2)
. We will refer to this as the
{\rm U}(2)
-valued
moving frame of
\gamma
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The moving frame
\Gamma
satisfies the Frenet-type equation

\Gamma_{s}=\Gamma U,\qquad U=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\mathrm{i}k\end{pmatrix},

(2.2)

where we designate
k(s)
as the curvature function of
\gamma
.

Proof.

Expanding
\gamma_{ss}
in terms of the moving frame gives

\gamma_{ss}=-\gamma+\mathrm{i}k\gamma_{s},

(2.3)

where the first coefficient is determined by differentiating (
2.1
), and differentiating the unit-speed condition
\langle\gamma_{s},\gamma_{s}\rangle=1

implies the second coefficient is purely imaginary. Its imaginary part is computed by
k=\operatorname{Im}\langle\gamma_{ss},\gamma_{s}\rangle
.
∎

For a more general parametrization, we have the following.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\gamma
is a regular Legendrian curve parametrized by an arbitrary variable
x
, its curvature is given by

k=\dfrac{\operatorname{Im}\langle\gamma_{xx},\gamma_{x}\rangle}{\quad\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma_{x}\rangle^{3/2}}.

(2.4)

Moreover, if
\beta:=\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma_{x}\rangle^{1/2}
denotes the speed of
\gamma
,
then the analogue of (
2.3
) is

\gamma_{xx}=-\beta^{2}\gamma+\left(\dfrac{\beta_{x}}{\beta}+\mathrm{i}\beta k\right)\gamma_{x}.

(2.5)

Proof.

Since any arclength parameter satisfies
{\rm d}s/{\rm d}x=\beta
, then

\gamma_{s}=\beta^{-1}\gamma_{x},\qquad\gamma_{ss}=\beta^{-1}\bigl(\beta^{-1}\gamma_{x}\bigr)_{x}=\beta^{-2}\gamma_{xx}-\beta^{-3}\beta_{x}\gamma_{x}.

It follows that

\langle\gamma_{ss},\gamma_{s}\rangle=\beta^{-3}\langle\gamma_{xx},\gamma_{x}\rangle-\beta^{-2}\beta_{x}.

Since
\beta
is real, we obtain (
2.4
) by taking the imaginary part of each side of the last equation. Using (
2.3
), we write

\beta^{-2}\gamma_{xx}-\beta^{-3}\beta_{x}\gamma_{x}=-\gamma+\mathrm{i}\beta^{-1}k\gamma_{x},

which, when solved for
\gamma_{xx}
, gives (
2.5
).
∎

Definition 2.3
.

If
\gamma
is periodic with minimal period
L
, we say that
\gamma
is a
closed Legendrian curve of period
L
, and we let
|[\gamma]|\subset S^{3}
denote the image or

trace
of
\gamma
.
We let
{\mathcal{P}}^{1}_{L}
denote the space of closed unit-speed Legendrian curves in
{\mathrm{S}}^{3}
with fixed length
L
. If the map
\gamma\colon S^{1}_{L}\to S^{3}
is injective, we say that
\gamma
is a (parametrized)
Legendrian knot
.

We remark that the elements of
{\mathcal{P}}^{1}_{L}
can be viewed as isometric immersions of the circle
S^{1}_{L}
of circumference
L
into
S^{3}
. Also note that the curvature function of a closed curve of period
L
must satisfy
k(x+L)=k(x)
, but
L
is not necessarily the
minimal period of
k
.

Clifford projections and Legendrian lifts

The well-known diffeomorphism between
S^{3}
and the group
{\rm SU}(2)
can be defined
by mapping a unit vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}\in S^{3}
to the matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}&-\overline{w_{2}}\\
w_{2}&\hphantom{-}\overline{w_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm SU}(2).

(2.6)

We also identify the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
with
\mathbb{R}^{3}
via the linear isomorphism

\mathsf{j}\colon\ \begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}x_{1}&\mathrm{i}x_{2}+x_{3}\\
\mathrm{i}x_{2}-x_{3}&-\mathrm{i}x_{1}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{\mathsf{T}}.

Combining these allows us to define the 2-to-1 spin-covering homomorphism
\sigma\colon S^{3}\to{\rm SO}(3)
as

\sigma(\mathbf{w}):=\mathsf{j}\circ\operatorname{Ad}_{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}\circ~\mathsf{j}^{-1},

(2.7)

where
\operatorname{Ad}
denotes the adjoint representation of
{\rm SU}(2)
, and we take the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We define the
Clifford map

\pi_{C}\colon S^{3}\to S^{2}
in terms of
\sigma
as follows. Let
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3}\}
be the standard basis on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
;
then

\pi_{C}(\mathbf{w}):=\sigma(\mathbf{w})\mathbf{e}_{1}.

Since
\sigma\bigl({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathbf{w}\bigr)
differs from
\sigma(\mathbf{w})
by rotation that fixes
\mathbf{e}_{1}
,
this endows
S^{3}
with the structure of a circle bundle whose fibers are the integral curves of the
characteristic vector field

\mathbf{w}\mapsto\mathrm{i}\mathbf{w}
. Thus, condition (
2.1
) can be interpreted as saying that a curve in
{\mathrm{S}}^{3}
is Legendrian if and only if it is orthogonal to the fibers of the Clifford map.

Definition 2.4
.

The
Clifford projection
of a Legendrian curve
\gamma
is the immersed curve
{\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\!\to\!S^{2}}
defined by
\eta=\pi_{C}\circ\gamma
. (Note that
\gamma
is regular if and only if
\eta
is regular.)

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\gamma
be a Legendrian curve parametrized by arclength
s
, with curvature function
k(s)
. Then its Clifford projection has speed
2
and Frenet curvature
k/2
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}
be the vector of components of
\gamma
. By applying (
2.7
) to the standard basis, we can write
\sigma(\mathbf{w})
in matrix form as

\sigma(\mathbf{w})=\begin{pmatrix}|w_{1}|^{2}-|w_{2}|^{2}&-w_{1}w_{2}&-\overline{w_{1}}\overline{w_{2}}\\
w_{1}\overline{w_{2}}+\overline{w_{1}}w_{2}&\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(w_{1}^{2}-w_{2}^{2}\bigr)&\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\overline{w_{1}}^{2}-\overline{w_{2}}^{2}\bigr)\\
\mathrm{i}(w_{1}\overline{w_{2}}-\overline{w_{1}}w_{2})&\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\bigl(w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}\bigr)&-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\bigl(\overline{w_{1}}^{2}+\overline{w_{2}}^{2}\bigr)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\hphantom{-}0\\
0&1&-\mathrm{i}\\
0&1&\hphantom{-}\mathrm{i}\end{pmatrix},

(2.8)

and compute

\sigma(\mathbf{w})_{s}=\sigma(\mathbf{w})\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\cos\alpha&-2\sin\alpha\\
2\cos\alpha&0&0\\
2\sin\alpha&0&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\cos\alpha+\mathrm{i}\sin\alpha=w_{1}w_{2}^{\prime}-w_{1}^{\prime}w_{2}
. (The fact that this has unit modulus follows from the Legendrian condition
w_{1}\overline{w_{1}}^{\prime}+w_{2}\overline{w_{2}}^{\prime}=0
and the unit-speed condition
w_{1}^{\prime}\overline{w_{1}}^{\prime}+w_{2}^{\prime}\overline{w_{2}}^{\prime}=1
.)
The last equation shows that
\sigma(\mathbf{w})
gives a parallel orthonormal along the Clifford projection.

In order to construct a Frenet frame for the Clifford projection, we modify
\sigma(\mathbf{w})
. From (
2.8
), one can check that

\sigma\bigl({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\mathbf{w}\bigr)=\sigma(\mathbf{w})\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos(2\phi)&\sin(2\phi)\\
0&-\sin(2\phi)&\cos(2\phi)\end{pmatrix}.

(Note that the first column of
\sigma(\mathbf{w})
, which gives the value of the Clifford map, is unchanged.)
In particular, setting
\phi=-\alpha/2
and differentiating this formula shows that
F=\sigma\bigl({\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha/2}\mathbf{w}\bigr)
satisfies

F_{s}=F\begin{pmatrix}0&-2&0\\
2&\hphantom{-}0&-\alpha_{s}\\
0&\hphantom{-}\alpha_{s}&0\end{pmatrix}.

On the other hand, the component-wise form of (
2.3
) gives

w_{1}^{\prime\prime}=-w_{1}+\mathrm{i}kw_{1}^{\prime},\qquad w_{2}^{\prime\prime}=-w_{2}+\mathrm{i}kw_{2}^{\prime},

from which we compute

\mathrm{i}\alpha_{s}=\frac{(w_{1}w_{2}^{\prime}-w_{1}^{\prime}w_{2})^{\prime}}{w_{1}w_{2}^{\prime}-w_{1}^{\prime}w_{2}}=\frac{w_{1}w_{2}^{\prime\prime}-w_{1}^{\prime\prime}w_{2}}{w_{1}w_{2}^{\prime}-w_{1}^{\prime}w_{2}}=\mathrm{i}k.

This gives
\alpha_{s}=k
and the result follows.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Conversely, suppose
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\to S^{2}
is a curve parametrized by
x
with constant speed 2 and curvature function
k/2
. Identify
{\rm SO}(3)
with the oriented orthonormal frame bundle of
S^{2}
, with the basepoint map
{\rm SO}(3)\to S^{2}
is given by the first column. Then the Frenet frame
F=(\eta,T,N)
is a lift of
\eta
into
{\rm SO}(3)
satisfying

F_{x}=F\begin{pmatrix}0&-2&\hphantom{-}0\\
2&\hphantom{-}0&-k\\
0&\hphantom{-}k&\hphantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}.

In turn, let
\widetilde{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}\to S^{3}
be a lift of
F
relative to the double cover
\sigma

(
i.e., such that
F=\sigma\circ\widetilde{\gamma}
)
, which is unique
up to a minus sign. Then, if
\alpha(x)
is an antiderivative of
k(x)
,

\gamma(x)={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha/2}\widetilde{\gamma}(x)
is a unit-speed Legendrian lift of
\eta
, unique up to multiplication by a unit modulus constant.

2.2
Closed Legendrian curves in
\boldsymbol{S^{3}}
and their discrete invariants

The classical invariants of closed Legendrian curves and Legendrian knots are the Maslov index (or rotation number) and the Bennequin invariant (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).
We first discuss how these are computed, before passing to less familiar discrete invariants.

For a given unit vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}\in S^{3}
, let
\mathbf{w}^{*}=(-\overline{w_{2}},\overline{w_{1}})^{\mathsf{T}}
(i.e., the second column in (
2.6
)).
Then vector fields
\mathbf{w}\mapsto\mathbf{w}^{*}
and
\mathbf{w}\mapsto\mathrm{i}\mathbf{w}^{*}
give an orthogonal parallelization of the contact distribution on
S^{3}
.
Hence, for any
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}^{1}_{L}
there is a unique smooth function
\theta\colon\mathbb{R}/(L\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
such that

\gamma_{s}={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\gamma^{*},

(2.9)

and the Maslov index
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\gamma}
is the degree of
\theta
.

If
|[\gamma]|
is a Legendrian knot
{\mathcal{K}}
, we define the Bennequin invariant
{\bf tb}_{\gamma}
by the following construction.
For
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, let
{\mathcal{K}}_{\varepsilon}={\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}{\mathcal{K}}
. Then there exists
a connected open interval
I\subset(0,1)
such that
{\mathcal{K}}\cap{\mathcal{K}}_{\varepsilon}=\varnothing
for every
\varepsilon\in I
, and the Bennequin invariant
{\bf tb}_{\gamma}
equals the linking number
{\rm Lk}({\mathcal{K}},{\mathcal{K}}_{\varepsilon})
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\eta
be the Clifford projection of
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}^{1}_{L}
and let
L_{\eta}
be its length. Since the Clifford map doubles the speed of the curve, there is a positive integer
\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}
called the
Clifford index

such that
2L=\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}L_{\eta}
.

We say that
\gamma
has
spin
1
if
\sigma\circ\gamma
has trivial homotopy class in
\pi_{1}({\rm SO}(3))=\mathbb{Z}/2
, or has
spin
1/2
if the homotopy class is non-trivial. Similarly, we say a closed curve in
S^{2}
has spin 1 if its Frenet frame
F
has trivial homotopy class,
and has spin 1/2 if the Frenet frame has non-trivial homotopy class.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}^{1}_{L}
. Then

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{L}k(s)\,{\rm d}s.

Hence, the total curvature
1
1

1

For the sake of convenience, for us the ‘total curvature’ will always mean the integral of the curvature function with respect to arclength,
divided by

2\pi
.
of a closed Legendrian curve is an integer. In addition, this implies that the total curvature of the Clifford projection is the rational number
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\gamma}/\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}
.

Proof.

Differentiating (
2.9
), we get

\gamma_{ss}=\mathrm{i}\theta_{s}\gamma_{s}+{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(\gamma^{*})_{s}=\mathrm{i}\theta_{s}\gamma_{s}+{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(\gamma_{s})^{*}=\mathrm{i}\theta_{s}\gamma_{s}-\gamma.

Comparing with (
2.3
) shows that
\theta_{s}=k
. Hence,
\operatorname{deg}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{L}k(s)\,{\rm d}s
.
On the other hand, if we use
\check{s}
to denote the arclength parameter along the Clifford projection, then
\check{s}=2s
and
\kappa(\check{s})=\tfrac{1}{2}k(2s)
, so

\int_{0}^{L}k(s)\,{\rm d}s=\int_{0}^{\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}L_{\eta}}\kappa(\check{s})\,{\rm d}\check{s}=\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{L_{\eta}}\kappa(\check{s})\,{\rm d}\check{s},

and the second assertion follows.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Conversely, let
\eta
be a closed curve in
S^{2}
of length
2T
, with total curvature equal to
a/b
, where

and

Hence, in the case of spin 1, the Clifford index is

if

\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}=2b

In the case of spin

\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}=b

\mathbf{cl}_{\gamma}=2b
if
a+b
is odd.

Definition 2.10
.

The CR-analogue of stereographic projection is the
Heisenberg projection

{\bf p}_{H}
, a
contactomorphism
from the punctured
sphere to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the contact form
{\rm d}z-y{\rm d}x+x{\rm d}y
.
Taking
{\rm S}=(-1,0)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
as the omitted point, then for
\bigl(z^{1},z^{2}\bigr)\in S^{3}\setminus\{{\rm S}\}
,

{{\bf p}_{H}}\colon\ \bigl(z^{1},z^{2}\bigr)\mapsto\biggl(\mathrm{Re\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}z_{2}}{1+z_{1}}\biggr),\mathrm{Im}\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}z_{2}}{1+z_{1}}\biggr)},\mathrm{Re}\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{i}(1-z_{1})}{1+z_{1}}\biggr)\biggr)^{\mathsf{T}}.

(2.10)

Remark 2.11
.

The Maslov index and the Bennequin invariant can be computed directly from the Heisenberg projection
\gamma_{H}={\bf p}_{H}\circ\gamma
, assuming that
{\rm S}\notin|[\gamma]|
.
The Maslov index is the turning number of the
Lagrangian projection
of
\gamma_{H}
, defined by
\alpha=\pi_{z}\circ\gamma_{H}
, where
\pi_{z}
denotes the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{3}
onto the
xy
-coordinate plane. If
\gamma
is a knot, the Bennequin number is the writhe of
\alpha
with respect to upward oriented
z
-axis (see, e.g.,
[
8

Lemma 3.1

{\mathcal{P}}^{1}_{L}
. By Proposition
3.5
, this vector field induces the curvature evolution

k_{t}=k_{sss}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(k^{3}\bigr)_{s}+4k_{s},

which differs by a Galilean transformation from the mKdV equation (with
s
as spatial variable). In what follows, we will identify an infinite hierarchy
of Hamiltonian vector fields that induce evolution equations for curvature which belong to the mKdV hierarchy.

3.2
The mKdV hierarchy

In this subsection, we review the recursive construction of the mKdV hierarchy.
What follows is essentially the same as in Olver
[
26
]
, with some changes in notation; for example,
we use
u
to denote a scalar function of spatial variable
s
and time
t
, and let
u_{0}=u
and
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots

denote its successive
s
-derivatives.
In this notation, the mKdV takes the form

u_{t}=u_{3}+\frac{3}{2}u^{2}u_{1}.

(3.10)

On the space
J[u]
of differential polynomials
in finitely many of the
u_{j}
, we will need the
total
s
-derivative
operator
\delta

and the
Euler operator

\mathtt{E}
, defined respectively by

\delta\mathsf{P}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{u}_{j+1}\dfrac{\partial\mathsf{P}}{\partial u_{j}},\qquad\mathtt{E}\mathsf{P}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-\delta)^{j}\biggl(\dfrac{\partial\mathsf{P}}{\partial u_{j}}\biggr),\qquad\mathsf{P}\in J[u].

The mKdV hierarchy is an infinite sequence of evolution equations

{u}_{t}=\mathsf{M}_{j},\qquad j\geq 1,

where
\mathsf{M}_{1}=u_{1}
and
\mathsf{M}_{2}
is the right-hand side of (
3.10
). It can be
constructed recursively as follows.
There is an infinite sequence
\{\rho_{j}\}
of differential polynomials in
{u}
with
\rho_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}{u}^{2}
and satisfying

\mathfrak{D}\mathtt{E}\rho_{j+1}=\mathfrak{E}\mathtt{E}\rho_{j},

(3.11)

where

\mathfrak{D}=\delta,\qquad\mathfrak{E}=\delta^{3}+\delta{u}\delta^{-1}{u}\delta.

Then
\mathsf{M}_{j}=\mathfrak{D}\mathtt{E}\rho_{j}
(e.g.,
\mathsf{M}_{1}={u}_{1}
).
Since
\mathfrak{D}
,
\mathfrak{E}
are a
Hamiltonian pair
of skew-adjoint differential operators (in the sense of
[
26
, Definition 7.19]
) each member of the mKdV hierarchy is a bi-Hamiltonian system; moreover, each
\rho_{j}
is a conserved density for each of these flows (see
[
26
, Theorem 7.24]
).

It follows from (
3.11
) that the
\mathsf{M}_{j}
satisfy the recurrence
\mathsf{M}_{j+1}=\mathfrak{R}\mathsf{M}_{j}
, where

\mathfrak{R}=\mathfrak{E}\circ\mathfrak{D}^{-1}=\delta^{2}+\delta{u}\delta^{-1}{u},

while the densities satisfy the recurrence
\mathtt{E}\rho_{j+1}=\mathfrak{R}^{*}\mathtt{E}\rho_{j}
for
\mathfrak{R}^{*}=\mathfrak{D}^{-1}\circ\mathfrak{E}
. For example,

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{1}=u_{1},\qquad\!

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{2}=u_{3}+\frac{3}{2}u^{2}u_{1},\qquad\!

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{3}=u_{{5}}+\frac{5}{2}u_{{0}}^{2}u_{{3}}+10\,u_{{0}}u_{{1}}u_{{2}}+\frac{5}{2}u_{{1}}^{3}+\frac{15}{8}u_{{1}}u_{{0}}^{4},\quad\!

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle\rho_{1}=\frac{1}{2}u^{2},\qquad\!

\displaystyle\rho_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}u^{4},\qquad\!

\displaystyle\rho_{3}=\frac{1}{2}u_{{2}}^{2}+\frac{5}{6}u_{{0}}^{3}u_{{2}}+\frac{5}{4}u_{{0}}^{2}u_{{1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{16}u_{{0}}^{6},\quad\!

\displaystyle\ldots

It also follows from
\mathsf{M}_{j+1}=\bigl(\delta^{2}+\delta{u}\delta^{-1}{u}\bigr)\mathsf{M}_{j}
that there are differential polynomials
\mathsf{N}_{j}
such that

{u}\mathsf{M}_{j}=\delta\mathsf{N}_{j}
.

Let
\mathsf{L}_{j}=2\mathtt{E}\rho_{j}
. The connection between the ingredients of the mKdV hierarchy and deformations of Legendrian curves becomes apparent when we observe that if

\mathbf{V}=p\gamma_{s}+q(\mathrm{i}\gamma_{s})+r(\mathrm{i}\gamma)\in T_{\gamma}{\mathcal{P}}^{1},

then by Corollary
3.4
,
p
,
q
and
r
satisfy the same relationships as
N_{j}
,
M_{j}
and
L_{j}
, respectively – provided we replace
{u}
by
k
. Moreover, from (
3.7
) the curvature of a unit-speed curve, under flow by
\mathbf{V}
, evolves by

k_{t}=(\mathfrak{R}+4)q,

where again we replace
u
by
k
in the expression for
\mathfrak{R}
.
Matters being so, we define the following
mKdV-type vector fields

\mathbf{V}_{j}=\mathsf{N}_{j}\gamma_{s}+\mathsf{M}_{j}(\mathrm{i}\gamma_{s})+\mathsf{L}_{j}(\mathrm{i}\gamma),\qquad j\geq 1.

(From now on, we will take
\mathsf{L}_{j}
,
\mathsf{M}_{j}
,
\mathsf{N}_{j}
, as well as the operators for the mKdV hierarchy, as having
u
replaced by
k
.)
As noted, these are tangent to the submanifold
{\mathcal{P}}^{1}
, and it is easy to see that on unit-speed curves they induce curvature evolutions by linear combinations of members of the mKdV hierarchy (see below). However, these vector fields are defined on the larger space
{\mathcal{P}}
, and in the periodic case we will explore their properties in relation to the symplectic structure.

For example, when we restrict our attention to the space
{\mathcal{P}}_{L}
of periodic curves, the vector field

\mathbf{V}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}k^{2}\gamma_{s}+k_{s}(\mathrm{i}\gamma_{s})+2k(\mathrm{i}\gamma)=\dfrac{k^{2}}{2\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma_{x}\rangle^{1/2}}\gamma_{x}+\dfrac{k_{x}}{\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma_{x}\rangle}\mathrm{i}\gamma_{x}+2k(\mathrm{i}\gamma)

is equivalent to the Hamiltonian vector field from Proposition
3.3
. Similarly, we will show below
that the rest of the vector fields
\mathbf{V}_{j}
represent Hamiltonian vector fields on
\mathcal{Q}_{L}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{j}
be the vector field on
\mathcal{Q}_{L}
represented by
\mathbf{V}_{j}
. Then
\Omega(\mathcal{V}_{m},\mathcal{V}_{j})=0
for
j=1,\ldots,m-1
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}_{L}
. By definition of
\Omega_{[\gamma]}
, the statement is equivalent to
\int_{0}^{L}\mathsf{L}_{m}(\mathsf{L}_{j})_{x}\,{\rm d}x=0
.
Because this equation is invariant under arbitrary reparametrizations, it
suffices to verify this when
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}^{1}_{L}
and
x=s
, an arclength parameter. The rest of the argument is essentially
the same as the last step in the proof of
[
26
, Lemma 7.25]
.
∎

To show that the
\mathcal{V}_{j}
are Hamiltonian, we need to compute the variations of reparametrization-invariant integrals involving the curvature and its derivatives.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}_{L}
, let
\widehat{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,t)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{L}
be a smooth variation of
\gamma
, and let
\mathbf{V}
be as
in (
3.3
), but with the subscript
V
omitted from the components
p
,
q
,
r
. Let
k_{j}
denote the
j
th derivative of curvature with respect to arclength and let
\beta=\langle\gamma_{x},\gamma_{x}\rangle^{1/2}
. Then for the functional

{\mathscr{H}}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{L}f(k,k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\beta\,{\rm d}x,

we have

\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}{\mathscr{H}}(\widehat{\gamma})=\int_{0}^{L}(\mathtt{E}f)\biggl(\dfrac{(\beta q_{x})_{x}}{\beta}+q\biggl(\dfrac{\beta_{x}}{\beta}\biggr)_{x}+\beta^{2}(k^{2}+4)q\biggr)-(\mathtt{F}f)\beta^{2}kq\,{\rm d}x,

where

\mathtt{E}f=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\biggl(-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial s}\biggr)^{m}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial k_{m}},\qquad\mathtt{F}f=f-\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}k_{m-j}\biggl(-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial s}\biggr)^{j}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial k_{m}}.

Proof.

By straightforward differentiation,

\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\bigg|_{t=0}{\mathscr{H}}(\widehat{\gamma})=\int_{0}^{L}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{n}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial k_{m}}\dot{k}_{m}\Bigg)\beta+f\dot{\beta}\,{\rm d}x,

(3.12)

where the dot indicates
\partial/\partial t
.
We wish to express
\dot{k}_{m}
in terms of
\dot{k}
. For this purpose, note that

\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right]=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\beta^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right]=-\beta^{-2}\dot{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}=-\beta^{-1}\dot{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}.

Applying this to
k_{j}
, we obtain

\dot{k}_{j+1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\dot{k}_{j}-\beta^{-1}\dot{\beta}k_{j+1}.

By iterating this formula, it follows that

\dot{k}_{m}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)^{m}\dot{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)^{j}\bigl(\beta^{-1}\dot{\beta}k_{m-j}\bigr).

We substitute this in (
3.12
), use
{\rm d}x=\beta^{-1}{\rm d}s
to apply integration by parts with respect to
s
, and substitute expressions for
\dot{k}
from (
3.6
) and for
\dot{\beta}
from (
3.9
), giving

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathscr{H}}[\mathbf{V}]

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{x=L}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{n}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial k_{m}}\left(\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)^{m}\dot{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)^{j}\bigl(\beta^{-1}\dot{\beta}k_{m-j}\bigr)\right)\right]+f\beta^{-1}\dot{\beta}\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{x=L}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left(\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)^{m}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial k_{m}}\right)\right]\dot{k}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}+\left[f-\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}k_{m-j}\left(\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)^{j}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial k_{m}}\right)\right]\beta^{-1}\dot{\beta}\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}(\mathtt{E}f)\biggl(\dfrac{(\beta q_{x})_{x}}{\beta}+q\left(\dfrac{\beta_{x}}{\beta}\right)_{x}+\beta k_{x}p+\beta^{2}\bigl(k^{2}+4\bigr)q\biggr)+(\mathtt{F}f)\bigl((\beta p)_{x}-\beta^{2}kq\bigr)\,{\rm d}x.

It is easy to check that

(\mathtt{E}f)k_{x}=(\mathtt{F}f)_{x},

(3.13)

and thus the terms in
{\rm d}{\mathscr{H}}[\mathbf{V}]
involving
p
make up an exact
x
-derivative of a periodic function, whose integral is zero. This gives the desired expression.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Each mKdV vector field
\mathbf{V}_{j}
represents a Hamiltonian vector field on
\mathcal{Q}_{L}
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}_{L}
, and let functional
{\mathscr{H}}
and vector field
\mathbf{V}
be as in Lemma
3.7
. Because of reparametrization invariance of
{\mathscr{H}}
, we can assume that
\gamma
is parametrized with constant speed
\beta
. Then, using
r_{x}=2\beta^{2}q
, we have

{\rm d}{\mathscr{H}}[\mathbf{V}]=\int_{0}^{L}(\mathtt{E}f)\bigl(q_{xx}+\tfrac{1}{2}r_{x}\bigl(k^{2}+4\bigr)\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathtt{F}f)kr_{x}\,{\rm d}x.

Applying integration by parts to the second derivative term gives

{\rm d}{\mathscr{H}}[\mathbf{V}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\bigl(\beta^{-2}(\mathtt{E}f)_{xx}+\bigl(k^{2}+4\bigr)\mathtt{E}f-k\mathtt{F}f\bigr)r_{x}\,{\rm d}x.

Since
(k\mathtt{E}f-\mathtt{F}f)_{s}=k(\mathtt{E}f)_{s}
from (
3.13
), we can express this in terms of an mKdV recursion operator applied to
\mathtt{E}f
:

{\rm d}{\mathscr{H}}[\mathbf{V}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}r_{x}\bigl(\delta^{2}+k\delta^{-1}k\delta+4\bigr)\mathtt{E}f\,{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}r_{x}(\mathfrak{R}^{*}+4)\mathtt{E}f\,{\rm d}x,

where
\delta
denotes the total
s
-derivative.

Recall that the third ‘
r
’ component of
\mathbf{V}_{j}
is
\mathsf{L}_{j}
. Since
\mathsf{L}_{j}=2\mathtt{E}\rho_{j}
and
\mathfrak{R}^{*}
is the recursion operator for the
\mathsf{L}_{j}
, then

\mathsf{L}_{j+1}+4\mathsf{L}_{j}=2(\mathfrak{R}^{*}+4)\mathtt{E}\rho_{j},\qquad j\geq 1.

Using this inductively, we have

\mathsf{L}_{j+1}-(-4)^{j}\mathsf{L}_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}(-4)^{j-k}(\mathsf{L}_{k+1}+4\mathsf{L}_{k})=2(\mathfrak{R}^{*}+4)\sum_{k=1}^{j}(-4)^{j-k}\mathtt{E}\rho_{k}.

In particular, if we set
f=\sum_{k=1}^{j}(-4)^{j-k}\rho_{k}
, we see that

{\rm d}{\mathscr{H}}[\mathbf{V}]=\Omega\bigl(\mathbf{V}_{j+1}-(-4)^{j}\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}\bigr).

Since it has already been shown that
\mathbf{V}_{1}
is Hamiltonian, it follows that
\mathbf{V}_{n}
is Hamiltonian for any
n\geq 1
.
∎

From (
3.7
), when restricted to unit-speed curves, the Hamiltonian vector field
\mathbf{V}_{j}
induces the curvature evolution

k_{t}=(\mathfrak{R}+4)\mathsf{M}_{j}=\mathsf{M}_{j+1}+4\mathsf{M}_{j}

(3.14)

for
j\geq 1
. For the sake of convenience, we define
\mathbf{V}_{0}=\gamma_{x}
, which induces the translation flow
k_{t}=k_{s}
.
If we define

\mathbf{Z}_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-4)^{n-j}\mathbf{V}_{j},

then
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
induces the curvature evolution

k_{t}=(-4)^{n}\mathsf{M}_{1}+(\mathfrak{R}+4)\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-4)^{n-j}\mathsf{M}_{j}\Biggr),

since
\mathsf{M}_{1}=k_{s}
. Using (
3.14
) and telescoping, this simplifies to

k_{t}=(-4)^{n}\mathsf{M}_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-4)^{n-j}(\mathsf{M}_{j+1}+4\mathsf{M}_{j})=\mathsf{M}_{n+1}.

Thus,
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
induces the
(n+1)
st mKdV equation for curvature.

For later use, we now derive the evolution of the Frenet frame under flow
\mathbf{Z}_{1}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\widehat{\Gamma}(s,t)
be the
{\rm U}(2)
-valued Frenet frame for a smooth family of curves
\widehat{\gamma}(s,t)
parametrized
by arclength
s
, such that

\dfrac{\partial\widehat{\gamma}}{\partial t}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}=\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\widehat{k}^{2}-4\biggr)\widehat{\gamma}_{s}+\widehat{k}_{s}(\mathrm{i}\gamma_{s})+2\widehat{k}(\mathrm{i}\gamma),

where
\widehat{k}(s,t)
is the curvature. Then
\widehat{\Gamma}
satisfies
\dfrac{\partial\widehat{\Gamma}}{\partial t}=\widehat{\Gamma}P
, where

P=\begin{pmatrix}2\mathrm{i}\widehat{k}&4-\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{k}^{2}+\mathrm{i}\widehat{k}_{s}\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{k}^{2}-4+\mathrm{i}\widehat{k}_{s}&\mathrm{i}\bigl(\widehat{k}_{ss}+\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{k}^{3}-2\widehat{k}\bigr)\end{pmatrix}.

(3.15)

Proof.

Since
\widehat{\gamma}
is the first column of
\widehat{\Gamma}
, then entries of the first column of
P
are dictated by the flow of
\widehat{\gamma}
.
Since
\widehat{\Gamma}
is
{\rm U}(2)
-valued then

takes value in
{\mathfrak{u}}(2)
, and this determines the upper-right entry. Finally,
since
\widehat{\Gamma}_{s}=\widehat{\Gamma}U
where
U=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\

, then equating mixed partials leads to the necessary compatibility condition

U_{t}-P_{s}-[U,P]=0,

which determines the lower right entry in
P
.
∎

4
Stationary curves

Let
\gamma
be a periodic unit-speed Legendrian curve with curvature function
k
and let
\widehat{\gamma}(s,t)
be its evolution by the flow of
{\bf Z}_{n}
.
We say that
\gamma
is
stationary
if
\widehat{\gamma}(s,t)={\rm A}(t)\gamma(s-at)
for some constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\rm A}\colon\mathbb{R}\to{\rm U}(2)
.
If
\gamma
is stationary, then the evolving curvature
\widehat{k}(s,t):=k(s-at)
is a periodic traveling wave solution of the
(n+1)
st mKdV equation.

This section focuses on closed curves with non-constant curvature which are stationary for
\mathbf{Z}_{1}
, which we will call
s
-loops
. By substituting
\widehat{k}
into the mKdV equation, we see that
k(s)=\widehat{k}(s,0)
is a solution of the third-order ODE

k^{\prime\prime\prime}+\frac{3}{2}k^{2}k^{\prime}+ak^{\prime}=0,

(4.1)

where prime denotes
{\rm d}/{\rm d}s
. Obviously the converse also holds: if
k
is a solution of (
4.1
), then
\widehat{k}(s,t):=k(s-at)
is a traveling wave solution of the mKdV equation.

Remark 4.1
.

Equation (
4.1
) implies that the Clifford projection of an
s
-loop is a closed elastic curve in
S^{2}
(see, e.g.,
[
13
]
and the literature therein). However, due to the constraint on the rationality of the total curvature, the Legendrian lifts of closed elastica of
S^{2}
are, in general, not closed.

We will refer to a unit-speed Legendrian curve whose curvature is a non-constant periodic solution of (
4.1
) as an
s
-curve
; thus, while
s
-loops are closed,
s
-curves are not necessarily closed despite having periodic curvature.

The study of
s
-loops is organized in two steps. The first is the analysis of the
s
-curves (obtained from periodic solutions of (
4.1
)) and the second is the derivation of the closure conditions, via integration of the Frenet equations (
2.2
). We describe this scheme for the general case, suppressing some details, before we apply it to a specific class of stationary curves.

4.1
The general scheme

By integrating (
4.1
) twice, we obtain the two conservation laws

\displaystyle k^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}k^{3}+ak+\frac{1}{2}{b}=0,

(4.2a)

\displaystyle(k^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}k^{4}+ak^{2}+bk+c=0.

(4.2b)

Thus,
k
is determined by inverting an Abelian integral along the
phase curve

y^{2}+P_{a,b,c}(x)=0
, where
P_{a,b,c}
is the fourth-degree polynomial

P_{a,b,c}(x)=\frac{1}{4}x^{4}+ax^{2}+bx+c.

As a consequence of the Poincaré–Bendixon theorem,
periodic solutions of (
4.2b
) exist if and only if
P_{a,b,c}
possesses either

•

four distinct real roots
e_{1}>e_{2}>e_{3}>e_{4}=-(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3})
, which we will call the
dnoidal case
; or

•

two distinct real roots
e_{1}>e_{2}
and two complex conjugate roots
-\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+e_{2})\pm\mathrm{i}e_{3}
where
e_{3}>0
, which we will call the
cnoidal case
; or

•

three distinct real roots, one them with multiplicity two, which is a degenerate limit of the cnoidal case.

In each case, the coefficients
a
,
b
and
c
of
P_{a,b,c}
can be written as functions of real parameters
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
. We will refer to
{\bf e}=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
as the
modulus
of
k
.

Assuming that
\gamma
is an
s
-curve, we can arrange (by translating in
s
, and reversing the orientation of
\gamma
if necessary)
that
k(0)=e_{2}
, from which it follows that
k^{\prime}(0)=0
.
With this choice of initial condition, the modulus
{\bf e}
uniquely determines
k(s)
.
Moreover, any other
s
-curve with the same modulus is of the form
A\gamma_{0}(s+c)
for some matrix
A\in{\rm U}(2)
and constant
c
, and is thus congruent to
\gamma
.
Consequently, the
congruence classes
of
s
-curves of dnoidal type are in one-to-one correspondence with the elements of

{\mathcal{D}}=\bigl\{(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid e_{1}>e_{2}>e_{3}>-(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3})\bigr\},

while the congruence classes of
s
-curves of cnoidal type are in one-to-one correspondence with the elements of

{\mathcal{C}}=\bigl\{(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid e_{1}>e_{2},e_{3}>0\bigr\}.

Sets
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
are the
moduli spaces
of the
s
-curves of dnoidal and cnoidal type, respectively.

The curvature of an
s
-curve with modulus
{\bf e}=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
is given by

\displaystyle k(s)=\frac{q_{11}+q_{12}\operatorname{dn}^{2}(qs,m)}{q_{21}+q_{22}\operatorname{dn}^{2}(qs,m)},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{dnoidal case},

\displaystyle k(s)=\frac{q_{11}+q_{12}\operatorname{cn}(qs,m)}{q_{21}+q_{22}\operatorname{cn}(qs,m)},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{cnoidal case},

(4.3)

where
\operatorname{cn}
and
\operatorname{dn}
are the Jacobi elliptic functions,
m
is the square of the Jacobi modulus
[
18
]
,
and coefficients
q_{ij}
and parameters
q
,
m
are certain functions of
{\bf e}
(see
[
1
, formulas (256.00) and (259.00)]
).
In the degenerate cnoidal case,
m=0
and
\operatorname{cn}(qs,m)
is replaced by
\cos(qs)
; in the sequel, we will not consider this case.

The wavelength (i.e., the least period of the curvature) is

\omega=\begin{cases}2{\rm K}(m)/{q},&\text{dnoidal case,}\\
4{\rm K}(m)/{q},&\text{cnoidal case,}\end{cases}

(4.4)

where
{\rm K}(m)
denotes the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. By integrating the curvature
over its wavelength, we obtain the
quantum of total curvature

\Phi_{1}({\bf e}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega}k\,{\rm d}s={\mathtt{A}}{\rm K}(m)+{\mathtt{B}}\Pi(n_{1},m),

(4.5)

which can be expressed in terms of
K(m)
and the complete elliptic integral of the third kind
\Pi(n_{1},m)
,
where
{\mathtt{A}}
,
{\mathtt{B}}
and
n_{1}
are functions of
{\bf e}
(see in
[
1
, formulas (340.03) and (341.03)]
).
By Proposition
2.8
, rationality of the quantum of curvature is a necessary condition for
an
s
-curve to be closed; however, this is not sufficient. In order to deduce sufficient conditions, we next define the conserved momentum and show how explicit
s
-curves can be obtained through integration by quadratures.

The momentum.

Let
\mathfrak{h}_{0}(2)
be the vector space of traceless
2\times 2
hermitian matrices. For every modulus
{\bf e}
, we consider the map
{\mathtt{H}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathfrak{h}_{0}(2)
defined by

{\mathtt{H}}=\begin{pmatrix}2k+\tfrac{1}{4}b&k^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}k^{2}+a-4\bigr)\\
k^{\prime}-\mathrm{i}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}k^{2}+a-4\bigr)&-2k-\tfrac{1}{4}b\end{pmatrix},

(4.6)

where
k
is as in (
4.3
). From (
4.2
), it follows that
{\mathtt{H}}
has constant eigenvalues
\pm\lambda
, where

\lambda=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{256-128a+16a^{2}+b^{2}-16c}>0.

(
Given the expression for
\lambda^{2}
in terms of the components of
{\bf e}
, it is straightforward to
use calculus to prove that it never vanishes in either the cnoidal or dnoidal cases.
)

Moreover, equation (

Let

Proposition 4.2

For every

{\mathtt{V}}^{1}(s)
and
{\mathtt{V}}^{2}(s)
belong to the
\mp\lambda
-eigenspaces of

is of dnoidal type, or if

vanish at

(

Proof.

The first part of the statement is a straightforward consequence of (
4.9
).
Moreover, if
{\mathtt{V}}^{1}
and
{\mathtt{V}}^{2}
are nonzero then they are
linearly independent as they are eigenvectors for different eigenvalues.

Since
\mathtt{V}^{1}
,
\mathtt{V}^{2}
are periodic, we can assume that
s\in[0,\omega)
.
Note that the derivative
k^{\prime}
vanishes at
s=0
or

and is nonzero elsewhere, so

\mathtt{V}^{1}(s)

\mathtt{V}^{2}(s)

and
s\neq\tfrac{1}{2}\omega

Note also that

and

.
Thus, we have

\mathtt{V}^{1}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathrm{i}}\bigl(e_{2}^{2}+2a-8\bigr)\\
-\frac{1}{4}(8e_{2}+b)-\lambda\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathtt{V}^{2}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{4}(8e_{2}+b)+\lambda\\
\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathrm{i}}\bigl(e_{2}^{2}+2a-8\bigr)\end{pmatrix},

and

\mathtt{V}^{1}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\omega\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{i}\bigl(e_{1}^{2}+2a-8\bigr)\\
-\frac{1}{4}(8e_{1}+b)-\lambda\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathtt{V}^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\omega\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{4}(8e_{1}+b)+\lambda\\
\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathrm{i}}\bigl(e_{1}^{2}+2a-8\bigr)\end{pmatrix}.

Hence, it suffices to prove that

{\mathtt{V}}^{1}
is nowhere zero in the dnoidal or generic cnoidal cases, and that, in the exceptional case,
{\mathtt{V}}^{1}
vanishes only at

.

Consider the cnoidal case. In this case, expressing

,
e_{2}
,
e_{3}

for one half of the imaginary part of the upper entry of

Using this equality and writing

{\mathtt{V}}^{1}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\omega\bigr)\neq 0
.
It is clear that if

Suppose that left-hand side
(

5
Discussion

To summarize, we have shown that, in the context of pseudo-Hermitian geometry on
S^{3}
,
there are flows for Legendrian curves that induce curvature evolution by any integrable PDE in the mKdV hierarchy. Moreover, we constructed a natural symplectic structure
on the space of periodic Legendrian curves relative to which each of these flows is Hamiltonian. For the flow
\mathbf{Z}_{1}
which induces evolution by the mKdV equation itself, we have carried out a detailed analysis of curves that are stationary (i.e., whose flows are congruent to the initial curve), identifying closure conditions and obtaining a complete description of periodic stationary curves in a significant special case. These results naturally suggest further questions and directions for research.

First, it is worth highlighting that the closure conditions for stationary curves could
only be obtained because these curves are integrable by quadratures (see Section
4.2
). In fact,
\mathbf{Z}_{1}
-stationary curves arise as projections to
S^{3}
of trajectories of a completely integrable contact-Hamiltonian system on
{\rm U}(2)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
It is natural to ask if this holds for higher flows in the hierarchy. That is, for
n>1

are
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
-stationary curves also the projections of the trajectories of some completely integrable finite-dimensional contact-Hamiltonian system?

Next, as a completely integrable PDE the mKdV equation has a rich structure, including infinitely many conservation laws (as mentioned above), but also a Bäcklund transformation which generates new solutions from old ones
[
14
,
29
]
. Since this transformation can be realized as a gauge transformation or ‘dressing’ at the level of the Lax pair, and these Lax equations are equivalent to the
AKNS-type system satisfied by our
{\rm U}(2)
-valued Frenet frame, it is natural to ask if
there is a geometric transformation for Legendrian curves that corresponds to the
Bäcklund transformation for curvature. If available, this transformation could be
used to generate new and interesting solutions to our flows, starting with some of the stationary curves we have obtained above.

Last, the pseudo-Hermitian 3-sphere has, as mentioned in the introduction, a non-compact dual
A^{3}
, the 3-dimensional
anti-de Sitter space equipped with its pseudo-Hermitian structure of constant Webster curvature
-1
. In this case, we expect that the defocusing mKdV equation and its associated hierarchy can be realized by flows of Legendrian curves in
A^{3}
. It is also possible that there are integrable flows for null curves in this space, which would likely be related to the KdV hierarchy. If this is the case, it is natural to ask whether the Miura transformation
[
20
]
between KdV and defocusing mKdV equations has some geometric realization that mediates between flows of Legendrian and null curves in
A^{3}
.
