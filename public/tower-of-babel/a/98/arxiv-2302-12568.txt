Abstract
. We study the topological dynamics of the Hénon maps. For a parameter set generalizing the Benedicks-Carleson parameters (the Wang-Young parameter set) we obtain the following: (1) The pruning front conjecture (due to Cvitanović); (2) A kneading theory (realizing a conjecture by Benedicks and Carleson); (3) A classification: two Hénon maps are conjugate on their strange attractors if and only if their sets of kneading sequences coincide, if and only if their folding patterns coincide. The folding pattern is a single sequence of 0 0 s and 1 1 s, which allows to distinguish two nonconjugate Hénon attractors in finitely many steps. The classification result relies on further development of the authors’ recent inverse limit description of Hénon attractors in terms of densely branching trees.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Hénon attractors

In 1976 M. Hénon introduced the two-parameter family of smooth diffeomorphisms on the plane
F_{a,b}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
F_{a,b}(x,y)=(1+y-ax^{2},bx)
, to show that numerical experiments for
a=1.4
,
b=0.3
indicate the existence of a strange attractor,
[
28
]
. Since then, this family, known today as the Hénon family of maps, has been the subject of intense research, both theoretical and numerical. However, its dynamics is still far from being completely understood. The Hénon attractor is the prototypical strange attractor and it is one of the most studied examples of dynamical systems exhibiting chaotic behavior. Understanding the Hénon-type strange attractors is a central problem of nonlinear dynamics because such attractors are believed to be generic for low-dimensional dynamical systems. The first proof of the existence of the Hénon strange attractors was given by M. Benedicks and L. Carleson in 1991,
[
4
]
. They proved that if
b>0
is small enough, then for a positive measure set of values
a
near
a^{*}=2
, the corresponding Hénon map
F_{a,b}
exhibits a strange attractor. In the introduction of this remarkable paper, they also stated four problems that they believed were possible to solve by further development of their ideas. The fourth problem, the only one unsolved until today, is the following one:

Problem

[
4
, p. 74, Problem (d)]

Develop a partial theory of kneading sequences for the Hénon maps.

After the first breakthrough, several generalizations of the Benedicks-Carleson result appeared. In 1993, L. Mora and M. Viana generalized the result to the so-called Hénon-like families of maps,
[
38
]
. In 2001, Q. Wang and L.-S. Young in
[
42
]
gave simple conditions for strongly dissipative maps that guarantee the existence of strange attractors. They developed a dynamical picture for the attractors in this class, including the geometry of fractal critical sets, nonuniform hyperbolic behavior, symbolic coding of orbits, formulas for topological entropy, and many statistical properties associated with chaos. Their results hold for the Hénon family of maps for a positive measure set of parameters
(a,b)
, arbitrarily near
(a^{*},0)
, where
a^{*}\in[1.5,2]
is such that
q_{a^{*}}(x)=1-a^{*}x^{2}
is a Misiurewicz map and

can be both, positive and negative. We call this set of parameters the
Wang-Young parameter set
and denote it by

. Note that the Benedicks-Carleson results in
[
4
]
is a version of the case for
a^{*}=2
and

. There is a vast literature on the Hénon family and strange attractors, as well as their generalizations, see e.g.
[

1.2.
Kneading theory in dimension 1

A very powerful tool in one-dimensional dynamics is the Milnor-Thurston kneading theory,
[
34
]
. Its central object for unimodal maps on an interval is the kneading sequence. This symbol sequence is defined as the itinerary of the critical value, and it is an invariant of the topological conjugacy classes of the unimodal maps with negative Schwartzian derivative and without periodic attractors. The kneading sequence completely characterizes the set of all possible itineraries of such unimodal maps. It also has a key role in various studies, like in proving the continuity and monotonicity of the topological entropy for the quadratic family (and the universality of the family).

1.3.
Pruning front conjecture

In 1988 in
[
24
]
, P. Cvitanović, G.H. Gunaatne, and I. Procaccia proposed a symbolic dynamics model for what they called Hénon-type maps, that is, for maps on the plane that fold the plane back into itself exactly once, and they especially addressed the case of the Hénon and Lozi maps. They stated that every allowed orbit has a unique binary label and that a binary tree that contains all allowed orbits is not complete, since disallowed orbits are ‘pruned’ from it. They called a boundary that separates the allowed and the forbidden orbits a ‘pruning front’. They write:

“A pruning front arises from the fact that the two-dimensional attractor is not a single curve, but is multisheeted. There is no single ‘maximal point’ as in the one-dimensional case; each sheet defines a locally highest allowed itinerary. The pruning front is computed therefore by determining the ‘primary’ sequence of homoclinic tangencies. The homoclinic tangencies are points where the unstable manifold and the stable manifold are tangent. ‘Primary’ tangencies are those tangencies that lie closest to the
y
axis […] We conjecture that the pruning front specifies the allowed symbolic dynamics (the union of all periodic points) fully; there are no orbits that are pruned out by other mechanisms. All the other disallowed regions of the symbol plane are obtained by backward and forward iterations of the primary pruned patch […]”

This conjecture is known as the pruning front conjecture and has received a lot of attention in the last 35 years (see for example
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
33
]
). Note that at the time when the aforementioned paper was published, a rigorous mathematical proof of the existence of the Hénon attractors did not exist. The pruning front conjecture as introduced in
[
24
]
is described intuitively and relies on numerical evidence to validate the models. A version of its precise mathematical statement (for the Hénon maps) is given in 2002 by A. de Carvalho and T. Hall in
[
21
]
, where they suggested a possible approach to a proof of the conjecture. This approach is based on the so-called braid type conjecture, which states that the periodic orbits of Hénon maps are of the same topological type as the periodic orbits of the horseshoe.

1.4.
Statement of results

The aim of this paper is as follows:

•

To prove the pruning front conjecture;

•

To develop the kneading theory for the Hénon maps within the Wang-Young set of parameters
\mathcal{WY}
;

•

To classify (up to topological conjugacy) the Hénon maps on their strange attractors within
\mathcal{WY}
(in terms of the kneading theory).

1.4.1.
Motivation for the approach and the construction of a critical locus.

Our approach to the pruning front conjecture is motivated in part by the piecewise affine case. For the Lozi family, the first proof of the conjecture was given by Y. Ishii in 1997,
[
30
]
. His approach is adapted to the piecewise affine nature of the Lozi family and is quite different from the one in
[
21
]
, and also from the one adopted here. Our approach is motivated by the kneading theory for the Lozi maps developed in 2016 by M. Misiurewicz and the second author of this paper,
[
36
]
. In 2018 the same authors generalized the kneading theory to the Lozi-like maps,
[
37
]
. Although in
[
36
]
the authors work only with the Lozi maps, they avoid explicit use of the formulas defining those maps and take the geometric approach. That allows us to follow the main ideas in
[
36
]
to prove the pruning front conjecture for the Hénon maps and to develop the kneading theory for them in this paper. However, since there are some major differences between the families of the Hénon and Lozi maps, we have to develop some new methods and tools to address these differences.

One of the main distinctions in developing symbolic dynamics for the Hénon and Lozi maps is the following: The idea of using the partition into the left and right half-planes as the base for the symbolic dynamics for the Lozi maps is natural and has been used for a long time. On the other hand, for the Hénon maps, as P. Cvitanović noticed in
[
24
]
, “a partition of the plane by the
y
-axis does not determine the periodic points uniquely. Numerically it appears that a line joining the homoclinic tangencies does in fact partition the plane as needed. […] we conjecture that this line partitions space so that the unstable periodic points are uniquely described by symbolic sequences.”

The first question is the existence of a such line that contains the mentioned homoclinic tangencies, that is, the set of all critical points, that we denote by
{\mathcal{C}}
. Note that in
[
4
]
the authors write:
“One might for example believe (although this is doubtful), that
{\mathcal{C}}
is located on a smooth curve cutting through the [unstable manifold of the fixed point]
W^{u}
[…]. We can however only prove this for a
C^{l/2}
-curve and this is based on the fact that
W^{u}
never intersects itself.” Since we could not find any such proof, for completeness we provide in Section
3
a proof of the existence of an arc
{\mathcal{K}}
which contains the critical set and call it the
critical locus
.

In Section
4
, we use
{\mathcal{K}}
to partition a forward invariant disc
D
, that contains the Hénon attractor, into the left and right regions as the base for the symbolic dynamics for the Hénon maps. Note that in
[
42
, Theorem 1.6]
, it is proved that the critical set partitions the attractor into finitely many disjoint sets (two for the Hénon maps), and that there exists a subshift of a full shift on finitely many symbols (two for the Hénon maps) which provides coding of orbits on the attractor that is given by the partition. In
[
42
, Corollary 1.1]
, it is mentioned that kneading sequences are the itineraries of images of the critical points. However, any relationship between kneading sequences and the itineraries of the other points is not studied. Our method in Section
4
gives an alternative proof of the existence of coding.

1.4.2.
Turning points and the kneading set

The question now is what should be the pruning front and/or what should replace the kneading sequence of unimodal maps? In our approach, the set of images of the basic critical points
F(\mathfrak{C})
has a key role. We denote by
\mathfrak{C}
the set of all critical points that lie on
W^{u}
,
\mathfrak{C}={\mathcal{C}}\cap W^{u}
, and for simplicity, we write
F=F_{a,b}
, assuming
(a,b)\in\mathcal{WY}
. We call the points of
\mathfrak{C}
the
basic critical points
, the points of
F(\mathfrak{C})
the
turning points
, and the itineraries of the turning points we call the
kneading sequences
. By
\mathfrak{K}_{F}
we denote the set of all kneading sequences and we call it the
kneading set
. Note that
\mathfrak{K}_{F}
is a countable infinite set. Every kneading sequence, denoted by
\overline{k}
, has the following form:

\overline{k}=\hskip 5.69046pt+^{\hskip-14.22636pt\infty}\hskip 5.69046ptw\pm\cdot\overrightarrow{k}_{\hskip-2.84544pt0},

where
\hskip 5.69046pt+^{\hskip-14.22636pt\infty}\hskip 5.69046pt=\dots+++
,
w=w_{0}\dots w_{m}
, for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
\overrightarrow{k}_{\hskip-2.84544pt0}=k_{0}k_{1}k_{2}\dots
,
w_{0}=-
,
k_{0}=+
,
w_{i},k_{j}\in\{-,+\}
for
i=1,\dots m
and
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, for
\pm
we can substitute any of
+
and
-
, and the dot shows where the 0th coordinate is. In Section
5
we prove the pruning front conjecture by proving that
\mathfrak{K}_{F}
characterizes all itineraries. The proof is given in two steps, by two theorems.

First, we prove that
\mathfrak{K}_{F}
characterizes all itineraries of points of the unstable manifold
W^{u}
of the fixed point
X
(what we call
W^{u}
-admissibility). Itineraries of all points of
W^{u}
start with
\hskip 5.69046pt+^{\hskip-14.22636pt\infty}\hskip 5.69046pt=\dots+++
. Moreover,
W^{u}
is invariant for
F
, so the set of all
W^{u}
-admissible sequences is invariant for the shift map

-admissible, because it is the itinerary of

), we only need a way of checking

-admissibility of sequences of the form
\hskip 5.69046pt+^{\hskip-14.22636pt\infty}\hskip 5.69046pt\cdot p_{0}p_{1}p_{2}\dots=\hskip 5.69046pt+^{\hskip-14.22636pt\infty}\hskip 5.69046pt\cdot\overrightarrow{p}_{\hskip-2.84544pt0}
, such that
p_{0}=-
. This tool is given by the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

A sequence
\hskip 5.69046pt+^{\hskip-14.22636pt\infty}\hskip 5.69046pt\cdot\overrightarrow{p}_{\hskip-2.84544pt0}
, such that
p_{0}=-
, is
W^{u}
-admissible if and only if for every kneading sequence
\hskip 5.69046pt+^{\hskip-14.22636pt\infty}\hskip 5.69046ptw\pm\cdot\overrightarrow{k}_{\hskip-2.84544pt0}
, such that

, where

Now that we know which sequences are
W^{u}
-admissible, and since the symbolic space is equipped with the product topology, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 1.2
.

A sequence
\overline{p}=\dots p_{-2}p_{-1}\cdot p_{0}p_{1}\dots
is admissible if and only if for every positive integer

there is a
W^{u}
-admissible sequence
\overline{q}=\dots q_{-2}q_{-1}\cdot q_{0}q_{1}\dots
such that
p_{-n}\dots p_{n}=q_{-n}\dots q_{n}
.

Note that the kneading set
\mathfrak{K}_{F}
is indeed the pruning front and that Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
prove that all the other disallowed regions of the symbol plane are obtained by backward and forward iterations of the primary pruned region, which is the statement of the pruning front conjecture in
[
24
]
. At the end of Section
5
we prove that the equality of two kneading sets implies topological conjugacy of the corresponding Hénon maps.

Theorem 1.3
.

Within
\mathcal{WY}
, the Hénon maps
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are topologically conjugate on their strange attractors if their sets of kneading sequences coincide; i.e.,
\mathfrak{K}_{F_{1}}=\mathfrak{K}_{F_{2}}
.

The Hénon maps are maps on the plane that fold the plane back into itself exactly once, and as such, they could be considered as a two-dimensional analogue of the unimodal maps of an interval. To characterize itineraries of all points for a unimodal map we need only one sequence of two symbols - the kneading sequence - and as we have already mentioned, this sequence is an invariant of the topological conjugacy classes of the unimodal maps with negative Schwartzian derivative and without periodic attractors. The question is: Is there such a good sequence of two symbols for the Hénon maps?

1.4.3.
The folding pattern and pruned trees

In Section
6
we introduce a new notion for the Hénon maps, where the whole information about the symbolic system is maximally compressed. This new notion we call the
folding pattern
. It is a bi-infinite sequence of two symbols and, as we prove in this paper, it is an invariant of the topological conjugacy classes of the Hénon maps within the Wang-Young parameter set, and it characterizes the set of itineraries of such Hénon maps; see Theorem
6.1
. The same holds also for the Lozi maps within the Misiurewicz set of parameters (the folding pattern is introduced in
[
36
]
for the Lozi maps). The folding patterns are introduced for the first time in
[
40
]
by the second author, for the classification of the inverse limit spaces of tent maps with finite critical orbit. They also played a key role in the proof of the Ingram conjecture (topological classification of the inverse limit spaces of tent maps) by M. Barge, H. Bruin, and the second author,
[
2
]
.

In addition, in Section
6
we construct pruned trees for the Hénon maps, mentioned in
[
24
]
, and prove that they are equivalent to the sets of kneading sequences and to the folding patterns, that is, given one of them, we can recover the other two, see Theorem
6.2
.

1.4.4.
The classification of Hénon attractors

In Section
7
we classify (up to topological conjugacy) the Hénon maps on their strange attractors within Wang-Young’s set of parameters.

Our proof exploits the notion of mild dissipation introduced by Crovisier and Pujals
[
16
]
in 2017, where they showed that for any
a\in(1,2)
and
b\in(-\frac{1}{4},0)\cup(0,\frac{1}{4})
the Hénon map
F_{a,b}
is mildly dissipative on the surface
{\mathbb{D}}=\{(x,y):|x|<1/2+1/a,|y|<1/2-a/4\}
and has a 1-dimensional structure in the following sense: using 1-dimensional pieces of stable manifolds of
\mu
-almost every point in the surface of dissipation, for any ergodic measure
\mu
not supported on a hyperbolic sink, a reduced 1-dimensional dynamics is obtained as a continuous non-invertible map acting on a tree. Our classification result relies on an extension of that result from our recent work in
[
15
]
, where we showed that for each orientation reversing Hénon map
F
there exists a densely branching tree
{\mathbb{T}}
, such that
F
on its strange attractor is conjugate to the shift homeomorphism on the inverse limit of
{\mathbb{T}}
. In the current paper, we first argue that the same result holds for
b<0
, and for that case, we slightly modify the invariant disc
D
to include the other fixed point of
F
, denoted by
Y
, to the boundary of
D
. Then we isolate two special sets
E_{X}
and
B_{X}
, which are subsets of the set of all endpoints and all branch points of
{\mathbb{T}}
, respectively. We show that the aforementioned conjugacy determines: (1) For
b>0
, a bijection between the elements of
E_{X}\cup B_{X}
and the orbits of basic critical points; (2) For
b<0
, a bijection between the elements of
E_{X}\cup B_{X}
and the orbits of quasi-critical points, that are the points of
{\mathcal{K}}\cap W^{u}_{Y}
. That, together with Theorem
1.3
and the results from Section
6
, leads to the classification result in terms of the kneading sequences and the folding pattern.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
F_{i}:D_{i}\to D_{i}
,
i=1,2
, be two Hénon maps with parameters in
\mathcal{WY}
. If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are topologically conjugate, then their sets of kneading sequences coincide, and their folding patterns coincide. Conversely,
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are topologically conjugate on their strange attractors if their sets of kneading sequences coincide, or equivalently if their folding patterns coincide.

1.4.5.
Generalizations to the piecewise affine case

In Section
8
we discuss how the same approach can be used to classify the Lozi maps for the Misiurewicz set of parameters, by proving that two such Lozi maps are conjugate if and only if their sets of kneading sequences coincide, if and only if their folding patterns coincide.

0

[z_{0}^{n+2},z_{0}^{n+1}]^{u}
, and the information on how they are marked, and how they are mapped by
F
. This completes the induction step.

Now suppose that we know the folding pattern and we want to recover the set of kneading sequences. As we observed, given a folding pattern, we can add to it the information about the signs, plus the information about the action of the map. Thus, we get
0
s and
1
s with subscripts and (some of them) superscripts. The turning points are the symbols
1
with the superscript 1. Now for every
1
which is a turning point, we follow the action of the map (the arrows), reading the signs of the symbols on this path. In such a way, we get the corresponding right tail
\overrightarrow{k}_{0}
of the kneading sequence (the signs do not change at
1
s, so the sign immediately to the left and to the right of a given
1
are the same, and moreover, this sign is the same as the sign of the component
D^{l}
or
D^{r}
, where the corresponding post-critical point lies). Going back to recover the arc code
w
of the kneading sequence is also simple. In the first step, we follow the arrow backward from the symbol
1
with the superscript 1 to the corresponding symbol
0
. Every symbol
0
is between two symbols
1
. To recover
w
we follow the action of the inverse of the map (we follow the arrows of the adjacent
1
s backward), reading the signs of the symbols on this path, until we get to the initial part of the folding pattern
1\,0\cdot 1
.
∎

6.2.
Pruned tree

We can think of the folding pattern as a countable Markov partition for the map
F
on
W^{u}
. Thus, we can consider the corresponding Markov graph (the graph of transitions). The vertices of this graph are the basic arcs and there is an arrow from
I_{u}
to
I_{v}
if and only if
F(I_{u})\supset I_{v}
. For simplicity we can index the basic arcs by integers: Let
I_{0}=I_{\emptyset}
,
I_{n}\subset W^{u+}
,
I_{-n}\subset W^{u-}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
I_{i}\cap I_{j}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
|i-j|\leq 1
. Now, for the vertices, instead of writing the basic arcs
I_{n}
, we write just the corresponding index
n
for them.

From the folding pattern shown in Figure
6
we get the graph shown in Figure
8
(of course this tree goes down and is infinite; we are showing only a part of it). This graph is almost a tree; except for
0
and the arrows beginning at
0
, it is a sub-tree of the full binary tree, so we call it the
pruned tree
of
F
.

Figure 8.
A pruned tree with numbers of basic arcs.

This tree is in a natural way divided into
levels
. The number
0
is at level
0
, the number
-1
is at level
1
, and in general, if the path from
-1
to
i
has
n
arrows then
i
is at level
n+1
. It is easy to see how the levels are arranged. Starting with level
1
, negative numbers are at odd levels, ordered with their moduli increasing from the left to the right. If level
n
ends with
-i
then level
n+2
starts with
-(i+1)
. Positive numbers are at even levels, ordered in a similar way. Therefore, if we have the same tree without the numbers, like in Figure
9
, we know where to put which number. Of course, we are talking about the tree embedded in the plane, so the order of the vertices at each level is given.

Figure 9.
A “naked” pruned tree.

In a similar way as for the folding pattern, we can add some information to the picture. The symbols
0
and
1
can be placed between the vertices of the tree. The ones that are between the last vertex of level
n
and the first vertex of level
n+2
, will be placed to the right of the last vertex of level
n
. The only exception is
0_{0}
, which has to be placed to the left of the unique vertex of level 1, in order to avoid a collision with other symbols.

We know which of the symbols are
0
s. By our construction,
0
s are those basic points that are in the interior of some
F(I_{n})
. This means that they are exactly the ones that are between the siblings (vertices where the arrows from the common vertex end). And once we have
0
s and
1
s marked, we can recover the signs of the vertices because we know that the signs change exactly at
0
s. Then we get the pruned tree marked as in Figure
10
.

Figure 10.
A pruned tree with
0
s,
1
s and signs.

Now that we have our three objects, the kneading sequences, the folding pattern, and the pruned tree, we prove that they carry
the same information.

Theorem 6.2
.

The folding pattern and the pruned tree are equivalent, that is, given one of them, we can recover the other one.

Proof.

From the folding pattern to the pruned tree.
This is described when we were defining the folding tree.

From the pruned tree to the kneading set.
As we observed, given a pruned tree, we can add to it the information about the signs and the positions of the
0
and
1
symbols. The turning points are the symbols
1
placed directly below
0
s, and additionally,
1_{0}
is the only symbol in the zeroth row. Now for every
1
which is a turning point, we go down along the tree, reading the signs immediately to the left of the symbols (see Figure
10
). In such a way, we get the corresponding right tail of the kneading sequence (recall, the signs do not change at
1
s). Going back (up) is even simpler since in two steps we get to a vertex and just go up the tree along the edges.

From the kneading set to the folding pattern.
This part is proved in Theorem
6.1
.
∎

7.
The classification of Hénon attractors

In this section, we classify (up to topological conjugacy) the Hénon maps for the Wang-Young parameters. Our proof relies on our recent work in
[
15
]
, where we showed that for each orientation reversing Hénon-like map
F
there exists a densely branching tree
{\mathbb{T}}
, such that
F
on its strange attractor is conjugate to the shift homeomorphism on the inverse limit of
{\mathbb{T}}
. Here, for completeness, we briefly recapitulate the construction for the orientation reversing case (
b>0
), and extend it to the orientation preserving case (
b<0
). That construction uses the existence of the critical locus
{\mathcal{K}}
, proved in Section
3
.

7.1.
The construction of densely branching trees

In
[
16
]
the notion of mild dissipation was used to show that for
a\in(1,2)
and
b\in(-\frac{1}{4},0)\cup(0,\frac{1}{4})
there exists a metric tree
{\mathbb{T}}
, and a continuous map

such that the Hénon map

is semi-conjugate to

\mathbb{D}=[-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{a},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{a}]\times[-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{4},\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{4}]

To recall that, we start with the set

It satisfies the following properties.

(A’) The elements of

(B’) Every

is the limit of arcs in
\Gamma
and is accumulated on both sides.

(C’) For

\Gamma

One denotes by

in

\Gamma

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Cl}(s_{n+1})\subset s_{n}
for each

. Set

if for any

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Cl}(s_{m})\subset s^{\prime}_{k}
. Now let

be the collection of sequences that are minimal for the relation

as the quotient of

by the relation

With

\psi([(s_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}])=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n},

which assigns to each equivalence class

a continuum

, which is a geometric realization of that class. We let

. There exists a continuous map

, where

is defined by

and

.

To obtain a similar result for

\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma_{1}
. Since
\gamma_{1}\subset W^{s}_{1}
and
\partial\gamma_{1}
are homoclinic points, there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that
\beta_{1}:=F_{1}^{n}(\gamma_{1})\subseteq\gamma_{X_{1}}
. Let
\beta_{2}:=H(\beta_{1})\subseteq\gamma_{X_{2}}
. Then
H(\gamma_{1})=F_{2}^{-n}\circ H\circ F_{1}^{n}(\gamma_{1})=F_{2}^{-n}(\beta_{2})=:\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{2}
, and hence
H
induces a homeomorphism
\widetilde{H}:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}
,
\widetilde{H}(\gamma_{1})=\gamma_{2}
, where
\gamma_{2}
is defined as above.

Since
\Gamma_{i}
is dense in
D_{i}
,
\widetilde{H}
induces a semi-conjugacy
h:{\mathbb{T}}_{1}\to{\mathbb{T}}_{2}
between
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
such that
\psi h\psi^{-1}|_{\Gamma_{1}}=\widetilde{H}
. Recall,
\psi
assigns to each equivalence class
[(s_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}]
in
{\mathbb{T}}
a continuum
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}
, which is a geometric realization of that class,

\psi([(s_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}])=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}.

Let us prove that
h
is an injection, and therefore a conjugacy. Let
x=[(s_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}],x^{\prime}=[(s^{\prime}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}]\in{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
and
x\neq x^{\prime}
. Then there exists
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
s_{k}\cap s^{\prime}_{m}=\emptyset
for all
k,m\geq N
. Thus,
H(s_{k})\cap H(s^{\prime}_{m})=\emptyset
, for all
k,m\geq N
, and
h(x)=[(H(s_{n}))_{n=1}^{\infty}]\neq[(H(s^{\prime}_{n}))_{n=1}^{\infty}]=h(x^{\prime})
. Therefore,
h
is a conjugacy. Moreover, it follows from construction that
h
preserves stems, that is a stem of level
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
of
{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
is mapped to a stem of level
n
of
{\mathbb{T}}_{2}
, and the natural order of stems is preserved.
∎

Note that the conjugacy
h:({\mathbb{T}}_{1},f_{1})\to({\mathbb{T}}_{2},f_{2})
induces a conjugacy

\widetilde{h}:(\displaystyle\lim_{\longleftarrow}({\mathbb{T}}_{1},f_{1}),\sigma_{1})\to(\displaystyle\lim_{\longleftarrow}({\mathbb{T}}_{2},f_{2}),\sigma_{2}),

given by
\widetilde{h}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n},\dots)=(h(t_{1}),\dots,h(t_{n}),\dots)
, such that
\widetilde{h}\circ\Pi_{1}=\Pi_{2}\circ H
.

Theorem 7.10
.

Suppose that
H:D_{1}\to D_{2}
is a conjugacy between two Hénon maps
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Then there exists
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
H(S_{1,i}^{n})=S_{2,i}^{k+n}
, for every
i,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a conjugacy between the Hénon maps
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
such that
H(\gamma_{X_{1}})=\gamma_{X_{2}}
, as in the proof of Proposition
7.9
. Then
h
maps every endpoint (every branch point) of
{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
to the corresponding endpoint (branch point) of
{\mathbb{T}}_{2}
.

Recall that for
b>0
we have
H|_{\Lambda_{1}}=\Pi_{2}^{-1}\circ\widetilde{h}\circ\Pi_{1}
, and for
b<0
we have
H|_{\Lambda_{1}\cup W^{u}_{1,Y}}=\Pi_{2}^{-1}\circ\widetilde{h}\circ\Pi_{1}

,

By Lemma
7.7
there exists

for every

. If

, by Lemma

for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By Proposition
7.9
,

preserves stems and ordering of stems, and hence

maps the endpoint of

of a stem of
{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
to the endpoint of

of the corresponding stem of
{\mathbb{T}}_{2}
, and the branch points of

of a stem of
{\mathbb{T}}_{1}
to the corresponding branch points of

\pi_{2}\circ F_{2}^{-n-j}\circ H(S_{1,i})=h\circ\pi_{1}\circ F_{1}^{-n-j}(S_{1,i})\in E_{X_{2}}\cup B_{X_{2}}

. By Lemma

and since we assumed that

, it follows that

for every

, for every
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In general (without the assumption that

and consequently

, for every

.
∎

Corollary 7.11

Corollary 7.12
.

Suppose that

Without loss of generality, we assume that

,

and

Appendix A
Infinitely many nonconjugate Hénon maps in terms of the Wang-Young parameters.

In this section we apply the classification results in terms of the kneading sets to show that there are infinitely many Wang-Young’s parameters such that the corresponding Hénon maps are not conjugate.

Let
q_{a}:[-1,1]\to[-1,1]
,
q_{a}(x):=1-ax^{2}
for
x\in[-1,1]
and
a\in(0,2]
be the quadratic family. Barge and Holte
[
1
]
showed that if
q_{a}
has an attracting cycle, then there exists a
b_{0}>0
such that for all
b\in[-b_{0},b_{0}]
the Hénon maps
F_{a,b}
are conjugate to the natural extension of
q_{a}
. Consequently, if
q_{a}
and
q_{a^{\prime}}
have attracting periodic cycles and are nonconjugate, then there exists a
b_{0}>0
such that for all
b,b^{\prime}\in[-b_{0},b_{0}]
the Hénon maps
F_{a,b}
and
F_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
are nonconjugate, resulting in a countable collection of nonconjugate Hénon maps. Below we extend this result to the case when
q_{a}
and
q_{a^{\prime}}
are Misiurewicz maps.

Recall that a map
q_{a}
is called a
Misiurewicz map
if:

(1)

There is no
x\in[-1,1]
with
f^{n}(x)=x
and
|(f^{n})^{\prime}(x)|\leq 1
,

(2)

\inf_{n>0}d(f^{n}(0),0)>0
.

Note that for two quadratic Misiurewicz maps
q_{a}
and
q_{a^{\prime}}
with parameters
a,a^{\prime}\in[1.5,2]
,
a\neq a^{\prime}
implies that
q_{a}
and
q_{a^{\prime}}
are not conjugate, and there are uncountable many nonconjugate quadratic Misiurewicz maps.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}\in[1.5,2]
be distinct and such that
q_{a^{\prime}},q_{a^{\prime\prime}}
are Misiurewicz maps. There exist a
\xi>0
, and positive measure sets
\Delta_{1}\subset([a^{\prime}-\xi,a^{\prime}+\xi]\times[-\xi,\xi])\cap\mathcal{WY}
and
\Delta_{2}\subset([a^{\prime\prime}-\xi,a^{\prime\prime}+\xi]\times[-\xi,\xi])\cap\mathcal{WY}
, such that for any
(a_{1},b_{1})\in\Delta_{1}
and
(a_{2},b_{2})\in\Delta_{2}
the Hénon maps
F_{a_{1},b_{1}}
and
F_{a_{2},b_{2}}
are not conjugate.

Proof.

Note that

(1)

F_{a,b}
converges uniformly to
F_{a_{0},0}
as
a\to a_{0}
and
b\to 0
, and
F_{a_{0},0}
is conjugate to the quadratic map
q_{a_{0}}
on its nonwandering set contained in
\{y=0\}
.

(2)

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists
\delta>0
such that, for every
(a,b)\in[1.5,2]\times([-1,1]\smallsetminus\{0\})
and
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, if
\operatorname{diam}(S)<\delta
then
\operatorname{diam}(F_{a,b}^{k}(S))<\varepsilon
, for every
k\leq k_{0}
.

(3)

By
[
42
, Theorem 1.1 (2)]
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
m_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
d_{\mathcal{C}}(z^{k})>\varepsilon
for every
(a,b)\in\mathcal{WY}
,
z\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
k\leq m_{\varepsilon}
. Also,
\varepsilon\to 0
implies
m_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
. (Recall that
d_{\mathcal{C}}(p)
denotes the distance between a point
p
and the critical set
{\mathcal{C}}
defined below Theorem
2.1
.)

Let
a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}\in[1.5,2]
be distinct and such that the quadratic maps
q_{a^{\prime}},q_{a^{\prime\prime}}
are Misiurewicz maps. Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Let
m_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
be as in (3). Let
\delta>0
be as in (2) for
k_{0}=m_{\varepsilon}
. By (1), there exists
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
[a^{\prime}-\frac{1}{n_{0}},a^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n_{0}}]
and
[a^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{n_{0}},a^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{n_{0}}]
are disjoint, and

(a,b)\in\mathcal{WY}\cap([a^{\prime}-\frac{1}{n_{0}},a^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n_{0}}]\cup[a^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{n_{0}},a^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{n_{0}}])\times[-\frac{1}{n_{0}},\frac{1}{n_{0}}]

implies
\operatorname{diam}({\mathcal{C}})<\delta
.

Suppose by contradiction that for each
n>n_{0}
there exist

(a_{1}^{n},b_{1}^{n})\in\mathcal{WY}\cap[a^{\prime}-\frac{1}{n},a^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n}]\times[-\frac{1}{n},\frac{1}{n}]

and

(a_{2}^{n},b_{2}^{n})\in\mathcal{WY}\cap[a^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{n},a^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{n}]\times[-\frac{1}{n},\frac{1}{n}],

such that the Hénon maps
F_{a^{n}_{1},b^{n}_{1}}
and
F_{a^{n}_{2},b^{n}_{2}}
are conjugate. Let
{\mathcal{C}}^{n}_{i}
denote the set of critical points of

for

and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>n_{0}
.

By the choice of
n_{0}
, we have that
\operatorname{diam}({\mathcal{C}}^{n}_{i})<\delta
for
i=1,2
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>n_{0}
, and by (2),
\operatorname{diam}(F_{a^{n}_{i},b^{n}_{i}}^{k}({\mathcal{C}}^{n}_{i}))<\varepsilon
, for every
k\leq m_{\varepsilon}
. In addition by (3),
d_{{\mathcal{C}}^{n}_{i}}(z^{k})>\varepsilon
for every

and

, implying that, for
i\in\{1,2\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>n_{0}
, all points of
F_{a^{n}_{i},b^{n}_{i}}^{k}({\mathcal{C}}^{n}_{i})
lie on the same side of the critical locus, and consequently, every kneading sequence
\overline{k}^{n}_{i}
of
F_{a^{n}_{i},b^{n}_{i}}
has its right tail
\overrightarrow{k}_{\hskip-2.84544pt0,i}^{n}
agreeing with the kneading sequence of

on the first

coordinates. Note that

as

. But this leads to a contradiction since the kneading sequences of

and

are distinct, and
\mathfrak{K}_{F_{a^{n}_{1},b^{n}_{1}}}=\mathfrak{K}_{F_{a^{n}_{2},b^{n}_{2}}}
. Therefore, there exist positive measure sets

\Delta_{1}\subset\mathcal{WY}\cap[a^{\prime}-\frac{1}{n_{0}},a^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n_{0}}]\times[-\frac{1}{n_{0}},\frac{1}{n_{0}}],

such that for any

,
(a_{2},b_{2})\in\Delta_{2}

∎

Appendix B
All branch points of
\mathbb{T}
come from tangencies and have order three.

In this section we prove that every branch point of
{\mathbb{T}}
has order three and represents a tangential intersection of the boundary of
D
and a stable manifold. As a corollary, we obtain that
{\mathbb{T}}
is a unique topological object, the same for all parameters in
\mathcal{WY}
, namely the universal dendrite of order three.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be a dendrite which is not a finite tree. Let us denote by
E_{\mathcal{D}}
the set of all end points of
{\mathcal{D}}
, and by
B_{\mathcal{D}}
the set of all branch points of
{\mathcal{D}}
. In Definition
7.3
we have defined a set of branch points
B_{X}
of our dendrite
{\mathbb{T}}
(assigned to the Hénon map
F
) as the set of all those branch points that lie in stems. We want to prove that
B_{X}=B_{\mathbb{T}}
, and that every branch point has order three.

Proposition B.1
.

Every branch point in
B_{X}
has order three.

It is noteworthy that the above result is an analogy with the result of Williams for hyperbolic attractors
[
44
]
, where the order of (finite) trees in the inverse limit representation is also three.

Proposition B.2
.

The set of branch points
B_{X}
coincides with the set of all branch points of
\mathbb{T}
.

Let us first recall some results from literature that we use in our proof of Propositions
B.1
and
B.2
.

Definition B.3
.

[
7
, bottom of p. 379 and a comment between Propositions 2.3 and 2.4]

A point
P
is called
expanding
if there exists
\lambda>e^{-20}
such that
\|DF^{j}(P)(1,0)\|\geq\lambda^{j}
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition B.4
.

[
7
, below Proposition 2.4]

Let
z
be a point. A curve
\zeta=\zeta(z)=\{(x(y),y):|y|\leq 1/10\}
is called a
long stable leaf
if the following holds:

(1)

z\in\zeta
,

(2)

|x^{\prime}|\leq C\sqrt{|b|}
and
|x^{\prime\prime}|\leq C\sqrt{|b|}
, where
C>1
is a constant that does not depend on
b
,

(3)

d(F^{n}(P),F^{n}(Q))\leq(Cb)^{n}d(P,Q)
, for every
P,Q\in\zeta
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Remark B.5
.

By
[
7
, Propositions 2.4 and 3.3]
, if
z
is an expanding point then its stable manifold
W^{s}_{z}
contains a long stable leaf
\zeta(z)
. Moreover, if
z_{1},z_{2}
are expanding points then
\operatorname{angle}(t(P_{1}),t(P_{2}))\leq C\sqrt{|b|}\,d(P_{1},P_{2})
, for every
P_{1}\in\zeta(z_{1}),P_{2}\in\zeta(z_{2})
, where
t(P_{i})
denotes any norm 1 vector tangent to
\zeta(z_{i})
at
P_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Also, given any
z\in F(\mathfrak{C})
, there exists a sequence of long stable leaves
(\zeta_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
accumulating
W^{s}_{z}
exponentially fast from the left.

Proof of Proposition
B.1
.

Let
b>0
. By Remark
B.5
, every basic critical point
z\in\mathfrak{C}
lies in its stable manifold
W^{s}_{z}
. Moreover, for a long stable leaf
\zeta=\zeta(z^{1})
we have
z\in F^{-1}(\zeta)\subset W^{s}_{z}
. Since every
\zeta\neq\zeta(z_{0}^{1})
intersects
\partial^{u}D
transversely at two points that lie on the opposite sides of
z_{0}^{1}
,
F^{-1}(\zeta)
intersects
\partial^{u}\Omega
transversely at two points that lie on the opposite sides of
z_{0}
. Therefore, for
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
z\in\alpha
, we have
\alpha\subset F^{-1}(\zeta(z^{1}))
and
\alpha
also intersects
\partial^{u}\Omega
‘transversely’ at two points that lie on the opposite sides of
z_{0}
. Moreover,
\alpha\subset W^{s}_{z}
intersects
W^{u}
tangentially at
z
, and
W^{s}_{z^{-j}}
and
W^{u}
intersect tangentially at
z^{-j}
for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

Let us now consider a special kind of basic critical points. Let
z\in\mathfrak{C}
and
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
be such that
z\in\alpha
and for every small
\varepsilon>0
,

(1)

(\alpha\smallsetminus\{z\})\cap B_{\varepsilon}(z)\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset.

We want to prove now that no long stable leaf
\zeta
contains any pre-critical point
z^{-j}
that satisfies (
1
). Let us assume by contradiction that
z^{-j}\in\zeta
for some
\zeta
,
z
and
j
as above. Let us consider
F^{j}(\zeta)
. Let
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
be such that
z\in\alpha
. The intersection of
\alpha
and
F^{j}(\zeta)
is an arc that contains
z
. By
[
42
, Theorem 1.1]
, arbitrarily close to
z
,
W^{u}
intersects
\alpha
in at least two points
Q_{1},Q_{2}
on the opposite sides of
z
,
z\in[Q_{1},Q_{2}]^{s}_{z}\subset\alpha\subset W^{s}_{z}
, and such that
[Q_{1},Q_{2}]^{u}\cap F^{n}({\mathcal{C}})=\emptyset
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(see
[
42
, Theorem 1.1.(1)(ii)-(iii)]
). Therefore, arbitrarily close to
z^{-j}
, there exists an arc
[Q^{\prime}_{1},Q^{\prime}_{2}]^{u}\subset W^{u}
that intersects
\zeta\subset W^{s}_{z^{-j}}
at points
Q^{\prime}_{1},Q^{\prime}_{2}
on the opposite sides of
z^{-j}
and such that
[Q^{\prime}_{1},Q^{\prime}_{2}]^{u}\cap F^{n}({\mathcal{C}})=\emptyset
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This contradicts Definition
B.4
(2), and so,
z^{-n}\notin\zeta
for every long stable leaf
\zeta
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and hence
z^{-n}\notin F^{-1}(\zeta)
for every long stable leaf
\zeta
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Note that, by definition of
B_{X}
, if
z^{-k}\in\alpha\cap\partial^{u}D
for some non-degenerate
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
,
z\in\mathfrak{C}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then
\pi(\alpha)\in B_{X}
. Moreover,
z^{-j}
satisfies (
1
) for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. If
z^{-k}
is the only point of tangential intersection of
\alpha
and
\partial^{u}D
, then order of
\pi(\alpha)
is three. For
\pi(\alpha)
to have order greater than three, it is necessary that
\alpha
intersects
\partial^{u}D
tangentially at more than one point. An immediate consequence of everything above is that if
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
contains
z_{-1}
, then
\pi(\alpha)\in B_{X}
is a branch point of order three (recall that
z_{-1}\in\partial^{u}D
).

Let
t\in B_{X}
and
\alpha_{t}:=\psi(t)
. We want to prove that
t
has order three. Let us assume by contradiction that the order of
t
is greater than three. Then
\alpha_{t}
intersects
\partial^{u}D
tangentially at two different points
z_{i}^{-k}
and
z_{j}^{-d}
. If
k=d
, then
i\neq j
and
z_{i},z_{j}\in F^{k}(\alpha_{t})
, a contradiction since, by the first paragraph of this proof, there exist
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}
,
z_{i}\in\alpha^{\prime}
,
z_{j}\in\alpha^{\prime\prime}
and
\alpha^{\prime}\cap\alpha^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
. If
d<k
then
z_{i},z_{j}^{d-k}\in F^{k}(\alpha_{t})\subset\alpha^{\prime}
and
d-k<0
, a contradiction, since
\alpha^{\prime}
does not contain a basic pre-critical point that satisfies (
1
). Therefore,
\alpha_{t}
intersects tangentially
\partial^{u}D
at only one basic (pre-)critical point and
t
has order three.

Let
b<0
. Note that the results in
[
7
]
mentioned in Remark
B.5
apply to this case as well when we replace basic (pre-)critical points with quasi (pre-)critical points, and the first paragraph of the proof also holds for the quasi (pre-)critical points. The only difference is that
W^{u}_{Y}
does not accumulate on itself, and hence (1) does not hold for the quasi-critical points. Nevertheless, we will prove that also in this case, no long stable leaf
\zeta
contains any quasi pre-critical point
z^{\prime-j}
. We again assume by contradiction that
z^{\prime-j}\in\zeta
for some
\zeta
,
z^{\prime}
and
j
. Let
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
be such that
z^{\prime}\in\alpha
. Since
z^{\prime}\in F^{j}(\zeta)
, intersection of
\alpha
and
F^{j}(\zeta)
is an arc that contains
z^{\prime}
. Let
\Omega_{\alpha}\subset\Omega
be the region bounded by
\alpha
and
\partial^{u}\Omega
. In this case, also by
[
42
, Theorem 1.1.(1)(ii)-(iii)]
, there exist arcs in
W^{u}_{Y}\cap\Omega_{\alpha}
that have boundary points in
\alpha
on the opposite sides of
z^{\prime}
, and among them there exists one arc that is the closest to the quasi-critical point
z^{\prime}
. If we denote the boundary points of that arc by
Q_{1},Q_{2}
, and let
Q^{\prime}_{1}=F^{-j}(Q_{1})
,
Q^{\prime}_{2}=F^{-j}(Q_{2})
, then the rest of the proof for the case
b<0
is analogous to the case
b>0
, with replacing basic (pre-)critical points with quasi (pre-)critical points.
∎

Theorem B.6
.

[
15
, Lemma 4.2 and Theorem 1.2]

The set of branch points
B_{X}
is a dense subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
.

If an arc
A
has the end points
a,b
, we write
A=ab
. A variant of the following result was proved in
[
14
, Proposition 3.2 and Corollary 3.5]
. For completeness, we include a proof.

Proposition B.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be a dendrite,
E_{\mathcal{D}}
be the set of its end points, and
S
be a dense subset of
{\mathcal{D}}
. Suppose that there exists a sequence of arcs
(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

(i)

S\subset\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}
, and

(ii)

\bigcup_{n=0}^{k}A_{n}
is connected for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then

{\mathcal{D}}=E_{\mathcal{D}}\cup\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists an
a\in{\mathcal{D}}\smallsetminus E_{\mathcal{D}}
such that
a\notin\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}
. Let
b\in\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}\smallsetminus E_{\mathcal{D}}
.
Since
a,b\notin E_{\mathcal{D}}
the arc
ab
can be extended from both ends to an arc
a^{\prime}b^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{D}}\smallsetminus E_{\mathcal{D}}

so that
a^{\prime}a\cap ab=\{a\}
and
ab\cap bb^{\prime}=\{b\}
. Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{a}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{b}
be dendrites contained in

{\mathcal{D}}\smallsetminus ab
such that
a^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Int}({\mathcal{D}}_{a})
and
b^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Int}({\mathcal{D}}_{b})
. Note that each point of
ab
separates

{\mathcal{D}}
between
{\mathcal{D}}_{a}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{b}
. Since
S
is dense in
{\mathcal{D}}
, there are points
s_{a}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a}
and
s_{b}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{b}
. By condition (i) there exists an
m
such that
s_{a},s_{b}\in\bigcup_{n=0}^{m}A_{n}
. By condition (ii)
\bigcup_{n=0}^{m}A_{n}
is connected, hence a tree, and so
s_{a}s_{b}\subset\bigcup_{n=0}^{m}A_{n}
. Since
ab\subset s_{a}s_{b}
it follows that
a\in\bigcup_{n=0}^{m}A_{n}
, leading to a contradiction.
∎

Definition B.8
.

(1)

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. We say that
\alpha
has
separation type

k
if
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
.

(2)

Let
K
be a component of
F^{-n}({\mathcal{K}})\cap D
. We say that
K
has the
separation type

k
if
K
separates two different elements of
{\mathcal{A}}^{k}_{i}
for some

, where

is the number of stems of level

(see Subsection
7.2
).

Remark B.9
.

Note that every
\alpha
such that
\pi(\alpha)
is a branch point has two separation types, since every branch point lies in the intersection of two stems. If
\pi(\alpha)
is not a branch point then
\alpha
has a unique separation type. This also holds for any component of
F^{-n}({\mathcal{K}})\cap D
.

Proof of Proposition
B.2
.

Since
B_{X}
is dense in
{\mathbb{T}}
, and each element of
B_{X}
belongs to a stem, it is enough to show that the union of all stems of the level at most
n
is connected and apply Proposition
B.7
.

Recall that
\pi({\mathcal{A}}^{0})=B^{0}
,
\pi({\mathcal{A}}^{1})=B^{1}
. Let
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
be such that
z_{-1}\in\alpha
. Then by the proof of Proposition

\{\alpha\}={\mathcal{A}}^{0}\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{1}
, so

is connected. Let us suppose that
\pi(\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}{\mathcal{A}}^{i})
is connected. We want to prove that
\pi(\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}{\mathcal{A}}^{i})
is connected.

Recall that
{\mathcal{A}}^{n}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m_{n}}{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{j}
, where
m_{n}
is the number of stems of level
n
. Also, each stem

has two end points, one of them is an end point of
{\mathbb{T}}
and the other one is a branch point of
{\mathbb{T}}
. Denote that branch point as

. Note that

. Also,
({\mathcal{A}}^{n}\smallsetminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\alpha_{b^{n}_{j}})\cap\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}{\mathcal{A}}^{i}=\emptyset
, so

.

Note also that
{\mathcal{K}}
has the separation type zero, as well as each component of

. Therefore, if

is a component of

, then the separation type of

is less than

.

To simplify notation, let

, for some

, and

. By Proposition

,
