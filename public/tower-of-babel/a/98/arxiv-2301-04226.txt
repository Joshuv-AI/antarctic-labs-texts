On the limit spectrum of a degenerate operator in the framework of periodic homogenization or singular perturbation problems
Kaïs Ammari, Ali Sili

Abstract
. In this paper we perform the analysis of the spectrum of a degenerate operator A ε A_{\varepsilon} corresponding to the stationary heat equation in a ε \varepsilon -periodic composite medium having two components with high contrast diffusivity. We prove that although A ε A_{\varepsilon} is a bounded self-adjoint operator with compact resolvent, the limits of its eigenvalues when the size ε \varepsilon of the medium tends to zero, make up a part of the spectrum of a unbounded operator A 0 A_{0} , namely the eigenvalues of A 0 A_{0} located on the left of the first eigenvalue of the bi-dimensional Laplacian with homogeneous Dirichlet condition on the boundary of the representative cell. We also show that the homogenized problem does not differ in any way from the one-dimensional problem obtained in the study of the local reduction of dimension induced by the homogenization.

Introduction, setting of the problem and statement of the results

The purpose of the present work is the asymptotic analysis of the eigenelements of a spectral problem in the framework of the homogenization of a periodic composite medium made up of a
\varepsilon
-periodic set of parallel vertical fibers
F_{\varepsilon}
surrounded by a matrix
M_{\varepsilon}
having better properties; more precisely, we consider the following problem

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}\quad\hbox{in}\,\,\,\Omega,\\
\\
\hbox{where}\,\,\,\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}u=\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u\chi_{F_{\varepsilon}}-\Delta u\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}\quad\forall\,u\in D({A}_{\varepsilon}),\\
\\
\hbox{with}\,\,\,\displaystyle D({A}_{\varepsilon})=\left\{u\in V_{h},\,\,\,A_{\varepsilon}u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\,\,\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial F_{\varepsilon}}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial M_{\varepsilon}}\right\},\end{array}\right.

with the following notations:

\Delta
denotes the classical Laplacian operator,
\Omega
denotes a bounded rectangular open set of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of the form
\Omega:=\omega\times(0,L)
,
\omega
being a domain of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
L
is a positive number,
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial M_{\varepsilon}}
(resp.
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial F_{\varepsilon}}
) denotes the outer normal to the lateral boundary of
M_{\varepsilon}
(resp.
F_{\varepsilon}
). The two horizontal variables
x^{\prime}:=(x_{1},x_{2})
or

will play a different role from that of the vertical one
x_{3}
. The gradient and the Laplacian with respect to the horizontal variables will be denoted respectively by
\nabla^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime}
.

The space
V_{h}
(

(1.2)

V_{h}:=\big\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega),\,\,u(x^{\prime},0)=u(x^{\prime},L)=0\,\hbox{ a.e.}\,x^{\prime}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\omega\big\},

hence,
V_{h}
is the subspace of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
which vanish on the lower and the upper faces of
\Omega
which is assumed to be the reference configuration of a composite medium whose two components are a set
F_{\varepsilon}
of vertical cylindrical fibers and its complement, the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}
.

Hence, the projection on the horizontal
x^{\prime}
-plane of the set
F_{\varepsilon}
is made up of a
\varepsilon
-periodic set of disks while the complement of such set represents the projection of
M_{\varepsilon}
. The characteristic functions of
F_{\varepsilon}
(resp.
M_{\varepsilon}
) are denoted by
\chi_{F_{\varepsilon}}
(resp.
\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}
). The fibers are distributed in

with a period of size

and the ratio between the conductivity coefficients of the two components is
\varepsilon^{-2}
. Throughout the paper, for a measurable set

we denote by

its Lebesgue measure and by

its characteristic function. A generic positive constant the value of which may change from a line to another will be denoted by

The geometric configuration of the medium may be described more precisely as follows.

Let
C
be a square of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
D
be a disk strictly contained in
C
. The complement of
\overline{D}
in

will be denoted by

in such a way that
C=M^{\prime}\cup\overline{D}
. We then define

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle C^{i}_{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon C+\varepsilon i)\times(0,L);\,\,\,I_{\varepsilon}=\{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\,\,C_{\varepsilon}^{i}\subset\Omega\},\quad\omega={\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}}(\varepsilon C+\varepsilon i);\\
\\
\Omega={\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}}C^{i}_{\varepsilon}=\omega\times(0,L),\quad F_{\varepsilon}={\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}}F^{i}_{\varepsilon},\quad F^{i}_{\varepsilon}=D_{\varepsilon}^{i}\times(0,L)=(\varepsilon\overline{D}+\varepsilon i)\times(0,L),\\
\\
M_{\varepsilon}={\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}}M^{i}_{\varepsilon},\,\,\,M^{i}_{\varepsilon}=(C^{i}_{\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{D}^{i}_{\varepsilon})\times(0,L)\,\,\mbox{in such a way}\,\quad\Omega=F_{\varepsilon}\bigcup M_{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

Y

In

Y:=C\times(0,L)=(\overline{D}\cup M^{\prime})\times(0,L)

When dealing with the homogenization of a problem posed on the domain
\Omega
with a geometry given by (
1.3
), a reduction of dimension
3d-1d
appears locally in each cell
\displaystyle C^{i}_{\varepsilon}
; a natural question then arises: what is the relationship between the homogenization process and the associated local reduction of dimension? One of the main points of this article is to show that homogenization does not lead to any new phenomena compared to the local reduction of dimension.
More precisely, we will show that homogenization is merely a periodic repetition of the local dimension reduction phenomenon.

The geometry of the reduction of dimension
3d-1d
problem is the following: the composite medium consists of a single fiber
F_{\varepsilon}:=(\varepsilon D)\times(0,L)
surrounded by the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon M^{\prime}\times(0,L)=\bigl(\varepsilon(C\setminus\overline{D})\bigr)\times(0,L)
in such a way the global domain depends now on the small parameter
\varepsilon
; it is defined by
\Omega_{\varepsilon}:=(\varepsilon C)\times(0,L)=F_{\varepsilon}\cup M_{\varepsilon}
and it may be viewed as the configuration of a thin structure with the characteristic parameter
\varepsilon
.

In this setting, the spectral problem (
1.1
) takes the following form

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}\quad\hbox{in}\,\,\,\Omega_{\varepsilon},\\
\\
\hbox{where}\,\,\,\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}v=\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta v\chi_{F_{\varepsilon}}-\Delta v\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}\quad\forall\,v\in D({A}_{\varepsilon}),\\
\\
\hbox{with}\,\,\,\displaystyle D({A}_{\varepsilon})=\left\{v\in V^{\varepsilon}_{s},\,\,A_{\varepsilon}v\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}),\,\,\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial(\varepsilon D)}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial(\varepsilon M^{\prime})}\right\},\end{array}\right.

where the space
V^{\varepsilon}_{s}
(the subscript "s" stands for singular perturbation) is now defined by

(1.5)

V_{s}^{\varepsilon}:=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}),\,\,v(x^{\prime},0)=v(x^{\prime},L)=0\,\hbox{ a.e.}\,x^{\prime}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\varepsilon C\right\}.

In order to deal with a problem posed on the fixed domain
\Omega:=C\times(0,L)
, we introduce the classical scaling
u_{\varepsilon}(y^{\prime},x_{3})=v_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y^{\prime},x_{3}),\,\,y^{\prime}\in C
which implies

(1.6)

\displaystyle\nabla^{\prime}_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(y,x_{3})=\varepsilon\nabla^{\prime}v_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y,x_{3})=\varepsilon\nabla^{\prime}v_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},x_{3}),\,\,\,\forall\,(x^{\prime},x_{3})\in(\varepsilon C)\times(0,L),

(this approach is of course not applicable in the homogenization setting in which we have to deal with
\varepsilon^{-2}
such thin structures). This change of variables transforms the problem (
1.4
) into the following singular perturbation problem (recall that
M^{\prime}:=C\setminus\overline{D}
),

(1.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A_{\varepsilon}u=\lambda_{\varepsilon}u\,\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
\\
\hbox{where}\,\,\,A_{\varepsilon}u=\displaystyle\left(-\Delta^{\prime}u-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{3}^{2}}\right)\chi_{D}+\left(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta^{\prime}u-\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{3}^{2}}\right)\chi_{M^{\prime}},\quad\forall\,u\in D({A}_{\varepsilon}),\\
\\
\hbox{with}\\
\\
\displaystyle D({A}_{\varepsilon})=\left\{\displaystyle u\in V_{s},\,\,A_{\varepsilon}u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\,\,\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial D}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\chi_{\partial M^{\prime}}\right\},\end{array}\right.

V_{s}
being the space
V_{s}^{\varepsilon}
corresponding to
\varepsilon=1
and defined in (
1.5
).

Note that the study of the asymptotic behavior of (
1.4
) is the so-called reduction of dimension problem

\varepsilon

(0,L)

To describe the behavior of the eigenvalues of (

) (

) (homogenization) we use the variational formulation. Note that for a fixed

Proposition 1.1
.

Problem (
1.1
) (or problem (
1.7
)) admits a sequence of eigenvalues
(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k})_{k},\,\,0<\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{1}\leq\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\leq...\leq\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{n}\leq...,
with
\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}=+\infty
while the associate eigenvectors
(u_{\varepsilon}^{k})_{k}
may be chosen as an orthonormal basis of

.

Taking into account this result, the variational formulation of (
1.1
) and of (
1.7
) are respectively the following ones

(1.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\in V_{h},\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon^{2}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\nabla\phi\chi_{F_{\varepsilon}}+\nabla u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\nabla\phi\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}\bigr)dx=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\phi\ dx,\\
\\
\forall\,\phi\in V_{h},\end{array}\right.

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\in V_{s},\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\nabla^{\prime}\phi+\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{3}}\right)\chi_{F}+\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\nabla^{\prime}\phi+\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{3}}\right)\chi_{M}\right)dy\ dx_{3}\\
\\
=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\phi\ dy\ dx_{3},\,\,\,\forall\,\phi\in V_{s},\end{array}\right.

where
F:=D\times(0,L)
and

.

We prove in Theorem 1.3 below that for each

of the sequence of eigenvalues

of (

is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}_{3}}
in

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition; more precisely,

is a solution of the following coupled system

(1.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{k}_{0}(y)\in H^{1}(C),\quad\displaystyle-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}u^{k}_{0}=\lambda_{k}u^{k}_{0}+1\,\,\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,D,\\
\\
u^{k}_{0}=0\quad\hbox{on}\,\,\,\partial D,\\
\\
v_{k}\in H^{1}_{0}(0,L)),\quad\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}v_{k}}{dx_{3}^{2}}=\lambda_{k}\Bigl(1+\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{|C\setminus D|}\displaystyle\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}\ dy\Bigr)v_{k}\quad\hbox{in}\,\,\,(0,L).\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}\ dy
arising in the second equation of the limit system must be calculated by the use of the first equation of the system but the function
u^{k}_{0}
itself depends on the corresponding eigenvalue as shown by the first equation; hence,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\Bigl(1+\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{|C\setminus D|}\displaystyle\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}\ dy\Bigr)
which is an eigenvalue of

\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
is not completely known in terms of
\lambda_{k}
. However, we will prove (see (
2.41
)) that for
0<\lambda_{k}<\mu_{1}
, the second equation describing the vibrations of the string
(0,L)
may be written as

(1.21)

\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}v_{k}}{dx_{3}^{2}}=\delta(\lambda_{k})v_{k}\,\hbox{with}\,\delta(\lambda):=C\lambda+C^{\prime}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c^{2}_{n}\lambda^{2}}{\mu_{n}-\lambda},

where
C,C^{\prime}
denote positive constants and
c_{n}:=\displaystyle\int_{D}f_{n}dy
where
(f_{n})_{n}
denotes the orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(D)
made up of eigenfunctions associated to the increasing sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n}
of eigenvalues of
-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}
with Dirichlet boundary condition. Of course, the spectrum
\sigma_{0}
of the limit operator
A_{0}
contains eigenvalues on the right of
\mu_{1}
; in particular, (
1.21
) shows that any eigenvalue
\mu_{n}
of
-\Delta^{\prime}
such that
c_{n}=\displaystyle\int_{D}f_{n}dy\not=0
is an accumulation point of
\sigma_{0}
. Our result states that the limits
\lambda_{k}
make up a part of the spectrum
\sigma_{0}
of
A_{0}
, namely the values of
\sigma_{0}
located on the left of
\mu_{1}
.

Remark also that in the homogenization setting, the analogous result of the convergence (
1.17
) is the convergence
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}-\frac{\partial v_{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\right|^{2}\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}(x^{\prime})\ dx\to 0
obtained from the corrector result (
1.20
). However, the latter does not mean that the sequence
u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}
converges strongly in
L^{2}(\omega;H_{0}^{1}(0,L))
to
\frac{|C\setminus D|}{|C|}v_{k}=|C\setminus D|v_{k}
(we have assumed
|C|=1
) in which case this convergence would be the exact analogue of (
1.17
). Unfortunately, because of the oscillations induced by the homogenization process, such exact analogue of (
1.17
) is false. This is one of the few differences between the
3d-1d
problem and the homogenization problem.

Finally we point out other possible scalings of the form
\varepsilon^{\gamma}\chi_{F_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon^{\delta}\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}
as addressed in
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
25
]
. For instance in the static case, one can refer to
[
11
]
. The critical case giving rise to a coupled system at the limit is the one corresponding to
\displaystyle\lim\varepsilon^{\delta-2}=l\in]0,+\infty[
which we consider here.

In order to highlight the close analogy between the
3d-1d
limit problem and the homogenized problem, the macroscopic variable
x
will be denoted by
x=(y,x_{3}),\,y\in C
in the study of the
3d-1d
problem for which
\Omega:=C\times(0,L)
while in the homogenization problem
x
will be denoted by
x=(x^{\prime},x_{3}),\,x^{\prime}\in\omega:=\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}(\varepsilon C+\varepsilon i)
since
\Omega:=\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}(\varepsilon C+\varepsilon i)\times(0,L)
so that each
x^{\prime}\in\omega
may be written as
x^{\prime}=\varepsilon y+\varepsilon i,\,\,i\in I_{\varepsilon}
. In the case of a single thin structure
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon C)\times(0,L)
,
\Omega:=C\times(0,L)
is obtained from
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
by the scaling
x^{\prime}=\varepsilon y,\,\,y\in C
, making our notations homogeneous.

Before proceeding to prove the results in the next sections, it should be pointed out the study can be extended to the case of operators in divergence form. In that case, we have to take into account at the limit the contribution of the anisotropy of the heavy part of the material (here the matrix) as shown in
[
24

. On the other hand, one can consider other scalings of the form
\varepsilon^{\gamma}\chi_{F_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon^{\delta}\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}
as addressed in
[

]
,
[
12
]
,
[
25
]
in the static case. For instance in the static case and under convenient assumptions on the source term, one can consider coefficients of order
\varepsilon^{\delta}
in the fiber
{F_{\varepsilon}}
and
1
in
{M_{\varepsilon}}
, then loosely speaking the structure of the limit problem depends on the limit of the ratio
\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\delta-2}
, the critical case giving rise to a coupled system at the limit is the one corresponding to
\displaystyle\lim\varepsilon^{\delta-2}=l\in]0,+\infty[
. Here we address the critical case in the framework of the Laplacian operator for the sake of simplicity and brevity.

.

2.
Proof of the results in the case of a single thin structure: the reduction of dimension
3d-1d

2.1.
Apriori estimate on the sequence of eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Proposition 2.1
.

For each
k=1,2,...
, the sequence
(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k},u_{\varepsilon}^{k})
of eigenpairs of (
1.9
) is bounded in
\mathbb{R}\times L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))
. There exist
(\lambda_{k},u_{k},v_{k})\in(0,\mu_{1})\times L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))\times H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
and a subsequence of
\varepsilon
still denoted by
\varepsilon
such that

(2.1)

u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\rightharpoonup u_{k}\,\,\hbox{weakly in}\,\,L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))\,\,\hbox{and}\,\,u_{k}(y,x_{3})=v_{k}(x_{3})\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,M=(C\setminus D)\times(0,L),

(2.2)

\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\chi_{M}\rightharpoonup\frac{dv_{k}}{dx_{3}}\chi_{M}\quad\hbox{weakly in }\,\,L^{2}(\Omega),

(2.3)

\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}\to\lambda_{k}.

Proof.

We first prove an apriori estimate on the sequence of eigenvalues which will play a key role in the sequel. Let
\lambda^{0}_{k}
be the k-th eigenvalue of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
in
(0,L)
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and let
\mu_{1}
be the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}
in
D
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition.

We claim that

(2.4)

\forall\,\varepsilon,\quad\forall\,k=1,2,...,\quad\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}\leq\mu_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\lambda^{0}_{k}.

Indeed, we use the well known min-max formula giving the k-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}
of (
1.9
),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}=\displaystyle\min_{V^{k}\subset V_{s}}\max_{u\in V^{k}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(\left|\nabla_{y}^{\prime}u\right|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{3}}\right|^{2}\right)\chi_{F}+\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left|\nabla_{y}^{\prime}u\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{3}}\right|^{2}\right)\chi_{M}\right)\ dy\ dx_{3}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|u\right|^{2}\ dy\ dx_{3}},

where the space
V_{s}
is defined by (
1.5
) (with
\varepsilon=1
) and the
\min
runs over all subspaces
V^{k}
of
V_{s}
with finite dimension
k
.

Let
\phi(y)
be an eigenvector associated to
\mu_{1}
extended by zero in
C\setminus D
. Then
\phi(y)\psi(x_{3})
belongs to
V_{s}
for any
\psi\in H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
and
\phi\psi=0
in
M:=(C\setminus D)\times(0,L)
.

Let
V^{k}
be the subspace of
V_{s}
spanned by
\left\{\phi v^{1},\phi v^{2},...,\phi v^{k}\right\}
where
v^{1},v^{2},...,v^{k}
denote the associated eigenvectors to the first k eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{0},\lambda_{2}^{0},...,\lambda_{k}^{0}
of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

For any
u=\alpha_{1}\phi v^{1}+...+\alpha_{k}\phi v^{k}\,\in V^{k}
, we have
u=0
in
M
and since
v^{1},v^{2},...,v^{k},...,
is an orthonormal basis in
H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
we also have

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}dy\ dx_{3}=\int_{D}\phi^{2}\ dy\ \int_{0}^{L}\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}(v^{1})^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}(v^{k})^{2}\bigr)\ dx_{3}\\
\\
=\displaystyle\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\int_{D}\phi^{2}\ dy,\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{\prime}_{y}u|^{2}dy\ dx_{3}=\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\int_{D}|\nabla^{\prime}_{y}\phi|^{2}\ dy\\
\\
=\displaystyle\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\mu_{1}\int_{D}|\phi|^{2}\ dy,\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon^{2}\left|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{3}}\right|^{2}dy\ dx_{3}=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}\left|\frac{dv^{1}}{dx_{3}}\right|^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\left|\frac{dv^{k}}{dx_{3}}\right|^{2}\right)\ dx_{3}\int_{D}|\phi|^{2}\ dy,\\
\ \displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{0}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}^{0}\bigr)\int_{D}|\phi|^{2}\ dy\leq\varepsilon^{2}\lambda_{k}^{0}\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\int_{D}|\phi|^{2}\ dy.\end{array}\right.

Note that the equality occurring in the fifth line of (
2.6
) is a consequence of the equation
-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}\phi=\mu_{1}\phi\quad\hbox{in}\,\,D
. Hence, using (
2.6
) in the min-max formula above, we get estimate (
2.4
).

We obtain that
\lambda_{k}\in(0,\mu_{1})
by passing to the limit (for a subsequence of
\varepsilon
) in (
2.4
).

Turning back to (
1.9
) and taking
u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
(with
\parallel u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\parallel_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
) as a test function, we get

(2.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(\left|\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left|\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\right|^{2}\right)\chi_{F}+\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left|\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\right|^{2}\right)\chi_{M}\right)\ dy\ dx_{3}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}\leq K.

The last estimate implies that
\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and thus
u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))
. Hence, there exist a sequence of
\varepsilon
and
u_{k}\in L^{2}(C;H_{0}^{1}(0,L))
such that the convergence (
2.1
) holds true.

One has
\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\chi_{M}(y)\rightharpoonup\nabla^{\prime}u_{k}\chi_{M}
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. But
\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\chi_{M}
which is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by
C\varepsilon
strongly converges to zero in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Hence,
\nabla^{\prime}u_{k}\chi_{M}=0
which means that
u_{k}=v_{k}(x_{3})
for some
v_{k}\in L^{2}(0,L)
a.e. in
M
.
The sequence
u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\chi_{M}(y)
(note that the characteristic functions
\chi_{F}
and
\chi_{M}
depend only on the horizontal variable
y
) is bounded in
L^{2}(C;H_{0}^{1}(0,L))
since
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\chi_{M}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
so that for a subsequence
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\chi_{M}\rightharpoonup\,\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\chi_{M}=\frac{dv_{k}}{dx_{3}}\chi_{M}\quad\hbox{weakly in}\,\,L^{2}(\Omega)
. Hence
v_{k}\in H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
and the convergence (
2.2
) holds true. The proof of Proposition
2.1
is complete.
∎

2.2.
The limit problem associated to (
1.9
)

Choosing a test function in (
1.9
) in the form
\phi=\bar{u}
with
\bar{u}=\bar{v}(x_{3})\,\hbox{in}\,\,M
and
(\bar{u},\bar{v})\in V_{s}\times H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
, we get from (
1.9
)

(2.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\nabla^{\prime}\bar{u}+\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\frac{\partial\bar{u}}{\partial x_{3}}\right)\chi_{F}+\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\frac{d\bar{v}}{dx_{3}}\chi_{M}\right)dy\ dx_{3}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\bar{u}\ dy\ dx_{3}.

Passing to the limit in this equation, we get with the help of (
2.1
)

(2.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(u_{k},v_{k})\in L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))\times H_{0}^{1}(0,L),\,\,u_{k}=v_{k}\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,M,\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla^{\prime}u_{k}\nabla^{\prime}\bar{u}\chi_{F}+\frac{dv_{k}}{dx_{3}}\frac{d\bar{v}}{dx_{3}}\chi_{M}\right)dy\ dx_{3}=\lambda_{k}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{k}\bar{u}\ dy\ dx_{3},\\
\\
\forall\,(\bar{u},\bar{v})\in V_{s}\times H_{0}^{1}(0,L),\,\,\bar{u}=\bar{v}\,\,\hbox{in}\,M.\end{array}\right.

Finally a density argument allows to extend (
2.9
) to all test functions
\bar{u}\in L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))
such that
\bar{u}=\bar{v}\,\hbox{in}\,M
and
\bar{v}\in H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
.

Choosing successively in (
2.9
)
\bar{u}\in\,L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))
such that

\bar{u}=0\,\hbox{in}\,\,M
and then
\bar{u}\in\,L^{2}(0,L;H^{1}(C))
such that
\bar{u}=\bar{v}\,\in H_{0}^{1}(0,L)\,\hbox{ almost everywhere in}\,\,\Omega
and bearing in mind the geometry of
\Omega:=C\times(0,L)=\bigl((C\setminus D)\cup D\bigr)\times(0,L)
, we get that the limit problem (
2.8
) may be split into two equations leading to the following equivalent system

(2.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{k}(y,x_{3})\in L^{2}((0,L);H^{1}(C)),\quad\displaystyle-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}u_{k}(y,x_{3})=\lambda_{k}u_{k}\,\,\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,D\times(0,L),\\
\\
u_{k}=v_{k}\quad\hbox{on}\,\,\,\partial D\times(0,L),\\
\\
v_{k}\in H^{1}_{0}(0,L)),\quad\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}v_{k}}{dx_{3}^{2}}=\lambda_{k}v_{k}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{|C\setminus D|}\displaystyle\int_{D}u_{k}\ dy\quad\hbox{in}\,\,\,(0,L).\end{array}\right.

Remark 2.2
.

Eigenvectors of (
2.10
) corresponding to eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}<\mu_{1}
are pairs
(u_{k},v_{k})
made up of two inseparable elements. In particular, if
v_{k}=0
then
u_{k}=0
as shown by (
2.10
). Indeed, otherwise
u_{k}
should be an eigenvector of
-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}
associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}<\mu_{1}
which is a contradiction. Conversely if
u_{k}=0
then
v_{k}=0
since almost everywhere in
(0,L)
, we have
v_{k}=u_{k}
on the boundary of
D
. Hence, the eigenvectors
(u_{k},v_{k})
of the limit operator are such that
u_{k}\not=0
and
v_{k}\not=0
.

We now prove that (
1.10
) and (
2.10
) are equivalent if one defines
u_{k}
by (
2.11
) and then we will improve the lower bound of the limit eigenvalues using (
1.10
).

Proposition 2.3
.

If
(\lambda_{k},u_{k},v_{k})
solves the system (
2.10
) with
0<\lambda_{k}<\mu_{1}
, then
v_{k}\not=0
and
u_{k}
writes as

(2.11)

u_{k}(y,x_{3})=(\lambda_{k}u_{0}^{k}(y)+1)v_{k}(x_{3})

where
(\lambda_{k},u_{0}^{k},v_{k})
solves (
1.10
). Furthermore, there exists a positive constant
\mu_{0}
depending both on
\mu_{1}
and on the first eigenvalue of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
in
H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
such that
\lambda_{k}\geq\mu_{0}
for all
k
.

Proof.

Assume that
(u_{k},v_{k})
is a non trivial solution of (
2.10
), i.e,
(u_{k},v_{k})
is an eigenvector of the limit operator. Then according to the Remark
2.2
above,
v_{k}\not=0
and
u_{k}\not=0
.

Dividing by
v_{k}
in the first system of (
2.10
), one can check easily that
w_{k}:=\frac{u_{k}}{v_{k}}-1
is the unique solution of

(2.12)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}w_{k}=\lambda_{k}w_{k}+\lambda_{k}\,\,\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,D\\
\\
w_{k}=0\quad\hbox{on}\,\,\,\partial D.\end{array}\right.

Note that the uniqueness of
w_{k}
is ensured since
\lambda_{k}<\mu_{1}
belongs to the resolvent of
-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}
. On the other hand, the function
\lambda_{k}u_{0}^{k}
where
u_{0}^{k}
is defined in (
1.10
) is also a solution of (
2.12
) so that the equality
w_{k}:=\frac{u_{k}}{v_{k}}-1=\lambda_{k}u_{0}^{k}
holds true and therefore (
2.11
) follows. Using (
2.11
) in (
2.10
) we get (
1.10
).

We now make more precise the lower bound of the sequence of eigenvalues and we prove at the meanwhile that
\displaystyle\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}(y)\ dy>0.

Multiplying the first equation of (
1.10
) by
u^{k}_{0}
and using
\mu_{1}^{-1}
as the constant (it is in fact the best one) in the Poincaré’s inequality, we get

(2.13)

\displaystyle\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}(y)\ dy=\int_{D}|\nabla^{\prime}_{y}u^{k}_{0}(y)|^{2}\ dy-\lambda_{k}\int_{D}|u^{k}_{0}(y)|^{2}\ dy\geq\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{1}}\right)\int_{D}|\nabla^{\prime}_{y}u^{k}_{0}(y)|^{2}\ dy.

On the other hand, the first eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
is characterized by

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\inf_{u\in H_{0}^{1}(D)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\parallel\nabla^{\prime}_{y}u\parallel^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}}{\parallel u\parallel^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}}.

Hence the following estimate holds true

(2.15)

\displaystyle\int_{D}|\nabla^{\prime}_{y}u^{k}_{0}(y)|^{2}\ dy\geq\mu_{1}\int_{D}|u^{k}_{0}(y)|^{2}\ dy.

From (
2.13
), we derive with the help of (
2.15
)

(2.16)

\displaystyle(\mu_{1}-\lambda_{k})\int_{D}|u^{k}_{0}(y)|^{2}\ dy\leq\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}(y)\ dy\leq\sqrt{|D|}\left(\int_{D}|u^{k}_{0}(y)|^{2}\ dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and then from (
2.16
) we deduce

(2.17)

\displaystyle 0<\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}(y)\ dy\leq\frac{|D|}{\mu_{1}-\lambda_{k}}.

By virtue of the last equation in (
1.10
),
\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{k}:=\lambda_{k}\bigl(1+\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{|C\setminus D|}\displaystyle\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}\ dy\bigr)
is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
so that
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\geq\lambda_{0}
where
\lambda_{0}
denotes the first eigenvalue of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
. Using the second inequality of (
2.17
) we get

(2.18)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\left(1+\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|}+\lambda_{k}\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|(\mu_{1}-\lambda_{k})}\right)\geq\hat{\lambda}_{k}\geq\lambda_{0}.

Hence,
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\geq\mu_{0}:=\phi^{-1}(\lambda_{0})
where
\phi
is the continuous increasing function defined on
(0,\mu_{1})
by

\phi(t)=t\left(1+\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|}+t\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|(\mu_{1}-t)}\right).

∎

So far, we have not yet proved that
(u_{k},v_{k})
is indeed an eigenvector of the limit operator; this is the purpose of the next subsection.

2.3.
The strong convergence of the eigenvectors

We prove the following compactness result.

Proposition 2.4
.

For each
k
, there exists a subsequence of
\varepsilon
such that the sequence of solutions
u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
of (
1.9
) converges strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
to the eigenvector
u_{k}
of (
2.10
).

Proof.

One can extend
u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
from
M
to the whole
\Omega
in such a way the extension
{U_{\varepsilon}^{k}}
fulfills
U_{\varepsilon}^{k}\in V_{s},\,\,\,U_{\varepsilon}^{k}=u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,M
and

(2.19)

\displaystyle\parallel\nabla^{\prime}U_{\varepsilon}^{k}\parallel_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq K\parallel\nabla^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\parallel_{L^{2}(M)},\,\,\,\,\left\|\frac{\partial U_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq K\left\|\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\right\|_{L^{2}(M)}.

Note that the extension only affects the horizontal variable
y
so that the Dirichlet boundary condition on the upper and lower faces of
\Omega
(
x_{3}=0
or
x_{3}=L
) is preserved, see for instance
[
6
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
29
]
.

In addition, one can assume that such extension satisfies the following equation

(2.20)

\displaystyle-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}U_{\varepsilon}^{k}-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}^{2}}=0\quad\hbox{in}\,\,F.

Indeed, if (
2.20
) is not true for
U_{\varepsilon}^{k}
, then one can introduce the function
W_{\varepsilon}^{k}
as the unique solution of

(2.21)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}^{k}\in V,\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{F}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla^{\prime}_{y}W_{\varepsilon}^{k}\nabla^{\prime}_{y}\phi+\frac{\partial W_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{3}}\right)dy\ dx_{3}=\int_{F}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla^{\prime}_{y}U_{\varepsilon}^{k}\nabla^{\prime}_{y}\phi+\frac{\partial U_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{3}}\right)dy\ dx_{3},\\
\\
\forall\,\phi\in V,\end{array}\right.

where
V:=\big\{u\in V_{s},u=0\,\,\,\hbox{on}\,\,\,\partial D\times(0,L)\big\}
(recall that
V_{s}:=V^{\varepsilon}_{s}
with
\varepsilon=1
where
V^{\varepsilon}_{s}
is defined by (
1.5
)).
Hence,
V
is the subspace of
V_{s}
of functions vanishing in
M
. By the Lax-Milgram Theorem we get existence and uniqueness for
W_{\varepsilon}^{k}
. Choosing
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(F)
, the last equation leads to

(2.22)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta^{\prime}_{y}W_{\varepsilon}^{k}-\frac{\partial^{2}W_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta^{\prime}_{y}U_{\varepsilon}^{k}-\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}^{2}}\quad\hbox{in}\,\,\,F.

On the other hand, using equation (
2.21
) with
\phi=W_{\varepsilon}^{k}
, we get the following estimate with the help of (
2.19
) and (
2.7

(2.23)

u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
converges to
u_{k}
for the whole sequence
\varepsilon
. Assuming that
\lambda_{k}
is a simple eigenvalue, the proof of the convergence of the eigenvectors for the whole sequence
\varepsilon
is known since the work of
[
26
]
(see also
[
10
]
). We sketch it in the vectorial setting for the convenience of the reader.

Assume that
\begin{pmatrix}u_{k}\\
v_{k}\end{pmatrix}
is an eigenvector associated to the simple eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.
Using the fact that the eigenvalues converge for the whole sequence
\varepsilon
, it is easy to check that the multiplicity of
\lambda_{k}
is equal or greater than that of
\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}
; hence
\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}
is simple and there are only two eigenvectors satisfying
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\rvert^{2}\ dx=1
, namely
u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
and
-u_{\varepsilon}^{k}
. Among these two eigenvectors, we choose the one satisfying the inequality

(2.54)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\chi_{F}u_{k}+u_{\varepsilon}^{k}\chi_{M}v_{k}\right)\ dydx_{3}>0.

Therefore if
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is a subsequence such that
\begin{pmatrix}u_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{k}\chi_{F}\\
u_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{k}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}
strongly converges in
(L^{2}(\Omega))^{2}
to the eigenvector

\begin{pmatrix}\hat{u}\chi_{F}\\
\hat{v}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}
associated to
\lambda_{k}
, we get by passing to the limit in (
2.54
),

(2.55)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\hat{u}\chi_{F}u_{k}+\hat{v}\chi_{M}v_{k})\ dydx_{3}>0.

On the other hand,
\begin{pmatrix}u_{k}\chi_{F}\\
v_{k}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{u}_{k}\chi_{F}\\
\hat{v}_{k}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}
or
\begin{pmatrix}u_{k}\chi_{F}\\
v_{k}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\hat{u}_{k}\chi_{F}\\
\hat{v}_{k}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}
since
\lambda_{k}
is a simple eigenvalue. The last equality is excluded thanks to (
2.55
) so that any subsequence is such that
\begin{pmatrix}u_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{k}\chi_{F}\\
u_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{k}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}
strongly converges in
(L^{2}(\Omega))^{2}
to
\begin{pmatrix}u_{k}\chi_{F}\\
v_{k}\chi_{M}\end{pmatrix}
.

Let us now prove that all the limit eigenvalues are simple eigenvalues.

Assume that for some
k
, (
1.13
) holds true for two orthogonal eigenvectors
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u_{k}\\
v_{k}\end{pmatrix}
and
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bar{u}_{k}\\
\bar{v}_{k}\end{pmatrix}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(D)\times L^{2}(0,L)
. By assumption, we have

(2.56)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\int_{D}u_{k}\bar{u}_{k}dydx_{3}+|C\setminus D|\int_{0}^{L}v_{k}\bar{v}_{k}dx_{3}=0.

We know that
u_{k}
and
\bar{u}_{k}
are given respectively by
u_{k}(y,x_{3})=(\lambda_{k}u_{0}^{k}(y)+1)v_{k}(x_{3})
and

\bar{u}_{k}(y,x_{3})=(\lambda_{k}u_{0}^{k}(y)+1)\bar{v}_{k}(x_{3})
where
u_{0}^{k}(y)
given by the first equation of (
1.10
) depends only on the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.

Turning back to (
2.56
), we infer

(2.57)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\left(\left(\int_{D}\left(\lambda_{k}u_{0}^{k}(y)+1\right)dy\right)^{2}+|C\setminus D|\right)v_{k}(x_{3})\bar{v}_{k}(x_{3})dx_{3}=0.

As remarked above
v_{k}
and
\bar{v}_{k}
are always eigenvectors of the operator
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
with Dirichlet condition so that (
2.57
) and the second equation of (
1.10
) would mean that
v_{k}
and
\bar{v}_{k}
eigenvectors associated to the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\Bigl(1+\frac{|D|}{|C\setminus D|}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{|C\setminus D|}\displaystyle\int_{D}u^{k}_{0}\ dy\Bigr)
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(0,L)
. This is a contradiction since all the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
with Dirichlet condition are simple eigenvalues.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is now complete.

Finally, let us indicate briefly in the following short section how to derive the analogous theorem in the homogenization setting using the same approach as in the reduction of dimension.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

In the spirit of the above section, the natural idea is to choose a test function vanishing outside the set
F_{\varepsilon}
of fibers to get the apriori estimate on the sequence of eigenvalues. To that aim, we consider an eigenvector
\phi(y)
corresponding to the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}
in
H_{0}^{1}(D)
. We extend
\phi
by zero over
C\setminus D
and then by periodicity to the whole
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The k-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}
of (
1.8
) is given by the same min-max formula, namely

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}=\displaystyle\min_{V^{k}\subset V_{h}}\max_{u\in V^{k}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\chi_{F_{\varepsilon}}+|\nabla u|^{2}\chi_{M_{\varepsilon}}\right)\ dx^{\prime}\ dx_{3}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}\ dx^{\prime}\ dx_{3}}.

For each
\varepsilon
, we choose
V_{\varepsilon}^{k}\subset V_{h}
as the subspace spanned by
\left\{\phi(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\varepsilon})v^{1},\phi(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\varepsilon})v^{2},...,\phi(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\varepsilon})v^{k}\right\}
with the same
v^{1},v^{2},...,v^{k}
as those defined in the previous section, i.e.,
k
normalized orthogonal eigenvectors associated to the first k eigenvalues of
\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{3}^{2}}
in
H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
.

Hence, by construction, the functions of
V_{\varepsilon}^{k}
vanish in
M_{\varepsilon}
so that making the change of variable
x^{\prime}:=\varepsilon y+\varepsilon i,\,y\in D
in each cell, we can perform the same calculations as those of (
2.6
) to get for
u\in V_{\varepsilon}^{k}
,

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}dx^{\prime}\ dx_{3}=\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\varepsilon D+\varepsilon i}\phi^{2}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\varepsilon}\right)\ dx^{\prime}\ \int_{0}^{L}\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}(v^{1})^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}(v^{k})^{2}\bigr)\ dx_{3}\\
\ =\displaystyle\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}\phi^{2}(y)\ dy,\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon^{2}|\nabla^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}u|^{2}dx^{\prime}\ dx_{3}=\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\varepsilon D+\varepsilon i}\left|\nabla^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}\phi\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{2}\ dx^{\prime}=\\
\\
\displaystyle\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\varepsilon^{4}\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\nabla^{\prime}_{y}\phi(y)|^{2}dy\displaystyle=\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\varepsilon^{2}\mu_{1}\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}|\phi(y)|^{2}dy,\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon^{2}\left|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{3}}\right|^{2}dx^{\prime}\ dx_{3}=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}\left(\frac{dv^{1}}{dx_{3}}\right)^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{dv^{k}}{dx_{3}}\right)^{2}\right)\ dx_{3}\ \varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}|\phi(y)|^{2}dy\\
\ \qquad\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{4}\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{0}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}^{0}\bigr)\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}|\phi|^{2}\ dy\leq\varepsilon^{4}\lambda_{k}^{0}\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\bigr)\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}|\phi|^{2}dy,\end{array}\right.

in such a way the following estimate holds true

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}\leq\frac{\displaystyle\Bigl(\mu_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\lambda_{k}^{0}\Bigr)\Bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\Bigr)\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}|\phi|^{2}\ dy}{\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace\varepsilon^{2}\Bigl(\alpha_{1}^{2}+...+\alpha_{k}^{2}\Bigr)\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}\int_{D}\phi^{2}(y)\ dy}=\displaystyle\mu_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\lambda_{k}^{0},

which is exactly the same estimate as that obtained in (
2.4
).

Remark 3.1
.

It is interesting to note in the proof of (
3.3
), we have chosen a test function verifying the same properties as those of the
3d-1d
case, namely: null in the matrix and with the regularity
H_{0}^{1}(0,L)
for almost all
x^{\prime}
.

Remark
3.1
is of general relevance since the other proofs in the homogenization setting
are similar in all points to the corresponding ones in the
3d-1d
problem, the main reason being that the vertical variable is not concerned by the homogenization process which occurs only with respect to the horizontal variable
x^{\prime}
in such a way basically, the local
3d-1d
effect is repeated periodically in the horizontal plane.
Hence all the proofs take up exactly the 3d-1d case while sticking to two principles: Dirichlet condition on
x_{3}=0
or
x_{3}=L
both for the
3d-1d
problem and the homogenization problem and when
x_{3}
plays the role of a parameter as it is the case for example in equation (
2.25
), it is x that will play the role of a parameter in the homogenization problem. Indeed for instance, the natural formulation of equation (
2.24
) in the homogenization setting is the following one

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle z_{\varepsilon}^{k}\in V_{h},\quad-\Delta^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}z_{\varepsilon}^{k}-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}z_{\varepsilon}^{k}}{\partial x_{3}^{2}}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}z_{\varepsilon}^{k}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{k}U_{\varepsilon}^{k}\quad\hbox{in}\,\,\,F_{\varepsilon},\\
\\
z_{\varepsilon}^{k}=0\quad\hbox{on}\,\,\,\partial D_{\varepsilon}^{i}\times(0,L),\end{array}\right.

in such a way passing to the two-scale limit in (
3.4
), we get the equivalent of (
2.25
)

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle z_{k}\in L^{2}(\Omega;H_{\#}^{1}(C)),\quad-\Delta^{\prime}_{y}z_{k}=\lambda_{k}z_{k}+\lambda_{k}U_{k}\quad\hbox{in}\,\,\,\Omega\times D,\\
\\
z_{k}=0\quad\hbox{on}\,\,\,\Omega\times\partial D.\end{array}\right.

The same approach may be applied to the other proofs following exactly the same steps and replacing the weak (resp. strong) convergence in

by the two-scale (resp. strong two-scale) convergence.
