An analogue of Mahler’s transference theorem for multiplicative Diophantine approximation
Oleg N. German

Abstract
Khintchine’s and Dyson’s transference theorems can be very easily deduced from Mahler’s transference theorem. In the multiplicative setting an obstacle appears, which does not allow deducing the multiplicative transference theorem immediately from Mahler’s theorem. Some extra considerations are required, for instance, induction by the dimension. In this paper we propose an analogue of Mahler’s theorem which implies the multiplicative transference theorem immediately.



1
Introduction

Consider a matrix

\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}&\cdots&\theta_{1m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{n1}&\cdots&\theta_{nm}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\theta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R},\quad m+n\geqslant 3,

and a system of linear equations

\Theta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}

with variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. One of the main questions in the theory od Diophantine approximation is how small the vector
\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
can be as
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
range independently through
\mathbb{Z}^{m}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
respectively. There are several classical ways of measuring the “size” of a vector. One can choose a norm, for instance, the sup-norm, one can alter it turning it into a so called weighted norm, or one can consider the product of the absolute values of a vector’s coordinates. In each of those settings there exist
transference theorems
— statements reflecting the relation between the approximation properties of
\Theta
and those of
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
. They are usually formulated in terms of
Diophantine exponents
, which are probably the simplest quantities responsible for the approximation properties.

Given a positive integer
k
and
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we denote

|\mathbf{z}|=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}|z_{i}|,\qquad\Pi(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}|z_{i}|^{1/k},\qquad\Pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\max\big(1,|z_{i}|\big)^{1/k}.

Definition 1
.

Supremum of real numbers
\gamma
for which there exists
t
however large such that the system of inequalities

|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant t,\qquad|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma}

(1)

admits a solution
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with nonzero
\mathbf{x}
is called the
Diophantine exponent
of
\Theta
and is denoted by
\omega(\Theta)
.

Definition 2
.

Supremum of real numbers
\gamma
for which there exists
t
however large such that the system of inequalities

\Pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\leqslant t,\qquad\Pi(\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\leqslant t^{-\gamma}

(2)

admits a solution
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with nonzero
\mathbf{x}
is called the
multiplicative Diophantine exponent
of
\Theta
and is denoted by
\omega_{\times}(\Theta)
.

For each
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
we have

\Pi(\mathbf{z})\leqslant|\mathbf{z}|,

and for each
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
we have

|\mathbf{z}|^{1/k}\leqslant\Pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z})\leqslant|\mathbf{z}|.

Hence

m/n\leqslant\omega(\Theta)\leqslant\omega_{\times}(\Theta)\leqslant\begin{cases}m\omega(\Theta)\quad\text{ for }n=1\\
+\infty\qquad\ \text{ for }n\geqslant 2\end{cases},

(3)

where the first inequality is a consequence of Minkowski’s convex body theorem.

The inequalities (
3
) can be called trivial. The transference theorems mentioned above provide the following nontrivial relations:

\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{n\omega(\Theta)+n-1}{(m-1)\omega(\Theta)+m}

(4)

and

\omega_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{n\omega_{\times}(\Theta)+n-1}{(m-1)\omega_{\times}(\Theta)+m}\,.

(5)

The inequality (
4
) belongs to Dyson
[
1
]
, the inequality (
5
) was proved by the author in
[
2
]
. One can notice that the inequalities look identical, however, there is an essential difference between their proofs. Dyson’s inequality follows almost immediately from Mahler’s transference theorem (see
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
, and also
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
), whereas the inequality for the multiplicative exponents, along with Mahler’s theorem, requires induction by
n
. Roughly speaking, the reason is that the functionals
\Pi(\cdot)
and
\Pi^{\prime}(\cdot)
are not the same.

The purpose of this paper is to find an analogue of Mahler’s transference theorem so that it would imply (
5
) as immediately as the classical Mahler theorem implies (
4
).

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we formulate Mahler’s theorem and show how to derive Dyson’s inequality from it. In Section
3
we formulate and prove the main result of this paper. In Section
4
we derive (
5
) from our result.

2
Mahler’s theorem and Dyson’s inequality

Set

d=m+n.

In his original paper
[
7
]
, Mahler formulated his famous theorem in terms of bilinear forms with integer coefficients (see also
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
)). In
[
6
]
Mahler’s theorem is interpreted in terms of pseudocompound parallelepipeds and dual lattices. We deem this interpretation more apt for applications. A pseudocompound parallelepiped is a concept proposed in Schmidt’s book
[
4
]
, it is a simplification of what Mahler calls in his papers
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
the
(d-1)
-th compound body of a parallelepiped.

Definition 3
.

Let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}
be positive real numbers. Consider the parallelepiped

\mathcal{P}=\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,|z_{i}|\leqslant\eta_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,d\Big\}.

(6)

The parallelepiped

\mathcal{P}^{\ast}=\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,|z_{i}|\leqslant\frac{1}{\eta_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j},\ i=1,\ldots,d\Big\}

is called the
pseudocompound
of
\mathcal{P}
.

We remind that, given a full-rank lattice
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its dual lattice
\Lambda^{\ast}
is defined as

\Lambda^{\ast}=\big\{\,\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\big|\ \langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for each }\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\Lambda\,\big\},

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,\rangle
denotes the inner product.

The following version of Mahler’s transference theorem is proposed in
[
6
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full-rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with determinant equal to
1
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a parallelepiped centered at the origin with faces parallel to the coordinate planes. Then

\mathcal{P}^{\ast}\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c\mathcal{P}\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}

with
c=\big(\sqrt{d}\big)^{1/(d-1)}
.

Theorem
1
is actually a strengthening of the original Mahler’s theorem. Mahler formulated his theorem with
d-1
instead of
c
. We note however that, from the point of view of Diophantine exponents, any constant (depending on
d
only) will do.

Let us show how to derive (
4
) from Theorem
1
. Recall that
d=m+n
.

Consider the lattices

\Lambda=\Lambda(\Theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&\\
-\Theta&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{d},\qquad\Lambda^{\ast}=\Lambda^{\ast}(\Theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&{\Theta}^{\intercal}\\
&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

(7)

Clearly,
\Lambda^{\ast}
is the dual lattice of
\Lambda
. Furthermore, for each set of positive
t
,
\gamma
,
s
,
\delta
, let us define the parallelepipeds

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}|z_{j}|\leqslant t,\qquad\ \ j=1,\ldots,m\\
|z_{m+i}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma},\ \ i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}\Bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}|z_{j}|\leqslant s^{-\delta},\quad\ \ j=1,\ldots,m\\
|z_{m+i}|\leqslant s,\quad\ \ i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}\Bigg\}.

Then

\displaystyle\omega(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\gamma\geqslant\frac{m}{n}\,\bigg|\ \forall\,t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,\ \exists\,t>t_{0}:\ \mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\},

(12)

\displaystyle\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\delta\geqslant\frac{n}{m}\ \bigg|\ \forall\,s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,\ \exists\,s>s_{0}:\ \mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\}.

If
t
,
\gamma
,
s
,
\delta
are related by

t=s^{((n-1)\delta+n)/(d-1)},\qquad\gamma=\frac{m\delta+m-1}{(n-1)\delta+n},

(13)

then
\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)
is the pseudocompound of
\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)
, that is
\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)=\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)^{\ast}
. By Theorem
1
we get

\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Hence, in view of (
12
),

\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\delta\implies\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\gamma=\frac{m\delta+m-1}{(n-1)\delta+n}\,.

Thus,

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\frac{\omega(m{\Theta}^{\intercal})+m-1}{(n-1)\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})+n}\,.

Swapping the triple
(\Theta,m,n)
for
({\Theta}^{\intercal},n,m)
, we get (
4
).

3
An analogue of Mahler’s theorem

For each tuple
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, we define the parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
as

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})=\Bigg\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}|z_{j}|\leqslant\lambda_{j},\quad\,\ j=1,\ldots,m\\
|z_{m+i}|\leqslant\mu_{i},\ \ i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}\Bigg\}.

(14)

Let us also set

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{\ast}=\lambda_{j}^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m,\vphantom{\bigg|}

(15)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\ast}=\mu_{i}^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k},\qquad\,i=1,\ldots,n.

Then, clearly,
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\ast}=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})
. Finally, we define the tuple
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\hat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{m})
as follows. Let us sort the elements of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
in ascending order:
\lambda_{j_{1}}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{j_{m}}
. If
\lambda_{j_{1}}\geqslant 1
, we set
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. If
\lambda_{j_{1}}<1
, we set
p
to be the greatest index such that
\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda_{j_{p}}<1
and define
\hat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{m}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{j_{i}}=1,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ i=1,\ldots,p,

(16)

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{j_{i}}=\lambda_{j_{i}}\big(\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda_{j_{k}}\big)^{1/(m-p)},\qquad\ \ i=p+1,\ldots,m.\vphantom{1^{\big|}}

The following theorem is the main result of the paper.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\ast}
be defined by (
7
). Consider arbitrary tuples
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Suppose

\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geqslant 1.

(17)

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast}
be defined by (
15
), and let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
be defined by (
16
). Then

\min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\hat{\lambda}_{j}\geqslant 1,\qquad\Pi^{\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\Pi(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),

(18)

and

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c_{1}\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}

(19)

with
c_{1}=\big(\sqrt{n+1}\big)^{1/n}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

\lambda_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{m}.

If
\lambda_{1}\geqslant 1
, then
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, (
18
) is obvious, and (
19
) is provided by Theorem
1
, since
c\leqslant c_{1}
. Let us assume that
\lambda_{1}<1
. Then
p
is correctly defined and
p<m
, since (
17
) holds. Hence (
18
) follows immediately.

Let us consider the truncated tuples

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\downarrow}=(\lambda_{p+1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}_{\downarrow}=(\lambda^{\ast}_{p+1},\ldots,\lambda^{\ast}_{m}),\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow}=(\hat{\lambda}_{p+1},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{m}).

Then

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\ast}=\Bigg\{\,(z_{p+1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-p}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}|z_{j}|\leqslant\varkappa\lambda_{j}^{\ast},\quad\,j=p+1,\ldots,m\\
|z_{m+i}|\leqslant\mu_{i}^{\ast},\quad i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}\Bigg\},

where
\varkappa=\big(\lambda_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda_{p}\big)^{-1/(m-p)}
. Since
\varkappa>1
, we have

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\downarrow}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\subset\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\ast}.

(20)

Let us also consider the matrix

\Theta_{\downarrow}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1\,p+1}&\cdots&\theta_{1m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{n\,p+1}&\cdots&\theta_{nm}\end{pmatrix}

obtained from
\Theta
by deleting the first
p
columns, and the lattices

\Lambda_{\downarrow}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m-p}&\\
-\Theta_{\downarrow}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{d-p},\qquad\Lambda^{\ast}_{\downarrow}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m-p}&{\Theta}^{\intercal}_{\downarrow}\\
&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{d-p}.

We make the following two crucial observations: first, the set

\Big\{(0,\ldots,0,z_{p+1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Big|\ (z_{p+1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\Lambda_{\downarrow}\Big\}

is a sublattice of
\Lambda
; second, the set

\Big\{(0,\ldots,0,z_{p+1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Big|\ (z_{p+1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\Lambda^{\ast}_{\downarrow}\Big\}

is the orthogonal projection of
\Lambda^{\ast}
onto the
(z_{p+1},\ldots,z_{d})
–coordinate plane. Hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}

\displaystyle\implies\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\cap\Lambda^{\ast}_{\downarrow}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda_{\downarrow}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}

\displaystyle\implies\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}.\vphantom{1^{\big|}}

Finally, by Theorem
1
we have

\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\ast}\cap\Lambda^{\ast}_{\downarrow}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c_{2}\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda_{\downarrow}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}

(22)

with
c_{2}=\big(\sqrt{d-p}\big)^{1/(d-p-1)}
. Gathering up together (
20
), (
21
), (
22
), and taking into account that
c_{2}\leqslant c_{1}
, we get the following chain of implications:

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\cap\Lambda^{\ast}_{\downarrow}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies\\
\implies\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\ast}\cap\Lambda^{\ast}_{\downarrow}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c_{2}\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\downarrow},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda_{\downarrow}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies\vphantom{\bigg|}\\
\implies c_{2}\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c_{1}\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\},

which proves (
19
).
∎

4
Proof of the multiplicative transference inequality

Let us show how to derive (
5
) from Theorem
2
.
For every positive
t
,
\gamma
,
s
,
\delta
, let us define the following two families of parallelepipeds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\,\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})\ \Big|\ \Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=t,\ \Pi(\boldsymbol{\mu})=t^{-\gamma},\ \min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\lambda_{j}\geqslant 1\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\,\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})\ \Big|\ \Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=s^{-\delta},\ \Pi(\boldsymbol{\mu})=s,\ \min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mu_{i}\geqslant 1\Big\}.

Each parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
satisfying the conditions

\Pi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\leqslant t,\qquad\Pi(\boldsymbol{\mu})\leqslant t^{-\gamma}

(23)

is contained in a parallelepiped from
\mathcal{F}(t,\gamma)
. Conversely, each parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
from
\mathcal{F}(t,\gamma)
satisfies (
23
). Similarly, each parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
satisfying the conditions

\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\leqslant s^{-\delta},\qquad\Pi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\leqslant s

(24)

is contained in a parallelepiped from
\mathcal{G}(s,\delta)
. And conversely, each parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
from
\mathcal{G}(s,\delta)
satisfies (
24
). Thus, for multiplicative exponents, the following analogue of (
12
) holds:

\displaystyle\omega_{\times}(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\gamma\geqslant\frac{m}{n}\,\bigg|\ \forall\,t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,\ \exists\,t>t_{0}:\,\ \exists\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{F}(t,\gamma):\ \mathcal{P}\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\},

(25)

\displaystyle\omega_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\delta\geqslant\frac{n}{m}\ \bigg|\ \forall\,s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,\ \exists\,s>s_{0}:\,\ \exists\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{G}(s,\delta):\ \mathcal{P}\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\}.

Let us assume again that
t
,
\gamma
,
s
,
\delta
are related by (
13
). Consider an arbitrary parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
such that
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\in\mathcal{G}(s,\delta)
. Then

\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=t,\qquad\Pi(\boldsymbol{\mu})=t^{-\gamma}.

We cannot guarantee that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
has no components strictly less that
1
, so generally it is not true that
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathcal{F}(t,\gamma)
. Nevertheless, if
t\geqslant 1
, then by Theorem
2
we do have
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathcal{F}(t,\gamma)
, and moreover,

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c_{1}\mathcal{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Hence, in view of (
25
),

\omega_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\delta\implies\omega_{\times}(\Theta)\geqslant\gamma=\frac{m\delta+m-1}{(n-1)\delta+n}\,.

Thus,

\omega_{\times}(\Theta)\geqslant\frac{\omega_{\times}(m{\Theta}^{\intercal})+m-1}{(n-1)\omega_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})+n}\,.

Swapping the triple
(\Theta,m,n)
for
({\Theta}^{\intercal},n,m)
, we get (
5
).
