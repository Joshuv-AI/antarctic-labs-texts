Noise-mitigated randomized measurements and self-calibrating shadow estimation
E. Onorati, J. Kitzinger, J. Helsen, M. Ioannou, A. H. Werner, I. Roth, J. Eisert

Abstract
Randomized measurements are increasingly appreciated as powerful tools to estimate properties of quantum systems, e.g., in the characterization of hybrid classical-quantum computation. On many platforms they constitute natively accessible measurements, serving as the building block of prominent schemes like shadow estimation. In the real world, however, the implementation of the random gates at the core of these schemes is susceptible to various sources of noise and imperfections, strongly limiting the applicability of protocols. To attenuate the impact of this shortcoming, in this work we introduce an error-mitigated method of randomized measurements, giving rise to a robust shadow estimation procedure. On the practical side, we show that error mitigation and shadow estimation can be carried out using the same session of quantum experiments, hence ensuring that we can address and mitigate the noise affecting the randomization measurements. Mathematically, we develop a picture derived from Fourier-transforms to connect randomized benchmarking and shadow estimation. We prove rigorous performance guarantees and show the functioning using comprehensive numerics. More conceptually, we demonstrate that, if properly used, easily accessible data from randomized benchmarking schemes already provide such valuable diagnostic information to inform about the noise dynamics and to assist in quantum learning procedures.



Randomized measurements are a ubiquitous tool in quantum technologies. They combine the application of random quantum gates with a measurement, often in the computational basis that is native to a given technological platform. From the practitioner’s perspective, thus, they are the naturally accessible resource on digital and analog quantum computing platforms.
From a theoretical perspective, they effectively implement measurement frames with desirable encoding properties of the quantum information of the device’s pre-measurement state. For this reason, randomized measurements have become the foundation of many important quantum characterization protocols, for benchmarking, certification, estimation and tomography

These schemes have been shown

A key insight in quantum systems identification is that for schemes to be experimentally feasible,
robustness
against experimental imperfections is crucial.
This not only
means that they must be able to tolerate
state-preparation and measurement
(SPAM) errors, but also to address implementation inaccuracy of the quantum gates assisting the measurement scheme.
In the context of shadow estimation, the effect of noise affecting the randomized measurements can – under certain assumptions – be canceled out in the classical post-processing
Chen et al. 2021
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
;
Brieger et al. 2021
.
These approaches can be seen as generalization of read-out error-mitigation using bit-flip randomization
Karalekas et al. 2020
;
van den Berg et al. 2022
.
Importantly, to inform the classical post-processing in the former approaches, the effect of the noise on the estimation needs to be characterized in a separate
calibration experiment
that itself can be affected by SPAM errors.
The recent Ref.
Zhao and Miyake 2023
overcomes the need of calibration when there are observables that encode symmetries of the system and thus have a-priori known values.
A different line of work focuses on the distinct goal of mitigating the noise in the state-preparation using non-linear shadow estimation
Seif et al. 2022
;
Huggins et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2022
or existing error-mitigation techniques
Jnane et al. 2024
.

In this work, we aim at making randomized measurements substantially more robust:
We address the problem of mitigating the noise of the quantum gates randomizing the measurements by directly making use of the arguably most common and successful technique for gate-noise characterization:
randomized benchmarking
(RB)
Knill et al. 2008
;
Emerson et al. 2005
;
Helsen et al. 2022
.
We start from the observation that
non-commutative generalized Fourier transforms
Gowers and Hatami 2017

naturally describe both, noisy implementation of randomized bases measurement and the general gate-dependent noise theory of RB
Merkel et al. 2021
;
Helsen et al. 2022
.
This perspective indicates how to use the approximate characterization of Fourier transform from RB experiments in the context of classical shadows.

Following this perspective, we devise multiple new protocols for robust shadow estimation where noise-effects are mitigated using information obtained from RB routines. Building our approach upon RB routines with sequences of random gates of different length, has multiple attractive features:
i) Making use of common RB, the data required for the noise-mitigation might already be acquired during tune-up and calibration of the gates.
ii) Going beyond this, we develop self-calibrating protocols where one makes use of the same experimental data for both the calibration and the estimation.
The self-calibrating protocols can in practice dramatically reduce the required experimental resources.
Our protocols inherit iii) the SPAM-robustness of RB when calibrating against the noise affecting the gates, and iv) the efficiency and scalability of RB.

More specifically,
we present four variants of our approach: one based on the multi-qubit Clifford group, one based on parallel application of single-qubit Clifford unitaries, and two protocols based on the CNOT-dihedral subgroup.
We show that under common assumptions, the noise parameters entering randomized measurements can be precisely related to exponential decay parameters of RB protocols.
Thus, we theoretically guarantee that using RB decay parameters, noise and errors affecting the estimation protocol can be perfectly mitigated in expectation.
We further find a favourable scaling of the efficiency of noise-mitigated estimation schemes using multi-qubit rotations that is comparable to their noise-less counterparts, if the noise strength itself does not scale with the system sizes.
We derive rigorous guarantees under the assumption of a simple gate-independent noise model.
However, the perspective from the general theory of RB already
makes the required approximate assumptions to guarantee functioning in the presence of gate-dependent noise apparent.
In numerical simulation, we validate the resulting expectation that also in the presence of ‘typical’ gate-dependent noise, our protocols significantly mitigate noise-induced estimation biases.

Using longer random sequences for “washing out” gate-dependent noise effects can further improve the stability of classical shadows, excluding pathological settings identified by Ref.
Brieger et al. 2023
.
From the perspective of error-mitigation techniques
Cai et al. 2022
, our scheme can also be seen as following the paradigm of zero-noise extrapolation
Temme et al. 2017
;
Li and Benjamin 2017
, here in form of the noise amplification at the heart of RB techniques.

Mindset of the protocol.

Randomized basis measurements provide a faithful interface between a quantum state and classical data.
The data can be post-processed or used for probabilistically re-preparing the state on a quantum device. To this end, one implements measurements in a random basis by rotating the state with a unitary, say, randomly drawn from a group
\mathcal{G}
, before read-out in the computational basis of the device.
In the absence of noise, the effective measurement channel, or
frame operator
of the implemented
informationally complete positive operator-valued measure
(IC-POVM), reads

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ideal}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\Ad^{\dagger}(g)B\Ad(g)\,,

(1)

where
\Ad(g)=U(g)\otimes U(g)^{\ast}
denotes the action of
g\in\mathcal{G}
as a unitary channel,
B=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{\times n}}\left.\left|{b,b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b,b}\right.\right|
is the dephasing channel in the computational basis, and the average is taken over the Haar measure.
This effective measurement channel needs to be inverted in order to reconstruct the information about the quantum state in the classical post-processing.
On an actual quantum device, the implementation of the rotation before the basis measurement will, however, inevitably suffer from noise. This is particularly daunting if implementing unitaries in
\mathcal{G}
requires long circuits with entangling gates.
A fairly general noise model can be written in terms of an implementation map
\phi
that associates to an intended operation
g\in\mathcal{G}
the corresponding quantum channel
\phi(g)
that actually happens on the device.
If instead of the ideal frame operator, we now invert its analog containing the noise

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\Ad^{\dagger}(g)B\phi(g)\,,

(2)

we are effectively canceling out the noise and arrive again at an unbiased estimator.
Now from a mathematical perspective, the expression
Eq.

2
for the ‘noisy’ frame operator can be understood as the non-commutative Fourier transform (FT) of
\phi
acting on
B
, that is,

\mathcal{M}=\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\Ad](B).

(3)

This can be thought of as a formal way of referring to ‘noisy channel twirls’.
The mindset behind our work is that exactly this Fourier transform for important gate sets is routinely characterized by nearly every quantum computing experiment on the planet, namely, when running RB experiments
Merkel et al. 2021
;
Helsen et al. 2022
.
More precisely, RB characterizes the dominant eigenvalues of these Fourier transforms as the decay parameters of signals that fade for longer and longer random sequences of gates drawn from
\mathcal{G}
.
Under the same assumptions as those required for output data of RB experiments to be related to average gate fidelities, these decay parameters already provide a full description of the entire operator
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\Ad]
.
Thus, RB experiments should in many settings naturally provide the required information for mitigating noise in randomized basis measurements.

Randomized benchmarking routine as starting point.

Randomized benchmarking protocols are a family of techniques built on sequences of random gates to extract data (usually, average gate fidelities of the implemented gate set). That is, given a set of gates
\{g\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
that have physical implementations
\{\phi(g)\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
that we want to characterize, an RB protocol samples gate sequences
\vec{g}(m)=(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{m})
.
from the gate set at random, computes a global inverse
g_{\mathrm{inv}}=(g_{m}\cdots g_{1})^{-1}
, and then estimates the probability

\mathcal{Q}(\rho_{0},E,\vec{g})\coloneqq\Tr[E\,\phi(g_{\mathrm{inv}})\phi(g_{m})\cdots\phi(g_{2})\phi(g_{1})\,(\rho_{0})],

(4)

where
E
and
\rho
are a measurement POVM element and input state, respectively. Note that
g_{\mathrm{inv}}
does not need to be physically implemented, it can also be accounted for in the classical post-processing through so-called filter functions
Helsen et al. 2019a
;
Onorati et al. 2019
;
Helsen et al. 2022
;
Heinrich et al. 2022
, which we will use in the classical post-processing stage of our schemes.
For different circuits lengths
m
, we can repeat this measurement for several random sequences and average to obtain a sample average
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}
, which is fit to an exponential model with decay parameters
\{\lambda_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
that constitute the figures of merit of an RB protocol,

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}(m)=\sum_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}(E,\rho)\lambda_{\alpha}^{m}.

(5)

Conveniently, by making use of the filtering functions, we can also extract each
\lambda_{\alpha}
separately.

One of the major features of RB protocols is that they are often sample-efficient in achieving multiplicative precision, and robust against SPAM errors. RB is therefore an ideal candidate to provide us with some characterization of noisy randomized measurements.

RB estimates for noisy frame operators.

In reasonably good experimental implementations, the implementation map
\phi
is close to a representation.
As a consequence, its Fourier transform maintains the distinctive structure of the Fourier transform of a representation:
Breaking down
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\Ad]
for each irreducible representation
\alpha
of
\mathcal{G}
occurring in
\Ad
,
we write using standard isomorphisms
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\,\alpha^{\ast}(g)\otimes\phi(g)
.
Let
m_{\alpha}
be the multiplicity of
\alpha
in
\Ad
.
The operator
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]
(being a perturbation of the projection
\Ad_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]
) has
m_{\alpha}
eigenvalues close to
1
and the remaining eigenvalues close to zero
Helsen et al. 2022
.
In a nutshell, RB signals are described by matrix powers of
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]

Merkel et al. 2021
;
Helsen et al. 2022
, thus, the observed decay parameters correspond to the dominant eigenvalue(s)

\lambda_{\alpha}
of
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]
.
In case of
m_{\alpha}>1
, we pick a representative value for the dominant eigenvalues, e.g. taking the average thereof.
Using
Eq.

3
, we can use
\lambda_{\alpha}
as follows to arrive at an estimate for
\mathcal{M}
in the presence of noise:
For each
\alpha
in the irreducible decomposition appearing in
\Ad
, we define

\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]\coloneqq\lambda_{\alpha}\Ad_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]\,.

(6)

For this estimate to be accurate, we implicitly assume that the noise mainly effects the spectrum of the channel twirl but preserves its subspace structure.

We will from now on restrict to multiplicity-free decompositions of
\Ad
for simplicity.
Evaluating
Eq.

3
from the estimator
Eq.

6
may require the substitution of the coefficients
\lambda_{\alpha}
, that is, associating
\Ad[\alpha]
with a decay parameter
\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime}}
of a group
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
which may possibly be different from
\mathcal{G}
.
The reason for this is the presence of
B
in
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
, which produces a shift in the invariant subspace of
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}[\Ad]
.
Namely, we construct

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})}\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime}}\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\alpha}B]}{\Tr[\Pi_{\alpha}]}\Pi_{\alpha},

(7)

where
\Pi_{\alpha}
is the projector onto the irrep
\alpha
of
\mathcal{G}
and
\lambda_{\alpha^{\prime}}
is the RB decay parameter of an irrep
\alpha^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.

Frame operator for robust shadow estimation.

As an application of the general approach explained in the previous section, we can construct noise-mitigated shadow estimation protocols.
We start with global Clifford measurements, i.e., we set
\mathcal{G}
to be the multi-qubit Clifford group, whose
\Ad
representation splits into two inequivalent irreducible components. The noise-free frame operator in this setting is given by
Huang et al. 2020

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ideal}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}/(d+1),

(8)

where
d
is the dimension of the system,
\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}
is the projector onto the fully mixed state, and
\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}=\mathds{1}-\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}
is its complement.
The frame operator is altered by noise
and thus must be corrected to yield reliable shadow estimates.
Such a correction has been proposed in Refs.
Koh and Grewal 2022
;
Chen et al. 2021
(which does so through a separate calibration experiment).

We propose a strategy (see
Protocol
) to estimate the frame operator by using an RB protocol drawing gates from the CNOT-dihedral group, a finite group generated by
\mathbb{K}=\langle\mathrm{CNOT},S,X\rangle
, with
S
being the phase gate, to extract the decay parameter
\lambda_{Z}
.
For gate-independent noise,
\lambda_{Z}
coincides with effective depolarizing strength of a noise channel restricted to the subspace of the computational basis (spanned by Pauli-
Z
operators) and might be refered to as the effective dephasing strength – cfr.
Eq.

18
.
Single-qubit dihedral and CNOT-dihedral RB has been proposed in Refs.
Cross et al. 2016
;
Carignan-Dugas et al. 2015
, respectively.
We here present a modified version for CNOT-dihedral RB using filter functions.
In the spirit of
Eq.

7
, we define an estimate for the frame operator in terms of the decay parameter
\lambda_{Z}
as

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\frac{\lambda_{Z}}{d+1}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}\,.

(9)

We give a more careful justification for using
\lambda_{Z}
arising in CNOT-dihedral RB in
Section

C.1
.
In particular, we show that in the case of gate-independent noise we arrive at perfect noise-cancellation in expectation.

The protocol explained above still requires a separate RB experiment for the calibration.
But we can actually devise an (informationally-complete) estimation protocol that is self-calibrating with a small adaptation: We pick a single Clifford gate
c
at random and implement it as the first gate of the circuit.
The remaining
m
gates will again be drawn from the CNOT-dihedral group.
Since this circuit corresponds to a random Clifford operator thanks to the first gate, we can use the measurement data from this RB experiment to directly construct the shadows.
At the same time, we can use the same set of measurement outcomes
\{b^{\prime}\}
to classically compute
(d+1)\Tr[E\,\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}\Ad^{\dagger}(g_{\mathrm{end}})(b^{\prime})]
(where
g_{\mathrm{end}}
is the group element resulting from the composition of the whole circuit) and use these quantities to classically extract
\lambda_{Z}
as the decay parameter of the fitting model for the sample average, that is,

\mathcal{Q}(m+1)=\Tr[E\,\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}(\rho)]\,\lambda_{Z}^{m+1}.

(10)

We will then use the extracted parameter to calibrate the frame operator,

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{m+1}\coloneqq\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\frac{\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)^{m+1}}{d+1}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}},

(11)

and produce the noise-mitigated classical shadow from every outcome
b^{\prime}
resulting from a random circuit of length
m+1
,

\hat{\rho}_{m+1}=\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}_{m+1}\Ad^{\dagger}(g_{\mathrm{end}})|{b^{\prime}}{\rangle\!\rangle}.

(12)

In this way, we can construct shadows
and
calibrate them using the same data from a single quantum experiment, with the striking advantage of addressing precisely the noise dynamics that is affecting the shadows.

A simpler scheme results when using classical shadows and conventional RB both with uniformly drawn multi-qubit Clifford gates.
We can again obtain calibration and estimation from the same experimental data.
For this to work, we require the additional assumption

\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}\approx\lambda_{Z},

(13)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}
is the parameter obtained from the conventional Clifford RB method, often related to the effective depolarizing strength.
We can use this figure to estimate the frame operator by classically computing

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj}}=\Pi+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}}{d+1}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}.

(14)

Remarkably, the approximation in
Eq.

13
turns out to be accurate for a number of noise models.
In
Section

D.1
we analytically derive the explicit ratio of the decay parameters for common noise models – depolarizing noise, bit-flip noise, and amplitude damping – and demonstrate numerically that in these scenarios the error bias in
Eq.

21
is significantly smaller than the uncalibrated bias in
Eq.

19
.

Finally, let us study a setting where we want to implement a protocol involving
local
Clifford gates only, considerably reducing the experimental requirements.
In this case, it is common to consider the task of learning expectation values of observables of the form
O=\sum_{i}O_{i}
, where each
O_{i}
has non-trivial support of bounded locality.
In this case, it suffices to produce a calibration of the frame operator restricted to the local supports. This is given by

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\{O_{i}\}_{i})\coloneqq\sum_{w\ :\ \supp{w}\subseteq\ \bigcup\supp{O_{i}}}p_{w,B}\,\Pi_{w},

(15)

where
\Pi_{w}
are the projectors onto the subset of operators with vanishing local partial trace on the subsystems in the support of
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, cfr.
Chen et al. 2021
.

The required calibration parameters
p_{w,B}
can be related to notions of an effective local dephasing strength.
Ref.
Helsen et al. 2023
studies extensions of RB protocols that allow for interleaving so-called probe operators in the post-processing, thereby constructing classical shadows of the average noise associated to a gate set.
Here, we use this gate-set shadow protocol with sequences of parallel local random Clifford unitaries to estimate the parameters
p_{w,B}
.
Roughly speaking, we interleave filter functions with the operator
B
in the post-processing to estimate local dephasing strengths instead of local depolarizing strengths.
The complexity bounds provided in Ref.
Helsen et al. 2023
ensure efficient learning of
p_{w,B}
of size
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, yielding an efficient estimation of
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\{O_{i}\}_{i})
in
Eq.

15
under gate-independent noise and observables with
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-bounded support.

Performance guarantees.

Assuming a gate-independent noise channel
\Lambda
acting after the implementation of the ideal unitary, we model the noisy implementation
\phi(g)
1
1

1

Note that the way we make use of this assumption in our work, this noise model does not include read-out noise (contrary to the similar assumption made in Ref.
Chen et al. 2021
).
We might assume that the read-out noise was already addressed by a separate read-out mitigation protocol
Maciejewski et al. 2020
;
Karalekas et al. 2020
;
van den Berg et al. 2022
.

of an ideal gate
\Ad(g)
as

\phi(g)=\Lambda\,\Ad(g).

(16)

Note that this noise model is even more restrictive as the gate-independent noise model often used in guarantees for RB, as we do not allow for noise to act before the gate.
Then, the
physical frame operator
(that is, the frame operator accounting for the noisy implementation of the quantum unitaries) corresponds (as per Ref.
Chen et al. 2021
) to

\mathcal{M^{\natural}}\coloneqq\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\frac{\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)}{d+1}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}},

(17)

where the
Z
-parameter
\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)
for gate-independent noise is

\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)=\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}B\Lambda]}{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}B]}\\
=\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}B\Lambda]}{d-1}.

(18)

An uncalibrated shadow estimation procedure thus yields a state estimator with a bias of the form

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ideal}}^{-1}\,\mathcal{M^{\natural}}-\mathds{1}=\left(\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)-1\right)\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}.

(19)

To mitigate this bias in the estimation, it is thus necessary to access the quantity
\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)
.
Strikingly, one of the decay parameters from an RB scheme drawing from the CNOT-dihedral group (plus the optional first gate from the Clifford group) coincides
exactly
with the quantity we need to extract to reconstruct the frame operator as per
Eq.

17
. Under the gate-independence assumption,
we can hence reconstruct the physical frame operator
\mathcal{M^{\natural}}
in full (and obtain its inversion for the shadow protocol).

The global Clifford scheme works under the assumption of
Eq.

13
, which for gate-independent noise translates to

\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda)=\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}\Lambda]}{d^{2}-1}\approx\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}B\Lambda]}{d-1}=\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda),

(20)

yielding an error bias

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{-1}\,\mathcal{M^{\natural}}-\mathds{1}=\left\{\frac{\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda)}-1\right\}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}.

(21)

It is key to the approach suggested in this work that not only
an error bias is mitigated, but also that the scalability of the sample complexity is precisely guaranteed. In
Appendix

E
we include error bounds on the variance of the derived variants of robust shadows estimation. In particular, in
Section

E.1
we demonstrate that the variance of the CNOT-dihedral scheme is
\mathcal{O}(1)
in the number of qubits and sequence lengths.

Numerical examples.

We numerically simulated the protocol for the task of estimating the fidelity of a GHZ-state where the state preparation is ideal for various noise models affecting the gates randomizing the measurement.
In this way, every deviation of the fidelity away from 1 is caused by noise and errors in the randomized measurement.
A selection of numerical results is shown in
Fig.

2
, further results are presented in
Appendix

D
and
Appendix

F
.
We find that the separate calibration with CNOT-dihedral RB can successfully correct the frame operator for arbitrary gate-independent noise.
The performance of the (global) Clifford group calibration depends on the type of (gate-independent) noise.
In accordance with our theoretical derivations, certain noise models still have a bias in the estimation.
Nonetheless, we consistently observe improvements over uncalibrated estimation for the parameter regime under consideration.
For gate-dependent noise, we observe that our method improves the accuracy of shadow estimation for all considered noise models, albeit leaving a small bias in all cases.

Conclusions and outlook.

In this work,
we have developed methods for mitigating the noise in randomized measurement schemes that is introduced by the gates randomizing the measurements.
We argued that the flexible toolkit provided by randomized benchmarking methods and its extension to gate-set shadows
can be successfully put to use in order to characterize the relevant parameters of the noise affecting randomized measurements.
As a result, using these calibration parameters, noise in the randomized measurements can be mitigated or even completely accounted for in classical post-processing.
We have exemplified different variants of the approach for randomization with the multi-qubit Clifford group, assisted by either standard RB or filtered RB using the CNOT-dihedral group, and with local Clifford unitaries.
In particular, with slight modifications the RB protocol for calibration and the estimation protocol can use the same measurement data, giving rise to self-calibrating shadow estimation schemes.
The performance of the scheme can be understood using a gate-independent noise assumption, and we provide rigorous guarantees in this setting.
This is complemented by numerical demonstrations of the protocol’s performance for practically motivated noise examples.
Besides its efficiency we inherit the robustness against state-preparation from RB.

Possibly the most important conceptual take-away message from this work is of highly practical nature:
sometimes the available set of techniques and data might already be
sufficient to mitigate errors, without the need to resort to more sophisticated characterization experiments.
Instead, much of the burden can be placed on the theoretical understanding of the relation between the parameters entering different protocols under plausible noise assumptions.
We here take a first step to draw such connections, still using arguably restrictive assumptions on the noise models.
We hope that this motivates further work on suitable approximations of average noise characterization under a more flexible set of assumptions.

B.2  Randomized benchmarking

Randomized benchmarking
(RB)

Knill et al. 2008
;
Magesan et al. 2011b
;
Helsen et al. 2022

refers to a large family of noise characterization schemes with similar characteristics. The common trait of all these procedures is that they make use of sequences of random quantum gates followed by a POVM measurement.
With the collected data, we compute a
sample average
for varying sequence lengths, which are inserted into a theoretical model to extrapolate and estimate a set of parameters. These serve as figures of merit to quantitatively assess the level of noise which is affecting the experimental realization of the gate-set.

The key point of RB is that a scalable number of samples suffices to closely approximate the theoretical average
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}
over all possible random samples. The fitting model for
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}
has the form of linear combination of matrix exponential decays
Helsen et al. 2022
, each of them connected to a different irreducible representation in the decomposition of the
\Ad
representation of the group from which the random sequences are drawn.
In the particular case where no irreducible representation appears more than once in the decomposition, that is, the
\Ad
representation is multiplicity-free, the fitting model simplifies into a sum of scalar parameter decays
\lambda^{m}
, one for each irreducible representation
\alpha
in the decomposition. Namely,

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}(m)=\sum_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}(E,\rho)\lambda_{\alpha}^{m},

(27)

where
\{y_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
are constants depending on
m
, the input state
\rho
, the POVM measurement
E
and the end gate
g_{\mathrm{end}}
resulting from the whole circuit. This gate can either be chosen to be a specific operator, or selected at random (which is a central requirement for shadow estimation).
By absorbing the measurement errors in the preparation of
\rho
and realization of
E
, they make RB protocols robust against this kind of errors.
A second highly favorable feature of RB is the fact that they are resilient against gate-dependent noise
Wallman 2018
;
Helsen et al. 2022
. This is a key advantage compared to other calibration schemes that are less stable under gate-dependent errors
Brieger et al. 2023
.

Among the many RB variants, for this work the relevant ones are the
uniform
type, where one considers a group as the gate set from which gates are drawn uniformly at random. That is, for an input state
\rho
and a given sequence length
m
, we produce measurements with respect to some POVM
E
of a circuit generated by
m
random gates and a final gate
g_{\mathrm{end}}
,

\mathcal{Q}(m,g_{\mathrm{end}},\rho,E)\coloneqq{\langle\!\langle}{E}|\phi(g_{\mathrm{end}}g_{\mathrm{inv}})\phi(g_{m})\dotsb\phi(g_{2})\phi(g_{1})|{\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle},

(28)

with
g_{\mathrm{inv}}:=(g_{m}\cdots g_{2}g_{1})^{-1}
.
The resulting measurement data
Q(m,g_{\mathrm{end}},\rho,E)
are then used in the post processing stage – possibly together with some other classically computed filtering functions – to generate the sample average
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}(m)
for the fitting model in
Eq.

27
.
Regarding the choice of the gate set, for our calibration routine we will employ uniform RB procedures drawing from the Clifford group
Knill et al. 2008
;
Magesan et al. 2011b
and the CNOT-dihedral group
Cross et al. 2016
.

B.3  Shadow estimation

Given an unknown quantum state we are interested in characterizing, the shadow estimation scheme
Huang et al. 2020
allows for the estimation of many observables from a so-called classical shadows of the state, i.e. measurement data from an IC-POVM implemented by measuring in random orthonormal bases.
In specific settings, e.g. fidelity estimation with multi-qubit Clifford rotations, the number of samples (or copies of the state) required scales efficiently in the system size and the estimation precision, and logarithmically with respect to the number of observables.

To carry out the procedure, we first randomly select an element
g\in\mathcal{C}
, and apply the physical quantum channel
\phi(g)
on
\rho

\{b\}_{b}

Finally, we apply the inverse of the frame operator to the classically and efficiently stored object

where the frame operator

is defined and calculated in Ref.
Scott 2006

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ideal}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}\Ad^{\dagger}(g)B\,\Ad(g)=\left(\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}B]}{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}]}\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\frac{\Tr[(\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}B)]}{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}]}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}\right)\\
=\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\frac{1}{d+1}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}},

(30)

with

\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}:={d}^{-1}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}|{j}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{k}|

(31)

being the vectorization representation of the rank-1 projection onto the identity operator connected to the trivial invariant subspace, and consequently
\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}=\mathds{1}_{d^{2}}-\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}
the projection onto its complement subspace related to the adjoint irreducible representation.

From

Set

Appendix C
CNOT-dihedral group calibration

Here the assumption is that the gate-independent noise channel of the Clifford group is the same as the gate-independent noise of the CNOT-dihedral subgroup.
This assumption is more subtle than the standard gate-independent noise assumption in the following sense. The gate-independent noise channel adopted to describe the gate implementation of a gate set can be considered as the noise averaged over the set. Requiring that the average noise of a subgroup correspond to the average noise of the entire group is hence a stricter assumption than the standard gate independent noise assumption. In practical sense however we can match the noise for the CNOT-dihedral subgroup to the one of the whole Clifford group by compiling the CNOT-dihedral gates using some Clifford gates.

\Ad

The representation we are interested in is the one carrying the Pauli-

defines

as per

While the above scheme is suited to extract the

We proceed as follows. We use our target unknown quantum state
\rho

We produce a random circuit of length

m

\mathbb{K}

We then measure in the computational basis.
From the outcome

Appendix D
Clifford group calibration

In this section, we turn to discussing in detail the full Clifford group calibration. When the the structure of the noise model is known, at least to the point that we can exclude the contribution of phase errors – given in the form of
Z
-Pauli strings in
\{\mathds{1},Z\}^{\otimes n}\backslash\{\mathds{1}^{\otimes n}\}
– we can employ a standard Clifford RB scheme to calibrate the frame operator. This may be more convenient if we want to use this very common routine and without having to setup a CNOT-dihedral random circuit.

In the following, we will first describe a calibration scheme based on the global Clifford group as an effective approach to construct noise-mitigated shadows. As we will observe in the following, the crucial point is that the conventional Clifford RB procedure will return
\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda)=(\Tr[\Lambda]-1)/({d^{2}-1})
: as long as the noise channel has negligible
Z
-noise contribution, this quantity will be close to
\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)
, hence providing a reliable correction of the classical shadows. We demonstrate this simpler approach with numerical examples.
Furthermore, we will describe an efficient
local
Clifford calibration scheme for the calibration parameters required for shadow estimation of observables with
\log n
support size.

D.1  Global Cliffords

We recall that the
\Ad
representation of the Clifford group conveniently splits into two non-degenerate representations only: the trivial and the adjoint. In other words,

\Ad\simeq 1\oplus\mathrm{adj}.

(51)

The former carries the one-dimensional subspace spanned by the identity,
\mathds{1}
, the latter carries the invariant subspace of trace-less matrices, that is,

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{adj}}=\mathrm{span}\{p_{j}\,:\,j=1,\dots,d^{2}-1\}.

(52)

The fitting model of the RB scheme is thus conveniently expressed by a single parameter decay
Magesan et al. 2011a
;
Magesan et al. 2012

Q_{\mathrm{avg}}(m,E,\rho)=a_{\mathrm{triv}}+a_{\mathrm{adj}}\,\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}^{m}

(53)

for some scalars
a_{\mathrm{triv}}
and
a_{\mathrm{adj}}
absorbing SPAM errors. Here, we have used that

\lambda_{\mathrm{triv}}(\Lambda)=\Tr[\Lambda(\mathds{1}/d)]=1,

(54)

which follows from the
trace preserving condition for
\Lambda
,
and

\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda)=\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}\Lambda]}{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}]}=\frac{\Tr[\Lambda]-1}{d^{2}-1}.

(55)

According to the formula in
Eq.

41
we construct the error-mitigated frame operator,

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda)}{d+1}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}.

(56)

Then, the error of the calibrated shadow procedure can be stated as
follows, combining
Eqs.

37
and
56
.

Lemma 5

(Bias for the calibrated frame operator)
.

It holds that

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{-1}\,\mathcal{M^{\natural}}-\mathds{1}=\left\{\frac{\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda)}-1\right\}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}=\left\{(d+1)\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}B\Lambda]}{\Tr[\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}\Lambda]}-1\right\}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}.

(57)

While this clearly does not correspond to a perfect calibration, there are different scenarios where the gap between the two quantities closes, yielding effective and stable calibration. More precisely, this favorably occurs when the noise channel does not overlap significantly with Pauli strings containing purely Pauli-
Z
operators and local identities, that is, has negligible support on the invariant subspace connected to the
Z
-parameter. Practical scenarios are:
(i) random Pauli noise (where the portion of
\{\mathds{1},Z\}^{\otimes 2n}
-Pauli strings is
1/2^{n}
of the total), (ii) considering
\Lambda
to be the depolarizing channel – where this conventional Clifford RB protocol provides a perfect calibration. This noise model is often sensible, especially for large system sizes (e.g., in the context of achieving a quantum advantage for NISQ devices
Boixo et al. 2018
;
Arute et al. 2019
;
Wu et al. 2021
;
Hangleiter and Eisert 2023
).

Indeed, previous work
Dalzell et al. 2021
has shown that for 2-local random quantum circuits whose single-gate noise is sufficiently weak and unital, after
n\log n
steps the resultant noise is the global depolarizing noise; in other words, random circuits scramble local gate-dependent noise.
In this context, if we consider the Clifford group on
n
-qubits to be generated by a local set such as the CNOT, Hadamard and phase gates, any Clifford operator generated by
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
CNOT gates is an ideal operator equipped with the depolarizing channel. Note that as the system size grows, randomly choosing a Clifford gate prepared by
n\log n
local CNOT is an event that occurs with probability 1.

Like the CNOT-dihedral scheme in
Section

C.2
, this scheme can also be used in a self-calibrating fashion, that is, producing the shadows and the calibrated frame operator from the same experiments. In this case, the calibrated operator will be

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj},m}=\Pi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda)^{m}}{d+1}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}},

(58)

yielding the same error bias as in
Lemma

5
, and a calibrated shadow obtained from a measurement of a global Clifford circuit of length
m

\hat{\rho}_{m}=\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj},m}^{-1}\Ad^{\dagger}(g_{\mathrm{end}})|{b^{\prime}}{\rangle\!\rangle},

(59)

where
g_{\mathrm{end}}=c_{m}\cdots c_{1}
.

We will observe the validity of this calibration scheme in following numerical investigation.

Lemma 7

(Local depolarizing noise)
.

We consider the noise channel

\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{dep,loc}})=\frac{(2-p)^{n}-1}{d-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{dep,loc}})=\frac{(4-3p)^{n}-1}{d^{2}-1},

and thus

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{-1}\,\mathcal{M^{\natural}}-\mathds{1}=\left\{(d+1)\frac{(2-p)^{n}-1}{(4-3p)^{n}-1}-1\right\}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}.

Proof.

We expand the expression for the

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{dep,loc}}

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{n}\mathds{1}_{2n}+(1-p)^{n-1}p\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha_{1}\in\{0,1,2,3\}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{\alpha_{1}}^{(j)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{j^{c}}_{n-1}\otimes g_{\alpha_{1}}^{(j)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{(j^{c})}

\displaystyle+(1-p)^{n-2}p^{2}\frac{1}{4^{2}}\sum_{\alpha_{2}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{\times 2}}\sum_{j\neq k}g_{\alpha_{2}}^{(j,k)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{(j,k)^{c}}\otimes g_{\alpha_{2}}^{(j,k)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{(j,k)^{c}}

\displaystyle+\dots+p^{n}\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{\times n}}g_{\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}(1-p)^{n-\ell}p^{\ell}\frac{1}{4^{\ell}}\sum_{\alpha_{\ell}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{\times\ell}}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{W}(n,\ell)}g_{\alpha_{\ell}}^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}}\otimes g_{\alpha_{\ell}}^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}},

where we denote
\mathcal{W}(n,\ell)
to be the set of combinations of
\ell
qubits out of
n
,
X^{w}
,
g_{\alpha_{\ell}}^{\mu}
is a Pauli string fo length
\ell

\alpha_{\ell}

\mu

.
Since every Pauli string made of identities and

\displaystyle\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{dep,loc}})

\displaystyle=

Lemma 8

(Bit-flip noise)
.

Consider the bit-flip channel
\Lambda_{\mathrm{flip}}
, where each qubit is errorless with probability
1-p
and experiences Pauli-

\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{flip}})=\frac{d(1-p)^{n}-1}{d-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{flip}})=\frac{d^{2}(1-p)^{n}-1}{d^{2}-1},

and therefore,

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{-1}\,\mathcal{M^{\natural}}-\mathds{1}=\left\{(d+1)\frac{d\,(1-p)^{n}-1}{d^{2}(1-p)^{n}-1}-1\right\}\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{flip}}

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{n}\mathds{1}_{2n}+(1-p)^{n-1}p\sum_{j=1}^{n}X^{(j)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{j^{c}}_{n-1}\otimes X^{(j)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{(j^{c})}

\displaystyle+(1-p)^{n-2}p^{2}\sum_{j\neq k}X^{(j,k)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{(j,k)^{c}}\otimes X^{(j,k)}\otimes\mathds{1}^{(j,k)^{c}}

\displaystyle+\dots+p^{n}X^{\otimes 2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}(1-p)^{n-\ell}p^{\ell}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{W}(n,\ell)}X^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}}\otimes X^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}},

where
X^{w}
is the application of
X
gates on the qubits in
\mu
and the identity acting on the complement system
\mu^{c}
.
We have in this case

\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{flip}})=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}^{n}{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Lambda_{\mathrm{flip}}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}-1\right)=\frac{d(1-p)^{n}-1}{d-1},

since any string containing a Pauli

-qubit identity element will contribute to the trace, hence

\Tr[\Lambda_{\mathrm{flip}}]=d^{2}(1-p)^{n}.

(72)

Lemma 9

(Amplitude damping noise)
.

Consider the amplitude damping channel
\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}}
, where each qubit is errorless with probability
p
, and damped with probability
1-p
according to a damping parameter
\gamma
. Then

\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}})=\frac{d+(2-p+(p-1)\gamma)^{n}-(2-p)^{n}-1}{d-1}\qquad

(73)

and

\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}})=\frac{\big[2-\gamma+2\sqrt{1-\gamma}+p(2+\gamma-2\sqrt{1-\gamma})\big]^{n}-1}{d^{2}-1}.

(74)

Proof.

As before, we expand the expression for the amplitude damping channel as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}p^{n-\ell}(1-p)^{\ell}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{W}(n,\ell)}\Big[E_{1}^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}}_{n-\ell}\otimes E_{1}^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}}_{n-\ell}+E_{2}^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}}_{n-\ell}\otimes E_{2}^{\mu}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\mu^{c}}_{n-\ell}\Big],

(75)

where
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are the amplitude damping operators.
We then have

\displaystyle\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}^{n}{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}-1}{d-1}

(76)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}^{n}\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}}^{\text{first copy}}\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle^{2}-1}{d-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^{n-k}(1-p)^{k}\Big(2^{n}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\binom{k}{\ell}((\sqrt{1-\gamma})^{2\ell}-1)\Big)-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(d-1+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^{n-k}(1-p)^{k}(2-\gamma)^{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^{n-k}(1-p)^{k}2^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d+[p+(1-p)(2-\gamma)]^{n}-[p+2(1-p)]^{n}-1}{d-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d+[2-p+(p-1)\gamma]^{n}-[2-p]^{n}-1}{d-1}

and

\displaystyle\Tr[\Lambda_{\mathrm{AD}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^{n-k}(1-p)^{k}4^{n-k}(2-\gamma+2\sqrt{1-\gamma})^{k}

(77)

\displaystyle=\big[4p+(2-\gamma+2\sqrt{1-\gamma})(1-p)\big]^{n}

\displaystyle=\big[2-\gamma+2\sqrt{1-\gamma}+p(2+\gamma-2\sqrt{1-\gamma})\big]^{n},

from which the claim follows.
∎

The asymptotic behavior for many-qubit systems is not evident from

9
, but we can observe from

that for an 8-qubit system, the calibration yields to an error-robust fidelity estimation even for significant levels of noise damping (represented by the coefficient
\gamma
).

implemented for a number of architectures. Here, we write the real frame operator in terms of the tensor of local projections

labeled by strings

Lemma 10

(Frame operator for local Clifford shadow estimation)
.

\mathcal{M^{\natural}}=\sum_{w}\frac{\Tr[\Pi_{w}B\Lambda]}{\Tr[\Pi_{w}]}\Pi_{w}\eqqcolon\sum_{w}c_{w}\Pi_{w},

(78)

with

c_{w}=\frac{1}{2^{n}3^{\lvert w\rvert}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{w\cdot(x\oplus y)}\left.\left\langle{y,y}\right.\right|\Lambda\left.\left|{x,x}\right.\right\rangle.

(79)

Proof.

To write
\Pi_{w}
in a convenient form we do some tensor manipulation and transform

|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}=\left.\left|{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n};b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}}\right.\right\rangle\mapsto\left.\left|{b_{1},b_{1},b_{2},b_{2},\dots,b_{n},b_{n}}\right.\right\rangle.

(80)

Under this isomorphism, we can consider for each set of projections with the same Hamming weight
\lvert w\rvert
a representative element

\Pi_{\lvert w\rvert}^{\mathrm{rep}}=P_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\otimes n-w}\otimes(\mathds{1}_{4}-P_{\mathrm{loc}})^{\otimes w}
with
P_{\mathrm{loc}}=\sum_{j,k\in\{0,1\}}\left.\left|{j,j}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{k,k}\right.\right|/2
.
We now compute

\displaystyle\Pi_{\lvert w\rvert}^{\mathrm{rep}}B=P_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\otimes n-w}\otimes(\mathds{1}_{4}-P_{\mathrm{loc}})^{\otimes w}\sum\left.\left|{b_{1},b_{1},\dots,b_{n},b_{n}}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b_{1},b_{1},\dots,b_{n},b_{n}}\right.\right|

(81)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,b\in\{0,1\}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|{j}{\rangle\!\rangle}{\langle\!\langle}{k}|b{\rangle\!\rangle}{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Big)^{\otimes n-w}\otimes\Big(\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{b}|-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,\ell,b}\left.\left|{\ell,\ell}\right.\right\rangle{\langle\!\langle}{m}|b{\rangle\!\rangle}{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Big)^{\otimes w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-w}}\Big(\sum_{j,b\in\{0,1\}}|{j}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Big)^{n-w}\otimes\Big(\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{b}|-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell,b\in\{0,1\}}|{\ell}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Big)^{\otimes w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-w}}\Big(\sum_{j,b\in\{0,1\}}|{j}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Big)^{n-w}\otimes\frac{1}{2^{w}}\Big(\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{b}|-\sum_{\ell,b\in\{0,1\}}|{b\oplus 1}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Big)^{\otimes w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|{x}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{y}|(-1)^{(\{0\}^{\times n-w}\times\{1\}^{\times w})\cdot(x\oplus y)},

where
x\oplus y
is a component-wise addition modulo 2.
From this calculation, we can then see that for a general projection
\Pi_{w}
for some label
w
we have

\Pi_{w}B=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|{x}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{y}|(-1)^{w\cdot(x\oplus y)},

(82)

and consequently (with
\Tr[\Pi_{w}]=3^{\lvert w\rvert}
)

c_{w}=\Tr[\Pi_{w}B\Lambda]/3^{\lvert w\rvert}=\frac{1}{2^{n}3^{\lvert w\rvert}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{w\cdot(x\oplus y)}{\langle\!\langle}{y}|\Lambda|{x}{\rangle\!\rangle}.

(83)

∎

We shall now consider the quantity that we can retrieve from the local Clifford gate-set shadow procedure. From Eq. (15) of Ref.
Helsen et al. 2023
, this is,

p_{w,B}(\Lambda)=\Tr[\Pi_{w}B\Pi_{w}\Lambda]/3^{\lvert w\rvert}=\Tr[B_{w}\Lambda]/3^{\lvert w\rvert},

(84)

where
B_{w}=\Pi_{w}B\Pi_{w}
.
We note that, for
B_{\mathrm{loc}}\coloneqq|{0}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{0}|+\left.\left|{1,1}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{1,1}\right.\right|
,

B_{\mathrm{loc}}P_{\mathrm{loc}}=B_{\mathrm{loc}}P_{\mathrm{loc}}=P_{\mathrm{loc}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathds{1}_{4}-P_{\mathrm{loc}})B_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathds{1}_{4}-P_{\mathrm{loc}})=(\mathds{1}_{4}-P_{\mathrm{loc}})B_{\mathrm{loc}}.

(85)

In our basis representation
Eq.

80

B=B_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\otimes n}
and thus from
Eq.

85
follows that
\Pi_{w}B\Pi_{w}=\Pi_{w}B
and consequently

p_{w,B}(\Lambda)=c_{w}

(86)

as defined in
Eq.

79
.

The sample complexity is controlled by Theorem 4, Eq. (16) in Ref.
Helsen et al. 2023
.
Since

\Tr[B_{w}B_{w}^{\dagger}]=\Tr[B_{w}]=1,

(87)

we have
p_{w,B_{w}}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{O}(3^{-\lvert w\rvert})
. In the regime
\lvert w\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, we will require an estimation of this quantity up to precision
1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
. According to the variance bounds in Eq. (16) of Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
we have scalable protocol in
n
. Note that here we did not assume any local structure for
\Lambda
.

From the above considerations, it follows that again we recover precisely (exponentially) many coefficients
c_{w}
with
\lvert w\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
characterizing
\mathcal{M^{\natural}}
.
If we want to learn a set of functions
\Tr[O_{i}\rho]
with individual support that can be broken down into parts
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, it suffices to recover only those coefficients whose support is contained in it.
Hence, we can efficiently build a calibrated frame operator

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\{O_{i}\}_{i})\coloneqq\sum_{w\ :\ \supp{w}\subseteq\ \bigcup\supp{O_{i}}}p_{w,B}(\Lambda)\,\Pi_{w},

(88)

which yields a error-robust local Clifford shadow scheme (under the gate-independent noise assumption).

(ii)

(iii)

(v)

Appendix E
Variance bounds

The variance of the calibrated shadow estimation procedure is asymptotically independent from the system dimension for observables having local support. The calculations are carried out under the gate-independent noise assumption.
We recall that the variance is the determining figure for characterizing the sample complexity of both shadow estimation
Huang et al. 2020
and RB
Helsen et al. 2019b
.

E.1  CNOT-dihedral shadow scheme

We have proposed a procedure to construct error-mitigated shadows through CNOT-dihedral benchmarking.
This has lead to the average outcome

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}(m)=\sum_{x}\mathbb{E}_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{k_{1},\ldots k_{m}\in\mathbb{K}}{\langle\!\langle}{O}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\Ad(g_{\mathrm{end}})^{\dagger}|{x}{\rangle\!\rangle}{\langle\!\langle}{x}|\Lambda\Ad(k_{m})\cdots\cdots\Lambda\Ad(k_{1})\Lambda\Ad(c)|{\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}

(89)

at sequence length
m
. As we have derived in
Lemma

3
, this average is given by

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}(m)={\langle\!\langle}{E}|\Pi_{\mathrm{adj}}|{\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}\,\lambda_{Z}(\Lambda)^{m+1}.

(90)

Hence one can estimate
{\langle\!\langle}{O}|\rho{\rangle\!\rangle}
in a robust way by measuring
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{avg}}(m)
for a few points and calculating the intercept with zero. This can be done efficiently
Harper et al. 2019
provided the variance of estimating
p(m)
is bounded for all
m
. We will see that this straightforwardly is the case. For convenience we assume that
O
is traceless.
We then have the following theorem.

Theorem 11
.

Let
O
be traceless. Then the variance of the CNOT-dihedral shadow scheme (at sequence length
m
) is bounded by

\mathbb{V}\leq c\,\Tr(O^{2})

for some constant
c
independent of
n
and
m
.

Proof.

We find

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle\leq(2^{n}+1)^{2}\sum_{x}\mathbb{E}_{c_{\mathrm{end}}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{k_{1},\ldots k_{m}\in\mathbb{K}}{\langle\!\langle}{O}|\Ad^{\dagger}(c_{\mathrm{end}})|{x}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}{\langle\!\langle}{x}|\Lambda\Ad(k_{m})\cdots\Lambda\Ad(k_{1})\Lambda\Ad(c)|{\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}

(91)

\displaystyle=(2^{n}+1)^{2}\sum_{x}\sum_{C}{\langle\!\langle}{x^{\otimes 3}}|(I^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Lambda T_{\mathcal{K}}(\Lambda)^{m})C^{\otimes 3}|{O^{\otimes 2}\otimes\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}

(92)

\displaystyle=(2^{n}+1)^{2}\sum_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in S_{3}}W_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}{\langle\!\langle}{x^{\otimes 3}}|(I^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Lambda T_{\mathcal{K}}(\Lambda)^{m})|{\pi}{\rangle\!\rangle}{\langle\!\langle}{\pi^{\prime}}||{O^{\otimes 2}\otimes\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq(2^{n}+1)^{2}\tr(O^{2})\left\|W\right\|_{1,\infty}\sum_{x}\big(3\left.\left\langle{x}\right.\right|\!\Lambda T_{\mathcal{K}}(\Lambda)^{m}(\left.\left|{x}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{x}\right.\right|)\!\left.\left|{x}\right.\right\rangle+3\left.\left\langle{x}\right.\right|\!\Lambda T_{\mathcal{K}}(\Lambda)^{m}(I)\!\left.\left|{x}\right.\right\rangle\big)

by Hölder’s
1-\infty
inequality and the fact that
\Lambda T_{\mathcal{K}}(\Lambda)^{m}
is completely positive. Since
\left\|W\right\|_{1,\infty}=\mathcal{O}(2^{-3n})
and
\Lambda T_{\mathcal{K}}(\Lambda)^{m}
is also trace preserving, it is straightforward to see that
\mathbb{V}=\mathcal{O}(1)
in both
n
and
m
.
By standard linear regression we can learn the intercept in with variance bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{V})=\mathcal{O}(1)
in
n
.
∎

E.2  Global Clifford shadow scheme

For completeness, we write down the variance for the noise-mitigated shadow scheme assisted by conventional global Clifford RB. Note that this is a straightforward adaptation of the results of Refs.
Huang et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
.

Theorem 12

(Variance bound)
.

The variance of a single-round estimation
\hat{\xi}\equiv\hat{\xi}(O,\rho,g,b)={\langle\!\langle}{O}|\hat{\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var[\hat{\xi}]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2^{n}+1)2^{2n-1}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{RB}}^{2}(\mathrm{adj})(2^{n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}\Big(\Tr^{2}[O]+\Tr[O^{2}]\Big)

(93)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1)\Big(\Tr^{2}[O]+\Tr[O^{2}]\Big),

implying that for any local observable
O
the variance is dimensionless.

Proof.

We find

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var[\hat{\xi}]}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\sum_{b}{\langle\!\langle}{O}|^{\otimes 2}(\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1})^{\otimes 2}(\Ad^{\dagger}(g))^{\otimes 2}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{\otimes 2}{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\phi(g)|{\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\sum_{b}{\langle\!\langle}{O}|^{\otimes 2}(\Ad^{\dagger}(g))^{\otimes 2}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{\otimes 2}{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Lambda\Ad(g)|{\rho}{\rangle\!\rangle}.

(94)

Now we consider
\Ad^{\dagger}(g))^{\otimes 2}|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{\otimes 2}{\langle\!\langle}{b}|\Lambda\Ad(g)
in the (column-stacking) vectorized form and turn to Weingarten calculus to compute the unitary 3-design,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\Ad(g)^{\otimes 3}\big(|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Lambda|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}w_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}|{\pi}{\rangle\!\rangle}\!{\langle\!\langle}{\pi^{\prime}}|(|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Lambda|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle})

(95)

The coefficients
w_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}
are well characterized (see e.g.
Collins et al. 2022
). Notably, the diagonal elements of
w
dominate the off diagonal elements, so in the high
n
limit it suffices to only consider these. Thus,

\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:3-design_WG}=\frac{2^{2n}-2}{2^{n}(2^{2n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}\Tr[\pi(|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Lambda|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle})]|{\pi}{\rangle\!\rangle}+O(2^{-n}).

(96)

Now we can go back to
Eq.

94
and recall that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}=\frac{2^{n}+1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}}

(97)

and building on Lemma 7 of Ref.
Gross et al. 2015
, we write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var[\hat{\xi}]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2^{n}+1)^{2}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{RB}}(\mathrm{adj})^{2}}\frac{2^{2n}-2}{2^{n}(2^{2n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}\sum_{b}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}\tr[\pi(O^{\otimes 2}\otimes\rho)]\Tr[\pi(|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Lambda|{b}{\rangle\!\rangle})]

(98)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2^{n}+1)2^{2n}}{6\lambda_{\mathrm{RB}}(\mathrm{adj})^{2}2^{n}(2^{n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}\sum_{b}\Big(\Tr[O]^{2}\Tr[\rho]\Tr[\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|]^{2}\Tr[\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)]

\displaystyle+\Tr[O^{2}]\Tr[\rho]\Tr[\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle b\middle|b\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|]\Tr[\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)]+2\Tr[O]\Tr[O\rho]\Tr[\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|]\Tr[\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)]

\displaystyle+2\Tr[O^{2}\rho]\Tr[\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle b\middle|b\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{)}\right.\right|]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2^{n}+1)2^{2n}}{6\lambda_{1}^{2}(\mathrm{adj})2^{n}(2^{n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}\sum_{b}\Big(\Tr[O]^{2}\Tr[\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)]+\Tr[O^{2}]\Tr[\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)]

\displaystyle+2\Tr[O]\Tr[O\rho]\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle+2\Tr[O^{2}\rho]\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2^{n}+1)2^{2n}}{6\lambda_{1}^{2}(\mathrm{adj})2^{n}(2^{n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}\Big(\Tr[O^{2}]+\Tr[O]^{2}\Big)\Big(\Tr[\Lambda(\mathds{1})]+2\sum_{b}\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|\Lambda(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|{b})\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\Big),

where in the last equality we have used
\Tr[O\rho]\leq\Tr[O]
and
\Tr[O^{2}\rho]\leq\Tr[O^{2}]
.
Now, by the fact that

\sum_{b}\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|\mathcal{C}(\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\!\!\left.\left\langle{b}\right.\right|)\left.\left|{b}\right.\right\rangle\leq 2^{n}
,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var[\hat{\xi}]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2^{n}+1)2^{2n}}{6\lambda_{1}^{2}(\mathrm{adj})2^{n}(2^{n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}(2^{n}+2^{n+1})\Big(\Tr[O^{2}]+\Tr[O]^{2}\Big)

(99)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2^{n}+1)2^{2n-1}}{\lambda_{1}^{2}(\mathrm{adj})(2^{n}-1)(2^{2n}-4)}\Big(\Tr[O^{2}]+\Tr[O]^{2}\Big).

∎

Appendix F
Gate-dependent noise

In
Section

B.3
we have observed that under the gate-independent noise assumption the noise is completely confined in the dominant subspaces, hence allowing us to fully determine
\mathcal{M^{\natural}}
as per
Eq.

37
using the schemes presented in
Appendix

C
.
In the more general case of gate-dependent noise however, our estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
as per
Eq.

41
differs from the actual physical frame operator
\mathcal{M^{\natural}}
also regarding the invariant subspaces (which are also perturbed by noise) in the Fourier transform picture presented in the main letter.
Unfortunately, RB is not able to capture information structure of the invariant eigenspace themselves.
We know, however, that while basis vectors of a degenerate subspace are ill-conditioned against perturbations (in the sense that the eigenvectors produced by a perturbation may completely differ from the chosen basis), the invariant subspaces themselves are, however, robust. A perturbed subspace can be obtained from a transformation of the original one according to some bounded operator
Stewart and Sun 1990
;
Kato 1995
;
Helsen et al. 2022
.
Secondly, we have not taken into consideration the subspace of
\phi_{\mathrm{FT}}(\alpha)
corresponding to the perturbation of the kernel of
\Ad_{\mathrm{FT}}[\alpha]
. We simply set all of its eigenvalues to 0 in our estimator.
Again, we know that each eigenvalue will be within the ball of the perturbation, but this bound alone is in general not sufficient to control the error in estimating
\mathcal{M^{\natural}}
for general noise.

Recent work
Brieger et al. 2023
has shown that, in some settings,
shadow estimation might be unstable in the presence of gate-dependent noise and
robust shadow estimation procedures can even increase a noise-induced bias in the estimation compared to uncalibrated shadows estimation, rather then eliminating it.
For large classes of observables already the uncalibrated shadow estimation protocol is shown to be (perhaps surprisingly) robust in the presence of gate-dependent noise, in the sense that the bias in the estimation does not scale with the system size.
To investigate how our proposed protocol performs with gate-dependent noise against the uncalibrated scheme, we numerically simulated several cases of simple gate-dependent noise models.
In all simulations, we observed that our calibration reduces the noise-induced bias.

In
Fig.

6
, numerics for two further gate-dependent noise models are shown, in addition to panel (b) of Fig. 2 in the main letter. Panel (a) again shows the performance under gate-dependent depolarizing noise: In addition to 2-qubit depolarizing noise following CNOT gates, each single-qubit gate experiences local depolarizing noise with a parameter that is
10\%
of the 2-qubit noise. Although the gate-dependence diminishes the overall performance, the CNOT-dihedral calibration still significantly reduces the noise-induced bias. As expected from the discussion on local depolarizing noise in
Section

D.1
, the Clifford calibration overcompensates the noise, increasingly so for higher levels of noise.

Panel (b) assumes that the shadow estimation unitaries are compiled with a gate set consisting of CNOT and Pauli-rotation gates
\{R_{X},R_{Y},R_{Z}\}
. Each single-qubit rotation comes with a coherent over-rotation of a constant angle
\theta
. The resulting decay of the GHZ estimation fidelity, as well as the calibrated estimates from Clifford and CNOT-dihedral RB, are plotted as functions of the over-rotation angle
\theta
in radians. In this case, the Clifford calibration performs visibly better than its CNOT-dihedral counterpart.

These examples illustrate that there is a trade-off involved in the choice of calibration group: While the CNOT-dihedral calibration is always preferable for gate-independent noise, in more realistic scenarios involving gate-dependence, a unitary 3-design like the Clifford group may provide better performance.

Panel (a) uses a noise model consisting of 2-qubit depolarizing noise on CNOTs and local depolarizing noise on single-qubit gates. Appendix G
Details on numerical simulations

The protocol has numerically been simulated using Qiskit
Qiskit contributors 2023
and cirq
Cirq Developers 2023
for the noisy simulation of the experimental part. The post-processing has been performed with custom Python code and Qiskit’s Quantum Information module.
The task considered in Figs.
3
–
6
and the main text is the estimation of the state fidelity with an
n
-qubit GHZ state. The ideal GHZ state is prepared without errors, followed by noisy gates implementing the shadow estimation protocol. We then performed separate randomized benchmarking simulations with the same noise model as in the shadow estimation, and used the estimated decay parameter in the calibrated classical post-processing. If not otherwise stated, we used a total of 100000 samples for each data point shown in the figures, and chose
N=10^{4}
,
K=10
for the median-of-means estimation. To estimate the statistical uncertainty, we use the bootstrapped standard deviation calculated from 200 resamplings with replacement. This standard deviation is displayed as error bars in all figures.

Although we show that the calibration using CNOT-dihedral RB parameters is exact for gate-independent noise in the infinite-sample limit, we still observe statistical fluctuations and what appears to be systematic deviations from the ideal value, for example, in
Fig.

4
. Fluctuations around the ideal value beyond the shown error bars are explained by the statistical uncertainty in the estimation of the RB decay parameter, which we do not include in the figures. As previously noted in Ref.
Chen et al. 2021
, calibrated estimates exceeding the ideal value might result from the use of a ratio estimator, which introduces a bias for finite sample sizes.
