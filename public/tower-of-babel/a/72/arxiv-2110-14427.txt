Introduction

The stochastic approximation (SA) method of Robbins and
Monro
[
51
]
was designed to solve the
d
-dimensional
root-finding problem
{\overline{f}}(\theta^{*})=0
,
where
{\overline{f}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as the expectation

{\overline{f}}(\theta)\mathbin{:=}{\sf E}[f(\theta,\Phi)]
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\sf X}
and
\Phi
a random variable with values in
{\sf X}
.
Interest in SA has
grown over the past decade with increasing interest in reinforcement
learning (RL) and other “stochastic algorithms”
[
57
,
37
,
1
]
.

Algorithm design begins with recognition that
\theta^{*}
is a stationary point of the
mean flow
,

{\mathchoice{\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{d}{dt}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{d}{dt}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{3}{d}{dt}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{3}{d}{dt}}}\upvartheta_{t}={\overline{f}}(\upvartheta_{t}).

(1)

A standard assumption is that this ODE is globally asymptotically stable, so that in particular solutions converge to
\theta^{*}
from each initial condition
\upvartheta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In applications, the first step in the “ODE method” is to construct the function
f
that determines
{\overline{f}}
so that stability and other desirable properties are satisfied.

The mean flow can be approximated using an Euler scheme. Subject to
standard Lipschitz conditions, it is recognized that the Euler approximation
is robust to “measurement noise”, which motivates the SA recursion,

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+\alpha_{n+1}f(\theta_{n},\Phi_{n+1}),

(2)

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is the non-negative
step-size sequence, and the distribution of
\Phi_{n}
converges to that of
\Phi
as
n\to\infty
.
Writing
\Delta_{n+1}\mathbin{:=}f(\theta_{n},\Phi_{n+1})-{\overline{f}}(\theta_{n})
, the interpretation as a noisy Euler approximation is made explicit:

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+\alpha_{n+1}[{\overline{f}}(\theta_{n})+\Delta_{n+1}]\,,\quad n\geq 0.

(3)

Analysis of the SA recursion traditionally proceeds by comparison with solutions of the mean flow (
1
)
[
7
,
3
]
.
This requires a time-scaling:
Take,
\uptau_{0}=0
and define,

\uptau_{k+1}=\uptau_{k}+\alpha_{k+1},\qquad k\geq 0.

(4)

Two continuous-time processes are then compared:

(i)

Interpolated parameter process
:

\Uptheta_{t}=\theta_{k}
when
t=\uptau_{k}
, for each
k\geq 0
,

(5)

and defined for all
t
through piecewise linear interpolation.

(ii)

Re-started ODE
: For each
n\geq 0
, let

\{\upvartheta^{(n)}_{t}:t\geq\uptau_{n}\}
denote the solution
to (
1
), initialized according to the current parameter estimate:

{\mathchoice{\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{d}{dt}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{d}{dt}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{3}{d}{dt}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{3}{d}{dt}}}\upvartheta^{(n)}_{t}={\overline{f}}(\upvartheta^{(n)}_{t})\,,\quad t\geq\uptau_{n}\,,\qquad\upvartheta^{(n)}_{\uptau_{n}}=\theta_{n}.

(6)

Iteration of (
3
) gives, for any
0<n<K
,

\displaystyle\Uptheta_{\uptau_{K}}=\theta_{n}+\sum_{i=n}^{K-1}\alpha_{i+1}{\overline{f}}(\theta_{i})+\sum_{i=n}^{K-1}\alpha_{i+1}\Delta_{i+1}=\Uptheta_{\uptau_{n}}+\int_{\uptau_{n}}^{\uptau_{K}}{\overline{f}}(\Uptheta_{\tau})\,d\tau+{\cal E}_{K}^{(n)},

(7)

where
{\cal E}_{K}^{(n)}
is the sum of cumulative disturbances and the error resulting from the Riemann-Stieltjes approximation of the integral.
The disturbance term
{\cal E}_{K}^{(n)}
will vanish with

uniformly in

subject to conditions on
\{\Delta_{i}\}
and the step-size. This is,
by definition, the ODE approximation
of
\{\theta_{n}\}

[
7
,
3
]
.

A closer inspection of the theory shows that two ingredients are required to establish convergence of SA: The first is stability of the mean flow (
1
), as previously noted. The second requirement is that the parameter sequence
\{\theta_{n}\}
obtained from the SA recursion is bounded with probability one. Rates of convergence through the
central limit theorem (CLT) and the law of the iterated logarithm require further assumptions
[
3
,
33
,
30
,
7
,
22
,
42
]
.

There are two well-known approaches to establishing boundedness of the parameter sequence based on properties of the mean-flow.
The relationship between these two approaches is discussed
in
[
37
, Ch. 4]
and in
[
60
,
59
]
.

Lyapunov criterion:
The existence of a smooth function

V\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that has a Lipschitz gradient
\nabla V
,
along with
\varepsilon>0

satisfying
{\overline{f}}(\theta)^{\intercal}\nabla V\,(\theta)\leq-\varepsilon\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|^{2}
for all
\theta
. See
[
28
]
for examples of this approach and further history.

ODE@
\infty
:
Suppose that the following limit exists for each
\theta
:

{\overline{f}}_{\infty}(\theta)\mathbin{:=}\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{-1}{\overline{f}}(r\theta).

(8)

This defines the vector field for the ODE@
\infty
of
[
8
,
7
]
.
The so-called (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
49
]
) “Borkar-Meyn theorem”
states that: If this ODE is globally asymptotically stable,
and the process
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}=\{\Phi_{n}\}
is such that the sequence
\{\Delta_{n}\}

appearing in (
3
) is a martingale difference sequence,
then the sequence
\{\theta_{n}\}
is bounded with probability one.
Relaxations of the assumptions of
[
8
]
are given
in
[
4
,
49
]
. Reference
[
50
]
presents an
extension of
[
8
]
in which
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}=\{\Phi_{n}\}

is parameter dependent. The setting is
adversarial: It is assumed that the ODE@
\infty
is stable under
the worst-case noise sequence.

The advantage of the ODE@
\infty
over Lyapunov techniques is that
{\overline{f}}_{\infty}
is often very simple compared to the vector field
{\overline{f}}
, and its stationary point is always the origin. Consider for example stochastic gradient descent for the Rastrigin loss function
\Gamma
,
whose gradient is given by
[\nabla\Upgamma\,(x)]_{i}=[2x_{i}+b\sin(2\uppi x_{i})]
, with

. In this case
{\overline{f}}=-\nabla\Upgamma

.

Of the many applications of the results in
[
8
]
,
the majority are in the context of RL. However, two essential assumptions
in this prior work appear to be often overlooked and may indeed be
violated, especially in RL applications:

\bullet
The martingale difference sequence assumption
in
[
8
]
holds only in very special cases,

Q-learning;
the martingale difference property
is exploited in
[
58
]
to obtain error bounds for this special case.

\bullet
It is assumed in
[
8
]
that
\Phi
is a Markov chain, which rules out parameter-dependent exploration, such as
\varepsilon

One of the main aims of this paper is to rectify these problems.
We provide much more realistic assumptions on the
“noise” process
\Phi
, which are valid in a broad range of applications
and lead to far stronger conclusions than anticipated in prior research.
Specifically, we consider a
family
of Markov processes

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}^{\theta}
and assume that the evolution of each

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}^{\theta}
is specified by a transition kernel
P^{\theta}

from family

\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

these define a conditional Markov property as
in
[
45

50

\Phi_{n+1}=g(\Phi_{n},\theta_{n},W_{n+1}),

(9)

in which

e.g., in analysis of Q-learning, of off-policy TD-learning with
parameter-dependent exploration,
and of actor-critic methods
[
57

37
]

It is assumed that each transition kernel

satisfies (DV3)—a slightly weaker version of the well-known Donsker-Varadhan

14
]
—which is

[

32

to establish exact large deviations asymptotics for the partial sums of functions of a Markov chain; see Section

Background and Assumptions

This section contains some of the main assumptions that will
remain in effect for most of our result, and an outline
of important background material.

2.1
SA model

The stochastic process
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}=\{\Phi_{n}\}
evolves on a Polish state
space
{\sf X}
, equipped with its Borel sigma-algebra
{{\cal B}({\sf X})}
. Its dynamics
are defined by a family of transition kernels
\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
.
In the special case (
9
) we define
P_{\theta}(x,A)\mathbin{:=}{\sf P}\{g(x,\theta,W_{n+1})\in A\}
, for each
n\geq 0
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\ x\in{\sf X}\,,\ A\in{{\cal B}({\sf X})}
.
This is consistent with the general model in which,

{\sf P}\{\Phi_{n+1}\in A\mid{\cal F}_{n};\theta_{n}=\theta\,,\ \Phi_{n}=x\}=P_{\theta}(x,A)\,,\quad\text{with}\ {\cal F}_{n}=\sigma(\theta_{k}\,,\ \Phi_{k}:k\leq n).

(14)

While
\Phi
is not necessarily Markovian, analysis is based on
consideration of the Markov chain
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}^{\theta}
with transition kernel

P_{\theta}
, for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
fixed. For the
realization (
9
), this evolves according to
the recursion,

\Phi_{n+1}^{\theta}\mathbin{:=}g(\Phi_{n}^{\theta},\theta,W_{n+1})
,
n\geq 0
.

It is assumed that each
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}^{\theta}
is geometrically ergodic with unique probability invariant measure
\pi_{\theta}
. The mean flow vector field is then defined as the expectation,

{\overline{f}}(\theta)=\int f(\theta,x)\,\pi_{\theta}(dx).

(15)

For any
{{\cal B}({\sf X})}
-measurable function
g\colon{\sf X}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

and any measure
\mu
on
({\sf X},{\cal B}({\sf X}))
,
we use the compact notation
\mu(g)\mathbin{:=}\int g(x)\,\mu(dx)
,
whenever the integral is well defined.

2.2
Markov chains

We next lay out notation for a single Markov chain
\Phi
on
{\sf X}
with transition kernel
P
. As above, it is assumed that
\Phi
is geometrically ergodic with unique probability invariant
measure
\pi
.

A pair
(C,\nu)
are called
small
if
C\in{{\cal B}({\sf X})}
and
\nu
is a
probability measure on
({\sf X},{{\cal B}({\sf X})})
, such that,
for some
\varepsilon>0
and
n_{0}\geq 1
the
minorization condition
holds:

P^{n_{0}}(x,A)\geq\varepsilon\nu(A)\,,\qquad\text{for $A\in{{\cal B}({\sf X})}$ and $x\in C$}.

If the particular
\nu
is not of interest, then we simply say that the set
C
is small.
It is sometimes convenient to replace the set
C
by a function
s\colon{\sf X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then we say that
s
is small if,

P^{n_{0}}(x,A)\geq\varepsilon s(x)\nu(A)\,,\qquad\text{for $A\in{{\cal B}({\sf X})}$ and all $x\in{\sf X}$}.

(16)

In analysis it is often more convenient to work with the
resolvent
R:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n-1}P^{n}
, which satisfies a one-step minorization condition under (
16
):

R(x,A)\geq s_{+}(x)\nu(A)\,,\quad\text{for $A\in{{\cal B}({\sf X})}$ and all $x\in{\sf X}$,}

(17)

where
s_{+}(x)=2^{-n_{0}-1}\varepsilon\int R(x,dy)s(y)
.

The Markov chain is called
aperiodic
if there exists a probability
measure
\nu
and
\varepsilon>0
such that, for each
x\in{\sf X}
and
A\in{{\cal B}({\sf X})}
,
there is
n(x,A)\geq 1
such that:

P^{n}(x,A)\geq\varepsilon\nu(A)\,,\qquad\text{for all $n\geq n(x,A)$.}

For any measurable function

z_{k}\mathbin{:=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}}{\tilde{\theta}}_{k}\,,\qquad Z_{\uptau_{k}}^{(n)}\mathbin{:=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}}\widetilde{\Uptheta}_{\uptau_{k}}^{(n)}\,,\qquad k\geq n\,.

The domain of the latter is extended to all

It is convenient to move the origin of the time axis as follows:

(38)

The two fundamental approximations of interest here are:

Central Limit Theorem
(CLT): For any bounded continuous
function

,

\lim_{k\to\infty}{\sf E}[g(z_{k})]={\sf E}[g(X)],\qquad\mbox{where}\;X\sim N(0,\Sigma_{\text{\tiny$\Theta$}}),

(39)

for an appropriate covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\text{\tiny$\Theta$}}
.

Functional Central Limit Theorem
(FCLT): The sequence
of stochastic processes
\{Z_{\,\cdot\,\mid{\hbox{\rule{3.01389pt}{0.60275pt}}}}^{(n)}:n\geq 1\}
converges in distribution to the solution of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation,

(40)

where

a standard
d

are
d\times d
matrices.

Consider the family of martingale difference sequences,
parametrized by
\theta

\zeta_{n}(\theta)\mathbin{:=}{\hat{f}}(\theta,\Phi_{n}^{\theta})-{\sf E}[{\hat{f}}(\theta,\Phi_{n}^{\theta})\mid{\cal F}_{n-1}],\quad\text{ ${\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}^{\theta}$ is Markovian with tr.\ kernel $P_{\theta}$. }

(41)

The steady-state covariance matrices are denoted:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\zeta}(\theta)

\displaystyle\mathbin{:=}{\sf E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\zeta_{n}(\theta)\zeta_{n}(\theta)^{\intercal}]\,,\quad

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\zeta}^{*}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{\zeta}(\theta^{*}).

(42)

The CLT is obtained under the assumption that

A^{*}
is Hurwitz, where
A^{*}\mathbin{:=}A(\theta^{*})
with

given in (A5a) below.
This assumption combined with
global asymptotic stability of the mean flow
implies that it is globally

for some

This bound is required in consideration of bias, starting with the approximation
{\sf E}[\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}]\approx\phi(\uptau_{k-n};\theta_{n})-\theta^{*}
for large

and all

. For the CLT to hold, the bias must decay faster than

. Assumption (A5b) is introduced to ensure this.

(A5a)

is continuously differentiable in
\theta
, and the Jacobian matrix

is uniformly bounded and uniformly Lipschitz continuous.

(A5b)
The step-size is

with
{\mathchoice{\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{2}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{2}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{3}{1}{2}}{\genfrac{}{}{}{3}{1}{2}}}<\rho\leq 1

(FCLT)
.

We write

3.3

Consider the M/M/1 queue with uniformization, that is,

4
Conclusions

This paper provides simple criteria for convergence and asymptotic statistics
for stochastic approximation and RL. There are of course
many important avenues for future research:

(i)
We are currently investigating the value of resetting as defined
in
[
23
]
. In particular, with resetting, can versions of
Theorems

4
and
5
be obtained under (V4) and appropriate assumptions on
f
?

(ii)
Extensions to two time-scale SA are of interest in many domains.
Such generalizations will be made possible
based on the present results,
combined with ideas from
[
29
,
27
,
61
]
.

(iii)
Is there any hope for finer asymptotic statistics,
such as an Edgeworth expansion or finite-
n
bounds of
Berry-Esséen type?

(iv)
Many practitioners in RL opt for constant step-size algorithms. When
\alpha_{n}\equiv\alpha_{0}
is fixed but sufficiently small, it is possible to obtain the drift condition (
82
) under the assumptions of
Theorem

2
(with (A1) abandoned). In
[
34
]
it is shown that this implies that the fourth moment of
{\tilde{\theta}}_{n}
is uniformly bounded. Open questions here include:

\circ
Can we establish a topological formulation of geometric ergodicity for
\{(\theta_{n},\Phi_{n}):n\geq 0\}
to justify steady-state bias and variance formulae?
Ideas in the prior work
[
25
]
might be combined with recent advances on topological ergodicity for Markov chains—see
[
12
,
48
]
and their references.

\circ
Once we have established some form of ergodicity, does it follow that
\{z_{k}\}
converges in distribution as
n\to\infty
?
Is the limit approximately Gaussian for small
\alpha_{0}
?
Can we obtain convergence of moments as in
Theorem

4
?

The following provides more useful bounds.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose that (
51
) and (

hold.
Then the fundamental kernels admit the uniform bound

{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}{\cal Z}_{\theta}{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}_{G,H}\leq b_{z}\mathbin{:=}b_{u}b_{\nu}+b_{u}[1+b_{u}b_{\nu}]

Proof.

Under the assumptions of the proposition

The minorization condition is expressed
R_{\theta}\geq s\otimes\nu
, which justifies the notation

U_{\theta}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(R_{\theta}-s\otimes\nu)^{n}

It is well known that

Based on this theory we obtain a representation for the fundamental kernel as follows:
First,

The identity

{\cal Z}_{\theta,0}\mathbin{:=}R_{\theta}{\cal Z}_{\theta,r}
solves

We add a rank one operator to obtain:

{\cal Z}_{\theta}\mathbin{:=}\mbox{\rm{\large{1}}}\otimes\pi_{\theta}+{\cal Z}_{\theta,0}=\mbox{\rm{\large{1}}}\otimes\pi_{\theta}+U_{\theta}R_{\theta}[I-\mbox{\rm{\large{1}}}\otimes\pi_{\theta}].

This solves
P{\cal Z}_{\theta}={\cal Z}_{\theta}-[I-\mbox{\rm{\large{1}}}\otimes\pi_{\theta}]

for any

53

The value of

will be obtained as an upper bound on the right-hand side of the inequality,

{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}{\cal Z}_{\theta}{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}_{G,H}\leq{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}\mbox{\rm{\large{1}}}\otimes\pi_{\theta}{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}_{G,H}+{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}U_{\theta}R_{\theta}{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}_{G,H}[1+{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}\mbox{\rm{\large{1}}}\otimes\pi_{\theta}{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}_{G}].

to each side and use

Following standard arguments (e.g.,
[
31

U_{\theta}R_{\theta}G\leq H+bU_{\theta}R_{\theta}s=H+bU_{\theta}R_{\theta}s.

we have
U_{\theta}R_{\theta}=U_{\theta}(R_{\theta}-s\otimes\nu)+[U_{\theta}s]\otimes\nu=U_{\theta}-I+[U_{\theta}s]\otimes\nu

Recalling
\mu_{\theta}(s)=1

U_{\theta}s\equiv 1
gives

{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}U_{\theta}R_{\theta}{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}_{G,H}\leq\sup_{x}\frac{1}{H(x)}[H(x)+bU_{\theta}R_{\theta}\,s(x)]\leq b_{u}\mathbin{:=}1+2b.

Next, consider
{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}\mbox{\rm{\large{1}}}\otimes\pi_{\theta}{\hbox{\hbox to4.55244pt{\hss\hbox{\hbox{$|\!|\!|$}}\hss}}}_{G,H}=\sup_{x}\pi_{\theta}(G)/H(x)\leq\pi_{\theta}(G)

. This and (

completes the proof.
