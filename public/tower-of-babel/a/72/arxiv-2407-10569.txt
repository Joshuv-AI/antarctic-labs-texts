A simple algorithm for checking equivalence of counting functions on free monoids
Petr Kiyashko, Alexey Talambutsa

Abstract
. In this note we propose a new algorithm for checking whether two counting functions on a free monoid M r M_{r} of rank r r are equivalent modulo a bounded function. The previously known algorithm has time complexity O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) for all ranks r > 2 r>2 , but for r = 2 r=2 it was estimated only to be O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) . We apply a new approach based on the explicit basis expansion and summation of weighted rectangles, which allows us to construct a much simpler algorithm with time complexity O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) for any r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 . We work in the multi-tape Turing machine model with nonconstant-time arithmetic operations.

1.
Introduction

Let
A_{r}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{r}\}
be an alphabet consisting of
r
letters, where
r\geq 2
. The free monoid
M_{r}
of rank
r
can be defined as the set of all finite words over
A_{r}
including the empty word
\varepsilon
with the standard operation of word concatenation. The length of a word
w
is denoted by
|w|
. A word
v\in M_{r}
is a
subword
of the word
u\in M_{r}
if there exist
x,y\in M_{r}
such that
u=xvy
, and a product decomposition of this form constitutes
an occurrence
of
v
in
u
. Two occurrences
u=x_{1}vy_{1}
and
u=x_{2}vy_{2}

intersect
if
||x_{1}|-|x_{2}||\leq|v|
.

Algorithms that find occurrences of a given subword (or a set of subwords) in another word are ubiquitous in modern data processing. A number of such algorithms working in linear time have been developed since the 1970s, among which the two most famous are probably the Knuth–Morris–Pratt and Aho–Corasick algorithms (for details see
[
5
]
). In these algorithms the existence of a subword
v
in the word
w
is checked by a precomputed finite automaton, which in fact describes the (prefix) subword structure for the word
v
. At the same time, the study of subwords constitutes the central topic in word combinatorics, an area of theoretical computer science that is closely related to the combinatorial group, semigroup, and ring theory (see
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
).

A notable application of subword search combinatorics in group theory is a construction of Brooks counting quasimorphisms on the free group
\mathbb{F}_{k}
, which enabled the study of many questions on the second bounded cohomology group
H^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{F}_{k},\mathbb{R})
of the free group. This approach was later generalized to many other important classes of groups (see
[
3
,
10
,
6
,
2
]
). A counting quasimorphism on a free group is defined as a symmetrization of the counting function on a free monoid; this function is the following quantitative generalization of the subword occurrence function.

For any fixed word
v\in M_{r}
we define the
elementary counting function

\rho_{v}:M_{r}\to\mathbb{Z}
. The value
\rho_{v}(w)
for a word
w\in M_{r}
equals the number of all (possibly intersecting) occurrences of the subword
v
in the word
w
.
A
counting function
on
M_{r}
is a finite linear combination of the form

(1)

f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}.

Here, all words
w_{i}
are elements of a fixed monoid
M_{r}
and the coefficients
\alpha_{i}
are usually taken from the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
or the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}
. We see that in the first case the counting functions on
M_{r}
form a linear space, which we denote by
{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
, and in the second case the counting functions form a
\mathbb{Z}
-module inside
{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.

Two counting functions
f,g\in{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
are considered equivalent if their difference
f-g
is a bounded function on
M_{r}
, i.e., there exists some constant
c>0
such that for any word
w\in M_{r}
it holds that

|f(w)-g(w)|\leq c.

We will use the symbol
\doteq
to denote this equivalence relation
1
1

1

Notably, as was shown in
[
8
, Corollary A.5]
, the equivalence
f\doteq g
takes place if and only if the counting functions
f
and
g
coincide for counting in the cyclic words, i.e., when the occurrences of the words
w_{i}
in the sum (
1
) are taken from a cyclic word
w
in the alphabet
A_{r}
.
. In addition, we adopt the standard notation
\ell^{\infty}(M_{r})
for the space of bounded functions on the monoid
M_{r}
.

Clearly, the equivalence classes in
\mathcal{C}_{r}
form a quotient linear space

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}=\mathcal{C}_{r}/(\ell^{\infty}(M_{r})\cap\mathcal{C}_{r}).

The combinatorial study of this space in
[
8
]
enabled to construct a polynomial-time algorithm for checking whether a given counting function is bounded, which was then extended to an algorithm for fast summation of counting quasimorphisms in
H^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{F}_{k},\mathbb{R})
. For integer coefficients and free group
\mathbb{F}_{k}
with
k\geq 2
or free monoids
M_{r}
with
r\geq 3
these algorithms were shown in
[
9
]
to have complexity
O(n)
. For rational coefficients in the same cases, one has complexity
O(n\log n)
. The main goal of this paper is to show that these complexities can also be achieved in the case of
r=2
. We do this by proposing a new algorithm, which uses a new approach to studying the space
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
from
[
11
]
.

To specify the input of the algorithm in the case of integer coefficients we present an element of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
in the input as a formal sum (
1
), where the coefficients
\alpha_{i}
are written in binary form, the words
w_{i}
are written in the alphabet
A_{r}
and the summands are written as
pairs of a coefficient and a word. In the case of rational coefficients, we assume that the coefficients are written as fractions, where the numerator and denominator are written in binary form. (The formalization of the input in the integer and rational cases can be found in
[
9
]
.) This specification defines the size
\|f\|
of the counting function
f
provided as input.

We emphasize that the words
w_{i}
in different summands may not be distinct: the transformation of a formal
sum into an equivalent one by combining coefficients at summands with equal words is not trivial.

Now we state the main result obtained in this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists an algorithm that takes as input two counting functions
f
and
g
represented by linear combinations of elementary counting functions over the monoid
M_{r}
and checks whether they are equivalent.
Furthermore, this algorithm has time complexity

O(r^{3}n)
for integer coefficients and
O(r^{3}n\log(rn))
for rational coefficients, where
n=\|f\|+\|g\|
is the input size.

The previous algorithm for solving this problem, which was constructed in
[
9
]
, had two disadvantages: the description of the algorithm was quite complicated, and its complexity was proven to be linear only for
r\geq 3
. Even though the same algorithm works in the case of
r=2
, the complexity was only estimated as
O(n^{2})
for integer coefficients and
O(n^{2}\log n)
for rational coefficients. This discrepancy has the following origin. For a counting function of size
n
the algorithm makes
O(n)
major steps, each of which has complexity
O(d)
, where
d
is the current size of the stored data. Initially
d=n
, and after each major step a particular data compression procedure with compression ratio
p(r)=\max(\frac{2r}{r+2},\frac{2r^{2}}{3r-2},\frac{r^{2}}{2r-1})
is executed. Since
p(r)\geq\frac{9}{7}
for all
r\geq 3
, in these cases the data size exponential decreasing along major steps, resulting in total complexity
O(n)
. However, if
r=2
, then
p(2)=1
, and in this case the data may potentially retain the same size along all the steps, which leads only to the estimation
O(n^{2})
(and
O(n^{2}\log n)
respectively).

Instead of constructing an algorithm for Theorem
1.1
directly, we will be looking at the algorithms that check whether a function is bounded.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists an algorithm that takes as input a counting function
f
represented by a linear combination of elementary counting functions over the monoid
M_{r}
and checks whether
f
is bounded.
Furthermore, this algorithm has time complexity

O(r^{3}\|f\|)
for integer coefficients and time complexity
O(r^{3}\|f\|\log(r\|f\|))
for rational coefficients, where
\|f\|
is the input size.

The existence of an algorithm satisfying Theorem
1.1
easily follows from Theorem
1.2
.

Besides the described algebraic motivation, there is another topic closely related to counting functions on monoids. Recently, a number of results were obtained in
[
1
,
4
]
on the boundedness problem for weighted automata (WA) and cost-register automata (CRA). These two classes of automata generalize probabilistic automata and carry additional computational information on transitions so that for any word
w
from the automata alphabet
\Sigma
a number
A(w)\geq 0
can be computed. Given an automaton as input, the
absolute boundedness problem
asks if there exists a constant
c>0
such that for any
w\in\Sigma^{*}
one has
|A(w)|\leq c
. The question whether a monoid counting function is bounded can be directly reduced to the absolute boundedness decision problem for linearly ambiguous WA and for the restricted model of copyless CRA. It was proven that the absolute boundedness problem is PSPACE-complete for polynomially ambiguous WA with integer coefficients (see
[
1
, Theorem 6.22]
), and for copyless CRA it was proved to be decidable in polynomial time for non-negative coefficients
[
4
, Theorem 3.3]
. However, restricting the coefficients to be non-negative does not allow a direct emulation of the monoid counting function anymore, since any counting function
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
with non-negative coefficients
\alpha_{0},\ldots,a_{k}
is clearly unbounded unless all these coefficients are zeros.

2.
Preliminary results

It is not hard to see that for any fixed word
w\in M_{r}
the functions

(2)

\displaystyle l_{w}=\rho_{w}-\sum_{s\in A_{r}}\rho_{sw},

\displaystyle r_{w}=\rho_{w}-\sum_{s\in A_{r}}\rho_{ws}

are bounded, as for any argument word
x\in M_{r}
one has
|l_{w}(x)|\leq 1
and
|r_{w}(x)|\leq 1
. Indeed, any occurrence of the word
w
in the word
x
besides the prefix can be uniquely extended to an occurrence of the word
sw
by the adjacent letter
s\in A_{r}
. Symmetrically, any occurrence of the word
w
in the word
x
besides the suffix is in one-to-one correspondence with its continuation
ws
by some letter
s
. Since the functions
l_{w}
and
r_{w}
are bounded, they vanish in the quotient space
\mathcal{\widehat{C}}_{r}
.
This explains why
l_{w}
and
r_{w}
are called
the left and right extension relation functions
respectively.

A key structural insight, established in the work
[
8
]
, shows that the functions (
2
) can be taken as the defining relations of the factor space over the bounded functions:

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 1.3 in
[
8
]
, Theorem 1.4 in
[
11
]
)
.

The subspace of bounded functions in
\mathcal{C}_{r}
(i. e. ones equivalent to
0
in the quotient space
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
) is spanned by the left and right extension relation functions
l_{w}
and
r_{w}
taken for all words
w\in M_{r}
.

From this point on, for brevity we will also use counting functions to denote the equivalence classes they represent in
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
. We will also say that
applying the left (or right) extension relation
to an elementary counting function
\rho_{w}
with
w=a_{i}v
or
w=va_{j}
means substituting
\rho_{w}
with the linear combination

\rho_{v}-\sum_{s\in S\setminus\{a_{i}\}}\rho_{sv}\ \ \ \text{ (left extension)}

and

\rho_{v}-\sum_{s\in S\setminus\{a_{j}\}}\rho_{vs}\ \ \ \text{ (right extension)}

respectively. This operation applied to a general counting function does not change its equivalence class because all extension relation functions are bounded. We will use this operation to rewrite the formal sums of type (
1
) and obtain their basis representations.

The bases of the space
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
that consist solely of elementary counting functions can be parametrized using spanning trees of de Bruijn graphs (see
[
8
, Proposition 3.1]
), and the easiest such basis is described using the words satisfying the following simple condition:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
W_{r}
be the set of all words from
M_{r}
, including the empty word
\varepsilon
, that do not start or end with the letter
a_{1}
.

Theorem 2.3

(Theorem 1.5 in
[
8
]
, Theorem 1.5 in
[
11
]
)
.

A basis of the space
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
is represented by the set

B_{r}=\{\rho_{w}\ \mid\ w\in W_{r}\}.

The basis
B_{r}
was used in
[
11
]
to find explicit formulas to decompose all elementary counting functions from
\mathcal{C}_{r}
in the basis
B_{r}
. These formulas allow us to describe a natural and straightforward algorithm to check if the input function
f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
is bounded: we represent all elementary counting functions from the input by linear combinations of the basis elements using the explicit formulas from
[
11
]
, and then combine the coefficients to see whether the result is a trivial combination (see formal details in
[
9
, Lemma 4.2]
). However, the application of this strategy with the basis
B_{r}
to the input of size
n
can produce a sum of counting functions that has length
\Theta(n^{3})
, thus the time complexity of such an algorithm will be bounded below as
\Omega(n^{3})
. The main technical tool of this paper is a new basis
S_{r}
, which reduces the length of the produced sums to
O(n)
and allows us to reach the desired linear time complexity.

The following result describes the time complexity of an auxiliary formal procedure that sums up the coefficients in a general counting function with repetitions and serves as a tool to combine similar terms in the expressions of the form
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
, where the functions
\rho_{w_{i}}
may coincide.

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 4.2 in
[
9
]
)
.

There exists a procedure
N
that takes a formal sum
f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
as input and produces a formal sum obtained from
f
by reducing the coefficients at summands with identical elementary counting functions. Furthermore, the time complexity of
N

is
O(rn)
for integer coefficients and
O(rn\log(rn))
for rational coefficients, where
n
is the input size.

Suppose that the function
f
is represented as a formal sum
f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
, where the elementary counting functions
\rho_{w_{i}}
belong to some basis
T
of the space
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
, but may repeat. Applying the procedure
N
to this sum produces a coordinate decomposition of
f
in
T
, thus checking whether the function
f
is bounded amounts to verifying whether the obtained decomposition is zero. The idea of the proposed algorithm is to obtain such a representation of the input function
f
, and then apply the procedure
N
to it.

3.
Algorithm for
M_{2}

In this section, we describe an algorithm to check boundedness of a counting function
for the case of monoid
M_{2}
, and in the next section we outline its extension to the general case.

3.1.
Basis decomposition

First, for any elementary counting function
\rho_{w}
we will find its explicit decomposition
D(\rho_{w})
in the basis
B_{2}
, which holds up to equivalence in
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
. The decomposition formulas will be used extensively in our further considerations.

First, we consider the general case of the word
w
having form
w=a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{m}
, where
v
does not start or end with the letter
a_{1}
. Then we find formulas for the remaining words of the form
w=a_{1}^{k}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any word
w=a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{m}\in M_{2}
with
v\in W_{2}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}

it holds that
\rho_{w}\doteq D(\rho_{w})
, where

(3)

D(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}v}-\sum_{j=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{va_{1}^{j}a_{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1,\\
j=0\,\ldots\,m-1\end{subarray}}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{j}a_{2}}\quad\text{for }k,m>0

(4)

D(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{va_{1}^{i}a_{2}}\quad\text{for }k=0\text{ and }m>0,

(5)

D(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}v}\quad\text{for }k>0\text{ and }m=0,

and
D(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}=\rho_{w}
for
k=m=0
.

Proof.

To establish the equality
\rho_{w}\doteq D(\rho_{w})
in the case (
3
)
we first iteratively apply the left extension relation

k
times to the function
\rho_{a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{m}}
and obtain

(6)

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{va_{1}^{m}}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{m}}.

Then, applying the right extension relation
m
times to the function
\rho_{va_{1}^{m}}
,
we get

\rho_{va_{1}^{m}}\doteq\rho_{v}-\sum_{j=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{va_{1}^{j}a_{2}}.

Finally, applying the right extension relation
i
times to every element of the sum in (
6
),
we obtain

\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{m}}\doteq\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\Bigl(\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}v}-\sum_{j=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{j}a_{2}}\Bigr)\doteq\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1\\
j=0\,\ldots\,m-1\end{subarray}}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{j}a_{2}},

which yields the desired representation.

The same proof can be carried out in the cases (
4
) and (
5
) where
k
or
m
is equal to
0
.
First, note that (
6
) from the proof of the Lemma is exactly equivalent
to (
5
) when
m=0
. Then, note that if instead of initially applying
the left extension relation
k
times to
\rho_{w}
we applied the right extension relation

m
times, we would have obtained

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{a_{1}^{k}v}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{i}a_{2}},

which is in turn equivalent to (
4
) when
k=0
.

The case of
k=m=0
where the decomposition
D
is defined as an identity is obvious.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For every word
w=a_{1}^{k}\in M_{2}
it holds that
\rho_{w}\doteq D(\rho_{w})
, where

(7)

D(\rho_{w})=\rho_{\varepsilon}-k\rho_{a_{2}}+\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-2}(k-i-1)\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}a_{2}}.

Proof.

Iteratively applying the left extension relation
k
times to the function
\rho_{a_{1}^{k}}
, we get

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{\varepsilon}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}}.

Then, applying the right extension relation
i
times to each function
\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}}
,
we obtain

\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}}\doteq\rho_{a_{2}}-\sum_{j=0\,\ldots\,i-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{j}a_{2}}.

Finally, summing all of these representations together yields

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{\varepsilon}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}(\rho_{a_{2}}-\sum_{j=0\,\ldots\,i-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{j}a_{2}})\doteq\rho_{\varepsilon}-k\rho_{a_{2}}+\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-2}(k-i-1)\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}a_{2}}.\qed

It is easy to see that for any
w\in M_{2}
the corresponding decomposition
D(\rho_{w})
from Lemma
3.1
or Lemma
3.2
is a formal sum of elements in the basis
B_{2}
. Hence, the direct application of
D
to the elementary counting functions in each summand of a formal sum representation of the input function allows us to obtain the desired basis representation.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
f
be a counting function in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
represented by a linear combination

f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
. Then
f
can be represented as the following combination
of elements of
B_{2}
:

(8)

f\doteq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}D(\rho_{w_{i}}).

3.2.
A naive
O(n^{3})
algorithm

The decomposition obtained above allows us to construct a naive algorithm that checks whether
f
is bounded. To do this, we use (
8
) and replace each term
\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
of
f
by the summands of
D(\rho_{w_{i}})
, each multiplied by
\alpha_{i}
. Then, we apply the procedure
N
from Lemma
2.4
to the obtained formal sum. Since all the summands in the new representation contain elementary counting functions from
B_{2}
, the function
f
is bounded if and only if the procedure
N
yields a trivial linear combination.

Remark 3.4
.

The time complexity of the naive algorithm is at least cubic, because the size of
D(\rho_{w})

can be as large as
|w|^{3}
. For example, consider
w=a_{1}^{k}a_{2}a_{1}^{k}
. Then, due to (
3
)
it holds that

\|D(\rho_{w})\|\geq\Bigl\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1\\
j=0\,\ldots\,k-1\end{subarray}}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}a_{2}a_{1}^{j}a_{2}}\Bigr\|.

We see that, each summand with
i,j\geq k/2
has size
\Theta(k)
, and there are
\Theta(k^{2})
such summands, from this one obtains that the total size of
D(\rho_{w})
is
\Theta(k^{3})=\Theta(|w|^{3})
. Therefore, the size of the basis representation is
\Omega(\|f\|^{3})
, and the running time of the procedure
N
can only be estimated as
O(\|f\|^{3})
for integers or
O(\|f\|^{3}\log\|f\|)
for rationals.

3.3.
Shorthand basis

As we saw in Remark
3.4
, the decomposition of an elementary counting function in the basis
B_{2}
may significantly increase the data size, which in turn causes a summation of too many coefficients. To overcome this problem, we will construct another basis
S_{2}
which allows us to make short decompositions of the counting functions in the space
\mathcal{\widehat{C}}_{2}
.
Clearly, the counting functions which are the sums from (
3
) allow to compress the decomposition
D
, but the key point is that some of these sums can also constitute the most significant part of the basis
S_{2}
.

Let us introduce the notation for the functions representing the sums from (
3
):

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}:W_{2}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}\to\mathbb{Z},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(v)=\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}v},

\displaystyle\sigma^{m}:W_{2}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}\to\mathbb{Z},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma^{m}(v)=\sum_{j=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{va_{1}^{j}a_{2}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{m}:W_{2}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}\to\mathbb{Z},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{m}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1,\\
j=0\,\ldots\,m-1\end{subarray}}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{j}a_{2}}.

In this notation, the first case of Lemma
3.1
can be reformulated as

(9)

\rho_{a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{m}}\doteq\rho_{v}-\sigma_{k}(v)-\sigma^{m}(v)+\sigma_{k}^{m}(v).

If we extend the definition of these notations to

\sigma_{0}(v)=\sigma^{0}(v)=\sigma_{0}^{m}(v)=\sigma_{k}^{0}(v)=0,

then the equation (
9
) also holds for
k
or
m
being equal to
0
.

Now, with the use of the counting functions
\sigma_{k}^{m}(v)
we are ready to describe the basis
S_{2}
, which will be used for the shorthand representations of any input counting function.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let

\Sigma_{2}=\{\sigma_{k}^{m}(v)\mid v\in W_{2}\setminus\{\varepsilon\},k,m>0\}

and let

\Lambda_{2}=\{\rho_{a_{1}^{k}}\mid k\geq 0\}.

Then,
S_{2}=\Sigma_{2}\cup\Lambda_{2}
is a basis of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
. Furthermore,
for an input function
f
there exists a representation of
f
in basis
S_{2}

with size
O(\|f\|)
that can be obtained in time
O(\|f\|)
.

The proof of the Proposition
3.5
is the main challenge of this paper. It is split into several lemmas and constitutes the Subsection
3.4
. Once such a representation is obtained, one can apply the procedure
N
to it to check whether the input function is bounded, and due to the size of the representation being
O(\|f\|)
the running time of
N
will be
O(\|f\|)
for integer coefficients and
O(\|f\|\log\|f\|)
for rational coefficients, which satisfies
Theorem
1.2
.

3.4.
Basis proof

Now, we prove that
S_{2}
is a basis for

Lemma 3.14
.

The elementary function

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{a_{1}^{k}}-2\rho_{a_{1}^{k+1}}+\rho_{a_{1}^{k+2}}

If

\rho_{w}=\rho_{a_{1}^{n}}-2\rho_{a_{1}^{n+1}}+\rho_{a_{1}^{n+2}}-\sigma_{k}^{n+1}(a_{2})+\sigma_{k}^{n}(a_{2})-\sigma_{n+1}^{m}(a_{2})+\sigma_{n}^{m}(a_{2})+\sigma_{k}^{m}(a_{2}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}).

7.

Otherwise,
w
can be written in the form
w=a_{1}^{k}a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2}a_{1}^{m}
with

and

, and

\rho_{w}=\sigma_{k^{\prime}+1}^{m^{\prime}+1}(v)-\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}+1}(v)-\sigma_{k^{\prime}+1}^{m^{\prime}}(v)+\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(v)-

-\sigma_{k}^{m^{\prime}+1}(a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}v)+\sigma_{k}^{m^{\prime}}(a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}v)-\sigma_{k^{\prime}+1}^{m}(va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2})+\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{m}(va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2})+

+\sigma_{k}^{m}(a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2}).

Proof.

Case 1 trivially follows from the definition of
\Lambda_{2}
.
Case 2 is proven by applying the left extension relation to
\rho_{a_{2}}
.

Case 3 is proven by applying the left extension relation to
\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{k}}
and the right extension

\rho_{a_{1}^{k}a_{2}}
respectively.

Case 4 is proven by applying the right extension relation to
\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{k}a_{2}}

to the two summands as follows:

\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{k}a_{2}}\doteq\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{k}}-\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{k+1}}\doteq(\rho_{a_{1}^{k}}-\rho_{a_{1}^{k+1}})-(\rho_{a_{1}^{k+1}}-\rho_{a_{1}^{k+2}})=\rho_{a_{1}^{k}}-2\rho_{a_{1}^{k+1}}+\rho_{a_{1}^{k+2}}.

Now, Cases 5-7 are the three different cases of how the representation (

Case 5 is the simpler of the three. In this case the equivalence (

\rho_{a_{1}^{k}a_{2}a_{1}^{m}}\doteq\rho_{a_{2}}-\sigma_{k}(a_{2})-\sigma^{m}(a_{2})+\sigma_{k}^{m}(a_{2})=\rho_{a_{2}}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}a_{2}}-\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{a_{2}a_{1}^{i}a_{2}}+\sigma_{k}^{m}(a_{2}).

(10)

\sigma_{k}(a_{2}a_{1}^{n}a_{2})=\sigma_{k}(v^{\prime}a_{1}^{n}a_{2})=

\sigma_{k}(a_{2}a_{1}^{n}a_{2})

\sigma^{m}(a_{2}a_{1}^{n}a_{2})=\sigma^{m}(a_{2}a_{1}^{n}v^{\prime\prime})=

3.5.
The main algorithm

We have identified a basis that allows us to obtain a shorthand representation of
any input function
f
, so now we can describe an algorithm that satisfies Theorem
1.2
.

Let
f
be represented by a linear combination
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\rho_{w_{i}}
, where each summand is encoded as a word-coefficient pair.

We encode each summand consisting of a coefficient
\alpha
and an elementary counting function

\rho_{w}
as a pair
(\alpha,w)
, and we encode the function
\sigma_{k}^{m}(v)
with a coefficient
\alpha
as a compressed tuple
\langle\alpha,k,m,v\rangle
. Since we have proven that
S_{2}
is a basis, we can use a mixed list of pairs
(\alpha,a_{1}^{n})
and tuples as input to the procedure
N
, where tuples and pairs can be processed separately, as the functions they represent are located in disjoint subspaces. Furthermore, this compressed tuple form is what allows us to avoid the size increase from double summations – the size of the tuple
\langle\alpha,k,m,v\rangle
is proportional to just
\|\alpha\|+k+m+|v|
.

We replace each pair
(\alpha_{i},w_{i})
from the input function by other pairs and tuples in one of the following ways based on the word
w_{i}
, mirroring
the cases from Lemma
3.14
:

1.

If
w_{i}=\varepsilon
or
w_{i}=a_{1}^{k}
, we leave the pair as is.

2.

Else if
w_{i}=a_{2}
, we replace by
(\alpha_{i},\varepsilon)
,
(-\alpha_{i},a_{1})
.

3.

Else if
w_{i}=a_{2}a_{1}^{k}
or
w_{i}=a_{1}^{k}a_{2}
with
k>0
,
we replace by
(\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{k})
,
(-\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{k+1})
.

4.

Else if
w_{i}=a_{2}a_{1}^{k}a_{2}
with
k\geq 0
,
we replace by
(\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{k})
,
(-2\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{k+1})
,
(\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{k+2})
.

5.

Else if
w_{i}=a_{1}^{k}a_{2}a_{1}^{m}
with
k,m>0
,
we replace by

(-\alpha_{i},\varepsilon)
,
(\alpha_{i},a_{1})
,
(\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{k})
,
(\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{m})
,

(-\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{k+1})
,
(-\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{m+1})
,

\langle\alpha_{i},k,m,a_{2}\rangle
.

6.

Else if
w_{i}=a_{1}^{k}a_{2}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}a_{1}^{m}
with
k,m,n\geq 0
,
we replace by

(\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{n})
,
(-2\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{n+1})
,
(\alpha_{i},a_{1}^{n+2})
,

\langle-\alpha_{i},k,n+1,a_{2}\rangle
,
\langle\alpha_{i},k,n,a_{2}\rangle
,

\langle-\alpha_{i},n+1,m,a_{2}\rangle
,
\langle\alpha_{i},n,m,a_{2}\rangle
,

\langle\alpha_{i},k,m,a_{2}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}\rangle
.

7.

Otherwise,
w_{i}=a_{1}^{k}a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2}a_{1}^{m}

with
k,m,k^{\prime},m^{\prime}\geq 0
and
v\in W_{2}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
,
and we replace by

\langle\alpha_{i},k^{\prime}+1,m^{\prime}+1,v\rangle
,

\langle-\alpha_{i},k^{\prime},m^{\prime}+1,v\rangle
,

\langle-\alpha_{i},k^{\prime}+1,m^{\prime},v\rangle
,

\langle\alpha_{i},k^{\prime},m^{\prime},v\rangle
,

\langle-\alpha_{i},k,m^{\prime}+1,a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}v\rangle
,
\langle\alpha_{i},k,m^{\prime},a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}v\rangle
,

\langle-\alpha_{i},k^{\prime}+1,m,va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2}\rangle
,
\langle\alpha_{i},k^{\prime},m,va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2}\rangle
,

\langle\alpha_{i},k,m,a_{2}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{2}\rangle
.

Finally, we apply the procedure
N
to the obtained list. If the result is a trivial sum, then we conclude that
f
is bounded; otherwise, we conclude that it is not.
Scanning the input and matching each word
w_{i}
to one of the 7 cases above clearly takes
O(\|f\|)
time.
Now note that the size of each new pair or tuple is proportional to the size of the pair that has been substituted, and each pair is substituted by no more than
9
new elements (each of which can be a tuple or a pair).
Thus, the size of the input to the invocation of
N
is
O(\|f\|)
. Hence, according to Lemma
2.4
, the running time of the procedure
N
is
O(\|f\|)
for integer coefficients and
O(\|f\|\log\|f\|)
for rational coefficients, thereby establishing Theorem
1.2
for the case
M_{2}
.

4.
Generalization for
M_{r}
,
r\geq 2

For the general case of the monoid
M_{r}
with an arbitrary
r\geq 2
, a similar method is also applicable.
Here we omit detailed proofs, since the core ideas in the general case are identical while the equations and exact formulations are more cumbersome.

First, let us extend the basis decomposition from Lemma
3.1
to the general case.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any word
w=a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{m}\in M_{r}
with
v\in W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}

it holds that
\rho_{w}\doteq D_{r}(\rho_{w})
, where

(13)

D_{r}(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1,\\
x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}\end{subarray}}\rho_{xa_{1}^{i}v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\,\ldots\,m-1,\\
x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}\end{subarray}}\rho_{va_{1}^{j}x}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1,\\
j=0\,\ldots\,m-1,\\
x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}\end{subarray}}\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{j}x_{2}}\quad\text{for }k,m>0

(14)

D_{r}(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,m-1,\\
x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}\end{subarray}}\rho_{va_{1}^{i}x}\quad\text{for }k=0\text{ and }m>0,

(15)

D_{r}(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1,\\
x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}\end{subarray}}\rho_{xa_{1}^{i}v}\quad\text{for }k>0\text{ and }m=0,

and
D_{r}(\rho_{w})=\rho_{v}=\rho_{w}
for
k=m=0
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Note that this representation is exactly the same as the one for the case of
M_{2}
, but wherever we had the letter
a_{2}
delimiting the word, in the general case one has a summation over all the letters of

A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}
. As in the case of
M_{2}
, we also extend the definition of
D_{r}
to all
the other words in
M_{r}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every word
w=a_{1}^{k}\in M_{r}
it holds that
\rho_{w}\doteq D_{r}(\rho_{w})
, where

(16)

D_{r}(\rho_{w})=\rho_{\varepsilon}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}k\rho_{x}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-2,\\
x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}\end{subarray}}(k-i-1)\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{i}x_{2}}.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of Lemma
3.2
.
∎

The decomposition (
16
) allows us to extend the naive algorithm from Section
3.2
to the general case of
M_{r}
. The only difference is in the running time: in the decomposition
D_{r}
each summand of the form
\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{j}x_{2}}
is included
(r-1)^{2}
times, which gives
the estimated size
O(r^{2}n^{3})
. Therefore, the running time of the procedure

N
when applied to this representation can only be estimated as

O(r^{3}n^{3})
for integer coefficients and
O(r^{3}n^{3}\log(rn))
for rational coefficients.

In the following, we will show that a shorthand basis exists in the general case. The notation for the sums from (
13
) is defined quite similarly to Section
3.1
, but now we need to introduce new letter parameters
x
,
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}:(W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\})\times(A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\})\to\mathbb{Z},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(v,x)=\sum_{i=0\,\ldots\,k-1}\rho_{xa_{1}^{i}v},

\displaystyle\sigma^{m}:(W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\})\times(A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\})\to\mathbb{Z},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma^{m}(v,x)=\sum_{j=0\,\ldots\,m-1}\rho_{va_{1}^{j}x},

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{m}:(W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\})\times(A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\})^{2}\to\mathbb{Z},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{m}(v,x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\,\ldots\,k-1,\\
j=0\,\ldots\,m-1\end{subarray}}\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{i}va_{1}^{j}x_{2}}.

Note that in the case of
M_{2}
the set
A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}
degenerates into
\{a_{2}\}
, thus
the letters
x,x_{1}
and
x_{2}
do not appear as parameters for
\sigma_{k},\sigma^{m}
and
\sigma_{k}^{m}

as they can only have the one value.

Now, in this notation the first case of Lemma
4.1
can be reformulated as

\rho_{a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{m}}\doteq\rho_{v}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}\sigma_{k}(v,x)-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}\sigma^{m}(v,x)+\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}\sigma_{k}^{m}(v,x_{1},x_{2}),

where there are only
O(r^{2})
shorthand summands. Now, we will describe the basis of

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
using the aforementioned sums.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let

\Sigma_{r}=\bigl\{\sigma_{k}^{m}(v,x_{1},x_{2})\mid v\in W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\},x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\},k,m>0\bigr\}

and let

\Lambda_{r}=\bigl\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\rho_{x}\mid x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{k}x_{2}}\mid x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\},k\geq 0\bigr\}.

Then,
S_{r}=\Sigma_{r}\cup\Lambda_{r}
is a basis of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
. Furthermore,
for an input function
f
there exists a representation of
f
in basis
S_{r}

having size
O(\|f\|)
that can be obtained in time
O(\|f\|)
.

The general structure of the proof is the same as in the case of
M_{2}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

\Lambda_{r}
is a linearly independent set.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma
3.6
. It follows from Theorem
2.3

that the set

\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}=\bigl\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\rho_{x}\mid x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\rho_{x_{1}vx_{2}}\mid x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\},v\in M_{r}\bigr\}

is a basis of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
, and
\Lambda_{r}\subset\Lambda_{r}^{\prime}
, therefore

\Lambda_{r}
is a linearly independent set.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

\Sigma_{r}
is a linearly independent set.

Proof.

First, note that the functions
\sigma_{k}^{m}(v,x_{1},x_{2})
are linearly independent for different
combinations of
v,x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. The proof of this fact is identical to the proof of Lemma
3.8
,
with the difference being the two additional degrees of freedom in the choice of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

Now, let
\Sigma_{r}^{(v,x_{1},x_{2})}
be the set
\{\sigma_{k}^{m}(v,x_{1},x_{2})\mid k,m>0\}
where

v\in W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}
.
Then, what is left to prove the linear independence of the set
\Sigma_{r}
is to prove the linear
independence of the set
\Sigma_{r}^{(v,x_{1},x_{2})}
for any fixed word
v
and letters
x_{1},x_{2}
.
Fortunately, the proof of Lemma
3.9
is fully applicable here as each of the
sets
\Sigma_{r}^{(v,x_{1},x_{2})}
also has only two degrees of freedom in
k
and
m
, and therefore
is representable by a
histogram
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

The span of
\Lambda_{r}
lies in the span of the set

B_{r}|_{\Lambda_{r}}=\bigl\{\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{k}x_{2}}\mid x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\},k\geq 0\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\rho_{a}\mid a\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\bigr\}\subset B_{r}

and the span of
\Sigma_{r}
lies in the span of the set

B_{r}|_{\Sigma_{r}}=\bigl\{\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{k}va_{1}^{m}x_{2}}\mid v\in W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\},x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\},k,m\geq 0\bigr\}\subset B_{r}.

Proof.

The statement about
\Lambda_{r}
follows immediately from the equation (
16
) and the statement about
\Sigma_{r}
follows immediately from the definition of
\sigma_{k}^{m}(v,x_{1},x_{2})
.
∎

Similarly to the case of
M_{2}
, here the sets
B_{r}|_{\Lambda_{r}}
and
B_{r}|_{\Sigma_{r}}
are also
disjoint subsets of the basis
B_{r}
, and therefore the set
S_{r}=\Lambda_{r}\cup\Sigma_{r}
is also
an independent set.

Finally, we show that the set
S_{r}
spans
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

The elementary function
\rho_{w}
for each word
w\in M_{r}
is representable
as a linear combination of elements of
S_{r}
.
Furthermore,

1.

If
w=\varepsilon
or
w=y
or
w=y_{1}a_{1}^{k}y_{2}
with
k\geq 0
and
y,y_{1},y_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}
, then
\rho_{w}\in\Lambda_{r}
.

2.

If
w=a_{2}
, then
\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{\varepsilon}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{x}
.

3.

If
w=a_{2}a_{1}^{k}y
or
w=ya_{1}^{k}a_{2}
with
k\geq 0
and
y\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}
in both cases, then

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{a_{1}^{k}y}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{xa_{1}^{k}y}\textit{ \, or \, }\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{ya_{1}^{k}}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{ya_{1}^{k}x}\quad\text{respectively.}

4.

If
w=a_{2}a_{1}^{k}a_{2}
with
k\geq 0
, then

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{a_{1}^{k}}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{xa_{1}^{k}}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{a_{1}^{k}x}+\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{x_{1}a_{1}^{k}x_{2}}.

5.

If
w=a_{1}^{k}ya_{1}^{m}
with
k,m>0
and
y\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
, then

\rho_{w}=-\rho_{y}+\rho_{a_{1}^{k}y}+\rho_{ya_{1}^{m}}+\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}\sigma_{k}^{m}(y,x_{1},x_{2}).

6.

If
w=a_{1}^{k}a_{2}a_{1}^{m}
with
k,m>0
, then

\rho_{w}=-\rho_{\varepsilon}+\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{x}+

+\rho_{a_{1}^{k}}+\rho_{a_{1}^{m}}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{a_{1}^{k}x}-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\rho_{xa_{1}^{m}}+\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}\sigma_{k}^{m}(a_{2},x_{1},x_{2}).

7.

If
w=a_{1}^{k}y_{1}a_{1}^{n}y_{2}a_{1}^{m}
with
k,m,n\geq 0
and
y_{1},y_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}
, then

\rho_{w}\doteq\rho_{y_{1}a_{1}^{n}y_{2}}-

-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{k}^{n+1}(y_{1},x,y_{2})+\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{k}^{n}(y_{1},x,y_{2})-

-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{n+1}^{m}(y_{2},y_{1},x)+\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{n}^{m}(y_{2},y_{1},x)+

+\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}\sigma_{k}^{m}(y_{1}a_{1}^{n}y_{2},x_{1},x_{2}),

where
\rho_{y_{1}a_{1}^{n}y_{2}}
is further decomposed by Cases 1, 3 or 4.

8.

Otherwise,
w
can be written in the form
w=a_{1}^{k}y_{1}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}y_{2}a_{1}^{m}
with
k,m,k^{\prime},m^{\prime}\geq 0
,

y_{1},y_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}
, and

v\in W_{r}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
, and

\rho_{w}=\sigma_{k^{\prime}+1}^{m^{\prime}+1}(v,y_{1},y_{2})-\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}+1}(v,y_{1},y_{2})-\sigma_{k^{\prime}+1}^{m^{\prime}}(v,y_{1},y_{2})+\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(v,y_{1},y_{2})-

-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{k}^{m^{\prime}+1}(y_{1}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}v,x,y_{2})+\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{k}^{m^{\prime}}(y_{1}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}v,x,y_{2})-

-\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{k^{\prime}+1}^{m}(va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}y_{2},y_{1},x)+\sum_{x\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{2}\}}\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{m}(va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}y_{2},y_{1},x)+

+\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{r}\setminus\{a_{1}\}}\sigma_{k}^{m}(y_{1}a_{1}^{k^{\prime}}va_{1}^{m^{\prime}}y_{2},x_{1},x_{2}).

All cases of Lemma
4.7
can be proven similarly to the cases in Lemma
3.14
.

We have established that
S_{r}
is indeed a basis of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{r}
, and from Lemma
4.7

it follows that any elementary counting function
\rho_{w}
can be represented in terms of this basis
in a compressed form with the size not exceeding
r^{2}\|\rho_{w}\|
. Therefore, if we use an algorithm
similar to the one described in Section
3.5
with the cases from Lemma
4.7

we can obtain a basis representation of any counting function
f
with the size not exceeding
O(r^{2}\|f\|)
,
and thus we can check the boundedness of
f
in
O(r^{3}\|f\|)
time for integer coefficients and

O(r^{3}\|f\|\log(r\|f\|))
time for rational coefficients by Lemma
2.4
. This concludes
the proof of Theorem
1.2
in the general case.
