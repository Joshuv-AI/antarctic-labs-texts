Presentations for tensor categories
James East

Abstract
We introduce new techniques for working with presentations for a large class of (strict) tensor categories. We then apply the general theory to obtain presentations for partition, Brauer and Temperley-Lieb categories, as well as several categories of (partial) braids, vines and mappings. Keywords : presentations, tensor categories, braided tensor categories, diagram categories, partition categories, Brauer categories, Temperley-Lieb categories, transformations, braids, vines. MSC(2020): 18M05, 18M15, 05C10, 20M05, 20M20, 20M50, 20F36, 16S15.

2
Categories

This section develops a general theory of presentations for a broad class of (strict) tensor categories satisfying natural connectivity assumptions, and possessing certain one-sided units. After fixing notation and gathering some basic ideas in Sections
2.1
and
2.2

2.3
and
2.4

2.19

Before we begin, we note that Higgins considered the relationship between presentations for (fundamental) groupoids and their vertex groups in
[

2.1
Preliminaries

All categories we consider will have object set
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
or
\mathbb{P}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
, and we write
\mathbb{S}
to stand for either of these. Moreover, the collections of morphisms
m\to n
(
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
) will always be a set. Thus, for simplicity throughout, “category” will always mean “small category with object set
\mathbb{S}
(being
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{P}
)”. We will always identify such a category
\mathcal{C}
with its set of morphisms. For
a\in\mathcal{C}
, we write
{\bf d}(a)
and
{\bf r}(a)
for the domain and range of
a
. We compose morphisms left-to-right, and often supress the composition symbol, so that for
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
,
ab=a\circ b
is defined if and only if
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
, in which case
{\bf d}(ab)={\bf d}(a)
and
{\bf r}(ab)={\bf r}(b)
. For
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
we write

\mathcal{C}_{m,n}=\{a\in\mathcal{C}:{\bf d}(a)=m,\ {\bf r}(a)=n\}

for the (possibly empty) set of all morphisms
m\to n
. For
n\in\mathbb{S}
we write
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}_{n,n}
for the endomorphism monoid at
n
.

As in
[
60
, p. 52]
, a congruence on a category
\mathcal{C}
is an equivalence
\xi
on (morphisms of)
\mathcal{C}
that preserves objects and is compatible with composition: i.e., for all
(a,b)\in\xi
and all
c,d\in\mathcal{C}
,

•

{\bf d}(a)={\bf d}(b)
and
{\bf r}(a)={\bf r}(b)
,

•

(ac,bc)\in\xi
and
(da,db)\in\xi
, whenever the stated products are defined.

The quotient category
\mathcal{C}/\xi
consists of all
\xi
-classes under the induced composition. For a set
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
of pairs satisfying
{\bf d}(u)={\bf d}(v)
and
{\bf r}(u)={\bf r}(v)
for all
(u,v)\in\Omega
, we write
\Omega^{\sharp}
for the congruence on
\mathcal{C}
generated by
\Omega
: i.e., the smallest congruence containing
\Omega
.

By a category morphism
\phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
we mean a functor that acts as the identity on objects: i.e., we have
{\bf d}(a\phi)={\bf d}(a)
and
{\bf r}(a\phi)={\bf r}(a)
for all
a\in\mathcal{C}
. The kernel of
\phi
is the congruence

\ker(\phi)=\big\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}:a\phi=b\phi\big\},

and we have
\mathcal{C}/\ker(\phi)\cong\operatorname{im}(\phi)
. A surmorphism is a surjective morphism.

The categories we consider are all strict tensor (a.k.a. monoidal) categories, in the sense of
[
49
]
; see also
[
60
, Chapters VII and XI]
. In fact, since all categories we consider have object set
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{P}
, certain simplifications arise, and we take
[
59
, Section 24]
as our reference.

A (strict) tensor category is a category
\mathcal{C}
(over
\mathbb{S}
) with an extra (totally defined) binary operation
\oplus
satisfying the following properties (writing
\iota_{n}
for the identity at
n\in\mathbb{S}
):

•

{\bf d}(a\oplus b)={\bf d}(a)+{\bf d}(b)
and
{\bf r}(a\oplus b)={\bf r}(a)+{\bf r}(b)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{C}
,

•

a\oplus(b\oplus c)=(a\oplus b)\oplus c
for all
a,b,c\in\mathcal{C}
,

•

a\oplus\iota_{0}=a=\iota_{0}\oplus a
for all
a\in\mathcal{C}
(in the case that
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{N}
),

•

\iota_{m}\oplus\iota_{n}=\iota_{m+n}
for all
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
,

•

(a\circ b)\oplus(c\circ d)=(a\oplus c)\circ(b\oplus d)
for all
a,b,c,d\in\mathcal{C}
with
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
and
{\bf r}(c)={\bf d}(d)
.

The symbol
\otimes
is often used in place of
\oplus
in the literature, but since the object part of the operation is addition on
\mathbb{S}
, we prefer
\oplus
.

The term “strict” refers to the fact that we have equalities in the above axioms; in more general monoidal categories only isomorphism is required. In what follows, we will generally drop the word “strict”, as all tensor categories we consider are strict. The following basic lemma will be used often.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a
,
b
and
c
be elements of a (strict) tensor category over
\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

If
{\bf r}(a)=0
and
{\bf r}(b)={\bf d}(c)
, then
a\oplus(b\circ c)=(a\oplus b)\circ c
.

(ii)

If
{\bf d}(a)=0
and
{\bf r}(b)={\bf d}(c)
, then
a\oplus(b\circ c)=b\circ(a\oplus c)
.

(iii)

If
{\bf d}(c)=0
and
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
, then
(a\circ b)\oplus c=a\circ(b\oplus c)
.

(iv)

If
{\bf r}(c)=0
and
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)
, then
(a\circ b)\oplus c=(a\oplus c)\circ b
.

Proof.

For
(i)
we have
a\oplus(b\circ c)=(a\circ\iota_{0})\oplus(b\circ c)=(a\oplus b)\circ(\iota_{0}\oplus c)=(a\oplus b)\circ c
. The others are similar.
∎

The next result can be deduced from the previous one, or can be similarly proved directly.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
a
and
b
are elements of a (strict) tensor category over
\mathbb{N}
, and if
{\bf r}(a)={\bf d}(b)=0
, then
a\circ b=a\oplus b=b\oplus a
. ∎

A tensor congruence on a tensor category
\mathcal{C}
is a congruence
\xi
(as above) such that for all
(a,b)\in\xi
, and for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
(a\oplus c,b\oplus c),(c\oplus a,c\oplus b)\in\xi
. For a set
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
of pairs satisfying
{\bf d}(u)={\bf d}(v)
and
{\bf r}(u)={\bf r}(v)
for all
(u,v)\in\Omega
, we write
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
for the tensor congruence on
\mathcal{C}
generated by
\Omega
.

Some of the tensor categories we consider have further structure. Namely, the endomorphism monoids
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}_{n,n}
(
n\in\mathbb{S}
) contain natural copies of the symmetric groups
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, which satisfy the so-called PROP axioms, as in
[
59
, Section 24]
. (The term PROP is a contraction of PROducts and Permutations.) Since the PROP structures of our categories will not play an explicit role, we will not give the full details here. But some rough ideas are worth noting. First, for
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
, and for
f\in\mathcal{S}_{m}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{m}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n}
, the permutation
f\oplus g\in\mathcal{S}_{m+n}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{m+n}
acts as expected: as
f
on the “first
m
points” and as
g
(suitably translated) on the “last
n
points”. Second, for each
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
there is a permutation
f_{m,n}\in\mathcal{S}_{m+n}
such that for all
m,n,k,l\in\mathbb{S}
, and for all
a\in\mathcal{C}_{m,n}
and
b\in\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
,
(a\oplus b)\circ f_{n,l}=f_{m,k}\circ(b\oplus a)
. Other categories we consider are PROBS, which have permutations replaced by braids
[
49
,
5
]
.

2.2
Free (tensor) categories and presentations

Let
X
be an alphabet (a set whose elements are called letters). The free monoid over
X
is the set
X^{*}
of all words over
X
under concatenation. The empty word
\iota
is the identity of
X^{*}
. Let
R\subseteq X^{*}\times X^{*}
be a set of pairs of words over
X
. We say a monoid
M
has presentation
\langle X:R\rangle
if
M\cong X^{*}/R^{\sharp}
(where, as above,
R^{\sharp}
is the congruence on
X^{*}
generated by
R
): i.e., if there exists a monoid surmorphism
X^{*}\to M
with kernel
R^{\sharp}
. If
\phi
is such a surmorphism, we say
M
has presentation
\langle X:R\rangle
via
\phi
. Elements of
X
and
R
are called generators and relations, respectively. A relation
(u,v)\in R
is typically displayed as an equation:
u=v
.

For category presentations, digraphs and paths play the role of alphabets and words. Let
\Gamma
be a digraph with vertex set
\mathbb{S}
(being
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{P}
), possibly with multiple/parallel edges, and possibly with loops. We identify
\Gamma
with its edge set, and denote the source and target of an edge
x\in\Gamma
by
{\bf d}(x)
and
{\bf r}(x)
, respectively. The free category over
\Gamma
is the set
\Gamma^{*}
of all paths in
\Gamma
under concatenation (where defined). The empty path at
n\in\mathbb{S}
will be denoted by
\iota_{n}
. Every other path can be thought of as a word of the form
w=x_{1}\cdots x_{k}
, where
k\geq 1
and
x_{i}\in\Gamma
for all
1\leq i\leq k
, and where
{\bf r}(x_{i})={\bf d}(x_{i+1})
for all
1\leq i<k
. For such a word/path, we have
{\bf d}(w)={\bf d}(x_{1})
and
{\bf r}(w)={\bf r}(x_{k})
. Now let
\Omega\subseteq\Gamma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
be a set of pairs of paths, such that
{\bf d}(u)={\bf d}(v)
and
{\bf r}(u)={\bf r}(v)
for all
(u,v)\in\Omega
. We say a category
\mathcal{C}
(over
\mathbb{S}
) has presentation
\langle\Gamma:\Omega\rangle
if
\mathcal{C}\cong\Gamma^{*}/\Omega^{\sharp}
: i.e., if there exists a surmorphism
\Gamma^{*}\to\mathcal{C}
with kernel
\Omega^{\sharp}
. If
\phi
is such a surmorphism, we say
\mathcal{C}
has presentation
\langle\Gamma:\Omega\rangle
via
\phi
.

There is also a natural notion of a (strict) tensor category presentation. Let
\Delta
be a digraph with vertex set
\mathbb{S}
, again identified with its edge set. We will denote the free tensor category over
\Delta
by
\Delta^{\oast}
. It consists of all terms constructed in the following way:

(T1)

All empty paths
\iota_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{S}
) are terms, with
{\bf d}(\iota_{n})={\bf r}(\iota_{n})=n
.

(T2)

All edges
x\in\Delta
are terms, with
{\bf d}(x)
and
{\bf r}(x)
the source and target of
x
, respectively.

(T3)

If
s
and
t
are terms, and if
{\bf r}(s)={\bf d}(t)
, then the formal expression
s\circ t
is a term, with
{\bf d}(s\circ t)={\bf d}(s)
and
{\bf r}(s\circ t)={\bf r}(t)
. If
{\bf d}(s)=m
and
{\bf r}(s)=n
, then
\iota_{m}\circ s=s=s\circ\iota_{n}
.

(T4)

If
s
and
t
are terms, then the formal expression
s\oplus t
is a term, with
{\bf d}(s\oplus t)={\bf d}(s)+{\bf d}(t)
and
{\bf r}(s\oplus t)={\bf r}(s)+{\bf r}(t)
.

Note that
(T1)
–
(T4)
describe the elements of
\Delta^{\oast}
, while
(T3)
and
(T4)
also give the definition of the
\circ
and
\oplus
operations. Now let
\Xi\subseteq\Delta^{\oast}\times\Delta^{\oast}
be a set of pairs of terms, such that
{\bf d}(u)={\bf d}(v)
and
{\bf r}(u)={\bf r}(v)
for all
(u,v)\in\Xi
. We say a tensor category
\mathcal{C}
(over
\mathbb{S}
) has tensor presentation
\langle\Delta:\Xi\rangle
if
\mathcal{C}\cong\Delta^{\oast}/\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
: i.e., if there is a surmorphism
\Delta^{\oast}\to\mathcal{C}
with kernel
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
. If
\phi
is such a surmorphism, we say
\mathcal{C}
has presentation
\langle\Delta:\Xi\rangle
via
\phi
.

An arbitrary term from
\Delta^{\oast}
, as in
(T1)
–
(T4)
, can be quite unwieldy. However, every such term
w
is equal in
\Delta^{\oast}
to one of the form

X_{1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{m}=(x_{1,1}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{1,k_{1}})\circ\cdots\circ(x_{m,1}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{m,k_{m}}),

(2.3)

where each
x_{i,j}
is either an empty path
\iota_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{S}
) or else an edge of
\Delta
, and where the domains and ranges of the sub-terms
X_{q}=x_{q,1}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{q,k_{q}}
are compatible as appropriate. Indeed, this is clear if
w
is itself of the form
(T1)
or
(T2)
as above. Otherwise,
w
is of the form
s\circ t
or
s\oplus t
, as in
(T3)
and
(T4)
, and inductively
s
and
t
are both of the form (
2.3
). It immediately follows that
s\circ t
is of the desired form. To see that
s\oplus t
also is, write
s=X_{1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{m}
and
t=Y_{1}\circ\cdots\circ Y_{n}
, as in (
2.3
). Replacing
s
by
s\circ\iota_{r}\circ\cdots\circ\iota_{r}
(
r={\bf r}(s)
), or
t
by
t\circ\iota_{r}\circ\cdots\circ\iota_{r}
(
r={\bf r}(t)
), if necessary, we may assume that
m=n
. We then have
s\oplus t=(X_{1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{n})\oplus(Y_{1}\circ\cdots\circ Y_{n})=(X_{1}\oplus Y_{1})\circ\cdots\circ(X_{n}\oplus Y_{n})
.

In fact, every term from
\Delta^{\oast}
is equal either to an empty path or to a term of the form

X_{1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{m}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where $m\geq 1$ and each $X_{i}=\iota_{p_{i}}\oplus x_{i}\oplus\iota_{q_{i}}$ for some $p_{i},q_{i}\in\mathbb{N}$ and $x_{i}\in\Delta$.}

(2.4)

(In the above, if
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{P}
, but
p_{i}=0
say, then we interpret
X_{i}=\iota_{0}\oplus x_{i}\oplus\iota_{q_{i}}=x_{i}\oplus\iota_{q_{i}}
, and so on.) Indeed, by (
2.3
), it suffices to show that this is true of every term of the form
{w=y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{k}}
, where each
y_{i}
is either an empty path or else an edge of
\Delta
. We now proceed by induction on
\mu=\mu(w)
, defined to be the number of
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
y_{i}\in\Delta
. For each
i
, write
r_{i}={\bf r}(y_{i})
. If
\mu=0
, then
w=\iota_{r_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\iota_{r_{k}}=\iota_{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}}
. If
\mu\geq 1
, say with
y_{i}\in\Delta
, then with
d={\bf d}(y_{i})
, we have

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=(y_{1}\circ\iota_{r_{1}})\oplus\cdots\oplus(y_{i-1}\circ\iota_{r_{i-1}})\oplus(\iota_{d}\circ y_{i})\oplus(y_{i+1}\circ\iota_{r_{i+1}})\oplus\cdots\oplus(y_{k}\circ\iota_{r_{k}})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{i-1}\oplus\iota_{d}\oplus y_{i+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{k})\circ(\iota_{r_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\iota_{r_{i-1}}\oplus y_{i}\oplus\iota_{r_{i+1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\iota_{r_{k}})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{i-1}\oplus\iota_{d}\oplus y_{i+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{k})\circ(\iota_{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{i-1}}\oplus y_{i}\oplus\iota_{r_{i+1}+\cdots+r_{k}}).

Since
\mu(y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{i-1}\oplus\iota_{d}\oplus y_{i+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{k})=\mu(w)-1
, the inductive assumption completes the proof.

2.3
First main result: category presentations

Our aim in this section is to prove Theorem
2.17
, which shows how to build a presentation for a category
\mathcal{C}
(with object set
\mathbb{S}
, being
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{P}
) out of presentations for the endomorphism monoids
\mathcal{C}_{n}
under certain natural assumptions stated below. In what follows, we write
\overline{\iota}_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
for the identity at
n\in\mathbb{S}
. The reason for this (and other) over-line notation will become clear shortly.

The first assumption concerns the concerns the connectivity of
\mathcal{C}
:

Assumption 1
.

We assume that there is an integer
d\geq 1
such that for all
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
,

\mathcal{C}_{m,n}\not=\varnothing\ \Leftrightarrow\ m\equiv n\ (\operatorname{mod}\ d).

In most of our applications, we will have
d=1
, so that all hom-sets are non-empty; in some cases we have
d=2
; in a small number of exceptional cases, we have a more complicated arrangement (see Section
2.5
). The next assumption asserts the existence of certain one-sided units, and is stated in terms of the integer
d
from Assumption
1
:

Assumption 2
.

We assume that for each
n\in\mathbb{S}
there exist
\overline{\lambda}_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,n+d}
and
\overline{\rho}_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n+d,n}
such that

\overline{\lambda}_{n}\overline{\rho}_{n}=\overline{\iota}_{n}.

For each
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
with
m\leq n
and
m\equiv n\ (\operatorname{mod}\ d)
, we define

\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}=\overline{\lambda}_{m}\overline{\lambda}_{m+d}\cdots\overline{\lambda}_{n-d}\in\mathcal{C}_{m,n}\and\overline{\rho}_{n,m}=\overline{\rho}_{n-d}\cdots\overline{\rho}_{m+d}\overline{\rho}_{m}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,m},

(2.5)

noting that
\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\overline{\rho}_{n,m}=\overline{\iota}_{m}
. (When
m=n
, we interpret these empty products as
\overline{\lambda}_{m,m}=\overline{\rho}_{m,m}=\overline{\iota}_{m}
.) For
m,n
as above, we define maps

\mathcal{R}_{m,n}:\mathcal{C}_{m,n}\to\mathcal{C}_{n}:a\mapsto\overline{\rho}_{n,m}a\and\mathcal{L}_{n,m}:\mathcal{C}_{n,m}\to\mathcal{C}_{n}:a\mapsto a\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}.

(2.6)

For any
a\in\mathcal{C}_{m,n}
and
b\in\mathcal{C}_{n,m}
, we have

a=\overline{\iota}_{m}a=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\overline{\rho}_{n,m}a=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(a)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003ptb=\mathcal{L}_{n,m}(b)\cdot\overline{\rho}_{n,m}.

(2.7)

Our next assumption essentially just fixes notation for presentations for the endomorphism monoids in
\mathcal{C}
.

Assumption 3
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{S}
, we assume that the endomorphism monoid
\mathcal{C}_{n}
has presentation
\langle X_{n}:R_{n}\rangle
via
\phi_{n}:X_{n}^{*}\to\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Further, we assume the alphabets
X_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{S}
) are pairwise disjoint, and denote by
\iota_{n}
the empty word over
X_{n}
.

Now let
\Gamma
be the digraph with vertex set
\mathbb{S}
, and edge set

L\cup R\cup X\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003ptL=\{\lambda_{n}:n\in\mathbb{S}\},\hskip 20.00003ptR=\{\rho_{n}:n\in\mathbb{S}\},\hskip 20.00003ptX=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{S}}X_{n},

(2.8)

with sources and targets given by

{\bf d}(x)={\bf r}(x)={\bf d}(\lambda_{n})={\bf r}(\rho_{n})=n\and{\bf r}(\lambda_{n})={\bf d}(\rho_{n})=n+d\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{S}$ and $x\in X_{n}$.}

(2.9)

As usual, we identify
\Gamma
with its edge set:
\Gamma\equiv L\cup R\cup X
. We have a morphism

\phi:\Gamma^{*}\to\mathcal{C}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{given by}\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda_{n}\phi=\overline{\lambda}_{n},\hskip 20.00003pt\rho_{n}\phi=\overline{\rho}_{n},\hskip 20.00003ptx\phi=x\phi_{n}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $n\in\mathbb{S}$ and $x\in X_{n}$.}

(2.10)

We extend the over-line notation to words/paths over
\Gamma
, writing
\overline{w}=w\phi
for all
w\in\Gamma^{*}
.

For
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
with
m\leq n
and
m\equiv n\ (\operatorname{mod}\ d)
, we define the words/paths

\lambda_{m,n}=\lambda_{m}\lambda_{m+d}\cdots\lambda_{n-d}\in\Gamma^{*}_{m,n}\and\rho_{n,m}=\rho_{n-d}\cdots\rho_{m+d}\rho_{m}\in\Gamma^{*}_{n,m},

where again we interpret
\lambda_{m,m}=\rho_{m,m}=\iota_{m}
. Note that
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n,n+d}
and
\rho_{n}=\rho_{n+d,n}
for all
n
, and that
\lambda_{l,m}\lambda_{m,n}=\lambda_{l,n}
and
\rho_{n,m}\rho_{m,l}=\rho_{n,l}
for appropriate
l,m,n
.

Lemma 2.11
.

The morphism
\phi:\Gamma^{*}\to\mathcal{C}
is surjective.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathcal{C}
, and write
m={\bf d}(a)
and
n={\bf r}(a)
; we assume that
m\leq n
, the other case being symmetrical. By (
2.7
), we have
a=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(a)
. Since
\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(a)\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, and since
\phi_{n}
is surjective (cf. Assumption
3
), we have
\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(a)=\overline{w}
for some
w\in X_{n}^{*}
. Thus,
a=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\overline{w}=(\lambda_{m,n}w)\phi
.
∎

The final assumption constructs an appropriate set of relations, and is again stated in terms of the integer
d
from Assumption
1
:

Assumption 4
.

We assume that
\Omega\subseteq\Gamma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
is a set of relations such that, writing
{\sim}
for the congruence
\Omega^{\sharp}
on
\Gamma^{*}
, the following all hold:

(i)

For every relation
(u,v)\in\Omega
, we have
\overline{u}=\overline{v}
.

(ii)

For all
n\in\mathbb{S}
,
\Omega
contains the relation
\lambda_{n}\rho_{n}=\iota_{n}
, and a relation of the form
\rho_{n}\lambda_{n}=w_{n}
for some word
w_{n}\in X_{n+d}^{*}
.

(iii)

For all
n\in\mathbb{S}
,
R_{n}\subseteq\Omega
.

(iv)

For all
n\in\mathbb{S}
, there are mappings

X_{n}\to X_{n+d}^{*}:x\mapsto x_{+}\and X_{n}\to X_{n+d}^{*}:x\mapsto x^{+},

and
\Omega
contains the relations

x\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n}x_{+}\and\rho_{n}x=x^{+}\rho_{n}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $x\in X_{n}$.}

(v)

For all
n\in\mathbb{S}
, and for all
w\in X_{n+d}^{*}
, we have
\lambda_{n}w\rho_{n}\sim u
for some
u\in X_{n}^{*}
.

Remark 2.12
.

In practice, there could be several choices of the one-sided units

\overline{\lambda}_{n},\overline{\rho}_{n}

from Assumption
2
, leading to different relations of the form
(ii)
and
(iv)
above. In the applications in Sections
3
and
4
, we have made choices that we believe lead to the simplest and most convenient versions of these relations. See also Remarks
3.5
and
3.19
.

For the remainder of Section
2.3
, we assume that Assumptions
1
–
4
all hold, and we continue to write
{\sim}=\Omega^{\sharp}
. Our ultimate goal is to show that
\mathcal{C}
has presentation
\langle\Gamma:\Omega\rangle
via
\phi
; see Theorem
2.17
.

In what follows, we extend the maps from Assumption
4
(iv)
to morphisms

X_{n}^{*}\to X_{n+d}^{*}:w\mapsto w_{+}\and X_{n}^{*}\to X_{n+d}^{*}:w\mapsto w^{+}.

It quickly follows that
w\lambda_{n}\sim\lambda_{n}w_{+}
and
\rho_{n}w\sim w^{+}\rho_{n}
for all
w\in X_{n}^{+}
.

Remark 2.13
.

When working with a specific category
\mathcal{C}
, item
(v)
in Assumption
4
generally involves the most work to verify. One way to do so is to show that for any
n\in\mathbb{S}
and
w\in X_{n+d}^{*}
, we have
w_{n}ww_{n}\sim w_{n}u^{+}w_{n}
or
w_{n}ww_{n}\sim w_{n}u_{+}w_{n}
for some
u\in X_{n}^{*}
; here
w_{n}
is the word from Assumption
4
(ii)
. Indeed, suppose
w_{n}ww_{n}\sim w_{n}u^{+}w_{n}
holds (with the other case being similar). First note that item
(ii)
gives

\lambda_{n}=\iota_{n}\lambda_{n}\sim\lambda_{n}\rho_{n}\lambda_{n}\sim\lambda_{n}w_{n}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and similarly}\hskip 20.00003pt\rho_{n}\sim w_{n}\rho_{n}.

Combining these with
(ii)
,
(iv)
and
w_{n}ww_{n}\sim w_{n}u^{+}w_{n}
, we obtain

\lambda_{n}w\rho_{n}\sim\lambda_{n}w_{n}ww_{n}\rho_{n}\sim\lambda_{n}w_{n}u^{+}w_{n}\rho_{n}\sim\lambda_{n}u^{+}\rho_{n}\sim\lambda_{n}\rho_{n}u\sim\iota_{n}u=u.

We now prove a sequence of lemmas, building up to Lemma
2.16
, which gives a set of normal forms that are crucial in the proof of the main result.

Lemma 2.14
.

If
m,n\in\mathbb{S}
are such that
m\leq n
and
m\equiv n\ (\operatorname{mod}\ d)
, then

(i)

\lambda_{m,n}\rho_{n,m}\sim\iota_{m}
,

(ii)

\rho_{n,m}\lambda_{m,n}\sim w
for some
w\in X_{n}^{*}
.

Proof.

We just prove
(ii)
, by induction on
n
;
(i)
is similar but easier. The claim being clear if
n=m
(take
w=\iota_{n}
), we assume that
n>m
. Then

\displaystyle\rho_{n,m}\lambda_{m,n}=\rho_{n-d}\rho_{n-d,m}\lambda_{m,n-d}\lambda_{n-d}

\displaystyle\sim\rho_{n-d}v\lambda_{n-d}

by induction, for some
v\in X_{n-d}^{*}

\displaystyle\sim\rho_{n-d}\lambda_{n-d}v_{+}

by Assumption
4
(iv)

\displaystyle\sim w_{n-d}v_{+}

by Assumption
4
(ii)
,

so we take
w=w_{n-d}v_{+}
.
∎

For the next proof, given
n\in\mathbb{S}
and
k\in\mathbb{P}
, we define a mapping
X_{n}^{*}\to X_{n+kd}^{*}:w\mapsto w^{+^{k}}
in the obvious way, by iteratively composing all the individual
w\mapsto w^{+}
mappings (
X_{n}^{*}\to X_{n+d}^{*}
,
X_{n+d}^{*}\to X_{n+2d}^{*}
, and so on).

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
w\in\Gamma^{*}
, with
m={\bf d}(\overline{w})
and
n={\bf r}(\overline{w})
.

(i)

If
m\leq n
, then
w\sim\lambda_{m,n}w^{\prime}
for some
w^{\prime}\in X_{n}^{*}
.

(ii)

If
m\geq n
, then
w\sim w^{\prime}\rho_{m,n}
for some
w^{\prime}\in X_{m}^{*}
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
k
, the length of
w
. If
k=0
, then
m=n
and we have
w=\lambda_{m,n}=\rho_{m,n}=\iota_{m}
, and
(i)
and
(ii)
both hold with
w^{\prime}=\iota_{m}
. Now suppose
k\geq 1
, and inductively assume that the lemma holds for words of length less than
k
. Write
w=x_{1}\cdots x_{k}
, where each
x_{i}\in\Gamma
. For simplicity, we write
u=x_{1}\cdots x_{k-1}
and
x=x_{k}
. Note that
{\bf d}(u)=m
and
{\bf r}(x)=n
. We also write
q={\bf r}(u)={\bf d}(x)
. By induction, since
u
has length
k-1
, one of the following holds:

(a)

m\leq q
, and
u\sim\lambda_{m,q}s
for some
s\in X_{q}^{*}
, or

(b)

m>q
, and
u\sim t\rho_{m,q}
for some
t\in X_{m}^{*}
.

Case 1.

First suppose
x\in X
, so that
q=n
.
If
(a)
holds then
w=ux\sim\lambda_{m,n}sx
, with
sx\in X_{n}^{*}
.
If
(b)
holds then, writing
m=n+kd
, we have
w=ux\sim t\rho_{m,n}x\sim tx^{+^{k}}\rho_{m,n}
, by Assumption
4
(iv)
, with
tx^{+^{k}}\in X_{m}^{*}
.

Case 2.

Next suppose
x\in L
, so that
q=n-d
and
x=\lambda_{n-d}
.
If
(a)
holds then from
s\in X_{n-d}^{*}
, it follows from Assumption
4
(iv)
that
w=u\lambda_{n-d}\sim\lambda_{m,n-d}s\lambda_{n-d}\sim\lambda_{m,n-d}\lambda_{n-d}s_{+}=\lambda_{m,n}s_{+}
, with
s_{+}\in X_{n}^{*}
.
If
(b)
holds then, writing
m=n+kd
, and using Assumption
4
(ii)
and
(iv)
, we have
w=u\lambda_{n-d}\sim t\rho_{m,n-d}\lambda_{n-d}=t\rho_{m,n}\rho_{n-d}\lambda_{n-d}\sim t\rho_{m,n}w_{n-d}\sim tw_{n-d}^{+^{k}}\rho_{m,n}
, with
tw_{n-d}^{+^{k}}\in X_{m}^{*}
.

Case 3.

Finally, suppose
x\in R
, so that
q=n+d
and
x=\rho_{n}
.
If
(a)
holds then either

•

m=q
, and
u\sim\lambda_{m,m}s=\iota_{m}s=s
, and so
w=u\rho_{n}\sim s\rho_{n}=s\rho_{n+d,n}=s\rho_{m,n}
, with
s\in X_{m}^{*}
, or

•

m<q
, and by Assumption
4
(v)
,
w=u\rho_{n}\sim\lambda_{m,n+d}s\rho_{n}=\lambda_{m,n}\lambda_{n}s\rho_{n}\sim\lambda_{m,n}u
for some
u\in X_{n}^{*}
.

If
(b)
holds then
w=u\rho_{n}\sim t\rho_{m,n+d}\rho_{n}=t\rho_{m,n}
.
∎

The next lemma strengthens the previous one, and is the main technical result we need. The statement refers to the maps
\mathcal{R}_{m,n}
and
\mathcal{L}_{m,n}
defined in (
2.6
).

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
w\in\Gamma^{*}
, with
m={\bf d}(\overline{w})
and
n={\bf r}(\overline{w})
.

(i)

If
m\leq n
, then
w\sim\lambda_{m,n}w^{\prime}
for some
w^{\prime}\in X_{n}^{*}
with
\overline{w}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{R}_{m,n})
.

(ii)

If
m\geq n
, then
w\sim w^{\prime}\rho_{m,n}
for some
w^{\prime}\in X_{m}^{*}
with
\overline{w}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{L}_{m,n})
.

Proof.

We assume that
m\leq n
, the other case being symmetrical. First note that

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\sim\lambda_{m,n}v

by Lemma
2.15
, for some
v\in X_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle=\iota_{m}\lambda_{m,n}v\sim\lambda_{m,n}\rho_{n,m}\lambda_{m,n}v

by Lemma
2.14
(i)
.

By Lemma
2.14
(ii)
, we have
\rho_{n,m}\lambda_{m,n}\sim u
for some
u\in X_{n}^{*}
. Then with
w^{\prime}=uv
, it follows from the above calculations that
w\sim\lambda_{m,n}w^{\prime}
. We also have
\overline{w}^{\prime}=\overline{uv}=\overline{\rho}_{n,m}\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\overline{v}=\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\overline{v})
.
∎

We now have all we need to prove our first main result:

Theorem 2.17
.

With notation as above, and subject to Assumptions
1
–
4
, the category
\mathcal{C}
has presentation
\langle\Gamma:\Omega\rangle
via
\phi
.

Proof.

We showed in Lemma
2.11
that
\phi
is surjective. It remains to show that
\ker(\phi)=\Omega^{\sharp}
. First note that Assumption
4
(i)
says
\Omega\subseteq\ker(\phi)
; since
\ker(\phi)
is a congruence it follows that
\Omega^{\sharp}\subseteq\ker(\phi)
.

For the reverse containment, suppose
(u,v)\in\ker(\phi)
, meaning that
u,v\in\Gamma^{*}
and
\overline{u}=\overline{v}
. Write
{m={\bf d}(\overline{u})={\bf d}(\overline{v})}
and
n={\bf r}(\overline{u})={\bf r}(\overline{v})
, and assume that
m\leq n
, the other case being symmetrical. By Lemma
2.16
(i)
, we have
u\sim\lambda_{m,n}u^{\prime}
and
v\sim\lambda_{m,n}v^{\prime}
for some
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in X_{n}^{*}
with
\overline{u}^{\prime},\overline{v}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{R}_{m,n})
, say
\overline{u}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(a)
and
\overline{v}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(b)
, where
a,b\in\mathcal{C}_{m,n}
. Then by (
2.7
) we have

a=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(a)=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\overline{u}^{\prime}=\overline{u}=\overline{v}=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\overline{v}^{\prime}=\overline{\lambda}_{m,n}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(b)=b.

But then
\overline{u}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(a)=\mathcal{R}_{m,n}(b)=\overline{v}^{\prime}
. Since
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in X_{n}^{*}
, it follows that
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\ker(\phi_{n})=R_{n}^{\sharp}
; cf. Assumption
3
. By Assumption
4
(iii)
, it follows that
u^{\prime}\sim v^{\prime}
. Putting all of this together, we deduce that
u\sim\lambda_{m,n}u^{\prime}\sim\lambda_{m,n}v^{\prime}\sim v
.
∎

Remark 2.18
.

Theorem
2.17
shows how to build a presentation for the category
\mathcal{C}
out of presentations for its endomorphism monoids
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}_{n,n}
(under appropriate assumptions).
It would be interesting to study the extent to which one could work in the opposite direction: i.e., begin with a presentation for
\mathcal{C}
, and deduce presentations for the monoids
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. It does not seem this is likely to work in general, however. For example, consider the category
\mathcal{B}
of all binary relations
\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\mathcal{B}
can be generated by a relatively simple set of relations akin to the partitions we use in Section
3
; see
[
24
]
. On the other hand, it is known that the minimal-size generating sets of the endomorphism monoids
\mathcal{B}_{n}
grow super-exponentially with
n
; see
[
42
, Corollary 3.1.8]
.

2.4
Second main result: tensor category presentations

We now show how to rewrite the presentation from Theorem
2.17
into a tensor presentation, again under certain natural assumptions, stated below. For the duration of Section
2.4
, we fix a category
\mathcal{C}
(with object set
\mathbb{S}
, being one of
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{P}
) satisfying Assumptions
1
–
4
. We also keep all the notation of Section
2.3
, in particular the presentation
\langle\Gamma:\Omega\rangle
from Theorem
2.17
, including the surmorphism
{\phi:\Gamma^{*}\to\mathcal{C}:w\mapsto\overline{w}}
.

Assumption 5
.

We assume that
\mathcal{C}
is a (strict) tensor category over
\mathbb{S}
.

As in Section
2.1
, we denote the tensor operation on
\mathcal{C}
by
\oplus
.

Assumption 6
.

We assume that

•

\Delta
is a digraph on vertex set
\mathbb{S}
,

•

\Xi\subseteq\Delta^{\oast}\times\Delta^{\oast}
is a set of relations over
\Delta
, and

•

\Phi:\Delta^{\oast}\to\mathcal{C}
is a morphism. For
w\in\Delta^{\oast}
we write
\underline{w}=w\Phi
.

Writing
\approx
for the congruence
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}
on
\Delta^{\oast}
, we also assume that the following all hold:

(i)

For every relation
(u,v)\in\Xi
, we have
\underline{u}=\underline{v}
.

(ii)

There is a mapping
\Gamma\to\Delta^{\oast}:x\mapsto\widehat{x}
such that
\underline{\hskip 1.99168pt\widehat{x}\hskip 1.99168pt}=\overline{x}
(i.e.,
\widehat{x}\Phi=x\phi
) for all
x\in\Gamma
. As
\Gamma^{*}
is freely generated by
\Gamma
, we can extend this mapping to a morphism
\Gamma^{*}\to\Delta^{\oast}:w\mapsto\widehat{w}
. (It quickly follows that
\underline{\hskip 1.99168pt\widehat{w}\hskip 1.99168pt}=\overline{w}
for all
w\in\Gamma^{*}
.)

(iii)

For a generator
x\in\Delta
, and for natural numbers
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the term

x_{m,n}=\iota_{m}\oplus x\oplus\iota_{n}\in\Delta^{\oast}.

(Again, when
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{P}
, we interpret
x_{0,n}=x\oplus\iota_{n}
, and so on.) We assume that for every such
x,m,n
, we have
x_{m,n}\approx\widehat{w}
for some
w\in\Gamma^{*}
.

(iv)

For every relation
(u,v)\in\Omega
, we have
\widehat{u}\approx\widehat{v}
.

Here is our second main result:

Theorem 2.19
.

With notation as above, and subject to Assumptions
1
–
6
, the category
\mathcal{C}
has tensor presentation
\langle\Delta:\Xi\rangle
via
\Phi
.

Proof.

To show that
\Phi
is surjective, suppose
a\in\mathcal{C}
. Since
\phi
is surjective, we have
a=w\phi
for some
w\in\Gamma^{*}
. But then
a=\widehat{w}\Phi
by Assumption
6
(ii)
.

As in the proof of Theorem
2.17
, Assumption
6
(i)
gives
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}^{\sharp}\subseteq\ker(\Phi)
. For the reverse inclusion, suppose
(u,v)\in\ker(\Phi)
, meaning that
u,v\in\Delta^{\oast}
and
\underline{u}=\underline{v}
. First writing
u
and
v
in the form (
2.4
), and then applying Assumption
6
(iii)
, we have
u\approx\widehat{s}
and
v\approx\widehat{t}
for some
s,t\in\Gamma^{*}
. Assumption
6
(i)
and
(ii)
then give
\overline{s}=\underline{\hskip 1.99168pt\widehat{s}\hskip 1.99168pt}=\underline{u}=\underline{v}=\underline{\hskip 1.99168pt\widehat{t}\hskip 1.99168pt}=\overline{t}
. It follows from Theorem
2.17
that there is a sequence

s=w_{0}\to w_{1}\to\cdots\to w_{k}=t,

where for each
1\leq i\leq k
,
w_{i}\in\Gamma^{*}
is obtained from
w_{i-1}
by a single application of a relation from
\Omega
. It then follows from Assumption
6

3.5
Linear diagram categories

Fix a field
\Bbbk
, and an element
\delta\in\Bbbk\setminus\{0\}
. For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{\delta}
the
\Bbbk
-vector space with basis
\mathcal{P}_{m,n}
: i.e., the set of all formal
\Bbbk
-linear combinations of partitions from
\mathcal{P}_{m,n}
. We also write

\mathcal{P}^{\delta}=\bigcup_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{\delta}

for the set of all such combinations. This set is a category (over
\mathbb{N}
) with composition
\star
defined as follows. Consider basis elements
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}_{m,n}
and
\beta\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
. We write
m(\alpha,\beta)
for the number of “floating” components in the product graph
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
, as defined in Section
3.1
: i.e., the number of components contained entirely in
[n]^{\prime\prime}
. (So
m(\alpha,\beta)=1
for
\alpha,\beta
in Figure
1
.) The product
\alpha\star\beta\in\mathcal{P}_{m,k}^{\delta}
is defined by

\alpha\star\beta=\delta^{m(\alpha,\beta)}\alpha\beta.

So
\alpha\star\beta
is a scalar multiple of the basis element
\alpha\beta=\alpha\circ\beta\in\mathcal{P}_{m,k}
. This composition on basis elements is then extended to all of
\mathcal{P}^{\delta}
by
\Bbbk
-linearity. The operation
\oplus
and the involution
∗
on
\mathcal{P}
also extend to corresponding operations on
\mathcal{P}^{\delta}
.

Here we call
\mathcal{P}^{\delta}
the linear partition category (associated to
\Bbbk
and
\delta
), in order to distinguish it from the partition category
\mathcal{P}
. Most authors simply call
\mathcal{P}^{\delta}
the partition category. We also have linear versions of the Brauer and Temperley-Lieb categories:
\mathcal{B}^{\delta}
and
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}^{\delta}
.

The presentations for the diagram categories given in Sections
3.2
–
3.4
may be easily modified to yield presentations for their linear counterparts. To do so, we use the method of
[
28

Let
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{B}

\Gamma

3.2
,
3.3
or
3.4

w\in\Gamma^{*}

w

m(w)=0
. Otherwise, write

k\geq 2
and each

\mathcal{C}^{\delta}

\overline{x}_{1}\star\cdots\star\overline{x}_{k}=\delta^{m(w)}\overline{w}
, where
m(w)=m(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2})+m(\overline{x_{1}x_{2}},\overline{x}_{3})+\cdots+m(\overline{x_{1}\cdots x_{k-1}},\overline{x}_{k})

, which can then be rewritten (using Theorem
2.19
) to yield the following:

Theorem 4.23
.

The category
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
has tensor presentation with generators

V\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{2,1},\hskip 20.00003ptU\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
-\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{1,0},\hskip 20.00003pt{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\mapsto(\varnothing)\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{0,1}

and relations

{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\circ U=\iota_{0},\hskip 10.00002ptV\circ U=U\oplus U,\hskip 10.00002pt(V\oplus I)\circ V=(I\oplus V)\circ V,\hskip 10.00002pt(I\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}})\circ V=I=({\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\oplus I)\circ V.

For the category
\mathcal{O}
, we first use the above-mentioned presentations for
\mathcal{O}_{n}

Remark 4.25
.

Theorems
4.23
and
4.24
both include the relation
(I\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}})\circ V=I=({\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\oplus I)\circ V
, whereas
(PV2)′
has only
(I\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}})\circ V=I
. The reason for this is that with the additional generator
X
, the second half of this relation follows from the first (and other parts of
(PV1)′
–
(PV6)′
); cf. (
4.8
).

For the category
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}
, we could apply Theorems
2.17
and
2.19
as usual, starting from presentations for the monoids
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}_{n}
as may be found in
[
33
]
for example. However, the relations from
[
33
]
are substantially different from
(PV1)
–
(PV13)
, so this would require a great deal more work. In any case, the category
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}
is simple enough that we can give a direct proof of the following:

Theorem 4.26
.

The category
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}
has tensor presentation with generators

U\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
-\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}_{1,0}\and{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\mapsto(\varnothing)\in\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}_{0,1}

and the single relation
{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\circ U=\iota_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta
be the digraph over
\mathbb{N}
with edges
U:1\to 0
and
{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}:0\to 1
, and let
\Phi:\Delta^{\oast}\to\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}
be the morphism in the statement. Let
\approx
be the congruence on
\Delta^{\oast}
generated by the relation
{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\circ U=\iota_{0}
.

To show that
\Phi
is surjective, let
f\in\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}_{m,n}
, and write
f=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{k}\\
b_{1}&\cdots&b_{k}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}
(and
b_{1}<\cdots<b_{k}
). For convenience, we also define
a_{0}=b_{0}=0
, and
a_{k+1}=m+1
and
b_{k+1}=n+1
. Then, defining
p_{i}=a_{i+1}-a_{i}-1
and
q_{i}=b_{i+1}-b_{i}-1
for all
0\leq i\leq k
, we have
f=w\Phi
, where

w=(U^{\oplus p_{0}}\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}^{\oplus q_{0}})\oplus I\oplus(U^{\oplus p_{1}}\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}^{\oplus q_{1}})\oplus I\oplus\cdots\oplus(U^{\oplus p_{k-1}}\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}^{\oplus q_{k-1}})\oplus I\oplus(U^{\oplus p_{k}}\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}^{\oplus q_{k}}).

(4.27)

Next,
{\approx}\subseteq\ker(\Phi)
is easily checked as usual. For the reverse inclusion, it suffices to show that every term from
\Delta^{\oast}
is
\approx
-equivalent to a term of the form (
4.27
), as this form uniquely determines
w\Phi\in\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}
. In fact, by (
2.4
) and a simple induction, it is enough to show that for any term
w
as in (
4.27
) and for
z=\iota_{c}\oplus x\oplus\iota_{d}
, with
c,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\{U,{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\}
and
{\bf r}(w)={\bf d}(z)
,
w\circ z
is
\approx
-equivalent to a term of the desired form. For convenience, we write
w=y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{l}
, where
y_{1},\ldots,y_{l}\in\{I,U,{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\}
are the symbols in the order they appear in (
4.27
). For
0\leq i\leq l
, we write
w_{i}^{-}=y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{i}
and
w_{i}^{+}=y_{i+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{l}
(interpreting
w_{0}^{-}=w_{l}^{+}=\iota_{0}
), so that
w=w_{i}^{-}\oplus w_{i}^{+}
for all
i
.

Case 1.
Suppose first that
x=U
. Noting that
{\bf r}(w)={\bf d}(z)=c+d+1>c
, let
0\leq m\leq l
be maximal so that
{\bf r}(w_{m}^{-})=c
. Since
{\bf r}(w_{m+1}^{-})>c
, we must have
y_{m+1}=I
or

U

, and we have
w=w_{m}^{-}\oplus y_{m+1}\oplus w_{m+1}^{+}
and
{\bf r}(w_{m+1}^{+})=d
. If
y_{m+1}=I
, then

w\circ z=(w_{m}^{-}\oplus I\oplus w_{m+1}^{+})\circ(\iota_{c}\oplus U\oplus\iota_{d})=(w_{m}^{-}\circ\iota_{c})\oplus(I\circ U)\oplus(w_{m+1}^{+}\circ\iota_{d})=w_{m}^{-}\oplus U\oplus w_{m+1}^{+}.

If
y_{m+1}={\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}
, then we similarly show (using
{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\circ U\approx\iota_{0}
) that
w\circ z\approx w_{m}^{-}\oplus w_{m+1}^{+}
. In both cases, the final expressions are either already of the form (
4.27
), or can be transformed into the desired form using the relation
U\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}={\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\oplus U
; cf. (
3.6
).

Case 2.
If
x={\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}
, then we again let
0\leq m\leq l
be maximal so that
{\bf r}(w_{m}^{-})=c
, and this time we have
w\circ z=w_{m}^{-}\oplus{\raise 6.88889pt\hbox{\rotatebox{180.0}{$U$}}}\oplus w_{m}^{+}
, which is again either already of the form (
4.27
), or can be transformed into such form using (
3.6
).
∎
