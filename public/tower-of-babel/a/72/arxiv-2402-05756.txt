Non-Markovian Quantum Mpemba effect
David J. Strachan, Archak Purkayastha, Stephen R. Clark

Abstract
Since its rediscovery in the twentieth century, the Mpemba effect, where a far-from-equilibrium state may relax faster than a state closer to equilibrium, has been extensively studied in classical systems and has recently received significant attention in quantum systems. Many theories explaining this counter-intuitive behavior in classical systems rely on memory effects. However, in quantum systems, the relation between the Mpemba effect and memory has remained unexplored. In this work, we consider general non-Markovian open quantum systems and reveal new classes of quantum Mpemba effects, with no analog in Markovian quantum dynamics. Generically, open quantum dynamics possess a finite memory time and a unique steady state. Due to non-Markovian dynamics, even if the system is initialized in the steady state it can take a long time to relax back. We find other initial states that reach the steady state much faster. Most notably, we demonstrate that there can be an initial state in which the system reaches the steady state within the finite memory time itself, therefore giving the fastest possible relaxation to stationarity. We verify the effect for quantum dot systems coupled to electronic reservoirs in equilibrium and non-equilibrium setups at weak, intermediate and strong coupling, and both with and without interactions. Our work provides new insights into the rich physics underlying accelerated relaxation in quantum systems.

Non-Markovian dynamics—

At
\tau=0

\hat{\rho}(0)

\hat{\rho}(\tau)=\hat{\Lambda}(\tau)[\hat{\rho}(0)]
where,

\hat{\Lambda}(\tau)[\bullet]=\textrm{Tr}_{B}\big(e^{-i\hat{H}\tau}\bullet\hat{\rho}_{B}(0)e^{i\hat{H}\tau}\big),

\hat{H}_{S}

\hat{H}_{B}

\hat{H}_{SB}

;

;
Chruściński 2022

\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}(\tau)}{\partial\tau}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)[\hat{\rho}(\tau)]

\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)=\frac{d}{d\tau}[\hat{\Lambda}(\tau)]\hat{\Lambda}^{-1}(\tau)

Two different memory times—

A crucial difference between the above non-Markovian description and Markovian quantum dynamics is that, both

\displaystyle||\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)[\hat{\rho}(\infty)]||<\epsilon~~\forall~~\tau\geq\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m},

(2)

Weak, strong and extreme NMQMpE—

There are three possible types of NMQMpE which we call weak, strong and extreme. Let
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
be some faster relaxing initial state such that
\alpha_{\nu_{0}}(\hat{\rho}_{\rm f})
and
\alpha_{\kappa_{0}}(\hat{\rho}(\infty))
are the components of the slowest non-zero modes excited by
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
when starting with
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
and
\hat{\rho}(\infty)
, respectively. The weak NMQMpE arises when
\nu_{0}=\kappa_{0}
and
\alpha_{\nu_{0}}(\hat{\rho}_{\rm f})<\alpha_{\kappa_{0}}(\hat{\rho}(\infty))
. This means the slowest non-zero modes excited by
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
are the same for both initial conditions, but
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
has a smaller amplitude in that mode. Then,
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
will relax faster, but not exponentially faster. If
\nu_{0}>\kappa_{0}
, there exists a strong NMQMpE where
\hat{\rho_{\rm f}}
relaxes exponentially faster than
\hat{\rho}(\infty)
by a rate given by the spectral gap
\textrm{Re}|\lambda_{\nu_{0}}-\lambda_{\kappa_{0}}|
. The weak and strong NMQMpE are analogs of weak and strong MpE observed in Markovian quantum dynamics
Carollo et al. 2021
;
Chatterjee et al. 2023a
;
Ivander et al. 2023
. However, for NMQMpE, we can have an additional extreme case
if
\hat{\alpha}_{\nu}(\hat{\rho}_{\rm f})=0
for all
\nu

\hat{\mathcal{S}}
removes all decay components from
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
to give the steady state
\hat{\rho}(\tau>\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m})=\hat{\mathcal{S}}[\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}]=\hat{\rho}(\infty)
. Thus, the initial state converges to the steady state within the memory time
\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m}
, giving the fastest possible relaxation. Formally,

\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}=\frac{\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}[\hat{\rho}(\infty)]}{\textrm{Tr}(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}[\hat{\rho}(\infty)])}.

(5)

with lower temperature consistent with the dynamics being more non-Markovian. A surprising feature of Fig.

\delta\mu=0
as
\bar{\beta}\to\infty
where both modes are always half filled with zero current. Here
\hat{\rho}(\tau)
can only differ by
\textrm{Re}(\langle\hat{s}_{1}^{{\dagger}}\hat{s}_{2}\rangle)
, such that the relaxation dynamics is entirely determined by the quantum coherence.

Possible experimental verification—
The QD setup lends itself to experimental verification of the NMQMpE by measuring the dynamics of the dot’s occupation using a quantum point contact charge sensor. Assuming a measurement time resolution of
\approx 10~\mu{\rm s}

Elzerman et al. 2004
requires a memory time
\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m}\geq 20~\mu{\rm s}
for the slippage to be observable. Further, assuming an occupation resolution of
1\%
for the measurement then requires
\delta\rm p\geq 0.01
so that
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
can be reliably distinguished from
\hat{\rho}(\infty)
. This is satisfied for the case shown in Fig.

(a) for a narrow bandwidth bath
D=1~\mu{\rm s}^{-1}
implying a low temperature
T\approx 5~\mu{\rm K}
. In an realistic setup the control of dot and bath couplings will not be a sudden quench. This poses no issue for NMQMpE as finite time quenches can simply be incorporated into the slippage
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
and thus accounted for in
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}\propto\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}[\hat{\rho}(\infty)]

sup
.

Conclusion—
The NMQMpE uncovered in this paper are quite generic effects in non-Markovian dynamics, with no parallel in Markovian dynamics, and are accessible to experimental verification. We anticipate that NMQMpE will find applications in control of open quantum systems, for example, in quantum state preparation and qubit reset
Christiane P. Koch et al. 2022
;
Koch 2016

;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Mukherjee et al. 2020
;

del Campo et al. 2013

;

;

I
Redfield Equation

Here we show the slippage
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
is always invertible in the weak coupling regime. Consider a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{B}+\gamma\hat{H}_{SB}
with
\hat{H}_{SB}=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{Q}_{\alpha}\hat{B}_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}+{\rm h.c.}
,
\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{H}_{B_{\alpha}}
where the sum over
\alpha
includes all baths in contact with the system and
||\hat{H}_{SB}||=O(1)
. The Redfield equation is then the second order approximation to the Nakajima-Zwanzig master equation
Purkayastha et al. 2016
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=i[\hat{\rho},\hat{H}]-\gamma^{2}\sum_{\alpha}([\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha},\hat{Q}^{1}_{\alpha}(\tau)\,\hat{\rho}]+[\hat{\rho}\,\hat{Q}^{2}_{\alpha}(\tau),\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}]+{\rm h.c.}),

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm RE}(\tau)\hat{\rho}(\tau),

(S1)

with

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{1}_{\alpha}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\hat{Q}^{I}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\langle\hat{B}_{\alpha}(0)\hat{B}^{I^{{\dagger}}}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\rangle_{B},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{2}_{\alpha}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\hat{Q}^{I}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\langle\hat{B}^{I}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\hat{B}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(0)\rangle_{B},

(S2)

where
\hat{Q}^{I}_{\alpha}(\tau)=e^{i\hat{H}_{S}\tau}\hat{Q}_{\alpha}e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}\tau}
,
\hat{B}^{I}_{\alpha}(\tau)=e^{i\hat{H}_{B_{\alpha}}\tau}\hat{B}_{\alpha}e^{-i\hat{H}_{B_{\alpha}}\tau}
and the average is taken over bath
\alpha
. This description works well when
\gamma
is small compared to the other energy scales present. The memory timescale is then given by the time such that

\displaystyle|\langle\hat{B}_{\alpha}(0)\hat{B}^{I^{{\dagger}}}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\rangle_{B}|<O(\epsilon)\quad\forall\tau>\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m},

\displaystyle|\langle\hat{B}^{I}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\hat{B}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(0)\rangle_{B}|<O(\epsilon)\quad\forall\tau>\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m},

(S3)

where
\epsilon
is an arbitrarily small error. This then gives a time independent generator for
\tau>\tau_{\rm m}^{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=i[\hat{\rho},\hat{H}]-\gamma^{2}\sum_{\alpha}([\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha},\hat{Q}^{1}_{\alpha}\,\hat{\rho}]+[\hat{\rho}\,\hat{Q}^{2}_{\alpha},\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}]+{\rm h.c.}),

(S4)

with

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{1}_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau^{\prime}\hat{Q}^{I}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\langle\hat{B}_{\alpha}(0)\hat{B}^{I^{{\dagger}}}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\rangle_{B},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{2}_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau^{\prime}\hat{Q}^{I}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\langle\hat{B}^{I}_{\alpha}(-\tau^{\prime})\hat{B}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(0)\rangle_{B}.

(S5)

We now calculate the fast state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}=\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}[\hat{\rho}_{\rm SS}
]. Solving Eq.(
S4
) up to
O(\gamma^{2})
gives

\hat{\rho}(\tau)=e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau)}\hat{\rho}(0)e^{i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau)}-\gamma^{2}\sum_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\bigg([\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha},\hat{Q}^{1}_{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})\,\hat{\rho}(\tau^{\prime})]+[\hat{\rho}(\tau^{\prime})\,\hat{Q}^{2}_{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime}),\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}]+{\rm h.c.}\bigg)e^{i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})},

(S6)

and hence

\hat{\rho}(0)=e^{i\hat{H}_{S}\tau}\hat{\rho}(\tau)e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}\tau}-\gamma^{2}\sum_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}e^{i\hat{H}_{S}\tau^{\prime}}\bigg([\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha},\hat{Q}^{1}_{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})\,\hat{\rho}]+[\hat{\rho}\,\hat{Q}^{2}_{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime}),\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}]+{\rm h.c.}\bigg)e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}\tau^{\prime}},

(S7)

with
\hat{\rho}(\tau^{\prime})=e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\hat{\rho}(\tau)e^{i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}+O(\gamma^{2})
. Setting
\tau=\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m}
and
\hat{\rho}(\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m})=\hat{\rho}_{\rm SS}
, we find the
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
up to
O(\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}

\displaystyle=\hat{\rho}(0)=e^{i\hat{H}_{S}\tau}\hat{\rho}(\tau)e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}\tau}

\displaystyle-\gamma^{2}\sum_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}e^{i\hat{H}_{S}\tau^{\prime}}\bigg([\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha},\hat{Q}^{1}_{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\hat{\rho}(\tau)e^{i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}]+[e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\hat{\rho}(\tau)e^{i\hat{H}_{S}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\hat{Q}^{2}_{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime}),\hat{Q}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}]+{\rm h.c.}\bigg)e^{-i\hat{H}_{S}\tau^{\prime}}.

(S8)

This formulation shows
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
is always defined in the weak coupling regime. This state may not be physical however, as the Redfield description preserves hermicity and trace but not positivity. If this solution gives non-negative eigenvalues for
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
, there exists a NMQMpE.

II
Calculation of
\hat{\Lambda}(\tau)

To extract
\hat{\Lambda}(\tau)
, we use the Choi-Jamiolkowski isomorphism
Choi 1975
;
Jamiołkowski 1972
which describes the correspondence between quantum maps and quantum states. Consider maximally entangling the system with an auxillary system
A
with the same Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
as
S
,
\ket{\Phi^{+}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\ket{i}_{S}\otimes\ket{i}_{A}
. Since
\hat{\Lambda}(\tau)
is a completely positive trace preserving map,
\hat{\rho}^{\Lambda}(\tau)=(\hat{\Lambda}(\tau)\otimes\mathds{1})\{\ket{\Psi^{+}}\bra{\Psi^{+}}\}
is a nonnegative operator. Conversely, for any nonnegative operator on
L(\mathcal{H}_{S})\otimes L(\mathcal{H}_{S})
, we can associate a quantum map from operators on
L(\mathcal{H}_{S})
to
L(\mathcal{H}_{S})
. This isomorphism assumes a tensor product Hilbert space structure and can be applied to 1D fermionic systems once they have been Jordan Wigner transformed into effective spins. The fermionic map can then be extracted once the anti-commuting behaviour of fermions is accounted for
Amosov and Filippov 2016
.

III
Time evolution and Bath description

For numerical calculations the continuum of modes for each bath
\alpha
is approximated using a finite number of modes
N_{\alpha}
. We use the same number of modes for each bath
N_{\alpha}=N_{b}
. To do this we employ a finite chain mapping using orthogonal polynomials and a thermofield purification scheme to describe the finite temperature bath initial states. This results in the following Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{S}+\sum_{\tilde{\alpha}}\bigg[\kappa_{\tilde{\alpha},0}^{-1}(\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},0}+\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},0}\hat{s})+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{b}}\bigg(\gamma_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}+\sqrt{\beta_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}+\sqrt{\beta_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\bigg)\bigg].

(S9)

Here
\tilde{\alpha}=L_{f},L_{e},R_{f},R_{e}
now denotes the four baths, left and right correspondingly filled and empty, with all their parameters fully defined by the bath spectral functions, temperatures and chemical potentials. The 1D mode ordering used is given by

\displaystyle\{\hat{o}_{i}\}=\{\hat{b}_{L_{f}1},\hat{b}_{L_{e}1},...,\hat{b}_{L_{f}N_{b}},\hat{b}_{L_{e}N_{b}},\hat{s}_{1},...\hat{s}_{N_{s}},\hat{a}_{1},...,\hat{a}_{N_{s}},\hat{b}_{R_{f}1},\hat{b}_{R_{e}1},...,\hat{b}_{R_{f}N_{b}},\hat{b}_{R_{e}N_{b}}\},

(S10)

where
\hat{a}_{i}
are the system ancilla modes. The details of this scheme is outlined in Sec.
IX
.

If
\hat{H}_{S}
is quadratic in mode operators, the exact dynamics can then be obtained via unitary evolution of the single-particle correlation matrix
\bm{C}_{ij}(t)=\textrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{o}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{o}_{i})
using the quadratic Hamiltonian defined via
\hat{H}=\sum_{ij}\bm{h}_{ij}\hat{o}^{{\dagger}}_{i}\hat{o}_{j}
and

\bm{C}(t)=e^{i\bm{h}t}\bm{C}(t_{0})e^{-i\bm{h}t},

(S11)

as shown in detail in Sec.
X
. The reduced density matrix of the combined system + ancilla setup is related to
C(t)
via
Cheong and Henley 2004

\hat{\rho}^{\Lambda}(t)=\mathrm{det}(\mathbb{1}-\bm{C}^{\rm T}(t))\mathrm{exp}\bigg\{\sum_{ij\in SA}[\mathrm{log}(\bm{C}^{\rm T}(t))(\mathbb{1}-\bm{C}^{\rm T}(t))^{-1}]_{ij}\hat{o}^{{\dagger}}_{i}\hat{o}_{j}\bigg\},

(S12)

where
SA
denotes the modes spanning the system + ancilla. This equation holds provided
\hat{\rho}^{\Lambda}(t)
is block diagonal in the number basis. For this reason, we perform a particle-hole transformation on the entangled states used in the Choi-Jamiolkowski isomorphism. If
\hat{H}_{S}
is not quadratic, we use two site time dependent variational principle
Fishman et al. 2022
to directly obtain
\hat{\rho}^{\Lambda}(t)
.

\tau=0

\Theta(\tau)

\hat{\mathcal{S}}

\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}

\tau_{Q}
but its form will not, i.e.
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}
=
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}(\tau_{Q})

\hat{\rho}_{f}(\tau_{Q}=0)

remains a fast converging state but as

Landauer Büttiker Theory

Here we briefly outline how to compute the currents in Landauer-Büttiker theory which act as our point of comparison for non-interacting system steady states. In the continuum limit for macroscopic baths, the particle and energy currents in the absence of system interactions are given by
Ryndyk 2017
;
Purkayastha 2019

J_{LB}^{P}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega\tau(\omega)[f_{L}(\omega)-f_{R}(\omega)],

(S15)

and

J_{LB}^{E}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega\omega\tau(\omega)[f_{L}(\omega)-f_{R}(\omega)],

(S16)

where
f_{\alpha}(\omega)
denotes the Fermi-Dirac distribution for bath
\alpha
and
\tau(\omega)
is the transmission function of the system. This can be calculated in terms of the non-equilibrium Green’s function
Dorda et al. 2014
;
Purkayastha 2019
. In our case this is given by

G(\omega)=\bm{M}^{-1}(\omega),

(S17)

with

\bm{M}(\omega)=\omega\mathds{1}-\bm{h}_{S}-\bm{\Sigma}^{(1)}(\omega)-\bm{\Sigma}^{(N_{S})}(\omega),

(S18)

where the only nonzero elements of the self-energy matrices of the leads
\bm{\Sigma}^{(j)}(\omega)
are

[\bm{\Sigma}^{(j)}]_{jj}(\omega)=\mathcal{P}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{\mathcal{J}_{j}(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}-\omega}-i\pi\mathcal{J}_{j}(\omega),

(S19)

using
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\omega)=\mathcal{J}_{L}(\omega)
,
\mathcal{J}_{N_{S}}(\omega)=\mathcal{J}_{R}(\omega)
and
\bm{h}_{S}
is defined via
\hat{H}_{S}=\sum_{ij}(\bm{h}_{S})_{ij}\hat{o}^{{\dagger}}_{i}\hat{o}_{j}
.
If the system Hamiltonian is of the form

\bm{h}_{S}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}\epsilon_{j}\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{s}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}-1}t_{i}(\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}_{j+1}\hat{s}_{j}+\text{h.c.}),

(S20)

the transmission function is given by

\tau(\omega)=4\pi^{2}\mathcal{J}_{L}(\omega)\mathcal{J}_{R}(\omega)|[G(\omega)]_{1D}|^{2}=\frac{\mathcal{J}_{L}(\omega)\mathcal{J}_{R}(\omega)}{|\text{det}[\bm{M}]|^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{S}-1}|t_{i}|^{2}.

(S21)

A single quantum dot is described by a spinless fermion mode as

(S22)

\hat{\rho}(\textrm{p})=(1-\textrm{p})\ket{0}\bra{0}+\textrm{p}\ket{1}\bra{1},

(S23)

with an occupation
0\leq{\rm p}\leq 1
. Using
\textrm{Tr}(\hat{F}_{2})=0
and
\textrm{Tr}(\hat{G}_{\mu}\hat{F}_{\nu})=\delta_{\mu\nu}
for a single mode we have

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{2}=\ket{0}\bra{0}-\ket{1}\bra{1},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{1}=(1-\textrm{p}(\infty))\ket{0}\bra{0}+\textrm{p}(\infty)\ket{1}\bra{1},

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{1}=\ket{0}\bra{0}+\ket{1}\bra{1},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{2}=\textrm{p}(\infty)\ket{0}\bra{0}+(\textrm{p}(\infty)-1)\ket{1}\bra{1},

where
\hat{\rho}(\infty)=\hat{\rho}(\textrm{p}(\infty))
.
Now consider the slippage
\hat{\mathcal{S}}=\hat{\Lambda}(\tau^{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm m})
. After vectorizing
\hat{\rho}
we can express
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
as a
2\times 2
matrix acting in the physical subspace
\{\ket{0}\bra{0},\ket{1}\bra{1}\}
. For this map to be CPTP, it must be of the form

\hat{\mathcal{S}}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu&\sigma\\
1-\nu&1-\sigma\end{pmatrix}

(S24)

with
\nu=\bra{0}\hat{\mathcal{S}}\{\ket{0}\bra{0}\}\ket{0}
,
\sigma=\bra{0}\hat{\mathcal{S}}\{\ket{1}\bra{1}\}\ket{0}
, with
0\leq\nu,\sigma\leq 1
. Now
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}
is manifestly trace preserving for the single mode,
\textrm{Tr}(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}[\hat{\rho}_{\rm SS}])=1
. The fast state is then given by
\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}=\hat{\rho}_{\rm f}(\textrm{p}_{\rm f})=\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}\hat{\rho}_{\rm SS}
where

\textrm{p}_{\rm f}=\frac{\nu-(1-\textrm{p}(\infty))}{\nu-\sigma}.

(S25)

If
\textrm{p}_{\rm f}\neq\textrm{p}(\infty)
and
0\leq{\rm p}_{\rm f}\leq 1
then we have a NMQMpE. The evolution of
\hat{\rho}(\tau)
is given by
\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}(\tau)}{\partial\tau}=\mathcal{L}(\tau)\hat{\rho}(\tau)
where
\mathcal{L}(\tau)=\sum_{\mu}\hat{F}_{\mu}\hat{G}^{{\dagger}}_{\mu}
in vectorised form, giving

\frac{\partial\textrm{p}(\tau)}{\partial\tau}=\lambda_{2}(\tau)\big(\textrm{p}(\tau)-\textrm{p}_{\text{TDFP}}(\tau)\big).

(S26)

where
\textrm{p}_{\text{TDFP}}(\tau)
is the occupation of the time-dependent fixed point of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)
. This shows that the evolution is controlled by the instantaneous steady state of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\tau)
, acting as an attractor.

IX
Thermofield Transformation and chain mapping

The thermofield formalism is a commonly used technique in quantum field theory
Takahashi and Umezawa 1996
;
Borrelli and Gelin 2021
;
Kohn and Santoro 2022
and statistical mechanics to relate the properties of a quantum system at finite temperature (impure state) to a doubled system at zero temperature (pure state). For a single fermionic bath mode
\hat{c}
with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{B}=\epsilon\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}
it’s thermal state at inverse temperature
\beta
and chemical potential
\mu
is

\rho_{\beta}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}}\big(\ket{0}\bra{0}+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\ket{1}\bra{1}\big),

(S27)

where
\ket{1}=\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}\ket{0}
. Introducing an ancilla mode
\hat{a}
with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{A}=\epsilon\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}\hat{a}
, we can express the bath’s thermal state as a partial trace of the thermofield double state in the enlarged system as

\ket{\Omega_{\beta}}=\sqrt{1-f}\ket{0}_{B}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}+\sqrt{f}\ket{1}_{B}\otimes\ket{1}_{A},

(S28)

where
f=(1+e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)})^{-1}
is the fermi factor. Performing a particle hole transformation on the ancilla mode, we have

\displaystyle\ket{\Omega_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-f}\ket{0}_{B}\otimes\ket{1}_{A}+\sqrt{f}\ket{1}_{B}\otimes\ket{0}_{A},

\displaystyle=\big(\sqrt{1-f}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}+\sqrt{f}\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}\big)\ket{\text{vac}}.

Defining two new fermionic mode operators,

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}=\sqrt{1-f}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}+\sqrt{f}\hat{c}^{{\dagger}},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{e}^{{\dagger}}=\sqrt{f}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}-\sqrt{1-f}\hat{c}^{{\dagger}},

we see that the thermal state can then be expressed as a single particle product state
\ket{\Omega_{\beta}}=\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}\ket{\text{vac}}
. We now transform our Hamiltonian into this basis. The self energy terms take a simple form,

\hat{{H}}_{A}+\hat{H}_{B}=\epsilon\big(\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}\hat{a}+\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}\big)=\epsilon\big(\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}\hat{f}+\hat{e}^{{\dagger}}\hat{e}\big),

(S29)

and if we assume the mode couples to the system via a hybridisation term
\hat{H}_{SB}
with one system mode
\hat{s}
we then have

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{SB}=v\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}\hat{s}+v^{*}\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{b}=v\sqrt{f}\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}\hat{s}-v\sqrt{1-f}\hat{e}^{{\dagger}}\hat{s}+v^{*}\sqrt{f}\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{f}-v^{*}\sqrt{1-f}\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{e}.

(S30)

Moving back to the continuum,
v\to\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(\omega)}
,
f\to f_{\alpha}(\omega)
,
\hat{c}\to\hat{c}_{\alpha}(\omega)
, we have

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{S}+\sum_{\alpha=L,R}\int_{-D}^{D}\omega\big(\hat{f}_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)\hat{f}_{\alpha}(\omega)+\hat{e}_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)\hat{e}_{\alpha}(\omega)\big)+\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{\alpha f}(\omega)}\big[\hat{f}_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)\hat{s}+\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{f}_{\alpha}(\omega)\big]-\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{\alpha e}(\omega)}\big[\hat{e}_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)\hat{s}+\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{e}_{\alpha}(\omega)\big]\mathrm{d}\omega,

(S31)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha f}(\omega)=f_{\alpha}(\omega)\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(\omega),\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha e}(\omega)=\big(1-f_{\alpha}(\omega)\big)\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(\omega).

This mapping has moved the dependence on temperature from the state into the Hamiltonian, where two separate baths are coupled to the system, one filled and one empty. This separation between the filled and empty modes gives us much greater freedom in optimising our Hamiltonian for matrix product state calculations. The thermal state
\hat{\rho}_{B}
has become a purified thermofield state
\ket{\Omega_{\beta}}=\prod_{k}\ket{\Omega_{\beta,k}}=\prod_{k}\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{k}\ket{\textrm{vac}}
.

Once the thermofield transformation is applied, we then map each of the continuous baths to finite chains using orthogonal polynomials
Chin et al. 2010
;
Sánchez-Barquilla and Feist 2021
. This is done numerically using the
orthopol
package
Mühlpfordt et al. 2020
which implements the following protocol. For simplicity we only consider one bath, but the analysis generalises straightforwardly. We have the following bath Hamiltonian terms

\hat{H}_{SB}=\sum_{c=1,2}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega\sqrt{J_{c}(\omega)}(\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{f}_{c}(\omega)+{\rm h.c.}),

(S32)

\hat{H}_{A}+\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{c=1,2}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega\omega\hat{f}_{c}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)\hat{f}_{c}(\omega),

(S33)

where we have denoted empty modes as
\hat{e}(\omega)=\hat{f}_{1}(\omega)
and the filled modes as
\hat{f}(\omega)=\hat{f}_{2}(\omega)
. To carry out the chain mapping, we define new fermionic operators

\hat{b}_{c,n}=\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega\sqrt{J_{c}(\omega)}\kappa_{c,n}\pi_{c,n}(\omega)\hat{f}_{c}(\omega),

(S34)

with an inverse transformation

\hat{f}_{c}(\omega)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{J_{c}(\omega)}\kappa_{c,n}\pi_{c,n}(\omega)\hat{b}_{c,n},

(S35)

where
\pi_{c,n}(x)
is an
n
th monic polynomial with a corresponding normalisation constant
\kappa_{c,n}
(defined below). We then have
\pi_{c,n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{nj}x^{j}
, where the monic condition means
c_{nn}=1
. We define
\pi_{c,n}(x)
such that they obey the following orthogonality condition

\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega J_{c}(\omega)\pi_{c,m}(\omega)\pi_{c,n}(\omega)=\kappa_{c,n}^{-2}\delta_{n,m},

(S36)

which also defines the normalisation constants
\kappa_{c,n}
.
Note that this transformation will leave the state invariant as the new creation (annihilation) operators are linear combinations of creation (annihilation) operators only, so a filled (empty) bath state is mapped to a filled (empty) chain. This is major advantages of the thermofield method for tackling finite temperature. This gives

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{SB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{c,n}(\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{b}_{c,n}+{\rm h.c.})\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega J_{c}(\omega)\pi_{c,n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{c,n}(\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{b}_{c,n}+{\rm h.c.})\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega J_{c}(\omega)\pi_{c,n}(\omega)\pi_{c,0}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}\kappa_{c,0}^{-1}\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{b}_{c,0}+{\rm h.c.}.

(S37)

Now consider the bath Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{A}+\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{c=1,2}\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n}\hat{b}_{c,m}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega\omega\pi_{c,n}(\omega)\pi_{c,m}(\omega).

(S38)

To progress, we make use of the following recurrence relation for the monic polynomials.

\pi_{c,n+1}(\omega)=(x-\gamma_{c,n})\pi_{c,n}(\omega)-\beta_{c,n}\pi_{c,n-1}(\omega),

(S39)

where
\gamma_{c,n}
and
\beta_{c,n}
are uniquely determined by the weight function as

\gamma_{c,n}=\kappa_{c,n}^{2}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega\omega J_{c}(\omega)\pi^{2}_{c,n}(\omega),

(S40)

and

\beta_{c,n}=\kappa_{c,n}\kappa_{c,n+1}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega J_{c}(\omega)\omega\pi_{c,n}(\omega)\pi_{c,n-1}(\omega).

(S41)

Using this, we have

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{A}+\hat{H}_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1,2}\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{c,n}^{2}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n}\hat{b}_{c,m}\int_{-D}^{D}d\omega J_{c}(\omega)\pi_{c,m}(\omega)\big[\pi_{c,n+1}(\omega)+\beta_{c,n}\pi_{c,n-1}(\omega)+\gamma_{c,n}\pi_{c,n}(\omega)\big],

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1,2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigg(\gamma_{c,n}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n}\hat{b}_{c,n}+\frac{\kappa_{c,n+1}\beta_{c,n+1}}{\kappa_{c,n}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n}\hat{b}_{c,n+1}+\frac{\kappa_{c,n}}{\kappa_{c,n+1}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n+1}\hat{b}_{c,n}\bigg),

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1,2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigg(\gamma_{c,n}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n}\hat{b}_{c,n}+\sqrt{\beta_{c,n+1}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n}\hat{b}_{c,n+1}+\sqrt{\beta_{c,n+1}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{c,n+1}\hat{b}_{c,n}\bigg).

(S42)

Combining this with the rest of the Hamiltonian and choosing a finite cutoff
N_{b}
for both baths, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{S}+\sum_{\tilde{\alpha}}\kappa_{\tilde{\alpha},0}^{-1}(\hat{s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},0}+\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},0}\hat{s})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\tilde{\alpha}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{n}}\bigg(\gamma_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}+\sqrt{\beta_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}+\sqrt{\beta_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{\tilde{\alpha},n+1}\hat{b}_{\tilde{\alpha},n}\bigg),

(S43)

where
\tilde{\alpha}
runs over all combinations of
\alpha,c
.

). Defining
\mathcal{U}(\tau)=e^{-i\hat{H}\tau}

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{ij}(\tau)

\hat{H}

\displaystyle\hat{H}\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}_{j}
