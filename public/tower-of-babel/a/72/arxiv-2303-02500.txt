Beyond the I-MMSE relation: Derivatives of mutual information in Gaussian channels
Minh-Toan Nguyen

Abstract
The I-MMSE formula connects two important quantities in information theory and estimation theory: the mutual information and the minimum mean-squared error (MMSE). It states that in a scalar Gaussian channel, the derivative of the mutual information with respect to the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is one-half of the MMSE. Although any derivative at a fixed order can be computed in principle, a general formula for all the derivatives is still unknown. In this paper, we derive this general formula for vector Gaussian channels. The obtained result is remarkably similar to the classic cumulant-moment relation in statistical theory.

Results for the scalar Gaussian channels generalize to linear vector Gaussian channels. Within this general model and with respect to arbitrary parameters of the model,

In this work, we derive a general formula for all higher derivatives of the mutual information with respect to the SNRs in a vector Gaussian channel. The obtained formula bears a striking similarity to the classical cumulant-moment relation

Our work relies on two key components: the reduction of multiple Gaussian channels with identical signals into a single channel without information loss, and the non-rigorous replica method originated from the physics of disordered systems

Several results in this paper, obtained through the non-rigorous replica method, still require rigorous proofs with explicit assumptions. Currently, we assume that every function and random variable introduced is sufficiently regular, so that operations performed on them are meaningful.

The paper is structured as follows. Section

Statement of results

Our results will be stated in terms of multisets and partitions. A

is a collection of elements where repetitions are allowed, and the arrangement of elements is disregarded. A

(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\})
of the multiset
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,1,2,2}}\right\}
is diverse while the partition
(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,1}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2,2}}\right\})
is not.

For a diverse partition
\pi
of the multiset
[n]_{2}
,

is defined as the number of

of
\pi
. Here, a twin of
\pi
is a pair of identical blocks in
\pi
. For example,

for
\pi=(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{3,4}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{3,4}}\right\})
and
s(\pi)=0
for
\pi=(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2,3,4}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{3,4}}\right\})
. Note that each block in a diverse partition of
[n]_{2}
appears at most twice.

With the basic notions of multisets and partitions, let us define the following important object that will allow us to express higher derivatives of the mutual information in a compact way.

Definition 1
.

The form
\tau
, defined with any

\displaystyle\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\frac{(-1)^{|\pi|-1}(|\pi|-2)!}{2^{s(\pi)}}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{B_{1}}]\dots\mathbb{E}[X_{B_{|\pi|}}],

(8)

where

are random variables, the sum is taken over all diverse partitions
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{|\pi|})
of
[n]_{2}
and

.

Definition 2
.

The form
\bar{\tau}
with

arguments are defined by the same combinatorial sum in (
1

\pi
of

with all block sizes greater than one.

Definition 3
.

\tau(\,\cdot\,|Y)
and
\bar{\tau}(\,\cdot\,|Y)
where

\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,]
in the definitions of

.

The multiset
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,1,2,2}}\right\}

(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\}),\quad(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2}}\right\})

\displaystyle(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2}}\right\}).

For the next two partitions,

is given by

\displaystyle\tau(X_{1},X_{2})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{2}]^{2}+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]^{2},

(10)

which can be written compactly in term of the covariance as

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}(X_{1},X_{2})=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{2}]^{2}.

(11)

The form
\tau
is remarkably similar to joint cumulants. Recall that the joint cumulant of random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle\kappa(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\dots\partial_{\lambda_{n}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})|_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{0}},

(12)

where

\displaystyle\psi_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\log\mathbb{E}e^{\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{X}\rangle}.

(13)

It is clear from the definition that the joint cumulant does not depends on the order of its arguments. The cumulant can be computed from the moments by the classical cumulant-moment formula
[
14
]
:

\displaystyle\kappa(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}(-1)^{|\pi|-1}(|\pi|-1)!\,\mathbb{E}[X_{B_{1}}]\dots\mathbb{E}[X_{B_{|\pi|}}],

(14)

where the sum is over all partitions
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{|\pi|})
of

. The joint cumulant is multilinear and
\kappa((X_{i})_{i\in[n]})=0
if

can be divided into two non-empty sets

and
J
such that
(X_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(X_{j})_{j\in J}
are independent.

.

a)
\tau
and

are multiquadratic.

b)

\displaystyle\tau(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\bar{\tau}(X_{1}-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}],\dots,X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]).

Here, a

on a vector space

such that there exists a bilinear form

such that

3
Tools

We present here the main tools of the paper: the equivalent of Gaussian channels and the replica method. The equivalent result implies that we can compute any derivative of the mutual information if we know how to compute expressions of the form
\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\dots\partial_{\lambda_{n}}I_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{0})
. The replica method, on the other hand, is used to obtain a combinatorial formula for
\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\dots\partial_{\lambda_{n}}I_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{0})
.

3.1
A nice property of Gaussian channels

Proposition 2
.

A set of Gaussian channels with the same signal
X
and independent noises is equivalent to a single Gaussian channel with signal
X
and SNR equal to the sum of individual SNRs.

Proof.

The proof relies on the concept of sufficient statistics and its connections to information theory
[
3
]
. Suppose the channels are

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}X+Z_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(25)

where
Z_{i}
are independent standard Gaussian noises independent of
X
. The posterior distribution of
X
given
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
is

\displaystyle P_{X|\boldsymbol{Y}}(dx)\propto P_{X}(dx)\exp\Big(\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}Y_{i}x-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}x^{2}\Big),

(26)

which implies that

\displaystyle S:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}Y_{i}

(27)

is a sufficient statistics for estimating
X
from the outputs. Moreover, let
\lambda=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
, then
S/\sqrt{\lambda}
can be written as
\sqrt{\lambda}X+\xi
, where

\displaystyle\xi=\sum_{i=t}^{n}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}/\lambda}\,Z_{i},

(28)

is independent of
X
and follows the standard normal distribution. The lemma follows from the fact that
S/\sqrt{\lambda}
, which contains all the information relevant to
X
that can be inferred from the outputs, is the output of a Gaussian channel with SNR
\lambda
.
∎

Remark 4
.

Proposition
2
implies that

\displaystyle I(X;\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}X+Z_{1},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}X+Z_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(X;\sqrt{\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{n}}X+Z),

(29)

where
Z,Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are i.i.d as
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and independent of the random variable
X
. This formula is at the base of the incremental channel approach in
[
4
]
and
[
6
]
. Our proof based on sufficient statistics gives a cleaner derivation of this result compared to the proofs given in these references, which rely on the language of signal processing.

3.2
Replica method

3.2.1
A quick introduction to the replica method

The replica method, which plays a central role in deriving our result, is based on the following simple formula:

\displaystyle\log Z=\partial_{r=0}Z^{r}.

(30)

In practice, the replica computations are performed assuming
r
to be an integer, but the final result is obtained by sending
r
to zero. Despite this lack of rigor, the replica method can derive results in a much more straightforward manner compared to other methods. Originating from statistical physics, the replica method has been widely used to analyze large information processing systems, notably in
[
5
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
15
]
. In our paper, however, the replica method is applied to a finite system and does not involves either high dimensional limits or the choice of replica ansatz. Instead, the computations are largely combinatorial. Moreover, our main result is not derived entirely from the replica computations, but also from the incremental channel approach.

As a quick illustration of the method, we present here a short and probably new derivation of the classic cumulant-moment relation (
2
) using the replica method.

By the replica trick, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=

\displaystyle\partial_{r=0}\Big[\mathbb{E}\exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}X_{i}\Big)\Big]^{r}.

(31)

By treating
r
as if it were an integer, we can write

\displaystyle\psi_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=

\displaystyle\partial_{r=0}\mathbb{E}\exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sum_{a=1}^{r}X_{ia}\Big),

(32)

where
(X_{ia})_{i=1}^{n}
for
a\in[r]
are independent random vectors with the same distribution as
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. Applying
\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\dots\partial_{\lambda_{n}}
at
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{0}
on both sides of (
32
), we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\partial_{r=0}\mathbb{E}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{r}X_{ia},

(33)

where on the right hand side, we exchanged the operator
\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\dots\partial_{\lambda_{n}}
with
\partial_{r=0}\mathbb{E}
. Expanding the product on the right hand side of this equation and bringing the expectation inside the sum, we have

\displaystyle\kappa(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\partial_{r=0}\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[X_{1a_{1}}X_{2a_{2}}\dots X_{na_{n}}].

(34)

Next we will construct the following map from
[r]^{n}
to the set of all partitions of
[n]
. Given
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in[r]^{n}
, a unique partition
\pi
of
[n]
can be obtained by putting
i
and
j
in the same block if
a_{i}=a_{j}
. Suppose that the blocks of
\pi
are
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1a_{1}}\dots X_{na_{n}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{B_{1}}]\dots\mathbb{E}[X_{B_{k}}],

3.2.2
Mutual information and replicas

Let

,

be random variables with values in

and
\mathcal{Y}
respectively. Suppose that

and

. Let

,

for simplicity. By definition, the mutual information between

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\log p(Y|X)-\mathbb{E}\log p_{Y}(Y).

(39)

Note that the mutual information does not depend on the choice of underlying measures.

Next, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\log p_{Y}(Y)

\displaystyle=\int\nu(dy)p_{Y}(y)\log p_{Y}(y)

(40)

\displaystyle=\partial_{r=1}\int\nu(dy)\,p_{Y}(y)^{r}.

(41)

Treating

as if it were an integer, we write

\displaystyle p_{Y}(y)^{r}=\mathbb{E}[p(y|X)]^{r}=\mathbb{E}[p(y|X_{1})\dots p(y|X_{r})],

(42)

where
X_{a}
for

are independent and identically distributed as
X
. As a result, we obtain the following replica representation of
I(X;Y)

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\log p(Y|X)

\displaystyle-\partial_{r=1}\mathbb{E}\int\nu(dy)p(y|X_{1})\dots p(y|X_{r}).

(43)
