1.
Introduction

Let
\pi(x)\varpropto\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}
be a probability density function, where
U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a potential function.
The classical Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) method is to use the Langevin diffusion processes to approximate the target distribution
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\pi(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
.
More precisely, consider the following stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=-\nabla U(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}B_{t},\ \ X_{0}=x,

(1.1)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion.
Under some regularity assumptions on
U
, it is well known that the above SDE admits a unique solution
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
and

\mu
is an invariant measure of the semigroup
P_{t}
generated by
X_{t}(x)
.
Moreover, under the following dissipativity assumption: for some
c_{0},c_{1}>0
,

\langle x,\nabla U(x)\rangle\geqslant c_{0}|x|^{2}-c_{1},

it is also well known that the law of
X_{t}
exponentially converges to the stationary distribution
\mu
in some sense
as
t\to\infty
(cf.
[
3
,
26
]
), i.e.,
P_{t}
is exponentially ergodic.
In particular, it provides a way to generate samples from
\mu
by the Euler discretization for SDE (
1.1
) (cf.
[
3
]
).
Nowadays, there are numerous works about the analysis of the performance of LMC algorithms (see
[
10
,
11
,
13
]
and references therein).

On the other hand, in statistics physics,
the Langevin diffusion
X_{t}
represents the position of a particle at time
t
which is influenced by some random force, where the random force is usually the sum of many i.i.d. random pulses with
finite
variance.
By the central limit theorem, the sum of the pulses
converges to a Gaussian distribution. While, if the random pulses have
infinite
variance, then the sum of the pulses would converge to an
\alpha
-stable distribution, a typical class of heavy-tailed distribution.
Moreover, it was already shown in
[
28
]
that for
d=1
and
U(x)=|x|^{\beta}
, the diffusion process defined by SDE (
1.1
) is exponentially ergodic if and only if
\beta\geqslant 1
.
Therefore, using continuous Langevin diffusion (
1.1
) to simulate the heavy-tailed distribution is not any more a good choice.
Thus, instead of SDE (
1.1
), it is quite natural to consider the following SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L^{\alpha}_{t},\ \ \alpha\in(0,2),

(1.2)

where
(L^{\alpha}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a
d
-dimensional rotationally invariant
\alpha
-stable process with generator
\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, the usual fractional Laplacian, and

b(x)=-\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-2+\alpha}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}2^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{2-\alpha}{2})}\mathrm{e}^{U(x)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-U(y)}\nabla U(y)}{|x-y|^{d-(2-\alpha)}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=\mathrm{e}^{U(x)}\Delta^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}(\nabla\mathrm{e}^{-U})(x),

where
d\geqslant 2
and
\Delta^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2}}
is the Riesz potential (cf.
[
31
, page117]
). Formally, one sees that

\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-U}-\mathord{{\rm div}}(b\mathrm{e}^{-U})\equiv 0,

which implies that
\mu
is an invariant measure of SDE (
1.2
).
Rigorous theoretical results for the exponential ergodicity of the above SDE (
1.2
) were given in
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
, and therein,
some explicit conditions on
U
are provided.
This approach called Fractional Langevin Monte Carlo was firstly introduced in
[
29
,
32
]
. It has been demonstrated to be useful in modern machine learning both in optimization and heavy-tailed sampling
[
15
,
30
,
36
]
.

Motivated by the above mentioned works, in this paper we consider the following SDE driven by multiplicative
\alpha
-stable noises:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma(X_{t-}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}L^{\alpha}_{t},\ \ X_{0}=x,

(1.3)

where
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
are two Borel measurable functions.
The main contributions of this paper are two-folds:

(i)

Under some dissipativity, non-degeneracy and locally Hölder regularity assumptions on
b
and
\sigma
, we establish
the exponential ergodicity of SDE (
1.3
) for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
, which, in particular, covers the supercritical regime
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

(ii)

For a large class of heavy-tailed distributions, we put forward a new ergodicity SDE driven by multiplicative
\alpha
-stable
processes to simulate it. Compared with the recent work
[
16
]
, our conditions on the potential functions are simpler and easier to realize in the computer.

Now we make the following assumptions on
b
and
\sigma
:

(H
loc
)

For any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is a constant
C_{m}\geqslant 1
such that for all
|x|\leqslant m
and
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

C_{m}^{-1}|\xi|\leqslant|\sigma(x)\xi|\leqslant C_{m}|\xi|.

Moreover, there are
\gamma\in((1-\alpha)^{+},1]
and locally bounded measurable functions
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
|h|\leqslant 1
,

\displaystyle|b(x+h)-b(x)|\leqslant\ell_{1}(x)|h|^{\gamma},\ |\sigma(x+h)-\sigma(x)|\leqslant\ell_{2}(x)|h|^{\gamma}.

(1.4)

(H
{}^{r,q}_{\rm glo}
)

For given
r>-\alpha
, there are
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1]
small enough and
c_{0},c_{1}>0
such that for all
|x|>1
,

\displaystyle\langle x,b(x)\rangle+\varepsilon_{0}\ell_{1}(x)|x|+q(\|\sigma(x)\|+\varepsilon_{0}\ell_{2}(x))^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}\leqslant-c_{0}|x|^{2+r}+c_{1},

(1.5)

where
q
is defined by (
3.1
) below.

Remark 1.1
.

Notice that (
1.4
) is equivalent to the locally
\gamma
-Hölder continuity of
b
and
\sigma
.
If
b,\sigma
are locally Lipschitz, i.e.
\gamma=1
in (
1.4
), then one can take
\varepsilon_{0}=0
in (
1.5
). When
\gamma<1
, in order to adopt the stopping time to localize the coefficients, we need to use the pathwise uniqueness
of strong solutions.
Thus we must mollify the coefficients and require
\varepsilon_{0}>0
in (
1.5
) to have some uniform estimates for the approximation coefficients.
Examples of
b,\sigma
satisfying
(H
{}^{r,q}_{\rm glo}
)
shall be provided below. In particular,
b
and
\sigma
are allowed to be polynomial growth. It is interesting that when
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\sigma
can grow faster than
b
(see Example
1.3
below).

Under the above assumptions, it is well known that there is a unique weak solution to SDE (
1.3
) (see
[
7
]
).
The semigroup associated with the Markov process
X_{t}(x)
is then defined by

P_{t}f(x):=\mathbb{E}f(X_{t}(x)),\ \ f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

By Itô’s formula, one sees that the generator of
P_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}f(x):=\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}f(x)+\langle b(x),\nabla f(x)\rangle,

(1.6)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}f(x)=\frac{\alpha 2^{\alpha}\Gamma(\frac{d+\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{2-\alpha}{2})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[f(x+\sigma(x)z)+f(x-\sigma(x)z)-2f(x)]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z/|z|^{d+\alpha}.

(1.7)

In particular,

\displaystyle\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}f(x)=\frac{\alpha 2^{\alpha}\Gamma(\frac{d+\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{2-\alpha}{2})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[f(x+z)+f(x-z)-2f(x)]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z/|z|^{d+\alpha}.

(1.8)

We call SDE (
1.3
) being supercritical for
\alpha\in(0,1)
since in this case, from the view point of PDE, the drift term plays a dominant role compared with
the diffusion term
{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}
(see
[
7
]
).

Our main result of this paper is

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
r>-\alpha
and
p\in((-r)\vee 0,\alpha)
. Define
V_{p}(x):=1+|x|^{p}
. Suppose that
(H
loc
)
and
(H
{}^{r,q}_{\rm glo}
)
hold.
Then there is a unique invariant probability measure
\mu
associated with
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. Moreover, we have

(i) If
r\geqslant 0
, then there are
\lambda,C_{p}>0
such that for all
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\sup_{\|\varphi/V_{p}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1}|P_{t}\varphi(x)-\mu(\varphi)|\leqslant C_{p}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}V_{p}(x).

(ii) If
r>0
, then there are
\lambda,C>0
such that for all
t>0
,

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|P_{t}(x.\cdot)-\mu\|_{\rm Var}\leqslant C\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}.

Below we provide two examples to illustrate our main result.

Example 1.3
.

The following example shows that
b
and
\sigma
can be polynomial decay and growth:

b(x)=-x(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta/2},\ \ \sigma(x)=(1+|x|^{2})^{\gamma/2}{\mathbb{I}},\ \ \beta\in(-\alpha,\infty),\ \gamma\in(-\infty,1+\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}).

For
|x|>1
, by Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\langle x,b(x)\rangle+q\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}=-|x|^{2}(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}+q(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{\alpha\gamma}{2}}|x|^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant-2^{\frac{\beta}{2}\wedge 0}|x|^{2+\beta}+2^{\frac{\alpha(\gamma\vee 0)}{2}}q|x|^{2-\alpha+\alpha(\gamma\vee 0)}\leqslant-2^{(\frac{\beta}{2}\wedge 0)-1}|x|^{2+\beta}+c_{1}.

In particular, when
\alpha>1
,
\beta
can be less than
-1
so that
b
is polynomial decay. Moreover, for fixed
\beta>0
, when
\alpha\downarrow 0
,
\gamma
can go to infinity.
In other words,
\sigma
can grow faster than
b
. This is not surprise since one can image that for the SDE driven by compound Poisson processes (in some sense, corresponding to
\alpha=0
),
\sigma
can be
arbitrarily growth.

Example 1.4
.

The following example shows that
b
and
\sigma
can even be exponentially growth:

b(x)=-x\mathrm{e}^{|x|},\ \ \sigma(x)=\mathrm{e}^{|x|/\beta}{\mathbb{I}},\ \ \beta\in(\alpha,2).

It is easy to see that for
|h|\leqslant 1
,

|b(x+h)-b(x)|\leqslant(1+|x|)\mathrm{e}^{|x|+1}|h|=:\ell_{1}(x)|h|,

and

|\sigma(x+h)-\sigma(x)|\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{(|x|+1)/\beta}|h|=:\ell_{2}(x)|h|.

Thus for any
|x|>1
and
q>1
, by Young’s inequality, one can choose
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough so that

\displaystyle\langle x,b(x)\rangle+\varepsilon_{0}\ell_{1}(x)|x|+q(\|\sigma(x)\|+\varepsilon_{0}\ell_{2}(x))^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad=-|x|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{|x|}+\varepsilon_{0}(1+|x|)|x|\mathrm{e}^{|x|+1}+q(\mathrm{e}^{|x|/\beta}+\varepsilon_{0}\mathrm{e}^{(|x|+1)/\beta})^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant-|x|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{|x|}+\tfrac{1}{4}|x|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{|x|+1}+2q\mathrm{e}^{\alpha|x|/\beta}|x|^{2-\alpha}\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{2}|x|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{|x|}+c_{1}.

Still, when
\alpha\downarrow 0
, the diffusion
\sigma
can grow faster than drift
b
.

When the driven noise is Brownian motion,
the ergodicity of SDEs (
1.3
) has been studied extensively in the literature
[
2
,
6
,
12
,
33
,
35
]
, etc.,
and the dissipativity condition (
1.5
)
usually takes the following form (for example, see
[
35
, (7.1)]
):

2\langle x,b(x)\rangle+\|\sigma(x)\|^{2}\leqslant-c_{0}|x|^{2}+c_{1}.

In recent years, there also has been a great interest in the study of the ergodicity for SDEs driven by Lévy processes, see
[
18
,
20
,
34
,
35
]
and the references therein.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there exist at least three well developed methods for studying the exponential ergodicity of a Markov process defined through an SDE:
Coupling method (cf.
[
16
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
), Functional inequality method (see
[
2
,
5
,
33
]
),
Meyn-Tweedie’s minorization condition (cf.
[
14
,
23
,
24
,
26
]
).
Each method has its own merits and may be adapted to different situations. For the coupling method, in order to construct a successful coupling it is usually assumed that the following one-sided monotone
condition holds (see
[
21
]
): for some
R>0
,

\langle x-y,\nabla U(x)-\nabla U(y)\rangle\geqslant\begin{cases}-L|x-y|^{2},\quad&|x-y|<R,\\
K|x-y|^{2},\quad&|x-y|\geqslant R.\end{cases}

(1.9)

While, for the functional inequality method, it is usually assumed that the potential function
U
is at least
C^{2}
-smooth (see
[
2
,
33
]
).
The obvious advantage of this method is that the quantitative convergence rate can be calculated from the parameters.
Finally, Meyn-Tweedie’s approach is based on the Lyapunov estimates together with verifying the strong Feller property and irreducibility for the associated semigroup.

To prove our main theorem, we adopt the Meyn-Tweedie method. As we know,
the strong Feller property reflects the regularization effects of the noise, which is usually related to the continuity of the transition density of a Markov process with respect to the starting point.
There are many ways to establish the strong Feller property, e.g., the gradient estimates, Harnack’s inequalities, etc.. For the irreducibility, when the driving noise is Brownian motion, there are several ways for choices:
by solving a control problem
[
24
]
, by Girsanov’s transformation
[
35
]
, by the classical supported theorem of SDE
[
4
]
.
However, when the driven noise is an
\alpha
-stable noise, the above method for irreducibility does not work any more.
In
[
35
]
, we directly verify the positivity of the Dirichlet heat kernel by localization method. Therein, it is restricted to
\alpha\in(1,2)
since in this case,
the heat kernel of the operator (
1.6
) is comparable with the one of the fractional Laplacian
\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
.
In a recent work
[
25
]
, when the conditions in
(H
loc
)
holds globally, i.e., the constant
C_{m}
does not depend on
m
and
\ell_{1}(x),\ell_{2}(x)
are constants,
we obtain the following two-sided estimates for the density of SDE (
1.3
): for any
T>0
and some
C>1
, and for all
t\in(0,T]
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle C^{-1}t(t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+|\theta_{t}(x)-y|)^{-d-\alpha}\leqslant p(t,x,y)\leqslant Ct(t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+|\theta_{t}(x)-y|)^{-d-\alpha},

(1.10)

where
\theta_{t}(x)
solves the following regularized ODE:

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{t}(x)=b*\phi_{t^{1/\alpha}}(\theta_{t}(x)),\ \ \theta_{0}(x)=x,

(1.11)

where
(\phi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is a family of standard mollifiers. The above estimates still allow us to show the irreducibility of
P_{t}
by localization.
For the existence of invariant measures, we use the standard Krylov-Bogoliubov’s theorem by showing some Lyapunov’s type estimate.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall some basic notions about the ergodicity of Markov processes. These notions are standard in literature (see
[
12
,
27
]
). Moreover,
we also prove a general criterion for the irreducibility of a Markov process in terms of the heat kernel estimates, which is motivated by the works of
[
9
,
35
]
.
In Section 3, we prove our main result Theorem
1.2
. For the existence of invariant measures, by the classical Krylov-Bogoliubov’s method, we show the Lyapunov type estimates for
X_{t}(x)
.
For the uniqueness of invariant measure as well as the exponential ergodicity, we show the strong Feller property and the irreducibility of
P_{t}
by the heat kernel estimates
and suitable localization and smoothing arguments. In Section 4, we present an application of our main result to the heavy-tailed sampling. In Appendix, the proof of weak convergence for mollifying SDEs
is provided for readers’ convenience.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we prepare some notions and criterion about the ergodicity and irreducibility of general Markov processes in Euclidean spaces.
Fix
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
{\mathbb{D}}
be the space of all cádlág functions from
[0,\infty)
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, which is endowed with the Skorokhod topology so that
{\mathbb{D}}
becomes a Polish space. Let
X_{t}(\omega):=\omega_{t}
be the coordinate process over
{\mathbb{D}}
. For
s>0
, let
\theta_{s}:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}
be the shift operator:

\theta_{s}(\omega)(t)=\omega(t+s),\ \ t\geqslant 0.

Let
({\mathbb{P}}_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}
be a family of probability measures over
{\mathbb{D}}
so that
{\mathscr{M}}:=\{(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},({\mathbb{P}}_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\}
forms a family of Markov processes with regard to the natural filtration
({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
More precisely,

(i) For each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{0}=x)=1
.

(ii) For each
A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{D}})
,
x\mapsto{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(A)
is Borel measurable.

(iii) For any
s<t
and
A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{D}})
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(A\circ\theta_{s}|{\mathscr{F}}_{s})={\mathbb{P}}_{X_{s}}(A).

(2.1)

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
be the space of all bounded measurable functions over
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. For
t\geqslant 0
and
f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
, the semigroup associated with
{\mathscr{M}}
is defined by

P_{t}f(x):={\mathbb{E}}_{x}f(X_{t}),\ \ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
stands for the expectation with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
.

The following notions are standard and can be found in
[
12
,
26
]
.

\bullet

A probability measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
is called an invariant probability measure of
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, if

\mu(P_{t}f)=\mu(f),\ \ \forall t\geqslant 0,\ f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}).

\bullet

We call
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
ergodic, if there exists a unique invariant probability measure
\mu
of
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, equivalently, for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
f\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}P_{s}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\mu(f).

\bullet

Let
V:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to[1,\infty)
be a measurable function. We call
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be
V
-uniformly exponential ergodic if there are constants
C,\lambda>0

and an invariant probability measure
\mu
such that

\sup_{\|\phi/V\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1}|P_{t}\phi(x)-\mu(\phi)|\leqslant C\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}V(x),\ \ t\geqslant 0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}.

In particular, if
V\equiv 1
, then we call
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be uniformly exponentially ergodic.

\bullet

(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is said to be strong Feller, if
P_{t}f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for any
t>0
and
f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

\bullet

(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is called irreducible, if for any
t>0
, open ball
B
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
P_{t}\mathbf{1}_{B}(x)>0
.

The following general result is taken from
[
14
, Theorem 2.5]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is strong Feller and irreducible. If there are
p,\lambda>0
and
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{x}|X_{t}|^{p}\leqslant C_{1}|x|^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+C_{2},\ \ t\geqslant 0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

then
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is
V
-uniformly ergodic with
V(x)=1+|x|^{p}
. If there are
p,T_{0},C_{3}>0
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{x}|X_{t}|^{p}\leqslant C_{3},\ \ t\geqslant T_{0},\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

then
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is uniformly exponentially ergodic.

Next we present a general criterion for irreducibility in terms of the heat kernel estimates. We believe that it could be used to other cases.

Let
\rho:(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a continuous function with the properties that
for any
t>0
,
r\mapsto\rho(t,r)
is decreasing on
(0,\infty)
, and
for any
0<\delta<T<\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[\delta,T]}\sup_{r>0}\rho(t,r)<\infty.

(2.2)

In the following we make the following assumptions about the Markov process
{\mathscr{M}}
:

(A
1
)

Suppose that for each
t>0
, with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
,
X_{t}
admits a family of transition probability density function
p(t,x,y)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)=\int_{A}p(t,x,y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y,\ \ \forall A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}),\ t>0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}.

(2.3)

Moreover,
t\mapsto X_{t}
is stochastically continuous, equivalently,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{t}\not=X_{t-})=0,\ \ \forall t>0,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}.

(2.4)

(A
2
)

We suppose that for any
T>0
, there are constants
\lambda_{0},C_{0}\geqslant 1
such that for any
t\in(0,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,
and for Lebesgue almost all

,

\displaystyle C_{0}^{-1}\rho(t,\lambda_{0}\Gamma_{t}(x,y))\leqslant p(t,x,y)\leqslant C_{0}\rho(t,\Gamma_{t}(x,y)/\lambda_{0}),

(2.5)

where
\Gamma_{t}(x,y):(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to(0,\infty)
is a measurable function, and for each
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,

(t,x)\mapsto\Gamma_{t}(x,y)
is continuous.

Remark 2.2
.

In
(A
2
)
, for the standard Gaussian case, one usually takes
\rho(t,r)=t^{-d/2}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/t}
and
\Gamma_{t}(x,y)=|x-y|
.
For the
\alpha
-stable case, if
\alpha\in[1,2)
, one takes
\rho(t,r)=t(t^{1/\alpha}+r)^{-d-\alpha}
and
\Gamma_{t}(x,y)=|x-y|
;
and if
\alpha\in(0,1)
, one takes
\Gamma_{t}(x,y)=|x-\theta_{t}(y)|
, where
\theta_{t}(y)
is the ODE flow associated with drift
b
(see (
1.11
) above).
We would like to emphasize that we do not make any continuity assumption about
y\mapsto p(t,x,y)
. Usually, the regularity of the density requires more regular coefficients
in the theory of SDEs.

Let
D
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The exit time of
X_{t}
from
D
is defined by

\tau_{D}:=\inf\big\{t>0,X_{t}\notin D\big\}.

We have the following general result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
D_{0}\Subset D
be two connected domains of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
so that
D
has Lebesgue-zero measure boundary. Suppose that
(A
1
)
and
(A
2
)
hold,
and the above
\Gamma_{t}(x,y)
and
\rho(t,r)
satisfy the following conditions:
there are
t_{0},\delta_{0}>0
small enough and functions
\ell_{0}(t)
,
\ell_{1}(t)
defined on
(0,t_{0}]
such that for all
t\in(0,t_{0}]
,
y_{0}\in D_{0}
,
x,y\in B_{\delta_{0}}(y_{0})\subset D_{0}
and
x^{\prime}\notin D
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}(x,y)\leqslant\ell_{0}(t),\ \Gamma_{t}(x^{\prime},y)\geqslant\ell_{1}(t),

(2.6)

and for the
C_{0},\lambda_{0}
in (
2.5
),
t\mapsto\rho(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0})
is increasing on
(0,t_{0}]
, and

\displaystyle C_{0}^{-1}\rho\big(t,\lambda_{0}\ell_{0}(t)\big)\geqslant 2C_{0}\rho\big(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0}).

(2.7)

Then for any
x_{0},y_{0}\in D_{0}
and
r_{1},r_{2}<{\rm dist}(D_{0},D^{c})
,

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in B_{r_{1}}(x_{0})}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\Big(X_{t}\in B_{r_{2}}(y_{0});t<\tau_{D}\Big)\geqslant c_{0}>0,

(2.8)

where

only depends on
\rho,\lambda_{0},C_{0},t_{0},\delta_{0}
and
r_{1},r_{2}
,
x_{0},y_{0},D_{0},D
.

Proof.

Let
t_{0}
and
\delta_{0}
be as in the assumptions. We divide the proofs into two steps.

(i) In this step we show that for all
y_{0}\in D_{0}
,
\delta\in(0,\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}]
and
t\in(0,t_{0}]
,

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in B_{2\delta}(y_{0})}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\Big(X_{t}\in B_{\delta}(y_{0});t<\tau_{D}\Big)\geqslant C_{0}\rho\big(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0}\big)|B_{\delta}(y_{0})|>0.

(2.9)

Fix
s\in(0,t)
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We define a stopping time
T_{n}
as follows:

T_{n}=\begin{cases}ks2^{-n}\quad&\text{if}\ (k-1)s2^{-n}\leqslant\tau_{D}<ks2^{-n},\\
\infty\quad&\text{if}\ \tau_{D}\geqslant s.\end{cases}

Clearly, on
\{\tau_{D}<s\}
,

s\geqslant T_{n}\downarrow\tau_{D}\ \ \mbox{as $n\to\infty$.}

For any bounded
A\in{\mathscr{B}}(D)
, by the Markov property and (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A;\tau_{D}<s)

\displaystyle=\sum^{2^{n}}_{k=1}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A;(k-1)s2^{-n}\leqslant\tau_{D}<ks2^{-n})

\displaystyle=\sum^{2^{n}}_{k=1}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A;T_{n}=ks2^{-n})

\displaystyle=\sum^{2^{n}}_{k=1}\mathbb{E}_{x}\Big({\mathbb{P}}_{X_{T_{n}}}(X_{t-T_{n}}\in A);T_{n}=ks2^{-n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum^{2^{n}}_{k=1}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{A}p(t-T_{n},X_{T_{n}},y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y;T_{n}=ks2^{-n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{A}p(t-T_{n},X_{T_{n}},y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y;\tau_{D}<s\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DD2}}}{{\leqslant}}C_{0}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{A}\rho\big(t-T_{n},\Gamma_{t-T_{n}}(X_{T_{n}},y)/\lambda_{0}\big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y;\tau_{D}<s\right).

Letting
n\to\infty
and by (
2.2
) and the dominated convergence theorem, we get

\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A;\tau_{D}<s)\leqslant C_{0}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{A}\rho\big(t-\tau_{D},\Gamma_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)/\lambda_{0}\big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y;\tau_{D}<s\right).

Below we take
A=B_{\delta}(y_{0})
. For
y\in B_{\delta}(y_{0})
, since
X_{\tau_{D}}\notin D
and

r\to\rho(t-\tau_{D},r)
is decreasing,
t\mapsto\rho(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0})
is increasing,
by (
2.6
) we have

\rho\big(t-\tau_{D},\Gamma_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)/\lambda_{0}\big)\leqslant\rho\big(t-\tau_{D},\ell_{1}(t-\tau_{D})/\lambda_{0}\big)\leqslant\rho\big(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0}\big).

Hence, for any
s<t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}{\Big(}X_{t}\in B_{\delta}(y_{0});\tau_{D}<s{\Big)}\leqslant C_{0}\int_{B_{\delta}(y_{0})}\rho\big(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0}\big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

On the other hand, by (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}=t)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}=t,X_{t}\in\partial D)+{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\tau_{D}=t,X_{t}\notin\partial D,X_{s}\in D,s<\tau_{D})

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{t}\in\partial D)+{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{t}\not=X_{t-})=0.

Thus, by the lower bound in (
2.5
) and the above estimates, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}{\Big(}X_{t}\in B_{\delta}(y_{0});t<\tau_{D}{\Big)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x}{\Big(}X_{t}\in B_{\delta}(y_{0}){\Big)}-\mathbb{P}_{x}{\Big(}X_{t}\in B_{\delta}(y_{0});\tau_{D}<t{\Big)}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\delta}(y_{0})}p(t,x,y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y-\lim_{s\uparrow t}\mathbb{P}_{x}{\Big(}X_{t}\in B_{\delta}(y_{0});\tau_{D}<s{\Big)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B_{\delta}(y_{0})}\Big(C_{0}^{-1}\rho\big(t,\lambda_{0}\Gamma_{t}(x,y)\big)-C_{0}\rho\big(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0}\big)\Big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

For
x\in B_{2\delta}(y_{0}),y\in B_{\delta}(y_{0})
, since
r\to\rho(t,r)
is decreasing, by (
2.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}{\Big(}X_{t}\in B_{\delta}(y_{0});t<\tau_{D}{\Big)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\Big(C_{0}^{-1}\rho\big(t,\lambda_{0}\ell_{0}(t)\big)-C_{0}\rho\big(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0}\big)\Big)|B_{\delta}(y_{0})|

\displaystyle\!\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{AA2}}}{{\geqslant}}C_{0}\rho\big(t,\ell_{1}(t)/\lambda_{0}\big)|B_{\delta}(y_{0})|.

Thus we get (
2.9
).

(ii) By (
2.3
), there is a density
p^{D}(t,x,y)
so that for each
A\in{\mathscr{B}}(D)
,

\int_{A}p^{D}(t,x,y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A;t<\tau_{D}).

Moreover, by the Markov property, we have for each
0<s<t
,

\displaystyle\int_{A}p^{D}(t,x,y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=\int_{D}p^{D}(s,x,z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z\int_{A}p^{D}(t-s,z,y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

(2.10)

Indeed, by
s+\tau_{D}\circ\theta_{s}=\tau_{D}
on
\{s<\tau_{D}\}
and (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A;t<\tau_{D})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{x}\Big(\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(X_{t}\in A;t<\tau_{D}|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{x}\Big(\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(X_{t-s}\circ\theta_{s}\in A;t<s+\tau_{D}\circ\theta_{s},s<\tau_{D}|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{x}\Big(\mathbb{P}_{X_{s}}\big(X_{t-s}\in A;t-s<\tau_{D}\big);s<\tau_{D}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}p^{D}(s,x,z)\int_{A}p^{D}(t-s,z,y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z.

Let
x_{0}\in D_{0}
. Since
D_{0}
is connected, there is a continuous curve
{\mathcal{C}}\subset D_{0}
connecting
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
. Let

\delta:=({\rm dist}(D_{0},\partial D)\wedge\delta_{0})/4.

It is easy to see that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough and
\{x_{i},i=0,1,\cdots,n+1\}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
such that

t\leqslant nt_{0},\ \ x_{i}\in B_{\delta}(x_{i-1}),\ i=1,\cdots,n+1,\ B_{\delta}(x_{n+1})\subset B_{r_{2}}(y_{0}).

For each
j=1,\cdots,n+1
, since
B_{\delta}(x_{j-1})\subset B_{2\delta}(x_{j})
, by what we have proved in Step (i),

\displaystyle\inf_{y_{j-1}\in B_{\delta}(x_{j-1})}\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{j})}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},y_{j-1},y_{j}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{j}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{y_{j-1}\in B_{2\delta}(x_{j})}\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{j})}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},y_{j-1},y_{j}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{j}

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{0}\rho\big(\tfrac{t}{n},\ell_{1}(\tfrac{t}{n})/\lambda_{0}\big)|B_{\delta}(y_{0})|>0.

Hence, for all
x\in B_{\delta}(x_{0})
, by (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{2}}(y_{0})}p^{D}(t,x,y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=\int_{D}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},x,y_{1}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}\cdots\int_{D}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},y_{n-1},y_{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{n}\int_{B_{r_{2}}(y_{0})}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},y_{n},y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{1})}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},x,y_{1}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}\cdots\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{n})}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},y_{n-1},y_{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{n}\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{n+1})}p^{D}(\tfrac{t}{n},y_{n},y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\Big(C_{0}\rho\big(\tfrac{t}{n},\ell_{1}(\tfrac{t}{n})/\lambda_{0}\big)|B_{\delta}(y_{0})|\Big)^{n+1}.

Thus,

\inf_{x\in B_{\delta}(x_{0})}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\Big(X_{t}\in B_{r_{2}}(y_{0});t<\tau_{D}\Big)\geqslant\Big(C_{0}\rho\big(\tfrac{t}{n},\ell_{1}(\tfrac{t}{n})/\lambda_{0}\big)|B_{\delta}(y_{0})|\Big)^{n+1}>0.

For general
r_{1}\in(0,{\rm dist}(D_{0},D))
, it follows by the finitely covering technique.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
by verifying the conditions in Theorem
2.1
.
Below we fix

r>-\alpha,\ \ p\in((-r)\vee 0,\alpha),

and define

\displaystyle V_{p}(x):=(1+|x|^{2})^{p/2},\quad q:=\frac{2^{4+2\alpha}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d+\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})^{2}\Gamma(\frac{2-\alpha}{2})}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}+\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha-p)p}\right).

(3.1)

First of all, we show that
V_{p}
is a Lyapunov function of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
b,\sigma
are locally bounded and for some
c_{0},c_{1}>0
,

\displaystyle q\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}+\langle x,b(x)\rangle\leqslant-c_{0}|x|^{2+r}+c_{1},\ \ |x|>1.

(3.2)

Then there are
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1}>0
such that for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}V_{p}(x)={\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}V_{p}(x)+\langle b(x),\nabla V_{p}(x)\rangle\leqslant-\kappa_{0}V_{p}(x)^{1+\frac{r}{p}}+\kappa_{1}.

(3.3)

Proof.

It suffices to prove (
3.3
) for
|x|>1
. By (
1.7
), we make the following decomposition:

\displaystyle c^{-1}_{d,\alpha}{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}V_{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{|z|\leqslant\frac{|x|}{2\|\sigma(x)\|}}\Big[V_{p}(x+\sigma(x)z)+V_{p}(x-\sigma(x)z)-2V_{p}(x)\Big]\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z}{|z|^{d+\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\int_{|z|>\frac{|x|}{2\|\sigma(x)\|}}\Big[V_{p}(x+\sigma(x)z)+V_{p}(x-\sigma(x)z)-2V_{p}(x)\Big]\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z}{|z|^{d+\alpha}}=:I_{1}+I_{2},

where
c_{d,\alpha}:=\frac{\alpha 2^{\alpha}\Gamma(\frac{d+\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{2-\alpha}{2})}
.
For
I_{1}
, denoting by
A(x):=(1+|x|^{2}){\mathbb{I}}-(2-p)x\otimes x
, we have

\nabla^{2}V_{p}(x)=p(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-2}A(x).

Noting that for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|\langle\xi,A(x)\xi\rangle|\leqslant(1+|x|^{2})|\xi|^{2}+(2-p)|\langle\xi,x\rangle|^{2}\leqslant 3(1+|x|^{2})|\xi|^{2}.

By Taylor’s expansion, for
|z|\leqslant\frac{|x|}{2\|\sigma(x)\|}
, we have

\displaystyle|V_{p}(x+\sigma(x)z)+V_{p}(x-\sigma(x)z)-2V_{p}(x)|

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant 3p|\sigma(x)z|^{2}\int^{1}_{0}\int^{1}_{-1}(1+|x+s_{1}s_{2}\sigma(x)z|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r_{1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant 3p\|\sigma(x)\|^{2}|z|^{2}\Big(1+\tfrac{|x|^{2}}{4}\Big)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}.

Hence, for
S_{d}:=2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})
,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3p\|\sigma(x)\|^{2}\Big(1+\tfrac{|x|^{2}}{4}\Big)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\int_{|z|\leqslant\frac{|x|}{2\|\sigma(x)\|}}|z|^{2-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z

\displaystyle=3pS_{d}\|\sigma(x)\|^{2}\Big(1+\tfrac{|x|^{2}}{4}\Big)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\int_{0}^{\frac{|x|}{2\|\sigma(x)\|}}r^{1-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{p2^{2+\alpha}S_{d}}{2-\alpha}\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{p-\alpha}.

For
I_{2}
, noting that

|(1+|x+z|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}-(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}|\leqslant||x+z|^{2}-|x|^{2}|^{\frac{p}{2}}\leqslant 2^{\frac{p}{2}}|x|^{\frac{p}{2}}|z|^{\frac{p}{2}}+|z|^{p},

we have

\displaystyle I_{2}\leqslant 2\int_{|z|>\frac{|x|}{2\|\sigma(x)\|}}\Big[2^{\frac{p}{2}}|x|^{\frac{p}{2}}|\sigma(x)z|^{\frac{p}{2}}+|\sigma(x)z|^{p}\Big]\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z}{|z|^{d+\alpha}}\leqslant\frac{2^{3+\alpha}S_{d}}{\alpha-p}\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{p-\alpha}.

Combining the above calculations, we get for
|x|>1
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}V_{p}(x)\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{3+\alpha}pS_{d}c_{d,\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{2-\alpha}+\frac{1}{(\alpha-p)p}\right)\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{p-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle p2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}q\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{p-\alpha}

Thus, then by (
3.2
) and
\nabla V_{p}(x)=px(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-1}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}V_{p}(x)+\langle b(x),\nabla V_{p}(x)\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant p2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}q\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{p-\alpha}+p(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\langle x,b(x)\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant p(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\Big(q\|\sigma(x)\|^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}+\langle x,b(x)\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant p(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\big(-c_{0}|x|^{2+r}+c_{1}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\kappa_{0}(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{p+r}{2}}+\kappa_{1}=-\kappa_{0}V_{p}(x)^{1+\frac{r}{p}}+\kappa_{1},

where in the second inequality we use
(1+|x|^{2})^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\leqslant 2^{1-\frac{p}{2}}|x|^{2-p}
with
|x|>1
, and
\kappa_{0}=pc_{0}
.
∎

As a consequence of the above Lyapunov-type estimate, we have the following result (see
[
35
, Lemma 7.1]
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X_{t}(x)
be any weak solution of SDE (
1.3
) starting from
x
.
Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.1
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}V_{p}(X_{s}(x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right]^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int^{t}_{0}V_{p}(X_{s}(x))^{1+\frac{r}{p}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\lesssim_{C}V_{p}(x)+t,

(3.4)

and for the
\kappa_{0}
in (
3.3
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}V_{p}(X_{t}(x))\leqslant\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{0}t}V_{p}(x)+C,\ \ &r=0,\\
&C(1+t^{-p/r}),\ \ &r>0.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.5)

Proof.

Let
N(t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)
be the Poisson random measure associated with
L^{\alpha}_{t}
, i.e.,

N(t,\Gamma):=\sum_{s\in(0,t]}{\mathbf{1}}_{\Gamma}(L^{\alpha}_{s}-L^{\alpha}_{s-}),\ \ \Gamma\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Let
{\widetilde{N}}(t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z):=N(t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)-t|z|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z
be the compensated Poisson random measure.
By Lévy-Itô’s decomposition, one can write

\displaystyle L^{\alpha}_{t}=\int_{|z|<1}z{\widetilde{N}}(t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)+\int_{|z|\geqslant 1}zN(t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z).

Thus SDE (
1.3
) can be written as

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\int_{|z|<1}\sigma(X_{t-})z{\widetilde{N}}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)+\int_{|z|\geqslant 1}\sigma(X_{t-})zN({\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z).

(3.6)

By Itô’s formula (see
[
1
, Theorem 4.4.7]
) and (
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle V_{p}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=V_{p}(x)+\int^{t}_{0}\Big[\langle\nabla V_{p}(X_{s}),b(X_{s})\rangle+{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}V_{p}(X_{s})\Big]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+M_{t}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leqslant V_{p}(x)+\int^{t}_{0}\Big[-\kappa_{0}V_{p}(X_{s})^{1+\frac{r}{p}}+\kappa_{1}\Big]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+M_{t},

(3.8)

where
M_{t}
is a local cádlág martingale. Let
\tau_{n}
be a sequence of stopping times localizing
M_{t}
, i.e.,
M_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}
is a martingale and
\tau_{n}\uparrow\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Then we have

{\mathbb{E}}V_{p}(X_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})+\kappa_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\int^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}_{0}V_{p}(X_{s})^{1+\frac{r}{p}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant V_{p}(x)+\kappa_{1}t.

In particular, letting
n\to\infty
and by Fatou’s lemma, we obtain that for all
t\geqslant 0
,

{\mathbb{E}}V_{p}(X_{t})+\kappa_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int^{t}_{0}V_{p}(X_{s})^{1+\frac{r}{p}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant V_{p}(x)+\kappa_{1}t.

Moreover, starting from (
3.8
), by stochastic Gronwall’s inequality (
[
35
, Lemma 3.7]
), we also have

\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}V_{p}(X_{s}(x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right]^{2}\lesssim_{C}V_{p}(x)+t.

On the other hand, starting from (
3.7
), as above, we have

{\mathbb{E}}V_{p}(X_{t})=V_{p}(x)+\int^{t}_{0}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\langle\nabla V_{p}(X_{s}),b(X_{s})\rangle+{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}V_{p}(X_{s})\Big]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

For
r\geqslant 0
, by (
3.3
) and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}\mathbb{E}V_{p}(X_{t})\leqslant-\kappa_{0}\mathbb{E}\Big[V_{p}(X_{t})^{1+\frac{r}{p}}\Big]+\kappa_{1}\leqslant-\kappa_{0}\Big[\mathbb{E}V_{p}(X_{t})\Big]^{1+\frac{r}{p}}+\kappa_{1}.

Let
f(t):=\mathbb{E}V_{p}(X_{t})
. The above inequality then reads

f(t)^{\prime}\leqslant-\kappa_{0}f(t)^{1+\frac{r}{p}}+\kappa_{1}.

If
r=0
, then

\displaystyle(\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{0}t}f(t))^{\prime}\leqslant\kappa_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{0}t}\Rightarrow f(t)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{0}(s-t)}f(s)+\tfrac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{0}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{0}(s-t)}),\ \ t>s\geqslant 0.

(3.9)

If
r>0
, then by Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle(t^{\frac{2p}{r}}f(t))^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leqslant\tfrac{2p}{r}t^{\frac{2p}{r}-1}f(t)-\kappa_{0}t^{\frac{2p}{r}}f(t)^{1+\frac{r}{p}}+\kappa_{1}t^{\frac{2p}{r}}\leqslant Ct^{\frac{p}{r}-1}+\kappa_{1}t^{\frac{2p}{r}}.

Integrating both sides from
0
to
t
, we obtain

t^{\frac{2p}{r}}f(t)\leqslant\tfrac{rC}{p}t^{\frac{p}{r}}+\tfrac{r\kappa_{1}}{r+2p}t^{\frac{2p}{r}+1}\Rightarrow f(t)\leqslant\tfrac{rC}{p}t^{-\frac{p}{r}}+\tfrac{r\kappa_{1}}{r+2p}t,

which together with (
3.9
) yields (
3.5
).
∎

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a smooth density function with support in the unit ball. Define

\phi_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-d}\phi(\varepsilon^{-1}x),\ \ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\ \varepsilon\in(0,1),

and

b_{\varepsilon}(x):=b*\phi_{\varepsilon}(x),\quad\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x):=\sigma*\phi_{\varepsilon}(x).

By
(H
loc
)
, it is easy to see that for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle|b_{\varepsilon}(x)-b_{\varepsilon}(y)|+\|\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)-\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y)\|\leqslant C_{m+1}|x-y|^{\gamma},\ \ \forall x,y\in B_{m},

(3.10)

and for some
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,1)
depending on
m
, and for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{1})
,

\displaystyle C_{m}^{-1}|\xi|\leqslant|\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)\xi|\leqslant C_{m}|\xi|,\ \ \forall x\in B_{m},\ \xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(3.11)

Moreover, note that by (
1.4
),

\displaystyle|b_{\varepsilon}(x)-b(x)|\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|b(x-y)-b(x)|\rho_{\varepsilon}(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\leqslant\varepsilon^{\gamma}\ell_{1}(x),

and

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)-\sigma(x)|\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\sigma(x-y)-\sigma(x)|\rho_{\varepsilon}(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\leqslant\varepsilon^{\gamma}\ell_{2}(x).

Hence, by
(H
{}^{r,q}_{\rm glo}
)
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon^{1/\gamma}_{0})
,

\displaystyle\langle x,b_{\varepsilon}(x)\rangle+q|\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle x,b(x)\rangle+\varepsilon^{\gamma}|x|\ell_{1}(x)+q(\|\sigma(x)\|+\ell_{2}(x)\varepsilon^{\gamma})^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle x,b(x)\rangle+\varepsilon_{0}|x|\ell_{1}(x)+q(\|\sigma(x)\|+\ell_{2}(x)\varepsilon_{0})^{\alpha}|x|^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant-c_{0}|x|^{2+r}+c_{1}.

(3.12)

Now, consider the following approximation SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b_{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma_{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon}_{t-}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}L^{\alpha}_{t},\quad X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x.

(3.13)

Since the coefficients are smooth, but, may be not bounded, by (
3.12
) and
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
, there is a unique strong solution
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)
to the above SDE.
Moreover, for any
f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))={\mathbb{E}}f(X_{t}(x)),

(3.14)

which is proven in Appendix. Notice that if
b
and
\sigma
are locally Lipschitz, then it is not necessary to mollify
b
and
\sigma
.
It suffices to consider the truncated
b
and
\sigma
as done below.

Let
\chi
be a cutoff function with

\chi(x)=1,\ \ |x|\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2},\ \ \chi(x)=0,\ \ |x|>1.

For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, set

\chi_{m}(x):=\chi(x/m),\ \ b^{\varepsilon}_{m}(x):=b_{\varepsilon}(x)\chi_{m}(x),\quad\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{m}(x):=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x\chi_{m}(x)).

By (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), it is easy to see that
b^{\varepsilon}_{m}
and
\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{m}
satisfy the following global assumptions:

\displaystyle|b^{\varepsilon}_{m}(x)-b^{\varepsilon}_{m}(y)|+\|\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{m}(x)-\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{m}(y)\|\leqslant C_{m}|x-y|^{\gamma},\ \ \forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(3.15)

and

\displaystyle C_{m}^{-1}|\xi|\leqslant|\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{m}(x)\xi|\leqslant C_{m}|\xi|,\ \ \forall x,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(3.16)

where the constant does not depend on
\varepsilon
.
Let
\theta_{t}(x)
solve the following ODE:

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{t}(x)=(b^{\varepsilon}_{m}*\phi_{t^{1/\alpha}})(\theta_{t}(x)),\quad\theta_{0}(x)=x.

(3.17)

We also consider the following SDE with cutoff coefficients:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}=b^{\varepsilon}_{m}(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{m}(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t-}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}L^{\alpha}_{t},\quad X^{\varepsilon,m}_{0}=x.

(3.18)

Let
X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x)
be the unique strong solution of the above SDE. By
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
,
X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x)
admits a density
p^{\varepsilon}_{m}(t,x,y)
with the following estimates:
For any
T>0
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
there is a constant
C_{0}\geqslant 1
depending on
m
, but
independent of

\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{1})
, such that for all
t\in(0,T]
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon}_{m}(t,x,y)\asymp_{C_{0}}t(t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+|x-\theta_{t}(y)|)^{-d-\alpha},

(3.19)

and

\displaystyle|\nabla\log p^{\varepsilon}_{m}(t,x,\cdot)|(y)\leqslant C_{0}t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(3.20)

For any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in B_{m}
, define the exit time of
X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x)
from
B_{m}
by

\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}:=\inf\big\{t>0:X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)\notin B_{m}\big\}.

By the uniqueness of strong solution, we have

\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)=X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x),\quad t<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}.

(3.21)

Lemma 3.3

(
Strong Feller property).

For any
t>0
and bounded measurable function
f
, the function
x\mapsto P_{t}f(x)
is bounded continuous.

Proof.

By a standard monotonic argument, it suffices to show that for any
t>0
,

\lim_{x\to y}\sup_{f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1}|\mathbb{E}(f(X_{t}(x))-f(X_{t}(y)))|=0.

Since for any
f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}(f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(y)))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DD9}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}(f(X_{t}(x))-f(X_{t}(y))),

furthermore, we only need to prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to y}\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1}|\mathbb{E}(f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(y)))|=0.

(3.22)

Given
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, let
m>|x|\vee|y|
. For given
f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}(f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(y)))|

\displaystyle\leqslant\big|\mathbb{E}\big[(f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(y)))\mathbf{1}_{t<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\wedge\tau^{\varepsilon,y}_{B_{m}}}\big]\big|+2\mathbb{P}\big(t\geqslant\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\wedge\tau^{\varepsilon,y}_{B_{m}}\big)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:loc}}}{{=}}\big|\mathbb{E}\big[(f(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(y)))\mathbf{1}_{t<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\wedge\tau^{\varepsilon,y}_{B_{m}}}\big]\big|+2\mathbb{P}\big(t\geqslant\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\wedge\tau^{\varepsilon,y}_{B_{m}}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\big|\mathbb{E}\big[(f(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(y))\big]\big|+2\mathbb{P}\big(t\geqslant\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\wedge\tau^{\varepsilon,y}_{B_{m}}\big).

By (
3.20
), we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}(f(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(y)))|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(z)(p^{\varepsilon}_{m}(t,x,z)-p^{\varepsilon}_{m}(t,x,z)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}|x-y|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int^{1}_{0}|\nabla p^{\varepsilon}_{m}(t,x+r(y-x),z)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{m}t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}|x-y|\int^{1}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\varepsilon}_{m}(t,x+r(y-x),z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=C_{m}t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}|x-y|.

Moreover, by Chebyshev’s inequality and (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(t\geqslant\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}})\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\leqslant t}|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}(x)|\geqslant m\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leqslant t}|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}(x)|^{p/2}\right)/m^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(V_{p}(x)+t)^{1/2}/m^{p/2}.

(3.23)

Combining the above calculations, we obtain that for any
f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
,

|\mathbb{E}(f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))-f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(y)))|\leqslant C_{m}t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}|x-y|+C_{0}(V_{p}(x)+V_{p}(y)+t)^{1/2}/m^{p/2},

where
C_{0}
does not depend on
m
.
Thus we obtain (
3.22
) by first letting
x\to y
and then
m\to\infty
.
∎

Lemma 3.4

(
Irreducibility).

For any
x_{0},y_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r,t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}(x)\in B_{r}(y_{0}))>0.

(3.24)

Proof.

Since
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)
weakly converges to
X_{t}(x)
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, for any open set
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t}(x)\in\overline{A})\geqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)\in A).

(3.25)

Below we fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
small enough, and
x,y_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
r>0
.
Let
m>2
be big enough such that
\{x\}\cup B_{r}(y_{0})\subset B_{m-2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)\in B_{r}(y_{0}))

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{P}{\Big(}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)\in B_{r}(y_{0});t<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}{\Big)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:loc}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}{\Big(}X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x)\in B_{r}(y_{0});t<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}{\Big)}.

(3.26)

In order to use Theorem
2.3
, we choose

\rho(t,r)=t(t^{1/\alpha}+r)^{-d-\alpha}.

Clearly, by (
3.19
), estimate (
2.5
) is satisfied for
\Gamma_{t}(x,y)=|x-\theta_{t}(y)|
. It remains to find
t_{0}
and
\delta_{0}
small enough as well as functions
\ell_{0}(t)
and
\ell_{1}(t)

so that the conditions in Theorem
2.3
are satisfied for domains
D_{0}=B_{m-2}
and
D=B_{m}
.
Note that by (
3.17
) and the definition of
b^{\varepsilon}_{m}
, for all
t\in[0,1]
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|\theta_{t}(y)-y|\leqslant\int^{t}_{0}|(b^{\varepsilon}_{m}*\phi_{s^{1/\alpha}})(\theta_{s}(y))|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant\sup_{|x|\leqslant m+1}|b(x)|\cdot t=:C_{1}t.

Let

\displaystyle\delta_{0}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\wedge{\rm dist}(y_{0},D_{0}),\ \ t_{0}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2C_{1}}\wedge\tfrac{\delta_{0}}{C_{1}}.

(3.27)

For
x^{\prime}\notin D
and
y\in B_{\delta_{0}}(y_{0})\subset D_{0}
, we have for
t\in(0,t_{0}]
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}(x^{\prime},y)=|x^{\prime}-\theta_{t}(y)|

\displaystyle\geqslant|x^{\prime}-y_{0}|-|y-y_{0}|-|\theta_{t}(y)-y|\geqslant 2-\delta_{0}-C_{1}t\geqslant 1.

On the other hand, for
x,y\in B_{\delta_{0}}(y_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}(x,y)=|x-\theta_{t}(y)|\leqslant|x-y|+|\theta_{t}(y)-y|\leqslant 2\delta_{0}+C_{1}t\leqslant 3\delta_{0}.

(3.28)

Now, for the
C_{0}
in (
3.19
), one can choose
\delta_{0},t_{0}>0
small enough such that (
3.27
) holds and

t\mapsto t/(t^{1/\alpha}+1)^{d+\alpha}\mbox{ is increasing on $(0,t_{0}]$,}

and

\displaystyle\frac{C_{0}^{-1}t}{(t^{1/\alpha}+3\delta_{0})^{d+\alpha}}>\frac{2C_{0}t}{(t^{1/\alpha}+1)^{d+\alpha}},\ \ t\in(0,t_{0}].

(3.29)

In particular, (
2.7
) is satisfied. Thus by Theorem
2.3
, we conclude

\mathbb{P}{\Big(}X^{\varepsilon,m}_{t}(x)\in B_{r}(y_{0});t<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}{\Big)}\geqslant c_{0},

where
c_{0}
is independent of
\varepsilon
. This, together with (
3.25
) and (
3.26
), yields

\mathbb{P}(X_{t}(x)\in\overline{B_{r}(y_{0})})\geqslant c_{0}.

The proof is thus complete by the strong Feller property that
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}(X_{t}(x)\in B_{r}(y_{0}))
is continuous.
∎

Now, we are in a position to give

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First of all, by (
3.4
) and the standard Krylov-Bogoliubov method (see
[
12
, Theorem 11.7]
), there is an invariant probability
measure
\mu
associated with
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
The uniqueness follows by the strong Feller property and irreducibility. The exponential convergence (i) and (ii) follow by (
3.5
), Theorem
2.1
and Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
.
∎

4.
Application to heavy-tailed sampling

In this section we introduce an application of Theorem
1.2
to the heavy-tailed sampling.
Let
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
, where
U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a continuous function.
Suppose that there are
\beta,C_{0}>0
such that for all
|x|\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle U(x)\geqslant(d+\beta)\log|x|-C_{0}.

(4.1)

The above assumption means that
\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}
has a polynomial decay rate as
|x|\to\infty
, which is usually called
the heavy-tailed distribution compared with the exponential or light tailed distribution.
Below we want to find an ergodic SDE so that the law of the solution
X_{t}
exponentially converges to
\mu
in some sense
as
t\to\infty
. Thus, one can sample
\mu
theoritically from
X_{t}
when
t
is large.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,2)
. To construct an ergodic SDE driven by
\alpha
-stable process, we introduce a vector field
B:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by

B(x):=c_{d,\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y[\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)-\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)]|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y,

where
c_{d,\alpha}=2^{\alpha}\Gamma(\frac{d+\alpha}{2})/\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{2-\alpha}{2})
and

\varrho_{\alpha}(x):=(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}}.

The vector field
B
enjoys the following important property.

Lemma 4.1
.

(i)

B\in C^{\infty}_{0}
satisfies that for some
\kappa_{0}=\kappa_{0}(d,\alpha)>0
,

\displaystyle|B(x)|\leqslant\kappa_{0}|x|^{1-d-\alpha},\ \ |\nabla B(x)|\leqslant\kappa_{0}|x|^{-d-\alpha}.

(4.2)

(ii)

There is a constant
\kappa_{1}>0
only depending on
d,\alpha
such that for all
|x|>1
,

\displaystyle\langle x,B(x)\rangle\leqslant-\kappa_{1}|x|^{2-d-\alpha}.

(4.3)

(iii)

\mathord{{\rm div}}B(x)=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\varrho_{\alpha}(x)
.

Proof.

(i) Since
\varrho_{\alpha}\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and for any
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\|\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha}/\varrho_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}<\infty,

we clearly have
B\in C^{\infty}_{0}
. To show the bounds (
4.2
), let
\chi:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
be a cutoff function with

\chi(r)=1,\ \ r\in[0,\tfrac{1}{4}],\ \ \chi(r)=0,\ \ r\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}].

Define

\varphi_{|x|}(y):=y|y|^{-d-\alpha}\chi\big(\tfrac{|y|}{|x|}\big),\ \ \widetilde{\varphi}_{|x|}(y):=y|y|^{-d-\alpha}\big(1-\chi\big(\tfrac{|y|}{|x|}\big)\big).

For
j=0,1
, by definition we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{j}B(x)

\displaystyle=c_{d,\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\Big(\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)-\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)\Big)|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=c_{d,\alpha}(I_{1}+I_{2}),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi_{|x|}(y)\Big(\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)-\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)\Big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{|x|}(y)\Big(\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)-\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)\Big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

For
I_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi_{|x|}(y)|y|\left(\int^{1}_{-1}|\nabla^{j+1}\varrho_{\alpha}(x+sy)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{|y|\leqslant\frac{|x|}{2}}|y|^{2-d-\alpha}\left(\int^{1}_{-1}(1+|x+sy|)^{-(d+\alpha+1+j)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|x|)^{-(d+\alpha+1+j)}\int_{|y|\leqslant\frac{|x|}{2}}|y|^{2-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|x|)^{-(d+\alpha+1+j)}|x|^{2-\alpha}\lesssim|x|^{1-j-d-2\alpha}.

For
I_{2}
, by the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\widetilde{\varphi}_{|x|}(x-y)-\widetilde{\varphi}_{|x|}(x+y)\Big)(\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha})(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=|x|^{d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\widetilde{\varphi}_{|x|}(|x|(\bar{x}-y))-\widetilde{\varphi}_{|x|}(|x|(\bar{x}+y))\Big)(\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha})(|x|y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=|x|^{1-\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x}-y)-\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x}+y)\Big)(\nabla^{j}\varrho_{\alpha})(|x|y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y,

where
\bar{x}=x/|x|
and in the last step we have used

\widetilde{\varphi}_{|x|}(|x|y)=|x|^{1-d-\alpha}y|y|^{-d-\alpha}\big(1-\chi\big(|y|\big)\big)=|x|^{1-d-\alpha}\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(y).

For
j=0
, since
|\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(y)|\leqslant|y|^{1-d-\alpha}{\mathbf{1}}_{|y|>1/4}\leqslant 4^{d+\alpha-1}
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\cdot 4^{d+\alpha-1}|x|^{1-\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|(1+|x||y|)^{-(d+\alpha)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\lesssim|x|^{1-\alpha-d}.

For
j=1
, since
\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{y}\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, by the integration by parts, we also have

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle=|x|^{-\alpha}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x}-y)-\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x}+y)\Big)\nabla_{y}\varrho_{\alpha}(|x|y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\right|

\displaystyle=|x|^{-\alpha}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\mathord{{\rm div}}_{y}\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x}-y)-\mathord{{\rm div}}_{y}\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x}+y)\Big)\varrho_{\alpha}(|x|y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{y}\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}\|_{\infty}|x|^{-\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|(1+|x||y|)^{-(d+\alpha)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\lesssim|x|^{-\alpha-d}.

Combining the above estimates we obtain (
4.2
).

(ii) By definition and the symmetry, we have

\displaystyle-\langle x,B(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{d,\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle[\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)-\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)]|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=2c_{d,\alpha}\int_{\langle x,y\rangle\geqslant 0}\langle x,y\rangle[\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)-\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)]|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

For
|x|\geqslant 1
and
\langle x,y\rangle\geqslant 0
, by the mean-valued formula, we have

\displaystyle\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)-\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)=(1+|x-y|^{2})^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}}-(1+|x+y|^{2})^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=2(d+\alpha)\langle x,y\rangle\int^{1}_{0}\big(1+s(|x-y|^{2}-|x+y|^{2})+|x+y|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\quad=2(d+\alpha)\langle x,y\rangle\int^{1}_{0}\big(1-4s\langle x,y\rangle+|x+y|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\quad=2(d+\alpha)\langle x,y\rangle\int^{1}_{0}\big(1+4s\langle x,y\rangle+|x-y|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\quad\geqslant 2(d+\alpha)\langle x,y\rangle\int^{|x|^{-2}}_{0}\big(1+4s\langle x,y\rangle+|x-y|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\quad\geqslant 2(d+\alpha)\langle x,y\rangle\big(1+4|x|^{-2}\langle x,y\rangle+|x-y|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}|x|^{-2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle-\langle x,B(x)\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{4(d+\alpha)c_{d,\alpha}}{|x|^{2}}\int_{\langle x,y\rangle\geqslant 0}\langle x,y\rangle^{2}\big(1+4|x|^{-2}\langle x,y\rangle+|x-y|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{4(d+\alpha)c_{d,\alpha}}{|x|^{2}}\int_{\langle x,y\rangle\geqslant 0,|x-y|\leqslant 1}\langle x,y\rangle^{2}\big(1+4|x|^{-2}\langle x,y\rangle+|x-y|^{2}\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

Since for
|x|\geqslant 1
,

|x-y|\leqslant 1\Rightarrow\tfrac{|y|^{2}}{2}\leqslant\langle x,y\rangle\leqslant|x|^{2}+|x|,

we further have

\displaystyle-\langle x,B(x)\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{4(d+\alpha)c_{d,\alpha}}{|x|^{2}}\int_{|x-y|\leqslant 1}\langle x,y\rangle^{2}\big(2+4(1+|x|^{-1})\big)^{-\frac{d+\alpha}{2}-1}|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{4(d+\alpha)c_{d,\alpha}}{|x|^{2}10^{\frac{d+\alpha}{2}+1}}\int_{|x-y|\leqslant 1}\langle x,y\rangle^{2}|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\geqslant\kappa_{1}|x|^{2-d-\alpha},

where
\kappa_{1}>1
only depends on
d,\alpha
. Thus we obtain (
4.3
).

(iii) By definition and the integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\mathord{{\rm div}}B(x)

\displaystyle=c_{d,\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle y,\nabla_{x}[\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)-\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)]\rangle|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=c_{d,\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle y,\nabla_{y}[\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)+\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)-2\varrho_{\alpha}(x)]\rangle|y|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=-c_{d,\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathord{{\rm div}}(y|y|^{-d-\alpha})[\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)+\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)-2\varrho_{\alpha}(x)]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y.

Since for
y\not=0
,

\mathord{{\rm div}}(y|y|^{-d-\alpha})=d|y|^{-d-\alpha}+\langle y,\nabla|y|^{-d-\alpha}\rangle=-\alpha|y|^{-d-\alpha},

we have by (
1.8
) and
c_{d,\alpha}=2^{\alpha}\Gamma(\frac{d+\alpha}{2})/\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{2-\alpha}{2})
,

\mathord{{\rm div}}B(x)=\alpha c_{d,\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{[\varrho_{\alpha}(x+y)+\varrho_{\alpha}(x-y)-2\varrho_{\alpha}(x)]}{|y|^{d+\alpha}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\varrho_{\alpha}(x).

The proof is complete.
∎

In this appendix we sketch the proof of weak convergence (
3.14
). First of all, by (
3.12
) and (
3.4
), there is a constant
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
T>0
,

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,T]}V_{p}(X^{\varepsilon}_{s}(x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right]^{2}\lesssim_{C}V_{p}(x)+T.

(5.1)

For
\theta\in(0,T)
, let
\eta,\eta^{\prime}
be two stopping times with
0\leqslant\eta\leqslant\eta^{\prime}+\theta\leqslant T
.
For any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(|X^{\varepsilon}_{\eta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{\eta^{\prime}}|\geqslant\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{P}}\left(|X^{\varepsilon}_{\eta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{\eta^{\prime}}|\geqslant\delta;T<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(T\geqslant\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(|X^{\varepsilon,m}_{\eta}-X^{\varepsilon,m}_{\eta^{\prime}}|\geqslant\delta;T<\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(T\geqslant\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{P}}\left(|X^{\varepsilon,m}_{\eta}-X^{\varepsilon,m}_{\eta^{\prime}}|\geqslant\delta\right)+2{\mathbb{P}}\left(T\geqslant\tau^{\varepsilon,x}_{B_{m}}\right).

By SDE (
3.18
) and (
3.15
), (
3.16
), it is by now standard to derive that for fixed
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\downarrow 0}\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{0\leqslant\eta\leqslant\eta^{\prime}+\theta\leqslant T}{\mathbb{P}}\left(|X^{\varepsilon,m}_{\eta}-X^{\varepsilon,m}_{\eta^{\prime}}|\geqslant\delta\right)=0,

which together with (
3.23
) yields that for any
T,\delta>0
,

\lim_{\theta\downarrow 0}\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{0\leqslant\eta\leqslant\eta^{\prime}+\theta\leqslant T}{\mathbb{P}}\left(|X^{\varepsilon}_{\eta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{\eta^{\prime}}|\geqslant\delta\right)=0.

Thus, by Aldous’ criterion (see
[
17
, p.356, Theorem 4.5]
), the law
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\varepsilon}
of
(X^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot},L^{\alpha}_{\cdot})
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
in
{\mathbb{D}}\times{\mathbb{D}}
is tight.
Let
{\mathbb{Q}}
be any accumulation point of
({\mathbb{Q}}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}

\varepsilon_{k}\to 0

({\mathbb{Q}}^{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}:=({\mathbb{Q}}^{\varepsilon_{k}})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

{\mathbb{Q}}

there is a probability space
(\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{\mathscr{F}},\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})
and
{\mathbb{D}}\times{\mathbb{D}}

(\widetilde{X}^{k},\widetilde{L}^{k})

(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{L})

(\widetilde{X}^{k},\widetilde{L}^{k})\to(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{L})\ \mbox{ in ${\mathbb{D}}\times{\mathbb{D}}$},\ \ \widetilde{\mathbb{P}}-a.s.,

and

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\circ(\widetilde{X}^{k},\widetilde{L}^{k})^{-1}={\mathbb{Q}}^{k},\ \ \widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\circ(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{L})^{-1}={\mathbb{Q}}.

Moreover,
\widetilde{L}^{k}
