Closed-form estimators for an exponential family derived from likelihood equations
Roberto Vila, Eduardo Nakano, Helton Saulo

Abstract
In this paper, we derive closed-form estimators for the parameters of some probability distributions belonging to the exponential family. A bootstrap bias-reduced version of these proposed closed-form estimators are also derived. A Monte Carlo simulation is performed for the assessment of the estimators. The results are seen to be quite favorable to the proposed bootstrap bias-reduce estimators.

Introduction

The maximum likelihood (ML) method is certainly the most popular technique for obtaining point estimates of the parameters of a distribution. However, it is rare for multi-parameter probability distributions to have closed-form expressions for the ML estimators. Although numerical optimization algorithms are used to solve ML estimation problems that have no closed-form expressions, the availability of a closed-form estimator can avoid some difficulties that can occur when using iterative methods. For example, the problems of estimation in the limits of parametric space can cause the non-convergence of the algorithm and the requirement to adopt a multi-start approach increases computing time. In fact, the low computational cost is the main advantage of closed-form estimators, especially in the field of real-time processing.

In this context, the aim of this work is to provide closed-form estimators for the parameters of a parametric set of probability distributions that belongs to the exponential family
(
Efron,, 2022
)
of the following form

\displaystyle f(x;\psi)=h(x)\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\theta_{j}(\psi)T_{j}(x)-\log(c(\theta(\psi)))\right),\quad x>0,\ \psi=(\mu,\sigma),\ \mu,\sigma>0,

where
\theta(\psi)=(\theta_{1}(\psi),\theta_{2}(\psi))
,

T_{1}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a
real strictly monotone twice differentiable function,

\displaystyle h(x)={|T_{1}^{\prime}(x)|\over T_{1}(x)},\quad\theta_{1}(\psi)=-\mu\sigma,\quad\theta_{2}(\psi)=\mu,\quad T_{2}(x)=\log(T_{1}(x)),

with

c(\theta(\psi))={\Gamma(\mu)/(\sigma^{\mu}\mu^{\mu})}

being the normalizing constant.
In simple terms,
f(x;\psi)
has the following simple form

\displaystyle f(x;\psi)={(\mu\sigma)^{\mu}\over\Gamma(\mu)}\,{|T_{1}^{\prime}(x)|\over T_{1}(x)}\,\exp\left\{-\mu\sigma T_{1}(x)+\mu\log(T_{1}(x))\right\},\quad x>0,\ \psi=(\mu,\sigma),\ \mu,\sigma>0.

(1)

In the above,
T_{1}^{\prime}(x)
denotes the derivative of
T_{1}(x)
with respect to
x
.
Note that a family of distributions equivalent to (
1
) has appeared in
Nascimento et al., (2014)
.

Since many of them are derived from generalized gamma-type models, they naturally inherit potential applications in areas where the gamma, Weibull, and exponential distributions are commonly employed, including reliability analysis, survival studies, hydrology, actuarial science, and income modeling. In this sense, the practical relevance of these new distributions is supported by the extensive literature and applications associated with their parent distributions. A detailed empirical assessment of these new models is beyond the scope of the present work and represents an interesting direction for future research.

Several studies have presented closed-form estimators derived from likelihood equations.
Ye and Chen, (2017)
derived a closed-form estimator of the parameters of the gamma distribution from generalised gamma distribution. The generalized gamma can be obtained by a power transformation

, where

2
A generalization of exponential family

It is worth noting that the simple power transformation
Y=X^{1/p}
,
p>0
, will be used repeatedly throughout the paper to generate additional members of the family introduced in Proposition
2.1
. Although this transformation is well known, its combination with Proposition
2.1
provides a convenient way to obtain new distributions within the proposed framework. As will be seen in Section
3
, many of these models also admit simple likelihood equations and closed-form estimators.

By using the increasing transformation
Y=X^{1/p}
,
p>0
, considered in
Ye and Chen, (2017)
and
Cheng and Beaulieu, (2002)
, where
X
has the probability density function (PDF) in (
1
), it is clear that the PDF of
Y
can be written as

\displaystyle f(y;\psi,p)=p\,{(\mu\sigma)^{\mu}\over\Gamma(\mu)}\,{|T_{1}^{\prime}(y^{p})|y^{p-1}\over T_{1}(y^{p})}\,\exp\left\{-\mu\sigma T_{1}(y^{p})+\mu\log(T_{1}(y^{p}))\right\},\quad y>0,

(2)

where
\psi=(\mu,\sigma)
and
\mu,\sigma,p>0
.

2.1
Stochastic representation

A random variable
X
is said to have a Gamma distribution with shape
parameter
\alpha>0
and rate parameter
\lambda>0
, denoted by

X\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)
, if its PDF is given by

f_{X}(x)=\frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\lambda x),\quad x>0.

In this parametrization,

\mathbb{E}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda},\quad\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda^{2}}.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma})
then
Y
has the stochastic representation

Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}[T^{-1}_{1}(Z)]^{1/p},

with
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
being equality in distribution and
T^{-1}_{1}
denoting the inverse function of
T_{1}
.

Conversely, if
Y
is distributed according (
2
), then

Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}T_{1}(Y^{p})\sim{\rm Gamma}(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma})

Proof.

Let
Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma})
. A simple observation shows that (for
y>0
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}([T^{-1}_{1}(Z)]^{1/p}\leqslant y)=\mathbb{P}(T^{-1}_{1}(Z)\leqslant y^{p})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(Z\leqslant T_{1}(y^{p})),&\text{if}\ T_{1}\ \text{is increasing},\\[5.69046pt]
1-\mathbb{P}(Z\leqslant T_{1}(y^{p})),&\text{if}\ T_{1}\ \text{is decreasing}.\end{cases}

(3)

Taking the derivative with respect to
y
both sides, the PDF of
[T^{-1}_{1}(Z)]^{1/p}
is written as

\displaystyle f_{[T^{-1}_{1}(Z)]^{1/p}}(y)

\displaystyle=py^{p-1}|T^{\prime}_{1}(y^{p})|f_{Z}(y^{p};\mu,1/(\mu\sigma)),\quad Z\sim{\rm Gamma}\left(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=f(y;\psi,p).

This completes the proof.
∎

2.2
Quantiles

Given
p\in(0,1)
, denote by
Q_{Y}(p)
the
p
-quantile of a random variable
Y
distributed according (
2
).

In what follows we find simple expressions for
Q_{Y}(p)
. Indeed,
from Proposition
2.1
and from (
3
), we have

\displaystyle p=\mathbb{P}(Y\leqslant Q_{Y}(p))=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(Z\leqslant T_{1}(Q_{Y}^{p}(p))),&\text{if}\ T_{1}\ \text{is increasing and}\ Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma}),\\[5.69046pt]
\mathbb{P}\big({Z^{*}}\leqslant{1\over T_{1}(Q_{Y}^{p}(p))}\big),&\text{if}\ T_{1}\ \text{is decreasing and}\ Z^{*}={1\over Z}\sim{\rm Inv}$-${\rm Gamma}(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma}).\end{cases}

This shows that

\displaystyle Q_{Z}(p)=T_{1}(Q_{Y}^{p}(p))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q_{Y}(p)=[T_{1}^{-1}(Q_{Z}(p))]^{1/p},

whenever
T_{1}
is increasing, and

\displaystyle Q_{Z^{*}}(p)={1\over T_{1}(Q_{Y}^{p}(p))}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q_{Y}(p)=\left[T_{1}^{-1}\left({1\over Q_{Z^{*}}(p)}\right)\right]^{1/p},

whenever
T_{1}
is decreasing.

2.3
Moments

Proposition 2.2
.

If
Y
is distributed according (
2
), then the positive order moments of
Y
, denoted by
\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})
,
0<q<ps,
exist
whenever
T_{1}(x)\geqslant Cx^{s}
,
x>0
,
T_{1}
increasing, for some
C>0
and
s>0
.

Proof.

By using the well-known formula

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})=q\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(Y>y){\rm d}y,\quad Y>0,\ q>0,

for
Y
distributed according (
2
),
we have (for some
0<a<\infty
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})

\displaystyle=q\int_{0}^{a}y^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(Y>y){\rm d}y+q\int_{a}^{\infty}y^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(Y>y){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leqslant a^{q}+q\int_{a}^{\infty}y^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(Y>y){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=a^{q}+q\int_{a}^{\infty}y^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(Z>T_{1}(y^{p})){\rm d}y,\quad Z\sim{\rm Gamma}\left(\mu,{1\over\mu\sigma}\right),

(4)

where in the last line we have used (
3
). By Markov’s inequality, the expression in (
4
) is at most

\displaystyle a^{q}+q\mathbb{E}(Z)\int_{a}^{\infty}{y^{q-1}\over T_{1}(y^{p})}{\rm d}y\leqslant a^{q}+{q\mathbb{E}(Z)\over C}\int_{a}^{\infty}{1\over y^{ps-q+1}}{\rm d}y,

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
T_{1}(x)\geqslant Cx^{s}
,
x>0
.
For
ps>q
the last integral converges. Hence the existence of the positive order moments of
Y
follows.
∎

.

Note that the generator

Proposition 2.4
.

If
Y
is distributed according (
2
) with

T_{1}(x)=\log(x^{s}+1)
,
x>0
, for some
s\neq 0
, then the real moments of
Y
, denoted by
\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})
,
q<\min\{0,ps\}
, exist.

Proof.

As a by-product from the proof of Proposition
2.2
, it is sufficient to prove that

\displaystyle I=\int_{a}^{\infty}{y^{q-1}\over T_{1}(y^{p})}{\rm d}y<\infty,

for some
0<a<\infty
. Indeed, by using the inequality
\log(x)>1-1/x
we have
T_{1}(x)\geqslant x^{s}/(x^{s}+1)
. Hence

\displaystyle I\leqslant\int_{a}^{\infty}{1\over y^{-q+1}}{\rm d}y+\int_{a}^{\infty}{1\over y^{ps-q+1}}{\rm d}y<\infty,

provided
q<\min\{0,ps\}
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Propositions
2.2
and
2.4

illustrate how the form of the transformation function
T_{1}

affects the tail behaviour of the resulting distribution and,
consequently, the existence of moments. Proposition
2.2

provides a simple sufficient condition for the existence of positive moments
for a broad class of models, whereas Proposition
2.4

shows that, for the specific choice
T_{1}(x)=\log(x^{s}+1)
,
the range of existing moments can be characterized explicitly.

.

Note that Proposition
2.4
includes the generators
T_{1}(x)
of Burr Type XII and Dagum distributions (see Table

).

Proposition 2.7
.

If
Y
is distributed according (
2
) with
T_{1}(x)=Cx^{-s}
,

, for some
C>0
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})=\left({\mu\sigma\over C}\right)^{q\over ps}\,\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\mu-{q\over ps}\right)}{\Gamma(\mu)},\quad\text{where}\ \mu-{q\over ps}>0.

Proof.

By using Proposition
2.1
, we have
\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})=\mathbb{E}\{[T^{-1}_{1}(Z)]^{q/p}\}=C^{-q/(ps)}\mathbb{E}[Z^{-q/(ps)}]
,
for

Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma})
.
Since
\mathbb{E}(X^{\nu})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lambda^{-\nu}}\Gamma(\nu+\alpha)/\Gamma(\alpha)
for
Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lambda})

3
The new estimators

Let
\{Y_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
be a univariate random sample of size
n
from
Y
, where
Y
has PDF given in (
2
).

The (random) likelihood function for
(\psi,p)
is written as

\displaystyle L(\psi,p)=p^{n}\,{(\mu\sigma)^{n\mu}\over\Gamma^{n}(\mu)}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}{|T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})|Y_{i}^{p-1}\over T_{1}(Y_{i}^{p})}\,\exp\left\{-\mu\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i}))\right\}.

Consequently, the (random) log-likelihood function for
(\psi,p)
is written as

\displaystyle\log(L(\psi,p))

\displaystyle=n\log(p)+{n\mu}\log(\mu)+{{n\mu}\log(\sigma)-n\log(\Gamma(\mu))}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(|T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})|)+(p-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(Y_{i})-\mu\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})+(\mu-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})).

A simple calculus shows that the elements of the (random) score vector are given by

\displaystyle{\partial\log(L(\psi,p))\over\partial\mu}

\displaystyle=n\log(\mu)+n\log(\sigma)+n-n\psi^{(0)}(\mu)-\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})),

\displaystyle{\partial\log(L(\psi,p))\over\partial\sigma}

\displaystyle={n\mu\over\sigma}-\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i}),

\displaystyle{\partial\log(L(\psi,p))\over\partial p}

\displaystyle={n\over p}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{T_{1}^{\prime\prime}(Y^{p}_{i})\over T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})}\,Y^{p}_{i}\log(Y_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(Y_{i})

\displaystyle-\mu\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}^{\prime}(Y^{p}_{i})Y^{p}_{i}\log(Y_{i})+(\mu-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{T_{1}^{\prime}(Y^{p}_{i})\over T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})}\,Y^{p}_{i}\log(Y_{i}),

where
\psi^{m}(x)=\partial^{m+1}\log(\Gamma(x))/\partial x^{m+1}
is the polygamma function of order
m
. Setting these equal to zero and solving the system of equations gives the ML estimators of
(\psi,p)
.
In particular, by resolving
{\partial\log(L(\psi,p))/\partial\sigma}=0
, we can express
\sigma
as a function of
p
:

\displaystyle{\sigma}(p)={n\over\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y_{i}^{{p}})}.

(5)

Substitute (
5
) into
{\partial\log(L(\psi,p))/\partial p}=0
to give

\displaystyle{\mu}(p)=\dfrac{{n\over p}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(Y_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[{T_{1}^{\prime\prime}(Y^{p}_{i})\over T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})}-{T_{1}^{\prime}(Y^{p}_{i})\over T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})}\right]Y^{p}_{i}\log(Y_{i})}{{\sigma}(p)\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}^{\prime}(Y^{p}_{i})Y^{p}_{i}\log(Y_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}{T_{1}^{\prime}(Y^{p}_{i})\over T_{1}(Y^{p}_{i})}\,Y^{p}_{i}\log(Y_{i})}.

(6)

Furthermore, notice that

{p}
is obtained solving the non-linear equation

\displaystyle\log({\mu}(p))-\psi^{(0)}({\mu}(p))=\log\left({1\over\sigma(p)}\right)-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(T_{1}(Y^{{p}}_{i})).

(7)

Now, return to the distribution in (
1
). We take
p=1
in (
5
) and (
6
) to obtain the new estimators for
\mu
and
\sigma
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}={1\over{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y_{i})}

(8)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}=\dfrac{{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y_{i})\left\{1+{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(Y_{i})+{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[{T_{1}^{\prime\prime}(Y_{i})\over T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i})}-{T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i})\over T_{1}(Y_{i})}\right]Y_{i}\log(Y_{i})\right\}}{{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\log(Y_{i})-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y_{i})\ {1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{T_{1}^{\prime}(Y_{i})\over T_{1}(Y_{i})}\,Y_{i}\log(Y_{i})}.

(9)

Note that the ML estimator of
\sigma
, denoted by
\widehat{\sigma}_{\rm MLE}
, satisfies the identity
\widehat{\sigma}_{\rm MLE}=\widehat{\sigma}
. Furthermore,
the ML estimator of
\mu
, denoted by

\displaystyle\log({\mu})-\psi^{(0)}({\mu})=\log\left({1\over\sigma}\right)-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(T_{1}(Y_{i})).

(10)

Proposition 3.1
.

The equation (
10
) is an unbiased estimating equation for
\mu
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\log({\mu})-\psi^{(0)}({\mu})=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left({1\over\sigma}\right)-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(T_{1}(Y_{i}))\right].

Proof.

The proof is immediate since

T_{1}(Y_{i})\sim{\rm Gamma}(\mu,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu\sigma})
(Proposition
2.1
)
and

\mathbb{E}[\log(T_{1}(Y_{i}))]=\psi^{(0)}(\mu)-\log(\mu\sigma)
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

The solution in
\mu
to equation (
10
) exists and is unique.

Proof.

Combining (
10
) with (
8
), equation (
10
) is written as

\displaystyle\log({\mu})-\psi^{(0)}({\mu})=\log\left({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{1}(Y_{i})\right)-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(T_{1}(Y_{i})).

(11)

We denote the right-hand side of (
11
) by
H(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
.
By using the Binet’s second integral for the gamma function
(
Mathar,, 2023
)
:

\displaystyle\log(x)-\psi^{(0)}(x)={1\over 2x}+2\int_{0}^{\infty}{t\over(t^{2}+x^{2})[\exp(2\pi t)-1]}{\rm d}t,

(12)

note that
\log({\mu})-\psi^{(0)}({\mu})
is a positive monotone decreasing function of
\mu
with a finite limit (
=0
) as
\mu\to\infty
. Since
H(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
is non-negative (by Jensen’s inequality) and is constant in
\mu

For illustrative purposes, we only present the results for the Nakagami, gamma, new log-generalized gamma and scaled inverse chi-squared distributions. We then propose a bootstrap bias-reduced version of the proposed ML estimators, as they are biased
(

Appendix A
Some technical results

In this section, we provide auxiliary technical results and asymptotic expansions for expectations of smooth functions of gamma random variables that facilitate the verification of the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let

Y\sim{\rm Gamma}(\mu,1),

with
\mu>0
. Then, for every
r>0
and every integer
m>r
,

\mathbb{E}|Y-\mu|^{r}\leqslant C_{m}^{\,r/m}\mu^{r/2},

for some constant
C_{m}>0
depending only on
m
.

Proof.

Let

W={(Y-\mu)}/{\sqrt{\mu}}

denote the standardized version of
Y
.
Since the cumulants of
W
are

\kappa_{1}(W)=0,

\kappa_{2}(W)=1,

and, for every
j\geqslant 3
,

\kappa_{j}(W)=(j-1)!\,\mu^{1-j/2},

we have

\sup_{\mu\geqslant 1}|\kappa_{j}(W)|\leqslant(j-1)!,\quad j\geqslant 2.

Since every moment of order
m
can be expressed as a polynomial in the
first
m
cumulants (see, e.g., the complete Bell polynomial formula),
for every integer
m\geqslant 1
there exists a constant
C_{m}>0
such that

\sup_{\mu\geqslant 1}\mathbb{E}|W|^{m}\leqslant C_{m}.

Now let
r>0
and choose an integer
m>r
. By Lyapunov’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}|W|^{r}\leqslant(\mathbb{E}|W|^{m})^{r/m}\leqslant C_{m}^{\,r/m}.

Finally, since
W={(Y-\mu)}/{\sqrt{\mu}}
, the proof follows.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of random variables such that

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=\mu,

\sigma_{n}^{2}={\rm Var}(X_{n})\to 0.

Assume that there exists
\delta>0
such that

\mathbb{E}|X_{n}-\mu|^{2+\delta}=O\mskip-3.0mu\left(\sigma_{n}^{2+\delta}\right).

If
g
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of
\mu
, then

\mathbb{E}[g(X_{n})]=g(\mu)+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}{2}\,\sigma_{n}^{2}+o(\sigma_{n}^{2}).

Proof.

By Taylor’s theorem,

\mathbb{E}[g(X_{n})]=g(\mu)+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\mu)}{2}\,\sigma_{n}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[R(X_{n})],

where

R(x)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[g^{\prime\prime}(\mu+\theta(x)(x-\mu))-g^{\prime\prime}(\mu)\bigr](x-\mu)^{2},\quad\theta(x)\in(0,1).

It remains to prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R(X_{n})]=o(\sigma_{n}^{2}).

(26)

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Since
g^{\prime\prime}
is continuous at
\mu
, there exists

\eta>0
such that

|y-\mu|<\eta\ \Longrightarrow\ |g^{\prime\prime}(y)-g^{\prime\prime}(\mu)|<\varepsilon.

Write

A_{n}=\{|X_{n}-\mu|\leqslant\eta\}.

Then

|\mathbb{E}[R(X_{n})]|\leqslant\mathbb{E}\mskip-3.0mu\left[|R(X_{n})|\,\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mskip-3.0mu\left[|R(X_{n})|\,\mathds{1}_{A_{n}^{c}}\right].

For the first term,

\mathbb{E}\mskip-3.0mu\left[|R(X_{n})|\,\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-\mu)^{2}]=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\sigma_{n}^{2}.

Since
g^{\prime\prime}
is bounded on a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\mu
,
there exists
M>0
such that

|g^{\prime\prime}(y)-g^{\prime\prime}(\mu)|\leqslant M

on that neighborhood. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\mskip-3.0mu\left[|R(X_{n})|\,\mathds{1}_{A_{n}^{c}}\right]\leqslant\frac{M}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\mskip-3.0mu\left[(X_{n}-\mu)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{|X_{n}-\mu|>\eta\}}\right].

Using

(X_{n}-\mu)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{|X_{n}-\mu|>\eta\}}\leqslant\eta^{-\delta}|X_{n}-\mu|^{2+\delta},

we obtain

\mathbb{E}\mskip-3.0mu\left[(X_{n}-\mu)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{|X_{n}-\mu|>\eta\}}\right]\leqslant\eta^{-\delta}\mathbb{E}|X_{n}-\mu|^{2+\delta}\leqslant\eta^{-\delta}\{\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-\mu)^{2}]\}^{1+\delta/2}=O\mskip-3.0mu\left(\sigma_{n}^{2+\delta}\right)=o(\sigma_{n}^{2}),

where the last inequality follows from Lyapunov’s inequality.

Consequently,

|\mathbb{E}[R(X_{n})]|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\sigma_{n}^{2}+o(\sigma_{n}^{2}).

Dividing by
\sigma_{n}^{2}
and taking the upper limit,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\mathbb{E}[R(X_{n})]|}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, (
26
) follows. This completes the proof.
∎
