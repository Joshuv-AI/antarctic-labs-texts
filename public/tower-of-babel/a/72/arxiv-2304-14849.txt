Spectroscopic signature of spin triplet odd-valley superconductivity in two-dimensional materials
T.H. Kokkeler, Chunli Huang, F.S. Bergeret, I. V. Tokatly

Abstract
Motivated by recent discoveries of superconductivity in lightly-doped multilayer graphene systems, we present a low-energy model to study superconductivity in 2D materials whose Fermi surface consists of two valleys at ± 𝑲 \pm\boldsymbol{K} -points. We assume a triplet odd-valley superconducting order with a pair potential that is isotropic in each valley but has a different sign in the two different valleys. Our theory predicts the emergence of an almost flat band of edge states centered at zero energy for certain edge orientations. As a result, a prominent experimental signature of this type of superconductivity is the presence of a large zero-energy peak in the local density of states near specific edges. The results of the effective low-energy theory are confirmed by numerically analyzing a specific microscopic tight-binding realization of odd-valley superconductivity, f-wave superconductivity on a honeycomb lattice in a ribbon geometry. Our work provides a test for odd-valley superconductivity through edge spectroscopy.

The Fermi surface of lightly-doped multilayer graphene systems is centered at the two valleys
(K,K^{\prime})
, i.e. the two inequivalent corners of the hexagonal Billouin zone.
When
\Delta
changes sign in a single valley and has nodal points on the Fermi surface,
the density-of-states (DOS) decreases continuously to zero as the energy approaches the Fermi level.
Such DOS profile can be probed by scanning-tunneling spectroscopy and leads to a
V-
shaped tunneling spectrum
Kashiwaya and Tanaka 2000
;
Kim et al. 2022
. By contrast,
if there are no nodes on the Fermi surface, it leads to the usual
U

\Delta
in the two valleys because in either case the excitation energy of all Bogoliubov quasiparticles is gapped. However, as we demonstrate below, the superconducting gap of an odd-valley superconductor can close at the boundary of the two-dimensional materials while the spectrum of an even-valley superconductor remains gapped. Moreover, the dispersion of the edge states in an odd-valley superconductor is anomalously flat, with
\omega\sim\Delta^{2}/\mu
where
\mu
is the chemical potential and these in gap states lead to a large local DOS as shown in Fig.
1
. On the contrary, for even-valley superconductors there are no edge states and a zero energy peak is absent, see Fig.
1
. Since the LDOS can be probed using STM measurements, our calculations provide a tool for identifying odd-valley superconductivity.

In what follows, we calculate the edge state dispersion of a superconductor with spin-triplet odd-valley pairing, under the assumptions that
\Delta\ll\mu
and that the Fermi surface consists of a single band. Using a universal property of Fermi liquids – the excitation energy is particle-hole symmetric at the Fermi surface – we demonstrate that the edge spectrum is macroscopically concentrated around zero energy.
In the second part of the Letter,
we use a tight-binding Hamiltonian to calculate the edge state dispersion and to confirm the results of our low-energy theory.

Low energy model:–

We start with a general 2D multiband superconductor described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\alpha}\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}&\Delta_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\\
\Delta_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}&-\xi_{\alpha,-\boldsymbol{k}}\end{bmatrix}\otimes|u_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\langle u_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}|\;\;.

(1)

Here
\xi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}},\Delta_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}
are the quasiparticle energy and pair potential and
\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}
are the band index and crystal momentum respectively,
|u_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\langle u_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}|

G_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)=(\omega-H)^{-1}

\displaystyle G_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{(\omega\mathbf{1}_{\tau}-\xi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\tau_{3}+\Delta_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\tau_{1})}{\omega^{2}-\xi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}-\Delta_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\otimes|u_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\langle u_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}|,

We are interested in describing the bound states at the edge of an odd-valley superconductor, that is, a superconductor whose pair potential has a different sign in each valley.
The sharp edge, located at the line

Wave functions

\displaystyle|\psi(x)\rangle=V\tau_{3}G_{x,k_{y}}(\omega)|\psi(x=0)\rangle.

Here

2

\displaystyle G_{x,k_{y}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{BZ}e^{ik_{x}x}G_{k_{x},k_{y}}(\omega)\frac{dk_{x}}{2\pi}

In the red case, the lines of integration may cross the Fermi contour only in a single valley. More interesting is the orientation of the edge corresponding to the blue lines in Fig.

In this case, the line of integration may cross either none, or both valleys.
In the former case, one can verify from the Lippmann-Schwinger equation that no edge states appear. Therefore, we focus on values of

To compute the integral over

(7)

The above results can be directly applied to graphene or other materials with hexagonal lattices.
Armchair edges with
\Delta\boldsymbol{K}=2K_{x}\hat{x}
, correspond to the blue lines of integration in Fig.
2
. In this case, our theory predicts the existence of a flat band of zero-energy edge states. This will manifest as a large peak in the density of states, localized at the edge over a coherence length.
On the other hand, zigzag edges with
\Delta\boldsymbol{K}=2K_{y}\hat{y}

2

These two features can be used to unequivocally characterize the odd-valley superconductivity in graphene-like materials. The superconductors described by our low-energy model are topologically trivial, since though they are odd-parity, the Fermi surface does not enclose time-reversal invariant momenta
Sato et al. 2011

Phong et al. 2023

Lattice model–

Since the ribbon Hamiltonian we consider is invariant under the mirror-plane
H(k_{y})=H(-k_{y})
, the counter-propagating edge states are located on the same position:
x_{0}(k_{y})=x_{0}(-k_{y})

\langle\psi_{0,k_{y}}|\tau_{3}V|\psi_{0,-k_{y}}\rangle\neq 0

The states are robust against smooth disorder, since this does not mix the opposite valleys and thus leaves our conclusions unaltered. Sharp disorder such as vacancies on the other hand have zero-energy states themselves following a mechanism very similar to edges. Therefore, as long as their density is not too high, the zero energy peak in the density of states remains. We also verified numerically using our tight-binding model that the resulting energy shift of the bound states is small as long as the density of edge vacancies not too large. Thus, the large zero energy peak is robust against edge impurities.

Conclusions.-

We have presented an effective low-energy theory to analyze odd-valley superconductivity in 2D materials that have a Fermi surface split into two valleys. This type of unconventional pairing is allowed by symmetry and involves an odd-parity pair potential that has an opposite sign in different valleys but remains isotropic within each valley. Our model predicts the existence of an almost flat band of edge states if the normal to the edge is such that the line of integration in k-space passes through both valleys. To confirm this prediction, we have also studied a tight-binding Hamiltonian for f-wave superconductivity in honeycomb lattices. Our findings showed massively degenerate edge states that appear as a pronounced zero energy peak in the density of states accessible through local spectroscopic techniques.

Our study has broad applicability to materials with hexagonal or triangular lattices, including graphene, NbSe,
\text{MoS}_{2}

Lu et al. 2015
, nitrides
Yamanaka et al. 1996
;
Yamanaka et al. 1998
, germanene
Xi et al. 2022
, and silicene
Zhao et al. 2016
.

Appendix A
Hamiltonian

In the main text, we use the low-energy Hamiltonian given by Eq. 1 of the main body. In this section, we elaborate on the structure of this Hamiltonian and show how this Hamiltonian can be obtained under general assumptions. We assume that the superconductivity is not nematic, that is, the crystal symmetry is respected and there are only two non-equivalent Dirac points. We consider zero-momentum Cooper pairs and triplet superconductivity. Lastly, we assume that the pair potential varies slowly with momentum on the scale of the Fermi momentum.

We introduce the following eight-component spinors, indexed by
\boldsymbol{k}
which satisfies
|\boldsymbol{k}|\ll|\boldsymbol{K}|

\displaystyle\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}\uparrow}\\
\psi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}\downarrow}\\
\psi_{\alpha,-\boldsymbol{k}\downarrow}^{\dagger}\\
-\psi_{\alpha,-\boldsymbol{k}\uparrow}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\otimes|u_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{K}+\delta\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle,

(11)

where
|u_{\boldsymbol{K}+\delta\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle
is the Bloch function at momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
.
The single particle energy is described by
\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for electrons, and thus
-\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for holes. We assume that the superconductivity is carried by zero-momentum Cooper pairs, so that
\langle\psi_{(\boldsymbol{K}+\delta\boldsymbol{k})\sigma}\psi_{(\boldsymbol{K}+\delta\boldsymbol{k})\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle

\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}

We consider general spin-triplet pairing

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}&\pm\Delta_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\\
\pm\Delta_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{k})&-\xi_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}}\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

Appendix B
Tight-binding

In this section, we analyze a tight-binding model with two different
types of pairings: nearest-neighbor and next-nearest-neighbor pairings.
The low-energy model obtained taking the limit
t\gg\mu\gg\Delta_{0}
, where
t
is the hopping parameter,
\mu
is the chemical potential and
\Delta_{0}
is the energy of the superconducting pairing of two electrons on different sites. We show that nearest-neighbor pairing produces only interband superconductivity, but next-nearest-neighbour hopping produces the intraband superconductivity discussed in this work.
In this section the following definitions are used:

•

\mu
, the chemical potential

•

t
, the hopping parameter

•

\Delta_{0}
the pairing potential between two lattice sites

•

a_{i},b_{i}
annihilation operators for the two different sublattices in real space.

•

a_{\boldsymbol{k}},b_{\boldsymbol{k}}
annihilation operators for the two different sublattices in momentum space.

•

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
annihilation operators in momentum space for conduction and valence band particles respectively.

•

The distance between nearest neighbours
a
is set to 1.

•

\Delta=\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{K})=\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\Delta_{0}
.

•

k_{F}=\frac{2\mu}{3t}
is the Fermi momentum.

•

V
is strength of impurities, for edges we take
V\xrightarrow{}\infty
.

•

Quantities denoted by
\psi
are wavefunctions. They have subscripts merely to number them.

•

\Theta
denotes the Heaviside function.

B.1
Choice of pair potential

We consider two types of pair potentials, one with nearest neighbour hopping, and one with next nearest neighbour hopping. We assume that the pair potential has the same symmetry as the underlying lattice with only two inequivalent

\boldsymbol{K}
-points. This leaves only one option for odd-parity superconductivity in each case.

A tight-binding Hamiltonian for graphene with nearest neighbour pairing is

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mu\sum_{i}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i})+t\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1,2,3}a_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i+\delta_{j}}

\displaystyle+i\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1,2,3}(a_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i+\delta_{j}}^{\dagger}-b_{i+\delta_{j}}^{\dagger}a_{i}^{\dagger})+\text{h.c.},

(14)

where
a,b
denote annihilation operators on the two nonequivalent sites in the unit cell,
\delta_{1}=(0,-1),\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{3},1),\delta_{3}=\frac{1}{2}(-\sqrt{3},1)
are vectors between nearest neighbours.
A 2D Fourier transform over real space results in

\displaystyle H_{1}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\mu(a_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\boldsymbol{k}}+b_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}b_{\boldsymbol{k}})+tf(\boldsymbol{k})a_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}+i\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2}\bigg(f(\boldsymbol{k})a_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}b_{-k}^{\dagger}-f^{*}(k)b_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}a_{-k}^{\dagger}\bigg)+\text{h.c.},

(15)

where the momentum dependence of the gap is given by form factor

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=e^{ik_{y}}+2e^{-\frac{i}{2}k_{y}}\cos(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{x}).

(16)

A tight-binding Hamiltonian for graphene with next-nearest neighbour pairing is

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mu\sum_{i}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i})+t\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1,2,3}a_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i+\delta_{j}}

\displaystyle+i\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1,2,3}(a_{i+\chi_{j}}^{\dagger}a_{i}^{\dagger}-a_{i-\chi_{j}}^{\dagger}a_{i}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle+i\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1,2,3}(b_{i+\chi_{j}}^{\dagger}b_{i}^{\dagger}-b_{i-\chi_{j}}^{\dagger}b_{i}^{\dagger})+\text{h.c.},

(17)

where
\chi_{1}=\delta_{3}-\delta_{2},\chi_{2}=\delta_{1}-\delta_{3},\chi_{3}=\delta_{2}-\delta_{1}
are vectors between next-nearest neighbours. This pairing is the f-wave pairing introduced in
Pangburn et al. 2022a
.
A Fourier transform over real space results in

\displaystyle H_{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\mu(a_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\boldsymbol{k}}+b_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}b_{\boldsymbol{k}})+tf(\boldsymbol{k})a_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})(a_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}a_{-k}^{\dagger}+b_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}b_{-k}^{\dagger})+\text{h.c.},

(18)

where the momentum dependence of the gap is given by form factor

\displaystyle s(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\sin(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{x}-\frac{3}{2}k_{y})+\sin(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{x}-\frac{3}{2}k_{y})-\sin(\sqrt{3}k_{x}).

(19)

First, we consider the hopping terms, which are the same for both types of pairing. We define
f(\boldsymbol{k})=F(\boldsymbol{k})e^{i\theta(\boldsymbol{k})}
, where
F,\theta
are real variables,
F
being positive. Since by symmetry
f
satisfies
f(\boldsymbol{k})=f^{*}(-\boldsymbol{k})
its magnitude and phase satisfy
F(\boldsymbol{k})=F(-\boldsymbol{k})
and
\theta(\boldsymbol{k})=-\theta(-\boldsymbol{k})
. The hopping terms are diagonalized by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}a_{\boldsymbol{k}}+e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}),

(20)

\displaystyle\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}a_{\boldsymbol{k}}-e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}).

(21)

In terms of
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
the Hamiltonian for nearest neighbour pairing is

\displaystyle H_{1}=-(\mu-tF(\boldsymbol{k}))\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}-(tF(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu)\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\Delta_{0}F(\boldsymbol{k})(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}-\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger})+\text{h.c.},

(22)

and the Hamiltonian for next nearest neighbour pairing is

\displaystyle H_{2}=-(\mu-tF(\boldsymbol{k}))\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}-(tF(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu)\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}+\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger})+\text{h.c.}.

(23)

Thus, nearest neighbour pairing gives inter-band pairing,
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}
, whereas next nearest neighbour pairing gives intra-band pairing,
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{-\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}
. Since the Fermi level only crosses one of the two bands and
\Delta\ll\mu
, interband pairing is heavily suppressed and only next nearest neighbour pairing should be considered. Since
s(\boldsymbol{K})=-s(-\boldsymbol{K})\neq 0
, we may conclude that our tight-binding model with next nearest neighbour pairing in the limit
t\gg\mu\gg\Delta_{0}
is described by the effective low-energy model described in the main body by Eq. (1). In the next section, we discuss the dispersion of the bound states in this tight-binding model.

\displaystyle-\frac{i\sin\theta}{iV}\cos(K_{x}+k_{F}\cos\theta)x\begin{bmatrix}0\\

(44)

Again, we can make the combinations
\psi_{A\pm}=\psi_{1}\pm\psi_{2}
and
\psi_{B\pm}=\psi_{3}\pm\psi_{4}
, and we obtain in the limit
V\xrightarrow{}\infty

Thus, also for
k_{y}\neq 0
there exist two orthogonal states on each side of the line. In this case the states do not have a specific sublattice structure or a well defined single periodicity.

B.2.2
Higher order terms

The analytical results presented in the main text and in the previous section, are obtained in the limit
\mu\gg\Delta
and
\mu\sin\theta\gg\Delta
.
For
\theta\approx\frac{\pi}{2}
the latter assumption is not valid, and the results of the tight-binding model do show a dispersion for
k_{y}\approx k_{F}
.
In this section we analytically derive that the dispersion for small
k_{y}
is of order
\Delta^{2}/\mu
. Moreover, we show that for large
k_{y}
the energy of the edge states approaches the conduction band.

The Green’s function can be written as

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{X}(AG_{1}+BG_{2}),

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\left((tF(\boldsymbol{k}))^{2}+\mu^{2}+(\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k}))^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)^{2}-4\mu^{2}t^{2}F(\boldsymbol{k})^{2},

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=(tF(\boldsymbol{k}))^{2}+\mu^{2}+\Delta_{0}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{k})^{2}-\omega^{2},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=2\mu,

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\omega+\mu&-tf&\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})&0\\
-t\tilde{f}&\omega+\mu&0&\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})\\
\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})&0&\omega-\mu&tf\\
0&\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})&t\tilde{f}&\omega-\mu\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-(tF)^{2}&(\omega+\mu)tf&0&\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})tf\\
(\omega+\mu)t\tilde{f}&-(tF)^{2}&\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})t\tilde{f}&0\\
0&\Delta s(\boldsymbol{k})tf&(tF)^{2}&(\omega-\mu)tf\\
\Delta_{0}s(\boldsymbol{k})t\tilde{f}&0&(\omega-\mu)t\tilde{f}&(tF)^{2}\end{bmatrix},

(49)

where
f(\boldsymbol{k})=e^{-ik_{y}}+2e^{i\frac{k_{y}}{2}}\cos\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{y}
and
\tilde{f}(k_{x},k_{y})=f(k_{x},-k_{y})
. Because we assume
\frac{\mu}{t}\ll 1
we may linearize around each
K
-point. Near the
K
-point,
f(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{K})\approx\tilde{t}(k_{x}+ik_{y})
, where
\tilde{t}=\frac{3t}{2}
, and
\Delta\approx\Delta_{0}3\sqrt{3}
.
For simplicity of notation we denote the product of
\tilde{t}
and
k_{x,y}
by
p_{x,y}
.

In the first part we do assume that
\frac{\mu}{t}
is small so that terms of higher order in
t/\mu
can be ignored, the influence of those terms is discussed in Sec. B4.
Under the assumption that
t\gg\mu
the denominator is a fourth order polynomial, hence we may rewrite the Green’s function
G
near a
\boldsymbol{K}
-point as

\displaystyle G=(AG_{1}+BG_{2})\prod_{i=1}^{4}\frac{1}{p_{x}-p_{xi}},p_{x1}=\alpha+i\beta,p_{x2}=\alpha-i\beta,p_{x3}=-\alpha+i\beta,p_{x4}=-\alpha-i\beta,

(50)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2},

\displaystyle\alpha\beta

\displaystyle=\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}},

\displaystyle F(p_{xi})

\displaystyle=\mu\pm i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}},

(51)

where the
+
sign is to be used for poles
1
and
4
, and the minus sign for poles 2 and 3.
Explicit expressions for
\alpha^{2}
and
\beta^{2}
are

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}-k_{y}^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2}+\sqrt{(\mu^{2}-k_{y}^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})^{2}+4\mu^{2}(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})},

(52)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}

\displaystyle=k_{y}^{2}-\mu^{2}+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}+\sqrt{(\mu^{2}-k_{y}^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})^{2}+4\mu^{2}(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})}.

(53)

For the evaluation of the residues in the following we need the quantities

\displaystyle\lim_{p_{x}\xrightarrow{}p_{xj}}(p_{x}-p_{xj})\prod_{i=1}^{4}\frac{1}{p_{xj}-p_{xi}}=\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{p_{xj}-p_{xi}}.

(54)

For
j=1,4
this quantity equals
\frac{1}{\alpha\beta(\alpha+i\beta)}
, for
j=2,3
it equals
\frac{1}{\alpha\beta(\alpha-i\beta)}

For
x>0
we have to evaluate poles 1 and 3. The diagonal elements in sublattice space of the blocks proportional diagonal in Nambu space, to be called the
(1,1)
-block and
(2,2)
-block, read

\displaystyle\frac{2\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha+i\beta)}\left((\mu+i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(\omega+\mu)-(\mu+i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})^{2}\right)e^{ip_{1}x}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha-i\beta)}\left((\mu-i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(\omega+\mu)-(\mu-i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})^{2}\right)e^{ip_{3}x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\Bigg((\alpha-i\beta)\left((\mu+i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(\omega-i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})\right)e^{ip_{1}x}+(\alpha+i\beta)\left((\mu-i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(\omega+i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})\right)e^{ip_{3}x}\Bigg).

(55)

In the limit
x\xrightarrow{}0
this becomes

\displaystyle\frac{4\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\left(\alpha(\mu\omega+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})+\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}(\omega-\mu)\right).

(56)

Similarly, the off-diagonal elements of this block at
x=0
are given by

\displaystyle\frac{2\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\Bigg((\alpha-i\beta)(\omega+\mu-\mu-i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(\alpha+i\beta\pm ik_{y})+(\alpha+i\beta)(\omega+\mu-\mu+i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(-\alpha+i\beta\pm ik_{y})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\Bigg(-(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}\pm ik_{y}(\alpha\omega-\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})\Bigg).

(57)

Now consider the Green’s function in the opposite valley. The Green’s function is similar in the two valleys. The only differences are that (i)
tf=-p_{x}+ip_{y}
in the
-K
-valley whereas
tf=p_{x}+ip_{y}
in the
K
-valley and (ii)
\Delta
has opposite sign in the opposite valleys. This means that the diagonal elements of the Green’s function are the same in both valleys, whereas in the off-diagonal elements of the
(1,1)
-block only those terms proportional to
p_{y}
survive. The
(1,1)
-block in Nambu space thus reads

\displaystyle\frac{8\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\left(\alpha(\mu\omega+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})+\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}(\omega-\mu)\mathbf{1}_{\rho}+k_{y}(\alpha\omega-\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})\rho_{y}\right),

(58)

where
\mathbf{1}_{\rho}
is the identity matrix in sublattice space. In an entirely similar way, the
(2,2)
-block in Nambu space is, multiplying the expression by -1 and then reversing the sign of
\omega

\displaystyle\frac{8\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\left(\alpha(\mu\omega-\Delta^{2}+\omega^{2})+\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}(\omega+\mu)\right)\mathbf{1}_{\rho}+k_{y}(\alpha\omega+\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})\rho_{y}.

(59)

This closes the discussion of the blocks diagonal in Nambu space, to be called the
(1,2)
-block and
(2,1)
-block.
Next consider the terms that are off-diagonal in Nambu space. The procedure is similar. However, since the sum of the pair potentials in both valleys is zero, only the terms proportional to
p_{x}\Delta
in the terms off-diagonal in sublattice space add up constructively for the two valleys, and the
\Delta
and
p_{y}\Delta
terms cancel out because they have opposite sign in the valleys. The surviving term is given by

\displaystyle\frac{2\mu}{\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\Delta\left((\alpha-i\beta)(\alpha+i\beta)+(\alpha+i\beta)(-\alpha+i\beta)\right))=0,

(60)

that is, the terms off-diagonal in Nambu space vanish exactly.

Therefore, bound states occur if the determinant of one of either the
(1,1)
or
(2,2)
-block vanishes to all orders in
\Delta/\mu
. Since the determinant of hole block can be obtained from the electron block by negation of
\omega
, it is sufficient to consider only the electron block and take into account that bound states always come in pairs at
\pm\omega
.
The determinant, ignoring the prefactor evaluates to

\displaystyle(\mu^{2}-k_{y}^{2})(\alpha\omega+\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})^{2}+(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})(\alpha\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}+\beta\omega)^{2}+2\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}((\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})\omega\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}+\alpha\beta(2\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})).

(61)

Evaluating this term and using that
\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}=\mu^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2}-p_{y}^{2}
and
\alpha\beta=\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}
, the expression can be written as

\displaystyle(\mu^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2}-k_{y}^{2})^{2}\omega^{2}+((\mu^{2}-k_{y}^{2})\beta^{2}+(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})\alpha^{2})\Delta^{2}+2\mu^{2}(2\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})

\displaystyle=\left((\mu^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2}-k_{y}^{2})^{2}+4\mu^{2}(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})\right)\omega^{2}+\left((\mu^{2}-k_{y}^{2})\beta^{2}+(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})\alpha^{2}-2\mu^{2}(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})\right)\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\left((\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}-(2\mu^{2}-\alpha^{2})\Delta^{2}\right)\omega^{2}+\left((\mu^{2}-k_{y}^{2})\beta^{2}+(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})\alpha^{2}-2\mu^{2}(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})\right)\Delta^{2}.

(62)

With this, we have an implicit expression for the bound state energies:

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=\frac{(2\mu^{2}-\alpha^{2})\Delta^{2}-(\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2})\beta^{2}}{(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}+(2\mu^{2}-\alpha^{2})\Delta^{2}}\Delta^{2}.

(63)

However, note that
\alpha
and
\beta
do depend on
\omega
, so that this is an indirect expression.

From Eq. B50 the bound state energy can be found as a function of
k_{y}
. The results for
\Delta/\mu=0.1
and
\Delta/\mu=0.05
are shown in Supplemental Fig. 1. The results are in agreement with the results of the tight-binding model that for small
k_{y}
the dispersion becomes smaller as the ratio
\Delta/\mu
is decreased. Close to the
k_{y}=k_{F}
the energy of the bound states increases, and for
k_{y}>k_{F}
the edge states approach the conduction band.

Figure 4:
The bound state energies as a function of
k_{y}
for
\Delta/\mu=0.1
(a) and
\Delta/\mu=0.05
(b). For smaller
\Delta/\mu
the dispersion around
k_{y}=0
becomes smaller. In both cases the conduction band is rapidly approached for
k_{y}>k_{F}
.

In certain limits, direct analytical expressions can be obtained. First consider the case
p_{y}\ll\mu
, that is,
k_{y}\ll k_{F}
. Then

\alpha^{2}=\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}-\frac{(\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})p_{y}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}+O(p_{y}/\mu)^{4}

and

\beta^{2}=\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}-\frac{(\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})p_{y}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}+O(p_{y}/\mu)^{4}.

Substituting this into Eq. B50, and ignoring terms of at least second order in
\Delta/\mu

\displaystyle\omega^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(\mu^{2}+p_{y}^{2})\Delta^{2}-(\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2})(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})+(\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})p_{y}^{2}}{(\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+(\mu^{2}+p_{y}^{2})\Delta^{2}}\Delta^{2}

(64)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mu^{2}\omega^{2}+p_{y}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{(\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+(\mu^{2}+p_{y}^{2})\Delta^{2}}\Delta^{2}.

(65)

From here we see that to lowest order in
\frac{\Delta}{\mu}

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\mu^{2}}p_{y}.

(66)

In terms of
k_{y}
this can be written as

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\mu}\frac{k_{y}}{k_{F}}.

(67)

The above expressions explain the dispersion around
k_{y}=0
as observed in the tight-binding model and show that they are indeed higher order in
\Delta/\mu
.

The second case that we consider is the case in which
p_{y}
is close to
\mu
, that is,
k_{y}
is close to
k_{F}
. To be precise, we assume
\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}\ll\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})+O(\frac{\Delta^{3}}{\mu}),

(68)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}+\omega^{2}-\Delta^{2})+O(\frac{\Delta}{\mu}).

(69)

It is enough to keep only the first order approximations,

\displaystyle\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mu^{2}\Delta^{2}-\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}(\Delta^{2}+\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2})}{\mu^{2}(6\Delta^{2}-4\omega^{2})-\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}\Delta^{2}}\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}}{2\mu}(1+\frac{\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}}{\Delta^{2}})}{3-2(\frac{\omega}{\Delta})^{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}}{2\mu}}\Delta^{2}.

(70)

To zeroth order approximation there are seemingly two solutions,
\omega=\Delta
and
\omega=\Delta/\sqrt{2}
. The former of the two is in fact not really a solution, as
\alpha
and
\beta
vanish as well, so that
G(x=0)
has finite determinant.
Thus, the only solution that should be considered is
\omega=\Delta/\sqrt{2}+\tilde{\omega}
with
\tilde{\omega}\ll\Delta
. Substituting this into Eq. B50 and ignoring any higher order term in
\tilde{\omega}
, we find

\displaystyle\omega\approx\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{8\mu}(1+2\frac{\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}).

(71)

Note that since
p_{y}\approx\mu
this implies that

\displaystyle\frac{d\omega}{dp_{y}}\approx\frac{1}{2}.

(72)

This implies that in a relatively small window, of order
\frac{\Delta}{\mu}k_{F}
around
k_{F}
the energy of the bound states changes by
\Delta
. Thus, for
\Delta/\mu\ll 1
the bound state energy increases from almost zero to close to the conduction band in a narrow energy window.

A third interesting limit is the one in which
k_{y}\gg k_{F}
.
Ignoring terms that are of order
\frac{1}{p_{y}^{2}}
, Eq. B50 becomes

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=\frac{2\mu^{2}\Delta^{2}+(p_{y}^{2}-\mu^{2})(p_{y}^{2}-\mu^{2}+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})}{2\mu^{2}\Delta^{2}+(p_{y}^{2}-\mu^{2}+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})(p_{y}^{2}-\mu^{2}+\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})}\Delta^{2}.

(73)

One of the solutions is
\omega=\Delta
. However, for
\omega=\Delta
, it is found that the term
\alpha
is actually vanishing as well, so that this is not a bound state. Thus, there are no bound states with
\omega\ll p_{y}
. Instead, Supplemental Fig. 1 suggests that the bound states are close to the conduction band, which is given by
E_{c}=(p_{y}-\mu)^{2}+\Delta^{2}
for
p_{y}>\mu
. Approximating
\omega\approx p_{y}-\mu+\tilde{\omega}
, where
|\tilde{\omega}|\ll p_{y}-\mu
, we can compute that

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle\approx-(p_{y}-\mu)\mu-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\sqrt{4\mu(p_{y}-\mu)+\Delta^{2}},

(74)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}

\displaystyle\approx(p_{y}-\mu)\mu+\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\sqrt{4\mu(p_{y}-\mu)+\Delta^{2}}.

(75)

Note that
\alpha^{2}<0
. This is not a problem. It indicates that the two pairs of complex conjugate poles now both have vanishing real part and a different value for the imaginary part.
Substituting this into Eq. B50, and ignoring any terms that are not of lowest order in
\Delta^{2}
or
\tilde{\omega}
, the following equation is found:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(p_{y}+\mu)(p_{y}-\mu)^{2}}{\mu k_{y}(-\tilde{\omega}+2\Delta^{2})}\Delta^{2}=(p_{y}-\mu)^{2},

(76)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}=2\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{2}\frac{\mu(p_{y}+\mu)(p_{y}-\mu)^{2}}{\mu p_{y}(p_{y}-\mu)^{2}}=2\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{2}(1+\frac{p_{y}}{\mu})=\Delta^{2}(1-\frac{p_{y}}{\mu}).

(77)

Thus,

\displaystyle\omega^{2}

\displaystyle\approx(p_{y}-\mu)^{2}+\Delta^{2}(1-\frac{\mu}{p_{y}})=E_{c}^{2}-\frac{\mu}{p_{y}}\Delta^{2},

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\approx E_{c}-\frac{\mu}{p_{y}}\frac{\Delta^{2}}{E_{c}}.

(78)

This shows that the bound state approaches the conduction band quickly as
k_{y}\gg\mu
.
With this, we have analytically understood all main features of Eq. B50, explaining the influence of a finite
\Delta/\mu
.

B.3
Localization length of edge states

An important feature of edge states is their localization,

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle=\frac{1}{a}\int x|\psi|^{2}dx\left(\int|\psi|^{2}dx\right)^{-1},

(79)

where
a
is the distance between two carbon atoms, here set to 1.
This quantity determines how far opposite edges must be apart to have small interaction between the two. For small
k_{y}
it holds that
\alpha\approx\mu^{2}-p_{y}^{2}>0
and
\beta^{2}=\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}>0
, and therefore

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle\sim\frac{1}{\text{Im}(k_{x})}=\frac{3}{2}t(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\approx\frac{3}{2}t\Delta^{-1},

(80)

Thus, the edge states are localized on the order of a superconducting coherence length. For
k\approx k_{F}
, we have

\alpha^{2}\approx\beta^{2}\approx(\frac{3}{2}t)^{-1}\mu\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}
, so

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle\sim\frac{1}{\text{Im}(k_{x})}=\frac{3}{2}t(\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2})^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\approx\frac{3}{4}t(\Delta\mu)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(81)

Since
\mu\gg\Delta
, these states are more localized than the states near
k_{y}=0
. If
k_{y}\gg k_{F}
we have that

\beta^{2}\approx-\alpha^{2}\approx(k_{y}-\mu)\mu
. Thus,
\alpha^{2}<0
, which means that the four solutions for
k_{x}
are all purely imaginary, and for
\alpha\approx i\beta
,

\displaystyle\text{Im}(k_{x})=\pm 2\beta=(\frac{3}{2}t)^{-1}\sqrt{(k_{y}-\mu)\mu},

(82)

while for
\alpha\approx-i\beta

\displaystyle\text{Im}(k_{x})\approx(\frac{3}{2}t)^{-1}2\Delta^{2}((k_{y}-\mu)\mu)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(83)

Thus, the Green’s function has two components which have different decay lengths. However, due to the prefactor
(\alpha+i\beta)^{-1}
, the first contribution is highly suppressed compared to the second one, by a factor
\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\mu(p_{y}-\mu)}
. Therefore, the localization is determined by the second term,

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle\approx\frac{3}{2}t((k_{y}-\mu)\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Delta^{-2}.

(84)

Thus, in this limit the localization length increases with increasing
k_{y}
and is much larger than a superconducting coherence length, indicating that these states are similar to bulk states.

The localization can also be computed numerically from the results of our tight-binding model using a model with 1024 sites. Thus, the integration should be over the 512 sites on the left (right) if the state is more localized to the left (right). The results are shown in the main text, Fig. 3(b). The two lowest energy states have the same localization. The numerical results confirm the analytical calculations above, the localization length decreases as
k_{y}
approaches
k_{F}
and then strongly increases, approaching the bulk value of 256. For the two lowest energy states the localization is well determined. For the two next lowest energy states there is a clear oscillation for
k_{y}\gg k_{F}
. The reason for this is is that they become so close to the conduction band that they mix with those states.

B.4
Finite bandwidth

In previous sections, we made the assumption that
\mu/t\gg 1
and used only the lowest order approximation. Here we discuss how a finite value of
\mu/t
influences the results by going to next order.
We consider the correction to energy of the edge states to lowest order in
\mu/t
. To do this, we need to use higher order terms in
f(\boldsymbol{k})
and
s(\boldsymbol{k})
in their expansion around the
\pm\boldsymbol{K}
-points.
Taking into account next order terms in
f(k)
and
s(k)
, they read

\displaystyle f(k)=p_{x}+ip_{y}+\frac{3}{4t}(p_{x}^{2}-p_{y}^{2}+ip_{x}p_{y}),

(85)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(k)=-p_{x}+ip_{y}+\frac{3}{4t}(p_{x}^{2}-p_{y}^{2}-ip_{x}p_{y}),

(86)

\displaystyle s(k)=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{3}+\frac{9}{4}\sqrt{3}(p_{x}^{2}-p_{y}^{2}).

(87)

We will exploit the symmetry of the poles. Terms that are either odd in valley, or have opposite sign for the two poles within a single valley cancel out.

The corrections to the Green’s function can be divided into two categories based on the decomposition introduced in Eq. B36. One is due to the change in the matrices
AG_{1}+BG_{2}
introduced in Eq. B36 at each pole. The other one due to the change evaluation of the residue of
1/X
in Eq. B36.
In the following we consider the corrections to each element.

To consider the change in the residue of
1/X
in Eq. B36 we must consider the correction to the location of the poles. The correction
\gamma
to
k_{x}
can be calculated by evaluating the zeros of
f
taking into account the second order terms. To lowest order this correction is

\displaystyle\gamma=\mp\frac{3}{4t}((\alpha+i\beta)^{2}-k_{y}^{2}).

(88)

For estimating the correction to the residue of
1/X
at the poles we may use that

\displaystyle\lim_{p\xrightarrow{}p_{i}}\frac{p-p_{i}}{X}=\left(\frac{dX}{dp}|_{p=p_{i}}\right)^{-1}.

(89)

Now,

\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dp_{x}}

\displaystyle=(A-2\mu^{2})\frac{d}{dp_{x}}(F^{2})+A\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{t^{2}}\frac{d}{dp}s^{2}\approx(A-2\mu^{2})\partial_{p_{x}}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+(\frac{3}{2}p_{x}p_{y}^{2}-\frac{3}{4}p_{x}^{3}))

\displaystyle=(A-2\mu^{2})(p_{x}+\frac{3}{2t}k_{y}^{2}-\frac{9}{4t}k_{x}^{2})\approx(A-2\mu^{2})(\alpha+i\beta\mp\frac{3}{2t}(\alpha+i\beta)^{2}),

(90)

where the derivative of
s(\boldsymbol{K})
has been omitted because it is of higher order in
(\Delta/t)
.
With this, the residue of
1/X
in the K-valley is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\frac{\alpha\mp i\beta}{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}(1-\frac{3}{2t}(\alpha+i\beta))}\approx\Bigg(\frac{\alpha\mp i\beta}{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}\pm\frac{3}{2t}\Bigg)\frac{1}{\alpha\beta},

(91)

that is, the correction equals
\pm\frac{3}{2t}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}
.
In the opposite valley, the corrections differ by a minus-sign, making the correction even in
\boldsymbol{k}
.
Now we may exploit the symmetry of the poles. First, we may use that terms for which the corrections to the residue of
X^{-1}(AG_{1}+BG_{2})
have opposite sign cancel out.
Because the corrections to the residue of
1/X
have opposite sign within a single valley, only terms proportional to
k_{x}
survive. This implies that the correction is proportional to
\rho_{x}
.
Moreover, since
s(\boldsymbol{k})
is odd in
\boldsymbol{k}
, the Nambu off-diagonal
\rho_{x}
- components are not corrected up to first order in
\frac{\mu}{t}
. Thus, the only correction is the
\rho_{x}
-contribution to the diagonal elements in Nambu space. It equals

\displaystyle-\frac{3\mu}{2t\alpha\beta}\Bigg((\omega-i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(\alpha+i\beta)-(\omega+i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})(-\alpha+i\beta)\Bigg)\rho_{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3\mu}{t\alpha\beta}\Bigg(\alpha\omega+\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}\Bigg)\rho_{x}.

(92)

Now consider the correction to
AG_{1}+BG_{2}
in Eq. B36. For this correction we need to take into account the correction to
f
,

\displaystyle f\approx\alpha+i\beta+\frac{3}{2t}\Bigg((\alpha+i\beta)^{2}-p_{y}^{2}\Bigg)+\frac{3}{4t}(\alpha+i\beta)p_{y},

(93)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}\approx-\alpha+i\beta+\frac{3}{2t}\Bigg((\alpha+i\beta)^{2}-p_{y}^{2}\Bigg)-\frac{3}{4t}(\alpha+i\beta)p_{y}.

(94)

According to the same symmetries as before, only the
\rho_{x}
-component diagonal in Nambu space may survive, and it equals

\displaystyle\frac{3\mu}{2t\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\Bigg((\alpha-i\beta)(\omega-i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})((\alpha+i\beta)^{2}-k_{y}^{2})+(\alpha+i\beta)(\omega+i\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}})((\alpha-i\beta)^{2}-k_{y}^{2})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\mu}{t\alpha\beta}\Bigg(\alpha\omega+\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}\Bigg)+\frac{3\mu}{2t\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\Bigg(\alpha\omega-\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}\Bigg)k_{y}^{2}.

(95)

Summing the two corrections, we see that two of the three terms cancel against each other. Hence the only correction of first order in
\frac{\mu}{t}
is

\displaystyle\frac{3\mu}{2t\alpha\beta(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})}\Bigg(\alpha\omega-\beta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}}\Bigg)k_{y}^{2}\rho_{x}.

(96)

That is, for
k_{y}=0
the correction vanishes and there is still a zero energy bound state.
Moreover, from this expression it follows we need to evaluate the
\beta
-term to have any nonzero energy bound states, which means that there exist no corrections to the energy of order
\mu/t
, lowest order corrections are of order
\Delta/\mu\mu/t=\frac{\Delta}{t}
.
Lastly, the correction to the energy is second order in
k_{y}
, and thus for
k_{y}\ll k_{F}
this corresponds to a negligible correction.
