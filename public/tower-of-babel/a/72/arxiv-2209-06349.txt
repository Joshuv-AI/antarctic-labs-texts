Abstract
. We show that the eigenpolytopes of graphs are universal in the sense that every polytope, up to affine equivalence, appears as the eigenpolytope of some positively weighted graph. We next extend the theory of graphical designs, which are quadrature rules for graphs, to positively weighted graphs. Through Gale duality for polytopes, we show a bijection between graphical designs and the faces of eigenpolytopes. This bijection proves the existence of graphical designs with positive quadrature weights, and upper bounds the size of a minimal graphical design. Connecting this bijection with the universality of eigenpolytopes, we establish three complexity results: it is strongly NP-complete to determine if there is a graphical design smaller than the mentioned upper bound, it is NP-hard to find a smallest graphical design, and it is #P-complete to count the number of minimal graphical designs.

Introduction

This paper unites two combinatorial constructions arising from the
combinatorial graph Laplacian
, namely
eigenpolytopes

[
God78
]
and
graphical designs

[
Ste20
]
, through the theory of
Gale duality
for polytopes
[
Gal56
,
Grü03
]
.
An eigenpolytope is a polytope arising from the eigenspaces of a graph Laplacian.
The structure and symmetries of these polytopes provide information about the structure and symmetries of the underlying graph, particularly when the underlying graph is highly structured (see, for instance
[
God98
]
).
A graphical design is a quadrature rule for a graph.
Classical numerical quadrature allows one to compute or approximate definite integrals of certain classes of functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by sampling at finitely many points.
Analogously, a graphical design is a rule to compute the average of certain Laplacian eigenvectors by sampling on a subset of graph vertices.
Previous work on graphical designs often focuses on unweighted regular graphs (e.g.
[
Gol20
,
Bab21
,
BT22
,
ST22
]
), where the eigenvectors of a graph are independent of the choice of graph Laplacian.
For regular, unweighted graphs, the bijection between graphical designs and eigenpolytopes we establish is precisely the one shown in
[
BT22
]
.
For more literature review and details on the connection between graphical designs and eigenpolytopes through Gale duality, we refer to
[
BT22
]
.

We summarize the main results of this paper.

•

The Laplacian eigenspaces of positively weighted graphs are general: for any collection of orthogonal subspaces which spans
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and contains the span of the all-ones vector as a subspace, there exists a connected, positively weighted graph on
n
vertices whose Laplacian eigenspaces are precisely the given subspaces
(Lemma
3.1
). The proof reveals a polyhedral structure underlying the possible eigenvalues of a graph with a given eigenbasis. We use this result to prove that every polytope up to affine equivalence appears as an eigenpolytope of a positively weighted graph (Theorem
3.2
).
Moreover, these are constructive proofs with strongly polynomial time algorithms.

•

We establish a combinatorial bijection between graphical designs and faces of eigenpolytopes using Gale duality (Theorem
5.3
). Polyhedral geometry then provides an upper bound on the size of the support of minimal graphical designs (Theorem
5.7
). We note that this bijection holds when the combinatorial Laplacian is replaced by any symmetric operator with the all-ones vector as an eigenvector (Remark
5.5
).

•

Through Gale duality and our algorithms, we establish complexity results for graphical designs as translations of polytope hardness results (Theorems
6.3
,
6.5
,
6.7
). We provide a linear program which finds a graphical design in polynomial time with a guaranteed sparseness, though minimality is not guaranteed (Remark
6.8
).

We organize the paper as follows.
Section
2
introduces eigenpolytopes.
Section
3
contains our algorithmic results showing the universality of Laplacian eigenspaces and eigenpolytopes.
Section
4
establishes definitions and notation for graphical designs on positively weighted graphs.
Section
5
briefly recounts the theory of Gale duality for polytopes and uses this machinery to connect graphical designs to the eigenpolytopes of the graph, a key structural result.
This correspondence provides a proof of existence and an upper bound on the size of a minimal graphical design.
Using our strongly polynomial time algorithms to create graphs with given eigenpolytopes, we establish the following complexity results in Section
6
by translating established hardness results for polytopes. It is strongly NP-complete to determine if there is a design smaller than the upper bound established in Section
5
, it is NP-hard to find a smallest graphical design, and it is #P-complete to count the number of support-minimal graphical designs.

Eigenpolytopes of Weighted Graphs

Let
G=([n],E,w)
be a connected graph with vertex set
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, edge set
E
, and positive edge weights
w:E\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
.
Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be the weighted
adjacency matrix
of
G
, with
A_{ij}=w(ij)
if
ij\in E
and 0 otherwise, and
D
be the weighted (diagonal)
degree matrix
of
G
, with
D_{ii}=\deg i=\sum_{ij\in E}w(ij)
.
The
(combinatorial) Laplacian
of
G
is the symmetric matrix
L=D-A
.
Since
L
is positive semidefinite, it has non-negative eigenvalues and an orthogonal set of eigenvectors
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
that form a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For general references on spectral and algebraic graph theory, we refer the reader to
[
Chu97
,
GR01
,
Spi19
]
.

Letting
\mathbbm{1}
denote the all-ones vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we always have that
L\mathbbm{1}=0
, since the
i
-th row of
A
sums to
\deg(i)=\sum_{ij\in E}w(ij)
. Therefore,
\mathbbm{1}
is an eigenvector of
L
with eigenvalue
0
. Moreover, since
G
is connected, the eigenspace of 0 is spanned by
\mathbbm{1}
; we denote this eigenspace by
\Lambda_{1}.

It is convenient to not normalize the eigenvectors
\varphi_{i}
so that we may take
\varphi_{1}=\mathbbm{1}
as the basis vector for
\Lambda_{1}
.
We will refer to the eigenspaces and eigenvalues of
L
as the eigenspaces and eigenvalues of the graph
G
.

.

3.
Weighted Graphs From Polytopes

In this section, we show that any polytope up to affine equivalence can appear as an eigenpolytope of a positively weighted graph.
We first show that any orthogonal basis for
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
which includes
\mathbbm{1}
as a basis vector, and any partition of this basis with
\{\mathbbm{1}\}
as a part, give rise to a positively weighted graph
G=([n],E,w)
where each part of the partition spans a distinct Laplacian eigenspace of
G
. Our proof reveals a polyhedral structure underlying the valid choices of eigenvalues that define such graphs. These results are phrased in terms of rational numbers for the purpose of time-complexity statements, but the same algorithms are valid for real numbers.

To state our complexity results, we recall some basics of encoding size. We reference a streamlined presentation tailored to our needs in
[
Sch86
, Chapter 2]
and refer further to
[
GJ79
]
for an in-depth treatment.

The
size
of a rational number
r=p/q
in reduced form is

\operatorname{size}(r)=1+\lceil\log_{2}(|p|+1)\rceil+\lceil\log_{2}(|q|+1)\rceil,

and the
size
of a rational matrix
M\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n\times m}
is

\operatorname{size}(M)=mn+\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{size}(M_{ij}).

An algorithm is
strongly polynomial time
if its run time is polynomial in size of the input, not just the dimension of the input. We will often present our input as a list of vectors
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
rather than as a matrix
V=[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{m}]\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n\times m}
. Clearly,
\operatorname{size}(\mathcal{V})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{size}(v_{i})=\operatorname{size}(V)

We overload the notation
\operatorname{Diag}
; for a vector
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\operatorname{Diag}(v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is the diagonal matrix with
\operatorname{Diag}(v)_{ii}=v_{i}
, and for a matrix
M\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
\operatorname{Diag}(M)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is the diagonal matrix which forgets the off-diagonal entries of
M
.

We now use Lemma

to show that given a polytope

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
be such that
P=\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{V})
is full dimensional. We can create a connected graph
G=([n],E,w)
with
w\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{E}_{>0}
which has a Laplacian eigenpolytope affinely equivalent to
P
in time polynomial in
\operatorname{size}(\mathcal{V}).

Proof.

We may translate
P
so that its centroid lies at the origin by mapping
v_{i}\mapsto v_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}
. This is an affine transformation, and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(v_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}-n\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}=0.

Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}=0
, or equivalently, that the rows of the matrix
V=[v_{1}\ldots v_{n}]
are orthogonal to
\mathbbm{1}.

Let
X=[x_{1}\,\ldots\,x_{n}]\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{(n-d-1)\times n}
have rows which form a basis for the kernel of the matrix
[\mathbbm{1}\,\,V^{\top}]^{\top}
. We can find
X
in strongly polynomial time using Gaussian elimination (see
[
Sch86
, Theorem 23.3]
).
Let

\hat{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}\\
V\\
X\end{bmatrix}.

We will think of the rows of
\hat{B}
as the Laplacian eigenvectors of the graph we will construct.
Perform Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization on the rows of
\hat{B}
to obtain a rational orthogonal matrix

B=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&\varphi_{2}&\ldots&\varphi_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization is strongly polynomial time (see
[
GLS88
, Section 1.4]
)
Note that
\operatorname{span}\{\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{d+1}\}=\operatorname{rowspan}V
because Gram-Schmidt sequentially preserves span.
By Lemma
3.1
, we can construct a graph with rational, positive edge weights with the following three eigenspaces in strongly polynomial time:
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\},\Lambda_{2}=\operatorname{span}\{\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{d+1}\},\Lambda_{3}=\operatorname{span}\{\varphi_{d+2},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\}.

Because
V
and

∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Up to affine equivalence, every polytope with

Remark 3.6
.

To end this section, we contrast Theorem
3.2
with related results on
Colin de Verdière matrices
(
[
Col90
]
, see also
[
VLS99
]
).
A Colin de Verdière matrix for an unweighted graph
G=([n],E)
is any matrix of the form
M=\delta-\hat{A}
, where

(1)

\hat{A}
is the weighted adjacency matrix of a positively weighted graph
\hat{G}=([n],E,w)
on the same edge set as
G
,

(2)

\delta
is an arbitrary diagonal matrix, and

(3)

M
has exactly one negative eigenvalue.

By definition,
M
is not positive semidefinite and need not have row sums equal to one.
For our purposes, we can think of these matrices as something like a Laplacian on a graph with not only positive edge weights, but also (arbitrary) vertex weights.
Lovász and Schrijver show in
[
LS99
]
that any 3-dimensional polytope is an eigenpolytope of its edge graph for every Colin de Verdière matrix with corank three.
Izmestiev extends this construction in
[
Izm10
]
to show that every polytope is an eigenpolytope of its edge graph for a specifically constructed Colin de Verdière matrix.
These universality results for eigenpolytopes of Colin de Verdière matrices guarantee a serious amount of sparsity in the graphs needed to represent a given polytope as an eigenpolytope.
We note, however, that
M
is not exactly a Laplacian, and the underlying graph has arbitrary vertex weights. To find the specific Colin de Verdière matrix in Izmestiev’s construction requires computing the volume of a polytope, which is a #P-hard problem
[
DF88
]
. Our construction generally produces a dense graph, but we do not require vertex weights, the matrix we use is the true combinatorial graph Laplacian, and the algorithm is strongly polynomial time.

4.
Graphical Designs

A
graphical design
[
Ste20
]
on
G=([n],E,w)
is a proper subset of graph vertices
S\subset[n]
on which the global averages of certain graph eigenvectors agree with the weighted averages over just the subset
S
. If
\varphi
is such an eigenvector, that means we seek
S\subset[n]
and quadrature weights
a_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}},\,s\in S
such that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(i)=\sum_{s\in S}a_{s}\varphi(s).

The
support
of a vector
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
\operatorname{supp}(a):=\{i\in[n]\,:\,a_{i}\neq 0\}
.
A graphical design
S
with weights
a_{s}
may be identified with the vector
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
where
S=\operatorname{supp}(a)
. We gloss over some motivation and explanation for the formal definition in Definition
4.1
. We refer those seeking more detail to
[
BT22
]
, where a nearly identical story is developed for regular unweighted graphs using the Laplacian
AD^{-1}
.

We recall our previous notation and establish some more. For a given graph
G
with
m
eigenspaces, we denote a fixed arbitrary ordering of the eigenspaces as
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m}
. The rows of the matrix
U
form an eigenbasis of
L
, and for
I\subset[m]
, we use
U_{I}
to denote the submatrix of
U
consisting of all rows corresponding to the eigenspaces
\Lambda_{i}
for
i\in I
. Lastly,
\mathcal{U}_{I}
denotes the collection of columns of
U_{I}
. We use
\boldsymbol{k}
to denote the particular index set
\{2,\ldots,k\}\subset[m]
. For a subset
S\subset[n]
, define
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
by
\mathbbm{1}_{S}(i)=1
if
i\in S
and 0 otherwise.

Definition 4.1

(
k
-graphical designs: see
[
Ste20
,
Bab21
,
BT22
]
)
.

Suppose
G=([n],E,w)
has eigenspaces
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m}.

(1)

A
weighted
k
-graphical design
of
G
is a subset
S\subset[n]
and real weights
(a_{s}\neq 0:s\in S)
such that
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}a=0
.

(2)

If additionally
a\geq 0
, we call
S
a
positively weighted
k
-graphical design
.

(3)

If
a=\mathbbm{1}_{S}
then
S
is a
combinatorial
k
-graphical design
.

Graphical designs were first introduced by Steinerberger in
[
Ste20
]
, which considered averaging eigenvectors, not entire eigenspaces, of the operator
AD^{-1}-I
. The issue of eigenspace multiplicity was left open; the first author resolved this problem in
[
Bab21
]
by amending the definition to consider entire eigenspaces of
AD^{-1}-I
, but investigated only combinatorial
k
-graphical designs. The definition that appears above is a slight generalization of
[
BT22
]
[Definition 2.2], which considered only unweighted regular graphs and the operator
AD^{-1}
. For regular graphs,
AD^{-1}
,
D-A
, and most other graph operators carry equivalent spectral information because the matrices differ by only an affine transformation.

We only consider graphical designs up to scaling. If
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}a=0
, then any scaling of
a
is also in the kernel of
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. For this reason we leave
\Lambda_{1}
out of the definition, as any vector
a
can be scaled to average this eigenspace.
We also note that no proper subset is an
m
-graphical design – this follows by the same argument as in
[
BT22
, Lemma 2.5]
. Though
\mathbbm{1}
is always a
k
-graphical design for all
k\in[m]
, the purpose of a graphical design is, in a sense, to compress data on a graph, so we restrict our attention to proper subsets of graph vertices.

We will often abbreviate
k
-graphical designs as
k
-designs.
We note that there are two kinds of weights at play in our setup: the edge weights
w
of the graph, and the quadrature weights
a_{s}
which define the design. Here, we will only consider positively weighted or combinatorial designs. Quadrature rules with negative weights are typically undesirable, as they can lead to nonconvergence and instability
[
Huy09
]
.

5.
A Bijection through Gale Duality

We now use Gale duality to connect graphical designs and eigenpolytopes, extending the results in
[
BT22
]
for unweighted regular graphs to positively weighted graphs without regularity assumptions. We begin with a brief introduction to Gale duality
[
Gal56
]
; see
[
Zie95
, Chapter 6]
or
[
Grü03
, Section 5.4]
for more details.

A
vector configuration

\mathcal{V}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
is a collection of real vectors with possible repetitions. We denote the matrix
[v_{1}\,\ldots\,v_{n}]
by
V
.
A
dependence
on
\mathcal{V}
is a vector
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
Va=0
. A support-minimal dependence is called a
circuit
of
\mathcal{V}
.

Theorem 5.1

(Gale duality: see
[
Zie95
,
Grü03
]
)
.

Suppose

\mathcal{V}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}),\,\,v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-d-1}\textup{ and }\mathcal{V}^{*}=(v^{*}_{1},\ldots,v^{*}_{n}),\,\,v_{i}^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}

are vector configurations satisfying the following properties.

(1)

V
and
V^{*}
are full rank matrices.

(2)

V(V^{*})^{\top}=0

(3)

\mathbbm{1}
is the first row of
V^{*}
.

Then, for any
I\subseteq[n]
,
\operatorname{conv}\{v_{i}^{\ast}:i\in[n]\setminus I\}
is a face of
P^{*}=\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{V}^{\ast})
if and only if
0
is in the relative interior of
\operatorname{conv}\{v_{i}:i\in I\}
.

For an index set
I\subseteq[n]
,
0
is in the relative interior of
\operatorname{conv}\{v_{i}:i\in I\}
if and only if there exists
c\geq 0
with
\operatorname{supp}(c)=I
and
Vc=0
. Equivalently
c
is a positive dependence of
\mathcal{V}
with
\operatorname{supp}(c)=I
.
Therefore we have the following consequence.

Corollary 5.2

(see
[
Grü03
, Theorem 5.4.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}^{*}
be as in Theorem
5.1
.
For any
I\subseteq[n]
,
\operatorname{conv}\{v_{i}^{\ast}:i\in[n]\setminus I\}
is a face (facet) of
P^{\ast}=\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{V}^{\ast})
if and only if
I
is the support of a positive dependence (circuit) of
\mathcal{V}
.

We now have the tools to connect
k
-graphical designs to eigenpolytopes. Recall that
G
has
m
eigenspaces, and
\boldsymbol{k}=\{2,\ldots,k\}\subset[m]
.

Theorem 5.3

(See also
[
BT22
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

Let
G=([n],E,w)
be a connected weighted graph with
m
eigenspaces
\Lambda_{1}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m}
.
A set
S\subset[n]
is a (minimal) positively weighted
k
-graphical design of
G
if and only if
[n]\setminus S
indexes the elements of
\mathcal{U}_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}
which lie on a face (facet) of the eigenpolytope
P_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}
.

Proof.

By definition, positively weighted
k
-graphical designs of
G
are positive dependences on the matrix
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
The matrices
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
U_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}
satisfy the hypotheses of Corollary
5.2
: they are full rank, the first row of
U_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}
is the eigenvector
\mathbbm{1}
, and by the orthogonality of eigenspaces,
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}U_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}^{\top}=0.

Therefore the faces (facets) of
P_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}
are dual to the dependences (circuits) of
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
in the sense of Gale duality:
S
indexes the elements of
\mathcal{U}_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}
lying on a face (facet) of
P_{[m]\setminus\boldsymbol{k}}
if and only if
[n]\setminus S
indexes the support of a (minimal) dependence on
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
∎

If one allows negative quadrature weights, arbitrary graphical designs are (essentially) in bijection with the complements of hyperplanes slicing through the interior of the corresponding eigenpolytope. This broader story is briefly touched on in
[
BT22
, Example 3.18]
and its preceding commentary.

Remark 5.5
.

The bijection established in this section remains true if

Remark 5.6
.

One could also define graphical designs for positively weighted graphs using the eigenvectors of

need not be orthogonal, hence the matrices

Theorem

provides an upper bound to the size of a minimal

[

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
G=([n],E,w)
be a connected, positively weighted graph with eigenspaces

\Lambda_{1}<\cdots<\Lambda_{m}
.
For every
k\in[m-1]
there is a positively weighted
k
-graphical design with at most
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dim\Lambda_{i}
vertices.

Proof.

Because
\dim(P_{[m]\setminus{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}\dim\Lambda_{i},
any facet of
P_{[m]\setminus{\boldsymbol{k}}}
has at least
\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}\dim\Lambda_{i}
distinct vertices. By Theorem
5.3
, any minimal positively weighted
k
-design then has at most
n-\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\dim\Lambda_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dim\Lambda_{i}
vertices.
∎

We note that this bound may be tight. The example of an unweighted regular graph and an eigenspace ordering for which this bound is tight for every

given in
[

, Example 3.16]
is also valid here.

Remark 5.9
.

Fix an ordering
\Lambda_{1}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m}
of a graph
G=([n],E,w)
. The

-th element of

lies in the interior of the eigenpolytope

if and only if

-graphical design.

We recall a quick result about combinatorial designs.

Lemma 5.10

(Lemma 2.4 of
[
BT22
]
)
.

If
S\subset[n]
is a combinatorial
k
-design, then so is
[n]\setminus S.

Proof.

Because
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}\perp\mathbbm{1}
,

U_{\boldsymbol{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{S}=0\iff U_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\mathbbm{1}-\mathbbm{1}_{S})=0\iff U_{\boldsymbol{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{[n]\setminus S}=0

Remark 5.11
.

If

is a combinatorial

and

partition

into two disjoint faces. Therefore, if

has a combinatorial

-design, no vertices lie in the interior of

.

We speculate that the symmetry needed for a combinatorial design to exist prevents a graph vertex from being ‘so important’ that it is in every design.

6.
Complexity Results

The hardness of some algorithmic problems for graphical designs follows by uniting all of the established theory. We first recall some basics of polytopes; see
[
Grü03
]
or
[
Zie95
]
for more.
A fundamental result of polyhedral combinatorics is that there are two equivalent definitions of a polytope.
A polytope
P
can be written as the convex hull of finitely many points
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\})
for some
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
, known as a
\mathcal{V}
-description. If
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m},
the size of a
\mathcal{V}
-polytope is
\operatorname{size}(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{size}(v_{i})
.
A polytope can also be written as the bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces,
P=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:Ax\leq b\}
for some
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
and
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, known as an
\mathcal{H}
-description.
If
A\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n\times m}
and
b\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
, the size of an
\mathcal{H}
-polytope is
\operatorname{size}(P)=\operatorname{size}(A)+\operatorname{size}(b)
.

We will assume
0
is in the interior of every polytope.
If
0
is in the interior of
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\})
, then
P^{*}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{x:v_{i}^{\top}x\leq 1\}
is also a polytope, known as the
dual polytope
to
P
. We note that
(P^{*})^{*}=P
.
A
d
-dimensional polytope is
simple
if every vertex is incident to exactly
d
edges, or equivalently, exactly
d
facets. We recall that a polytope is
simplicial
if each of its facets is a simplex. Simplicial polytopes and simple polytopes are dual in this sense: if
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots v_{n}\})
is simple (resp. simplicial), then
P^{*}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{x:v_{i}^{\top}x\leq 1\}
is simplicial (resp. simple).

It was established independently in
[
CKM82
]
and
[
Dye83
]
that it is NP-complete to determine whether an
\mathcal{H}
-polytope is simple. The improvement to strong NP-completeness is due to
[
FLM97
]
.

Lemma 6.1

(
[
CKM82
,
Dye83
,
FLM97
]
)
.

The following decision problem is strongly NP-complete.

Input:
A rational
\mathcal{H}
-polytope
P=\{x:Ax\leq b
}
,
A\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m\times n}
,
b\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
.

Question:
Is
P
nondegenerate (simple)?

Passing to the dual polytope provides an immediate corollary.

Corollary 6.2
.

The following decision problem is strongly NP-complete.

Input:
A rational
\mathcal{V}
-polytope
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots v_{n}\})
,
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}

Question:
Is
P
simplicial?

We now use this corollary to show that it is strongly NP-complete to determine whether a graph has a positively weighted
k
-design of size smaller than the facet bound shown in Theorem
5.7
.

Theorem 6.3
.

The following decision problem is strongly NP-complete.

Input:

(1)

The Laplacian matrix
L=D-A\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n\times n}
of a connected, positively weighted graph
G=([n],E,w)
which has
m
Laplacian eigenspaces
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}<\Lambda_{2}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m}
, and

(2)

an integer
k\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
.

Question:
Is there a positively weighted
k
-graphical design of
G
consisting of fewer than
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dim(\Lambda_{i})
vertices?

Proof.

We first show that this problem is in NP. Given a weight vector
a\geq 0
with
\operatorname{supp}(a)=S\subset[n]
, it is polynomial time in
n
to check whether
a
defines a
k
-graphical design by checking if
U_{\boldsymbol{k}}a=0
and further whether
|S|<\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dim(\Lambda_{i}).

To show strong NP-completeness, we will show that the decision problem in Corollary
6.2
is a sub-problem of the decision problem in the theorem statement. Let
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}^{d})
be a
\mathcal{V}
-polytope with
d\leq n-2
. We note that if
d=n-1
, then the polytope
P
must be a simplex and hence simplicial. By Theorem
3.2
, there is an algorithm with time polynomial in
\operatorname{size}(P)
to create a graph
G=([n],E,w)
with rational positive edge weights and eigenspaces
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\},\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}
for which
P
is affinely equivalent to
P_{\{1,3\}}\simeq P_{\{3\}}
. Consider the eigenspace ordering
\Lambda_{1}<\Lambda_{2}<\Lambda_{3}
. By Theorem
5.3
, the facets of
P
are in bijection with the minimal positively weighted
2
-graphical designs. A facet of
P
is a simplex if and only if it contains exactly
d
vertices. The polytope
P
is then simplicial if and only if every minimal positively weighted
2
-graphical design has exactly
n-d=1+\dim(\Lambda_{2})
vertices. Thus if we could determine whether a graph
G=([n],E,w)
has a
2
-graphical design of size smaller than the bound in Theorem
5.7
, we could determine whether an arbitrary polytope
P
was simplicial. Any
k
-design is also a
k^{\prime}
-design for all
k^{\prime}<k
, so the case of general
k
-designs must also be hard.
∎

We note a further straightforward consequence of Theorem
6.1
which we have not found explicitly stated in the literature. We also credit Alexander E. Black for making the same observation independently.

Corollary 6.4
.

The following problem is NP-hard.

Input:
A rational
\mathcal{V}
-polytope
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\})
,
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
.

Output:
A facet
F=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{j}}\})
of
P
containing the maximum number of vertices of
P
among all facets of
P
.

Proof.

A solution to this problem implies a solution to Corollary
6.2
. Suppose there was an algorithm to find such a facet
F=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{j}}\})
. If
j>d
, then
F
is a non-simplex facet of
P
, hence
P
is not simplicial. If
j=d
, then every facet of
P
has at most
d
vertices by the maximality of
F
. Since a facet of a
d
-dimensional polytope must have at least
d
vertices, it follows that every facet is the convex hull of exactly
d
vertices, i.e. is a simplex. Thus
P
is simplicial.
∎

Thus we can make the following translation to graphical designs.

Theorem 6.5
.

The following problem is NP-hard.

Input:

(1)

The Laplacian matrix
L=D-A\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n\times n}
of a connected, positively weighted graph
G=([n],E,w)
with
m
Laplacian eigenspaces
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}<\Lambda_{2}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m}
, and

(2)

an integer
k\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
.

Output:
A minimum cardinality positively weighted
k
-graphical design.

Proof.

The proof is nearly identical to that of Theorem
6.3
, so we will be brief. A minimum cardinality positively weighted
k
-design is in bijection with a facet of the corresponding eigenpolytope containing a maximum number of eigenpolytope vertices. Given a
\mathcal{V}
-polytope
P
, we can create a positively weighted graph
G
which has an eigenpolytope that is affinely equivalent to
P
in time polynomial in
\operatorname{size}(P)
. Thus an algorithm for this problem implies an algorithm for Corollary
6.4
.
∎

Using a similar argument, we can show that it is #P-complete to count the number of positively weighted
k
-designs of a graph. It was shown independently in
[
Dye83
]
and
[
Lin86
]
that it is #P-complete to count the number of facets of a polytope given by its vertices.

Lemma 6.6

(
[
Dye83
,
Lin86
]
)
.

The following counting problem is #P-complete.

Input:
A rational
\mathcal{V}
-polytope
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\dots v_{n}\})
,
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
.

Output:
Number of facets of
P.

We again use Theorem
3.2
to hide the facet-counting polytope problem in the problem of counting graphical designs.

Theorem 6.7
.

The following counting problem is #P-complete.

Input:

(1)

The Laplacian matrix
L=D-A\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n\times n}
of a connected, positively weighted graph
G=([n],E,w)
which has
m
Laplacian eigenspaces
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}<\Lambda_{2}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m}
, and

(2)

an integer
k\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
.

Output:
The number of minimal positively weighted
k
-graphical designs of
G
.

Proof.

We will show that the counting problem in Lemma
6.6
is a sub-problem of this counting problem. Let
P=\operatorname{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}^{d})
be a polytope with
d\leq n-2
. We note that if
d=n-1
, then the polytope
P
must be a simplex and hence has
n
facets. By Theorem
3.2
, there is a (strongly) polynomial time algorithm to create a graph
G=([n],E,w\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}^{E})
with eigenspaces
\Lambda_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\},\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}
for which
P
is affinely equivalent to
P_{\{1,3\}}\simeq P_{\{3\}}
. Consider the eigenspace ordering
\Lambda_{1}<\Lambda_{2}<\Lambda_{3}
. By Theorem
5.3
, the facets of
P
are in bijection with the minimal positively weighted
2
-graphical designs. Thus any enumeration of the minimal positively weighted
2
-graphical designs of
G
would count the facets of
P
, hence this counting problem must be #P-complete. This problem must be hard for any
k
because a
k
-graphical design is also a
k^{\prime}
-graphical design for all
k^{\prime}<k
.
∎

Many algorithmic polytope questions are open when the dimension is not fixed; we refer to
[
KP03
]
for a survey of algorithmic problems regarding polytopes. In particular, the complexity of providing an
\mathcal{H}
-description of a polytope given a
\mathcal{V}
-description is unknown. This is known as
facet enumeration
or the
convex hull problem
, and is widely believed to be difficult. A complexity result for the facet enumeration would provide a complexity result for the problem of listing all minimal
k
-graphical design, using the same framework as in the proofs of this section.

Remark 6.8
.

We note that it is polynomial time to find a not necessarily minimal
k
-graphical design. A solution to the linear program

(1)

\min c^{\top}x\qquad\text{ s.t. }U_{\boldsymbol{k}}x=0,\mathbbm{1}^{\top}x=1,x\geq 0

provides a positively weighted
k
-graphical design for any cost vector
c\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, though the design need not be minimum or minimal.
We recall that linear programming is polynomial time by
[
Kha79
,
Kar84
]
, and moreover one can find an
extreme point solution
in polynomial time
[
LRS11
, Theorem 2.1.6]
. The Rank Lemma from linear programming guarantees some sparsity of extreme point solutions.

Lemma 6.9
.

[
LRS11
, Lemma 1.2.3]

An extreme point solution
x^{*}
of

\min c^{\top}x\qquad\text{ s.t. }Ax=b,x\geq 0

is zero on
\textup{corank}(A)
coordinates.

Thus an extreme point solution of the LP in (
1
) has
n-k
zero coordinates, which is to say the design contains
k
vertices.
The one-norm is often used as a proxy for sparseness, but the constraints
\mathbbm{1}^{\top}x=1,x\geq 0
imply that
\|x\|_{1}=1
for all feasible
x
in (
1
). While it thus theoretically makes no sense to minimize the one-norm, doing so in practice using MATLAB and Gurobi
[
MAT21
,
Gur23
]
has yielded very sparse graphical designs, often sparser than the guarantee of the Rank Lemma.
