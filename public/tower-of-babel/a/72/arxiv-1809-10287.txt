Electronic spectrum and superconductivity in the t t - J J model on the honeycomb lattice Thanks: The paper is devoted to the 80-th birthday of Professor I.V. Stasyuk.
N.M. Plakida

Abstract
A microscopic theory of electronic spectrum and superconductivity within the t t - J J model on the honeycomb lattice is formulated. The Dyson equation for the normal and anomalous Green functions for the two-band model in terms of the Hubbard operators is derived by applying the Mori-type projection technique. The self-energy is evaluated in the self-consistent Born approximation for electron scattering on spin and charge fluctuations induced by the kinematical interaction for the Hubbard operators. Superconducting pairing mediated by the antiferromagnetic exchange and spin fluctuations is discussed. Key words: strong electron correlations, t t - J J model, honeycomb lattice, superconductivity PACS: 74.20.Mn, 71.27.+a, 71.10.Fd, 74.72.-h

Introduction

One of the crucial issues in the current theory of condensed matter is the study of
superconductivity in strongly correlated electronic systems, as in the cuprate
high-temperature superconductors (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). The basic model commonly
used in these studies is the Hubbard model which is specified by two parameters: the
single-electron hopping matrix element
t
and the single-site Coulomb energy
U
. A
rigorous analytical treatment of the model is based on the Hubbard operator (HO)
technique
[
2
]
since in this representation the local constraint of no double
occupancy of any lattice site is rigorously implemented by the Hubbard operator algebra.
A superconduction pairing due to the kinematical interaction in the Hubbard model in the
limit of strong electron correlations (
U\to\infty
) was first considered by Zaitsev
and Ivanov
[
3
]
who used a diagram technique for the HOs. Subsequently,
superconducting pairing in the Hubbard model was studied by Plakida and
Stasyuk
[
6
]
by applying the equation of motion method for the thermodynamic
Green functions (GFs)
[
7
,
8
]
. In the later studies
[
9
,
10
]

of the
t
-
J
model within
the Mori-type projection technique
[
11
,
12
]
, a formula for the
superconduction temperature similar to
[
3
]
was obtained in the mean-field
approximation (MFA). A self-consistent solution of Dyson equations beyond the MFA for
normal and anomalous GFs and respective self-energies within the
t
-
J
model was
performed in
[
13
]
that confirmed the results of the previous studies in
MFA
[
9
,
10
]
. Detailed studies of the extended Hubbard model on the
square lattice were performed in
[
14
,
15
]
. Taking into account an
intersite Coulomb repulsion and electron-phonon interaction, a microscopic theory of
superconductivity in cuprates was formulated. It was proved that the spin-fluctuation
pairing mechanism induced by the strong kinematical interaction is the leading
interaction which results in high-temperature superconductivity.

In recent years, the two-dimensional carbon honeycomb lattice, the graphene, has been
extensively studied due its peculiar electronic properties (for a review
see
[
16
,
17
]
). Studies of the graphene beyond the simple model of
noninteracting electrons by taking into account the Coulomb interaction reveal a rich
phase diagram with phase transitions to the antiferromagnetic (AF) state, spin-density
wave (SDW), charge-density wave (CDW), and nonconventional superconductivity (SC).
Superconducting phase transitions in the Hubbard model on the honeycomb lattice have
been considered in several publications. The renormalization group approach was used
in
[
18
]
to study phase transitions in the extended Hubbard model with a
moderate on-site interaction
U
. Close to half-filling, the SDW or CDW orders occur,
while for a large doping,
f
-wave triplet-pairing and
d+\mathrm{i}d
-wave singlet-pairing
appear. Using the dynamic cluster approximation for the Hubbard model with
U/t=2-6
, a transition from the
d+\mathrm{i}d
-wave singlet pairing at weak coupling to the
p
-wave
triplet pairing at larger coupling was observed in
[
19
]
.

In the limit of strong correlations,
U\gg t
, two conduction bands of electrons in the
honeycomb lattice split into the singly- and doubly-occupied Hubbard subbands. In this
limit, the Hubbard model can be reduced to the two-band
t
-
J
model for the projected
electron operators. A detailed study of the
t
-
J
model on the honeycomb lattice was
presented in
[
20
]
. The ground-state energy and the staggered magnetization in the
AF phase as function of doping
\delta
have been calculated using the Grassmann tensor
product state approach, exact diagonalization and density-matrix renormalization methods.
The occurrence of the time-reversal symmetry breaking
d+\mathrm{i}d
-wave SC at large doping
was found. Moreover, a coexisting of the SC and AF order was observed for low doping,
0<\delta<0.1
, where the triplet pairing is induced.

In the above cited papers, mostly the phase diagram of the models with the Coulomb
interaction on the honeycomb lattice was studied at zero temperature by numerical
methods. To investigate temperature and doping dependence of the phase diagram and, in
particular, superconduction phase transition analytical methods should be used. The
honeycomb Heisenberg model at half-filling over the whole temperature region both in the
AF and paramagnetic phases was studied in
[
21
]
. The electronic and
spin-fluctuation spectra in this model were studied in
[
22
]
using the
generalized mean-field approximation (GMFA). In the present paper, we formulate a
microscopic theory of superconductivity within the two-band
t
-
J
model on the
honeycomb lattice. We derive Dyson equation for the normal and anomalous matrix GFs with
the self-energy evaluated in the self-consistent Born approximation (SCBA) as for the
one-band
t
-
J
model in
[
13
]
and the Hubbard model
in
[
14
,
15
]
on the square lattice. Electronic spectrum and
superconductivity is considered in the MFA.

In section
2
, we formulate the
t
-
J
model in terms of Hubbard operators.
The Dyson equation is derived in section
3
. The results of calculation of the
electronic spectrum and the superconduction gap function are presented in
section
4
. The conclusion can be found in section
5
.

2
The
t
-
J
model

We consider the
t
-
J
on the honeycomb lattice. The honeycomb lattice is bipartite
with two triangular sublattices A and B. Each of the
N
sites on the A sublattice
is connected to three nearest-neighbor (nn) sites belonging to the B sublattice by
vectors
{{\bm{\delta}}_{\alpha}}
, and
N
sites on B are connected to A by vectors
-{{\bm{\delta}}_{\alpha}}
:

{{\bm{\delta}}_{1}}=\frac{a_{0}}{2}\big(\sqrt{3},-1\big)\,,\;{{\bm{\delta}}_{2}}=-\frac{a_{0}}{2}\big(\sqrt{3},1\big)\,,\;{{\bm{\delta}}_{3}}=a_{0}(0,1).

(2.1)

The basis vectors are
{\bf a}_{1}={{\bm{\delta}}_{3}}-{{\bm{\delta}}_{2}}=({a_{0}}/{2})(\sqrt{3},3)
and
{\bf a}_{2}={{\bm{\delta}}_{3}}-{{\bm{\delta}}_{1}}=({a_{0}}/{2})(-\sqrt{3},3)
, the lattice constant is
a=|{\bf a}_{1}|=|{\bf a}_{2}|=\sqrt{3}a_{0}
, where
a_{0}
is the nn distance; hereafter we put
a_{0}=1
. The
reciprocal lattice vectors are
{\bf k}_{1}=({2\piup}/{3})(\sqrt{3},1)
and
{\bf k}_{2}=({2\piup}/{3})(-\sqrt{3},1)
.

In conventional notation, the
t
-
J
model reads:

H=-t\,\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\sigma}\tilde{a}_{i,\sigma}^{+}\tilde{a}_{j,\sigma}-\mu\sum_{i,\sigma}\,n_{i,\sigma}+\frac{J}{2}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\;\left({\bf S}_{i}\;{\bf S}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}n_{i}\,n_{j}\right),

(2.2)

where for the two-sublattice representation A, B of the site indices on the honeycomb
lattice, a shorter notation
i\alpha\rightarrow i
is used. The projected electron
operators in the singly occupied Hubbard subband in the model (
2.2
) are defined as

\tilde{a}_{i,\sigma}^{+}=a_{i,\sigma}^{+}(1-n_{i,\bar{\sigma}})\;
and
\tilde{a}_{i\sigma}=a_{i\sigma}(1-n_{i,\bar{\sigma}})
for creation and annihilation of an
electron with spin
\sigma/2
(
\sigma=\pm 1,\;\bar{\sigma}=-\sigma
) ,
n_{i,\sigma}=\tilde{a}_{i,\sigma}^{+}\,\tilde{a}_{i,\sigma}
, and
n_{i}=\sum_{\sigma}n_{i,\sigma}
. Here,
t
is the nn electron hopping energy and
J=4t^{2}/U
is the nn
AF exchange interaction for electronic spins

{\bf S}_{i}
.

To take into account, on a rigorous basis, the no-double-occupancy constraint for the
projected electron operators
\tilde{a}_{i,\sigma}^{+}
, we employ the Hubbard operator
(HO) technique
[
2
]
. The HOs
\,X_{i}^{nm}=|i,n\rangle\langle i,m|\,

determine transitions between three possible states on a lattice site
i
:

|i,n\rangle=|i,0\rangle
and
|i,\sigma\rangle
for an empty site and for a singly
occupied site by an electron with spin
\sigma/2
, respectively. The electron number
operator and the spin operators in terms of HOs are defined as

\displaystyle n_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}X_{i}^{\sigma\sigma}=X_{i}^{++}+X_{i}^{--},

(2.3)

\displaystyle S_{i}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\sigma\bar{\sigma}},\quad S_{i}^{z}=(\sigma/2)\,[X_{i}^{\sigma\sigma}-X_{i}^{\bar{\sigma}\bar{\sigma}}].

(2.4)

The completeness relation for the HOs,
\,X_{i}^{00}+\sum_{\sigma}X_{i}^{\sigma\sigma}=1
, rigorously preserves the constraint of no-double-occupancy
for a quantum state
|i,n\rangle
on any lattice site
i
. From the multiplication rule

\,X_{i}^{nm}X_{i}^{kl}=\delta_{mk}X_{i}^{nl}\,
for HOs, there follow the commutation
relations:

\left[X_{i}^{nm},X_{j}^{kl}\right]_{\pm}=\delta_{ij}\left(\delta_{mk}X_{i}^{nl}\pm\delta_{nl}X_{i}^{km}\right).

(2.5)

The upper sign refers to Fermi-type operators such as
X_{i}^{0\sigma}
, while the
lower sign refers to Bose-type operators such as
n_{i}
(
2.3
) or the spin
operators (
2.4
).

Using the Hubbard operator representation for
\tilde{a}_{i\sigma}^{+}=X_{i}^{\sigma 0}
,
\tilde{a}_{j\sigma}=X_{j}^{0\sigma}
and equations (
2.3
) and (
2.4
),
we write the Hamiltonian of the
t
-
J
model (
2.2
) in the form:

\displaystyle H=-t\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\sigma}\,X_{i}^{\sigma 0}\,X_{j}^{0\sigma}-\mu\sum_{i\sigma}X_{i}^{\sigma\sigma}+\frac{J}{4}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\sigma}\,\left(X_{i}^{\sigma\bar{\sigma}}X_{j}^{\bar{\sigma}\sigma}-X_{i}^{\sigma\sigma}X_{j}^{\bar{\sigma}\bar{\sigma}}\right).

(2.6)

The chemical potential
\mu
depends on the average electron occupation number
n=n_{\text{A}}=n_{\text{B}}
,

n_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,\sigma}\langle\,n_{i\alpha,\sigma}\rangle\,,

(2.7)

where
N
is the number of unit cells and
\langle...\rangle
denotes the statistical
average with the Hamiltonian (
2.6
).

3
Dyson equation

To consider superconductivity within the model (
2.6
), we introduce the
anticommutator two-time matrix Green function (GF)
[
7
,
8
]

\displaystyle{\sf G}_{ij\sigma}(t-t^{\prime})=-\text{i}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle\{{\hat{X}}_{i\sigma}(t),\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle\equiv\langle\langle{\hat{X}}_{i\sigma}(t),{\hat{X}}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t^{\prime})\rangle\rangle\,,

(3.1)

where
X(t)={\rm e}^{\text{i}Ht}X{\rm e}^{-\text{i}Ht}
and
\,\theta(x)
is the Heaviside
function. Here, we use Nambu notation and introduce the Hubbard operators in the
two-sublattice representation
\hat{X}_{i\sigma}
and
\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}
where

\hat{X}_{i\sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma}\\
X_{i\text{B}}^{0\sigma}\\
X_{i\text{A}}^{\bar{\sigma}0}\\
X_{i\text{B}}^{\bar{\sigma}0}\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}=\left(X_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma 0}\,X_{j\text{B}}^{\sigma 0}\,X_{j\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{j\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\right).

(3.2)

The Fourier representation in
({\bf k},\omega)
-space is defined by

\displaystyle{\sf G}_{ij\sigma}(t-t^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\piup}{\rm e}^{-\textrm{i}\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\frac{1}{N}\,\sum_{\bf k}{\rm e}^{\textrm{i}{{\bf k}({\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j})}}{\sf G}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega).

(3.3)

The
4\times 4
matrix GF (
3.1
) can be written as

{\sf G}_{ij\sigma}(\omega)=\langle\langle{\hat{X}}_{i\sigma}\mid\hat{X}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}={\hat{G}_{ij\sigma}(\omega)\quad\quad\hat{F}_{ij\sigma}(\omega)\choose\hat{F}_{ij\sigma}^{\dagger}(\omega)\quad-\hat{G}_{ji\bar{\sigma}}(-\omega)},

(3.4)

where the normal
\hat{G}_{ij\sigma}
and anomalous
\hat{F}_{ij\sigma}
components of the
GF are
2\times 2
matrices, which are coupled by the symmetry relations for the
anticommutator retarded GF
[
7
,
8
]
.

To calculate the GF (
3.1
), we use the equation of motion method. Differentiating
the GF with respect to time
t
, its Fourier representation leads to the equation

\displaystyle\omega{\sf G}_{ij\sigma}(\omega)=\delta_{ij}{\sf Q}+\langle\langle[\hat{X}_{i\sigma},H]\,,\,\hat{X}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,,

(3.5)

where
{\sf Q}=\langle\{\hat{X}_{i\sigma},\hat{X}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\}\rangle=\tilde{\tau}_{0}Q\,
. Here,
\tilde{\tau}_{0}
is the
4\times 4
unit matrix and in a
paramagnetic state, the coefficient
\,Q=\langle X_{i\alpha}^{00}+X_{i\alpha}^{\sigma\sigma}\rangle=1-n_{\alpha}/2\,
depends on the occupation number
of electrons (
2.7
) only.

For a system of strongly correlated electrons as in the
t
-
J
model, there is no
well-defined quasi-particle (QP) excitations specified by zeroth-order kinetic energy.
Therefore, it is convenient to choose the mean-field contribution to the energy of QPs
in the equations of motion (
3.5
) as the zeroth-order QP energy. To identify this
contribution, we use the projection operator method developed for the GF
[
12
]
. To this end, we
write the operator
\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}=[\hat{X}_{i\sigma},H]
in (
3.5
) as a sum
of the linear part, proportional to the original operator
\hat{X}_{i\sigma}
, and the
irreducible part
\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}^{(\text{ir})}
orthogonal to
\hat{X}_{i\sigma}
:

\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}=[\hat{X}_{i\sigma},H]=\sum_{l}{\sf E}_{il\sigma}\hat{X}_{l\sigma}+\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}^{(\text{ir})}.

(3.6)

The orthogonality condition

\,\langle\{\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}^{(\text{ir})},\hat{X}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\}\rangle=\langle\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}^{(\text{ir})}\hat{X}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}+\hat{X}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}^{(\text{ir})}\rangle=0\,

determines the linear part, the frequency matrix:

\displaystyle{\sf E}_{ij\sigma}=\langle\{[\hat{X}_{i\sigma},H],\hat{X}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\}\rangle{\sf Q}^{-1}={\hat{E}_{ij}\quad\quad\hat{\Delta}_{ij\sigma}\choose\hat{\Delta}_{ji\sigma}^{*}\quad-\hat{E}_{ji}}\,.

(3.7)

Frequency matrix (
3.7
) determines QP spectrum
\hat{E}_{ij}
and the gap function

\hat{\Delta}_{ij\sigma}
in the GMFA. The corresponding zeroth-order GF in the Fourier
representation reads

{\sf G}^{0}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)=\bigl[\omega\tilde{\tau}_{0}-{\sf E}_{\sigma}({\bf k})\bigr]^{-1}{\sf Q}\,,\quad{\sf E}({\bf k})={\hat{E}({\bf k})\quad\quad\hat{\Delta}_{\sigma}({\bf k})\choose\hat{\Delta}_{\sigma}^{*}({\bf k})\quad-\hat{E}({\bf k})}\,.

(3.8)

Differentiating the multiparticle GF
\,\langle\langle\hat{Z}_{i\sigma}(t)\,,\,\hat{X}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\rangle\rangle\,
in (
3.5
) with respect
to the second time
t^{\prime}
and using the same projection procedure as in (
3.6
) leads to
the Dyson equation for the GF (
3.1
). In the
({\bf k},\omega)
-representation, the
Dyson equation reads

\left[{\sf G}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)\right]^{-1}=\left[{\sf G}_{\sigma}^{0}({\bf k},\omega)\right]^{-1}-\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega).

(3.9)

The self-energy operator
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)
is defined by the

proper
part of the scattering matrix which has no parts connected by the zeroth
order GF (
3.8
):

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)={\sf Q}^{-1}\langle\langle{\hat{Z}}_{{\bf k}\sigma}^{(\text{ir})}\mid{\hat{Z}}_{{\bf k}\sigma}^{(\text{ir})\dagger}\rangle\rangle^{(\text{prop})}_{\omega}\;{\sf Q}^{-1}.

(3.10)

The self-energy operator (
3.10
) can be written in the same form as GF (
3.4
):

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij\sigma}(\omega)={\sf Q}^{-1}\,{\hat{M}_{ij}(\omega)\quad\quad\hat{\Phi}_{ij\sigma}(\omega)\choose\hat{\Phi}_{ij\sigma}^{\dagger}(\omega)\quad-\hat{M}_{ji}(-\omega)}{\sf Q}^{-1}\,.

(3.11)

The normal
\hat{M}
and anomalous (pair)
\hat{\Phi}
components of the self-energy
operator (
3.11
) are given by the
2\times 2
matrices [see (
3.34
),
(
3.35
)].

The system of equations for the
(4\times 4)
matrix GF (
3.4
) and the self-energy
(
3.11
) can be reduced to a system of equations for the normal
{\hat{G}}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)
and anomalous
{\hat{F}}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)

(2\times 2)

matrix components. By using representations for the frequency matrix (
3.7
) and the
self-energy (
3.11
), we derive the following system of matrix equations for them:

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl[\hat{G}_{N}({\bf k},\omega)^{-1}+\hat{\varphi}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)\,\hat{G}_{N}({\bf k},-\omega)\,\hat{\varphi}^{*}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)\Bigr]^{-1}{Q}\,,

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\hat{F}}^{{\dagger}}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\hat{G}_{N}({\bf k},-\omega)\,\hat{\varphi}^{{\dagger}}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)\,\hat{G}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega).

(3.13)

In (
3.12
), we introduced the normal state matrix GF and the matrix superconduction
gap function:

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{N}({\bf k},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl[\omega\hat{\tau}_{0}-\hat{\varepsilon}({\bf k})-\hat{M}({\bf k},\omega)/\hat{Q}\Bigr]^{-1},

(3.14)

\displaystyle{\hat{\varphi}}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{\sigma}({\bf k})+\hat{\Phi}_{\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)/\hat{Q}.

(3.15)

To obtain a closed system of equations, we should evaluate the multiparticle GF in the
self-energy operator (
3.11
) which describes the processes of inelastic scattering
of electrons on charge and spin fluctuations due to the kinematic interaction.

3.1
Mean-field approximation

The superconducting pairing in the Hubbard model already occurs in the MFA and is caused
by the kinetic exchange interaction as proposed by Anderson
[
24
,
25
]
. It is,
therefore, reasonable to consider the MFA described by zeroth-order GF (
3.8
)
separately. Using commutation relations for Hubbard operators (
2.5
), we evaluate the
frequency matrix (
3.7
). The matrix
\hat{E}({\bf k})
determines the QP spectrum in
the normal phase (see
[
22
]
):

\displaystyle\hat{E}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j}\exp[\text{i}{{\bf k}({\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j})}]\hat{E}_{ij}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon_{\text{A}}({\bf k})&\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})\\
\varepsilon_{\text{BA}}({\bf k})&\varepsilon_{\text{B}}({\bf k})\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{A}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\{[X_{{\bf k}\text{A}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{{\bf k}\text{A}}^{\sigma 0}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},\quad\varepsilon_{\text{B}}({\bf k})=\langle\{[X_{{\bf k}\text{B}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{{\bf k}\text{B}}^{\sigma 0}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\{[X_{{\bf k}\text{A}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{{\bf k}\text{B}}^{\sigma 0}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},\quad\varepsilon_{\text{BA}}({\bf k})=(\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k}))^{\dagger}.

(3.19)

The solution of the matrix equation for the zero-order GF (
3.8
) in the normal state
reads:

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{0}_{N}({\bf k},\omega)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}G^{0}_{\text{AA}}({\bf k},\omega)\quad G^{0}_{\text{AB}}({\bf k},\omega)\\
G^{0}_{\text{BA}}({\bf k},\omega)\quad G^{0}_{\text{BB}}({\bf k},\omega)\\
\end{array}\right)=\frac{Q}{D({\bf k},\omega)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega-\varepsilon_{\text{B}}({\bf k})\quad\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})\\
\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})^{*}\quad\omega-\varepsilon_{\text{A}}({\bf k})\\
\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle D({\bf k},\omega)=[\omega-\varepsilon_{\text{A}}({\bf k})][\omega-\varepsilon_{\text{B}}({\bf k})]-|\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})|^{2}=[\omega-\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})][\omega-\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})].

(3.25)

The electronic spectrum has two branches:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\pm}({\bf k})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\varepsilon_{\text{A}}({\bf k})+\varepsilon_{\text{B}}({\bf k})\right]\pm\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\left[\varepsilon_{\text{A}}({\bf k})-\varepsilon_{\text{B}}({\bf k})\right]^{2}+4|\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})|^{2}\Big\}^{1/2}=\varepsilon({\bf k})\pm|\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})|\,,

(3.26)

where we take into account the fact that in the paramagnetic state, the sublattices are equivalent
and, therefore,
\varepsilon_{\text{A}}({\bf k})=\varepsilon_{\text{B}}({\bf k})=\varepsilon({\bf k})
. We calculate the spectrum and consider the Fermi surface in the normal state in the
next section.

Now, we evaluate the anomalous component
\hat{\Delta}_{ij\sigma}
of the matrix
(
3.7
), which determines the superconducting gap. Similar to the normal-state
frequency matrix (
3.19
), we obtain the representation

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{\sigma}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j}\exp[\text{i}{{\bf k}({\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j})}]\,\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Delta_{ij,\text{A}\sigma}&\Delta_{ij,\text{AB}\sigma}\\
\Delta_{ij,\text{BA}\sigma}&\Delta_{ij,\text{B}\sigma}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}({\bf k})&\Delta_{\text{AB}\sigma}({\bf k})\\
\Delta_{\text{BA}\sigma}({\bf k})&\Delta_{\text{B}\sigma}({\bf k})\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\{[X_{{\bf k}\text{A}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{{\bf k}\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},\quad\Delta_{\text{B}\sigma}({\bf k})=\langle\{[X_{{\bf k}\text{B}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{{\bf k}\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{AB}\sigma}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\{[X_{{\bf k}\text{A}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{{\bf k}\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},\quad\Delta_{\text{BA}\sigma}({\bf k})=\Delta_{\text{AB}\sigma}^{\dagger}({\bf k}).

(3.32)

Diagonalization of the gap matrix (
3.32
) shows a two-gap structure:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}_{\sigma}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big[\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}({\bf k})+\Delta_{\text{B}\sigma}({\bf k})\big]\pm\frac{1}{2}\Big\{[\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}({\bf k})-\Delta_{\text{B}\sigma}({\bf k})]^{2}+4|\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})|^{2}\Big\}^{1/2}

(3.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}({\bf k})\pm|\Delta_{\text{AB}\sigma}({\bf k})|\,,

where we take into account the fact that in the paramagnetic state, the sublattices are equivalent
and, therefore,
\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}({\bf k})=\Delta_{\text{B}\sigma}({\bf k})=\Delta_{\sigma}({\bf k})
. We consider the gap components (
3.32
) in the next
section.

3.2
Self-energy operator

The normal
\hat{M}
and anomalous (pair)
\hat{\Phi}
components of the self-energy
operator (
3.11
) are given by the
2\times 2
matrices:

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{ij}(\omega)=\bigg\langle\bigg\langle{[\widetilde{X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma}},H]\choose[\widetilde{X_{i\text{B}}^{0\sigma}},H]}\mid([H,\widetilde{X_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma 0}}]\,[H,\widetilde{X_{j\text{B}}^{\sigma 0}}])\bigg\rangle\bigg\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega},\quad

(3.34)

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{ij\sigma}(\omega)=\bigg\langle\bigg\langle{[\widetilde{X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma}},H]\choose[\widetilde{X_{i\text{B}}^{0\sigma}},H]}\mid([H,\widetilde{X_{j\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}}][H,\widetilde{X_{j\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}}])\bigg\rangle\bigg\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega},\quad

(3.35)

where
\,[\widetilde{X_{i\alpha}^{n,m}},H]\,
are the irreducible parts of the
commutators determined by equation (
3.6
). Taking into account equations for the
[X_{i\alpha}^{0\sigma},H]
operators, we obtain the following representation for the
matrix elements of the normal self-energy

\displaystyle{M}_{ij\text{A}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{2}\sum_{l,\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime\prime}}\,\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}X_{l\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}\mid X_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}0}\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega}

(3.36)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{J^{2}}{4}\sum_{l,\sigma^{\prime}}\,\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime\prime}}\,\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{l\text{B}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}\mid X_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}0}\widetilde{B}_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega},

\displaystyle M_{ij\text{AB}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ t^{2}\sum_{l,\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime\prime}}\,\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}X_{l\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}\mid X_{k\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}0}\widetilde{B}_{j\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega}

(3.37)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{J^{2}}{4}\sum_{l,\sigma^{\prime}}\,\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime\prime}}\,\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{l\text{B}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}\mid X_{jB}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}0}\widetilde{B}_{k\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega},

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{i\alpha}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=(S_{i\alpha}^{z}-n_{i\alpha}/2)\,\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}+X_{i\alpha}^{{\sigma}^{\prime}\sigma}\,\delta_{\sigma^{\prime},\bar{\sigma}}\,,\quad(\widetilde{B}_{i\alpha}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}})^{\dagger}=\widetilde{B}_{i\alpha}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}.

(3.38)

In (
3.36
), (
3.37
), we take into account only the diagonal in respect to the
interaction terms. Using equations for the operators
[X_{i\alpha}^{\bar{\sigma}0},H]
, a
similar representation is derived for the anomalous matrix elements (
3.35
):

\displaystyle{\Phi}_{ij\text{A}}(\omega)=\langle\langle[\widetilde{X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma}},H]\mid[H,\widetilde{X_{j\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}}]\rangle\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega},\quad

(3.39)

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij\text{AB}}(\omega)=\langle\langle[\widetilde{X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma}},H]\mid[H,\widetilde{X_{j\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}}]\rangle\rangle^{\text{(pp)}}_{\omega}.

(3.40)

To calculate the self-energy matrix elements in (
3.36
), (
3.37
), and
(
3.39
), (
3.40
), we use the SCBA for the multiparticle GFs. For this, it is
convenient to write the self-energy in terms of time-dependent correlation functions. In
particular, for the normal component (
3.36
), we have the spectral representation:

\displaystyle{M}_{ij\text{A}}(\omega)=\sum_{l,\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime\prime}}\,\frac{1}{2\piup}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\frac{{\rm e}^{\,\beta z}+1}{(\omega-z)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t{\rm e}^{\text{i}zt}

\displaystyle\times\;\Big[t^{2}\langle X_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}0}\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma}\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)X_{l\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}(t)\rangle+\frac{J^{2}}{4}\langle X_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}0}\widetilde{B}_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma}\widetilde{B}_{l\text{B}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}(t)\rangle\Big].

(3.41)

In the SCBA, a propagation of excitations described by the Fermi-like operators
\,\hat{X}_{i}^{\sigma 0}\,
and the Bose-like operators
\widetilde{B}_{j}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}

for
i\neq j
is assumed to be independent. Therefore, the corresponding time-dependent
multiparticle correlation functions can be written as a product of fermionic and
bosonic correlation functions,

\displaystyle\langle X_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}0}\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma}\;\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)X_{l\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}(t)\rangle=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\,\langle X_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime}0}X_{l\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}(t)\rangle\;\langle\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)\rangle.

(3.42)

The time-dependent correlation functions in (
3.42
) are calculated self-consistently
using the corresponding GFs, as e.g.,

\displaystyle\langle X_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime}0}X_{j\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega n(\omega){\rm e}^{-\text{i}\omega t}[-(1/\piup)]\,{\rm Im}\langle\langle X_{j\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}|X_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime}0}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,,

(3.43)

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega N(\omega){\rm e}^{-\text{i}\omega t}[-(1/\piup)]\,{\rm Im}\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,,

(3.44)

where
n(\omega)
and
N(\omega)
are the Fermi- and Bose-function, respectively.
Integration over time
t
in (
3.41
) yields

\displaystyle{M}_{ij\text{A}}(\omega)=\sum_{l,k}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}}\,\int\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}\omega_{2}}{\piup^{2}}\;\frac{1-n(\omega_{1})+N(\omega_{2})}{\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\;\Big(t^{2}{\rm Im}\langle\langle X_{l\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}|X_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime}0}\rangle\rangle_{\omega_{1}}\,{\rm Im}\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle_{\omega_{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{J^{2}}{4}{\rm Im}\langle\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}}|X_{j\text{B}}^{\sigma^{\prime}0}\rangle\rangle_{\omega_{1}}\,{\rm Im}\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{l\text{B}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|\widetilde{B}_{k\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle_{\omega_{2}}\Big).

(3.45)

Taking into account the definition of the bosonic operator (
3.38
), the bosonic GFs
in these equations can be written as

\displaystyle\langle\langle\widetilde{B}_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|\widetilde{B}_{j\text{A}}^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}=\frac{1}{4}\langle\langle n_{i\text{A}}|n_{j\text{A}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}+\langle\langle S^{z}_{i\text{A}}|S^{z}_{j\text{A}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}+\langle\langle X^{\bar{\sigma}\sigma}_{i\text{A}}|X^{\sigma\bar{\sigma}}_{j\text{A}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}\,.

(3.46)

After summation over
\sigma^{\prime}
in equation (
3.45
) for the bosonic GF (
3.46
) and
taking into account that the normal GF in the paramagnetic state is independent of spin,
the spin-fluctuation contribution can be written in the form:

\displaystyle\langle\langle S^{z}_{i\text{A}}|S^{z}_{j\text{A}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,+\langle\langle X^{\bar{\sigma}\sigma}_{i\text{A}}|X^{\sigma\bar{\sigma}}_{j\text{A}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}=\langle\langle{\bf S}_{i\text{A}}|{\bf S}_{j\text{A}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\equiv-\chi_{ij,\text{A}}(\omega).

(3.47)

Introducing the
\bf k
-representation for the GFs in the self-energy (
3.45
), we
obtain the expression:

\displaystyle{M}_{\text{A}}({\bf k},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bf q}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!{\rm d}z\,\frac{1}{\piup}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\Omega\frac{1+N(\Omega)-n(z)}{\omega-z-\Omega}\,

(3.48)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\Big\{t^{2}\,\left[-(1/\piup)\right]\,{\rm Im}\langle\langle X_{\text{A}{\bf q}}^{0\sigma}|X_{\text{A}{\bf q}}^{\sigma 0}\rangle\rangle_{z}\,{\rm Im}\chi_{\text{A}}({\bf k-q},\Omega)

\displaystyle+\frac{J^{2}}{4}\,[-(1/\piup)]\,{\rm Im}\langle\langle X_{\text{A}{\bf q}}^{0\sigma}|X_{\text{B}{\bf q}}^{\sigma 0}\rangle\rangle_{z}{\rm Im}\chi_{\text{BA}}({\bf k-q},\Omega)\Big\}.

The SCBA for
{M}_{ij\text{AB}}(\omega)
(
3.37
) and for the anomalous self-energies
(
3.39
), (
3.40
) gives similar results.

To find out the normal state GF (

) should be calculated. Similar calculations of the
anomalous self-energy (

)

). However,
implementation of this program demands complicated self-consistent numerical
computations for the two-band

-

similar calculations performed for the one-band
t
-

in
[

. Therefore, in the present paper, we restrict our consideration only

4.1
Electronic spectrum

Calculation of the matrix elements (
3.19
) gives the following results:

\displaystyle\varepsilon({\bf k})=-\mu+\frac{3t}{Q}\,D_{1}-\frac{3J}{4}n_{\alpha}+\frac{3J}{2Q}\,C_{1}\equiv-\widetilde{\mu}\,,\;

(4.1)

where we introduce the nn correlation functions for electrons and spins,

\displaystyle D_{1}=\langle X^{\sigma 0}_{i\text{A}}X^{0\sigma}_{i+\delta_{1},\text{B}}\rangle\,,\quad C_{1}=\langle S^{z}_{i\text{A}}{S}^{z}_{i+\delta_{1},\text{B}}\rangle.

(4.2)

For the off-diagonal energy, we have:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})=-\widetilde{t}\,\gamma_{1}({\bf k})\,,\quad\widetilde{t}=tQ\left(1+\frac{3C_{1}}{2Q^{2}}\right)+J\frac{D_{1}}{2Q}\,,

(4.3)

where
\gamma_{1}({\bf k})=\sum_{\alpha}\exp(\text{i}{\bf k}\bm{\delta}_{\alpha})
and
|\gamma_{1}({\bf k})|^{2}={1+4\cos(\sqrt{3}k_{x}/2)[\cos(\sqrt{3}k_{x}/2)+\cos({3}k_{y}/{2})]}
. Thus, the electronic spectrum has two branches

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\pm}({\bf k})=-\mu\pm\widetilde{t}\,|\gamma_{1}({\bf k})|\,,

(4.4)

which is similar to the spectrum of the graphene (see, e.g., reference
[
16
]
) but with
the renormalized chemical potential
\widetilde{\mu}
due to strong correlations,
equation (
4.1
), and the hopping parameter
\widetilde{t}\,
, equation (
4.3
).
Therefore, the spectrum shows a cone-type behaviour at Dirac points
K
and
K^{\prime}
at
the corners of the graphene Brillouin zone (BZ) as shown in figure
1
. In
figure
2
, the BZ and the Fermi surfaces (FS) are shown for holes at the electronic
occupation numbers
n=0.95
,
0.76,
and
0.7
. At
n\lesssim 1
, the hole FS is
small and centered at the
\Gamma
point. With a decreasing
n
, the FS becomes larger, and
at some characteristic value
n_{0}=0.76
, the FS touches the BZ at M-points. At a larger
hole doping, six pockets centered at the
K
-points emerge which shrink to points for
the half-filled band at
n=2/3
.

spectrum

in units of
\widetilde{t}

at
\tilde{\mu}=0
.

For the diagonal GF, we have

\displaystyle G_{\alpha\alpha,\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{Q}{2}\,\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})-\omega}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})-\omega}\right].

(4.5)

The average occupation number of electrons in one sublattice is equal to

\displaystyle n_{\alpha\sigma}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle X_{{\bf k}\alpha}^{\sigma\sigma}\rangle=\langle X_{{\bf k}\alpha}^{\sigma 0}X_{{\bf k}\alpha}^{0\sigma}\rangle

(4.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{{\rm e}^{\omega/T}+1}\,\left[-\frac{1}{\piup}\right]\mbox{Im}G_{\alpha\alpha,\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)\,,

which yields in MFA

n_{\alpha,\sigma}({\bf k})=Q\,\frac{1}{2}[N(\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k}))+N(\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k}))],

(4.7)

where
N(\varepsilon_{\pm}({\bf k}))=\{\exp[\varepsilon_{\pm}({\bf k})/T]+1\}^{-1}
,
and the average number of electrons in one sublattice is given by

\displaystyle n_{\alpha}=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{{\bf k}}n_{\alpha\sigma}({\bf k})

(4.8)

with
n_{\alpha}\leqslant 1
. For the off-diagonal GF in equation (
3.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle G_{\text{AB},\sigma}({\bf k},\omega)=Q\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})}{2\,|\varepsilon_{\text{AB}}({\bf k})|}\Big[\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})-\omega}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})-\omega}\Big].

(4.9)

For the nn correlation function, we get

\displaystyle\langle X_{{\bf k}\text{B}}^{\sigma 0}X_{{\bf k}\text{A}}^{0\sigma}\rangle=Q\,\frac{\gamma_{1}({\bf k})}{2|\gamma_{1}({\bf k})|}[N(\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k}))-N(\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k}))].

(4.10)

These results can be used in calculating the self-energy in the normal state
(
3.48
).

4.2
Gap equation

The matrix elements of the gap function (
3.32
) are given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij,\text{A}\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\{[X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{j\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},\quad\Delta_{ij,\text{AB}\sigma}=\langle\{[X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma},H],X_{j\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1}.

(4.11)

Using the commutation relations for the HOs, for these functions we obtain the following
representation:

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij,\text{A}\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime}}\,\langle\{B_{i\text{A}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}X_{k\text{B}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}},X_{j\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\}\rangle{Q}^{-1}+\frac{J}{2}\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime}}\,\langle\{(B_{k\text{B}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}-\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}})X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma^{\prime}},X_{j\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\}\rangle{Q}^{-1}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\delta_{i,j}\sum_{k}\,\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\sigma}\,X_{k\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}-X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{k\text{B}}^{0\sigma}\rangle{Q}^{-1}=\delta_{i,j}2t\sum_{\delta_{\alpha}}\,\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{i+\delta_{\alpha}\text{B}}^{0\sigma}\rangle{Q}^{-1},

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij,\text{AB}\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J}{2}\sum_{k,\sigma^{\prime}}\,\delta_{k,j}\left(\delta_{\sigma^{\prime},\bar{\sigma}}\langle X_{j\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\rangle-\delta_{\sigma^{\prime},{\sigma}}\langle X_{j\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{i\text{A}}^{0{\sigma}}\rangle\right){Q}^{-1}

(4.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-J\,\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{j\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}\rangle\;{Q}^{-1},

where the symmetry relations were used:
\,\langle X_{j\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\rangle=-\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{j\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}\rangle\,
and
\,\langle X_{j\text{B}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{i\text{A}}^{0{\sigma}}\rangle=-\langle X_{j\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\rangle\,
.

Fourier transformation results in the gap function components:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2t\,\sum_{\delta_{\alpha}}\,\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{i+\delta_{\alpha}\text{B}}^{0\sigma}\rangle{Q}^{-1},

(4.14)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{AB}\sigma}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-J\sum_{\delta_{\alpha}}\,\exp[\text{i}{{\bf k}\bm{\delta}_{\alpha}}]\ \langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{i+\delta_{\alpha}\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}\rangle{Q}^{-1},

(4.15)

where
{\bm{\delta}}_{\alpha}=\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3}
. Introducing the
bond-dependent anomalous correlation functions

\displaystyle F^{\alpha}_{\text{AB}\sigma}=\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{i+\delta_{\alpha}\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}\rangle\,,

(4.16)

the gaps can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}=2t\,\sum_{\delta_{\alpha}}\,F^{\alpha}_{\text{AB}\sigma}{Q}^{-1},\quad\Delta_{\text{AB}\sigma}({\bf k})=-J\sum_{\delta_{\alpha}}\,\exp[\text{i}{{\bf k}\bm{\delta}_{\alpha}}]\,F^{\alpha}_{\text{AB}\sigma}{Q}^{-1}.

(4.17)

Here, the nn correlation function (
4.16
) is calculated using the spectral
representation:

\displaystyle\langle X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}X_{j\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega n(\omega)[-(1/\piup)]\,{\rm Im}\langle\langle X_{j\text{B}}^{0{\sigma}}|X_{i\text{A}}^{0\bar{\sigma}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,.

(4.18)

For the bond-independent correlation functions (
s
-wave pairing),
F^{\alpha}_{\text{AB}\sigma}=F_{\text{AB}\sigma}
, we have the results similar to the normal state energy:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{A}\sigma}=6tF_{\text{AB}\sigma}\;{Q}^{-1},\quad\Delta_{\text{AB}\sigma}({\bf k})=-J\gamma({\bf k})F_{\text{AB}\sigma}\;{Q}^{-1}.

(4.19)

Using the GMFA for the anomalous GF in (
4.18
), we can derive the Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) type equations as was proposed in several publications for electrons with the Dirac spectrum. In particular, in
[
23
]
, the
s
-wave BCS model was considered with a phenomenological coupling constant. In the case of the two-subband honeycomb lattice, we expect to obtain a more complicated pairing symmetry, e.g.,
d+\mathrm{i}d
singlet pairing or
p
triplet pairing as proposed in
[
20
]
. In the case of the
t
-
J
on the square lattice in
[
13
]
, we have also found a more complicated
d
-wave pairing induced by spin-fluctuations. A self-consistent solution of the gap equations (
4.17
) with microscopic coupling constants and anomalous GF (
3.13
) and a resulting
equation for determining the superconduction transition temperature will be considered
in a subsequent publication.

5
Conclusions

In the present paper, a microscopic theory of the electron spectrum and superconductivity
within the two-band
t
-
J
model for strongly correlated electrons on the honeycomb
lattice is presented. Using the projection operator method for
thermodynamic GFs, we derived a self-consistent system of equations for the matrix GF
and the self-energy in the SCBA. The latter is similar to the Migdal-Eliasberg
strong-coupling theory for the electron-phonon system. In the present paper, we consider
only the results obtained in the MFA.
However, as it was pointed out in the microscopic theory of superconductivity
formulated for the Hubbard model on the square lattice in
[
14
,
15
]
,
consideration of the self-energy effects is very important. The normal self-energy
component determines a reduction of the QP weight which can be quite large. The anomalous
(pair) self-energy component strongly enhances the superconduction pairing induced by
the kinematical interaction of electrons with spin-fluctuations. This interaction being
proportional to the hopping parameter
t
[see equation (
3.48
)] is an order
of magnitude larger than the
exchange interaction
J
considered in the Anderson’s theory of the resonating valence
bonds
[
24
,
25
]
. We are planning to take into account the self-energy effects in
a subsequent publication.
