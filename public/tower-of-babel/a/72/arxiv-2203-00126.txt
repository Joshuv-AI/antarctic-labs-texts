Learning Low-Dimensional Nonlinear Structures from High-Dimensional Noisy Data: An Integral Operator Approach
Xiucai Ding, Rong Ma

Abstract
We propose a kernel-spectral embedding algorithm for learning low-dimensional nonlinear structures from noisy and high-dimensional observations, where the data sets are assumed to be sampled from a nonlinear manifold model and corrupted by high-dimensional noise. The algorithm employs an adaptive bandwidth selection procedure which does not rely on prior knowledge of the underlying manifold. The obtained low-dimensional embeddings can be further utilized for downstream purposes such as data visualization, clustering and prediction. Our method is theoretically justified and practically interpretable. Specifically, for a general class of kernel functions, we establish the convergence of the final embeddings to their noiseless counterparts when the dimension grows polynomially with the size, and characterize the effect of the signal-to-noise ratio on the rate of convergence and phase transition. We also prove the convergence of the embeddings to the eigenfunctions of an integral operator defined by the kernel map of some reproducing kernel Hilbert space capturing the underlying nonlinear structures. Our results hold even when the dimension of the manifold grows with the sample size. Numerical simulations and analysis of real data sets show the superior empirical performance of the proposed method, compared to many existing methods, on learning various nonlinear manifolds in diverse applications.

Introduction

With rapid technological advancements in data collection and processing, massive large-scale and high-dimensional data sets are widely available nowadays in diverse research fields such as astronomy, business analytics, human genetics and microbiology. A common feature of these data sets is that their statistical and geometric properties can be well understood via a meaningful low-rank representation of reduced dimensionality. Learning low-dimensional structures from these high-dimensional noisy data is one of the central topics in statistics and data science.
Moreover, nonlinear structures have been found predominant and intrinsic in many real-world data sets, which may not be easily captured or preserved in commonly used linear or quasi-linear methods such as principal component analysis (PCA), singular value decomposition (SVD)
[
53
]
and multidimensional scaling (MDS)
[
18
]
. As a longstanding and well-recognized technique for analyzing data sets with possibly nonlinear structures, kernel methods have been shown effective in various applications ranging from clustering, data visualization to classification and prediction
[
75
,
47
,
59
]
. On the other hand, spectral methods
[
23
]
, as a fundamental tool for dimension reduction, are oftentimes applied in combination with kernel methods to better capture the underlying low-dimensional nonlinear structure in the data. These approaches are commonly referred to as nonlinear dimension reduction techniques; see Section
1.1
below for a brief overview.

Despite the effectiveness and success of the kernel-spectral methods in many applications, these methods are usually applied heuristically, especially in terms of tuning parameter selection and interpretation of the results. For example, as a key step in these methods,
the construction of the kernel (or affinity) matrices requires specifying a proper bandwidth parameter, which is usually determined by certain heuristics or conventional empiricism. This is mainly due to a lack of theoretical understanding of the methods, including their intrinsic objective, their range of applicability, and their susceptibility to noise or dimensionality of the data. Likewise, the absence of a theoretical foundation may significantly constrain the users’ recognition or exploitation of the full potential of the method. Given the indispensable status and the practical power of these methods in many fields of applications, such as single-cell transcriptomics
[
94
,
68
,
56
]
and medical informatics
[
2
,
80
]
, there is a pressing need of theoretically justified kernel-spectral method that accounts for both the high-dimensionality and the noisiness of the data.

In this study, we focus on an integral operator approach to learning low-dimensional nonlinear structures from high-dimensional noisy data. We propose a kernel-spectral embedding algorithm with a data-adaptive bandwidth selection procedure (cf. Algorithm
1
), justified by rigorous theoretical analysis, and provide solid interpretations of the low-dimensional embeddings by establishing their asymptotic behavior.

Specifically, we consider a manifold "signal-plus-noise" data-generative model

\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

(1)

where
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are observed samples,
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are the underlying noiseless samples drawn independently from a nonlinear manifold model, embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{p}
(see Assumption
1
below for more detail), and
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are independent sub-Gaussian noise (see Section
1.3
for the definition) with covariance
\sigma^{2}{\bf I}
. As will be seen in Section
3.1
, (
1
) is closely related to the spiked covariance model
[
52
]
extensively studied in the past two decades. Throughout, we focus on the high-dimensional setting where the number of variables
p
grows polynomially with the sample size
n
, that is, for some constant
0<\eta<\infty
, we have

p\asymp n^{\eta}.

(2)

As a general and important problem in nonlinear dimension reduction and manifold learning, our goal is to find a well-justified low-dimensional embedding of
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
that captures the nonlinear structure of the underlying manifold, as encapsulated in the noiseless random samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
. To facilitate our discussion, we construct the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
using the observations
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}
as follows

\mathbf{K}_{n}=\big(K(i,j)\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},\qquad K(i,j)=f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}}{h^{1/2}_{n}}\right),

(3)

for some function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
and
h_{n}>0
is some bandwidth.

1.1
Some Related Works

Unlike our general setup (
1
) under high-dimensionality (
2
), most of the existing works concern computational algorithms for learning nonlinear manifolds, developed under either the noiseless (i.e.,
\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}
) or low-dimensional settings where
p
is fixed. These methods can be roughly separated into two categories, depending on how the data is conceptually represented. On the one hand, there are spectral graph based methods, including Laplacian eigenmap
[
10
]
, diffusion map (DM)
[
26
]
, vector diffusion map (VDM)
[
82
]
, ISOMAP
[
87
]
, maximal variance unfolding (MVU)
[
96
]
, locally linear embedding (LLE)
[
72
]
, Hessian LLE
[
35
]
, t-SNE
[
88
]
, UMAP
[
66
]
and local tangent space alignment (LTSA)
[
101
]
, among many others. For more details of these methods, see
[
60
,
89
]
. Generally speaking, these methods start by constructing a possibly sparse graph using kernels in which the nodes represent the input objects and the edges represent the neighborhood relations. Consequently, the resulting graph can be viewed as a discretized approximation of the manifold sampled by the inputs. From these graphs, one may apply results from spectral graph theory and construct matrices whose spectral decomposition reveals the low-dimensional structure of the manifold. Incidentally, these methods have been used to facilitate various downstream statistical applications, such as spectral clustering
[
91
]
, regression analysis
[
24
]
, among others.
The main differences between these methods are the kernels used to construct the spectral graphs and the associated nonlinear eigenfunctions (or feature maps
[
47
]
) employed for embedding (or data representation). On the other hand, there are reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) based methods, that use
eigenfunctions of some kernel map of the RKHS to infer the nonlinear structures of the manifold based on noiseless observations
[
75
,
47
,
59
]
. Specifically, these methods start from a certain similarity matrix based on some positive kernel function, and infer the geometric properties from the integral operator associated with the kernel. Similarly, various downstream tasks such as clustering, regression and classification
[
47
,
73
,
79
,
92
]
have been treated in combination with the RKHS-based methods. In addition to these, kernel principal component analysis (kPCA)
[
74
]
has also been widely used.

From a mathematical viewpoint, in the low-dimensional and noiseless setting, theoretical properties of some of these methods have been explored. For the graph-based methods, existing theoretical results reveal that, under some regularity conditions, the discretized approximation obtained from various kernel-spectral methods will converge to a continuum quantity, which is usually represented as a certain operator capturing the underlying structures of the manifold. For example, it has been shown that the discrete Graph Laplacian (GL) matrices (cf. (
9
) and (
10
)) used by Laplacian eigenmap and DM would converge to the Laplace-Beltrami operator of the manifold under various settings and assumptions
[
11
,
21
,
37
,
44
,
45
,
81
,
83
,
98
]
. For the LLE, as shown in
[
99
]
, after being properly normalized, the similarity matrix would converge to the LLE kernel which is closely related to the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Convergence of t-SNE, MVU, VDM and LTSA have been studied in
[
5
,
62
,
20
,
4
,
82
,
86
]
. For the RKHS-based methods, it has been shown that the empirical eigenvalues and eigenfunctions would converge to those of an integral operator associated with some reproducing kernel
[
19
,
58
,
70
,
79
,
84
,
85
,
92
]
. Finally, kPCA has been studied under the noiseless setting
[
15
]
.

Another important difference between the graph-based methods and the RKHS-based methods lies in the treatment of the kernel matrices (cf. (
3
)). The graph-based methods usually involve some GL-type operations, where the kernel affinity matrix is normalized by the graph degrees
[
32
,
89
]
, whereas the RKHS-based methods commonly employ the kernel matrix directly without further normalization. Consequently, in terms of interpretations, existing theory under the low-dimensional noiseless setting indicates that the embeddings from the graph-based methods are associated with the eigenfunctions of Laplace-Beltrami operator of
\mathcal{M}
, whereas those from the RKHS-based methods are related to the eigenfunctions of some reproducing kernels of the manifold.

Despite these endeavors, much less is known when the data is high-dimensional and noisy as modeled by (
1
) and (
2
), nor does the behavior of the associated kernel random matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
. In the null case (i.e.,
\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{i}
), it has been shown in
[
17
,
25
,
30
,
34
,
38
,
42
]
that when
h_{n}=p
and
\eta=1
in (
2
)
, the random kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
can be well approximated by a low-rank perturbed random Gram matrix. Consequently, studying
\mathbf{K}_{n}
under pure noise is closely related to PCA with some low-rank perturbations. Moreover, since in this case the degree matrix is close to a scalar matrix
[
30
]
, the graph-based methods and the RKHS-based methods are asymptotically equivalent. As for the non-null cases, spectral convergence of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
has been studied in some special cases in
[
39
]
when
h_{n}=p
, and more recently under a general setting in
[
32
]
. These results show how the eigenvalues of the kernel matrices of
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
relate to those of
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}.
Moreover, in the non-null cases, the degree matrix is usually non-trivial
[
32
]
, so that the graph-based methods can be very different from the RKHS-based methods.

In what follows, we focus on an RKHS-based integral operator approach to nonlinear dimension reduction and manifold learning under the general setup (
1
) and (
2
) which includes the settings
n\asymp p
,
n\gg p
and
n\ll p
. Even though in this setting
\mathbf{K}_{n}
has been studied to some extent in
[
39
]
and
[
32
]
, several pieces are still missing for rigorous and interpretable statistical applications. Firstly, it is unclear how to select the bandwidth adaptively for embedding, that is, free from prior knowledge of the manifold. Secondly, the limiting behavior of the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
have not been analyzed. Such results are particularly relevant for embedding purposes. Finally, even though the convergence of kernel matrices associated to
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
(cf. (
3
)) and
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
(cf. (
19
)) can be established, it is unclear how they are related to the underlying manifold. Such results are important for appropriate interpretations of the embeddings. We will accomplish these goals in this paper.

1.2
Overview of Main Results and Contributions

Briefly speaking, our proposed kernel-spectral embedding algorithm starts by constructing some kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as in (
3
),
where
h_{n}
is carefully selected by a theory-guided data-adaptive procedure proposed in Section
2
. Then we obtain the kernel-spectral embedding of the original data
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
by conducting an eigen-decomposition for the scaled kernel matrix
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{K}_{n}
, and define the embeddings as the leading eigenvectors of
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{K}_{n}
, weighted by their associated eigenvalues (Algorithm
1
). As we will show later, as
n,p\to\infty
with respect to (
2
), the thus constructed final embeddings are essentially related to and determined by a population integral operator defined by the reproducing kernel of an RKHS associated to the underlying manifold
\mathcal{M}
. It is in this sense our proposed method is an integral operator approach.

Rigorous theoretical understanding is obtained for the proposed algorithm, including the theoretical justifications for the bandwidth selection procedure, the limiting behavior of the low-dimensional embeddings, and their interpretations in relation to the underlying manifolds. Compared to the existing works on kernel-spectral embeddings, the current study has the following methodological advantages and theoretical significance:

•

We propose a kernel-spectral embedding algorithm for learning low-dimensional nonlinear structure from high-dimensional noisy data. Unlike most existing methods, our proposal takes into account both the noisiness of the data, characterized by our model (
1
), and its high-dimensionality as in (
2
). Moreover, we do not impose assumptions on the dimension of the underlying manifold and allow it to diverge with the sample size
n.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first kernel-spectral embedding method with theoretically guaranteed performance for high-dimensional and noisy data under non-null settings.

•

A key component in any kernel-based method is to select a proper bandwidth parameter. One major innovation of our proposed method lies in a careful analysis of the asymptotic behavior of kernel random matrices constructed from high-dimensional noisy data, which in turn leads to a theory-informed bandwidth selection procedure guaranteeing the strong performance of the method in a data-adaptive manner. In particular, our bandwidth selection procedure does not rely on prior knowledge about the underlying manifold, and can be efficiently applied to a large family of kernel functions.

•

We also provide an in-depth theoretical understanding of the proposed method. On the one hand, we study the convergence of the final low-dimensional embeddings to their oracle counterparts based on the noiseless samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
as
n,p\to\infty
, and characterize explicitly the effect of the overall signal-to-noise ratio on the respective rates of convergence and phase transition. On the other hand, we establish the convergence of the kernel matrices to the population integral operator that captures the nonlinear structures. The second result is essential for our understanding of the final low-dimensional embeddings, interpreted as a finite approximation of the samples projected onto the leading eigenfunctions of the population integral operator.

Our theoretical results explain the empirically observed data-adaptive feature of the proposed method (Section
4
), that captures the nonlinear structures regardless of the manifold structures. Moreover, our recognition of the limiting integral operator also suggests potential advantages of our method over alternative approaches targeting distinct geometric features of the manifold. For example, we may conclude from the theory that our method differs significantly from the graph-based methods, such as Laplacian eigenmap, DM, and LLE, as they all essentially aim at the Laplace-Beltrami operator rather than an integral operator (see Section
2.2
and Section
C
of our supplement
[
29
]
for more discussions).

Our theoretical analysis, on the one hand, relies on a general model reduction scheme, detailed in Section
3.1
, that simplifies the analysis by connecting the noisy nonlinear manifold model (
1
) with a potentially divergent spiked covariance matrix model, in which we allow the dimension of the manifold to diverge with the sample size
n.
On the other hand, we also leverage operator theory and random matrix theory, to prove our main results.

The proposed method admits strong empirical performance. In Section
4.1
, we present simulation studies that show the superiority and flexibility of the proposed bandwidth selection method over some existing alternatives.
In Section
4.2
and Section
E.3
of
[
29
]
, we analyze three real-world high-dimensional datasets with distinct nonlinear structures, including a path manifold, a circle manifold, and a multiclass mixture manifold, to demonstrate the usefulness of the method. In particular, for each of the examples, our proposed method shows significant improvements over the existing state-of-the-art methods in inferring the respective underlying nonlinear structures.

is stochastically dominated by
\mathsf{Y}

so that

\sup_{u\in\mathsf{U}^{(n)}}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathsf{X}^{(n)}(u)>n^{\upsilon}\mathsf{Y}^{(n)}(u)\Big)\leq n^{-D},

for all
n\geq n_{0}(\upsilon,D)
. In addition, we say that an
n
-dependent event
\Omega\equiv\Omega(n)
holds with high probability if for any large
D>1
, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(D)>0
so that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega)\geq 1-n^{-D},

for all
n\geq n_{0}.

We interchangeably use the notation
\mathsf{X}=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\mathsf{Y})
,
\mathsf{X}\prec\mathsf{Y}
or
\mathsf{Y}\succ\mathsf{X}
if
\mathsf{X}
is stochastically dominated by
\mathsf{Y}
, uniformly in

, when there is no risk of confusion. For two sequences of deterministic positive values

In addition, if both

Moreover, we write

if
a_{n}\leq c_{n}b_{n}
for some positive sequence

For any probability measure
\mathsf{P}
over
\Omega
, we denote
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Omega,\mathsf{P})
as the collection of

-integrable functions with respect to
\mathsf{P}
, that is, for any

For a vector

2
Kernel-Spectral Embedding for High-Dimensional Noisy Data

In this section, we introduce our proposed bandwidth selection and kernel-spectral embedding algorithm. After that, we discuss its unique features compared with some popular existing methods.

2.1
Data-Adaptive Embedding Algorithm

Our proposed embedding method is summarized as Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

High-dimensional noisy kernel-spectral embedding

Input:
Observed samples
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, kernel function
f
, percentile
\omega\in(0,1)
and eigenvector index set
\Omega\subseteq\{1,2,...,n\}
.

1.
Bandwidth selection:

(i) let
d_{ij}=\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
, and define the empirical cumulative distribution function

\nu_{n}(t)=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}1_{\{d_{ij}\leq t\}},

(4)

(ii) define the bandwidth
h_{n}
to be the solution to the equation

\nu_{n}(h_{n})=\omega.

(5)

2.
Kernel matrix construction:
define the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}=(K(i,j))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
using the above bandwidth
h_{n}
by letting

K(i,j)=f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}}{h^{1/2}_{n}}\right).

(6)

3.
Spectral embedding:

(i) obtain the eigendecomposition of the scaled kernel matrix
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
as

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{K}_{n}=\mathbb{U}{\bm{\Lambda}}\mathbb{U}^{\top},

(7)

where
\bm{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq...\geq\lambda_{n}
being the eigenvalues of
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
, and
\mathbb{U}=[\mathbf{u}_{1}\quad\mathbf{u}_{2}\quad...\quad\mathbf{u}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
being the corresponding eigenvectors.

(ii) define the kernel-spectral embeddings as the rows of
\mathbb{U}_{\Omega}\bm{\Lambda}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|\Omega|}
, where
\mathbb{U}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|\Omega|}
and
\bm{\Lambda}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|\times|\Omega|}
only contain the eigenvectors and eigenvalues indexed by the elements in
\Omega
.

Output:
the embedding matrix
\mathbb{U}_{\Omega}\bm{\Lambda}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|\Omega|}
.

In Step 1 of the algorithm, a data-adaptive bandwidth parameter
h_{n}
is defined as the
\omega
-percentile of the empirical cumulative distribution function
\nu_{n}(t)
of the pairwise squared-distances
\{d_{ij}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
among the observed data. Such a strategy is motivated by our theoretical analysis of the spectrum of kernel random matrices and its dependence on the associated bandwidth parameter. It ensures the thus determined bandwidth
h_{n}
adapts well to the unknown nonlinear structure and the signal-to-noise ratio of the data, so that the associated kernel matrix captures the respective underlying low-dimensional structure via an integral operator; see Section
3
for more detail. The percentile
\omega
is a tunable parameter. In Section
3
, we show in theory that under the assumption of (
2
)
\omega
can be chosen as any constant between 0 and 1 to have the final embeddings achieve the same asymptotic behavior; in Section
4
, we also demonstrate numerically that the final embeddings are insensitive to the choice of
\omega
. In practice, to optimize the empirical performance and improve automation of the method, we recommend using a resampling approach, described in Section
E.1
of our supplement
[
29
]
, to determine the percentile
\omega
.

In Step 2, some function
f
is adopted for the construction of the kernel matrix. The choice of
f
is flexible and depends on specific applications. In general, our theoretical results indicate that any kernel function that is bounded, Hölder continuous, and positive semidefinite (cf. Assumption
3
), can be used here with guaranteed performance. Specifically, the kernel matrices under these kernel functions are provably consistent in terms of their spectral convergence to the associated underlying population integral operators; see Theorem
4
for more detail.

In Step 3, the final embeddings are defined as the
\Omega
-indexed leading eigenvectors of the scaled kernel matrix, weighted by their eigenvalues. Similar forms of embeddings have been considered in
[
3
,
48
]
for spectral clustering and in
[
102
]
for network clustering. The eigenvector index set
\Omega
of the embedding space is determined by the users, depending on the specific aims or downstream applications of the low-dimensional embeddings. For example, in Section
4.2
and Section
E.3
of
[
29
]
, we choose
\Omega=\{1,2\}
for visualizing a path manifold and
\Omega=\{2\}
for the downstream ranking task, choose
\Omega=\{2,3\}
for learning circle manifolds, and set
\Omega=\{1,2,...,\mathsf{r}\}
for a variety of integers
\mathsf{r}
for downstream clustering purposes.

2.2
Comparison with Existing Kernel-Spectral Embedding Methods

First, regardless of the kernel functions being used, Algorithm
1
has important differences from the existing kernel-spectral embedding methods. We now focus on kPCA, Lapalcian eigenmap, and DM due to their close relations to our proposal.

Compared to Step 3 of Algorithm
1
, these methods rely on eigendecomposition of matrices distinct from
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
. Specifically, in the standard kPCA implementation (Section 12.3 of
[
14
]
), the eigendecomposition is applied to the centred kernel matrix

\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{n}=\mathbf{K}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}{\bf 11^{\top}}\mathbf{K}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{K}_{n}{\bf 11^{\top}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\bf 11^{\top}}\mathbf{K}_{n}{\bf 11^{\top}},

(8)

where
{\bf 1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an all-one vector;
in the Laplacian eigenmap
[
10
]
, the eigendecomposition is applied to the kernelized graph Lapalcian matrix

\mathbf{L}_{n}={\bf I}-\mathbf{D}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n},

(9)

where
\mathbf{D}_{n}=\text{diag}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}K(1,j),...,\sum_{j=1}^{n}K(n,j))
; in the diffusion map
[
26
]
, the eigendecomposition is applied to the normalized kernel matrix (i.e., transition matrix)

\mathbb{M}_{n}={\mathbf{D}^{\prime}}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{\prime}_{n},\qquad\mathbf{K}^{\prime}_{n}=\mathbf{D}_{n}^{-\zeta}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{D}_{n}^{-\zeta},

(10)

where
\zeta>0
is some tunable parameter and
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}_{n}=\text{diag}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}K^{\prime}(1,j),...,\sum_{j=1}^{n}K^{\prime}(n,j))
. More importantly, our numerical results (Section
4
and Section
E.3
of
[
29
]
) indicate that such differences may lead to low-dimensional embeddings distinct from our method. Theoretically (see Section
3.3
), we show that our proposed method may capture geometric features that are not likely to be captured by these methods.

As for other existing methods, it is largely unclear to what extent their performance is guaranteed, or if their interpretations continue to hold, for general high-dimensional and noisy datasets.
Empirically, our real applications in Section
4.2
and Section
E.3
of
[
29
]
show that our proposed method outperforms various existing methods, especially when dimension increases. For example, see Figure
1
and Figure
E.6
of
[
29
]
for illustrations.

Compared to many existing methods, Algorithm
1
has a bandwidth selection procedure that is provably coherent with the subsequent steps, in the sense that both the high-dimensionality and noisiness of the data are intrinsically accounted for in the constructed kernel matrix and the final embeddings. In contrast, graph-based methods, usually require the bandwidth to decrease to zero, to ensure a meaningful convergence. As a result, these methods may suffer from sub-optimality for high-dimensional and noisy datasets (see discussions at the end of Section
3.2
). In this respect, we are only aware of the recent work
[
32
]
, in which a data-driven bandwidth selection procedure for GL was developed. In Section
4
, we compare various bandwidths and demonstrate the advantage of the proposed bandwidth.

Second, in terms of downstream analysis like clustering, our method also differs from many existing methods.
A graph-cut based spectral clustering algorithm was proposed in
[
48
]
. Instead of using the kernel matrix eigendecomposition as in (
7
),
[
48
]
focuses on the graph Laplacian matrix of a zero-diagonal Gaussian kernel matrix. Moreover, it was noted in
[
48
]
that the bandwidth
h_{n}\asymp n^{-1/(r+4)}
which relied on prior knowledge of the ambient dimension
r
was chosen to ensure the consistency of the normalized cut rather than the kernel matrices.

[
63
]
studied the spectral embedding and clustering of high-dimensional data under the Gaussian mixture model, where the embedding was obtained based on spectral decomposition of the observed data matrix, rather than kernel matrices. In addition,
[
1
]
developed a perturbation theory for a zero-diagonal version of PCA in general Hilbert spaces. Similar to our paper,
[
1
]
considered spectral embedding of noisy high-dimensional data and studied the eigenvector convergence to their counterparts associated with the noiseless samples. However, unlike the current work,
[
1
]
focused on the eigendecomposition of some general Gram matrices, rather than distance kernel matrices as in (
3
) and (
19
). Moreover,
[
1
]
considered a model in the RKHS rather than in the original sample space as in (
1
) and Assumption
1
. Although the theoretical assumptions are comparable to ours in some special cases (see Section
C.2
of our supplement
[
29
]
for more detail), the assumptions of
[
1
]
can be relatively less interpretable compared to ours, which are made directly on the underlying manifolds, the observed samples and the kernel functions. Eigenvector perturbation bounds were also obtained in
[
1
]
under various discrepancy measures, which were distinct from ours (
29
). Although the results of
[
1
]
also highlighted the role of eigengap in the final rates of convergence, our results, as shown below, unveiled the impact of the underlying manifold structures and the choice of kernel functions on the final rate of convergence, as well as the proper interpretation of the eigenvectors with respect to the underlying population integral operators. For more details of the discussions, we refer the readers to Section
C.2
of our supplement
[
29
]
. Comparisons of the empirical performance with these methods can be found in Section
4.1.2
.

3
Theoretical Properties: Justifications and Interpretations

We develop a theoretical framework to rigorously justify the embedding algorithm proposed in Section
2.1
. Our theory suggests an asymptotic geometric interpretation that helps to better understand the low-dimensional embeddings.

3.1
Manifold Model and Reduction Scheme

To facilitate our analysis of the proposed embedding algorithm under model (
1
), we introduce the commonly used nonlinear manifold model and a useful model reduction scheme, that significantly simplifies the theoretical analysis without sacrificing the generality of our discussions. For some necessary background on smooth manifold and Riemannian geometry, we refer the readers to Section
A.2
of our supplement
[
29
]
for more detail. We first introduce the general manifold model as considered in
[
8
,
24
,
31
,
36
,
76
,
77
,
99
]
.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\mathbf{x}_{i},\ 1\leq i\leq n,
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples of a random vector
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
with respect to some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}).
Furthermore, we assume that the range of
X
is supported on an
m
-dimensional connected Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
isometrically embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
via
\iota:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}.
Suppose that the dimension of the space
\iota(\mathcal{M})
satisfies

\operatorname{dim}(\iota(\mathcal{M}))=r.

Note that
r\leq p.
To properly define the probability density function (p.d.f) of
X,
let
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
be the Borel sigma algebra of
\iota(\mathcal{M})
and denote
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
as the probability measure of
X
defined on
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
induced from
\mathbb{P}.
We assume that
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the volume measure on
\iota(\mathcal{M}).

Assumption
1
is commonly used in machine learning and manifold learning literature to model the nonlinear structures of the observed data sets. First, the connectedness of
\mathcal{M}
guarantees that
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
corresponds to a continuous random vector. Second, in our current paper, instead of using the restrictive setting of
[
32
]
where
r=1
, we allow
r
to be generic, which can diverge with
p.
Consequently, our discussion does not depend on the specific mapping
\iota
. This allows us to model the nonlinear structure in a more flexible way. In fact, according to Nash’s embedding theorem
[
69
]
(see Theorem
6
of our supplement
[
29
]
), it is possible that
r\leq m(3m+11)/2
for compact
\mathcal{M}
and
r\leq m(m+1)(3m+11)/2
for non-compact
\mathcal{M}.
In this sense, we can allow either
m
or
r
to diverge with
p.
Finally, in real applications, usually it is the embedded submanifold
\iota(\mathcal{M})
that matters since the observations are sampled according to
X
which is supported on
\iota(\mathcal{M}).
Consequently, we focus on the understanding of the geometric structures of
\iota(\mathcal{M})
rather
\mathcal{M}
and
\iota
separately. However, our discussion is naturally related to
\mathcal{M}
in the following sense. Let
\mathrm{g}
be the metric associated with the Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathrm{d}V
be the volume form associated with
\mathrm{g}
and
\iota_{*}\mathrm{d}V
be the induced measure on
\iota(\mathcal{M})
. Then by Radon-Nikodym theorem (e.g.,
[
13
]
), for some differentiable function
\mathsf{f}
defined on
\mathcal{M}
, under Assumption
1
, we have that for
x\in\iota(\mathcal{M})

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}(x)=\mathsf{f}(\iota^{-1}(x))\iota_{*}\mathrm{d}V(x).

(11)

\mathsf{f}
is commonly referred as the p.d.f of
X
on
\mathcal{M}.
For example, if
\mathsf{f}
is constant, we call
X
a uniform random sampling scheme. With the above setup, we can easily calculate the expectation with respect to
X
on the embedded manifold
\iota(\mathcal{M}).
More specifically, for an integrable function
\zeta:\iota(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\zeta(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\zeta(X(\omega))\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{\iota(\mathcal{M})}\zeta(x)\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\zeta(x)\mathsf{f}(\iota^{-1}(x))\iota_{*}\mathrm{d}V(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\zeta(\iota(y))\mathsf{f}(y)\mathrm{d}V(y).

Under Assumption
1
, there exists a rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
only depending on
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathrm{g}
that

\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},x_{i2},\cdots,x_{ir},{0,\cdots,0})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(12)

Recall that
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are drawn independently according to
X
. Let
\bm{\Sigma}
be the covariance matrix of
(x_{i1},x_{i2},\cdots,x_{ir})^{\top}
, and denote its eigendecomposition as

\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{V}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{V}^{\top},

(13)

where
\bm{\Gamma}=\text{diag}(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{r})
contains the eigenvalues of
\bm{\Sigma}
, arranged in nonincreasing order, and the columns of
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
are the corresponding eigenvectors. Given
\mathbf{V}
, we could define an orthonormal matrix
{\mathbf{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
as

{\mathbf{O}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\top}&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&{\bf I}_{p-r}\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

Then it is clear that for each
i\in[n]
, the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}_{i}
is diagonal, with

\text{Cov}(\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}_{i})=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\Gamma}&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&{\bm{0}}\end{pmatrix}.

(15)

Therefore, by rotating the original model (
1
) with the matrix
\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}
, we obtain that

\mathbf{y}_{i}^{0}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}+\mathbf{z}_{i}^{0},

(16)

where
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{0}=\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{y}_{i}
,
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}=\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{z}_{i}^{0}=\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}_{i}.

In particular, after rotation
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
become independent block random vectors in the sense that

\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i}=(\bm{x}_{i}^{0},0,\cdots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

(17)

where
\bm{x}^{0}_{i}=(x_{i1}^{0},\cdots,x_{ir}^{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and

\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_{i}^{0})=\bm{\Gamma}=\operatorname{diag}(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{r}),

(18)

whereas the random noises
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
remains independent and centred.
Now a key observation is that the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
from (
6
) only depends on the pairwise Euclidean distances of the data
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, which are invariant to any rotations. In other words, we have

f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}}{h_{n}^{1/2}}\right)=f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{y}^{0}_{i}-\mathbf{y}^{0}_{j}\|_{2}}{h_{n}^{1/2}}\right),\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq n.

Therefore, to theoretically analyze the kernel random matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
and the subsequent spectral embeddings, we can invariably start from the structurally reduced model as characterized by (
16
) to (
18
). Based on the above discussion, we arrive at the following result.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. Then the kernel random matrices defined under model (
1
) are equivalent to those under the reduced model characterized by (
16
) to (
18
).

The main benefit of such a reduction scheme is two-fold. On the one hand, it helps to identify a natural coordinate system for the noiseless samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
with fewer informative components, compared to the original coordinate system. On the other hand, the reparametrization leads to a parsimonious model in which the sampling distribution from the nonlinear manifold model as in Assumption
1
has a simple diagonal covariance structure. Both aspects contribute to improving the theoretical accessibility of the problem without loss of generality.

3.2
Spectral Convergence to Noiseless Kernel Matrix

We show the convergence of the low-dimensional embeddings to their noiseless counterparts, by establishing the spectral convergence of the scaled kernel random matrix
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
to the noiseless kernel matrix
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
, where

\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}=(K^{*}(i,j))_{1\leq i,j\leq n},\qquad K^{*}(i,j)=f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}}{\mathsf{h}^{1/2}}\right),

(19)

In (
19
),
\mathsf{h}\equiv\mathsf{h}_{n}
is the noiseless counterpart of
h_{n}
, defined as

\nu^{*}_{n}(\mathsf{h})=\omega,

(20)

where
\nu^{*}_{n}(t)=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}1_{\{d^{*}_{ij}\leq t\}}
,
and
d^{*}_{ij}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
. To simplify our presentation, in the following two subsections we will first consider the Gaussian kernel function (cf.
f(x)=\exp(-x^{2})
) as a demonstrating example due to its wide applications. The theoretical results concerning other kernel functions and their properties are deferred to Section
3.4
.

By the invariance
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}=\|\bm{x}^{0}_{i}-\bm{x}^{0}_{j}\|_{2}
, the noiseless kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
can also be treated as a characterization of the noiseless samples in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
To this end, we first introduce and discuss the theoretical assumptions we made throughout our analysis. According to Lemma
1
, it suffices to focus on the reduced model (
16
) to (
18
).

Assumption 2
.

Suppose (
2
) holds. We assume that both
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\{\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
in (
16
) are independent sub-Gaussian random vectors with independent entries, and

\mathbb{E}\mathbf{z}_{i}=0,\qquad\textup{Cov}(\mathbf{z}_{i})=\sigma^{2}{\bf I}_{p},

(21)

with
\sigma^{2}\asymp n^{\beta}
for some constant
\beta\geq 0
.
Moreover, we assume that
\bm{x}_{i}^{0}
has a continuous density function which is bounded below away from zero. Finally, in (
18
), we assume that for each
1\leq i\leq r
, we have
\theta_{i}\asymp n^{\alpha_{i}},
for some constants
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{r}\geq 0,
and

p\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{o}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\right),

or equivalently,

{n^{\beta+\eta}=\mathrm{o}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}}\right).}

(22)

A few remarks on Assumption
2
are in order. First, in terms of the notations in Assumption
1
, we require
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
to be sub-Gaussian and the density function
\mathsf{f}
defined in (
11
) to be bounded away from zero. Moreover, if the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is compact,
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
will be bounded and hence sub-Gaussian. In this sense, our results hold for both compact manifold and non-compact manifold with some mild decay assumptions on the induced measure
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}.
Second, unlike most of the existing literature on manifold learning where
r
is usually assumed to be independent of
n
and bounded
[
24
,
32
,
31
,
36
,
76
,
77
,
99
]
, our assumption allows
r
to possibly diverge with
n
.
The general signal-to-noise ratio assumption (
22
) or its equivalence
\sigma^{2}p=\mathrm{o}(\sum_{i}\theta_{i})
guarantees the ability to learn the structures of the embedded submanifold through the noisy kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
. When
r
is bounded, we only need one signal that
\alpha_{i}\gg\beta+\eta
for some
1\leq i\leq r.
In fact, it has been shown in
[
32
]
that when
r=1
and
\eta=1
, such a minimal signal condition is also necessary–if this condition fails, we will not able to obtain useful information through
\mathbf{K}_{n}
as the noise will dominate the signals.
Third, our model does not require the signals
\bm{x}_{i}^{0}
to be isotropic, that is, we allow their marginal variances to differ freely as in (
18
). Finally, in the current paper, we assume that
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and therefore
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are white noise as in (
21
) for simplicity. Nevertheless, our discussion applies invariably to the colored noise setting where
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{z}_{i})
has the same eigenspace as
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_{i}).
For example, in light of (
18
), we can allow a diagonal but non-scalar covariance matrix for
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{z}^{0}_{i}).

Before stating the main result of this part, we introduce a few more notations.
We denote the eigenvalues of
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
as
\{\mu_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
where
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq...\geq\mu_{n}
, and denote the corresponding eigenvectors as
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
. Let
\mathsf{P}
be the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
for the independent subvectors
\{\bm{x}^{0}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
of the reduced noiseless samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
. We define the associated integral operator
\mathcal{K}
with the kernel function
f
such that, for any
g\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{r},\mathsf{P}),

\mathcal{K}g(\bm{x})=\int f\left(\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}}{\mathsf{h}^{1/2}}\right)g(\bm{y})\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{d}\bm{y}),\qquad\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}.

(23)

By Mercer’s theorem (e.g.,
[
54
]
), there exist a sequence of nonnegative eigenvalues
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
in the decreasing order and orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{r},\mathsf{P}),
known as eigenfunctions so that

\mathcal{K}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})=\gamma_{i}\phi_{i}(\bm{x}),\qquad\text{for $i\geq 1$.}

(24)

Finally, we also recall that the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
are
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, respectively.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds and the kernel function
f(x)=\exp(-x^{2})
. Define

{\psi_{n}:=\frac{\sigma}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i})^{1/2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}p}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}}\asymp\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}.}

(25)

Then the following holds.

1.

(Eigenvalue convergence) We have

\left\|n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}\right\|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\psi_{n}\right).

(26)

Therefore, by Weyl’s inequality, we have

\max_{i\in[n]}|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\psi_{n}\right).

(27)

2.

(Eigenvector convergence) For any
i\in[n]
, if the
i
-th population eigen-gap
\mathsf{r}_{i}:=\min\{\gamma_{i-1}-\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{i+1}\}

satisfies that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}=\mathrm{o}(\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}),

(28)

then we have

|\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle^{2}-1|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{\psi_{n}}{\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}}\right).

(29)

Theorem
2
establishes the asymptotic spectral equivalence between the noisy kernel matrix
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
and the noiseless kernel matrix
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
. Note that both matrices are random with respect to the manifold model and noise. We first provide some explanation of the convergence rates. For the eigenvalues, the rate
\psi_{n}
consists of two parts. Under Assumption
2
, we note that the second term of the right-hand side of (
25
) satisfies
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}\right)^{-1}\asymp p\sigma^{2}/(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}).
Here, the factor
p\sigma^{2}
can be understood as the overall noise level and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}
is the overall signal strength. Therefore, this term quantifies the impact of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Technically, the pairwise distance has three parts following
\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+2(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}).
As proved in Proposition
1
below, with high probability,
h_{n}\asymp\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}.
Therefore, such an SNR term comes from the pure noise component
\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/h_{n}.
Similarly, for the first term of the right-hand side of (
25
), it comes from the cross-product
(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j})/h_{n}.
This explains the rate
\psi_{n}
heuristically. For the eigenvectors, the rate in (
29
) contains two parts. The
\mathsf{r}_{i}
part concerns the eigengap which appears frequently in the analysis of eigenvectors of large random matrices
[
6
,
7
,
41
]
;
the
\psi_{n}
part comes from the eigenvalue convergence. Note that the left-hand side of (
28
) is much larger than
\psi_{n}
so that (
29
) implies consistent estimation. The rate function
\psi_{n}
reveals the impact of
p
and
\sigma^{2}
on the rates of convergence. Specifically, when
n\gg p,
or equivalently
\eta\in(0,1)
in (
2
), the second term
(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta})^{-1}
of
\psi_{n}
will be smaller which suggests a faster rate of convergence compared to some exiting results assuming
r=1
and
\eta=1

[
32
]
. On the other hand, when
p\gg n
, our theory suggests that spectral convergence is still possible, although in this case a stronger overall signal strength
\sum_{i}n^{\alpha_{i}}
is required in light of (
22
) for larger
\eta
. We also point out that comparing to the Davis-Kahan theorem
[
90
, Theorem 4.5.5]
, our result in (
29
) is general in the sense that
\mathsf{r}_{i}
only depends on three population eigenvalues and is allowed to decay to zero. Moreover, if we have stronger assumption that
\delta
is bounded from below by a constant, one may directly apply Davis-Kahan theorem
[
90
, Theorem 4.5.5]
so that the right-hand side of (
29
) can be improved to
\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\psi_{n}^{2}).
For generality, we choose to keep the current (
29
). For more details, we refer the readers to Section
C.3
of our supplement
[
29
]
.

We emphasize that unlike
[
32
]
, in the current paper, we do not impose assumptions on
r.
In general,
r
is some generic parameter and can grow with
n.
The only condition we used is the overall SNR in (
22
). In fact, (
26
) improves the results of
[
32
]
(Theorem 3.1) in the following sense. In
[
32
]
, the authors mainly consider
r=\eta=1
and
\beta=0
; under the assumption of (
22
), our rate
\psi_{n}\asymp n^{-\alpha_{1}/2}+n^{-\alpha_{1}+1}
improves the rate in
[
32
]
which reads as
n^{-1/2}+n^{-\alpha_{1}+1}.
In addition, when
r
diverges, unlike
[
32
]
, which requires
\alpha_{i}>\beta+\eta
for all
1\leq i\leq r,
we only need a much weaker condition (
22
). In fact, our Theorem
2
reveals the role of
r
in the convergence rate in a more general setting. For example, setting
\eta=1
and
\beta=0,
when
r
is divergent in the sense that
r\gg n^{1-\delta}
for some small
\delta>0,
we only need
\alpha_{i}\geq\delta,1\leq i\leq r.
This significantly weakens the conditions for the signals used in
[
32
,
39
,
40
]
.

Remark 1
.

We remark that as shown in Corollary 3.2 of
[
32
]
, for
i\gg\log n
and
r=\mathrm{O}(\log n),
we have
\mu_{i}=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(n^{-1}).
Consequently, in practice, we only need to focus on recovering the first few (at most at an order of
\log n
) eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
when
r
diverges slowly. Moreover, the results concerning the eigengap
\mathsf{r}_{i}
can be made more precise given additional information. For example, if
\mathsf{P}
is multivariate Gaussian and
\eta=\sigma=1
, then from Section
D
of our supplement
[
29
]
, for any finite
i,
we have
\mathsf{r}_{i}\asymp 1.
If we further assume that
r=\mathrm{O}(1)
, whenever
\alpha_{k}>3/2
for some
k\in\{1,2,...,r\}
, we have
\sqrt{n}
-consistency for the leading
N=\mathrm{O}(1)
eigenvalues
\max_{i\in[N]}|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(n^{-1/2})
,
and eigenvectors
1-\min_{i\in[n]}\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle^{2}=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(n^{-1/2}).

Moreover, we believe our assumption on the signal-to-noise ratio in (
22
) is actually necessary to guarantee convergence. Considering the setting when
\eta=1
and
r=\mathrm{O}(1),
as proved in part (i) of Theorem 3.1 of
[
32
]
, when
\alpha_{i}<\beta+1
for all
i\leq r
, the noise dominates the signal and the convergence between
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
and
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{*}_{n}
fails to hold. Indeed, if
\sigma=1
and
\alpha_{1}<1
, we have
h_{n}\asymp p
, so that asymptotically
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
can be approximated by the Gram matrix of the noise vectors
\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{z}_{i}\}
, plus a few noninformative rank-one spikes. In particular, these rank-one spikes, as pointed out by Remark 2.4 of
[
32
]
, only depend on the kernel function, and do not contain any information about
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
; meanwhile, the empirical spectral distribution of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
will be governed by that of the noise Gram matrix and converges to the Marchenko-Pastur law
[
65
]
. See Section
C
of
[
29
]
for more discussions.

As has been seen from Theorem
2
, there are fundamental advantages of the proposed bandwidth. Under the current setup (
2
), most of the existing literature regarding the kernel random matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
focus on a pre-specified fixed bandwidth
[
25
,
30
,
34
,
38
]
. In particular, when
\sigma=\eta=1,
as they mainly deal with the null cases,
h_{n}=Cp
is chosen for some constant
C>0
, since the total noise is
\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{z}_{i}))=p
. In contrast, under the non-null regime (
22
) considered in the current study, if we still choose
h_{n}=Cp,
the bandwidth may be too small compared to the signal (
\asymp\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}}
) so that the decay of the kernel function forces each sample to be "blind" of the other nearby samples. Especially, following the proof of Theorem 2.7 of
[
32
]
, when the signals are strong enough so that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}>\beta+3,
if we still choose
h_{n}=Cp,
then we have
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{n})\rightarrow 1
for all
1\leq i\leq n
with high probability. In this case, the kernel matrix converges to the identity matrix and becomes useless.
In comparison, under our non-null regime (
22
), the above discussion suggests that one should choose
h_{n}\asymp\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}
, so that the kernel matrix does not degenerate. In this connection, the following result plays an important role in the proof of Theorem
2
, and justifies the efficacy of the proposed bandwidth selection procedure, without requiring any prior knowledge about the underlying structures such as
m,\iota,\{\theta_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq r}
, or the noise level
\sigma
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds and
h_{n}
is selected according to Algorithm
1
and
\mathsf{h}
is defined in (
20
). Then we have
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\prec\mathsf{h}\prec\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}
and
|h_{n}/\mathsf{h}-1|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\psi_{n})
.

Proposition
1
, on the one hand, implies that with high probability, the population (clean) bandwidth satisfies that
\mathsf{h}\asymp\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}.
We claim that this is a useful choice of bandwidth in the following sense. First, if
\mathsf{h}\ll\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i},
we have that with high probability for
i\neq j,

\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/\mathsf{h}\rightarrow\infty
and consequently for any decay kernel function
f(x)
(e.g.
f(x)=\exp(-x^{2})
),
f(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/\mathsf{h})=\mathrm{o}(1).
As a result, when
\mathsf{h}
is too small, following the proof of Theorem 2.9 of
[
32
]
,
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
becomes trivial in the sense that
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}\approx\mathbf{I}
with high probability, where
\mathbf{I}
is an
n\times n
identity matrix. Similarly, if
\mathsf{h}\gg\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i},
we have that with high probability, for
i\neq j,

\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/\mathsf{h}\rightarrow 0
and consequently
f(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/\mathsf{h})=f(0)+\mathrm{o}(1).
This shows that when
\mathsf{h}
is too large,
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
will become trivial again in the sense that
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}\approx(1-f(0))\mathbf{I}+f(0)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
with high probability, where
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector with all entries being unity. For illustrations on the usefulness of our population bandwidth
\mathsf{h}
, we refer the readers to Section
4.1.1
and Figure
E.4
of our supplement
[
29
]
. Together with the above arguments, we find that our proposed
\mathsf{h}
is a natural choice to make
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
nontrivial. Second, with such a choice of
\mathsf{h},
it will be seen from Section
3.3
that together with the kernel function
f(x),

n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
will converge to some integral operator of some RKHS asymptotically.

On the other hand, the above proposition also guarantees that the proposed bandwidth
h_{n}
is sufficiently close to
\mathsf{h}
to
distinguish signals from the noise. Moreover, by the definition of
h_{n}
, the validity of the above proposition does not rely on the specific form of the kernel function, making the proposed bandwidth selection method readily applicable for other types of kernel functions; see Section
3.4
for more detail.

3.3
Spectral Convergence to Population Integral Operator

So far we have shown that our proposed low-dimensional spectral embeddings converge to those associated with the noiseless random samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
. However, it is still unclear how these embeddings relate to
\iota(\mathcal{M})
. Answering this question is important as to understand the proper interpretation of these embeddings.

Our next result concerns the limiting behavior of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
, as characterized by a deterministic integral operator defined over the manifold
\iota(\mathcal{M})
. Before stating our main result, we first introduce a few notations from operator theory. Recall that
\mathsf{P}
is the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
for
\{\bm{x}^{0}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
in (
17
) and
\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}
is the rotation matrix satisfying (
15
). Let
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathbf{O}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{x}\in{\iota(\mathcal{M})}\}
be the clean sample space after rotation. We define
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
as the probability measure on
\mathcal{S}
for
\{\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, which is also
\mathsf{P}
embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that for
\mathbf{x}=(\bm{x},0,...,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathsf{P}(\bm{x}).

Accordingly, we define the population integral operator

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
such that for any
g\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}),
we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}g(\mathbf{x})=\int f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}}{\mathsf{h}^{1/2}}\right)g(\mathbf{y})\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}.

(30)

Clearly, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}},
denoted as
\{\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
and
\{\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}_{i\geq 1}
satisfy

\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}=\gamma_{i},\quad\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{i}(\bm{x}),\qquad\text{for $i\geq 1$.}

(31)

Moreover, let
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}
be the empirical distribution of the noisy samples
\{\mathbf{y}^{0}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
. Then for
\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},
the corresponding empirical operator
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{n}
is defined by

\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{n}g(\mathbf{x})=\int f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}}{h_{n}^{1/2}}\right)g(\mathbf{y})\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}^{0}_{i}\|_{2}}{h^{1/2}_{n}}\right)g(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{i}).

(32)

It is easy to see that (for example, see the discussion in Section 2.2 of
[
79
]
), for
h_{n}\asymp\mathsf{h}
, the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{n}
coincide with
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
, and for any eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}>0
of
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{n},
the corresponding eigenfunction
\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}
satisfies

\widehat{\phi}^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}^{0}_{j}\|_{2}}{h^{1/2}_{n}}\right)u_{ij},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

(33)

and
\|\widehat{\phi}^{(n)}_{i}\|_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{n}}=1
,
where
u_{ij}
is the
j
-th component of
\mathbf{u}_{i}
, the
i
-th eigenvector of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
. In particular, we have

\widehat{\phi}^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{j})=\sqrt{n}u_{ij},\qquad\text{for all $j\in[n]$,}

(34)

so that the eigenvector
\mathbf{u}_{i}
can be interpreted as the discretized
i
-th empirical eigenfunction
\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}
evaluated at the noisy samples
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
. Finally, for some constant
\delta>0,
we define the integer

\mathsf{K}\equiv\mathsf{K}(\delta):=\arg\operatorname{max}\left\{1\leq i\leq n:\gamma_{i}\geq\delta\right\}.

(35)

To prove the convergence of eigenfunctions, we consider an RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
for functions defined on
\text{supp}(\widetilde{\mathsf{P}})={\mathcal{S}}
, associated with the kernel function
K(\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=f\bigg(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}}{\mathsf{h}^{1/2}}\bigg).

Specifically, we define
\mathcal{H}_{K}
as the completion of the linear span of the set of functions
\{K_{\mathbf{x}}=K(\mathbf{x},\cdot):\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}\}
, with the inner product denoted as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{K}
satisfying
\langle K(\mathbf{x},\cdot),K(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\rangle_{K}=K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and the reproducing property
\langle K(\mathbf{x},\cdot),g\rangle_{K}=g(\mathbf{x})
for any
g\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
(see
[
12
,
64
]
for systematic treatments of RKHS).

Theorem 3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, the following results hold.

1.

(Eigenvalue convergence) We have

\max_{i\in[n]}|\lambda_{i}-\gamma_{i}|={\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}\right).}

(36)

2.

(Eigenfunction convergence) For
\mathsf{K}
in (
35
) and
1\leq i\leq\mathsf{K}
, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

|\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}{\widetilde{\phi}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\psi_{n}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}}\bigg[\psi_{n}^{1/2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg]\right),

(37)

where
\psi_{n}
is defined in (
25
).

Theorem
3
establishes the spectral convergence of the kernel matrix
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
to the population integral operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
given by (
30
) in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. In addition to the convergence rates obtained in Theorem
2
, an additional
n^{-1/2}
comes into play. Such a rate is standard in the RKHS theory when using
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
to approximate the integral operator; see Section
A.1
of our supplement
[
29
]
for a summary. Based on the above theorem, on the one hand, (
36
) demonstrates that the deterministic limits of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
are those of the integral operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
. The convergence rates consist of two parts. The
n^{-1/2}
part is the rate when we use
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
to approximate the integral operator and the
(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta})^{-1}
part is related to the overall signal-to-noise ratio and appears when we use
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
to approximate
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}.
On the other hand, (
37
) indicates that the empirical eigenfunctions
\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}
, after proper rescaling, can also be regarded as an approximation of the population eigenfunctions
\widetilde{\phi}_{i}
in a pointwise manner. For the convergence rate in (
37
), the understanding is similar to that in (
36
) where the
\psi_{n}^{1/2}/\mathsf{r}_{i}
is from (
29
).
In particular, Theorem
3
implies that for any
1\leq i\leq\mathsf{K}
,

{\lambda_{i}}\mathbf{u}_{i}={\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}}\cdot(\widetilde{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{1}),\widetilde{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{2}),...,\widetilde{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{n}))^{\top}\approx\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot(\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{1}),\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{2}),...,\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{0}_{n}))^{\top},

so that as long as
\Omega\subseteq\{1,2,...,\mathsf{K}\}
, the final low-dimensional embedding
\mathbb{U}_{\Omega}\bm{\Lambda}_{\Omega}
is approximately the leading
\mathsf{K}
eigenfunctions evaluated at the rotated data
\{\mathbf{y}^{0}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, and weighted by their corresponding eigenvalues
\{\gamma_{i}\}
. In other words, each coordinate of the final embedding is essentially a nonlinear transform of the original data, by some functions uniquely determined by the population integral operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
.
This explains why our proposed method may capture important geometric features of
\iota(\mathcal{M})
, and what kind of geometric features our method aims at.

As a comparison, existing methods such as Laplacian eigenmap, DM, or LLE, have their final embeddings corresponding asymptotically to the Laplace-Beltrami differential operator evaluated at low-dimensional noiseless data. The fundamental distinction between integral and differential operators indicates the potential advantage of the proposed method over these existing methods, especially for high-dimensional noisy datasets as indicated by our real data analysis (Section
4.2
and Section
E.3
of
[
29
]
).
In this respect, Theorem
3
provides a systematic way to understand the general geometric interpretation of the proposed low-dimensional embedding, although their concrete meanings may vary from case to case. To better illustrate this, in Section
D
of our supplement
[
29
]
we consider a specific probability measure
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
, obtain the explicit forms of its corresponding eigenvalues and eigenfunctions, and demonstrate their geometric interpretations accordingly.

3.4
Extension to General Kernel Functions

In this subsection, we show that in addition to the Gaussian kernel function,
our analysis can be extended to fit other kernel functions satisfying the following regularity conditions.

Assumption 3
.

We assume the kernel function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies

(i)

Positive-semidefiniteness: for any sequence of real values
\{c_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, and real vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}f(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2})\geq 0.

(ii)

Boundedness:
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}f(x)\leq C<\infty
for some absolute constant
C>0
.

(iii)

Hölder-like continuity: there is a constant
L>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L\{|x|^{\nu(x)}+|y|^{\nu(y)}\}\cdot|x-y|^{\tau(x,y)}
for some
0<\tau(x,y)\leq 1
and
\nu(x)\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\nu(x)\geq 0
for
0\leq x\leq 1
and
\nu(x)\leq 0
for
x>1
.

Conditions (i) and (ii) in Assumption
3
are commonly used in RKHS theory
[
85
,
70
]
for the spectral convergence of the integral operator, whereas Condition (iii) concerns the local behavior of the kernel function, which is useful for characterizing the noise and bandwidth effects. In particular, in Condition (iii), the parameters
\nu(x)
for large
x
and
\tau(x,y)
characterize the decay rate of the kernel function, whereas
\nu(x)
for small
x
characterizes the flatness of the kernel function around 0. Similar condition called Lipschitz-like continuity (or pseudo-Lipschitz continuity), that is
\tau(x,y)\equiv 1
, has been used in statistical learning theory, for example, see
[
9
]
.

In addition to the Gaussian kernel function
f(x)=\exp(-x^{2})
, which satisfies Assumption
3
with
\tau(x,y)=1
and
\nu(x)=1\{0\leq x\leq 1\}-1\{x>1\}
, there are many other kernel functions satisfying Assumption
3
. For example, see Chapter 4 of
[
97
]
. For the readers’ convenience, we provide a few examples as follows.

•

Laplacian kernels:
f(x)=\exp(-x/\ell)
for some constant
\ell>0
, which satisfies Assumption
3
with
\tau(x,y)=1
and
\nu(x)=-1\{x>1\}
.

•

Rational quadratic (polynomial) kernels:
f(x)=\big(1+\frac{x^{2}}{2\alpha\ell^{2}}\big)^{-\alpha}
for some constants
\alpha,\ell>0
, which satisfies Assumption
3
with
\tau(x,y)=1
and
\nu(x)=-(2\alpha+1)\cdot 1\{x>1\}+1\{0\leq x\leq 1\}
.

•

Matérn kernels:
f(x)=\frac{2^{1-\zeta}}{\Gamma(\zeta)}\big(\frac{\sqrt{2\zeta}x}{\ell}\big)^{\zeta}B_{\zeta}\big(\frac{\sqrt{2\zeta}x}{\ell}\big)
for some constants
\zeta,\ell>0
, where
B_{\zeta}(x)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
\zeta
. One interesting example is
f_{\zeta=3/2}(x)=\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}x}{\ell}\right)\exp(-{\sqrt{3}x}/{\ell})
, which satisfies Assumption
3
with
\tau(x,y)=1
and
\nu(x)=-1\{x>1\}
.

•

Truncated kernels:
f(x)=(1-x)_{+}^{\lfloor\alpha/2\rfloor+1}
, where
\alpha\geq 0
is some constant and
(x)_{+}:=\max\{x,0\}
, which satisfies Assumption
3
with
\tau(x,y)=1
and
\nu(x)=0
.

We point out that in addition to the aforementioned kernel functions, according to the properties of positive-definite kernel functions
[
97
,
27
]
, it is also straightforward to see that, for any finite numbers of kernel functions
f_{1},...,f_{R}
satisfying Assumption
3
, both their additive mixture
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{R}f_{i}(x)
and its multiplicative mixture
f(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{R}f_{i}(x)
also satisfy Assumption
3
, suggesting the wide range of kernel functions covered by our theory. The following theorem shows the spectral convergence of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
under these general kernel functions.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Assumptions
2
and
3
hold. Denote
\nu_{0}=\inf_{0\leq x\leq 1}\nu(x)
,
\tau_{0}=\inf_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}}\tau(x,y)
, and define

\xi=\xi(\nu_{0},\tau_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau_{0},&\textrm{ if $\nu_{0}\geq\tau_{0}$}\\
{(\tau_{0}+\nu_{0})}/{2},&\textrm{ if $0\leq\nu_{0}<\tau_{0}$}\end{array}\right..

(38)

Then the following holds.

1.

(Eigenvalue convergence) The eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
satisfy

\max_{i\in[n]}|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\psi_{n}^{\xi}\right),

(39)

and

\max_{i\in[n]}|\lambda_{i}-\gamma_{i}|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\psi_{n}^{\xi}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(40)

where
\psi_{n}
is defined in (
25
).

2.

(Eigenvector convergence) For any
i\in[n]
, if the
i
-th population eigengap
\mathsf{r}_{i}:=\min\{\gamma_{i-1}-\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{i+1}\}
,
satisfies

\psi_{n}^{\xi}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathrm{o}(\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}).

(41)

Then we have that

|\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle^{2}-1|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{\psi_{n}^{\xi}}{\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}}\right),

(42)

where
\mathbf{u}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}
are
i
-th eigenvector of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
and
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
, respectively. In addition,
for
\mathsf{K}
in (
35
) and
1\leq i\leq\mathsf{K}
, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

|\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}{\widetilde{\phi}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})|=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\psi_{n}^{\xi}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}}\bigg[\psi_{n}^{\xi/2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg]\right),

(43)

where
\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}
and
\widetilde{\phi}_{i}
are defined in (
33
) and (
31
).

Theorem
4
obtains similar rates of convergence as in our previous results under the Gaussian kernel, but also highlights the dependence of the convergence rates on the properties of specific kernel functions. For example, for the eigenvalue convergence in (
39
), the rate depends on both
\psi_{n}
and
\xi.

\psi_{n}
appears because of the same reasoning as explained after Theorem
2
, whereas the exponent
\xi
, which depends on specific kernel functions, appears when we approximate the entries of the kernel matrices. For illustrations on how the values of
\xi
of different kernel functions affect the convergence rates, we refer the readers to Section
4.1.1
and Figures
E.3
and
E.5
of our supplement
[
29
]
. We point out that Gaussian kernel corresponds to
\tau_{0}=\nu_{0}=1
or
\xi=1
in (
38
) so that Theorem
4
recovers the results of Theorems
2
and
3
. In fact, technically, the generalization to the class of kernel functions considered in Assumption
3
is highly motivated by the analysis of the Gaussian kernel function. We refer the readers to Section
3.5
below for more elaborations.

3.5
Proof Strategy and Novelty

In this subsection, we briefly describe our proof strategy and highlight the novelty. We start by reviewing the assumptions, results and strategies in a most related work
[
32
]
which extensively generalizes the results of
[
38
,
39
]
, and then point out the significant differences from the current paper.

In
[
32
]
, the authors studied the eigenvalue convergence of
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
to
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
, constructed using the Gaussian kernel function, under a mathematically simple setting
r=1.
That is to say, the nonlinear manifold is 1-dimensional and the reduced model is a single-spiked covariance matrix model. Moreover, they fixed
\beta=0
and
\eta=1
. In such a setting, our condition (
22
) reads as
\alpha\equiv\alpha_{1}>1.
Under these conditions, they showed that

\left\|n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}\right\|\prec n^{-1/2}+n^{-\alpha+1}.

(44)

For the technical proof, similar to the ideas of
[
38
,
39
]
, to control the difference between
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
and
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*},

[
32
]
directly applied entrywise Taylor expansion to
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
and
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
utilizing the smoothness and decay property of the Gaussian kernel function. To establish the convergence rates,
[
32
]
proved some concentration inequalities for the pairwise distances of
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\},\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
and
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}
and then studied the convergence rates and limits for the spectrum of kernel random matrices. We point out that technical proof of
[
32
]
on the one hand depends crucially on the assumption that
r=1
even though a generalization to some fixed integer is possible; on the other hand it relies on the properties of Gaussian kernel function.

In our current paper, we consider both Gaussian and a general class of kernel functions. Our results apply to the general nonlinear manifold model as in Section
3.1
. Moreover, we no longer impose assumptions on
r
(or the dimension of the submanifold
\mathcal{M}
in Assumption
1
). Instead, we set
r
to be a generic parameter, allowed to diverge with
n.
Unlike
[
32
]
, our results in Theorems
2
and
4
demonstrate that

Furthermore, motivated by our applications, in addition to the eigenvalue convergence, we also studied the eigenvector convergence in the current paper and connected the noisy kernel matrices with an integral operator of some RKHS.

We now discuss the proof strategies of the main results. We start with the Gaussian kernel (Section

). For the eigenvalue convergence of

\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}.

\mathbf{K}_{n}-\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}=(\mathbf{K}_{n}-\mathbf{K}_{s})+(\mathbf{K}_{s}-\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}).
The second term can be controlled by

and the bandwidth

relies crucially on Proposition

On the other hand, we show that the order statistics of

With the eigenvalue convergence, we proceed to prove the eigenvector convergence. Instead of applying the Davis-Kahan theorem (which requires the eigen-gap to be bounded from below), we utilized the integral representation of the eigenvectors via the resolvent. That is

\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}(z-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathrm{d}z,

One advantage of our proof strategy is that it can be easily modified to fit other kernel functions satisfying Assumption

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}(n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{v}_{i}=1,

and

\displaystyle\|\phi_{i}^{(n)}\|_{\mathsf{P}_{n}}^{2}=\bigg\langle\frac{1}{{\sqrt{n}\mu_{i}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\cdot)v_{ij},\frac{1}{{\sqrt{n}\mu_{i}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\cdot)v_{ij}\bigg\rangle_{P_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\mu_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{ij}v_{ik}\int K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y})K(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y})\mathsf{P}_{n}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}\mu_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{ij}v_{ik}K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{l})K(\mathbf{x}_{l},\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{i}^{2}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}(n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*})^{2}\mathbf{v}_{i}=1.

∎

A.2
Brief Summary of Riemannian Manifold and Embedding Theorems

We provide a succinct overview of definitions, important results and the embedding theorems in the theory of smooth manifolds and Riemannian geometry. For a complete introduction, we refer the readers to the monographs
[
16
,
61
]
.

We start by introducing some important definitions. A
topological
m
-manifold
is a second countable Hausdorff topological space and locally Euclidean of dimension
m
. The topological manifold can be characterized by a collection of
charts
, commonly called
atlas
. In order to do calculus on the manifold, we need to provide a smooth structure which is usually known as a
complete atlas
. A
smooth
m
-manifold
is a topological
m
-manifold with a smooth structure. The calculus of a smooth manifold is conducted on its
tangent space

T_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathcal{M}
,
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{M}.
A
Riemannian metric

\mathrm{g}
on the smooth
m
-manifold is a symmetric and positive definite smooth tensor field. Roughly speaking,
\mathrm{g}=(\mathrm{g}|_{\mathfrak{p}},\ \mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{M})
is a collection of inner products (bilinear forms) defined on
T_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathcal{M}\times T_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathcal{M}
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{M}
and all
\mathrm{g}|_{\mathfrak{p}}
vary smoothly. With
\mathrm{g},
we can define other important geometric quantities like the norm, angle and curvature.

A
Riemannian manifold
is a smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
equipped with a Riemannian metric
\mathrm{g},
denoted as the pair
(\mathcal{M},\mathrm{g}).
When there is no confusion, we usually omit the metric
\mathrm{g}
and simply write
\mathcal{M}.
For a Riemannian manifold, it is useful to use the
Riemannian density or volume form
to do the integration on the manifold, denoted as
\mathrm{d}V,
where
V\equiv V_{g}.
Especially, if
f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a compactly supported continuous function, we denote the integral of
f
over
\mathcal{M}
as
\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\mathrm{d}V.

In statistics and data science, observations are oftentimes collected in Euclidean space. Moreover, it is much easier to do calculations in Euclidean space. Motivated by these aspects, it is useful to link an arbitrary Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},\mathrm{g})
isometrically to some subspace of the Euclidean space with a specific metric. The feasibility of the above statement is guaranteed by the embedding theory. An
embedding

\iota:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r},
for some
r\geq m,
is a smooth map and a homeomorphism onto its image. When this happens,
\mathcal{M}
is called a
m
-dimensional
submanifold
of the Euclidean space and
\mathbb{R}^{r}
is said to be its
ambient space
. Moreover, we call
\iota(\mathcal{M})
the
embedded submanifold
. From the computational perspective, we are interested in the
isometric embedding
so that the calculations of the distances, angles and curvatures reduce to those in the Euclidean space.

The following theorem of John Nash
[
69
]
indicates that every Riemannian manifold can be considered as a submanifold of some ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{r}.

Theorem 6
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\mathrm{g})
be a
m
-dimensional Riemannian manifold. Then there exists an isometric embedding
\iota:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathbb{R}^{r}
for some
r
. Moreover, when
\mathcal{M}
is compact, it is possible that

r\leq\frac{m(3m+11)}{2};

when
\mathcal{M}
is non-compact, it is possible that

r\leq\frac{m(m+1)(3m+11)}{2}.

With the above embedding
\iota
and the
pushforward
, we consider the induced metric
\iota_{*}\mathrm{g}
on the embedded submanifold
\iota(\mathcal{M})
which is clearly another Riemannian manifold. For integration, we consider the induced volume form
\iota_{*}\mathrm{d}V
which is the Riemannian density on
\iota(\mathcal{M}).
Consequently, for any integrable function
f:\iota(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
we can define the associated integral as
\int_{\iota(\mathcal{M})}f\iota_{*}\mathrm{d}V.

Finally, as in Section
3.1
, we explain how the above manifold model is connected to our statistical applications. Suppose we observe i.i.d. samples
\mathbf{x}_{i},1\leq i\leq n,
according to the random vector
X
as in Assumption
1
. Moreover, we assume that the support of
X
is
\iota(\mathcal{M}).
Therefore, for any integrable function
\zeta:\iota(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
since
X
is supported on
\iota(\mathcal{M}),
the calculations of
\mathbb{E}(\zeta(X))
can be efficiently reduced to the integration on
\mathcal{M}
using the above induced measure and volume form (Section
3.1
). We emphasize that in real applications, it is the embedded submanifold that matters since the observations are sampled according to
X
which is supported on
\iota(\mathcal{M}).
Consequently, we focus on the understanding of the geometric structure of
\iota(\mathcal{M})
rather
\mathcal{M}
and
\iota
separately. For more discussions on this perspective, we refer the readers to
[
24
,
37
,
76
,
99
]
.

(52)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{K}_{y}-\mathbf{K}_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\neq j}|\mathbf{K}_{y}(i,j)-\mathbf{K}_{n}(i,j)|

\displaystyle={\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta})^{1/2}}\right).}

(75)

Combining (
71
) and (
B.1
), we conclude the proof of (
26
). (
27
) simply comes from Weyl’s inequality.
∎

We prove the eigenvector convergence by leveraging the eigenvalue convergence results. In particular, note that the obtained rate of convergence for
\left\|\mathbf{K}_{s}-\mathbf{K}_{y}\right\|
as in (
69
) is in general faster than the obtained rate of convergence
\left\|\mathbf{K}_{x}-\mathbf{K}_{y}\right\|
as in (
71
). Moreover,
by definition we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{s}=\mathbf{K}_{x}\circ\mathbf{E}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{x}\circ\exp\left(-\frac{2\sigma^{2}p}{\mathsf{h}}\right)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{K}_{x}\circ\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{2\sigma^{2}p}{\mathsf{h}}\right)\right){\bf I}_{n}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{2\sigma^{2}p}{\mathsf{h}}\right)\mathbf{K}_{x}+\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{2\sigma^{2}p}{\mathsf{h}}\right)\right){\bf I}_{n},

which indicates
\mathbf{K}_{x}
and
\mathbf{K}_{s}
share the same set of eigenvectors
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
, and their eigenvalues differ only up to a small isotropic shift, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{s})

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(-\frac{2\sigma^{2}p}{\mathsf{h}}\bigg)\mu_{i}+\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{2\sigma^{2}p}{\mathsf{h}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle:=A_{n}\mu_{i}+B_{n},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(76)

Therefore, instead of directly using Davis-Kahan theorem
[
100
]
, we can take advantage of these facts and obtain potentially faster rate of convergence for the eigenvectors.

Our discussion relies on the following identity, which follows from the residual theorem and the spectral decomposition. Specifically, for any positive definite matrix
\mathbf{A}
admitting the spectral decomposition
\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A})\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i}^{\top},
we have

\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i}^{\top}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}(z{\bf I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathrm{d}z,

(77)

where
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}
is some simply connected contour only containing
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A})
and no other eigenvalues. Based on the above representation, for each
i
satisfying (
28
), we have

\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{i}}(z{\bf I}-\mathbf{K}_{n})^{-1}\mathrm{d}z,

and

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{s})^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\left[(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{y})^{-1}-(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{s})^{-1}\right]\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\left[(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n})^{-1}-(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{y})^{-1}\right]\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{L}_{1}+\mathsf{L}_{2}+\mathsf{L}_{3},

(78)

for some contour
\Gamma_{i}:=\mathbb{B}(\gamma_{i},\frac{\mathsf{r}_{i}}{C}),
for some large constant
C>0,
where
\mathbb{B}(\gamma_{i},\frac{\mathsf{r}_{i}}{C})
is the disk centred at
\gamma_{i}
with radius
\mathsf{r}_{i}/C
and we recall
\mathsf{r}_{i}:=\min\{\gamma_{i-1}-\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{i+1}\}
. In particular,
\Gamma_{i}
only contains
\lambda_{i}
and no other eigenvalues, since

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}-\gamma_{i}|\leq|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|+|\gamma_{i}-\mu_{i}|

\displaystyle\prec{\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}=\mathrm{o}(\mathsf{r}_{i}),}

where the last inequality follows from the assumption of (
28
) and that

\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}=\mathrm{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(79)

and the second last inequality follows from Part I of Theorem
2
, the bound

|\mu_{i}-\gamma_{i}|\leq\|\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{n}\|\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

(80)

based on Lemma
5
and Weyl’s inequality.
To control (
B.1
), on the one hand, it follows that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{s})-\gamma_{i}|=|A_{n}\mu_{i}+B_{n}-\gamma_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq|A_{n}-1|\cdot\mu_{i}+|\mu_{i}-\gamma_{i}|+B_{n}

\displaystyle\prec{\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}},

(81)

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mu_{i}\leq\|\mathbf{K}_{x}\|\prec 1
and
|A_{n}-1|\asymp 1/(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta})
(e.g. see (
66
)) and the definition of
B_{n}
. Now under the assumption of (
28
), we conclude that when
n
is sufficiently large,
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{s})
is the only simple pole of the resolvent
(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}{\mathbf{K}}_{s})^{-1}
inside
\Gamma_{i}
. Using the spectral decomposition of
(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{s})^{-1}
and residual theorem, we have that

\mathsf{L}_{1}=1.

(82)

On the other hand, the control of
\mathsf{L}_{2}
relies on the
resolvent identity (see equation (5.5) of
[
55
]
) that

\displaystyle(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{y})^{-1}-(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{s})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{y})^{-1}\left[n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{y}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{s}\right](z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{s})^{-1}.

(83)

By the definition of
\Gamma_{i},
we have

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-\gamma_{i}|\leq|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-\gamma_{i}|\leq|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-\mu_{i}|+|\gamma_{i}-\mu_{i}|

\displaystyle\prec{\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}},}

where the last inequality follows from
using (
71
) and (
80
) as well as the definition of
B_{n}
. Thus by (
79
), under the same event we have that

\inf_{z\in\Gamma_{i}}\min\{|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-z|,|\lambda_{i-1}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-z|,|\lambda_{i+1}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-z|\}\asymp\mathsf{r}_{i}.

Here we used the assumption of (
28
), which implies that for
\mathsf{r}_{i}\gg(n^{-1/2}+1/(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}))
regardless whether
\mathsf{r}_{i}
is less than 1 or not. Moreover, by the definition of the resolvent, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{i}}\|(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{y})^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-z|}+\frac{1}{|\lambda_{i-1}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-z|}+\frac{1}{|\lambda_{i+1}(\mathbf{K}_{y})-z|}\right)

\displaystyle\prec\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}}.

Similarly, by (
B.1
), we also have

|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{s})-\gamma_{i}|\leq|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}_{s})-\mu_{i}|+|\mu_{i}-\gamma_{i}|\prec\mathsf{r}_{i},

which implies

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{i}}\|(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{s})^{-1}\|\prec\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}}.

Together with (
B.1
) and (
69
), we have that

\mathsf{L}_{2}\prec\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}\right].

Similarly, by (
B.1
), we have

\inf_{z\in\Gamma_{i}}\min\{|\lambda_{i}-z|,|\lambda_{i-1}-z|,|\lambda_{i+1}-z|\}\asymp\mathsf{r}_{i},

which implies

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{i}}\|(z{\bf I}-n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n})^{-1}\|\prec\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}},

and

\mathsf{L}_{3}\prec{\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta})^{1/2}}\right).}

Insert the above bounds and (
82
) back into (
B.1
), we immediately obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle^{2}-1\right|\prec{\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta})^{1/2}}\right).}

This completes our proof.
∎

B.2
Convergence to Population Integral Operator: Proof of Theorem
3

By the triangle inequality, we have

|\lambda_{i}-\gamma_{i}|\leq|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|+|\mu_{i}-\gamma_{i}|.

The first term of the right-hand side of the above equation can be controlled by Theorem
2
. For the second term, since
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{*}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
have the same bandwidth chosen as (
20
) in the main paper, we can use a sample splitting argument on
\mathsf{h}

and apply Lemma
5
to obtain the final upper bound. The proof then follows from (
79
).
∎

By Proposition
1
, one can apply the same argument leading to (
B.1
) above, to replace the bandwidth
h_{n}
in
\mathbf{K}_{n}
by
\mathsf{h}
defined in (
20
) of the main paper, with an negligible error compared with the final result. In the following, we focus on the setting where the bandwidth is
\mathsf{h}
. As before, without loss of generality, we will consider the reduced data
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and their related quantities
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{0}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
. For simplicity, we omit their superscripts and denote them as
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}
,
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
and
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}
.

We denote
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
as

\mathbf{y}=(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\perp}),\ \bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\ \text{and}\ \bm{y}^{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-r},

and set
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(\bm{z}_{i},\bm{z}_{i}^{\perp})^{\top}
in a similar fashion. Consequently, by the fact that
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(\bm{x}_{i},0,...,0)
, we have that

\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{y}^{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}-2(\bm{y}^{\perp})^{\top}\bm{z}_{i}^{\perp}-2(\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\top}\bm{z}_{i}.

(84)

This leads to

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)\cdot\ell,

where
\ell
is denoted as

\ell:=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{y}^{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}-2(\bm{y}^{\perp})^{\top}\bm{z}_{i}^{\perp}-2(\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\top}\bm{z}_{i}}{\mathsf{h}}\right).

As we will examine the RKHS norm in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
, containing functions defined only on the support of
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}
, without loss of generality, we can ignore
\bm{y}^{\perp}
(that is, set
\bm{y}^{\perp}=0
) and only focus on
\bm{y}.
Since
\bm{y}
is sub-Gaussian, using the elementary estimate
\exp(-x)\sim 1-x
as
x\to 0,
we obtain

\ell=1+{\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}\bigg),}

uniformly in
\mathbf{y}
on the support of
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
.
This immediately shows that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)

\displaystyle+{\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}\bigg).}

(85)

With the above preparation, we start analyzing the eigenfunctions. Recall the definition in (
48
). In the first step, we study the following term

\left(\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\bm{y})\right)^{2}=\left(\mathrm{E}_{1}+\mathrm{E}_{2}+\mathrm{E}_{3}\right)^{2},

(86)

where
\mathrm{E}_{k},k=1,2,3,
are denoted as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{1}:=\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)v_{ij},

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\mu_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)\right]v_{ij},

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{3}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\mu_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)(v_{ij}-u_{ij}).

First, by Theorem
2
, when
1\leq i\leq\mathsf{K},
we have that
\lambda_{i}\geq\gamma_{i}-|\lambda_{i}-\gamma_{i}|\geq c
for sufficiently large
n
with high probability, and thus

\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\right|\prec\frac{|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|}{\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}}\prec{\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\bigg(\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}\bigg).}

Consequently, using the boundedness of the kernels and the fact that
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=\sqrt{n}
for any unit vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we conclude that for
i\leq\mathsf{K}

\mathrm{E}_{1}\prec{\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\bigg(\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}\bigg).}

Second, by (
B.2
), we readily have that for
i\leq\mathsf{K}

\mathrm{E}_{2}\prec{\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\bigg(\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}\bigg).}

Third, we control
\mathrm{E}_{3}.
On one hand, for
i\leq\mathsf{K},
by Lemma
5
,
we have that
\mathrm{E}_{3}=\mathrm{O}(1).
One the other hand, by setting

\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})^{\top},\ \mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n})^{\top},

where
a_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\mu_{i}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\right)
and
b_{j}=v_{ij}-u_{ij},
we can rewrite

\mathrm{E}_{3}^{2}=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}.

Note that
\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.
Then we have that

\mathrm{E}_{3}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}\prec{\frac{1}{\mu_{i}^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta})^{1/2}}\right),}

(87)

where in the second step we used Part 2 of Theorem
2
. Combining all the above bounds, using (
86
), when
i\leq\mathsf{K},
we arrive at

\displaystyle\mu_{i}\left(\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\bm{y})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\prec{\bigg[\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}\bigg]^{2}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta})^{1/2}}\right),}

(88)

for any
\mathbf{y}
on the support of
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}
. Finally, since by Part 1 of Theorem
2
, we have for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})\leq(\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}-\sqrt{\mu_{i}})\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})\prec{\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}},}

(89)

and similarly

\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})\prec{\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}.}

(90)

Combining the above bounds, we have that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}{\widetilde{\phi}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})+\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})+\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}-\sqrt{\gamma}_{i}\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq|\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|+|\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|+2|\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|

\displaystyle\quad+|\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\gamma}_{i}\widetilde{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{y})|

\displaystyle\prec{|\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|+\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{{\alpha_{i}-\beta}})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\mathsf{r}_{i}},}

(91)

where in the last inequality we used

|f(\mathbf{y})|=|\langle f,K(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\rangle_{K}|\leq\|f\|_{K}\sqrt{K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})}\leq C\|f\|_{K},

(92)

and Lemma
5
. Finally, plugging in (

), we obtain the final result as
i\leq\mathsf{K}
.
∎

B.3
Guarantee of Bandwith Selection: Proof of Proposition
1

We first obtain upper and lower bounds for
\mathsf{h}
defined in (
20
). Recall from (
63
) that

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\prec\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}.

(93)

This implies the upper bound that

\mathsf{h}\prec\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}.

(94)

For the lower bound, we show that, with high probability, for any sufficiently small constant
\epsilon>0
, there exist at most
\frac{n^{2}}{\log^{\epsilon}n}
pairs of
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
so that

\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i},

(95)

where
C>0
is some universal constant. To see this, we define the events

E(i,j)=\bigg\{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\bigg\},\ \ 1\leq i\neq j\leq n.

We need the following lemma, whose proof will be given at the end of this section.

Lemma 15
.

For any given
i\in\{1,2,...,n\}
, with high probability, at most
\frac{n}{\log^{\epsilon}n}
events in
\{E(i,j):1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i\}
are true.

By Lemma
15
, we can apply the union bound over all
1\leq i\leq n
, and conclude that, with high probability, at most
\frac{n^{2}}{\log^{\epsilon}n}
pairs of
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
satisfy (
95
). Now for any percentile parameter
\omega\in(0,1)
that is independent of
n
, when
n
is sufficiently large, we always have
n(n-1)\omega>\frac{n^{2}}{\log^{\epsilon}n}
, which means the signal bandwidth
\mathsf{h}
selected according to (
20
) satisfies

\mathsf{h}\geq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i},

with high probability. This leads to the lower bound

\mathsf{h}\succ\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}.

(96)

Next we control
|\mathsf{h}/h_{n}-1|
.
Note that

\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-2(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}).

(97)

By Lemma
10
and Assumption
2
, we have that

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\prec{\sigma^{2}p\asymp n^{\beta+\eta}.}

By an argument similar to that leading to (
64
) and a union bound, we have

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}|2(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j})|\prec\sigma\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\bigg)^{1/2}\asymp\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}+\beta}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(98)

Combining the above arguments, we find that for any
(i,j),i\neq j

\displaystyle\big|\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-2(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j})\big|

\displaystyle\prec{n^{\beta+\eta}+n^{\beta/2}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\big)^{1/2}.}

(99)

Moreover, for any
k
such that
k=\omega n
for some
\omega\in(0,1).
Without loss of generality, we assume that
k
is an integer. Let
X_{(k)}
be the
k
-th largest element in
\{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}:i\neq j\}
, then by Lemma
15
, we have that

X_{(k)}\geq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}.

(100)

Together with (
99
) and (
97
), we find that there are at least
k
elements in
\{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}:i\neq j\}
satisfying

\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\geq X_{(k)}-{\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(n^{\beta+\eta}+n^{\beta/2}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\big)^{1/2}\bigg).}

which follows from the fact that the first
k
elements
\{X_{(i)}:1\leq i\leq k\}
are no smaller than
X_{(k)}
and hence they do not satisfy (
95
). As a result, the
k
-th largest element
Y_{(k)}
in
\{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}:i\neq j\}
satisfies

Y_{(k)}\geq X_{(k)}-{\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(n^{\beta+\eta}+n^{\beta/2}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\big)^{1/2}\bigg).}

(101)

On the other hand, we claim that there are at least
n-k+1
elements in
\{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}:i\neq j\}
satisfying

\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq X_{(k)}+{\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(n^{\beta+\eta}+n^{\beta/2}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\big)^{1/2}\bigg).}

This is because Lemma
15
and (
99
) imply that the last
n-k+1
elements
\{X_{(i)}:k\leq i\leq n\}
are no greater than
X_{(k)}
. As a result, we also have

Y_{(k)}\leq X_{(k)}+{\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(n^{\beta+\eta}+n^{\beta/2}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\big)^{1/2}\bigg).}

(102)

Hence, using the definitions of
\mathsf{h}
and
h_{n},
by (
101
), (
102
) and (
100
), we have that

\frac{\mathsf{h}}{h_{n}}=1+{\mathrm{O}_{\prec}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta-\eta}}+\frac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}-\beta})^{1/2}}\bigg)\equiv 1+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\psi_{n}).}

(103)

Finally, we prove Lemma
15
.

Define the indicator function
1_{E(i,j)}
so that
1_{E(i,j)}=1
if
E(i,j)
is true and
1_{E(i,j)}=0
otherwise. We shall prove that, for any fixed
i\in\{1,2,...,n\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i}1_{E(i,j)}\leq\frac{n}{\log^{\epsilon}n}\bigg)\geq 1-n^{-C},

(104)

for any large constant
C>0
and sufficiently large
n
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i}1_{E(i,j)}\leq\frac{n}{\log^{\epsilon}n}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i}1_{E(i,j)}\leq\frac{n}{\log^{\epsilon}n}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}.

It suffices to show that, for any
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\text{supp}(\widetilde{\mathsf{P}})
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i}1_{E(i,j)}\leq\frac{n}{\log^{\epsilon}n}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)\geq 1-n^{-C}.

(105)

Now since
conditional on
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, the events
\{E(i,j):1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i\}
are mutually independent, so that
\{1_{E(i,j)}:1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i\}
are
i.i.d.
Bernoulli random variables, with mean

\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}=\mathbb{P}(E(i,j)|\mathbf{x}_{i})=\mathbb{E}[1_{E(i,j)}|\mathbf{x}_{i}].

Note that when

\frac{C\log n}{n}\leq\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{\epsilon}n},

(106)

using Lemma
8
with
t=en\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
leads to

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i}1_{E(i,j)}\geq en\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)\leq\exp(-n\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}),

which implies (
105
) and therefore (
104
). Moreover, if

0\leq\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\leq\frac{C\log n}{n},

using Lemma
8
with
t=\frac{n}{\log^{\epsilon}n}
leads to

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n,j\neq i}1_{E(i,j)}\geq\frac{n}{\log^{\epsilon}n}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)\leq\exp(-n\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}})(e\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\log^{\epsilon}n)^{n/\log^{\epsilon}n}\leq n^{-C},

for any large constant
C>0
and sufficiently large
n
. As a result, we only need to show that

\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{\epsilon}n}.

(107)

The proof of (
107
) is separated into two cases as follows.

Case I:
r=\mathrm{o}(\log^{\epsilon}n).

For each
j=\{1,2,...,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcap_{k=1}^{r}\bigg\{(x_{ik}-x_{jk})^{2}\geq\frac{C\theta_{k}}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}\bigg\{(x_{ik}-x_{jk})^{2}<\frac{C\theta_{k}}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\bigg\}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{P}\bigg((x_{ik}-x_{jk})^{2}<\frac{C\theta_{k}}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg).

Now under the assumption that, for
1\leq k\leq r
, the marginal probability density of
x_{jk}/\sqrt{\theta_{k}}
is continuous, bounded, and strictly bounded away from zero on
\text{supp}(\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{k})/\sqrt{\theta_{k}}
, where
\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{k}
is the marginal distribution of
x_{jk},j=1,2,...,n
, by the mean value theorem, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(|x_{ik}-x_{jk}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{\theta_{k}}}{\log^{2\epsilon}n}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)\asymp\frac{1}{\log^{2\epsilon}n},\qquad k=1,2,...,r.

(108)

Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)\geq 1-\frac{Cr}{\log^{2\epsilon}n}.

So that (
107
) holds whenever
r=\mathrm{o}(\log^{\epsilon}n)
.

Case II:
r\gtrsim\log^{\epsilon}n
. The argument depends on the magnitude of the ratio
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}/\theta_{1}
. On the one hand, when
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}/\theta_{1}\geq c_{1}\log\log^{\epsilon}n
for some large constant
c_{1}>0
, since
(x_{ik}-x_{jk})^{2},1\leq k\leq n
are i.i.d. sub-exponential random variables, we can apply Lemma
9
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\bigg(-c\min\bigg\{\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i})^{2}+\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{4}}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}^{2}},\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}+\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\theta_{1}}\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\bigg(-c\min\bigg\{\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}^{2}},\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}}{\theta_{1}}\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\bigg(-c\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}}{\theta_{1}}\bigg)\leq\log^{-\epsilon c_{2}}n,

for some
c_{2}>1
and sufficiently large
r
, where the third step follows from the Hölder’s inequality
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}^{2}\leq\theta_{1}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}
. This implies (
107
) for sufficiently large
n
. On the other hand when
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{i}/\theta_{1}<c_{1}\log\log^{\epsilon}n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\theta_{i}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{C_{2}\log\log^{\epsilon}n}{\log^{4\epsilon}n}\theta_{1}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg((x_{i1}-x_{j1})^{2}\leq\frac{C_{2}\theta_{1}}{\log^{3\epsilon}n}\bigg|\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigg)\lesssim\frac{1}{\log^{3\epsilon/2}n},

where the last inequality follows from (
108
). This again implies (
107
) for sufficiently large
n
. This proves (
107
) and therefore completes the proof of Lemma
15
.
∎

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}

4
, we have

\displaystyle|\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}-\sqrt{\mu_{i}})|\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|\prec{\psi_{n}^{\xi},}

and similarly

|\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|\leq(\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}-\sqrt{\mu_{i}})|\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|\prec{\psi_{n}^{\xi}.}

(119)

Then by a discussion similar to (
91
), we have

\displaystyle|\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}{\widetilde{\phi}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})|\prec|\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\widehat{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})-\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{y})|+{\psi_{n}^{\xi}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\mathsf{r}_{i}}.

Plugging the above bounds into (
B.4.2
), we obtain the final result.
∎

C
Some Further Discussions

C.1
Extensions and Related Problems

We propose a kernel-spectral embedding algorithm for learning low-dimensional nonlinear structure from noisy and high-dimensional datasets under a manifold setup. A key component is to develop an adaptive bandwidth selection procedure that is free from prior knowledge of the manifold. Our proposed method is theoretically justified and our embeddings correspond to an integral operator from the RKHS associated to the underlying manifold. While our results pave the way towards statistical foundations for nonlinear dimension reduction, manifold learning, among others, there are various problems to be investigated further. We now highlight a few of them.

First, although this paper focuses on a general class of kernel functions for the construction of the low-dimensional embeddings, there are still many other interesting kernel functions, such as
f(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}
and
f(x)=\frac{1}{e^{x}+e^{-x}}
, that are excluded from our discussion. Moreover, the current paper only concerns the distance-type kernel matrices as in (
3
), leaving the inner-product kernel matrices with
K(i,j)=f(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{j}/h_{n})
unaddressed. Nevertheless, we believe this is only an initial step towards interpretable manifold learning under high-dimensional noisy data, and our results stand as a vantage point for exploring and understanding other kernel-spectral embedding algorithms.

Second, in Part 2 of Theorem
3
, the convergence of eigenfunctions is obtained in a pointwise manner. In some applications, a more precise local characterization of the embeddings is needed. For example, one may need to study the eigenfunction convergence under the
L_{\infty}
norm, defined by
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}}|f(\mathbf{x})|
. However, to our best knowledge, even in the noiseless setting, the
L_{\infty}
convergence has only been studied for the normalized integral operators
[
93
,
92
]
and the linear differential Laplace–Beltrami operator
[
21
,
37
,
98
]
. Less is known for the unnormalized integral operator considered in our paper. Moreover, to understand the
L_{\infty}
convergence under noisy datasets, in contrast to (
29
), we also need to study the
\ell_{\infty}
convergence of the eigenvectors. The analysis requires a more sophisticated argument from random matrix theory as in
[
6
,
41
]
. We will pursue these directions in future works.

Additionally, the current work mainly concerns the super-critical non-null regime
n^{\beta+\eta}=\mathrm{o}(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}})
. When
n^{\beta+\eta}\asymp\sum_{i=1}^{r}n^{\alpha_{i}}
, according to (2) of Theorem 3.1 of
[
32
]
, since the signal part has a comparable strength as the noise part, neither of them dominates the other in terms of the behavior of
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
. Similar results have also been established in
[
39
]
. In this critical regime, to better utilize the kernel matrix
n^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n},
one needs to develop a high-dimensional nonlinear signal and noise separation procedure.

Finally, in the current paper, we use an integral operator to learn the geometric structure of the underlying manifold. To enhance our understanding of the nonlinear structures, it is of interest to explore the relationship between the eigenfunctions of our integral operator and those of the Laplace-Beltrami operator.
The main challenge is that, in the high-dimensional noisy setting, the normalized graph Laplacian would no longer converge to the Laplace-Beltrami operator
[
32
]
, so that novel methodologies are needed to facilitate the comparison. Moreover, in the current paper, we focus on an RKHS-based method. It will be interesting to study the graph-based methods for high-dimensional noisy datasets under the current setting.

C.2
Comparisons with Some Existing Works

In Section
2.2
of the our manuscript, we have briefly summarized the differences between the current work and those of
[
48
,
63
,
1
]
. In what follows, we provide some more details.

First, in
[
48
]
, the author proposed a graph-cut based spectral clustering algorithm ("SPUDS"), in which a major step was to obtain the spectral embedding based on some graph Laplacian matrix. Specifically, the method starts by constructing a Gaussian kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}=(K(i,j))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
using the following bandwidth

h_{n}=sn^{-1/(r+4)},

(120)

where
s
is some user-specified value which is bounded both above and away from zero, and then obtains the eigendecomposition of the graph Laplacian matrix
{\bf L}_{n}={\bf I}-\mathbf{D}_{n}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{n}\mathbf{D}_{n}^{-1/2}
, where
\mathbf{D}=\text{diag}(\sum_{j\neq 1}K(1,j),...,\sum_{j\neq n}K(n,j))
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{n}=({K}(i,j)\cdot 1_{\{i\neq j\}})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is the zero-diagonal kernel matrix. For some given initial value for the number of clusters, K-means is then used along with the spectral embedding (i.e., the eigenvectors of
{\bf L}_{n}
) to determine the cluster labels and update the number of clusters iteratively using the normalized cut. In general, both SPUDS and our proposed method rely on some kernel spectral embeddings based on some kernel matrices, and require careful selection of the bandwidths. However, there are many significant differences between the two methods. First,
[
48
]
focuses on the noiseless data sets when clusters are closely related to the modes of the density functions whereas we consider high-dimensional noisy data sets. Second, the two methods focus on the spectrum of two very different matrices: our method relies on the eigendecomposition of the kernel matrix itself, whereas SPUDS relies on the graph Laplacian matrix of the zero-diagonal kernel matrix. Third, the bandwidth selection schemes are also very different. On the one hand, in
[
48
]
,
h_{n}
in (
120
) is chosen to ensure the consistency of the normalized cut (see Theorem 2 therein) rather than the kernel matrices or graph Laplacian matrices. In fact, it is not clear from
[
48
]
that whether such a choice of bandwidth ensures consistency and meaningful convergence of the kernel matrices or graph Laplacian matrices. In contrast, our selected bandwidth is capable of capturing the underlying signals and therefore guarantees the convergence of the kernel matrices to some population integral operator. On the other hand, our bandwidth selection procedure is data-driven and does not require any prior knowledge. In contrast, (
120
) relies on the values of both the (ambient) dimension
r
which is in general unknown and some constant
s.
To choose
s,

for practical purpose, in Section 4 of
[
48
]
, the author suggest

h_{n}=1.2\sqrt{\bar{\lambda}_{r}}\bigg(\frac{4}{(2+r)n}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{r+4}},

(121)

where
\bar{\lambda}_{r}
is the average of the largest
r
eigenvalues of the covariance matrix of the data. However, in our setting even when
r
is fixed,
\bar{\lambda}_{r}
usually diverges which violates the boundedness requirement of

in (
120
). Numerically, in Section
4.1.2
and Figure
1
of the main manuscript, compared to the method in
[
48
]
, we show that our proposed method has better performance in terms of spectral clustering using two high-dimensional noisy data sets.

Second, in
[
63
]
, the authors studied the spectral embedding and clustering of high-dimensional data under the Gaussian mixture model. The spectral embedding
\hat{\bf Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
was obtained based on the spectral decomposition

{\bf Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{n,p\}}\hat{\lambda}_{i}\hat{u}_{i}\hat{v}_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},\qquad\hat{\lambda}_{1}\geq...\geq\hat{\lambda}_{\min\{p,n\}},

of the observed data matrix
{\bf Y}
, rather than some kernel matrices. Consequently, they do not need to select a bandwidth. For clustering, they used
\hat{\bf Y}=\hat{\bf U}\hat{\bf\Lambda}
, where
\hat{\bf\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\hat{\lambda}_{1},...\hat{\lambda}_{k})

\hat{\bf U}=(\hat{u}_{1},...,\hat{u}_{k})

1

[

performed equally well as ours under the Gaussian mixture model, its performance was significantly worse than our method under the nonlinear nested sphere model.

Finally, in
[
1
]
, the authors developed a perturbation theory for a hollowed version of PCA in general Hilbert spaces. Specifically, the model

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{i}={\bf x}^{\prime}_{i}+{\bf z}^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{H},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

(122)

was considered for some reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)

C.3
Some additional remarks

In this subsection, we provide a few more remarks.

First, we make a comparison with Davis-Kahan theorem in terms of the results in (
29
). In the literature, there exist different variants of Davis-Kahan theorem. For definiteness and easy comparison, we focus on the one from the popular monograph
[
90
]
(see Theorem 4.5.5 therein); that is

Theorem 16
(Davis Kahan Theorem).

Let
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{T}
be two symmetric matrices with the same dimension. For each fixed
i,
denote

\delta:=\min_{j:j\neq i}|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{S})-\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{S})|.

(123)

Assuming that
\delta\geq\tau>0
for some constant
\tau>0,
we have that

|\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle^{2}-1|\leq\frac{4\|\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{T}\|^{2}}{\delta^{2}},

(124)

where
\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}
are respectively the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{T}.

We see that in order to correctly apply Davis-Kahan and obtain (
124
), we need that
\delta
is bounded from below by some constant. In contrast, in our equation (
29
), our bound reads
\psi_{n}/\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}.

We now make a comparison for both the denominators and numerators of the bounds. For the
\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}
part, on the one hand, our
\mathsf{r}_{i}
only depends on three population eigenvalues, i.e.,
\mathsf{r}_{i}=\min\{\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{i+1},\gamma_{i-1}-\gamma_{i}\}
. In fact,
\delta
used in Davis-Kahan theorem, i.e., (
123
), requires much more information than our
\mathsf{r}_{i}
(even though this condition may be relaxed using the arguments in
[
100
]
). On the other hand and more importantly, our
\mathsf{r}_{i}
is allowed to decay to zero once equation (
28
) is satisfied. For the
\psi_{n}
part, it is weaker than the results
\psi_{n}^{2}
from Davis-Kahan which relies on stronger assumption on
\mathsf{r}_{i}.
Nevertheless, if
\delta
is bounded from below by a constant, we can improve our results using Davis-Kahan. To summarize, Davis-Kahan may result in faster convergence rates but at the cost of much stronger assumptions on the eigenvalues separation whereas our results are obtained under general and weaker separation conditions. In the current paper, for the purpose of generality and practical usability, we keep the current results in (29).

Second, it is true that the left-hand side of (
28
) is large than
\psi_{n}.
Therefore, (
28
) means that
\psi_{n}
is much smaller than
\mathsf{r}_{i}^{2}
so that (
123
) implies consistent estimation. Sorry for the confusion. Finally, the reason that the left-hand side of (
28
) is larger than the ideal term
\psi_{n}
is technical. Our proof relies on an integral representation via the resolvent, such an assumption is needed to make sure that we can find a contour which only contains
\lambda_{i}
so that the residual theorem can be applied; see the discussions between equations (
B.1
) and (

E.1
A Resampling Method for Selecting Percentile
\omega
in (
5
)

As shown by our theory in Section
3
, under our assumptions,
\omega
can be chosen as any constant between 0 and 1 to have the final embeddings that achieve the same asymptotic behavior. Especially, our proof of Proposition
1
depends on the aspect ratio (
2
) fundamentally. In practice, to optimize the empirical performance and improve automation of the method, we recommend using a resampling approach as follows.

A resampling-based algorithm for determining the percentile parameter was proposed in Algorithm 1 of
[
32
]
, which has also been used in
[
28
,
33
]
. The method provides a choice of
\mathsf{s}
using resampling method to distinguish the larger outlier eigenvalues and bulk eigenvalues of the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
, where the outlier eigenvalues stand for the signal parts and bulk eigenvalues are for the noise part. A key observation underlying this approach is that the bulk eigenvalues are close to each other so that the ratios of two consecutive eigenvalues are close to one (see Remark 2.9 of
[
32
]
for more details). Given the choice of
\mathsf{s},
the algorithm is summarized below for self-completeness.

1.

For a pre-selected sequence of percentiles
\{\omega\}_{i=1}^{T},
calculated the associated bandwidths according to (
5
), denoted as
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}.

2.

For each
1\leq i\leq T,
calculate the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{K}_{n,i}
, constructed using the bandwidth
h_{i}.
Denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{K}_{n,i}
in the decreasing order as
\{\lambda_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k=1}^{n}.

3.

For some
\mathsf{s}>0,
denote

\mathsf{k}(\omega_{i}):=\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}\left\{k\bigg|\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(i)}}{\lambda_{k+1}^{(i)}}\geq 1+\mathsf{s}\right\}.

Choose the percentile
\omega
such that

\omega=\max_{i}[\argmax_{\omega_{i}}\mathsf{k}(\omega_{i})].

(127)

In particular, the last step can be replaced by other criteria according to users’ purpose. Here we choose the largest one for the purpose of robustness.
To demonstrate the empirical performance of the proposed percentile selection algorithm, we consider the four examples in Section
4.1
, and evaluate the empirical percentile chosen by (
127
), across a variety of sample sizes. In each setting, we used
\textsf{s}=1
. Figure
E.1
shows the empirical percentile
\omega
selected by the resampling method in each example. Along with Figures
E.3
and
E.5
below, these results indicate the usefulness and robustness of the percentile selection algorithm.

Our first example concerns the reconstruction of cell cycles from single-cell RNA-Seq data. The cell cycle, or cell-division cycle, is the series of events that take place in a cell that cause it to divide into two daughter cells
3
3

3

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_cycle
