Harnack inequality and asymptotic lower bounds for the relativistic Fokker-Planck operator
Francesca Anceschi, Sergio Polidoro, Annalaura Rebucci

Abstract
. We consider a class of second order degenerate kinetic operators ℒ \mathscr{L} in the framework of special relativity. We first describe ℒ \mathscr{L} as an Hörmander operator which is invariant with respect to Lorentz transformations. Then we prove a Lorentz-invariant Harnack type inequality, and we derive accurate asymptotic lower bounds for positive solutions to ℒ ​ f = 0 \mathscr{L}f=0 . As a consequence we obtain a lower bound for the density of the relativistic stochastic process associated to ℒ \mathscr{L} . Key words: special relativity, Kolmogorov equations, fundamental solution, Harnack inequalities, asymptotic bounds. AMS subject classifications: 35K70, 35Q75, 35Q84, 35A08



1.
Introduction

This work is devoted to the study of the following second order partial differential equation (PDE in the following)

(1.1)

\mathscr{L}f(p,y,t)={\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}}\,\nabla_{p}\cdot\left(D\,\nabla_{p}f\right)-p\cdot\nabla_{y}f-\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}\,\partial_{t}f=0,

where
(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
and
D
is the
relativistic diffusion matrix
given by

D=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}}\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}+p\otimes p\right).

Here and in the following,
\mathbb{I}_{d}
denotes the
d\times d
identity matrix and
p\otimes p=\left(p_{i}p_{j}\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,d}
. In this context, a solution
f=f(p,y,t)
to (
1.1
) denotes the density of particles in the phase space with momentum
p
and position
y
, at time
t
.

We observe that
\mathscr{L}
is a strongly degenerate differential operator, since only second order derivatives with respect to the momentum variable
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
appear. However, the first order part of
\mathscr{L}
induces a strong regularizing property. More precisely,
\mathscr{L}
is hypoelliptic, namely every distributional solution
u
to
\mathscr{L}u=f
defined in some open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
belongs to
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, whenever
f
belongs to
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. As a consequence, we only need to consider classical solutions to
\mathscr{L}f=0
. Indeed, as we will show in Appendix
A
, we can write
\mathscr{L}
in the form

(1.2)

\mathscr{L}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{j}^{2}+X_{d+1},

with

(1.3)

X_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\delta_{jk}+\tfrac{p_{j}p_{k}}{1+\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}}\right)\tfrac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}},\quad j=1,\dots,d,\quad\text{and}\quad X_{d+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}(p)X_{k}-Y,

where
c_{1},\dots c_{d}
are smooth functions and

(1.4)

Y=p\cdot\nabla_{y}+{\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}}\,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}.

Moreover,
\mathscr{L}
does satisfy the Hörmander’s rank condition
[
15
]

(1.5)

{\rm rank}\,{\rm Lie}\,\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d},X_{d+1}\}\,(p,y,t)=2d+1,\qquad\forall(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d+1},

which is a well-known criterion for the hypoellipticity of an operator in the form (
1.2
). We recall that in (
1.5
),
{\rm Lie}\,\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d},X_{d+1}\}
denotes the vector space generated by the vector fields
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d},X_{d+1}\}
and by their commutators.

The aforementioned regularity property of operator
\mathscr{L}
is related to a non-Euclidean structure on the space
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
and its study needs to be addressed via an
ad hoc
approach. In particular, as we will see in the sequel,
\mathscr{L}
is the relativistic version of a kinetic diffusion operator and it is invariant with respect to Lorentz transformations. Moreover,
\mathscr{L}
is the Kolmogorov equation of a suitable relativistic stochastic process
\left(P_{s},Y_{s},T_{s}\right)_{s\geq 0}
, that will be introduced in (
1.13
) below.
Our main result is a lower bound for the density of the stochastic process
\left(P_{s},Y_{s},T_{s}\right)_{s\geq 0}
. This is the first step in developing a systematic study of
\mathscr{L}
in its appropriate framework of the PDEs theory.
Indeed, our final aim is to extend the classical theory considered in
[
4
]
to the relativistic case. In particular, we plan to prove asymptotic results such as
[
19
,
20
,
18
,
6
]
in this more general setting.

As we will see in Appendix
A
, the treatment of operator
\mathscr{L}
in dimension

involves cumbersome notation and computations. Thus, for the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the one-dimensional case, and we expect that the corresponding generalization of our main results to higher dimension does not imply substantial difficulties. In the one-dimensional case
\mathscr{L}
writes in the following form

(1.6)

\mathscr{L}f(p,y,t)={\sqrt{p^{2}+1}}\,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial p}\left({\sqrt{p^{2}+1}}\,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial p}\right)-p\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}-\sqrt{p^{2}+1}\,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial t},\qquad(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

and takes the Hörmander’s form
\mathscr{L}=X^{2}-Y
if we set

(1.7)

X={\sqrt{p^{2}+1}}\,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y=p\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}+\sqrt{p^{2}+1}\,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}.

1.1.
Physical interpretation

Operator
\mathscr{L}
is the relativistic version of the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation
introduced by Kolmogorov
[
17
]
in
1934

(1.8)

\mathscr{K}f(p,y,t)=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p^{2}}(p,y,t)-p\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(p,y,t)-\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(p,y,t)=0\qquad(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

From the physical point of view, Fokker-Planck equations provide a continuous description of the dynamics of the
distribution of Brownian test particles immersed in a fluid in thermodynamical equilibrium. More precisely, the distribution function
f
of a test particle evolves according to the linear Fokker-Planck defined in (
1.8
), provided that the test particle is much heavier than the molecules of the fluid and that there is no friction. In particular, equation (
1.8
) is the backward Kolmogorov equation of the Langevin process

(1.9)

\begin{cases}&P_{t}=p_{0}+\sqrt{2}W_{t},\\
&Y_{t}=y_{0}+\int\limits_{0}^{t}P(s)ds,\end{cases}

where
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
denotes a
1-
dimensional Wiener process. We refer the reader to
[
4
,
24
]
, and the reference therein, for an exhaustive treatment of Fokker-Planck equations, and their applications.

In this article, we address a possible improvement of the model described in (
1.8
) which is in accordance with special relativity.
Indeed, a questionable feature of (
1.8
) is that its diffusion term
\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p^{2}}(p,y,t)
operates with infinite velocity, as in classical mechanics the velocity is proportional to the momentum. In particular, it is known that, if we consider a continuous, non-negative and compactly supported initial distribution
f(p,y,0)
, then the unique non-negative solution
f(p,y,t)
to the Cauchy problem relevant to (
1.8
) is strictly positive for every positive
t
(see, for instance,
[
4
, Theorem 3.3]
). In this scenario, there would be therefore a non-zero probability to find particles everywhere in space. This feature is clearly incompatible with the physical law that prevents particles from moving faster than light. To overcome this issue, we rely on the
relativistic velocity

(1.10)

v=\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^{2}+1}},

which clearly satisfies

\left|\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^{2}+1}}\right|<1\quad\text{for every}\ p\in\mathbb{R},

in accordance with the relativity principles
1
1

1

Here, we adopt a natural unit system with
c=1
, where
c
is the speed of light.
. We consider the
finite velocity Langevin process
analogous to (
1.9
)

(1.11)

\begin{cases}&P_{t}=p_{0}+\sqrt{2}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\sqrt{P_{s}^{2}+1}dW_{s}\\
&Y_{t}=y_{0}+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{P_{s}}{\sqrt{P_{s}^{2}+1}}ds,\end{cases}

and we recall that, by applying the relativistic Itô calculus, Dunkel and Hänggi find in
[
13
,
16
]
the Kolmogorov equation

(1.12)

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}f(p,y,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\left(\sqrt{p^{2}+1}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}(p,y,t)\right)-\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^{2}+1}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(p,y,t)-\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(p,y,t)=0\end{split}

satisfied by the density of the stochastic process
\left(P_{t},Y_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
in (
1.11
). We refer the reader to
[
11
,
13
]
for an overview to the relativistic theory of Brownian motions and corresponding relativistic kinetic equations.

Alcàntara and Calogero find the same equation (
1.12
) in
[
2
]
following a different approach, i.e. by merely requiring that some relevant properties of the non-relativistic equation are preserved in the relativistic setting. More precisely, as the non-relativistic operator is known to be Galilean invariant, the first requirement is the invariance with respect to the equivalent relativistic transformations, namely the Lorentz transformations. In addition, the authors of
[
2
]
impose that the relativistic Maxwellian distribution (or Jüttner distribution)
e^{-\gamma\sqrt{p^{2}+1}},\gamma>0
, needs to be a stationary solution of equation (
1.1
) with friction, mirroring the fact that the Maxwellian distribution is a stationary solution of (
1.8
) with friction.

We emphasize that

is a solution to
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}f=0
if, and only if, it is a solution to
\mathscr{L}f=0
with
\mathscr{L}
defined in (
1.6
). We prefer to focus our attention on the differential operator
\mathscr{L}
because it is invariant with respect to Lorentz transformations, as we will see in the following Subsection
1.2
. We finally observe that (
1.6
) is the relativistic deterministic equation describing the density of the following stochastic process

(1.13)

\begin{cases}&P_{s}=p_{0}+\sqrt{2}\int\limits_{0}^{s}\sqrt{P_{\tau}^{2}+1}\,dW_{\tau},\\
&Y_{s}=y_{0}+\int\limits_{0}^{s}P_{\tau}d\tau,\\
&T_{s}=t_{0}+\int\limits_{0}^{s}\sqrt{P_{\tau}^{2}+1}\,d\tau,\end{cases}

where the third component is the time, which is not an absolute quantity in the relativistic setting.

1.2.
Invariance properties

As it will be widely used in the sequel, we now focus on the invariance properties of operators
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{K}
.
As stated above, it is well known that
\mathscr{K}
is invariant with respect to the Galilean change of variables

(1.14)

(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\circ_{\mathcal{G}}(p,y,t)=(p_{0}+p,y_{0}+y+tp_{0},t_{0}+t)\qquad\text{for every }\,\,(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0}),(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Indeed, if
g(p,y,t)=f(p_{0}+p,y_{0}+y+tp_{0},t_{0}+t)
and
g^{*}(p,y,t)=f^{*}(p_{0}+p,y_{0}+y+tp_{0},t_{0}+t)
, then

(1.15)

\mathscr{K}f=f^{*}\quad\iff\quad\mathscr{K}g=g^{*},\quad\text{for every $(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$}.

In a natural way, operator
\mathscr{L}
satisfies the relativistic analogue of property (
1.15
), i.e. it is invariant under Lorentz transformations. To show that, let us first summarize basic definitions and a few properties of those transformations. We recall that the relativistic momentum
p(t)
and energy
E(t)
of a particle with position
y(t)
and velocity
v(t)=dy(t)/dt
are given by

p(t)=v\gamma(v(t))=\frac{v(t)}{\sqrt{1-v(t)^{2}}},\qquad E(t)=\sqrt{p(t)^{2}+1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v(t)^{2}}}=\gamma(v(t)),

respectively, with
\gamma
denoting the Lorentz factor

\gamma(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}.

We combine time with position, and energy with momentum, to obtain the contravariant four-vectors
3
3

3

We use the term ”four-vector” independently of the actual number of spatial dimensions.

\begin{pmatrix}t\\
y\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{pmatrix}E\\
p\end{pmatrix}.

The above definitions refer to the intertial lab frame
\Sigma
, defined as the rest frame of the fluid. We now consider a second intertial frame
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, moving with constant velocity
\beta
with respect to
\Sigma
. According to Einstein’s theory of special relativity, values of physical quantities in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
can be related to those in
\Sigma
by means of the Lorentz transformations. In the one-dimensional case, the Lorentz transformation matrix reads as follows

\Lambda(\beta)=\gamma(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}&1&-\beta\\
&-\beta&1\end{pmatrix},

and its inverse is
\Lambda(-\beta)
. The matrices are representations of the Lorentz group acting on the four-vectors. The transformation law of arbitrary four-vectors is computed as follows

(1.16)

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{t}\\
\widetilde{y}\end{pmatrix}&=\gamma(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}t-\beta y\\
y-\beta t\end{pmatrix},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{E}\\
\widetilde{p}\end{pmatrix}=\gamma(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}E-\beta p\\
p-\beta E\end{pmatrix}\\
\begin{pmatrix}t\\
y\end{pmatrix}&=\gamma(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{t}+\beta\widetilde{y}\\
\widetilde{y}+\beta\widetilde{t}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}E\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\gamma(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{E}+\beta\widetilde{p}\\
\widetilde{p}+\beta\widetilde{E}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

Let us consider the function,
\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})=f(p(\widetilde{p}),y(\widetilde{t},\widetilde{y}),t(\widetilde{t},\widetilde{y}))
that represents the one-particle phase space probability density function measured in the moving frame
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, which, according to
[
23
]
, transforms as a Lorentz scalar. If we set
g(p,y,t):=f(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})
and
g^{*}(p,y,t)=f^{*}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})
, then, applying the chain rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}(p,y,t)

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(1-\frac{\beta p}{E}\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{p}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(p,y,t)

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{y}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})-\beta\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{t}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}(p,y,t)

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(-\beta\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{y}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{t}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})\right).

Then, the vector field
X
defined in (
1.6
) is invariant with respect to Lorentz transformations, since

(1.17)

\begin{split}X(f(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t}))&=E\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}(p,y,t)=\gamma\left(E-\beta p\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})\\
&=\widetilde{E}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{p}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})=(Xf)(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t}).\end{split}

From (
1.17
), it immediately follows that the diffusion term
X^{2}f
in (
1.6
) is also invariant with respect to Lorentz transformations. As far as we are concerned with the drift term
Y
in (
1.6
), we obtain

(1.18)

\begin{split}Y(f(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t}))&=p\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(p,y,t)+E\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}(p,y,t)\\
&=\gamma\left(p-\beta E\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{y}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})+\gamma(-\beta p+E)\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{t}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})\\
&=\widetilde{p}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{y}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})+\widetilde{E}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\widetilde{t}}(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})=(Yf)(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t}).\end{split}

In virtue of (
1.17
) and (
1.18

\mathscr{L}
is invariant with respect to the Lorentz transformations (

), i.e.

(1.19)

\mathscr{L}f=f^{*}\quad\iff\quad\mathscr{L}g=g^{*},\quad\text{for every $(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$}.

Hence, owing to (

is invariant with respect to the following group operation on

(1.20)

(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\circ_{\mathcal{L}}(p,y,t)=\left(p\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+1}+p_{0}\sqrt{1+p^{2}},y_{0}+y\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+1}+p_{0}t,t_{0}+t\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+1}+p_{0}y\right).

We remark that for small velocities
\sqrt{1+p_{0}^{2}}\approx 1
, and therefore (
1.20

) for variables

and

Moreover,

is a Lie group with identity

Then, in particular, we have that

\displaystyle(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})^{-1}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}(p,y,t)

\displaystyle\quad=\left(p\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+1}-p_{0}\sqrt{p^{2}+1},\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+1}(y-y_{0})-p_{0}(t-t_{0}),\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+1}(t-t_{0})-p_{0}(y-y_{0})\right),

so that (
1.19
) is equivalent to

(1.21)

f(p,y,t)=g((p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})^{-1}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}(p,y,t)).

2.
Harnack inequality

This section is devoted to the proof of a scale-invariant Harnack inequality for solutions to (

\begin{split}H_{r}(0)&:=\left\{(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid\left|p\right|<r,\left|y\right|<r^{3},-r^{2}<t<0\right\},\\

\displaystyle H_{r}^{\mathcal{L}}(z_{0}):=z_{0}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}H_{r}(0),\qquad S_{r}^{\mathcal{L}}(z_{0}):=z_{0}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}S_{r}(0).

We are now in a position to state the following result.

Theorem 2.1

There exist two constants

\mathscr{L}
, such that

\sup\limits_{S^{\mathcal{L}}_{\theta r}(z_{0})}f\,\leq\,C_{H}f(z_{0}),

for every
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},r\in]0,1/2]
, and for every non negative solution
f
to
\mathscr{L}f=0
in
H^{\mathcal{L}}_{r}(z_{0})
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is obtained from the analogous Harnack inequality for the

acting as

(2.3)

\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}f=a(p,y,t)\,\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p^{2}}+b(p,y,t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}-p\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}.

In the following,

.

Suppose that the coefficients

and

(H)

There exist two constants

\qquad|b(p,y,t)|\leq{\lambda}^{+}\qquad\text{for every}\ (p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Then there exist two constants

\sup\limits_{S^{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta r}(z_{0})}f\,\leq\,C_{H}f(z_{0}).

for every

2.1.
Change of variable

In order to prove Theorem
2.1
, we perform an appropriate change of variable that allows us to write solutions to
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}f=0
(and therefore to
\mathscr{L}f=0
) in the form (
2.3
). To this end, we denote by
\varphi
the function defined as follows

(2.4)

\varphi(p):=\frac{p}{\sqrt{1+p^{2}}},\qquad p\in\mathbb{R},

where we remark that
{\varphi}(p)
is actually the relativistic velocity defined in (
1.10
). By a direct computation, we easily obtain

(2.5)

\varphi^{\prime}(p):=\frac{1}{(1+p^{2})^{3/2}},\qquad\varphi^{\prime\prime}(p):=-\frac{3p}{(1+p^{2})^{5/2}}.

Moeover, it is easy to verify the function

(2.6)

\psi(x):=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

is the inverse of
\varphi
, and the following identity holds

(2.7)

1-\varphi^{2}(p)=\frac{1}{1+p^{2}},\qquad p\in\mathbb{R}.

We are now in a position to state and prove the following preliminary result.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f
be a solution to
\mathscr{L}f=0
. For every
(x,y,t)\in]-1,1[\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define the function

(2.8)

u(x,y,t):=f\left(\psi\left(x\right),y,t\right),

where
\psi
was defined in (
2.6
).
Then
u
is a solution to the following equation

(2.9)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,y,t)+x\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x,y,t)=\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{5/2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t)-2x\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t).

Proof.

By inverting the change of variable in (
2.8
) we find that

(2.10)

x={\varphi}(p),

and therefore

f(p,y,t)=u\left({\varphi}(p),y,t\right).

Hence, from the chain rule it follows immediately

(2.11)

\frac{p}{\sqrt{1+p^{2}}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(p,y,t)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(p,y,t)=\varphi(p)\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x,y,t)+\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,y,t).

Moreover, exploiting identities (
2.5
), (
2.7
) and (
2.10
), we obtain

(2.12)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}(p,y,t)\,&=\varphi^{\prime}(p)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t),\\
\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p^{2}}(p,y,t)\,&=(\varphi^{\prime}(p))^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t)+\varphi^{\prime\prime}(p)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t)\\
&=\frac{1}{(1+p^{2})^{3}}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t)-\frac{3p}{(1+p^{2})^{5/2}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t)\\
&=\left(1-\varphi^{2}(p)\right)^{3}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t)-3\varphi(p)\left(1-\varphi^{2}(p)\right)^{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t)\\
&=\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{3}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t)-3x\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t).\end{split}

As a consequence, the diffusion term in equation (
1.12
) becomes

(2.13)

\begin{split}&\sqrt{p^{2}+1}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p^{2}}(p,y,t)+\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^{2}+1}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}(p,y,t)\\
&\quad=\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{5/2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t)-3x\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t)+x\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t)\\
&\quad=\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{5/2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t)-2x\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y,t).\end{split}

The claim then follows combining (
2.11
) and (
2.13
) and observing that
\mathscr{L}f=0
if and only if
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}f=0
.
∎

3.
Optimal control problem

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.2
. In order to provide a clear treatment, we first recall some fundamental notions from control theory and prove an equivalent statement of the Harnack inequality, more suitable
to the construction of Harnack chains (see Proposition
3.6
). We then prove an estimate for positive solutions to
\mathscr{L}f=0
(see Proposition
3.7
) depending on the
norm of the control. Finally, we conclude this section with a preliminary study of the optimal control problem associated to
\mathscr{L}
.

3.1.
\mathscr{L}
-admissible paths and Harnack chains

Along with the Harnack inequality Theorem
2.1
, the main tool in the proof of our asymptotic estimates for the fundamental solution are
Harnack chains
, whose definition we recall below.

Definition 3.1

(Harnack chain)
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We say that a finite set of points
\{z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\}\in\Omega
is a
Harnack chain
connecting
z_{0}
to
z_{k}
if there exist positive constants
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
such that

u(z_{j})\leq C_{j}u(z_{j-1})\qquad j=1,\ldots,k,

for every positive solution
u
to
\mathscr{L}u=0
.

In the present setting, we construct Harnack chains by connecting points belonging to appropriate trajectories, which naturally substitute segment lines in our non- Euclidean setting and are defined as follows.

Definition 3.2

(
\mathscr{L}
–admissible path)
.

A curve
\gamma(s)=(p(s),y(s),t(s)):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be
a
\mathscr{L}
–admissible path
if it is absolutely continuous and solves
the following differential equation

(3.1)

\gamma^{\prime}(s)=\omega(s)X(\gamma(s))+Y(\gamma(s)),

for almost every
s\in[0,T]
, where
X
and
Y
are defined in (
1.7
). Moreover, we say that
\gamma
steers
(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})
to
(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1})
, with
t_{0}>t_{1}
, if

(3.2)

\gamma(0)=(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0}),\qquad\gamma(T)=(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1}).

In the definition of
\mathscr{L}
–admissible path we assume
\omega\in L^{2}([0,T])
and we refer to the function
\omega
as the control of problem (
3.1
). Let us remark that, owing to (
1.7
), equation (
3.1
) can be explicitly written as follows

(3.3)

\begin{split}&\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\begin{cases}p^{\prime}(s)&=\omega(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1},\\
y^{\prime}(s)&=-p(s),\\
t^{\prime}(s)&=-\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1},\end{cases}\end{split}

for almost every
s\in[0,T]
.

Moreover, we observe that such optimal control problem is invariant with respect to the group operation (
1.20
). Indeed, let us consider a control
\omega(\cdot)
steering
(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})
to
(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1})
with trajectory
(p(s),y(s),t(s))
. Then, it is easy to prove the trajectory
(\widetilde{p}(s),\widetilde{y}(s),\widetilde{t}(s)):=(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})^{-1}\,\circ_{\mathcal{L}}\,(p(s),y(s),t(s))
is a solution to (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) with the same control
\omega(\cdot)
. Additionally, the newly defined trajectory
(\widetilde{p}(s),\widetilde{y}(s),\widetilde{t}(s))
satisfies the properties
(\widetilde{p}(0),\widetilde{y}(0),\widetilde{t}(0))=(0,0,0)
.

Finally, we introduce the standard definition of attainable set from control theory.

Definition 3.3

(Attainable set)
.

For every
z_{0}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the
attainable set
\mathscr{A}_{z_{0}}
of
z_{0}
in
\Omega
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{z_{0}}=\left\{z\in\Omega\,:\,\textit{there exists $\bar{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}$ and a $\mathscr{L}$-admissible path $\gamma:[0,\bar{t}]\rightarrow\Omega$ }\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\textit{ such that $\gamma(0)=z_{0}$, $\gamma(\bar{t})=z$}\right\}

Now, our aim is to derive from Theorem
2.1
a statement of the Harnack inequality which is
useful for the construction of Harnack chains. First of all, we define the positive cone

(3.4)

\displaystyle P_{r}(0)=\Big\{(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,

\displaystyle|p|<t^{\frac{1}{2}},|y|<t^{\frac{3}{2}},\,-\theta^{2}r^{2}\leq t<0\Big\}.

Moreover, in analogy with the definition of
H_{r}^{\mathcal{L}}(z_{0})
and
S_{r}^{\mathcal{L}}(z_{0})
in (
2.2
), we set

P^{\mathcal{L}}_{r}(z_{0}):=z_{0}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}P_{r}(0)
. We are now in a position to state the following result, whose proof can be found in
[
21
, Proposition 3.2]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\Omega
be an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
containing
H_{r}(z_{0})
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
r\in\left]0,\frac{1}{2}\right[
. Then

f(z_{0}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}z)\leq C_{H}f(z_{0})

for every non negative solution
f
of
\mathcal{L}f=0
in
\Omega
and for every
z\in P_{r}(0)
.

Next, we show that the trajectories defined in (
3.1
) belong to a certain positive cone provided a suitable choice of the parameter
s\in[0,T]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
s\in[0,T]
,
\omega\in L^{2}([0,T])
be a control and let
\gamma(s)=(p(s),y(s),t(s))
be an
\mathscr{L}
-admissible
path starting from
z_{0}=(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Then for every
r\in\left]0,\frac{1}{2}\right[
there exist two positive constants

k_{0}:=2\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)
and
\theta\in]0,1[
,
only depending on operator
\mathscr{L}
, such that

\displaystyle\gamma(s)\in P^{\mathcal{L}}_{r}(z_{0}),

for every
s\in\left[0,\sqrt{\frac{{2}}{{3}}}\theta^{2}\,r^{2}\right]
such that

\int_{0}^{s}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau\leq k_{0}^{2}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we fix
z_{0}=(0,0,0)
and we give proof of this result for a given
\mathscr{L}
-admissible path starting from
(0,0,0)
.
The general case directly follows from the translation invariance with respect to the group law (
1.20
).

Thus, we begin by considering the first component of
\gamma(s)
. In virtue of (
3.3
), for every
s>0
, we have

\int\limits_{0}^{s}p^{\prime}(\tau)d\tau=\int\limits_{0}^{s}\omega(\tau)\sqrt{p^{2}(\tau)+1}\,d\tau

and therefore

(3.5)

\int\limits_{0}^{s}\omega(\tau)\,d\tau=\int\limits_{0}^{s}\frac{p^{\prime}(\tau)}{\sqrt{p^{2}(\tau)+1}}d\tau=\sinh^{-1}(p(s))=\ln\left(p(s)+\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}\right).

Now, we apply Hölder’s inequality and we estimate the
L^{2}
norm of the control with
k_{0}
to get

(3.6)

\left|\int\limits_{0}^{s}\omega(\tau)\,d\tau\right|\leq\left(\int\limits_{0}^{s}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{s}\leq\,k_{0}\sqrt{s}\leq\ln(1+\sqrt{s}),\qquad\forall s\in\left[0,\frac{1}{4}\right],

We observe that the last inequality follows from our choice of
k_{0}
and the concavity of
\ln(1+x)
, which in particular implies that
\ln(1+x)\geq 2\ln(3/2)x
for every
x\in[0,1/2]
.
As a consequence,

(3.7)

\left|e^{\int_{0}^{s}\omega(\tau)\,d\tau}-1\right|\leq e^{\left|\int_{0}^{s}\omega(\tau)\,d\tau\right|}-1\,\leq\sqrt{s},\qquad\forall s\in\left[0,\sqrt{\frac{{2}}{{3}}}\theta^{2}\,r^{2}\right].

Then, combining (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), we obtain

(3.8)

|p(s)|\leq|p(s)+\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}-1|\leq\sqrt{s},\qquad\forall s\in\left[0,\sqrt{\frac{{2}}{{3}}}\theta^{2}\,r^{2}\right].

Next, we consider the second component of
\gamma(s)
, that is

y(s)=-\int\limits_{0}^{s}p(\tau)\,d\tau.

Owing to (
3.8
), we immediately get

(3.9)

\left|y(s)\right|\leq\int\limits_{0}^{s}\sqrt{\tau}\,d\tau=\frac{2}{3}s^{\frac{3}{2}}<s^{\frac{3}{2}},\qquad\forall s\in\left[0,\sqrt{\frac{{2}}{{3}}}\theta^{2}\,r^{2}\right].

By combining the above inequality, with (
3.8
), we obtain

(3.10)

\displaystyle 0\leq-t(s)=\int\limits_{0}^{s}\sqrt{p^{2}(\tau)+1}\,d\tau\leq\int\limits_{0}^{s}\sqrt{\tau+1}d\tau\leq\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,s\leq\theta^{2}r^{2},\qquad\forall s\in\left[0,\sqrt{\frac{{2}}{{3}}}\theta^{2}\,r^{2}\right].

Hence,

|p(s)|\leq s^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\left|y(s)\right|\leq s^{\frac{3}{2}},\quad-\theta^{2}r^{2}\leq t(s)\leq 0,\qquad\forall s\in\left[0,\sqrt{\frac{{2}}{{3}}}\theta^{2}\,r^{2}\right].

This concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we are in a position to prove a more suitable statement of the Harnack inequality for points of
an admissible trajectory.

.

Let
T>0
,
R>0
and
z_{0}=(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

Let
s\in[0,T]
,
\omega\in L^{2}([0,T])

be an
\mathscr{L}
-admissible

.
Then, for every non negative solution

to
\mathscr{L}f=0
in

,
there exist three positive constants

,
C_{H}
and
\theta\in]0,1[
,
only depending on operator
\mathscr{L}
, such that

f(\gamma(s))\,\leq\,C_{H}f(z_{0}),

for every

such that

\int_{0}^{s}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau\leq k_{0}^{2}.

Proof.

The result directly follows by combining Theorem
2.1
with Proposition
3.5
.
∎

3.2.
Optimal control problem

f
to

\mathscr{L}f=0
at any point of a given
\mathscr{L}
-admissible path in terms of the

-norm of the control. Results of this kind are
usually referred as

,

.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
z_{0}=(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,\times\,]T_{0},T_{1}]
and let
\omega\in L^{2}([0,T])
be a control and
\gamma(s)=(p(s),y(s),t(s))
be the corresponding
\mathscr{L}
-admissible path starting
from
z_{0}=(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Moreover, let us fix
\,T_{0}<t(s)<t_{0}<T_{1}\,
, with
t_{0}-t(s)\leq\theta^{2}(t_{0}-T_{0})\leq\frac{\theta^{4}}{4}
.
Then, for every non negative solution
f
to
\mathscr{L}f=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\,\times\,]T_{0},T_{1}]
, there exist three positive constants

k_{0}
,
\theta
,
C_{H}
, only depending on operator
\mathscr{L}
, such that

(3.11)

f(\gamma(s))\leq C_{H}^{\frac{\Phi(\omega)}{k_{0}^{2}}+1}f(z_{0}),

where

(3.12)

\Phi(\omega)=\int_{0}^{s}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau.

Proof.

Let
k_{0}
,
C_{H}
and
\theta
be the constants of Proposition
3.6
.
We first observe that, if

\int_{0}^{s}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau\leq k_{0}^{2},

then

\gamma(s)\in P^{\mathcal{L}}_{r}(z_{0}),\quad r:=\sqrt{t_{0}-T_{0}}\leq\frac{1}{2},

in virtue of Proposition
3.5
and assumption
t_{0}-t(s)\leq\theta^{2}(t_{0}-T_{0})
. Since
H^{\mathcal{L}}_{r}(z_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\,\times\,\times]T_{0},T_{1}[
thanks to our choice of
r
, Proposition
3.6
can be applied and there holds
f(p,y,t)\leq C_{H}\,f(z_{0})
, where
C_{H}
is the constant given by Theorem
2.1
.

If the above inequality is not satisfied, we set

(3.13)

k=\max\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\int_{0}^{s}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau>jk_{0}^{2}\right\}

and define recursively a sequence of times starting from
\sigma_{0}\equiv 0
as follows

(3.14)

\sigma_{j}=\min\Bigg\{s,\,\inf\left\{\sigma>0\,:\,\int_{0}^{\sigma}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau>jk_{0}^{2}\right\}\Bigg\},

for every
j=1,\ldots,k+1
.
Thanks to (
3.13
), the sequence in (
3.14
) ends after a finite number of steps when the upper bound
\sigma_{k+1}\equiv s
is reached. Moreover, for every
j=0,\ldots,k+1
, we define the sequence
t_{j}=t(\sigma_{j})
, which satisfies
t(s)\equiv t_{k+1}<t_{k}<t_{k-1}<\ldots t_{1}<t_{0}
. We now observe that

H^{\mathcal{L}}_{r_{j}}(\gamma(\sigma_{j}))\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\times]T_{0},T_{1}[,\quad\textit{for $r_{j}=\frac{\sqrt{t_{j}-t_{j+1}}}{\theta}$, $j=1,\ldots,k$}.

In addition, we clearly have
t_{j}-t_{j+1}\leq\theta^{2}\,r_{j}^{2}
and
r_{j}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, since
\frac{t_{j}-t_{j+1}}{\theta^{2}}\leq\frac{t_{0}-T_{0}}{\theta^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}
.
Finally, as
\int_{0}^{\sigma_{1}}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau\leq k_{0}^{2}
, we can apply Proposition
3.6
and get

f(\gamma(\sigma_{1}))\leq C_{H}f(\gamma(0))=C_{H}f(z_{0})
. Similarly, owing to
\int_{\sigma_{1}}^{\sigma_{2}}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau\leq k_{0}^{2}
and applying again Proposition
3.6
to the trajectory steering
(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1}):=\gamma(\sigma_{1})
to
(p_{2},y_{2},t_{2}):=\gamma(\sigma_{2})
, we obtain
f(\gamma(\sigma_{2}))\leq C_{H}f(\gamma(\sigma_{1}))\leq C_{H}^{2}f(z_{0})
.
We then iterate the above argument until at step
k+1
and we obtain

f(\gamma(s))\leq C_{H}^{k+1}f(z_{0}).

We point out that the points
(\gamma(\sigma_{j}))_{j=1}^{k}
, chosen along the trajectory

\gamma(\cdot)
, define a Harnack chain. Finally, from (
3.13
), it follows that

k<\frac{\int_{0}^{s}|\omega(\tau)|^{2}d\tau}{k_{0}^{2}},

and this concludes the proof of Proposition
3.7
.
∎

Estimate (
3.11
) provides us with a bound dependent on the choice of the
\mathscr{L}
-admissible path steering
z_{0}
to
\gamma(s)
.
Hence, we introduce the
value function

(3.15)

\Psi(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0};p_{1},y_{1},t_{1}):=\inf_{\omega\in L^{2}([0,T])}\Phi(\omega),

where the infimum is taken over all the
\mathscr{L}
-admissible paths steering
z_{0}:=(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
to
z_{1}:=(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Then, as a straightforward consequence of Proposition
3.7
, we obtain

(3.16)

f(\gamma(s))\leq M^{\frac{\Psi(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0};p(s),y(s),t(s))}{k_{0}^{2}}+1}f(z_{0}),

whenever
f
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
3.7
.
As it will be clear in the following of this section, equation (
3.16
) is a key step in proving the lower bound for the fundamental solution of
\mathscr{L}
.
Thus, in order to characterize the minimizing cost
\Psi
, and hence to obtain the
best exponent in
(
3.11
), we formulate the natural optimal control problem, i.e. we consider the function
\omega
as the
control
of the path
\gamma
in (
3.1
) and we look for the one
minimizing the
total cost

\Phi
defined in (
3.12
).
As observed above, given a solution to (
3.1
)-(
3.2
), the same control steers
(0,0,0)
to
(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})^{-1}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1})
. As the cost
\Phi
depends on the control only, the two trajectories have the same cost. Hence,

\Psi(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0};p_{1},y_{1},t_{1})=\Psi\left(0,0,0;(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})^{-1}\circ_{\mathcal{L}}(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1})\right).

As a consequence, we will fix the initial condition
(p_{0},y_{0},t_{0})=(0,0,0)
in (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) and then use the invariance property to solve it with a
general initial condition. Thus, our aim is to study the optimal control problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\omega\in L^{2}([0,T])}\int_{0}^{T}\omega^{2}(\tau)d\tau\quad\textit{ subject to the constraint}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\begin{cases}p^{\prime}(s)&=\omega(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1},\\
y^{\prime}(s)&=-p(s),\qquad 0\leq s\leq T,\\
t^{\prime}(s)&=-\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\textit{with }\,(p,y,t)(0)=(0,0,0),\quad(p,y,t)(T)=(p_{1},y_{1},t_{1}),\quad\textit{with $t_{1}<0$}.

To solve problem (
3.2
), one possible approach could be to apply the Pontryagin Maximum Principle (see
[
25
, Chapter 6]
) and to compute the Hamiltonian

(3.18)

\begin{split}&H(p,y,t,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},m_{0},\omega)=\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\lambda_{1}(s)\omega(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}-\lambda_{2}(s)p(s)-\lambda_{3}(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}+m_{0}\omega^{2}(s),\end{split}

where
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
are the coordinates of the covector
\lambda
.

We recall the first order optimality condition is ever considered to be sufficient, unless the
normality condition
holds, that is
when the Lagrange multiplier
m_{0}
is not vanishing, see
[
1
]
.
Hence, we first show the normality condition holds true in the case of our interest.

Proposition 3.8
.

Problem (
3.2
) admits no abnormal extremals.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction by assuming
m_{0}=0
in (
3.18
).
Given this choice of
m_{0}
, (
3.18
) now reads as follows

H(p,y,t,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},0,\omega)=\lambda_{1}(s)\omega(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}-\lambda_{2}(s)p(s)-\lambda_{3}(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}.

In this case, the maximization of the Hamiltonian reads as follows

\frac{\partial H}{\partial\omega}(p,y,t,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},0,\omega)=\lambda_{1}(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_{1}(s)=0,\quad\forall s\in[0,T].

Moreover, owing to
\lambda_{1}(s)=0
, for every
s\in[0,T]
there holds

\lambda_{1}^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}(p,y,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},0,\omega)=\lambda_{2}(s)+\frac{\lambda_{3}(s)p(s)}{\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_{2}(s)=-\frac{\lambda_{3}(s)}{\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}}.

Additionally, as
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}=0
and
\lambda_{3}^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}=0
, we directly compute
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}(s)
and we obtain

\lambda_{2}^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{\lambda_{3}(s)}{\left(p^{2}(s)+1\right)^{3/2}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_{3}(s)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_{2}(s)=0,\quad\forall s\in[0,T].

Thus, we conclude that

(\lambda_{1}(s),\lambda_{2}(s),\lambda_{3}(s),m_{0})=(0,0,0,0),\quad\forall s\in[0,T],

which contradicts the fact that
(\lambda_{1}(s),\lambda_{2}(s),\lambda_{3}(s),m_{0})
is never vanishing.
∎

Since no abnormal extramals occur, we choose
m_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}
and we compute the optimal control
\omega^{*}
as the unique minimizer of
H(p,y,t,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},-\frac{1}{2},\omega)
, i.e.

(3.19)

\omega^{*}(s)=\lambda_{1}(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}.

As a consequence, the maximized Hamiltonian
H^{*}
is

(3.20)

H^{*}(p,y,t,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},-\frac{1}{2},\omega^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}_{1}(s)(p^{2}(s)+1)-\lambda_{2}(s)p(s)-\lambda_{3}(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1},

and the corresponding Hamiltonian system reads as follows

(3.21)

\begin{cases}p^{\prime}(s)&=\lambda_{1}(s)\left(p^{2}(s)+1\right),\\
y^{\prime}(s)&=-p(s),\\
t^{\prime}(s)&=-\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}\\
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}(s)&=-p(s)\lambda_{1}^{2}(s)+\lambda_{2}(s)+\frac{\lambda_{3}(s)}{\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}},\\
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}(s)&=0\\
\lambda_{3}^{\prime}(s)&=0.\end{cases}

We observe that, from the last equation in (
3.21
), it follows

\lambda_{2}(s)=c_{2},\,\,\lambda_{3}(s)=c_{3},\qquad\forall s\in[0,T].

Moreover, we choose the parameter
k:=\lambda_{1}(0)
as the initial condition for the first extremal, which is the unique solution to (
3.21
), with initial condition

(p,y,t,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})(0)=(0,0,0,k,c_{2},c_{3}).

Furthermore, as the Hamiltonian is a constant of motion, we set

(3.22)

E:=\lambda_{1}^{2}(s)\left(p^{2}(s)+1\right)-2\lambda_{2}(s){p(s)}-2\lambda_{3}(s)\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}=k^{2}-2c_{3}.

Moreover, in virtue of (
3.19
) and equations
y^{\prime}(s)=-p(s)
,
t^{\prime}(s)=-\sqrt{p^{2}(s)+1}
, we can compute the cost for extremals as follows

(3.23)

\begin{split}C(\omega(\cdot))&=\int_{0}^{T}\omega^{2}(\tau)d\tau=\int_{0}^{T}\lambda_{1}^{2}(\tau)\left(p^{2}(\tau)+1\right)d\tau\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\left(E-2c_{2}y^{\prime}(\tau)-2c_{3}t^{\prime}(\tau)\right)d\tau=ET-2c_{2}y_{1}-2c_{3}t_{1}.\end{split}

Remark 3.9
.

Since solving analytically (
3.21
) is a real challenge, we are not able to further proceed in our characterization of the optimal control.
For this reason, the statement of Theorem
1.2
explicitly reports the value function
\Psi
. In the future, it will be interesting to study this problem from a numerical perspective, as already proposed in

]
for the pricing problem for Asian Options.

Proof of Theorem

In this section we prove a lower bound for the fundamental solution
\Gamma
of
\mathscr{L}
. We follow the approach proposed in
[
9
]
, where an analogous result is proved about an operator arising in Finance. A key tool in this argument is a lower bound for a Green function
G
for operator
\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}
introduced in (

First of all, we consider the functions
a
and

in (

in order to have ontinuous coefficients satisfying assumption

(4.1)

\begin{split}a(x,y,t)&=\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{5/2}\quad\text{and}\quad b(x,y,t)=-2x\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{3/2},\quad\text{for}\quad-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq x\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\\

Then,
[
22

provides us with a fundamental solution
\Gamma_{\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}}

of the Kolmogorov operator
\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}
introduced in (

\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}
in a suitable cylinder
\mathcal{H}
defined as follows.

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{S}\times]0,T[,\qquad{\text{with }}\quad\mathcal{S}=B((1,0),3/2)\cap B((-1,0),3/2),

where

, and

12

\mathcal{H}

f

\mathcal{H}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\partial_{P}\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{S}\times\{0\})\cup(\partial\mathcal{S}\times[0,T]).

The Green function for

\displaystyle G(x,y,t;\xi,\eta,\tau):=\Gamma_{\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}}(x,y,t;\xi,\eta,\tau)-h(x,y,t;\xi,\eta,\tau),

{\varphi}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle v(x,y,t):=\int\limits_{\mathcal{H}}G(x,y,t;\xi,\eta,\tau)g(\xi,\eta,\tau)\,d\xi\,d\eta\,d\tau+\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}}G(x,y,t;\xi,\eta,0){\varphi}(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}f=-g\qquad&\text{in }\mathcal{H},\\

We point out that the above property is stated in

. We finally recall the statement of a local lower bound for the Green function given in

.

There exists two positive constants

We are now in a position to prove a local lower bound for the fundamental solution

of the relativistic operator
\mathscr{L}

The proof of this result is an adaptation of
[
9

to the case of our interest.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every positive constant

, there exists a psitive constant

,
only depending on the constants appearing in assumption

, such that

\Gamma(0,0,t;0,0,0)\geq\frac{\kappa_{T}}{t^{2}},\qquad\forall t\in\left]0,T\right[.

Proof.

In order to prove our claim, we just need to show that there holds

\Gamma(p,y,t;\xi,\eta,\tau)\geq G(p,y,t;\xi,\eta,\tau)\qquad\forall(p,y,t;\xi,\eta,\tau)\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}\times\mathcal{H}.

) holds true, then the result for

. The result for any

Thus, it is only left to prove inequality (

{\varphi}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{S})
and for every

\displaystyle v(p,y,t)

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}}G(p,y,t;\xi,\eta,0){\varphi}(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\Gamma(p,y,t;\xi,\eta,0)-G(p,y,t;\xi,\eta,0)\right){\varphi}(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta\geq 0

Appendix A
Higher dimensional case

A.1.
Hörmander’s operators

In this section, we check that the
d
-dimensional operator
\mathscr{L}
in (
1.1
) writes in the form (
1.2
) and satisfies the Hörmander’s condition (
1.5
).

We first explain how to choose the vector fields
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
in (
1.3
). As a first step, we observe that equation (
1.1
) can be written in its non-divergence form

(1.5)

\mathscr{L}f(p,y,t)=\textrm{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}+p\otimes p\right)\nabla^{2}_{p}f\right)+d\,p\cdot\nabla_{p}f-Yf=0.

We consider the
d\times d
symmetric matrix
\mathbf{X}

\mathbf{X}(p)=\left(X_{1}(p),\dots,X_{d}(p)\right),

whose columns are the coefficients of the vector fields
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
.
We have

\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{j}f=\mathbf{X}\,\nabla_{p}f,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{j}^{2}f=\mathbf{X}^{2}\,\nabla^{2}_{p}f+\widetilde{c}\cdot\nabla_{p}f,

for some vector
\widetilde{c}=\widetilde{c}(p)
. We then determine
\mathbf{X}
such that
\mathbf{X}^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{d}+p\otimes p
. To do this, we recall that, for any given
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}+q\otimes q\right)^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{d}+(2+|q|^{2})\,q\otimes q.

Then,

(1.6)

\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}+q\otimes q\right)^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{d}+p\otimes p

if we choose

(1.7)

q=\alpha\,p\qquad\text{for some $\alpha$ such that}\qquad(2+|q|^{2})|q|^{2}=|p|^{2}.

Direct computations show that the second equality in (
1.7
) implies that

(1.8)

1+|q|^{2}=\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}

and therefore

(1.9)

\alpha=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}}}.

Hence, by choosing

\mathbf{X}=\mathbb{I}_{d}+q\otimes q,\qquad q=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}}}\,p

we find the vector fields
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
introduced in (
1.3
). Moreover, the components of
\widetilde{c}
are

(1.10)

\widetilde{c}_{j}(p)=\sum_{i,k=1}^{d}\left(\delta_{ik}+q_{i}q_{k}\right)\tfrac{\partial(q_{j}q_{k})}{\partial p_{i}},\quad j=1,\dots,d.

Thus, from (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) we obtain the following identity

\mathscr{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{j}^{2}+(d\,p-\widetilde{c}(p))\cdot\nabla_{p}-Y.

In order to conclude that
\mathscr{L}
writes in the form (
1.2
), we observe that the matrix
\mathbb{I}_{d}-\tfrac{1}{1+|q|^{2}}\,q\otimes q
is the inverse of
\mathbb{I}_{d}+q\otimes q
.
As a consequnce, we have

\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}-\tfrac{1}{1+|q|^{2}}\,q\otimes q\right)\left(X_{1}(p),\dots,X_{d}(p)\right)=\nabla_{p}.

This concludes the proof of (
1.2
), where the vector
c(p)=\left(c_{1}(p),\dots,c_{d}(p)\right)
is defined as

c(p)=(d\,p-\widetilde{c}(p))^{T}\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}-\tfrac{1}{1+|q|^{2}}\,q\otimes q\right)

and has smooth coefficients, in virtue of (
1.7
) and (
1.9
).

We next prove that
\mathscr{L}
does satisfy the Hörmander’s condition (
1.5
). We first note that the Lie algebra generated by
X_{1},\dots,X_{d},X_{d+1}
agrees with the Lie algebra generated by
X_{1},\dots,X_{d},Y
, and then we claim that

(1.11)

{\rm rank}\,{\rm Lie}\,\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d},Y\}\,(p,y,t)=2d+1,\qquad\forall(p,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}.

We compute the commutator
[X_{j},Y]
for
j=1,\dots,d
. We find that

[X_{j},Y]f:=X_{j}Yf-YX_{j}f=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\delta_{jk}+q_{j}q_{k}\right)\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial y_{k}}+p_{j}\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}.

We now consider the
(2d+1)\times(2d+1)
matrix
\mathbf{M}
whose columns are the coefficients of
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
,
[X_{1},Y],\dots,[X_{d},Y],Y
and we prove that

(1.12)

\textrm{det}\,\mathbf{M}=\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\Big(X_{1},\dots,X_{d},[X_{1},Y],\dots,[X_{d},Y],Y\Big)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{d}+q\oplus q&\mathbb{O}_{d}&0_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}+q\oplus q&p\\
0_{d}^{T}&p^{T}&\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbb{O}_{d}
is the
d\times d
matrix whose entries are zeros, and
0_{d}
is the zero column vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Up to a change of basis in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it is not restrictive to assume that
q=|q|\,\mathbf{e}_{d}
, being
\mathbf{e}_{d}
the
d
-th vector of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the matrix
{\mathbf{M}}=
takes the simpler form

{\mathbf{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{D}&0_{2d-1}&0_{2d-1}\\
0_{2d-1}^{T}&1+|q|^{2}&|p|\\
0_{2d-1}^{T}&|p|&\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbf{D}=\mathbb{I}_{2d-1}+(1+|q|^{2})\,\mathbf{e}_{d}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{d}
. Thus, (
1.12
) follows from the first equality in (
1.9
).

A.2.
Lorentz invariance

The invariance with respect to Lorentz transformations is also preserved in the higher dimensional case. Indeed, it is sufficient to observe that the diffusion operator in (
1.1
) is the Laplace-Beltrami operator over the Riemannian manifold
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d},g\right)
, where
g
is the metric induced by the Minkonwski metric over the hyperboloid
\mathfrak{g}=\{(E,p):E=\sqrt{|p|^{2}+1}\}
. We recall that the Laplace-Beltrami operator is invariant with respect to isometries. Then, the invariance with of
\mathscr{L}
follows from the fact that the Lorentz transformation in the momentum component corresponds to a translation over
\mathfrak{g}
. Moreover, the invariance of the drift term
Y
in (
1.1
) follows immediately from (
1.18
), which clearly still holds true in the higher dimensional case.
