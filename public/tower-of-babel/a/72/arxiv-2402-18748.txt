Fast Bootstrapping Nonparametric Maximum Likelihood for Latent Mixture Models
Shijie Wang, Minsuk Shin, and Ray Bai

Abstract
Estimating the mixing density of a latent mixture model is an important task in signal processing. Nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation is one popular approach to this problem. If the latent variable distribution is assumed to be continuous, then bootstrapping can be used to approximate it. However, traditional bootstrapping requires repeated evaluations on resampled data and is not scalable. In this letter, we construct a generative process to rapidly produce nonparametric maximum likelihood bootstrap estimates. Our method requires only a single evaluation of a novel two-stage optimization algorithm. Simulations and real data analyses demonstrate that our procedure accurately estimates the mixing density with little computational cost even when there are a hundred thousand observations.



I
Introduction

Nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation (
NPMLE
) is a popular methodology in signal processing applications such as pattern recognition
[
1
,
2
]
, channel estimation
[
3
]
, signal recovery
[
4
]
, and positron emission tomography
[
5
,
6
]
. NPMLE also has applications in empirical Bayes and regression modeling
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
Suppose that we observe
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
where

y_{i}\mid\theta_{i}\sim f(y_{i}\mid\theta_{i}),~~\theta_{i}\sim\pi(\theta),~~i=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

and the density
f
is known but
\pi
is unknown. NPMLE aims to estimate the mixing density (or the prior)
\pi
.

The NPMLE estimator
\widehat{\pi}
in the latent mixture model (
1
)
[
10
,
11
]
was introduced by
[
12
]
and solves the optimization,

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}

\displaystyle=\underset{\pi\in\mathrm{\Pi}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{log}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\{f(y_{i}\mid\theta_{i})\}]

\displaystyle=\underset{\pi\in\mathrm{\Pi}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{log}\left\{\int_{\Theta}f(y_{i}\mid\theta_{i})d\pi(\theta_{i})\right\},

(2)

where
\mathrm{\Pi}
denotes the family of all probability distributions on the parameter space
\Theta
. Under mild regularity conditions,
[
13
]
showed that the solution
\widehat{\pi}
of (
I
) exists and is unique, and even if the true
\pi
is continuous,
\widehat{\pi}
is almost surely discrete with a support of at most
n
points. Exploiting this property, there exist many algorithms to solve (
I
), including the EM algorithm
[
14
,
15
]
and convex optimizers
[
16
,
17
]
.

Nevertheless, a discrete NPMLE estimator
\widehat{\pi}
is unsatisfactory when the latent distribution is reasonably assumed to be continuous
[
5
,
18
]
.
As a result, smoothed variants of NPMLE have been proposed
[
5
,
7
,
2
,
19
]
. However, these methods typically require careful tuning of smoothing parameters such as bandwidth
[
19
,
2
]
, roughness penalty term
[
5
,
7
]
, and/or spline degrees of freedom
[
2
,
7
]
.

As an alternative to smoothing, bootstrapping has been shown to be an effective way to simulate from both prior and posterior continuous densities
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. However, bootstrapping is seldomly employed for NPMLE. This may be because the bootstrap traditionally involves resampling the data with replacement and repeatedly optimizing weighted objective functions.
In the context of NPMLE, the standard bootstrap procedure requires one to repetitively optimize

\widehat{\pi}^{(b)}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{(b)}\mathrm{log}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\{f(y_{i}\mid\theta_{i})\}],~~\bm{w}^{(b)}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{w}},

(3)

for some probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{w}}
. For example, if
\bm{w}\sim\text{Multinomial}(n,\mathbbm{1}_{n}/n)
, where
\mathbbm{1}_{n}
denotes an
n
-dimensional one vector, we have the nonparametric bootstrap
[
24
]
. If
\bm{w}\sim n\times\text{Dirichlet}(n,\mathbbm{1}_{n})
, we have the weighted likelihood bootstrap
[
22
]
. Repeatedly solving (
3
) can be time-consuming.

To resolve this computational bottleneck and broaden the applicability of bootstrapping, we build upon the generative bootstrap sampling (GBS) approach of
[
25
]
. We introduce a new generative framework called Generative Bootstrapping for NPMLE (
GB-NPMLE
). Our contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose GB-NPMLE for estimating a continuous prior density via bootstrapping. Different from GBS which only requires weights
\bm{w}
as inputs
[
25
]
, GB-NPMLE needs
additional
noise inputs
\bm{z}
to capture the latent representation of
\pi
in (
1
).

2.

To optimize GB-NPMLE, we introduce a novel two-stage algorithm. In contrast to traditional bootstrapping, GB-NPMLE requires only a
single
evaluation of this two-stage algorithm instead of repetitive evaluations of (
3
).

II
Proposed Method

II-A

GB-NPMLE

Recent developments in generative learning such as variational autoencoders (VAEs)
[
26
]
and generative adversarial networks (GANs)
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
provide another perspective for NPMLE.
Instead of estimating
\pi
, we can construct a generator
T
that matches to the target density
\pi
, i.e.
T(\bm{z})\sim\pi
where
\bm{z}

\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
follows a known distribution such as a Gaussian or a uniform.
We then reformulate NPMLE optimization (
I
) via a generative framework,

\widehat{T}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{log}[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\{f(y_{i}\mid T({\bm{z}})\}],

(4)

where
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
follows (for example) a standard uniform.

[
30
]
considered (
4
) where the generator
T
was constructed by a VAE
[
26
]
with a Langevin bias-correction algorithm
[
31
]
.
Unfortuantely, both the classical NPMLE
\widehat{\pi}
in (
I
) and the generative NPMLE
\widehat{T}
in (
4
) result in discrete solutions
[
13
]
.
This discreteness is less attractive when the true latent
\pi
is continuous, as in many signal processing applications
[
5
,
6
]
.

To efficiently obtain a bootstrapped NPMLE distribution as a smooth estimator for
\pi
in (
1
), we introduce GB-NPMLE. Our framework (approximately) generates NPMLE bootstrap estimates via a generator function. The generator
G:=G(\bm{w},\bm{z})
takes
both
noise
\bm{z}\in

\mathbb{R}^{q}

and
bootstrap weights
\bm{w}\in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
as inputs. We construct
G
using a feedforward neural network (FNN)
[
32
,
33
]
, because of the FNN’s universal approximation properties for a large class of functions
[
32
,
33
]
.

GB-NPMLE incorporates the GBS approach of
[
25
]
.
However, GBS is only geared towards uncertainty quantification of a single
point estimate
. Thus, GBS takes
only
weights
\bm{w}
as inputs and
cannot
be directly used to learn an entire
probability density

\pi
as in (
3
). In order to learn an unknown density
\pi
, GB-NPMLE
also
requires noise inputs
\bm{z}
, as in (
4
).
In summary, GB-NPMLE approximates the bootstrap distribution for NPMLE with the objective function,

\widehat{G}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{G}\ \mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\ \mathrm{log}[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\{f(y_{i}\mid G({\bm{w},\bm{z}})\}]\Big],

(5)

where
G(\bm{w},\bm{z}):

\mathbb{R}^{n+q}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. Once we have optimized
G
(i.e. the weights and biases of the FNN), it is then effortless to generate novel NPMLE bootstrap estimates as
\widehat{G}(\bm{w}_{\text{new}},\bm{z}_{\text{new}})
.

To optimize (
5
) in practice, the expectations
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}}
are approximated by Monte Carlo averaging, similarly as in VAEs
[
26
]
and GANs
[
27
]
. Specifically, we independently sample
z\sim\text{Unif}(0,1)
and
\bm{w}

\sim n\times\text{Dirichlet}(n,\mathbbm{1}_{n})
a sufficiently large number of times and then approximate the expectations in (
5
) with sample averages.

Directly optimizing
G
in (
5
) using stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
34
]
is a nontrivial task. In practice, the Monte Carlo approximation for
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}
introduces high variance to the approximate gradient for SGD because the log functions in (
5
) greatly shatter the linearity of
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}

[
35
]
.
Consequently, this can lead to slow convergence when optimizing the GB-NPMLE objective function (
5
)
[
35
]
. We address these estimation difficulties with a novel two-stage algorithm.

II-B

GB-NPMLE Two-Stage Algorithm

To efficiently optimize
G
in (
5
), we generalize the generator output
\theta=G(\bm{w},\bm{z})
to be a multi-dimensional
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
where
l>1
is the number of realizations of
\theta
.
The resulting sequence
\bm{\theta}=\{\theta^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{l}
serves as the bootstrap sample
candidates
. As we explain shortly, multiple realizations of
\theta
help to stabilize the gradient approximation in SGD for the Monte Carlo estimate of
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}
in (
5
).
Unfortunately, this generalization also leads to correlation across the bootstrap samples.

One way to overcome the correlation issue is to take a random draw of one
\theta^{(j)}
from
\bm{\theta}
according to mixing probabilities
\bm{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{l})
for the entries of
\bm{\theta}
. Repeating this procedure
B
times produces
B
independent bootstrap samples.
However, in addition to generator
G
, we now
also
need to estimate
\bm{\tau}
. Accordingly, we propose a two-stage algorithm where in Stage I, we train
G
, and in Stage II, we estimate
\bm{\tau}
.

GB-NPMLE Algorithm Stage I:
Fix
\bm{\tau}=(\tau_{1},...,\tau_{l})
where
\tau_{1}=...=\tau_{l}=1/l
, and solve the modified GB-NPMLE objective function,

\widehat{G}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{G}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\ \mathrm{log}[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z},\gamma}\{f(y_{i}\mid\bm{e}_{\gamma}^{\top}G({\bm{w},\bm{z}})\}]\Big],

(6)

where
\gamma\sim
Multinomial
(1,\bm{\tau})
,
\bm{e}_{\gamma}
is the
\gamma
-th unit vector, and
\bm{e}_{\gamma}^{\top}G({\bm{w},\bm{z}})\in\mathbb{R}
is the
\gamma
-th entry of generator output
\bm{\theta}
.

To see how the modified GB-NPMLE objective (
6
) helps to relieve high Monte Carlo variance, let
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z},\gamma}\{f(y_{i}|\bm{e}_{\gamma}^{\top}G({\bm{w},\bm{z}})\}
in (
6
) be denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\{\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\{f(\bm{\theta}[\bm{w},\bm{z},\gamma])\}\}
, and similarly, let
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\{f(y_{i}|G(\bm{w},\bm{z}))\}
be
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\{f(\bm{\theta}[\bm{w},\bm{z}])\}
in (
5
). By the law of total variance,
\text{Var}\{f(\bm{\theta}[\bm{w},\bm{z}])\}\geq\text{Var}\{\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}f(\bm{\theta}[\bm{w},\bm{z},\gamma])\}
. Hence, the generalization of the generator output dimension to
l>1
reduces Monte Carlo variance.

GB-NPMLE Algorithm Stage II:
Fix the well-trained generator
\widehat{G}
from (
6
) and estimate
\bm{\tau}
via the Monte Carlo EM (MCEM) algorithm
[
36
]
. In particular, the MCEM updates for each
k
th entry of
\bm{\tau}
have the closed form,

\tau_{k}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\tau_{k}^{(t)}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w},\bm{z}}\{f(y_{i}\mid\bm{e}_{k}^{\top}\widehat{G}({\bm{w}},\bm{z})\}}{\sum_{k=1}^{l}\tau_{k}^{(t)}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w},\bm{z}}\{f(y_{i}\mid\bm{e}_{k}^{\top}\widehat{G}({\bm{w}},\bm{z})\}}.

(7)

Note that we
already
obtained an efficient estimator of
\bm{\theta}
from Stage I, and hence, the MCEM algorithm tends to have very fast convergence. In the literature, slow convergence of MCEM mostly lies in obtaining a sequence of estimators
\bm{\theta}

[
30
]
. We circumvent this issue by
fixing
the generator from Stage I.

Once we have estimated both
G
(in Stage I) and
\bm{\tau}
(in Stage II), we can easily generate a new bootstrap estimate by sampling
\bm{w}_{\text{new}}
,
\bm{z}_{\text{new}}
,
\gamma_{\text{new}}
, and then taking
\theta_{\text{new}}=\bm{e}_{\gamma_{\text{new}}}^{\top}\widehat{G}(\bm{w}_{\text{new}},\bm{z}_{\text{new}})
. Repeating this procedure
B
times results in
B
independent bootstrap estimates. Thus, in contrast to bootstrapped NPMLE which requires
B
total repetitive evaluations of (
3
), GB-NPMLE requires only a
single
evaluation of the two-stage algorithm. The entire two-stage GB-NPMLE procedure is given in Algorithm
1
. Empirical evidence (reported in Appendix
-C
) demonstrates sufficient convergence of our algorithm.

III
Experiments and Results

III-A

Simulation Studies

We investigate the performance of GB-NPMLE optimized by the two-stage algorithm from Section
II-B
.
The generator
G
is an FNN with two hidden layers and 500 neurons per layer. Sensitivity analysis to this choice of architecture is presented in Appendix
-A
.
The expectations
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{w}}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}
in the modified GB-NPMLE objective (
6
) are approximated by Monte Carlo averages of
100

\bm{w}\sim n\times\text{Dirichlet}(n,\mathbbm{1}_{n})
,

(0,1) and
\gamma\sim
Multinomial
(1,\bm{\tau})
, and the length of
\bm{\tau}
is set to be
l=100
.
To train
G
, we use SGD with the Adam optimizer
[
37
]
. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti graphics processing unit (GPU) with 11GB RAM.

We consider three simulation settings for the prior
\pi
in the mixture model (
1
): (i) bimodal, (ii) unimodal and bounded on
(0,1)
, and (iii) skewed right. Additional simulations are conducted in Appendix
-B
.
Here, we consider:

(i)

Gaussian mixture model (
GMM
):
y\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)
and
\theta=0.5\mathcal{N}(-3,2)+0.5\mathcal{N}(3,1)

(ii)

\theta\sim\text{Beta}(10,5)
;

(iii)

Poisson mixture model (

and

.

We compare GB-NPMLE to bootstrapped NPMLE (
3
) and a smoothed version of the discrete NPMLE (

). We use

) a total of

in (

) with the

IV
Conclusion

In this letter, we introduced GB-NPMLE, a generative framework for estimating a mixing density through bootstrapping. A novel two-stage algorithm was proposed to rapidly and accurately obtain bootstrap estimates. Bootstrapping has rarely been employed for NPMLE in the past, and our work paves the way for bootstrapping to serve as an attractive alternative to smoothing NPMLE. Simulations and real data analyses
demonstrated the effectiveness and scalability of our approach. Codes to implement GB-NPMLE and the real datasets analyzed in this article are available on GitHub at

https://github.com/shijiew97/GBnpmle
.

Our methodology is only presented for estimating a univariate latent density. However, it will be useful to extend GB-NPMLE to
multivariate
density estimation. If covariates are also observed, then GB-NPMLE can be extended to mixture of regression models
[
8
]

-C

Convergence Analysis

To empirically check the convergence of the proposed two-stage algorithm, we plot the GB-NPMLE loss vs. epoch number for training the generator
G
in Stage I. For Stage II, we plot the log-likelihood vs. MCEM iteration number for learning the multinomial weights
\bm{\tau}
. Fig.
5
plots these solution paths for all 40 replications of the Gaussian and Gamma mixture models (i.e. Experiments (i) and (ii) in Section
III-A
, denoted as GMM and GaMM respectively.

Fig.
5
shows that Stage I converges quickly, typically within 50 epochs.
Meanwhile, Stage II converges even faster – typically within one or two iterations, resulting in a very flat solution path. The convergence plots for our other numerical experiments were very similar to those in Fig.
5
. Although empirical evidence suggests that the GB-NPMLE two-stage algorithm converges quickly, a rigorous theoretical analysis of convergence rate should be done for future work.
