Coherent Smith-Purcell γ \gamma -Ray Emission
Kamran Akbari, Simone Gargiulo, Fabrizio Carbone, F. Javier García de Abajo

Abstract
We investigate the Smith-Purcell emission produced by electron- or ion-beam-driven coherent excitation of nuclei arranged in periodic crystal lattices. The excitation and subsequent radiative decay of the nuclei can leave the target in the initial ground state after γ \gamma -ray emission, thus generating a coherent superposition of the far-field photon amplitude emanating from different nuclei that results in sharp angular patterns at spectrally narrow nuclear transition energies. We focus on Fe-57 as an example of two-level nuclear lossy system giving rise to Smith-Purcell emission at 14.4 keV with a characteristic delay of 142 ns relative to the excitation time. These properties enable a clean separation from faster and spectrally broader emission mechanisms, such as bremsstrahlung. Besides its fundamental interest, our study holds potential for the design of high-energy, narrow-band, highly-directive photon sources, as well as a means to store energy in the form of nuclear excitations.

I
Introduction

The interaction of electron beams with periodic gratings enables the generation of coherent radiation over a wide spectral range in synchrotrons and free-electron lasers
Jackson 1999
;
Saldin et al. 2000
. Indeed, far-field radiative components are produced in general upon scattering of the evanescent electromagnetic field that accompanies electrons in motion when they cross or pass near a material structure, giving rise to cathodoluminescence emission
García de Abajo 2010
. If the structure is periodically patterned with spatial period
d
along the electron velocity vector
{\bf v}
, Smith-Purcell (SP) radiation
Smith and Purcell 1953
is emitted over a broad range of wavelengths
\lambda
and angles
\theta_{n}
relative to
{\bf v}
satisfying the condition of far-field constructive interference

\displaystyle c/v-\cos\theta_{n}=n\lambda/d,

(1)

where the integer
n

van den Berg 1973
;
Moran 1992
;
Ishizuka et al. 2000
;
García de Abajo 2000
;
Yamaguti et al. 2002
;
Korbly et al. 2005
;
Ochiai and Ohtaka 2005
;
Blackmore et al. 2008
;
Remez et al. 2017
;
Remez et al. 2019
, as well as atomic layers in stacked van der Waals materials
Shentcis et al. 2020
. In addition, electrons act in unison if they are bunched within small beam regions compared to the emission wavelength in the moving frame, giving rise to superradiant light generation ranging from the terahertz
Urata et al. 1998
to the x-ray
Schachter and Ron 1989
;
Andrews and Brau 2004
spectral domains. Incidentally, the inverse process (electron energy gain near an illuminated grating) has also been demonstrated
Mizuno et al. 1987
and explored as a route to realise laser-based table-top particle accelerators
Kimura et al. 1995
.

Resonances in the periodic structure can enhance the emission, as demonstrated using plasmonic gratings
Yamamoto et al. 2015
. Likewise, atoms in a crystal structure can be resonantly excited by each passing electron, so that subsequent radiative decay to their initial ground states leaves the target unchanged and thus gives rise to a coherent superposition of amplitudes contributed by different atoms in the far field. The resulting wavelengths and directions of light emission are equally described by Eq. (
1
).

Nuclear transitions can also be excited by electromagnetic interaction with free electrons
Winther and Alder 1979
and ions
Alder et al. 1956
, and an analogous SP effect responding to the same kinematic condition as with electronic transitions is expected to take place. Supporting this possibility, coherent photon scattering from arrays of nuclei is inherent in the Mössbauer effect
Vandegrift and Fultz 1998
, which has prompted further studies of nuclear-based coherent optical response
Hannon and Trammell 1999
;
Smirnov 1999
. Given the large variety of nuclear excitation energies extending from
\sim 10\,
eV to megaelectronvolts, as well as the lifetimes of such excitations ranging from subnanoseconds to many years
IAEA
, electron- or ion-driven excitation of nuclei in a solid crystal offers a unique platform for exploring new physics with potential application in extreme light sources. A particularly fascinating opportunity is open by the coherent superposition of the emission from different nuclei when their radiative decay occurs with a long characteristic delay time after the passage of the charged projectiles, so that the angular distribution of the generated
\gamma
rays is controlled by the direction and velocity of the exciting beam.

In this work, we investigate coherent
\gamma
-ray emission associated with the nuclear excitations produced when relativistic electrons or ions traverse a periodic crystal target. Specifically, we focus on nuclear excitations in Fe-57 as an example of two-level lossy system in which the emission probability is relatively intense. The resulting SP emission takes place over a temporal scale dictated by the lifetime of these excitations (142 ns), and therefore, it can be neatly separated from faster and more intense coherent emission mechanisms, such as bremsstrahlung (BR)
Sáenz and Überall 1985
, which occurs during the projectile crossing of the target (e.g., within
<1\,
fs for a 100 nm-thick crystal). Besides its fundamental interest, our work emphasizes the potential of nuclear SP emission for application in extreme high-energy photon sources, capable of producing strongly directional angular patterns over the relatively long times associated with the slow decay of the selected nuclear excitations.

III
Conclusions

In conclusion, a periodic array of nuclei in a solid crystal film can be coherently excited by electrons or ions traversing the material, giving rise to the emission of
\gamma
rays along well-defined directions in analogy to the SP effect. Although we explore this effect here for Fe-57, it should also be observable in other nuclei, which configure a vast range of excitation energies and lifetimes
IAEA
. The predicted yield of nuclear SP emission is predicted to increase with the mass of the probe, which should enable closer collisions with the nuclei in the structure. Further improvement of the yield could be obtained by operating under channeling conditions similar to what happens in the Okorokov effect
Okorokov et al. 1973
;
Krause et al. 1993
;
García de Abajo and Echenique 1996
. Besides the fundamental interest lying in the demonstration of coherence between different nuclei in a sample by means of the observation of sharp angular emission profiles, our results hold potential as a characterization technique that should shed light into the spatial distribution of different isotopes in a material. The fact that
\gamma
rays are released from the sample over an interval controlled by the lifetime of the nuclear excitation, extending well beyond the time of passage of the beam, suggests a strategy for storing energy in the form of nuclear excitations, which can be later liberated as
\gamma
-ray emission along narrowly peaked angular directions.

Appendix B
Coherent
\gamma
-ray cathodoluminescence

We work in the nonrecoil approximation (i.e., the electron or ion velocity
{\bf v}
is assumed to remain constant during the interaction with the target), which is valid when the excitation energies are small compared with the kinetic energy of the probe, so that it is not strongly deflected by close collisions with the nuclei. In addition, the probability that a given electron or ion emits one
\gamma
-ray photon is much smaller than unity, so we can describe the interaction within first-order perturbation theory. Moreover, we consider monochromatic, collimated beams, moving along a well-defined direction. Under these conditions, a rigorous quantum-mechanical treatment of the probe-target interaction problem
García de Abajo and Di
Giulio 2020
shows that the excitation probability is identical with the one calculated by assimilating the electron or ion to the classical evanescent electromagnetic field that accompanies a moving point charge
eZ
(e.g.,
Z=-1
for electrons). For beams of finite extension along the transverse directions (
\perp{\bf v}
), this probability needs to be averaged over the beam density profile. In the present work, where the studied nuclei respond through their magnetic polarizability, we just need to plug the external magnetic field. Considering a trajectory
{\bf r}={\bf R}_{p}+{\bf v}t
, where
{\bf R}_{p}\perp{\bf v}
acts as an impact parameter, the probe produces an evanescent magnetic field
{\bf H}^{\rm ext}({\bf r},t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int d\omega\;{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t}{\bf H}^{\rm ext}({\bf r},\omega)
with spectral components
García de Abajo 2010

\displaystyle{\bf H}^{\rm ext}({\bf r},\omega)=\frac{2eZ\omega}{vc\gamma}\,K_{1}\left(\frac{\omega|{\bf R}-{\bf R}_{p}|}{v\gamma}\right){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega z/v}\,\hat{\varphi},

(15)

where we use cylindrical coordinates
{\bf r}=(R,\varphi,z)
and transverse coordinates
{\bf R}=(x,y)
, and
\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}
is the relativistic Lorentz factor.

For a collection of nuclei distributed at positions
{\bf r}_{j}
, coherent
\gamma
-ray emission is mediated by the frequency-dependent magnetic dipoles
{\bf m}_{j}(\omega)=\alpha_{M}(\omega){\bf H}^{\rm ext}({\bf r}_{j},\omega)
created in response to the field in Eq. (
15
) via the polarizability
\alpha_{M}(\omega)
given in Eq. (
14
). The dipole induced at a nucleus
j
produces an electric field
-k^{2}(\hat{\bf r}\times{\bf m}_{j})\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k|{\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j}|}/|{\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j}|
. In the far-field limit (
kr\gg 1
and
r\gg r_{j}
), this expression can be approximated as
-k^{2}(\hat{\bf r}\times{\bf m}_{j})\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}_{j}}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}kr}/r
with
{\bf k}=k\hat{\bf r}
. Summing over all dipoles, the resulting induced (emitted) electric field reduces to

\displaystyle{\bf E}^{\rm ind}({\bf r},\omega)={\bf f}(\omega)\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}kr}}{r},

where

\displaystyle{\bf f}(\Omega,\omega)=

\displaystyle-\alpha_{M}(\omega)\frac{2eZk^{3}}{v\gamma}

(16)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{j}K_{1}\left(\frac{\omega R_{jp}}{v\gamma}\right)\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega z_{j}/v}\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}_{j}}\,\hat{\bf r}\times\hat{\varphi}_{jp}

is the emission amplitude. Here,
\Omega
denotes the direction of
\hat{\bf r}
and we have defined the relative transverse coordinates
{\bf R}_{jp}={\bf R}_{j}-{\bf R}_{p}
and the azimuthal vectors
\hat{\varphi}_{jp}\perp{\bf R}_{jp},\,\hat{\bf z}
. Following the procedure described in Ref.
García de Abajo 2010
, the coherent photon emission probability
\Gamma^{\rm coh}
can be obtained by integrating the radial Poynting vector over time and directions of emission
\Omega
, and then dividing the result by the photon energy
\hbar\omega
. We find
\Gamma^{\rm coh}=\int d\Omega\,\Gamma^{\rm coh}(\Omega)
with

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rm coh}(\Omega)=\frac{c}{4\pi^{2}\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\left|{\bf f}(\Omega,\omega)\right|^{2}.

(17)

Inserting Eq. (
16
) into Eq. (
17
), we can carry out the
\omega
integral by approximating
\omega\approx\omega_{0}
outside
\alpha_{M}(\omega)
because the emission concentrates around a narrow spectral range of width
\kappa\ll\omega_{0}
around that frequency. This leads to

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rm coh}(\Omega)=\frac{9Z^{2}\,\alpha}{8\pi(v/c)^{2}\gamma^{2}}\;\frac{\kappa_{r}^{2}}{\omega_{0}\kappa}\;\left|\hat{\bf r}\times{\bf g}(\Omega)\right|^{2},

(18)

where
\alpha\approx 1/137
is the fine structure constant and

\displaystyle{\bf g}(\Omega)=\sum_{j}K_{1}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}R_{jp}}{v\gamma}\right)\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}z_{j}/v}\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{\bf k}_{0}\cdot{\bf r}_{j}}\,\hat{\varphi}_{jp}

(19)

depends on the emission direction
\Omega
through
{\bf k}_{0}=(\omega_{0}/c)\,\hat{\bf r}
.

For a single nucleus
j
, the angle-integrated probability reduces to

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rm coh}_{j}=\frac{3Z^{2}\alpha}{(v/c)^{2}\gamma^{2}}\;\frac{\kappa_{r}^{2}}{\omega_{0}\kappa}\;K_{1}^{2}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}R_{jp}}{v\gamma}\right),

(20)

which decays exponentially at large nucleus-beam distances
R_{jp}
but diverges as
\sim 1/R_{jp}
for close encounters.

Appendix C
Smith-Purcell
\gamma
-ray emission from a solid crystal target

The coherent emission from nuclei arranged in a crystal lattice gives rise to
\gamma
-ray emission along directions determined by the condition of constructive far-field interference, similar to the Smith-Purcell effect for grating structures
Smith and Purcell 1953
;
van den Berg 1973
. We consider a homogeneous crystalline film consisting of
N
atomic planes with one emitting nucleus in each two-dimensional (2D) unit cell of each of those planes. For simplicity, we take the film to be normal to the
z
axis (i.e., we consider normally impinging beams). The crystal structure can then be defined in terms of the 2D reciprocal lattice and the three-dimensional displacement vector
{\bf b}={\bf b}_{\parallel}+b_{z}\hat{\bf z}
separating the reference nuclei in two consecutive atomic planes.

At this point, we need to multiplex the sum over nuclei sites
{\bf r}_{j}
in Eq. (
19
) into two sums: one over 2D real-lattice sites
{\bf R}_{j}
and the other over atomic planes
z=z_{l}
, keeping in mind that we replace
{\bf r}_{j}
by
{\bf R}_{j}+l{\bf b}
. In addition, the 2D lattice sum can be transformed into a sum over 2D reciprocal lattice vectors
{\bf G}
by consecutively applying the identities

\displaystyle K_{1}(\Delta R)\hat{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta}(\hat{\bf x}\partial_{y}-\hat{\bf y}\partial_{x})K_{0}(\Delta R),

\displaystyle K_{0}(\Delta R)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d^{2}{\bf Q}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\bf Q}\cdot{\bf R}}}{Q^{2}+\Delta^{2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf R}_{j}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\bf Q}\cdot{\bf R}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{A}\sum_{\bf G}\delta({\bf Q}-{\bf G}),

where
A
is the unit cell area and
\Delta=\omega_{0}/v\gamma
. We find

\displaystyle{\bf g}(\Omega)=-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}v\gamma}{A\omega_{0}}\,\sum_{\bf G}

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}({\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G})\cdot{\bf R}_{p}}\,\frac{|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|}{|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|^{2}+(\omega_{0}/v\gamma)^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\hat{\varphi}_{{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}}\;S_{\bf G},

(21)

where
{\bf k}_{\parallel}
is the in-plane component of
{\bf k}_{0}=(\omega_{0}/c)\hat{\bf r}
, we define the azimuthal vector
\hat{\varphi}_{\bf Q}=(-Q_{y}\hat{\bf x}+Q_{x}\hat{\bf y})/Q
, and the coefficient

\displaystyle S_{\bf G}=\sum_{\ell}\exp\left\{{\rm i}\ell\left[(\omega_{0}b_{z}/c)(c/v-\cos\theta)+{\bf G}\cdot{\bf b}_{\parallel}\right]\right\}

(22)

encapsulates the sum over atomic planes and depends on the polar angle of emission
\theta
relative to
\hat{\bf z}
.

We now insert Eq. (
21
) into Eq. (
18
) and take the average of the result over the beam impact parameter
{\bf R}_{e}
(i.e., we average over lateral film positions). This allows to simplify the double sum over reciprocal lattice vectors emerging when squaring
{\bf g}(\Omega)
in Eq. (
18
) by applying
A^{-1}\int_{A}d^{2}{\bf R}_{p}\,\left|\sum_{\bf G}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{\bf G}\cdot{\bf R}_{p}}{\bf a}_{\bf G}\right|^{2}=\sum_{\bf G}|{\bf a}_{\bf G}|^{2}
, which is a consequence of the identity
A^{-1}\int_{A}d^{2}{\bf R}_{p}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}({\bf G}-{\bf G}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf R}_{p}}=\delta_{{\bf G},{\bf G}^{\prime}}
, where the integrals extend over a 2D unit cell. In addition, the geometric sum over atomic planes in Eq. (
22
) can also be carry out analytically to yield
|S_{\bf G}|^{2}=\sin^{2}(N\xi_{\bf G}/2)/\sin^{2}(\xi_{\bf G}/2)
, where

\xi_{\bf G}=(\omega_{0}b_{z}/c)(c/v-\cos\theta)+{\bf G}\cdot{\bf b}_{\parallel}

and
N
is the number of atomic layers. For
N\gg 1
, we have
|S_{\bf G}|^{2}\approx 2\pi N\sum_{m}\delta(\xi_{\bf G}-2\pi m)
, where
m
runs over integer numbers. Putting these elements together, we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rm coh}(\Omega)\approx

\displaystyle N\;\frac{9\pi^{2}Z^{2}\alpha\,c^{2}\kappa_{r}^{2}}{A^{2}\omega_{0}^{3}\kappa}\;\sum_{\bf G}\frac{|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|^{2}}{\left[|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|^{2}+(\omega_{0}/v\gamma)^{2}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left|\hat{\bf r}\times\hat{\varphi}_{{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}}\right|^{2}\;\sum_{m}\delta\left(\xi_{\bf G}-2\pi m\right).

The
\delta
-function in each
m
term determines a polar cone of emission through the condition
\xi_{\bf G}=2\pi m
, where the dependence on
{\bf G}
can be partially or completely eliminated by redefining
m
for different types of crystal lattices. In particular, for a (100) film made of a simple cubic crystal with lattice constant
a
, we can take
{\bf b}_{\parallel}=0
and
b_{z}=a
, so the
{\bf G}
dependence disappears from
\xi_{\bf G}
and we recover the familiar Smith-Purcell condition
\cos\theta_{n}=c/v-n\lambda/a
in terms of the light wavelength
\lambda
and the interatomic plane spacing
a

Iron forms a bcc lattice, so considering a (100) film orientation, we can take
{\bf b}_{\parallel}=(a/2,a/2)
,
b_{z}=a/2
,
A=a^{2}
, and
{\bf G}=(2\pi/a)(i,j)
, where
a=2.856\,{\AA}
is the lattice constant, while
i
and
j
run over integer numbers. For such Fe(100) film, we have
{\bf G}\cdot{\bf b}_{\parallel}=(i+j)\,\pi
, so we can redefine
n=2m-i-j
and separate the emission as a sum over components directed along the cones determined by

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{n}=c/v-n\lambda/d

(23)

according to

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rm coh}(\Omega)=\sum_{n}\Gamma^{\rm coh}_{n}(\varphi)\;\delta(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_{n}),

where
d=a
gives the periodicity along the out-of-plane direction,
\lambda=2\pi c/\omega_{0}
is the emission wavelength, and

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rm coh}_{n}(\varphi)\approx

\displaystyle N\;\frac{18\pi^{2}Z^{2}\alpha\,\kappa_{r}^{2}}{dc(\omega_{0}a/c)^{4}\kappa}\;{\sum_{\bf G}}^{\prime}\frac{|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|^{2}}{\left[|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|^{2}+(\omega_{0}/v\gamma)^{2}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left|\hat{\bf r}\times\hat{\varphi}_{{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}}\right|^{2}

(24)

is the probability density as a function of the azimuthal angle of emission
\varphi

\theta_{n}
arises in Eq. (
24

{\bf k}_{\parallel}=k\sin\theta_{n}\;(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)

vectors such that
i+j+n
(

For Dy-161, we consider a (100) film of DyN, which forms an fcc crystal structure with a Dy-Dy nearest-neighbors distance

Shinde et al. 2015

Appendix D
Incoherent
\gamma
-ray emission

Each beam-excited nucleus can decay to a state that differs from the original ground state (i.e., to a different sublevel
|\mu\rangle
) by emitting one
\gamma
-ray photon. The probability
\Gamma^{\rm incoh}
associated with this incoherent process is the sum of the probabilities contributed by all nuclei in the target. Then, as we argue in Sec.
A
, each nucleus emits incoherently with an angular profile given by the average of magnetic dipoles oriented perpendicularly to the external magnetic field. For a given nucleus, since the magnetic field of the probe is parallel to the azimuthal direction
\hat{\varphi}_{jp}
[see Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
)], we have to average the field intensities produced by two magnetic dipoles of equal magnitude oriented along
{\bf R}_{jp}
and
\hat{\bf z}
. This results in an angular profile
\propto|\hat{\bf r}\times\hat{\bf R}_{jp}|^{2}+|\hat{\bf r}\times\hat{\bf z}|^{2}=1+\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}(\varphi-\varphi_{jp})
, where the angles
\Omega=(\theta,\varphi)
define the emission direction. Making use of the fact that the respective fractions of coherent and incoherent emission are
f
and
1-f
(see Sec.
A
and Table
1
), we can readily write the angle-resolved incoherent emission probability as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rm incoh}(\Omega)=

\displaystyle\frac{9Z^{2}\alpha}{16\pi(v/c)^{2}\gamma^{2}}\;\frac{\kappa_{r}^{2}}{\omega_{0}\kappa}\;\left(\frac{1}{f}-1\right)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{j}K_{1}^{2}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}R_{jp}}{v\gamma}\right)\;\left[1+\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}(\varphi-\varphi_{jp})\right].

To obtain this result, we have added the coherent emission probability coming from each nuclei
j
[Eq. (
20
)], previously multiplied by both
(1-f)/f
and the normalized angular profile.

Appendix E
Bremsstrahlung emission

Under the conditions here investigated, particles in the beam can be deflected by the Coulomb potential of the target nuclei, leading to Bremsstrahlung (BR) emission. The radiated energy can be expressed as
\int d\Omega\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\hbar\omega\,\Gamma^{\mathrm{BR}}(\Omega,\omega)
, where
\Gamma^{\mathrm{BR}}(\Omega,\omega)
gives the spectral and angular distribution of the emission probability. For an arbitrary time-dependent velocity vector
{\bf v}(t)
and trajectory
{\bf r}_{p}(t)
of the particle, this probability admits the expression
Jackson 1999

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{BR}}

\displaystyle(\Omega,\omega)=\frac{\alpha Z^{2}}{4\pi^{2}\omega}

(25)

\displaystyle\times\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\;{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega\left[t-\hat{\bf r}\cdot{\bf r}_{p}(t)/c\right]}\;\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{\hat{\bf r}\times{\bf v}(t)/c}{1-\hat{\bf r}\cdot{\bf v}(t)/c}\right]\right|^{2}.

Assuming that the interaction with a target nucleus only produces a small perturbation in the particle velocity relative to its initial value
{\bf v}(-\infty)=v_{0}\hat{\bf z}
, we can retain corrections to first order in the Coulomb potential and write the equation of motion
\dot{{\bf v}}(t)\approx\big(ZZ_{n}e^{2}/M\gamma\big)\big({\bf R}+\hat{\bf z}\,v_{0}t/\gamma^{2}\big)/(R^{2}+v_{0}^{2}t^{2})^{3/2}
, where
{\bf R}
is the beam-nucleus separation vector,
eZ
and
eZ_{n}
are the probe and nucleus charges,
M
is the mass of the projectile, the Lorentz factor
\gamma
is evaluated from
v_{0}
, and we neglect any effect arising from electrons surrounding the nucleus in the actual material. We now expand Eq. (
25
) to first order in
\dot{{\bf v}}
and use the above equation of motion to work out the time integral, which yields the result

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{BR}}

\displaystyle(\Omega,\omega)=\frac{\alpha^{3}Z^{4}Z_{n}^{2}\hbar^{2}\omega}{\pi^{2}M^{2}\gamma^{2}v_{0}^{4}}

(26)

\displaystyle\times\left|\left(1-\beta\cos\theta\right)\hat{\bf r}\times{\bf F}+\beta\left(\hat{\bf r}\times\hat{\bf z}\right)\left(\hat{\bf r}\cdot{\bf F}\right)\right|^{2},

where
{\bf F}=K_{1}\left(\zeta\right)\hat{\bf R}+({\rm i}/\gamma^{2})K_{0}\left(\zeta\right)\hat{\bf z}
,
\zeta=(1-\beta\cos\theta)\,\omega R/v_{0}
, and
\beta=v_{0}/c
. We use Eq. (
26
) to obtain the BR curves in Fig. 2 of the main text, as it gives reasonable results for the velocities under consideration (e.g., as compared to a virtual-quanta analysis
Jackson 1999
).
