The Regularity of ODEs and Thimble Integrals with Respect to Borel Summation
Veronica FANTINI a and Aaron FENYES b

Abstract
Through Borel summation, one can often reconstruct an analytic solution of a problem from its asymptotic expansion. We view the effectiveness of Borel summation as a regularity property of the solution, and we show that the solutions of certain differential equation and integration problems are regular in this sense. By taking a geometric perspective on the Laplace and Borel transforms, we also clarify why “Borel regular” solutions are associated with special points on the Borel plane. The particular classes of problems we look at are level 1 1 ODEs and exponential period integrals over one-dimensional Lefschetz thimbles. To expand the variety of examples available in the literature, we treat various examples of these problems in detail.

1.2.2
Borel regularity sometimes explains why Borel summation works

The idea of Borel regularity can help us understand why Borel summation is so effective in some situations. Roughly speaking, Borel summation works well for problems that admit solutions in terms of the Laplace transform.

1.3
Goals and Results

We present the results, proofs, and examples in this paper with a few central goals in mind. Our first goal is to discuss Borel summability in a way that carefully separates the analytic and formal sides of the theory. In particular, we highlight results that can be proven in purely analytic ways, like Theorem
1.5
for level
1
ODEs and Lemma
1.9
for thimble integrals.

Our second goal is to emphasize how the problems we study become more regular when posed in the position domain (the Borel plane). When we take level
1
differential equations from the frequency domain to the position domain, we not only turn their irregular singularities into regular ones, but also turn them into integral equations, which provide more smoothness and can be solved more constructively. When we write thimble integrals as Laplace transforms, their irregularly shaped integration contours collapse to rays in the position domain.

Our third goal is to illustrate the results we discuss with detailed examples involving a diversity of Borel regular functions. We treat several examples both as thimble integrals and as solutions of level
1
ODEs, so you can compare these parallel explanations of how Borel regularity arises. We include ODEs of both second degree and higher degree. Using the widely discussed example of the Airy function, we compare our treatment with other approaches and conventions found in the literature.

We state our main results in Sections
1.3.1
–
1.3.2
and prove them in Section
4
. Our examples are given in Section
5
. Section
1.4
describes the layout of the paper in more detail.

1.3.1
Why does Borel summation work for solutions of level 1 ODEs?

Consider a linear level
1
differential operator
\mathcal{P}
of the form described in Section
1.2.2
. This operator will always have an irregular singularity at
z=\infty
. We can find holomorphic solutions of the equation
\mathcal{P}\Phi=0
by taking the Borel sums of formal trans-monomial solutions. However, this only works when there is a coincidence between the exponential factor
{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}
in the formal solution, the base point
\zeta=\alpha
we use for Borel summation, and the characteristic equation
{P(-\alpha)=0}
. Why?

A first clue is the observation that every solution we get from Borel summation is, by definition, the Laplace transform
\Phi=\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}^{\theta}\phi
of a function
\phi
on the position domain. In fact, the differential equation
\mathcal{P}\Phi=0
is equivalent to an integral equation
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha}\phi=0
.

Furthermore, when
-\alpha
is a root of
P
, the integral equation
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha}\phi=0
has a special solution
\psi_{\alpha}
which is distinguished, up to scaling, by its power-law asymptotics at
\zeta=\alpha

[
45
, Theorem 4.6]
. This solution is regular enough to have a well-defined Laplace transform
\Psi_{\alpha}
, which is a special solution of
\mathcal{P}\Phi=0
distinguished by its own characteristic asymptotics.

Theorem 1.5

(Theorem
4.1
)
.

Let
-\alpha
be a root of
P
where
\tau_{\alpha}
is real and positive. Consider an open sector
\Omega_{\alpha}
which has an opening angle of
\pi
or less, has
\zeta=\alpha
at its tip, and does not touch any other root of
P(-\zeta)
. The equation
\mathcal{P}\Phi=0
has a unique solution
\Psi_{\alpha}
in the affine subspace
{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}\bigl[z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}+\widehat{\mathcal{H}}L^{\infty}_{-\tau_{\alpha}-1}\bigl(\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}^{\bullet}\bigr)\bigr]

of the space
{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}\widehat{\mathcal{H}}L^{\infty}_{-\tau_{\alpha}}\bigl(\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}^{\bullet}\bigr)
from Definition
3.4
.

The special solution
\psi_{\alpha}
is also regular enough to have a “fractionally shifted” power series expansion
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}
, whose formal Laplace transform
\tilde{\Psi}_{\alpha}
is a formal solution of
\mathcal{P}\tilde{\Phi}=0
. By construction,
\tilde{\Psi}_{\alpha}
is Borel summable, with Borel sum
\Psi_{\alpha}
.

Theorem 1.6

(Theorem
4.2
)
.

The analytic solution
\Psi_{\alpha}
from Theorem
1.5
is the Borel sum of a formal trans-monomial solution
\tilde{\Psi}_{\alpha}\in{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
of the equation
\mathcal{P}\tilde{\Phi}=0
.

We can use this result to show that
\Psi_{\alpha}
is Borel regular. Because of the fractional power
z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}
, we use Lemma
3.19
instead of a more standard result on the asymptotics of a Borel sum, such as
[
79
, Theorem 5.20]
.

Corollary 1.7

(Corollary
4.3
)
.

The analytic solution
\Psi_{\alpha}
from Theorem
1.5
is Borel regular. This is because
\Psi_{\alpha}
is asymptotic to the formal solution
\tilde{\Psi}_{\alpha}
from Theorem
1.6
.

In many cases, by choosing from among the solutions
\Psi_{\alpha}
, we can build a frame of Borel-regular solutions of the equation
\mathcal{P}\Phi=0
.

From an analytic perspective, Theorem
1.6
justifies the ansatz that Poincaré used to solve the equation
\mathcal{P}\tilde{\Phi}=0
. On the other hand, from a formal perspective, one can start from the computation of the Poincaré solutions and the observation that their Borel transforms converge, and then use Theorem
1.5
to establish their Borel summability. We do this in Section
4.1.2
.

As discussed in Section
2.2
, the novel aspect of Theorem
1.5
is that it is stated and proved in the world of functional analysis, rather than the world of formal series. Theorem
1.6
is a new proof of a special case of an old result mentioned in Section

: the multisummability of formal solutions of linear systems of ODEs
[
16
,
91
]
. The new proof is interesting because it starts from the analytic solutions found in Theorem
1.5
, rather than the formal solutions found by Poincaré.

1.3.2
Why does Borel summation work for thimble integrals?

In Section
1.2.2
, we gave the general form of a thimble integral associated with a map
f\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
. The important thing about

here turns out to be its

Given a translation chart
\zeta

\mathcal{J}_{\zeta,\alpha}^{\theta}
to denote the ray in direction

\zeta=\alpha

runs through one of the critical points that

sends to
\zeta=\alpha

is one-dimensional, as it will be throughout this paper, a

is an oriented component of

that runs through a single, non-degenerate critical point of

. A one-dimensional Lefschetz thimble is determined up to orientation by the critical point
a
that it runs through and the direction
\theta
of the ray that it projects to, so we will often denote it with a symbol like
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{a}
. Because, by our definition, a Lefschetz thimble can only run through a single critical point
a
, the ray that it projects to cannot touch any critical value except

2.2.2
Écalle’s theory of resurgence

Écalle’s theory of resurgence introduced a new perspective on formal solutions, mostly focused on the analysis of the position domain
[
42
,
70
]
. In a nutshell, resurgence provides information about the analytic continuation of the Borel transform of a divergent series. In particular, it reveals the locations of singularities in the position domain, and it quantifies the Stokes phenomenon in terms of “Stokes constants” describing analytic continuation around the singularities.

A series is resurgent if its Borel transform converges to an endlessly analytically continuable function on the position domain. Thus, resurgence creates a bridge between formal and analytic solutions of problems in the position domain.

The formal solutions of a level
1
ODE are always resurgent series
[
42
, Proposition 2.2.1]
. Écalle proved this by using formal perturbation theory to solve a corresponding integral equation in the position domain. Using the “formalism of singularities” to understand how the singularities of the perturbative solution would propagate from one order of perturbation to the next, Écalle showed that the perturbation series would converge to an endlessly analytically continuable function. Loday-Richaud and Remy went on to show that this function always has a well-defined Laplace transform, so the corresponding formal solution in the frequency domain is Borel summable
[
70
]
. Malgrange got similar results by working with differential equations, rather than integral equations, on the position domain
[
72
]
.

Écalle’s theory not only shed light on Borel summation, but also generalized it. Écalle introduced generalized resummation methods that use “well-behaved averages” to handle infinite sequences of singularities along the summation axis
[

43
,

.

In many cases, the solutions of Riemann–Hilbert problems can be found explicitly

2.2.4
Laplace transform methods and fixed point iteration

All of the historical approaches we have discussed so far start by looking for formal solutions. In this paper, we will focus on a different kind of approach, where we start by looking for analytic solutions expressed as Laplace transforms. By carefully choosing the starting point of the Laplace transform, we get analytic solutions which are asymptotic to Poincaré’s formal ones. These solutions will turn out to be Borel regular.

We use the Laplace transform to turn differential equations on the frequency domain into integral equations on the position domain. This runs parallel to Écalle’s use of the Borel transform. Building on the existence and uniqueness results we proved in
[
45
]

The function spaces mentioned in Section

[
16

3.1.4
Change of translation chart

Suppose
\zeta
is a translation chart on
B
, and
\zeta=\alpha
is an ordinary point. Let
\zeta_{\alpha}
be the translation chart with
\zeta=\alpha+\zeta_{\alpha}
. The Laplace transforms
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta_{\alpha},0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}
, which both turn functions on
B
into functions on

, are related in the following way.

Lemma 3.2
.

If the Laplace transform of
\varphi
is well defined, then

{\rm e}^{-z\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta_{\alpha},0}\varphi=\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}\varphi.

(3.2)

Proof.

With a change of variable in the integral that defines the Laplace transform, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{\zeta,\alpha}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta}\varphi\,{\rm d}\zeta=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{\zeta_{\alpha},0}}{\rm e}^{-z(\alpha+\zeta_{\alpha})}\varphi\,{\rm d}\zeta_{\alpha}={\rm e}^{-z\alpha}\int_{\mathcal{J}_{\zeta_{\alpha},0}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta_{\alpha}}\varphi\,{\rm d}\zeta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-z\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta_{\alpha},0}\varphi.

∎

We now consider a rescaling of the translation structure of
B
, expanding displacements by a factor of
\mu\in(0,\infty)
. The coordinate
\xi=\mu\zeta
is a chart for the new translation structure. The corresponding frequency coordinate
x\colon T^{*}B\to B
is given by
{\rm d}\xi\mapsto 1
, so
x=\mu^{-1}z
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\Lambda}(\Omega)
with
\sigma>-1
and for some
\Lambda>0
, then

\mathcal{L}_{\xi,0}\varphi=\mu\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,0}\varphi
.

Proof.

From the computation

\mathcal{L}_{\xi,0}\varphi=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{\xi,0}}{\rm e}^{-x\xi}\varphi\,{\rm d}\xi=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{\zeta,0}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta}\varphi\mu\,{\rm d}\zeta=\mu\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,0}\varphi,

we get the desired result.
∎

The Borel and Laplace transforms as algebra homomorphisms

The Laplace transform as algebra homomorphism.

The Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}
is defined on the space of holomorphic functions in the position domain which are integrable at
\zeta=\alpha
and exponentially bounded at infinity. This subspace of holomorphic functions on
B

\mathbb{C}

Definition 3.8
.

Choose a translation coordinate
\zeta
on
B
. Let
\Omega_{\alpha}\subset B
be an open set with the point
\zeta=\alpha
on its boundary, and let
\Omega_{\beta}\subset B
be an open set with
\zeta=\beta

\phi\in\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\bullet}(\Omega_{\alpha})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\sigma^{\prime},\bullet}(\Omega_{\beta})
with
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in(-1,\infty)

\phi\ast_{\zeta,\alpha,\beta}\psi
is the function on

\phi\ast_{\zeta,\alpha,\beta}\psi:=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\zeta,\alpha,\beta}}\phi(a)\psi\bigl(a^{\prime}\bigr)\,{\rm d}\zeta(a),

where
\mathcal{S}_{\zeta,\alpha,\beta}
is the path
[0,1]\to B^{2}
given by

\zeta_{\alpha}(a)=t\zeta_{\alpha+\beta},\qquad\zeta_{\beta}\bigl(a^{\prime}\bigr)=(1-t)\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}

on
[0,1]

Notation

We denote
\ast_{\zeta,\alpha,\alpha}

Lemma 3.9

Take
\phi\in\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\Lambda}(\Omega_{\alpha})

Proof.

From Definition

, we get the bound

\displaystyle\left|\phi\ast_{\zeta,\alpha,\beta}\psi\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\zeta,\alpha,\beta}}|\phi(a)||\psi\bigl(a^{\prime}\bigr)|\,|{\rm d}\zeta(a)|=\int_{0}^{1}|\phi(a)||\psi\bigl(a^{\prime}\bigr)||\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|\,{\rm d}t,

using the fact that

\displaystyle|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|\int_{0}^{1}|\phi(a)||\psi\bigl(a^{\prime}\bigr)|\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\|\phi\|_{\sigma,\Lambda}\|\psi\|_{\sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}}|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|\int_{0}^{1}|\zeta_{\alpha}(a)|^{\sigma}{\rm e}^{\Lambda|\zeta_{\alpha}(a)|}|\zeta_{\beta}\bigl(a^{\prime}\bigr)|^{\sigma^{\prime}}{\rm e}^{\Lambda^{\prime}|\zeta_{\beta}\bigl(a^{\prime}\bigr)|}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\|\phi\|_{\sigma,\Lambda}\|\psi\|_{\sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}}|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|^{1+\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}|t|^{\sigma}{\rm e}^{\Lambda t|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|}|1-t|^{\sigma^{\prime}}{\rm e}^{\Lambda^{\prime}(1-t)|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|^{1+\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}}{\rm e}^{(\Lambda+\Lambda^{\prime})|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|}\int_{0}^{1}t^{\sigma}(1-t)^{\sigma^{\prime}}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|^{1+\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}}{\rm e}^{(\Lambda+\Lambda^{\prime})|\zeta_{\alpha+\beta}|}.

The product of two Laplace transforms based at different points is the Laplace transform of a convolution.

The Borel transform as algebra isomorphism.

The Borel transform
\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}
is defined on the space of formal power series
z^{-1}\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
, which is a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra, with product given by the Cauchy product of formal power series. In addition, introducing the formal unit
\delta
, the Borel transform can be extended to
\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
(including constants), as
\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}(1)=:\delta
. Therefore, the Borel transform is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map

\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\colon\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket\to\mathbb{C}\delta+\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket
.
In fact, since
\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}
is defined as the formal inverse of
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,0}
we can introduce the
formal Laplace transform
as the map from
\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket
to
z^{-1}\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
which acts as
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,0}
on monomials
\zeta^{k}
and then it extends by countable linearity. As a result, we find that the Borel transform is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear invertible map whose inverse is the formal Laplace transform.

In addition, on both
\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket
and
\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
there is the natural product given by the Cauchy product of formal power series. However, the Borel transform does not extend to a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra isomorphism, because
\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\bigl(\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Psi}\bigr)
is different from the Cauchy product
\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\tilde{\Phi}\bigr)\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\tilde{\Psi}\bigr)
.

To see the Borel transform as an algebra isomorphism, we must use a different product on
\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket
: the convolution product for formal power series. Let
\zeta
be a coordinate on
B
. For any integers
m
,
n
,

\frac{\zeta^{m}}{m!}\ast_{\zeta}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}=\frac{\zeta^{m+n+1}}{(m+n+1)!}.

(3.6)

Definition 3.13
.

We extend the convolution product
\ast_{\zeta}
by countable linearity to the whole ring of formal power series
\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}\right)\ast_{\zeta}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}b_{n-j}\left[\frac{\zeta^{j}}{j!}\ast_{\zeta}\frac{\zeta^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}\right]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}b_{n-j}\frac{\zeta^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.

Furthermore, we define the convolution product of
\delta

,
\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}\ast_{\zeta}\delta:=\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}
,
in other words,
\delta
is the unit for the convolution.

Notice that from equation (

\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}

endowed with the Cauchy product to
\mathbb{C}\delta+\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket
endowed with the convolution product.

3.5
Bridging the gap between formal and analytic objects

3.5.1
Formal to analytic

If we want to get analytic information from the Borel transform—for example, by doing Borel summation—we should focus on the series whose Borel transforms converge. These series, called
1
-Gevrey
series, form a subspace
\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket_{1}
within
\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
. They are characterized by the factorial growth of their coefficients.

Definition 3.16
.

A series

\sum_{n\geq-1}a_{n}z^{-n-1}

is
1
-Gevrey
if there is some
A>0
with
|a_{n}|\lesssim A^{n}n!
over all
n\geq 0
.

Proposition 3.17
.

A series

\tilde{\Phi}=\sum_{n\geq-1}a_{n}z^{-n-1}

is
1
-Gevrey if and only if its Borel transform
{\tilde{\phi}=\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\tilde{\Phi}}
is convergent.

Proof.

From the expression

\tilde{\phi}=a_{-1}\delta+\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!},

we see that
\tilde{\phi}
converges if and only if there is some
A>0
with
|a_{n}/n!|\lesssim A^{n}
over all
n\geq 0
. The constant
A
is the radius of convergence of
\tilde{\phi}
.
∎

The space of
1
-Gevrey series is closed under the Cauchy product. In addition,

\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\colon\ \mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket_{1}\to\mathbb{C}\delta+\mathbb{C}\{\zeta\}

extends to an algebra homomorphism, where
\mathbb{C}\{\zeta\}
is endowed with the convolution product defined in Definition
3.13
. In fact, we deduce that the Borel transform induces an algebra homomorphism on

-Gevrey series. Similarly, if
z
is coordinate on the fiber over

as a regular singular Volterra equation
f=\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}f
that satisfies the conditions of the underlying existence and uniqueness result
[
45
, Theorem 1.6]
. The operator
\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}
is the sum of the “prototypical” part

\mathcal{V}_{0}^{\alpha}=-\frac{1}{p}\circ\partial^{-1}_{\zeta,\alpha}\circ q

and the perturbation

\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{p}\circ\partial^{-2}_{\zeta,\alpha}\circ R\bigl(\partial^{-1}_{\zeta,\alpha}\bigr)
.

Let us run through the proof of Theorem 1.6, specialized to the case we consider in Theorem 4.6. First, picking an arbitrary point

, we show that the
prototype solution

f_{0}(a)=\frac{1}{p(a)}\exp\left(-\int_{b}^{a}\frac{q}{p}\,{\rm d}\zeta_{\alpha}\right)

lies in the space
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha}-1,\bullet}(\Upsilon)
and satisfies the equation
f_{0}=\mathcal{V}_{0}^{\alpha}f_{0}
. We then look for a perturbation
f_{\star}
that makes
f=f_{0}+f_{\star}
a solution of the Volterra equation we are trying to solve. This is equivalent to solving the inhomogeneous equation

f_{\star}=\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}f_{0}+\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}f_{\star}.

(4.3)

The central idea of the proof is to show that
\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}
maps
f_{0}
, and in fact all of
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha}-1,\bullet}(\Upsilon)
, into
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha},\bullet}(\Upsilon)
, and that
\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}
is a contraction of
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha},\Lambda}(\Upsilon)
when
\Lambda
is large enough. It follows, by the contraction mapping theorem, that equation (
4.3
) has a unique solution
f_{\star}
in
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha},\Lambda}(\Upsilon)
. More explicitly, we can solve equation (
4.3
) by fixed-point iteration. Defining

\displaystyle f_{\star}^{(0)}=\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}f_{0},\qquad f_{\star}^{(1)}=\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}f_{0}+\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}f_{\star}^{(0)},\qquad f_{\star}^{(2)}=\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}f_{0}+\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}f_{\star}^{(1)},

\displaystyle f_{\star}^{(3)}=\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}f_{0}+\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}f_{\star}^{(2)},\qquad\dots,

we get a sequence of functions that converges in
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha},\Lambda}(\Upsilon)
to a solution

.

In
[
45
, Section 3.2.1]
, we rewrite the prototype solution in the form

f_{0}(a)=\frac{1}{p(a)}\left(\frac{\zeta_{\alpha}(a)}{\zeta_{\alpha}(b)}\right)^{\tau_{\alpha}}\exp\left[-\int_{b}^{a}\left(\frac{q}{p}+\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{\zeta_{\alpha}}\right){\rm d}\zeta_{\alpha}\right],

which shows that it represents a germ in
\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}
. By working with Taylor series, we can show that
\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\alpha}
maps

\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}\to\zeta^{\tau_{\alpha}}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}

and
\mathcal{V}^{\alpha}
maps
\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}
to itself. It follows that the sequence
f_{\star}^{(0)},f_{\star}^{(1)},f_{\star}^{(2)},\dots
lies in
\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}\cap\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha},\Lambda}(\Upsilon)
, which by Lemma
3.18
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha},\Lambda}(\Upsilon)
. The limit
f_{*}
, which satisfies equation (
4.3
), therefore represents a germ in
\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}
. Recalling that the prototype solution represents a germ in
\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}
, we can deduce that
\psi_{\alpha}
represents a germ in
\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}
too.

We now cross over into the formal world, expanding
\psi_{\alpha}
as a shifted Taylor series
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}\in\zeta_{\alpha}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\mathbb{C}\{\zeta\}
. By definition, the formal Laplace transform
\tilde{\Psi}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}
lies in
{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
.

When we take the Borel sum of
\tilde{\Psi}_{\alpha}
, we start by retracing the construction steps described above. The Borel transform inverts the formal Laplace transform, bringing us back to
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}
, and summation then inverts the Taylor expansion, bringing us back to
\psi_{\alpha}
. We finish the Borel summation by taking the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}
, giving
\Psi_{\alpha}
by definition.
∎

Corollary 4.3

(restatement of Corollary
1.7
)
.

The analytic solution
\Psi_{\alpha}
from Theorem

is Borel regular. This is because
\Psi_{\alpha}
is asymptotic to the formal solution

from Theorem

.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
4.2
, we showed that the position-domain solution

is the sum of a shifted Taylor series
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}\in\smash{\zeta^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\mathbb{C}\{\zeta\}}
. Since

4.1.2
A new proof of the Borel summability of the Poincaré solutions

In

Proof.

By Proposition

, the Borel transform

Since the series

4.2
Borel regularity for thimble integrals

Let us recall the setting from Section

5.2
Airy–Lucas

The Airy–Lucas equation is

\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)^{2}-(m-1)y^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}-y^{n-2}\right]\Phi=0

with
n\in\{3,4,5,\dots\}
and
m\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
. A few solutions, indexed by
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, are given by the Airy–Lucas functions
[
20

\widehat{\Ai}^{(j)}_{n,m-1}(y)=\left\{\begin{matrix}i&j\text{ odd}\\
1&j\text{ even}\end{matrix}\right\}\frac{y^{m/2}}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}}\exp\left[\frac{2}{n}y^{n/2}T_{n}(u)\right]U_{m-1}(u)\,{\rm d}u,

(5.2)

where
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}
is the contour that passes through the point
u=\cos\bigl(\frac{j}{n}\pi\bigr)
and projects to the ray
\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\pm 1}
under the mapping
-\zeta=T_{n}(u)
. The ray starts at the critical value
1
when
j
is odd, and
-1
when
j
is even. To ensure that the integral converges,
\theta
should be near
-\frac{n}{2}\arg(y)
. Figure
12
shows the contours
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}
, at a suitable
\theta
, in the
n=5
case.

5.2.1
Rewriting as a modified Bessel
\boldsymbol{\frac{m}{n}}
equation

We can distill the most interesting parts of the Airy–Lucas function by writing

\widehat{\Ai}^{(j)}_{n,m-1}(y)=\frac{2\sinh\bigl(\frac{m}{n}{\rm i}\pi\bigr)}{n\pi}\left\{\begin{matrix}{\rm i}&j\text{ odd}\\
1&j\text{ even}\end{matrix}\right\}y^{{m/2}}K^{(j)}_{m/n}\left(\frac{2}{n}y^{n/2}\right),

where

K^{(j)}_{m/n}(z)=\frac{n}{2\sinh\bigl(\frac{m}{n}{\rm i}\pi\bigr)}\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}}\exp[zT_{n}(u)]U_{m-1}(u)\,{\rm d}u.

(5.3)

Saying that
\widehat{\Ai}^{(j)}_{n,m-1}
satisfies the Airy–Lucas equation is equivalent to saying that
K^{(j)}_{m/n}
satisfies the modified Bessel equation with parameter
\frac{m}{n}

\left[z^{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{2}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\left[\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{2}+z^{2}\right]\right]\Phi=0.

(5.4)

Let us put equation (
5.4
) in the form
\mathcal{P}\Phi=0
with
\mathcal{P}
as in equation (
4.1
)

\left[\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{2}-1\right]+z^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\left({\frac{m}{n}}\right)^{2}z^{-2}\right]\Phi=0.

(5.5)

5.2.2
Asymptotic analysis

From the work of Poincaré and the Ramis index theorem, as discussed at the beginning of Section
4.1.2
, we know that equation (
5.5
) has a frame of formal
1
-Gevrey trans-monomial solutions

\big\{{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}\tilde{W}_{\alpha}\mid\alpha^{2}-1=0\big\}
,
where
\tau_{\alpha}=1/2
and
\tilde{W}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket_{1}
. From
[
84
, equations (10.40.2) and (10.17.1)]
, we learn that
K_{m/n}\sim\bigl(\frac{\pi}{2}\bigr)^{1/2}{\rm e}^{-z}z^{-1/2}\tilde{W}_{1}
, with

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{1}=1-\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.5pt-\hskip-0.5pt\frac{m}{n}\bigr)_{1}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.5pt+\hskip-0.5pt\frac{m}{n}\bigr)_{1}}{2^{1}\cdot 1!}z^{-1}+\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.5pt-\hskip-0.5pt\frac{m}{n}\bigr)_{2}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.5pt+\hskip-0.5pt\frac{m}{n}\bigr)_{2}}{2^{2}\cdot 2!}z^{-2}-\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.5pt+\hskip-0.5pt\frac{m}{n}\bigr)_{3}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\hskip-0.5pt+\hskip-0.5pt\frac{m}{n}\bigr)_{3}}{2^{3}\cdot 3!}z^{-3}+\cdots\!.

The holomorphic analysis in Section
5.2.3
will give us holomorphic solutions

\big\{{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}W_{\alpha}\mid\alpha^{2}-1=0\big\}
,
which seem analogous to the trans-monomials above. Borel summation makes the analogy precise. We will see in Section
5.2.6
that each
z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}W_{\alpha}
is a scalar multiple of the Borel sum of
z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}\tilde{W}_{\alpha}
, as required by Theorem
4.4
.

5.2.3
The big idea

We are going to look for functions
v_{\alpha}
whose Laplace transforms
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}v_{\alpha}
satisfy equation (
5.5
). We will succeed when
\alpha^{2}-1=0
, and we will see that
K_{m/n}
is a scalar multiple of
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,1}v_{1}
.

By the properties of the Laplace transform laid out in Proposition
3.23
, a function
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}v
satisfies the differential equation (
5.5
) if and only if
v
satisfies the integral equation

\left[\bigl[\zeta^{2}-1\bigr]-\partial^{-1}_{\zeta,\alpha}\circ\zeta-\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{2}\partial^{-2}_{\zeta,\alpha}\right]v=0.

(5.6)

It is tempting to differentiate both sides of this equation until we get

\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)^{2}\circ\bigl[\zeta^{2}-1\bigr]-\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\circ\zeta-\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{2}\right]v=0,

(5.7)

which is easier to solve. Unfortunately, an analytic solution of equation (
5.7
) will not satisfy equation (
5.6
) in general. However, as we show in Appendix
B
, a solution of equation (
5.7
)
will
satisfy equation (
5.6
) if it belongs to
(\zeta-\alpha)^{\sigma}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}
for some
\sigma\in(-1,0)
.

This is good news, because equation (
5.7
) has a regular singularity at each root of
\zeta^{2}-1
, and the Frobenius method often gives a solution of the desired kind at each regular singular point. We can see the regular singularities by rewriting equation (
5.7
) as follows

\left[\bigl(\zeta^{2}-1\bigr)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)^{2}+3\zeta\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}+\left[1-\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{2}\right]\right]v=0.

In Sections
5.2.4
–
5.2.5
, we will see this approach succeed. For each root
\alpha
, we will find a solution
v_{\alpha}
of equation (
5.7
) that belongs to
(\zeta-\alpha)^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=\alpha}
for some constant
\tau_{\alpha}\in(0,1)
. We will express
v_{\alpha}
explicitly enough to see that it extends to a function in
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{\tau_{\alpha}-1,\bullet}(\Omega_{\alpha})
on any sector
\Omega_{\alpha}
that has its tip at
\zeta=\alpha
and does not touch the other roots (see Figure
9
). We will also be able to see that
v_{\alpha}
is uniformly of exponential type
\Lambda
for any
\Lambda>0
. It follows by Proposition
3.5
that for any ray
\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\alpha}
in
\Omega_{\alpha}
, the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\alpha}v_{\alpha}
is a well-defined element of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}L^{\infty}_{-\tau_{\alpha}}\bigl(\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}^{\Lambda}\bigr)
that satisfies equation (
5.5
). Using the technique from the proof of Lemma
3.19
, we can see more precisely that
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\alpha}v_{\alpha}
belongs to

cz^{-\tau_{\alpha}}+\widehat{\mathcal{H}}L^{\infty}_{-\tau_{\alpha}-1}\bigl(\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}^{\Lambda}\bigr)

for some non-zero constant
c
, confirming the existence part of Theorem
4.1
.

We know from Section
3.1.4
that

\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\alpha}v_{\alpha}={\rm e}^{-\alpha z}V_{\alpha}

for
V_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta_{\alpha},0}v_{\alpha}
and
\zeta=\alpha+\zeta_{\alpha}
. Observing that multiplication by
z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}
gives an isometry
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}L^{\infty}_{0}\bigl(\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}^{\Lambda}\bigr)\to\widehat{\mathcal{H}}L^{\infty}_{-\tau_{\alpha}}\bigl(\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}^{\Lambda}\bigr)
, we can do the further decomposition

\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\alpha}v_{\alpha}={\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-\tau_{\alpha}}W_{\alpha}
,
where
W_{\alpha}
is a bounded holomorphic function on
\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}^{\Lambda}
.

5.2.4
Focus on
\boldsymbol{\zeta=1}

Let us find a germ in
(\zeta-1)^{\tau_{1}-1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=1}
, for some
\tau_{1}\in(0,1)
, that satisfies equation (
5.7
). Define a new coordinate
\zeta_{1}
on
\mathbb{C}
so that
\zeta=1+\zeta_{1}
. In this coordinate, equation (
5.7
) looks like

\left[\zeta_{1}(2+\zeta_{1})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta_{1}}\right)^{2}+3(1+\zeta_{1})\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta_{1}}+\left[1-\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{2}\right]\right]v=0.

With another change of coordinate, given by
\zeta_{1}=-2\xi_{1}
, we can rewrite equation (
5.7
) as the hypergeometric equation

\left[\xi_{1}(1-\xi_{1})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{1}}\right)^{2}+3(\frac{1}{2}-\xi_{1})\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{1}}-\left[1-\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{2}\right]\right]v=0.

Looking through the twenty-four expressions for Kummer’s six solutions, we find one
[
84
, formula (15.10.12)]
that represents a germ in
\xi_{1}^{-1/2}\mathcal{O}_{\xi_{1}=0}

\displaystyle v_{1}=\xi_{1}^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\xi_{1}\right)=-{\rm i}\sqrt{2}\zeta_{1}^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{1}\right).

From the expression above, we can see that
v_{1}
has only two singularities in the complex plane: one at
\zeta_{1}=0
from the factor of
\zeta_{1}^{-1/2}
, and one at
\zeta_{1}=-2
from the hypergeometric function. Therefore,
v_{1}
is holomorphic throughout the sector
\Omega_{1}=\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\mathcal{J}^{\pi}_{\zeta_{1},0}
. Since
v_{1}
has a power law singularity at
\infty
, it is uniformly of exponential type
\Lambda
for any
\Lambda>0
. Therefore, it belongs to the space
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{-1/2,\Lambda}(\Omega_{1})
for all
\Lambda>0
. By thinking about its shifted Taylor expansion around
{\zeta_{1}=0}
, we can see more precisely that
v_{1}
belongs to

-{\rm i}\sqrt{2}\zeta_{1}^{-1/2}+\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{1/2,\Lambda}(\Omega_{1})

for all
{\Lambda>0}
. As discussed in Section
5.2.3
, we conclude that
v_{1}
has a well-defined Laplace transform along any ray from
\zeta=1
except the one going directly left. Its Laplace transform can be written as
{\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,1}v_{1}={\rm e}^{-z}z^{-1/2}W_{1}}
,
where
W_{1}
is a holomorphic function on the slit plane
{z\notin(-\infty,0]}
which is bounded outside any constant-radius neighborhood of the ray
z\in(-\infty,0]
.

Focus on
\boldsymbol{\zeta=-1}

Let us find a germ in
(\zeta+1)^{\tau_{-1}-1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=-1}
, for some
\tau_{-1}\in(0,1)
, that satisfies equation (
5.7
). In the rescaled coordinate from Section
5.2.4
, this is the point
\xi_{1}=1
. Looking again through Kummer’s table of solutions, we find another expression
[
84
, formula (15.10.14)]
that represents a germ in

\displaystyle v_{-1}

\displaystyle=(1-\xi_{1})^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};1-\xi_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\zeta_{-1}^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{-1}\right),

where
\zeta_{-1}
is the coordinate with

v_{-1}
is holomorphic throughout the sector
\Omega_{-1}=\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\zeta_{-1},0}

In this example,

and

happen to be related by a symmetry: the Möbius transformation that pulls
\zeta
back to

5.2.6
Abstract argument for Borel regularity

The analysis in Sections
5.2.3
–
5.2.5
picks out a frame in the space of analytic solutions of (
5.5
). The frame is generated by solutions of the form
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,1}v_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,-1}v_{-1}
, with
v_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{-1/2,\bullet}(\Omega_{\alpha})
.

The Poincaré algorithm, as discussed in Section
5.2.2
picks out a frame in a space of formal solutions of equation (
5.5
). The frame is generated by
1
-Gevrey trans-monomial solutions of the form
{\rm e}^{-z}z^{-1/2}\tilde{W}_{1}
and

, with
\tilde{W}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket_{1}
. Reprising the proof of Theorem
4.4
, we will show that these solutions are Borel summable, and their Borel sums generate the same frame as
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,1}v_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,-1}v_{-1}
.

The Borel transform
\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}
maps
{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-1/2}\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket_{1}
into
\zeta_{\alpha}^{-1/2}\mathbb{C}\{\zeta_{\alpha}\}
, as discussed in Sections
3.3.1
and
4.1.2
. In particular, it sends each Poincaré solution
{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-1/2}\tilde{W}_{\alpha}
to a convergent series
\tilde{v}_{\alpha}\in\zeta_{\alpha}^{-1/2}\mathbb{C}\{\zeta_{\alpha}\}
. Make a bounded sector
\Omega^{\prime}_{\alpha}
by intersecting
\Omega_{\alpha}
with a disk around
\zeta_{\alpha}=0
whose radius is smaller than the radius of convergence of
\tilde{v}_{\alpha}
. On
\Omega^{\prime}_{\alpha}
, the sum
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
of
\tilde{v}_{\alpha}
can be bounded by a shifted geometric series, showing that
\zeta_{\alpha}^{1/2}\hat{v}_{\alpha}
is bounded by a constant. Therefore,
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{-1/2,\bullet}(\Omega^{\prime}_{\alpha})
.

The Borel transform

\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\colon\ {\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-1/2}\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket_{1}\to\zeta_{\alpha}^{-1/2}\mathbb{C}\{\zeta_{\alpha}\}

also turns formal trans-monomial solutions of equation (
5.5
) into formal power series solutions of equation (
5.6
). Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem,
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
is an analytic solution of equation (
5.6
) on
\Omega^{\prime}_{\alpha}
. The uniqueness part of
[
45
, Theorem 4.6]
then shows that
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
must be a scalar multiple of the solution
v_{\alpha}
we found in Sections
5.2.3
–
5.2.5
. Since
v_{\alpha}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}L^{\infty}_{-1/2,\bullet}(\Omega_{\alpha})
for the full, unbounded sector
\Omega_{\alpha}
, it has a well-defined Laplace transform along any ray
\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\alpha}
in
\Omega_{\alpha}
. Therefore, the Poincaré solution
{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}z^{-1/2}\tilde{W}_{\alpha}
has a well-defined Borel sum along
\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\alpha}

5.2.8
Thimble projection reasoning

We can also study the Airy–Lucas functions by applying the thimble projection formula to integral (
5.3
), specializing the reasoning behind Lemma
4.5
. We recast integral (
5.3
) into the
\zeta
plane by setting
-\zeta=T_{n}(u)
, which implies that
-{\rm d}\zeta=nU_{n-1}(u){\rm d}u
. Recall from below equation (
5.2
) that the integration contour
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}
runs through the critical point
u=\cos\bigl(\frac{j}{n}\pi\bigr)
, with its direction determined by the parameter
\theta
. The critical point splits
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}
into two pieces: the
incoming
branch, where the orientation of the thimble runs toward the critical point, and the
outgoing
branch, where the orientation points away. Recalling that
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}
is a preimage of
\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\mp 1}
, we get

\displaystyle K^{(j)}_{m/n}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2\sinh\bigl(\frac{m}{n}{\rm i}\pi\bigr)}\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}}\exp\left[zT_{n}(u)\right]U_{m-1}(u)\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\sinh\bigl(\frac{m}{n}{\rm i}\pi\bigr)}\left[\int_{\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\mp 1}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta}\frac{U_{m-1}(u_{+})}{U_{n-1}(u_{+})}\,{\rm d}\zeta-\int_{\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\mp 1}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta}\frac{U_{m-1}(u_{-})}{U_{n-1}(u_{-})}\,{\rm d}\zeta\right],

where
u_{-}
and
u_{+}
are the lifts to the incoming and outgoing branches of
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}
, respectively.

Since the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{n}
is defined by the identity
T_{n}(\cos(\phi))=\cos(n\phi)
, we introduce a new variable
\phi
with
u=\cos(\phi)
and
-\zeta=\cos(n\phi)
. On the
\phi
plane, which is an infinite branched cover of the
u
plane, we can lift
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}_{j}
to the path
\frac{j}{n}\pi+{\rm i}\mathbb{R}
. The incoming and outgoing branches lift to
\frac{j}{n}\pi-{\rm i}[0,\infty)
and
\frac{j}{n}\pi+{\rm i}[0,\infty)
, respectively.

We can now use
[
84
, identity (15.4.16)]
to write the integrand explicitly in terms of
\zeta

\displaystyle\frac{U_{m-1}(\cos(\phi))}{U_{n-1}(\cos(\phi))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(m\phi)}{\sin(\phi)}\frac{\sin(\phi)}{\sin(n\phi)}=\frac{\sin(m\phi)}{\sin(n\phi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{2n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{2n};\frac{3}{2};\sin(n\phi)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{2n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{2n};\frac{3}{2};1-\zeta^{2}\right).

Initially, we only know that this equation holds in the disk
|\phi|<\frac{\pi}{2n}
. However, observing that the left-hand side is meromorphic throughout the
\phi
plane, we can deduce by analytic continuation that the equation holds throughout the
\phi
plane.

Next, we simplify the integrand using identities (15.8.4) and (15.8.27)–(15.8.28) from
[
84
]
, which tell us that

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{2n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{2n};\frac{3}{2};1-\zeta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\hphantom{+}\frac{\pi}{\Gamma\bigl(1-\frac{m}{2n}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(1+\frac{m}{2n}\bigr)}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{2n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{2n};\frac{1}{2};\zeta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}-\frac{\pi\zeta}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{2n}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{2n}\bigr)}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{2n},1+\frac{m}{2n};\frac{3}{2};\zeta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad={}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)+{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}+\frac{1}{2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{3}{2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)

away from the line
\Re(\zeta)=0
and the rays
\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\zeta,1}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\pi}_{\zeta,-1}
.

Returning to the projected thimble integral

K^{(j)}_{m/n}(z)=-\frac{1}{2\sinh\bigl(\frac{m}{n}{\rm i}\pi\bigr)}\int_{\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\mp 1}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta}\left[\frac{U_{m-1}(u_{+})}{U_{n-1}(u_{+})}-\frac{U_{m-1}(u_{-})}{U_{n-1}(u_{-})}\right]{\rm d}\zeta,

we can see the integrand as the variation of the function

\frac{U_{m-1}(\cos(\phi))}{U_{n-1}(\cos(\phi))}=\frac{3m}{2n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)+\frac{m}{2n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{m}{n},1+\frac{m}{n};\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)

across the branch cut
\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\pm 1}
. When
j
is odd, only the second term will contribute to the jump, because the first term is regular at
\zeta=1
. Similarly, when
j
is even, only the first term will contribute to the jump. We can write the jump explicitly using identity (15.2.3) from
[

]
. For odd

, we have

K^{(j)}_{m/n}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,1}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta}\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right){\rm d}\zeta,

and for even
j
, we have

K^{(j)}_{m/n}(z)=\frac{3}{2}\int_{\mathcal{J}^{\theta}_{\zeta,-1}}{\rm e}^{-z\zeta}\left(-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right){\rm d}\zeta.

Comparing the expressions above with the expressions for

computed in Sections

, we notice that

K^{(j)}_{m/n}(z)=\begin{cases}\hphantom{-}\dfrac{\rm i}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,1}v_{1}&j\text{ odd},\\
-\dfrac{3{\rm i}}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,-1}v_{-1}&j\text{ even}.\end{cases}

5.2.9
A flavor of resurgence: the Stokes phenomenon in the position domain

So far, we have treated the Airy–Lucas functions as separate elements of a frame of solutions. However, Écalle’s theory of
resurgence
reveals that these solutions are deeply intertwined. In fact, you can recover the whole frame from any one of its elements by studying the analytic continuation of the corresponding function on the position domain.

As an example, consider the Borel regular solution
{\rm e}^{-z}V_{1}
of the modified Bessel equation (
5.4
), which we found by solving an integral equation in Section
5.2.3
and by evaluating a thimble integral in Section
5.2.8
. This solution arises as the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,1}v_{1}
of the function

v_{1}=\left(-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right),

on the position domain, with
\zeta=1+\zeta_{1}
as before. The function
v_{1}
is singular at
\zeta=1
, but the hypergeometric factor
{}_{2}F_{1}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\bigr)
is analytic at that point, so
v_{1}
has the typical form of a
singularity
in the formalism of resurgent functions
[
93
, Section 2]
. We can see
{{}_{2}F_{1}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\bigr)}
as a new holomorphic function, analytically continued from a germ at
\zeta=1
. This new function has a branch cut singularity at
\zeta=-1
, and we can find its jump across the branch cut
\zeta\in(-\infty,-1]
using
[
84
, equation (15.2.3)]
. Along the branch cut, we have

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}+{\rm i}\varepsilon\right)-{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}-{\rm i}\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n}\bigr)}\left(-\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{m}{n},-\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2{\rm i}\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)\left(-\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{m}{n},-\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)\left(\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{m}{n},-\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)\left(\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}\left(-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)^{1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)^{1/2}2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)v_{-1}.

Multiplying both sides by
(-\zeta_{1}/2)^{-1/2}
reveals a formula for the jump of
v_{1}
across the branch cut. For any point
p
on the branch cut,

v_{1}(p_{-})-v_{1}(p_{+})=2\cos\bigl(\frac{m}{n}\pi\bigr)v_{-1}(p),

(5.8)

where
v_{1}(p_{-})
and
v_{1}(p_{+})
respectively denote the evaluation of
v_{1}(p)
by analytic continuation from the regions
\Im(\zeta)<0
and
\Im(\zeta)>0
. The appearance of the function
v_{-1}
corresponding to the other Borel regular solution
{\rm e}^{z}V_{-1}
is an example of the
resurgence
phenomenon
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
.

Reviews like
[
3
,
35
,
79
]
discuss many details and applications of resurgence. Berry and Howls also discuss the optimal truncation of thimble integrals from a resurgence point of view
[
13
]
. We would like to emphasize one application: through resurgence, we can use calculations in the position domain to understand the Stokes phenomenon in the frequency domain. This lets us skip the last and often trickiest step of Borel summation: using the Laplace transform to map the functions under consideration back into the frequency domain. The
formalism of singularities
and the
alien calculus
are two of the tools that make this possible.

To compute the Stokes constants for the Airy–Lucas functions, we first repeat the calculation above for

v_{-1}=\left(\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right).

We find that

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}+i\varepsilon\right)-{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}-i\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n}\bigr)}\left(\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{m}{n},-\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2{\rm i}\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)\left(\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{m}{n},-\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=-2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)\left(-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{m}{n},-\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=-2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)\left(-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)^{1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{n},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{n};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(\frac{\zeta_{-1}}{2}\right)^{1/2}(-2)\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)v_{1}.

Multiplying both sides by
(\zeta_{-1}/2)^{-1/2}
gives the jump formula

v_{-1}(p_{+})-v_{-1}(p_{-})=-2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right)v_{1}(p)

(5.9)

for any point
p
on the branch cut
\zeta\in[1,\infty)
, where again
v_{-1}(p_{+})
and
v_{-1}(p_{-})
denote evaluation by analytic continuation from
\Im(\zeta)>0
and
\Im(\zeta)<0
.

Equations (
5.8
) and (
5.9
) are a manifestation of the Stokes phenomenon in the position domain. When we vary the direction of integration for the Laplace transform, we expect discontinuities at
\mathcal{L}^{\pi}_{\zeta,1}
and
\mathcal{L}^{0}_{\zeta,-1}
, because each of the contours
\mathcal{J}^{\pi}_{\zeta,1}
and
\mathcal{J}^{0}_{\zeta,-1}
hits a singularity other than its starting point. The Stokes constant for each critical direction relates the results of the Laplace transforms on either side. In our example,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,1}^{\pi+\varepsilon}v_{1}-\hphantom{{}_{-}}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,1}^{\pi-\varepsilon}v_{1}=\hphantom{-}2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right){\rm e}^{-z}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,-1}^{\pi}v_{-1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,-1}^{\varepsilon}v_{-1}-\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,-1}^{-\varepsilon}v_{-1}=-2\cos\left(\frac{m}{n}\pi\right){\rm e}^{z}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,1}v_{1},

so the Stokes constants for the directions
\pi
and
0
are
2\cos\bigl(\frac{m}{n}\pi\bigr)
and
-2\cos\bigl(\frac{m}{n}\pi\bigr)
, respectively.

Because the Airy–Lucas functions can always be expressed as thimble integrals, it may seem surprising that the Stokes constants are not always integers. For thimble integrals given by Morse functions, the Stokes constants are the intersection numbers of
dual pairs of thimbles
, according to Picard–Lefschetz formula (see
[
86
, Section 5]
and
[
5
, Chapter 1]
). However, the functions
T_{n}(u)
defining the Airy–Lucas thimble integrals (
5.3
) are only Morse in the Airy case
n=3
and the degenerate limit
n\to\infty
that we will discuss in Section
5.3.2
. In the other cases, we speculate that the Stokes constants are non-integer because there are multiple thimbles over each critical value.

5.3
Modified Bessel

Our analysis of the Airy–Lucas functions boiled down to an analysis of equation (
5.4
): the modified Bessel equation with a rational parameter
\mu=\frac{m}{n}
. We now do a general analysis of the modified Bessel equation, allowing the parameter
\mu
to be any complex number

\left[z^{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{2}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\bigl[\mu^{2}+z^{2}\bigr]\right]\Phi=0.

(5.10)

The ODE reasoning of Sections

–

works basically the same in this more general setting, so we will only briefly state the analogous results.

Asymptotic analysis.

Equation (

) has a basis of formal solutions
{\rm e}^{-z}z^{-1/2}\tilde{W}_{1}
and
{\rm e}^{z}z^{-1/2}\tilde{W}_{-1}

given by

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{\mu,1}=1-\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\mu\bigr)\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\mu\bigr)}{2\cdot 1!}z^{-1}+\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\mu\bigr)_{2}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\mu\bigr)_{2}}{2^{2}\cdot 2!}z^{-2}-\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\mu\bigr)_{3}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\mu\bigr)_{3}}{2^{3}\cdot 3!}z^{-3}+\cdots,

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{\mu,-1}=1+\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\mu\bigr)\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\mu\bigr)}{2\cdot 1!}z^{-1}+\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\mu\bigr)_{2}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\mu\bigr)_{2}}{2^{2}\cdot 2!}z^{-2}+\frac{\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\mu\bigr)_{3}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\mu\bigr)_{3}}{2^{3}\cdot 3!}z^{-3}+\cdots.

Frame of analytic solutions.

Equation (
5.10
) also has a basis of analytic solutions
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,1}v_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,-1}v_{-1}
, which are the Laplace transforms of the functions

\displaystyle v_{1}=-{\rm i}\sqrt{2}\zeta_{1}^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu,\frac{1}{2}+\mu;\frac{1}{2};-\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{1}\right),

\displaystyle v_{-1}=\sqrt{2}\zeta_{-1}^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu,\frac{1}{2}+\mu;\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{-1}\right)

along the directions
\theta\neq\pi
and
\theta\neq 0
, respectively.

Borel regularity.

The Borel transforms
\mathcal{B}_{\zeta}\bigl[z^{-1/2}{\rm e}^{-\alpha z}\tilde{W}_{\pm 1}\bigr]
sum to scalar multiples of
v_{\pm 1}
, implying through Lemma
3.19
that the solutions
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}_{\zeta,\pm 1}v_{\pm 1}
are Borel regular.

On the other hand, the thimble projection reasoning of Section
5.2.8
has to be generalized, as we will discuss in Section
5.3.1
below.

5.5
Third-degree thimble integrals

In this subsection, we consider the thimble integral

I(z)=\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-zg}\,{\rm d}u

defined by a general third-degree polynomial map
g\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
. By definition, the Lefschetz thimble
\mathcal{C}
is a connected component of the preimage of a critical value of
g
. After taking various symmetries into account, we will find that there are essentially only two cases, distinguished by whether
g
has two distinct critical points or a single degenerate one.

5.5.1
Symmetries of the integral

First, we step back and consider the completely general thimble integral

J(z)=\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-zf}\nu

defined by an arbitrary holomorphic map
f\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
and an arbitrary
1
-form
\nu
on a complex manifold
X
. The affine group of
\mathbb{C}
acts on
f
by post-composition, and this action affects
J
in a simple way. The affine group is generated by two kinds of transformations, which we consider separately.

Translations.

For any
c\in\mathbb{C}
, the translation action
f\mapsto f+c
on maps corresponds to the action

J(z)\mapsto{\rm e}^{-cz}J(z)

on integrals. This is because

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-z(f+c)}\nu

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-cz}\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-zf}\nu={\rm e}^{-cz}J(z).

Scaling-rotations.

For any
r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the scaling-rotation action
f\mapsto rf
corresponds to the action

J(z)\mapsto J(rz)

on integrals. This is because

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-z(rf)}\nu

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-(rz)f}\nu=J(rz).

Next, we specialize to the case where
X
is the complex plane and
\nu={\rm d}u
, where
u
is the standard coordinate. The affine group of
\mathbb{C}
now acts on
f
by pre-composition as well. To make sure that
\mathcal{C}
remains a Lefschetz thimble, we act on it simultaneously by the inverse of whatever we precompose with
f
.

Translations.

For any
c\in\mathbb{C}
, pulling
f
back along the translation
\mathsf{T}_{c}^{*}u=u-c
has no effect on the integral. This is because
{\rm d}u
is translation-invariant.

Scaling-rotations.

For any
s\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the scaling-rotation action
\mathsf{M}_{s}^{*}u=s^{-1}u
corresponds to the action

J(z)\mapsto sJ(z)

on integrals. This is because

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{M}_{1/s}\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-z\mathsf{M}_{s}^{*}f}\,{\rm d}u=\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-zf}\mathsf{M}_{1/s}^{*}\,{\rm d}u=\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\rm e}^{-zf}s\,{\rm d}u=sJ(z).

A.2
Comparison with other treatments of the Airy equation

A.2.1
Other conventions for the Borel transform

Physicists often use a different version of the Borel transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{{\rm phys}}\colon\ \mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket\to\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket,\qquad z^{-n}\mapsto\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}.

This version avoids sending
1
to the convolution unit
\delta
, at the cost of no longer mapping multiplication to convolution or inverting the formal Laplace transform. It is related to the mathematician’s Borel transform by the identity
\mathcal{B}_{\rm phys}(f)=\mathcal{B}\bigl(z^{-1}f\bigr)
.

For problems involving a small parameter
\hbar
rather than a large parameter
z
, physicists also define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{{\rm phys}}\colon\ \mathbb{C}\llbracket\hbar\rrbracket\to\mathbb{C}\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket,\qquad\hbar^{n}\mapsto\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}.

From a combinatorial perspective, this is just the transformation that sends an ordinary generating function to the corresponding exponential generating function.

In
[
75
]
, Mariño studies the Airy functions as an example of resurgent functions. He starts with the formal trans-monomial solutions of the Airy equation

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{Ai}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}x^{-1/4}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}}\tilde{W}_{1}\bigl(x^{-3/2}\bigr),\qquad\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{Bi}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}x^{-1/4}{\rm e}^{\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}}\tilde{W}_{2}\bigl(x^{-3/2}\bigr),

where

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{1,2}(\hbar)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\mp\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(n+\frac{5}{6}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(n+\frac{1}{6}\bigr)}{n!}\hbar^{n}.

By applying the two versions
\mathcal{B}_{{\rm phys}}
and
\mathcal{B}
of the Borel transform, we get

\displaystyle w_{1,2}(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{B}_{{\rm phys}}\bigl(\tilde{W}_{1,2}\bigr)(\zeta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\mp\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(n+\frac{5}{6}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(n+\frac{1}{6}\bigr)}{n!}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{6},\frac{5}{6};1;\mp\frac{3}{4}\zeta\right)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\bigl(\tilde{W}_{1,2}\bigr)(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\mp\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(n+\frac{5}{6}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(n+\frac{1}{6}\bigr)}{n!}\frac{\zeta^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\mp\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n+1}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(n+1+\frac{5}{6}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(n+1+\frac{1}{6}\bigr)}{(n+1)!}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta\mp\frac{3}{4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\mp\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n}\frac{\Gamma(n+\frac{11}{6})\Gamma\bigl(n+\frac{7}{6}\bigr)}{\Gamma(n+2)}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta\mp\frac{5}{48}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{7}{6},\frac{11}{6};2;\mp\frac{3}{4}\zeta\right).

Comparing the two solutions, we notice that up to the factor of
\delta
,

\mathcal{B}\bigl(\tilde{W}_{1,2}\bigr)(\zeta)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta=\mp\frac{5}{48}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{7}{6},\frac{11}{6};2;\mp\frac{3}{4}\zeta\right)=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\zeta}\mathcal{B}_{{\rm phys}}\bigl(\tilde{W}_{1,2}\bigr)(\zeta).

(A.5)

More generally, if
\tilde{\Phi}(z)\in z^{-1}\mathbb{C}\big\llbracket z^{-1}\big\rrbracket
, i.e., it has no constant term, then

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\zeta}\circ\mathcal{B}_{{\rm phys}}\tilde{\Phi}=\mathcal{B}\tilde{\Phi}
.
In particular,
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\zeta}\circ\mathcal{B}_{{\rm phys}}\bigl[z^{-1/2}\tilde{W}_{1}\bigr]\left(\frac{2}{3}\zeta\right)=v_{1}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\phi_{+}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{1}{x}s^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{6},\frac{5}{6};\frac{1}{2};s\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{-,B}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\phi_{-}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{1}{x}(1-s)^{-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{6},\frac{5}{6};\frac{1}{2};1-s\right),

where
s=t/2+1/2
. We found the same hypergeometric functions in the position domain while solving the Airy equation

\big[\big(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}w}\big)^{2}-w\big]f(w)=0

in Appendix
A.1
. Although the two equations look closely related, and they are equivalent under the change of coordinates
w=x\eta^{2/3}
, they differ in an important way. The Borel transform of
\psi
is computed with respect to
\frac{2}{3}\eta x^{3/2}
, the conjugate variable of
t
, while the Borel transform of
f(w)
is computed with respect to
w
. We need to find a different change of coordinates to explain why the Borel transforms of
\psi(x,\eta)
and
f(w)
are given by the same hypergeometric function.

To do this, first notice that if
\eta
and
y
are conjugate variables under Borel transform, meaning

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\eta^{-n-1}\overset{\mathcal{B}}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}y^{n}

then
t=\frac{3}{2}yx^{-3/2}
is the conjugate variable of
q=\frac{2}{3}\eta x^{3/2}
up to correction by a factor of
\frac{3}{2}x^{-3/2}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}q^{-n-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{-3/2(n+1)}\left(\frac{2}{3}\eta\right)^{-n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{\mathcal{B}}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}x^{-3/2(n+1)}}{n!}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{n+1}y^{n}=\frac{3}{2}x^{-3/2}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}t^{n}.

In addition,
\psi_{B,\pm}(x,y)=\frac{1}{x}\phi_{\pm}(t)
, therefore we expect that
\psi(x,\eta)=x^{1/2}\Phi(q)
. Assume that
\psi(x,y)
is a solution of (
A.6
). Then
\Phi(q)
satisfies

\left[\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{2}+x^{-1}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}-\frac{1}{4}x^{-2}-\eta^{2}x\right]\Phi(q)=0,

(A.7)

because

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{2}-\eta^{2}x\right]\psi(x,\eta)=0,\qquad\left[\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{2}-\eta^{2}x\right]x^{1/2}\Phi(q)=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\left[\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\Phi+x^{1/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\Phi\right]-\eta^{2}x^{3/2}\Phi=0,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}x^{-3/2}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\Phi+x^{1/2}\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{2}\Phi-\eta^{2}x^{3/2}\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\left[x^{1/2}\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{2}+x^{-1/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}-\frac{1}{4}x^{-3/2}-\eta^{2}x^{3/2}\right]\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{2}+x^{-1}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}-\frac{1}{4}x^{-2}-\eta^{2}x\right]\Phi=0.

Now, rewrite (
A.7
) in the coordinates
q=\frac{2}{3}\eta x^{3/2}
,

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{2}+x^{-1}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}-\frac{1}{4}x^{-2}-\eta^{2}x\right]\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\left[\eta^{2}x\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\eta x^{-1/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}+x^{-1}\cdot\eta x^{1/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}-\frac{1}{4}x^{-2}-\eta^{2}x\right]\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\left[\eta^{2}x\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}\right)^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\eta x^{-1/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}-\frac{1}{4}x^{-2}-\eta^{2}x\right]\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}\right)^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\eta^{-1}x^{-3/2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}-\frac{1}{4}\eta^{-2}x^{-3}-1\right]\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}\right)^{2}+q^{-1}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}q}-\frac{1}{9}q^{-2}-1\right]\Phi=0.

This shows that
\Phi(q)
satisfies the transformed Airy equation (
A.2
).

Remark A.1
.

The new coordinate
q=\frac{2}{3}\eta x^{3/2}
is carefully chosen. Recall that the WKB ansatz for a Schrödinger equation is

\psi(x,\eta)=\exp\left(\int_{x_{0}}^{x}S(\eta,x^{\prime})\,{\rm d}x^{\prime}\right),

with
S(\eta,x)=\sum_{k\geq-1}S_{k}(x)\eta^{-k}
. For the Airy-type Schrödinger equation, we have
S_{-1}^{2}=x
, so up to a choice of sign for the square root,

q=\frac{2}{3}\eta x^{3/2}=\eta\int_{0}^{x}(x^{\prime})^{1/2}\,{\rm d}x^{\prime}=\eta\int_{0}^{x}S_{-1}(x^{\prime})\,{\rm d}x^{\prime}.

We expect that the change of coordinates
q=\eta\int_{0}^{x}S_{-1}(x^{\prime})\,{\rm d}x^{\prime}
would explain the analogies between the Borel transform of the WKB solution of a Schrödinger equation and the Borel transform of the associated ODE.

Appendix B
Order shifting for integro-differential equations

For a differential equation with a regular singularity at
\zeta=0
, the Frobenius method typically gives a convergent power series solution in
\zeta^{\rho}\mathbb{C}\{\zeta\}
for some
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, which sums to a germ in
\zeta^{\rho}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=0}
. When
\rho
is a non-integer greater than
-1
, we can do a nice trick in this space of germs: we can pass back and forth between differential and integral equations without worrying about boundary conditions.

Proposition B.1
.

Consider a germ
\phi\in\zeta^{\rho}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=0}
. When
\rho
is a non-integer greater than
-1
,

\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)^{k}\circ h_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\partial^{-k}_{\zeta,0}\circ h_{-k}\right]\phi=0,

if and only if

\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)^{k-1}\circ h_{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{m}\partial^{-k-1}_{\zeta,0}\circ h_{-k}\right]\phi=0,

assuming that the coefficients
h_{n},\dots,h_{-m}
are in
\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=0}
.

Proof.

The reverse implication holds without any special condition on
\phi
, because
\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\partial^{-1}_{\zeta,0}
acts as the identity on functions which are locally integrable at
\zeta=0
.

To prove the forward implication, rewrite the first equation in the statement as

\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)^{k-1}\circ h_{k}\right]\psi=-\left[h_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\partial^{-k}_{\zeta,0}\circ h_{-k}\right]\psi.

(B.1)

The germ

f:=\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)^{k-1}\circ h_{k}\right]\psi

belongs to
\zeta^{\rho-(n-1)}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=0}
. This tells us, in particular, that
f
has a shifted Taylor expansion with no constant term. Looking at the right-hand side of equation (
B.1
), we can also see that
\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}f
belongs to
\zeta^{\rho}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=0}
. Combining these observations, we can deduce that
f
belongs to
\zeta^{\rho+1}\mathcal{O}_{\zeta=0}
. Since
\rho>-1
, that means
f
vanishes at
\zeta=0
.

Integrating both sides of equation (
B.1
), we get

\partial^{-1}_{\zeta,0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}f=-\left[\partial^{-1}_{\zeta,0}\circ h_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\partial^{-k-1}_{\zeta,0}\circ h_{-k}\right]\phi.

Since
\partial^{-1}_{\zeta,0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}
acts as the identity on functions that vanish at
\zeta=0
, this simplifies to

f=-\left[\sum_{k=0}^{m}\partial^{-k-1}_{\zeta,0}\circ h_{-k}\right]\phi,

which rearranges to the second equation in the statement.
∎
