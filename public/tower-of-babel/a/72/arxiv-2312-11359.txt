Combining Game Design and Data Visualization to Inform Plastics Policy: Fostering Collaboration between Science, Decision-Makers, and Artificial Intelligence
A Samuel Pottinger, Nivedita Biyani, Roland Geyer, Douglas J McCauley, Magali de Bruyn, Molly R Morse, Neil Nathan, Kevin Koy, Ciera Martinez

Abstract




Abstract
: This multi-disciplinary case study details how a
public web application combines information and game design to visualize
effects of user-defined policies intended to reduce plastic waste.
Contextualizing this open source software within a broader lineage of
digital media research, this user experience exploration outlines
potential directions for facilitating conversation between artificial
intelligence, scientists, and decision makers during an iterative policy
building process. Furthermore, this system dissection reveals how this
interactive science effort considers the practicalities of a treaty’s
shifting priorities and proposals in its designs. Specifically, this
historically situated investigation of the tool’s approach highlights
options for centering human decision making where artificial
intelligence helps reason about interventions but does not prescribe
them. Finally, analysis summarizes this application’s specific game
design-inspired mechanics and their efforts to: enable users’ agency to
explore solution possibilities freely, invite deep engagement with
scientific findings, and simultaneously serve multiple audiences with
divergent objectives and expertise.

1
Introduction

In his influential “Humane Representation of Thought”, Bret Victor
(2014) explores digital media’s unique ability to “respond” to the
user, detailing how interactivity enables new ways of reasoning to
unlock thoughts we “couldn’t think” before. This paper explores one
such visualization application of these powerful design techniques: the
open source

global-plastics-tool.org
.
Constructed by this article’s cross-disciplinary research team, this
public web application seeks to extend the reach of mathematical
modeling by offering iterative simulation of policy scenario effects,
attempting to improve usability of scientific insights for negotiators
and stakeholders engaging in the development of a “once-in-a-lifetime”
global treaty: the United Nation’s “International Legally Binding
Instrument on Plastic Pollution, Including in the Marine Environment”
(Legaspi, 2023; UNEP, 2022, 2023b). However, during this decision
support tool’s journey into global negotiation, its designers encounter
three central challenges:

•

Providing
user agency
to explore different policy scenarios
freely.

•

Inviting diverse stakeholders to holistically consider model outputs
through sophisticated
multi-variate displays
.

•

Serving
divergent audiences
of varying prior levels of domain
knowledge.

Detailing the design of this decision support tool and contextualizing
its place within a broader journey in science communication, this paper
distills how interactive visualization may address those policy
engagement hurdles:

•

Hidden Hayashida Tutorials
: A game design-inspired approach
which embeds narrative in structure and aims to orient users to a
sophisticated interface and complex topic while avoiding the
“ludonarrative dissonance” that may arise from a more traditional
“tutorial” feature (Brown, 2015a; Hocking, 2007; JM8, 2023).

•

Connected Zelda Valleys
: Applying “landmark”
visualizations, this design seeks to orient the user navigationally
while also inviting explorations of data or results across multiple
views which limit the moment to moment complexity of the interface
(Brown, 2023a, 2023b; Brown, n.d.; Portnow, 2022).

•

Data Rumors
: Unobtrusive design gestures towards additional
optional content which try to allow users to “read at multiple
levels” of detail, promising to support those seeking both in-depth
knowledge and high level takeaways without interrupting the regular
interaction flow through the broader tool (Brown, 2017; Takahashi and
Nelson, 2018; Victor, 2011).

•

Environmental Explanations
: Using the structure of the
visualization to reflect the underlying AI, an activation of
information architecture attempting to afford model mechanics
explanations (Brown, 2020; Carson, 2000; Victor, 2011).

Furthermore, this cross-disciplinary case study more broadly discusses
the intersection between information and game design that underpins
these patterns.

1.1
Background

The recently released

global-plastics-tool.org

participates in a broader intellectual conversation on the unique
dialectic opportunities of digital media.

Explorable explanations
: Building on interactive communications
research reaching back to 1960’s PLATO (Bitzer et al., 1961), Bret
Victor (2011) draws attention to the possibilities of “explorable
explanations” for “active” reading. This user experience theory
observes that static media cannot invite response and, thus, leave “the
reader’s line of thought” both “internal and invisible” (Victor,
2011). In contrast, Victor (2011) observes that new interactive
technologies allow readers to “as[k] questions, conside[r]
alternatives, questio[n] assumptions, and even questio[n] the
trustworthiness of the author.” Since proposing these profound
concepts, these design ideas see a home in education, engineering, and
industry (Bostock, 2023a; Brusilovsky, 1994; Case et al., 2021; Hayes,
2023).

Scientific communications
: However, years after their
formulation, growth opportunities still remain for using these
“explorable explanations” in science communication (Victor, 2011).
Discussing interactivity for scientific publications, Pierre Dragicevic
et al. (2019) recently write that there remain “very few concrete
examples” that use these important ideas. Continuing to speak in
unresponsive media of papers and slides, unidirectional artifacts convey
scientific findings but cannot listen to stakeholders’ perspectives or
“respond” to their ideas (Victor, 2014). Digital but trapped in
virtual pieces of paper unable to “react” to users, this way of
speaking forces audiences to “blindly trust” and miss out on “the
deep understanding that comes from dialogue and exploration” (Victor,
2011).

Policy and science
: With this historic communication challenge
in mind, the open source global-plastics-tool.org project considers how
to interactively engage with policy makers in finding science-informed
solutions to pollution, building an interface to artificial intelligence
(AI) which predicts future plastics volumes and flows under various
scenarios. The media of communication matters in these policy efforts
because, as Jan Nolin (1999) remarks in her analysis of the European
Union’s engagement in the Kyoto Proposal, “scientific work is usually
slow while the pace of policy is erratic.” To understand why
legislation like the global plastics treaty sits within this fluid
state, consider the “processual” and “integrative” approaches in
negotiation theory: learning, information exchange, and collaboration
happen across multiple “phases” (Alfredson and Cungu, 2008).
Therefore, the information needs of stakeholders naturally adjust as
perspective and context evolve or, as Nolin (1999) writes, “scientific
input to be effective must thus be timed for readiness when a new phase
of policy shifts in….” With these demands in mind, communication
of scientific findings may need interaction’s ability to “react” and
keep pace with shifting dialogue (Victor, 2012).

1.2
Related work

Though Dragicevic et al. (2019) observe that relatively few projects
realize the power of interactivity in scientific communication, some
recent tools try to enable this timely iterative information delivery to
support policy design through simulation. In environment and
sustainability, notable examples include:

•

Driving the Future
: Simulation of automotive markets under
different incentive, tax, and policy scenarios (Keith et al., 2017).

•

En-ROADS
: Interactive simulation for policy impact on
greenhouse gas emissions and global warming (Jean et al., 2018;
Rooney-Varga et al., 2020).

•

Protect High Seas
: Tool to identify potential marine
protected area candidates using configurable conservation plans
(Visalli et al., 2020).

•

MSP Challenge
: A multi-user environment for collaborative
marine spatial planning with simulation of policy effects (Abspoel et
al., 2021).

Enabling dialectic design of science-informed solutions beyond what a
print out makes possible, all of these digital environments turn static
information into engaging software which allow users to bi-directionally
converse with data, creating “interactive papers” or “explorable
explanations” that still remain the minority in science (Dragicevic et
al., 2019).

2
Challenges

This study builds on prior work’s foundation by further constructing
vocabulary to identify and address policy tool challenges. Specifically,
we explore hurdles for supporting treaty processes through the open
source
global-plastics-tool.org

which builds a user interface around machine learning-based plastic
modeling for international negotiation.

2.1
User agency

Policy makers need “agency” to explore questions from their own
expertise and in their own timeline, two crucial aspects not well
supported by traditional scientific communication.

Requirement for collaboration
: Providing suggestions for
engaging with policy makers, the United Kingdom’s Chris Tyler
(2013)

1

Former Director of the Parliamentary Office of Science
and Techology.
warns that scientists “often consider themselves as
the ‘experts’ who engage with policy makers” but they should not assume
“that [they] are the only expert in the room.” Similarly, Nolin’s
(1999) experiences in the European Union echo these benefits of
bidirectional dialogue, highlighting the value of integrating science
and policy in advancing solutions. With this need for mutual
collaboration in mind, the medium of scientific communication may limit
reader agency as static documents “displa[y] the author’s argument,
and nothing else” (Victor, 2011). In this structural challenge,
instruction benefits users and orients them to scientific findings but
also risks “lecturing at” decision-makers which clashes with their
need to provide input and inquiry via their perspective and knowledge.

Requirement for timeliness
: Both Nolin (1999) and Tyler (2013)
note that policy “decisions usually need to be made pretty quickly”
with strong constraints on timeline for additional research. Again, this
reflects an inherent limitation of static media on audience agency where
readers can “form questions, but can’t answer them” (Victor, 2011). To
this end, Victor (2011) questions how digital alternatives may respond
to inquiry in the moment itself, addressing concerns otherwise only
“answered by a phone call or heavy research.”

Together, these needs for timely collaboration challenge this scientific
tool to consider how interactivity can not only inform policy makers but
work with them to enable their own agency to answer questions freely in
the moment as new information becomes necessary.

2.2
High dimensional outputs

Visualization of highly multi-variate data encounter “visual perception
and cognition … limits on the number of variables that [can
be] encode[d] in a single graph” where even established patterns
from information design for addressing high dimensional complexity can
sometimes require reader’s “extensive study and practice” to use (Few,
2019). Worryingly, the simulation of policy impacts requires exploring
effects across many variables and thus asks tool design to support
comprehensive evaluation without becoming overwhelming. This challenge
of taming complexity asks the project how it may adapt prior work using
interactivity to teach sophisticated interfaces, maintaining the
richness of the data without becoming complicated (Berlow, 2010;
Pottinger and Zarpellon, 2023).

2.3
Levels of speaking

The policy context of the tool’s usage demands it walk a tightrope in
supporting multiple levels of reading:

•

On one hand, recommendations to the scientific community on policy
engagement often emphasize the need for brief summaries in plain
language (Hetherington and Phillips, 2020).

•

Meanwhile, information designers warn that traditional media like
slides cause oversimplification and result in poor decision making
(Tufte, 2006).

This web application steps into this tension: it must serve audiences of
diverse prior experience to support both the high level overviews
recommended to scientists in policy engagement while simultaneously
inviting deeper understanding of the details needed for effective
decision making.

3
Design

This plastics policy tool looks to video games for answers to these
three central issues, building up a language of solutions to address
this vocabulary of challenges.

3.1
Role of game design

To solve for these three concerns, this policy tool observes that prior
work exploring interactivity looks to the overlap between information
and game design for inspiration:

•

Elijah Meeks (2018) provides an early “aspirational guide” which, in
particular, considers the analogies offered by the overall structure
of games to data visualization.

•

A Samuel Pottinger (2022) considers the opportunity for incorporating
user expertise in finding solutions in a form of “participatory”
design.

•

A Samuel Pottinger and Giulia Zarpellon (2023) explore specific
tutorial techniques pioneered by games.

Therefore, we also turn to game design for advice in tackling these
aforementioned challenges, further understanding patterns like
Hayashida-style tutorials, connected levels, open worlds, environmental
storytelling, and side-quest rumors for data visualization.

3.2
Design for user agency

Interactive science products often require “sophistication to
navigate” and learning new concepts within a novel interface (Pottinger
and Zarpellon, 2023). Of course, video games must also orient new
players quickly and, borrowing from that discipline, prior
visualizations feature “Hayashida-style” tutorials, leveraging a
powerful pedagogical technique successfully used in Nintendo’s work to
similarly teach users about a new technical tool (Nutt and Hayashida,
2012; Pottinger and Zarpellon, 2023). This simple process includes four
steps (Brown, 2015b):

1.

Introduction
: Show the mechanic to the user.

2.

Development
: Give players a simplified task to try the
action.

3.

Twist
: Highlight something unexpected about the skill.

4.

Conclusion
: Challenge users to demonstrate mastery of the
lesson.

That said, tools like Pyafscgap using this structure often lead through
written tutorials and example analyses where they highlight certain
prototypical observations (Pottinger and Zarpellon, 2023). This explicit
guiding of the user may detract from a sense of freedom or make the tool
appear non-objective, echoing earlier concerns about user agency and
asking if simulations can orient readers without “lecturing at” them.

This “ludonarrative dissonance” problem also appears in games which,
as detailed by Clint Hocking (2007) and refined by JM8 (2023), arises
when a mechanic used by a player feels out of place with the
experience’s message or narrative. In this case, a written introductory
tutorial may contrast with the intended message of providing user agency
by:

•

Making users feel like novices even though the tool invites them to
bring their expertise to designing a solution.

•

Showing example insights, biasing self-directed exploration.

•

Forcing attention to specific policy aspects in a way that threatens a
feeling of the tool’s scientific neutrality.

Believing users should discover policy pros and cons instead of having
opinions prescribed, the tool therefore looks to games for possible
solutions. To that end, analysis of Nintendo’s 2017 action-adventure
“The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild” by Cam Shea (2021) offers a
possible “open world” remedy: start journeys in beginning areas with
reduced complexity not identified as a tutorial but which still preamble
the broader experience.

Figure 1:
Hierarchy pulls the user through a Hayashida sequence.

With Zelda in mind, this interactive tool similarly “gates” details
behind exposure to the concepts required for the larger experience.
Visual hierarchy constructs a simplified “starting region” to
introduce mechanics that blossom in the “full” world in the details
tab, reaching towards what designer Mark Brown (2015a) calls making the
tutorial an “invisible” feature.

3.3
Design for multi-variate
evaluation

The subtle Hayashida sequence considers how to design policies but
evaluating their effects can span up to 90 views in the details tab. To
balance rich functionality with simplicity, the software’s design learns
from video games’ separation of play into short term “primary loops”
of moment to moment actions and “secondary loops” of longer term
objectives (Brown, n.d.).

Consider the loops of two genres adept at managing large amounts of
content:

•

Games with
connected stage designs
like in Rare’s
“Banjo-Tooie” allow actions in one area to impact another even as it
still separates content into discrete levels. This creates a deep
world in the secondary loop while building a primary loop that
naturally narrows the moment to moment available subset of that
complexity (Brown, 2023b).

•

Designer James Portnow (2022) observes that
open world games

advertise content through “landmarks” which orient players without
explicit instruction. As analyzed by Brown (2023a), Zelda BoW achieves
this by peeking tall secondary loop landmarks over the world’s hilly
terrain which hide primary loop landmarks in distant areas.

The tool operates in a way similar to a valley seen in Zelda.

Figure 2:
The details tab use “valleys” to structure primary and
secondary loops.

Though users can only take a limited number of actions at any time
related to their current view in the primary loop, the tool keeps
secondary loop landmarks (like other lifecycle stages or regions)
visible “in the distance” through small previews opening into those
other spaces. These sight-lines try to encourage exploration and pull
users “over the hill” into a new valley. Furthermore, like
Banjo-Tooie, changes in one area reflect in others, creating the
potential for the user to traverse a vast possibility space without
having to grapple with it all at once. This attempts to construct a
“vista” of secondary loop elements alongside a smaller dynamic set of
primary loop opportunities (Majmudar, 2020).

3.4
Design for levels of
speaking

Even if hidden tutorials and manipulated sightlines to landmarks
introduce the visualization tool’s mechanics and orient users in a
complex multi-dimensional data space, this sophisticated software still
serves various audiences with different objectives or, as Victor (2011)
writes, explorable explanations should consider how to empower users to
“read at multiple levels” of depth. Those knowledgeable of plastics
concepts or only seeking high level takeaways may engage with the tool
superficially. Meanwhile, others may “read closely” to learn
unfamiliar terminology or consider expert details. If design must
support both altitudes of exploration simultaneously, how might video
game design help manage these parallel ways of speaking?

To build these multiple simultaneous levels of engagement, producer Rob
Nelson observes that a unique “ability that games have and other
mediums don’t [is] optional, additional narrative that you can
choose to engage with or skip over” (Takahashi and Nelson, 2018). In
games:

•

Don Carson’s
environmental storytelling
unobtrusively layers
content into “setting” like graffiti or signage that the player
encounters “along their journey” if they are paying close attention,
allowing the user to naturally choose their preferred depth of
engagement (Brown, 2020; Carson, 2000).

•

Rumors
which are subtle mentions of “side-quests” afford
unobtrusive opt-in: invitations naturally missed by users who do not
want that content or are busy rushing to another objective (Brown,
2017).

These designs aim to support a deep path for those moving slowly and
another streamlined route for those moving quickly in which optional
elements blend into the background. Indeed, the tool’s interface
prepares a similar two track path by scattering tooltips next to
keywords that “experienced” users would recognize.

Figure 3:
A tooltip describes a technical phrase and beacons deeper.

Low within the visual hierarchy, those in a rush can easily skip these
“rumors” even as they still direct close readers (Brown, 2017).

4
Implementation

Supporting these nuanced interaction-heavy mechanics from game design
requires careful front-end software engineering, influencing the
architecture of the code sitting around the software’s underlying AI.
Therefore, to support further practical consideration of these potential
approaches, this multi-disciplinary study next examines implementation
strategies.

Note that this project targets the web browser for improved portability
and low friction access (AWS, n.d.).

4.1
Open source

The tool itself does not use a prebuilt user interface framework in
order to support some specific interaction patterns as discussed in the
architecture section below. That said, major open source technologies
may still help achieve these designs:

•

Visualization library D3.js for custom crafting of visual elements
required to build landmarks and exert careful control over visual
hierarchy (Bostock, 2023b).

•

Popper and Tippy.js to support tooltips and rumors (Atomiks, 2017;
Popper, 2021).

•

Tabby to structure the tabbed interface required by the Hayashida
sequence (Ferdinandi and Aukia, n.d.).

•

Simplebar for additional control over scrollbars to improve affordance
of scrolling necessary for slightline manipulation (Denat, 2023).

In general, this approach collects a series of smaller components in a
custom architecture the enables the design’s bespoke operation.

4.2
Architecture

To encourage separation of concerns while still supporting the tool’s
specific interaction patterns inspired by game design, this project
generally uses a model-view-presenter architecture with strong historic
precedent (Osmani, 2023). However, the sophisticated web application
decomposes the interface into multiple presenters fitting into a general
hierarchy where the bottom controls individual views while higher level
components increasingly focus on coordination. Note that “facades”
manage access to underlying data and simulation mechanics that make up
the analogized model component (Shvets, n.d.).

Figure 4:
Overview of presenter structure.

This structure reinterprets events as they move up and down the
hierarchy to ensure proper handling across different sections of the
coordinated interface. Achieving the connected level design of
Banjo-Tooie, this architectural approach specifically allows
visualization code to span across multiple tabs within the broader
interface, keeping different sections of the software in sync while
still achieving separation of concerns and centering the same
user-designed policy scenario (Brown, 2023b).

4.3
Offline access

This design and architectural approach supporting these game-inspired
mechanics involves propagation of many events that could cause
significant network traffic. That in mind, practical advice from
research team members experienced in negotiation process emphasize that
use of the tool in actual treaty settings (including “side meetings”)
cannot expect constant internet connectivity. Therefore, this
negotiation-focused project employs locally installable progressive web
applications which offer three options for offline usage (MDN, 2023b).

•

Cache first
: Always prioritizing cached content, this
strategy only executes network requests in the case of a cache miss.

•

Cache first with refresh
: Always prioritizing cached content,
this approach still requests updated copies of resources after
returning cached material to the client.

•

Network first
: This tactic always goes to the network but
uses the cache as a fall back if requests fail.

To prioritize positive experience during weak connectivity where
requests may still succeed slowly while still affording for somewhat
frequent updates, this software package elects for the cache first with
refresh approach.

4.4
Domain-specific language

The sophisticated visualizations called for by these game-inspired
designs involves updating many coordinated complex components as users
refine their policy scenarios. A tool frequently used by games, this
software turns to a domain specific language or “DSL” to enable the
project to “reduce the communication costs” between collaborators in
constructing this complex logic (Strumenta, 2022; West, 2008). Also
cutting down on development time, this approach adds a custom runtime
environment deployed in the browser and tied to visualizations
(Strumenta, 2022). Furthermore, this language includes specific
integration of certain keywords and operators for common plastic
simulation actions like calculating product lifecycle distributions and
proportionally distributing effects as part of simulation propagation.
To that end, this tool runs the open source ANTLR 4 technology in the
browser to offer a custom interpreter using a bespoke grammar as further
discussed in the

project
repository
(Parr, 2023). Various open source ANTLR language
implementations provide precedent for this design (Everett, 2013; Kiers,
2020; Pottinger, 2023; Sturluson, 2019).

5.2
Evaluative constraints

To understand this case study’s decision to limit usage insights to this
single piece of software itself, consider that this policy tool operates
in an ongoing international process.

Negotiation and evaluation
: Various approaches in negotiation
theory focus on the importance of exchanges and communication, a framing
where intentional information flow matters (Alfredson and Cungu, 2008).
This complicates common evaluative approaches as they may obtain data
about an individual and their insights within the context of the treaty,
making them potentially “private information” (NIH, n.d.). For
example, visualization evaluations like Multi-dimensional In-depth
Long-term Case-studies (MILC) would involve the disclosure of not just
experiences of the tool but also example findings from the data,
perspective which may become crucial to negotiation where unintended
exposure of that information could cause harm (Shneiderman and Plaisant,
2006).

Prioritization of participation
: Even if taking steps to ensure
data safety, consideration of the geopolitical context and desire to
serve policy makers as partners

in this
“once-in-a-lifetime” treaty process leads this tool building effort to
forgo collection of information about individuals and to avoid research
on their treaty insights (Legaspi, 2023). Respecting these practical
needs for elevated privacy both enables this interactive science project
to participate in this multi-national process and offers a public
scientific resource to the community addressing a “worsening global
crisis” (WWF, n.d.). Indeed, doing so answers a growing call to go
beyond the paper and “blend research and engagement” to “respond
rapidly to real-world events and public demand” (Swain, 2023). However,
this pragmatic stance also prevents this case study from providing
findings from controlled experiments or diary studies, asking it to
instead focus on “quality assurance” and “quality improvement” tasks
where information is only collected on the software itself (CPHS, n.d.).

Limitations
: As this research team turns to careful reflection
on the tool itself instead of conducting research about users and their
insights, this paper offers caution regarding important limitations on
its usage observations. Notably, these narrower results do not seek to
provide “generalizable knowledge” about user populations such as
negotiators, a larger and separate task potentially possible through
later human subjects research (CPHS, n.d.).

5.3
Observations

Despite these constraints on formal evaluation, reflection on tool
engagement during this time still offers some perspective on its
practical use and mechanics.

Policy selection
: Though specific policy designs remain subject
to change throughout the aforementioned multiple rounds of “exchanges”
during the fluid nature of negotiation, the political process itself
often offers guidance as to the general families of policies under
consideration (Alfredson and Cungu, 2008). For example, the so called
“zero draft” put out by the United Nations itself may offer
perspective where “the text is guided, as per the mandate received from
the committee, by the views expressed at the committee’s first and
second sessions” (UNEP, 2023b). In addition to direct user feedback,
these articulations of collective perspective may offer guidance to
interactive science projects like global-plastics-tool.org with regards
to which high level “policy levers” to emphasize in the user
interface.

Role of facilitator
: Though user-guided exploration remains the
goal, the tool’s sessions quickly adopted the En-ROADS approach where
workshops or presentations using the software feature a “facilitator”
who first introduces the motivations and science behind the tool,
offering an invitation on behalf of the tool for policy makers to engage
with the results through their own inquiry (Jones et al., 2022). As
discussed below, continued use of this precedent from the prior
literature in the future may offer an “ice-breaking” for the
interactive science product to enter into productive collaborative
discussion.

Non-linearity
: While this interactive tool appears to
successfully execute the design’s intended primary loop where the
simulation software sparks conversations about incremental edits that
drive forward the larger secondary loop of comparing different policy
scenarios, this hidden tutorial may also unexpectedly repeat (Brown,
n.d.). Though designers imagine a progression from overview to details
tab as users become more experienced, the tool’s current structure
instead may appear to encourage a cycle:

•

Start on the overview tab to discuss a policy at high level.

•

Go to the details tab when hitting a deeper question.

•

Return to the overview tab to chart out next topics.

This may suggest that the visualization platform in its current form
uses its sequence both to tutorialize mechanics as well as policies
themselves.

Multiple play styles
: While the tool sparks multiple rounds of
inquiry which feature expected policy refinement loops, sometimes the
software also sees its sessions stay within the overview tab. In
consideration of the existing tool’s current design, this may indicate
additional affordance to the details tab could help spur users to deeper
understanding. However, a games lens offers an alternative explanation.
Prior work suggests that different segments of audiences often naturally
form divergent but successful “play styles” and, for this tool, this
theory could suggest that one such mode of engagement may only require
the high level overview to provide insight (Stewart, 2011). Indeed, the
tool often only navigates to the details tab in response to a deeper
question. Therefore, this “play styles” hypothesis may further suggest
that the overview tab serves higher level thinking through surveying
major ideas and that the conclusion of the Hayashida sequence only
becomes necessary for users encountering deeper inquiry. Furthermore,
this may provide another form of multiple levels of reading suggested by
Victor (2011).

Scientist usage
: The tool also unexpectedly benefits the
science itself: helping its own authors evolve the AI models by showing
data in more holistic views that highlight unexpected implications or
behaviors. Though not required, the tool frequently explores different
model outputs in service of the research team itself, revealing new
insights or searching for implementation issues which would cause
unexpected policy effects.

Model transparency
: Using interactivity to answer questions
which seek to understand something unexpected or interesting hinted at
in its landmarks, the tool appears to spur discussion using its small
contextual charts, potentially suggesting those design features’
anticipated use in facilitating navigation and provoking follow up
inquiry that pulls to adjacent views. That being said, the same
mechanics also appear to unexpectedly offer the software a method of
model explainability. In response to modeling questions, the details tab
itself outlines the variables included and incidentally addresses if
simulation incorporates certain dynamics. In fulfillment of Victor’s
(2011) expectations for explorable explanations, the system and
financial parameter panels in particular document underlying assumptions
of the simulation and invite users to understand the impact of changing
those variables. Put another way, the tool may sometimes respond to
questions using its “rumor” tooltips but other times use a form of
environmental storytelling: the concepts simply listed on the details
tab itself convey model architecture (Brown, 2017, 2020).

Progressive web application
: With research team members having
gone physically to INC-3, the tool sees frequent offline use, possibly
providing the anticipated valuable flexibility where, due to the
practicalities of that complex event, ongoing connectivity proves
difficult as activities move between various venues.

6
Discussion

With these reflections in mind, this study contextualizes this open
source tool, understanding opportunities for further study and potential
additional connections to other concepts.

6.1
Overview of design
patterns

To improve utility of discussion, this case study summarizes and names
the designs that it considers:

•

Hidden Hayashida Tutorials
: Building on prior work in both
data visualization and game design, the use of visual hierarchy to
embed the four step Hayashida sequence into the architecture of a data
visualization to construct an “invisible” tutorial (Brown, 2015a;
Nutt and Hayashida, 2012; Pottinger and Zarpellon, 2023). This
structural narrative approach promises to orient users while avoiding
the ludonarrative dissonance that an explicit guide may generate
(Hocking, 2007; JM8, 2023). This tool’s two tab connected layout
attempts to embody this possible instructional pattern.

•

Connected Zelda Valleys
: Bringing the concept of “primary”
and “secondary loop” gameplay to data visualization, the combination
of open world and connected level designs deploys small “landmark”
visualizations which both attempt to orient the player navigationally
and invite deeper exploration of alternative perspectives into data or
results (Brown, 2023a, 2023b; Brown, n.d.). Building this
“geography” into visualizations aims for sophisticated tools to tame
their complexity by subsetting the actions available to the user at
any single moment, hoping to maintain interface simplicity (Portnow,
2022). This tool’s summary charts surrounding the timeseries display
seeks to demonstrate this potential navigational method.

•

Data Rumors
: Creating small “side-quests” within a
visualization, the use of subtle hints towards additional optional
content seeks to allow users to “read at multiple levels” without
interrupting the regular interaction flow through the broader tool
(Brown, 2017; Takahashi and Nelson, 2018; Victor, 2011). This
project’s tooltips attempt to address this exploration opportunity.

•

Environmental Explanations
: Mirroring the structure of the
model into the structure of the visualization affords model mechanics
explanations and tries to enable readers to “play with the premise
and assumptions of various claims” (Victor, 2011). The details tab
and, in particular, the assumptions drawers try to embody this context
setting approach.

Given this vocabulary, this study encourages future researchers to
consider these design concepts for further exploration.

6.2
Permission to play

The potential usefulness for a facilitator to open meetings may reflect
what Andrew Walsh (2019) calls a need for “permission to play” in tool
sessions. This may suggest that traditional context of communication
sets expectations regarding unidirectional sharing that the tool may
break. That said, future work could better understand these potential
underlying sociological barriers and how design itself may serve to
similarly offer this permission without a team member present.

6.3
Backtracking

Cycling between the overview and details tab may indicate the unexpected
emergence of “backtracking behavior” often seen in open world and
“Metroidvania” games (Brown, 2018). This pattern has players take new
skills or knowledge to revisit prior content and, while this can
recontextualize earlier experiences with new understanding and deeper
meaning, poor design can also cause backtracking to become tedious
repetition (Brown, 2023b). Later work may further expand this study’s
design language to understand how intentional management of this
behavior can reinforce insight and engagement.

6.4
Inquiry-based design

With the tool seeing questions naturally pull users to the deeper parts
of the interface, this open world design visualization approach may
spark the “inquiry-based” learning suggested by Nicolette Hayes
(2023). This may indicate the educational visualizations outside policy
can benefit from similar analogies to video game design.

6.5
Model explanation
algorithms

Though not the central focus of this tool, the potential utility of this
software to explain model structure may suggest that visualizations
trying to offer “model explainability” might consider including
similar game design techniques to those explored here alongside
algorithms like Local Interpretable Model Explanations (Ribeiro et al.,
2016).

6.6
Role of AI

This tool could try to use multi-variate optimization to propose
solutions to users, offering its own “policy answer” alongside
user-generated suggestions (Blank and Deb, 2020). However, using the
video games lens, doing so could create ludonarrative dissonance. As an
extension of the projects’s existing modeling, this hypothetical feature
would see the software claiming to invite outside expertise and
exploration (with AI as a “helper” not a “driver”) while
simultaneously offering a mechanic providing “optimized” solutions.

In this case of interacting with actual policy negotiation, this project
intentionally excludes this AI-driven auto-optimization option despite
access to the machinery required. Again, policy makers offer expertise
necessary for finding good solutions (Tyler, 2013). In this framing,
visualization and game design allow that knowledge to participate in
co-creation of solutions, potentially reaching beyond the limits of
blind mathematical optimization.

Nevertheless, future work may consider if different design contexts
could benefit from this kind of AI-generated solution or may offer
studies on evolving policy maker perceptions of these algorithmic
designs.

6.7
Future human subjects
research

This case study considers quality assurance and improvement activity for
a single tool, providing information about only this software and only
one case of these potential design patterns. That said, while this
simulation currently remains constrained by the nature of ongoing treaty
processes and a desire to avoid collecting information about individuals
within this sensitive context, later research could seek generalizable
knowledge through human subjects research approaches like MILC which may
be more appropriate outside active negotiation (Shneiderman and
Plaisant, 2006).

Still, in imagining future research, this effort cautions that this
interactive science tool does not serve the general public and instead
focuses on active and often knowledgeable treaty participants (Tyler,
2013). Therefore, it remains possible that this unique target audience
may engage with this tool with specific “play styles” different than
other users (Stewart, 2011). Indeed, the formulation of MILC responds to
this situated nature of data visualization where evaluation cannot
consider just any user but a specific population where professional
objectives and background mediate their experience of a technology
(Shneiderman and Plaisant, 2006).

In short, later research hoping to build generalizable knowledge should
ensure study takes place in-situ. To that end, this study also
recognizes a potential opportunity for additional methods formulation
for this kind of active policy negotiation context.

6.8
Technical measures for
privacy

To further this effort’s ethical stance regarding the avoidance of
collecting information about policy makers and their insights within the
sensitive context of treaty negotiation, the decision support web
application itself takes a number of privacy-preserving technical steps:

•

Providing a
single page application
where computation runs
client-side so that navigation between elements does not generate
network requests that may reveal intentions or activity, a
privacy-preserving practice paired with also avoiding use of
technologies like “beacons” which would reveal the questions being
explored or results being considered by a user (MDN, 2023a).

•

Though not intentionally requesting information about users, adopting

HTTPS
to avoid malicious manipulation or traffic interception
(Basques, 2015).

•

Disabling or curtailing certain web provider defaults for

logging
analysis, in particular strictly limiting use of IP
addresses to only what is required for basic operation including
traffic routing, security, abuse prevention, and reliability (Bruno,
2022).

•

Providing an
offline version
also allows users to leverage
the application with intentionally disabled network connectivity which
may further help avoid generating web traffic.

Future work in the policy space should also consider the privacy aspects
of not just research methods but implementation.

6.9
Use of a DSL

The domain specific language displaces earlier prototypes written either
in the R or Python programming languages. Teammates observe that
migration both:

•

Improves readability of code through integration of domain specific
keywords (JetBrains, n.d.).

•

Reduces the length of scripts by removing “boilerplate” code
(O’Sullivan, 2018).

Indeed, code for interventions in the DSL achieves sufficient brevity
that the visualization tool displays this logic within the interface and
even allows users to modify that code in their local copy of the live
application.

This in-situ programming capability deserves additional discussion in
that it creates what Maggie Appleton (2022) calls a “Programming
Portal” feature. This further realizes conception of explorable
explanations by Victor (2011) in that it allows “the reader to play
with the author’s assumptions and analyses, and see the consquences.”
Furthermore, this paper observes that the game design and development
communities also find similar benefits both from incorporation of DSLs
and this “hot reload” ability to change the programming behind the
experience in-situ (West, 2008; Zylinski, 2023).

This live development capability serves not just users but the
developers of the tool as well. In practice, most of the tool’s current
policy options were created from within the tool itself by using the web
application as the first development environment with the ability to see
how new logic interacts with the model outputs in real time without the
delay of a “compile” step, a pattern embodied in the “prototype”
drawer in the details tab. Returning to requirements identified by Nolin
(1999) for timeliness, this approach may offer agility in keeping pace
with policy conversation.

6.10
Mediation

By allowing changing of systems and financial assumptions, this tool
answers the call made by Victor (2011) for explorable explanations to
allow the questioning of modeling. That in mind, this software focuses
on media for “active reading” and iterative policy design in service
of providing actionable scientific information to decision-makers,
considering how policy makers may question the tool and bring forward
scientific perspective during negotiation (Victor, 2011). Note, however,
that this dialectic experience executes within existing treaty process
and does not consider how interactive software may mediate or alter that
process itself. Even though tools to help in discussion and resolution
of disagreements between stakeholders represents a different goal than
this projects’ intention potentially beyond what it could practically
consider in the context of this specific treaty opportunity, this
possibility of mediated disagreement resolution remains a possible area
for future research.

7
Conclusion

When Bret Victor (2012) implores audiences to “stop drawing dead fish”
he challenges them to consider the fundamentally responsive nature of
interactive tools: digital fish should react to users. Where the
rigidity of static media limit their mutual embrace, this project
suggests that interactivity facilitates the union of science and policy.
Adding to the early discourse between video game and information design,
this tool’s analogies offer not just an open source exemplar but also
help to identify a language for building these dialectic experiences in
policy contexts.

In facilitating this human-machine conversation, consider a concept from
Jesse Schell (2019) central to the game design at the heart of the tool:
experiences are co-created by game and player. In that spirit, these
cross-disciplinary methods aim to create a balanced conversation in the
space between AI, scientist, and decision-maker. For that, these
approaches may reach beyond policy to other collaborative design
contexts, reasserting the importance of human input even in a world
further saturated with AI. This gestures to an exciting promise, a
future in which computation does not displace experience but instead
where both add to each other.
