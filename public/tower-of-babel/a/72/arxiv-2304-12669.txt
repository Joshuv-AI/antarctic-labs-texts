On Fourier–Mukai type autoequivalences of Kuznetsov components of cubic threefolds
Ziqi Liu

Abstract
. We determine the group of all Fourier–Mukai type autoequivalences of Kuznetsov components of smooth complex cubic threefolds, and provide yet another proof for the Fourier–Mukai version of categorical Torelli theorem for smooth complex cubic threefolds.



1.
Introduction

1.1.

The seminal results of Bondal and Orlov motivate the study on derived categories of smooth projective varieties. This interest later spreads to other triangulated categories, and an important viewpoint is that a nice enough triangulated category can be regarded as (the derived category of) a noncommutative variety.

Among these triangulated categories, the Kuznetsov component
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
of a (smooth complex) cubic threefold
Y
is one of the most studied ones. It carries enough information to determine
Y
(see, for example,
[
4
,
5
,
8
,
21
]
). However, many geometric aspects for
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
as a noncommutative variety have not been well-understood.

In this paper, we study the autoequivalences of
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
, and particularly determine all Fourier–Mukai type ones. Also, we give an elementary proof for the Fourier–Mukai version of categorical Torelli theorem for cubic threefolds.

1.2.

Given a cubic threefold
Y
, we denote by
\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
the set of all Fourier–Mukai type autoequivalences of
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
. According to
[
13
]
, many natural autoequivalences, such as the identity functor
{\textup{{{Id}}}}_{\mathcal{A}_{Y}}
and the Serre functor
S
, are in
\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
. So one can see that
\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
is really a group under the functor composition.

Set
j^{*}\colon D^{b}(Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{Y}
to be the left adjoint functor of the inclusion
j_{*}\colon\mathcal{A}_{Y}\subset D^{b}(Y)
, then the image
j^{*}k(x)
is isomorphic to a stable sheaf
\mathscr{K}^{x}
on
Y
up to a shift. Thanks to
[
4
,
5
]
, we are able to make a decomposition

\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\Aut(Y)\times G_{0}

where
G_{0}:=\{\Phi\in\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})\,|\,\Phi(\mathscr{K}^{x})\cong\mathscr{K}^{x}\,\,\textup{ for each point }x\in Y\}
. To decode
G_{0}
, we need to understand the relationship between certain flat families of stable sheaves on
Y
with class
v=v(\mathscr{K}^{x})
. It turns out that
G_{0}
is trivial, due to the observation that the moduli space
M_{Y}(v)
of stable sheaves considered in
[
4
]
is indeed a fine moduli space and admits a universal sheaf.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
4.13
)
.

One has
\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\Aut(Y)
.

This result is obtained independently also in
[
8
]
among other things, using a complete different method. Explicitly, they prove that each element in
\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
can be extended to an autoequivalence of
D^{b}(Y)
and then conclude by
[
10
, Proposition 4.17]
.

1.3.

It is believed that any equivalence between Kuznetsov components of cubic threefolds is of Fourier–Mukai type, which is indeed the case for cubic fourfolds cf.
[
16
]
. Therefore, it should be sufficient to consider the following form of categorical Torelli theorem for cubic threefolds.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
5.1
)
.

Consider two cubic threefolds
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
, and assume that there exists a Fourier–Mukai type equivalence
\mathcal{A}_{Y_{1}}\cong\mathcal{A}_{Y_{2}}
, then
Y_{1}\cong Y_{2}
.

This specific form of categorical Torelli theorem has been proved in
[
6
,
20
]
using certain motivic constructions of intermediate Jacobians, and proved in
[
17
]
using matrix factorization. Here we provide another proof for this theorem based on
[
19
]
and the spirit of
[
13
]
. Our argument is similar to that for
[
20
, Proposition 5.23]
but more elementary.

Organization

1.5.
Conventions

In this paper, all varieties are defined over
\mathbb{C}
, all triangulated categories are assumed to be complex linear, and functors between triangulated categories are asked to be over
\mathbb{C}
. Given a variety
X
, we set
D^{b}(X):=D^{b}({\textbf{{Coh}}}(X))
to be the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on
X
. By a cubic threefold, we always mean a smooth projective one, which will be denoted by
Y
or
Y_{i}
; by a point, we always mean a closed point.

2.
Kuznetsov components of cubic threefolds

Here we introduce some basic aspects of Kuznetsov components of cubic threefolds and a couple of important results. Standard notions and notations can be found in
[

,

.

2.1.

Let
\iota\colon Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
be a cubic threefold polarized by
\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1):=\iota^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{4}}(1)
. The
Kuznetsov component

\mathcal{A}_{Y}
of
Y
is defined to be the right orthogonal complement

\mathcal{A}_{Y}:=\{E\in D^{b}(Y)\,|\,\Hom(\mathscr{O}_{Y},E[n])=\Hom(\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1),E[n])=0\textup{ for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}\}

of the exceptional collection
\mathscr{O}_{Y},\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1)
in
D^{b}(Y)
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
\ell\subset Y
be any line, then the ideal sheaf
\mathscr{I}_{\ell}
lies in
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
by definition.

The subcategory
\mathcal{A}_{Y}\subset D^{b}(Y)
is admissible, and we will denote the inclusion functor and its right (resp. left) adjoint functor by
j_{*}
and
j^{!}
(resp.
j^{*}
). Explicitly, one has

j^{*}\colon D^{b}(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\textup{{{L}}}}_{\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1)}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\langle\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathscr{O}_{Y}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\textup{{{L}}}}_{\mathscr{O}_{Y}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{A}_{Y}

where
{\textup{{{L}}}}_{E_{0}}\colon\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\langle E_{0}\rangle^{\perp}
is the left mutation functor represented by the mapping

E\mapsto\textsf{C}(\ev\colon\bigoplus_{m}\Hom(E_{0},E[m])\otimes E_{0}[-m]\rightarrow E)

for any exceptional object
E_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
of any given triangulated category.

Example 2.2
.

Let
k(x)
be the skyscraper sheaf at a point
x\in Y
, then

j^{*}k(x)={\textup{{{L}}}}_{\mathscr{O}_{Y}}\circ{\textup{{{L}}}}_{\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1)}k(x)={\textup{{{L}}}}_{\mathscr{O}_{Y}}\mathscr{I}_{x}(1)[1]=\textup{{C}}\left(\mathscr{O}_{Y}\otimes H^{0}(Y,\mathscr{I}_{x}(1))\rightarrow\mathscr{I}_{x}(1)\right)[1]\cong\mathscr{K}^{x}[2]

where
\mathscr{I}_{x}
is the ideal sheaf of the point
x
and
\mathscr{K}^{x}
is the coherent sheaf defined by the short exact sequence
0\rightarrow\mathscr{K}^{x}\rightarrow\mathscr{O}_{Y}\otimes H^{0}(Y,\mathscr{I}_{x}(1))\rightarrow\mathscr{I}_{x}(1)\rightarrow 0
.

2.2.

The Kuznetsov component
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
has many natural non-trivial autoequivalences.

Example 2.3
.

Let
f\colon Y\rightarrow Y
be an automorphism, then the derived direct image
\textup{{R}}f_{*}
and the derived inverse image
\textup{{L}}f^{*}
restrict to mutually inverse autoequivalences of
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
.

Example 2.4
.

Since
\mathcal{A}_{Y}\subset D^{b}(Y)
is admissible, it has a Serre functor
S
which can be represented by
E\mapsto j^{!}(j_{*}E\otimes\mathscr{O}_{Y}(-2))[3]
. Accordingly, one has
{\textup{{{S}}}}^{-1}\cong j^{*}(j_{*}(-)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{Y}(2))[-3]
.

Example 2.5
.

The tensor product with a line bundle on
Y
does not restrict to an autoequivalence of
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
. Instead, one has the
degree shift
functor
{\textup{{{T}}}}\colon E\mapsto j^{*}(j_{*}E\otimes\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1))
such that

{\textup{{{T}}}}^{n}\cong j^{*}(j_{*}(-)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{Y}(n))

for
n\geq 0
. In particular, one has
{\textup{{{S}}}}^{-1}\cong{\textup{{{T}}}}^{2}\circ[-3]
.

2.3.

It is proved in

Proposition 2.6

Consider the ideal sheaf

\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\

Example 2.7

One computes that

3.
Fourier–Mukai transforms

Here we introduce some necessary facts about Fourier–Mukai transforms. We will tactically use the analytic topology when we talk about cohomology and topological
K
-theory and will not notationally distinguish a variety and its analytification.

3.1.

At first we collect some standard materials from
[
10
]
. Let
X
and
Z
be two smooth projective varieties and choose an object
P\in D^{b}(X\times Z)
, we define

\Phi_{P}\colon D^{b}(X)\rightarrow D^{b}(Z),\quad E\mapsto\textup{{R}}p_{*}(P\otimes^{\textup{{L}}}\textup{{L}}q^{*}E)

to be the
Fourier–Mukai transform with kernel
P
where
p\colon X\times Z\rightarrow Z
and
q\colon X\times Z\rightarrow X
are the projections. Here
\textup{{L}}q^{*}
is indeed just the non-derived
q^{*}
as the projection
q
is flat.

The composition
\Phi_{P}\circ\Phi_{Q}
of two Fourier–Mukai transforms
\Phi_{Q}\colon D^{b}(X)\rightarrow D^{b}(Z)
and
\Phi_{P}\colon D^{b}(Z)\rightarrow D^{b}(W)
is isomorphic to a Fourier–Mukai transform
\Phi_{P\circ Q}
with kernel

P\circ Q:=\textup{{R}}\pi_{XW*}(\pi^{*}_{ZW}P\otimes^{\textup{{L}}}\pi^{*}_{XZ}Q)

where
\pi_{XZ},\pi_{ZW},\pi_{XW}
are the projections from
X\times Z\times W
onto the respective factors.

The Fourier–Mukai transform
\Phi_{P}\colon D^{b}(X)\rightarrow D^{b}(Z)
admits a left adjoint functor
\Phi_{P_{\textrm{L}}}
and a right adjoint functor
\Phi_{P_{\textrm{R}}}
with kernels

P_{\textrm{L}}\cong P^{\vee}\otimes p^{*}\omega_{Z}[\dim{Z}]\quad\textup{and}\quad P_{\textrm{R}}\cong P^{\vee}\otimes q^{*}\omega_{X}[\dim{X}]

where
P^{\vee}:=\textup{{R}}\mathscr{H}om(P,\mathscr{O}_{X\times X})
is the derived dual.

3.2.

The idea of Fourier–Mukai transforms can be applied to maps between other invariants in complex algebraic geometry. The most commonly used ones are cohomological Fourier–Mukai maps. Under the same assumption as before, one defines

\Phi^{H}_{P}\colon H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{\bullet}(Z,\mathbb{Q}),\quad v\mapsto p_{*}(q^{*}v\cup\textup{{v}}(P))

where
p_{*},q^{*}
are pushforward and pullback in cohomology and
\textup{{v}}(P):=\textup{{ch}}([P])\cup\sqrt{\textup{{td}}(X\times Z)}
is the Mukai vector of the kernel
P\in D^{b}(X\times Z)
. Though cohomological Fourier–Mukai maps do not preserve graded structure on
H^{\bullet}
in general, one has

\Phi_{P}^{H}(H^{p,q}(X))\subset\bigoplus_{p-q=r-s}H^{r,s}(Z)

In particular, a group isomorphism
\Phi_{P}^{H}\colon H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{\bullet}(Z,\mathbb{Q})
will restrict to isomorphisms

\bigoplus_{p-q=i}H^{p,q}(X)\cong\bigoplus_{p-q=i}H^{p,q}(Z)

Moreover, one always has a commutative diagram

3.3.

Similar to the cohomological Fourier–Mukai maps, one can also consider the topological

[

. Under the same assumption as before, one defines

\Phi^{K}_{P}\colon K^{0}(X)\rightarrow K^{0}(Z),\quad\kappa\mapsto p_{*}(q^{*}\kappa\cup[P])

where
p_{*},q^{*}
are pushforward and pullback in topological
K
-theory and

The pullback in topological

3.4.

Orlov proved in
[
18
]
that the kernel for a fully faithful Fourier–Mukai transform is unique. In general, the kernel is not unique but one always has

Proposition 3.5

(
[
7

)
.

Consider smooth projective varieties
X,Z
and
P,Q\in D^{b}(X\times Z)
such that
\Phi_{P}\cong\Phi_{Q}
, then
\mathscr{H}^{i}(P)\cong\mathscr{H}^{i}(Q)
for all

.

Proposition
3.5
is usually applied together with the following criterion cf.
[

to show that the kernel of certain Fourier–Mukai transform is unique.

Lemma 3.6

(Bridgeland)
.

Consider smooth projective varieties
X,Z
and a Fourier–Mukai transform
\Phi_{P}\colon D^{b}(X)\rightarrow D^{b}(Z)
such that for any point
x\in X
there exists a coherent sheaf
\mathscr{K}^{x}
on
Z
with
\Phi_{P}(k(x))\cong\mathscr{K}^{x}[m]
in
D^{b}(Z)
for some

, then
P\cong\mathscr{P}[m]
for some coherent sheaf
\mathscr{P}
on
X\times Z
which is flat over
X
via the canonical projection.

Example 3.7
.

Let
Y
be a cubic threefold, then the left mutation functor

{\textup{{{L}}}}_{\mathscr{O}_{Y}(n)}\colon E\mapsto\textsf{C}(\ev\colon\bigoplus_{m}\Hom(\mathscr{O}_{Y}(n),E[m])\otimes\mathscr{O}_{Y}(n)[-m]\rightarrow E)

is a Fourier–Mukai transform of kernel

where
\mathscr{O}(a,b):=q^{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}(a)\otimes p^{*}\mathscr{O}_{X}(b)
and
\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}

. Hence

or

\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}

. On the other hand, one has

4.
The group of Fourier–Mukai type autoequivalences

4.1.

Now we are prepared to work on
\Aut_{FM}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
for a cubic threefold
Y
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given two cubic threefolds
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
, an exact functor
\Phi\colon\mathcal{A}_{Y_{1}}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{Y_{2}}
is said to be
of Fourier–Mukai type of kernel

5.
The Fourier–Mukai type categorical Torelli theorem revisited

5.1.

Here we present our proof for the Fourier–Mukai version of categorical Torelli theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider two cubic threefolds
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
, and assume that there exists a Fourier–Mukai type equivalence
\mathcal{A}_{Y_{2}}\cong\mathcal{A}_{Y_{2}}
, then
Y_{1}\cong Y_{2}
.

Given such an equivalence
\Phi
and name the associated Fourier–Mukai transform
\Phi_{P}
, we will have a cohomological Fourier–Mukai map

\Phi_{P}^{H}\colon H^{\textup{odd}}(Y_{1},\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{\textup{odd}}(Y_{2},\mathbb{Q})

Since the only non-trivial odd-degree cohomology group of a cubic threefold
Y
is
H^{3}
, it then restricts to a group homomorphism
\Phi_{P}^{H}\colon H^{3}(Y_{1},\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{3}(Y_{2},\mathbb{Q})
.

We will show that the group homomorphism is indeed a Hodge isomorphism, so that we can apply the arguments in
[
19
]
to see that
\Phi_{P}^{H}
restricts again to a Hodge isometry

\Phi_{P}^{H}\colon H^{3}(Y_{1},\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow H^{3}(Y_{2},\mathbb{Z})

In this case, one can conclude by the classic Torelli theorem for cubic threefolds.

5.2.

The key point is the following lemma, which is pointed out to me by Daniel Huybrechts.

Lemma 5.2
.

Consider a cubic threefold
Y
and any two coherent sheaves
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{F}
on
Y
, then the cohomological Fourier–Mukai map
\Phi^{H}_{\mathscr{E}\boxtimes\mathscr{F}}\colon H^{3}(Y,\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{3}(Y,\mathbb{Q})
is the zero map. Here the kernel
\mathscr{E}\boxtimes\mathscr{F}:=q^{*}\mathscr{E}\otimes p^{*}\mathscr{F}
as before.

Proof.

Since Betti cohomology is a classic Weil cohomology theory, one has

\Phi^{H}_{\mathscr{E}\boxtimes\mathscr{F}}(v)=p_{*}(q^{*}(v\cup\textup{{v}}(\mathscr{E}))\cup p^{*}\textup{{v}}(\mathscr{F}))=\left(\int_{Y}v\cup\textup{{v}}(\mathscr{E})\right)\cup\textup{{v}}(\mathscr{F})

for any class
v\in H^{3}(Y,\mathbb{Q})
. The cohomology class
v\cup\textup{{v}}(\mathscr{E})
has no components in the top cohomology group, so it is zero after the trace map. Hence
\Phi^{H}_{\mathscr{E}\boxtimes\mathscr{F}}(v)
has to be zero.
∎

Since
p_{*},q^{*},\cup
are additive, one can see that

p_{*}(q^{*}v\cup(\textup{{v}}([E]+[F])))=p_{*}(q^{*}v\cup(\textup{{v}}([E])))+p_{*}(q^{*}v\cup(\textup{{v}}([F])))

for any
E,F\in D^{b}(Y\times Y)
and cohomology class
v
. Moreover, one has the following observations:

Corollary 5.3
.

One has
\Phi^{H}_{U}=\Phi^{H}_{\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}}=\textup{Id}_{H^{3}}
for the Fourier–Mukai transform
j_{*}\circ j^{*}\cong\Phi_{U}
.

Proof.

According to Example
3.7
, one has

[U]=[\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}]+5[\mathscr{O}(-1,0)]-[\mathscr{O}(-1,1)]-[\mathscr{O}(0,0)]

in the topological
K
-group
K^{0}(Y\times Y)
, which implies
\Phi_{U}^{H}=\Phi^{H}_{\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}}=\textup{Id}_{H^{3}}
by Lemma
5.2
and the fact that the Mukai vector
\textup{{v}}\colon K^{0}(Y\times Y)\rightarrow H^{\textup{even}}(Y\times Y)
is also additive.
∎

The functor
j_{*}\circ j^{!}
is the right adjoint of the Fourier–Mukai transform
\Phi_{U}\cong j_{*}\circ j^{*}
, so one has
j_{*}\circ j^{!}\cong\Phi_{U_{\textup{{R}}}}
for the kernel
U_{\textup{{R}}}=U^{\vee}\otimes q^{*}\mathscr{O}_{Y}(-2)[3]
.

Corollary 5.4
.

One has
\Phi^{H}_{U_{\textup{{R}}}}=\Phi^{H}_{\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}}=\textup{Id}_{H^{3}}
for the Fourier–Mukai transform
j_{*}\circ j^{!}\cong\Phi_{U_{\textup{{R}}}}
.

Proof.

The derived dual of a locally free sheaf on
Y
is just the usual dual, so one has

\mathscr{O}(a,b)^{\vee}\otimes q^{*}\mathscr{O}_{Y}(-2)[3]\cong\mathscr{O}(-a-2,-b)[3]

Since
\Phi_{\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}}\cong{\textup{{{Id}}}}_{D^{b}(Y)}
, its right adjoint functor is also the identity functor. Hence one has

\mathscr{O}_{\Delta,\textup{{R}}}:=\mathscr{O}^{\vee}_{\Delta}\otimes q^{*}\mathscr{O}_{Y}(-2)[3]\cong\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}

due to Proposition
3.5
. Therefore, one can conclude by Lemma
5.2
and the isomorphism

U\cong\left(\mathscr{O}(-1,0)^{\oplus 5}\rightarrow\mathscr{O}(-1,1)\oplus\mathscr{O}(0,0)\rightarrow\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}\right)

that one has
\Phi^{H}_{U_{\textup{{R}}}}=\Phi^{H}_{\mathscr{O}_{\Delta,\textup{{R}}}}=\Phi_{\mathscr{O}_{\Delta}}^{H}=\textup{Id}_{H^{3}}
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Consider two cubic threefolds
Y_{1},Y_{2}
and a Fourier–Mukai type equivalence
\mathcal{A}_{Y_{1}}\cong\mathcal{A}_{Y_{2}}
of kernel
P
, then
\Phi_{P}^{H}\colon H^{3}(Y_{1},\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{3}(Y_{2},\mathbb{Q})
is a group isomorphism.

Proof.

One has
\Phi_{P_{\textup{{R}}}\circ P}\cong\Phi_{P_{\textup{{R}}}}\circ\Phi_{P}\cong j_{1*}\circ j^{*}_{1}
, so by Corollary
5.3
the map

\Phi^{H}_{P_{\textup{{R}}}}\circ\Phi^{H}_{P}\cong\Phi_{P_{\textup{{R}}}\circ P}^{H}\colon H^{3}(Y_{1},\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{3}(Y_{1},\mathbb{Q})

is the identity map. Similarly, the map
\Phi^{H}_{P}\circ\Phi^{H}_{P_{\textup{{R}}}}
is also the identity due to Corollary
5.4
.
∎

In this case, the cohomological Fourier–Mukai map
\Phi_{P}^{H}
restricts to isomorphisms

\bigoplus_{p-q=-1}H^{p,q}(Y_{1})\cong\bigoplus_{p-q=-1}H^{p,q}(Y_{2})\quad\textup{and}\quad\bigoplus_{p-q=1}H^{p,q}(Y_{1})\cong\bigoplus_{p-q=1}H^{p,q}(Y_{2})

which implies that
\Phi_{P}^{H}\colon H^{3}(Y_{1},\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow H^{3}(Y_{2},\mathbb{Q})
is a Hodge isomorphism. The remaining part of our proof is the same as that for
[
19
, Proposition 2.1]
.
