The Eisenhart Lift and Hamiltonian Systems
Amir Babak Aazami

Abstract
. It is well known in general relativity that trajectories of Hamiltonian systems lift to geodesics of pp-wave spacetimes, an example of a more general phenomenon known as the “Eisenhart lift.” We review and expand upon the benefits of this correspondence for dynamical systems theory. One benefit is the use of curvature and conjugate points to study the stability of Hamiltonian systems. Another benefit is that this lift unfolds a Hamiltonian system into a family of ODEs akin to a moduli space. One such family arises from the conformal invariance of lightlike geodesics, by which any Hamiltonian system unfolds into a “conformal class” of non-diffeomorphic ODEs with solutions in common. By utilizing higher-index versions of pp-waves, a similar lift and conformal class are shown to exist for certain second-order complex ODEs. Another such family occurs by lifting to a Riemannian metric that is dual to a pp-wave, a process that in certain cases yields a “square root” for the Hamiltonian. We prove a two-point boundary result for the family of ODEs arising from this lift, as well as the existence of a constant of the motion generalizing conservation of energy.

Introduction

Consider the Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V,

(1)

i.
Given a solution of (

1

By lifting solutions

2.
Review of Hamiltonian systems, pp-waves, and the Eisenhart lift

First, recall that a
Lorentzian manifold

(M,g)
is a smooth manifold
M
on which is defined a smooth, nondegenerate metric
g
of signature
(-++\cdots+)
. Because of the absence of positive-definiteness, any nonzero tangent vector
X\in TM
comes in three flavors:

\displaystyle\text{$X$ is}~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\text{``timelike"}&\text{if}&g(X,X)<0,\\
\text{``spacelike"}&\text{if}&g(X,X)>0,\\
\text{``lightlike"}&\text{if}&g(X,X)=0.\end{array}\right.

(A tangent vector’s designation as timelike, spacelike, or lightlike is known as its
causal character
; in general, metrics of signature
(--\cdots-++\cdots+)
are called
semi-Riemannian
.)
The Lorentzian manifolds we have in mind are
pp-waves
, which have a distinguished history. They are the simplest examples of Lorentzian manifolds endowed with a parallel (i.e., covariantly constant) lightlike vector field, a class of metrics that was first introduced mathematically in
[
Bri25
]
. From a physical perspective, pp-waves model radiation propagating at the velocity of light in general relativity; see, e.g.,
[
BEE96
, Chapter 13]
,
[
FS03
]
, and
[
SHN+17
]
. The definition of pp-wave that we give here, taken from
[
GL16
]
, is a coordinate-independent version of the standard definition appearing in the physics literature:

Definition 1

(pp-wave)
.

On a
(
compact or noncompact
)
manifold
M
, a Lorentzian metric
g
is a
pp-wave
if it admits a globally defined lightlike vector field
N
that is parallel,
\nabla N=0

(
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection of
g
)
, and if its curvature endomorphism
R_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}
satisfies

\displaystyle R_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(X,Y)\,\cdot=0~~~\text{for all $X,Y\in\Gamma(N^{\perp})$.}

(7)

Locally, pp-waves always take the following form, now known as “Brinkmann coordinates” (for our purposes, it suffices that
V
be at least
C^{2}
):

Proposition 1

(Brinkmann coordinates)
.

Let
(M,g)
be an
(n+2)
-dimensional pp-wave with parallel lightlike vector field
N
. Then there exist local coordinates
(v,t,x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
in which
N=\partial_{v}
and

\displaystyle g\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=2dvdt-2V(t,x^{1},\dots,x^{n})(dt)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(dx^{i})^{2},

(8)

for some smooth function
V(t,x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
independent of
v
.
If (
8
) exists globally on
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, then
(\mathbb{R}^{n+2},g)
is called a
standard pp-wave
.

Proof.

For a proof of this fact, consult
[
GL16
]
. (We also mention here that, as proved in
[
LS16
]
, if a pp-wave is compact, then its universal cover is globally isometric to a standard pp-wave.)
∎

The relationship between pp-waves and Hamiltonian systems is due to the geodesics of (
8
); in what follows, we will always regard our pp-waves as
(n+2)
-dimensional and our Hamiltonian systems as
n
-dimensional.

Proposition 2

(Geodesics of a pp-wave)
.

For a pp-wave expressed locally in the Brinkmann coordinates
(v,t,x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
of Proposition
1
, its geodesic equations of motion are

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{lcl}\ddot{v}&=&\!\!\!\Big(2\frac{dV}{ds}-V_{t}\,\dot{t}\Big)\dot{t},\\
\ddot{t}&=&\!\!\!0,\\
\ddot{x}^{i}&=&\!\!\!-V_{i}\,\dot{t}^{2}\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pti=1,\dots,n,\end{array}\right\}

where
V_{t}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial t},V_{i}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^{i}}
, and
s
is the affine parameter.

Proof.

The only nonvanishing Christoffel symbols of (
8
) are

\displaystyle\Gamma_{it}^{v}=-V_{i}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\Gamma_{tt}^{v}=-V_{t}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\Gamma_{tt}^{i}=V_{i},

(13)

from which the equations (
2
) are easily derived from the geodesic equations
\ddot{z}^{\alpha}+\sum_{\beta,\gamma}\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\beta\gamma}\dot{z}^{\beta}\dot{z}^{\gamma}=0

∎

To see the relationship between the
(n+2)
-dimensional system (
2
) and the
n
-dimensional Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptx=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(14)

\gamma(s)=(v(s),t(s),x^{i}(s))

2

, we must have

. It follows that for

, the

2
) comprise precisely the Hamiltonian system (
14
), with

equal to the affine parameter
s
. Moreover, as

make no appearance in any equation in (
2
), the
\ddot{v}
-equation is completely determined by (
14
). One therefore says that the solutions of (
14

2
) of the
(n+2)
-dimensional pp-wave metric (
8
). Let us formalize this now:

Definition 2

(Hamiltonian/pp-wave correspondence)
.

Let
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
be an
n
-dimensional Hamiltonian system with
C^{2}
function
V(t,x)
defined on an open connected set

\displaystyle g\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=2dvdt-2V(t,x)(dt)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(dx^{i})^{2},

defined on

. Given any maximal solution
x(t)
of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
, the geodesics of

of the form
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
, with the same domain as

, are the

.

Note that because
\gamma
’s affine parameter

, its domain is indeed equal to
x(t)
’s. (This is always possible by a linear rescaling unless
\dot{t}(s)=0
, in which case
\gamma(s)
is a straight line and there is no input from
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
; we will therefore ignore this case henceforth.) We can easily obtain a one-to-one lift by fixing the starting point and the causal character of the geodesic — either spacelike, timelike, or lightlike — as follows (see
[
Eis28
]
,
[
CFS03a
, Proposition 3.1]
, and
[
Min07

for generalizations):

Proposition 3

(Eisenhart lift)
.

Let
V(t,x)
be a
C^{2}
function defined on an open connected set
I\times\mathscr{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
(\mathbb{R}\times I\times\mathscr{U},g)
its pp-wave lift. Then
x(t)
is the maximal solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
with initial data
(x_{0},\dot{x}_{0})
at time
t_{0}
if and only if
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
, with
v(t)
satisfying
\ddot{v}=2\frac{dV(t,x(t))}{dt}-\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\big|_{(t,x(t))}
and with initial data

\displaystyle\gamma(t_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=(0,t_{0},x_{0})\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pt\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Big(\!\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\dot{x}_{0}^{2}+V(t_{0},x_{0})+\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}},1,\dot{x}_{0}\Big),

(16)

is the geodesic lift of
x(t)
. The constant
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}
equals
0,-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}
depending on whether
\gamma(t)
is, respectively, lightlike, unit timelike, or unit spacelike.

Proof.

That the given
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
solves (
2
) is clear; it is maximal if and only if
x(t)
is maximal. In general, any such geodesic has initial velocity
\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0})=(\dot{v}_{0},1,\dot{x}_{0})
, but if we stipulate that
\gamma(t)
is, say, lightlike, then

g(\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0}),\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0}))=0\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt2\dot{v}_{0}-2V(t_{0},x_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\dot{x}_{0}^{i})^{2}=0.

Since
\dot{x}_{0}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\dot{x}_{0}^{i})^{2}
, solving for
\dot{v}_{0}
yields precisely (
16
) with
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}=0
. The unit-timelike and -spacelike cases (
g(\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0}),\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0}))=\mp 1
) are similar.
∎

Fixing
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}
thus gives a one-to-one lift. The choice of
v_{0}=0
in (
16
) is arbitrary and will play no role in what follows. We close this section by reviewing a particularly important aspect of the Eisenhart Lift, namely, that it allows one to exploit the
conformal invariance of lightlike geodesics
(see, e.g.,
[
CS08
,
MS08
]
). To that end, let us recall the following facts regarding the conformal geometry of semi-Riemannian metrics. Given a semi-Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
, and any metric
e^{2f}g
in its conformal class (with
f
a
C^{2}
function on
M
), an application of the Koszul formula shows that the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle f}_{\!}{}
of
e^{2f}g
is related to
\nabla
as follows: For any vector fields
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
,

\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle f}_{\!X}{Y}=\nabla_{\!X}{Y}+X(f)Y+Y(f)X-g(X,Y)\text{grad}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}f,

where
\text{grad}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}f
is the gradient of
f
with respect to
g
(in any choice of local coordinates,
\text{grad}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}f=g^{ij}f_{i}\partial_{j}
). The key difference between the conformal properties of Riemannian vs. semi-Riemannian metrics is that the gradient term vanishes when

vector field:

g(N,N)=0\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle f}_{\!N}{N}=\nabla_{\!N}{N}+2N(f)N.

In particular, if
N
has
g

\nabla_{\!N}{N}=0
, then

will be

-geodesic with respect to
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle f}_{\!}{}
; i.e.,
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle f}_{\!N}{N}
will be proportional to

g(N,N)=0\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \nabla_{\!N}{N}=0\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle f}_{\!N}{N}=2N(f)N.

Of course, this also holds for the covariant derivative along a curve
\gamma(t)
:

\displaystyle g(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})=0\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \nabla_{\!\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma^{\prime}}=0\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle f}_{\!\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma^{\prime}}=2\frac{d(f\circ\gamma)}{dt}\gamma^{\prime}.

(17)

The key property of pre-geodesics is that they can be rendered into geodesics by a reparametrization; this fact is well known, though we present its proof here for convenience, since we will call on this result in Theorem

below:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.25998pt}{\resizebox{284172}{294912}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\displaystyle f$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.25998pt}{\resizebox{284172}{294912}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\textstyle f$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.26pt}{\resizebox{253625}{294912}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.95277pt}{$\scriptstyle f$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.25998pt}{\resizebox{253626}{294912}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.68054pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}}}}}(s)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\gamma(\tau^{-1}(s))\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pts=\tau(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{2f(\gamma(r))}dr

is a lightl
C^{2}

.

Proof.

As

(Conformal class of a Hamiltonian system)

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{lcl}\ddot{v}&=&\!\!\!\Big(2\frac{dV}{ds}-V_{t}\,\dot{t}\Big)\dot{t}-2Vf_{t}\,\dot{t}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(2f_{i}\dot{v}-f_{t}\dot{x}^{i}\big)\dot{x}^{i},\\

&&\hskip 72.26999pt-2\dot{x}^{i}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}f_{j}\dot{x}^{j}-2f_{t}\,\dot{t}\dot{x}^{i}\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pti=1,\dots,n.\end{array}\right\}

Proof.

In the coordinate basis

e^{2f}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\

0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pte^{-2f}\begin{pmatrix}2V(t,x)&1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\cdot

Together, these yield the following nonvanishing Christoffel symbols:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{vi}^{v}=\Gamma_{ti}^{t}=\Gamma_{ii}^{i}=-\Gamma_{jj}^{i}=-\Gamma_{vt}^{i}=\Gamma_{ji}^{j}=f_{i}\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pt

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!-\Gamma_{ii}^{v}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{tt}^{t}=\Gamma_{it}^{i}=f_{t},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{tt}^{v}=2Vf_{t}-V_{t}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\Gamma_{ti}^{v}=-V_{i}\!\!\!

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt

3.
Curvature and the stability of Hamiltonian trajectories

Let us now consider
conjugate points
along
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
; i.e., those points along
\gamma(t)
at which “neighboring” geodesics accumulate. Projecting onto
x(t)
then gives conditions on
V
under which trajectories of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
will accumulate. Our treatment follows
[
FS03
]
— Theorems
2
and
3
below complement
[
FS03
, Proposition 6.4, Remark 6.5]
. (See also
[
CPC00
]
, where conjugate points are applied to chaos theory.)

Definition 3

(Conjugate point)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a semi-Riemannian manifold and
\gamma\colon[0,b]\longrightarrow M
a geodesic of any causal character. A
C^{2}
vector field
J(t)
along
\gamma(t)
is a
Jacobi field
if

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}+R_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(J,\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}\,\Big|_{t}=0,

(24)

where
R_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}
is the curvature endomorphism of
g
. The points
\gamma(0)
and
\gamma(b)
are
conjugate
if there is a nontrivial Jacobi field
J(t)
along
\gamma(t)
satisfying
J(0)=J(b)=0
. Their
multiplicity
is the dimension of the linear subspace of Jacobi fields
J(t)
along
\gamma(t)
satisfying
J(0)=J(b)=0
.

If
\gamma(b)
is conjugate to
\gamma(0)=(v(0),0,x(0))
, then there is a one-parameter family
\gamma_{s}(t)
of geodesics (with
\gamma_{0}(t)=\gamma(t)
) all starting at
\gamma(0)
and “accumulating” at
\gamma(b)
— i.e., the corresponding Jacobi field
J(t)
, which equals the infinitesimal variation
\frac{\partial\gamma_{s}(t)}{\partial s}\big|_{s=0}
of
\gamma_{s}(t)
from
\gamma(t)
, will satisfy
J(b)=0
(see Lemma
1
below, and note that
\gamma_{s}(b)
needn’t actually equal
\gamma(b)
). If
g
is a pp-wave, then projecting Definition
3
down to
x(t)
leads naturally to a notion of “accumulation point” for (
1
). This correspondence was formulated and studied in
[
FS03
]
(in fact for manifolds more general than pp-waves). The following is a special case of
[
FS03
, Definition 6.1]
:

Definition 4

(Conjugate point of a Hamiltonian trajectory)
.

Let
V(t,x)
be a
C^{2}
function,
x\colon[0,b]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
a nonconstant solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
, and
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
its geodesic lift (of any causal character). Then
x(b)
is conjugate to
x(0)
if
\gamma(b)
is conjugate to
\gamma(0)
as in Definition
3
.

Our first task is to show how conjugate points along
x(t)
give rise to a one-parameter family of
distinct
solutions of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
all starting at
x(0)
and accumulating at
x(b)
, and to make precise the notion of “accumulation point” just described:

Lemma 1
.

Let
x\colon[0,b]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonconstant solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
. If
x(b)
is conjugate to
x(0)
, then there is a one-parameter family of distinct solutions of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
starting at
x(0)
and accumulating at
x(b)
.

Proof.

By Definition
4
, the geodesic lift
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
of
x(t)
has
\gamma(b)
conjugate to
\gamma(0)
(the value of
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}
in (
16
) will not matter in what follows). Thus there is a nontrivial Jacobi field
J(t)
along
\gamma(t)
satisfying
J(0)=J(b)=0
. That
J(t)
is nontrivial implies that
J^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
; that it vanishes at two points implies that it must also be orthogonal to
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
(by a similar reasoning as in (
25
) below). Writing

J(t)=(J^{v}(t),J^{t}(t),J^{1}(t),\dots,J^{n}(t))

in the Brinkmann coordinates of Proposition
1
, let us start by observing that the
t
-component
J^{t}(t)=g(J,\partial_{v})\big|_{\gamma(t)}
must be linear in
t
because
\partial_{v}
is a parallel vector field:

\displaystyle\ddot{J}^{t}(t)=g(J^{\prime\prime},\partial_{v})\Big|_{\gamma(t)}\overset{\eqref{eqn:J1}}{=}-\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(J,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\partial_{v})\Big|_{\gamma(t)}=0.

(25)

But if
J(t)
is to be zero at two distinct points, then we must have
J^{t}(t)=0
. Next, the orthogonality condition,
g(J,\gamma^{\prime})\big|_{\gamma(t)}=0
, implies that
J^{v}(t)=-\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}J^{i}(t)\dot{x}^{i}(t)
, so that
J(t)
takes the form

J(t)=-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}J^{i}(t)\dot{x}^{i}(t)\Big)\partial_{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}J^{i}(t)\partial_{i}\,\Big|_{\gamma(t)}.

Taking now the covariant derivative of
J(t)
along
\gamma(t)
(and recalling (
13
)), it is straightforward to verify that

J^{\prime}(t)=\bigg[\!\!-\!\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}J^{i}(t)\dot{x}^{i}(t)\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}J^{i}(t)V_{i}(t,x(t))\bigg]\partial_{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{J}^{i}(t)\partial_{i}\,\Big|_{\gamma(t)}.

At
t=0
, this simplifies because each
J^{i}(0)=0
:

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(0)=-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{J}^{i}(0)\dot{x}^{i}(0)\Big)\partial_{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{J}^{i}(0)\partial_{i}\,\Big|_{\gamma(0)}\neq 0.

(26)

Now consider the one-parameter family of geodesics
\gamma_{s}(t)
defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{s}(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\text{exp}_{\gamma(0)}\big(t(\gamma^{\prime}(0)+sJ^{\prime}(0))\big)\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pts\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\hskip 3.61371pt,\hskip 3.61371ptt\in[0,b],

(27)

where
\text{exp}_{\gamma(0)}
is the exponential map and
\varepsilon
is small enough; note that
\gamma_{0}(t)=\gamma(t)
and that each
\gamma_{s}(t)
starts at
\gamma(0)=(v(0),0,x(0))
in the direction

\underbrace{\,\gamma^{\prime}(0)+sJ^{\prime}(0)\,}_{\text{$\gamma_{s}^{\prime}(0)$}}\overset{\eqref{eqn:J'}}{=}\Big(\dot{v}(0)-s\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{J}^{i}(0)\dot{x}^{i}(0)\Big)\partial_{v}+\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\dot{x}^{i}(0)+s\dot{J}^{i}(0)\big)\partial_{i}\,\Big|_{\gamma(t)}.

Furthermore,
\gamma_{s}(t)=(v_{s}(t),t,x_{s}(t))
(though
g(\gamma_{s}^{\prime},\gamma_{s}^{\prime})
may not equal
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}
), hence is a lift of a solution
x_{s}(t)
of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
with
x_{s}(0)=x(0)
and
\dot{x}_{s}(0)=\dot{x}(0)+s\dot{J}(0)
. As
\frac{\partial\gamma_{s}(t)}{\partial s}\big|_{s=0}=J(t)
(cf.
[
O’N83

),

indicates the accumulation of the

’s, and thus their

’s. By (
26
), some
\dot{J}^{i}(0)\neq 0
, hence each
\dot{x}_{s}(0)
(and thus each

) is distinct.
∎

With this established, let us now recall how conjugate points behave with respect to conformal transformations of
g
; our treatment follows
[
MS08
]
. First, observe that
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
itself is a Jacobi field along
\gamma(t)
; as a consequence, for any Jacobi field
J(t)
and any

along
\gamma(t)
,

\displaystyle(J+f\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime\prime}+\underbrace{\,R(J,\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}+f\cancelto{0}{R(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}}\,}_{\text{$R(J+f\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}$}}=\underbrace{\,(J^{\prime\prime}+R(J,\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime})\,}_{0}+f^{\prime\prime}\gamma^{\prime}.

(28)

For lightlike geodesics, (
28
) has the following consequence. First, consider the subspace
\mathscr{N}(\gamma)
of
C^{2}
vector fields along
\gamma(t)
that are orthogonal to
\gamma(t)
; as mentioned in Lemma
1
, this includes all Jacobi fields that vanish at two points along

. Now, if
\gamma(t)
is a

\gamma^{\prime}(t)\in\mathscr{N}(\gamma)

\mathscr{N}(\gamma)/\gamma^{\prime}
, whose equivalence classes are defined by
[J_{1}]=[J_{2}]
if and only if
J_{2}=J_{1}+f\gamma^{\prime}

along
\gamma(t)
. Furthermore, we can take derivatives of equivalence classes along
\gamma(t)
by defining, for any

[X]^{\prime}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=[X^{\prime}]\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\nabla_{\!\gamma^{\prime}}{[X]}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=[\nabla_{\!\gamma^{\prime}}{X}],

both of which are well defined when
\gamma(t)
is a lightlike geodesic. As a consequence, the Jacobi equation (
24

. Indeed, (

satisfies the Jacobi equation,

then every representative

satisfies the following “quotient Jacobi equation”:

[J_{2}]^{\prime\prime}+[R(J_{2},\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}]=[J_{2}^{\prime\prime}+R(J_{2},\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}]\overset{\eqref{eqn:J2}}{=}[f^{\prime\prime}\gamma^{\prime}]=0.

, the Jacobi equation effectively only sees the conformal structure. The following well known result confirms that this is indeed the case:

Proof.

We refer the reader to
[
MS08
, Theorem 2.36]
for a proof.
∎

With this in hand, we can add to the conclusions of Theorem
1
:

Corollary 1
.

In Theorem
1
, the conjugate points of the lightlike geodesic lifts, together with their multiplicities, are the same for the system of ODEs (
2
) and (
1
).

We now present, in Theorems
2
and
3
, two cases in which conjugate points exist; these complement
[
FS03
, Proposition 6.4, Remark 6.5]
, which gave a condition for their presence/absence in terms of the convexity of
V
’s Hessian and the sign of the pp-wave’s sectional curvature. Here we focus on the trace of
V
’s Hessian,
\Delta_{x}V
. Our first case assumes that the latter has a positive lower bound:

Theorem 2
.

Let
V(t,x)
be a
C^{2}
function and
x\colon[0,b]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
a nonconstant solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
. If
\Delta_{x}V\big|_{(t,x(t))}\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi^{2}}{b^{2}}
, then there is a one-parameter family of distinct solutions of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
beginning at
x(0)
and accumulating at some later point
x(t_{1})
, where
0<t_{1}\leq b
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
be the unit-timelike geodesic lift of
x(t)
; i.e., with
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}=-\frac{1}{2}
in (
16
). Because
\gamma(t)

[0,b]
, its length is
\int_{0}^{b}\sqrt{-g(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})}\,dt=\int_{0}^{b}dt=b
. Furthermore, the Ricci tensor
\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}
of the pp-wave lift
g
, evaluated at each
\gamma^{\prime}(t)=\dot{v}\partial_{v}+\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{x}^{i}\partial_{i}\big|_{\gamma(t)}
, is

\displaystyle\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})\Big|_{\gamma(t)}=\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})\Big|_{\gamma(t)}=\Delta_{x}V\Big|_{(t,x(t))}\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi^{2}}{b^{2}}\cdot

(29)

(In the Brinkmann coordinates (
8
),
\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})
is the only nonvanishing component of the Ricci tensor of

\Delta_{x}V
can be confirmed, e.g., in
[
LS16
]
, or via (
13
).) Via
[

, Lemma 23, p. 278]
, (

conjugate to
\gamma(0)
, with
0<t_{1}\leq b
. By Definition

,
x(t_{1})
is therefore conjugate to
x(0)
; by Lemma
1
, there is thus a one-parameter family of distinct solutions of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
beginning at
x(0)
and accumulating at
x(t_{1})
.
∎

Theorem
2
required the Laplacian
\Delta_{x}V
to be bounded away from zero. In fact one can still obtain conjugate points by assuming the function to be merely subharmonic along
x(t)
,
\Delta_{x}V\big|_{(t,x(t))}\geq 0
, provided
x(t)
can now be defined for all time. Here, then, is the second case:

Theorem 3
.

Let
V(t,x)
be a
C^{2}
function and
x(t)
a maximal, nonconstant solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
satisfying the following properties:

i.

There is a time
t_{0}
at which
\dot{x}^{i}(t_{0})=0
for some
i=1,\dots,n
,

ii.

V_{ij}\big|_{(t_{0},x(t_{0}))}\neq 0
for some
j=1,\dots,n
,

iii.

\Delta_{x}V\big|_{(t,x(t))}\geq 0
.

If
x(t)
is defined for all time, then there is a one-parameter family of distinct solutions of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
starting at some point
x(t_{1})
and accumulating at some later point
x(t_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t))
be the lightlike geodesic lift of
x(t)
; i.e., with
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}=0
in (
16
). Because
\dot{\gamma}^{i}(t_{0})=\dot{x}^{i}(t_{0})=0
, the vector
\partial_{i}|_{\gamma(t_{0})}
is orthogonal to
\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0})
, and

\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(\partial_{i},\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\partial_{j})\Big|_{\gamma(t_{0})}=\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{t},\partial_{t},\partial_{j})\Big|_{\gamma(t_{0})}=V_{ij}\Big|_{(t_{0},x(t_{0}))}\neq 0.

(Once again, this computation can be confirmed via
[
LS16
]
or (
13
).) Furthermore,
\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})\big|_{\gamma(t)}=\Delta_{x}V\big|_{(t,x(t))}\geq 0
, while
\gamma(t)
is defined for all time because its projection
x(t)
is assumed to be. By
[
BEE96
, Proposition 12.17, p. 444]
, these conditions come together to guarantee that
\gamma(t)
will have a pair of conjugate points. The result now follows once again from Lemma
1
. (Note that Proposition
5
also applies here, to yield conjugate points along lightlike solutions of the system (
1
).)
∎

4.
Eisenhart Lifts of Second-order complex ODEs

For our next application, we move to the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. Let
F\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function and consider the complex second-order ODE

\displaystyle\ddot{z}=F(z(t))\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptz=x+iy.

(30)

We would like to lift this ODE as with the Eisenhart lift, namely, as the geodesic equations of motion of a (real) pp-wave. As we now show, this can be done, but it will require four-dimensional “pp-waves” of signature
(-\!-\!++)
. Before defining such semi-Riemannian metrics, let us recast
F
:

Lemma 2
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function. Then there exists a harmonic function
V(x,y)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle F(z)=V_{x}+i(-V_{y}).

(31)

Proof.

Let us write
F
as

F(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y),

where, via the Cauchy-Riemann equations,
u
and
v
satisfy
u_{x}=v_{y}
and
u_{y}=-v_{x}
. To express
F
as in (
31
), we need
V
to satisfy
V_{x}=u,V_{y}=-v
, which is the case if and only if

u_{y}=V_{xy}=V_{yx}=-v_{x};

this the second of the Cauchy-Riemann equations. The first,
u_{x}=v_{y}
, then guarantees that
V
must be harmonic.
∎

As a consequence, the complex ODE (
30
) can be recast as

\displaystyle\ddot{x}=V_{x}(x(t),y(t))\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\ddot{y}=-V_{y}(x(t),y(t)).

(32)

It turns out that these lift to geodesics of the following metric:

Proposition 6

(Split-signature pp-wave)
.

On
\mathbb{R}^{4}=\{(v,t,x,y)\}
, consider the semi-Riemannian metric
g
of signature
(-\!-\!++)
defined by

\displaystyle g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$S$}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=2dvdt-2V(t,x,y)(dt)^{2}-(dx)^{2}+(dy)^{2},

(33)

where
V
is any
C^{2}
function independent of
v
. Then the geodesic equations of motion of
(\mathbb{R}^{4},g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$S$}})
are

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{lcl}\ddot{v}&=&\!\!\!\Big(2\frac{dV}{ds}-V_{t}\,\dot{t}\Big)\dot{t}\\
\ddot{t}&=&\!\!\!0,\\
\ddot{x}&=&\!\!\!V_{x}\,\dot{t}^{2},\\
\ddot{y}&=&\!\!\!-V_{y}\,\dot{t}^{2},\end{array}\right\}

where
V_{t}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial t},V_{i}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^{i}}
, and
s
is the affine parameter.

Proof.

This is similar to Proposition
2
; indeed, the nonvanishing Christoffel symbols of (
33
) are

\Gamma_{it}^{v}=-V_{i}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\Gamma_{tt}^{v}=-V_{t}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\Gamma_{tt}^{x}=-V_{x}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\Gamma_{tt}^{y}=V_{y},

the only difference being that
\Gamma_{tt}^{x}=-V_{x}
instead of
\Gamma_{tt}^{x}=V_{x}
, as would have been the case for a Lorentzian-signature pp-wave.
∎

(Such higher-signature metrics have also been considered for physical reasons, in
[
Gib20
]
.) The relationship between (
6
) and (
32
) is identical to that between (
2
) and (
14
), and thus we arrive at the analogue of Proposition
3
:

Proposition 7

(Complex version of Eisenhart lift)
.

Let
V(x,y)
be a harmonic function defined on an open connected set
\mathscr{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathscr{U},g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$S$}})
be the corresponding semi-Riemannian metric
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$S$}}
given by (
33
). Set
F\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=V_{x}+i(-V_{y})
. Then
z(t)=x(t)+iy(t)
is a maximal solution of the complex ODE
\ddot{z}=F(z(t))
with initial data
(z_{0},\dot{z}_{0})
at
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
\gamma(t)=(v(t),t,x(t),y(t))
, with
v(t)
satisfying
\ddot{v}=2\frac{dV(x(t),y(t))}{dt}
and with initial data

\displaystyle\gamma(t_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=(0,t_{0},z_{0})\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pt\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Big(\!\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\dot{z}_{0}^{2}+V(z_{0})+\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}},1,\dot{z}_{0}\Big)\cdot

(39)

is the geodesic lift of
x(t)
. The constant
\varepsilon_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\gamma$}}
equals
0,-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}
depending on whether
\gamma(t)
is, respectively, lightlike, unit timelike, or unit spacelike.
If
F
is entire but not linear in
x,y
, then there are solutions of
\ddot{z}=F(z(t))
and geodesics of
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$S$}}
that blow up in finite time.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Proposition
3
. The statement about blow-ups is due to
[
For96
, Corollary 7.4]
.
∎

We also have the analogue of Theorem
1
:

Theorem 4

(Conformal class of the complex ODE
\ddot{z}=F(z(t))
)
.

Let
V(x,y)
be a harmonic function defined on an open connected set
\mathscr{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathscr{U},g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$S$}})
be the corresponding semi-Riemannian metric
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$S$}}
given by (

). Set

. By Proposition

, there is a bijection between solutions
(z(t),z_{0},\dot{z}_{0})
of
\ddot{z}=F(z(t))
and lightlike geodesics
\gamma(t)
of

with initial data (

). By Proposition

and this bijection, each solution

is, after the reparametrization (

), also a solution of the geodesic equations of motion of the conformal metric

for any

on

. By Proposition

Let us close by mentioning that — as was already noted in
[
Eis28
]
(see also
[
CPC00
]
) — if we consider more general pp-wave metrics of the form

then we see that its geodesics are lifts of

Hamiltonian systems; i.e., of the form

with

By the analogue of Theorem

, these also “unfold” into an infinite family of “conformal ODEs” with shared solutions and accumulation points.

5.
Riemannian lifts dual to Eisenhart lifts

Finally, we show that, for Hamiltonian systems with
nonnegative, time-independent
functions, there is a Riemannian version of the Eisenhart lift that is “dual” to it in a sense that we make precise now. Thus, fix a
C^{2}
function
\widetilde{V}(x)\geq 0
and consider

\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}\widetilde{V}.

We claim that solutions of this
n
-dimensional Hamiltonian system lift to the geodesics of the
(n+2)
-dimensional
Riemannian
metric
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}}
defined by

g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g+2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptT\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Big(\!\!-\!V+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\partial_{v}-\partial_{t}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptV(t,x)\in\mathbb{R},

where
V(t,x)
is a
C^{2}
function to be determined, and
g
is a pp-wave in Brinkmann coordinates
(v,t,x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
:

\displaystyle((g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}})_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}2&-2V&0&\cdots&0\\
-2V&\frac{1}{2}(1+4V^{2})&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\cdot

(40)

Such metrics “dual to pp-waves” were considered in
[
AR22
]
and shown to be
almost-Kähler
metrics;
[
LS23
]
then showed that included among them are distinguished almost-Kähler metrics, namely, so called
extremal
and
second-Chern–Einstein
examples. Our interest here is solely in their geodesics, which were analyzed in
[
AR22
, Proposition 1]
. In the coordinates
(v,t,x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
, and with
s
the affine parameter, they simplify to

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ddot{v}&=&\!\!\frac{d}{ds}(V\dot{t}),\\
\ddot{t}&=&\!\!2c_{0}\Big(\!\frac{dV}{ds}-V_{t}\,\dot{t}\Big),\\
\ddot{x}^{i}&=&\!\!-c_{0}V_{i}\,\dot{t},\\
\end{array}\right\}

where the constant
c_{0}
is the constant of the motion obtained via the Killing vector field
\partial_{v}
,

\displaystyle c_{0}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}}(\partial_{v},\gamma^{\prime}(s))=2\dot{v}-2V\dot{t}\,\Big|_{s},

(45)

with the geodesic
\gamma(s)=(v(s),t(s),x(s))
satisfying (
5
). In fact
c_{0}
can also be obtained by integrating
\ddot{v}
; with initial data
(v_{0},t_{0},x_{0})
and
(\dot{v}_{0},\dot{t}_{0},\dot{x}_{0})
,

\dot{v}-\dot{v}_{0}=V\dot{t}-V(t_{0},x_{0})\dot{t}_{0}\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\dot{v}-V\dot{t}\,\Big|_{s}=\dot{v}_{0}-V(t_{0},x_{0})\dot{t}_{0}\overset{\eqref{eqn:v_end}}{=}\frac{c_{0}}{2}\cdot

Given that
\ddot{x}=-c_{0}\,\dot{t}\,\nabla_{\!x}V
in (
5
) resembles a Hamiltonian system, we will be interested in the case when
c_{0}\neq 0
. Next, comparing (
5
) with (
2
), observe that the
\ddot{v}
-equation is once again redundant (there being no appearance of
v
or
\dot{v}
terms), but that now the
t
-coordinate is no longer linearly related to the affine parameter
s
, as it was in (
2
):

\dot{t}=\dot{t}_{0}+2c_{0}\!\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\underbrace{\Big(\frac{dV}{ds}-V_{t}\,\dot{t}\Big)}_{\text{$\nabla_{\!x}V\cdot\dot{x}$}}ds=\dot{t}_{0}+2c_{0}\!\!\underbrace{\,\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\nabla_{\!x}V\cdot\dot{x}\,ds\,}_{\text{$\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.72334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.72334pt}{$\cdot$}}=F(s)$}}.

In particular, if
V_{t}=0
, then
F(s)=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{dV}{ds}\,ds=V(x(s))-V(x_{0})
, so that, setting
c_{1}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\dot{t}_{0}}{2}-c_{0}V(x_{0})
, the
\ddot{x}
-equation becomes

\displaystyle\ddot{x}=-c_{0}\big(2c_{0}V(x(s))+2c_{1}\big)\nabla_{\!x}V(x(s))=-\nabla_{\!x}(c_{0}V+c_{1})^{2}.

(46)

We therefore have the following “Riemannian Eisenhart Lift,” lifting (time-independent) potentials of the form
V^{2}
to their “square roots”:

Theorem 5

(Riemannian “square root” of a time-independent Hamiltonian)
.

Let
V(x)
be a
C^{2}
function globally defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any choice of constants
c_{0},c_{1}
, every solution
x(s)
of

\displaystyle\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}(c_{0}V+c_{1})^{2}

(47)

lifts to a geodesic
\gamma(s)=(\gamma^{v}(s),\gamma^{t}(s),x(s))
of the
(n+2)
-dimensional Riemannian metric (
40
) defined via
V(x)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
.

Proof.

Let
x(s)
have initial data
x(s_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=x_{0},\dot{x}(s_{0})\!\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\dot{x}_{0}
. Given
c_{0},c_{1}
in (
47
), choose
\dot{t}_{0}=2c_{0}V(x_{0})+2c_{1}
and
\dot{v}_{0}=V(x_{0})\dot{t}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}c_{0}.

Then for any choice of
v_{0},t_{0}
, the geodesic
\gamma(s)
of (
40
) starting at
\gamma(s_{0})=(v_{0},t_{0},x_{0})
with initial velocity
\gamma^{\prime}(s_{0})=(\dot{t}_{0},\dot{v}_{0},\dot{x}_{0})
will, as we saw in (
46
), have
x^{i}
-components
\gamma^{i}(s)
satisfying
\ddot{\gamma}^{i}=-\partial_{i}(c_{0}V+c_{1})^{2}
. This is precisely (
47
).
∎

Regarding the domains of
x(s)
and
\gamma(s)
: By
[
Gor70
,
Ebi70
]
, it is well known that
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}(c_{0}V+c_{1})^{2}
always has complete solutions when
V(x)
is globally defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, as shown in
[
AR22
, Proposition 1]
, (
40
) is always geodesically complete when
V(t,x)
is globally defined (even if
V_{t}\neq 0
). Let us now show that, for the following class of generalized Hamiltonian systems, the “Riemannian Eisenhart Lift” of Theorem
5
yields a two-point boundary result and generalizes the well known conservation of energy equation:

Theorem 6

(Generalized Hamiltonian system)
.

Let
V(t,x)
be a
C^{2}
function globally defined on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any two points
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exist a constant
c
and a
C^{2}
function
\tau(t)
, both depending on
x_{0},x_{1}
, such that the second-order ODE

\displaystyle\ddot{x}=-2\Big(c+\!\!\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla_{\!x}\widetilde{V}\cdot\dot{x})\,dt\Big)\nabla_{\!x}\widetilde{V}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\widetilde{V}(t,x)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=V(\tau(t),x),

(48)

has a complete solution
x(t)
passing through
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\dot{x}(t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\dot{\tau}(t)^{2}=\emph{\text{const.}}

(49)

If
V_{t}=0
, then (
48
) reduces to the Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}(V+\bar{c})^{2}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\bar{c}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=c-V(x_{0}),

(50)

and (
49
) the conservation of energy equation for the potential
(V+\bar{c})^{2}
.

Proof.

As mentioned above, for any
C^{2}
function
V(t,x)
globally defined on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the corresponding Riemannian manifold
(\mathbb{R}^{n+2},g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}})
defined by (
40
) is geodesically complete. By the Hopf-Rinow Theorem, any two points in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
can thus be connected by a
C^{2}
-geodesic segment of
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}}
(in fact a length-minimizing one with respect to the Riemannian distance function
d_{\text{$g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}}$}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
induced by
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}}
; see, e.g.,
[
Lee18
, Corollary 6.21]
). Let us take
(0,0,x_{0}),(v_{1},1,x_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, where
v_{1}
is any number such that

\displaystyle v_{1}\neq\int_{0}^{1}V(u,(x_{1}-x_{0})u+x_{0})\,du.

(51)

Now let
\gamma(s)=(\gamma^{v}(s),\gamma^{t}(s),\gamma^{1}(s),\dots,\gamma^{n}(s))\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\gamma^{v}(s),\gamma^{t}(s),\gamma^{x}(s))
be a geodesic of
(\mathbb{R}^{n+2},g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}})
connecting
(0,0,x_{0})
and
(v_{1},1,x_{1})
; by linearly rescaling the affine parameter
s
if necessary, we may ensure that
\gamma(0)=(0,0,x_{0})
and
\gamma(1)=(v_{1},1,x_{1})
(note that a linear rescaling of
s
does not affect the status of
\gamma(s)
as a geodesic of
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{$R$}}
). Recalling (
5
) and (
45
), the geodesic
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, which has some initial velocity
\dot{\gamma}(0)=(\dot{\gamma}^{v}_{0},\dot{\gamma}^{t}_{0},\dot{\gamma}^{1}_{0},\dots,\dot{\gamma}^{n}_{0})
, satisfies

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ddot{\gamma}^{v}&=&\!\!\frac{d}{ds}(V\dot{\gamma}^{t}),\\
\ddot{\gamma}^{t}&=&\!\!2c_{0}\Big(\frac{dV}{ds}-V_{t}\,\dot{\gamma}^{t}\Big),\\
\ddot{\gamma}^{i}&=&\!\!-c_{0}V_{i}\,\dot{\gamma}^{t},\\
\end{array}\right\}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptc_{0}=2\dot{\gamma}^{v}_{0}-2V(0,x_{0})\dot{\gamma}^{t}_{0}.

Integrating
\ddot{\gamma}^{t}
yields

\dot{\gamma}^{t}=\dot{\gamma}^{t}_{0}+2c_{0}\int_{0}^{s}(\nabla_{\!x}V\cdot\dot{\gamma}^{x})\,ds
, and thus

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}^{x}=-c_{0}\Big(\dot{\gamma}^{t}_{0}+2c_{0}\!\!\int_{0}^{s}(\nabla_{\!x}V\cdot\dot{\gamma}^{x})\,ds\Big)\nabla_{\!x}V(\gamma^{t}(s),\gamma^{x}(s)).

(56)

We now show that, by a further rescaling of
\gamma(s)
if necessary,
c_{0}
can always be chosen to equal
1
. To begin with, suppose that
\dot{\gamma}^{v}_{0}
and
\dot{\gamma}^{t}_{0}
of
\gamma(s)
are such that
c_{0}=0
; then
\gamma^{t}(s)=s
and

\gamma^{x}(s)=(x_{1}-x_{0})s+x_{0}\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pt\dot{\gamma}^{v}(s)=V(s,(x_{1}-x_{0})s+x_{0})+\underbrace{\,\dot{\gamma}^{v}_{0}-V(0,x_{0})\,}_{\text{$=0$ by \eqref{eqn:c=0}}}.

In particular,
v_{1}=\gamma^{v}(1)=\int_{0}^{1}V((x_{1}-x_{0})u+x_{0})du
, which is impossible by (
51
). Having thus ensured that
c_{0}\neq 0
, the linear rescaling
\tilde{\gamma}(s)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\gamma(c_{0}^{-1}s)
yields

\tilde{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)=c_{0}^{-1}\gamma^{\prime}(0)\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\tilde{c}_{0}\overset{\eqref{eqn:c=0}}{=}2\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}^{v}_{0}-2V(0,x_{0})\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}^{t}_{0}=1.

Thus
\tilde{\gamma}(s)
passes through
\tilde{\gamma}(0)=(0,0,x_{0})
and
\tilde{\gamma}(c_{0})=(v_{1},1,x_{1})
and has
\tilde{c}_{0}=1
. Inserting this information into (
56
) and changing notation
s\mapsto t
yields (
48
) with
c\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}^{t}_{0}/2,\tau(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\tilde{\gamma}^{t}(t)
, and
x(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\tilde{\gamma}^{x}(t)
. In particular,
x(t)
passes through
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, and is complete because
\tilde{\gamma}(s)
is so. We now verify the constant of the motion (
49
). Replacing
\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}^{t}
with
\dot{\tau}
and
\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}^{i}
with
\dot{x}^{i}
in (
5
), note that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ddot{x}^{i}\dot{x}^{i}=-\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\,\dot{\tau}\,\dot{x}^{i}=-\dot{\tau}\Big(\frac{dV}{ds}-V_{t}\,\dot{\tau}\Big)=-\frac{1}{2}\ddot{\tau}\dot{\tau},

from which (
49
) follows. Finally, if
V_{t}=0
, then
\dot{\tau}=\dot{\tau}_{0}+2\big(V(x(s))-V(x_{0})\big)
, hence
\frac{1}{4}\dot{\tau}^{2}=(V+\bar{c})^{2}
with
\bar{c}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\dot{\tau}_{0}}{2}-V(x_{0})=c-V(x_{0})
. The remainder of the proof now follows.

By way of comparison, recall that the “Bolza two-point boundary problem” for a Hamiltonian system asks whether, given two points
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists at least one solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
connecting
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
. Although this is a long-standing question, it was, to the best of our knowledge, solved only recently for time-independent potentials, in
[
BGS02
, Corollary 4.1]
. Specifically, using Jacobi metrics and conservation of energy
E=\frac{1}{2}|\dot{x}(t)|^{2}+V(x(t))
,
[
BGS02
, Corollary 4.1]
showed, for each
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the existence of an energy
E_{0}>0
such that, for all
E\geq E_{0}
, there is a solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
with energy
E
from
x_{0}
to
x_{1}
. (For results prior to this, see, e.g.,
[
EGT96
, Theorem 1]
and
[
Bol99
, Theorem 2]
.) As for the time-dependent case,
[
CFS03b
, Theorem 1.1]
showed that for a given
C^{1}
-potential
V\colon[0,\delta]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, if there exist
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},p\in[0,2)
and
\lambda,\mu,k\in\mathbb{R}
such that
V(t,x)
satisfies the at-most-quadratic inequality

V(t,x)\leq\lambda d_{0}(x,\bar{x})^{2}+\mu d_{0}(x,\bar{x})^{p}+k\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptd_{0}(x,\bar{x})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=|x-\bar{x}|,

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then there is a solution of
\ddot{x}=-\nabla_{\!x}V
connecting any two distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(in fact this result also applies to any complete Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with distance function
d_{\scalebox{0.6}{$g$}}
in place of
d_{0}
).
