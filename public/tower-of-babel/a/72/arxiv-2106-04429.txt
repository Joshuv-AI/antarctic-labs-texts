Conic decomposition of a toric variety and its application to cohomology
Seonjeong Park, Jongbaek Song

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of a conic sequence of a convex polytope. It is a way of building up a polytope starting from a vertex and attaching faces one by one with certain regulations. We apply this to a toric variety to obtain an iterated cofibration structure on it. This allows us to prove several vanishing results in the rational cohomology of a toric variety and to calculate Poincaré polynomials for a large class of singular toric varieties.

1.
Introduction

Let
P
be a convex polytope of dimension
n
and
f(P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
its face vector, i.e.,
f_{i}
is the number of
i
-dimensional faces of
P
. In literature, the vector
f(P)
is often called the
f
-vector
or the
face vector
of
P
. If
P
is a simple polytope, one can associate the
h
-vector

h(P)=(h_{0},\dots,h_{n})
defined by the following equation

(1.1)

\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{k}(x-1)^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}h_{k}x^{k}.

From the geometric point of view, if
P
is a full dimensional lattice polytope in
M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
for some
n
-dimensional lattice
M
, one can associate a projective toric variety
X_{P}
of complex dimension
n
whose orbit space with respect to the action of the
n
-dimensional compact torus
T^{n}
is combinatorially equivalent to
P
. In particular, if
P
is a simple lattice polytope, the corresponding toric variety
X_{P}
is a rationally smooth projective toric variety, which could be understood as a
toric orbifold

[
DJ91
,
PS10
,
BSS17
]
. In this case, the singular cohomology of
X_{P}
with rational coefficients vanishes in odd degrees. Moreover, plugging
t^{2}
into
x
in the right-hand side of (
1.1
), we get the Poincaré polynomial
\Poin(X_{P},t)
of
X_{P}
, namely
h_{k}
is the rank of the
2k
-th cohomology group. In other words, the Betti numbers of a rationally smooth toric variety are completely determined by its underlying combinatorics. This is one of the key observations in Stanley’s proof of the
g
-conjecture, we refer to
[
McM71
,
Sta80
]
.

If we turn our attention to more general lattice polytopes other than simple polytopes, then the associated projective toric varieties are no longer rationally smooth. In general, for a singular projective variety, we often consider the intersection cohomology
\mathit{IH}^{\ast}(-)
because it carries geometric and topological properties of singular strata. Moreover, there are certain similarities between
\mathit{IH}^{\ast}(-)
and the singular cohomology of a smooth variety, for instance
Poincaré duality or the hard Lefschetz property. Especially for a projective toric variety
X_{P}
, the Betti numbers of
\mathit{IH}^{\ast}(X_{P})
are combinatorial invariants. Indeed, Stanley
[
Sta87
]
defines the
toric
h
-polynomial
of
P
, which agrees with (
1.1
) when
P
is a simple polytope, and he shows that the coefficients in the toric
h
-polynomial give the Betti numbers of
\mathit{IH}^{\ast}(X_{P})
.

However, if we stick to the singular cohomology of a toric variety with singularities beyond orbifold singularities, it is somehow more subtle to deal with than the intersection cohomology because it is far from the Poincaré duality, as well as its rational cohomology is not concentrated in even degrees in general. For instance, the
6
-dimensional toric variety associated with the
3
-dimensional cross polytope, that is the convex hull of
(\pm 1,0,0),(0,\pm 1,0)
and
(0,0,\pm 1)
in
\mathbb{Z}^{3}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
, has a nontrivial rational cohomology class in degree
3
. We refer to
[
CLS11
, Example 12.3.8]
whose computation is based on the results in
[
Fis92
,
Jor98
]
. Moreover, Barthel, et. al.
[
BBFK96
, Example 3.5]
provide two
6
-dimensional toric varieties
X
and
X^{\prime}
associated with combinatorially equivalent polytopes such that
\rank H^{3}(X;\mathbb{Q})\neq\rank H^{3}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})
. This tells us that the Betti numbers are neither combinatorial invariants in general, nor obtainable by the
h
-vector defined in (
1.1
).

The aim of this paper is to explore the category of toric varieties other than toric orbifolds whose Betti numbers for the singular cohomology can be calculated by the underlying combinatorics. Our main idea is to consider a topological stratification

(1.2)

\{\text{a point}\}=X_{1}\subset X_{2}\subset\cdots\subset X_{P}

on
X_{P}
such that each stratum is a mapping cone, see (
3.3
). We call such a stratification a
conic decomposition
of
X_{P}
, which is named after the notion of a conic decomposition of a map
f\colon X\to Y
introduced by Jeffrey A. Strom.
In this case, we consider the map
\{\text{a point}\}\hookrightarrow X_{P}
, regarding
X_{P}
as a pointed space. A conic decomposition of
X_{P}
can be parametrized by a sequence of certain subspaces in
P
, which we call a
conic sequence
of
P
. Beginning with
P
itself as the initial term, the sequence continues inductively by deleting faces whose geometric realization agrees with a cone on a polytope if it has, and the sequence stops otherwise. We discuss explicit combinatorial and topological definitions of a conic sequence of
P
in Section
2
.

We emphasize again that each stratum
X_{i}
in (
1.2
) is a mapping cone of a map
\Phi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to X_{i-1}
for some topological space
Y_{i}
. Hence, the topology of
X_{i}
is dominated by the topology of
Y_{i}
together with the attaching map
\Phi_{i}
. Accordingly, if a toric variety
X_{P}
admits a conic decomposition, then one can study the topology of
X_{P}
by analyzing each cofiber sequence

(1.3)

Y_{i}\xrightarrow{\Phi_{i}}X_{i-1}\to X_{i}

successively.

In Section
3
, we focus on the case where the conic sequence of
P
is given by iterated deletions of the cones of simple polytopes, where we denote these simple polytopes by
C_{i}
’s throughout this paper. This is equivalent to say that
Y_{i}
in (
1.3
) is an orbifold
S^{1}
-fibration over a toric orbifold corresponding to
C_{i}
(Lemma
3.2
). A particular example of this setup is the case where each
C_{i}
is given by a simplex, which has been introduced in
[
BSS17
]
and further studied in subsequent papers
[
BSS19
,
BNSS21
,
SS21
,
LMPS21
]
, where the conic sequence of this particular type is referred as a
retraction sequence
.

In Section
4
, we use a conic decomposition of
X_{P}
to calculate the cohomology group of
X_{P}
with rational coefficients. We first see how the cohomology group of
Y_{i}
affects the cohomology of
X_{i}
. Then we prove several vanishing theorems for the cases discussed in Section
3
. In particular, we verify in Theorem
4.5
that a certain class of toric varieties including all toric orbifolds enjoys (
1.1
) to obtain their Poincaré polynomials.

2.
Conic sequence of a convex polytope

In this section, we introduce the notion of a conic sequence of a convex polytope.
A polytopal complex
\mathcal{P}
is a finite collection of polytopes in an Euclidean space such that

(i)

the empty polytope is in
\mathcal{P}
,

(ii)

if
E\in\mathcal{P}
, then all the faces of
E
are also in
\mathcal{P}
, and

(iii)

the intersection
E\cap F
of two polytopes
E,F\in\mathcal{P}
is a face both of
E
and of
F
.

The dimension
\dim\mathcal{P}
is the largest dimension of a polytope in
\mathcal{P}
. The geometric realization of
\mathcal{P}
is the point set
|\mathcal{P}|\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{P}}E
.
The combinatorial structure of a polytopal complex
\mathcal{P}
is captured by its face poset
L(\mathcal{P}):=(\mathcal{P},\subseteq)
, that is, the finite set of polytopes in
\mathcal{P}
, ordered by inclusion. We define two polytopal complexes to be combinatorially equivalent if their face posets are isomorphic as posets. We refer to
[
Zie95
, Chapter 5]
for more details.
In what follows, we assume that
\mathcal{P}
is of dimension
n
otherwise stated and we denote by
\ell
the number of all the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
.

We now define a sequence
\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\ell}
of polytopal subcomplexes
\mathcal{P}_{i}
of
\mathcal{P}
inductively as follows. For technical reasons, we construct the sequence with decreasing indices. We set the initial term
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
by
\mathcal{P}
. Given
\mathcal{P}_{i}
for
i\leq\ell
, we take a vertex
v_{i}
of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
such that there exists a unique maximal element
E_{i}
in the subposet
\{F\in L(\mathcal{P}_{i})\mid v_{i}\subseteq F\}
. We call this vertex
v_{i}
a
cone vertex
of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
. Then we define the next term
\mathcal{P}_{i-1}
by

(2.1)

\mathcal{P}_{i-1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathcal{P}_{i}-[v_{i},E_{i}],

where
[v_{i},E_{i}]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{F\in\mathcal{P}_{i}\mid v_{i}\subseteq F\subseteq E_{i}\}
. We call the sequence
\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\ell}
defined as above a
conic sequence
of
\mathcal{P}
if
the sequence ends up with
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{\emptyset,v_{1}\}
for some vertex
v_{1}
of
\mathcal{P}
. If in particular
\mathcal{P}
is the complex associated with a polytope
P
, namely the complex of all faces of
P
, then we simply call the sequence
\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\ell}
a conic sequence of a polytope

.

3.
Conic decomposition of a toric variety

We begin this section with a topological model of a projective toric variety
X_{P}
associated with a lattice polytope
P
, following
[
Jor98
, Chapter 1]
which is attributed to MacPherson. This gives us a way to understand the underlying topological space of
X_{P}
. We also investigate several vanishing theorems for the singular cohomology of the toric variety
X_{P}
, associated with a conic polytope
P
.

3.1.
Topological model of a toric variety

Let
M
be the character lattice of the
n
-dimensional compact torus
T^{n}
. We consider an
n
-dimensional lattice polytope
P
in
M_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathcal{F}(P)
be the set of facets of
P
and
\lambda(F)
be the primitive outward normal vector of
F\in\mathcal{F}(P)
. Regarding
\lambda(F)
as an element in the lattice
N
of one-parameter subgroups of
T^{n}
, we denote by
T_{F}
the circle subgroup of
T^{n}
generated by
\lambda(F)
. In general, given a face
E
of
P
,
we denote by
N_{E}
the submodule generated by

(3.1)

\{\lambda(F)\in N\mid F\in\mathcal{F}(P),~F\cap E\neq\emptyset\}.

Then
N_{E}
determines the subtorus
T_{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}N_{E}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}/\left((N_{E}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R})\cap N\right)
of
T^{n}
.

The topological model of the toric variety
X_{P}
associated with a lattice polytope
P
is given

(3.2)

X_{P}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(P\times T^{n})/_{\sim},

where we identify
(p,t)\sim(q,s)
whenever
p=q
and
t^{-1}s\in T_{E}
if
p
is in the relative interior of
E
. Notice that the action of
T^{n}
on
X_{P}
given by the multiplication on the second factor induces the orbit map
\pi\colon X_{P}\to P
, that is the projection onto the first factor.

If
P
is a simple lattice polytope, then each face
E
of codimension-
k
in
P
is the transversal intersection of
k
facets of
P
by definition. Hence the elements in (
3.1
) are linearly independent. In this case, the resulting toric variety
X_{P}
has at worst finite quotient singularities and
X_{P}
is often called a toric orbifold, see
[
CLS11
, Theorem 11.4.8]
for instance. We also refer to
[
DJ91
, Section 7]
and
[
PS10
]
for more rigorous topological interpretation of a toric orbifold.

3.2.
Toric variety with a conic decomposition

In what follows, we mainly consider a conic lattice polytope
P
in
M_{\mathbb{R}}
. We may regard each
C_{i}\in\mathscr{C}_{\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}}
as a rational polytope lying in an affine subspace of
M_{\mathbb{R}}
. This yields the following diagram, which we call a
conic decomposition
of
X_{P}
;

(3.3)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{i+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{\ell}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{i-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\ell-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\ell}=X,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{i+1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{\ell}}

where
X_{i}=\pi^{-1}(P_{i})
and
Y_{i}=\pi^{-1}(C_{i})
with respect to the orbit map
\pi\colon X_{P}\to P
. Moreover, the attaching map
\phi_{i}
defined in (
2.3
) induces a map
\Phi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to X_{i-1}
by the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{i}\times T^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(C_{i}\times T^{n})/_{\sim}=Y_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{i-1}\times T^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(P_{i-1}\times T^{n})/_{\sim}=X_{i-1}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{i}\times id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{i}}

This leads us to understand each
X_{i}
as the cofiber of
\Phi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to X_{i-1}
.

The next two lemmas describe the topology of
Y_{i}
when (i)
C_{i}
is a simplex or (ii)
C_{i}
is a simple polytope more generally.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
C_{i}
is a simplex
\Delta^{d_{i}-1}
for some
d_{i}\geq 1
, then
Y_{i}
is homeomorphic to
S^{2d_{i}-1}/K_{i}
for some finite group
K_{i}
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as the proof of
[
BNSS21
, Propsotion 4.4]
and
[
SS21
, Corollary 3.7]
. However, for the reader’s convenience, we shall provide a sketch of the proof. Consider the conic decomposition in (
3.3
), which gives us

X_{i}-X_{i-1}=\pi^{-1}(P_{i})-\pi^{-1}(P_{i-1})\cong\pi^{-1}(U_{i}),

where
U_{i}=P_{i}-P_{i-1}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}_{\geq}
as a manifold with corners because
C_{i}=\Delta^{d_{i}-1}
. Then (
3.2
) implies that

\pi^{-1}(U_{i})\cong U_{i}\times T^{d_{i}}/_{\sim}\cong D^{2d_{i}}/K_{i}

for some finite subgroup
K_{i}
of
T^{d_{i}+1}
acting linearly on
D^{2d_{i}}
. Since
X_{i}
is a mapping cone of the map
\Phi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to X_{i-1}
, we conclude that

Y_{i}\cong S^{2d_{i}-1}/K_{i}
as desired.
∎

We notice that the space
S^{2d_{i}-1}/K_{i}
in Lemma
3.1
above is called an
orbifold lens space
in
[
BSS17
]
. In Lemma
3.2
below, we consider the case where
C_{i}
is a simple polytope. We refer the readers to
[
Rua02
]
for the rigorous definition of an orbifold fiber bundle.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
C_{i}
is a simple polytope, then
Y_{i}
is an orbifold
S^{1}
-fiber bundle over a toric orbifold.

Proof.

Let
E_{i}
be the face of
P
defined in (
2.1
). Then it is the minimal face of
P
containing
C_{i}
. Since the equivalence relation
\sim
given in (
3.2
) collapses
T_{E_{i}}
globally on
C_{i}
, we may write

Y_{i}=(C_{i}\times T^{n}/T_{E_{i}})/_{\sim}.

Take an affine subspace
\mathcal{A}_{C_{i}}\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}
intersecting
E_{i}
transversely such that
C_{i}
is a rational polytope in
\mathcal{A}_{C_{i}}
with respect to the lattice
\mathcal{A}_{C_{i}}\cap M
. Let
\mu_{C_{i}}\in N\setminus N_{E_{i}}
be a primitive element which determines
\mathcal{A}_{C_{i}}
. Then the submodule
N_{C_{i}}\subset N
, generated by all elements of
N_{E_{i}}
together with
\mu_{C_{i}}
, gives rise to the torus

T_{C_{i}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}N_{C_{i}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}/\left((N_{C_{i}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R})\cap N\right)

with
\dim T_{C_{i}}=\dim T_{E_{i}}+1
. Accordingly, we have
\dim T^{n}/T_{E_{i}}=\dim T^{n}/T_{C_{i}}+1
.

Let
S^{1}_{\mu_{C_{i}}}
be the image of the composition

(3.4)

S^{1}\xrightarrow{\mu_{C_{i}}}T^{n}\twoheadrightarrow T^{n}/T_{E_{i}}

regarding
\mu_{C_{i}}
as a one-parameter subgroup of
T^{n}
.
We consider the
S^{1}
-action on
Y_{i}
given by (
3.4
). Then we claim that the corresponding orbit map

Y_{i}\twoheadrightarrow Y_{i}/S^{1}_{\mu_{C_{i}}}.

is the desired orbifold
S^{1}
-fiber bundle over a toric orbifold.
Since
\mu_{C_{i}}\in N\setminus N_{E_{i}}
, one can see that

(3.5)

Y_{i}/S^{1}_{\mu_{C_{i}}}\cong(C_{i}\times T^{n}/T_{C_{i}})/_{\sim},

where the identification
\sim
is the one induced from (
3.2
).
Since
C_{i}
is a simple polytope by the assumption and
P_{i}
is the mapping cone of the map
\phi_{i}\colon C_{i}\to P_{i-1}
such that

P_{i}-P_{i-1}=\bigcup_{F\in[v_{i},E_{i}]}\relint(F),

any vertex of
C_{i}
, say
w
, is the transversal intersection of
C_{i}
and an edge of
E_{i}
, namely

w=C_{i}\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{\dim C_{i}}(E_{i}\cap F_{i_{j}})

for some facets
F_{i_{j}}
’s of
P
. This implies that the image

\{\rho_{i}(\lambda(F_{i_{j}}))\mid j=1,\dots,\dim C_{i}\}

with respect to the projection
\rho_{i}\colon N\twoheadrightarrow N/N_{C_{i}}
is linearly independent, and this is true for each vertex of
C_{i}
. Therefore,
Y_{i}/S^{1}_{\mu_{C_{i}}}
in (
3.5
) is a toric orbifold, which establishes the claim.
∎

4.
Application to singular cohomology

In this section, we prove several vanishing theorems for the singular cohomology of a toric variety
X_{P}
with a conic decomposition. In particular, we prove that if
P
admits a
\Delta
-conic sequence, then the (cohomology) Betti numbers of
X_{P}
are completely determined by the face numbers of
P
. Throughout this section, we mean by
H^{\ast}(-)
the singular cohomology with rational coefficients.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X_{P}
be a projective toric variety and
P
the associated polytope. If there exists a conic sequence on
P
such that
\widetilde{H}^{2k}(Y_{i})=0
for all
i=2,\dots,\ell
, then
H^{2k+1}(X)=0
.

Proof.

Consider the conic decomposition in (
3.3
) induced from the given conic sequence on
P
. We prove the claim by the induction on the indices of the following filtration

(4.1)

X_{1}\hookrightarrow X_{2}\hookrightarrow\cdots\hookrightarrow X_{\ell-1}\hookrightarrow X_{\ell}=X

induced from the conic decomposition of
X_{P}
as in (
3.3
).

First, since
P_{1}
is a vertex of
P
,
X_{1}
is zero-dimensional. Since
P_{2}
is an edge of
P
,
X_{2}=\pi^{-1}(P_{2})
is homeomorphic to
S^{2}
, which means
H^{odd}(X_{2})
vanishes. We now claim that
H^{2k+1}(X_{i})=0
, provided
H^{2k+1}(X_{i-1})=0
for
i>2
.
Since each composition

(4.2)

Y_{i}\xrightarrow{\Phi_{i}}X_{i-1}\to X_{i}

is a cofibration, we have the following long exact sequence of the cohomology groups

(4.3)

\cdots\to\widetilde{H}^{j-1}(Y_{i})\to\widetilde{H}^{j}(X_{i})\to\widetilde{H}^{j}(X_{i-1})\to\widetilde{H}^{j}(Y_{i})\to\cdots.

Thus, when
j=2k+1
for some
k>0
, the assumption of
H^{2k}(Y_{i})=0
together with the induction hypothesis establishes the claim.
∎

We now introduce several applications of Theorem
4.1
. Recall from Lemma
3.2
that if
P
admits a conic sequence such that
\mathscr{C}_{\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}}
consists of simple polytopes, then each
Y_{i}
is an orbifold
S^{1}
-fiber bundle over a toric orbifold. The following proposition has been studied in
[
Luo13
]
, where the author mainly discusses smooth cases. However, if we restrict our attention to the singular cohomology with rational coefficients, then the same statement holds for an orbifold
S^{1}
-fiber bundle over a toric orbifold. The main tool for the proof is the Gysin sequence. We refer to
[
PS10
, Section 5]
for the Gysin sequence in the orbifold setup.

Proposition 4.2
.

[
Luo13
, Remark 2.5.10, Theorem 2.5.48]

Let
C_{i}
be a simple polytope and
Y_{i}=\pi^{-1}(C_{i})
as above. Then
H^{k}(Y_{i})=0
if
k
is even with
k>\dim C_{i}
or if
k
is odd with
k\leq\dim C_{i}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
P
be a lattice polytope admitting a conic sequence as in (
2.4
) such that
\mathscr{C}_{\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}}
consists of simple polytopes, and
X_{P}
be the associated toric variety.
Then
H^{2k-1}(X)=0
for all
2k-1>\frac{1}{2}\dim X_{P}
.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we prove the claim by the induction on the filtration in (
4.1
). Let
2d_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dim X_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
. Then we have
d_{i}\geq d_{i-1}
and the condition for a conic sequence on
P
implies that

(4.4)

\dim Y_{i}\leq 2d_{i}-1.

We assume that
H^{j}(X_{i-1})=0
for odd
j
with
j>d_{i-1}
, and claim that
H^{j}(X_{i})=0
for odd
j
with
j>d_{i}
.

Consider the long exact sequence in (
4.3
) induced from the cofibration in (
4.2
). Since
\dim Y_{i}=2\dim C_{i}+1
, Proposition
4.2
implies that
\widetilde{H}^{j-1}(Y_{i})=0
for all even
j-1
with
j-1>\frac{1}{2}(\dim Y_{i}-1)
. In particular, since

d_{i}\geq\dim C_{i}+1=\frac{1}{2}(\dim Y_{i}+1),

by (
4.4
), we have
\widetilde{H}^{j-1}(Y_{i})=0
for all even
j-1
with
j-1\geq d_{i}
. Moreover, the induction hypothesis implies that
\widetilde{H}^{j}(X_{i-1})=0
for all odd
j
with
j>d_{i-1}
.
Since
d_{i}\geq d_{i-1}
, we have
\widetilde{H}^{j}(X_{i-1})=0
for all odd
j>d_{i}
. Therefore,
H^{j}(X_{i})=0
if
j
is odd with
j>\frac{1}{2}\dim X_{i}
, which completes the induction.
∎

Especially, if a
3
-dimensional polytope
P
admits a conic sequence, then
\mathscr{C}_{\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}}
consists of simple polytopes because every element in
\mathscr{C}_{\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}}
is of dimension at most
2
. Hence Theorem
4.3
implies that
H^{5}(X_{P})=0
in this case. We refer to
[
Jor98
, Proposition 3.5.3-(f)]
.

The next corollary specializes the
\Delta
-conic sequence of
P
, which is proved in
[
SS21
, Corollary 3.7]
as well.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
P
is
\Delta
-conic, then
H^{\text{odd}}(X_{P})=0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, each
Y_{i}
is homeomorphic to
S^{2\dim C_{i}+1}/K_{i}
for some finite group
K_{i}
. Now the transfer homomorphism (see for instance
[
Bor60
, III.2]
) implies that

(4.5)

\widetilde{H}^{j}(S^{2\dim C_{i}+1}/K_{i})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Q}&\text{ if }j=2\dim C_{i}+1,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Hence the proof follows directly from Theorem
4.1
.
∎

The converse of Corollary
4.4
is not true in general. Indeed, G. Barthel, et. al.
[
BBFK96
, Example 3.5]
introduced two examples of toric varieties
X
and
X^{\prime}
over the polytope
P
given in Example
2.3
such that
H^{3}(X)=0
and
H^{3}(X^{\prime})=\mathbb{Q}
. Note that
H^{1}(X)=H^{1}(X^{\prime})=0
because a projective toric variety is simply connected
[
CLS11
, Theorem 12.1.10]
and
H^{5}(X)=H^{5}(X^{\prime})=0
by Theorem
4.3
.

Our last application of Theorem
4.1
is the Poincaré polynomial

\Poin(X_{P},t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\dim X_{P}}\beta_{i}t^{i}

of a toric variety
X_{P}
, where
\beta_{i}
denotes the
i
-th cohomology Betti number. When
P
is a simple lattice polytope, that is, the resulting toric variety
X_{P}
is a toric orbifold, it is well-known that
H^{\ast}(X_{P})
vanishes in odd degrees. One can also conclude this using Corollary
4.4
because every simple polytope admits at least one
\Delta
-conic sequence. In this case,
(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{2i},\dots,\beta_{2n})
agrees with the
h
-vector
h(P)=(h_{0},\dots,h_{i},\dots,h_{n})
of
P
, namely, the vector obtained by the equation given in (
2.7
).
We refer to
[
McM71
,
Sta80
]
.

The next theorem allows us to enjoy Equation (
2.7
) in a wider class of polytopes including all simple polytopes, namely, the category of polytopes admitting
\Delta
-conic sequences.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
X_{P}
be the toric variety associated with a lattice polytope with a
\Delta
-conic sequence. Then the Poincaré polynomial
\Poin(X_{P},t)
of
X_{P}
is given by

(4.6)

\mathsf{Poin}(X_{P},t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{k}(t^{2}-1)^{k}.

where
f_{k}
is the number of
k
-dimensional faces of
P
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{C}_{\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}}=\{C_{2},\dots,C_{\ell}\}=\{\Delta^{d_{2}-1},\dots,\Delta^{d_{\ell}-1}\}
be the sequence of simplices corresponding to the given
\Delta
-conic sequence
\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\ell}
of
P
. Notice that
d_{2}=1
,
d_{\ell}=n
and
1\leq d_{i}\leq n-1
for
i=2,\dots,\ell-1
. By Lemma
3.1
together with (
4.5
), we have that
\widetilde{H}^{2d_{i}-1}(Y_{i})
is of rank
1
and
\widetilde{H}^{j}(Y_{i})
vanishes for
j<2d_{i}-1
. Therefore, the long exact sequence in (
4.3
) implies that the rank of
\widetilde{H}^{\ast}(X_{i})
increases by
1
whenever
\ast
meets
2d_{i}
. Repeating this process for each
i=2,\dots,\ell
, we conclude that

(4.7)

\beta_{2j}=\#\{i\mid d_{i}=j\}.

Since
C_{i}=\Delta^{d_{i}-1}
for some
d_{i}\geq 1
, we have

\Phi_{C_{i}}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{i}}{d_{i}\choose k}x^{k-1}
. Thus we get

(4.8)

1+x\Phi_{C_{i}}(x)=(x+1)^{d_{i}}.

Therefore, the result of Proposition
2.4
together with (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) implies that

(4.9)

\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{k}(P)x^{k}=\sum_{i=2}^{\ell}(x+1)^{d_{i}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{2k}(x+1)^{k}.

Since
H^{\ast}(X_{P})
vanishes in odd degrees, the result follows by plugging
t^{2}-1
into
x
in (
4.9
).
∎
