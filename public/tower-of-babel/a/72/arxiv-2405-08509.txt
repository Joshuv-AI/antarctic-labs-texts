Abstract
. We classify all solution triples with Fibonacci components to the equation a 2 + b 2 + c 2 = 3 ​ a ​ b ​ c + m , a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}=3abc+m, for positive m m . We show that for m = 2 m=2 they are precisely ( 1 , F ⁡ ( b ) , F ⁡ ( b + 2 ) ) (1,F(b),F(b+2)) , with even b b ; for m = 21 m=21 , there exist exactly two Fibonacci solutions ( 1 , 2 , 8 ) (1,2,8) and ( 2 , 2 , 13 ) (2,2,13) and for any other m m there exists at most one Fibonacci solution, which, in case it exists, is always minimal (i.e. it is a root of a Markoff tree). Moreover, we show that there is an infinite number of values of m m admitting exactly one such solution.



1.
Introduction

Markoff triples constitute a fascinating area of research in number theory. They are the solution triples in positive integers of the Markoff equation

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=3xyz.

Originally introduced by A. A. Markoff in his work
[
M1
,
M2
]
, in the context of minima of quadratic forms, these triples exhibit interesting properties; for instance, they are all distributed through a tree structure, called the Markoff tree.

In recent years, many authors have studied generalizations of this equation (
[
Mor
]
,
[
GS
]
). In
[
SC
]
, Markoff
m
-triples are introduced as positive integer solutions of the
m
-Markoff equation

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=3xyz+m,

(1.1)

where
m
is a positive integer. By the symmetry of the equation, any permutation of a solution is also a solution, and hence the Markoff
m
-triples
(x,y,z)
are assumed to be ordered with
0<x\leq y\leq z
. These triples are also organized into tree structures. However, in this case, multiple trees or none could exist. The authors in
[
SC
]
showed that the number of trees, for every
m,
is equal to the number of Markoff
m
-triples
(x,y,z)
that are minimal, that is to say, those that satisfy the inequality

z\geq 3xy.

(1.2)

Moreover, these minimal triples are the roots of the trees generated by Vieta transformations.

Several authors have studied various generalized Markoff equations, looking for solutions of these equations from sequences such as Fibonacci, Pell, Lucas, and so on (see
[
AL
]
,
[
B
]
,
[
GGL
]
,
[
H
]
,
[
HT
]
,
[
HST
]
,
[
RSP
]
,
[
R
]
and
[
T
]
). In this paper, we study Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples for
m>0
, which are Markoff
m
-triples such that all the components are Fibonacci numbers. The case of Markoff-Fibonacci triples
(m=0),
was previously studied in
[
LS
]
, where it was found that the only Fibonacci solutions to the Markoff equation were triples of the form
(1,F(b),F(b+2)),
where
b
is odd.

We found that an analogous family of Fibonacci solutions exists for the generalized
m
-Markoff equation with
m=2
, containing all possible non-minimal solutions. Concretely, if
b
is an even number, then
(1,F(b),F(b+2))
is always a solution of the
m
-Markoff equation for
m=2
. This family forms the superior branch of the Markoff

For each

2.
Some inequalities of Fibonacci numbers

Let us start by recalling some facts about Fibonacci numbers which will be useful in the study of Fibonacci solutions of the
m
-Markoff equation.

The well-known recursion formula for the Fibonacci sequence is
given by
F(0)=0
,
F(1)=1
and
F(n+1)=F(n)+F(n-1)
, for all

n\geq 1
. Throughout this paper, we denote

\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},\quad\quad\bar{\varphi}=\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}

so that for each
n\geq 0
, the
n
-th Fibonacci number can be written through Binet’s formula as

F(n)=\frac{\varphi^{n}-\bar{\varphi}^{n}}{\sqrt{5}}\,.

(2.1)

The following bounds on quotients of Fibonacci numbers will be used in later sections to compute bounds on Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a
be an integer and let
N>0
be an integer. Let

k_{N,a}=\min\left(\frac{F(N)}{F(N+a)},\frac{F(N+1)}{F(N+1+a)}\right),\hskip 14.22636ptK_{N,a}=\max\left(\frac{F(N)}{F(N+a)},\frac{F(N+1)}{F(N+1+a)}\right).

Then, for each
n\geq N
, the following inequalities hold.

k_{N,a}\leq\frac{F(n)}{F(n+a)}\leq K_{N,a}.

Proof.

Let
k,K\in\mathbb{R}
be any pair of numbers such that for some
N

k\leq\frac{F(N)}{F(N+a)},\frac{F(N+1)}{F(N+a+1)}\leq K.

We will show that

kF(n+a)\leq F(n)\leq KF(n+a)

for each
n\geq N
. We achieve this by induction on
n
. The result clearly holds for
n=N
and
n=N+1
by hypothesis. Let
n\geq N+2
and assume that the statement is true for all
n^{\prime}
with
N\leq n^{\prime}<n
. In particular, it follows that

kF(n+a-2)\leq F(n-2)\leq KF(n+a-2)\,,

kF(n+a-1)\leq F(n-1)\leq KF(n+a-1)\,.

Adding both expressions yields

kF(n+a)=kF(n+a-1)+kF(n+a-2)\leq F(n)=F(n-1)+F(n-2)\\
\leq KF(n+a-1)+KF(n+a-2)=KF(n+a)\,.

The lemma now follows on taking
k=k_{N,a}
and
K=K_{N,a}
.
∎

Remark 1
.

In Appendix
A.2
, Tables
1
and
2
have been computed by rounding down and up respectively the values of
k_{N,a}
and
K_{N,a}
provided by Lemma
2.1
for small values of
N
and
a
. We will use these concrete lower and upper bounds for the quotients
F(n)/F(n+a)
in the proofs of some inequalities in the following sections.

Remark 2
.

For the sake of exposition, we recall the following identity on Fibonacci numbers, usually known as Vajda’s identity
[
V
]
which will be used in several proofs throughout the paper:

F(n+i)F(n+j)-F(n)F(n+i+j)=(-1)^{n}F(i)F(j),

for any positive integers
i,j
and
n.
In particular, taking
n=1,\,\,i=a
and
j=b-1
we obtain the famous equality
F(a+b)=F(a+1)F(b)+F(a)F(b-1)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
a,b,c\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle F(c)\leq 3F(a)F(b)

\displaystyle\quad\text{if and only if}\quad c\leq a+b,

(2.2)

\displaystyle F(c)>3F(a)F(b)

\displaystyle\quad\text{if and only if}\quad c\geq a+b+1,\text{and}

(2.3)

\displaystyle F(c)=3F(a)F(b)

\displaystyle\quad\text{if and only if}\quad a=b=2,\,c=4\,.

(2.4)

Proof.

By Remark
2
, substituting
F(a+1)=F(a)+F(a-1)
we obtain

F(a+b)=F(a)F(b)+F(a-1)F(b)+F(a)F(b-1)\leq 3F(a)F(b)\,.

(2.5)

As
F(c)
is increasing in
c
, this gives (
2.2
).

Suppose that
c\geq a+b+1
. Since
F(c)\geq F(a+b+1)
, to prove (
2.3
) it is enough to show that

F(a+b+1)>3F(a)F(b).

(2.6)

As before, developing the left-hand side of (
2.6
) using Vajda’s identity yields

F(a+b+1)=F(a+1)F(b+1)+F(a)F(b)=2F(a)F(b)+F(a)F(b-1)+F(a-1)F(b)+F(a-1)F(b-1)\\
=2F(a)F(b)+F(a)F(b-1)+2F(a-1)F(b-1)+F(a-1)F(b-2)\,.

On the other hand, working on the right-hand side of (
2.6
) we obtain

3F(a)F(b)=2F(a)F(b)+F(a)F(b)=2F(a)F(b)+F(a)F(b-1)+F(a)F(b-2)=\\
2F(a)F(b)+F(a)F(b-1)+F(a-1)F(b-2)+F(a-2)F(b-2)\,.

Comparing both sides, we obtain that
2F(a-1)F(b-1)>F(a-2)F(b-2)
, which is clearly true for
a\geq 2
and
b\geq 2
. Finally, we study the equality case. By (
2.3
), we must have
c\leq a+b
. As
a+b\geq 4
, then for each
c<a+b
it holds that

F(c)<F(a+b)\leq 3F(a)F(b)

consequently
c=a+b
. The equality in (
2.5
) is only attained if
F(a-1)=F(a)
and
F(b-1)=F(b)
. That only occurs in the case
a=b=2
, proving (
2.4
).
∎

3.
Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples

Henceforth, we shall denote

m(a,b,c)=F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}+F(c)^{2}-3F(a)F(b)F(c)

so that
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
is a Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple if and only if
m=m(a,b,c)>0
. In this section, we derive conditions on
(a,b,c)
to ensure
m(a,b,c)>0
.

Since we want to study ordered triples
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
composed of positive numbers, without loss of generality, we will assume from now on that
2\leq a\leq b\leq c
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
2\leq a\leq b\leq c
with
c\geq a+b+1
, then
m(a,b,c)>0
.

Proof.

Since

m(a,b,c)=F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}+F(c)\left(F(c)-3F(a)F(b)\right)\,,

and by Lemma
2.2
,
F(c)>3F(a)F(b)
, then all of the terms in the previous factorization are positive, and thus
m(a,b,c)>0
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

If
3\leq a\leq b\leq c\leq a+b,
then
m(a,b,c)<0
.

Proof.

As
F(x)
is an increasing function,
1<F(a)\leq F(b)\leq F(c)\leq F(a+b).

Consider the parabola
f(x)=x^{2}-3F(a)F(b)x+F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}.
Since
f(x)
is an upward-opening parabola, its absolute maximum in

F(b)\leq x\leq F(a+b)
is attained at one of the endpoints. Therefore, to prove that
m(a,b,c)=f(F(c))<0,
it suffices to prove that
f(F(b))
and
f(F(a+b))
are both negative.

First, we prove that
f(F(b))<0.
Indeed,

f(F(b))=F(a)^{2}+2F(b)^{2}-3F(a)F(b)^{2}\leq F(b)^{2}(3-3F(a))<0.

Let us prove that
f(F(a+b))<0
. We have
F(a)=F(a-1)+F(a-2)\leq 2F(a-1)
. Thus,
F(2a)=F(a)^{2}+2F(a)F(a-1)\geq 2F(a)^{2}
. Equation (
2.5
) implies
F(2a)-3F(a)^{2}<-1
for
a\geq 3
because
F(a-1)<F(a)
. Therefore, in the case
a=b
,

f(F(2a))=m(a,a,2a)=2F(a)^{2}+F(2a)(F(2a)-3F(a)^{2})<2F(a)^{2}-F(2a)\leq 0.

Thus, we may assume that
a\neq b
. From Remark
2
we have
\frac{F(a+b)}{F(a)F(b)}=\frac{F(a+1)}{F(a)}+\frac{F(b-1)}{F(b)}\leq\frac{1}{0.6}+0.6667<2.4
. Using Tables
1
and
2
it follows that

\frac{1}{F(a)}+\frac{F(a+b)}{F(a)F(b)}<3
and thus

F(b)+F(a+b)<3F(a)F(b)
. Hence

F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}<F(b)F(b+1)<(3F(a)F(b)-F(a+b))F(a+b)

giving
f(F(a+b))=m(a,b,a+b)<0
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

If
b\geq 2,
then
m(2,b,b+1)\leq 0
, and the equality is only attained if
b=2
.

Proof.

This is equivalent to proving

1+F(b)^{2}+F(b+1)^{2}\leq 3F(b)F(b+1).

Since
b\geq 2
, then

1+F(b)^{2}+F(b+1)^{2}=F(1)^{2}+F(b)^{2}+F(b+1)^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{b+1}F(k)^{2}=F(b+1)F(b+2)

and, by Remark
1
,
F(b+2)\leq 3F(b)
, with equality attained at
b=2
, so the lemma follows.
∎

As a consequence of the previous lemmata, we obtain a complete classification of triples

(F(a),F(b),F(c))
that are
m
-triples for a positive
m
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
2\leq a\leq b\leq c
. Then
m(a,b,c)>0
if and only if
a+b+1\leq c
or
(a,b,c)=(2,b,b+2),
for some even
b\geq 2
.

Proof.

If
c\geq a+b+1,
then
m(a,b,c)>0
by Lemma
3.1
. If
(a,b,c)=(2,b,b+2)
, Vajda’s identity (see Remark
2
) for
(i=1,j=1,n=b)
implies

m(2,b,b+2)=1+F(b)^{2}+F(b+2)^{2}-3F(b)F(b+2)=1+(F(b+2)-F(b))^{2}-F(b)F(b+2)\\
=1+F(b+1)^{2}-F(b)F(b+2)=1+(-1)^{b}\,,

(3.1)

which is positive if and only if
b
is even. If
b
is odd, we obtain a branch of the Markoff tree (
m=0
) described in the article
[
LS
]
. For
b>2
even, we obtain the superior branch of the
2
-Markoff tree with minimal triple
(1,1,3)
, represented in Figure
1
.

Suppose now that
c\leq a+b
and that
(a,b,c)\neq(2,b,b+2)
. If
a\geq 3
, then Lemma
3.2
shows that
m(a,b,c)<0
. If
a=2
and
c\neq b+2
but
c\leq a+b=b+2
, then either
(a,b,c)=(2,b,b)
or
(a,b,c)=(2,b,b+1)
. If
(a,b,c)=(2,b,b),
we obtain

m(a,b,c)=m(2,b,b)=1+2F(b)^{2}-3F(b)^{2}=1-F(b)^{2}\leq 0.

If
(a,b,c)=(2,b,b+1)
then Lemma
3.3
shows that
m(a,b,c)=m(2,b,b+1)\leq 0
.
∎

4.
Proofs of main results

Using Proposition
3.1
, we obtain a specific characterisation of minimal and non-minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples, when
m>0
. Recall that a Markoff
m
-triple
(x,y,z)
with
m>0
is called minimal (c.f.
[
SC
, Definition 2.2]
) if
z\geq 3xy\,.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
a\geq 2
. Then for
a\leq b\leq c
, the
m
-triple
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
, with
m>0
, is minimal if and only if either
c\geq a+b+1
or
a=b=2
and
c=4
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
, the values of
(a,b,c)
for which
m(a,b,c)>0
are those with
c\geq a+b+1
or
(a,b,c)=(2,b,b+2),
for some even
b\geq 2
. Amongst those triples, the minimal ones are those which also satisfy
F(c)\geq 3F(a)F(b)
. By Lemma
2.2
, these are precisely the triples such that
c\geq a+b+1
or
(a,b,c)=(2,2,4)
.
∎

Using this proposition, we will split the problem of classifying the Fibonacci solutions to the
m
-Markoff equation (
1.1
) into classifying the non-minimal and minimal triples, starting by the classification of non-minimal
m
-triples.

4.1.
Non-minimal case

Proposition 4.2
.

Every non-minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple with
m>0
is a
2
-triple of the form
(1,F(b),F(b+2)),
where
b>2
is an even number.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
, any Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
with
m>0
must either satisfy
c\geq a+b+1
, or be of the form
(1,F(b),F(b+2))
for some even
b\geq 2
. By Proposition
4.1
, non-minimal
m
-triples satisfy
c\leq a+b
with
(a,b,c)\neq(2,2,4)
. Thus, all non-minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples must be of the form
(1,F(b),F(b+2))
with even
b\geq 3
and, by (
3.1
), they are all
2
-triples. In particular, they form a branch of the
2
-Markoff tree spanned by
(1,3,8)
.
∎

Remark 3
.

From
[
SC
]
, we know that for
m=2
there exists exactly one minimal
2
-triple, which is the only remaining Fibonacci solution to the
m
-Markoff equation for
m=2
, namely,
(1,1,3)=(F(2),F(2),F(4))
.

4.2.
Minimal case

We will now focus on the minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples, with
m>0
. The following proposition shows that contrary to the non-minimal Fibonacci triples, which only exist for
m=2
, minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples exist for an infinite number of values of
m
.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists an infinite number of values of
m>0
for which the equation (
1.1
)
admits a solution composed of Fibonacci numbers. Moreover, these solution triples are minimal.

Proof.

By the characterisation of minimal
m
-triples from Proposition
4.1
and by Proposition
3.1
, we know that for each
a
and
b
at least 2 and each
c\geq a+b+1
, the triple
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
is an
m
-triple for some
m>0
and it is minimal. Observe that if we fix
a
and
b
then

m(a,b,c)=F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}+F(c)^{2}-3F(a)F(b)F(c)

is a quadratic equation in
F(c)
which is positive and strictly increasing in
c
for each
c\geq a+b+1
because
F(a+b+1)>3F(a)F(b)>\frac{3}{2}F(a)F(b)
due to Lemma
2.2
. This shows that, for each
a
and
b
, there is an infinite number of different values of
m>0
and
c
such that
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
is a minimal
m
-triple.
∎

Next, we will show that, except for
m=21
, if
m>0
admits a minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple, it is unique up to order. We will devote the rest of the paper to proving such a claim in full generality.

Through the rest of the section, we will assume that
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
and
(F(a^{\prime}),F(b^{\prime}),F(c^{\prime}))
are two different ordered Markoff-Fibonacci triples for the same
m>0
, i.e.,
2\leq a\leq b\leq c
,
2\leq a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}\leq c^{\prime}
with
(a,b,c)\neq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
and

m(a,b,c)=m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})=m

and, without loss of generality, we may assume that
c\geq c^{\prime}
.

If we fix an upper bound for
c
, there only exists a finite number of pairs of such triples. The following Lemma has been checked computationally (see Appendix
A.1
for the implementation details) and shows that the claim holds if
c
is small.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
2\leq a\leq b\leq c<20
and
2\leq a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}\leq c^{\prime}\leq c<20
. If
m=m(a,b,c)=m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})>0
, then either

•

a=a^{\prime}
,
b=b^{\prime}
, and
c=c^{\prime}

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
2\leq a\leq b\leq c
and
c\geq 5
. Suppose that
2\leq a\leq a^{\prime}\leq c
and
b\leq b^{\prime}\leq c
. Then

m(a,b,c)\geq m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)

and the equality holds if and only if
a=a^{\prime}
and
b=b^{\prime}
. In particular, if
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
is an ordered minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple with
m>0
, then

m(2,2,c)\geq m(a,b,c)\geq m(a,c-a-1,c).

Proof.

The parabolas

p(x)=x^{2}-3F(b)F(c)x+F(b)^{2}+F(c)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad q(x)=x^{2}-3F(a)F(c)x+F(a)^{2}+F(c)^{2}

have their vertices at
x=\frac{3}{2}F(b)F(c)>F(c)
and
x=\frac{3}{2}F(a)F(c)>F(c)
respectively, so they are both strictly decreasing for
0\leq x\leq F(c)
for all values of
a
and
b
. As the function mapping
n
to
F(n)
is increasing, then for any given fixed
c
the map

m_{c}:(a,b)\mapsto m(a,b,c)=F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}+F(c)^{2}-3F(a)F(b)F(c)

is strictly decreasing both in
a
and
b
in the region
2\leq a,b\leq c
. Given
a,b,c
such that
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
is a minimal triple, Proposition
4.1
implies that
a+b\leq c-1
so
2\leq a\leq b\leq c-1-a<c
and, therefore

m_{c}(a,b)\geq m_{c}(a,c-1-a)\quad\text{and}\quad m_{c}(a,b)\leq m_{c}(2,b)\leq m_{c}(2,2)\,.

∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
and
(F(a^{\prime}),F(b^{\prime}),F(c^{\prime}))
be two ordered minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples such that
c^{\prime}=c-1
. If
c\geq 11
, then
(a,a^{\prime})\neq(2,2)
.

Proof.

Assume that
m(2,b,c)=m(2,b^{\prime},c-1)
for some
b
,
b^{\prime}
and
c,
with
c\geq 11
. By Lemma
4.7
,
b=c-3
. Thus, we obtain

1+F(c-3)^{2}+F(c)^{2}-3F(c-3)F(c)=m(2,c-3,c)=\\
m(2,b^{\prime},c-1)=1+F(b^{\prime})^{2}+F(c-1)^{2}-3F(b^{\prime})F(c-1)\,.

We can simplify this equality to get

F(b^{\prime})^{2}-3F(b^{\prime})F(c-1)=F(c-3)^{2}+F(c)^{2}-F(c-1)^{2}-3F(c-3)F(c)\\
=F(c-3)^{2}+F(c+1)F(c-2)-3F(c-3)F(c)=k\,.

By Vajda’s identity (Remark
2
) we have
F(c+1)F(c-2)=F(c+2)F(c-3)+3(-1)^{c-3}
, so

k=F(c-3)\left(F(c-3)+F(c+2)-3F(c)\right)+3(-1)^{c-3}=-F(c-3)F(c-4)+3(-1)^{c-3}\,.

The equation
F(b^{\prime})^{2}-3F(b^{\prime})F(c-1)=k
is a quadratic equation in
F(b^{\prime})
. Let
p(x)=x^{2}-3F(c-1)x
. Since
k<0
for all
c\geq 7
, the parabola
p(x)=k
has two positive roots. One of them is greater than its vertex,
\frac{3}{2}F(c-1)
, which is greater than
F(c-1)
, so it cannot be
F(b^{\prime})
, since
b^{\prime}\leq c-1
. We will prove that the other root, belonging to the interval
(0,\frac{3}{2}F(c-1))
is not a Fibonacci number by showing that there exists a Fibonacci number
F(b^{*})<F(c-1)
such that
p(F(b^{*}-1))>k>p(F(b^{*}))
. Since the function
p(x)
is strictly decreasing in the interval
(0,F(c-1))
, this will prove that the root belongs to the open interval
(F(b^{*}-1),F(b^{*}))
and, therefore, that it cannot be a Fibonacci number. Concretely, we will show that for all
c\geq 11

p(F(c-9))>k>p(F(c-8))\,.

Dividing both sides of the equation by
F(c-1)^{2}
, this inequality is equivalent to

\frac{F(c-9)^{2}}{F(c-1)^{2}}-3\frac{F(c-9)}{F(c-1)}>-\frac{F(c-3)}{F(c-1)}\frac{F(c-4)}{F(c-1)}+\frac{3(-1)^{c-3}}{F(c-1)^{2}}>\frac{F(c-8)^{2}}{F(c-1)^{2}}-3\frac{F(c-8)}{F(c-1)}\,.

(4.19)

By Remark
1
, we can bound above and below the left-hand side, right-hand side and middle part of the previous equation as follows. For each
c\geq 11,
the following holds

\frac{F(c-9)^{2}}{F(c-1)^{2}}-3\frac{F(c-9)}{F(c-1)}\geq-0.0671,\qquad\frac{F(c-8)^{2}}{F(c-1)^{2}}-3\frac{F(c-8)}{F(c-1)}\leq-0.0998.

-0.0891\geq-\frac{F(c-4)}{F(c-1)}\frac{F(c-3)}{F(c-1)}+\frac{3}{F(c-1)^{2}}\geq-\frac{F(c-4)}{F(c-1)}\frac{F(c-3)}{F(c-1)}+\frac{3(-1)^{c-3}}{F(c-1)^{2}}\\
\geq-\frac{F(c-4)}{F(c-1)}\frac{F(c-3)}{F(c-1)}-\frac{3}{F(c-1)^{2}}\geq-0.0913\,.

As
-0.0671>-0.0891
and
-0.0913>-0.0998
, the lemma follows.
∎

We can now combine all the previous results to show that no pair of minimal Markoff-Fibonacci

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
and
(F(a^{\prime}),F(b^{\prime}),F(c^{\prime}))
be two ordered minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples. If

.

Proof.

As a consequence of Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.6
, we know that either
c^{\prime}=c
or
c^{\prime}=c-1
. It is therefore enough to prove that the case
c^{\prime}=c-1
is impossible. Assume that
c^{\prime}=c-1
. By Lemma
4.7
, we know that
a+b=c-1
. Due to Lemma
4.9
, we know that
(a,a^{\prime})\neq(2,2)
, so we can apply Lemma
4.8
and obtain that
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-1
if
a\neq 4
and that
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-1
or
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-2
if
a=4
or that
c-6=c^{\prime}-5\leq a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\leq c^{\prime}-1=c-2
if
a=2
.

Let us first analyse the case where
a\geq 3
and
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-1=c-2
. Suppose that

m(a,c-1-a,c)=m(a^{\prime},c-2-a^{\prime},c-1)\,.

Applying Lemma
4.3
, we have that, as
a\geq 3
and
c\geq 9
, then

\displaystyle m(a,c-1-a,c)>L_{3,1,9}\frac{1}{5}\varphi^{2c}

\displaystyle m(a^{\prime},c-2-a^{\prime},c-1)<U_{2,1,8}\frac{1}{5}\varphi^{2(c-1)}=\varphi^{-2}U_{2,1,8}\frac{1}{5}\varphi^{2c}

A direct computation shows that
L_{3,1,9}>0.14>0.139>\varphi^{-2}U_{2,1,8}
, so
m(a,c-1-a-c)>m(a^{\prime},c-2-a^{\prime},c-1)
and, therefore, the case
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-1
is impossible for
a\geq 3
. By Lemma
4.8
, the only two remaining cases to prove the result are the following: either
a=2
and
c-6=c^{\prime}-5\leq a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\leq c^{\prime}-1=c-2
or
a=4
and
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-2=c-3
.

Let us begin analysing the
a=2
case. Here, we find

m(a,c-1-a,c)=m(2,c-3,c)=1+F(c-3)^{2}+F(c)^{2}-3F(c-3)F(c)\\
>1+\frac{1}{5}\varphi^{2c}+\frac{1}{5}\varphi^{2c-6}-\frac{4}{5}-\frac{3}{5}(\varphi^{c-3}-1)(\varphi^{c}-1)>\frac{1}{5}\varphi^{2c}+\frac{1}{5}\varphi^{2c-6}-\frac{3}{5}\varphi^{2c-3}\,.

Thus, for
c\geq 11

\frac{5m(a,c-1-a,c)}{\varphi^{2c}}>1+\varphi^{-6}-3\varphi^{-22}>0.347\,.

On the other hand, using Lemma
4.3
for each

it follows that

\frac{5m(a^{\prime},c-1-t-a^{\prime},c-1)}{\varphi^{2c}}<\varphi^{-2}-\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\varphi^{-2-t}+\left(1+\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\varphi^{t}\right)(\varphi^{-2t-6}+\varphi^{6-2c})+9\varphi^{-2c}\,.

The maximum of the right hand side for
1\leq t\leq 5
and
c\geq 11
is attained at

and
c=11
, and yields

\frac{5\,m(a^{\prime},c-1-t-a^{\prime},c-1)}{\varphi^{2c}}<\varphi^{-2}-\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\varphi^{-7}+\left(1+\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\varphi^{7}\right)2\varphi^{-16}+9\varphi^{-22}<0.346\,.

Therefore,

\frac{5\,m(a,c-1-a,c)}{\varphi^{2c}}>0.347>0.346>\frac{5\,m(a^{\prime},c-1-t-a^{\prime},c-1)}{\varphi^{2c}}

Now, let us examine the case
a=4
and
c^{\prime}=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}+2
. In this instance, the value of

\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}=1+\frac{F(c-5)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{9}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{9F(c-5)}{F(c)}.

(4.20)

On the other hand, if
a^{\prime}\geq 7
, then
c-1=c^{\prime}=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}+2\geq b^{\prime}+9
, implying
b^{\prime}\leq c-10
. Also,
c-1=c^{\prime}=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}+2\geq 2a^{\prime}+2
, hence
a^{\prime}\leq\lfloor\frac{c-3}{2}\rfloor
. Consequently, the left-hand side of equation (

) has the following lower bound:

\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})}{F(c)^{2}}\geq 1+\frac{F(c-5)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{9}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{9F(c-5)}{F(c)}\\
-\left(\frac{F(c-1)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{F(\lfloor\frac{c-3}{2}\rfloor)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{F(c-10)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}-3\frac{F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})}{F(c)}\frac{F(c-1)}{F(c)}\right).

3\frac{F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})}{F(c)}\frac{F(c-1)}{F(c)}\geq\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\frac{(\varphi^{a^{\prime}}-1)(\varphi^{b^{\prime}}-1)}{\varphi^{c}+1}\frac{F(c-1)}{F(c)}\\

This gives the following lower bound for
\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})}{F(c)^{2}}

\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})}{F(c)^{2}}\geq 1+\frac{F(c-5)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{9}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{9F(c-5)}{F(c)}\\
-\left(\frac{F(c-1)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{F(\lfloor\frac{c-3}{2}\rfloor)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{F(c-10)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\frac{(\varphi^{c-3}-\varphi^{\lfloor\frac{c-3}{2}\rfloor}-\varphi^{c-10}+1)}{\varphi^{c}+1}\frac{F(c-1)}{F(c)}\right)\geq 0.01\

for
c\geq 20
. This contradicts (
4.7
).

For the case
a^{\prime}=6
, then
b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-8=c-9
and it can be seen that the value of
\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})}{F(c)^{2}}
has the following lower bound

\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})}{F(c)^{2}}=1+\frac{F(c-5)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{9}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{9F(c-5)}{F(c)}\\
-\left(\frac{F(c-1)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{64}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{F(c-9)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}-24\frac{F(c-9)F(c-1)}{F(c)^{2}}\right)\geq 0.004\,

for
c\geq 13
, contradicting again (
4.7
).

The cases
3\leq a^{\prime}\leq 5
are managed in the same way and we omit them.

Finally, if
a^{\prime}=2
, then
b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-4=c-5
and the value of
\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})}{F(c)^{2}}
now has the following upper bound

\frac{m(a,b,c)}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})}{F(c)^{2}}=1+\frac{F(c-5)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{9}{F(c)^{2}}-\frac{9F(c-5)}{F(c)}\\
-\left(\frac{F(c-1)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{1}{F(c)^{2}}+\frac{F(c-4)^{2}}{F(c)^{2}}-3\frac{F(c-4)F(c-1)}{F(c)^{2}}\right)\leq-0.005<0\,.

for

, a contradiction. ∎

To complete our result, we need to prove that there do not exist two minimal Markoff-Fibonacci triples with the same highest element and the same
m
. In other words, let us prove now that if

m(a,b,c)=m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)

then

and

.

Let us suppose that
m(a,b,c)=m(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c),
for some
(a,b)\neq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. Assume without loss of generality that
a\leq a^{\prime}
. By Lemma
4.2
, if
b\leq b^{\prime}
and
(a,b)\neq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
then

. Thus, we must have
a\leq a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<b
. On the other hand, if
a=a^{\prime}
and

then again by Lemma
4.2
it follows that
m(a,b^{\prime},c)>m(a,b,c)
. Consequently, we can infer without loss of generality that

a<a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<b\leq c\,.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
and
(F(a^{\prime}),F(b^{\prime}),F(c))
be two ordered minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples with
2\leq a<a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<b\leq c
, then
a+b=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Rearranging the equation

F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}+F(c)^{2}-3F(a)F(b)F(c)=F(a^{\prime})^{2}+F(b^{\prime})^{2}+F(c)^{2}-3F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})F(c)

yields

F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}-F(a^{\prime})^{2}-F(b^{\prime})^{2}=3F(c)\left(F(a)F(b)-F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})\right)\,.

(4.21)

The left-hand side is always positive because, as
b\geq 4
and
a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<b
, by Remark
1
it follows that

F(b)^{2}>2F(b-1)^{2}\geq F(b^{\prime})^{2}+F(a^{\prime})^{2}.

Let us see that this is impossible if
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}>a+b
. Assume that
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=a+b+t
with
t>0
. As a result

\frac{F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})}{F(a)F(b)}=\frac{(\varphi^{a^{\prime}}-\bar{\varphi}^{a^{\prime}})(\varphi^{b^{\prime}}-\bar{\varphi}^{b^{\prime}})}{(\varphi^{a}-\bar{\varphi}^{a})(\varphi^{b}-\bar{\varphi}^{b})}\geq\frac{(\varphi^{a^{\prime}}-\varphi^{-a^{\prime}})(\varphi^{b^{\prime}}-\varphi^{-b^{\prime}})}{(\varphi^{a}+\varphi^{-a})(\varphi^{b}+\varphi^{-b})}=\frac{\varphi^{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}-\varphi^{b^{\prime}-a^{\prime}}-\varphi^{a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}}}{\varphi^{a+b}+\varphi^{b-a}+\varphi^{a-b}+\varphi^{-a-b}}\,.

Let
s=a+b
, then
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=s+t
. Dividing the numerator and denominator by
\varphi^{s}
yields

\frac{\varphi^{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}-\varphi^{b^{\prime}-a^{\prime}}-\varphi^{a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}}}{\varphi^{a+b}+\varphi^{b-a}+\varphi^{a-b}+\varphi^{-a-b}}=\frac{\varphi^{t}-\varphi^{t-2a^{\prime}}-\varphi^{t-2b^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-2s-t}}{1+\varphi^{-2a}+\varphi^{-2b}+\varphi^{-2s}}=\varphi^{t}\frac{1-\varphi^{-2a^{\prime}}-\varphi^{-2b^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-2s-2t}}{1+\varphi^{-2a}+\varphi^{-2b}+\varphi^{-2s}}\,.

As
2\leq a<a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<b
, then
a\geq 2
,
a^{\prime}\geq 3
,
b^{\prime}\geq 3
,
b\geq 4
and
s=a+b\geq 6
. Thus

\varphi^{t}\frac{1-\varphi^{-2a^{\prime}}-\varphi^{-2b^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-2s-2t}}{1+\varphi^{-2a}+\varphi^{-2b}+\varphi^{-2s}}\geq\varphi\frac{1-2\varphi^{-6}}{1+\varphi^{-4}+\varphi^{-8}+\varphi^{-12}}\geq 1.22>1\,.

Therefore,
F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})>F(a)F(b)
, which contradicts the positivity of both sides of equation (
4.21
).

Thus, we must have
a+b\geq a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
. Assume that
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=a+b-t
with
t>0
and let
s=a+b
as before. Analogously to the previous case,

\frac{F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})}{F(a)F(b)}\leq\frac{(\varphi^{a^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-a^{\prime}})(\varphi^{b^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-b^{\prime}})}{(\varphi^{a}-\varphi^{-a})(\varphi^{b}-\varphi^{-b})}=\varphi^{-t}\frac{1+\varphi^{-2a^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-2b^{\prime}}+\varphi^{-2s-2t}}{1-\varphi^{-2a}-\varphi^{-2b}+\varphi^{-2s}}\leq\\
\varphi^{-1}\frac{1+2\varphi^{-6}+\varphi^{-14}}{1-\varphi^{-4}-\varphi^{-8}}<0.83<\frac{8}{9}\,.

As a consequence, it follows that

1-\frac{F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})}{F(a)F(b)}>1-\frac{8}{9}=\frac{1}{9}\geq\frac{1}{9F(a)^{2}}\,.

Multiplying both sides by
3F(a)F(b)F(c),
yields

3F(c)\left(F(a)F(b)-F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})\right)>\frac{F(c)F(b)}{3F(a)}\,.

As we assumed that
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
is minimal, then
F(c)\geq 3F(a)F(b)
therefore

3F(c)\left(F(a)F(b)-F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})\right)>\frac{F(c)F(b)}{3F(a)}\geq F(b)^{2}>F(b)^{2}-F(b^{\prime})^{2}+F(a)^{2}-F(a^{\prime})^{2}\,.

This contradicts equation (
4.21
), so
a+b
cannot be greater than
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
. Thus
a+b=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
.
∎

4.3.
Proof of the main theorem

Finally, we combine all the previous results to establish the main theorem of the paper (Theorem
1.1
), proving first an intermediary proposition.

Proposition 4.4
.

For each
m>0,
except
m=21
, there exists at most one minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple. For
m=21,
there exist exactly two minimal Fibonacci triples:
(F(3),F(3),F(7))
and
(F(2),F(3),F(6))
.

Proof.

Let
(F(a),F(b),F(c))
and
(F(a^{\prime}),F(b^{\prime}),F(c^{\prime}))
be a pair of ordered minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples with
2\leq a\leq b\leq c
,
2\leq a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}\leq c^{\prime}
contradicting the proposition. Assume without loss of generality that
c\geq c^{\prime}
. From the computational verification stated in Lemma
4.1
, we know that any counterexample to this theorem must have
c\geq 20
. By Lemma
4.10
it follows that
c=c^{\prime}
. Moreover, by Lemma
4.11
, we must have
a+b=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
. Taking
n=a
,
i=b^{\prime}-a
and
j=b-b^{\prime}=a^{\prime}-a
in Vajda’s identity (Remark
2
), we can transform equation (
4.21
) into

F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}-F(a^{\prime})^{2}-F(b^{\prime})^{2}=3F(c)\left(F(a)F(b)-F(a^{\prime})F(b^{\prime})\right)=(-1)^{a+1}3F(c)F(b^{\prime}-a)F(b-b^{\prime})\,,

From the proof of Lemma
4.11
, we know that the left-hand side of this equality is positive, therefore
a
is odd, and then

F(a)^{2}+F(b)^{2}-F(a^{\prime})^{2}-F(b^{\prime})^{2}=3F(c)F(b^{\prime}-a)F(b-b^{\prime})\,.

(4.22)

However, using Lemma
2.2
, we know that

F(b)\leq 3F(b^{\prime})F(b-b^{\prime})\leq 9F(a)F(b^{\prime}-a)F(b-b^{\prime})\,.

Multiplying by
F(b)
and using minimality,
3F(a)F(b)\leq F(c)
, yields

F(b)^{2}\leq 3F(c)F(b^{\prime}-a)F(b-b^{\prime})

and, therefore,

F(b)^{2}-F(b^{\prime})^{2}+F(a)^{2}-F(a^{\prime})^{2}<F(b)^{2}\leq 3F(c)F(b^{\prime}-a)F(b-b^{\prime})

which contradicts equation (
4.22
). Consequently, there is no possible counterexample to the theorem with
c\geq 20
and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
: Suppose that
m=2
. By Proposition
4.2
, the non-minimal Markoff-Fibonacci
2
-triples are
(1,F(b),F(b+2))
for even
b>2
. By
[
SC
]
, there only exists a minimal
2
-triple, which is
(1,1,3)=(1,F(2),F(4))
. Thus all
2
-triples are given by
(1,F(b),F(b+2)),
for even
b
.

For
m=21
,
[
SC
]
showed that there exist exactly two minimal
21
-triples, which are
(1,2,8)=(F(2),F(3),F(6))
and
(2,2,13)=(F(3),F(3),F(7))
, so both of them are Markoff-Fibonacci triples. From Proposition
4.2
we know that all the non-minimal Markoff-Fibonacci triples have
m=2
, so there are no more Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triples for
m=21
.

Let us assume now that
m>0,\,m\neq 2
and
m\neq 21
. Again, from Proposition
4.2
, we know that
m
cannot admit a non-minimal Markoff-Fibonacci triple. Thus, any Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple must be minimal and, by Proposition
4.4
, one such triple must exist at most.

Finally, by Proposition
4.3
, we know that there is an infinite number of values of
m
for which the
m
-Markoff equation admits a Markoff-Fibonacci
m
-triple and only two values (2 and 21) admit more than one triple. The rest admit exactly one solution which, according to the previous argument, must be a minimal triple.
