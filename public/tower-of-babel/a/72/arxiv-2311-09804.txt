Average Jaccard Index of Random Graphs
Qunqiang Feng, Shuai Guo, Zhishui Hu

Abstract
The asymptotic behavior of the Jaccard index in G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) , the classical Erdös-Rényi random graphs model, is studied in this paper, as n n goes to infinity. We first derive the asymptotic distribution of the Jaccard index of any pair of distinct vertices, as well as the first two moments of this index. Then the average of the Jaccard indices over all vertex pairs in G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) is shown to be asymptotically normal under an additional mild condition that n ​ p → ∞ np\to\infty and n 2 ​ ( 1 − p ) → ∞ n^{2}(1-p)\to\infty . Keywords: Erdös-Rényi Random graph; Jaccard similarity; asymptotic distribution; inverse moment AMS 2020 Subject Classification : Primary 05C80, 60C05 secondary 60F05

Introduction

The
Jaccard index
, also known as the
Jaccard similarity coefficient
,
was originally introduced by Paul Jaccard to measure the similarity between two sets
[
13
]
.
For any two finite sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
,
the Jaccard index
J(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is the ratio of the size of their intersection
to the size of their union.
That is,

\displaystyle J(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\frac{|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}|}{|\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}|}=\frac{|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}|}{|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|-|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}|},

where the symbol
|\cdot|
denotes the cardinality of a set.
It is clear that this index ranges from 0 to 1.
The associated
Jaccard distance

for quantifying the dissimilarity between two sets
is defined as one minus the Jaccard index (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
17
]
).
In statistics and data science, the Jaccard index is employed as a statistic to
measure the similarity between
sample sets, especially for binary and categorical data
(see, e.g.,
[
6
,
15
]
).
For extensive generalizations of the Jaccard index in many other mathematical structures,
such as scalars, vectors, matrices and multisets,
we refer the readers to
[
7
]
.
Due to simplicity and popularity, many applications of the Jaccard index
and its variants have been founded in various fields,
such as cell formation
[
29
]
, pattern recognition
[
12
]
,
data mining
[
24
]
, natural language processing
[
27
]
,
recommendation system
[
3
]
, medical
image segmentation
[
8
]
, and machine learning
[
1
]
.

Following the original definition on the sets,
the Jaccard index of two vertices in a graph can naturally be extended to equal
the number of common neighbors divided by the number of vertices
that are neighbors of at least one of them
(see, e.g.,
[
9
]
).
As a graph benchmark suitable for real-world applications,
the Jaccard index has also been proposed to determine the similarity
in graphs or networks
[
16
]
,
because of its clear interpretability and computational scalability.
This index, as well as its variants, is employed to find core nodes
for community detection in complex networks
[
4
,
20
]
,
to estimate the coupling strength between temporal graphs
[
19
]
,
to do link prediction
[
30
,
21
,
18
]
, and among others.

Erdös-Rényi random graph is widely used as a benchmark model
in statistical network analysis (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
26
]
).
In the simulation study of
[
22
]
, it is shown that
the empirical cumulative distribution functions of Jaccard indices over all vertex pairs in two network models,
Erdös-Rényi random graph and the stochastic block model, are quite different.
Despite the widespread applications of the Jaccard index in network analysis,
to the best of our knowledge, there is lack of comprehensive study of theoretical results
on this simple index defined on statistical graph models.
As the first step to fill this gap,
our main concern in this paper is derive the asymptotic behavior of the basic Jaccard index in Erdös-Rényi random graphs.
For numerous probabilistic results on this classical random graph model,
we refer the readers to the monographs
[
14
]
,
[
5
]
,
and
[
25
]
.

Throughout this paper, we shall use the notation as follows.
For any integer
n\geq 2
, we denote by
[n]
the vertex set
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
.
For an event
{\cal E}
, let
|{\cal E}|
be the cardinality,
\overline{\cal E}
the complement,
and
{\bm{I}}({\cal E})
the indicator of
{\cal E}
.
For real numbers
a,b
, we write
a\vee b
to denote the maximum of
a
and
b
.
For probabilistic convergence,
we use
\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}
and
\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm P}}}
to denote convergence in distribution
and in probability, respectively.

The rest of this paper is organized as follow.
The Jaccard index of any pair of distinct vertices in the Erdös-Rényi random graph

G(n,p)
is considered in Section 2.
We first compute the mean and variance of this index,
and then obtain the phase changes of its asymptotic distribution for
p\in[0,1]
in all regimes, as
n\to\infty
.
In Section 3, we prove the asymptotic normality of the average of the Jaccard indices over

G(n,p)
, as

and

.

2
Jaccard index of a vertex pair

Let us denote by
G(n,p)
an Erdös-Rényi random graph on the vertex set
[n]
,
where each edge is present independently with probability
p
.
In this paper, we shall consider
p=p(n)
as a function of the graph size
n
.
For any two vertices
i,j\in[n]
, let
I_{ij}
be the indicator
that takes the value 1 if an edge between
i
and
j
is present in
G(n,p)
, and takes the value 0 otherwise.
It follows that
I_{ii}=0
,
I_{ij}=I_{ji}
, and
\{I_{ij}:1\leq i<j\leq n\}

is a sequence of independent Bernoulli variables with success rate
p
.
The
n\times n
matrix
{\bm{A}}=(I_{ij})
is usually called the adjacent matrix of
G(n,p)
,
and is a symmetric matrix with all diagonal entries equal to zero.

For any vertex
i\in[n]
, let the set
{\cal N}_{i}
be its neighborhood, i.e.,

{\cal N}_{i}=\{k:I_{ik}=1,k\in[n]\}
.
For any pair of vertices
i,j\in[n]
,
we also define their union neighborhood as

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{ij}=\big\{k:I_{ik}\vee I_{jk}=1,~k\in[n]~\mbox{and}~k\neq i,j\big\},\quad i\neq j.

Notice that here the neighborhood set
{\cal N}_{ij}
does not contain vertices

i
and
j
themselves, even if
I_{ij}=1
.
Then the Jaccard index of vertices
i
and
j
in
G(n,p)
is formally defined as

\displaystyle J_{ij}^{(n)}=\frac{|{\cal N}_{i}\cap{\cal N}_{j}|}{|{\cal N}_{ij}|}=:\frac{S_{ij}^{(n)}}{T_{ij}^{(n)}},\quad i\neq j.

(1)

One may see that the index
J_{ij}^{(n)}

given in (
1
) is not well-defined
when
{\cal N}_{ij}
is a empty set or
T_{ij}^{(n)}=0
.
For convenience, following the idea in
[
6
]

we shall define
J_{ij}^{(n)}=p/(2-p)
in this special case.
Indeed, it is shown later that the conditional expectation of
J_{ij}^{(n)}
is exactly
p/(2-p)

given that
T_{ij}^{(n)}>0
.
In terms of the adjacent matrix
{\bm{A}}
, the numerator and denominator in (
1
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle S_{ij}^{(n)}=\sum\limits_{k\neq i,j}I_{ik}I_{jk},\quad T_{ij}^{(n)}=\sum\limits_{k\neq i,j}I_{ik}\vee I_{jk}.

(2)

Due to independence of the elements in
{\bm{A}}
, it is clear that the random variables

S_{ij}^{(n)}
and
T_{ij}^{(n)}
follow the binomial distributions

{\rm Bin}(n-2,p^{2})
and
{\rm Bin}(n-2,p(2-p))
,
respectively. Hence, the Jaccard index of a vertex pair in
G(n,p)

is a quotient of two dependent binomial random variables.

2.1
Mean and variance

We first calculate the mean and variance of the Jaccard index
of any pair of vertices in
G
. By (
1
) and (
2
),
one can see that
\{J_{ij}^{(n)},1\leq i<j\leq n\}
is a sequence of random variables that are
pairwise dependent but identically distributed.
Without loss of generality, we only consider
J_{12}^{(n)}
.

For any vertex
3\leq k\leq n
, the conditional probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(I_{1k}I_{2k}=1\,|\,I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}=1\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(I_{1k}I_{2k}=1\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}=1\right)}=\frac{p}{2-p},

which is regardless of
k
.
Then for any positive integer
1\leq m\leq n-2
, given the event
T_{12}^{(n)}=m
,
the conditional distribution of
S_{12}^{(n)}
is
{\rm Bin}(m,p/(2-p))
,
due to independence of the indicators
\{I_{1k},I_{2k},3\leq k\leq n\}
.
Consequently, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[S_{12}^{(n)}\Big|T_{12}^{(n)}=m\right]=\frac{mp}{2-p},

(3)

and

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[S_{12}^{(n)}\Big|T_{12}^{(n)}=m\right]=\frac{2mp(1-p)}{(2-p)^{2}}.

(4)

Noting that
J_{12}^{(n)}=p/(2-p)
in the special case
T_{12}^{(n)}=0
,
by (
1
) and (
3
) we thus have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[J_{12}^{(n)}\Big|T_{12}^{(n)}\Big]=\frac{p}{2-p},

which implies that
{\mathbb{E}}[J_{12}^{(n)}]=p/(2-p)
, and

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left(J_{12}^{(n)}\Big|T_{12}^{(n)}\right)\right]=0.

(5)

Using the law of total variance, it follows by (
4
) and (
5
) that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[J_{12}^{(n)}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm Var}\left(J_{12}^{(n)}\Big|T_{12}^{(n)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{n-2}{\mathbb{P}}\left(T_{12}^{(n)}=m\right){\rm Var}\Big[\frac{S_{12}^{(n)}}{m}\Big|T_{12}^{(n)}=m\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2p(1-p)}{(2-p)^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{n-2}\frac{1}{m}{\mathbb{P}}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}=m\big),

(6)

which involves the first inverse moment of the binomial distribution.
Recalling that
T_{12}^{(n)}
has the distribution
{\rm Bin}(n-2,p(2-p))
,
it follows by Corollary 1 in
[
28
]
that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n-2}\frac{1}{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left(T_{12}^{(n)}=m\right)=\frac{1}{np(2-p)}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{np}\Big)\Big),

(7)

as
np\to\infty
.
By (
2.1
) and (
7
), we thus have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\Big[J_{12}^{(n)}\Big]=\frac{2(1-p)}{n(2-p)^{3}}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{np}\Big)\Big).

Collecting the above findings, by Chebyshev’s inequality we thus prove the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Let
J_{ij}^{(n)}
be the Jaccard index of any distinct vertices
i,j\in[n]
in
G(n,p)
.
Then
{\mathbb{E}}[J_{ij}^{(n)}]=p/(2-p)
for all
n\geq 2
.
In particular, as
np\to\infty
, it further follows that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\Big[J_{ij}^{(n)}\Big]=\frac{2(1-p)}{n(2-p)^{3}}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{np}\Big)\Big),

and
J_{ij}^{(n)}-p/(2-p)
converges to 0 in probability.

2.2
Asymptotic distribution

We now establish the asymptotic distribution of the Jaccard index of any vertex pair in
G(n,p)

in the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
J_{ij}^{(n)}
be the Jaccard distance of any distinct vertices
i,j\in[n]
in
G(n,p)
.

(i)

If
np^{2}(1-p)\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n(2-p)^{3}}{2(1-p)}}\Big(J_{ij}^{(n)}-\frac{p}{2-p}\Big)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}Z,

where
Z
denotes a standard normal random variable;

(ii)

If
np^{2}\to\lambda
for some constant
\lambda>0
,
then
2npJ_{ij}^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}{\rm Poi}(\lambda)
,
where
{\rm Poi}(\lambda)
denotes the Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
;

(iii)

If
np^{2}\to 0
, then
npJ_{ij}^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm P}}}0
.

(iv)

If
n(1-p)\to c
for some constant
c>0
, then
n(1-J_{ij}^{(n)})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}{\rm Poi}(2c)
.

(v)

If
n(1-p)\to 0
, then
n(1-J_{ij}^{(n)})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm P}}}0
.

Proof.

As presented in the previous subsection, it is sufficient to consider a single index
J_{12}^{(n)}
.
To prove (i), we first rewrite

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(n-2)(2-p)^{3}}{2(1-p)}}\Big(J_{12}^{(n)}-\frac{p}{2-p}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(n-2)(2-p)}{2(1-p)}}\cdot\frac{(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}-pT_{12}^{(n)}}{T_{12}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}-pT_{12}^{(n)}}{\sqrt{2(n-2)p^{2}(1-p)(2-p)}}\cdot\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{T_{12}^{(n)}}.

(8)

For any distinct vertices
i,j,k\in[n]
, let us denote

\displaystyle V_{ij,k}=(2-p)I_{ik}I_{jk}-p\big(I_{ik}\vee I_{jk}\big).

(9)

Then, for any fixed two vertices
i,j\in[n]
, the random variables

\{V_{ij,k},~k\in[n],k\neq i,j\}
are independent and
identically distributed with common mean 0
and common variance
2p^{2}(1-p)(2-p)
.
Since it follows by (
2
) that

\displaystyle(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}-pT_{12}^{(n)}=\sum_{k=3}^{n}V_{12,k},

(10)

a direct application of the Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem yields that

\displaystyle\frac{(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}-pT_{12}^{(n)}}{\sqrt{2(n-2)p^{2}(1-p)(2-p)}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}Z,

(11)

whenever
np^{2}(1-p)\to\infty
.
By Chebyshev’s inequality, the fact
T_{12}^{(n)}\sim{\rm Bin}((n-2),p(2-p))
gives that as
np\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{12}^{(n)}}{(n-2)p(2-p)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm P}}}1,

(12)

which, together with (
2.2
) and (
11
), proves (i) by Slutsky’s lemma.

If
np^{2}\to\lambda
for some constant
\lambda>0
, we must have that
p\to 0
and
np\to\infty
.
Since
S_{12}^{(n)}\sim{\rm Bin}(n-2,p^{2})
,
Poisson limit theorem yields that
S_{12}^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}{\rm Poi}(\lambda)
.
By (
12
), again using Slutsky’s lemma, one can obtain that

\displaystyle 2npJ_{12}^{(n)}=\frac{2np}{T_{12}^{(n)}}\cdot S_{12}^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}\text{Poi}(\lambda),

which proves (ii).

If
np^{2}\to 0
, to prove (iii) we only need to prove that the probability
{\mathbb{P}}(npJ_{ij}^{(n)}>np^{2}/(2-p))
tends to 0.
Note that if the event
\{S_{12}^{(n)}=0\}
occurs, the Jaccard index
J_{ij}^{(n)}
must be 0 or
p/(2-p)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(npJ_{ij}^{(n)}>\frac{np^{2}}{2-p}\Big)={\mathbb{P}}\Big(J_{ij}^{(n)}>\frac{p}{2-p}\Big)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{12}^{(n)}\neq 0\big)=1-{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{12}^{(n)}=0\big).

Again by the fact
S_{12}^{(n)}\sim{\rm Bin}(n-2,p^{2})
, we have that
1-{\mathbb{P}}\big(S_{12}^{(n)}=0\big)\to 0
, if
np^{2}\to 0
.
This implies (iii).

Analogously to (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{p}{2-p}-J_{12}^{(n)}=\frac{-(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}+pT_{12}^{(n)}}{(n-2)p(2-p)^{2}}\cdot\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{T_{12}^{(n)}}.

(13)

Note that (
12
) still holds and
n[1-p/(2-p)]
has the limit
2c
, if
n(1-p)\to c
for some constant
c>0
.
To prove (iv), by (
13
) it is now sufficient to show that

\displaystyle-(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}+pT_{12}^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}{\rm Poi}(2c)-2c.

(14)

For any distinct vertices
i,j,k\in[n]
, one can directly obtain from (
9
) that the characteristic function of
-V_{ij,k}
is

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)={\mathbb{E}}\big[{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}tV_{ij,k}}\big]=p^{2}{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}t(1-p)}+2p(1-p){\rm e}^{{\rm i}tp}+(1-p)^{2},

where
{\rm i}=\sqrt{-1}
denotes the imaginary unit.
Then, by (
10
) and independence, we have that the characteristic function of
-(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}+pT_{12}^{(n)}
is
equal to

\displaystyle f_{n}^{n-2}(t)

\displaystyle=\big[p^{2}{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}t(1-p)}+2p(1-p){\rm e}^{{\rm i}tp}+(1-p)^{2}\big]^{n-2},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\big[p^{2}{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}t(1-p)}+2p(1-p){\rm e}^{{\rm i}tp}+(1-p)^{2}-1\big]

\displaystyle=2c{\rm e}^{{\rm i}t}+\lim_{n\to\infty}n\big[p^{2}{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}t(1-p)}-1\big]

\displaystyle=2c{\rm e}^{{\rm i}t}+\lim_{n\to\infty}n\big[p^{2}\big({\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}t(1-p)}-1)+(p^{2}-1)\big]

\displaystyle=2c\big({\rm e}^{{\rm i}t}-{\rm i}t-1\big),

if
n(1-p)\to c
. Therefore, the limit of the characteristic function of
-(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}+pT_{12}^{(n)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}^{n-2}(t)=\exp\big\{2c\big({\rm e}^{{\rm i}t}-{\rm i}t-1\big)\big\},

which implies (
14
) and completes the proof of (iv).

We only sketch the proof of (v), since it is very similar to (iv). By (
12
) and (
13
),
it is sufficient to show that
-(2-p)S_{12}^{(n)}+pT_{12}^{(n)}
converges in probability to 0 under the condition
n(1-p)\to 0
.
In fact, following the proof of (
14
), in this case one can deduce that its characteristic function
f_{n}^{n-2}(t)\to 1
.
∎

3
Average Jaccard index

In this section, we derive asymptotic properties of
the average Jaccard index of
G(n,p)
, which is given by

\displaystyle J_{n}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}J_{ij}^{(n)}.

(15)

That is, the average Jaccard index
J_{n}
is the average of the Jaccard indices over all vertex pairs in
the Erdös-Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
.
An immediate consequence of Proposition
1
is that the expectation of
J_{n}
is equal to
p/(2-p)
.

We now state our main results of this paper in the following.

Theorem 2
.

Let
J_{n}
be the average Jaccard index of
G(n,p)
.
If
np\to\infty
and
n^{2}(1-p)\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\frac{n(2-p)^{2}}{\sqrt{8p(1-p)}}\Big(J_{n}-\frac{p}{2-p}\Big)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}Z,

where
Z
denotes a standard normal random variable.

It is remarkable that the quantity
n^{2}(1-p)/2
is the asymptotic expected number of unoccupied edges in
G(n,p)
.
The above theorem suggests that if
G(n,p)
is neither too sparse (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Section 1.8]
)
nor close to a complete graph,
then its average Jaccard index
J_{n}
has asymptotic normality.
In order to prove Theorem
2
, we first introduce two auxiliary lemmas,
both of which involve the inverse moments of the binomial distribution.

Lemma 1
.

If random variable
X_{n}
has a binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
,
then for any fixed constants
a\geq 0
and
b>0
, as
np\to\infty
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{(n+a)p}{b+X_{n}}-1\Big)^{2}\Big]=O\Big(\frac{1}{np}\Big).

(16)

Proof.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we define

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{n}:=\big\{(1-\varepsilon)(n+a)p\leq b+X_{n}\leq(1+\varepsilon)(n+a)p\big\}.

Applying the Chernoff’s bound for the binomial distribution (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Corollary 2.3]
) gives that
for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{{\cal A}}_{n})\leq{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\frac{X_{n}}{np}-1\Big|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big)\leq 2\exp\Big\{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{12}np\Big\}=O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{3}p^{3}}\Big).

Then for sufficiently large
n
, by noting that

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{(n+a)p}{b+X_{n}}-1\Big)^{2}\leq\Big(\frac{(n+a)p}{b}\Big)^{2}+1\leq 2n^{2}p^{2},

we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{(n+a)p}{b+X_{n}}-1\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{(n+a)p}{b+X_{n}}-1\Big)^{2}{\rm I}({\cal A}_{n})\Big]+{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{(n+a)p}{b+X_{n}}-1\Big)^{2}{\rm I}(\overline{{\cal A}}_{n})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\frac{(n+a)^{2}p^{2}}{(b+X_{n})^{2}}\Big(\frac{b+X_{n}}{(n+a)p}-1\Big)^{2}{\rm I}({\cal A}_{n})\Big]+2n^{2}p^{2}\mathbb{P}(\overline{{\cal A}}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-2}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{b+X_{n}}{(n+a)p}-1\Big)^{2}\Big]+2n^{2}p^{2}\mathbb{P}(\overline{{\cal A}}_{n})

\displaystyle=O\Big(\frac{1}{np}\Big),

where we used the inequality

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{b+X_{n}}{(n+a)p}-1\Big)^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{2}{(n+a)^{2}p^{2}}\big(\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-np)^{2}]+(b-ap)^{2}\big)=O\Big(\frac{1}{np}\Big).

The proof of Lemma
1
is complete.
∎

Lemma 2
.

If random variable
X_{n}
has a binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
,
then for any fixed positive constants
b
and
\alpha
, as
np\to\infty
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\frac{1}{(b+X_{n})^{\alpha}}\Big]=\frac{1}{(np)^{\alpha}}(1+o(1)).

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Theorem 2 in
[
23
]
.
∎

We now give a formal proof of Theorem
2
in the following.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By (
13
), the index
J_{ij}^{(n)}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle J_{ij}^{(n)}=\frac{p}{2-p}+\frac{(2-p)S_{ij}^{(n)}-pT_{ij}^{(n)}}{(n-2)p(2-p)^{2}}+R_{ij}^{(n)},\quad 1\leq i\neq j\leq n,

(17)

where the remainder term

\displaystyle R_{ij}^{(n)}=\frac{(2-p)S_{ij}^{(n)}-pT_{ij}^{(n)}}{(n-2)p(2-p)^{2}}\left(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{T_{ij}^{(n)}}-1\right).

(18)

Note the special case in (
17
) that the remainder term

R_{ij}^{(n)}
vanishes if
T_{ij}^{(n)}=0
.
Taking expectation on both sides of (
17
) gives that for any distinct vertices
i,j\in[n]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[R_{ij}^{(n)}\big]=0.

(19)

Denote by
R_{n}
the sum of all the remainder terms, i.e.,

\displaystyle R_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}R_{ij}^{(n)}.

(20)

Then it follows by (
19
) that
{\mathbb{E}}[R_{n}]=0
.
By (
2
) and the simple fact
I_{ik}\vee I_{jk}=I_{ik}+I_{jk}-I_{ik}I_{jk}
,
we have that for any
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
,

\displaystyle(2-p)S_{ij}^{(n)}-pT_{ij}^{(n)}=\sum_{k\neq i,j}\big[(2-p)I_{ik}I_{jk}-pI_{ik}\vee I_{jk}\big]=\sum_{k\neq i,j}\big[2I_{ik}I_{jk}-p\big(I_{ik}+I_{jk}\big)\big],

which, together with (
15
) and (
17
), implies that

\displaystyle J_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2-p}+\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\left(\frac{(2-p)S_{ij}^{(n)}-pT_{ij}^{(n)}}{(n-2)p(2-p)^{2}}+R_{ij}^{(n)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2-p}+\frac{2}{n(n-1)(n-2)(2-p)^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\sum_{k\neq i,j}\Big(\frac{2I_{ik}I_{jk}}{p}-\big(I_{ik}+I_{jk}\big)\Big)+\frac{2}{n(n-1)}R_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2-p}-\frac{4P_{1,n}}{n(n-1)(2-p)^{2}}+\frac{4P_{2,n}}{n(n-1)(n-2)p(2-p)^{2}}+\frac{2}{n(n-1)}R_{n},

(21)

where

\displaystyle P_{1,n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}I_{ij}\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{2,n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\sum_{k\neq i,j}I_{ij}I_{ik}

denote the number of edges and the number of paths of length two in
G(n,p)
, respectively.
Further, we can rewrite (
3
) as

\displaystyle\frac{(n-1)(2-p)^{2}}{\sqrt{8p(1-p)}}\Big(J_{n}-\frac{p}{2-p}\Big)=\sqrt{\frac{2p}{1-p}}\left(-\frac{P_{1,n}}{np}+\frac{P_{2,n}}{n(n-2)p^{2}}\right)+\frac{(2-p)^{2}}{n\sqrt{2p(1-p)}}R_{n}.

(22)

For
P_{1,n}
and
P_{2,n}
, it is not hard to obtain that their expectations are given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[P_{1,n}]=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)p,\quad{\mathbb{E}}[P_{2,n}]=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)(n-2)p^{2}.

Applying Theorem 3(iii) in
[
10
]
yields that
if
np\to\infty
and
n^{2}(1-p)\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2p}{1-p}}\left(\frac{P_{1,n}-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)p}{np},\frac{P_{2,n}-\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)(n-2)p^{2}}{2n(n-2)p^{2}}\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}(Z,Z),

which implies that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2p}{1-p}}\left(-\frac{P_{1,n}}{np}+\frac{P_{2,n}}{n(n-2)p^{2}}\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm D}}}Z.

(23)

To prove Theorem
2
,
by (
22
) and (
23
) it is sufficient to show

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}}{n\sqrt{p(1-p)}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\rm P}}}0.

That is, by Chebyshev’s inequality and the fact
{\mathbb{E}}[R_{n}]=0
, we only need to prove

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[R_{n}]=o\big(n^{2}p(1-p)\big).

(24)

On the other hand, by symmetry, it follows by (
20
) that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[R_{n}]

\displaystyle={\rm Cov}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}R_{ij}^{(n)},~\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}R_{ij}^{(n)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}{\rm Cov}\Bigg(R_{12}^{(n)},~\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}R_{ij}^{(n)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1){\rm Var}\big[R_{12}^{(n)}\big]+n(n-1)(n-2){\rm Cov}\big(R_{12}^{(n)},R_{13}^{(n)}\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3){\rm Cov}\big(R_{12}^{(n)},R_{34}^{(n)}\big).

(25)

To prove (
24
), we next estimate the variance and covariances in (
3
) separately.
By the law of total expectation and (
19
), for the variance of
R_{12}^{(n)}
we have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\big[R_{12}^{(n)}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(R_{12}^{(n)}\big)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(R_{12}^{(n)}\big)^{2}\big|T_{12}^{(n)}\big]\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(R_{12}^{(n)}\big)^{2}\big|T_{12}^{(n)}=m\big]{\mathbb{P}}(T_{12}^{(n)}=m).

(26)

Recalling that
T_{12}^{(n)}\sim{\rm Bin}(n-2,p(2-p))
, and
R_{12}^{(n)}=0
if
T_{12}^{(n)}=0
.
By (
3
), (
4
) and (
18
), it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(R_{12}^{(n)}\big)^{2}\big|T_{12}^{(n)}=m\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{2}}\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{m}-1\Big)^{2}{\rm Var}\big(S_{12}^{(n)}\big|T_{12}^{(n)}=m\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1-p)}{(n-2)^{2}p(2-p)^{4}}\Big(\frac{(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{2}}{m}-2(n-2)p(2-p)+m\Big),

which, together with (
7
) and (
3
), implies that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\big[R_{12}^{(n)}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1-p)}{(n-2)^{2}p(2-p)^{4}}\sum_{m=1}^{n-2}\Big(\frac{(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{2}}{m}-2(n-2)p(2-p)+m\Big){\mathbb{P}}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}=m\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1-p)}{(n-2)(2-p)^{3}}\Big((n-2)p(2-p)\sum_{m=1}^{n-2}\frac{1}{m}{\mathbb{P}}(T_{12}^{(n)}=m)-1+2{\mathbb{P}}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}=0\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1-p}{n^{2}p}\right),

(27)

where in the last equality we used the simple fact

\displaystyle 0<{\mathbb{P}}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}=0\big)=(1-p)^{2(n-2)}\leq{\rm e}^{-2(n-2)p}=o\left(\frac{1}{np}\right).

To calculate the covariance of
R_{12}^{(n)}
and
R_{13}^{(n)}
,
by convention we introduce the following shorthand notation

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{ij}^{(n)}:=\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{T_{ij}^{(n)}}-1,

for distinct vertices
i,j\in[n]
. Recalling (
9
), we have

\displaystyle(2-p)S_{ij}^{(n)}-pT_{ij}^{(n)}=\sum_{k\neq i,j}V_{ij,k},\quad i,j\in[n].

By symmetry and (
18
), it thus follows that

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(R_{12}^{(n)},R_{13}^{(n)}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{4}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}\sum_{l\neq 1,3}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,k}V_{13,l}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{13}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{13}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-2)p^{2}(2-p)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,2}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{13}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{13}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(n-3)}{(n-2)p^{2}(2-p)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,4}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{13}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{13}^{(n)}>0\big)\big],

(28)

which splits the covariance into two parts.
Notice that, by (
9
), the discrete random variable
V_{ij,k}\neq 0
if and only if
I_{ik}\vee I_{jk}=1
.
This implies that if
V_{12,3}V_{13,2}\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle T_{12}^{(n)}=1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{13}^{(n)}=1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{3k},

and they have the same distribution. Since it follows that, by conditioning on
I_{23}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[V_{12,3}V_{13,2}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[\big((2-p)I_{13}I_{23}-p(I_{13}\vee I_{23})\big)\big((2-p)I_{12}I_{23}-p(I_{12}\vee I_{23})\big)\big]

\displaystyle=p{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big((2-p)I_{13}-p\big)\big((2-p)I_{12}-p\big)\big]+(1-p){\mathbb{E}}\big[p^{2}I_{13}I_{12}\big]

\displaystyle=p^{3}(1-p)^{2}+p^{4}(1-p)

\displaystyle=p^{3}(1-p),

and that, by Lemma
1
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}}-1\Big)\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{3k}}-1\Big)\Big]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}}-1\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=O\Big(\frac{1}{np}\Big),

we have

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,2}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{13}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{13}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}\Big[V_{12,3}V_{13,2}\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}}-1\Big)\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{3k}}-1\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~p^{3}(1-p)\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}}-1\Big)\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k=4}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{3k}}-1\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~O\Big(\frac{p^{2}(1-p)}{n}\Big).

(29)

Let us define

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{ab}:=\big\{I_{12}\vee I_{23}=a,~I_{14}\vee I_{24}=b\big\},\quad a,b\in\{0,1\}.

Noting that
\mathbb{E}[V_{12,3}]=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}{\rm I}(I_{12}\vee I_{23}=1)\big]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}{\rm I}(I_{12}\vee I_{23}=0)\big]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}\big[I_{13}{\rm I}(I_{12}=I_{23}=0)\big]

\displaystyle=p^{2}(1-p)^{2},

which implies that for any
a,b=0
or 1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,4}{\rm I}({\cal B}_{ab})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}{\rm I}(I_{12}\vee I_{23}=a)\big]\mathbb{E}\big[V_{13,4}{\rm I}(I_{14}\vee I_{24}=b)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}{\rm I}(I_{12}\vee I_{23}=a)\big]\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}{\rm I}(I_{12}\vee I_{23}=b)\big]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{a+b}p^{4}(1-p)^{4}.

Analogously to (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,4}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{13}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{13}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{b=0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,4}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{13}^{(n)}{\rm I}({\cal B}_{ab})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{b=0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,4}W_{12}(b)W_{13}(a){\rm I}({\cal B}_{ab})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{b=0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[V_{12,3}V_{13,4}{\rm I}({\cal B}_{ab})\big]\mathbb{E}\big[W_{12}(b)W_{13}(a)\big]

\displaystyle=p^{4}(1-p)^{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(W_{12}(0)-W_{12}(1)\right)\left(W_{13}(0)-W_{13}(1)\right)\right],

(30)

where

\displaystyle W_{ij}(a):=\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+a+\sum_{k=5}^{n}I_{ik}\vee I_{jk}}-1,\quad i,j\in[n],~a=0,1.

Noting that
W_{12}(0)-W_{12}(1)
and
W_{13}(0)-W_{13}(1)
have the same distribution,
by Lemma
2
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big|\big(W_{12}(0)-W_{12}(1)\big)\big(W_{13}(0)-W_{13}(1)\big)\big|

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(W_{12}(0)-W_{12}(1)\big)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{\big(1+\sum_{k=5}^{n}I_{1k}\vee I_{2k}\big)^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}}\right),

which, together with (
3
), (
3
) and (
3
), implies that

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(R_{12}^{(n)},R_{13}^{(n)}\big)=O\Big(\frac{1-p}{n^{2}}\Big).

(31)

It remains to calculate the second covariance
\mathbf{Cov}(R_{12}^{(n)},R_{34}^{(n)})
on the right-hand side of (
3
),
and the procedure is similar to the previous one.
We shall sketch the calculations below, and omit a few specific interpretations.
Analogously to (
3
), we have

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(R_{12}^{(n)},R_{34}^{(n)}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{4}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}\sum_{l\neq 3,4}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,k}V_{34,l}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{34}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{34}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{(n-2)p^{2}(2-p)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,5}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{34}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(n-4)^{2}}{(n-2)^{2}p^{2}(2-p)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,5}V_{34,5}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{34}^{(n)}>0\big)\big].

(32)

Define

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{ab}:=\big\{I_{14}\vee I_{24}=a,~I_{23}\vee I_{24}=b\big\},\quad a,b\in\{0,1\}.

After straightforward calculations, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}{\rm I}({\cal C}_{00})\big]

\displaystyle=p^{3}(1-p)^{3},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}{\rm I}({\cal C}_{11})\big]

\displaystyle=p^{3}(1-p)\big(1+3(1-p)^{2}\big),

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}{\rm I}({\cal C}_{01})\big]={\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}{\rm I}({\cal C}_{10})\big]=-2p^{3}(1-p)^{3}.

Then one can conclude that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}{\rm I}({\cal C}_{ab})\big]=O\big(p^{3}(1-p)\big).

Hence, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{34}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{b=0}^{1}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}W_{12}(a)W_{34}(b){\rm I}({\cal C}_{ab})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{b=0}^{1}{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,1}{\rm I}({\cal C}_{ab})\big]{\mathbb{E}}[W_{12}(a)]{\mathbb{E}}[W_{34}(b)]

\displaystyle=O\Big(\frac{p^{2}(1-p)}{n}\Big).

(33)

Since
V_{12,3}
and
V_{34,5}
are independent and have the common mean 0,
it follows that

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,5}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{34}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,3}V_{34,5}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(I_{13}\vee I_{23}=1\big){\rm I}\big(I_{35}\vee I_{45}=1\big)\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{34,5}{\rm I}\big(I_{35}\vee I_{45}=1\big)\big]{\mathbb{E}}\Big[V_{12,3}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(I_{13}\vee I_{23}=1\big)\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k\neq 3,4,5}(I_{3k}\vee I_{4k})}-1\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{34,5}\big]{\mathbb{E}}\Big[V_{12,3}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(I_{13}\vee I_{23}=1\big)\Big(\frac{(n-2)p(2-p)}{1+\sum_{k\neq 3,4,5}(I_{3k}\vee I_{4k})}-1\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=0.

(34)

Similarly, we also have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[V_{12,5}V_{34,5}\widetilde{T}_{12}^{(n)}\widetilde{T}_{34}^{(n)}{\rm I}\big(T_{12}^{(n)}T_{34}^{(n)}>0\big)\big]=0.

(35)

Plugging (
3
), (
3
) and (
35
)
into (
3
) yields that

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(R_{12}^{(n)},R_{34}^{(n)}\big)=O\Big(\frac{1-p}{n^{3}}\Big),

which, together with (
3
), (
3
) and (
31
),
implies that

\displaystyle{\bf Var}[R_{n}]=O\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)+O\big(n(1-p)\big)+O\big(n(1-p)\big)=O\big(n(1-p)\big),

as
np\to\infty
.
This proves (
24
), and thus completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎
