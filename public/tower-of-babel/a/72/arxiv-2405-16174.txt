Abstract
To meet the stringent requirements of next-generation wireless networks, MIMO ( MIMO ) technology is expected to become massive and pervasive. Unfortunately, this could pose scalability issues in terms of complexity, power consumption, cost, and processing latency. Therefore, novel technologies and design approaches, such as the recently introduced holographic MIMO paradigm, must be investigated to make future networks sustainable. In this context, we propose the concept of a DSA ( DSA ) as a versatile 3D structure capable of performing joint wave-based computing and radiation by moving the processing from the digital domain to the EM ( EM ) domain. We provide a general analytical framework for modeling DSA , introduce specific design algorithms, and apply them to various use cases. The examples presented in the numerical results demonstrate the potential of DSA to further reduce complexity and the number of RF ( RF ) chains in holographic MIMO systems while achieving enhanced EM wave processing and radiation flexibility for tasks such as beamforming and single- and multi-user MIMO .

I-A

Related State of the Art

The idea of introducing passive scattering elements close to an antenna to exploit mutual coupling in the reactive near-field region and shape its radiation properties dates back to the early days of wireless transmissions. A pioneering work is that by H. Yagi published in 1928 that introduced the classic Yagi-Uda array antenna. This antenna consists of a set of linear parallel dipoles with a spacing of about
0.2-0.3\lambda
, where the first dipole is active (i.e., fed with the signal) and the others act as passive scatterers
[
28
]
.
Over the subsequent decades, this idea has been extensively developed in various directions within the antenna theory community.

In more recent times, the introduction of new technologies and materials has enabled the development of new antenna structures designed to meet the demands of higher performance, flexibility, frequency, and lower cost, as required by new-generation wireless systems.
Among these advancements, metasurface antennas have received particular attention. They are composed of subwavelength elements printed on a grounded dielectric slab. These antennas exploit the interaction between a cylindrical surface wave, excited by a monopole, and an anisotropic impedance boundary condition realized with passive printed elements to produce an almost arbitrary aperture field
[
29
]
.
The dynamic counterpart to static metasurface antennas is the
DMA
(
DMA

RF
chain is needed for each row of the planar array,
DMA
offer a compelling solution, balancing flexibility with a reduction in
RF
chains
[
30
,
16
]

Static and dynamic metasurface antennas represent a promising avenue for the research aimed at reducing power consumption in wireless systems, thanks to their ability to shape signals at the
EM
level.
Other recent technologies include fluid and reconfigurable antennas
[
31
,
32
,
33
]

However, they still lack the necessary flexibility to completely replace their digital counterparts, especially for complex linear processing operations.

Similarly, a

By properly configuring the transmission properties of each meta-atom in the

The study of

For instance, the distance between the layers must be several wavelengths to validate the currently adopted cascade model. Power losses and signal distortion caused by the multiple layers and the bounding box require further investigation. A key limitation of

I-B

Our Contribution

In this paper, we propose a 3D
EM
antenna structure, termed
DSA
, that offers significant flexibility by providing enhanced processing capabilities directly at the
EM
level. Specifically, a
DSA
consists of a limited number of active antenna elements, each associated with an
RF
chain, surrounded by a cloud of reconfigurable passive scatterers that interact with each other in the reactive near field.

We first characterize the input-output characteristic of the
DSA
as a function of its configuration parameters and provide a fully analytical modeling using Hertzian dipoles.
Subsequently, we propose a strategy to optimize these parameters according to the desired processing functionality and apply this strategy to three different use cases of interest: beamforming, multi-user
MISO
(
MISO
), and
MIMO
.
Numerical results for each use case demonstrate the great flexibility of the
DSA
in performing various signal processing tasks. For instance, we will show that the tight coupling of
DSA
elements allows for the realization of superdirectivity behavior independently of the beam’s direction, in contrast to standard arrays where superdirectivity can only be achieved in the end-fire direction
[
39
,
20
]
.

Compared to
SIM
, in a
DSA
, all the elements contribute jointly to the processing and radiation processes, thereby enhancing flexibility with more compact structures.
Indeed, in a
SIM
, only the elements of the last layer radiate, and thus, the maximum achievable gain is proportional to their number, similar to a conventional antenna array. Conversely, in the numerical results, we demonstrate that a significantly higher gain can be achieved with a
DSA
compared to a standard array.
Furthermore,
SIM
assume only forward propagation from one layer to the next, with no coupling between the elements of each layer. While this simplifies modeling and optimization significantly, it presents critical challenges in decoupling implementation and imposes size constraints. For example, in
[
27
]
and
[
34
]
, a separation of
5\lambda
between each layer is considered, limiting the possibility of obtaining compact structures. In contrast, our numerical results demonstrate that with a
DSA
, better performance can be achieved with a spacing between elements of
\lambda/4
, thanks to the joint processing and radiation involving all the elements composing the
DSA
.

The results obtained in this paper demonstrate the potential of
DSA
to realize wave-based computation “over the air” by leveraging the coupling between active and scattering elements, thus minimizing the number of
RF
chains and baseband processing in the digital domain. This is achieved through a semi-passive structure with minimal energy consumption and nearly zero latency, as the processing occurs at the speed of light.

III
DSA with Hertzian Dipoles

To further develop our framework and derive some numerical examples from which obtaining important insights about the potential of
DSA
, we suppose that all the antenna elements in the
DSA
, both active and scatterers, are modeled as Hertzian dipoles.
In addition to making the derivation analytically tractable, the consideration of Hertzian dipoles can also be justified by the fact that the discrete infinitesimal dipole approximation is a flexible technique in computational electromagnetics for modeling and solving more complex antenna/scatterers structures
[
43
]
.

Accordingly, we model the
n
th dipole as a very short wire of length
l_{n}
and radius
r_{n}
, with
l_{n},r_{n}\ll\lambda
and generic orientation
\bm{\Omega}_{n}=\left[\sin(\phi_{n})\,\cos(\psi_{n})\,,\sin(\phi_{n})\,\sin(\psi_{n})\,,\cos(\phi_{n})\right]^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
\phi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}
represent, respectively, the elevation angle and the angle with respect to axis
z
, according to the reference coordinates system illustrated in Fig.
1
.
The
n
th element
i_{n}
of vector
\mathbf{i}
is the current present at the port of the
n
th dipole.

The total current density distribution in the
DSA
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{J}}({\bf{p}})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{\Omega}_{n}\,l_{n}\,i_{n}\,\delta({\bf{p}}-{\bf{p}}_{n})\,.

(28)

Therefore, the
EM
field at the generic location
{\bf{p}}
excited by the current density
{\mathsf{J}}({\bf{p}})
in free space can be evaluated as
[
25
]

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}({\bf{p}})=\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{p}}-{\bf{s}}}\right)\,{\mathsf{J}}({\bf{s}})\,d{\bf{s}}

(29)

where
\mathcal{V}
is the volume in which the current flows and
\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{r}}}\right)
is the Green’s dyadic given by
[
41
]

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{r}}}\right)=-\jmath\frac{\eta\,e^{-\jmath\kappa_{0}\,|{\bf{r}}|}}{2\lambda|{\bf{r}}|}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\cdot\left[\left({\bf{I}}-\hat{{\bf{r}}}\,\hat{{\bf{r}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)+\frac{\jmath\lambda}{2\pi|{\bf{r}}|}\left({\bf{I}}-3\hat{{\bf{r}}}\,\hat{{\bf{r}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(2\pi|{\bf{r}}|)^{2}}\left({\bf{I}}-3\hat{{\bf{r}}}\,\hat{{\bf{r}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right]

(30)

with
\kappa_{0}=2\pi/\lambda
being the wave number and where we grouped the terms multiplying the factors
1/|{\bf{r}}|
,
1/|{\bf{r}}|^{2}
, and
1/|{\bf{r}}|^{3}
.
By substituting (
28
) in (
29
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}({\bf{p}})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\,l_{n}\,i_{n}\,\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{p}}-{\bf{p}}_{n}}\right)\,\bm{\Omega}_{n}\,.

(31)

The open-circuit voltage at the
m
th dipole caused by the current
i_{n}
of the
n
th dipole by assuming all the other currents are equal to zero is

\displaystyle V_{m}^{(\text{oc})}=l_{m}\,l_{n}\,i_{n}\,\bm{\Omega}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{p}}_{m}-{\bf{p}}_{n}}\right)\,\bm{\Omega}_{n}\,.

(32)

From (
32
), the generic element
Z_{m,n}=[\mathbf{Z}]_{m,n}
of the impedance matrix
\mathbf{Z}
associated with the
DSA
can be easily obtained as

\displaystyle Z_{m,n}=\frac{V_{m}^{(\text{oc})}}{i_{n}}=l_{n}\,l_{m}\,\bm{\Omega}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{p}}_{n}-{\bf{p}}_{m}}\right)\,\bm{\Omega}_{m}

(33)

for
n\neq m
. For
n=m
,
Z_{n,n}
corresponds to the self-impedance of the Hertzian dipole which is given by
[
41
]

\displaystyle Z_{n,n}=\frac{2}{3}\pi\eta\left(\frac{l_{n}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}+\frac{\ln\left(\frac{l_{n}}{r_{n}}\right)}{\jmath f_{0}\pi^{2}\epsilon l_{n}}\,.

(34)

In the free-space propagation condition, the channel transimpedance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}
in (
27
) can be easily derived.
To elaborate, the electrical field intensity at the
k
receiving test antenna located at
{\bf{t}}_{k}
can be computed once the currents
\mathbf{i}
, flowing into the dipoles (active and scatterers), are known

\displaystyle e_{k}=\bm{\Theta}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\,{\mathsf{E}}({\bf{t}}_{k})=\sum_{n_{1}}^{N}l_{n}\,\bm{\Theta}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{t}}_{k}-{\bf{p}}_{n}}\right)\,\bm{\Omega}_{n}\,i_{n}

(35)

where
\bm{\Theta}_{k}
is the polarization direction of the
k
th receiving antenna. As a consequence, the received signal (assuming matched load and normalized to an impedance
R_{\text{L}}=1\,
Ohm) is given by
y_{k}=e_{k}\sqrt{\frac{A_{\text{eff}}R_{\text{L}}}{\eta}}=e_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{2}\,G_{\text{R}}}{4\pi\eta}}
.
Finally, the
(k,n)
th element of the transimpedance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}
is given by

[{H_{\text{c}}}]_{k,n}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{2}\,G_{\text{R}}}{4\pi\eta}}l_{n}\,\bm{\Theta}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{t}}_{k}-{\bf{p}}_{n}}\right)\,\bm{\Omega}_{n}\,.

(36)

Since it is
|{\bf{r}}_{k,n}|=|{\bf{t}}_{k}-{\bf{p}}_{n}|\gg\lambda
, (
36
) can be well approximated by neglecting the
1/|{\bf{r}}|^{2}
and
1/|{\bf{r}}|^{3}
components (reactive field) in (
30
) thus leading to

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{{EJ}}\left({{\bf{r}}}\right)\simeq-\jmath\frac{\eta\kappa_{0}}{4\pi|{\bf{r}}|}\,e^{-\jmath\kappa_{0}\,|{\bf{r}}|}\,\left({\bf{I}}_{3}-\hat{{\bf{r}}}_{k,n}\,\hat{{\bf{r}}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{k,n}\right)

(37)

and

\displaystyle[{H_{\text{c}}}]_{k,n}\simeq-\jmath\frac{\eta}{2\lambda|{\bf{r}}|}\sqrt{\frac{G_{\text{R}}}{4\pi\eta}}l_{n}\,e^{-\jmath\kappa_{0}\,|{\bf{r}}|}\bm{\Theta}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\left({\bf{I}}_{3}-\hat{{\bf{r}}}_{k,n}\,\hat{{\bf{r}}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{k,n}\right)\bm{\Omega}_{n}\,.

(38)

IV
DSA Optimization

In this section, we illustrate a possible optimization strategy of the
DSA
along with its application to 3 different use cases. Numerical results associated with each use case will be reported in Sec.
V
.

IV-A

Joint Optimization of the DSA and the Digital Precoder

For a given propagation scenario characterized by the transimpedance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}
and
K
test points, suppose we fix a specification
{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}
on the desired end-to-end channel matrix. The reconfigurable parameters of the
DSA
and the digital precoder can be obtained by solving the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{{\bm{\theta},\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}},\,\alpha}}{\operatorname{minimize}}\left\|\alpha\,\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\bm{\theta})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}-{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}\right\|_{\text{F}}

(39)

\displaystyle s.t.\quad\|\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=R\,N_{\text{A}}

where the scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
accounts for the possible lack in the link budget to achieve
{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}
that has to be eventually coped with increased transmitted power.

Unfortunately, in general, the constrained optimization problem in (
39
) is not convex thus making its numerical solution more challenging. A possible approach to translating it into an unconstrained optimization problem is to resort to the following alternate optimization procedure:

•

STEP 0:
We set the candidate vector
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
to an initial guess value, for instance randomly chosen or equal to zero.

•

STEP 1:

For a fixed
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
, it is possible to find in closed form the values of
\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}
and
\alpha
that minimize the objective function and satisfy the constraint in (
39
). In particular, we solve the following equation

\displaystyle\alpha\,\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}={\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}

(40)

of which the minimum norm solution is

\displaystyle\alpha\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}=\left(\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\right)^{{\dagger}}{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}\,.

(41)

The corresponding values of
\hat{\alpha}
and
{\hat{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{D}}
are, therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R\,N_{\text{A}}}}\left\|\left(\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\right)^{{\dagger}}{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}\right\|_{\text{F}}

(42)

and

\displaystyle{\hat{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{D}}=\frac{1}{\hat{\alpha}}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\right)^{{\dagger}}{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}\,.

(43)

•

STEP 2:

Starting from the candidates
\hat{\alpha}
and
{\hat{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{D}}
from STEP 1, the following unconstrained minimization problem involving only
\bm{\theta}
is solved numerically

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\underset{{\bm{\theta}}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\left\|\hat{\alpha}\,\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}-{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}\right\|_{\text{F}}\,.

(44)

•

STEP 3: Repeat STEPS 1 and 2 for a given number
N_{\text{alt}}
iterations or until no significant variations of the parameters take place.

The inclusion of the digital precoder, while not strictly necessary, may aid in the optimization process of the
DSA
by handling a portion of the processing when
N_{\text{A}}>1
. If omitted, we set
{\hat{\mathbf{W}}}_{\text{D}}=\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}=\sqrt{R}\,{\bf{I}}
, and only proceed with STEP 2 once.
Of course, alternative optimization problems apart from (
39
) can also be considered, such as those involving the
Q
-factor as an optimization target or constraint.
In many applications (e.g., beam steering, as seen in use case 1 below), the optimization process can be performed offline, computed once to create a dictionary of various
DSA
parameters for different steering directions of interest or, more broadly, different beam forms. In such cases, the complexity of the optimization algorithm is not a concern.
However, in other scenarios, performing optimization offline may not be feasible, necessitating online optimization based, for example, on the
CSI
(
CSI
). In this situation, developing efficient algorithms to minimize (
44
), potentially considering the structure of
\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})
, becomes pertinent. This aspect exceeds the scope of this paper and is reserved for future investigations.
In the numerical results, the standard numerical tool based on the quasi-Newton method
[
44
]
with
N_{\text{i}}
iterations is utilized to minimize (
44
).

Use Case 1: Beam Forming

Suppose we want to design a single-input

(
N_{\text{A}}=1
, no digital precoding) realizing a generic radiation diagram, for instance, imposing that at the test locations
{\bf{t}}_{k}
, the channel matrix
[{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}]_{k,1}
,

, assumes the desired value (see Fig.
2

For instance, one might define a uniform set of test points
{\bf{t}}_{k}=[d\sin\phi_{k},0,d\cos\phi_{k}]^{\mathsf{T}}
on the
x-z
plane a distance
d
in the far-field region of the
DSA
, with
\phi_{k}=2\pi k/K
, and maximize the radiation diagram at the
k=\tilde{k}
th direction
\phi_{\tilde{k}}
(beam steering). This can be achieved by setting
[{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}]_{k,1}=\sqrt{P_{\text{R}}}
, for

, and zero otherwise, being

the desired received power. Incidentally, after the optimization process, the resulting parameter
\hat{\alpha}^{2}
will represent the required transmitted power
P_{\text{T}}
to satisfy the link budget.
It is worth noticing that, in principle, only the optimization at the single test point

Use Case 2: Multi-user

In this second use case, we consider a multi-user

RF

single-antenna users located in generic positions
{\bf{t}}_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,K
, by maximizing the

(
SINR
) at each user according to the zero-forcing criterium
[
45
]
. Users can be both in the radiating near-field and far-field regions of the
DSA
.

Given the channel transimpedance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}=\left[{\mathbf{h}_{\text{c}}}_{1};{\mathbf{h}_{\text{c}}}_{2};\ldots;{\mathbf{h}_{\text{c}}}_{N_{\text{A}}}\right]
, where
{\mathbf{h}_{\text{c}}}_{k}
is the
N\times 1
channel vector associated with the
k
th user, the precoding matrix is
{\bf{V}}=\beta\,\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}^{{\dagger}}
, where
\beta
is a normalization factor to make
{\bf{V}}
unitary, i.e.,
{\bf{V}}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf{V}}={\bf{I}}_{N}

[
45
]
. Therefore, the
DSA
and the digital precoder must be designed such that

\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}={\bf{V}}=\beta\,\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}^{{\dagger}}

(45)

leading to

{\bf{y}}=\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}\,{\bf{x}}=\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}^{{\dagger}}\,{\bf{x}}\,.

(46)

This is equivalent to setting
{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}=\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}^{{\dagger}}
in the optimization problem in (
39
).

IV-D

Use Case 3: Multi-layer
MIMO
Communication

In this use case, we consider a
MIMO
communication with a receiving conventional
ULA
(
ULA
) with
K
elements spaced at
\lambda/2
illustrated in Fig.
4
. The purpose is to design the
DSA
in such a way an optimal multi-layer
MIMO
link is established between the transmitter and the receiver. It is well known from
MIMO
theory that for a given generic propagation scenario with channel transimpedance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}
characterized by rank
r
, up to
r
parallel orthogonal links (layers) can be established between the transmitter and the receiver on which independent data stream can be transmitted
[
45
]
. To exploit all of them, it must be
N_{\text{A}}=r
and the
DSA
must implement a suitable precoding strategy, i.e., act as an
Holographic
EM
precoder
.

Let us introduce the
SVD
of the channel transimpedance

\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}={\bf{U}}\,\bm{\Lambda}\,{\bf{V}}^{\mathsf{H}}

(47)

where
{\bf{U}}
and
{\bf{V}}
contain the left and right eigenvectors, respectively, and
\bm{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix gathering the singular values of the channel transimpedance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}
.
A typical
MIMO
setup, assuming the
CSI
is available at the transmitter, requires a precoding operation
{\bf{V}}
at the transmitter and a combining operation
{\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}
at the receiver such that the channel is diagonalized, i.e., the end-to-end channel matrix is proportional to the diagonal matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
. Assuming the receiver performs the combining operation
\tilde{{\bf{y}}}={\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}\,{\bf{y}}
, the
MIMO
channel is diagonalized if we design the
DSA
implementing the precoding
{\bf{V}}
by setting

\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}={\bf{V}}

(48)

or, equivalently,

{\bf{H}_{\text{opt}}}={\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}\,\bm{\Lambda}\,.

(49)

In this manner, it results

\tilde{{\bf{y}}}={\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf{H}}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}})\,{\bf{x}}={\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}\,{\bf{x}}=\bm{\Lambda}\,{\bf{V}}^{\mathsf{H}}\,{\bf{V}}\,{\bf{x}}=\bm{\Lambda}\,{\bf{x}}

(50)

and
N_{\text{A}}
parallel communication layers can be established. The designed
DSA
implements the optimal precoding by using no more than
N_{\text{A}}=r
RF chains (i.e., the minimum possible value) which is much simpler and less energy consuming than any conventional full digital or hybrid solution.

In our numerical investigations, we discovered that a more robust optimization is obtained by solving the following equivalent problem instead of (
44
)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\underset{{\bm{\theta}}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\left\|\hat{\alpha}\,{\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{H}_{\text{c}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{EM}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{D}}-\bm{\Lambda}\right\|_{\text{F}}

(51)

that consists in multiplying both terms in (
44
) by
{\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}
.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced the concept of the
DSA
(
DSA
) as an appealing technology aimed at shifting part of the signal processing from the digital domain to the
EM
domain. This is achieved through the joint optimization of
EM
processing and radiation of active and reconfigurable scattering elements interacting in the reactive near field.
We have shown that the
DSA
provides unprecedented flexibility in managing the
EM
field by reducing or circumventing digital processing at the baseband, thereby surpassing the flexibility offered by classical
MIMO
and recent
SIM
implementations. Consequently, the reduction in
RF
chains and digital processing facilitates the realization of energy-efficient, low-latency, and cost-effective holographic
MIMO
systems, then addressing the sustainability concerns of future wireless networks.

Although numerous implementation challenges must be addressed before
DSA
can become a reality, such as the design of flexible reconfigurable scatterers, we hope that our promising theoretical results will inspire further research into technologies for the efficient implementation of
DSA
as a key enabler of holographic
MIMO
within the
ESIT
paradigm.
