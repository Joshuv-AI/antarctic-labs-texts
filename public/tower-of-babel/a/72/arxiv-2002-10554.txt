Irreducibility of Random Polynomials of Bounded Degree
Max Wenqiang Xu

Abstract
It is known that random monic integral polynomials of bounded degree d d and integral coefficients distributed uniformly and independently in [ − H , H ] [-H,H] are irreducible over ℤ \mathbb{Z} with probability tending to 1 1 as H → ∞ H\to\infty . In this paper, we give a general criterion for guaranteeing the same conclusion under much more general coefficient distributions, allowing them to be nonuniformly and dependently distributed over arbitrary sets.

Introduction

A lot of research has been done in studying the irreducibility and other arithmetic properties of random integral polynomials. In general, there are two different models often considered, namely, the bounded degree model, e.g., see
[
18
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
8
]
; and the bounded height model, e.g., see
[
6
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
7
]
. An excellent survey of this active research area can be found in
[
5
]
. In the bounded height model, the polynomial
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{0}
has growing degree
d\to\infty
while the coefficients
a_{i}
’s are chosen independently and uniformly at random from a set of fixed size. In the bounded degree model, the polynomial has fixed degree
d
while the integral coefficients
a_{i}
’s are chosen independently and uniformly at random from
[-H,H]
with
H\to\infty
. Most of the previous results in the bounded degree model applied only to this specific distribution of the coefficients. In this paper we focus on studying irreducibility over
\mathbb{Z}
of random polynomials in a wide class of bounded degree models.

1.1
Background

Van der Waerden
[
18
]
proved that the probability of irreducibility over
\mathbb{Z}
of a random polynomial where all integral coefficients are distributed independently and uniformly at random in
[-H,H]
tends to 1. Chela
[
15
]
found the tight bound
(1+o(1))C_{d}/H
(where
C_{d}
is a constant only depending on degree
d
) for the probability that such a polynomial is reducible.

It would be interesting to know if this phenomenon of irreducibility over
\mathbb{Z}
of random polynomials is more general, as the previous proofs depend heavily on the fact that the integral coefficients are drawn uniformly and independently at random from the interval
[-H,H]
. However, it does not seem to be the case that irreducibility over
\mathbb{Z}
depends heavily on the exact distributions of the coefficients. In fact, it is intuitive that for a generic distribution of random integral polynomials, the random polynomial is irreducible with high probability. Some numerical evidence that supports this can be found in
[
5
]
. Indeed, in this paper, we show that under much more general distributions of the coefficients, the polynomial is reducible with small probability.

The methods used in
[
18
]
and
[
15
]
heavily used the multiplicative property of the Mahler measure (see
[
4
]
for details), which leads to the multiplicative property of the height of polynomials, where the height of an integral polynomial is the largest absolute value of the coefficients. When the integral coefficients are uniformly and independently distributed in an interval, this multiplicative property of the height allows us to efficiently constrain the possible factors of the polynomial (if there exists one), from which we can count the number of polynomials that admit a nontrivial factorization over
\mathbb{Z}
. However, when the integral coefficients are not uniformly distributed in an interval, for example, when they are distributed on a sparse subset of a big interval, then the height is much larger than the size of the support of the coefficients, and the above argument does not give good bounds. In this paper, we will introduce new methods to cover such cases, even allowing for the support of the coefficients to be arbitrary sets.

In
[
13
]
, Rivin introduced a method to show that random polynomials are irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
with high probability by exploiting the cases where the constant coefficient has few divisors. This method can be used to quickly obtain van der Waerden’s result. If the constant integral coefficient is distributed uniformly in
[-H,H]
, then it is not hard to show it has very few divisors on average. This can be used to prove an upper bound
O(\log H/H)
on the probability that the polynomial is reducible. A version of this method was utilized by Bary-Soboker and Kozma
[
1
]
and Chern
[
8
]
to prove irreducibility of random integral polynomials generalizing the uniform distribution over
[-H,H]
to other distributions of random polynomials which satisfy certain conditions. In particular, both papers consider the case where the coefficients are chosen independently and uniformly from a set
S
of integers such that there is some small prime
p
(not much larger than
|S|
) for which the elements of
S
are distinct modulo
p
. They then combine the factorization of the constant coefficient over
\mathbb{Z}
together with the factorization of the polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
to deduce the bounds on the probability of reducibility.

It is crucial in
[
1
]
and
[
8
]
that the coefficients are distributed independently and further satisfied strong congruence relations modulo a small prime. Thus, these results do not apply to the case of coefficients distributed over sets that are not well-behaved modulo
p
, such as the set of polynomial values of an integral polynomial, or to the case of dependent coefficients.

In another direction, one can ask if similar results hold when only a subset of the coefficients is randomized. Cohen
[
11
,
12
]
(together with
[
10
]
) showed that the probability of irreducibility tends to one when all but two coefficients are fixed and appropriate conditions hold. In fact, Cohen’s results apply to the Galois group of the polynomial. However, they come with weaker probability bounds that are suboptimal under our settings, and require the coefficients to be uniformly and independently distributed in a box.

Our main contribution in this paper is to address the above restrictions of the previous methods. In particular, we give nearly optimal bounds for the probability of reducibility even when we allow for the coefficients to have nontrivial dependency and non-uniform distribution over sparse sets. Our results also hold even if we only randomize one appropriate coefficient of the polynomial.
By working directly in a field of charateristic
0
instead of mapping to a finite field, we completely avoid the congruence restrictions on the distribution of the coefficients.
Our technique is also based on the surprising observations of
[
13
]
. However, we crucially make explicit and quantitative some objects in the argument. Via this explicit construction, we verify the key assumption for the validity of the argument by using complex analysis.

1.2
Main Result

We consider random polynomials with random coefficients where the degree
d
of the polynomial is fixed and the distribution of the coefficients has support growing to infinity. We show that, under mild assumptions on the joint distribution of the coefficients, the probability that the random polynomial is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
tends to 1 as the support of the coefficient distribution tends to infinity. In particular, we show that this holds even when the coefficients are not independently or uniformly distributed. Moreover, we allow the coefficients to be supported on arbitrary subsets of integers with size tending to infinity instead of the interval
[-H,H]
. In fact, our result applies even when only one appropriate coefficient is random, while the remaining coefficients are fixed. These give significant generalizations of previous results in the literature. We remark that we do not need the degree
d
of the polynomial to be fixed, but our bounds are meaningful only when
d
is quite small compared to the size of the support of the coefficient distributions.

Our method considers separately the distribution of the constant coefficient and the distributions of the higher-degree coefficients. For the constant coefficient, we only need to require its expected number of divisors to be small. For higher-degree coefficients, we allow
a_{i}
’s to be a polynomial change of variable from a product distribution that is not too far from the uniform distribution, satisfying a certain non-degeneracy condition. This relaxes both the uniformity and the independence of the coefficients. In all cases, we allow the support of the coefficients
a_{i}
to be completely arbitrary, significantly generalizing the bounded box model. For appropriate instances, we also show that it suffices to have one random coefficient
a_{i}
for
i
coprime to the degree
d
(or a subset of coefficients
a_{i},i\in I
for
\operatorname{gcd}(\{d\}\cup I)=1
) while the rest of the coefficients are fixed or arbitrarily distributed.

Our most general result, Theorem
2.3
, gives a bound for the probability that the random polynomial is reducible under a very general class of coefficient distributions. Theorem
2.3
involves checking a mild non-degeneracy condition that is not straightforward to verify. Theorem
1.5
gives a special case of Theorem
2.3
where we can verify the condition, while still applying to general coefficient distributions. In the following two theorems, Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, we describe some straightforward specific applications of Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
2.3
in simplified settings, where already previous results in the literature do not apply. We expect that the general result, Theorem
2.3
, could be applicable to many more interesting situations.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, where
a_{0},\dots,a_{d-1}
are distributed randomly according to one of the following distributions.

1.

Sparse coefficient distribution:
a_{0}
is uniformly distributed over the set of polynomial values
P(n)
for
n\in[-H,H]
and
P
not the zero polynomial, the remaining
a_{j}
for
j\neq 0
are uniformly distributed over an arbitrary set of size
H
, and
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1}
are independent. Then there exists a constant
C(P)
only depending on
P(x)
such that

\mathbb{P}(f(x)\textrm{ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$})\geq 1-\frac{\text{deg}(P)}{2H+1}-\frac{2d2^{d}(\log{H})^{C(P)}}{H}.

2.

Nonuniform coefficient distribution:
a_{j}
is distributed according to the binomial distribution

for
\min(Hp,H(1-p))\to\infty
, and
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1}
are independent. Then

\mathbb{P}(f(x)\textrm{ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$})\geq 1-o(1).

3.

Dependent coefficient distribution:
a_{0}
is uniformly distributed over the set of polynomial values
P(n)
for
n\in[-H,H]
and
P
not the zero polynomial, and for

a_{i}=P_{i}(a_{i-1},\dots,a_{0},t_{i})

are polynomials of degree at most

for any fixed nonzero value of

are independently and uniformly chosen from an arbitrary set of size

. Then there exists a constant

only depending on

such that

\mathbb{P}(f(x)\textrm{ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$})\geq 1-\frac{\text{deg}(P)}{2H+1}-\frac{2d^{2}(2L)^{d}(\log{H})^{C(P)}}{H}.

In Case (1) of Theorem
1.1
, if we choose

, and

distributed uniformly over

, then we recover the classical model. Already in this case, we observe that the probability bound we provide is optimal up to polylogarithmic factors in

. By relaxing congruence conditions in
[
1
,
8
]
, we can allow for arbitrary distribution of

, for example, over polynomial values of any given integral polynomial. In
[
5
]
, a question is raised about irreducibility of polynomials whose coefficients are distributed according to the Binomial distribution. This motivates us to consider non-uniform coefficient distributions, of which Case (2) of Theorem
1.1
is an example. Case (3) of Theorem
1.1
is an example where the coefficients are not independently distributed. In general, the most general version of our result, Theorem
2.3
, allows for the coefficients
(a_{0},\dots,a_{d-1})
to be any polynomial function of a set of independent random variables
(t_{0},\dots,t_{m})
.

In many situations we can guarantee the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
under much weaker assumptions, where only one appropriate coefficient or an appropriate subset of coefficients of the polynomial is varied while the remaining coefficients are fixed or distributed according to an arbitrary distribution (similar to the setting of
[
11
,
12
]
). We denote by
\tau(n)
the number of divisors of

.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, where
a_{0},\dots,a_{d-1}
are distributed randomly according to the following distribution. For some

is uniformly distributed in an arbitrary set of size at least

Then

\mathbb{P}(f(x)\textrm{ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$})\geq 1-\mathbb{P}(a_{0}=0)-\frac{2d2^{d}\mathbb{E}[\tau(a_{0})]}{H}.

Furthermore, the same conclusion holds if for a subset

with

is uniformly distributed in an arbitrary set of size at least

Theorem
1.2
shows that

is irreducible with high probability even when we fix arbitrarily all coefficients apart from one appropriate coefficient, or an appropriate subset of coefficients.

We next introduce several useful definitions before stating a more general result, Theorem

Definition 1.3
.

We call a positive integer

if

.

In particular, any integer with bounded number of prime factors (counted with repeat, that is,

), is

, infinitely many integers (primes) are

-simple and

-simple if

.

We also quantify the notion of being close to the uniform distribution.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
D
be a distribution supported on a finite subset of

of size

is
(C,t)
-uniform
if for any subset

The uniform distribution over

-uniform.

The next theorem gives a general class of coefficient distribution where we can give a quantitative bound on the probability of irreducibility. The statement of our most general result, Theorem

.

\displaystyle a_{k}

In particular:

1.

If

As we mentioned before, for the constant coefficient, our method only requires it to have a small number of divisors on average. This is true for uniform distribution over polynomial values of any given integral polynomial or any distribution over

Theorem
1.5
is a corollary of our most general result, Theorem
2.3
, which allows for more general coefficient distribution. As evident from the proof later given, we actually show that the probability that there exists a factorization

, where
g,h
are polynomials with complex coefficients whose constant coefficients are integral, is small. Since a polynomial is reducible over
\mathbb{Z}
only if such a decomposition exists, this gives an upper bound on the probability that the polynomial is reducible over
\mathbb{Z}
. Theorem
2.3
involves a non-degeneracy condition, which essentially captures cases when the probability of having a factorization into complex polynomials with integral constant coefficients is small. This condition can be verified in the setting of Theorem
1.5
, although it should be true in more general settings.

Our proof also suggests that significant new insights would be needed in order to deal with models where the non-degeneracy condition fails to hold, since considering the factorization of the constant coefficient alone would not suffice to provide a nontrivial bound on the probability of being reducible. See Remark
3.4
for more details on an interesting situation where this condition does not hold.

1.3
Strategy

The proof of Theorem
2.3
consists of two parts.
First, we transfer the question about studying irreducibility of the random polynomial
f
to studying the number of zeros of the polynomials
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
defined in Definition
2.1
. This idea was first used by Rivin in
[
13
]
. Here we provide an explicit construction of
P
in Lemma
2.2
, instead of using an elimination process in
[
13
]
, which is crucial to our verification of the non-degeneracy condition that a certain transformation of
P
remains nonzero. More discussions about this non-degeneracy condition can be found in Remark
3.4
. We complete the proof by using a version of Schwartz-Zippel Lemma, Lemma
2.4
, to bound the number of zeros over
\mathbb{C}
of multivariate polynomials.

Theorem
1.5
is a straightforward application of Theorem
2.3
. In the proof of Theorem
1.5
, we need to check the non-degeneracy condition of a transformation of
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
, which is studied in Theorem
3.3
via tools from complex analysis.

2
A general criterion for irreducibility of random polynomials

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.3
, which is a general sufficient criterion for irreducibility over
\mathbb{Z}
of random polynomials. Before stating the theorem, we need some preparations.

Let
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
in
\mathbb{C}[X]
.
We will introduce some explicit polynomials which can decode some information about the factorization of
f(x)
.

Definition 2.1
.

For any positive integers
d,k
, and integers
a_{0},b_{0}
, define

P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1}):=\prod_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq d}(b_{0}-\alpha_{i_{1}}\alpha_{i_{2}}\dots\alpha_{i_{k}}),

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}
are the (complex) roots of
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
in
\mathbb{C}[X]
.

We remark that the polynomial
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
defined above depends only on
d,k,a_{0},b_{0}
, since the product in the definition of
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
is a symmetric polynomial in the roots. Furthermore, the degree of
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
is at most
\binom{d}{k}<2^{d}
.

The following lemma relates the polynomial
P
defined above to the reducibility over
\mathbb{Z}
of the polynomial
f
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix complex numbers
a_{0},b_{0}\neq 0
. The polynomial
f(x)
in
\mathbb{C}[x]
with degree
d
can be factorized as a product of polynomials
g(x)
and
h(x)
in
\mathbb{C}[x]
such that
\text{deg}(g)=k
and the constant coefficient of
g
is
b_{0}
if and only if
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1})=0.
Here
k
is a positive integer smaller than
d
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}
be the roots of polynomial
f(x)
. Then

f(x)=g(x)h(x)
with fixed
a_{0},b_{0}
is equivalent to there is a product of
k
roots of
f
which is equal to
b_{0}
, i.e.,

\prod_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq d}(b_{0}-\alpha_{i_{1}}\alpha_{i_{2}}\dots\alpha_{i_{k}})=0.

By definition,
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1})=0.

∎

Now we can state our main theorem which gives sufficient conditions for a random polynomial to be irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
with high probability.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
L
be a positive integer. Let
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{0}
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
with positive degree
d>1
. Let
D_{t}
be a product distribution on
T_{0}\times\dots\times T_{m}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that the distribution on each
T_{i}
is
(C,2^{d}L)
-uniform, where
C
is a positive real number, and
|T_{i}|=k_{i}>0
. Suppose that the following two conditions hold:

1.

There is a deterministic polynomial function
F:\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
of degree at most
L
such that the distribution
D_{a}
is the push-forward of
D_{t}
under the map
(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\mapsto(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1}):=(t_{0},F(t_{0},\dots,t_{m}))
.

2.

For all fixed positive integers
k<d,
and
a_{0}\neq 0
and
b_{0}|a_{0}
, all the polynomials

P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}(F(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}))

are not identically zero, where
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
is defined explicitly as in Definition
2.1
.

Then the probability that
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{0}
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
is at least

1-\mathbb{P}_{D_{0}}(a_{0}=0)-2Cd2^{d}L\tau(D_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k_{i}},

where
D_{0}
is the distribution of
a_{0}
, and
\tau(D_{0}):=\mathbb{E}_{D_{0}}[\tau(a_{0})I(a_{0}\neq 0)]
.

Comparing to Theorem
1.5
,
Theorem
2.3
applies more generally to distributions of coefficients which are a polynomial change of variables from a product and close-to-uniform distribution that satisfies a further mild non-degeneracy condition.

To prove Theorem
2.3
, we use Lemma
2.4
, which is a version of the Schwartz-Zippel Lemma which bounds the number of zeros of a multivariate polynomial on a grid over
\mathbb{C}
. The proof is a straightforward generalization of the original proof of the Schwartz-Zippel Lemma, see
[
16
]
and
[
20
]
.

For a polynomial
P(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})
, we let
d_{m}
be the degree of
x_{m}
in
P
. We can then write
P
as

P(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})=x_{m}^{d_{m}}P_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})+Q_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),

where the degree of
x_{m}
in
Q_{m}
is at most
d_{m}-1
. Having defined
d_{m}
and
P_{m}
, for
2\leq i\leq m
, we then recursively define
d_{i-1}
to be the degree of
x_{i-1}
in
P_{i}
, and define
P_{i-1}
so that

P_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1})=x_{i-1}^{d_{i-1}}P_{i-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-2})+Q_{i-1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1}),

where the degree of
x_{i-1}
in
Q_{i-1}
is at most
d_{i-1}-1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
P(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})
be a polynomial of degree
d
which is not identically
0
and define
d_{i}
as above for
i\in[m]
. Let
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{m}
be
m
subsets of
\mathbb{C}
such that
|T_{i}|=k_{i}
. Let
D_{i}
be a distribution on
T_{i}
which is
(C,d_{i})
-uniform. Let
D=\prod_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\sim D}(P(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=0)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Cd_{i}}{k_{i}}.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
m
. The result is clearly true when
m=1
, as a nonzero univariate polynomial of degree
d
has at most
d
roots.

Assuming the result is true for all
m\leq h
, and consider
m=h+1
. Write

P(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})=P_{h+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{h})x_{h+1}^{d_{h+1}}+\sum_{i<d_{h+1}}Q_{h+1,i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{h})x_{h+1}^{i},

where
P_{h+1}
and
Q_{h+1,i}
are polynomials in
x_{1},\dots,x_{h}
, and
P_{h+1}
is not identically
0
. If
(x_{1},\dots,x_{h})
is so that
P_{h+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{h})\neq 0
, then there are at most
d_{h+1}
values of
x_{h+1}
so that
P(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})=0
. Thus, conditioned on
P_{h+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{h})\neq 0
, the probability that
P(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})=0
is at most
\frac{Cd_{h+1}}{k_{h+1}}
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})\sim D}(P(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})=0)\leq\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})\sim D}(P_{h+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{h})=0)+\frac{Cd_{h+1}}{k_{h+1}}.

By the inductive hypothesis,

\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})\sim D}(P_{h+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{h})=0)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{h}\frac{Cd_{i}}{k_{i}},

which gives

\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})\sim D}(P(x_{1},\dots,x_{h+1})=0)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{h+1}\frac{Cd_{i}}{k_{i}}.

Thus the conclusion is true for
m=h+1
as well, and we complete the proof.
∎

Observe that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}\leq\text{deg}(P)
. Hence, in the special case where
k_{i}=k
for all
i\in[m]
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\sim D}(P(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=0)\leq\frac{C\text{deg}(P)}{k}.

Also, if the degree of
P
is small, we can bound
d_{i}\leq\text{deg}(P)
and obtain the more convenient bound

\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\sim D}(P(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=0)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{C\text{deg}(P)}{k_{i}}.

Next we give the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

If
a_{0}=0
then
f(x)
is reducible over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Consider
a_{0}\neq 0
. Suppose
f(x)
is reducible, then there exists a positive integer
1\leq k\leq d-1
such

f(x)=g(x)h(x),

where

g(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j}x^{j}~\text{and}~h(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d-k}c_{j}x^{j}~~~\text{in }~\mathbb{Z}[X].

This gives a factorization
a_{0}=b_{0}c_{0}
, and there are at most
\tau(a_{0})
choices of
b_{0}
and
c_{0}
. We fix a choice of
b_{0}
. By Lemma
2.2
, since
f(x)
admits a factorization into
g(x)h(x)
with
g
having degree
k
and constant coefficient
b_{0}
, we must have
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1})=0
for some polynomial
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
depending on
d,k,a_{0},b_{0}
of degree at most
2^{d}
. Note that we even allow the coefficients of
g
except the constant coefficient to be any complex number. This gives a polynomial
Q(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})=0
via assumption (1), where
Q
has degree at most
\text{deg}(P)\text{deg}(F)\leq 2^{d}\text{deg}(F)
and
Q
is not identically zero by assumption (2). By Lemma
2.4
, the probability that
f
admits such a factorization is at most
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{C2^{d}\text{deg}(F)}{k_{i}}
. Finally, by taking the union bound over
b_{0}
and
k
, we get for nonzero
a_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}(f~\text{is reducible}\,|\,a_{0})\leq 2\tau(a_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Cd2^{d}\text{deg}(F)}{k_{i}}\leq 2\tau(a_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Cd2^{d}L}{k_{i}}.

Hence, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(f~\text{is irreducible})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}_{D_{0}}(a_{0}=0)-2\mathbb{E}_{D_{0}}\left[\tau(a_{0})I(a_{0}\neq 0)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Cd2^{d}L}{k_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}_{D_{0}}(a_{0}=0)-2Cd2^{d}L\tau(D_{a})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k_{i}}.

∎

3
Models of random polynomials

3.1
Models of the constant coefficient

In this subsection, we discuss various models of the distribution
D_{0}
of the constant coefficient
a_{0}
such that
\tau(D_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{D_{0}}[\tau(a_{0})I(a_{0}\neq 0)]
is not large.

Model 1: Uniform distribution on integral polynomial values

We use the following result due to van der Corput
[
17
]
.

Lemma 3.1

(van der Corput)
.

Suppose that
P(x)
is a polynomial with integral coefficients, then

\sum_{-H\leq n\leq H:P(n)\neq 0}\tau(P(n))\leq H(\log{H})^{C(P)}.

Here
C(P)
is a constant which only depends on the polynomial
P(x)
.

In other words, if
D_{0}
is the uniform distribution on the polynomial values
\{P(n):-H\leq n\leq H\}
, then there exists a constant
C(P)
depending on
P
such that

\tau(D_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{D_{0}}[\tau(a_{0})I(a_{0}\neq 0)]\leq(\log{H})^{C(P)}.

Notice that when
P(x)=x
, this is the same as the classical model where the coefficient
a_{0}
is chosen uniformly at random from
[-H,H]
.

Model 2: Arbitrary distributions supported on integers with few divisors

By our definition of
s
-simple integers, we immediately obtain the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any distribution
D_{0}
supported on
s
-simple integers,

\tau(D_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{D_{0}}[\tau(a_{0})I(a_{0}\neq 0)]\leq s.

3.2
Models of random polynomials

In this section, we apply our Theorem
2.3
to various models of random polynomials and give several concrete applications of our main theorem. In particular, we will prove Theorem
1.5
.

In order to apply Theorem
2.3
to specific models, the main condition to verify is that

P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}(F(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1}))

is not identically zero.
We show that this is the case when
F
is of the following form: for some integer
h\leq d
which is coprime to
d
,

\displaystyle(a_{j})_{1\leq j\leq d,j\neq h}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{h-1},a_{h+1},\dots,a_{d})

\displaystyle=F_{1}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1}),

\displaystyle a_{h}

\displaystyle=F_{2}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1},t_{m}).

Here,
F_{1}
is a polynomial mapping
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d-2}
, and
F_{2}
is a polynomial mapping
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}
such that

F_{2}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1})=\sum_{i=0}^{\text{deg}(t_{m})}P_{i}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1})t_{m}^{i},

and not all
P_{i}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1})
for
i\geq 1
are polynomials only in
t_{0}
.

Theorem 3.3

(Checking non-degeneracy)
.

Let
h\leq d
be coprime to
d
. Assume that
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1}
,
t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1}
are variables taking values in
\mathbb{R}
such that
a_{0}=t_{0}
. Let
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
be two fixed polynomials from
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d-2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}
respectively, such that
(a_{j})_{1\leq j\leq d-1,j\neq h}=F_{1}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1})
and
a_{h}=F_{2}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m})
, where

F_{2}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m})=\sum_{i=0}^{\text{deg}(t_{m})}P_{i}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1})t_{m}^{i},

and not all
P_{i}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1})
for
i\geq 1
are polynomials only in
t_{0}
.

Then the polynomial
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}((F_{1}(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1}),F_{2}(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m})))
in the variables
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
defined as in Definition
2.1
is not identically zero for all integers
1\leq k<d
, and nonzero
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
.

Proof.

By the definition of
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}
, it suffices to show that there exists a fixed choice of
t_{1}=s_{1},t_{2}=s_{2},\dots,t_{m-1}=s_{m-1}
, such that for some
t_{m}
the polynomial
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots a_{1}x+a_{0}
does not have
k
roots whose product is
b_{0}
.

Since
F_{2}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m})=\sum_{i=0}^{\text{deg}(t_{m})}P_{i}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1})t_{m}^{i}
and not all
P_{i}(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1})
for
i\geq 1
is a polynomial in
t_{0}
, there exists a choice of
t_{1}=s_{1},\dots,t_{m-1}=s_{m-1}
such that
F_{2}(a_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{m-1},t_{m})
is not identically zero as a polynomial in
t_{m}
. Fix such choice of
t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1}
. Since we fixed
a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m-1}
, the coefficients
a_{j}
for
j\neq h
are fixed constants. Since
F_{2}(a_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{m-1},t_{m})
is not identically zero, it is a univariate polynomial in
t_{m}
, hence
|a_{h}|
tends to infinity as
t_{m}
tends to infinity.

We prove that for fixed
a_{j},j\neq h
and
k,b_{0}
, for all
|a_{h}|
sufficiently large, the polynomial
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
does not have
k
roots whose product is
b_{0}
.

In particular, we first show that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
sufficiently small, if
|a_{h}|
is sufficiently large in
\epsilon
, for each root
\alpha
of the equation
x^{d-h}+\alpha=0
,
f
has a root within
\varepsilon|a_{h}|^{1/(d-h)}
of
\alpha
, and furthermore, the remaining
h
roots of
f
are within
\varepsilon
of
0
.

By the argument principle, the number of roots of the polynomial
f
in the region bounded by a circle
C
in the complex plane is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{dx^{d-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}(j+1)a_{j+1}x^{j}}{x^{d}+\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}a_{j}x^{j}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{hx^{h-1}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}(dx^{d-1}+\sum_{j\in[0,d-2],j\neq h}(j+1)a_{j+1}x^{j})}{x^{h}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}(x^{d}+\sum_{j\in[0,d-1],j\neq h}a_{j}x^{j})}dx.

Let
\delta>0
be arbitrary. Letting
C=\{x:|x|=\varepsilon\}
, if
|a_{h}|
is large enough (in
\varepsilon
and
\delta
), we have
for all
x\in C
,

\left|\frac{hx^{h-1}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}(dx^{d-1}+\sum_{j\in[0,d-2],j\neq h}(j+1)a_{j+1}x^{j})}{x^{h}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}(x^{d}+\sum_{j\in[0,d-1],j\neq h}a_{j}x^{j})}-\frac{h}{x}\right|<\delta.

Thus,

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}dx-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{h}{x}dx\right|<\delta.

Notice that

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{h}{x}dx=h,

and
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}dx
is an integer, we have

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}dx=h.

Hence
f
has
h
roots whose magnitude is at most
\varepsilon
.

Next, let
C=\{x:|x-\alpha|=\varepsilon|\alpha|\}
where
\alpha
is a root of
x^{d-h}+a_{h}=0
. Observe that
|\alpha|=|a_{h}|^{1/(d-h)}
. Assume that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. For
\delta>0
arbitrary, if
|a_{h}|
is large enough in
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, we claim that for
x\in C
,

\left|\frac{hx^{h-1}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}dx^{d-1}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}\sum_{j\in[0,d-2],j\neq h}(j+1)a_{j+1}x^{j}}{x^{h}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}x^{d}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}\sum_{j\in[0,d-1],j\neq h}a_{j}x^{j}}-\frac{hx^{h-1}+dx^{d-1}/a_{h}}{x^{h}+x^{d}/a_{h}}\right|<\delta.

To see this, note that for
x\in C
, we can write
x=\alpha+\varepsilon y
with
|y|=|\alpha|=|a_{h}|^{1/(d-h)}
. For
\varepsilon
sufficiently small and
a_{h}
sufficiently large,

|x^{h}+x^{d}/a_{h}|=|x|^{h}|(a_{h}+(\alpha+\varepsilon y)^{d-h})/a_{h}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{h}\cdot\varepsilon|y||\alpha|^{d-h-1}/|a_{h}|=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon|a_{h}|^{h/(d-h)}.

Since
\left|\frac{1}{a_{h}}x^{j}\right|\leq 2|a_{h}|^{(h-1)/(d-h)}
for
j\notin\{d,h\}
and
x\in C
, we can easily obtain the desired claim. Since

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{hx^{h-1}+dx^{d-1}/a_{h}}{x^{h}+x^{d}/a_{h}}dx=1,

as the function
g(x)=x^{h}+x^{d}/a_{h}
has a unique root
\alpha
in the region bounded by
C
, we have that

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{hx^{h-1}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}(dx^{d-1}+\sum_{j\in[0,d-2],j\neq h}(j+1)a_{j+1}x^{j})}{x^{h}+\frac{1}{a_{h}}(x^{d}+\sum_{j\in[0,d-1],j\neq h}a_{j}x^{j})}dx=1.

Hence, there is one root of
f
within
\varepsilon|a_{h}|^{1/(d-h)}
of each root
\alpha
of
x^{d-h}+a_{h}=0
.

The roots
\beta
of
f
with
|\beta|<\varepsilon
satisfy
a_{h}\beta^{h}=-a_{0}(1+o_{a_{h}\to\infty}(1))
, so
|\beta|=(1+o_{a_{h}\to\infty}(1))|a_{0}/a_{h}|^{1/h}
.
Thus, the polynomial
f
has
d-h
roots of magnitude
(1+o_{a_{h}\to\infty}(1))|a_{0}/a_{h}|^{1/h}
, and
h
roots of magnitude
(1+o_{a_{h}\to\infty}(1))|a_{h}|^{1/(d-h)}
. For any
k
roots of
f
, if there are
k_{1}
roots of magnitude
(1+o_{a_{h}\to\infty}(1))|a_{0}/a_{h}|^{1/h}
and
k_{2}
roots of magnitude
(1+o_{a_{h}\to\infty}(1))|a_{h}|^{1/(d-h)}
, then the product of the roots has magnitude
(1+o_{a_{h}\to\infty}(1))|a_{0}|^{k_{1}/h}|a_{h}|^{k_{2}/(d-h)-k_{1}/h}
. For
|a_{h}|
sufficiently large, in order for this product to be equal to
b_{0}\neq 0
, we need to have
k_{2}/(d-h)=k_{1}/h
. However, since
\operatorname{gcd}(d,h)=1
, this can only happen when
k=k_{1}+k_{2}=d
, contradiction.

Thus, there exists a choice of
t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1}
so that
f
does not have
k
roots whose product is
b_{0}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

We remark that while the nondegeneracy condition of
P_{d,k,a_{0},b_{0}}(F(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}))
being not identically
0
is likely generically true, it is important to verify this condition carefully. Indeed, there are some interesting families of random polynomials where it turns out that this condition fails to hold. In such cases, the technique of this paper breaks down and cannot give nontrivial bounds on the probability that the random polynomial is reducible over
\mathbb{Z}
. Hence, in such cases, considering only the factorization of the constant coefficient over
\mathbb{Z}
is not sufficient to show that the polynomial is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
with high probability. We next highlight one such case.

Let
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots a_{1}x+a_{0}
where each integer
a_{i}\in[-H,H]
. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}
be the (complex) roots of
f
.
A famous conjecture of van der Waerden from 1936 states that the Galois group
G_{f}
of polynomial
f
is equal to symmetric group
S_{d}
with probability
1-O(1/H)
.

Consider the following polynomial

f_{6}(x)=\prod_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{6}\leq d}(x-\alpha_{i_{1}}\alpha_{i_{2}}\dots\alpha_{i_{6}}).

This is polynomial of degree
\binom{d}{6}
in
x
with constant coefficient
a_{0}^{\binom{d-1}{5}}
. We denote each of its roots
\beta_{i}
.
For
d\geq 12
, it turns out that Galois Group of
f
is
6
-transitive (i.e., equals to
S_{d}
or
A_{d}
) if and only if
f_{6}(x)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Thus, to resolve van der Waerden’s conjecture, it would be interesting to first show that
f_{6}
is irreducible with probability
1-O(1/H)
. This was discovered by Rivin in
[
13
]
, and motivated us to consider the irreducibility of random polynomials with more general coefficient distributions. Unfortunately, if one wants to directly use the method in this paper, then it would fail since

P_{\binom{d}{6},6d,a_{0}^{\binom{d-1}{5}},a_{0}^{6}}(F(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1}))

is identically
0
. This is because there always exists a subset of tuples of
6
roots of
f
of size
d
whose product is
a_{0}^{6}
. Thus the probability that there exists a factorization of
f=gh
as complex polynomials where the constant coefficients of
g
and
h
are integers is
1
. This is a particular example where considering only the factorization of the constant coefficient is not sufficient to get nontrivial bounds on the probability of reducibility.

We next give the proofs of Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.5
, which readily follow from Theorem
2.3
and Theorem
3.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem
1.5
follows directly from Theorem
2.3
, Theorem
3.3
, Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Case (1): This follows directly from Theorem
1.5
with
L=1
and
C=1
.

Case (2): This follows from Theorem
1.5
and the observation that the binomial distribution
B(H,p)
is
((1+o_{pH\to\infty}(1))\sqrt{H/(2\pi p(1-p))},H)
-uniform.

Case (3): This follows from Theorem
1.5
, noting that we have

\displaystyle a_{d-1}

\displaystyle=F_{2}(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{d-1}),

\displaystyle(a_{j})_{1\leq j\leq d-2}

\displaystyle=F_{1}(a_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{d-2}),

where the polynomials
F_{1},F_{2}
have degree at most
L^{d}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The first claim of Theorem
1.2
follows from Theorem
1.5
and Lemma
3.1
.

To show the second claim, we need to verify the non-degeneracy condition in Theorem
2.3
via a modification of Theorem
3.3
. The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
3.3
, we only give a sketch of the key modifications in the following.

Let
I
be a subset of
[d-1]
for which
\operatorname{gcd}(\{d\}\cup I)=1
. Arrange the elements of
I
in decreasing order
i_{1}>i_{2}>\dots>i_{s}
. By fixing all coefficients outside
I
(so that
a_{0}\neq 0
), and considering an asymptotic where
a_{i_{1}}\to\infty
and
\omega_{t}:=a_{i_{t}}/a_{i_{t}-1}\to\infty
at appropriate rates:
\omega_{t}
tends to infinity sufficiently slow in
\omega_{t-1}
for
3\leq t\leq s
, and
\omega_{2}
tends to infinite sufficiently slow in
|a_{i_{1}}|
. By a similar argument to the proof of Theorem
3.3
, we can show that for each
2\leq t\leq k
, there are
i_{t-1}-i_{t}
roots of
f(x)=x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{0}
that are of order
\omega_{t}^{1/(i_{t-1}-i_{t})}
. Furthermore, there are
d-i_{1}
roots of order
|a_{i_{1}}|^{1/(d-i_{1})}
, and
i_{s}
roots of order
|a_{i_{s}}|^{-1/i_{s}}
. Then, as in Theorem
3.3
, we can verify that for any
k<d
, it cannot be the case that there always exists a subset of
k
roots whose product is equal to a fixed constant. This implies the second claim in Theorem
1.2
.
∎
