A Quantum Theory of Temporally Mismatched Homodyne Measurements with Applications to Optical Frequency Comb Metrology
Noah Lordi, Eugene J. Tsao, Alexander J. Lind, Scott A. Diddams, Joshua Combes

Abstract
The fields of precision timekeeping and spectroscopy increasingly rely on optical frequency comb interferometry. However, comb-based measurements are not described by existing quantum theory because they exhibit both large mode mismatch and finite strength local oscillators. To establish this quantum theory, we derive measurement operators for homodyne with arbitrary mode overlap. These operators are a combination of quadrature and intensity-like measurements, which inform a filter that maximizes the quadrature measurement signal-to-noise ratio. Furthermore, these operators establish a foundation to extend frequency-comb interferometry to a wide range of scenarios, including metrology with nonclassical states of light.

A temporal mode decomposition yields measurement operators comprising two parts: a quadrature measurement and an intensity-like measurement. This analysis informs a new quantum limit for comb-based measurement and provides quantum theoretic grounds for experimental results demonstrating better-than-shot noise-limited performance by Deschênes and Genest

Finally, we discuss the implications of our results for heterodyne, which is the standard measurement with frequency combs.

In conclusion, we have found the measurement operators for multimode homodyne that are valid for arbitrary time dependence of both the LO and signal. In our construction the measurement decomposes naturally into two parts: a quadrature measurement in the temporal mode of the LO and an intensity like measurement on the other modes.
We show that perfect filtering of this intensity noise achieves a quadrature noise-limited measurement. In comb-based measurements characterized by large mode-mismatch and strong signals, this establishes a significantly lower quantum limit than conventionally considered. This limit should be achieved before pursuing non-classical states of light. Moreover, because we have developed a fully quantum theory, we can analyze the measurement on any signal state including squeezed states. Our analysis complements related work on POVMs for electro-optic sampling
Hubenschmid et al. 2022

Many comb-based measurements are based on heterodyne rather than homodyne techniques. Although there are many reasons to prefer homodyne over heterodyne for quantum metrology, the technical limitations of frequency comb measurements makes quantum limited homodyne more difficult to achieve than heterodyne.
We conjecture that the heterodyne SNR is simply half that of homodyne due to sampling of both
x
and
p
quadratures, but a complete analysis of the heterodyne measurement operators is left as future work.

\langle\xi_{1},\xi\rangle=\gamma_{1}
and
\langle\xi_{2},\xi\rangle=\gamma_{2}
. For the case where we are just decomposing into two modes we have
\xi=\gamma_{1}\xi_{1}+\gamma_{2}\xi_{2}
and thus
|\gamma_{1}|^{2}+|\gamma_{2}|^{2}=1
. The idea of this derivation is now to show that
D(\alpha,\xi)\propto D(\gamma_{1}\alpha,\xi_{1})D(\gamma_{2}\alpha,\xi_{2})
where the constant of proportionality should be 1 in the cases we are concerned with. Start by writing the coherent state as a displacement operator acting on vacuum.

\displaystyle|\alpha(\xi)\rangle

\displaystyle=D(\alpha,\xi)|0\rangle=\exp\left[\alpha\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi)-\alpha^{*}\hat{A}(\xi)\right]|0\rangle

(26)

We briefly note that from the integral from of the mode operators it is simple to show that
A(\gamma_{1}\xi_{2}+\gamma_{2}\xi_{2})=\gamma_{1}^{*}A(\xi_{1})+\gamma_{2}^{*}(\xi_{2})
.
Now we just sub in the expression for
\xi
in the
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
basis and we have,

\displaystyle|\alpha(\xi)\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\alpha\left(\gamma_{1}\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi_{1})+\gamma_{2}\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi_{2})\right)-\alpha^{*}\left(\gamma_{1}^{*}\hat{A}(\xi_{1})+\gamma_{2}^{*}\hat{A}(\xi_{2})\right)\right]|0\rangle

Now just apply BCH to get this in terms of
D(\gamma_{1}\alpha,\xi_{1})D(\gamma_{2}\alpha,\xi_{2})C
where
C
is some correction factor.

\displaystyle\exp\left[\alpha\gamma_{1}\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi_{1})-\alpha^{*}\gamma_{1}^{*}\hat{A}(\xi_{1})+\alpha\gamma_{2}\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi_{2})-\alpha^{*}\gamma_{2}^{*}\hat{A}(\xi_{2})\right]

(27)

\displaystyle=D(\gamma_{1}\alpha,\xi_{1})D(\gamma_{2}\alpha,\xi_{2})\exp\left\{\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}\left(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{*}\left[\hat{A}(\xi_{2}),\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi_{1})\right]-\gamma_{1}^{*}\gamma_{2}\left[\hat{A}(\xi_{1}),\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi_{2})\right]\right)\right\}

Here we just need to use the fact that
\left[\hat{A}(\xi_{1}),\hat{A}^{\dagger}(\xi_{2})\right]=\langle\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\rangle
and we get the simplified form,

D(\alpha,\gamma_{1}\xi_{1}+\gamma_{2}\xi_{2})=D(\gamma_{1}\alpha,\xi_{1})D(\gamma_{2}\alpha,\xi_{2})\exp\left\{|\alpha|^{2}\left(\text{Im}(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{*}\langle\xi_{2},\xi_{1}\rangle\right)\right\}.

(28)

For the situation in the paper we have
\langle\xi_{\perp},\xi_{\text{LO}}\rangle=0
so the correction term is
0
and we recover the expected,

|\alpha(\xi_{2})\rangle=|\gamma\alpha_{\xi_{\text{LO}}}\rangle\otimes|\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\alpha_{\xi_{\perp}}\rangle\,.

(29)

Appendix C
Mode Decomposition of Beamsplitter

We show the the beamsplitter acts the same for every mode, under the assumption that the transmission and reflection coefficients of the beamsplitter are constants over the relevant frequencies of the signal and LO mode. From our assumption we have the the action of the beamsplitter in every mode is described by the following input output relations,

\displaystyle U_{BS}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{A}(\xi)U_{BS}(\xi)=\frac{\hat{A}(\xi)+\hat{B}(\xi)}{\sqrt{2}}\quad\&\quad U_{BS}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{B}(\xi)U_{BS}(\xi)=\frac{\hat{A}(\xi)-\hat{B}(\xi)}{\sqrt{2}}\,.

(30)

We want to show that this implies a tensor product structure to the beamsplitter unitary, i.e we can split the unitary into a beamsplitter for each mode independently. We can demonstrate this by considering decomposing a single mode into a combination of modes and seeing how the unitary must act. Consider a annihilation operator in some mode
\hat{A}(\xi)
. Further allow
\xi
to be decomposed as
\xi=c_{1}\xi_{1}+c_{2}\xi_{2}+\dots
.
When we conjugate
\hat{A}(\xi)
by the beamsplitter we have,

\displaystyle U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi)U_{BS}

\displaystyle=U_{BS}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{n}c_{n}\hat{A}(\xi_{n})\right)U_{BS}\,.

(31)

but from
eq.

30
we know this must produce a sum of operators in the
\xi
mode.

U_{BS}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{n}c_{n}\hat{A}(\xi_{n})\right)U_{BS}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n}c_{n}\big(\hat{A}(\xi_{n})+\hat{B}(\xi_{n})\big)\,.

(32)

In order for this to hold we need the following to be true,

U_{BS}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}(\xi_{n})U_{BS}=\frac{\hat{A}(\xi_{n})+\hat{B}(\xi_{n})}{\sqrt{2}}.

(33)

This equation allows us to write
U_{BS}(\xi)
by its action on every mode. Meaning we can decompose the beamsplitter unitary into modes as

U_{BS}=U_{BS}(\xi_{1})\otimes U_{BS}(\xi_{2})\otimes\dots,

(34)

as desired.

We have omitted the mode label since every operator in this equation acts on the perpendicular mode. We now insert identity, i.e.

U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{A}U_{BS}=(\hat{A}+\hat{B})/\sqrt{2}

where the

\displaystyle M_{r,s}(\xi_{\perp})=\langle\mathrm{vac}|\frac{\hat{A}^{r+s}}{\sqrt{r!s!}2^{(r+s)/2}}

\displaystyle=\langle r+s|\frac{1}{2^{(r+s)/2}}\sqrt{{r+s\choose r}}

For these reasons we will scale the difference variable, but leave it discrete at the POVM level. Depending on the input state the difference variable can be made continous in a variety of ways, most commonly by approximating the binomial distribution as normal. The resulting marginalization over w will be difficult under this approximation but can easliy be solved numberically. After appropriately scaling variables we get the following POVM:

E_{x,w}dx=dx|w\rangle\langle w|\text{Bin}\Big(\frac{|\beta|x}{\sqrt{2}}\Big|w,\frac{1}{2},0\Big)

where

Appendix E
Photon number considerations

When discussing the large LO limit we can break the signals photons into the two modes and compare only the number of photons in the LO mode to the number of photons in the LO. There is a subtlety here because we effectively saying that
\langle\psi_{\text{S}}|\hat{n}(\xi_{\text{S}})|\psi_{\text{S}}\rangle=\langle\psi_{\xi_{\text{LO}}}|\hat{n}(\xi_{\text{LO}})|\psi_{\xi_{\text{LO}}}\rangle+\langle\psi_{\xi_{\perp}}|\hat{n}(\xi_{\perp})|\psi_{\xi_{\perp}}\rangle
, which is surprisingly nontrivial to prove because as an operator equation
\hat{n}(\xi_{\text{S}})\neq\hat{n}(\xi_{\text{LO}})+\hat{n}(\xi_{\perp})
. We know that the first equation must be true because it says that the total number of photons in the signal is equal to the number of photons from the signal in the LO mode plus the number of photons from the signal in the perpendicular mode. Since the signal mode is described by a linear superposition of just those two modes we know that no photons could fall in any other mode in our decomposition.

We can also provide evidence that it is true by taking an example of a coherent state signal. We use the decomposition of
eq.

3
before taking the expectation value,

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{\xi_{\text{S}}}|\hat{n}(\xi_{s})|\alpha_{\xi_{\text{S}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\gamma\alpha_{\xi_{\text{LO}}}|\hat{n}(\xi_{\text{LO}})|\gamma\alpha_{\xi_{\text{LO}}}\rangle+\langle\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\alpha_{\xi_{\perp}}|\hat{n}(\xi_{\perp})|\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\alpha_{\xi_{\perp}}\rangle

(52)

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\gamma|^{2}|\alpha|^{2}+(1-|\gamma|^{2})|\alpha|^{2}.

A proof of this fact for any signal state is more subtle, but it begins by introducing an auxiliary mode with no photons in it so that we can treat the change of mode basis thoroughly. This is always allowed since we are extending our single mode basis to include other modes which have no weight.

We write the signal as
|\pi_{\text{S}}\rangle=|\psi_{\xi_{\text{S}}}\rangle\otimes|0(\xi_{\text{aux}})\rangle
. We can now decompose our mode operators in the normal way where
\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})\to\gamma\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})+\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})
, only now we add in the auxilary mode so we can write the change of basis as the action of a unitary,

\begin{pmatrix}A(\xi_{\text{S}})\\
A(\xi_{\text{aux}})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}&-\gamma^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A(\xi_{\text{LO}})\\
A(\xi_{\perp})\end{pmatrix}

(53)

where we have filled in the bottom row by require the matrix to be unitary. Now we can calculate
\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})
and see that there are terms present that depend on both modes,

\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})=|\gamma|^{2}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})+(1-|\gamma|^{2})\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})+\gamma\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})+\gamma^{*}\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}}).

(54)

Similarly we carry out the same calculation for the auxilary mode

\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{aux}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{aux}})=|\gamma|^{2}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})+(1-|\gamma|^{2})\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})-\gamma\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})-\gamma^{*}\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}}).

(55)

We now take expectations of the auxilary mode
\langle\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{aux}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{aux}})\rangle=0
and we get the following condition,

\langle|\gamma|^{2}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})+(1-|\gamma|^{2})\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})\rangle=\langle\gamma\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})+\gamma^{*}\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})\rangle.

(56)

Now take expectation in the signal mode,

\langle\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})\rangle=\langle|\gamma|^{2}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})+(1-|\gamma|^{2})\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})\rangle+\langle\gamma\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})+\gamma^{*}\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})\rangle.

(57)

Finally just plug in the condition we derived above to get

\langle\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{S}})\rangle=\langle\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\perp})\rangle+\langle\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})^{\dagger}\hat{A}(\xi_{\text{LO}})\rangle,

(58)

as desired.

Combination Rule for POVM

Starting from the total Kraus operator in
n
and
m
variables

M_{n,m}=\sum_{p,q}M_{p,q}(\xi_{LO})\otimes M_{n-p,m-q}(\xi_{\perp})\,.

We can rewrite the order of this sum in terms of discrete sum and difference variables,

M_{n,m}=\sum_{p+q=0}^{n+m}\,\sum_{p-q=x_{\rm min}}^{x_{\rm max}}M_{p,q}(\xi_{LO})\otimes M_{n-p,m-q}(\xi_{\perp}),

x_{\rm max}=-x_{\rm min}

M_{n,m}=\sum_{p+q=0}^{n+m}\,\,\sum_{p-q=-p+q}^{p+q}M_{p,q}(\xi_{LO})\otimes M_{n-p,m-q}(\xi_{\perp})\,.

(61)

Now move to the sum and difference variables,
x=(p-q)/\sqrt{2}|\beta|
,
w=p+q
. Since we have scaled the difference variable so that it is small, we can approximate the sum over it as an integral. Applying all this in the large LO limit gives

This demonstrates that the total Kraus operator is a convolution of the two constituent Kraus operators. \displaystyle E_{n,m}

Now we need to remember the form of the single mode Kraus operators using

8

\displaystyle M^{\dagger}_{p,q}(\xi_{\text{LO}})(\xi_{\perp})M_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\xi_{\text{LO}})

\displaystyle=\langle\beta|U_{BS}^{\dagger}|p\rangle|q\rangle\langle p|\langle q||q^{\prime}\rangle|p^{\prime}\rangle\langle q^{\prime}|\langle p^{\prime}|U_{BS}|\beta\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\beta|U_{BS}^{\dagger}|p\rangle|q\rangle\langle q|\langle p|U_{BS}|\beta\rangle\delta_{p,p^{\prime}}\delta_{q,q^{\prime}},

E_{x,w}=\sum_{w^{\prime}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{|\beta|dx^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}E_{x^{\prime},w^{\prime}}(\xi_{LO})\otimes E_{x-x^{\prime},w-w^{\prime}}(\xi_{\perp})\,.

Appendix G
Time Dependent Photo Record

If we now assume that our detector does produce timing information then we can still get the same answer but our measurement operators must change. We still want to average the time-dependent photo-record which will be accomplished by coarse-graining over time. First we must make some assumptions about our detector. We will model the detector time dependence by saying each detection even is contained in a time bin
(t_{i},t_{i}+\Delta t)
. We will also assume that
\Delta t
is small with respect to the total detection time
T
.

With these assumptions, we can write a corrected form of our detector.

\displaystyle|n\rangle_{D}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=|0\rangle\otimes\dots|0\rangle\otimes|n\rangle\otimes|0\rangle\otimes\dots|0\rangle

(67)

\displaystyle\equiv|n_{i}\rangle

This represents getting n clicks in the
i^{\text{th}}
time bucket. With these, we can write the most general measurement operator for our apparatus as

M_{n,m}(t_{i},t_{j})=\langle n_{i}|\langle m_{j}|U_{BS}.

(68)

which corresponds to n clicks on one detector in the
i^{\text{th}}
and m clicks in the other in the
j^{\text{th}}
bucket. Up to this point, our measurement operators are fully time-dependent and indeed this analysis could be continued without coarse-graining, but the resulting theory is difficult to parse analytically and seems more suited for numerics. For this reason, we will consider the case of the averaged photo current which requires coarse-graining over time.

Once we have the measurement operators we can assemble the POVMs. The fully time-dependent POVMs would be

E_{n,m}(t_{i},t_{j})=M_{n,m}^{\dagger}(t_{i},t_{j})M_{n,m}(t_{i},t_{j})

(69)

and the naively the coarse-grained POVMs should be

\displaystyle E_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}M_{n,m}^{\dagger}(t_{i},t_{j})M_{n,m}(t_{i},t_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}U_{BS}^{\dagger}|n_{i}\rangle|m_{j}\rangle\langle n_{i}|\langle m_{j}|U_{BS}

n/2

\displaystyle E_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{i}}^{n}\sum_{m_{j}}^{m}U_{BS}^{\dagger}|n_{i}\rangle|m_{j}\rangle\langle n_{i}|\langle m_{j}|U_{BS}

(71)

where
\sum_{n_{i}}^{n}
indicates a sum over all possible values of
n_{1},n_{2},\dots
such that
\sum_{i}n_{i}=n
. An illustrative special case is when there are only two detection windows,
(t_{1},t_{2})
and we get:

\displaystyle E_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\sum_{q}U_{BS}^{\dagger}\left(|p\rangle_{1}\otimes|n-p\rangle_{2}\right)\left(|q\rangle_{1}\otimes|m-q\rangle_{2}\right)\left(\langle p|_{1}\otimes\langle n-p|_{2}\right)\left(\langle q|_{1}\otimes\langle m-q|_{2}\right)U_{BS}

Now we note that POVMs are basis independent i.e the measurement statistics are the same regardless of any change of bases we make on the POVMs. So we can conjugate our POVM by some unitary

\Delta t\ll T
so that our bin basis spans the same set of functions as our Gramm-Schmidt basis. Now we have

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}E_{n,m}V=\sum_{p}\sum_{q}U_{BS}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\left(|p_{\xi_{\text{LO}}}\rangle\otimes|n-p_{\xi_{\perp}}\rangle\right)\left(|q_{\xi_{\text{LO}}}\rangle\otimes|m-q_{\xi_{\perp}}\rangle\right)\times

(73)

where

Appendix I
Filtering Theory

In single mode homodyne we know that the measurement reduces to a measurement of a quadrature defined by the phase of the LO. As long as the LO is large enough for the LO shot noise to dominate the signal shot noise we can reduce the effective quadrature noise to that of vacuum fluctuations. For the multimode case we show in
eq.

11
, that the measurement is a quadrature measurement convolved with additional intensity noise from the measurement of the mismatch portion of the signal. The goal of filtering in this context is to reduce the intensity like noise present in our time-averaged outcome, while not affecting the quadrature measurement at all.

We will first imagine that we have very fast detectors that collect photons in a very small time bin
d\tau
for each data point. Over the entire detection interval,
T
we will assume we have many data points i.e
d\tau\ll T
. We will consider the filtering operation as applying some time-dependent set of weights
f(t)
to the photocurrent, and then averaging over the filtered data. This means that any outcome of our measurement
x
is given by

x=\int_{0}^{T}f(t)x(t)dt,

(84)

where we can easily replace this integral be a sum if the the detector window
d\tau
is not infinitesimal.

In order to ensure that the operator being measured is
\hat{Q}(\xi_{\text{LO}})
any filter we apply must be constant over the LO mode. If this is not the case then the measurement will have reduced sensitivity to the the portions of the LO mode when
f(t)
is small. The extreme example of this is when
f(t)=0
on some interval
(t_{0},t_{1})
, clearly since these data points are completely removed from the final measurement outcome our measurement has no sensitivity to the part of the LO mode. So we conclude that the filter must leave the Lo mode unchanged so the measurement is maintained , i.e
f(t)\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)\propto\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)

The set of all possible filters
f(t)
under these restrictions becomes

f(t)=\begin{cases}c&\text{if }\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)\neq 0\\
g(t)&\text{if }\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)=0\end{cases}.

(85)

where
c
is some constant. Without loss of generality we will assume that
c=1
because any other choice of constant would merely scale the value of all outcomes, leaving the SNR unchanged. The problem is now to find
g(t)
so that we have the minimum perpendicular mode noise in our measurement.

At this point we should note that in many cases
\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)
is never zero. While ultimately this means that there is no filter that will leave the LO mode unchanged, it may still be desirable to find an approximate filter which greatly increases SNR at the cost of a small change in the measurement. For example if the LO is a Gaussian pulse then 5 standard deviations away from the mean might be sufficient to approximate
\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)\approx 0
. If we want to ensure that some small fraction
p
of the total LO mode photons are excluded by our gate then we have the condition

|\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)|^{2}<\frac{p}{d\tau}.

(86)

Lets now consider the case where the signal is a coherent state
|\alpha_{\xi_{\text{S}}}\rangle
. Here semiclassical analysis tells us how the mean and variance will change under the proposed filter,

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\to 2\text{Re}(\alpha\beta^{*})\text{Re}(\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\xi_{\text{LO}}^{*}(t)\xi_{s}(t)dt)

(87)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\to|\beta|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}|\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)|^{2}dt+|\alpha|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}|\xi_{\text{S}}(t)|^{2}dt.

Now we apply the fact that
f(t)\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)=\xi_{\text{LO}}(t)
and the convention that
\beta
is real to get the simplified mean

\mu=2|\beta|\text{Re}(\gamma\alpha),

(88)

and the variance

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=|\beta|^{2}+|\alpha|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}|\xi_{\text{S}}(t)|^{2}

(89)

\displaystyle=|\beta|^{2}+|\alpha|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}|\gamma\xi_{\text{LO}}+\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}\xi_{\perp}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\beta|^{2}+|\alpha|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}\left[|\gamma\xi_{\text{LO}}|^{2}+(1-|\gamma|^{2})|\xi_{\perp}|^{2}+\gamma\sqrt{1-|\gamma|^{2}}(\xi_{\text{LO}}^{*}\xi_{\perp}+\xi_{\text{LO}}\xi_{\perp}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=|\beta|^{2}+|\alpha|^{2}\left[|\gamma|^{2}+(1-|\gamma|^{2})\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}|\xi_{\perp}|^{2}\right]

where we have again used that fact that
f\xi_{\text{LO}}=\xi_{\text{LO}}
and that
\langle\xi_{\text{LO}},\xi_{\perp}\rangle=0
. Finally we can apply the assumption that
|\beta|^{2}\gg|\gamma\alpha|^{2}
to get

\text{SNR}=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{4|\beta|^{2}\text{Re}(\gamma\alpha)}{|\beta|^{2}+\eta|\alpha|^{2}}

(90)

where
\eta=\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}|\xi_{\perp}|^{2}

Appendix J
Example with known signal mode

Here we demonstrate an example where the bounds we propose by changing the effective quadrature being measured in a way that increases SNR. The simplest example of this is to consider explicitly the signal and LO modes to both be top hat functions,

\xi_{\text{S}}\propto\begin{cases}1&\text{if }t\in(0,t_{1})\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}~~~~~~\xi_{\text{LO}}\propto\begin{cases}1&\text{if }t\in(t_{0},T)\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases},

(91)

Figure 4:
We show the modes used to demonstrate filtering performance that exceeds our bounds. Here it is clear that the optimal gate would be the one that only count clicks from the overlapped portion of the measurement and remove all additional shot noise from the LO and signal mode.

where the constant of proportionality is normalization of the modes. We plot a mock up of this example in
fig.

4
. We note that on the interval
(0,t_{0})
only the signal is nonzero, so no quadrature measurement is taking place here. Similarly on the interval
(t_{1},T)
only the LO is nonzero this means here we are making a quadrature measurement of vacuum. Since we are only concerned with the total measurement, averaging over the vacuum noise will increase variance without changing the mean. If the only objective is to increase the SNR then we can remove LO mode shot noise by changing the effective quadrature measurement to just the interval
(t_{0},t_{1})
. This is achieved with the filter

f(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }t\in(t_{0},t_{1})\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

(92)

In this case for a coherent signal
|\alpha_{\xi_{\text{S}}}\rangle
we would get the following SNR

\text{SNR}=\frac{4\text{Re}(\gamma\alpha\beta^{*})^{2}}{\eta_{\text{LO}}|\beta|^{2}+\eta_{\text{S}}|\alpha|^{2}},

(93)

where

\eta_{\text{LO}}=\frac{t_{1}-t_{0}}{T-t_{0}}~~~~~\eta_{\text{S}}=\frac{t_{1}-t_{0}}{t_{1}}.

(94)

Assuming we still were operating in the large LO regime even after the filter is applied we get the simplified expression

\text{SNR}=\frac{4\text{Re}(\gamma\alpha)^{2}}{\eta_{\text{LO}}},

(95)

where we note that
\eta_{\text{LO}}
us between 0 and 1, so this exceeds the bound we propose of
\text{SNR}=4\text{Re}(\gamma\alpha)^{2}
by a factor of
\eta_{\text{LO}}^{-1}
.

We will note that the effective operator that was measured after the filtering step was
\hat{Q}(f(t)\xi_{\text{LO}})
rather than
\hat{Q}(\xi_{\text{LO}})
. While in this case the SNR was higher in the altered measurement, we do not believe that comparing these two cases is fair because they measured different things. This is really a problem with comparing SNR where any post processing of a mean zero measurement that can shift the mean, will always result in higher SNR regardless of the impact on the measured quantity.

Deschênes and Genest 2013

P_{\text{LO}}
is the total comb power,
n
is the number of comb teeth,
P_{\text{s}}

B

\tau=\frac{0.315}{1.76\Delta\nu}
