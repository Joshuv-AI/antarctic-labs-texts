Flat equivariant gerbes: holonomies and dualities
Ruben Minasian

Abstract
We examine the role of global topological data associated to choices of holonomy for flat gauge fields in string compactification. Our study begins with perturbative string compactification on compact flat manifolds preserving 8 8 supercharges in 5 5 dimensions. By including non-trivial holonomy for Wilson lines in the heterotic string and for the B-field gerbe in the type II string we find worldsheet dualities that relate these backgrounds to other string compactifications. While our simple examples allow for explicit analysis, the concepts and some of the methods extend to a broader class of compactifications and have implications for string dualities, perturbative and otherwise.

\displaystyle g=\underbrace{\exp\left[{\textstyle\frac{2\pi i}{N}}J^{\prime}_{0}\right]}_{=g_{\gamma}}~~\underbrace{\exp\left[{\textstyle\frac{2\pi i}{N}}\overline{J}^{\prime}_{0})\right]}_{=g_{\psi}}~.

(2.9)

The
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
action is diagonally embedded in
\operatorname{U{}}(1)_{L}^{\prime}\times\operatorname{U{}}(1)_{R}^{\prime}
. For
N=2
the action is contained in the center of each corresponding
\operatorname{SU}(2)
factor, so that the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)_{L}^{\prime}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)_{R}^{\prime}
algebra is left invariant, while for
N>2
the invariant subalgebra is
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)_{L}^{\prime}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)_{R}^{\prime}
. We are of course by no means the first to consider these orbifolds—see, for example
[
42
,
33
,
34
,
43
,
35
]
and references therein for earlier work and some asymmetric generalizations. However, we will recall some of the details and give a view that will be useful for what follows.

Untwisted massless states

To describe the untwisted massless states, we just need to apply the projection onto
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
–invariant states. We first observe that for all
N>0
the special anti-holomorphic states with
\overline{h}=1/2
are projected out, and therefore the spacetime supersymmetry is reduced. However, since the projection keeps the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)_{R}
current algebra and leaves the vacuum of the internal theory invariant, we are guaranteed (1,0) supersymmetry. This makes the preceding discussion of the multiplet structure well-adapted to understand the projection.

The invariant content of the (1,1) supergravity multiplet is exactly the (1,0) supergravity and tensor multiplets. To analyze the invariant content of the (1,1) vector multiplets, we use the decomposition

\displaystyle\text{ (1,1) vector}=\text{(1,0) vector}\oplus\text{ (1,0) hyper}~.

(2.10)

The
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
action on each multiplet is a combination of an
\operatorname{SU}(2)^{\prime}_{L}
gauge symmetry, an
\operatorname{SU}(2)^{\prime}_{R}
R-symmetry, as well as
G_{\text{ab}}\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
, which acts on the abelian (1,1) vector multiplets corresponding to the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\oplus 4}
factor according to (
2.3
). To describe that action, we package the
4
vector multiplets into two complex combinations that transform according to

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{\zeta_{N}}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{\zeta_{N}^{-1}}~

(2.11)

under the action of
G_{\text{ab}}
.

Consider first the (1,1) vector multiplets corresponding to the hidden
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}
symmetry. While the (1,0) vector submultiplets of these are left invariant, every
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}
-hyper is projected out, so we are left with just the
(1,0)
vector multiples of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}
.

Next, we examine the
(1,1)
abelian vectors: now each
(1,0)
vector is projected out, but because of the additional action in
\operatorname{SU}(2)_{R}^{\prime}
, the
(1,0)
hypermultiplet content is more interesting: under
\operatorname{SU}(2)_{R}^{\prime}\times G_{\text{ab}}
the
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}
–hypermultiplets transform as

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{2}}}_{\zeta_{N}}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{2}}}_{\zeta_{N}^{-1}}~,

(2.12)

or decomposing further with respect to
\operatorname{U{}}(1)_{R}^{\prime}
, as

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{1,\zeta_{N}}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{-1,\zeta_{N}}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{1,\zeta_{N}^{-1}}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{-1,\zeta_{N}^{-1}}~.

(2.13)

Thus, if
N=2
, then all of these are invariant, while if
N>2
half of these are invariant. So, we find
4
neutral hypers for
N=2
and
2
neutral hypers for
N>2
.

To discuss the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}
vectors, we decompose

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}

\displaystyle\supset\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\prime}_{L}

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{248}}}

\displaystyle={{\boldsymbol{133}}}_{0}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{0}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{-1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{+2}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{-2}~.

(2.14)

From this we see that for
N>2
the invariant
(1,0)
vectors transform in the adjoint of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\prime}
, while for
N=2
we find the adjoint of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)^{\prime}_{L}
. To obtain the invariant
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}
-hypers, we tensor this with
{{\boldsymbol{2}}}
of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)_{R}^{\prime}
, or equivalently with
{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{-1}
of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)_{R}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{248}}}

\displaystyle={{\boldsymbol{133}}}_{0,+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{133}}}_{0,-1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{0,+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{0,-1}

\displaystyle\quad\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{+1,+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{+1,-1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{-1,+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56_{-1,+1}}}}

\displaystyle\quad\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{+2,+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{+2,-1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{-2,+1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{-2,-1}~.

(2.15)

The invariant
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}
-hypers are those with
q^{\prime}+\overline{q}^{\prime}=0\mod N
. The result is the following invariant spectrum from the untwisted sector (as promised, we ignore the hidden
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}
):

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}

gauge symmetry

hypers

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)_{L}^{\prime}

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{0}^{\oplus 4}\oplus({{\boldsymbol{56}}},{{\boldsymbol{2}}})

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)_{L}^{\prime}

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 2}_{0}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{2}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{1}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}}_{4,6}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)_{L}^{\prime}

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 2}_{0}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{1}

(2.16)

Twisted sector contributions

A simple approach to work out the twisted sector contributions is to model each fixed point of the orbifold by
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
and use free field techniques to calculate the quantum numbers of states in the twisted sector. It is then not too difficult to read off the twisted sector states that yield spacetime massless states. We will not have need for details of the construction, so we will simply quote the results from the literature
[
33
,
35
]
. The twisted sectors make no contribution to the gauge symmetry, but they do produce additional hypermultiplets arranged as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}&&\text{twisted hypers}\\[5.69054pt]
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}&&({{\boldsymbol{2}}},{{\boldsymbol{1}}})^{\oplus 32}\oplus({{\boldsymbol{1}}},{{\boldsymbol{56}}})^{\oplus 8}\\[5.69054pt]
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}&&{{\boldsymbol{56}}}^{\oplus 9}_{-1/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 45}_{2/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 18}_{-4/3}\\[5.69054pt]
{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}&&{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{-1/2}^{\oplus 4}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{3/2}^{\oplus 8}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{1/2}^{\oplus 24}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{0}^{\oplus 5}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{1}^{\oplus 32}\\[5.69054pt]
{\mathbb{Z}}_{6}&&{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 8}_{1/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 2}_{-5/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{-2/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 22}_{2/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\oplus 10}_{-4/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}^{\oplus 5}_{-1/3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{1}^{\oplus 22}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{56}}}_{0}^{\oplus 3}\end{array}

The reader can check that in all cases the six-dimensional anomaly cancelation condition
N_{\text{hyper}}-N_{\text{vector}}=244
is satisfied.

Each of these theories has a Higgs branch: we can Higgs the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)_{L}^{\prime}
for
N=2
and the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)_{L}^{\prime}
for
N>2
to obtain a spectrum with gauge algebra
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}
and hypermultiplets in
({{\boldsymbol{56}}})^{\oplus 10}
---which is exactly the massless spectrum of heterotic compactification on K3 with standard embedding. We expect this result since each of these singularities is a degeneration limit of the K3 surface.
7
7

7

The limit in the non-linear sigma model’s moduli space is subtle due to a choice of
B
-field on the collapsing two-cycles of the degenerating K3 surface: see
[
36
,
37
]
for further discussion.

2.4
Compactification on
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\times S^{1}

Having obtained the
d=6
theory, it is a simple matter to compactify further on a circle. If we treat the circle as completely decoupled, then the massless spectrum is obtained by standard Kaluza-Klein reduction
[
41
]
:

1.

the reduction of the (1,0) supergravity and tensor multiplets leads to a (minimal)
d=5
supergravity multiplet and
2
abelian vector multiplets
8
8

8

One of these is obtained by dualizing a
d=5
abelian tensor multiplet.
;

2.

each
d=6
vector multiplet in representation
{{\boldsymbol{r}}}
of the gauge algebra reduces to a
d=5
vector multiplet, which now has a real scalar transforming in the same representation;

3.

similarly, each
d=6
hypermultiplet reduces to a
d=5
hypermultiplet in the same representation of the gauge algebra.

All in all, the gauge algebra is now
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}
, where the first factor is due to the vector multiplets obtained from the reduction of supergravity and tensor (1,0) multiplets, and the hypermultiplet spectrum is unmodified.

The new feature in
d=5
is the existence of a Coulomb branch. Giving a generic set of expectation values to the scalars in the vector multiplets breaks the gauge group to
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\oplus 18}
and leaves
4
neutral hypermultiplets in the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
orbifold and just
2
neutral hypermultiplets for
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3,4,6}
. Observing that under
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\supset\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{6}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{56}}}={{\boldsymbol{27}}}_{+1}\oplus{{\overline{\boldsymbol{27}}}}_{-1}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{+3}\oplus{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{-3}~,

(2.21)

we see that for the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3,4,6}
compactifications there is a Coulomb branch with unbroken
\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}=\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{2}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{6}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\prime}
where all of the twisted sector matter is lifted, and there are
2
neural massless hypermultiplets. A special feature of the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
example is that it is possible to lift all of the twisted sector states by just going on the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\prime}
Coulomb branch, where the full gauge algebra
\operatorname{\mathfrak{g}}
is preserved.

2.5
Compactification on
X_{5}

Having reviewed the well-known compactifications on
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\times S^{1}
, we now consider compactification on
X_{5}
. From the worldsheet point of view, the new ingredient relative to the preceding discussion is a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
shift orbifold of the circle. By itself this theory is easy to understand: starting with a CFT for a circle of radius
r
, the shift orbifold is a CFT of radius
r/N

[
44
]
. In the full quotient CFT for
X_{5}=({{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}\times S^{1})/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
, we just need to combine the twisted sectors of the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
and
S^{1}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
CFTs and impose the orbifold projection. With a view to later developments, we will first describe the construction of the
S^{1}
shift orbifold in some detail.

The
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
shift orbifold of the circle

Consider a compact
c=1
boson at a generic radius
r
. Splitting the worldsheet field
\Phi(z,\overline{z})
into left- and right-moving components, we have the defining OPEs

\displaystyle\Phi_{L}(z)\Phi_{L}(w)

\displaystyle\sim-{\textstyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}}\log(z-w)~,

\displaystyle\Phi_{R}(\overline{z})\Phi_{R}(\overline{w})

\displaystyle\sim-{\textstyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}}\log(\overline{z}-\overline{w})~.

(2.22)

The theory enjoys a Kac-Moody
\operatorname{U{}}(1)^{\text{shift}}_{L}\times\operatorname{U{}}(1)^{\text{shift}}_{R}
symmetry with currents

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=ir\partial\Phi_{L}~,

\displaystyle\overline{J}

\displaystyle=ir\bar{\partial}\Phi_{R}~,

(2.23)

and the (Kac-Moody) primary states
|p\rangle
are labeled by the momentum and winding modes
n,w\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. More precisely,

\displaystyle p=w\boldsymbol{e}+n\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}\in\Gamma_{1,1}~,

(2.24)

with
\Gamma_{1,1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{1,1}
the even self-dual lattice spanned by two lattice vectors
\boldsymbol{e}
and
\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}
satisfying
\boldsymbol{e}.\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}.\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}=0
and
\boldsymbol{e}.\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}=1
. Here
a.b
denotes the inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1,1}
induced by the bilinear pairing on the lattice. Note that for our discussions of lattices here and in what follows, we will take
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{L},n_{R}}
to have Lorentzian metric

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-{\mathbbm{1}}_{n_{L}}&0\\
0&+{\mathbbm{1}}_{n_{R}}\end{pmatrix}~.

(2.25)

Our theory has a one-dimensional moduli space, which we can think of as a specification of a spacelike
1
-plane
\Pi\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{1,1}
. We can always choose a basis vector for
\Pi

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}+r^{2}\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}~,

(2.26)

so that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{e}-r^{2}\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}
spans the orthogonal complement
\Pi_{\perp}
.

With that preparation, we write the operator corresponding to the state
|p\rangle
as

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{p}

\displaystyle=~:~\boldsymbol{C}(p)\exp\left[{\textstyle\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}}(\boldsymbol{\pi}.p)\Phi_{R}(\overline{z})+{\textstyle\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}.p)\Phi_{L}(z)\right]~:~.

(2.27)

\boldsymbol{C}(p)
is an operator constructed from the momentum zero modes that we will discuss further below; this “cocycle operator” is necessary to ensure proper commutation relations of the vertex operators. Using the OPEs, it is now straightforward to see that
{\cal V}_{p}
carries
\operatorname{U{}}(1)^{\text{shift}}_{L}\times\operatorname{U{}}(1)^{\text{shift}}_{R}
charges

\displaystyle q_{L}^{\text{sh}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}.p}{r\sqrt{2}}~,

\displaystyle q_{R}^{\text{sh}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\pi}.p}{r\sqrt{2}}~

(2.28)

and weights

\displaystyle h_{L}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(q_{L}^{\text{sh}})^{2}={\textstyle\frac{1}{4r^{2}}}\left(n-wr^{2}\right)^{2}~,

\displaystyle h_{R}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(q_{R}^{\text{sh}})^{2}={\textstyle\frac{1}{4r^{2}}}\left(n+wr^{2}\right)^{2}~.

(2.29)

The spin of the operator is

\displaystyle s(p)=h_{L}-h_{R}=-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}p.p=-nw~.

(2.30)

Note that in these conventions the self-dual radius is
r=1
, with the T-duality map being
r\mapsto 1/r
and
(n,w)\mapsto(w,n)
.

With this preparation, we define the action of the shift symmetry
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}^{\text{shift}}
, taking its generator
g_{\text{sh}}
to act as

\displaystyle g_{\text{sh}}|p\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi in/N}|p\rangle~.

(2.31)

Since the momentum quantum number
n
is conserved, this is clearly a symmetry of the spectrum, of the OPE, and of the correlation functions. Moreover, we can represent this action in terms of the conserved shift currents (or, rather, the corresponding conserved charges):

\displaystyle g_{\text{sh}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{N}Q}~,

(2.32)

with the charge
Q
given by

\displaystyle Q={\textstyle\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}}\left(J_{L,0}^{\text{sh}}+J_{R,0}^{\text{sh}}\right)~.

(2.33)

The orbifold projection is then onto states with
Q\in N{\mathbb{Z}}
.

This is a key simplification in the orbifold analysis, since it allows us to directly construct the twisted Hilbert space—see
[
40
]
for a recent pedagogical discussion.
9
9

9

A recent discussion of the more general situation is given in
[
45
]
.
To carry this out, we find a twist field of the form

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}(z,\overline{z})=\exp\left[-i{\textstyle\frac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{2}}}\tau_{k}\Phi_{R}-i{\textstyle\frac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{2}}}\widetilde{\tau}_{k}\Phi_{L}\right]~,

(2.34)

where the parameters
\tau_{k}
and
\widetilde{\tau}_{k}
are chosen so that the OPE of
{\cal V}_{p}(z,\overline{z})
and
\Sigma_{k}(z,\overline{z})
has the correct monodromy, i.e. so that under a continuous rotation
z\to e^{i\theta}z
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{p}(e^{2\pi i}z,e^{-2\pi i}\overline{z})\Sigma_{k}(0)=e^{{\textstyle\frac{2\pi ikn}{N}}}{\cal V}_{p}(z,\overline{z})~.\Sigma_{k}(0)

(2.35)

The field
\Sigma
then gives the
k
-th twisted ground state
|0;k\rangle=\lim_{z,\overline{z}\to 0}\Sigma(z,\overline{z})|0\rangle
, and the full Hilbert space in the
k
-th twisted sector is constructed from
|p;k\rangle
—obtained by acting further with the
{\cal V}_{p}
on the
|0;k\rangle
—by acting with all possible oscillators.

In our example a quick computation shows that choosing

\displaystyle\tau_{k}

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle\frac{k}{N}}~,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}_{k}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{k}{N}}~

(2.36)

produces the correct monodromy. Moreover, it is straightforward to read off the shifts in the weights and charges due to the twist, i.e. the weights and charges of the state
|p;k\rangle
:

\displaystyle q^{\text{sh}}_{L}(p;k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\pi}.p-\widetilde{\tau}_{k}r^{2}}{r\sqrt{2}}~,

\displaystyle q^{\text{sh}}_{R}(p;k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi.p-\tau_{k}r^{2}}{r\sqrt{2}}~,

(2.37)

and the weights are

\displaystyle h_{L}(p;k)

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(q_{L}^{\text{sh}}(p;k))^{2}~,

\displaystyle h_{R}(p;k)

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(q_{R}^{\text{sh}}(p;k))^{2}~.

(2.38)

Not only do we know the twisted Hilbert spaces, but we also know how to implement the projection onto invariant states: we just need to project onto states with

\displaystyle Q(p;k)=\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\left(q_{L}^{\text{sh}}(p;k)+q_{R}^{\text{sh}}(p;k)\right)\in N{\mathbb{Z}}~.

(2.39)

For our circle shift orbifold it is easy to check that
Q(p;k)=Q(p)
, and also that the spin satisfies

\displaystyle s(p;k)=h_{L}(p;k)-h_{R}(p;k)=s(p)-\frac{k}{N}Q(p;k)~,

(2.40)

so that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
–invariant states are guaranteed to have integer spin.

Carrying out the construction for the
N-1
twisted sectors, we find the expected structure: the projection sets the momentum modes to be valued in
N{\mathbb{Z}}
, as is consistent with a circle of radius
r/N
, while the
k
-th twisted sector adds in the winding modes with
w\in{\textstyle\frac{k}{N}}+{\mathbb{Z}}
, which are the “extra” winding modes for a circle of radius
r/N
relative to that of radius
r
.

The
X_{5}
orbifold

Having understood the shift orbifold in detail, we could easily construct the full partition function for the
X_{5}
CFT. The only modification to our previous discussion of the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
compactification is to treat the right-moving superpartner of
\Phi(z,\overline{z})
as part of the internal CFT rather than belonging to the CFT describing the
{\mathbb{R}}^{1,5}
degrees of freedom.
10
10

10

There are some subtleties in applying the usual RNS rules in odd-dimensional compactification, but they can be easily avoided by introducing an additional spectator circle with its superpartner; see e.g.
[
46
]
.

If our interest is in the massless spectrum, then the effect of the extra shift at generic radius is simple to understand: the untwisted massless states are exactly those of the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\times S^{1}
CFT, while all of the twisted sector states are massive. This observation was the starting point for our study, since it suggests a close relationship between the two theories.

We start in the
d=6
(1,1) obtained by heterotic compactification on
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}
. The worldsheet symmetries we identified in our discussion of the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
orbifold are interpreted as spacetime gauge symmetries, and in particular there is a discrete gauge symmetry
G={\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
which is a subgroup

\displaystyle G\subset G_{\text{ab}}\times\operatorname{SU}(2)^{\prime}_{L}\times\operatorname{SU}(2)^{\prime}_{R}~,

(2.41)

the latter action being part of the spacetime R-symmetry group of the
d=6
(1,1) theory. Given the presence of this discrete spacetime gauge symmetry, when we compactify the theory on a circle with coordinate
\theta\sim\theta+2\pi
, we can choose to turn on a holonomy for
G
. That is, we modify the periodicity conditions on the fields as

\displaystyle\phi(x,\theta+2\pi)=g\cdot\phi(x,\theta)~.

(2.42)

The holonomy will have the effect that only
G
–invariant fields will have zero modes in the
\theta
expansion, so that the low energy theory obtained in this way will have the same massless spectrum as compactification on
X_{5}
. Because
G
involves an
\operatorname{SU}(2)^{\prime}_{R}
action, half of the gravitinos will be lifted in the process, reducing supersymmetry from
16
to
8
supercharges.

Our point of view is that the orbifold construction of the heterotic string on
X_{5}
is a UV completion of circle compactification of the spacetime theory with a
G
-holonomy. Note we are not suggesting a spacetime interpretation relating compactification based on a CFT to that based on the orbifold CFT. Instead, the relationship is between a compactification based on a freely acting orbifold and a circle compactification of a theory from one dimension higher. In the appendix we present a toy bosonic string model illustrating this structure for the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
quotient.

It is clear that the existence of a UV completion is a non-trivial condition because not every global discrete symmetry of the heterotic CFT leads to a modular-invariant orbifold, yet we expect such symmetries to still correspond to gauge symmetries in the spacetime theory. Thus, there should in general be obstructions to turning on non-trivial holonomy for a discrete spacetime gauge symmetry
G
. We hope to return to a study of these in the future.

3
The heterotic worldsheet and duality

In the previous section we presented two classes of
d=5
heterotic compactifications that preserve
8
supercharges. While we saw that their spectra are closely related—in fact identical when restricted to the massless untwisted states—in general the theories appear to be distinct. As a stark difference we noted that
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\times S^{1}
compactifications can be deformed to
\text{K3}\times S^{1}
by going on the Higgs branch, while no such Higgs branch appears to be present in the
X_{5}
compactifications.

Nevertheless, we will now show that by enlarging the moduli space to include Wilson lines we can establish an isomorphism between these classes of theories. Thus, not only are these compactifications connected in moduli space, they are in fact identical.

3.2
Two orbifolds

Starting with this presentation of the heterotic string on
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{5}
, we now consider the special locus where the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}
factor admits a
G={\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
symmetry with generator
g
.
The
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
symmetry leads to significant simplification in the CFT moduli: both
g_{i5}
and
b_{i5}
must be zero for
i\neq 5
, and the
A_{i\neq 5}
may be set to zero as well.
12
12

12

Note the
A_{i\neq 5}
Wilson lines are not required to be set to zero by our
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
symmetry because there is an accompanying action on the left-moving fermions, or equivalently on the
{\cal X}_{a}
. In the orbifold theory these symmetry-preserving Wilson line parameters describe hypermultiplet expectation values.

To understand how
G
acts on the states, we reconsider the actions described in our RNS discussion. These were the geometric orbifold action on
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}
, accompanied by its supersymmetric extension to the right-moving fermions, the translation on
S^{1}
, and the action on the left-moving fermions
\gamma^{1,2}
. We will now translate each of these into the bosonic description.

First, the geometric action on the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}
bosons induces an action on the associated vectors
\vec{p}\in\Gamma_{d,d}
. If the
\Phi^{i}
coordinates transform as

\displaystyle g_{T}\cdot\Phi^{i}=R^{i}_{j}\Phi^{j}~,

(3.9)

then

\displaystyle g_{T}\cdot w^{i}

\displaystyle=R^{i}_{j}w^{j}~,

\displaystyle g_{T}\cdot n_{i}

\displaystyle=(R^{-1})^{j}_{i}n_{i}~.

(3.10)

This preserves the spin of every
{\cal V}_{p}
, and, because it is a torus isometry, it preserves the weights as well. The right-moving fermions also transform accordingly, and as we discussed around equation (
2.9
), it is convenient to represent the action by
g_{\psi}=\exp[{\textstyle\frac{2\pi i}{N}}\overline{J}^{\prime}_{0}]
.

Second, the translation in the circle direction is precisely the shift symmetry
g_{\text{sh}}
described in section
2.5
. It leaves the lattice vectors invariant and acts on states by a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
-valued phase.

Finally, we have the action on the
\gamma
fermions, represented by
g_{\gamma}=\exp[{\textstyle\frac{2\pi i}{N}}J^{\prime}_{0}]
. This is the only one that is not quite obvious in the bosonized description, but because we can write it in terms of the current
J^{\prime}
, finding the corresponding action on the
{\cal X}^{a}
is not too difficult. Taking a look at our Dynkin diagram, we see that

\displaystyle J^{\prime}

\displaystyle=i\partial{\cal X}^{7}-i\partial{\cal X}^{8}~,

(3.11)

i.e. it is the current that corresponds to the extended root of the diagram. Thus,
g_{\gamma}
turns out to be another shift action in the bosonized description:

\displaystyle g_{\gamma}|p\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{{\textstyle\frac{2\pi i}{N}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{7}-\boldsymbol{v}_{8}).p}|p\rangle~.

(3.12)

We can see this gives the expected structure for the unbroken symmetry with
A=0
. In this case the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{8}
currents that survive the projection are the Cartan currents
i\partial{\cal X}^{a}
, as well as the roots
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{I}
orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{v}_{7}-\boldsymbol{v}_{8}
. For
N>2
this gives rise to the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{e}}_{7}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\prime}
current algebra, and in the special case of
N=2
the state corresponding to the
\boldsymbol{v}_{7}-\boldsymbol{v}_{8}
root is left invariant as well, leading to the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\prime}\to\operatorname{\mathfrak{su}}(2)^{\prime}
enhancement.

As above, we have two
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
symmetries by which we can orbifold:

G
, generated by
g=g_{T}g_{\psi}g_{\gamma}g_{\text{sh}}
, leads to the
X_{5}
orbifold,
while
G^{\prime}
, generated by
g^{\prime}=g_{T}g_{\psi}g_{\gamma}
, produces the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\times S^{1}
orbifold.

Both of the symmetries are consistent with turning on a Wilson line
A_{5}
along the shift circle, and by including this degree of freedom, we will demonstrate that, despite appearances, the two orbifolds are equivalent. We will do this by finding an element
t
of the T-duality group
\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma_{17,1})\subset\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma_{21,5})
such that

\displaystyle g^{\prime}=t^{-1}gt~.

(3.13)

To give this construction, we will have to delve a little bit into the structure of the heterotic T-duality group.

3.3
Elements of
\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma_{17,1})

The T-duality group
\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma_{16+d,d})
arises from lattice isomorphisms, and each such isomorphism induces a (possibly trivial) action on the moduli. A discussion of its generators is given in
[
50
,
7
]
. In our discussion two elements will play a role: the T-duality transformation on the
5

\Gamma_{8}

Typically, the induced action on the moduli is complicated. For example, a T-duality on a single circle usually involves a non-trivial action on the
g_{ij}
,

and

components
[
50
,
7
]

{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}

\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma_{17,1})

A vector in
\Gamma_{17,1}
has the form

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=w\boldsymbol{e}+n\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}+\lambda~,

and using (

\displaystyle h_{R}(p)

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{4r^{2}}}\left(\pi.p\right)^{2}~,

\displaystyle s(p)

\displaystyle=h_{L}(p)-h_{R}(p)=-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}p.p=-nw-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\cdot\lambda~,

(3.15)

with

\displaystyle\pi.p

\displaystyle=n+\left(r^{2}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}A\cdot A\right)w+A\cdot\lambda~.

(3.16)

The T-duality action on the lattice is simply the exchange

. Let us call this action

. Clearly

\displaystyle(g_{1}^{\ast}h_{R})(p)=h_{R}(g_{1}(p))~:

\displaystyle(r^{\prime},A^{\prime})

\displaystyle=g_{1}^{\ast}(r,A)~,

\displaystyle r^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{r^{2}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}A\cdot A}~,

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{r^{2}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}A\cdot A}~.

The lattice vector shift of the Wilson line arises through a more elaborate lattice isomorphism, depending on a choice of lattice vector

\displaystyle g_{2}(p)=p+(\rho\cdot\lambda-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\rho\cdot\rho w)\boldsymbol{e}^{\ast}-\rho w~,

and the

3.4
Heterotic isomorphism

Let

. We claim that this combination of
\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma_{17,1})

3.13
) for an appropriate choice of lattice vector
\rho

{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}

We set the

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=(1-N)\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{6}~,

(3.22)

and we define for convenience

\displaystyle t|n,w,\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle=|w,n+\rho\cdot\lambda-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\rho\cdot\rho w,\lambda-\rho w\rangle~,

(3.23)

and therefore

\displaystyle t^{-1}gt|n,w,\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left[{\textstyle\frac{2\pi i}{N}}\left(w+\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot(\lambda-\rho w)\right)\right]|n,w,\lambda\rangle=e^{{\textstyle\frac{2\pi i}{N}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\lambda}|n,w,\lambda\rangle=g^{\prime}|n,w,\lambda\rangle~.

(3.24)

In the second equality the phase dependence on the winding mode

Special features of
N=3

As we remarked above, the correspondence between the theories is particularly nice when
N=3
, since there it is possible to match the massless spectra by turning on a single Wilson line for the
\operatorname{\mathfrak{u}}(1)^{\prime}
vector multiplet. We can see these special features from the point of view of our isomorphism as well. Precisely when

we can set the Willson line shift to be
\rho=\boldsymbol{\gamma}

In this case, then, we can identify the
X_{5}

and circle Wilson line

with the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}\times S^{1}

and circle Wilson line

via

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{r^{\prime}}{r^{\prime 2}+(a^{\prime}-1)^{2}}}~,

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{a^{\prime}-1}{r^{\prime 2}+(a^{\prime}-1)^{2}}}~.

(3.25)

As expected, the duality is a stringy one. For example, setting
a^{\prime}=1
, which corresponds to the
a=0

X_{5}
theory analyzed above, we find that a large radius circle on the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}\times S^{1}

X_{5}
with a small shift circle.

Note that the theory with
a^{\prime}=1
is equivalent to
a^{\prime}=0
in the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{5}
theory, but that is not the case in the orbifold, where instead the equivalence is
a^{\prime}\sim a^{\prime}+3
. This is closely related to the appearance of the fractional
\operatorname{U{}}(1)^{\prime}_{L}
charges in the twisted sector states, and it is thus not surprising that such massless states are lifted for
a^{\prime}=1
.

The
a^{\prime}=0
locus is also interesting, since it corresponds to the
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}\times S^{1}
theory with the massless hypermultiplets coming from the twisted sectors. It would be surprising if we could identify it in the
X_{5}
description at large radius, and indeed this is not so. When written in terms of the
X_{5}
moduli, the
a^{\prime}=0
locus corresponds to the semicircle with
r\geq 0
and

\displaystyle(2r)^{2}+(1+2a)^{2}=1~,

(3.26)

so that
r\leq 1/2
.

3.5
Cocycle subtleties and their resolution

The reader who has made it this far may wonder if we have not introduced more formalism than we need to describe our results: we have not used the structure of the vertex operators
{\cal V}_{p}
in any detail, and the operators
\boldsymbol{C}(p)
, which are typically relegated to a last section or appendix have played no role in our discussion. We will now explain the need for these terms and point out that in general their presence can lead to subtleties in the analysis of CFT symmetries. We will also see, however, that in this case we are lucky: all of the potential subtle factors drop out, and the duality conclusions reached in the previous section remain unmodified. Nevertheless, since the general observations here may lead to subtleties in closely related discussions, we will include them. In addition to the standard textbook references
[
51
,
52
]
, which supply some of the background, our thinking about these issues was guided by the work
[
53
]
.

A class of CFT symmetries

We are interested in discussing a class of CFT symmetries of Narain compactification that are realized as the following
G
action on the vertex operators. For every
g\in G
there is a map
\varphi_{g}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
and a factor
U(g,p)\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\displaystyle g\circ{\cal V}_{p}=U(g,p){\cal V}_{\varphi_{g}(p)}~.

(3.27)

Not all CFT symmetries can be realized in this fashion. For example, the
\operatorname{SU}(2)\times\operatorname{SU}(2)
symmetries of the compact boson at self-dual radius take a more general form and also mix the
{\cal V}_{p}
with non-Kac-Moody primary operators such as
\partial\Phi
and
\bar{\partial}\Phi
.

We wish the
g
-action to be invertible and to be consistent with the OPE, and it must preserve the weights of the operators. Therefore it must be that for every
g
the factor
U(g,p)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
, and
\varphi_{g}
is a lattice isomorphism. We will insist that the action is unitary, which means
U(g,p)
is a pure phase. Since we also want composition to be consistent with the group product structure, i.e.

\displaystyle(g_{2}g_{1})\circ{\cal V}_{p}=g_{2}\circ\left(g_{1}\circ{\cal V}_{p}\right)~,

(3.28)

We also learn that the phases must obey

\displaystyle U(g_{2}g_{1},p)=U(g_{2},g_{1}(p))U(g_{1},p)~,

(3.29)

and similarly the
\varphi_{g}
should satisfy
\varphi_{g_{1}g_{2}}(p)=\varphi_{g_{1}}(\varphi_{g_{2}}(p))
. We now see that
\varphi
must be a map to the group of automorphisms of the lattice, i.e.
\varphi:G\to\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma)
. The resulting subgroup of
\operatorname{O{}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle G_{\Gamma}\simeq G/\ker(\varphi)

(3.30)

is in general smaller than
G
: for example, the circle shift symmetry has
G={\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
and
G_{\Gamma}=1
.

These are sensible constraints determined by the group structure, but there are further constraints on the factors
U(g,p)
, and this is where the cocycles make an appearance.

A look at the cocycles

The factors
\boldsymbol{C}(p)
are introduced to resolve an issue with commutation properties of the naive vertex operators
{\cal V}_{p}^{\text{naive}}
which lack these factors: the OPE of two such operators has a non-trivial monodromy as we transport one operator around the other, which leads to

\displaystyle:{\cal V}^{\text{naive}}_{p_{1}}(-z/2):~:{\cal V}^{\text{naive}}_{p_{2}}(z/2):~=e^{i\pi p_{1}.p_{2}}:{\cal V}^{\text{naive}}_{p_{2}}(z/2):~:{\cal V}^{\text{naive}}_{p_{1}}(-z/2):~.

(3.31)

Thus, the operators appear to anticommute whenever
p_{1}.p_{2}
is odd. To resolve this, the operators are modified to include the
\boldsymbol{C}(p)
, which are chosen so that

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{q}\boldsymbol{C}_{p}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(q,p)\boldsymbol{C}_{p}{{\cal V}_{q}}~,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{p}\boldsymbol{C}_{q}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{C}_{p+q}~

(3.32)

for some phase
\varepsilon(q,p)
. Including these factors will remove the unwanted factor
e^{i\pi p_{1}.p_{2}}
if the phases obey

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p_{2},p_{1})=(-1)^{p_{1}.p_{2}}\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2})~.

(3.33)

Associativity of the OPE places further non-trivial conditions on the phases:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{3})\varepsilon(p_{2},p_{3})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(p_{1}+p_{2},p_{3})~,

(3.34)

and

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2})\varepsilon(p_{1}+p_{2},p_{3})=\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2}+p_{3})\varepsilon(p_{2},p_{3})~.

(3.35)

The second of these justifies the name of cocycle, i.e.
\varepsilon\in H^{2}(\Gamma,\operatorname{U{}}(1))
. Consider the group homomorphisms
\Gamma\times\Gamma\to\operatorname{U{}}(1)
, denoted by
c(p_{1},p_{2})
. The coboundary map to the group of homomorphisms
\Gamma\times\Gamma\times\Gamma\to\operatorname{U{}}(1)
is taken to be

\displaystyle\delta_{32}c(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{c(p_{2},p_{3})}{c(p_{1}+p_{2},p_{3})}\frac{c(p_{1},p_{2}+p_{3})}{c(p_{1},p_{2})}~,

(3.36)

Similarly, starting with homomorphisms
\Gamma\to\operatorname{U{}}(1)
denoted by
f(p)
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{21}f(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(p_{1})f(p_{2})}{f(p_{1}+p_{2})}~,

(3.37)

and the reader can check
\delta_{32}(\delta_{21}f)=1
. The condition (
3.35
) is the statement that
\delta_{32}\varepsilon=1
, i.e. that
\varepsilon
is a cocycle that defines a class in
H^{2}(\Gamma,\operatorname{U{}}(1))
. On the other hand, the condition (
3.33
) shows that
\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2})
cannot be a coboundary.

As shown in the references
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
, it is possible to choose the
\boldsymbol{C}(p)
so that
\varepsilon
take the following form:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\exp\left[{\textstyle\frac{i\pi}{2}}(n_{1i}w_{2}^{i}-w_{1}^{i}n_{2i})+i\pi\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\right]~,

(3.38)

where the bilinear antisymmetric form
\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is obtained by writing
\lambda_{1,2}=\lambda_{1,2}^{I}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{I}
in terms of the simple roots and then setting

\displaystyle\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{I>J}\left(\lambda_{1}^{I}\lambda_{2}^{J}-\lambda_{2}^{I}\lambda_{1}^{J}\right)\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{I}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{J}~.

(3.39)

Cocycles and symmetry phases

Finally we come to our key point: the cocycle factors and the phases
U(g,p)
are intertwined, and the consistency of the OPE with the symmetry action, i.e.

\displaystyle~:g\circ{\cal V}_{p}(z_{1}):~:g\circ{\cal V}_{q}(z_{2}):~=~g\circ\left(~:{\cal V}_{p}(z_{1}):~:{\cal V}_{q}(z_{2}):~\right)~

(3.40)

require the phase factors to obey
[
53
]

\displaystyle\frac{U(g,p+q)}{U(g,p)U(g,q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon(\varphi_{g}(p),\varphi_{g}(q))}{\varepsilon(p,q)}~.

(3.41)

There are two ways to read this equation. On one hand, it shows that the ratio of cocycles
\frac{\varepsilon(\varphi_{g}(p),\varphi_{g}(q))}{\varepsilon(p,q)}
is a coboundary, and
U(g,p)
is the trivializing cycle. On the other hand, whenever the ratio is not
1
, it gives an obstruction to choosing
U(g,p)
to be a homomorphism from the lattice to
\operatorname{U{}}(1)
.

There are two obvious questions about this result: how does the it depend on the choice of cocycle? to what extent does it determine the phases
U(g,p)
? We leave it to the reader to check that a modification of
\varepsilon
by a coboundary
f(p)
modifies
U(g,p)\to U(g,p)f(g(p))/f(p)
, and the new
U(g,p)
so obtained is consistent with the group structure. As far as the second question goes, it is clear that if
U_{1}(g,p)
and
U_{2}(g,p)
both solve (
3.41
) with the same
\varepsilon
, then their ratio
U_{1}/U_{2}
is a homomorphism from
\Gamma
to
\operatorname{U{}}(1)
for all
g
.

Some examples

Let us consider some simple examples that illustrate the appearance (and non-appearance) of non-trivial phase factors
U(g,p)
. By non-trivial we mean here factors that are not in
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,\operatorname{U{}}(1))
and whose appearance is necessitated by (
3.41
).

By far the simplest example is any action where
\varphi_{g}(p)=p
for all
p
. In this case,
U(g,p)
must be in
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,\operatorname{U{}}(1))
. The circle shift orbifold yields a simple example. A bit more interesting is any geometric symmetry that arises from a
\operatorname{GL}(d,{\mathbb{Z}})
change of coordinates on the torus, such as our symmetry
g_{T}
in (
3.10
), acting on the winding and momentum modes as in (
3.10
). However, in this case too we find
\varepsilon(\varphi_{g}(p),\varphi_{g}(q))=\varepsilon(p,q)
, so that again the phase can be taken to be trivial.

For a more interesting example in
\Gamma_{d,d}
we can consider integral shifts of the
b
-field background, for which

\displaystyle\varphi_{g}(n_{i},w^{i})=(n_{i}+\Theta_{ij}w^{j},w^{i})~,

(3.42)

where
\Theta_{ij}
is an arbitrary integral antisymmetric matrix. In this case

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon(g(p_{1}),g(p_{2}))}{\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2})}=\exp\left[i\pi\textstyle\sum_{i>j}\Theta_{ij}(w_{1}^{i}w_{2}^{j}-w_{2}^{i}w_{1}^{j})\right]\neq 1~,

(3.43)

and a non-trivial phase is required to satisfy (
3.41
). A choice that works is to take

\displaystyle U(g,p)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[i\pi\textstyle\sum_{i>j}\Theta_{ij}w^{i}w^{j}\right]~.

(3.44)

Since the
g
–action preserves winding numbers
U(g,g(p))=U(g,p)
, so that
U(g^{k},p)=U(g,p)^{k}
, which is consistent with the group structure.

For a more elaborate example, we consider the Wilson line shift in
\Gamma_{17,1}
relevant to our construction:

\displaystyle\varphi_{g}(n,w,\tau)=(n+\rho\cdot\lambda-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\rho\cdot\rho w,w,\lambda-\rho w)~.

(3.45)

In this case a somewhat lengthy computation, making creative use of mod
2
conditions in the exponential, leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon(\varphi_{g}(p_{1}),\varphi_{g}(p_{2}))}{\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2})}=\exp\left[i\pi\left(w_{1}S(\rho,\lambda_{2})+w_{2}S(\rho,\lambda_{1})\right)\right]~,

(3.46)

where the bilinear map
S
is

\displaystyle S(\rho,\lambda)=\sum_{I}\rho^{I}\lambda^{I}-\sum_{I>J}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{I}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{J}\rho^{I}\lambda^{J}\in{\mathbb{Z}}~.

(3.47)

The naive guess
U(g,p)=e^{i\pi wS(\rho,\lambda)}
, while solving (
3.41
), does not satisfy the group property

\displaystyle U(g^{2},p)=U(g,g(p))U(g,p)~.

(3.48)

We can fix this by multiplying the naive guess by an element of
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,\operatorname{U{}}(1))
. We find that

\displaystyle U(g,p)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[i\pi wS(\rho,\lambda+\rho/2)\right]~

(3.49)

satisfies all of the desired properties.

Duality unmodified

We just discussed at length that symmetry actions of the sort we used to prove the duality between
X_{5}
and
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\times S^{1}
heterotic compactifications in general come with extra phase factors. Since the whole point of our duality claim was a match of phase factors for two group actions, this is a non-trivial concern.

To allay this worry, we write the correct form for the symmetry action on the states, taking into account the possible extra phases. In keeping with the spirit of this section, we distinguish in our notation between the action on the states and the action on the lattice vectors, so that we have, coming back to (
3.13
),

\displaystyle t^{-1}gt|p\rangle

\displaystyle=U(t,p)U(g,\varphi_{t}(p))U(t^{-1},\varphi_{gt}(p))|\varphi_{t^{-1}gt}(p)\rangle~.

(3.50)

The group multiplication properties imply the relation

\displaystyle 1=U(t^{-1}t,\varphi_{t^{-1}}(p))=U(t^{-1},p)U(t,\varphi_{t}^{-1}(p))~,

(3.51)

so that

\displaystyle U(t^{-1},p)=\frac{1}{U(t,\varphi_{t}^{-1}(p))}~.

(3.52)

But, since we have
\varphi_{t^{-1}gt}(p)=p
, the phase factor becomes

\displaystyle t^{-1}gt|p\rangle

\displaystyle=U(t,p)U(g,\varphi_{t}(p))\frac{1}{U(t,p)}|\varphi_{t^{-1}gt}(p)\rangle=U(g,\varphi_{t}(p))|p\rangle~,

(3.53)

but that is exactly the factor we showed to be equal to
U(g^{\prime},p)
.

We neglected two points in this discussion: the phases associated to the right-moving fermions, and the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
action on
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}
. As we saw above, the latter does not lead to any additional phase factors, and the former is identical in both theories.

4
Gerbes and duality in type II compactification

We have seen so far that in the context of the heterotic string T-duality can relate compactifications on

Correspondence spaces and vertical gerbes

Suppose
X
is the total space of a principal circle bundle
\pi_{g}:P_{g}\to M

V
and a dual form
\Theta_{g}
that satisfies

\displaystyle d\Theta_{g}=\pi^{\ast}_{g}(F_{g})~,

(4.1)

where
F_{g}
is the curvature of the circle bundle. An
H

is said to be T-dualizable if
dH=0

for some closed

When this is the case,

\displaystyle H=\Theta_{g}\wedge\pi^{\ast}_{g}(F_{b})-\pi^{\ast}_{g}(\Omega)~,

(4.2)

where
\Omega
is a

that satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{H}=\Theta_{b}\wedge\pi^{\ast}_{b}(F_{g})-\pi^{\ast}_{b}(\Omega)~.

(4.3)

This

[

]
.

4.1
Equivariant flat line bundles

Let
X
be a compact smooth manifold with an action by a discrete group
G
, i.e. there are diffeomorphisms
\varphi_{a}:X\to X
obeying
\varphi_{a}(\varphi_{b}(x))=\varphi_{ab}(x)
for all

M=X/G
, and this can accomplished by describing
G
–equivariant line bundles on
L\to X
.

Recall that a line bundle is flat if and only if its curvature is zero. In this case it is possible to choose a trivialization such that the transition functions are constant, and the connection is identically zero. Since line bundles are topologically classified by
c_{1}(L)\in H^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
, the first Chern class of a flat line bundle is a torsion class. When
H^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
(or, equivalently,
H_{1}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})

A line bundle
\pi:L\to X
is
G
-equivariant if there is a lift of the
G
-action on
X
to a
G
-action on
L
compatible with the projection
\pi

G
acts linearly on the fiber directions. The quotient
L/G
is then naturally a line bundle over
M=X/G
. While in general a bundle may not admit a lift, every trivial bundle does admit a lift. In our setting, where
X={{\mathbb{T}}}^{d}
, every flat line bundle is trivial: we can always find a lift of the
G
action, and the characterization of flat line bundles over
M
is then equivalent to the characterization of these lifts.

The circle and “non-geometry”

Much like the inexhaustible electron
[
56
]
, the compact boson is a seemingly perpetual source of insights and lessons, and we end this section by specializing our analysis to it.

The compact boson CFT admits various orbifold actions, and some of these, such as the shift orbifold which acts by the phase
e^{2\pi in/N}
or the reflection orbifold, have a straightforward geometric interpretation: for example, the former is a quotient that leads to a circle of radius
r/N
. On the other hand, there are also orbifolds that seemingly do not have such a straightforward interpretation, such as the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
orbifold that acts by the phase

. If we are willing to include the gerbe data as part of our geometric information, then we can assign a geometric meaning to this phase: it corresponds to turning on a gerbe on

Applying this logic to the compact boson CFT is surely an indication that one has spent too much time with gerbes, but the example has an important lesson for more elaborate theories: the gerbe data should be included in any discussion of “non-geometric” features of string compactification.

4.5
Frozen singularities and a dual perspective

The geometry

5
Discussion

It is remarkable that after so many years of intensive study theories with
8
supercharges continue to furnish surprises and provide insights into the structure of quantum field theory and string theory. In this work we have seen new connections between disparate geometries, both in the context of heterotic and type II theories in
5
dimensions. In the former we found that duality in the
\operatorname{O{}}(21,5)
moduli space leads to relations between topologically distinct compactifications. In the latter, we saw that by including the global data of a pure holonomy gerbe, T-duality is enlarged beyond the conventional arena of Buscher rules and exchange of data encoded in the differential forms associated to a circle fibration equipped with
H
-flux.

The original motivation for our work was to understand how a particularly simple class of compact flat geometries fits into the framework of string compactification. We showed that such compactifications are related to more familiar ones, and, via T-duality, they provide models for a number of phenomena. Perhaps the most striking of these is the freezing of singularities in a
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\times S^{1}
compactification achieved by turning on a pure holonomy gerbe. Such phenomena arise in a number of settings, often involving strongly coupled physics or other degrees of freedom that are difficult to access directly (e.g. background Ramond-Ramond fields). Our work provides an example where the mechanism can be understood directly in the worldsheet theory and has a geometric interpretation via the gerbe.

There are a number of promising directions where extensions of our ideas may be of use, and in the remainder of this section we will highlight a few of them.

First, we have seen that inclusion of the gerbe data leads to new classes of and new equivalences between orbifold conformal field theories. While our analysis was carried out in the context of
8
supercharges and
5
dimensions, it should be possible to extend our techniques to other orbifolds, and the classification of
[
5
]
offers an obvious place to start.
22
22

22

It appears that the type II
6
-dimensional orbifolds that preserve
16
supercharges do not allow non-trivial equivariant gerbes beyond those explored in this work. However, we have not explored the situation with type II orbifolds that only preserve
8
supercharges.

On one hand, inclusion of the pure holonomy gerbes will enlarge the class of conformal field theories, but, on the other hand, there will also be new equivalences between geometrically distinct orbifolds equipped with the gerbe data. For general orbifold groups the possibility of discrete torsion should also be included in a general analysis.

Sticking to the
5
–dimensional theories, perhaps the simplest extension is to consider replacing the base geometry in our circle fibrations to
\text{K3}/G
. Although it will not be possible to solve the worldsheet theory exactly, it is possible to preserve
8
supercharges, and therefore to have a reasonable amount of control on the theory. At the same time, the non-trivial topology of K3 opens up new possibilities. For example, in the context of the heterotic string there is the possibility of choosing a principal circle fibration that has a non-torsion first Chern class
[
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
]
. Generalizing such fibrations to include a shift in the
S^{1}
leads to a large class of examples where one can explore the topology and geometry of gerbes and perhaps find new dualities relating these background to each other beyond those obtained by conventional T-duality with
H
-flux
[
22
,
23
]
.

A more ambitious direction would be to pursue our ideas in the context of the recent constructions
[
59
]
that generalize the geometries of
[
61
]
by allowing the base K3 geometries to have a number of orbifold singularities: the total space of the fibration is nevertheless smooth, precisely because the local geometry near each singularity is
({\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times S^{1})/G
, where the
G
-action on
{\mathbb{C}}
is accompanied by a shift on the
S^{1}
fiber. Thus, these are models without a global
(\text{K3}\times S^{1})/G
orbifold description. It will be interesting to explore the interplay of the differential geometry and gerbe holonomy in this context. Is it possible to obtain “local” T-dual descriptions relating the smooth
({\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times S^{1})/G
geometry to a singular
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/G\times S^{1}
with a non-trivial gerbe holonomy? What are the global constraints on the set of singularities that can be treated in this fashion?

Returning to the simpler
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}
geometry as the starting point, we can study how our results fit into type II/ heterotic duality. Since the
6
-dimensional duality between type IIA on K3 and heterotic string on
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{4}
is well-understood, we can hope to use the spacetime perspective, where we view heterotic compactification on
X_{5}
as a reduction of the
6
-dimensional theory on a circle with a non-trivial holonomy for the
G
action, to map our results to type II. Can we interpret the dual type II theory as an orbifold? How do such dual pairs relate to the work
[
19
,
20
]
on non-geometric type II compactifications and their heterotic duals? More generally, what is the translation of the gerbe data in either duality frame to the other one?

Finally, our work shows that inclusion of the global gerbe data is crucial in understanding T-duality, and even our simple examples indicate that much remains to be understood. We indicated some of the directions in the main text, as well as in appendix
B
. Another direction is to consider our results in the context of heterotic T-duality, where the gerbe data is more complicated, and there can be interesting interplay between discrete torsion and more conventional shift orbifolds
[
64
]
. It would be very interesting to understand these phenomena in the context of a heterotic correspondence space
[
23
]
.

Scalar KK modes. These are associated with the factor

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{T}^{-}_{\Delta}+\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{\Delta})=\sum_{n\in 2{\mathbb{Z}},n^{2}\leq 4r^{2}}({\textbf{q}}{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}})^{n^{2}/4r^{2}}=\sum_{n^{2}\leq 4(r/2)^{2}}({\textbf{q}}{{{\overline{q}}}})^{{\textstyle\frac{1}{4}}n^{2}(r/2)^{-2}}

2.

Circle Vector KK modes. These are associated with the factor

\displaystyle({\textbf{q}}+{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}})\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{T}^{-}_{1}+\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{1})~,

(A.16)

and again have an interpretation of a KK tower for a circle of radius

3.

{{\mathbb{T}}}^{d}
Vector KK modes. These are associated with the factor

\displaystyle d({\textbf{q}}+{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}})\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{1}-\boldsymbol{T}^{-}_{1})

(A.17)

This time we look at a slightly different KK term:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{\Delta}-\boldsymbol{T}^{-}_{\Delta})=\sum_{n\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1,\Delta_{m,0}\leq 2}({\textbf{q}}{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}})^{n^{2}/4r^{2}}=\sum_{(n+1/2)^{2}\leq 4(r/2)^{2}}({\textbf{q}}{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}})^{{\textstyle\frac{1}{4}}(n+1/2)^{2}(r/2)^{-2}}~.

These states constitute the KK spectrum we would get for a field that is anti-periodic on the circle of radius

\displaystyle A(\theta+2\pi)=-A(\theta)~.

(A.19)

Such a field has a Fourier expansion

\displaystyle A=\sum_{n}a_{n}e^{i(n+1/2)\theta}~.

(A.20)

4.

The moduli are responsible for the
(d^{2}+1){\textbf{q}}{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}}

5.

The canonical spin
2

{\textbf{q}}^{2}

6.

The towers over
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{d}
momentum/winding modes. These arise from the second to last term that we kept in the expansion, which we rewrite as

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{p}\neq 0}{\textbf{q}}^{h(\vec{p})}{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}}^{\overline{h}(\vec{p})}\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{2-\Delta_{\vec{p}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\vec{p}\neq 0}{\textbf{q}}^{h(\vec{p})}{\overline{{\textbf{q}}}}^{\overline{h}(\vec{p})}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{2-\Delta_{\vec{p}}}+\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{2-\Delta_{\vec{p}}})+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{2-\Delta_{\vec{p}}}-\boldsymbol{T}^{+}_{2-\Delta_{\vec{p}}})\right)~.

These have a clear interpretation. The
{{\mathbb{T}}}^{d}

The

In summary then, we see in this bosonic model that in the

Taking the worldsheet to be a torus, we fix a map from

This phase factor is precisely the holonomy of the gerbe on

1.

It is independent of the basepoint

.

First observe that

is base-point independent because

and

\displaystyle-i\delta_{v}\log P_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\text{Lie}_{v}\left[\int_{x}^{\varphi_{a}(x)}{\cal A}^{b}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}{\cal A}^{a}\right]-i\text{Lie}_{v}\log\frac{h^{a,b}}{h^{b,a}}(x)~

\displaystyle=v\llcorner\left(\varphi_{a}^{\ast}({\cal A}^{b})-{\cal A}^{b}-\varphi_{b}^{\ast}({\cal A}^{a})+{\cal A}^{a}\right)-iv\llcorner d\log\frac{h^{a,b}}{h^{b,a}}=0~.

(B.46)

2.

It is invariant under gerbe gauge transformations.

This follows because

\displaystyle\int_{S_{a,b}}B_{\text{new}}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{a,b}}B+\left(\int_{x}^{\varphi_{a}(x)}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}+\int_{\varphi_{a}(x)}^{\varphi_{ab}(x)}-\int_{\varphi_{b}(x)}^{\varphi_{ab}(x)}\right)\Lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{a,b}}B+\int_{x}^{\varphi_{a}(x)}\left(\Lambda-\varphi_{b}^{\ast}(\Lambda)\right)-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}\left(\Lambda-\varphi_{a}^{\ast}(\Lambda)\right)~,

(B.47)

and the last two terms are canceled by the
\Lambda

from (

).

3.

It is invariant under bundle gauge transformations.

To see this, we note that
B

\displaystyle\left[\int_{x}^{\varphi_{a}(x)}{\cal A}^{b}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}{\cal A}^{a}\right]\to\left[\int_{x}^{\varphi_{a}(x)}{\cal A}^{b}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}{\cal A}^{a}\right]\times\frac{f^{a}\varphi_{a}^{\ast}(f^{b})}{f^{b}\varphi_{b}^{\ast}(f^{a})}~.

(B.48)

The last factor is then canceled by the transformation of the ratio
h^{a,b}/h^{b,a}
.

\displaystyle=1~,

\displaystyle P_{a,a}

\displaystyle=1~,

\displaystyle P_{ab,c}

) we see that

and

To study the last condition, we write

The ratio of the

\bullet

\bullet

\bullet

Next we tackle the

\displaystyle\frac{P^{{\cal A}}_{ab,c}}{P^{{\cal A}}_{a,c}P^{{\cal A}}_{b,c}}=\exp i\left[\left(\int_{x}^{\varphi_{ab(x)}}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{a}(x)}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}\right){\cal A}^{c}+\int_{x}^{\varphi_{c}(x)}({\cal A}^{a}-\varphi_{b}^{\ast}({\cal A}^{a}))\right]\frac{\varphi_{c}^{\ast}(h^{a,b})}{h^{a,b}}~.

We rewrite the first term as

\displaystyle\left(\int_{x}^{\varphi_{ab(x)}}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{a}(x)}-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}\right){\cal A}^{c}=-\int_{\Sigma_{a,b}}d{\cal A}^{c}~-\int_{x}^{\varphi_{b}(x)}({\cal A}^{c}-\varphi_{a}^{\ast}({\cal A}^{c})),

\displaystyle\frac{P_{ab,c}}{P_{a,c}P_{b,c}}=\frac{\psi^{a,b;c}\psi^{c,a;b}}{\psi^{a,c;b}}=1~.

(B.64)

We saw above that in the case

we have
\psi^{a,b;c}=1
, so the orbifold partition function is consistent for every equivariant gerbe with trivial
T^{a,b}
.

Possibilities for new equivariant gerbes

While trivial
T^{a,b}
imply that
\psi^{a,b;c}=1
, the consistency condition in (
B.64
) appears to be weaker than
\psi^{a,b;c}=1
. Since the general requirements for a
G
–action on the gerbe— (
B.26
) for a general gerbe or (
B.34
) for a trivial gerbe—also do not appear to require
\psi^{a,b;c}=1
, this naively suggests that there may be consistent equivariant gerbes with
\psi^{a,b;c}\neq 1
satisfying (
B.64
). We do not believe this to be the case: for example, it is hard to understand how a non-trivial class in
H^{3}(G,\operatorname{U{}}(1))
would show up in the computation of
H^{2}(X/G,\operatorname{U{}}(1))
—the cohomology group that classifies flat gerbes with connections.

On the other hand, it seems more likely that it is possible to include non-trivial bundles
T^{a,b}
, provided that (
B.21
) is obeyed with
\psi^{a,b;c}=1
. This could potentially enlarge the class of equivariant gerbes on
X
with
\left(H^{2}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})\right)^{\text{tors}}\neq 0
. If our goal is to use the equivariant gerbes to describe gerbes on the quotient
M=X/G
, then we can avoid the complications associated to the
T^{a,b}
by replacing
X
with its universal cover
X^{\circ}
, so that
X=X^{\circ}/G^{\circ}
. Since
(H^{2}(X^{\circ},{\mathbb{Z}}))^{\text{tors}}=(H_{1}(X^{\circ},{\mathbb{Z}}))^{\text{tors}}=0
, all flat line bundles on
X^{\circ}
are trivial, and we can describe the gerbes on
M
with the simpler construction of equivariant gerbes on
X^{\circ}
with respect to the group
G^{\circ}\times G
.
