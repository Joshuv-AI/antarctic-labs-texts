Contribution

On a high level, the extra randomness in

2
Preliminary

In the classical secretary problem, a decision maker wants to maximize the probability of picking the largest number out of
n
numbers that arrive in a uniform random order. Each time a number arrives, its relative ranking among all numbers that have arrived is revealed, and the decision maker must decide whether to pick it or not immediately and irrevocably. In this work, we consider the variant where, instead of a fixed, known
n
, the total number to be randomly permuted is a random variable
N
independent of the random permutation, and the decision maker’s goal is to maximize the probability of picking the largest number
before the process stops (at time
N
)
.

Before formally stating the problem in Section
2.1
, we briefly discuss the impacts due to the randomness in
N
. The secretary problem and the variant studied in this work can be viewed as optimal stopping problems, where stopping at time
t
means picking the
t
-th arriving number. A decision maker who knows the value of
N
(as in the classical setting) is equivalent to a stopping time that is adapted to the filtration generated by the observations (relative rankings) together with
N
. By contrast, in our setting, the stopping time is adapted to the filtration generated by the relative rankings with only
\mathbf{1}[N\geq i]
for
i=1,2,\ldots
, which are sub-
\sigma
-algebras of those in the previous case, thereby leading to an optimal value which can never be greater than the previous case (more details in Appendix
A.2
).

On the positive side, modeling
N
as a random variable is more realistic than some other common ways to model parameter uncertainty. Consider the setting where
N
has an upper bound
n
, and
N
is picked by an adversary who has access to the same “rolling” information as the decision maker. That is, at time
t
, the adversary is given the relative ranking of the
t
-th arriving number, as well as the decision maker’s decision at time
t-1
, and then needs to decide immediately and irrevocably if
N=t
. We show then that the adversary has a strategy such that the decision maker cannot succeed with probability more than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
(more details in Appendix
A.3
). As a comparison, if
N\leq n
is a random variable, as opposed to be picked by an adversary, the decision maker has a strategy which guarantees a
\frac{1}{1+H_{n-1}}\approx\frac{1}{\log(n)}
probability of success in the worst case (Theorem
3.2
). In addition, modeling
N
as a random variable allows us to explore how prior information about its distribution (such as upper bound or samples) might help in choosing strategies.

2.1
Set up

We assume that there is a sequence of numbers
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
(assuming no tie), and a distribution
\mathbf{p}\in\Delta
, where
\Delta:=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}=1,~y_{i}\geq 0~\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\}

First, nature generates

\mathbf{p}
such that
\mathbb{P}[N=i]=p_{i}

\{x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(t)}\}

, the decision maker using the strategy

must decide whether to accept or reject

The goal of the decision maker is to maximize the probability of success, i.e. picking the largest number before the process stops. We denote this event under strategy

With these notations, the classical secretary problem corresponds to the case when the decision maker knows, before picking the strategy, that
\mathbf{p}=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(n)}
is the delta distribution supported on
\{n\}

\delta^{(n)}_{i}=\mathbf{1}[n=i]

\mathbf{p}

\mathbf{p}

\mathbf{p}

, or iid samples under

To avoid confusion, we distinguish between

4
Beyond secretary: matroid, prophet, online LP

We apply the idea of Theorem
3.2
– randomizing over algorithms (strategies) designed for problems with known time horizon – to three other classical online problems: matroid secretary problem, iid prophet inequality, and online packing LP. In the classical setting, the performance of algorithms for these problems are often evaluated using the competitive ratio, defined as the expectation of the ratio between the output of the algorithm and the optimal. When the time horizon
N
for these problems is random, we can look at a modified performance metric: the expectation (over the randomness in
N
) of the classical metric restricted to the first
N
arrivals. Detailed setup and comparison with previous results are deferred to Appendix
B
.

We assume here that
N
is a random variable with known upper and lower bound. Interestingly, for these three problems, all algorithms that achieve the optimal or best known performance require the time horizon as an input. Let
\{\mathcal{A}^{(s)}\}_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of algorithms designed for problem instances when the time horizon is
s
. Just like in the random horizon secretary problem, the key quantity here is how sensitive the performance of
\{\mathcal{A}^{(s)}\}_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
is to “misspeficiation” of the time horizon. More concretely, let
M:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be the performance of
\mathcal{A}^{(s)}
applied to a problem whose time horizon is
n
in the metrics of interest, and assume that
\underline{n}\leq N\leq\overline{n}
almost surely, where
\underline{n},\overline{n}\in\mathbb{N}
are known. We have the following result:

.

Assume that there exists a constant

and a nondecreasing function

, such that for each

, and
\mathcal{A}^{(S)}
is used, then the expected performance of this randomized algorithm is at least
\frac{c_{0}}{1+\log(f(\overline{n})/f(\underline{n}))}
for any distribution of
N
that is supported on
\{\underline{n},\ldots,\overline{n}\}
.

Surprisingly, independent of the actual algorithms
\{\mathcal{A}^{(s)}\}_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
, just due to the random permutation or iid assumption, the specific form

•

matroid secretary problem:

for rank-

•

is the observed sequence, for instance

–

if

–

if

A.2
Why the problem becomes harder?

In this section, we provide some intuition on how the randomness in the time horizon increases the uncertainty in the reward of the decisions, thereby making the problem harder.

We start with the following optimal stopping problem: there is a sequence of random variables
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
defined on the same probability space.
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
is a filtration on this probability space. (Different from the classical optimal stopping problem, we don’t assume that
X_{j}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{i}
for
j\leq i\leq n
.)

For convenience, let “
\tau\in\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
” denote a stopping time
\tau
which is adapted to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and satisfies
\tau\leq n
almost surely. In addition, we define

V^{*}_{\mathcal{F}}:=\sup_{\tau\in\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}}\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}].

By a simple conditioning argument, we can reduce the problem to the classical setting:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\mathbf{1}[\tau=i]]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\mathbf{1}[\tau=i]]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\mathbf{1}[\tau=i]|\mathcal{F}_{i}]]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]\cdot\mathbf{1}[\tau=i]]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]\cdot\mathbf{1}[\tau=i]]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{\tau}]

where
\tilde{X}_{i}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]
. Thus, under mild conditions, classical results about optimal stopping (e.g. in
[
13
]
) applied to the sequence
\{\tilde{X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
gives that

V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}=\tilde{X}_{n},\quad V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i)}=\max(\tilde{X}_{i},\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{F}_{i}])~i\in[n-1],\quad V^{*}_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(1)}].

Now let
\{\mathcal{G}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be another filtration such that
\mathcal{G}_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. That is,
\mathcal{G}_{i}
contains no more information than
\mathcal{F}_{i}
for all
i
. Similarly, let
\tilde{Y}_{i}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i}]
, and

V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(n)}=\tilde{Y}_{n},\quad V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(i)}=\max(\tilde{Y}_{i},\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{G}_{i}])~i\in[n-1],\quad V^{*}_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}].

Intuitively, the performance of a stopping time should not be hurt by having more information. The lemma below formalizes this idea.

Lemma A.1
.

V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(i)}\leq\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i)}|\mathcal{G}_{i}]
for all
i\in[n]
. Thus,
V^{*}_{\mathcal{G}}\leq V^{*}_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Proof.

We prove it by induction. For
i=n
, since
\mathcal{G}_{n}\subset\mathcal{F}_{n}
,

\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(n)}|\mathcal{G}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n}]|\mathcal{G}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{n}|\mathcal{G}_{n}]=V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(n)}.

Now suppose the statement is true for
i+1
, then for
i
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i)}|\mathcal{G}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\max(\tilde{X}_{i},\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{F}_{i}])|\mathcal{G}_{i}]

\displaystyle\geq\max(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i}],\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]|\mathcal{G}_{i}])

\displaystyle=\max(\tilde{Y}_{i},\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{G}_{i}])

\displaystyle=\max(\tilde{Y}_{i},\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{G}_{i+1}]|\mathcal{G}_{i}])

\displaystyle\geq\max(\tilde{Y}_{i},\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{G}_{i}])=V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(i)}.

This finishes the induction. The second statement is by noticing

V^{*}_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathbb{E}[V^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[V^{(1)}_{\mathcal{F}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}]]\geq\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}]=V^{*}_{\mathcal{G}}.

∎

The key step in the proof above is the inequality

\mathbb{E}[\max(\tilde{X}_{i},\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{F}_{i}])|\mathcal{G}_{i}]\geq\max(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i}],\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathcal{F}}^{(i+1)}|\mathcal{F}_{i}]|\mathcal{G}_{i}])

which captures the idea that making decision based on
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
(taking maximum over
\mathcal{F}_{i}
-measurable random variables, then taking expectation over
\mathcal{G}_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}_{i}
) gives higher return than making decision based on
\{\mathcal{G}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
only (averaging the variable over
\mathcal{G}_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}_{i}
first, then taking maximum over
\mathcal{G}_{i}
-measurable random variables).

Going back to the problem when the time horizon is a random variable, assume that there is an underlying process
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
which the algorithm observes, a random time horizon
N\leq n
, and the reward
X_{i}=X_{i}(Z_{i},N)
of picking the
i
-th number, which depends on
Z_{i}
and possibly also on
N
. The information we have at time
i
is
\mathcal{G}_{i}=\sigma(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i},\mathbf{1}[N\geq 1],\ldots,\mathbf{1}[N\geq i])
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the observations
Z_{j}
and
\mathbf{1}[N\geq j]
for
j\leq i
.

As a comparison, define
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\sigma(N,Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i})
, which consists of all information about
N
as well as the observations up till time
i
, thus
\mathcal{G}_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Under this setting, an algorithm which doesn’t know (the realization of) the time horizon needs to choose
\tau\in\{\mathcal{G}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, while an algorithm that knows (the realization of) the time horizon picks
\tau\in\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
. Then by Lemma
A.1
, the algorithm that knows
N
can achieve better return.

More concretely, in the setting of the secretary problem,
Z_{i}\sim Bernoulli(\frac{1}{i})
denotes whether the
i
-th number is the best among the first
i
numbers, and

X_{i}(z_{i},k)=\mathbb{P}[i\text{-th number is the best among all }N\text{ numbers}|Z_{i}=z_{i},N=k]=\begin{cases}\frac{i}{k}&\quad z_{i}=1,~i\leq k\\
0&\quad o.w.\end{cases}

For the prophet inequality problem with the sequence of random variable
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, let
X_{i}(z_{i},k)=\frac{z_{i}\mathbf{1}[i\leq k]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[k]}Z_{i}]}
, then the metric of interest becomes

\mathbb{E}[\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[N]}Z_{i}|N]}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\frac{Z_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[N]}Z_{i}|N]}|N]]=\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]

where
\tau\in\{\mathcal{G}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
.

A.3
Adversarially picked
N

Assume that
N\leq n
has an upper bound, and
N
is picked by an adversary. At time
t
, the adversary is given the relative ranking of the
t
-th arriving number together with the decision maker’s decision at time
t-1
, and needs to decide if
N=t
.

Consider the following strategy for the adversary: for
t=1,2,\ldots,\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
, the adversary chooses
N>t
. At time
t=\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor+1
, if the decision maker has picked a number (among the first
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
arrivals), then the adversary chooses
N=n
, otherwise the adversary chooses
N=\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor+1
.

Lemma A.2
.

Under the above strategy of the adversary, for any decision maker, the probability that he picks the largest number among the
N
numbers that arrive is no more than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

Proof.

We consider the two cases in the strategy. If the decision maker picks some number among the first
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
arrivals, then the probability that he picks the largest one among
N=n
numbers is upper bounded by the probability that the largest one of
n
numbers is among the first
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
arrivals, which due to the random permutation has probability upper bounded by
\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor}{n}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

If no number is picked among the first
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
arrivals, if the decision maker does not pick the
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor+1
-th number, he automatically fails, and if he picks, due to the random permutation, the probability that the
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor+1
-th number is the largest one among the first
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor+1
arrivals is
\frac{1}{\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor+1}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

In both cases, the decision maker cannot succeed with probability more than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

∎

B.2
IID prophet inequality

In the iid prophet inequality problem, there are
n

drawn independently according to some known distribution

[0,\infty)

X_{t}
or not. Let
X_{\tau}
be the number being picked. Then
the performance is measured in terms of
\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}]}
.

[
15
]
proposes a threshold rule algorithm, where the thresholds depend only on the distribution
F
and the number of numbers

. The algorithm achieves a
\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}]}>0.745=c
, which matches the known upper bound
[
14
,
29
]
. We denote this threshold rule algorithm for problems with
n
numbers as
\mathcal{A}^{(n)}
.

In the random time horizon setting, we take

to be a random variable with distribution
\mathbf{p}
, independent of

\mathbb{E}[\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[N]}X_{i}|N]}]

\mathcal{A}^{(s)}
, we have

\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N=k]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[N]}X_{i}|N=k]}\geq c\cdot\sum_{s=1}^{k}x_{s}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[s]}X_{i}]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[k]}X_{i}]}.

Thus taking the expectation of the above equation with respect to

Now for

[x,1]

B.3
Online Packing LP

For the online (packing) linear programming problem, the early lines of research (e.g.
[
10
,
9
]
) usually don’t assume that the time horizon is known (or even finite), and the arrival order can be arbitrary. However, it has been shown that the optimal competitive ratio is
O(\log(m\frac{a_{\max}}{a_{\min}})^{-1})
where
m
is the number of constraints, and
a_{\max},a_{\min}
are the upper and lower(non-zero) bound on the constraint coefficients. As a result, the competitive ratio is usually bounded away from
1
. In contrast, recent lines of research have been focusing on achieving high (close to
1
) competitive ratio under the random permutation, large budget-to-bid ratio model
[
2
,
30
,
35
]
. However, the proposed algorithms all require the time horizon, or at least an approximation of the time horizon within
1\pm\epsilon
factor
[
2
]
, as part of the input in order to achieve
1-O(\epsilon)
competitiveness for small
\epsilon
. The sampling based algorithm we propose in Section
4
, when given such approximation, also achieves similar performance. Recently,
[
7
]
studies the online LP problem with known upper bound
\overline{n}
and lower bound
\underline{n}
on the time horizon. The dual mirror descent method they propose achieves a competitive ratio of
1/(1+\log(\overline{n}/\underline{n})
against the worst case horizon
n\in\{\underline{n},\ldots,\overline{n}\}
, which is optimal up to a
\log\log(\overline{n}/\underline{n})
factor.

Below, we first state the setup for the classical online packing LP:

\max_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\pi_{t}x_{t}\quad s.t.~\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{a}_{t}x_{t}\leq\mathbf{b},~x_{t}\in[0,1]

(3)

where
\mathbf{a}_{t},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, and
\pi_{t}\geq 0
for all
t
. The algorithm knows the budget
\mathbf{b}
in the beginning, but not the coefficients
(\pi_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1}),\ldots,(\pi_{n},\mathbf{a}_{n})
. Instead, a permutation
\sigma:[n]\to[n]
is chosen uniformly at random, and
(\pi_{\sigma(t)},\mathbf{a}_{\sigma(t)})
is revealed at time
t
. The algorithm needs to decide irrevocably
x_{\sigma(t)}
before time
t+1
while satisfying the constraints. The performance is measured using competitive ratio defined as
\mathbb{E}[\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\pi_{t}\hat{x}_{t}}{\mathsf{OPT}}]
where
\mathsf{OPT}
is the optimal value to the problem (
3
), and
\hat{x}_{t}
is the algorithm’s decision for
x_{t}
.
[
35
]
has proposed a dual-resolving algorithm for
\epsilon\in(0,1/100)
which, under the assumption that
\max_{i\in[m],t\in[n]}\frac{b_{i}}{a_{t,i}}=\Omega(\frac{m^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log(\frac{m}{\epsilon}))
, achieves the competitive ratio
1-50\epsilon
when
n
is known, and we denote this algorithm as
\mathcal{A}^{(n)}
.

Now consider the variant where the time horizon is
N\leq n
a random variable, and for
n^{\prime}\leq n
,
\sigma:[n]\to[n]
,
\mathsf{OPT}(\sigma,n^{\prime})
denotes the solution to the following problem:

\max_{x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n^{\prime})}}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\pi_{\sigma(t)}x_{\sigma(t)}\quad s.t.~\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\mathbf{a}_{\sigma(t)}x_{\sigma(t)}\leq\mathbf{b},~x_{\sigma(t)}\in[0,1].

Then the performance measure of interest is
\mathbb{E}[\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{N}\pi_{\sigma}(t)\hat{x}_{\sigma(t)}}{\mathsf{OPT}(\sigma,N)}]
. And similar to the argument for the matroid secretary problem, we can take
M^{(LP)}(s,n)\geq(1-50\epsilon)\frac{s}{n}\mathbf{1}[s\leq n]
, leading to a performance of
\mathbb{E}[\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{N}\pi_{\sigma}(t)\hat{x}_{\sigma(t)}}{\mathsf{OPT}(\sigma,N)}]\geq(1-50\epsilon)/(1+\log(\overline{n}/\underline{n}))
, matching the result in
[
7
]
.

suboptimal for

S
takes with probability at least
2/3
using
m

For simplicity, for any distribution

to denote

the
m

\mathbf{p}
. Then from classical results (textbook reference
[
41
]
), the optimal test for
S
makes mistake with probability

To achieve

,

\displaystyle\quad 2D^{2}_{TV}(\otimes^{m}\mathbf{w}^{(n,s_{n}^{*}+\epsilon)},\otimes^{m}\mathbf{w}^{(n,s_{n}^{*}-\epsilon)})

\displaystyle\leq D_{KL}(\otimes^{m}\mathbf{w}^{(n,s_{n}^{*}+\epsilon)}||\otimes^{m}\mathbf{w}^{(n,s_{n}^{*}-\epsilon)})\quad\text{Pinsker's inequality}

\displaystyle=mD_{KL}(\mathbf{w}^{(n,s_{n}^{*}+\epsilon)}||\mathbf{w}^{(n,s_{n}^{*}-\epsilon)})\quad\text{tensorization for KL-divergence}

By reverse Pinsker inequality
[
40

Taking

\mathbb{P}[\tau\in I_{i}|N=k_{l},A]=\frac{\mathbb{P}[\tau\in I_{i},N=k_{l},A]}{\mathbb{P}[N=k_{l},A]}=\frac{\mathbb{P}[\tau\in I_{i},A]\mathbb{P}[N=k_{l}]}{\mathbb{P}[A]\mathbb{P}[N=k_{l}]}=\mathbb{P}[\tau\in I_{i}|A]:=q_{i}.

And so

\mathbb{E}_{A}[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N=k_{l}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}\theta_{i}q_{i}\implies\frac{\mathbb{E}_{A}[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N=k_{l}]}{\mathbb{E}_{A}[\max_{i\in[N]}X_{i}|N=k_{l}]}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\theta_{l-1}}q_{i}.

The ratio of interest can be upper bounded by

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[N]}X_{i}|N]}]\leq\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\theta_{l-1}}q_{i}p_{l}=x^{-1/n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\theta_{l}}q_{i}p_{l}.

With
p_{l}=c\cdot\begin{cases}1-x^{1/n}&\quad l\in[n-1]\\
1&\quad l=n\end{cases}
, where
c=(1+(n-1)(1-x^{1/n}))^{-1}
, thus

\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\theta_{j}}\cdot p_{j}=c~\forall i\in[n]\implies\mathbb{E}_{A}[\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[N]}X_{i}|N]}]\leq x^{-1/n}c.

Since on
A^{c}
the above ratio is bounded by
1
, we get the desired result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
B.1
.

Take
G_{x,n}
to be the distribution in Theorem
E.2
, with
\xi_{n}=1/n
(or any sequence such that
\xi_{n}\to 0
), and
P_{n}
correspondingly, then by Theorem
E.2

\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{N\sim P_{n},X_{i}\sim G_{x,n}}[\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq N]|N]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[N]}X_{i}|N]}]\leq(x^{1/n}(1+(n-1)(1-x^{1/n})))^{-1}+\xi_{n}.

Taking
n\to\infty
proves the result.
∎

Appendix F
Auxiliary results

Lemma F.1
.

For
T\geq 3
, let
s_{T}^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i}\leq 1
but
\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i}>1
, then
\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}(\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i})\geq 1/e
.

Proof.

For
T\leq 9
,
[
24
]
lists the values of
\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}(\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i})
, which are all
\geq 1/e
. Below we assume that
T\geq 10
. Since
\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i}\geq 1
, if
\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}\geq 1/e
, the result holds. Below, we consider the case when
\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}<1/e
. Next, we give a lower bound for
\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}
.

1\geq\sum_{i=s_{T}*}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i}\geq\int_{s_{T}^{*}}^{T}\frac{1}{x}dx=\log(\frac{T}{s_{T}^{*}})\implies\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}\geq\frac{1}{e}-\frac{1}{T}.

Since for any
a\geq 1
, we have
\frac{1}{a}\geq\int_{a}^{a+1}\frac{1}{z}dz+\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{a+1})

\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i}\geq\int_{s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T}\frac{1}{x}dx+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T-1}(\frac{1}{i}-\frac{1}{i+1})=\log(\frac{T}{s_{T}^{*}-1})+\frac{1}{2T}(\frac{T}{s_{T}^{*}-1}-1).

Thus, defining
g_{T}(x)=-x\log(x)+\frac{1}{2T}(1-x)
, we have

\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}(\sum_{i=s_{T}^{*}-1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{i})\geq g_{T}(\frac{s_{T}^{*}-1}{T}).

Since
g_{T}(\frac{1}{e})=\frac{1}{e}+(1-\frac{1}{e})/2T>\frac{1}{e}
, and
g_{T}(x)
is concave in
x
, as long as
g_{T}(\frac{1}{e}-\frac{1}{T})\geq\frac{1}{e}
, we have that
g_{T}(x)\geq\frac{1}{e}
for all
x\in[\frac{1}{e}-\frac{1}{T},\frac{1}{e}]
. So it remains to show
g_{T}(\frac{1}{e}-\frac{1}{T})\geq\frac{1}{e}
. Let’s define
h(y)=-(\frac{1}{e}-y)\log(\frac{1}{e}-y)+\frac{1}{2}y(1-\frac{1}{e}+y)
for
y\in[0,\frac{1}{10}]
, then
g_{T}(\frac{1}{e}-\frac{1}{T})=h(\frac{1}{T})
.
Notice that

h^{\prime}(y)=\log(\frac{1}{e}-y)+1+y+\frac{1}{2}\cdot(1-\frac{1}{e}),\quad h^{\prime\prime}(y)=-\frac{1}{1/e-y}+1\leq 0.

And so for
y\in[0,\frac{1}{10}]
,

h^{\prime}(y)\geq h^{\prime}(\frac{1}{10})=\log(\frac{1}{e}-\frac{1}{10})+1+\frac{1}{10}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot(1-\frac{1}{e})>0.09>0.

Thus
h
is non-decreasing on
[0,\frac{1}{10}]
, and so for all
y\in[0,\frac{1}{10}]
,

h(y)\geq h(0)=\frac{1}{e}.

∎

Lemma F.2
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{l})
be a random vector uniformly sampled on the
l
-dimensional probability simplex, i.e.
\mathbf{Y}\sim Dir(1,1,\ldots,1)
. For any fixed
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{l}
, let
\overline{a}=\frac{1}{l}\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{1}\rangle
and
a^{*}=\max_{i\in[l]}a_{i}
, then
\forall 0\leq\epsilon\leq\overline{a}
,

\mathbb{P}[\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{Y}\rangle\leq\epsilon]\leq(h_{\overline{a}/a^{*}}(\epsilon/\overline{a}))^{l}

where
h_{\alpha}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined for
0<\alpha\leq 1
,
h_{\alpha}(x):=x^{\alpha}(\frac{1/\alpha+1}{1/\alpha+x})^{\alpha+1}
, and
h_{\alpha}(x)<1
for
x<1
.

Proof of Lemma
F.2
.

It’s well known that for a random vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{l})
where
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[l]}
are iid
Exp(1)
,
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{l})=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}}\mathbf{X}
follows the uniform distribution on
\Delta^{(l)}
. For any
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{l}
,

\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{Y}\rangle\leq\epsilon\iff\langle\epsilon\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X}\rangle\geq 0.

Consider the moment generating function of
\langle\epsilon\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X}\rangle

\phi(\xi)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\xi\langle\epsilon\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X}\rangle}]=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\mathbb{E}[e^{\xi(\epsilon-a_{i})X_{i}}]=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\frac{1}{1-\xi(\epsilon-a_{i})}

defined for
\xi
such that
\xi(\epsilon-a_{i})<1
for all
i\in[l]
. Since
(1-\xi\epsilon)(1+\xi a_{i})=1-\xi(\epsilon-a_{i})-\xi^{2}\epsilon a_{i}\leq 1-\xi(\epsilon-a_{i})
, we have

\frac{1}{1-\xi(\epsilon-a_{i})}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\xi\epsilon)(1+\xi a_{i})}.

By convexity of the exponential function, for any
\overline{x}>0
fixed,
\forall x\in[0,\overline{x}]
, we have
1+x\geq e^{\frac{\log(1+\overline{x})}{\overline{x}}x}
. Taking
a^{*}=\max_{i\in[l]}a_{i}
,
\overline{x}=\xi a^{*}
, and
\overline{a}=\sum_{i\in[l]}a_{i}/l
, then

\phi(\xi)\leq(1-\xi\epsilon)^{-l}\cdot e^{-\frac{\log(1+\overline{x})}{\overline{x}}\xi\sum_{i\in[l]}a_{i}}=(\frac{(1+\xi a^{*})^{-\frac{\overline{a}}{a^{*}}}}{1-\xi\epsilon})^{l}.

For
\epsilon\leq\overline{a}
, take
\xi^{*}=\frac{1/\epsilon-1/\overline{a}}{a^{*}/\overline{a}+1}<1/\epsilon
, denote
\alpha_{0}=\overline{a}/a^{*}
,

\frac{(1+\xi^{*}a^{*})^{-\frac{\overline{a}}{a^{*}}}}{1-\xi^{*}\epsilon}=\frac{(\epsilon/\overline{a})^{\overline{a}/a^{*}}}{((a^{*}+\epsilon)/(a^{*}+\overline{a}))^{\overline{a}/a^{*}+1}}=(\epsilon/\overline{a})^{\alpha_{0}}\cdot(\frac{1/\alpha_{0}+1}{1/\alpha_{0}+\epsilon/\overline{a}})^{\alpha_{0}+1}=h_{\alpha_{0}}(\epsilon/\overline{a}).

Thus, for
\epsilon\leq\overline{a}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{Y}\rangle\leq\epsilon]=\mathbb{P}[\langle\epsilon\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X}\rangle\geq 0]\leq\phi(\xi^{*})\leq(h_{\alpha_{0}}(\epsilon/\overline{a}))^{l}.

Since
h_{\alpha}(1)=1
,

\frac{d}{dx}h_{\alpha}(x)=(\frac{\alpha}{x}-\frac{\alpha+1}{1/\alpha+x})h_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1-x}{x(1/\alpha+x)}h_{\alpha}(x).

Thus
h_{\alpha}
is increasing on
(0,1)
, and so
\forall x\in[0,1)
,
h_{\alpha}(x)<1
.

∎

Lemma F.3
.

Let
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be two sequences in
\mathbb{R}
such that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq a_{i}\leq b_{i}
. In addition,
\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=0
. Then there exists an index
i^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{i^{*}}\geq a_{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

If
a_{i}=0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then taking
i^{*}=1
works. Otherwise, let
l\in\mathbb{N}
be the first index such that

, there exists
l^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}

∎

Lemma F.4

(Hoeffding inequality)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variable such that
0\leq X_{i}\leq 1
for all
i\in[n]
almost surely. Then for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}]\geq t]\leq e^{-2t^{2}/n}.

Lemma F.5

(Bernstein’s inequality)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variable. Suppose that there exists
M\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]|\leq M
for all
i\in[n]
almost surely. Then for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}]\geq t]\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}/2}{V+Mt/3}}

where
V:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Var[X_{i}]
.

Lemma F.6

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent Bernoulli random variables. Denote
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
. Then for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}[X\geq(1+t)\mu]\leq e^{-t^{2}\mu/(2+t)}.

For all
0<t<1
,

\mathbb{P}[X\leq(1-t)\mu]\leq e^{-t^{2}\mu/2}.
