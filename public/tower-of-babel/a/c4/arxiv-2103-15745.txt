On N N -Unital Functions
Jianqiang Zhao

Abstract




Abstract.

Let
N
be a positive integer. We say a non-constant rational function
U(x)\in{\mathbb{C}}(x)
is
N
-
unital
if all the zeros and poles of both
U(x)
and
1-U(x)
are either 0 or
N
-th roots of unity. These functions are called admissible functions by Au in a recent paper arXiv:2007.03957 and used to study some central binomial series of Apéry type via their iterated integral expression related to multiple polylogrithms and colored multiple zeta values. In this paper we determine the complete set of these functions for
N\leq 4
by elementary method, and briefly study some cases at level 5.

1
Introduction

The study of central binomial series of Apéry type has gradually attracted the attention of a lot number theorist ever after
Apéry
[
2
]
successfully proved the irrationality of
\zeta(3)
by using one of such series. Z.-W. Sun
posted many conjectured identities in
[
5
]
. Oftentimes, these series are related to powers of
\pi
of special values
of the Riemann zeta function. Recently, Au
[
1
]
showed that some of these series can expressed
using the colored multiple zeta values, i.e., special values of multiple polylogarithms at roots of unities.

Let
N
be a positive integer.
We say a non-constant rational function
U(x)\in\mathbb{C}(x)
is
N
-unital if all the zeros and poles of both
U(x)

and
1-U(x)
are either 0 or
N
-th roots of unity. Let
\mathsf{U}_{N}
be the set of all
N
-unital functions.
For convenience, for any rational function
f
we define its
S_{3}
orbit by

\langle f\rangle_{6}=\left\{f,1-f,\frac{1}{f},\frac{f}{f-1},\frac{f-1}{f},\frac{-1}{f-1}\right\}.

Then it is not hard to show that
\mathsf{U}_{1}=\langle x\rangle_{6}
. In
[
1
]
, Au used the name “admissible”
for such functions. We changed the name to avoid confusion with admissibility of multiple zeta values
whose study is intimately related to these functions.

Au conjectured in
[
1
]
that
\mathsf{U}_{N}
is finite for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
. As kindly pointed out to me by F. Brunault,
this in fact follows from a more general theorem on the
S
-unit equations of Mason
[
4
]
, see
[
3
, Cor. 2.2]
. He also graciously shared his MAGMA program he used to compute these functions which detected some missing 3- and 4-unital functions in a previous draft.

Au
[
1
]
made some conjectures on the set
{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
of possible values of such functions at
0 when
N=2
and 4, which are crucial in his study of some central binomial series of Apéry type via their iterated integral expression related to multiple polylogrithms and colored multiple zeta values. In this paper we will determine
\mathsf{U}_{N}
precisely for
N\leq 4
so that we can compute
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}
and
{\mathcal{C}}^{4}
precisely. We also make a conjecture on
{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
for all
N
.

2
Statements of the main results

Au explained in
[
1
, Appendix B]
how one might discover some
4
-unital functions,
and his list covers most of these function although it is not complete. In fact, his list contains
168 such functions while the true number should be 252.

For any positive integer
N
we denote the set of
N
-th roots of unity by
\Gamma_{N}=\{\exp(2\pi i/j):j=1,\dots,N\}
.
First, Au observed that the orbit of
x
under the octahedral symmetry
S_{4}
of 24 elements and the
S_{3}
symmetry of 6 elements

\langle f\rangle_{6}
produces 72 rational functions:

\displaystyle O(x):=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{{\varepsilon}\in\Gamma_{4}}\langle{\varepsilon}x\rangle_{6}\cup\left\langle\frac{i({\varepsilon}x+1)}{{\varepsilon}x-1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{{\varepsilon}x+1}{{\varepsilon}x+i}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}.

Similarly, the orbit of
x^{2}
has 36 rational functions:

\displaystyle O(x^{2}):=\bigcup_{{\varepsilon}=\pm 1}\langle{\varepsilon}x^{2}\rangle_{6}\cup\left\langle{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x-i}{x+i}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}.

The orbit of
x^{4}
has 18 rational functions:

\displaystyle O(x^{4}):=\langle x^{4}\rangle_{6}\cup\left\langle\left(\frac{x+1}{x-1}\right)^{4}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\left(\frac{x-i}{x+i}\right)^{4}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}.

Au also noticed the following two orbits: one with 36 elements

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\right):=\bigcup_{{\varepsilon}=\pm 1}\left\langle\frac{2{\varepsilon}x}{x^{2}+1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{2{\varepsilon}ix}{x^{2}-1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{{\varepsilon}(x^{2}-1)}{x^{2}+1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6},

and the other with 6 elements:
\left\langle\frac{4x^{2}}{(x^{2}+1)^{2}}\right\rangle_{\hskip-2.0pt6}
.

Now, it is not difficult to check that
\mathsf{U}_{4}
contains three more orbits
of 48, 24 and 12 elements (the non-trivial symmetries are from
C_{4}\times S_{3}
where

C_{4}
is the cyclic symmetry determined by
i\to i^{n}
,
0\leq n\leq 4
):

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{2x}{x+1}\right):=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{{\varepsilon}=\pm 1}\left\langle\frac{{\varepsilon}(x-1)}{x+1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{{\varepsilon}(x-i)}{x+i}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{(1-i)x}{x+{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{(1+i)x}{x+{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6},

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{x(x-1)}{x^{2}+1}\right):=

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{x(x-1)}{x^{2}+1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{x(x+1)}{x^{2}+1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{x(x-i)}{x^{2}-1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{x(x+i)}{x^{2}-1}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6},

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{2(1+i)x}{(x+1)(x+i)}\right):=

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{2(1+i)x}{(x+1)(x+i)}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{2(1-i)x}{(x+1)(x-i)}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}.

Graphically, we have the following explanation of the two orbits above:

Theorem 1
.

There are exactly
252

4
-unital functions which are given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}_{4}=

\displaystyle\,O(x)\cup O(x^{2})\cup O(x^{4})\cup O\left(\frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\right)\cup\left\langle\frac{4x^{2}}{(x^{2}+1)^{2}}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}

\displaystyle\,\cup O\left(\frac{2x}{x+1}\right)\cup O\left(\frac{x(x-1)}{x^{2}+1}\right)\cup O\left(\frac{2(1+i)x}{(x+1)(x+i)}\right).

Corollary 2
.

The set of all
2
-unital functions is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle x\rangle\cup\langle-x\rangle\cup\langle x^{2}\rangle\cup\left\langle\frac{1+x}{2}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{1+x}{2x}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{(1+x)^{2}}{4x}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}.

We list all the
2
- and
4
-unital functions explicitly in Appendix A.

Using the same approach we also find the complete set of
3
-unital functions and list them in Appendix B.

Theorem 3
.

Let
u=\exp(2\pi i/3)
. There are exactly
84

3
-unital functions which are given by

\mathsf{U}_{3}=\langle x^{3}\rangle_{6}\cup\left\langle\frac{(1-{\bar{u}})x}{x-u}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\bigcup_{{\varepsilon}\in\Gamma_{3}}\langle{\varepsilon}x\rangle_{6}\cup\left\langle\frac{1-u}{{\varepsilon}x-u}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{1-u{\varepsilon}x}{1-{\varepsilon}x}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}\cup\left\langle\frac{-3{\varepsilon}x}{({\varepsilon}x-1)^{2}}\right\rangle_{\hskip-4.0pt6}.

For any positive integer
N
, we set

{\mathcal{C}}^{N}=\{U(0):U(x)\in\mathsf{U}_{N}\}.

Corollary 4
.

Let
u=\exp(2\pi i/3)
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\left\{0,\pm 1,\frac{1}{2},2,\infty\right\},\quad{\mathcal{C}}^{4}=\left\{0,\pm 1,\frac{1}{2},2,\pm i,1\pm i,\frac{1\pm i}{2},\infty\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}^{3}=

\displaystyle\left\{0,1,\pm u,\pm{\bar{u}},1-u,1-{\bar{u}},\frac{1}{1-u},\frac{1}{1-{\bar{u}}},\infty\right\}.

From the explicit computation of this paper, we find the following result.

Proposition 5
.

Let
N\leq 4
and
u_{j}=\exp(2j\pi i/N)
for all
j=1,\dots,N-1
. Then

{\mathcal{C}}^{N}=\{0,1,\infty\}\cup\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{\frac{N}{2}}\langle\mu_{j}\rangle_{6},&\hbox{if $N$ is even;}\\
\displaystyle\bigcup_{{\varepsilon}=\pm 1}\bigcup_{j=1}^{\frac{N-1}{2}}\langle{\varepsilon}\mu_{j}\rangle_{6},&\hbox{if $N$ is odd.}\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

Then
U(0)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
for all
U(x)\in\mathsf{U}_{N}
.

Note that both
\langle 1\rangle_{6}
and
\langle-1\rangle_{6}
each has only 3 elements. Moreover, the sets in the proposition may not be disjoint as shown when
N=3
.

Unfortunately, even for
N=5
the set defined by the right-hand side of (
2.1
) is too small to contain all values of 5-unital functions at 0. When
N=5
, setting
u=u_{5}=\exp(2\pi i/5)
we find the following exceptional 5-unital functions:

\displaystyle f(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{(u^{3}+u^{2}+2)x^{2}+(u^{4}+u^{2})x-u^{2}+u-1}{x^{2}-(u^{4}+u^{2})x+u},

\displaystyle g(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{(u^{2}+u+1)x-u^{2}-u-1}{x-u^{2}}.

For example, one can easily check that

f(x)=\frac{(u^{3}+u^{2}+2)(x-1)(x-u)}{(x-u^{2})(x-u^{4})},\quad g(x)=\frac{(u^{2}+u+1)(x-1)}{x-u^{2}}\in\mathsf{U}_{5}

since

1-f(x)=-\frac{(u^{3}+u^{2}+1)(x-u^{3})^{2}}{(x-u^{2})(x-u^{4})},\quad 1-g(x)=\frac{-(u^{2}+u)(x-u^{4})}{x-u^{2}}\in\mathsf{U}_{5}.

But none of the following values are in the set on the right-hand side of (
2.1
):

\displaystyle\langle f(0)\rangle_{6}=

\displaystyle\,\Big\{\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2},\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2},\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2},-\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2},\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}\Big\},

\displaystyle\langle g(0)\rangle_{6}=

\displaystyle\,\Big\{u+u^{2},1-u-u^{2},-u-u^{2},1+u+u^{2},-u^{3}-u^{4},1+u^{3}+u^{4}\Big\}.

3
Some general strategy

For any positive integer
N
we denote the set of
N
-th roots of unity by
\Gamma_{N}=\{\exp(2\pi i/j):j=1,\dots,N\}

and
\Gamma_{N}^{0}=\{0\}\cup\Gamma_{N}
. If
f\in\mathsf{U}_{N}
, then we may assume
1-g=f
for some
g\in\mathsf{U}_{N}
. By considering
possible poles and zeros for
f
and
g
, we see that
1-g=f
can be transformed to the following form

\prod_{l\in L}(x-\mu_{l})^{n_{l}}-C\prod_{k\in K}(x-\mu_{k})^{\alpha_{k}}=D\prod_{j\in J}(x-\mu_{j})^{a_{j}}

(3.2)

with
J\coprod K\coprod L\subset\Gamma_{N}^{0}
where
\mu_{0}=0
and all the exponents
n_{j},\alpha_{j},a_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Namely, we may assume
f
and
g
have exactly the same poles (counting multiplicities) but disjoint zero sets.

The following tricks (techniques?) will be used repeatedly in our proofs of the main theorems.

T1.

If
0\in K
then we see that
D\in\pm\Gamma_{N}\subseteq\Gamma_{2N}
by setting
x=0
. Similarly,

0\in J\Rightarrow C\in\Gamma_{2N}
while
0\in L\Rightarrow C/D\in\Gamma_{2N}
.

T2.

Suppose
N=p
is a prime. If
0,k\in K
and
k\neq 0
then
by setting
x=\mu_{k}
we see that
\sum_{l\in L}n_{l}=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}
by
p
-adic evaluation of
both sides of (
3.2
). Similarly,
0,j\in J,j\neq 0\Rightarrow\sum_{l\in L}n_{l}=\sum_{k\in K}\alpha_{k}

and
0,l\in L,l\neq 0\Rightarrow\sum_{k\in K}\alpha_{k}=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}
. We call this idea the
p
-adic argument.

T3.

Assume
N=p
is a prime. Assume
K=\{0\}
and
l\in L\neq\emptyset,j\in J\neq\emptyset
then
by
x=0\Rightarrow D\in\Gamma_{2N}
. If
\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}<\alpha_{0}
then the highest degree must be
\alpha_{0}

on the LHS and thus
-C=D
by comparing the leading coefficient. But then

x=\mu_{l}\Rightarrow-\mu^{\alpha_{0}}=\prod_{j\in J}(\mu_{l}-\mu_{j})^{a_{j}}
which is absurd by the
p
-adic evaluation.
Similarly,
\sum_{j\in L}k_{j}\geq\alpha_{0}
and therefore
\sum_{j\in L}k_{j}=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}
.
We call this the
single power
N
-adic argument.

T4.

If
N=4
, we will have to modify the above argument by considering the
2
-divisibility of the terms after
evaluating
x
at
\Gamma_{4}^{0}
. For any

0\neq a\in\mathbb{Q}[i]
, define
\ord_{2}(a)=v
if
|a|=2^{v}u
where
u
is a 2-unit, i.e.,
u=p/q
where
p,q
are both odd integers.
We call this idea of considering
2
-divisibility
2
-adic argument.

We will mainly apply T2 and T3 when
N=3
. Note that the
p
-adic argument can imply impossibility
if one of
J,K,L
is empty while the single power
p
-adic argument cannot since
J,K,L
are always nonempty
when this argument applies.

In the proof of the main theorems, we often need to compares coefficients of polynomials.
For convenience, we use the shorthand
n
HD for “the
n
th highest degree term”
for all
n\geq 2
. For example, 2HD stands for “the second highest degree term”.

4
Proof of Theorem
1
: complete set of 4-unital functions

Suppose
f=Dx^{a}(x-1)^{b}(x+i)^{c}(x-i)^{d}(x+i)^{e},g=Cx^{\alpha}(x-1)^{\beta}(x+i)^{\gamma}(x-i)^{\delta}(x+i)^{\epsilon}\in\mathsf{U}_{4}
such that
1-g=f
.
We now break into different cases according the signs of the exponents. Observe that we can simplify our proof by using the fact
that
U(x)\in\mathsf{U}_{4}\Leftrightarrow U(-x)\in\mathsf{U}_{4}\Leftrightarrow U(ix)\in\mathsf{U}_{4}\Leftrightarrow U(-ix)\in\mathsf{U}_{4}
. This will
reduce the number of cases to consider significantly.

I
.
a>0,b,c,d\geq 0,e=0
.

1)

a>0,b=c=d=e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=0,\beta,\gamma,\delta,{\epsilon}\geq 0
. Then

1-C(x-1)^{\beta}(x+1)^{\gamma}(x-i)^{\delta}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}.

Clearly
C=-D
and
a=\beta+\gamma+\delta+{\epsilon}
.

i) If
a=1
, then
\beta+\gamma+\delta+{\epsilon}=1
.

•

\beta=1\Rightarrow\gamma=\delta={\epsilon}=0
. Then
x=1\Rightarrow D=1\Rightarrow\boxed{f=x}.

•

\gamma=1\Rightarrow\beta=\delta={\epsilon}=0
. Then
x=-1\Rightarrow D=-1\Rightarrow\boxed{f=-x}.

•

\delta=1\Rightarrow\beta=\gamma={\epsilon}=0
. Then
x=i\Rightarrow D=-i\Rightarrow\boxed{f=-ix}.

•

{\epsilon}=1\Rightarrow\beta=\gamma=\delta=0
. Then
x=-i\Rightarrow D=i\Rightarrow\boxed{f=ix}.

ii) If
a=\beta+\gamma+\delta+{\epsilon}\geq 2\Rightarrow\beta-\gamma-i(\delta-{\epsilon})=0
by 2HD. So
\beta=\gamma,\delta={\epsilon}
and the equation becomes
1-C(x^{2}-1)^{\beta}(x^{2}+1)^{\gamma}=Dx^{a}
which yields three subcases:

•

\beta=0,\gamma=1
. Then
\boxed{f=-x^{2}}.

•

\beta=1,\gamma=0
. Then
\boxed{f=x^{2}}.

•

\beta+\gamma\geq 2
. Then
\beta=\gamma=1
by 2HD. Thus
\boxed{f=x^{4}}.

2)

a>0,b>0,c=d=e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=\beta=0,\gamma\geq 0,\delta\geq 0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
. Then

1-C(x+1)^{\gamma}(x-i)^{\delta}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}(x-1)^{b}.

This is impossible by the 2-adic argument if we consider
x=0
and
x=1
.

3)

a>0,b=0,c>0,d=e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=\gamma=0,\beta\geq 0,\delta\geq 0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
. Then

1-C(x-1)^{\beta}(x-i)^{\delta}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}(x+1)^{c}.

This is impossible by the 2-adic argument by taking
x=0
and
x=-1
.

4)

a>0,b=c=0,d>0,e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=\delta=0,\beta\geq 0,\gamma\geq 0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
. Then

1-C(x-1)^{\beta}(x+1)^{\gamma}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}(x-i)^{d}.

This is impossible by the 2-adic argument by taking
x=0
and
x=i
.

5)

a>0,b>0,c>0,d=e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=\beta=\gamma=0,\delta\geq 0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
.

1-C(x-i)^{\delta}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}(x-1)^{b}(x+1)^{c}.

This is impossible by the 2-adic argument by taking
x=0
and
x=1
.

6)

a>0,b>0,c=0,d>0,e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=\beta=\delta=0,\gamma\geq 0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
.

1-C(x+1)^{\gamma}(x+i)^{g}g=Dx^{a}(x-1)^{b}(x-i)^{d}.

This is impossible by the 2-adic argument by taking
x=0
and
x=i
.

7)

a>0,b=0,c>0,d>0,e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=\gamma=\delta=0,\beta\geq 0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
.

1-C(x-1)^{\beta}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}(x+1)^{c}(x-i)^{d}.

This is impossible by the 2-adic argument by taking
x=0
and
x=i
.

8)

a>0,b>0,c>0,d>0,e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=\gamma=\beta=0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
.

1-C(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}(x-1)^{b}(x+1)^{c}(x-i)^{d}.

This is impossible by the 2-adic argument by taking
x=0
and
x=1
.

II
.
a>0,b<0,e=0
.

9)

a>0,b<0,c=0,d=e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=0,\beta=b=-k,\gamma\geq 0,\delta\geq 0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
. Then

(x-1)^{k}-C(x+1)^{\gamma}(x-i)^{\delta}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}.

First
x=0\Rightarrow C=\pm 1,\pm i
.
Note that
\gamma\neq 0,x=-1\Rightarrow\ord_{2}(D)=k
;
\delta\neq 0,x=i\Rightarrow\ord_{2}(D)=k/2
;
{\epsilon}\neq 0,x=-i\Rightarrow\ord_{2}(D)=k/2
.
So we have four cases to consider.

i)
\delta={\epsilon}=0.
First
x=0\Rightarrow C=(-1)^{k}
. Rewriting it as
(1-x)^{k}=(1+x)^{\gamma}+(-1)^{k}Dx^{a}

we see that
(-1)^{k}D<0
and
a=1
since
the linear term is
-kx
on the LHS. Moreover, if
k>2
then there are at least two negative terms on the left
which is impossible. If
k=1
then
\gamma\leq 1
while if
k=2
then
\gamma=2
.

•

If
k=1
,
\gamma=0
then
D=1\Rightarrow\boxed{f=\frac{x}{x-1}}.

•

If
k=1
,
\gamma=1
then
D=2\Rightarrow\boxed{f=\frac{2x}{x-1}}.

•

If
k=2
then
\gamma+D=-2\Rightarrow D=-4\Rightarrow\boxed{f=\frac{-4x}{(x-1)^{2}}}.

ii)
\delta=0,{\epsilon}\geq 1,\gamma=0
.

•

k\geq a+1\geq 2
. Then
k={\epsilon}
and
C=1
. If
a<k-1
then by 2HD
k-{\epsilon}i=0
which is absurd. So
a=k-1
and
k-li=D
.
Since
k\geq 2
then 3HD
\binom{k}{2}-\binom{k}{2}i^{2}=0
which is impossible.

•

k=a
. If
a={\epsilon}+1\geq 2
then
D=1
and by 2HD
k=C\Rightarrow k=1
which is absurd.
a\geq{\epsilon}+2\geq 3

then
D=1
and by 2HD
k=0
, again impossible. So we must have
a={\epsilon}
and by 2HD
k=Cki\Rightarrow C=-i,D=1+i.

Note that
k\geq 2
is impossible since otherwise by 3HD we would get
\binom{k}{2}=-i\binom{k}{2}i^{2}
. Hence
\boxed{f=\frac{(1+i)x}{x-1}}.

iii)
\delta\geq 1,{\epsilon}=0,\gamma=0
. Taking complex conjugation of the coefficients in case ii) above we get
\boxed{f=\frac{(1-i)x}{x-1}}.

iv)
\delta\geq 1,{\epsilon}\geq 1,\gamma=0
. If
\delta+{\epsilon}>k
then
a=k
and
C=-D
which contradicts to
\ord_{2}(D)=k/2
. Thus
\delta+{\epsilon}=k
.

•

If
a=k
then
1-C=D
and by 2HD
k+C(\delta-{\epsilon})i=0
which is impossible for
C=\pm 1,\pm i
.

•

If
a=k-1
then
C=1
. If
a\geq 2
then from the constant term and the linear term we say that

1=(-i)^{\delta}i^{\epsilon}
and
k=\delta(-i)^{\delta-1}i^{\epsilon}+{\epsilon}(-i)^{\delta}i^{{\epsilon}-1}=(\delta-{\epsilon})i
which is absurd. So
a=1,k=2,\delta={\epsilon}=1\Rightarrow\boxed{f=\frac{-2x}{(x-1)^{2}}}
.

•

If
a<k-1
then
C=1
and
k+(\delta-{\epsilon})i=0
by 2HD, which is absurd.

10)

a>0,b<0,c=0,d>0,e=0\Rightarrow\alpha=0,\beta=b=-k,\gamma\geq 0,\delta=0,{\epsilon}\geq 0
. Then

(x-1)^{k}-C(x+1)^{\gamma}(x+i)^{\epsilon}=Dx^{a}(x-i)^{d}.

First
x=0\Rightarrow C=\pm 1,\pm i
;
x=i\Rightarrow k=\gamma+2{\epsilon}
by the 2-adic argument.
Similarly,
\gamma\neq 0,x=-1\Rightarrow\ord_{2}(D)=k-d/2
while
{\epsilon}\neq 0,x=-i\Rightarrow\ord_{2}(D)=k/2-d
.
Thus
\gamma{\epsilon}=0
.

i)
{\epsilon}=0
. Then
k=\gamma
.

•

a+d=k,1-C=D,k+C\gamma=Ddi=(C-1)di
by 2HD. Then
C=-i
is the only possible choice.
So
d=\gamma=k>0
which contradicts
k=a+d
.

•

a+d=k-1\geq 2
then
