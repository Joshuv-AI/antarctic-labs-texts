Bounds on causal effects in 2 K 2^{K} factorial experiments with non-compliance Thanks: Thanks to Zach Branson for helpful comments. Working paper. Comments welcome.
Matthew Blackwell, Nicole E. Pashley

Abstract
Factorial experiments are ubiquitous in the social and biomedical sciences, but when units fail to comply with each assigned factors, identification and estimation of the average treatment effects become impossible without strong assumptions. Leveraging an instrumental variables approach, previous studies have shown how to identify and estimate the causal effect of treatment uptake among respondents who comply with treatment. A major caveat is that these identification results rely on strong assumptions on the effect of randomization on treatment uptake. This paper shows how to bound these complier average treatment effects for bounded outcomes under more mild assumptions on non-compliance.



1
Introduction

Factorial experiments are widespread in the social and biomedical sciences for assessing how different interventions (or different features of an intervention) affect a unit’s response. Unfortunately, in many of these experiments, units fail to comply with one or more of the factors, making it difficult to identify and estimate the actual effect of treatment uptake rather than the effect of treatment randomization. Instrumental variables (IV) has been a common approach to estimating causal effects in randomized experiments with non-compliance
(
Angrist et al., 1996
)
. The key intuition of this approach is that while the average effect of treatment uptake is unidentified, with some additional assumptions we may be able to identify those effects among
compliers
, or those who would always comply with the treatment assignment they receive.

With a binary treatment, these additional assumptions, often called the instrumental variables (IV) assumptions, can be benign or be ensured to hold through the design of the experiment.
In factorial settings, however, point identification of complier average effects has required a treatment exclusion restriction that restricts the assignment of each factor to only affect that factor
(
Blackwell, 2017
;
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
)
.
This assumption rules out plausible interactions or spillovers between factors in the first stage. In this paper, we derive partial identification bounds on complier average factorial effects under weaker assumptions about non-compliance.
We focus on two assumptions: for the factor of interest, we assume (1) the existence of a “least compliant profile” of other factors; and (2) a weak exclusion restriction that allows for spillovers between factors on treatment uptake.
We use these assumption to bound the main effects of factors among units that always comply with assignment on that factor, whom we call the
constant compliers
. These bounds can be informative of the direction of the constant complier main effects.

Several studies have investigated partial identification bounds in the IV context.
Balke and Pearl, 1997
used linear programming to obtain bounds on the overall average treatment effects under the IV assumptions with a binary treatment.
Cheng and Small, 2006
derived bounds on the complier average treatment effect in a three-arm trial under an additional “compliance monotonicity” assumption that we generalize to the “least compliant profile” assumption for the factorial setting.
Song et al., 2024
derive bounds on the joint counterfactual distribution of the outcome when the treatment and outcome are discrete with a finite number of states. Our analysis leverages the factorial structure to generate plausible assumptions and narrower bounds.

2
Setting

Consider an experiment on
n
units with
K
two-level factors, with levels labeled as
-1
and
+1
, and the potential for noncompliance on any of the factors.
There are
J=2^{K}
possible treatment assignments, the number of combinations of the
K
factors, which we label
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{J}\}
where each
\bm{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{K})\in\{-1,1\}^{K}
.
Let
\bm{Z}_{i}
be the treatment assigned to unit
i
.
Further, let
\bm{z}_{-k}\in\{-1,1\}^{K-1}
denote a treatment assignment less the
k
th factor and similarly
\mathcal{Z}_{-k}
be the set of possible assignments on
K-1
factors.

Assumption 1

(Complete randomization)
.

We assume assignment to the treatment combinations is completely at random, fixing the number of units assigned to treatment
\bm{z}
as
N_{\bm{z}}
such that
N_{\bm{z}}\geq 2
for all
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\sum_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}N_{\bm{z}}=N
.

See
Dasgupta et al., 2015
for more on standard factorial designs.
Because of the noncompliance, we define
\bm{D}_{i}(\bm{z})=(D_{i1}(\bm{z}),D_{i2}(\bm{z}),\dots,D_{iK}(\bm{z}))
to be the treatment uptake for unit
i
on each factor 1 through
K
if assigned to treatment
\bm{z}
where each
D_{ik}(\bm{z})\in\{-1,1\}
.

Assuming the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) that there are no hidden forms of treatment and no interference between units, each unit has a potential outcome for each combination of assignment and uptake,
Y_{i}(\bm{z},\bm{d})
.
We assume that
Y_{i}(\bm{z},\bm{d})\in[0,1]
, although extensions of our results to other bounded outcomes is straightforward.

Assumption 2

(Exclusion restriction)
.

For
\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime}
such that
D_{i}(\bm{z})=D_{i}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
,
Y_{i}(\bm{z},D_{i}(\bm{z}))=Y_{i}(\bm{z}^{\prime},D_{i}(\bm{z}^{\prime}))
.

Under Assumption
2
, each unit has only
2^{K}
potential outcomes, one for each possible treatment assignment.
Define the average of potential outcomes under treatment
\bm{z}
as
\overline{Y}(\bm{z})=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\bm{z})
and the average uptake value for factor
k
under treatment
\bm{z}
as
\overline{D}_{k}(\bm{z})=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{ik}(\bm{z})
.

2.1
Compliance types in factorial experiments

A compliance type is a complete description of a unit’s uptake for every possible treatment assignment, or the set
\{\bm{D}_{i}(\bm{z})\mid\mathbb{P}(\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})>0\}
. Without any restrictions on compliance, there are
(2^{K})^{2^{K}}
such types, typically making it difficult to learn anything about the effect of treatment uptake. A common solution to this problem in the binary IV literature is to restrict the compliance types in such a way that we can identify the effect of treatment uptake for some subset of compliance types. One common assumption is monotonicity, which assumes that the effect of treatment assignment on uptake is never negative.
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
generalized this assumption to the factorial setting by having this hold conditionally for each factor. We write
(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})
and
(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
as the
\bm{z}
vector with the
k
th entry as
+1
and
-1
, respectively.

Assumption 3

(Conditional monotonicity)
.

For each
\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})\geq D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
for all
i
.

With conditional monotonicity, we can characterize the compliance types for a particular factor
k
as they vary by treatment assignment on the other factors
\bm{z}_{-k}
as follows. Compliers (c) for
\bm{z}_{-k}
have
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})=+1
and
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})=-1
; always-takers (a) have
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})=D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})=+1
; and never takers have
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})=D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})=-1
.
Of course, a unit’s compliance might vary as a function of assignment to the other factors if those other factors can change compliance behavior.
For example, in a
2^{2}
experiment, there are nine compliance types for factor 1 denoted by their type when assigned to
+1
for factor 2 and
-1
for factor 2: {cc, ca, cn, ac, aa, an, nc, na, nn}.

We focus our attention on units that would comply with treatment assignment on factor
k
no matter the treatment assignment on other factors (those of type cc in the prior example).
We call these units the
constant compliers
for factor
k
and define them as the units with

D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})=2,\quad\forall\;\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}.

Let
C_{ik}=1
if unit
i
is a constant complier for factor
k
.
We define
\rho_{c_{k}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{{ik}}
as the proportion of units that are constant compliers for factor
k
and define the average potential outcome under treatment assignment
\bm{z}
for the constant compliers for factor
k
as

\overline{Y}_{c_{k}}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{\rho_{c_{k}}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{ik}Y_{i}(\bm{z}).

We will group other compliance groups together by whether they ever comply with assignment on factor
k
. For any particular assignment,
\bm{z}_{-k}
, we can partition units into constant compliers, those who comply for
\bm{z}_{-k}
but fail to comply for other assignments, and those who do not comply for
\bm{z}_{-k}
. We call the latter two groups the
conditional compliers
for
\bm{z}_{-k}
and the
conditional noncompliers
for
\bm{z}_{-k}
, and denote their shares in the population as
\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}
and
\rho_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}
, respectively. We also define their average potential outcomes similarly to the constant compliers as
\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z})
and
\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z})
.

Our identification results will focus on the factorial effects for the constant compliers. For example, the main effect of factor
k
would be

\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\sum_{\bm{z}_{{-k}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}}\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k}}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k}}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right].

3
Identification of main effects for constant compliers

What we can learn about the average factorial effects for constant compliers will depend on the assumptions about compliance that we can maintain.
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
assumed that compliance for one factor does not vary with the assignment of other factors and obtained point identification. Given the strength of that assumption, here we consider weaker assumptions that might be applicable in a wide variety of empirical settings.

We begin with a useful decomposition of the intent-to-treat (ITT) effect of factor
k
conditional on assignment on the other factors,
\bm{z}_{-k}
. Recall that the ITT is the effect of assignment on the outcome, ignoring uptake entirely, which we can decompose as

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})

\displaystyle\equiv\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})=\rho_{c_{k}}\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k}}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k}}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right]

(1)

\displaystyle+\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}\left[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right]

\displaystyle+\rho_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}\left[\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right].

We can see that the ITT is a mixture of three effects: (1) the effect for constant compliers, (2) the effect for conditional compliers, and (3) the effect for conditional noncompliers.
It may seem odd to have the effect for conditional noncompliers, but recall that without further assumptions
Z_{ik}
may affect compliance on other factors (
D_{ij}
for
j\neq k
) even when it has no effect on
D_{ik}
.

To bring a more compact notation to this framework, we now define a series of vectors that encode which treatments we will contrast for each factorial effect
(
Dasgupta et al., 2015
;
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
)
. We begin by defining
\bm{Y}=(\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{1}),\dots,\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{J}))^{T}
, which is the vector of average potential outcomes for different treatment assignments. We then defined a series of
J
-length vectors,
\bm{g}_{j}
, each of which has one half of its entries as
+1
and the other half as
-1
to select the appropriate entries of
\bm{Y}
when we use the product
\bm{g}^{T}_{j}\bm{Y}
. We index these contrast vectors by the interaction level, so that the first
K
vectors,
\bm{g}_{1},\ldots,\bm{g}_{K}
, correspond to the main factorial effects of the
K
factors, the next
\binom{K}{2}
vectors correspond to the two-ways interactive effects, and so on. As one example,
\bm{g}_{k}
where
k\leq K
encodes the
k
th main effect so any treatment assignment vector
(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})
has a corresponding
+1
entry in
\bm{g}_{k}
and any assignment vector
(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
has a corresponding
-1
entry in
\bm{g}_{k}
.

With this notation in hand, we can write the main factorial ITT for factor
k
as

\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}}\gamma_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})=\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\bm{g}_{k}^{T}\bm{Y}.

Plugging this into Equation (
1
), we can write the constant complier average factorial effects as

\displaystyle\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{K-1}\rho_{c_{k}}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{k}^{T}\bm{Y}-\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}}\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}\left[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right]

\displaystyle-\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}}\rho_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}\left[\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right]\Bigg].

It is possible to identify the ITT effect on treatment uptake as well. Let
\nu_{k}^{+}(\bm{z}_{-k})=\frac{1}{2}(\overline{D}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})+1)
be the proportion of units receiving
+1
for factor
k
under assignment
(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})
and
\nu_{k}^{-}(\bm{z}_{-k})=\frac{1}{2}(\overline{D}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})+1)
be the proportion of units receiving
+1
for factor
k
under assignment
(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
. Then we can write the ITT for treatment uptake as

\nu_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})=\nu_{k}^{+}(\bm{z}_{-k})-\nu_{k}^{-}(\bm{z}_{-k})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\overline{D}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{D}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right]=\rho_{k}+\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})},

which tells us the proportion of respondents that are either conditional or constant compliers. Without further assumptions, we cannot break apart these two groups, so we introduce an assumption on the compliance behavior.

3.1
Bounds under a least compliant profile

Without further assumptions, the bounds for the constant complier effects will be from
-1
to
1
because there is insufficient information about the size of the constant complier group.
To put structure on this problem, we first assume there is a profile of the other factors that is the worst in terms of compliance for factor
k
for all units.

Assumption 4

(Least compliant profile for factor
k
)
.

There is a
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}
, such that
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\geq D_{ik}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-D_{ik}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
for all other
\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}
and for all
i
.

Assumption
4
implies that there is a “most difficult” profile of the other factors,
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k}
, in terms of compliance. In other words, if a unit complies with assignment on factor
k
under
Z_{i,-k}=\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k}
, then we know it would comply with all other possible assignments.
This implies that we can identify the proportion of constant compliers as

\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\overline{D}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{D}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\right]=\rho_{k}.

Then we have
\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-k})}=\nu_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})-\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})
and
\rho_{cn(\bm{z}_{-k})}=1-\nu_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})
. In Supplemental Materials C, we discuss this and other assumptions in the context of a data example.

Our bounding strategy will rely on average effects for conditional compliers being bounded between
-1
and
1
. Furthermore, noncompliers actually provide additional information because we can observe certain average outcomes for those groups. For instance, suppose that a unit is an always-taker for factor
k
with assignment
\bm{z}_{-k}
, so that
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})=D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})=+1
. Under conditional monotonicity, we can distinguish one set of always-takers: those that take treatment when assigned control,
D_{i}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})=+1
. Let
\overline{Y}_{a(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
and
\overline{Y}_{n(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})
be the average potential outcomes for conditional always- and never-takers, respectively, which we can identify under conditional monotonicity.

.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2

3

4
holds with respect to factor

. Then,
\delta_{k}\in[\widetilde{\delta}_{k}-b^{-}_{k},\widetilde{\delta}_{k}+b^{+}_{k}]\cap[-1,1]

\displaystyle b^{-}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{{K-1}}\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}\Big[\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}}\nu_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})-\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})+\nu_{k}^{-}(\bm{z}_{-k})\Big],

(2)

\displaystyle b^{+}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{{K-1}}\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}\Big[\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}}\nu_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})-\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})+(1-\nu_{k}^{+}(\bm{z}_{-k}))\Big],

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{{K-1}}\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}\Big[\bm{g}_{k}^{T}\bm{Y}-\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}}(1-\nu_{k}^{+}(\bm{z}_{-k}))\overline{Y}_{n(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}}\nu_{k}^{-}(\bm{z}_{-k})\overline{Y}_{a(\bm{z}_{-k})}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})\Big].

Remark 1
.

There is a set of bounds that are larger if more interpretable than those in Proposition
1
. In particular, we can show that

\delta_{k}\in\frac{\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\bm{g}_{k}^{T}\bm{Y}}{\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}\pm\frac{1-\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}{\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}.

The first term in this bound is similar to the Wald estimator in that it is a ratio of two ITT estimators. The second term, which determines the width of the bounds, captures how much information we lose to conditional compliance. The width of the bounds,
2(1-\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k}))/\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})
, will only be informative when constant compliance is very high.

3.2
Bounds under weak treatment exclusion

We now derive bounds for the constant complier effects when we place restrictions on the first stage relationship between treatment assignment and treatment take-up. In particular, we investigate an assumption that a factor’s assignment can only affect overall treatment uptake if it affects uptake on that particular factor.

Assumption 5

(Weak treatment exclusion for factor
k
)
.

For any
\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}
, if
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})=D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
, then
\bm{D}_{i}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})=\bm{D}_{i}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
for all
i
.

Assumption
5
is inspired by a similar assumption in the context of noncompliance and interference in
Imai et al., 2020
and is a weaker version of the treatment exclusion assumption from
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
, which held that
D_{ik}(z_{k},\bm{z}_{-k})=D_{ik}(z_{k},\bm{z}^{\prime}_{-k})
for all
z_{k},\bm{z}_{-k}
,
\bm{z}^{\prime}_{-k}
, and
k
.
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
used this stronger assumption to point identify the effects of various complier average factorial effects. One downside to the stronger treatment exclusion assumption, however, is that it forbids any interactions between factors on the relationship between
\bm{Z}_{i}
and
\bm{D}_{i}
. Assumption
5
allows for a structured interaction, where assignment to factor
k
can only affect uptake on other factors if unit
i
is a complier for
k
.
If all units are constant compliers on all factors except for factor
k
, then Assumption
5
is automatically satisfied.

Remark 2
.

An interesting nuance of the factorial setting is the consequence of increasing the number of factors,
K
.
There are two potential consequences for the identification results.
First, the assumptions, especially
4
and
5
, may be more difficult to justify, because increasing the number of factors could lead to more heterogeneity of behavior across the possible treatments and more complicated relationships among the factors.
Second, as additional factors are added to an experiment, we might expect the proportion of constant compliers for a given factor to decrease.
That is, we expect the proportion of individuals who always comply with factor
k
when varying the levels of
K
factors to be less than or equal to the proportion of individuals who always comply with factor
k
when varying the levels of
K-1
factors.
If this is the case and the average proportion of conditional compliers remains the same or increases, the bounds will increase (or at least not decrease) in width as
K
increases and
\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})
decreases (or remains the same).

Remark 3
.

Consider the case where we believe Assumption
4
holds but we do not know which combination of other factors,
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k}
leads to the “least” compliance.
A seemingly reasonable strategy might be to estimate
\rho_{k}
based on the assignment of other factors with the smallest observed compliance.
That is,
\widehat{\rho}_{k}=\min_{\bm{z}_{-k}}\widehat{\overline{D}}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\widehat{\overline{D}}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
.
This is not unbiased for
\rho_{k}
, but rather will equal in expectation some value
t_{k}\leq\rho_{k}
.
The question is: Will this be conservative, in the sense that the bound resulting from using
t_{k}
will cover the bound using
\rho_{k}
?
As we show in the Supplemental Materials, it is, in fact, conservative (and will be wider than using the true value
\rho_{k}
).
Two further comments on this results are warranted:
1. Although the bounds using
t_{k}
are conservative in the way described, they will be centered on an estimate strictly greater in magnitude due to dividing by a smaller proportion.
2. This conservativeness only holds if Assumption
4
holds. Importantly, this assumption is for every unit in the experiment and thus not directly testable. Arguably, if researchers do not have a substantive reason to believe a particular treatment combination
\bm{z}_{-k}
will yield lower compliance on factor
k
for all units (or if the observed data provides strong evidence against the hypothesized combination), then the assumption in general might be called into question.

4
Identification of interaction effects for constant compliers

Any factorial effect involving factor
k
can be written as a linear combination of the conditional effects
\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
.
Therefore, we can use the same identification strategy as above to estimate the interaction effect among constant compliers for factor
k
, for any interaction involving factor
k
.
In particular, consider estimating some interaction with factor
k
, called
\delta_{f(k)}
which has contrast vector
\bm{g}_{f(k)}

5
Estimation and inference

The bounds in the previous sections are unobserveable because they are functions of the potential outcomes. We can derive estimators for the bounds with a plug-in estimation approach that uses observed means.

\overline{Y}^{\text{obs}}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{N_{\bm{z}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})Y_{i},\qquad\overline{D}_{k}^{\text{obs}}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{N_{\bm{z}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})D_{ik}.

Let
\widehat{\bm{Y}}=(\overline{Y}^{\text{obs}}(\bm{z}_{1}),\ldots,\overline{Y}^{\text{obs}}(\bm{z}_{J}))
and let
\widehat{\nu}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})=\overline{D}_{k}^{\text{obs}}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{+})-\overline{D}_{k}^{\text{obs}}(\bm{z}_{-k},z_{k}^{-})
. Then, a natural estimator for the bounds from Proposition
2
would be

\displaystyle\widehat{\tilde{b}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{K-1}\widehat{\nu}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}\left(\sum_{\bm{z}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-k}}(\widehat{\nu}_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})-\widehat{\nu}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k}))\right),

\displaystyle\qquad\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{K-1}\widehat{\nu}_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})}\bm{g}_{k}^{T}\widehat{\bm{Y}}.

It is straightforward to obtain variance estimators for these quantities using the delta method, based on either the superpopulation or finite-population perspectives
(
Pashley, 2019
;
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
, see, for example)
. With these variance estimators, we can use the usual constructions to obtain confidence intervals for each end of the interval, though we can also obtain confidence intervals for the parameter of interest,
\delta_{k}
, rather than the intervals, as described by
Imbens and Manski, 2004
. This latter procedure involves altering the critical values from the confidence intervals to account for how the true parameter cannot simultaneously be close to the upper and lower bound when they are far apart. Thus, when the bounds are sufficiently far apart, this allows us to construct
1-\alpha
confidence intervals for
\delta_{k}
using
\alpha
-level critical values on both the upper and lower bound, rather than the usual
\alpha/2
values.

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{D}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\left[\overline{D}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{D}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]

\displaystyle=4\rho_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}+4\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}.

Further, from Assumption
SM.6
, we must have
\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})=\rho_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}
.

Next we show the bounds.
We have the conditional intent-to-treat interactive effect between factors
k
and
k^{\prime}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})

\displaystyle\equiv\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\left[\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]

\displaystyle=\rho_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}\Big[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\left[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\rho_{cn(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}\Big[\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\left[\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}_{cn(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]\Big].

Consider a unit
i
who is a conditional noncomplier on either
k
or
k^{\prime}
, conditional on
\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})}
.
Without loss of generality, take unit
i
to be a noncomplier on factor
k
.
Then under Assumptions
2
, and
3
, and
SM.7
,

\displaystyle Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\left[Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]

\displaystyle=0.

This follows because under Assumptions
3
and
SM.7
, for this unit
D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}})=D_{ik}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}})
for
z_{k^{\prime}}\in\{z_{k^{\prime}}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-}\}
.
Then under Assumption
5
, we must have
Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})=0
and
Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-Y_{i}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})=0
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=\rho_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}\Big[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\left[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]\Big].

Rearranging and bounding the conditional complier term gives us

\displaystyle\rho_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\left[\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})-\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}\Big[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{+})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\left[\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{+},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})-\overline{Y}_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})},z_{k}^{-},z_{k^{\prime}}^{-})\right]\Big]

\displaystyle\in\left(\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})-2\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})},\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})+2\rho_{cc(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})-2[\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})-\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})],\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})+2[\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})-\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})]\right).

Aggregating across
\bm{z}_{-k,k^{\prime}}
, note that

\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\bm{g}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}^{T}\bm{Y}=\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\sum_{\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})}}\gamma_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})

and

\frac{1}{2^{K}}\bm{g}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}^{T}\bm{D}_{kk^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2^{K-2}}\sum_{\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})}}\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{-(k,k^{\prime})}).

So then we have the result

\displaystyle\delta_{kk^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\in\Bigg(\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{\rho_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}}\left[\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\bm{g}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}^{T}\bm{Y}-\frac{1}{2^{K}}\bm{g}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}^{T}\bm{D}_{kk^{\prime}}+\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})\right]\right\},

\displaystyle\qquad\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{\rho_{c_{k,k^{\prime}}}}\left[\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\bm{g}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}^{T}\bm{Y}+\frac{1}{2^{K}}\bm{g}_{k\circ k^{\prime}}^{T}\bm{D}_{kk^{\prime}}-\nu_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-(k,k^{\prime})})\right]\right\}\Bigg).

Appendix C
Data illustration

We reanalyze data from
Blattman et al., 2017
, an experiment on the use of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and cash infusions to change behavioral and criminal behaviors among at-risk young men in Liberia, to illustrate our methods.
We focus on the outcome of whether individuals reported selling drugs following the experimental intervention assigned, which was a binary outcome (yes or no).
The experiment was a
2^{2}
factorial design, with factors CBT and cash each having levels receive or do not receive.
Compliance was one-sided in this experiment in that individuals assigned not to receive an intervention (CBT or cash) would not be able to receive that intervention.
There was some noncompliance on both factors with, unsurprisingly, lower compliance on CBT (63% compliance on CBT and 98% compliance on cash).

Assumption
3
is assured due to the one-sided nature of the compliance.
Moreover, because of one-sided noncompliance, there are 5 compliance types for CBT (or cash): {cc, ca, cn, nc, nn}, where the first type refers to CBT (or cash) compliance when assigned to receive cash (or CBT) (complier is c, never taker is n) and the second type is CBT (or cash) compliance when not assigned to receive cash (or CBT).

Consider possible compliance behaviors and how they relates to the least compliant profile for factor
k
assumption (Assumption
4
) and the weak-treatment exclusion restriction assumption (Assumption
5
).
It is plausible that individuals’ compliance with cash is not impacted by their assignment on CBT.
In this case, Assumption
4
automatically holds for cash.
On the other hand, being assigned to receive a cash transfer might change compliance with the CBT assignment.
A unit might be a conditional never-taker for CBT if not assigned the cash transfer, but a conditional complier if they were assigned the cash transfer.
If it’s more generally true that a conditional complier for CBT when not assigned the cash transfer will definitely be a conditional complier for CBT when assigned the cash transfer, then Assumption
4
holds for CBT with no cash transfer being the level giving least compliance.
Under the scenarios laid out, Assumption
5
would hold for CBT but not for cash.
These assumptions must always be assessed based on subject-matter knowledge.

Figure
SM.2
shows the point estimates of the bounds from Proposition
2
in red with the black points and lines showing the point estimates and confidence intervals under the stronger treatment exclusion assumption of
Blackwell and Pashley, 2023
for comparison.
We quickly see that the bounds on both effects do not include 0, though the confidence interval for cash based on the strong assumption does.
The point identified estimate based on the stronger assumption falls within the bounds for each effect.
Under Assumption
5
for CBT but not cash, we would not trust the red bounds (or the stronger point identification) on the cash estimate.
However, it provides a useful illustration here in comparison to CBT: as the compliance rate increases, the bounds and confidence intervals will both become narrower.
Further, because the bounds depend on estimates of
(\nu_{k}(\bm{z}_{-k})-\nu_{k}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{-k})
, the width of the bounds will also depend on how much variability there is in observed compliance across the treatment arms.
