From the simplex to the sphere: Faster constrained optimization using the Hadamard parametrization
Qiuwei LiDaniel McKenzie, Wotao Yin

Abstract
The standard simplex in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , also known as the probability simplex, is the set of nonnegative vectors whose entries sum up to 1. They frequently appear as constraints in optimization problems that arise in machine learning, statistics, data science, operations research, and beyond. We convert the standard simplex to the unit sphere and thus transform the corresponding constrained optimization problem into an optimization problem on a simple, smooth manifold. We show that KKT points and strict-saddle points of the minimization problem on the standard simplex all correspond to those of the transformed problem, and vice versa. So, solving one problem is equivalent to solving the other problem. Then, we propose several simple, efficient, and projection-free algorithms using the manifold structure. The equivalence and the proposed algorithm can be extended to optimization problems with unit simplex, weighted probability simplex, or ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm sphere constraints. Numerical experiments between the new algorithms and existing ones show the advantages of the new approach. Open source code is available at here .

Riemannian Optimization

We recall a few notions required for discussing optimization on Riemannian manifolds, specialized to the case of the sphere,
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
. For any
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
the
tangent space
is
T_{z}\mathcal{S}_{n-1}=\{v\in\bbR^{n}:\ v^{\top}z=0\}
. Let
\operatorname{Proj}_{z}
denote the projection onto

. One can verify that
\operatorname{Proj}_{z}(w)=w-(w^{\top}z)z
. For any smooth

the

at

is the projection of the regular gradient onto
T_{z}\mathcal{S}_{n-1}

\operatorname{grad}_{z}g=\operatorname{Proj}_{z}\nabla g(z)
. Similarly, we may define the
Riemannian Hessian
at

as the operator
\operatorname{Hess}g(z)=\operatorname{Proj}_{z}\circ\left(\nabla^{2}g(z)-\nabla g(z)^{\top}z\right)\circ\operatorname{Proj}_{z}
; see
(
Boumal, 2020
, Sec. 7)
for further details.

Given
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
and
v\in T_{z}\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
with
\|v\|=1
, there exists a unique
geodesic
emanating from
z
in the direction of
v
. Geogescis generalize the role of the straight lines in Euclidean geometry. On
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
the geodesics are also known as the great circles.

Define
\gamma_{z,v}(t):\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
as the geodesic mapping at
z
with direction
v\in T_{z}\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
and the
exponential map
at
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{exp}_{z}:

\displaystyle T_{z}\mathcal{S}_{n-1}\to\mathcal{S}_{n-1}

\displaystyle v\mapsto\gamma_{z,\hat{v}}(\|v\|)\text{ where }\hat{v}:=v\big/\|v\|

Riemannian Gradient Descent or RGD mimics regular (
i.e.
Euclidean) gradient descent, except instead of using
\nabla g(z_{k})
we use
\operatorname{grad}g(z_{k})
, and instead of stepping along the straight line in the direction
-\nabla g(z_{k})
, we move along the geodesic in the direction
-\operatorname{grad}g(z_{k})
:

z_{k+1}=\operatorname{exp}_{z_{k}}(-\alpha_{k}\operatorname{grad}g(z_{k}))

(6)

By construction,
z_{k+1}\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
, so RGD is a
feasible algorithm
. Algorithm
1
, which we dub HadRGD, describes how to apply RGD to (

P

⁢
1
a

) via the Hadamard parametrization. The converted objective

inherits the smoothness of
f
:

Lemma 3.1
.

If

is

is
\tilde{L}
-Lipschitz differentiable with
\tilde{L}=4L+2M
where

.

See Appendix

for the proof. As
\nabla f(x)
is continuous and
\Delta_{n}
is compact,

.

We consider again the underdetermined simplex-constrained least squares problem (

). We focus on this test problem as it is a key component of algorithms for applications as diverse as portfolio optimization
(

)
, archetypal analysis
(

is sampled uniformly at random from the interior of

10
Conclusion

In this paper we presented a new framework for transforming a non-smooth constraint set to the unit sphere or ball. We showed that the landscape of the transformed problem is benign if the landscape of the original problem is. Using techniques from Riemannian optimization, we constructed a new algorithm guaranteed to converge to second-order KKT points of the original problem, and proved a rate of convergence for a certain special case. We explored augmenting our new algorithm with various line-search sub-routines. Empirically, we showed that our algorithm could be significantly faster than existing algorithms, particularly when the solution to the original problem lies in the interior of the simplex. Future work could combine our Hadamard reparametrization trick with proximal or sub-gradient methods for non-smooth
f
, or SGD-style methods for finite sum objectives:
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)
.

Second-order KKT conditions of Problem (

First of all, let us compute the Hessian of

-\operatorname*{diag}(z_{v}^{\star})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

The second-order KKT conditions is given by

2\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname*{diag}(\nabla f(\tilde{x}))-\lambda_{N}^{\star}I_{n}&\\

Denote

is any first-order KKT point of (

is the Lagrangian dual variable in (

1.

Suppose that

is a second-order KKT point of (

). Take any
(z_{u}^{\star},z_{v}^{\star})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\star}

(z_{u}^{\star},z_{v}^{\star})\in\sZ^{\star}

)–(

) for Problem (

).
First of all, since
(z_{u}^{\star},z_{v}^{\star})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\star}
, we have
\tilde{x}=x^{\star}
. Then the first-order KKT conditions (

)–(
83e
) directly follow from (
81b
)–(
81d
) by choosing
\lambda_{N}^{\star}=\lambda^{\star}
.
It suffices to show the first-order KKT conditions (
83a
)–(
83b
) with
\lambda_{N}^{\star}=\lambda^{\star}
.
Rewriting (

) we obtain:

\displaystyle\nabla[f(x^{\star})]_{i}=\lambda^{\star}

\displaystyle\text{ if }[x^{\star}]_{i}>0

\displaystyle\nabla[f(x^{\star})]_{i}=-\lambda^{\star}

\displaystyle\text{ if }[x^{\star}]_{i}<0

\displaystyle\nabla[f(x^{\star})]_{i}\in[\lambda^{\star}-\lambda^{\star}]

\displaystyle\text{ if }[x^{\star}]_{i}=0

Case 1:

\lambda^{\star}=0
. Then we have
\nabla f(x^{\star})=0
,

\nabla f(\tilde{x})=0

\lambda_{N}^{\star}=\lambda^{\star}=0

Case 2:

\lambda^{\star}\neq 0

Therefore,

[x^{\star}]_{i}=[\tilde{x}]_{i}=\begin{cases}{[z_{u}^{\star}]_{i}}^{2}&\text{if }[z_{u}^{\star}]_{i}\neq 0\\
-{[z_{v}^{\star}]_{i}}^{2}&\text{if }[z_{v}^{\star}]_{i}\neq 0\end{cases}

which further indicates that for all

\displaystyle\nabla[f(\tilde{x})]_{i}=\lambda^{\star}

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }[\tilde{x}]_{i}=[x^{\star}]_{i}>0\iff[z_{u}^{\star}]_{i}\neq 0,[z_{v}^{\star}]_{i}=0

\displaystyle\nabla[f(\tilde{x})]_{i}=-\lambda^{\star}

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }[\tilde{x}]_{i}=[x^{\star}]_{i}<0\iff[z_{v}^{\star}]_{i}\neq 0,[z_{u}^{\star}]_{i}=0

\nabla f(\tilde{x})

z_{u}^{\star}
and
z_{v}^{\star}
respectively, and combining the above three, we then get the KKT conditions (

) and (

), respectively.

satisfies the second-order optimality condition (

) with

Using this, to show the second-order optimality condition (

2\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname*{diag}(\nabla f(\tilde{x}))-\lambda_{N}^{\star}I_{n}&\\
&-\operatorname*{diag}(\nabla f(\tilde{x}))-\lambda_{N}^{\star}I_{n}\end{bmatrix}+4\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname*{diag}(z_{u}^{\star})\\

\displaystyle 4\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname*{diag}(z_{u}^{\star})\\
-\operatorname*{diag}(z_{v}^{\star})\end{bmatrix}\nabla^{2}f(\tilde{x})\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname*{diag}(z_{u}^{\star})\\
-\operatorname*{diag}(z_{v}^{\star})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\succeq 0

(87)

which follows from the convexity assumption of

(z_{u}^{\star},z_{v}^{\star})

). First of all, the combination of (

, we must have

\tilde{x}\neq x^{\star}

This is because, otherwise, we can choose

to certify the global optimality of
\tilde{x})
in Problem (

4

),
i.e.

)–(

) by choosing

. This means that the suboptimality condition (

Also in view of (

Our large-scale experiments (results presented in Figures
