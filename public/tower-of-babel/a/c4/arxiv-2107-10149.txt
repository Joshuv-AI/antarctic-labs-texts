A Note On the Global Dimension of Shifted Orders
Özgür Esentepe

Abstract
. We consider the dominant dimension of an order over a Cohen-Macaulay ring in the category of centrally Cohen-Macaulay modules. There is a canonical tilting module in the case of positive dominant dimension and we give an upper bound on the global dimension of its endomorphism ring.



1.  Introduction

In a recent article “
Special tilting modules for algebras with positive dominant dimension
”
[
PS20
]
, Matthew Pressland and Julia Sauter study what they advertise in their title. They call such tilting modules
shifted modules
and they study their
shifted algebras
. One particular theorem they prove is the following.

Theorem

(
[
PS20
]
, Proposition 2.13)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite dimensional algebra with dominant dimension
d
and let
0\leq k\leq d
. Then, for the
k
-shifted algebra
B_{k}
of
\Gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{gldim}B_{k}\leq\mathrm{gldim}\Gamma.

The aim of this note is to show that this theorem holds true in the setting of Cohen-Macaulay representation theory. In our setting, we replace finite dimensional algebras over a field with orders over a Cohen-Macaulay local ring with canonical module. Therefore, we replace the module category of an algebra with the category of Cohen-Macaulay modules over an order. We study the dominant dimension in this category. This idea appears also in
[
AR72
]
where the authors characterize orders of finite lattice type.

We spend Section 2 to introduce the necessary background and notation in Cohen-Macaulay representation theory while we emphasize the similarities between the theory of finite dimensional algebras and the theory of orders over Cohen-Macaulay local rings.

In Section 3, we introduce the notion of Cohen-Macaulay dominant dimension. This is a notion which proves useful only in the noncommutative setting as the dominant dimension of a commutative Gorenstein local ring is infinite while it is zero when we have a Cohen-Macaulay local ring which is not Gorenstein. We show computations for one noncommutative example and discuss the Cohen-Macaulay Nakayama conjecture.

When an order over a Cohen-Macaulay local ring has positive Cohen-Macaulay dominany dimension, there is a special family of tilting modules. We call their endomorphism rings
shifted orders
. While in general derived equivalence preserves finiteness of global dimension, the exact value of the global dimension is not preserved. We prove that the global dimension of the shifted orders can not be greater than the original order.

Theorem
.

Suppose that
\Lambda
is an
n
-canonical QF-3 order of finite global dimension over a Cohen-Macaulay local ring
R
with canonical module. Then, the
\ell^{\rm{th}}
-shifted order
\Gamma_{\ell}
has global dimension

\displaystyle\mathrm{gldim}\Gamma_{\ell}\leq\mathrm{gldim}\Lambda.

2.  Background and Motivation

The aim is that this short note is as self-contained as possible. We will start with the definitions from commutative world and then discuss how it generalizes to the noncommutative landscape.

2.1
.

Setting, conventions and notation.

Throughout this paper, we denote by
R
a commutative Noetherian local ring
(R,\mathfrak{m})
of Krull dimension
d
and by
\Lambda
a module-finite
R
-algebra with Jacobson radical
J
. We always consider right
\Lambda
-modules and we treat
\Lambda^{op}
-modules as left
\Lambda
-modules. Unless otherwise stated all modules are finitely generated.

2.2
.

Commutative algebra.
Let
M
be an
R
-module. We say that a ring element
r\in R
is a non-zerodivisor on
M
if
rm\neq 0
for any nonzero
m\in M
. A regular sequence on
M
is a sequence
r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}
of elements in
\mathfrak{m}
such that
r_{1}
is a non-zerodivisor on
M
and
r_{j}
is a non-zerodivisor on
M/(r_{1},\ldots,r_{j-1})M
for
1<j\leq\ell
. The maximal length of a regular sequence on
M
is an important invariant and it is called the depth of
M
. There is also a homological characterisation of depth:

\displaystyle 0pt_{R}M=\inf\left\{i\colon\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{i}(R/\mathfrak{m},M)\neq 0\right\}.

Another important invariant of
M
is the dimension of
M
which is defined as the Krull dimension of the quotient ring
R/\mathrm{ann}_{R}M
. These two invariants satisfy the following inequalities for any nonzero
M
:
0pt_{R}M\leq\dim_{R}M\leq d.
We say that
M
is a
maximal Cohen-Macaulay
module if there is equality. We also assume that the zero module is maximal Cohen-Macaulay and denote the category of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules by
\mathrm{MCM}(R)
.

If
M
has finite projective dimension, then the depth and the projective dimension of
M
add up to
d
. This is the
Auslander-Buchsbaum formula.
As an immediate consequence, we see that projective modules are maximal Cohen-Macaulay and non-projective maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules have infinite projective dimension. Therefore, if
R
has finite global dimension (for instance when it is the coordinate ring of a smooth irreducible variety) we see that every maximal Cohen-Macaulay module is projective. The converse is also true.

We say that
R
is a
Cohen-Macaulay
ring if the regular module
R
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay. For a Cohen-Macaulay ring
R
, we denote by
\omega_{R}
an
R
-module which has finite injective dimension, is maximal Cohen-Macaulay and satisfies
\dim\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{d}(k,\omega_{R})=1
where
k=R/\mathfrak{m}
is the residue field. This module, if exists, is unique up to isomorphism and we call it the
canonical module
of
R
. In this paper, we will always be interested in Cohen-Macaulay base rings with canonical module. Existence of a canonical module is not a big ask. For example, quotients of power series rings have canonical modules.

The following conditions are equivalent for a Cohen-Macaulay local ring
R
with canonical module
\omega_{R}
. (1)
\omega_{R}
is free of rank one, (2)
R
has finite injective dimension as a module over itself, (3)
R
has injective dimension
d
and finally (4)
\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{n}(k,R)=0
for
n\neq 0,d
and
\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{d}(k,R)\cong k
. In this case we say that
R
is a
Gorenstein
ring.

Over a Gorenstein ring, a module
M
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{i}(M,R)=0
for all
i>0
.

2.3
.

Noncommutative algebra.
In the noncommutative landscape there are different notions of Gorenstein rings and maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules. We start with Iwanaga-Gorenstein rings. Let
\Lambda
be as in Setting
2.1
.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that
\Lambda
is an
Iwanaga-Gorenstein
ring if it has finite injective dimension as a module over itself and as a module over its opposite ring. In this case, a finitely generated
\Lambda
-module
M
is called
maximal Cohen-Macaulay
if
\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(M,\Lambda)=0
for all
i>0
.

Remark 2.5
.

In different contexts, these modules are also called Gorenstein-projective, Gorenstein dimension zero or totally reflexive. This definition of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules follow Buchweitz’s soon-to-be published famous manuscript
[
Buc
]
where the triangle equivalence between the stable category of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules and the singularity category was proved. It also follows Iyama’s 2018 ICM plenary talk
[
Iya18
]
in Rio on tilting Cohen-Macaulay representations. However, this is not the definition we will follow in this paper.

Remark 2.6
.

If
R
is a field and
\Lambda
is a self-injective
R
-algebra, then we see that every module is maximal Cohen-Macaulay.

We will now give another approach to maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules over noncommutative rings. Again, assume that
\Lambda
is as in Setting
2.1
and let
M
be a
\Lambda
-module. A theorem from 2002 due to Shiro Goto and Kenji Nishida
[
GN02
, Corollary 3.2]
says that the depth of
M
as an
R
-module does not depend on the base ring. That is, if
S
is another commutative Noetherian ring over which
\Lambda
is a module-finite algebra, then
0pt_{R}M=0pt_{S}M
. Indeed, one has the following equality:

\displaystyle 0pt_{R}M=\inf\left\{i\colon\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(\Lambda/J,M)\neq 0\right\}

where the right hand side does not depend on
R
. Thus, when we talk about the depth of a
\Lambda
-module, we can drop the subscript
R
. According to
[
IR08
, Section 2]
, the dimension of
M
is also independent of the base ring. Therefore, it makes sense to consider the following definition.

Definition 2.7
.

A
\Lambda
-module is said to be
maximal Cohen-Macaulay
if it is maximal Cohen-Macaulay as an
R
-module. We say that
\Lambda
is an
R
-order
if it is maximal Cohen-Macaulay as a module over itself.

Remark 2.8
.

According to this definition, if
R
is a field, orders are precisely finite dimensional algebras. And the category of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules over an order coincides with the module category: every module is maximal Cohen-Macaulay.

This is the definition of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules we will consider in this paper. We will denote the category of maximal Cohen-Macaulay
\Lambda
-modules by
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
.

2.9
.

When do the two definitions agree?
Remarks
2.6
and
2.8
tell us that if the base ring is a field, the two definitions of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules agree when the algebra is self-injective. When the base ring is a Cohen-Macaulay local ring with canonical module
\omega_{R}
, we have a similar phenomenon.

Definition 2.10
.

We call the
\Lambda
-bimodule
\omega=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(\Lambda,\omega_{R})
the
canonical (bi)module
of
\Lambda
. We say that
\Lambda
is a
Gorenstein order
if
\omega
is a projective
\Lambda
-module.

Note that if
\Lambda=R
, then
R
is Gorenstein if and only if
R
is a Gorenstein order. So, this is another generalization of Gorenstein rings to the noncommutative world.

If
\Lambda
is a Gorenstein
R
-order, then the two definitions of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules agree. Moreover, for a Gorenstein order, the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula holds: for every
\Lambda
-module
M
of finite projective dimension, the depth of
M
and the projective dimension of
M
adds up to the Krull dimension of
R
.

In general, when
\Lambda
is an
R
-order, the canonical bimodule plays an important role in
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
: for every
X\in\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
, we have
\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{>0}(X,\omega)=0
. This means that
\omega
is an injective object in
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
. Moreover,
\omega
is in fact an additive generator for the subcategory of injective objects in
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
.

The functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(-,\omega)
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-,\omega_{R})
on
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
by hom-tensor adjunction and it is an exact duality from
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
to
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda^{\rm op})
. Given a
\Lambda
-module
M
, consider the
\Lambda^{\rm op}
-module
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(M,\omega)
and take a projective resolution of it as a
\Lambda^{\rm op}
. Then, dualizing it again with the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-,\omega_{R})
gives that every maximal Cohen-Macaulay
\Lambda
-module has an injective resolution in
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
. This gives a well-defined definition of
\mathrm{CM}
-injective dimension.

Definition 2.11
.

We say that
\Lambda
is an
n
-
canonical order
if the canonical module
\omega
has projective dimension
n
as a
\Lambda^{\rm op}
-module (equivalently, as a
\Lambda
-module) or equivalently, the regular module
\Lambda
has CM-injective dimension equal to
n
.

If
\Lambda
is an
n
-canonical order, then the injective dimension of
\Lambda
is equal to
d+n

[
GN02
, Proposition 1.1(3)]
. In particular, if
\Lambda
has finite global dimension, then
\mathrm{gldim}\Lambda=d+n
. So, the CM-injective dimension of
\Lambda
measures the global dimension, in a sense. When
\Lambda
is a Gorenstein order, the injective dimension is equal to
d
.

3.  Dominant Dimension

We have seen that Gorenstein orders are in some sense generalizations of self-injective algebras. Self-injective algebras are ubiquitous. To name a few examples, let us start with a finite group
G
and consider the group algebra
kG
whose module category is equivalent to the category of
k
-representations of
G
. Other examples include cohomology rings of closed orientable manifolds and Artinian coordinate rings
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
given by regular sequences.

Dominant dimension is a measure of how far away an algebra is from being a self-injective algebra. The pioneers of the theory include Nakayama, Tachikawa, Müller and others. The dominant dimension of an algebra
A
is the minimal number
\ell
in a minimal injective resolution

\displaystyle 0\to A\to I^{0}\to\ldots\to I^{\ell}\to\ldots

such that
I^{\ell}
is not projective. If such a number does not exist, we say that the dominant dimension is infinite. For self-injective algebras, the dominant dimension is infinite. The converse of this statement is the famous Nakayama conjecture: It is still an open problem whether infinite dominant dimension implies that the algebra
A
is self-injective.

In this paper, motivated by the previous section, we will consider the dominant dimension in the category of Cohen-Macaulay modules.

3.1
.

Setting.
Throughout this section, we assume that
R
is a Cohen-Macaulay local ring of Krull dimension
d
with canonical module and
\Lambda
is an
R
-order. When we say
\Lambda
is an
n
-canonical order, we will tacitly assume that
0\neq n<\infty
. By
\Pi
, we denote a
\Lambda
-module such that
\mathrm{add}\Pi=\mathrm{add}\Lambda\cap\mathrm{add}\omega
. That is, we let
\Pi
be an additive generator for the subcategory of CM-projective-injective
\Lambda
-modules. We will also assume for convenience that
\Lambda
is semiperfect and therefore minimal resolutions exist. This can be guaranteed, for instance, by assuming that
R
is complete.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that
\Lambda
has
CM-dominant dimension

\ell
if
\ell
is the smallest number in a minimal CM-injective coresolution

\displaystyle 0\to\Lambda\to I^{0}\to\ldots\to I^{\ell}\to\ldots

such that
I^{\ell}
is not projective (or
\infty
if such
\ell
does not exist). We denote the CM-dominant dimension of
\Lambda
by
\mathrm{CMdomdim}(\Lambda)
.

Note that this definition only proves useful in the noncommutative setting. Indeed, in the commutative case, we have
\mathrm{pd}_{R}\omega_{R}=0
if and only if
R
is Gorenstein. In this case, the Cohen-Macaulay dominant dimension is
\infty
. Otherwise, that is if
R
is a non-Gorenstein Cohen-Macaulay local ring, then
\mathrm{pd}_{R}\omega_{R}=\infty
as a consequence of the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula and the CM-dominant dimension is zero.

It is immediate to see that Gorenstein orders have infinite Cohen-Macaulay dominant dimension. We shall now consider a nontrivial noncommutative example.

3.3
.

A nontrivial example.

Let
k
be an infinite field and consider
R=k[[x,y,z,u,v]]/I
where
I
is the ideal generated by the
2\times 2
minors of the generic matrix

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x&y&u\\
y&z&v\end{bmatrix}.

This ring is an example of a scroll. Scrolls have nice properties and geometric interpretations for which refer to
[
EH87
]
. In particular, our ring
R
is an integrally closed Cohen-Macaulay normal domain of Krull dimension 3 and it is a toric isolated singularity. If we consider the
\mathbb{Z}
-graded algebra

\displaystyle S=k[[X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}]]=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}S_{i}

with
\deg X_{1}=2,\deg X_{2}=1
and
\deg X_{3}=\deg X_{4}=-1
, then we can identify
R
with
S_{0}
.

As an application of the theory of almost split sequences, Auslander and Reiten proved in
[
AR89
, Theorem 2.1]
that
R
has finite Cohen-Macaulay type: it has only finitely many indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules up to isomorphism. Using the notation from
[
Yos90
, Proposition 16.12]
and the above identification, the indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules are
S_{1},S_{0}\cong R,S_{-1}\cong\omega_{R},S_{-2}
and a rank two module
M
. Let us set notation: We put
X=R\oplus\omega
and
\Lambda=\mathrm{End}_{R}(X)
. Then,
\Lambda
is an
R
-order. We put

\displaystyle D=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-,\omega_{R})

\displaystyle:\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)\to\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda^{\mathrm{op}})

\displaystyle F=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(X,-)

\displaystyle:\mathrm{mod}R\to\mathrm{mod}\Lambda

\displaystyle G=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-,X)

\displaystyle:\mathrm{mod}R\to\mathrm{mod}\Lambda^{\mathrm{op}}.

Note that
F
restricts to an equivalence
\mathrm{add}X\cong\mathrm{proj}\Lambda
and
G
restricts to an equivalence
\mathrm{add}X\cong\mathrm{proj}\Lambda^{\mathrm{op}}
. We are now ready to start. The first thing we do is to understand CM-injective
\Lambda
-modules. To do so, we compute
\omega
.

\displaystyle\omega=D\Lambda=D\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(X,X)=DG(X)=DG(R)\oplus DG(\omega_{R}).

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle DG(R)=D\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(R,X)=DX=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(X,\omega_{R})=F(\omega_{R})

and

\displaystyle DG(\omega_{R})=D\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(\omega,X)

\displaystyle=D\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(S_{-1},S_{0}\oplus S_{-1})

\displaystyle=D(S_{1}\oplus R)=S_{2}\oplus S_{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(S_{0}\oplus S_{-1},S_{-2})=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(X,S_{-2})=F(S_{-2}).

Therefore, we conclude that
\omega=F(\omega_{R}\oplus S_{-2})
. In other words, the only indecomposable CM-injective
\Lambda
-modules are
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(X,\omega_{R})
and
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(X,S_{-2})
up to isomorphism as
F
is fully faithful.

Let us now compute a minimal CM-injective resolution of
\Lambda
. We will start with a projective resolution of
\omega
. We have a short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to R\to\omega_{R}^{\oplus 2}\to S_{-2}\to 0.

If we apply
F
to this exact sequence, we get

\displaystyle 0\to FR\to F\omega_{R}^{\oplus 2}\to FS_{-2}\to\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{1}(X,R)\cong 0.

Since
F\omega_{R}
is CM-injective, this short exact sequence yields

\displaystyle 0\to FR\to F\omega_{R}^{\oplus 3}\to F\omega_{R}\oplus F{S_{-2}}\cong\omega\to 0

which is a minimal projective resolution of the
\Lambda
-module
\omega
. Now, applying
D
to this resolution, we get the following CM-injective resolution of
\Lambda
in
\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda^{op})
:

\displaystyle 0\to\Lambda\to DF\omega_{R}^{\oplus 3}\to DFR\to 0.

We have seen that the only indecomposable CM-injective
\Lambda
-modules are
F\omega_{R}
and
FS_{-2}
. Therefore,
FR
is not a CM-injective
\Lambda
-module and
DFR
is not a projective
\Lambda^{op}
-module which shows us that the CM-dominant dimension of
\Lambda
is
1
.

3.4
.

Cohen-Macaulay Nakayama conjecture.
We have said that the dominant dimension of a finite dimensional algebra measures how far away it is from being a self-injective algebra: If the algebra is self-injective, then the dominant dimension is infinite. We have mentioned that the converse of this statement is the Nakayama conjecture. It is easy to see from our definition of Cohen-Macaulay dominant dimension, Gorenstein orders have infinite CM-dominant dimension. Indeed, the canonical module of a Gorenstein order is projective and projective modules and CM-injective modules coincide. The Cohen-Macaulay Nakayama conjecture states, then, that if an order over a Cohen-Macaulay local ring has infinite dominant dimension, then it must be a Gorenstein order. The following proposotion shows that if one can find a counterexample to the Cohen-Macaulay Nakayama conjecture, then it gives a counterexample to the original Nakayama conjecture.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be as in Setting
3.1
and
x
be a central nonzerodivisor on
\Lambda
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{CMdomdim}(\Lambda)=\mathrm{CMdomdim}(\Lambda/x\Lambda).

Proof.

It is standard that if the depth of
\Lambda
is
d
as an
R
-module, then the depth of
\Lambda/x\Lambda
as an
R/xR
-module is
d-1
. That is,
\Lambda/x\Lambda
is an
R/xR
-order and our definitions make sense.

We know that if
P
is a projective
\Lambda
-module, then
P/xP
is a projective
\Lambda/x\Lambda
-module. We also have that the canonical module of
R/xR
is isomorphic to
\omega_{R}/x\omega_{R}
. From here, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\omega_{\Lambda/x\Lambda}=\mathrm{Hom}_{R/xR}(\Lambda/x\Lambda,\omega_{R/xR})\cong\omega_{\Lambda}/x\omega_{\Lambda}.

Now, we can complete the proof by taking a projective resolution of
\omega_{\Lambda}
as a
\Lambda^{\rm{op}}
-module and tensoring it with
\Lambda/x\Lambda
since by doing so still gives us an exact seqeunce and we have already observed that CM-projective-injective
\Lambda
-modules go to CM-projective-injective
\Lambda/x\Lambda
-modules.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be as in Setting
3.1
and let
\mathbf{x}
be a regular sequence of length
d
on
\Lambda
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{CMdomdim}(\Lambda)=\rm{domdim}(\Lambda/\mathbf{x}\Lambda).

Hence a counterexample to the Cohen-Macaulay Nakayama conjecture would imply a counterexample to the original Nakayama conjecture.

Remark 3.7
.

It is well-known in the representation theory of finite dimensional algebras that the Nakayama conjecture holds true for those algebras which have finite finitistic dimension. Similar arguments also can be made for orders over Cohen-Macaulay local rings. When
\Lambda
is an
n
-canonical order over a Cohen-Macaulay local ring of Krull dimension
d
, there is an inequality version of Auslander-Buchsbaum formula which was proved by Stangle in their thesis
[
Sta17
]
: for any
\Lambda
-module
X
with finite projective dimension, we have

d\leq\mathrm{pd}_{\Lambda}X+0ptX\leq d+n.

Hence
n
-canonical orders have finite finitistic dimension. Therefore, if one wants to find a counterexample to the Nakayama conjecture, then they need to look at orders over which the canonical module has infinite projective dimension.

4.  Shifted Orders

In this section, we keep assuming Setting
3.1
. We start with an
R
-order
\Lambda
of CM-dominant dimension at least
\ell
so that the minimal CM-injective coresolution of
\Lambda
is of the form

(1)

\displaystyle 0\to\Lambda\to\Pi^{0}\to\Pi^{1}\to\ldots\to\Pi^{\ell-2}\to\Pi^{\ell-1}\to\ldots

with
\Pi^{j}
’s CM-projective-injective for
j=0,1,\ldots,\ell-1
. We say that
\Lambda
is a
QF-3 order
if the CM-dominant dimension of
\Lambda
is at least
1
. The letters QF come from quasi-Frobenius rings and QF-3 rings were introduced by Robert Thrall as a generalization of quasi-Frobenius rings
[
Thr48
]
, see also
[
Tac73
]
.

We denote by
K_{\ell}
the cokernel of
\Pi^{\ell-2}\to\Pi^{\ell-1}
. It is clear by definition that the projective dimension of
K_{\ell}
is at most
\ell
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The
\Lambda
-module
K_{\ell}
is a Cohen-Macaulay
\Lambda
-module.

Proof.

If the CM-injective dimension of
\Lambda
is finite, then let
X
be the last nonzero CM-injective module in the coresolution (
1
). In particular,
X
is a maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module and by applying the depth lemma successively, we see that
K_{\ell}
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay as an
R
-module. In case
\Lambda
has infinite CM-injective dimension, then
K_{\ell}
is the
d
th syzygy of a
\Lambda
-module. Once again, the depth lemma does the trick.
∎

Recall that a
\Lambda
-module
T
is called an
\ell
-
tilting
module if

(1)

the projective dimension of
T
is at most
\ell
,

(2)

there is an exact sequence
0\to\Lambda\to t_{0}\to t_{1}\to\ldots\to t_{\ell}\to 0
with
t_{0},\ldots,t_{\ell}\in\mathrm{add}T
,

(3)

there are no self-extensions of
T
in the sense that
\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{>0}(T,T)=0
.

If
T
is a tilting
\Lambda
-module, then
\Lambda
and
\mathrm{End}_{\Lambda}(T)
are derived equivalent. Hence, this is a derived version of Morita theory. The following lemma is about extensions with CM-projective-injective middle terms.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
0\to A\to B\to C\to 0
be a short exact sequence of maximal Cohen-Macaulay
\Lambda
-modules with
B
CM-projective-injective. Then, for every
i>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(A,A)\cong\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(C,C).

\displaystyle\ldots\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(B,A)\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(A,A)\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i+1}(C,A)\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i+1}(B,A)\to\ldots

The outer terms vanish as

\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(A,A)\cong\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i+1}(C,A).

If we apply

\displaystyle\ldots\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(C,B)\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i}(C,C)\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i+1}(C,A)\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{i+1}(C,B)\to\ldots

Once again, the outer terms vanish as

Combining the two isomorphisms, we get the result.

Proposition 4.4
.

The endomorphism ring
\Gamma_{\ell}=\mathrm{End}_{\Lambda}(T_{\ell})
is again an
R
-order.

Proof.

We need to show that
\mathrm{End}_{\Lambda}(T_{\ell})
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay as an
R
-module. Let us start with the direct sum decomposition
\mathrm{End}_{\Lambda}(T_{\ell})\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Pi,T_{\ell})\oplus\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},T_{\ell})
. We know that
\Pi
is a projective
\Lambda
-module which tells us that
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Pi,T_{\ell})\in\mathrm{add}_{\Lambda}T_{\ell}
. Hence,
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Pi,T_{\ell})
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay since so is
T_{\ell}
. Therefore, it is enough to show that
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},T_{\ell})
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay. We further decompose this module as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},T_{\ell})\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},K_{\ell})\oplus\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},\Pi).

Since
\Pi
is a projective module, we have
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},\Pi)
is a summand in
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},\Lambda)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(K,\omega_{R})
which is a maximal Cohen-Macaulay module. So, it is enough to show that
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{\ell},K_{\ell})
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay. We will do this by showing that
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{j},K_{\ell})
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay for every
1\leq j\leq\ell
.

We start with the short exact sequence
0\to\Lambda\to\Pi^{0}\to K_{1}\to 0
defining
K_{1}
and we apply
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(-,K_{\ell})
to it to get an exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{1},K_{\ell})\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Pi^{0},K_{\ell})\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Lambda,K_{\ell})\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{1}(K_{1},K_{\ell}).

The rightmost term is zero. Indeed,
K_{1}
is the
(\ell-1)^{th}
syzygy of
K_{\ell}
as a
\Lambda
-module. Therefore,
\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{1}(K_{1},K_{\ell})\cong\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{\ell}(K_{\ell},K_{\ell})\cong 0
by the previous lemma. We have that
\Pi^{0}
and
\Lambda
both projective. So, both
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Pi^{0},K_{\ell})
and
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Lambda,K_{\ell})
are in
\mathrm{add}_{\Lambda}K_{\ell}
. Thus, they are both maximal Cohen-Macaulay. By the depth lemma,
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{1},K_{\ell})
is also maximal Cohen-Macaulay. Now, assume that the result holds for
j<\ell
. We have a short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to K_{j}\to\Pi^{j}\to K_{j+1}\to 0

to which we apply
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(-,K_{\ell})
. We get an exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{j+1},K_{\ell})\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Pi^{j},K_{\ell})\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{j},K_{\ell})\to\mathrm{Ext}_{\Lambda}^{1}(K_{j+1},K_{\ell}).

By the same argument as above, the rightmost term is zero and the second term is maximal Cohen-Macaulay. By the induction hypothesis, the third terms is also maximal Cohen-Macaulay. Therefore, by the depth lemma,
\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(K_{j+1},K_{\ell})
is maximal Cohen-Macaulay which finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.13
.

Suppose that
\Lambda
is an
R
-order of finite global dimension and
M\in\mathrm{CM}(\Lambda)
is an
\ell
-tilting module of CM-injective dimension
m
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{gldim}\mathrm{End}_{\Lambda}(M)\leq\ell+m+d.

Proof.

Let us start with putting
\Gamma=\mathrm{End}_{\Lambda}(M)
. Since
\Lambda
is of finite global dimension and
\Gamma
is derived equivalent to
\Lambda
, we know that
\Gamma
also has finite global dimension. We will show that any
X\in\mathrm{mod}\Gamma
has projective dimension at most
\ell+m+d
.

Let
P
be a minimal projective resolution of
X
. Since
P
is minimal, the width of
P
is
\mathrm{pd}X+1
. The equivalence between
D^{b}(\Lambda)
and
D^{b}(\Gamma)
restricts to an equivalence between
K^{-,b}(\mathrm{add}M)
and
K^{-,b}(\mathrm{proj}\Gamma)
. The image of
P\in K^{-,b}(\mathrm{proj}\Gamma)
under this equivalence is
M\otimes_{\Gamma}P
. Since
P
is minimal, it has no nonzero acyclic summands and the same holds for
M\otimes_{\Gamma}P
.

Since
M
is a tilting
\Lambda
-module of projective dimension at most
\ell
, it has projective dimension at most
\ell
as a
\Gamma
-module. Hence,

\displaystyle H^{i}(M\otimes_{\Gamma}P)=\mathrm{Tor}_{-i}^{\Gamma}(M,X)=0

for
i<-\ell
. Thus,
M\otimes_{\Gamma}P[-\ell-1]
has no non-negative cohomology and we can apply Corollary
4.12
. This gives us a direct sum decomposition
M\otimes_{\Gamma}P[-\ell-1]=Y\oplus Z
where
Y
is acylic and
Z^{i}=0
for
i<1-m-d
. But since
M\otimes_{\Gamma}P
does not have any nonzero acylic summands, we must have
Y=0
. Therefore, we actually have
M\otimes_{\Gamma}P[-1-\ell]\cong Z
. So, we can conclude that
Z^{i}
is only allowed to be nonzero in the interval
1-d-m\leq i\leq\ell+1
. Consequently, we have an upper bound on the width of
M\otimes_{\Gamma}P
. Namely,
\ell+m+d+1
. This means that the width of
P
is bounded above by
\ell+m+d+1
and thus the projective dimension of
X
is bounded above by
\ell+m+d
.
∎
