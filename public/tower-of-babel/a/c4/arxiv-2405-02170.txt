Fourier-Laplace transforms in polynomial Ornstein-Uhlenbeck volatility models
Eduardo Abi Jaber, Shaun (Xiaoyuan) Li, Xuyang Lin

Abstract
We consider the Fourier-Laplace transforms of a broad class of polynomial Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) volatility models, including the well-known Stein-Stein, Schöbel-Zhu, one-factor Bergomi, and the recently introduced quintic OU models motivated by the SPX-VIX joint calibration problem. We show the connection between the joint Fourier-Laplace functional of the log-price and the integrated variance, and the solution of an infinite-dimensional system of Riccati equations. Next, under some non-vanishing conditions on the Fourier-Laplace transforms, we establish an existence result for such Riccati equations and show how the Fourier-Laplace functional can be seen as the limit of a sequence of entire functions. On the practical side, we develop a numerical scheme to solve the stiff infinite-dimensional system of Riccati equations and demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of the scheme for pricing SPX options and volatility swaps using Fourier and Laplace inversions, with specific examples of the quintic OU and the one-factor Bergomi models and their calibration to real market data.

Introduction

Fourier inversion techniques hold a pivotal role in stochastic volatility modelling, particularly in the context of option pricing and hedging (
Andersen and Andreasen [10]
,
Carr and Madan [13]
,
Eberlein, Glau and Papapantoleon [20]
,
Fang and Oosterlee [23]
,
Lewis [30]
,
Lipton [32, Chaps. 8 and 10]
). They offer the dual advantage of significantly reducing computational time while maintaining a remarkable degree of accuracy, especially when compared to standard Monte Carlo methods. When it comes to model calibration, the applicability of Fourier methods is paramount, as thousands of derivatives across various maturities and strikes need to be evaluated simultaneously in real time.

Despite their numerical advantage, Fourier techniques have traditionally been confined to specific continuous stochastic volatility models where the characteristic function of the log-price is known in (semi-)closed form. These models usually exhibit Markovian affine structures in the sense of
Duffie, Filipović and Schachermayer [18]
such as the renowned Heston model by
Heston [28]
, the Stein–Stein model by
Stein and Stein [39]
, and the Schöbel–Zhu model by
Schöbel and Zhu [36]
, together with some of their non-Markovian Volterra counterparts (
Abi Jaber [8]
,
Abi Jaber [9]
,
Abi Jaber, Larsson and Pulido [6]
,
Cuchiero and Teichmann [17]
,
El Euch and Rosenbaum [22]
,
Gatheral and Keller-Ressel [26]
). The key ingredient in all these models is to compute the characteristic function of the log-price by solving a specific system of deterministic Riccati equations.

More recently, Fourier techniques have also found applications in models of signature volatility in
Abi Jaber and Gérard [3]
and
Cuchiero, Gazzani, Möller and Svaluto-Ferro [15]
, where the volatility process is modelled as a linear functional of the path-signature of semimartingales (e.g. a Brownian motion). In such models, certain characteristic functionals have been related to non-standard infinite-dimensional systems of Riccati ordinary differential equations (ODEs). However, there exists no general theory regarding the existence of solutions for such equations, except for the specific result in
Cuchiero, Svaluto-Ferro and Teichmann [16, Proposition 5.8]
which provides the existence of a solution to the infinite-dimensional ODE system fonew
r the case of the characteristic function of powers of a single Brownian motion modulo a non-vanishing condition on the characteristic function. The primary challenge comes from the intricate questions on analyticity of the logarithm of the characteristic function. Furthermore, numerically solving these equations poses challenges due to their stiffness and complexity. This forms our primary motivation: to establish theoretical results within a specific framework for a larger class of Riccati equations related to integrated quantities of power series of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes and to develop more suitable numerical schemes.

We demonstrate that Fourier techniques can be effectively extended to a broad class of flexible models previously considered infeasible as they fall beyond the conventional class of affine diffusions, including the celebrated one-factor Bergomi model (
Bergomi [12]
,
Dupire [19]
) that has been shown to fit well to the SPX smiles, and the recently introduced quintic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) model of
Abi Jaber, Illand and Li [5]
, which has demonstrated remarkable capabilities in fitting jointly the SPX–VIX volatility surface for maturities between one week to three months, supported by extensive empirical studies on more than 10 years of data in
Abi Jaber and Li [7]
,
Abi Jaber, Illand and Li [4]
.

We refer to the class of models covered in this paper as the class of polynomial OU models, where the volatility of the log-price, denoted by
\sigma
, is defined as a power series of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Our three main theoretical results regarding the joint Fourier–Laplace functional of the log-price and integrated variance process are:

(i)

A verification result on the expression of the joint Fourier–Laplace functional in terms of a solution to an infinite-dimensional system of Riccati ODEs in Theorem
3.2
.

(ii)

The existence of a solution to such a system of ODEs modulo a non-vanishing condition on the joint characteristic functional in Theorem
3.6
.

(iii)

A limiting characterisation of the joint Fourier–Laplace functional via a sequence of entire functions in Theorem
3.14
.

On the practical side, we devise a numerical scheme to solve this infinite-dimensional (stiff) system of Riccati equations, making the application of Fourier pricing for models such as the one-factor Bergomi and the quintic OU models usable in practice. We demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of the scheme for pricing SPX options and volatility swaps using Fourier and Laplace inversions. We also successfully calibrate using Fourier techniques the quintic OU and the one-factor Bergomi models on real market data to highlight the stability and robustness of our numerical method across a wide range of realistic parameters values.

Polynomial Ornstein–Uhlenbeck volatility models

We consider the class of polynomial Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) models for a (discounted) stock price

\displaystyle dS_{t}

\displaystyle=S_{t}\sigma_{t}\,dB_{t},\qquad S_{0}>0,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=g_{0}(t)p(X_{t}),\qquad p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}x^{k},

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=(a+bX_{t})\,dt+c\,dW_{t},\qquad X_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds\right]=\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{0}(s)\,ds,\qquad t\geq 0.

Remark 2.1

The martingale property of the stock price process

Definition 2.2
.

A power series

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\bigl(|p_{k}|(k-1)!!\bigr)^{1/k}=0.

The double factorial

n!!:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{(n-2)/2}(n-2k)&\text{if $n$ is even},\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{(n-1)/2}(n-2k)&\text{if $n$ is odd},\end{cases}

with the convention
(-1)!!=0!!=1
.

Remark 2.3
.

A power series
p:x\mapsto\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}x^{k}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, with an infinite radius of convergence can be naturally extended to a power series

being an entire function of order strictly less than

By Definition

Lemma 2.4

If

(i)

For all

|p(x)|\leq|p|(|x|)

(ii)

|p|(x+y+z)\leq\bigl(|p|(3x)+|p|(3y)+|p|(3z)\bigr)

Proof.

(i) is obvious; for (ii), it suffices to notice that

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
g_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable and bounded function. Let

is negligible to double factorial. Then

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[p^{2}(X_{t})]\,dt<\infty,

so that the stochastic integral

We set

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}|p^{2}|\bigl(e^{bs}X_{0}+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\bigr)\,ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}|p^{2}|\bigl(e^{bs}|X_{0}|+|w(s)|+|\widetilde{W}_{s}|\bigr)\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|p^{2}|_{k}\bigl((3e^{bs}|X_{0}|)^{k}+|3w(s)|^{k}+|3\widetilde{W}_{s}|^{k}\bigr)\,ds,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|p^{2}|_{k}\bigl((3e^{bs}|X_{0}|)^{k}+|3w(s)|^{k}+|3\widetilde{W}_{s}|^{k}\bigr)\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

Example 2.7
.

Polynomials of finite degree (e.g. the Stein–Stein and the quintic OU models) and the exponential function (e.g. the one-factor Bergomi model) are clearly negligible to double factorial. For the exponential function

Spreij and Veerman [38]
) under the condition

\Re\left(g_{2}(t)+\frac{g_{1}(t)}{2}(g_{1}(t)-1)\right)\leq 0,\qquad t\leq T,

(3.8)

which is satisfied whenever
g_{1}(t),g_{2}(t)\in\mathbb{C}
,
\Re(g_{1})=0
and
\Re(g_{2})\leq 0
. Hence the characteristic functional of the classical Stein–Stein model is affine in
(1,X,X^{2})
, i.e.,

F(t,x)=\exp\bigl(\psi_{0}(T-t)+\psi_{1}(T-t)x+\psi_{2}(T-t)x^{2}\bigr),\qquad t\leq T,

(3.9)

for which
\psi_{0},\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
can even be found explicitly when
g_{0},g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are constants, as shown by
Lord and Kahl [33]
.

In addition to the existence of a solution to the finite-dimensional Riccati equations, the other assumptions in Theorem
3.2
can also be verified for the Stein–Stein model. From (
3.9
), it is obvious that the finite sum
\sum_{k=0}^{2}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)|x^{k}
has an infinite radius of convergence in
x
. Furthermore, the process
(\exp(U_{t}))_{t\leq T}
is a true martingale in the Stein–Stein model as shown below.

Proposition 3.5
.

Fix functions
g_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
,
g_{1},g_{2}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
measurable and bounded such that
\Re(g_{1})=0
and
\Re(g_{2})\leq 0
. If in addition
p(x)=x
, then the process
(\exp(U_{t}))_{t\leq T}
in Theorem
3.2
is a true martingale.

Proof.

By
\Re(g_{1})=0
,
\Re(g_{2})\leq 0
and
Abi Jaber and De Carvalho [1, Theorem 2.4]
, we deduce that
\Re(\psi_{2})\leq 0
. Next,
|\psi_{0}|
and
|\psi_{1}|
are both bounded by positive constants due to continuity in
t\in[0,T]
. Recall that
X_{t}=e^{bt}X_{0}+a\int_{0}^{t}e^{b(t-s)}\,ds+ce^{bt}Y_{t}
with
Y:=\int e^{-bs}\,dW_{s}
a martingale. There exist positive constants
C_{0},C_{1}
such that

\displaystyle|\exp(U_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigl(|\psi_{0}(T-t)|+|\psi_{1}(T-t)||X_{t}|\bigr)\leq C_{0}\bigl(\exp(2D_{t}^{+})+\exp(2D_{t}^{-})\bigr),

where
D_{t}^{\pm}:=\pm(C_{1}/2)Y_{t}-(C_{1}^{2}/8)((1-e^{-2bt})/(2b))
, with the convention
(1-e^{-2bt})/(2b):=t
if
b=0
. Then
\exp(D^{\pm})
are true martingales by Novikov’s condition. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[\exp(2D_{t}^{\pm})]=\exp\bigl((C_{1}^{2}/4)((1-e^{-2bt})/(2b))\bigr)
. Hence by Doob’s
L^{2}
inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq r\leq T}\exp(2D_{r}^{\pm})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq r\leq T}|\exp(D_{r}^{\pm})|^{2}\right]\leq 4\mathbb{E}[\exp(2D_{T}^{\pm})]=4\exp\bigl((C_{1}^{2}/4)((1-e^{-2bT})/(2b))\bigr).

Together with the preceding bound on
\exp(U)
, we get
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\exp(U_{t})|]<\infty
. As
\exp(U)
is a local martingale, it must be a true martingale on
[0,T]
.
∎

In summary, obtaining the representation (
3.9
) under the Stein–Stein model can be attributed to the finite number of terms
(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
of the involved Riccati equations. However, when dealing with an infinite sum, a notable challenge arises in proving that the log-characteristic functional
\log F
is entire in the variable
x
on
\mathbb{R}
. In Section
3.2
, we show how to generate a solution for the infinite-dimensional system of Riccati equations modulo a non-vanishing condition on
F
. In Section
3.3
, we express the Fourier–Laplace transform as the limit of a sequence of entire functions, thanks to the Gaussianity of the process
X
.

In Section
4
, we numerically illustrate the validity of Fourier pricing in the context of the Bergomi and quintic OU models by using the representation (
3.5
). Readers interested in the numerical implementation can jump directly to Section
4
.

3.2
Existence of a solution to the Riccati equations

Our second main result generates a solution to the infinite-dimensional system (

) and (

) of Riccati equations by differentiating the logarithm of the characteristic functional

\psi_{k}(t):=\left.\frac{1}{k!}\partial_{x}^{k}\log F(T-t,x)\right|_{x=0},\qquad t\geq 0.

This requires the logarithm of a complex-valued function; see Appendix

for its precise definition.

.

Fix
g_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}

. In particular, the family of functions

(3.10)

solves the system (

) and (

) of Riccati ODEs.

Proof.

The proof is given in Section

.

Theorem
3.6
establishes the existence of a solution to the Riccati ODEs (

) and (
3.3
) when the coefficients are real as shown in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

Fix
g_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
,
g_{1}=0
and
g_{2}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{-}

Proof.

In this case,
F
reduces to the Laplace transform of the integrated variance, i.e.,

F(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}g_{2}(T-s)\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds\right){\ \Big|\ }X_{t}=x\right],

Remark 3.8
.

Theorem
3.6
is in the spirit of
Cuchiero, Svaluto-Ferro and Teichmann [16, Proposition 5.8]
which provides the existence of a solution to the infinite-dimensional system of ODEs for the case of the characteristic function of powers of a single Brownian motion, modulo similar assumptions. In contrast, Theorem
3.6
deals with more involved time-integrated quantities and requires a more delicate analysis.

Although Theorem
3.6
does not establish that
\log F(t,\cdot)
is entire, its analyticity can be inferred from the properties of solutions to parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs).

Remark 3.9
.

To prove Theorem
3.6
, we establish in Theorem
6.11
for the function
f(t,x)=F(t,x)\exp(v(t)q(x))
the PDE

\begin{cases}\displaystyle f_{t}(t,x)+f_{x}(t,x)(a+bx)+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}(t,x)+f(t,x)\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t)p_{i}(x)=0,\\[4.30554pt]
f(T,x)=\exp\bigl(v(T)q(x)\bigr).\end{cases}

Here
v,u_{i}
are continuous and
p_{i}
are analytic functions as defined in Lemma
3.10
, with
a+bx,p_{i}(x)
that are bounded, Hölder-continuous and analytic in the variable
x
within any open bounded set of
\mathbb{R}
. We can thus apply
Eidelman [21, Theorem 6.2 in Section II.2.]
to show that both
f(t,\cdot)
and
F(t,\cdot)
are analytic. Since analyticity is a local property, if
F
does not vanish, then
\log F(t,\cdot)
is also analytic. Therefore we can deduce a local representation: for any
t\leq T
, there exists
r_{t}>0
such that

F(t,x)=\exp\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}\right),\qquad x\in(-r_{t},r_{t}),

where the
(\psi_{k})_{k\geq 0}
are defined by (
3.10
). In our case, both
p_{i}
and
q
are not only analytic but also entire. Extending the proof of analyticity using PDE techniques to establish that
F(t,\cdot)
is entire might be possible, and that would lead to a global representation as in (
3.5
) as well as the uniqueness of the solution to the associated infinite-dimensional system of Riccati equations. Such a question holds independent interest for PDEs in its own right, but requires a more delicate analysis at the PDE level that lies beyond our probabilistic approach. Instead, in the next section, we show how
F
(and
\log F
) can be interpreted as the limit of a sequence of entire functions.

3.3
The Fourier–Laplace transform as a limit of entire functions

In this section, our objective is to construct a sequence
(F_{n})
of entire functions that converges to
F
. In addition, with a natural non-vanishing condition, we can show that
\log F_{n}
is also entire and admits a power series expansion. This allows us to interpret
F
and
\log F
as limits of entire functions.

The idea is to approximate the Riemann sum on the sample paths of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
X
to exploit the underlying Gaussian density in finite dimensions. To achieve this, we first need to get rid of the stochastic integrals and express them in terms of functions of
X_{T}
and Lebesgue integrals of
(X_{s})_{t\leq s\leq T}
. This is done by combining Itô’s lemma and the conditioning trick of
Romano and Touzi [34]
in the next result.

Lemma 3.10
.

Fix a power series
p
negligible to double factorial, and continuously differentiable functions
g_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
,
g_{1},g_{2}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(g_{1})=0
,
\Re(g_{2})\leq 0
. Then there exist power series
q,p_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, with an infinite radius of convergence and functions
v,u_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, such that

F(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(s)p_{i}(X_{s})\,ds+v(T)q(X_{T})-v(t)q(X_{t})\right){\ \Big|\ }X_{t}=x\right]

with

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=p^{2}(x),

\displaystyle u_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho^{2})g_{1}^{2}(T-s)-\frac{1}{2}g_{1}(T-s)+g_{2}(T-s)\right)g_{0}^{2}(s),

(3.11)

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{a+bx}{c}p(x)-\frac{c}{2}p^{\prime}(x),

\displaystyle u_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=\rho g_{1}(T-s)g_{0}(s),

\displaystyle p_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{x}p(y)\,dy,

\displaystyle u_{3}(s)

\displaystyle=-\rho g_{1}^{\prime}(T-s)g_{0}(s)+\rho g_{1}(T-s)g_{0}^{\prime}(s),

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{x}p(y)\,dy,

\displaystyle v(s)

\displaystyle=\rho g_{1}(T-s)g_{0}(s).

In addition,
u_{i},v
are continuous on
[0,T]
,
\Re(u_{1})\leq 0
,
\Re(u_{2}),\Re(u_{3}),\Re(v)=0
and
p_{i},q
are all negligible to double factorial.

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
A
.
∎

We now introduce
F_{n}
, the discretised version of
F
.

Definition 3.11
.

For
\mu_{n}=\frac{T}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{iT/n}
, where
\delta_{t}
is the Dirac mass at a point
t
, we define the function

F_{n}(t,x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(s)p_{i}(X_{s})\,\mu_{n}(ds)+v(T)q(X_{T})-v(t)q(X_{t})\right){\ \Big|\ }X_{t}=x\right].

Definition 3.12
.

Given an entire function
g
on
\mathbb{R}
, we define the extension of
g
to the complex plane by
g_{c}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(k)}(0)}{k!}z^{k}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 3.13
.

Given an entire function
g
on
\mathbb{R}
, the power series
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^{k}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, has an infinite radius of convergence. So does
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(k)}(0)}{k!}z^{k}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
, and thus
g_{c}
is well defined on
\mathbb{C}
and is entire on
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Fix a power series
p
negligible to double factorial, continuously differentiable functions
g_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
,
g_{1},g_{2}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(g_{1})=0
,
\Re(g_{2})\leq 0
. Then
F_{n}(t,\cdot)
as specified in Definition
3.11
is well defined and entire in
x\in\mathbb{R}
. If in addition the extension
(F_{n}(t,\cdot))_{c}
of
F_{n}(t,\cdot)
to the complex plane does not vanish for all

and

, then

is entire in

and

F_{n}(t,x)=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi_{n,k}(T-t)x^{k}\right),\qquad t\leq T,

and

where for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the family of functions
\psi_{n,k}
is defined by

\displaystyle\psi_{n,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{n,k}(t)-\rho g_{1}(t)g_{0}(T-t)\frac{p_{k-1}}{ck},\qquad k\geq 1,

\displaystyle\psi_{n,0}(t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{n,0}(t),

where the family

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\uparrow t}\phi_{n,k}(s)+\frac{T}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(T-t)(p_{i})_{k},\qquad\frac{nt}{T}\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\},

\displaystyle\phi_{n,k}(0)

Proof.

The proof is given in Section

∎

Remark 3.15
.

One of the features of Theorem

is that the functions

are entire in

with no additional assumptions. However, due to the delicate nature of the complex logarithm function,

inherits this property if and only if the extension

Numerical illustration

4.1
Numerical scheme for high-dimensional Riccati equations

In this section, we show how to solve the infinite-dimensional system (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) of Riccati equations numerically for derivative pricing and hedging. The idea is to solve a truncated version of the ODE by assuming
\psi_{k}\approx 0
,
k>M
, for some integer
M
.

Even the truncated version of the infinite-dimensional system of Riccati equations can be challenging to solve. Standard techniques such as the Runge–Kutta methods can fail to solve the truncated version, for example when the parameters
b
and
c
in (
2.1
) are large; see
Cuchiero, Svaluto-Ferro and Teichmann [16, Figs. 2 and 3]
for other attempts to solve a similar problem.

We present a customised algorithm that combines variation of constants and the implicit Euler scheme. For this section alone, all matrix and vector indices start from
0
. With some abuse of notation, we rewrite the Riccati ODE (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) in matrix form as

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=P(t)+A\psi(t)+Q\psi(t)+\frac{c^{2}}{2}\widetilde{\psi}(t)*\widetilde{\psi}(t)+\ell(t)N\psi(t),

(4.1)

\displaystyle\psi(0)

\displaystyle=0,

with
\psi(t)=(\psi_{0}(t),\psi_{1}(t),\ldots)^{\top}
and
\psi^{\prime}(t)
its element-wise derivative. Here
P(t)
is a vector with
k
th element being
(g_{2}(t)+\frac{g_{1}(t)}{2}(g_{1}(t)-1))g_{0}^{2}(T-t)(p*p)_{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
A
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal
A_{k,k}=bk
, and
Q
is a square matrix with
Q_{k,k+1}=a(k+1)
,
Q_{k,k+2}=c^{2}(k+1)(k+2)/2
and zero elsewhere. The convolution term
\widetilde{\psi}(t)*\widetilde{\psi}(t)
denotes the discrete convolution of the vectors
\widetilde{\psi}(t)
, with
k
th element given by
(\widetilde{\psi}(t)*\widetilde{\psi}(t))_{k}
. Finally,
N
is a matrix with
N_{j,k}=kp_{j+1-k}
, where
p_{m}
denotes the power series coefficient such that
p_{m}=0
whenever
m<0
, with
\ell(t)
collecting the term
c\rho g_{1}(t)g_{0}(T-t)
.

Given a partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{\widehat{N}}=T
of the interval
[0,T]
, variation of constants gives

\displaystyle\psi(t_{i+1})

\displaystyle=e^{A\Delta_{i}}\psi(t_{i})+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}e^{A(t_{i+1}-s)}P(s)\,ds+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}e^{A(t_{i+1}-s)}Q\psi(s)\,ds

(4.2)

\displaystyle+\frac{c^{2}}{2}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}e^{A(t_{i+1}-s)}\big(\widetilde{\psi}(s)*\widetilde{\psi}(s)\big)\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}e^{A(t_{i+1}-s)}\ell(s)N\psi(s)\,ds

with
\Delta_{i}=t_{i+1}-t_{i}
. By truncating the vector
\psi
up to some level
M
and assuming
(\psi_{k})_{k>M}=0
, we approximate the solution of
\psi(t_{i+1})
in (
4.2
) by the quasi-implicit scheme

\displaystyle\psi(t_{i+1})

\displaystyle\approx e^{A\Delta_{i}}\psi(t_{i})+G_{i}P(t_{i})+G_{i}Q\psi(t_{i+1})+\frac{c^{2}}{2}G_{i}\big(\widetilde{\psi}(t_{i})*\widetilde{\psi}(t_{i+1})\big)+G_{i}\ell(t_{i})N\psi(t_{i+1}),

(4.3)

where
G_{i}=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}e^{A(t_{i+1}-s)}\,ds
is a diagonal matrix whose
k
th diagonal entry is
(e^{b(k-1)\Delta_{i}}-1)/(b(k-1))
,
1<k\leq M+1
, and first diagonal entry
\Delta_{i}
. Similarly,
Q
and
N
are now
(M+1)\times(M+1)
matrices as defined above, with
\psi
and
P(t_{i})
both vectors of dimension
M+1
.

For the quadratic term
\widetilde{\psi}(t_{i})*\widetilde{\psi}(t_{i+1})
, we define

R^{(i)}\psi(t_{i+1}):=\widetilde{\psi}(t_{i})*\widetilde{\psi}(t_{i+1}),

where
R^{(i)}
is an
(M+1)\times(M+1)
matrix with
R^{(i)}_{j,k}=(j+2-k)k\psi_{j+2-k}(t_{i})
, where we complement the definitions by setting
(\psi_{z}(t_{i}))_{z<0}=0
. After rearranging terms, we can now rewrite (
4.3
) as

J_{i}\psi(t_{i+1})\approx\big(e^{A\Delta_{i}}\psi(t_{i})+G_{i}P(t_{i})\big),\qquad J_{i}=\left(I-G_{i}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{2}R^{(i)}+\ell(t_{i})N+Q\right)\right).

We are therefore interested in a system of complex-valued linear equations of the form
J_{i}x=y
. In practice, this can be efficiently solved using the lower-upper (LU) decomposition, avoiding the need to invert the matrix
J_{i}
at each time step which can be computationally expensive. Finally, the matrix
Q
can contain large coefficients resulting from the term
c^{2}(k+2)(k+1)/2
when
k
is large. This can cause numerical instability; so we capped
(k+2)(k+1)
to some fixed level
k_{\max}^{2}=15^{2}
, which is vital in ensuring that the scheme does not blow up. A detailed implementation of our algorithm can be found at
https://colab.research.google.com/drive/1VCVyN1qQmLgOWjOy4fbWftyDqEQdm5n5?usp=sharing
(a Python notebook).

We first test our algorithm to compute
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(-W_{t}^{4}/4!)]
as considered in
Cuchiero, Svaluto-Ferro and Teichmann [16, Figs. 2 and 3]
, which can be expressed by the solution of a particular case of the Riccati ODE in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), with a slight modification to the initial value, given by

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+2)(k+1)}{2}\psi_{k+2}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\big(\widetilde{\psi}(t)*\widetilde{\psi}(t)\big)_{k},

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4!}\mathbf{1}_{\{k=4\}},

where
\mathbf{1}
is the indicator function. Alternatively, the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(-W_{t}^{4}/4!)]
can also be computed via a numerical integration quadrature with respect to the Gaussian density, allowing us to evaluate our algorithm’s performance. Figure
1
illustrates numerical convergence in terms of
M
, with step size
n=100
. We observe the instability of the Runge–Kutta scheme with increasing truncation
M
, whereas the scheme we propose remains stable with an increase of
M
.

Figure 1:
Numerical solution of
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(-W_{t}^{4}/4!)]

Our scheme, in a nutshell, is essentially an implicit scheme of order one. To further improve its efficiency, we can choose at time step
t_{i}
an adaptive step size
\Delta_{i}
such that the contribution from the second order term
\psi^{\prime\prime}(t_{i})
does not exceed a certain threshold
\zeta>0
. From (
4.1
), assuming that
g_{1}
,
g_{2}
and
g_{0}
are continuously differentiable in
t
, we have

\psi(t_{i+1})-\psi(t_{i})=\psi^{\prime}(t_{i})\Delta_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(t_{i})\Delta_{i}^{2}+o(\Delta_{i}^{2}),

where
\psi^{\prime\prime}(t)
can be computed explicitly for all
t\leq T
. We can then choose

\Delta_{i}=\min\left(\max\left(\sqrt{2\zeta/|\psi^{\prime\prime}(t_{i})|},\Delta_{\min}\right),\Delta_{\max}\right),

with
\Delta_{\min}
(
\Delta_{\max}
) some pre-determined minimum (maximum) step size. Intuitively, the smaller
|\psi^{\prime\prime}(t_{i})|
, the larger a time step
\Delta_{i}
one can take without sacrificing too much accuracy, provided that
\Delta_{\min}\leq\Delta_{i}\leq\Delta_{\max}
. For all numerical experiments below, we chose
\zeta=1\times 10^{-9}
,
\Delta_{\min}=5\times 10^{-5}
and
\Delta_{\max}=\max(1/252\times 10^{-1},T\times 10^{-1})
.

4.2
Pricing SPX derivatives via Fourier

Our numerical scheme gives us direct access to the characteristic function of
\log S
to price derivatives via Fourier inversion techniques, using the expression (
3.5
) with
g_{2}(t)=0
and
g_{1}(t)=iu
, for
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Specifically, we show how one can price European options via Fourier techniques for the quintic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model (
Abi Jaber, Illand and Li [5]
) and the one-factor Bergomi model (
Bergomi [12]
,
Dupire [19]
). This also serves as a numerical validation of the representation (
3.5
).

There are several Fourier inversion techniques available in the literature to price European-style vanilla call and put options. Here we adapt the pricing formula suggested in
Lewis [30]
, which involves only one integral to evaluate

\displaystyle C_{t}(S_{t},K,T)

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}[(S_{T}-K)^{+}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=S_{t}-\frac{K}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Re\left(e^{(iu+\frac{1}{2})k_{t}}\varphi\left(u-\frac{i}{2}\right)\right)\frac{du}{u^{2}+\frac{1}{4}},

(4.4)

where
C_{t}(S_{t},K,T)
denotes the European call option price with strike
K
and time to maturity
T-t
,
k_{t}:=\log(K/S_{t})
is the log-moneyness and
\varphi(u)
is the Fourier–Laplace transform of
\log(S_{T}/S_{t})
, conditionally on
X_{t}=x
. More precisely,

\varphi(u):={\mathbb{E}}[e^{iu\log(S_{T}/S_{t})}\mid X_{t}=x].

Equivalently,
\varphi(u)
can be obtained from the expression of
F(t,x)
in (
3.1
): for fixed
(t,x)
,
\varphi(u)
is obtained by setting
g_{1}\equiv iu
,
g_{2}\equiv 0
to obtain

\varphi(u):=\left.F(t,x)\right|_{g_{1}\equiv iu,\,g_{2}\equiv 0}={\mathbb{E}}[e^{iu\log(S_{T}/S_{t})}\mid X_{t}=x].

To compute the improper integral, we make use of the equivalent representation of
F(t,x)
in (
3.5
) via the Gauss–Laguerre quadrature which has been demonstrated to be efficient; see
Abi Jaber and Gérard [3]
,
Hurd and Zhou [29]
,
Bayer et al. [11]
.

To speed up the computation of
C_{t}
, we add a control variate to reduce the number of evaluations of
\varphi(u)
for different
u
via

\displaystyle C_{t}(S_{t},K,T)

\displaystyle=\widehat{C}_{t}(S_{t},K,T)-\frac{K}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Re\left(e^{(iu+\frac{1}{2})k_{t}}\left(\varphi\left(u-\frac{i}{2}\right)-\widehat{\varphi}\left(u-\frac{i}{2}\right)\right)\right)\frac{du}{u^{2}+\frac{1}{4}},

(4.5)

where
\widehat{C}_{t}(S_{t},K,T)
is the appropriate call price of the control variate and
\widehat{\varphi}(u)
is the Fourier–Laplace transform of
\log(S_{T}/S_{t})
of the control variate.

In this section, we choose as the control variate the Heston model, which admits a closed-form characteristic function that is also affine in its state variables; see
Heston [28]
. The Heston model parameters can be efficiently selected via a standard optimisation algorithm such that the difference between
\varphi(u-i/2)
and
\widehat{\varphi}(u-i/2)
is minimised. Of course, other control variates with explicit characteristic functions, such as the Black–Scholes model, can also be used.

4.2.1
Quintic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck volatility model

The volatility process
\sigma_{t}
under the quintic OU model takes the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\xi_{0}(t)}{{\mathbb{E}}[p(X_{t})^{2}]}}p(X_{t}),\qquad p(x)=p_{0}+p_{1}x+p_{3}x^{3}+p_{5}x^{5},

(4.6)

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\alpha\varepsilon^{-1}(t-s)}\,dW_{s},

with
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\leq 0
. The nonnegative coefficients
p_{0},p_{1},p_{3},p_{5}\geq 0
(
p_{2}=p_{4}=0
) ensure a negative leverage effect as well as the martingality of
S
whenever
\rho\leq 0
; see
Abi Jaber, Illand and Li [5]
. The particular parametrisation with
X
means
a=0
,
b=\alpha\varepsilon^{-1}
and
c=\varepsilon^{\alpha}
in (
2.1
). This model has been shown to produce a remarkable joint fit to both SPX and VIX implied volatility surfaces (
Abi Jaber, Illand and Li [5]
,
Abi Jaber, Illand and Li [4]
).

Since
X
is an OU process that can be simulated exactly, one could be tempted to use Monte Carlo to estimate the SPX derivatives with appropriate control variates. However, the calibrated values of
\varepsilon
are usually very small, and
\alpha
is negative, pushing the model effectively into a fast regime with large mean reversion of order
\alpha\varepsilon^{-1}
and large vol of vol
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
. It is known that the standard Euler scheme for pricing SPX derivatives can produce a large estimation bias for longer maturities
T
due to the highly erratic paths of
\sigma
, requiring a finer step size or other asymptotic approximation techniques as in
Fouque, Papanicolaou, Sircar and Solna [25]
,
Fouque, Lorig and Sircar [24]
. Pricing via Fourier methods hence presents an attractive alternative given the increased efficiency and accuracy. To demonstrate, we choose the parameters
\rho=-0.65
,
\alpha=-0.6
,
(p_{0},p_{1},p_{3},p_{5})=(0.01,1,0.214,0.227)
,
\xi_{0}(t)=0.025
,
\varepsilon=1/52
. Figure
2
shows the convergence of SPX implied volatility for different maturities as the truncation level
M
increases. We include in Appendix
C
a cost comparison between Fourier vs. Monte Carlo methods to demonstrate the efficiency of the Fourier methods using our proposed numerical scheme.

Figure 2:
SPX implied volatility for different maturities in the quintic OU model, computed with our algorithm with different levels
M
. Dotted red lines are Monte Carlo 95% confidence interval computed with 500,000 simulations and
n=10,000
number of steps per maturity slice.

4.2.2
One-factor Bergomi model

The one-factor Bergomi model (
Bergomi [12]
,
Dupire [19]
) assumes
\sigma_{t}
to be log-normal and of the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\xi_{0}(t)}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\eta X_{t}-\frac{1}{4}\eta^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[X_{t}^{2}]\right),

(4.7)

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\alpha\varepsilon^{-1}(t-s)}\,dW_{s},

with
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\leq 0
. Similarly to the quintic OU model before, we have
a=0
,
b=\alpha\varepsilon^{-1}
and
c=\varepsilon^{\alpha}
. We now approximate the exponential as a truncated sum up to level
L
, i.e.,

\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{\xi_{0}(t)}{{\mathbb{E}}[p(X_{t})^{2}]}}p(X_{t}),\qquad p(X_{t})=\sum_{k=0}^{L}p_{k}X_{t}^{k},\qquad p_{k}=\frac{\eta^{k}}{2^{k}k!},

where
\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}
converges to
\sigma_{t}
in (
4.7
) pointwise a.s. when sending
L\to\infty
. We fix
\rho=-0.7
,
\varepsilon=1/52
,
\alpha=-0.7
,
\eta=1.2
,
\xi_{0}(t)=0.025
for the numerical experiment and set
L=8
. Figure
3
shows that our numerical scheme quickly converges to Monte Carlo estimates of the original one-factor Bergomi model.

. Dotted red lines are Monte Carlo 95% confidence interval computed with 500,000 simulations and
n=10,000
number of steps per maturity slice.

4.3
Pricing
q
-volatility swaps via Laplace inversion

The
q
-volatility swap rate
K_{q}(t,T)
is defined by

K_{q}(t,T):={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{T-t}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds\right)^{q}\Bigm|X_{t}=x\right],\qquad q\in[0,1].

For the case of a standard volatility swap (i.e.,
q=1/2
), one can price the swap rate
K_{q}(t,T)
via the inverse Laplace transform in
Schürger [37]
as

K_{\frac{1}{2}}(t,T):={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{T-t}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds\right)^{1/2}\Bigm|X_{t}=x\right]=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\widetilde{F}(u)}{u^{3/2}}\,du,

where
\widetilde{F}(u)
is the Laplace transform of
\frac{1}{T-t}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds
conditionally on
X_{t}=x
. More precisely,

\widetilde{F}(u):={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\frac{u}{T-t}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds}\mid X_{t}=x\right].

Equivalently,
\widetilde{F}(u)
can be obtained from the expression of
F(t,x)
in (
3.1
): for fixed
(t,x)
,
\widetilde{F}(u)
is obtained by setting
g_{1}\equiv 0
,
g_{2}\equiv-u/(T-t)
so that

\widetilde{F}(u):=\left.F(t,x)\right|_{g_{1}\equiv 0,\,g_{2}\equiv-\frac{u}{T-t}}={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\frac{u}{T-t}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds}\mid X_{t}=x\right].

Again, we can accelerate the computation via a control variate, for example the Black–Scholes control variate

K_{\frac{1}{2}}(t,T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\widetilde{F}_{\mathrm{BS}}(u)-\widetilde{F}(u)}{u^{3/2}}\,du

with
\widetilde{F}_{\mathrm{BS}}(u)=\exp(-u\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}
is an arbitrary level of volatility that can be fixed upfront.

Compared to Sect.
4.2
, we are even more confident of our numerical scheme thanks to Corollary
3.7
. For demonstration purposes, we use the same model parameters for the quintic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck and the one-factor Bergomi model as in Sect.
4.2
and adopt a parametric forward variance curve in the form
\xi_{0}(t)=V_{0}e^{-kt}+V_{\infty}(1-e^{-kt})
with
V_{0}=0.025
,
k=5
and
V_{\infty}=0.06
. Figure
4
shows the volatility swaps of the two models computed by inverse Laplace transform with truncation level
M=32
vs. that computed by Monte Carlo with 400,000 simulations and 10,000 steps.

Proof of Theorem
3.2

We first introduce a lemma to justify the application of Itô’s formula to the series of functions
\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi_{k}(T-t)X_{t}^{k}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.2
,
h(t,x):=\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
is
C^{2}
in
x
and
C^{1}
in
t
, and its partial derivatives are
h_{x}(t,x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(k+1)\psi_{k+1}(T-t)x^{k}
,
h_{xx}(t,x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(k+2)(k+1)\psi_{k+2}(T-t)x^{k}
,
h_{t}(t,x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}-\psi^{\prime}_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
.

Proof.

First, we clearly have

|\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}|\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)||x|^{k},

and by assumption,
\sum_{k\geq 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)||x|^{k}<\infty
. So
h(t,x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
is well defined. If we restrict
x
to a bounded set of
{\mathbb{R}}
, then
h^{n}(t,x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
is continuous and converges uniformly on bounded subsets of
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}
to
h(t,x)
when
n\rightarrow\infty
, so
h(t,x)
is continuous.

Notice that the domain of convergence for
\sum_{k\geq 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)|x^{k}<\infty
is
{\mathbb{R}}
, and so the same holds for
\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
. So
h_{x}(t,x),h_{xx}(t,x)
are well-defined, continuous, and have the expression in the statement for the same reason as for
h(t,x)
.

To treat
h_{t}(t,x)
, we should first prove that
\sum_{k\geq 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi^{\prime}_{k}(t)|x^{k}
has also an infinite radius of convergence.

Indeed, using the Riccati expression (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we only have to check that we have an infinite radius of convergence for

\sum_{k\geq 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{t,k}|x^{k},

(5.1)

where
a_{t,k}
is chosen among
(p*p)_{k}
,
k\psi_{k}(t)
,
(k+1)\psi_{k+1}(t)
,
\frac{(k+2)(k+1)}{2}\psi_{k+2}(t)
,
(\widetilde{\psi}(t)*\widetilde{\psi}(t))_{k}
and
(p*\widetilde{\psi}(t))_{k}
.

By the assumption of Lemma
5.1
and the Cauchy–Hadamard theorem, we know that

\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)|)^{\frac{1}{k}}=0.

We claim that when
a_{t,k}=k\psi_{k}(t),(k+1)\psi_{k+1}(t)=\widetilde{\psi}(t)_{k},\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}\psi_{k+2}(t)
, (
5.1
) has an infinite radius of convergence. We only give the proof for the case when
a_{t,k}=(k+1)\psi_{k+1}(t)=\widetilde{\psi}(t)_{k}
, the other two cases can be argued in the same way. As
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(k+1)}{k}=0
, we have that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(k+1)^{\frac{1}{k}}=1
. Besides, as
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)|)^{\frac{1}{k}}=0
, we have for all
k
big enough,
(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)|)^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq 1
, which leads to
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k}(t)|\leq 1
. Thus

\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k+1}(t)|)^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k+1}(t)|)^{\frac{1}{k+1}}=0.

Thus by the Cauchy–Hadamard theorem,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widetilde{\psi}(t)|_{k}x^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi_{k+1}(t)|x^{k}

has an infinite radius of convergence.

For two power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
and
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}x^{n}
both with an infinite radius of convergence, their product
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(a*b)_{n}x^{n}
also has an infinite radius of convergence. Notice also that
(p*p)_{k},(\widetilde{\psi}(t)*\widetilde{\psi}(t))_{k},(p*\widetilde{\psi}(t))_{k}
are dominated, respectively, by
(|p|*|p|)_{k},(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widetilde{\psi}(t)|*\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widetilde{\psi}(t)|)_{k}
, and
(|p|*\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widetilde{\psi}(t)|)_{k}
. Thus when
a_{t,k}
is among
(p*p)_{k},(\widetilde{\psi}(t)*\widetilde{\psi}(t))_{k},(p*\widetilde{\psi}(t))_{k}
, (
5.1
) has also an infinite radius of convergence. Therefore
\sum_{k\geq 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\psi^{\prime}_{k}(t)|x^{k}
has an infinite radius of convergence, so that
\sum_{k\geq 0}-\psi^{\prime}_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
also has an infinite radius of convergence and converges uniformly as above. So
\sum_{k\geq 0}-\psi^{\prime}_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
is well-defined and also continuous.

Next, notice that
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}-\psi^{\prime}_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
and given the uniform convergence of both series
\sum_{k\geq 0}-\psi^{\prime}_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
and
\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}
, we deduce that
h_{t}(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi_{k}(T-t)x^{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}-\psi^{\prime}_{k}(T-t)x^{k}.

∎

6.1
Properties of power series negligible to double factorial

We collect some properties of power series that are negligible to double factorial as defined in Definition
2.2
.

Given a power series
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}x^{k}
, we define the set
\mathcal{A}_{D}(p)
as the
{\mathbb{R}}
-algebra generated by all higher-order derivatives
\{p,p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime},\ldots\}
of
p
, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}_{D}(p):=\left\{\sum_{s=0}^{\ell}c_{s}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{s}}f_{s,i}:\ell,m_{s}\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ c_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}},\ f_{s,i}\in\{p,p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime},\ldots\}\right\}.

(6.3)

Notice that if
m_{s}=0
, then by convention the empty product
\prod_{i=1}^{0}f_{s,i}
equals
1
.

Remark 6.1
.

This definition comes from the calculations of the successive partial derivatives
F_{x},F_{xx},\ldots
.
For example, in the simple case when
g_{0}=1,g_{1}=0,g_{2}=-1
, by setting
Z=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T-t}p^{2}\left(X_{s}\right)ds\right)
, we have
F(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[Z|X_{0}=x\right]
by the Markov property of
X
. Since we can write
X_{s}=e^{bs}X_{0}+Y_{s}
, where
Y_{s}
does not depend on
X_{0}
, this means that formally,
F_{x}=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{x}|X_{0}=x\right]
, where
Z_{x}=-Z\int_{0}^{T-t}2e^{bs}(pp^{\prime})\left(X_{s}\right)ds
and
pp^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{D}(p)
appears. Higher order derivatives like
Z_{xx},Z_{xxx}
also formally generate elements in
\mathcal{A}_{D}(p)
.

Now we prove some properties of power series that are negligible to double factorial. For the result below, we recall the convention
(-1)!!=(0)!!=1
. Notice that although the proof is a little cumbersome, the essential idea is the observation that
\ell!!
behaves like

(\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor)!2^{\frac{\ell}{2}}
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}=2^{\ell}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\ell\geq 2
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{(k-1)!!(\ell-k-1)!!}\leq\frac{C{\ell}^{3}2^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell-1)!!}.

Proof.

For
k=0,\ell
, we have
\frac{1}{(k-1)!!(\ell-k-1)!!}=\frac{1}{(\ell-1)!!}
. So we only need to prove that for a constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{1}{(k-1)!!(\ell-k-1)!!}=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}\frac{1}{k!!(\ell-2-k)!!}\leq\frac{C{\ell}^{3}2^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell-1)!!}.

(6.4)

Case 1:

\ell=2\ell_{1}
is an even number. If
k=2k_{1}+1
is an odd number,
k!!(\ell-2-k)!!=(2k_{1}+1)!!(\ell-3-2k_{1})!!\geq\frac{(2k_{1}+1)!!(\ell-1-2k_{1})!!}{\ell-1-2k_{1}}\geq\frac{(2k_{1})!!(\ell-2-2k_{1})!!}{\ell}=\frac{2^{\ell_{1}-1}k_{1}!(\ell_{1}-1-k_{1})!}{\ell}
. If
k=2k_{1}
is an even number, then
k!!(\ell-2-k)!!=2^{\ell_{1}-1}k_{1}!(\ell_{1}-1-k_{1})!
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}\frac{1}{k!!(\ell-2-k)!!}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\ell_{1}-1}\frac{\ell+1}{2^{\ell_{1}-1}k_{1}!(\ell_{1}-1-k_{1})!}=\frac{\ell+1}{(\ell_{1}-1)!}=\frac{(\ell+1)2^{\ell_{1}-1}}{(\ell-2)!!}

(6.5)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\ell+1)(\ell-1)2^{\ell_{1}-1}}{(\ell-1)!!}\leq\frac{\ell^{2}2^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell-1)!!}.

(6.6)

Case 2:

\ell=2\ell_{1}+1
is an odd number, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}\frac{1}{k!!(\ell-2-k)!!}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-3}\frac{1}{k!!(\ell-3-k)!!}+\frac{1}{(\ell-2)!!},

(6.7)

and since
\ell-3
is even, as in Case 1, we have for a constant
C>0
that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-3}\frac{1}{k!!(\ell-3-k)!!}+\frac{1}{(\ell-2)!!}\leq\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}2^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}}{(\ell-2)!!}+\frac{1}{(\ell-2)!!}\leq\frac{C\ell^{3}2^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell-1)!!}.

(6.8)

Therefore there exists a constant
C
such that for all
\ell
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}\frac{1}{k!!(\ell-2-k)!!}\leq\frac{C\ell^{3}2^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell-1)!!}
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

The following statements are true.

1)

If
p
is negligible to double factorial, then so is
q(x)=\int_{0}^{x}p(y)dy
.

2)

The set of power series which are negligible to double factorial is closed under addition, differentiation, scalar multiplication, and multiplication. Thus if
p
is negligible to double factorial, then for any
q\in\mathcal{A}_{D}(p)
,
q
is also negligible to double factorial.

3)

If
p(x)=\sum p_{k}x^{k}
is negligible to double factorial, then so is
q(x)=\sum p_{k}c^{k}x^{k}
, where
c
is a constant.

4)

If
p
is negligible to double factorial, then so is
|p|
.

Proof.

1)

Note that
q(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k-1}}{k}x^{k}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|q_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}((|p_{k-1}|\frac{(k-1)!!}{k})^{\frac{1}{k-1}})^{\frac{k-1}{k}}

(6.9)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}((|p_{k-1}|(k-2)!!)^{\frac{1}{k-1}})^{\frac{k-1}{k}}=0,

(6.10)

using that
\frac{(k-1)!!}{k}\leq\frac{k!!}{k}=(k-2)!!
.

2)

Take
p,q
negligible to double factorial. Note that
q^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)q_{k+1}x^{k}
. Then,

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|q^{\prime}_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}((|q_{k+1}|(k+1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k+1}})^{\frac{k+1}{k}},

(6.11)

then by
(k+1)!!\leq(k+1)k!!
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|q^{\prime}_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}((|q_{k+1}|k!!)^{\frac{1}{k+1}})^{\frac{k+1}{k}}(k+1)^{\frac{1}{k}}=0.

(6.12)

By
|a+b|^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq|a|^{\frac{1}{k}}+|b|^{\frac{1}{k}}
,
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|q_{k}+p_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}=0
, and
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|cq_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}=0
for any constant
c
.

Next we show that the power series
pq
is also negligible to double factorial. For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N
, such that for all
k>N
, we have that
(|q_{k}|(k-1)!!)\leq\epsilon^{k},(|p_{k}|(k-1)!!)\leq\epsilon^{k}
. So there is a constant
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that
(|q_{k}|(k-1)!!)\leq C_{\epsilon}\epsilon^{k},(|p_{k}|(k-1)!!)\leq C_{\epsilon}\epsilon^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Thus by Lemma
6.2
, for
\ell\geq 2
,

\displaystyle|(pq)_{\ell}|=|\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}p_{k}q_{\ell-k}|

(6.13)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}|p_{k}||q_{\ell-k}|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\frac{{C_{\epsilon}}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell}}{(k-1)!!(\ell-k-1)!!}\leq\frac{{C_{\epsilon}}^{2}C(\sqrt{2}\epsilon)^{\ell}\ell^{3}}{(\ell-1)!!},

(6.14)

thus
\limsup_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(|(pq)_{\ell}|(\ell-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\leq\sqrt{2}\epsilon
. Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we obtain

\limsup_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(|(pq)_{\ell}|(\ell-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{\ell}}=0.

Therefore,
pq
is also negligible to double factorial.

3)

Notice that

\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|q_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}=|c|\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|p_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}=0.

4)

This is obvious by noticing that taking the modulus of
p
has no impact on the behaviour of
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}(|p_{k}|(k-1)!!)^{\frac{1}{k}}
.

∎

6.2
Regularity in space

In this subsection, in preparation for the Feynman–Kac formula in Section
6.3
, we prove the regularity in
x
of
f
given by (
6.2
), i.e., that
f
is
C^{\infty}
in
x
and such that the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{k}}
are bounded in a suitable sense.

Theorem 6.4
.

The function
f
given by (
6.2
) is
C^{\infty}
in
x
, i.e. the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{k}}
exist and are continuous in
(t,x)
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a power series
q_{k}
negligible to double factorial and a constant
C_{k}
independent of
(t,x)
, such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{k}}(t,x)\right|\leq|q_{k}|(|x|)+C_{k},\quad t\in[0,T],\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(6.15)

Before proving Theorem
6.4
, we first simplify the expression of
f
in (
6.2
) to get rid of the conditioning. Thanks to the Markovianity of
X
, we can write
f
after a change of variable as

\displaystyle f(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Z(t,x)\right],

(6.16)

with

\displaystyle Z(t,x):=\exp\bigg(

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-t}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t+s)p_{i}\left(e^{bs}x+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\right)ds

(6.17)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+v(T)q(e^{b(T-t)}x+w(T-t)+\widetilde{W}_{T-t})\bigg).

(6.18)

Notice
X_{t}=e^{bt}x+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}
when
X_{0}=x
, with
w(t):=a\int_{0}^{t}e^{{b}(t-u)}du
and
{\widetilde{{W}}_{t}}:=c\int_{0}^{t}e^{{b}(t-u)}dW_{u}
. From now on, we consider mainly the representation (
6.16
) for
f(t,x)
.

The main idea for proving Theorem
6.4
consists in taking successive derivatives in
x
inside the expectation above and applying the dominated convergence theorem.
To illustrate this, we first formally calculate
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
. Notice that

the derivative in
x
for the term inside the exponential of
Z(t,x)
is:

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{T-t}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t+s)e^{bs}p_{i}^{\prime}\left(e^{bs}x+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\right)ds

(6.19)

\displaystyle+v(T)e^{b(T-t)}q^{\prime}\left(e^{b(T-t)}x+w(T-t)+\widetilde{W}_{T-t}\right),

(6.20)

so that one expects

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[h(x)Z(t,x)\right].

(6.21)

To prove this equality, we aim to use the definition of derivative and the dominated convergence theorem, which requires, roughly speaking, bounding
h(x)Z(t,x)
in some sense which we clarify. Notice that
|Z(t,x)|\leq 1
since the terms inside the exponential of
Z(t,x)
have a nonpositive real part as a result of Lemma
3.10
. Therefore, we only need to bound
h(x)
.
Notice that
u_{i},v
are continuous and thus bounded on
[0,T]
,
e^{bs}
is also bounded on
[0,T]
,
p_{i}^{\prime},q^{\prime}
are negligible to double factorial by part 2) of Lemma
6.3
. So we only need to bound expressions of the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}q\left(e^{bs}x+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\right)ds\quad\text{and}\quad q\left(e^{bt}x+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right),

(6.22)

where
q
is any power series negligible to double factorial.

First of all, we introduce a new definition and a lemma to help bound in a certain sense the quantities in (
6.22
). We also introduce the notation
\widetilde{W}^{*}_{t}:=\sup_{s\leq t}|\widetilde{W}_{s}|
, which will be useful for applying Doob’s inequality.

Definition 6.5
.

We say that a family of processes
(M_{t}(x))_{t\leq T,x\in{\mathbb{R}}}
is
estimable
if there exists a power series
q
negligible to double factorial such that for all fixed
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

|M_{t}(x)|\leq|q|(|x|)+|q|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T}),\quad a.s.

We prove in Proposition
6.7
below that the expressions in (
6.22
) are estimable.
For now, let us first prove that
|q|(|x|)+|q|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})
is integrable via the following result.

Lemma 6.6
.

The set of estimable families of processes is closed under addition and multiplication. In addition, for all power series
q
negligible to double factorial,
\mathbb{E}\left[|q|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})\right]<\infty
. Therefore, if
M_{t}(x)
is estimable, then there exists a power series
q
negligible to double factorial and a constant
C
, such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\mathbb{E}[|M_{t}(x)|]\leq|q|(|x|)+C
.

Proof.

For the first part, recall that by parts 2) and 4) of Lemma
6.3
, the property of being negligible to double factorial is closed under addition, multiplication, and taking absolute power series.
So the set of estimable families of processes is obviously closed under addition.
For the multiplication, it suffices to use the basic inequality
(a+b)(c+d)\leq a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}
.

To show that

\mathbb{E}\left[|q|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})\right]<\infty
, recall
\widetilde{W}_{t}=e^{bt}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-bs}cdW_{s}
, where
\int_{0}^{t}e^{-bs}cdW_{s}
is a true martingale. There exists a positive constant
C_{T}
such that
\frac{1}{C_{T}}<e^{bt}<C_{T}
when
0\leq t\leq T
. We use
\overline{W}_{t}
to denote
\int_{0}^{t}e^{-bs}cdW_{s}
and
\overline{W}_{t}^{*}
to denote its maximal process
\sup_{s\leq t}|\overline{W}_{s}|
.
By Doob’s maximal inequality for
k\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})^{k}\right]\leq C_{T}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[(\overline{W}^{*}_{T})^{k}\right]\leq C_{T}^{k}\left(\frac{k}{k-1}\right)^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[|{\overline{W}_{T}}|^{k}\right]\leq 4C_{T}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[|{\overline{W}_{T}}|^{k}\right]\leq 4C_{T}^{2k}\mathbb{E}\left[|{\widetilde{W}_{T}}|^{k}\right].

For
k=1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})^{2}\right]\right)\leq 1+4C_{T}^{4}\mathbb{E}\left[({\widetilde{W}_{T}})^{2}\right].

Notice that
\widetilde{W}_{T}
has a centred normal distribution and that the constant
C_{T}
can be taken large enough so that the variance of
{\widetilde{W}_{T}}
is smaller than
C_{T}^{2}
.
It follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[|{\widetilde{W}_{T}}|^{k}\right]\leq(C_{T}^{2})^{\frac{k}{2}}(k-1)!!
.
By modifying a little the coefficients
|q|_{0},|q|_{1},|q|_{2}
in the power series
|q|
, which does not influence the conclusion, we obtain that
C=4\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|q|_{k}(k-1)!!C_{T}^{3k}
satisfies the requirements and is finite, since
q
is negligible to double factorial.

∎

Proposition 6.7
.

If
q
is negligible to double factorial, then the two expressions in (
6.22
) are estimable, and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}q\left(e^{bs}x+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\right)ds\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|q\left(e^{bt}x+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right)\right|\right]\leq|\tilde{q}|(|x|)+C,\quad t\in[0,T],\;x\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
\tilde{q}
is a power series negligible to double factorial,
C
is a constant, and they both depend only on
T
and
q
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We first bound
q\left(e^{bt}x+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right)
:

\displaystyle\left|q\left(e^{bt}x+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq|q|\left(e^{bt}|x|+|w(t)|+|\widetilde{W}_{t}|\right)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|q|_{k}\left((3e^{bt}|x|)^{k}+|3w(t)|^{k}+|3\widetilde{W}_{t}|^{k}\right)

(6.23)

by Lemma
2.4
. Since
e^{bt}
and
w(t)
are both bounded on
[0,T]
, there exists a constant
C_{T}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|q|_{k}\left((3e^{bt}|x|)^{k}+|3w(t)|^{k}+|3\widetilde{W}_{t}|^{k}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|q|_{k}\left(|3C_{T}x|^{k}+(3C_{T})^{k}+|3\widetilde{W}_{t}|^{k}\right)

(6.24)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|q|(|3C_{T}x|)+|q|(|3C_{T}|)+|q|(3\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T}).

(6.25)

Setting
\tilde{q}(x)=|q|(3(C_{T}+1)x)+|q|(|3C_{T}|)
, we get
\left|q\left(e^{bt}x+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right)\right|\leq\tilde{q}(|x|)+\tilde{q}(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})=|\tilde{q}|(|x|)+|\tilde{q}|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})
. By Lemma
6.3
,
|q|
is also negligible to double factorial, and
\tilde{q}
is still negligible to double factorial. So
q\left(e^{bt}x+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right)
is estimable.
An application of Lemma
6.6
yields the bound for the expectation. Furthermore, the term
\int_{0}^{t}q\left(e^{bs}x+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\right)ds
is clearly bounded by
T|\tilde{q}|(|x|)+T|\tilde{q}|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})
.
So we can update
\tilde{q}
to
\tilde{q}(x)=(T\vee 1)|q|(3(C_{T}+1)x)+(T\vee 1)|q|(|3C_{T}|)
to obtain the claimed bound and end the proof.

∎

Now going back to (
6.21
), it suffices to show that for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(t,x+\Delta x)-f(t,x)}{\Delta x}=\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{Z(t,x+\Delta x)-Z(t,x)}{\Delta x}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[h(x)Z(t,x)\right].

Notice that
\frac{\partial Z(t,x)}{\partial x}=h(x)Z(t,x),
to apply the dominated convergence theorem to deduce the second equality, it suffices to prove that
\frac{Z(t,x+\Delta x)-Z(t,x)}{\Delta x}
is dominated by an integrable random variable for
\Delta x\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
. In the following, we suppose
\Delta x\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
.

First of all, as
\frac{\partial Z(t,x)}{\partial x}=h(x)Z(t,x)
,

\frac{\partial\Re(Z(t,x))}{\partial x}=\Re h(x)\Re Z(t,x)-\Im h(x)\Im Z(t,x)
, then

\displaystyle\left|\Re\frac{Z(t,x+\Delta x)-Z(t,x)}{\Delta x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in[x-1,x+1]}|\Re h(y)\Re Z(t,y)-\Im h(y)\Im Z(t,y)|

(6.26)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in[x-1,x+1]}|h(y)|,

(6.27)

where we use Cauchy–Schwarz and
|Z(t,x)|\leq 1
. The same bound also works for
\Im\frac{Z(t,x+\Delta x)-Z(t,x)}{\Delta x}
. To bound
|h(y)|
,
y\in[x-1,x+1]
, recall
h(x)
from (
6.19
), and that
u_{i},v
are bounded,
e^{bs}
is bounded on
[0,T]
,
p_{i}^{\prime},q^{\prime}
are negligible to double factorial, we need only to bound
\left|\int_{0}^{t}q\left(e^{bs}y+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\right)ds\right|
and
\left|q\left(e^{bt}y+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right)\right|,
for
q
an arbitrary power series negligible to double factorial.

By the proof of Proposition
6.7
,
\left|\int_{0}^{t}q\left(e^{bs}y+w(s)+\widetilde{W}_{s}\right)ds\right|\leq|\tilde{q}|(|y|)+|\tilde{q}|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})\leq|\tilde{q}|(|x|+1)+|\tilde{q}|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})
, with
|\tilde{q}|(|x|+1)+|\tilde{q}|(\widetilde{W}^{*}_{T})
being integrable. Moreover,
\left|q\left(e^{bt}y+w(t)+\widetilde{W}_{t}\right)\right|
can be bounded in the same way. Thus for
\Delta x\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
,
\left|\frac{Z(t,x+\Delta x)-Z(t,x)}{\Delta x}\right|
is dominated by
\sup_{y\in[x-1,x+1]}|h(y)|
, which justifies the use of the dominated convergence theorem as
\Delta x\to 0
.

Therefore, (
6.21
) holds. Of course, we should like to prove by induction that

\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{k}}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}(t,x)Z(t,x)\right]

holds for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
to show that
f
is
C^{\infty}
in
x
, where

\displaystyle H_{k+1}(t,x)=\frac{\partial H_{k}(t,x)}{\partial x}+H_{k}(t,x)h(x),\quad H_{0}(t,x)=1,\quad H_{1}(t,x)=h(x).

(6.28)

To achieve this, we need to prove first that
H_{k}
is well-defined. Then similarly as before, we bound
H_{k+1}(x)
so that we can by induction apply dominated convergence to get

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}\frac{\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{k}}(t,x+\Delta x)-\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{k}}(t,x)}{\Delta x}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k+1}(t,x)Z(t,x)\right]}.

Definition 6.8

6.3
Feynman–Kac

In this section, we derive the Feynman–Kac formula in Theorem
6.11
. Since we do not have a priori that
f_{t}
exists in our setting, we prove its existence and obtain the Feynman–Kac formula at the same time.

First, we introduce a result that will be useful later.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(z)\leq 0
. Then
|\exp(z)-1|\leq 3|z|
.

Proof.

We write
z=x+yi
,
x\leq 0
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then

\displaystyle|\exp(z)-1|

\displaystyle=|\exp(x+yi)-1|

\displaystyle=\big|\exp(yi)(\exp(x)-1)+\exp(yi)-1\big|

\displaystyle\leq|\exp(x)-1|+|\cos y-1|+|\sin y|.

If
x=0
, then
|\exp(x)-1|=0
. If
x<0
,
|\frac{\exp(x)-1}{z}|\leq|\frac{\exp(x)-1}{x}|\leq 1
. If
y=0
, then
|\cos y-1|=0
,
|\sin y|=0
. If
y\neq 0
,
|\frac{\cos y-1}{z}|\leq|\frac{\cos y-1}{y}|\leq 1
,
|\frac{\sin y}{z}|\leq|\frac{\sin y}{y}|\leq 1
.
∎

Theorem 6.11
.

The function
f
given by (
6.2
) or equivalently (
6.16
) is
C^{\infty}
in
x
,
C^{1}
in
t
, and solves the partial differential equation (PDE)

\begin{cases}f_{t}(t,x)+f_{x}(t,x)(a+bx)+\dfrac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}(t,x)+f(t,x)\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t)p_{i}(x)=0,\\[4.30554pt]
f(T,x)=\exp\big(v(T)q(x)\big).\end{cases}

(6.41)

Proof.

By Theorem
6.4
,
f
is
C^{\infty}
in
x
. We compute the infinitesimal generator

\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(t_{0},X_{r})-f(t_{0},x)]

in two ways to obtain the PDE and the existence of
f_{t}
at the same time. Here
\mathbb{E}^{x}
means the expectation with
X_{0}=x
.

First way:
Fix
t_{0}
; we compute the quantity using the classical definition of the generator. By Itô’s formula,

df(t_{0},X_{t})=f_{x}\,dX_{t}+\frac{1}{2}f_{xx}\,d\langle X\rangle_{t}=cf_{x}\,dW_{t}+\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}+(a+bX_{t})f_{x}\right)dt.

Note that
f_{x},f_{xx}
are evaluated at
(t_{0},X_{t})
. By Theorem
6.4
and part 2) of Lemma
6.3
,
f_{x}(t,x)
,
(a+bx)f_{x}(t,x)
,
f_{xx}(t,x)
,
|f_{x}(t,x)|^{2}
are all bounded by
|q|(|x|)+C
, where
q
is negligible to double factorial. By Proposition
6.7
,
\mathbb{E}^{x}[\int_{0}^{r}|f_{x}(t_{0},X_{t})|^{2}dt]
is bounded by
|\widetilde{q}|(|x|)+C
for
r\in[0,T]
with
\widetilde{q}
negligible to double factorial. Thus
\int cf_{x}(t_{0},X_{t})dW_{t}
is a square-integrable true martingale on
[0,T]
. Similarly, the Riemann integral
\int_{0}^{r}(\frac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}(t_{0},X_{t})+(a+bX_{t})f_{x}(t_{0},X_{t}))dt
is estimable and has finite expectation. In addition, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}(t_{0},X_{t})+(a+bX_{t})f_{x}(t_{0},X_{t})\right)dt\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{s\in[0,r]}\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{2}|f_{xx}(t_{0},X_{s})|+|(a+bX_{s})f_{x}(t_{0},X_{s})|\right).

where the right-hand side is still estimable and dominated by a random variable with finite expectation. By the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(t_{0},X_{r})-f(t_{0},x)]

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}(t_{0},X_{t})+(a+bX_{t})f_{x}(t_{0},X_{t})\right)dt\right]

(6.42)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}(t_{0},x)+(a+bx)f_{x}(t_{0},x).

Second way:
Compute the generator
\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(t_{0},X_{r})-f(t_{0},x)]
directly by the Markov property of
X
. We define
Z_{t}=\exp(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(T-t+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds)
and
Y_{t}=v(T)q(X_{t})
, and apply the Markov property of

to the representation of

in (
6.2
), i.e.,

f(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds+v(T)q(X_{T-t})\right){\ \Big|\ }X_{0}=x\right],

and by the Markov property for
r\in[0,t]
,

f(t,X_{r})=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{r}^{T-t+r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds+v(T)q(X_{T-t+r})\right){\ \Big|\ }X_{r}\right].

By the tower property of conditional expectations, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(t,X_{r})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{r}^{T-t+r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds+v(T)q(X_{T-t+r})\right){\ \Big|\ }X_{r}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{r}^{T-t+r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds+v(T)q(X_{T-t+r})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[Z_{T-t+r}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)\exp(Y_{T-t+r})\right].

Therefore we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(t_{0},X_{r})-f(t_{0},x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\Bigg[Z_{T-t_{0}+r}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}+r})-Z_{T-t_{0}}\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}+r})Z_{T-t_{0}+r}-\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}})Z_{T-t_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}+r})Z_{T-t_{0}+r}\left(\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\big(f(t_{0}-r,x)-f(t_{0},x)\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}+r})Z_{T-t_{0}+r}\left(\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)-1\right)\right].

(6.43)

Now we want to apply dominated convergence when
r\downarrow 0
to the term

\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}+r})Z_{T-t_{0}+r}\left(\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)-1\right)\right].

To dominate the term inside the expectation, we use

\Re(Y_{t})=\Re(v(T)q(X_{t}))=0,

thus
|\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}+r})|=1
. In addition,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{r}Z_{T-t_{0}+r}\left(\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)-1\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{r}\exp\left(\int_{r}^{T-t_{0}+r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)\left(1-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)\right)\right|.

Since
\Re(u_{1})\leq 0
,
\Re(u_{2})=\Re(u_{3})=0
and
p_{1}=p^{2}
, we therefore get

\displaystyle\Re\left(\int_{r}^{T-t_{0}+r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{r}^{T-t_{0}+r}\Re\big(u_{1}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{1}(X_{s})\big)ds\leq 0,

and thus
|\exp(\int_{r}^{T-t_{0}+r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds)|\leq 1
. Therefore we only need to dominate the term

\frac{1}{r}\left(\exp\left(\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)-1\right).

Since
\Re(\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds)\leq 0
, by Lemma
6.10
, we just need to dominate the term

\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\|u_{i}\|_{\infty}\sup_{s\in[0,r]}|p_{i}(X_{s})|.

Notice that the
u_{i}
are bounded and the
p_{i}
are negligible to double factorial. Thus we obtain by Proposition
6.7
that
|p_{i}(X_{s})|
,

, are dominated when conditioned on
X_{0}=x
. Thus by dominated convergence,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\exp(Y_{T-t_{0}+r})Z_{T-t_{0}+r}\left(\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0}-r+s)p_{i}(X_{s})ds\right)-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}(t_{0})p_{i}(x)f(t_{0},x),

which is finite. Equating this with (
6.42
), the term

is thus well defined. Of course, one can also replace

to obtain the existence of

). A subtle point to note is that for the first method, we do not apply Itô’s formula to

. Instead, we obtain the existence of

by applying Itô’s formula to

in

to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(t_{0}+r,X_{r})-f(t_{0}+r,x)]=\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{2}f_{xx}(t_{0}+r,X_{t})+(a+bX_{t})f_{x}(t_{0}+r,X_{t})\right)dt\right]

and take the limit for

as above. The continuity of

is directly obtained from the continuity of the other terms in this equation, with the boundary condition at

∎

7.1
Entire property

In this section, we do not need any additional condition imposed on

or
F_{n}
about their zeros.

Theorem 7.3
.

For fixed
t
,
f_{n}(t,\cdot)
is entire in
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We first introduce a lemma that will be useful in proving Theorem
7.3
. The idea of the proof is standard and comes from exploiting the fact that the Gaussian density is entire. A similar argument has been used for single marginals in
Cuchiero, Svaluto-Ferro and Teichmann [16, Lemma 5.7]
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Suppose that
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a centered Gaussian process with independent increments such that
W_{t}-W_{s}
has non-zero variance when
t>s
. Let the partition be
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
. Fix
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
bounded measurable. Then the expectation
\mathbb{E}[g(x+W_{t_{1}},x+W_{t_{2}},\ldots,x+W_{t_{n}})]
is entire in
x
.

Proof.

Denoting the variance of
W_{t_{i}}-W_{t_{i-1}}
as
v_{i}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(x+W_{t_{1}},x+W_{t_{2}},\ldots,x+W_{t_{n}})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{i}}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y_{1},y_{1}+y_{2},\ldots,y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n})\times\exp\left(-\frac{(y_{1}-x)^{2}}{2v_{1}}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2v_{i}}\right)\,dy_{1}dy_{2}\cdots dy_{n}.

With
g(y)=g(y_{1},y_{1}+y_{2},\ldots,y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(x+W_{t_{1}},x+W_{t_{2}},\ldots,x+W_{t_{n}})]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2v_{1}}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{i}}}\right)\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2v_{i}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{xy_{1}}{v_{1}}\right)\,dy_{1}dy_{2}\cdots dy_{n}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2v_{1}}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{i}}}\right)\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2v_{i}}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{xy_{1}}{v_{1}}\right)^{k}\,dy_{1}dy_{2}\cdots dy_{n}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2v_{1}}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{i}}}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2v_{i}}\right)\left(\frac{y_{1}}{v_{1}}\right)^{k}\,dy_{1}dy_{2}\cdots dy_{n},

where we applied Fubini in the last step since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|g(y)|\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2v_{i}}\right)\frac{1}{k!}\left|\frac{xy_{1}}{v_{1}}\right|^{k}\,dy_{1}dy_{2}\cdots dy_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2v_{i}}+\frac{|xy_{1}|}{v_{1}}\right)\,dy_{1}dy_{2}\cdots dy_{n}<\infty.

This implies an infinite radius of convergence for

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2v_{1}}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v_{i}}}\right)\times\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2v_{i}}\right)\left(\frac{y_{1}}{v_{1}}\right)^{k}\,dy_{1}dy_{2}\cdots dy_{n},

i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[g(x+W_{t_{1}},x+W_{t_{2}},\ldots,x+W_{t_{n}})]
is equal to a power series with an infinite radius of convergence. So it is entire in
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

In this section, we use the condition that the extension

Proof of Theorem

Appendix B
A small remark on the complex logarithm

For complex-valued functions such as
F
in (
3.1
), the definition of
\log F
is not trivial, especially if the range of
F
is not simply connected. We use the lifting property in algebraic topology.

Lemma B.1
.

Suppose
f:I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
is a continuous function, where
I
is an interval of
\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists a continuous function
g:I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\exp(g)=f
. If the value of
g
at one point is specified, then
g
is fully specified, i.e., there exists only one
g
that satisfies these conditions in this case.

Proof.

Notice that
p=\exp(z)
is a covering projection from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, i.e., a local homeomorphism, and
I\times\mathbb{R}
is both path-connected and locally path-connected and has a trivial fundamental group. Then by
Hatcher [27, Proposition 1.33]
, for the continuous function
f:I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, there exists a continuous function
g:I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
f=p\circ g=\exp(g)
. Furthermore, the domain
I\times\mathbb{R}
is connected. Therefore by the uniqueness property
[
27
, Proposition 1.34]

g
is uniquely specified once its value at a single point is given.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
f:I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
be a continuous function, where
I
is an interval of
\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
f
is
C^{1}
in the first variable
t
and
C^{\infty}
in the second variable
x
. Then the function
g
defined in Lemma
B.1
is also
C^{1}
in
t
and
C^{\infty}
in
x
.

Proof.

Suppose we define
g
as in Lemma
B.1
. We only need to prove that for each fixed
(t_{0},x_{0})\in I\times\mathbb{R}
,
g
is
C^{1}
in
t
and
C^{\infty}
in
x
in a neighborhood of
(t_{0},x_{0})
. Since
f(t_{0},x_{0})\neq 0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that for all
(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}
with
|t-t_{0}|+|x-x_{0}|<\delta
, we have
|f(t,x)-f(t_{0},x_{0})|<|f(t_{0},x_{0})|
. So for all such
(t,x)
,
f(t,x)
is contained in an open ball
B
with center
f(t_{0},x_{0})
and radius
|f(t_{0},x_{0})|
. In particular,
B
is simply connected and does not contain
0
. We define
h:B\to\mathbb{C}
as

h(z)=\int_{\ell_{f(t_{0},x_{0}),z}}\frac{1}{w}\,dw+g(t_{0},x_{0}),

where
\ell_{f(t_{0},x_{0}),z}
is the line segment between
f(t_{0},x_{0})
and
z
. We aim to prove that
g(t,x)=h(f(t,x))
for all
(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}
with
|t-t_{0}|+|x-x_{0}|<\delta
.

By the definition of
h
,
h^{\prime}(z)=1/z
; thus
(\exp(h(z))/z)^{\prime}=0
which yields that
\exp(h(z))/z
is constant on
B
. In addition,

\frac{\exp(h(f(t_{0},x_{0})))}{f(t_{0},x_{0})}=\frac{\exp(g(t_{0},x_{0}))}{f(t_{0},x_{0})}=1.

So
\exp(h(z))=z
and thus
\exp(h(f(t,x)))=f(t,x)=\exp(g(t,x))
for all
(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}
with
|t-t_{0}|+|x-x_{0}|<\delta
. Thus
h(f(t,x))-g(t,x)\in\{2k\pi i:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Notice again that
h(f(t_{0},x_{0}))-g(t_{0},x_{0})=0
, and by continuity, we have
g(t,x)=h(f(t,x))
for all
(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}
with
|t-t_{0}|+|x-x_{0}|<\delta
. Notice that
h^{\prime}(z)=1/z
; so
h
is holomorphic on
B
. Therefore
h
is
C^{\infty}
on
B
when regarding
B
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then as a composition of
f
and
h
,
g
is also
C^{1}
in
t
and
C^{\infty}
in
x
.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
be an entire function on
\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
a continuous function with
\exp(g(x))=f(x)
. Then
g
is entire on
\mathbb{R}
if and only if the extension
f_{c}
of
f
to the complex plane as in Definition
3.12
does not vanish on
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

“
\Leftarrow
”: Assume that
f_{c}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
does not vanish. Then for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we can define

h(z)=\int_{\ell_{0,z}}\frac{f_{c}^{\prime}(w)}{f_{c}(w)}\,dw+g(0),

where
\ell_{0,z}
is the line segment between
0
and
z
. It follows that
h^{\prime}(z)=f_{c}^{\prime}(z)/f_{c}(z)
, showing that
h
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, for
F(z)=f_{c}(z)\exp(-h(z))
, we know that

F(0)=f_{c}(0)\exp\bigl(-h(0)\bigr)=f(0)\exp\bigl(-g(0)\bigr)=1,\qquad F^{\prime}(z)=0.

Thus
F(z)=1
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\exp\bigl(g(x)-h(x)\bigr)=\exp\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\exp\bigl(-h(x)\bigr)=f(x)\exp\bigl(-h(x)\bigr)=F(x)=1.

Thus there exists a
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
g(x)-h(x)=2k\pi i
. Because
h(0)=g(0)
, we obtain
g(x)=h(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
h
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}
; so it is entire on
\mathbb{C}
and thus on
\mathbb{R}
, and then so is
g
.

“
\Rightarrow
”: Assume
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is entire on
\mathbb{R}
. Then it can be written in the form of a power series
g(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
with an infinite radius of convergence. Then
g_{c}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
is entire on
\mathbb{C}
, and so is
\exp(g_{c}(z))
. Notice that
\exp(g_{c}(z))
and
f_{c}(z)
are both entire on
\mathbb{C}
, and
\exp(g_{c}(x))=\exp(g(x))=f(x)=f_{c}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Since the zeros of an entire function are isolated, except for the zero function, we have that
f_{c}(z)=\exp(g_{c}(z))
, and hence
f_{c}(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
∎

Remark B.4
.

We point out that for a real entire function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
that does not vanish on
\mathbb{R}
, there exists a continuous function
\log f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\exp(\log f)=f
, (choosing
I=[0,0]=\{0\}
in Lemma
B.1
). However,
\log f
is not necessarily entire on
\mathbb{R}
. For instance, the function
f(x)=1+ix
is entire on
\mathbb{R}
and does not vanish on
\mathbb{R}
. However,
\log f=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+x^{2})+\arctan(x)i+2k\pi i
,

, is no longer entire on
\mathbb{R}
. In fact, the Taylor series at
x=0
for both
\log(1+x^{2})
and
\arctan x
have a finite convergence radius of

(can be checked easily) and not
\infty
.

Definition B.5
.

If the joint characteristic functional
F(t,x)
in (
3.1
) is continuous on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
and

, we define
\log F
via Lemma

as the function

such that
\exp(g)=F
and

.

Remark B.6
.

Notice that by the definition of
F
in (

),

. So we should have

. In particular, we can choose
