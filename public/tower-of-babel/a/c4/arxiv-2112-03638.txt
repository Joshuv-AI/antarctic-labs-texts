Scaling Structured Inference with Randomization
Yao Fu, John P. Cunningham, Mirella Lapata

Abstract
Deep discrete structured models have seen considerable progress recently, but traditional inference using dynamic programming (DP) typically works with a small number of states (less than hundreds), which severely limits model capacity. At the same time, across machine learning, there is a recent trend of using randomized truncation techniques to accelerate computations involving large sums. Here, we propose a family of randomized dynamic programming (RDP) algorithms for scaling structured models to tens of thousands of latent states. Our method is widely applicable to classical DP-based inference (partition function, marginal, reparameterization, entropy) and different graph structures (chains, trees, and more general hypergraphs). It is also compatible with automatic differentiation: it can be integrated with neural networks seamlessly and learned with gradient-based optimizers. Our core technique approximates the sum-product by restricting and reweighting DP on a small subset of nodes, which reduces computation by orders of magnitude. We further achieve low bias and variance via Rao-Blackwellization and importance sampling. Experiments over different graphs demonstrate the accuracy and efficiency of our approach. RDP can also be used to learn a structured variational autoencoder with a scaled inference network which outperforms baselines in terms of test likelihood and successfully prevents collapse.

Within our randomization framework,

We thus recast the computation

(3) statistically principled controllability of bias and variance.
As a concrete application, we show that RDP can be used for learning a structured VAE with a scaled inference network.
In experiments, we first demonstrate that RDP algorithms estimate

(

(

Sum-Product Recap
Learning models in Eq.

Suppose

\displaystyle q_{\theta}({\bm{X}})\propto\exp\left\{\sum_{s\in V}\phi_{s}(x_{s})+\sum_{(s,t)\in E}\phi_{st}(x_{s},x_{t})\right\}

\displaystyle\mu_{ts}(x_{s})\propto\sum_{x_{t}^{\prime}=1}^{N}\Big\{\phi_{st}(x_{s},x_{t}^{\prime})\phi_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime})\prod_{u\in V_{t}^{s}}\mu_{ut}(x_{t}^{\prime})\Big\}

then used in gradient based updates of the potentials

(detailed
in Sec.

this approach becomes exact and convergence is linear in the

Challenges in Scaling
The challenge is how to scale the sum-product computation for
q_{\theta}
.
Specifically, gradient-based learning requires three types of
inference: (a) partition estimation (for maximizing likelihood);
(b) re-parameterized sampling (for gradient estimation); and
(c) entropy estimation (for regularization).
Existing sum-product variants (Table
1
)
provide exact solutions, but only for a small latent space (e.g., a
linear-chain CRF with state number smaller than 100). Since the
complexity of DP-based inference is usually at least quadratic to the
size of the latent states, it would induce memory overflow if we
wanted to scale it to tens of thousands.

Restrictions from Automatic Differentiation
At first sight, one may think the memory requirement for some

O(10^{9})

given multiple implementation tricks (e.g., dropping the intermediate

However, this is not the case
under automatic
differentiation
, primarily because AD requires
storing
all intermediate computations

Previous Efforts
Certainly there exist several excellent scaling

In addition to AD-compatibility restrictions, many existing techniques

Scaling Inference with Randomization

In this section, we introduce our randomized dynamic programming
algorithms. We first introduce a generic randomized sum-product framework
for approximating the partition function of any graph structure.
Then we instantiate this framework on
two classical structures, chains and hypertrees, respectively.
When running the randomized sum-product in a left-to-right (or bottom-up) order,
we get a randomized version of the classicial Forward (or Inside) algorithm for
estimating the partition function for chains like HMMs and Linear-chain CRFs (or hypertrees like PCFGs and Dependency CRFs in
Kim et al. 2019
).
We call these two algorithms
first-order RDPs
because they run
the computation graph in one pass. Next, we generalize RDP to two
more inference operations (beyond partition estimation): entropy
estimation (for the purposes of regularized training) and
reparameterization (for gradient-based optimization). We collectively
use the term
second-order RDPs
to refer to entropy and
reparameterization algorithms because they call first-order RDPs as a
subroutine and run a second pass of computation upon the first-order
graphs.
We will compose these algorithms into a structured VAE in
Sec.
4
.

3.1
Framework: Randomized Sum-Product

Our key technique for scaling the sum-product is a

randomized message estimator
.
Specifically, for each node
X_{t}
, given a pre-constructed proposal
{\bm{q}}_{t}=[q_{t}(1),...,q_{t}(N)]

\displaystyle[\sigma_{t,1},...,\sigma_{t,K_{1}},...,\sigma_{t,N}]=\arg\text{sort}\{q_{t}(i)\}_{i=1}^{N}

(5)

\displaystyle\delta_{t,j}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Categorical}\{q_{t}(\sigma_{t,K_{1}+1}),...,q_{t}(\sigma_{t,N})\}

(6)

\displaystyle\Omega_{t}^{K_{1}}=\{\sigma_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{K_{1}}\quad\quad\Omega_{t}^{K_{2}}=\{\delta_{t,j}\}_{j=1}^{K_{2}}

(7)

Then, we substitute the full index
\{1,...,N\}
with the the top

\Omega_{t}^{K_{1}}
and the sampled index
\Omega_{t}^{K_{2}}

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{ts}(x_{s})\propto\sum_{\sigma_{t}\in\Omega_{t}^{K_{1}}}\Big\{\phi_{st}(x_{s},\sigma_{t})\phi_{t}(\sigma_{t})\prod_{u\in V_{t}^{s}}\hat{\mu}_{ut}(\sigma_{t})\Big\}+

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta_{t}\in\Omega_{t}^{K_{2}}}\Big\{\frac{1}{K_{2}q_{t}(\delta_{t})}\phi_{st}(x_{s},\delta_{t})\phi_{t}(\delta_{t})\prod_{u\in V_{t}^{s}}\hat{\mu}_{ut}(\delta_{t})\Big\}

Here indices are pre-computed outside DP.

We now show that estimator Eq.

can be interpreted as a combination of Rao-Blackwellization (RB) and importance sampling (IS).
Firstly, RB says that a function

depending on two random variables
X,Y
has larger variance than its conditional expectation
\hat{J}(X)=\mathbb{E}[J(X,Y)|X]

(
Ranganath et al. 2014
)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{J}(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}[J(X,Y)]-\mathbb{E}[(J(X,Y)-\hat{J}(X))^{2}]

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{V}[J(X,Y)]

(10)

This is because
Y
has been integrated out and the source of randomness is
reduced to
X
.
Now let
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
be uniform random indices taking values from
\Omega_{t}^{K_{1}}
and
\Omega_{t}^{K_{2}}
, respectively.
Consider a simple unbiased estimate
S
for the message
\mu_{st}

\displaystyle m_{st}(i)

\displaystyle=\phi_{st}(x_{s},i)\phi_{t}(i)\prod_{u\in V_{t}^{s}}\mu_{ut}(i)

(11)

\displaystyle S(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

\displaystyle=K_{1}m_{st}(\delta_{1})+(N-K_{1})m_{st}(\delta_{2})

(12)

The conditional expectation
\hat{S}(\delta_{2})=\mathbb{E}[S(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})|\delta_{2}]
is:

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\delta_{2})=\sum_{\sigma_{t}\in\Omega_{t}^{K_{1}}}m_{st}(\sigma_{t})+(N-K_{1})m_{st}(\delta_{2})

(13)

Plugging
S(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}),\hat{S}(\delta_{2})
in Eq.
10
, we get
variance reduction:

\mathbb{V}[\hat{S}(\delta_{2})]\leq\mathbb{V}[S(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})]
.
Note that the variance will becomes smaller as

becomes larger.
Now change
\delta_{2}
in Eq.
13
to from the proposal in Eq.
6
then take expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{2}\sim\text{Uniform}(\Omega_{t}^{K_{2}})}[(N-K_{1})m_{st}(\delta_{2})]

(14)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{2}\sim{\bm{q}}_{t}}[\frac{1}{q_{t}(\delta_{2})(N-K_{1})}(N-K_{1})m_{st}(\delta_{2})]

(15)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{2}\sim{\bm{q}}_{t}}[\frac{1}{q_{t}(\delta_{2})}m_{st}(\delta_{2})]

This is an instance of importance sampling where the importance weight is

Variance can be reduced if

3.3
Second-Order Randomized DP

We now generalize RDP from partition function estimation to two more inference operations: entropy and reparameterized sampling.
When composing graphical models with neural networks, entropy is usually required for regularization, and reparameterized sampling is required for Monte-Carlo gradient estimation
(
Mohamed et al. 2020
)
.
Again, we call our methods
second-order
because they
reuse the computational graph and intermediate outputs of first-order RDPs.
We focus on chain structures (HMMs and linear-chain CRFs) for simplicity.

3.3.1
Randomized Entropy DP

Algorithm

It reuses the chosen index

Again, all computation is differentiable, which means that the

3.3.2

When training VAEs with a structured inference network, one usually requires differentiable samples from the inference network.

4
Scaling Structured VAE with RDP

We study a concrete structured VAE example that uses our RDP
algorithms for scaling. We focus on the language domain, however our
method generalizes to other types of sequences and structures. We will use
the randomized Gumbel backward sampling algorithm for gradient estimation and the
randomized Entropy DP for regularization.

Generative Model
Let
{\bm{x}}=[x_{1},...,x_{T}]
be a sequence of discrete latent states, and
{\bm{y}}=[y_{1},...,y_{T}]
a sequence of observed words (a word is an observed categorical variable).
We consider an autoregressive generative model parameterized by an LSTM decoder:

\displaystyle p_{\psi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\prod_{t}p_{\psi}(x_{t}|x_{<t},y_{<t})\cdot p_{\psi}(y_{t}|x_{t},x_{<t},y_{<t})

\displaystyle p_{\psi}(x_{t}|x_{<t},y_{<t})=\mathrm{softmax}(\text{MLP}(\text{LSTM}(x_{<t},y_{<t})))

\displaystyle p_{\psi}(y_{t}|x_{t},x_{<t},y_{<t})=\mathrm{softmax}(\text{MLP}(\text{LSTM}(x_{1:t},y_{<t})))

Inference Model
Since the posterior is intractable, we
use a structured inference model parameterized by a CRF with a neural
encoder:

\displaystyle q_{\theta}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})=\prod_{t}\Phi(x_{t-1},x_{t})\phi(x_{t},y_{t})/Z

(29)

where
\Phi
is the
N\times N
transition potential matrix and
\phi
is the emission potential.
This formulation has been previously used in sequence tagging and
sentence generation
(
Ammar et al. 2014
;
Li & Rush 2020
)
for a small
N
. With large
N
,
parametrization and inference require careful treatment.
Specifically, we firstly use a neural encoder to map
{\bm{y}}
to a set of contextualized representations:

\displaystyle[{\bm{r}}_{1},...,{\bm{r}}_{T}]=\text{Enc}(y_{1},...,y_{t})

(30)

As direct parametrization of an
N\times N
transition matrix would be memory expensive when
N
is large (e.g., when
N=10^{5}
), we decompose it by associating each state
i
with a state embedding
{\bm{e}}_{i}
.
Then, the potentials are:

\displaystyle\Phi(x_{t-1},x_{t})={\bm{e}}_{x_{t-1}}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{x_{t}}\quad\quad\phi(x_{t},y_{t})={\bm{e}}_{x_{t}}^{\intercal}{\bm{r}}_{t}

(31)

Training uses the following format of ELBO:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\theta}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})}[\log p_{\psi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})]+H(q_{\theta}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}}))

(32)

where the entropy (and its gradients) is estimated by Alg.
3
.
Stochastic gradients of the first term is obtained by differentiating samples from randomized gumbel sampling:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\theta}}[\log p_{\psi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})]\approx\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\psi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}(\theta),{\bm{y}}),\tilde{{\bm{x}}}(\theta)\sim\text{Alg.~\ref{alg:gumbel_ffbs}}

(33)

note that the reparameterized sample
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}(\theta)
is a function of
\theta
.
Equation
33
gives low-variance gradients of the inference network, which is ready for gradient-based optimization.

Proposal Construction
we consider the following:

\displaystyle q_{t}(i)=\frac{\phi(i,y_{t})}{2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi(j,y_{t})}+\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{e}}_{i}\right\rVert_{1}}{2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lVert{\bm{e}}_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}

(34)

which consists of: (a). local weights (the first term is the normalized
local emissions) where the intuitions is that states with larger local weights are more
likely to be observed and (b). a global prior (the second
term) where the intuition is that larger norms are usually
associated with large dot products, and thus larger potentials.
Note that there is no single fixed recipe of proposal construction since it is usually related to specific parameterization.
For general structured models,
we recommend considering utilizing any information that is potentially correlated with the actual summands, like the local and global weights we consider here.

Exploration-Exploitation Tradeoff
It is important to note that gradients only pass through the top
K_{1}

indices
\Omega_{t}^{K_{1}}
and tail samples
\Omega_{t}^{K_{2}}
during
training. This means that
increasing
K_{1}
leads to exploiting states that are already confident enough while
increasing
K_{2}
leads to exploring less confident states. So the ratio
K_{1}/K_{2}
also represents an exploration-exploitation tradeoff when
searching for meaningful posterior structures.
We will further see its effect in the experiments.

Experimental Setting

orders of magnitudes larger than previous
work
(
Wiseman et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2021
)
. We run RDPs

, and

)

(

;

)

6
Conclusion

This work proposes
the randomized sum-product
algorithms for scaling structured models to tens of thousands of
latent states. Our method is widely applicable to classical DP-based
inference
and different graph structures.
Our methods reduce computation complexity by orders of
magnitude compared to existing alternatives and is compatible with
automatic differentiation and modern neural networks. We hope this
method will open new possibilities of large-scale deep structured
prediction models.

Appendix A
Biasedness Analysis of Randomized Forward

In this section, we discuss the unbiasedness and variance of the Randomized Forward algorithm.
We first show that Randomized Forward gives an unbiased estimator of the partition function.

Theorem A.1

(Unbiasedness)
.

For all
t\in[1,2,...,T]
, the sampled sum
\hat{\alpha}_{t}
(Eq.
17
) is an unbiased estimator of the forward variable
\alpha_{t}
.
The final sampled sum
\hat{Z}
is an unbiased estimator of the partition function
Z
.

Proof.

By the Second Principle of Mathematical Induction. Assume initialization
\alpha_{1}(i)=\phi(x_{1},i)
. Firstly at
t=2
, for all
i
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{2}(i)]=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}}\alpha_{1}(\sigma_{1,j})\Phi(\sigma_{1,j},i)\phi(x_{2},i)+\frac{1}{K_{2}}\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{K_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}}\bigg[\frac{\tilde{Z}_{1}}{\tilde{q}_{1}(\delta_{1,j})}\alpha_{1}(\delta_{1,j})\Phi(\delta_{1,j})\phi(x_{2},i)}_{=A}\bigg]

(35)

where the second term can be expanded as a masked summation with the index rearranged from
\sigma_{2,K_{1}+1}
to
\sigma_{2,N}
:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}}\bigg[\frac{\tilde{Z}_{1}}{\tilde{q}_{1}(\delta_{1,j})}\alpha_{1}(\delta_{1,j})\Phi(\delta_{1,j})\phi(x_{2},i)\bigg]

(36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{2}}\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}}\bigg[\frac{1}{q_{1}(\delta_{1,k})}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{1,k}=j)\alpha_{1}(\sigma_{1,j})\Phi(\sigma_{1,j})\phi(x_{2},i)\bigg]

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{2}}\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}}\bigg[\frac{1}{q_{1}(\delta_{1,k})}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{1,k}=j)\bigg]}_{=1}\alpha_{1}(\sigma_{1,j})\Phi(\sigma_{1,j})\phi(x_{2},i)

(38)

\displaystyle=K_{2}\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\alpha_{1}(\sigma_{1,j})\Phi(\sigma_{1,j})\phi(x_{2},i)

(39)

Notice how we re-index the sum. Now put it back to Eq.
35
to get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{2}(i)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}}\alpha_{1}(\sigma_{1,j})\Phi(\sigma_{1,j},i)\phi(x_{2},i)+\frac{1}{K_{2}}\cdot K_{2}\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\alpha_{1}(\sigma_{1,j})\Phi(\sigma_{1,j})\phi(x_{2},i)

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{1}(\sigma_{1,j})\Phi(\sigma_{,j},i)\phi(x_{2},i)

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{1}(j)\Phi(j,i)\phi(x_{2},i)

(42)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}(i)

(43)

This verifies the induction foundation that
\mathbb{E}_{q_{1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{2}(i)]=\alpha_{2}(i)
for all
i
.

Now assume for all time index up to
t
we have:

\displaystyle\forall i,\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(i)]=\alpha_{t}(i)

(44)

Consider
t+1
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1:q_{t}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:{t-1}}}\big[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\sigma_{t,j})\big]\Phi(\sigma_{t,j},i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)

(45)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{K_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}\big[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\delta_{t,j})\big]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\big[\frac{\tilde{Z}_{t}}{\tilde{q}_{t}(\delta_{t,j})}\Phi(\delta_{t,j})\phi(x_{t+1},i)]\big]

(46)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}}\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{t,j})\Phi(\sigma_{t,j},i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)

(47)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{K_{2}}\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{K_{2}}\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{t,j})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\big[\frac{\tilde{Z}_{t}}{\tilde{q}_{t}(\delta_{t,j})}\Phi(\delta_{t,j})\phi(x_{t+1},i)]\big]}_{=A^{\prime}}

(48)

Note how we decompose the expectation by using the independence:
q_{1:t}=q_{1:t-1}\cdot q_{t}

With a similar masked summation trick as
A
, we have:

\displaystyle A^{\prime}=K_{2}\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{t,j})\Phi(\sigma_{t,j},i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)

(49)

This gives us:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1:q_{t}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}}\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{t,j})\Phi(\sigma_{t,j},i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)+\frac{1}{K_{2}}\cdot K_{2}\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{t,j})\Phi(\sigma_{t,j},i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)

(50)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{t}(\sigma_{t,j})\Phi(\sigma_{t,j},i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)

(51)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{t}(j)\Phi(j,i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)

(52)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t+1}(i)

(53)

Thus showing
\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}
is also an unbiased estimator for
\alpha_{t+1}
at each step
t+1
.
Setting
t=T
, the last step, gives us
\mathbb{E}[\hat{Z}]=Z
(details similar to the above).
∎

Corollary A.2
.

When we change the (sum, product) semiring to the (log-sum-exp, sum) semiring, the expectation of the estimator will become a lower bound of
\log\alpha_{t}
and
\log Z
.

Proof.

Denote
l_{t}(i)=\log\alpha_{t}(i)
and
\Omega
the set of sampled indices where
|\Omega|=K_{2}
. For simplicity, we omit the top
K_{1}
summation and only show the summation of the sample.
Cases where
t>2
can be derived similarly as following:

\displaystyle\hat{l}_{2}(i)

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{j\in\Omega}\exp(l_{1}(j)+\log\Phi(j,i)+\log\phi(x_{t},i))-\log K_{2}

(54)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{l}_{2}(i)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\sum_{j\in\Omega}\exp(l_{1}(j)+\log\Phi(j,i)+\log\phi(x_{t},i))\bigg]-\log K_{2}

(55)

\displaystyle\leq\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{j\in\Omega}\exp(l_{1}(j)+\log\Phi(j,i)+\log\phi(x_{t},i))\bigg]-\log K_{2}

(56)

\displaystyle=\log K_{2}-\log K_{2}+\log\sum_{j\in\Omega}\exp(l_{1}(j)+\log\Phi(j,i)+\log\phi(x_{t},i))

(57)

\displaystyle=l_{2}(i)

(58)

where Eq.
56
comes from Jensen’s inequality.
Then by induction one can show at everystep, we have
\mathbb{E}[\hat{l}_{t}(i)]\leq l_{t}(i)
.
∎

Although implementation in the log space makes the estimate biased, it reduces the variance exponentially in a rather trivial way.
It also provides numerical stability.
So, in practice we use it for training.

Appendix B
Variance Analysis of Randomized Forward

Now we analyze variance. We start with the estimator
a_{\delta}/q_{\delta}
,
\delta\sim\text{Categorical}\{q_{K_{1}+1},...q_{K_{N}}\}
in Eq.
4
. Firstly, this is an unbiased estimator of the tail sum:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[a_{\delta}/q_{\delta}]=\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}

(59)

We have the folling variance arguments:

Theorem B.1

(Variance of the tail estimator)
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[a_{\delta}/q_{\delta}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}/q_{i}-(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i})^{2}

(60)

\displaystyle=S_{K_{1}}^{2}(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}^{2}/q_{i}+2\epsilon_{i})

(61)

where

\displaystyle S_{K_{1}}=\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}\quad\quad\quad\quad\epsilon_{i}=a_{i}/S_{K_{1}}-q_{i}

(62)

which says the variance is:

•

quadratic to the tail sum
S_{K_{1}}
, which will can be reduced by increasing
K_{1}
as the effect of Rao-Blackwellization.

•

approximately quadratic to the gap
\epsilon_{i}
, as the differences between the proposal
q_{i}
and the oracle
a_{i}/S_{K_{1}}
, which can be reduced by choosing a correlated proposal as the effect of importance sampling.

Optimal zero variance is achieved on

\displaystyle q_{i}^{*}=a_{i}/S_{K_{1}}

(63)

Proof.

The variance is then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[a_{\delta}/q_{\delta}]=\mathbb{E}[(a_{\delta}/q_{\delta})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[a_{\delta}/q_{\delta}]^{2}

(64)

where the first term is the second moment:

\displaystyle(a_{\delta}/q_{\delta})^{2}

\displaystyle=(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}\mathbbm{1}[\delta=i]/q_{i})^{2}

(65)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\delta=i]/q_{i}^{2}+\underbrace{2\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\frac{a_{i}a_{j}\mathbbm{1}[\delta=i]\mathbbm{1}[\delta=j]}{q_{i}q_{j}}}_{=0}

(66)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\delta=i]/q_{i}^{2}

(67)

taking the expection of it we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(a_{\delta}/q_{\delta})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\delta=i]/q_{i}^{2}]

(68)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}/q_{i}

(69)

Plug this back, variance is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[a_{\delta}/q_{\delta}]=\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}/q_{i}-(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i})^{2}

(70)

To get the optimal proposal, we solve the constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{q_{i}}\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}/q_{i}-(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i})^{2}

(71)

\displaystyle s.t.\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}q_{i}=1

(72)

By solving the corresponding Lagrangian equation system (omitted here), we get the optimal value achieved at:

\displaystyle q_{i}^{*}=\frac{a_{i}}{\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}/q_{i}^{*}-(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}a_{i})^{2}=0

(73)

This says zero variance is achieved by a proposal equal to the normalized summands.

Then define the gap between the proposal and the normalized summands as:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{S_{K_{1}}}-q_{i}

(74)

Plug this to the variance expression we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[a_{\delta}/q_{\delta}]=S_{K_{1}}^{2}(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}^{2}/q_{i}+2\epsilon_{i})

(75)

∎

Corollary B.2
.

When increasing the sample size to
K_{2}
, the variance will reduce to

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\frac{1}{K_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{a_{\delta_{j}}}{q_{\delta_{j}}}]=\frac{1}{K_{2}}S_{K_{1}}^{2}(\sum_{i=K_{1}+1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}^{2}/q_{i}+2\epsilon_{i})

(76)

Now we consider the variance of the Sampled Forward algorithm.
An exact computation would give complicated results.
For simplification, we give an asymptotic argument with regard to Rao-Blackwellization and importance sampling:

Theorem B.3

(Single Step Asymptotic Variance of Sampled Forward)
.

At each step, the alpha varible estimator has the following asymptotic variance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(i)]=O\Big(\frac{1}{K_{2}}\alpha^{2}_{t,K_{1}}(i)\cdot\epsilon^{2}_{t}(i)\Big)

(77)

where:

•

\alpha_{t+1,K_{1}}(i)=\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}(j,i)=\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}[\hat{\alpha}^{2}_{t}(j)]}\Phi(j,i)\phi(x_{t},i)
is a tail sum after the top
K_{1}
summands. This term will reduce if we increase
K_{1}
, as an instance of Rao-Blackwellization.

•

\epsilon^{2}_{t}(i)=\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\epsilon^{2}_{t-1}(j,i)/q_{t-1}(j)
and
\epsilon_{t-1}(j,i)
is the difference between the proposal
q_{t-1}(j)
and the oracle proposal. This term will reduce if the proposal is more correlated to the oracle, as an instance of Importance Sampling.

Proof.

We start with a simple setting where
K_{2}=1
. At step
t+1
we have the following variance recursion:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t}}[\hat{\alpha}^{2}_{t+1}(i)]=\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\frac{\Phi^{2}(j,i)\phi^{2}(x_{t+1},i)}{q_{t}(j)}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{2}(j)]

(78)

This is derived by plugging estimator
17
to the variance Eq.
60
we have just derived. Denote:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1,K_{1}}(i)=\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}(j,i)=\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}[\hat{\alpha}^{2}_{t}(j)]}\Phi(j,i)\phi(x_{t+1},i)

(79)

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{q_{1:t}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]=\alpha^{2}_{t+1,K_{1}}(i)\big(\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(i)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)\big)

(80)

where
\epsilon_{t}(j,i)
is the differences between the proposal and the normalized exact summands at step
t
state
i
:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}(j,i)=\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{t}(j,i)}{\sum_{j=K_{1}=1}^{N}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}(j,i)}-q_{t}(j)

(81)

Dropping out the first order errors and increasing the number of sample to
K_{2}
, we have the asymptotics:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(i)]=O\Big(\frac{1}{K_{2}}\alpha^{2}_{t,K_{1}}(i)\cdot\epsilon^{2}_{t}(i)\Big)

(82)

∎

Theorem B.4

(Asymptotic Variance of Sampled Forward Partition Estimation)
.

The alpha variable estimators has the following asymptotic variance recurrsion:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]=O(\frac{1}{K_{2}}\cdot\phi_{t,K_{1}}^{2}\cdot\epsilon_{t,K_{1}}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}])

(83)

Compared with Eq.
77
, this expression:

•

Uses the product of the factors
\phi_{t,K_{1}}
(a function of the sum-prod of the factor at step
t
) and the previous step variance
\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}]
to substitute the
\alpha_{t,K_{1}}
in equation
77
. Again, this term will decrease with a larger
K_{1}
(Rao-Blackwellization).

•

\epsilon^{2}_{t,K_{1}}(i)=\sum_{j=K_{1}+1}^{N}\epsilon^{2}_{t-1}(j,i)/q_{t-1}(j)
and
\epsilon_{t-1}(j,i)
is the difference between the proposal
q_{t-1}(j)
and the oracle proposal. This term will reduce if the proposal is more correlated to the oracle, as an instance of Importance Sampling (same as Eq.
77
).

Consequently, the partition function has the following asymptotic variance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{Z}]=O(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{K_{2}}\cdot\phi_{t,K_{1}}^{2}\cdot\epsilon_{t,K_{1}}^{2})

(84)

When implemented in the log space, the variance is trivially reduced exponentially:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\log\hat{Z}]=O(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{1}{K_{2}}+2\log\phi_{t,K_{1}}+2\log\epsilon_{t,K_{1}})

(85)

Proof.

Firstly for simplcity we assume
K_{1}=0
and
K_{2}=1
. The the estimator variance is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}^{2}(i)]-\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)

(86)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{2}(j)\Phi^{2}(j,i)\phi^{2}(x_{t},i)}{q_{t}(j)}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}\bigg]-\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)

(87)

Recall:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{t}(j)\phi(j,i)\phi(x_{t},i)

(88)

Let:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}(j,i)=\underbrace{\frac{\alpha_{t}(j)\phi(j,i)\phi(x_{t},i)}{\alpha_{t+1}(i)}}_{=q(z_{t}=j|z_{t+1}=i)}-q_{t}(j)

(89)

Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)\big(\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(j)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)+q_{t}(j)\big)\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{2}(j)}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}\bigg]-\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)

(90)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{2}(j)]}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}q_{t}(j)\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(j)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)\big)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{2}(j)]}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}\bigg)

(91)

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)

(92)

Note that there exist
J_{t}
such that:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q_{1:t-1}}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{2}(j)]}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}<=J_{t}

(93)

This is because of the bounded gap of the Jensen’s inequality. Also recall:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{t}(j)=1

(94)

So we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]

\displaystyle<=\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)\bigg(J_{t}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(j)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)\big)\cdot\big(\frac{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(j)]}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}-1\big)\bigg)

(95)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)\bigg(J_{t}-1-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(j)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)\big)\bigg)

(96)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(j)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)\big)\cdot\big(\frac{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(j)]}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}\big)\bigg)

(97)

empirically
J_{t}
is not the dominate source of variance (but could be in the worst case, depending on the tightness of Jensen’s inequality). We focus on the second term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]

\displaystyle=O\bigg(\alpha_{t+1}^{2}(i)\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(j)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)\big)\cdot\big(\frac{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(j)]}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}\big)\bigg)

(98)

\displaystyle=O\bigg(\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{t}(j)\underbrace{\Phi(j,i)\phi(x_{t},j)}_{O(\phi_{t}^{2})}\Big)^{2}\cdot\Big(\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}(j,i)}{q_{t}(j)}+2\epsilon_{t}(j,i)\big)}_{O(\epsilon_{t}^{2})}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}^{2}(j)}\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t}(j)]}_{O(\mathbb{V}[\alpha_{t}])}\Big)\bigg)

(99)

\displaystyle=O(\phi_{t}^{2}\cdot\epsilon_{t}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{V}[\alpha_{t}])

(100)

Note that a lot of higher-order sum-products are simplified here. Adding top
K_{1}
summation and increasing the sample size to
K_{2}
leads to variance reduction as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{\alpha}_{t+1}(i)]

\displaystyle=O(\frac{1}{K_{2}}\cdot\phi_{t,K_{1}}^{2}\cdot\epsilon_{t,K_{1}}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{V}[\alpha_{t}])

(101)

Recursively expand this equation we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{Z}]=O(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{K_{2}}\cdot\phi_{t,K_{1}}^{2}\cdot\epsilon_{t,K_{1}}^{2})

(102)

Chaning the implementation to the log space we reduce the variance exponentially:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\log\hat{Z}]=O(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{1}{K_{2}}+2\log\phi_{t,K_{1}}+2\log\epsilon_{t,K_{1}})

(103)

∎
