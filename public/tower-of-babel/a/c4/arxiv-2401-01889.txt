Bridging relativistic jets from black hole scales to long-term electromagnetic radiation distances: A moving-mesh general relativistic hydrodynamics code with the HLLC Riemann solver
Xiaoyi Xie ID, Alan Tsz-Lok Lam ID

Abstract
Relativistic jets accompany the collapse of massive stars, the merger of compact objects, or the accretion of gas in active galactic nuclei. They carry information about the central engine and generate electromagnetic radiation. No self-consistent simulations have been able to follow these jets from their birth at the black hole scale to the Newtonian dissipation phase, making the inference of central engine property through astronomical observations undetermined. We present the general relativistic moving-mesh framework to achieve the continuity of jet simulations throughout space and time. We implement the general relativistic extension for the moving-mesh relativistic hydrodynamic code, JET , and develop a tetrad formulation to utilize the Harten–Lax–van Leer Contact (HLLC) Riemann solver in the general relativistic moving-mesh code. The new framework is able to trace the radial movement of relativistic jets from central regions where strong gravity holds all the way to distances of jet dissipation.



I
Introduction

Relativistic collimated outflows, known as jets, are associated with many astrophysical systems of vastly different scales, from stellar to galactic and even to extra-galactic levels. Phenomena like microquasars, young stellar objects, gamma-ray bursts (GRBs), active galactic nuclei (AGN), and quasars demonstrate the prevalence of relativistic jets and highlight the ubiquity of the underlying physical processes that give rise to these phenomena.

A central aspect shared by these varied astrophysical systems is the phenomenon of accretion, in which matter is attracted and pulled into a dense celestial body, like a black hole or neutron star. As matter falls onto these objects, gravitational and magnetic forces play crucial roles in launching and collimating the relativistic jets. Studying relativistic jets across different scales provides astronomers with a unique opportunity to probe fundamental astrophysical processes and test our understanding of high-energy physics in extreme environments.

Commencing with the Penrose process
Penrose 1969
;
Penrose and Floyd 1971
, numerous theoretical investigations have been undertaken to explore jets and mass outflows near black holes. The Penrose process initially elucidates energy extraction from in-falling matter into a rotating black hole. Subsequently, the seminal work by
Blandford and Znajek 1977
(BZ) demonstrated that jet energy could be extracted from the rotational energy of large-scale magnetic fields surrounding spinning black holes. Later,
Blandford and Payne 1982
(BP) highlighted that matter could also depart from the surface of the accretion disk due to magneto-centrifugal acceleration.

One of the fundamental questions in accretion disk physics is how the angular momentum transfers within the disk. Initially,
Shakura and Sunyaev 1973
introduced the ’
\alpha
-disc’ model in a groundbreaking paper. However, the source of the ad hoc viscosity in this model remains questionable. In contrast, recent years have seen widespread acceptance of magneto-rotational instability (MRI;
Balbus and Hawley 1991
) as the primary mechanism for angular momentum transport in accretion flows.

Another fundamental question in accretion disk physics is the generation of the large poloidal magnetic field as it is pretty natural to assume a toroidal field configuration for accretion flows. To begin with, the orbital differential shear would predominantly amplify the toroidal magnetic field by the shearing of seed poloidal magnetic field, the so-called
\Omega
effect. It took simulators many years to achieve the necessary resolutions and finally report the self generation of the large-scale poloidal magnetic field in black hole accretion disk due to the
\alpha
-effect (which relies on the buoyancy and Coriolis forces to convert toroidal into poloidal magnetic flux)
Liska et al. 2020
;
Komissarov and Porth 2021
. The general mean-field dynamo theory (see, e.g.,
Moffatt 1978
;
Krause and Raedler 1980
;
Cowling 1981
;
Roberts and Soward 1992
;
Brandenburg and Subramanian 2005
) has been widely used to investigate the generation of large-scale magnetic fields from small-scale turbulence.

Recent long-term general-relativistic (GR) neutrino-radiation magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) simulations of the merger of the binary neutron star and black hole neutron star have shown that effective viscous processes,
\alpha-\Omega
magnetic dynamo can lead to the generation of large-scale magnetic field, and post-merger mass ejection
Kiuchi et al. 2023
;
Kiuchi et al. 2024
;
Hayashi et al. 2023
. The analysis of the binary neutron star (BNS) merger remnant and post-merger ejecta has been investigated in detail (see, e.g.,
Fujibayashi et al. 2018
;
Kawaguchi et al. 2021
;
Fujibayashi et al. 2023
). Still, the process of successfully launching a relativistic jet is undoubtedly complex. For a comprehensive understanding of the launching mechanism, general relativistic magnetohydrodynamic (GRMHD) simulations that integrate intricate microphysical processes are imperative. On the other hand, relativistic outflows play a pivotal role in a multitude of astronomical phenomena. For example, it has been speculated that the BNS merger remnants and relativistic ejecta are the central engines of gamma-ray bursts
Eichler et al. 1989

Woosley 1993

Understanding these electromagnetic observations requires tracking the propagation of relativistic jets and their interaction with the ambient medium for a long period of time. However, simulating the complete journey of relativistic jets and the related emission process is numerically challenging. Studies in literature split focus on various parts of the whole process. II
General relativistic hydrodynamics in a reference metric formulation

The 2D special relativistic moving-mesh hydrodynamic code
JET
adopts spherical coordinates assuming axisymmetry. The cell interfaces orthogonal to the radial direction are allowed to move radially. The code is essentially Lagrangian in the radial direction, coupled laterally by transverse flux. This setup is particularly suitable for modeling relativistic radial outflows
Duffell and MacFadyen 2011
. To minimize the modifications for the code, we derive the general relativistic hydrodynamic equations in a way that resembles the special relativistic counterparts.
In the following, we lay out the implementation steps for clarity. Despite of the axisymmetry property of the
JET

III
Tetrad Formation and the HLLC Riemann solver

To evaluate the numerical flux through cell interfaces, HLL-type (HLLE/HLLC) Riemann solvers have been designed for relativistic hydrodynamics in Minkowski spacetime
Mignone and Bodo 2005
;
Toro et al. 1994
. Most of the GRHD/GRMHD codes in the literature use HLLE Riemann solver in curved spacetime (see, e.g.
Shibata and Sekiguchi 2005
;
Font 2000
;
Cheong et al. 2021
;
Gammie et al. 2003

;
Kiuchi et al. 2022
;
Bucciantini and Del
Zanna 2011
. We follow previous works for the implementation of the HLLC Riemann solver in general relativity

;
White et al. 2016
;
White et al. 2016
;
Kiuchi et al. 2022
. The basic idea is based on the equivalence principle: physical laws in a local inertial frame of a curved spacetime have the same form as in special relativity. When we define such inertial frame, we can then use the solution of Riemann problems in a local Minkowskian frame to construct the corresponding solution in curved spacetime. The previous section derives the general relativistic hydrodynamic equations in a reference metric formulation.
For the benefit of the coming discussion, we will revert to the original formulation
Font 2000
in this section

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\left(\frac{\partial\sqrt{-g}\mathbf{F}^{0}}{\partial x^{0}}+\frac{\partial\sqrt{-g}\mathbf{F}^{i}}{\partial x^{i}}\right)=\mathbf{S}

is the integrated spatial flux across the cell interfaces
x^{i}_{\pm}
given by

\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\pm}:=\int_{\Sigma_{x^{i}_{\pm}}}\sqrt{-g}\mathbf{F}^{i}dx^{0}\prod_{j\neq i}dx^{j}

(25)

III.1
Tetrad formulation

Instead of attempting a direct resolution of the Riemann problem within the curved spacetime, our approach entails deliberately converting the left and right states at a given interface into a local Minkowskian frame of reference. This methodology enables the utilization of developments in the realm of special relativistic Riemann problems, as proposed by
Pons et al. 1998
;
Antón et al. 2006
.

To begin with, we define a new tetrad basis
e_{(\hat{\mu})}
that satisfies a list of properties as shown in
White et al. 2016
:

1.

e_{(\hat{\mu})}
must be orthogonal to
e_{(\hat{\nu})}
for all
\mu\neq\nu
.

2.

Each
e_{(\hat{\mu})}
must be normalized to have an inner product of
\pm 1
with itself, with
e_{(\hat{0})}
being timelike and
e_{(\hat{i})}
being spacelike.

3.

e_{(\hat{0})}
must be orthogonal to surfaces of constant
x^{0}
.

4.

The projection of
e_{(\hat{i})}
onto to the surfaces of constant
x^{0}
is orthogonal to the surface of constant
x^{i}
within that submanifold.

Without loss of generality,
let us only consider the conversion of the volume integral
\left(\bar{\mathbf{F}}^{0}\right)_{x^{0}}
in Eq. (
24
) and the first spatial flux integral
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{+}
in Eq. (
23
).

We define the following tetrad basis in the spherical coordinates with
x^{\mu}=(t,r,\theta,\phi)

(the detailed derivation can be found in Appendix of
White et al. 2016
;
Kiuchi et al. 2022
) as

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{t})}{}^{\mu}=n^{\mu},

(26)

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{r})}{}^{\mu}=\hat{B}\left(0,\gamma^{rr},\gamma^{r\theta},\gamma^{r\phi}\right),

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{\theta})}{}^{\mu}=\hat{D}\left(0,0,\gamma_{\phi\phi},-\gamma_{\theta\phi}\right),

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{\phi})}{}^{\mu}=\hat{C}(0,0,0,1),

where the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\hat{B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}},

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\phi\phi}}},

\displaystyle\hat{D}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\phi\phi}\left(\gamma_{\theta\theta}\gamma_{\phi\phi}-\gamma_{\theta\phi}^{2}\right)}}.

The covariant components of the tetrad basis are given by
e_{(\hat{\mu})\mu}=g_{\mu\nu}e_{(\hat{\mu})}{}^{\nu}
. Specifically

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{t})\mu}

\displaystyle=n_{\mu},

(28)

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{r})\mu}

\displaystyle=\hat{B}(\beta^{r},1,0,0),

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{\theta})\mu}

\displaystyle=\hat{D}(\beta_{\theta}\gamma_{\phi\phi}-\beta_{\phi}\gamma_{\theta\phi},\gamma_{r\theta}\gamma_{\phi\phi}-\gamma_{r\phi}\gamma_{\theta\phi}

\displaystyle,\gamma_{\theta\theta}\gamma_{\phi\phi}-\gamma_{\theta\phi}^{2},0),

\displaystyle e_{(\hat{\phi})\mu}

\displaystyle=\hat{C}\left(\beta_{\phi},\gamma_{r\phi},\gamma_{\theta\phi},\gamma_{\phi\phi}\right).

The transformation of vector and tensor between the tetrad frame and the original Eulerian observer frame follows

\displaystyle V_{(\hat{\mu})}=e_{(\hat{\mu})}{}^{\mu}V_{\mu},

(29)

\displaystyle Q_{(\hat{\mu})(\hat{\nu})}=e_{(\hat{\mu})}{}^{\mu}e_{(\hat{\nu})}{}^{\nu}Q_{\mu\nu},

and

\displaystyle V_{\mu}=e_{(\hat{\mu})\mu}V^{(\hat{\mu})},

(30)

\displaystyle Q_{\mu\nu}=e_{(\hat{\mu})\mu}e_{(\hat{\nu})\nu}Q^{(\hat{\mu})(\hat{\nu})}.

Note that the upper and lower spatial tetrad components are the same
V^{(\hat{i})}=V_{(\hat{i})}

while we have
V^{(\hat{t})}=-V_{(\hat{t})}
for temporal component
in the local Minkowskian frame
\hat{\eta}_{(\hat{\mu})(\hat{\nu})}:=e_{(\hat{\mu})}{}^{\mu}e_{(\hat{\nu})}{}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}(-1,1,1,1)
.

Therefore, we can define
\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{\mu})}
as the tetrad transformation of
\mathbf{F}^{\mu}
in the form

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{\mu})}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{F}_{D})^{(\hat{\mu})}\\
(\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{j})}})^{(\hat{\mu})}\\
(\hat{F}_{\tau})^{(\hat{\mu})}\end{pmatrix}=e^{(\hat{\mu})}{}_{\mu}\begin{pmatrix}\rho u^{\mu}\\
e_{(\hat{j})}{}^{\nu}(\rho hu^{\mu}u_{\nu}+P\delta^{\mu}_{\nu})\\
\rho hWu^{\mu}-Pn^{\mu}-\rho u^{\mu}\end{pmatrix},

(31)

Here for momentum components
(\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{j})}})^{(\hat{\mu})}
of
\mathbf{\hat{F}}^{(\hat{\mu})}
we need to perform one more tetrad transformation due to its tensorial nature.
Since we only focus on the flux along
r
direction,
the components
\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{t})}
and
\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{r})}
are written as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{t})}

\displaystyle=(D,S_{(\hat{j})},\tau),

(32a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{r})}&=(D\frac{u^{(\hat{r})}}{W},S_{(\hat{j})}\frac{u^{(\hat{r})}}{W}+P\delta^{(\hat{r})}_{(\hat{j})},(\tau+P)\frac{u^{(\hat{r})}}{W})\\
&=(D\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})},S_{(\hat{j})}\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}+P\delta^{(\hat{r})}_{(\hat{j})},(\tau+P)\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}),\end{split}

(32b)

where

\displaystyle u^{(\hat{t})}

\displaystyle=e^{(\hat{t})}{}_{\nu}u^{\nu}=-e_{(\hat{t})t}u^{t}=\alpha u^{t}=W

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{(\hat{j})}&=e_{(\hat{j})}{}^{\mu}u_{\mu}\\
&=(Wv^{r}/\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}},\hat{D}(u_{\theta}\gamma_{\phi\phi}-u_{\phi}\gamma_{\theta\phi}),\hat{C}u_{\phi})\end{split}

(34)

\displaystyle S_{(\hat{j})}

\displaystyle=\rho hWu_{(\hat{j})},

(35)

\displaystyle\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}

\displaystyle:=\frac{u^{(\hat{r})}}{W}=v^{(\hat{r})}-\beta^{(\hat{r})}/\alpha=\frac{v^{r}}{\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}},

(36)

\displaystyle\delta^{(\hat{r})}_{(\hat{j})}

\displaystyle=1\text{ for }(\hat{r})=(\hat{j}),\text{ otherwise $0$}.

(37)

The inverse transformation is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\mu}=e_{(\hat{\mu})}{}^{\mu}\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{F}_{D})^{(\hat{\mu})}\\
e^{(\hat{j})}{}_{j}(\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{j})}})^{(\hat{\mu})}\\
(\hat{F}_{\tau})^{(\hat{\mu})}\end{pmatrix},

(38)

which gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{F}_{D})^{(\hat{t})}\\
e^{(\hat{j})}{}_{j}(\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{j})}})^{(\hat{t})}\\
(\hat{F}_{\tau})^{(\hat{t})}\end{pmatrix},

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{r}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}\left[-\frac{\beta^{(\hat{r})}}{\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{F}_{D})^{(\hat{t})}\\
e^{(\hat{j})}{}_{j}(\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{j})}})^{(\hat{t})}\\
(\hat{F}_{\tau})^{(\hat{t})}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{F}_{D})^{(\hat{r})}\\
e^{(\hat{j})}{}_{j}(\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{j})}})^{(\hat{r})}\\
(\hat{F}_{\tau})^{(\hat{r})}\end{pmatrix}\right].

(40)

In addition, we can reformulate the conservation form Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) with the tetrad basis.
Note that the indexes

and

and

and transformation rule

we can get (see also

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{\hat{t}}}\sqrt{-\hat{g}}d\hat{r}d\hat{\theta}d\hat{\phi}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{t}}\frac{1}{\alpha}\sqrt{-g}drd\theta d\phi,

(43a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{\hat{r}_{\pm}}}\sqrt{-\hat{g}}d\hat{t}d\hat{\theta}d\hat{\phi}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{r_{\pm}}}\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}\sqrt{-g}dtd\theta d\phi,

where

This gives the volume integral of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\bar{\mathbf{F}}^{0}\right)_{x^{0}}&=\int_{\Sigma_{x^{0}}}\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{F}_{D})^{(\hat{t})}\\

(\hat{F}_{\tau})^{(\hat{t})}\end{pmatrix}d\hat{r}d\hat{\theta}d\hat{\phi},\end{split}

(44)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{F}}^{r}_{\pm}&=\int_{\Sigma_{r_{\pm}}}d\hat{r}d\hat{\theta}d\hat{\phi}\\
&\left[-\frac{\beta^{(\hat{r})}}{\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{F}_{D})^{(\hat{t})}\\

e^{(\hat{j})}{}_{j}(\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{j})}})^{(\hat{r})}\\
(\hat{F}_{\tau})^{(\hat{r})}\end{pmatrix}\right],\end{split}

(45)

with nonzero interface velocity

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}&:=\frac{d\hat{r}}{d\hat{t}}\\
&=\frac{\beta^{(\hat{r})}}{\alpha}=\frac{\beta^{r}}{\alpha\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}},\end{split}

from a nonzero drift in the direction of interest, in agreement with

III.2
HLLC Riemann Solver in the tetrad frame

We solve the Riemann problem in the tetrad frame by adopting a special relativity form.
We calculate the HLLC flux
{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})}
by solving the one-dimensional conservation law
Kiuchi et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\partial_{(\hat{t})}\hat{\mathbf{U}}+\partial_{(\hat{r})}{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})}=0,

(47)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{U}}

\displaystyle:={\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{t})}}=\begin{pmatrix}D\\
S_{(\hat{j})}\\
\tau\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}D\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\\
(S_{(\hat{j})}\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}+P\delta^{(\hat{r})}_{(\hat{j})})\\
(\tau+P)\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})})\end{pmatrix}.

(48)

Given an initial condition at cell interface
\hat{r}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
described by

\hat{\mathbf{U}}(\hat{r},0)=\begin{cases}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{L}&\text{ if }\hat{r}<\hat{r}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{R}&\text{ if }\hat{r}>\hat{r}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\end{cases},

(49)

three characteristic waves and four states will be established inside the Riemann fan as

\hat{\mathbf{U}}(0,\hat{t})=\begin{cases}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{L}&\text{ if }\quad\hat{\lambda}_{L}\geqslant V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{L}&\text{ if }\quad\hat{\lambda}_{L}<V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}\leqslant\hat{\lambda}^{*}\\
\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{R}&\text{ if }\quad\hat{\lambda}^{*}<V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}<\hat{\lambda}_{R}\\
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{R}&\text{ if }\quad\hat{\lambda}_{R}\leqslant V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}\end{cases},

(50)

and the corresponding numerical flux across interface
\hat{r}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is

\displaystyle\left({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})}\right)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\begin{cases}({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})})_{L}&\text{ if }\hat{\lambda}_{L}\geqslant V_{\text{interface }}^{(\hat{r})}\\
({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})})^{*}_{L}&\text{ if }\hat{\lambda}_{L}<V_{\text{interface }}^{(\hat{r})}\leqslant\hat{\lambda}^{*}\\
({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})})^{*}_{R}&\text{ if }\hat{\lambda}^{*}<V_{\text{interface }}^{(\hat{r})}<\hat{\lambda}_{R}\\
({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})})_{R}&\text{ if }\hat{\lambda}_{R}\leqslant V_{\text{interface }}^{(\hat{r})}\end{cases},

(51)

where
\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}
is the characteristic speed of the left/right going nonlinear wave
and
(\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{r})})_{L/R}:=\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{r})}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{L/R})
.
The intermediate state flux
({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})})^{*}_{L/R}
may be expressed in terms of
\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{L/R}
through the jump condition

({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})})^{*}_{L/R}=({\hat{\mathbf{F}}}^{(\hat{r})})_{L/R}+\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{L/R}-\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{L/R}).

(52)

Explicitly, we have the left or the right state as

\displaystyle D^{*}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=D\left(\hat{\lambda}-\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\right),

(53a)

\displaystyle S_{(\hat{r})}^{*}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=S_{(\hat{r})}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\right)+P^{*}-P,

(53b)

\displaystyle S_{(\hat{\theta})}^{*}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=S_{(\hat{\theta})}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\right),

(53c)

\displaystyle S_{(\hat{\phi})}^{*}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=S_{(\hat{\phi})}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\right),

(53d)

\displaystyle\tau^{*}\left(\hat{\lambda}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\tau\left(\hat{\lambda}-\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\right)+P^{*}\hat{\lambda}^{*}-P\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}.

(53e)

To reduce the number of unknowns and have a well-posed problem, we assume that
S^{*}_{(\hat{r})}=(\tau^{*}+P^{*}+D^{*})\hat{\lambda}^{*}
(see
Mignone and Bodo 2005
). If one defines
E:=\tau+D
and performs the calculation of

({\rm\ref{eq:2}})-\hat{\lambda}^{*}\times[({\rm\ref{eq:1}})+({\rm\ref{eq:5}})]
, one will get the following expression, giving
\hat{\lambda}^{*}
in terms of
P^{*}

Mignone and Bodo 2005
:

(\hat{\lambda}E-S_{(\hat{r})}+\hat{\lambda}P^{*})\hat{\lambda}^{*}=S_{(\hat{r})}(\hat{\lambda}-\bar{v}_{(\hat{r})})-P+P^{*}.

(54)

By imposing
P^{*}_{L}=P^{*}_{R}
across the contact discontinuity, we find the following quadratic equation for
\hat{\lambda}^{*}

F^{\mathrm{HLL}}_{E}(\hat{\lambda}^{*})^{2}-(E^{\mathrm{HLL}}+F^{\mathrm{HLL}}_{S_{(\hat{r})}})\hat{\lambda}^{*}+S_{(\hat{r})}^{\mathrm{HLL}}=0,

(55)

where

\displaystyle S_{(\hat{r})}^{\mathrm{HLL}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{R}S_{(\hat{r})}^{R}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}S_{(\hat{r})}^{L}+\hat{F}_{D,L}^{(\hat{r})}-\hat{F}_{D,R}^{(\hat{r})}}{\hat{\lambda}_{R}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}},

(56a)

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{HLL}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{R}E^{R}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}E^{L}+\hat{F}_{E,L}^{(\hat{r})}-\hat{F}_{E,R}^{(\hat{r})}}{\hat{\lambda}_{R}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}},

(56b)

\displaystyle F_{S_{(\hat{r})}}^{\mathrm{HLL}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{R}\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{r})},L}^{(\hat{r})}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}\hat{F}_{S_{(\hat{r})},R}^{(\hat{r})}+\hat{\lambda}_{R}\hat{\lambda}_{L}\left(S_{(\hat{r})}^{R}-S_{(\hat{r})}^{L}\right)}{\hat{\lambda}_{R}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}},

(56c)

\displaystyle F_{E}^{\mathrm{HLL}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{R}\hat{F}_{E,L}^{(\hat{r})}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}\hat{F}_{E,R}^{(\hat{r})}+\hat{\lambda}_{R}\hat{\lambda}_{L}\left(E^{R}-E^{L}\right)}{\hat{\lambda}_{R}-\hat{\lambda}_{L}},

(56d)

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{E,L/R}^{(\hat{r})}

\displaystyle:=\hat{F}_{D,L/R}^{(\hat{r})}+\hat{F}_{\tau,L/R}^{(\hat{r})}.

(56e)

Once we obtain the speed of the contact discontinuity
\hat{\lambda}^{*}
,
P^{*}
can be obtained from Eq. (
54
). The conserved quantities in the intermediate states are given by

\displaystyle D^{*}_{L/R}=\frac{D_{L/R}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}-\bar{v}_{L/R}^{(\hat{r})}\right)}{\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}},

(57a)

\displaystyle\left(S_{(\hat{j})}\right)^{*}_{L/R}=\frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}}

(57b)

\displaystyle\times\left[\left(S_{(\hat{j})}\right)_{L/R}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}-\bar{v}_{L/R}^{(\hat{r})}\right)+\left(P^{*}-P_{L/R}\right)\delta^{(\hat{r})}_{(\hat{j})}\right],

(57c)

\displaystyle E^{*}_{L/R}=\frac{E_{L/R}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}-\bar{v}_{L/R}^{(\hat{r})}\right)+P^{*}\hat{\lambda}^{*}-P_{L/R}\bar{v}_{L/R}^{(\hat{r})}}{\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}},

(57d)

\displaystyle\tau^{*}_{L/R}=E^{*}_{L/R}-D^{*}_{L/R}.

(57e)

The left and right characteristic speeds
\hat{\lambda}_{L/R}
follow Davis’s estimate
Mignone and Bodo 2005

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min(\hat{\lambda}^{-}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{L}),\hat{\lambda}^{-}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{R})),

(58)

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{R}

\displaystyle\max(\hat{\lambda}^{+}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{L}),\hat{\lambda}^{+}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{R})),

(59)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-v^{2}c_{s}^{2}}\left[\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\left(1-c_{s}^{2}\right)\right.

(60)

\displaystyle\left.\pm c_{s}\sqrt{\left(1-v^{2}\right)\left(1-v^{2}c_{s}^{2}-\left(1-c_{s}^{2}\right)\left(\bar{v}^{(\hat{r})}\right)^{2}\right)}\right],

III.3
HLLC Riemann Solver for the Moving-Mesh GR

For the moving mesh in the simulation domain, naturally, we need to solve the Riemann problem on the moving interface with its own coordinate velocity
\mathbf{V}:=\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=(V^{r},V^{\theta},V^{\phi})
. Let us denote the corresponding four-velocity as
u_{\rm{gridface}}^{\mu}
. In general, when we consider the
3+1
spacetime foliation
\Sigma_{t}
, we define a unit normal vector as
n^{\mu}
, and this unit normal vector corresponds by deﬁnition to the four-velocity of
the Eulerian observer
Alcubierre 2008
. When we define the fluid’s four-velocity as
u^{\mu}
, the velocity of the fluid with respect to the Eulerian observer (
v^{\mu}=(0,v^{i})
) has the following relation:

u^{\mu}=W(n^{\mu}+v^{\mu}),

(68)

where
W=(1-g_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu})^{-1/2}
is the Lorentz factor of the fluid with respect to the Eulerian observer.
When we move from a given hypersurface to the next following the normal direction, the change in the spatial coordinates is given as
Alcubierre 2008
:

x_{t+dt}^{i}=x_{t}^{i}-\beta^{i}dt,

(69)

\mathbf{\beta}^{\mu}=(0,\mathbf{\beta}^{i})
being the shift vector. Then

v^{i}
is related to the coordinate velocity
\mathbf{V}
by
v^{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha}(V^{i}+\beta^{i})
.
In our case, only the cell interface orthogonal to the radial direction can move with a coordinate velocity denoted as
V^{r}
.
Then the four-velocity of our radially-moving interface is

\displaystyle u_{\rm{gridface}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W\left(\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{V^{r}}{\alpha},0,0\right),

(70)

\displaystyle v^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{V^{r}+\beta^{r}}{\alpha},\frac{\beta^{\theta}}{\alpha},\frac{\beta^{\phi}}{\alpha}\right)

(71)

In the above, we illustrate the explicit definition of different velocities for clarity. For our moving-mesh code, the grid moves radially, the integral of the radial flux at a short time interval
dt
becomes

\int_{\Sigma_{r}}\sqrt{-g}(\mathbf{F}^{r}-V^{r}\mathbf{F}^{t})dtd\theta d\phi

(72)

With

V^{r}=\frac{dx^{r}}{dt}=\frac{e_{(\hat{\mu})}{}^{r}dx^{(\hat{\mu})}}{e_{(\hat{\nu})}{}^{t}dx^{(\hat{\nu})}}=-\beta^{r}+\alpha\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}V^{(\hat{r})},

(73)

Note that the above velocity equation relates to Eq. (
46
) and Eq. (
63
). From Eqs. (
39
), and (
40
), we have:

\mathbf{F}^{r}-V^{r}\mathbf{F}^{t}=\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}(\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{r})}-V^{(\hat{r})}\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{(\hat{t})})

(74)

Compared with the tetrad formulation for the static mesh, we replace the interface velocity
V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}=\frac{\beta^{r}}{\alpha\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}}
by
V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}=V^{(\hat{r})}=\frac{(\beta^{r}+V^{r})}{\alpha\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}}
to incorporate the effect of the moving interface into the flux integral.

In principle, the coordinate velocity for the moving interface can be set freely. At each instantaneous time, on the cell interface, the three characteristic waves and four states inside the Riemann fan depends only on the values of the primitive variables on the left and right sides of the interface. The interface velocity will influence which state the numerical flux across the interface will be selected [see Eqs. (
50
], and (
51
) ). Based on this flexibility, we choose the contact discontinuity velocity as the interface velocity:

V^{(\hat{r})}_{\rm{interface}}=\hat{\lambda}^{*},V^{r}_{\rm{interface}}=-\beta^{r}+\alpha\sqrt{\gamma^{rr}}\hat{\lambda}^{*}.

(75)

We find this choice performs well for the simulation of ultrarelativistic jets.

For the derivation of the tetrad formulation and HLLC Riemann solver, we express every metric and fluid variable in the coordinate basis. For the implementation, we utilize those variables in the orthonormal basis instead. For example, in the tetrad basis calculation, we will use
\gamma_{\{i\}\{j\}}=\psi^{4}\bar{\gamma}_{\{i\}\{j\}}=\psi^{4}(\delta_{\{i\}\{j\}}+\epsilon_{\{i\}\{j\}})
instead of
\gamma_{ij}
itself. In this way, the geometric factors will not directly appear in the tetrad basis calculation. The derivation itself remains the same because of the invariance of spacetime interval under coordinate transformation

ds^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=g_{\{\mu\}\{\nu\}}dx^{\{\mu\}}dx^{\{\nu\}}.

(76)

Making use of this invariance principle, we can handle the moving mesh in another way. First, boost the coordinate basis into the comoving coordinate basis of the interface:

dx^{\langle\mu\rangle}=\frac{\partial x^{\langle\mu\rangle}}{\partial x^{\nu}}dx^{\nu}=\Lambda_{\nu}{}^{\langle\mu\rangle}dx^{\nu}

(77)

Second, boost the primitive velocities into the comoving coordinate basis:

v^{\langle\mu\rangle}=\Lambda_{\nu}{}^{\langle\mu\rangle}v^{\nu}

(78)

Third, making use of the invariance, calculate the corresponding metric
g_{\langle\mu\rangle\langle\nu\rangle}
components:

ds^{2}=g_{\langle\mu\rangle\langle\nu\rangle}dx^{\langle\mu\rangle}dx^{\langle\nu\rangle}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

(79)

Once we have the new lapse, shift and spatial metric in the comoving frame, we can derive the tetrad basis in the comoving coordinate basis, and solve the HLLC Riemann problem accordingly. We lay out this approach for readers’ interest as well as for a more complete discussion.

IV.1
Implementation of equations

For the numerical implementation, we discretize the volume averages of Eq. (
9
). Using divergence theorem, the discretized version of equation
9
in the cell
(i,j)
can be expressed as (Since our code is 2.5D, we will ignore the discretization in the
\phi
direction.)
Cheong et al. 2021
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\boldsymbol{q}\rangle_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\Delta V_{i,j}}

(80)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\left[\left.\left(\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle^{1}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{1}\right)\right|_{i+1/2,j}-\left.\left(\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle^{1}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{1}\right)\right|_{i-1/2,j}\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left[\left.\left(\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle^{2}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{2}\right)\right|_{i,j+1/2}-\left.\left(\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle^{2}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{2}\right)\right|_{i,j-1/2}\right]\}

\displaystyle+\langle\boldsymbol{s}\rangle_{i,j}

where the cell volume and volume average are defined as

\displaystyle\Delta V\equiv\int_{\text{cell }}\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\mathrm{~d}x^{2}\mathrm{~d}x^{3},

(81)

\displaystyle\langle\bullet\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta V}\int_{\text{cell }}\bullet\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\mathrm{~d}x^{2}\mathrm{~d}x^{3},

while the surface area and surface average is deﬁned as

\begin{aligned} &\Delta\mathcal{A}^{i}\equiv\int_{\text{surface }}\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}\mathrm{d}x^{j,j\neq i}\\
&\langle\bullet\rangle^{i}\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta A^{i}}\int_{\text{surface }}\bullet^{i}\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}\mathrm{d}x^{j,j\neq i}\end{aligned}.

(82)

Note that when we perform the volumn average
\langle\bullet\rangle
or surface average
\langle\bullet\rangle^{i}
, we could strip out the geometric factor from the tensorial expressions in coordinate basis and integrate them together with the volume factor
\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}
. In this way, the tensorial variables in orthonormal basis like
S_{\{\theta\}}
become truly independent of the underlining geometry. For example, in the spherical coordinates, the volume average for the conserved momentum
q_{S_{\theta}}
will be calculated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle q_{S_{\theta}}\rangle_{i,j}\Delta V&=\int_{\text{cell }}\mathcal{M}S_{\{\theta\}}r^{3}\sin\theta drd\theta d\phi\\
&=\left(\mathcal{M}S_{\{\theta\}}\right)_{i,j}\int_{\text{cell }}r^{3}\sin\theta drd\theta d\phi.\end{split}

(83)

For our moving-mesh scheme, the cells in the radial direction will continuously merge and divide. When we perform the above integral, the variables in orthonormal basis like
\mathcal{M}S_{\{\theta\}}
will be better conserved. As an example, if we assume
\mathcal{M}S_{\{\theta\}}
is constant across
{\rm{cell}}_{(i,j)}
and
{\rm{cell}}_{(i+1,j)}
, when we merge these two cells, it gives a combined conserved momentum as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle q_{S_{\theta}}\rangle_{i_{\rm{new}},j}&\Delta V_{i_{\rm{new}},j}=\langle q_{S_{\theta}}\rangle_{i,j}\Delta V_{i,j}+\langle q_{S_{\theta}}\rangle_{i+1,j}\Delta V_{i+1,j}\\
&=\left(\mathcal{M}S_{\{\theta\}}\right)_{i_{\rm{new}},j}\int_{{\rm{cell}}_{(i_{\rm{new}},j)}}r^{3}\sin\theta drd\theta d\phi,\end{split}

(84)

where
{\rm cell}_{(i_{\rm{new}},j)}
is the combined cells of
{\rm cell}_{(i,j)}
and
{\rm cell}_{(i+1,j)}
with
\Delta V_{i_{\rm{new}},j}=\Delta V_{i,j}+\Delta V_{i+1,j}
. From the combined momentum, we can recover the variable
\mathcal{M}S_{\{\theta\}}
accurately.

In code implementation, the contribution of the source term to the conserved variables inside a cell is defined as
\langle\boldsymbol{s}\rangle_{i,j}\Delta V_{i,j}
. We perform volume integral on the singular factors such as
1/r
and
\rm{cot}(\theta)
that appear in the source term [see Eq. (
17
)]. Explicitly, the integral of
1/r
factor gives
(r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2})/2
while the integral of
\rm{cot}(\theta)
leads to
(\rm{sin}(\theta_{+})-\rm{sin}(\theta_{-}))
. This practice turns out to reduce numerical error for the source term calculation near singular points.

Finally, to work out the cell volume, cell surface, we make the following definition

r_{\pm}=r\pm\frac{1}{2}\Delta r,\quad\theta_{\pm}=\theta\pm\frac{1}{2}\Delta\theta,

(85)

and calculate the area and volume as

\displaystyle\left.\Delta\mathcal{A}_{r}\right|_{r_{\pm}}=2\pi\left(r\pm\frac{\Delta r}{2}\right)^{2}\left(2\sin\theta\sin\left(\frac{\Delta\theta}{2}\right)\right),

(86)

\displaystyle\left.\Delta\mathcal{A}_{\theta}\right|_{\theta_{\pm}}=2\pi\left(r^{2}+\frac{1}{12}(\Delta r)^{2}\right)\left(\Delta r\right)\left(\sin\left(\theta\pm\frac{\Delta\theta}{2}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\Delta V=2\pi\left(r^{2}+\frac{1}{12}(\Delta r)^{2}\right)\left(\Delta r\right)\left(2\sin\theta\sin\left(\frac{\Delta\theta}{2}\right)\right).

IV.2
Recovery of primitive variables

There are many possible ways to make the conversion between conserved variables and primitive variables (e.g.,
Noble et al. 2006
). Our current research focuses on relativistic jets propagating in an ambient medium. We need to deal with large variations of density and pressure in the jet simulations. The following cons-to-prim method proves to be robust for such a task. We use
\rho,P,\ Wv^{\{i\}}=u^{\{i\}}+W\beta^{\{i\}}/\alpha
as our primitive variables where
Wv^{\{i\}}
is the projected fluid velocity in orthonormal basis. For the equation of state (EOS), we only consider the case of a single-component perfect gas for now. In this case, the specific enthalpy
h\equiv 1+\varepsilon+P/\rho
is a function of a temperaturelike variable
\Theta=P/\rho
(see
Synge and Morse 1958
).
In the literature, the most widely used EOS is the ideal gas EOS:
P=(\Gamma-1)\rho\varepsilon
, where
P
is the gas pressure,
\varepsilon
is the specific internal energy density. Which can be expressed as:

h(\Theta)=1+\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma-1}\Theta,

(87)

where
\Gamma
is the adiabatic index. The ideal gas EOS has been applied to the gas of either subrelativistic temperature with
\Gamma=5/3
or ultrarelativistic temperature with
\Gamma=4/3
. For our simulations of relativistic outflow propagating in a cold ambient medium, a variable equivalent adiabatic index
\Gamma_{\rm{eq}}=(h-1)/(h-1-\Theta)
is desirable to account for transitions between the nonrelativistic and the relativistic temperature regime. There have been efforts to find EOSs that better describe the thermal dynamics of relativistic gas.
Synge and Morse 1958
derives the correct EOS for the single-component perfect gas in a relativistic regime using modiﬁed Bessel functions.
Mignone et al. 2005
proposes an approximate EOS (denoted as TM EOS) that is consistent with the Taub’s
inequality
Taub 1948
:

(h-\Theta)(h-4\Theta)\geq 1

(88)

for all temperatures. It differs by less than
4\%
from the theoretical value given in
Synge and Morse 1958
.
Ryu et al. 2006
proposes a new EOS (RC EOS), which better fits the theoretical value. Let us write the expression of the specific enthalpy for the RC EOS:

h(\Theta)=2\frac{6\Theta^{2}+4\Theta+1}{3\Theta+2}.

(89)

Following the definition of the general form of polytropic index
n
and the general form of sound speed
c_{s}
:

n=\rho\frac{\partial h}{\partial p}-1,c_{s}^{2}=-\frac{\rho}{nh}\frac{\partial h}{\partial\rho},

(90)

their values can be calculated for RC EOS as:

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\frac{9\Theta^{2}+12\Theta+2}{(3\Theta+2)^{2}},

\displaystyle c_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Theta(3\Theta+2)\left(18\Theta^{2}+24\Theta+5\right)}{3\left(6\Theta^{2}+4\Theta+1\right)\left(9\Theta^{2}+12\Theta+2\right)}.

(91)

For both TM and RC, we have correctly
c_{s}^{2}\rightarrow 5\Theta/3
in the nonrelativistic temperature limit and
c_{s}^{2}\rightarrow 1/3
in the ultrarelativistic temperature limit
Ryu et al. 2006
.

We can use these expressions to convert the conservative variables into primitive ones with a standard Newton–Raphson method (NRM)
De Colle et al. 2012
, using
\Theta
as our independent variable. We will use the (known) values of the conservative variables.

D=\rho W,S_{\{i\}}=\rho hW^{2}v_{\{i\}},\tau=\rho hW^{2}-P-D

(92)

First, by squaring the momentum equation, we get

W^{2}=1+\frac{S^{2}}{D^{2}h^{2}},S^{2}=\gamma^{\{i\}\{j\}}S_{\{i\}}S_{\{j\}}

(93)

with
h=h(\Theta)
given by the EOS.
Using the relation
p=D\Theta/W
, we get the energy density (excluding rest mass),
\tau=DhW-D\Theta/W-D
.
We can then derive the following identity
De Colle et al. 2012
:

f(\Theta)=h(\Theta)W(\Theta)-\frac{\Theta}{W(\Theta)}-1-\frac{\tau}{D}=0.

(94)

Together with Eq. (
93
), the derivative
df/d\Theta
has the form:

\frac{df(\Theta)}{d\Theta}=\frac{h^{\prime}}{W}\left(1-\frac{\Theta}{h}\frac{W^{2}-1}{W^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{W}

(95)

where the relation
W^{\prime}=-h^{\prime}(W^{2}-1)/(hW)
has been used (derived from Eq. (
93
), see also
De Colle et al. 2012
).

The derivative
dh/d\Theta
depends on the particular EOS used. We adopt the RC EOS [see Eq. (
89
)] for the simulations of relativistic jets and the ideal gas EOS for the remaining numerical tests.

IV.3
Reconstruction

We reconstruct the primitive variable (denoted with
Q
) to the left and right sides of each cell with the total variation diminishing (TVD) method described in
Mignone 2014
:

Q_{i}^{\pm}=\left<Q\right>_{i}+\overline{\Delta Q_{i}}\frac{\xi_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}-\bar{\xi}_{i}}{\Delta\xi_{i}}

(96)

where
\Delta\xi_{i}=\xi_{i+1/2}-\xi_{i-1/2}
is the cell width, and
\bar{\xi}
is the cell center. And
\overline{\Delta Q_{i}}
is a slope-limited gradient function written in terms of a nonlinear limiter function
\varphi(v)
:

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta Q}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{i}^{F}\varphi(v)\quad\text{ where }v=\frac{\Delta Q_{i}^{B}}{\Delta Q_{i}^{F}}

(97)

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{i}^{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\xi_{i}\left(\frac{\langle Q\rangle_{i+1}-\langle Q\rangle_{i}}{\bar{\xi}_{i+1}-\bar{\xi}_{i}}\right)

(98)

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{i}^{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\xi_{i}\left(\frac{\langle Q\rangle_{i}-\langle Q\rangle_{i-1}}{\bar{\xi}_{i}-\bar{\xi}_{i-1}}\right)

(99)

We adopt the same modified monotonized central (MC) limiter in
Mignone 2014
.

\displaystyle\varphi^{MC}(v)

\displaystyle=\max\left[0,\min\left(\frac{1+v}{2},c_{i}^{F},c_{i}^{B}v\right)\right]

(100)

where

\displaystyle c_{i}^{F}=\frac{\bar{\xi}_{i+1}-\bar{\xi}_{i}}{\xi_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\bar{\xi}_{i}};c_{i}^{B}=\frac{\bar{\xi}_{i}-\bar{\xi}_{i-1}}{\bar{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}

(101)

To reconstruct the left and right state of the cell
i
, the above stop limiter utilizes the cell average values of
\left<Q\right>_{i-1}
,
\left<Q\right>_{i}
, and
\left<Q\right>_{i+1}
, defined at the cell center
\bar{\xi}_{i-1},\bar{\xi}_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i+1}
. This algorithm takes into account nonuniform spacing. The cell center position
\bar{\xi}_{i}
can be taken as the volume-averaged cell center (“centroids of volume”) or arithmetic-mean cell center. In this study, we adopt the arithmetic-mean cell center for our simulations.

IV.4
Treatment of numerical conditions

Robust numerical simulations require the treatment of several numerical conditions. One of them is the Courant-Friedrich-Levy (CFL) condition
Courant et al. 1967
, which limits the time step size in explicit numerical methods. The simulation domain of the
JET
code allocates cells at the same temporal level. A global time step will be used to evolve simulation time. To find the global time step, we first calculate the time step
\delta t
of individual cells in the domain according to:

\displaystyle\delta t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textit{CFL}\cdot\rm{min}(\delta t^{r},\delta t^{\theta})

(102)

\displaystyle\delta t^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta r}{\rm{max}(|(\lambda^{r})^{+}-V^{r}_{\rm{cell}}|,|(\lambda^{r})^{-}-V^{r}_{\rm{cell}}|)}

(103)

\displaystyle\delta t^{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r\Delta\theta}{\rm{max}(|(\lambda^{\theta})^{+}|,|(\lambda^{\theta})^{-}|)}

(104)

where
CFL
is the CFL number. Its value has been set to 0.4 for simulations performed in this study.
(\lambda^{r})^{\pm}
and
(\lambda^{\theta})^{\pm}
are again the minimum and maximum characteristic speeds for the cell in the radial and polar direction, respectively.
V^{r}_{\rm{cell}}
is the cell’s radial velocity which approximates the cell’s upper interface velocity. We then pick the smallest time step as the global one. The subtraction of the cell’s radial velocity in Eq. (
103
) leads to a much larger time step, making the long-term simulation of relativistic jets computationally efficient.
Another numerical condition that needs to be taken care of is the boundary condition. For our cell-centered grid structure in spherical polar coordinates, we follow the boundary treatment described in
Baumgarte et al. 2013
;
Mewes et al. 2020
. We first allocate two layers of ghost zones for each of the four boundaries (two in the radial direction, and two in the polar direction), and then fill the boundary ghost zones at the radial origin, and at the
\theta
boundary with values copied from the corresponding points in the interior of the grid, accounting for appropriate parity factors. For the outer boundary in the radial direction, we adopt the Dirichlet boundary condition and use the initial data routine to set their ghost zone values.

IV.5
The adjusted moving-mesh scheme

Since the initial development of the
JET
code
Duffell and MacFadyen 2013
, the moving-mesh scheme has kept being updated to improve the accuracy and efficiency of relativistic jet simulations. The adjusted moving-mesh scheme in this study contains the following rules: inside the simulation domain, the radial interface of a grid cell will move at local contact discontinuity velocity of the flow. Each radial track moves independently. The inner and outer radial boundaries of the domain can also move. At each time step, the longest and shortest cell in each radial track will be marked for refinement or derefinement according to the maximum or minimum aspect ratio of grid cell (
a:=\Delta r/r\Delta\theta
) allowed in the simulation (see
Duffell and MacFadyen 2013
for more information). In ultrarelativistic jet simulations, we find the domain cells can squeeze into an ultrathin shell with the cell’s aspect ratio reaching 1/100 or even smaller. In order to resolve the relativistic thin shell, only cells with length
\Delta r<r/(8\Gamma^{2})
will be marked for derefinement. In addition, we deﬁne an approximate second derivative of a ﬂuid variable as a measurement of error
E_{i}
to mark the region of interest. At each time step, the cell along each radial track with the maximum measurement of error will be marked for refinement if its aspect ratio is larger than twice the minimum aspect ratio and its measurement error
E_{i}>0.9
. The cells with
E_{i}<0.002
will be considered for derefinement. The cell to be dereﬁned is the one that has the smallest time step (see
Xie and MacFadyen 2019
). To reduce load imbalance of CPUs, the number of grids in each radial track will be balanced dynamically during the simulation.

VII
Conclusion

This paper presents an advancement in computational astrophysics: developing and implementing a general relativistic moving-mesh hydrodynamic code featuring an advanced Riemann solver in curvilinear coordinates.
We showcase the details of integrating a general relativistic framework into the hydrodynamic simulation code
JET
, achieved through applying the reference metric method.

With its ability to elegantly handle the intricate spacetime geometries inherent in general relativity, the tetrad formulation is an ideal choice to address the HLLC Riemann problem under strong gravity. The achievement of our work is the successful adaptation of the tetrad formulation to incorporate the HLLC Riemann solver into the general relativistic moving-mesh code. We have conducted a series of numerical simulations to validate and demonstrate the efficacy of our newly developed code. These simulations encompass both fixed-mesh and moving-mesh scenarios, allowing us to test the code’s performance under various conditions. The results from these simulations are particularly noteworthy in demonstrating the code’s robustness and reliability in simulating fluid flows under the influence of strong gravitational fields.

Compared to the fixed-mesh approach, a moving-mesh scheme can increase the time step for fluid regions with high velocity since it removes the limitation imposed by the bulk velocity (see, e.g.,
Ayache et al. 2022
). The moving-mesh approach makes the long-term simulation of relativistic jets computationally feasible (see, e.g.,
Duffell and MacFadyen 2015
;
Xie et al. 2018
;
Xie and MacFadyen 2019
). By extending the
JET
code’s capability of handling relativistic jets from an astronomical scale to the scale of a black hole, we have opened new avenues for the full-time-domain simulation of relativistic jets, from their genesis to dissipation. To demonstrate this possibility, we design a representative prototype model which features a torus around a central black hole. A jet is manually launched in the polar direction, near the black hole-torus system. For the underlining jet launching mechanism, we refer readers to Blandford-Payne
Blandford and Payne 1982
or Blandford-Znajek
Blandford and Znajek 1977
and related dynamo processes (see e.g.
Brandenburg and Subramanian 2005
;
Kiuchi et al. 2023
). In this work, we prescribe an engine profile to imitate the jet launching process. This setup allows us to explore the dynamics of the jet’s journey from its origin near the black hole-torus system to its final Newtonian phase. We found multiple new hydrodynamical features from this end-to-end simulation. For the first time, we have been able to simulate the complete life cycle of a relativistic jet, providing insights into the detailed structures of the cocoon and emerged jet over the whole journey.

Furthermore, these full-time-domain jet simulations enable the joint investigation of various electromagnetic phenomena associated with relativistic outflows. For the case of BNS mergers, the observational phenomena include the kilo-nova emission from the remnant ejecta (see e.g.
Li and Paczyński 1998
;
Kulkarni 2005
;
Metzger et al. 2010
), the GRB prompt and afterglow emission (see e.g.
Mészáros 2006
;
Berger 2014
;
Kumar and Zhang 2015
), and other related processes. By combining our simulations with GRMHD models of jet-launching processes, we will be able to extend the evolution of outflows to distances relevant to long-term electromagnetic radiation observations. This integrative approach aligns perfectly with the era of multimessenger astronomy, allowing for an unprecedented understanding of the underlying physics in jet-launching systems.

While this paper sets the foundational steps in this direction, the complete realization of these ambitious goals remains a pursuit for future research. The potential for further advancements and discoveries in the field is vast, and our work may catalyze the next generation of astrophysical jet simulations, potentially revolutionizing our understanding of relativistic jets and their associated physics.

Appendix A
General relativistic hydrodynamic equations in reference metric formulation

A.1
The continuity equation

The covariant divergence of a vector
V^{\mu}
gives

\nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{-g}V^{\mu}\right),

(108)

which holds for any metric and its associated covariant derivative. The covariant divergence of a mixed-index second-rank tensor
A_{\mu}{}^{\nu}
, on the other hand, follows (see e.g.
Montero et al. 2014
)

\nabla_{\nu}A_{\mu}{}^{\nu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{-g}A_{\mu}{}^{\nu}\right)-A_{\xi}{}^{\nu}\Gamma_{\nu\mu}^{\xi}.

(109)

We will utilize these two rules to derive the general relativistic hydrodynamic equations.

Let us first apply Eq. (
108
) to the continuity Eq. (
5
)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\nu}\left(\rho u^{\nu}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{\nu}\right),

(110)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\left(\partial_{0}\left(\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{0}\right)+\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{j}\right)\right).

The determinant of the spacetime metric leads to

\sqrt{-g}=\alpha\sqrt{\gamma}=\alpha\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}}=\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}\alpha\psi^{6}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{\hat{\gamma}}}.

(111)

Inserting the previous result into Eq. (
110
) we obtain

\partial_{0}\left(\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}D\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}}\partial_{j}\left[\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}\left(f_{D}\right)^{j}\right]=0,

(112)

which is sometimes written as

\partial_{0}\left(\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}D\right)+\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\left[\left(f_{D}\right)^{j}\right]=0,

(113)

where
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
is the covariant derivative with respect to the reference metric
\hat{\gamma}
.
In the above expression, we have defined the density as seen by a normal observer as

D=W\rho,

(114)

and the corresponding flux

(f_{D})^{j}=\alpha\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}D(v^{j}-\beta^{j}/\alpha).

(115)

Note that, the fluid velocity as measured by a normal observer,
v^{i}
, is given by the ratio between the projection of the four-velocity,
u^{\mu}
, in the space orthogonal to
n^{\mu}
and the Lorentz factor of
u^{\mu}
as measured by a normal observer,
W=-n_{\mu}u^{\mu}=\alpha u^{t}
:

v^{i}=\frac{\gamma^{i}_{\mu}u^{\mu}}{\alpha u^{t}}=\frac{u^{i}}{W}+\frac{\beta^{i}}{\alpha}.

(116)

A.2
The Euler equation

To derive the Euler equation, we apply Eq. (
109
) to the projected conservation of energy momentum (
6
)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\gamma_{i\mu}\nabla_{\nu}T^{\nu\mu}=g_{i\mu}\nabla_{\nu}T^{\nu\mu}=\nabla_{\nu}\left(g_{i\mu}T^{\nu\mu}\right)

(117)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{-g}T_{i}^{\nu}\right)-T_{\nu}^{\mu}\Gamma_{i\mu}^{\nu}.

We then get the following Euler equation

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\left(\partial_{0}\left(\sqrt{-g}T_{i}^{0}\right)+\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{-g}T_{i}^{j}\right)\right)=T_{\nu}^{\mu}\Gamma_{i\mu}^{\nu}.

(118)

Note that the source term leads to

T^{\mu}_{\nu}\Gamma_{i\mu}^{\nu}=T^{\mu\xi}g_{\xi\nu}\Gamma^{\nu}_{i\mu}=\frac{1}{2}T^{\mu\xi}g_{\xi\mu,i}.

(119)

Let us define

\displaystyle\begin{split}T^{\mu\nu}&=\rho hu^{\mu}u^{\nu}+Pg^{\mu\nu}\\

\displaystyle=\rho hW^{2}-P,

(120b)

\displaystyle=\rho hW^{2}v^{i},

(120c)

A.3
The energy equation

For the energy equation, we consider a projection along the normal
n_{\mu}
of the conservation of
energy-momentum (
6
) and add the conservation of
rest mass (
5
)

n_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}+\nabla_{\mu}\left(\rho u^{\mu}\right)=0,

(125)

which can be rewritten as

\nabla_{\mu}\left(n_{\nu}T^{\nu\mu}+\rho u^{\mu}\right)=T^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}n_{\nu}.

(126)

We again evaluate the divergence of a vector on the left-hand side, and proceed exactly the same as for the continuity equation, which leads to

\partial_{0}\left(\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}\tau\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}}\partial_{j}\left[\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}\left(f_{\tau}\right)^{j}\right]=-\alpha\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}T^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}n_{\nu},

(127)

where we have defined
\tau
as the internal energy observed by a normal observer

\tau\equiv n_{\mu}n_{\nu}T^{\mu\nu}-D\equiv\rho hW^{2}-P-D,

(128)

and the corresponding flux

\left(f_{\tau}\right)^{j}\equiv\alpha\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}\left(\tau\left(v^{j}-\beta^{j}/\alpha\right)+Pv^{j}\right).

(129)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
127
) (denoted as
s_{\tau}
) can be derived as
Montero et al. 2014

\displaystyle s_{\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv\alpha\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}\left(T^{00}\left(\beta^{i}\beta^{j}K_{ij}-\beta^{i}\partial_{i}\alpha\right)\right.

(130)

\displaystyle\left.+T^{0i}\left(2\beta^{j}K_{ij}-\partial_{i}\alpha\right)+T^{ij}K_{ij}\right).

Finally, we get the energy equation as

\partial_{t}\left(\psi^{6}\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}/\hat{\gamma}}\tau\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}}\partial_{j}\left[\sqrt{\hat{\gamma}}(f_{\tau})^{j}\right]=s_{\tau}.

(131)

Appendix B
Transformation between Schwarzschild coordinates and maximally sliced trumpet coordinate

Starting from a family of stationary, maximal slicing of the Schwarzschild spacetime

\displaystyle\begin{split}&ds^{2}=-\alpha^{2}dt^{2}+\gamma_{ij}(dx^{i}+\beta^{i}dt)(dx^{j}+\beta^{j}dt)\\
&=-\left(\alpha^{2}-\beta_{R}\beta^{R}\right)dt^{2}+2\beta_{R}dtdR+f^{-2}dR^{2}+R^{2}d\Omega^{2},\end{split}

(133)

with lapse
\alpha=f
, shift vector
\beta^{R}=\frac{Cf}{R^{2}}
,

f=\left(1-\frac{2M}{R}+\frac{C^{2}}{R^{4}}\right)^{1/2}
and
C
being the integration constant.
The transformation into isotropic coordinates follows

f^{-2}dR^{2}+R^{2}d^{2}\Omega=\psi^{4}\left(dr^{2}+r^{2}d^{2}\Omega\right),

(134)

so we have
dR/dr=\psi^{2}f,R=\psi^{2}r
.
Two simple solutions of
r
and
\psi
can be found for the cases
C=0
and
C=3\sqrt{3}M^{2}/4
.
For the case of
C=0
, the solution yields the familiar isotropic coordinate of the Schwarzschild metric,

\displaystyle\begin{split}r=&\frac{R-M+\sqrt{R^{2}-2MR}}{2},\\
\alpha=&\left(1-\frac{2M}{R}\right)^{1/2}=\frac{1-M/(2r)}{1+M/(2r)}.\end{split}

(135)

;
Kiuchi et al. 2022

\displaystyle\begin{split}r=&{\left[\frac{2R+M+\left(4R^{2}+4MR+3M^{2}\right)^{1/2}}{4}\right]}\\
&\times\left[\frac{(4+3\sqrt{2})(2R-3M)}{8R+6M+3\left(8R^{2}+8MR+6M^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\right]^{1/\sqrt{2}},\\
\alpha=&\left(1-\frac{2M}{R}+\frac{27M^{4}}{16R^{4}}\right)^{1/2},\\

which represents a maximal slicing of the Schwarzschild spacetime with limitng surface at
