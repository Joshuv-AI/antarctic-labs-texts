Abstract
This paper details the first application of a software tagging algorithm to reduce radon-induced backgrounds in liquid noble element time projection chambers, such as XENON1T and XENONnT. The convection velocity field in XENON1T was mapped out using Rn 222 {}^{222}\mathrm{Rn} and Po 218 {}^{218}\mathrm{Po} events, and the root-mean-square convection speed was measured to be 0.30 ± 0.01 ​ cm / s 0.30\pm 0.01\,\mathrm{cm/s} . Given this velocity field, Pb 214 {}^{214}\mathrm{Pb} background events can be tagged when they are followed by Bi 214 {}^{214}\mathrm{Bi} and Po 214 {}^{214}\mathrm{Po} decays, or preceded by Po 218 {}^{218}\mathrm{Po} decays. This was achieved by evolving a point cloud in the direction of a measured convection velocity field, and searching for Bi 214 {}^{214}\mathrm{Bi} and Po 214 {}^{214}\mathrm{Po} decays or Po 218 {}^{218}\mathrm{Po} decays within a volume defined by the point cloud. In XENON1T, this tagging system achieved a Pb 214 {}^{214}\mathrm{Pb} background reduction of 6.2 − 0.9 + 0.4 % 6.2^{+0.4}_{-0.9}\% with an exposure loss of 1.8 ± 0.2 % 1.8\pm 0.2\% , despite the timescales of convection being smaller than the relevant decay times. We show that the performance can be improved in XENONnT, and that the performance of such a software-tagging approach can be expected to be further improved in a diffusion-limited scenario. Finally, a similar method might be useful to tag the cosmogenic Xe 137 {}^{137}\mathrm{Xe} background, which is relevant to the search for neutrinoless double-beta decay.

I.1
XENON1T and XENONnT

The XENON1T experiment used a cylindrical dual-phase TPC, with a diameter of
96\,\mathrm{cm}
and a
2.0\,\mathrm{tonnes}
active liquid xenon target
Aprile et al. 2017a
. Two arrays of photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) were installed in the top and bottom of the TPC, respectively. The vertical sides of the TPC are constructed out of UV-reflective Polytetrafluoroethlyene to increase the light collection efficiency. A cathode at the bottom of the TPC and a gate electrode
97\,\mathrm{cm}
above the cathode produced a drift field of
81\,\mathrm{V/cm}
. The anode was
5\,\mathrm{mm}
above the gate and
\sim 2.5\,\mathrm{mm}
above the liquid-gas interface, and with the gate produced an extraction field of
8.1\,\mathrm{kV/cm}
. The cryostat containing the TPC was positioned inside a
740\,\mathrm{m^{3}}
water Cherenkov muon veto which allowed for the active detection and veto of muons and muon-induced backgrounds.

XENONnT is an in-place upgrade of XENON1T that uses much of the existing infrastructure. It features an enlarged TPC, a novel neutron veto system, and various improvements in the xenon handling system, which allow for an improved xenon purity. The TPC diameter is
1.3\,\mathrm{m}
, and the separation between gate and cathode extended to
1.5\,\mathrm{m}
. This results in an active liquid xenon target mass of
5.9\,\mathrm{tonnes}

Aprile et al. 2020a
. The muon veto uses the same design as in XENON1T. However, there is now a neutron veto surrounding the main cryostat containing the TPC, which is optically separated from the muon veto. The neutron veto aims to reduce the radiogenic neutron background by detecting neutrons which scatter in the TPC volume and are then captured in the neutron veto
Aprile et al. 2020a
.

Events in the TPC are detected via two signals. First, a prompt scintillation signal (S1) comprised of
175\,\mathrm{nm}
photons
Fujii et al. 2015
is produced at the site of a particle interaction due to the decay of excited atoms
Szydagis et al. 2022
. Additionally, ionization electrons are produced; some of these recombine with xenon ions, contributing to the S1 as well
Szydagis et al. 2022
. These photons are detected by the PMTs. Ionization electrons that do not recombine are drifted towards the liquid-gas interface by the drift field, and extracted into the xenon gas by the extraction field, where a secondary scintillation signal (S2) is produced. The size of these S1 and S2 signals are measured in units of photoelectrons (PE).

The 3D position of the interaction can be reconstructed using the hit pattern of the S2 signal on the top PMT array
(x,y)
and the drift time between the S1 and S2 signals
(z)
.
Details regarding position reconstruction methods in XENON1T can be found in
Aprile et al. 2019b
.
The reconstructed position also allows for the computation of corrected S1 and S2 signal sizes, based on position-dependent signal efficiencies
Aprile et al. 2019b
.

II
Measuring convection in the XENON1T detector

II.1
Mapping the convection velocity field

Convection has been observed in earlier dual phase liquid xenon TPCs, such as XENON100 and LUX
Aprile et al. 2017c
;
Malling 2014
. While the exact boundary conditions driving the convection are not known, the convective flow is likely driven by the thermal flux into the TPC, possibly from both recirculation flows and from the cryostat. The relevant temperature gradient might be either horizontal or vertical.

To measure the convective flow in the XENON1T detector,
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
events were used.
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
undergo alpha decay.
The
3\,\mathrm{min}
half-life of
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
is short enough that the
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
events can be paired up, but long enough that there can be significant displacement between pairs of events at
\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{cm/s}
speeds. As such, the decays of
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
are particularly suited to the measurement of convection.

These events were selected using Gaussian Mixture clustering
Pedregosa et al. 2011
using the position-corrected S1, position-corrected S2, width of the S2 peak in nanoseconds, radial coordinate, and z-coordinate of each event
Masson 2018
.

After event selection,
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
events have to be paired to construct velocity vectors corresponding to the convective flow. However, the rate of
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
decays exceeds
10\,\mathrm{\mu Bq/kg}

Aprile et al. 2021
, corresponding to approximately two
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
events every
3\,\mathrm{min}
in a
1\,\mathrm{tonne}
fiducial mass. As the half-life of
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
is
\sim 3\,\mathrm{min}
, the pairing of
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
events cannot be done in a naive manner where every
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
event is considered to be the daughter of the preceding
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
event. Instead, for each pair of
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
events, the time difference
(\Delta t)
and displacement
(\Delta s)
were plotted on a histogram, see
Figure 2
. An excess of pairs where
\Delta s<20\,\mathrm{cm}
and
0\,\mathrm{s}<\Delta t<40\,\mathrm{s}
becomes apparent. This excess is due to correctly-paired events. In addition, the distribution of
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
events that are not correctly paired is independent of
\Delta t
, and can be determined using pairs where
\Delta t<0
. The observed distribution of these incorrect pairs is largely due to the TPC geometry, and the maxima at
\sim 60\,\mathrm{cm}
is what one would expect from the pair-wise distances of two uniform random distributions in the TPC. One can then compute the purity of each histogram bin as
f_{\mathrm{pure}}=1-{N_{bg}}/{N_{bin}}
, where
N_{bg}
is the number of incorrect pairs in a bin at the given
\Delta t
estimated using negative-time bins, and
N_{bin}
is the total number pairs in a bin.

Filtering of the velocity field

Figure 4:

The convection field, of which a slice is shown in
Figure 3
, was then filtered and discretized onto a grid. The purpose of this was to reduce noise and to speed up computation, as finding the nearest velocity vector to a given position is much faster with data on a regular grid. First, every vector of purity
f_{\mathrm{pure}}
was oversampled
25\times f_{\mathrm{pure}}
times, rounded to the nearest integer. The value of
25
was chosen to avoid significant computational cost. The x-y position reconstruction uncertainty of
\alpha

\sigma_{x}=0.3\,\mathrm{cm},\sigma_{y}=0.3\,\mathrm{cm}
, based on the spread observed in
{}^{210}\mathrm{Pb}
decays on the TPC surface

\sigma_{z}=0.17\,\mathrm{cm}
from the displacement of the two decays in BiPo events. Diffusion is not considered as it is much smaller than the position resolution for the relevant timescales of
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
decay; this can be seen from
IV.3
. During oversampling, each vector was perturbed randomly based on the position reconstruction uncertainty. The oversampled population of vectors were then put onto a grid with a grid spacing of
1/3\,\mathrm{cm}

II.3
Root-mean-square convection speed

The convection vectors obtained in
subsection II.1
allow a measurement of the bulk convection properties. To avoid biases due to uneven event densities, the detector was divided into 11 bins in
r^{2}\in[0,47.9^{2}]\mathrm{cm}^{2}
, 10 bins in azimuth
\phi\in[-\pi,\pi]
, and 9 bins in
z\in[-96.9,0]\mathrm{cm}
. Every vector was then assigned to a bin, and given a weight
w_{i}
equal to the reciprocal of the number of vectors in that bin. This procedure allows for the computation of a volume-averaged root-mean-square speed:

v_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum^{N}_{i=1}v_{i}^{2}w_{i}}{\sum^{N}_{i=1}w_{i}}}

(1)

is used as a proxy for the surface area to volume ratio. It can be seen that convection speed decreases linearly. EXO-200 and LZ are excluded from this plot as convection was not observed in EXO-200

The uncertainty on each velocity vector can be estimated using the position reconstruction uncertainty as
\sigma_{i}=\sqrt{2\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}+\sigma_{z}^{2}}{\Delta t_{i}^{2}}}
. The total uncertainty is then given by

\sigma_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum^{N}_{i=1}v_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}w_{i}}{\sum^{N}_{i=1}w_{i}}}.

(2)

The root-mean-square speed was thus found to be
0.30\pm 0.01\,\mathrm{cm/s}
. This is significantly slower than what was observed in XENON100 and LUX
Aprile et al. 2017c
;
Malling 2014
; however, it is significantly higher than EXO-200 and LZ where convection is sub-dominant to the mobility of charged ions
Albert et al. 2015
;
Aalbers et al. 2022a
. This is shown in
Figure 5
, plotted against the cube-root of the target mass, which is a proxy for linear dimension.

The heat flux into a TPC is likely proportional to the surface area, which is the square of linear dimension; when distributed over the entire target mass, which is related to the cube of linear dimension, one expects convection speed to vary linearly with linear dimension. It can be seen that convection speed decreases linearly with larger detectors.

It is notable that the convection velocity implies a convection timescale of
\sim\frac{100\,\mathrm{cm}}{0.3\,\mathrm{cm/s}}=300\,\mathrm{s}
, which is significantly smaller than the decay time of both
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
and
{}^{214}\mathrm{Bi}
, underscoring the difficulty of a software veto.

III
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
veto algorithm

The veto algorithm first starts from an ER event that is in the energy range to be due to
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
decay, termed the
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
candidate. A normally-distributed set of points centered around the location of this event is generated, and propagated forwards or backwards along the velocity field just described in
II
to search for BiPo or
{}^{218}\mathrm{Pb}
events respectively. If a BiPo or
{}^{218}\mathrm{Pb}
event is found within the point clouds, then the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Pb}
candidate event is labelled as the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Pb}
background and can be vetoed. In the rest of this section section, we detail the algorithm used to select
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
events.

III.1
Generation of noise fields

There is likely uncertainty in the velocity field due to both limited statistics of
{}^{220}\mathrm{Rn}
-
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
pairs and the position reconstruction uncertainty; this needs to be properly accounted for. We address this by adding noise fields to the velocity field to induce fluctuations. This is done because one cannot directly use the uncertainties from the position reconstruction uncertainty when integrating the trajectories of the propagated points; furthermore, there is additional uncertainty introduced due to the purity of the selected vector population (see
II
). These are constrained by two conditions; first, the noise fields have to be divergence-free to avoid introducing sources and sinks, and second, the fields have to behave smoothly at the TPC boundaries. The constraints are expected to be sufficiently strong to make the arbitrary noise field a realistic proxy for the real conditions. Generation of the noise field started with smoothed Gaussian noise where a
\sigma=0.8\,\mathrm{cm}
Gaussian kernel was used for smoothing, chosen to be significantly smaller than the length scale of the convection field as visible in
4
. After this, the curl was taken to ensure the noise is divergence free. 16 noise fields were generated, and then permuted by mirroring and rotating the fields, resulting in a total of 256 noise fields.

TPC surfaces must also be handled smoothly, and the velocity component perpendicular to the surfaces must approach zero at the surfaces. This was ensured by smoothly scaling the perpendicular component of the field to zero, starting
3\,\mathrm{cm}
away from surfaces. This method of using the curl to generate divergence-free noise and handling boundaries is described in
Bridson et al. 2007
. A section of the resultant noise field can be seen in
Figure 6
.

Zoomed-in sample of the noise field. The edge of the detector is shown in red. It can be seen that the boundaries are handled smoothly, and that the field has no sinks that can trap propagating points.

III.2
Point cloud propagation

A veto volume within which one looks for predecessor or daughter events was constructed using a point cloud. The predecessor and daughter events for
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
are
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
and BiPo events (see
Figure 1
). BiPo events were selected by choosing events that have two interactions, corresponding to the
{}^{214}\mathrm{Bi}
and
{}^{214}\mathrm{Po}
decays. These interactions were required to be less than
5\,\mathrm{cm}
apart in each of the x, y, and z directions, and the
\alpha
-event is further required to have appropriate position-corrected S1 and S2 values. First, a random event was picked from the set of electronic recoils in XENON1T as the
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
candidate event. A point cloud was then generated around this
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
candidate event and then propagated using the convection and noise velocity fields, with every point in the point cloud exposed to a different randomly-assigned noise field (see
subsection III.1
). This noise field is introduced to account for the uncertainty of the velocity field; hence, exposing each point to a different noise field can be understood conceptually as exposing each point to a different version of the velocity field, allowing the uncertainty to be sampled. As one only needs to consider
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
candidate events within the energy region of interest to a given analysis, propagating a point cloud from every
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
and BiPo event is more computationally expensive than from the smaller number of low-energy
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
events. Point clouds from each
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
candidate event are thus propagated in the forward and backward directions to look for BiPo and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
events, respectively. These search directions are termed the BiPo and
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
channels for the rest of this paper. The point clouds are then culled in likelihood-space, based on the log-likelihood of each point. An illustration of a point cloud propagated along the velocity field can be seen in
Figure 7
.

1.

A

2.

3.

Every 600 timesteps (

0.3\,\mathrm{sec}
in the time-axis is used. Points that fall below a threshold of log-likelihood
(\xi)
are culled to speed up computation. This log likelihood threshold is a free parameter.

4.

DBScan clustering
Ester et al. 1996

5.

The algorithm repeats from step 3, until all points have been removed.

If a signal is much smaller than the background, the median asymptotic discovery significance of a counting experiment scales as
{\text{signal}}/{\sqrt{\text{background}}}

Cowan et al. 2011
. The reduction in signal can be computed as the probability for events that are not
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
to be vetoed. The background reduction can be computed by the reduction in
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
background multiplied by the fraction of the background represented by
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
, summed with the probability for events that are not
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
to be vetoed multiplied by the fraction of background events that are not
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
. An event is vetoed is vetoed by either the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
or the BiPo channel, thus the probabilities for an event to survive each of the two channels can be multiplied; a graphical depiction of these probabilities can be found in
Figure 10
. One can thus compute a normalised sensitivity for a dark matter search:

Z=\frac{\mathrm{signal}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{background}}}

(6)

where

\begin{split}\mathrm{signal}=&\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{coinc,BiPo}}\times\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{coinc,Po}},\\
\mathrm{background}=&1-(1-\alpha)\times\\
&\left[1-\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{coinc,BiPo}}\times\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{coinc,Po}}\right]\\
&-\alpha\left[1-\left(\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{true,BiPo}}\times\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{true,Po}}\right)\right]\\
\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{coinc,BiPo}}=&1-p_{\mathrm{coinc,BiPo}}(\xi_{BiPo})\\
\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{coinc,Po}}=&1-p_{\mathrm{coinc,Po}}(\xi_{Po})\\
\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{true,BiPo}}=&1-p_{\mathrm{true,BiPo}}(\xi_{BiPo})\\
\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{true,Po}}=&1-p_{\mathrm{true,Po}}(\xi_{Po})\end{split}

\xi_{Po}
and
\xi_{BiPo}
refer to the likelihood threshold parameters being optimised for the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
and BiPo channels, respectively, and
\alpha
refers to the fraction of the background that can be attributed to
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
. This is energy dependent in principle, but is approximated to be a constant
\alpha=0.8
for the purposes of this optimisation, as given by the average between
0\,\mathrm{keV}
and
30\,\mathrm{keV}
. A tilde above a parameter, such as
\tilde{p}
denotes the best fit value of said parameter. This normalised sensitivity is used as a proxy for optimisation of algorithm parameters and evaluation of performance.

Finally, this process was repeated for multiple values of the threshold parameters that govern the veto volume size in the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
and BiPo channels.
This procedure gives an optimal
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
background reduction of
6.3\%
, and a exposure loss of
1.8\%
, as defined by the signal and background components in
Equation (6)
, with optimal thresholds of 9.7 and 9.0 for the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
and BiPo channels respectively. From this, the sensitivity improvement estimated via this procedure is a modest
1.4\%
in XENON1T. However, as this is an analysis technique, it can still be a cost-effective addition to hardware radon-mitigation efforts, such as the cryogenic distillation system in XENONnT
Murra et al. 2022
. In addition, as will be shown later in sections
IV.3
, much higher performance is possible in systems with lower background radon levels and which have slower convective flows.

IV.3
Projection of performance in XENONnT and future TPCs

To project the performance of this technique to XENONnT, a model of how the software radon veto performs under various conditions must be constructed. Such a model needs to be constructed because the analyses shown in earlier sections is data-driven and done using XENON1T data; repeating the full analysis on XENONnT data has not been done yet as of this publication. For each channel, the probability of incorrectly vetoing an event that is not
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
is simply given by the size of the point cloud used to construct the veto volume, multiplied by the rate of
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
or BiPo events. When propagating particles along a 3-dimensional flow, chaotic mixing is expected to occur. This makes the point cloud size diverge exponentially with time
Speetjens et al. 2021
. As such, the growth of the point cloud volume can be modelled with a Lyapunov exponent – the characteristic exponential divergence of two close trajectories
Taylor 2005
. The probability of incorrectly vetoing an event that is not
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
as a function of the time the point cloud is propagated for is given by:

\begin{split}p_{\text{coinc}}(t)&=A\cdot C\int^{t}_{0}e^{v\lambda\tau}d\tau\\
&=A\cdot C\frac{e^{v\lambda t}-1}{v\lambda}\end{split}

(7)

where
A
is the activity of
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
or BiPo events, depending on the channel being modelled,
v
is the convection speed,
t
is the time the point cloud is being propagated, and
C
and
\lambda
are fitting constants.

The probability of correctly vetoing an event that is
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
, on the other hand, can be modelled with the exponential decay of the radioactive species, multiplied by the probability of there being a correctly reconstructed
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
alpha or BiPo event in the detector,
p_{\text{branch}}
. As the efficiency of detecting alphas is high, the probability for the
{}^{218}\text{Po}
channel is approximated as
p_{\text{branch, Po}}=1
. The probability for the BiPo channel has to account for the effect of plate-out onto surfaces in the detector
Bruenner et al. 2021
and less efficient selections, and as such is taken to be the ratio of the BiPo rate as measured using fully-reconstructed BiPo events in the XENON1T detector and the rate of
{}^{214}\text{Pb}
events from the search of dark matter in the electronic recoil channel
Aprile et al. 2020b
as
p_{\text{branch, BiPo}}=0.25
. As this includes selection efficiencies and plate-out, this number might change between detectors, but is kept constant here to estimate the XENONnT performance. The probability of correctly vetoing an event that is
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
is thus given by:

\begin{split}p_{\text{true}}(t)&=p_{\text{branch}}\lambda_{\text{decay}}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\lambda_{\text{decay}}\tau}d\tau\\
&=p_{\text{branch}}\left(1-e^{-\lambda_{\text{decay}}t}\right)\end{split}

(8)

where
\lambda_{\text{decay}}
is the decay constant of the radioactive species relevant to the specific channel,
p_{\text{branch}}
is the multiplicative factor stemming from selection efficiencies and plate out as detailed above, and
t
is the time the point cloud is being propagated. Equations (
7
) and (
8
) can then be combined to eliminate the time variable and produce

p_{\text{coinc}}=\frac{A\cdot C}{v\lambda}\left(\left(1-\frac{p_{\text{true}}}{p_{\text{branch}}}\right)^{-\frac{v\lambda}{\lambda_{\text{decay}}}}-1\right).

(9)

With
Equation (9)
, there are only two free parameters,
C
and
\lambda
. These two free parameters can be fit by running the software radon veto on XENON1T data with different veto volumes using both
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
and BiPo channels. The resultant values from both the channels,
p_{\mathrm{true,Po}}
,
p_{\mathrm{true,BiPo}}
,
p_{\mathrm{coinc,Po}}
, and
p_{\mathrm{coinc,BiPo}}
, are used to fit the values of the fitting constants, as shown in
Figure 16
. This is done in a single fit, thus the fit procedure only produces one value each of
C
and
\lambda
. These data points differ from the exposure loss and
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
reduction values in
Figure 11
as the values from the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
and BiPo channels are presented separately.

Future large dual-phase TPCs might also not have a convective flow; in such a situation, the movement of daughter nuclides after a radioactive decay becomes dominated by ion-drift
Albert et al. 2015

Here, we consider the performance of software tagging of radon-chain backgrounds in the limiting case of this regime, where any stochastic motion is entirely due to diffusion. For simplicity, and because of the unknown effects of plate-out and BiPo reconstruction in future detectors, only the
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
channel is considered here, resulting in a conservative estimate of the algorithm’s performance.

A simple analytic model can be used to estimate the performance of software tagging in the true diffusion-limited regime. The probability density function of the displacement of a particle diffusing in one dimension is given by the 1D diffusion equation
Einstein 1956

\frac{\partial\rho_{x}}{\partial t}=D\frac{\partial^{2}\rho_{x}}{\partial x^{2}}.

(10)

Using the 1D diffusion equation leads to no loss of generality because the distribution of displacement of a diffusing point is independent in different orthogonal axes.

The solution to
Equation (10)
with an initial Dirac delta function,
\delta(x)
, corresponding to the known position of the original particle, is a normal distribution with
\mu=0
and
\sigma^{2}=2Dt
; in 3D, this corresponds to a spherical normal distribution with
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{y}=\sigma_{z}=\sqrt{2Dt}
. It can be noted here that these are also the Green’s function of the 1D and 3D heat equations, respectively, as the isotropic diffusion equation is the heat equation
Polianin 2002
.

The true diffusion constant can be estimated using Einstein’s relation
Richert et al. 1989
and the mobility of
\mu=0.219\pm 0.004\,\mathrm{cm^{2}/(kV\,s)}
as measured by EXO-200
Albert et al. 2015
:

\begin{split}D&=\frac{\mu k_{b}T}{q}\\
&=\frac{k_{b}0.219\,\mathrm{cm^{2}/(kV\,s)}170\,\mathrm{K}}{q_{e}}\\
&\approx 3.2\times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{cm^{2}/s}\end{split}

(11)

With the diffusion constant shown in
Equation (11)
, the daughter of a
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
decay would diffuse approximately
\sqrt{3\times 2\times D\times\left(5\times 27.06\,\mathrm{min}\right)}\approx 0.4\,\mathrm{cm}
in 5 half-lives. An activity of
1.7\,\mathrm{\mu Bq/kg}
as achieved in XENONnT SR0
Aprile et al. 2022d
, and a liquid xenon density of
\sim 3\,\mathrm{g/cm^{3}}

Aprile and Doke 2010
corresponds to an activity per unit volume of
4.6\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{Bq/cm^{3}}
, or 0.4 decays per
10\,\mathrm{L}
per day. As
1\,\mathrm{L}=(10\,\mathrm{cm})^{3}
, this implies that as long as the position reconstruction uncertainty remains significantly below
10\,\mathrm{cm}
, decays would be essentially spatially isolated without fluid flows, and hence one can reject radon-chain backgrounds with a tagging efficiency of near-unity.

V
Conclusions

In this paper, the design and performance of an algorithm for tagging radon-chain backgrounds in liquid noble element TPCs were presented. The presented algorithm performs tagging of the
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
background, which is part of the
{}^{222}\mathrm{Rn}
decay chain. This was demonstrated on XENON1T datasets used for the ER analysis and the search for neutrinoless double beta decay; the original analyses can be found in
Aprile et al. 2020b
;
Aprile et al. 2022a
. It was shown that for the ER analysis, an exposure loss of
1.8\pm 0.2\%
and a
6.2^{+0.4}_{-0.9}\%
reduction in the
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
background can be expected. The neutrinoless double beta decay data set is used to produce a high-purity sample of
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
decay events, as can be seen from a spectral fit. This sample also displays relevant features in the spectrum such as the peak at
352\,\mathrm{keV}
and falling off at the Q-value of approximately
1\,\mathrm{MeV}
.

While the demonstrated background reduction is small, the cost of such a software-based background-reduction technique can be minimal, making deployment cost effective. In addition, much higher performance can be expected in larger detectors with lower intrinsic radon levels, due to individual radon-chain events being further apart in the detector in both space and time. In XENONnT due to the lower radon level, performance is expected to be significantly higher than in XENON1T, with an optimal
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
background reduction of between
25\%
and
75\%
, depending on the convection speed in the detector, with a corresponding exposure loss of between
3.5\%
and
11\%
.
If the motion is dominated by diffusion, near-perfect tagging of radon chain backgrounds can be expected.

The fact that the performance of a software veto for
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
backgrounds improves with larger detectors and lower intrinsic radon levels makes it complementary to hardware-based approaches such as the cryogenic distillation system used by XENONnT
Murra et al. 2022
, or a charcoal trap
Pushkin et al. 2018
.
This is because as detector size increases, these hardware-based approaches require increasing mass flow rates to retain the same performance, whereas algorithmic approaches do not suffer from this scaling. In addition, software-based approaches perform better if the radon level is already low due to radiopurity controls or hardware-based radon removal methods; in the limiting case where there is on average much less than one
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
in the TPC at any given time, there can simply be a veto on all data within a few half-lives of a
{}^{218}\mathrm{Po}
alpha decay to remove almost all of the
{}^{214}\mathrm{Pb}
background. It should be noted that there are also hardware approaches that do require increasing mass flow rates to retain performance, such as material selection and screening
Aprile et al. 2017b
;
Akerib et al. 2020b
;
Aprile et al. 2022c
, detector design
Dierle et al. 2023
, and material coating
Jörg 2022
.

The methods outlined in this paper can also be used to suppress radon chain backgrounds in liquid argon TPCs, where hardware-based approaches for the mitigation of radon-chain backgrounds are similarly being pursued
Avasthi et al. 2022
.

In addition, the performance of a similar approach applied to reduce the cosmogenic
{}^{137}\mathrm{Xe}
background was also estimated. This background is expected to be a major background in the search for
0\nu\beta\beta
decay in
{}^{136}\mathrm{Xe}
in XENONnT
Aprile et al. 2022a
, LZ
Akerib et al. 2020c
, and next-generation liquid xenon TPCs
Aalbers et al. 2022b
.
It was found that if neutron capture gammas can be selected with high efficiency, then the
{}^{137}\mathrm{Xe}
background can be tagged in XENONnT with an efficiency of
>90\%
, resulting in a
<9\%
background reduction, depending on the convection speed.
