Hyperoperations in Exponential Fields
Juan D. Jaramillo

Abstract
. New sequences of hyperoperations [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] are presented together with their algebraic properties. The commutative hyperoperations reported by Bennett [ 1 ] are defined as a sequence of monoids. After identifying the semirings along the sequence, the corresponding fields are constructed via inverse completion.



1.
Introduction

Hyperoperations are infinite sequences of binary operations extending recursively the definition of the ordinary addition and multiplication
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. The earliest known report on hyperoperations is due to Bennet
[
1
]
in 1914, where commutative hyperoperations are defined starting from the multiplicative identity of the exponential function. More than a decade later, Ackermann
[
2
]
introduces a sequence of hyperoperations that extends the definition of the ordinary addition, multiplication and exponentiation. The latter sequence is here referred as regular hyperoperations, noting that they are noncommutative beyond multiplication. A numerical system based on regular hyperoperations is introduced by Goodstein
[
3
]
in 1947: the complete hereditary representation of nonnegative integers. He also coined the names of tetration, pentation, etc. to denote the regular hyperoperations after exponentiation, and included the
successor
as the primitive regular hyperoperation.

This study presents an algebraic characterization of Bennett’s sequence of commutative hyperoperations and a recursive procedure to create new sequences. The general goal is to provide a more solid ground to the study of hyperoperations. The content is relevant for the study of commutative fields. Possible applications could be found in computer arithmetic, where research remains active in the search for alternatives to the IEEE floating-point to overcome numeric overflow/underflow
[
5
,
6
]
. In particular, it provides a formal background to proposals involving generalized exponentiation such as the Elias
\omega
code
[
7
]
and the level-index codes
[
8
,
9
]
. It is also of interest to the subfield of weighted automata in computer science, where semirings play a central role
[
10
,
11
]
.

This paper is organized as follows: The section
2
reviews the original sequence of commutative hyperoperations and proves that consecutive hyperoperations form a commutative semiring. The section
3
constructs new sequences of hyperoperations from the insight that each commutative hyperoperation is the initial object of a new sequence of hyperopertations. In the section
4
an algorithm to represent nonnegative numbers using commutative hyperoperations is presented. In the section
5
the method of inverse completion
[
12
]
is used to construct a quotient field and its Cauchy completion for each of the former commutative semirings. The section
6
closes the paper embedding the former structures in the usual real numbers and makes remarks pointing to future research.

2.
Monoids and Semirings

Notation
.

Denote the set of natural numbers from the Peano axioms as
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,...\}
, together with its successor function
S:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and its inverse function
S^{-1}
.

Definition 1
.

The sequence of regular hyperoperations generated from the succesor function in
\mathbb{N}
is the sequence of binary operations
H_{n}:(\mathbb{N})^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, defined recursively as

(1)

H_{n}(a,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S(b),&\text{if}\ n=0;\\
a,&\text{if}\ n=1\ \text{and}\ b=0;\\
0,&\text{if}\ n=2\ \text{and}\ b=0;\\
1,&\text{if}\ n\geq 3\ \text{and}\ b=0;\\
H_{n-1}(a,H_{n}(a,S^{-1}(b))),&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proposition 1
.

The tuple
(\mathbb{N},H_{1},H_{2})
is a commutative semiring.

Proof.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

Closure of addition.
By definition
H_{1}(a,0)=a\in\mathbb{N}
. For

(2c)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{0}(a,H_{0}(a,\ldots H_{0}(a,H_{1}(a,0))\ldots))}_{b\ \text{copies of}\ H_{0}}

(2d)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{0}(a,H_{0}(a,\ldots H_{0}(a,H_{0}(a,a))\ldots))}_{b\ \text{copies of}\ H_{0}}\in\mathbb{N}.

(2)

Additive identity.
By definition
H_{1}(a,0)=a

(3a)

\displaystyle=H_{0}(0,a-1)

(3e)

\displaystyle=a.

(3)

(4)

Associativty of addition.

(5a)

\displaystyle H_{1}(a,H_{1}(b,c))

(5c)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{0}(a,H_{0}(a,\ldots H_{0}(a,H_{0}(a,H_{1}(a,b)-1))\ldots))}_{c+1\ \text{copies of}\ H_{0}}

(5d)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{0}(a,H_{0}(a,\ldots H_{0}(a,H_{0}(a,H_{1}(a,b)))\ldots))}_{c\ \text{copies of}\ H_{0}}

(5e)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{1}(a,b),c).

(5)

Closure of multiplication.

(6)

\displaystyle H_{2}(a,b)=\underbrace{H_{1}(a,H_{1}(\ldots H_{1}(a,H_{1}(a,a))\ldots))}_{b\ \text{copies of}\ a}\in\mathbb{N}.

(6)

Multiplicative identity.

(7a)

\displaystyle H_{2}(a,1)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(a,H_{2}(a,0))

(7b)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(a,0)

(7c)

\displaystyle=a

\displaystyle=H_{1}(1,a-1)

(8e)

\displaystyle=a.

(7)

Distributivity.

(9a)

\displaystyle H_{2}(a,H_{1}(b,c))

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{1}(a,H_{1}(a,\ldots H_{1}(a,H_{1}(a,a))\ldots))}_{H_{1}(b,c)-1\ \text{copies of}\ H_{1}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{1}(a,H_{1}(a,\ldots H_{1}(a,H_{2}(a,2))\ldots))}_{H_{1}(b,c)-2\ \text{copies of}\ H_{1}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{1}(a,H_{1}(a,\ldots H_{1}(a,H_{2}(a,3))\ldots))}_{H_{1}(b,c)-3\ \text{copies of}\ H_{1}}

(9d)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{1}(a,H_{1}(a,\ldots H_{1}(a,H_{1}(a,H_{2}(a,c)))\ldots))}_{b\ \text{copies of}\ H_{1}}

(9f)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{1}(a,H_{2}(a,b-1)),H_{2}(a,c))

(9j)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{2}(a,b),H_{2}(a,c)).

\displaystyle H_{2}(H_{1}(b,c),1)

(10b)

(10c)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{2}(b,1),H_{2}(c,1)).

(11a)

\displaystyle H_{2}(H_{1}(b,c),H_{1}(a,1))

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{2}(H_{1}(b,c),a),H_{2}(H_{1}(b,c),1))

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{2}(H_{1}(b,c),a),H_{1}(b,c))

(11c)

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{1}(H_{2}(b,a),H_{2}(c,a)),H_{1}(b,c))

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{1}(H_{2}(b,a),b),H_{1}(H_{2}(c,a),c))

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{1}(H_{2}(b,a),H_{2}(b,1)),H_{1}(H_{2}(c,a),H_{2}(c,1)))

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{2}(b,H_{1}(a,1)),H_{2}(c,H_{1}(a,1))).

The case

.

\displaystyle H_{2}(a,H_{1}(1,b))

\displaystyle=H_{1}(H_{2}(1,a),H_{2}(b,a))

Definition 2
.

Define the exponential function
E:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}:E(a)=H_{3}(w_{0},a)
for a given base
w_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}
. Denote by
L
its inverse function.

Notation
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=E(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})
with
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=\mathbb{N}
. Denote
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=\{\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{1}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{2}}},\ldots\}
such that for
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{k}}}\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
and
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{k}}}\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{k}}}=E(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{k}}})
.

The following results are only valid for those values of
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

.

Remark
.

The function
E
generates a descending filtration in
\mathbb{N}
, meaning
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\subset\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then

(15)

H_{2}(a,b)=E(H_{1}(L(a),L(b))).

Proof.

Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then

(16a)

\displaystyle E(H_{1}(L(a),L(b)))

\displaystyle=H_{3}(w_{0},H_{1}(L(a),L(b)))

(16b)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},\ldots H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},w_{0}))\ldots))}_{H_{1}(L(a),L(b))-1\ \text{copies of}\ H_{2}}

(16c)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},\ldots H_{2}(w_{0},H_{3}(w_{0},2))\ldots))}_{H_{1}(L(a),L(b))-2\ \text{copies of}\ H_{2}}

(16d)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},\ldots H_{2}(w_{0},H_{3}(w_{0},3))\ldots))}_{H_{1}(L(a),L(b))-3\ \text{copies of}\ H_{2}}

(16e)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},\ldots H_{2}(w_{0},H_{3}(w_{0},L(a)))\ldots))}_{L(b)\ \text{copies of}\ H_{2}}

(16f)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},\ldots H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(a,w_{0}))\ldots))}_{L(b)\ \text{copies of}\ H_{2}}

(16g)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},\ldots H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(a,H_{3}(w_{0},2)))\ldots))}_{L(b)-1\ \text{copies of}\ H_{2}}

(16h)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(w_{0},\ldots H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(a,H_{3}(w_{0},3)))\ldots))}_{L(b)-2\ \text{copies of}\ H_{2}}

(16i)

\displaystyle=H_{2}(w_{0},H_{2}(a,H_{3}(w_{0},L(b)-1)))

(16j)

\displaystyle=H_{2}(a,H_{3}(w_{0},L(b)))

(16k)

\displaystyle=H_{2}(a,b).

Definition 3
.

The sequence of commutative hyperoperations generated from the abelian monoid
(\mathbb{N},H_{2})
is the sequence of binary operations
F_{n}:(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})^{2}\rightarrow\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
for
n=2,3,4,\dotsc
, defined recursively as

(17)

F_{n}(a,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}E(H_{2}(L(a),L(b))),&\text{if}\ n=2;\\
E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(b))),&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

For
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, denote

(18)

F_{n}(a,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{1}(a,b),&\text{if}\ n=0;\\
H_{2}(a,b),&\text{if}\ n=1.\end{array}\right.

In Bennett’s original report
[
1
]
is less constructive there is no discussion of the sets
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, instead focusing on the usual real and complex numbers.

Theorem 1
.

The tuple
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n},F_{n+1})
is a commutative semiring.

Proof.

By induction. From Proposition
1
the tuple
(\mathbb{N},H_{1},H_{2})
is a commutative semiring and by definition
(\mathbb{N},H_{1},H_{2})=(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{0},F_{1})
. Assume the tuple
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n-1},F_{n})
is a commutative semiring, with additive identity
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{0}}}
and multiplicative identity
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{1}}}
. The following is the proof that
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n},F_{n+1})
is a commutative semiring. Let
a,b,c\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
.

(1)

Closure of addition.

(19)

F_{n}(a,b)=E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(b)))\in E(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}.

(2)

Additive identity.

(20a)

\displaystyle F_{n}(a,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})

\displaystyle=E(F_{n-1}(L(a),\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{0}}}))

(20b)

\displaystyle=E(L(a))

(20c)

\displaystyle=a.

(3)

Commutativity of addition.

(21a)

\displaystyle F_{n}(a,b)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(b)))

(21b)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n-1}(L(b),L(a)))

(21c)

\displaystyle=F_{n}(b,a).

(4)

Associativity of addition.

(22a)

\displaystyle F_{n}(a,F_{n}(b,c))

\displaystyle=E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(F_{n}(b,c))))

(22b)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(E(F_{n-1}(L(b),L(c))))))

(22c)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n-1}(L(a),F_{n-1}(L(b),L(c))))

(22d)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n-1}(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(b)),L(c)))

(22e)

\displaystyle=F_{n}(E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(b))),E(L(c)))

(5)

Closure of multiplication.

(23)

F_{n+1}(a,b)=E(F_{n}(L(a),L(b)))\in E(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}.

Multiplicative identity.

(24a)

\displaystyle F_{n+1}(a,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{1}}})

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(a),\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{1}}}))

(24b)

\displaystyle=E(L(a))

(24c)

\displaystyle=a.

(7)

Distributivity.

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(a),L(F_{n}(b,c))))

(25b)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(a),F_{n-1}(L(b),L(c))))

(25c)

(26a)

\displaystyle F_{n+1}(a,b)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(a),L(b)))

(26b)

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(b),L(a)))

(26c)

\displaystyle=F_{n+1}(b,a).

(9)

(27a)

\displaystyle F_{n+1}(a,F_{n+1}(b,c))

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(a),L(F_{n+1}(b,c))))

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(a),L(E(F_{n}(L(b),L(c))))))

Definition 4
.

Define the ordering relation

Proposition 3
.

Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then

(29)

a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b\iff E(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}E(b).

Proof.

The proof that
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b\Rightarrow E(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}E(b)
is the following. The relation
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b
is equivalent to the existence of an element
c\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
such that
F_{n}(a,c)=b
. The latter is equivalent to the equation
F_{n+1}(E(a),E(c))=E(b)
. Since
F_{n+1}(E(a),E(c))=F_{n}(E(a),c^{\prime})
, where
c^{\prime}=S^{-1}(E(c))
,
then
E(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}E(b)
. The converse statement is proven in a similar way.
∎

.

a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{<}}b\iff a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b.

Let

a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{<}}b\Rightarrow a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b
is the following: If
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{<}}b
, the strict monotonicity of
L
in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, from Proposition
3
, implies
L(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{<}}L(b)
, thereby
\exists c^{\prime}:F_{n-1}(L(a),c^{\prime})=L(b)
. Without loss of generality let
c^{\prime}=L(c)
, where
c\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
. Then

(31)

E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(c)))=E(L(b)),

i.e.,
F_{n}(a,c)=b
, thereby
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b
. The proof that
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{<}}b\Leftarrow a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b
is the following: If
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}b
, then
\exists c\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
such that
F_{n}(a,c)=b
, that is

(32)

E(F_{n-1}(L(a),L(c)))=E(L(b)).

Since
E
is an injective function, then

(33)

F_{n-1}(L(a),L(c))=L(b),

implying
L(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{<}}L(b)
. Since
L
is strictly monotone in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{<}}b
.
∎

Remark
.

The ordering relation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}
in the monoid
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n})
is equivalent to the restriction of the standard ordering
<
in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
as a subset of
\mathbb{N}
.

Remark
.

Since
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{1}}}=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{0}}}
, all identity elements can be written as
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}}
, for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}
. They can be generated as

(34a)

\displaystyle\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{0}}}

\displaystyle=H_{4}(w_{0},-1)=0,

(34b)

\displaystyle\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{0}}}

\displaystyle=H_{4}(w_{0},0)=1,

(34c)

\displaystyle\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{0}}}

\displaystyle=H_{4}(w_{0},1)=w_{0},

(34d)

\displaystyle\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{0}}}

\displaystyle=H_{4}(w_{0},n)=\underbrace{H_{3}(w_{0},H_{3}(w_{0},\dotsc,H_{3}(w_{0},w_{0})))}_{n\ \text{copies of}\ w_{0}},

In the expression
H_{4}(w_{0},n)
, the integer argument
w_{0}
is known as the

and the integer argument

is known as the

or

. For completness, in the Eq. (

H_{4}
is known as

, restricted to the natural numbers.

Underlying the sequence of commutative hyperoperations is the fact that any injective function
f
with domain in a monoid, generates an isomorphism between monoids. Let
a,b\in f(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})
. The right conjugation of ordinary addition
F_{0}
by
f
is

(35)

{\rm Ad}_{f}(F_{0})(a,b)=f(F_{0}(f^{-1}(a),f^{-1}(b)).

The operation

forms a monoid in

which is isomorphic to
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{0})
. In fact all properties studied so far can be reproduced in this new setting; if one starts from the successor function
{\rm Ad}_{f}(S)
in
f(\mathbb{N})
, it is easy to prove by induction that the corresponding regular hyperoperations take the form
{\rm Ad}_{f}(H_{n})
.
Further assuming

is composable with itself, the tuples

become a sequence of commutative monoids. A sequence of commutative semirings can be formed as
\{(f^{n}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}),{\rm Ad}_{f^{n}}(F_{0}),{\rm Ad}_{f^{n}}(F_{1}))\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
f
as the generator of semiring isomorphisms. A special case is when the function
f
is
exponential
along the sequence of semirings, meaning that
f^{n}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})\cap f^{n+1}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})
is a nonempty set where,

(36)

{\rm Ad}_{f^{n}}(F_{1})={\rm Ad}_{f^{n+1}}(F_{0}).

3.
New Sequences of Hyperoperations

This section presents new sequences of hyperoperations originated from Peano systems initially associated to the semirings
\{(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n},F_{n+1})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The commutative hyperoperations
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are shown to be regular hyperoperations. A recursive procedure to create new sequences of hyperoperations is stablished.

Definition 5
.

Define the successor function in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
as the recursive function

(37)

S_{n}:\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\rightarrow\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\setminus\{\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}}\}:S_{n}(a)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S(a),&\text{if}\ n=0;\\
E(S_{n-1}(L(a))),&\text{if}\ n>0.\end{array}\right.

Denote its inverse function as
S_{n}^{-1}
.

Remark
.

The tuples
\{(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},S_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of Peano systems with initial element
(\mathbb{N},0,S)
.

Definition 6
.

The sequence of regular hyperoperations generated from the succesor in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
is the sequence of binary operations
[n:m]:(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})^{2}\rightarrow\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, defined recursively as

(38)

a\>[n:m]\>b=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S_{n}(b),&\text{if}\ m=0;\\
a,&\text{if}\ m=1\ \text{and}\ b=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}};\\
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},&\text{if}\ m=2\ \text{and}\ b=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}};\\
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{1}}},&\text{if}\ m\geq 3\ \text{and}\ b=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}};\\
a\>[n:m-1]\>(a\>[n:m]\>S_{n}^{-1}(b)),&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Note that
a\>[0:m]\>b=H_{m}(a,b)
for
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
.

The following results are valid for those values of
[n:m]
such that
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 5
.

The tuple
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},[n:1],[n:2])
is a commutative semiring with additive identity
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}}
and multiplicative identity
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{1}}}
.

Proof.

The case
n=0
is proven in Theorem
1
, in account that
a\>[0:i]\>b=H_{i}(a,b)
, for
i=1,2
. The remaining cases are proven analogously, using the Peano system
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},S_{n})
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then

(39)

a\>[n:m]\>b=E(L(a)\>[n-1:m]\>L(b)).

Proof.

By induction. Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
. Without loss of generality let
b=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{k}}}
where
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The case
m=0
is trivial. For
m=1
, one obtains

(40a)

\displaystyle a\>[n:1]\>b

\displaystyle=\underbrace{a\>[n:0]\>(a\>[n:0]\>\dotsb(a\>[n:0]\>(a\>[n:0]\>a))\dotsb)}_{k\ \text{copies of}\ [n:0]}

(40b)

\displaystyle=S_{n}^{k}(a)

(40c)

\displaystyle=E\circ S_{n-1}^{k}(L(a))

(40d)

\displaystyle=E(L(a)\>[n-1:1]\>L(b)).

Assume

(41)

a\>[n:m-1]\>b=E(L(a)\>[n-1:m-1]\>L(b)),

then for
m>1
, one obtains

(42a)

\displaystyle a\>[n:m]\>b=\underbrace{a\>[n:m-1]\>(a\>[n:m-1]\>\dotsb(a\>[n:m-1]\>(a\>[n:m-1]\>a))\dotsb)}_{L^{n}(b)\ \text{copies of}\ a}

(42b)

\displaystyle=E(\underbrace{L(a)\>[n-1:m-1]\>(L(a)\>[n-1:m-1]\>\dotsb(L(a)\>[n-1:m-1]\>(L(a)\>[n-1:m-1]\>L(a)))\dotsb)}_{L^{n}(b)\ \text{copies of}\ L(a)})

(42c)

\displaystyle=E(L(a)\>[n-1:m]\>L(b)).

∎

The following corollary states that the commutative hyperoperations from Definition
3
are regular hyperoperations for a given Peano system, as depicted in Definition
6
.

.

Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then

By induction. Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

and

one obtains that

Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
. From Theorem

one obtains

(45a)

\displaystyle a\>[n:2]\>b

\displaystyle=E(L(a)\>[n-1:2]\>L(b))

\displaystyle=E(F_{n}(L(a),L(b)))

(45c)

∎

By induction. Let
a,b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

and Proposition

(47d)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{a\>[0:1]\>\dotsb\>[0:1]\>a}_{b\ \text{copies of}\ a}.

.

Let

The sequence
\{(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},S_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is generated from the system
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{0}}},S_{0})
via the exponential function

. In general, a Peano system induces an exponential semiring from its regular hyperoperations. The exponential function of this semiring is used to generate a sequence of Peano systems. This is a recursive procedure. Identifying a new sequence requires recording previous choices of generating systems.

Notation
.

The index of a sequence of Peano systems of level

, is an element of the set

(55)

I_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{N},&\text{if}\ k=1;\\

where
{\bf n}_{k-1}=(n_{1},n_{2},\dotsc,n_{k-1})
is a fixed element in
\mathbb{N}^{k-1}
, recording previous choices of Peano systems. For
k>1
, let
{\bf n}_{k}=({\bf n}_{k-1},n_{k})\in I_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}
and
l\in\mathbb{Z}
. Whenever
n_{k}+l\in\mathbb{N}
, denote

(56)

{\bf n}_{k}+l=({\bf n}_{k-1},n_{k}+l).

\displaystyle\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{0}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=\mathbb{N},\quad S_{{\bf n}_{0}}=S,\quad E_{{\bf n}_{0}}=E,\quad L_{{\bf n}_{0}}=L.

The function

\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}+1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\subset\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
. Further note that
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\subset\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k-1}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

Definition 8
.

Define the successor function in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
as the recursive function

(59)

S_{{\bf n}_{k}}:\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\rightarrow\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\setminus\{\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{0}}}\}:S_{{\bf n}_{k}}(a)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S(a),&\text{if}\ k=0;\\

E_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}(S_{{\bf n}_{k}-1}(L_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}(a))),&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Denote its inverse function as
S_{{\bf n}_{k}}^{-1}
.

Definition 9
.

The sequence of regular hyperoperations generated from the
succesor function in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

[{\bf n}_{k}:m]:(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})^{2}\rightarrow\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{0}}},&\text{if}\ m=2\ \text{and}\ b=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{0}}};\\
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{1}}},&\text{if}\ m\geq 3\ \text{and}\ b=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{0}}};\\
a\>[{\bf n}_{k}:m-1]\>(a\>[{\bf n}_{k}:m]\>S_{{\bf n}_{k}}^{-1}(b)),&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Note that if

From Definitions

.

Proposition 11
.

\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k-1}+n_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\quad\text{and}\quad a\>[{\bf n}_{k}:m]\>b=a\>[{\bf n}_{k-1}+n_{k}:m]\>b.

Proof.

Note that
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=E^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle({\bf n}_{k-1},0)}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})=E^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k-1}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})

\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k-1}+n_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=E^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-2}}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k-1}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})
. Let
w_{k-1}=w_{k-2}\>[{\bf n}_{k-1}:4]\>(n_{k-1}+1)
. From Proposition

, therefore
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf n}_{k-1}+n_{k}}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}

and
10

(71a)

\displaystyle a\>[{\bf n}_{k}:m]\>b

\displaystyle=E^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}(L^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}(a)\>[{\bf n}_{k-1}:m]\>L^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-1}}(b))

(71b)

\displaystyle=E^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-2}}(L^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-2}}(a)\>[{\bf n}_{k-1}:m]\>L^{n_{k}}_{{\bf n}_{k-2}}(b))

\displaystyle=a\>[{\bf n}_{k-1}+n_{k}:m]\>b.

∎

4.
Representation of Natural Numbers

A recursive representation of nonnegative integers using commutative hyperoperations can be obtained from its power series expansion. Let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, the reader is reminded of ordinary notation
a+b=H_{1}(a,b)
,
a\times b=H_{2}(a,b)
and
a^{b}=H_{3}(a,b)
. It is well known that any integer
a\in\mathbb{N}
can be represented uniquely as a power series base
w
in the form

(72)

a=d_{0}+d_{1}\times w+d_{2}\times w^{2}+\dotsb+d_{m}\times w^{m},

where the coefficients
d_{k}\in\{0,1,2,\dotsc,S^{-1}(w)\}
are the digits and the maximum exponent
m\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
d_{k>m}=0
. In the power series of Eq. (
72
) one can write each exponent as a power series base
w
and proceed iteratively. This is known as the hereditary representation of a natural number. Let
w
be the base of the exponential function
E
, underlying the definition of the commutative hyperoperations
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. This power series can be written recursively as

(73)

a_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d_{0},&\text{if}\ k=0;\\
F_{0}(F_{1}(d_{k},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{k}}}),a_{k-1}),&\text{if}\ 0<k\leq m.\end{array}\right.

The term
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{k}}}
is the scale of the digit
d_{k}
and
a_{m}=a
. Now consider the coefficients
e_{k}\in\{\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{1}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},\dotsc,S_{n}^{-1}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{w}}})\}
for
0\leq k\leq m
. Applying the function
E^{n}
to both sides of Eq. (
73
) one arrives at the more general statement that any element
b\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
can be represented as a power series in the semiring
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n},F_{n+1})
, taking the form

(74)

b_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{0},&\text{if}\ k=0;\\
F_{n}(F_{n+1}(e_{k},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{k}}}),b_{k-1}),&\text{if}\ 0<k\leq m.\end{array}\right.

The term
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{k}}}
is the
scale
of the
digit

e_{k}
and
b_{m}=b
. The Eq. (
74
) allows hereditary representations written in terms of commutative hyperoperations. The scale
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{k}}}\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, can be represented as a power series in the semiring
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{1},F_{2})
and so on. The procedure repeats iteratively until all scales are expanded. For example, the power series expansion of
266
base
w=3
is

(75)

In the usual hereditary representation, the exponent
k=5
is replaced by its power series expansion
k=2\times 3^{0}+1\times 3^{1}

) with

and

(76b)

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=F_{0}(F_{1}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{1}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{1}}}),a_{0}),

(76c)

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=F_{0}(F_{1}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{2}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{2}}}),a_{1}),

(76d)

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=a_{2},

(76e)

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle=a_{3},

(76f)

\displaystyle a_{5}

\displaystyle=F_{0}(F_{1}(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{1}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{5}}}),a_{4})=a.

According to the hereditary representation, the scale
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{5}}}
in
a_{5}
from Eq. (
76

5.
Groups and Fields

This section presents the standard construction of the rational numbers
[
12
]
as a parallel construction departing from the initial sets
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The procedure of inverse completion is applied to each commutative monoid
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n})
, obtaining the abelian group
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square})
. A multiplication
\otimes_{n,\square}
is defined in
\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}
, making the tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
an integral domain. After applying inverse completion to the latter, it is obtained the field of quotients
(\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes},\oplus_{n,\boxtimes},\otimes_{n,\boxtimes})
.

5.1.
Construction of abelian groups from commutative monoids

Definition 11
.

Define the cartesian product
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\oplus_{0})
such that

(83)

\forall(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}:(x,y)\oplus_{0}(u,v)=(F_{0}(x,u),F_{0}(y,v)).

Definition 12
.

Define the function
\vec{E}
in
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
as

(84)

\vec{E}(x,y)=(E(x),E(y)).

Denote by
\vec{L}
its inverse function.

Remark
.

Since
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\subset\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then
\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\subset\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n-1}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
.

Definition 13
.

Define the binary operation

\displaystyle\oplus_{n}:(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})^{2}\rightarrow\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}:

(85)

\displaystyle(x,y)\oplus_{n}(u,v)=(F_{n}(x,u),F_{n}(y,v)).

Note that

(86)

(x,y)\oplus_{n}(u,v)=\vec{E}(\vec{L}(x,y)\oplus_{n-1}\vec{L}(u,v)).

Proposition 12
.

Let
(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
. The relation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}
defined as

(87)

(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(u,v)\iff F_{n}(x,v)=F_{n}(y,u),

is a congruence relation on the commutative monoid
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\oplus_{n})
.

Proof.

Reflexivity and symmetry are straightforward. It remains to prove transitivity and compatibility:

(1)

Transitivity.
Let
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
for
i=1,2,3
, such that

(88)

(x_{1},y_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(x_{2},y_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad(x_{2},y_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(x_{3},y_{3}).

Since

(89)

F_{n}(x_{1},y_{2})=F_{n}(x_{2},y_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad F_{n}(x_{2},y_{3})=F_{n}(x_{3},y_{2}),

adding both equations one obtains

(90)

F_{n}(x_{1},y_{3})=F_{n}(x_{3},y_{1}).

That is
(x_{1},y_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(x_{3},y_{3})
.

(2)

Compatibility.
Let
(x_{i},y_{i}),(u_{i},v_{i})\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
for
i=1,2
, such that

(91)

(x_{1},y_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(x_{2},y_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad(u_{1},v_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(u_{2},v_{2}).

For
i=1,2
, denote

(92)

(a_{i},b_{i})=(x_{i},y_{i})\oplus_{n}(u_{i},v_{i})=(F_{n}(x_{i},u_{i}),F_{n}(y_{i},v_{i})).

From Eq. (
91
) it follows that

(93)

F_{n}(a_{1},b_{2})=F_{n}(a_{2},b_{1}),

implying
(a_{1},b_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(a_{2},b_{2})
.

∎

Notation
.

Consider the quotient structure

(94)

(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square})=\left(\frac{\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}},\oplus_{n,\square}\right),

where
\oplus_{n,\square}
is the operation induced on
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}})/\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}
by
\oplus_{n}
. Denote by
[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}
the equivalence class of
(x,y)
under
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}
.

Remark
.

For all
(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, if
(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(u,v)
, then
(y,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(v,u)
.

Definition 14
.

Define the involution

\displaystyle T_{n,\square}\>:\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}:

(95)

\displaystyle T_{n,\square}\>[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}=[\![(y,x)]\!]_{n,\square}.

Proposition 13
.

The tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square})
is an abelian group.

Proof.

Since
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}
is a congruence relation on the commutative monoid
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\oplus_{n})
, the corresponding quotient structure
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square})
is also a commutative monoid. It is straightforward to verify that the involution
T_{n,\square}
is the group inverse in
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square})
, therefore it is a group.
∎

Definition 15
.

The ordering
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}
is defined as

(96)

[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}[\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}\iff F_{n}(x,v)<F_{n}(y,u).

Proposition 14
.

Let
(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, then

(97)

(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(u,v)\iff\vec{E}((x,y))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\square}}\vec{E}((u,v)).

Proof.

The proof that
(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(u,v)\Rightarrow\vec{E}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\square}}\vec{E}(u,v)
is the following: If
(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(u,v)
, then
F_{n}(x,v)=F_{n}(y,u)
. Applying the exponential
E
at both sides, one obtains
F_{n+1}(E(x),E(v))=F_{n+1}(E(y),E(u))
. From the definition of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\square}}
, this implies
(E(x),E(y))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\square}}(E(u),E(v))
, that is,
\vec{E}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\square}}\vec{E}(u,v)
. Noting that
E
is an injective function, the converse statement is proven in a similar way.
∎

Definition 16
.

Define the function
E_{\square}
in
U_{\mathbb{Z}}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}
as

(98)

E_{\square}([\![a]\!]_{n,\square})=[\![\vec{E}(a)]\!]_{n+1,\square}.

Denote by
L_{\square}
its inverse function.

Remark
.

Note that
\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}=E_{\square}(\mathbb{Z}_{n-1,\square})
.

Proposition 15
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}
, then

(99)

a\oplus_{n,\square}b=E_{\square}(L_{\square}(a)\oplus_{n-1,\square}L_{\square}(b)).

Proof.

Let
a=[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}
and
b=[\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}
, then

(100a)

\displaystyle[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}\oplus_{n,\square}[\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}

\displaystyle=[\![(x,y)\oplus_{n}(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}

(100b)

\displaystyle=[\![\vec{E}(\vec{L}(x,y)\oplus_{n-1}\vec{L}(u,v))]\!]_{n,\square}

(100c)

\displaystyle=E_{\square}([\![\vec{L}(x,y)\oplus_{n-1}\vec{L}(u,v)]\!]_{n-1,\square})

(100d)

\displaystyle=E_{\square}([\![\vec{L}(x,y)]\!]_{n-1,\square}\oplus_{n-1,\square}[\![\vec{L}(u,v)]\!]_{n-1,\square})

(100e)

\displaystyle=E_{\square}(L_{\square}([\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square})\oplus_{n-1,\square}L_{\square}([\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square})).

∎

Notation
.

Denote
k_{n,\square}=[\![(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{k}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}
and
\mathbb{N}_{n,\square}=[\![(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}
.

Remark
.

Every element in the abelian group
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square})
can be written as

, where

.

5.2.
Construction of integral domains from abelian groups

Definition 17
.

Define the binary operation

\displaystyle(x,y)\otimes_{n}(u,v)=(F_{n}(F_{n+1}(x,u),F_{n+1}(y,v)),F_{n}(F_{n+1}(y,u),F_{n+1}(x,v))).

.

Let
(x_{i},y_{i}),(u_{i},v_{i})\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}\times\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
for

, such that

(102)

(103)

((x_{1},y_{1})\otimes_{n}(u_{1},v_{1}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}((x_{2},y_{2})\otimes_{n}(u_{2},v_{2})).

For
i=1,2
, denote

(104)

(a_{i},b_{i})=((x_{i},y_{i})\otimes_{n}(u_{i},v_{i}))=(F_{n}(F_{n+1}(x_{i},u_{i}),F_{n+1}(y_{i},v_{i})),F_{n}(F_{n+1}(y_{i},u_{i}),F_{n+1}(x_{i},v_{i}))).

From Eq. (
102
) it follows that

(105)

F_{n}(a_{1},b_{2})=F_{n}(b_{1},a_{2}),

therefore

(106)

(a_{1},b_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\square}}(a_{2},b_{2}).

∎

Definition 18
.

Define the binary operation

\displaystyle\otimes_{n,\square}:(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square})^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}:

(107)

\displaystyle[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}\otimes_{n,\square}[\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}=[\![(x,y)\otimes_{n}(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}.

.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}
, then

(108)

a\otimes_{n,\square}b=E_{\square}(L_{\square}(a)\otimes_{n-1,\square}L_{\square}(b)).

Let
a=[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}
and
b=[\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}
, then

(109a)

\displaystyle[\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square}\otimes_{n,\square}[\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square}

(109b)

\displaystyle=[\![(F_{n}(F_{n+1}(x,u),F_{n+1}(y,v)),F_{n}(F_{n+1}(x,v),F_{n+1}(y,u)))]\!]_{n,\square}

(109c)

\displaystyle=E_{\square}([\![(F_{n-1}(F_{n}(L(x),L(u)),F_{n}(L(y),L(v))),F_{n-1}(F_{n}(L(x),L(v)),F_{n}(L(y),L(u))))]\!]_{n-1,\square})

(109d)

\displaystyle=E_{\square}([\![(L(x),L(y))]\!]_{n-1,\square}\otimes_{n-1,\square}[\![(L(u),L(v))]\!]_{n-1,\square})

(109e)

\displaystyle=E_{\square}(L_{\square}([\![(x,y)]\!]_{n,\square})\otimes_{n-1,\square}L_{\square}([\![(u,v)]\!]_{n,\square})).

∎

Proposition 18
.

The tuple
(\mathbb{N}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is a commutative semiring.

Proof.

The semiring structure in
(\mathbb{N}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is induced by
(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}},F_{n},F_{n+1})
, in account that

(110a)

\displaystyle x_{n,\square}\oplus_{n,\square}y_{n,\square}

\displaystyle=[\![(F_{n}(x,y),\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square},

(110b)

\displaystyle x_{n,\square}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{n,\square}

\displaystyle=[\![(F_{n+1}(x,y),\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}.

∎

Remark
.

The additive inverse
T_{n,\square}
in
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
, exhibits the following properties:

\displaystyle T_{n,\square}\>(T_{n,\square}\>a)=a,

(111b)

\displaystyle(T_{n,\square}\>a)\oplus_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>b)=T_{n,\square}\>(a\oplus_{n,\square}b),

\displaystyle(T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}b=a\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>b)=T_{n,\square}\>(a\otimes_{n,\square}b),

\displaystyle(T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>b)=a\otimes_{n,\square}b.

.

From Proposition

.
∎

Theorem 3
.

The tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is an integral domain.

Proof.

From Proposition
13
the tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square})
is an abelian group and from Proposition
19
the tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is a commutative monoid. The tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is a commutative ring, if it fulfills distributivity. Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}_{n,\square}
. It is sufficient to prove the following eight cases:

(112a)

\displaystyle a\otimes_{n,\square}(b\oplus_{n,\square}c)

\displaystyle=(a\otimes_{n,\square}b)\oplus_{n,\square}(a\otimes_{n,\square}c),

(112b)

\displaystyle a\otimes_{n,\square}(b\oplus_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c))

\displaystyle=(a\otimes_{n,\square}b)\oplus_{n,\square}(a\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c)),

(112c)

\displaystyle a\otimes_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>b)\oplus_{n,\square}c)

\displaystyle=(a\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>b))\oplus_{n,\square}(a\otimes_{n,\square}c),

(112d)

\displaystyle a\otimes_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>b)\oplus_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c))

\displaystyle=(a\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>b))\oplus_{n,\square}(a\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c)),

(112e)

\displaystyle(T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}(b\oplus_{n,\square}c)

\displaystyle=((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}b)\oplus_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}c),

(112f)

\displaystyle(T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}(b\oplus_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c))

\displaystyle=((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}b)\oplus_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c)),

(112g)

\displaystyle(T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>b)\oplus_{n,\square}c)

\displaystyle=((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>b))\oplus_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}c),

(112h)

\displaystyle(T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>b)\oplus_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c))

\displaystyle=((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>b))\oplus_{n,\square}((T_{n,\square}\>a)\otimes_{n,\square}(T_{n,\square}\>c)).

Let
x,y,z\in\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\mathbb{N}}}}
such that
a=[\![(x,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square},b=[\![(y,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square},c=[\![(z,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}
. The Eq. (
112b
) becomes,

(113a)

\displaystyle[\![(x,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}\otimes_{n,\square}([\![(y,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}\oplus_{n,\square}[\![(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},z)]\!]_{n,\square})

(113b)

\displaystyle=[\![(x,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}\otimes_{n,\square}[\![(y,z)]\!]_{n,\square}

(113c)

\displaystyle=[\![(F_{n+1}(x,y),F_{n+1}(x,z))]\!]_{n,\square}

(113d)

\displaystyle=[\![(F_{n+1}(x,y),\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}\oplus_{n,\square}[\![(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},F_{n+1}(x,z))]\!]_{n,\square}

(113e)

\displaystyle=([\![(x,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}\otimes_{n,\square}[\![(y,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square})\oplus_{n,\square}([\![(x,\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}})]\!]_{n,\square}\otimes_{n,\square}[\![(\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},z)]\!]_{n,\square}).

Similarly, one proves Eqs. (
112c
), (
112f
) and (
112g
). The remaining cases follow from the fact that
(\mathbb{N}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is a commutative semiring and from the properties in Eq. (
111
). To prove that the commutative ring

is an integral domain one has to prove that the multiplication
\otimes_{n,\square}
is closed in
\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\setminus\{0_{n,\square}\}
. If follows from the fact that the equations

(114)

F_{n}(F_{n+1}(x,u),F_{n+1}(y,v))=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{n}(F_{n+1}(y,u),F_{n+1}(x,v))=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}},

from Definition

, except for

or
(u,v)
equal to
(0,0)
.
∎

Proposition 21
.

The tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast},\oplus^{\prime}_{n})

Proof.

Some expressions below are simplified using the standard order of operations where multiplication has precedence over addition.

(1)

Closure.
Let
(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}
, be such that
(x,y)\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(u,v)=(a,b)
. From Theorem
3
, the tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is an integral domain. Since the latter is a ring and it has no nonzero zero divisors, then
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}
.

(2)

Identity element.
It is straightforward to verify that for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}
, it holds that

(120)

(x,y)\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(0_{n,\square},1_{n,\square})=(0_{n,\square},1_{n,\square})\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(x,y)=(x,y).

(3)

Commutativity.

(121a)

\displaystyle(x,y)\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle=((x\otimes_{n,\square}v)\oplus_{n,\square}(y\otimes_{n,\square}u),y\otimes_{n,\square}v)

(121b)

\displaystyle=((u\otimes_{n,\square}y)\oplus_{n,\square}(v\otimes_{n,\square}x),v\otimes_{n,\square}y)

(121c)

\displaystyle=(u,v)\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(x,y).

(4)

Associativity.
Let
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}
for

.

(122a)

\displaystyle(x_{1},y_{1})\oplus^{\prime}_{n}((x_{2},y_{2})\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(x_{3},y_{3}))

(122b)

\displaystyle=(x_{1},y_{1})\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(x_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}\oplus_{n,\square}y_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}x_{3},y_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3})

(122c)

\displaystyle=(x_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}\oplus_{n,\square}y_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}(x_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}\oplus_{n,\square}x_{3}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2}),y_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3})

(122d)

Proposition 22
.

Let
(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}
. The cross-relation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}
defined as

(123)

(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(u,v)\iff x\otimes_{n,\square}v=u\otimes_{n,\square}y,

is a congruence relation on the tuple
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast},\oplus^{\prime}_{n},\otimes^{\prime}_{n})
.

Proof.

Reflexivity and symmetry are straightforward. It remains to prove transitivity and compatibility:

(1)

Transitivity.
Let
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}
for
i=1,2,3
, such that

(124)

(x_{1},y_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(x_{2},y_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad(x_{2},y_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(x_{3},y_{3}),

that is

(125)

x_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2}=x_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}=x_{3}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2}.

Multiplying both equations one obtains obtains

(126)

x_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}x_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}=x_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}x_{3}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2},

that is

(127)

x_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}(x_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}\oplus_{n,\square}T_{n,\square}\>(y_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}x_{3}))=0_{n,\square}.

Since
(\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square},\oplus_{n,\square},\otimes_{n,\square})
is an integral domain, either
x_{2}=0_{n,\square}
or
x_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}\oplus_{n,\square}T_{n,\square}\>(y_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}x_{3})=\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{0}}}
. If
x_{2}=0_{n,\square}
, the Eq. (
125
) implies
x_{1}=x_{3}=0_{n,\square}
, therefore
(x_{1},y_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(x_{3},y_{3})
. If
x_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}\oplus_{n,\square}T_{n,\square}\>(y_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}x_{3})=0_{n,\square}
, then
x_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{3}=x_{3}\otimes_{n,\square}y_{1}
, that is
(x_{1},y_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(x_{3},y_{3})
.

(2)

Compatibility.
Let
(x_{i},y_{i}),(u_{i},v_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}
for
i=1,2
, such that

(128)

(x_{1},y_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(x_{2},y_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad(u_{1},v_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(u_{2},v_{2}).

For
i=1,2
, denote

(129a)

\displaystyle(a_{i},b_{i})

\displaystyle=(x_{i},y_{i})\oplus^{\prime}_{n}(u_{i},v_{i})=(x_{i}\otimes_{n,\square}v_{i}\oplus_{n,\square}y_{i}\otimes_{n,\square}u_{i},y_{i}\otimes_{n,\square}v_{i}),

(129b)

\displaystyle(a^{\prime}_{i},b^{\prime}_{i})

\displaystyle=(x_{i},y_{i})\otimes^{\prime}_{n}(u_{i},v_{i})=(x_{i}\otimes_{n,\square}u_{i},y_{i}\otimes_{n,\square}v_{i}).

From Eq. (
128
) it follows that

(130)

a_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}b_{2}=a_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}b_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad a^{\prime}_{1}\otimes_{n,\square}b^{\prime}_{2}=a^{\prime}_{2}\otimes_{n,\square}b^{\prime}_{1},

implying

(131)

(a_{1},b_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(a_{2},b_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad(a^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(a^{\prime}_{2},b^{\prime}_{2}).

∎

Consider the quotient structure

Theorem 4

The tuple
(\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes},\oplus_{n,\boxtimes},\otimes_{n,\boxtimes})

Proof.

From Propositions
20
,
21

and complete the proof that the tuple

(137a)

(137d)

Proposition 23
.

Let
(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n,\square}^{\ast}

(139)

(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(u,v)\iff\vec{E}_{\square}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\boxtimes}}\vec{E}_{\square}(u,v).

Proof.

The proof that
(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(u,v)\Rightarrow\vec{E}_{\square}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\boxtimes}}\vec{E}_{\square}(u,v)
is the following: If
(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\boxtimes}}(u,v)
then
F_{n+1}(x,v)=F_{n+1}(y,u)
. Applying the exponential
E
at both sides, one obtains
F_{n+2}(E(x),E(v))=F_{n+2}(E(y),E(u))
. From the definition of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\boxtimes}}
, this implies
(E(x),E(y))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\boxtimes}}(E(u),E(v))
, that is,
\vec{E}_{\square}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n+1}}{{\boxtimes}}\vec{E}_{\square}(u,v)
. Noting that
E
is an injective function, the converse statement is proven in a similar way.

Proposition 24
.

(141b)

Let

5.4.
Construction of real numbers from fields of quotients

There are several ways to construct the usual real numbers (
\mathbb{R}_{0,\boxtimes}
) from the usual rational numbers (
\mathbb{Q}_{0,\boxtimes}
). Analogously, one can construct the reals
\mathbb{R}_{n,\boxtimes}
from
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes}
. Define the absolute value in
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes}
as

(144)

|a|_{n,\boxtimes}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{n,\boxtimes}\>a,&\text{if}\ a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{<}}0_{n,\boxtimes};\\
0_{n,\boxtimes},&\text{if}\ a=0_{n,\boxtimes};\\
a,&\text{if}\ a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{>}}0_{n,\boxtimes}.\end{array}\right.

In the method of Cauchy sequences, the set
\mathbb{R}_{n,\boxtimes}
is the Cauchy completion of
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes}
with respect to the metric

(145)

d_{n,\boxtimes}(a,b)=|a\oplus_{n,\boxtimes}T_{n,\boxtimes}\>b|_{n,\boxtimes}.

Any number in
\mathbb{R}_{n,\boxtimes}
is associated to the equivalence class of a Cauchy sequence whose elements belongs to
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes}
. The equivalence class, denoted
[\![\cdot]\!]_{n,\mathbb{R}}
, is that of Cauchy sequences whose distance in the metric
d_{n,\boxtimes}
tends to the additive identity
0_{n,\boxtimes}
. That is, if
a=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dotsc)
and
b=(b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},\dotsc)
are two Cauchy sequences in
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes}
, then

(146)

[\![a]\!]_{n,\mathbb{R}}=[\![b]\!]_{n,\mathbb{R}}\iff\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}d_{n,\boxtimes}(a_{k},b_{k})=0_{n,\boxtimes}.

Any function in
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\boxtimes}
is naturally extended to
\mathbb{R}_{n,\boxtimes}
by applying the former to every element of the representative Cauchy sequence.
