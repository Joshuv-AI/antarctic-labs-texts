1.
Introduction

To study the transition from localized to delocalized phase of Anderson operators, Kritchevski, Valkó and Virag
[
8
]
introduced the following random Schrödinger operator defined on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}:=\{0,1,\cdots,n\}\subset\mathbb{Z}

(1.1)

(H_{n}\psi)_{\ell}=\psi_{\ell-1,n}+\psi_{\ell+1,n}+v_{\ell,n}\psi_{\ell,n},\quad\psi_{0}=\psi_{n+1}=0,

and considered the
vanishing model

(1.2)

v_{k,n}=\sigma\omega_{k}/n^{\alpha},

and the
decaying model

(1.3)

\quad v_{k,n}=\sigma\omega_{k}/k^{\alpha}

for some
\alpha>0
. The random variables
\omega_{k}
are independent, mean 0 and variance 1 random variables. The phase transition takes place at the value
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, where interesting point process scaling limits of the eigenvalue distributions are expected. Prior to the work
[
8
]
, the spectral properties of the decaying model were investigated in
[
6
]
,
[
5
]
, showing that for the infinite volume version of the decaying model, the spectrum is absolutely continuous for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2},
is pure point for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and has mixed behavior for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}.
Moreover, Poisson statistics is expected for eigenvalues of the decaying model when
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, in the sense of
[
10
]
,
[
2
]
.

As the potentials
v_{k,n}
are vanishing, the eigenvalues of
H_{n}
should approximate that of the free Laplacian, which has density of states described by the arcsine law
\frac{\rho}{2\pi}
where

(1.4)

\rho=\rho(E)=1/\sqrt{1-E^{2}/4}.

To obtain nontrivial scaling limits, we consider the spectrum
\Lambda_{n}
of the rescaled operator

(1.5)

\rho n(H_{n}-E)

for any
E\in(-2,2)
.
Define

z=E/2+i\sqrt{1-(E/2)^{2}},

then it is proved in
[
8
]
that for the vanishing model (
1.2
) with
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, for
0<|E|<2
and with
\tau=(\sigma\rho)^{2}
, we have the convergence in distribution of the point process
\Lambda_{n}-\arg(z^{2n+2})-\pi
to the point process

\text{Sch}_{\tau}:=\{\lambda:\varphi^{\lambda/\tau}(\tau)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}\}
,
where
\tau=(\sigma\rho)^{2}
and
\varphi^{\lambda}
solves the family of SDEs

(1.6)

d\varphi^{\lambda}(t)=\lambda dt+d\mathcal{B}+\text{Re}[e^{-i\varphi^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}],\quad\varphi^{\lambda}(0)=0

and
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{W}
are independent real and complex standard Brownian motions. For the decaying model (
1.3
) with
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, they proved the point process
\Lambda_{n}
converges to a point process
\Lambda
that agrees with the
\text{Sine}_{\beta}
process from random matrix theory
[
13
]
, with
\beta=\frac{2}{(\sigma\rho)^{2}}
.

When
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
and the potentials decay faster,
\Lambda_{n}
converges to a deterministic limit, called the clock (or picket fence) process

\text{clock}_{\eta}=\{2\pi k+\pi+2\eta,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

See
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
9
]
for convergence to the clock process in related models.
When
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, then the potentials decay slower than the critical value, and the point process
\Lambda_{n}
should have a Poisson limit, though the Poisson limit is currently only proved in
[
7
]
for a continuous time model with randomness arising from a Brownian motion.

We will now further investigate the intermediate case
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
. Inspired from the vanishing and decaying models, we consider the
mixed vanishing-decaying model

(1.7)

v_{k,n}=\sigma\frac{\omega_{k}}{n^{\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}

for some
\eta\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. In choosing
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
we recover the vanishing model, and in choosing
\eta=0
and relabeling
\ell
by
n+1-\ell
, we recover the decaying model.

To simplify some computations, in this paper we use the convention that at at site
\ell
the weight is
\frac{1}{n^{\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}
rather than the more natural choice
\frac{1}{n^{\eta}\ell^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}
. A great portion of the computations in this paper can be carried over to more general decaying profiles that are locally slow varying and has order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
in most of the sites.

Throughout this paper we assume the random variables
\omega_{k}
are independent, have mean
0
and variance
1
, and their third moments are bounded.

1.1.
Scaling limits of of transfer matrices

The first step to study our mixed vanishing-decaying model
H_{n}
is to work out the scaling limits of transfer matrices. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
define

T(x):=\begin{pmatrix}x&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

We consider eigenvalues
E+\frac{\lambda}{\rho n}
near some bulk energy
E
, and define

(1.8)

\epsilon_{\ell}=\frac{\lambda}{\rho n}-\frac{\sigma\omega_{\ell}}{n^{\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}.

Then the transfer matrix is

(1.9)

M_{\ell}^{\lambda}=T(E+\epsilon_{\ell})T(E+\epsilon_{\ell-1})\cdots T(E+\epsilon_{1}),\quad 0\leq\ell\leq n,

and
E+\frac{\lambda}{\rho n}
is an eigenvalue of
H_{n}
if and only if

M_{n}^{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\parallel\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

To determine a limit of
M_{\ell}^{\lambda}
as
n\to\infty
, we need to work with
Q_{\ell}^{\lambda}
defined as

(1.10)

Q_{\ell}^{\lambda}=T^{-\ell}(E)M_{\ell}^{\lambda},\quad 0\leq\ell\leq n.

and study the scaling limit of
Q_{\ell}^{\lambda}
.

For any
E\in(-2,2)
, it will be convenient to diagonalize
T(E):=ZDZ^{-1}
, where

(1.11)

D=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{z}&0\\
0&z,\end{pmatrix},\quad Z=\frac{i\rho(E)}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{z}&-z\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad Z^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-z\\
1&-\bar{z}\end{pmatrix},\quad z=\frac{E}{2}+i\sqrt{1-\frac{E^{2}}{4}}.

We prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

First assume
0<|E|<2
and
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Given
\mathcal{B}(t),

\mathcal{B}_{2}(t)
and
\mathcal{B}_{3}(t)
standard Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}
and mutually independent, denote by
\mathcal{W}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathcal{B}_{2}(t)+i\mathcal{B}_{3}(t)).
Then

(Q^{\lambda}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor},0\leq t\leq 1)\Rightarrow(Q^{\lambda}(t),0\leq t\leq 1),\quad n\to\infty,\quad

where
Q^{\lambda}
is the unique strong solution to the SDE

(1.12)

dQ^{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}Z\left(\begin{pmatrix}i\lambda&0\\
0&-i\lambda\end{pmatrix}dt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\begin{pmatrix}id\mathcal{B}&d\mathcal{W}\\
d\bar{\mathcal{W}}&-id\mathcal{B}\end{pmatrix}\right)Z^{-1}Q^{\lambda},\quad Q^{\lambda}(0)=I.

The convergence takes place in
\lambda
- finite dimensional distributions, uniformly in
t\in[0,1-\epsilon]
for any
\epsilon>0
.

In the case
E=0
, we still have the convergence but now
Q^{\lambda}
solves the SDE

(1.13)

dQ^{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}Z\left(\begin{pmatrix}i\lambda&0\\
0&-i\lambda\end{pmatrix}dt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\begin{pmatrix}id\mathcal{B}_{1}&id\mathcal{B}_{2}\\
-id\mathcal{B}_{2}&-id\mathcal{B}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right)Z^{-1}Q^{\lambda},\quad Q^{\lambda}(0)=I.

In the meantime, we will establish tightness estimates for the transfer matrices and expected number of eigenvalues in a small interval. We prove:

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\sigma<\infty
. Then

(1)

Fix
E\in(-2,2)
and
R<\infty
, we can find
c>0
such that for any
t>0
and
n
large, on an event of probability no less than
1-c/t
,

(a)

there holds

(1.14)

\max_{0\leq\ell\leq n,|\lambda|\leq R}\text{Tr}M_{\ell}^{\lambda}M_{\ell}^{\lambda*}<t.

(b)

Given an eigenvector
\psi
of
H_{n}
that is
L^{2}
-normalized and has eigenvalue in
[E-\frac{R}{\rho n},E+\frac{R}{\rho n}]
,

(1.15)

\frac{2}{(n+1)t^{2}}<|\psi_{\ell}|^{2}+|\psi_{\ell+1}|^{2}<\frac{2t^{2}}{n+1},\quad 0\leq\ell\leq n.

(2)

For any
R>0
consider the interval
\Delta_{n}(E)=(E-\frac{R}{n\rho(E)},E+\frac{R}{n\rho(E)})
. Denote by
N_{n}(E)
the number of eigenvalues of
H_{n}
belonging to
\Delta_{n}(E)
. Then for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
,

(1.16)

\sup_{n}\sup_{E\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)}\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(E)]^{3/2}<\infty.

The estimate
1.16
leads to Wegner’s estimate (see for example
[
4
]
) and a weak version of Minami’s estimate
[
10
]
for the Anderson operator
H_{n}
. These estimates are essential for us to establish process level scaling limits, as stated in the next paragraph.

1.2.
The point process limit

Now we investigate the point process limit of
\Lambda_{n}
as
n\to\infty
. The limiting point process is not the clock process or Poisson process, and does not match with point processes from existing random matrix models. Rather, they are characterized as zeros of complex analytic functions.

We introduce the following set of Prüfer coordinates: Since for any real vector
(a,b)^{t}
,

Z^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a-bz\\
\overline{a-bz}\end{pmatrix},

we shall write

(1.17)

\begin{pmatrix}q^{\lambda}(t)\\
\overline{q^{\lambda}(t)}\end{pmatrix}:=Z^{-1}Q(\lambda,t)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

where
q^{\lambda}(t)
satisfies
q^{\lambda}(0)=1
. Define also

(1.18)

(q^{\lambda}(t))^{2}=e^{r^{\lambda}(t)+i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}.

It will be shown in Proposition
4.1
that
\theta^{\lambda}
solves the SDE

(1.19)

d\theta^{\lambda}(t)=\lambda dt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}[d\mathcal{B}+\text{Im}[e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}]],\quad\theta^{\lambda}(0)=0.

Now we define the following point process analogous to the
sch_{\tau}
process in
[
8
]
: for each
\phi\in[0,2\pi),
define

(1.20)

\eta Sch^{\phi}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\theta^{\lambda}(1)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}+\phi\},

and also consider

\eta Sch^{*}=^{d}\eta Sch^{U}

where
U
is uniform random variable on
[0,2\pi]
independent of all other random variables.

Note that by a slight abuse of notation, the definition of
\eta Sch
also involves
\sigma
and
\rho
. We choose to keep the dependence throughout this article as it will not cause confusions and will clarify what coefficients the estimates depend on.

Now we can show that the point process
\Lambda_{n,E}
converge to the
\eta Sch
process, and that the rescaled eigenfunctions also converge to
q^{\lambda}
. We prove:

Corollary 1.3
.

For any subsequence
n_{j}
such that
z^{n_{j}}
converges, we have
T^{n_{j}}(E)\to\tilde{T}
for some
2\times 2
matrix
\tilde{T}
. Then the following claims are true:

(1)

The analytic function
M_{n_{j}}^{\lambda}
(with
n=n_{j}
in the definition (
1.8
),(
1.9
)) has a distributional limit
\tilde{T}Q^{\lambda}(1)
.

(2)

The point process
\Lambda_{n_{j}}
converges in distribution to the counting measure on the zero set of the analytic function
\lambda\mapsto[\tilde{T}Q^{\lambda}(1)]_{11}.

(3)

Define an analytic function
Q_{n,E}(\lambda,t)
as the piecewise linear interpolation of the step function
Q^{\lambda}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}
defined in (
1.10
). Then
Q_{n,E}(\lambda,t)
converges to
Q(\lambda,t)
in distribution for the uniform topology of
\mathbb{C}\times[0,1-\epsilon]
and any
\epsilon>0
.

1.3.
Shape of eigenfunctions for the vanishing-decaying model

Having worked out the point process limit of eigenvalues of
H_{n}
near a bulk energy
E
, we also study the joint scaling limit of eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs. This has been done in Rifkind and Virag
[
12
]
for the vanishing model in discrete time, and recently in Nakano
[
11
]
in a continuous time model driven by Brownian motion, for both vanishing and decaying model. In this paper we generalize these results to the mixed vanishing-decaying model (
1.7
).

For
\mu
some eigenvalue of
H_{n}
and the corresponding eigenvector
\psi^{\mu}
, we consider a probability measure on
[0,1]
with density

n|\psi^{\mu}(\lfloor nt\rfloor)|^{2}dt

where the leading factor
n
is added to normalize it into a probability measure. Consider
\mathcal{M}([0,1])
the space of probability measures on
[0,1]
, endowed with the weak topology, that is,
\mu_{n}\to\mu
if and only if
\int fd\mu_{n}\to fd\mu
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{\infty}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
.
The main result concerns joint convergence in distribution of the eigenvalue-eigenvector pair

(\mu,n|\psi^{\mu}(\lfloor nt\rfloor)^{2}|dt)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{M}[0,1]

once we uniformly choose
\mu
out of eigenvalues of
H_{n}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Given a random variable
E
following the arcsine law and
U
the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. Consider
\mathcal{Z}
standard two-sided Brownian motion. Assume
E,\mathcal{Z},U
are mutually independent.

Uniformly choose
\mu
from eigenvalues of
H_{n}
and denote by
\psi^{\mu}
the normalized eigenvector. Then we have the convergence in distribution

\displaystyle\left(\mu,n|\psi^{\mu}(\lfloor nt\rfloor)|^{2}dt\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left(E,\frac{\exp\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-t)^{2\eta}]}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{8\eta}\left|(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-t)^{2\eta}\right|\right)dt}{\int_{0}^{1}ds\exp\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-s)^{2\eta}]}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{8\eta}\left|(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-s)^{2\eta}\right|\right)}\right)

Remark 1.5
.

If we take
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
(i.e., the vanishing model), and
\sigma\rho=1
, we obtain after Brownian scaling the expression
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathcal{Z}_{t-U}-\frac{1}{4}|t-U|
in the exponential. This is consistent with the result of
[
12
]
. If we take
\eta\to 0
, we get
\frac{\log|1-U|}{\log|1-t|}
terms in the exponential, which is consistent with the result of
[
11
]
, Theorem 1.1 after a time reversal (the coefficients are different because
[
11
]
considered a continuous time model).

We may also consider potentials that vanish faster than the critical exponent
\frac{1}{2}
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Now we consider, instead of (
1.7
),

(1.21)

v_{k,n}:=\frac{\sigma\omega_{k}}{n^{\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{\tau-\eta}}

for some given
\tau>\frac{1}{2}
and
\eta\in[0,\tau]
. Then Theorem
1.1
holds straightforwardly in this setting if we take
\sigma=0
in (
1.12
). Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1.3
hold true with the same proof. The result of Theorem
1.4
now becomes, upon taking
\sigma=0
,

\left(\mu,n|\psi^{\mu}(\lfloor nt\rfloor)|^{2}dt\right)\Rightarrow(E,1_{[0,1]}(t)dt).

That is, the scaled eigenfunctions are uniformly spread out.

This shape of eigenvalue result is consistent with the result of
[
11
]
, Theorem 1.1 which considers a model in continuous time. The proof of Corollary
1.6
will be omitted as it only involves straightforward adaptations of previous proofs.

Remark 1.7
.

For potentials vanishing slower than the critical rate
\frac{1}{2}
, Poisson statistics is expected and the rescaled eigenvector should converge to Dirac measure with a uniformly distributed center. However, a proof of these claims are currently only available in the continuous model with randomness from a Brownian motion, see
[
7
]
,
[
11
]
.

1.4.
Properties of the point process

The point process
\eta Sch
has many properties that are similar to the point process
Sch_{\tau}
introduced in
[
8
]
, which correspond to the vanishing model, i.e.
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
. We prove that the point process
\eta Sch
has the following properties:

Proposition 1.8
.

(Eigenvalue repulsion).
Fix
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. For any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
,

(1.22)

\mathbb{P}\{\eta Sch[\mu,\mu+\epsilon]\geq 2\}\leq 4\exp(-C_{\eta,\sigma,\rho}(\log(1/\epsilon)-d(1))^{2}),

for some constant
C_{\eta,\sigma,\rho}>0
, and where
d(1)=1+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}dt
. We implicitly require that the expression holds when the value in the square is positive.

For GOE, GUE and GSE, the probability on the left hand side of (
1.22
) has order
\epsilon^{2+\beta}
with
\beta=1,2,4
, which is much larger than the right hand side of (
1.22
). We also have:

Proposition 1.9
.

( Large gap probability). Fix
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. The probability that the point process
\eta-Sch
has a gap of length
\lambda
is

\mathbb{P}(\eta Sch[0,\lambda]=0)=\exp(-c_{\eta}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}(1+o(1)),

where
o(1)\to 0
for fixed
\eta,\rho,\sigma
as
\lambda\to 0
, and
c_{\eta}
is some constant that depends only on
\eta
.

We have seen that the
\eta Sch
point process has many similar features as the
Sech_{\tau}
process and
\text{Sine}_{\beta}
process, but they have qualitatively different behaviours at finer scales. When we consider central limit theorem of
\eta Sch
, we can currently only prove the following crude upper bound. We leave the investigation of Gaussian fluctuations for
\eta Sch
to a future work.

Proposition 1.10
.

Assume
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
Consider any increasing function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)=\infty
. Then as
\lambda\to\infty
, we have the convergence

\frac{1}{f(\lambda)}\left(\eta Sch[0,\lambda]-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\right)\to 0

in probability.

When
\eta=0
, i.e. we consider the critical decaying model, it is proved in
[
8
]
, Theorem 17 that for the corresponding scaling limit, the
Sine_{\beta}
process, we have Gaussian fluctuations

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log(\lambda)}}\left(Sine_{\beta}[0,\lambda]-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\right)\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{2}{\beta\pi^{2}}).

When
\eta=\frac{1}{2},
i.e. we consider the critical vanishing model, the scaling limit
Sech_{\tau}
process satisfies a more refined form of central limit theorem (
[
8
]
, Theorem 13)

(1.23)

Sch_{\tau}[0,2\pi k+\theta]-k\Rightarrow\lfloor\frac{\xi_{0}+\xi_{2}+\theta}{2\pi}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}}{2\pi}\rfloor

with
\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
independent normals with mean
0
and variance
\tau,\tau/2,\tau/2
. We will briefly sketch the proof at the end of this paper, but show why we could not generalize this argument to the general case
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.

2.
Transfer matrices evolution

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

We fix the value of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and remove it from the notation. From the iteration
M_{\ell}=T(E+\epsilon_{\ell})M_{\ell-1}
we may write

(2.1)

Q_{\ell}=T^{-\ell}(E)\begin{pmatrix}1&\epsilon_{\ell}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}T^{\ell}(E)Q_{\ell-1}.

Therefore
Q_{\ell},0\leq\ell\leq n
defines a Markov process with
Q_{0}=I
. Rewriting
T
for
T(E)
, we use the following matrices to diagonalize
T
:

T^{-\ell}\begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon_{\ell}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}T^{\ell}=\frac{i\rho\epsilon_{\ell}}{2}ZO_{\ell}Z^{-1},\quad O_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}1&z^{2\ell}\\
-\bar{z}^{2\ell}&-1,\end{pmatrix}

from this we see that
X_{\ell}:=Z^{-1}Q_{\ell}Z
becomes a Markov process with
X_{0}=I
with increments

(2.2)

X_{\ell}=X_{\ell-1}+U_{\ell}X_{\ell-1},\quad U_{\ell}=i\rho\epsilon_{\ell}O_{\ell}/2,

It suffices to show the Markov process
X_{\ell}^{n}
converges to the limiting SDE as stated in the theorem. Since
z
is complex with
|z|=1
,
U_{\ell}X_{\ell}
does not converge to a limit. But for
K
large,
X_{\ell+K}-X_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}U_{\ell_{j}}X_{\ell+j-1}
should converge to a limiting SDE, noting that the sum
\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}z^{2\ell}
is uniformly upper bounded however large
K
is. A rigorous proof uses the convergence result of discrete time Markov chains to SDEs, stated in
[
8
]
, Proposition 26. We briefly recall the statement of this proposition: consider the Markov chain

(X_{\ell}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ell=0,\cdots,\lfloor nT\rfloor),

and let
Y_{\ell}^{n}(x)
denote the expectation of
X_{\ell+1}^{n}-X_{\ell}^{n}
conditioned on
X_{\ell}^{n}=x
, and consider

b^{n}(t,x):=n\mathbb{E}Y^{n}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(x),\quad a^{n}(t,x):=n\mathbb{E}Y^{n}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(x)Y^{n}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(x)^{T}.

Assume
a^{n}
and
b^{n}
converges to some
C^{2}
functions
a
and
b
, in the sense that

(2.3)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T,|x|\leq R}\|\int_{0}^{t}(a^{n}(s,x)-a(s,x))ds\|+\sup_{0\leq t\leq T,|x|\leq R}\|\int_{0}^{t}(b^{n}(s,x)-b(s,x))ds\|\to 0,

and that some mild integrability and regularity assumptions are satisfied, then
(X_{\lfloor nT\rfloor}^{n},0\leq t\leq T)
converges in distribution in
D([0,T])
to the stochastic differential equation

(2.4)

dX(t)=b(t,X(t))dt+g(t,X(t))d\mathcal{B}(t),\quad X(0)=X_{0},

in which
g
is a
C^{2}
function with
g(t,x)g(t,x)^{T}=a(t,x)
and
\mathcal{B}(t)
a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. In our setting of mixed vanishing-decaying potentials, the coefficient
a(t,x)
tends to infinity as
t\to 1
and is thus not globally Lipschitz continuous. This is not an issue as we may apply the Proposition on
[0,1-\epsilon]
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Now we turn back to the proof of the theorem. Conditioning on
X_{\ell}^{n}=x
,
X_{\ell+1}^{n}-X_{\ell}^{n}
equals

Y_{\ell}^{n}(x):=\left(\frac{i\lambda}{2n}-\frac{i\sigma\rho\omega_{\ell+1}}{2n^{\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}1&z^{2(\ell+1)}\\
-\bar{z}^{2(\ell+1)}&-1\end{pmatrix}x.

Then we set
b^{n}(t,x)
to be

\frac{i\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&z^{2(\ell+1)}\\
-\bar{z}^{2(\ell+1)}&-1\end{pmatrix}x,\quad\ell=\lfloor nt\rfloor.

Now we estimate the variance
a^{n}(t,x).

a^{n}(t,x)
should be linearly composed of
A_{j}A_{k}
,
A_{j}\bar{A}_{k}
,
\bar{A}_{j}\bar{A}_{k}
for
j,k\in\{1,2\}^{2}
, given

A=-\frac{i\sigma\rho}{2}(\frac{n}{n+1-\ell})^{1-2\eta}\begin{pmatrix}1&z^{2(\ell+1)}\\
-\bar{z}^{2(\ell+1)}&-1\end{pmatrix}x,\quad\ell=\lfloor nt\rfloor.

Since
\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z^{2j}\to 0
and
\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}z^{4j}\to 0
as
n
tends to infinity, the limit
a(t,x)
and
b(t,x)
are obtained from
a^{n}
and
b^{n}
by taking
n\to\infty
and replacing all the powers of
z
and
\bar{z}
by
0
. The convergence (
2.3
) then follows.

Now we can verify that the limiting SDE should be

(2.5)

dX=\begin{pmatrix}i\lambda/2&0\\
0&-i\lambda/2\end{pmatrix}Xdt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{2}\frac{1}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\begin{pmatrix}id\mathcal{B}&d\mathcal{W}\\
d\bar{\mathcal{W}}&-id\mathcal{B}\end{pmatrix}X,\quad X(0)=I.

The drift of this SDE agrees with
b(t,x)
. For the variance, consider the random vector

-\frac{\sigma\rho}{2}\begin{pmatrix}iB&W\\
\bar{W}&-iB\end{pmatrix}x

with
B
and
W
having standard real and complex normal distribution. Thus the variance of the SDE agrees with that of
a(t,x)
. This finishes the proof.

In the case
E=0
, we take
z=i
so
z^{4j}=1
and the SDE limit is

(2.6)

dX=\begin{pmatrix}i\lambda/2&0\\
0&-i\lambda/2\end{pmatrix}Xdt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{2}\frac{1}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\begin{pmatrix}id\mathcal{B}_{1}&id\mathcal{B}_{2}\\
-id\mathcal{B}_{2}&-id\mathcal{B}_{1}\end{pmatrix}X,\quad X(0)=I.

∎

3.
Tightness estimates

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

We essentially follow the steps of
[
8
]
, Theorem 1 and
[
12
]
, Lemma 4.1. The main adaptation follows from the following: for any
n>0,

(3.1)

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\frac{1}{n^{2\eta}(n+1-i)^{1-2\eta}})\leq C_{\eta}<\infty

for some constant
C_{\eta}>0
. Indeed, we take the logarithm of the left hand side and use

\log(1+\frac{1}{n^{2\eta}(n+1-i)^{1-2\eta}})\leq\frac{1}{n^{2\eta}(n+1-i)^{1-2\eta}}

and

(3.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{1-2\eta}}\leq 1+\int_{1}^{n}\frac{1}{x^{1-2\eta}}dx=\frac{n^{2\eta}-1}{2\eta}+1.

The proof of this theorem follows from the following claim: we can find a function
f
continuous and bounded on
(-2,2)
so that

(3.3)

\sup_{n}\max_{0\leq\ell\leq n}\mathbb{E}\|M_{n}(E,\ell)-I\|^{3}<f(E),\quad E\in(-2,2).

Once this is shown, (
1.16
) follows from the upper bound on number of eigenvalues by transfer matrices norms, see Theorem 6.1 of
[
12
]
. Estimate (
1.15
) also follows from standard, deterministic arguments.

Now we show (
3.3
). By the diagonalization (
1.11
), we see that
\|T^{-\ell}(E)\|\leq f(E)<\infty
uniformly in
\ell\geq 1,
so it suffices to prove

(3.4)

\sup_{n}\max_{0\leq\ell\leq n}\mathbb{E}\|X_{\ell}-I\|^{3}<f(E),\quad E\in(-2,2),

where
X_{\ell}
is defined in (
2.2
) and is a Markov chain.

Using the recursion (
2.2
) and that the variance of the random variables at site 1 through
\ell
is no more than
\frac{1}{n^{2\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{1-2\eta}}
, expanding the squares:
we have for any
\ell\geq 1,

\mathbb{E}\max_{k\leq\ell}\|X_{k}-I\|^{3}\leq c\rho(E)^{3}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\|X_{k-1}\|^{3}(\frac{n}{n+1-k})^{1-2\eta}.

Now using

\|X_{k-1}\|\leq 1+\|X_{k-1}-I\|,

we further estimate, taking into account (
3.2
),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\max_{k\leq\ell}\|X_{k}-I\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq c\rho(E)^{3}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(\frac{n}{n+1-k})^{1-2\eta}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\|X_{k-1}-I|^{3}(\frac{n}{n+1-k})^{1-2\eta}

\displaystyle\leq c\rho(E)^{3}(1+\frac{S_{\ell-1}}{n^{2\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{1-2\eta}}),

where we define
S_{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\|X_{k}-I\|^{3}.

Now

S_{\ell}-S_{\ell-1}=\mathbb{E}\|X_{\ell}-I\|^{3}\leq c\rho(E)^{3}(1+\frac{S_{\ell-1}}{n^{2\eta}(n+1-\ell)^{1-2\eta}}).

Considering
R_{\ell}=1+\frac{S_{\ell}}{n^{2\eta}(n-\ell)^{1-2\eta}}
, we see that

R_{\ell}-R_{\ell-1}\leq\frac{c\rho(E)^{3}}{n^{2\eta}(n-\ell)^{1-2\eta}}R_{\ell-1}.

Now we complete the proof: from (
3.1
) we conclude that
R_{\ell}\leq C_{\eta}<\infty
uniformly in
\eta=1,\cdots,n
and
n\geq 0
. Then by definition of
R_{\ell}
and (
3.5
) we deduce

\mathbb{E}\max_{k\leq\ell}\|X_{k}-I\|^{3}\leq c_{\eta}\rho(E)^{3}<\infty.

∎

Then we characterize the evolution of

Proposition 4.1
.

r^{\lambda}
and

satisfies the following set of stochastic differential equations:

(4.2)

Defining

5.
Shape of eigenfunctions

We separate the proof of Theorem
1.4

5.1.
Technical preparations

Lemma 5.1
.

Given
0<|E|<2
, consider
\boldsymbol{m}_{n}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{q}^{\lambda}
to be measures on
[0,1]
having densities

d\boldsymbol{m}_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=|((2/\rho(E))M_{n,E}(\lambda,\lfloor nt\rfloor)_{11}|^{2}dt

Given any sequence
n_{j}
such that

\{(\lambda,\boldsymbol{n}_{n}^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\Lambda_{n_{j},E}\}\Rightarrow\{(\lambda,2\boldsymbol{q}^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\tau-Sch^{2\arg z^{\prime}}\}.

The proof of this lemma is the same as in
[
12
]
, Lemma 3.4, so we omit the details.

Lemma 5.2
.

Given
U
a uniform distribution on
[0,2\pi]
, we have for any
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
,

\{(\lambda+U,\boldsymbol{q}^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{\phi}\}=^{d}\{(\lambda,\boldsymbol{q}^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{*}\}.

Proof.

Since
r^{\lambda}=2\log|q^{\lambda}|,
we will show

\{(\lambda+U,r^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{\phi}\}=^{d}\{(\lambda,r^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{*}.

Recall that
\theta^{\lambda}
and
r^{\lambda}
solve the SDE

(5.1)

d\theta^{\lambda}(t)=\lambda dt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}[d\mathcal{B}+\text{Im}[e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}]],\quad\theta^{\lambda}(0)=0,

(5.2)

dr^{\lambda}(t)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}dt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\text{Re}[e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}],\quad r^{\lambda}(0)=0.

To understand the effect of shifting by
u
, define
\tilde{\theta}^{\lambda}(t):=\theta^{\lambda-u}(t)+ut
and
\tilde{r}^{\lambda}(t):=r^{\lambda-u}(t)
, and notice that
\tilde{\theta}
and
\tilde{r}
solve the same SDE with
d\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}=d\mathcal{B}
and
d\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}=e^{iut}d\mathcal{W}.

Now since
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
has the same law as
\mathcal{W},
by uniqueness of solutions to the SDEs,
(r^{\lambda},\theta^{\lambda})
has the same law as
(\tilde{r}^{\lambda},\tilde{\theta}^{\lambda})
. Since
\theta^{\lambda-u}(1)=\tilde{\theta}(1)-u
, we have

\displaystyle\{(\lambda+u,r^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{\phi}\}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda,r^{\lambda-u}:\lambda\in\eta Sch^{\phi}+u\}

\displaystyle=\{(\lambda,\tilde{r}^{\lambda}):\tilde{\theta}^{\lambda}(1)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}+\phi+u\}

\displaystyle=^{d}\{(\lambda,r^{\lambda}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{\phi+u}\}.

For
U
uniform on
[0,2\pi]
,

is also uniform on
[0,2\pi]
. This finishes the proof.

∎

5.2.
Proof of local joint scaling limit

Lemma 5.3
.

Given
\mathcal{B}
a standard Brownian motion with
\mathcal{B}_{0}=0
and
U
uniform on
[0,1]
, define
f^{u}(t)=(u-|u-t|)
. We have for any
G\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}\times C[0,1]),

\mathbb{E}\sum_{\lambda\in\eta Sch^{*}}G(\lambda,|q^{\lambda}|^{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[G\left(\lambda,\exp(\mathcal{B}_{v(\cdot)}+\frac{f^{v(U)}(v(\cdot))}{2})\right)\right],

where the function
v:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined by

(5.3)

v(t)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[1-(1-t)^{2\eta}].

Proof.

By definition,
\eta Sch^{*}=\{\lambda:\theta^{\lambda}(1)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}+U\}
and
U
uniform on
[0,2\pi]
. Take expectation over
U
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{\lambda\in\eta Sch^{*}}G(\lambda,r^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{2\pi}du\sum_{\lambda:\theta^{\lambda}(1)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}+u}G(\lambda,r^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{E}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\sum_{\lambda:\theta^{\lambda}(1)=u}G(\lambda,r^{\lambda}).

Now we know
\theta^{\lambda}(1)
is a.s. analytic in
\lambda
, and
r^{\lambda}
is a.s. continuous in
\lambda
. By the co-area formula,

(5.4)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{E}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\sum_{\lambda:\theta^{\lambda}(1)=u}G(\lambda,r^{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\lambda\mathbb{E}[G(\lambda,r^{\lambda})|\frac{\partial\theta^{\lambda}(1)}{\partial\lambda}|].

Notice that
\phi^{\lambda}(t):=\frac{\partial\theta^{\lambda}(t)}{\partial\lambda}
solves the SDE (see (
4.3
))

d\phi^{\lambda}=dt-\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\text{Re}\left[e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}\right]\phi^{\lambda}.

Recall also that
r^{\lambda}
solves the SDE

dr^{\lambda}=\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}dt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\text{Re}[e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}],\quad r^{\lambda}(0)=0.

Since
e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}}d\mathcal{W}=^{d}d\mathcal{W}
, the distribution of
(r^{\lambda},\phi^{\lambda})
is
\lambda
-independent. We may temporarily write
d\mathcal{B}=\sqrt{2}\text{Re}[e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}]
where
\mathcal{B}
is some two-sided Brownian motion, and solve (omitting
\lambda
in the expression),

\displaystyle d\phi(t)=

\displaystyle dt-\phi dr+\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}\phi dt.

By Itô’s formula, the solution is

\phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}due^{r(u)-r(t)}.

From Fubini’s theorem,

\mathbb{E}\left[G(\lambda,r^{\lambda})\mid\frac{\partial\theta^{\lambda}(1)}{\partial\lambda}|\right]=\int_{0}^{1}du\mathbb{E}[e^{r(u)-r(1)}G(\lambda,r)].

Meanwhile,
r^{\lambda}
solves

r^{\lambda}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4(1-s)^{1-2\eta}}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-s)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}\sqrt{2}}d\mathcal{B}_{s}.

For simplicity, we introduce a time change

(5.5)

v(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{2(1-s)^{1-2\eta}}ds=\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[1-(1-t)^{2\eta}],

then
r^{\lambda}
has the same law as

r^{\lambda}(v)=\frac{1}{2}v+\mathcal{Z}_{v}

for some standard two-sided Brownian motion
\mathcal{Z}
with
\mathcal{Z}_{0}=0
.

Now let
\mathcal{R}
denote the distribution of
r(v)
,
v\in[0,v(1)]
, on
\mathcal{C}[0,v(1)]
and let
\mathcal{P}
denote the law of
\mathcal{Z}_{v}
on
\mathcal{C}[0,v(1)]
. We may characterize the law of

\exp(\omega_{v}-\omega_{1})d\mathcal{R}(\omega)

via Girsanov transform, as in Lemma 3.6 of
[
12
]
, see also
[
11
]

\exp(\omega_{u}-\omega_{1})d\mathcal{R}(\omega)
on
\mathcal{C}[0,v(1)],
a path

has distribution
\mathcal{Z}+\frac{1}{2}f^{u}
where

and
\mathcal{Z}
is the standard Brownian motion. Now reworking the time change we have

\mathbb{E}[e^{(r(u)-r(1))}G(\lambda,r)]=\mathbb{E}[G(\lambda,\mathcal{B}_{v(\cdot)}+\frac{f^{v(u)}(v(\cdot))}{2}].

Taking the sum,

\mathbb{E}\sum_{\lambda\in\eta Sch^{*}}G(\lambda,r^{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\lambda\int_{0}^{1}du\mathbb{E}[G(\lambda,\mathcal{B}_{v(\cdot)}+\frac{f^{v(u)}(v(\cdot))}{2}].

The proof finishes via the continuous mapping theorem applied to

∎

Theorem 5.4
.

For fixed
0<|E|<2
, we set
\tau=\tau(E)=(\sigma\rho(E))^{2}
. Given
U
uniform on
[0,2\pi]
, the point process defined on

\displaystyle F(\lambda,\nu)d\mu_{E}(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\lambda\mathbb{E}F\left(\lambda,\frac{\exp\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-t)^{2\eta}]}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{8\eta}\left|(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-t)^{2\eta}\right|\right)dt}{\int_{0}^{1}ds\exp\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-s)^{2\eta}]}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{8\eta}\left|(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-s)^{2\eta}\right|\right)}\right),

Along a subsequence

\displaystyle=^{d}\{(\lambda,2\boldsymbol{q}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{*}\}.

For each subsequence we can find a sub-subsequence along which

and the functional on

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\{(\lambda,\frac{\boldsymbol{q}^{\lambda}}{\boldsymbol{q}^{\lambda}([0,1])}):\lambda\in\eta Sch^{\tilde{\phi}}\}.

Recall that

, now we use Lemma

\frac{\exp(\mathcal{B}_{v(t)}+\frac{1}{2}v(u)-\frac{1}{2}|v(u)-v(t)|)}{\int_{0}^{1}ds\exp(\mathcal{B}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}v(u)-\frac{1}{2}|v(u)-v(s)|)}=^{d}\frac{\exp(\mathcal{Z}_{v(t)-v(u)}-|v(t)-v(u)|/2)}{\int_{0}^{1}ds\exp(\mathcal{Z}_{v(s)-v(u)}-|v(s)-v(u)|/2)}

∎

5.3.
Proof of global joint scaling limit

Now we are finally in the position to prove Theorem
1.4
, the characterization of shape of eigenvalues.

Proof.

We essentially follow the proof of
[
12
]
, Theorem 1.1 and only give s sketch to some main arguments. For
\theta
uniform distribution on
[0,2\pi]
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}[0,1]

|\psi^{\mu}(\lfloor nt\rfloor)|^{2}
. By Theorem, the point process

\mathcal{P}_{E,n}=\{(n,\rho(E)(\mu-E)+\theta,n\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\mu}):\mu\in\Lambda_{n}\}

has a limit
\mathcal{P}_{E}
. Fix
g_{1}=(1-|x|)1_{|x|\leq 1}
and
g_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{M}[0,1]),
consider

G_{n}(E):=\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda_{n}}g_{1}(n\rho(E)(\mu-E))g_{2}(\mu,\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\mu}).

Then given
|E|<2
it converges in law to
G(E)
with

(5.6)

\mathbb{E}G(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{E}g_{2}\left(E,\frac{\exp\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-t)^{2\eta}]}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{8\eta}\left|(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-t)^{2\eta}\right|\right)dt}{\int_{0}^{1}ds\exp\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{4\eta}[(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-s)^{2\eta}]}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{8\eta}\left|(1-U)^{2\eta}-(1-s)^{2\eta}\right|\right)}\right).

The proof follows if we can show

\int\mathbb{E}G_{n}(E)\rho(E)dE\to\int\mathbb{E}G(E)\rho(E)dE.

Denote by

N_{n}(E)=|\{\mu\in\Lambda_{n}:|\mu-E|\leq 1/(n\rho(E))\}|,

so that
G_{n}(E)\leq\|g_{1}\|_{\infty}\|g_{2}\|_{\infty}N_{n}(E).

By the tightness estimate in Theorem
1.2
,
G_{n}(E)1_{|E|\leq 2-\epsilon}
is uniformly integrable, so

(5.7)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\mathbb{E}[G_{n}(E)1_{|E|\leq 2-\epsilon}]\rho(E)dE=\int\mathbb{E}[G(E)1_{|E|\leq 2-\epsilon}]\rho(E)dE.

The left hand side is equal to, via Fubini,

\mathbb{E}\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda_{n}}g_{2}(\mu,\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\mu})\int_{-2+\epsilon}^{2-\epsilon}g_{1}(n\rho(E)(\mu-E))\rho(E)dE.

Now set some
\delta>\epsilon
and consider
A_{n}(\delta)=\{\mu\in\Lambda_{n}:|\mu|<2-\delta\}
and
B_{n}(\delta)=\{\mu\in\Lambda_{n}:|\mu|\geq 2-\delta\}
. The summation over
B_{n}(\delta)
is bounded thanks to Lemma 7.4 of
[
11
]
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}|B_{n}(\delta)|=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{2-\delta}^{2}\rho(s)ds\leq C\sqrt{\delta},

and that for
n
large and
\mu\in A_{n}(\delta)
,

\int_{-2+\epsilon}^{2-\epsilon}g_{1}(n\rho(x)(x-\mu))\rho(x)dx=\frac{1}{n}\int g_{1}(x)dx+o(1/n)=\frac{1}{n}+o(1/n),

see
[
11
]
, Section 4 for the proof of this claim. Finally, we have

\int\mathbb{E}[G(E)1_{|E|<2-\epsilon}]\rho(E)dE=\int\mathbb{E}[G(E)]\rho(E)dE+O(\epsilon).

Combining everything, the proof of the theorem follows.
∎

6.
Properties of the point process

In this section we give the proof of Propositions
1.8
,
1.9
,
1.10
, characterizing the properties of the
\eta Sch
point process we just introduced. These properties are similar to those of the
Sech_{\tau}
process discussed in
[
8
]
.

First we prove Proposition
1.8
, repulsion of nearby eigenvalues.

Proof.

In the proof of Lemma
5.2
we have illustrated that
\theta
has the following invariance property: for every
u\in\mathbb{R}
,

\theta^{\lambda-u}(t)+ut=^{d}\theta^{\lambda}(t).

Thus for (
1.22
) we only need to consider the case
\mu=0
.

Define the relative phase function
\alpha^{\lambda}(t)=\theta^{\lambda}(t)-\theta^{0}(t)
, then
\alpha^{\lambda}(t)
solves the SDE

(6.1)

d\alpha^{\lambda}(t)=\lambda dt+\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\text{Im}[(e^{-i\alpha^{\lambda}(t)}-1)d\mathcal{Z}],\quad\alpha^{\lambda}(0)=0,

where
\mathcal{Z}
satisfies
d\mathcal{Z}=e^{-i\theta^{0}}d\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{W}
is another complex Brownian motion.
We shall rewrite it as

(6.2)

d\alpha^{\lambda}=\lambda dt+\sqrt{2}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\sin(\alpha^{\lambda}/2)d\mathcal{B}^{\lambda},\quad\alpha^{\lambda}(0)=0,

with
\mathcal{B}^{\lambda}
satisfying
d\mathcal{B}^{\lambda}=-\sqrt{2}\text{Re}[e^{-i\alpha^{\lambda}/2}d\mathcal{Z}]
is a standard Brownian motion.

The relative phase
\alpha^{\lambda}(t)
provides a very nice way to estimate the number of points of
\eta Sch.
This follows from the inequality

(6.3)

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\alpha^{\lambda}(1)-\eta Sch[0,\lambda]\right|\leq 1.

Now we fix
\epsilon>0
and define a process
Y=\log(\tan(\alpha/4))
. From Itô’s formula
Y
solves the SDE

(6.4)

dY=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\cosh(Y)dt+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}\tanh(Y)dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}dB,

with initial value
Y(0)=-\infty.
By definition,
\{\alpha^{\epsilon}(1)\geq 2\pi\}=\{Y\text{ explodes on }[0,1]\}
. Now define the solution
\tilde{Y}
that solves the same SDE (
6.4
), but with
\tilde{Y}(0)=0.
Then

Y\text{ explodes on }[0,1]\Rightarrow\tilde{Y}\text{ explodes on }[0,1]\Rightarrow\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\tilde{Y}(t)|\geq\log(1/\epsilon).

Given
\epsilon\leq 1
,
|y|\leq\log(1/\epsilon)
leads to

|\epsilon\cosh(y)/2|\leq 1,\quad|\tanh(y)/4|\leq 1.

From this we get, for any
s\geq 0
,

\sup_{t\in[0,s]}|\tilde{Y}(t)|\leq\log(1/\epsilon)\Rightarrow\sup_{t\in[0,s]}|\tilde{Y}(t)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}Z(t)|\leq d(s),

where we define
Z(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}dB_{t}
and
d(s)=s+\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}dt,
so that, denoting
T
the first time
\tilde{Y}
reaches
\log(1/\epsilon)
, we must have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|Z(T)|\geq\log(1/\epsilon)-d(T),
which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\tilde{Y}(t)|\geq\log(1/\epsilon))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|Z(t)|\geq\log(1/\epsilon)-d(1))

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-C_{\eta}(\log(1/\epsilon)-d(1))^{2}).

Since
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, we know that
d(1)<\infty.

In the last inequality, we use that
\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|Z(t)|
has sub-exponential tails. This follows from representing
Z(t)
as a time-changed Brownian motion, and then use the reflection principle of Brownian motion and Brownian scaling.
∎

Then we prove Proposition
1.9
, the chance of a very long gap in the spectrum.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=\alpha^{\lambda}
be defined in (
6.1
). The desired probability can be bounded via

(6.5)

\mathbb{P}(\theta^{0}(1)\in(0,\epsilon)\text{ mod }2\pi,\text{ and }\alpha(1)\leq 2\pi-\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}(\eta Sch[0,\lambda]=0)\leq\mathbb{P}(\alpha(1)\leq 2\pi).

Recall (
4.1
) that

\theta^{0}(1)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}d\mathcal{B}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\text{Im}[d\mathcal{Z}]

and
\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
d\mathcal{Z}_{t}=e^{-i\theta^{0}(t)}d\mathcal{W}_{t}
is thus independent of
\mathcal{B}
. Therefore by independence,

\mathbb{E}[1\{\varphi^{0}(1)\in(0,\epsilon)\text{ mod }2\pi\}\mid\mathcal{Z}]\geq\epsilon\min_{x}f_{2\pi}(x)=c\epsilon>0,

uniform in
\lambda
, where
f_{2\pi}(x)
is the density of
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}}d\mathcal{B}
mod
2\pi
. Thus we can lower bound the left hand side by
c\epsilon\mathbb{P}(\alpha(1)\leq 2\pi-\epsilon),
where
c
depends implicitly on
\eta,\sigma,\rho
,

The rest of the argument is about estimating the asymptotic of
\mathbb{P}(\alpha^{\lambda}(1)\leq 2\pi)
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Recall that
\alpha^{\lambda}
solves

(6.6)

d\alpha^{\lambda}=\lambda dt+\sqrt{2}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\sin(\alpha^{\lambda}/2)d\mathcal{B}^{\lambda},\quad\alpha^{\lambda}(0)=0.

To make better use of existing results, we use the time change as in equation (
5.5
). Then
\alpha^{\lambda}
solves

(6.7)

d\alpha^{\lambda}=\frac{2(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}\lambda dv+d\mathcal{B}^{\lambda}_{v},\quad\alpha^{\lambda}(0)=0.

for
v\in[0,v(1)]
.
By adapting the proof of
[
13
]
, Theorem 13 to our setting, where they considered the SDE

d\tilde{\alpha}=\lambda fdt+2\sin(\tilde{\alpha}/2)dB,\quad\tilde{\alpha}(0)=0

and obtained a two-sided estimate
\exp(-\lambda^{2}\|f\|_{2}^{2}/8+o(1)),

we see that we may find some
c_{\eta}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\alpha^{\lambda}(1)\leq 2\pi)=\exp(-c_{\eta}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{{4\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}}(1+o(1))).

This finishes the proof. A careful check of the time change shows that the constant
c_{\eta}
depends only on
\eta>0
but not
\sigma
and
\rho
. When we consider random Schrödinger operators with more general decay speed profile, the resulting constant (in place of
c_{\eta}
) still depends only on the decaying profile and not
\sigma,\rho
, but the expression
c_{\eta}
could be rather complicated.

∎

Finally we prove Proposition
1.10
, on the fluctuation of number of points in the
\eta Sch
point process.

Proof.

Recall the SDE solved by
\alpha^{\lambda}
in (
6.1
). Since
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}dt<\infty,
we deduce that
\alpha^{\lambda}(1)-\lambda
is a Gaussian with finite variance. Then the claim follows from, with
\lambda=2\pi k+\theta
,

\eta Sch[0,2\pi k+\theta]-k=\#\{[\varphi^{0}(1),\varphi^{\lambda}(1)]\cap 2\pi\mathbb{Z}\}

and an application of Markov’s inequality.
∎

It could be possible to prove the point process
\eta Sch
satisfies the following limit theorem. As we did not get through some steps in the proof, we only provide a heuristic argument rather than an actual proof. For
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
the claim is already proved in
[
8
]
, Theorem 13.

Conjecture 6.1
.

(Gaussian fluctuations). Fix
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. As
\lambda\to\infty
we have

(\theta^{0}(1),\theta^{\lambda}(1)-\lambda)\Rightarrow(\xi_{0}+\xi_{1},\xi_{0}+\xi_{2}),

where
\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
are independent normal distributions with mean
0
, variance
V_{\eta},V_{\eta}/2,V_{\eta}/2
, and

V_{\eta}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}dt.

Further, for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
k\to\infty
, we have

(6.8)

\eta Sch[0,2\pi k+\theta]-k\Rightarrow\lfloor\frac{\xi_{0}+\xi_{2}+\theta}{2\pi}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}}{2\pi}\rfloor.

Heuristic argument.

The main argument follows from
[
8
]
, Theorem 13.

We have
(\theta^{0}(1),\theta^{\lambda}(1)-\lambda)=(\xi_{0}+\xi_{1},\xi_{0}+\xi_{2})
where

\displaystyle\xi_{0}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}d\mathcal{B},

\displaystyle\xi_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\text{Im}[e^{-i\theta^{0}(t)}d\mathcal{W}],

\displaystyle\xi_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\text{Im}[e^{-i\theta^{\lambda}(t)}d\mathcal{W}].

It is clear that
\xi_{0},\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
are Gaussians with mean 0 and variance
V_{\eta},V_{\eta}/2,V_{\eta}/2
where

V_{\eta}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}dt.

Moreover,
\xi_{0}
is independent from
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
but
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
is not jointly Gaussian. To prove the claim it suffices to show that as
\lambda\to\infty
, the joint law of
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
exists and factorizes into an independent pair of normal distribution.

Recall the factorization
\theta^{\lambda}(t)-\theta^{0}(t)=\alpha^{\lambda}(t)
, we introduce
\mathcal{Z}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i\theta^{0}(s)}d\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{Z}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(i\mathcal{B}_{1}+\mathcal{B}_{2})
, we have

\xi_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}d\mathcal{B}_{1},

\xi_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\cos(\alpha^{\lambda})d\mathcal{B}_{1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma\rho}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\sin(\alpha^{\lambda})d\mathcal{B}_{2}:=\xi_{2,1}+\xi_{2,2}.

Now we show that for any
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have
v^{\lambda}:=a_{1}\xi_{1}+a_{2}\xi_{2,1}+a_{3}\xi_{2,2}
will converge to a normal with mean 0 and variance
V_{\eta}(a_{1}^{2}/2+a_{2}^{2}/4+a_{3}^{2}/4)
. This implies that
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2,1},\xi_{2,2})
converges to independent mean zero normals with the correct variance, and hence completes the proof. By time change,
v^{\lambda}
has the same law as
\hat{\mathcal{B}}(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}[(a_{1}+a_{2}\cos(\alpha^{\lambda})^{2}+a_{3}^{2}\sin(\alpha^{\lambda})^{2}]dt)
for some standard Brownian
\hat{\mathcal{B}}.

We hope to show convergence in probability of

2\int_{0}^{1}(a_{1}+a_{2}\cos(\alpha^{\lambda}))^{2}+a_{3}^{2}\sin(\alpha^{\lambda})^{2}dt\to V_{\eta}(2a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}).

Since
\cos(2x)=2\cos^{2}(x)-1=1-2\sin^{2}(x),
it suffices to show

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}\cos(2\alpha^{\lambda})dt=\text{Re}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}e^{2i\alpha^{\lambda}}dt\to 0,

and

(6.9)

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}\cos(\alpha^{\lambda})dt=\text{Re}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}e^{i\alpha^{\lambda}}dt\to 0.

We attempt to verify the second as the first is very similar.
From Itô’s formula and (
6.2
),

\frac{1}{i\lambda}d(e^{i\alpha^{\lambda}})=e^{i\alpha^{\lambda}}dt+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\lambda}\frac{\sigma\rho e^{i\alpha^{\lambda}}}{(1-t)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta}}\sin(\alpha^{\lambda}/2)d\mathcal{B}^{\lambda}+\frac{i}{\lambda}\frac{\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}e^{2i\alpha^{\lambda}}}{(1-t)^{1-2\eta}}\sin(\alpha^{\lambda}/2)^{2}dt.

Now replacing
e^{i\alpha^{\lambda}}dt
in equation (
6.9
) by the other terms in this expression, it may be possible to show the convergence to
0
in (
6.9
) holds in probability, as
\lambda\to\infty
. Unfortunately, after integration by parts we encounter terms like
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2-2\eta}}dt
, which diverges for any
\eta\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Thus one really needs some techniques to make precise the integration by parts procedure, otherwise the argument is not rigorous. We leave it for a future work.
∎
