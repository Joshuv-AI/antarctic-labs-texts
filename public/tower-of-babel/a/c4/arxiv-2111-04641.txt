Statistical properties of large data sets with linear latent features Preprint: APS/123-QED
Philipp Fleig, Ilya Nemenman

Abstract
Analytical understanding of how low-dimensional latent features reveal themselves in large-dimensional data is still lacking. We study this by defining a linear latent feature model with additive noise constructed from probabilistic matrices, and analytically and numerically computing the statistical distributions of pairwise correlations and eigenvalues of the correlation matrix. This allows us to resolve the latent feature structure across a wide range of data regimes set by the number of recorded variables, observations, latent features and the signal-to-noise ratio. We find a characteristic imprint of latent features in the distribution of correlations and eigenvalues and provide an analytic estimate for the boundary between signal and noise even in the absence of a clear spectral gap.



Introduction.

Massively parallel experiments are now standard in all fields of science. They record the state of the system through a large number,
N
, of variables
x_{i},\,i=1\dots N
. These variables could be positions of particles, agents, or tracers in dusty plasmas
Killer et al. 2016
, soft matter
Valentine et al. 2001
, insect swarming
Sinhuber et al. 2019
, and dynamical systems
Lusch et al. 2018
. Or they can be field values at different spatial points in fluids
Schanz et al. 2016
, climate data
NOAA Physical Sciences
Laboratory
, or activity of “nodes” in gene expression networks
Natale et al. 2018
, neural recordings
Meshulam et al. 2017
, postures
Stephens et al. 2008
;
Berman et al. 2014
, biodiversity
Weisser et al. 2017
, ecology
Dell et al. 2014
, etc. Crucially, the number of recorded variables is often much larger than the true number of (latent) degrees of freedom in the system
Pandarinath et al. 2018
;
Schwab et al. 2014
;
Morrell et al. 2021
. This allows to use correlations among the measured variables to detect the latent state variables, which can then be included in models of the system
Gallego et al. 2017
;
Pandarinath et al. 2018
;
Page et al. 2020
;
Nieh et al. 2021
. Such approaches generally go under the name of
dimensionality reduction
. While many dimensionality reduction methods have been developed, the classic Principal Components Analysis (PCA) still reigns supreme in its widespread use. PCA assumes the recorded data to be Gaussian, and the latent features to be linear combinations of the recorded variable activities. It then calculates the variable-variable correlation matrix and identifies the eigendirections (principal components) corresponding to the large eigenvalues of the correlation matrix as the latent features. If there is a gap between
m\ll N
largest eigenvalues and the rest, then
m
principal components capture a large part of the variance of all variables, achieving the dimensionality reduction.

While the number of variables may be (much) larger than the number of latent components, modern experiments are usually undersampled since nonstationarity precludes making measurements for a long time. Thus the number of independent measurements,
T
, is often of the same order as
N
. Because of this, statistical fluctuations in the correlation matrix are large (in fact, the matrix is degenerate for
T\leq N
), and the principal components calculated from samples often only vaguely resemble their true values, with many correlations emerging as statistical artifacts. To identify if findings of the PCA analysis can be trusted, one refers to Random Matrix Theory
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
, and in particular the Marčenko-Pastur (MP) eigenvalue density of a pure noise correlation matrix
Marčenko and Pastur 1967
. Specifically, one calculates the upper and the lower bounds of eigenvalues expected by pure chance from
T
measurements of
N
independent variables, and eigenvalues outside this interval (and their corresponding eigenvectors) are deemed to be statistically significant.

This straightforward approach assumes that signal-induced correlations among the variables do not influence the spectrum of the noise-induced correlations. This has never been proven and, as we will show, is, in fact, incorrect. More generally, we are not aware of results extending the MP analysis to produce correlation eigenvalue densities when the correlations come from the sampling noise
and
from true, low-dimensional, latent signals, with the latter coming from some known distribution in its own turn (though see work on spiked covariance matrix models
Sengupta and Mitra 1999
;
Loubaton and Vallet 2011
;
Capitaine and Donati-Martin 2016
). Even statistics of the entries of the correlation matrix (rather than of its eigenvalues) have not been reported in this case. In this Letter, we fill in these gaps and calculate – analytically and numerically – various statistical properties of correlation matrices emerging from data sets with low-dimensional latent feature structure. We show that the distribution of pairwise correlations and the spectra of eigenvalues of these correlation matrices carry signatures of the number of latent features, allowing one not only to choose, rigorously, which of the principal components are above the noise floor, but also to see if the overall model of latent features plus noise is a good description for a particular data set.

We do our analysis in two limits. First is the
classical statistics
limit, where the number of variables is constant (though possibly large) and the number of latent features is small, while the number of observations grows to infinity, such that
N/T\rightarrow 0
. Second is the
intensive
limit, where both the number of the measured variables and the number of observations grows, such that
N/T=\text{const}
, while the number of latent features stays finite
m={\rm const}
. Both of these limits routinely happen in modern datasets. We leave considerations of the
extensive
limit, where the number of latent features grows with the number of the measured variables, to a future publication. We believe that these results are an important step in the development of analytical tools for understanding large datasets.

The model and its limits.
We consider observations produced by a random matrix model that combines latent signals and uncorrelated noise:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}+\sigma\mathbf{R}\,.

(1)

The component matrices of the latent signal term
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
have dimensions
T\times m
and
m\times N
, respectively. Thus
m
latent features get randomly sampled
T
times (matrix
\mathbf{U}
), and each of the
N
measured variables is a random linear combination of the latent features (matrix
\mathbf{V}
). We assume
m\leq T,N
throughout this work, such that the rank of the signal matrix
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}
is equal to the number of latent features
m
. In other words, an estimate of the features can be inferred from the samples uniquely.

The entries of
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are Gaussian random variables with zero mean and variances
\sigma_{U}^{2}
and
\sigma_{V}^{2}
, respectively:

\displaystyle U_{t\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{U}^{2})\,,\quad V_{\mu n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{V}^{2})\,,

(2)

\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,T,\;\mu=1,\ldots,m,\;n=1,\dots,N.

(3)

We make this choice for analytic tractability; in applications to real data, the variances of the entries may need to be matched to variances of each measured variable. Finally, the elements of the noise matrix
\mathbf{R}
are i.i.d. unit variance Gaussian random variables, so that the noise in every observation has variance
\sigma^{2}
.

From Eqs. (
1
,
2
), the elements of the signal matrix
\mathbf{UV}
are a sum of
m
products of two Gaussian variables with variances
\sigma_{U}^{2}
and
\sigma_{V}^{2}
. In
Online Supplementary Materials
A
we use characteristic functions to derive the probability density of these entries and show that their variance is

\displaystyle\sigma_{UV}^{2}=m\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}\,.

(4)

In other words, as expected from addition of independent random variables, each of the latent components adds
\sigma^{2}_{U}\sigma^{2}_{V}
to the variance of the observations.

Further, for a large number of latent features
m
, the probability density of
{\mathbf{X}}
approaches a Gaussian with zero mean, see Fig.
S2
. This allows us to define a Gaussian signal-to-noise ratio,
\textsf{SNR}\equiv\sigma_{UV}^{2}/\sigma^{2}=m\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}/\sigma^{2}
. Since our goal is to calculate properties of the data matrix independent of the units chosen to measure each of the variables, we normalize the data matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\equiv\mathbf{X}/\sigma_{X},\;\sigma_{X}^{2}\equiv\sigma_{UV}^{2}+\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}(1+\textsf{SNR}).

(5)

Note that this normalization is by an expected standard deviation, and is different from subtracting empirical means and standardizing by an empirical standard deviation. However, we expect the difference to be of order
T^{-1/2}
, and thus negligible in what follows.

In this work, we will be exploring the properties of the normalized empirical covariance matrix (NECM)

{\mathbf{C}}=\frac{1}{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}=\frac{1}{T}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})^{T}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})+\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}\\
\quad+\tilde{\sigma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})^{T}\mathbf{R}+\tilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}\,,

(6)

as well as the matrix of correlation coefficients

c_{pq}=\frac{C_{pq}}{\sqrt{C_{pp}}\sqrt{C_{qq}}}\,.

(7)

To explore different regimes of the problem, we use the following parameters:

\displaystyle q\equiv N/T,\;q_{U}\equiv m/T,\;\mbox{and}\;q_{V^{T}}\equiv m/N,

(8)

only two of which are independent. These parameters emerge naturally in our theoretical analysis in
Online Supplementary Materials
C.1
as controlling behavior of various observables in the model. Then the classical statistics and the intensive limits, introduced above, become:

Classical stats.:

\displaystyle q\rightarrow 0,\;q_{U}\rightarrow 0,\;q_{V^{T}}=\text{const}\,,

(9)

Intensive:

\displaystyle q=\text{const},\;q_{U}\rightarrow 0,\;q_{V^{T}}\rightarrow 0\,,

(10)

together with
\textsf{SNR}=\text{const}
in both limits. Notably,
q_{U}^{-1}
, gives the number of observations available per latent feature to be learned. Since the parameter
q_{U}
is small in both limits, it means that the latent features are well sampled in our model, even if the measured variables (controlled by
q
) may not be.

The first observable statistics we calculate is the probability density of correlations
c_{pq}
of the standardized variables
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
in our latent features model. Our goal is to analyze the dependence of the density of the matrix entries
c_{pq}
on
m
,
T
, and the noise strength. The numerator in the correlation matrix in Eq. (
7
) has three contributions:
(\mathbf{UV})^{T}(\mathbf{UV})
from the pure latent features signal,
\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}
from the pure noise, and two cross terms between the signal and the noise, e. g.
(\mathbf{UV})^{T}\mathbf{R}
. Each of these terms is analyzed separately in
Online Supplementary Materials
B
, and we reduce each term to correlations between independent Gaussian vectors. Such correlations are distributed according to the symmetric Beta distribution
Hotelling 1953

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdf}(r)=\text{Beta}(r;\alpha,\alpha;\ell=-1;s=2)\,,

(11)

where the location
\ell
and scale
s
of the Beta distribution are set such that correlations fall on the interval
[-1,1]
. The shape parameter
\alpha
is determined individually for each contribution. For the signal-signal, noise-noise, and signal-noise contribution we find

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{s}}=\frac{m-1}{2}\,,\;\alpha_{\text{n}}=\frac{T-1}{2}\,,\;\alpha_{\text{sn}}=\frac{m^{1/2}T^{1/2}-1}{2}\,,

(12)

respectively.
The sum of Beta distributions can be well approximated by a single Beta distribution
() https://math.stackexchange.com/users/6460/henry
. This finally allows us to approximate the distribution of the entries of the full correlation matrix by a single Beta distribution of the form Eq. (
11
). In
Online Supplementary Materials
B
, we show that in the limit when contributions of
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
and
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1/2})
can be neglected, the parameter of the approximating distribution is

\displaystyle\alpha\approx\frac{\left(\sqrt{m(1+1/\textsf{SNR})}^{-1}+\sqrt{T(1+\textsf{SNR})}^{-1}\right)^{-2}-1}{2}\,.

(13)

Notably,
\alpha
is a function of
m
, and hence the shape of the distribution depends on
m
. Thus the number of latent dimensions in data can be estimated from the empirical distribution of the correlation coefficients.

Numerical validation of this result in the pure signal limit
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow\infty
, is shown in Fig.

. For small

, the distribution distinctly changes shape as
m
varies. However, when the number of latent features becomes comparable to the number of variables, and thus
q_{V^{T}}\rightarrow 1
, the distribution approaches a Gaussian, making it increasingly difficult to infer the precise value of
m
from its shape. The quality of the analytic approximation increases the smaller
q_{U}
, i. e., when more observation per latent feature are available. The analytic approximation is also exact in the large noise limit
\mathsf{SNR}\rightarrow 0
. However, deviations appear for finite
\mathsf{SNR}
when

is small, see Fig.
S3

Discussion.

We calculated statistical properties of data with latent linear features, including the density of pairwise correlations, and density of eigenvalues of the NECM. Our analysis provides two important insights. First, by looking at the distribution of the correlations and their eigenvalues, one can understand whether the latent features model is a reasonable model for the data at hand, and also ballpark the number of the latent components. Importantly, even if the eigenvalue density does not have a prominent gap, one can understand that the underlying model has a latent structure, which manifests as a distortion of the MP sea of eigenvalues. This is because our signal matrix in Eq. (
1
) is stochastic, in contrast to spiked covariance models, where deterministic perturbations appear as delta functions in the spectrum and are detectable only as true outliers
Sengupta and Mitra 1999
;
Loubaton and Vallet 2011
;
Capitaine and Donati-Martin 2016
. Second, since the spectrum of the noise correlations in the latent features model is shifted compared to the MP model, one should not use a simple truncation at the right edge of the MP density to distinguish which of the principal components are statistically significant.

We note that a lot of ink has been expended to decide whether a few variables measured well many times are more or less valuable than many variables measured infrequently and with high noise. We find that, in the intensive limit, the many measured variables lead to separation of the noise and the signal eigenvalues, resulting in a potentially more accurate discrimination of noise from signal. However, the number of observations, and their quality contribute to the
SNR
, a high value of which is also required for the opening of the signal-noise gap. Thus the quality and the quantity of measurements all contribute to the value data in very specific ways, which we now understand.

Our analysis involved a few approximations. The strongest of these was in neglecting the signal-noise contributions in the computation of the eigenvalue density. Including these contributions would make the polynomial equation for the Stieltjes transform substantially more complicated. However, we do not expect significant qualitative changes to the structure of the eigenvalue density in the limits considered. An additional limitation is that the approximation for the bounds of the eigenvalue density, Eq. (
22
), is strictly only valid in the extreme noise limits,
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow 0
and
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow\infty
. In deriving the analytic density of correlations, we assumed
q_{U}\rightarrow 0
in accordance with the limits in Eq. (
9
) and Eq.
(
10
)
, and worked to leading order in
T
. We expect the quality of the analytic density to improve if these assumptions are removed, in particular in the regime of finite
\mathsf{SNR}
, cf. Fig.
S3
.

Finally, to connect our results with the analysis of real data, additional steps are required. To model data, the means and variances of rows and columns of our matrix model have to be fit to the data. Similarly, methods to estimate the
SNR
from the data and to determine whether the Gaussian assumption for the distribution of the noise and the latent components is valid will need to be developed. Some of such extension may remain analytically tractable. Further, for our model and its extensions, it is also important to calculate the expected overlap of empirical eigenvectors with their true values.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,\frac{\cos(q)}{\sqrt{|x|^{2}+q^{2}}}e^{it\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}x}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi}\,e^{-itX}\frac{1}{\left(1+\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi}\,e^{-itX}

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,\frac{\delta(\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}t+q)+\delta(\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}t-q)}{\left(1+q^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq

\displaystyle\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi}\,e^{-\frac{itX}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}}\frac{\delta(t+q)+\delta(t-q)}{\left(1+q^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\frac{\cos(\frac{q|X|}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}})}{\left(1+q^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{|X|}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\right]^{m-1}\frac{1}{\pi\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\frac{\cos(q)}{\left(\frac{|X|^{2}}{\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}}+q^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{|X|}{2}\right]^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\frac{K_{\frac{m-1}{2}}\left(\frac{|X|}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\right)}{(\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V})^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{m}{2}\right)}.

(30)

Since the probability density function of

is symmetric around zero, the mean of the distribution vanishes:

\displaystyle\mu_{{UV}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dXX{\rm pdf}(X)=0\,.

(31)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dXX^{2}{\rm pdf}(X)

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dX}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\left[\frac{|X|}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\right]^{\frac{m-1}{2}}|X|^{2}K_{\frac{m-1}{2}}\left(\frac{|X|}{\sigma_{U}\sigma_{V}}\right)

Appendix B
Probability density of the correlation coefficients

For our latent features model with noise, here we calculate the probability distribution of entries in the empirical data correlation matrix. Before doing this, a few notes are in order.
First, the correlations depend on the basis, in which variables are measured, becoming a diagonal matrix in the special case when the measured variables are the principal axes of the data cloud. Thus to make statements independent of the basis, we consider the distribution of typical correlations, or correlations in the basis random w. r. t. the principal axes of the data. For a given realization, the
N
-dimensional data cloud is typically anisotropic, with
m<N
long directions dominated by the latent feature signal and
N-m
short directions dominated by noise. When
N\gg m
, principal axes of the data cloud do not align with the measured variables for the vast majority of random rotations, and correlations between any random pair of variables have contributions from all latent dimensions. Thus we expect the number of latent dimensions to be imprinted in the distribution of the elements of the correlation matrix, so that the statistics of the elements carries information about the underlying structure of the model.

B.1
Preliminaries: Density of the correlation coefficient of two random Gaussian variables

The correlation coefficient of two independent zero-mean variables
x
and
y
sampled
T
times is

\displaystyle r=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\frac{x_{t}y_{t}}{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}\,,

(40)

where the vectors’ components are mutually independent, i.i.d. random variables. The correlation coefficient is distributed according to
Hotelling 1953

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdf}(r)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{T}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{T-1}{2}\right)}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{T-3}{2}}\,.

(41)

This can be rewritten in terms of a Beta distribution

\displaystyle\text{Beta}(x;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{\text{B}(\alpha,\beta)}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\,,

(42)

where
x\in[0,1]
and
\text{B}(\alpha,\beta)
is the Beta function. Specifically, the density of correlations is given by the symmetric Beta distribution

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdf}(r)=\text{Beta}\left(r;\alpha,\alpha;\ell=-1,s=2\right)\,,

(43)

where the location
\ell
and scale
s
are set such that the density is defined on the interval of correlation values [-1,1], and

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{T-1}{2}\,.

(44)

We also note that the variance of a symmetric Beta distribution with the scale
s=2
is

\displaystyle\text{var}=\frac{s^{2}}{4(2\alpha+1)}=\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}\,.

(45)

B.2
Density of correlations in the latent feature model

Figure S3:
Distribution of pairwise correlations in the regimes of finite and small signal-to-noise ratio with
m=2,4
and
30
latent features. Analytic form (magenta) and simulated data (gray). (a)
\mathsf{SNR}=20
and (b)
\mathsf{SNR}=10^{-5}
(large noise limit). Each simulation is run with

variables and
T=2048

There are multiple contributions to the correlations among the measured variables. We compute them individually, and then combine the contributions. We find that each contribution is distributed according to a symmetric Beta distribution. To obtain the overall density, we approximate the sum of Beta distributions by a single Beta distribution, the parameter of which is obtained by matching the variance to the sum of the variances of the individual components. To perform these analyses, we only keep terms to the leading order in the
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow 0
or the
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow\infty
limit. Further, we assume that
q_{U}
is small in accordance with the classical and intensive regimes limits.

We start with the pure noise contribution to the correlations

\displaystyle(c_{R})_{pq}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\frac{R^{T}_{pt}R_{tq}}{\sigma_{p}^{\mathrm{n}}\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}}\,,

(46)

\displaystyle({\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}R^{T}_{qt}R_{tq}\,.

(47)

The expression on the right-hand side is the correlation coefficient between two random Gaussian variables. Using Eq. (
43
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdf}((c_{R})_{pq})=\text{Beta}\left((c_{R})_{pq};\alpha_{\text{n}},\alpha_{\text{n}};-1,2\right),\;p\neq q,

(48)

with

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{n}}=\frac{T-1}{2}\,.

(49)

and the variance of this density is

\displaystyle\text{var}_{\text{n}}=T^{-1}\,.

(50)

Next we compute the density of the pure signal contribution

\displaystyle(c_{UV})_{pq}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}}\sum_{t}\left(\sum_{\mu}V_{p\mu}U_{\mu t}\right)\left(\sum_{\nu}U_{t\nu}V_{\nu q}\right)\,,

(51)

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\left(\sum_{\mu}V_{p\mu}U_{\mu t}\right)\left(\sum_{\nu}U_{t\nu}V_{\nu p}\right)\,,

(52)

and similarly for
\sigma_{q}
. Rearranging, we find

\displaystyle(c_{UV})_{pq}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}}\sum_{\mu\nu}V_{p\mu}V_{\nu q}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}U_{\mu t}U_{t\nu}\right)\,,

(53)

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu}V_{p\mu}V_{\nu p}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}U_{\mu t}U_{t\nu}\right)\,.

(54)

The expression in parentheses of both of the equations above is a (co)-variance of Gaussian random numbers. For
\mu=\nu
, it follows the scaled
\chi^{2}
-distribution with
T
degrees of freedom. For
\mu\neq\nu
, it is given by a rescaled version of the distribution in Eq. (
30
), with
T
instead of
m
. Crucially, the variance of either is
1/T
. Thus in the limit

, the terms in parentheses are
\sigma^{2}_{U}\delta_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
, where the correction
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
is probabilistic, but will be neglected in what follows. We get

\displaystyle(c_{UV})_{pq}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{U}^{2}\sum_{\mu\nu}\frac{V_{p\mu}V_{\nu q}\delta_{\mu\nu}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}}=m\sigma_{U}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\mu}\frac{V_{p\mu}V_{\mu q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}}\right)\,,

(55)

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p})^{2}

\displaystyle=m\sigma_{U}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\mu}V_{p\mu}^{2}\right)\,,

(56)

We see that the sought after correlation is a correlation coefficient between Gaussian variables, but with
m
samples instead of
T
. Using again Eq. (
43
), we write

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdf}\left((c_{UV})_{pq}\right)=\text{Beta}\left((c_{UV})_{pq};\alpha_{\text{s}},\alpha_{\text{s}};-1,2\right),\,p\neq q,

(57)

with parameter

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{s}}=\frac{m-1}{2}\,.

(58)

We remind the reader that Eq. (

) holds to
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
. The variance of this density is

\displaystyle\text{var}_{\text{s}}

\displaystyle=m^{-1}\,.

(59)

This expression agrees with numerical simulations very well, cf. Fig.
1
.

Finally, for the signal-noise cross terms in the correlation, we have

\displaystyle(c_{(UV)^{T}R})_{pq}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}}\sum_{t}\sum_{\mu}V_{p\mu}U_{\mu t}R_{tq}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}}\sum_{\mu}V_{p\mu}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}U_{\mu t}R_{tq}\right)\,.

(60)

For the quantity in parentheses in Eq. (
60
), we define

\displaystyle r_{\mu p}\equiv\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}U_{\mu t}R_{tq}\,.

(61)

This is a covariance between two independent Gaussian random numbers and again follows a rescaled form of the distribution in Eq. (
30
) with variance
\sigma_{U}^{2}{\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}}^{2}T^{-1}
. Since
T
is large, the distribution approaches a Gaussian and we further define
r_{\mu q}\equiv\sigma_{U}\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}T^{-1/2}r_{\mu q}^{\prime}
, such that
r_{\mu q}^{\prime}
is a unit Gaussian random variable. Thus we obtain

(c_{(UV)^{T}R})_{pq}=\frac{\sigma_{U}m\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}T^{-1/2}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma_{q}^{\mathrm{n}}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\mu}V_{p\mu}r^{\prime}_{\mu q}\right)\\
=m^{1/2}T^{-1/2}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\mu}\frac{V_{p\mu}r^{\prime}_{\mu q}}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\mu}{V_{p\mu}}^{2}}\right)\,,

(62)

where we have extracted the factor of
m
to highlight that the expression in parenthesis is the correlation between Gaussian random numbers. From this, using Eq. (
43
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdf}

\displaystyle\left((c_{(UV)^{T}R})_{pq}\right)=

\displaystyle\text{Beta}\left((c_{(UV)^{T}R})_{pq};\alpha_{\text{sn}},\alpha_{\text{sn}};-1,2\right),\,p\neq q\,,

(63)

with parameter

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{sn}}=\frac{m^{1/2}T^{1/2}-1}{2}\,.

(64)

The variance of this density is

\displaystyle\text{var}_{\text{sn}}

\displaystyle=m^{-1/2}T^{-1/2}=\sqrt{\text{var}_{\text{s}}\cdot\text{var}_{\text{n}}}\,.

(65)

An analogous expression holds for the
R^{T}UV
contribution.

The empirical correlation matrix is given by

\displaystyle c_{pq}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\frac{X_{pt}X_{tq}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}\,,

(66)

where

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p})^{2}=(\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p})^{2}+(\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{p})^{2}\,.

(67)

Using Eqs (

\displaystyle c_{pq}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}(c_{UV})_{pq}+\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}(c_{(UV)^{T}R})_{pq}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}(c_{R^{T}UV})_{pq}+\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}(c_{R})_{pq}

Each term on the right-hand side of this equation follows a Beta distribution as computed above. However, the

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{s}}\,

(69)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{n}}\,

(70)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sn}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{s}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{n}}_{q}}{\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{p}\sigma^{\mathrm{sn}}_{q}}\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{sn}}\,.

(71)

To determine an expression for the combined distribution of signal and noise correlations, we make use of the observation that the sum of Beta distributions can be well approximated by a single Beta distribution
() https://math.stackexchange.com/users/6460/henry
. We determine the parameters of the Beta distribution by adding the means and variances of the distributions in the sum and analytically match the parameter of the single Beta distribution.

The means of the Beta distributions in Eq. (

\displaystyle\rightarrow m\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle(\sigma_{p}^{\mathrm{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle(\sigma_{p}^{\mathrm{sn}})^{2}\,,(\sigma_{p}^{\mathrm{ns}})^{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow m\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\,.

(75)

Consequently the variances of the contributions take the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{m^{-1}}{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}\,,

(76)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{n}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{T^{-1}}{1+\textsf{SNR}}\,,

(77)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sn}}\,,\mathrm{var}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{ns}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{m^{-1/2}T^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+\textsf{SNR}}\sqrt{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}}\,,

(78)

Thus, in this limit, the variance of the Beta distribution, Eq. (

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{m^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}}+\frac{T^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+\textsf{SNR}}}\right)^{2}\,.

(80)

Finally, from the relation in Eq. (

of the sought after Beta distribution.

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{\text{var}^{-1}-1}{2}\,.

(81)

Appendix C
Spectrum of the normalized empirical covariance Matrix

To compute the eigenvalue density of the NECM
\mathbf{C}
, we use methods of Random Matrix Theory
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
. The standard approach is to compute the finite size Stieljtes transform

\displaystyle g^{N}_{\mathbf{C}}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\text{Tr}(z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C})^{-1}\,,

(82)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix,
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
g^{N}_{\mathbf{C}}
is a complex function. In the limit of large matrices – large
N
or thermodynamic limit – the finite size Stieltjes transform becomes,
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}(z)
. Then the eigenvalue density is obtained as the imaginary part of the limit of the Stieltjes transform:

\displaystyle\rho(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im\mathfrak{g}(z=\lambda-i\eta)\,,

(83)

where
\Im
denotes the imaginary part.

We start with writing again the definition of the normalized empirical covariance matrix (NECM), which differs from the correlation matrix only by
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}=\frac{1}{T}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}+\sigma\mathbf{R}})^{T}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}+\sigma\mathbf{R}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\Big((\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})^{T}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})+\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle+\tilde{\sigma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})^{T}\mathbf{R}+\tilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}\Big)\,.

(84)

The NECM contains three different contributions: the
(\mathbf{UV})^{T}(\mathbf{UV})
from the pure latent feature signal,
\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}
from pure noise, and two terms of the type
(\mathbf{UV})^{T}\mathbf{R}
, which are cross terms between the latent signal and the noise. Each contribution is an
N\times N
random matrix. Critical to computing the eigenvalue density of random matrices is the concept of
matrix freeness

Voiculescu et al. 1992
, which is the generalization of statistical independence to matrices. The eigenvalue spectrum of sums and products of free matrices can be computed from spectra of summands and factors using the
\mathcal{R}
- and the
\mathcal{S}
-transforms, which are related to the Stieltjes transform
\mathfrak{g}
and are additive and multiplicative, respectively. The signal-signal and the noise-noise contributions in the NECM definition are certainly free w. r. t. each other. We will argue in
Appendix
C.3
that, in our regimes of interest (the zero-noise limit (
\mathsf{SNR}\rightarrow\infty
), the classical statistics limit from Eq. (
9
), and intensive limit from Eq. (
10
)), the cross-term contributions are negligible, so that we can drop them and approximate the NECM as

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V})^{T}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V})+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}}{\sigma_{X}^{2}T}:=\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}+\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}}\,,

(85)

so that free matrix theory applies.

C.1
Parameterizing the random matrix problem and the large matrix limit

To calculate the spectrum of the signal-signal contribution to the NECM,

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{X}^{2}T}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V})^{T}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V})\,,

(86)

we note that, assuming
m<T,N
, this
N\times N
matrix is of rank
m
. Thus we can work in the basis, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\,,

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{X}^{2}T}\left(\mathbf{U}^{T}\mathbf{U}\right)\left(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{T}\right)\,.

(89)

There are
m
non-trivial eigenvalues associated with
\mathbf{H}
, while the remaining
N-m
eigenvalues are zero.
The finite size Stieltjes transform,
g_{\mathbf{C}}^{N}=N^{-1}\text{Tr}(z\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{C}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}})^{-1}
, is then of the form

\displaystyle g^{N}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(m\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{\mu}}+\frac{N-m}{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(m\,h^{m}_{\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}(z)+\frac{N-m}{z}\right)\,,

(90)

where
\lambda_{\mu}
are the
m
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}
and
h^{m}_{\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}(z)
is its finite size Stieltjes transform.

Now we note that
\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}
in Eq. (
89
) is the product of two white Wishart matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}=\frac{N}{\sigma_{X}^{2}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{V}^{T}}\,,

(91)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{Y}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{Y}^{T}\mathbf{Y}\,,

(92)

is the Wishart matrix, and
\mathbf{Y}
is a
T\times N
matrix with i.i.d. standard normal entries. The key parameter characterizing such standard
\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{Y}}
is the ratio of the number of columns to that of rows

\displaystyle q\equiv\frac{N}{T}\,.

(93)

Since
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}^{T}
are
T\times m
and
N\times m
matrices, respectively, a natural characterisation of
\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}
is then

\displaystyle q\equiv\frac{N}{T},\quad q_{U}\equiv\frac{m}{T},\quad q_{V^{T}}\equiv\frac{m}{N}\,,

(94)

with
q\,q_{V^{T}}=q_{U}
, so that there are only two independent parameters.

It is now convenient to define

\displaystyle\sigma_{X}^{2}=m\left(\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{m}\right)\equiv m\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\,,

(95)

where we used Eq. (
38
), so that Eq. (
91
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}=\frac{1}{q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{V}^{T}}\,.

(96)

In the following, we only consider the limit of large matrices. Here
T
,
N
,
m
and
\sigma^{2}
go to infinity in such a way that
q
,
q_{V^{T}}
and
SNR
are all constant. Then in the thermodynamic limit the finite size Stieltjes transform in Eq. (
C.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}}

\displaystyle=q_{V^{T}}\mathfrak{h}+\frac{1-q_{V^{T}}}{z}\,,

(97)

where
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}}
and
\mathfrak{h}
are the large matrices limits of the Stieltjes transforms of
g^{N}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}
and
h^{m}_{\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}
, respectively.

C.2
The spectrum of
\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}

We now compute the eigenvalue density of
\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}
. The first step is to compute the Stieljtes transform
\mathfrak{h}
. From Eq. (
96
), it is clear that this reduces to the problem of computing the eigenvalue spectrum of a product of two Wishart matrices.

The spectrum of a product of two free matrices can be computed with the help of the
\mathcal{S}
-transform, which is defined for a random matrix
\mathbf{A}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{A}}(t)=\frac{t+1}{t{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}(t)}\,,

(98)

where
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}(t)
is the functional inverse of the
\mathcal{T}
-transform
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)
. In turn, the
\mathcal{T}
-transform is related to the Stieltjes transform of
\mathbf{A}
through the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)=z\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)-1\,.

(99)

Crucially, for free matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, the
\mathcal{S}
-transform is multiplicative

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(t)=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{A}}(t)\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}}(t)\,,

(100)

and, furthermore, for a scalar
a
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{a\mathbf{A}}(t)=a^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{A}}(t)\,.

(101)

For the white Wishart matrix, Eq. (
92
), the
\mathcal{S}
-transform is known to be
Potters and Bouchaud 2020

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{W_{Y}}}(t)=\frac{1}{1+qt}\,.

(102)

Thus we only need to use the multiplicative property of the
\mathcal{S}
-transform to compute the signal-signal contributions to the NECM. Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}(t)

\displaystyle=q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{U}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{V}^{T}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}{(1+q_{U}t)(1+q_{V^{T}}t)}\,.

(103)

Equation (
98
) then yields

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}_{\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}(t)=\frac{t+1}{t\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{H}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}(t)}=\frac{t+1}{t}\frac{(1+q_{U}t)(1+q_{V^{T}}t)}{q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}\,.

(104)

We now solve the equation for the functional inverse,
{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}(\mathcal{T}(z))=z
, using the definition of the
\mathcal{T}
-transform, Eq. (
99
), and dividing by a common factor of
z
. We obtain a cubic equation for the Stieltjes transform
\mathfrak{h}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{3}

\displaystyle z^{2}q_{U}q_{V^{T}}+\mathfrak{h}^{2}z\left(q_{V^{T}}(1-q_{U})+q_{U}(1-q_{V^{T}})\right)

\displaystyle+\mathfrak{h}\left((1-q_{U})(1-q_{V^{T}})-zq_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\right)+q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}=0\,.

(105)

Finally, we divide by
q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{3}\frac{z^{2}q_{U}}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\mathfrak{h}^{2}\frac{z}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}\left(1+q-2q_{U}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathfrak{h}\left(\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}-q-1+q_{U}}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}-z\right)+1=0\,.

(106)

Similar equations for the Stieljtes transform of the product of two random matrices have been stated in
Müller 2002
;
Burda et al. 2010
;
Dupic and Castillo 2014
. Their polynomials differ from Eq. (
C.2
) in details, because we consider the Stieljtes transform of the covariance matrix including a theoretical normalisation factor.

The next step is to solve Eq. (
C.2
) analytically in the classical statistics limit and the intensive limit. We remind the reader that, for the pure signal contribution, we work in the zero noise limit
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow\infty
, such that

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\equiv\frac{\sigma_{X}^{2}}{m}=\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1})=\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}\,.

(107)

C.2.1
Classical statistics limit

In the
classical statistics
limit, Eq. (
9
), the polynomial equation for the Stieltjes transform, Eq. (
C.2
), becomes:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{2}\frac{z}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}+\mathfrak{h}\left(\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}-z\right)+1=0\,.

(108)

The discriminant is

\displaystyle\Delta=z^{2}-2\frac{1+q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}z+\left(\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}\right)^{2},

(109)

and the roots of the discriminant are

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}^{\infty}=\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{q_{V^{T}}}^{-1}\right)^{2}.

(110)

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{\pm}=\frac{-\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}+z\pm\sqrt{(z-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(z-\lambda_{+}^{\infty})}}{2z{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}}\,.

(111)

To obtain
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}
, we now need to add the contribution of the zero eigenvalues:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}

\displaystyle=q_{V^{T}}\mathfrak{h}_{\pm}+\frac{1-q_{V^{T}}}{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-q_{V^{T}}}{2z}+\frac{q_{V^{T}}}{2{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}}\pm\frac{\sqrt{(z-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(z-\lambda_{+}^{\infty})}}{2zq_{V^{T}}^{-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}}\,.

(112)

We are now ready to obtain the eigenvalue density, as in Eq. (
83
). While this is a standard calculation
Livan et al. 2018
, we summarise it here for the reader’s benefit. The second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
C.2.1
) is real, does not contribute to the imaginary part, and we ignore it. For the first and the third terms, we multiply the numerators and the denominators by
z^{*}=\lambda+i\eta
. The imaginary part of the first term is then

\displaystyle\Im\left(\frac{1-q_{V^{T}}}{2z}\right)=\frac{(1-q_{V^{T}})\eta}{2(\eta^{2}+\lambda^{2})}=\frac{(1-q_{V^{T}})\pi}{2}\delta_{\eta}(\lambda)\,,

(113)

where we have used the definition of the Lorentz curve,
\delta_{\eta}(\lambda)=\pi^{-1}\eta/(\eta^{2}+\lambda^{2})
.
For the third term, the crucial step is to rewrite the square root using the relation

\displaystyle\sqrt{a+ib}=P+iQ\,,

(114)

where
a
and
b
are real,
b\neq 0
and

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}+a},

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{sgn}(b)}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-a}\,,

(115)

where
\mathrm{sgn}(x)=1
for
x>0
and
-1
for
x<0

Rabinowitz 1993
. For the argument of the square root in the third term of Eq. (
C.2.1
), we find

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}-\eta^{2}+\lambda_{+}^{\infty}\lambda_{-}^{\infty}-(\lambda_{+}^{\infty}+\lambda_{-}^{\infty})\lambda,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=(-2\lambda+\lambda_{+}^{\infty}+\lambda_{-}^{\infty})\eta\,.

(116)

The imaginary part of the third term takes the form

\displaystyle\Im{\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{(z-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(z-\lambda_{+}^{\infty})}}{2zq_{V^{T}}^{-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}}\right)}=

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\Im\left(z^{*}\left[P+iQ\right]\right)}{2q_{V^{T}}^{-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}|z|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{2q_{V^{T}}^{-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}}\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta^{2}+\lambda^{2}}P+\frac{\lambda}{\eta^{2}+\lambda^{2}}Q\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{2q_{V^{T}}^{-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}}\left(\pi\delta_{\eta}(\lambda)P+\frac{\lambda}{\eta^{2}+\lambda^{2}}Q\right).

(117)

The final step to evaluate Eq. (
83
) and to obtain the eigenvalue density, is to take the limit
\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. In this limit, the Lorentz curve in Eq. (
113
) converges to the Dirac
\delta
-function. Combining Eqs. (
113
,
C.2.1
) yields

\displaystyle\rho^{\infty}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im\,\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}P}{2q_{V^{T}}^{-1}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}}\delta(\lambda)\pm\frac{\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}Q}{2\pi\lambda\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}+\frac{1-q_{V^{T}}}{2}\delta(\lambda)

(118)

with

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}P=\sqrt{\lambda_{+}^{\infty}\lambda_{-}^{\infty}}=\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}(1-q_{V^{T}}^{-1})\,,

(119)

where we have used the expression for the zero noise eigenvalue bounds in Eq. (
110
), and

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}Q

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{sgn}(b)}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{2|(\lambda-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(\lambda_{+}^{\infty}-\lambda)|}

\displaystyle=\text{sgn}(b)\sqrt{(\lambda-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(\lambda_{+}^{\infty}-\lambda)},

(120)

when
\lambda\in[\lambda_{-}^{\infty},\lambda_{+}^{\infty}]
, and the expression vanishes elsewhere.
The
\pm
signs in Eq. (
C.2.1
) are chosen such as to obtain a physically meaningful eigenvalue density.
Finally, we find the following form of the eigenvalue density

\displaystyle\rho^{\infty}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{(\lambda-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(\lambda_{+}^{\infty}-\lambda)}}{2\pi\lambda{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}+(1-q_{V^{T}})\delta(\lambda)\,,

(121)

with
\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\equiv\sigma_{X}^{2}/m=\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}
. We note that in
Cui et al. 2020
an expression for an eigenvalue density was given in the special case when
T=N=m
and not including our theoretical normalisation factor.

C.2.2
Intensive limit

For the
intensive limit
, Eq. (
10
), the polynomial equation for the Stieltjes transform, Eq. (
C.2
), becomes

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{2}\frac{z}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}\left(1+q\right)+\mathfrak{h}\left(\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}-q-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}-z\right)+1=0\,.

(122)

The discriminant is

\displaystyle\Delta=z^{2}-2\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}+q+1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}z+\left(\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}-q-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}\right)^{2}\,.

(123)

The roots of the discriminant are

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}^{\infty}=\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}\left(\sqrt{1+q}\pm\sqrt{q_{V^{T}}}^{-1}\right)^{2}\,.

(124)

Then the solution of the quadratic equation is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{\pm}=\frac{-\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}-q-1}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}+z\pm\sqrt{(z-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(z-\lambda_{+}^{\infty})}}{2z{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}(1+q)}.

(125)

Following a calculation analogous to the classical limit, we now add the contribution of the zero eigenvalues and then determine the density of the eigenvalues. We find:

\displaystyle\rho^{\infty}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{(\lambda-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})(\lambda_{+}^{\infty}-\lambda)}}{2\pi\lambda{\bar{\sigma}_{X}}^{-2}(1+q)q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}+(1-q_{V^{T}})\delta(\lambda)\,,

(126)

with
\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\equiv\sigma_{X}^{2}/m=\sigma_{U}^{2}\sigma_{V}^{2}
.

C.3
Approximation to neglect the signal-noise cross terms

Now we explore when the contribution of the signal-noise cross terms to the NECM can be neglected. Specifically, we will show that it can be done if
q_{U}\rightarrow 0
(that is, the number of measurements is much larger than the number of latent features), which we always assume. To show this, we compute the eigenvalue bounds,
\lambda_{\pm}^{\text{signal-noise}}
, of the signal-noise contribution and compare their scaling with
T
to the scaling of the pure signal and the pure noise eigenvalue bounds.

For the pure signal contribution, the previous section shows that the eigenvalue bounds
\lambda^{\infty}_{\pm}
are
\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}\sim\mathcal{O}(T^{0})
. The pure noise eigenvalue bounds, given by the Marčenko-Pastur bounds, scale as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}^{\mathrm{MP}}\sim 1\pm T^{-1/2}\,,

(127)

where
1
is due to self correlations. On the other hand, the signal-noise cross terms do not have self-correlations, and thus we expect their bounds to scale as

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mathrm{signal-noise}}_{\pm}\sim T^{-1/2}\,,

(128)

becoming negligible for
T\to\infty
. In
Appendix
C.3.1
, we show this analytically in the classical statistics limit. We have not been able to achieve similar results more generally. However, since
q_{U}\rightarrow 0
also in the intensive limit, we expect similar results to hold there too. To show this, we resort to numerical simulations.

Figure S4:
Difference between the eigenvalue density of the NECM spectrum with and without the signal-noise cross terms quantified by
d^{\prime}
. (a) Classical limit (orange), intensive limit (green) and neither of the two limits (blue). (b) Magnified view of
d^{\prime}
in the limits of interest: classical limit (blue, orange and green) and intensive limit (red and purple). Eigenvalue densities are computed from
120
realisations of the random matrix model.

Specifically, we numerically estimate the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the numerically evaluated eigenvalue densities of the NECM, computed with and without the signal-noise cross terms. To obtain a perceptually intuitive measure of the difference between these distributions, we convert the Jensen-Shannon divergence to the effective sensitivity index
d^{\prime}
– the distance between the means of two unit variance normal distribution with the same Jensen-Shannon divergence as the two analyzed eigenvalue spectra. We investigate the dependence of
d^{\prime}
on various choices of our model parameters. The comparison between the spectra of the full and the approximate NECM is shown in Fig.
S4
. We observe that the sensitivity index reaches a maximum for
\mathsf{SNR}\sim 10^{-1}
, and falls off in the limits of small or large
\mathsf{SNR}
, where the noise or the signal dominate, respectively. Crucially, the maximum value of
d^{\prime}
is small for
q_{U}\to 0
. Thus neglecting the cross-term contributions to the NECM spectrum in our limits of interest is warranted.

C.3.1
Scaling behavior of the signal-noise eigenvalue bounds in the classical limit

We now derive the scaling of the signal-noise eigenvalue spectrum bounds in Eq. (
128
) in the classical statistics limit. From the NECM in Eq. (
C
), the signal-noise cross terms are of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\equiv\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{T}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})^{T}{\mathbf{R}}\,.

(129)

To compute the spectrum of this matrix, we use the following trick. The singular values of
\mathbf{M}
are equal to the square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues of its square,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{T}=\frac{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}{T^{2}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})^{T}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}\,.

(130)

In turn, the non-zero eigenvalues of this
N\times N
matrix, are equal to the eigenvalues of the
T\times T
matrix

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}\equiv q^{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\frac{{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}^{T}}}{N}\frac{(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}})^{T}}{N}\,.

(131)

We note that the r.h.s. of the above equation is a product of the
T\times T
dual correlation matrices
\mathbf{C}_{\tilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}^{T}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}
. To compute the spectrum of the product, we employ the
\mathcal{S}
-transform formalism, as explained above. The first step is to obtain the
\mathcal{S}
-transforms of the dual correlation matrices, which we compute from the Stieltjes transform
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
. For the noise part, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{R}^{T}}}(z)=q^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{R}}}(qz)+\frac{1-q}{z}.

(132)

For the signal part, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}(z)=q^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}(qz)+\frac{1-q}{z}\,.

(133)

For the noise Wishart matrix, the Stieljtes transform is (cf. Eq. (
102
)):

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}_{{{\mathbf{R}}}^{T}}}=\frac{1}{1+q^{-1}t}\,.

(134)

Including the renormalized noise strength,
\tilde{\sigma}
, and the additional factor of
q
from Eq. (
131
), by using the scaling relation Eq. (
101
), the
\mathcal{S}
-transform is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}_{{{q\tilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}}}^{T}}}=\frac{q^{-2}\tilde{\sigma}^{-2}}{1+q^{-1}t}\,.

(135)

Next, we write the
\mathcal{S}
-transform of the pure signal part. Evaluating Eq. (
133
) using Eq. (
C.2.1
), we find

\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}=\\
\frac{\frac{2-q-qq_{V^{T}}}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}+q^{2}q_{V^{T}}z\pm qq_{V^{T}}\sqrt{(qz-\lambda^{\infty}_{+})(qz-\lambda^{\infty}_{-})}}{2z\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}}\,,

(136)

from which we obtain the following equation for the Stieltjes transform:

\displaystyle\left(2z\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{-2}\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}-\frac{2-q-qq_{V^{T}}}{\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}-q^{2}q_{V^{T}}z\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-q^{2}q_{V^{T}}^{2}(qz-\lambda_{+}^{\infty})(qz-\lambda_{-}^{\infty})=0\,.

(137)

Using the relation,
\mathcal{T}=z\mathfrak{g}-1
, we find the equation for the
\mathcal{T}
-transform

\mathcal{T}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}+\\
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}(-zq^{2}q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}+qq_{V^{T}}+q)+q^{2}q_{V^{T}}=0\,.

(138)

Interpreted as an equation for the functional inverse transform
\mathcal{T}^{-1}
, this becomes

\displaystyle t^{2}+t(-\mathcal{T}^{-1}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}q^{2}q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}+qq_{V^{T}}+q)+q^{2}q_{V^{T}}=0\,.

(139)

Now solving for the functional inverse transform, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{-1}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}=\frac{(t+q)(t+q_{U})}{tqq_{U}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}},

(140)

from which we determine the
\mathcal{S}
-transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}=\frac{t+1}{t\mathcal{T}^{-1}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}}=\frac{qq_{U}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}(t+1)}{(t+q)(t+q_{U})}\,.

(141)

The
\mathcal{S}
-transform of the product now reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}_{(\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}})^{T}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}_{{q\tilde{\sigma}{\mathbf{R}}}^{T}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t+1}{t}\frac{tqq_{U}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}{(t+q)(t+q_{U})}\frac{q^{-2}\tilde{\sigma}^{-2}}{1+q^{-1}t}.

(142)

From this we read off the inverse transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{-1}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(t+q)(t+q_{U})(1+q^{-1}t)}{tq^{-1}q_{U}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{-2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(t+q)(t+q_{U})(1+q^{-1}t)}{tq^{-1}q_{U}\mathsf{SNR}^{-1}}.

(143)

The equation for the
\mathcal{T}
-transform is now

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}+q)(\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}+q_{U})(1+q^{-1}\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}})=\dfrac{zq_{U}\mathcal{T}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}}{q\,\mathsf{SNR}}.

(144)

Using
\mathcal{T}=z\mathfrak{g}-1
we write down the cubic polynomial equation for
\mathfrak{g}
:

\displaystyle a\mathfrak{g}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}^{3}+b\mathfrak{g}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}^{2}+c\mathfrak{g}_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}}+d=0\,,

(145)

with coefficients

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=z^{3},

(146)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=2qz^{2}+q_{U}z^{2}-3z^{2},

(147)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=q^{2}z+2qq_{U}z-4qz-2q_{U}z+3z-\frac{q_{U}z^{2}}{\textsf{SNR}},

(148)

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=q^{2}q_{U}-q^{2}-2qq_{U}+2q+q_{U}-1+\frac{q_{U}z}{\textsf{SNR}}.

(149)

The eigenvalue density is non-zero for complex solutions of the equation. The equation admits complex solutions when the discriminant
\Delta
is negative:

\displaystyle\Delta=4P^{3}+27Q^{2}\,,

(150)

where

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{3ac-b^{2}}{3a^{2}},

(151)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\frac{2b^{2}-9abc+27a^{2}d}{27a^{3}}.

(152)

Written out explicitly, the determinant takes the form

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{4(3ac-b^{2})^{3}+(2b^{3}-9abc+27a^{2}d)^{2}}{27a^{6}}\,.

(153)

The equation
\Delta=0
yields a quadratic equation in
z
, giving the bounds on the eigenvalue density of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}

\displaystyle z_{\pm}=\frac{8q^{2}+20qq_{U}-q_{U}^{2}\pm\sqrt{q_{U}(8q+q_{U})^{3}}}{8q_{U}\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}.

(154)

From the definitions of
q=N/T
and
q_{U}=m/T
, we see that these bounds scale as

\displaystyle z_{\pm}\sim T^{-1}\,.

(155)

Since the singular values of
\mathbf{M}
are equal to the square root of the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{2}
, the eigenvalue bounds of the signal-noise cross terms thus scale as

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mathrm{signal-noise}}_{\pm}\sim T^{-1/2}\,.

(156)

Thus the contribution of the cross-terms can be neglected in the classical statistics limit.

C.4
Adding the noise contribution
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{\tilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}}}

In
Appendix
C.2
we computed the spectrum of the pure signal contribution
\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}
to the NECM in the classical statistics and the intensive limits. Now we will add the pure noise contribution
\mathbf{C}_{\tilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}}
to obtain the spectrum of the approximate NECM. Since the noise and the signal contributions are free matrices with respect to each other, the spectrum of their sum can be computed using the
\mathcal{R}
-transform. The
\mathcal{R}
-transform of a random matrix
\mathbf{A}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)-1/z\,,

(157)

where the
\mathcal{B}
-transform is the functional inverse of the Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{A}}[\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{A}}]=z.

(158)

The
\mathcal{R}
-transform is additive for free matrices:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}}(z)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{A}}(z)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{B}}(z)\,.

(159)

It scales according to

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{a\mathbf{A}}(z)=a\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{A}}(az)\,,

(160)

where
a
is a real number. For a white Wishart matrix, Eq. (
92
), the
\mathcal{R}
-transform is known to be
Potters and Bouchaud 2020

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{Y}}}(z)=\frac{1}{1-qz}\,.

(161)

For the pure noise contribution to the NECM,
\mathbf{C}_{\tilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}}\equiv\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}/T
, this results in

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}}}(z)=\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}{1-qz\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}\,.

(162)

Our goal is to first obtain the
\mathcal{R}
-transform of the sum of the signal and the noise contributions

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{C}}(z)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}}(z)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\sigma}\mathbf{R}}}(z),

(163)

and from this to compute the Stieltjes transform to extract the eigenvalue density. Computing the
\mathcal{R}
-transform of the pure signal contribution
\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}
in the classical statistics and the intensive limit requires additional steps.

C.4.1
Classical statistics limit

First, we compute the
\mathcal{R}
-transform of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}
. This is done by solving the functional inverse equation,
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}[\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}}]=z
, which gives us the
\mathcal{B}
-transform, from which we compute the
\mathcal{R}
-transform. From Eq. (
C.2.1
), we see that
\mathcal{B}
-transform satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}}\left(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}}z(-q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}+z)+q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}+q_{V^{T}}z-z\right)=0.

(164)

A non-trivial solution of this equation is

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}}=\frac{q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}+q_{V^{T}}z-z}{z(q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}-z)}\,.

(165)

Using Eq. (
157
), this gives us the
\mathcal{R}
-transform,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}}}}
. To it we add the
\mathcal{R}
-transform of the noise, Eq. (
162
), and subtract
-1/z
to get the
\mathcal{B}
-transform of the approximate NECM:

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{C}}=\\
\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}z(q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}-z)+(-q\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}z+1)(q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}+q_{V^{T}}z-z)}{z(q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}-z)(-q\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}z+1)}\,.

(166)

The final step is to write down and solve the inverse function equation
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{C}}[\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}]=z
. This is now equivalent to solving the third order polynomial equation

\displaystyle a\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}^{3}+b\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}+c\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}+d=0\,,\;\mbox{with}

(167)

\displaystyle a=qz\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}\,,

(168)

\displaystyle b=-qq_{V^{T}}z\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\big((q_{V^{T}}-1)q+1\big)\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}-z\,,

(169)

\displaystyle c=(q-1)q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+q_{V^{T}}z\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}-q_{V^{T}}+1\,,

(170)

\displaystyle d=-q_{V^{T}}\bar{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\,.

(171)

Written in terms of the signal-to-noise ratio,
SNR
, the coefficients take the form

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{qz}{1+\textsf{SNR}}\,,

(172)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=-\frac{qq_{V^{T}}z}{\textsf{SNR}}+\frac{(q_{V^{T}}-1)q+1}{1+\textsf{SNR}}-z\,,

(173)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\frac{(q-1)q_{V^{T}}}{\textsf{SNR}}+q_{V^{T}}z\big(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\big)-q_{V^{T}}+1\,,

(174)

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=-q_{V^{T}}\big(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\big)\,.

(175)

It is possible to solve this cubic equation analytically. However, the expressions become lengthy and provide little insight. Therefore, we rely on the numerical solution of the equation as shown in Fig.
2
, as well as on the following analyses in the limits of small and large noise.

First, in the limit of the pure signal,
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow\infty
, we recover Eq. (
20
). Similarly, by truncating the polynomial coefficients in the pure noise limit,
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow 0
, at order
\mathcal{O}(\textsf{SNR}^{-1})
, the MP density is recovered.

We also derive an approximate analytic expression for the bounds of the eigenvalue density,
\lambda_{\pm}^{\textsf{SNR}}
, which is valid around both noise limits,
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow 0
and
\textsf{SNR}\rightarrow\infty
. For this, we approximate the coefficients of the polynomial, noting that the smallest contribution to the coefficients common to both limits comes from terms of order
\mathcal{O}(q/(1+\textsf{SNR}))
(recall that
q\rightarrow 0
in the classical limit). Neglecting these terms leads to a quadratic polynomial equation for the Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle r\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}+s\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}+t\approx 0\,,\;\mbox{with}

(176)

\displaystyle r=-\frac{qq_{V^{T}}z}{\textsf{SNR}}+\frac{1}{1+\textsf{SNR}}-z\,,

(177)

\displaystyle s=\frac{(q-1)q_{V^{T}}}{\textsf{SNR}}+q_{V^{T}}z\big(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\big)-q_{V^{T}}+1\,,

(178)

\displaystyle t=-q_{V^{T}}\big(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\big)\,.

(179)

The approximate bounds of the eigenvalue density then are given by the roots of the discriminant,
\Delta\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}\approx s^{2}-4rt
, which gives the following bounds for the nonzero range of the eigenvalue density:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}^{\textsf{SNR}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1+q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}+\frac{1+q}{1+\textsf{SNR}}\pm 2\sqrt{\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\right)^{2}}+\frac{q}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}\right)^{2}}+\frac{q}{\left(\sqrt{\textsf{SNR}}+\sqrt{\textsf{SNR}}^{-1}\right)^{2}}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1+q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}+\frac{1+q}{1+\textsf{SNR}}\pm 2\sqrt{\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\right)^{2}}+\frac{q}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}\right)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}+\frac{1+q}{1+\textsf{SNR}}\pm 2\sqrt{\left[\sqrt{\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\right)^{2}}}+\sqrt{\frac{q}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}\right)^{2}}}\right]^{2}-\frac{2\sqrt{qq_{V^{T}}^{-1}}}{(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1})(1+\textsf{SNR})}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1+q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}+\frac{1+q}{1+\textsf{SNR}}\pm 2\left[\sqrt{\frac{q_{V^{T}}^{-1}}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}\right)^{2}}}+\sqrt{\frac{q}{\left(1+\textsf{SNR}\right)^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1}}\lambda_{\pm}^{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+\textsf{SNR}}\lambda_{\pm}^{\text{MP}}\,.

(180)

In the second line, we drop the third term under the square root, since it is small in either of the two noise limits.
In the third line, we have used
(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})^{2}=a+b+2\sqrt{ab}
, and in the fourth line, we dropped the last term under the square root since it is also small in either of the two noise limits. In the final line we recognize that the terms form the weighted average of
\lambda^{\infty}_{\pm}
, Eq. (
21
), and the Marčenko-Pastur bounds
\lambda_{\pm}^{\text{MP}}
.

C.4.2
Intensive limit

First we compute the
\mathcal{R}
-transform of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}

\mathcal{B}
-transform, from which we compute the
\mathcal{R}

for which we find the solution

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{C}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{UV}}}}

We solve this polynomial equation numerically, looking for complex roots which yield non-zero values of the eigenvalue density. For large signal-to-noise ratio, we encounter numerical instabilities trying to determine the eigenvalue density bounds. We run into these instabilities in the determination of the true density bounds in Fig.
2
(d). To fix this, we start at the peak of the density and determine the values of
\lambda
, for which it hits zero for the first time, to either side of the peak. All other zero density crossings are assumed to be due to numerical instabilities.

The ranges of nonzero density are shown in Fig.
2
(d). We see that, as the signal-to-noise ratio increases, there is a bifurcation point where the density splits into two bumps. The left bump is associated with the noise, and the right bump is associated with pure latent feature signal. From our approximate expression for the eigenvalue bounds, Eq. (
22
), we can estimate the value of the
SNR
at which the splitting occurs. The defining equation for this is given by the intersection between the right boundary of the noise region and the left boundary of the signal part of the densit in Eq. (
22
), resulting in:

\displaystyle(1+\textsf{SNR}^{-1})\lambda_{+}^{\text{MP}}=\lambda_{-}^{\textsf{SNR}}\,.

(192)

Solving for
SNR
, we obtain the following estimation for the splitting point:

\displaystyle\textsf{SNR}_{\text{split}}\approx\frac{\lambda_{+}^{\text{MP}}-\lambda_{-}^{\text{MP}}}{\lambda_{-}^{\infty}}\,.

(193)
