Bias- and Variance-Aware Probabilistic Rounding Error Analysis for Floating-Point Arithmetic Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Sahil Bhola, Karthik Duraisamy

Abstract
Probabilistic rounding error analysis can yield much sharper bounds than classical worst-case theory, but existing results typically rely on zero-mean rounding errors and often leave the confidence parameter implicit. This work revisits probabilistic rounding error analysis in a moment-aware setting. We first derive a confidence-calibrated reformulation of the Higham and Mary [ 13 ] bound that makes its confidence parameter explicit. We then introduce a variance-informed probabilistic backward error bound based on the first two moments of log ⁡ ( 1 + δ ) \log(1+\delta) , where δ \delta is the relative rounding error. This allows the analysis to accommodate biased rounding error models rather than relying on a zero-mean assumption. To illustrate this framework, we study both a uniform model and a log-space Beta \operatorname{Beta} model for rounding errors, the latter of which provides a simple way to represent bias. This perspective shows that the growth of probabilistic rounding error bounds is not universal: near-zero-mean regimes recover n \sqrt{n} -like behavior, while biased models can exhibit faster accumulation. CUDA experiments in single and half precision on dot products, sparse matrix-vector products, and a stochastic boundary-value problem show that the proposed framework is especially useful in low-precision regimes where deterministic bounds are overly conservative and where bias-aware modeling better matches observed error growth.

Introduction

Modern computer architectures increasingly support low- or mixed-precision arithmetic to reduce computational complexity, memory access time, and energy footprint.
This has enabled the use of low-precision floating-point formats in a wide-range of applications such as edge computing
[
1
,
29
]
, deep learning
[
9
,
34
,
18
,
31
]
, climate modeling
[
11
,
28
,
23
]
, fluid dynamics
[
25
,
30
,
21
]
, and natural sciences
[
24
]
.
Despite the computational advantages, operating in a low-precision format introduces significant rounding errors that can accumulate across successive computations and ultimately degrade accuracy.
This trade-off between efficiency and reliability makes it essential to quantify the effects of rounding errors alongside other numerical errors and statistical uncertainties (e.g., discretization error, parametric uncertainty, and sampling uncertainty).

Rigorously characterizing rounding error accumulation and deriving reliable error bounds are essential for designing numerical algorithms and statistical models that achieve computational efficiency while retaining provable reliability
.

Traditional rounding error analysis adopts a deterministic worst-case model
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
26
,
22
]
, leading to bounds expressed in terms of the operation-count-dependent constant
\gamma_{n}(\mathrm{u})\triangleq n\mathrm{u}/(1-n\mathrm{u})
, where
\mathrm{u}
is the unit roundoff and
n
denotes the number of arithmetic operations with a floating-point number.
Such estimates require
n\mathrm{u}<1
and neglect cancellation effects, making them overly pessimistic when performing a large number of arithmetic operations using low-precision arithmetic
[
17
]
.
For example, even for simple kernels such as the sequential dot product computed in IEEE half precision, deterministic bounds can overestimate the accumulated rounding errors by several orders of magnitude
[
13
]
.

To overcome the limitations of deterministic analysis, several works model rounding errors as random variables to capture cancellation effects during successive operations
[
33
,
12
,
13
,
20
,
4
]
.
In this probabilistic framework, rounding error accumulation is interpreted as uncertainty induced by floating-point arithmetic.
A key heuristic emerging from this viewpoint is that operation-count-dependent constants can often be replaced by their square roots, leading to substantially sharper bounds for algorithms such as dot products, matrix multiplication, and dense linear system solves.
Higham and Mary
[
13
]
rigorously justified this rule of thumb by modeling rounding errors as independent, zero-mean random variables and applying Hoeffding’s concentration inequality, thereby replacing the classical operation-count-dependent constant
\gamma_{n}
with a probabilistic counterpart
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
exhibiting
\sqrt{n}
growth.
Ipsen and Zhou
[
20
]
obtained comparable results under the weaker assumption of mean-independence, again assuming zero-mean rounding errors and employing the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality.
Connolly et al.
[
3
]
further extended this framework to stochastic rounding.
Higham and Mary
[
14
]
retained the same assumptions on the rounding errors as
[
20
]
, but additionally assumed independence of the numerical data, enabling sharper bounds when the data distribution has near-zero mean.
Similarly,
[
10
]
analyzed summation over general computational trees and showed that, for tree reductions, sharper bounds can be derived that depend on the tree height instead of the number of inputs for summation.
Collectively, these studies demonstrate that statistically modeling rounding errors yields significantly tighter estimates of the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic.
However, existing approaches primarily exploit first-moment information through Hoeffding-type concentration inequalities and typically rely on zero-mean assumptions for rounding errors.
As observed in
[
13
]
, such assumptions may not hold in practice, limiting the applicability of these probabilistic bounds.

In this work, we develop a general probabilistic rounding error analysis that leverages the
exact first and second moments of the rounding error random variable and applies to arbitrary parameterizations defined in log-space
.
Our work is closely related to the study by El et al.
[
7
]
, which exploits the variance of rounding errors to derive estimates of the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in stochastic rounding.
However, unlike
[
7
]
, our analysis exploits the full characterization of the rounding error random variable rather than relying on bounds on its variance.
Moreover, we do not impose an explicit zero-mean assumption on the rounding errors, and therefore, our framework can accommodate systematic bias via explicit rounding error random variable models.
In summary, the main contributions are as follows:

1.

Variance-informed probabilistic rounding error analysis.

We introduce a new operation-count-dependent constant
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
that incorporates both the first and second moments of the rounding error random variable, enabling sharper and more flexible quantification of floating-point uncertainty beyond zero-mean assumptions.

2.

Explicit and confidence-calibrated probabilistic bounds.

We derive a corollary of Theorem 2.4 of Higham and Mary
[
13
]
that rigorously recovers the
\sqrt{n}
growth in
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
and provides a closed-form expression for the confidence parameter
\lambda
. Unlike prior formulations where
\lambda
appears as an arbitrary constant, we express it explicitly in terms of the unit roundoff and required confidence, recovering the scaling
\lambda\propto(1-\mathrm{u})^{-1}
consistent with the empirical findings of Connolly et al.
[
3
]

3.

Moment-driven control of accumulation growth.

We show that the growth of the operation-count-dependent constant in a probabilistic rounding error analysis stems not merely from stochastic assumptions, but also from how the rounding error distribution is characterized. By modeling bias directly in the log-domain, we demonstrate that the growth of
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
can be systematically controlled.

4.

GPU-scale numerical validation in low precision.

We validate the proposed bounds with
CUDA
experiments using single precision (
float
) and half precision (
__half
) data types on (i) dot products, (ii) sparse matrix-vector multiplication using matrices from the
SuiteSparse

[
6
]
collection, and (iii) a stochastic ODE where floating-point uncertainty interacts with discretization error and uncertainties from sampling and parameters.

Background

Performing arithmetic operations
op\in\{+,-,\times,/\}
in floating-point arithmetic introduces rounding errors due to the finite precision of the representation.
For IEEE-754-compliant arithmetic, and in the absence of overflow or underflow, the result of a floating-point arithmetic operation can be modeled as

\displaystyle\mathrm{fl}(x\;op\;y)=(x\;op\;y)(1+\delta)^{\rho},\quad|\delta|\leq\mathrm{u},\quad\rho=\pm 1,

(1)

where
\mathrm{fl}(\cdot)
denotes floating-point evaluation and
\mathrm{u}\triangleq\tfrac{1}{2}r^{1-p}

[
19
,
17
]
.
Consequently, evaluating a function
\mathbf{{y}}=f(\mathbf{{x}})
with
\mathbf{{x}}\in\mathbb{F}
yields a numerical approximation
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}=\hat{f}(\mathbf{{x}})
, where
\hat{f}
denotes the function perturbed by rounding errors introduced at each arithmetic operation.
The accumulation of these errors can be analyzed using either forward or backward error analysis
[
17
, Chapter 1]
.
Forward error analysis bounds the deviation between the exact result
\mathbf{{y}}
and the computed result
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
, whereas backward error analysis seeks the smallest perturbation in the input that explains the computed result.
Specifically, the backward error
\varepsilon_{\text{bwd}}
is the solution of the minimization problem

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{bwd}}\triangleq\min\left\{\varepsilon\geq 0:\hat{f}(\mathbf{{x}})=f(\mathbf{{x}}+\Delta\mathbf{{x}}),\;\frac{|\Delta\mathbf{{x}}|}{|\mathbf{{x}}|}\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(2)

In this work, we focus on backward error analysis because it provides a natural framework to compare uncertainty in the input
\mathbf{{x}}
with errors induced by
floating-point arithmetic and subsequently derive forward error bounds.

In performing backward error analysis using
eq.

1
, the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\delta_{i})^{\rho_{i}}
with
|\delta_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}
and
\rho_{i}=\pm 1
often arises when performing
n
arithmetic operations with a floating-point number
[
17
, Chapter 3]
.
To simplify these product terms and obtain a bound for the backward error, the traditional deterministic rounding error analysis (called
drea
, here) assumes the worst-case scenario that only utilizes the bounds for
\delta_{i}
, as stated in the following lemma
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 1
(Deterministic rounding error analysis).

If
|\delta_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}
and
\rho_{i}=\pm 1
for all
i
, and
n\mathrm{u}<1
, then

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\delta_{i})^{\rho_{i}}=1+\theta_{n},\qquad|\theta_{n}|\leq\gamma_{n}(\mathrm{u})\triangleq\frac{n\mathrm{u}}{1-n\mathrm{u}}.

However, such a deterministic approach yields pessimistic estimates of the accumulated error because it cannot account for the cancellation of rounding errors during computation
[
13
,
5
]
.
Further, for
n\mathrm{u}<1
, the constant
\gamma_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n\mathrm{u})
increases linearly with the number of arithmetic operations
n
and can lead to significant overestimation of the accumulated error when using low-precision arithmetic.

2.1
Probabilistic Modeling of Rounding Errors

To account for cancellation of rounding errors during computation, the rounding error can be modeled as a bounded random variable
\delta:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
\Omega
denotes the sample space.

Under this statistical description, quantifying the accumulation of rounding errors can be interpreted as estimating the uncertainty induced by floating-point arithmetic.

We emphasize that this differs from stochastic rounding, where the rounding direction itself is random
[
5
]
.
Higham and Mary
[
13
, Theorem 2.4]
established the first rigorous result for probabilistic rounding error analysis by modeling rounding errors as independent random variables with zero mean.
They showed that
\gamma_{n}
in
lemma

1
can be replaced by

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{n}(\mathrm{u};\lambda)\triangleq e^{\lambda\sqrt{n}\mathrm{u}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}}}-1,\quad\forall\mathrm{u}<1,\ \lambda>0,

(3)

such that
|\theta_{n}|\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{n}(\mathrm{u};\lambda)
holds with probability at least

\mathpzc{p}(\mathrm{u};\lambda)=1-2e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\mathrm{u})^{2}}{2}}
.
Here
\lambda
is a hyperparameter that controls the confidence level of the bound.
We refer to this result as
Mean-informed Probabilistic Rounding Error Analysis
(
mprea
).

Unlike deterministic analysis, the probabilistic formulation remains valid for any number of operations
n
.
However, it introduces additional modeling assumptions.
In particular, Higham and Mary introduce the
\sqrt{n}
scaling in
eq.

3
to make the associated probability measure independent of
n
, an assumption that is not intrinsic to floating-point arithmetic itself but imposed for analytical tractability.
Moreover, the bound depends only on the first moment of the rounding error distribution due to the use of Hoeffding’s concentration inequality
[
2
]
.
Finally, the analysis assumes that rounding errors have zero mean, an assumption that may not hold in practice
[
13
]
.

Lemma 2
(Hoeffding’s Inequality).

Let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be
n
independent random variables with
|Z_{i}|\leq c_{i}
for all
i
. Then, the random variable
S_{n}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
satisfies

\mathpzc{p}(|S_{n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]|\geq t)\leq 2e^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}},

for all positive
t
.

3
Methodology

In this section, we first present a corollary of the probabilistic rounding error analysis of Higham and Mary
[
13
]
that (a) rigorously establishes the
\sqrt{n}
dependence in
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
and (b) improves the interpretability of
mprea
by deriving a closed-form expression for
\lambda
in
eq.

3
in terms of the desired confidence level.
We then introduce the central contribution of this work,
Variance-informed Probabilistic Rounding Error Analysis
(
vprea
), which leverages both the first and second moments of the rounding error random variable to quantify uncertainty induced by floating-point arithmetic.
We show that
vprea
applies to any independent and identically distributed rounding error random variable, provided closed-form expressions for the first two moments of
\log(1+\delta)
are available.

3.1
An Interpretable Mean-informed Probabilistic Rounding Error Analysis

We now present a corollary of Theorem
2.4
in
[
13
]
that introduces an exact functional form of
\lambda
and the rounding error bounds in terms of the required confidence.
We show that
\lambda
depends on the unit roundoff and derive the
\lambda\propto(1-\mathrm{u})^{-1}
, as empirically found by Connolly et al.
[
3
]
.
The goal is to improve the interpretability of probabilistic rounding error bounds by eliminating unnecessary modeling assumptions.

Corollary 3
(Mean-informed probabilistic rounding error analysis).

Let
\{\delta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be
n
independent random variables with zero mean with
|\delta_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}<1
for all
i
, where
\mathrm{u}
is the unit roundoff.
Then, for
\rho_{i}=\pm 1
, the relation

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\delta_{i})^{\rho_{i}}=1+\theta_{n},\qquad|\theta_{n}|\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{n}(\mathrm{u};\zeta)\triangleq e^{t+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}}}-1,

holds with confidence
\zeta\in[0,1)
, where
t\triangleq\frac{\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}\sqrt{-2n\log\Big(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\Big)}
.

Proof.

Consider the random variable
\log\phi\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}\log(1+\delta_{i})
that is the sum of
n
independent random variables
\rho_{i}\log(1+\delta_{i})
.
Then, for
|\delta_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}<1
for all
i
, we can use the Taylor series expansion
\log(1+\delta_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}\delta_{i}^{k}}{k}
to obtain the bounds

\displaystyle\delta_{i}-\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{1-|\delta_{i}|}\leq\log(1+\delta_{i})\leq\delta_{i}+\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{1-|\delta_{i}|}.

Therefore,
|\rho_{i}\log(1+\delta_{i})|=|\log(1+\delta_{i})|\leq\mathrm{u}+\frac{\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}}=\frac{\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}]=0
, we have
|\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\delta_{i})]|\leq\mathbb{E}[|\log(1+\delta_{i})|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|\delta_{i}+\frac{\delta_{i}^{2}}{1-|\delta_{i}|}|]\leq\frac{\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}}
.
Now, we can use
lemma

2
with
Z_{i}=\rho_{i}\log(1+\delta_{i})
and
c_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\geq t)\leq 2e^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2nc^{2}}},

\displaystyle\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\leq t)\geq 1-2e^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2nc^{2}}},

where
c\triangleq\frac{\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}
.
Let
|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\leq t
hold with confidence
\zeta\in[0,1)
.
For a confidence
\zeta
, we can now obtain the value of the distance parameter
t
by solving the quadratic

\displaystyle t^{2}+2nc^{2}\log\Big(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\Big)=0.

The roots of this quadratic are given as

\displaystyle t_{-}\triangleq-c\sqrt{2n\log\Big(\frac{2}{1-\zeta}\Big)},\qquad t_{+}\triangleq c\sqrt{2n\log\Big(\frac{2}{1-\zeta}\Big)}.

Since
t_{-}<0
for all
n>0
,
\mathrm{u}<1
, and
\zeta\in(0,1]
, the inequality
|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\leq t_{-}
will never hold true.
Similarly,
t_{+}>0
for all
n>0
,
\mathrm{u}<1
, and
\zeta\in(0,1]
, such that
|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\leq t_{+}
holds with confidence at least
\zeta
.

Now, we can invoke the inequality
|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\geq|\log\phi|-|\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\geq|\log\phi|-\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi|-\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}}\leq t_{+})\geq\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\leq t_{+})\geq\zeta,

such that
\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi|\leq t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}})\geq\zeta
.
Thus,
\log\phi\in[-(t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}}),(t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}})]
with confidence at least
\zeta
, which gives

\displaystyle\phi-1\in[e^{-(t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}})}-1,e^{(t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}})}-1].

Therefore, we can obtain
|\phi-1|\leq\max\{|e^{-(t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}})}-1|,|e^{(t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}})}-1|\}=e^{(t_{+}+\frac{n\mathrm{u}^{2}}{1-\mathrm{u}})}-1=\tilde{\gamma}_{n}(\mathrm{u};\zeta)
,
that holds with confidence at least
\zeta
.

Note that the probabilistic analysis of Higham and Mary assumes the specific functional form
t=\lambda\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{u}
in order to eliminate the explicit dependence of the probability measure on
n
.
More generally, for any scalar-valued function
g(\mathrm{u};\lambda)
, setting
t=\sqrt{n}\,g(\mathrm{u};\lambda)
would preserve this independence and leave the analysis formally unchanged.
Consequently, the particular choice of functional form is not uniquely determined by the theory and is instead a modeling assumption.
On the contrary, we show that the functional form of
t
can be obtained rigorously by defining a confidence parameter
\zeta\in[0,1)
with which the rounding error bounds are satisfied.
Comparing the expression of
t
in
corollary

3
with
\lambda\sqrt{n}\mathrm{u}
, we can identify an exact functional form
\lambda(\mathrm{u};\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{u}}\sqrt{2\log\Big(\frac{2}{1-\zeta}\Big)}
,
thus rigorously obtaining the
\lambda\propto(1-\mathrm{u})^{-1}
dependence as empirically found by Connolly et al.
[
3
]
.

3.2
Variance-informed Probabilistic Rounding Error Analysis

We now introduce a new approach to probabilistic rounding error analysis that uses the first and second moments of the rounding error random variable.
To do so, we leverage Bernstein’s concentration inequality
[
2
]
as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 4
(Bernstein’s Inequality).

Let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be
n
independent random variables such that
|Z_{i}|\leq c
for all
i
.
Then, the random variable
S_{n}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathpzc{p}(|S_{n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]|\geq t)\leq 2\exp\Big(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+\frac{ct}{3})}\Big),

for all positive
t
, where
\sigma^{2}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[Z_{i}]
.

Theorem 5
(Variance-informed probabilistic rounding error analysis).

Let
\{\delta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be
n
independent and identically distributed random variables with
|\delta_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}<1
for all
i
, where
\mathrm{u}
is the unit roundoff.
Then, for
\rho_{i}=\pm 1
, the relation

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\delta_{i})^{\rho_{i}}=1+\theta_{n},\qquad|\theta_{n}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}(\mathrm{u};\zeta)\triangleq e^{t+n|\hat{\mu}|}-1,

holds with confidence
\zeta\in[0,1)
, where

\displaystyle t\triangleq\frac{1}{3}\left(c\log\left(\frac{2}{1-\zeta}\right)+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}\log\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\right)\right)^{2}-18n\log\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\right)\hat{\sigma}^{2}}\right),

with
\hat{\mu}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\delta_{i})]
,
\hat{\sigma}^{2}\triangleq\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\log(1+\delta_{i})]
, and
|\log(1+\delta_{i})|\leq c
for all
i
.

Proof.

Consider the random variable
\log\phi\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}\log(1+\delta_{i})
that is the sum of
n
independent and identically distributed random variables
\rho_{i}\log(1+\delta_{i})
.
We can then apply
lemma

4
with
Z_{i}=\rho_{i}\log(1+\delta_{i})
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathpzc{p}(|\log(\phi)-\mathbb{E}[\log(\phi)]|\geq t)\leq 2\exp\Big(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(n\hat{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{ct}{3})}\Big),

\displaystyle\mathpzc{p}(|\log(\phi)-\mathbb{E}[\log(\phi)]|\leq t)\geq 1-2\exp\Big(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(n\hat{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{ct}{3})}\Big),

where
\sigma^{2}=n\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\log(1+\delta_{i})]
for independent and identically distributed random variables, and
c
denotes the bound on
|\log(1+\delta_{i})|
for all
i
.
Let
|\log(\phi)-\mathbb{E}[\log(\phi)]|\leq t
hold with confidence
\zeta\in[0,1)
.
For such confidence
\zeta
, we can now obtain the value of the distance parameter
t
by solving the quadratic

\displaystyle t^{2}+\frac{2ct}{3}\log\Big(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\Big)+2n\hat{\sigma}^{2}\log\Big(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\Big)=0.

The roots of this quadratic are

\displaystyle t_{\pm}=\frac{1}{3}\left(-c\log\!\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\right)\pm\sqrt{\left(c\log\!\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\right)\right)^{2}-18n\log\!\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{2}\right)\hat{\sigma}^{2}}\right),

where
t_{+}
and
t_{-}
correspond to the positive and negative branches, respectively.
For positive
\hat{\sigma},n,
and
\mathrm{u}
, both
t_{-}
and
t_{+}
are real-valued and distinct for all
\zeta\in[0,1)
.
Under such a condition for
\hat{\sigma},n,
and
\mathrm{u}
, we will always have a negative root
t_{-}
and a positive root
t_{+}
for all
\zeta\in[0,1)
.
Thus, the inequality
|\log(\phi)-\mathbb{E}[\log(\phi)]|\leq t_{-}
will never hold true.
Therefore, using the positive root
t_{+}
, the inequality
|\log(\phi)-\mathbb{E}[\log(\phi)]|\leq t_{+}
holds with confidence at least
\zeta
.

Now, we can invoke the inequality
|\log(\phi)-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\geq|\log\phi|-|\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\geq|\log\phi|-n|\hat{\mu}|
, where
\hat{\mu}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\delta_{i})]
, to obtain

\displaystyle\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi|-n|\hat{\mu}|\leq t_{+})\geq\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi-\mathbb{E}[\log\phi]|\leq t_{+})\geq\zeta,

such that
\mathpzc{p}(|\log\phi|\leq t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|)\geq\zeta
.
Thus,
\log\phi\in[-(t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|),(t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|)]
with confidence at least
\zeta
, which gives

\displaystyle\phi-1\in[e^{-(t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|)}-1,e^{(t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|)}-1].

Therefore, we can obtain
|\phi-1|\leq\max\{|e^{-(t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|)}-1|,|e^{(t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|)}-1|\}=e^{(t_{+}+n|\hat{\mu}|)}-1=\hat{\gamma}_{n}(\mathrm{u};\zeta)
, that holds with confidence at least
\zeta
.

Note that
\hat{\gamma}_{n+1}>\hat{\gamma}_{n}
for all positive
n
, that is, the rounding error bounds monotonically increase with the number of arithmetic operations.

Theorem

5
presents a general framework for quantifying uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic when the statistics of the rounding error random variables are known and finite.
It generalizes
corollary

3
by utilizing both first and second moments of the rounding error random variable, with the additional assumption that the rounding errors are identically distributed.
Furthermore, the proposed approach does not require an explicit assumption about the moments of the rounding error random variable, unlike
corollary

3
, which requires
\mathbb{E}[\delta]=0
.

3.2.1
Modeling rounding error random variable

To quantify the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic using
theorem

5
, we introduce two models for the rounding error random variable
\delta
.
The motivation of these models is (a) to remain agnostic to the underlying floating-point operation, (b) to obtain closed-form moments required in
theorem

5
, and (c) to account for potential bias in rounding errors.

First, we consider an uninformative uniform distribution as outlined in
definition

6
.

Definition 6
(
\mathcal{U}
-model).

The rounding errors due to finite-precision floating point arithmetic is modeled as an independent and identically distributed random variable with an uninformative uniform distribution
\delta\sim\mathcal{U}(-\mathrm{u},\mathrm{u})
.

Under
definition

6
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2u+(-1+u)\log(1-u)+(1+u)\log(1+u)}{2u},

(4a)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4u^{2}+\kappa[\log^{2}(1-u)-2\log(1-u)\log(1+u)+\log^{2}(1+u)]}{4u^{2}},

(4b)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\log(1+\mathrm{u}),

(4c)

as defined in
theorem

5
, where
\kappa\triangleq(-1+\mathrm{u}^{2})
.
An obvious limitation of such a model is that it may not accurately quantify uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic when rounding errors are biased, that is,
\mathbb{E}[\delta]\neq 0
.
This arises because
definition

6
implicitly enforces
\mathbb{E}[\delta]=0
, thereby embedding a zero-mean assumption on the rounding error, similar to
corollary

3
, where this requirement is imposed explicitly.
However, such an assumption, implicit or explicit, may not always hold true in practical computations.
For example, when adding a very large positive number with a very small positive number, the rounding errors tend to be negatively biased, as shown in
fig.

1
.

To account for the potential bias in rounding errors distribution, we also consider a
\operatorname{Beta}
distribution to model
\log(1+\delta)
random variable, as outlined in
definition

7
.

Definition 7
(
\beta
-model).

Let
\delta
denote the rounding error arising from finite-precision floating-point arithmetic, and define the random variable
Y=\log(1+\delta)
.
We model
Y
as an independent and identically distributed random variable of the form

\displaystyle Y=\log(1-\mathrm{u})+\log\!\left(\frac{1+\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}\right)\,Z,\qquad Z\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),

where
\mathrm{u}
denotes the unit roundoff, and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are the shape parameters of the
\operatorname{Beta}
distribution.

Under
definition

7
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}

\displaystyle=\log(1-\mathrm{u})+\log\Big(\frac{1+\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}\Big)\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},

(5a)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\log\Big(\frac{1+\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}\Big)\Big)^{2}\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)},

(5b)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\log(1+\mathrm{u}),

(5c)

as defined in
theorem

5
.
Using
definition

7
, we can bias rounding errors by appropriately selecting the shape parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
, as formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 8
.

Let
Z\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
and define

Y=\log(1-\mathrm{u})+\log(\ell)Z
, where

\ell\triangleq\frac{1+\mathrm{u}}{1-\mathrm{u}}>1
, and

h(\mathrm{u})\triangleq-\log(1-\mathrm{u})/\log(\ell)
.
Then the expectation of the rounding error random variable
\delta
is strictly positive for all
\alpha>\frac{h(\mathrm{u})}{1-h(\mathrm{u})}\,\beta
and is strictly negative for all
\alpha<\frac{h(\mathrm{u})}{1-h(\mathrm{u})}\,\beta
.

Proof.

Using
definition

7
, we have
\delta=e^{Y}-1
that is convex function.
Thus, using Jensen’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{E}[\delta]\geq e^{\mathbb{E}[Y]}-1
.
We can now obtain the condition for
\mathbb{E}[\delta]>0
by requiring
\mathbb{E}[Y]>0
.
Using
eq.

5
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\delta]>0
when
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}>h(\mathrm{u})
, which gives the condition
\alpha>\frac{h(\mathrm{u})}{1-h(\mathrm{u})}\,\beta
.
Now, we can obtain the condition for making
\mathbb{E}[\delta]<0
.
Using the definition of
Y
, we can obtain
\delta=(1-\mathrm{u})\ell^{Z}-1
.
To obtain a strictly negative expectation, we require
(1-\mathrm{u})\mathbb{E}[\ell^{Z}]<1
.
Note, for
\ell>1
and
Z\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
,
\ell^{Z}
is a convex function with
\mathbb{E}[\ell^{Z}]\geq\ell^{\mathbb{E}[Z]}
via Jenson’s inequality.
Thus, we can obtain the condition of making
\mathbb{E}[\delta]<0
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z]\log(\ell)\leq\log(\mathbb{E}[\ell^{Z}])<-\log(1-\mathrm{u}),

which gives the condition
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}<h(\mathrm{u})
, or equivalently,
\alpha<\frac{h(\mathrm{u})}{1-h(\mathrm{u})}\,\beta
.

4
Uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in numerical linear algebra kernels

Using
theorem

5
, we now derive statistical bounds to quantify uncertainty arising from floating-point arithmetic in three fundamental kernels: (a) dot products, (b) matrix-vector products, and (c) the Thomas algorithm for solving tridiagonal linear systems.
These operations form the computational backbone of many scientific computing tasks, including matrix factorizations and linear systems arising from numerical discretizations.
In particular, the Thomas algorithm is widely used for banded systems encountered in fluid dynamics, structural dynamics, and control applications.
Reliable estimates of the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic enable the safe use of low-precision arithmetic in large-scale simulations and support the development of low-computational-fidelity solvers within multi-fidelity statistical frameworks.
Since practical interest lies in the accumulated rounding error incurred by representing the statistical model in floating-point arithmetic, we assume identical precision for inputs and computations.
For probabilistic rounding error analysis, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(n;\zeta)\triangleq 1-n(1-\zeta),

which follows from the inclusion-exclusion principle and yields a lower bound on the probability that all
n
bounds hold simultaneously, provided each bound holds with probability at least
\zeta\in[0,1)
.

4.1
Dot product

In the following, we present the statistical bounds for the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic when performing a dot product of two vectors using
theorem

5
.

Theorem 9
(Dot product).

Let
y=\mathbf{{a}}^{T}\mathbf{{b}}
, where
\mathbf{{a}},\mathbf{{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then, the computed dot product
\hat{y}
satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{y}=(\mathbf{a}+\Delta\mathbf{a})^{T}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}^{T}(\mathbf{b}+\Delta\mathbf{b}),

where
|\Delta\mathbf{a}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}|\mathbf{{a}}|
and
|\Delta\mathbf{b}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}|\mathbf{{b}}|
hold true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(n;\zeta)
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
y=s_{n}
, where
s_{i}=s_{i-1}+\mathbf{{a}}_{i}\mathbf{{b}}_{i}
is a recursive relation with
s_{0}=0
.
Here
\mathbf{{a}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{{b}}_{i}
are the
i
-th elements of
\mathbf{{a}}
and
\mathbf{{b}}
, respectively.
Then, the computed dot product
\hat{y}
can be represented as

\displaystyle\hat{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{{a}}_{i}\mathbf{{b}}_{i}(1+\eta_{i})\prod_{j=\max\{2,i\}}^{n}(1+\xi_{j}),

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{{a}}_{i}\mathbf{{b}}_{i}(1+\theta_{n-\max\{2,i\}+2}),

where
\eta_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
are the rounding errors due to multiplication and addition, respectively, such that
|\eta_{i}|,|\xi_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}
.
We can invoke
theorem

5
to write the computed dot product as using perturbed vectors as

\displaystyle\hat{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{{a}}_{i}+\Delta\mathbf{{a}}_{i})\mathbf{{b}}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{{a}}_{i}(\mathbf{{b}}_{i}+\Delta\mathbf{{b}}_{i}),

where
\Delta\mathbf{{a}}_{i}\triangleq\mathbf{{a}}_{i}\theta_{n-\max\{2,i\}+2}
and
\Delta\mathbf{{b}}_{i}\triangleq\mathbf{{b}}_{i}\theta_{n-\max\{2,i\}+2}
, such that,

\displaystyle|\Delta\mathbf{{a}}_{i}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n-\max\{2,i\}+2}|\mathbf{{a}}_{i}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}|\mathbf{{a}}_{i}|,

\displaystyle|\Delta\mathbf{{b}}_{i}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n-\max\{2,i\}+2}|\mathbf{{b}}_{i}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}|\mathbf{{b}}_{i}|,

is satisfied with a probability of at least
\zeta
and will fail with at most
1-\zeta
.
Thus, for all
i
, the bounds will hold with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(n;\zeta)
using the principles of inclusion and exclusion of probabilities.

Using
theorem

9
, we can obtain the estimate for uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in computing the dot product as

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{y}-y|}{|y|}\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}\frac{|\mathbf{{a}}^{T}||\mathbf{{b}}|}{|\mathbf{{a}}^{T}\mathbf{{b}}|},

(6)

that holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(n;\zeta)
.

4.2
Matrix-vector product

Using
theorem

9
, we can now derive the statistical bounds for the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in computing matrix-vector products.

Theorem 10
(Matrix-vector product).

Let
\mathbf{{y}}=\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{x}}
, where
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\mathbf{{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Then, the computed matrix-vector product
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}=(\mathbf{{A}}+\Delta\mathbf{{A}})\mathbf{{x}},

where
|\Delta\mathbf{{A}}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}|\mathbf{{A}}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(mn;\zeta)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{{y}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{{a}}_{i}
denote the
i
-th element of
\mathbf{{y}}
and the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{{A}}
, respectively.
Then, we can write
\mathbf{{y}}_{i}=\mathbf{{a}}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{{x}}
for all
i
.
Using
theorem

9
, we can now write the
i
-th element of the computed matrix-vector product
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{{a}}_{i}+\Delta\mathbf{{a}}_{i})^{T}\mathbf{{x}},

where
|\Delta\mathbf{{a}}_{i}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}|\mathbf{{a}}_{i}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(n;\zeta)
.
Note that, to compute all the elements of
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
, we need to perform
m
dot products, each of size
n
.
Thus, for all
i
, the bounds will hold true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(mn;\zeta)
using the principles of inclusion and exclusion of probabilities.

Using
theorem

10
, we can obtain the estimate for uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in computing matrix-vector products as

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}-\mathbf{{y}}|}{|\mathbf{{y}}|}\leq\hat{\gamma}_{n}\frac{|\mathbf{{A}}||\mathbf{{x}}|}{|\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{x}}|},

(7)

that holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(mn;\zeta)
.
We can similarly derive the statistical bounds for matrix-matrix products using
theorem

10
by considering each column of the resulting matrix as a matrix-vector product.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathbf{{C}}=\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{B}}
, where
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times t},\mathbf{{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times n}
and
\mathbf{{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
Then, the computed matrix-matrix product
\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}-\mathbf{{C}}|}{|\mathbf{{C}}|}\leq\hat{\gamma}_{t}\frac{|\mathbf{{A}}||\mathbf{{B}}|}{|\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{B}}|},

that holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(mnt;\zeta)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{{c}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{{b}}_{i}
denote the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{{C}}
and
\mathbf{{B}}
, respectively.
Then, we can write
\mathbf{{c}}_{i}=\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{b}}_{i}
for all
i
.
Using
theorem

10
, we can now write the
i
-th column of the computed matrix-matrix product
\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{c}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{{A}}+\Delta\mathbf{{A}})\mathbf{{b}}_{i},

where
|\Delta\mathbf{{A}}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{t}|\mathbf{{A}}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(mt;\zeta)
.
Note that, to compute all the columns of
\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}
we need to perform
n
matrix-vector products.
Thus, for all
i
, the bound
|\hat{\mathbf{{c}}}_{i}-\mathbf{{c}}_{i}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{t}|\mathbf{{A}}||\mathbf{{b}}_{i}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(mnt;\zeta)
using the principles of inclusion and exclusion of probabilities.

4.3
Thomas algorithm for solving a tri-diagonal linear system

Thomas’ algorithm is a specialized method for solving a linear system
\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{x}}=\mathbf{{b}}
via LU-factorization when the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is tridiagonal
[
32
]
.
Broadly, it consists of three sequential steps, namely (a) LU-factorization, (b) forward substitution, and (c) backward substitution.
As a result, to quantify the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in the Thomas algorithm, we must first individually quantify the uncertainty accumulated at each of the outlined steps.

LU-factorization

Consider the tridiagonal linear system
\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{x}}=\mathbf{{B}}
, where
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has subdiagonal, diagonal, and superdiagonal entries denoted by
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
, and
\nu_{i}
at the
i
-th row, respectively.
Applying Doolittle’s method
[
17
]
, the matrix
\mathbf{{A}}
admits an LU-actorization
\mathbf{{A}}=\mathbf{{L}}\mathbf{{U}}
, where
\mathbf{{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is unit lower triangular and
\mathbf{{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is upper triangular.
The nonzero entries of
\mathbf{{L}}
and
\mathbf{{U}}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{{L}}_{i,i-1}

\displaystyle\triangleq l_{i}=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\mathbf{{U}}_{i-1,i-1}},\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{U}}_{i,i}

\displaystyle\triangleq u_{i}=\beta_{i}-\mathbf{{L}}_{i,i-1}\nu_{i-1},\quad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n,

(8b)

with
\mathbf{{L}}_{i,i}=1
for all
i
,
\mathbf{{U}}_{i,i+1}=\nu_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
, and
\mathbf{{U}}_{1,1}=\beta_{1}
.
In the following lemma, we present the statistical bounds for the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in the LU-factorization of a tridiagonal matrix via Doolittle’s method.

Lemma 12
(LU-factorization of a tri-diagonal system).

Let
\mathbf{{A}}=\mathbf{{LU}}
be the
LU
-factorization of a tri-diagonal matrix
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
that has
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
, and
\nu_{i}
as its subdiagonal, diagonal, and superdiagonal entries at the
i
-th row, respectively.
Then, the computed factorization
\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
via Doolittle’s method satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}=\mathbf{{A}}+\Delta\mathbf{{A}},

where
|\Delta\mathbf{A}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{1}\hat{|\mathbf{L}|}\hat{|\mathbf{U}|}
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(3(n-1);\zeta)
.

Proof.

Since the arithmetic operations involved in computing the LU-factorization of a tri-diagonal matrix are given by
eq.

8
, the computed nonzero entries
\hat{l}_{i}
and
\hat{u}_{i}
are given as

\displaystyle\hat{l}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\hat{u}_{i-1}(1+\eta_{i})},\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\beta_{i}-\hat{l}_{i}\nu_{i-1}(1+\psi_{i})\right)}{(1+\xi_{i})},\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

where
\eta_{i}
and
\psi_{i}
denote the rounding errors arising from multiplications, and
\xi_{i}
denotes the rounding error arising from additions.
Here,
|\eta_{i}|,|\psi_{i}|,|\xi_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}
.
We can now invoke
theorem

5
to express the computed entries
\hat{l}_{i}
and
\hat{u}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\hat{l}_{i}\hat{u}_{i-1}+\theta_{1}\hat{l}_{i}u_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i},\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{i}+\hat{l}_{i}\nu_{i-1}+\theta_{1}^{\prime}\hat{u}_{i}+\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\hat{l}_{i}\nu_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{i},\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

where
|\theta|_{1},|\theta_{1}^{\prime}|,|\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{1}
hold true with a probability of at least
\zeta
.
Thus, for the
i
-th row of the computed LU-factorization, all the bounds are satisfied with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(3;\zeta)
using the principles of inclusion and exclusion of probabilities.
Since we have
n-1
such computations, all the bounds will hold true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(3(n-1);\zeta)
.

Forward substitution

Once the LU-factorization of the tridiagonal matrix
\mathbf{{A}}
is computed, we can perform forward substitution to solve the system
\mathbf{{L}}\mathbf{{y}}=\mathbf{{b}}
.
In the following lemma, we present statistical bounds for the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic during forward substitution in the Thomas algorithm.

Lemma 13
(Forward substitution in Thomas algorithm).

Let
\mathbf{{L}}\mathbf{{y}}=\mathbf{{b}}
be a tri-diagonal system where
\mathbf{{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the lower-triangular matrix obtained from the LU-factorization of a tri-diagonal matrix
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Then, the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
via forward substitution satisfies

\displaystyle(\mathbf{{L}}+\Delta\mathbf{{L}})\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}=\mathbf{{b}},

where
|\Delta\mathbf{{L}}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{1}|\mathbf{{L}}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(2(n-1);\zeta)
.

Proof.

Consider the lower-triangular system
\mathbf{{L}}\mathbf{{y}}=\mathbf{{b}}
, where the nonzero entries of
\mathbf{{L}}
are given by
eq.

8a
.
Thus, the solution
\mathbf{{y}}
using forward substitution is given via the recursive relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{{y}}_{i}=\mathbf{{b}}_{i}-l_{i}\mathbf{{y}}_{i-1},\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

where
\mathbf{{y}}_{1}=\mathbf{{b}}_{1}
and
l_{i}
is defined in
eq.

8a
.
Thus, we can obtain the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}_{i}(1+\xi_{i})=\mathbf{{b}}_{i}-\hat{l}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}_{i-1}(1+\eta_{i}),\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

where
\eta_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
are the rounding errors due to multiplication and addition, respectively, such that
|\eta_{i}|,|\xi_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}
.
We can now invoke
theorem

5
to express the computed solution as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}_{i}+l_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}_{i-1}+\theta_{1}\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}_{i}+\theta_{1}^{\prime}l_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}_{i-1}=\mathbf{{b}}_{i},\quad

\displaystyle i=2,\dots,n,

where
|\theta_{1}|,|\theta_{1}^{\prime}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{1}
hold true with a probability of at least
\zeta
.
Thus, for the
i
-th row of the forward substitution, all the bounds are satisfied with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(2;\zeta)
using the principles of inclusion and exclusion of probabilities.
Since we have
n-1
such computations, all the bounds will hold true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(2(n-1);\zeta)
.

Backward substitution

Once the forward substitution is performed, we can perform backward substitution to solve the system
\mathbf{{U}}\mathbf{{x}}=\mathbf{{y}}
to obtain the solution of the tri-diagonal system
\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{x}}=\mathbf{{b}}
.
In the following lemma, we present statistical bounds on the uncertainty arising from floating-point arithmetic during backward substitution in the Thomas algorithm.

Lemma 14
(Backward substitution in Thomas algorithm).

Let
\mathbf{{U}}\mathbf{{x}}=\mathbf{{y}}
be a tri-diagonal system where
\mathbf{{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the upper-triangular matrix obtained from the LU-factorization of a tri-diagonal matrix
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Then, the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}
via backward substitution satisfies

\displaystyle(\mathbf{{U}}+\Delta\mathbf{{U}})\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}=\mathbf{{y}},

where
|\Delta\mathbf{{U}}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{2}|\mathbf{{U}}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(2n-1;\zeta)
.

Proof.

Consider the upper-triangular system
\mathbf{{U}}\mathbf{{x}}=\mathbf{{y}}
, where the nonzero entries of
\mathbf{{U}}
are given by
eq.

8b
.
Thus, the solution
\mathbf{{x}}
using backward substitution is given via the recursive relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{{x}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{{y}}_{i}-\nu_{i}\mathbf{{x}}_{i+1}}{u_{i}},\quad

\displaystyle i=n-1,\dots,1,

where
\mathbf{{x}}_{n}=\frac{y_{n}}{u_{n}}
and
u_{i}
is defined in
eq.

8b
.
Thus, we can obtain the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{n}}{u_{n}(1+\eta_{n})},

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{{y}}_{i}-\nu_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{i+1}(1+\psi_{i})}{u_{i}(1+\xi_{i})(1+\chi_{i})},\qquad i=n-1,\ldots,1.

where
\eta_{i}
,
\psi_{i}
, and
\xi_{i}
denote the rounding errors arising from multiplications, and
\chi_{i}
denotes the rounding error arising from additions.
Here,
|\eta_{i}|,|\psi_{i}|,|\xi_{i}|,|\chi_{i}|\leq\mathrm{u}
.
We can now invoke
theorem

5
to express the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}
as

\displaystyle u_{n}\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{n}+\theta_{1}u_{n}\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{n}=\mathbf{{y}}_{n},

\displaystyle u_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{i}+\nu_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{i+1}+\theta_{2}u_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{i}+\theta_{1}^{\prime}\nu_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}_{i+1}=\mathbf{{y}}_{i},\quad

\displaystyle i=n-1,\dots,1,

where
|\theta_{1}|,|\theta_{1}^{\prime}|,|\theta_{2}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{2}
hold true with a probability of at least
\zeta
.
Thus, for the
n
-th row of the backward substitution, all the bounds are satisfied with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(1;\zeta)
using the principles of inclusion and exclusion of probabilities.
Similarly, for any
i<n
, the bounds are satisfied with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(2;\zeta)
.
Therefore, all the bounds will hold true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(2n-1;\zeta)
.

Thomas algorithm

Using
lemmas

12
,
13
, and
14
, we can now obtain the statistical bounds for the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in solving a tri-diagonal system of equations via the Thomas algorithm.
We present this in the following theorem.

Theorem 15
(Thomas algorithm).

Let
\mathbf{{A}}\mathbf{{x}}=\mathbf{{b}}
be a tri-diagonal system with
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\mathbf{{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Assume that
\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}
are the computed LU-factorization of the matrix
\mathbf{{A}}
via Doolittle’s method.
Then, the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}
satisfies

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}+\Delta\mathbf{A})\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{b},

where
|\Delta\mathbf{A}|\leq(2\hat{\gamma}_{1}+\hat{\gamma}_{2}+\hat{\gamma}_{1}\hat{\gamma}_{2})|\hat{\mathbf{L}}||\hat{\mathbf{U}}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(7n-6;\zeta)
.

Proof.

Consider the computed
LU
-factorization
(\hat{\mathbf{{L}}},\hat{\mathbf{{U}}})
of the tridiagonal matrix
\mathbf{{A}}
satisfying
\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}=\mathbf{{A}}+\Delta\mathbf{{A}}_{1}
, where
|\Delta\mathbf{{A}}_{1}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{1}|\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}||\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}|
holds with probability at least
\mathcal{Q}(3(n-1);\zeta)
, as established in
lemma

12
.
Now, using
lemma

13
, we have the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
of the system
\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\mathbf{{y}}=\mathbf{{b}}
satisfying
(\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}})\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}=\mathbf{{b}}
, where
|\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{1}|\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}|
holds with probability at least
\mathcal{Q}(2(n-1);\zeta)
.
Finally, using
lemma

14
, we have the computed solution
\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}
of the system
\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}\mathbf{{x}}=\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
satisfying
(\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}})\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}=\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}
, where
|\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}|\leq\hat{\gamma}_{2}|\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}|
holds with probability at least
\mathcal{Q}(2n-1;\zeta)
.
Combining these results, we obtain

\displaystyle(\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}})(\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}})\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}=\mathbf{{b}},

\displaystyle(\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}})\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}=\mathbf{{b}},

\displaystyle(\mathbf{{A}}+\Delta\mathbf{{A}}_{1}+\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}})\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}=\mathbf{{b}},

\displaystyle(\mathbf{{A}}+\Delta\mathbf{{A}})\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}=\mathbf{{b}},

where
\Delta\mathbf{{A}}\triangleq\Delta\mathbf{{A}}_{1}+\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}+\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}
, such that
|\mathbf{{A}}|\leq(2\hat{\gamma}_{1}+\hat{\gamma}_{2}+\hat{\gamma}_{1}\hat{\gamma}_{2})|\hat{\mathbf{{L}}}||\hat{\mathbf{{U}}}|
holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(7n-6;\zeta)

via the principles of inclusion and exclusion of probabilities.

Using
theorem

15
, we can now obtain the estimate for uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic in solving a tri-diagonal system of equations as

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}-\mathbf{{x}}|}{|\mathbf{{x}}|}\leq\frac{(2\hat{\gamma}_{1}+\hat{\gamma}_{2}+\hat{\gamma}_{1}\hat{\gamma}_{2})|\mathbf{{A}}^{-1}||\mathbf{{A}}||\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}|}{|\mathbf{{x}}|},

(9)

that holds true with a probability of at least
\mathcal{Q}(7n-6;\zeta)
.

5.3
Stochastic Boundary Value Problem

We now consider a boundary-value ordinary differential equation (ODE) with random coefficients and random forcing

\frac{d}{dx}\Big((1+\theta_{1}x)\frac{du}{dx}\Big)=-50\theta_{2}^{2};\quad x\in[0,1];~u(0)=u(1)=0,

(12)

where
\theta_{1}\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,1.1)
and
\theta_{2}\sim\mathcal{U}(1,2)
, similar to
[
8
]
.
Here,
u:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is the solution function, and
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are independent random variables representing model parameters.
The quantity of interest is
q=\mathbb{E}[P]
, where
P
is a random variable with a realization
p\triangleq\int_{x}u\,dx
which also has a closed-form expression given as

\displaystyle p(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{25\theta_{2}^{2}(-2\theta_{1}+(2+\theta_{1})\log(1+\theta_{1}))}{\theta_{1}^{2}\log(1+\theta_{1})}.

(13)

Numerically computing the quantity of interest
q
is associated with multiple sources of uncertainty and numerical errors, namely, (a) parameter uncertainty, (b) Monte Carlo sampling uncertainty, (c) uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic, and (d) numerical discretization error.
Here, we quantify the uncertainty arising from floating-point arithmetic in computing the quantity of interest, accounting for multiple sources of uncertainty and numerical errors.

5.3.1
Finite-dimensional Approximation

Computing the quantity of interest
q
numerically first involves obtaining a finite-dimensional approximation of the ODE in
eq.

12
via numerical discretization.
For any given model parameters
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, the ODE system in
eq.

12
can be discretized via the finite-difference method using
M
intervals of size
\Delta x\triangleq\frac{1}{M}
.
Using second-order central difference approximation for the first- and second-order derivatives, the discretized form of
eq.

12
is given as

\displaystyle\Big(1+\frac{\theta_{1}}{M}(i-\frac{1}{2})\Big)\mathbf{{u}}_{i-1}-2\Big(1+\frac{\theta_{1}}{M}i\Big)\mathbf{{u}}_{i}+\Big(1+\frac{\theta_{1}}{M}(i+\frac{1}{2})\Big)\mathbf{{u}}_{i+1}=-50\theta_{2}^{2}\Delta x^{2},

(14)

where
\mathbf{{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1}
is the discrete solution of
u(x)
at
x=i\Delta x
for

.
This results in a tri-diagonal system of equations
\mathbf{{A}}(\theta_{1})\mathbf{{u}}=\mathbf{{b}}(\theta_{2})
, where
\mathbf{{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times(M-1)},\mathbf{{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)},
and
\mathbf{{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)}
.
Such a tri-diagonal system can be solved efficiently via the Thomas algorithm, as discussed in
section

4.3
.
Once the discrete solution
\mathbf{{u}}
is obtained, an approximation of the integral quantity
p
can be obtained via Riemann integration as
p\approx\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\mathbf{{u}}_{i}\Delta x
.
Lastly, the finite-dimensional approximation to the quantity of interest
q
can be obtained via Monte Carlo integration using
N_{s}
samples.

Obtaining an approximation for the realization
p
using a finite-dimensional approximation
\mathbf{{u}}
introduces numerical discretization error.
This error then propagates when computing an approximation to the quantity of interest
q
using Monte Carlo integration.
Moreover, using finite samples of parameters to approximate the expectation
\mathbb{E}[P]
via Monte Carlo integration introduces sampling uncertainty.

5.3.2
Discretization- and Sampling-Aware Computational Uncertainty due to Floating-Point Arithmetic

Computing an approximation of the quantity of interest

using finite-precision arithmetic results in several rounding errors.
Broadly, rounding errors accumulate when (a) obtaining the finite-dimensional numerical solution of

12

14

\mathbb{E}[P]
using finite realizations.
Given a tri-diagonal system of equations, here, we propagate the uncertainty due to floating-point arithmetic through each of the aforementioned computational steps.

Concluding remarks

In this work, we strengthen the probabilistic backward error framework of Higham and Mary
[
13
]
by introducing
Variance-informed Probabilistic Rounding Error Analysis
(
vprea
). The proposed framework defines a confidence-calibrated, operation-count-dependent constant
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
that incorporates distributional structure beyond the classical zero-mean assumption.
The analysis relies only on the first two moments of the rounding error random variable, characterized in log-space, and avoids higher-order moment assumptions. Specifically, we leverage Bernstein’s concentration inequality to analyze the logarithm of multiplicative rounding error terms, transforming their product into a sum of random variables and enabling probabilistic bounds on error accumulation.
Under the assumption that rounding errors are independent and identically distributed,
vprea
yields exact backward error bounds valid for arbitrary operation counts and precision levels, providing a systematic framework for quantifying floating-point uncertainty in modern low-precision computing environments.

A central contribution is the explicit characterization of bias in rounding error propagation.
We introduce two parametric models for the rounding error distribution: a
\mathcal{U}
-model, which assumes a uniform distribution and recovers the classical zero-mean setting, and a
\beta
-model, in which rounding errors follow a
\operatorname{Beta}
distribution that enables controlled introduction of bias.
We derive explicit conditions under which positive or negative bias can be introduced within
vprea
through the
\beta
-model.

Importantly, we show that the growth of the probabilistic constant depends on how the rounding error distribution is parameterized.
Our analysis shows that distributional parametrization can alter the growth of probabilistic bounds; in particular, under the
\beta
-model, transient growth can transition from
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
to
\mathcal{O}(n)
.
Using the dot product as a canonical example, we demonstrate that bias-aware modeling yields tight bounds in both half and single precision arithmetic, accurately capturing observed accumulation across precisions.

Numerical experiments on sparse matrix-vector products show that accounting for sparsity is essential for obtaining meaningful bounds, leading to a corollary that incorporates sparsity into the backward error analysis. For a stochastic boundary value problem, we develop discretization- and sampling-aware probabilistic bounds that quantify floating-point uncertainty alongside discretization and statistical errors; the resulting bounds improve classical deterministic guarantees by nearly an order of magnitude.

Overall, these results that probabilistic rounding error bound growth is not intrinsic but depends on how rounding errors are modeled. By making this dependence explicit,
vprea
provides a principled framework for tighter, confidence-calibrated error bounds in large-scale, low-precision scientific computing.
