Geometric effects on the electronic structure of curved nanotubes and curved graphene: the case of the helix, catenary, helicoid, and catenoid Preprint: APS/123-QED
J.D.M. de Lima, E. Gomes, F.F. da Silva Filho, F. Moraes, R. Teixeira

Abstract
Since electrons in a ballistic regime perceive a carbon nanotube or a graphene layer structure as a continuous medium, we can use the study of the quantum dynamics of one electron constrained to a curve or surface to obtain a qualitative description of the conduction electrons’ behavior. The confinement process of a quantum particle to a curve or surface leads us, in the so-called “confining potential formalism” (CPF), to a geometry-induced potential (GIP) in the effective Schrödinger equation. With these considerations, this work aims to study in detail the consequences of constraining a quantum particle to a helix, catenary, helicoid, or catenoid, exploring the relations between these curves and surfaces using differential geometry. Initially, we use the variational method to estimate the energy of the particle in its ground state, and thus, we obtain better approximations with the use of the confluent Heun function through numerical calculations. Thus, we conclude that a quantum particle constrained to an infinite helix has its angular momentum quantized due to the geometry of the curve, while in the cases of the catenary, helicoid, and catenoid the particle can be found either in a single bound state or in excited states which constitute a continuous energy band. Additionally, we propose measurements of physical observables capable of discriminating the topologies of the studied surfaces, in the context of topological metrology.



I
Introduction

Due to the great advance of materials science, it became possible to synthesize increasingly smaller carbon-based structures. Along with the discovery of graphene, the first truly two-dimensional system, several theoretical techniques have been proposed to describe the quantum dynamics of its charge carriers. Many other carbon nanostructures have also been synthesized with different shapes, such as fullerenes, nanotubes, nanocones, and structures with non-trivial geometries
Terrones and Terrones 2003
. In this sense, it is necessary to understand the effects of the geometry on the quantum dynamics of a particle in such curved structures.

One of the first formal approaches addressed to solve this problem was proposed by H. Jensen and H. Koppe in 1971, which studied quantum particles constrained to move between two parallel surfaces in the limit where the inter-surface distance goes to zero
Jensen and Koppe 1971
. In 1981, R.C.T. da Costa considered that the constraint procedure can be realized through a smooth potential well
da Costa 1981
;
da Costa 1982
. In both cases, it was showed that it leads to a geometry-induced potential (GIP) which acts upon the dynamics. If the particle is constrained to a space curve, the GIP depends on its curvature. On the other hand, when dealing with curved surfaces the GIP dependence is on its mean and Gaussian curvatures. This is known
Schuster and Jaffe 2003
as “confining potential formalism” (CPF). Experimental verification of geometric effects due to confinement were verified first in photonic topological crystals
Szameit et al. 2010
and in a peanut-shaped
C_{60}
polymer
Onoe et al. 2012
.

As the electronic transport in graphene is mesoscopically ballistic, such electrons perceive the structure as a continuous medium
Zhang et al. 2014
. This implies that the study of the quantum dynamics of an electron confined to a surface (or a curve) leads to a qualitative description of the behavior of electrons in the ballistic regime in a graphene layer (or in a carbon nanotube) with topological defects, such that the geometry has an important role in the dynamics
Santos et al. 2016
.

The study of curved structures can be essential for the understanding of their properties and possible applications, especially for electronic devices. For example, Ref.
Joglekar and Saxena 2009
suggests that p-n junctions can be induced by curvature in a graphene bilayer, whose geometry is related to a catenoid
Silva et al. 2020
. In Ref.
Atanasov et al. 2009
it was shown that a helicoidal ribbon can induce, due to its curvature, a charge separation which results in a electrical field crossing the molecule.

Furthermore, the catenoid can be considered in the study of wormholes
Dandoloff and Truong 2004
;
dos S Azevedo et al. 2021
and as an approximation to the geometries of ion-channel membrane proteins
Gupta and Saxena 2012
;
Gupta and Saxena 2011
. Similarly, the helix and the helicoid can also be considered as approximations to the geometries of
\beta
-sheet proteins, DNA molecules
Atanasov et al. 2009
;
Gupta and Saxena 2012
;
Gupta and Saxena 2018
, and helicenes
Gingras 2013
, for example.

The description of regular curves and surfaces is very well established by differential geometry. Two of the simplest regular curves that we can study in this context are the helix and the catenary. The surface formed by continuously varying the radius of the helix is called helicoid, while the surface of revolution associated with the catenary is called catenoid. As the helicoid and the catenoid are locally isometric and both of them are minimal surfaces, their properties have been reported in detail by several authors
Stoker 1989
;
Struik 1988
;
do Carmo 2016
.

The confinement of a quantum particle to a helicoid or catenoid with the CPF has been the subject of some studies. V. Atanasov, R. Dandoloff, and A. Saxena applied the CPF to the helicoid in a work published in 2009, where they showed that the constrained particle is subject to an effective radial potential that can be attractive or repulsive, depending on the angular momentum
Atanasov et al. 2009
. For the catenoid, the CPF was used in 2010 by R. Dandoloff, A. Saxena, and B. Jensen to understand the GIP in a two-dimensional section of a wormhole
Dandoloff et al. 2010
. Recently, in 2020, J.E.G. Silva et al. used the electron confinement to the catenoid to study the electronic properties of bilayer graphene connected by a bridge
Silva et al. 2020
. Also in 2020, M.C.R. Ribeiro Jr. et al. studied the confinement problem on the helicoid considering the presence of an harmonic oscillator potential and anisotropic mass
Ribeiro Jr. et al. 2020
.

With the above motivations, we study the Schrödinger equation for a quantum particle constrained to a helix, catenary, helicoid, or catenoid, using the CPF. We intend here to understand the particularities of the confinement problem in each one of them. In section
II
we present a brief review of the differential geometry of curves and surfaces, while the CPF is described in section
III
. In section
IV
we solve the problem for the helix, and we introduce the problems for the catenary, helicoid, and catenoid (solving the angular part for the surfaces). In section
V
we estimate the ground state energy using the variational method for the cases of the catenary, helicoid, and catenoid, besides we present solutions in terms of the confluent Heun functions. In section
VI
we discuss the solutions for the ground state. Finally, topological aspects are discussed in section
VII
and our conclusions and perspectives for future works are presented in Section
VIII
.

II
Overview of differential geometry

In this section we do a brief presentation of some concepts of differential geometry that are necessary for the understanding of this text. More details can be found in Ref.
do Carmo 2016
. The procedure that we will follow in this section is entirely focused on developing a mathematical support to be used in the discussion about the CPF.

II.1
Curves

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
be a regular curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose parametrization is given by

\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)=\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right),

(1)

where
t
is a parameter in an open interval
T\subset\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the arc length of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, from some arbitrary
t_{0}\in T
, is defined by

s(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}(\tau)\right|d\tau.

(2)

When it is possible to obtain
s(t)
and its inverse,
s^{-1}(t)
, we can find an arc length parametrization by making
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\circ s^{-1}
, which is convenient since
|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)|=1,\,\forall\,s
. In this parametrization, the curvature of the curve is given by

\displaystyle\kappa(s)=\left|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(s)\right|,

(3)

where
\kappa(s)
is always positive
do Carmo 2016
;
Stoker 1989
.

The helix (see Fig.
1
) with radius
b>0
and pitch (vertical distance between each loop) given by
2\pi|a|
, for
a\in\mathbb{R}
, is a regular space curve parametrized by

\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\phi)=\left(b\cos(\phi),b\sin(\phi),a\phi\right),

(4)

where
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
is the curve parameter. Consequently, its arc length is
s=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\phi
and, after obtaining
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)
, we get from equation (
3
) the constant curvature

\kappa(s)=\dfrac{b}{a^{2}+b^{2}}.

(5)

Figure 1:
Three-dimensional representation of the helix.

The catenary (see Fig.
2
) is a regular plane curve parametrized by

\boldsymbol{\alpha}(v)=(v,a\cosh(v/a)),

(6)

for
v\in\mathbb{R}
. Here, the constant
a>0
is associated with the catenary opening. From Eq. (
2
) we get
s=a\sinh(v/a)
, such that (
6
) gives us

\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)=\left(a\arcsinh(s/a),\sqrt{a^{2}+s^{2}}\right),

(7)

and

\kappa(s)=\dfrac{a}{a^{2}+s^{2}}.

(8)

Figure 2:
Two-dimensional representation of a catenary.

II.2
Surfaces

Let
S
be a regular surface embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose parametrization is

\mathbf{r}(q_{1},q_{2})=\left(x(q_{1},q_{2}),y(q_{1},q_{2}),z(q_{1},q_{2})\right),

(9)

where
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are parameters in an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. According to Ref.
do Carmo 2016
, the regularity condition implies that
\mathbf{r}_{1}=\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{r}}{\partial q_{1}}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}=\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{r}}{\partial q_{2}}
are linearly independent,
i.e.
,
\mathbf{r}_{1}\times\mathbf{r}_{2}\neq 0
at all points of
S
, which allows us to define the unit vector normal to the surface,
\mathbf{N}=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}_{1}\times\mathbf{r}_{2}}{|\mathbf{r}_{1}\times\mathbf{r}_{2}|}
.

The metric tensor of the surface
S
is given by

\mathbf{g}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where
g_{ij}=\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}
(for
i,j=1,2
) are the coefficients of the first fundamental form. Thus, denoting by
g^{ij}
the elements of the inverse matrix
\mathbf{g}^{-1}
, we get

\mathbf{g}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}g^{11}&g^{12}\\
g^{21}&g^{22}\end{pmatrix}=\dfrac{\adj(\mathbf{g})}{\det(\mathbf{g})}=\dfrac{1}{g}\begin{pmatrix}g_{22}&-g_{12}\\
-g_{21}&g_{11}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

for
g=\det(\mathbf{g})
, the determinant of the metric tensor. Further, for
\mathbf{r}_{ij}=\dfrac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{r}}{\partial q_{i}\partial q_{j}}
, the coefficients of the second fundamental form are defined as
h_{ij}=\mathbf{N}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{ij}
. Thus, the mean and Gaussian curvatures are, respectively,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{g_{11}h_{22}-2g_{12}h_{12}+g_{22}h_{11}}{g_{11}g_{22}-g_{12}^{2}},

(12)

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\dfrac{h_{11}h_{22}-h_{12}^{2}}{g_{11}g_{22}-g_{12}^{2}}.

(13)

For the helicoid, we consider
q_{1}=\phi\in\mathbb{R}
and
q_{2}=u\in\mathbb{R}
, which are coordinates on
S
. The parametrization of the helicoid (see Fig.
3
) is given by

\mathbf{r}(\phi,u)=\left(u\cos(\phi),u\sin(\phi),a\phi\right),

(14)

where
a
is a real constant. As we can see, for each fixed
u
we get a different helix. From (
14
), the coefficients of the first fundamental form are
g_{11}=a^{2}+u^{2}
,
g_{12}=g_{21}=0
, and
g_{22}=1
, which give us the metric

ds^{2}=(a^{2}+u^{2})d\phi^{2}+du^{2}.

(15)

In the same way, the coefficients of the second fundamental form are
h_{11}=0
,
h_{12}=h_{21}=\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}}
, and
h_{22}=0
. Thus, from Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
), we get

M=0\quad\text{and}\quad K=-\dfrac{a^{2}}{(a^{2}+u^{2})^{2}}.

(16)

Figure 3:
Three-dimensional graph of the helicoid with
0<\phi<2\pi
.

The catenoid (see Fig.
4
) is a surface generated by the revolution of a catenary. Making
q_{1}=\phi\in(0,2\pi)
and
q_{2}=u\in\mathbb{R}
, a parametrization for the catenoid is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}(\phi,u)=\Big(\sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}\cos(\phi),\sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}\sin(\phi),

(17)

\displaystyle a\arcsinh(u/a

\displaystyle)\Big),

where
a
, the “throat radius”, is a positive constant. Thus, from (
17
), we get
g_{11}=a^{2}+u^{2}
,
g_{12}=g_{21}=0
, and
g_{22}=1
, which give us the same metric in Eq. (
15
). The coefficients of the second fundamental form are
h_{11}=-a
,
h_{12}=h_{21}=0
, and
h_{22}=\dfrac{a}{a^{2}+u^{2}}
. Since the helicoid and the catenoid have the same coefficients of the first fundamental form in these parametrizations, they are locally isometric. In other words, it is possible to deform a catenoid onto a helicoid with
0<\phi<2\pi
while maintaining the same metric. It follows from Gauss’s Theorema Egregium that they must have the same Gaussian curvature. In fact, from Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
), we get for the catenoid the same mean and Gaussian curvatures of the helicoid, which are given by Eqs. (
16
) (cf.
do Carmo 2016
;
Stoker 1989
). The regions of same color in Figs.
3
and
4
indicate corresponding regions on the respective surfaces. In other words, the colored helicoid of Fig.
3
becomes the catenoid of Fig.
4
under a suitable deformation.

III
Confining potential formalism

In order to maintain a particle constrained to a surface
S
, it is proposed that the particle is under the action of forces orthogonal to
S
in all its points, such that the “spreading” of the wave function in the transverse direction is “squeezed”
Jensen and Koppe 1971
;
da Costa 1981
;
da Costa 1982
. Therefore, the proposal consists of considering a potential that is always constant on
S
but increases sharply in the normal direction, in such a way that the confinement occurs at the limit where an infinite potential keeps the particle bound to
S

Bastos et al. 2016
;
da Silva et al. 2017
. An analogous procedure is done for curves. Thus, a GIP appears in the effective Schrödinger equation of a particle constrained to a curve or to a surface as a consequence of the confinement.

III.1
Particle constrained to a curve

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
be a regular curve (embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
) which is parametrized by its arc length,
s
, and has curvature
\kappa=\kappa(s)
. As shown in Refs.
Jensen and Koppe 1971
;
da Costa 1981
, a quantum particle of mass
m
, constrained to
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, is subject to a GIP

\displaystyle V_{C}(s)=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\kappa^{2}.

(18)

From Eq. (
18
) we get the time-independent Schrödinger equation

-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\dfrac{d^{2}\psi}{ds^{2}}-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\kappa^{2}\psi=E\psi,

(19)

where
\psi=\psi(s)
is the wave function and
E
is the energy of the particle. The attractive potential indicates that the particle may be found in bound states due to geometric effects.

III.2
Particle constrained to a surface

Let
S
be a regular surface embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with parameters
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
. Following the CPF, a quantum particle of mass
m
, constrained to
S
, is subjected to a GIP

V_{S}(q_{1},q_{2})=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(M^{2}-K\right),

(20)

where
M
and
K
are defined in (
12
) and (
13
), respectively
Bastos et al. 2016
;
da Costa 1982
;
da Costa 1981
. Thus, from (
20
) we arrive at the time-independent Schrödinger equation

-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta_{g}\chi-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(M^{2}-K\right)\chi=E\chi,

(21)

where
\chi=\chi(q_{1},q_{2})
is the wave function and
\Delta_{g}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator which is given by
Bernard and Voon 2013

\Delta_{g}\chi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}\left(\sqrt{g}g^{ij}\dfrac{\partial\chi}{\partial q_{j}}\right).

(22)

Isometric surfaces have the same Gaussian curvature but their respective mean curvatures differ
da Costa 1981
;
do Carmo 2016
, except in the case of minimal surfaces, which have
M=0
. This is the case of the object of our study in this article, the helicoid and the catenoid, which have the same GIPs, given by Eq. (
20
).

IV
Schrödinger equations

In this Section, we apply Eq. (
19
) to the helix and the catenary, and Eq. (
21
) to the helicoid and the catenoid. In addition, we introduce the respective boundary conditions for the wave function in both cases and discuss their implications.

IV.1
Helix

From Eqs. (
5
) and (
18
), we get the GIP that acts on a particle constrained to a helix,

V_{C}=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\dfrac{b^{2}}{(a^{2}+b^{2})^{2}},

(23)

which is constant. Thus, recalling that
s=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\phi
and using Eqs. (
19
) and (
23
), we write

\dfrac{d^{2}\psi}{d\phi^{2}}+l^{2}\psi=0,

(24)

where

l^{2}=\dfrac{b^{2}}{4(a^{2}+b^{2})}+\dfrac{2m(a^{2}+b^{2})}{\hbar^{2}}E.

(25)

Now, let us discuss the boundary conditions for Eq. (
24
) when we have an infinite helix (
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
). Let
p
be an arbitrary point on the helix and
B(p)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a ball of radius
\varepsilon_{B}
centered on
p
. Within that ball, the particle perceives the surrounding environment in the same way at any point of the helix, because
B
is flat, and the helix has constant curvature and torsion. Thus, the wave function is distributed in the same way in the surrounding of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\phi)
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\phi+2\pi)
. Therefore, we have the appropriate boundary condition
\psi(\phi)=\psi(\phi+2\pi)
. This immediately leads to the quantization of the angular momentum,
\hat{L}_{\phi}=-i\hbar\dfrac{d}{d\phi}
. In other words, if
\psi
is a eigenfunction of
\hat{L}_{\phi}
, we have the eigenvalues
l\hbar
, for
l=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots
, such that Eq. (
24
) is retrieved in terms of
\hat{L}_{\phi}^{2}
. The quantized energy is obtained from Eq. (
25
):

E_{l}=\dfrac{l^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2m(a^{2}+b^{2})}-\frac{\hbar^{2}b^{2}}{8m(a^{2}+b^{2})^{2}}.

(26)

The corresponding propagating modes are degenerate, since they can move either “up” or “down” on the helix, depending on the sign of
l
. The delta-function normalized wave functions are given by

\psi_{l}(\phi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{il\phi}.

(27)

For a finite helix with
0<\phi<\phi_{0}
, for some fixed
\phi_{0}
, we have the boundary conditions
\psi(0)=\psi(\phi_{0})=0
, such that we get
l=\dfrac{j\pi}{\phi_{0}}
, therefore, the solutions of Eq. (
24
) are

\psi_{j}(\phi)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\phi_{0}}}\sin\left(\dfrac{j\pi}{\phi_{0}}\phi\right),

(28)

where
j=1,2,3,\dots
is a quantum number. Thus, the possible energies are given by

E_{j}=\dfrac{j^{2}\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2m(a^{2}+b^{2}){\phi_{0}}^{2}}-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}b^{2}}{8m(a^{2}+b^{2})^{2}}.

(29)

Note that both Eqs. (
26
) and (
29
) are similar to those obtained for a particle in an infinite potential well with the geometric contribution from the GIP (
23
).

Catenary

From Eqs. (
8
) and (
18
) we get

V_{C}(s)=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\dfrac{a^{2}}{(a^{2}+s^{2})^{2}},

(30)

which is the GIP for a particle constrained to the catenary (see Fig.
5
). Thus, from (
19
) we get the Schrödinger equation

-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\dfrac{d^{2}\psi}{ds^{2}}-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\dfrac{a^{2}}{(a^{2}+s^{2})^{2}}\psi=E\psi,

(31)

whose solutions for bound states obey the boundary conditions:
\displaystyle{\lim_{s\rightarrow\pm\infty}\psi=0}
. Due to the presence of the GIP, finding the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of (

) is not a simple task. For now, it is more convenient developing the CPF to the helicoid and catenoid, thus, we will return to the problem of confinement to a catenary in section
V
.

IV.3
Helicoid and catenoid

Substituting (
16
) into (
20
) we can obtain the GIP for both helicoid and catenoid surfaces,

V_{S}(u)=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\dfrac{a^{2}}{(a^{2}+u^{2})^{2}},

(32)

which is independent of
\phi
and has the same behavior as the potential in Fig.
5
(up to the multiplicative factor
1/4
). From Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
), we find
g^{11}=\dfrac{1}{a^{2}+u^{2}},

g^{12}=g^{21}=0
,
g^{22}=1
, and
g=a^{2}+u^{2}
, consequently, the Laplace-Beltrami operator is

\Delta_{g}=\dfrac{1}{a^{2}+u^{2}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}+\dfrac{u}{a^{2}+u^{2}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial u},

(33)

from (
22
).
Using Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) in Eq. (
21
), we get the Schrödinger equation

\begin{split}-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\bigg(\dfrac{1}{a^{2}+u^{2}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\chi}{\partial\phi^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\chi}{\partial u^{2}}+\dfrac{u}{a^{2}+u^{2}}\dfrac{\partial\chi}{\partial u}\bigg)&\\
-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\dfrac{a^{2}}{(a^{2}+u^{2})^{2}}\chi&=E\chi,\end{split}

(34)

for the wave function
\chi=\chi(\phi,u)
.

With the element of area given by
dA=\sqrt{g}d\phi du
, the probability of finding the particle in a given region of the surface is

\begin{split}P_{A}&=\iint|\chi(\phi,u)|^{2}(a^{2}+u^{2})^{1/2}d\phi du\\
&=\iint|\chi_{g}(\phi,u)|^{2}d\phi du,\end{split}

(35)

where (see
Kar et al. 1994
;
Silva et al. 2020
)
\chi_{g}(\phi,u)=(a^{2}+u^{2})^{1/4}\chi(\phi,u)
. Thus, Eq. (
34
) becomes

\begin{split}-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\bigg(\dfrac{1}{a^{2}+u^{2}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\chi_{g}}{\partial\phi^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\chi_{g}}{\partial u^{2}}\bigg)&\\
-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\bigg(\dfrac{a^{2}}{4(a^{2}+u^{2})^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{4(a^{2}+u^{2})}\bigg)\chi_{g}&=E\chi_{g}.\end{split}

(36)

Considering a separation of variables for the wave function,
\chi_{g}(\phi,u)=\Phi(\phi)\psi(u)
, we write from (
36
) an angular equation,

\dfrac{d^{2}\Phi}{d\phi^{2}}+l^{2}\Phi=0,

(37)

and a radial equation,

\begin{split}-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\dfrac{d^{2}\psi}{du^{2}}-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\bigg(&\dfrac{a^{2}}{4(a^{2}+u^{2})^{2}}\\
&+\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4(a^{2}+u^{2})}\bigg)\psi=E\psi,\end{split}

(38)

where
l^{2}
is a separation constant associated with the angular momentum of the particle.

As we can see, Eq. (
37
) admits the normalized solutions

\Phi_{l}(\phi)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{il\phi}

(39)

for the catenoid. Thus, in this specific case, it is clear that the parametrization of the catenoid is periodic in
\phi
, because we have
\mathbf{r}(\phi+2\pi,u)=\mathbf{r}(\phi,u)
, such that the angular wave function must also have periodicity
2\pi
,
i.e.
,
\Phi_{l}(\phi+2\pi)=\Phi_{l}(\phi)
, which implies that the angular momentum quantum number
l
assumes only integer values (
l=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots
).

The angular wave functions in the case of the helicoid behave similarly to the solutions found in the case of the helix. Due to the geometry of the infinite helicoid (
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
), equation (
39
) is obtained for
l=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots
, exactly as in the case of the catenoid. On the other hand, for a finite helicoid at
0<\phi<\phi_{0}
, we find the wave functions

\Phi_{j}(\phi)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\phi_{0}}}\sin\left(\dfrac{j\pi}{\phi_{0}}\phi\right),

(40)

for
l=\dfrac{j\pi}{\phi_{0}}
, where
j=1,2,3,\dots
is the quantum number. Thus, despite the well-known isometry between a catenoid and a helicoid limited by
0<\phi<2\pi
, the respective wave functions differ due to the boundary conditions employed. Thereby, in section
V
we will look for solutions of the radial equation (
38
) for possible bound states,
i.e.
, considering the usual boundary condition
\displaystyle{\lim_{u\rightarrow\pm\infty}\psi=0}
.

V
Radial Wave Functions

As we can see, Eq. (
38
) can be conveniently rewritten as

-\dfrac{d^{2}\psi}{d\tilde{u}^{2}}-\left(\dfrac{1}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}+\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})}\right)\psi=\epsilon\psi,

(41)

where
\tilde{u}=u/a
is the radial variable and
\epsilon=2ma^{2}E/\hbar^{2}
is the energy, both in dimensionless units
Dandoloff et al. 2010
. Thus, (
41
) is a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation with an effective potential (also dimensionless) given by

V_{l}(\tilde{u})=-\dfrac{1}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}-\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})},

(42)

which is always attractive only if
|l|\leq 1/2
. Effective potentials which are equivalent to (
42
) were previously found in Refs.
Atanasov et al. 2009
;
Dandoloff et al. 2010
;
da Silva et al. 2017
;
Silva et al. 2020
.

We see that Eq. (
38
) becomes exactly (
31
) when considering
l=1/2
and the arc length
s=u
. Since
l
is associated with the angular momentum, it does not appear in the CPF when considering a particle in the catenary, but
l=1/2
can be used as a mere mathematical artifice such that the solutions of the Schrödinger equation for the catenary can be obtained through Eq. (
38
).

On the other hand, for the finite helicoid we have that
l=\dfrac{j\pi}{\phi_{0}}
, such that the effective potential in (
42
) is always attractive for
j\leq\dfrac{\phi_{0}}{2\pi}
. Thus, if
\phi_{0}
is a multiple of
2\pi
, the maximum value of
j
for which
V_{l}(\tilde{u})
is always attractive is precisely the number of complete loops of the helicoid. Due to the local isometry between the catenoid and the helicoid, we will discuss only the case where
\phi_{0}=2\pi
, which implies that
V_{l}(\tilde{u})
is attractive only for
j=1
,
i.e.
, for
l=1/2
. Finally, for the infinite helicoid and the catenoid we have
l=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots
, thus in these cases the effective potential is always attractive only if
l=0
,
i.e.
, when the particle has zero angular momentum. Concisely, to find the possible bound states due to the effective potential we will consider in (
42
) that

l=\begin{cases}1/2\text{ for the catenary,}\\
1/2\text{ for the finite helicoid }(\phi_{0}=2\pi),\\
0\text{ for the catenoid,}\\
0\text{ for the infinite helicoid }(\phi\in\mathbb{R}).\end{cases}

(43)

In Fig.
6
we present the graphs of the effective potential
V_{l}(\tilde{u})
for some values of
l
.

The second term in the effective potential (
42
) can be separated in both repulsive and attractive parts, given by
\dfrac{l^{2}}{1+\tilde{u}^{2}}
and
-\dfrac{1}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})}
, respectively. The repulsive part, which naturally disappears for
l=0
, corresponds to the usual centrifugal term in quantum mechanics. On the other hand, the attractive one arises due to the confinement of the particle, and it is related to the change made in Eq. (
35
), being associated with the so-called “quantum anticentrifugal force”, as discussed in Refs.
Cirone et al. 2001
;
Atanasov and Dandoloff 2007
.

V.1
Variational method

Considering the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
41
),

\hat{\mathcal{H}}=-\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\tilde{u}^{2}}-\left(\dfrac{1}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}+\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})}\right),

(44)

we can use the variational method (also known as the Rayleigh-Ritz method) to obtain approximations for the energy. For a given tentative wave function
\psi_{n,l}=\psi_{n,l}(\tilde{u})
, which is normalized, the expectation value of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
is

\begin{split}\epsilon_{n,l}=&-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{n,l}^{*}\dfrac{d^{2}\psi_{n,l}}{d\tilde{u}^{2}}d\tilde{u}\\
&-\dfrac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{\psi_{n,l}^{*}\psi_{n,l}}{(1+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}d\tilde{u}\\
&-\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{\psi_{n,l}^{*}\psi_{n,l}}{1+\tilde{u}^{2}}d\tilde{u},\end{split}

(45)

where
n=1,2,3,\dots
is the quantum number associated with the energy levels. Such method consists of intuitively proposing a wave function
\psi_{n,l}
which depends on a parameter
\beta_{l}
adjusted in order to minimize the energy
\epsilon_{n,l}(\beta_{l})
in Eq. (
45
). Consequently, this method give us an upper limit for the true energy
Zettili 2009
;
Sakurai and Napolitano 2017
.

V.1.1
Gaussian function

For the ground state (
n=1
) we are looking for bell-shaped functions. We firstly propose the Gaussian wave function

\psi_{1,l}(\tilde{u})=\left(\dfrac{\beta_{l}}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\beta_{l}\tilde{u}^{2}/2},

(46)

where
\beta_{l}>0
is a parameter to be adjusted in order to obtain a minimum value for the energy of the particle. By replacing (
46
) in (
45
), we obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1,l}=-\sqrt{\dfrac{\beta_{l}}{\pi}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\beta_{l}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\beta_{l}\tilde{u}^{2}-1\right)e^{-\beta_{l}\tilde{u}^{2}}d\tilde{u}

(47)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{e^{-\beta_{l}\tilde{u}^{2}}}{(1+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}d\tilde{u}

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{e^{-\beta_{l}\tilde{u}^{2}}}{1+\tilde{u}^{2}}d\tilde{u}\Bigg],

which leads us to

\begin{split}\epsilon_{1,l}=\ &\dfrac{\sqrt{\pi\beta_{l}}e^{\beta_{l}}}{4}\left(\beta_{l}+4l^{2}-\dfrac{3}{2}\right)\left[1-\erf\left(\sqrt{\beta_{l}}\right)\right]\\
&+\dfrac{\beta_{l}}{4},\end{split}

(48)

where
\erf
is the error function
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
;
Olver et al. 2010
. By minimizing the energy with respect to
\beta_{l}
, we find

\begin{split}\dfrac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{\beta_{l}}}{4\sqrt{\beta_{l}}}\left[\beta_{l}^{2}+2l^{2}\left(2\beta_{l}+1\right)-\dfrac{3}{4}\right]\left[1-\erf\left(\sqrt{\beta_{l}}\right)\right]&\\
+\dfrac{5}{8}-l^{2}-\dfrac{\beta_{l}}{4}=\ &0.\end{split}

(49)

Now, we need to solve Eq. (
49
) numerically for the corresponding values of
l
. For the infinite helicoid and for the catenoid we have
l=0
, such that (
49
) becomes

\dfrac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{\beta_{0}}}{4\sqrt{\beta_{0}}}\left(\beta_{0}^{2}-\dfrac{3}{4}\right)\left[1-\erf\left(\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\right)\right]+\dfrac{5}{8}-\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{4}=0,

(50)

whose approximate solution is
\beta_{0}\approx 0.14133
, therefore, we get the energy
\epsilon_{1,0}\approx-0.11977
. On the other hand, we have
l=1/2
in the cases of the finite helicoid (
\phi_{0}=2\pi
) and the catenary, such that we find

\begin{split}\dfrac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{\beta_{1/2}}}{4\sqrt{\beta_{1/2}}}\left(\beta_{1/2}^{2}+\beta_{1/2}-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)\left[1-\erf\left(\sqrt{\beta_{1/2}}\ \right)\right]&\\
+\dfrac{3}{8}-\dfrac{\beta_{1/2}}{4}=\ &0,\end{split}

(51)

thus
\beta_{1/2}\approx 0.04120
and
\epsilon_{1,1/2}\approx-0.02299
. Fig.
7
shows the graphs of the corresponding tentative wave functions from Eq. (
46
).

V.1.2
Lorentzian function

Also for the ground state, we propose (for comparison purposes) the Lorentzian wave function

\psi_{1,l}(\tilde{u})=\left(\dfrac{2\beta_{l}^{3}}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}\dfrac{1}{\beta_{l}^{2}+\tilde{u}^{2}},

(52)

where
\beta_{l}>0
. By replacing Eq. (
52
) in (
45
), we obtain

\begin{split}\epsilon_{1.l}=-\dfrac{2\beta_{l}^{3}}{\pi}&\Bigg[2\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{3\tilde{u}^{2}-\beta_{l}^{2}}{(\beta_{l}^{2}+\tilde{u}^{2})^{4}}d\tilde{u}\\
&+\dfrac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{d\tilde{u}}{(1+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}(\beta_{l}^{2}+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}\\
&+\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{d\tilde{u}}{(1+\tilde{u}^{2})(\beta_{l}^{2}+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}\Bigg].\end{split}

(53)

From equation (
53
) we get

\epsilon_{1,l}=\dfrac{1}{2\beta_{l}^{2}}-\dfrac{\beta_{l}^{2}+3\beta_{l}+1}{4(\beta_{l}+1)^{3}}+\dfrac{4l^{2}-1}{4}\dfrac{2\beta_{l}+1}{(\beta_{l}+1)^{2}},

(54)

whose minimization gives us

\begin{split}(3-8l^{2})\beta_{l}^{5}+\left(2-8l^{2}\right)\beta_{l}^{4}-16\beta_{l}^{3}&\\
-24\beta_{l}^{2}-16\beta_{l}-4&=0.\end{split}

(55)

Thus, for the infinite helicoid and for the catenoid we set
l=0
in Eq. (
55
), which give us

3\beta_{0}^{5}+2\beta_{0}^{4}-16\beta_{0}^{3}-24\beta_{0}^{2}-16\beta_{0}-4=0,

(56)

whose positive solution is approximately
\beta_{0}\approx 2.70072
, and from that, we get the energy
\epsilon_{1,0}\approx-0.12918
. Similarly, we have
l=1/2
in the cases of the finite helicoid (
\phi_{0}=2\pi
) and of the catenary, thus

\beta_{1/2}^{5}-16\beta_{1/2}^{3}-24\beta_{1/2}^{2}-16\beta_{1/2}-4=0,

(57)

therefore
\beta_{1/2}\approx 4.67957
and
\epsilon_{1,1/2}\approx-0.02757
. Figure
8
shows the graphs of the tentative wave functions obtained with the respective
\beta_{l}
.

for the finite helicoid (

) and the catenary.

As we can see with the Gaussian and Lorentzian functions, the wave function is more concentrated at the origin for
l=0
than for

. This was expected, as the second term in Eq. (
42
) cancels out for

, but is negative for
l=0

52

Sakurai and Napolitano 2017
.

V.2
Confluent Heun function

The variational method provides reasonable approximations for the ground state of the problems in question, but it does not necessarily works well in the search for possible excited states. Consequently, we need to turn our attention again to the radial equation,

\dfrac{d^{2}\psi}{d\tilde{u}^{2}}+\left(\dfrac{1}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})^{2}}+\dfrac{1-4l^{2}}{4(1+\tilde{u}^{2})}+\epsilon\right)\psi=0,

(58)

in order to find exact solutions or better approximations.

Introducing a new variable
\xi=-\tilde{u}^{2}
, such that
\xi\in(-\infty,0],
we find from Eq. (
58
) that

\begin{split}&\dfrac{d^{2}\psi}{d\xi^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{2\xi}\dfrac{d\psi}{d\xi}\\
&-\dfrac{4\epsilon\xi^{2}+(4l^{2}-8\epsilon-1)\xi+4\epsilon-4l^{2}+2}{16\xi(1-\xi)^{2}}\psi=0.\end{split}

(59)

Considering similar changes to those used in Refs.
Hartmann 2014
;
Hartmann and Portnoi 2017
;
Ishkhanyan 2016
, we use the
ansatz

\psi(\xi)=(1-\xi)^{(\tilde{\gamma}+1)/2}H(\xi)
, where
\tilde{\gamma}>0
is a parameter to be conveniently adjusted.
From (
59
) we get

\dfrac{d^{2}H}{d\xi^{2}}+\left[\dfrac{1}{2\xi}-\dfrac{\tilde{\gamma}+1}{1-\xi}\right]\dfrac{dH}{d\xi}+\dfrac{\mathcal{A}(\xi)}{16\xi(1-\xi)^{2}}H=0,

(60)

where we have the function

\mathcal{A}(\xi)=(1-\xi)\left[4l^{2}-4\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{\gamma}+1)-1-4\epsilon(1-\xi)\right]+4{\tilde{\gamma}}^{2}-5.

Since
\tilde{\gamma}
is “arbitrary”, we can consider it in a way that Eq. (
60
) becomes as simple as possible. Thus, choosing
\tilde{\gamma}
such that
4{\tilde{\gamma}}^{2}-5=0
is the most convenient here, because these are precisely the terms that are not being multiplied by
(1-\xi)
in
\mathcal{A}(\xi)
. Therefore, we have the parameter
\tilde{\gamma}=\sqrt{5}/2
, thus it follows from Eq. (
60
) that

\begin{split}\dfrac{d^{2}H}{d\xi^{2}}+\left[\tilde{\alpha}+\dfrac{\tilde{\beta}+1}{\xi}+\dfrac{\tilde{\gamma}+1}{\xi-1}\right]\dfrac{dH}{d\xi}&\\
+\left[\dfrac{\tilde{\mu}}{\xi}+\dfrac{\tilde{\nu}}{\xi-1}\right]H&=0,\end{split}

(61)

for
\tilde{\alpha}=0
,
\tilde{\beta}=-1/2
,
\tilde{\mu}=\left(2l^{2}-\sqrt{5}-3-2\epsilon\right)/8
, and
\tilde{\nu}=\left(\sqrt{5}+3-2l^{2}\right)/8
. Equation (
61
) is known as the confluent Heun equation
Karayer et al. 2015
;
Ronveaux 1995
;
Ishkhanyan 2016
;
Kristensson 2010
;
Ishkhanyan 2018
.

Around the regular singular point
\xi=0
, the power series solution of Eq. (
61
) converges on the unit disc,
|\xi|<1
, and it is called the confluent Heun function,

\HeunC\left(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta},\xi\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}v_{n}\left(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta}\right)\xi^{n},

(62)

with the parameters

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}+\tilde{\nu}-\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}}{2}\left(\tilde{\beta}+\tilde{\gamma}+2\right)=-\dfrac{\epsilon}{4},

(63)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}}{2}\left(\tilde{\beta}+1\right)-\tilde{\mu}-\dfrac{\tilde{\beta}+\tilde{\gamma}+\tilde{\beta}\tilde{\gamma}}{2}=\dfrac{2\epsilon-2l^{2}+5}{8},

(64)

and where
v_{n}=v_{n}\left(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta}\right)
are coefficients
Karayer et al. 2015
;
Downing 2013
. Even though the convergence of (
62
) is assured for
|\xi|<1
, in many cases it is possible to use numerical methods of extrapolation (like those implemented in Maple) to obtain approximations of
H(\xi)
when
|\xi|\geq 1
, depending on the parameters
\tilde{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\beta}
,
\tilde{\gamma}
,
\tilde{\delta}
, and
\tilde{\eta}
.

Since
\tilde{\beta}
is non-integer, a second solution for (
61
) can be written as
\xi^{-\tilde{\beta}}\HeunC\left(\tilde{\alpha},-\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta},\xi\right)

Boyack and Lekner 2011
;
Olver et al. 2010
. Thus, from (
62
), the solutions of Eq. (
58
) are separable into an even function,

\psi_{\text{e}}(\tilde{u})=C_{0}\left(1+\tilde{u}^{2}\right)^{\frac{\sqrt{5}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}}\HeunC\left(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta},-\tilde{u}^{2}\right),

(65)

and an odd function,

\psi_{\text{o}}(\tilde{u})=C_{1}\left(1+\tilde{u}^{2}\right)^{\frac{\sqrt{5}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{u}\HeunC\left(\tilde{\alpha},-\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta},-\tilde{u}^{2}\right),

(66)

where
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
are normalization constants.

Once the analytical solutions of (
58
) have been found, the next step is to apply the boundary conditions
\displaystyle{\lim_{\tilde{u}\rightarrow\pm\infty}\psi=0}
. We cannot use here the approach of reducing
\HeunC
to a polynomial (see
Ronveaux 1995
;
Downing 2013
;
Fiziev 2009
), because we would get solutions that do not obey the boundary conditions. Similar problems have been reported in some recent works
Dong et al. 2016a
;
Dong et al. 2016b
;
Dong et al. 2018
;
Dong et al. 2019
. An alternative would be writing a solution to (
61
) around the regular singular point
\xi=1
and use the so-called Wronskian method to obtain the corresponding eigenvalues
Hartmann 2014
;
Boyack and Lekner 2011
;
Fernández 2011
. However, here we have the variable
\xi=-\tilde{u}^{2}
, which implies that
\xi=1
is not part of the solutions domain of Eq. (
61
) in the problem of interest, therefore, the Wronskian method cannot be applied here.

To circumvent the above mentioned adversities, we propose that the particle is initially in a system limited in
\tilde{u}
,
i.e.
, subject to the potential

V(\tilde{u})=\begin{cases}+\infty\text{ for }\tilde{u}\leq-\tilde{u}_{0}\text{ or }\tilde{u}\geq\tilde{u}_{0},\\
V_{l}(\tilde{u})\text{ for }-\tilde{u}_{0}<\tilde{u}<\tilde{u}_{0},\end{cases}

(67)

where
V_{l}(\tilde{u})
is the effective potential in (
42
) and
\pm\tilde{u}_{0}
corresponds to the edges of the one-dimensional box, whose width is
2\tilde{u}_{0}
. Thus, when we have finite catenaries, helicoids, or catenoids in
\tilde{u}
, both equations

\lim_{\tilde{u}\rightarrow\pm\tilde{u}_{0}}\HeunC\left(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta},-\tilde{u}^{2}\right)=0

(68)

and

\lim_{\tilde{u}\rightarrow\pm\tilde{u}_{0}}\HeunC\left(\tilde{\alpha},-\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\eta},-\tilde{u}^{2}\right)=0

(69)

can be used to obtain the energies
\epsilon_{n,l}
(for
n=1,2,3,\dots
) that cause wave functions to tend to zero at
\pm\tilde{u}_{0}

Sitnitsky 2017
. When assigning values to
\tilde{u}_{0}
, solutions to (
68
) and (
69
) can be found using Maple’s
fsolve
command and its numeric methods for
|\xi|\geq 1

MAPLE 2017 2017
. Next, we will conduct a study of how the energies and the wave functions behave as the box size increases. We call this “the box method”.

VII
Topological aspects

As mentioned before, the helicoid and catenoid are locally isometric to each other, that is, their local geometry is indistinguishable. But, as it is obvious from Figs.
3
and
4
, they are quite different from a global point of view. In fact, these surfaces have different topologies. Any closed curve on the helicoid can be shrunk to a point, while there is a class of loops on the catenoid that will end up stuck at the catenoid’s neck upon reduction. Quantum mechanics, being inherently delocalized, offers many possibilities as a probe for topology. A well known example, the Aharonov-Bohm
Aharonov and Bohm 1959
phase stands as a self-evident quantum observable that is directly related to topology.

Topology has become an important tool both in the study of the physical properties as well as in the design of new materials
Gupta and Saxena 2014
. Physical systems may have very rich hidden topologies, like in Fermi surfaces, for instance. A change of topology of the Fermi surface signals an electronic phase transition, as observed by Lifshitz
Lifshitz et al. 1960
already in 1960. A large class of modern materials, the so-called Topological Materials
Vergniory et al. 2019
, rely their unusual, and sometimes exotic properties, in the intrinsic topology of their electronic band structures. A detailed account of the role of topology in modern materials is given in Ref.
Gupta and Saxena 2018
. It is clear then, the importance of probing the topology of a physical system by means of specific measurements. The concept of using the measurement of a physical observable to identify the topology of a system, or topological metrology
Gupta and Saxena 2014
, becomes then an important tool for the geometric/topological characterization of novel materials. How to apply this idea to the systems studied here? How an experiment can distinguish between two isometric surfaces with different topologies? A hint comes from the aforementioned Aharonov-Bohm phase. The original A-B setup
Aharonov and Bohm 1959
involves a non simply-connected (shielded) region of space which is threaded by a magnetic flux. Electron beams traveling on either side of the “hole” that contains the flux acquire different quantum phases whose difference can be measured by interference. This establishes the A-B phase as a topological metric in the sense described above. On the other hand, geometry and topology give rise to analog A-B effects. For instance, the parallel transport of vectors and spinors around cosmic strings (literally a cylindrical hole in spacetime) gives rise
Ford and Vilenkin 1981
to nontrivial phases that reflects the locally flat spacetime geometry of this cosmic object and its conical topology. Disclinations, which are topological line defects in nematic liquid crystals, present a similar effect for light propagation
Carvalho et al. 2007
. A measurement of the phase acquired by the polarization (a spinor) of the light traveling around the disclination can give information on its topology. Both the cosmic string and disclination examples can be unified under the single view of a A-B-type effect where the magnetic field is substituted by a curvature flux
Carvalho et al. 2013
.

Wavefunctions, being scalar, are trivially parallel-transported, not yielding any topological information in this way. Nevertheless, upon scattering, the acquired phase shift of the wave function might provide topological information as in the case of diffraction of light by disclinations in liquid crystals
Pereira and Moraes 2011
. The phase shift appears then as possible topological metric. Another option comes from higher order tensors like vectors or spinors which, as discussed above, may reveal topological features when subjected to parallel transport. In modeling curved graphene with the Dirac equation, one naturally deals with spinors, which can then be used as probes for topology in the A-B scheme. Recent tight-binding results
Stegmann and Szpak 2018
indicated that curvature of elastically deformed graphene can be used to split the electric current into two beams of differently valley-polarized electrons. The curvature, represented by the strain field, acts on the electrons as a pseudo-magnetic field splitting the otherwise degenerate valley states. This could be a strategy for an Aharonov-Bohm-type of experiment that would compare the phases acquired by the valley spinors in each beam as they travel on opposing sides of the curved region (see Fig. 1 of Ref.
Stegmann and Szpak 2018
). Then, parallel transport of the spinors, measured by interference of valley-polarized electron beams traveling along equivalent paths, like the ones shown on the catenoid of Fig.
17
, can be used as a tool for topological metrology and thus distinguish between two isometric surfaces with different topologies, like the ones studied here.

VIII
Conclusions

In this work, we studied the confinement of a quantum particle to a helix, catenary, helicoid, or catenoid using the Confining Potential Formalism to curves and surfaces. First, it was shown that a particle constrained to an infinite helix has its angular momentum quantized. On the other hand, for the confinement to a finite helix, the problem becomes similar to that of a quantum particle in a one-dimensional potential well.

The local isometry between the finite helicoid and the catenoid was explored, such that it was shown that the quantum dynamics on the surfaces is governed by the same Schrödinger equation. From this, an effective Schrödinger equation was obtained, in the radial variable
\tilde{u}
, with an effective potential
V_{l}(\tilde{u})
that is attractive only if
|l|\leq 1/2
. In the case of the infinite helicoid and the catenoid, it is only possible to have bound states when
l=0
, while in the case of a helicoid limited to
0<\phi<2\pi
this occurs only for
l=1/2
. In addition, it was observed that, by making
l=1/2
in the radial equation for the helicoid and the catenoid, the same Schrödinger equation is obtained for the case of the catenary.

The use of the variational method (with the Gaussian and the Lorentzian functions) guarantees the existence of a bound state in the cases of the catenary, the helicoid (finite and infinite in
\phi
) and the catenoid. Thus, it was shown that the radial Schrödinger equation has exact solutions that are written in terms of the confluent Heun function, however, the eigenvalues cannot be obtained analytically, so it was necessary to use numerical methods to estimate them. In fact, both methods were in agreement with each other, and it was possible to infer that, in these three cases, the particle can be found in a single bound state, which is due to the GIP that comes from the CPF.

To sum up, we recall that Refs.
Atanasov et al. 2009
;
Dandoloff et al. 2010
;
Silva et al. 2020
provided detailed analysis of the geometry-induced quantum potential and some of its effects due to confinement of a particle to either a helix, catenary, helicoid, or catenoid. We extend and complement these previous works by providing a unified treatment of the Schrödinger equations of the corresponding cases. Furthermore, we determine the single bound state that appears in the infinite versions of the catenary, helicoid, and catenoid and show that the remaining states combine into a continuous energy band.

As perspectives for future works, we intend to study the confinement of a quantum particle with spin in the helicoid and catenoid, as well as to study the confinement of identical particles under these conditions. Since the spin is sensitive to torsion
Dandoloff and Truong 2004
, it may be able to distinguish the surfaces in spite of their isometry. Another interesting approach is the addition of a geometry-induced magnetic field to the helicoid as done in Ref.
Wang et al. 2020
. We also intend to use the methods used in the present work for the confinement problem in other regular curves and surfaces.
