Solving graph equipartition SDPs on an algebraic variety
Tianyun Tang, Kim-Chuan Toh

Abstract
Semidefinite programs are generally challenging to solve due to their high dimensionality. Burer and Monteiro developed a non-convex approach to solve linear SDP problems by applying its low rank property. Their approach is fast because they used factorization to reduce the problem size. In this paper, we focus on solving the SDP relaxation of a graph equipartition problem, which involves an additional semidefinite upper bound constraint over the traditional linear SDP. By applying the factorization approach, we get a non-convex problem with an additional non-smooth spectral inequality constraint. We discuss when the non-convex problem is equivalent to the original SDP, and when a second order stationary point of the non-convex problem is also a global minimum. Our results generalize previous works on smooth non-convex factorization approaches for linear SDP to the non-smooth case. Moreover, the constraints of the non-convex problem involve an algebraic variety with some conducive properties that allow us to use Riemannian optimization techniques and non-convex augmented Lagrangian method to solve the SDP problem very efficiently with certified global optimality.



keywords:
Graph equipartition, Burer and Monteiro method, low rank SDP, algebraic variety, Riemannian optimization, augmented Lagrangian method

Introduction

1.1
Literature Review

Many combinatorial optimization problems such as max-cut and maximum stable set problems are NP-hard, which means that it is almost impossible to solve them exactly in polynomial time.
These problems are often solved approximately via their semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations
which typically result in linear SDP problems of the following form:

{\rm SDP:\ }\min_{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\left\{\left\langle C,X\right\rangle:\ \mathcal{A}\left(X\right)=b,\ X\succeq 0\right\},

(1)

where
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
C\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
are given data, and
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{S}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a given linear mapping (see
[
16
,
20
,
31
]
for examples of SDP relaxation).

The above linear SDP problem (
1
) is a convex programming problem
which can be solved by various well developed solvers such as SDPT3
[
36
,
37
]
,
MOSEK
[
25
]
, and SDPNAL
[
42
,
44
]
. Those solvers are efficient and accurate if
n
is moderate (say less than
2000
) and
in the case of SDPT3 and MOSEK,
m
is also not too large (say less than
20000
). However, if
n
is large, the SDP will be challenging to solve by the aforementioned solvers because they will incur excessive computing cost and memory usage due to the high dimensionality in
n
. To overcome this difficulty, Burer and Monteiro
[
9
,
10
]

proposed to solve the linear SDP (
1
) by applying the factorization
X=RR^{\top}

to reformulate it as the following non-convex problem:

{\rm SDPLR:\ }\min\left\{\big\langle C,\,RR^{\top}\big\rangle:\ \mathcal{A}\big(RR^{\top}\big)=b,\ R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\right\}.

(2)

Due to the low rank property (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
14
,
1
]
) of the SDP (
1
), which states that if the feasible region of (
1
) is compact, then it will have an optimal solution of rank
\leq\sqrt{2m}.
So (
1
) is equivalent to (
2
) when
r\geq\sqrt{2m}
. Burer and Monteiro used an augmented Lagrangian method to solve (

) and its high efficiency was verified in numerical comparison to other algorithms such as the spectral bundle
[
18
]
and interior point methods
[
3
]
.

Since (
2
) is a non-convex problem, it may have spurious local minima. As far as we know, the first foundational and thorough analysis about the global optimality of (
2
) is done in a series of works
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
29
]
,
by Boumal et al. They proved that under LICQ-like regularity assumptions on the constraints of (

), if

, then for almost all

except for a set of measure zero, every second order stationary point of (
2
) is a global optimal solution. This result explains why the BM approach can almost always find the global minimum in practice. They also did a smoothed analysis for problem (
2
) and its penalty version, which was useful in choosing the stopping criterion for the augmented Lagrangian subproblems. Moreover, with their regularity assumption, the set
\left\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:\ \mathcal{A}\left(RR^{\top}\right)=b\right\}
is a Riemannian manifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},
so they can use an optimization algorithm on manifold to solve (
2
). They also developed a widely used toolbox called manopt
[
8
]
to handle
optimization problems on a variety of manifolds.

One specific example where Riemannian algorithms are expected to be
efficient for solving
the problem (
2
) is the max-cut problem for which
\mathcal{A}(X)={\rm diag}(X)
, and the underlying manifold is the oblique manifold:

{\rm OB}_{n,r}:=\left\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:\ {\rm diag}\big(RR^{\top}\big)=e\right\},

whose tangent space and retraction mapping have a simple formula. In this case, Boumal et al.
[
6
]
used manopt to solve (
2
) and verified its high efficiency as compared to Burer and Monteiro’s augmented Lagrangian method and other convex algorithms.

The research by Boumal et al. in
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
29
]

was a milestone in
studying the
global optimality of the non-convex factorized formulation of a linear SDP problem
by combining the geometric and low-rank property of the SDP problem.
Their results have since been generalized by Cifuentes in
[
11
]
to linear SDP problems with
multiple positive semidefinite block variables and inequality constraints.
One of our goals in this paper is to generalize the theory of Boumal et al. to a class of SDP problems
with an additional semidefinite upper bound constraint, where the feasible sets
of the non-convex factorized
models are no longer smooth manifolds.

The set

to the oblique manifold

In fact, later we will show that

12
]
chapter 3 definition 1). However, even though it is not a manifold, we can still derive an explicit formula to describe the tangent cones and second order tangent sets of its singular points.
More importantly, we find that one possible retraction mapping of

is related to the well known geometric median problem, which means that we can compute the retraction efficiently. These findings are crucial for our algorithmic design. We will design an algorithm that equips the underlying Riemannian optimization method with a strategy to check the global optimality of a singular point and escape any non-optimal singular point efficiently. To solve (
7
) with the spectral upper bound, we use an augmented Lagrangian method on the algebraic variety

Now, we summarize our contributions as follows:

•

We study low rank factorization of graph equipartition SDP and prove the equivalence between the factorized problem and the original convex problem in terms of global optimality. We also prove that under the constraint nondegeneracy condition
[
34
]
, any
second order stationary point of the factorized problem is a global optimal solution with probability 1. These results generalize the results of Boumal et al. from
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
29
]
since we have an extra non-smooth inequality constraint and our feasible region is not necessarily a smooth manifold. Our study here may serve as a prototype for extending the
non-convex factorization approach to other types of SDP problems whose
resulting feasible regions may not be smooth manifolds.

•

We study a special algebraic variety
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
, which is the set of zeros of the equality constraints of the factorized problem. We study the local geometric properties of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
on both smooth points and singular points. We also show that retraction is equivalent to the geometric median problem under certain conditions. These properties allow us to conduct manifold optimization methods on the smooth part of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
while escaping from non-optimal singular points of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
.

•

We develop a gradient descent method and an augmented Lagrangian method on an algebraic variety to solve graph equipartition SDP problems and conduct numerical experiments to demonstrate their high efficiency. Although manifold structures are increasingly used in algorithms
for solving SDP problems (see
[
6
,
24
,
40
]
), our novelty here is in developing algorithms to solve the SDP problems
on an algebraic variety that is not necessarily a smooth manifold.

3
An algebraic variety

In this section, we consider the case where the mapping
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)={\rm diag}(\cdot)
. The constraints of (
6
) come from two parts, one of them is the following set,

\mathcal{B}_{n,r}=\left\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:\ {\rm diag}\big(RR^{\top}\big)=e,\ R^{\top}e=0\right\}.

(32)

We remind the reader that
{\rm diag}(RR^{\top})=e
is equivalent to
\|e_{i}^{\top}R\|_{2}^{2}=1
for all

i\in[n].
We call a point
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
smooth (nonsingular) point if the linear independent constraint qualification (LICQ) holds at
R
. Otherwise we say that
R
is nonsmooth (singular). Strictly speaking, LICQ is a sufficient but not necessary condition for a point to be smooth. However, we identify smoothness and LICQ because LICQ is convenient for us to use in optimization.

Observe that
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is an algebraic variety defined as the common zeros of
n+r
polynomials (see
[
12
,
17
]
). Its tangent space at a smooth point
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is given by

T_{R}\mathcal{B}_{n,r}:=\left\{H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:\ H^{\top}e=0,\ {\rm diag}(HR^{\top})=0\right\}.

(33)

3.1
Local geometric properties of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}

From now on, we let
E^{n}:=\left\{v\in\{-1,1\}^{n}:\ e^{\top}v=0\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is not always a smooth manifold and we have the following decomposition result.

Proposition 3.1
.

\mathcal{B}_{n,r}=\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{0}\sqcup\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{1}
, where
\sqcup
denotes the disjoint union of the sets
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{0}:=\left\{vb^{\top}:\ v\in E^{n},\ b\in{\rm S}^{r-1}\right\}
and

\mathcal{B}^{1}_{n,r}:=\left\{R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}:\ {\rm rank}\left(R\right)\geq 2\right\}.
Moreover,
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{0}
contains the singular points of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
and
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{1}
contains the smooth points of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.
Note that when
n
is odd,
\mathcal{B}^{0}_{n,r}=\emptyset.

Proof.

The decomposition comes from the fact that any
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
with rank equal to 1 has the form
R=vb^{\top},
for some
v\in E^{n}
and
b\in{\rm S}^{r-1}
. For any
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
, define the linear mapping

\mathcal{A}_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:\ \rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},
such that for any
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
,
\mathcal{A}_{R}(H)=\left(H^{\top}e,{\rm diag}(HR^{\top})\right)
. Then for any
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{*}\left((\lambda,\mu)\right)=e\lambda^{\top}+{\rm diag}(\mu)R.
It is easy to see that LICQ is equivalent to that
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{*}
is an injection. For
R=vb^{\top}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{0},
we have that
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{*}\left((-b,v)\right)=0.
So,
R
is singular. For any
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{1}
, we only have to prove that
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{*}
is an injection. Suppose
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{*}\left((\lambda,\mu)\right)=e\lambda^{\top}+{\rm diag}(\mu)R=0
. If there exists
i\in[n]
such that
\mu_{i}=0
, then
\lambda=0
and
{\rm diag}(\mu)R=0.
Because
{\rm diag}(RR^{\top})=e
, we have
\mu=0.
If for all
i\in[n]
,
\mu_{i}\neq 0
, then
R=-{\rm diag}(\mu)^{-1}ea^{\top}
, which is rank 1, and we get a contradiction. Therefore,
(\lambda,\mu)=0
and
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{*}
is injective. We have that
R
is a smooth point of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
.

Proposition
3.1
shows that the only singular points in
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
are rank-1 points. We should note that when using the Burer and Monteiro factorization,
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is equivalent to
RQ
for any orthogonal
Q\in{\rm St}(r,r).
This is because
RR^{\top}=RQQ^{\top}R^{\top}.
Thus, for any singular point
R=vb^{\top}
such that
v\in E^{n}
and
b\in{\rm S}^{r-1},
there exists an orthogonal matrix
Q\in{\rm St}(r,r)
such that
RQ=ve_{1}^{\top}.
This implies that there are only finitely many classes of singular points. Moreover, the number of classes is
\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{\frac{n}{2}}
if
n
is even and
0
if
n
is odd. For convenience, we only consider singular points of the form
R=ae_{1}^{\top}
for some
a\in E^{n}.

Now we move on to characterize the tangent cone and second order tangent set at a singular point of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
in the following two propositions. We defer their proofs to Appendix C.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
r>1
and
R=ae_{1}^{\top}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is a singular point for some
a\in E^{n}
, then its tangent cone is given by:

\mathcal{T}^{i}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R)=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R)=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}:=\big\{[0,H_{1}]:H_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r-1)},e^{\top}H_{1}=0,a^{\top}{\rm diag}\big(H_{1}H_{1}^{\top}\big)=0\big\}.

(34)

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
r>1.
Let
R=ae_{1}^{\top}
be a singular point for some
a\in E^{n}.
Then for any
H=[0,H_{1}]\in\mathcal{T}^{i}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R)=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R).

(i)

If
H_{1}\notin a\mathbb{R}^{1\times(r-1)},
then

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{i,2}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R,H)=\mathcal{T}^{2}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R,H)=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(R,H)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\left[-a\circ{\rm diag}\big(H_{1}H_{1}^{\top}\big),W_{1}\right]:\ a^{\top}{\rm diag}\big(H_{1}W^{\top}_{1}\big)=0,\,e^{\top}W_{1}=0,\,W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r-1)}\Big\}.

(ii)

If
H_{1}=a\lambda^{\top}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{r-1},
then

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{i,2}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R,H)=\mathcal{T}^{2}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R,H)=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{2}(R,H)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\left[-a\circ{\rm diag}\big(H_{1}H_{1}^{\top}\big),W_{1}\right]:\ a^{\top}{\rm diag}\big(W_{1}W^{\top}_{1}\big)=0,\,e^{\top}W_{1}=0,\,W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r-1)}\Big\}.

Proposition
3.2
and
3.3
together imply that
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is second order directionally differentiable at every singular point (see definition 3.32 of
[
5
]
). With these geometric properties, we can move on to study operations on
\mathcal{B}_{n,r},
which are crucial for designing algorithms. We will first discuss some important operations on the smooth part of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
such as projection and retraction in the next two sections, while
the discussion on how to handle the singular points will be given later in Appendix B
since we rarely encounter singular points in practical computations.

3.2
Projection onto tangent spaces of smooth points on
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}

For notational convenience, let
{\rm Proj}_{R}(C):={\rm Proj}_{T_{R}\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(C).

Theorem 3.4
.

For any smooth point
\bar{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},

{\rm Proj}_{\bar{R}}(C)=J\left(C-{\rm diag}\left(\lambda_{C}^{\bar{R}}\right)\bar{R}\right),

(35)

where

\lambda_{C}^{\bar{R}}:=\Big(I+\bar{R}\left(I-\bar{R}^{\top}\bar{R}/n\right)^{-1}\bar{R}^{\top}/n\Big){\rm diag}\big(JC\bar{R}^{\top}\big).

(36)

Proof.

Suppose
\bar{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{1}
, we have

{\rm Proj}_{\bar{R}}(C):=\arg\min_{H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|H-C\|^{2}:\ H^{\top}e=0,\ {\rm diag}(H\bar{R}^{\top})=0\right\}.

(37)

Since, the feasible region in (
37
) is convex and the objective function is strongly convex, it has a unique optimal solution, say
H.
From LICQ property of
R
,
H
also satisfies LICQ. Then
H
must be a KKT solution, that is, there exists
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle H-C+e\lambda_{1}^{\top}+{\rm diag}(\lambda_{2})\bar{R}=0

(38)

\displaystyle H^{\top}e=0

(39)

\displaystyle{\rm diag}(H\bar{R}^{\top})=0

(40)

Multiplying
J
to the left hand side of (
38
), and noting that
e^{\top}H=0
, we have

H=J(C-{\rm diag}(\lambda_{2})\bar{R}).

Plug this into (
40
), we have

{\rm diag}\big(J{\rm diag}(\lambda_{2})\bar{R}\bar{R}^{\top}\big)={\rm diag}\big(JC\bar{R}^{\top}\big).

(41)

Apply the following formula to (
41
)

{\rm diag}\left(A{\rm diag}(\lambda)B\right)=\big(A\circ B^{\top}\big)\lambda,

(42)

we have

\left(I-\bar{R}\bar{R}^{\top}/n\right)\lambda_{2}={\rm diag}\big(JC\bar{R}^{\top}\big)

(43)

Since
\bar{R}\bar{R}^{\top}/n\succeq 0
and
{\rm Tr}(\bar{R}\bar{R}^{\top}/n)={\rm Tr}(I/n)=1,
we have
\|\bar{R}\bar{R}^{\top}/n\|_{2}\leq 1
and the equality holds if and only if
\bar{R}
is rank 1. Because
{\rm rank}(\bar{R})\geq 2
, we must have
I-\bar{R}\bar{R}^{\top}/n\succ 0.
From the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula, we have

\lambda_{2}=\Big(I+\bar{R}\left(I-\bar{R}^{\top}\bar{R}/n\right)^{-1}\bar{R}^{\top}/n\Big){\rm diag}\big(JC\bar{R}^{\top}\big),

(44)

which is exactly
\lambda_{C}^{\bar{R}}.

3.3
Retraction around smooth points of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}

For
\bar{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
and
H\in T_{\bar{R}}\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
, define

\mathcal{R}_{\bar{R}}(H):={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(\bar{R}+H)\in\arg\min\left\{\|R-(\bar{R}+H)\|^{2}/2:\ R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}.

(45)

Since
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is compact,
the optimization problem (
45
) always has an optimal solution, but it may not be unique as
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is non-convex. Now, we focus on the following optimization problem. Given
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},

\min\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\|R-Y\|^{2}:\ R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\Big\}.

(46)

For
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, we say
Y_{1}\sim Y_{2}
if there exists
b\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
Y_{1}=Y_{2}+eb^{\top}.
It is easy to check that
\sim
is an equivalence relation, which partitions
\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
into equivalence classes
[Y]:=\left\{Y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:\ Y_{1}\sim Y\right\}.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, and
Y_{1}\in[Y],

\arg\min\left\{\|R-Y\|^{2}/2:\ R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}=\arg\min\left\{\|R-Y_{1}\|^{2}/2:\ R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}.

(47)

Proof.

Since
Y_{1}\in[Y]
,
Y_{1}=Y+eb^{\top}
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Because for any
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
,
R^{\top}e=0
, the two objective functions in (
47
) only differs by a constant. Thus, the optimal solutions set are the same.

Now, we move on to show that problem (
46
) is related to the well known geometric median problem. This is the key point for solving (
46
) efficiently. For
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
such that every row is non-zero, define the mapping

\mathcal{O}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
such that
\mathcal{O}(Y):=Y\left({\rm Diag}(YY^{\top})\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},
which is derived from
Y
by normalizing each row of
Y
to have unit
2
-norm. For any
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},
define

\mathcal{F}(Y):=\{\mathcal{O}(Y_{1}):\,Y_{1}\in[Y]\}\,\cap\,\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, either
\mathcal{F}(Y)=\emptyset
or

\mathcal{F}(Y)=\arg\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|R-Y\|^{2}:\,R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}
and
|\mathcal{F}(Y)|=1.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset
. Then there exists
Y_{1}\in[Y]
such that
\mathcal{O}(Y_{1})\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.
Because
\mathcal{O}(Y_{1})={\rm Proj}_{{\rm OB}_{n,r}}(Y_{1})
and
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\subset{\rm OB}_{n,r},

we have
\{\mathcal{O}(Y_{1})\}=\arg\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|R-Y_{1}\|^{2}:\,R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}.
From Lemma
3.5
,
\{\mathcal{O}(Y_{1})\}=\arg\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|R-Y\|^{2}:\,R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}.
From the arbitrariness of
Y_{1}
, we have
\mathcal{F}(Y)\subset\arg\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|R-Y\|^{2}:\,R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}
. Thus,

\mathcal{O}(Y_{1})\in\mathcal{F}(Y)\subset\arg\min\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\|R-Y\|^{2}:\,R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\Big\}=\{\mathcal{O}(Y_{1})\},

and the proof is completed.

From Lemma
3.6
, we know that if
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{F}(Y)={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(Y).
The next proposition gives a sufficient and necessary condition for
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset.

Proposition 3.7
.

For any
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
,
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset
if and only if the following geometric median problem,

\min\Big\{f(b):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|y_{i}-b\|_{2}:\ b\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\Big\},

(48)

has an optimal solution
b_{0}\notin\{y_{i}:i\in[n]\}.
Here,
y_{i}^{\top}
is the
i
th row of
Y.

Proof.

Note that (
48
) is a convex optimization problem, it has an optimal solution

b_{0}\notin\{y_{i}:i\in[n]\}
if and only if

\nabla f(b)\Big|_{b=b_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{b_{0}-y_{i}}{\|b_{0}-y_{i}\|}=0.

The latter condition is equivalent to
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset.

From Proposition
3.7
and Lemma
3.6
, we may solve (
46
) by solving (
48
). Thus, a nonconvex problem is reduced to a well studied convex problem. There are many algorithms to solve (
48
) with guaranteed convergence, one simple method is the Weiszfeld algorithm (
[
41
]
), which performs the following iteration:

b_{i+1}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{y_{j}}{\|b_{i}-y_{j}\|}\right)\Big/\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\|b_{i}-y_{j}\|}\right).

(49)

However, the condition
b_{0}\notin\{y_{i}:i\in[n]\}
in Proposition
3.7
may not always hold. Consider
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
, whose rows are not collinear (not on the same line), it is well known that the geometric median of
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}
belongs to
\{y_{i}:\ i\in[3]\}
if and only if the triangle
y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}
has an angle
\geq 120^{\circ}.
This means that the condition
b_{0}\notin\{y_{i}:i\in[n]\}
may not hold with probability 1.
Moreover, the following example show that one cannot find a neighbourhood of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
such that every
Y
in it satisfies
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset.

Example 3.8
.

Consider
\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}.
For any
\epsilon>0
, define
Y_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}
such that

Y_{\epsilon}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\\
1&1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}&-1&-1+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

We have
{\rm dist}\left(Y_{\epsilon},\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right)<\epsilon
. Because the first three rows of
Y_{\epsilon}
are on the same line, the only geometric median of its rows vector is
(0,1-\frac{\epsilon}{2})^{\top}
, so
\mathcal{F}\left(Y_{\epsilon}\right)=\emptyset.
However, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
,
Y_{\epsilon}
can be arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{B}_{4,2}.

The following theorem is a sufficient condition for the geometric median method to be useful.

Theorem 3.9
.

Suppose
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n,r}
satisfies
\|Y\|_{2}<(1-\beta)\sqrt{n}
for some
0<\beta<1,
and
{\rm dist}\left(Y,\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right)<\frac{\beta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}+1}<1.
Then
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

Let
R
be an optimal solution of (
46
). Then
\|R-Y\|_{F}={\rm dist}\left(Y,\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right).

Let
\|R-Y\|_{F}=\alpha\sqrt{n}.
Then we have
\alpha<\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{n}+1}
and hence

\beta-\alpha>\alpha\sqrt{n}.

Moreover
\|R\|_{2}\leq\|Y\|_{2}+\|Y-R\|_{F}<\left(1-\beta+\alpha\right)\sqrt{n}.

Since any rank-one solution
of (
46
) must have spectral norm equal to
\sqrt{n}
, the last inequality
implies that
{\rm rank}\left(R\right)\geq 2

and

\Big\|\left(I-RR^{\top}/n\right)^{-1}\Big\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-\|RR^{\top}/n\|_{2}}<\frac{1}{1-\left(1-\beta+\alpha\right)^{2}}.

By Proposition
3.1
,
R
is smooth. So it is a KKT solution of (
46
). There exist
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the following system holds:

\displaystyle R-Y+e\mu^{\top}+{\rm diag}(\lambda)R=0

(50)

\displaystyle R^{\top}e=0

(51)

\displaystyle{\rm diag}(RR^{\top})=e

(52)

Multiplying
J
to the left hand side of (
50
) and using (
51
), we have

R=J\left(Y-{\rm diag}(\lambda)R\right).

Then

{\rm diag}(RR^{\top})={\rm diag}\big(J\left(Y-{\rm diag}(\lambda)R\right)R^{\top}\big).

Using (
52
) and (
42
), we have

\left(I-RR^{\top}/n\right)\lambda={\rm diag}\big(JYR^{\top}\big)-e.

Thus we have that

\lambda=\left(I-RR^{\top}/n\right)^{-1}\big({\rm diag}(JYR^{\top})-e\big)=\left(I-RR^{\top}/n\right)^{-1}{\rm diag}\big(J(Y-R)R^{\top}\big).

Hence

\displaystyle\|\lambda\|_{2}\leq\Big\|\left(I-RR^{\top}/n\right)^{-1}\Big\|_{2}\,\big\|{\rm diag}\big(J(Y-R)R^{\top}\big)\big\|_{2}\leq\frac{\big\|{\rm diag}\left(J(Y-R)R^{\top}\right)\big\|_{2}}{1-\left(1-\beta+\alpha\right)^{2}}.

(53)

Note that we also have

\displaystyle\Big\|{\rm diag}\big(J(Y-R)R^{\top}\big)\Big\|_{2}\leq\big\|{\rm diag}\big((Y-R)R^{\top}\big)\big\|_{2}+\big\|R(Y-R)^{\top}e/n\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|Y-R\|_{F}+\|R\|_{2}\|Y-R\|_{2}\|e\|_{2}/n<\left(2-\beta+\alpha\right)\alpha\sqrt{n},

(54)

where we used Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
{\rm diag}\left(RR^{\top}\right)=e
in the second inequality of (
54
). Plug (
54
) into (
53
), we have

\|\lambda\|_{2}\leq\frac{\alpha\sqrt{n}}{\beta-\alpha}<1.

Now, from (
50
), we have
\left(I+{\rm diag}\left(\lambda\right)\right)R=Y-e\mu^{\top}.
Since
\|\lambda\|_{2}<1
, we have
\left(I+{\rm diag}\left(\lambda\right)\right)\succ 0
. Hence
R=\mathcal{O}(Y-e\mu^{\top})
and so
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset.

Remark 3.10
.

One may get the impression that the result in Theorem
3.9
contradicts Example
3.8
. Intuitively speaking, Theorem
3.9
requires that a point’s distance to
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
is smaller than its distance to a rank-1 matrix up to some constant, which is not equivalent to saying that the matrix is sufficiently close to
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.

Theorem
3.9
is useful for designing algorithms for
solving the
k-
equipartition problem GEP1. Consider the problem in (
45
). In practice,
R
is usually far from being rank-1. Moreover, for
k\geq 3
, the
k-
equipartition problem has the constraints
\|R\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{k-1}}.
If we choose
\beta\in\big(0,\frac{\sqrt{n}+1}{\sqrt{n}+2}\big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{k-1}}\big)\big)
in Theorem
3.9

and
H
to be sufficiently small such that
{\rm dist}\left(R+H,\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right)\leq\|H\|_{F}<\frac{\beta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}+1},
then we have

\|Y\|_{2}=\|R+H\|_{2}\leq\|R\|_{2}+\|H\|_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{k-1}}+\frac{\beta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}+1}<(1-\beta)\sqrt{n}.

This means that, if
{\rm rank}\left(R\right)\geq 2
, we can first compute the retraction

\mathcal{R}_{R}(H)
by solving (
48
), with
Y=R+H
. If
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset
, then we have computed the retraction successfully, otherwise, we can perform a backtracking step:

H\leftarrow H/\sigma
for some
\sigma>1
. Theorem
3.9
tells us that after a finite number of backtracking steps,
\mathcal{F}(Y)\neq\emptyset
and the retraction computation
will be successful. Note that the condition
{\rm rank}\left(R\right)\geq 2
is usually satisfied in our experiments. Moreover, for
k\geq 3
, any feasible
R
must satisfy
{\rm rank}(R)\geq 2

since we have

\|R\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{k-1}}<\sqrt{n}
. The last inequality rules out
the possibility for
R
to have rank one since in that case its spectral norm must be
equal to
\sqrt{n}
.

Up to now, we are able to compute the retraction of a point only if it is “smooth”. It is an interesting mathematical problem to compute the retraction in the general case. Suppose
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}=vb^{\top}
such that
b\in{\rm S}^{r-1}
is a rank-1 matrix. Without loss of generality, assume that
the entries of
v
are arranged in an ascending order:
v_{1}\leq v_{2}\leq\ldots,\leq v_{n}
. If
n
is an even number, then
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}\left(Y\right)=wb^{\top},
where
w_{1}=\ldots=w_{n/2}=-1
,
w_{n/2+1}=\ldots=w_{n}=1.
This can be proved by first assuming
v_{1}<v_{2}<\ldots<v_{n}
, in which case we can apply Proposition
3.7
. Then use the continuity of
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}\left(Y\right)
to prove the case for
v_{1}\leq v_{2}\leq\ldots\leq v_{n}.

Algorithms

In this section, we consider algorithms to solve (

\mathcal{A}(\cdot)={\rm diag}(\cdot)
and

4.1
Minimum bisection

In this subsection, we consider the case
k=2
and hence
\alpha=n
. In this case,
since
{\rm diag}(RR^{\top})=e
implies that
\|R\|_{F}=\sqrt{n}
, the constraint

\|R\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\alpha}
is redundant and it can be removed.
We rewrite the minimum bisection problems as follows:

\min\Big\{\left\langle C,X\right\rangle:\ {\rm diag}\left(X\right)=e,\ Xe=0,\ \left\langle X,ee^{\top}\right\rangle=0,\ X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\Big\},

(55)

\min\Big\{f(R):=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle C,RR^{\top}\right\rangle:\ R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\Big\}.

(56)

From section 3, we know that
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
can be viewed as an affine variety with finitely many classes of singular points. In particular, for any
\delta\in(0,1),

\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
can be separated as
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta-}
and
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta+}
such that

\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta-}:=\left\{R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}:\ \|R\|_{2}<\sqrt{1-\delta}\sqrt{n}\right\},

(57)

\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta+}:=\Big\{R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}:\ \|R\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{1-\delta}\sqrt{n}\Big\}.

(58)

For any
\delta\in(0,1),
all singular points are contained in
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta+}.
If there exists some constant
\delta\in(0,1)
such that all iterations are in
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta-},
then we can use any kind of Riemannian optimization algorithm to solve (
56
). Actually, this is often the case in practice. Only in very rare situation will one encounter a singular point. For example, if the optimal solution of (
56
) is rank-1, then the iterations will approach a singular point. Note that our escaping strategy in Appendix B only works for an exact singular point. Thus, we will use a rounding procedure that will round any “nearly singular point” to a singular point and then apply Theorem
B.3
to handle it. For
\delta<\frac{1}{2},
define the
function
\texttt{Round}:\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
such that for any
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta+},

\texttt{Round}(R):=\texttt{sgn}(u)e_{1}^{\top},
where
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the singular vector (with
its first nonzero component being positive)
that corresponds to the largest singular value of
R
and
sgn
is the sign function. Note that since
\|R\|_{F}^{2}=n
for
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r},
so when
\delta<\frac{1}{2},

the leading singular value has multiplicity one and
u
must be unique.
Hence
\texttt{Round}(.)
is well-defined. We now state our algorithm.

Algorithm 1:

Choose

and
\delta_{0}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}).
Set

) such that every iteration

. If for some iteration, we obtain a point

goto Step 2.

Step 2.

set

goto Step 3.

Case 2.

and is optimal, stop.

\texttt{Round}\big(\widehat{R}_{k}\big)\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}

such that

where

\epsilon\big(\texttt{Round}(\widehat{R}_{k})\big)>0
is only related to

\texttt{Round}(\widehat{R}_{k})
. If

R\leftarrow R^{+};
Otherwise, set
R\leftarrow\widehat{R}_{k}.

Goto Step 3.

Step 3.

In Step 1, many Riemannian optimization methods with linesearch can satisfy

4.2
Graph multi-equipartition

In this section, we consider the case where
k\geq 3
. In this case, the spectral upper bound in (
6
) already implies that any feasible point
R
is a smooth point of

\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
, then we simply treat
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
as a manifold. We use an augmented Lagrangian method on
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
to solve the problem SDPLR1 in (
6
). Note that recently the convergence of ALM on manifold has been studied in
[
24
,
40
,
45
]
. We reformulate problem (
6
) as follows:

\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\big\langle L,\,RR^{\top}\big\rangle+\delta_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{r}}\left(Y\right):\ R^{\top}R+Y=\alpha I,\ R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}.

(60)

The Lagrangian function is:

\mathcal{L}\left(R,Y,Z\right):=\frac{1}{2}\big\langle L,\,RR^{\top}\big\rangle-\big\langle Z,\,R^{\top}R+Y-\alpha I\big\rangle+\delta_{\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+}}\left(Y\right)+\delta_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}\left(R\right).

(61)

Let
\Pi_{\mathbb{S}^{r}_{-}}(\cdot)
be the projection operator onto
\mathbb{S}^{r}_{-}
, the cone of

r\times r
symmetric negative semidefinite matrices.
The reduced augmented Lagrangian function is:

\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\left(R,Z\right)=\frac{1}{2}\big\langle L,\,RR^{\top}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{2\beta}\left(\|\Pi_{\mathbb{S}_{-}^{r}}\left(Z-\beta\left(R^{\top}R-\alpha I\right)\right)\|^{2}-\|Z\|^{2}\right).

(62)

\nabla_{R}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\left(R,Z\right)=LR-2R\,\Pi_{\mathbb{S}_{-}^{r}}\left(Z-\beta\left(R^{\top}R-\alpha I\right)\right).

The template of the augmented Lagrangian method is as follows:

Algorithm 2:

Choose
R_{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r},

Z_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{r},

\beta_{0}>0.
Set
k=0.

Step 1.

R_{k}\in\arg\min\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\beta_{k}}\left(R,Z_{k-1}\right):\ R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right\}.

Step 2.

Z_{k}=\Pi_{\mathbb{S}_{-}^{r}}\left(Z_{k-1}-\beta_{k}\left(R_{k}^{\top}R_{k}-\alpha I\right)\right).

Step 3.

Choose
\beta_{k+1}
, set
k\leftarrow k+1.
Goto Step 1.

Proposition 4.2
.

Any saddle point of the Lagrangian function (
61
) satisfies the first order KKT condition of (
6
).

Proof.

Suppose
\left(R,Y,Z\right)
is a saddle point of (
61
). We have

R\in\arg\min_{R^{\prime}}\left\{\mathcal{L}\left(R^{\prime},Y,Z\right)\right\},\ Y\in\arg\min_{Y^{\prime}}\left\{\mathcal{L}\left(R,Y^{\prime},Z\right)\right\},\ Z\in\arg\max_{Z^{\prime}}\left\{\mathcal{L}\left(R,Y,Z^{\prime}\right)\right\},

which implies that

\displaystyle{\rm Proj}_{R}\left(LR-2RZ\right)=0

(63)

\displaystyle Z\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+}}\left(Y\right)

(64)

\displaystyle R^{\top}R+Y-\alpha I=0.

(65)

The condition (
64
) implies that
Y\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{r},

Z\in\mathbb{S}_{-}^{r}
and
\left\langle Z,Y\right\rangle=0.
From (
65
),
RR^{\top}-\alpha I\preceq 0
and so
R\in\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}.
Since
\left\langle Z,Y\right\rangle=0
, we have that
\left\langle Z,R^{\top}R-\alpha I\right\rangle=0.

Because
Z,\ R^{\top}R-\alpha I\preceq 0
, the last complementarity
condition implies that
Z(R^{\top}R-\alpha I)=0
and hence

Z
and
R^{\top}R-\alpha I
are simultaneously diagonalizable.
By considering the SVD of
R
as in (
16
),
we have
Z=V_{1}Z_{1}V_{1}^{\top}
for some
Z_{1}\preceq 0.
So
RZ=U_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}Z_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\in-\mathcal{N}_{\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}}\left(R\right).

The condition (
63
) implies
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
and

J\left(LR-2RZ-{\rm diag}\left(\lambda_{LR-2RZ}^{R}\right)R\right)=0,

where
\lambda_{LR-2RZ}^{R}
is defined as in (
36
).
Thus,

\left(L-{\rm diag}\left(\lambda_{LR-2RZ}^{R}\right)\right)R-e\mu^{\top}=2RZ\in-\mathcal{N}_{\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}}\left(R\right),

which implies (

),
where

.
Therefore,

satisfies the first order KKT condition of (

We terminate the augmented Lagrangian method based on the relative dual feasibility,

\max\Bigg\{\frac{\|{\rm Proj}_{R}\left(LR-2RZ\right)\|_{F}}{1+\|R\|_{F}+\|Z\|_{F}},\ \frac{\|Y-{\rm Proj}_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{r}}\left(Y+Z\right)\|_{F}}{1+\|Y\|_{F}+\|Z\|_{F}}\Bigg\},

{\rm tol}

5
Numerical experiment

In this section, we conduct numerical experiments to verify the efficiency of our methods. Note that our goal is not to show that the Riemannian optimization methods in section 4 are efficient since they are already well studied the literature. Our purpose is to show that by using the geometric structure of the algebraic variety
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
, we are able to solve the graph equipartition SDP problem (
6
) much more efficiently than other methods that do not exploit the
geometric structure of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
or only partially exploiting geometric structure like oblique manifold. All the experiments were run in
Matlab
R2020b on a MacBook Pro with 1.4 GHz Quad-Core Intel Core i5 processor and 8GB RAM.

5.1
A rank adaptive strategy

In our implementation, we choose the parameter
r
adaptively to further increase computational efficiency. We first set
r=k-1+\lceil\sqrt{2(n+1)}\rceil,
which is the theoretical upper bound. Suppose
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is an iteration point with singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\ldots\geq\sigma_{r}>0.
We consider the ratio of them
\big\{\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i+1}}:\ i\in[r-1]\big\}.

If there exists
i\in[r-1]
such that
\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i+1}}>10
, then we choose

and drop
the singular values
\sigma_{j+1},\sigma_{j+2},\ldots,\sigma_{r}
and their corresponding singular vectors to save storage. We perform such rank reduction check
in every 10 steps. Note that such a rank adaptive strategy is
adopted from
[
15
]
.

5.3
Experiments on graph equipartition SDPs

In this section, we test on graph equipartition SDP problems with more than 2 partitions. Since RALM and SDPLR cannot handle the extra SDP upper bound constraint

X\preceq\alpha I
, we do not test them in the experiments.
Instead, we compare our method with the interior point method (which we denote as IPM) in section 6 of
[
38
]
that can handle the SDP upper bound constraint directly.
Note that applying the IPM in
[
38
]
to handle the semidefinite upper bound constraint directly is much more efficient than applying an IPM to the reformulated problem of converting the bound constraint to an affine constraint with an additional slack SDP variable.

We call our method AVALM where “AV” stands for “algebraic variety”. We use a Riemannian gradient method with BB step and nonmonotone linesearch to solve the ALM subproblem to the accuracy of
10^{-6}
in the relative norm of
the Riemannian gradient. We also set the maximum number of iterations for solving the subproblem to be 200. We set the initial penalty parameter
\beta=0.1
and set

\beta_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\max\{\beta_{k}/1.2,0.1\}&{\rm pfeas<dfeas/1000}\\
\min\{1.2\beta_{k},10\}&{\rm pfeas\geq\max\{dfeas/1000,10\cdot tol\}}.\end{cases}

We stop our ALM algorithm if both the primal feasibility (pfeas) and dual feasibility (dfeas)
are less than
10^{-6}.

We also stop our algorithm if there is no progress for many iterations. After we get a solution
(R,Z)
, let
{\bf S}=S_{\lambda_{LR-2RZ}}
as in (
66
), then we check the following relative KKT residues for the original convex SDP problem (
5
):

\displaystyle{\rm primal\ residue:}\ {\rm Rp}=\max\left\{\frac{\|\mathcal{A}\left(RR^{\top}\right)-b\|_{2}}{1+\|b\|_{2}},\|R\|_{2}-\sqrt{\alpha}\right\},

\displaystyle{\rm dual\ residue:}\ {\rm Rd}=\frac{\max\left\{\lambda_{\max}\left(U_{1}^{\top}{\bf S}U_{1}\right),\ \max\left\{-\lambda_{\min}\left(J\left({\bf S}-U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}{\bf S}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\right)J\right),0\right\}\right\}}{1+\|L\|_{F}},

\displaystyle{\rm complementarity:}\ {\rm Rc}=\frac{\Big|\left\langle{\bf S}-U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}{\bf S}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top},RR^{\top}\right\rangle\Big|}{1+\|L\|_{F}},

(68)

where
U_{1}
is defined in (
16
). If
\max\{{\rm Rp},{\rm Rd},{\rm Rc}\}
is small, then we have
obtained an accurate approximate optimal solution to the SDP problem (
5
).

Since the interior point method IPM cannot handle graph equipartition SDP problems
arising from
large graphs, we only compare AVALM with IPM on small datasets in
[
35
]
,
where we set the number of partitions
k=5.
From
Table
2
, we see that both AVALM and IPM can solve all of these problems accurately. However, AVALM is much more efficient than IPM, and for some instances AVALM can be 20-100 times faster.

Conclusion

In this paper, we study the SDP relaxation of a graph equipartition problem. We study the optimality condition of the low-rank factorization model of the SDP problem, which contains an
additional SDP upper bound as compared to the standard linear SDP problem. We prove the equivalence between the SDP problem and its factorized version. In addition,
we prove that under the constraint nondegeneracy condition, any rank-deficient second order stationary point of the factorized problem is a global optimal solution. To solve the SDP problem, we study the properties of a special algebraic variety
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
, which comes from adding an extra coupling affine constraint to the oblique manifold
{\rm OB}_{n,r}.
We find a closed form solution of the projection mapping onto
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
, and more importantly, we prove that the retraction is equivalent to a geometric median problem under some condition. We also study the local geometric properties of singular points on
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.
With these properties, we are able to use a gradient descent method with BB step and nonmonotone line search and an augmented Lagrangian method on the algebraic variety
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
to solve the factorized SDP problem efficiently. The results from our numerical experiments verify the high efficiency of our method. Our work shows that by making use of the geometric property of the feasible region, one can design algorithms that are significantly faster than other algorithms that do not rely on the geometric property like SDPLR or only partially rely on its geometric property like RALM.

A.1
A corollary of the implicit function theorem

Lemma A.1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a continuously differentiable mapping in a neighbourhood of
\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Suppose
F(x_{0},y_{0})=0
and
{\rm D}_{y}F(x_{0},y_{0}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a surjective linear mapping. Then there exists
\delta>0
and a continuously differentiable mapping
y:B_{\delta}(x_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
y(x_{0})=y_{0}
and
F(x,y(x))=0.
Here,
B_{\delta}(x_{0}):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ {\rm dist}(x,x_{0})<\delta\right\}.

Proof.

Since
{\rm D}_{y}F(x_{0},y_{0})
is surjective, we have that
m\geq k.
If
m=k,
then Lemma
A.1
directly follows from the implicit function theorem and the mapping
y
is unique. If
m>k
, we may find a linear mapping
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m-k}
such that the linear operator
\triangle y\rightarrow\left({\rm D}_{y}F(x_{0},y_{0})[\triangle y],\mathcal{A}(\triangle y)\right)
is a bijective mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Thus, we can define a new mapping
\widehat{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\widehat{F}(x,y):=\left(F(x,y),\mathcal{A}(y)-\mathcal{A}(y_{0})\right)
and apply the implicit function theorem to
\widehat{F}.

A.2
Properties of the rounding procedure

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
and
\delta<\frac{1}{2}.
Then for any
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}^{\delta+},

\left\|\texttt{Round}\left(R\right)\texttt{Round}\left(R\right)^{\top}-RR^{\top}\right\|\leq 2\sqrt{\delta}n.

(69)

Moreover, if
\delta<\frac{1}{n^{2}},
then
\texttt{Round}(R)\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.

Proof.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
be the unique largest singular value of
R.
Its corresponding singular vectors are
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{r}.
Then we have that

\|usv^{\top}-R\|^{2}=\|R\|^{2}-s^{2}=n-\|R\|_{2}^{2}\leq\delta n.

(70)

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\texttt{sgn}(u)-us\|=\|\texttt{sgn}(u)v^{\top}-usv^{\top}\|\leq{\rm dist}\left(usv^{\top},{\rm OB}_{n,r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\rm dist}\left(usv^{\top},\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\right)\leq\|usv^{\top}-R\|\leq\sqrt{\delta}\sqrt{n},

(71)

where the first inequality comes from the retraction of oblique manifold, the second inequality comes from
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\subset{\rm OB}_{n,r},
the third inequality comes from
R\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.
We have that,

\displaystyle\left\|\texttt{Round}\left(R\right)\texttt{Round}\left(R\right)^{\top}-RR^{\top}\right\|=\left\|\texttt{sgn}(u)\texttt{sgn}(u)^{\top}-us^{2}u^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\texttt{sgn}(u)\texttt{sgn}(u)^{\top}-\texttt{sgn}(u)su^{\top}\right\|+\left\|\texttt{sgn}(u)su^{\top}-us^{2}u^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\texttt{sgn}(u)\right\|\left\|\left(\texttt{sgn}(u)^{\top}-su^{\top}\right)\right\|+\left\|\left(\texttt{sgn}(u)-us\right)\right\|\left\|su^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{n}.\sqrt{\delta}\sqrt{n}=2\sqrt{\delta}n.

(72)

Now suppose that
\delta<\frac{1}{n^{2}}.
From (
70
),
\|usv^{\top}-R\|^{2}<1.
Since every row of
R
has unit length, we have that every entry of
u
is nonzero. Thus,
\texttt{sgn}(u)\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and
\texttt{Round}(R)=\texttt{sgn}(u)e_{1}^{\top}\in{\rm OB}_{n,r}.
From (
A.2
), we have that

\left|e^{\top}\texttt{sgn}(u)\right|=\left|e^{\top}\left(\texttt{sgn}(u)-us\right)\right|\leq\sqrt{n}\|\texttt{sgn}(u)-us\|\leq n\sqrt{\delta}<1,

(73)

where the first equality comes from that because
R^{\top}e=0
and
u
is an singular vector of
R,

e^{\top}u=0.
Because
\left|e^{\top}\texttt{sgn}(u)\right|
is an integer, we have that
\left|e^{\top}\texttt{sgn}(u)\right|=0
and so
\texttt{sgn}(u)\in E^{n}.
Therefore,
\texttt{Round}(R)=\texttt{sgn}(u)e_{1}^{\top}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,r}.

R=ae_{1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is a singular point of
\mathcal{B}_{n,r}
for some
a\in E^{n}
, and
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r-1)}
is an escaping direction of
(\ref{BCsmo})
at
R=ae_{1}^{\top}.
There exists
\delta>0
such that for any
0<|t|<\delta,

there exists
0<\beta<1
such that
\|\widehat{R}(t,H,0)\|<(1-\beta)\sqrt{n}
and

{\rm dist}\big(\widehat{R}(t,H,0),\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\big)<\frac{\beta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}+1}.

Proof.

From (
74
),

\displaystyle\widehat{R}(t,H,0)\widehat{R}(t,H,0)^{\top}=

\displaystyle\;\;aa^{\top}-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(a\,{\rm diag}\big(HH^{\top}\big)^{\top}{\rm diag}(a)+{\rm diag}(a){\rm diag}\big(HH^{\top}\big)a^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle+t^{2}HH^{\top}+O(t^{3}).

(97)

Thus, from (
96
), we have that

\lambda_{\max}\big(\widehat{R}(t,H,0)\widehat{R}(t,H,0)^{\top}\big)=n-t^{2}\big(n\|H\|^{2}-\|a^{\top}H\|^{2}\big)+O(t^{3}),

(98)

where we have used the fact that
a\in E^{n}
and so
a^{\top}a=n,

{\rm diag}(a)a=e.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on every entry of
a^{\top}H
, we have that

\|a^{\top}H\|^{2}\leq\|a\|^{2}\|H\|^{2}=n\|H\|^{2}.

(99)

The equality is attained in (
99
) if and only if
H=ab^{\top}
for some vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{r-1}.
However, this implies that the objective function value of problem (
79
) is
0,
which contradicts to that
H
is an escaping direction. Thus, we have that
\|a^{\top}H\|^{2}<n\|H\|^{2}.
From the (
98
) and relation between eigenvalue and singular value, we have that

\|\widehat{R}(t,H,0)\|_{2}=\sqrt{n}\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\left(n\|H\|^{2}-\|a^{\top}H\|^{2}\right)\right)+O(t^{3}).

(100)

Also, from (
75
), we have that

{\rm dist}\big(\widehat{R}(t,H,0),\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\big)=o(t^{2}).

(101)

(
100
) together with (
101
) implies that if we choose
\beta_{t}:=\frac{t^{2}}{4n}\left(n\|H\|^{2}-\|a^{\top}H\|^{2}\right)>0,
then there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
0<|t|<\delta,
such that
\|\widehat{R}(t,H,0)\|<(1-\beta_{t})\sqrt{n}
and

{\rm dist}\big(\widehat{R}(t,H,0),\mathcal{B}_{n,r}\big)<\frac{\beta_{t}\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}+1}.

Proposition
B.4
and Proposition

For
k
sufficiently large, we also have that

R_{k}=\left({\rm diag}\left(a\right)\sqrt{e-{\rm diag}\left(\left(t_{k}H+o(t_{k})\right)\left(t_{k}H+o(t_{k})\right)^{\top}\right)},\ t_{k}H+o(t_{k})\right),

(102)

where the square root above means componentwise operations. Note that in (
102
), the first column of
R_{k}
comes from the fact that
{\rm diag}\left(R_{k}R_{k}^{\top}\right)=e
and
R_{k}
is close enough to
R.
The Taylor expansion of (
102
) gives:

R_{k}=\left({\rm diag}\left(a\right)\Big(e-\frac{t_{k}^{2}{\rm diag}\left(HH^{\top}\right)}{2}\Big)+o(t_{k}^{2}),\ t_{k}H+o(t_{k})\right).

(103)

Comparing (
103
) with
R_{k}=\left(a+t_{k}h+o(t_{k}),\ t_{k}H+o(t_{k})\right),
we have that

h=0
and so
U=[0,H].
Also, because
e^{\top}R_{k}=0,
we have that
e^{\top}H=0
and
a^{\top}{\rm diag}\left(HH^{\top}\right)=0.
Therefore, we have that

, and hence

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}(R)\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}.

Step 2.

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathcal{T}^{i}_{\mathcal{B}_{n,r}}\left(R\right).

For any nonzero
U=[0,H]\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}},
we have that
e^{\top}H=0
and
a^{\top}{\rm diag}\left(HH^{\top}\right)=0.
Consider the following mapping
\widehat{R}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r-1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}

\widehat{R}(t,W):=\left({\rm diag}\left(a\right)\sqrt{e-{\rm diag}\left(\Big(tH+\frac{t^{2}W}{2}\Big)\Big(tH+\frac{t^{2}W}{2}\Big)^{\top}\right)},\;tH+\frac{t^{2}W}{2}\right).

It is easy to see that
\widehat{R}(t,W)\in{\rm OB}_{n,r}
for
t
small enough. Our next step is to find
W
that satisfies

In order to do this, consider the Taylor expansion of
e^{\top}\widehat{R}(t,W)
with respect to
t

\displaystyle e^{\top}\widehat{R}(t,W)=\left(a^{\top}\sqrt{e-{\rm diag}\left(\Big(tH+\frac{t^{2}W}{2}\Big)\Big(tH+\frac{t^{2}W}{2}\Big)^{\top}\right)},\;te^{\top}H+\frac{t^{2}e^{\top}W}{2}\right)

(105)

\displaystyle=\left(a^{\top}\left(e-\frac{1}{2}{\rm diag}\left(\Big(tH+\frac{t^{2}W}{2}\Big)\Big(tH+\frac{t^{2}W}{2}\Big)^{\top}\right)-\frac{1}{8}t^{4}{\rm diag}^{2}\big(HH^{\top}\big)+t^{5}g(t,W)\right),\frac{t^{2}e^{\top}W}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{t^{3}a^{\top}{\rm diag}\left(HW^{\top}\right)}{2}-\frac{t^{4}a^{\top}{\rm diag}\left(WW^{\top}\right)}{8}-\frac{t^{4}a^{\top}{\rm diag}^{2}\left(HH^{\top}\right)}{8}+t^{5}a^{\top}g(t,W),\frac{t^{2}e^{\top}W}{2}\right),

where

is bounded and
t
is small enough. Note that we have used
e^{\top}H=0
in the second equality and
a^{\top}{\rm diag}\left(HH^{\top}\right)=0,

e^{\top}a=0
in the third equality. Consider the following two cases:

Case 1.

H\notin a\mathbb{R}^{1\times(r-1)}

In this case, consider the following mapping:

(106)

We have that

and
{\rm D}_{W}F(0,0)[\triangle W]=\left(-\frac{1}{2}{a^{\top}{\rm diag}\left(H\triangle W^{\top}\right)},\frac{1}{2}{e^{\top}\triangle W}\right).

we have that

This implies that

is surjective (or full rank). From Lemma

, we have that there exists

and a continuously differentiable mapping

sufficiently small. This implies that when

Because

Case 2.

H=a\lambda^{\top}
