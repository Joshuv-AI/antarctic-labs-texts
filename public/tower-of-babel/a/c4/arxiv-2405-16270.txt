Complexity of Multiple-Hamiltonicity in Graphs of Bounded Degree
Brian Liu, Nathan Sheffield, Alek Westover

Abstract
We study the following generalization of the Hamiltonian cycle problem: Given integers a , b a,b and graph G G , does there exist a closed walk in G G that visits every vertex at least a a times and at most b b times? Equivalently, does there exist a connected [ 2 ​ a , 2 ​ b ] [2a,2b] factor of 2 ​ b ⋅ G 2b\cdot G with all degrees even? This problem is 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -hard for any constants 1 ≤ a ≤ b 1\leq a\leq b . However, the graphs produced by known reductions have maximum degree growing linearly in b b . The case a = b = 1 a=b=1 — i.e. Hamiltonicity — remains 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard even in 3 3 -regular graphs; a natural question is whether this is true for other a a , b b . In this work, we study which a , b a,b permit polynomial time algorithms and which lead to 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -hardness in graphs with constrained degrees. We give tight characterizations for regular graphs and graphs of bounded max-degree, both directed and undirected.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background and Previous Work

The Hamiltonian cycle problem asks, given a graph
G
, whether
G
admits a closed walk visiting every vertex exactly once.
A classic result of Garey, Johnson and Tarjan is that Hamiltonicity is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even in graphs of max degree 3, and in fact even in 3-regular graphs
[
GJT76
]
.

In 1990, Broersma and Göbel proposed two variants of the Hamiltonian cycle problem: for fixed
k
, is there a closed walk that visits each vertex exactly
k
times, or at least once and at most
k
times, respectively? They showed that for constant
k
, both of these problems are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
BG90
]
. Jackson and Wormald subsequently proved that both problems remain
\mathsf{NP}
-hard in
j
-connected graphs for any constant
j

[
JW90
]
. These proofs extend to give hardness of the following generalization:

Definition 1
.

For fixed integers
1\leq a\leq b
, a graph
G
is called
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
if there exists a closed walk on
G
that visits each vertex at least
a
times and at most
b
times.

Being
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
can be considered as a graph factor problem. Letting
2b\cdot G
be the multigraph obtained by making
2b
copies of each edge in
G
,
G
is
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
if and only if
2b\cdot G
contains a
spanning even
[2a,2b]
factor
; that is, a connected subgraph containing all vertices of
G
, with every degree even and in
[2a,2b]
. The equivalence of this formulation follows from taking an Euler tour.

There is a substantial body of combinatorics work seeking sufficient conditions for the existence of graph factors and walks; see Plummer’s survey
[
Plu07
]
, the book by Akiyama and Kano
[
AK11
]
, or the survey by Kouider and Vestergaard which focuses specifically on connected factors
[
KV05
]
. For example, Gao and Richter showed that any
3
-connected planar graph is
[1,2]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}

[
GR94
]
, and Schmid and Schmidt gave a polynomial-time algorithm to find such a walk in which only the 3-separators are visited twice
[
SS18
]
. Several variants of the problem have also been consider from an algorithmic complexity standpoint. Ganian et al consider the case where
a
is nonconstant in
n
, showing that
[a,a]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard for
a\leq O(n^{1-\varepsilon})
, but in
{\mathsf{RP}}
for
a\geq\Omega(n/\log n)

[
Gan+19
]
. Nishiyama et al show that there is a polynomial-time algorithm to determine whether a graph has a walk visiting every vertex an odd number of times, but that the problem becomes
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard when each edge can be traversed at most
3
times
[
Nis+18
]
.

In this work, we will study the complexity of
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in regular graphs and graphs of constant maximum degree. Prior papers have investigated degree conditions sufficient to guarantee
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
— for instance Kouider and Vestergaard showed that, for
a\geq 2
, any 2-connected graph with minimum degree
an/(a+b)
is
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}

[
KV04
]
, and independent papers by Gao and Richter, Jin and Li respectively showed that any
2
-connected graph with maximum degree
2b-2
is
[1,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}

[
JL04
,
Kai+07
]
. However, to our knowledge we are the first to consider the algorithmic complexity of
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in general bounded degree graphs.

1.2
Results

Our main results are tight characterizations for the hardness of
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in regular graphs, graphs of bounded max-degree, and bounded max-degree digraphs. Interestingly, the thresholds for which
a,b
lead to
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness are very different in each of these settings. In regular graphs, we find that hardness is determined by the maximum visit count
b
in comparison to the degree
d
, where the parity of
d
is very important:

1
.

When restricted to
d
-regular graphs,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard if either

d
is even and
b<d/2
, or

d
is odd and
b<d
.
Otherwise,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}\in{\mathsf{P}}
.

In graphs of bounded max degree, we find instead that hardness is determined by the ratio of
b
to
a
in comparison to the max degree
d
, and that
a=1
is a special case:

2
.

When restricted to graphs of maximum degree
d
,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard if either

a=1
and
b<d
, or

a>1
and
\frac{b}{a}<d-1
.
Otherwise,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}\in{\mathsf{P}}
.

For directed graphs,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
turns out to always be hard in graphs of maximum degree
4
, and hard in graphs of maximum degree
3
unless
a=b
or
b=1
:

3
.

In directed graphs of maximum degree
3
, for
1\leq a\leq b
,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}\in{\mathsf{P}}
if
a=b>1
, and
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard otherwise.

4
.

For all
b\geq a\geq 1
,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard in directed graphs of maximum degree 4.

The hardness proofs for maximum degree digraphs easily extend to show hardness in regular digraphs.

1.3
Preliminaries

Introducing Depletors

Now we introduce the notion of a
depletor
gadget which will be used in all of our hardness reductions.
We introduce this notion by showing that
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is hard in unrestricted graphs.

Lemma 1

(Broersma, Göbel
[
BG90
]
)
.

In unrestricted graphs, for any constants
1\leq a\leq b
,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

Proof.

We reduce from
[1,1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
(i.e. the standard Hamiltonian cycle problem). Given a graph
G
, we build a new graph
G^{\prime}
by gluing to each vertex
b-1
triangles, as shown in
Figure

1
. In any
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle each of these triangles must be visited — this accounts for
b-1
of the visits for each of the vertices in
G
, so the rest of the walk when restricted to
G
must be a Hamiltonian cycle. For the other direction, note that it is possible to satisfy the visit requirements of the each triangle by stepping up to it, walking back and forth
a-1
times, and then stepping back down, contributing only
1
additional visit to the vertex we attached the triangles to. So, if
G
is Hamiltonian then
G^{\prime}
is
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
.
∎

Figure 1:
Reducing
[1,1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
to
[3,3]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
.

In general a depletor gadget is some small graph that we glue to the starting graph
G
that forces vertices to be visited many times. In
1
, triangles are serving as depletors, since they each contribute a visit to the vertex they’re glued to. In future sections, we will need depletors with stronger properties, such as forcing an attachment edge to be traversed many times.

Introducing Labellings

Given an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle, we can label each edge of the graph
G
with the number of times it’s traversed in the cycle. These labels satisfy the following properties:

•

the sum of the labels on the edges adjacent to each vertex is an even number at least
2a
and at most
2b
, and

•

the subgraph on edges with non-zero labels is connected.

Now, given such a labeling, we could create a multigraph by including each edge with multiplicity given by its label. Since all vertices in this multigraph have even degree, we can (efficiently) find an Eulerian cycle in the multigraph, which is then an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle in the original graph. So, finding a labeling with these properties is equivalent to finding an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle; this perspective will make the problem easier to reason about.

A Technical Note

Finally, we remark that, throughout this paper, degree bounds will be assumed to
be greater than or equal to
3
. All problems we consider are trivial in graphs
of maximum degree
2
, and so we will refrain from mentioning this special case.
We will use “maximum degree” to refer to any upper bound on the degrees of a
graph. In particular, a graph with no degree larger than
d
is a max-degree
d

graph even if it does not have a vertex achieving that degree. In directed
graphs, degree refers to the sum of in-degree and out-degree.

Another piece of notation that is important to note is that we will write
x\mod y
to denote the remainder when
x
is divided by
y
(as opposed to the equivalence class
x+y\mathbb{Z}
).

2
Complexity characterization in
d
-regular graphs

2.1
Easiness

We claim that
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
can be solved in polynomial time for
d
-regular graphs, as long as either
d
is even and
b\geq d/2
, or
d
is odd and
b\geq d
. The reason for this is simple: the following two lemmas demonstrate that
any
connected
d
-regular graph is
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
for such
b
.

Lemma 2
.

For even
d
, any
d
-regular graph is
[d/2,d/2]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
. For odd
d
, any
d
-regular graph is
[d,d]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
.

Proof.

If
d
is even, then the graph is Eulerian — taking an Euler tour will visit every vertex exactly
d/2
times. If
d
is odd, we can duplicate all its edges to get a
2d
-regular multigraph — an Euler tour in that multigraph visits every vertex exactly
d
times.
∎

Now we use Tutte’s two-factor theorem to extend
2
to more
[a,b]
. Specifically, the form of Tutte’s two-factor theorem that we use is:

Fact 1
.

If
H
is a
2k
-regular multigraph, its edges can be partitioned into
k
distinct
2
-factors (i.e.
2
-regular sub-multigraphs).

Using
1
we obtain:

Lemma 3

(Broersma, Göbel
[
BG90
]
)
.

If a graph
G
is
[b,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
for some
b
, then it’s also
[b^{\prime},b^{\prime}]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
for all
b^{\prime}>b
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the multigraph corresponding to a
[b,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
walk on
G
. This is a
2b
-regular multigraph, so we can find a multigraph two-factor by Tutte’s two-factor theorem. Duplicating all edges in that two-factor, we have a multigraph corresponding to a
[b+1,b+1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
walk on
G
. Repeating this process gives the desired claim.
∎

Since a
[b,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle is also an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle for
a<b
, we’ve shown the easiness part of the claim.

2.2
Hardness

2.2.1
Depletors

The first step in the hardness argument is to construct depletors. We would like a small structure that can always be part of an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle as long as it’s visited at least once.

We will describe such a
d
-regular depletor for even
d
with two attachment edges and
2d
vertices. Add an edge between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
whenever
(i-j)\mod 2d\in\{-d/2,\dots,d/2\}
. Now, cut the edges
(v_{2d},v_{3d/2})
and
(v_{1},v_{d/2+1})
, and add the edge
(v_{3d/2},v_{d/2+1})
— note that this is in fact a new edge since

(3d/2-d/2-1)\bmod 2d\not\in\{-d/2,\dots,d/2\}.

So, if we add an edge leaving the gadget from each of
v_{2d}
and
v_{1}
, the construction will be
d
-regular.

Labellings corresponding to
[b,b]
walks on 4-regular and 5-regular depletors, respectively.

Lemma 4
.

For any
b\geq a\geq 1
, there is a valid
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
traversal of this even-regularity depletor in which each attachment edge is traversed exactly once.

Proof.

Assign each edge
(v_{i},v_{i+1})
of the gadget to be traversed
b
times, and assign the edge
(v_{2d},v_{1})
to be traversed
b-1
times; see
Figure

2
.
∎

Note that this gadget required two attachment edges. This is a necessary consequence of the regularity constraint: since
d
is even, there must be an even number of half-edges within the gadget, meaning that the number of edges leaving the gadget must be even.

When
d
is odd, we can construct a
d
-regular depletor needing only one
leaving edge. Start with the complete graph
K_{d+1}
, and then delete all
edges
(v_{i},v_{i+(d+1)/2})
except for the edge
(v_{1},v_{(d+1)/2+1})
.
At this point, every vertex has degree
(d-1)
, except for
v_{1}
and
v_{(d+1)/2+1}
, which have degree
d
. So, we add a
(d+2)
-th vertex
v^{*}
, with edges
to every other vertex except
v_{1}
and
v_{(d+1)/2+1}
, and a single edge
leaving the construction — note that the result is now
d
-regular.

Lemma 5
.

For any
b\geq 2
, there is a valid
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
traversal of this odd-regularity depletor in which the attachment edge is traversed exactly twice.

Proof.

Assign all the edges in the loop passing through all vertices to be traversed
b-1
times, and then add one more traversal to the edges
(v_{i},v_{(i+1)\bmod d+2})
; see
Figure

2
.
∎

2.2.2
Reductions

These depletor gadgets let us use the construction from
1
to resolve the case of even
d
.

Lemma 6
.

For even
d
, if
a\leq b<d/2
, then
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard in
d
-regular graphs.

Proof.

We reduce from
[1,1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in
(d-2b+2)
-regular graphs, which is known to be
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard as long as
(d-2b+2)>2
— i.e. as long as
b<d/2
. Given a
(d-2b+2)
-regular graph
G
, we construct
G^{\prime}
by attaching
(b-1)
copies of the
d
-regular depletor of
4
to each vertex. Recall that this depletor required two attachment edges, so
G^{\prime}
is
d
-regular. But now, note that in the process of visiting each depletor gadget once, each vertex from
G
must be visited at least
b-1
times, leaving room for only
1
additional visit. So, if
G
was not Hamiltonian,
G^{\prime}
is not
[a,b]
-Hamiltonian for any
a
. Conversely, if
G
was Hamiltonian, then by
4
we can extend the Hamiltonian cycle to a
[b,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
cycle by visiting each depletor exactly once.
∎

This argument is not immediately sufficient for the case of odd regularity, however. Applying the same construction would yield the following:

Lemma 7
.

For odd
d
, if
a\leq b<d-1
, then
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard in
d
-regular graphs.

To be tight with our easiness results, however, we would like to show hardness of
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
when
b<d
. The gap here is coming from our base case: the hard problem we have to reduce from in
3
-regular graphs is
[1,1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
, whereas we need to show that even
[1,2]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
and
[2,2]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
are hard. Fortunately, a slightly more involved reduction closes this gap.

Lemma 8
.

For odd
d
, if
a\leq d-1
, then
[a,d-1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard in
d
-regular graphs.

Proof.

We reduce from
[1,1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in
3
-regular graphs. Let
G
be a
3
-regular graph; we construct
G^{\prime}
as follows:

•

Glue
d-3
depletors to each vertex in
G
.

•

Trisect each of
G
’s original edges — that is, replace them with paths containing
2
intermediate nodes.

•

Glue
d-2
depletors to each of the intermediate nodes just added.

Using the
d
-regular depletors of
5
, this construction is
d
-regular. We would like to show that
G^{\prime}
is
[d-1,d-1]
Hamiltonian if
G
is Hamiltonian, and otherwise not even
[1,d-1]
Hamiltonian — this suffices to prove the lemma.

Figure 3:
Reduction from
[1,1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in
3
-regular graphs to
[4,4]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in
5
-regular graphs. Red outgoing edges represent depletors.

First, note that if you ignore the depletors, each intermediate node must be visited exactly once, since the depletors contribute
d-2
of the
d-1
allowed visits. Thus, each edge gadget must be labeled either
1,1,1
or
2,0,2
; see
Figure

4
.

Figure 4:
The two possible labelings of the edge gadget.

The depletors contribute
d-3
visits to each of the original vertices, so each has an additional label budget of only
4
; this means that for every original vertex, exactly two of the three incident edge gadgets must be labeled
1,1,1
. So, the edges of
G
corresponding to
1,1,1
edge gadgets in
G^{\prime}
form a 2-factor; see
Figure

5
. But now, suppose this
2
-factor had multiple connected components. Since
2,0,2
edges do not create connectivity, this would mean that the labeling of
G^{\prime}
was not connected, and so couldn’t correspond to a
[1,d-1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
walk. Thus,
G^{\prime}
can be
[1,d-1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
only if
G
had a connected 2-factor, i.e. a Hamiltonian cycle.

The edge gadgets labeled
1,1,1
must correspond to a Hamiltonian path.

The reverse direction follows by the same logic: given a Hamiltonian cycle in
G
, we label all edge gadgets corresponding to the cycle
1,1,1
and all edge gadgets not in the cycle
2,0,2
; visiting each depletor once gives a
[d-1,d-1]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
labeling.

∎

Combining the above lemmas gives a complete characterization of the hardness of
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in regular graphs.

Theorem 1
.

When restricted to
d
-regular graphs,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard if either
d
is even and
b<d/2
, or
d
is odd and
b<d
. Otherwise,
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}\in{\mathsf{P}}
.

3
Complexity characterization in graphs of max-degree
d

We now have a tight understanding of when
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
is hard in regular graphs. If, instead, we just cared about graphs with constant maximum degree, the hardness results would translate, but easiness would not. We show in this section that in fact the true threshold for hardness in this setting looks very different: complexity is determined not by
b
but by the ratio
\frac{b}{a}
.

For our easiness result, we will note that testing
[a,b]
-Hamiltonicity is easy in trees, and then show that when
\frac{b}{a}\leq d-1
, for
a>1
, any failure of
[a,b]
-Hamiltonicity is witnessed by a tree-like subgraph. To show hardness, we will exploit a few low-degree vertices to construct and fine-tune highly efficient depletor gadgets.

3.1
Easiness

First, we observe that without loss of generality an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
walk never traverses a given edge more than
2a
times:

Lemma 9
.

Let
G
be a graph. If
G
has an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
labeling, it has such a labeling with maximum label at most
2a
.

Proof.

Take such an
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
walk, and consider an edge with label more than
2a
. Each of the vertices incident to that edge is therefore visited at least
a+1
times by the walk — so, if we subtract
2
from the label of that edge, each of them will still be visited at least
a
times, and the labeling will still be valid.
∎

Now, we give an algorithm for
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in trees, which will be an important component of our algorithm for
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in general graphs.

Lemma 10
.

Let
b\geq a\geq 1
, and let
G
be a tree. There is a linear-time algorithm to determine whether
G
is
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
.

Proof.

Arbitrarily root the tree. We will work from the bottom-up, recording for each edge the set of label values that can extend to a valid labeling in the subtree below it.
In fact, we will show that for each edge
e
the set of labels for
e
that can extend to a valid labelling in the subtree below
e
is of the form
\left\{\ell_{e},\ell_{e}+2,\ldots,u_{e}\right\}
for even integers
\ell_{e},u_{e}
. Thus, it will suffice to record for each edge
e
the minimum and maximum labels
\ell_{e},u_{e}
which can extend to valid labellings in the subtree below
e
.

Start by setting
\ell_{e}=2a,u_{e}=2b
for each edge
e
incident to a leaf of the tree: these edges must be traversed at least
2a
times in order to visit the leaves
a
times.
Now we repeatedly apply the following process to find
u_{e},\ell_{e}
for all edges in the tree:

Find a vertex
v
such that all edges in the subtree rooted at
v
have been labelled.
Let
L_{v},U_{v}
be the sum of
\ell_{e^{\prime}},u_{e^{\prime}}
respectively over edges
e^{\prime}
going down from
v
.
Let
e
be the edge going up the tree from
v
.
We claim that the valid labels for
e
are precisely the even numbers in the interval
[\ell_{e},u_{e}]
defined by

\ell_{e}=\max(2,2a-U_{v}),\quad u_{e}=2b-L_{v}.

First we argue that labelling for
e
must lie in this set; then we will argue that any labels in this set can be extending to labellings on the subtree.
The label on
e
must be even because all of the edges going down from
v
have even labels (by induction). The label on
e
cannot be zero, or else the labelling is not connected.
The label on
e
must be at least
2a-U_{v}
or else vertex
v
is not visited enough times.
The label on
e
cannot be larger than
2b-L_{v}
or else vertex
v
is visited too many times.
It is clear that for any even
k\in[\ell_{e},u_{e}]
, if we give
e
label
k
, then there is a compatible labelling of the subtree rooted at
v
.

If at any step in the process an edge
e
is given
\ell_{e}>u_{e}
, then we deduce that there is no valid labelling of the tree.
Otherwise, the process will terminate with every edge having
\ell_{e},u_{e}
with
\ell_{e}\leq u_{e}
.
To finish our check of whether the tree is
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
we simply iterate over all possible ways of assigning labels to the edges incident to the root, taken from their corresponding allowed sets
[\ell_{e},u_{e}]\cap 2\mathbb{Z}
.
If there is any combination of labels of the edges incident to the root that satisfies the root, then by construction of the allowed label sets, we can extend the labellings given to each
e
incident to the root to the entire subtree below
e
. Thus, we can label the entire tree.
∎

We now use this procedure for trees as part of an algorithm for
[a,b]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}
in general graphs in the case
a>1
. Afterwards we will analyze the case

Complexity characterization in directed graphs

In contrast to the case of undirected graphs, where we found that sufficiently large size of the

Just as in the directed case, we can think of the problem of

if and only if there is a connected labeling of the edges such that every vertex has in-label sum

and out-label sum

4.1
Easiness

First we give a simple algorithm for the easy cases of

in directed graphs.

Lemma 16
.

For

Proof.

Fix a directed graph of maximum degree

If any vertex has in-degree or out-degree

Our algorithm for

[a,a]{\mathsf{\text{-}HAM}}

Since

during the initial phase, if connectivity fails then no labeling can achieve connectivity.
