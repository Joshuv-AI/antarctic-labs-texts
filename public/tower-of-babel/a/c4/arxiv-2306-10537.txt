Asymptotic results for nonparametric regression estimators after sufficient dimension reduction estimation
Liliana Forzani, Daniela Rodriguez, Mariela Sued

Abstract
Prediction, in regression and classification, is one of the main aims in modern data science. When the number of predictors is large, a common first step is to reduce the dimension of the data. Sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) is a well established paradigm of reduction that keeps all the relevant information in the covariates X X that is necessary for the prediction of Y Y . In practice, SDR has been successfully used as an exploratory tool for modelling after estimation of the sufficient reduction. Nevertheless, even if the estimated reduction is a consistent estimator of the population, there is no theory that supports this step when non-parametric regression is used in the imputed estimator. In this paper, we show that the asymptotic distribution of the non-parametric regression estimator is the same regardless if the true SDR or its estimator is used. This result allows making inferences, for example, computing confidence intervals for the regression function avoiding the curse of dimensionality.

Introduction

The prediction of a response
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}
based on a random vector
\mathbf{X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is one of the main goals in modern science and may be achieved estimating the conditional mean function
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})
. When the number of predictors
p

is large, most methods include some
type of dimension reduction for
\mathbf{X}
.
Sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) has proven to be a powerful tool for this task, extracting sufficient information
in regression and classification settings (see
cook2009regression
and references thereby).
SDR includes a family of methods that seek a reduction
R(\mathbf{X})
, where
R:{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d<p
, that capture all the information that
\mathbf{X}
contains about
Y
in the sense that
Y\mid\mathbf{X}\sim Y\mid R(\mathbf{X})
. In particular, this implies that
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid R(\mathbf{X}))
.

There are three general paradigms for determining and estimating a sufficient reduction. The first of them is based on moments or functions of moments
of the conditional distribution of
\mathbf{X}\mid Y
(SIR,
li91
; SAVE,
cookweisberg
; pHd,
li92
,
PIR,
bura01
; MAVE,
Xia02
,
CookLi
,
CookNi
,
Li05
,
Zhu06
; DR,
LiWang
). The second methodology postulates a parametric model for the inverse regression of
\mathbf{X}
on
Y
. In this model-based approach, minimal sufficient reductions can be determined from the model itself (
Cook2007
;
cook_forzani_2008
;
Cook:2009aa
;
cook_forzani_2009
;
Cook:2008aa
;
adragni2009sufficient
;
Pfeiffer:2012aa
;
Zhong:2015aa
;
BuraDuarteForzani2016
;
Tomassi:2017aa
).
Under this setting, the SDR,
R(\mathbf{X})
, is not necessary linear on
\mathbf{X}
as in the case of moment based methodology (see
BuraDuarteForzani2016
).
Finally, if the target is
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})
, instead of the whole conditional distribution
Y\mid\mathbf{X}
,
li2003dimension
developed methods to get linear reductions under mild conditions. In the same lines, recently
cook2021pls
found that under a non-linear forward model, Partial least square (PLS) reduction gives sufficient linear combinations to estimate
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X}).

Reducing the dimension of
\mathbf{X}
to predict
Y
is desirable because it makes statistical methods more efficient.
For instance, the performance of nonparametric regression techniques, such as Nadaraya
nadaraya1964estimating
- Watson
watson1964smooth
,
depends on the dimension of the predictor.
Thus, using such methods on a reduction should result in better performance than if those procedures were applied without reducing.
Namely, estimators based on
(\mathbf{X}_{i},Y_{i})
gives rise to estimations with
\sqrt{nh^{p}}
rates, while using
(R(\mathbf{X}_{i}),Y_{i})
, allows to attempt
\sqrt{nh^{d}_{n}}
rate and, in this way,
the course of dimensionality may be broken.
However, even when the reduction can be characterized in terms of the joint distribution of
(\mathbf{X},Y)
, it must be estimated.
In practice,
(\widehat{R}(\mathbf{X}_{i}),Y_{i})
is available to predict where
\widehat{R}
stands for an estimator of the reduction
R
.
This is the way sufficient dimension reduction has been used by practitioners, to great success. However, no theoretical results are available to quantify the impact of using the estimated reduction in lieu of the true one in this non parametric setting.
For instance, in parametric frameworks this problem has been addressed by
kim2020post
. They show that treating the estimated sufficient predictors as the true ones leads to distorted results when performing inference about the finite dimensional parameter of interest. Prediction after reduction is not discussed in their work. The only asymptotic
theoretical results related to prediction after reduction is the one given by
forzani2019sufficient
when using the model based paradigm to get the reduction. Using a prediction method
developed by
adragni2009sufficient
they prove that
when
\mathbf{X}\mid Y
follows a generalized linear model.

The main goal of this work is to prove that nonparametric estimators based on estimated reductions perform as well as if the true reduction were used. As we discuss below, and in contrast to the parametric setting described, the imputation of an estimated reduction with better rate than that of the regression method allows to neglect the impact of the imputation. Up to now, only heuristic grounds were presented to justify this fact and here we provide a rigorous theoretical proof, filling an important gap which remained open up to now.

In Section
2
we defined an estimator of
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})
using the estimated reduction.
In Section
3

the asymptotic behaviour of the estimator is given. The uniform consistency
is also discussed at the end of this section. In Section
4
we show how the existing estimators of the reduction can be used to fit the framework developed in this work.
We report the results of a simulation study in Section
5.1
.
In Section
5.3
, using the example considered in
cook2018introduction
;
basa2023asymptotic
;
cook2023libro
,
we show how to compute in practice approximate confidence intervals for the conditional mean.

2
Estimating the regression function after a linear reduction

Consider
(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
. Assume that there exists
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
such that

\mathbb{E}({Y\mid\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{X}}),

(1)

and therefore,
R(\mathbf{X})=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{X}
is a sufficient reduction for
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})
.
Let
\mathbf{W}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define

\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}_{0})\;,\quad\xi(\mathbf{w})=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{w}).

(2)

Thus, for
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{0}
with fixed
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have that

\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\xi(\mathbf{w}_{0})
. We are interested in estimating
\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0})
for a given
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Note that if
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
were known,
we could estimate
\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\xi(\mathbf{w}_{0})
with

\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{w}_{0})=\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}K\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{W}_{i}}{h_{n}}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{W}_{i}}{h_{n}}\right)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}K\left(\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{X}_{i}}{h_{n}}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{X}_{i}}{h_{n}}\right)},

(3)

where
h_{n}>0
. In this case, under the conditions established in Theorem
4
, it is known that
\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{w}_{0})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{p}}\xi(\mathbf{w}_{0})=\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0})
and
\sqrt{nh_{n}^{d}}\{\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{w}_{0})-\xi(\mathbf{w}_{0})\}
converges in distribution to a Gaussian law.

\textcolor
blackAn exhaustive review of these results is unfeasible or, at least, out of the scope of this work. For the sake of completeness, inspired by
ziegler2003asymptotic
we include the proof of this result.

In practice,
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
is unknown and thus the estimator
\color
black
\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{w}_{0})
with
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{0}
cannot be computed.
Guided by the plug-in spirit, we input an estimator
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}
of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
and define

\widehat{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}K\left(\frac{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{X}_{i})}{h_{n}}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{X}_{i})}{h_{n}}\right)}.

(4)

In this work we establish that, under regularity conditions stated in Theorem
1
,

\widehat{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is asymptotically equivalent to
\widehat{\xi}(\mathbf{w}_{0})
as far as
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0})=O_{P}(1)
.
Thus, the reduction drastically improves the rate of convergence of the estimator without losing efficiency when the estimated reduction is used instead of the true one.

Assumptions and main results

Let
(\mathbf{X}_{i},Y_{i})
,
i\geq 1
, be independent and identically distributed copies of
(\mathbf{X},Y)
.
Consider
\mathbf{W}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{0}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}
are a fixed matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
and a point in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, respectively. Define

\displaystyle\xi(\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{w}),

\displaystyle\xi_{2}(\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{2}\mid\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{w}),

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left\{Y-\xi(\mathbf{W})\right\}^{2}\mid\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{w}\right).

(5)

3.1
Pointwise consistency and asymptotic normality

We state the assumptions needed regarding the joint distribution of
(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

A.1

\mathbf{W}
has a density
f_{\mathbf{W}}
, which is continuous and positive at
\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

A.2

There exists
\delta>0
such that
\mathbb{E}(|Y|^{2+\delta})<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}(|Y|^{2+\delta}\mid\mathbf{W}=\cdot)
is continuous at
\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

A.3

\xi
,
\xi_{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
, defined in (
3
), are continuous functions at
\mathbf{w}_{0}
and
\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{w}_{0})>0
.

A.4

There exists
q\geq 2
such that
f_{\mathbf{W}}
and
\xi
are of class
C^{q}
on an open convex set containing
\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

A.5

\mathbb{E}(Y^{2}\|\mathbf{X}\|^{2})<\infty
and, for
a=0,1
,

\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{a}\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{W}=\cdot\right)
,
\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2a}\|\mathbf{X}\|^{2}\mid\mathbf{W}=\cdot\right)
and
\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2a}\|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{X}\|^{2}\mid{\mathbf{W}}=\cdot\right)

are continuous at
\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

Now, we present the conditions to be satisfied by the kernel.

K.1

The kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded, integrable with
\displaystyle{\lim_{\|\mathbf{w}\|\to\infty}\|\mathbf{w}K(\mathbf{w})\|=0}
, and

\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(\mathbf{w})d\mathbf{w}=1}.

(6)

K.2

\int_{\mathcal{R}^{d}}\mathbf{v}^{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}K(\mathbf{v})\;d\mathbf{v}=0\;,\hbox{for all ${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$ with $1\leq|{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}|\leq q-1\!\!$},

for
q
considered in A.4.

K.3

K(\mathbf{u})=k(\|\mathbf{u}\|)
, for
k:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with two continuous derivative and compact support satisfying

\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k^{\prime}\left(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\right)\frac{\mathbf{w}^{T}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\;d\mathbf{w}=0.}

K.4

There exists a positive constant
C>0
such that
|k^{\prime}(t)|\leq C|t|
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

An example of such a kernel for
q=2
is given by
K(\mathbf{x})=k(||\mathbf{x}||)
with
k(s)=c(1-s^{2})^{3}I_{(-1<s<1)}
where
c
is chosen so that (
6
) holds.

Condition K.1 includes requirement (
6
), which is crucial for non parametric density estimation. However, in the regression setting, the normalization factor required to satisfy (
6
) cancels both expressions (
3
) and (
4
).
Thus, in practice, the normalization condition is not needed
but it makes proofs more elegant.
Condition K.3 implies K.1, but we decided to include both of them to emphasize at which point of the proofs each one is used. K.1 and K.2 are typically assumed in non parametric settings.
K.3 and K.4 are needed to control the effect of substituting
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
with
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0})=O_{P}(1)
. Under A.1-A.5 and K.1-K.4 the estimator
\widehat{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, defined in (
4
), consistently estimates
\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0})
if
h_{n}\to 0
and
nh^{d}_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
; i.e.

\widehat{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{p}}\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0}).

(7)

For
d=1
, we also assume that
nh^{2}\to\infty
.
Additionally, if
nh_{n}^{d+2q}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we get that

\sqrt{nh^{d}_{n}}\left\{\widehat{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\eta(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{D}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{w}_{0})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K^{2}(\mathbf{u})\;d\mathbf{u}}{f_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{w}_{0})}\right)\;.

(8)

The relation between
n
and
h_{n}
in Theorem
1
is that which is typically assumed in the non parametric regression framework when the predictors are in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, in this work we prove that non parametric post dimension reduction procedures based on the estimated reduction behave as well as those based on the true reduction, in the sense that are asymptotically equivalent. This is a consequence of the different rates of convergence of each procedure. Namely, the
\sqrt{nh^{d}}
rate of the non parametric technique kills the impact of replacing the true reduction by its estimator. I.e., it has a better rate of convergence, which is
\sqrt{n}
in the present case. This is a drastic difference from the parametric setting presented in
kim2020post
, where the use of the estimated sufficient predictors as the true predictors for parametric inference impacts the asymptotic variance of the estimator. For instance, the results presented in the Appendix (Theorem
4
and Proposition
1
) show that we can also estimate non-parametrically the density of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mathbf{X}\mid Y=a
, for
a=0,1
, based on
(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{i},Y_{i})
, with the same rates as if the reduction were known. This can be used in classification tasks by invoking a generative approach, i.e. when the conditional distribution of
X\mid Y
is used instead of
Y\mid X
.

Uniform Consistency

Theorem
1
guarantees the consistency of the non-parametric estimation under some conditions.
In this section we will show that this convergence is preserved when taking the supreme with
\mathbf{x}_{0}
varying in a compact set
\mathcal{K}
if certain conditions are met. The proof is based on results established in the empirical processes literature.
We will assume that:

S.1

K(m)=k(\|m\|)
, for

S.2

The density function

of

is continuous in

and for every compact set

(11)

S.3

The regression function

is continuous
in

.

Note that S.1 is satisfied, for example, if

.

Assume that
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}\overset{P}{\rightarrow}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}

be a compact set.
If either

1.

There exists a constant
C
such that

with probability one,

and

2.

\mathbb{E}Y^{2}<\infty
,

Projections

In the literature of SDR, given
(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
there are methods to characterize
an orthogonal projection of rank
d<p
,
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, such that

Y\mid\mathbf{X}\sim Y\mid\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{0}\mathbf{X}.

(12)

See for example
li2003dimension
;
cook_forzani_2008
;
cook2021pls
;
li91
;
cookweisberg
;
li92
;
bura01
;
Xia02
;
Li05
;
CookNi
;
Zhu06
;
CookLi
;

.
In particular, (
12
) implies

\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{0}\mathbf{X}).

(13)

Therefore for any fixed
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
,
with

\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}^{T}=I_{d}

.

Asymptotically normal estimators
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}
of

, satisfying (

), are presented in the same literature. However, nothing is mentioned about the existence of
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}

or K.3 we have

, and

presented in display (

but on

Proposition 3
.

Assume that
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, and
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{0})=O_{P}(1)

\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}

\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}^{T}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}^{T}

\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}^{T}=I_{d}

such that

is a basis of

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}^{T}=I_{d}
,
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}^{T}=\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}^{T}

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0})=O_{P}(1).

As a consequence, if a sufficient dimension reduction estimator is found for the regression of

we could use

Model 1. Forward regression
