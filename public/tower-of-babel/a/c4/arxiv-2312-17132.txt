Bootstrapping Smooth Conformal Defects in Chern-Simons-Matter Theories
Barak Gabai, Amit Sever, and De-liang Zhong

Abstract
The expectation value of a smooth conformal line defect in a CFT is a conformal invariant functional of its path in space-time. For example, in large N N holographic theories, these fundamental observables are dual to the open-string partition function in AdS. In this paper, we develop a bootstrap method for studying them and apply it to conformal line defects in Chern-Simons matter theories. In these cases, the line bootstrap is based on three minimal assumptions – conformal invariance of the line defect, large N N factorization, and the spectrum of the two lowest-lying operators at the end of the line. On the basis of these assumptions, we solve the one-dimensional CFT on the line and systematically compute the defect expectation value in an expansion around the straight line. We find that the conformal symmetry of a straight defect is insufficient to fix the answer. Instead, imposing the conformal symmetry of the defect along an arbitrary curved line leads to a functional bootstrap constraint. The solution to this constraint is found to be unique.

Introduction

We study line defects in conformal field theory (CFT). Such one-dimensional operators undergo a defect Renormalization Group (RG) flow,
Affleck:1991tk
;
Polchinski:2011im
;

;

Due to the absence of one-dimensional anomalies, such operators can be placed along an arbitrary smooth path without breaking their conformal symmetry. In particular, the expectation value of such an operator is a conformal invariant functional of the path. This functional is a fundamental probe of the theory. For example, it can serve as an order parameter for the confinement/deconfinement transition. In holographic large-
N

The only known examples where such a functional is explicitly known are in 2d YM

in

In this paper, we develop a bootstrap method to study smooth line defects. We apply it to one of the simplest, yet nontrivial, examples of conformal defects. These are the ones that are realized in the t’ Hooft large
N

We find that imposing the conformal symmetry of a flat (straight) defect is insufficient for fixing its expectation value when the line is not straight. On the other hand, imposing the conformal symmetry of the defect along an arbitrary smooth path leads to new nontrivial functional constraints.
In particular, they result in a unique bootstrap solution. We use it to solve the line CFT and compute the defect expectation value in a systematic expansion around the straight line. Our results are in perfect agreement with those that we have obtained in

2
Setup

Our main objective in this paper is to determine the expectation value of mesonic line operators

M[x(\cdot)]=\langle{\cal M}[x(\cdot)]\rangle=\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}(x_{1}){\cal W}[x(\cdot)]\mathcal{O}(x_{0})\rangle\,,

(2.1)

where
\cal W
is a conformal line operator along a smooth path, and
x(s)
is some parameterization of the path. This path is oriented from the right boundary operator
\mathcal{O}
at
x_{0}\equiv x(0)
to the conjugate left boundary operator
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
at
x_{1}\equiv x(1)
. Instead of the mesonic line, we can consider the expectation value of a closed loop. As will become clear in the following, the latter is determined from the former.

Such operators are realized in CS theory with matter in the fundamental representation. For example, in CS theory with one fermion,
{\cal W}
can be a Wilson line in the fundamental representation that is stretched between fundamental and anti-fundamental fermion fields,
Gabai:2022mya
.

We now detail three minimal properties of this observable, which we assume to hold throughout this paper. They were derived in
Gabai:2022mya
by an explicit resummation of the ’t Hooft perturbative expansion.

2.1
Bootstrap Axioms

Axiom I - Conformal invariance

The first axiom concerns the conformal invariance of the line operator
\cal W
. Under a conformal transformation,
x\to\tilde{x}
, the line operator transforms as

{\cal W}[x(\cdot)]\quad\mapsto\quad\widetilde{\cal W}[\tilde{x}(\cdot)]={\cal W}[\tilde{x}(\cdot)]\,.

(2.2)

It follows from this axiom that an infinite straight line preserves an
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})\times U(1)
subgroup of the three-dimensional conformal group. Here,
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is the conformal symmetry of the line, and the
U(1)
factor stands for rotations in the transverse plane to the line. Consequently, the operators on the line and those on which the line can end are classified by their (unitary)
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
representation and their
U(1)
transverse spin. Such operators can be divided into
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
primaries and descendants. The latter can be obtained from the former using longitudinal derivatives. The primaries are characterized by their dimension
\Delta
, and their transverse spin
\mathfrak{s}
. We define the sign of the transverse spin to be correlated with the orientation of the line so that the spin of
x^{+}=(x^{1}+\mathrm{i}x^{2})/\sqrt{2}
is
+1
for a line oriented in the
\hat{x}^{3}
direction.

When the line is smooth (but not necessarily straight), we can zoom in close to a point on the line. Locally, it is approximately straight, and we can use the same classification of line and boundary operators as those on, and at the ends of, a straight line.

2
2

2

Namely,
\Delta
and
\mathfrak{s}
dictate how the operator transforms under the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})\times U(1)
transformations that preserve the straight line that is tangent to the line at that point.

Operators with non-zero transverse spin are equipped with a transverse polarization vector
n
, with
{\cal O}={\cal O}_{\Delta,{\mathfrak{s}}}(x,n)
. A transverse rotation of this polarization vector results in an overall phase factor of
e^{\pm\mathrm{i}{\mathfrak{s}}\theta}
. The transverse spin of these operators may be factional. As a result, the line expectation value acquires a phase under
2\pi
rotation of one of the boundary polarization vectors in the plane transverse to the line. In this paper, we will be concerned with the deformation of a straight line with an arbitrary normalization, which will be factored out. Therefore, keeping track of this
e^{\pm 2\pi\mathrm{i}{\mathfrak{s}}}
phase is not relevant to us.
3
3

3

In order to have a single-valued expectation value, one can introduce a framing vector, which is a transverse polarization along the line. The boundary value of this vector is the boundary polarization vector, whereas the dependence of the mesonic line on the bulk value of this vector is topological. Having such a topological framing vector is natural from the perturbative definition of Wilson lines in CS theory
Witten:1988hf
.

Axiom II- Factorization

We are concerned with a “large
N
CFT”, where correlation functions factorize as

\langle{\cal M}[x(\cdot),n]\,\widetilde{\cal M}[\tilde{x}(\cdot),\tilde{n}]\rangle=\langle{\cal M}[x(\cdot),n]\rangle\,\times\,\langle\widetilde{\cal M}[\tilde{x}(\cdot),\tilde{n}]\rangle\,\times\,\left(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)\right)\,.

(2.3)

Another large
N
simplification is the factorization of line operators into a product of two boundary operators,

{\cal O}_{\text{line}}=\mathcal{O}\times\overline{\mathcal{O}}\,.

(2.4)

In large
N
CS-matter theory, the operators
{\cal O}_{\text{line}}
transform in the adjoint representation, and the two boundary operators,
\mathcal{O}
and
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
, transform in the fundamental and anti-fundamental, respectively.
4
4

4

The singlet line operators are suppressed at large
N
.
Here, the transverse polarization vectors of the right and left boundary operators are the same.

Therefore, the properties of the line operators follow those of the left and right boundary operators in the product (
2.4
). In particular, it follows from (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) that the correlation function of operators on the line factorizes into a product of expectation values of mesonic line operators (
2.1
)

\langle\ldots{\cal W}{\cal O}_{\text{line}}{\cal W}\ldots\rangle=\langle\ldots{\cal W}\mathcal{O}\rangle\times\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}{\cal W}\ldots\rangle\,\times\,\left(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)\right)\,.

(2.5)

Note that axioms I and II do not imply that the correlation functions of operators on a straight line are trivial. Although their dependence on the positions of the operators factorizes, their spectrum and (properly normalized) overall coefficient are nontrivial. As a result, the dependence of
M
on the shape of the path is nontrivial. We will bootstrap them using one more assumption about the low-lying operator spectrum.

Axiom III - Boundary spectrum

The spectrum of boundary operators on the left end of the line is the same as that on the right, with the only difference being that the transverse spin is flipped,

(\Delta_{\overline{{\cal O}}},\mathfrak{s}_{\overline{{\cal O}}})=(\Delta_{{{\cal O}}},-\mathfrak{s}_{{{\cal O}}})\,.

(2.6)

This relation is inherited from the CPT symmetry of the theory, which simultaneously reflects the transverse plane and interchanges the left and right ends of the oriented line.

The third axiom concerns the spectrum of the low-lying
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
primary boundary operators at the right end of the line.
It states that the primary operator with
\mathfrak{s}\leq{1\over 2}
and minimal conformal dimension is

(\Delta_{\cal O},\mathfrak{s}_{\cal O})=(\Delta,1/2-\Delta)\,,

(2.7)

and that the one with
\mathfrak{s}\geq{1\over 2}
is

(\Delta_{\cal O},\mathfrak{s}_{\cal O})=(2-\Delta,3/2-\Delta)\,,

(2.8)

where
\Delta\in[0,1]
. We also assume that the transverse spin of these primary operators is not degenerate. That is, all other primary operators have a different transverse spin than those in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
). This assumption can be traced back to having a CS-matter theory with a single flavor.

2.2
Discussion

One direct consequence of the axioms is that the primary operator on the line with the lowest dimension is given by a factorized product of the right operator (
2.7
) and its CPT conjugate

{\cal O}_{\text{bi-scalar}}\equiv\mathcal{O}_{\Delta,1/2-\Delta}\times\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta,\Delta-1/2}\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\Delta_{\text{bi-scalar}}=2\Delta\,,\quad\mathfrak{s}_{\text{bi-scalar}}=0\,.

(2.9)

For
\Delta\in[0,1/2)
, it is relevant, and therefore the conformal fixed point on the line is unstable. On the other hand, for
\Delta\in(1/2,1]
, that scalar primary operator is irrelevant and the fixed point is stable.
5
5

5

In the case where
\Delta=1/2
, the two fixed points collide. At that point, one sign of the coupling in front of
{\cal O}_{\text{bi-scalar}}
is marginally irrelevant and the other is marginally relevant.

Chern-Simons theory with bosonic or fermionic fundamental matter has two different conformal line operators, one unstable and the other stable,
Gabai:2022vri
;
Gabai:2022mya
.
By allowing
\Delta
to take a value in
[0,1]
, we cover both types of lines. In particular, the case where
\Delta=1/2
corresponds to the free bosonic description, and the cases where
\Delta=0
or
\Delta=1
correspond to the free fermionic description. In these cases, the bootstrap deserves special care because, at these values, certain relevant or irrelevant operators become marginal. In this paper, we will avoid them by further assuming that
\Delta\neq 0,1/2,1
. These values can still be obtained by analytic continuation.

The axiom
I
(
2.2
) states that the line is conformally invariant. Hence, the only source in a conformal transformation of the mesonic line operators comes from the boundary. These are taken to be
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
primary operators of dimension
\Delta_{\cal O}
and transverse spin
\mathfrak{s}_{\cal O}
. Consequently, if we transform both the path and the boundary polarizations, the only source comes from the boundary conformal weight

{\widetilde{}\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta^{\prime},\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}}(\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{n}_{1}){\cal W}[\tilde{x}(\cdot)]\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta,\mathfrak{s}}(\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{n}_{0})\over\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta^{\prime},\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}}(x_{1},n_{1}){\cal W}[x(\cdot)]\mathcal{O}_{\Delta,\mathfrak{s}}(x_{0},n_{0})}=[\text{conformal factor}]\,.

(2.10)

To compute this factor, we decompose the conformal transformation at the boundary of the line,
x\to\tilde{x}
, into translation, three-dimensional rotation, and dilation

\frac{\partial\tilde{x}^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\nu}}=\Omega(x){\Lambda^{\mu}}_{\nu}(x)\,,\qquad\text{where}\quad\det{\Lambda^{\mu}}_{\nu}=1\,.

(2.11)

The dilatation
\Omega
is part of the
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
subgroup, under which the boundary operator has dimension
\Delta_{\cal O}
. Hence,

[\text{conformal factor}]=\Omega(x_{0})^{-\Delta}\times\Omega(x_{1})^{-\Delta^{\prime}}\,.

(2.12)

One can also rewrite this equation in a form where the polarization vector is not transformed and the boundary spin dependence is manifest. Note that the rotation matrix
\Lambda\in SO(3)
is, however, not a transverse rotation. Hence, the transverse planes before and after the conformal transformation are not the same. The only way that the boundary polarization vector on the transformed path would point in the same direction as that on the original path is if it points in the direction of the intersection between these two transverse planes. This is equivalent to starting with (
2.10
) and rotating both
n
and
\tilde{n}
, each in its transverse plane, so that they point in the same direction. Doing so results in the phase factor

[\text{spin factor}]=e^{\mathrm{i}{\mathfrak{s}}\theta_{0}}\times e^{\mathrm{i}{\mathfrak{s}}^{\prime}\theta_{1}}\,,

(2.13)

where
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
are the total transverse rotation angles at the two end points. Let us focus on the right angle,
\theta_{0}
. We denote by
e_{0}
and
\tilde{e}_{0}^{\mu}={\Lambda^{\mu}}_{\nu}e_{0}^{\nu}
the unit tangent vectors before and after the conformal transformation. The deformation angle,
\theta_{0}
, is equal to the difference between two angles. One is the angle between
\tilde{n}
and the intersection of the two transverse planes,
n^{\text{int}}\propto e_{0}\times\tilde{e}_{0}
, which comes from the numerator in (
2.10
). From it we subtract the angle between
n
and
n^{\text{int}}
, which comes from the denominator in (
2.10
). This difference is defined in such a way that it vanishes when the conformal transformation is trivial. All together, we have

{\widetilde{}\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta^{\prime},\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}}(\tilde{x}_{1},n_{1}^{\text{int}}){\cal W}[\tilde{x}(\cdot)]\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta,\mathfrak{s}}(\tilde{x}_{0},n_{0}^{\text{int}})\over\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta^{\prime},\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}}(x_{1},n_{1}^{\text{int}}){\cal W}[x(\cdot)]\mathcal{O}_{\Delta,\mathfrak{s}}(x_{0},n_{0}^{\text{int}})}=[\text{conformal factor}]\times[\text{spin factor}]\,.

(2.14)

Consider, for example, the case where the two boundary operators are primaries with the same conformal dimension and opposite transverse spin. In this case, the expectation value of the mesonic line operator takes the form

\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\Delta,-\mathfrak{s}}(x_{1},n_{1}){\cal W}[x(\cdot)]\mathcal{O}_{\Delta,\mathfrak{s}}(x_{0},n_{0})\rangle={(2n_{0}^{+}n_{1}^{-})^{\mathfrak{s}}\over|x_{0}-x_{1}|^{2\Delta}}\times F_{\Delta}[x(\cdot)]\,,

(2.15)

where
F
is a conformal invariant functional of the path that we would like to compute. Here, we have used light-cone coordinates to parameterize the transverse planes at the two endpoints,
\mathrm{d}^{2}s_{\perp}=2\mathrm{d}x^{+}\mathrm{d}x^{-}
.

Notations

We adopt the notation where the right and left boundary operators are denoted by

{\cal O}^{(m,\ell)}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{{\cal O}}^{(m,\ell)}\,,

(2.16)

where,
\ell
is the transverse spin at
\Delta=1/2
. That is, in the free bosonic theory. Here,

In this notation, the only primary operators that we assume to exist are

\{\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)},\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)},\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,-1)}\}\,.

(2.17)

Nevertheless, we adopt the general notation (

(\Delta^{(0,\ell)},\mathfrak{s}^{(0,\ell)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{llcl}\left(\Delta,\frac{1}{2}-\Delta\right)&\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+(|\ell|,\ell)&&\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell\leq 0\,,\\
\left(2-\Delta,\frac{3}{2}-\Delta\right)&\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+\left(\ell-1,\ell-1\right)&&\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell\geq 1\,,\end{array}\right.\,

(2.18)

where
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The spectrum given above is invariant under the transformation of the parameter
\Delta\to(2-\Delta)
together with a sign flip of the transverse spin. This symmetry transformation interchanges the two towers. We denote it
tower-swap
.

We will also use the shorthand notation for the expectation value of mesonic lines with primary boundary operators

M^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}_{st}[x(\cdot)]\equiv\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,\ell)}(x_{s}){\cal W}[x(\cdot)]\mathcal{O}^{(0,\ell^{\prime})}(x_{t})\rangle\,,

(2.19)

where we have dropped the dependence of the boundary operators on their transverse polarization vectors. In the case when the line is straight we will also drop the dependence of
M

and express (

M^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}_{st}[x_{\text{straight}}(\cdot)]\equiv M^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}_{st}\equiv\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,\ell)}(x_{s})\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,\ell^{\prime})}(x_{t})\rangle\!\rangle\,.

(2.20)

2.4
Bootstrap Strategy

Using axioms
I
,
II
and
III
, we can now systematically bootstrap the expectation value of mesonic line operators along a smooth path. Our strategy is to start from a straight mesonic line, whose only nonzero expectation values are those that preserve the boundary spin

\ell^{\prime}=-\ell
. Their dependence on the length of the line is fixed by symmetry to be given by

M^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}_{10}=\delta_{\ell^{\prime},-\ell}\,\frac{c_{\ell}}{|x_{10}|^{2\Delta_{\ell}}}\,,

(2.21)

where
\Delta_{\ell}=\Delta(\mathcal{O}^{(0,\ell)})
is the dimension of
\mathcal{O}^{(0,\ell)}
and
c_{\ell}
is a number that depends on our normalization of the boundary operators. It is convenient to choose the two boundary polarization vectors to be the same, so that the factor
2n_{0}^{+}n_{1}^{-}=n^{2}=1
in (
2.15
) and
c_{l}
is real.

We then smoothly deform the straight line

x_{\text{straight}}(s)\quad\rightarrow\quad x(s)=x_{\text{straight}}(s)+{\rm v}(s)\,,

(2.22)

and bootstrap
M[x(\cdot)]
order by order in the smooth deformation profile
{\rm v}(s)/|x_{1}-x_{0}|
. More concretely, we express the deformed operator in terms of operator insertions along the straight line and bootstrap their coefficients. This allows us to explicitly compute the mesonic line expectation value, order-by-order, in the deformation.

To impose conformal symmetry on the expansion, we construct a one-parameter family of transformations,
x_{s}\to\tilde{x}_{\beta}(x_{s})
. They can be any combination of translations, rotations, dilatation, and special conformal transformations, that are smoothly connected to the identity at
\beta=0
.
6
6

6

For example, a special conformal transformation can be parametrized as
\tilde{x}^{\mu}_{\beta}(x)=\frac{x^{\mu}-\beta^{2}\hat{b}^{\mu}(\hat{b}\cdot x)}{1-2\beta(\hat{b}\cdot x)+\beta^{2}\hat{b}^{2}x^{2}}
, where
\hat{b}
is some unit vector.
For convenience, we tune the deformation so that it deforms the endpoints in the transverse plane only. We then expand the deformed path
\tilde{x}_{\beta}(x_{s})=x_{s}+{\rm v}_{s}(\beta)
in powers of
\beta
and reparameterize it so that at any point along the line the deformation vector is transverse to the original straight line,
{\rm v}_{s}(\beta)\cdot\dot{x}_{s}=0
. Because
{\rm v}_{s}(\beta)
depends nonlinearly on
\beta
, this expansion in powers of
\beta
is not the same as the expansions in powers of
{\rm v}(\beta)
. The two expansions, however, only start to differ at the third order (see Appendix
A
for details).

Consider the variation of the path (

3.2
Boundary Variation

The boundary variation may include derivatives of
{\rm v}
,

\delta{\cal O}={\rm v}^{\mu}\delta_{\mu}^{(0)}{\cal O}+\mathrm{d}{\rm v}^{\mu}\delta_{\mu}^{(1)}{\cal O}+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}^{\mu}\delta_{\mu}^{(2)}{\cal O}+\dots\,.

{\rm v}

\Delta({\rm v})=-1

\Delta(\mathrm{d}{\rm v})=0

. For a transverse variation, the operators
\delta_{\mu}^{(n)}{\cal O}
have one more unit of transverse spin with respect to that of
{\cal O}
, while for a longitudinal variation, it remains the same. Note that because we have already fixed the line variation to be given by (
3.2
), we no longer have the freedom to shift terms between
\delta{\cal O}
,
\delta\overline{{\cal O}}
, and
\delta{\cal W}
in (
3.8
) using integration by parts.

We refer to the operator
\delta_{\mu}{\cal O}\equiv\delta_{\mu}^{(0)}{\cal O}
as the
path derivative
of the boundary operator
{\cal O}
. As we go to higher orders in the path deformation, we will have more boundary and line operator insertions. We denote the boundary operator with higher powers of the deformation as

{\rm v}^{\mu_{1}}{\rm v}^{\mu_{2}}\dots{\rm v}^{\mu_{m}}\delta_{\mu_{1}}\delta_{\mu_{2}}\dots\delta_{\mu_{m}}{\cal O}\,.

(3.9)

Importantly, this multi-path derivative is not a local deformation of the path. Instead, it is the operator that stands in front of
{\rm v}^{\mu_{1}}{\rm v}^{\mu_{2}}\dots{\rm v}^{\mu_{m}}
in a
smooth
variation of the path. Hence, unlike covariant derivatives, these path variations commute by definition,

\delta_{\mu}\delta_{\nu}{\cal O}=\delta_{\nu}\delta_{\mu}{\cal O}\neq\delta_{\nu}\left(\delta_{\mu}{\cal O}\right)\,.

(3.10)

Using path derivatives, the descendant boundary operators are defined as

\mathcal{O}^{(n+1,\ell)}=\delta_{3}\mathcal{O}^{(n,\ell)}\,,\qquad\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(n+1,\ell)}=\delta_{3}\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(n,\ell)}

(3.11)

where
\delta_{3}
is the longitudinal path derivative. It is oriented in the direction of the path, from
\mathcal{O}
to
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
.

3.3
Boundary Counter-terms

The perturbative expansion of the mesonic line operator around the straight line (
2.22
) is a conformal perturbation theory, parameterized by the deformation profile. To carry it out order by order in
{\rm v}(s)
we have to introduce a UV cutoff and renormalize the line. We denote by
\epsilon
the corresponding UV cutoff, which has the dimension of length.

The resulting structure of the boundary variations is largely constrained by symmetry. Consider, for example, the operator
\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}
. It has transverse spin
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{s}(\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)})-1=1/2-\Delta
. According to axiom
III
, the only boundary operators with this transverse spin are
\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}
and its descendants
\mathcal{O}^{(n,0)}
. However, their conformal dimension differs from that of
\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}
by a non-integer value
\Delta(\mathcal{O}^{(n,0)})-\Delta(\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)})=n-3+2\Delta
. Therefore, the decomposition of
\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}
in terms of boundary operators takes the form

\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3-2\Delta}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}\epsilon^{n}\,\mathcal{O}^{(n,0)}\,,

(3.12)

where all coefficients,
\{b_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, are scheme dependent.

The divergent terms in (
3.12
) are counter-terms. Their coefficients are fixed so that they cancel out divergences that result from the integration of the displacement operator. For the case of
\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}
, this integral takes the form

\langle\!\langle\ldots\,\delta{\cal W}\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\rangle\!\rangle=\int\limits_{0}\mathrm{d}s\,|\dot{x}|\,\langle\!\langle\ldots\,\left[{\rm v}_{s}^{+}{\mathbb{D}}_{+}(x_{s})+{\rm v}_{s}^{-}{\mathbb{D}}_{-}(x_{s})\right]\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}(x_{0})\rangle\!\rangle\,,

(3.13)

where the dots stand for some left boundary operator. Only the term with
{\mathbb{D}}_{-}
contributes to the expectation value; see (
3.4
). As a result, the integral may have a local divergence coming from the
s\to 0
region of integration.

We choose a proper time parameterization of the straight line

x_{\text{straight}}(s)=x_{0}+s(x_{1}-x_{0})\,,

(3.14)

and use a point splitting regulator,
\ell>\tilde{\epsilon}=|x_{10}|\epsilon
. With this choice, the divergence takes the form

\displaystyle|x_{10}|\int\limits_{\tilde{\epsilon}}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm v}_{s}^{+}\,\langle\!\langle\ldots\,{\mathbb{D}}_{-}(x_{s})\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}(x_{0})\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta|x_{10}|\int\limits_{\tilde{\epsilon}}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm v}_{s}^{+}\,\langle\!\langle\ldots\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}(x_{s})\rangle\!\rangle M_{s0}^{(-1,1)}

(3.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\eta\,c_{-1}\over|x_{10}|^{3-2\Delta}}\left[{\rm v}_{0}^{+}\langle\!\langle\ldots\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}(x_{0})\rangle\!\rangle\int\limits_{\tilde{\epsilon}}{\mathrm{d}s\over s^{4-2\Delta}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\ \ \left.+\left(\dot{\rm v}_{0}^{+}\langle\!\langle\ldots\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}(x_{0})\rangle\!\rangle+{\rm v}_{0}^{+}|x_{10}|\langle\!\langle\ldots\,\mathcal{O}^{(1,0)}(x_{0})\rangle\!\rangle\right)\int\limits_{\tilde{\epsilon}}{\mathrm{d}s\over s^{3-2\Delta}}+\ldots\right]\,.

The divergences
{\rm v}_{0}^{+}/\epsilon^{3-2\Delta}
and
{\rm v}_{0}^{+}/\epsilon^{2-2\Delta}
are canceled by setting
b_{0}=-\eta c_{-1}/(3-2\Delta)
and
b_{1}=-\eta c_{-1}/(2-2\Delta)
in (
3.12
). Namely, we have

\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}=-{\eta c_{-1}\over(3-2\Delta)\epsilon^{3-2\Delta}}\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}-{\eta c_{-1}\over(2-2\Delta)\epsilon^{2-2\Delta}}\mathcal{O}^{(1,0)}+O(\epsilon^{2\Delta-1})\,.

(3.16)

Similarly, the
\dot{\rm v}_{0}^{+}/\epsilon^{2-2\Delta}
divergences are canceled by setting in (
3.8
)

\delta_{-}^{(1)}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}=-{\eta c_{-1}\over(2-2\Delta)\epsilon^{2-2\Delta}}\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}+O(\epsilon^{2\Delta-1})\,.

(3.17)

In this way, all the divergent terms in
\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}
are fixed.

Similarly, for
\delta_{+}\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}
we have

\delta_{+}\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2\Delta-1}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{b}_{n}\epsilon^{n}\,\mathcal{O}^{(n,1)}\,.

(3.18)

The coefficients of the divergent terms are tuned to cancel those that result from the integration of
{\mathbb{D}}_{+}
in
\langle\!\langle\ldots\,\delta{\cal W}\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}\rangle\!\rangle
.

3.4
The Keyhole Prescription

Consider, for example, the first-order variation

\delta M^{(0,1)}=\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\delta{\cal W}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle\delta\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\delta\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\rangle\!\rangle\,,

(3.19)

In this case, the corresponding decomposition of
\delta\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}
and
\delta\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}
in terms of boundary operators (
3.8
) comes with fractional powers of
\epsilon
. As discussed above, the role of these boundary operators is to remove divergences that result from the integration of the displacement operator near the boundary in

\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\,\delta{\cal W}\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\rangle\!\rangle=|x_{10}|\int\limits_{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{1-\tilde{\epsilon}}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm v}_{s}^{-}\,\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\,{\mathbb{D}}_{-}(x_{s})\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\rangle\!\rangle\,.

(3.20)

This integral takes the form

\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\,\delta{\cal W}\,\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}\rangle\!\rangle={\eta\,c_{0}\,c_{-1}\over|x_{10}|^{3}}\int\limits_{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{1-\tilde{\epsilon}}\mathrm{d}s\,{{\rm v}_{s}^{-}\over(1-s)^{4}}\left({1-s\over s}\right)^{2\Delta}\,.

(3.21)

It has a branch cut of order
\Delta
, with branch points
s=0
and
s=1
. The result of regularizing this integral and canceling the divergences with the boundary variations in (
3.19
) is equivalent to letting the cut run between the two branch points and converting this integral into a contour integral around it

\delta M^{(0,1)}_{10}={\eta|x_{10}|\over{2\mathrm{i}}\sin(2\pi\Delta)}\oint\limits_{[0,1]}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm v}_{s}^{-}M^{(0,0)}_{1s}M^{(-1,1)}_{s0}+[\text{positive powers of }\epsilon]\,,

(3.22)

where we have assumed that the function
{\rm v}^{-}(s)
does not have singularities along the segment
s\in[0,1]
. For convenience, we have also assumed that the variation at the boundary acts transversely to the line,
{\rm v}_{0}^{3}={\rm v}_{1}^{3}=0
. Longitudinal boundary variations act trivially by rescaling the length of the line,
|x_{01}|
in (
2.21
). The cancellation of divergent boundary terms against the terms in (
3.18
) and the conjugate of (
3.12
) can be understood as providing the cup or keyhole around the end of the cut, making the integral finite.

We define the keyhole regularization prescription as
8
8

8

For generic
\Delta
, all divergent terms near the boundary are power-like, therefore there is no ambiguity in the finite part in the
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
limit, which only happens for a logarithmic divergence.

\delta M^{(0,1)}_{10}={\eta|x_{10}|\over{2\mathrm{i}}\sin(2\pi\Delta)}\oint\limits_{[0,1]}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm v}_{s}^{-}M^{(0,0)}_{1s}M^{(-1,1)}_{s0}={\eta\,c_{0}\,c_{-1}|x_{10}|\over{2\mathrm{i}}\sin(2\pi\Delta)}\oint\limits_{[0,1]}\mathrm{d}s\,{{\rm v}_{s}^{-}\over(1-s)^{4}}\left({s-1\over s}\right)^{2\Delta}\,,

(3.23)

without additional positive powers of
\epsilon
. This prescription amounts to a certain choice of coefficients in the expansion of the boundary variation. Apart from divergent counterterms, these series consist of boundary operators with increasing dimensions and (fractional) powers of
\epsilon
; see, for example, (
3.12
). In the keyhole prescription, they are fixed so that (
3.23
) holds at finite
\epsilon
.

At higher orders in the line deformation, these scheme-dependent terms with a positive power of
\epsilon
may combine with divergent ones to give finite contributions. The total finite part, however, is scheme-independent. In Appendix
B
, we demonstrate this explicitly for two different schemes.

Similarly to (
3.23
), the keyhole prescription for
\delta M^{(-1,0)}
reads

\delta M^{(-1,0)}_{10}={\mathrm{i}\eta|x_{10}|\over 2\sin(2\pi\Delta)}\oint\limits_{[0,1]}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm v}_{s}^{+}M^{(-1,1)}_{1s}M^{(0,0)}_{s0}\,.

(3.24)

In particular, for a polynomial
{\rm v}_{s}^{-}\propto s^{n}
in (
3.22
), we end up with the integral

\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}\sin 2\pi\Delta}\oint\limits_{[0,1]}\mathrm{d}s\frac{s^{n}}{s^{2\Delta}(s-1)^{2(2-\Delta)}}=-\frac{\Gamma(2\Delta-3)\Gamma(n-2\Delta+1)}{\Gamma(n-2)}\,.

(3.25)

Note the factor of
\Gamma(n-2)
in the denominator. It means that
\delta M^{(0,1)}=0
for
{\rm v}^{-}\propto s^{0}
,
{\rm v}^{-}\propto s^{1}
, and
{\rm v}^{-}\propto s^{2}
. These correspond in turn to global translation, rotation, and special conformal transformations, which are indeed symmetries of the mesonic line expectation value. To be precise,
\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}{\cal W}\mathcal{O}^{(0,1)}
transforms covariantly under conformal transformations, with non-zero weight at the boundaries, (
2.10
). These weights only start to contribute at second order and are analyzed in the next section. Hence, to the linear order,
M^{(-1,0)}
is invariant under these transformations. There are no further constraints imposed by conformal symmetry at this order for mesonic line operators that end on the four operators in (
2.17
).

We can now use (
3.23
) and (
3.24
) to compute the first-order variation of
M^{(0,1)}
and
M^{(-1,0)}
as functional of the shape of the path. The overall coefficient depends on our normalization of these operators. This freedom is reflected in the factor of
\eta
that we have chosen to keep.

4
Second Order

In the second order, for the first time, finite terms with undetermined coefficients enter the line variation. We list them explicitly in Section
4.1
and then bootstrap them in Section
4.2
.

4.1
Line Variation

The second-order variation can be divided into line and boundary contributions as

\displaystyle\delta^{2}{\cal M}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\bar{O}\,\mathcal{W}\,O+\delta\,\bar{O}\,\mathcal{W}\,\delta O+\bar{O}\,\mathcal{W}\,\delta^{2}O\,

(4.1)

\displaystyle+\delta\bar{O}\,\delta\mathcal{W}\,O+\bar{O}\,\delta\mathcal{W}\,\delta O+\bar{O}\,\delta^{2}\mathcal{W}\,O\,.

Because the dynamics on the line is that of a local CFT, any term in which the two variations act at two separated points on the line is given by two first-order variations. New operators appear when the two variations act at the same point, either at the boundary or in the bulk of the line. At the boundary of the line, the second-order variation takes a similar form to the first-order one in (
3.8
) and is given by

\delta^{2}{\cal O}={\rm v}^{\mu}{\rm v}^{\nu}\delta_{\mu}^{(0)}\delta_{\nu}^{(0)}{\cal O}+\mathrm{d}{\rm v}^{\mu}{\rm v}^{\nu}\delta_{\mu}^{(1)}\delta_{\nu}^{(0)}{\cal O}+\mathrm{d}{\rm v}^{\mu}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}^{\nu}\delta_{\mu}^{(1)}\delta_{\nu}^{(1)}{\cal O}+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}^{\mu}{\rm v}^{\nu}\delta_{\mu}^{(2)}\delta_{\nu}^{(0)}{\cal O}+\dots\,,

(4.2)

where in our parametrization (
3.14
),
\mathrm{d}{\rm v}=\dot{\rm v}/|x_{10}|
, etc, see definition below (
3.8
). At the bulk of the line, the second order line variation reads

\delta^{2}\mathcal{W}=|x_{10}|^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm v}_{s}^{\mu}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\,{\rm v}_{t}^{\nu}\,{\cal P}[{\mathbb{D}}_{\mu}(x_{s}){\mathbb{D}}_{\nu}(x_{t}){\cal W}]+|x_{10}|\int\limits_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,{\cal P}[\delta{\mathbb{D}}(x_{s}){\cal W}]\,.

(4.3)

Here, using integration by parts, we have chosen the double integral to run over two displacement operators, with no derivatives of
{\rm v}
. With this choice, the operator
\delta{\mathbb{D}}
takes a form similar to
\delta^{2}{\cal O}
in (
4.2
). That is, it is a sum of operators that are weighted by
{\rm v}^{\mu}_{s}{\rm v}^{\nu}_{s}
, and higher derivatives such as
{\rm v}^{\mu}_{s}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}^{\nu}_{s}
, etc.

We can now repeat the analogous treatment to the first-order variation, canceling divergences using counter-terms and keeping all terms that are finite at
\epsilon\to 0
. The conclusion of this tedious computation is, however, very simple. We may list only the regularized integrals and boundary terms that are finite as
\epsilon\to 0
and can appear in (
4.1
). The rest of the terms are either divergent and are tuned to cancel out or go to zero as the UV cutoff is removed. The coefficients in front of the finite terms are either fixed from the first-order variation or are new, and therefore left unfixed. In the next section, we will bootstrap them using the axioms.

In what follows, we will first focus on the case where
M=M^{(0,0)}
. As before, we do not consider longitudinal boundary variations because they act trivially.

4.1.1
Boundary Terms with One Variation

The boundary term with one variation on the right and the other on the left is given by

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\delta\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\delta\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle({\rm v}_{1}^{-}\delta_{-}+\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{1}^{-}\delta_{-}^{(1)}+\dots)\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}({\rm v}_{0}^{+}\delta_{+}+\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{0}^{+}\delta_{+}^{(1)}+\dots)\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}\rangle\!\rangle

(4.4)

\displaystyle+\langle\!\langle({\rm v}_{1}^{+}\delta_{+}+\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{1}^{+}\delta_{+}^{(1)}+\dots)\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}({\rm v}_{0}^{-}\delta_{-}+\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{0}^{-}\delta_{-}^{(1)}+\dots)\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}\rangle\!\rangle\,.

The operators in the first line have expansions of the form (
3.18
), with fractional powers of
\epsilon
. The ones with negative powers of
\epsilon
are counter-terms that cancel divergences arising from the integrals considered below. In this order, those with positive powers of
\epsilon
vanish as
\epsilon\to 0
. In summary, none of the terms in the first line of (
4.4
) leads to a finite contribution.

The terms in the second line are operators with transverse spins
\mathfrak{s}=-\bar{\mathfrak{s}}=(1/2-\Delta)-1
. Therefore, if they exist, they are new operators. According to the axiom
III
, their dimension must be strictly larger than that of
{\cal O}^{(0,0)}
. In particular, the operators
\delta_{-}^{(l)}\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}
and
\delta_{+}^{(l)}\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}
with
l\geq 1
come with a positive power of
\epsilon
and do not contribute as
\epsilon\to 0
.

We remained with the boundary operator

\delta_{-}\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}\equiv\epsilon^{\Delta^{(0,-1)}-\Delta^{(0,0)}-1}\Big(\mathcal{O}^{(0,-1)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\epsilon^{n}\mathcal{O}^{(n,-1)}\Big)\,,

(4.5)

and its CPT conjugate. The power of
\epsilon
cannot be negative because there is no other contribution at that transverse spin that can cancel with it. So
\Delta^{(0,0)}<\Delta^{(0,-1)}\leq\Delta^{(0,0)}+1
. The operator
\mathcal{O}^{(0,-1)}
appears multiplying
{\rm v}^{-}_{0}
in (
4.4
) and is the operator of the lowest dimension with that transverse spin. Therefore, if it exists, it is an
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
primary. The operators
\mathcal{O}^{(n,-1)}
are the corresponding
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
descendants and the coefficients
a_{n}
are scheme dependent. For the second line in (
4.4
) to yield a finite contribute, the operator
\mathcal{O}^{(0,-1)}
must have conformal dimension

\Delta^{(0,-1)}=\Delta^{(0,0)}+1=\Delta+1\,.

(4.6)

Below we will find that, indeed, such an operator must exist. Assuming that it does, equation (
4.4
) reduces to

\langle\!\langle\delta\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\delta\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}\rangle\!\rangle={\rm v}_{1}^{+}{\rm v}_{0}^{-}M_{10}^{(1,-1)}+[\text{positive powers of }\epsilon]+[\text{negative powers of }\epsilon]\,,

(4.7)

where

M_{10}^{(1,-1)}\equiv\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,1)}\mathcal{O}^{(0,-1)}\rangle\!\rangle\equiv{c_{1}\over|x_{10}|^{2\Delta+2}}\,.

(4.8)

and
\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,1)}
is the CPT conjugate of
\mathcal{O}^{(0,-1)}
.

4.1.2
Boundary Terms with Two Variations

Next, we have new boundary terms corresponding to second-order variation at the same boundary points, one in the plus direction and the other in the minus direction. These have the same transverse spin as that of
\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}
and include all possible scalar operators

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bar{O}^{(0,0)}\delta^{2}O^{(0,0)}\rangle\!\rangle=

\displaystyle\Big[{\rm v}_{0}^{+}{\rm v}_{0}^{-}\big({a_{0}\over\epsilon^{2}}+{a_{1}\over\epsilon}\delta_{3}+\gamma_{0}\delta_{3}^{2}\big)+\left({\rm v}_{0}^{+}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{0}^{-}+{\rm v}_{0}^{-}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{0}^{+}\right)\big({a_{2}\over\epsilon}+\gamma_{1}\delta_{3}\big)

Similarly, we have

The other type of one integral has two variations at the same point on the line and is given by

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{(0,0)}\delta{\mathbb{D}}(x_{s})\mathcal{O}^{(0,0)}\rangle\!\rangle

4.2

4.2.1
Constraints from Conformal Symmetry of a Straight Line

Consider a general conformal transformation,
x_{s}\to\tilde{x}_{\beta}(x_{s})

In the case of

\displaystyle[\text{spin factor}]\qquad\ \,=1+{\mathfrak{s}}\left(\delta\theta_{0}-\delta\theta_{1}\right)+\dots\,,

\displaystyle\delta\log|x_{10}|

\displaystyle=\left.({\rm v}_{c}^{+})\right|_{0}^{1}\left.({\rm v}_{c}^{-})\right|_{0}^{1}/|x_{10}|^{2}=(\mathfrak{c}_{1}^{-}+\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{-})(\mathfrak{c}_{1}^{+}+\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{+})/|x_{10}|^{2}\,,

(4.30)

\displaystyle(\delta\theta_{0}-\delta\theta_{1})

\displaystyle={1\over 2}\left.\left({\rm v}_{c}^{+}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{c}^{-}-{\rm v}_{c}^{-}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}{\rm v}_{c}^{+}\right)\right|_{0}^{1}=(\mathfrak{c}_{1}^{-}\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{+}-\mathfrak{c}_{1}^{+}\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{-})/|x_{10}|^{2}\,.

4.2.2

We see that the conformal symmetry of a straight line is not sufficient to fix all the coefficients that appear in the second-order variation (

To determine the remaining coefficients, we demand that the expectation value of a mesonic line operator along an arbitrary smooth path, not necessarily straight, respects the conformal symmetry (

Explicitly, we consider a path that we decompose into a straight line and an arbitrary smooth deformation

5
Third Order

We can apply the bootstrap method developed in the previous sections to higher-order deformations. At each order, we pick a regularization scheme, list all the finite terms that can appear, and fix their coefficients by imposing the conformal symmetry of a curved line. All the ingredients that appear in higher orders are already present at the third order. In this section, we carry out the method in third order explicitly and discuss the higher-order generalizations. Our main point in doing so is to demonstrate that the bootstrap solution is unique and that it allows one to systematically compute the expectation value of mesonic lines to any order.

5.3
Higher Orders

The third-order analysis is sufficient to demonstrate the general structure. We work recursively in the order of the deformation. At the
n
’th order, we list all finite terms that can contribute to the mesonic line expectation value. Due to the locality of the line effective action, the coefficients of most of these terms are determined from lower orders, provided that we keep using the same regularization scheme. The only new terms have all the
n
variations at the same point. This point can either be integrated along the line or at the boundary.
18
18

18

In the case of the third-order deformation of
M^{(0,1)}
, the terms with the three variations at the same boundary point are either irrelevant or that they are divergent counter-term.
To fix these coefficients, we impose that the deformed line, along
x_{a}
(
4.31
), transforms covariantly under conformal transformations. For that aim, we expand
\tilde{x}_{\beta}(x_{a})
in powers of
\beta
and
{\rm v}_{a}
, keeping the sum of the two powers equal to
n
. We then collect all terms with fixed power
\beta^{m}{\rm v}_{a}^{n-m}
in the variation of the straight line. These receive contributions from all orders in the line variation, up to order
n
. We equate the result to the expansion of the spin source and the conformal source at order
\beta^{m}{\rm v}_{a}^{n-m}
.

By considering a polynomial deformation profile,
{\rm v}_{a}
, we generate a sufficient set of equations to fix the solution. These equations are all linear in the new coefficients. Generating more equations serves as a consistency check for the solution. Once all coefficients are fixed, we can go back and evaluate the
n
’th order line variation explicitly.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we have developed a bootstrap method for studying conformal line defects. We have found that imposing the conformal symmetry of line defects along an arbitrary smooth path leads to new nontrivial functional constraints. Focusing on line defects in CS-matter theories at large
N
, we have observed that these constraints lead to a unique bootstrap solution and are therefore sufficient to fix the defect expectation value. The following is a list of future directions that we find interesting.

The one-dimensional defect CFT that we considered is relatively simple. This is largely due to the factorization of the correlators on the line. In particular, we used it to compute the correlation functions between
n
insertions of the displacement operator that arise at the
n
’th order of the line deformation. In other theories, with more complicated line defects, such correlators are unknown functions of the conformal cross-ratios. It would be interesting to see if these functions can be bootstrapped in a similar way and used to compute the expectation value of the defect. What makes us optimistic is that the smooth line deformation profile,
{\rm v}_{a}(s)
, entering the bootstrap constraints, is arbitrary. Therefore, it generates a functional bootstrap equation that can be used to gradually localize the individual deformation points and fix the
n
-point correlation of the displacement operator.

Large
N
CS-matter theories are expected to be holographically dual to parity-breaking versions of Vasiliev’s higher-spin theory,
Vasiliev:1992av
;
Giombi:2011kc
;
Aharony:2011jz
;
Aharony:2012nh
;
Klebanov:2002ja
;
Sezgin:2003pt
;
Chang:2012kt
;
Leigh:2003gk
. The massless high-spin spectrum of this theory suggests that a unified description in terms of a tensionless, partly topological string may exist. In particular, it is not known how to incorporate the mesonic lines into Vasiliev’s higher-spin theory. We expect that doing so will require a stringy description and that the mesonic lines may even be instrumental in constructing it.

The bootstrap method that we have employed gives the defect expectation value in an expansion around the straight line. It would be interesting to resum this expansion and find a more explicit representation of the conformal invariant functional of the path
F[x(\cdot)]
in (
2.15
). We expect the holographic description of the mesonic line to be such a representation.

Finally, CS-matter theories possess a higher spin symmetry that is broken only at order
1/N
. An implication of this symmetry is the existence of protected primary tilt operators on the line,
Gabai:2022mya
. It would be interesting to fix these operators and use them to derive bootstrap constraints in the same way that we have used the conformal symmetry. These high-spin constraints are expected to become important at the next order in
1/N
, while at the planar order at which we are working, they are redundant.

Appendix A
Conformal Transformation of a Curved Line

In this section, we study conformal transformations of arbitrary paths.
We parameterize the path by its projection on the
x^{3}
axis,

x_{a}(s)=({\rm v}_{a}^{1}(s),{\rm v}_{a}^{2}(s),s)\,,

(A.1)

assuming that the transverse vector function
\vec{{\rm v}}_{a}(s)\equiv\{{\rm v}_{a}^{1}(s),{\rm v}_{a}^{2}(s)\}
is single valued and smooth.
We then apply the following conformal transformation to it

x_{a}\quad\rightarrow\quad x_{ac}=\mathcal{R}_{\hat{x}^{1}}(\mathfrak{c}_{1}^{2})\cdot\mathcal{R}_{\hat{x}^{2}}(\mathfrak{c}_{1}^{1})\cdot\mathcal{B}_{b}(x_{a})+\{\mathfrak{c}_{0}^{1},\mathfrak{c}_{0}^{2},a_{3}\}\,,

(A.2)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\hat{n}}(\theta)
denotes the finite rotation of angle
\theta

\hat{n}

\mathcal{B}_{b}(x_{a})
denotes the special conformal transformation

\mathcal{B}_{b}(x)^{\mu}\equiv\frac{x^{\mu}-b^{\mu}x^{2}}{1-2b\cdot x+b^{2}x^{2}}\,,\qquad{\rm with}\qquad b=(-\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{1},-\mathfrak{c}_{2}^{2},b_{3})\,.

(A.3)

The last term in (
A.2
) is a constant shift. The transverse vectors
\vec{\mathfrak{c}}_{0}
,
\vec{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}
, and
\vec{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}
are a set of continuous parameters that we use to parameterize the conformal transformation (
4.28
). Here, we did not include rotations around the
\hat{x}^{3}
axis, which leaves the straight line invariant.

For generic values of the conformal transformation parameters, the two endpoints transform in all three directions. Using translation invariance, we can assume without loss of generality that the endpoint
x_{0}
transforms only in the transverse direction. This choice fixes the value of
a_{3}
. We then further impose that the endpoint
x_{1}
also transforms in the transverse direction.
This condition fixes
b_{3}
in terms of the other parameters. There are two solutions for

and we pick the one that connects smoothly to the identity.
