Abstract
. In this paper, we consider a nonlinear PDE system governed by a parabolic heat equation coupled in a nonlinear way with a hyperbolic momentum equation describing the behavior of a displacement field coupled with a nonlinear elliptic equation based on an internal damage variable. We present a numerical scheme based on a low-order Galerkin finite element method (FEM) for the space discretization of the time-dependent nonlinear PDE system and an implicit finite difference method (FDM) to discretize in the direction of the time variable. Moreover, we present a priori estimates for the exact and discrete solutions for the pointwise-in-time L 2 L^{2} -norm. Based on the a priori estimates, we rigorously prove the convergence of the solutions of the fully discretized system to the exact solutions. Denoting the properties of the internal parameters, we find the order of convergence concerning the discretization parameters.

Introduction

Damage models consist of a system of nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) that enable us to monitor and observe the behavior of occurred failure, fracture, and displacements in different materials, especially in brittle, quasi-brittle, and thermoelastic solids
[
BFM00
,
MHSA15
]
. In thermoelastic materials, a temperature change (e.g., a thermal shock) leads to a non-uniform volume change and thermal stress. Exceeding the material tensile strength initiates a fracture that can continue until full separation. The material separation
occurs by mechanical forces or thermoelastic effects. For instance, lasers can be used effectively to cut ceramic substrates and glasses.

In this paper, we consider a nonlinear PDE system, including three coupled equations to model the thermal and mechanical behavior of thermoelastic materials. This system consists of a nonlinear hyperbolic momentum equation coupled with a parabolic heat equation that describe the behavior of the displacement field and the heat distribution, respectively. Moreover, the momentum equation is coupled with a nonlinear elliptic equation that describes the behavior of an internal variable.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are only a few papers studying the existence, uniqueness, and regularity properties of the solutions of such nonlinear PDE systems (in the presence of the thermoelastic materials) with respect to the time and space variables in the weak from as well as in the time discretized version.
A model to simulate the thermoelastic fracture problems is presented in
[
MHSA15
]
to describe a phase-field fracture equation coupled with heat conduction.
Another model that studies damage
[
HKRR17
]
uses a system of nonlinear PDE system including viscous Cahn–Hilliard equation (to model the phase separation), and momentum balance (to model the displacement) coupled with a thermal system.
A thermodynamic consistent PDE system for phase transition and damage addressing the existence of the weak solution is given in
[
RR15
]
.
In
[
Rou10
]
, the author presents a damage model governed by a PDE system consisting of the momentum equation for the displacement coupled with a heat equation as well as coupled with a rate-independent flow equation for the damage variable in a strongly nonlinear way.
Concerning thermo-viscoelastic materials, the weak formulation and the existence of solutions for the coupled system are given in
[
LRTT18
,
Rou10
]
.

In the presence of material damage or regularized fractures, one approach is to introduce an
internal variable that determines the current state of the process. In such cases,
additional nonlinearities, in terms of inequalities, appear in the auxiliary equations. Additionally, the new internal parameters, i.e.,
\kappa
and
\ell
, interact
with each other in a certain way.
In the discrete setting of the damage models, to guarantee a reliable approximation of the solution, we assume
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
and
h=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
where

h
is the spatial discretization parameter. We notice that the relations
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
and
h=\mathcal{O}(\ell)

go into the direction of
\Gamma
-convergence
[
AT90
,
Bra98
]
, but are in general, weaker than the assumptions of the
\Gamma
-convergence theorems.

In
[
MHSA15
]
, the authors introduce a thermodynamically consistent model for regularized fracture, and present a finite element method for discretization.

The main aim of this paper is to study the stability and convergence of a discrete
scheme based on a low-order Galerkin FEM (for the space discretization), and an implicit finite
difference scheme (for the time discretization) for solving the time-dependent nonlinear
PDE system.
In the model described in this paper,
we allow that certain internal parameters interact with each other in a certain way.

List of difficulties.

Below, we list the problems that should be overcome to achieve the main findings of this paper:

•

The internal variable (
\varphi
) may reach zero leading to an elliptic degeneracy in the momentum equation
[
RR14
]
. To avoid this problem, a parameter
\kappa
is inserted into the equation, and we need to study the effect of the degenerate limit
\kappa\downarrow 0
in our analysis, especially in the stability estimates of the discrete solutions and the order of convergence.

•

Due to the assumptions
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
and
h=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
, we have the same consideration as above for the length scale parameter
\ell
.

•

The highly nonlinear nature of the PDE system, i.e.,
nonlinear quadratic terms appearing in the PDE system,
requires several techniques to study
convergence of the discrete solutions to the continuous solutions.

•

The imposed irreversibility condition guarantees that the crack never heals (no crack reverse). This inequality will add the complexity of the system.

Our results.
For the time-dependent nonlinear PDE system,
we present a discretization scheme based on a low-order Galerkin FEM, and an implicit finite difference scheme to discretize in space and time, respectively.
The results of this paper can be summarized as follows:

•

We present a priori estimates for the exact and discrete solutions of the momentum equation as well as the heat equation, i.e., estimates for the pointwise-in-time
L^{2}
-norms of the displacement field, the strain tensor of displacement, the pointwise derivative of the strain tensor, and the heat function (as well as for their discrete counterparts).

•

Defining the
\mathcal{A}
-norm as the norm associated with the linear elasticity operator
\mathcal{A}
, we also provide an a priori estimate for the pointwise-in-time
\mathcal{A}
-norm of the strain tensor of the displacement field (and the strain tensor of the discrete counterpart of the displacement field).

•

For
\tau
defined as the time discretization parameter, we rigorously
prove the convergence of the discrete displacement field, heat function, and internal variable to their continuous counterparts in the pointwise-in-time
L^{2}
-norms with the order of convergence
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1/2}(\tau+h))
. Additionally, since we are allowed to consider the relations
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
and
h=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
, we prove that if
the assumptions
{\tau}{\ell^{-1}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
{\tau}{\kappa^{-1}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
are satisfied, the convergence of the discrete solutions to the exact solutions is obtained in the pointwise-in-time
L^{2}
-norm with the order of convergence
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1/2}\tau+(\kappa^{-1/2}+\ell^{-1/2})h)
.

Outline of the paper.
In Section
2
, we start with fixing some notations and continue with a short introduction to the time-dependent model PDE system. Section
3
contains a fully discretized scheme based on
a FDM to discretize in time, and a Galerkin FEM for the spatial discretization. We also provide a priori estimates for the solutions of the semi-discrete formulation as well as for the solutions of the fully discretized one.
Section
4
is concerned with the main result of this paper, i.e., we investigate the convergence of the solutions of the fully discretized system to the exact solutions. Finally, in Section
5
, we present a numerical example to illustrate our theoretical results.

For

\left|\cdot\right|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}

Let

square matrices with entries in
\mathbb{R}
and
\textbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

We also define the following tensor space

\displaystyle[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{i,j})_{i,j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}}\quad:\quad\tau_{i,j}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\right\},

\displaystyle\left<\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right>:=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\tau}:\boldsymbol{\zeta}\,dx\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau},\,\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d},

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\|_{[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}}=\sqrt{\left<\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\tau}\right>}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}.

Given the tensors
\boldsymbol{\tau}:=(\tau_{ij})\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:=(\sigma_{ij})\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
, we consider the following notations for the trace of a tensor and the scalar product of two tensors

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\tau}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\tau_{ii},\qquad\boldsymbol{\tau}:\boldsymbol{\sigma}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\tau_{ij}\sigma_{ij}.

Moreover, the deviatoric
part of a tensor
\operatorname{\mathbf{dev}}:[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}\rightarrow[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
is defined as
\operatorname{\mathbf{dev}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=\boldsymbol{\sigma}-1/d\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\textbf{I}
.
Given the Lamé parameters
\lambda,\,\mu>0
, the linear mapping
\mathcal{A}:[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}\rightarrow[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}
is defined as follows

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=\lambda\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\textbf{I}+2\mu\boldsymbol{\sigma}\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}.

The operator
\mathcal{A}
is positive definite and symmetric. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}^{1/2}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=\sqrt{2\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\frac{\sqrt{2\mu+d\lambda}-\sqrt{2\mu}}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\textbf{I}\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}.

With the elasticity tensor
\mathcal{A}
, we define the following
\mathcal{A}
-norm

\left\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}:=\left\|\mathcal{A}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\|^{2}_{[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}}:=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):\boldsymbol{\tau}\,dx\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}.

Furthermore, we define the operator

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):={\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):\boldsymbol{\tau}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}.

We continue with the definition of the vector space

\displaystyle\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega):=\left\{\textbf{v}=(v_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\qquad:\qquad v_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\right\},

with the inner product

\left<\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\right>_{\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}:=\int_{\Omega}\textbf{u}\cdot\textbf{v}\,dx

for all
\textbf{u},\,\textbf{v}\in\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
which induces the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
.
When there is no risk of confusion, we use the notation
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
to denote both of the norms
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
, and we also drop
{\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
from the subscript
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
.
Moreover, for
s\geq 0
, we set

\displaystyle\textbf{H}^{s}(\Omega):=\left\{\textbf{u}=(u_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\quad:\quad u_{i}\in H^{s}(\Omega)\right\},\quad\textbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega):=\left\{\textbf{u}\in\textbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\,:\,\textbf{u}|_{\Gamma}=0\right\},

with the corresponding norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textbf{H}^{s}(\Omega)}
and semi-norm
\left|\cdot\right|_{\textbf{H}^{s}(\Omega)}
.
We also denote the strain tensor of displacement by

for
\textbf{u}\in\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Let the operator
\operatorname{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot:[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{d\times d}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{H^{-1}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\left<\operatorname{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot\,\boldsymbol{\tau},\textbf{v}\right>:=-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\tau}:\mathcal{E}(\textbf{v})dx\qquad\forall\textbf{v}\in\textbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

where
\boldsymbol{H^{-1}}(\Omega)
denotes the dual of
\boldsymbol{H_{0}^{1}}(\Omega)
.
We also define the positive part of a scalar

as follows

[a]_{+}:=\begin{cases}a&\text{if}\,\,\,a>0\\
0&\text{o.w},\end{cases}

satisfying the following properties

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left([c]_{+}-[d]_{+}\right)\left(c-d\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left([c]_{+}-[d]_{+}\right)^{2}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall c,\,d\in\mathbb{R},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left|[c]_{+}-[d]_{+}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|c-d\right|\qquad

\displaystyle\forall c,\,d\in\mathbb{R}.

2.1.
Model Problem

Let
I:=(0,T]
be a time interval where
T>0
is an arbitrary real number.
Let
\varphi:\Omega\times I\rightarrow[0,1]

]

[

]

(2.5a)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\geq}0

(2.5c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vartheta+\rho\vartheta\,\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\nabla\cdot\left(K(\vartheta)\nabla\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\,\Omega\times I,

(2.5d)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\varphi\left(-\ell\Delta\varphi+\frac{1}{\ell}\varphi+\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}_{c}}g_{c}(\varphi,\mathcal{E}(\textbf{u}))\right)}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\,\Omega\times I,

(2.5e)

u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\,\,\Gamma\times I,

(2.5f)

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi\cdot\textbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\,\,\Gamma\times I,

(2.5g)

\displaystyle\left(K(\vartheta)\,\nabla\vartheta\right)\cdot\textbf{n}

\displaystyle=\overline{{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\text{on}\,\,\Gamma\times I,

(2.5h)

\displaystyle\textbf{u}(\cdot,0)=\textbf{u}_{0},\quad\partial_{t}\textbf{u}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\textbf{v}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\,\Omega\times\{0\},

(2.5i)

\displaystyle\varphi(\cdot,0)=\varphi_{0},\quad\vartheta(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\vartheta_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\,\Omega\times\{0\},

where

denotes the outward unit vector normal to

In (
2.5a
), the parameter

is the thermal expansion constant, the function

is defined as

,

is a positive stability constant for the bulk regularization,
and

In (

is the length scale (i.e., damage regularization) parameter,

fulfils the irreversibility condition, i.e.,
\partial_{t}\varphi\leq 0
and we assume

. Using these assumptions as well as

, one can easily see that

In (
2.5c

Following

there holds

(2.6)

\displaystyle g_{c}(z_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau})(z_{1}-z_{2})\geq g(z_{1})\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):\boldsymbol{\tau}-g(z_{2})\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):\boldsymbol{\tau}\qquad\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

and hence

(2.7)

\displaystyle g_{c}(z_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau})(z_{1}-z_{2})-g_{c}(z_{2},\boldsymbol{\tau})(z_{1}-z_{2})\geq 0.

We also impose the following assumptions on the initial data

Lemma 3.2
