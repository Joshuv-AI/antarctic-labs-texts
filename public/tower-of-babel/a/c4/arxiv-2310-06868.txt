A Quantitative Hasse Principle for Weighted Quartic Forms
Daniel Flores

Abstract
. We derive, via the Hardy-Littlewood method, an asymptotic formula for the number of integral zeros of a particular class of weighted quartic forms under the assumption of non-singular local solubility. Our polynomials F ⁡ ( 𝐱 , 𝐲 ) ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x s 1 , y 1 , … , y s 2 ] F({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{s_{1}},y_{1},\ldots,y_{s_{2}}] satisfy the condition that F ⁡ ( λ 2 ​ 𝐱 , λ ​ 𝐲 ) = λ 4 ​ F ​ ( 𝐱 , 𝐲 ) F(\lambda^{2}{\mathbf{x}},\lambda{\mathbf{y}})=\lambda^{4}F({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}) . 1.
Introduction

Historically, the Hardy-Littlewood method has been largely successful when employed to show the existence of simultaneous zeros to families of diagonal polynomials. The most famous applications being those devoted to Waring’s problem and the ternary Goldbach problem. This is mainly due to the well-developed nature of the theory of exponential sums in one variable. More generally, there has been progress toward applying the circle method to show the existence of simultaneous zeros to families of forms. Most of these results stem from the work of Birch
[
1
]
, in which he uses the geometry of numbers to obtain an analogue of Weyl’s inequality in the homogeneous setting with the additional restriction that the dimension of the singular locus is not too large. In these results the number of variables required grows exponentially with the degree of these forms. In this paper we consider a slightly more general case in which the polynomials of interest are integral weighted forms.

Definition 1.1
.

We define a polynomial
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}]
to be an
integral weighted form
of degree
d
if there exists a vector
{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{s}
, satisfying
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{s})=1
, for which the equation

F(\lambda^{w_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{w_{s}}x_{s})=\lambda^{d}F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}),

holds for all complex
\lambda
. With this notation we say that the variable
x_{i}
has weight
w_{i}
.

We aim to leverage variables with large weight to achieve additional cancellation over the minor arcs. This allows us to reduce the number of variables needed to employ the circle method. In general, this problem is difficult without an analogue Weyl’s inequality, such as that of Lemma 2.1 from
[
1
]
, for weighted forms which takes into consideration the variables with large weight. A natural starting point would be to investigate weighted forms of low degree which are nearly diagonalizable, such that this problem may be tractable using known methods. In this paper, our focus lies on a specific family of weighted quartic forms. After establishing our main theorem, we provide a concise overview of other challenges potentially receptive to these concepts in section
6
.

First, we develop some notation. Let
s
be a fixed natural number and suppose for each
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
we are given integers
0\leqslant i_{k},j_{k}\leqslant 2
satisfying the inequality
i_{k}+j_{k}>0
for each
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
. Due to the nature of the weighted quartic form we consider here it is necessary to develop an indexing system for our variables which groups them in a useful manner. We accomplish this by denoting

{\mathbf{x}}_{k}=(x_{k,1},\ldots,x_{k,i_{k}})\text{ and }{\mathbf{y}}_{k}=(y_{k,1},\ldots,y_{k,j_{k}}).

We then define the integral weighted quartic forms

(1.1)

F_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{k};{\mathbf{y}}_{k})=H^{(2)}_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})+\sum_{1\leqslant l\leqslant i_{k}}H^{(2,l)}_{k}({\mathbf{y}}_{k})x_{k,l}+H^{(4)}_{k}({\mathbf{y}}_{k}),

where
H_{k}^{(2)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{i_{k}}]
and
H_{k}^{(2,l)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{j_{k}}]
are quadratic forms, and
H_{k}^{(4)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{j_{k}}]
are quartic forms. It will be important later to reference the coefficients of the polynomial
H_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})
, we do this via the labeling

(1.2)

H_{k}^{(2)}(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{0\leqslant i\leqslant 2}a_{k,i}x_{1}^{i}x_{2}^{2-i},

where if
i_{k}=1
then we simply take
a_{k,1}=a_{k,2}=0
and similarly if
i_{k}=0
then
a_{k,0}=a_{k,1}=a_{k,2}=0
. We finish this setup by implementing one final notational convention, we denote

{\mathfrak{X}}=({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{s})\text{ and }{\mathfrak{Y}}=({\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}_{s}).

Upon setting

s_{1}=\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}i_{k}\quad\text{ and}\quad s_{2}=\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}j_{k},

one sees that
{\mathfrak{X}}
is
s_{1}
-dimensional and
{\mathfrak{Y}}
is
s_{2}
-dimensional and we may sometimes write these in the form

{\mathfrak{X}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s_{1}})\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathfrak{Y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{s_{2}}).

We now define the integral weighted quartic form of interest to be

(1.3)

\Phi({\mathfrak{X}};{\mathfrak{Y}})=F_{1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1};{\mathbf{y}}_{1})+\cdots+F_{s}({\mathbf{x}}_{s};{\mathbf{y}}_{s}).

One sees that
\Phi
is an integral weighted form where
s_{1}
counts the number of variables with weight two and similarly
s_{2}
counts the number of variables with weight one. If one were to look at the related form

\tilde{\Phi}({\mathfrak{X}};{\mathfrak{Y}})=\Phi(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{s_{1}}^{2};y_{1},\ldots,y_{s_{2}}),

then Birch’s methods show us that supposing
\tilde{\Phi}
is non-singular it satisfies the
smooth
Hasse principle (as in
[
2
]
) as soon as
s_{1}+s_{2}
is at least 49, this can even be improved to 30 variables by a result of Marmon and Vishe
[
4
]
. This then allows us to say that our weighted form
\Phi
also satisfies the smooth Hasse principle as soon as
s_{1}+s_{2}\geq 30
. We improve on this as by Theorem
1.2
our weighted form
\Phi
may satisfy the smooth Hasse principle with as little as six variables.

Take
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}
be cubes of equal side lengths which are at most one, in
{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{1}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{2}}
. We define
R(X;\Phi;{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})
to be the number of integer zeros of
\Phi
where

{\mathfrak{X}}\in X^{1/2}{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathfrak{Y}}\in X^{1/4}{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}.

We call a binary form
F(x,y)
of degree
d>1
degenerate if there exist complex numbers
{\alpha},{\beta}
such that
F(x,y)=({\alpha}x+{\beta}y)^{d}
. We may then, naturally, call the polynomials
F_{k}
from (
1.1
) non-degenerate if every binary form used to define it is non-degenerate. With this notation in place we are now ready to state our main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Phi
be an integral weighted quartic form of the type defined in (
1.3
) where for each
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
the polynomial
F_{k}
is non-degenerate and the coefficient
a_{k,0}
of
H_{k}^{(2)}
is nonzero provided that
i_{k}>0
. Then as long as
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
satisfy

s_{2}>16\quad\text{when}\quad s_{1}=0,

s_{2}>10\quad\text{when}\quad s_{1}=1,

or

s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4>2\quad\text{when}\quad s_{1}\geqslant 2,

then we have the asymptotic formula

R(X;\Phi;{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})=X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}\left(\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}+o(1)\right),

where

\sigma_{p}=\lim_{h\to\infty}p^{h(1-s_{1}-s_{2})}\#\left\{({\mathfrak{X}},{\mathfrak{Y}})\in({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{h}{\mathbb{Z}})^{s_{1}+s_{2}}:\Phi({\mathfrak{X}};{\mathfrak{Y}})\equiv 0\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,p^{h}\right\},

and

\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)})=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{|{\beta}|\leqslant Q}\int_{{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}}\int_{{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}}e({\beta}\Phi({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};{\boldsymbol{\rho}})){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}{\,{\rm d}}{\beta}.

Additionally, if we assume
\Phi
has non-singular solutions locally everywhere then

\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}\asymp 1,

and there exists a choice of cubes
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}
for which one may show that
\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})\asymp 1
.

One sees that when
s_{1}\geq 2
the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
is near-optimal with respect to the number of variables required by the Hardy-Littlewood circle method. This may be seen by looking at the case where there are no mixed terms and every coefficient is 1, that is to say

\Phi({\mathfrak{X}};{\mathfrak{Y}})=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s_{2}}y_{j}^{4}.

This diagonal case is a matter of classical theory. We refer the interested reader to section 15 of
[
7
]
for further reading. From this classical case we have by the convexity bound that the expected asymptotic formula from Theorem
1.2
may only be obtained via a conventional circle method approach when
s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4>2.

As is the case with many applications of the circle method the main difficulty we encounter are the minor arcs, here we give a rough outline of our methods for the minor arcs. Although we assume that
s_{2}
is even for the purpose of outlining our strategy, our proof also covers the case when
s_{2}
is odd. We begin by using Proposition
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
to essentially remove all the monomials which contain variables of mixed weights and additionally diagonalize the variables with weight two because it cost us no extra effort to do so. We may do this at the cost of losing a factor of
(\log X)^{s_{1}}
which is ultimately harmless. This process, in essence, allows us to bound the minor arc contribution relevant to our problem by bounding the minor arc contribution of an associated counting function. The latter function counts the number of integral zeros of the weighted quartic form

\tilde{\Phi}({\mathfrak{X}};{\mathfrak{Y}})=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s_{2}/2}H_{j}^{(4)}(y_{2j-1},y_{2j}),

where for each
j
, the binary quartic form
H_{j}^{(4)}
is non-degenerate and our solutions satisfy

{\mathfrak{X}}\in X^{1/2}\tilde{{\mathfrak{B}}}^{(1)}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathfrak{Y}}\in X^{1/4}\tilde{{\mathfrak{B}}}^{(2)},

where
\tilde{{\mathfrak{B}}}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{{\mathfrak{B}}}^{(2)}
are slight modifications of the initial boxes
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}
. At this point, we can easily get enough savings in the minor arcs if
s_{1}\geq 4
. When
s_{1}=0
a generalized version of this problem has already been looked at by Wooley
[
8
]
where his lower bound of
s_{2}>16
agrees with ours. The interesting cases are when
1\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant 3
. We take care of these cases via the use of the optimal mean value estimate, Lemma
3.3
.

Preliminary Setup

Our basic parameter is
X
, an (eventually) large positive number. Whenever
\varepsilon
appears in a statement, either implicitly or explicitly, we assert that the statement holds for every
\varepsilon>0
. In this paper, implicit constants in Vinogradov’s notation
\ll
and
\gg
may depend on
\varepsilon
and on the coefficients of the polynomial
\Phi
. We also make use of the vector notation
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
where
r
is dependent on the context of the argument. Whenever the notation
|{\mathbf{x}}|
is used for a vector
{\mathbf{x}}
we mean
\max_{i}{|x_{i}|}
. Also, when the notation
\|f\|_{p}
is used for an integrable function
f
we mean the usual norm on
L^{p}([0,1])
, and we write
\|f\|_{L^{p}(E)}
to denote the
L^{p}(E)
norm where
E
is some real measurable set. As is conventional in analytic number theory, we write
e(z)
for
e^{2\pi iz}
.

Consider cubes
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{1}}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{2}}
, each having side length
2\eta
for some positive
\eta\leqslant 1
. Let
{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*}\in[-1,1]^{s_{1}}
denote the center of
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}
and
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}\in[-1,1]^{s_{2}}
denote the center of
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}
. Following the same indexing system that was set up in the introduction, we write

{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*}=({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*}_{s})\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}=({\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}_{s}),

where
{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*}_{k}=(\gamma_{k,1},\ldots,\gamma_{k,i_{k}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{i_{k}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}_{k}=(\rho_{k,1},\ldots,\rho_{k,j_{k}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{j_{k}}
. Now, for a given set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the boxes

{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}_{k}(X,E)=\left\{{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in E^{i_{k}}:\left|\frac{{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}}{X^{1/2}}-{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*}_{k}\right|<\eta\right\},

and

{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}(X,E)=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in E^{j_{k}}:\left|\frac{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{X^{1/4}}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}_{k}\right|<\eta\right\}.

Then we define

(2.1)

f_{k}({\alpha})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}_{k}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})\\
{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})\end{subarray}}e\left({\alpha}F_{k}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})\right),\quad f({\alpha})=\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}f_{k}({\alpha}),

(2.2)

S_{k}(q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{r}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{i_{k}}\\
{\mathbf{s}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{j_{k}}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a}{q}F_{k}({\mathbf{r}};{\mathbf{s}})\right),\quad S(q,a)=\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}S_{k}(q,a),

and

(2.3)

v_{k}(X,{\beta})=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}_{k}(X,{\mathbb{R}})\\
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}(X,{\mathbb{R}})\end{subarray}}e\left({\beta}F_{k}({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\right){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\quad I(X,{\beta})=\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}v_{k}(X,{\beta}).

With this notation in hand we have by orthogonality

(2.4)

R(X;\Phi;{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})=\int_{0}^{1}f({\alpha}){\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}.

We begin by first estimating the exponential sums
f_{k}
near rational points.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have that

f_{k}({\alpha})=q^{-(i_{k}+j_{k})}S_{k}(q,a)v_{k}(X,{\alpha}-a/q)+O\left(X^{i_{k}/2+j_{k}/4-1/4}\left(q+|q{\alpha}-a|X\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
k
be fixed, then we may parametrize
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}_{k}
and
{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}
by writing

{\mathbf{x}}=q{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{r}},\quad{\mathbf{y}}=q{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{s}}.

Then upon setting
{\beta}={\alpha}-a/q
one obtains

(2.5)

f_{k}({\alpha})=\sum_{({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{s}})\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{i_{k}+j_{k}}}e\left(\frac{a}{q}F_{k}({\mathbf{r}};{\mathbf{s}})\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{r}})\\
{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}({\mathbf{s}})\end{subarray}}e\left({\beta}F_{k}(q{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{r}};q{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{s}})\right),

where

{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{r}})=\left\{{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{i_{k}}:\left|\frac{q{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{r}}}{X^{1/2}}-{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*}_{k}\right|<\eta\right\},

and

{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}({\mathbf{s}})=\left\{{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{j_{k}}:\left|\frac{q{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{r}}}{X^{1/4}}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}_{k}\right|<\eta\right\}.

Then by making use of the mean value theorem as in
[
3
, Lemma 4.2]
and noting that
0<i_{k}+j_{k}
, we obtain that inner sum over
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
in equation
2.5
is equal to

q^{-(i_{k}+j_{k})}v_{k}(X,{\beta})+O\left(\frac{X^{i_{k}/2+j_{k}/4-1/4}}{q^{i_{k}+j_{k}-1}}(1+|{\beta}|X)\right).

Upon substituting this into (
2.5
) we obtain the desired result.
∎

For any
\delta>0
and pair
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define

{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}(q,a)=\{{\alpha}\in[0,1):|{\alpha}-a/q|\leqslant X^{\delta-1}\},

and so our major arcs are defined as

{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant a\leqslant q\leqslant X^{\delta}\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}(q,a),

with the minor arcs being
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}=[0,1)\backslash{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}
. With our major/minor arcs established we immediately obtain, via Lemma
2.1
and equation (
2.1
), a good estimate for the exponential sum
f({\alpha})
over the major arcs.

Corollary 2.2
.

Given
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}(q,a)
we have that

f({\alpha})=q^{-(s_{1}+s_{2})}S(q,a)I(X,{\alpha}-a/q)+O\left(X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4+2\delta-1/4}\right).

Next, we define

{\mathfrak{S}}(Q)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}^{q}q^{-(s_{1}+s_{2})}S(q,a),

J(Q,X)=\int_{|{\beta}|\leqslant QX^{-1}}I(X,{\beta}){\,{\rm d}}{\beta}.

We conclude with a Lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
0<\delta<1/20
the asymptotic formula

\int_{{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}}f({\alpha}){\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}={\mathfrak{S}}(X^{\delta})J(X^{\delta},1)X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}+o(X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}),

holds

Proof.

By applying Corollary
2.2
to each interval in the major arcs and noting that the measure of the major arcs is
O(X^{3\delta-1})
we see that the major arc contribution is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant q\leqslant X^{\delta}\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}q^{-(s_{1}+s_{2})}S(q,a)\int_{|{\alpha}-a/q|\leqslant X^{\delta-1}}I(X,{\alpha}-a/q){\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}+o(X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}).

Via the substitution
{\beta}={\alpha}-a/q
we note that the inner integral is now independent of
a,q
. Thus we see that

\int_{{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}}f({\alpha}){\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}={\mathfrak{S}}(X^{\delta})J(X^{\delta},X)+o(X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}).

All that is left to show is that

J(X^{\delta},X)=J(X^{\delta},1)X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}.

One establishes this by first recalling (
1.3
) and note that via a change of variables we have

I(X,{\beta})=X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4}I(1,X{\beta}),

whence

\displaystyle J(X^{\delta},X)

\displaystyle=X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4}\int_{|{\beta}|\leqslant X^{\delta-1}}I(1,X{\beta}){\,{\rm d}}{\beta}

\displaystyle=X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}\int_{|{\beta}^{\prime}|\leqslant X^{\delta}}I(1,{\beta}^{\prime}){\,{\rm d}}{\beta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}J(X^{\delta},1).

∎

3.
The Minor Arcs

Before we begin, some notation must be established. Given any particular
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
, let
M_{k}
be the largest coefficient in absolute value of the polynomial
F_{k}
, and then define

(3.1)

M=\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}20M_{k}^{2}.

This number will be useful later. Referencing our labeling scheme for the coefficients of
H_{k}^{(2)}
as in (
1.2
), we define the constant
\Delta_{k}=a_{k,1}^{2}-4a_{k,0}a_{k,2}
and the polynomial

\delta_{k}({\mathbf{v}})=a_{k,1}H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})-2a_{k,0}H_{k}^{(2,2)}({\mathbf{v}}).

It is important to note that by our hypothesis in Theorem
1.2
, we have that if
i_{k}=2
then
a_{k,0}
and
\Delta_{k}
are nonzero, and if
i_{k}=1
then we only have that
a_{k,0}
is nonzero. It is important that we now develop robust notation which will allow us to concisely state our results regardless of which situation we find ourselves in, it is for this reason that we take some time to establish the following quantities.

For any
\textbf{b}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
we define

\chi_{k}^{(i)}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\begin{cases}\frac{b_{1}}{2a_{k,0}}-\Delta_{k}\xi_{1},&\text{when }i=1,\\
\frac{a_{k,1}b_{1}}{2a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}}-a_{k,1}\xi_{1}-\frac{b_{2}}{\Delta_{k}}+\xi_{2},&\text{when }i=2.\end{cases}

Also, for any set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, real variables
Y,Z
, and
{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{i_{k}}
we define the sets
B_{k}(Y,Z,E,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})\subset E^{i_{k}}
to be

\begin{array}[]{ll}\{0\}&\text{when }i_{k}=0,\\
\left\{b_{1}\in E:\left|\frac{b_{1}}{2a_{k,0}Z^{1/2}}-\gamma_{1}\right|\leqslant Y\right\}&\text{when }i_{k}=1,\\
\left\{(b_{1},b_{2})\in E^{2}:\left|\frac{b_{1}}{2a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}Z^{1/2}}-\gamma_{1}\right|\leqslant Y,\left|\frac{b_{2}}{\Delta_{k}Z^{1/2}}-\gamma_{2}\right|\leqslant Y\right\}&\text{when }i_{k}=2.\end{array}

Here, whenever it is convenient to do so, we abbreviate
B_{k}(Y,Z,E,\bf 0)
to
B_{k}(Y,Z,E)
.

Now for the sake of brevity and generality, we define the following table of functions

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}H^{(4)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}}),&i_{k}=0,\\
H^{(4)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})-\frac{1}{4a_{k,0}}\left(H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2},&i_{k}=1,\\
H^{(4)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})-\frac{1}{4a_{k,0}}\left(H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}}\left(\delta_{k}({\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2},&i_{k}=2,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{(2)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&i_{k}=0,\\
-\chi_{k}^{(1)}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}}),&i_{k}=1,\\
-\chi_{k}^{(1)}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})+\chi_{k}^{(2)}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\delta_{k}({\mathbf{v}}),&i_{k}=2,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&i_{k}=0,\\
\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}},&i_{k}=1,\\
\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}}-\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}},&i_{k}=2,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}^{(2)}_{k}({\mathbf{u}};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&i_{k}=0,\\
\chi_{k}^{(1)}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u_{1},&i_{k}=1,\\
\chi_{k}^{(1)}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u_{1}-\chi_{k}^{(2)}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u_{2},&i_{k}=2,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}_{k}({\mathbf{n}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&i_{k}=0,\\
n_{1}\xi_{1},&i_{k}=1,\\
n_{1}\xi_{1}+n_{2}\xi_{2},&i_{k}=2.\end{cases}

We are now well enough equipped to state the following technical result.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any real
{\alpha}
, and integer
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
we have that

f_{k}({\alpha})=\frac{1}{\#B_{k}(1,1,{\mathbb{N}})}\sum_{\textbf{b}\in B_{k}(1,1,{\mathbb{N}})}\int_{[0,1]^{i_{k}}}K_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})g_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})h_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}},

where

h_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})}e\left({\alpha}\mathfrak{h}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})+\mathfrak{h}^{(2)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right),

g_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{|{\mathbf{u}}|\leqslant MX^{1/2}}e\left({\alpha}\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{u}})+\mathfrak{g}^{(2)}_{k}({\mathbf{u}};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right),

and

K_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}\in B_{k}(\eta,X,{\mathbb{Z}},{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}_{k}^{*})}e(\mathfrak{K}_{k}({\mathbf{n}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})).

Proof.

We begin by considering the case
i_{k}=2
. Using the substitutions
{\mathbf{v}}={\mathbf{y}}
,

u_{1}=2a_{k,0}x_{1}+a_{k,1}x_{2}+H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}}),

and

u_{2}=\Delta_{k}x_{2}+\delta_{k}({\mathbf{v}}),

one obtains

F_{k}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})=\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}}-\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}}+\mathfrak{h}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}}).

This lets us rewrite the exponential sum for
f_{k}({\alpha})
as long as we are careful to sum over the correct range for
u_{1},u_{2}
. Let

s_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})=u_{2}-\delta_{k}({\mathbf{v}}),

and

t_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\Delta_{k}u_{1}-\Delta_{k}H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})-a_{k,1}s_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}}).

We may then define the correct range for
u_{1},u_{2}
by introducing the sets

\displaystyle S_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=\left\{u_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}:s_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})\equiv 0\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,\Delta_{k}\right\},

\displaystyle T_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{v}},u_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\{u_{1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}:t_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\equiv 0\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,2a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}\right\},

\displaystyle S_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=\left\{u_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}:\left|\frac{s_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})}{\Delta_{k}X^{1/2}}-\gamma^{*}_{k,2}\right|\leqslant\eta\right\},

\displaystyle T_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{v}},u_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\{u_{1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}:\left|\frac{t_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})}{2a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}X^{1/2}}-\gamma^{*}_{k,1}\right|\leqslant\eta\right\}.

Then setting
S_{k}({\mathbf{v}})=S_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{v}})\cap S_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{v}})
and
T_{k}({\mathbf{v}},u_{2})=T_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{v}},u_{2})\cap T_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{v}},u_{2})
we may write

f_{k}({\alpha})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{2}\in S_{k}({\mathbf{v}})\end{subarray}}\sum_{u_{1}\in T_{k}({\mathbf{v}},u_{2})}e\left({\alpha}\left(\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}}-\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}}+\mathfrak{h}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})\right)\right).

Given the definitions of
u_{1},u_{2}
and noting that
|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}_{k}^{*}|,|\eta|\leqslant 1
, one easily sees that
|{\mathbf{u}}|\leqslant MX^{1/2}
where
M
is defined in (
3.1
). This bound will be used later once we get rid of the pesky restrictions in the summand. We may immediately do this by using detector functions which pick up membership in
S_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{v}})
and
T_{k}^{(1)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})
. We do this by introducing the functions

\kappa_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\frac{1}{2|a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}|}\sum_{b_{1}=1}^{2|a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}|}e\left(\frac{b_{1}}{2a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}}t_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\right),

\kappa_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})=\frac{1}{|\Delta_{k}|}\sum_{b_{2}=1}^{|\Delta_{k}|}e\left(\frac{b_{2}}{\Delta_{k}}s_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})\right).

We may similarly pick up membership in
S_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{v}})
and
T_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})
by introducing the functions

\lambda_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\left|\frac{n_{1}}{2a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}X^{1/2}}-\gamma^{*}_{k,1}\right|\leqslant\eta}e\left(\left(n_{1}-t_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\right)\xi_{1}\right){\,{\rm d}}\xi_{1},

\lambda_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\left|\frac{n_{2}}{\Delta_{k}X^{1/2}}-\gamma^{*}_{k,2}\right|\leqslant\eta}e\left(\left(n_{2}-s_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})\right)\xi_{2}\right){\,{\rm d}}\xi_{2}.

Thus
\kappa_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})\lambda_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{k}({\mathbf{v}})}(u_{2})
and
\kappa_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\lambda_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\mathbbm{1}_{T_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})}(u_{1})
. Plugging in these indicator functions we see that
f_{k}({\alpha})
is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}_{k}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})\\
|{\mathbf{u}}|\leqslant MX^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{T_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})}(u_{1})\mathbbm{1}_{S_{k}({\mathbf{v}})}(u_{2})e\left({\alpha}\left(\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}}-\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{4a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}}+\mathfrak{h}^{(1)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})\right)\right).

By expanding
\mathbbm{1}_{T_{k}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})}(u_{1})\mathbbm{1}_{S_{k}({\mathbf{v}})}(u_{2})
as exponential sums using our detector functions, we can interchange the order of summation and integration, making the summation ranges independent of each other. After this, we can group terms into separate exponential sums and arrive at the following equation.

f_{k}({\alpha})=\frac{1}{2|a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant b_{1}\leqslant 2|a_{k,0}|\\
1\leqslant b_{2}\leqslant|\Delta_{k}|\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}K_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})g_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})h_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

This establishes the case
i_{k}=2
of the Proposition. One may then, via a similar yet simpler proof, show that the case
i_{k}=1
follows and the case
i_{k}=0
is trivially true.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
E\subset[0,1]
be any Lebesgue measurable set and following our previously established indexing suppose that
\textbf{b}=(\textbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{b}_{s})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s_{1}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{s})\in[0,1]^{s_{1}}
. In addition suppose that one has a bound of the form

\Xi_{E}(\textbf{b};{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\int_{E}\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}|g_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})h_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})|{\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}\ll X^{\sigma},

where
\sigma
is fixed and the implicit constants are independent of
b
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. Then we have

\int_{E}|f({\alpha})|{\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}\ll X^{\sigma}\log(X)^{s_{1}}.

Proof.

Set

B=\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}B_{k}(1,1,{\mathbb{N}}),\quad K({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}|K_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})|.

We may apply Proposition
3.1
to each polynomial
f_{k}
where
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
. Then, upon applying the triangle inequality and switching the order of summation and integration one obtains the relation

(3.2)

\int_{E}|f({\alpha})|{\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}\leqslant\frac{1}{\#B}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in B}\int_{[0,1]^{s_{1}}}\Xi(\textbf{b};{\boldsymbol{\xi}})K({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

Substituting the bound
\Xi(\textbf{b};{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\ll X^{\sigma}
into (
3.2
) and noting that the implicit constant is independent of
\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
we obtain

\int_{E}|f({\alpha})|{\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}\ll\frac{X^{\sigma}}{\#B}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in B}\int_{[0,1]^{s_{1}}}K({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

All that is left to prove is that

\frac{1}{\#B}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in B}\int_{[0,1]^{s_{1}}}K({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\ll(\log X)^{s_{1}},

this is however immediately clear upon recalling the definition of
K({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
and making use of the basic estimate

\int_{0}^{1}\biggl|\sum_{|n|\leqslant X}e(-n\xi)\biggr|{\,{\rm d}}\xi\ll\log X.

∎

As a consequence of Lemma
3.2
, if we can obtain a bound for
\Xi_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}}(\textbf{b};{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, we may transfer this to a bound over the minor arcs. Before obtaining such a bound we first require a mean value estimate that allows us to deal with the cases when
1\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant 3
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G^{(1)}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
,
G^{(2)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Also, let
H^{(1)}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[y_{1},y_{2}]
be a non-degenerate homogeneous quartic, and
H^{(2)}\in{\mathbb{R}}[y_{1},y_{2}]
be a non-degenerate homogeneous quadratic. Then for positive numbers
P,Q
,
|\eta|\leqslant 1
, and
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in[-1,1]^{2}
, we define the exponential sums

G({\alpha})=\sum_{|x|\leqslant P}e({\alpha}G^{(1)}x^{2}+G^{(2)}x),

H({\alpha})=\sum_{\left|\frac{{\mathbf{y}}}{Q}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\leqslant\eta}e\left({\alpha}H^{(1)}({\mathbf{y}})+H^{(2)}({\mathbf{y}})\right).

Then for large
P,Q
we have the following mean value bound

\left\|G({\alpha})H({\alpha})\right\|_{2}^{2}\ll PQ^{2+\varepsilon}+Q^{4}P^{\varepsilon},

where the implicit constant is only dependent on
G^{(1)}
and the coefficients of
H^{(1)}
.

Proof.

Let
N
be the smallest natural number such that
NG^{(1)}H^{(1)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[y_{1},y_{2}]
, then by a change of variables one has that

\left\|G({\alpha})H({\alpha})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant N\left\|G(N{\alpha})H(N{\alpha})\right\|_{2}^{2},

where, by orthogonality, the right hand side is bounded by
N
times the number of integer solutions of

NG^{(1)}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})=NH^{(1)}({\mathbf{y}}_{1})-NH^{(1)}({\mathbf{y}}_{2}),

where

|x_{i}|\leqslant P\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\frac{{\mathbf{y}}_{i}}{Q}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\leqslant\eta.

We then we split into two cases, if
|x_{1}|=|x_{2}|
, of which there are
O(P)
many ways this can happen, then we count the number of solutions of
NH^{(1)}({\mathbf{y}}_{1})-NH^{(1)}({\mathbf{y}}_{2})=0
. By orthogonality this quantity is equal to

\Biggl\|\sum_{\left|\frac{{\mathbf{y}}}{Q}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\leqslant\eta}e\left({\alpha}NH^{(1)}({\mathbf{y}})\right)\Biggr\|^{2}{\,{\rm d}}{\alpha}\ll Q^{2+\varepsilon},

where the bound comes from Theorem 2 of
[
8
]
. So in the case
|x_{1}|=|x_{2}|
there are
O(PQ^{2+\varepsilon})
choices for our variables. If
|x_{1}|\neq|x_{2}|
, then let
n=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\neq 0
, then there are at most
O(Q^{4})
choices for the
y_{i}
and by the divisor estimate at most
O(P^{\varepsilon})
choices for
x_{1},x_{2}
. So in the case
|x_{1}|\neq|x_{2}|
there are at most
O(Q^{4}P^{\varepsilon})
choices for our variables. The fact that the implicit constant is dependent only on
G^{(1)}
and the coefficients of
H^{(1)}
is clear from our application of orthogonality and the way we chose
N
.
∎

With this mean value estimate, we are now ready to bound
\Xi_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}}(\textbf{b};{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\textbf{b}=(\textbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{b}_{s})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s_{1}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{s})\in[0,1]^{s_{1}}
. Then we have that

\Xi_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}}(\textbf{b};{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\ll X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1-\delta/32+\varepsilon},

where the implicit constant is dependent only on the coefficients of
\Phi
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Referencing the definition of the exponential sums
g_{k}
from Proposition
3.1
one notes that in the case that
i_{k}=2
this exponential sum may be split and written as the product of two single variable exponential sums. Thus one may write

(3.3)

\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}g_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})=\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}G_{i}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

where, in reference to the notation of Lemma
3.3
, each of the exponential sums
G_{i}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
satisfy the conditions on
G
with the rational number
G^{(1)}
dependent only on the coefficients of
\Phi
, the real number
G^{(2)}
dependent on the coefficients of
\Phi
,
b
, and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. Finally, the length of the summation is
P=MX^{1/2}
.

By a similar thought process, we may write

(3.4)

\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}h_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})=\left(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s_{2}/2}H_{j}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)H_{0}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

where instead of splitting exponential sums we sometimes combine exponential sums over a single variable into an exponential sum over two variables. In reference to the notation of Lemma
3.3
, each of the exponential sums
H_{i}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant s_{2}/2
satisfy the conditions on
H
with the rational binary quartic
H^{(1)}
dependent only on the coefficients of
\Phi
, the real binary quadratic
H^{(2)}
dependent on the coefficients of
\Phi
,
b
, and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. Finally, the length of the sum being
Q=X^{1/4}
and some center
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
coming from
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}
. Additionally, the function
H_{0}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is either
1
if
s_{2}
is even, or it is equal to some
h_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})
where
j_{k}=1
if
s_{2}
is odd. It is important to note here that with respect to
{\alpha}
the arguments of all of these exponential sums are dependent only on the coefficients of
\Phi
. Setting
m=\min\{s_{1},4\}
we define

S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\begin{cases}\prod\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 8}H_{j}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),&s_{1}=0,\\
G_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\prod\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 5}H_{j}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),&s_{1}=1,\\
\prod\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}G_{i}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\prod\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 4-m}H_{j}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),&s_{1}\geqslant 2,\end{cases}

then in reference to equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) we implicitly define
S_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
to be the product of the remaining exponential sums such that it satisfies

S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})S_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant s}g_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})h_{k}({\alpha};\textbf{b}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}).

Thus, by an application of Hölder’s inequality we obtain the bound

(3.5)

\Xi_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}}(\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\leqslant\left\|S_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta})}\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}.

By our conditions on the number of variables from Theorem
1.2
, it must be the case that the function
S_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is a non-empty product of exponential sums which contains at least one of the following three exponential sums,

G_{s_{1}}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),H_{s_{2}/2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\text{ or }H_{0}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

Via a direct application of either Weyl’s inequality (see
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
) or Theorem 1 from
[
8
]
we obtain the following bounds,

\sup_{{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}}|G_{s_{1}}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\ll X^{1/2-\delta/4+\varepsilon},

\sup_{{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}}|H_{0}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\ll X^{1/4-\delta/32+\varepsilon},

and

\sup_{{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}}|H_{s_{2}/2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\ll X^{1/2-\delta/16+\varepsilon}.

We note here that, by our previous comment, the implicit constants arising from these pointwise bounds are only dependent on the coefficients of
\Phi
and
\varepsilon
. Regardless of which situation we find ourselves in we, at worst, obtain the bound

(3.6)

\left\|S_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta})}\ll\begin{cases}X^{s_{2}/4-4-\delta/32+\varepsilon},&s_{1}=0,\\
X^{s_{2}/4-5/2-\delta/32+\varepsilon},&s_{1}=1,\\
X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-2-\delta/32+\varepsilon},&s_{1}\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

All that is left is to bound
\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}
. There are a few cases to consider. If
s_{1}\geq 4
, by Hölder’s inequality and Hua’s Lemma (see
[
6
, Lemma 2.5]
) we have that
\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}
is bounded above by

\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}\left\|G_{i}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{4}\ll X^{1+\varepsilon}.

If
s_{1}=3
, by Hölder’s inequality, Hua’s Lemma, and Lemma
3.3
we see that
\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}
is bounded above by

\left\|G_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})H_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{2}\left\|G_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{4}\left\|G_{3}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{4}\ll X^{1+\varepsilon}.

If
s_{1}=2
, by Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
3.3
we see that again
\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}
is bounded above by

\left\|G_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})H_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{2}\left\|G_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})H_{2}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{2}\ll X^{1+\varepsilon},

If
s_{1}=1
, by Hölder’s inequality, Lemma
3.3
, and Theorem 2 from
[
8
]
have that
\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}
is bounded above by

\|G_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})H_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{2}\prod_{2\leqslant j\leqslant 5}\|H_{j}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{8}\ll X^{2+\varepsilon}.

Finally, if
s_{1}=0
, by Hölder’s inequality and Theorem 2 from
[
8
]
have that
\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}
is bounded above by

\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 8}\|H_{j}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{8}\ll X^{3+\varepsilon}.

Combining these bounds we obtain,

(3.7)

\left\|S_{1}({\alpha};\textbf{b},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|_{1}\ll\begin{cases}X^{3+\varepsilon},&s_{1}=0,\\
X^{2+\varepsilon},&s_{2}=1,\\
X^{1+\varepsilon},&s_{2}\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

We, again, note that all of of our implicit constants are only dependent on the coefficients of
\Phi
and
\varepsilon
. Combining the bounds (
3.5
), (
3.6
), and (
3.7
) we obtain the desired result.
∎

Combining the result of Proposition
3.4
with Lemma
3.2
with
E={\mathfrak{m}}_{\delta}
, and Lemma
2.3
we deduce the following result.

Corollary 3.5
.

For any
0<\delta<1/20
we have that

R(X;{\mathfrak{B}}_{1},{\mathfrak{B}}_{2})=\mathfrak{S}(X^{\delta})J(X^{\delta},1)X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}+o(X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}).

4.
The Singular Series

We begin by establishing the absolute convergence of the
complete
singular series

(4.1)

{\mathfrak{S}}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}A(q),\text{ where}\quad A(q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}^{q}q^{-(s_{1}+s_{2})}S(q,a).

In order to achieve this we introduce the auxiliary functions, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
and fixed
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{i_{k}}
,
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{j_{k}}
, defined by

x_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=2a_{k,0}u_{1}+a_{k,1}u_{2}+H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})\quad,\quad x_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})=\Delta_{k}u_{2}+\delta_{k}({\mathbf{v}}),

where, if
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we take
u_{2}=0
. Next, define

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&i_{k}=0,\\
4a_{k,0},&i_{k}=1,\\
4a_{k,0}\Delta_{k},&i_{k}=2,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&i_{k}=0,\\
x_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})^{2},&i_{k}=1,\\
\Delta_{k}x_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})^{2}-x_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})^{2},&i_{k}=2,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}H^{(4)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}}),&i_{k}=0,\\
4a_{k,0}H^{(4)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})-\left(H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2},&i_{k}=1,\\
4a_{k,0}\Delta_{k}H^{(4)}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})-\Delta_{k}\left(H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2}+\left(\delta_{k}({\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2},&i_{k}=2.\end{cases}

Then we define the exponential sum

T_{k}(q,a)=\sum_{{\mathbf{v}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{j_{k}}}e\left(\frac{a}{q}\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})\right)\sum_{{\mathbf{u}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{i_{k}}}e\left(\frac{a}{q}\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\right).

One then has a relation between
S_{k}
from (
2.2
) and the function
T_{k}.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any natural numbers
a,q
one has that

S_{k}(q,a)=\left(\frac{(a,A_{k})}{A_{k}}\right)^{i_{k}+j_{k}}T_{k}\left(\frac{A_{k}q}{(a,A_{k})},\frac{a}{(a,A_{k})}\right).

Proof.

One begins by noting that

\displaystyle S_{k}(q,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{u}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{i_{k}}\\
{\mathbf{v}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{j_{k}}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a}{qA_{k}}\left(\tilde{{\mathfrak{h}}}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})+\tilde{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\right)\right).

Upon setting

a^{\prime}=\frac{a}{(a,A_{k})}\quad\text{and}\quad q^{\prime}=\frac{A_{k}q}{(a,A_{k})},

one sees from periodicity that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{u}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{i_{k}}\\
{\mathbf{v}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{j_{k}}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a}{qA_{k}}\left(\tilde{{\mathfrak{h}}}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})+\tilde{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\right)\right)=\left(\frac{q}{q^{\prime}}\right)^{i_{k}+j_{k}}T_{k}(q^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

∎

We now note that both
S_{k}
and
T_{k}
satisfy the quasi-multiplicative property that when
(a,q)=(b,r)=(q,r)=1
we have

(4.2)

S_{k}(qr,ar+bq)=S_{k}(q,a)S_{k}(r,b),

and

(4.3)

T_{k}(qr,ar+bq)=T_{k}(q,a)T_{k}(r,b).

The proof of this is exactly the same as the one given for Lemma 2.10 in
[
6
]
. Upon recalling (
4.1
) and (
2.2
) we see that (
4.2
) and an application of the Chinese Remainder Theorem leads us to conclude that
A(q)
is a multiplicative function, thus to establish the absolute convergence of the complete singular series we need only provide a good bound on
A(q)
when
q
is a prime power.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given a prime
p
and any
h\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have that

A(p^{h})\ll\begin{cases}p^{-5/4},&h=1,\\
p^{-9h/16},&h\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

First, we may suppose that
p>M
or else the result is trivial since
2\leqslant p\leqslant M=O(1)
whence
p\asymp 1
. First, we proceed by bounding
T_{k}(p^{h},a)
for some
a
which is relatively prime to
p
and transferring this information to a bound on
S_{k}(p^{h},a)
.

Consider the case in which
i_{k}=2
. Since
p>M
it must be the case that
(p^{h},A_{k})=1
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
, thus for any fixed
u_{2},{\mathbf{v}}
we see that the set of residues

\left\{2a_{k,0}u_{1}+a_{k,1}u_{2}+H_{k}^{(2,1)}({\mathbf{v}})\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,p^{h}:1\leqslant u_{1}\leqslant p^{h}\right\},

is in bijective correspondence with
{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{h}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant u_{1}\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(\frac{a\Delta_{k}}{p^{h}}\left(x_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(\frac{a\Delta_{k}}{p^{h}}x^{2}\right),

note that this is independent of
u_{2}
and
{\mathbf{v}}
. The same reasoning also leads to the conclusion that

\sum_{1\leqslant u_{2}\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(-\frac{a}{p^{h}}\left(x_{k}^{(2)}(u_{2},{\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2}\right)=\sum_{1\leqslant y\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(-\frac{a}{p^{h}}y^{2}\right),

which is independent of
{\mathbf{v}}
. Combining these we see that

\sum_{{\mathbf{u}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{h}{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}}e\left(\frac{a}{p}\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\right)=\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(\frac{a\Delta_{k}}{p^{h}}x^{2}\right)\sum_{1\leqslant y\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(-\frac{a}{p^{h}}y^{2}\right),

since this is independent of
{\mathbf{v}}
we conclude that
T_{k}(p^{h},a)
is bounded by

\left|\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(\frac{a\Delta_{k}}{p^{h}}x^{2}\right)\right|\left|\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant p^{h}}e\left(-\frac{a}{p^{h}}x^{2}\right)\right|\left|\sum_{{\mathbf{v}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{h}{\mathbb{Z}})^{j_{k}}}e\left(\frac{a}{p^{h}}\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}_{k}({\mathbf{v}})\right)\right|.

In the case
h=1
, one may use the standard Gauss sum estimate to obtain square root cancellation in the first two exponential sums. We then utilize Corollary 2F of
[
5
]
on the third exponential sum to obtain square root cancellation over one of the variables while trivially summing over any remaining variables. This yields the bound

T_{k}(p,a)\ll p^{1+3j_{k}/4}.

In the case
h\geqslant 2
we may apply Weyl’s inequality to the first two exponential sums and either Weyl’s inequality if
j_{k}=1
or Theorem 1 from
[
8
]
if
j_{k}=2
to obtain the bound

T_{k}(p^{h},a)\ll p^{h(1+7j_{k}/8+\varepsilon)}.

All in all we have established that for
i_{k}=2
we have the bound

(4.4)

T_{k}(p^{h},a)\ll\begin{cases}p^{i_{k}/2+3j_{k}/4},&h=1,\\
p^{h(i_{k}/2+7j_{k}/8+\varepsilon)},&h\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

One may easily extend this bound to work for
i_{k}=1
or
0
by noting that the only element of our argument that may change would be that we need only consider
i_{k}
many quadratic exponential sums each of which obtains square root cancellation.

Now we will show that the bound (
4.4
) may also be applied to
S_{k}(p^{h},a)
. Since
(p^{h},A_{k})=1
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
and that
a
is relatively prime to
p
, we have by the Chinese Remainder Theorem that there exists

b\in({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{h}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{k}\in\left({\mathbb{Z}}/\frac{A_{k}}{(a,A_{k})}{\mathbb{Z}}\right)^{\times},

such that

\frac{a}{(a,A_{k})}=b\frac{A_{k}}{(a,A_{k})}+c_{k}p^{h}.

Then by Lemma
4.1
and (
4.3
) we conclude that

\displaystyle S_{k}(p^{h},a)=\left(\frac{(a,A_{k})}{A_{k}}\right)^{i_{k}+j_{k}}T_{k}\left(\frac{A_{k}}{(a,A_{k})},c_{k}\right)T_{k}\left(p^{h},b\right)\ll T_{k}(p^{h},b),

whence by (
4.4
)

S_{k}(p^{h},a)\ll\begin{cases}p^{i_{k}/2+3j_{k}/4},&h=1,\\
p^{h(i_{k}/2+7j_{k}/8+\varepsilon)},&h\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

Upon recalling (
2.2
) and (
4.1
) one may then conclude that

A(p^{h})\ll\begin{cases}p^{1-s_{1}/2-s_{2}/4},&h=1,\\
p^{h(1-s_{1}/2-s_{2}/8+\varepsilon)},&h\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

One may derive that

\frac{s_{1}}{2}+\frac{s_{2}}{4}\geqslant\frac{9}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{s_{1}}{2}+\frac{s_{2}}{8}\geqslant\frac{13}{8},

for integer values of
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
which satisfy our hypothesis concerning
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
in Theorem
1.2
, hence we conclude that

A(p^{h})\ll\begin{cases}p^{-5/4},&h=1,\\
p^{-h(5/8-\varepsilon)},&h\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

Which proves the result by simply substituting
5/8-\varepsilon
with
9/16
.
∎

With this bound established we are ready to show the absolute convergence of the singular series.

Lemma 4.3
.

The complete singular series
{\mathfrak{S}}
is absolutely convergent and additionally we have that

|{\mathfrak{S}}-{\mathfrak{S}}(Q)|\ll Q^{-1/32}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
there exists a constant
C
dependent only on the coefficients of the polynomial
\Phi
such that for every prime
p
, we have that

\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}p^{h/32}\left|A(p^{h})\right|\leqslant Cp^{-1-1/16}.

Hence by the Euler product

\sum_{q>Q}|A(q)|\leqslant\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{q}{Q}\right)^{1/32}|A(q)|\leqslant Q^{-1/32}\prod_{p}\left(1+Cp^{-1-1/16}\right)\ll Q^{-1/32}.

Where the last inequality is due to the infinite product being known to be absolutely convergent.
∎

With this result we have by the same analysis as in section 2.6 in
[
6
]
that

{\mathfrak{S}}=\prod_{p}\sigma(p),

where

\sigma_{p}=\lim_{h\to\infty}p^{h(1-s_{1}-s_{2})}\#\{({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{h}{\mathbb{Z}})^{s_{1}+s_{2}}:\Phi({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\equiv 0\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,p^{h}\}.

Combining Lemma
4.3
with Corollary
3.5
, one may apply Hensel’s Lemma to derive that the existence of non-singular
p
-adic zeroes guarantees that
\sigma(p)>0
for all primes.

We summarize our findings with a Corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

For any
0<\delta<1/20
we have that

R(X;{\mathfrak{B}}_{1},{\mathfrak{B}}_{2})=J(X^{\delta},1)\prod_{p}\sigma(p)X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}+o(X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1}).

If we additionally assume
p
-adic solubility of
\Phi
then
\prod_{p}\sigma(p)>0
.

5.
The Singular Integral

Here, we establish the convergence of the
complete
singular integral

\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})=\lim_{Q\to\infty}J(Q,1)=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{|{\beta}|\leqslant Q}I(1,{\beta}){\,{\rm d}}{\beta}.

Thus far we have not specified the center,
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}:=({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*})
, nor the side length,
2\eta
, of the cubes
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}
,
{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}
. Here, we show that there exists a choice of these free variables such that one may show the convergence of the
complete
singular integral. Our strategy will be the same as outlined in chapter 16 of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
\Phi
has non-singular real solutions. Then there exists a cube
{\mathfrak{B}}={\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)}\times{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}
with center
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}
and side length
2\eta^{*}
such that the limit

\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{|{\beta}|\leqslant Q}I(1,{\beta}){\,{\rm d}}{\beta},

exists and is strictly positive, hence

J(Q,1)=\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})+o(1).

Proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}
be a non-singular real solution to
\Phi
, then define

c_{i}=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\xi_{i}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}),

for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}+s_{2}
. Without loss of generality we may suppose that
c_{1}\neq 0
then by Taylor’s Theorem

\Phi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}+s_{2}}c_{i}\xi_{i}+P({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

where
P
is a polynomial which is
O(|\xi|^{2})
near
\xi=0
. Call
\zeta=\Phi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, then by the implicit function Theorem there then exists a strictly positive real number
\eta
for which on the neighborhood
|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|<\eta
the variable
\xi_{1}
can be defined in terms of
\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}}
and
\zeta
. From the above we see that this function is of the form

\xi_{1}=\frac{\zeta}{c_{1}}-\sum_{2\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}+s_{2}}\frac{c_{i}}{c_{1}}\xi_{i}+P_{1}(\zeta,\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}}),

where
P_{1}
is a multiple power series with terms of degree at least two. Taking partial derivatives with respect to
\zeta
yields

\frac{\partial\xi_{1}}{\partial\zeta}=\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{\partial P_{1}}{\partial\zeta}(\zeta,\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}}).

By continuity there exists a function
\sigma(\eta)
which goes to zero as
\eta
goes to zero which satisfies the statement

|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\leqslant\eta\text{ implies that }|\zeta({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\leqslant\sigma(\eta).

Thus, we choose
\eta^{*}
to be sufficiently small such that

\left|\frac{\partial P_{1}}{\partial\zeta}(\zeta,\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}})\right|<\frac{1}{2|c_{1}|},

holds for
|\zeta|\leqslant\sigma(\eta^{*})
and
|\xi_{i}|\leqslant\eta^{*}
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}+s_{2}
.

Thus, having chosen the center of our cube
{\mathfrak{B}}
to be
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}
and the side length to be
2\eta^{*}
we have by our above analysis that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{[-\eta^{*},\eta^{*}]^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\frac{\sin(2\pi Q\Phi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))}{\pi\Phi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{-\sigma(\eta^{*})}^{\sigma(\eta^{*})}\frac{\sin(2\pi Q\zeta)}{\pi\zeta}V(\zeta){\,{\rm d}}\zeta.

Where

V(\zeta)=\int_{{\mathfrak{B}}(\eta^{*},\zeta)}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{\partial P_{1}}{\partial\zeta}(\zeta,\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}})\right){\,{\rm d}}\xi_{2}\cdots{\,{\rm d}}\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}},

and
{\mathfrak{B}}(\eta^{*},\zeta)
is the set of
\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}}
satisfying
|\xi_{i}|<\eta
for which

|\xi_{1}(\zeta,\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}})|<\eta^{*}.

It is clear that
V(\zeta)
is of bounded variation because it has both left and right bounded derivatives everywhere. Thus by Fourier’s integral Theorem the limit as
Q\to\infty
exists and we conclude that

\sigma_{\infty}({\mathfrak{B}}^{(1)},{\mathfrak{B}}^{(2)})=V(0).

The value
V(0)
is strictly positive by construction since

\left|\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{\partial P_{1}}{\partial\zeta}(0,\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}})\right|>\frac{1}{2|c_{1}|},

for
(\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{s_{1}+s_{2}})\in{\mathfrak{B}}(\eta^{*},0)
and
\text{mes}\{{\mathfrak{B}}(\eta^{*},0)\}\gg 1
.
∎

Upon combining the result of Lemma
5.1
with Corollary
4.4
and setting
\delta<1/20
we have finally proven Theorem
1.2
.

6.
Further Problems

As referenced in section
1
, there are many possible weighted forms one could investigate. Here, we outline a general class of problems that may be tackled in a manner similar to the one we discuss in this paper. Let
d\geqslant 2
be given, then if we let
H^{(n)}
denote a form of degree
n
one may investigate the zeros of the weighted
2d
form

\Phi_{2d}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})=H^{(2)}({\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}x_{i}H_{i}^{(d)}({\mathbf{y}})+H^{(2d)}({\mathbf{y}}).

Note that
\Phi_{2d}
is a general weighted
2d
form in which the variable weights are either
1
or
d
, up to a relabeling of variables. The most pressing issue is non-trivially bounding the minor arcs. We start by first setting
{\mathbf{v}}={\mathbf{y}}
and finding a suitable non-singular linear substitution
{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\tilde{{\mathbf{u}}}
such that

H^{(2)}({\mathbf{x}})+\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}x_{i}H^{(d)}({\mathbf{y}})=\frac{1}{Q}\left(\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}\tilde{a}_{i}\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}+\tilde{u}_{i}\tilde{H}_{i}^{(d)}({\mathbf{v}})\right),

where
Q,\tilde{a}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{s_{1}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}
and the
\tilde{H}_{i}^{(d)}
are integral linear combinations of the
H_{i}^{(d)}
. Upon making the substitutions

u_{i}=2\tilde{a}_{i}\tilde{u}_{i}+\tilde{H}_{i}^{(d)}({\mathbf{v}}),\quad A=\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}\tilde{a}_{i},\quad a_{i}=\frac{A}{\tilde{a}_{i}},

and

\tilde{H}^{(2d)}({\mathbf{v}})=4AQH^{(2d)}({\mathbf{v}})-\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}a_{i}\left(\tilde{H}^{(d)}({\mathbf{v}})\right)^{2},

one has that

\Phi_{2d}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})=\frac{1}{4AQ}\left(\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}a_{i}u_{i}^{2}+\tilde{H}^{(2d)}({\mathbf{v}})\right).

Hence, counting zeros of
\Phi_{2d}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})
is equivalent to counting zeros of

\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s_{1}}a_{i}u_{i}^{2}+\tilde{H}^{(2d)}({\mathbf{v}}),

where given a particular choice of
{\mathbf{v}}
there are certain congruence conditions on the variables
{\mathbf{u}}
which depend on the coefficients of
H^{(2)}
and
H_{i}^{(d)}({\mathbf{v}})
.

It seems reasonable that the arguments present in section
3
should allow one to understand the minor arcs in this problem given bounds on an integral of the shape

I({\mathfrak{m}})=\int_{{\mathfrak{m}}}|g({\alpha})^{s_{1}}h({\alpha})|d{\alpha}

where

g({\alpha})=\sum_{|x|\leqslant X^{1/2}}e({\alpha}x^{2}),\quad h({\alpha})=\sum_{|{\mathbf{v}}|\leqslant X^{1/2d}}e({\alpha}\tilde{H}^{(2d)}({\mathbf{v}})),

and some suitable definition of the minor arcs
{\mathfrak{m}}
. Assuming some reasonable non-singularity conditions on the form
\tilde{H}^{(2d)}({\mathbf{v}})
one could apply Hölder’s inequality, classical exponential sum bounds, and Lemma 4.3 from
[
1
]
to establish that

I({\mathfrak{m}})=o(X^{s_{1}/2+s_{2}/4-1})

whenever
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
satisfy

\frac{s_{1}}{2}+\frac{s_{2}}{2^{2d-1}(2d-1)}>2.

However, there are some technical challenges with this approach. Thus, in this paper, we decided to focus on cases where the polynomial
\tilde{H}^{(2d)}({\mathbf{v}})
can be decomposed as a sum of binary forms. This approach enables us to elucidate the key concepts without delving too deeply into intricate technical details.

We finish by noting that the problem of general weighted forms appears to be beyond the scope of our current techniques as we require some way to separate the variables of large weight. To see this consider the case of a weighted
12
form
\Phi({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{z}})
where the variable
{\mathbf{x}}
has weight
4
,
{\mathbf{y}}
has weight
3
, and
{\mathbf{z}}
has weight
2
. This weighted
12
form contains integral linear combinations of forms of the shape

H^{(3)}({\mathbf{x}})H^{(4)}({\mathbf{y}})H^{(6)}({\mathbf{z}}).

The main tool we have been using so far is completing the square which allows us to separate the variables of large weight, which is not applicable to the above form. It appears that new ideas will be required to make further progress.
