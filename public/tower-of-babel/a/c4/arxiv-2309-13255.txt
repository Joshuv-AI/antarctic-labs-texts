Adaptive Quasicontinuum Methods and Simulations for Crystal Defects with a Theory based Unified a Posteriori Error Estimate
Hao Wang, Yangshuai Wang

Abstract
. Adaptive quasicontinuum (QC) methods are important methodologies in molecular mechanics for the simulations of materials with defects, intending to achieve the optimal balance of accuracy and efficiency on the fly. In this study, we propose a residual-force based a posteriori error estimate that is simple and is unified for consistent quasicontinuum methods, as opposed to the widely adopted residual-stress based a posteriori error estimates which are complicated and need to be derived for the particular QC method under consideration. The simple and unified formulation of the estimator, together with certain sampling techniques, leads to a highly efficient and adaptable implementation. We also prove in theory that the unified error estimator provides an upper bound of the true error. We develop adaptive algorithms based on this unified estimator and validate the algorithms by several representative quasicontinuum methods for various types of crystalline defects, in terms of convergence and efficiency. In particular, the adaptive simulations of the anti-plane crack, of which we possess little a priori knowledge, demonstrate the necessity and significance of the proposed a posteriori estimates and the adaptive strategies.

There are two major approaches for the theory based

The present work concentrates on the second approach which is the residual based

However, it was later noticed that the residual based estimates suffer from high computational cost which essentially stems from the mismatch of the lattices and the meshes for models of different scales. Despite the possible further extensions of the existing residual based approach to a wider range of QC methods and types of defects, there are critical limitations for such approach. The first one is that the

The second one is that the formulations of the estimators are often involved which results in the high cost in both implementation and computation. In particular, the residual based

The purpose of the current work is to address the critical limitations just described. By deriving an error estimator based on the
residual force
, we are able to give a unified
a posteriori
error estimate for general consistent QC methods. The unified error estimator is then employed to design the adaptive algorithms for several representative QC methods. These algorithms are then implemented for the adaptive simulations of a few crystalline defects with practical importance. To be precise, our contribution lies in the following aspects.

First, we derive a unified
a posteriori

residual force

,
58
,

,
35
,

. The residual-force based estimate is independent of the particular QC method we consider and we provide a theoretical framework to establish that such estimator provides an upper bound of the true error in a discrete
H^{1}
-norm.

Second, we develop novel adaptive algorithms for a series of QC methods ranging from sharp interfaced to blended, energy-based to force-based. The algorithms aim to automatically adjust the mesh structures in the continuum region as well as the allocations of the atomistic region and the blending region, if applicable, on the fly. All the adaptive algorithms are essentially based on the force based unified error estimator and only algorithmic adjustments are needed. We note that theory based
a posteriori
error estimate and fully adaptive algorithms (taking both mesh refinement and allocation of the regions into account at the same time) for the widely adopted blended QC methods
[
33
,
40
,
48

are developed for the first time in more than 1D (cf.
[
74
,
27
]
).

Third, we provide numerical validations of our error control strategies in the adaptive simulations for various types of crystalline defects, including crack, which to the best knowledge of the authors is implemented for the first time in the context of QC. The adaptive simulations demonstrate that the proposed algorithms produce optimal convergence rates of the error and (quasi-)optimal decomposition of the domain. The systematic study of the adaptive QC methods for cracks represents a pioneering effort, leveraging the attributes of the unified residual-force based
a posteriori
error estimator. Conversely, the
a priori
analysis poses challenges that require advanced technicalities to be addressed effectively. The inherent effectiveness and remarkable flexibility of this approach not only broaden the horizons but also provide a robust framework for investigating practical crystalline defects such as grain boundaries.

To ensure the clarity of presentation, we focus on the atomistic system with finite range interactions in two dimensions. However, the proposed unified framework may be potentially extended to other consistent multiscale coupling schemes and three-dimensional problems. Further investigations including more efficient strategies for other coupling schemes and the extension to realistic crystalline defects in three dimensions, such as partial dislocations and grain boundaries, are also discussed at the end of this paper.

The Atomistic Model and Quasicontinuum Methods

,

,

,

,

. We defer the presentation of the detailed formulations of specific methods to Section

2.1.
Atomistic model

We first give a heuristic introduction to the problem we solve. Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an infinite single lattice with defects and
y
be a lattice function defined on
\Lambda
representing a deformed configuration. In molecular mechanics, we consider an atomistic energy functional of the form

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(y):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}\Phi_{\ell}\big(\{y(\ell+\rho)-y(\ell)\}_{\rho\in\mathscr{R}_{\ell}}),

where
\Phi_{\ell}:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathscr{R}_{\ell}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the energy distributed to each atomic site or simply the site potential, and
\mathscr{R}_{\ell}:=\{\ell^{\prime}-\ell~|~\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\}
is the interaction range with an interaction neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}:=\{\ell^{\prime}\in\Lambda~|~0<|\ell^{\prime}-\ell|\leq r_{\textrm{cut}}\}
of a given cut-off radius
r_{\textrm{cut}}
. The atomistic problem is to locally minimize
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(y)
, i.e., by denoting “
\arg\min
” as the set of local minimizers, we seek
y^{\rm a}
such that

(2.2)

y^{\rm a}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}(y)\big\}.

However, (
2.2
) is in fact not a meaningful problem since
\Lambda
is infinite. A more mathematically rigorous description of the problem is given as follows.

The defected lattice
\Lambda
stems from the perfect single lattice
{\Lambda^{\rm hom}}:={\textsf{A}}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for some non-singular matrix
{\textsf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
. The mismatch between
\Lambda
and
{\Lambda^{\rm hom}}
represents possible defects that are often contained in some localized defect cores
\Omega^{\rm DEF}
so that
\Lambda\backslash\Omega^{\rm DEF}={\Lambda^{\rm hom}}\backslash\Omega^{\rm DEF}
. We illustrate three typical defects considered in this work in Figure
1
.

(a)
Micro-crack

(b)
Anti-plane screw dislocation

(c)
Anti-plane crack

Figure 1.
Illustration of three typical defects considered in this work.

We often decomposed the deformed configuration
y\in\mathscr{U}
with
\mathscr{U}:=\{v:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
as

(2.3)

\displaystyle y(\ell)=\textsf{F}\ell+u_{0}(\ell)+u(\ell),\qquad\forall~\ell\in\Lambda,

where
F
is a macroscopic deformation gradient,
u_{0}\in\mathscr{U}
is a
far-field predictor
resulting from the presence of the defect which is often precomputed by solving a continuum linearized elasticity (CLE) (see
Appendix A
), and
u\in\mathscr{U}
is a
corrector
which is the solution we actually seek for.

To measure the local “regularity” of a displacement
u\in\mathscr{U}
, it is convenient to use a background mesh, for example, a
canonical
triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose nodes are the reference lattice sites in
\Lambda
, see
[
24
, Figure 1]
for an illustration. We define
I_{\textrm{a}}u
as the standard piecewise affine interpolation of
u
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}

[
73
, Appendix 1]
.
To be more precise, if we let
\zeta_{\ell}(x)\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
be the
\mathcal{P}_{1}
nodal basis function associated with
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
, we can then extend all lattice displacement
u\in\mathscr{U}
via their nodal interpolants,

(2.4)

\displaystyle I_{\textrm{a}}u(x):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}u(\ell)\zeta_{\ell}(x).

When no confusion arises, we identify
u
with its interpolation
I_{\rm a}u
and denote the piecewise constant gradient
\nabla u:=\nabla I_{\rm a}u
. We then introduce the functional space of finite-energy displacements

(2.5)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda):=\big\{u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}~\big\lvert~\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}<\infty\big\}

with the associated norm
\|v\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}:=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}
for
v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)
.
We also define the following subspace of compact displacements which is dense in
{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}

[
41
, Proposition 3.3]
:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}^{\rm c}(\Lambda):=\big\{u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}~\big\lvert~\textrm{supp}(\nabla u)~~\textrm{is compact}\big\}.

By introducing the finite difference stencil for
u\in\mathscr{U}

\displaystyle Du(\ell):=\{D_{\rho}u(\ell)\}_{\rho\in\mathscr{R}_{\ell}}:=\{u(\ell+\rho)-u(\ell)\}_{\rho\in\mathscr{R}_{\ell}},

and with a slight abuse of notation, we can redefine the atomistic energy-difference functional by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)

\displaystyle=~\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}\Big(V_{\ell}\big(Du_{0}(\ell)+Du(\ell)\big)-V_{\ell}\big(Du_{0}(\ell)\big)\Big)=:\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}V^{\prime}_{\ell}\big(Du(\ell)\big),

where
V_{\ell}(Du(\ell)):=\Phi(\{\textsf{F}\rho+u(\ell+\rho)-u(\ell)\}_{\rho\in\mathscr{R}_{\ell}})
and
V^{\prime}_{\ell}
is the
first renormalization
of
V_{\ell}

[
60
]
.

It was shown that
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)
is well-defined on the space
{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}
under suitable assumptions
[
41
, Section 2.1]
. The atomistic problem can then be reformulated as

(2.8)

u^{\rm a}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}(u)~|~u\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}(\Lambda)\big\},

with the following first and second order optimality conditions

(2.9)

\displaystyle\big\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u),v\big\rangle=0,\qquad\big\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)v,v\big\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall~v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda).

To establish the
a posteriori
error bounds, a stronger stability assumption is made:

Assumption 2.1
.

\exists~\gamma>0
, such that for all
v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)
,

\big\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}(u)v,v\big\rangle\geq\gamma\|v\|^{2}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}
.

2.2.
The general formulation of the quasicontinuum methods

Since the atomistic model (
2.8
) is defined on an infinite domain and considers every atom as a degree of freedom with
nonlocal
interactions (the energy of an atom is influenced by all neighbors within its interaction range), it is not solvable in practice. To make the problem efficiently computable, the QC methods
[
41
,
33
,
60
]
aim to construct and solve the following approximated variational nonlinear system

(2.10)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm qc}(u_{h}),v_{h}\rangle_{\textrm{ac}}=0,\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h},

by certain approximations which are described immediately.

The first approximation is the restriction of the computational domain from infinite to a simply connected polygon
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The error committed by such restriction is called the
truncation error
.

The second approximation is essentially a model reduction. Recall that the displacement is smooth in the region away from the defect core so that the
nonlocal
interactions may be approximated by a
local
continuum elasticity model in the far field with sufficient accuracy. A typical choice of the model is the Cauchy-Born nonlinear elasticity
[
22
,
56
]
, where the strain energy function
W:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
measures the energy per unit volume under a globally homogeneous deformation and is defined by

(2.11)

W(\textsf{F}):=\det({\textsf{A}}^{-1})\cdot V(\textsf{F}\mathscr{R}).

The definition of the
first renormalization
of
W
is then given by
W^{\prime}(\textsf{F}):=W(\textsf{F})-W({\bf 0})
.

The third approximation is a domain decomposition with further coarse-graining by the finite element discretization. We essentially decompose the computational domain
\Omega
into the
atomistic region

\Omega_{\textrm{a}}
, the
interface region

\Omega_{\rm i}
and the
continuum region

\Omega_{\textrm{c}}
, so that
\Omega=\Omega_{\textrm{a}}\cup\Omega_{\rm i}\cup\Omega_{\textrm{c}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We assume that the defect core is contained in the atomistic region, i.e.,
\Omega^{\rm DEF}\subset\Omega_{\textrm{a}}
and the displacement
u
is sufficiently smooth in
\Omega_{\textrm{c}}
. Here
\Omega_{\rm i}
is the region between
\Omega
and
\Omega_{\textrm{c}}
where the transition of the
nonlocal
and the
local
models takes place. The set of core atoms can then be defined by
\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega_{\textrm{a}}
, the set of the interface atoms by
\Lambda_{\rm i}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega_{\rm i}
and the set of atoms in the continuum region by
\Lambda_{\rm c}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega_{\rm c}
. Subsequently, we can define
\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
to be the
canonical
triangulation induced by
\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}\cup\Lambda_{\rm i}
,
\mathcal{T}_{\rm c}
be a shape-regular simplicial partition of the continuum region and
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\mathcal{T}_{\textrm{a}}\cup\mathcal{T}_{\textrm{c}}
be the triangulation of the computational domain for the QC methods.

According to the three approximations, we define the
coarse-grained
space of displacements by

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{h}:=\big\{u_{h}:\Omega_{h}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}~\big|~\text{$u_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})$, $u_{h}=0$ in $\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega_{h}$}\big\},

where
\Omega_{h}=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}T
if a
\mathcal{P}_{1}
finite element discretization is utilized in the continuum region so that
\mathscr{U}_{h}
serves as an approximation of
{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)
.

We now illustrate the construction of
\mathcal{F}^{\rm qc}_{h}
by an example of the energy-based QC methods
[
59
,
40
]
. For
u_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}
, we define the QC energy functional

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm qc}_{h}(u_{h}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}}V^{\prime}_{\ell}(Du_{h})+\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}V^{\rm i}_{\ell}(Du_{h})+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{c}}\omega_{T}\big(W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{h}|_{T}+\nabla u_{0}|_{T})-W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{0}|_{T})\big),

where
V^{\rm i}_{\ell}\in C^{k}((\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathcal{R}})
is an
interface potential
and
\omega_{T}
is the
effective volume
of
T
which specify individual coupling schemes and are subtly defined to deal with the spurious forces (usually referred to as “ghost force”) around the interface where the
nonlocal
and
local
models meet. In this context, the nonlinear map
\mathcal{F}^{\rm qc}_{h}:\mathscr{U}_{h}\rightarrow(\mathscr{U}_{h})^{*}
in (
2.10
), which is defined by the first variation of the coupling energy functional
\mathcal{E}^{\rm qc}_{h}
with respect to the space
\mathscr{U}_{h}
that
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm qc}:=\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm qc}_{h}
, serves as an approximation of the first order optimality of the atomistic model (
2.9
) and the duality
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\textrm{ac}}
is then induced by
\mathscr{U}_{h}
. The energy-based QC problem we would like to solve is to find

(2.14)

u^{\rm qc}_{h}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm qc}_{h}(u_{h}),~u_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}\big\}.

The solution to the energy minimization problem in (
2.14
) then satisfies the variational problem in (
2.10
). However, it is important to note that the reverse is only true when the corresponding coupling scheme is stable
[
55
,
41
]
.

We note that the general formulation (
2.10
) fits most (if not all) of the quasicontinuum methods. However, since our
a posterior
error estimates in Section
3

do not depend on
the individual formulation of a coupling scheme, we postpone the construction detail of specific QC schemes to Section
4
where the adaptive algorithms, which
do depend on
the corresponding coupling scheme, are developed. What essentially makes our
a posterior
error estimates possible is the
a priori
estimate/consistency of the QC method which we introduce in the immediate following section.

2.3.
A priori estimates and consistency of the quasicontinuum methods

Let
N
denote the total number of degrees of freedom of the QC method. We say that the method is consistent if the approximation error decreases as
N
increases. To be more precise, under certain assumptions (e.g., mesh qualities
[
25
, Assumption 1]
), for
N
sufficiently large,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|u-u^{\rm qc}_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\leq CN^{-k}(\log N)^{s},

where
C
is a constant independent of any model parameters, and
s,k\geq 0
are related to the types of defects and specify the convergence rate of the approximation error with respect to
N
. We will present the
a priori
estimates for different methods and defects considered in this work in Table
1
in Section
4
. We note that these
a priori
estimates provide a solid theoretical foundation of the current work and plays a key role in developing the
a posteriori
error estimate.

3.
The Unified a Posteriori Error Estimates based on Residual Forces

In this section, we propose a unified framework for the
a posteriori
error estimate which is applicable to generally consistent QC methods satisfying (
2.15
). Such estimate provides the groundwork for the adaptive algorithms for a range of QC methods in Section
4
. We essentially adapt the analytical framework originally introduced by our previous study on the adaptive QM/MM methods for simple point defects
[
16
]
to the setting of QC and extend it to substantially more complicated defects. The framework can be summarized in four steps: the proof of the equivalence of the true approximation error and the residual in certain dual norm, the derivation of the
a posteriori
error estimator based on the residual forces which gives the upper bound of the residual, the estimate of the error committed by the truncation of the computational domain for different types of defects and an efficient sampling of the residual forces which forms the local error contributions. We present the four steps in detail in the following subsections.

3.1.
Equivalence of the residual and the approximation error

For the simplicity of presentation, we denote the solution to the atomistic problem (
2.8
) as
u
and that to the QC problem (
2.10
) or (
2.14
) as
u_{h}
. Recall that
\mathscr{U}^{\rm c}
is dense in
{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}
, we have
I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}
where
I_{\textrm{a}}
is the standard piecewise affine interpolation operator defined in (
2.4
). The residual
\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
is then defined as an operator on
\mathscr{U}^{1,2}
such that

(3.1)

\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})[v]:=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}),v\rangle,\quad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}.

The theorem below shows that the dual norm of the residual
\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
gives both the upper and the lower bounds for the true approximation error
\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{\mathscr{U}^{1,2}}
.

Theorem 3.1.

Let
u
be a strongly stable solution of (
2.8
). Suppose that
\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}
and
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}
are Lipschitz continuous in
B_{r}(u)
with uniform constants
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, the QC method is consistent in the sense of (
2.15
) and
u_{h}
satisfies the same decay as
u
. Then for
N
sufficiently large, there exist constants
c
and
C
independent of the approximation parameters such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle c\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\leq\|\mathsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}\leq C\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}},

where the residual
\mathsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
is defined by (
3.1
).

Proof.

Let
r>0
satisfies
B_{r}(u)\subset{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}
. By the Lipschitz continuity of
\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}
and
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}
in
B_{r}(u)
with uniform constants
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, for any
w\in B_{r}(u)
, we have

\displaystyle\|\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)-\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(w)\|\leq L_{1}\|u-w\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}},

\displaystyle\|\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(w)\|\leq L_{2}\|u-w\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

By the first optimality condition of the atomistic problem
\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u),v\rangle=0
, we have that for
N
sufficiently large

\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})[v]=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h})-\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u),v\rangle\leq L_{1}\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\|v\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}},\quad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}.

To estimate
\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}
, we obtain from the
a priori
estimate (
2.15
) that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u-u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}+\|u_{h}-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\lesssim N^{-k}(\log N)^{s},

where the last inequality holds by the assumption that
u_{h}
and
u
share the same decay estimate.

The above result leads to the lower bound of the true approximation error

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}\leq L_{1}\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}=:C\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

By considering
\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
as an operator on
\mathscr{U}^{1,2}
and the Galerkin orthogonality introduced by the first optimality condition of the atomistic problem, we obtain the upper bound

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\geq\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}),u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}\rangle=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h})-\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u),u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}\rangle.

A further application of the intermediate value theorem yields

(3.6)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h})-\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u),u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}\rangle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(w)(u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}),u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}\rangle,

where
w=tu+(1-t)I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}
for some
t\in(0,1)
. To analyze the second variation, we add and subtract the same term and separate it into two parts

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(w)v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(w)v,v\rangle-\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)v,v\rangle+\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)v,v\rangle

(3.7)

\displaystyle=:S_{1}+S_{2}.

For
N
sufficiently large (e.g.
N\geq(\frac{\gamma}{2}L_{2})^{k}
), we bound
S_{1}
by the Lipschitz continuity of
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}
that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\big|S_{1}\big|\leq L_{2}\|u-w\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\|v\|^{2}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}\|v\|^{2}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}},

where the second inequality holds by the fact that
\|u-w\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\leq\|u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}
and (
3.3
). Since
u
is a strongly stable solution of the atomistic problem (
2.8
) satisfying Assumption
2.1
, we obtain the following stability estimate by (
3.1
) and (
3.8
) that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(w)v,v\rangle\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\|v\|^{2}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

Let
v=u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}
in (
3.9
), we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(w)(u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}),u-I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}\rangle\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|^{2}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

Combining (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.10
) and dividing both sides by
\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}
lead to the upper bound of the true approximation error

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}\geq\gamma/2\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}=:c\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

The stated results are obtained by (
3.4
) and (
3.11
).
∎

Theorem
3.1
establishes the equivalence of the residual
\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
in the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}
and the true approximation error
\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{\mathscr{U}^{1,2}}
. We note that such equivalence is independent of the coupling scheme by which
u_{h}
is obtained. The conditions on which the equivalence does rely are essentially the following three: the Lipschitz continuity of the derivatives or the variations of
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}
, the consistency of the coupling method defined by (
2.15
) and the assumption that
u_{h}
shares the same decay estimates as
u
. The first condition is often guaranteed by the regularity of the (empirical) interatomic potential (cf.
[
24
, Lemma 2.1]
). The second condition is the focus of the
a priori
analysis of any QC method (cf.
[
41
,
33
]
). The third condition is not easy to prove but often proposed as an assumption (cf.
[
77
]
). One possibility to rigorously derive this condition is to utilize the technique for constructing the effective Green’s functions of QC methods which is initially introduced in
[
24
]
. We also refer the interested reader to the analysis in
[
76
, Section 5.1]
for further detail. In the current work, we numerically verify this condition in
Appendix C
.

We define the
ideal

a posteriori
error estimator to be

\eta^{\rm ideal}(u_{h}):=\|\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}},

which is only a abstract quantity defined in a dual norm. Hence, in the following section we derive a
concrete

a posteriori
error estimator which is an upper bound of the
ideal
estimator.

3.2.
a Posteriori
error estimator based on residual forces

Since
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(\cdot)
is an energy functional on
{\mathscr{U}^{1,2}}
, the first variation of
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}
naturally defines a stress-strain relation in the language of continuum mechanics. For example, the direct calculation of the first variation of
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}
at
I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h}

often
gives

(3.12)

\textsf{R}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h})[v]=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\rm a}u_{h}),v\rangle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}}|T|\sigma^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\rm a}u_{h}):\nabla_{T}v,\quad\forall v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2},

where
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
is the
canonical
triangulation induced by the reference lattice
\Lambda
and
\sigma^{\textrm{a}}
can be defined as the atomistic stress tensor which we refer to
[
56
]
for the detailed formulation. We note that, by a proper application of the summation by parts or Abel transform in higher dimension, the residual
\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
can also be expressed in terms of the
residual forces
such that

(3.13)

\textsf{R}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h})[v]=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\rm a}u_{h}),v\rangle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}}|T|\sigma^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\rm a}u_{h}):\nabla_{T}v=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\cdot v(\ell),\quad\forall v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}

where the
residual force
on each atom
\ell
is given by
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h}):=\partial_{u(\ell)}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u)\big|_{u={I_{\rm a}u_{h}}}
. Adopting the residual-force based representation of the residual given in (
3.13
), the
concrete

a posteriori
error estimator
\eta(u_{h})
is given by the following lemma and theorem.

Lemma 3.2.

Let
d=2
. Then for any
\ell\in\Lambda
there exists a constant
C^{\rm d2}>0
such that

|v(\ell)-v(0)|\leq C^{\rm d2}\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\|v\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}},\qquad\forall v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}.

Let
d=3
. Then for each
v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}
, there exist a constant
C^{\rm d3}>0
and a
v_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that the following estimate holds

\|v-v_{\infty}\|_{\ell^{6}}\leq C^{\rm d3}\|v\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

Theorem 3.3.

Let the residual force
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(u_{h})
be defined by (
3.13
). Under the conditions of Theorem
3.1
, there exists a constant
C^{\rm resF}
such that

(3.14)

\displaystyle\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{\mathscr{U}^{1,2}}\leq C\|\mathsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{(\mathscr{U}^{1,2})^{*}}\leq C^{\rm resF}\eta(u_{h}),

where

\displaystyle\eta(u_{h}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\big\|\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\big\|_{\ell^{1}},&d=2\\[4.30554pt]
\|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{\ell^{\frac{6}{5}}},&d=3\end{array}\right..

Lemma
3.2
essentially serves as the Poincaré inequality for
{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}
and Theorem
3.2
establishes an
concrete
upper bound of
\|\textsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}
using the residual force
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
on each lattice point
\ell\in\Lambda
. The proof of Lemma
3.2
can be found in
[
17
, Lemma A.2]
while the proof of Theorem
3.2
follows the same line as that for
a posteriori
error estimate in QM/MM coupling method
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
and thus are both omitted.

The
a posteriori
analysis up to now is valid for both two and three dimensions and the theoretical framework can be easily extended to three dimensions with suitable modifications, e.g. estimates of the truncation error that will be discussed immediately. However, the adaptive QC in three dimensions requires a reliable three-dimensional mesh generator and adaptive techniques, which are out of the scope of the current work. We therefore concentrate on the two dimensional case from now on and refer the interested readers to
[
27
]
for a recent effort in this direction.

We note that the evaluation of the
concrete

a posteriori
error estimator
\eta(u_{h})
requires the computation of the
residual force
at each lattice point of the infinite lattice
\Lambda
and is thus not computable. Therefore, our next task is to develop a computable approximation of
\eta(u_{h})
.

3.3.
Estimates of the truncation error

In this section, we consider the estimate of the error originated from the truncation of the infinite domain. Recall that
r_{\rm cut}
is the maximum radius of the interaction range, we can define the extension of the computational domain
\Omega
as
\Omega_{\rm ext}:=\bigcup_{\ell\in\Lambda\cap\Omega}B_{r_{\rm cut}+1}(\ell)
.
We then define
\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
and the
coupling error estimator

\eta^{\rm qc}(u_{h})
respectively by

(3.17)

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h}):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda\cap(\Omega_{\rm ext}\setminus\Omega)}\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\big|\text{ and }\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h}):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda\cap\Omega}\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\big|.

The following Lemma shows that the
concrete

a posteriori
error estimator
\eta(u_{h})
can be bounded by the
coupling error estimator

\eta^{\rm qc}(u_{h})
and the
truncation error estimator

\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
based on
\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
, and is defined in the body of the Lemma.

Lemma 3.4.

Let
\eta(u_{h})
and
\eta^{\rm qc}(u_{h})
be defined by (
3.2
) and (
3.17
). We have

\eta(u_{h})\leq\eta^{\rm qc}(u_{h})+\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h}),

where the truncation error estimator
\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
is defined by

\displaystyle\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h}),&{\rm Point~defects}\\[4.30554pt]
\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h})+C^{\rm disloc}\big(3+\log(R_{\rm ext})\big)\cdot R_{\rm ext}^{-1},&{\rm Dislocations}\end{array}\right.,

where
R_{\rm ext}:=R+r_{\rm cut}+1
,
\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
is defined by (
3.17
) and
C^{\rm disloc}
is a generic constant.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda_{\rm ext}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega_{\rm ext}
. We have by the definitions of
\eta(u_{h})
,
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
and
\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
that

(3.20)

\displaystyle\eta(u_{h})=\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})+\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h})+\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{\rm ext}}\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}({\bf 0})\big|.

For the case of point defects, the far-field predictor is
u_{0}=0
. Hence, for
\ell\in\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{\rm est}
, we have
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}({\bf 0})=0
, which leads to

\eta(u_{h})=\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})+\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h}).

For general straight dislocations, the far-field predictor is not zero. Therefore the above equation no longer holds. However, according to the
sharp
decay estimates of the residual force
|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}({\bf 0})|\leq C^{\rm disloc}\cdot|\ell|^{-3}
(cf.
[
24
, Lemma 5.8]
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{\rm ext}}\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}({\bf 0})\big|

\displaystyle\leq~C^{\rm disloc}\int^{\infty}_{R_{\rm ext}}\log(2+r)\cdot r^{-2}\,{\textrm{d}}r

\displaystyle\leq~C^{\rm disloc}\big(3+\log(R_{\rm ext})\big)\cdot R_{\rm ext}^{-1}.

The stated result for dislocations is then obtained by applying the above estimate in (
3.20
).
∎

Remark 3.5 (cracks).

The sharp decay estimate of the
residual force
for cracks are yet to be rigorously established. However, it is reasonable to expect that
|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}({\bf 0})|\leq C^{\rm crack}\cdot|\ell|^{-2.5}
by exploiting the fact that the decay of the far-field predictor for a crack is
|Du_{0}(\ell)|\lesssim|\ell|^{-0.5}

[
14
,
13
]
whereas
|Du_{0}(\ell)|\lesssim|\ell|^{-1}
for a straight dislocation. This speculation will be numerically verified in Figure
9
. Hence, for anti-plane crack we consider in this work, the truncation error indicator is given by

\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h}):=\rho^{\rm tr}(u_{h})+C^{\rm crack}\big(3+\log(R_{\rm ext})\big)\cdot R_{\rm ext}^{-0.5}.

∎

The
truncation error estimator

\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
serves as a measure of the error resulting from the finite computational domain. The constants
C^{\rm disloc}
and
C^{\rm crack}
in the definition of
\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
can be empirically determined by fitting the residual forces (see Figure
5
and Figure
9
for anti-plane screw dislocation and anti-plane crack). More importantly,
\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
is computable since the sum of the residual forces
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
is only evaluated in
\Lambda\cap(\Omega_{\rm ext}\setminus\Omega)
which is in a small circle around the the computational domain
\Omega
, but is estimated by an integral in an infinite domain.

3.4.
Local error contribution by sampling

We then consider the
coupling error estimator

\eta^{\rm qc}(u_{h})
defined in (
3.17
), which is
globally
defined on the computational domain
\Omega
. However, it is necessary to distribute
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
into local contributions for the mesh refinement in the continuum region and the optimal allocations of the atomistic region (and the blending region for the blended type of methods). Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is the finite element partition of the computational domain
\Omega
defined in Section
2.2
, we can rewrite
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
in terms of the element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
as

\displaystyle\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda\cap\Omega}\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\big|

\displaystyle=~\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\ell\in T}\log(2+|\ell|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\big|

(3.21)

\displaystyle=:\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta^{\textrm{ac}}_{T}(u_{h}).

It is important to note that, although the definition of the elementwise error estimator
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}_{T}(u_{h})
is conceptually straightforward, the computation can be expensive. In particular, the evaluation of
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}_{T}(u_{h})
for each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
requires a careful consideration of the geometric relationship between
\Lambda
and
T
. The estimated computational cost for this process would be
O(N^{2})
, where
N
denotes the total number of degrees of freedom of the QC problem. See our recent work
[
77
]
for more detail for such geometric consideration. On the other hand, since
u_{h}
is smooth in the region far from the defect core, the residual force
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})
should also vary smoothly so that certain approximation for
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
and
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}_{T}(u_{h})
should be accurate enough.

Motivated by the sampling strategy given in
[
16
, Section 3.3]
, we propose the following
computable
approximation of the
coupling error estimator

\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
by

(3.22)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\omega(T)\log(2+|\tilde{{\ell}}(T)|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\tilde{{\ell}}(T)}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\big|=:\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tilde{\eta}^{\textrm{ac}}_{T}(u_{h}),

with the corresponding approximated
computable elementwise coupling error estimator

(3.23)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}^{\textrm{ac}}_{T}(u_{h}):=\omega(T)\log(2+|\tilde{\ell}(T)|)\cdot\big|\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\tilde{\ell}(T)}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\big|,

where
\tilde{\ell}(T)
is the “repatom” of
T
and
w(T)
gives the weight of the element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. In Section
5
, we choose
\tilde{\ell}(T)
to be the barycenter of
T
and
w(T)
to be the area of
T
. By such sampling strategy, the evaluation of
\tilde{\eta}^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
requires a significantly lower computational cost as that of
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
, which is now only proportional to
N
and is discussed in detail in Section
5
.

The following theorem shows that
\tilde{\eta}^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
provides an accurate enough approximation for
\eta^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
, given that the residual force is sufficiently smooth and the atomistic region is sufficiently large. The proof is based on an interpolation error analysis and is given in
Appendix B
.

Theorem 3.6.

Let
\tilde{\eta}^{\rm qc}(u_{h})
and
\eta^{\rm qc}(u_{h})
be defined by (
3.4
) and (
3.22
), respectively. Denote the radius of
\Omega
by
R_{\Omega}
and let
\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}^{\rm a}
be a
C^{2}
-interpolation of
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}
in
\Omega
. For sufficiently large
R_{\rm a}
, we have

(3.24)

\big|\tilde{\eta}^{\rm qc}(u_{h})-\eta^{\rm qc}(u_{h})\big|\lesssim\log(R_{\Omega})\cdot\|\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

3.5.
Discussion

Having the
computable truncation error estimator

\eta^{\rm tr}(u_{h})
and the
computable coupling error estimator

\tilde{\eta}^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h})
in hand, we are at the stage of developing adaptive algorithms. However, before we proceed, a thorough discussion on the idea behind which we derive the
concrete
error estimator
\eta(u_{h})
in Section
3.2
is necessary. The idea is essentially the key for our unified
a posteriori
estimate that is independent of the individual QC method we consider,

We first compare our approach with the classical residual based
a posteriori
error estimate in the finite element method. The usual procedure in FEM is to insert the numerical solution into the variational formulation of the elliptic equation and apply an elementwise divergence theorem to obtain elementwise residual forces together with gradient jumps on the edges of the elements (cf.
[
72
, Chapter I, Equation (1.14)]
and
[
11
, Chapter III, Equation (8.16)]
). In contrast, (
3.13
) holds by a global, rather than a local or elementwise,
2D/3D summation by parts
, which is an analogy of a global divergence theorem. We adopt such approach because the variational formulation of the atomistic problem (
3.12
) is essentially discrete and a local summation by parts may not give proper elementwise residual since
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}
have no hierarchical structure, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
does not corresponds to a refinement of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We refer to
[
77
, Section 3.3]
and in particular Figure 1 therein for our recent effort on the explanation of the challenges brought by the lack of hierarchy between
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
.

We then discuss the difference between the current estimate and our previous analysis, for example in
[
58
,
73
,
74
,
77
]
. The common practice of the very first step in the previous works is to introduce the corresponding variational or stress-strain formulation of the QC method analyzed. Take the energy-based QC method in (
2.13
) as an example. By adding the first variation of
\mathcal{E}^{\rm qc}_{h}(u_{h})
, we obtain the following representation of the residual

(3.25)

\textsf{R}(I_{\textrm{a}}u_{h})[v]=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}}|T|\sigma^{\textrm{a}}(I_{\rm a}u_{h}):\nabla_{T}v-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|T|\sigma^{\textrm{ac}}(u_{h};T):\nabla_{T}v_{h},

where
\sigma^{\textrm{ac}}
is the coupling stress tensor and
v_{h}
is certain interpolation of
v
in
\mathscr{U}_{h}
. Such approach, which we refer to as the
residual-stress based

a posteriori
error estimates for QC methods, essentially stems from the error analysis for nonconforming finite element methods and in particular the First Lemma of Strang
[
11
, Lemma,
\lx@sectionsign
1, Chapter III]
. By properly introducing additional terms, we are able to clearly separate the error into different parts. This procedure is crucial in the
a priori
analysis of the QC methods to obtain the convergence
[
41
,
57
,
52
]
. However, such approach is also responsible for the two critical limitations described in the introduction which are the dependence of the estimator on the QC method and the complicated formulation of the estimator. In contrast, our
residual-force based estimate

\eta(u_{h})
is unified that only depends on the atomistic model.

We finally comment on the efficiency. We note that
\eta(u_{h})
only provides an upper bound for the
ideal
estimator
\|\mathsf{R}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})\|_{(\mathscr{U}^{1,2})^{*}}
and thus for the true approximation error
\|u-I_{\rm a}u_{h}\|_{\mathscr{U}^{1,2}}
. It is ideal to prove that the estimator, up to some constant, also provides a lower bound, which are called the efficiency of the estimator. We refer to
[
75
]
in this direction for the residual-stress based
a posteriori
error estimator and a more recent progress in
[
76
]
on the Riesz representation of the residual force
\mathscr{F}^{\rm a}_{\ell}(u_{h})
in the context of QM/MM methods which, in principle, could be extended to the current work but with substantial additional effort. However, we illustrate in Section
5
that the efficiency of the estimator has little impact on our adaptive simulations.

4.
Adaptive Algorithms for Various Quasicontinuum Methods

In this section, we develop the adaptive algorithms for the
a posteriori
error control problems for various QC coupling schemes based on our
unified residual-force based a posteriori
error estimator. We concentrate on two major classes of QC methods most often encountered in the literature of QC, which are the sharp interfaced QC methods and the blended QC methods
[
71
,
41
,
43
]
. Though the QC methods can be further categorized as energy based and force based from the modeling perspective, the classification for the methods into sharp interfaced type and blended type is suitable in the context of the
a posteriori
error control as the methods in each class share the same adaptive algorithm which we will develop subsequently. For each class of methods, we will first present the detailed formulations of typical coupling schemes and then develop the corresponding adaptive algorithms. Possible applications of our
unified residual-force based a posteriori
error estimate to other classes of multiscale coupling methods are also briefly commented at the end of this section.

4.1.
The adaptive algorithm for the sharp interfaced QC methods

We first consider the consistent QC methods with sharp interface. The most important feature of this class of methods is to have a very narrow transition from the atomistic region to the continuum region (often a few layers of atoms depending on the interaction range). This is achieved either by introducing an modified interface potential (e.g. GRAC method
[
59
,
51
]
and GRC method
[
23
,
32
]
) or using a sharp characteristic function to couple force balance equations (e.g. QCF method
[
19
,
39
]
). We also note that most of the existing literatures on the adaptive QC focus on this class of methods
[
58
,
73
,
74
]
and thus it provide us a good benchmark problem to illustrate the advantage of our unified
a posteriori
error estimates.

We now present the formulations of two consistent QC methods with sharp interface.

The first method is the geometric reconstruction based consistent QC (GRAC) method which is an energy based method initially proposed in
[
23
,
59
]
and further developed in
[
51
]
. The GRAC method match exactly the formulation as shown in (
2.13
), that is,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm grac}_{h}(u_{h}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}}V^{\prime}_{\ell}(Du_{h})+\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}V^{\rm i}_{\ell}(Du_{h})+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{c}}\omega_{T}\big(W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{h}|_{T}+\nabla u_{0}|_{T})-W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{0}|_{T})\big),

where
V_{\ell}^{\textrm{i}}
is a modified interface site potential and
\omega_{T}
is the effective volumes of elements (see
[
59
, Section 2.2]
for a detailed discussion). The modified interface site potential
V_{\ell}^{\textrm{i}}
is determined by the geometric reconstruction parameters
C_{\ell;\rho,\varsigma}
so that for each
\ell\in\Lambda^{\textrm{i}},\rho,\varsigma\in\mathscr{R}_{\ell}
,
V_{\ell}^{\textrm{i}}(Du_{h}):=V\Big(\big({\textstyle\sum_{\varsigma\in\mathscr{R}_{\ell}}C_{\ell;\rho,\varsigma}D_{\varsigma}u_{h}(\ell)}\big)_{\rho\in\mathscr{R}_{\ell}}\Big)
and the energy and force patch test consistency conditions are satisfied as follows
[
53
]
:

(4.2)

V_{\ell}^{\textrm{i}}({\bf 0})=V({\bf 0}),\quad\forall\ell\in\Lambda_{\textrm{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm grac}_{h}({\bf 0}),v\rangle=0\quad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}_{h}.

The QC problem of the GRAC method is to solve the energy minimization problem

(4.3)

u^{\rm grac}_{h}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm grac}_{h}(u_{h}),~u_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}\big\}.

The second method is the QCF method which is a force based QC method with sharp interface. We first define the pure Cauchy-Born finite element functional
[
33
]
for
u_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}
by,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm cb}_{h}(u_{h}):=\int_{\Omega_{h}}Q_{h}\big[W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{h}+\nabla u_{0})-W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{0})\big]\rm dx,

where the piecewise constant mid-point interpolant
Q_{h}v\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
for a function
v:\Omega_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined such that
Q_{h}v(x):=v(x_{T})
for
x\in T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, where
x_{T}:=\frac{1}{|T|}\int_{T}x\rm dx
. The variational formulation of the QCF method is then given by

(4.5)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm qcf}(u_{h}),v_{h}\rangle:=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}(u_{h}),\chi_{\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}}v_{h}\rangle+\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm cb}_{h}(u_{h}),(1-\chi_{\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}})v_{h}\rangle,

where
\chi_{\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}}
is the characteristic function of the atomistic region
\Lambda_{\textrm{a}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}
is the atomistic energy defined in (
2.7
). The QC problem of the QCF method is to solve the variational nonlinear system

(4.6)

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{F}_{h}^{\rm qcf}(u^{\rm qcf}_{h}),v_{h}\rangle=0,\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}.

4.2.
The adaptive algorithm for the blended QC methods

We then consider the blended QC methods and the corresponding adaptive algorithm. The primary advantage of the blended methods is the simplicity of modeling and implementation, which make these methods well-received for the simulations of complex defects in real-world applications.

All the blended QC methods depend on a so-called blending function
\beta\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\beta=0
in
\Omega_{\textrm{a}}
,
\beta=1
in
\Omega_{\textrm{c}}
and
{\rm supp}(\nabla\beta)\subset\Omega_{\rm b}
which characterizes the transition of the atomistic model in the defect core to the continuum model in the far field. The choice of the blending function
\beta
significantly influceds the accuracy of the corresponding blended QC coupling methods and is detailed analyzed in
[
41
,
40
,
33
]
. In the current work we employ the same blending function as that in
[
60
,
40
]
, which is obtained in a preprocessing step by approximately minimizing
\|\nabla^{2}\beta\|_{L^{2}}
.

We introduce three typical blended QC methods. The first one is the BQCE method which is an energy-based method
[
40
,
33
]
. For
u_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}
, the BQCE energy functional is obtained by combining the atomistic and continuum energy functionals via a blending function
\beta
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{h}^{\rm bqce}(u_{h}):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda\cap\Omega_{\rm a}}V^{\prime}_{\ell}(Du_{h})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda\cap\Omega_{\rm b}}\big(1-\beta(\ell)\big)V^{\prime}_{\ell}(Du_{h})+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm b}}\beta_{T}\big(W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{h}|_{T}+\nabla u_{0}|_{T})-W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{0}|_{T})\big)

(4.8)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{\textrm{c}}}Q_{h}\big(W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{h}+\nabla u_{0})-W^{\prime}(\nabla u_{0})\big)\rm dx

where
V^{\prime}_{\ell}
and
W^{\prime}
are defined by (
2.7
) and (
2.11
) respectively,
Q_{h}
is the same piecewise constant mid-point interpolation operator as that in (
4.4
),
\beta_{T}:=\beta(x_{T})
with
x_{T}
being the barycenter of
T
and
\mathcal{T}_{\rm b}
is the
canonical
triangulation induced by
\Lambda\cap\Omega_{\rm b}
. The BQCE problem is then to find

(4.9)

u^{\rm bqce}_{h}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm bqce}_{h}(u_{h}),~u_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}\big\}.

While the BQCE method (
4.2
) blends the atomistic and the continuum energies, the BQCF method
[
34
,
33
]
blends the atomistic and the continuum force balance equations and is thus a force-based method. Recall the definition of the blending function
\beta\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the atomistic energy
\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}
and the pure Cauchy-Born energy
\mathcal{E}^{\rm cb}_{h}
given in (
2.7
) and (
4.4
) respectively. The BQCF operator is the nonlinear map
\mathscr{F}^{\rm bqcf}_{h}:\mathscr{U}_{h}\rightarrow(\mathscr{U}_{h})^{*}
, defined by

(4.10)

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{F}^{\rm bqcf}_{h}(u_{h}),v_{h}\rangle:=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm a}(u_{h}),(1-\beta)v_{h}\rangle+\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm cb}_{h}(u_{h}),\beta v_{h}\rangle.

In the BQCF method we solve the following variational nonlinear system

(4.11)

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{F}_{h}^{\rm bqcf}(u^{\rm bqcf}_{h}),v_{h}\rangle=0,\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}.

The third method is the BGFC method which combines the benefits of blending
[
33
]
and ghost force correction
[
66
]
.
The BGFC method is first proposed and analyzed in
[
60
]
and here we introduce an alternative but simplified formulation. Let
\mathcal{E}^{\rm bqce}_{\rm hom}(\hat{u}_{0})
be the BQCE energy at a suitable “predictor”
\hat{u}_{0}
. Then the energy funtional of BGFC method can be written as

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm bgfc}(u_{h})=\mathcal{E}^{\rm bqce}(u_{h})-\big\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\rm bqce}_{\rm hom}(\hat{u}_{0}),u_{h}\big\rangle.

Motivated by
[
60
, Section 2.7 and Section 4.2]
, we use (
4.12
) with
\hat{u}_{0}=0
for all types of crystalline defects in our implementation.

The BGFC problem we would like to solve is to find

(4.13)

u^{\rm bgfc}_{h}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm bgfc}_{h}(u_{h}),~u_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}\big\}.

The consistency conditions of the three methods just introduced are listed in Table
1
. The consistency conditions for point defects and for anti-plane dislocation essentially follow from
[
41
]
and
[
60
,
33
]
but are adapted to the setting of the current work, while that for anti-plane crack is in fact a new speculated result. It is worth mentioning that to obtain the consistency, we exploit the
a priori
knowledge that
R_{\textrm{a}}=R_{\rm b}
, i.e., the radius of the atomistic region is the same as the width of the blending region. This raises the additional task for the adaptive algorithm of the blended QC methods compared with Algorithm
1
, which is the proper allocation of the atomistic and the blending regions on the fly.

Indeed, the only existing work for blended type of methods
[
74
]
, which considers the
a posteriori
error control for a BQCF method in 1D, avoids this additional task by fixing
R_{\textrm{a}}
and
R_{\rm b}

[
74

fulfill this additional task. In particular, the

(Step 3) utilizes a modified Döfler strategy
[
21

to allocate the two regions based on a ratio
\alpha
computed according to the distribution of the
a posteriori
error estimator of the atomistic and the blending regions. More precisely, we construct a set
\mathcal{M}^{k}\subset\mathcal{M}
to contain the marked elements that are within
k
layers to
\Lambda_{\rm a}

then contains the elements to be refined in the continuum region. For
T\in\mathcal{M}^{k}

{\rm dist}(T,\Lambda_{\textrm{a}})\leq{\rm dist}(T,\Lambda_{\textrm{c}})
, we assign it to constitute a new subset
\mathcal{M}^{k}_{\textrm{a}}
. We compute the ratio of the summation of the error estimator for
T\in\mathcal{M}^{k}_{\textrm{a}}
and that for

by (
4.15
). According to this ratio

[\alpha k]
and
k-[\alpha k]

5

4.3.
Discussion and possible extension to other QC coupling methods

Apart from the blended and the sharp interfaced coupling methods, there are other QC coupling methods employed in both engineering and mathematical communities. To comment on the versatility of the error estimator proposed in this work, we present two examples as follows.

The first one is the so-called flexible boundary condition (FBC) method
[
29
,
28
]
, which applies the continuum solutions as the boundary conditions of the atomistic problem in the core region. The FBC method can be formulated as: find
u^{\rm fbc}_{h}:=\{u^{\rm a},u^{\rm c}\}
such that

(4.16)

\displaystyle({\bf P}^{\rm a})\;\left\{\begin{aligned} \;\mathcal{L}[u^{\rm a}]&=0&\qquad&\text{in}\;\Lambda_{\rm a},\\
\;u&=u^{\rm c}&&\text{in}\;\Lambda_{\rm i},\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle({\bf P}^{\rm c})\;\left\{\begin{aligned} \;\mathcal{L}_{\rm cb}[u^{\rm c}]&=0&\qquad&\text{in}\;\Lambda_{\rm c},\\
\;u&=u^{\rm a}&&\text{on}\;\Lambda_{\rm i}.\end{aligned}\right.,

where
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm cb}
are the force operator of the atomistic and Cauchy-Born models, respectively.

The second one is the optimization-based QC methods, which requires the atomistic and the continuum subdomains with an overlap region
\Omega_{\textrm{i}}:=\Omega_{\textrm{a}}\cap\Omega_{\textrm{c}}
. This is an alternative ghost-force-free method. The optimization-based QC method is to solve the constrained minimization problem:
find
u^{\rm opt}_{h}:=\{u^{\rm a},u^{\rm c}\}
such that
\|\nabla u^{\textrm{a}}-\nabla I_{\textrm{a}}u^{\textrm{c}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\rm i})}
is minimized
subject to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{a}}(u^{\textrm{a}}),v\rangle=0&\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{\textrm{a}}_{0}\\[4.30554pt]
\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{c}}(u^{\textrm{c}}),v\rangle=0&\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{\textrm{c}}_{0}\end{array}\right.\quad\textrm{and}\quad\int_{\Omega_{\textrm{i}}}u^{\textrm{a}}-I_{\textrm{a}}u^{\textrm{c}}\rm dx=0.

The objective ensures that the mismatch between
\bar{u}^{\textrm{a}}
and
\bar{u}^{\textrm{c}}
over
\Omega_{\textrm{i}}
is as small as possible.

Both of these aforementioned methods are consistent under proper constructions
[
49
,
29
]
and are of substantial significance, yet a noticeable gap exists in the availability of the corresponding adaptive algorithms. In light of the framework shown in Section
3
, the proposed residual-force based error estimator can in principle be applied to both methods. This primarily attributes to the fact that it is independent from the detailed formulations of the QC methods. Notably, in the FBC method, the residual-force based error estimator can be interpreted as a generalized speed for an interface motion problem. This perspective enables its resolution through the utilization of the well-established fast marching method
[
65
]
. Conversely, in the case of optimization-based adaptive/corrective methods, the integration of the adaptive algorithm necessitates its incorporation within the interface minimization problem. A thorough exploration of this promising direction will be a focal point of our future work.

with the parameters

specifically, which are the same as the numerical experiments presented in

5.1.
Micro-crack

The first defect we consider is the micro-crack, which is a prototypical example of point defects which serves as an example of a localized defect with an anisotropic shape. To generate this defect, we remove
k
atoms from
{\Lambda^{\rm hom}}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}^{\rm def}:=\{-(k/2)e_{1},\ldots,(k/2-1)e_{1})\},

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm if}\quad k\quad\textrm{is even},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}^{\rm def}:=\{-(k-1)/2e_{1},\ldots,(k-1)/2e_{1})\},

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm if}\quad k\quad\textrm{is odd},

and
\Lambda={\Lambda^{\rm hom}}\setminus\Lambda_{k}^{\rm def}

We set

and apply an isotropic stretch
\mathrm{S}
and a shear
\gamma_{II}

5.1.1.
Adaptive simulations for a micro-crack by sharp interfaced QC methods

We first present the adaptive simulations of a micro-crack by adaptive QC methods with sharp interface. In particular, we consider the a specific GRAC method which is adopted in the rest of this paper, whose geometric reconstruction parameters

for

C_{\ell,\rho,\rho}=1
for
\ell+\rho\in\Lambda^{\textrm{a}}
; (e)
C_{\ell,\rho,\rho}=2/3
for
\ell+\rho\in\Lambda^{\textrm{c}}

We test the adaptive GRAC method based on three different

,
[

5.2.3.
Discussion

Besides the capability of guiding the adaptivity for the blended QC methods in more than one dimension which is yet to be developed for the residual-stress based approach, we have to comment on two additional advantages of the residual-force based error estimates particularly for sharp interfaced methods that are not easily illustrated by figures (c.f
[
73
,
77
,
35
]
).

The first one is the significant reduction of complexity in the implementation. Besides a unified formulation and thus the reusability of the codes for different methods, the residual-force based estimator does not rely on the so-called “stress tensor correction” which involves a laborious optimization problem
[
73
, Algorithm 1]
. The necessity of such correction stems from the inherent lack of uniqueness in the formulation of the residual stress. Without such correction, the adaptive algorithm will tend to put redundant degrees of freedom around the interface in each adaptive step so that the optimal convergence rate may not be achieved. In addition, according to our sampling strategy, there is no need of a manually selected ”interface buffer” region where the finite element nodes are deliberately set to coincide with the lattice points
[
77
, Figure 5]
.

The second one is the possibility to be extended to the systems with finite range interactions. The residual-stress based error estimate for the GRAC method with finite range interactions has been discussed in
[
35
]
. However, in the setting of finite range interactions, the atomistic stress loses its locality and thus prompts certain “ad hoc” approximations to render the residual amenable to the elementwise summation. Moreover, such approximations are effective only for simple point defects but become untenable for more intricate defects such as dislocations and cracks which are discussed immediately in the subsequent section.

5.3.
Anti-plane crack

We finally consider the defect of anti-plane crack, which is of more practical interest and considerably more complicated than the previous two types of defects. To the best knowledge of the authors, it is also the first time that such type of defect is simulated by QC methods. We apply the same setting as that for the screw dislocation. The only difference is that the far-field
predictor

u_{0}
is more complicated, which is briefly reviewed in
Appendix A.2
.

The significance and motivation behind the adaptive simulations of cracks, distinct from micro-cracks and screw dislocations, encompass the following key aspects. First of all, the construction of the energy based sharp interfaced method for cracks presents considerable challenges. In particular, according to the fundamental principles of the GRAC method
[
59
]
, the determination of reconstruction parameters
C_{\ell;\rho,\zeta}
around the interface for cracks is hindered by the presence of crack tips. Consequently, the blended QC methods appear to be a more suitable choice for simulating cracks. Second, conducting rigorous
a priori
analysis of QC methods proves considerably more challenging. Within the framework proposed in
[
24
]
, the critical component of the
a priori
analysis is the establishment of rigorous equilibrium decay estimates. This involves an intricate analysis of the lattice Green’s function along with the theoretical proof of the estimates of the residual forces. However, when it comes to the
a posteriori
error estimates, the intricate technical nuances aforementioned are somehow circumvented and the estimates can be applied to improve the efficiency of the simulations of the cracks, which highlight the novelty and the significance of our proposed methodology. In light of these considerations, we proceed with numerical tests employing the residual-force based error estimator for all three blended QC methods.

6.
Conclusion

In this work we propose a unified framework for theory based
a posteriori
error estimate of consistent quasicontinuum (QC) methods for the molecular mechanics simulations of crystal defects. Based on the unified error estimate, we develop the adaptive algorithms for a range of QC methods and conduct the adaptive simulations for several important crystalline defects, including the micro-crack, the anti-plane dislocation and the anti-plan crack. In particular, we have essentially made three important advancements in this study compared with previous researches on the adaptive QC methods
[
58
,
73
,
27
,
77
]
. The first one is the derivation of the unified residual-force based
a posteriori
error estimate that is independent of particular QC method we consider. This leads to the save of a significant amount of effort for the implementation of the
a posteriori
error control problems. The second one is the development of the adaptive strategy for the blended QC methods. Such adaptive strategy is able to simultaneously guide the refinement of the mesh in the continuum region as well as the allocations of the atomistic and blending regions. The third one is the simulations of a crack by adaptive QC methods for the first time. It significant since the geometry of a crack is complicated and the
a priori
analysis possesses substantial challenges in the context of QC methods.

Although we believe that the
a posteriori
error estimate and the adaptive algorithms in this work are generally applicable to a wide range of concurrent multiscale methods, this research still raises a few open problems for further investigation.

The first one is the
a priori
error estimates of QC methods for the straight edge dislocation and the anti-plane crack. To address this issue, a possible solution is to investigate an equivalent ghost force removal formulation (
4.12
), where a suitable “predictor”
\hat{u}_{0}
needs to be constructed. The second one is the adaptive simulations for defects in three dimensions. This presents significant challenges in mesh generation and adaptation in the implementation. Recent advancements
[
27
]
may provide a preliminary in this direction. The third one is the extension to more complex crystal defects. Practical crystal defects such as dislocation nucleation and grain boundaries have already attracted significant attention. Since the
a priori
analysis for such problems is difficult, these are precisely the scenarios where the adaptive methods are expected to demonstrate their great advantage. We believe that the approach presented in the current research contributes a solid groundwork for these practical and important problems.

P(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\quad\text{for}~\ell\in B\mathbb{Z}^{3}.
