Many-to-few for non-local branching Markov process
Simon C. Harris, Emma Horton, Andreas E. Kyprianou, Ellen Powell

Abstract
We provide a many-to-few formula in the general setting of non-local branching Markov processes. This formula allows one to compute expectations of k \displaystyle k -fold sums over functions of the population at k \displaystyle k different times. The result generalises [ 14 ] to the non-local setting, as introduced in [ 11 ] and [ 8 ] . As an application, we consider the case when the branching process is critical, and conditioned to survive for a large time. In this setting, we prove a general formula for the limiting law of the death time of the most recent common ancestor of two particles selected uniformly from the population at two different times, as t → ∞ \displaystyle t\to\infty . Moreover, we describe the limiting law of the population sizes at two different times, in the same asymptotic regime. Key words: non-local branching processes, many-to-few, spines. MSC 2020: 60J80, 60J25

Introduction

1.1
Main results

Our main result, the so called
many-to-few formula
, is a way to rewrite the expectation of a general
\displaystyle k
-fold sum, depending on the entire configuration of a branching Markov process at
\displaystyle k
different times, as an expectation with respect to the behaviour of
\displaystyle k
distinguished lines of descent under a tilted measure. We generalise the original and well cited main result of
[
14
]
, by allowing for non-local branching, and not requiring the
\displaystyle k
individuals to be sampled at the same time.

The many-to-few formula generalises the role of the classical spine decomposition for spatial branching processes, which converts expectation identities for additive functionals of spatial branching processes to Feynman-Kac formulae for a single Markov particle trajectory. The latter has proved to be an important tool in analysing the growth and spread of a rich variety of branching Markov processes and related models; see for example the monographs
[
1
,
6
,
18
,
15
,
2
]
among a wide base of research literature that is to too extensive to exhaustively list here.
The many-to-few formula has already played an important and similar role to the classical spine decomposition as a tool to interrogate various questions pertaining to particle correlation that arise in e.g. genealogical coalescent structure,
[
12
]
, martingale convergence,
[
10
]
, maximal displacement of extreme particles,
[
17
]
, the structure of level sets for branching Brownian motion,
[
4
]
and the analysis of certain models from the theory of stochastic genetics,
[
7
]
. We further remark that the many-to-few formula is related to recent work pertaining to asymptotic moment convergence in
[
8
]
.

We refrain from attempting to give a precise statement of the many-to-few formula here, deferring instead to Lemma
6
below, as we will need to introduce several objects in order for the formula to be understood in a meaningful way. We note that, simultaneously to the results we present here a general branching Markov process setting, similar ideas have been developed in
[
7
]
.

Our main motivating application is to understand the limiting genealogy of a so-called
critical
branching Markov process, when conditioned to survive for an arbitrarily long time. Our second main result, Proposition
8
, is a general statement in this direction. More precisely, for a critical non-local branching Markov process conditioned to survive until a large time
\displaystyle t
, we provide a precise asymptotic for the death time of the most recent common ancestor of two individuals sampled uniformly from the population at two different times. In Proposition
9
, we also describe the limiting law of the population sizes at two different times, in the same asymptotic regime.

1.2
Set-up and assumptions

Let
\displaystyle E
be a Lusin space. Throughout, will write
\displaystyle B(E)
for the Banach space of bounded measurable functions on
\displaystyle E
with norm
\displaystyle\lVert\cdot\rVert
,
\displaystyle B^{+}(E)
for non-negative bounded measurable functions on
\displaystyle E
and
\displaystyle B^{+}_{1}(E)
for the subset of functions in
\displaystyle B^{+}(E)
which are uniformly bounded by unity.

We consider a spatial branching process in which, given their point of creation, particles evolve independently according to a Markov process,
\displaystyle(\xi,\mathbf{P})
, which can be characterised via the semigroup
\displaystyle\mathtt{P}_{t}[f](x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(\xi_{t})]
, for
\displaystyle x\in E
,
\displaystyle t\geq 0
and
\displaystyle f\in B^{+}_{1}(E)
. In an event which we refer to as ‘branching’, particles positioned at
\displaystyle x
die at rate
\displaystyle\beta(x)
where
\displaystyle\beta\in B^{+}(E)
and instantaneously, new particles are created in
\displaystyle E
according to a point process. The configurations of these offspring are described by the random counting measure

\mathcal{Z}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}(A),

(1)

for Borel
\displaystyle A
in
\displaystyle E
. The law of the aforementioned point process may depend on
\displaystyle x
, the point of death of the parent, and we denote it by
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}
,
\displaystyle x\in E
, with associated expectation operator given by
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{x}
,
\displaystyle x\in E
. This information is captured in the so-called branching mechanism

\mathtt{G}[f](x):=\beta(x)\mathcal{E}_{x}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i})-f(x)\right],\qquad x\in E,\,f\in B^{+}_{1}(E).

(2)

Without loss of generality we can assume that
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}(N=1)=0
for all
\displaystyle x\in E
by viewing a branching event with one offspring as an extra jump in the motion. On the other hand, we do allow for the possibility that
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}(N=0)>0
for some or all
\displaystyle x\in E
.

Moreover, we do not need
\displaystyle\mathtt{P}
to have the Feller property, and it is not necessary that
\displaystyle\mathtt{P}
is conservative. That said, if so desired, we can append a cemetery state
\displaystyle\{\dagger\}
to
\displaystyle E
, which is to be treated as an absorbing state, and regard
\displaystyle\mathtt{P}
as conservative on the extended space
\displaystyle E\cup\{\dagger\}
, which can also be treated as a Lusin space. Equally, we can extend
\displaystyle\mathtt{G}
to
\displaystyle E\cup\{\dagger\}
by defining it to be zero on
\displaystyle\{\dagger\}
, that is no branching activity on the cemetery state.

Henceforth we refer to this spatial branching process as a
\displaystyle(\mathtt{P},\mathtt{G})
-branching Markov process. It is well known that if we arbitrarily choose an order for the particles at each branching event then we may denote the configuration of particles at time
\displaystyle t
by
\displaystyle\{x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{N_{t}}(t)\}
(where
\displaystyle N_{t}
denotes the number of particles alive at time
\displaystyle t
) and we can also associate an Ulam-Harris label

v_{i}(t)\in\Omega:=\{\emptyset\}\cup\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{N}^{n}

to the
\displaystyle i
-th particle at time
\displaystyle t
. For
\displaystyle v,w\in\Omega
, we write
\displaystyle v\preceq w
to mean that
\displaystyle v
is an ancestor of
\displaystyle w
, which means there exists
\displaystyle u\in\Omega
such that
\displaystyle vu=w
. Moreover, we write
\displaystyle v\prec w
to mean that
\displaystyle v\preceq w
in the strict sense, that is, the possibility that
\displaystyle v=w
is excluded. We say that
\displaystyle v,w\in\Omega
are siblings if there exists
\displaystyle u\in\Omega
and
\displaystyle i\neq j
such that
\displaystyle v=ui
and
\displaystyle w=uj
.

The branching Markov process can be described via the co-ordinate process
\displaystyle X=(X_{t},t\geq 0)
in the space of counting measures on
\displaystyle E\times\Omega
with non-negative integer total mass, denoted by
\displaystyle M(E\times\Omega)
, where

X_{t}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\delta_{(x_{i}(t),v_{i}(t))}(\cdot),\qquad t\geq 0.

In particular,
\displaystyle X

is Markovian in
\displaystyle M(E\times\Omega)
. Its probabilities will be denoted
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}:=(\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{x}},x\in E)
where for
\displaystyle x\in E
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{x}}
denotes the law of the process starting from
\displaystyle\delta_{(x,\emptyset)}\in M(E\times\Omega)
.

Under the additional assumption that
\displaystyle\mathrm{sup}_{x\in E}\,\mathcal{E}_{x}(N)<\infty
, where we recall that
\displaystyle N
is the (random) number of offspring produced at a branching event, we define the linear semigroup

\mathtt{T}_{t}[f](x):=\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{x}}[X_{t}[f]]:=\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{x}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}f(x_{i}(t))\right],\quad f\in B^{+}(E).

Now let us introduce an assumption, that we will use throughout the article unless stated otherwise. We will use the notation

\langle f,\mu\rangle:=\int_{E}f(x)\mu({\rm d}x),\quad f\in B(E),\mu\in M(E),

where
\displaystyle M(E)
is the set of finite measures on
\displaystyle E
.

Assumption 1
.

For the Markov process
\displaystyle(\xi,\mathbf{P})
, we assume that

(a)

it admits a càdlàg modification;

(b)

there exists an eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and a corresponding right eigenfunction
\displaystyle\varphi\in B^{+}(E)
and finite left eigenmeasure
\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}
such that, for
\displaystyle f\in B^{+}(E)
,

\langle\mathtt{T}_{t}[\varphi],\mu\rangle={\rm e}^{\lambda t}\langle{\varphi},{\mu}\rangle\text{ and }\langle{\mathtt{T}_{t}[f]},{\tilde{\varphi}}\rangle={\rm e}^{\lambda t}\langle f,{\tilde{\varphi}}\rangle,

for all
\displaystyle\mu\in M(E)
.

The first part of the above assumption is a regularity assumption on the Markov process
\displaystyle(\xi,\mathbf{P})
, which ensures that we can use the theory of martingale changes of measure. The second is a Perron Frobenius assumption that ensures the existence of the leading eigenvalue and corresponding eigenfunctions.

1.3
Outline

The many-to-few formula, Lemma
6
, will ultimately allow us to express expectations of general
\displaystyle k
-fold sums depending on the entire branching process under
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
, in terms of an expectation with respect to
\displaystyle k
so-called
spine particles
under a different measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
. In Section
2
, we define this measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
, introduce the notion of spines, and give an explicit expression for the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
, with respect to the original law of our branching process plus some uniformly chosen marked lines of descent (we call this measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
). We then state and prove our main result, the many-to-few formula, in Section
3
. We also explain some special cases in which the formula simplifies nicely. Section
4
is devoted to our main application, which is to derive some two-point asymptotics for the geneologies of critical branching processes, when they are conditioned to survive for a large time
\displaystyle t
. More precisely, we provide an asymptotic (as
\displaystyle t\to\infty
) for the death time of the most recent common ancestor of two particles sampled uniformly from the population at two different times.

2
Spines, martingales and changes of measure

In this section, we introduce two measures under which our class of branching Markov process additionally identifies
\displaystyle k
distinguished genealogical lines of descent, or
spines
. The first of these two measures is a simple adaptation of the original law. The second measure is identified via a change of measure with respect to a certain multiplicative martingale.

2.1
Definition of the measures
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}

2.1.1
Definition of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}

We first introduce a measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
on the set of processes taking values in
\displaystyle M(E\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,k\}))
, the space of counting measures on
\displaystyle E\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,k\})
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,k\})
is the set of subsets of
\displaystyle\{1,\dots,k\}
. For convenience, let
\displaystyle\tilde{X}
denote the branching process on the space
\displaystyle M(E\times\Omega\times\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,k\}))
, that is

\tilde{X}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\delta_{(x_{i}(t),v_{i}(t),\mathbf{b}_{i}(t))},

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{P}(\{1,\dots,k\})
denotes the set of marks carried by the
\displaystyle i
-th particle alive at time
\displaystyle t
. Whenever
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{i}(t)\neq\emptyset
, we refer to the individual
\displaystyle i
as a
spine
. In that case, we say that the spine
carries
\displaystyle|\mathbf{b}_{i}(t)|
marks
.
Given
\displaystyle\tilde{X}
, define
\displaystyle X
to be its projection onto
\displaystyle M(E\times\Omega)
.
With this notation in hand, we let
\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t},t\geq 0)
denote the natural filtration generated by
\displaystyle X
and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{k},t\geq 0)
denote the natural filtration generated by
\displaystyle\tilde{X}
.

Then, we have the following description of the measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
.

Definition 1
.

The construction of
\displaystyle\tilde{X}
under
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
goes as follows.

1.

We start with a single particle at
\displaystyle x\in E
which carries
\displaystyle k\geq 1
marks.

2.

All particles move according to the semigroup
\displaystyle\mathtt{P}
, independently of each other given their birth times and configurations.

3.

Let
\displaystyle\xi_{t}^{i}
denote the position of the particle that carries the mark
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq k
at time
\displaystyle t\geq 0
.

4.

A particle at
\displaystyle y\in E
carrying
\displaystyle j
marks
\displaystyle b_{1}<b_{2}<\dots<b_{j}
, dies at rate
\displaystyle\beta(y)
and simultaneously produces a random number of new particles according to
\displaystyle(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{P}_{y})
. The
\displaystyle j
marks each choose a particle to follow independently and uniformly from the
\displaystyle N=\langle 1,\mathcal{Z}\rangle
available particles.

5.

In the event that a particle carrying
\displaystyle j>0
marks dies and is replaced by
\displaystyle 0
offspring, it is sent to the cemetery state, along with its marks.

Note that the above definition of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
is such that
\displaystyle X
has the same law under both
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
.

2.1.2
The measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}

We will now introduce a second measure,
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
, under which particles not carrying any marks evolve in the same manner as particles under
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
, while spines (that is, particles carrying marks) evolve differently. In the next section we will show that
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
can be defined via a change of measure of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
. Before describing the process, we need to introduce some more notation.

Suppose that we are given a functional
\displaystyle(\zeta(\cdot,t),t\geq 0)
such that
\displaystyle\zeta(\xi,t)
is a non-negative unit-mean martingale with respect to the natural filtration of the Markov process
\displaystyle(\xi_{t},t\geq 0)
with semigroup
\displaystyle(\mathtt{P}_{t},t\geq 0)
. We will assume that
\displaystyle\zeta
takes the value
\displaystyle 0
whenever
\displaystyle\xi_{t}=\dagger
.
Now for
\displaystyle k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{k,n}={\bf 1}_{(n\leq N)}\sum_{[k_{1},\dots,k_{N}]_{k}^{n}}{{k}\choose{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}}\prod_{i:k_{i}>0}\varphi(x_{i}),

(3)

where
\displaystyle(x_{i},i=1,\dots,N)
are as in (
1
) and
\displaystyle[k_{1},\dots,k_{N}]_{k}^{n}
is the set of non-negative integer
\displaystyle N
-tuples
\displaystyle(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
such that
\displaystyle k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}=k
and exactly
\displaystyle n
of the
\displaystyle k_{i}
s are positive. If
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}
corresponds to the offspring of a particle carrying
\displaystyle k
marks, one can think of a single term in the sum
\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{k,n}
as a weight associated to the event that the
\displaystyle k
marks are distributed among the offspring, by giving exactly
\displaystyle k_{i}
marks to the
\displaystyle i
th offspring particle,
\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,N
(see below for a more precise interpretation).
With this notation in hand, now define

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leq n\leq k}\varphi(x)^{-n}\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{k,n}

(4)

and set
\displaystyle{\mathtt{m}}_{k}(x)=\mathcal{E}_{x}(\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{k}(x))
.
Note that in the case of local branching,
\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{k}\equiv N^{k}
for any
\displaystyle x\in E
.

Definition 2
.

The construction of
\displaystyle\tilde{X}
under
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
goes as follows.

1.

Again, we begin with one particle at
\displaystyle x\in E
carrying all the marks
\displaystyle\{1,\dots,k\}
. In what follows, particles carrying marks are referred to as spines.

2.

Any spine (that is, any particle carrying any number of marks) moves according to the semigroup

\mathtt{P}_{t}[{g}](x):=\frac{1}{\zeta(\xi,0)}\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\zeta(\xi,t)g(\xi_{t})\right],\qquad x\in E,

(5)

where
\displaystyle(\xi_{s},s\geq 0)
denotes the motion.

3.

Suppose a spine carries marks
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{j})
. Then for each
\displaystyle 1\leq n\leq j
, an independent exponential clock rings at rate
\displaystyle\beta(x){\mathtt{m}}_{j,n}(x),

{\mathtt{m}}_{j,n}(x):=\varphi(x)^{-n}\mathcal{E}_{x}(\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{j,n}),\qquad x\in E.

When the first of these clocks rings, a branching event occurs, and if it is the
\displaystyle n
th clock, the
\displaystyle j
marks carried by the parent will be given to exactly
\displaystyle n
distinct offspring particles.

More precisely, if the first clock to ring is the
\displaystyle n
th one, the positions of the offspring are described by
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}
with law
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}^{(j,n)}
defined by

\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{P}_{x}^{(j,n)}}{{\rm d}\mathcal{P}_{x}}:=\frac{\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{j,n}}{\mathcal{E}_{x}(\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{j,n})}.

(6)

Then given
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}
, for each
\displaystyle(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\in[k_{1},\dots,k_{N}]^{n}_{j}
, the probability that the
\displaystyle i
th offspring particle receives exactly
\displaystyle k_{i}
marks for each
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq N
, is given by

\frac{{{k}\choose{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}}\prod_{i:k_{i}>0}\varphi(x_{i})}{\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{j,n}}.

On this event, the way that the marks
\displaystyle b_{1},\dots,b_{j}
are distributed among the offspring is that such that any valid configuration (that is, satisfying the constraint that exactly
\displaystyle k_{i}
marks are given to offspring particle
\displaystyle i
for each
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq N
) has the same probability:

\frac{1}{{{k}\choose{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}}}.

4.

Particles that do not carry marks issue independent copies of
\displaystyle(X,\mathbb{P})
. Marked particles then continue from Step 2.

Remark 1
.

An alternative description of the third step above, is in terms of the
total
branching rate. Namely, suppose a spine carries marks
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{j})
. Then it branches at rate
\displaystyle\beta(x)\mathtt{m}_{j}(x)
, and on such a branching event, the offspring positions are described by
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}
whose law is weighted (with respect to
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}
) by
\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{j}/\mathtt{m}_{j}(x)
. Moreover, given
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}
(and the position
\displaystyle x
of the spine before branching), any particular allocation of the marks
\displaystyle b_{1},\ldots,b_{j}
among the
\displaystyle N
offspring (that is, a partition
\displaystyle S_{1},\dots,S_{N}
of
\displaystyle\{b_{1},\dots,b_{j}\}
) has probability equal to
\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\mathcal{Z}\rangle_{j}^{-1}\textstyle\prod_{i:|S_{i}|>0}(\varphi(x_{i})/\varphi(x))
.
However, we prefer to highlight the decomposition according to
\displaystyle n
(as given in the description of the measure above), since this will be the one we use in practice.

Remark 2
.

There are many different variants of the measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
that we could have described, that would also be related to
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
by a martingale change of measure, and would also lead to a many-to-few type formula. Our specific choice of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
is motivated by our main application: describing the genealogical structure of the branching process when it is conditioned on survival. In particular, when we use our many-to-few formula for this purpose, we get an extremely simple structure - see (
3.2
). Note that in the case of local branching, this measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
is identical to that which appears in
[
14
]
. When
\displaystyle k=1
, and we make a particular choice for
\displaystyle\zeta
, this also agrees with the “spine decomposition” given in
[
11
]
.

2.2
The martingale change of measure

We will now explain how the measures
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{k}
are connected via a change of measure.
Let us first introduce some further notation.

Given
\displaystyle v\in\Omega
, note that
\displaystyle X_{t}(E\times\{v\})=0
except on some unique (possibly empty) interval
\displaystyle[\sigma_{v},\tau_{v})
, on which
\displaystyle X_{t}(E\times\{v\})=1
. We will often use the notation
\displaystyle\tau_{v}^{-}
for the left limit of
\displaystyle\tau_{v}
. If
\displaystyle t\in[\sigma_{v},\tau_{v})
, there exists a unique
\displaystyle X_{v}(t)\in E
such that
\displaystyle X_{t}(X_{v}(t)\times\{v\})=1
and a unique
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{v}\in\mathcal{P}(\{1,\dots,k\})
such that
\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t}(E\times\{v\}\times\mathbf{b}_{v})=1
for all
\displaystyle t\in[\sigma_{v},\tau_{v})
. Heuristically,
\displaystyle\sigma_{v}
and
\displaystyle\tau_{v}
are the birth and death times of particle
\displaystyle v
, respectively,
\displaystyle X_{v}(t)
represents its position at time
\displaystyle t
during its lifetime, and
\displaystyle{\bf b}_{v}
represents the set of marks it carries. We further set
\displaystyle D_{v}=|\mathbf{b}_{v}|
to be the number of marks carried by the particle with label
\displaystyle v
. For each
\displaystyle v\in\Omega
, let
\displaystyle N_{v}
denote the number of offspring produced by
\displaystyle X_{v}(\tau_{v})
.

Set
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{t}:=\{v\in\Omega:t\in[\sigma_{v},\tau_{v})\}
so that
\displaystyle N_{t}=|\mathcal{N}_{t}|
. For each
\displaystyle t\geq 0
and
\displaystyle j=1,\dots,k
, let
\displaystyle\psi_{t}^{j}
and
\displaystyle\xi_{t}^{j}
denote the unique elements of
\displaystyle\Omega
and
\displaystyle E
, respectively, such that there exists
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{P}(\{1,\dots,k\})
with
\displaystyle j\in\mathbf{b}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t}(\xi_{t}^{j}\times\{\psi_{t}^{j}\}\times\mathbf{b})=1
. Finally, let us define the skeleton at time
\displaystyle t
to be
\displaystyle S_{k}(t):=\{\psi_{s}^{1},\ldots,\psi_{s}^{k}:s\leq t\}
, so that
\displaystyle S_{k}(t)
is a subset of labels in the tree. For
\displaystyle v\in S_{k}(t)
, let
\displaystyle M_{v}
denote the number of distinct offspring of
\displaystyle v
that are given a mark (that is, the number of distinct spine offspring).

Definition 3
.

Define the
\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{k}
-adapted process
\displaystyle({W}_{t}^{k},t\geq 0)
by

\displaystyle\displaystyle{W}_{t}^{k}:=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\prod_{v\in S_{k}(t)}\frac{\zeta(X_{v},\tau_{v}^{-}\wedge t)}{\zeta(X_{v},\sigma_{v})}\prod_{v\in S_{k}(t)}{\rm e}^{-\int_{\sigma_{v}}^{\tau_{v}\wedge t}\beta(X_{v}(s))({\mathtt{m}}_{D_{v}}(X_{v}(s))-1){\rm d}s}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\prod_{v\in S_{k}(t)\backslash\{\emptyset\}}{\varphi(X_{v}(\sigma_{v}))}\prod_{v\in S_{k}(t)\backslash\mathcal{N}_{t}}\frac{N_{v}^{D_{v}}}{\varphi(X_{v}(\tau_{v}^{-}))^{M_{v}}}.

(7)

Remark 3
.

Henceforth, for
\displaystyle s\leq t
and
\displaystyle x,y\in E
, we set
\displaystyle\zeta(x,t)/\zeta(y,s)=1
whenever
\displaystyle\zeta(x,t)=\zeta(y,s)=0
.

Remark 4
.

We emphasise that then when a branching event occurs and the spines all choose the same particle to follow, this is included as an element of the skeleton,
\displaystyle S_{k}(t)
.

Remark 5
.

Note that we may equivalently write

\displaystyle\displaystyle W_{t}^{k}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\prod_{v\in S_{k}(t)}\frac{\zeta(X_{v},\tau_{v}^{-}\wedge t)}{\zeta(X_{v},\sigma_{v})}

3.1

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle t^{-1}

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mathtt{Exponential}({2}/{\Sigma(1-a)\langle 1,\tilde{\varphi}\rangle})

\displaystyle\mathtt{Gamma}(1,{2}/{\Sigma(1-a)\langle 1,\tilde{\varphi}\rangle})

(This roughly corresponds to Step 5 in the full proof below).

•

Plugging these asymptotics into the
