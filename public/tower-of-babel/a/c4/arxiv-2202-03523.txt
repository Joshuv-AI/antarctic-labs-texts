Resource Marginal Problems
Chung-Yun Hsieh, Gelo Noel M. Tabia, Yu-Chun Yin, Yeong-Cherng Liang

Abstract
We introduce the resource marginal problems , which concern the possibility of having a resource-free target subsystem compatible with a given collection of marginal density matrices. By identifying an appropriate choice of resource R R and target subsystem T {\rm T} , our problems reduce, respectively, to the well-known marginal problems for quantum states and the problem of determining if a given quantum system is a resource. More generally, we say that a set of marginal states is resource-free incompatible with a target subsystem T {\rm T} if all global states compatible with this set must result in a resourceful state in T {\rm T} of type R R . We show that this incompatibility induces a resource theory that can be quantified by a monotone and obtain necessary and sufficient conditions for this monotone to be computable as a conic program with finite optimum. We further show, via the corresponding witnesses, that (1) resource-free incompatibility is equivalent to an operational advantage in some channel-discrimination tasks, and (2) some specific cases of such tasks fully characterize the convertibility between marginal density matrices exhibiting resource-free incompatibility. Through our framework, one sees a clear connection between any marginal problem—which implicitly involves some notion of incompatibility—for quantum states and a resource theory for quantum states. We also establish a close connection between the physical relevance of resource marginal problems and the ground state properties of certain many-body Hamiltonians. In terms of application, the universality of our framework leads, for example, to a further quantitative understanding of the incompatibility associated with the recently-proposed entanglement marginal problems and entanglement transitivity problems.

2
Preliminary Notions

2.1
Quantum Resource Theories: a Brief Introduction

In the study of quantum information science, once a new resource has been discovered, such as entanglement, coherence, etc., it is important to ask the following three questions:

(Q1)

How to characterize it?
That is, can we find necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of this resource so that we can somehow detect it?

(Q2)

How to compare its contents?
Sometimes, merely knowing the existence of the resource is not enough.
For instance, a perfect performance in teleportation
[
51
]
not only needs entanglement, but also a maximally entangled one.
Hence, when we have two states both equipped with the resource, it is important to know how to compare them.

(Q3)

How to quantify it?
Also, it is useful to know whether the strength of this resource can be evaluated in a quantitative way.

It turns out that
quantum resource theories
provide a systematic, general way to do these.
We now recall the main ingredients of a quantum resource theory, or simply a resource theory.
For further details, see, e.g., Ref.
[
10
]
.
Formally, a resource theory for quantum states is specified by a triplet
(R,\mathcal{F}_{R},\mathcal{O}_{R})
, where
R
represents the given resource.

\mathcal{F}_{R}
is the set of
(resource-) free states
, which contains all states that
do not
possess the given resource
R
.
Finally,
\mathcal{O}_{R}
is the set of
free operations
, which are physical transformations of states that
cannot
generate the given resource
R
.
For instance, when we aim to study entanglement in a given bipartition (e.g., specified by two agents A and B), we can consider

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\text{entanglement}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{R}

\displaystyle=\text{separable states}

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{R}

\displaystyle=\text{local operations and classical communications (LOCC)}

Note that, throughout, we allow
\mathcal{F}_{R}
and
\mathcal{O}_{R}
to be of
any finite
dimension.
For entanglement,
\mathcal{F}_{R}
contains separable states with
all
possible system sizes in the given bipartition AB.
They can be two-qubit separable states shared by A and B, an
(N+1)
-qubit separable states where A holds
N
qubits, B holds one, etc.
Similarly,
\mathcal{O}_{R}
contains LOCC acting separately on all the subsystems held by each agent.

Of course, resource theories are not only for entanglement.
Let us consider another example, which is the resource theory of non-equilibrium thermodynamics:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\text{athermality}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{R}

\displaystyle=\text{thermal states}

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{R}

\displaystyle=\text{thermal operations}

In this case, the resource is the status of being out-of-equilibrium (i.e.,
athermality
), and the free states are thermal equilibrium states associated with the given system Hamiltonian and background temperature.
Finally, the allowed dynamics are thermal operations that cannot drive such states out of thermal equilibrium (see, e.g., Refs.
[
26
,
28
,
27
]
for details).

In general, once we are able to define
\mathcal{F}_{R}
and
\mathcal{O}_{R}
, a resource theory can be formulated to study the corresponding resource
R
.
If we start by setting
\mathcal{F}_{R}
, then we identify all states in
\mathcal{F}_{R}
as
free
.
Hence, a suitable characterization of this mathematical set can allow us to characterize states
outside
this set, which can further answer question
(Q1)
.

Note that once we choose
\mathcal{F}_{R}
, the structure of
\mathcal{O}_{R}
cannot be arbitrary.
Internal consistency of a resource theory demands that any free operation
\mathcal{E}
acting on a free state
\eta

cannot
generate a resource state
[
10
]
, hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\eta)\in\mathcal{F}_{R}\quad\;\forall\;\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{R}\;\&\;\forall\;\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{O}_{R}.

(1)

This is sometimes called the
golden rule

[
10
]
, which is a central necessary condition in every resource theory.
With this golden rule, channels in
\mathcal{O}_{R}
are understood to have
no
ability to generate
R
.
Now, if we can use a free operation
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{O}_{R}
to map a state
\rho
to another
\sigma
; i.e.,
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sigma
, then
by definition
the output
cannot
be more resourceful than the input, since free operation cannot generate
R
.
Hence, a suitable characterization of convertibility under free operations can help us to
compare
the resource content of two different states, thereby answering question
(Q2)
.

Finally, for the quantification of
R
[i.e., question
(Q3)
], one makes use of a
resource monotone

Q_{R}
, which satisfies

•

Q_{R}(\rho)\geq 0
where equality holds if
\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{R}
.

•

Q_{R}[\mathcal{E}(\rho)]\leq Q_{R}(\rho)\;\forall\;\rho
and
\forall\;\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{O}_{R}
.

Every
Q_{R}
satisfying these two conditions can be understood as an appropriate quantifier of the resource
R
, thereby answering question
(Q3)
.
While one can impose more axioms on the definition of
Q_{R}
, we shall keep only the minimal requirements in this work.

Finally, note that although we only mention resource theories of quantum states, this general approach is not restricted to states.
One may also consider resource theories for measurements
[
52
,
53
]
, channels
[
54
,

,

,

,
58
,
59

, measurement assemblages
[

]
, etc.—as long as the mathematical properties can be well-defined and analyzed.

2.2
Marginal Problems

Next, let us recall the marginal problem for quantum states.
Consider a finite-dimensional
n
-partite global system
{\rm S}
and let
{\bf S}
be the collection of all
2^{n}-1
nontrivial combinations of the subsystems of
{\rm S}
.
Moreover, let
\bm{\Lambda}
be a collection of such subsystems, i.e.,
\bm{\Lambda}\subseteq{\bf S}
. Obviously, any combination
{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}
of subsystems satisfies
{\rm X\in{\bf S}}
.
We say that a set of marginal states
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\coloneqq\{\sigma_{\rm X}\}_{{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}}
indexed by
\bm{\Lambda}
is
compatible
if there exists a global state
\rho_{\rm S}
such that each
\sigma_{\rm X}
is recovered by performing the respective partial trace on
\rho_{\rm S}
:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus X}(\rho_{\rm S})=\sigma_{\rm X}\quad\;\forall\;{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda};

(2)

the corresponding
\rho_{\rm S}
is then said to be compatible with
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
. When there is no such
\rho_{\rm S}
,
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is said to be
incompatible
.
For the above setting, the corresponding
marginal problem
consists in answering the following question:

Is
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
compatible?

As mentioned in
Section

1
, this type of problems are relevant to different aspects of quantum information science, many-body physics, and quantum chemistry.
An important motivation for considering such problems lies on understanding when the parts indeed determine the whole, see, e.g.,
[
72
,
73
,
74
]
.

One can also consider marginal problems for probability distributions (which can be viewed as classical marginal problems). Importantly, there are fundamental differences between classical marginal problems and the marginal problems for quantum states.
Consider, for example, the tripartite setting ABC.
In this setting, if two bipartite probability distributions
\{P_{\rm AB}(ab)\},\{P_{\rm BC}(bc)\}
are
locally compatible
, in the sense that
\sum_{a}P_{\rm AB}(ab)=P_{\rm B}(b)=\sum_{c}P_{\rm BC}(bc)
, then they
must
be compatible, since (see, e.g., Ref.
[
75
]
)

\displaystyle P_{\rm ABC}(abc)\coloneqq\frac{P_{\rm AB}(ab)P_{\rm BC}(bc)}{P_{\rm B}(b)},

(3)

is a valid probability distribution satisfying

\sum_{a}P_{\rm ABC}(abc)=P_{\rm BC}(bc)

and

\sum_{c}P_{\rm ABC}(abc)=P_{\rm AB}(ab)
.
Hence, in this setting, a classical marginal problem is always trivial since local compatibility implies global compatibility.
Nevertheless, in the quantum case, this simplest setting is already
non-trivial
—one can consider maximally entangled states in AB and BC, which obviously have the same marginal state in B.
However, since entanglement is monogamous, it is impossible to find a tripartite state to realize these reduced states at the same time.
This demonstrates the gap between classical and quantum marginal problems, which further shows that the involvement of quantum resources can considerably change the structure of marginal problems.

2.3
Conic Programming

We now briefly review
conic programming
.
Rather than being mathematically general, we focus on a specific form relevant to this work’s physical setting (similar to the one used in, e.g., Ref.
[
43
]
).

To start with, consider a vector space
\mathcal{H}
describing matrices with a given finite dimension.
For instance, it can be the space of all
2\times 2
matrices, which includes all qubit density matrices.
In this vector space, a
proper cone
denoted by
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty, convex and closed subset of
\mathcal{H}
such that

•

\alpha x\in\mathcal{C}
for every
\alpha\geq 0
if
x\in\mathcal{C}
.

•

x\in\mathcal{C}
and
-x\in\mathcal{C}
imply that
x
is the null vector in
\mathcal{H}
.

Roughly speaking, the first condition means that
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of “rays” emitted from the origin, which can be visualized as a cone.
On the other hand, the second condition means that this cone has a preferred direction, since it cannot contain a line passing through the origin.
With a given proper cone
\mathcal{C}
, we can define a partial order—we can
compare

x,y
via
\mathcal{C}
by defining
x\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}y
whenever
y-x\in\mathcal{C}
.
In this work,
x\preceq y
means that
y-x
is a positive semi-definite matrix, which can be viewed as the partial order defined by the positive semi-definite cone.

Now, consider two vector spaces
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of matrices with possibly different finite dimensions.
Suppose that they are equipped with some inner products, which we write as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for simplicity.
In this work, we consider the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product or some other forms induced by it.
Then, following Refs.
[
43
,
77
,
39
,
78
,
76
]
, the
primal problem
of a conic program can be written as
[
78
]

\begin{split}\max_{x}\quad&\langle A,x\rangle\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&x\in\mathcal{C};\;\mathfrak{L}(x)\preceq B,\end{split}

(4)

where
\mathfrak{L}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is a linear map, and
A\in\mathcal{H},B\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are some constants.
The (Lagrange)
dual problem
of Eq. (
4
) may then be written as
[
78
]
:

\begin{split}\min_{z}\quad&\langle B,z\rangle\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&z\succeq 0;\;\left\langle\mathfrak{L}^{\dagger}(z)-A,x\right\rangle\geq 0\;\forall\;x\in\mathcal{C},\end{split}

(5)

where
\mathfrak{L}^{\dagger}
is the map dual to
\mathfrak{L}
.
By construction, the optimum of
Eq.

5
always upper bounds that of
Eq.

4
.
When these optimum values coincide, one says that
strong duality
holds
[
76
]
.
This happens, for example, when the primal problem is convex and Slater’s conditions hold.
In our setting, it is equivalent to checking whether there exists a point
x
in the relative interior of
\mathcal{C}
such that
B-\mathfrak{L}(x)
is strictly positive (see Refs.
[
43
,
78
]
, Appendices
A
, and
B
for further details).

3.1
Framework: Resource-Free Compatibility

Importantly, as will become evident from our framework, the problem of whether a given physical system
{\rm T}
is a resource of type
R
can also be phrased as a compatibility problem.
Our interest is to provide a unified framework for addressing both types of compatibility problems at the same time.
Henceforth, we adopt the shorthand
\rho_{\rm X}\coloneqq{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus X}(\rho_{\rm S})
and denote by
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
the set of all free states with respect to the resource
R
in the subsystem
{\rm T}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}=\mathcal{F}_{R}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\rm T}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{\rm T}
is the set of all states on
{\rm T}
.
A central notion capturing both compatibilities is then given as follows.
For any given subsystem
{\rm T}
of
{\rm S}
and resource of type
R
, the collection of density matrices
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is said to be
R
-free compatible in
{\rm T}
(or simply
R
-free compatible
when there is no risk of confusion) if

\displaystyle\exists\;\rho_{\rm S}\;\;{\rm compatible\;with}\;\;\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\;{\rm s.t.}\;\rho_{\rm T}\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}.

(6)

Namely, if
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is
R
-free compatible, one can find a global state
\rho_{\rm S}
such that all density matrices in
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
are its marginals and, in particular, its marginal in
{\rm T}
is
free
.
In other words, density matrices in
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
are not only compatible among themselves, but also with a target system T that is
not
a quantum resource of type
R
.

Conversely,
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is called
R
-free incompatible in
{\rm T}
(or simply
R
-free incompatible
) if it does not satisfy the above condition, namely, either
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is incompatible or

\displaystyle\forall\;\rho_{\rm S}\;\;{\rm compatible\;with}\;\;\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\;\Rightarrow\;\rho_{\rm T}\notin\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}.

(7)

When
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is compatible
and

R
-free incompatible at the same time, one can use these density matrices to
certify
the resourceful nature of the target system—since
every
global state having
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
as its marginals
must
possess the quantum resource of type
R
in T.
This is the notion of resource that we aim to study in this work.

Our central question is then defined as follows:
For any given triplet (
R
,
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
,
{\rm T}
), the corresponding
resource marginal problem
consists in answering the following question:

Is
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}

R
-free compatible in
{\rm T}
?

To answer this question, consider now, for the given triplet (
R
,
\bm{\Lambda}
,
{\rm T}
), the set of density matrices indexed by
\bm{\Lambda}
that are
R
-free compatible in
{\rm T}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}\coloneqq\{\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\,|\,\exists\,\rho_{\rm S}\;{\rm compatible\;with}\;\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\;{\rm s.t.}\;\rho_{\rm T}\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}\}.

(8)

Clearly, the set
\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
of
all
compatible marginal density matrices associated with subsystems specified by
\bm{\Lambda}
is a superset of
\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
.
Then, the complement of
\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
in
\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\setminus\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, is simply the set of compatible
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
that must necessarily result in a resourceful marginal in
{\rm T}
.

Among others, resource marginal problems contain as special cases the usual marginal problem for quantum states and the problem of deciding whether a given system is a resourceful state.
Of course, our unified framework allows us to obtain a general, quantitative analysis beyond these special cases, see
Fig.

1
.
Note further that assuming
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
may be very natural, e.g., in certifying the resource nature of
{\rm T}
.
However, this assumption does not have to be imposed
a priori
since the characterization of
\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
represents a specific instance of our general problem. Indeed, checking the membership of
\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\setminus\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is equivalent to determining the membership of both
\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
and
\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
. Hence, we shall stick to the most general setting given by the definition of
R
-free compatibility in subsequent discussions.

3.2
R
-Free Incompatibility as a Resource

Inspired by the approach of Refs.
[
43
,
77
]
, we now introduce the
R
-free incompatibility robustness

\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})

to quantify resource-free incompatibility. Formally, for every set of states
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})\coloneqq\log_{2}\inf\left\{\lambda\geq 0\,|\,\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\preceq\lambda\bm{\kappa}_{\bm{\Lambda}},\bm{\kappa}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}\right\},

(9)

where the inequality
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\preceq\lambda\bm{\kappa}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
means
\sigma_{\rm X}\preceq\lambda\kappa_{\rm X}
for every
{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}
.
To understand the meaning of this figure-of-merit, note that it can be
equivalently
defined as the minimization over a real number
\mu\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle 2^{-\mu}\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}+\left(1-2^{-\mu}\right)\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}},

(10)

where
\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
is any collection of density matrices, playing the role of
noise
in the above convex mixture.
In this form, one can see that
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}
measures “how robust” the
R
-free incompatibility of the given collection
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is against noise described by the term
\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
.
Importantly, there is no restriction on the noise
\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
here, and for this reason this type of robustness is sometimes called
generalized
robustness.

Evidently,
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})\geq 0
, and equality holds if
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\penalty\ \in\penalty\ \mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
.
This means that
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}>0
certifies
R
-free incompatibility.
When the set
\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is closed (in a suitably chosen topology),
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})=0

only if

\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
; in this case, the robustness measure becomes
faithful
.
Now, for a given set of operators
{\bf O}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\coloneqq\{O_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, we define
\langle\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},{\bf O}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}}{\rm tr}(\sigma_{\rm X}O_{\rm X})
.
Following the methodologies of Refs.
[
43
,
77
,
39
]
, some general results can be obtained by imposing the following assumptions on
R
,
{\rm T}
, and
\bm{\Lambda}
:

Assumptions A
.

(Working Hypothesis)

(A1)

\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
is convex and compact.

(A2)

There exists
\rho_{\rm S}
such that
\rho_{\rm T}\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
and
\rho_{\rm X}
is full-rank for every
{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}
.

Specifically, we prove the following in
Section

D.1
.

Theorem 1
.

(
R
-Free Incompatibility Witness)

Let
R
,
T
, and
\bm{\Lambda}
satisfy Assumptions
A
, then
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\notin\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
if and only if there exists
{\bf W}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\coloneqq\{W_{\rm X}\succeq 0\}_{{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}\langle\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}},{\bf W}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\rangle<\langle\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},{\bf W}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\rangle.

(11)

Theorem
1
can be understood as the witness of
R
-free incompatibility, i.e., the
R
-free incompatibility of any given
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
can always be detected by a separating hyperplane formed by finitely many positive semi-definite
W_{\rm X}
.
It is also worth mentioning that Theorem
1
answers the question
(Q1)
.
As we shall demonstrate in the later section, questions
(Q2)
and
(Q3)
can also be answered as desired for
R
-free incompatibility.

3.3
Robustness Measure as a Conic Program and the Minimal Assumptions for Its Regularity

In Appendix
C
we show that
2^{\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})}
is the solution of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{V}\quad&{\rm tr}(V)\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&V\in\mathbfcal{C}_{R|{\rm T}};\;\sigma_{\rm X}\preceq{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus X}(V)\quad\forall\;{\rm X\in}{\bm{\Lambda}},\end{aligned}

(12a)

where

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{C}_{R|{\rm T}}\coloneqq\{\alpha\eta_{\rm S}\,|\,\alpha\geq 0,\eta_{\rm S}:{\rm state\;s.t.}\;{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus T}(\eta_{\rm S})\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}\}.

(12b)

Thus, as long as
\mathbfcal{C}_{R|{\rm T}}
is a cone, we may cast the computation of
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}
as a conic program, cf.
Eq.

4
.
However, even then, strong duality may not be guaranteed to hold, or
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}
could be unbounded. Next, we present the
minimal
assumptions required to meet these desirable features.

In Appendix
D.2
we prove the following result:

Proposition 2
.

(Minimal Assumptions)

Given
R
,
{\rm T}
, and
\bm{\Lambda}
, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

Assumptions
A
hold.

2.

(i)
\mathbfcal{C}_{R|{\rm T}}
is a proper cone. (ii) For every
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, Eq. (
12
) is a finite, feasible conic program with strong duality.

Note that Statement
2
guarantees the feasibility of
Eq.

12
, and hence
Eq.

9
as an optimization problem. Moreover, Proposition
2
illustrates that Assumptions
A
are both necessary and sufficient for Eq. (
12
) [and hence
Eq.

9
] to be a finite conic program with strong duality.
In particular, Assumptions
A
are shared by several common state resource theories (see discussions below), thus making evident the generality of our approach.

Let us now comment on the significance of these assumptions.
The convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
in Assumption
(A1)
implies that probabilistic mixtures of free states are again free.
The compactness of
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
, on the other hand, means that when a state
\rho
can be approximated by free states with arbitrary precision, then
\rho
is also free.
These features are shared by many resources, such as entanglement, coherence, athermality, asymmetry, steerability, and nonlocality (see Appendix
A.1
).

However, it is important to remark that not every state resource theory satisfies Assumption
(A1)
.
First, convexity implicitly allows shared randomness for free.
This is no longer true, for instance, when
\mathcal{F}_{R}
is the set of multipartite states that are separable with respect to
some
bipartition.
Since a non-trivial convex combination of two states separable in different bipartitions will generally
not
result in a state separable with respect to
any
bipartition,
\mathcal{F}_{R}
, and hence
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
can be non-convex.
Likewise, if one identifies all pure states as the resource, then
\mathcal{F}_{R}
will be the set of all non-pure states, which is not closed.

Assumption
(A2)
implies that there exists a free state
\eta_{\rm T}
in
{\rm T}
that may be extended to
{\rm S}
as
\rho_{\rm S}
such that all the corresponding
\rho_{\rm X}
are full-rank.
By considering an extension of the kind
\rho_{\rm S}=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{S\setminus T}}{d_{S\setminus T}}\otimes\eta_{\rm T}
, we see that Assumption
(A2)
holds whenever the following sufficient condition holds:

(A2enumi)

There exists a full-rank
\eta_{\rm T}\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
.

This is, however, not necessary, and a counterexample is given in Appendix
D.3
.
From here, we learn that Assumption
(A2)
is satisfied when a maximally mixed state is a free state.
Hence, all but athermality of the above-mentioned resources fulfill this assumption.
In the case of athermality, when the given thermal state is full-rank, then this property is again satisfied.
This also provides an example where Assumption
(A2)
becomes invalid, e.g., when the given thermal state is a
product
pure state, which can be understood as the zero temperature limit without entanglement.
We also learn that:

Corollary 3
.

If Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2enumi)
hold for a given state resource
R
, then
\mathbfcal{C}_{R|{\rm T}}
is a proper cone.
Also, for every
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, Eq. (
12
) is a finite conic program with strong duality.

3.4
Operational Interpretation of
R
-Free Incompatibility

Interestingly,
Theorem

1
can be used to give an operational interpretation showing how
R
-free incompatibility leads to an advantage in
channel discrimination tasks
analogous to those discussed in Refs.
[
40
,
39
]
.
For any given
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, let
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{I},\bm{\Lambda}}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{E}_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i,{\rm X}}
be the set of channels to be distinguished, then the task consists in the following steps:

1.

With probability
p_{\rm X}
, the agent at
{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}
is chosen.

2.

With probability
p_{i|{\rm X}}
, the channel
\mathcal{E}_{i|{\rm X}}\in\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{I},\bm{\Lambda}}
is chosen to be implemented at
{\rm X}
.

3.

The agent at
{\rm X}
inputs the quantum state
\sigma_{\rm X}
through the channel and applies the measurement associated with the
positive operator-valued measures
(POVMs)
[
79
]

\{E_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i}
(
\sum_{i}E_{i|{\rm X}}=\mathbb{I}_{\rm X}
and
E_{i|{\rm X}}\succeq 0
) to the channel’s output.

4.

If the measurement outcome is
j
, the agent guesses
\mathcal{E}_{j|{\rm X}}
as the channel implemented.

See also Fig.
2
for a schematic illustration of this task.
Together,
D\coloneqq\left(\{p_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X},\{p_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i,{\rm X}},\{E_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i,{\rm X}}\right)
and
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{I},\bm{\Lambda}}
define the discrimination task.
For any chosen input states
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}=\{\sigma_{\rm X}\}_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, the probability of successfully distinguishing members of
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{I},\bm{\Lambda}}
in this task, when averaged over multiple rounds, is evidently

\displaystyle P_{D}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{I},\bm{\Lambda}})\coloneqq\sum_{{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}}\sum_{i}p_{\rm X}p_{i|{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[E_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{E}_{i|{\rm X}}(\sigma_{\rm X})\right].

(13)

Hereafter, we focus on tasks
D
that are
strictly positive
, i.e.,
p_{\rm X},p_{i|{\rm X}}>0,E_{i|{\rm X}}\succ 0\;\forall\;i\;\&\;{\rm X}
and where the ensemble of channels is unitary, i.e.,
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{I},\bm{\Lambda}}=\mathbfcal{U}:=\{\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i,{\rm X}}
and each
\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}
is unitary.
Then in Appendix
D.4
we show the following:

Theorem 4
.

(Advantage in Channel Discrimination)

If
R
,
T
, and
\bm{\Lambda}
satisfy Assumptions
A
, then
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\notin\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
if and only if for every unitary
\mathbfcal{U}=\{\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i=1;{\rm X\in\bm{\Lambda}}}^{d_{\rm X}+1}
there exists a strictly positive channel-discrimination task
D
such that

\sup_{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}P_{D}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}},\mathbfcal{U})<P_{D}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{U}).

Hence,
R
-free incompatibility implies an advantage in distinguishing reversible channels. A few remarks are now in order. Firstly, as the discrimination task is derived from the witness
{\bf W}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
of Theorem
1
, it is specific to the
given

\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
. In general, we should think of the agents somehow having access to
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
and, upon chosen, use the corresponding
\sigma_{\rm X}
to perform the discrimination task. If
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is compatible, then these
\sigma_{\rm X}
’s are simply the reduced states of some global state
\rho_{S}
. The resourceful nature of
\rho_{\rm T}
then guarantees an advantage in the aforementioned discrimination task. Note also that an operational advantage of a wide range of resources in the form of a discrimination task has been discussed, e.g., in Refs.
[
39
,
43
,
77
]
.
Here, we show that this advantage extends to
R
-free incompatibility and can be manifested by considering a discrimination task that is strictly positive and that involves
only
unitaries.

In fact,
Theorem

4
holds even when one considers the unusual situation where
\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}=\mathcal{U}_{{\rm X}}
, i.e., where these channels to be “distinguished” are the same for all
i
. Evidently, in this case,
D
and
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{I},\bm{\Lambda}}=\{\mathbfcal{U}_{\rm X}\}
do not carry the meaning of a channel discrimination task. Still, one can see it as a kind of
communication game
where the player, knowing
D
, tries to maximize the score given in
Eq.

13
by sending in the best possible, possibly
R
-free incompatible
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
. The relevance of such a game will become clear when we discuss the convertibility among different
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
’s in
Section

3.6
.

Figure 2:

Schematic illustration of the channel discrimination task.

With probability
p_{\rm X}
, the subsystem
{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}
is selected, and one needs to distinguish channels
\{\mathcal{E}_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i}
implemented with the probability
\{p_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i}
.
To do so, the local agent prepares the local state
\sigma_{\rm X}
as the input of the unknown selected channel, and uses the POVM
\{E_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i}
to measure channel’s output.
The agent then guesses the channel is
\mathcal{E}_{j|{\rm X}}
if the measurement outcome is
j
.

3.5
Completing the Resource Theory of
R
-Free Incompatibility

The above observation suggests that
R
-free incompatibility
itself
is a resource since it provides advantages in a nontrivial discrimination task that is otherwise absent.
Accordingly, the free quantities are simply members of
\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
. To define the free operations, let us first recall the notion of channel compatibility recently introduced in Ref.
[
75
]
.
Throughout, we use
\mathcal{E}_{\rm A\to B}
to represent a channel mapping from systems
{\rm A}
to
{\rm B}
.
If the input and output Hilbert space are the same, say,
{\rm X}
, we simply write the map as
\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}
.
Then, a global channel
\mathcal{E}_{\rm S^{\prime}\to S}
mapping from
{\rm S^{\prime}}
to
{\rm S}
is said to have a
well-defined marginal
in the input-output pair
{\rm X^{\prime}\to X}
(with
{\rm X^{\prime}\subseteq S^{\prime}}
and
{\rm X\subseteq S}
) if there exists a
marginal channel
, denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{\rm X^{\prime}\to X}
, from
{\rm X^{\prime}}
to
{\rm X}
such that the following commutation relation holds
[
75
]
:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus X}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\rm S^{\prime}\to S}=\mathcal{E}_{\rm X^{\prime}\to X}\circ{\rm tr}_{\rm S^{\prime}\setminus X^{\prime}}.

(14)

Once such a marginal exists, it is provably unique
[
75
]
, and we use

{\rm Tr}_{\rm S^{\prime}\setminus X^{\prime}\to S\setminus X}\mathcal{E}_{\rm S^{\prime}\to S}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{\rm X^{\prime}\to X}

to denote the marginal channel of
\mathcal{E}_{\rm S^{\prime}\to S}
from
{\rm X^{\prime}\to X}
. Notice that, apart from the capitalization in
{\rm Tr}(\cdot)
, the notation for this marginalization operation also differs from the usual partial trace operation
{\rm tr}(\cdot)
in that the subscripts associated with
{\rm Tr}
always take the form of “
\cdot\to\cdot
”.
For
\mathcal{E}_{\rm S^{\prime}\to S}
, the existence of
{\rm Tr}_{\rm S^{\prime}\setminus X^{\prime}\to S\setminus X}\mathcal{E}_{\rm S^{\prime}\to S}
is equivalent to being semi-causal in
{\rm X}

[
80
,
81
,
82
]
, semi-localizable in
{\rm X}

[
82
]
, and no-signaling from
{\rm S^{\prime}\setminus X^{\prime}}
to
{\rm X}

[
83
]
.

In these terms, we say that a global channel
\mathcal{E}_{{\rm S}}
on
{\rm S}
is
compatible
with a set of channels
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}=\{\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}\}_{{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}}
acting on subsystem(s)
{\rm X}
if
{\rm Tr}_{\rm S\setminus X\to S\setminus X}\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}=\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}\;\forall\;{{\rm X\in}\bm{\Lambda}}
.
We are now ready to define the free operation of the resource theory associated with
R
-free incompatibility as:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}\coloneqq\{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\;|\;\exists\;\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}\;{\rm compatible\;with}\;\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\;{\rm s.t.}\;{\rm Tr}_{\rm S\setminus T\to S\setminus T}\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}\in\mathcal{O}_{R}\},

(15)

where
\mathcal{O}_{R}
denotes the set of free operations of the given state resource
R
.
Indeed, the legitimacy of this choice follows directly (see
Section

D.5
for details) from the definition of
\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
given in
Eq.

15
and that of
\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
given in
Eq.

8
:

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}})\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}\;\forall\;\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}\text{ and }\forall\;\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}.

(16)

Moreover, as we show in
Section

D.6
,
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}
is a monotone with respect to this choice of free operations.

Theorem 5
.

(
R
-Free Incompatibility Monotone)

If
R
,
T
, and
\bm{\Lambda}
satisfy Assumptions
A
, then

1.

\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}})=0
if and only if
\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
.

2.

\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}\left[\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})\right]\leq\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})

\forall\;\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},\forall\;\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
.

Hence, not only can
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}
quantify
R
-free incompatibility, but it is also a monotone for the judicious choice of free operations given in
Eq.

15
.
Together with Theorem
5
, we thus complete specifying the resource theory associated with
R
-free incompatibility.
Note that Theorem
5
also helps us to answer question
(Q3)
for
R
-free incompatibility.

3.6
Convertibility Conditions

As mentioned in
Section

2.1
, a key question in the study of resource theory is to characterize the convertibility of resources under free operations [question
(Q2)
].
It turns out that we can achieve this by using a specific kind of communication game, c.f.,
Section

3.4
.
For a given resource
R
and a game specified by
D\coloneqq\left(\{p_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X},\{p_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i,{\rm X}},\{E_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i,{\rm X}}\right)
, we consider the following figure-of-merit:

\displaystyle{\rm Score}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},D)\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}}P_{D}\left(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\right),

(17)

where
P_{D}\left(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\right)
has been defined in Eq. (
13
), and
\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
is the set of free operations associated with the resource theory of
R
-free incompatibility, as defined in Eq. (
15
).
In other words, “
{\rm Score}
” is the highest “success probability” obtained by optimizing over all channels coming from the free operations of
R
-free incompatibility.
Note that
{\rm Score}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},D)
is also dependent on
\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
but we keep this dependency implicit.

Unlike in
Section

3.4
, we now allow subnormalized probabilities such that
\sum_{\rm X}p_{\rm X}\leq 1
and
\sum_{i}p_{i|{\rm X}}\leq 1
.
We call such tasks
D

non-deterministic
communication games.
Then we show in
Section

D.7
that non-deterministic communication games can fully characterize the convertibility of
R
-free incompatibility:

Theorem 6
.

(Convertibility of
R
-Free Incompatibility)

Suppose
\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
has the following three properties:

•

It is convex and compact.

•

\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\circ\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
if
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime},\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
.

•

Identity is a free operation.

Then the following two statements are equivalent:

1.

There exists
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
such that
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}})=\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
.

2.

{\rm Score}({\bm{\tau}}_{\bm{\Lambda}},D)\geq{\rm Score}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},D)
for every non-deterministic communication game
D
.

Theorem
6
provides a complete characterization of the partial ordering in the resource theory of
R
-free incompatibility; that is, being able to convert an
R
-free incompatible
\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
to
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is equivalent to the former achieving
{\rm Score}
at least as high as the latter in
all
non-deterministic communication games.

As a summary, we have provided a general and thorough analysis of the resource-theoretic aspects of
R
-free incompatibility.
Especially, we have fully addressed the three important questions mentioned at the beginning: questions
(Q1)
,
(Q2)
, and
(Q3)
are addressed by Theorem
1
, Theorem
5
, and Theorem
6
, respectively.

4
Applications and Implications

4.1
Characterizing Resource Marginal Problems by Many-Body Physics

As mentioned previously, our general framework does not require
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
a priori.
To this end, we say that a resource marginal problem has
non-trivial
solutions if
\mathfrak{C}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\setminus\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is
non-empty
.
In other words, a non-trivial solution involves a
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
that is compatible and can demonstrate
R
-free incompatibility.
Clearly, for any given
R
,
{\rm T}
, and
{\bm{\Lambda}}
, understanding when non-trivial solutions exist is both physically relevant and important.
Thanks to Theorem
1
, we can achieve this by analyzing the ground state behavior in a many-body system.

We argue as follows.
Let
{\bm{\Lambda}},{\rm T}
be given, and suppose
R
satisfies Assumptions
A
so that Theorem
1
is applicable.
Let us further suppose that there exists non-trivial examples of
R
-free incompatibility.
From Theorem
1
, we can find operators
\{W_{\rm X}\}_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}{\rm tr}(\sigma_{\rm X}W_{\rm X})<\inf_{\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}{\rm tr}(\tau_{\rm X}W_{\rm X}).

(18)

Consider now the (dimensionless) Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\rm total}\coloneqq\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}W_{\rm X}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{{\rm S\setminus X}}

(19)

and let
\eta_{\rm S}
be a ground state of this Hamiltonian.
Then we
must
have

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\eta_{\rm S}H_{\rm total}\right)\leq{\rm tr}\left(\rho_{\rm S}H_{\rm total}\right)<\inf_{\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}{\rm tr}(\tau_{\rm X}W_{\rm X}),

(20)

where
\rho_{\rm S}
is any state compatible with
\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
(and, importantly, there exists at least one such state since
\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
is a non-trivial example).
Eq. (
20
) implies that
all
ground states of
H_{\rm total}
must have their marginals demonstrating
R
-free incompatibility.
In other words,

every state from the ground energy subspace of
H_{\rm total}
must be
R
-resourceful in
T
.

Conversely, suppose that
all
ground states of a given Hamiltonian
H_{\rm total}=\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}H_{\rm X}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{{\rm S\setminus X}}
are
R
-resourceful in
T
.
Then, we want to know whether this implies any non-trivial example of
R
-free incompatibility for any given
R
, T, and
\bm{\Lambda}
.
Let us pick a ground state
\eta_{\rm S}
and compute its marginal states
\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}=\{\sigma_{\rm X}\coloneqq\eta_{\rm X}\}_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}
.
Then this set is, by definition, compatible.
Moreover, for every
\rho_{\rm S}
compatible with
\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\rho_{\rm S}H_{\rm total}\right)=\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}{\rm tr}\left(\sigma_{\rm X}H_{\rm X}\right)={\rm tr}\left(\eta_{\rm S}H_{\rm total}\right)=\text{ground energy}.

(21)

Hence,
\rho_{\rm S}
is also a ground state, which, by assumption,
must
be
R
-resourceful in
T
.
Consequently,
\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}
is a non-trivial example of
R
-free incompatibility.
Therefore, if there exists a many-body Hamiltonian
H_{\rm total}=\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}H_{\rm X}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{{\rm S\setminus X}}
whose ground states are all
R
-resourceful in some subsystem
T
, then

there must exist at least one non-trivial example of
R
-free incompatibility.

What we have argued can be formally summarized as the following theorem:

Theorem 7
.

(Hamiltonian Interpretation)

Let
{\bm{\Lambda}}
and
{\rm T}
be the given set of subsystems and target system.
Suppose
R
,
T
, and
\bm{\Lambda}
satisfy Assumptions
A
.
Then the following two statements are equivalent:

•

There exists at least one non-trivial example of
R
-free incompatibility.

•

There exists a many-body Hamiltonian
H_{\rm total}=\sum_{{\rm X}\in{\bm{\Lambda}}}H_{\rm X}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{{\rm S\setminus X}}
such that all its ground states are

5
Discussion

Motivated by the importance and the generality of quantum states marginal problems as well as resource theories, we provide an overarching framework that includes not only these two important topics in quantum information theory, but also a variety of other unexplored possibilities.
The key notion underlying our framework is resource-free (
R
-free) incompatibility in a target subsystem
{\rm T}
, i.e., the
impossibility
of having a resource-free subsystem
{\rm T}
for some given set of marginal states
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
.
The question of whether this impossibility holds for any given
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
leads naturally to what we dub the
resource marginal problems
, which includes the quantum state marginal problems as a special case.

To quantify this incompatibility, we introduce a robustness measure
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})
and show that, provided two necessary and sufficient conditions are satisfied,
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})
can be evaluated as a finite-valued conic program where strong duality holds.
Whenever
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
is not
R
-free compatible, we demonstrate how a witness can be extracted from the conic program to manifest this fact. Moreover, a channel-discrimination task involving
arbitrary
unitary channels can be defined to illustrate the operational advantage of
R
-free incompatible
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
over those compatible ones in this task.
By identifying appropriate free operations, we further prove that a resource theory of
R
-free incompatibility can be formulated with the robustness measure
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})
serving as the corresponding monotone.
The corresponding
resource theory
for
R
is then recovered as a special case of our resource theory.
To complete the story, we provide a complete characterization of convertibility of the
R
-free incompatibility of marginals via a communication game.

Apart from recovering the known results, our framework makes evident the fact that an incompatibility problem can be defined for
any
resource theory for quantum states, and
vice versa
.
For example, a resource theory can be defined for the usual quantum states marginal problems, an incompatibility problem can be defined for the resource theory of entanglement, and so on. In particular, since a resource theory of entanglement-free incompatibility can be defined for the recently introduced entanglement marginal problems
[
6
]
, our robustness measure, etc., can be applied to this incompatibility.
More generally, if a resource theory or a marginal problem can be cast in a form that fits our framework, the results that we have derived are readily applicable.
Finally, as an unexpected finding, we report a correspondence between resource marginal problems and ground state properties of many-body Hamiltonians.
More precisely, we have found that the existence of non-trivial solutions to a resource marginal problem is equivalent to the existence of
a many-body Hamiltonian having specific ground state properties.
This gives resource marginal problems a necessary and sufficient description in a physical setting.

Let us conclude by naming some further possibilities for future work.
First, as is now well known, not only can resource theories be defined for quantum states, but also for quantum channels (see, e.g., Refs.
[
77
,

,

100
,
101
,
102
,
75
,
103
]
).
By following a treatment very similar to that of this work, we also establish the
dynamical
analogs of many of the results mentioned above.
In the dynamical regime, it would be interesting to see how our framework can be used to obtain further insights into related problems such as channel broadcasting, measurement incompatibility, causal structures, channel extendibility, etc.
We refer the reader to the follow-up paper Ref.
[
104
]
(see also Ref.
[

Also, with our framework, existing results from various works, including those in

But given the versatility of resource marginal problems, it should be clear that there remain many other possibilities that are worth exploring beyond those explicitly discussed here.
For instance, it is rewarding to study whether

One may also wonder whether it is possible to have some
R

\sigma_{\rm X}

R

\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
.
Specifically, could it be that by verifying that all
\sigma_{\rm X}\in\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}

R

\sigma_{\rm T}

Note that such conditions are

For instance, with

Lemma A.7
.

For every finite-dimensional system
{\rm S}
,
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm S}
is compact if
R\in\{
athermality, entanglement, coherence, asymmetry, nonlocality, and steerability
\}
.

First, it is straightforward to see the compactness for athermality, since, in this resource theory, there is only one free state, which is the thermal state (for some given Hamiltonian and temperature).
On the other hand, the compactness of the set of separable states in finite dimensions is a well-known fact
[
9
]

which explains the validity of Assumption
(A1)
for entanglement.
Now, we note the following observation:

Fact A.8
.

For every finite-dimensional
{\rm S}
,
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm S}
is compact if a continuous resource-destroying map of
R
exists.

To be precise, a
resource destroying map

[
35
]

\mathcal{L}
of the given state resource
R
in a system
{\rm S}
is a (not necessarily linear) map such that
\mathcal{L}(\eta)=\eta\;\forall\;\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm S}
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{S}_{\rm S})=\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm S}
.
Thus, if
\mathcal{L}
is continuous, then
\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm S}
is compact since a continuous function maps a compact set to a compact set (see, e.g., Refs.
[
114
,
115
]
).
One can check that this is indeed the case for coherence and asymmetry: For the former, one can use the dephasing channel
(\cdot)\mapsto\sum_{i}|i\rangle\!\langle i|\cdot|i\rangle\!\langle i|
, and for the latter one can use
G
-twirling operation, where
G
is the group defining the symmetry.
Hence, Assumption
(A1)
is indeed satisfied by coherence and asymmetry.

It remains to address Bell-nonlocality
[
31
]
and steerability, and we focus on the former since the structure of the proof is the same.
We shall use a bipartite system
{\rm AB}
to illustrate the idea.
Let us first recapitulate the notion of a Bell inequality. In a bipartite system
{\rm AB}
, a probability distribution
{\bf P}=\{P(ab|xy)\}
is said to be describable by a
local-hidden-variable
(LHV) model, denoted by
{\bf P}\in{\rm LHV}
, if
P(ab|xy)=\sum_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}P(a|x,\lambda)P(b|y,\lambda)

for some probability distributions
\{p_{\lambda}\},\{P(a|x,\lambda)\},\{P(b|y,\lambda)\}
.
A linear
Bell inequality
in a constraint satisfied by all
{\bf P}\in{\rm LHV}
and may be characterized by a vector
{\bf B}=\{B_{ab|xy}\}
and a real number
\omega({\bf B})
such that

\displaystyle\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{a,b,x,y}B_{ab|xy}P(ab|xy)\leq\omega({\bf B}),

(30)

where each
B_{ab|xy}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\omega({\bf B})\coloneqq\sup_{{\bf P}\in{\rm LHV}}\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}\rangle

is the largest value of
\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}\rangle
achievable by members in
{\rm LHV}
.
A violation of such an inequality certifies the non-classical nature of the given probability distribution
{\bf P}
, and it is an intriguing fact that this can be attained by certain
{\bf P}
given by quantum theory.

Formally,
{\bf P}
is called
quantum
if there exists a state
\rho_{\rm AB}
and a set of local POVMs
{\bf E}_{\rm AB}\coloneqq\{E^{a|x}_{\rm A},E^{b|y}_{\rm B}\}
(i.e.,
\sum_{a}E^{a|x}_{\rm A}=\mathbb{I}_{\rm A}\;\forall\;x
and
\sum_{b}E^{b|y}_{\rm B}=\mathbb{I}_{\rm B}\;\forall\;y
) such that

\displaystyle P(ab|xy)={\rm tr}\left[\left(E^{a|x}_{\rm A}\otimes E^{b|y}_{\rm B}\right)\rho_{\rm AB}\right]\quad\forall\;a,b,x,y.

(31)

We write
{\bf P}_{\rho_{\rm AB}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\coloneqq\left\{{\rm tr}\left[\left(E^{a|x}_{\rm A}\otimes E^{b|y}_{\rm B}\right)\rho_{\rm AB}\right]\right\}
to be the probability distribution induced by the state
\rho_{\rm AB}
and POVMs
{\bf E}_{\rm AB}
.
In these notations, one can define the set of states that cannot violate
any
Bell inequality as :

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm AB}\coloneqq\left\{\eta_{\rm AB}\;|\;\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}_{\eta_{\rm AB}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\rangle\leq\omega({\bf B})\;\forall\;{\bf B}\;\&\;{\bf E}_{\rm AB}\right\}.

(32)

We call them
local
states and states that are not local are called
nonlocal
.
Now we can show that:

Lemma A.9
.

\mathcal{L}_{\rm AB}
is compact.

Proof.

It suffices to show the closedness since it is a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{\rm AB}
(see Lemma
A.4
).
Let
\rho_{\rm AB}\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm AB}
and
\{\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\rm AB}
be a sequence of states such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\rho_{\rm AB}-\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}\right\|_{1}=0
.
For every Bell inequality specified by
{\bf B}
and local POVMs
{\bf E}_{\rm AB}
, we may define the (Hermitian) Bell operator
[
116
]
as
\mathcal{B}:=\sum_{a,b,x,y}B_{ab|xy}E_{\rm A}^{a|x}\otimes E_{\rm B}^{b|y}
, then

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}_{\rho_{\rm AB}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}_{\rho_{\rm AB}-\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}_{\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\rho_{\rm AB}-\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}\right)\right]+\left\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}_{\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{B}\right\|_{\infty}\times\left\|\rho_{\rm AB}-\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}\right\|_{1}+\omega({\bf B}),

(33)

where the second equality follows from the fact that the Bell value
\left\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}_{\rho_{\rm AB}-\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\right\rangle
can be written as the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product between the Bell operator
\mathcal{B}
and the difference in the density matrices
\rho_{\rm AB}-\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}
,
and the inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality [see, e.g., Eq. (6) in Ref.
[
117
]
and Section 1.1 in Ref.
[
118
]
)
and the assumption that
\eta_{\rm AB}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{L}_{\rm AB}
.
Since this is true for every
k
, we learn that in the limit of
k\to\infty
,
\langle{\bf B},{\bf P}_{\rho_{\rm AB}|{\bf E}_{\rm AB}}\rangle\leq\omega({\bf B})
, which shows that
\rho_{\rm AB}\in\mathcal{L}_{\rm AB}
.
∎

Hence, Assumption
(A1)
holds when the underlying state resource is nonlocality.
Note that here, the nonlocality is not specified to a particular Bell inequality.

Appendix B
Remarks on Strong Duality of Conic Program

As mentioned in the main text, the Slater’s condition is equivalent to checking whether there exists a point
x\in{\rm relint}\left(\mathcal{C}\right)
such that
\mathfrak{L}(x)\prec B
.
When the primal problem is convex, Slater’s condition guarantees the strong duality
[
76
]
.
However, there exist examples where the primal problem is convex, yet strong duality does not hold. For example, the primal may be infeasible, and the dual is unbounded.

Fact B.1
.

There exist examples where strong duality does not hold, even though the primal problem is convex.

Proof.

For instance, consider
\mathcal{C}=\{\alpha|0\rangle\!\langle 0|\,|\,\alpha\geq 0\}
in a qubit system.
Then the following conic program

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{V}\quad&{\rm tr}(V)\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&\frac{\mathbb{I}_{2}}{2}\preceq V;\;V\in\{\alpha|0\rangle\!\langle 0|\,|\,\alpha\geq 0\}\end{aligned}

(34)

is clearly convex but has no feasible
V
.
Indeed, regardless of the value of
\alpha
, the (matrix) inequality constraint can never be satisfied. Meanwhile, its dual reads

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \max_{Y}\quad&\frac{{\rm tr}(Y)}{2}\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&Y\succeq 0;\;\langle 0|Y|0\rangle\leq 1,\end{aligned}

(35)

which is infinite since
\langle 1|Y|1\rangle
can be arbitrarily large.
∎

-Free Incompatibility

C.1
Dual Problem of
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}

Recall that
{\rm S}
is always assumed to be finite-dimensional.
Then, we start with the following result, which explicitly explains our motivation for imposing Assumptions

Suppose Assumptions

\sigma_{\rm X}\prec{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus X}(V_{*})\;\forall\;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}.

From the form of the constraint given in

9

(A2)

for every
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}

This implies that
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})<\infty

\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}

So it suffices to prove Slater’s condition.
Suppose
\eta_{\rm S}\in\mathcal{S}_{{R|{\rm T}}}
satisfies that
\eta_{\rm X}
is full-rank
\forall\;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}

(A2)

Together with Assumption
(A1)

Hence, its relative interior

[

112

Let

From the definition Eq. (

\epsilon>0

\mathcal{B}(\tau_{\rm S};\epsilon)\cap{\rm aff}(\mathcal{S}_{{R|{\rm T}}})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{{R|{\rm T}}}.

Here, one can choose the open ball with the trace norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}

Footnote

3

Now, pick

\kappa_{\rm S}=(1-p)\tau_{\rm S}+p\eta_{\rm S}

Then, when

•

\kappa_{\rm X}

•

\left\|\kappa_{\rm S}-\tau_{\rm S}\right\|_{1}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\kappa_{\rm S}\in\mathcal{S}_{{R|{\rm T}}}

The last condition is due to the convexity of

Using the triangle inequality of

[

\mathcal{B}\left(\kappa_{\rm S};\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\tau_{\rm S};\epsilon)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left(\kappa_{\rm S};\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\cap{\rm aff}(\mathcal{S}_{{R|{\rm T}}})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{{R|{\rm T}}}.

(36)

From Eq. (

Being a full-rank state in a finite-dimensional system

p_{\rm min}(\kappa_{\rm X})>0\;\forall\;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}

By choosing

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm X}>\frac{1}{p_{\rm min}(\kappa_{\rm X})}\geq 1,

(38)

which is finite, we have

D.3
Remark on Assumption
(A2)

A question that follows from the above result is whether one can relax Assumption
(A2)
into Assumption
(A2enumi)
; namely, the existence of a full-rank
\eta_{\rm T}\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}
.
This is, however, not necessary for Statement

, as we will show using the following counterexample.
Consider a bipartite system
{\rm S=T=AB}
with equal local dimension
d<\infty
, and
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\{{\rm A,B}\}
with
\mathcal{F}_{R|{\rm AB}}=\{|\Psi_{\rm AB}^{+}\rangle\}
, where
|\Psi_{\rm AB}^{+}\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}|i\rangle_{\rm A}\otimes|i\rangle_{\rm B}
is a maximally entangled state.
This can be understood as the resource theory of athermality at zero temperature where the thermal state for some non-degenerate Hamiltonian is the entangled ground state
|\Psi_{\rm AB}^{+}\rangle
.
Now, since the single party marginal of
|\Psi_{\rm AB}^{+}\rangle
is maximally mixed, one can check that Assumption
(A2)
is satisfied.
On the other hand, it is clear that the only free state is not full-rank.

D.4
Proof of Theorem
4

Proof.

From Theorem
1
,
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\notin\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
if and only if there exist
\{W_{{\rm X}}\succeq 0\}_{{{\rm X}}\in\bm{\Lambda}}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}\sum_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}{\rm tr}(\tau_{\rm X}W_{\rm X})<\sum_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}{\rm tr}(\sigma_{\rm X}W_{\rm X}).

(47)

Without loss of generality, we may assume that for every
{{\rm X}}\in\bm{\Lambda}
,
W_{\rm X}
is strictly positive (
W_{\rm X}\succ 0
), i.e., having only positive eigenvalues.
This is because we can add
\Delta=\Delta{\rm tr}(\tau_{\rm X})=\Delta{\rm tr}(\sigma_{\rm X})
on both sides and still preserve the strict inequality, where
\Delta>0
is a positive number.
Now, for each
{\rm X}
, the spectral decomposition of
W_{{\rm X}}\succ 0
can be written as
W_{\rm X}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}}\omega_{i|{\rm X}}|\psi_{i|{\rm X}}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i|{\rm X}}|
, where
d_{\rm X}
is the dimension of the system
{\rm X}
and
\omega_{i|{\rm X}}>0\;\forall\;i,{\rm X}
.
For any set of unitary channels in
{\rm X}
given by
\mathbfcal{U}=\{\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i=1;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}^{d_{\rm X}}
, with
\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(\cdot)\coloneqq U_{i|{\rm X}}(\cdot)U_{i|{\rm X}}^{\dagger}
for a given unitary operator
U_{i|{\rm X}}
, we can write

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\sigma_{\rm X}W_{\rm X}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}}\omega_{i|{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(\sigma_{\rm X})\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(|\psi_{i|{\rm X}}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i|{\rm X}}|)\right]=\frac{1}{d_{\rm X}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[M_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(\sigma_{\rm X})\right],

(48)

where

M_{i|{\rm X}}\coloneqq d_{\rm X}\omega_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(|\psi_{i|{\rm X}}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i|{\rm X}}|),

which is again a non-zero positive semi-definite operator.
From here we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}\sum_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{d_{\rm X}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[M_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(\tau_{\rm X})\right]<\sum_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{d_{\rm X}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[M_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(\sigma_{\rm X})\right].

(49)

Note that the inequality remains valid if we perform the mapping
M_{i|{\rm X}}\to\alpha(M_{i|{\rm X}}+\Delta_{0}\mathbb{I})
for
\alpha,\Delta_{0}>0
. In particular, for judiciously chosen positive
\alpha<1
, we then have

\displaystyle M_{i|{\rm X}}\succ 0\quad\forall\;i,{\rm X}\quad\&\quad\sum_{i}M_{i|{\rm X}}\prec\mathbb{I}_{\rm X}\quad\forall\;{\rm X},

(50)

thus allowing one to interpret
\{M_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i}
, for each
{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}
, as an incomplete POVM.
For any set of states
{\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}=\{\kappa_{\rm X}\}_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}
we define the probability of success in the task
D_{-}
as:

\displaystyle P_{D_{-}}({\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{U})\coloneqq\frac{1}{|{\bm{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{d_{\rm X}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[M_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(\kappa_{{\rm X}})\right],

(51)

which can be understood as the success probability of using
{\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}
to discriminate
\mathbfcal{U}
in the “non-deterministic” task
D_{-}\coloneqq\left(\{p_{\rm X}=\frac{1}{|{\bm{\Lambda}}|}\},\{p_{i|{\rm X}}=\frac{1}{d_{\rm X}}\},\{M_{i|{\rm X}}\}\right)
.
With this notation, Eq. (
49
) reads

\sup_{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}P_{D_{-}}(\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}},\mathbfcal{U})<P_{D_{-}}(\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}},\mathbfcal{U}).

This means that there exists a finite value
\Delta_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle P_{D_{-}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{U})=\Delta_{1}+\sup_{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}P_{D_{-}}(\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}},\mathbfcal{U}).

(52)

Now consider the task
D=\left(\{p_{\rm X}\},\{p_{i|{\rm X}}\},\{E_{i|{\rm X}}\}\right)
with
\mathbfcal{E}=\{\mathcal{E}_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i=1;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}^{d_{\rm X}+1}
defined as:

\displaystyle p_{\rm X}=\frac{1}{|{\bm{\Lambda}}|};

(53a)

\displaystyle p_{i|{\rm X}}=\frac{1-\epsilon}{d_{\rm X}}\quad{\rm if}\;i\leq d_{\rm X}\quad\&\quad p_{d_{\rm X}+1|{\rm X}}=\epsilon;

(53b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i|{\rm X}}=\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}\quad{\rm if}\;i\leq d_{\rm X}\quad\&\quad\mathcal{E}_{d_{\rm X}+1|{\rm X}}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm X}\;

(53c)

\displaystyle E_{i|{\rm X}}=M_{i|{\rm X}}\quad{\rm if}\;i\leq d_{\rm X}\quad\&\quad E_{d_{\rm X}+1|{\rm X}}=\mathbb{I}_{\rm X}-\sum_{\rm i=1}^{d_{\rm X}}M_{i|{\rm X}},

(53d)

where
\epsilon\in[0,1]
is a parameter that will be specified later, and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm X}
is an arbitrary channel (hence, we can choose it to be unitary).
From here we learn that
\{E_{i|{\rm X}}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}+1}
is a POVM for every
{\rm X}
, which implies that
D
is a channel-discrimination task.
Furthermore,
D
is strictly positive when
0<\epsilon<1
[see also Eq. (
50
)].
Hence,
D
and
\mathbfcal{E}
satisfy the description of Theorem
4
.

As with
Eq.

51
, a probability of success can be defined for the task
D
:

\displaystyle P_{D}({\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\bm{\Lambda}|}\sum_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm X}+1}p_{i|{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[E_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{E}_{i|{\rm X}}(\kappa_{\rm X})\right]

(54)

which can be decomposed as (see also Ref.
[
75
]
):

\displaystyle P_{D}({\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})=P_{D_{-}}({\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{U})+\epsilon\,\Gamma({\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E}),

(55)

where the second term is defined via Eq. (
51
) and Eq. (
53
) as:

\displaystyle\Gamma({\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})\coloneqq\frac{1}{|{\bm{\Lambda}}|}\sum_{{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}}{\rm tr}\left[E_{d_{{\rm X}}+1|{\rm X}}\mathcal{L}_{\rm X}(\kappa_{\rm X})\right]-P_{D_{-}}({\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{U}).

(56)

It then follows from
Eq.

52
and
Eq.

55
that

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}P_{D}({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}P_{D_{-}}({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{U})+\epsilon\times\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}\Gamma({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})

\displaystyle=P_{D_{-}}({\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{U})-\Delta_{1}+\epsilon\times\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}\Gamma({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})

\displaystyle=P_{D}({\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})-\Delta_{1}+\epsilon\Delta_{2},

(57)

where
\Delta_{2}\coloneqq\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}\Gamma({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})-\Gamma({\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})
is finite since
\Gamma
is bounded for every
({\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})
.
Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}P_{D}({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})\leq P_{D}({\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})-\Delta_{1}+\epsilon\Delta_{2}.

(58)

Then if
\Delta_{2}\leq 0
, we have
\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}P_{D}({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})<P_{D}({\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})

\forall\;\epsilon\in[0,1]
.
When
\Delta_{2}>0
, one can take
\epsilon<\min\left\{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\Delta_{2}},1\right\}
, c.f.
Eq.

53b
, to conclude that
\sup_{{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}}P_{D}({\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})<P_{D}({\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}}},\mathbfcal{E})
.
The result follows.
∎

D.5
Proof of Eq. (
16
)

Proof.

For every
{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}}
and
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T}}
, we have
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}(\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}})\coloneqq\{\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}(\tau_{\rm X})\}_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}
, where, according to the definitions,

\displaystyle\exists\;\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}\;{\rm compatible\;with}\;\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\;{\rm s.t.}\;{\rm Tr}_{\rm S\setminus T\to S\setminus T}\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}\in\mathcal{O}_{R|{\rm T}};

(59)

\displaystyle\exists\,\eta_{\rm S}\;{\rm compatible\;with}\;{\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}}}\;{\rm s.t.}\;{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus T}(\eta_{\rm S})\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T}.

(60)

In other words, we have
{\rm Tr}_{\rm S\setminus X\to S\setminus X}\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}=\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}
and
{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus X}(\eta_{\rm S})=\tau_{\rm X}
for every
{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}
.
This means that

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus X}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}(\eta_{\rm S})\right]=\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}\left(\tau_{\rm X}\right)\quad\forall\;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda};

(61)

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus T}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}(\eta_{\rm S})\right]={\rm Tr}_{\rm S\setminus T\to S\setminus T}\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}\left[{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus T}(\eta_{\rm S})\right]\in\mathcal{F}_{R|\rm T},

(62)

where we have used Eq. (
14
).
Thus, there exists a global state,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}(\eta_{\rm S})
, whose marginal state in
{\rm T}
,
{\rm tr}_{\rm S\setminus T}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\rm S}(\eta_{\rm S})\right]
, is free, such that it is compatible with
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}(\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}})
.
Hence, we conclude that
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}(\bm{\tau_{\Lambda}})\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
.
∎

D.6
Proof of Theorem
5

Proof.

The first statement follows directly from the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}
, it thus suffices to prove the second statement.
For every
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T}}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}(\bm{\sigma}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\lambda,\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}\log_{2}\left\{\lambda\,|\,\sigma_{\rm X}\preceq\lambda\kappa_{\rm X}\;\forall\;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda};\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\lambda,\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}\log_{2}\left\{\lambda\,|\,0\preceq\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}\left(\lambda\kappa_{\rm X}-\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\;\forall\;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda};\bm{\kappa_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\lambda,\bm{\eta_{\Lambda}}}\log_{2}\left\{\lambda\,|\,0\preceq\lambda\eta_{\rm X}-\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}\left(\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\;\forall\;{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda};\bm{\eta_{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{R|{\rm T}}\left[\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}(\bm{\sigma_{\Lambda}})\right].

(63)

The first inequality holds because each
\mathcal{E}_{{\rm X}}
maps positive operators (and possibly some non-positive operators) to positive operators, thereby making the range of minimization larger. The second inequality follows from the linearity of
\mathcal{E}_{{\rm X}}
and the fact that
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T}}
implies
\{\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}(\kappa_{\rm X})\}_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
for every
\{\kappa_{\rm X}\}_{{\rm X}\in\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R|{\rm T},{\bm{\Lambda}}}
[i.e., Eq. (
16
)], thereby (potentially) making the minimization range larger.
∎

D.7
Proof of Theorem
6

Proof.

We first show that Statement
1
implies Statement
2
.
For every given
D
, a direct computation shows that

\displaystyle{\rm Score}\left(\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}},{D}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm Score}\left[\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\left(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\right),{D}\right]\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}}\sum_{{\rm X},i}p_{\rm X}p_{i|{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[E_{i|{\rm X}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}\right)(\tau_{\rm X})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}^{\prime}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}}\sum_{{\rm X},i}p_{\rm X}p_{i|{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[E_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}^{\prime}(\tau_{\rm X})\right]={\rm Score}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}},{D}),

(64)

where we have used
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\circ\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
if
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime},\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}
.

It remains to show that Statement
2
implies Statement
1
.
First, we note that Statement
2
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{{D}}\left[{\rm Score}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}},{D})-{\rm Score}(\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}},{D})\right]\leq\inf_{{D}}\left[{\rm Score}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}},{D})-\sum_{{\rm X},i}p_{\rm X}p_{i|{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left(E_{i|{\rm X}}\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{{D}}f({D}),

(65)

where the minimization is taken over all non-deterministic
D
,

\displaystyle f({D})\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}}\sum_{{\rm X},i}p_{\rm X}p_{i|{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[E_{i|{\rm X}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}(\tau_{\rm X})-\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\right],

(66)

and we have used the fact that identity map is a free operation in arriving at the second inequality.
Consider another function

\displaystyle g\left(\{L_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X}\right)\coloneqq\max_{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}}\sum_{{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[L_{\rm X}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}(\tau_{\rm X})-\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\right].

(67)

Then one can check that Eq. (
D.7
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle 0\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq L_{\rm X}\\
\sum_{\rm X}{\rm tr}(L_{\rm X})\leq d_{\rm max}\end{subarray}}g\left(\{L_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X}\right),

(68)

where
d_{\rm max}\coloneqq\max_{\rm X}d_{\rm X}
.
To see why this is true, consider
L_{\rm X}=\sum_{i}p_{\rm X}p_{i|{\rm X}}E_{i|{\rm X}}
, we have
\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq L_{\rm X}\\
\sum_{\rm X}{\rm tr}(L_{\rm X})\leq d_{\rm max}\end{subarray}}g\left(\{L_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X}\right)\leq\inf_{{D}}f({D})
.
Conversely, for every feasible
\{L_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X}
of the minimization in Eq. (
68
), one can define

\displaystyle E_{0|{\rm X}}=\frac{L_{\rm X}}{\max_{\rm X}\left\|L_{\rm X}\right\|_{\infty}+\delta}\quad\&\quad p_{\rm X}=\frac{1}{|{\bm{\Lambda}}|},

(69)

where
\delta>0
is some positive value.
Then one can see that

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}}\sum_{{\rm X}}\frac{{\rm tr}\left[L_{\rm X}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}(\tau_{\rm X})-\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\right]}{|{\bm{\Lambda}}|(\max_{\rm X}\left\|L_{\rm X}\right\|_{\infty}+\delta)}\geq\inf_{{D}}f({D})

(70)

for every feasible
\{L_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X}
, which implies Eq. (
68
).

Using the above observation, we apply Sion’s minimax theorem to Eq. (
68
) (see also, e.g., Ref.
[
52
]
) and obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\max_{\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathfrak{O}_{R|{\rm T},\bm{\Lambda}}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq L_{\rm X}\\
\sum_{\rm X}{\rm tr}(L_{\rm X})\leq d_{\rm max}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[L_{\rm X}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}(\tau_{\rm X})-\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\right].

(71)

Let
\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\rm opt}
be the one achieving the maximisation.
Then we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq L_{\rm X}\\
\sum_{\rm X}{\rm tr}(L_{\rm X})\leq d_{\rm max}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\rm X}}{\rm tr}\left[L_{\rm X}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}^{\rm opt}(\tau_{\rm X})-\sigma_{\rm X}\right)\right].

(72)

This means

\mathcal{E}_{\rm X}^{\rm opt}(\tau_{\rm X})\geq\sigma_{\rm X}

for every
{\rm X}
.
Since a semi-definite positive trace-less operator must be a zero operator, we conclude that

\mathbfcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\rm opt}(\bm{\tau}_{\bm{\Lambda}})=\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}.

The proof is thus completed.
∎

D.8
POVM Elements for the Numerical Example

For each
{\rm X}\in\{{\rm AB,AC}\}
, the POVM elements that we use in the example in the main text are constructed from the operators

M_{i|{\rm X}}\coloneqq d_{\rm X}\omega_{i|{\rm X}}\mathcal{U}_{i|{\rm X}}(|\psi_{i|{\rm X}}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{i|{\rm X}}|)

where (with respect to the computational basis
\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}
)

\displaystyle\omega_{1|{\rm X}}

\displaystyle=0.010000027026545,\quad\omega_{2|{\rm X}}=0.010000058075968,

\displaystyle\omega_{3|{\rm X}}

\displaystyle=0.458638621962197,\quad\omega_{4|{\rm X}}=0.537143367559183.

(73)

and

\displaystyle|\psi_{1|{\rm X}}\rangle

\displaystyle=0.668877697040469|01\rangle-0.743372468148935|10\rangle,

\displaystyle|\psi_{2|{\rm X}}\rangle

\displaystyle=|11\rangle,\quad|\psi_{3|{\rm X}}\rangle=|00\rangle,

\displaystyle|\psi_{4|{\rm X}}\rangle

\displaystyle=0.743372468148935|01\rangle+0.668877697040469|10\rangle.

(74)

These are taken from the eigenstates and eigenvalues of
W_{\rm X}+0.01\times\mathbb{I}_{\rm X}
, where
\{W_{\rm X}\}_{\rm X}
are the witness operators from Theorem
1
that can be found by SDP (using the terminology in the proof of Theorem
4
, it also means that we choose
\Delta=0.01
).
The actual POVMs used in the calculation are given by

\displaystyle E_{i|{\rm X}}=\frac{M_{i|{\rm X}}+0.01\times\mathbb{I}_{\rm X}}{\mu_{\rm X}+0.01}\;\;{\rm for}\;i=1,2,3,4\quad\&\quad E_{5|{\rm X}}=\mathbb{I}_{\rm X}-\sum_{i=1}^{4}E_{i|{\rm X}},

(75a)

where
\mu_{\rm X}={\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{4}M_{i|{\rm X}}+0.01\times\mathbb{I}_{\rm X}\rVert_{\infty}}
.
These POVM elements are obtained following the construction given in the proof of
Theorem

4
.
The witness operators
W_{\bm{\Lambda}}
, in turn, were obtained by solving a semidefinite program (see Ref.
[
46
]
) that can be used to certify the entanglement transitivity of
\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{W}
.
