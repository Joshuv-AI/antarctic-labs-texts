Harnessing S-Duality in 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 SYM


Abstract
We develop a new approach to extracting the physical consequences of S-duality of four-dimensional 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 super Yang-Mills (SYM) and its string theory dual, based on S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℤ ) SL(2,\mathbb{Z}) spectral theory. We observe that CFT observables 𝒪 \mathcal{O} , invariant under S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℤ ) SL(2,\mathbb{Z}) transformations of a complexified gauge coupling τ \tau , admit a unique spectral decomposition into a basis of square-integrable functions. This formulation has direct implications for the analytic structure of 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 SYM data, both perturbatively and non-perturbatively in all parameters. These are especially constraining for the structure of instantons: k k -instanton sectors are uniquely determined by the zero- and one-instanton sectors, and Borel summable series around k k -instantons have convergence radii with simple k k -dependence. In large N N limits, we derive the existence and scaling of non-perturbative effects, in both N N and the ’t Hooft coupling, which we exhibit for certain 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 SYM observables. An elegant benchmark for these techniques is the integrated stress tensor multiplet four-point function, conjecturally determined by [ 1 ] for all τ \tau for S ​ U ​ ( N ) SU(N) gauge group; we derive and elucidate its form, and explain how the S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) case is the simplest possible observable consistent with S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℤ ) SL(2,\mathbb{Z}) -invariant perturbation theory. These results have ramifications for holography. We explain how ⟨ 𝒪 ⟩ \langle\mathcal{O}\rangle , the ensemble average of 𝒪 {\cal O} over the 𝒩 = 4 \mathcal{N}=4 supersymmetric conformal manifold with respect to the Zamolodchikov measure, is cleanly isolated by the spectral decomposition. We prove that the large N N limit of ⟨ 𝒪 ⟩ \langle\mathcal{O}\rangle equals the large N N , large ’t Hooft coupling limit of 𝒪 \mathcal{O} . Holographically speaking, ⟨ 𝒪 ⟩ = 𝒪 sugra \langle\mathcal{O}\rangle={\cal O}_{\rm sugra} , its value in type IIB supergravity on AdS 5 × S 5 {}_{5}\times S^{5} . This result, which extends to all orders in 1 / N 1/N , embeds ensemble averaging into the traditional AdS/CFT paradigm. The statistics of the S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℤ ) SL(2,\mathbb{Z}) ensemble exhibit both perturbative and non-perturbative 1 / N 1/N effects. We discuss further implications and generalizations to other AdS compactifications of string/M-theory.

Introduction

{sec:intro}

This paper pursues two intertwined endeavors.

The first is to understand how to extract the full implications of S-duality for the observables of superconformal field theories, focusing specifically on four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=4
super Yang-Mills theory.

The second is to reframe the AdS/CFT Correspondence, namely the original duality between
\mathcal{N}=4
super Yang-Mills and type IIB string theory on AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
, by applying new lessons from S-duality.

The
\mathcal{N}=4
super Yang-Mills (SYM) theory is, at risk of stating the obvious, a beautiful theory from myriad points of view. Maximal supersymmetry imposes rigid structure and regularity of CFT data, yet provides a route to computing certain observables that coincide with those in less supersymmetric theories. For a finite number of colors
N

N
’t Hooft limit, new symmetries emerge, leading to integrability solutions at the planar level and beyond. Not to be forgotten, the holographic correspondence with type IIB string theory on AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
furnishes our most explicit definition of a theory of quantum gravity, shedding light on both sides of the duality.

\mathcal{N}=4
SYM also enjoys S-duality

(P)SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
transformations of
\tau

Our idea is to introduce certain methods from the mathematics literature that we argue are perfectly tailored to the

Our result exhibits a role for spacetime wormholes even within the conventional holographic paradigm. (See Figure

4.2
Spectral decomposition

Let us start with the
SU(2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{2}(\tau)={1\over 2}+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,\left({\pi\over\sin\pi s}s(1-s)(2s-1)^{2}\right)E^{*}_{s}(\tau)\end{split}

In terms of spectral overlaps, we have

\begin{split}\{\mathcal{G}_{2},E_{s}\}={\pi\over\sin\pi s}\,c_{s}^{(2)}\,,\qquad(\mathcal{G}_{2},\phi_{n})=0\end{split}

(4.13)

where

). The claim is that
\mathcal{G}_{2}(\tau)
is given

by this expression, for all
\tau\in{\cal F}
: there is no need for regularization or resummation, a fact which is built into the spectral decomposition, convergent by definition. Note that the spectral representation (
4.12
) is functionally rather simpler than (
4.5
), being a one-dimensional integral
without
a lattice sum on top of it.

It is easiest to first recover the formal expression (
4.8
) for
N=2
from (
4.12
). This is done by contour deformation, with the expansion coefficients
c_{2}^{(N)}
encoded as residues of
(\mathcal{G}_{2},E_{s})
at

. This deformation also generates a constant contribution from
s=1
, where

has a simple pole, cf. (
2.17
): if we deform to
{\rm Re\,}s>{1\over 2}
, we pick up a constant term
-1/4
. This combines to give the correct constant in (
4.8
). Now, recall that (
4.8
) is not convergent, but rather required Borel resummation to become well-defined. This is precisely encoded in the fact that the integrand of (
4.13
) diverges factorially at
s\rightarrow\pm\infty
, due to the completed zeta function
\Lambda(s)
. The spectral representation (
4.12
) may thus be thought of as the Borel resummation of the weak coupling expansion.

If (
4.12
) is correct, then it should equal the (conjectural) expression (
4.5
) too, which is also convergent for all
\tau\in{\cal F}
. We will establish this equality in Section
5.5
as an example of a more general equality between spectral decompositions and lattice-integral representations of the same quantity

, and in doing so, prove the validity of the formula (
4.5
) given only the perturbative expansions of the

The result for
SU(N)

4.10

\begin{split}\boxed{{\mathcal{G}_{N}(\tau)={N(N-1)\over 4}+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,{\pi\over\sin\pi s}\,c_{s}^{(N)}E^{*}_{s}(\tau)}}\end{split}

(4.14)

i.e.

\begin{split}\boxed{\{\mathcal{G}_{N},E_{s}\}={\pi\over\sin\pi s}\,c_{s}^{(N)}\,,\qquad(\mathcal{G}_{N},\phi_{n})=0}\end{split}

(4.15)

4.8

as follows. The constant term is determined by

\begin{split}\Res_{s=1}R_{s}[\mathcal{G}_{N}]&=P^{(N)}(1)\times\Res_{s=1}R_{s}[\mathcal{G}_{2}]\\
&={P^{(N)}(1)\over 2}\end{split}

So our claim is that

5
Instantons and the Analytic Structure of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM

{sec:instantonsN4AnalyticStructure}

We now systematically explore the consequences of spectral decomposition for
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant observables
{\cal O}(\tau)
. For clarity, we begin this section by writing formulas assuming
({\cal O},\phi_{n})=0
, generalizing in due course. We remind the reader that
\phi_{n}(\tau)
has no zero mode, so all conclusions about
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
are independent of this assumption. It will prove convenient to write out the Fourier modes explicitly: suppressing the cusp forms in
{\cal O}_{k>0}(y)
,

\begin{split}{\cal O}_{0}(y)&=\langle{\cal O}\rangle+{1\over 2\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}\Lambda(s)y^{s}\\
{\cal O}_{k>0}(y)&={1\over 2\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}\left({\sigma_{2s-1}(k)\over k^{s-{1\over 2}}}\sqrt{y}K_{s-{1\over 2}}(2\pi ky)\right)\end{split}

(5.1)

where the mode expansion was developed in (
3.7
).

5.1
Perturbation theory

Our starting point is to understand how to recover weak coupling perturbation theory from this formalism. This is straightforward, and elegant. Weak coupling perturbation theory is an expansion around
y\rightarrow\infty
. For
k=0
, the perturbative expansion is therefore read off from contour deformation of (
5.1
) in the appropriate direction. In particular, since
y\rightarrow\infty
, we must deform
y^{s}
to the left. This seems in tension with the fact that the integrand may not fall off sufficiently fast at
s\rightarrow-\infty
for this procedure to give a finite result. However, exactly this fact encodes the convergence, or lack thereof, of the perturbative expansion of
{\cal O}(\tau)
. For example, if
{\cal O}(\tau)
admits an asymptotic series, then
({\cal O},E_{s})
will diverge factorially at
s\rightarrow-\infty
. Thus, the spectral formalism for gauge theory observables embeds the asymptotic properties of perturbative expansions in the
|s|\rightarrow\infty
asymptotics of spectral overlaps. The virtue of the spectral decomposition lies in the existence of a
convergent
expression from which one can, if desired, develop the weak coupling expansion.

For
k>0
, things work differently. We note the asymptotics

\sqrt{y}K_{s-{1\over 2}}(2\pi ky)={1\over 2\sqrt{k}}e^{-2\pi ky}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{a_{n}(s)\over(2\pi ky)^{n}}

(5.2)

where

a_{n}(s)={(s)_{n}(1-s)_{n}\over(-2)^{n}n!}.

(5.3)

We highlight that the complete asymptotic expansion of
K_{s-{1\over 2}}(2\pi ky)
is a
single
exponential times a power series in
1/y
, for all
s
. Inserting in (
5.1
),

{\cal O}_{k>0}(y)={e^{-2\pi ky}\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}\left({\sigma_{2s-1}(k)k^{-s}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{a_{n}(s)\over(2\pi ky)^{n}}\right)

(5.4)

Suppose we swap the sum and integral in (
5.4
),

{\cal O}_{k>0}(y)\approx{e^{-2\pi ky}\over 4\pi i}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}y^{-n}\left({1\over(-4\pi k)^{n}n!}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}{\sigma_{2s-1}(k)k^{-s}}(s)_{n}(1-s)_{n}\right)

(5.5)

If
this is allowed, then it is clear that
{\cal O}_{k}(y)
receives no non-perturbative, i.e. instanton-anti-instanton, contributions: such contributions would dress the
e^{-2\pi ky}
with extra powers of
q\bar{q}=e^{-4\pi y}
, but (
5.5
) is a power series in
1/y
. The factor
\sigma_{2it}(k)k^{-{1\over 2}+it}\in\mathbb{R}
for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is oscillatory in
t
but bounded in
k
. Therefore, the convergence properties of the bracketed integral are uniform in
k
. By inspection, convergence for fixed
k
demands that the overlap decays faster than any polynomial as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

But clearly, this cannot always hold for
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM observables, which
can
have non-perturbative corrections. Indeed, a short calculation (see Appendix
A
) shows that convergence of the spectral decomposition only requires

\big|\{{\cal O},E_{{1\over 2}+it}\}\big|\lesssim O(e^{\pi t/2})\qquad(t\rightarrow\infty)

(5.6)

where we have suppressed power law corrections.

Thus, the presence or absence of non-perturbative corrections to
{\cal O}_{k>0}(y)
boils down to whether the integrals in (
5.5
) converge, which in turn is determined by whether
\{{\cal O},E_{{1\over 2}+it}\}
decays super-polynomially as
t\rightarrow\infty
. We show below that this asymptotic is elegantly determined by the Borel summability
of the zero mode
,
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
! The connection, of course, is that
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
and
{\cal O}_{k>0}(y)
are fixed (modulo cusp forms) by the same spectral overlap.

5.2
The analytic structure of CFT spectral overlaps

Let us now understand how the mathematical structure of the overlaps is constrained by our physics problem. We are interested in CFT observables
{\cal O}(\tau)
. These must have a good perturbative expansion: specifically, we allow only non-negative integer powers of
1/y
, and no powers of
\log y
. This strongly constrains the form of
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
, because we can set
k=0
, and extract the perturbative expansion by contour deformation to the real axis.

13

13

{sec:analyticStructureCFTOverlaps}

The most general such

\begin{split}\boxed{\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}={\pi\over\sin\pi s}s(1-s)f_{\rm p}(s)+f_{\rm np}(s)}\end{split}

(5.7)

The labeling of these functions is hopefully clear. By contour deformation,

14
14

14

The zero mode of

The functions

and
f_{\rm np}(s)
must obey the following properties:

1)
Invariant under reflections,

2)

3)

These constraints follow immediately from the preceding exposition. In fact, these functions obey even stronger constraints, which we prove using number theory in Appendix

3a)

3b)
At

\lim_{s\rightarrow 1}\,\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}=2\,\overline{{\cal O}}

(5.9)

3c)
On the critical line
s={1\over 2}+it

\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
is finite for finite

.

4)
If
f_{\rm np}(s)=0
(resp.
f_{\rm p}(s)=0

(resp.
f_{\rm np}(s)

To summarize,
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
is a meromorphic function, regular everywhere on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{s\in\mathbb{R}\}
with poles at
s=2,3,\ldots
and their reflections coming only from the
\sin\pi s
factor. Determining the (continuous part of the) spectral decomposition of
{\cal O}(\tau)
boils down to finding the two real functions
f_{\rm p}(s)
and
f_{\rm np}(s)
with these fairly rigid properties.

Since
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
is defined as a Mellin transform of the zero mode,

\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}={1\over\Lambda(1-s)}\mathfrak{M}\left[{{\cal O}_{0}(y)\over y};s\right]

it is simple to read off the values of
f_{\rm p}(s)
at

from the perturbative series: inserting (
5.7

{\cal O}_{0}(y\rightarrow\infty)\sim\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n}c_{n}\,y^{-n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad c_{n}=-n(n+1)\Lambda\Big(n+{1\over 2}\Big)f_{\rm p}(n+1)

(5.11)

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

f_{\rm p}(n\rightarrow\infty)\sim-{\pi^{n+{1\over 2}}\over n^{2}}{c_{n\rightarrow\infty}\over\Gamma(n+{1\over 2})}\,.

(5.12)

This exhibits the encoding of the asymptotics of the perturbative series
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
in the
s\rightarrow\infty

f_{\rm p}(s)
.

As for
f_{\rm np}(s)

for constants

\begin{split}\mathfrak{M}\left[y^{-\alpha}e^{-4\pi ny};-s\right]=(4\pi n)^{s+\alpha}\,\Gamma(-s-\alpha)\end{split}

(5.13)

Parameterizing the most general series of non-perturbative corrections as

{\cal O}_{0}(y)\supset\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}c_{\alpha}({\cal O})y^{-\alpha}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kappa^{(\alpha)}_{n}({\cal O})e^{-4\pi ny}\,,

where

are constants, translates into the general formula for the non-perturbative overlap

{f_{\rm np}(s)=\Lambda(s)^{-1}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}c_{\alpha}({\cal O})\Phi^{(\alpha)}_{\cal O}(-s-\alpha)\Gamma(-s-\alpha)}

where we have introduced a Dirichlet series

\Phi^{(\alpha)}_{\cal O}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\kappa^{(\alpha)}_{n}({\cal O})\over(4\pi n)^{s}}

(5.16)

This is associated to the non-perturbative corrections

around each perturbative correction to
{\cal O}(\tau)

An interesting fact is that the coefficients

\Psi^{(\alpha)}_{\cal O}(s)\coloneqq\Lambda(s)^{-1}\Phi^{(\alpha)}_{\cal O}(-s-\alpha)\Gamma(-s-\alpha)

we have

{f_{\rm np}(s)=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}c_{\alpha}({\cal O})\Psi^{(\alpha)}_{\cal O}(s)=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}c_{\alpha}({\cal O})\Psi^{(\alpha)}_{\cal O}(1-s)}

(5.18)

5.3
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
Borel transform

Suppose that
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
has a factorially divergent, Borel summable expansion. Here and throughout, following the literature on resurgence, we take “Borel summable” to mean that the Borel transform
B[\xi]
is non-singular in a wedge of the complex
\xi
plane that includes
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
16
16

16

Generically, non-perturbative additions are not required for a Borel resummed quantity. However, there are “non-generic” cases where non-perturbative contributions are present, indeed necessary, even if the perturbative series is Borel summable. Some representative examples include “Cheshire cat resurgence”
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
as well as certain effects in string perturbation theory
[

. We thank Daniele Dorigoni for correspondence on the status of this question.
This is a standard situation in
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM perturbation theory at finite
N
. If
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
has factorial growth, then
f_{\rm p}(n\rightarrow\infty)
is sub-factorial:

c_{n}\sim(\pi R)^{-n}n!\quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{\rm p}(n)\sim-n^{-{3\over 2}}R^{-n}\qquad(n\rightarrow\infty)

(5.19)

The constant
R
is the radius of convergence of the
n\gg 1
expansion of
f_{\rm p}(n)
.

For resumming
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM perturbation theory, it is convenient to introduce a new, non-standard Borel transform, which we call the
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
Borel transform
. If
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
has the expansion (
5.11
), then we define its
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
Borel transform
B[\xi]
as

B[\xi]\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{(-1)^{n}c_{n}\over\Lambda(n+{1\over 2})}\,\xi^{n+1}\qquad\text{($SL(2,\mathbb{Z})$ Borel transform)}

(5.20)

This may be written in terms of the overlap using (
5.11

B[\xi]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}n(n+1)f_{\rm p}(n+1)\,\xi^{n+1}

(5.21)

Its utility follows upon noticing that

\Lambda(s)=\mathfrak{M}\left[{\theta_{3}(y)-1\over 2};s\right]

(5.22)

where
\theta_{3}(y)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\pi n^{2}y}
is the Jacobi theta function. The Borel resummation of the original series may then be neatly inverted:

\begin{split}{\widehat{\cal O}_{0}(y)}\coloneqq y^{{1\over 2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{d\xi\over\xi^{3\over 2}}\left({\theta_{3}(y\xi)-1\over 2}\right){B[\xi]}\end{split}

(5.23)

This can be confirmed to return the original sum term-wise upon using (
5.22

R
in (
5.19

SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
Borel transform.

The
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})

SL(2,\mathbb{Z})

\Gamma(n+1)

SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
Borel transform also properly eliminates the Riemann zeta factor.

17

17

\begin{split}\langle{\cal O}\rangle&={1\over 2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{d\xi}{B[\xi]\over\xi}\end{split}

This result transcends the usual philosophy of resurgence methods. In typical applications of resurgence, one must perform independent resummations in different instanton sectors. In the presence of extra symmetries, more is possible. What we are seeing here is that

-invariance is strong enough to relate all

for all

Finally, for the sake of clarity, we remind that these conclusions hold modulo possible contributions from cusp forms. We will reinstate the cusp forms in Subsection

5.5
\mathcal{N}=4

The previous section derived quantitative formulas for

-instanton dynamics from the zero-instanton dynamics. That this should be possible followed from the fundamental determinism baked into the

The starting point is (

At a technical level, this means that it is infeasible to study the sum over

5.8
On the strong coupling expansion

To conclude this section, let us ask what the spectral decomposition implies about the
strong
coupling expansion,
y\rightarrow 0
. To be clear, S-duality implies that strong coupling can be mapped to weak coupling. The purpose of this short coda is to derive some precise consequences of this.

To develop the
y\rightarrow 0
expansion of
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
, we again start from the first equation of (
5.1
), but now deforming to the right,
{\rm Re\,}s>{1\over 2}
. This yields

{\cal O}_{0}(y)=\langle{\cal O}\rangle+\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{s}s(s-1)f_{\rm p}(s)\Lambda(s)y^{s}\qquad(y\rightarrow 0)

(5.71)

There are a few notable features here. First, there is a universal factor
\varphi(s)
relating the coefficients of the weak and strong coupling expansions of
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
, seen by comparing (
5.11
) and (
5.71
). In
[
1
]
, this was regarded as a striking observation for
\mathcal{G}_{N}(\tau)
. We are showing that it is a simple consequence of
SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
-invariance for any square-integrable observable. Note that
\varphi(s\gg 1)\sim 1/\sqrt{s}
, so the asymptotics at
y\rightarrow 0
and
y\rightarrow\infty
are essentially identical. Second, there is never an
O(y)
term, which follows from
\Res_{s=1}R_{1-s}[{\cal O}]=\Res_{s=1}\left(\varphi(s)^{-1}R_{s}[{\cal O}]\right)=0
. Finally, the ensemble average equals the zero mode at
y=0
:

\langle{\cal O}\rangle={\cal O}_{0}(y=0)

(5.72)

In other words, dialing
{\cal O}_{0}(y)
from weak to strong coupling adds a factor of the ensemble average. This is a non-trivial statement, notwithstanding S-duality: we are taking the strong coupling limit of the
zero mode
, which is not
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
invariant, not of
{\cal O}(\tau)
, which is
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
invariant. We will see a large
N
version of this statement in Section
10
, with powerful implications.

6
Large
N
: Generalities

{sec:largeN}

We will be concerned with two types of large
N
limits.

The first limit is the ’t Hooft limit, defined as
N\rightarrow\infty
,
g\rightarrow 0
with
\lambda\coloneqq g^{2}N
fixed. In terms of our variable
y={\rm Im\,}\tau
,

N\rightarrow\infty,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lambda={4\pi N\over y}\penalty\ \text{fixed}\qquad\text{('t Hooft)}

(6.1)

In the ’t Hooft limit, the perturbative
1/N
expansion organizes into a genus expansion. Instantons are non-perturbatively suppressed as

{\cal O}_{k}(y)\propto e^{-2\pi ky}\sim e^{-8\pi^{2}k{N\over\lambda}}

(6.2)

so the genus expansion applies to the zero mode,

{\cal O}_{0}(y)=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\,{\cal O}_{0}^{(g)}(\lambda)

(6.3)

There are also other non-perturbative corrections in
N
which correct the zero mode itself at each order in the genus expansion, to be derived below.

The second limit is
N\rightarrow\infty
with
g
fixed, sometimes called the “very strongly coupled limit”
[
20
]
:

N\rightarrow\infty,\penalty\ \penalty\ y\penalty\ \text{fixed}\qquad\text{(very strongly coupled)}

(6.4)

The genus expansion (
6.3
) does not hold in the very strongly coupled limit, but taking
y
fixed and then sending
y\rightarrow\infty
recovers the leading
\lambda\gg 1
behavior in the ’t Hooft limit.

Before delving into details, let us state one of the main overarching points here. As first explained in the previous section on perturbation theory at finite
N
, to develop a perturbative expansion in some parameter from the spectral decomposition, one first expands the spectral overlap
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
, and then performs a contour deformation to pick up poles. The presence or absence of
non-perturbative
corrections to the resulting expansion is encoded in the
|s|\rightarrow\infty
asymptotics of the overlap. In the large
N
expansions to follow, this approach will reveal various corrections. The power of
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
is that perturbative and non-perturbative physics are intertwined into one and the same automorphic object.

We first need to understand how the spectral overlaps are constrained. Let us present, then explain, the result. At large
N
, the spectral overlap
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
is of the following form:

\begin{split}\boxed{\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\left({\pi\over\sin\pi s}s(1-s)\left(N^{-s}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)+(s\rightarrow 1-s)\right)+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}N^{-3-{m\over 2}}f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)\right)}\end{split}

(6.5)

where
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariance requires the reflection symmetry
f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)=f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(1-s)
.

These two sets of terms are the large
N
expansions of the perturbative and non-perturbative parts of (
5.7
), respectively. Let us now explain (
6.5
) in slightly more detail. The reader interested only in its consequences may skip to the next section.

The first, perturbative term in (
6.5
) can be understood by considering the ’t Hooft limit, but applies to any large
N
limit since
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
is independent of
y
. The key observation is that any function
f(\lambda)
is produced by an overlap with
N^{-s}
scaling: recalling that
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
is
\Lambda(s)^{-1}
times the RS transform, we have

\begin{split}R_{1-s}[f(\lambda)]&=\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,y^{-1-s}f(\lambda)\\
&=(4\pi N)^{-s}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\lambda^{s-1}f(\lambda)\\
&=(4\pi N)^{-s}\,\mathfrak{M}[f(\lambda);s]\end{split}

(6.6)

where
\lambda
is the Mellin integration variable in the final line. Therefore, the genus expansion (
6.3
) implies that
f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)
contains information of the ’t Hooft expansion at genus
g
, for
any

\lambda
. The details of the
\lambda\ll 1
and
\lambda\gg 1
expansions — including possible non-perturbative corrections in
\lambda
— are encoded in the polar structure of
f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
. The presence of the reflected solution in (
6.5
) follows from the
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariance of the spectral decomposition, i.e. the reflection symmetry of
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
. Note that since we are expanding at
N\rightarrow\infty
first, the polar structure of
f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(s)
can be non-trivial, unlike
f_{\rm p}(s)
at finite
N
.

The second, non-perturbative term in (
6.5
) generates all exponential terms
\sim e^{-4\pi ny}
, as discussed in Section
5.2
. These are non-perturbative in the ’t Hooft limit; consistent with the previous paragraph, their
N
-scaling is independent of
s
. It may not be immediately obvious why, or how,
f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)
generates exponentials. After all, to obtain the contribution of
f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)
, we simply integrate along the critical line
s={1\over 2}+it
; why is this non-perturbative at
y\rightarrow\infty
? The answer can be nicely understood from basic properties of the Rankin-Selberg method as adapted by Zagier
[
54
]
. At a fixed order in the
1/N
expansion,
{\cal O}(\tau)
must be
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
invariant, although it need not be square-integrable. However, consistency with the genus expansion in the ’t Hooft limit means that the leading power of
y
at fixed order in
1/N
is bounded above. As shown in
[
54
]
, if an
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant function
F(\tau)
with bounded power-law growth at
y\rightarrow\infty
has a zero mode

F_{0}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{c_{i}\over n_{i}!}y^{\alpha_{i}}\log^{n_{i}}y+\text{(exponential)}

(6.7)

for some finite
m
, with
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
c_{i},\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
with bounded
{\rm Re\,}(\alpha_{i})
, then
F(\tau)
can be written as

F(\tau)\coloneqq\sum_{i\,|\,\alpha_{i}\geq{1\over 2}}\left.c_{i}{\partial^{n_{i}}\over\partial s^{n_{i}}}E_{s}(\tau)\right|_{s=\alpha_{i}}+F_{\rm spec}(\tau)

(6.8)

The Eisenstein series subtract the powers of
y^{\alpha_{i}\geq{1\over 2}}
in a modular-invariant way to restore square-integrability, such that
F_{\rm spec}(\tau)\in L^{2}({\cal F})
and hence admits a spectral decomposition. Then since the zero mode of the Eisenstein series has no exponential terms in
y
, any exponential terms in
F_{0}(y)
must come solely from
F_{\rm spec}(\tau)
.

We pause to briefly note a small subtlety. Taking
F_{0}(y)
to be free of logs, as in perturbative CFT applications, we have

F_{{\rm spec},0}(y)=\sum_{i\,|\,\alpha_{i}<{1\over 2}}c_{i}y^{\alpha_{i}}-\sum_{i\,|\,\alpha_{i}\geq{1\over 2}}c_{i}\varphi(\alpha_{i})y^{1-\alpha_{i}}+\text{(exponential)}

(6.9)

If the powers of
y
do not exactly cancel (and they need not), then
F_{{\rm spec},0}(y)
has, in addition to the exponentials, spurious powers of
y
that are not present in
F_{0}(y)
(and vice-versa). In order to obtain these powers from the spectral integral
F_{\rm spec}(\tau)
, one should shift the spectral contour, picking up residues of spurious poles of
\{F_{\rm spec},E_{s}\}
along the way.
24
24

24

If there is a finite number of such spurious poles, this shift incurs no residue at infinity. All examples we know are of this type. This is very neatly exhibited by a certain class of solutions to inhomogeneous Laplace equations appearing in perturbative string theory and
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM correlators, as we recall in detail in Appendix
B
.
The remaining spectral integral has the form of an inverse Mellin transform.

Finally, we comment on the range of the sum over
m
in (
6.5
). Here, unlike elsewhere in this work, we use some mild input from holography: in particular, that the first non-perturbative terms in the AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
effective action appear at 14-derivative order, via
D^{6}R^{4}
invariants and their superpartners. This may be seen as a fact about type IIB string theory, or as following on general grounds from on-shell constraints in theories of maximal supergravity combined with the flat space limit of AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}

[
64
,
65
]
.

Summarizing and returning to our problem: the function
f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)
contributes non-perturbative terms
(q\bar{q})^{n}
, plus a possible set of spurious poles. Moreover, since
f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)
generates exponentials in
y
,

\Lambda(s)f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)\sim\Gamma(|s|)\qquad(s\rightarrow\pm\infty)

(6.10)

for every
(g,m)
.

One more comment before moving on. Although above we discussed non-perturbative contributions to the spectral overlap
\{\mathcal{O},E_{s}\}
between a CFT observable and the Eisenstein series, essentially identical considerations apply to the overlap with the Maass cusp forms,
(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})
. In particular, the cusp forms do not enter the AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
effective action until 14-derivative order, and so
(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})
is suppressed by a factor of
1/N^{3}
compared to the leading perturbative effect
f_{p}(s)
. Moreover, like
f_{\rm np}(s)
, the large
N
expansion of
(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})
must proceed in half-integer powers of

:

(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}N^{-3-{m\over 2}}(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})^{(g,m)}.

(6.11)

Indeed, in the concrete example of large
N
expansion of the integrated correlator
{\cal F}_{N}(\tau)
in the very strongly coupled limit in Section
8.2
, we will see that these two contributions to the spectral overlap (
f_{\rm np}(s)
and
(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})
) are intertwined, as they descend from a common modular invariant, the solution to the inhomogeneous Laplace equation described in Appendix
B
.

We now consider the two large
N
limits. It will prove beneficial to treat the ’t Hooft limit first, where consistency with the genus expansion will strongly constrain the allowed poles of the overlap. For brevity, when referring to the non-perturbative part of (
6.5
) we sometimes refer to
f_{\rm np}(s)
without the
(g,m)
superscript, bearing in mind its large

expansion.

7
’t Hooft Limit

{sectH}

The strategy to analyze the ’t Hooft limit is clear: we substitute
y=4\pi N/\lambda
in the spectral decomposition (
5.1
) and analyze it at fixed
\lambda
, taking further perturbative
\lambda\ll 1
or
\lambda\gg 1
limits afterwards. We introduce a useful notation

\tilde{\lambda}\coloneqq{\lambda\over 4\pi}

(7.1)

We safely ignore the cusp forms, which are suppressed as
\sim e^{-N}
in the ’t Hooft limit. Importantly, while we will generalize in due course (see Section
7.4
), we begin by setting

f_{\rm np}(s)=0

(7.2)

This turns off the non-perturbative terms
\sim(q\bar{q})^{n}
. We employ this strategy because it leads to very strong constraints, and will allow us to cleanly identify signatures of non-perturbative physics in the large
N
limit.

In the ’t Hooft limit, the zero mode of the completed Eisenstein series is

E^{*}_{s,0}(y)=\Lambda(s)N^{s}\tilde{\lambda}^{-s}+(s\rightarrow 1-s)

(7.3)

Plugging (
7.3
) and the overlap (
6.5
) into the spectral decomposition (
5.1

{\cal O}_{0}(\lambda)=\langle{\cal O}\rangle+{1\over 2\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,{\pi\over\sin\pi s}s(1-s)\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\left(\Lambda(s)\tilde{\lambda}^{-s}+\Lambda(1-s)N^{1-2s}\tilde{\lambda}^{s-1}\right)f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)

(7.4)

The two terms under the integral are of a very different nature:

\bullet
The first term is manifestly consistent with the ’t Hooft limit, and can be expanded at weak or strong coupling, or evaluated at finite
\tilde{\lambda}
. At
\tilde{\lambda}\ll 1
, we deform to the left, while at
\tilde{\lambda}\gg 1
, we deform to the right.

\bullet
The second term has an explicit
N^{1-2s}
factor. Because we are taking the large
N
limit, we are forced to deform to the right, for any
\tilde{\lambda}
.
25
25

25

Bearing in mind earlier remarks, we must ask whether the coefficient has a factorial divergence at
s\rightarrow\infty
, thus generating potential non-perturbative corrections in
N
. We analyze this question in the next subsection.
This generates two constraints on
f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)
:

1
)

Consistency with the genus expansion:
For
{\rm Re\,}s>{1\over 2}
, the poles of the integrand must lie at
s={1\over 2}+m
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. In particular, the
\sin\pi s
poles must be cancelled by zeros,

{f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(s)=0\,,\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}_{-}}

(7.5)

On the other hand, simple poles for half-integer

are allowed,

\Res_{s={1\over 2}-m}\left[f_{\rm p}^{(g)}\left(s\right)\right]\neq 0\,,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(7.6)

In addition,
\Lambda(1-s)
has a pole at
s=1
, which contributes a constant term. Taking this all into account, we have, for fixed
\tilde{\lambda}
,

\begin{split}{\cal O}_{0}(\lambda)=&\left(\langle{\cal O}\rangle-{1\over 2}\sum_{g=0}N^{1-2g}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(0)\right)-\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}N^{2-2g-2m}\varphi\Big({1\over 2}+m\Big)\mathsf{R}_{m}^{(g)}\tilde{\lambda}^{m-{1\over 2}}\\
&+{1\over 2\pi i}\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,{\pi\over\sin\pi s}s(1-s)\Lambda(s)\tilde{\lambda}^{-s}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)\end{split}

(7.7)

where we define a “residue function”

\begin{split}\mathsf{R}_{m}^{(g)}&\coloneqq\Res_{s={1\over 2}+m}\Big[{\pi\over\sin\pi s}s(1-s)\Lambda(s)f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)\Big]\end{split}

(7.8)

We can now deform this as needed to develop perturbative expansions.

2
)

Consistency with the
\tilde{\lambda}\ll 1
expansion:
These residues at

generate allowed powers of
1/N
, but multiplied by

\tilde{\lambda}

\tilde{\lambda}

\tilde{\lambda}\ll 1

\tilde{\lambda}
are allowed. Developing the
\tilde{\lambda}\ll 1

) to the left, the offending terms are cancelled if and only if the following condition on the residues is met:

\varphi\Big({1\over 2}+m\Big)\,\mathsf{R}_{m}^{(g)}=\mathsf{R}_{-m}^{(g+m)}

(7.9)

This intriguing condition relates perturbative data of different genera.

After the dust settles, we have the following weak coupling expansion

\begin{split}{{\cal O}_{0}(\lambda\ll 1)\approx\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m+1}m(m+1)\Lambda\Big({1\over 2}+m\Big)f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1+m)\,\tilde{\lambda}^{m}}\end{split}

(7.10)

In terms of the residue function,

\begin{split}\boxed{{\cal O}_{0}(\lambda\ll 1)\approx\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{R}_{-m-{1\over 2}}^{(g)}\,\tilde{\lambda}^{m}}\end{split}

The first term is a constant,

\mathsf{C}(N)\coloneqq\langle{\cal O}\rangle-{1\over 2}\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{1-2g}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(0)

(7.13)

The next group of terms are the terms described earlier, but in the strong coupling expansion they do not cancel with anything. They may at first seem peculiar — a finite set of

\lambda

expansion at strong coupling. As explained in

{}_{5}\times S^{5}
supergravity, where
g+m=L

1/N^{2}
suppressed compared to the leading order.
26
26

26

At
N^{0}

(g,m)=(0,1)
gives a
\sqrt{\lambda}
term, which is precisely the regularization of one-loop supergravity by

(and its superpartners) in AdS

What we are mainly interested in is the last group of terms, containing the conventional genus sum over perturbative series in

7.2
Comments + String theory interpretation

{secstringnp}

The
1/N
expansion of CFT observables is expected to be asymptotic. Our analysis shows that for
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant observables in
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM or other SCFTs, not only is it asymptotic, but non-perturbative corrections in
N
are necessary. This is clearly true if
f_{\rm np}(s)\neq 0
, which by definition introduces instanton-anti-instanton terms controlled by the scale
q\bar{q}\sim\exp({-16\pi^{2}{N\over\lambda}})
. If, on the other hand,
f_{\rm np}(s)=0
, the analysis above shows that there are
still
non-perturbative corrections, instead controlled by the scale
\sim\exp({-{8\pi^{2}\over\sqrt{\lambda_{*}}}{N\over\sqrt{\lambda}}})
. It would be valuable to confirm the growth condition assumed in Subsection
7.1
, on which these conclusions rely, in full generality.

Let us explain the physical nature of the non-perturbative scales. We take
\lambda_{*}=\pi^{2}
here to reduce clutter. In the ’t Hooft limit, the Eisenstein zero mode generates two types of terms in the integrand of (
7.4
). One term generates functions of
\lambda
at every genus
g
, including terms at
\lambda\gg 1
suppressed by integer powers of the square of a non-perturbative scale
31
31

31

We have chosen to write the corrections as the square of a non-perturbative scale in the spirit of
[
69
]
(where the non-perturbative scale in
\Gamma_{\rm cusp}
is interpretable as a mass gap of the
O(6)
sigma model) and of various other applications of resurgence to quantum systems (see e.g.
[
70
]
for a recent discussion).

\Lambda_{\lambda}\coloneqq\exp\left(-\sqrt{\lambda}\right)

(7.27)

The other term generates functions of
N^{2}/\lambda
. This may be thought of as the worldsheet coupling in what we call the
S-dual ’t Hooft limit
:

\begin{split}N\rightarrow\infty\,,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lambda_{{\mathsf{S}}}={16\pi^{2}N\over g^{2}}\penalty\ \text{fixed}\qquad(\text{S-dual 't Hooft})\end{split}

(7.28)

In terms of
\lambda
,

\lambda_{{\mathsf{S}}}={16\pi^{2}N^{2}\over\lambda}

(7.29)

Given a sequence of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM theories parameterized by
N
, each of which enjoys an
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
invariance, one takes the S-dual ’t Hooft limit by approaching large Yang-Mills coupling as

. From this point of view, the non-perturbative corrections in

that we have derived are simply the S-dual of the non-perturbative corrections (

\Lambda_{\lambda_{{\mathsf{S}}}}\coloneqq\exp\left(-\sqrt{\lambda_{{\mathsf{S}}}}\right)

(7.30)

Since

for any fixed
\lambda

in the ordinary ‘t Hooft limit.

We can now understand the type IIB string theory duals of the various non-perturbative corrections. The AdS/CFT dictionary includes the basic entry

\sqrt{\lambda}={1\over\alpha^{\prime}}=2\pi T_{\rm F1}\,,\quad\sqrt{\lambda_{{\mathsf{S}}}}={1\over\alpha^{\prime}g_{s}}=2\pi T_{\rm D1}

(7.31)

where
T_{\rm F1}
and

are fundamental string and D-string tensions, respectively, in AdS units. Thus,

•

Terms controlled by
\Lambda_{\lambda}
are fundamental string worldsheet instantons, with endpoints on the AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
boundary. In AdS units, the non-perturbative scale is
\Lambda_{\lambda}=e^{-2\pi T_{\rm F1}}
.

•

Terms controlled by
\Lambda_{\lambda_{{\mathsf{S}}}}
are D-string worldsheet instantons, with endpoints on the AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
boundary. In AdS units, the non-perturbative scale is
\Lambda_{\lambda_{{\mathsf{S}}}}=e^{-2\pi T_{\rm D1}}
.

•

Terms controlled by
q\bar{q}=e^{-16\pi^{2}/g_{s}}
are spacetime instanton-anti-instantons.

To summarize the main result in bulk terms, we have holographically argued for the non-Borel summability of string perturbation theory on AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
: the
g_{s}\ll 1
expansion must be completed by spacetime instantons, D-string instantons, or both. This generalizes previous observations about string perturbation theory in flat space
[
71

72
]
. We also point out previous studies of non-perturbative corrections to sphere free energies in ABJM theory in the ’t Hooft regime
[

]
.
32
32

32

Since the dual AdS
{}_{4}\times\mathbb{CP}^{3}
background solves type IIA, there are no D-strings.

Our starting point is equation (

\lambda\gg 1

invariance of an observable

\begin{split}{\cal O}(\lambda\gg 1)\approx\mathsf{C}(N)-\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathsf{a}_{m}^{(g)}\lambda^{-{3+m\over 2}}+N^{-2-2m}\,\mathsf{b}_{\alpha_{m}}^{(g)}\lambda^{\alpha_{m}}\right)\end{split}

(10.1)

The coefficients
\mathsf{a}_{m}^{(g)}
and
\mathsf{b}_{\alpha_{m}}^{(g)}
, independent of
N
and
\lambda
, take explicit forms in terms of residues of the perturbative and non-perturbative pieces — denoted
f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(s)
and
f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)
, respectively — of the spectral overlap
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
at genus
g
, written in (
6.5
). The
\mathsf{a}_{m}^{(g)}
terms generate the usual
1/\lambda
expansion, while the
\mathsf{b}_{\alpha_{m}}^{(g)}
terms are “renormalization terms” that cut off divergences in the
1/N
expansion at large
\lambda

[
66
]
(see Subsection
7.4
for details). Their explicit forms are not important here. However, the function
\mathsf{C}(N)
, constant in
\lambda

is
important:

\begin{split}\mathsf{C}(N)=\langle{\cal O}\rangle-{1\over 2}\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\left(N^{-1}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(0)+N^{-3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}N^{-{m\over 2}}f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(0)\right)\end{split}

(10.2)

\langle{\cal O}\rangle
is the ensemble average with respect to the Zamolodchikov measure. Let us simplify this expression. By definition,

\begin{split}\langle{\cal O}\rangle&=\Res_{s=1}\big[\Lambda(1-s)\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}\big]\\
&={1\over 2}\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\left(f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1)+N^{-1}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(0)+N^{-3}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}N^{-{m\over 2}}f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(0)\right)\end{split}

(10.3)

where we used the general form of the spectral overlap (
6.5
). This simplifies the constant
\mathsf{C}(N)
considerably, yielding the expansion

\begin{split}{\cal O}(\lambda\gg 1)&=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\left({1\over 2}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1)+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathsf{a}_{m}^{(g)}\lambda^{-{3+m\over 2}}+N^{-2-2m}\,\mathsf{b}_{\alpha_{m}}^{(g)}\lambda^{\alpha_{m}}\right)\right)\end{split}

(10.4)

In particular, the leading-order contribution to the average (
10.3
) at genus
g
,

\begin{split}\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{(g)}\rangle\!\rangle&\coloneqq\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}N^{2g-2}\langle{\cal O}^{(g)}\rangle={1\over 2}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1)\,,\end{split}

(10.5)

furnishes the entire constant term in (
10.4

\begin{split}\boxed{{{\cal O}(\lambda\gg 1)=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\left(\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{(g)}\rangle\!\rangle+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathsf{a}_{m}^{(g)}\lambda^{-{3+m\over 2}}+N^{-2-2m}\,\mathsf{b}_{\alpha_{m}}^{(g)}\lambda^{\alpha_{m}}\right)\right)}}\end{split}

(10.6)

This is a remarkable expression. It is the usual sum over genera, expanded in
1/\lambda
(and renormalized) at every genus, with one key property:
the strong coupling limit is simply the ensemble average!
Focusing on leading order in large
N
, we therefore conclude that

\begin{split}{\cal O}(\lambda\rightarrow\infty)=\langle{\cal O}\rangle\end{split}

(10.7)

Since the
1/\lambda
expansion is, by the AdS/CFT correspondence, dual to the low-energy expansion of classical type IIB string theory around the supergravity limit, this is equivalently stated as

\begin{split}{\cal O}_{\rm sugra}=\langle{\cal O}\rangle\end{split}

(10.8)

In other words,
an observable in classical type IIB supergravity is simply the classical limit of its
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
ensemble average over type IIB string moduli space.

Let us exhibit this equivalence for the integrated correlator
\mathcal{G}_{N}(\tau)
. At large
N
and large
\lambda

[
16
]
,

\mathcal{G}_{N}(\lambda\gg 1)\approx{N^{2}\over 4}\left(1+O(\lambda^{-3/2})\right)\qquad(N\gg 1,\lambda\gg 1)

(10.9)

On the other hand, (
4.18
) implies

\langle\mathcal{G}_{N}\rangle\approx{N^{2}\over 4}\qquad(N\gg 1)

(10.10)

So indeed,
\mathcal{G}_{N,\,{\rm sugra}}=\langle\mathcal{G}_{N}\rangle
at large
N
.

The extension of this correspondence to all orders in
1/N
is manifest from (
10.6
). The constant term at genus
g
is the large
N
limit of the genus-
g
average:

{\cal O}^{(g)}(\lambda\rightarrow\infty)=\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{(g)}\rangle\!\rangle

(10.11)

Holographically, the
\lambda\rightarrow\infty

{\cal O}(\tau)
at genus

is dual to the
(g-g_{*})

Since the quantity
\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{(g)}\rangle\!\rangle
is finite, the supergravity computation at each order in
G_{N}\sim 1/N^{2}
is to be understood as regularized by the string scale cutoff. As noted earlier, this is exactly the role of the
\mathsf{b}_{\alpha_{m}}^{(g)}
terms, dual to local counterterms in AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
implementing the string theory regularization of gravitational UV divergences.

49

49

We note in passing that

defined by conformal manifold integration.

It is important to remember that the large

limit and the ensemble average do not commute: one must take the ensemble average of
{\cal O}(\tau)
first. For one, the order-by-order average of the

are not square-integrable, as discussed around (

What is more, attempting to bypass this issue by applying standard regularization techniques to these formally divergent quantities yields incorrect results. This is clearly on display for the integrated correlator
\mathcal{G}_{N}(\tau)
, studied earlier. One may write it formally as a sum of integer-index Eisenstein series

, as in (

. On a more physical level, non-commutativity is also visible in the fact that

50

50

This term is the “S-dual” of the leading,
O(N^{2})
term. Its origin is not obvious from the bulk point of view. Perhaps it should be understood using D-branes.

To recapitulate, we have discovered that AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
supergravity is, simultaneously, the classical limit of ensemble averaged type IIB string theory, and the low-energy limit of classical type IIB string theory, with the analogous statements for
\mathcal{N}=4

The traditional holographic paradigm is left intact — individual
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM theories are dual to full type IIB string theory on AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
, sans ensemble averaging — while nevertheless, as seen in other holographic dualities in lower dimensions, an ensemble average
does
generate a simple bulk dual, which in this case is AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
supergravity. The ensemble average here is literal, a well-defined integration over a conformal manifold
\mathcal{M}
, equivalently, a string moduli space. And indeed, the resulting theory is
extremal
: having specified an
\mathcal{N}=4
superconformal gauge theory, the large
N
averaged CFT has the largest possible spectral gap consistent with the symmetries, given by the infinite-coupling limit of the microscopic planar CFT.

This result also provides new strategies for
deriving
supergravity observables from
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM. Instead of computing
{\cal O}(\tau)
in the planar limit as a function of
\lambda

\lambda\rightarrow\infty

\langle{\cal O}\rangle
as a function of
N
and take
N\rightarrow\infty

N

weak

{\cal O}(\lambda)-{\cal O}(0)

{\cal O}(0)
is the free result, is given in (

{\cal O}(\lambda)-{\cal O}(0)=\sum_{g=g_{*}}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{c}^{(g)}_{m}({\cal O})\lambda^{m}\qquad(\lambda\ll 1)

(10.13)

and comparing to (

\begin{split}{\cal O}_{\rm sugra}=-\lim_{m\rightarrow 0}\mathsf{c}^{(g_{*})}_{m}({\cal O})\end{split}

(10.14)

This is a tantalizing, and more mysterious, sense in which the strong coupling dynamics are directly encoded in the weak coupling expansion, beyond the usual tenets of integrability or resurgence.

What is the broader meaning of this for holography? It is natural to posit that this paradigm extends beyond the
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM/AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
duality to other string/M-theory compactifications. The general point of view is that in the large
N

Comment on modularity of string states

{modstring}

One reading of (
10.4
) is that any planar observable
{\cal O}(\tau)
which diverges as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
cannot be
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
invariant. From the CFT point of view, this includes anomalous dimensions of all operators which become single-trace at large
N
, e.g. twist-two operators. These dimensions are not non-perturbatively well-defined for all
\tau
, so one should not — and as we verify from a symmetry perspective,
must
not — treat them as
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
invariant. From the bulk point of view, one may ask how the
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
symmetry of string theory acts on stringy states. They are not
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
invariant, but one would like to characterize the representation of
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
, e.g. whether it is finite- or infinite-dimensional. The latter seems more likely, perhaps with the size of the representation controlled by
\lambda
. It is also unclear (to us) whether
all
states whose masses scale with
\alpha^{\prime}
mix under
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
, or whether (say) “short string” states with
\Delta\sim\lambda^{1/4}
and “semiclassical” states
[
109
]
with
\Delta\sim\sqrt{\lambda}
are distinguished by the action of
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

Statistics of the

{sec:SL2ZStatistics}

Motivated by the large
N

SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
ensemble. This will allow us to quantify the extent to which supergravity approximates an individual member of the
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
ensemble at large
N
.

11.1
Higher moments of CFT observables

We have seen that the spectral decomposition of S-duality-invariant observables
{\cal O}(\tau)
in
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM cleanly distinguishes the ensemble average over the conformal manifold with respect to the Zamolodchikov measure.
51
51

51

From now on, every reference to the ensemble average assumes the Zamolodchikov measure unless otherwise noted.
In particular, the spectral decomposition
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
encodes the deviation from the ensemble average
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle
, which we call
\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}(\tau)
:

\mathcal{O}(\tau)=\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle+\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}(\tau).

(11.1)

A natural question is the extent to which more intricate statistics of observables in the
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
ensemble are captured by their spectral overlaps.

For example, the variance of an observable in the
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
ensemble,

{\cal V}(\mathcal{O})\coloneqq\langle\mathcal{O}^{2}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle^{2}\,,

(11.2)

is given by the modular (ensemble) average of
\mathcal{O}^{2}_{\rm spec}(\tau)
:

{\cal V}(\mathcal{O})=\langle\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2}\rangle=\vvol({\cal F})^{-1}\int_{{\cal F}}{dxdy\over y^{2}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2}(\tau)=\Res_{s=1}R_{s}[\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2}].

(11.3)

Although the RS transform on the right-hand side of this equation could be computed directly by integrating the triple products of eigenfunctions using the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients of
[
54
,
110
,
44
]
(and reviewed in Appendix
B
), there is a shortcut. The ensemble average of
\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2}(\tau)
can be written in terms of the Petersson inner product of
\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}(\tau)
with itself,

\langle\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2}\rangle=\vvol({\cal F})^{-1}(\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec},\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec})\,,

(11.4)

which can be evaluated using Parseval’s identity, leading to the following elegant formula for the variance in terms of the spectral overlaps of
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
:

\boxed{{\cal V}(\mathcal{O})=\vvol({\cal F})^{-1}\left({1\over 4\pi i}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,|(\mathcal{O},E_{s})|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})^{2}\right)}

(11.5)

Thus the second moment of
\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}(\tau)
integrated over the fundamental domain
{\cal F}
is equal to the second moment of the spectral overlaps. Given the convergence properties of the spectral integral in the decomposition of
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
itself, this manifestly converges. We note that the variance (
11.5
) is non-vanishing for any CFT observable with a non-trivial spectral decomposition — which is to say, for any CFT observable that varies non-trivially on the conformal manifold.

For example, the variance of the integrated correlator
\mathcal{G}_{N}(\tau)
in the
SU(2)
theory is easily computed from its spectral decomposition as

{\cal V}(\mathcal{G}_{2})={\vvol({\cal F})^{-1}\over 4\pi i}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,\left({\pi\over\sin(\pi s)}s(1-s)(2s-1)^{2}\right)^{2}\Lambda(s)\Lambda(1-s)\approx 0.0214690

(11.6)

Recall that the average of
\mathcal{G}_{2}(\tau)
over the conformal manifold is given by
\langle\mathcal{G}_{2}\rangle={1\over 2}
.

The higher moments of
\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}(\tau)
are not simply given by the corresponding higher moments of the spectral overlaps. For example, the fourth moment
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{4}\rangle
is proportional to the inner product
(\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2},\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2})
, whose computation via the Parseval identity would involve the projections
(\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2},E_{s})
and
(\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2},\phi_{n})
. The latter can be evaluated by integrating the triple products of Appendix
B
weighted by spectral overlaps, but in particular they are not simply given by the square of the overlaps
(\mathcal{O},E_{s})
and
(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})
. For example, even if
(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})=0
, it is not necessarily true that
(\mathcal{O}_{\rm spec}^{2},\phi_{n})=0
. This is due to the nonzero triple product
K^{n}_{s_{1},s_{2}}
given in equation (
B.7
), which reflects the fact that the product of Eisenstein series has cusp forms in its spectral decomposition. These overlaps are written explicitly in (
12.3
).

11.2
The variance at large
N

Here we will study the contribution of
|({\cal O},E_{s})|^{2}
to the variance in the large
N
expansion. One expects on general principles that the variance
{\cal V}({\cal O})
is suppressed compared to the squared average
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle^{2}
at large
N
, and we will see explicitly that this is indeed the case.

We will start by considering the perturbative part, i.e. setting
f_{\rm np}(s)=0
. Since the variance involves the second moment of the full spectral overlap
(\mathcal{O},E_{s})
(rather than the rescaled overlap
\{{\cal O},E_{s}\}
), it will be convenient to define

\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g)}(s)\coloneqq{\pi\over\sin\pi s}s(1-s)\Lambda(s)f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(s)

(11.7)

so that the large
N
expansion of the spectral overlap can be written as

(\mathcal{O},E_{s})=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{2-2g}\left(N^{s-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g)}(s)+\varphi(s)^{-1}N^{-s}\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)\right).

(11.8)

On the critical line
\rre s={1\over 2}
, we then have the following expansion of the squared spectral overlap:

|(\mathcal{O},E_{s})|^{2}=\sum_{g_{1},g_{2}=0}^{\infty}N^{4-2(g_{1}+g_{2})}\left[N^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g_{1})}(1-s)\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g_{2})}(s)+N^{-2s}\varphi(s)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g_{1})}(1-s)\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g_{2})}(1-s)+(s\to 1-s)\right].

(11.9)

Assuming that
{\cal O}(\tau)
starts at
g=0
(the generalization to
g_{*}>0
is obvious), the leading contributions to the variance in the
1/N
expansion are thus given by

\displaystyle\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,|(\mathcal{O},E_{s})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\,2N^{3}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(s)\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(1-s)

(11.10)

\displaystyle+2\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,N^{4-2s}\varphi(s)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(1-s)\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(1-s)+\ldots,

where the
\ldots
denote higher-genus contributions whose
1/N
suppression is manifest. The first term is of order
N^{3}
, with manifestly positive coefficient. The spectral contour for the second term must be deformed to the right. The poles of the integrand for
{\rm Re\,}s>{1\over 2}
, in order of increasing
{\rm Re\,}s
, include a simple pole at
s=1
, a double pole at
s={3\over 2}
(double because
\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(1-s)
appears squared), and others with
{\rm Re\,}s>{3\over 2}
. The simple pole at
s=1
gives an order
N^{2}
term, while the double pole at
s={3\over 2}
gives order
N\log N
and order
N
terms. Using the definition of
\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)
and the relation

\Res_{s=1}\left[\Lambda(s)\Lambda(1-s)\right]={\pi\over 12}

(11.11)

one finds

\begin{split}{\cal V}({\cal O})=N^{3}\left({\vvol({\cal F})^{-1}\over 2\pi i}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(s)\,\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(1-s)\right)-{N^{2}\over 4}\left(f_{\rm p}^{(0)}(0)\right)^{2}+O(N\log N)\end{split}

(11.12)

As we note in the following subsection, the
1/N
expansion is asymptotically divergent.

Since the average is of order
N^{2}
, we thus conclude that the variance is parametrically suppressed at large
N
:

{{\cal V}(\mathcal{O})\over\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle^{2}}\sim{1\over N}\penalty\ .

(11.13)

An identical suppression holds if the leading contribution to
{\cal O}(\tau)
enters at higher genus
g_{*}>0
. In that case, one would have
{\cal V}(\mathcal{O})\sim N^{3-4g_{*}}
and
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle^{2}\sim N^{4-4g_{*}}
.

As an example, let’s again study the variance of the integrated correlator
\mathcal{G}_{N}(\tau)
(which recall has the special property that
f_{\rm np}(s)=0
), now at large
N
. The genus-zero contribution to the variance is given to leading order in
1/N
by

\displaystyle{\cal V}(\mathcal{G}_{N})

\displaystyle\approx{3N^{3}\over 2\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,{16\pi t^{2}\csch^{2}(\pi t)\zeta(-2it)\zeta(2it)\over 4t^{2}+9}+\ldots,

(11.14)

where we used
\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(0)}(s)
from (
7.33
) and the
\ldots
denote both contributions that are subleading at genus zero (i.e. the terms on the second line of (
11.10
)) and the parametrically suppressed contributions from higher genera. This can be evaluated to give

{2\pi^{2}\over 3N^{3}}{\cal V}(\mathcal{G}_{N})\approx 0.115551\,.

(11.15)

We can check this against the exact brute force evaluation of
{\cal V}(\mathcal{G}_{N})
at large but finite values of
N
. In Figure
3
we plot the result of direct numerical integration of
|(\mathcal{G}_{N},E_{s})|^{2}
for
N\leq 107
. The result nicely confirms (
11.15
).

Figure 3:
A plot of
{2\pi^{2}\over 3N^{3}}{\cal V}(\mathcal{G}_{N})
as a function of
N
for
N\leq 107
. This matches well onto large

).

The
1/N
suppression of the variance is a quantitative measure of the sense in which (super)gravity is self-averaging: in any typical member of the
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
ensemble at large
N
, observables
{\cal O}(\tau)
are well-approximated by their supergravity values,
{\cal O}_{\rm sugra}
. This may be compared to recent discussions of “apparent ensemble averaging” in holographic duality — where moduli are either not present, or fixed and identified between the bulk and boundary — in which gravity is said to reliably compute those quantities which are self-averaging in the large
N
random matrix theory that ostensibly governs black hole microstates (i.e. it computes large
N
limits of smooth functions of
N
which well-approximate the actual, discrete sequences at large integer
N
)
[
111
]
. The relation between gravity and averaging derived in our work, where the averaging is over couplings, is different. Both appear to be true for
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM/AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
holography.

One might ask how the re-introduction of non-perturbative
q\bar{q}
physics would modify the discussion here. From (
8.3
) we see that the non-perturbative contributions to the spectral overlap

are suppressed by a factor of

compared to the leading perturbative effects, as required by consistency with the AdS

effective action (as we describe in Section
6
). They would thus give contributions subleading in
1/N
compared to (
11.10
), and would not affect the suppression (
11.13
). The same argument applies to cusp form contributions
({\cal O},\phi_{n})\neq 0
. Therefore, the
1/N
suppression derived above is robust.

11.3
Non-perturbative corrections to the variance

We now show that, if the
\lambda\ll 1
expansion of
{\cal O}(\tau)
converges, the large
N
variance receives non-perturbative corrections in
N
, with strength controlled by the radius of convergence of the
\lambda\ll 1
expansion, precisely analogously to the non-perturbative effects in the ’t Hooft limit treated in Section
7.1
. In particular, we will see that these corrections appear at every genus.

The basic logic is the same as that described in Section
7.1
in the ’t Hooft limit. Let us again start by suppressing
q\bar{q}
contributions, setting
f_{\rm np}(s)=0
. It is convenient to grade the perturbative contributions to the variance by the total genus
g=g_{1}+g_{2}
, so that

{\cal V}(\mathcal{O})=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}N^{4-2g}{\cal V}^{(g)}(\mathcal{O})

(11.16)

where

\begin{split}{\cal V}^{(g)}(\mathcal{O})={{{\rm vol}({\cal F})^{-1}}\over 2\pi i}\sum_{g_{1}=0}^{g}\Bigg[{1\over N}&\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g_{1})}(s)\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g-g_{1})}(1-s)\\
+&\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\,N^{-2s}\varphi(s)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g_{1})}(1-s)\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}^{(g-g_{1})}(1-s)\Bigg]\end{split}

(11.17)

As derived in the previous subsection, the first term in (
11.17
) enters at a fixed order in
1/N
, while contour deformation of the second term to
{\rm Re\,}s>{1\over 2}
furnishes an expansion in powers of
1/N
. This sum is badly divergent. To see this, recall our previous result (
7.17
) (subject to an assumption) on the factorial growth of the residues of the perturbative part of the spectral overlap at negative odd half-integer values of
s
, and that
\mathfrak{f}_{\rm p}(s)
carries an additional factor of
\Lambda(s)
compared to
f_{\rm p}(s)

Remarks on the AdS/CFT Paradigm

12.1
Wormholes in moduli space and emergent averaged holographic duality

The results of Sections

In recent years, a new paradigm involving low-dimensional holographic dualities has emerged. 12.2
Generalizations

An obvious question is whether a version of the present mechanism in
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM extends to other string/M-theory vacua and their dual CFT pairs, in other spacetime dimensions and with less supersymmetry. It seems quite likely to us that this is the case. Let us articulate some possibilities in order of increasing strength. To set up, we consider sequences of CFTs
\mathcal{T}_{N}
with conformal manifolds
\mathcal{M}
that admit large
N
limits, with a (possibly trivial) generalized S-duality group,
\mathbb{S}

[
145

. We do not assume that the exactly marginal couplings are gauge couplings. We take there to be a large
N
“double-scaling limit” that organizes into a sum over genera à la ’t Hooft, possibly with boundaries, with couplings
\lambda_{i}
on which local observables in the limit theory
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
depend. In particular, we are interested in classes of theories where
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
includes a “’t Hooft coupling”
\lambda
, constructed from an exactly marginal coupling on
\mathcal{M}
, that controls the low-energy expansion of the bulk effective field theory; this is characteristic of CFTs with string (rather than M-) theory duals. So as to simplify language, we assume supersymmetry.

The most plausible scenario applies to sequences where
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
contains a strongly coupled regime, dual to two-derivative AdS supergravity.
57
57

57

[

Scenario I.
When

\mathbb{S}
, i.e. over the U-duality group of the dual string theory, localizes onto a strongly coupled theory at large
N
.

Where should we look to test such a scenario? The other maximally supersymmetric duality involving exactly marginal couplings, namely type IIB string theory on AdS
{}_{3}\times S^{3}\times T^{4}/K3
and its duality to the deformed symmetric orbifold theories, is a prime candidate (discussed further below). It may be technically simpler to consider other 4d SCFTs with AdS
{}_{5}\times M

We emphasize two relevant conceptual points here. First, for
\mathcal{N}=4

\mathbb{S}

\mathcal{N}=4

\mathcal{M}
and the S-duality-invariant measure on
{\cal F}

\langle{\cal O}\rangle=\overline{{\cal O}}
. However, more generally, they are not equal; see e.g.
[
148

for the nice example of 4d
\mathcal{N}=2

\mathcal{N}=4

\mathbb{S}
-invariant measure. One may take this as an argument in favor of the automorphic average, i.e. the ensemble average with respect to the
\mathbb{S}
-invariant measure.

Having said that, we may imagine the slightly stronger possibility:

Scenario II.
When
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
contains a strongly coupled regime, dual to an AdS supergravity, the ensemble average over
\mathcal{M}
localizes onto a strongly coupled theory at large
N

In other words,
every
AdS supergravity background dual to a limit CFT
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
may be thought of as an ensemble average.

58

\mathcal{T}_{N}
does not include this class of theories.
Of course, without S-duality, the only natural choice of measure is the Zamolodchikov measure, and harmonic analysis for
\mathbb{S}

Conclusion

{sec:conclusion}

An overarching message of this work is that harmonic analysis for
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
is an insightful and incisive tool for extracting the physical ramifications of S-duality of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM. This approach uncovered a direct role for ensemble averaging in the holographic duality with type IIB string theory on AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
, revealing supergravity as an emergent large
N
ensemble average. Our philosophy was simple, and familiar: to manifest the symmetries of the theory. By using an S-duality-invariant formulation from the start, tucking away all functional dependence on the coupling into an
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
eigenbasis, many non-perturbative phenomena presented themselves, often tied up with perturbative physics in a manner characteristic of strong-weak duality in quantum field theory.

Given their grounding in symmetry considerations — and how elemental they are mathematically — we are optimistic that spectral methods for S-duality have more physics in the offing. The results of this paper fell out very efficiently from few ingredients. Our computations are quite different than existing computations of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM observables. It would be productive to reformulate what we know in the spectral language.

We close by listing some specific future directions of keen interest.

More
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM observables:
As an aid to future progress we mention some observables one could analyze using

More substantial (and obvious) targets are

); this is due to the fact that for
s_{2}\neq s_{1},1-s_{1}
,
K_{s,s_{1},s_{2}}
is non-singular at
s=1
. In the main text we will encounter a rescaled solution of the inhomogeneous Laplace equation
\widetilde{F}_{r;s_{1},s_{2}}=\chi(s_{1})\chi(s_{2})F_{r;s_{1},s_{2}}
with
\chi(s)
given by (
8.7
), so it will be convenient to work with rescaled versions of these spectral coefficients

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{s,s_{1},s_{2}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\chi(s_{1})\chi(s_{2})K_{s,s_{1},s_{2}}

(B.11)

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}^{n}_{s_{1},s_{2}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widetilde{a}_{1}^{(n)}\chi(s_{1})\chi(s_{2})K^{n}_{s_{1},s_{2}}.

(B.12)

The spectral decomposition of
F_{r;s_{1},s_{2}}
is then given by inverting the action of the Laplacian on
E_{s_{1}}E_{s_{2}}

\displaystyle F_{r;s_{1},s_{2}}(\tau)=

\displaystyle(\Delta_{\tau}-\mu(r+1))^{-1}\left[H_{s_{1},s_{2}}(\tau)+E_{s_{1}+s_{2}}(\tau)+\varphi(s_{2})E_{1-s_{2}+s_{1}}(\tau)\right]

(B.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle E_{s_{1}+s_{2}}(\tau)\over\mu(s_{1}+s_{2})-\mu(r+1)}+{\varphi(s_{2})E_{1-s_{2}+s_{1}}(\tau)\over\mu(1-s_{2}+s_{1})-\mu(r+1)}+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds{K_{1-s,s_{1},s_{2}}\over\mu(s)-\mu(r+1)}E_{s}(\tau)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\widetilde{a}_{1}^{(n)}K^{n}_{s_{1},s_{2}}\over\mu_{n}-\mu(r+1)}\phi_{n}(\tau).

We pause to make some comments on the structure of the spectral decomposition (
B.13
), which will also broadly apply to the result (
B.22
) in the special case of
s_{2}=s_{1},1-s_{1}
that we consider next.

In the main text, we see that these solutions to the inhomogeneous Laplace equation appear at integer powers of
1/N
in the large
N
expansion of the integrated four-point function
{\cal F}_{N}(\tau)
of Section
8.2
. Thus the spectral coefficients in (

) furnish the large
N
expansion of the spectral coefficients of the integrated correlator. For example, the Eisenstein series on the first line are associated with the perturbative part of the overlap,
f_{\rm p}(s)
. In particular, they arise as certain residues of
N^{2-2g-s}{\pi s(1-s)\over\sin(\pi s)}f_{\rm p}^{(g)}(1-s)E_{s}^{*}
in the spectral decomposition in the large
N
limit. In the application to
{\cal F}_{N}(\tau)
,
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are odd half-integers, and the Eisenstein series in (
B.13
) arise as the residues of the perturbative part of the spectral integrand (at different genera) at certain integer values of
s
. On the other hand, the third term on the first line of (
B.13
) corresponds to a genuinely non-perturbative contribution to the spectral overlap
(\mathcal{O},E_{s})
. In particular, we would have
\Lambda(s)f_{\rm np}^{(g,m)}(s)\propto{K_{1-s,s_{1},s_{2}}\over\mu(s)-\mu(r+1)}
for appropriate values of
g,m
in the notation of Section
7.4
. Indeed, this term cleanly exhibits
|s|\to\infty

6.10
) of non-perturbative contributions to the spectral overlap

K_{1-s,s_{1},s_{2}}\sim\Gamma(|s|),\quad|s|\to\infty.

(B.14)

The combination
K_{1-s,s_{1},s_{2}}\over\mu(s)-\mu(r+1)
is also characterized by a finite number of spurious poles away from the critical contour in the complex
s
-plane. This is due to the fact that the solutions to the inhomogeneous Laplace equations are not themselves strictly square-integrable as they exhibit power-law growth at the cusp (as discussed in Section
8

N

(\mathcal{O},\phi_{n})\propto{K^{n}_{s_{1},s_{2}}\over\mu_{n}-\mu(r+1)}
.

B.2
s_{1}=s_{2}

We now consider the special case of
s_{2}=s_{1}=s
. The reflected case of
s_{2}=1-s_{1}
can be treated essentially identically. In this case, the right-hand side of (
B.1
) has the following growth at the cusp

E_{s}(\tau)^{2}\sim y^{2s}+2\varphi(s)y+\varphi(s)^{2}y^{2-2s}.

(B.15)

We proceed assuming that
\rre s>{1\over 2}
, so that the following combination is square integrable

H_{s}(\tau)\coloneqq E_{s}(\tau)^{2}-E_{2s}(\tau)-2\varphi(s)\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau).

(B.16)

where
\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)
is the regular part of the Eisenstein series at
s=1
,

\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)\coloneqq\lim_{s\to 1}\left(E_{s}(\tau)-{3\over\pi(s-1)}\right)

(B.17)

It is not an eigenfunction of the Laplacian

\Delta_{\tau}\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)=-{3\over\pi}

(B.18)

and has the following Fourier decomposition

\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)=y-{3\over\pi}\log y+{6\over\pi}\left(12\log A-\log 4\pi\right)+{12\over\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\cos(2\pi kx)\sigma_{1}(k)e^{-2\pi ky}\over k},

(B.19)

where
A
is Glaisher’s constant,
\log A={1\over 12}-\zeta^{\prime}(-1)
. Its role in (
B.16
) is to subtract the linear term in (
B.15
).
H_{s}(\tau)
then has the following spectral decomposition

H_{s}(\tau)=K_{s}+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds^{\prime}\,K_{1-s^{\prime},s,s}E_{s^{\prime}}(\tau)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{a}_{1}^{(n)}K^{n}_{s,s}\nu_{n}(\tau).

(B.20)

We note that there is now a constant term in the spectral decomposition, due to the fact that
K_{s^{\prime},s,s}
is now singular at
s^{\prime}=1
and that there are constants in the Fourier decomposition of
\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)
. The constant term is given by

K_{s}=\Res_{s^{\prime}=1}K_{s^{\prime},s,s}-2\varphi(s)\omega=-{3\over\pi}\varphi^{\prime}(s),

(B.21)

where
\omega\coloneqq{6\over\pi}\left(12\log A-\log(4\pi)\right)
is the constant in the Fourier decomposition of
\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)
(cf. (
B.19
)).

Assembling the pieces, this leads to the following spectral decomposition for
F_{r;s,s}(\tau)

\displaystyle F_{r;s,s}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{\tau}-\mu(r+1)\right)^{-1}E_{s}(\tau)^{2}

(B.22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle E_{2s}(\tau)\over\mu(2s)-\mu(r+1)}+2\varphi(s)\left({3\over\pi\mu(r+1)^{2}}-{1\over\mu(r+1)}\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)\right)-{K_{s}\over\mu(r+1)}

\displaystyle+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{\rre s^{\prime}={1\over 2}}ds^{\prime}{K_{1-s^{\prime},s,s}\over\mu(s^{\prime})-\mu(r+1)}E_{s^{\prime}}(\tau)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\widetilde{a}_{1}^{(n)}K^{n}_{s,s}\over\mu_{n}-\mu(r+1)}\phi_{n}(\tau).

B.3
A worked example:
r=3
,
s_{1}=s_{2}={3\over 2}

To elucidate the sense in which the perturbative and non-perturbative contributions (in
y^{-1}
) to
F_{r;s_{1},s_{2}}
are contained in its spectral decomposition, we will study the example of
r=3
,
s_{1}=s_{2}={3\over 2}
in detail. In particular, we will study the modular invariant

{appb3}

{\cal E}_{3;{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}(\tau)=4\zeta(3)^{2}F_{3;{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}(\tau)

(B.23)

that appears as the coefficient of the
D^{6}R^{4}
interaction in the low-energy expansion of the effective action of type IIB string theory and at order
1/N
in the large
N
expansion of the integrated
\langle 2222\rangle
correlator
{\cal F}_{N}
of Section
8.2
.

It will turn out that reproducing the perturbative expansion of the zero-instanton sector of this function from the spectral decomposition is an instructive exercise. In
[
52
]
, it is shown that the zero mode of
\mathcal{E}_{3;{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}
is given by

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{E}_{3;{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}\right)_{0}(y)=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle 2\over 3}\zeta(3)^{2}y^{3}+{4\over 3}\zeta(3)\zeta(2)y+4\zeta(4)y^{-1}+{4\over 27}\zeta(6)y^{-3}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f^{P}_{k,-k}(y)

(B.24)

where

f^{P}_{k,-k}(y)={32\pi\over 315k^{3}}\sigma_{2}(k)^{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}q_{3}^{i,j}(\pi ky)K_{i}(2\pi ky)K_{j}(2\pi ky).

(B.25)

The first four terms in (
B.24
) are the perturbative contributions to the zero mode, while the sum over terms involving (
B.25
) capture non-perturbative
(q\bar{q})^{k}
contributions.
Here the coefficients
q_{3}^{i,j}
are given by
[
52
]

\displaystyle q_{3}^{0,0}(z)

\displaystyle=z(-512z^{4}+48z^{2}-15)

(B.26)

\displaystyle q_{3}^{0,1}(z)

\displaystyle=-128z^{4}-12z^{2}-15=q_{3}^{1,0}(z)

\displaystyle q_{3}^{1,1}(z)

\displaystyle=512z^{5}+16z^{3}+33z-15z^{-1}.

We would like to understand how (
B.24
) is reproduced by the spectral decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3;{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}(\tau)=

\displaystyle 4\zeta(3)^{2}\bigg[{1\over 6}E_{3}(\tau)+2\varphi(3/2)\left({3\over 144\pi}+{1\over 12}\widehat{E}_{1}(\tau)\right)+{1\over 12}K_{3/2}

(B.27)

\displaystyle+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,{K_{1-s,{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}\over\mu(s)+12}E_{s}(\tau)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\widetilde{a}_{1}^{(n)}K^{n}_{{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}\over\mu_{n}+12}\phi_{n}(\tau)\bigg].

In particular, the zero mode is given by

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{E}_{3;{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}\right)_{0}(y)=

\displaystyle 4\zeta(3)^{2}\bigg[{1\over 6}\left(y^{3}+\varphi(3)y^{-2}\right)+2\varphi(3/2)\left({3\over 144\pi}+{1\over 12}\left(y-{3\over\pi}\log y+\omega\right)\right)+{1\over 12}K_{3/2}

(B.28)

\displaystyle+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{\rre s={1\over 2}}ds\,{K_{1-s,{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}\over\mu(s)+12}\left(y^{s}+\varphi(s)y^{1-s}\right)\bigg].

It is not immediately clear how (
B.28
) is consistent with (
B.24
). For instance, the latter has terms of order
y^{-1}
and
y^{-3}
that are not obviously present in the former, while the former has terms of order
\log y
,
y^{-2}
and
y^{0}
that must be cancelled in order to reproduce the latter. The resolution is of course contained in the spectral integral in the second line of (
B.28
) — the spectral integrand has poles in the complex
s
plane with residues that precisely resolve these perturbative discrepancies upon deformation of the spectral contour so that all such poles lie to one side of the contour. In particular, the integrand
{K_{1-s,{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}\over\mu(s)+12}\varphi(s)y^{1-s}
has poles at
s=1,2,3,4
in the
s
half-plane to the right of the critical contour (along with reflected poles in the left half-plane). We now imagine deforming the contour to
\rre s>4
. In doing so, we pick up residues at the intervening poles. The residue at
s=1
precisely cancels the spurious constant terms and
\log y
term. The residue at
s=3
exactly cancels the spurious term of order
y^{-2}
. And finally, the residues at
s=2
and
s=4
exactly reproduce the missing terms of order
y^{-1}
and
y^{-3}
, respectively. Thus the deformed spectral integral precisely accounts for the non-perturbative (instanton-anti-instanton) contributions to the zero-instanton sector of
{\cal E}
. In particular, we have

{4\zeta(3)^{2}\over 2\pi i}\int_{\rre s>4}ds\,{K_{1-s,{3\over 2},{3\over 2}}\over\mu(s)+12}\varphi(s)y^{1-s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f^{P}_{k,-k}(y),

(B.29)

which we have checked numerically.
