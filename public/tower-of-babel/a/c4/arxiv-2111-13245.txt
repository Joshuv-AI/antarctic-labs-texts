Well-posedness of an integro-differential model for active Brownian particles Thanks:
Maria Bruna, Martin Burger, Antonio Esposito, Simon Schulz

Abstract
We propose a general strategy for solving nonlinear integro-differential evolution problems with periodic boundary conditions, where no direct maximum/minimum principle is available. This is motivated by the study of recent macroscopic models for active Brownian particles with repulsive interactions, consisting of advection-diffusion processes in the space of particle position and orientation. We focus on one of such models, namely a semilinear parabolic equation with a nonlinear active drift term, whereby the velocity depends on the particle orientation and angle-independent overall particle density (leading to a nonlocal term by integrating out the angular variable). The main idea of the existence analysis is to exploit a-priori estimates from (approximate) entropy dissipation. The global existence and uniqueness of weak solutions is shown using a two-step Galerkin approximation with appropriate cutoff in order to obtain nonnegativity, an upper bound on the overall density and preserve a-priori estimates. Our analysis naturally includes the case of finite systems, corresponding to the case of a finite number of directions. The Duhamel principle is then used to obtain additional regularity of the solution, namely continuity in time-space. Motivated by the class of initial data relevant for the application, which includes perfectly aligned particles (same orientation), we extend the well-posedness result to very weak solutions allowing distributional initial data with low regularity.



keywords

active particles, space-periodic problems, parabolic equations, Galerkin approximation, periodic heat kernel

Introduction

In this paper, we present a well-posedness theory (existence, uniqueness, and regularity) for an integro-differential equation describing a system of interacting active Brownian particles.
Active Brownian particles are a model system for self-propelled particles, and have wide-ranging applications such as molecular motors, bacteria, animal swarms and pedestrian flows (
cf.

e.g.

[
5
,
8
,
12
,
13
]
). Denote by
{\bf X}_{i}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Theta_{i}\in\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
the position and orientation of particle number
i
, respectively. The evolution of the system with
N
indistinguishable particles is given by the following stochastic differential equations:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{\bf X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2D_{T}}\mathrm{d}{\bf W}_{i}+v_{0}{\bf e}(\Theta_{i})\mathrm{d}t-\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla u(({\bf X}_{i}-{\bf X}_{j})/\varepsilon)\mathrm{d}t,

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2D_{R}}\mathrm{d}W_{i},

(1b)

where
D_{T}
and
D_{R}
are the translational and rotational diffusion coefficients, respectively,
{\bf W}_{i}
and
W_{i}
are
N
independent Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}
respectively,
v_{0}
is the self-propulsion speed,
{\bf e}(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
, and
u
is the pairwise interaction potential, assumed to be radially symmetric, purely repulsive and short-ranged (
\varepsilon\ll 1
). In contrast to passive Brownian particles (that is
D_{R}=0
in
eq.

1b
), active Brownian particles perform a biased Brownian walk in the direction given by their orientation.

Starting from
eq.

1
and considering the associated Fokker–Planck equation for the joint probability density in
(\Omega\times\mathbb{T})^{N}
, one can derive the BBGKY hierarchy of coupled equations for the marginal probabilities. Depending on the assumptions on the interaction
u
, one can obtain different continuum models for the one-particle marginal density
[
4
]
. One of such models is studied by Speck et al.
[
16
]
, where they obtain a closed equation by assuming that the system is homogeneous and neglecting the time-dependence of the pair correlation function. Following a rescaling of time and space, leading to a rescaled velocity or Péclet number
\text{Pe}=v_{0}/\sqrt{D_{R}D_{T}}\geq 0
, the resulting equation is (see
[
4
]
for details)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\text{Pe}\nabla\cdot[f(1-\rho){\bf e}(\theta)]=D_{e}\Delta f+\partial_{\theta}^{2}f,

(2)

where
D_{e}\in(0,1]
is an effective translational diffusion, the unknown
f=f(t,{\bf x},\theta)
is the phase space density and
\rho=\rho(t,{\bf x})
is the spatial density obtained through

\displaystyle\rho(t,{\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(t,{\bf x},\theta)~\mathrm{d}\theta.

Both densities are scaled such that they have mass
\phi\in[0,1)
; this represents the effective occupied fraction of particles in
\Omega
.
By integrating
eq.

2
with respect to
\theta
, we obtain the following equation for
\rho

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\text{Pe}\nabla\cdot[(1-\rho){\bf p}]=D_{e}\Delta\rho,

(3)

where
{\bf p}(t,{\bf x})
is the polarisation, defined by
{\bf p}(t,{\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\bf e}(\theta)f(t,{\bf x},\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta
. As we shall see later on, the equation for
\rho
,
eq.

3
, plays an important role in the analysis of
eq.

2
in order to determine the sign of the nonlocality (in angle)
1-\rho
, which is the reason why usual maximum/minimum principle does not work in our case. It is also interesting to consider
eq.

2
in one spatial dimension,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
. In this case, the set of possible orientations reduces to
\theta=0,\pi
, or right- and left-moving particles, and the rotational diffusion reduces to discrete jumps between these two orientations. The one-dimensional version of
eq.

2
reads

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}f_{R}+\text{Pe}\partial_{x}[f_{R}(1-\rho)]&=\partial_{xx}f_{R}+f_{L}-f_{R},\\
\partial_{t}f_{L}-\text{Pe}\partial_{x}[f_{L}(1-\rho)]&=\partial_{xx}f_{L}+f_{R}-f_{L}.\end{aligned}

(4)

where
f_{R}
and
f_{L}
are the densities of right- and left-moving particles, respectively, and the space density is
\rho=f_{R}+f_{L}
. This model is derived in
[
4
]
from the one-dimensional version of
eq.

1
, and in
[
14
]
as the hydrodynamic limit of an active lattice gas. In fact, the two-dimensional model
eq.

2
can also be derived starting from an active lattice gas model, with a partial exclusion rule (only one particle is allowed by site, but particles can exchange sites in the diffusive step). This explains why models
eqs.

2
and
4
present linear diffusion. If instead one considers a complete exclusion rule (resulting in an asymmetric simple exclusion process), nonlinearities of cross-diffusion type (involving terms
\rho\nabla f,f\nabla\rho
) appear in the continuum model
[
4
]
.
Equation

4
coincides with the crowded version (with the additional
1-\rho
term in the advection) of the Goldstein–Taylor model
[
11
,
17
]
, and for this reason we refer to it as the
crowded Goldstein–Taylor model
.
We shall see that well-posedness for the above system follows directly from that of
eq.

2
, as consequence of our strategy based on a two-step approximation.

To the best of our knowledge, most of the results in literature focus on time-periodic evolution equations or initial-value problems with Neumann or zero-flux boundary conditions,
cf.

e.g.

[
15
]
, while periodicity in space has not been studied in detail. Moreover, the mobility
f(1-\rho)
in the drift term does not yield a maximum principle due to the nonlocal dependence of
\rho
on
f
. In particular, it is not immediately clear whether one can use the theory of Ladyzhenskaya and collaborators
[
15
]
, or that established by Amann
[
1
]
, based on fixed point theorems.
Moreover, we note that the equation does not present a usual Wasserstein gradient flow structure, even in the absence of angular diffusion, again because of the mobility
f(1-\rho)
in the transport drift term,
cf.
for instance,
[
3
]
. In this regard, we also point out that this problem does not fall in the theory of generalised Wasserstein gradient flows for equations with nonlinear mobility studied for instance in
[
9
,
7
]
and the references therein, as the mobility in
eq.

2
is nonlocal in addition to being, effectively, nonlinear.

For these reasons, we provide a valuable strategy to tackle space-periodic nonlinear evolution PDEs with a nonlocal in angle drift term and periodic boundary conditions by means of a double space-discretisation purely using periodicity. More precisely, in order to prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions in the sense of
definition

2
, we first consider a Galerkin approximation with respect to the angular variable
\theta
. We emphasise that, in the sequel, we make the choice to expand in terms of the sine and cosine basis instead of the complex exponentials; this is so that all coefficients are real-valued, which is required to make sense of the positive part in the parabolic system that we study;
cf.

eq.

13
in
section

3
. Existence of solutions to such a system is proven by means of a further Galerkin approximation in the space variable, again taking into account periodicity of the problem in the choice of the basis.

In this paper we provide the first rigorous result on a macroscopic model for Brownian active particles in phase space, thus starting with the possibly simplest model without cross-diffusion effects. However, it still carries two characteristic issues of these problems, namely the nonlinear drift and the nonlocal effects due to the overall density
\rho
. Note that here we work in the setting of a continuous angular variable, with discrete angles or directions as in the one-dimensional version of the model we directly see the relation to nonlinear parabolic systems.
We stress that this strategy may be extended to the analysis of a larger class of space-periodic evolution equations, even some cross-diffusion systems, see
[
4
]
. This will indeed be the object of further investigation.
Let us mention at this point the importance of the space-periodic setting also for pattern formation effects. A closed system with no-flux boundary conditions might lead to very different stationary flux behaviour and may not lead to the phase separation phenomena observed in many microscopic simulations and discussed in
[
4
]
.

This article is organised as follows.
In
section

2
we provide the general set of assumptions and the summary of the main results.

Section

3
contains the proof of existence and uniqueness of weak solutions of
eq.

2
and
eq.

4
. Here we provide a detailed explanation of the strategy used, which can be potentially adapted to a larger class of space-periodic nonlinear evolution problems. More precisely, well-posedness is proven via a two-step Galerkin discretisation. This involves solving a semilinear parabolic system outside what is covered by standard parabolic theory, not due to particular complications, but rather the periodic nature of the problem. In
section

4
we obtain higher regularity of solutions by applying the Duhamel principle.

Section

5
is devoted to enlarging the class of initial data, among them those presenting a Dirac delta in the angular variable. This is relevant to the application of active Brownian particles, as it corresponds to all particles being originally perfectly aligned (same orientation).
Finally, the appendices collect some technical results used in the paper.

2
Preliminaries and main results

In what follows, we refer to
eq.

2
and
eq.

4
as 2D and 1D problems, respectively, in reference to the dimension of the spatial domain
\Omega
. Note that, mathematically, the two-dimensional problem
eq.

2
lives in the three-dimensional space
\Upsilon:=\Omega\times\mathbb{T}
.
We choose the spatial domain
\Omega
to be the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, where
d\in\{1,2\}
and
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
. This imposes periodic boundary conditions as considered in
[
4
,
16
]
. The
2\pi
-period is chosen by mathematical convenience, so that in the two-dimensional problem
\Upsilon\equiv\mathbb{T}^{3}
. In the sequel, we say that a function

is

-periodic
(for

) if, for all
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and all

, there holds
f(\bm{\xi})=f(\bm{\xi}+2\pi\mathbf{e}_{j})
, where
\{\mathbf{e}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
are the standard unit vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

Definition 1
(Function spaces).

Let
M\in\{1,2,3\}
. We define the following Banach spaces

\displaystyle L^{p}_{per}(\mathbb{T}^{M}):=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}\big|f\text{ is a.e.~}\mathbb{T}^{M}\text{-periodic, and }f\in L^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{M})\},

\displaystyle H^{m}_{per}(\mathbb{T}^{M}):=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}\big|f\text{ is a.e.~}\mathbb{T}^{M}\text{-periodic, and }f\in H^{m}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{M})\},

with
p\geq 1,m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
endowed with the norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}((0,2\pi)^{M})}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{m}((0,2\pi)^{M})}
, respectively. The topological dual of
H^{m}_{per}(\mathbb{T}^{M})
is denoted by
(H^{m}_{per})^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{M})
. Similarly, we define the spaces

C^{k}_{per}(\bar{\mathbb{T}}^{M}):=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}\big|f\text{ is }\mathbb{T}^{M}\text{-periodic, and }f\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{M})\},

for
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\cup\{\infty\}
, equipped with their respective norm
\|\cdot\|_{C^{k}([0,2\pi]^{M})}
.

For every
f,g:\Upsilon\to\mathbb{R}
, we use the notation
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}
to denote both the
L^{2}
scalar product in
\Upsilon
and duality between
(H^{1})^{\prime}
and
H^{1}
as a Gelfand triple with

, while for any

all such pairings (in

and

) are denoted by

. Moreover, let us specify that, by slight abuse of notation, any curve

will map

, for any Banach space
B
we consider.

Definition 2
(Weak solution to 2D model).

Let
f_{0}\in L^{2}_{per}(\Upsilon)
be such that

\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{0}(\mathbf{x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\in[0,1]\qquad\text{for a.e.~}\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

(5)

A curve

is a

to
eq.

2
if
f\in C([0,T];L^{2}_{per}(\Upsilon))\cap L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{per}(\Upsilon))

\begin{split}\left\langle\partial_{t}f(t),\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!&=\!\left\langle\text{Pe}(1\!-\!\rho(t))f(t){\bf e}(\theta),\nabla\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!-\!\left\langle D_{e}\nabla f(t),\nabla\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!-\!\left\langle\partial_{\theta}f(t),\partial_{\theta}\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon},\\

with periodic boundary conditions on
\Upsilon

Our first main result is the following.

Theorem 3
(Existence and uniqueness for the 2D model).

Let

In the proof of

An analogous theorem is obtained for the 1D model

.
We then consider the regularity of weak solutions. More precisely, we provide the following regularity result up to the initial time.

Theorem 5
(Regularity for the 2D model).

Let

. Then the unique weak solution of

6

.
Furthermore, if

The previous statement also holds true for the 1D model

). Note that initial data of this form are of significantly lower regularity than considered in the original

Theorem 6
(Existence of very weak solutions for 2D model).

Let

,

be a nonnegative element of

and

. Then there exists a very weak solution of

Note that the result of

3
Well-posedness

In this section we consider the analysis of the 1D and 2D models. We tackle the 2D model first, and show in
section

3.5
that the result for the 1D model follows from that of the 2D model.

A crucial ingredient for the well-posedness of
eq.

2
and
eq.

4
is that
\rho\in[0,1]
for all times. Equation
eq.

3
for the density
\rho
does not have an obvious maximum or minimum principle, since the drift term neither has a sign nor is zero. One can show that any solution to
eq.

3
(provided it exists) satisfies
\rho\leq 1
, starting with an initial datum
\rho_{0}\in[0,1]
. However, at this early stage of the analysis, the nonnegativity of the solution is a delicate issue. For this reason we consider a variant of
eq.

2
, where we have
(1-(\rho)_{+})_{+}
in the transport term. It will turn out that we are indeed solving the original equation,
i.e.

eq.

2
. This final step is shown in Step 5 of the Proof of
theorem

3
;
cf.

section

3.3
. The modified version of
eq.

2
we start with is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\text{Pe}\nabla\cdot(f(1-(\rho)_{+})_{+}{\bf e}(\theta))=D_{e}\Delta f+\partial_{\theta}^{2}f,

(7)

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{x},\theta)=f_{0}({\bf x},\theta),

in
\Upsilon=\mathbb{T}^{3}
, where
(a)_{+}=\max\{a,0\}
, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Later we show that we recover the solution to the original equation
eq.

2
(see
section

3.3
). We remind the reader that the nonlocality in the drift term means that there is no inherent maximum/minimum principle for
eq.

7
. Our strategy for solving the equation is to construct an approximating sequence
(f^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
whose limit solves
eq.

7
.

3.1
Approximated system in angle

We introduce the following Galerkin approximation in
\theta
. Given the periodic boundary conditions and the term
{\bf e}(\theta)
in
eq.

7
, it is convenient to consider the basis
\{1,\cos(k(\cdot)),\sin(k(\cdot))\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
L^{2}_{per}([0,2\pi))
. Since
f_{0}({\bf x},\cdot)\in L^{2}_{per}([0,2\pi))
for a.e.
{\bf x}\in\Omega
, we can write

f_{0}({\bf x},\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}(0,{\bf x})+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq 1}\big(a_{k}(0,{\bf x})\cos(k\theta)+b_{k}(0,\mathbf{x})\sin(k\theta)\big),

(8)

where
a_{k}(0,\cdot),b_{k}(0,\cdot)
are the Fourier coefficients of
f_{0}(\cdot,\theta)
.
Note that the requirement
0\leq\rho_{0}({\bf x})\leq 1
(
cf.

definition

2
) implies that
0\leq a_{0}(0,{\bf x})\leq 1
for a.e.
{\bf x}\in\Omega
.

For some fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the finite-dimensional space

X_{n}=\text{span}\{\cos(k(\cdot)),\sin(k(\cdot))\}_{k=0,\dots,n}.

(9)

We try to find a solution
f^{n}
to the weak-formulation
eq.

6
adapted to
eq.

7
of the approximated system given by

\left\langle\partial_{t}f^{n},\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!=\!\left\langle\text{Pe}(1\!-\!(\rho^{n})_{+})_{+}f^{n}(t){\bf e}(\theta),\nabla\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!-\!\left\langle D_{e}\nabla f^{n},\nabla\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!-\!\left\langle\partial_{\theta}f^{n},\partial_{\theta}\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon},

(10)

where
\rho^{n}=\int f^{n}\mathrm{d}\theta
, for test functions
\varphi=\chi(\theta)\psi({\bf x})
with
\chi\in X_{n}
and
\psi\in H^{1}_{\text{per}}(\Omega)
. We can express
f^{n}
as

f^{n}(t,{\bf x},\theta):=\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}^{n}(t,\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(a_{k}^{n}(t,\mathbf{x})\cos(k\theta)+b_{k}^{n}(t,\mathbf{x})\sin(k\theta)\big),

(11)

where the Fourier coefficients
a_{k}^{n}(t,{\bf x}),b_{k}^{n}(t,{\bf x})
need to be determined. We note that
\rho^{n}
above corresponds to the constant coefficient, that is,

\rho^{n}(t,{\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{n}(t,{\bf x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\equiv a_{0}^{n}(t,{\bf x}).

(12)

From
eq.

10
we obtain a
(2n+1)
-dimensional system of semilinear parabolic equations for
a_{k}^{n}(t,{\bf x}),b_{k}^{n}(t,{\bf x})
. In particular, testing
eq.

10

\chi(\theta)
in the basis of
X_{n}
gives the equation for the corresponding Fourier coefficient of

. More precisely, choosing
\chi=1
results in

\partial_{t}a_{0}^{n}+\text{Pe}\nabla\cdot\left[(1-(a_{0}^{n})_{+})_{+}{\bf J}_{0}^{n}\right]=D_{e}\Delta a_{0}^{n},\quad{\bf J}_{0}^{n}=(a_{1}^{n},b_{1}^{n}).

(13a)

Repeating for
\chi=\cos(k\theta),\sin(k\theta)
for
k=1,\dots,n
leads to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}a_{k}^{n}+\frac{\text{Pe}}{2}\nabla\cdot\left[(1-(a_{0}^{n})_{+})_{+}{\bf J}_{k}^{n}\right]

\displaystyle=D_{e}\Delta a_{k}^{n}-k^{2}a_{k}^{n},

(13b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}b_{k}^{n}+\frac{\text{Pe}}{2}\nabla\cdot\left[(1-(a_{0}^{n})_{+})_{+}{\bf Q}_{k}^{n}\right]

\displaystyle=D_{e}\Delta b_{k}^{n}-k^{2}b_{k}^{n},

(13c)

in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, where the velocities
{\bf J}_{k}^{n}
and
{\bf Q}_{k}^{n}
are given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bf J}_{1}^{n}&=(2a_{0}^{n}+a_{2}^{n},b_{2}^{n}),&{\bf Q}_{1}^{n}&=(b_{2}^{n},2a_{0}^{n}-a_{2}^{n}),\\
{\bf J}_{l}^{n}&=(a_{l+1}^{n}+a_{l-1}^{n},b_{l+1}^{n}-b_{l-1}^{n}),&{\bf Q}_{l}^{n}&=(b_{l+1}^{n}+b_{l-1}^{n},a_{l-1}^{n}-a_{l+1}^{n}),\\
{\bf J}_{n}^{n}&=(a_{n-1}^{n},-b_{n-1}^{n}),&{\bf Q}_{n}^{n}&=(b_{n-1}^{n},a_{n-1}^{n}),\end{aligned}

(13d)

for

, with periodic boundary conditions on
\Omega
and initial conditions

given by the corresponding Fourier coefficients of

in
eq.

8
.

Provided that
eq.

13
can be solved, we obtain an explicit description of each

Lemma 8
.

Given
f_{0}\in L^{2}_{per}(\Upsilon)
nonnegative such that its corresponding
\rho_{0}
satisfies
\rho_{0}({\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{0}({\bf x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\in[0,1]
for a.e.
{\bf x}\in\Omega

definition

2

eq.

6
.

Proof.

Let us consider two weak solutions to
eq.

2
,
f_{1},f_{2}
, in the sense of
definition

2
, and set
\bar{f}:=f_{1}-f_{2}
to be their difference. We refer to
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
as the corresponding space densities, and to
\bar{\rho}:=\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}
as their difference. By means of a direct computation,
\bar{f}
is a weak solution to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{f}+\text{Pe}\nabla\cdot((1-\rho_{1})\bar{f}{\bf e}(\theta)-\bar{\rho}f_{2}{\bf e}(\theta))=D_{e}\Delta\bar{f}+\partial_{\theta}^{2}\bar{f},

and
\bar{\rho}
solves
\partial_{t}\bar{\rho}+\text{Pe}\nabla\cdot((1-\rho_{1})\bar{{\bf p}}-\bar{\rho}{\bf p}_{2})=D_{e}\Delta\bar{\rho}
, where we remind the reader
\bar{{\bf p}}(t,{\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{f}(t,{\bf x},\theta)\mathrm{d}{\theta}
and
{\bf p}_{2}(t,{\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{2}(t,{\bf x},\theta)\mathrm{d}{\theta}
, for any
(t,{\bf x},\theta)\in[0,T]\times\Upsilon
. Note that, by directly integrating the previous equation for
\bar{\rho}
,
\int_{\Omega}\bar{\rho}({\bf x})\mathrm{d}{\bf x}=0
, and thus
\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{f}(t,{\bf x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}=0
. Motivated by this latter “mean-zero condition”, we consider the space

H^{1}_{z}(\Upsilon):=\left\{f\in H^{1}(\Upsilon):\int_{\Upsilon}f(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi=0\right\},

and its dual
(H_{z}^{1})^{\prime}(\Upsilon)
, where subscript
z
refers to the mean-zero property in question. Let
L:=(-\Delta)^{-1}:(H^{1}_{z})^{\prime}(\Upsilon)\to(H^{1}_{z})(\Upsilon)
be the inverse of the solution operator for the Poisson equation with periodic boundary condition, that is
Lg=u
, where
\int_{\Upsilon}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=\left\langle g,\varphi\right\rangle
for all
\varphi\in H^{1}_{z}(\Upsilon)
.
In the following we shall use that
L
maps continuously
L^{2}_{z}(\Upsilon)
into
H^{2}_{z}(\Upsilon)
. Moreover, we note the self-adjointness property

\left\langle\varphi,L\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle(-\Delta)\circ(-\Delta)^{-1}\varphi,L\varphi\right\rangle=\|\nabla L\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}=\langle L\varphi,\varphi\rangle,

for every
\varphi\in H^{1}_{z}(\Upsilon)
, where the duality product is understood in the sense of
L^{2}(\Upsilon)
. Similarly, using the commutativity of the partial derivatives, for any
\varphi\in H^{1}_{z}(\Upsilon)
,

\langle\partial_{\theta}\varphi,\partial_{\theta}L\varphi\rangle=\langle(-\Delta)\partial_{\theta}L\varphi,\partial_{\theta}L\varphi\rangle=\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}L\varphi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}.

Let
\lambda>0
. For the next lines of computation, we use
definition

2
, Young’s inequality, the bound
\rho_{1}\leq 1
, and the self-adjointness property. We drop the time dependence for ease of presentation. We obtain

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\!\left(\!\|\nabla L\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\!+\!\lambda\|\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\!\right)&\!=\left\langle\partial_{t}\bar{f},L\bar{f}\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle\partial_{t}\bar{\rho},\bar{\rho}\right\rangle\\
&\!=\!-D_{e}\!\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\!-\!\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}L\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\!-\!\lambda\!D_{e}\!\|\nabla\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
&\quad\!+\!\text{Pe}\left\langle(1-\rho_{1})\bar{f}{\bf e}(\theta),\nabla L\bar{f}\right\rangle\!-\!\text{Pe}\lambda\!\left\langle\bar{\rho}{\bf p}_{2},\!\nabla\bar{\rho}\right\rangle\\
&\quad+\!\text{Pe}\lambda\left\langle(1\!-\!\rho_{1})\bar{{\bf p}},\nabla\bar{\rho}\right\rangle\!-\!\text{Pe}\!\left\langle\bar{\rho}f_{2}{\bf e}(\theta),\!\nabla L\bar{f}\right\rangle\\
&\!\leq\!-D_{e}\left(1-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}-\frac{\lambda}{\sigma}\pi\right)\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\\
&\quad-\!\lambda\!\left(\!D_{e}\!-\!\frac{\text{Pe}^{2}\sigma}{D_{e}}\right)\!\|\nabla\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\lambda D_{e}}{2\sigma}\|\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
&\quad+\frac{\text{Pe}^{2}\varepsilon}{2D_{e}}\|\nabla L\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\!-\!\text{Pe}\!\left\langle\bar{\rho}f_{2}{\bf e}(\theta),\!\nabla L\bar{f}\right\rangle,\end{split}

(34)

where we also used
\|\bar{{\bf p}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2\pi\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}
, and noticed that for any
(t,{\bf x})\in\Omega\times[0,T]
,
|{\bf p}_{2}(t,{\bf x})|\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{2}(t,{\bf x},\theta)\mathrm{d}{\theta}=\rho_{2}(t,{\bf x})\leq 1
, which implies
\|\bar{\rho}{\bf p}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
. In order to apply the Grönwall inequality and conclude the argument we must estimate the final term in
eq.

34
. Since the operator
L
maps continuously
L^{2}_{z}(\Upsilon)
into
H^{2}_{z}(\Upsilon)
, we have
L\bar{f}\in H^{2}_{z}(\Upsilon)
, thus
\nabla L\bar{f}\in H^{1}_{z}(\Upsilon)
. By means of the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\Upsilon)\hookrightarrow L^{p_{*}}(\Upsilon)
for
p_{*}=6
, and that

, we infer that

for any

f_{2}\in L^{q}(\Upsilon)
for any

. In particular, for

and

is bounded by

\begin{split}\text{Pe}\|\bar{\rho}f_{2}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\|\nabla L\bar{f}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\Upsilon)}&\leq C(\text{Pe},|\Upsilon|,p^{\prime},p_{*})\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\|\bar{\rho}f_{2}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\\

(35)

where the constants

\Upsilon,p,q

35

34

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\left(\|\nabla L\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}+\lambda\|\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\\

. Finally, since

3.5
Existence and uniqueness of solutions to the 1D Model

The analysis carried out previously for
eq.

2
can be applied to obtain well-posedness for the 1D model
eq.

4
. Therefore, we will not provide proofs but only point out the main differences.

Let
f_{L}(x,t)
and

be the densities of the left- and right-moving particles, respectively. The (rescaled) one dimensional version of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f_{R}+\text{Pe}\partial_{x}(f_{R}(1-\rho))=\partial_{xx}f_{R}+f_{L}-f_{R}\\

(36)

The

and the

Definition 9
(Weak solution to 1D model).

Let

be nonnegative functions such that

to system
eq.

36
is a pair of curves

such that

and any

it holds

\begin{split}&\langle\partial_{t}f_{R}(t),\varphi\rangle_{\Omega}\!=\!\text{Pe}\langle f_{R}(t)(1\!-\!\rho(t)),\partial_{x}\varphi\rangle_{\Omega}\!-\!\langle\partial_{x}f_{R}(t),\partial_{x}\varphi\rangle_{\Omega}\!+\langle f_{L}(t)\!-\!f_{R}(t),\varphi\rangle_{\Omega}\\
&\langle\partial_{t}f_{L}(t),\phi\rangle_{\Omega}\!=\!-\text{Pe}\langle f_{L}(t)(1\!-\!\rho(t)),\partial_{x}\phi\rangle_{\Omega}\!-\!\langle\partial_{x}f_{L}(t),\partial_{x}\phi\rangle_{\Omega}\!+\langle f_{R}(t)\!-\!f_{L}(t),\phi\rangle_{\Omega},\\

\Omega

in

Notice that adding/subracting the equations in system

36

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\text{Pe}\partial_{x}(p(1-\rho))=\partial_{xx}\rho\\

(37)

The main difference is the absence of the angular variable, which simplifies the discretisation of the system; indeed the angular variable is already discretised, as there are only two possible directions (left and right). , which is performed in
lemmas

and

. For ease of presentation we start with the one-dimensional model.

4.1
Regularity for the 1D model

Our strategy is to apply the Duhamel principle so as to treat
eq.

36
as a forced one-dimensional heat equation. Inspired by
[
6
, Chapter 9]
, we make the following definition.

Definition 12
.

Define
\Phi:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by the explicit formula

\Phi(t,x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-n^{2}t}\cos nx\qquad\forall(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}.

(38)

Note that the above may be rewritten in terms of the complex exponential basis as
\Phi(t,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{-n^{2}t}}{2\pi}e^{inx}
for all
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. In what follows it will be useful to rewrite
\Phi
in terms of the Jacobi theta-3 function (denoted by
\theta_{3}
), and in terms of the Jacobi theta function (denoted by
\vartheta
, also sometimes by
\vartheta_{00}
),

\Phi(t,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\theta_{3}\left(\frac{x}{2},e^{-t}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\vartheta\left(\frac{x}{2\pi},\frac{it}{\pi}\right)\qquad\forall(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}.

(39)

Lemma 13
.

The function
\Phi
of
definition

12
is a smooth
2\pi
-periodic function that satisfies
\partial_{t}\Phi-\partial_{xx}\Phi=0
as a pointwise equality in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, is nonnegative in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, and
\|\Phi(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}=1
for every
t\in(0,\infty)
. Additionally,

\int_{0}^{t}\|\Phi(\tau,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])}\,\mathrm{d}\tau=t+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2n^{2}}(1-e^{-2n^{2}t})\qquad\forall t>0,

(40)

and
\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t-s,x-y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\|\Phi(\tau,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])}\,\mathrm{d}\tau
for all
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times[0,2\pi]
. Finally, for any
2\pi
-periodic
C^{2}
function
\psi
,
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x)\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\psi(0)
.

The proof of
lemma

13
is in
appendix

B
. We use the function
\Phi
as a periodic heat kernel, in the sense made precise by the following result. The initial data
\psi
is chosen in
L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
to justify the expansion in terms of the sine/cosine basis.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\psi\in L^{1}_{per}([0,2\pi])
. Then,
\Psi(t,x):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\psi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
for all
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
is a smooth
2\pi
-periodic function defined on the full-space
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, which solves the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\Psi-\partial_{xx}\Psi=0\qquad&&\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R},\\
&\Psi|_{t=0}=\psi\qquad&&\text{on }\mathbb{R},\end{aligned}\right.

(41)

where the initial data is achieved in
L^{1}_{loc}
, i.e.,
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|\Psi(t,\cdot)-\psi\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}=0
.

The proof of this result is in
appendix

B
. An alternative way of generating periodic solutions of the heat equation is to convolve the classical one-dimensional heat kernel with
2\pi
-periodic test functions. However, when performing integration by parts in the Duhamel formula (
cf.

eq.

42
in the lemma below) the boundary terms do not vanish unless both the kernel and the “test function” are periodic. Hence, the kernel of
definition

12
is better suited to our analysis.

Lemma 15
(Regularity away from initial time).

Suppose that the periodic initial data for problem
eq.

36
is such that
f_{R,0},f_{L,0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then, the unique weak solutions
f_{R}
and
f_{L}
of
eq.

36
belong to
C((0,T]\times\bar{\Omega})
.

Proof.

Recall from
theorem

10
that we have
f_{R},f_{L},\rho\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\Omega)
.

The key observation is that the equations
eq.

36
can be interpreted as heat equations with a source on the right-hand side. Since we already derived the fundamental solution of the periodic heat equation in
lemmas

13
and
14
, and we have already shown uniqueness in the relevant space of solutions to
eq.

36
, we are in a position to apply the Duhamel principle and obtain implicit integral relations for
f_{R}
and
f_{L}
. In the first step of the proof, we justify that these Duhamel formulas are well-defined. In a second step, we prove the continuity of these functions away from the initial time.

Step 1 (Duhamel formula):
We justify that the formula for
f_{R}

\displaystyle f_{R}(t,x)\!=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)f_{R,0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

(42)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\Phi(t\!-\!s,\!x\!-\!y)\Big(\text{Pe}\partial_{y}\{f_{R}(s,y)[1-\rho(s,y)]\}+f_{R}(s,y)\!-\!f_{L}(s,y)\Big)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s,

is well-defined, for a.e.
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\bar{\Omega}
. To begin with, whenever
t>0
, the function
\Phi(t,\cdot)
is smooth and is therefore square-integrable on the compact interval
[0,2\pi]
.
This shows that the integrand in the first integral in
eq.

42
is integrable. Meanwhile, the equality
eq.

40
shows that
\Phi\in L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}([0,2\pi]))
, whence, using the boundedness of
\rho
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t-s,x-y)\big|\partial_{y}\{f_{R}(s,y)[1-\rho(s,y)]\}\big|\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\!2\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\bigg[\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\Phi(t-s,x-y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\!\bigg]^{1/2}\!\bigg[\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!|\partial_{y}f_{R}(s,y)|^{2}\!+\!f_{R}(s,y)^{2}|\partial_{y}\rho(s,y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg]^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\Phi(t\!-\!s,\!x\!-\!y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s\!+\!\|f_{R}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\!+\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!f_{R}(s,y)^{2}|\partial_{y}\rho(s,y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s,

where we have used the Young inequality. As before, we use a change of variable and the constraint
x\in[0,2\pi]
to bound the first term on the right-hand side of the above by
2\|\Phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}([0,2\pi]))}
. Meanwhile,

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{R}(s,y)^{2}|\partial_{y}\rho(s,y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\|f_{R}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)}\|\rho\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))},

using that
f_{R}\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)
from
theorem

10
. Similarly, using
lemma

13

\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\Phi(t\!-\!s,\!x\!-\!y)|f_{R}(s,y)\!-\!f_{L}(s,y)|\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s\leq 2\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\|f_{R}\!-\!f_{L}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Therefore, the integrand in the second integral of
eq.

42
is also integrable. It is an exercise to verify that
eq.

42
, and the equivalent formula for
f_{L}
satisfy
eq.

36
and are periodic on
\Omega
. These formulas therefore coincide with the unique solutions of
eq.

36
.

Step 2 (continuity away from initial time):
Fix
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\bar{\Omega}
and let
(t_{n},x_{n})\in(0,T]\times\Omega
be converging to
(t,x)
. Using the integrability from Step 1, the continuity of
\Phi
for
t>0
, and the Dominated Convergence Theorem on
eq.

42
and the analogous formula for
f_{L}
, we directly obtain
|f_{R}(t_{n},x_{n})-f_{R}(t,x)|\to 0
and
|f_{L}(t_{n},x_{n})-f_{L}(t,x)|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. It follows that
f_{R},f_{L}\in C((0,T]\times\bar{\Omega})
. In turn, we also obtain
\rho\in C((0,T]\times\bar{\Omega})
.

Lemma 16
.

Suppose that
f_{R,0},f_{L,0}\in C(\bar{\Omega})
and are periodic. Then, the unique weak solutions
f_{R}
and
f_{L}
of
eq.

36
belong to
C([0,T]\times\bar{\Omega})
.

Proof.

We only write the proof for
f_{R}
, as the proof for
f_{L}
is identical. In view of the uniform continuity of
f_{R}
on the compact
\bar{\Omega}
, given
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)
for which if

are such that
|x-y|<\delta

|f_{R,0}(x)-f_{R,0}(y)|<\varepsilon/4
. Now, given
t\in(0,\infty)
and
x^{*}\in(0,2\pi)
, there holds from
eq.

42

\displaystyle|f_{R}(t,x)-f_{R,0}(x^{*})|\leq

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)f_{R,0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-f_{R,0}(x^{*})\bigg|

\displaystyle+\text{Pe}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t-s,x-y)|\partial_{y}\big(f_{R}(s,y)(1-\rho(s,y))\big)|\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+k\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t-s,x-y)\big|f_{R}(s,y)-f_{L}(s,y)\big|\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,x)+I_{2}(t,x)+I_{3}(t,x),

where we used the nonnegativity of
\Phi
and
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
for every
t>0
from
lemma

13
. Using Hölder’s inequality, the
L^{2}
-boundedness of
\partial_{x}f_{R},f_{R},f_{L}
, and
lemma

13
, we obtain
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|I_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,2\pi])}=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|I_{3}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,2\pi])}=0
.

It remains to control
I_{1}
. Recall that since
f_{R,0}\in C(\bar{\Omega})
is periodic, there exists a sequence
(\psi_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
of periodic elements of
C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
such that
\|f_{R,0}-\psi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then, since
\psi_{m}\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we know that it has Fourier coefficients
a_{m,n},b_{m,n}
that decay as
n^{-2}
;
cf.

eq.

68
-
eq.

69
. Then, using the convolution result for Fourier series,

\displaystyle\bigg|\psi_{m}(x)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\psi_{m}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\bigg|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{-n^{2}t})\big(a_{m,n}\cos nx+b_{m,n}\sin nx\big)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}(1-e^{-n^{2}t})\to 0\qquad\text{as }t\to 0^{+},

where we applied the Dominated Convergence Theorem applied to the atomic measure in the final line. Note that

\displaystyle I_{1}(t

\displaystyle,x)\leq|f_{R,0}(x^{*})-f_{R,0}(x)|+|f_{R,0}(x)-\psi_{m}(x)|

\displaystyle+\bigg|\psi_{m}(x)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\psi_{m}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|+\bigg|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\big(\psi_{m}(y)-f_{R,0}(y)\big)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|,

and elementary manipulations (
cf.
Step 3 of Proof of
lemma

13
) relying also on the periodicity of

yield
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\psi_{m}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\psi_{m}(x+y)\,\mathrm{d}y
. Thus, given
\varepsilon>0
, by first picking
x
such that
|x-x^{*}|<\delta
and then choosing
m
sufficiently large such that
\|f_{R,0}-\psi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})}<\varepsilon/4
, and finally taking

sufficiently close to zero for this particular

(
cf.
convergence to initial data of
lemma

13
), it follows that

I_{1}(t,x)<\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon+\|\psi_{m}-f_{R,0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y<\varepsilon,

where we used the unit integral property of
\Phi
. Hence,
\lim_{(t,x)\to(0^{+},x^{*})}I_{1}(t,x)=0
, and we conclude
\lim_{(t,x)\to(0^{+},x^{*})}|f_{R,0}(x^{*})-f_{R}(t,x)|=0
, which verifies the required continuity at the initial time. The same argument shows the result for
f_{L}
.

Remark 17
.

We showed that the weak solutions of the 1D model, which (cf.
remark

11
) in principle only belong to
C([0,T];L^{2}_{per}([0,2\pi]))\cap L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{per}([0,2\pi])
, are in fact continuous under mild assumptions on the initial data. Although the authors suspect that it is possible to show higher regularity of the solutions, the method of proof required for such a result would be rather different to the current approach. For this reason, we leave this for further investigation.

4.2
Regularity for the 2D model

In accordance with the expression for the one-dimensional periodic kernel in terms of complex exponentials (
cf.

section

4.1
), we find that the periodic heat kernel in three spatial dimensions (
i.e.
when
\Upsilon=[0,2\pi]^{3}
) is given by the explicit formula

\tilde{\Phi}(t,{\bf x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}+\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{-|\mathbf{n}|^{2}t}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{n}\cdot{\bf x}}\qquad\forall(t,{\bf x})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(43)

Note that the above may be rewritten in terms of the Jacobi theta-3 function as
\tilde{\Phi}(t,{\bf x})=(2\pi)^{-3}\theta_{3}({x_{1}}/{2},e^{-t})\theta_{3}({x_{2}}/{2},e^{-t})\theta_{3}({x_{3}}/{2},e^{-t})
for every
(t,{\bf x})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. As per the proof of
lemma

13
, we obtain that
\tilde{\Phi}
is nonnegative in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
with

\|\tilde{\Phi}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Upsilon)}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{t}\|\tilde{\Phi}(\tau,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau=t+\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\frac{1}{2|\mathbf{n}|^{2}}(1-e^{-|\mathbf{n}|^{2}t})\qquad\forall t>0,

(44)

and that
\tilde{\Phi}
is a smooth
\Upsilon
-periodic solution of the heat equation in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In turn, an identical proof to that of
lemma

14
yields the following result.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\psi\in L^{1}_{per}(\Upsilon)
. Then,
\tilde{\Psi}(t,\bm{\xi}):=\int_{\Upsilon}\tilde{\Phi}(t,\bm{\xi}-\mathbf{z})\psi(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}
for all
(t,\bm{\xi})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a smooth
\Upsilon
-periodic function defined on the full-space
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and solves the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\tilde{\Psi}-\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}\tilde{\Psi}=0\qquad&&\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&\Psi|_{t=0}=\psi\qquad&&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{3},\end{aligned}\right.

(45)

where the initial data is achieved in
L^{1}_{loc}
, i.e.,
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|\tilde{\Psi}(t,\cdot)-\psi\|_{L^{1}(\Upsilon)}=0
.

In turn, we have the following regularity result.

Lemma 19
.

Suppose the initial data for problem
eq.

2
is such that
f_{0}\in L^{\infty}_{per}(\Upsilon)
. Then unique weak solution
f
of
eq.

2
supplied by
theorem

3
belongs to
C((0,T]\times\bar{\Upsilon})
.

Proof.

There are two steps to the proof: the first is to show the boundedness of
f
(which requires building an appropriate test function); the second is to argue via a Duhamel formula (
cf.
Proof of
lemma

15
).

Step 1 (boundedness of
f
):
Recall from
theorem

3
that the weak solution of
eq.

2
,
f
, a priori only belongs to the space
L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}_{per}(\Upsilon))\cap L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{per}(\Upsilon))
. We show that
f\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Upsilon)
.

For any
k>0
, one verifies that
(f-k)_{+}\in L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}_{per}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{per}(\Upsilon))
and
\partial_{t}(f-k)_{+}\in L^{2}([0,T];(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(\Upsilon))
. Indeed, there holds
(f-k)_{+}=\mathds{1}_{\{f>k\}}(f-k)
, from which it follows that, in the sense of distributions,
\nabla_{t,{\bf x},\theta}(f-k)_{+}=\mathds{1}_{\{f>k\}}\nabla_{t,{\bf x},\theta}f
a.e. in
(0,T)\times\Upsilon
, where
\nabla_{t,{\bf x},\theta}
is the vector of derivatives
(\partial_{t},\nabla,\partial_{\theta})
, and we directly deduce the boundedness of the relevant norms. Thus,
(f(t)-k)_{+}
is an admissible test function for the weak formulation of
definition

2
. Note additionally that, by
[
18
, Ch. 2 Lem. 1.2]
, there holds in the sense of distributions, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Upsilon}(f(t)-k)_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}=\langle\partial_{t}f(t),(f(t)-k)_{+}\rangle_{\Upsilon}
. Using
(f(t)-k)_{+}
as the test function in the weak formulation of
definition

2
, integrating in
{\bf x}
, using the boundedness of
\rho
, Hölder’s inequality, and the Young inequality, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|(f(t)-k)_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\right)+\int_{\Upsilon}\bigg(\frac{D_{e}}{2}|\nabla(f(t)-k)_{+}|^{2}+

\displaystyle|\partial_{\theta}(f(t)-k)_{+}|^{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\text{Pe}^{2}}{2D_{e}}\|(f(t)-k)_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2},

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. It follows from the Grönwall Lemma that there holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|(f(t)-k)_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|(f_{0}-k)_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\exp\left({\frac{\text{Pe}^{2}}{D_{e}}t}\right)\qquad\text{a.e.~}t\in(0,T).

By selecting
k=\|f_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}
, the right-hand side of the above is null. It follows that
f(t)\leq\|f_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,
i.e.
,
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Upsilon)}\leq\|f_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}
.

Step 2 (Duhamel formula for
f
):
Without loss of generality for this final step we only consider the case where we have normalised the
eq.

2
by the positive constant
D_{e}
, so that
\partial_{t}-D_{e}\Delta-\partial^{2}_{\theta}
may be rewritten as
\partial_{t}-\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}
with
\bm{\xi}
the usual 3-dimensional vector.

As per the proof of
lemma

15
, in view of the uniqueness provided by
theorem

3
, it follows from
eq.

2
and the Duhamel principle that
f
satisfies the following implicit equation, where the kernel
\tilde{\Phi}
was defined in
eq.

43
(
cf.

lemma

18
),

f(t,\bm{\xi})\!=\!\int_{\Upsilon}\tilde{\Phi}(t,\bm{\xi}-\mathbf{z})f_{0}(\mathbf{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}+\text{Pe}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Upsilon}\tilde{\Phi}(t-s,\bm{\xi}-\mathbf{z})\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\cdot\big(f(s,\mathbf{z})(1-\rho(s,\mathbf{z}))\mathbf{e}(\theta)\big)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}\mathrm{d}s

(46)

for a.e.
(t,\bm{\xi})\in[0,T]\times\bar{\Upsilon}
. Note that the above is well-defined: using the boundedness of
f
from Step 1, we proceed to show the integrability of the integrand in
eq.

46
as per Step 1 of the proof of
lemma

15
. We now deduce that
f\in C((0,T]\times\bar{\Omega})
using the continuity of
\tilde{\Phi}
for
t>0
and the Dominated Convergence Theorem, exactly as we did in Step 2 of the proof of
lemma

15
.

Finally, by following the strategy of proof of
lemma

16
to the letter, we obtain the following result concerning continuity up to the initial time.

Lemma 20
.

Suppose that the periodic initial data
f_{0}
for problem
eq.

2
belongs to
C(\bar{\Upsilon})
. Then, the unique weak solution

of

We note, as per

5
Extension to very weak solutions for the 2D model

The assumption on the initial data in
L^{2}_{per}(\Upsilon)
rules out initial configurations with a fixed orientation, that is,
f_{0}(x,\theta)=h^{0}(x)\delta_{\theta=\theta^{*}}
for some
\theta^{*}\in[0,2\pi)
and
h^{0}\in L^{2}_{per}(\Omega)
, where the latter is the usual Dirac delta in the angular variable only. Since such initial data belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega;H^{s}(0,2\pi))
for
s<-1/2
, we turn our attention to initial data
f_{0}\in L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))
. For the convenience of the reader, we clarify that, for any normed vector space
V
,

f\in L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;V)\iff\left\{\begin{aligned} &f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to V,\\
&\int_{\Omega}\|f({\bf x},\cdot)\|_{V}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}<+\infty,\text{ and}\\
&f({\bf x}-\mathbf{w},\cdot)=f({\bf x},\cdot)\quad\text{a.e. }{\bf x}\in\Omega,\quad\forall\mathbf{w}\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2},\end{aligned}\right.

(47)

where the final equality on the right-hand side holds in
V
.

In view of the previous discussion, we define the following Hilbert spaces, to give meaning to a weaker formulation of the equation (
cf.

definition

21
),

\displaystyle X:=L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{per}(\Upsilon))\cap L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;H^{2}_{per}(0,2\pi))),

(48)

\displaystyle Y:=H^{1}_{per}(\Upsilon)\cap L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;H^{2}_{per}(0,2\pi)).

Note that
\|u\|_{X^{\prime}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{Y^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}t
, and
\|u(t)\|_{Y^{\prime}}=\sup_{\overset{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{per}(\bar{\Upsilon})}{\|\varphi\|_{Y}\leq 1}}|\langle u(t),\varphi\rangle_{\Upsilon}|
.

The initial density must now be defined in duality,
i.e.
,

\rho_{0}({\bf x}):=\langle f_{0}({\bf x},\cdot),1\rangle_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}\times H^{1}_{per}}\qquad\mbox{a.e. }x\in\Omega,

(49)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}\times H^{1}_{per}}
is the duality product on
(0,2\pi)
. Note that for
f_{0}({\bf x},\theta)=h^{0}({\bf x})\delta_{\theta=\theta^{*}}
, the initial space density is
\rho_{0}({\bf x})=h^{0}({\bf x})
.

We now extend the concept of solution established in

2
to admit a larger class of functions (
i.e.
smaller class of test functions). With slight abuse of terminology, we name these solutions
very weak solutions
, even though only the regularity in the angular variable is affected.

Definition 21
.

A
very weak solution
of Eq.
eq.

2
is a curve
f
belonging to
L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)))\cap L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)))\cap L^{2}((0,T)\times\Upsilon)
, with
\partial_{t}f\in X^{\prime}
such that, for every test function
\varphi\in Y
, there holds for a.e.
t\in[0,T]

\begin{split}&\left\langle\partial_{t}f(t),\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!=\!\left\langle\text{Pe}(1-\rho(t))f(t){\bf e}(\theta),\nabla\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!-\!\left\langle D_{e}\nabla f(t),\nabla\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon}+\left\langle f(t),\partial_{\theta}^{2}\varphi\right\rangle_{\Upsilon},\\
&f(0,{\bf x},\theta)=f_{0}({\bf x},\theta),\qquad\text{on }\Upsilon\times\{0\},\end{split}

(50)

with periodic boundary conditions on
\Upsilon
,
where

\rho(t,\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(t,{\bf x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
, and where the initial data is achieved in the sense
f(0)=f_{0}
in
Y^{\prime}
.

Since
Y\subset L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;H^{2}_{per}(0,2\pi))
, we have the inclusions
L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)))\subset L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H_{per}^{2})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))\subset Y^{\prime}
. It follows that
f\in L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)))
and
\partial_{t}f\in X^{\prime}
imply
f\in H^{1}([0,T];Y^{\prime})
. Hence, by
[
10
, Sec. 5.9.2 Th. 2]
,
f\in C([0,T];Y^{\prime})
. Therefore,
f(0)\in Y^{\prime}
is well-defined in the previous definition.

We now give the proof of
theorem

6
; the main theorem of this section. Since a very weak solution is an element of
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Upsilon)
, this result implies that the solutions never concentrate in angle to a Dirac mass, even when the initial data does. This illustrates an instantaneous smoothing property of the equation.

Proof of
theorem

.

The idea of the proof is to construct a sequence of regularised initial data that approaches
f_{0}
in the required norm, and to show that the corresponding regularised solutions converge to a very weak solution.

Step 1 (regularised sequence of initial data and solutions):
Due to
lemma

22
in
appendix

C
,
there exists
(f_{0}^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
nonnegative elements of

\|f_{0}-f_{0}^{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}\to 0\qquad\text{as }m\to\infty,

(51)

with
\rho_{0}^{m}({\bf x}):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{0}^{m}({\bf x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\in[0,1]
for a.e.
{\bf x}\in\Upsilon
. Note that

of course implies that there exists a positive constant

(52)

Since each

is an admissible initial datum for

Step 2 (an auxiliary Dirichlet problem):
In contrast to the inverse Laplacian introduced in
section

3.4
, we introduce
L_{\theta}:=(-\partial^{2}_{\theta})^{-1}:(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)\to H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(-\partial_{\theta}^{2})L_{\theta}f=f\qquad\text{in }(0,2\pi),\\
&L_{\theta}f(0)=L_{\theta}f(2\pi)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(53)

Let us note that for any function

, there holds

\|f\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}=\sup_{{\overset{\varphi\in H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)}{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1}}}\langle f,\varphi\rangle_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}\times H^{1}_{per}}\leq\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f\|_{L^{2}_{per}(0,2\pi)}.

Indeed, using the property
(-\partial_{\theta}^{2})L_{\theta}f=f

and its dual,

\begin{split}|\langle f,\varphi\rangle|&=|\langle(-\partial_{\theta}^{2})\circ(-\partial_{\theta}^{2})^{-1}f,\varphi\rangle|=|\langle\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f,\partial_{\theta}\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}|\leq\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{\theta}\varphi\|_{L^{2}}.\end{split}

Note that the integration by parts in the above involve no additional boundary term due to the periodicity of

and
L_{\theta}f

\|f\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}=\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f\|_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}.

Since

\|L_{\theta}f\|_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}^{2}\leq C_{P}\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}=C_{P}\|f\|^{2}_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}\leq C_{P}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{per}(0,2\pi)},

(55)

where the constant

depends only on the domain
(0,2\pi)
, and
eq.

54
was used to obtain the equality. In turn, given a function

such that
\nabla f\in L^{2}_{per}(0,2\pi)\subset(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)
, there holds the estimate

\|L_{\theta}\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}^{2}\leq C_{P}\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}\leq C_{P}\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)},

(56)

and, directly from

54

\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}^{2}=\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}\leq\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}.

(57)

Step 3 (energy estimate on first derivative independent of

):
We now apply the operator
L_{\theta}
to the sequence
(f_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
. Note that, for a.e.

\|L_{\theta}f_{m}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Upsilon)}^{2}=\|L_{\theta}f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}+\|\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}.

It then follows from

55
and

56

\displaystyle\|L_{\theta}f_{m}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\|L_{\theta}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}+\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2C_{P}\int_{\Omega}\big(\|f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}+\|\nabla f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}\big)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}.

Thus,
\|L_{\theta}f_{m}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\leq 2C_{P}\|f_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\Upsilon)}^{2}<+\infty
. Therefore,
L_{\theta}f_{m}(t)
is an admissible test function for the weak formulation of
definition

2
. This latter observation enables us to test the equation with

and to obtain an estimate independent of

. We get, for a.e.
(t,{\bf x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}^{2}=&\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,2\pi)}^{2}\\
=&\langle\partial_{t}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot),L_{\theta}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\rangle_{(H^{1})^{\prime}\times H^{1}},\end{split}

where we used that
\partial_{t}f\in L^{2}([0,T];(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(\Upsilon))
and
L_{\theta}f_{m}\in L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{per}(\Upsilon))

\langle\nabla f_{m},\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon}=\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}
and
\langle\partial_{\theta}f_{m},\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon}=\|f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}^{2}=\text{Pe}\langle(1\!&-\!\rho_{m})f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta),\!\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon}\!\\
&-\!\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}-\!\|f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2},\end{split}

(58)

where we used the equality
(-\partial^{2}_{\theta})L_{\theta}f_{m}=f_{m}
, integrated by parts in
\theta
the last two duality products, and used the Dirichlet problem
eq.

53
. Next, observe that
f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta)=(-\partial_{\theta}^{2})L_{\theta}f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta)=-\partial_{\theta}(\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta))-\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}{\bf e}^{\perp}(\theta)

\begin{split}\langle(1-\rho_{m})f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta),\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon}=\langle(1-\rho_{m})&\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta),\nabla\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon}\\
&-\langle(1-\rho_{m})\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}{\bf e}^{\perp}(\theta),\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon},\end{split}

whence the Hölder inequality and the bound for
\rho_{m}
give
|\langle(1-\rho_{m})f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta),\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon}|\leq\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\|\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}

\displaystyle|\langle(1\!-\!\rho_{m})f_{m}{\bf e}(\theta),\!\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\rangle_{\Upsilon}|\leq\text{Pe}(1\!+\!C_{P})\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\!+\!\frac{1}{2\text{Pe}}\|\partial_{\theta}\nabla L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}.

Combining the above with
eq.

58

57

\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}
and
eq.

54

\|\partial_{\theta}L_{\theta}f_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}
, and integrating in time, we get, for a.e.

,

\displaystyle\|f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla f_{m}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+2\int_{0}^{t}\|f_{m}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|f_{0}^{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}^{2}+\text{Pe}^{2}(1+C_{P})\int_{0}^{t}\|f_{m}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle.

Grönwall’s Lemma yields

a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, and we recall from

52

independent of

,

\displaystyle\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\!\|f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}^{2}

\displaystyle+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\|\nabla f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

(59)

\displaystyle+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\|f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq M_{1}.

Thus,
\|f_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)))}
,
\|f_{m}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)))}
,
\|f_{m}\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\Upsilon)}
are bounded independently of

.

Step 4 (corresponding estimate on the time derivatives):
We now return to

2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\text{Pe}\|f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\|\partial_{\theta}^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla f_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;H^{1}(0,2\pi))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big((1+\text{Pe})\|f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\nabla f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))}\big)\big(\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(\Upsilon)}

\displaystyle+\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;H^{2}(0,2\pi))}\big),

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. We therefore obtain, using

48

\|\partial_{t}f_{m}(t)\|_{Y^{\prime}}\leq((1+\text{Pe})\|f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\nabla f_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))})
, whence, using

59

a positive constant independent of

\|\partial_{t}f_{m}\|_{X^{\prime}}\leq M_{2}.

(60)

Step 5 (estimates on

):
We infer uniform estimates on the sequence

from those on

. Firstly,
\rho_{m}(t,{\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\qquad
for a.e.

and has norm equal to

|\rho_{m}(t,{\bf x})|\leq 2\pi\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1}\bigg|\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\theta)\varphi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\bigg|=2\pi\|f_{m}(t,{\bf x},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)},

(61)

for a.e.
(t,{\bf x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega
. The same estimate on the derivative yields

for some

Due to the uniform estimate

Appendix A
The system of ODEs for the Galerkin approximation in space

Throughout this section,
m\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed. For
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, we define

\displaystyle a_{k}^{n,m}(t,x,y):=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{m}\alpha_{1,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\cos px\cos qy+\sum_{q=1}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{m}\alpha_{2,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\cos px\sin qy

(64)

\displaystyle+\sum_{q=0}^{m}\sum_{p=1}^{m}\alpha_{3,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\sin px\cos qy+\sum_{q=1}^{m}\sum_{p=1}^{m}\alpha_{4,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\sin px\sin qy,

\displaystyle b_{k}^{n,m}(t,x,y):=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{m}\beta_{1,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\cos px\cos qy+\sum_{q=1}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{m}\beta_{2,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\cos px\sin qy

\displaystyle+\sum_{q=0}^{m}\sum_{p=1}^{m}\beta_{3,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\sin px\cos qy+\sum_{q=1}^{m}\sum_{p=1}^{m}\beta_{4,p,q}^{k,m}(t)\sin px\sin qy,

where

Appendix B
Properties of the periodic heat kernel

Below we provide proofs of
lemma

13
and
lemma

14
, respectively.

Proof of
lemma

13
.

Step 1 (smoothness away from initial time):
Begin by noting that, by a standard argument using the Weierstraß M-test, the series representation
eq.

38
for
\Phi
is well-defined, and moreover can repeatedly be differentiated term-by-term for positive times. As such,
\Phi\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
, and
\partial_{t}\Phi(t,x)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2}e^{-n^{2}t}\cos nx=\partial_{xx}\Phi(t,x)
for all
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Step 2 (nonnegativity and
L^{1}
-norm):
Recall (
cf.

[
19
, Section 21.3]
) the infinite product representation for the Jacobi theta-3 function. For every
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
,

\Phi(t,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\theta_{3}\left(\frac{x}{2},e^{-t}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{-2nt})(1+2e^{-t(2n-1)}\cos x+e^{-t(4n-2)}).

By the Young inequality,
1+e^{-t(4n-2)}\geq 2e^{-t(2n-1)}
, and hence every term in the above product is nonnegative. As such,
\Phi
itself is nonnegative in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. Meanwhile, integrating the series term by term, we obtain
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
for every
t\in(0,\infty)
. It therefore follows that
\|\Phi(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
for all
t\in(0,\infty)
. Also, the Plancherel Theorem yields
\|\Phi(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-2n^{2}t}
. Integrating in time (using also
\sum n^{-2}<+\infty
) yields
eq.

40
.

Step 3 (constant
L^{2}
-norm):
Furthermore, given any
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times[0,2\pi]
, successive changes of variables yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t-s,x-y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(\tau,x-y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\!\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{x}\!\Phi(\tau,w)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{d}\tau\!+\!\int_{0}^{t}\int_{x-2\pi}^{0}\!\Phi(\tau,w\!+\!2\pi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(\tau,w)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

as required, where we also used the periodicity of
\Phi
to obtain the second equality.

Step 4 (convergence to initial data):
It remains to verify the initial data. Fix any
2\pi
-periodic
C^{2}
function
\psi
. Then, the elementary theory of Fourier series shows that
\psi
admits the Fourier representation
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\cos nx+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\sin nx
a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle a_{0}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle a_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi(x)\cos nx\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad b_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi(x)\sin nx\,\mathrm{d}x.

(68)

Note that, due to the
C^{2}
-regularity of
\psi
and its periodicity, two consecutive integration by parts yields
a_{n}=-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)\cos nx\,\mathrm{d}x
and
b_{n}=-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)\sin nx\,\mathrm{d}x
so that there exists a positive constant
C_{\psi}
depending on
\psi
alone such that

|a_{n}|+|b_{n}|\leq C_{\psi}n^{-2}\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(69)

Hence,
|\psi(0)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x)\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x|=\frac{1}{\pi}|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{-n^{2}t})a_{n}|\leq\frac{C_{\psi}}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-2}(1-e^{-n^{2}t})
, and, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem applied to the atomic measure (using that
\sum n^{-2}<+\infty
), the right-hand side vanishes in the limit as
t\to 0^{+}
.

Proof of
lemma

14
.

Recall from
lemma

13
that
\Phi\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
. Since
\psi\in L^{1}([0,2\pi])
, an application of the Dominated Convergence Theorem shows that
\Psi\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
, and that for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, there holds
\partial_{t}^{(m)}\partial_{x}^{(n)}\Psi(t,x)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{t}^{(m)}\partial_{x}^{(n)}\Phi(t,x-y)\psi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
for all
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. In turn, we directly obtain that
\partial_{t}\Psi-\partial_{xx}\Psi=0
pointwise in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.

It remains to verify the initial condition. Suppose for the time being that
\psi
is smooth enough, say
\psi\in C^{2}_{per}([0,2\pi])
. From the Fourier series decomposition
eq.

68
for
\psi
we compute, using the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem (or directly using the convolution result for Fourier series), the Fourier coefficients of
\Psi
:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{0}(t)=a_{0},\quad\tilde{a}_{n}(t)=e^{-n^{2}t}a_{n},\quad\tilde{b}_{n}(t)=e^{-n^{2}t}b_{n}\qquad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(70)

Using the Plancherel Theorem for Fourier series of square integrable functions we get (up to a multiplicative constant)

\|\Psi(t,\cdot)-\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{-n^{2}t})^{2}(a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2})\to 0\qquad\text{as }t\to 0^{+},

(71)

where we used the decay estimate
eq.

69

When

\displaystyle\|\Psi(t,\cdot)-\psi\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}\leq\!\|\Psi(t,\!\cdot)-\Psi_{m}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}

\displaystyle+\|\Psi_{m}(t,\cdot)-\psi_{m}\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}

\displaystyle+\|\psi_{m}-\psi\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])},

where
\Psi_{m}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(t,x-y)\psi_{m}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
. Note that the second term in the previous expression vanishes in the limit as
t\to 0^{+}
by the argument in the previous paragraph. Next, using the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem to exchange the order of the integrals,
\|\Psi(t,\cdot)-\Psi_{m}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}\leq\|\Phi(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}\|\psi-\psi_{m}\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}=\|\psi-\psi_{m}\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])},
where we used from

that
\|\Phi(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}=1
for every

. It follows that
\|\Psi(t,\cdot)-\psi\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}<2\varepsilon/3+\|\Psi_{m}-\psi_{m}\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}
for all

. Thus, having fixed

and using the result from the previous paragraph, we get
\|\Psi(t,\cdot)-\psi\|_{L^{1}([0,2\pi])}
vanishes in the limit as

.

and
\gamma\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be nonnegative smooth bump functions, chosen such that
\beta
is compactly supported inside
\Omega
and
\gamma
is compactly supported inside the interval
[0,2\pi)
, and without loss of generality

\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\bigg)\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\bigg)=1.

(73)

Moreover, we impose that
\beta
is radially symmetric about the point
(\pi,\pi)
and that
\gamma
is symmetric about the point
\pi
. Consider the associated sequences
(\beta_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
and
(\gamma_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}

\beta_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}):=\varepsilon^{-2}\beta({\bf x}/\varepsilon),\qquad\gamma_{\varepsilon}(\theta):=\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\gamma(\theta/\varepsilon^{\alpha}),

for
\alpha>2
. These functions are smooth and nonnegative, and
\beta_{\varepsilon}
is compactly supported in the square
\varepsilon\Omega
while
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is compactly supported in
[0,2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}]
. We define
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
to be the
2\pi
-periodic extension of
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
from
[0,2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}]
to all of
\mathbb{R}
, so that
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)
. Similarly, we define
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
to be the
\Omega
-periodic extension of
\beta_{\varepsilon}
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

In turn, we define the sequence of approximations
(f_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
by the explicit formula

f_{\varepsilon}({\bf x},\theta):=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\langle f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\qquad\text{a.e.~}({\bf x},\theta)\in\Upsilon,

where, throughout the rest of this proof, the duality product is understood to be in the angle variable only, and denotes the duality product of
H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)
.

Observe firstly that, due to the assumption that
f
is nonnegative, we immediately have
f_{\varepsilon}
nonnegative a.e. in
\Upsilon
. Moreover, one may readily check (for instance by means of difference quotients) that
f_{\varepsilon}
is smooth. Similarly, it is straightforward to verify that
f_{\varepsilon}
is itself
\Upsilon
-periodic. Thus,
f_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{per}(\bar{\Upsilon})
for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Step 2 (boundedness the approximate density):
Note that, in view of the aforementioned nonnegativity of
f_{\varepsilon}
, there holds the inequality
0\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{\varepsilon}({\bf x},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\rho_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})
. Additionally, we have
\rho_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\langle f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\theta
, and a standard argument involving Riemann sums and the Arzelà–Ascoli Theorem (using also the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem and the Dominated Convergence Theorem) leads to being able to commute the duality bracket with the integral in the angle variable,
i.e.
,

\rho_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\big\langle f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

(74)

Observe now that the integral inside the duality bracket is a constant. Indeed, since
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
is smooth, and therefore bounded over any compact interval, the Dominated Convergence Theorem yields
\frac{d}{ds}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\theta-s)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(-s)-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(2\pi-s)=0
, where the final equality follows from the periodicity of
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
; recall that it is the periodic extension to all of
\mathbb{R}
of the original mollifier
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{R},

(75)

where we recall in passing that
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is supported in
[0,2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}]
, while
\gamma
is supported in
[0,2\pi]
. Hence, we get
\rho_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})=(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta)(\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\langle f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),1\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})
.

Also, applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem and the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem gives
\partial_{x_{1}}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{1}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}-y_{1},x_{2}-y_{2})\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}-2\pi,x_{2}-y_{2})-\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2}-y_{2}))\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}=0
, where the final equality follows from the periodicity of
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
; recall that this latter function is the
\Omega
-periodic extension of
\beta_{\varepsilon}
. An identical argument shows that
\partial_{x_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=0
. Arguing as per
eq.

75
and making use of the symmetry property of
\beta
about the point
(\pi,\pi)
, we deduce

\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\qquad\forall{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(76)

We emphasise that the above property is not true for the function
\beta_{\varepsilon}
; one must take its periodic extension in order to obtain this translation invariance, since the integral ranges only over the square
\Omega
, and not over all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Returning to
eq.

74
and using that
\langle f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),1\rangle=\rho(\mathbf{y})
which takes values in the interval
[0,1]
, it follows that
0\leq\rho_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})\leq(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta)(\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})=(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta)(\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})=1
, as required, where we used the nonnegativity of
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
in the second inequality and
eq.

73
to obtain the final equality.

Step 3 (convergence of approximations):
We now study the convergence of the sequence
(f_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
in
L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))
. Recall that

\displaystyle\|f({\bf x},\cdot)-f_{\varepsilon}({\bf x},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\overset{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{per}(0,2\pi)}{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1}}|\langle f({\bf x},\cdot)-f_{\varepsilon}({\bf x},\cdot),\varphi\rangle|,

for a.e.
{\bf x}\in\Omega
, where the duality product above is that of
(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)
.
Note that, due to the regularity of
f_{\varepsilon}
, one may write explicitly

\langle f({\bf x},\cdot)-f_{\varepsilon}({\bf x},\cdot),\varphi\rangle=\langle f({\bf x},\cdot),\varphi\rangle-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\langle f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\bigg)\varphi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(77)

Using also the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem and the Dominated Convergence Theorem, a standard argument involving Riemann sums and the Arzelà–Ascoli Theorem shows that this latter term is equal to
\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\big\langle f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\varphi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
. Using the equalities in
eq.

73
,
eq.

75
, and
eq.

76
, the first term on the right-hand side of
eq.

77
can be written as
\langle f({\bf x},\cdot),\varphi\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\big\langle f({\bf x},\cdot),\varphi(\cdot)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
a.e.
{\bf x}\in\Omega
. It therefore follows from
eq.

77
that

\displaystyle\langle f({\bf x},\cdot)-f_{\varepsilon}({\bf x},\cdot),\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\big\langle f({\bf x},\cdot)-f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),\varphi(\cdot)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

(78)

\displaystyle+\!\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}\!-\!\mathbf{y})\big\langle\!f(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\!,\!\varphi(\cdot)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\!-\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta\!-\!\cdot)\varphi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=:I_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})+J_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}).

The term
I_{\varepsilon}
may be rewritten as
I_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})=c_{\gamma}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\langle f({\bf x},\cdot)-f(\mathbf{y},\cdot),\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
, where
c_{\gamma}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
. Using that
\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1
, we get

|I_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})|\leq c_{\gamma}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\|f({\bf x},\cdot)-f(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(79)

however, for the time being, we concentrate on the term
J_{\varepsilon}
. We have

|J_{\varepsilon}({\bf x})|\!\leq\!\int_{\Omega}\!\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\|f(\mathbf{y}\!,\!\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}\big\|\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\big(\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\varphi(\cdot)-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\varphi(\theta)\big)\mathrm{d}\theta\big\|_{H^{1}(0,2\pi)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(80)

and, in view of
eq.

75
, we notice that, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\varphi(s)-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)\varphi(\theta)\big)\mathrm{d}\theta=-\int^{2\pi-s}_{-s}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(w)\big(\varphi(w+s)-\varphi(s)\big)\mathrm{d}w
. Thus, using also the Dominated Convergence Theorem to differentiate under the integral in the previous right-hand side, we get, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\frac{d}{ds}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\varphi(s)-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)\varphi(\theta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-\int^{2\pi-s}_{-s}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(w)\big(\varphi^{\prime}(w+s)-\varphi^{\prime}(s)\big)\,\mathrm{d}w
, where we remark that the derivative contribution due to the integral limits vanishes due to the fact that
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
and
\varphi
are
2\pi
-periodic. It follows that

\displaystyle\big\|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(\theta)\varphi(\cdot)-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\varphi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big\|_{H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)}^{2}=

(81)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big|\int^{2\pi-s}_{-s}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(w)\big(\varphi(w+s)-\varphi(s)\big)\,\mathrm{d}w\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big|\int^{2\pi-s}_{-s}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(w)\big(\varphi^{\prime}(w+s)-\varphi^{\prime}(s)\big)\,\mathrm{d}w\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=:K^{1}_{\varepsilon}+K^{2}_{\varepsilon}.

Using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus along with the fact that
\|\varphi^{(m)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<+\infty
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
due to the smoothness and periodicity of
\varphi
, we get
|\int^{2\pi-s}_{-s}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(w)\big(\varphi(w+s)-\varphi(s)\big)\,\mathrm{d}w|\leq\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{-s}^{2\pi-s}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(w)|w|\,\mathrm{d}w
, and (using the change of variables
\theta=w+s
)

K^{1}_{\varepsilon}\leq\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)|\theta-s|\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Our strategy is to split the previous right-hand side into three regions in order to bound it. While
s
is constrained to the interval
[0,2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})]
, since
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, we have that
-2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})\leq\theta-s\leq 2\pi
, in which case
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)
is nonzero only when
\theta-s\in[0,2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}]
; we call this
Region 1
. On the other hand, when
s
is constrained to the interval
[2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha}),2\pi]
, since
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, we have that
-2\pi\leq\theta-s\leq 2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}
, in which case
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)
is nonzero when
\theta-s\in[-2\pi,-2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})]\cup[0,2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}]
. We therefore have two more regions;
Region 2
, where
s\in[2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha}),2\pi]
and
\theta-s\in[0,2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}]
, and
Region 3
, where
s\in[2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha}),2\pi]
and
\theta-s\in[-2\pi,-2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})]
. It follows from the triangle inequality and the fact that the function
x\mapsto x^{2}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
that
K^{1}_{\varepsilon}
is bounded above by the sum of the following three terms, each corresponding to Regions 1, 2, and 3, respectively:

\displaystyle L^{1}_{\varepsilon}:=4\pi^{2}\varepsilon^{2\alpha}\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})}\big(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\varepsilon}:=4\pi^{2}\varepsilon^{2\alpha}\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\int_{2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})}^{2\pi}\big(\int_{2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle L^{3}_{\varepsilon}:=\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\int_{2\pi(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha})}^{2\pi}\big(\int_{0}^{2\pi\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-s)(s-\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

and it is straightforward to verify, using the nonnegativity of
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
and
eq.

75
, the estimates
|L^{1}_{\varepsilon}|+|L^{2}_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\varepsilon^{2\alpha}
and
|L^{3}_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha}
, for some positive constant
C
depending only on
c_{\gamma}
and
\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. It follows that, for a possibly larger constant
C
depending only on
c_{\gamma}
and
\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, there holds
K^{1}_{\varepsilon}\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha}
and, by the same argument, the same bound holds for
K^{2}_{\varepsilon}
(with the only change being that the constant
C
also depends on
\|\varphi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
). We therefore obtain from
eq.

81
, for a possibly larger constant
C
depending only on
c_{\gamma}
,
\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, and
\|\varphi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, there holds

\displaystyle\big\|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\varphi(\cdot)-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\theta-\cdot)\varphi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big\|_{H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)}\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha/2}.

Thus, returning to the bound
eq.

80
on
|J_{\varepsilon}|
, taking supremum over test functions and integrating, followed by applying the Jensen inequality and using the monotonicity of the functions
x\mapsto x^{2}
and
x\mapsto x^{1/2}
on
[0,\infty)
, we get

\|\sup_{\overset{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{per}(0,2\pi)}{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1}}J_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{per}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Omega}\varepsilon^{\alpha/2}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})^{2}\|f(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}\bigg)^{1/2},

(82)

where
C_{\Omega}
is a positive constant depending only on
|\Omega|
,
c_{\gamma}
,
\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, and
\|\varphi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. Using the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem to evaluate the double integral exactly, using also the same procedure as the one used for
eq.

76
to get
\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}=\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{y})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
,

\|\sup_{\overset{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{per}(0,2\pi)}{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1}}J_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{per}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Omega}\varepsilon^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-1}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))},

(83)

for some new positive constant
C_{\Omega}
which again only depends on
|\Omega|
,
c_{\gamma}
,
\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, and
\|\varphi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. Notice that, since
\alpha>2
, the above vanishes in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Finally, we return to the term
I_{\varepsilon}
. By taking the supremum over test functions and integrating, we find that
\|\sup_{\overset{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{per}(0,2\pi)}{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1}}I_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{per}(\Omega)}
is bounded above by

\displaystyle c_{\gamma}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\bigg|\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}({\bf x}-\mathbf{y})\|f({\bf x},\cdot)-f(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\bigg|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}\bigg)^{1/2}=:c_{\gamma}L_{\varepsilon}.

We rewrite the innermost integral above using the change of variables
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{z}={\bf x}-\mathbf{y}
. Note that
\mathbf{z}\in[-2\pi,2\pi]\times[-2\pi,2\pi]
and, moreover,
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})
is only nonzero when
\mathbf{z}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{4}A^{j}_{\varepsilon}
, where
A^{1}_{\varepsilon}=[-2\pi,-2\pi(1-\varepsilon)]\times[0,2\pi\varepsilon]
,
A^{2}_{\varepsilon}=[0,2\pi\varepsilon]\times[0,2\pi\varepsilon]
,
A^{3}_{\varepsilon}=[-2\pi,-2\pi(1-\varepsilon)]\times[-2\pi,-2\pi(1-\varepsilon)]
,
A^{4}_{\varepsilon}=[0,2\pi\varepsilon]\times[-2\pi,-2\pi(1-\varepsilon)]
. It therefore follows that, using the triangle inequality for the norm of
L^{2}(\Omega)
in the variable
{\bf x}
, there holds the inequality

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{4}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\bigg|\int_{A^{j}_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})\|f({\bf x},\cdot)-f({\bf x}-\mathbf{z},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}\bigg|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}\bigg)^{1/2}=:\sum_{j=1}^{4}L^{j}_{\varepsilon}.

Using the Minkowski integral inequality for finite measure spaces, we get

\displaystyle L^{j}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A^{j}_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\|f({\bf x},\cdot)-f({\bf x}-\mathbf{z},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x}\bigg)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}.

(84)

We now have the following claim, the proof of which we omit as it is a standard exercise. The proof relies on observing that
\mathbf{f}({\bf x})=\mathbf{f}({\bf x}-\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathbf{z}_{j})
in
(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)
for a.e.
{\bf x}\in\Omega
and then integrating in
{\bf x}
. In order to pass to the limit in
j
, one needs to use the strong continuity property of the shift operator in
L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;X)
,
i.e.
,
\lim_{\mathbf{w}\to 0}\|\tau_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;X)}=0
, where
\tau_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}(\cdot+\mathbf{w})
. The proof of this latter result is also straightforward (relying on identifying a convenient dense subset and applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem) and so we omit it.

Claim 23
.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{per}(\Omega;(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi))
. Suppose that
(\mathbf{z}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
converging towards the point
(2\pi m,2\pi n)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
for some fixed
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then,

\lim_{j\to\infty}(\int_{\Omega}\|f({\bf x},\cdot)-f({\bf x}-\mathbf{z}_{j},\cdot)\|_{(H^{1}_{per})^{\prime}(0,2\pi)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf x})^{1/2}=0
.

By rewriting
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})
in each of the squares
\{A^{j}_{\varepsilon}\}_{j=1}^{4}
in terms of the original bump function
\beta
, using the previous claim, and applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem to the right-hand side of
eq.

84
, we get
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}L^{j}_{\varepsilon}=0
for
j=1,\dots,4
. Hence
\|\sup_{\overset{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{per}(0,2\pi)}{\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(0,2\pi)}\leq 1}}I_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}_{per}(\Omega)}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This,
eq.

83
, and
eq.

78
, proves
eq.

72
.
