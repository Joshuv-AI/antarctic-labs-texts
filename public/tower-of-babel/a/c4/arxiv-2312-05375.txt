The Thermodynamic Costs of Pure Dephasing in Quantum Heat Engines: Quasistatic Efficiency at Finite Power
Raphael Weber, Susana F. Huelga, Martin B. Plenio

Abstract
Quantum heat engines are commonly believed to achieve their optimal efficiency when operated under quasi-static conditions. However, when running at finite power, they suffer effective friction due to the generation of coherences and transitions between energy eigenstates. It was noted that it is possible to increase the power of a quantum heat engine using external control schemes or suitable dephasing noise. Here, we investigate the thermodynamic cost associated with dephasing noise schemes using both numerical and analytical methods. Our findings unveil that the observed gain in power is generally not free of thermodynamic costs, as it involves heat flows from thermal baths into the dephasing bath. These contributions must be duly accounted for when determining the engine’s overall efficiency. Interestingly, we identify a particular working regime where these costs become negligible, demonstrating that quantum heat engines can be operated at any power with an efficiency per cycle that approaches arbitrarily closely that under quasistatic operation.



1
Introduction

The development of thermodynamics arose from the desire to understand the limitations of heat engines, both in realistic practical settings and under ideal operation. This pursuit led to the establishment of the fundamental laws of macroscopic equilibrium thermodynamics, which have remained remarkably stable over time, particularly in regards to macroscopic systems in equilibrium. These laws are indispensable for our understanding of energy transfer, conversion, and utilisation in science and technology. For instance, the second law of thermodynamics enables us to determine the maximum possible efficiency of heat engines and refrigerators operating in the quasi-static regime under various possible thermodynamic cycles that power for example Carnot or Otto engines.

While the requirement of remaining close to equilibrium leads to highly efficient operation, it also means that the system has to be operated quasi-statically, which ultimately leads to vanishing power output. Although this mode of operation is well tailored towards the application of the laws of thermodynamics, in practical applications, machines need to operate at finite power, which leads to departures from the ideal quasi-static regime and results in friction and losses. Thus we must aim to achieve at the same time high efficiency and high power output, and it is not immediately clear to what extent operating at finite power reduces efficiency. Novikov
[
1
]
, Chambadal
[
2
]
, as well as later Curzon and Ahlborn
[
3
]
analysed the impact of a finite heat conductance from the hot and cold heat baths to the working medium. They discovered a limit, now known as the Curzon-Ahlborn bound, on the attainable efficiency, which, at maximum power, remains independent of the heat conductance value and is considerably more restrictive than the Carnot bound. These bounds, firmly rooted in classical physics and applied to macroscopic heat engines, illustrate the fundamental trade-off that can exist between efficiency and power output in thermodynamic systems.

The exploration of microscopic or even atomic-scale heat engines, whose function may depend crucially on the laws of quantum mechanics, was initiated by the work of Scovil and Schulz-DuBois in
[
4
]
, which developed a thermodynamic model of the maser operation in the form of a quantum mechanical three-level system, and has spawned a vast body of work (see e.g.
[
5
]
for a recent monograph). In quantum heat engines, the impact of finite speed of operation on efficiency becomes even more important. A portion of the thermodynamic cycle, which is intended to be executed quasi-statically, corresponds to a mode of operation that meets the conditions of adiabaticity—namely, a slow change of parameters relative to the internal system dynamics, ensuring the system remains in the eigenstate of the instantaneous Hamiltonian throughout the process
[
6
]
. A violation of adiabaticity results in the system deviating from its instantaneous eigenstate through the generation of quantum mechanical coherences, eventually leading to transitions to other energy levels. It is well recognised that the creation of these quantum coherences represents a source of inefficiency in the thermodynamic cycle beyond those imposed by the Curzon-Ahlborn bound
[

,

,

,
10
,
11

.

To address this issue, researchers have explored external interventions that enable the restoration of adiabaticity, thereby improving efficiency at fixed power or enhancing power at fixed efficiency. For instance,
[
12
]
introduced dephasing noise in the instantaneous eigenbasis of the thermodynamic cycle of an Otto engine to suppress the build-up of coherences and thus deviations from the adiabatic trajectory. Alternatively, shortcuts to adiabaticity
[

16

However, these types of interventions raise an important question: Do they entail hidden thermodynamic costs that offset some, if not all, of the gains in efficiency when analysing a quantum heat engine subject to external controls or dephasing noise? While it is recognised that such costs can exist in the case of counter-adiabatic driving
[
17
]
and in specific studies of dephasing noise due to fermionic environments without the presence of a quantum heat engine
[
18
]
, the question remained open whether this cost is necessarily present or whether it can be made negligible in a suitable regime.

In this work, we answer this question by analysing the thermodynamic cost of pure dephasing noise in the instantaneous eigenbasis during the operation of a quantum heat engine. To achieve this, we attach a real bosonic dephasing environment to the heat engine during its thermodynamic cycle. We employ the numerically exact simulation methods of Dissipation-assisted matrix product factorization (DAMPF)
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
and Time Evolving Density matrices using Orthogonal Polynomials Algorithm (TEDOPA)
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
to examine the internal dynamics of the working medium and the dephasing bath, as well as their mutual interaction. This enables us to track all work and heat flows within the quantum heat engine, including those involving the dephasing environment. We utilize these numerical methods to validate an Ansatz that forms the basis of our analytical treatment of the observed phenomenology. On the one hand, our analysis reveals a heat transport from the thermal baths to the dephasing bath that remains hidden when the dephasing noise is modeled with a Markovian master equation. On the other hand, we demonstrate that for a suitable choice of the parameter regime of the dephasing environment, we can reduce its thermodynamic cost to an arbitrary degree. This allows us to increase the efficiency of the heat engine at any given fixed power to approach that of a quasistatically working heat engine.

2.3
Effects of finite cycle time

A QHE that uses strokes of finite duration experiences a reduction in efficiency due to two different effects. First, assuming a finite coupling strength between work medium and thermal bath, the working medium cannot thermalise completely to the temperature of the attached thermal bath anymore. This imperfect equilibration, even when assuming that the working medium is always in a Gibbs state, leads to the well-known Curzon-Ahlborn bound

\displaystyle\eta_{\max}\leq\eta_{\text{CA}}=1-\sqrt{\frac{T_{\text{c}}}{T_{\text{h}}}}

(24)

for the efficiency at maximum power,
\eta_{\max}

[
3
]
.
However, as the coupling strength to the thermal baths may, at least in principle, be increased arbitrarily, and therefore the thermalisation time made arbitrarily small, we will not consider the role of imperfect thermalisation in this work.

\hat{\sigma}^{+}
is the raising operator of the spin (i.e. the work medium) while
\hat{a}
is the annihilation operator of a harmonic oscillator of the environment. In that case, the work medium equilibrates rapidly, and coherences between the work medium and the environment vanish asymptotically. Therefore, the expectation value of the coupling Hamiltonian with the steady state of work medium and environment
[
35
]
vanishes, which, in turn, implies a vanishing thermodynamical cost for decoupling the environment from the work medium. This can be confirmed in both the DAMPF or reaction rate model, as well as in a TEDOPA picture of the interaction between work medium and environment.
Instead we focus on a second cause of loss of efficiency in QHE operated at finite power, quantum friction
[
12
,
36
]
, which appears in compression and expansion strokes of finite duration.

As described in
Section

2.1
, the eigenbasis of the time-dependent Hamiltonian of the working medium evolves during the compression and expansion strokes. When executed at finite rate, the working medium is not able to follow the energy eigenbasis adiabatically. As a result, coherences between instantaneous energy eigenstates are typically generated which, in turn, lead to changes in the population of the instantaneous energy eigenstates. This process requires additional work therefore reducing the extracted work per cycle.

Quantum friction can be suppressed if coherences are made to vanish and populations remain invariant. In
[
12
]
it was suggested to reduce quantum friction and the associated heat dissipation with the help of pure dephasing in the instantaneous eigenbasis of the working medium. The stronger the dephasing noise, the more effectively it suppresses the creation of coherences between energy eigenstates and forces the state to remain diagonal in the eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian thus following the adiabatic trajectory.

As dephasing can be made arbitrarily strong, this observation seems to suggest that a heat engine can be run at arbitrarily high speed while remaining arbitrarily close to the efficiency of a quasi-static heat engine. In the following chapters, we will examine this statement critically and investigate the effect of dephasing noise on the performance of the QHE defined in
Section

2.1
by taking into account any thermodynamic cost that it may incur.

. Additionally, the harmonic mode is damped by a thermal Lindblad dissipator at zero temperature with damping rate
\gamma
. As described in the main text, this particular configuration acts in the same way on the reduced density operator of the working medium as a microscopically described bath with a Lorentzian spectral density
J(\omega)
, centered at
\omega_{0}
with width
\gamma
and height
2\Gamma^{2}/\gamma
.

We begin with the microscopic description of a dephasing bath which is coupled to the working medium which is described by the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}(t)
from
Section

2.1
. The total microscopic Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}(t)+\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)+\hat{H}_{\text{B}}

(25)

consists of three terms. The time-independent bath Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{B}}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential\omega\,\omega\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\omega}\hat{a}_{\omega}

(26)

describes the free evolution of the bath that consists of continuously distributed harmonic modes with creation (annihilation) operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\omega}
(
\hat{a}_{\omega}
). The interaction of this bosonic environment with the working medium is described by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)=\hat{\sigma}_{\text{z}}(t)\otimes\!\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\differential\omega\,\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{J}(\omega)}{\pi}}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\omega}+\hat{a}_{\omega}\right).

(27)

The interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)
which commutes with the Hamiltonian of the working medium at all times thus describes pure dephasing in the instantaneous eigenbasis
\{\ket{\g(t)},\ket{\e(t)}\}
of the working medium. The dynamics of the working medium under the action of this environment is fully characterized by the spectral density
\mathcal{J}(\omega)

[
27
,
41
]
. The continuum of harmonic modes and their coupling structure is not best suited for numerical simulations. The TEDOPA approach overcomes this difficulty by introducing a unitary mode transformation
U_{n}(x)
to an infinite but discrete set of eigenmodes with annihilation operators

\hat{b}_{n}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential\omega\,U_{n}(\omega)\,\hat{a}_{\omega},

(28)

for which the free bath Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{B}}
and the interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)
can be re-expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{B}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{b}_{n}+t_{n}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{b}_{n+1}+\hat{b}_{n}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n+1})

(29)

and

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)=c_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\text{z}}(t)\otimes\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{0}+\hat{b}_{0}\right).

(30)

Thus, the new modes describe a one-dimensional semi-infinite harmonic chain with nearest-neighbor interaction, which couples to the working medium exclusively via the first site of the chain - a geometry that is particularly well suited for efficient simulation with tensor networks
[
23
,
22
]
. Moreover, in TEDOPA we can keep track of the exact state of the environment for the entire duration of the simulation, allowing for the calculation of arbitrary expectation values involving environment operators. These features enable a detailed analysis of correlations and energy flows between the working medium and dephasing environment, which is necessary for the analysis of the thermodynamical cost of dephasing noise. In the numerical simulations, we truncate the dimension of the local Hilbert spaces of each harmonic mode and the length of the semi-infinite chain to a finite size (the incurred error can be bounded
[
42
,
43
]
rigorously). The finite length of the chain limits the total simulation time, as excitations from the working medium travel through the chain with finite speed and are reflected at the end of the chain. The time-evolution of the working medium accumulates significant error only after the reflected excitations reach the working medium again. This can be prevented by choosing a sufficiently long chain or the addition of a Markovian closure at the end of a shorter chain
[
44
]
. Despite such improvements, TEDOPA simulations require significant computational resources, especially for strong dephasing and fast operation of the QHE. In these regimes, we observe strong correlations between working medium and bath, as well as highly populated harmonic modes in the initial parts of the TEDOPA chain, requiring accurate description with large local dimensions in the tensor network simulations.

In the next section we address this challenge by specialising the environmental spectral density in a manner that allows us to obtain the numerically exact dynamics of the working medium and, at the same time, allows us to keep track of the energy which is transferred from the working medium to the dephasing bath. This is achieved within the framework of the Dissipation-Assisted Matrix Product Factorization (DAMPF) approach
[
19
,
20
]

(see also the reaction coordinate approach in thermodynamical machines

[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
) which we introduce in the next
Sections

3.2
and
3.3
.

3.2
Dephasing DAMPF bath

The DAMPF approach
[
19
,
20
]
represents the environmental spectral density as a combination of Lorentzians which correspond to harmonic oscillators that are damped with local dissipators to a Markovian environment. For the following we simplify this description further by restricting attention to a single damped harmonic oscillator with creation (annihilation) operator
\hat{b}^{\dagger}\,(\hat{b})
that is coupled to the working medium and, to facilitate analytical expressions, to a heat bath at zero temperature as shown in
Fig.

2
. The finite temperature case will be treated later. This model yields the same bath correlation function as the microscopic description
Eq.

25
with a Lorentzian spectral density

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{2\Gamma^{2}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+4(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}}

(31)

and therefore leads to the same dynamics in the working medium
[
45
]
. The Hamiltonian of this DAMPF system reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{\text{D}}}(t)=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}(t)+\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)+\hat{H}_{\text{osc}}

(32)

with the interaction Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)=\Gamma\,\hat{\sigma}_{\text{z}}(t)\otimes\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}\right)

(33)

and the free Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{osc}}=\omega_{0}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}

(34)

of the harmonic mode. The coupling of this harmonic mode to a zero temperature Markovian bath is described by the Lindbladian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{osc}}[\hat{\rho}]=\gamma\,\left(\hat{b}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b},\hat{\rho}\}\right).

(35)

The full master equation of the working medium and the harmonic mode reads

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\,\commutator{\Ht[\DAMPF][t]}{\rhot[][t]}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{osc}}[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]

(36)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}(t)[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]+\mathcal{L}_{\text{h}}(t)[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]

where we also include the thermal baths from
Section

2.1
.

In the DAMPF picture we have access only to the effective mode
\hat{b}
and we are not able to keep track of the state of the continuum of modes that realise the Lorentzian spectral density. Despite of this limitation, we can determine the bath energy
\langle\hat{H}_{\text{B}}(t)\rangle
which is required to quantify the thermodynamical cost of dephasing noise by separating the unitary and non-unitary part in the time-evolution of
Eq.

36
via the Suzuki-Trotter decomposition
[
46
,
47
]
. In leading order, we find that the map that evolves the state unitarily from time
t
to time
t+\differential t
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\differential t}[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]=\text{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{\text{\text{D}}}(t)\differential t}\,\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)\,\text{e}^{\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{\text{\text{D}}}(t)\differential t}

(37)

The non-unitary part of the time-evolution

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(tot)}}[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]=

\displaystyle\text{e}^{(\mathcal{L}_{\text{osc}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}(t)+\mathcal{L}_{\text{h}}(t))\differential t}[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]

(38)

can be split into three consecutive maps

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(tot)}}=\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(\text{osc})}}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(\text{c})}}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(\text{h})}}

(39)

without any Trotter error since
\commutator{\mathcal{L}_\x(t)}{\mathcal{L}_\osc(t)}=0
at all times
t
and for
\text{x}\in\{\text{h},\text{c}\}
and
\commutator{\mathcal{L}_\hot(t)}{\mathcal{L}_\cold(t)}=0
as those two environments do not couple to the working medium at the same time.
We define the formal time-evolution of each Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{\text{x}}(t)
via the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(\text{x})}}[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]=\text{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{\text{x}}(t)\differential t}[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)].

(40)

There is no need for time-ordering as all Lindbladians are piecewise constant. Combining the unitary and the Lindbladian evolutions, the second order Suzuki-Trotter decomposition is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t+\differential t)=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{U}_{\differential t/2}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(th)}}\circ\mathcal{G}^{(\text{osc})}_{\differential t}\circ\mathcal{U}_{\differential t/2}\right)[\hat{\rho}_{\text{}}(t)]

\displaystyle+\order{\dd t^3}\,,

(41)

where the thermal propagator
\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(th)}}=\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(\text{h})}}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\differential t}^{\text{(\text{c})}}
accounts for the time-evolution of both thermal baths.
Now, we measure the energy of the system before and after the non-unitary evolution to obtain the differential energy

\displaystyle\differential E_{\text{x}}(t)=\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF][t] \left(\mathcal{G}^{(\x)}_{\dd t} [\rhot[][t]] - \rhot[][t]\right)}

(42)

that is exchanged between the system and the Lindbladian bath
\text{x}\in\{\text{osc},\text{h},\text{c}\}
within the time
\differential t
. The total exchanged energy is calculated with a numerical integration of the differential energy. The dephasing bath consists of the harmonic mode and the Lindblad dissipator. Hence, the energy stored in the dephasing bath

\displaystyle E_{\text{deph}}(t)=\Tr{\hat{H}_{\osc}\hat{\rho}(t)}-\int\limits_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,\frac{\differential E_{\text{osc}}(t^{\prime})}{\differential t^{\prime}}

(43)

consists of those two energy contributions. The minus sign accounts for the fact that dissipated energy
\differential E_{\text{osc}}
from
Eq.

42
is negative if energy leaves the system to the bath. We have verified the accuracy of
Eq.

43
in simulations with finite
\differential t
using the numerical exact TEDOPA simulations of the microscopic description of the dephasing bath from
Eq.

25
using the python library
Mpnum

[
48
]
. The numerical results are presented in
Fig.

3
, where the calculations of the time-dependent interaction energy
\langle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)\rangle
are compared in (a) and (d) and the results for the time-dependent bath energy
E_{\text{deph}}(t)=\langle\hat{H}_{\text{B}}(t)\rangle
are compared in (b) and (e) for both numerical methods. We use a finite support
[\omega_{0}-a,\omega_{0}+a]
for the TEDOPA simulations as the couplings
t_{\mathrm{i}}
of the TEDOPA chain Hamiltonian from
Eq.

29
diverge in the limit
a\rightarrow\infty
as shown in panel (f). Large coupling strengths require significant computational resources as the characteristic time-scale of the interaction between neighboring sites is proportional to the reciprocal coupling strength. Nevertheless, the numerical results of the TEDOPA method converge towards those of DAMPF in the limit
a\rightarrow\infty
according to panels (d) and (e).

3.4
Power maximisation

In
Section

4.2
, we investigate the performance of the QHE for different dephasing strengths, specifically focusing on the efficiency at maximum power output. To achieve this, we fix the parameters of the working medium and the dephasing bath, and then optimise the power of the QHE with respect to the stroke durations
\tau_{\text{x}}
for
\text{x}\in\{\text{com},\text{h},\text{exp},\text{c}\}
. The optimisation is performed by a gradient search in the four search parameters
3
3

3

This optimisation algorithm does not guarantee to find the global power maximum. However, the results provide a lower bound of the power, which supports the main statement of this work.
.

There are two different possible optimisation targets for the QHE under the action of a dephasing environment. The first restricts the definition of the power from
Eq.

14
to the energetic changes of the two-level system, which is considered to be the only working medium. In other words, we take the definitions of work and heat exchange from
Eqs.

9
,
10
,
12
and
13
with the reduced system states
\hat{\rho}_{\text{S,i}}=\Tr_{\text{osc}}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\text{i}}\right\}
where the indices
\mathrm{i}
label the end points of the strokes in the Otto cycle (see
Fig.

1
).

The alternative is providing a more complete definition of work and heat that also accounts for the energy of the dephasing environment which can be interpreted as an extension of the working medium that has to be taken into account in thermodynamic considerations. Hence, we redefine the work that is extracted during the compression stroke from
Eq.

9
by also considering the interaction energy
\langle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)\rangle
and the energy
E_{\text{deph}}(t)
contained in the dephasing bath from
Eq.

43
. The final expression of the extracted work with respect to the total energy in working medium and dephasing bath reads

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\text{com}}^{\text{(tot)}}=

\displaystyle\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\hot] \, \rhot[1]}-\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\cold] \, \rhot[0]}

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{1}}\differential t\,\frac{\differential E_{\text{osc}}(t)}{\differential t}\,,

(44)

where the times
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
are the start and end times of the compression stroke. The work done on the working medium during the expansion stroke

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\text{exp}}^{\text{(tot)}}=

\displaystyle\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\cold] \, \rhot[3]}-\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\hot] \, \rhot[2]}

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\tau_{2}}^{\tau_{3}}\differential t\,\frac{\differential E_{\text{osc}}(t)}{\differential t}

(45)

is redefined analogously. The heat exchanges during the thermalisation can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\text{h}}^{\text{(tot)}}=

\displaystyle\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\hot] \, \rhot[2]}-\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\hot] \, \rhot[1]}

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}\differential t\,\frac{\differential E_{\text{osc}}(t)}{\differential t}

(46)

for the hot bath and as

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\text{c}}^{\text{(tot)}}=

\displaystyle\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\cold] \, \rhot[4]}-\Tr{\Ht[\DAMPF,\cold] \, \rhot[3]}

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\tau_{3}}^{\tau_{4}}\differential t\,\frac{\differential E_{\text{osc}}(t)}{\differential t}

(47)

for the cold bath.
Moreover, we define the total extracted work per cycle

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\text{ext.}}^{\text{(tot)}}=-\left(\Delta W_{\text{com}}^{\text{(tot)}}+\Delta W_{\text{exp}}^{\text{(tot)}}\right)

(48)

analogously to the extracted work
\Delta W_{\text{ext.}}^{\text{(sys)}}
from
Eq.

11
that only considers the energy of the working medium. The resulting definition for the power reads

\displaystyle P^{\text{(tot)}}=\frac{\Delta W_{\text{ext}}^{\text{(tot)}}}{\tau_{\text{cycle}}}

(49)

that includes the total energy of working medium and dephasing bath. The corresponding efficiency is given by

\displaystyle\eta^{\text{(tot)}}=\frac{\Delta W_{\text{ext}}^{\text{(tot)}}}{\Delta Q_{\text{h}}^{\text{(tot)}}}.

(50)

The optimisation is performed with respect to the power in steady state operation. For that, we simulate the dynamics of the heat engine for some cycles until we see convergence of the results of two consecutive cycles. Our simulation results show that this stationary regime is reached very fast, typically after three cycles.

3.5
Effective dephasing rate

The dephasing bath is fully described by the spectral density
J(\omega)
from
Eq.

31
with the parameters
\Gamma
,
\gamma
and
\omega_{0}
. However, it will be convenient to define a single parameter that characterizes the effect of the bath in order to facilitate the comparison of different dephasing baths. Hence, we calculate an effective dephasing rate
\Gamma_{\text{eff}}(\Gamma,\gamma,\omega_{0})
that is then related to the ability of the bath to boost the performance of the QHE. To this end, we analyse the time-evolution of an initial preparation of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\e(t_0)}_{\text{S}}+\ket{\g(t_0)}_{\text{S}})\otimes\ket{0}_{\text{B}},

(51)

that contains coherences between the energy eigenstates
\ket{\e(t_0)}
and
\ket{\g(t_0)}
, under the time-independent microscopic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{}}(t_{0})
from
Eq.

25
with fixed time parameter
t_{0}
. We can solve the time-evolution

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}=\mathcal{U}(t)\ket{\psi_0}

(52)

analytically as the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{}}(t_{0})
describes the time-independent spin-boson model
[
41
]
. Afterwards, we use this result to calculate the absolute value of the coherences

\displaystyle|c(t)|=|\innerproduct{\psi(t)}{\g}_{\text{S}}\innerproduct{\e}{\psi(t)}|=\frac{1}{2}\text{e}^{\Gamma(t)}

(53)

and the decoherence function
\Gamma(t)
. The factor
\frac{1}{2}
ensures the initial condition
\Gamma(0)=0
for the initial state that was chosen in
Eq.

51
. The analytical solution of the spin-boson model
[
41
]
yields the decoherence function

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential\omega\,\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{\omega}(t)|^{2}

(54)

with the amplitudes

\displaystyle\alpha_{\omega}(t)=2\sqrt{\frac{J(\omega)}{\pi}}\frac{1-\text{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}{\omega}.

(55)

The decoherence function shows oscillations for small times
t
as the environment is not Markovian. However, we aim for the calculation of an effective dephasing rate
\Gamma_{\text{eff}}
which corresponds to a linear approximation

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)\approx-\Gamma_{\text{eff}}\,t

(56)

of the decoherence function which is, as we will show now, valid in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
. For this, we take the spectral density from
Eq.

31
and plug it into
Eq.

54
, which, after substituting
\omega t=x
, yields the integral

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{8\Gamma^{2}\gamma\,t}{\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\frac{1-\cos(x)}{x^{2}\,\left(\gamma^{2}+4\left(\frac{x}{t}-\omega_{0}\right)^{2}\right)}

(57)

that we would like to solve in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
.
The integrand

\displaystyle g(x,t)=\frac{1-\cos(x)}{x^{2}\,\left(\gamma^{2}+4\left(\frac{x}{t}-\omega_{0}\right)^{2}\right)}

(58)

converges uniformly

\displaystyle g(x,t)\xrightarrow{\text{unif.}}g(x)=\frac{1-\cos(x)}{x^{2}\,\left(\gamma^{2}+4\omega_{0}^{2}\right)}

(59)

which implies that we can calculate the integral over
g(x,t)
in the limit of large times as

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\differential x\,g(x,t)=\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\differential x\,g(x)=\frac{\pi}{\gamma^{2}+4\omega_{0}^{2}}.

(60)

We use this result to determine the expression for the decoherence function as

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)\approx-\frac{8\Gamma^{2}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+4\omega_{0}^{2}}\,t\,,

(61)

which is valid in the limit of large times
t\rightarrow\infty
. This linear approximation allows us to define the effective decoherence rate

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{eff}}=\frac{8\Gamma^{2}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+4\omega_{0}^{2}}

(62)

that characterizes the time-scale on which coherences are damped in the QHE. Hence, this quantity determines the ability of the dephasing bath to boost the power of the QHE. For this reason, the effective dephasing rate
Eq.

62
will be used as the main parameter describing the dephasing bath in the next chapters.

p_{\text{g}}(t)=\bra{\g(t)}\hat{\rho}_{\text{S}}(t)\ket{\g(t)}
, occurring at finite driving speeds. These oscillations enable a fine-tuning of the compression and expansion stroke durations,
\tau_{\text{com}}
and
\tau_{\text{exp}}
, respectively, so that the final populations closely match the initial populations. This approach approximates a quasi-adiabatic process despite the intermediate non-adiabatic evolution - an effect utilised in the so-called shortcuts to adiabaticity
[
36
,
16
]
. However, the stroke duration is constrained by the characteristic time-scale of these oscillations, as the initial and final populations cannot be made to match if the strokes are shorter than one period of these oscillations. Indeed, the optimal cycle times
\tau_{\text{cycle}}
remain essentially constant over a wide range of dephasing rates, as shown in
Fig.

4
(b).

In the presence of dephasing noise, those coherent oscillations are damped, leading to losses. As a result, we can no longer obtain a revival of the initial populations at the end of the compression/expansion stroke. The extracted work per cycle is therefore reduced, as shown in
Fig.

4
(d), and the power drops, as observed in
Fig.

4
(a). However, the QHE can operate faster in the regime of strong dephasing rates, as the coherent oscillations of the populations are overdamped due to the strong dephasing noise. This overdamping allows for fast, nearly adiabatic compression and expansion strokes. The optimal cycle time drops rapidly in the regime of strong dephasing noise, as shown in
Fig.

4
(b), and overcompensates the frictional losses. Hence, the maximum power increases, even though less work is extracted from the QHE in each cycle, as observed in
Fig.

4
(d).

Finally, we shift our focus to the efficiencies of the QHE, corresponding to the two different power definitions in
Fig.

4
(c). We observe that if the energy stored in the dephasing bath from
Eq.

43
is included in the thermodynamical considerations, the corresponding efficiency
\eta^{\text{(tot)}}
drops significantly for large dephasing rates. In contrast, the efficiency
\eta^{\text{(sys)}}
, which only considers the energy changes within the working medium, remains near the maximum efficiency
\eta^{\text{(sys)}}_{\max}=0.106
from
Eq.

23
over the entire range of effective damping rates.

The major difference in the behaviour of the efficiencies
\eta^{\text{(sys)}}_{\max}
and
\eta^{\text{(tot)}}_{\max}
indicates that the consideration of the energy of the dephasing bath is crucial for a correct thermodynamical description of dephasing assisted QHEs. If it is ignored, the thermodynamical cost of dephasing noise is hidden and it appears to be possible to increase the power
P_{\max}^{\text{(sys)}}
without loss of efficiency
\eta^{\text{(sys)}}
. In the following
Section

4.3
we investigate the origin of the drop of the efficiency
\eta^{\text{(tot)}}
in more detail.

The exploration of thermodynamical uncertainty relations for our dephasing assisted quantum heat engines will form the subject of a future work.

7
Conclusions

The fundamental model of a thermodynamic heat engine considers an engine connected
to two heat baths: one hot and one cold. Through judicious cyclic operation, the
engine can extract work by transferring heat from the hot bath to the cold bath.
The ultimate efficiency of such an engine is constrained by the laws of thermodynamics
and is achieved through quasi-static operation, that is, operating at zero power.
These limitations also extend to quantum heat engines.

However, operating at a finite power introduces additional quantum friction due to
the creation of coherences during the operation of the QHE. While the laws of
thermodynamics do not explicitly dictate fundamental limits on efficiency at
finite power, it has been suggested that applying appropriate dephasing mechanisms
could enhance efficiency and power. Nonetheless, subjecting the machine to a dephasing
environment introduces in effect a third heat bath, potentially leading to additional
heat flows. These additional flows must be considered when accurately assessing the
thermodynamic efficiency.

Our study demonstrates that dephasing might increase power, often but not always, at
the expense of efficiency. The central outcome of our work is a meticulous evaluation
of the efficiency of a quantum heat engine under dephasing and a proof that, under
specific conditions, a quantum heat engine can achieve for any finite power an efficiency
approaching that under quasi-static operation.

Appendix B
Enhancing power at constant efficiency with a dephasing bath at
T=0

Here we aim to find parameter scalings that allow to increase the total power of the QHE

\displaystyle P^{\text{(tot)}}=\frac{\Delta W_{\text{ext.}}^{\text{(tot)}}}{\tau_{\text{cycle}}}

(118)

while keeping the total efficiency

\displaystyle\eta^{\text{(tot)}}=\frac{\Delta W_{\text{ext.}}^{\text{(tot)}}}{\Delta Q_{\text{h}}^{\text{(sys)}}+\Delta Q_{\text{h}}^{\text{(diss)}}}

(119)

at a constant level. To this end, the stroke durations of the machine

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{x}}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\tau_{\text{x}}

(120)

are reduced by the factor
\lambda>1
for all
\text{x}\in\{\text{com},\text{h},\exp,\text{c}\}
resulting in the shorter cycle time
\tau_{\text{cycle}}^{\prime}=\lambda^{-1}\tau_{\text{cycle}}
. At the same time, we keep the heat exchange between system and thermal bath
\Delta Q^{\text{(sys)}}_{\text{th}}
constant by ensuring that the total thermalisation of the working medium, characterized by

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{th}}^{\prime}\,\tau_{\text{th}}^{\prime}=\text{const},

(121)

does not change, i.e. the coupling to the thermal baths increases as
\gamma_{\text{th}}^{\prime}=\lambda\gamma_{\text{th}}
. Next, we consider the conditions that have to be fulfilled in order to keep
\Delta W^{\text{(tot)}}_{\text{ext.}}
constant. In addition to condition
Eq.

121
, that fixes the initial population before compression and expansion, one has to consider that faster compression and expansion strokes lead to stronger quantum friction in the working medium, that has to be suppressed with stronger dephasing noise. The extracted work per cycle does not change if a bigger effective dephasing rate
\Gamma_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}=\lambda\Gamma_{\text{eff}}
compensates for the faster strokes such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}\,\tau_{\text{x}}^{\prime}=\text{const},

(122)

as the effective dephasing rate determines the ability of the machine to suppress quantum friction. We explicitly focus on the Lorentzian spectrum as this can be simulated easily. The corresponding effective dephasing rate from
Eq.

62
scales as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}=\frac{8\Gamma^{\prime 2}\,\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma^{\prime 2}+4\omega_{0}^{\prime 2}}\propto\lambda

(123)

according to
Eqs.

120
and
122
. We encode this scaling in the three free parameters via

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}=\sqrt{\lambda}\Gamma.

(124)

which leaves the condition

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma^{\prime 2}+4\omega_{0}^{\prime 2}}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+4\omega_{0}^{2}}=\text{const.}

(125)

for
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\omega^{\prime}_{0}
. We eliminate one variable by solving for
\omega_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\omega_{0}^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\gamma^{\prime}\left(\gamma_{0}-\gamma^{\prime}\right)}

(126)

with

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}=\frac{4\omega_{0}^{2}+\gamma^{2}}{\gamma}=32896

(127)

which is equivalent to
Eqs.

82
and
83
. Finally, we aim for the scaling of the variable
\gamma^{\prime}
by enforcing constant dissipated heat during the hot thermalisation

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\text{h}}^{\text{(diss)}}\propto\Gamma_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}\frac{\omega_{0}^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})}{\gamma^{\prime}}\gamma_{\text{h}}^{\prime}\tau_{\text{h}}^{\prime}=\text{const.}

(128)

which is obtained from
Eq.

78
by using the explicit expression of the effective dephasing rate
Eq.

62
. This expression reduces to

\displaystyle\lambda\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{0}-\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma^{\prime}}}=\text{const}

(129)

with the help of
Eqs.

121
,
123
and
126
. We introduce the new variable
\Delta\gamma=\gamma_{0}-\gamma
and we notice that the dissipated heat from
Eq.

128

\Delta\gamma\rightarrow 0
as
\omega_{0}^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})\rightarrow 0\,(\gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow 0)
. We are in particular interested in the regime
\Delta\gamma\ll\gamma_{0}
and expand
Eq.

129
to the lowest order to obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{0}-\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma^{\prime}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma_{0}}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\Delta\gamma^{\prime}}{\gamma_{0}}\right)\right),

(130)

\displaystyle\Delta\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\Delta\gamma.

(131)

For the simulations, we use the same parameters as in
Section

6.1
and fix the damping rate
\gamma

\gamma_{0}

\gamma=\gamma_{0}-\Delta\gamma
where
\Delta\gamma

We observe that the extracted work per cycle and the efficiency of the QHE remain nearly constant over a large range of the scaling parameter
\lambda

\lambda

\lambda

5.1

The trend of the trace distance suggests that the QHE’s performance will deteriorate further if larger values of
\lambda
are considered. As a result the scaling
relations used here, which are based on our analytical Ansatz for the state of the QHE, will need to be adapted to achieve full
convergence to the quasi-static efficiency of the QHE. Nonetheless, by utilizing a dephasing bath, we manage to increase the power

\penalty\ 10^{3}
