Effective ℤ 3 {\mathbb{Z}}_{3} model for finite-density QCD with tensor networks
*

Abstract
The tensor renormalization group is a promising numerical method used to study lattice statistical field theories. However, this approach is computationally expensive in 2+1 and 3+1 dimensions. Here we use tensor renormalization group methods to study an effective three-dimensional ℤ 3 {\mathbb{Z}}_{3} model for the heavy-quark, high-temperature, strong-coupling limit of single-flavor 3+1 dimensional quantum chromodynamics. Our results are cross-checked using the worm Monte Carlo algorithm. We present the phase diagram of the model through the measurement of the Polyakov loop, the nearest-neighbor Polyakov loop correlator, and their susceptibilities. The tensor renormalization group results are in good agreement with the literature.

1
Introduction

Tensor-network methods provide a tool to approximately coarse-grain a classical partition function. Moreover, in certain cases, the method is immune to the sign problem, since there is no probabilistic sampling involved. Here we apply two tensor methods—the higher-order tensor renormalization group (HOTRG)
[
1
]
, and the the triad tensor renormalization group (TTRG)
[
2
]
—in conjunction with the worm Monte Carlo algorithm
[
3
]
, to study an effective theory for one-flavor, finite-density, 3+1-dimensional quantum chromodynamics (QCD). The effective theory has a sign problem in the original formulation, however this can be overcome with a change of variables, making the model ideal to test the tensor methods against the Monte Carlo results. We first derive the effective theory, then provide a tensor representation of the theory, and finally report our results.

2
Single-flavor, finite-density QCD

The starting action is the standard lattice Wilson gauge action, and Wilson single-flavor fermion action.
The action has two parts,
S=S_{g}+S_{f}
, with the gauge action given by

\displaystyle S_{g}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{3}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\left[\frac{a}{a_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\text{Re}[\text{Tr}\left[U_{x,i4}\right]]+\frac{a_{t}}{a}\sum_{i<j=1}^{3}\text{Re}[\text{Tr}\left[U_{x,ij}\right]]\right]

(1)

and the fermion action by

\displaystyle S_{f}=\sum_{x,y}\bar{\psi}_{x}M_{xy}\psi_{y},\quad M

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}-\kappa\frac{a_{t}}{a}H_{s}-\kappa H_{t}

(2)

with the spatial and temporal hoppings

\displaystyle H_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(T_{i}^{+}+T_{i}^{-}\right),\quad H_{t}=e^{\tilde{\mu}a_{t}}T_{4}^{+}+e^{-\tilde{\mu}a_{t}}T_{4}^{-}

(3)

and

\displaystyle(T_{\nu}^{\pm})_{xy}

\displaystyle=(1\pm\gamma_{\nu})U_{x,\pm\nu}\delta_{y,x\pm\hat{\nu}}.

(4)

Here, the gauge coupling enters through
\beta=6/g^{2}
and we identify
\kappa
as the fermion hopping parameter. The chemical potential is
\tilde{\mu}
, and
a
and
a_{t}
are the spatial and temporal lattice spacings, respectively. In anticipation of deriving the effective theory, we define the temperature as given by the inverse physical extent of the lattice,

T=\frac{1}{N_{t}a_{t}},

(5)

with
N_{t}
the number of lattice sites in the temporal direction. We work on a finite lattice with anti-periodic boundary conditions in the temporal direction, and periodic boundary conditions in the spatial directions.

3
The effective action

The effective action we study corresponds to a particular limiting case. To derive it we follow Ref.
[
4
]
closely. We consider the strong-coupling, high-temperature limit, where we take the temporal extent of the lattice very short—in this case we set
N_{t}=1
—and shrink the temporal lattice spacing. This corresponds to

\displaystyle\beta\ll 1,\quad a_{t}\ll 1.

(6)

In addition we take the large-chemical-potential, large-quark-mass limit, which in the action corresponds to

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}\gg 1,\quad\kappa\ll 1.

(7)

In
S_{g}
spatial plaquettes are suppressed by a factor of
a_{t}^{2}
relative to the temporal plaquettes, and in
S_{f}
spatial hopping is suppressed. Since the confinement-deconfinement transition is believed to be related to center-symmetry breaking, we replace the
SU(3)
group elements by elements of the center of the group,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
, which is an Abelian group. For
S_{g}
we find

\displaystyle S_{g}\rightarrow-\frac{\beta}{2a_{t}}\sum_{x}\sum_{\nu=1}^{3}P^{*}_{x}P_{x+\hat{\nu}}+\text{c.c.}

(8)

and for
S_{f}

\displaystyle S_{f}\rightarrow-\kappa\sum_{x}\left[e^{\mu}P_{x}+e^{-\mu}P^{*}_{x}\right]

(9)

with
P_{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
, which can be interpreted as a Polyakov loop in the short, compactified temporal direction, and
\mu=a_{t}\tilde{\mu}
. This results in an effective action

S_{\text{eff}}=-\sum_{x}\left[\tau\sum_{\nu=1}^{3}\left(P^{*}_{x}P_{x+\hat{\nu}}+\text{c.c.}\right)+\left(\eta P_{x}+\bar{\eta}P^{*}_{x}\right)\right]

(10)

with
\tau\equiv\beta/2a_{t}
and
\eta\equiv\kappa e^{\mu}
and
\bar{\eta}=\kappa e^{-\mu}
. A graphical illustration of the Polyakov loop variables on the lattice can be seen on the left-hand side of Fig.
1
. The effective theory lives in three Euclidean dimensions, and is to be interpreted as a statistical theory, in equilibrium, in three spatial dimensions.

4
The tensor network

Figure 1:
(left) An illustration of the original degrees of freedom. At each site there is a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
element (a Polyakov loop) which interacts with its nearest neighbors. (right) In the Fourier basis, there are “charges” and “currents” on the sites, and links, respectively, which must obey a conservation-law constraint at each site.

The partition function for the effective action describing
this theory of interacting
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
“spins” is given by,

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{\{P\}}e^{-S_{\text{eff}}},

(11)

where the sum,
\sum_{\{P\}}
, means a sum over all possible configurations of the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
variables. This can be recast to a theory of “currents” and “charges” by Fourier expansion of the elementary Boltzmann weights
[
5
]
,

\displaystyle\exp(\tau[P_{x}P_{x+\hat{\nu}}^{*}+\text{c.c.}])

\displaystyle=C\hskip-12.91663pt\sum_{n_{x,\nu}\in\{-1,0,1\}}\hskip-12.91663ptB^{|n_{x,\nu}|}(P_{x}P_{x+\hat{\nu}}^{*})^{n_{x,\nu}}

(12)

and

\displaystyle\exp(\eta P_{x}+\bar{\eta}P_{x}^{*})

\displaystyle=\hskip-8.61108pt\sum_{m_{x}\in\{-1,0,1\}}\hskip-8.61108ptV_{m_{x}}P_{x}^{m_{x}}

(13)

where
B
and
V
can be found by inverting the above. These expansions allow the original
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
variables to be summed over, leaving only the
n
and
m
integer variables as the new degrees of freedom. The resulting partition function is written as,

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{\{n\}}\sum_{\{m\}}\left(\prod_{x,\nu}B_{n_{x,\nu}}(\tau)\right)\left(\prod_{x}V_{m_{x}}(\eta,\bar{\eta})\Delta^{(3)}(j_{x})\right),

(14)

with
j_{x}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{3}(n_{x,\nu}-n_{x-\hat{\nu},\nu})+m_{x}
, and
\Delta^{(3)}(j_{x})=\delta_{j_{x}\text{mod}3,0}
. A graphical illustration of the new degrees of freedom on the lattice can be seen in Fig.
1
on the right-hand side.

A tensor network can be built from the tensor,

\displaystyle T^{(x)}_{n_{x-\hat{1},1}n_{x,1}n_{x-\hat{2},2}n_{x,2}n_{x-\hat{3},3}n_{x,3}}=\sqrt{B_{n_{x-\hat{1},1}}B_{n_{x,1}}B_{n_{x-\hat{2},2}}B_{n_{x,2}}B_{n_{x-\hat{3},3}}B_{n_{x,3}}(\tau)}V_{m_{x}}(\eta,\bar{\eta})\Delta^{(3)}(j_{x})

(15)

and the partition function written as a tensor trace

\displaystyle Z=\text{Tr}\left[\prod_{x}T^{(x)}\right].

(16)

We calculate the average Polyakov loop and average nearest-neighbor correlator, and their susceptibilities,

\displaystyle\langle P\rangle=\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\eta},\quad\langle E\rangle=-\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\tau}

(17)

\displaystyle\chi_{P}=V(\langle P^{2}\rangle-\langle P\rangle^{2}),\quad\chi_{E}=V(\langle E^{2}\rangle-\langle E\rangle^{2}).

(18)

We do this using “impure” tensors
[
6
,
7
]
, and finite differences of the logarithm of the partition function.

7
Conclusion

We have studied 3+1-dimensional, single-flavor, finite-density QCD in the strong-coupling, high-temperature limit where the model undergoes dimensional reduction to a three-dimensional Abelian spin model. We studied this effective theory using three different methods: the triad tensor renormalization group, the higher-order tensor renormalization group, and the worm Monte Carlo sampling method. Where calculations were clean, the three methods agree with each other well. We found the tensor methods were able to reproduce known features of the phase diagram well; however, calculations at very small values of
\kappa
, and small values of
\mu
were noisy and unreliable in all three methods.
