Simple Buehler-optimal confidence intervals on the average success probability of independent Bernoulli trials
Jean-Daniel Bancal, Pavel Sekatski

Abstract
One-sided confidence intervals are presented for the average of non-identical Bernoulli parameters. These confidence intervals are expressed as analytical functions of the total number of Bernoulli games won, the number of rounds and the confidence level. Tightness of these bounds in the sense of Buehler, i.e. as the strictest possible monotonic intervals, is demonstrated for all confidence levels. A simple interval valid for all confidence levels is also provided with a tightness guarantee. Finally, an application of the proposed confidence intervals to sequential sampling is discussed.



I
Introduction

Let
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots T_{n}=0,1
be independent Bernoulli random variables with possibly distinct first moments
P(T_{i}=1)=q_{i}\in[0,1]
. The average success probability of these
n
independent binary games,

\bar{q}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i},

(1)

is an important quantity. Given a confidence level
1-\alpha\in[0,1]
, we are interested in one-sided confidence intervals
[\hat{q},1]
on
{\bar{q}}
, i.e. statistics
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
such that
Fisher35
;
Neyman37

P({\bar{q}}\geq\hat{q})\geq 1-\alpha.

(2)

In particular, we focus on the case where the statistics
\hat{q}=\hat{q}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
only depends on the confidence parameter
\alpha
, the number of rounds
n
, and the total number of successes

n{\bar{T}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}.

(3)

The random variable
n{\bar{T}}
follows a Poisson binomial distribution, which has a number of well known properties, see
Tang19
for a recent review.

When considering random variables that are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), the central limit theorem
Esseen58
allows to construct confidence intervals in a generic way and with little information on the underlying distribution. Since each parameter
q_{i}
can be different here, we do not wish to make the i.i.d. assumption. Nevertheless, generic statistical techniques still allow for the construction of valid confidence intervals in this case. Non-i.i.d. generalizations of Esseen’s result constitute one possibility
Goldstein10
. Another well known option is given by Hoeffding’s 1963 inequality
P({\bar{T}}-{\bar{q}}\geq t)\leq\exp(-2nt^{2})

Hoeffding63
, which can be rewitten as Eq. (
2
) with the lower bound

\hat{q}^{\text{H}}={\bar{T}}-\sqrt{-\log(\alpha)/2n}.

(4)

While admitting a simple analytical expression, confidence intervals based on generic bounds are typically not optimal. This can be problematic when the amount of available data is limited
Munich19
. Optimal confidence intervals providing the best possible results within a meaningful class of bounds are then particularly interesting
Buehler57
;
Lloyd03
.

In 1974, Agnew used a theorem by Hoeffding (distinct from Hoeffding’s 1963 inequality) to construct a confidence interval for the average success probability of independent Bernoulli trials
Agnew74
. This interval is tighter than Eq. (
4
), but still not always optimal. It is sometimes understood that exact and optimal confidence intervals can be formulated, but may be computationally intractable. Recently, a Buehler-optimal confidence interval for the average success probability of independent Bernoulli trials was described in
Mattner15
. This work also provides a confidence interval which takes a simple form, but it is not applicable to arbitrary values of the confidence parameter
\alpha
(see Remark 1.4 therein). Here, using Hoeffding’s characterization for the cumulative distribution of the number of successes with independent trials already employed by Agnew
Hoeffding56
, we provide a compact formula for the optimal one-sided confidence interval in terms of standard analytical functions for any confidence parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
. We then also present a simple expression for a confidence interval that is valid for all
\alpha
and tight for high enough confidence levels.

More precisely, our main result given in Theorem
1
is an expression of the monotonically tight one-sided confidence interval on
{\bar{q}}
which only depends on the confidence parameter
\alpha
, the total number of rounds
n
, and the number of Bernoulli games won
n{\bar{T}}
. When
\alpha\leq 1/4
, our bound is given by Corollary
1
as the simple formula:

\hat{q}^{g}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)=\begin{cases}0&n{\bar{T}}=0\\
{\bar{T}}-\frac{1-\alpha}{n(1-\alpha^{*}(n{\bar{T}},n))}&n{\bar{T}}>0,\ \alpha^{*}(n{\bar{T}},n)\leq\alpha\leq 1\\
I^{-1}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n(1-{\bar{T}})+1)&n{\bar{T}}>0,\ 0\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{*}(n{\bar{T}},n),\end{cases}

(5)

where

\alpha^{*}(n{\bar{T}},n)=I_{(n{\bar{T}}-1)/n}(n{\bar{T}},n(1-{\bar{T}})+1).

(6)

Here,
I_{x}(a,b)
is a standard mathematical function, namely the regularized incomplete Beta function
Wikipedia
(see also Appendix
A
), and
I_{\alpha}^{-1}(a,b)
is its inverse. We show that the bound given by Eq. (
5
) remains valid when
\alpha>\frac{1}{4}
, allowing its use for all confidence levels.

III
One-sided confidence intervals

Having recalled the form of the function
f_{\bar{q}}(d,n)
, which bounds the cumulative distribution of the sum of
n
Bernoulli random variables
n{\bar{T}}
, Eq. (
7

{\bar{q}}

Lemma 3
.

The random variable

IV
Optimality

Having established a lower bound on
{\bar{q}}
, we now study its optimality. Since
\mathbb{E}(n{\bar{T}})=n{\bar{q}}
monotonically increases with
{\bar{q}}
, observing a larger value of
n{\bar{T}}
suggests that
{\bar{q}}
is larger. Hence, we consider confidence intervals
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
which are increasing in
n{\bar{T}}
, i.e. satisfying

\hat{q}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)\leq\hat{q}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}}+1,n)

(53)

for
0\leq n{\bar{T}}\leq n
. Following Buehler

;

\hat{q}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
is optimal if

\hat{q}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)\geq\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)\ \ \ \forall\ 0\leq n{\bar{T}}\leq n

(54)

holds for all other increasing confidence interval bound
\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)

Proposition 2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\hat{q}_{\alpha}^{f}(n{\bar{T}},n)

P({\bar{q}}\geq\hat{q})\geq 1-\alpha

(55)

for all sets of independent Bernoulli random variables
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, …,
T_{n}
.

Proof.

Since
q_{i}=0\ \forall i
produces
n{\bar{T}}=0
with
{\bar{q}}=0
, it is clear that
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(0,n)
must be equal to zero whenever
\alpha<1
and therefore
\hat{q}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)=\hat{q}^{f}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
is optimal in this case. We thus restrict our attention now to the case
n{\bar{T}}\geq 1
, for which
\tilde{f}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}}-1,n)=f^{-1}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}}-1,n)
.

To see that
\hat{q}^{f}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
constitutes the best lower bound when
n{\bar{T}}\geq 1
, consider any increasing confidence interval
\hat{q}^{?}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
on
{\bar{q}}
which associates to a point
n{\bar{T}}=d^{*}+1
some value
y
, and let us show that we must have
y\leq f^{-1}_{\alpha}(d^{*},n)
. This guarantees that
\hat{q}^{?}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)\leq\hat{q}_{\alpha}^{f}(n{\bar{T}},n)
at any value
d^{*}\in[1,n]
.

To do so let us introduce the smallest increasing statistic
\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
, that satisfy
\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(d^{*}+1,n)=y
. It has the shape of a step function:

\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)=\begin{cases}0&n{\bar{T}}<d^{*}+1\\
y&n{\bar{T}}\geq d^{*}+1,\end{cases}

(56)

and satisfies
\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)\leq\hat{q}^{?}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
, and therefore
P({\bar{q}}\geq\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n))\geq P({\bar{q}}\geq\hat{q}^{?}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n))\geq 1-\alpha
, by construction: since
[q^{?}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n),1]
is a confidence interval, so is
[q^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n),1]
. In other words, if some choice of
y
prevents
[q^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n),1]
from being a confidence interval, this choice of
y
is not admissible to
q^{?}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
either. So it is sufficient to prove that we must have
y\leq f^{-1}_{\alpha}(d^{*},n)
for
[\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},b),1]
to be a valid confidence interval.

For a model with some parameter
{\bar{q}}
, the probability that the estimator
\hat{q}^{\prime}
gives a valid lower bound on
{\bar{q}}
is given by

P({\bar{q}}\geq\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n))=\begin{cases}1&{\bar{q}}\geq y\\
P(n{\bar{T}}<d^{*}+1)&{\bar{q}}<y.\end{cases}

(57)

In particular, for a model with average winning probability
{\bar{q}}=y-\delta
, with
\delta>0
, the above probability is given by

\displaystyle P({\bar{q}}\geq\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n))

\displaystyle=P(n{\bar{T}}<d^{*}+1)

(58)

\displaystyle=P(n{\bar{T}}\leq d^{*})

(59)

\displaystyle\geq 1-f_{y-\delta}(d^{*},n)

(60)

where we used the bound on the cumulative distribution of
n
independent Bernoulli trials from Eq. (
7
). In fact, this bound is achievable by a choice of parameters
\{q_{i}\}_{i}
satisfying
\sum_{i}q_{i}=n(y-\delta)

Hoeffding56
. Thus, in this case we have:

\begin{split}P({\bar{q}}\geq\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n))&=1-f_{y-\delta}(d^{*},n).\end{split}

(61)

Therefore, for
\hat{q}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
to be a valid lower bound on
{\bar{q}}
with confidence parameter
\alpha
, we must have

f_{y-\delta}(d^{*},n)\leq\alpha

(62)

Since
\alpha<1
, this is only possible if
y-\delta\leq\frac{d^{*}+1}{n}
. In this case
f_{y-\delta}(d^{*},n)
is bijective and its inverse is given by Proposition
1
. Hence we find

y-\delta\leq f^{-1}_{\alpha}(d^{*},n).

(63)

Since this condition has to be true for all
\delta>0
, we find the maximum possible value of
y
in Eq. (
56
):
y\leq f^{-1}_{\alpha}(d^{*},n)
.
∎

Note that a similar argument implies that Eq. (
47
) is also a Buehler optimal upper bound on
{\bar{q}}
.

Another consequence from this proposition is that the statistic
\hat{q}^{0}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)=I^{-1}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n-n{\bar{T}}+1)
, sometimes referred to as the Clopper-Pearson bound, does not provide a valid confidence interval for
{\bar{q}}
, as already noticed earlier
Mattner15
. This statistic would yield the optimal confidence interval in presence of i.i.d. Bernoulli variables, i.e. for a binomial trial, but it is not valid here when the trials need not be identical: for the value
n{\bar{T}}=1
,
\hat{q}^{0}_{\alpha}(1,n)=I^{-1}_{\alpha}(1,n)=1-(1-\alpha)^{1/n}>\hat{q}_{\alpha}(1,n)=\tilde{f}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}}-1,n)=\frac{\alpha}{n}
. The tightest binomial-like confidence interval is thus given by the statistic

\hat{q}^{1}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)=\begin{cases}0&n{\bar{T}}\leq 1\\
I^{-1}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}}-1,n-n{\bar{T}}+2)&n{\bar{T}}\geq 2,\end{cases}

(64)

shown to yield a valid confidence interval when
\alpha\leq 1/2
in
Munich19
. Since
\lim_{\alpha\to 1}I^{-1}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}}-1,n-n{\bar{T}}+2)=1
for
n{\bar{T}}\geq 2
, it is clear that this bound cannot be true for all confidence parameters
\alpha
, contrary to
\hat{q}^{f}_{\alpha}(n{\bar{T}},n)
.

A simpler explicit bound

The expression for the confidence interval given in Theorem

{S}_{n}(c,\alpha)
function. This maximization requires at most

evaluations of the function

, which scales favorably with

and

Lemma 4
.

For
\frac{c}{n}\leq p\leq\frac{c+1}{n}
,
0\leq c\leq n-1
,
\alpha^{\dagger}\leq\alpha\leq 1

\displaystyle{Q}_{n}(c,p)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{n}(c,p)=1-(1-\alpha^{\dagger})(np-c)

(65)

\displaystyle{S}_{n}(c,\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle U_{n}(c,\alpha)=\frac{1}{n}\left(c+\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha^{\dagger}}\right)

(66)

Moreover, these inequalities are tight for
c=n-1
.

Proof.

We start by showing the bound Eq. (
65
). As
{Q}_{n}(c,p)=\max_{s}{{\text{Q}}}_{n}(c,p,s)
, it is implied by

{{\text{Q}}}_{n}(c,p,s)=1-I_{\frac{np-s}{n-s}}(c-s+1,n-c)\leq 1-(1-\alpha^{\dagger})(np-c)

(67)

for all integer
0\leq s\leq c
. Let us introduce the functions

\displaystyle x(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{np-s}{n-s}=1-\frac{n}{n-s}(1-p)\in\left[0,\frac{c}{n}\right]

(68)

\displaystyle\ell(p)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha^{\dagger})(np-c)\in[0,1-\alpha^{\dagger}],

(69)

where we recalled that
\alpha^{\dagger}
can be expressed as a function of
c
through Eq. (
18
), and rewrite the bound Eq. (
67
) as

I_{x(p)}(a,b)\geq\ell(p),

(70)

where
a=c-s+1\geq 1
,
b=n-c\geq 1
. To demonstrate it we proceed by considering several cases.

For
b=1
, i.e.
c=n-1
and
a=n-s
, one gets
\alpha^{\dagger}=0
and the inequality (
70
) becomes

\displaystyle I_{x(p)}(n-s,1)\geq np-c

(71)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left(1-\frac{n}{n-s}(1-p)\right)^{n-s}\geq 1-n(1-p)

(72)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left(1-\frac{1-\delta}{n-s}\right)^{n-s}\geq\delta.

(73)

In the last line we introduced the variable
\delta=1-n(1-p)
, which satisfies

as guaranteed by

and

. We note that the inequality is manifestly tight for

(it becomes

), which shows that Eq. (

as claimed. To prove that the bound holds in general, i.e. for

. Equivalently, the function

f(v)=\left(1-\frac{1-\delta}{v}\right)^{v}

(74)

. This can be done by computing its derivative, which satisfies

\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left(1-\frac{1-\delta}{v}\right)^{v}\propto\frac{1-z}{z}+\log(z)\geq 0

, using the standard lower bound on the natural logarithm

Next we consider the case
b\geq 2
. As a preliminary step we analyze the concavity/convexity of

as a function of

in the interval

with respect to

I_{x}(a,b)=a\binom{a+b-1}{b-1}\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}dt.

(76)

For the first derivative we obtain

\displaystyle I_{x}^{\prime}(a,b)\

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}I_{x(p)}(a,b)=\frac{\partial x(p)}{\partial p}\frac{\partial I_{x}(a,b)}{\partial x}

(79)

\displaystyle=a\binom{a+b-1}{b-1}x^{\prime}(p)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}dt

\displaystyle=a\binom{a+b-1}{b-1}\frac{n}{n-s}\,x(p)^{a-1}\big(1-x(p)\big)^{b-1}.

Computing the second derivative is also straightforward and gives

\displaystyle I_{x}^{\prime\prime}(a,b)\

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}I_{x(p)}(a,b)

(82)

\displaystyle=a\binom{a+b-1}{b-1}\frac{n}{n-s}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p}x(p)^{a-1}\big(1-x(p)\big)^{b-1}

-(b-1)(1-x)^{b-2}&a=1\\

where we wrote

to improve readability. We will now analyze the two regimes separately.

\displaystyle I_{x(\frac{c+1}{n})}(a,b)\geq\ell\left(\frac{c+1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle(iii)\qquad

To show Eq. (

Lemma 5
.

For two integers
N>y>0
, the following bound holds

\binom{N}{y}\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)^{y}\left(\frac{N-y}{N}\right)^{N-y}\leq\frac{1}{2}

(109)

Proof.

Using the bound on the binomial coefficient

\binom{N}{y}\leq\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi y(N-y)}}\frac{N^{N}}{y^{y}(N-y)^{N-y}}

(110)

\binom{N}{y}\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)^{y}\left(\frac{N-y}{N}\right)^{N-y}\leq\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi y(N-y)}}.

(111)

Now for
1\leq y\leq N-1
and
N\geq 3
, this quantity is strictly smaller than
1/2
. Indeed, this function is convex in
y
and symmetric under exchange

, so it is upper bounded by its value at

, which is

\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi(N-1)}}.

(112)

The derivative of this function in N is negative, so its largest value
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}<\frac{1}{2}
is obtained for
N=3
.

It remains to examine the special cases

and

satisfying

, and

\binom{N}{y}\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)^{y}\left(\frac{N-y}{N}\right)^{N-y}=\frac{1}{2}.

(113)

VI.1
Application to sequential sampling with and without independence between the samples

Consider the sampling of
n
binary variables
T_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
in a sequential fashion, i.e. where the variables are sampled one after the other one: first
T_{1}
, then
T_{2}
, followed by
T_{3}
, etc. If the samples are independent from each other, it is possible to associate a winning probability
q_{i}\in[0,1]
to each
T_{i}
, even before the start of the experiment. The confidence intervals given by
\hat{q}^{f}
and
\hat{q}^{g}
can then be applied directly to bound the average winning probability
{\bar{q}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
as a function of
n
,
n{\bar{T}}
and
\alpha
. These bounds apply even if the random variables are not identically distributed, i.e. if
q_{i}
depends on
i
.

In the case where the samples are not guarantees to be independent from each other, however, the winning probability of each variable
T_{i}
cannot be described anymore by a single parameter
q_{i}
set a priori, i.e. independently of the other random variables. Since the
n
random variables may be correlated with each other in this case, they must be described by a general joint probability distribution of the form

P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}).

(144)

In a single instance of a sequential scenario in which
T_{i}
is only observed after
T_{i-1}
, this joint probability distribution cannot be fully explored. Rather, the sampling of
T_{i}
in this case is described by the conditional probability distribution

P(T_{i}|T_{1}=t_{1},\ldots,T_{i-1}=t_{i-1}),

(145)

where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{i-1}=0,1
are the results observed in the first
i-1
samples of the experiment. In this situation, a difference arises between the average winning probability over both all rounds
i
and multiple realizations of the experiment, and the average winning probability over all rounds
i
for the single realization occurring in a specific experiment (unlike in the previous case of independent samples where both averages are equal). Interestingly, the second quantity can be evaluated precisely in a single run of the experiment. Indeed, this average winning probability over all rounds can be obtained by identifying the parameter
q_{i}
with the winning probability of the
i^{\text{th}}
random variable
T_{i}
conditioned on the results of the previous samples, i.e. defining

q_{i}=P(T_{i}=1|T_{1}=t_{1},\ldots,T_{i-1}=t_{i-1}).

(146)

The confidence intervals
\hat{q}^{f}
and
\hat{q}^{g}
described in this work can then be employed to bound the average winning probability
{\bar{q}}
of the variables that were actually sampled during the course of a specific one-shot non-i.i.d. experiment.
