Optimal Schauder estimates for kinetic Kolmogorov equations with time measurable coefficients
Giacomo Lucertini, Stefano Pagliarani, Andrea Pascucci

Abstract
We prove global Schauder estimates for kinetic Kolmogorov equations with coefficients that are Hölder continuous in the spatial variables but only measurable in time. Compared to other available results in the literature, our estimates are optimal in the sense that the inherent Hölder spaces are the strongest possible under the given assumptions: in particular, under a parabolic Hörmander condition, we introduce Hölder norms defined in terms of the intrinsic geometry that the operator induces on the space-time variables. The technique is based on the existence and the regularity estimates of the fundamental solution of the equation, recently proved in [ 17 ] . These results are essential for studying backward Kolmogorov equations associated with kinetic-type diffusions, e.g. stochastic Langevin equation.



Keywords
: global Schauder estimates,
Hörmander condition, Kolmogorov equation, Langevin equation, kinetic diffusions, anisotropic diffusions, intrinsic Hölder spaces, fundamental solution.

Introduction

In recent years, several sharp Schauder estimates have been proved for Kolmogorov equations with
coefficients that are Hölder-continuous in the space-variables but only measurable in time.
In this article we prove global Schauder estimates which we claim to be
optimal
, meaning
that the inherent Hölder spaces are the strongest possible under the given assumptions on the
coefficients. In particular, our results
include and improve some known estimates in the framework of non-divergence form operators satisfying a parabolic Hörmander condition.

A prototype example of the class under consideration is

\frac{{\sigma^{2}(t,v,x)}}{2}{\partial}_{vv}+v{\partial}_{x}+{\partial}_{t},\qquad(t,v,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},

(1.1)

which is the backward Kolmogorov operator of the system of stochastic differential equations

\begin{cases}dV_{t}={\sigma(t,V_{t},X_{t})}dW_{t},&\\
dX_{t}=V_{t}dt,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
W
is a real Brownian motion. The study of these models is motivated by several applications, including kinetic theory and
finance. In the classical Langevin model,
(V,X)
describes velocity and position of a particle
in the phase space and is a pilot example of more complex kinetic models (cf.
[
13
]
,

[
10
]
,
[
11
]
). In mathematical finance,
(V,X)
represents the log-price and
average processes utilized in modeling path-dependent financial derivatives, such as Asian options
(cf.
[
1
]
,
[
22
]
).

We now introduce the general class under consideration. Let
d,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, with
d\leq N
, be fixed throughout the paper. We denote by
(t,x)
the point in

{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and consider the second-order operator in non-divergence form

\mathscr{L}:=\mathscr{A}+Y

(1.3)

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(t,x){\partial}_{x_{i}x_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}(t,x){\partial}_{x_{i}}+a(t,x),

(1.4)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:={\partial}_{t}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{ij}x_{j}{\partial}_{x_{i}}={\partial}_{t}+\langle Bx,\nabla\rangle,

(1.5)

and
B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1,\cdots,d}
is a constant matrix of dimension
N\times N
. The diffusion
part
\mathscr{A}
is an elliptic operator on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, while the drift (or transport) term
Y
is a first
order differential operator
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
. We impose two main structural hypotheses:

\mathbf{(H.1)}

the matrix
A=\left(a_{ij}\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,d}
is
symmetric and there exists a positive constant
{\mu}
such that

{{\mu}^{-1}}{|{\eta}|^{2}}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(t,x){\eta}_{i}{\eta}_{j}\leq{\mu}|{\eta}|^{2},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},\ {\eta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(1.6)

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
where
T>0
is fixed once for all;

\mathbf{(H.2)}

the following Hörmander condition is satisfied:

\text{rank }\text{Lie}(\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}},Y)=N+1.

(1.7)

The focus of the paper is on the case
d<N
, which is
\mathscr{L}
is fully degenerate, namely
no coercivity condition on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is satisfied. Condition (
1.7
) is also known as
parabolic
Hörmander condition since the drift
Y
plays a key role in the generation of the Lie algebra.

We consider the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}u=f&\text{on }\mathcal{S}_{T},\\
u(T,\cdot)=g&\text{on }{\mathbb{R}}^{N},\end{cases}

(1.8)

posed on the strip

\mathcal{S}_{T}:=\,]0,T[\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}.

(1.9)

Generally speaking, Schauder estimates give a bound of some Hölder norm of the solution
u
in
terms of some (possibly different) Hölder norms of the data, namely the coefficients of
\mathscr{L}
,
the non-homogeneous term
f
and the datum
g
. The “strength” of a Schauder estimate depends on
the norms involved and this is a sensitive issue in the theory of degenerate
PDEs: clearly, for a given Hölder norm on the solution
u
, the weaker the norms on the data, the
stronger the Schauder estimate; conversely, if the norms on the data are given, then the stronger the norm on
u
the stronger the
Schauder estimate. Now, in the literature on degenerate Kolmogorov equations, we may recognize at
least two notions of Hölder norm as well as variants of them:
the so-called
anisotropic
and
intrinsic
norms, whose precise definitions are given in Section
2.1
. Intuitively, the former norm takes into account the anisotropic behavior in space induced by the
underlying diffusion, but does not require any time-regularity. The intrinsic norm, by opposite,
is induced by the geometric properties of the differential operator
\mathscr{L}
and takes into account
the regularity along the vector field
Y
(and thus along the time variable). Therefore, the
intrinsic norm is stronger in the sense that it allows to see the full regularizing effect (in
both space and time) of the fundamental solution of
\mathscr{L}
.

Roughly speaking, we may catalogue the known Schauder estimates for solutions to
(
1.8
) as follows:

—

anisotropic-to-anisotropic
: the anisotropic norms of the data bound the anisotropic norm of the solution, as in
[
18
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
26
]
and
[
3
]
;

—

intrinsic-to-intrinsic
: the intrinsic norms of the data bound the intrinsic norm of the solution, as in
[
19
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
25
]
;

—

anisotropic-to-intrinsic
: the anisotropic norms of the data bound the intrinsic norm of the
solution. This class is stronger than the two above: only recently, partial results were proved in
[
6
]
and
[
2
]
.

Our main result, Theorem
2.7
, provides an optimal
anisotropic-to-intrinsic
global Schauder estimate which improves the
results in
[
6
]
and
[
2
]
in a subtle but crucial way, as
explained in Section
2.3
. For instance, the Hölder norm we adopt for the solution
u
is strong enough to derive intrinsic
Taylor formulas and therefore also an Itô formula for the underlying diffusion processes, which
is a fundamental tool in stochastic calculus.

Our estimate is global in that it holds all the way up to the boundary, with an explosion factor
that depends on the regularity of
g
, namely
(T-t)^{-\frac{2+\alpha-\beta}{2}}
: here
\alpha

and
\beta
represent the Hölder exponents of the solution
u
and of the terminal datum
g
,
respectively. In particular, it is possible to recognize two limiting cases:
\beta=2+\alpha
, no
explosion close to the boundary;
\beta=0
(
g
only continuous), maximum explosion rate. As a
corollary, we obtain a sharp regularity estimate (Corollary
2.8
) in the
Y
direction,
at the boundary
t=T
.
Although this is an expected phenomenon, the quantitative characterization of the penalty term is
novel, to the best of our knowledge, for degenerate Kolmogorov operators in the context of
variable coefficients or of intrinsic Hölder spaces. We refer the reader to

[
27
]
for the case of constant diffusion coefficients and anisotropic
Besov-Hölder spaces. We also mention that intrinsic embedding theorems of Sobolev type were
recently proved in
[
8
]
and
[
23
]
.

Theorem
2.7
comprises a well-posedness result for (
1.8
). The proof of Theorem
2.7
goes as follows. First we define a candidate solution
u
via
Duhamel principle. After proving that it is actually a solution to (
1.8
), we
prove the regularity estimates for the two convolution terms that constitute
u
, from which the
Schauder estimate follows. The proof critically relies on the recent results in

[
17
]
, where the existence of the fundamental solution of
\mathscr{L}
was
established, together with optimal Hölder estimates, by means of a suitable modification of the
parametrix technique, already employed in
[
24
]
and
[
4
]
in the case of
intrinsic Hölder-continuous coefficients.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. Section
2
contains the main results and a detailed comparison with the related literature. Precisely, in Section
2.1
we define both the anisotropic and intrinsic Hölder spaces; in Section
2.2
we state our main result, Theorem
2.7
, and comment on it; Section
2.3
contains the comparison with the literature. Section
3
is entirely devoted to the proof Theorem
2.7
.

Schauder estimates

We begin by fixing some general notation that will be utilized throughout the rest of the
article.
Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. For any
i=1,\dots,N
, we denote by
\partial_{i}g(x)
the partial
derivatives of
u
with respect to
x_{i}
. We also denote by
\nabla_{d}
the gradient operator

(\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{d})
with respect to the first
d
components and by
\nabla^{2}_{d}
the
Hessian operator
(\partial_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,d}
with respect to the first
d
components. We
also consider the natural extensions of the operators
\nabla_{d}
and
\nabla^{2}_{d}
to functions

f=f(t,x)
defined on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. We set the following basic functional spaces:

-

C_{b}
, the set of bounded continuous functions
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, equipped with the norm

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}:=\sup\limits_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}|g(x)|;

(2.1)

-

L^{\infty}_{t}
, for
t>0
, the set of measurable functions
f
, defined on the strip
\mathcal{S}_{t}
(cf. (
1.9
)), such that the norm

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}}:=\sup_{(s,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}|f(s,x)|

is finite.

Finally, all the normed spaces in this article are defined for scalar valued functions and naturally extend to vector
valued functions by considering the sum of the norms of their single components.

2.1
Hölder spaces

In this section, we introduce the
anisotropic
and
intrinsic
Hölder spaces that appear in the Schauder estimates for (
1.8
).
Loosely speaking, the general idea behind the definition of these spaces is the following:

—

anisotropic Hölder spaces are defined for functions of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,
assuming regularity in
all
N
directions
w.r.t. an anisotropic distance that reflects the different time-scaling
properties of the underlying diffusion process. This distance is defined in term of an anisotropic
norm that assigns to each component of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
a different weight corresponding to the number
of commutators of

\nabla_{d}
and
Y
that are required to reach that direction. The definition then extends to functions defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
by only requiring measurability and local boundedness with respect to the time variable.

—

intrinsic Hölder spaces are defined for functions of
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
that are assumed to be
anisotropically Hölder continuous, in the sense above, uniformly in time. Additional Hölder
regularity in the direction of the drift
Y
is also assumed. By means of the Hörmander
condition, it is then possible to infer Hölder regularity jointly with respect to all variables.

Let us first recall that the parabolic Hörmander condition (
1.7
) is equivalent to the
well-known Kalman rank condition for controllability in linear systems theory (cf., for instance,

[
22
]
). Also, it was shown in
[
14
]
that (
1.7
) is equivalent to
B

having the block-form

B=\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
B_{1}&\ast&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
0&B_{2}&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&B_{q}&\ast\end{pmatrix}

(2.3)

where the
*
-blocks are arbitrary and
B_{j}
is a
(d_{j-1}\times d_{j})
-matrix of rank
d_{j}
with

d\equiv d_{0}\geq d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{q}\geq 1,\qquad\sum_{i=0}^{q}d_{i}=N.

(2.4)

The block decomposition (
2.3
) induces naturally an anisotropic norm for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
defined as

|x|_{B}:=\sum_{j=0}^{q}\,\sum_{i=\bar{d}_{j-1}+1}^{\bar{d}_{j}}|x_{i}|^{\frac{1}{2j+1}},\qquad{\bar{d}_{j}:=\sum_{k=0}^{j}d_{k}}.

(2.5)

Definition 2.1

(
Anisotropic Hölder spaces).

Let
{{\alpha}}\in\,]0,3]
.

—

The anisotropic Hölder norms on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
are
defined recursively as

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}}}:=\begin{cases}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup\limits_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\frac{|g(x)-g(y)|}{|x-y|^{{{\alpha}}}_{B}},&{{\alpha}}\in\,]0,1],\\
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla_{\!d}g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}-1}}+\sup\limits_{(x,{\eta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N-d}}\frac{|g(x+(0,{\eta}))-g(x)|}{|(0,{\eta})|^{{{\alpha}}}_{B}},&{{\alpha}}\in\,]1,3].\end{cases}

(2.6)

We denote by
\mathbf{C}^{\alpha}
the set of functions
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that the norm
\|g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}}}
is finite. Set also
\mathbf{C}^{0}:=C_{b}
.

—

For
t>0
, the anisotropic Hölder norms on
\mathcal{S}_{t}
are

\displaystyle\text{(weighted)}\quad\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{s\in\,]0,t[}\Big((t-s)^{\gamma}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{\alpha}}\Big),\quad\gamma\in[0,1[,\qquad

(2.7)

\displaystyle\text{(non-weighted)}\quad\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle:=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,0}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}.\qquad\qquad\qquad

(2.8)

We denote by
L^{\infty}_{t,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})
and
L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})
the set of measurable
functions
f:\mathcal{S}_{t}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that the norms
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}
and
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}
, respectively, are finite.

Before introducing the intrinsic Hölder spaces, we recall the following

Definition 2.2

(
Lie derivative).

For
t>0
and
{{\alpha}}\in\,]0,2]
we set

\displaystyle\|f\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{Y,t}}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{(\tau,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\sup_{s\in\,]0,t[}\frac{\left|f(s,e^{(s-\tau)B}x)-f(\tau,x)\right|}{|s-\tau|^{\frac{{{\alpha}}}{2}}}.

(2.9)

Moreover, we say that
f
is a.e. Lie-differentiable along
Y
on
\mathcal{S}_{t}
if there
exists
F\in L_{\text{\rm loc}}^{1}(]0,t[;C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))
such that

f(s,e^{(s-\tau)B}x)=f(\tau,x)+\int_{\tau}^{s}F(r,e^{(r-\tau)B}x)dr,\qquad(\tau,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{t},\ s\in\,]0,t[.

(2.10)

In that case, we set
Yf=F
and call it an
a.e. Lie derivative of
f
on
\mathcal{S}_{t}
.

Definition 2.3

(
Intrinsic Hölder spaces).

Let
t>0
. The
intrinsic Hölder norms on
\mathcal{S}_{t}
are defined recursively as:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}}:=\begin{cases}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}+\|f\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{Y,t}},&\hskip 0.0pt\quad{{\alpha}}\in\,]0,1],\\
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}+\|\nabla_{\!d}f\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}-1}}+\|f\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{Y,t}},&\hskip 0.0pt\quad{{\alpha}}\in\,]1,2],\\
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{\alpha})}+\|\nabla_{\!d}f\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}-1}}+\|{Yf}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}-2})},&\hskip 0.0pt\quad{{\alpha}}\in\,]2,3].\end{cases}

(2.11)

For
{{\alpha}}\in\,]0,3]
, we denote by
\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}
the set of functions

f:\mathcal{S}_{t}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that the norm
\|f\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}}
is finite.

Remark 2.4

(
Intrinsic vs anisotropic spaces).

Obviously, the intrinsic space
\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}
is strictly included in the anisotropic space

L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})
. Note that, for
\alpha\in\,]0,1]
, the addition in the

\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}
-norm of the term
\|f\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{Y,t}}
yields Hölder regularity
jointly in the time and space variables: in particular, it is standard to show that if
f\in\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}
then

|f(\tau,x)-f(s,y)|\leq C\|f\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}}\big(|\tau-s|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}+|x-e^{(\tau-s)}y|^{\alpha}_{B}\big),\qquad(\tau,x),(s,y)\in\mathcal{S}_{t}.

(2.12)

Remark 2.5

(
Intrinsic Taylor formula).

For
\alpha\in\,]0,1]
, the intrinsic spaces
\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}
and

\mathbf{C}_{t}^{1+{{\alpha}}}
are equivalent to those in
[
20
]
. However,

\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}
is slightly weaker than the one in
[
20
]
in that the Lie derivative
Yf
is not
required to be in
\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}}
but only in
L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})
. This difference is dictated
by our assumptions on the coefficients that are merely measurable in the temporal variable: so,
for a solution
u
of (
1.8
), one may expect that
Yu
exists in the strong sense
but, in general, is not more than bounded in the
Y
-direction. Despite this,

\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}
in Definition
2.3
is still strong enough to prove the following

intrinsic Taylor formula
as in
[
20
, Theorem 2.10]
:
if
f\in\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}
then

|f(\tau,x)-T_{2}f\big(s,y;x-e^{(\tau-s)B}y\big)|\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}\big(|\tau-s|+|x-e^{(\tau-s)B}y|^{2+\alpha}_{B}\big),\qquad(\tau,x),(s,y)\in\mathcal{S}_{t},

(2.13)

where

is the second order intrinsic Taylor polinomial

T_{2}f(s,y;z)=f(s,y)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}\,\partial_{i}f(s,y)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}z_{i}z_{j}\,\partial_{ij}f(s,y).

(2.14)

Furthermore, by adding the term
(\tau-s)Yu(s,y)
to
T_{2}
, the estimate can be improved by
obtaining a term of order
o(|\tau-s|)
, as
{\tau}-s\to 0
, in place of
|\tau-s|
in
(
2.13
).

It is worth noting that, for
f
in the anisotropic space
L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})
,
estimate
(
2.13
) generally holds only for
s=\tau
: this is the best result that
one can deduce from Schauder estimates of anisotropic-to-anisotropic type.

2.2
Main result

We remark that if
u\in L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{2+{{\alpha}}})
or
u\in\mathbf{C}_{T}^{2+{{\alpha}}}
then the derivatives

{\partial}_{x_{i}}u,{\partial}_{x_{i}x_{j}}u
exist in the classical sense only for
1\leq i,j\leq d
. Indeed,
u

is not regular enough to support even the first-order derivatives
{\partial}_{x_{i}}
for
d<i\leq N
and
the full gradient
\nabla u
appearing in the drift of
\mathscr{L}
must be interpreted in a suitable way.
Thus, in accordance with the intrinsic Hölder spaces defined above, we employ the following natural definition of solution to the kinetic equation.

Definition 2.6

(
Strong Lie solution).

Let
f\in L_{\text{\rm loc}}^{1}(]0,T[;C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))
. A solution to equation

\mathcal{A}u+Yu=f\ \text{on }\mathcal{S}_{T}

(2.15)

is a continuous function
u:\mathcal{S}_{T}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that there exist

\nabla_{d}u,\nabla^{2}_{d}u\in L_{\text{\rm loc}}^{1}(]0,T[;C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))
and
Yu=f-\mathcal{A}u
in the sense of Definition
2.2
.

For
g\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
, a solution to the Cauchy problem (
1.8
) is a solution
u
to
(
2.15
), which can be extended continuously to
]0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with
u(T,\cdot)=g
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let assumptions
(H.1)
and
(H.2)
be in force and assume also the coefficients
a_{ij},a_{i},a
of
\mathscr{L}

be in
L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{\bar{{\alpha}}})
for some
\bar{{\alpha}}\in\,]0,1]
. Then, for any
{{\alpha}}\in\,]0,\bar{{\alpha}}[
,
g\in\mathbf{C}^{\beta}
with
\beta\in[0,2+{{\alpha}}]
and

f\in L^{\infty}_{T,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})
with
\gamma\in[0,1[
, there exists a unique bounded strong Lie solution
u
to the Cauchy problem (
1.8
). Furthermore, we have

\|{u}\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}\leq C\left((T-t)^{-\frac{2+{{\alpha}}-\beta}{2}}\|g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{\beta}}+(T-t)^{-{\gamma}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}\right),\qquad 0<t<T,

(2.16)

where
C
is a positive constant which depends only on
T,B,\bar{{\alpha}},{{\alpha}},\beta,\gamma
and on the
norms

of the coefficients of

. In particular,

does not depend on

.

We illustrate the Schauder estimate through particular instances; by linearity, we can treat the

and

—

[Case

]
Estimate (

\|{u}\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}\leq C(T-t)^{-{\gamma}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})},\qquad 0<t<T.

In particular, if

\|{u}\|_{\mathbf{C}_{T}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}\leq C\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}.

(2.18)

—

[Case
f=0
]
We have two extreme cases:

\diamond

If
{\beta}=0
, that is

is only bounded and continuous, then
the solution has the same explosion behavior of the fundamental solution as

(cf.
[

\|{u}\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}\leq C(T-t)^{-\frac{2+{{\alpha}}}{2}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}.

\diamond

If

then the solution is
(2+{{\alpha}})
-Hölder continuous up to the boundary

\|{u}\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}\leq C\|g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2+{\alpha}}}.

(2.19)

, which is reported in the following

Corollary 2.8
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem

be in force with

\|u(t,e^{B(T-t)}\cdot)-u(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(T-t)^{\frac{\beta}{2}},\qquad 0<t<T,

.

Remark 2.9
.

Recall that (see Remark
2.5
) Theorem
2.7
does not imply, under the assumptions therein, that the Lie derivative
Yu
is jointly continuous in time and space variables. However, under the additional assumption that
f
and
a_{ij},a_{i},a
are continuous on
\mathcal{S}_{T}
, it can be seen by Definitions
2.3
and
2.6
, together with Remark
2.4
, that
Yu
turns out to be continuous on
S_{T}
. In particular,
u
is Lie differentiable along

everywhere on

, and thus equation (

) is satisfied pointwise everywhere on

.

2.3
Comparison with the literature

Global
anisotropic-to-anisotropic
Schauder estimates were proved by Lunardi

[
18
]
, Lorenzi
[
16
]
, Priola
[
26
]
, Menozzi
[
3
]

under different assumptions on the coefficients,
stronger or equivalent to ours. These estimates read as follows: if
u
is a solution of the Cauchy problem (
1.8
) then

\|{u}\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{2+{{\alpha}}})}\leq C\left(\|g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}\right).

(2.22)

Estimate (
2.22
) is similar to (
2.16
) for the particular choice
{\beta}=2
and

{\gamma}=0
; however, by Remark
2.4
, estimate (
2.22
) is weaker than
(
2.16
) due to the strict inclusion of intrinsic into anisotropic spaces. Moreover,
the above mentioned papers assume a smooth datum,
g\in\mathbf{C}^{2+{{\alpha}}}
, missing the smoothing
effect of the equation, which is well-known in the uniformly parabolic case (cf.

[
15
]
). On the other hand, the class of equations considered by Menozzi

[
3
]
allows for more general drift terms. Zhang
[
27
]
proved general
estimates in the context of Besov-Holder spaces, which, at order two read as

\|{u}\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{2+{{\alpha}}})}\leq C(T-t)^{-\frac{2+{{\alpha}}-\beta}{2}}\|g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{\beta}}.

(2.23)

Once more, this estimate is proved for the anisotropic Hölder norm and thus only catches the
smoothing effect of the kernel with respect to the spatial variables. Also, (
2.23
)
is proved in the case of
\mathscr{A}
with constant coefficients.

Global intrinsic-to-intrinsic Schauder estimates were obtained by Imbert and Mouhot

[
10
]
for
B
as in (
2.25
), assuming coefficients in
\mathbf{C}_{T}^{{{\alpha}}}
. As noticed
in
[
6
]
, the results in
[
10
]
do not cover the case of some elementary
smooth functions, for instance,
d=1
and
f(t,x_{1},x_{2})=\sin x_{2}
.
We also mention that
interior estimates
were obtained by Manfredini

[
19
]
, Di Francesco and Polidoro
[
5
]
, Henderson and Snelson
[
9
]
and very recently by Polidoro, Rebucci and
Stroffolini
[
25
]
for operators with Dini continuous coefficients.

Global anisotropic-to-intrinsic Schauder estimates were obtained by Biagi and Bramanti
[
2
]
under the additional assumption that
the
\ast
-blocks in (
2.3
) are null and therefore
\mathscr{L}
is
homogeneous w.r.t. a suitable family of dilations: if
u
is a solution to
\mathscr{L}u=f
then

\displaystyle{\|\nabla^{2}_{d}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}}+\|Yu\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}+{\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{\mathbf{C}_{T}^{{{\alpha}}}}+\|u\|_{\mathbf{C}_{T}^{{{\alpha}}}}}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}\right).

(2.24)

Estimate (
2.24
) is weaker than (
2.16
) because
the norm on the l.h.s. of (
2.24
) is smaller than the norm in
\mathbf{C}_{T}^{2+{{\alpha}}}
: the Hölder semi-norms
\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{C^{1+{{\alpha}}}_{Y,T}}
and
\|\nabla^{2}_{d}u\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{Y,T}}
along the direction
Y
are missing.
Moreover, the optimal regularity for the second derivatives
\nabla^{2}_{d}u
is obtained only

locally
.

The closest results to ours were recently obtained by Dong and Yastrzhembskiy

[
6
]
for the Langevin operator in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d+1}
, that is for
B
in (
2.3
) in
the particular form

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
I_{d}&0\ \end{pmatrix},

(2.25)

where
I_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix and for the Cauchy problem with null-terminal
datum,
g=0
. The techniques in
[
6
]
are based on a kernel free approach
inspired by Campanato’s ideas. Despite the proof is quite different, the estimates are very
similar to ours except they miss the optimal regularity along
Y
of the first order derivatives

\nabla_{d}u
: we remark that this piece of information is crucial to guarantee the validity of
Taylor formulas like (
2.13
). As already mentioned, the latter is a
basic tool to prove probabilistic results such as the Itô formula (cf.

[
12
]
), as well as analytical (cf.
[
10
]
) and asymptotic results
(cf.
[
21
]
).

Proof of Theorem
2.7

The proof of Theorem
2.7
relies on the results of
[
17
]
where a
fundamental solution to the operator
\mathscr{L}={\mathcal{A}+Y}
was constructed in the form

p(t,x;T,y)=\mathbf{P}(t,x;T,y)+\Phi(t,x;T,y),\qquad 0<t<{T},\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

(3.1)

where:

—

the function
\mathbf{P}
is a so-called
parametrix
, which is defined as

\mathbf{P}(t,x;s,y):={\Gamma}^{(s,y)}(t,x;s,y),\qquad 0<t<s<T,\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

(3.2)

where, for
(\tau,v)\in\mathcal{S}_{T}
, we set

{\Gamma}^{(\tau,v)}(t,x;s,y):=\mathbf{G}\big(\mathcal{C}^{(\tau,v)}(t,s),y-e^{(s-t)B}x\big),\qquad 0<t<s<T,\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

(3.3)

with

\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{C},z):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N}\det\mathcal{C}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}z,z\rangle},

(3.4)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{(\tau,v)}(t,s)

\displaystyle:=\int_{t}^{s}e^{(s-r)B}A^{(\tau,{v})}(r)e^{(s-r)B^{*}}dr,

(3.5)

\displaystyle A^{(\tau,v)}(r)

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{0}(r,e^{(r-\tau)B}v)&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_{0}=\left(a_{ij}\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,d};

(3.6)

—

the function
\Phi(t,x;T,y)
is a remainder enjoying suitable regularity estimates, which are recalled in Proposition
3.6
below.

The strategy of our proof is to define a candidate solution to the Cauchy problem
(
1.8
) in the form

u(t,x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}p(t,x;T,{\eta})g({\eta})d{\eta}-\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}p(t,x;{\tau},{\eta})f({\tau},{\eta})d{\eta}d{\tau},\qquad(t,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{T},

(3.7)

and prove that
u
:
(i) is the unique bounded solution to (
1.8
) and (ii)
satisfies the estimate (
2.16
).

Note that
u
in (
3.7
) can be written as

u(t,x)=V_{g}(t,x)-V_{\mathbf{P},f}(t,x)-V_{\Phi,f}(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{T}

(3.8)

with

V_{g}(t,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}p(t,x;T,{\eta})g({\eta})d{\eta},

(3.9)

and

V_{\mathbf{P},f}(t,x):=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\mathbf{P}(t,x;{\tau},{\eta})f({\tau},{\eta})d{\eta}d{\tau},\qquad V_{\Phi,f}(t,x):=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\Phi(t,x;{\tau},{\eta})f({\tau},{\eta})d{\eta}d{\tau}.

(3.10)

We now prove our main result, Theorem
2.7
. The proof is based on the sharp regularity
estimates for
V_{g}
,
V_{\mathbf{P},f}
and
V_{\Phi,f}
contained in Propositions

3.11
and
3.10
.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
(well-posedness of (
1.8
)).

The uniqueness of the solution follows from standard arguments: we refer to

[
7
]
for a detailed proof.

We prove that
u
as defined in (
3.7
) is a solution to the Cauchy problem
(
1.8
) in the sense of Definition
2.6
. The facts that
\nabla_{d}u,\nabla^{2}_{d}u
have the required regularity on
\mathcal{S}_{T}
, and that
u
can be
extended continuously to the closure of
\mathcal{S}_{T}
in a way that
u(T,\cdot)\equiv g
, are
straightforward consequences of the estimates of Propositions

3.10
-
3.11
and of the Dirac delta property
of
p
. To prove that
Yu=f-\mathcal{A}u
in the sense of Definition
2.2
, we can consider separately, by linearity, two
cases. We state here, once and for all, that all the applications of Fubini’s theorem throughout
this proof are justified by the estimates of Propositions
3.10
and

3.11
.

Case
f\equiv 0
.
We have

\displaystyle u(s,e^{(s-t)B}x)-u(t,x)

\displaystyle=V_{g}(s,e^{(s-t)B}x)-V_{g}(t,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\big(p(s,e^{(s-t)B}x;T,{\eta})-p(t,x;T,{\eta})\big)g({\eta})d{\eta}

(3.11)

(
{(Y+\mathcal{A})}p(\cdot,\cdot;T,{\eta})=0
on
\mathcal{S}_{T}
in the sense of Definition
2.6
)

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{t}^{s}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;T,{\eta})dr\,g({\eta})d{\eta}=-\int_{t}^{s}\mathscr{A}u(r,e^{(r-t)B}x)dr,

(3.12)

where, in the last equality, we employed Fubini’s theorem and
(
3.52
)-(
3.53
) to move
the operator
\mathscr{A}
out of the integral on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

Case
g\equiv 0
.
We have

\displaystyle u(s,e^{(s-t)B}x)-u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\int_{s}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\big(p(s,e^{(s-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})-p(t,x;\tau,{\eta})\big)f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}d\tau}_{=:I}

(3.13)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}p(t,x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}d\tau.

(3.14)

As
{(Y+\mathcal{A})}p(\cdot,\cdot;\tau,{\eta})=0
on
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
in the sense of Definition

2.6
, for any
\tau\in]s,T[
, we have

p(s,e^{(s-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})-p(t,x;\tau,{\eta})=-\int_{t}^{s}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})dr.

(3.15)

Therefore, by Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{s}\int_{s}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}d\tau dr

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{t}^{s}\int_{r}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}d\tau dr}_{=:I_{1}}-\underbrace{\int_{t}^{s}\int_{r}^{s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}d\tau dr}_{=:I_{2}}.

(3.17)

By moving the operator
\mathscr{A}
out of the integral in
d{\eta}d\tau
, we obtain

I_{1}=-\int_{t}^{s}\mathscr{A}u(r,e^{(r-t)B}x)dr,

(3.18)

and thus, to show
{(Y+\mathcal{A})}u=0
on
\mathcal{S}_{T}
, we need to prove that

I_{2}=-\int_{t}^{s}f(r,e^{(r-t)B}x)dr+\int_{t}^{s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}p(t,x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}d\tau.

(3.19)

By applying Fubini’s theorem we obtain

I_{2}=\int_{t}^{s}\underbrace{\int_{t}^{\tau}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}\,dr}_{=:J(\tau)}d\tau.

(3.20)

Now, for any
{\varepsilon}\in\,]0,\tau-t[
, we can write

J(\tau)=\underbrace{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{t}^{\tau-{\varepsilon}}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})drd{\eta}}_{=:J^{{\varepsilon}}_{1}(\tau)}+\underbrace{\int_{\tau-{\varepsilon}}^{\tau}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\mathscr{A}p(r,e^{(r-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta}\,dr}_{=:J^{{\varepsilon}}_{2}(\tau)}.

(3.21)

As
{(Y+\mathcal{A})}p(\cdot,\cdot;\tau,{\eta})=0
on
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
, we obtain

J^{{\varepsilon}}_{1}(\tau)=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\big(p(\tau-{\varepsilon},e^{(\tau-{\varepsilon}-t)B}x;\tau,{\eta})-p(t,x;\tau,{\eta})\big)f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta},

(3.22)

and since
f(\tau,\cdot)
is bounded and continuous, the Dirac delta property of
p
yields

J^{{\varepsilon}}_{1}(\tau)\to-f(\tau,e^{(\tau-t)B}x)+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}p(t,x;\tau,{\eta})f(\tau,{\eta})d{\eta},\qquad\text{as }{\varepsilon}\to 0^{+}.

(3.23)

On the other hand, the estimates of Lemma
3.4
and of Proposition
3.6
yield

J^{{\varepsilon}}_{2}(\tau)\to 0,\qquad\text{as }{\varepsilon}\to 0^{+}.

(3.24)

This and (
3.23
), by Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, yield
(
3.19
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
(estimate (
2.16
)).

As
u
is a solution to (
1.8
),
Yu=f-\mathscr{A}u
is a Lie derivative of
u
on

\mathcal{S}_{T}
. Therefore, we have

\|u\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{2+{{\alpha}}}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{2+{{\alpha}}})}+\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{1+{{\alpha}}}}+\|\mathscr{A}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}.

(3.25)

Now we recall that, for
\alpha\in]0,1]
, the intrinsic spaces
\mathbf{C}_{t}^{{{\alpha}}},\mathbf{C}_{t}^{1+{{\alpha}}}
are exactly equivalent to those in
[
20
]
. This is a consequence of the intrinsic Taylor formula of Theorem 2.10 in the latter reference, with
n=0,1
. In particular, we have

\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{1+{{\alpha}}}}\leq C(\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}}+\|\nabla^{2}_{d}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}}+\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{C^{1+{{\alpha}}}_{Y,t}}+\|\nabla^{2}_{d}u\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{Y,t}}+\|\nabla^{2}_{d}u\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{\nabla_{d},t}}),

(3.26)

where

\|\nabla^{2}_{d}u\|_{C^{{{\alpha}}}_{\nabla_{d},t}}:=\sup_{(s,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\sup_{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{|f(s,x+(h,0))-f(s,x)|}{|h|^{{{\alpha}}}}.

(3.27)

Therefore, the estimates of Propositions
3.10
and
3.11
yield

\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{1+{{\alpha}}}}\leq C\Big((T-t)^{-\frac{2+{{\alpha}}-\beta}{2}}\|g\|_{\mathbf{C}^{\beta}}+(T-t)^{-{\gamma}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}\Big).

(3.28)

To obtain the same estimate for
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{2+{{\alpha}}})}
, it is enough to prove it for

\sup_{s\in]0,t[}\ \sup\limits_{(x,{\eta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N-d}}\frac{|u(s,x+(0,{\eta}))-u(s,x)|}{|(0,{\eta})|^{2+{{\alpha}}}_{B}},

(3.29)

which can be done by proceeding as in the proofs of (
3.58
) and
(
3.102
): we omit the details for brevity.
Furthermore, by the regularity assumptions on the coefficients of
\mathscr{A}
, we have

\|\mathscr{A}u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}\leq C(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}+\|\nabla_{d}u\|_{\mathbf{C}_{t}^{1+{{\alpha}}}}).

(3.30)

Finally, we have
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}\leq(T-t)^{-\gamma}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T,\gamma}(\mathbf{C}^{{{\alpha}}})}

and thus (
2.16
).

∎
