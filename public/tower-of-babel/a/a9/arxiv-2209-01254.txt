A Fredholm Alternative for Elliptic Equations with Interior and Boundary Nonlinear Reactions
Dan Maroncelli & M.A. Rivas

Abstract
. In this paper we study the existence of solutions to the following generalized nonlinear two-parameter problem a ⁡ ( u , v ) = λ ​ b ​ ( u , m ) + μ ​ m ​ ( u , v ) + ε ​ F ​ ( u , v ) , a(u,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(u,m)+\mu\,m(u,v)+\varepsilon\,F(u,v), for a triple ( a , b , m ) (a,b,m) of continuous, symmetric bilinear forms on a real separable Hilbert space V V and nonlinear form F F . This problem is a natural abstraction of nonlinear problems that occur for a large class of differential operators, various elliptic pde’s with nonlinearities in either the differential equation and/or the boundary conditions being a special subclass. First, a Fredholm alternative for the associated linear two-parameter eigenvalue problem is developed, and then this is used to construct a nonlinear version of the Fredholm alternative. Lastly, the Steklov-Robin Fredholm equation is used to exemplify the abstract results.

1.
Introduction

This paper studies the existence of solutions to the generalized nonlinear two-parameter problem given by

(1.1)

a(u,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(u,m)+\mu\,m(u,v)+\varepsilon\,F(u,v),

where
(a,b,m)
is a triple of continous, symmetric bilinear forms on a separable Hilbert space
V
and
F
is a nonlinear form.
Our work has two distinct parts.
Formulated first is a
two-parameter Fredholm alternative
for the triple
(a,b,m)
.
The alternative and the explicit spectral formulae for the solutions are given in terms of the eigendata associated with the
variational eigencurves
of
(a,b,m)
as obtained in
[
18
]
.
Then existence results to (
1.1
) for cases of
nonresonance
and
resonance
are described in detail.
The formulation (
1.1
) is called the nonlinear
Fredholm equation
for
(a,b,m)
as it subsumes various versions of such equations.

Our abstract setting enables the study of large classes of PDEs posed in weak form since the conditions imposed on
a,b,m
are simple enough that they allow treating, for example, very general second-order elliptic equations with (possibly) nonlinear boundary conditions posed in weak form.
This is because data coefficients of the equations may be quite general, including sign-changing weights, and yet it is easy to verify that the bilinear forms arising from the weak formulation of these differential equations satisfy our general assumptions.
Moreover, the use of bilinear forms directly, as opposed to densely defined linear operators, facilitates imposing other boundary conditions, such as Robin, Steklov, or in some cases mixed boundary conditions, on these equations (see §8, 9, 10 of
[
3
]
concerning tensor product spaces.).
The methodology provides for a straightforward, but not trivial, inspection of such problems and for much simpler proofs.

Our study is motivated by the renewed interest and applications of two-parameter nonlinear problems with non-Dirichlet boundary conditions.
For instance, in some recent papers of Shivaji and collaborators, such as
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
, or
[
10
]
, classes of singular or exact reaction diffusion equations where a parameter in the differential equation influences another parameter in the boundary condition are examined.
A related first eigencurve is often a tool in describing stability or bifurcation results for such problems.
Sign-changing eigenvalue problems for triples of bilinear forms are studied by Kielty in
[
11
]
that are applicable to, for example, nonlinear population dynyamics.
There the author describes how the spectrum of the problem converges as the negative part of the weight is taken to negative infinity.
This can be rephrased as increasing one eigenparameter while maintaining the other fixed.
See
[
18
]
that cites other recent uses of eigencurves in advanced scientific applications.

An interesting related work is that of Peitgen and Schmitt in
[
17
]
.
There the authors treat very general two-parameter second-order elliptic equations, but with the nonlinearity only on the differential equation and only the Dirichlet boundary condition being imposed.
Their method of proof includes linearization and (Learay-Schauder) continuation techniques.
A wealth of detailed examples, as well as numerical work, is also provided in
[
17
]
.
The present work can be regarded as a supplement of the analysis of Peitgen and Schmitt as our approach is both topological and variational in nature.

In addition, the results obtained in this paper complement both the geometrical results in
[
18
]
for the variational eigencurves of
(a,b,m)
and the solvability results of Mavinga and Nakashama in
[
16
]
.
In
[
18
]
, attention is given to producing and describing the spectrum of the triple
(a,b,m)
.
Here, the focus is on using the spectrum of
(a,b,m)
in studying the solvability of nonlinear elliptic problems with nonlinear boundary conditions.
In
[
16
]
, existence results are obtained according to how their nonlinearities interact with so-called “eigenvalue-lines”, and here we give existence results based on how the nonlinearity
F
in (
1.1
) interacts with the variational eigencurves.

Similar spectral representations and decompositions as used here have recently been invoked by Manki Cho in
[
6
]
and De Gournay, Allaire and Jouve in
[
7
]

to provide numerical results in different areas.
In
[
6
]
, new
meshless
numerical methods are introduced in the study of regularized Laplacian boundary value problems that yield effective and accurate approximations.
In
[
7
]
, both theorerical and numerical results are given for the robust compliance problem that appears in shape and topology optimization.
A nice version of the Fredholm alternative is given in Section 2.3.1 of
[
7
]
and may be compared to that given here.

The paper is organized as follows. In §
2
, the notation and assumptions are presented.
The
canonical eigendata
, and their related properties, for the triple
(a,b,m)
of bilinear forms are detailed in §
3
.
The two-parameter Fredholm Alternative for
(a,b,m)
is stated and proved in §
4
.
The nonlinear Fredholm Alternative for
(a,b,m)
is treated next, with the nonresonance case in §
5
and the resonance case in §
6
.
The abstract results are then exemplified in §
7.1
that treats the
Steklov-Robin Fredholm Equation
.
To finish, the appendix in §
8
provides conditions for which the Fréchet differentiability requirement of our main results, theorems
5.1
and
6.1
, is satisfied. For simplicity, the result is stated for a class of nonlinearities in the one-dimensional setting, but higher-dimensional analogs exist under appropriate assumptions.

2.
Definitions and Notation

In this paper,
V
will denote a real separable Hilbert space with inner product and norm given by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}
and
\|\cdot\|_{V}
, respectively, and whose dual space is
V^{*}
.
The
two-parameter eigenproblem
for the triple
(a,b,m)
of bilinear forms on
V
is that of finding
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and nontrivial
e\in V
satisfying

(2.1)

a(e,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(e,v)+\mu\,m(e,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

A nonzero
e
is called an
eigenvector
associated to the
eigenpair

(\lambda,\mu)
of
(a,b,m)
, and the problem corresponding to (
2.1
) will be called the
(a,b,m)
-
eigenproblem
.
The subset
\sigma(a,b,m)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
comprising all eigenpairs
(\lambda,\mu)
will be called the
spectrum
of
(a,b,m)
.

We will denote the associated quadratic forms on
V
for
a,b
, and
m
by
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathcal{M}
, respectively, so then

(2.2)

\mathcal{A}(u)\;:=\;a(u,u)\;,\qquad\mathcal{B}(u)\;:=\;b(u,u)\;,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{M}(u)\;:=\;m(u,u)\qquad\text{for all }u\in V.

Our assumptions on the bilinear forms
a,b,m
are:

(A1):

a(\cdot,\cdot)
is a coercive, continuous, symmetric bilinear form on
V
, so there are constants
0<k_{0}\leq k_{1}<\infty
such that

(2.3)

\qquad k_{0}\|u\|_{V}^{2}\;\leq\;\mathcal{A}(u)\;\leq\;k_{1}\|u\|_{V}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }u\in V.

(A2):

b(\cdot,\cdot)
is a weakly continuous, symmetric bilinear form on
V
, and
\mathcal{B}
may be an indefinite form so that it could attain positive, negative, and zero values.

(A3):

m(\cdot,\cdot)
is a weakly continuous, symmetric bilinear form on
V
that satisfies

(2.4)

\mathcal{M}(u)\;>\;0\qquad\text{for all nonzero }u\in V.

When these assumptions hold, both
a,m
will be inner products on
V
, with
a(\cdot,\cdot)
equivalent to the
V
-inner product, and there is an implicit compactness that underpins the spectral analysis for bilinear forms.

For concreteness, we mention here that assumptions A1-A3 do not hold for all weak formulations of differential equations with arbitrary weight functions. An easy example not satisfying our assumptions, often overlooked by experts, is

V\;=\;H^{1}(\mathbb{R}),\quad a(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}[u^{\prime}v^{\prime}+uv]\,dx,\quad b(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}b_{0}\,u\,v\,dx\,\quad m(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}m_{0}\,u\,v\,dx,

with
b_{0},m_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
m_{0}(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, since
m
is not weakly continuous.
Thus, to apply the results given in this paper it is vital to verify that all assumptions on
a,b,m
hold.

3.
Canonical spectral data for two-parameter eigenproblems

To determine the spectral data for the two-parameter problem (
2.1
), the problem in
[
18
]
is shifted to get, for fixed
\lambda
, the one-parameter eigenproblem of finding
\mu+\tau
and nontrivial
e
satisfying

(3.1)

a_{\lambda,\tau}(e,v)\;=\;(\mu+\tau)\,m(e,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V,

where fixed
\tau>0
is large enough so that by Theorem 3.1 of
[
18
]
the form

(3.2)

a_{\lambda,\tau}:=a-\lambda\,b+\tau\,m

is coercive on
V
.
Using the algorithm described in
[
2
]
, the eigendata for
(a_{\lambda,\tau}\,,\,m)
, i.e. solutions of (
3.1
), is found to be

\mu+\tau=\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\qquad\text{and}\qquad e=\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\;,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},

where the eigenvectors are
a_{\lambda,\tau}
-normalized, i.e.

(3.3)

\|\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau}}^{2}\;:=\;\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\tau}(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau))=1.

Rearranging the eigenequation for
(a_{\lambda,\tau}\,,\,m)
gives that this eigendata satisfies, for fixed
\lambda,\tau
:

(3.4)

a(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau),v)\;\;=\;\;\lambda\,b(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau),v)\;+\;(\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)-\tau)\,m(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau),v)\quad\text{for all }v\in V.

For each
i=1,2,\cdots
, define
E_{i}(\lambda,\tau)
to be the eigenspace generated by the eigenvectors of
(a_{\lambda,\tau}\,,\,m)
associated with eigenvalue
\tilde{\mu}_{i}(\lambda,\tau)
.
Eigenvalues may have multiplicity higher than 1, so if
\tilde{\mu}_{1}(\lambda,\tau)<\tilde{\mu}_{2}(\lambda,\tau)=\tilde{\mu}_{3}(\lambda,\tau)
, then
\tilde{e}_{3}(\lambda,\tau)\in E_{2}(\lambda,\tau)
.
The next result shows that
\tilde{\mu}_{k}
is a linear function of
\tau
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume
(A1),(A2)
, and
(A3)
hold, and
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau),\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
, and
E_{i}(\lambda,\tau)
are as above for fixed
\lambda,\tau
.
If
\delta\geq 0
, then

(3.5)

\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)=\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)=E_{k}(\lambda,\tau).

Proof.

Rearranging the
(a_{\lambda,\tau+\delta}\,,\,m)
-eigenequation for
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)\,,\,\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)
gives

a_{\lambda,\tau}(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)\,,\,v)\;=\;(\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)-\delta)\,m(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)\,,\,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V,

so
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)-\delta\;=\;\tilde{\mu}_{i}(\lambda,\tau)
for some
i
.
Separately, adding
\delta\,m(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\,,\,v)
to both sides of the
(a_{\lambda,\tau}\,,\,m)
-eigenequation for
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\,,\,\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
leads to

a_{\lambda,\tau+\delta}(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\,,\,v)\;=\;(\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta)\,m(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\,,\,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V,

so
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta\;=\;\tilde{\mu}_{j}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)
for some
j
.
These two relations give the desired results.
∎

For the next result, using (
3.4
) define the new values

(3.6)

\mu_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\;:=\;\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)-\tau\qquad\text{for each }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
(A1),(A2)
and
(A3)
hold.
For fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the new values
\mu_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
given in (
3.6
) are independent of
\tau
.

Proof.

For
\delta>0
, the linear functional relation proved above gives

\mu_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)\;=\;\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)-(\tau+\delta)\;=\;\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta-(\tau+\delta)\;=\;\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)-\tau\;=\;\mu_{k}(\lambda,\tau)

as claimed.
∎

Thus,
\mu_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
and
E_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
are written simply as
\mu_{k}(\lambda)
and
E_{k}(\lambda)
.
Although, the eigenspaces are
\tau
-invariant, the eigenvectors
\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
are, in general, not.
However, since for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
E_{k}(\lambda,\gamma)
is independent of
\gamma
, we may assume that
\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)=c(\lambda,\delta)\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
, for some constant
c(\lambda,\delta)
. To obtain “cononical” spectral representations, we define the following:

(3.7)

e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\;:=\;\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)}\,\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau).

The next lemma shows these scaled eigenvectors satisfy certain norm properties, where

(3.8)

\|u\|_{m}\;:=\;\sqrt{\mathcal{M}(u)}\;=\;\sqrt{m(u,u)}\qquad\text{defined for all }u\in V,

is called the
m
-norm on
V
and the
a_{\lambda\tau}
-norm is given above in (
3.3
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
(A1),(A2)
, and
(A3)
hold, and
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau),\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
, and
E_{i}(\lambda,\tau)
are as above for fixed
\lambda,\tau
.
Then

(i)

the
e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
are
m
-normalized:
\|e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\|_{m}\;=\;1
.

(ii)

for
\delta>0
,
\|e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau+\delta}}\;=\;\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta}
.

Proof.

The first assertions follows from

\|e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\|_{m}^{2}\;=\;\mathscr{M}(e_{k}(\lambda,\tau))\;=\;\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\mathscr{M}(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau))\;=\;\mathscr{A}_{\lambda,\tau}(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau))\;=\;1,

using the
(a_{\lambda,\tau}\,,\,m)
-eigenequation. The same eigenequation also gives

\|e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau+\delta}}^{2}\;=\;\mathscr{A}_{\lambda,\tau+\delta}(e_{k}(\lambda,\tau))\;=\;\mathscr{A}_{\lambda,\tau}(e_{k}(\lambda,\tau))+\delta\,\mathscr{M}(e_{k}(\lambda,\tau))\;=\;\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta

so the second assertion holds.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Since
\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)
has
a_{\lambda,\tau+\delta}
-norm one, part
(ii)
of this lemma and equation (
3.7
) imply

(3.9)

\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)\;=\;\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)}}{\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta}}\;\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
hold.
Then the
e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
given in (
3.7
) are independent of
\tau
.

Proof.

Multiplying equation (
3.9
) by
\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)+\delta}
gives

e_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)\;=\;\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)}\,\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau+\delta)\;=\;\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)}\,\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\;=\;e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)

and so the claim holds.
∎

This justifies writing
e_{k}(\lambda)
instead of
e_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
.
The pairs

(3.10)

(\mu_{k}(\lambda)\,,\,e_{k}(\lambda))\qquad\text{with }k\in\mathbb{N},

are regarded as the
canonical spectral data
for the triple
(a,b,m)
.

4.
Two-parameter Fredholm Alternative

This and the next section treat the linear version of (
1.1
) stated as:
for fixed
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}
and
\ell\in V^{*}
consider the problem of finding
u
satisfying

(4.1)

a(u,v)-\lambda\,b(u,v)-\mu\,m(u,v)\;\;=\;\;\ell(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

This equation is called the linear
Fredholm equation
for
(a,b,m)
.
The result for the nonresonance case; that is, the case where
(\lambda,\mu)\not\in\sigma(a,b,m)
, is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
hold and suppose
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
.
Then
\mu\neq\mu_{k}(\lambda)
for all
k
, and there is a unique solution
\hat{u}
to problem (
4.1
) which has the spectral representation

(4.2)

\hat{u}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\,\ell(e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda).

The proof of this also yields the following estimates for the unique solution that are particularly useful in the applications.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
hold and suppose
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
.
Let
\tau>0
be large enough so that
a_{\lambda\tau}
in (
3.2
) is coercive, and denote by
\|\cdot\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}^{*}}
the dual norm on
V^{*}
when
V
is equipped with the norm
\|u\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau}}:=\sqrt{a_{\lambda\tau}(u,u)}
.

(i)

When
\mu<\mu_{1}(\lambda)
, then the unique solution
\hat{u}
to (
4.1
) satisfies the bound

(4.3)

\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}\;\leq\;\left(\frac{\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\right)\,\|\ell\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}^{*}}

(ii)

When
\mu_{k_{0}}(\lambda)<\mu<\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)
, then the unique solution
\hat{u}
to (
4.1
) satisfies the bound

(4.4)

\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}\;\;\leq\;\;\frac{(\mu+\tau)(\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu_{1}(\lambda)}{(\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau)(\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu)}\,\|P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}\;+\;\left(\frac{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu}\right)\|\ell\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}^{*}}

where

(4.5)

P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}\;:=\;\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\frac{\ell(e_{k}(\lambda))}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}e_{k}(\lambda).

Proof.

The following argument yields the results for both theorems.

Rewrite equation (
4.1
) to get

(4.6)

a(u,v)-\lambda\,b(u,v)+\tau\,m(u,v)-(\mu+\tau)\,m(u,v)\;\;=\;\;\ell(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V,

where
\tau>0
is large enough so that
a_{\lambda,\tau}:=a-\lambda\,b+\tau\,m
is coercive.
Rewritten once more this reads as

(4.7)

a_{\lambda,\tau}(u,v)-(\mu+\tau)\,m(u,v)\;\;=\;\;\ell(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

Since
\mu\neq\mu_{k}(\lambda)
we also have
\mu+\tau\neq\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
for all
k
.
Then Theorem 10.1 of
[
2
]
shows that the unique solution
\hat{u}
of (
4.7
) is

\hat{u}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)}{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)-(\mu+\tau)}\,\ell(\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau))\,\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)

Splitting
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)
as
\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)}\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)}
and using the linearity of
\ell
and the scaling (
3.7
) for
e_{k}(\lambda)
, together with (
3.4
) for
\mu_{k}(\lambda)
, leads to desired spectral representation for
\hat{u}
.

Substituting
u=v=\hat{u}
in (
4.7
) gives

(4.8)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)}\right)\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;\;=\;\;\ell(\hat{u})

upon applying Parseval’s equality
\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau}}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}
with respect to the basis
\{\tilde{e}_{k}(\lambda,\tau)\}
.
When
\mu<\mu_{1}(\lambda)
, then
\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)
for all
k
.
Hence,

\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;\leq\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;=\;\ell(\hat{u})

The extreme sides of this relation lead to

\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau}}\;\leq\;\;\;\ell\left(\frac{\hat{u}}{\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau}}}\right)

Taking the supremum of
\ell(u)
over all
\|u\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau}}=1
, and rearranging gives the bound in (
4.3
).

When
\mu_{k_{0}}(\lambda)\,<\,\mu\,<\,\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)
, then
\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)
for all
k\geq k_{0}+1
.
This gives

\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;\leq\;\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;+\;\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}.

Adding and subtracting
\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}
the left side becomes

\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;-\;\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;+\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}.

This expression is majorized by

\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;-\;\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;+\;\ell(\hat{u}).

since the series is equal to
\ell(\hat{u})
and
-\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\leq-\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)
for all
k=1,\ldots k_{0}
.
Combining the two sums and simplifying thus shows that

\left(1-\frac{\mu+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;\;\leq\;\;\frac{(\mu+\tau)[\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu_{1}(\lambda)]}{(\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau)(\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau)}\,\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\;+\;\ell(\hat{u}).

Rearrange this inequality to get

\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}^{2}\;\;\leq\;\;\frac{(\mu+\tau)[\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu_{1}(\lambda)]}{(\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau)(\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu)}\|P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}^{2}\;+\;\left(\frac{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu}\right)\,\ell(\hat{u}),

where
\|P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}
is the norm (squared) of the projection
P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}
.
Inequality (
4.4
) follows from this by dividing by
\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}
, using that
\frac{\|P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}}{\|\hat{u}|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}}\leq 1
and taking the supremum over all
\|u\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}=1
to get the dual norm of
\ell
with respect to the
a_{\lambda\tau}
norm.
∎

We finish this section with a Fredholm alternative in the case of resonance, that is, the case where
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
.
The result for the two-parameter Fredholm Equation (
4.1
) is this case is stated in the next theorem, where for fixed
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
the notation

(4.9)

J_{\lambda,\mu}\;:=\;\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\mu_{k}(\lambda)=\mu\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{\lambda,\mu}\;:=\;{\rm span}\{e_{k}(\lambda):k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}\}

is used instead of the previous
E_{k}(\lambda)
that denoted the distinct eigenspaces of
(a_{\lambda}\,,\,m)
; so
E_{\lambda,\mu}=E_{k}(\lambda)
for some
k
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
hold, and let
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
and
\ell\in V^{*}
be fixed.
Then
\mu=\mu_{k_{0}}(\lambda)
for some
k_{0}
and

(i)

there are solutions
\hat{u}
to (
4.1
) if and only if
\ell(e)=0
for all
e\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
.

(ii)

the solution set
S_{\lambda,\mu}
to (
4.1
) is the affine subspace comprised of all
\hat{u}
of the form

(4.10)

\hat{u}\;=\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\,\ell(e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;+\;\hat{v}\qquad\text{where }\quad\hat{v}\in E_{\lambda,\mu}.

Proof.

When
\ell(e)\neq 0
for some
e\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
, then equation (
4.1
) does not hold for
v=e
.
Now suppose
\ell(e)=0
for all
e\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
.
Substituting
\hat{u}
from (
4.10
) into the left side of (
4.1
) simplifies to

a\left(\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}\ell(e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;,\;v\right)\;\;=\;\;\ell(v)

which implies that
\hat{u}
is a solution, so assertion
(i)
holds.
Since this holds for any
\hat{v}\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
, hence any
\hat{u}
as in (
4.10
), assertion
(ii)
also holds.
∎

The special solution
\hat{u}_{0}
obtained by taking
\hat{v}
in (
4.10
) to be zero is often used in practice since then
\hat{u}
has additional orthogonality structure.

5.
Nonresonance

With Theorem
4.1
and
4.3
in hand, we now turn our attention to the main interest of this paper, which is the study of the nonlinear bilinear form problem (
1.1
).
This problem is a nonlinear perturbation of (
4.1
) and as such, the Fredholm alternatives developed in the previous section will play an important role in our analysis.
For ease of reference, we remind the reader that we are now considering the problem of finding
u
satisfying

(5.1)

a(u,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(u,m)+\mu\,m(u,v)+\varepsilon\,F(u,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

This will be called the
nonlinear Fredholm equation
for
(a,b,m)
.
At the moment we will be focusing on the nonresonant case; that is, the case in which
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
.
Throughout,
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
will be fixed, and we will suppose that
F
is such that
F(u,\cdot)\in V^{*}
for all
u\in V
.
We present two results in this context which give the existence of solutions to (
5.1
) under assumptions on the mapping
u\to F(u,\cdot)
from
V
to
V^{*}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
and that
u\to F(u,\cdot)
is continuously differentiable. Then there exists a
\delta>0
such that if
-\delta<\varepsilon<\delta
, then (
5.1
) has a locally unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}
. Further, the mapping
u:(-\delta,\delta)\to V
, defined by
u(\varepsilon)=u_{\varepsilon}
, is continuously differentiable with
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u(\varepsilon)=0
.

Proof.

Define the map
G:\mathbb{R}\times V\rightarrow V
by

(5.2)

G(\varepsilon,u)\;\;=\;\;u\;-\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\varepsilon F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)

From the spectral representation of Theorem
4.1
, the vector

(5.3)

w_{u}\;\;:=\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\varepsilon F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)

satisfies

(5.4)

a(w_{u}\,,\,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(w_{u}\,,\,v)+\mu\,m(w_{u}\,,\,v)+\varepsilon F(u,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

If
G(\varepsilon,u)=0
, then we get
u=w_{u}
and this last eigenequation simplifies to equation (
5.1
).
Thus, the solutions of (
5.1
) are precisely the zeros of
G
.

Let
D_{u}F
denote the derivative of
u\to F(u,\cdot)
.
Then

D_{u}G(\varepsilon,u)[v]\;\;=\;\;v\;-\;\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}D_{u}F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))[v]\,e_{k}(\lambda).

It follows that
D_{u}G(0,0)=I
, where
I
is the identity operator on V.
Since
G(0,0)=0
, the
Implicit Function Theorem
implies there exists a
\delta>0
and a continuously differentiable mapping
u:(-\delta,\delta)\to V
such that
G(\varepsilon,u(\varepsilon))=0
for each
\varepsilon\in(-\delta,\delta)
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u(\varepsilon)=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

We finish this section with a result which requires slightly weaker assumptions on the nonlinearity
F
. As a consequence we loose the differentiabilty of the solution
\varepsilon\to u(\varepsilon)
that we had above.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
and that that
u\to F(u,\cdot)
is a Lipschitz mapping. Then there exists a
\delta>0
such that if
-\delta<\varepsilon<\delta
, then (
5.1
) has a unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the mapping
u\to F(u,\cdot)
is Lipschitz, with Lipschitz constant
\kappa
with respect to the
m
-norm. Define

(5.5)

H(\varepsilon,u)\;\;=\;\;\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda).

We then have

\displaystyle\|H(\varepsilon,u)-H(\varepsilon,w)\|_{m}^{2}\;\;=

\displaystyle\;\;\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\left(F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\;-\;F(w,e_{k}(\lambda))\right)\,e_{k}(\lambda)\right\|_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\;\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu|^{2}}\|F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\;-\;F(w,e_{k}(\lambda))\|_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\;\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu|^{2}}\,\kappa\,\|u-w\|_{m}^{2}

which is a contraction for small enough
\varepsilon>0
. The result now follows from the
Contraction Mapping Theorem
.
∎

6.
Resonance

In this section we continue our study of problem (
5.1
); that is, we are again interested in finding elements
u\in V
such that

a(u,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(u,m)+\mu\,m(u,v)+\varepsilon\,F(u,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

Our focus in this section, however, will be on the case of resonance; that is, the case in which
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
. The solvability of (
5.1
) in this case of resonance is more delicate and requires a more careful analysis than was needed in the nonresonant case. Note, in particular, that the mapping
G
defined in (
5.2
) is no longer well-defined. Thus, analysis of (
5.1
) in this resonant case must proceed along a different route. The results we establish provide a substantial generalization of similar ideas found in
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
.

As before,
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
will be fixed, and we will suppose that
F
is such that
F(u,\cdot)\in V^{*}
for all
u\in V
.
We will again assume that the mapping
u\to F(u,\cdot)
is continuously differentiable, and in order to simplify the statement of the theorem below, the notation

J_{\lambda,\mu}\;:=\;\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\mu_{k}(\lambda)=\mu\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{\lambda,\mu}\;:=\;\text{span}\{e_{k}(\lambda):k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}\}

that was used, for fixed
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
, in the linear two-parameter Fredholm alternative at resonance will also be used here.
Finally, for every
w\in V
, define
\Lambda_{w}:V\to E_{\lambda,\mu}
by

\Lambda_{w}(v)\;:=\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}D_{u}F(w,e_{k}(\lambda))[v]e_{k}(\lambda).

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
and that
u\to F(u,\cdot)
is continuously differentiable. Suppose further that there exists
u_{0}\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
with
F(u_{0},e_{k}(\lambda))=0
for each
k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}
. If the restriction of
\Lambda_{u_{0}}
to
E_{\lambda,\mu}
is an injection, then (
5.1
) has a solution for small
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

In this resonance case, define the map
G:\mathbb{R}\times V\rightarrow V
by

(6.1)

G(\varepsilon,u)\;\;=\;\;u\;-\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}[m(u,e_{k}(\lambda))-F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))]e_{k}(\lambda)-\varepsilon\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda).

From the spectral representation, Theorem
4.3
, the vector

(6.2)

w_{u}\;=\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}[m(u,e_{k}(\lambda))-F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))]\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;+\;\varepsilon\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)

satisfies

a(w_{u}\,,\,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(w_{u}\,,\,v)+\mu\,m(w_{u}\,,\,v)+\varepsilon F(u,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V

if and only if

F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\;=\;0\qquad\text{for all }k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}.

However, if
G(\varepsilon,u)=0
, then
u=w_{u}
and from the uniqueness of the orthogonal decomposition

u\;=\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;+\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)

we get that

\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;\;=\;\;0\qquad\text{or that}\qquad F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))\;=\;0\quad\forall k\in J_{\lambda,\mu},

so that the zeros of
G
correspond to the solutions of (
5.1
).

Now suppose
u_{0}\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
and that
F(u_{0},e_{k}(\lambda))=0
for all
k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}
.
Under this assumption,
G(0,u_{0})\;=\;0
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle D_{u}G(\varepsilon,u)[v]\;=\;v\;-\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(v,e_{k}(\lambda))e_{k}(\lambda)\;-

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}D_{u}F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))[v]e_{k}(\lambda)

\displaystyle-\;

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}D_{u}F(u,e_{k}(\lambda))[v]e_{k}(\lambda)

so that

\displaystyle D_{u}G(0,u_{0})[v]\;=

\displaystyle\;v\,-\,\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(v,e_{k}(\lambda))e_{k}(\lambda)\;-\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}D_{u}F(u_{0},e_{k}(\lambda))[v]e_{k}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(v,e_{k}(\lambda))e_{k}(\lambda)\;-\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}D_{u}F(u_{0},e_{k}(\lambda))[v]e_{k}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(v,e_{k}(\lambda))e_{k}(\lambda)\;-\;\Lambda_{u_{0}}(v).

Since

\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(v,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)
and
\Lambda_{u_{0}}(v)
are orthogonal, it follows that if
D_{u}G(0,u_{0})[v]\;=\;0
, then

\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(v,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;=\;0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{u_{0}}(v)\;=\;0.

The first equation implies
v\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
, but then the second implies
v=0
, since
\Lambda_{u_{0}}
is injective on
E_{\lambda,\mu}
. It follows that
D_{u}G(0,u_{0})
is injective.

We now show that
D_{u}G(0,u_{0})
is a surjection. To see this, let
h\in V
and define

\begin{split}v_{1}\;&:=\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}\left(m(h,e_{k}(\lambda))\;+\;D_{u}F(u_{0},e_{k}(\lambda))\left[\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k}(\lambda))e_{k}(\lambda)\right]\right)\,e_{k}(\lambda)\\
&=\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k}(\lambda))e_{k}(\lambda)+\Lambda_{u_{0}}\left(\sum_{k\not\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\right).\end{split}

Since
E_{\lambda,\mu}
is finite dimensional and
\Lambda_{{u_{0}}\big|_{E_{\lambda,\mu}}}
is injective, it is actually a bijection. Thus, there is a unique
v_{0}\in E_{\lambda,\mu}
with
\Lambda_{u_{0}}(v_{0})=v_{1}
.
Define

v_{h}\;:=\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;-\;v_{0}.

From the following calculation, suppressing the dependence on
\lambda
, we get

\displaystyle D_{u}G(0,u_{0})[v_{h}]\;=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(v_{h},e_{k})\,e_{k}\;-\;\Lambda_{u_{0}}(v_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k})\,e_{k}\;-\;\Lambda_{u_{0}}\left(\sum_{k\not\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k})\,e_{k}\right)\;+\;\Lambda_{u_{0}}(v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k})\,e_{k}\;-\;\Lambda_{u_{0}}\left(\sum_{k\not\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k})\,e_{k}\right)\;+\;v_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k})\,e_{k}\;+\;\sum_{k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}}m(h,e_{k})\,e_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\;h,

so that
D_{u}G(0,u_{0})
is surjective.
The
Open Mapping Theorem
now gives that
D_{u}G(0,u_{0})
has a continuous inverse.
Therefore, by the
Implicit Function Theorem
, solutions to (
5.1
) exist for small enough
\varepsilon
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

If we refer to the solutions obtained in Theorem
6.1
as
u_{0}(\varepsilon)
, then the mapping
\varepsilon\to u_{0}(\varepsilon)
is again locally unique, continuously differentiable, and we have
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u_{0}(\varepsilon)=u_{0}
.

7.
Example

7.1.
Steklov-Robin eigenproblems and Steklov-Robin Fredholm equations

To exemplify our abstract results, this section studies the (weak) solvability of the following elliptic equation with specific nonlinear reaction terms
f,g
stated below:

(7.1)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(A\nabla u)\,+\,c(x)\,u\;=\;\mu\,m_{0}(x)\,u\,+\,\varepsilon\,f(x,u)\qquad\text{in }\Omega\\
\\
(A\nabla u)\cdot\nu\,+\,b_{c}(x)\,u\;=\;\lambda\,b_{0}(x)\,u\,+\,\varepsilon\,g(x,u)\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}.

When
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
is zero, this is called a
Steklov-Robin eigenproblem
on
\Omega
, and
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is said to be a
Steklov-Robin eigenpair
when (
7.1
) has a nontrivial solution.
When
\varepsilon\neq 0
, equation (
7.1
) will be called a
Steklov-Robin Fredholm equation
.

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
of class
C^{0,1}
,
\nu
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega
so that
(A\nabla u)\cdot\nu
is the outward unit conormal derivative and the given data of the equation is assumed to satisfy:

(C1):

A(x):=(a_{ij}(x))
is a real symmetric matrix whose components are bounded Lebesgue-measurable functions on
\Omega
and there are constants
0<\kappa_{0}\leq\kappa_{1}
satisfying

\kappa_{0}|\xi|^{2}\;\leq\langle\,A(x)\xi\,,\,\xi\,\rangle\;\leq\;\kappa_{1}|\xi|^{2}\qquad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,,\;x\in\Omega;

(C2):

c\geq 0
on
\Omega
and
c\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for some
p\geq N/2
when
N\geq 3
, or
p>1
when
N=2
, where
L^{p}(\Omega)
are the usual Lebesgue spaces on
\Omega
;

(C3):

b_{c}\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
with
b\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
and
c
and
b_{c}
satisfy the compatibility condition:

\int_{\Omega}c\,dx\,+\,\int_{\partial\Omega}b_{c}\,d\sigma\;\;>\;\;0,

where
\sigma
is Hausdorff measure on
\partial\Omega
;

(C4):

m_{0}>0
on
\Omega
with
m_{0}\in L^{q}(\Omega)
for some
q>N/2
, and satisfies

\int_{\Omega}m_{0}\,u^{2}\,dx\;>\;0\qquad\text{for all non-zero }u\in H^{1}(\Omega),

where
H^{1}(\Omega)
denotes the usual real Sobolev space of functions on
\Omega
;

(C5):

b_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
and
b_{0}
may be sign-changing on
\partial\Omega
.

The specific nonlinearities considered in this example are constructed as follows:

(Cf):
Let
f_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be such that
\int_{\Omega}f_{0}(x)\,dx>0
, and for
2<p<\infty
define

\begin{cases}\ell_{f}(u)\;:=\;\int_{\Omega}f_{0}\,u\,dx\\
\\
f(x,u)\;:=\;|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{f}(u)f_{0}(x)\qquad\text{for }u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\text{ and }x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(Cg):
Let
g_{0}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
be such that
\int_{\partial\Omega}g_{0}(x)\,d\sigma>0
, and for
2<p<\infty
define

\begin{cases}\ell_{g}(u)\;:=\;\int_{\partial\Omega}g_{0}\,u\,d\sigma\\
\\
g(x,u)\;:=\;|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{g}(u)g_{0}(x)\qquad\text{for }u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\text{ and }x\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Remark 7.1
.

When similar operators
f,g
in (Cf) and (Cg) are taken to be (appropriately) defined on the Banach-Sobolev space
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
, then the analysis of Auchmuty
[
1
]
leads to very general versions of Poincaré- and Friedrichs-type inequalities.
So these
f,g
operators are of importance in the analysis of partial differential equations on Sobolev spaces.

Remark 7.2
.

Readers interested in more general nonlinearities (in the interior and on the boundary equations) may consult Mavinga and Nkashama
[
16
]
, where
f,g
are assumed to satisfy:

(Cf’):

f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R})
and there are constants
b_{1},b_{2}>0
such that

|f(x,u)|\;\leq\;b_{1}+b_{2}|u|^{s}\qquad\text{with }0\leq s<\frac{N+2}{N-2};

(Cg’):

g\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R})
and there are constants
a_{1},a_{2}>0
such that

|g(x,u)|\;\leq\;a_{1}+a_{2}|u|^{s}\qquad\text{with }0\leq s<\frac{N}{N-2}.

The functional on
H^{1}(\Omega)
associated with (Cf’),(Cg’) is
F(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}f(x,u)\,v\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x,u)\,v\,d\sigma
, and the map
u\mapsto F(u,\cdot)
is shown in
[
16
]
to be continuous from
H^{1}(\Omega)
to its dual.
A nice detailed proof that also argues the boundary term is given in Lemma 4.2 of
[
16
]
.
It is worth noting that in
[
16
]
the nonlinearity
F
interacts with “eigenvalue-lines” and here the focus is on how
F
interacts with eigencurves.

Remark 7.3
.

A simple one-dimensional case in which the condition

(C7):

The map
F:H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}
, as in (
7.3
) below, is Frechét differentiable.

holds for quite general
F
is given in the Appendix in Section
8
below; the higher dimensional case with much more general
F
requires its own detailed careful analysis as in
[
16
]
referenced above.
In this paper, attention is given to
f,g
satisfying (Cf) and (Cg).

When (C1) - (C5) above hold, the bilinear forms on
H^{1}(\Omega)
associated with (
7.1
) are

(7.2)

\begin{cases}a(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\Omega}[(A\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v\,+\,c\,u\,v]\,dx\,+\,\int_{\partial\Omega}b_{c}\,u\,v\,d\sigma,\\
\\
b(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\partial\Omega}b_{0}\,u\,v\,d\sigma,\\
\\
m(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\Omega}m_{0}\,u\,v\,dx.\end{cases}

The assumptions on the data
A,b_{0},b_{c},c,m_{0}
imply that these forms, respectively, satisfy assumptions (A1), (A2), (A3) of Section
2
.

When (Cf) and (Cg) hold, the map
F
on
H^{1}(\Omega)
associated with the interior and boundary nonlinear terms of equation (
7.1
) is

(7.3)

F(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\Omega}f(x,u)\,v\,dx\;+\;\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x,u)\,v\,d\sigma\qquad\text{with }u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega).

With these bilinear and nonlinear forms defined, this section studies the following
nonlinear elliptic boundary-value problem
: for fixed
\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, find nontrivial
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying

(7.4)

a(u,v)\;=\;\lambda\,b(u,v)\,+\,\mu\,m(u,v)\,+\,\varepsilon\,F(u,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}(\Omega),

where
a,b,m
are given by (
7.2
) and
F
is given by (
7.3
).

7.2.
Linear Steklov-Robin Fredholm Alternative

When
\varepsilon=0
, equation (
7.4
) is called a
Steklov-Robin eigenvalue problem
.
The
variational eigencurves

\lambda\mapsto\mu_{k}(\lambda)
for this two-parameter bilinear eigenproblem are obtained in Section 10 of
[
18
]
.
That section also provides regularity, orthogonality, asymptotic, and geometrical results for these eigencurves.
Then Section
3
above yields, for fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the
canonical
spectral data

(7.5)

(\mu_{k}(\lambda)\,,\,e_{k}(\lambda))\qquad\text{with }k\in\mathbb{N}

that satisfies, for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, the following identity:

(7.6)

\int_{\Omega}[A\nabla e_{k}(\lambda)\cdot\nabla v+c\,e_{k}(\lambda)\,v\,-\mu_{k}(\lambda)\,m_{0}\,e_{k}(\lambda)\,v]\,dx\;+\;\int_{\partial\Omega}[b_{c}\,e_{k}(\lambda)\,v\,-\,\lambda b_{0}\,e_{k}(\lambda)\,v]\,d\sigma\;\;=\;\;0.

The
linear Steklov-Robin Fredholm equation
, for fixed
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\ell\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}
, and whose solution
u
is sought in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, is given by

(7.7)

\int_{\Omega}[A\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+c\,u\,v\,-\mu_{k}(\lambda)\,m_{0}\,u\,v]\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}[b_{c}\,u\,v\,-\,\lambda b_{0}\,u\,v]\,d\sigma\;=\;\ell(v)

and is to be satisfied for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

The nonresonance alternative for the linear Steklov-Robin Fredholm equation is:

Theorem 7.4
.

Assume
(C1)
-
(C5)
, hold, and let
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
and
\ell\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}
be fixed.
Then
\mu\neq\mu_{k}(\lambda)
for all
k
, there is a unique function
\hat{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying (
7.7
) for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and
\hat{u}
has the spectral representation

(7.8)

\hat{u}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\,\ell(e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda).

Bounds for the solution
\hat{u}
of this nonresonance problem are given by specific equivalent norms on
H^{1}(\Omega)
and related to Steklov-Robin eigencurves as stated next.

Theorem 7.5
.

Assume
(C1)
-
(C5)
, hold, and let
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
and
\ell\in(H^{1}(\Omega)))^{*}
be fixed.
Let
\tau>0
be large enough so that the bilinear form on
H^{1}(\Omega)
given by

(7.9)

a_{\lambda\tau}(u,v)\;:=\;\int_{\Omega}[A\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+c\,u\,v\,+\;\tau\,m_{0}\,u\,v]\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}[b_{c}\,u\,v\,-\,\lambda b_{0}\,u\,v]\,d\sigma

is coercive, and denote by
\|\cdot\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}^{*}}
the dual norm on
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}
when
H^{1}(\Omega)
is equipped with the norm
\|u\|_{a_{\lambda,\tau}}:=\sqrt{a_{\lambda\tau}(u,u)}
.

(i)

When
\mu<\mu_{1}(\lambda)
, then the unique solution
\hat{u}
to (
7.7
) is bounded by

(7.10)

\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}\;\leq\;\left(\frac{\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\right)\,\|\ell\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}^{*}}

(ii)

When
\mu_{k_{0}}(\lambda)<\mu<\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)
, then the unique solution
\hat{u}
to (
7.7
) is bounded by

(7.11)

\|\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}\;\leq\;\frac{(\mu+\tau)(\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu_{1}(\lambda)}{(\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\tau)(\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu)}\,\|P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}}\;+\;\left(\frac{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)+\tau}{\mu_{k_{0}+1}(\lambda)-\mu}\right)\|\ell\|_{a_{\lambda\tau}^{*}}

where

(7.12)

P_{k_{0}}\hat{u}\;:=\;\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\frac{\ell(e_{k}(\lambda))}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}e_{k}(\lambda)

is the
k_{0}^{th}
-spectral approximation of
\hat{u}
.

Since the bilinear forms
(a,b,m)
for the Steklov-Robin system satisfy (C1)-(C5), and thus satisfy conditions (A1)-(A3) in §
2
above, with
V=H^{1}(\Omega)
, these two theorems are a special case of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
of §
4
.

The resonance alternative for the linear Steklov-Robin Fredholm equation (
7.7
) is given in the next theorem, and is a special case of Theorem
4.3
.
To simplify the statement, for fixed
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
define

J_{\lambda,\mu}\;:=\;\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\mu_{k}(\lambda)=\mu\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{\lambda,\mu}\;:=\;\text{span}\{e_{k}(\lambda):k\in J_{\lambda,\mu}\};

this notation was used in Theorems
4.3
and
6.1
for the abstract results at resonance.

Theorem 7.6
.

Assume
(C1)
-
(C5)
hold, and let
(\lambda,\mu)\in\sigma(a,b,m)
and
\ell\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}
be fixed.
Then
\mu=\mu_{k_{0}}(\lambda)
for some
k_{0}
and

(i)

there are solutions
\hat{u}
to (
7.7
) if and only if
\ell(e)=0
for all
e\in E_{\lambda,\mu}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
.

(ii)

the solution set
S_{\lambda\mu}
to (
7.7
) is the affine subspace comprised of all
\hat{u}
of the form

(7.13)

\hat{u}\;=\;\sum_{k\notin J_{\lambda,\mu}}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\lambda)-\mu}\,\ell(e_{k}(\lambda))\,e_{k}(\lambda)\;+\;\hat{v}\qquad\text{where }\quad\hat{v}\in E_{\lambda,\mu}

where
e_{k}(\lambda)\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfy the Steklov-Robin eigenequation (
7.6
) with
\mu=\mu_{k}(\lambda)
and are normalized with respect to the
m
-norm, i.e.
\|e_{k}(\lambda)\|_{m}^{2}:=m(e_{k}(\lambda),e_{k}(\lambda))=1
.

7.3.
Nonlinear Steklov-Robin Fredholm Alternative

Let
F:H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the form given by (
7.3
), assume
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
.
The
nonlinear Steklov-Robin Fredholm equation
, whose solution
u
is sought in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, is given by

(7.14)

\int_{\Omega}[A\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+c\,u\,v\,-\mu_{k}(\lambda)\,m_{0}\,u\,v]\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}[b_{c}\,u\,v\,-\,\lambda b_{0}\,u\,v]\,d\sigma\;=\;\varepsilon F(u,v)

and is to be satisfied for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

To treat the nonlinear problem (
7.14
), first define the functional
\mathscr{F}:H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

(7.15)

\mathscr{F}(u)\;:=\;\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}f(x,u)\,u\,dx\;+\;\frac{1}{p}\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x,u)\,u\,d\sigma

The basic regularity of this functional is the following.

Theorem 7.7
.

Assume
(Cf)
and
(Cg)
hold, and
\mathscr{F}
is given by (
7.15
).

(i)

\mathscr{F}
is positive, convex, and weakly continuous on
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

(ii)

\mathscr{F}
is Gâteaux differentiable at
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with first variation given by

(7.16)

\delta\mathscr{F}(u;v)\;=\;|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{f}(u)\ell_{f}(v)\;+\;|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{g}(u)\ell_{g}(v)

with direction vector
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

(iii)

\mathscr{F}
has a second variation at
u
in the directions
v,w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
given by

(7.17)

\delta^{2}\mathscr{F}(u;v,w)\;=\;(p-1)|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{f}(v)\ell_{f}(w)\;+\;(p-1)|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{g}(v)\ell_{g}(w)

Proof.

The real-valued function
s\mapsto|s|^{p}
is convex, positive, and continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
.
Rewriting the functional in (
7.15
) as

\mathscr{F}(u)=\frac{1}{p}|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p}+\frac{1}{p}|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p}

it follows from results on compositions, and that
\ell_{f}
and
\ell_{g}
are continuous linear functionals, that
\mathscr{F}
is convex, positive and continuous.
This implies
\mathscr{F}
is weakly
l.s.c.
on
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
By the chain rule for Gâteaux derivatives, and the formulae for the first and second (classical) derivatives of
s\mapsto|s|^{p}
, the formulae for the first and second variations of
\mathscr{F}
hold.
∎

Corollary 7.8
.

If conditions
(Cf)
,
(Cg)
hold, then the functional
\mathscr{F}
given by (
7.15
) is twice Fréchet differentiable

Proof.

The image of the map

u\mapsto\delta\mathscr{F}(u,\cdot)\;=\;|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{f}(u)\ell_{f}(\cdot)\;+\;|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{g}(u)\ell_{g}(\cdot)

is a sum of
\ell_{f}
and
\ell_{g}
in
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}
with coefficients
|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{f}(u)
and
|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{g}(u)
being continuous in
u
as
p>2
.
Then the image of the map

u\;\mapsto\;\delta^{2}\mathscr{F}(u;\cdot,\cdot)\;=\;(p-1)|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{f}(\cdot)\ell_{f}(\cdot)\;+\;(p-1)|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p-2}\ell_{g}(\cdot)\ell_{g}(\cdot)

is a sum of the continuous symmetric bilinear forms
\ell_{f}\otimes\ell_{f}
and
\ell_{g}\otimes\ell_{g}
with coefficients
(p-1)|\ell_{f}(u)|^{p-2}
and
(p-1)|\ell_{g}(u)|^{p-2}
continuous in
u
as
p>2
.
By the basic relationship between Gâteaux derivatives and Fréchet derivatives, the desired result follows; see, for instance, Lemma 2.3.2 of Blanchard and Brüning
[
4
]
.
∎

With this regularity established for
\mathscr{F}
, the nonresonance alternative for the nonlinear Steklov-Robin Fredholm equation (
7.14
) can now be stated.

Theorem 7.9
.

Assume
(C1)
-
(C5)
,
(Cf)
and
(Cg)
hold, and that the map
F
is as in (
7.3
).
If
(\lambda,\mu)\notin\sigma(a,b,m)
, then there exists a
\delta>0
such that if
-\delta<\varepsilon<\delta
, then (
7.14
) has a locally unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}
.
Further, the mapping
u:(-\delta,\delta)\to H^{1}(\Omega)
, defined by
u(\varepsilon)=u_{\varepsilon}
, is continuously differentiable with
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u(\varepsilon)=0
.

Proof.

The map
u\mapsto F(u,\cdot)
is Fréchet differentiable by the previous corollary, so this theorem is a special case of Theorem (
5.1
).
∎

Applications in the case of resonance are similar, but more delicate. For readers interested in a concrete example applying ideas similar to those in theorem

8.
Appendix: Fréchet Differentiability of the Nonlinearity

In this appendix, we give an example of when the differentiability condition of Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
6.1
may hold. For simplicity, the example is 1-dimsensional, but similar results hold in higher dimensions.

Let
H:=\{u\in AC[0,1]\;:\;u^{\prime}\in L^{2}[0,1]\}
be the Sobolev space with the usual norm

\|u\|_{1,2}\;:=\;\|u\|_{2}+\|u^{\prime}\|_{2}

and suppose
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a given function.

Theorem 8.1
.

If
F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and both
F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
are bounded, then the operator
T_{F}:H\rightarrow H
defined by
T_{F}(u)(t)\;:=\;F(u(t))
is Fréchet differentiable with
DT_{F}(u)(h)=F^{\prime}(u)h
for any
u,h\in H.

Proof.

First we show
T_{F}
is well-defined, so let
u\in H
be fixed.
The Mean-Value Theorem together with the boundedness of
F^{\prime}
imply

|F(u_{2})-F(u_{1})|\;\leq\;\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}|u_{2}-u_{1}|\qquad\text{for all }u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
u\in AC[0,1]
, choose
\delta>0
so that

0\leq a_{1}\leq b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}\leq b_{n}\leq 1\text{ with }\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}-a_{i}\;<\;\delta\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u(b_{i})-u(a_{i})|\;<\;\varepsilon.

Putting these two results together gives

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|F(u(b_{i}))-F(u(a_{i}))|\;\;\leq\;\;\|F\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u(b_{i})-u(a_{i})|\;\;<\;\;\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\cdot\varepsilon,

so that
T_{F}(u)\in AC[0,1]
.
Since
T_{F}(u)^{\prime}(t)=F^{\prime}(u(t))u^{\prime}(t)
holds for almost every
t\in[0,1]
,

\|T_{F}(u)^{\prime}\|_{2}\;=\;\|F^{\prime}(u)\,u^{\prime}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|u^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Hence,
T_{F}(u)\in H
since
u^{\prime}\in L^{2}[0,1]
.

Boundedness of
F^{\prime}
implies continuity of
T_{F}
since for
u,v\in H
the work above gives

\|T_{F}(u)-T_{F}(v)\|_{1,2}\;\leq\;\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\left(\|u-v\|_{2}+\|u^{\prime}-v^{\prime}\|_{2}\right)\;=\;\|F\|_{\infty}\|u-v\|_{1,2}.

We finish the proof by showing that
T_{F}
is continuously differentiable with derivative
DT_{F}(u)=F^{\prime}(u)
, that is,
DT_{F}(u)(h)\;=\;F^{\prime}(u)\,h
for all
h\in H
. Note that with our notation above
DT_{F}(u)(h)=T_{F^{\prime}}(u)h
.
As was shown above, since
F^{\prime}
is bounded,
T_{F^{\prime}}
is a map from
H
to
H
, and since products of absolutely continuous functions are again absolutely continuous,
DT_{F}(u)(h)
belongs to
AC[0,1]
.
For almost every
t\in[0,1]
,

(DT_{F}(u)(h))^{\prime}(t)\;=\;F^{\prime\prime}(u(t))u^{\prime}(t)h(t)\;+\;F^{\prime}(u(t))h^{\prime}(t).

The boundedness of
F^{\prime},F^{\prime\prime}
and
h
(since
h\in AC[0,1]
) give the bound

\|(DT_{F}(u)(h))^{\prime}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\|F^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\|h\|_{\infty}\|u^{\prime}\|_{2}\;+\;\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|h^{\prime}\|_{2},

so that
(DT_{F}(u)(h))^{\prime}\in L^{2}[0,1]
and therefore
DT_{F}(u)(h)\in H
.
The estimates above give

\displaystyle\|DT_{F}(u)h\|_{1,2}\;\;=

\displaystyle\;\;\|DT_{F}(u)h\|_{2}\;+\;\|(DT_{F}(u)h)^{\prime}\|_{2}\;

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\;\|F\|_{\infty}\|h\|_{2}\;+\;\|F^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\|h\|_{\infty}\|u^{\prime}\|_{2}\;+\;\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|h^{\prime}\|_{2}.

By the Sobolev imbedding theorem,
\|h\|_{\infty}\leq C\|h\|_{1,2}
for some constant
C>0
, see Brezis
[
5
]
.
Thus,
DT_{F}(u)
is a bounded linear map from
H
to
H
.

A more careful estimate gives

\displaystyle\|(DT_{F}(u)-DT_{F}(v))h\|_{1,2}\;\leq

\displaystyle\;\|(F^{\prime}(u)-F^{\prime}(v))h\|_{2}\;+\;\|(F^{\prime\prime}(u)u^{\prime}-F^{\prime\prime}(v)v^{\prime})h+(F^{\prime}(u)-F^{\prime}(v))h^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;C\|F^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\|u-v\|_{1,2}\|h\|_{1,2},

where
C
is a new constant.
It follows that
\|DT_{F}(u)-DT_{F}(v)\|_{1,2}\;\leq\;\|F^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\|u-v\|_{1,2}
, so the the map
u\mapsto DT_{F}(u)
is continuous.

We now finish the proof by showing that
DT_{F}(u)(h)=F^{\prime}(u)h
. To see this note that for
u,h\in H
we have

\begin{split}|T_{F}(u+h)(t)-T_{F}(u)(t)-F^{\prime}(u(t))h(t)|&=|F(u(t)+h(t))-F(u(t))-F^{\prime}(u(t))h(t)|\\
&=|F^{\prime}(c(h,t))-F^{\prime}(u(t))||h(t)|\end{split}

where
c(h,t)\in[u(t),u(t)+h(t)]
.
Calculating

|T_{F}(u+h)^{\prime}(t)-T_{F}(u)^{\prime}(t)-(F^{\prime}(u(t))h(t))^{\prime}|

we get, at least for almost every
t\in[0,1]
,

\begin{split}|F^{\prime}(u(t)+h(t))(u^{\prime}(t)+h^{\prime}(t))&-F^{\prime}(u(t))u^{\prime}(t)-F^{\prime\prime}(u(t))u^{\prime}(t)h(t)-F^{\prime}(u(t))h^{\prime}(t)|=\\
&|F^{\prime\prime}(d(h,t))-F^{\prime\prime}(u(t))||h(t)||u^{\prime}(t)|+|F^{\prime}(u(t)+h(t))-F^{\prime}(u(t))||h^{\prime}(t)|\\
\end{split}

where
d(h,t)\in[x(t),x(t)+h(t)]
.
The continuity of
F^{\prime},F^{\prime\prime}
imply the differentiability of
T_{F}
, since by the Sobolev embedding theorem,
||h||_{1,2}\to 0
implies
||h||_{\infty}\to 0
.
∎
