A General Class of Trimodal Distributions: Properties and Inference
Roberto Vila, Victor Serra, Mehmet N. Çankaya, Felipe Quintino

Abstract
The modality is important topic for modelling. Using parametric models is an efficient way when real data set shows trimodality. In this paper we propose a new class of trimodal probability distributions, that is, probability distributions that have up to three modes. Trimodality itself is achieved by applying a proper transformation to density function of certain continuous probability distributions. At first, we obtain preliminary results for an arbitratry density function g ⁡ ( x ) g(x) and, next, we focus on the Gaussian case, studying trimodal Gaussian model more deeply. The Gaussian distribution is applied to produce the trimodal form of Gaussian known as normal distribution. The tractability of analytical expression of normal distribution, and properties of the trimodal normal distribution are important reasons why we choose normal distribution. Furthermore, the existing distributions should be improved to be capable of modelling efficiently when there exists a trimodal form in a data set. After new density function is proposed, estimating its parameters is important. Since Mathematica 12.0 software has optimization tools and important modelling techniques, computational steps are performed by using this software. The bootstrapped form of real data sets are applied to show the modelling ability of the proposed distribution when real data sets show trimodality.

A class of continuous probability distributions

Let
g:D\subset\mathbbm{R}\to[0,\infty)
,
D={\rm supp}(g)\neq\emptyset
, be a kernel density with corresponding cumulative distribution function (CDF) denoted by
G
.
The function
g
can be associated (or not) with an additional parameter
\xi
(or vector
\bm{\xi}

For a random variable

\displaystyle f(x;\bm{\theta})={\sigma\over Z_{\bm{\theta}}}\,\biggl[\rho+\delta T\biggl({x-\mu\over\sigma};\alpha,p\biggr)\biggr]\,g\biggl({x-\mu\over\sigma}\biggr),\quad{x-\mu\over\sigma}\in D,

\displaystyle T(x;\alpha;p)={\gamma(p,x^{2}/\alpha^{2})\over\Gamma(p)}\quad\text{with}\ p>0\ \text{known}.

(2.2)

\displaystyle Z_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(\rho+\delta)\sigma+\delta\sigma\left\{{\mathbbm{E}}\big[G(-\alpha\sqrt{Y})\big]-{\mathbbm{E}}\big[G(\alpha\sqrt{Y})\big]\right\},

Trimodality for a class of symmetric kernel densities

In this subsection, we suposse that the kernel density

\displaystyle g(x)=g(0)\,{\rm e}^{{}^{\textstyle-\int_{0}^{x}t\mathfrak{h}(t^{2})\,{\rm d}t}},\quad x\in\mathbbm{R},

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)=-x\mathfrak{h}(x^{2})g(x),\quad x\in\mathbbm{R}.

(3.2)

It is immediate to verify that

on the real line
D=\mathbbm{R}
.
For example, in the Laplace, Cauchy, Student-
t
and Normal kernel densities (see Table
1

\mathfrak{h}(y)=1/\sqrt{y}
,
\mathfrak{h}(y)=2/(1+y)
,
\mathfrak{h}(y)=(\nu+1)/(\nu+{y})
and
\mathfrak{h}(y)=1

\forall y>0

Moreover, we assume that

\mathfrak{h}
has the following form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}(y)={C\over(\alpha^{2}A+y)^{\beta-p}}\quad\text{for }\,C\geqslant 1,A\geqslant 0\ \text{and}\ \beta>p.

(3.3)

That is,
\mathfrak{h}(y)

, decays polynomially.
The function

In this paper, the Gaussian case

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be as in (
3.3
).
For some
\rho>0
the function
\mathfrak{R}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(y)={2\delta}\biggl[{(1/\alpha^{2})^{p}\over\Gamma(p)}\,y^{p-1}{\rm e}^{-y/\alpha^{2}}\biggr]-\biggl[\rho+\delta\,{\gamma(p,y/\alpha^{2})\over\Gamma(p)}\biggr]\mathfrak{h}(y),\quad y>0,

has at most two real roots.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
g
be a kernel density as in (
3.1
), with
\mathfrak{h}
as in (
3.3
).
A point

is a critical point of density (
2.1

\mathfrak{R}[(x-\mu)^{2}/\sigma^{2}]=0,

where
\mathfrak{R}
is as in Lemma

Proof.

The proof is immediate since,
by using Equation (
3.2
), the first-order derivative of

, with respect to

, is given by

f^{\prime}(x;\bm{\theta})=[(x-\mu)/\sigma]g((x-\mu)/\sigma)\mathfrak{R}[(x-\mu)^{2}/\sigma^{2}]/(\sigma Z_{\bm{\theta}})

Theorem 3.3

(Uni- bi- or trimodality)
.

If
X\sim{\rm TD}(\bm{\theta})

then the following hold:

(1)

If
\mathfrak{R}
has no real roots then
f(x;\bm{\theta})
is unimodal with mode
x=\mu
.

(2)

If
\mathfrak{R}
has one real root then
f(x;\bm{\theta})
is bimodal with minimum point
x=\mu
.

(3)

If
\mathfrak{R}
has two distinct real roots then
f(x;\bm{\theta})
is trimodal where
x=\mu
is one of the modes.

Proof.

It is clear that if
\mathfrak{R}
has no real roots, by Proposition
3.2
,
x=\mu
is the only critical point of the density
f
. Since
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x;\bm{\theta})=0
, the point
x=\mu
is a mode. This proves Item (1).

In order to prove Item (2), we suppose that
\mathfrak{R}
has one real root, denoted by
a
. By Proposition
3.2
, it follows that
x=\mu
and
x=\mu\pm\sigma\sqrt{a}
are three critical points of
f
. Since
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x;\bm{\theta})=0
, the point
x=\mu
is a minimum and
x=\mu\pm\sigma\sqrt{a}
are two symmetrical modes. This proves the second item.

Now, we assume that
\mathfrak{R}
has two distinct real roots, denoted by
a
and
b
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
a<b
. Again, by Proposition
3.2
we have that
x=\mu
,
x=\mu\pm\sigma\sqrt{a}
and
x=\mu\pm\sigma\sqrt{b}
are five critical points of
f
. Since
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x;\bm{\theta})=0
and
a<b
, the critical points
x=\mu
and
x=\mu\pm\sigma\sqrt{b}
are modes and
x=\mu\pm\sigma\sqrt{a}
are minimum points. Hence, the proof of Item (3) follows.
∎

4.5
Rate of the random variable
X\sim{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})

Following the reference
[
23
]
, the rate of a continuous random variable
X
is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{X}=-\lim_{x\to\infty}{{\rm d}\log f_{X}(x)\over{\rm d}x},

where
f_{X}(x)
denotes its respective PDF.

A simple computation shows that, the rate of
X\sim{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})
is

\displaystyle\tau_{{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})}={1\over\sigma}\lim_{x\to\infty}\left[{x-\mu\over\sigma}-{2\over\alpha\Gamma(p)}\,{({x-\mu\over\alpha\sigma})^{2p-1}{\rm e}^{-({x-\mu\over\alpha\sigma})^{2}}\over\rho+T({x-\mu\over\sigma};\alpha,p)}\right]=\infty.

Then, far enough out in the tail, the distribution of
X\sim{{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})}
looks like a Normal distribution, as expected.
In addition, we have some comparisons between the rate of
X\sim{{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})}
with the rates of some random variables with known distributions in the literature: Inverse-gamma, Log-normal, Generalized-Pareto, BGumbel
[
26
]
, BWeibull
[
9
]
, BGamma
[
8
]
, exponential
and Normal;

\displaystyle\tau_{{\rm InvGamma}(\alpha,\beta)}=\tau_{{\rm LogNorm}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}

\displaystyle=\tau_{{\rm GenPareto}(\alpha,\beta,\xi)}=\tau_{\rm BWeibull(\alpha<1,\beta,\delta)}=0

\displaystyle<\tau_{\rm BGumbel(\mu,\beta,\delta)}=\tau_{\rm BWeibull(\alpha=1,\beta,\delta)}=\tau_{{\rm BGamma}(\alpha,1/\beta,\delta)}=\tau_{{\rm exp}(1/\beta)}=1/\beta

\displaystyle<\tau_{\rm BWeibull(\alpha>1,\beta,\delta)}=\tau_{{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})}=\tau_{{\rm Normal}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}=\infty.

In other words, the tail of the normal distribution, of
X\sim{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})
and of
{\rm BWeibull}(\alpha>1,\beta,\delta)
are lighter than the tail of the other distributions specified above.

5
The Model arising from a Generalized Mixture

The weighted distributions of random variable
(W-\mu)/\sigma
, with weight function

w_{k}
, have its PDF defined by

\displaystyle f_{k}(x;\mu,\sigma)={1\over\int_{D}w_{k}(y)g(y){\rm d}y}\,w_{k}{\biggl({x-\mu\over\sigma}\biggr)}g\biggl({x-\mu\over\sigma}\biggr),\quad{x-\mu\over\sigma}\in D,\ k=0,1,\ldots.

By using the power series expansion of the incomplete gamma function in
(
C.4
),
note that the PDF
f(x;\bm{\theta})
in (
2.1
) interprets as an infinite (generalized) mixture of weighted distributions of
(W-\mu)/\sigma
with the weight functions

w_{k}(y)=y^{2k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots
, and with same parameter
vector
(\mu,\sigma)
. That is,

\displaystyle f(x;\bm{\theta})=c_{0}f_{0}(x;\mu,\sigma)+\sum_{k=p}^{\infty}c_{k}f_{k}(x;\mu,\sigma),

(5.1)

where the constants
c_{0}
,
c_{k}
, with
k=p,p+1,\ldots
, that depends only on
\bm{\theta}
, respectively, are given by

\displaystyle c_{0}={\rho\sigma\over Z_{\bm{\theta}}};\quad c_{k}={\delta\sigma\over\Gamma(p)Z_{\bm{\theta}}}\,{(-1)^{k-p}\mathbbm{E}(W^{2k})\over k(k-p)!\alpha^{2k}},\quad k=p,p+1,\ldots.

It is straightforward to verify that
c_{0}+\sum_{k=p}^{\infty}c_{k}=1
. Note that the weights
c_{k}
can take on negative values.

If
X\sim{\rm TD}_{\Phi}(\bm{\theta})
and
L:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}
is a Borel-measurable function, then, by representation (
5.1
), the expectation of truncated random variable
\mathds{1}_{\{X\leqslant b\}}L(X)
, with

, is given by

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{X\leqslant b\}}L(X)\big]=c_{0}\mathbbm{E}_{0}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{X\leqslant b\}}L(X)\big]+\sum_{k=p}^{\infty}c_{k}\mathbbm{E}_{k}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{X\leqslant b\}}L(X)\big].

But this representation as infinite sum is not as informative as that representation of Theorem
3.4
. For this reason in the paper, we had been concerned with providing mathematical representations that allow us to find the closed expressions for some characteristics of the distribution (such as normalizing factor, CDF, moments and entropies) as a function of the known mathematical functions.

6.1
The Log

-Likelihood Function

For a random sample

from the random variable

, with parameter vector
\bm{\theta}=(\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\rho,\delta)
, let us suppose that

are the observed values of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}

The ML
q
E of
\bm{\theta}
is defined as

\displaystyle l_{q}(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log_{q}f(x_{i};\bm{\theta}),\quad\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbbm{R}^{n},

(6.1)

where
\log_{q}(x)
is the deformed logarithm defined in (
3.5
)
[
20
,
32
]
.

By taking partial derivatives in (
6.1
), with respect to
\theta\in\{\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\rho,\delta\}
, we have

\displaystyle{\partial l_{q}(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})\over\partial\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{1-q}(x_{i};\bm{\theta})\,{\partial\log f(x_{i};\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta},

with

\displaystyle\log f(x;\bm{\theta})=-\log(Z_{\bm{\theta}})+\log\biggl[\rho+\delta T\biggl({x-\mu\over\sigma};\alpha,p\biggr)\biggr]+\log g\biggl({x-\mu\over\sigma}\biggr)

(6.2)

and
Z_{\bm{\theta}}
,
T
are as in (
2.3
) and (
2.2
), respectively.
In general, the estimating equations for the parameters have the form

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{1-q}(x_{i};\bm{\theta})\,{\partial\log f(x_{i};\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta}=0,\quad\theta\in\{\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\rho,\delta\},

(6.3)

where the first-order partial derivatives of the function
\log f(x;\bm{\theta})
are given in Section
D
of the Appendix.
A solution of the system of equations in (
6.3
) is called ML
q
E estimator.
It is not possible to derive analytical solution for the ML
q
E
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
.

8
Conclusions

Since recent times show that an increasing popularity has been observed in the modelling for data sets having modality, producing the trimodal form of any PDF has been proposed. The trimodal form is constructed by using the technique which includes the cumulative function of Maxwell distribution, the existing unimodal distribution and the corresponding normalizing constant of the proposed distribution. The properties of trimodality have been examined. The application of producing the trimodality has been conducted for the normal distribution which is symmetric and unimodal form with two parameters which are location and scale. The properties of trimodal normal distribution have been examined. Thus, the applicability of this distribution have been tested. The trimodal normal distribution can have different forms such as strict and soft modalities to perform a precise fitting when there exists three modes in the empirical distribution of the data sets.

A comparison among trimodal normal, the kernel type estimation method, the probable parametric distribution driven by Mathematica software has been performed in order to make applications for numerical evaluation of TD
Φ
. The
\log_{q}
-likelihood estimation method and its special form with
q\to 1
have been used to estimate of parameters of trimodal normal distribution. The proofs, properties of TD
Φ
distribution and codes used for application have been given by appendices if the researchers perform to model the data sets by use of TD
Φ
distribution.

The future will be an application on the different areas of statistics such as regression modelling, the tools in the multivariate statistics and other tools based on the distribution theory. The order statistic form of TD
Φ
in the least informative distribution will be studied for the trimodal forms of the existing distributions in the applied field of science. Additionally, the precise modelling for inliers into data sets can also be performed by use of trimodality, the generalized logarithms, entropy functions, order statistic and different estimation methods all together
[
35
,
36
]
. A package in R software will be prepared for practitioners after the special function in R software are improved.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
Y
be a random variable with
{\rm Gamma}(p,1/\alpha^{2})
distribution. The corresponding PDF and CDF of
Y
, respectively, are given by
f(y;\alpha,p)={(1/\alpha^{2})^{p}}\,y^{p-1}{\rm e}^{-y/\alpha^{2}}/\Gamma(p)
and

F(y;\alpha,p)={\gamma(p,y/\alpha^{2})/\Gamma(p)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(y)={2\delta}f(y;\alpha,p)-[\rho+\delta F(y;\alpha,p)]\mathfrak{h}(y),\quad y>0.

(A.1)

Suppose
y\geqslant\alpha^{2}(1-A)
. Then

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{(\alpha^{2}A+y)/\alpha^{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{(\alpha^{2}A+y)^{k}/\alpha^{2k}\over k!}\geqslant{(\alpha^{2}A+y)^{n+1}/\alpha^{2(n+1)}\over(n+1)!},

for each
n\in\mathbbm{N}
, such that

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-y/\alpha^{2}}\leqslant{(n+1)!\alpha^{2(n+1)}{\rm e}^{A}\over(\alpha^{2}A+y)^{n+1}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle{2\delta}f(y;\alpha,p)

\displaystyle\leqslant{2\delta}\biggl[{(1/\alpha^{2})^{p}\over\Gamma(p)}\,y^{p-1}\,{(n+1)!\alpha^{2(n+1)}{\rm e}^{A}\over(\alpha^{2}A+y)^{n+1}}\biggr]

\displaystyle\leqslant{2\delta}\biggl[{(1/\alpha^{2})^{p}\over\Gamma(p)}\,{(n+1)!\alpha^{2(n+1)}{\rm e}^{A}\over(\alpha^{2}A+y)^{n+1-p}}\biggr]\leqslant{\rho C\over(\alpha^{2}A+y)^{\beta-p}}=\rho\mathfrak{h}(y),

(A.2)

for
\beta<n+1
,
C\geqslant 1
and some
\rho>0
, so that

\displaystyle\rho\geqslant{2\delta}\biggl[{(1/\alpha^{2})^{p}\over\Gamma(p)}\,{(n+1)!\alpha^{2(n+1)}{\rm e}^{A}}\biggr].

(A.3)

Since
F(y;\alpha,p)
is a CDF, from (
A.2
) it follows that

\displaystyle{2\delta}f(y;\alpha,p)\leqslant[\rho+\delta F(y;\alpha,p)]\mathfrak{h}(y)\leqslant(\rho+\delta)\mathfrak{h}(y),\quad\forall y\geqslant\alpha^{2}(1-A).

(A.4)

In other words, for every
y\geqslant\alpha^{2}(1-A)
and
