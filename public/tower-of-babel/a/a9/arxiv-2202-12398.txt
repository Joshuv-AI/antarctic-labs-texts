1
Introduction

A
Hopf manifold
is a quotient of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
by a cyclic group
\langle A\rangle

generated by a holomorphic contraction
A
. This definition is motivated by Kodaira’s
research on complex surfaces. In
[
K
, Theorem 34]

Kodaira proved that a complex surface with
b_{1}=1
and

b_{2}=0
is a Hopf surface, if it
contains a curve and has no non-constant meromorphic
functions. Using the Poincaré classification
of contractions of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, and the further works by
Dulac, Lattès and Sternberg

[
L
,
Du
,
S
]
,
Kodaira has written a normal form
for a Hopf surface, expressing a given
contraction as a polynomial map
in appropriate complex coordinates.

If the contraction
A
is linear, then we speak about a
linear Hopf manifold
.

It is easily seen that a Hopf manifold (be it linear or
non-linear) of complex dimension
n
is diffeomorphic
with
S^{1}\times S^{2n-1}
and hence does not admit
Kähler metrics. It is then natural to ask what kind of
Hermitian metric can a Hopf manifold bear. For linear Hopf
surfaces, the answer is known since long: they have
locally conformally Kähler (LCK) metrics (see
Section
2.1
, also
[
DO
]
). This
was proven by various authors, first for diagonal linear
contractions (
[
V
]
), then for non-diagonalizable
linear contractions (
[
GO
,
B1
]
). In
[
KO
]
,
this constructon was generalized to arbitrary dimension.
This construction of LCK metric was based on deformation
arguments. First, one builds an LCK metric on a linear Hopf
manifold given by diagonalizable linear contrations
(we call such manifolds “diagonal Hopf manifolds”),
and then one extends this metric to the non-diagonal
linear Hopf manifolds using deformation stability of
LCK metric with potential (
2.1
,

[
OV2
]
).

In
[
OV4
]
, we gave a more conceptual construction of
LCK potential on any linear Hopf manifold.

In this paper, we complete this picture by proving that also non-linear Hopf manifolds
do bear LCK metrics with potential (
4
).

One way to attack the problem is to find a normal form for a general holomorphic contraction. In dimension greater that 2, this requires a careful analysis of resonant contractions (a case not considered in
[
S
]
). This is the method suggested by Belgun in
[
B2
]
.

We didn’t follow this path. Our proof consists in
embedding a non-linear Hopf manifold into a linear Hopf manifold.

In fact, the result we prove is even stronger.
Let
V
be a Stein variety with an isolated
singularity
x
, and
\gamma:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}V
an
invertible holomorphic contraction centered in
x\in V
.
Then
M:=\frac{V\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

is a compact, smooth complex variety. We prove that
M
can be
embedded to a linear Hopf manifold. Then

2.1
implies that

M
is LCK with potential whenever it is smooth.

The proof of this result relies on several results in
functional analysis, mainly on the Riesz-Schauder theorem,
that we briefly recall in Subsections

2.2
and

2.3
. We used a similar
approach in
[
OV5
]
.

2
Prerequisites

2.1
Locally conformally Kähler manifolds

We gather here the minimal necessary background on locally
conformally Kähler structures, as is needed for our main
result.

Definition 2.1:
A complex Hermitian manifold
(M,I)
is
locally conformally
Kähler
(LCK, for short) if it admits a
covering
(\tilde{M},I)
equipped with a Kähler metric

\tilde{\omega}
such that the deck group of the
cover acts on
(\tilde{M},\tilde{\omega})
by holomorphic
homotheties. An
LCK metric
on an LCK manifold
is an Hermitian metric on
(M,I)
such that its
pullback to
\tilde{M}
is conformal with
\tilde{\omega}
.

A special class of LCK manifolds is the following:

Definition 2.2:
An LCK manifold has
LCK potential
if it
admits a Kähler covering on which the Kähler metric
has a global and positive potential function
\psi
such that
the deck group multiplies
\psi
by a
constant.
3
3

3

In the sequel, a
differential form which is multiplied by a constant
factor by the action of the deck group is called
automorphic
.

In this case,
M
is called
LCK manifold with
potential
.

Example 2.3:
Let
H:=({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus 0)/\langle A\rangle
,
A=\lambda\operatorname{Id}
,

|\lambda|>1
, be a
diagonal Hopf manifold
. It is LCK because the flat Kähler metric
\tilde{g}_{0}=\sum dz_{i}\otimes d\overline{z}_{i}
is multiplied by 2 by the deck group
{\mathbb{Z}}
; and it is LCK with potential because
\tilde{g}_{0}
has the global automorphic potential
\psi:=\sum|z_{i}|^{2}
.

By
[
OV2
]
, compact LCK manifolds with potential
are stable to small deformations of the complex
structure. This implies that
all
linear Hopf manifolds

({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus 0)/\langle A\rangle
,
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})

with eigenvalues of absolute value
>1
, are LCK with potential.

Remark 2.4:
By
[
OVV
]
, the blowup of an LCK manifold
along a Kähler submanifold is still LCK. However, its
Kähler cover cannot admit global potentials. Hence, the
class of LCK manifolds with potential is strict.

Remark 2.5:
Clearly, any
complex submanifold of an LCK manifold with potential is
also LCK with potential (
[
OV2
]
).

Remark 2.6:
Let
M
be a compact LCK manifold
with potential. We say that the potential is
proper

if it is proper as a smooth function, that is, all its
level sets are compact.
It was shown in
[
OV1
,
OV3
]
that
the LCK metric can be approximated
in the
{C}^{\infty}
-topology by an LCK
metric with
proper
LCK potential on the same
complex manifold. Therefore, as long as we are interested
in the complex geometry of LCK manifolds with potential,
we may assume that the potential is a proper
function. This, in turn, is equivalent with the deck group
of the Kähler covering being isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}

(
[
OV2
]
).

Theorem 2.7:
(
[
OV2
,
OV4
]
)
Let
M
be a compact LCK manifold with proper potential,
\operatorname{\sf dim}_{\mathbb{C}}M\geqslant 3
,
and
\tilde{M}
its Kähler
{\mathbb{Z}}
-covering.
Then the metric completion
\tilde{M}_{c}

admits a structure of a complex variety,
compatible with the complex structure on

\tilde{M}\subset\tilde{M}_{c}
, and
the complement
\tilde{M}_{c}\setminus\tilde{M}

is just one point. Moreover,
\tilde{M}_{c}
is an affine
algebraic variety obtained as an affine cone
over a projective orbifold.
4
4

4

\tilde{M}_{c}
is indeed the
Stein completion
of
\tilde{M}
in the sense of
[
AS
]
. The restriction
\operatorname{\sf dim}_{\mathbb{C}}M\geqslant 3
is imposed by our proof which makes use of the filling theorem
by Rossi and Andreotti-Siu (
[
R
,
AS
]
).

This result is essential in
the proof of the following Kodaira
embedding type result:

Theorem 2.8:
(
[
OV2
]
)
Any compact LCK manifold with potential, of complex dimension at least 3, admits a
holomorphic embedding into a linear Hopf manifold.

2.2
The Riesz-Schauder theorem

The following theorem can be used to obtain a version of Jordan normal
form for a compact operator on a Banach space. Recall
that the
spectrum
of a linear operator
F
is the set of all

\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
F-\mu\operatorname{Id}
is not invertible.

Theorem 2.9:
(Riesz-Schauder,
[
F
, Section 5.2]
)

Let
F:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}V
be a compact operator on a Banach
space. Then the spectrum
\operatorname{{\sf Spec}}F\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is
compact and discrete outside of
0\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover,
for each non-zero
\mu\in\operatorname{{\sf Spec}}F
, there exists a sufficiently
big number
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\geqslant 0}
such that for each
n>N
one has

V=\ker(F-\mu\operatorname{Id})^{n}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{im}(F-\mu\operatorname{Id})^{n}},

where
\overline{\operatorname{im}(F-\mu\operatorname{Id})^{n}}
is the closure of the image.
Finally, the space
\ker(F-\mu\operatorname{Id})^{n}
is finite-dimensional.

Remark 2.10:

Recall that
the root space of an operator

F\in\operatorname{End}(V)
, associated with an eigenvalue
\mu
,
is
\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\ker(F-\mu\operatorname{Id})^{n}
.
In the finite-dimensional case, the root spaces
coincide with the Jordan cells of the corresponding
matrix. Then

2.2
can be reformulated
by saying that any compact operator
F\in\operatorname{End}(V)
admits a
Jordan cell decomposition, with
V
identified with
a completed direct sum of the root spaces, which are
all finite-dimensional; moreover, the eigenvalues
of
F
converge to zero.

We shall need the following corollary of the Riesz-Schauder theorem, which is
obtained using the same arguments as in the finite-dimensional case.

Theorem 2.11:

Let
F:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}V
be a compact operator on a Banach space.
We say that
v
is
a root vector
for
F
if
v
lies in
a root space of
F
, for some eigenvalue
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Then the space generated by the root vectors is dense in
V
.

2.3
Normal families and
F
-finite vectors

Definition 2.12:

Let
M
be a complex manifold,
and
{\cal F}\subset H^{0}({\cal O}_{M})
a
family of holomorphic functions. We call
{\cal F}

a normal family
if for each compact

K\subset M
there exists
C_{K}>0
such that
for each
f\in{\cal F}
,
{\operatorname{{\sf sup}}}_{K}|f|\leqslant C_{K}
.

Definition 2.13:
The
C^{0}
-topology
on the space
of functions on
M
is the topology
of uniform convergence on compacts.

Theorem 2.14:

(Montel theorem)
Let
{\cal F}\subset H^{0}({\cal O}_{M})

be a normal family of functions,
and
\overline{\cal F}\subset H^{0}({\cal O}_{M})
its closure
in the
C^{0}
-topology. Then
\overline{\cal F}
is compact
in
C^{0}
-topology.
5
5

5

A set which has compact
closure is called
precompact
.

Proof:
[
Wu
]
.

Definition 2.15:

Let
F\in\operatorname{End}(V)
be an endomorphism of a vector space.
A vector
v\in V
is called
F
-finite

if the space generated by
v,F(v),F(F(v)),...

is finite-dimensional.

Remark 2.16:
Clearly, a vector is

F
-finite if and only if it is obtained as a combination
of root vectors. Then
2.2

implies the following.

Corollary 2.17:

Let
F:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}V
be a compact operator on a Banach space,
and
V_{0}\subset V
the space of all
F
-finite vectors.
Then
V_{0}
is dense in
V
.

3
Holomorphic contractions on Stein varieties

Recall that
a holomorphic contraction

of a variety
V
with center in
x\in V

is a map
\gamma:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}V
,
\gamma(x)=x
, such that
for each compact subset
K\subset V
and
any open neighbourhood
U\ni x
, there
exists
N>0
such that
\gamma^{n}(K)\subset U

for any integer
n>N
. In this case,

\gamma
is called
the contraction centered in
x
.

Theorem 3.1:

Let
V
be a Stein variety, and
\gamma:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}V

an invertible holomorphic contraction centered in
x
.
Assume that the group
\langle\gamma\rangle
acts on
V\backslash x
properly
discontinuously. Then the quotient variety

M:=\frac{V\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}
is
compact. Moreover, it admits
a holomorphic embedding to a linear Hopf manifold.

Proof. Step 1:
Let
V_{0}\ni x
be a precompact Stein neighbourhood of
x\in V
.
By the definition of a contraction, for
k
sufficiently big,
we have
\gamma^{k}(V_{0})\Subset V_{0}
.
6
6

6

Here, as elsewhere,
the notation
A\Subset B
means that
A
is
relatively compact
in
B
,
that is,
A
is a subset of
B
and its closure is compact.

Let
H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})
be the space of bounded holomorphic
functions on
V_{0}
, equipped with the
{\operatorname{{\sf sup}}}
-metric,
and
B(C)\subset H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})
the set of all
functions
f
with
|f|\leqslant C
. This is a normal family
(
2.3
), hence it is precompact in
C^{0}
-topology
(that is, in the topology of uniform convergence on compacts).
However, it is not compact in the
{\operatorname{{\sf sup}}}
-topology, because
the closed ball in a Banach space is never compact
(this statement is called “the Riesz theorem”, see

[
Di
, Chapter 1, Theorem 4]
.
7
7

7

Compare with the proof of

[
OV5
, Theorem 2.14]
.

Consider
(\gamma^{k})^{*}:\;H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V}){\>\longrightarrow\>}H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})

as a linear map of Banach spaces.
The map
(\gamma^{k})^{*}
takes any sequence
\{f_{i}\}
converging in

C^{0}
-topology to a sequence converging in the
{\operatorname{{\sf sup}}}
-topology.
Indeed,
\{f_{i}\}
uniformly converges on the closure

\overline{\gamma^{k}(V_{0})}
which is compact because
\gamma^{k}(V_{0})\Subset V_{0}
.
Since
(\gamma^{k})^{*}(f_{i})(z)=f_{i}(\gamma^{k}(z))
, the
function
f_{i}
takes the same values on
\gamma^{k}(V_{0})
as

(\gamma^{k})^{*}(f_{i})
takes on
V_{0}
.
Therefore, the sequence
\{(\gamma^{k})^{*}(f_{i})\}
uniformly converges
on
V_{0}
for any
k>0
.

Since an open ball in
H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})
is precompact
in the
C^{0}
-topology, the operator

(\gamma^{k})^{*}:\;H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V}){\>\longrightarrow\>}H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})

takes an open ball to a precompact set in the Banach space

H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})
. Therefore, the operator

(\gamma^{k})^{*}
is compact as a map of Banach spaces.

Step 2:

A function
f\in H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})

is called
\gamma^{k}
-finite
if
the sequence

\{f,(\gamma^{k})^{*}(f),(\gamma^{2k})^{*}(f),(\gamma^{3k})^{*}(f),...\}
belongs to a finite-dimensional
subspace of
H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})
(in the language used in

2.3
,
we would have to say
(\gamma^{k})^{*}
-finite
).
Let
R
be a compact injective operator
on a Banach space
B
. From
2.2

(see also
2.2
), it follows that

B
admits a Schauder basis
8
8

8

A
Schauder basis
in a Banach space
W

is a set
\{x_{i}\}
of vectors such that the closure of the
space generated by
x_{i}
is
W
, and the closure of the space generated
by all of
\{x_{i}\}
except one of them does not contain the last one.

such that the matrix for
R
in this basis
is a sum of finite-dimensional Jordan blocks. This implies
that the set of
\gamma^{k}
-finite vectors in
H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})

is dense (see also
2.3
).

Step 3:

Every
\gamma^{k}
-finite function
f
can be extended
to a holomorphic function on
V
. Indeed,
let
W_{f}
be the finite-dimensional subspace
of
H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})
generated by

\{f,(\gamma^{k})^{*}(f),(\gamma^{2k})^{*}(f),(\gamma^{3k})^{*}(f),...\}
. Since

(\gamma^{k})^{*}
takes
W_{f}
to itself, and
W_{f}
is finite-dimensional,
the map
(\gamma^{k})^{*}
is invertible on
W_{f}
.
However,
((\gamma^{k})^{*})^{-1}
takes functions
defined on
V_{0}
to functions defined on
\gamma^{-k}(V_{0})
,
hence any
w\in W_{f}
can be holomorphically extended to

\gamma^{-k}(V_{0})
. Repeating this argument, we extend

w\in W_{f}
from
V_{0}
to the sets

\gamma^{-mk}(V_{0})\supset\gamma^{(-m+1)k}(V_{0})\supset...\supset\gamma^{-2k}(V_{0})\supset\gamma^{-k}(V_{0})\supset V_{0}
.
Since
\gamma
is a contraction, we have

\bigcup_{m}\gamma^{-mk}(V_{0})=V
, hence all elements of
W_{f}

are holomorphically extended to
V
; this includes
f\in W_{f}
.

Proof. Step 4:

It is clear that every
\gamma
-finite function
f_{1}\in H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})

is
\gamma^{k}
-finite. Now we prove that, conversely,
every
\gamma^{k}
-finite function
f\in H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})

is in fact
\gamma
-finite. Indeed, let

W_{f}\subset H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})
be
the subspace generated by

\{f,(\gamma^{k})^{*}(f),(\gamma^{2k})^{*}(f),(\gamma^{3k})^{*}(f),...\}
, and
W^{\prime}_{f}\supset W_{f}
the subspace
generated by
\{f,(\gamma)^{*}(f),(\gamma^{2})^{*}(f),(\gamma^{3})^{*}(f),...\}
.

Until the rest of this step, we
use
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}
as a shorthand
for the subspace generated by the
vectors
\{z_{i}\}
.
Since
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\geqslant 0}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}\big[i+(k{\mathbb{Z}}^{\geqslant 0})\big],

the space

W^{\prime}_{f}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\gamma^{i})^{*}\sum_{l\geqslant 0}^{\infty}(\gamma^{lk})^{*}(f)

is equal to the subspace generated by the finite-dimensional
spaces
W_{f},\gamma^{*}(W_{f}),...,(\gamma^{k-1})^{*}(W_{f})
,
hence it is also finite-dimensional.

Proof. Step 5:

Since the space
V
is Stein, it admits
a closed holomorphic embedding to
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
In other words, there exists a

d
-dimensional subspace
A\subset H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})

such that the corresponding map
V{\>\longrightarrow\>}{\mathbb{C}}^{d}

is a closed embedding.
Since the space of
(\gamma^{k})
-finite functions is dense in

H^{0}_{b}(V_{0},{\cal O}_{V})
, the space of
\gamma
-finite functions
is dense in
H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})
with
C^{0}
-topology. Let
P
be a
precompact fundamental domain for the action of
\langle\gamma\rangle
on
V
.
Since
H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})^{\gamma}
is
C^{0}
-dense in
H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})
,
there exists a finite-dimensional
space
A_{1}={\mathbb{C}}^{d}
of
\gamma
-finite functions such that the
corresponding map
P{\>\longrightarrow\>}{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is an embedding.
Let
W\subset H^{0}(V,{\cal O}_{V})
be the smallest

\gamma^{*}
-invariant subspace generated by
A_{1}
;
since all vectors in
A_{1}
are
\gamma
-finite,
the space
W
is finite-dimensional.
This makes the following diagram commutative

\begin{CD}V@>{\Psi}>{}>W\\
@V{\gamma}V{}V@V{}V{{\gamma^{*}}}V\\
V@>{\Psi}>{}>W\end{CD}

were
\Psi:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}W
is a closed embedding
of
V
to
W={\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. Removing the zero and taking
the quotient over
\gamma
, respectively
\gamma^{*}
, this gives a
closed holomorphic embedding

\frac{V\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}\hookrightarrow\frac{W\backslash 0}{\langle\gamma^{*}\rangle}
,
where
\frac{W\backslash 0}{\langle\gamma^{*}\rangle}
is by construction
a linear Hopf manifold.

Corollary 3.2:

Let
V
be a Stein variety, and
\gamma:\;V{\>\longrightarrow\>}V

an invertible holomorphic contraction centered in
x
.
Assume that the group
\langle\gamma\rangle
acts on
V\backslash x
properly
discontinuously, and
V\backslash x
is smooth. Then the quotient manifold

\frac{V\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}
admits
an LCK metric with potential.

Proof:
By
3
,

\frac{V\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}
admits
a holomorphic embedding to a linear Hopf manifold
H
.
By
[
OV4
, Section 2.5]
,

H
admits an LCK metric with potential.
Any closed complex submanifold of an LCK manifold with potential
is again LCK with potential, hence

\frac{V\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

is LCK with potential.

4
Non-linear Hopf manifolds are LCK

Definition 4.1:

Let
\gamma:\;{\mathbb{C}}^{n}{\>\longrightarrow\>}{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be an invertible holomorphic
contraction centered in 0. Then the quotient

\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

is called
a Hopf manifold
.

Remark 4.2:
A Hopf manifold is diffeomorphic to
S^{1}\times S^{2n-1}
.
In particular, it is never Kähler.

The following corollary is directly implied
by
3
.

Corollary 4.3:

Let
H
be a Hopf manifold. Then
H
admits an LCK
metric with potential.

Remark 4.4:
Most of the results about the LCK manifolds with potential
rely upon the Andreotti-Siu and Rossi theorem used to construct
the Stein completion of the Kähler
{\mathbb{Z}}
-cover. This is why we
restrict most of the statements to manifolds of dimension
\geqslant 3
.
However, a Hopf manifold
H
is covered by
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
,
and this manifold already has the Stein completion
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Therefore, in the results about the Hopf manifolds
we don’t need the restriction
\operatorname{\sf dim}_{\mathbb{C}}H\geqslant 3
.
