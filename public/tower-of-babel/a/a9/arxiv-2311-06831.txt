Abstract
Latent variable models are widely used to account for unobserved determinants of economic behavior. This paper introduces a quasi-Bayes approach to nonparametrically estimate a large class of latent variable models. As an application, we model U.S. individual log earnings from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) as the sum of latent permanent and transitory components. Simulations illustrate the favorable performance of quasi-Bayes estimators relative to common alternatives. Keywords: Latent variables, quasi-Bayes, nonparametric Bayes, deconvolution, wasserstein distance, earnings dynamics



1

Introduction

A large class of models explicitly account for the influence of latent unobservables on observed economic behavior. These models are constructed to be informative at the population in that the distribution of observables uniquely identifies the latent distribution of interest. However, when the link between observables and latent heterogeneity is tenuous, the finite sample identifying strength can be very weak. As a consequence, classical nonparametric estimators often display several undesirable properties, such as large finite-sample variability, irregularity and extreme sensitivity to small perturbations of the data and user-selected tuning parameters.

In empirical settings, it is common practice to impose strong distributional restrictions on the unobservables.
1
1

1

Gaussian restrictions are particularly common, see
Storesletten et al. 2004
;
Agostinelli and Wiswall 2016
;
Attanasio et al. 2020a
;
Attanasio et al. 2020b
, among many others.
Intuitively, these restrictions limit the model complexity, and thereby require weaker information content from the data. These approaches are attractive in that they significantly reduce finite sample uncertainty and provide a researcher with a tractable distribution (e.g. Gaussian) to use for counterfactual analysis. On the downside, they often introduce significant misspecification bias if the restrictions are incorrect. This bias is particularly evident in the descriptive evidence and estimates of non-Gaussian models (e.g.
Bonhomme and Robin 2010
;
Guvenen et al. 2014
;
Arellano et al. 2017
).

Motivated by these concerns, this paper proposes a class of nonparametric estimators that arise as solutions to quasi-Bayesian decision rules. Traditional quasi-Bayes (
Kim 2002
;
Chernozhukov and Hong 2003
) combines a GMM objective function
Q_{n}(\theta)
and a prior
\nu(\theta)
for a finite dimensional parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to create a quasi-posterior distribution:

\displaystyle\nu(\theta\>|\>\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto e^{-Q_{n}(\theta)}d\nu(\theta).

(1)

In this paper, we generalize the representation in (
1
) to settings where the parameter is an infinite dimensional latent distribution of interest. We maintain the classical quasi-Bayes interpretation in that we construct
Q_{n}(.)
to be a quasi-likelihood that only depends on the observables through a collection of identifying population moments. In this setup,
Q_{n}(.)
can be viewed as a generalized GMM objective and
\nu(.)
is a nonparametric prior on distributions. As is standard, the effect of the prior is negligible in settings with strong identification. However, in settings with weakly informative data and a near-flat objective function
Q_{n}(.)
, the prior offers significant regularization and provides a researcher with the flexibility to incorporate information (e.g. moment and shape restrictions) to improve the finite sample information content.

We propose a theoretically motivated class of priors that are supported on nonparametric Gaussian mixtures. Our focus on this class is partially motivated from the observation that finite Gaussian mixtures are widely used to model rich forms of heterogeneity. As Gaussian mixtures can be efficiently sampled from, they provide a researcher with a convenient framework to perform counterfactual analysis. One consequence of building a framework around this structure is that we also obtain decision rules with particularly desirable theoretical guarantees in settings where the true data generating process closely resembles a (possibly infinite) Gaussian mixture, while remaining viable in the broader fully nonparametric setting. As we illustrate in our analysis, this choice is also computationally attractive in that it greatly facilitates the interplay analysis between the identifying moments and the prior.

The main theoretical contributions of this paper are as follows. We provide a unified treatment of quasi-Bayes for a large class of nonparametric latent variable models. This includes all models in which characteristic function based restrictions are employed to identify the latent distribution. As we illustrate in Section
2
, this encompasses a large class of commonly used latent variable models in economics. In each model, we take these identifying restrictions as given and use them to build a moment-based quasi-likelihood for the model. We then derive posterior contraction rates for the quasi-Bayes posterior in a variety of metrics (e.g. Wasserstein and
L^{2}
). As our work is the first quasi-Bayesian approach to nonparametric latent heterogeneity, we expect the analysis to be of independent interest and useful towards a wide range of related extensions.

As an application, we apply our methodology to model individual earnings data from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID), revisiting an earlier study by
Bonhomme and Robin 2010
. Our results in this setting are consistent with the literature (e.g.
Lilliard and Willis 1978
;
Arellano et al. 2017
) in that the distribution of U.S wage shocks appear to be non-Gaussian and leptokurtic. As our priors are supported on nonparametric Gaussian mixtures, our results also complement previous approaches (e.g.
Geweke and Keane 2000
) that advocate for Gaussian mixtures to model earnings dynamics. To assess the performance of our method, we compare the implied fit of the wage growth moments to the observed data. Overall, we find that our approach is capable of reproducing the higher moments observed in U.S wage growth data.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the quasi-Bayes framework, provides a detailed description of our procedures and outlines the class of models to which our methodology may apply. Section
3
provides the main assumptions states the main results. Section
4
verifies the main assumptions for several distinct latent variable models and further develops the main results in a model specific context. Section
5
provides simulation evidence on quasi-Bayes estimators relative to common alternatives. In Section
6
, we apply our methodology to study earnings dynamics in U.S wages, using data from the PSID.

Related Literature

Latent variable models have a long history in econometrics and statistics. In Section
2
, we provide an overview of several commonly used models in the literature. For a more comprehensive discussion, see
Chen et al. 2011
;
Schennach 2016
;
Schennach 2022
.

Bayesian and quasi-Bayesian methods are frequently used to estimate finite dimensional parameters that are strongly identified through moment conditions.
Chamberlain and Imbens 2003
use a Dirichlet process prior on the distribution of observables and consider the induced posterior distribution on a GMM parameter.
Chernozhukov and Hong 2003
develop the quasi-Bayes limit theory for parameters that are strongly identified through a finite set of moments.
Schennach 2005
;
Schennach 2007b
;
Chib et al. 2018
examine the Bayesian properties of an exponentially tilted empirical quasi-likelihood. For applications of quasi-Bayes to conditional moment models, see
Liao and Jiang 2011
;
Kato 2013
;
Kankanala 2023
.

Our work contributes to a growing literature on Bayesian inference in density estimation and deconvolution. For Bayesian approaches to density estimation, see
Ghosal et al. 2000
;
Ghosal and Van Der Vaart 2001
;
Ghosal and Van Der Vaart 2007
;
Shen et al. 2013
;
Kruijer et al. 2010
, among many others. For Bayesian estimation of a conditional density, see
Norets 2010
;
Norets and Pelenis 2014
;
Norets and Pelenis 2012
;
Norets and Pati 2017
. For Bayesian estimation in deconvolution with a known error distribution, see
Donnet et al. 2018
;
Rousseau and Scricciolo 2024
. To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to propose a quasi-Bayes framework to estimate a density and latent distribution.

Quasi-Bayesian methods are also used in parametric models with weak or non-standard identification.
Chen et al. 2018
;
Andrews and Mikusheva 2022
establish several desirable properties of quasi-Bayesian decision rules in these settings. As the finite sample information content in latent variable models is usually limited, we view our work as complementary to this line of research and particularly informative for the potential of quasi-Bayesian decision rules in the broader class of weakly identified nonparametric models.

Models and Procedures

2.1

Framework

Let
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
denote a vector of observables. We are interested in the distribution of a latent unobservable
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that influences
Y
through a known economic model. In many natural settings, identification of the latent distribution is established by verifying that the latent characteristic function
\varphi_{X}(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}X}]
, or its derivatives, can be identified through suitable population moments of
Y
. To fix ideas, suppose
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{q})\times C(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow C(\mathbb{R}^{v})
is a known operator. Then, the latent distribution is assumed to satisfy a restriction of the form

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\mathbf{0}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{R}^{v}.

(2)

The argument of
\mathcal{G}(.)
consists of two inputs, a probability distribution
P
on
Y
and a characteristic function
\varphi_{F}
. In most cases, we can view (
2
) as a generalized moment restriction in that the operator
\mathcal{G}(.)
only depends on
P
through a collection of moments on
Y
. We say the restriction is an identifying restriction if

F\neq F_{X}\implies\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F})\neq\mathbf{0}.

In this paper, we focus on a general class of latent variable models for which a generalized moment restriction of the form in (
2
) is known to hold. As the following examples illustrate, this encompasses a large class of commonly used models in economics. Furthermore, the map
\varphi_{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F})
is usually an elementary transformation of
\varphi_{F}
or its derivatives.

Example 1

(Classical Measurement Error)
.

Consider a classical measurement error model where we observe a random sample from

\displaystyle W=X+\epsilon\;\;\;\;,\;\;\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0.

(3)

Here,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an observed vector,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a latent vector whose distribution is of interest, and
\epsilon
is a nuisance error that is independent of
X
. Since the individual contributions of
X
and
\epsilon
cannot be separately identified from an observation of
W
, identification of
F_{X}
typically requires some auxiliary information about the distribution
F_{\epsilon}
. In statistics, it is common to assume that
F_{\epsilon}
is fully known. For instance, in the classical empirical Bayes framework
(
Efron 2016
;
Gu and Koenker 2023
)
,
\epsilon
is modeled as a known Gaussian distribution. In settings with differential privacy,
\epsilon
is a known Laplace distribution (
Agarwal and Singh 2021
;
Rousseau and Scricciolo 2024
).

As is common in the literature (e.g.
Kato and Sasaki 2018
;
Arellano and Bonhomme 2023
), we consider the weaker setting where a researcher has auxiliary information in the form of a random sample
\epsilon_{1}^{\star},\dots,\epsilon_{n}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}F_{\epsilon}
. If the nuisance error
\epsilon
is independent of
X
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}W}]=\varphi_{X}(t)\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\epsilon}]\;\;\;\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We view
Y=(W,\epsilon^{\star})
as the observable, and the restriction map is defined as:

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}W}]-\varphi_{X}(t)\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\epsilon}].

In some settings (e.g.
Kline et al. 2024
), the errors may exhibit heterogeneity, such that we have independent, but not necessarily identically distributed, errors
\epsilon_{i}\sim F_{i}
for observations
i=1,\dots,n
. In this case, the restriction map is

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\bigg(n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}W_{j}}\big]\bigg)-\varphi_{X}(t)\bigg(n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\epsilon_{j}}\big]\bigg).

Example 2

(Repeated Measurements)
.

Suppose we observe measurements
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
from the model:

\displaystyle Y_{1}=X+\epsilon_{1}\;,\;\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{1}]=0,

(4)

\displaystyle Y_{2}=X+\epsilon_{2}\;,\;\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{2}]=0.

Here,
X\in\mathbb{R}
is a latent variable of interest, and
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
represent unobserved nuisance errors. In this setup,
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
are interpreted as imperfect or noisy proxies for
X
. For example, in
Cunha et al. 2010
,
X
represents latent child ability, while
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
denote test scores in different subjects.

If the nuisance errors satisfy
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{1}|X,\epsilon_{2}]=0
and
\epsilon_{2}\perp X
, it is well known (
Li and Vuong 1998
;
Cunha et al. 2010
) that the latent density admits the representation

\displaystyle f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\mathbf{i}tx}\exp\bigg( \int_{0}^t \frac{\E[\mathbf{i} Y_1 e^{\mathbf{i} \zeta Y_2} ]}{\E[ e^{\mathbf{i} \zeta Y_2} ]} d \zeta\bigg)dt.

(5)

From the representation in (
5
), it follows that the latent distribution satisfies

\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}tY_{2}}]\partial_{t}\log\varphi_{X}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{i}Y_{1}e^{\mathbf{i}tY_{2}}]\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\forall\>t\in\mathbb{R}.

(6)

In this case, we view
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})
as the observable and the restriction map is

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}tY_{2}}]\partial_{t}\log\varphi_{X}(t)-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{i}Y_{1}e^{\mathbf{i}tY_{2}}].

The identifying restrictions in (
6
) are always valid, even when
X
does not admit a density. Beyond applications to labor economics, repeated measurements and variants of the identity in (
5
) are also used to identify and estimate unobserved heterogeneity in auctions (e.g.
Asker 2010
;
Krasnokutskaya 2011
).

Example 3

(Random Coefficient Models)
.

Consider the classical random coefficient model

\displaystyle W=Z^{\prime}\beta

(7)

where
W\in\mathbb{R}
is an observed scalar response,
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of observed covariates and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of random unobserved coefficients that are independent from
X
. We are interested in recovering the latent distribution of
\beta
. It is shown in
Hoderlein et al. 2010
that the distribution of
\beta
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}tW}|Z=z]=\varphi_{\beta}(tz)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\forall\;\;t\in\mathbb{R}.

We view
Y=(W,Z)
as the observable and the restriction map is given by

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{\beta})(t,z)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}tW}|Z=z]-\varphi_{\beta}(tz).

Similar restrictions have been derived to identify and estimate the distribution of random coefficients in discrete choice
(
Gautier and Kitamura 2013
)
and simultaneous equation models
(
Masten 2018
;
Hoderlein et al. 2017
)
.

Example 4

(Multi-Factor Models)
.

Consider the linear multi-factor model

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}

(8)

where
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{L})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
is a vector of
L
measurements,
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{K})^{\prime}
is a vector of
K
latent and and mutually independent factors and
\mathbf{A}
is a known (or estimable)
L\times K
matrix of factor loadings. Without loss of generality, we assume the factors are demeaned, i.e
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]=\mathbf{0}
. In
Bonhomme and Robin 2010
, it is shown that each latent distribution
F_{X_{k}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\log\varphi_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathbf{A}_{k}^{\prime}(\log\varphi_{X_{k}})^{\prime\prime}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k})\;\;\;\;\;\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\;,

(9)

where
\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\log\varphi_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
denotes the Hessian of the map
t\rightarrow\log\varphi_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
denote the vector of upper triangular elements of
\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\log\varphi_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{X}}(t)
denote the vector with elements
\{(\log\varphi_{X_{i}})^{\prime\prime}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{K}
. Let
V(\mathbf{A}_{k})
denote the vector of upper triangular elements of

\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathbf{A}_{k}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{Q}=[V(\mathbf{A}_{1}),\dots,V(\mathbf{A}_{K})]
the matrix with columns composed of those vectors. As the matrices in (
9
) are symmetric, the identifying restrictions may be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)=\mathbf{Q}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{X}}(t)

Fix any
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and let
X_{k}
denote the random variable whose distribution
F_{X_{k}}
is of interest. If
\mathbf{Q}^{*}=(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{k}^{*}
denotes the
k^{th}
row of
\mathbf{Q}^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle(\log\varphi_{X_{k}})^{\prime\prime}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k})=\mathbf{Q}_{k}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)\;\;\;\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{R}^{L}.

If the characteristic function of
\mathbf{Y}
is non vanishing, a suitable functional restriction is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X_{k}})(t)=\varphi_{\mathbf{Y}}^{2}(t)\big[\mathbf{Q}_{k}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)-(\log\varphi_{X_{k}})^{\prime\prime}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k})].

Remark 1

(On Nuisance Factors)
.

As shown in previous examples, the latent distribution of interest can often be constructively identified through a generalized moment restriction on the observables. These restrictions are particularly appealing for estimation because they do not require the researcher to explicitly model the distribution of the nuisance unobservables, which may be very complex.

The preceding examples and their extensions encompass a broad class of commonly used latent variable models in economics. We note that this list is far from exhaustive. Similar characteristic moment-based identifying restrictions arise in various other contexts, such as nonlinear error-in-variables regression
(
Hausman et al. 1991
;
Schennach 2004
;
Schennach 2007a
)
, nonparametric panel data (
Arellano and Bonhomme 2012
;
Wilhelm 2015
;
Botosaru and Sasaki 2018
) and workhorse models for consumption and earnings dynamics
(
Arellano et al. 2017
;
Arellano et al. 2024
)
.

In nearly all the settings described above, the standard approach to estimating the latent distribution involves inverting the identifying restrictions. This typically entails solving a finite-sample analog of

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})=\mathbf{0}

to obtain an estimate
\widehat{\varphi}_{X}
. The estimated latent density
\widehat{f}_{X}
is then constructed using an inverse Fourier transform.
2
2

2

For applications of this approach to specific models, see
Horowitz and Markatou 1996
;
Bonhomme and Robin 2010
;
Krasnokutskaya 2011
;
Gautier and Kitamura 2013
;
Masten 2018
, among many others.
As noted in the literature (e.g.
Efron 2016
;
Arellano and Bonhomme 2023
), these solutions often exhibit significant finite sample variability and are highly sensitive to small perturbations in the data and user-selected tuning parameters. Intuitively, the large finite sample variability of these estimators arises from their representation as an inverse of an ill-posed objective function over an excessively large parameter space.
3
3

3

A standard deconvolution estimator allows for any square integrable function- the effective parameter space is
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As a consequence, the estimator
\widehat{f}_{X}
is often negative on a subset of the domain and
\int\widehat{f}_{X}(t)dt\neq 1
.

Two natural questions arise from the preceding discussion:
(i)
Can the information content in the moments be utilized more efficiently for the class of nonparametric latent variable models described above?
(ii)
Is the identifying strength in the moments significantly amplified in settings where some regularity is imposed on the parameter space? To address these questions, this paper proposes and examines a class of nonparametric estimators that arise as solutions to quasi-Bayesian decision rules.

In parametric models, where a finite-dimensional parameter
\theta
is identified through finite moment restrictions, the classical quasi-Bayes approach (e.g.,
Chernozhukov and Hong 2003
) treats a monotonic transformation of the GMM objective function as a likelihood for
\theta
. In our setting, we view the latent distribution
F_{X}
as an infinite-dimensional parameter of interest. The identifying restriction
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{\mathbf{X}})=\mathbf{0}
then motivates a quasi-Bayes likelihood of the form

L^{\star}(F)=\exp\bigg( - \frac{n}{2} \| \mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_Y, \varphi_{F}) \|_{L^2} \bigg).

While ideal, this choice is infeasible as the true distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
is unknown and must be estimated from the data. In this paper, we proceed by replacing
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
with the empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n,Y}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
. As the preceding examples illustrate, the explicit dependence of
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F})
on
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
is usually through an elementary transformation of
\varphi_{Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}Y}]
. In particular, the quantity
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{F})(t)
will typically depend on
\widehat{\varphi}_{Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{n}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}Y}]
and as a consequence, it cannot be consistently estimated uniformly over
t\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
4
4

4

Under standard regularity conditions, we have
\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\widehat{\varphi}_{Y}(t)-\varphi_{Y}(t)\right|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log T})
.
To control the finite sample uncertainty, we follow the usual approach and consider a bounded set of restrictions that grows with the sample size
n
. Specifically, given any fixed
T>0
, we denote the ball of radius
T
and the
L^{2}
magnitude (norm) of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
over the ball by

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(T)=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T\}\;\;,\;\;\;\|f\|_{\mathbb{B}(T)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{B}(T)}\|f(t)\|^{2}dt.

(10)

Denote the observed data by
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
. Given a deterministic, or possibly data driven, sequence of constants
T_{n}\uparrow\infty
that grows with the sample size
n
, we define the characteristic likelihood by

L(F)=\exp\bigg( - \frac{n}{2} \| \mathcal{G}( \mathbb{P}_{n,Y}, \varphi_{F}) \|_{\mathbb{B}(T_n)}^2 \bigg).

(11)

When combined with a (possibly data dependent) prior
\nu
that is supported on a set of distributions
\mathcal{F}
, we obtain the quasi-Bayes posterior:

\displaystyle\nu(F\>|\>\mathcal{D}_{n})=\frac{\exp\big( - \frac{n}{2} \| \mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y}, \varphi_{F}) \|_{\mathbb{B}(T_n)}^2 \big)d\nu(F)}{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\exp\big( - \frac{n}{2} \| \mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y}, \varphi_{F}) \|_{\mathbb{B}(T_n)}^2 \big)d\nu(F)}.

(12)

2.2

Classes of Priors

In principle, many distinct families of priors are suitable to construct the quasi-Bayes posterior in (
12
). In Section
3
, we provide general results that are applicable to a wide class of priors. However, in the interest of sampling efficiently from the quasi-posterior and using the samples in a tractable form, it is convenient to focus on priors that satisfy two basic properties. First, they have a known and tractable characteristic function. This ensures that the quasi-likelihood in (
11
) and suitable derivatives of it can be easily evaluated. Second, the prior is supported on distributions that are tractable to sample from. This property is convenient for any post estimation analysis, especially if the overall goal is to estimate many complicated nonlinear functionals of the latent distribution. In the remainder of this section, we describe a class of priors that satisfy both of these requirements.

2.2.1

Dirichlet Process Gaussian Mixtures

Given a covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, denote by
\phi_{\Sigma}
the
N(\mathbf{0},\Sigma)
density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given a probability distribution
P\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we denote the Gaussian mixture with mixing distribution
P
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
by

\displaystyle\phi_{P,\Sigma}(x)=\phi_{\Sigma}\star P(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\Sigma}(x-z)dP(z).

(13)

If
P
is a discrete distribution, i.e
P=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p_{j}\delta_{\mu_{j}}
which assigns probability mass
p_{j}
to a point
\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the preceding definition reduces to

\displaystyle\phi_{P,\Sigma}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p_{j}\phi_{\Sigma}(x-\mu_{j}).

Let
\varphi_{F}(t)=\int e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}dF(x)
denotes the characteristic function (CF) of a distribution
F
. If
F=\phi_{P,\Sigma}
, we denote it by
\varphi_{P,\Sigma}
. By elementary properties of the CF, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{P,\Sigma}(t)=\varphi_{\phi_{\Sigma}\star P}(t)=\varphi_{\phi_{\Sigma}}(t)\times\varphi_{P}(t)=e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t/2}\varphi_{F}(t).

(14)

For a discrete distribution
P=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p_{j}\delta_{\mu_{j}}
, the preceding expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\varphi_{P,\Sigma}(t)=e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t/2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p_{j}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu_{j}}.

(15)

A Gaussian mixture is completely characterized by its mixing distribution
P
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. In particular, a prior on
(P,\Sigma)
is equivalent to placing a prior on infinite Gaussian mixtures. For a prior on
G
, a common choice is the Inverse-Wishart distribution. In Section
4
, we show that a large class of covariance priors can be accommodated for the models considered in this paper. For the mixing distribution
P
, we focus on the canonical choice of a prior for a distribution, the Dirichlet process.

Definition 1
.

A random distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a Dirichlet process distribution with base measure
\alpha
if for every finite measurable partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle(P(A_{1}),P(A_{2}),\dots,P(A_{k}))\sim\text{Dir}(k,\alpha(A_{1}),\dots,\alpha(A_{k}))\>,

(16)

where
\text{Dir}(.)
is the Dirichlet distribution with parameters
k
and
\alpha_{1}(A_{1}),\dots,\alpha_{k}(A_{k})>0
on the
k
dimensional unit simplex
\Delta=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:x_{i}\geq 0\;,\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}=1\}
.
5
5

5

The probability density of
\text{Dir}(k,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
, with respect to the
k-1
dimensional Lebesgue measure on the unit simplex
\Delta
, is proportional to
\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}
.

An alternative definition that is especially useful in simulating the process is the following.

Definition 2

(Dirichlet process)
.

Fix
\beta>0
and a base probability distribution
\alpha
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
V_{1},V_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}\text{Beta}(1,\beta)
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}\alpha
. Define the weights

\displaystyle p_{1}=V_{1}\;\;,\;p_{j}=V_{j}\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(1-V_{i})\;\;\;\;\;\;\;j\geq 2.

It can be shown
(
Ghosal and Van Der Vaart 2017
, Lemma 3.4)
that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}=1
almost surely. The DP with concentration parameter
\beta
and base measure
\alpha
is the law of the discrete measure:

P=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}}\>,

where
\delta_{\mu_{i}}
is the point mass at
\mu_{i}
. That is,
P
takes value
\mu_{i}
with probability
p_{i}
. The parameter
\beta
controls the spread around the base measure
\alpha
, with larger values of
\beta
leading to tighter concentration. Notationally, we suppress the dependence on
\beta
and express this as
P\sim\text{DP}_{\alpha}
.

A Dirichlet process prior can be used to build a prior on Gaussian mixtures in (
13
). Specifically, given a Dirichlet process prior
\text{DP}_{\alpha}
and an independent prior
G
on the set
\mathbf{S}_{+}^{d}
of positive definite matrices, the induced prior on Gaussian mixtures is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{P,\Sigma}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\Sigma}(x-z)dP(z)\;,\;\;\;(P,\Sigma)\sim\text{DP}_{\alpha}\otimes G.

(17)

An advantage of focusing on Gaussian mixture-based priors is that they greatly facilitate the interplay analysis between the prior and the quasi-likelihood. As shown in (
15
), Gaussian mixtures admit a simple characteristic function, which in turn leads to a tractable quasi-likelihood. Importantly, priors on Gaussian mixtures translate seamlessly to priors on characteristic functions and vice versa.

In the remaining sections, we will frequently refer to the prior in (
17
). For ease of notation, we denote this product prior by
\nu_{\alpha,G}=\text{DP}_{\alpha}\otimes G
.

Theory

We begin by outlining the main regularity conditions in Section
3.1
. In Section
3.2
, we present and discuss the main results for the general quasi-Bayes posterior in (
12
). Given that our framework accommodates a wide range of models and priors, it is necessary to state our conditions with some degree of generality. In Section
4
, we verify these assumptions for the models in Examples
1
–
4
using low level conditions and discuss their main implications in detail.

3.1

Assumptions

We state and discuss the assumptions that we impose on the model and prior. Throughout this section, let
\nu_{n}
denote a, possibly data dependent, prior that is supported on a set of distributions
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
T_{n}\uparrow\infty
denote a deterministic sequence of positive constants. Let
(\epsilon_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
denote a deterministic sequence of positive constants that converge to zero at a slower than parametric rate :
\epsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
and
n\epsilon_{n}^{2}\uparrow\infty
.

Assumption 1

(Sampling Uncertainty)
.

For some set
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n}
and a universal constant
D>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{F\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{F})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}>D\epsilon_{n}\bigg)\rightarrow 0.

Assumption
1
provides bounds on the sampling uncertainty that arises from the true population distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
being unknown. Typically,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
represents a ball (in an suitable metric) centered around a fixed distribution
F_{n}
. In certain settings, such as Examples
2
and
4
,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
may also include additional constraints (e.g. moment bounds) on
F
, which help in controling the sampling uncertainty.

Assumption 2

(Weak Bias)
.

For some probability measure
F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and a universal constant
D>0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F_{n}})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}\leq D\epsilon_{n}

Assumption
2
imposes bounds on the bias between the latent distribution
F_{X}
and a distribution
F_{n}
within the support of the prior. The distribution
F_{n}
can be interpreted as one that minimizes the distance to
F_{X}
with respect to the objective function induced weak metric
d_{\mathcal{G}}(F,F^{\prime})=\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F^{\prime}})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}
. In some settings, it is natural to set
F_{n}=F_{X}
when the latent distribution
F_{X}
shares structural similarities with the prior family. An important setting where this arises is when a researcher models the latent distribution as a nonparametric Gaussian mixture. In this case, there exists a positive definite matrix
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and a mixing distribution
F_{0}
such that the density of
X
can be expressed as

\displaystyle f_{X}(x)=\phi_{\Sigma_{0}}\star F_{0}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\Sigma_{0}}(x-z)dF_{0}(z).

(18)

If the prior is supported on a sufficiently rich subset of Gaussian mixtures (e.g., the DP-GM prior discussed in Section
2.2.1
), the bias is typically negligible at
f_{X}
.

Assumption 3

(Local Concentration)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
F_{n}
be as in Assumption
1
and
2
. For some set
\mathcal{R}_{n}\supseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle(i)\;\;\;\;\;\;\nu_{n}(F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}:\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{F_{n}})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}\leq D\epsilon_{n})\geq c\exp(- C' n \epsilon_n^2)

\displaystyle(ii)\;\;\;\;\;\nu_{n}(\mathcal{R}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n})\leq C\exp( - B_n )

where
c,C,C^{\prime},D>0
are universal constants and
B_{n}\uparrow\infty
is any sequence of constants which satisfies
n\epsilon_{n}^{2}=o(B_{n})
.

The set
\mathcal{R}_{n}
in Assumption
3
is introduced to provide some flexibility when direct verification of a local concentration bound is challenging for the
\mathcal{S}_{n}
in Assumption
1
. In such cases,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
relaxes certain restrictions (e.g. moment bounds) imposed on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Assumption
3
(ii)
further requires that the subset of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
where these restrictions fail to hold is sufficiently negligible. Typically,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
is a ball around a fixed distribution
F_{n}
that is close to
F_{X}
(in a suitable metric). Assumption
3
(i)
then imposes a standard small-ball local concentration condition on the prior.

3.2

Results

In this section, we verify that the quasi-Bayes posterior in (
12
) asymptotically concentrates on local neighborhoods of the latent distribution.

Theorem 1

(Weak Contraction)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
3
hold with a sequence
\epsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
. Then, there exists a universal constant
L>0
such that

\nu_{n}\bigg(F:\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{F})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}>L\epsilon_{n}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

(19)

Theorem
1
establishes contraction with the respect to the objective function weak metric
d_{\mathcal{G}}^{w}(F,F_{X})=\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{F})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}
. The interpretation of this convergence varies from model to model, but in general, it is meant to be interpreted as a preliminary contraction that can then be subsequently used to deduce results in a stronger metric. In particular, if (
19
) holds and the bulk of the posterior mass is contained in a well-behaved subset, it is often possible to deduce results in a stronger metric like
d_{\mathcal{K}}(F,F_{X})=\sup_{x}\left|F(x)-F_{X}(x)\right|
or
d_{2}(F,F_{X})=\|f-f_{X}\|_{L^{2}}
. To fix ideas, given a metric
d(.)
and a class of distributions
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, we define the modulus of continuity by

\omega_{n}(d,\mathcal{H}_{n},\epsilon)=\sup\{d(F,F_{X}):F\in\mathcal{H}_{n},\>\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{F})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}\leq\epsilon\}.

(20)

The following result is a straightforward consequence of Theorem
1
.

Corollary 1

(Contraction)
.

Suppose the hypothesis of Theorem
1
holds. Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}
be any subset of distributions for which

\nu_{n}(F\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}:\|\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{F})-\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}\leq L\epsilon_{n})\leq C\exp(-D' n \epsilon_n^2)

holds for some
C>0
and a sufficiently large
D^{\prime}>0
. Then

\nu_{n}\bigg(F:d(F,F_{X})>\omega_{n}(d,\mathcal{H}_{n},L\epsilon_{n})\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

(21)

Corollary
1
provides contraction rates in terms of the modulus
\omega_{n}(d,\mathcal{H}_{n},L\epsilon_{n})
. The constant
D^{\prime}
, which regulates the decay of mass on
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{c}
, is required to be larger than some of the preceding constants that appear in Assumption
1
-
3
. Usually, the set
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is chosen as a function of
D^{\prime}
so as to ensure the desired bound holds trivially.

4

Applications

In this section, we verify the main assumptions outlined in Section
3
and explore their implications for various latent variable models. In all cases, we focus on the DP-GM prior discussed in Section
2.2.1
. In this case, the prior is supported on infinite Gaussian mixtures through the mixture representation

\displaystyle\phi_{P,\Sigma}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\Sigma}(x-z)dP(z)\;,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;(P,\Sigma)\sim\text{DP}_{\alpha}\otimes G.

(22)

This induces a prior
\nu
on the space of probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For ease of notation, we will reference the induced prior using the same notation as above, i.e
\nu=\text{DP}_{\alpha}\otimes G
. Before proceeding further, we state and discuss the main assumptions that we impose on the Dirichlet process base measure
\alpha
and covariance prior
G
.

Assumption 4

(Dirichlet Prior)
.

The Dirichlet process
\text{DP}_{\alpha}
, in the sense of Definition
2
, arises from a finite Gaussian mixture base measure
\alpha
. That is,
\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j}^{*},\Sigma_{j}^{*})
for some
K\in\mathbb{N}
, mean vectors
(\mu_{i}^{*})_{i=1}^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and positive definite matrices
(\Sigma_{i}^{*})_{i=1}^{K}\in\mathbf{S}_{+}^{d}
.

Assumption 5

(Covariance Prior)
.

(i)

G
is a probability measure with support contained in the space of positive
semi-definite matrices on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
(ii)
There exists
\kappa\in(0,1],v_{1}\geq 0,v_{2}\geq 0,v_{3}\geq 0
and universal constants
C,C^{\prime},D,D^{\prime},L^{\prime}>0
that satisfy

\displaystyle(i)\;\;\;\;\;G\big(\Sigma:\lambda_{d}(\Sigma^{-1})>t_{d}\big)\leq C\exp(- C' t_d^{\kappa})\;,

\displaystyle(ii)\;\;\;\;\;G\bigg(\Sigma:\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq d}\bigg\{t_{j}\leq\lambda_{j}(\Sigma^{-1})\leq t_{j}(1+\delta)\bigg\}\bigg)\geq Dt_{1}^{v_{1}}\delta^{v_{2}}\exp(-D' t_d^{\kappa})\;,

\displaystyle(iii)\;\;\;\;\;G(\Sigma:\lambda_{1}(\Sigma^{-1}<t_{1})\leq C\exp(-L' t_1^{-v_3} )

for every
\delta\in(0,1)
and
0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{d}<\infty
.

Since the researcher chooses the prior, Assumptions
4
and
5
can always be satisfied. Restricting
\alpha
to be a finite Gaussian mixture in Assumption
4
facilitates the computation as it is necessary to sample from the base measure. It is not to be interpreted as restrictive, as any distribution can be approximated arbitrarily well by a Gaussian mixture with sufficiently many components. Assumption
5

(i-ii)
allows for a large class of covariance priors and is commonly used in density estimation (
Shen et al. 2013
;
Ghosal and Van Der Vaart 2017
). In dimension
d=1
, it holds with
\kappa=1/2
if
G
is the distribution of the square of an inverse-Gamma distribution. In dimension
d>1
, it holds with
\kappa=1
if
G
is the inverse-Wishart distribution.
6
6

6

In higher dimensions, a computationally attractive choice is the class of Lewandowski-Kurowicka-Joe (LKJ) priors
(
Lewandowski et al. 2009
)
.
Assumption
5

(iii)
, for
v_{3}>0
, is equivalent to Assumption 4.2 in
Rousseau and Scricciolo 2024
. In
d=1
, it holds with a truncated inverse-Gamma prior.

In some cases, researchers may have prior estimates of the latent distribution from a preliminary parametric model, or certain features (e.g. moments) may be estimable using the observables. Similar to the analysis in
McAuliffe et al. 2006
, this can be accomodated through a data-dependent (empirical Bayes)
\text{DP}_{\alpha}
prior. For example, in the measurement error model (Example
1
), a reasonable choice is
\widehat{\alpha}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{E}_{n}(Y),\widehat{\text{Var}}(X))
. The following remark provides some clarification on the effect of data-dependent priors.

Remark 2

(On Empirical Bayes Priors)
.

In Assumption
4
, the base measure
\alpha
can be data dependent. All our main results remain valid with a data dependent prior, provided that, asymptotically in probability,
\|\widehat{\mu}_{j}\|
is bounded, and the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}
are bounded away from zero and infinity. This is analogous to the treatment of empirical Bayes priors in pure Bayesian models (
Rousseau and Szabo 2017
;
Donnet et al. 2018
).

In the remainder of this section, we focus on specific results for the latent variable models introduced in Examples
1
-
4
. For each model, we examine the following questions in detail: (i) What are the minimal conditions on
F_{X}

that ensure quasi-Bayes consistency? (ii) How do convergence rates depend on the smoothness of

F_{X}
? (iii) Are rates faster for certain classes of distributions (e.g. infinite Gaussian mixtures), and what is the interplay with the model ill-posedness?

4.1

Measurement Error

Consider the classical measurement error model, introduced in Example
1
, where we observe a random sample from

\displaystyle W=X+\epsilon\;\;\;\;,\;\;\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0.

(23)

In this setting,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the observed vector,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an unobserved vector, and
\epsilon
is a nuisance error that is
independent of
X
. We are interested in recovering the latent distribution of
X
. As the individual contributions of
X
and
\epsilon
cannot be separately identified from an observation of
W
, identification of the latent distribution of
X
generally requires some further auxiliary information on the distribution
F_{\epsilon}
of the unobserved error
\epsilon
. As in
Kato and Sasaki 2018
;
Arellano and Bonhomme 2023
, we consider the setting where a researcher has auxiliary information in the form of a random sample
{\epsilon}_{1}^{\star},\dots,{\epsilon}_{n}^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}F_{\epsilon}
.
7
7

7

If the auxiliary sample has size
m_{n}=o(n)
, the results remain valid, albeit with a possibly slower rate of convergence. There are no restrictions on the dependence of the auxiliary sample with the original sample.
As a basic premise, we impose the following low level condition on the observable
Y=(W,\epsilon^{\star})
.

Condition 4.1

(Data)
.

(i)

(W_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random vectors.
(ii)

(\epsilon_{i}^{\star})_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}F_{\epsilon}
where
F_{\epsilon}
is the distribution of the unknown error
\epsilon
in (
23
).
(iii)
The distributions have finite second moments:
\mathbb{E}\big(\|Y\|^{2}\big)<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\big(\|\epsilon\|^{2}\big)<\infty
.

We now proceed with stating the main results for the model in detail. From the discussion in Example
1
, the population characteristic restriction is given by

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}W}]-\varphi_{X}(t)\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\epsilon}].

(24)

With
Y=(W,\epsilon^{\star})
as the observable, the feasible analog
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X})
, obtained by using the empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n,Y}
, is given by

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\mathbb{E}_{n}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}W}]-\varphi_{X}(t)\mathbb{E}_{n}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\epsilon}].

(25)

Our first result examines the limiting behavior of quasi-Bayes under minimal distributional assumptions on the data generating process. Specifically, we avoid imposing any strong distributional structure on
(X,\epsilon)
, instead relying solely on primitive moment and tail bounds. As these basic assumptions will not preclude distributions with irregular components (e.g. mass points), it is necessary to quantify the behavior through a metric that is well-defined for a broad class of probability measures. To that end, we make use of the Wasserstein metric:

\mathbf{W}_{1}(F,F^{\prime})=\inf\bigg\{\mathbb{E}\big(\|Z-Z^{\prime}\|\big):Z\sim F\>,\>Z^{\prime}\sim F^{\prime}\bigg\}.

(26)

Among other desirable properties, it is well known (see, e.g.,
Villani 2009
) that convergence in
\mathbf{W}_{1}(.)
implies convergence in distribution:
\mathbf{W}_{1}(F_{n},F_{X})\to 0\implies F_{n}\rightsquigarrow F_{X}.
The following result demonstrates that the quasi-Bayes posterior contracts to the latent distribution in Wasserstein distance.

Theorem 2

(Wasserstein Contraction)
.

Suppose
d=1
. Assume that
(i)

F_{X}(t\in\mathbb{R}:\left|t\right|>z)\leq C\exp(- C' z^{\chi})
for some
\chi,C,C^{\prime}>0
,
(ii)

\inf_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|\geq b\exp(-B T^{\zeta})
for some
b,B,\zeta>0
,
(iii)
Assumption
5
holds with
\kappa\leq 1/2
and some
v_{3}\geq 1
. Then, for any sequence
T_{n}=o(n^{1/5})
, there exists a universal constant
D>0
such that

\displaystyle\nu\bigg(F:\mathbf{W}_{1}(F,F_{X})>D\max\bigg\{\frac{1}{T_{n}},(\log n)^{-1/\zeta}\bigg\}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

In particular, for any
T_{n}
satisfying
(\log n)^{-1/\zeta}\lessapprox T_{n}=o(n^{1/5})
, we have

\displaystyle\nu\bigg(F:\mathbf{W}_{1}(F,F_{X})>D(\log n)^{-1/\zeta}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

The exponential lower bound on
\varsigma_{T}=\inf_{\|t\|\leq T}\left|\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|
allows for measurement errors with a rapidly decreasing characteristic function. For example, the lower bound is strictly attained at
\zeta=2
if
\epsilon
is a Gaussian distribution. Remarkably, the contraction in Theorem
2
occurs for any sequence
T_{n}=o(n^{1/5})
, independent of the measurement error
\epsilon
. This result can be extended to dimension
d>1
, although in such cases, additional restrictions are required on the growth rate of
T_{n}
. Finally, we remark that the logarithmically slow rate of convergence in Theorem
2
is common in deconvolution problems (see e.g.
Carroll and Hall 1988
). Indeed, without further regularity conditions on
(F_{X},F_{\epsilon})
, the rate in Theorem
2
cannot be improved—it is minimax optimal
(
Dedecker and Michel 2013
)
.

In the remainder of this section, we focus on establishing contraction rates in settings where the latent distribution or measurement error are known to be sufficiently regular, either through Sobolev type constraints or restrictions on the nonparametric functional form. An important example of such a setting is when the latent distribution
F_{X}
is a nonparametric Gaussian mixture:

\displaystyle f_{X}(x)=\phi_{\Sigma_{0}}\star F_{0}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\Sigma_{0}}(x-z)dF_{0}(z).

(27)

Here,
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbf{S}_{+}^{d}
is a positive definite matrix and
F_{0}
is a mixing distribution. If
F_{0}
is a discrete distribution with finite support, this reduces to a Gaussian mixture with finitely many components. Our focus on this class is partially motivated by the observation that finite Gaussian mixtures are widely used to model complex heterogeneity in latent variable models (e.g.
Geweke and Keane 2000
;
Cunha et al. 2010
;
Attanasio et al. 2020b
). Importantly, if the mixing distribution
F_{0}
is continuously distributed or has unbounded support, the specification in (
27
) allows for Gaussian mixtures with infinite components.

As the specification in (
27
) lies between a parametric and fully nonparametric model, a natural question is whether fast rates of convergence can be expected in this setup and, in particular, how is this influenced by the model ill-posedness. Intuitively, as the prior concentrates on infinite Gaussian mixtures, the expectation is that the prior should exhibit negligible bias in approximating a latent density with Gaussian mixture structure as in (
27
). In the next result, we formalize this intuition. To this end, our main assumption regarding the Gaussian mixture specification is as follows.

Condition 4.2

(Nonparametric Mixture)
.

f_{X}=\phi_{\Sigma_{0}}\star F_{0}
where
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a positive definite matrix and
F_{0}
is a mixing distribution that satisfies
F_{0}\big(t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|t\|>z\big)\leq C\exp(- C' z ^{\chi})
for some universal constants
C,C^{\prime},\chi>0
.

Condition (
4.2
) imposes an exponential tail on the mixing distribution
F_{0}
. Importantly, this covers all Gaussian mixtures that have (possibly infinite) modes contained in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The following result demonstrates that, with an appropriately scaled covariance prior, the quasi-Bayes posterior contracts towards the true latent density at a polynomial (and sometimes nearly parametric) rate.

Theorem 3

(Rates with Gaussian Mixtures)
.

Suppose Condition
4.2
holds and the covariance prior is taken as
G_{n}\sim G/\sigma_{n}^{2}
where
G
satisfies Assumption
5
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}
is as specified below. Let
\kappa,\chi>0
be as in Assumption
5
and Condition
4.2
, respectively.

(a)

Let
\lambda=\max\{\chi^{-1}(d+2)+d/2,d+1\}
. Suppose the model is mildly ill-posed:
\inf_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|\geq cT^{-\zeta}
for some
c>0
and
\zeta\geq 0
. Suppose
T_{n}\asymp\sqrt{\log n}\sqrt{\log\log n}
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}\asymp(\log n)^{-\lambda/\kappa}
. Then there exists a universal constant
D>0
such that

\displaystyle\nu\bigg(\phi_{P,\Sigma}:\|f_{X}-\phi_{P,\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}>D\frac{(\log n)^{(\lambda+\zeta)/2}(\log\log n)^{\zeta/2}}{\sqrt{n}}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0

(b)

Let
\lambda=\max\{\chi^{-1}(d+2)+d/2,d+1,d/\zeta+1/2\}
. Suppose the model is severely ill-posed:
\inf_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|\geq c\exp(-B T^{\zeta} )
for some
c,B>0
and
\zeta\in(0,2]
. Suppose
T_{n}\asymp(\log n)^{1/\zeta}
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}\asymp(\log n)^{-\lambda/\kappa}
. Then, there exists a universal constant
D>0
, sequence
L_{n}\uparrow\infty
and
V\in(0,1/2)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nu\bigg(\phi_{P,\Sigma}:\|f_{X}-\phi_{P,\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}>L_{n}n^{-1/2}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0&\zeta\in(0,2)\\
\nu\bigg(\phi_{P,\Sigma}:\|f_{X}-\phi_{P,\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}>Dn^{-V}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0&\zeta=2.\end{cases}

Furthermore, the sequence
(L_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
grows slower than any polynomial in the sense that
L_{n}=o(n^{\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Under a mildly ill-posed regime, the quasi-Bayes posterior attains nearly (up to a logarithmic factors) parametric rates of convergence. If the model is severely ill-posed at a rate
\zeta
that is lower than Gaussian type ill-posedness, nearly parametric rates in the form
n^{-1/2+\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
can be obtained. In the extreme case where the model exhibits Gaussian ill-posedness, the model bias (which is determined by the decay rate of a Gaussian mixture characteristic function) has similar order to the variance. In this case, the rate is still polynomial but the exponent
V
depends on second order factors such as the constant
B
and the eigenvalues of the matrix
\Sigma_{0}
in representation (
27
). In a univariate framework with a kernel deconvolution based estimator, a similar finding is established in
Butucea and Tsybakov 2008b
;
Butucea and Tsybakov 2008a
.

Remark 3

(On Covariance Scaling)
.

Theorem
3
differs from Theorem
2
in that the covariance prior must be appropriately scaled. The strategy of scaling the covariance prior can be traced back to

Shen et al. 2013
. We conjecture that the scaling may be an artifact of our proof and can possibly be relaxed as well. We leave this investigation for future work. In our simulations and empirical illustrations, we do not apply any scaling to the covariance prior.

As Gaussian mixtures can approximate a density arbitrarily well, it is straightforward to modify the preceding results to obtain consistency for a general density.

Remark 4

(Bias Bounds)
.

For a conservative upper bound on the rate at which
\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
tends to
0
as
\sigma\rightarrow 0
, observe that by picking
P=F_{X}
and
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\sigma)\leq\|f_{X}\star f_{\epsilon}-\phi_{F_{X},\sigma^{2}I}\|_{L^{2}}\asymp\|(e^{-\|t\|^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}-1)\varphi_{X}(t)\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\lessapprox\sigma^{2}.

The preceding bound follows from
\left|e^{-\|t\|^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}-1\right|\leq\|t\|^{2}\sigma^{2}/2
. As such, the concluding estimate is valid provided that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|t\|^{4}\left|\varphi_{X}(t)\right|^{2}\left|\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|^{2}<\infty
or equivalently
f_{X}\star f_{\epsilon}\in\mathbf{H}^{2}
. For
f_{X}\star f_{\epsilon}
with smoothness order greater than
2
, this argument does not provide better rates. In particular, with higher smoothness, the choice
P=f_{X}
is not optimal in (
28
): one can typically do better by allowing
P=P_{X,\epsilon,\sigma}
to depend implicitly on all the features
(f_{\epsilon},f_{X},\sigma)
.

Intuitively, the smoothness of the convolution
f_{X}\star f_{\epsilon}
is the sum of the smoothness of the individual functions. As such, we expect the Gaussian mixture bias to decay at a rate proportional to the combined Sobolev smoothness (or equivalently the characteristic function decay) of
f_{X}
and
f_{\epsilon}
. To derive the limit theory, we consider the following popular characterization of the Gaussian mixture bias.

Condition 4.3

(Gaussian Mixture Bias)
.

f_{X}\in\mathbf{H}^{p}
for some
p>1/2
. For all
\sigma>0
sufficiently small and some
\chi>0
, there exists
(i)
a mixing distribution
F_{\sigma}=F_{X,\epsilon,\sigma}
supported on the cube
I_{\sigma}=[-C(\log\sigma^{-1})^{1/\chi},C(\log\sigma^{-1})^{1/\chi}]^{d}
and
(ii)
a covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\sigma}=\Sigma_{X,\epsilon,\sigma}
with
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\sigma})\geq\sigma^{2}
such that the Gaussian mixture
\phi_{F_{\sigma},\Sigma_{\sigma}}
satisfies
\|f_{X}\star F_{\epsilon}-\phi_{F_{\sigma},\Sigma_{\sigma}}\star F_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq D\sigma^{p+\zeta}
for some
\zeta>0
, where
C,D<\infty
are universal constants.

Variations of Condition
4.3
, usually with additional smoothness constraints on
\log f_{X}
, are commonly used in Bayesian density estimation and deconvolution. For examples of explicit constructions of
(F_{\sigma},\Sigma_{\sigma})
in Condition
4.3
, see
Shen et al. 2013
;
Ghosal and Van Der Vaart 2017
for density estimation and
Rousseau and Scricciolo 2024
for deconvolution. We note that, at least in our quasi-Bayes setup, Condition
4.3
can be weakened further. In particular, it suffices that the approximation holds with respect to the weaker norm
\|.\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}
and for a sequence
\sigma_{n}
, where
(\sigma_{n}^{-1},T_{n})
grow at a suitable rate. Finally, we note that our procedures do not require knowledge of the optimal mixing distribution
F_{\sigma}
in Condition
4.3
for implementation. The existence is purely a proof device towards obtaining theoretical guarantees.

The following result derives the quasi-Bayes posterior contraction rate when the Gaussian mixture bias is as in Condition
4.3
and
\varphi_{\epsilon}(.)
decays at a mildly ill-posed rate of order
\zeta\geq 0
.

Theorem 4

(Rates with Gaussian Mixture Bias)
.

Suppose Condition
4.3
holds with
\chi>0
. Let
\kappa>0
be as in Assumption
5
and define
\lambda=\max\{\chi^{-1}(d+2),\chi^{-1}d+1\}
. Suppose the covariance prior is
G_{n}\sim G/\sigma_{n}^{2}
where
G
satisfies Assumption
5
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}\asymp(\log\log n)^{-1}T_{n}^{2-d/\kappa}(\log n)^{-\lambda/\kappa-1}
. If
\inf_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|\gtrapprox T^{-\zeta}
for some
\zeta\geq 0
and
T_{n}\asymp n^{1/[2(p+\zeta)+d]}\sqrt{\log n}
, there exists a universal constant
D>0
such that

\displaystyle\nu\bigg(\phi_{P,\Sigma}:\|f_{X}-\phi_{P,\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}>Dn^{\frac{-p}{2(p+\zeta)+d}}(\log n)^{(\lambda+\zeta)/2+d/4}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

The obtained rates are minimax-optimal, up to a logarithmic factor. When
\zeta=0
, we recover the usual optimal rates for multivariate density estimation. In dimension
d=1
, the rates agree with the univariate Bayesian deconvolution rates (with known error distribution) obtained in
Donnet et al. 2018
.

Remark 5

(Convergence in stronger metrics)
.

An interesting aspect of the quasi-Bayes framework is that all formal analysis occurs directly in the Fourier domain. In particular, in our analysis, we first derive contraction rates in the Fourier domain and use those to derive
\|.\|_{L^{2}}
and
\mathbf{W}_{1}
rates for the distribution. This approach can be extended to other metrics. Fourier distances are widely used to derive bounds in a variety of metrics (see
Bobkov 2016
for a comprehensive survey). For instance, we have the elementary bound
\|f_{X}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\varphi_{X}\|_{L^{1}}
, which leads to the following variant of Theorem
4
.

Corollary 2

(
L^{\infty}
Rates with Gaussian Mixture Bias)
.

Suppose the hypothesis of Theorem
4
holds. Then, there exists a universal constant
D>0
such that

\displaystyle\nu\bigg(\phi_{P,\Sigma}:\|f_{X}-\phi_{P,\Sigma}\|_{L^{\infty}}>Dn^{\frac{-p+d/2}{2(p+\zeta)+d}}(\log n)^{(\lambda+\zeta+d)/2}\>\bigg|\>\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

Interestingly, when
d=1
and
\zeta=0
, the
L^{\infty}
quasi-Bayes contraction rates in Corollary
2
coincide with the univariate density pure-Bayes rates obtained in
Giné and Nickl 2011
. For
d>1
and
\zeta\geq 0
, the contraction rates appear to be novel; in particular, we could not find any pure-Bayes results for these cases in the literature. For simplicity of exposition, all remaining results in Section
4
are stated exclusively for the Wasserstein and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
metrics. By analogous arguments to those used in Corollary
2
, these results can be extended to other metrics.

4.2

Repeated Measurements

Consider the repeated measurements model, introduced in Example
2

\displaystyle Y_{1}=X+\epsilon_{1}\;,\;\;\;\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{1}]=0,

\displaystyle Y_{2}=X+\epsilon_{2}\;,\;\;\;\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{2}]=0.

In this setting,
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are observed measurements,
X\in\mathbb{R}
is unobserved, and
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}

\epsilon_{2}

Condition 4.4

(Data)
.

(i)

(Y_{1,i},Y_{2,i})_{i=1}^{n}

(ii)
Finite second moments:

(29)

4.1

Theorem 5

(Wasserstein Contraction)
.

Assume

for some

(ii)

(iii)

Remark 6

(On Symmetric Restrictions)
.

If
(X,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
are mutually independent, the roles of
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
become interchangeable in the identifying restrictions. This provides us with two sets of moment identifying restrictions. The simplest approach to accommodate this setting is to construct the quasi-Bayes posterior using both sets of moment restrictions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}Y_{2}}]\partial_{t}\log\varphi_{X}(t)-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{i}Y_{1}e^{\mathbf{i}tY_{2}}]

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X})(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}Y_{1}}]\partial_{t}\log\varphi_{X}(t)-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{i}Y_{2}e^{\mathbf{i}tY_{1}}].

We recommend this choice unless there are compelling arguments to treat the nuisance errors asymmetrically with respect to their conditional mean and independence restrictions.

4.3

Multi-Factor Models

Following
Bonhomme and Robin 2010
, consider the multi-factor model, where we observe a random sample from the model

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}.

(32)

In this setting,
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{L})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}

L

K

Condition 4.6

(Data)
.

(i)

(\mathbf{Y}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random vectors.
(ii)
Finite second moment:
\mathbb{E}\big(\|\mathbf{Y}\|^{2}\big)<\infty

(iii)
The factors

\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=0

Suppose we are interested in the latent distribution
F_{X_{k}}
for some
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}

\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
denote the vector of upper triangular elements of
\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\log\varphi_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)

V(\mathbf{A}_{k})
denote the vector of upper triangular elements of

\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathbf{A}_{k}^{\prime}
and define
\mathbf{Q}=[V(\mathbf{A}_{1}),\dots,V(\mathbf{A}_{K})]
to be the matrix with columns given by those vectors. From the discussion in Example
4

\mathbf{Q}^{*}=(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{k}^{*}
denotes the

\mathbf{Q}^{*}

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{Y},\varphi_{X_{k}})(t)=\varphi_{\mathbf{Y}}^{2}(t)\big[\mathbf{Q}_{k}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)-(\log\varphi_{X_{k}})^{\prime\prime}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k})].

(33)

With
\mathbf{Y}
as the observable, the feasible analog
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X})
, obtained by using the empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n,Y}

\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{P}_{n,Y},\varphi_{X_{k}})(t)=\widehat{\varphi}_{\mathbf{Y}}^{2}(t)\big[\mathbf{Q}_{k}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{Y}}(t)-(\log\varphi_{X_{k}})^{\prime\prime}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k})],

Remark 7

Application: Earnings Dynamics

In this section, we apply our methodology to study the latent structure of permanent and transitory components in the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) data. Our analysis closely follows
Bonhomme and Robin 2010
in which deconvolution-based methods were used to nonparametrically estimate the latent structure.

6.1

Data and Model

We begin with a brief summary of the data and model. The data is from the PSID, between 1978 and 1987. Let
y_{i,t}
denotes annual log earnings for individual
i
at time period
t
and
x_{i,t}
an associated set of regressors. The regressors are a quadratic polynomial in age and indicators for education, race, geography and year. The OLS residuals of
y_{i,t}
on
x_{i,t}
are denoted by
w_{i,t}
. The residual differences are denoted by
\Delta w_{i,t}=w_{i,t}-w_{i,t-1}
. After restricting the sample to male workers with no missing observations for
\Delta w_{i,t}
and a wage growth that does not exceed
150\%
in absolute value, our sample size consists of
n=624
individuals. For each individual, we observe wages between 1978 and 1987, for a total of
M=10
time periods.

The standard model is given by
12
12

12

Variations of (
38
) are widely used to model income dynamics. see
Hall and Mishkin 1982
;
Abowd and Card 1989
;
Geweke and Keane 2000
, among many others.

\displaystyle w_{i,t}=f_{i}+w_{i,t}^{P}+w_{i,t}^{T}\>,\;\;\;\;\;\;i=1,\dots,n\;,\;t=1,\dots,M

(38)

\displaystyle w_{i,t}^{P}=w_{i,t-1}^{P}+\epsilon_{i,t}\>,

\displaystyle w_{i,t}^{T}=\eta_{i,t}

\displaystyle\eta_{i,1}=\eta_{i,M}=0.

Here,
f_{i}
is an individual level fixed effect and
\{\epsilon_{i,t}\}_{t=1}^{M}
and
\{\eta_{i,t}\}_{t=2}^{M-1}
are mean zero errors. We think of
w_{i,t}^{P}
as the permanent component and
\eta_{i,t}
as the transitory component. Thus, the distribution of the permanent component of income is determined by
\epsilon_{i,t}
and the distribution of the transitory component by
\eta_{i,t}
. From first differencing the model in (
38
), we can write

\displaystyle\Delta w_{i,t}=\epsilon_{i,t}+\eta_{i,t}-\eta_{i,t-1}\>,\;\;\;\;\;\;i=1,\dots,n\;,\;t=2,\dots,M.

(39)

We view
\mathbf{Y}_{i}=(\Delta w_{i,2},\dots,\Delta w_{i,M})
as the observations for
i=1,\dots,n
. The model in (
39
) is a special case of the multi-factor model
\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{i}
in Section
4.3
. Here,
\mathbf{A}
is a known matrix with elements in
\{-1,0,1\}
and the latent factors are
\mathbf{X}_{i}=\{\eta_{i,2},\dots,\eta_{i,M-1},\epsilon_{i,2},\dots,\epsilon_{i,M}\}
.

As noted in the literature (e.g.
Geweke and Keane 2000
), a standard Gaussian model significantly underestimates the kurtosis in the wage growth residuals
\Delta w
, leading to concern that the wage shocks
\mathbf{X}_{i}
may be non-Gaussian. Over
M=10
time periods, there are
17
(the dimension of
\mathbf{X}_{i}
) latent distributions that must be estimated.

6.2

Implementation

We use the measurements in (
39
) to construct the joint multi-factor quasi-Bayes posterior. From the descriptives in Table
4
, the measurements exhibit low finite sample variance and large kurtosis, suggesting that the latent wage shocks are tightly concentrated around zero and exhibit outliers. For the
\text{DP}_{\alpha}
prior, we use
\alpha=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\beta=1
as the
\text{DP}_{\alpha}
concentration parameter. For the prior on the standard deviation component
\sigma
, we use a Half-Cauchy
(0,10)
prior. Given the low dispersion in the observables, we view this as a relatively weak prior.
13
13

13

If the latent distribution is an exact Gaussian mixture,
f_{X}=F_{0}\star\phi_{\sigma_{0}^{2}}

\sigma_{0}
is linked to the dispersion in the observables. Half-Cauchy priors with a large standard deviation are an attractive choice for a weakly informative prior on the scale, particularly in settings with low dispersion
(
Gelman 2006
)
.
Samples from the quasi-Bayes posterior are generated using
Stan

(
Team 2024

To choose the grid of identifying restrictions

14

We view this as a heuristic choice. Optimal selection of

7

Conclusion

This paper introduces and develops a quasi-Bayes framework for a large class of latent variable models. Simulations demonstrate that our quasi-Bayes procedures are viable and perform remarkably well relative to existing alternatives. As an application, we used our method to model the latent structure in U.S earnings data. We end this section by highlighting some future directions for research.

Empirical Bayes selection of priors and tuning parameters that our procedures depend on is an important topic to be investigated. A more challenging task would be to establish the frequentist validity of quasi-Bayes credible intervals. This would, at the very least, require the quasi-Bayes objective function to be optimally-weighted. We hope to address these issues in future work.

at
z_{j}
. Then, for any probability measure
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}dF(x)<\infty
, we have that

1.

\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\right|\leq\|t\|\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|.

2.

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}dF(x)\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|\bigg\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\bigg(1+\sup_{v\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}}\|v\|\|t\|\bigg)\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})+\sup_{i=1,\dots,N}\|z_{i}\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|.

3.

\displaystyle\sup_{k=1,\dots,d}\left|\partial_{t_{k}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\partial_{t_{k}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|_{\infty}^{4}dF(x)\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|\bigg\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\bigg(2\sup_{v\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}}\|v\|+\sup_{v\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}}\|v\|^{2}\|t\|\bigg)\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})

\displaystyle+\sup_{i=1,\dots,N}\|z_{i}\|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|.

Here,
\mathcal{D}(A)
denotes the diameter of a set
A
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}dF(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}dF^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}dF(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z_{j}}

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{0}}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}dF(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{V_{j}}\big(e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}-e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z_{j}}\big)dF(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z_{j}}\big[F(V_{j})-w_{j}].

Since the mapping
\mu\rightarrow e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu}
has Lipschitz constant at most
\|t\|
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}F(V_{j})=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq F(V_{0})+\|t\|\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})\sum_{j=1}^{N}F(V_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq F(V_{0})+\|t\|\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|.

Since
V_{0}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\cup_{j\geq 1}V_{j}
and
F(\mathbb{R}^{d})=1=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}
, we obtain

F(V_{0})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}F(V_{j})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|.

For the second claim, first observe that the moment condition on
F
ensures that the gradient exists. By differentiating the preceding expression for
\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{i}\int_{V_{0}}xe^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}dF(x)+\mathbf{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{V_{j}}\big(xe^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}-z_{j}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z_{j}}\big)dF(x)+\mathbf{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z_{j}}\big[F(V_{j})-w_{j}].

For
\mu\in V_{j}
, the mapping
\mu\rightarrow\mu e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu}
has Lipschitz constant at most
1+\sup_{v\in V_{j}}\|v\|\|t\|
and by Cauchy-Schwarz,
\int_{V_{0}}\|x\|dF(x)\leq\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}dF(x)\big)^{1/2}\big\{F(V_{0})\big\}^{1/2}
. By using the same bound for
F(V_{0})
as above, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}dF(x)\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|\bigg\}^{1/2}+\bigg(1+\sup_{v\in\cup_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}}\|v\|\|t\|\bigg)\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})

\displaystyle\;+\sup_{i=1,\dots,N}\|z_{i}\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|.

For the third claim, let
z_{j,k}
denote the
k^{th}
element of
z_{j}
. Twice differentiating (with respect to
t_{k}
) the expression for
\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)
yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{k}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\partial_{t_{k}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{V_{0}}x_{k}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}dF(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{V_{j}}\big(x_{k}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}x}-z_{j,k}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z_{j}}\big)dF(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j,k}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z_{j}}\big[F(V_{j})-w_{j}].

For
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{d})\in V_{j}
and a fixed component
\mu_{k}
the mapping
\mu\rightarrow\mu_{k}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu}
has Lipschitz constant at most
2\sup_{v\in V_{j}}\|v\|+\sup_{v\in V_{j}}\|v\|^{2}\|t\|
and by Cauchy-Schwarz, we have the bound
\int_{V_{0}}x_{k}^{2}dF(x)\leq\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|_{\infty}^{4}dF(x)\big)^{1/2}\big\{F(V_{0})\big\}^{1/2}
. The claim follows from an analogous bound to the preceding case.

∎

Lemma 4
.

Consider a measurable partition
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{N}V_{j}
and points
z_{j}\in V_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,N
. Let
F^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\delta_{z_{j}}
denote the discrete probability measure with weight
w_{j}
at
z_{j}
. Then, there exists a universal constant
D>0
such that for any positive definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with minimum eigenvalue
\underline{\sigma}^{2}>0
and probability measure
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that

\displaystyle(i)\;\;\;\|\varphi_{F,\Sigma}-\varphi_{F^{*},\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}\leq D\bigg[\underline{\sigma}^{-(d+2)/2}\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})+\underline{\sigma}^{-d/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|\bigg],

\displaystyle(ii)\;\;\|\varphi_{F,\Sigma}-\varphi_{F^{*},\Sigma}\|_{\mathbb{B}(T)}\leq D\bigg[T^{(d+2)/2}\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})+T^{d/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|\bigg].

Proof of Lemma
4
.

By Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\right|\leq\|t\|\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{D}(V_{j})+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|.

Since
e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t}\leq e^{-\|t\|^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{F,\Sigma}-\varphi_{F^{*},\sigma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\varphi_{\Sigma}(t)\right|^{2}\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t}\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\|t\|^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\{\mathcal{D}(V_{j})\}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\|t\|^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}\|t\|^{2}dt+8\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|F(V_{j})-w_{j}\right|\bigg\}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\|t\|^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}dt.

The first claim follows from observing that the two integrals scale with rate at most
\underline{\sigma}^{-(d+2)}
and
\underline{\sigma}^{-d}
, respectively. The second claim follows by an analogous argument from truncating the integral to the set
\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T\}
and using the trivial bound
e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t}\leq 1
.

∎

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
F
is a probability measure supported on
[-R,R]^{d}
for some
R>0
. Then, given any
k\geq 1
, there exists a discrete probability measure
F^{\prime}
with at most
(k+1)^{d}+1
support points in
[-R,R]^{d}
such that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle(i)\;\;\;\;\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|\leq 2\frac{\|t\|^{k+1}(e\sqrt{d}R)^{k+1}}{(k+1)^{k+1}}\;,

\displaystyle(ii)\;\;\;\;\|\nabla\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\|\leq 2\sqrt{d}R\frac{\|t\|^{k}(e\sqrt{d}R)^{k}}{k^{k}}\;,

\displaystyle(iii)\;\;\sup_{l=1,\dots,d}\left|\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|\leq 2dR^{2}\frac{\|t\|^{k-1}(e\sqrt{d}R)^{k-1}}{(k-1)^{k-1}}.

In particular, there exists universal constants
C,D>0
such that for all
T,R
sufficiently large and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the choice
k=\lceil C\max\{\log(\epsilon^{-1}),RT\}\rceil
satisfies

\displaystyle(i)\;\;\;\;\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|\leq D\epsilon\;,

\displaystyle(ii)\;\;\;\;\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\|\nabla\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\|\leq D\epsilon\;,

\displaystyle(iii)\;\;\;\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\sup_{l=1,\dots,d}\left|\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|\leq D\epsilon.

Furthermore, the support points of
F^{\prime}
can be chosen on the grid
\mathcal{Z}=\{T^{-1}\epsilon(z_{1},\dots,z_{d}):z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\;,\;\left|z_{i}\right|\leq\lceil R/(T^{-1}\epsilon)\rceil\}
, with a multiplictive penalty of at most
R
and
R^{2}
in cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
, respectively.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

Given any
k\geq 1
, by
(
Ghosal and Van Der Vaart 2001
, Lemma A.1)
, there exists a discrete measure
F^{\prime}
with at most
(k+1)^{d}+1
support points on
[-R,R]^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z_{1}^{l_{1}}\dots z_{d}^{l_{d}}dF(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z_{1}^{l_{1}}\dots z_{d}^{l_{d}}dF^{\prime}(z)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\forall\;\;\;0\leq l_{1},\dots,l_{d}\leq k.

For any
t,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, a multinomial expansion yields

(t^{\prime}z)^{j}=\sum_{k_{1}+\dots+k_{d}=j,k_{1}\geq 0,\dots,k_{d}\geq 0}\frac{j!}{k_{1}!\dots k_{d}!}\prod_{i=1}^{d}t_{i}^{k_{i}}z_{i}^{k_{i}}.

It follows that
F
and
F^{\prime}
assign the same expectation to
(t^{\prime}z)^{j}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, provided that
j\leq k
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z}d(F-F^{\prime})(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}d(F-F^{\prime})(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}d(F-F^{\prime})(z).

Observe that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the bound
\left|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}(\mathbf{i}x)^{j}/j!\right|=\left|e^{\mathbf{i}x}-\sum_{j=0}^{k}(\mathbf{i}x)^{j}/j!\right|\leq\left|x\right|^{k+1}/(k+1)!\leq\left|x\right|^{k+1}e^{k+1}/(k+1)^{k+1}
. For any
z\in[-R,R]^{d}
, we have that
\left|t^{\prime}z\right|\leq\|t\|\|z\|\leq\|t\|\sqrt{d}R
. Since
F,F^{\prime}
are probability measures and have support contained in
[-R,R]^{d}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}\right|dF+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}\right|dF^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\|t\|^{k+1}(e\sqrt{d}R)^{k+1}}{(k+1)^{k+1}}.

For the second claim, the same reasoning as above implies that
F
and
F^{\prime}
assign the same expectation to the vector
z(t^{\prime}z)^{j}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, provided that
j\leq k-1
. This yields

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)=\mathbf{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ze^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z}d(F-F^{\prime})(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}d(F-F^{\prime})(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}d(F-F^{\prime})(z).

We have
\|z\|\leq\sqrt{d}R
for every
z
in the support of
F,F^{\prime}
. From using the bound in the preceding case with
k
replacing
k+1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}R\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}\right|dF+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}\right|dF^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d}R\frac{\|t\|^{k}(e\sqrt{d}R)^{k}}{k^{k}}.

For the third claim, let
z_{l}
denote the
l^{th}
coordinate of
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})
. The same reasoning as above implies that
F
and
F^{\prime}
assign the same expectation to the vector
z_{l}^{2}(t^{\prime}z)^{j}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, provided that
j\leq k-2
. This yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z_{l}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z}d(F-F^{\prime})(z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z_{l}^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}d(F-F^{\prime})(z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z_{l}^{2}\sum_{j=k-1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}z)^{j}}{j!}d(F-F^{\prime})(z).

Since
z_{l}^{2}\leq\|z\|^{2}\leq dR^{2}
, the claim follows from an analogous bound to the preceding case.

For the final claim regarding the location of the support points, suppose
F^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}}
is a discrete probability measure that satisfies the requirements of the first part of the Lemma. Let
F^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}^{*}}
denote the probability measure obtained by replacing each
\mu_{i}
with
\mu_{i}^{*}\in\arg\min\limits_{t\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\mu_{i}-t\|
. From the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
, it follows that
\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{i}^{*}\|\leq DT^{-1}\epsilon
. We claim
F^{\prime}
satisfies all the same bounds. For the first bound, observe that

\displaystyle\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}]-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}]\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}\big[e^{\mathbf{i}t\mu_{j}}-e^{\mathbf{i}t\mu_{j}^{*}}]\right|\leq\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\left|e^{\mathbf{i}t\mu_{j}}-e^{\mathbf{i}t\mu_{j}^{*}}\right|.

Since the mapping
\mu\rightarrow e^{\mathbf{i}t\mu}
has Lipschitz constant bounded by
\|t\|
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\left|\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}]-\mathcal{F}[F^{*}]\right|\leq\sqrt{d}T\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\|\mu_{j}-\mu_{j}^{*}\|\leq D\epsilon.

For the second bound, observe that for
\mu\in[-R,R]^{d}
, the mapping
\mu\rightarrow\mu e^{\mathbf{i}t\mu}
has Lipschitz constant at most
1+\sqrt{d}R\|t\|
. Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\|\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)-\nabla\mathcal{F}[F^{*}](t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\|\mu_{j}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu_{j}}-\mu_{j}^{*}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu_{j}^{*}}\|

\displaystyle\leq D(RT+1)\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\|\mu_{j}-\mu_{j}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq DR\epsilon.

For the third bound, let
\mu_{j,l}
denote the
l^{th}
component of
\mu_{j}
. On
[-R,R]^{d}
, the mapping
\mu_{j}\rightarrow\mu_{j,l}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu_{j}}
has Lipschitz constant at most
2\sqrt{d}R+dR^{2}\|t\|
. Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\sup_{l=1,\dots,d}\left|\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\partial_{t_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|t\|_{\infty}\leq T}\sup_{l=1,\dots,d}\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\left|\mu_{j,l}^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu_{j}}-(\mu_{j,l}^{*})^{2}e^{\mathbf{i}t^{\prime}\mu_{j}^{*}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq D(R+R^{2}T)\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\|\mu_{j}-\mu_{j}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq DR^{2}\epsilon.

∎

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
F
is a probability measure supported on
[-L,L]^{d}
for some
L>0
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a positive-definite matrix with smallest eigenvalue
\underline{\sigma}^{2}>0
. Then, for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a discrete probability measure
F^{\prime}
with at most
D\max\big\{(\log(\epsilon^{-1}))^{d},(L/\underline{\sigma})^{d}(\log(\epsilon^{-1}))^{d/2}\big\}
support points on
[-L,L]^{d}
such that
\|\varphi_{F,\Sigma}-\varphi_{F^{\prime},\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}\leq D^{\prime}\underline{\sigma}^{-d/2}\epsilon
, where
D,D^{\prime}>0
are universal constants. Furthermore, the support points can be chosen such that
\inf_{i\neq j}\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\|\geq\underline{\sigma}\epsilon
.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

Let
D
denote a generic universal constant that may change from line to line. By Lemma
5
, there exists a discrete measure
F^{\prime}
with at most
k^{d}+1
support points on
[-L,L]^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|\leq 2\frac{\|t\|^{k}(e\sqrt{d}L)^{k}}{k^{k}}\;\;\;\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Observe that
\left|\varphi_{\Sigma}\right|=e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t/2}\leq e^{-\|t\|^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}/2}
. From using the preceding bound, we obtain for every
M>0
, the estimate

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{F,\Sigma}-\varphi_{F^{\prime},\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\varphi_{\Sigma}(t)\right|^{2}\left|\mathcal{F}[F](t)-\mathcal{F}[F^{\prime}](t)\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq D\bigg[\int_{\|t\|>M}\left|\varphi_{\Sigma}(t)\right|^{2}dt+\bigg(\frac{Le\sqrt{d}}{k}\bigg)^{2k}\int_{\|t\|\leq M}\|t\|^{2k}dt\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq D\bigg[\int_{\|t\|>M}e^{-\|t\|^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}dt+\bigg(\frac{Le\sqrt{d}}{k}\bigg)^{2k}\int_{\|t\|\leq M}\|t\|^{2k}dt\bigg].

By change of variables (to spherical coordinates) the first integral scales at rate
DM^{d-2}e^{-M^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}\underline{\sigma}^{-2}
and the second integral scales at rate
DM^{2k+d}/(2k+d)
. The choice
M=\sqrt{4\log(\epsilon^{-1})}/\underline{\sigma}
leads to

\int_{\|t\|>M}e^{-\|t\|^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}dt\leq DM^{d-2}e^{-M^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}}\underline{\sigma}^{-2}\leq D\epsilon^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{-d}.

Furthermore, if
k\geq e^{3}\sqrt{d}\max\{LM,\log(\epsilon^{-1})\}
, we have that

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{Le\sqrt{d}}{k}\bigg)^{2k}\int_{\|t\|\leq M}\|t\|^{2k}dt\leq D\bigg(\frac{Le\sqrt{d}M}{k}\bigg)^{2k}\frac{M^{d}}{2k+d}\leq D\epsilon^{2}.

The claim follows from observing that the number of support points in
F^{\prime}
is
N=k^{d}+1
.

For the final claim regarding the separation of the support points, suppose
F^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}}
is a discrete probability measure that satisfies the requirements of the first part of the Lemma. Let
\mathcal{Z}
denote a maximal
\underline{\sigma}\epsilon
separated subset of
[-L,L]^{d}
. For each
\mu_{i}
, select
\mu_{i}^{*}\in\arg\min\limits_{t\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\mu_{i}-t\|
and let
F^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}^{*}}
. From the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
, it follows that
\sup_{i=1}^{N}\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{i}^{*}\|\leq\underline{\sigma}\epsilon
. Observe that

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{F^{\prime},\Sigma}-\varphi_{F^{*},\Sigma}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}\big[e^{it\mu_{j}}-e^{it\mu_{j}^{*}}\big]e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t/2}\bigg\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq D\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}\|\big(e^{it\mu_{j}}-e^{it\mu_{j}^{*}}\big)e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t/2}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq D\sup_{j=1,\dots,N}\|\big(e^{it\mu_{j}}-e^{it\mu_{j}^{*}}\big)e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t/2}\|_{L^{2}}.

Since the mapping
\mu\rightarrow e^{it\mu}
has Lipschitz constant bounded by
\|t\|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\big(e^{it\mu_{j}}-e^{it\mu_{j}^{*}}\big)e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|e^{it\mu_{j}}-e^{it\mu_{j}^{*}}\right|^{2}e^{-t^{\prime}\Sigma t}dt

c_{U}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k}
. It follows that for any non-negative Borel function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}f(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k})dt=\left|a_{1}\right|^{-1}(2T_{n})^{L-1}\int_{c_{L}}^{c_{U}}f(z_{1})dz_{1}.

In particular, since
c_{U}\geq a_{1}T_{n}
and
c_{L}\leq-a_{1}T_{n}
, we have
\|f\|_{\mathbb{B}(a_{1}T_{n})}^{2}\leq DT_{n}^{1-L}\|f(t^{\prime}\mathbf{A}_{k})\|_{\mathbb{B}(T_{n})}^{2}
for some universal constant
D>0
. From combining the preceding bounds, it follows that the modulus satisfies

\omega(d,\mathcal{H}_{n},\epsilon_{n})\leq D\bigg[R_{n}^{\frac{4(s-1)}{1+2s}}T_{n}^{\frac{(s-1)(1-L)}{1+2s}}n^{-\delta+\xi}+R_{n}^{-1}\bigg]\leq DR_{n}^{-1}.

Since
R_{n}=\min\{c_{0}(\log n)^{1/2},a_{1}T_{n}\}
, the claim follows.

∎
