Decomposition of the regular representation for dihedral quandles
Mohamed Elhamdadi, Prasad Senesi, Emanuele Zappala

Abstract
. We decompose the regular quandle representation of a dihedral quandle ℛ n \mathcal{R}_{n} into irreducible quandle subrepresentations.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{R}_{n}
be the dihedral quandle of order
n
, and let
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
be the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of all
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions on
\mathcal{R}_{n}
. Then
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
has the structure of a quandle representation of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, which we call the regular representation of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
. In this note we provide, for all positive integers
n
, the decomposition of this regular representation into irreducible quandle subrepresentations. This problem was first addressed using the notion of a
quandle ring
in
[
EFT
]
in 2019, although the results given there are incomplete and do not address dihedral quandles of odd order. Our main result here was recently reported by one of the authors at the
Knots in Washington Meeting
on April 23, 2022, and will be included in a more comprehensive forthcoming publication on the representation theory of quandles. The authors would also like to note the recent arxiv submission
[
KS
]
, where these results are obtained with a different approach for the dihedral quandles of even order.

2.
Review of racks and quandles

We give a quick review of racks and quandles in this section.

Definition 2.1
.

[
EN
,
Joyce
,
Matveev
]

A
rack
is a set
X
provided with a binary operation

\begin{array}[]{lccc}\triangleright:&X\times X&\longrightarrow&X\\
&(x,y)&\longmapsto&x\triangleright y\end{array}

such that

(i)

for all
x,y\in X
, there is a unique
z\in X
such that
y=z\triangleright x
;

(ii)

(
right distributivity
) for all
x,y,z\in X
, we have
(x\triangleright y)\triangleright z=(x\triangleright z)\triangleright(y\triangleright z)
.

For
x\in X
, we denote by
R_{x}:X\rightarrow X
the map given by

R_{x}(y)=y\triangleright x.

Observe that property (i) also reads that for any fixed element
x\in X
, the map
R_{x}
is a
bijection. Also, notice that the right distributivity condition is equivalent to the relation
R_{x}(y\triangleright z)=R_{x}(y)\triangleright R_{x}(z)
for all
y,z\in X
; that is,
R_{x}
is an automorphism of the quandle
X
.

Unless otherwise stated, we will always assume our racks (or quandles) to be finite racks (or quandles).

Definition 2.2
.

A
quandle
is a rack such that

x\triangleright x=x,\forall x\in X
.

As each
R_{x}
permutes the elements of
X
, we can consider the subgroup
\left\langle R_{x}\right\rangle_{x\in X}
in
S_{X}
(the symmetric group on
X
) generated by the
R_{x}
, which we denote by
\operatorname{Inn}(X)
(the group of inner automorphisms of
X
). This group acts on the set
X
, and so the group action partitions
X
into distinct orbits. We say the quandle
X
is connected if
\text{Inn}(X)
acts transitively on
X
(and hence there is only one orbit). If
(X,\triangleright)
and
(Y,\triangleright)
are two quandles, a map
f:X\rightarrow Y

is a
quandle homomorphism
if
f(x\triangleright y)=f(x)\triangleright f(y)
for all
x,y\in X
.

Example 2.3
.

Any group
G
with the operation
x\triangleright y=yxy^{-1}
is a quandle, called conjugation quandle and denoted by Conj(G).

Example 2.4
.

Any group
G
with the operation
x\triangleright y=yx^{-1}y
is a quandle, called the Core quandle of
G
and denoted Core(G).

Example 2.5
.

Let
G
be a group and
f\in{\rm Aut}(G)
. Then one can define a quandle structure on
G
by
x\triangleright y=f(xy^{-1})y
. It is called a
generalized Alexander quandle
. If
G
is abelian, the operation becomes
x\triangleright y=f(x)+(Id-f)(y)
, where
Id
stands for the identity map. This quandle is called an
Alexander quandle
.

Example 2.6
.

The set
\mathcal{R}_{n}=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, with quandle operation
x\triangleright y=2y-x
(
mod

n)
is a quandle, called a
dihedral
quandle. This is an Alexander quandle on the the group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
where
f
is multiplication by
-1
.

For the remainder of this manuscript,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
will denote the dihedral quandle of order
n
.

3.
Representation theory

As mentioned in the introduction, the results here will be included in a later manuscript providing more results for the (complex) representation theory of (finite) quandles. The terminology used here to describe this theory is adapted from group representation theory, a topic for which there is a wealth of literature. There are many accessible introductions to this topic; see, for example, Chapters 4 and 5 of
[
MR2808160
]
.

All vector spaces henceforth are defined over
\mathbb{C}
. For any positive integer
k
, we set
\omega_{k}=e^{2\pi i/k}
. A
representation
of a finite quandle
(X,*)
on a finite dimensional complex vector space
V
is a quandle homomorphism
\rho:X\rightarrow\text{Conj}(GL(V))
. In other words, for all
x,y\in X
, we have
\rho(x*y)=\rho(y)\rho(x)\rho(y)^{-1}.
To simplify the notation, we will denote
\rho(x)
by just
\rho_{x}
. Let
V
and
W
be two representations of a quandle
X
. If
\rho^{V}:X\rightarrow\text{Conj}(GL(V))
and
\rho^{W}:X\rightarrow\text{Conj}(GL(W))
are two representations of
X
, then a linear map
\phi:V\rightarrow W
for which the following square commutes

\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{V}_{x}}
\textstyle{W\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{W}_{x}}
\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\textstyle{W.}

for all
x
is called an intertwiner. If, furthermore,
\phi
is an isomorphism then we say that the two representations are isomorphic as representations of
X
, or
equivalent
. If
\phi:V\hookrightarrow W
is the inclusion of the linear subspace
V
into
W
, then we say that
V
is a
subrepresentation
of
W
. Equivalently, a subspace
U
of
W
is a subrepresentation of
W
if
U
is invariant under
\rho^{W}(X)
.

A representation
V
of a quandle
X
is called
irreducible
if its only quandle subrepresentations are
\{0\}
and
V
. A representation ia called
indecompasable
if it cannot be written as direct sum of nontrivial subrepresentations.

If
\phi:\text{Inn}(X)\rightarrow\text{Aut}(V)
is a group homomorphism, then the induced map

\tilde{\phi}:X\rightarrow\text{Conj}(\text{Aut}(V)),

\tilde{\phi}(x):=\phi(R_{x}),

is a quandle homomorphism. Therefore any group representation of
\text{Inn}(X)
induces a quandle representation of
X
.

4.
The regular representation of a quandle

For any finite quandle
X
, we denote by
\mathbb{C}X
the
\mathbb{C}
–vector space of
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions on
X
; equivalently it is the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space generated by basis vectors
\left\{e_{x}\right\}_{x\in X}
, whose elements are formal sums
f=\sum_{x\in X}a_{x}e_{x}
,
a_{x}\in\mathbb{C}
. The
regular representation
of
X
is

\lambda:X\rightarrow\text{Conj}(GL(\mathbb{C}X)),

where
\lambda_{t}(f)(x):=f(R_{t}^{-1}(x))
. This action of
X
is equivalent to the right
X
–action on
\mathbb{C}X
given by the linear extension of
e_{x}\cdot t=e_{R_{t}(x)}=e_{x*t}
. We note that a subspace
W
of
\mathbb{C}X
is a quandle subrepresentation of
\mathbb{C}X
if and only if
W
is a subrepresentation of
\mathbb{C}X
as a
group
representation of
\operatorname{Inn}(X)
. If a distinction is necessary, we will refer to a vector space as either a
quandle representation
or a
group representation
. If two vector spaces
U,V
are isomorphic as quandle representations of
X
, we will write
U\cong_{X}V
; if they are isomorphic as group representations of some group
G
, we will write
U\cong_{G}V
.

When
|X|\geq 2
, The regular representation always has two nontrivial subrepresentations, one of which is always irreducible: the one–dimensional subspace
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the constant function
\mathbf{1}(x)=1
, for all
x\in X
. Its vector space complement,
(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1})^{\perp}
, is also a subrepresentation, since
(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1})^{\perp}=\left\{\sum_{x\in X}a_{x}e_{x}|\;\sum a_{x}=0\right\}
, and action by any
t\in X
only permutes the coefficients
a_{x}
. So
\mathbb{C}X
decomposes into a direct sum of subrepresentations

\mathbb{C}X=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}\oplus(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1})^{\perp}.

For
x,y\in X
let
v_{xy}=e_{x}-e_{y}
. If we enumerate
X=\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\right\}
, then a basis for
(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1})^{\perp}
is
\left\{v_{x_{1}x_{2}},v_{x_{2}x_{3}},\ldots,v_{x_{n-1}x_{n}}\right\}
.

5.
The regular representation of a dihedral quandle

The rest of this paper is devoted to the regular quandle representation of a dihedral quandle
\mathcal{R}_{n}
. We first explicitly describe some quandle representations of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

Since quandle subrepresentations of
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
are in correspondence with group subrepresentations of
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
(under the action of
\text{Inn}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
), we obtain the full decomposition of
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
as a quandle representation of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
by finding the decomposition as a group representation of
\text{Inn}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
. We note that this correspondence between group and quandle representations does not hold in general, but it is
characteristic
of the regular representation. In general, there is no reason to expect any correspondence or similarity between quandle representations of a quandle
X
and group representations of
\text{Inn}(X)
.

By virtue of the aforementioned correspondence, and the fact that the group
\text{Inn}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
is a dihedral group, we will consider in detail the representation theory of dihedral groups.
We will let
D_{m}
be the dihedral group of order
2m
. Then it is shown in
[
EMR
]
that

\text{Inn}(\mathcal{R}_{n})\cong\begin{cases}D_{n/2},&n\text{ even},\\
D_{n},&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

We describe the (isomorphism classes of) finite–dimensional irreducible group representations of the dihedral group
D_{m}
here, to facilitate statement of results below. The dihedral group
D_{m}
has a presentation given by

D_{m}=\left\langle\alpha,\beta\;\;|\;\;|\alpha|=|\beta|=2,\;|\alpha\beta|=m\right\rangle.

We fix generators
\alpha,\beta
for this presentation. The classification of the finite–dimensional irreducible group representations of
D_{m}
falls into two cases:
m
even and
m
odd. If
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, we will denote by
\mathbb{C}(\lambda,\mu)
the one–dimensional group representation of
D_{m}
on which
\alpha
and
\beta
act by scalar multiplication by
\lambda
and
\mu
, respectively. And for nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we will denote by
W(\lambda)
the two–dimensional group representation of
D_{m}
for which the matrix representations of
\alpha
and
\beta
are

\alpha\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;\;\beta\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda\\
\lambda^{-1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

With this notation fixed, we provide the classification of the finite–dimensional irreducible group representations of
D_{m}

\Gamma(D_{8})=\left\{\mathbb{C}(\pm 1,\pm 1),\;\;\mathbb{C}(\pm 1,\mp 1),\;\;W(\omega_{8}),\;\;W(\omega_{8}^{2}),\;\;W(\omega_{8}^{3})\right\},

\Gamma(D_{9})=\left\{\mathbb{C}(\pm 1,\pm 1),\;\;W(\omega_{9}),\;\;W(\omega_{9}^{2}),\;\;W(\omega_{9}^{3}),\;\;W(\omega_{9}^{4})\right\}.

Since
\text{Inn}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
is a dihedral group generated by

and

\mathcal{R}_{n}

\mathbb{C}(\lambda,\mu)

for which
R_{1}

R_{1}

have matrix representations

R_{1}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\

Now we consider the regular quandle representation
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
and the subrepresentation
(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1})^{\perp}
, in cases
n

(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1})^{\perp}
is spanned by
\left\{v_{ij}\right\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

If

is even,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
has two orbits: the even orbit
\left\{2,4,\ldots,2r\right\}
, and the odd orbit
\left\{1,3,\ldots,2r-1\right\}

\Phi_{n,0}=\text{sp}\left\{v_{ij}\right\}_{i\neq j\text{ even}},\;\;\;\Delta_{n,0}=\left\{v_{24},v_{46},\ldots,v_{2r-2\;2r}\right\},

\Phi_{n,1}=\text{sp}\left\{v_{ij}\right\}_{i\neq j\text{ odd}},\;\;\;\Delta_{n,1}=\left\{v_{13},v_{35},\ldots,v_{2r-3\;2r-1}\right\}.

\Phi_{n,0}

\Phi_{n,1}

\Delta_{n,i}

\Phi_{n,i}

If
n
is odd,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
has a single orbit. In this case, we set

\Phi_{n}=\text{sp}\left\{v_{ij}\right\}_{i\neq j},\;\;\;\Delta_{n}=\left\{v_{12},v_{23},\ldots,v_{n-1\;n}\right\},

and
\Delta_{n}

\Phi_{n}

\mathcal{R}_{n}

\Phi_{n,i}

\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

.

If

of

\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}\cong_{\mathcal{R}_{n}}\mathbb{C}(1,1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus\mathbb{C}(\pm 1,\mp 1)\bigoplus_{s=1}^{n/4-1}W(\omega_{n/2}^{s})^{\oplus 2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{s}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left[1-\omega_{r}^{s},1-(\omega_{r}^{s})^{2},\cdots,1-(\omega_{r}^{s})^{r-1}\right]_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\left(1-(\omega_{r}^{s})^{i}\right)v_{2i-1,2i+1},\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\mathbf{v}_{s}:=\omega_{r}^{-s}R_{n/2}(\mathbf{u}_{s}).

Then we can show that
R_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{s})=\mathbf{v}_{s}
, and since

R_{2}=R_{n/2+2}=R_{n/2\,\triangleright 1}=R_{1}R_{n/2}R_{1}^{-1}=R_{1}R_{n/2}R_{1},

we have
R_{2}(\mathbf{u}_{s})=R_{1}R_{n/2}R_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{s})=\omega_{r}^{s}\mathbf{v}_{s}
. For
1\leq s\leq r-1
, we define
U_{s,1}
to be the subspace of
\Phi_{n,1}
spanned by
\mathbf{u}_{s}
and
\mathbf{v}_{s}
. Then, as above, we have

\dim(U_{s,1})=\begin{cases}1,&s=r/2,\,\,r\text{ even}\\
2,&s\neq r/2,\end{cases}

and for
s\neq r/2
, the matrix representations of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are

\left[R_{1}\right]_{\left\{\mathbf{u}_{s},\mathbf{v}_{s}\right\}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;\;\left[R_{2}\right]_{\left\{\mathbf{u}_{s},\mathbf{v}_{s}\right\}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega_{r}^{s}\\
\omega_{r}^{-s}&0\end{bmatrix}.

This shows

\Phi_{n,1}\cong\bigoplus_{s=1}^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}U_{s,1}\cong_{\mathcal{R}_{n}}\begin{cases}W(\omega_{r})\oplus\cdots\oplus W\left(\omega_{r}^{r/2-1}\right)\oplus\mathbb{C}(1,-1),&r\;\text{even},\\
W(\omega_{r})\oplus\cdots\oplus W\left(\omega_{r}^{(r-1)/2}\right),&r\;\text{odd}.\end{cases}

For
r
even (
n\equiv 0\bmod 4
), the one-dimensional quandle subrepresentations of
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
are:

\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}\cong\mathbb{C}(1,1),\;\;\;\;\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathbf{1}}\cong\mathbb{C}(1,1),\;\;\;U_{r/2,0}\cong\mathbb{C}(-1,1),\;\;\;U_{r/2,1}\cong\mathbb{C}(1,-1),

and the two-dimensional irreducible quandle subrepresentations are
U_{s,0}\cong U_{s,1}\cong W(\omega_{r}^{s})
,
1\leq s\leq r/2-1
.

For
r
odd (
n\equiv 2\bmod 4)
, the one-dimensional quandle subrepresentations of
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}
are:

\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}\cong\mathbb{C}(1,1)\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathbf{1}}\cong\mathbb{C}(1,1),

and the two-dimensional irreducible quandle subrepresentations are
U_{s,0}\cong U_{s,1}\cong W(\omega_{r}^{s})
,
1\leq s\leq(r~-~1)~/~2
.

Case 2:

n
odd. Let
n=2r+1
. With respect to the basis
\Delta_{n}=\left\{v_{12},v_{23},\ldots,v_{n-1,n}\right\}
, the elements
R_{1}
,
R_{2}\in\operatorname{Inn}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
have matrix representations

\left[R_{1}\right]_{\Delta_{n}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&-1&1\\
0&\cdots&-1&0&1\\
\vdots&\reflectbox{$\ddots$}&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
-1&0&\cdots&0&1\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;\;\left[R_{2}\right]_{\Delta_{n}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
1&0&\cdots&0&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\reflectbox{$\ddots$}&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
1&-1&\cdots&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

For
1\leq s\leq n-1
, let

\mathbf{u}_{s}=\left[1-\omega_{n}^{s},1-(\omega_{n}^{s})^{2},\cdots,1-(\omega_{n}^{s})^{n-1}\right],\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\mathbf{v}_{s}=R_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{s}).

We can then show that
\mathbf{v}_{s}=\omega_{n}^{-s}\left(\omega_{n}^{-s}-1,(\omega_{n}^{-s})^{2}-1,\ldots,(\omega_{n}^{-s})^{n-1}-1\right)
.

Let
U_{s}
be the subspace of
\Phi_{n}
spanned by
\mathbf{u}_{s}
and
\mathbf{v}_{s}
. We can show that
\mathbf{u}_{s}=\mathbf{v}_{n-s}
, hence
U_{s}=U_{n-s}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle R_{2}(\mathbf{u}_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{2}(1-\omega_{n}^{s},1-(\omega_{n}^{s})^{2},\ldots,1-(\omega_{n}^{s})^{n-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\omega_{n}^{s},(\omega_{n}^{s})^{n-1}-\omega_{n}^{s},(\omega_{n}^{s})^{n-2}-\omega_{n}^{s},\ldots,(\omega_{n}^{s})^{2}-\omega_{n}^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{2s}\omega_{n}^{-s}(\omega_{n}^{-s}-1,(\omega_{n}^{-s})^{2}-1,\ldots,(\omega_{n}^{-s})^{n-1}-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{2s}\mathbf{v}_{s}.

Therefore, if
\mathbf{u}_{s}
and
\mathbf{v}_{s}
are linearly independent, the matrix representations of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
with respect to the basis
\left\{\mathbf{u}_{s},\mathbf{v}_{s}\right\}
of
U_{s}
are again

\left[R_{1}\right]_{\left\{\mathbf{u}_{s},\mathbf{v}_{s}\right\}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;\;\left[R_{2}\right]_{\left\{\mathbf{u}_{s},\mathbf{v}_{s}\right\}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega_{n}^{2s}\\
\omega_{n}^{-2s}&0\end{bmatrix},

We can show linear independence of
\mathbf{u}_{s}
and
\mathbf{v}_{s}
for
1\leq s\leq r
, which gives us the decomposition

(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1})^{\perp}=\bigoplus_{s=1}^{r}U_{s},

and we obtain the desired decomposition into irreducible representations

\mathbb{C}\mathcal{R}_{n}\cong\mathbb{C}(1,1)\bigoplus_{s=1}^{r}W(\omega_{n}^{2s}).

∎
