Stability, bounded generation and strong boundedness
Alexander A. Trost

Abstract
. We provide bounds linear in the rank for the generalized conjugacy diameters, introduced by Kedra, Libman and Martin, for the special linear and symplectic groups defined over the rings of integers of global fields by way of using certain stability considerations familiar from classical algebraic K-theory. This determines the growth rates of these generalized conjugacy diameters.



1.
Introduction

In finite groups the speed of generation by non-trivial conjugacy classes for finite simple groups has been extensively investigated under the term of covering numbers
[
1
,
17
]
. Similar considerations for non-finite, non-simple groups lead to the study of conjugation-invariant word norms on groups first introduced in
[
7
]
. If all conjugation-invariant norms on a group
G
have finite diameters (such groups
G
are called
bounded
). Conjugation-invariant norms on arithmetic Chevalley groups (more specifically
{\rm SL}_{n\geq 3}(\mathbb{Z})
) were one of the first non-trivial examples considered in
[
7
, Example 1.1]
where it was shown that
{\rm SL}_{n\geq 3}(\mathbb{Z})
is bounded.

A more precise question concerning conjugation-invariant norms is how the diameter of conjugation-invariant word norms
\|\cdot\|_{T}
induced by finitely many, generating conjugacy classes
T
of
{\rm SL}_{n\geq 3}(\mathbb{Z})
depend on the set
T:
One of the first such results was proven by Morris
[
20
]
and Kedra, Libman and Martin
[
14
]
and shows that
{\rm SL}_{n\geq 3}(\mathbb{Z})
for example satisfy the stronger property of being
strongly bounded
; that is for a group
G
and each natural number
k
, there is a bound
\Delta_{k}(G)\in\mathbb{N}
such that each word norm
\|\cdot\|_{T}
given by
k
generating conjugacy classes
T
of
G
has diameter at most
\Delta_{k}(G)
:

Theorem 1.1
.

[
14
, Corollary 6.2]

Let
R:=\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}
be the ring of S-algebraic integers in a number field whose class number is one and
n\geq 3.
Then
{\rm SL}_{n}({\mathcal{O}})
is normally generated by any elementary matrix
E_{ij}(1)
and
k\leq\Delta_{k}({\rm SL}_{n}(\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}))\leq(4n+51)(4n+4)k
holds for all
k\in\mathbb{N}.

We have obtained similar results for other arithmetic Chevalley groups
\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,R))

[
31
,
30
,
28
]
, but a common issue of all such prior results is that the upper bounds on
\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,R))
are either non-explicit or don’t agree with the known lower bounds not even in regards to their growth behavior: For example in Theorem
1.1
, the upper bound on
\frac{\Delta_{k}({\rm SL}_{n}(R))}{k}\leq(4n+51)(4n+4)
is quadratic in
n,
but the best known lower bound on
\frac{\Delta_{k}({\rm SL}_{n}(R))}{k}
is precisely
n.
In this paper, we will solve this issue (at least for some Chevalley groups) by showing that there are also upper bounds on
\frac{\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,R))}{k}
which are linear in
{\rm rank}(\Phi):

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K
be a global field and
S
a finite non-empty set of valuations of
K
containing all archimedean valuations of
K
. Let
R:=\mathscr{O}_{K}^{S}
be the ring of all S-algebraic integers of
K.
Further, let
\Phi\in\{A_{n\geq 2},C_{n\geq 2},E_{6},E_{7},E_{8},F_{4},G_{2}\}
be given. Then there is a constant
C\in\mathbb{N}
independent of
\Phi,K
and
S
such that for all sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N},
the following chain of inequalities holds:
k\cdot{\rm rank}(\Phi)\leq\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,R))\leq Ck\cdot{\rm rank}(\Phi).

Unfortunately, how large
k
has to be precisely is quite technical and depends very much on the underlying global field
K
. But even disregarding the situation for large
k,
the proof of Theorem
1.2
will produce upper bounds on
\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}))
of the form
{\rm rank}(\Phi)\cdot C(K)+Dk\cdot{\rm rank}(\Phi)
with
C(K)
some constant depending on the number field (in an explicit way in most cases) and
D
some (usually explicit) universal constant. In particular, the threshold on
k
for universal upper bounds on
\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,R))
does not depend on the root system but only on the global field in question.

The main difference between the proof of Theorem
1.2
and earlier results like Theorem
1.1
and our results from
[
30
]
is a difference in viewpoint: In the earlier papers, one proceeds along a two-step process: One first produces from a given collection of generating conjugacy classes
T
of
G(\Phi,\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)})
a specific generating set, the so-called root elements and then one applies bounded generation results for these root elements. This two-step strategy is somewhat inefficient; the first step needs linear in
{\rm rank}(\Phi)
many elements from
T
and the second step requires
{\rm rank}(\Phi)
many conjugates of root elements. Together this results in the quadratic bounds as in Theorem
1.1
.

In our strategy, we instead produce from a single non-central element
B
of
T
, a congruence subgroup
C(\Phi,I)
of
G(\Phi,\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)})
sitting in a
\|\cdot\|_{T}
-ball of radius linear in
{\rm rank}(\Phi)
(already this step is non-trivial). We then use this congruence subgroup to transform the problem of understanding
\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}))
into the problem of understanding
\Delta_{k}(G(\Phi,R_{I}))
for a semi-local ring
R_{I}
arising essentially as the
I
-adic completion of
\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}
following an idea similar to one in
[
3
]
. For such semi-local rings the
\Delta_{k}
are much better understood from an asymptotic view point (
[
14
, Theorem 6.3]
and
[
30
, Theorem 2]
) and this fact together with a simple commutator trick to treat the direct factors of
R_{I}
all at once proves Theorem
1.2
. Along the way of the proof, we will also prove the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
F
be an algebraic extension of a finite field,
C
a nonsingular, geometrically integral, affine curve defined over
F
,
U
a proper, non-empty Zariski-open set in
C
defined over
F
. Further, let
R:=F_{C}[U]=\mathscr{O}_{C}(U)
be the ring of regular functions on
U
and
\Phi\in\{A_{n\geq 2},C_{n\geq 2},E_{6},E_{7},E_{8},F_{4},G_{2}\}
. Then there is a constant
D
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

{\rm rank}(\Phi)\cdot k\leq\Delta_{k}\left(G(\Phi,R)\right)\leq D\cdot{\rm rank}(\Phi)\cdot k.

and this constant
D
does not depend on
F,C,U
or
\Phi.
Further, for
\Phi=A_{n\geq 3},C_{n\geq 4},
one can choose
D
as less than
333900.

Lastly, one of the main ingredients (and the one used to actually controll the diameter of
C(\Phi,I)
with respect
\|\cdot\|_{T}
in the proof of Theorem
1.2
) is a more general version of the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
R
and
\Phi
be as in Theorem
1.3
and
I
be an ideal in
R
. Then there is a constant such that each element of
\bar{E}(\Phi,I)
can be written as a product of
L\cdot{\rm rank}(\Phi)
many elements from the set
\left\{A\varepsilon_{\phi}(x)A^{-1}\mid A\in G(\Phi,R),x\in I,\phi\in\Phi\right\}
and this constant
L
does not depend on
F,C,U
or
I
.

2.
Basic definitions and notions

2.1.
Chevalley groups and congruence subgroups

The main subject of this paper are simply-connected split Chevalley groups defined over certain rings of integers of global (and local) fields and conjugation-invariant word norms defined on these groups. Chevalley groups
G(\Phi,\cdot)
are certain algebraic groups (cf.
[
25
,
31
]
for more details).

For
\Phi
an irreducible root system and
R
a commutative ring with
1,
the simply-connected Chevalley group
G(\Phi,R)
contains the so-called
root elements
modulo a choice of a maximal torus in
G(\Phi,\mathbb{C})
: For each
\phi\in\Phi
and
x\in R
, there are elements
\varepsilon_{\phi}(x)\in G(\Phi,R)
. These root elements satisfy a couple of commutator formulas (called
Steinberg commutator relations
, cf.
[
12
, Proposition 33.3-5]
). We further need two different congruence subgroups of
G(\Phi,R)
:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with
1
,
I
a proper non-zero ideal of
R
and
\Phi
an irreducible root system. Than the kernel
C(\Phi,I)
of the group homomorphism
G(\Phi,R)\to G(\Phi,R/I)
induced by the quotient map
R\to R/I
is called the
principal
I
-congruence subgroup of
G(\Phi,R)
. The subgroup
\bar{E}(\Phi,I)
of
G(\Phi,R)
generated by the set
\{A\varepsilon_{\phi}(x)A^{-1}\mid x\in I,A\in G(\Phi,R)\}=:Z(I,\Phi)
is called the
elementary
I
-congruence subgroup of
G(\Phi,R).

Remark 2.2
.

It is more common to consider
\bar{E}(\Phi,I)
to be a normal subgroup of the so-called elementary Chevalley group
E(\Phi,R)
generated by all root elements of
G(\Phi,R)
, but we are only interested in rings with
G(\Phi,R)=E(\Phi,R)
in this paper.

We mostly consider the two root systems
\Phi=A_{n}
and
C_{n}.
For these the corresponding Chevalley groups
G(A_{n},R)
and
G(C_{n},R)
are the two well-known matrix groups
{\rm SL}_{n+1}(R):=\{A\in R^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}\mid\det(A)=1\}
and

{\rm Sp}_{2n}(R):=\{A\in R^{n\times n}\mid A^{T}\cdot J\cdot A=J\}\text{ for }J:=\begin{pmatrix}0_{n}&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

With respect to these identifications (and a suitable choice of maximal torus) the root elements in
G(A_{n},R)
can be chosen as
E_{ij}(x):=I_{n+1}+xe_{ij}
for
x\in R
and
1\leq i\neq j\leq n+1
with
e_{ij}
the matrix whose sole non-zero entry is at the position
(i,j).

In contrast the root elements in
{\rm Sp}_{2n}(R)
are slightly more complicated: The root elements associated to positive, short roots have the form
I_{n}+x(e_{ij}-e_{n+j,n+i}),I_{n}+x(e_{k,n+l}+e_{l,n+k})
for
n\geq j>i\geq 1,1\leq k,l\leq n,k\neq l
and
x\in R.
The positive long root elements have the form
I_{n}+xe_{k,n+k}
for
1\leq k\leq n
and
x\in R.
Additionally in
{\rm Sp}_{2n}(R)
, one has further negative root elements given by the transpose matrices of these positive root elements.

For
A=(a_{ij})\in{\rm GL}_{n}(R)
, one can further define the
level ideal
l(A)\unlhd R
of
A
as the ideal in
R
generated by the elements
a_{ij}
and
a_{ii}-a_{jj}
. This definition of
l(A)
is obviously sensible for elements of
G(A_{n},R)
and
G(C_{n},R)
given the explicit description as matrix groups above, but as seen in
[
31
, Section 2]
, this definition makes sense also for general
G(\Phi,R)
modulo a suitable representation of the corresponding
G(\Phi,\mathbb{C})
.

In this context, we will to simplify notation refer to both elements
a_{ij}
and
a_{ii}-a_{jj}
of
R
as
entries of
A
for for
A\in G(\Phi,R)
. We further define the ideal
l(T)\unlhd R
for
T\subset G(\Phi,R)
as the sum of the ideals
l(A)
for
A\in T.
Further, we recall the notation

\Pi(T):=\{{\mathcal{P}}\text{ maximal ideal in }R\mid T\text{ maps into }Z\left(G(\Phi,G/{\mathcal{P}})\right)\}

for
T\subset G(\Phi,R)
from
[
31
]
. Especially in Section
4
, we will often consider the same set
T
as being contained in different
G(\Phi,R)
for varying rings
R.
To avoid confusion, we will consequently often write
\Pi_{R}(T)
and
l_{R}(T)
to make clear which
G(\Phi,R)
is considered at any given point.

2.2.
Conjugation-invariant norms and generalized conjugacy diameters

The main object of study in this preprint are generalized conjugacy diameters on Chevalley groups:

Definition 2.3
.

[
14
, P. 1]

Let
G
be a group and
T
a collection of conjugacy classes in
T
generating
G.
Then for
g\in G
, the number
\|g\|_{T}
denotes length of the shortest word in elements of
T
and
T^{-1}
needed to represent
g.
Further,

(1)

the number
\|G\|_{T}:=\sup\{\|g\|_{T}\mid g\in G\}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called the
diameter of
G
wrt
\|\cdot\|_{T}
.

(2)

for
k\in\mathbb{N},
the set
B_{T}(k):=\{g\in G\mid\|g\|_{T}\leq k\}\cup\{1_{G}\}
is called the
k
-ball wrt
\|\cdot\|_{T}.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
.th generalized conjugacy diameter
\Delta_{k}(G)
of
G
is defined by

\Delta_{k}(G):=\sup\{\|G\|_{T}\mid T\text{ a collection of at most }k\text{ conjugacy classes in }G\text{ generating }G\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}

We further note that if
T
consists of only one conjugacy class
B^{G}
for
B\in G
, than we will write
\|\cdot\|_{B}
and
B_{B}(k)
instead of
\|\cdot\|_{\{B^{G}\}}
or
B_{\{B^{G}\}}(k)
for simplicity. Next, we introduce the following notation from
[
14
,
31
]
that is useful when discussing conjugation-invariant word norms on Chevalley groups:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with
1
,
\Phi
an irreducible root system,
\phi\in\Phi,\phi_{s}\in\Phi
short,
\phi_{l}\in\Phi
long,
T
a collection of conjugacy classes in
G(\Phi,R)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}.
We then set

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\phi}(T,k):=\{x\in R\mid\varepsilon_{\phi}(x)\in B_{T}(k)\},\varepsilon_{s}(T,k):=\varepsilon_{\phi_{s}}(T,k),\varepsilon_{l}(T,k):=\varepsilon_{\phi_{l}}(T,k)\text{ and }

\displaystyle\varepsilon(T,k):=\varepsilon_{s}(T,k)\cap\varepsilon_{l}(T,k).

Remark 2.5
.

There is the obvious question why the sets
\varepsilon_{s}(T,k),\varepsilon_{l}(T,k)
and
\varepsilon(T,k)
are well-defined. This can be seen by considering the action of the Weyl group
W(\Phi)
on root elements.

Similar to the notation of
\Pi_{R}(T)
and
l_{R}(T),
we also use the clarifying notation of
B_{T}^{(R)}(k)
for the
k
-ball
B_{T}(k)\subset G(\Phi,R)
when there are multiple different relevant rings
R
at play.

2.3.
Rings of integers and stable range conditions

Next, we recall the definition of global and local fields and their rings of integers.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
K
be a field.

(1)

A surjective homomorphism
v:(K^{*},\cdot)\to\mathbb{Z}
is called a
non-archimedean valuation of
K
, if it satisfies
v(a+b)\geq\min\{v(a),v(b)\}
for all
a,b\in K^{*}
.(One further sets
v(0):=+\infty
in this context.) The set
\mathscr{O}_{v}:=\{a\in K\mid v(a)\geq 0\}
is called the
ring of integers of
v
,
m_{v}:=\{a\in K\mid v(a)>0\}

its maximal ideal
and
\kappa_{v}:=\mathscr{O}_{v}/m_{v}
its
residue field
.

(2)

K
is called a
local field
, iff it is
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
or if it admits a non-archimedean valuation
v
such that
\kappa_{v}
is finite and
K
is complete wrt. to the topology induced by
v
on it. In this case, the ring of integers of the local field
K
is
\mathscr{O}_{v}.

(3)

A
real archimedean valuation of
K
is a field homomorphism
K\to\mathbb{R}
and a
complex archimedean valuation of
K
is a complex-conjugate pair of field homomorphisms
K\to\mathbb{C}.
The set of all these archimedean valuations is denoted by
S_{\infty}.

(4)

K
is called a
global field
if it is a finite field extension of either
\mathbb{Q}
or of the field of rational function
\mathbb{F}(T)
for
\mathbb{F}
a finite field. Finite extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
are called
number fields
and finite extensions of
\mathbb{F}(T)
are called
global function fields
.

(5)

If
K
is a number field, then its
ring of (
S_{\infty}
-)algebraic integers
\mathscr{O}_{K}:=\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S_{\infty})}
is the integral closure of
\mathbb{Z}
in
K.

(6)

If
K
is a number field not admitting an embedding
K\to\mathbb{R},
then
K
is called a
totally imaginary number field.

(7)

If
K
is a global field and
S
a non-empty, finite set of valuations of
K
properly containing
S_{\infty}
, then
the ring
\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}
of
S
-algebraic integers of
K
is defined as

\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}:=\{a\in K\mid\forall v\in S-S_{\infty}:v(a)\geq 0\}.

We also want to note that each number field has at least one archimedean valuation (because it is algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}
and so contained in the algebraically closed field
\mathbb{C}
). Further, for any finite, non-empty set of valuations
S
of a global function field
K
, there is also a (non-unique) element
x_{S}\in K
such that
S=\{v\text{ valuation of K}\mid v(x_{S})<0\}
and for all such
x_{S}
, the ring
\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}
is precisely the integral closure of the ring
\mathbb{F}[x_{S}]
in
K.
(A proof of this can be found in
[
21
, Theorem 14.5]
.)
We will also need various stable range concepts of rings a couple of times in the paper:

Definition 2.7
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with
1.
Then for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the ring
R

(1)

has (Bass) stable range at most
k,
if for all
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in R
with
(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=R
, there are
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in R
such that
(a_{1}+x_{1}a_{0},a_{2}+x_{2}a_{0},\dots,a_{k}+x_{k}a_{0})=R.

(2)

satisfies the absolute stable range condition
ASR(k),
iff for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in R
, there are
x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}
such that each maximal ideal
{\mathcal{P}}
of R containing the ideal
(a_{1}+x_{1}a_{k},a_{2}+x_{2}a_{k}\dots,a_{k-1}+x_{k-1}a_{k})
also contains
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}).

These concepts are quite classical. For example, Bass stable range was introduced by Bass in
[
6
]
(albeit indexed differently) and absolute stable range by Stein
[
24
]
.

3.
Bounded generation by conjugates of root elements in Chevalley groups

The main goal of this section is the following variant of Theorem
1.4
we are going to use to prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
4
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
K
be a global field and
S
a finite non-empty set of valuations of
K
containing all archimedean valuations of
K
. Let
R:=\mathscr{O}_{K}^{S}
be the ring of all S-algebraic integers of
K.
Further, let
\Phi\in\{A_{n\geq 2},C_{n\geq 2},E_{6},E_{7},E_{8},F_{4},G_{2}\}
be given and let
I
be a non-zero ideal in
R
.
If
R
is a principal ideal domain or
K
is a global function field set
\Delta:=\Delta(K):=1.
For other number fields
K
, let
\Delta:=\Delta(K)
be the number of prime divisors of the discriminant of
K
. Assume further, if
K
is a number field that
K
has a real embedding or
I
is coprime to the number
m(K)
of roots of unity of
K
, when considering
m(K)
as an element of
R.
Further, set
L_{A}:=L_{A}(K):=68\Delta(K)+11
and
L_{C}:=L_{C}(K):=180\Delta(K)+25.

Then

\|\bar{E}(\Phi,I)\|_{Z(I,\Phi)}\leq\begin{cases}L_{A}+8(n-2)&\text{, if }\Phi=A_{n},\\
L_{C}+9(n-2)&\text{, if }\Phi=C_{n},\\
L_{A}|\Phi|&\text{, if }\Phi=E_{6},E_{7},E_{8},\\
48{\max\{L_{A},L_{C}\}}&\text{, if }\Phi=F_{4},\\
L_{A}+21&\text{, if }\Phi=G_{2}\end{cases}

holds.

Remark 3.2
.

The
A_{2}
-case for number fields is almost directly taken from
[
11
, Corollary 3.3]
and the result for the other roots system also uses the strategy from
[
11
]
. However, one could avoid
[
11
]
entirely and replace it with results from
[
20
,
27
]
. This approach could still be used to prove Theorem
1.2
, but it would be completely unclear how large
k
has to be chosen beyond the vague fact that it has to be chosen larger than some threshold determined by an unknown function admitting only the root system
\Phi
and the numbers
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
(in the number field case) and
[K:\mathbb{F}(T)],|\mathbb{F}|
(in the global function field case) as arguments. Using the approach from
[
11
]
produces explicit thresholds (at least in most cases) and makes it clear which quantities have to be determined to make non-explicit threshold functions explicit. We discuss this issue in more detail after the proof of Theorem
1.2
on P.
4.7

Before coming to proof of Theorem
3.1
, we want to note that Theorem
1.4
is a consequence of it: First, in Theorem
1.4
, we may assume that the field
F
is finite: The ring
R
is a localization of a noetherian ring, so noetherian itself. Hence
I
is finitely generated. Further, if
X\in C(\Phi,I)
is given, then there is a finite field extension
\mathbb{E}
of
\mathbb{F}
contained in
F
such that the following three conditions hold:

(1)

The curve
C
and the Zariski-open set
U
are already defined over
\mathbb{E},

(2)

The set of generators
M
of the ideal
I
is already contained in the subring
S:=\mathbb{E}[U]
of
R
and

(3)

considering the ideal
J:=M\cdot S
, one has that
X
is an element of the subgroup
C(\Phi,J)
of
G(\Phi,S).

Thus if we can prove Theorem
1.4
for
S
and
J
, we obtain it for Theorem
1.4
given that
X
was arbitrary and so Theorem
1.4
indeed follows from Theorem
3.1
.

The main definition needed to prove Theorem
3.1
is the double of a ring:

Definition 3.3
.

Let
R
be a ring and
I
a proper ideal in
R.
Then define
\tilde{R}_{I}
(or
\tilde{R}_{I}
, if there is no risk of confusion) as the subring of the cartesian product ring
R^{2}
given by the following subset
\{(a,b)\in R^{2}\mid a\equiv bI\}.

Remark 3.4
.

These rings were first introduced in Stein’s paper
[
23
]
in which he studies the stability properties of these type of rings. We will quote and use some of these results in Subsubsection
3.1.3
below.

For a commutative ring
R
with
1
and an irreducible root system, the number
\|E(\Phi,R)\|_{E}
denotes the diameter of
E(\Phi,R)
wrt the (
non-conjugation-invariant
) word norm given by its generating set of root elements. One proves Theorem
3.1
by connecting it to bounded elementary generation of
G(\Phi,\tilde{R})
via the following lemma:

Lemma 3.5
.

[
11
, Proof of Corollary 3.3]

Let
R
be a commutative ring with
1
and
I
a proper ideal in
R
and
K\in\mathbb{N}.
Then
\|E(\tilde{R},\Phi)\|_{E}\leq K
implies that
\|\bar{E}(\Phi,I)\|_{Z(I,\Phi)}\leq K.

The paper
[
11
]
only contains a special version of this fact in case of
\Phi=A_{2}

3.2.
Stability

The proof of Proposition
3.7
relies mostly on the following stability property that allows to rewrite large products of root elements to a product of just two modulo conjugation in
G(\Phi,R)
:

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
R
be a Dedekind domain and
n\geq 2.
Further, assume that
v\in R^{n}
. Then there is an element
A\in{\rm SL}_{n}(R)
such that
A\cdot v\in Re_{1}\oplus Re_{2}.

Proof.

We will assume that
v\neq 0,
as otherwise there is nothing to show. Consider the prime factorization
\prod_{j=1}^{L}{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{k_{j}}
of the ideal
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
in
R.
Next, pick using the Chinese Remainder Theorem a
y_{1}\in R
such that for all prime ideals
{\mathcal{P}}
not among the
{\mathcal{Q}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{Q}}_{L}
but dividing
(v_{2},v_{3},\dots,v_{n-1})
, one has

\displaystyle y_{1}\equiv 1\text{ mod }{\mathcal{P}}\text{, if }{\mathcal{P}}\text{ divides }v_{1}

\displaystyle y_{1}\equiv 0\text{ mod }{\mathcal{P}}\text{, if }{\mathcal{P}}\text{ does not divide }v_{1}.

(If
(v_{2},\dots,v_{n-1})
is
(0)
then one is done after multiplying with a suitable Weyl group element). Furthermore for
j=1,\dots,L
, the element
y_{1}
shall satisfy the following conditions

\displaystyle y_{1}\equiv 1\text{ mod }{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{k_{j}+1}\text{, if }v_{1}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{k_{j}+1}

\displaystyle y_{1}\equiv 0\text{ mod }{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{k_{j}+1}\text{, if }v_{1}\notin{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{k_{j}+1}.

Next, consider the ideal
J:=(v_{1}+y_{1}v_{n},v_{2},\dots,v_{n-1}).
Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a prime divisor of
J
. So
v_{2},\dots,v_{n-1}
are contained in
{\mathcal{P}}.
Assume for contradiction that
{\mathcal{P}}
is not among the
{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}.
If
v_{1}\notin{\mathcal{P}},
then
y_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}
by construction and so
v_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}},
a contradiction. Hence
v_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}.
But this implies that
y_{1}\equiv 1\text{ mod }{\mathcal{P}}
and so
v_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}.
Thus
{\mathcal{P}}
is a divisor of the ideal
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
, that is one of the
{\mathcal{Q}}_{j},
a contradiction. Hence
J=\prod_{j=1}^{L}{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{n_{j}}
for some
n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.
However, note that
J\subset(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
and so
n_{j}\geq k_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,l.
If say
n_{1}>k_{1}
were to hold however, then
v_{2},\dots,v_{n-1}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}.
If
v_{1}\notin{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}
, then
y_{1}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}
and so
v_{1}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1},
a contradiction. Hence
v_{1}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}
and so
y_{1}\equiv 1\text{ mod }{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}
and so
v_{n}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}.
Hence the ideal
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
is actually contained in
{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}
, a contradiction. So
J=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
holds. Hence the ideal
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
is already generated by the elements
v_{1}+y_{1}v_{n},v_{2},\dots,v_{n-1}.
Iterating this process, one can find further elements
y_{2},\dots,y_{n-3}
such that the ideal
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
is already generated by the two elements
v_{2}
and
v_{1}+\sum_{l=1}^{n-2}y_{l}\cdot v_{n-l+1}.
Hence considering the product
M\cdot v
for
M:=I_{n}+\sum_{l=1}^{n-2}y_{l}e_{1,n-l+1}
, one can assume that
v_{1},v_{2}
already generate the entire ideal
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}).
But then for each
j\geq 3
, we can find
c_{j},d_{j}\in R
such that
v_{j}+a_{j}\cdot v_{1}+b_{j}\cdot v_{2}=0.
Hence one obtains for
N:=I_{n}+\sum_{j=3}^{n}a_{j}\cdot e_{j,1}+b_{j}\cdot e_{j,2}
that
N\cdot v=(v_{1},v_{2},0,\dots,0)^{T}.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Obviously for specific examples of global fields

, but we will restrict ourselves to the following four:

Corollary 4.8

Let

be a global field,

a non-empty set of valuations in

containing all archimedean valuations of

and

the corresponding ring of S-algebraic integers. Further, assume that

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}({\rm SL}_{n}(R))\leq 3024+48n(k+1),\Delta_{k}({\rm SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq 3024+48n(k+1),

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}({\rm Sp}_{2n}(R))\leq 333900+28620n+6912nk\text{ and }\Delta_{k}({\rm Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq 333900+24804n+6912nk.

For the ring

5.
Closing Remarks and open problems

We want to point out that for
\Phi=A_{n\geq 2},C_{n\geq 4}
and all global fields

and rings of S-algebraic integers
R
, one can (combining the arguments from this paper and
[
14
,
30
]
) produce for all

upper bounds on

of the form

for an explicit number

. As we saw in Corollary

for global function fields there is linear bounds (in
n
) on
\Delta_{k}
for all
k
and so we ask instead for number fields:

Conjecture 5.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define the number

F(n):=\sup\left\{\frac{\Delta_{k}({\rm SL}_{n+2}(\mathscr{O}_{K}))}{k}\mid K\text{ a number field}\right\}.

What is the growth of the function

?

Clearly
n+2\leq F(n)\leq Zn^{2}
holds, but essentially the question is whether the number field
K
is relevant at all for the generalized conjugacy diameters.

It seems likely that combining the idea of considering the double from
[
11
]
with the arguments from
[
19
]
that one can find upper bounds on
\|\bar{E}(A_{2},I)\|_{Z(I,A_{2})}
(and consequently on

) completely independent of the number field if said number field is not totally imaginary, but it would be interesting to see this done explicitly. The hard case is the totally imaginary case though and here it seems doubtful (to me at least) that bounds on

independent of
K
and
I
exist. However, even bounds only dependent on the number
m(I)
from Theorem
4.2
would be highly interesting. This would also allow to rid Theorem
1.2
of the slightly artificial dependency on
\Delta(K).

Having such a result would also resolve the issues of
{\rm Sp}_{4}
and
G_{2}:
In the proof of Theorem
1.2
these two cases used a different bounded generation result from the other root systems (namely
[
27

and
[
20
, Corollary 3.13]
instead of Theorem
3.1
). This is due to the fact that the ideal
I
constructed in this case might not be coprime to the number
m(K)
of roots of unity in the number field
K
in question which prohibited the use of Theorem
3.1
in this case. However, if
\|\bar{E}(A_{2},I)\|_{Z(I,A_{2})}
(and presumably
\|\bar{E}(C_{2},I)\|_{Z(I,C_{2})}
and

) were controlled by
m(I)
than we would also obtain bounds on
\Delta_{k}
independent of the global field in case of
{\rm Sp}_{4}
and
G_{2}
, too. There remains also the case of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathscr{C}_{K}^{(S)})
for
(\mathscr{C}_{K}^{(S)}
a ring of S-integers with infinitely many units. The main problem here is to generalize the bounded generation results from
[
11
]
similar to
G_{2}
and
{\rm Sp}_{4}.

Disregarding these arithmetic difficulties, it would further be interesting whether one can determine better asymptotic bounds on, say,
\Delta_{k}({\rm SL}_{n}(\mathscr{O}_{K}^{(S)}))
. A first straightforward way of improving even our strategy it is to produce better upper bounds on

for a discrete valuation domain

than the bound

from

]
. A second way is finding a better method to reduce the integer case to the local case, besides the two-commutator trick we use in the proof of Theorem

.

Last, we should mention that while working on this preprint Chen Meiri and Nir Avni published a preprint which provides bounds on the conjugacy width of anisotropic orthogonal groups
[

and it would be interesting to see whether the methods they use can be used to produce bounds on

for

for groups
