A uniformly accurate scheme for the numerical integration of penalized Langevin dynamics
Adrien Laurent

Abstract
In molecular dynamics, penalized overdamped Langevin dynamics are used to model the motion of a set of particles that follow constraints up to a parameter ε \varepsilon . The most used schemes for simulating these dynamics are the Euler integrator in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} and the constrained Euler integrator. Both have weak order one of accuracy, but work properly only in specific regimes depending on the size of the parameter ε \varepsilon . We propose in this paper a new consistent method with an accuracy independent of ε \varepsilon for solving penalized dynamics on a manifold of any dimension. Moreover, this method converges to the constrained Euler scheme when ε \varepsilon goes to zero. The numerical experiments confirm the theoretical findings, in the context of weak convergence and for the invariant measure, on a torus and on the orthogonal group in high dimension and high codimension. Keywords: constrained stochastic differential equations, penalized Langevin dynamics, manifolds, uniform accuracy, invariant measure. AMS subject classification (2020): 60H35, 70H45, 37M25.

Introduction

In molecular dynamics, the overdamped Langevin equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is often used for modeling the behavior of a large set of particles in a high friction regime. It is given by

dX(t)=f(X(t))dt+\sigma dW(t),\quad X(0)=X_{0},

(1.1)

where
f
is a smooth Lipschitz function (typically of the form
f=-\nabla V
for
V
a smooth potential),
\sigma>0
is a constant scalar, and
W
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on a probability space equipped with a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
and fulfilling the usual assumptions.
If the particles are subject to smooth constraints
\zeta\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
, such as strong covalent bonds between atoms or fixed angles in molecules, the dynamics follow the constrained overdamped Langevin equation

dX^{0}(t)=\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(X^{0}(t))f(X^{0}(t))dt+\sigma\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(X^{0}(t))\circ dW(t),\quad X^{0}(0)=X_{0}\in\mathcal{M},

(1.2)

where the solution lies on the manifold
\mathcal{M}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\zeta(x)=0\}
with codimension
q
thanks to
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the orthogonal projection on the tangent bundle of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
.

In physical applications, constrained systems are often used as a limit model for stiff equations. For instance, in the dynamics of a diatomic molecule, the distance between the two atoms oscillates around an average length, called the bond length (see, for instance,
[
34
, Sect. 1.2.1]
on the interactions of particles). One can work with a simpler constrained dynamics where the distance between the atoms is fixed as a constraint, or with the original (possibly stiff) dynamics in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We refer the reader to
[
41
,
34
]
, and references therein, for discussions on the use of constraints and penalizations in molecular dynamics.
For overdamped Langevin dynamics (
1.1
), choosing a function
\widetilde{f}=-\nabla\widetilde{V}
with the potential

\widetilde{V}=V-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\ln(\det(G))+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left|\zeta\right|^{2}

gives penalized Langevin dynamics
[
16
]
of the form

dY^{\varepsilon}(t)=f(Y^{\varepsilon}(t))dt+\sigma dW(t)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\nabla\ln(\det(G))(Y^{\varepsilon}(t))dt-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(g\zeta)(Y^{\varepsilon}(t))dt,

(1.3)

where we fix
Y^{\varepsilon}(0)=X_{0}
, the parameter
\varepsilon>0
is fixed with arbitrary size,
f=-\nabla V
,
g=\nabla\zeta\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
, and
G=g^{T}g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
is the Gram matrix.
It was shown in
[
16
, Appx. C]
that the solution
Y^{\varepsilon}
of (
1.3
) converges strongly to the solution
X^{0}
of the constrained dynamics (
1.2
) if
X_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
.
The additional term
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\nabla\ln(\det(G))
is a correction term (called the Fixman correction) that is needed to obtain the convergence to the constrained dynamics (
1.2
) (see
[
34
, Sect. 3.2.3.4]
and references therein).
Thus, for
\varepsilon
small, the trajectory of the solution of (
1.3
) lies in the vicinity of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. This penalization can also appear naturally when simulating Langevin dynamics with a stiff potential (see, for instance,
[
45
, Sect. 5.1]
). One is then interested in numerical schemes that are robust with respect to the parameter
\varepsilon
and that lie on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
In this paper, we study the following similar penalized dynamics in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to simulate trajectories in a vicinity of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=f(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt+\sigma dW(t)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\nabla\ln(\det(G))(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(gG^{-1}\zeta)(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt,

(1.4)

where
X^{\varepsilon}(0)=X_{0}
. It is a simpler version of (
1.3
) that also evolves in a vicinity of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
One result of this paper is the strong convergence of the solution
X^{\varepsilon}
of (
1.4
) to the solution
X^{0}
of (
1.2
) if
X_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
.
We mention that in the deterministic setting, that is, when
\sigma=0
, equation (
1.4
) is a singular perturbation problem, and it converges to a differential algebraic equation (DAE) of index two in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
(see
[
22
, Chaps. VI-VII]
).
We propose in this article a method that is robust with respect to the parameter
\varepsilon
for solving equations of the form (
1.4
), and we leave the creation of robust integrators for solving (
1.3
) for future work for the sake of clarity.

There are different ways to approximate the solution of the dynamics (
1.4
).
A strong approximation focuses on approximating the realization of a single trajectory of (
1.4
) for a given realization of the Wiener process
W
.
A weak approximation approximates the average of functionals of the solution at a fixed time
T
, that is, quantities of the form
\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}(T))]
for
\phi
a smooth test function.
In addition, under growth and smoothness assumptions on the vector fields in (
1.4
) (see, for instance,
[
23
]
), the dynamics (
1.4
) naturally satisfy an ergodicity property; that is, there exists a unique invariant measure
d\mu_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that has a density
\rho_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure, such that for all test functions
\phi
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi(X(t))dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)d\mu_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}(x)\quad\text{almost surely}.

An approximation for the invariant measure focuses on approximating the average of a functional in the stationary state, that is, the quantity
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)d\mu_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}(x)
.
This is a computational challenge when the dimension
d
is high, which is the case in the context of molecular dynamics where the dimension is proportional to the number of particles, as a standard quadrature formula becomes prohibitively expensive in high dimension.
We emphasize that the invariant measure
\mu_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}
becomes singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, and tends weakly as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
to
d\mu_{\infty}^{0}
, a measure that is absolutely continuous to
d\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}
, the canonical measure on
\mathcal{M}
induced by the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In this paper, we propose weak convergence results for a new uniformly accurate integrator for solving (
1.4
), and numerical experiments in the weak context and for the invariant measure, as we recall that a scheme of weak order
r
automatically has order
p\geq r
for the invariant measure (see, for instance,
[
37
]
).

The most used discretization for solving (
1.4
) is the explicit Euler integrator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
[
15
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
, for instance),

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+\sqrt{h}\sigma\xi+hf(X_{n})+h\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\nabla\ln(\det(G))(X_{n})-\frac{h}{\varepsilon}(gG^{-1}\zeta)(X_{n}).

(1.5)

This integrator has weak order one of accuracy, but it faces some severe stepsize restriction due to its instability, typically of the form
h\ll\varepsilon
, in order to be accurate in the regime
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Since the solution
X^{\varepsilon}(t)
of (
1.4
) converges to the solution
X^{0}(t)
of (
1.2
) when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, one can use integrators for the limit equation (
1.2
) and apply them to solve the original problem (
1.4
) when
\varepsilon
is close to zero.
Indeed, one can prove that the solution
X^{\varepsilon}(t)
of (
1.4
) stays at distance
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
of the constrained solution
X^{0}(t)
of (
1.2
) (see Theorem
2.3
).
Thus, if the timestep of the integrator is small enough and satisfies
\varepsilon\ll h
, then this integrator is consistent for solving (
1.4
).
The alternative for the discretization of (
1.2
) on the manifold of the explicit Euler scheme (
1.5
) is the constrained Euler scheme

X^{0}_{n+1}=X^{0}_{n}+\sqrt{h}\sigma\xi+hf(X^{0}_{n})+g(X^{0}_{n})\lambda^{0}_{n+1},\quad\zeta(X^{0}_{n+1})=0,

(1.6)

where
\lambda^{0}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
acts as a Lagrange multiplier, and is entirely determined by the constraint
\zeta(X^{0}_{n+1})=0
. This integrator has weak order one for solving (
1.2
) (see
[
34
, Sect. 3.2.4]
) and it lies on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. It is a consistent approximation of (
1.4
) if
\varepsilon
is close to zero and
\varepsilon\ll h
.
This integrator is, however, not appropriate for solving (
1.4
) if the size of
\varepsilon
is of the order of one, since the exact solution does not evolve in a neighborhood of the manifold in this regime.
We mention a few other techniques to integrate numerically (
1.4
) or (
1.2
).
In
[
28
,
30
]
, high order Runge-Kutta methods are proposed for sampling the invariant measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and on manifolds.
The paper
[
36
]
presents a constrained integrator based on the RATTLE scheme (see
[
42
,
5
,
22
]
) in the context of the underdamped Langevin dynamics.
Some of the previously cited discretizations can be combined with Metropolis-Hastings rejection procedures
[
39
,
24
]
, as done, for instance, in
[
20
,
8
,
35
,
46
,
36
]
.
As for the Euler integrators (
1.5
)-(
1.6
), all of the previously mentioned methods are consistent provided that the parameter
\varepsilon
and the timestep satisfy
h\ll\varepsilon
in the context of methods in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and satisfy
\varepsilon\ll h
in the context of methods on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. When applied in the regime where
\varepsilon
and
h
share the same order of magnitude, the accuracy of these methods quickly deteriorates, and they may face stability issues.

In past decades, different solutions were proposed for treating the loss of accuracy in the intermediate regime
h\sim\varepsilon
in the context of multiscale problems with the help of uniformly accurate (UA) methods.
These methods are capable of solving dynamics indexed by a possibly stiff parameter
\varepsilon
with an accuracy and a cost both independent of
\varepsilon
. A uniformly accurate method is automatically asymptotic-preserving (AP), that is, it converges in the two regimes
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and
\varepsilon\sim 1
, but the converse is not true in general.
We refer the reader to the review
[
25
]
and references therein for examples of AP integrators for solving multiscale problems.
We mention in particular the paper
[
41
]
, which proposes a penalized Hamiltonian dynamics, and AP discretizations for solving it, and the paper
[
7
]
that gives an AP scheme for the approximation of a class of multiscale SDEs.
In
[
17
,
44
,
29
]
, trigonometric and multirevolution integrators are considered for solving highly oscillatory SDEs (see also the deterministic works
[
38
,
9
,
10
,
14
]
).
We mention the recent papers
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
4
]
(see also the references therein) that introduce uniformly accurate methods for solving a variety of multiscale problems.
There is a rich literature on AP and UA methods, but, to the best of our knowledge, the problem we study here and the techniques we consider are new.
We propose in this paper a new consistent integrator with uniform accuracy and uniform cost for solving penalized Langevin dynamics, that is, a method for solving (
1.4
) whose accuracy and cost do not depend on the parameter
\varepsilon
.

The article is organized as follows.
Section
2
is devoted to the presentation of the new integrator and to the main convergence results.
In Section
3
, we build a weak asymptotic expansion of the solution of (
1.4
) that is uniform in
\varepsilon
, and we use it for proving the uniform accuracy of our integrator.
We compare in Section
4
the new integrator with the explicit Euler scheme in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
1.5
) and the constrained Euler scheme (
1.6
) on
\mathcal{M}
in numerical experiments on a torus and on the orthogonal group to confirm its order of convergence in the weak context and for sampling the invariant measure.
Finally, we present some possible extensions and future work in Section
5
.

2
Uniformly accurate integrator for penalized Langevin dynamics

In this section, we present the new uniformly accurate integrator and the main convergence results of this paper. The proofs are postponed to Section
3
.
Let us first lay down a few notations and assumptions. We assume in the rest of the article that
\mathcal{M}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\zeta(x)=0\}
is a compact and smooth manifold of codimension
q\geq 1
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the constraints are given by the smooth map
\zeta\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
. We write
g=\nabla\zeta\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
and we assume that the Gram matrix
G(x)=g^{T}(x)g(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
is invertible for all
x
in
\mathcal{M}
. With these notations, the projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}
on the tangent bundle is given by
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(x)=I_{d}-G^{-1}(x)g(x)g^{T}(x)
.
The test functions typically belong to a subspace of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
, the vector space of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
functions
\phi(x)
such that all partial derivatives up to any order have a polynomial growth of the form

\big|\phi^{(k)}(x)\big|\leq C(1+\left|x\right|^{K}),

where the constants
C
and
K
are independent of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(but can depend on
k
), and where we denote by
\left|x\right|=(x^{T}x)^{1/2}
the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Similarly, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
the space of
\mathcal{C}^{p}
functions whose partial derivatives up to order
p
have polynomial growth.
Letting
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
, we use the following notations for differentials: for all vectors
x
,
a^{1}
, …,
a^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote

\varphi^{(m)}(x)(a^{1},\dots,a^{m})=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{m}\varphi}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\dots\partial x_{i_{m}}}(x)\thinspace a^{1}_{i_{1}}\dots a^{m}_{i_{m}}.

For the sake of clarity, we will often drop the coefficient
x
, and if
m=1
, we also use the notation
\varphi^{\prime}
for the Jacobian matrix of
\varphi
.
Moreover, for
(e_{i})
the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write

\Delta\varphi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)(e_{i},e_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad(\Div\varphi(x))_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{ij}^{\prime}(x)(e_{i}).

In the rest of the paper, we make the following assumption in the spirit of the regularity assumptions made in
[
16
, Appx. C]
and
[
40
, Chap. 2]
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The map
f
is bounded, is Lipschitz and lies in
\mathcal{C}^{3}_{P}
. The maps
g
and
g^{\prime}
are bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and there exists
c>0
such that, for
x
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left|G_{y}(x)\right|\geq c(1+\left|y\right|)^{-1},\quad\text{where}\quad G_{y}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{T}(x+\tau y)d\tau g(x).

(2.1)

In addition, there exists a smooth change of coordinate

\displaystyle\psi\colon

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle x\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\varphi(x)\\
\zeta(x)\end{pmatrix}

where
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d-q}
satisfies
\varphi^{\prime}g=0
.
The map
\psi
lies in
\mathcal{C}^{5}_{P}
and is invertible,
\psi^{\prime}
and
\psi^{\prime\prime}
are Lipschitz and there exist two constants
c
,
C>0
such that
c\leq\left|\psi^{\prime}(x)\right|\leq C
.

Remark 2.2
.

Assumption
2.1
is almost the same as the one given in
[
16
, Appx. C]
to prove the strong convergence of the dynamics (
1.3
) to the constrained dynamics (
1.2
). The difference lies in the additional estimate (
2.1
) that replaces the weaker assumption that the Gram matrix
G(x)=G_{0}(x)
is invertible on
\mathcal{M}
.
We use the estimate (
2.1
) for obtaining a uniform expansion of the Lagrange multipliers in the new method and for proving that the new method evolves in a neighborhood of the manifold (see Lemma
3.8
).
Note that the existence of the change of coordinate
\psi
is always valid in a neighborhood of the smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
. The same goes for the estimate (
2.1
) for
x
in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{M}
and
y
in a ball centered on zero.
Assumption
2.1
is valid in particular if
\mathcal{M}
is a vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but it is quite restrictive. It would be interesting to extend the results of this paper under simpler regularity assumptions made only on the manifold, as numerical experiments hint that the results presented in this paper still stand without global assumptions. This is matter for future work.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the problems (
1.2
) and (
1.4
) are well posed, and we obtain the strong convergence of the penalized dynamics (
1.4
) to the constrained dynamics (
1.2
).

Theorem 2.3
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the solution
X^{\varepsilon}
of the penalized dynamics (
1.4
) converges strongly to
X^{0}
, the solution of the constrained dynamics (
1.2
); that is, for all
t\leq T
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\left|X^{\varepsilon}(t)-X^{0}(t)\right|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon.

Moreover,
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))
satisfies

\sup_{t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\left|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))\right|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon.

This result was first introduced in
[
16
, Appx. C]
for slightly different penalized dynamics. The proof is almost identical, but we present it in Appx.
A
for the sake of completeness. In the deterministic setting with
\sigma=0
, the convergence to the manifold is of order
1
in
\varepsilon
instead of order
1/2
.
We introduce the following additional assumption.

Assumption 2.4
.

There exists
c>0
such that, for
x
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left|G_{y}(x)\right|\geq c,\quad\text{where}\quad G_{y}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{T}(x+\tau y)d\tau g(x).

Assumption (
2.4
) is a stronger version of the inequality (
2.1
) and is in the spirit of the concept of admissible Lagrange multipliers
[
36
]
. It is always satisfied for
x
in a neighborhood of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
and
y
in a ball centered on zero if we assume that the Gram matrix
G(x)=G_{0}(x)
is invertible on
\mathcal{M}
.
We emphasize that we do not need this assumption for proving the uniform accuracy property of the new method, but we use it for obtaining uniform estimates in the regime
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and on the numerical implementation of the uniformly accurate method.

We introduce the new integrator for approximating the penalized dynamics (
1.4
) with cost and accuracy independent of the parameter
\varepsilon
, and a cost comparable to that of the constrained Euler scheme (
1.6
) in terms of the number of evaluations of the functions
f
,
\zeta
,
g
, and
g^{\prime}
.

New Method

(Uniform discretization of penalized overdamped Langevin dynamics)

X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=X_{0}

\in\mathcal{M}

for

n\geq 0

do

\displaystyle X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{n}^{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{h}\sigma\xi_{n}+hf(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})^{2}}{2}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta)(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{8}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})\nabla\ln(\det(G))(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})+g(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}g^{T}(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\xi_{n}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})\big(g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g)\big)(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\Big(\varepsilon(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon h}{2}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}\Big)

\displaystyle\times\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}(g^{\prime}(e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}\Big)(X_{n}^{\varepsilon}).

end

for

The new method works in a way similar to the constrained Euler integrator (
1.6
). Knowing the approximation
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
of
X^{\varepsilon}(nh)
, we project a modified Euler step on a modified manifold defined by the constraint given in (
2.2
) (in place of
\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})=0
for the constrained Euler integrator (
1.6
)). We project the modified step in the direction
g(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
with the help of a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}
. For the implementation of the method, one can use, for instance, a fixed point iteration or a Newton method at each step to find the solution
(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon},\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1})
of the implicit system of equations (
2.2
).

In order for the discretization (
2.2
) to be well-defined, we use bounded random variables.
The
\xi_{n}
are independent and bounded discrete random vectors that have the same moments as standard Gaussian random vectors up to order four, in the spirit of
[
40
, Chap. 2]
, that is, for instance, that their components satisfy

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}=0)=\frac{2}{3}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}=\pm\sqrt{3})=\frac{1}{6},\quad i=1,\dots,d.

(2.3)

Note that using truncated Gaussian random variables would also work.

Remark 2.5
.

The new integrator is related to the popular idea of backward error analysis and modified equations for SDEs (see, for instance,
[
48
,
1
,
18
,
26
,
27
]
).
The idea is to define a projection method (see
[
21
, Sect. IV.4]
) with a modified constraint in place of
\zeta(X_{n})=0
.
Instead of evaluating the stiff term
\frac{h}{\varepsilon}gG^{-1}\zeta
as in the Euler scheme (
1.5
), we project a modified step of the explicit Euler scheme in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on a manifold that is close to
\mathcal{M}
when
\varepsilon\ll 1
and whose constraint is given by a truncation of a uniform expansion of
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon})
.
When
\varepsilon\rightarrow\infty
, the expression of the constraint
\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})
in (
2.2
) tends to a truncated Taylor expansion in
h
around
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
, while for
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,
\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})
tends to zero, which enforces that the integrator lies on
\mathcal{M}
. These intuitions will be made rigorous in Section
3
.

Remark 2.6
.

In the context of a manifold
\mathcal{M}
of codimension
q=1
, the Gram matrix
G(x)
and
g(x)^{T}e_{i}=g_{i}(x)
are real numbers, so that
\nabla\ln(\det(G))=2G^{-1}g^{\prime}(g)
and

\sum_{i=1}^{d}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}=G^{-1}(g^{\prime}(g))^{T}g=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(g^{\prime}(e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}.

The discretization (

\displaystyle X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{n}^{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{h}\sigma\xi_{n}+hf(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})^{2}}{2}(\zeta^{2}G^{-2}g^{\prime}(g))(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{4}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})(G^{-1}g^{\prime}(g))(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})+g(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1},

\displaystyle\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}g^{T}(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\xi_{n}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})(g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}G^{-1}g^{T}g^{\prime}(g)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g))(X_{n}^{\varepsilon}).

We now state the main result of this work, that is, the uniform accuracy of the discretization (

) for approximating the solution of the penalized Langevin dynamics (

).

Theorem 2.7
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the integrator
(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
given by (
2.2
) is a consistent uniformly accurate approximation of the solution
X^{\varepsilon}(t)
of the penalized Langevin dynamics (

); that is, for a given test function

h_{0}>0
,

such that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})]-\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}(nh))]\right|\leq C\sqrt{h},\quad n=0,1,\dots,N,\quad Nh=T.

(2.5)

Remark 2.8
.

Note that Theorem
2.7
states the uniform consistency, but not the uniform weak order one, as one could expect.
The discretization (
2.2
) has weak order one if
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}
is fixed or in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, but an order reduction occurs in the intermediate regime

, and the integrator only has weak order
1/2
with respect to

in general.
For the sake of simplicity, we leave the creation of uniformly accurate integrators of higher weak order for future works.

We present the proof of Theorem
2.7
in Section
3
.
It relies on a weak expansion in
h
of the solution of (
1.4
) that is uniform in
\varepsilon
.
One could directly use this uniform expansion as an explicit numerical integrator for solving (
1.4
). It would also yield a uniformly accurate scheme and would not require one to solve a fixed point problem.
However, in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, this integrator would almost surely not stay on the manifold.
The crucial geometric property that the integrator lies on the manifold when

is satisfied for the new method, as stated in the following result.

Theorem 2.9
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the integrator
(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
in (

) converges to the Euler scheme on the manifold (

) when

; that is, for

, there exists a constant

that depends on

\left|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n})\right|\leq C_{h}\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad n=0,1,\dots,N.

In addition, if Assumption
2.4
is satisfied, then, for
h_{0}
small enough, for

fixed, there exists a constant
C_{h}>0
such that

\left|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n}^{0}\right|\leq C_{h}\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad n=0,1,\dots,N,\quad Nh=T.

(2.6)

Remark 2.10
.

In the deterministic context (i.e. when

of both the exact solution and the integrator are in

.
To the best of our knowledge, the integrator given by (

) with

.
However, similar expansions that are uniform with respect to

are presented in

Remark 2.11
.

Another widely used scheme on the manifold is the Euler scheme with implicit projection direction,

X^{0}_{n+1}=X^{0}_{n}+hf(X^{0}_{n})+\sqrt{h}\sigma\xi_{n}+g(X^{0}_{n+1})\lambda^{0}_{n+1},\quad\zeta(X^{0}_{n+1})=0,

where the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{0}_{n+1}
is determined by the constraint
\zeta(X^{0}_{n+1})=0
.
The uniformly accurate discretization given in (

) can be modified so that it converges to the integrator (

) when

.
It suffices to replace the first line of (

\displaystyle X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{n}^{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{h}\sigma\xi_{n}+hf(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})-\frac{(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})^{2}}{2}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta)(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\Big(\sqrt{2\varepsilon h(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})\Big)\nabla\ln(\det(G))(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})+g(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1},

and to keep the same expansion for the constraint

extends to this context, so that the convergence results persist.
Similarly, one could change the direction of projection

into

, where

is any consistent one-step approximation of

, in the spirit of the class of projected Runge-Kutta methods presented in
[

]
.
Finding a class of uniformly accurate discretizations that converge to a more general class of Runge-Kutta methods on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is matter for future works.

The uniform discretization (

) is implicit and requires one to solve a fixed point problem at each step with, for instance, a fixed point iteration or a Newton method.

Theorem 2.12
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, each step of the integrator
(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
given by (

) can be rewritten as a solution of a fixed point problem of the form

where

,
\xi_{n}
,
h
, and

.

3
Weak convergence analysis

In this section, we present the uniform weak expansion and the stability properties of the solution
X^{\varepsilon}(t)
to the penalized dynamic (
1.4
) and of the uniform integrator
(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
given in (
2.2
). We then use these results to prove Theorem
2.7
, Theorem
2.9
and Theorem
2.12
.
Let us begin the analysis with a few technical lemmas and notations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
be given by (
2.2
), then, under Assumption
2.1
, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
independent of
X_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
,
\varepsilon
, and
h
such that, for all
n\geq 0
,

(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})\left|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n})\right|\leq C_{0}\sqrt{h}.

(3.1)

Proof.

As
f
,
\xi_{n}
,
g
, and
g^{\prime}
are bounded, we obtain from the definition of the integrator given by (
2.2
) that

|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n+1})|\leq e^{-h/\varepsilon}|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n})|+C\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})},

where we used that the function
(1-e^{-x})/x
is bounded for
x>0
.
Thus, as
\zeta(X_{0})=0
,

\displaystyle|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n})|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-nh/\varepsilon}|\zeta(X_{0})|+C\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-kh/\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}}{1-e^{-h/\varepsilon}}\leq C_{0}\frac{\sqrt{h}}{1-e^{-h/\varepsilon}},

where the constant
C_{0}
does not depend on
X_{0}
,
\varepsilon
,
n
, and
h
. This yields the estimate (
3.1
).
∎

The estimate (
3.1
) is a direct consequence of our choice of using bounded random variables in (
2.2
).
We shall use this estimate extensively in the rest of this section.
Thus, we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
the set of vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfy the estimate

(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})\left|\zeta(x)\right|\leq C_{0}\sqrt{h},

(3.2)

where
C_{0}
is the constant given in Lemma
3.1
. The set
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that contains
\mathcal{M}
. The numerical scheme given by (
2.2
) takes values in
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
. We mention that the convergence results are still valid if the initial condition
X_{0}
of (
2.2
) is chosen in
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
instead of
\mathcal{M}
.

As we aim at writing uniform expansions, we introduce the convenient notation
R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)
for any remainder that satisfies at least
\left|\mathbb{E}[R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)]\right|\leq Ch^{3/2}
, where
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
,
h
, and
x
.

The following result serves as a technical tool for simplifying the calculations in the uniform expansions of Subsection
3.1
and Subsection
3.2
. It is proved with elementary computations.

Lemma 3.2
.

The Fixman correction can be rewritten in the following way:

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\nabla\ln(\det(G))=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}g^{\prime}(e_{i})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i},

where
(e_{i})
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Using that
G=g^{T}g
is a symmetric matrix, that
g=\nabla\zeta
is a gradient (which implies
x^{T}g^{\prime}(y)=y^{T}g^{\prime}(x)
) and the standard properties of the trace operator
\Trace
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{j}(\ln(\det(G)))

\displaystyle=\Trace(G^{-1}\partial_{j}G)=2\Trace(G^{-1}(g^{\prime}(e_{j}))^{T}g)=2\Trace(g^{\prime}(e_{j})G^{-1}g^{T})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}^{T}g^{\prime}(e_{j})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{j}^{T}g^{\prime}(e_{i})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i},

that is,
\nabla\ln(\det(G))=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}g^{\prime}(e_{i})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}
. For the second equality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{j}^{T}g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}g^{\prime}(e_{j})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}

\displaystyle=\Trace(gG^{-1}g^{T}g^{\prime}(e_{j})G^{-1}g^{T})=\Trace(g^{\prime}(e_{j})G^{-1}g^{T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}^{T}g^{\prime}(e_{j})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{j}^{T}g^{\prime}(e_{i})G^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}.

Hence we get the result.
∎

.

(3.3)

where
C
is independent of
h
and
\varepsilon
and where the functions

and

\displaystyle A^{\varepsilon}_{h}

\displaystyle=\sigma\xi+\frac{e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1}{\sqrt{h}}gG^{-1}\zeta+\sigma\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{2h}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}-1\Big)gG^{-1}g^{T}\xi,

\displaystyle B^{\varepsilon}_{h}

\displaystyle=f+\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{h}(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})-1\Big)gG^{-1}g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{h}(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})-1\Big)gG^{-1}\Div(g)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{8h}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{8h}(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})^{2}gG^{-1}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2h}(e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1)^{2}\Big(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta-gG^{-1}g^{T}g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta

\displaystyle-gG^{-1}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta))^{T}gG^{-1}\zeta\Big)+\sigma^{2}\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{h}(e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1)\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{d}gG^{-1}(g^{\prime}(e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{h}(e^{-2h/\varepsilon}-4e^{-h/\varepsilon}+3)-2\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{d}gG^{-1}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i},

with
\xi
a discrete bounded random vector that satisfies (
2.3
), and where the functions

and

are bounded uniformly in

and

on

.

Note that for

.

Lemma 3.4
.

With the same notations and assumptions as in Proposition

, the following estimates hold for all

and all

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{\varepsilon}(h)-x|^{2}]^{1/2}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{\varepsilon}(h)-(x+\sqrt{h}\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x))|^{2}]^{1/2}

(3.5)

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

The uniform bounds on

Let

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))-\phi(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))]\Big|

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

As

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{\varepsilon}(h)-x|^{2}]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[|\psi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))-\psi(x)|^{2}]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))-\varphi(x)|^{2}]^{1/2}+C\mathbb{E}[|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(h))-\zeta(x)|^{2}]^{1/2}.

On the one hand, applying the Itô formula to

yields

\displaystyle\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))

\displaystyle=\varphi(x)+\sigma\int_{0}^{h}\varphi^{\prime}(X^{\varepsilon}(s))dW(s)

(3.8)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{h}[\varphi^{\prime}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi](X^{\varepsilon}(s))ds,

where the term in
\varepsilon

Assumption
2.1
allows us to write the uniform strong expansion

\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))-\varphi(x)|^{2}]^{1/2}\leq C\sqrt{h}.

On the other hand, for
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon})
, we have

d\zeta(X^{\varepsilon})=\sigma g^{T}(X^{\varepsilon})dW+\Big[g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\zeta\Big](X^{\varepsilon})dt.

With the variation of constants formula, it rewrites into

\displaystyle\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(h))

\displaystyle=e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(x)+\sigma\int_{0}^{h}e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}g^{T}(X^{\varepsilon}(s))dW(s)

(3.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{h}e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}\Big[g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g)\Big](X^{\varepsilon}(s))ds.

As the integrands in (
3.9
) are bounded (using Assumption
2.1
), we get

\mathbb{E}[|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(h))-\zeta(x)|^{2}]^{1/2}\leq C((e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1)^{2}\left|\zeta(x)\right|^{2}+h)^{1/2}\leq C\sqrt{h},

where we used that
x\in\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
.
We thus get the desired estimate (
3.4
).
The estimate (
3.5
) is obtained with the same arguments by keeping track of the terms of size
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})
in the expansions.

We now prove the weak estimate (
3.6
).
We denote for simplicity
Y^{\varepsilon}(h)=x+\sqrt{h}\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)+hB^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)
.
Let us first look at the approximation of
\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))
.
On the one hand, applying the Itô formula to
\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(t))
gives

\displaystyle\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))

\displaystyle=\varphi(x)+h\varphi^{\prime}f(x)+h\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G))(x)+h\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi(x)+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x),

where we used (
3.4
) and we put in
R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)
all the terms that are zero in average.
On the other hand, an expansion in
h
of
\varphi(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))
yields

\displaystyle\varphi(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))

\displaystyle=\varphi+\sqrt{h}\varphi^{\prime}\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}+h\Big[\varphi^{\prime}B^{\varepsilon}_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h},\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h})\Big]+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}

\displaystyle=\varphi+h\varphi^{\prime}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{8}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{1}{2}(e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1)^{2}\varphi^{\prime}g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(W(h),W(h))+\frac{1}{2}(e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1)^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta,gG^{-1}\zeta)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{h}(e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}-1)(e^{(u-h)/\varepsilon}-1)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}dW(s),gG^{-1}g^{T}dW(u))

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{h}(e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}-1)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(W(h),gG^{-1}g^{T}dW(s))+R^{\varepsilon}_{h},

where we use that
\varphi^{\prime}g=0
, we omit the dependence in
x
for conciseness, and we put in
R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)
all the terms that are zero in average.
We now replace the random terms by their expectation,

\displaystyle\varphi(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))

\displaystyle=\varphi+h\varphi^{\prime}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{8}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+h\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1)^{2}(\varphi^{\prime}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta)+\varphi^{\prime\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta,gG^{-1}\zeta))

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{h}(e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}-1)^{2}ds\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i},gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i})

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{h}(e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}-1)ds\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(e_{i},gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i})+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}.

Letting
M\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, we differentiate the equality
\varphi^{\prime}(gM)=0
. We obtain that for any
M\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\varphi^{\prime}(g^{\prime}(v)M)+\varphi^{\prime\prime}(gM,v)=0
. We deduce that

\varphi^{\prime}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta)+\varphi^{\prime\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta,gG^{-1}\zeta)=0.

Applying Lemma
3.2
, we get by a direct calculation that

\varphi(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))=\varphi(x)+h\varphi^{\prime}f(x)+h\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G))(x)+h\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi(x)+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x),

which gives the desired estimate

\left|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(h))]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x+\sqrt{h}\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)+hB^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x))]\right|\leq Ch^{3/2}.

(3.10)

For the one-step approximation of
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(h))
, the Itô formula and the variation of constants formula yield

\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(h))=e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(x)+\varepsilon(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})(g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g))(x)+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x).

For
\zeta(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))
, using
g^{T}g=G
, we get with the same arguments as for
\varphi(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))
that

\displaystyle\zeta(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))

\displaystyle=\zeta+\sqrt{h}g^{T}\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}+h\Big[g^{T}B^{\varepsilon}_{h}+\frac{1}{2}(g^{\prime}(\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}))^{T}\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}\Big]+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}

\displaystyle=e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta+\varepsilon(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big(\varepsilon(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})-h\Big)\Div(g)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(g^{\prime}(W(h)))^{T}W(h)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{4}(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})^{2}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\sigma^{2}\Big(h+\varepsilon(e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1)\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{d}(g^{\prime}(e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\Big(\varepsilon(e^{-2h/\varepsilon}-4e^{-h/\varepsilon}+3)-2h\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{d}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}e_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{h}(e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}-1)(e^{(u-h)/\varepsilon}-1)(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}g^{T}dW(u)))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}dW(s)

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{h}(e^{(s-h)/\varepsilon}-1)(g^{\prime}(W(h)))^{T}gG^{-1}g^{T}dW(s)+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}.

We now replace the stochastic integrals by their expectations (putting the remainders in
R^{\varepsilon}_{h}
), and we use Lemma
3.2
to simplify the expansion. It yields

\displaystyle\zeta(Y^{\varepsilon}(h))

\displaystyle=e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta+\varepsilon(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})(g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g))+R^{\varepsilon}_{h},

which implies

\left|\mathbb{E}[\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(h))]-\mathbb{E}[\zeta(x+\sqrt{h}\widehat{A}^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)+hB^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x))]\right|\leq Ch^{3/2}.

(3.11)

Combining the inequalities (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) gives the desired weak estimate (
3.6
).
∎

To end this subsection, we recall the growth properties of
X^{\varepsilon}
that will be of use in Subsection
3.3
.
For this particular result, we add the dependency in the initial condition
x
of the exact solution of (
1.4
) with the notation
X^{\varepsilon}(t,x)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{5}_{P}
and
t\leq T
fixed, the function
\widetilde{\phi}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}(t,x))]
lies in
\mathcal{C}^{3}_{P}
with constants independent of
t
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

The standard theory (see, for instance, the textbook
[
40
]
) gives
\widetilde{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{C}^{3}
.
With the regularity assumptions on
\psi
and its derivatives, it is sufficient to prove that

\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y))]

is in
\mathcal{C}^{3}_{P}
, where
Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)=\psi(X^{\varepsilon}(t,\psi^{-1}(x)))
(replacing
\phi
by
\phi\circ\psi^{-1}
).
We recall from the proof of Lemma
3.4
that
\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(t,x))
satisfies the integral formulation (
3.8
) and that
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t,x))
satisfies (
3.9
).
Putting together these equations, we deduce that
Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)
satisfies an equation of the form

Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)=A^{\varepsilon}(t)y+\int_{0}^{t}A^{\varepsilon}(t-s)F(Y^{\varepsilon}(s,y))ds+\int_{0}^{t}A^{\varepsilon}(t-s)G(Y^{\varepsilon}(s,y))dW(s),

where
F
and
G
are in
\mathcal{C}^{3}_{P}
and do not depend on
\varepsilon
, and

A^{\varepsilon}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d-q}&0\\
0&e^{-t/\varepsilon}I_{q}\end{pmatrix}.

As
\left|A^{\varepsilon}(t)\right|\leq C
, the process
Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\left|Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)\right|^{2p}]\leq C(1+\left|y\right|^{K})
, with
C
and
K
independent of
\varepsilon
.
Thus we have

\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y))]\leq C(1+\mathbb{E}[\left|Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)\right|^{K}])\leq C(1+\mathbb{E}[\left|y\right|^{K}]).

For the derivatives, we recall from
[
19
]
that
Z^{\varepsilon}(t,y)=\partial_{y}Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle Z^{\varepsilon}(t,y)

\displaystyle=A^{\varepsilon}(t)I_{d}+\int_{0}^{t}A^{\varepsilon}(t-s)F^{\prime}(Y^{\varepsilon}(s,y))Z^{\varepsilon}(t,y)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}A^{\varepsilon}(t-s)G^{\prime}(Y^{\varepsilon}(s,y))Z^{\varepsilon}(t,y)dW(s).

Applying the same arguments as for
Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)
yields that
Z^{\varepsilon}(t,y)
has bounded moments of all order and that
\mathbb{E}[\phi(Z^{\varepsilon}(t,y))]\leq C(1+\mathbb{E}[\left|y\right|^{K}])
.
The same methodology extends to
\partial_{y}^{2}Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)
and
\partial_{y}^{3}Y^{\varepsilon}(t,y)
.
∎

3.2
Uniform expansion and bounded moments of the numerical solution

In this subsection, we show that the integrator given by (
2.2
) has bounded moments of all order, that it lies on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, and that it satisfies the same local weak uniform expansion as the exact solution of (
1.4
) (see Proposition
3.3
).

First, the integrator given by (
2.2
) satisfies the following bounded moments property.

Proposition 3.6
.

Under Assumption
2.1
,
(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
has bounded moments of any order along time; i.e., for all timestep
h\leq h_{0}
small enough such that
Nh=T
is fixed, for all integer
m\geq 0
,

\sup_{n\leq N}\mathbb{E}[\left|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2m}]\leq C_{m},

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
\varepsilon
and
h
.

The integrator
(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})
given in (
2.2
) also satisfies a uniform local expansion that is similar to its continuous counterpart presented in Proposition
3.3
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exists
h_{0}>0
such that for all
h\leq h_{0}
, if
(X^{\varepsilon}_{n})
is the numerical discretization given by (
2.2
) beginning at
x\in\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
(assumed deterministic for simplicity), then, for all test functions
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{3}_{P}
, the following estimate holds:

\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}_{1})]-\mathbb{E}[\phi(x+\sqrt{h}A^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)+hB^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x))]\right|\leq C(1+\left|x\right|^{K})h^{3/2},

(3.12)

where
C
is independent of
h
and
\varepsilon
, and
A^{\varepsilon}_{h}
and
B^{\varepsilon}_{h}
are the functions given in Proposition
3.3
.

To prove Proposition
3.6
and Proposition
3.7
, we rely on the following lemma, whose proof is postponed to the end of this subsection.
We emphasize that an inequality of the form
\left|\zeta(x)\right|\leq C
does not imply in general that
x
stays close to
\mathcal{M}
. That is why we rely in Lemma
3.8
on an estimate of the Lagrange multipliers (using the inequality (
2.1
)).
This estimate ensures that the method evolves in a neighborhood of the manifold.

Lemma 3.8
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and if
x\in\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
, there exists
h_{0}>0
such that, for all timestep
h\leq h_{0}
, the one-step approximation
X_{1}^{\varepsilon}
and the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}
in the discretization (
2.2
) satisfy

\left|X_{1}^{\varepsilon}-x\right|\leq C\sqrt{h},\qquad\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}=\sqrt{h}G^{-1}(x)\lambda_{1,(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}+hG^{-1}(x)\lambda_{1,(1)}^{\varepsilon}+R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x),

where
|\lambda_{1,(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C
,
|\lambda_{1,(1)}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C
, and
|R^{\varepsilon}_{h}(x)|\leq Ch^{3/2}
with
C
independent of
\varepsilon
,
h
and
x
.

For proving Proposition
3.6
, we apply the change of variable
\psi
and we adapt the standard methodology presented in
[
40
, Lemma 1.1.6 & Lemma 2.2.2]
.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

We derive from (
2.2
) that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})-e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ch,

\displaystyle\left|\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})-e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{h}.

We prove that
\zeta(X_{n})
has bounded moments by induction on
n
. The binomial formula yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\left|e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n})+\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n+1})-e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{n})\right|^{2m}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2mh/\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m}]

\displaystyle+C\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m-1}\left|\mathbb{E}[\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})-e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}]\right|]

\displaystyle+C\sum_{k=2}^{2m}\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m-k}\left|\zeta(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})-e^{-h/\varepsilon}\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{k}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m}]+C(1+\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m}])h.

Following
[
40
, Lemma 1.1.6]
, as
X_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is bounded, it implies that
\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n})\right|^{2m}]
is bounded uniformly in
n=0,\dots,N
and
\varepsilon
.

Using Lemma
3.8
and the equality
\varphi^{\prime}g=0
, a direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})-\varphi(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ch,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\left|\varphi(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})-\varphi(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{h}.

(3.14)

Following the same methodology as for
\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n})\right|^{2m}]
, the estimates (
3.13
)-(
3.14
) imply that the quantity
\mathbb{E}[\left|\varphi(X_{n})\right|^{2m}]
is bounded uniformly in
n=0,\dots,N
and
\varepsilon
.
Then, as
\psi^{-1}
is Lipschitz, we have

\mathbb{E}[\left|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2m}]\leq C(1+\mathbb{E}[\left|\psi(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m}])\leq C(1+\mathbb{E}[\left|\varphi(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m}]+\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|^{2m}])\leq C.

Hence we get the result.
∎

We obtain the uniform expansion of the numerical solution by writing explicitly a uniform expansion of the Lagrange multiplier

[

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

Using Lemma
3.8

, we obtain

X^{\varepsilon}_{1}=x+\sqrt{h}\Big[\sigma\xi+(gG^{-1})(x)\lambda_{1,(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}\Big]+R_{h}^{\varepsilon}(x),

The constraint is then given by

\displaystyle\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{1})

\displaystyle=\zeta(x)+\sqrt{h}\Big[\sigma g^{T}(x)\xi+\lambda_{1,(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}\Big]+R_{h}^{\varepsilon}(x).

On the other hand, we get from the definition of the integrator (

\displaystyle\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{1})

\displaystyle=\zeta(x)+\sqrt{h}\Big[\frac{e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1}{\sqrt{h}}\zeta(x)+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{2h}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}g^{T}(x)\xi\Big]+R_{h}^{\varepsilon}(x).

By identifying the two terms in
\sqrt{h}
in the expansions of
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}_{1})
, we deduce the value of
\lambda_{1,(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}
, that is,

\lambda_{1,(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1}{\sqrt{h}}\zeta+\sigma\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{2h}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}-1\Big)g^{T}\xi.

The expression of

\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}_{1}

(3.15)

where

\left|R_{h}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\leq Ch

A Taylor expansion of
\phi(X^{\varepsilon}_{1})

∎

The proof of Lemma
3.8

Proof of Lemma
3.8
.

Let

\displaystyle X_{1}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=x+\sqrt{h}Y_{(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}(x)+hY_{(1)}^{\varepsilon}(x)+g(x)\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1},

\displaystyle\zeta(X_{1}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\zeta(x)+\sqrt{h}\zeta_{(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}(x)+h\zeta_{(1)}^{\varepsilon}(x),

Y_{(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle=f+\frac{(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})^{2}}{2h}(g^{\prime}(gG^{-1}\zeta)G^{-1}\zeta)+\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{8h}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})\nabla\ln(\det(G)),

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-h/\varepsilon}-1}{\sqrt{h}}\zeta+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{2h}(1-e^{-2h/\varepsilon})}g^{T}\xi,

\displaystyle\zeta_{(1)}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{h}(1-e^{-h/\varepsilon})\big(g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g)\big)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}

3.3
Proofs of the convergence theorems

Now that we have the local uniform expansion of the exact solution and the numerical scheme, as well as the stability property of Proposition
3.6
, we are able to prove the main convergence theorems.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

We derive the global weak consistency (
2.5
) with techniques similar to the ones presented in
[
40
, Chap. 2]
.
We denote by
X^{\varepsilon}(t,x)
the solution of the penalized dynamics with initial condition
x
and
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}(x)
the numerical solution with initial condition
x
.
For
x\in\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
, Proposition
3.3
and Proposition
3.7
yield

\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}(h,x))-\phi(X_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x))|x]\right|\leq C(1+\left|x\right|^{K})h^{3/2},

where
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{3}_{P}
.
Lemma
3.5
gives that
\phi_{n}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}((n-1)h,x))|x]
is in
\mathcal{C}^{3}_{P}
.
We rewrite the global error, given by
E^{\varepsilon}_{h}=\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}(T,X_{0}))-\phi(X_{N}^{\varepsilon}(X_{0}))]\right|
, with a telescopic sum,

\displaystyle E^{\varepsilon}_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\varepsilon}(nh,X^{\varepsilon}_{N-n}(X_{0})))-\phi(X^{\varepsilon}((n-1)h,X^{\varepsilon}_{N-n+1}(X_{0})))]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi_{n}(X^{\varepsilon}(h,X^{\varepsilon}_{N-n}(X_{0})))-\phi_{n}(X^{\varepsilon}_{1}(X^{\varepsilon}_{N-n}(X_{0})))]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\left|\mathbb{E}[\phi_{n}(X^{\varepsilon}(h,x))-\phi_{n}(X^{\varepsilon}_{1}(x))|x=X^{\varepsilon}_{N-n}(X_{0})]\right|]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}C(1+\mathbb{E}[\left|X^{\varepsilon}_{N-n}(X_{0})\right|^{K}])h^{3/2}\leq Ch^{1/2},

where we used the bounded moments property of Proposition
3.6
and
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{h}
(Lemma
3.1
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

We obtain straightforwardly from the expression of the integrator (
2.2
) that
\left|\zeta(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|\leq C_{h}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
Using this estimate and the notation introduced in the proof of Lemma
3.8
, we observe that

\left|Y_{(i)}^{\varepsilon}(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})-Y_{(i)}^{0}(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|\leq C_{h}\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad\left|\zeta_{(i)}^{\varepsilon}(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\right|\leq C_{h}\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad i\in\{1/2,1\},

where
Y_{(1/2)}^{0}(x)=\sigma\xi_{n}
and
Y_{(1)}^{0}(x)=f(x)
.
The Lagrange multiplier given by (
3.16
) therefore satisfies
\left|\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}-\widetilde{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}}\right|\leq C_{h}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, where

\widetilde{\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}}=-G_{X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n}^{\varepsilon}}^{-1}(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\int_{0}^{1}g^{T}(X_{n}^{\varepsilon}+\tau(X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n}^{\varepsilon}))d\tau(\sigma\sqrt{h}\xi_{n}+hf(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})).

Similarly to (
3.16
), the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{0}_{n+1}
of the constrained Euler integrator (
1.6
) is given by

\lambda^{0}_{n+1}=-G_{X_{n+1}^{0}-X_{n}^{0}}^{-1}(X_{n}^{0})\int_{0}^{1}g^{T}(X_{n}^{0}+\tau(X_{n+1}^{0}-X_{n}^{0}))d\tau(\sigma\sqrt{h}\xi_{n}+hf(X_{n}^{0})).

Using that
G_{y}^{-1}(x)
is bounded, a straightforward calculation shows that
G_{y}^{-1}(x)
is Lipschitz in
x
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is, there exists a constant
L>0
such that

\left|G_{y_{1}}^{-1}(x_{1})-G_{y_{2}}^{-1}(x_{2})\right|\leq L(\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|+\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|),\quad x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Thus, we get the estimate

\left|\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}-\lambda^{0}_{n+1}\right|\leq C_{h}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\left|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n}^{0}\right|)+C\sqrt{h}\left|X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n+1}^{0}\right|,

and, as
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}
and
\lambda^{0}_{n+1}
are bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
, we have

\left|g(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}-g(X_{n}^{0})\lambda^{0}_{n+1}\right|\leq C_{h}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\left|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n}^{0}\right|)+C\sqrt{h}\left|X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n+1}^{0}\right|.

From the definitions of
X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}
in (
2.2
) and
X_{n+1}^{0}
in (
1.6
), we deduce that

\left|X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n+1}^{0}\right|\leq C_{h}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\left|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n}^{0}\right|)+C\sqrt{h}\left|X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n+1}^{0}\right|.

If
h\leq h_{0}
is small enough, we obtain

\left|X_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n+1}^{0}\right|\leq C_{h}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\left|X_{n}^{\varepsilon}-X_{n}^{0}\right|).

We deduce the estimate (
2.6
) by iterating this inequality for
n\leq N=T/h
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.12
.

We take over the notations and the expression (
3.16
) of the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}
that we used in the proof of Lemma
3.8
. Without loss of generality, we concentrate on the first step of the algorithm with
X_{0}=x
.
Replacing
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}
by the explicit formula (
3.16
) in (
2.2
) yields that
X_{1}^{\varepsilon}
is a fixed point of the following map:

\displaystyle F_{h}^{\varepsilon}(y)

\displaystyle=x+\sqrt{h}Y_{(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}(x)+hY_{(1)}^{\varepsilon}(x)+g(x)G_{y-x}^{-1}(x)\Big[\sqrt{h}\zeta_{(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}(x)+h\zeta_{(1)}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}g^{T}(x+\tau(y-x))d\tau(\sqrt{h}Y_{(1/2)}^{\varepsilon}(x)+hY_{(1)}^{\varepsilon}(x))\Big].

For
y_{1}
,
y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, Assumption
2.1
gives

\displaystyle\left|F_{h}^{\varepsilon}(y_{2})-F_{h}^{\varepsilon}(y_{1})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{h}\left|y_{2}-y_{1}\right|,

where we used that
G_{y}^{-1}(x)
is Lipschitz in
x
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We deduce that
F_{h}^{\varepsilon}
is a uniform contraction for
h\leq h_{0}
small enough.
∎

5
Conclusion and future work

In this work, we presented a new method for the weak numerical integration of penalized Langevin dynamics evolving in the vicinity of manifolds of any dimension and codimension.
On the contrary of the other existing discretizations, the accuracy of the proposed integrator is independent of the size of the stiff parameter
\varepsilon
.
Moreover, its cost does not depend on
\varepsilon
, and it converges to the Euler scheme on the manifold when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Throughout the analysis, we gave an expansion in time of the solution to the penalized Langevin dynamics that is uniform in
\varepsilon
, as well as new tools for the study of stochastic projection methods for solving stiff SDEs.

Multiple questions arise from the work presented in this paper, with many of great interest for physical applications.
First, it would be interesting to get convergence results with weaker assumptions or to develop uniformly accurate integrators for different penalized dynamics with the same limit when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
such as the original penalized dynamics (
1.3
) (see
[
16
]
).
One could build integrators for penalized dynamics of the form

dX^{\varepsilon}=f(X^{\varepsilon})dt+\sigma dW+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\nabla\ln(\det(G))-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(gG^{-1}\zeta_{1})(X^{\varepsilon})dt-\frac{1}{\nu}(gG^{-1}\zeta_{2})(X^{\varepsilon})dt,

where
\varepsilon
and
\nu
do not share the same order of magnitude, or for constrained dynamics with a penalized term.
One could also build a uniformly accurate numerical scheme with high order in the weak context, or just in the context of the invariant measure (in the spirit of the works
[
6
,
31
,
2
,
3
,
32
,
28
,
30
]
where numerical schemes of high order for the invariant measure and weak order one were introduced).
Postprocessors
[
45
]
proved to be an efficient tool for reaching high order for the invariant measure without increasing the cost of the method and could be used in this context.
Moreover, the order conditions presented in
[
28
]
for Runge-Kutta methods for solving Langevin dynamics in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
both in the weak sense and for the invariant measure do not match with the order conditions for solving Langevin dynamics constrained on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
, as presented in
[
30
]
. It would be interesting to create a unified class of high order Runge-Kutta methods with the same order conditions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
and in the vicinity of
\mathcal{M}
.
The discretizations presented in this paper could also be combined with Metropolis-Hastings rejection procedures
[
39
,
24
]
, in the spirit of the works
[
20
,
8
,
35
,
46
,
36
]
, in order to get an exact approximation for the invariant measure with a rejection rate that does not deteriorate in the regime
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
2.3

In this section, we prove the convergence of the penalized Langevin dynamics (
1.4
) to the constrained dynamics (
1.2
) when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, as stated in Theorem
2.3
. The proof uses techniques and arguments similar to those in
[
16
, Appx. C]
. However, since we rescaled the stiff term in (
1.4
), there is no need for a change of time to prove the convergence to the constrained dynamics.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

In the orthogonal coordinates system given by Assumption
2.1
, equation (
1.4
) becomes

\displaystyle d\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(t))

\displaystyle=(\varphi^{\prime}f)(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt+\sigma\varphi^{\prime}(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dW(t)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}(\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G)))(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt,

\displaystyle d\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))

\displaystyle=(g^{T}f)(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt+\sigma g^{T}(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dW(t)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}(g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G)))(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g)(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))dt,

where we used that
\varphi^{\prime}g=0
and that
\zeta^{\prime}=g^{T}
.
Therefore,
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))
satisfies

\displaystyle\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\varepsilon}g^{T}(X^{\varepsilon}(s))dW

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\varepsilon}\Big[g^{T}f+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}g^{T}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Div(g)\Big](X^{\varepsilon}(s))dt,

and a bound on
\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))\right|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}e^{2(s-t)/\varepsilon}dt+C\Big(\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\varepsilon}dt\Big)^{2}\leq C\varepsilon.

(A.1)

On the other hand, we rewrite (
1.2
) in the orthogonal coordinates as

\displaystyle d\varphi(X^{0}(t))

\displaystyle=(\varphi^{\prime}f)(X^{0}(t))dt+\sigma\varphi^{\prime}(X^{0}(t))dW

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}(\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G)))(X^{0}(t))dt+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi(X^{0}(t))dt,

\displaystyle d\zeta(X^{0}(t))

\displaystyle=0,

where we used that

\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{\prime}\nabla\ln(\det(G))+\Delta\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi^{\prime}(\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}e_{i})e_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}e_{i},\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}e_{i}).

With Assumption
2.1
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\left|\varphi(X^{\varepsilon}(t))-\varphi(X^{0}(t))\right|^{2}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\left|X^{\varepsilon}(s)-X^{0}(s)\right|^{2}]ds,

and from (
A.1
) we deduce that

\mathbb{E}[\left|\zeta(X^{\varepsilon}(t))-\zeta(X^{0}(t))\right|^{2}]\leq C\varepsilon.

Thus,
\psi(X^{\varepsilon}(t))
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\left|\psi(X^{\varepsilon}(t))-\psi(X^{0}(t))\right|^{2}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\left|X^{\varepsilon}(s)-X^{0}(s)\right|^{2}]ds+C\varepsilon,

and, as
\psi^{-1}
is Lipschitz, we have

\mathbb{E}[\left|X^{\varepsilon}(t)-X^{0}(t)\right|^{2}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\left|X^{\varepsilon}(s)-X^{0}(s)\right|^{2}]ds+C\varepsilon,

which, with the use of the Gronwall lemma, gives the desired estimate.
∎
