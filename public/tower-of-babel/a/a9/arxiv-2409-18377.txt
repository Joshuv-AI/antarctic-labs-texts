The Comparison of Riemannian Geometric Matrix-CFAR Signal Detectors
Yusuke Ono, Linyu Peng

Abstract
Essential characteristics of signal data can be captured by the autocovariance matrix, which, in the stationary scenarios, is Toeplitz Hermitian positive definite (HPD). In this paper, several well-known Riemannian geometric structures of HPD matrix manifolds are applied to signal detection, including the affine invariant Riemannian metric, the log-Euclidean metric, and the Bures–Wasserstein (BW) metric, the last of which was recently extended to HPD manifolds. Riemannian gradient descent algorithms are proposed to solve the corresponding geometric means and medians, that play fundamental roles in the detection process. Simulations within the scenario using the ideal steering vector as the target signal provide compelling evidence that the BW detectors outperform the other geometric detectors as well as the conventional adaptive matched filter and adaptive normalized matched filter when observation data are limited. Further simulations demonstrate that the matrix-CFAR is robust in scenarios where the signal is mismatched. In addition to detection performances, robustness of the geometric detectors to outliers and computational complexity of the algorithms are analysed.

INTRODUCTION

In signal detection, the constant false alarm rate (CFAR) method has gained wide recognition as a conventional technique for suppressing clutter
[
1
]
.
The cell-averaged CFAR (CA-CFAR), known as a popular CFAR method, that employs the Fourier transform and utilises the Doppler spectral density for differentiating targets from clutter which arises from the backscattering of objects, however, tends to perform inadequately in the case of short waveforms with dense and non-homogeneous clutter
[
2
]
.
To improve discrimination between targets and clutter, one statistical method is to test the likelihood ratio by using an estimator of the autocovariance matrix.
A classical estimator of the autocovariance matrix is the sample covariance matrix (SCM), which is the maximum likelihood estimator
[
3
]
.
However, it can be accurately estimated only when a sufficient number of observation data are independent and identically distributed
[
4
]
.
In order to achieve efficient detection performance within non-homogeneous clutter while reducing the required number of observation data, several methods utilising a priori information have been proposed in
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
Additionally, in
[
10
,
11
]
, the autocovariance matrix was estimated utilising the Bayesian methods.
The strength of clutter varies depending on the condition of surroundings, transmitted frequency, and other factors
[
12
]
.
Therefore, in practical implementations, the clutter environment can be range-dependent and non-stationary. In such situations, it is often challenging to obtain secondary data that are free from target components.
Accurately capturing the statistical property about clutter precedently can be difficult, and observation data are often contaminated.
Insufficient information about clutter environment can make detection performance degraded in the methods using a priori information.

A novel clutter suppression method, the matrix-CFAR, was recently proposed and developed, which does not require any a priori information and leverages the autocovariance matrix of the signals
[
13
,
14
]
.
This method operates under the premise that the autocovariance matrix, which characterizes the autocorrelation of observation data, is Hermitian positive-definite (HPD) and
has been leveraged for detection problems
[
15
,
16
]
.
Its detection efficiency has been demonstrated in the observation of wake eddy turbulence
[
17
]
, Burg estimate methods of radar scatter matrices
[
18
]
, detection of X-band radar clutter
[
19
]
, and so on.
In these applications, the affine invariant Riemannian metric (AIRM) of the HPD manifolds was used.
However, the computational cost of the AIRM is relatively high
[
20
]
.
Recently, matrix-CFAR, based on various divergences has been proposed, which has shown improved detection performance and robustness to outliers in comparison with the AIRM
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.
In
[
24
]
, the comparison between the log-Euclidean (LE) metric and the AIRM was conducted and their detection performances were shown almost the same.
Regarding the computational complexity that will be studied in Section
III-E
, while the LE mean can be derived by closed form, the AIRM mean has to be obtained numerically.
As structure of HPD manifolds is of great importance in the matrix-CFAR, various geometric structures of HPD manifolds have been proposed
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
.

The Bures–Wasserstein (BW) distance, also known as the Earth mover’s distance or Kantorovich–Rubinstein distance, was proposed in the context of optimal transport

,
29
]

,

The BW distance has been applied in various fields, including quantum information

, optimisation theory
[

,
35
,
36
]
and machine learning
[
37
,
38
]
.
In particular, in the field of machine learning, the BW distance is referred to as the Wasserstein distance and serves as a measure of dissimilarity in the space of mean-zero Gaussian densities
[
39
]
.
In quantum information theory, it, referred to as the Bures distance, is utilised to quantify the dissimilarity between quantum states or density matrices
[
40
]
. In the current paper, the BW metric of HPD manifolds is applied to signal detection and compared with other Riemannian geometric structures.
The contributions of this study are summarised as follows.

(1)

The BW metric is applied to signal detection, where the BW mean and median are used to catch essential statistical properties of the observation data. The corresponding optimisation problems for BW mean and median are solved by Riemannian gradient descent algorithms; their computational complexity is studied and compared with other numerical algorithms.

(2)

Numerical detection performance of the BW detectors is compared with that of the AIRM, LE metric, adaptive matched filter (AMF) and adaptive normalized matched filter (ANMF) detectors as follows.
In one scenario, the ideal steering vector is regarded as the target signal, while in the other, we consider the presence of steering signal mismatches.
In the first scenario, the BW detectors outperform the other methods when observation data are limited.
To address the limitations of AMF, we propose AMFs by replacing the SCM with the Riemannian geometric means or medians.
The relation between detection performance and the normalized Doppler frequency of target is further studied numerically.
In the second scenario, it is shown that the matrix-CFAR is more robust with respect to mismatched signals compared to the AMF and ANMF.

(3)

Robustness of the detectors about outliers is analysed by the corresponding exact influence functions defined in
[
41

In Section

Numerical detection performance of the BW detectors is conducted in Section

In Section

II
THE DETECTION SETTING AND THEORY

In this section, we introduce the problem formulation of signal detection and then recall the conventional detection methods and the matrix-CFAR.
In this study, it is assumed that we detect the observation data from
N
stationary channels.
The detection problem will be modelled as a statistical hypothesis test: the null hypothesis is denoted as
H_{0}
and represents the complex data
\bm{x}
includes only clutter
\bm{c}
. On the other hand, the alternative hypothesis is denoted as
H_{1}
and represents
\bm{x}
includes both clutter
\bm{c}
and the target
\bm{s}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}H_{0}:\begin{cases}\mathrm{target\ is\ absent,}\\
\bm{x}=\bm{c},\\
\end{cases}\vskip 5.69046pt\\
H_{1}:\begin{cases}\mathrm{target\ is\ present,}\\
\bm{x}=a\bm{s}+\bm{c}.\\
\end{cases}\\
\end{cases}

(1)

Here
a
denotes the unknown amplitude coefficient.
The observation data
\bm{x}
is given by

\bm{x}=\left(x_{0},\ldots,x_{N-1}\right)^{\operatorname{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N},\\

(2)

where
\mathbb{C}^{N}
is the
N
-dimensional complex space and
(\cdot)^{\operatorname{T}}
represents the transpose of vectors or matrices.
The SCM of observation data
\left\{\bm{x}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
is derived by
[
3
]

\bm{R}_{\mathrm{SCM}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{H}},\ \ \bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}.

(3)

Here
(\cdot)^{\mathsf{H}}
denotes the conjugate transpose of vectors or matrices. It has been commonly used;
in signal processing, the AMF reads
[
42
]

\frac{\left|\bm{x}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R}^{-1}_{\mathrm{SCM}}\bm{s}\right|^{2}}{\bm{s}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R}^{-1}_{\mathrm{SCM}}\bm{s}}\overset{H_{1}}{\underset{H_{0}}{\gtrless}}\gamma

(4)

and the ANMF reads
[
43
]

\frac{\left|\bm{x}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R}^{-1}_{\mathrm{SCM}}\bm{s}\right|^{2}}{\left(\bm{x}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R}^{-1}_{\mathrm{SCM}}\bm{x}\right)\left(\bm{s}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R}^{-1}_{\mathrm{SCM}}\bm{s}\right)}\overset{H_{1}}{\underset{H_{0}}{\gtrless}}\gamma,

(5)

where
\gamma
denotes the detection threshold.

In the matrix-CFAR, to decide whether a target
\bm{s}
is present within the cell under test (CUT) or not, the autocovariance matrix of the observation data can be modelled by a Toeplitz HPD matrix:
[
44
,
45
]

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91692pt\bm{R}=\begin{pmatrix}r_{0}&\cdots&\overline{r}_{k}&\cdots&\overline{r}_{N-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
r_{k}&\ddots&r_{0}&\ddots&\overline{r}_{k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
r_{N-1}&\cdots&r_{k}&\cdots&r_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(6)

where
0\leq k\leq N-1
.
Its component is the
k
-th autocorrelation function given by

r_{k}=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{l}\overline{x}_{l+k}\right],\ \ 0\leq l\leq N-k-1.

(7)

Here
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
is the statistical expectation, and
\overline{r}_{k}
is the conjugate of
r_{k}
.
It is known that the Toeplitz structure leads to the improvement in an estimate of the autocovariance matrix
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
.
The
k
-th autocorrelation function
r_{k}
can be estimated by the observation data as

\displaystyle r_{k}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N-k-1}x_{l}\overline{x}_{l+k},\ \ 0\leq k\leq N-1.

(8)

Equation (
8
) to estimate autocorrelation coefficients is not robust, and under assumption of local stationarity of signal, these autocorrelation coefficients could be estimated by regularised Burg algorithm
[
49
]
.
An estimator of the clutter autocovariance matrix is derived as
\bm{R}_{g}
by autocovariance matrices of the observation data
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.
As a consequence, the detection problem (
1
) can be formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}H_{0}:\bm{R}_{\mathrm{CUT}}=\bm{R}_{g},\vskip 5.69046pt\\
H_{1}:\bm{R}_{\mathrm{CUT}}\neq\bm{R}_{g}.\\
\end{cases}

(9)

Here the matrix
\bm{R}_{\mathrm{CUT}}
is the autocovariance matrix within the CUT.
Considering a threshold
\gamma
, we can approach the detection problem as the task of distinguishing
\bm{R}_{\mathrm{CUT}}
from
\bm{R}_{g}
by

\displaystyle d\left(\bm{R}_{g},\bm{R}_{\mathrm{CUT}}\right)\overset{H_{1}}{\underset{H_{0}}{\gtrless}}\gamma

(10)

where
d\left(\bm{R}_{g},\bm{R}_{\mathrm{CUT}}\right)
is either a geodesic distance or a divergence. In the current study, we focus on geodesic distances only, which will be introduced in the following section.
Fig.
1
shows the process of the matrix-CFAR which is described as follows. Firstly we compute autocovariance matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of the observation data
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
using (
6
) and (
8
).
Riemannian geometric mean or median
\bm{R}_{g}
of
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
can be derived as the solutions of optimisation problems about the geodesic distances.
The matrix
\bm{R}_{g}
can be regarded as an estimator of the clutter autocovariance matrix since almost all observation data
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are considered as clutter, that is of the essence.
The detection decision is completed by comparing the distance of
\bm{R}_{\mathrm{CUT}}
and
\bm{R}_{g}
with the threshold
\gamma
.

Fig. 1
:
The process of matrix-CFAR.

III
THE GEOMETRY OF HPD MATRIX MANIFOLDS

In this section, we review Riemannian geometry of HPD manifolds equipped with the AIRM, the LE metric, and the BW metric.
The corresponding Riemannian geometric means and medians are defined and solved numerically by Riemannian gradient descent algorithms.

III-A

The AIRM

The
N
-dimensional HPD matrix manifolds are represented by
\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
and
the tangent space at a point
\bm{P}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
is the space of
N
-dimensional Hermitian matrices denoted by
T_{\bm{P}}\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathscr{H}(N,\mathbb{C})
.
The tangent bundle is represented by
T\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})=\cup_{\bm{P}}T_{\bm{P}}\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
.
The AIRM is defined by

\displaystyle\langle\bm{A},\bm{B}\rangle_{\bm{P}}:=\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{A}\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{B}\right)

(11)

for
\bm{A},\bm{B}\in T_{\bm{P}}\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
.
The geodesic between two points
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{P}_{2}\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{t}\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}},\ t\in[0,1],

(12)

and its tangent vector at the point
\bm{P}_{1}
is

\dot{\bm{P}}(0)=\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{P}_{2}\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}.\\

(13)

Under the AIRM, the geodesic distance connecting two points
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
is
[
50
]

\displaystyle d_{\text{AIRM}}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\right)=\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{P}_{2}\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right\rVert_{F},

(14)

where
\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert_{F}:=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\right)}
is the Frobenius norm. The exponential map in the Riemannian manifold
\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
denotes
\operatorname{Exp}:T\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
defined by using geodesics.
In case of the AIRM, considering
\bm{P}(t)
as the geodesic (
12
) between two arbitrary HPD matrices
\bm{P}_{1}=\bm{P}(0),\bm{P}_{2}=\bm{P}(1)
, the exponential map leads to the endpoint
\bm{P}_{2}
of
\bm{P}(t)
, namely

\displaystyle\operatorname{Exp}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\dot{\bm{P}}(0)\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\dot{\bm{P}}(0)\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(15)

At the point
\bm{P}_{1}
, the logarithm map
\operatorname{Log}:\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})\times\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathscr{H}(N,\mathbb{C})
is the inverse of the exponential map, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{P}_{2}\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(16)

The midpoint/mean between
\bm{P}_{1}
and
\bm{P}_{2}
is denoted by
\bm{P}_{1}\#\bm{P}_{2}
and given by the Pusz–Woronowicz formula
[
51
]

\bm{P}_{1}\#\bm{P}_{2}=\bm{P}\left(t=\frac{1}{2}\right)=\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{P}_{2}\bm{P}_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(17)

This mean is symmetric with respect to
\bm{P}_{1}
and
\bm{P}_{2}
and is the only solution of the Riccati equation
[
52
]

\bm{X}\bm{P}_{1}^{-1}\bm{X}=\bm{P}_{2}.

(18)

The LE Metric

The LE metric has been applied to, for instance, the tensor filed and image data

53
,
54
,
55
]
.
In radar detection, it has been utilised for the outlier rejection
[

,

,

]
.
Some important structures and properties of HPD manifolds under the LE metric are summarised in Fig.

.

The LE metric is derived by leveraging the Lie group structure of
\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
under the group operation

\bm{P}_{1}\odot\bm{P}_{2}:=\exp\left(\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}+\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}\right)

(19)

for
\forall\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
,
where
\exp
and
\operatorname{Log}
denote the matrix exponential and logarithm; the latter is unique for HPD matrices, and should not be confused with the logarithmic map (
16
).
The LE metric of two elements
\bm{V}_{1},\bm{V}_{2}
in the tangent space at a point
\bm{P}
is defined as

\left\langle\bm{V}_{1},\bm{V}_{2}\right\rangle_{\bm{P}}=\left\langle\left(D_{\bm{P}}\operatorname{Log}\right)\bm{V}_{1},\left(D_{\bm{P}}\operatorname{Log}\right)\bm{V}_{2}\right\rangle_{\bm{I}},

(20)

where
\left(D_{\bm{P}}\operatorname{Log}\right)\bm{V}_{1}
is the directional derivative of the matrix logarithm at
\bm{P}
along
\bm{V}
and
\bm{I}
denotes the
N
-dimensional identity matrix.
The geodesic between
\bm{P}_{1}
and
\bm{P}_{2}
is given by

\bm{P}(t)=\exp\left((1-t)\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}+t\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}\right).

(21)

The exponential and logarithm maps for the LE metric are respectively given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Exp}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\dot{\bm{P}}(0)\right)=\exp\left(\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}+\left(D_{\bm{P}}\operatorname{Log}\right)\dot{\bm{P}}(0)\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\right)=\left(D_{\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}}\exp\right)\left(\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}\right),

(23)

where

\dot{\bm{P}}(0)=\exp\left(\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}\right).

(24)

Using (
20
), (
22
) and (
23
), the geodesic distance between
\bm{P}_{1}
and
\bm{P}_{2}
is derived as

\displaystyle d^{2}_{\text{LE}}(\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(D_{\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}}\exp\right)\left(\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}\right),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left(D_{\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}}\exp\right)\left(\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}\right)\right\rangle_{\bm{P}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1},\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}\right\rangle_{\bm{I}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{2}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{P}_{1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

III-C

The BW Metric

Here we introduce the BW metric on
\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
, and the BW distance between HPD matrices.
The BW metric at a point
\bm{P}
is defined by
[
52
,
60
]

\langle\bm{A},\bm{B}\rangle_{\bm{P}}:=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\bm{P}}\left[\bm{A}\right]\bm{B}\right)

(25)

for
\bm{A},\bm{B}\in T_{\bm{P}}\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\bm{P}}
is called the Lyapunov operator and
\mathcal{L}_{\bm{P}}[\bm{A}]
is the solution of the Lyapunov equation

\bm{P}\mathcal{L}_{\bm{P}}\left[\bm{A}\right]+\mathcal{L}_{\bm{P}}\left[\bm{A}\right]\bm{P}=\bm{A}.

(26)

The geodesic between two points
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
is derived by
[
52
,
60
]

\displaystyle\bm{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(1-t\right)^{2}\bm{P}_{1}+t^{2}\bm{P}_{2}

\displaystyle+t\left(1-t\right)\left(\bm{P}_{1}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-1}\#\bm{P}_{2}\right)+\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-1}\#\bm{P}_{2}\right)\bm{P}_{1}\right),

and we have

\dot{\bm{P}}(0)=\bm{P}_{1}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-1}\#\bm{P}_{2}-\bm{I}\right)+\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-1}\#\bm{P}_{2}-\bm{I}\right)\bm{P}_{1}.

(27)

The exponential map is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Exp}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\dot{\bm{P}}(0)\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{I}+\mathcal{L}_{\bm{P}_{1}}\left[\dot{\bm{P}}(0)\right]\right)\bm{P}_{1}\left(\bm{I}+\mathcal{L}_{\bm{P}_{1}}\left[\dot{\bm{P}}(0)\right]\right),

while the logarithm map is

\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\right)=\bm{P}_{1}\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-1}\#\bm{P}_{2}-\bm{I}\right)+\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{-1}\#\bm{P}_{2}-\bm{I}\right)\bm{P}_{1}.

For the BW metric, the geodesic distance, called the BW distance, connecting two arbitrary points
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}
is

d^{2}_{\text{BW}}\left(\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{P}_{1}\right)+\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{P}_{2}\right)-2\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{P}_{2}\bm{P}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

The BW geodesic distance connecting two arbitrary points has been introduced by Fréchet in
[
61
]
(see also
[
62
]
).

III-D

Riemannian Geometric Means and Medians

Based on Riemannian geometry introduced in Subsections
III-A
,
III-B
and
III-C
, the corresponding geometric means and medians of HPD matrices can be defined.

Definition 1
.

The Riemannian geometric mean of the HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is defined by

\underset{\bm{R}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})}{\operatorname{arg}\min}\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}d^{2}(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}),

(29)

where the function
d
is a geodesic distance on
\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
.
The geometric median of the HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is defined by

\underset{\bm{R}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})}{\operatorname{arg}\min}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}\right).

(30)

For example, when the function
d
is the Euclidean distance, the Riemannian (actually Euclidean) geometric mean is given by

\underset{\bm{R}\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})}{\operatorname{arg}\min}\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert\bm{R}-\bm{R}_{i}\right\rVert_{F}^{2},

(31)

which yields

\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\bm{R}_{i}.

(32)

This is exactly the arithmetic mean.

Generally, it can be quite challenging to show the uniqueness and existence of geometric means and medians. In
[
63
]
, the geometric median for a probability measure on a Riemannian manifold
was defined, with a natural condition to ensure its uniqueness.
The uniqueness and existence of the Riemannian geometric means under the AIRM and the LE metric were proved analytically (see, for instance,
[
64
,
54
]
).
The BW mean is unique and is the solution of equation (
48
) below
[
52
,
65
]
.
The uniqueness and existence of the Riemannian geometric medians are related to the curvature of the space.
Non-positive curvature leads to the unique median
[
66
]
.
For the LE metric, the curvature is zero
[
54
]
, and hence, the LE median exists uniquely.
However, the curvatures with respect to the AIRM and the BW metric do not satisfy the non-positiveness property, that makes an analytic proof difficult.
Under the BW metric, specifically, the curvature is nonnegative and then uniqueness and existence of barycenter cannot be proved mathematically.
In consequence, in some cases, barycenter computation could provide aberrant values.

In many cases, Riemannian geometrical means and medians cannot be obtained analytically and one often resorts to numerical methods such as the Riemannian gradient descent algorithm and the fixed-point algorithm
[
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
]
.

AIRM mean and median.

Theorem 2
.

The AIRM mean and median of the HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
can respectively be derived by the Riemannian gradient descent algorithms as follows
[
67
,
68
]

\bm{R}_{t+1}=\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2\eta_{t}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{i}^{-1}\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(33)

and

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\eta_{t}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\operatorname{Log}\left(\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{i}^{-1}\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)}{d_{\text{AIRM}}\left(\bm{R}_{t},\bm{R}_{i}\right)}\right)\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(34)

where
\eta_{t}
is the step size.

LE mean and median.

Theorem 3
.

For the HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, the LE mean can be derived in the closed form

\exp\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m}_{i=1}\operatorname{Log}\bm{R}_{i}\right),

(35)

a proof of which can be found in
[
54
]
.

The exponential map of the LE metric (
22
) is not explicit, and instead, we utilise the fixed-point algorithm for the LE median
[
71
]
.

Theorem 4
.

For
m
HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, the LE median can be derived by the fixed-point algorithm as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{t+1}=\exp

\displaystyle\left(\left(\sum\limits^{m}_{i=1}\frac{\operatorname{Log}\bm{R}_{i}}{d_{\text{LE}}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}_{t}\right)}\right)\right.

(36)

\displaystyle\left.\times\left(\sum\limits^{m}_{i=1}\frac{1}{d_{\text{LE}}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}_{t}\right)}\right)^{-1}\right).

Proof.

The LE median is the solution of the optimisation problem of which the objective function
G(\bm{R})
is given by

G(\bm{R})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{\text{LE}}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}\right).

(37)

By using the Fréchet derivative
[
72
]
, its Riemannian gradient,
\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)
, is derived
[
73
]
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right),\bm{Y}\right\rangle_{\bm{R}}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}G\left(\bm{R}+\varepsilon\bm{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{1}\left((\bm{R}-\bm{I})s+\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\frac{\operatorname{Log}\bm{R}-\operatorname{Log}\bm{R}_{i}}{d_{\text{LE}}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}\right)}\left((\bm{R}-\bm{I})s+\bm{I}\right)^{-1}ds\bm{Y}\right).

Solving
\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)=\bm{0}
, we obtain

\sum\limits^{m}_{i=1}\frac{\operatorname{Log}\bm{R}}{d_{\text{LE}}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}\right)}=\sum\limits^{m}_{i=1}\frac{\operatorname{Log}\bm{R}_{i}}{d_{\text{LE}}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}\right)},

(38)

which gives the fixed-point algorithm (
36
).
∎

BW mean and median.

The BW mean can be calculated by fixed-points algorithms ((
39
) and (
40
) below), whose convergence is known to be slow
[
74
]
.
To obtain the BW mean and median, we will introduce the Riemannian gradient descent algorithms which are relatively faster.

Theorem 5
.

For
m
HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, the BW mean can be computed by either of the following fixed-point algorithms
[
52
,
74
]
,

\bm{R}_{t+1}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\\

(39)

and

\bm{R}_{t+1}=\bm{R}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}\bm{R}_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(40)

Proof.

A detailed proof seems not available in the literature and is provided here.
Let
G(\bm{R})
be the objective function for the BW mean which is given by

G(\bm{R})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d^{2}_{\text{BW}}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}\right).

(41)

By using the Fréchet derivative
[
72
]
, its Riemannian gradient satisfies

\displaystyle\left\langle\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right),\bm{Y}\right\rangle_{\bm{R}}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}G\left(\bm{R}+\varepsilon\bm{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\bm{R}_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{R}_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\bm{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}\right)\bm{Y}\right),

(42)

where
\bm{Y}\in T_{\bm{R}}\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C}).

Note that we used the fact

\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bm{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\varepsilon)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\varepsilon)\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bm{P}(\varepsilon)\right),

where
\bm{P}(\varepsilon)\in\mathscr{P}(N,\mathbb{C})
.
From the definition of the BW metric (
25
), we have

\left\langle\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right),\bm{Y}\right\rangle_{\bm{R}}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\bm{R}}\left[\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)\right]\bm{Y}\right).

(43)

Comparing (
42
) and (
43
), we obtain

\mathcal{L}_{\bm{R}}\left[\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)\right]=\frac{2}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}\right).

(44)

Consequently, from the definition of the Lyapunov operator, the Riemannian gradient is

\displaystyle\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{R}\mathcal{L}_{\bm{R}}\left[\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)\right]+\mathcal{L}_{\bm{R}}\left[\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)\right]\bm{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\bm{R}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}\right)\bm{R}.

Since the BW mean is the solution of
\operatorname{grad}G\left(\bm{R}\right)=\bm{0}
, we have

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\bm{R}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}\right)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}\right)\bm{R}=\bm{0}.

(45)

By the Appendix, equation (
45
) is equivalent to

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}\right)=\bm{0}.

(46)

By using the symmetry
\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}^{-1}=\bm{R}^{-1}\#\bm{R}_{i}
, we obtain

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{R}^{-1}\#\bm{R}_{i}\right)=\bm{0},

(47)

and consequently,

\bm{R}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(48)

which yields the iterations (
39
) and (
40
).
∎

Theorem 6
.

For
m
HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, the BW mean and median can respectively be computed by the Riemannian gradient descent algorithms

\bm{R}_{t+1}=\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}_{t}^{-1}\right)\right)\bm{R}_{t}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bm{R}_{i}\#\bm{R}_{t}^{-1}\right)\right),

Proof.

V
ROBUST ANALYSIS THROUGH INFLUENCE FUNCTIONS

In the application to real problems, the observation data may also contain outliers, thus robustness about the estimator of the autocovariance matrix is of importance.
The robust analysis against outliers through the influence function has been commonly used
[
80
,
81
]
.
In this section, we review the definition of the influence function and conduct the robustness analysis.

Let
\overline{\bm{R}}
be the Riemannian geometric mean or median of given
m
HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, and let
\widehat{\bm{R}}
be the Riemannian geometric mean or median of the contaminated HPD matrices containing
n
outliers
\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
.
By considering the outliers as a perturbation
\varepsilon\left(\varepsilon\ll 1\right)
of the matrix
\overline{\bm{R}}
,
\widehat{\bm{R}}
can be rewritten as

\widehat{\bm{R}}=\overline{\bm{R}}+\varepsilon\bm{H}\left(\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right),

(60)

where
\bm{H}\left(\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\right)
is a Hermitian matrix and depends on the given HPD matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and the outliers
\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
.
We define the function

f\left(\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\right)\coloneqq\dfrac{\left\lVert\bm{H}\left(\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\right)\right\rVert_{F}}{\left\lVert\overline{\bm{R}}\right\rVert_{F}}

as the
influence function
.

We consider the mean case here and the median case is similar.
To compute the corresponding influence functions, the objective function
F(\bm{R})
of
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is defined by

F(\bm{R}):=(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d^{2}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}\right)+\varepsilon\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d^{2}\left(\bm{P}_{j},\bm{R}\right),

where
d
is the distance function for each mean. For medians, the discrete ‘integrand’ is replaced by
d
.
Since
\bm{\widehat{R}}
is the solution of
\operatorname{grad}F(\bm{R})=\bm{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{grad}d^{2}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{\widehat{R}}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{grad}d^{2}\left(\bm{P}_{j},\bm{\widehat{R}}\right)=\bm{0}.

Differentiating
\operatorname{grad}F(\widehat{\bm{R}})=\bm{0}
about
\varepsilon
at
\varepsilon=0
, a matrix equation with respect to
\bm{H}
is derived by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{grad}d^{2}\left(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{\widehat{R}}\right)\right)

(62)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{grad}d^{2}\left(\bm{P}_{j},\bm{\widehat{R}}\right)=\bm{0}.

An orthonormal basis of Hermitian matrices leads to the formulation of the matrix equation (
62
) as a linear system equation.
Details can be found in
[
41
,
82
]
.

In the next simulations,
m
number of
N
-dimensional data are sampled from
CN\left(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}\right)
given by Section
IV
.
The matrix
\overline{\bm{R}}
are computed from the
m
autocovariance matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of the sampling data.
Next,
n
outliers
\{\bm{P}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are mixed with
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and are modelled as the autocovariance matrix of the signal,
a\bm{s}+\bm{c}
, where
\bm{s}
is the target in (
52
),
\bm{c}
is the Gaussian clutter of
CN\left(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}\right)
,
and
a
is calculated from the SCR which is 40 dB.
The numerical simulations are conducted in the setting of
m
chosen as 50 and
n
ranging from 1 to 40.

Fig.
8
shows the influence functions computed by averaging 1000 times and displayed in the log scale.
The lower the influence functions are, the more robust the detector is.
Therefore, from Fig.

VI
CONCLUSION

The BW metric defined in HPD manifolds was applied to the matrix-CFAR detector and compared with other Riemannian metrics, including the AIRM and the LE metric, as well as the conventional AMF and ANMF. To derive the BW mean and median, Riemannian gradient descent algorithms were proposed and their computational complexity was investigated.
It was shown that the Riemannian gradient descent algorithm was more convergent than the fixed-point algorithms which have been proposed elsewhere.

Numerical simulations of the scenario using the ideal steering vector as the target showed that the matrix-CFAR with the BW metric outperformed all other detectors when the number of observation data is limited.
Additionally, it was observed that the detection performance of the AMF can be improved by replacing the SCM with the Riemannian geometric means and medians.
In the signal mismatched scenario, it was noted that the matrix-CFAR is more robust than the AMF and ANMF.
Robustness analysis and simulations indicated that the BW mean is among the most robust ones to outliers, of a similar order as the AIRM mean.

As future research, we plan to explore the practical applications of the matrix-CFAR technique on real-world field data.
