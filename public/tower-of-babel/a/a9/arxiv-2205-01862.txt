The Hardy-Weyl algebra
Jim Agler, John E. M c Carthy

Abstract
We study the algebra 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} generated by the Hardy operator H H and the operator M x M_{x} of multiplication by x x on L 2 ​ [ 0 , 1 ] L^{2}[0,1] . We call 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} the Hardy-Weyl algebra. We show that its quotient by the compact operators is isomorphic to the algebra of functions that are continuous on Λ \Lambda and analytic on the interior of Λ \Lambda for a planar set Λ \Lambda = [ − 1 , 0 ] ∪ 𝔻 ⁡ ( 1 , 1 ) ¯ [-1,0]\cup\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)} , which we call the lollipop. We find a Toeplitz-like short exact sequence for the C ∗ C^{*} -algebra generated by 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} . We study the operator Z = H − M x Z=H-M_{x} , show that its point spectrum is ( − 1 , 0 ] ∪ 𝔻 ( 1 , 1 ) (-1,0]\cup\mathbb{D}(1,1) , and that the eigenvalues grow in multiplicity as the points move to 0 0 from the left.

Introduction

The classical Weyl algebra is generated by the operators of
multiplication by
x
, denoted
M_{x}
, and differentiation, denoted by
D
. These operators satisfy the commutator relation

DM_{x}-M_{x}D\ =\ 1.

(1.1)

The algebra generated by these relations has been studied extensively in both algebra and operator theory—see e.g.
the books
[
14
,
12
,
19
,
9
]
.
In operator theory, the study is complicated by the fact that no bounded operators satisfy (
1.1
).
In this note, we shall study the associated algebras that arise when one replaces the differentiation operator
D
by
a bounded integration operator.

The Hardy operator
H
is defined on
L^{2}[0,1]
by

Hf(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt.

Let
V=M_{x}H
denote the Volterra operator

V:f\mapsto\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt,

which is a right inverse of
D
.
These operators give rise to a new set of relations instead of (
1.1
), namely

\displaystyle VM_{x}-M_{x}V

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle-V^{2}

\displaystyle HM_{x}-M_{x}H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-HM_{x}H\ =\ -HV.

We shall use
L^{2}
to mean
L^{2}[0,1]
throughout.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
denote the closure of the unital algebra generated by
H
and
M_{x}
in the norm topology of
B(L^{2})
.
We call
{\mathcal{A}}
the Hardy-Weyl algebra.

When is an operator in
{\mathcal{A}}
? Can all the elements be described?
Since the commutator of
H
and
M_{x}
is compact, we first describe
the quotient of
{\mathcal{A}}
by the compact operators. Define
\Lambda
, a subset of the plane, by

\Lambda\ =\ [-1,0]\cup\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)}.

We call
\Lambda
the
lollipop
. The
lollipop algebra

is the Banach algebra of functions that are continuous on
\Lambda
and analytic on
{\rm int}(\Lambda)
,
equipped with the maximum modulus norm.
Let
{\mathcal{K}}
denote the compact operators on
L^{2}
,
and let
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}={\mathcal{K}}\cap{\mathcal{A}}
,

Theorem 1.3
.

There is a Banach algebra isomorphism
\gamma
from
A(\Lambda)
onto
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
.
It is given by

\gamma(f)\ =\ f|_{[-1,0]}(-M_{x})+f|_{\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)}}(H)-f(0).

Let
\theta:{\mathcal{A}}\to A(\Lambda)
be defined by
\theta(T)=\gamma^{-1}([T])
, where
[T]
is the projection
of
T
onto
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
.
As a corollary to Theorem
1.3
we obtain that every element

{\mathcal{A}}
can be written uniquely as

T\ =\ M_{\phi}+g(H)+K,

(1.4)

where
\phi\in C([0,1])
,

,

, and

.

If we look at

, the

-algebra generated by

, we get something similar to the Toeplitz algebra short exact sequence.
See

There is a short exact sequence of

-algebras

In addition to studying the algebra generated by

and

.

Preliminaries

Definition 2.1
.

A
monomial operator
is a bounded linear operator
T:L^{2}[0,1]\to L^{2}[0,1]
with the property that there exist constants
c_{n}
and
p_{n}
so that

T:x^{n}\mapsto c_{n}\,x^{p_{n}}\qquad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(2.2)

We call it a
flat monomial operator
if there exists some
\tau
so that
p_{n}=n+\tau

for all
n
.

In
[
2
]
we showed that every flat monomial operator is in
{\rm AlgLat}(V)
, and hence by Theorem

1.7
in the weak closure of
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
.
The Volterra operator
V
is Hilbert-Schmidt, and hence compact. See e.g.
[
11
]
for a proof.

Let
H^{2}
denote the Hardy space of the unit disk, and
k_{w}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{w}z}
the Szegő kernel.
We shall let
S:f(z)\mapsto zf(z)
denote the unilateral shift on
H^{2}
.
Let

\beta(z)\ =\ \frac{1}{2-z},

and let
C_{\beta}:f\mapsto f\circ\beta
denote the composition operator of composing with
\beta
.

There is a unitary
U:L^{2}\to H^{2}
that is defined on monomials by

U:x^{s}\mapsto\frac{1}{s+1}k_{\frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{s}+1}},

(2.3)

and extended by linearity and continuity to the whole space.
If
T\in B(L^{2})
, we shall let
\widehat{T}
denote
UTU^{*}
.
It is easy to see that
U
is unitary, as it preserves inner products.
In
[
1
]
we prove that
U
is given by the formula

Uf(z)\ =\ \frac{1}{1-z}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)x^{\frac{z}{1-z}}dx,

(2.4)

and show that

\displaystyle\widehat{M_{x}}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle S^{*}C_{\beta}^{*}

\displaystyle\widehat{V}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle(1-S^{*})C_{\beta}^{*}

\displaystyle\widehat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-S^{*}.

If
X
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
, we shall let

C(X)
denote the Banach algebra of functions that are continuous on
X
, with the maximum modulus norm.
We shall let

A(X)
denote the subalgebra of functions that are continuous on
X

and analytic on the interior of

, and
P(X)
denote the closure of the polynomials in
C(X)
.
A theorem of Mergelyan
[
13
]
says that if the complement of

is connected, then
A(X)=P(X)
.

3
The Calkin Hardy-Weyl Algebra

We let
\mathcal{K}
denote the ideal of compact operators acting on
L^{2}
and set

\mathcal{K}_{0}={\mathcal{A}}\cap\mathcal{K}.

Evidently,
\mathcal{K}_{0}
is a 2-sided ideal in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Consequently, we may define an algebra
\mathcal{C}
, the
Calkin Hardy-Weyl algebra
, by

\mathcal{C}={\mathcal{A}}/\mathcal{K}_{0}

If
T\in{\mathcal{A}}
we let
[T]
denote the coset of
T
in
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,

[T]=\{T+K\,|\,K\in\mathcal{K}_{0}\}.

Proposition 3.1
.

\mathcal{C}
is an abelian Banach algebra.

Proof.

That
\mathcal{C}
is a Banach algebra follows from the fact that
\mathcal{K}_{0}
is closed in
{\mathcal{A}}
. To see that
\mathcal{C}
is abelian, observe that as
M_{x}
and
H
generate
{\mathcal{A}}
,

[M_{x}]
and
[H]
generate
\mathcal{C}
. Furthermore, as
M_{x}H=V\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
,

[M_{x}][H]=0.

(3.2)

Likewise, as
HM_{x}=(1-H)V\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
,

[H][M_{x}]=0,

(3.3)

so that in particular we have that

[M_{x}][H]=[H][M_{x}].

As
[M_{x}]
and
[H]
commute and generate
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{C}
is abelian.
∎

3.1
A Uniform Algebra Homeomorphically Isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}

We begin by defining an algebra by gluing together two simpler algebras whose maximal ideal spaces overlap at a single point. Let

{\mathcal{P}}\ =\ \{\ f=(f_{-},f_{+})\ :\ f_{-}\in C([-1,0]),\ f_{+}\in A(\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)})\text{ and }f_{-}(0)=f_{+}(0)\ \}

where we view
{\mathcal{P}}
as an algebra with the operations

cf=(cf_{-},cf_{+}),\ f+g=(f_{-}+g_{-},f_{+}+g_{+}),\text{and }fg=(f_{-}g_{-},f_{+}g_{+}),

and the norm

\|f\|=\max\big\{\max_{t\in[-1,0]}|f_{-}(t)|,\max_{z\in(\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)}}|f_{+}(z)|\ \big\}.

We abuse notation by letting

f(0)=f_{-}(0)

when
f\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

We note that if
f\in C([-1,0])
, then as
-M_{x}
is self-adjoint and has spectrum equal to
[-1,0]
, we may form the operator
f(-M_{x})
. Likewise, as
H
is cosubnormal and has spectrum equal to
\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)}
, if
g\in A(\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)})
, then we may form the operator
g(H)
. Concretely,

f(-M_{x})=M_{f(-x)}\ \text{ and }\ \widehat{g(H)}=M_{h}^{*},

where
h(z)=\overline{g(1-\bar{z})}
, and
M_{h}
denotes multiplication by
h
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
f\in C([-1,0])
and
g\in A(\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)})
, then

[f(-M_{x})][g(H)]=g(0)[f(-M_{x})]+f(0)[g(H)]-f(0)g(0).

Proof.

Since
[-1,0]
is a spectral set for
-M_{x}
and
\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)}
is a spectral set for
H
it suffices to prove the lemma in the special case when
f
and
g
are polynomials. Let
f(x)=f(0)+xf_{1}(x)
and
g(x)=g(0)+xg_{1}(x)
. Using (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) we see that

\displaystyle[f(-M_{x})][g(H)]

\displaystyle=\big([f(0)]+[-M_{x}][f_{1}(-M_{x})]\big)\big([g(0)]+[H][g_{1}(H)]\big)

\displaystyle=f(0)g(0)+g(0)[-M_{x}][f_{1}(-M_{x})]+f(0)[H][g_{1}(H)]

\displaystyle=f(0)g(0)+g(0)[f(-M_{x})-f(0)]+f(0)[g(H)-g(0)]

\displaystyle=g(0)[f(-M_{x})]+f(0)[g(H)]-f(0)g(0).

∎

If
f\in{\mathcal{P}}
we define
\gamma(f)\in{\mathcal{A}}
by the formula

\gamma(f)=f_{-}(-M_{x})+f_{+}(H)-f(0).

We also define
\Gamma:{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathcal{C}
by the formula

\Gamma(f)=[\gamma(f)]

Proposition 3.5
.

\Gamma
is a continuous unital homomorphism.

Proof.

\gamma
is linear and
\gamma(1)=1
. Therefore,
\Gamma
is linear and
\Gamma(1)=1
. Also,

\displaystyle\|\Gamma(f)\|

\displaystyle=\|[\gamma(f)]\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\gamma(f)\|

\displaystyle=\|f_{-}(-M_{x})+f_{+}(H)-f(0)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{-}(-M_{x})\|+\|f_{+}(H)\|+|f(0)|

\displaystyle=\max_{t\in[-1,0]}|f_{-}(t)|+\max_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)}}|f_{+}(z)|+|f(0)|

\displaystyle\leq 3\|f\|,

so
\Gamma
is continuous.

Finally, to see that
\Gamma
preserves products, fix
f,g\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

\displaystyle\Gamma(f)\Gamma(g)

\displaystyle=[\gamma(f)]\ [\gamma(g)]

\displaystyle=[f_{-}(-M_{x})+f_{+}(H)-f(0)]\ [g_{-}(-M_{x})+g_{+}(H)-g(0)]

\displaystyle=\Big([f_{-}(-M_{x})][g_{-}(-M_{x})]+[f_{+}(H)][g_{+}(H)]\Big)

\displaystyle\ \ +\Big([f_{-}(-M_{x})][g_{+}(H)]+[g_{-}(-M_{x})][f_{+}(H)]\Big)

\displaystyle\ \ -\Big(f(0)[g_{-}(-M_{x})+g_{+}(H)]+g(0)[f_{-}(-M_{x})+f_{+}(H)]\Big)

\displaystyle\ \ +f(0)g(0)

\displaystyle=\ \ A+B-C+f(0)g(0).

But

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\Big([f_{-}(-M_{x})][g_{-}(-M_{x})]+[f_{+}(H)][g_{+}(H)]\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big([f_{-}g_{-}(-M_{x})]+[f_{+}g_{+}(H)]-f(0)g(0)\Big)+f(0)g(0)

\displaystyle=[\gamma(fg)]+f(0)g(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(fg)+f(0)g(0),

and using Lemma
3.4
, we see that

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\Big([f_{-}(-M_{x})][g_{+}(H)]\Big)+\Big([g_{-}(-M_{x})][f_{+}(H)]\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(g(0)[f_{-}(-M_{x})]+[f(0)g_{+}(H)]-f(0)g(0)\Big)+\Big(f(0)[g_{-}(-M_{x})]+[g(0)f_{+}(H)]-f(0)g(0)\Big)

\displaystyle=C-2f(0)g(0).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Gamma(f)\Gamma(g)

\displaystyle=A+B-C+f(0)g(0)

\displaystyle=(\Gamma(fg)+f(0)g(0))+(C-2f(0)g(0))-C+f(0)g(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(fg).

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

If
p
is a polynomial in two variables and we define
f\in{\mathcal{P}}
by letting

f_{-}(t)=p(t,0),\ t\in[-1,0]\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ f_{+}(z)=p(0,z),\ z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)},

then

p([-M_{x}],[H])=\Gamma(f).

Proof.

If
p=p(x,y)
is a polynomial in two variables and we let

q(x,y)=p(x,y)-p(x,0)-p(0,y)+p(0,0),

then

p(x,y)=p(x,0)+p(0,y)-p(0,0)+q(x,y)

and

q([-M_{x}],[H])=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle p([-M_{x}],[H])

\displaystyle=p([-M_{x}],0)+p(0,[H])-p(0,0)

\displaystyle=f_{-}([M_{x}])+f_{+}([h])-f(0)

\displaystyle=[\gamma(f)]

\displaystyle=\Gamma(f).

∎

Corollary 3.7
.

The range of

\Gamma

\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma

by recalling that
[-M_{x}]
and

Lemma
3.6
suggests that we consider the subset
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
of
{\mathcal{P}}
defined by

{\mathcal{P}}_{0}=\{f\in{\mathcal{P}}\,|\,f_{-}\text{ and }f_{+}\text{ are polynomials}\}.

We note that it follows from the facts that the polynomials are dense in both

Lemma 3.8
.

If
s\in[-1,0]
, then

|f_{-}(s)|\leq\|\Gamma(f)\|

(3.9)

for all
f\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

As
f
is continuous, it suffices to prove the lemma under the assumption that
s\in(-1,0)
. For
n
satisfying
1/n<\min\{s,1-s\}
we define a unit vector
\chi_{n}\in L^{2}
by the formula

\chi_{n}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}&\mbox{if }|t-s|\leq 1/n\\
0&\mbox{if }|t-s|>1/n\end{array}\right.

We observe that the mean value theorem for integrals implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle g\,\chi_{n},\chi_{n}\rangle=g(s)

whenever
g\in C([0,1])
. Also, as
\chi_{n}\to 0
weakly,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|K\chi_{n}\|=0

whenever
K
is a compact operator acting on
L^{2}
. In particular, as
V
is compact and
V\chi_{n}(t)=0
when
t\in[0,s-1/n)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|H\chi_{n}\|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|M_{1/x}V\chi_{n}\|=0.

More generally, if
q
is a polynomial and
q(0)=0
, write
q(z)=zr(z)
, and we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|q(H)\chi_{n}\|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|r(H)\ H\chi_{n}\|=0.

Now fix
f\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
and a compact operator
K
acting on
L^{2}
. Using the observations in the previous paragraph we have that

\displaystyle\langle(\gamma(f)+K)\ \chi_{n},\chi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\langle(f_{-}(-M_{x})+f_{+}(H)-f(0)+K)\ \chi_{n},\chi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\langle f_{-}(-x)\chi_{n},\chi_{n}\rangle+\langle(f_{+}-f_{+}(0))(H)\ \chi_{n},\chi_{n}\rangle+\langle K\ \chi_{n},\chi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\to\

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ f_{-}(s)\qquad\qquad+\qquad\qquad\ \ 0\qquad\qquad\qquad\ +\qquad 0

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\ \ f_{-}(s).

Therefore, as
\|\chi_{n}\|=1
,

|f_{-}(s)|\leq\|\gamma(f)+K\|

for all
f\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
and
K
any compact operator acting on
L^{2}
.
Hence,

|f_{-}(s)|\leq\inf_{K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{0}}\|\gamma(f)+K\|=\|\Gamma(f)\|

for all
f\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
. As
\Gamma
is continuous and
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
is dense in
{\mathcal{P}}
, it follows that (
3.9
) holds for all
f\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

If
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)}
, then

|f_{+}(z)|\leq\|\Gamma(f)\|

(3.11)

for all
f\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

We first observe that as
f_{+}\in A(\overline{\mathbb{D}(1,1)})
, by the Maximum Modulus Theorem it suffices to prove the lemma under the assumption that
z=1+\tau
where
\tau\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{-1\}
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}
, let

\Upsilon_{\alpha}=U^{*}\frac{k_{-\bar{\alpha}}}{\|k_{-\bar{\alpha}}\|},

where
U
is as in (
2.3
).
Clearly, as
k_{-\bar{\alpha}}/\|k_{-\bar{\alpha}}\|
is a unit vector and
U^{*}
is unitary,
\Upsilon_{\alpha}
is a unit vector. Also, as

(1-S^{*})\frac{k_{-\bar{\alpha}}}{\|k_{-\bar{\alpha}}\|}=(1+\alpha)\frac{k_{-\bar{\alpha}}}{\|k_{-\bar{\alpha}}\|},

it follows that
H\Upsilon_{\alpha}=(1+\alpha)\Upsilon_{\alpha}
, and more generally,

f_{+}(H)\Upsilon_{\alpha}\ =\ f_{+}(1+\alpha)\Upsilon_{\alpha}

(3.12)

for all
f\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

Now notice that (
2.3
) implies that

\Upsilon_{\alpha}\ =\ \frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha|^{2}}}{1+\alpha}x^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}.

Claim 3.13
.

If
\rho>0
and
\tau\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{-1\}
, then

\lim_{\alpha\to\tau}\langle x^{\rho}\Upsilon_{\alpha},\Upsilon_{\alpha}\rangle=0.

(3.14)

Proof.

First note that

\rho-(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}+\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{1+\bar{\alpha}})+1=\rho+\frac{1-|\alpha|^{2}}{|1+\alpha|^{2}},

so that

\int_{0}^{1}x^{\rho-(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}+\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{1+\bar{\alpha}})}dx\ =\ \Big(\rho+\frac{1-|\alpha|^{2}}{|1+\alpha|^{2}}\Big)^{-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\langle x^{\rho}\Upsilon_{\alpha},\Upsilon_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1-|\alpha|^{2}}{|1+\alpha|^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}x^{\rho-(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}+\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{1+\bar{\alpha}})}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1-|\alpha|^{2}}{|1+\alpha|^{2}}\Big(\rho+\frac{1-|\alpha|^{2}}{|1+\alpha|^{2}}\Big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-|\alpha|^{2}}{|1+\alpha|^{2}\rho\ +1-|\alpha|^{2}}.

Therefore, if
\rho>0
and
\tau\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{-1\}
, (
3.14
) holds.
∎

Observe that if
q
is a polynomial and
\tau\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{-1\}
, then Claim
3.13
implies that
\langle q\Upsilon_{\alpha},\Upsilon_{\alpha}\rangle\to q(0)
as

. In particular,

\lim_{\alpha\to\tau}\langle q(-M_{x})\Upsilon_{\alpha},\Upsilon_{\alpha}\rangle=0

(3.15)

whenever
\tau\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{-1\}
and
q
is a polynomial satisfying

.

We now conclude the proof of the lemma. We need to show that if

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\langle(f_{-}-f_{-}(0))(-M_{x})\Upsilon_{\alpha},\Upsilon_{\alpha}\rangle+\langle f_{+}(H)\Upsilon_{\alpha},\Upsilon_{\alpha}\rangle+\langle K\Upsilon_{\alpha},\Upsilon_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad 0\qquad\qquad\qquad+\ \ \ \ f_{+}(1+\tau)\ \ \ \ +\ \ 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{+}(1+\tau).

as

∎

Lemma 3.16

\Gamma
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

In the proof of Proposition

for all

∎

Putting together the results of Subsection

Theorem 3.17

Theorem 4.2

(4.3)

Recall from Definition

.

For

, let us write

for the subspace of

that vanishes a.e.
off

, and let

denote projection onto

. For

we write

to denote the rank one operator

\phi\otimes\psi:f\ \mapsto\ \langle f,\psi\rangle\phi.

The key observation is the following:

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
\phi\in L^{2}[t,1]
and
\psi\in L^{2}[0,t]
. Then
\phi\otimes\psi=M_{\phi}VM_{\psi}^{*}
.

Proof:
We have

M_{\phi}VM_{\psi}^{*}f(x)\ =\ \phi(x)\int_{0}^{x}f(s)\overline{\psi(s)}ds.

The right-hand side is
0
if

, and
\phi(x)\langle f,\psi\rangle
if

.

\Box

Lemma 5.2
.

Every finite rank operator on

can be written as an integral operator whose kernel is in

Proof:
Let

. Define

Then

, and

is in

.

\Box

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

be in

. Then

is in

if and only if

for

.

Proof:
Sufficiency is clear. To prove necessity, assume that for
some

, the kernel

is not
0
a.e. As an integral operator is zero if and only if the kernel is
0
a.e.,
this means that the corresponding integral operator is non-zero, and hence
T
maps a
function in

to a function that is not

a.e. on

.

\Box

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

be a rank-one operator. Then

is in
{\rm AlgLat}(V)

if and only if for some

is in

(i.e.

Proof:
The first part follows from Lemma

and

.

For the general case, choose continuous functions

and

that converge to

and

.
It follows from Lemma

converges to

.

\Box

Theorem 5.5
.

Let

be a compact operator in
{\rm AlgLat}(V)
. Then

,
and can be approximated in norm by finite rank operators in

Proof:
Note that

means that for all

.

Let

. First, consider

by a finite rank

.

\Box

6
The operator
H-M_{x}

Let us write
Z
for the operator
H-M_{x}
.
We know that
[Z]
generates the Calkin Hardy-Weyl algebra
\mathcal{C}
.
By Theorem
3.17
we know that the spectrum of
[Z]
in
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
is
\Lambda
.
It is not surprising that
\mathbb{D}(1,1)
are eigenvalues of
Z
, since they are eigenvalues of
H
.
It is perhaps surprising that every point in the stick, except
-1
, is also an eigenvalue.
Moreover as we move up the stick to the bulb of the lollipop, the eigenvalues increase in multiplicity.

Theorem 6.1
.

(i)
\sigma_{p}(Z)=\mathbb{D}(1,1)\cup(-1,0]
.

(ii) The point spectrum of
Z^{*}
is empty.

(iii) The spectrum of
Z
is
\Lambda
.

Proof:
(i) Suppose
(Z-\lambda)f=0
.
Let
F(x)=Vf(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt
.
Then we have

\frac{1}{x}F(x)\ =\ (x+\lambda)f(x).

As
F^{\prime}(x)=f(x)
, we get the equation

\frac{1}{x}F(x)=(x+\lambda)F^{\prime}(x),

(6.2)

with the boundary condition

\quad F(0)\ =\ 0.

(6.3)

The function
F
is continuous. Let
\Omega
denote the relatively open subset of
[0,1]
on which it is non-zero.

We get that the solution of
(
6.2
), with
\lambda\neq 0
, is

F(x)\ =\ c\left(\frac{x}{x+\lambda}\right)^{1/\lambda}\chi_{\Omega}(x)\ ,

(6.4)

where the constant
c
can a priori be different on different components of
\Omega
(though we show
below that
\Omega
is actually connected). Hence

f(x)\ =\ cx^{\frac{1}{\lambda}-1}(x+\lambda)^{-1-\frac{1}{\lambda}}\chi_{\Omega}(x).

(6.5)

Case:
\Omega=[0,1]
.

For
f
to be in
L^{2}
with
c\neq 0
, we need

\Re(\frac{1}{\lambda}-1)\ >\ -\frac{1}{2},

which is the same as
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}(1,1)
.
For
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}(1,1)
, we get the eigenfunctions

f(x)\ =\ x^{\frac{1}{\lambda}-1}(x+\lambda)^{-1-\frac{1}{\lambda}}.

(6.6)

When
\lambda=0
, we get

F(x)\ =\ ce^{-1/x},

and

f(x)\ =\ c\frac{1}{x^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{x}}.

(6.7)

Now suppose that
\Omega
is not all of
[0,1]
. Decompose
\Omega
as a union of disjoint non-empty intervals. On each interval, we have that
f
is given by (
6.5
), with some
constant
c
that can depend on the interval.
Choose such an interval,
I
. Then
0
cannot be an end-point of
I
, or we would have that
F
is given by (
6.4
), and this is discontinuous at the right-hand end-point of
I
.

So assume that the left-hand end-point of
I
is
t>0
. Then the boundary condition (
6.3
) is replaced by
F(t)=0
.
On
I
, we have

F(x)\ =\ c\left(\frac{x}{x+\lambda}\right)^{1/\lambda},

so to have
F(t)=0
we need
\lambda=-t
. By continuity,
F
cannot vanish again, so we conclude that
I=(t,1]
and that for

\lambda\in(-1,0)

the function

f(x)\ =\ x^{\frac{1}{\lambda}-1}(x+\lambda)^{-1-\frac{1}{\lambda}}\chi_{[-\lambda,1]}(x)

(6.8)

is an eigenvector of
Z
with eigenvalue
\lambda
.

(ii) As
H^{*}f(x)=\int_{x}^{1}\frac{1}{t}f(t)dt
, the eigenvalue equation becomes

G(x)\ =\ (x+\lambda)f(x),

where

G(x)\ =\ \int_{x}^{1}\frac{1}{t}f(t)dt

As
G^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{x}f(x)
, we get the differential equation

G(x)\ =\ -x(x+\lambda)G^{\prime}(x),\quad G(1)=0.

(6.9)

Solving for
G
, we get

G(x)\ =\ cx^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}}(x+\lambda)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\,\chi_{\{G\neq 0\}}.

The only solution on an interval that vanishes on the right end-point is the zero solution.

(iii) We know

\sigma_{e}(Z)=\partial\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda=\overline{\sigma_{p}(Z)}\subseteq\sigma(Z).

If
\lambda
is a point in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Lambda
, it must be a Fredholm point.
By (i), it is not an eigenvalue of
Z
, and by (ii) it is not an eigenvalue of
Z^{*}
.
Therefore
Z-\lambda
has trivial kernel and cokernel, and closed range. Therefore it is invertible,
and
\lambda
is in the resolvent of
Z
.

\Box

Not only are points on the stick of the lollipop eigenvalues, there is some additional smoothness.
By a generalized eigenvector of order
n
we
mean a vector
f
that satisfies
(Z-\lambda)^{n+1}f=0
but
(Z-\lambda)^{n}f\neq 0
.

We shall prove the case
\lambda=0
first.

Lemma 6.10
.

At

, the operator
Z
has generalized eigenvectors of all orders.

Proof:
We want to show that if we let
f_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{x^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{x}}
from
(
6.7
), then for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists

so that

Zf_{n+1}\ =\ f_{n}.

(6.11)

Claim 6.12
.

For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a polynomial
p_{n}
of degree
2n+2
, with lowest order term
of degree
n+2
, so that the functions

satisfy (

).

We prove this by induction on

. It is true when
n=0
. Assume we have proved it up to level

,
and we want to prove it for

.
So we wish to solve the equation

Zf_{n+1}\ =\ f_{n}=p_{n}(\frac{1}{x})e^{-\frac{1}{x}}

(6.13)

and show that the solution is of the form

f_{n+1}(x)\ =\ p_{n+1}(\frac{1}{x})e^{-\frac{1}{x}}.

(6.14)

Writing
F_{n+1}
for

, equation (

) is

\displaystyle(H-M_{x})f_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f_{n+1}(t)dt-xf_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}F_{n+1}(x)-xF_{n+1}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{n}(x).

This gives us the linear differential equation

F_{n+1}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{x^{2}}F_{n+1}(x)\ =\ -\frac{1}{x}f_{n}(x).

Multiply by the integrating factor
e^{\frac{1}{x}}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[e^{\frac{1}{x}}F_{n+1}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x}e^{\frac{1}{x}}f_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x}p_{n}(\frac{1}{x}).

Therefore

e^{\frac{1}{x}}F_{n+1}(x)\ =\ q_{n}(\frac{1}{x}),

where
q_{n}
is a polynomial of degree
2n+2
that may have a constant term, and whose next lowest order term is of degree
n+2
.
This gives

\displaystyle f_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[e^{-\frac{1}{x}}q_{n}(\frac{1}{x})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{x}}\left[\frac{1}{x^{2}}q_{n}(\frac{1}{x})-\frac{1}{x^{2}}q_{n}^{\prime}(\frac{1}{x})\right]

Let

p_{n+1}(x)\ =\ x^{2}q_{n}(x)-x^{2}q_{n}^{\prime}(x).

The degree of
p_{n+1}
is two higher than
q_{n}
, so it is
2(n+1)+2
. There may be a term of order
2
;
the next lowest order term is
n+3=(n+1)+2
.
But as
Zf_{0}=0
, one can subtract a multiple of
f_{0}
from
f_{n+1}
without changing
(
6.13
), so we can assume that
p_{n+1}
has no term of order
2
.

So we have proved Claim
6.12
. As any function of the form (
6.14
) is in
L^{2}
,
we are done.

\Box

For points in the stick, a similar method works, but there are restrictions when requiring the
generalized eigenvectors to be in
L^{2}
. Here is one result.

Lemma 6.15
.

Let
\lambda\in(-1,0)
. Then
Z
has a generalized eigenvalue of order
1
at
\lambda
if and only if

-\frac{2}{3}<\lambda<0
.

Proof:
Let
s=-\lambda
.

f_{0}(x)\ =\ \left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}\frac{1}{x(x-s)}\chi_{[s,1]}.

(6.16)

All the functions below are supported on
[s,1]
.
We wish to find a function
f_{1}
that satisfies

(Z+s)f_{1}\ =\ f_{0}.

(6.17)

Writing
F_{1}
for
Vf_{1}(x)=\int_{s}^{x}f_{1}(t)dt
, this becomes

\frac{1}{x}F_{1}-(x-s)F_{1}^{\prime}\ =\ f_{0}.

or

F_{1}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{x(x-s)}F_{1}\ =\ -\frac{1}{x-s}f_{0}.

(6.18)

An integrating factor for (
6.18
) is
\left(\frac{x}{x-s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}
.
This yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[\left(\frac{x}{x-s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}F_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{x}{x-s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}\frac{1}{x-s}f_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x(x-s)^{2}}.

Integrating, we get

\left(\frac{x}{x-s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}F_{1}\ =\ \frac{1}{s^{2}}\log\frac{x-s}{x}+\frac{1}{s}\frac{1}{x-s}+c.

Dividing through by the integrating factor and differentiating, we get

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s^{2}}\left[\left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}-1}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\log\frac{x-s}{x}+\left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}\frac{s}{x(x-s)}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{s}\left[-\frac{1}{(x-s)^{2}}\left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}+\frac{1}{x-s}\left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}-1}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle+c\left[\left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}-1}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right].

We can choose
c=0
, since it is the coefficient of
f_{0}
. This gives

f_{1}(x)\ =\ \left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}\left[\frac{1}{s^{2}}\frac{1}{x(x-s)}\log\frac{x-s}{x}+\frac{1-s}{s}\frac{1}{x(x-s)^{2}}\right].

(6.19)

Examining this expression, we see that
f_{1}
is smooth on
(s,1]
, and the first term

\frac{1}{s^{2}}\frac{(x-s)^{\frac{1}{s}-1}}{x^{\frac{1}{s}+1}}\log\frac{x-s}{x}

vanishes at
s
for every
s<1
. However the second term

\frac{1-s}{s}\ \frac{(x-s)^{\frac{1}{s}-2}}{x^{\frac{1}{s}+1}}

has a singularity that grows like

(x-s)^{\frac{1}{s}-2}
.
This is integrable for every
s<1
, but it is only in
L^{2}[s,1]
for
s<\frac{2}{3}
.
So we have shown that (
6.17
) has a solution
f_{1}
in
L^{2}
if and only if

\lambda>-\frac{2}{3}
.

\Box

One can repeat the argument of Lemma
6.15
to get higher order generalized eigenvectors,
as
\lambda
gets closer to
0
.

Lemma 6.20
.

Let
m\geq 1
.
Let
\lambda
lie in the interval
(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)
. Then
Z
has generalized eigenvectors up to order
m
at
\lambda
.

Proof:
We shall inductively find functions
f_{n}
satisfying
(Z-\lambda)f_{n+1}=f_{n}
, with
f_{0}
as in (
6.16
).
Let
s=-\lambda
, and
write

\Phi(x)\ =\ \left(\frac{x-s}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}\chi_{[s,1]}(x).

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{0}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\Phi(x)\frac{1}{x(x-s)}

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\Phi(x)\left[\frac{1}{s^{2}}\frac{1}{x(x-s)}\log\frac{x-s}{x}+\frac{1-s}{s}\frac{1}{x(x-s)^{2}}\right].

Writing
F_{n+1}
for
Vf_{n+1}=\int_{s}^{x}f_{n+1}(t)dt
, we want to solve

\frac{1}{x}F_{n+1}(x)-(x-s)F_{n+1}^{\prime}(x)\ =\ f_{n}(x).

(6.21)

After multiplying by the integrating factor
1/\Phi
, we have

\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\Phi}F_{n+1}\right]\ =\ -\frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{1}{x-s}f_{n}(x).

(6.22)

Claim 6.23
.

There are constants
M_{n}
such that the functions
f_{n}
satisfy

|f_{n}(x)|\ \leq\ M_{n}(x-s)^{\frac{1}{s}-n-1}\qquad\forall x\in(s,1].

(6.24)

Proof of Claim
6.23
:

By induction on
n
. It is true when
n=0
. Assume it is true up to
n
.
From (
6.22
) we get for
x\in(s,1]
:

\frac{1}{\Phi(x)}F_{n+1}(x)\ =\ \int_{x}^{1}\frac{1}{\Phi(t)}\frac{1}{t-s}f_{n}(t)dt+c_{n}.

(6.25)

By the inductive hypothesis, the integrand in (
6.25
) is
O(t-s)^{-n-2}
, so the integral is

O(x-s)^{-n-1}
.
So
F_{n+1}
satisfies

F_{n+1}(x)\ =\ c_{n}\Phi(x)+O(x-s)^{\frac{1}{s}-n-1}.

(6.26)

From (
6.21
) we have

f_{n+1}(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{x(x-s)}F_{n+1}(x)-\frac{1}{x-s}f_{n}(x).

(6.27)

When we use (
6.26
) for
F_{n+1}
,
we get

f_{n+1}(x)\ =\ c_{n}f_{0}(x)-\frac{1}{x-s}f_{n}(x)+O(x-s)^{\frac{1}{s}-n-2}.

Now
the claim follows from the inductive hypothesis on
f_{n}
.
\Box

It follows from (
6.27
) that that
f_{m}
is continuous on
(s,1]
, and Claim
6.23
shows that its singularity at
s
is of order

(x-s)^{\frac{1}{s}-m-1}
. This means
f_{m}
is in
L^{2}
provided

\frac{1}{s}-m-1\ >\ -\frac{1}{2},

which is the same as
s<\frac{2}{2m+1}
.

\Box

For later use, let us note that if you track the constants
M_{n}
in Claim
6.23
, you can show:

Lemma 6.28
.

In Claim
6.23
one can take
M_{0}=\frac{1}{(s^{1+1/s})}
and the consants
M_{n}
satisfy

M_{n+1}\leq M_{n}\left(1+\frac{M_{0}}{n+1}\right).

Lemma 6.29
.

Let
m\geq 1
.
On the interval
(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)
one can choose the generalized eigenvectors of order
n

of
Z-\lambda
continuously in
\lambda
, for every
n\leq m
, and satisfying
(Z-\lambda)f_{\lambda,n}=f_{\lambda,n-1}
,
for every
1\leq n\leq m
.

Proof:
Let us write
f_{\lambda,n}
for the choice of generalized eigenvector of order
n
at
\lambda
.
Write

\Phi(\lambda,x)\ =\ \left(\frac{x+\lambda}{x}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}}\chi_{[-\lambda,1]}(x).

We have

f_{\lambda,0}(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{x(x+\lambda)}\Phi(\lambda,x).

On every compact subset
K
, of
(-1,0)
the functions
\{f_{\lambda,0}:\lambda\in K\}
are uniformly bounded,
and
\lim_{\lambda^{\prime}\to\lambda}f_{\lambda^{\prime},0}(x)=f_{\lambda,0}(x)
a.e.,
so the map
\lambda\mapsto f_{\lambda,0}
is continuous as a map from
(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)
into
L^{2}
.

For higher
n
, we find
f_{n+1}
as in Lemma
6.20
. At each stage, we take the constant
c_{n}
in (
6.26
) to be
0
. (We can do this because
c_{n}f_{\lambda,0}
will be in the kernel of
Z-\lambda
.)
This gives us

f_{\lambda,n+1}(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{x(x-s)}\Phi(\lambda,x)\int_{x}^{1}\frac{1}{\Phi(\lambda,t)}\frac{1}{t-s}f_{\lambda,n}(t)dt-\frac{1}{x-s}f_{\lambda,n}(x).

Moreover we have
f_{\lambda,n}(x)=0
if
x<-\lambda
and

|f_{\lambda,n}(x)|\ \leq\ M_{n,\lambda}(x+\lambda)^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}-n-1},\qquad x>-\lambda.

By Lemma
6.28
, we have that
each
M_{n,\lambda}
can be chosen uniformly in
\lambda

for
\lambda
in
(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)
.
For any interval
I
of length
\delta
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{I}|f_{\lambda,n}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle M_{n}\int_{0}^{\delta}|x+\lambda|^{-\frac{2}{\lambda}-2n-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{n}\frac{1}{-\frac{2}{\lambda}-2n-1}\delta^{-\frac{2}{\lambda}-2n-1}.

Therefore, as
\lambda
ranges over any compact subset of
(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)
, the functions
|f_{\lambda,n}(x)|^{2}
are uniformly
integrable in
x
. So, by the Vitali convergence theorem, the map

\displaystyle(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)

\displaystyle\ \to\

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle f_{\lambda,n}

is continuous.

\Box

These lemmas say that operators in the closed algebra generated by
Z
have certain smoothness properties when mapped by
\sigma
into

A(\Lambda)
.
The functions get smoother as we get closer to
0
.

Theorem 6.30
.

Let
X
be in the norm-closed algebra generated by
Z
. Then
\theta(X)
is
C^{m}

on
(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)
.

Proof:
Let
\theta(X)=\phi\in A(\Lambda)
.
Let
p_{j}
be a sequence of polynomials so that
\|p_{j}(Z)-X\|\to 0
.
It follows from Theorem
3.17
that
p_{j}
converges to
\phi
uniformly on
\Lambda
.

Case:
m=1
.
Let
f_{\lambda,n}
be as in Lemma
6.29
.
For any polynomial
p
, we have

\langle p(Z)f_{\lambda,0},f_{\lambda,0}\rangle\ =\ \|f_{\lambda,0}\|^{2}p(\lambda).

Moreover,

p(Z)f_{\lambda,1}\ =\ p(\lambda)f_{\lambda,1}+p^{\prime}(\lambda)f_{\lambda,0}.

Let
g^{1}_{\lambda}
be the linear combination of
f_{\lambda,0}
and
f_{\lambda,1}
that satisfies
\langle f_{\lambda,0},g^{1}_{\lambda}\rangle=1
and
\langle f_{\lambda,1},g^{1}_{\lambda}\rangle=0
.
Then

\langle p(Z)f_{\lambda,1},g^{1}_{\lambda}\rangle\ =\ p^{\prime}(\lambda).

So as functions on
(-\frac{2}{3},0)
, we get that
p_{j}^{\prime}(\lambda)
converges to some function
\psi(\lambda)=\langle Xf_{\lambda,1},g^{1}_{\lambda}\rangle
.

Claim 6.31
.

For all
x
in
(-\frac{2}{3},0)
, we have

\phi^{\prime}(x)=\psi(x)
, and
\psi
is continuous.

We have
g^{1}_{\lambda}=a_{\lambda,0}f_{\lambda,0}+a_{\lambda,1}f_{\lambda,1}
,
where the coefficients
a_{\lambda,0}
and
a_{\lambda,1}
solve the linear system

\begin{pmatrix}\langle f_{\lambda,0},f_{\lambda,0}\rangle&\langle f_{\lambda,0},f_{\lambda,1}\rangle\\
\langle f_{\lambda,1},f_{\lambda,0}\rangle&\langle f_{\lambda,1},f_{\lambda,1}\rangle\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{\lambda,0}\\
a_{\lambda,1}\end{pmatrix}\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

By Lemma
6.29
, since
f_{\lambda,0}
and
f_{\lambda,1}
are continuous in
\lambda
, so
as they are linearly independent,
we have that
g^{1}_{\lambda}
is also continuous in
\lambda
.
Therefore
\psi
is continuous, and

p_{j}^{\prime}
converges to
\psi
locally uniformly on
(-\frac{2}{3},0)
.
As
\int_{-\frac{1}{3}}^{x}p_{j}^{\prime}(t)dt=\phi(x)-\phi(-\frac{1}{3})
converges to
\int_{-\frac{1}{3}}^{x}\psi(t)dt
, we get that

\phi^{\prime}(x)=\psi(x)
.

\Box

We have shown that
\phi
is in
C^{1}(-\frac{2}{3},0)
. A similar argument with higher derivatives proves that
\phi
is in
C^{m}(-\frac{2}{2m+1},0)
.

\Box

Of course one can also find generalized eigenvectors for
Z
of all orders at points in
\mathbb{D}(1,1)
,
but we already know that
\theta(X)
is analytic on
\mathbb{D}(1,1)
for every
X\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
