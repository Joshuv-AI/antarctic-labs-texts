A geometric reduction method for some fully nonlinear first-order PDEs on semi-Riemannian manifolds
Juan Carlos Fernández 1, Eddaly Guerra-Velasco 2, Oscar Palmas 3, Boris A. Percino-Figueroa 4

Abstract
Given a semi-Riemannian manifold ( M , ⟨ ⋅ , ⋅ ⟩ g ) , (M,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{g}), we use the transnormal functions defined on M M to reduce fully nonlinear first order PDEs of the form F ⁡ ( x , u , ⟨ ∇ g u , ∇ g u ⟩ g ) = 0 , on ​ M F(x,u,\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}u\rangle_{g})=0,\qquad\text{on }M into ODEs and obtain local existence results of solutions which are constant along the level sets of the transnormal functions. In particular, we apply this reduction method to obtain new solutions to eikonal equations with a prescribed geometry. MSC: 34A05, 35B06, 35F20, 53C21, 58J70.

Introduction

Let
(M,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{g})
be a semi-Riemannian manifold without boundary of dimension
n\geq 3
. In this paper, we are interested in solutions to a fully nonlinear partial differential equation of the form

F(x,u(x),\left\langle\nabla_{g}u(x),\nabla_{g}u(x)\right\rangle_{g})=0,\qquad\text{on }M.

(1)

where
u\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is a differentiable function. Notice that, when
F
is independent of
u
, (
1
) becomes

F(x,\left\langle\nabla_{g}u(x),\nabla_{g}u(x)\right\rangle_{g})=0,\qquad\text{on }M.

which is a generalization of the eikonal equation

\langle\nabla_{g(x)}u(x),\nabla_{g(x)}u(x)\rangle_{g}=U(x),\quad U\in C^{\infty}(M).

(2)

In this paper, we will prove the existence of local solutions to problem (
1
) with a prescribed geometry on the level sets in two different ways. To establish our main results, we define and recall some concepts.

For a smooth function
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we will say that
F\colon M\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is
f
-invariant in the first variable
if for any

x_{1},x_{2}\in M
such that
f(x_{1})=f(x_{2})
, we have that

F(x_{1},r,p)=F(x_{2},r,p),

for all
(r,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This is equivalent to saying that there is a unique smooth function
\widehat{F}\colon\mathrm{Im}\,f\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k+2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
(r,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

F(x,r,p)=\widehat{F}(f(x),r,p),\qquad x\in M.

We say that a neighborhood
\Omega\subset M
is
f
-invariant if
x\in\Omega
implies
y\in\Omega
for any
y\in M
such that
f(x)=f(y)
. In other words,
\Omega
is the union of level sets of
f
.

We make extensive use of transnormal functions. Given a semi-Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, we say that
f\colon M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
transnormal
function if there exists a smooth function
a\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\langle\nabla_{g}f,\nabla_{g}f\rangle_{g}=a\circ f.

Examples of transnormal functions include isoparametric functions (see, for example,
[
4
,
7
,
8
]
for the semi-Riemannian setting and the book
[
1
]
in the Riemannian setting). In the Riemannian setting, there are explicit examples of transnormal functions which are not isoparametric (cf.
[
9
]
), but almost nothing is known in the semi-Riemannian case.

Isoparametric functions have proven to be useful to find solutions for many PDEs on manifolds; for example, in nonlinear second order elliptic and ultrahyperbolic PDEs
[
3
,
4
]
, nonlinear higher order elliptic PDEs
[
2
,
5
,
6
]
and mean curvature flow equations
[
8
,
10
]
. The main idea in this context is to use isoparametric functions to reduce the PDEs into ODEs which are simpler to analyze, generalizing the idea of looking for radial solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or in a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. However, for many fully nonlinear first order PDEs, as equation (
1
), it suffices to perform the reduction method by using only transnormal functions.

In this paper we show two instances of this method, reducing problem (
1
) to an ODE or a two-dimensional PDE defined in a rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let us describe briefly the method, main results and applications for each instance. In first place, for the reduction into an ODE, the key result is the following one.

Proposition 1

Let
f\colon M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a transnormal function,
w\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
smooth and suppose
F\colon M\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is
f
-invariant in the first variable. Then
u=w\circ f
is a solution to the equation

F(x,u,\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}u\rangle_{g})=0,\qquad\text{on }M

if and only if
w
is a solution to

\widehat{F}\left(t,w(t),a(t)|w^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\right)=0,\quad\text{in }\mathrm{Im}\,f\subset\mathbb{R}.

(3)

The proof of this result is a straightforward computation. As a consequence, we obtain our first main result, a local existence result for equation (
1
):

Theorem 1.1

Let
(M,g)
be a semi-Riemannian manifold,
f\colon M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a transnormal function with

\langle\nabla_{g}f,\nabla_{g}f\rangle_{g}=a\circ f
and let
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
be a regular value of
f
. Define
M_{0}:=f^{-1}(t_{0}).
Then, for any
C^{1}
function
F\colon M\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is
f
-invariant in the first variable, and for any
(x_{0},r_{0},p_{0})\in M_{0}\times\mathbb{R}\times(\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\{0\})
satisfying

F(x_{0},r_{0},p_{0})=0,\quad\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}(x_{0},r_{0},p_{0})\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad a(t_{0})p_{0}>0,

(4)

equation (
1
) posseses a unique
f
-invariant solution defined in an
f
-invariant domain
\Omega\subset M
such that
M_{0}\subset\Omega
.

We prove this result in Section
2
, which is an easy consequence of the Theorem of Existence and Uniqueness of ODEs. In the same section, and in order to illustrate the method behind the proof, we give some applications in case of the eikonal equation (
2
) in semi-Riemannian and Riemannian manifolds. In particular, using isoparametric functions defined in the semi-Euclidean space and in the pseudo-sphere
[
4
,
7
]
, we construct, in Examples
1
and
2
, several solutions to this problem in both manifolds, with prescribed nontrivial geometry on the level sets. In Example
3
, we do the same reduction for cohomogeneity-one Riemannian manifolds
[
5
]
. Moreover, in Example
4
, we give a concrete and interesting example of a solution to equation (
2
) defined in the hyperbolic Riemannian space, which is constant along the level sets of a transnormal function having non-isoparametric level sets
[
9
]
. In particular, we have the following result for the Riemannian eikonal equation. We denote by
\mathrm{Isom}(M,g)
the group of isometries of
M
.

Corollary 1

Let
c>0
and consider the eikonal equation

\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}u\rangle_{g}=c,\qquad\text{on }M,

(5)

where
(M,g)
is Riemannian.

(i)

If
(M,g)
is a closed Riemannian manifold and
\Gamma
is a closed subgroup of
\mathrm{Isom}(M,g)
acting by cohomogeneity one on
M
, then there exists a
\Gamma
-invariant solution to (
5
), defined in
M\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is the union of the singular orbits of the action.

(ii)

If
(M,g)
is the sphere with its canonical metric
(\mathbb{S}^{n},g_{0})
, then, for any isoparametric hypersurface
M_{0}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
, there exists a solution to equation (
5
), constant on
M_{0}
and defined in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is a union of two submanifolds of
M
of codimension
\geq 2
.

(iii)

If
(M,g)
is the hyperbolic space with its canonical metric
(\mathbb{H}^{n},g_{0})
, there exists a non isoparametric hypersurface
M_{0}
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and a globally defined solution
u:\mathbb{H}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
u
is constant on
M_{0}
.

We prove this Corollary at the end of Section
2
.

In Section
3
we will explore the local existence of solutions to problem (
1
) with a different qualitative behavior. To describe it, let
(L,g_{L})
and
(N,g_{N})
be two connected and complete Riemannian manifolds, without boundary, of dimensions
s
and
k
, respectively, and consider the semi-Riemannian warped product

L\times_{\alpha}N:=(L\times N,g:=-g_{L}+\alpha^{2}g_{N}),

(6)

where
\alpha\colon L\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth and positive.

Let
f_{L}\colon L\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{N}\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
be transnormal functions and consider a
C^{2}
function
F\colon(L\times N)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
which is
(f_{L}\times f_{N})
-invariant in the first variable, that is,

F(x_{1},z_{1},r,\tau)=F(x_{2},z_{2},r,\tau),

whenever
f_{L}(x_{1})=f_{L}(x_{2})
and
f_{N}(z_{1})=f_{N}(z_{2})
. As before, this implies the existence of a smooth function
\widehat{F}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

F(x,z,r,\tau)=\widehat{F}(f_{L}(x),f_{N}(z),r,\tau).

(7)

In this setting, problem (
1
) can be written as:

F(x,y,u,\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}u\rangle_{g})=0\qquad\text{on }\ L\times_{\alpha}N.

(8)

In Section
3
we will show the local existence of functions
u\in C^{\infty}(L\times N)
satisfying (
8
) and such that, for fixed
x\in L
and
y\in N
, the functions
u(\cdot,y)
and
u(x,\cdot)
are constant on the level sets of
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
, respectively. The key step is to reduce the problem (
8
) into a
2
-dimensional PDE of the first order in a rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, easier to handle and visualize, for instance, by numerical methods.

We state the reduction method in the following proposition. In what follows, we define the intervals
I_{f_{L}}:=\mathrm{Im}\,f_{L}
and
I_{f_{N}}:=\mathrm{Im}\,f_{N}
, the parameters
t:=f_{L}(x)\in I_{f_{N}}
and
s:=f_{N}(y)\in I_{f_{N}}
, so that
\widehat{F}=\widehat{F}(t,s,r,\tau)
is defined in
I_{f_{L}}\times I_{f_{N}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the functions
a_{f_{L}}
and
a_{f_{N}}
are such that
|\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}|^{2}_{g}=a_{f_{L}}\circ f
and
|\nabla_{g_{N}}f_{N}|^{2}_{g}=a_{f_{N}}\circ f
.

Proposition 2

Let
F
be
(f_{L}\times f_{N})
-invariant in the first variable as in (
7
), and let
w\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth. If
\alpha=\widehat{\alpha}\circ f_{L}
with
\widehat{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
smooth, then
u=w(f_{L},f_{N})
is a solution of (
8
) if and only if
w
satisfies

\widehat{F}\left(t,s,w(t,s),-a_{f_{L}}(t)w_{t}^{2}(t,s)+\frac{a_{f_{N}}(s)}{\widehat{\alpha}^{2}(t)}w_{s}^{2}(t,s)\right)=0,\quad\text{in }I_{f_{L}}\times I_{f_{N}},

(9)

where
w_{t}:=\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}
and
w_{s}:=\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}
.

We prove this proposition in Section
3.1
. As before, this proposition also implies a local existence result of
(f_{L}\times f_{N})
-invariant solutions. In order to state it, define the singular Riemannian foliations in
L
and
N
, induced by the transnormal functions
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
,

\mathcal{F}_{f_{L}}:=\{f_{L}^{-1}(t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}_{f_{N}}:=\{f_{N}^{-1}(s):s\in\mathbb{R}\},

respectively. Since
(L,g_{L})
and
(N,g_{N})
are Riemannian manifolds, the leaves of both foliations are also smooth manifolds (see
[
12
]
). Notice also that
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
induce functions
C_{f_{L}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{f_{L}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
C_{f_{N}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{f_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
given as
C_{f_{L}}(f_{L}^{-1}(t))=f_{L}(x)
for any
x\in f_{L}^{-1}(t)
and
C_{f_{N}}(f_{N}^{-1}(s))=f_{N}(z)
for any
z\in f^{-1}(s)
. We say that a function
\Sigma_{f_{L}}\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{f_{L}}
is a
smooth uniparametric family of leaves in
L
if
C_{f_{L}}\circ\Sigma_{f_{L}}\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is smooth. In the same way, we define a smooth uniparametric family of leaves in
N
.
As a consequence, we have the following local existence result.

Theorem 1.2

Let
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
be transnormal in
L
and
N
, and let
t_{0}
and
s_{0}
be regular values of
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
, respectively. Define
L_{0}:=f_{L}^{-1}(t_{0})
and
N_{0}:=f_{N}^{-1}(s_{0}).
Let
F\colon(L\times N)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
,
(f_{L}\times f_{N})
-invariant function on the first variable. Suppose that
(x_{0},z_{0},r_{0},p_{0},q_{0})\in L_{0}\times N_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
and

\tau_{0}:=-a_{f_{L}}(t_{0})p_{0}^{2}+\frac{a_{f_{N}}(s_{0})}{\widehat{\alpha}^{2}(t_{0})}q_{0}^{2}

are such that
(p_{0},q_{0})\neq(0,0)
,

F(x_{0},z_{0},r_{0},\tau_{0})=0,

(10)

and suppose further that

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\tau}(x,z,r,\tau)\neq 0

(11)

in a neighborhood of
(t_{0},s_{0},r_{0},\tau_{0})
. Then, for any given smooth function
R\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and any pair of smooth one parameter families
\Sigma_{f_{L}},\Sigma_{f_{N}}
defined in
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
such that

(
\Sigma.1
)

\Sigma_{f_{L}}(0)=L_{0}\
and
\ \Sigma_{f_{N}}(0)=N_{0}
,

(
\Sigma.2
)

q_{0}\;\frac{d}{d\zeta}\big|_{\zeta=0}\left(\Sigma_{f_{L}}\circ C_{f_{L}}\right)\neq\ p_{0}\;\frac{d}{d\zeta}\big|_{\zeta=0}\left(\Sigma_{f_{N}}\circ C_{f_{N}}\right)
, and

(
\Sigma.3
)

R^{\prime}(0)=p_{0}\;\frac{d}{d\zeta}\big|_{\zeta=0}\left(\Sigma_{f_{L}}\circ C_{f_{L}}\right)+q_{0}\;\frac{d}{d\zeta}\big|_{\zeta=0}\left(\Sigma_{f_{N}}\circ C_{f_{N}}\right)
,

the problem (
8
) has an
(f_{L}\times f_{N})
-invariant solution
u
defined in an
(f_{L}\times f_{N})
-invariant open neighborhood of
L_{0}\times N_{0}
, satisfying the condition

u(\Sigma_{f_{L}}(\zeta),\Sigma_{f_{N}}(\zeta))=R(\zeta),\quad\zeta\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon).

We prove this Theorem in Section
3.2
. In the same section, we give a concrete example in the case of the de Sitter space in order to sketch the idea behind the method.

Our paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we prove Theorem
1.1
, give concrete examples where the one dimensional reduction method holds true and prove Corollary
1
. In Section
3.1
, we compute several identities involving norms of gradients of functions defined in warped products of the form (
6
) and prove Proposition
2
. Finally, in Section
3.2
, we prove Theorem
1.2
and give a concrete example of the two-dimensional reduction method. In this same section, in order to simplify equation (
9
), we briefly introduce the theory of distance functions induced by transnormal functions on Riemannian manifolds.

2
The one dimensional reduction method

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
and apply it in several examples.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
(x_{0},r_{0},p_{0})\in M_{0}\times\mathbb{R}\times\left(\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\{0\}\right)
be a point satisfying the hypothesis (
4
). Without loss of generality, suppose that
p_{0}>0
, so that
a(t_{0})>0
. By Proposition
1
, solving (
1
) is equivalent to solving (
3
). The hypotheses imply that

\widehat{F}(t_{0},r_{0},p_{0})=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial\widehat{F}}{\partial p}(t_{0},r_{0},p_{0})\neq 0.

By the Implicit Function Theorem there exists an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a
C^{1}
function
H\colon U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
H(t_{0},r_{0})=p_{0}
,
\widehat{F}(t,r,H(t,r))=0
and
H(t,r)>0
for any
(t,r)\in U
.
Consider now the Cauchy problem

w^{\prime}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{H(t,w(t))}{a(t)}},\qquad w(t_{0})=r_{0}.

As
H
and
a
have the same sign near
t_{0}
, and as
a(t_{0})\neq 0
, by the Theorem of Existence and Uniqueness of solutions to initial value ODEs, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a unique
C^{1}
solution
w\colon(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}+\varepsilon)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to this problem. Hence
a(t)w^{\prime}(t)^{2}=H(t,w(t))
and
w
solves the problem (
3
) in the same interval. By Proposition
1
, the function
u:=w\circ f
is
f
-invariant and satisfies equation (
1
) in
\Omega:=f^{-1}(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}+\varepsilon)
. By construction,
\Omega
is an
f
-invariant open set and
M_{0}\subset\Omega
.
\square

In what follows, we develop some examples to sketch the reduction method and see how the local existence holds true.

Example 1

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{s}
be the semi-Euclidean space of signature
s<n
. For any
A\in\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{s})
,
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{s}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(A-\alpha I)A=0
, it is shown in
(
7
, Proposition 2.3)
that the function

f\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}_{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad f(x)=\langle Ax,x\rangle+2\langle a,x\rangle

is transnormal, in fact isoparametric, and satisfies that

\langle\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle=4\alpha f+\beta,

where
\beta=4\langle a,a\rangle
.
Consider the eikonal equation (
2
) and suppose
U
is
f
-invariant. Let
\widehat{U}\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function satisfying
U=\widehat{U}\circ f
. Hence, (
2
) reduces to

(4\alpha t+\beta)|w^{\prime}(t)|^{2}=\widehat{U}(t),\quad t\in\mathrm{Im}\,f,

where
\mathrm{Im}\,f
depends on the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
; see, for instance
(
4
, Appendix A)
. If
\alpha\neq 0
, this equation has a unique singularity at
t_{\ast}:=-\frac{\beta}{4\alpha}
. Note that, for any
t\neq t^{*}
,
4\alpha t+\beta
and
\widehat{U}(t)
must have the same sign. Hence, for any
t\neq t_{\ast}
, we can write

w^{\prime}(t)=\pm\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{U}(t)}{4\alpha t+\beta}}.

For instance, if
t_{0}<t_{\ast}
, we have an explicit expression for
w
given as follows:

w(t)=\pm\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{U}(s)}{4\alpha s+\beta}}\ ds,\qquad t\in(-\infty,t_{\ast}).

Hence, we can explicitly define the function
u\colon f^{-1}(-\infty,t_{\ast})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

u(x)=w\circ f(x)
, which is
f
-invariant by definition. The open set
\Omega:=f^{-1}(-\infty,t_{\ast})
is just an
f
-invariant neighborhood of the regular level set
M_{0}:=f^{-1}(t_{0})
.
\square

Example 2

Consider the pseudo-sphere
(\mathbb{S}^{n}_{s},g_{0})
of dimension
n\geq 3
and signature
0\leq s<n
, and let
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}_{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an isoparametric function defined by a Cartan-Münzner polynomial in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{s}
, see
(
4
, Section 2.2)
. Thus,
f
is transnormal and satisfies

\langle\nabla_{g_{0}}f,\nabla_{g_{0}}f\rangle_{g_{0}}=a\circ f,\quad a(t)=\ell^{2}(1-t^{2}),

where
\ell\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
are the number of distinct principal curvatures of the hypersurfaces induced by
f
. In this case,
\mathrm{Im}\,f
is
[-1,1]
in the Riemannian case (
s=0
) and could be either
\mathbb{R}
,
(-\infty,-1],(-\infty,1],[-1,\infty)
or
[1,\infty)
in the semi-Riemannian case (
s>0
), see
(
4
, Appendix B)
. Let
U\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}_{s}^{n})
be
f
-invariant, so that
U=\widehat{U}\circ f
and suppose further that
\widehat{U}
is positive in
(-1,1)
and negative in
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus[-1,1]
. Then, by Proposition
1
, a function
u=w\circ f
is a solution to the eikonal equation (
2
) in
\mathbb{S}_{s}^{n}
if and only if
w
satisfies the equation

\ell^{2}(1-t^{2})|w^{\prime}(t)|^{2}=\widehat{U}(t),\qquad t\in\mathrm{Im}\,f.

(12)

Observe that this equation has one or two singularities at
t=\pm 1
, corresponding to the focal manifolds
M_{\pm}:=f^{-1}(\pm 1)
, where, possibly, one is empty, depending on
\mathrm{Im}\,f
. We next solve separately the cases
t\in(-1,1)
and
t\in\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus[-1,1]
.

For
t\in(-1,1)
, consider the change of variables
t=\cos s
and
v(s):=w(t)
,
s\in(0,\pi)
; then
w
solves equation (
12
) if and only if
v
solves

|v^{\prime}(s)|^{2}=\frac{1}{\ell}\widehat{U}(\cos s),\qquad s\in(0,\pi).

As
\widehat{U}>0
in
[-1,1]
, we can solve explicitly for any
s_{0},s\in(0,\pi)
and obtain

v(s)=\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\sqrt{\widehat{U}(\cos(\zeta))}d\zeta.

Hence,
u=v\circ\arccos f
solves (
2
) in
\Omega:=f^{-1}(-1,1)
and
M_{0}:=f^{-1}(t_{0})\subset\Omega
, where
t_{0}=\arccos s_{0}
.

Next, observe that
w(t)
is a solution to (
12
) for
t\in(1,\infty)
if and only if
\widetilde{w}(t)=w(-t)
is a solution to the same equation for
t\in(-\infty,1)
. So, it suffices to solve equation (
12
) for
t>1
. To do this, consider the change of variables
t=\cosh s
and let
v(s):=w(t)
,
s\in(0,\infty)
. Then
w
solves (
12
) in
(1,\infty)
if and only if
v
solves

|v^{\prime}(s)|^{2}=-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\widehat{U}(\cosh s),\qquad s\in(0,\infty).

As
\widehat{U}<0
in
(1,\infty)

3
The two dimensional reduction method

3.1
Some computations on warped products

In this section, we prove Proposition
2
, beginning with some elementary computations for the warped product manifold
(L^{s}\times_{\alpha}N^{k},g=-g_{L}+\alpha^{2}g_{N}).
First, notice that in local coordinates, the metric
g
and its inverse have the following expressions

g_{ij}=\begin{cases}-(g_{L})_{ij},&1\leq i,j\leq s,\\
\alpha^{2}(g_{N})_{ij},&s+1\leq i,j\leq s+k,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(14)

and

g^{ij}=\begin{cases}-g_{L}^{ij},&1\leq i,j\leq s,\\
\alpha^{-2}g_{N}^{ij},&s+1\leq i,j\leq s+k,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(15)

respectively. Now, recall that the gradient of a function
u
is given in coordinates
y=(x,z)
as

\nabla_{g}u=\sum_{j}\left(\sum_{k}g^{jk}\partial_{y_{k}}u\right)\partial_{y_{j}}.

We have the following computations for the gradient and its interior products.

Lemma 1

For any
u\in C^{\infty}(L\times N)
we have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{g}u

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{g_{L}}u+\alpha^{-2}\nabla_{g_{N}}u,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}u\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=-|\nabla_{g_{L}}u|^{2}_{g_{L}}+\alpha^{-2}|\nabla_{g_{N}}u|^{2}_{g_{N}}.

Proof.

For a point
y=(x,z)
in a coordinate chart, from (
14
) and (
15
), we have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{g}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}^{s+k}\left(\sum_{i}^{s+k}g^{ji}\partial_{y_{i}}u\right)\partial_{y_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s+k}g^{ji}\partial_{y_{i}}u\right)\partial_{x_{j}}+\sum_{j=s+1}^{s+k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s+k}g^{ji}\partial_{y_{i}}u\right)\partial_{z_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}-g_{L}^{ji}\partial_{x_{i}}u\right)\partial_{x_{j}}+\sum_{j=s+1}^{s+k}\left(\sum_{i=s+1}^{s+k}\alpha^{-2}g_{N}^{ji}\partial_{z_{i}}u\right)\partial_{z_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{g_{L}}u+\alpha^{-2}\nabla_{g_{N}}u.

Since
\nabla_{g_{L}}u\in\Gamma(TL)
and
\nabla_{g_{N}}u\in\Gamma(TN)
, if
\nabla_{g_{L}}u=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
and
\nabla_{g_{N}}u=\sum_{i=s+1}^{s+k}b_{i}\partial_{z_{i}}
, for some smooth coefficients
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
, then

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{g}u,

\displaystyle\nabla_{g}u\rangle_{g}=\langle-\nabla_{g_{L}}u+\alpha^{-2}\nabla_{g_{N}}u,-\nabla_{g_{L}}u+\alpha^{-2}\nabla_{g_{N}}u\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{g_{L}}u,\nabla_{g_{L}}u\rangle_{g}+2\langle-\nabla_{g_{L}}u,\alpha^{-2}\nabla_{g_{N}}u\rangle+\langle\alpha^{-2}\nabla_{g_{N}}u,\alpha^{-2}\nabla_{g_{N}}u\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\right\rangle_{g}+\alpha^{-4}\left\langle\sum_{i=s+1}^{s+k}b_{i}\partial_{z_{i}},\sum_{i=s+1}^{s+k}b_{i}\partial_{z_{i}}\right\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}a_{i}a_{j}\langle{\partial}_{x_{i}},{\partial}_{x_{j}}\rangle_{g}+\alpha^{-4}\sum_{i,j=s+1}^{s+k}b_{i}b_{j}\langle{\partial}_{z_{i}},{\partial}_{z_{j}}\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=-|\nabla_{g_{L}}u|^{2}+\alpha^{-2}|\nabla_{g_{N}}u|^{2}.

For later use, we compute the norms of the gradients of
(f_{L}\times f_{N})
-invariant functions, not necessarily transnormal. In what follows,
t:=f_{L}(x),s:=f_{N}(z).

Lemma 2

Let
w\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and let
f_{L}\colon L\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
f_{N}\colon N\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth. If
u:=w(f_{L},f_{N})
, then

|\nabla_{g_{L}}u|^{2}_{g_{L}}=w_{t}^{2}(f_{L},f_{N})|\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}|^{2}_{g_{L}},\quad\text{and}\quad|\nabla_{g_{N}}u|^{2}_{g_{N}}=w_{s}^{2}(f_{L},f_{N})|\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}|^{2}_{g_{L}}.

Proof.

In local coordinates
y=(x,z)
, the differential of
u=w(f_{L},f_{N})
is given by

\displaystyle d(u)

\displaystyle=d(w\circ(f_{L},f_{N}))=dw(f_{L},f_{N})\circ d(f_{L}\times f_{N})

\displaystyle=dw\circ(df_{L}\times df_{N})

\displaystyle=\big(w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N}),w_{s}(f_{L},f_{N})\big)\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}&0\\
0&\nabla_{g_{N}}f_{N}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\big(w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N})\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L},w_{s}(f_{L},f_{N})\nabla_{g_{N}}f_{N}\big).

Hence

\partial_{x_{i}}u=w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N})\partial_{x_{i}}f_{L},\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{z_{i}}u=w_{s}(f_{L},f_{N})\partial_{z_{i}}f_{N}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\nabla_{g_{L}}(w(f_{L},f_{N}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}g_{L}^{ij}\partial_{x_{i}}(w(f_{L},f_{N}))\right)\partial_{x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}g_{L}^{ij}w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N})\partial_{x_{i}}f_{L}\right)\partial_{x_{j}}

\displaystyle=w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N})\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}g_{L}^{ij}\partial_{x_{i}}f_{L}\right)\partial_{x_{j}}

\displaystyle=w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N})\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L},

where we conclude that

\displaystyle|\nabla_{g_{L}}u|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N})\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L},w_{t}(f_{L},f_{N})\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}\rangle_{g_{L}}

\displaystyle=w_{t}^{2}(f_{L},f_{N})|\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}|^{2}_{g_{L}}.

Similar computations give

\nabla_{g_{N}}u=w_{s}(f_{L},f_{N})\nabla_{g_{N}}f_{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\nabla_{g_{L}}u|^{2}=w_{s}^{2}(f_{L},f_{N})|\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}|^{2}_{g_{L}},

as we wanted.

Now suppose that
f_{L}\colon L\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{N}\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
are transnormal functions satisfying

|\nabla_{g_{L}}f_{L}|_{g_{L}}^{2}=a_{L}\circ f_{L}\quad\text{and}\quad|\nabla_{g_{N}}f_{N}|_{g_{N}}^{2}=a_{N}\circ f_{N},

for some smooth functions
a_{L},a_{N}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. We have the following immediate consequence of the previous two lemmas.

Corollary 2

For
f_{L},f_{N}
transnormal,
u=(f_{L},f_{N})
and
\alpha=\widehat{\alpha}\circ f_{L}
,

\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}u\rangle_{g}=-w_{t}^{2}(f_{L},f_{N})a_{L}\circ f_{L}+\frac{a_{N}\circ f_{N}}{\widehat{\alpha}^{2}\circ f_{L}}w_{s}^{2}(f_{L},f_{N}).

Proof of Proposition
2
.
Using the identity in Corollary
2
, the proof is straightforward.
\square

3.2
Reduction using distance functions

In this subsection, we simplify
(
9
) via distance functions, and prove Theorem
1.2
. By the theory of transnormal functions for Riemannian manifolds (see
[
9
]
and
[
12
]
), the connected components of the critical sets of
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
are submanifolds of
L
and
N
respectively, called
focal submanifolds
. There are, at most, two focal submanifolds for a given transnormal function, and, in some cases, this set is empty. In what follows, we will suppose further that
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
admit, at least, one focal manifold, say
L_{0}
and
N_{0}
, and we will denote by
L_{1}
and
N_{1}
the second, possibly empty, focal submanifold. In this case, we can define the distance functions
d_{L}\colon L\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
d_{N}\colon N\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

d_{L}(x):=\mathrm{dist}_{g_{L}}(x,L_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad d_{N}(y):=\mathrm{dist}_{g_{N}}(y,N_{0}).

Let
\delta_{L},\delta_{N}\in(0,\infty]
be the supremum of these functions in
L
and
N
, respectively. The case of infinite distance corresponds to the non-compact case of
L
or
N
, while, in the case of compact manifolds,
\delta_{L}=\mathrm{dist}_{g_{L}}(L_{1},L_{0})
and
\delta_{N}=\mathrm{dist}_{g_{N}}(N_{1},N_{0})
. Moreover, for any
x\in L\smallsetminus(L_{0}\cup L_{1})
and any
z\in N\smallsetminus(N_{0}\cup N_{1})
, the distance functions satisfy

\langle\nabla_{g_{L}}d_{L}(x),\nabla_{g_{L}}d_{L}(x)\rangle_{g_{L}}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\nabla_{g_{N}}d_{N}(z),\nabla_{g_{N}}d_{N}(z)\rangle_{g_{N}}=1

and, so, they are transnormal in
L\smallsetminus(L_{0}\cup L_{1})
and in
N\smallsetminus(N_{0}\cup N_{1})
respectively, with
a_{d_{L}}=1
and
a_{d_{N}}=1
, and
I_{d_{L}}=[0,\delta_{L}]
and
I_{d_{N}}=[0,\delta_{N}]
.

Now, observe that
d_{L}
and
f_{L}
have the same level sets and, hence,
f_{L}
-invariant functions are
d_{L}
-invariant functions and vice-versa. The same is true for
d_{N}
and
f_{N}
. In fact,
d_{L}
and
d_{N}
are just reparametrizations of
f_{L}
and
f_{N}
. For this reason, and without loss of generality, we will consider directly that
F
is
(d_{L}\times d_{N})
-invariant in its first variable and that
\alpha
is
d_{L}
-invariant. Moreover, since
\mathcal{F}_{f_{L}}=\mathcal{F}_{d_{L}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{f_{N}}=\mathcal{F}_{d_{N}}
, the one parameter families of hypersurfaces satisfy that
\Sigma_{f_{L}}=\Sigma_{d_{L}}
and
\Sigma_{f_{N}}=\Sigma_{d_{N}}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.
By the previous remarks, it suffices to prove the result in the
d_{L}
and
d_{N}
-invariant setting, that is, suppose that
F
is
(d_{L}\times d_{N})
-invariant in the first variable, and let
\widehat{F}\colon I_{d_{L}}\times I_{d_{N}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
F=\widehat{F}(d_{L}\times d_{N},\cdot,\cdot)
in
L\times N\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the same way, as
\alpha
is
f_{L}
-invariant, it is
d_{L}
invariant and
\alpha=\widehat{\alpha}\circ d_{L}
in
L\smallsetminus(L_{0}\cup L_{1})
. Accordingly, we consider the one parameter families
\Sigma_{d_{L}}
and
\Sigma_{d_{N}}
such that
\Sigma_{d_{L}}(0)=L_{0}=d_{L}^{-1}(t_{0})
and
\Sigma_{d_{N}}(0)=N_{0}=d_{L}^{-1}(s_{0})
, for regular values
t_{0}\in(0,\delta_{L})
and
s_{0}\in(0,\delta_{N})
of
d_{L}
and
d_{N}
, respectively. To simplify notation, let
T:=C_{d_{L}}\circ\Sigma_{d_{L}}
and
S:=C_{d_{N}}\circ\Sigma_{d_{N}}
.

Since
d_{L}
and
d_{N}
are transnormal in
L\smallsetminus(L_{0}\cup L_{1})
and
N\smallsetminus(N_{0}\cup N_{1})
, respectively, by Proposition
2
, to solve equation (
1
) with
(d_{L}\times d_{N})
-invariance, it suffices to find
w\in C^{1}\left((0,\delta_{L})\times(0,\delta_{N})\right)
solving the reduced equation

\widehat{F}\left(t,s,w(t,s),-w_{t}^{2}(t,s)+\frac{1}{\widehat{\alpha}^{2}(t)}w_{s}^{2}(t,s)\right)=0,\quad\emph{\text{ in }}\ (0,\delta_{L})\times(0,\delta_{N}).

Observe this equation can be written as a first order fully nonlinear PDE of the form

H(t,s,w,w_{t},w_{s})=0\qquad\text{ in }(0,\delta_{L})\times(0,\delta_{N}),

where
H\colon(0,\delta_{L})\times(0,\delta_{N})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

H(t,s,r,p,q)=\widehat{F}\left(t,s,r,-p^{2}+\widehat{\alpha}^{-2}(t)q^{2}\right).

As

\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}(t,s,r,p,q)=2p\frac{\partial\widehat{F}}{\partial\tau}(t,s,r,-p^{2}+\widehat{\alpha}^{-2}q^{2})

and

\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}(t,s,r,p,q)=2q\frac{\partial\widehat{F}}{\partial\tau}(t,s,r,-p^{2}+\widehat{\alpha}^{-2}q^{2}),

then, for any
(x_{0},z_{0})\in L_{0}\times N_{0}
, by the hypotheses (
10
), (
11
),
(\Sigma.2)

and
(\Sigma.3)

, we have that

H(t_{0},s_{0},r_{0},p_{0},q_{0})=0,\qquad p_{0}T^{\prime}(0)+q_{0}S^{\prime}(0)=R^{\prime}(0),

that

T^{\prime}(0)\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}(t_{0},s_{0},r_{0},p_{0},q_{0})-S^{\prime}(0)\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}(t_{0},s_{0},r_{0},p_{0},q_{0})\neq 0,

and that

\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\right)^{2}(t,s,r,p,q)+\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\right)^{2}(t,s,r,p,q)\neq 0

in a small neighborhood of
(t_{0},s_{0},r_{0},p_{0},q_{0})
. Hence, by the standard theory of first-order fully nonlinear equations (see, for instance,
(
11
, Section 4.6)
), the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\widehat{F}\left(t,s,w(t,s),w_{t}^{2}(t,s)+\widehat{\alpha}^{2}w_{s}^{2}(t,s)\right)=0,\\
w(T(\zeta),S(\zeta))=R(\zeta),\end{cases}

has a unique solution

of class
C^{1}
defined in a small neighborhood of
(t_{0},s_{0})
, and again, using Proposition
2
, the function

is
(d_{L}\times d_{N})
-invariant and satisfies equation (
8
) in a small

-invariant neighborhood of

, as we wanted to prove.
\square

Example 5

Let
n\geq 3
and let
(\mathbb{S}^{n},g_{0})
be the sphere with the usual metric and consider the de Sitter space

(M,g)=(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n},dt^{2}+\cosh^{2}t\,g_{0}).

For
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n_{1}+n_{2}=n+1,\,n_{1},n_{2}\geq 2
, take

\mathbb{S}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}

and for
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
define the function

f(z)=\arccos(|z_{1}|^{2}-|z_{2}|^{2}).

This function is transnormal and satisfies that
\mathrm{Im}\,f=[0,\pi]
. The sets

f^{-1}(0)=\mathbb{S}^{n_{1}-1}\times\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad f^{-1}(\pi)=\{0\}\times\mathbb{S}^{n_{2}-1}

are the focal submanifolds, while

f^{-1}(s)=\mathbb{S}^{n_{1}-1}_{\cos s}\times\mathbb{S}^{n_{2}-1}_{\sin s}

are regular hypersurfaces for
s\in(0,\pi)
and

|\nabla_{g_{0}}f|^{2}_{g_{0}}=4,\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{S}^{n}\smallsetminus f^{-1}\{0,\pi\}.

For any
(t_{0},z_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\smallsetminus f^{-1}\{0,\pi\}\right),
and any smooth function
\widehat{U}\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
, we will show next that the eikonal equation on
M

\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}u\rangle=\widehat{U}(t,z),

(16)

has a solution which is
f
-invariant in the

-variable and is defined in a neighborhood of
\{t_{0}\}\times\mathbb{S}_{\cos s_{0}}^{n_{1}-1}\times\mathbb{S}_{\sin s_{0}}^{n_{2}-1}
for suitable one-parameter families
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}
, where
s_{0}=f(z_{0})
.

First, notice that
\mathrm{Id}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is transnormal, since
|\nabla\mathrm{Id}(t)|=1
for any

. So the function defining the eikonal equation (

) is just

F(t,z,r,\tau)=\tau-U(t,z).

This function is
(\mathrm{Id}\times f)
-invariant on the

-variables. Therefore, by Proposition
2
, any function of the form
u=w\circ(\mathrm{Id}\times f)
solving (

) must satisfy the equation

-w_{t}^{2}(t,s)+4\cosh^{-2}(t)w_{s}^{2}(t,s)=\widehat{U}(t,s)

(17)

in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\pi]

which can be written as

\widehat{F}\left(t,s,w(t,s),-w_{t}^{2}(t,s))+4\cosh^{-2}(t)w_{s}^{2}(t,s)\right)=0

where
\widehat{F}(t,s,w,\tau)=\tau-\widehat{U}(t,s)
.
We proceed to solve this equation with the theory of fully nonlinear equations; cf.
[
11
]
.

Fix
(t_{0},z_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n}\smallsetminus f^{-1}(\{0\})
and let

Take
(p_{0},q_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}
such that

-p_{0}^{2}+4\cosh^{-2}(t_{0})q_{0}^{2}-\widehat{U}(t_{0},s_{0})=0.

(18)

Notice that such
(p_{0},q_{0})\neq(0,0)
exists because (

) defines a hyperbola in the

-variables.

Then, the function
H\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,\pi]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle H(t,s,r,p,q)

\displaystyle=\widehat{F}\left(t,s,r,-p^{2}+4\cosh^{-2}(t)q^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-p^{2}+4\cosh^{-2}(t)q^{2}-\widehat{U}(t,s)

is used to rewrite (
17
) as

H(t,s,w(t,s),w_{t}(t,s),w_{s}(t,s))=0.

So, for any
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the point
(t_{0},z_{0},r_{0},p_{0},q_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\smallsetminus f^{-1}(\{0,\pi\})\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
is such that

where

and, therefore

Since
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\tau}\equiv 1\neq 0,

H_{p}(t,s,r,p,q)=-2p,\quad H_{q}(t,s,r,p,q)=8\cosh^{-2}(t)q,

and the nontriviality hypothesis

H_{p}^{2}+H_{q}^{2}=4p^{2}+64\cosh^{-2}(t)q^{2}

is satisfied for any
(p,q)\neq(0,0)
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
T,S\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be such that

(T(\zeta),S(\zeta))\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi),\quad\zeta\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),

with

T(0)=t_{0},\qquad S(0)=s_{0}\quad\text{ and }\quad T^{\prime}(0)q_{0},S^{\prime}(0)p_{0}>0.

Then, for any smooth function
R\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

R^{\prime}(0)=p_{0}T^{\prime}(0)+q_{0}S^{\prime}(0),

the initial value problem

\begin{cases}H(t,s,w(t,s),w_{t}(t.s),w_{s}(t,s))=0,\\
w(T(\zeta),S(\zeta))=R(\zeta),\end{cases}

admits a
C^{2}
solution in a neighborhood of
(t_{0},s_{0}).

Defining
\Sigma_{\mathrm{Id}}(\zeta):=\{T(\zeta)\}
and
\Sigma_{f}(\zeta):=f^{-1}(S(\zeta))\,
we have that

\Sigma_{\mathrm{Id}}(\zeta)\circ C_{\mathrm{Id}}=T,\quad\Sigma_{f}(\zeta)\circ C_{f}=S,

and
u=w\circ(\mathrm{Id}\times f)
is a
(\mathrm{Id}\times f)
-invariant solution defined in a neighborhood of
\{t_{0}\}\times\mathbb{S}_{\cos s_{0}}^{n_{1}-1}\times\mathbb{S}_{\sin s_{0}}^{n_{2}-1}

of the form

\Omega=(t_{0}-\varepsilon_{1},t_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})\times f^{-1}(s_{0}-\varepsilon_{2},s_{0}+\varepsilon_{2}),

for some
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
small enough. Moreover,
u
satisfies that

u(\Sigma_{Id}(\zeta),\Sigma_{f}(\zeta))=R(\zeta)\quad\text{ in }(-\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{3}),

for some
\varepsilon_{3}>0
small enough.
\square

.
