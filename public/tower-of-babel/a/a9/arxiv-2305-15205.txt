Parameter estimation in rough Bessel model
Yuliya Mishura yuliyamishura@knu.ua, Anton Yurchenko-Tytarenko antony@math.uio.no

Abstract
In this paper, we construct consistent statistical estimators of the Hurst index, volatility coefficient, and drift parameter for Bessel processes driven by fractional Brownian motion with H < 1 / 2 H<1/2 . As an auxiliary result, we also prove the continuity of the fractional Bessel process. The results are illustrated with simulations.



1
Introduction

The Bessel process, defined as the square root
X=\sqrt{Z}
of the solution
Z=\{Z(t),~t\geq 1\}
to the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\displaystyle dZ(t)=kdt+2\sqrt{Z(t)}dW(t),\quad Z(0)=x_{0}^{2}>0,

(1.1)

is a well-known probabilistic model used in a wide range of fields including physics
[
6
,
21
,
24
]
and finance
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
23
]
. If
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
X
can be interpreted as the Euclidean norm

X(t)=\sqrt{B^{2}_{1}(t)+\cdots+B^{2}_{k}(t)}

(1.2)

of a
k
-dimensional Brownian motion
(B_{1},...,B_{k})
that is connected with
W
in (
1.1
) via the relation

W(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{B_{i}(s)}{X(s)}dB_{i}(s),

and therefore the parameter
k
is often referred to as the
dimension
of the Bessel process
X
. Moreover, as it is shown in
[
8
, Section 3]
(see also
[
34
]
), if
k>1
, the Bessel process
X
is the unique nonnegative strong solution to the SDE

dX(t)=\frac{k-1}{X(t)}dt+dW(t),\quad X(0)=x_{0}>0.

(1.3)

For more details on various properties of Bessel processes, we refer the reader to
[
35
, Chapter XI]
, the book
[
9
]
which considers general equations of the type (
1.3
) or
[
3
,
4
,
30
]
which deal with the case
0<k<1
.

However, in many applications, standard Brownian motion may not adequately capture the desired level of complexity observed in real-life phenomena. For example, a number of empirical studies
[
5
,
7
,
10
,
15
,
16
]
point out the presence of memory in financial markets. Other sources
[
1
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
indicate that models exhibiting very low Hölder regularity are better suited to reflect the behavior of market volatility. Given that Bessel-type processes are frequently employed in stochastic volatility modeling (see e.g.
[
26
, Chapter 6]
), there is a natural inclination to enhance them by incorporating the aforementioned memory or roughness. A common way to achieve such an effect is to replace (in some sense) the standard Brownian driver
W
with a fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}=\{B^{H}(t),~t\geq 0\}
, i.e. a centered Gaussian process with covariance function

\mathbb{E}\left[B^{H}(t)B^{H}(s)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right),\quad s,t\geq 0.

For instance,
[
17
,
22
,
25
]
used the property (
1.2
) as the starting point of their modification and defined fractional Bessel process
\rho^{H}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
as

\rho^{H}(t)=\sqrt{(B^{H}_{1}(t))^{2}+\cdots+(B^{H}_{k}(t))^{2}}

with
B^{H}_{1}
,…,
B^{H}_{k}
being
k
independent fractional Brownian motions. In particular, they prove that
\rho^{H}
admits a representation

d\rho^{H}(t)=H(k-1)\frac{t^{2H-1}}{\rho^{H}(t)}dt+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{B^{H}_{i}(t)}{\rho^{H}(t)}dB^{H}_{i}(t),

(1.4)

where the integrals w.r.t. fractional Brownian motions are understood in the divergence sense.

Another possible “
fractionalization
” of the Bessel process may be achieved by replacing
W
with
B^{H}
directly in (
1.3
). This approach was discussed in detail in the series of papers
[
14
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
: according to it, the fractional Bessel process
X^{H}=\{X^{H}(t),~t\geq 0\}
is defined as the a.s. pointwise limit

X^{H}(t):=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}X_{\varepsilon}^{H}(t)

(1.5)

of stochastic processes given by the SDE

X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds+\sigma B^{H}(t).

In the present paper, we consider the latter notion of the fractional Bessel process and perform a statistical estimation of parameters
H
,
\sigma
and
a
when
H\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
.

•

In order to estimate
H
and
\sigma
, we use the standard technique based on quadratic variations of fractional Brownian motion. The main challenge arises in the limit
L^{H}(t):=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds
: when
H<\frac{1}{2}
,
[
33
, Corollary 3.3]
only guarantees its continuity in
t
almost everywhere w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure. It is not enough for the analysis of quadratic variations of
L^{H}
, so we prove that
L^{H}
is continuous at every
t\geq 0
and utilize this fact to obtain consistent estimators of
H
and
\sigma
.

•

Our estimator of
a
is, in turn, based on the unconventional technique tailored for our specific model. The problem here is that it is currently not known whether the fractional Bessel process exhibits any ergodic properties typically utilized for drift parameter estimation. In our analysis, we exploit the explicit dynamics of
X^{H}
instead to compare the behavior of
X^{H}(T)
and
\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(t)}dt
when
T\to\infty
.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the definition of the fractional Bessel process and prove the continuity of the latter. Section
3
is devoted to the estimation of
H
and
\sigma
. In Section
4
, we present our estimator of the drift and prove its consistency. Section
5
contains simulations. In Appendix
A
, we prove a technical result related to the finiteness of the limit (
1.5
).

2
Rough Bessel processes

Let
B^{H}=\{B^{H}(t),~t\geq 0\}
be a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
.

Remark 2.1
.

Using the Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem, it is possible to prove that
B^{H}
has a modification with paths that are locally Hölder continuous up to the order
H
, i.e. for any
T>0
and
\lambda\in(0,H)
there exists a positive random variable
\Lambda=\Lambda_{T,\lambda}
such that

|B^{H}(t_{1})-B^{H}(t_{2})|\leq\Lambda|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\lambda},\quad t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T].

(2.1)

Moreover, by
[
2
]
, the random variable
\Lambda
can be chosen in such a way that for any
p>0

\mathbb{E}[\Lambda^{p}]<\infty.

In what follows, we always consider this modification of
B^{H}
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,
x_{0}>0
,
a>0
,
b\geq 0
and
\sigma>0
, consider a random process
X^{H}_{\varepsilon}=\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t),~t\geq 0\}
given by a stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds+\sigma B^{H}(t).

(2.2)

Note that the SDE (
2.2
) has Lipschitz continuous drift and additive noise and hence, by the standard Picard iteration argument applied pathwisely, (
2.2
) has a unique solution for each
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover, by
[
33
, Lemma 2.1]
(see also
[
14
, Lemma 1.6]
), for any
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}
,

X^{H}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(t)\geq X^{H}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(t),\quad t\in[0,T],\quad\omega\in\Omega,

(2.3)

and hence, for any
t\geq 0
, one can define the limit

X^{H}(t):=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t).

(2.4)

Definition 2.2
.

The process
X^{H}
defined by (
2.4
) will be called a
fractional
or
rough Bessel process
.

Remark 2.3
.

This construction of rough Bessel processes was introduced and extensively studied in
[
33
]
. In particular, it was shown that

1)

the limit (
2.4
) is finite, i.e., with probability 1,

X^{H}(t)<\infty

(2.5)

for all
t\geq 0
;

2)

with probability 1,
X^{H}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and, moreover,
X^{H}(t)>0
for almost all
t\geq 0
.

It should be noted that one of the possible cases is missing in Step 2 of the proof of
[
33
, Theorem 2.1]
concerning (
2.5
). For the reader’s convenience, we provide the completed version of it in Appendix
A
.

Since the limit (
2.4
) is well-defined and finite, the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\left(X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)-x_{0}-\sigma B^{H}(t)\right)

(2.6)

\displaystyle=X^{H}(t)-x_{0}-\sigma B^{H}(t)

also exists and is finite. In what follows, we will use the notation

L^{H}(t):=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds,

(2.7)

i.e.
X^{H}
satisfies the equation

X^{H}(t)=x_{0}+aL^{H}(t)+\sigma B^{H}(t),\quad t\geq 0.

(2.8)

By Fatou’s lemma, for any
t\geq 0
,

\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds=L^{H}(t)<\infty.

Denote

R^{H}(t):=L^{H}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds

and re-write (
2.8
) as

X^{H}(t)=x_{0}+a\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds+\sigma B^{H}(t)+aR^{H}(t).

(2.9)

At the moment, it is not clear whether
R^{H}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
. However, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.4
.

1)

With probability 1, there exists
\tau>0
such that for all
t\geq\tau

X^{H}(t)>0.

2)

Let
\tau>0
be such that
X^{H}(\tau)>0
. Then there exists a neighbourhood
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\ni\tau
such that for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
,
R^{H}(t_{1})-R^{H}(t_{2})=0
, i.e.

L^{H}(t_{2})-L^{H}(t_{1})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds.

(2.10)

Proof.

Item 1) follows directly from
[
31
, Theorem 3.3]
so let us prove the claim 2). Let
\tau>0
be such that
X^{H}(\tau)>0
. Since
X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\uparrow X^{H}(\tau)
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)>0
. Moreover, the process
X^{H}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
is continuous w.r.t.
t
hence there exists a neighbourhood
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\ni\tau
and some positive value
x>0
such that for all
t\in(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

X^{H}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(t)>x.

In particular, (
2.3
) implies that for all
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}

X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)>x,\quad t\in(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

Therefore, for any
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
t\in(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)>0\}}+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{x}

and hence, by the dominated convergence theorem, for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
,
t_{1}<t_{2}
,

\displaystyle L^{H}(t_{2})-L^{H}(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds.

∎

Corollary 2.1
.

With probability 1, there exists
\tau>0
such that for all
t_{2}>t_{1}\geq\tau

R^{H}(t_{1})=R^{H}(t_{2})

and

L^{H}(t_{2})-L^{H}(t_{1})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds.

Note that
[
33
, Corollary 3.3]
establishes only continuity of
L^{H}

almost everywhere
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure. It is not enough for our purposes: in order to estimate
H
and
\sigma
, we want to utilize the behavior of quadratic variations of
X^{H}
and any possible discontinuities of
L^{H}
would create substantial obstacles for our analysis. It turns out, however, that
L^{H}
(and hence
X^{H}
) is continuous at
all
points
t\geq 0
. The corresponding theorem is provided below.

Theorem 2.5
.

1.

The process
L^{H}
defined by (
2.7
) is non-decreasing.

2.

The processes
X^{H}=\{X^{H}(t),~t\geq 0\}
and
L^{H}=\{L^{H}(t),~t\geq 0\}
have continuous paths.

Proof.

First of all, observe that for any
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T

\displaystyle L^{H}(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\frac{1}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds

\displaystyle=L^{H}(t_{2}),

i.e. the process
L^{H}
is indeed non-decreasing. Next, fix an arbitrary deterministic
T>0
, choose an arbitrary
\lambda\in(0,H)
and let
\Lambda_{T}
be such that for all
s,t\in[0,T]

|B^{H}(t)-B^{H}(s)|\leq\Lambda|t-s|^{\lambda}.

(2.11)

Note that the monotonicity of
L^{H}
implies that its discontinuities can only take the form of positive jumps and, moreover, left and right limits

L^{H}(t-):=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}L^{H}(t-\delta),\quad L^{H}(t+):=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}L^{H}(t+\delta)

are well-defined at any point
t\in(0,T)
. Next, observe that the limit process
X^{H}
defined by (
2.4
) satisfies the equation

X^{H}(t)=x_{0}+aL^{H}(t)-b\int_{0}^{t}X^{H}(s)ds+\sigma B^{H}(t),\quad t\in[0,T],

and hence points of discontinuity of
X^{H}
coincide with the ones of
L^{H}
, can occur only in the form of positive jumps of the same size as the corresponding jumps of
L^{H}
and, finally, one can define limits

X^{H}(t-):=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}X^{H}(t-\delta),\quad X^{H}(t+):=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}X^{H}(t+\delta).

Assume that
\tau\in[0,T]
is a point of discontinuity of
L^{H}
(and hence
X^{H}
) for some
\omega\in\Omega
and observe that
X^{H}(\tau)=0
since otherwise
\tau
cannot be a point of discontinuity of
X^{H}
and
L^{H}
by (
2.10
). Let
\alpha>0
be such that

X^{H}(\tau+)=L^{H}(\tau+)=\alpha

and
\delta>0
be such that for all
t\in(\tau,\tau+\delta)

X^{H}(t)>\frac{9\alpha}{10}.

Next, choose
\delta_{0}<\delta
such that

\frac{4a}{\alpha}\delta_{0}+\sigma\Lambda_{T}\delta_{0}^{\lambda}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4},

where
\lambda\in(0,H)
and
\Lambda_{T}>0
are from (
2.11
). Since
X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(\tau+\delta_{0})\uparrow X^{H}(\tau+\delta_{0})
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]

X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(\tau+\delta_{0})>\frac{4\alpha}{5},\quad X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\leq 0

Furthermore,
X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\uparrow X^{H}(\tau)=0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
hence
X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\leq 0
for all
\varepsilon>0
and therefore for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
one can define

\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-}:=\sup\left\{s\in(\tau,\tau+\delta_{0}):~X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)=\frac{\alpha}{4}\right\}

and

\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+}:=\sup\left\{s\in(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-},\tau+\delta_{0}):~X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)=\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right\}.

By continuity, it is evident that
X_{\varepsilon}^{H}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-})=\frac{\alpha}{4}
,
X_{\varepsilon}^{H}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+})=\frac{3\alpha}{4}
and
X_{\varepsilon}^{H}(t)\geq\frac{\alpha}{4}
for any
t\in[\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-},\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+}]
. Therefore for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}

\displaystyle=X_{\varepsilon}^{H}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+})-X_{\varepsilon}^{H}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-})

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-}}^{\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+}}\frac{a}{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(s)>0\}}+\varepsilon}ds-b\int_{\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-}}^{\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+}}X_{\varepsilon}^{H}(s)ds+\sigma\left(B^{H}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+})-B^{H}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4a}{\alpha}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\sigma\Lambda_{T}(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-})^{\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4a}{\alpha}\delta_{0}+\sigma\Lambda_{T}\delta_{0}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle<\frac{\alpha}{4},

which gives a contradiction. Therefore,
\alpha=0
and
X^{H}(\tau)=X^{H}(\tau+)
, i.e.
X^{H}
(and hence
L^{H}
) is right-continuous.

It remains to notice that
X^{H}(t-)=X^{H}(t)
for any
t\in(0,T]
. Indeed, as mentioned above,
X^{H}
is continuous at
t
if
X^{H}(t)>0
. If
X^{H}(t)=0
, since
X^{H}(t)\geq 0
can only have positive jumps,

X^{H}(t)-X^{H}(t-)=-X^{H}(t-)

and, since
X^{H}
can potentially have only positive jumps,
X^{H}(t-)=0
.
∎

Note that the pre-limit processes
X^{H}_{\varepsilon}
given by (
2.2
) are monotonically non-decreasing w.r.t.
\varepsilon
. Moreover, with probability 1, their pointwise limit is continuous function by Theorem
2.5
. Therefore, by Dini’s theorem, we immediately obtain uniform convergence which is summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

For any
T>0
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X^{H}(t)-X^{H}_{\varepsilon}(t)|\to 0\quad\text{a.s.},\quad\varepsilon\downarrow 0.

3
Estimation of Hurst index and volatility coefficient

Let us now move on to the parameter estimation for the rough Bessel processes. Our first goal is to estimate the Hurst index
H\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and the volatility parameter
\sigma>0
. Note that, by Theorem
2.5
, the process
L^{H}
is continuous and non-decreasing and hence has zero quadratic variation. Therefore, we can utilize the standard estimation technique based on power variations. For more details on this method, we refer the reader to
[
28
]
.

Throughout this section, we assume that the rough Bessel process
X^{H}=\{X^{H}(t),~t\geq 0\}
satisfies equation (
2.9
) with (unknown) parameters
a,\sigma>0
,
H<1/2
and is observed on a discrete uniform partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{n}=T
of a fixed compact
[0,T]
,
t_{k}:=\frac{kT}{n}
.

3.1
Estimation of
H

Let us start with some useful notation for quadratic variations.

Notation 3.1
.

For a stochastic process
\xi=\{\xi(t),~t\in[0,T]\}
observed on a discrete uniform partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{n}=T
,
t_{k}:=\frac{kT}{n}
, denote

V^{n}_{1,2}(\xi):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\xi(t_{k+1})-\xi(t_{k}))^{2}

(3.1)

and

V^{n}_{2,2}(\xi):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(\xi(t_{k+2})-2\xi(t_{k+1})+\xi(t_{k}))^{2}.

(3.2)

Note that the zero-mean Gaussian sequence
\{B^{H}(n)-B^{H}(n-1),n\geq 1\}
, is stationary and it is easy to check that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B^{H}(1)\left(B^{H}(n)-B^{H}(n-1)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(n^{2H}-2(n-1)^{2H}+(n-2)^{2H}\right)

\displaystyle=O(n^{2H-2})\to 0

as
n\to\infty
. Hence
\{B^{H}(n)-B^{H}(n-1),k\geq 1\}
is ergodic and, by the ergodic theorem, with probability 1,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(B^{H}(k+1)-B^{H}(k))^{2}\to\mathbb{E}[(B^{H}(1))^{2}]=1

and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(B^{H}(k+2)-2B^{H}(k+1)+B^{H}(k))^{2}\to\mathbb{E}[(B^{H}(2)-2B^{H}(1))^{2}]=4-2^{2H}

as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore, by the self-similarity property of
B^{H}
,

\frac{n^{-1+2H}}{T^{2H}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(B^{H}(t_{k+1})-B^{H}(t_{k})\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{law}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(B^{H}(k+1)-B^{H}(k))^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{n^{-1+2H}}{T^{2H}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(B^{H}(t_{k+2})-2B^{H}(t_{k+1})+B^{H}(t_{k}))^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{law}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(B^{H}(k+2)-2B^{H}(k+1)+B^{H}(k))^{2},

and we have the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
H\in(0,1)
and
\sigma>0
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\left(\frac{n}{T}\right)^{-1+2H}V^{n}_{1,2}(\sigma B^{H})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\sigma^{2}T

and

\left(\frac{n}{T}\right)^{-1+2H}V^{n}_{2,2}(\sigma B^{H})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(4-2^{2H})\sigma^{2}T.

Remark 3.3
.

A more general result related to pathwise integrals w.r.t.
B^{H}
can be found in
[
29
, Theorem 1]
. More detailed information on the convergence rates can be found in
[
28
, Chapter 2]
.

As established in Theorem
2.5
, the process
aL^{H}
in the right-hand side of (
2.9
) is a continuous process of bounded variation and hence, with probability 1,

V^{n}_{1,2}(aL^{H})\to 0,\quad V^{n}_{2,2}(aL^{H})\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Therefore, we immediately get the following corollary.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
X^{H}
be a rough Bessel process given by (
2.9
) observed on a discrete uniform partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{n}=T
,
t_{k}=\frac{kT}{n}
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\left(\frac{n}{T}\right)^{-1+2H}V^{n}_{1,2}(X^{H})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\sigma^{2}T

and

\left(\frac{n}{T}\right)^{-1+2H}V^{n}_{2,2}(X^{H})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(4-2^{2H})\sigma^{2}T.

Next, define

\widehat{H}:=\frac{\log\left(4-\frac{V^{n}_{2,2}(X^{H})}{V^{n}_{1,2}(X^{H})}\right)}{2\log 2}.

(3.3)

Theorem 3.4
.

The estimator
\widehat{H}
given by (
3.3
) is (weakly) consistent estimator of the Hurst index
H
, i.e.

\widehat{H}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}H,\quad n\to\infty.

Proof.

The statement follows immediately from Corollary
3.1
and the continuous mapping theorem.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

By Corollary
3.1
,

4-\frac{V^{n}_{2,2}(X^{H})}{V^{n}_{1,2}(X^{H})}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}2^{2H}>1,\quad n\to\infty,

and hence, in practice, the logarithm in (
3.3
) is well-defined for large enough values of
n
. Moreover,

\displaystyle V^{n}_{2,2}(X^{H})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(X^{H}(t_{k+2})-2X^{H}(t_{k+1})+X^{H}(t_{k}))^{2}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\left((X^{H}(t_{k+2})-X^{H}(t_{k+1}))-(X^{H}(t_{k+1})-X^{H}(t_{k}))\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(X^{H}(t_{k+2})-X^{H}(t_{k+1}))^{2}+2\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(X^{H}(t_{k+1})-X^{H}(t_{k}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4V^{n}_{1,2}(X^{H}),

i.e.

4-\frac{V^{n}_{2,2}(X^{H})}{V^{n}_{1,2}(X^{H})}\geq 0,

and the equality in (
3.4
) occurs only if
x_{0}=X^{H}(t_{0})=X^{H}(t_{1})=...=X^{H}(t_{n})
. It remains an open question whether such an event can occur with positive probability but, in practice, it is never the case.

3.2
Estimation of
\sigma

Next, assume that the Hurst index
H
is known and the goal is to estimate the volatility coefficient
\sigma
. Using Corollary
3.1
, it can be done in a straightforward manner. Namely, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let the Hurst index
H
be known. Then the estimator

\widehat{\sigma}=\frac{1}{T^{H}}\sqrt{n^{-1+2H}V^{n}_{1,2}(X^{H})}

(3.5)

is (weakly) consistent estimator of the volatility coefficient
\sigma
, i.e.

\widehat{\sigma}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\sigma,\quad n\to\infty.

Proof.

The statement follows immediately from Corollary
3.1
and the continuous mapping theorem.
∎

4
Drift parameter estimation

Let us move to the estimation of the drift parameter
a
in (
2.9
). In contrast to the high-frequency setting considered in Section
3
, we will now assume that we observe a continuous path of
X^{H}
on
[0,T]
with
T\to\infty
.
We study the following estimator of the parameter
a

\widehat{a}(T):=\frac{X^{H}(T)}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(t)}dt}.

(4.1)

Remark 4.1
.

Note that we do not assume any prior knowledge of the parameters
H
or
\sigma
.

Let us start by presenting a well-known result related to the growth of fractional Brownian motion (for more details, see
[
27
]
,
[
28
, Section B 3.5]
or
[
31
, Corollary 2.2]
).

Theorem 4.2
.

For any
\delta>0
there exist random time
\tau_{\delta}
and positive random variable
\xi
such that, with probability 1,

\max_{s\in[0,T]}|B^{H}(t)|\leq\xi T^{H+\delta}

(4.2)

for all
T>\tau_{\delta}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X^{H}
be a rough Bessel process satisfying (
2.9
). Then, with probability 1,

\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(t)}dt>0.

(4.3)

Proof.

Take an arbitrary
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}-H\right)
and fix an
\omega\in\Omega
such that the corresponding path of
B^{H}
is continuous, (
4.2
) holds and
R^{H}
becomes constant after some random time point
\tau
as described in Corollary
2.1
.

Assume that (
4.3
) does not hold, i.e. there exists an increasing sequence
\{T_{n},~n\geq 1\}
such that
T_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
T_{1}
exceeds
\tau_{\delta}
from Theorem
4.2
, and

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{X^{H}(t)}dt\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Integrating both sides of (
2.9
) from
0
to
T_{n}
, we obtain that for all
n\geq 1

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{n}}X^{H}(t)dt

\displaystyle=x_{0}T_{n}+a\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}dsdt

(4.4)

\displaystyle+\sigma\int_{0}^{T_{n}}B^{H}(t)dt+a\int_{0}^{T_{n}}R^{H}(t)dt.

Let us prove that the equality (
4.4
) cannot hold by comparing the asymptotics of its left- and right-hand sides.

On the one hand, using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, it is easy to see that for all
T>0

\int_{0}^{T}X^{H}(t)dt\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(t)}dt\geq T^{2},

so

\frac{1}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}X^{H}(s)ds\to\infty,\quad n\to\infty.

On the other hand,

\frac{x_{0}T_{n}}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}dsdt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{X^{H}(t)}(T_{n}-t)dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T_{n}}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\frac{1}{X^{H}(t)}dt

\displaystyle\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,

and, by (
4.2
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\left|\int_{0}^{T_{n}}B^{H}(t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T^{1+H+\delta}}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\frac{\xi}{1+H+\delta}\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Finally, since
R^{H}
is constant after some time point
\tau
,

\frac{1}{T^{3/2}_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}R^{H}(t)dt\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

In other words, if such sequence
\{T_{n},~n\geq 1\}
exists, the left-hand side of (
4.4
) divided by
T_{n}^{3/2}
converges to
\infty
whereas the right-hand side divided by
T_{n}^{3/2}
converges to zero. We get a contradiction that proves (
4.3
).
∎

We are now ready to move to the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.4
.

With probability 1,

\widehat{a}(T)\to a,\quad T\to\infty.

Proof.

First of all, note that, by Lemma
4.3
,

\frac{x_{0}}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

when
T\to\infty
. Next, since there exists
\tau>0
such that
R^{H}(t)=0
for all
t\geq\tau
, Lemma
4.3
also implies that

\frac{aR^{H}(t)}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

when
T\to\infty
. Finally, fixing
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}-H\right)
and using (
4.2
), we can deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{|B^{H}(T)|}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T^{\frac{1}{2}-H-\delta}}\frac{\xi}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

as
T\to\infty
. Therefore, with probability 1,

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{1}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{0}+a\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds+\sigma B^{H}(t)+aR^{H}(t)}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}

\displaystyle=a+\frac{x_{0}}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}+\frac{\sigma B^{H}(t)}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}+\frac{aR^{H}(t)}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{X^{H}(s)}ds}

\displaystyle\to a,\quad T\to\infty.

∎
