On the Interplay between Self-Driving Cars and Public Transportation
Nicolas Lanzetti, Maximilian Schiffer, Michael Ostrovsky, Marco Pavone

Abstract
Cities worldwide struggle with overloaded transportation systems and their externalities. The emerging autonomous transportation technology has the potential to alleviate these issues, but the decisions of profit-maximizing operators running large autonomous fleets could negatively impact other stakeholders and the transportation system. An analysis of these tradeoffs requires modeling the modes of transportation in a unified framework. In this paper, we propose such a framework, which allows us to study the interplay among mobility service providers (MSPs), public transport authorities, and customers. Our framework combines a graph-theoretic network model for the transportation system with a game-theoretic market model in which MSPs are profit maximizers while customers select individually-optimal transportation options. We apply our framework to data for the city of Berlin and present sensitivity analyses to study parameters that MSPs or municipalities can strategically influence. We show that autonomous ride-hailing systems may cannibalize a public transportation system, serving between 7 % and 80 % of all customers, depending on market conditions and policy restrictions.



I
Introduction

Worldwide, cities struggle with overstrained transportation systems whose externalities cause economic and environmental harm such as working hours lost in congestion or health dangers caused by particulate matters, NO
x
, and stress
[
1
]
. In 2017, traffic-related externalities cost U.S. citizens 305 billion
\mathrm{USD}

[
2

Municipalities try to resolve these problems by improving existing transportation systems but face several obstacles
[
3

4

. Public transport not based on surface roads (e.g., subway lines) is often hindered by spatial limitations in urban areas and by long lead times of infrastructure projects. Improving road infrastructure often faces similar obstacles. Accordingly, better utilization of urban infrastructure using new technologies and mobility concepts is necessary to resolve the root cause of problems in today’s transportation systems.

In recent years, various new mobility concepts emerged. However, all of them struggle with specific obstacles. Car-sharing systems such as Zipcar offer the opportunity to reduce the number of individually owned cars in cities. However, given the small fleet sizes, customers are reluctant to use such vehicles due to inconvenient accessibility; vice versa, these concepts are often still not economically viable for
mobility service providers (MSPs)
. Ride-hailing services such as Uber or Lyft appear as an affordable alternative to traditional taxi services and decrease the need for individually owned cars in cities. However, the uncontrolled growth of such services can worsen congestion; e.g., in Manhattan, an increase of
68,000\text{\,}
for-hire vehicles from 2013 to 2018 correlated with a decrease in average travel speed from
6.2\text{\,}\mathrm{mph}
to
4.7\text{\,}\mathrm{mph}

[
4
]
. Ride-pooling services may reduce traffic in cities by pairing passengers with similar trips into a vehicle
[
5
,
6
]
. However, such systems only work efficiently when the ride-hailing system itself is efficient. So far, customers appear to be reluctant to use ride-pooling due to unsatisfactory user experiences
[
7
]
.

An emerging mobility concept, namely autonomous mobility-on-demand (
AMoD
), has the potential to help address the challenges outlined above. An
AMoD
system consists of a fleet of robotic self-driving cars, which transport passengers from their origins to their destinations.
An operator controls the fleet by dispatching passenger trips to vehicles while simultaneously deciding on their routes. Such a system overcomes the limitations of free-floating car-sharing systems by remedying the limited accessibility of cars. Further, it overcomes ride-hailing systems’ inefficiencies as a single operator centrally decides on the fleet’s size and all operations. It also eliminates the largest cost component of ride-hailing systems: driver’s time. Central coordination and information transparency also allow for more efficient ride-pooling with fair compensation schemes among customers.

While
AMoD
has the potential to address many challenges of urban transportation, it also introduces a major risk: it may significantly impact and possibly cannibalize public transportation such as buses, trams, and subways
[
8
]
. While such cannibalization effects have already been discussed for similar modes of travel, e.g., ride-hailing services
[
9
,
10
]
, an
AMoD
system bears a unique feature: for the first time, the centralized, fleet-wide control and coordination of vehicles allows one to employ a perfect pricing strategy; i.e., an operator can tailor her pricing strategy to her individual objective, e.g., profit maximization, without any disturbances usually resulting from driver behavior and vehicle availability. The net effect of such a system and its pricing policy on cities is mostly an empirical question, whose answer depends on many specifics of various cities. A principled approach to answer this question entails developing a unifying framework that incorporates various transportation modes to study customers’ travel behavior and choices. In this paper, we provide such a framework, which makes it possible to analyze the interactions among
MSPs
,
public transport authorities (PTAs)
controlled by municipalities, and customers in today’s and future transportation systems. In particular, we tailor this framework to the specific case of
AMoD
systems and base it on a game-theoretic perspective.

Mobility-on-demand related games

Further, researchers studied the joint design and operation of

Contribution

To fill this research gap, we provide the first game-theoretic framework that captures the dynamics among multiple
MSPs
and customers while accounting for the system’s operational constraints. Specifically, our scientific contribution is fourfold.
First, we develop a generic mathematical framework that allows us to analyze the dynamics of complex transportation problems by combining graph-theoretic network models with game-theoretic approaches that consider the interconnections between a transportation network and its corresponding market place.
Second, we tailor this framework to the specific cases of two
AMoD
operators interacting between themselves and with public transport as well as customers. Moreover, we consider the degenerate case of a monopolistic
AMoD
operator and study the equilibria of the arising games.
Third, we provide a real-world case study for the city of Berlin.
Fourth, we present extensive numerical results and sensitivity analyses, yielding managerial findings on the interaction between
AMoD
operators and public transport. Among others, we show that the system equilibria yield similar modal shares for both the degenerate monopolistic and the competitive cases, while we observe significant differences in the operators’ profit.

II
Problem Setting

In this work, we focus on intra-city passenger transportation, where an
AMoD
fleet substitutes the service of current taxi or ride-hailing fleets. In such a system, different
MSPs
interact with each other and with customers. We distinguish
MSPs
between commercial
MSPs
and municipalities and focus on three stakeholder groups.

Mobility service providers
offer transportation services to customers in order to maximize their profit.
MSPs
require cost-effective operations, ignoring the resulting externalities and their effect on the overall system or on other players. Accordingly, an
MSP
aims to influence customer decisions towards her own benefit via dedicated pricing strategies. The effect of such pricing strategies reaches a maximum for an
AMoD
fleet, where no intermediaries (e.g., drivers) may perturb the operator’s strategy, and the operator remains in full control of the fleet behavior.

Municipalities
offer transportation services via a
PTA
to customers while aiming to sustain infrastructure services, accessibility, and quality of life. While
MSPs
complement the transportation services offered by municipalities, they also cause externalities and dissatisfaction. Accordingly, municipalities try to influence
MSPs
, e.g., through subsidies or taxes.

Customers
represent the demand side and request for transportation services. Customers can choose between different transportation modes or combine them to complete their ride. Each customer selects a trip in line with her preferences, e.g., minimizing her cost, travel time, or a combination of both.

To adequately capture such a system’s dynamics and the interactions between stakeholders, we model the city’s transportation system on two different levels: a transportation network and its market place (see
Figure

1
).

Transportation market place:
The interaction between the different stakeholders occurs in the system’s
market place
, e.g., via a smartphone app. Here,
MSPs
and municipalities offer several transportation services to customers. Customers have different transportation demands and respond to these offers depending on their individual rationale. These interactions happen at the operational level in a short time horizon. At the strategic level,
MSPs
interact and therefore influence each other as their business models may interfere, i.e., a customer may substitute the service of one provider with that of another provider, depending on quality and price. In this setting, an
AMoD
operator has a specific advantage over other operators. Compared to a
PTA
, the
AMoD
operator can leverage short-term pricing policies to influence customer behavior. While a regular ride-hailing operator may utilize short-term pricing policies as well, an
AMoD
operator is in full control of the fleet and does not face disturbances from intermediaries such as drivers. This makes a short-term pricing policy more effective and usable as a surgical tool to steer the system. Assessing the impact of this advantage is a central motivation for this work.

Transportation network:
The realization of a demand and supply match between customers and mobility service providers occurs in the transportation network, consisting of the city’s road and public transportation networks. Accordingly, the transportation network imposes boundaries on offers in the transportation market place as it determines the available infrastructure and comprises externalities such as congestion and travel times.

In this paper, we focus specifically on the interaction between
AMoD
operators and a municipality, offering transportation service through a
PTA
. Here, we focus on a short-term perspective, i.e., the equilibrium for a snapshot of a day. Given this time horizon, the
PTA
does not change her prices at such an operational level because public transport tariffs are set strategically for a significantly longer time horizon. Conversely,
AMoD
operators change their prices at the operational level to maximize their profit. When taking the pricing decision, an
AMoD
operator considers potential profits from serving customers and additional costs resulting from relocating vehicles after finished trips. To this end, rebalancing empty vehicles can be interpreted as a reorganization of vehicle positions to match anticipated demand.

Methodology

We now develop the methodology to analyze the problem setting introduced in

III-A

Graph Representation of a Multi-stakeholder Transportation System

We represent a transportation network on a multigraph
\dagger

\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},s^{\mathrm{o}},s^{\mathrm{d}})
with a vertex set
\mathcal{V}
, an arc set
\mathcal{A}
, and identifiers
s^{\mathrm{o}}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{V}
and
s^{\mathrm{d}}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{V}
assigning each arc to its source and sink.
Each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
denotes a location where a customer can start or end her trip. Each arc
a\in\mathcal{A}
represents a certain transportation mode for a trip, e.g., a self-driving car or a subway line. Accordingly, multiple arcs may exist between any two vertices
v_{1},v_{2}
to model the available modes of transportation.
We define arc subsets
\mathcal{A}_{j}\subset\mathcal{A}
, inducing subgraphs
\mathcal{G}_{j}=(\mathcal{V}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j},s_{j}^{\mathrm{o}},s_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})
with
\mathcal{V}_{j}\coloneqq\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}\{s^{\mathrm{o}}(a),s^{\mathrm{d}}(a)\}
,
s_{j}^{\mathrm{o}}\coloneqq s^{\mathrm{o}}|_{\mathcal{A}_{j}}
, and
s_{j}^{\mathrm{d}}\coloneqq s^{\mathrm{d}}|_{\mathcal{A}_{j}}
(i.e., the restrictions of
s^{\mathrm{o}}
and
s^{\mathrm{d}}
to
\mathcal{A}_{j}
). Each subgraph denotes a homogeneous mode of transportation, e.g., the subway network or the service region of an
AMoD
system.
Additionally, each subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{j}
is controlled by an
MSP
(e.g., through price setting), from here on referred to as its operator.
The subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{0}=(\mathcal{V}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0},s_{0}^{\mathrm{o}},s_{0}^{\mathrm{d}})
denotes a part of the overall network where customers can move free of (monetary) charge, e.g., by walking.
Accordingly, neither an operator nor prices exist for the subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

To reflect customers’ choice options in a real transportation system, we assume
\mathcal{G}_{0}
to be non-trivial (i.e.,
\mathcal{V}_{0}\neq\emptyset
) and strongly connected
\dagger
. Further, the subgraphs’ arc sets are disjoint: formally
\mathcal{A}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}_{l}=\emptyset,\ \forall j\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\},l\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{j\}
, where
N
denotes the number of operators in the system.
While these properties allow customers to choose between all available transportation modes, they do not prevent various operators from providing service on the same road, as multigraphs allow multiple arcs for an origin-destination pair.

We use a time-invariant network flow representation for customer movements and differentiate between transportation requests and transportation demands. A request refers to a single customer
k
and is well-defined by a pair
(o_{k},d_{k})\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
, which states the origin
o_{k}
and the destination
d_{k}
of her trip. Conversely, a demand
\dagger

q_{i}
aggregates identical requests from different customers via origin, destination, and demand rate; it therefore refers to a customer flow.

Definition 1
(Demand)

A demand
q_{i}
is a triple
(o_{i},d_{i},\alpha_{i})\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
uniquely defined by its origin
o_{i}
, its destination
d_{i}
, and a demand rate
\alpha_{i}
, which results from all requests coinciding in
o_{i}
and
d_{i}
.
For an arbitrary set of
M
demands with label set
\{1,\ldots,M\}
, we define
\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{M}\}
as the set of all demands.

We stipulate that each demand starts and ends on the subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
; else, if a trip starts or ends on the subgraph of
MSP

j
, then
MSP

j
can pick arbitrarily large prices and the customer is forced with using the transportation service.
Formally, for all
q_{i}=(o_{i},d_{i},\alpha_{i})\in\mathcal{Q}
we have
o_{i},d_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
.
In general, a given demand may be satisfied by multiple paths
\dagger
. With
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
being the set of all paths in
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},s^{\mathrm{o}},s^{\mathrm{d}})
, we can define the demand-satisfying paths.

Definition 2
(Demand-satisfying paths)

A set of
L
paths
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{L}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
satisfies a demand
q_{i}=(o_{i},d_{i},\alpha_{i})
if the origins
\dagger

\pi_{\mathrm{o}}{}(p_{j})
and destinations
\dagger

\pi_{\mathrm{d}}{}(p_{j})
of the paths and of the demand coincide, i.e., if
\pi_{\mathrm{o}}{}(p_{j})=o_{i}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{d}}{}(p_{j})=d_{i}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{S}(q_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
the set of all paths satisfying demand
q_{i}
.

Then, according to the customers’ choices, the demand induces flows
\dagger
, defined by a path and a flow rate. We denote the path and the rate corresponding to a flow
f
by
\pi_{\mathrm{p}}(f)
and
\pi_{\mathrm{r}}(f)
.

III-B
1
Customers’ Reactions and Operators’ Decisions

Formally, we focus on a simultaneous game between
N
operators.
Herein, customers act as non-strategic reactive players.

Customer reaction

Customers may travel free of charge by using arcs in the subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
(i.e., they walk) or may request an
MSP
’s service. If they request such a service, they cannot influence the
MSP
’s operations. Instead, they only ask for a mobility service between an origin and a destination. Formally, customers select arcs in the
fully-connected
\dagger
operators’ subgraph denoted by
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{j}=(\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{j},\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{j},\bar{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{o}},\bar{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})
.
More specifically, customers select paths in the graphs’ arc sets
\mathcal{A}_{0}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
. As customers may choose different services, each demand
q_{i}
splits over its demand-satisfying paths
\mathcal{S}(q_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{0}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{j})
.
To model this customer behavior, we use a reaction curve
\phi_{i}:\mathcal{S}(q_{i})\to[0,\alpha_{i}]
for each demand
q_{i}=(o_{i},d_{i},\alpha_{i})
which assigns a share of the total demand to each demand-satisfying path.
We introduce the customers’ action space
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}
to capture (
i
) demand conservation, i.e.,
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}(q_{i})}\phi_{i}(p)=\alpha_{i}
,
and (
ii
) additional constraints customers might face.
For instance, if demand
q_{i}
is not allowed to travel through the path
\bar{p}\in\mathcal{S}(q_{i})
, then the corresponding action space reads
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}=\{\phi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{S}(q_{i})}\,|\,\phi(\bar{p})=0,\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}(q_{i})}\phi_{i}(p)=\alpha_{i}\}
.
Finally, each reaction curve is associated with a non-negative cost
J_{i}:\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}\times\Xi_{1}\times\ldots\times\Xi_{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, with
\Xi_{j}
denoting the set of pricing strategies available to operator
{j}
as elaborated below.
For instance, this cost could represent the sum of the fares paid throughout the trip or the total cost, consisting of the fares paid and a customer’s monetary value of time.

Operator decision

Each operator
{j}
serves customer demands, herein operating her fleet to maximize profit.
Her profit depends on the customer fares and the operational costs, resulting from customer-transporting vehicles and operating empty vehicles, which the operator relocates in the system to balance her flows.
Recall that customers move on the
fully-connected
version of the operators’ subgraph and on the non-controlled graph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.
Accordingly, an arc on the operator’s fully-connected subgraph denotes the customers’ possibility to request a ride from a source vertex to a sink vertex. A ride is only realized if the arc lies on a demand-satisfying path
p\in\mathcal{S}(q_{i})
with
\phi_{i}(p)>0
for some demand
q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}
.
To satisfy these requests on her subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{j}
, operator
{j}
selects vehicle flows: for each requested ride, there must be a vehicle flow (
i
) whose origin and destination match the ones of the ride and (
ii
) whose rate equals the rate of customers who requested the ride. Further, we require that each vehicle flow does not simultaneously serve two rides. We call such a set of flows potentially active.

Definition 3
(Potentially active set of flows)

Consider a demand
q_{i}
with reaction curve
\phi_{i}
. A set of flows
\mathcal{F}_{i}
on operator
{j}
’s subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{j}
is potentially active for demand
q_{i}
if for each demand-satisfying path
p\in\mathcal{S}(q_{i})
with
\phi_{i}(p)>0
, and each arc
a\in p\cap\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
there are flows
f_{i}^{a,p}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
which satisfy the mobility service induced by demand
q_{i}
such that
(i) its origin and destination coincide with
\bar{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{o}}(a)
and
\bar{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{d}}(a)
and (ii) its rate matches the demand share on path
p
, i.e.,
\pi_{\mathrm{r}}(f_{i}^{a,p})=\phi_{i}(p)
.
To prevent a flow from serving multiple rides, we require that the flows
f_{i}^{a,p}
do not coincide; else, a flow
f=f_{i}^{a_{1},p_{1}}=f_{i}^{a_{2},p_{2}}
would simultaneously serve ride
a_{1}
on path
p_{1}
and ride
a_{2}
on path
p_{2}
. We denote the set of potentially active flow sets by
\mathcal{H}_{i}(\phi_{i})
.

The operator may additionally select a set of flows of rebalancing vehicles
\mathcal{F}_{0}
.
To ensure that flows are balanced and that additional constraints (e.g., limited vehicles availability) are fulfilled, we introduce the operator’s action set
\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{o}}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{G}_{j}})}\times\ldots\times 2^{\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{G}_{j}})}
and impose
(\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{M},\mathcal{F}_{0})\in\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{o}}
.
In this setting, the revenue of an operator reads

(1)

depending on the prices set by the operator, where we refer to a representation of all prices set by the operator as a pricing strategy
\xi
.

Definition 4
(Pricing strategy)

Consider a multigraph
\mathcal{G}_{j}=(\mathcal{V}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j},s_{j}^{\mathrm{o}},s_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})
.
A pricing strategy
\xi:\mathcal{V}_{j}\times\mathcal{V}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{G}_{j}
is a function assigning a non-negative price
\xi(o,d)
to each origin-destination pair
(o,d)\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\times\mathcal{V}_{j}
.

As multiple pricing strategies can exist, we collect the feasible pricing strategies on a graph
\mathcal{G}_{j}=(\mathcal{V}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j},s_{j}^{\mathrm{o}},s_{j}^{\mathrm{d}})
in the set
\Xi_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{V}_{j}\times\mathcal{V}_{j}}
. In line with realistic constraints on pricing strategies, we assume
\Xi_{j}
to be closed, convex, and of the form

\Xi_{j}=\{\left.\xi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{V}_{j}\times\mathcal{V}_{j}}\,\right|\,g_{j}(\xi(o,d),o,d)\leq 0\;\forall o,d\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\}

for some
g_{j}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathcal{V}_{j}\times\mathcal{V}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
, with
l\in\mathbb{N}
, expressing the constraints on the price
\xi(o,d)
; e.g., if prices are regulated by an upper bound
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, then
g_{j}(\xi_{j}(o,d),o,d)=\xi_{j}(o,d)-B
.

To model the operator’s costs (e.g., energy consumption, maintenance, and depreciation of the vehicles), we introduce a cost
c_{j}(a)
for traversing arc
a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}
, resulting in the cost
\pi_{\mathrm{r}}(f)\sum_{a\in\pi_{\mathrm{p}}(f)}c_{j}(a)
for a flow
f
. Thus, the total cost is

\!\!\!\!\!\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}(\phi_{i}),\mathcal{F}_{0}\in 2^{\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{G}_{j}})},\\
(\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M},\mathcal{F}_{0})\in\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{o}}\end{subarray}}\,\sum_{i\in\{0,1,\ldots,M\},f\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\pi_{\mathrm{r}}(f)\sum_{a\in\pi_{\mathrm{p}}(f)}c_{j}(a).

(2)

Finally, the operator’s profit
U_{j}:\Xi_{j}\times\prod_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}\to\mathbb{R}
, depending on the operator’s pricing strategy and on the customers’ reaction curves, results from the excess of revenue (Eq. (
1
)) over costs (Eq. (
2
)).

A few comments on this general setting are in order.
First, we do not include direct interactions among customers, but our model can be extended to accommodate them. In particular, one can define customers as strategic players, interacting simultaneously with themselves and sequentially with
MSPs
. In line with this, we leave effects such as ride pooling to future research.
Second, we assume without loss of generality that an operator serves all customer requests.
However, an operator can technically drop a customer by imposing an artificially high transportation fare, which causes the customer to refuse to choose a ride.
Third, we neglect the operator’s fixed costs because they do not affect the operational decisions.
Fourth, we consider a time-invariant transportation system. This assumption reflects the mesoscopic nature of our study, i.e., we consider a representative snapshot of the transportation system to account for a realistic behavior of
MSPs
and customers within the studied game.
Fifth, we assume that a customer always decides to travel. One may easily relax this assumption by adding an auxiliary subgraph representing customers who refrain from traveling.

III-B
2
Game Equilibrium

As a basis for the definition of game equilibria, we first introduce a customer demand’s optimal reaction: A demand reacts optimally if its reaction curve minimizes its cost for given operators’ pricing strategies.

Definition 5
(Optimal reaction)

Given the operators’ pricing strategies
(\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\in\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Xi_{j}
, the reaction curve
{\phi}^{\star}_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}
is optimal for demand
q_{i}
if
J_{i}({\phi}^{\star}_{i},\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\leq J_{i}(\phi_{i},\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})

for all
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}
.
Let
\mathcal{E}_{i}(\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})
be the set of all optimal reactions.

Since operators interact simultaneously, we say that the game is at equilibrium if none of the operators can increase her profit by unilaterally changing her pricing strategy, given that customers react optimally.
To keep the definition of such an equilibrium concise, we assume without loss of generality that for all operators’ pricing strategies
(\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\in\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Xi_{j}
and all demands
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, the set
\mathcal{E}_{i}(\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})
is a singleton which ensures profit uniqueness for a pricing strategy. A relaxation of this assumption is however straightforward; e.g., by introducing a selection function for the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}(\{\xi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})
.

Denoting the operator’s profit as
U_{j}(\xi_{j},\{\mathcal{E}_{i}(\xi_{j},\xi_{-j})\}_{i=1}^{M})
, where
\xi_{-j}\in\prod_{k=1,k\neq j}^{N}\Xi_{k}
is the tuple of the pricing strategies of all operators but operator
j
, we can define equilibria.

Definition 6
(Game equilibrium)

The pricing strategies
(\{{\xi}^{\star}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\in\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Xi_{j}
are an equilibrium of the game if no operator can increase her profit by unilaterally deviating from her pricing strategy:
(\{{\xi}^{\star}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})
is an equilibrium if for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and all
\xi_{j}\in\Xi_{j}

U_{j}({\xi}^{\star}_{j},\{\mathcal{E}_{i}({\xi}^{\star}_{j},{\xi}^{\star}_{-j})\}_{i=1}^{M})\geq U_{j}(\xi_{j},\{\mathcal{E}_{i}(\xi_{j},,{\xi}^{\star}_{-j})\}_{i=1}^{M})
.

IV
The Interplay between AMoD Systems and Public Transport

We now tailor our general framework to the case where two
AMoD
operators interact with themselves and a public transport system. Further, we show how this case simplifies when a single
AMoD
operator monopolizes the market and competes only with the public transport system.
We believe these two cases are realistic for many real-world settings, either because only one firm can operate the autonomous taxis or because (e.g., due to natural economies of scale) there are only two competing autonomous ride-hailing systems.
Our game-theoretic model offers two use cases. First,
AMoD
operators can solve the underlying game at set intervals (e.g., every hour) to determine profit-maximizing prices on short-term demand predictions. Second, the game can guide strategic decisions and policymaking in mobility systems, as we illustrate in our case studies in
Sections

V
and
VI
; e.g., municipalities can use our game-theoretic model to assess the impact of various transport pricing policies on user behavior, modal split, and costs.

General Setting

Formally, we focus on a game with three operators, all providing service to customers but with different pricing strategies. The municipality (Operator 3) operates a public transport system through the
PTA
. Here, prices are fixed for a medium-term time horizon. Hence, we treat the municipality’s pricing strategy as fixed, i.e.,
\Xi_{3}=\{\xi_{3}\}
.
With this assumption, the game consists of a simultaneous game between two
AMoD
operators (Operators 1 and 2), offering mobility services on the road network. The operators take customer requests and the
PTA

AMoD
operators act as strategic players maximizing their profit.

The problem of
AMoD
operator

The operator selects a short-term pricing strategy

to maximize profit. All

operators provide mobility service on the road, so

Operators face a transportation system in steady state and operate a fleet of

\displaystyle\sum_{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{1,\ldots,M\},f\in\mathcal{F}_{i}\end{subarray}}}\quad\pi_{\mathrm{r}}(f)\cdot t_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}}+\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{0}}\pi_{\mathrm{r}}(f)\cdot t_{j}(\pi_{\mathrm{p}}(f))\leq N_{j}^{\mathrm{veh}}

Since travel time in urban settings mainly depends on traffic conditions and not on a single fleet’s vehicles, we assume that

Problem Analysis

In this section, we prove existence of an equilibrium and give a tractability result. We start by providing an expression for the reaction curves.

Lemma 1
(Rational reaction)

Rational reaction curves are characterized by the pointwise minimum of affine functions:

\phi_{i}(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}})=\max\{\min\{h_{i,j}(\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})),\alpha_{i}\},0\},

(3)

where

\displaystyle h_{i,j}(\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{\begin{aligned} &m_{i,1}\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})+q_{i,1},\\
&m_{i,2}\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})+q_{i,2}(\xi_{-j}(o_{i},d_{i})),\\
&m_{i,3}\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})+q_{i,3}(\xi_{-j}(o_{i},d_{i}))\},\end{aligned}

and
m_{i,1},m_{i,2},m_{i,3}<0
.
Define
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{min}}
by
h_{i,j}(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{min}})=\alpha_{i}
and
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}}
by
h_{i,j}(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}})=0
, both well-defined and uniformly bounded.
Then,
h_{i,j}
defines a homeomorphism between
\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})\in[p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{min}},p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}}]
and
\phi(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}})\in[0,\alpha_{i}]
. Moreover,
q_{i,2}
and
q_{i,3}
change linearly with
\xi_{-j}(o_{i},d_{i})
, such that
h_{i,j}
changes continuously with the price of the adversary
AMoD
.

Lemma

1
gives three major insights.
First, the existence of a homeomorphism between prices and reaction curves allows us to use reaction curves as optimization variables rather than the pricing strategy itself.
Second, it shows that reaction curves are concave and therefore amenable to convex reformulations.
Third, it tells us that reaction curves are continuous in the pricing strategy of the adversary
AMoD
operator, which will be one of the key ingredients of the existence result at the end of this section.
As a result, we obtain a tractable reformulation for the computation of best responses.

Proposition 1
(Best response)

The best response of each
AMoD
operator results from a tractable convex second-order conic program. Specifically, we obtain best response prices for origin-destination pairs subject to demand from
h_{i,j}^{-1}(x_{i})
, whereby
x_{i}
results from

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in[0,\alpha_{i}],\\
f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}_{j}|}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}r_{i}-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}c_{j}(a)\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}f_{i,j}^{\ast}x_{i}+f_{0}\right]_{a}

(4a)

subject to

\displaystyle 0\leq r_{i}\leq x_{i}\cdot(x_{i}-q_{i,k})/m_{i,k}\>\forall k\in\{1,2,3\}

(4b)

\displaystyle B^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}f^{\ast}_{i,j}x_{i}+f_{0}\right)=0

(4c)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}t_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}}x_{i}+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}t_{j}(a)[f_{0}]_{a}\leq N_{j}^{\mathrm{veh}},

(4d)

where
f^{\ast}_{i,j}
corresponds to the shortest path from the demand’s origin to the destination,
[f]_{a}
denotes the entry corresponding to arc
a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}
, and
B
is the incidence matrix of the
AMoD
operator’s graph.

In words, (
4a
) is the profit, consisting of the revenue, captured by the auxiliary variable
r_{i}
, and the costs for serving the customers and for rebalancing the fleet. Constraint (
4b
) ensures consistency between the revenue and
x_{i}
, via the demand curve (
3
). Constraint (
4c
) ensures that flows are balanced; i.e., the number of vehicles entering and exiting a node coincide. Finally, constraint (
4d
) takes care of the fleet size.
The result of
Lemma

1
is crucial to obtain a tractable best response: straightforwardly using prices as optimization variables would lead to a non-convex optimization problem. Specifically, the vehicle conservation constraint (
4c
) would in this case be non-convex, as
x_{i}
is not an affine function of the pricing strategy.
With
Lemmas

1
and
1
, we can establish existence of an equilibrium.

Theorem 1
(Existence of an equilibrium)

The game admits an equilibrium.

As it is typically the case in game theory, the equilibrium is not unique. In practice, one may iteratively run best response to compute one of the equilibria. It is well-known that best response might not converge, yet it converges to an equilibrium if it converges.

VII
Conclusions

With this work, we focused on the interplay between
AMoD
fleets and public transportation. To this end, we developed a general methodological framework to model interactions among
MSPs
and between
MSPs
and customers.
Our framework combines a graph-theoretic network flow model with a game-theoretic approach to capture both the interactions between
MSPs
and customers on the transportation market place and the constraints that result from the transportation network. We specified this framework for our application case, focusing on the interactions among two
AMoD
fleet operators, a municipality, and customers. We developed a computationally tractable second-order conic program to compute best responses, which can be used to find an equilibrium of the resulting game.
We applied our methodology to a real-world case study for the city of Berlin, and we presented results for various settings to identify major impact factors.

Appendix A
Graph Theory

Definition 7
(Flow)

A flow
f
is a pair
f=(p,\beta)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, denoting the customer rate
\beta
that uses a path
p
. We introduce
\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{G}})
as the set of all flows on
\mathcal{G}
and the projection operators
\pi_{\mathrm{p}}(f)=p
and
\pi_{\mathrm{r}}(f)=\beta
mapping a flow to its path
p
and its flow rate
\beta
, respectively.

Definition 8
(Multigraph)

A multigraph
\mathcal{G}
is a quadruple
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},s^{\mathrm{o}},s^{\mathrm{d}})
such that
\mathcal{V}
is the set of vertices,
\mathcal{A}
is the set of arcs,
s^{\mathrm{o}}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{V}
assigns to each arc its source vertex, and
s^{\mathrm{d}}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{V}
assigns to each arc its sink vertex.

Definition 9
(Path)

We refer to a path of length
L\in\mathbb{N}
as a set of distinct arcs
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{L-1}\}
for which there exists a set of exactly
L+1
distinct vertices
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{L}\}
such that
s^{\mathrm{o}}(a_{i})=v_{i}
and
s^{\mathrm{d}}(a_{i})=v_{i+1}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,L-1\}
. Note that by definition such a path cannot contain cycles.
Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
be the set of all paths.

Definition 10
(Origin and destination)

Given a path
p=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{L-1}\}
on
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},s^{\mathrm{o}},s^{\mathrm{d}})
we define the path origin and destination functions as
\pi_{\mathrm{o}}:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{V}
,
\pi_{\mathrm{d}}:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 11
(Fully-connected)

A multigraph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},s^{\mathrm{o}},s^{\mathrm{d}})
is fully-connected if for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
,
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
, there is
a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
s^{\mathrm{o}}(a)=v_{1}
,
s^{\mathrm{d}}(a)=v_{2}
.

Definition 12
(Fully-conn. graph)

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},s^{\mathrm{o}},s^{\mathrm{d}})
with
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{|\mathcal{V}|}\}
. The fully-connected version of
\mathcal{G}
is
\bar{\mathcal{G}}=(\bar{\mathcal{V}},\bar{\mathcal{A}},\bar{s}^{\mathrm{o}},\bar{s}^{\mathrm{d}})
, where
\bar{\mathcal{V}}=\mathcal{V}
,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}=\{a_{1,2},a_{1,3},\ldots,a_{|\mathcal{V}|,|\mathcal{V}|-1}\}
,
\bar{s}^{\mathrm{o}}(a_{i,j})=v_{i}
, and
\bar{s}^{\mathrm{d}}(a_{i,j})=v_{j}
.

Definition 13
(Shortest path)

Consider a multigraph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},s^{\mathrm{o}},s^{\mathrm{d}})
and a non-negative function
f:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. A path
p
is a shortest path between
v_{1}\in\mathcal{V}
and
v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
if (i)
\pi_{\mathrm{o}}(p)=v_{1}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{d}}(p)=v_{2}
and (ii) it minimizes
\sum_{a\in p}f(a)
. We denote the set of shortest paths by
\mathcal{P}^{*}(v_{1},v_{2})
.

Proof of
Proposition

1
:

We prove the proposition in four steps.
First, we observe that prices above
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}}
do not lead to larger profits. Indeed, such a price will result in
\phi_{i}(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}})=0
, and therefore in the same revenue as the price
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}}
.
Similarly, prices below
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{min}}
are never optimal. Indeed, they lead to the same reaction curve as
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{min}}
, namely
\phi_{i}(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}})=\alpha_{i}
, but result in a smaller profit due to a smaller revenue but equal costs. Hence, we can without loss of generality assume
\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})\in[p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{min}},p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}}]
.
Second, by the first step and
Lemma

1
, operator
j
can optimize her profit by taking
x_{i}\coloneqq\phi_{i}(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}})
instead of the pricing strategies
\xi_{j}
as optimization variables. Indeed, there exists a bijection between
\phi_{i}(p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{AMoD}})
and
\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})
. Accordingly, the revenue of serving demand
q_{i}
becomes
x_{i}h_{i,j}^{-1}(x_{i})
. Using an epigraph formulation, we can equivalently express it through the variable
r_{i}
. Imposing
r_{i}\geq 0
ensures that only non-negative prices are chosen.
Third, we notice that the optimal profit is attained when the set of flows that serves the
i
th
demand coincides with the shortest path, computed by weighing each arc
a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}
with
c_{j}(a)
. Hence,
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{f_{i,j}^{\ast}\}
. Indeed, any other set of flows would incur larger costs, and thus smaller profit.
Fourth, combining all observations leads to the second-order conic program (
4

x_{i}^{\ast}

x_{i}^{\ast}>0
then
\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})=h_{i,j}^{-1}(x_{i}^{\ast})
. If
x_{i}^{\ast}=0
, then
\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})\in[p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}},+\infty)

and
d_{i}
, then
\xi_{j}(o_{i},d_{i})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

For
Theorem

1
we need the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 2

Identify
\Xi_{j}
with a subset of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{V}_{j}|^{2}}
and assume it is non-empty, compact, and convex.
Define the best response map
B_{j}:\Xi_{-j}\to 2^{\Xi_{j}}
of operator
j
as
B_{j}(\xi_{-j})\coloneqq\arg\max_{\xi_{j}}U_{j}(\xi_{j},\{\mathcal{E}_{i}(\xi_{j},\xi_{-j})\}_{i=1}^{M}).

Assume that for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
the best response map
B_{j}
has closed graph and that
B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
is non-empty and convex for each
\xi_{-j}\in\prod_{k=1,k\neq j}^{N}\Xi_{k}
.
Then, the game admits an equilibrium.

Proof of
Lemma

2
:

Define
\Xi\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Xi_{j}
and
B:\Xi\to 2^{\Xi}
by

B(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N})\coloneqq\left\{(\bar{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{N})\,|\,\bar{\xi}_{j}\in B_{j}(\xi_{-j})\>\forall\,j=1,\ldots,N\right\}.

Since
\Xi
is the Cartesian product of non-empty, convex, and compact sets, it is non-empty, convex, and compact.
Similarly,
B
has closed graph and
B(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})
is non-empty and convex for all
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}\in\Xi
.
Then, an equilibrium is a fixed point of
B
. Its existence follow from Kakutani’s fixed point theorem and is a standard result in game theory.

Proof of
Theorem

1
:

It suffices to show that the assumptions of
Lemma

2
hold true. Then, existence of an equilibrium follows directly.
First, we identify the space of pricing strategies with
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{V}_{j}|^{2}}
. Then, convexity and non-emptiness of
\Xi_{j}
follow straightforwardly.
We now show that there is no loss of generality in assuming compactness of
\Xi_{j}
. Recall that by
Lemma

1

p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}}
is uniformly upper bounded by some
M>0
. Consider a modified version of the game whereby all prices are upper bounded by
M
. If this game possesses an equilibrium, then this equilibrium is an equilibrium in the original game as well, as showed in the first step of the proof of
Proposition

1
.
Hence,
\Xi_{j}
can be assumed to be compact.
Second, the proof of
Proposition

1
shows that
B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
is non-empty and convex for each
\xi_{-j}
. Indeed,
B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
consists of the cartesian product of sets of the form
\{p\}
for appropriate
p\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
[p_{ij}^{\mathrm{max}},M]
, or
[0,M]
. Note also that
B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
is closed, being the cartesian product of closed sets.
Third, we need to establish that
B_{j}
has closed graph. Since
B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
is closed, it suffices to prove that it is upper hemicontinuous; i.e., if
\xi_{-j,n}\to\xi_{-j}
and
\xi_{j,n}\to\xi_{j}
with
\xi_{j,n}\in B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
, then
\xi_{j}\in B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
.
The optimization problem is strongly convex in each
x_{i}
, so its optimal
x_{i}^{\ast}
is unique and continuous with respect to the parameters
q_{1,i}
and
q_{2,i}
. Moreover,
Lemma

1
implies that
q_{1,i}
and
q_{2,i}
are continuous in
\xi_{-j}(o_{i},d_{i})
, and in particular that
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{max}}
and
p_{i,j}^{\mathrm{min}}
are continuous in
\xi_{-j}(o_{i},d_{i})
. Thus,
B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
results from the cartesian product of closed sets, whose limits are continuous in
\xi_{-j}
. Hence, let
\xi_{-j,n}\to\xi_{-j}
and
\xi_{j,n}\to\xi_{j}
with
\xi_{j,n}\in B_{j}(\xi_{-j,n})
. Since all bounds of the involved sets are continuous in
\xi_{-j}
, we only need to focus on the case when
\xi_{j,n}\in B_{j}(\xi_{-j,n})
is of the form
[p,M]
for all
n
sufficiently large, but
\xi_{j}\in B_{j}(\xi_{-j})
is a singleton. Yet, we can rule out this case by observing that a
\xi_{-j,n}
that results in this case does not exist, due to continuity and closedness of all sets.
Then,
Lemma

2
gives existence of an equilibrium.

Proof of
Theorem

2
:

We can see the single operator case as the special case, simply by letting
\xi_{-j}(o,d)\to\infty
for all
o,d\in\mathcal{V}_{-j}
. If so,
q_{2,i},q_{3,i}\to\infty
, which gives
r_{i}=x_{i}\cdot(x_{i}-q_{1,i})/m_{1,i}
. This simplifies the computation of the best response (and thus of the equilibium) to a quadratic program.
Since
m_{1,i}<0
, the objective function is strictly concave in
x_{i}
, which establishes the uniqueness of the optimal solution
x_{i}^{\ast}
. Thus, we can conclude that all equilibria result in the same reaction curves and profits.
