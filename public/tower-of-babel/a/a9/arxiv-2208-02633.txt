Computing Candidate Prices in Budget-Constrained Product-Mix Auctions
Maximilian Fichtl

Abstract
We study the problem of computing optimal prices for a version of the Product-Mix auction with budget constraints. In contrast to the “standard” Product-Mix auction, the objective is to maximize revenue instead of social welfare. We prove correctness of an algorithm proposed by Paul Klemperer and DotEcon which is sufficiently efficient in smaller markets.



1
Introduction

In this paper we consider the arctic version of the Product-Mix auction which was designed by Paul Klemperer for the Icelandic’s government’s strategy for exiting capital controls
(
Klemperer 2010
)
. The name “arctic” is chosen in contrast to the maybe more familiar version of the Product-Mix auction with quasi-linear utilities that can be analyzed with methods from tropical geometry
(
Baldwin and Klemperer 2019
)
. In contrast to many other auction formats, it allows bidders to express budget constraints. Additionally, the supply of goods is not assumed to be fixed, but depends on the seller’s cost of providing a certain amount goods.

There are
n
different goods
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and a finite number of bidders, each submitting exactly one bid. A bid is a vector
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in\R^{n}_{\geq 0}
together with a budget
\beta(\mathbf{b})>0
. Here,
b_{i}
represents the value of bid
\mathbf{b}
for one unit of good
i
. We identify bidders by their unique bid
\mathbf{b}
and denote the set of all bids by
\mathcal{B}
. We assume that for each good
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there is at least one bid with
b_{i}>0
– otherwise we could just exclude good
i
from the auction. A bundle is a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\R^{n}_{\geq 0}
where
x_{i}
denotes the possibly fractional amount of good
i
contained in
\mathbf{x}
. An allocation is an assignment of bundles to bidders, where
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}
denotes the bundle assigned to bidder
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}
. The auctioneer selects a linear and anonymous price vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in\R^{n}_{>0}
and determines an allocation
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\}_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}
. Note that all coordinates of the price vector are strictly positive - a price of
0
for some good would cause an infinite amount of that good to be demanded. We say that an allocation is feasible, if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}x^{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\leq\beta(\mathbf{b})
for all bidders
\mathbf{b}
, i.e., the cost of the assigned bundle does not exceed the available budget. A price vector
\mathbf{p}
together with a feasible allocation
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\}_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}
generates the utility

\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}x^{\mathbf{b}}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}x^{\mathbf{b}}_{i}=\langle\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\rangle

for bidder
\mathbf{b}
. Given a price vector
\mathbf{p}
, the demand set of bidder
\mathbf{b}
is the set of all bundles
\mathbf{x}
maximizing their utility:

D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})=\argmax\left\{\langle\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x}\rangle\,:\,\mathbf{x}\in\R^{n}_{\geq 0}\text{ and }\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x}\rangle\leq\beta(\mathbf{b})\right\}.

We call an allocation
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\}_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}
envy-free, if at given prices
\mathbf{p}
bidders receive bundles in their respective demand sets, i.e.,
x^{\mathbf{b}}\in D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})\,\forall\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}
.

The auctioneer must provide enough goods such that the selected allocation
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\}_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}
can be realized. The cost of providing the required amount of goods is
\psi(\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}})
. We assume
\psi:\R^{n}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\R\cup\{+\infty\}
to be a lower semi-continuous convex function with
\psi(\mathbf{0})=0
which is monotonically increasing: if
\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{y}
coordinate-wise, then
\psi(\mathbf{x})\leq\psi(\mathbf{y})
. The auctioneer’s revenue for setting prices to
\mathbf{p}
, assigning bundles
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}
to the bidders and receiving their payment is then given by

\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\rangle-\psi\left(\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\right).

The budget-constrained Product-Mix auction tries to balance the interests of the seller and the bidders: the auctioneer’s revenue should be maximized among all envy-free allocations:

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\rangle-\psi\left(\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\right)

(1.1)

s.t.

\displaystyle\,\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\in D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})\,\forall\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\,\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}.

2
Contribution

Due to the non-linear constraints
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\in D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})
, we may not expect existence of an efficient polynomial-time algorithm that finds a global optimal solution to Problem (
1.1
). Paul Klemperer and DotEcon proposed an algorithm with good running time behavior when the number of different goods is relatively small (they assumed
n=3
in their practical application). We state and analyze this algorithm formally. Our main contribution is a proof of correctness for their algorithm.

3
The Algorithm

The basic idea of the algorithm is as follows. Suppose we fix a price vector
\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}
. In order to determine a revenue maximizing allocation at this specific price, we must solve

\displaystyle r(\mathbf{p})=\max

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\rangle-\psi\left(\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\right)

(3.2)

s.t.

\displaystyle\,\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\in D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})\,\forall\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}.

Since the demand sets
D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})
are convex and compact and the objective function is concave, an optimal solution to this problem exists and can be determined efficiently. Problem (
1.1
) can now be rewritten as
\max_{\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}}r(\mathbf{p})
. Our strategy is to determine a finite list of prices
\mathbf{p}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}^{m}
among which a maximizer of
r(\mathbf{p})
is guaranteed to be found. By computing
r(\mathbf{p}^{j})
for all these prices, we find a global optimal solution to Problem (
1.1
). The
Filtered Prices
Algorithm
2
iterates over all tuples
(\mathbf{b}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}^{n})\in\mathcal{B}^{n}
of bids and permutations
\sigma
and calls the generate_candidate Algorithm
1
for all these inputs, which either returns a candidate price, or returns INFEASIBLE. Whenever the latter occurs, this allows us to ignore certain permutations as inputs to Algorithm
1
, reducing the number of iterations of the Filtered Prices Algorithm. Our main result is that the list of prices generated by Algorithm
2
contains a globally optimal solution to Problem (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
P
be the set of prices returned by Algorithm
2
. Then
P
contains a price
\mathbf{p}^{*}
which is an optimal solution to Problem (
1.1
). Consequently, by evaluating
r(\mathbf{p})
for all
\mathbf{p}\in P
, we solve Problem (
1.1
) globally.

Algorithm
2
is based on the insight that we can restrict our search for possible maximizing prices to points where bidders are indifferent between multiple goods. For the ease of notation, we introduce a zeroth dummy good, corresponding to the empty bundle. We add an additional zeroth entry to prices
\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}
by setting
p_{0}=1
and do the same for all bids
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}
by also setting
b_{0}=1
. For
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we write
\mathbf{e}_{i}
for the
i
-th standard vector and set
\mathbf{e}_{0}=\mathbf{0}\in\R^{n}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}
be a price vector and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}
a bid. Then

D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})=\conv\left\{(\beta(\mathbf{b})/p_{i})\mathbf{e}_{i}\,:\,i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\text{ and }b_{i}p_{j}\geq b_{j}p_{i}\,\forall j\in\{0,\dots,n\}\right\}.

The proof directly follows from the definition of the demand set and is omitted. We say that bidder
\mathbf{b}
demands good
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
at prices
\mathbf{p}
, if
(\beta(\mathbf{b})/p_{i})\mathbf{e}_{i}\in D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})
. Equivalently, good
i
is demanded if and only if
b_{i}p_{j}\geq b_{j}p_{i}
for all
j
. Given two distinct goods
i,j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, we consider the region in price space where a bidder
\mathbf{b}
is indifferent between these two goods, i.e., demands both of them.

Definition 3.3
.

A pair
(\mathbf{b},I)
, where
\mathbf{b}
is a bid and
I=\{i,j\}\subset\{0,\dots,n\}
with
|I|=2
is an unordered pair of indices, induces the
indifference hyperplane

H(\mathbf{b},I)=\{\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}\,:\,b_{i}p_{j}=b_{j}p_{i}\text{ and }b_{i}p_{k}\geq b_{k}p_{i}\,\forall k=0,\dots,n\}.

We say that a collection
(\mathbf{b}^{1},I^{1}),\dots,(\mathbf{b}^{m},I^{m})
of indifference hyperplanes is
linearly independent
, if their defining linear equations
b_{i}p_{j}=b_{j}p_{i}
are linearly independent (where we interpret
p_{0}
as the constant
1
, not as a variable).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(\mathbf{b}^{1},I^{1}),\dots,(\mathbf{b}^{n},I^{n})
be
n
linearly independent indifference hyperplanes intersecting in a price
\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}

with

and
|\mathcal{I}|<n+1

Proof.

Assume this is not the case. Then for any indifference hyperplane
(\mathbf{b}^{l},I^{l})
, either both indices of the variables of the defining linear equation are in
\mathcal{I}
, or none of them. So by linear independence, there must be equations with both variables in
\mathcal{I}^{c}
and those determine the values

for

uniquely. These equations are all of the form
b^{l}_{i}p_{j}=b^{l}_{j}p_{i}
for

and some

for

Remark
.

The number of all combinations of
n
-tuples of bids and all permutations is equal to
|\mathcal{B}|^{n}n!
. While this grows very fast in the number of goods
n
, it is polynomial in the number of bids when the number of goods is fixed. We also may expect that due to the feasibility check in steps
1
-
1
of the generate_candidate algorithm, the practical runtime is reduced significantly: whenever INFEASIBLE is returned in step
k
, we remove
(n-k)!
permutations from
S
. So in practice we may expect the algorithm to iterate over much less than
|\mathcal{B}|^{n}n!
combinations of inputs, and consequently to return the list of candidate prices reasonably fast when the number of different goods is not too large.

4
Conclusion

We have studied an algorithm proposed by Paul Klemperer and DotEcon for finding revenue maximizing prizes in the arctic Product-Mix auction and formally proved correctness of their approach. The algorithm is sufficiently fast when the number of different goods is small. Further research questions include a formal proof of the hardness of the problem, as well as the design of efficient approximation algorithms.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.4

We prove Proposition
3.4
with the help of two lemmas. Let us define the set
G^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})
of all goods demanded by bidder
\mathbf{b}
at prices
\mathbf{p}
:

\displaystyle G^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})=\left\{i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\,:\,(\beta(\mathbf{b})/p_{i})\mathbf{e}_{i}\in D^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})\right\}=

\displaystyle=\left\{i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\,:\,b_{i}/p_{i}\text{ maximal}\right\}.

The first lemma states that we only need to consider prices at which every good is demanded by some bidder.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\bar{\mathbf{p}}\in\R^{n}_{>0}
be arbitrary. Then there exists
\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}
with

\bigcup_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}G^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})\supseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
and
r(\mathbf{p})\geq r(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
k\not\in\bigcup_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}G^{\mathbf{b}}(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
. Then for all bids
\mathbf{b}
and all
i\in G^{\mathbf{b}}(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
we have
\bar{p}_{k}>(b_{k}/b_{i})\bar{p}_{i}
. Define
\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}
by
p_{i}=\bar{p}_{i}
for
i\neq k
and
p_{k}=\max_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\max_{i\in G^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})}(b_{k}/b_{i})\bar{p}_{i}
. Then we have
b_{i}/p_{i}\geq b_{k}/p_{k}
for all
\mathbf{b}
and all

, so
G^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})\supseteq G^{\mathbf{b}}(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
. Moreover, for maximizing bids
\mathbf{b}
and indices
i\in G^{\mathbf{b}}(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
in the definition of
p_{k}
we have
b_{i}/p_{i}=b_{k}/p_{k}
, so
k\in G^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p})
. It can easily be seen from Lemma
3.2
that every feasible solution for
r(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
is also feasible for
r(\mathbf{p})
. Moreover, any assigned bundle
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}
in an optimal solution of

has

, so
\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\rangle=\langle\bar{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}\rangle
. This implies

.
∎

The second lemma says that if we increase prices such that the set of demanded goods get larger, the revenue does no decrease.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\bar{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{p}\in\R^{n}_{>0}
such that
\mathbf{p}\geq\bar{\mathbf{p}}

Let

p^{*}_{i}\geq\bar{p}_{i}/2
can be tight, so there are
n
linearly independent equalities of the form
b_{j}p^{*}_{i}=b_{i}p^{*}_{j}
for some
i\neq j
. By definition of
P
, for each of the equalities
b_{j}p^{*}_{i}=b_{i}p^{*}_{j}
, we have that
b_{i}p^{*}_{k}\geq b_{k}p^{*}_{i}
for all
k
, so
\mathbf{p}^{*}\in H(\mathbf{b},\{i,j\})
. It follows that
\mathbf{p}^{*}
is the unique intersection point of the
n
linearly independent indifference hyperplanes corresponding to these equations. By definition of
P
we have that
G^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{p}^{*})\supseteq G^{\mathbf{b}}(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
, so
r(\mathbf{p}^{*})\geq r(\bar{\mathbf{p}})
by Lemma
A.2
.
∎
