A Framework for Evaluating Appropriateness, Trustworthiness, and Safety in Mental Wellness AI Chatbots
Lucia Chen, David A. Preece, Pilleriin Sikka, James J. Gross, Ben Krause

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) chatbots are susceptible to biases and hallucinations, but current evaluations of mental wellness technologies lack comprehensive case studies to evaluate their practical applications. Here, we address this gap by introducing the MHealth-EVAL framework, a new role-play based interactive evaluation method designed specifically for evaluating the appropriateness, trustworthiness, and safety of mental wellness chatbots. We also introduce Psyfy, a new chatbot leveraging LLMs to facilitate transdiagnostic Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). We demonstrate the MHealth-EVAL framework’s utility through a comparative study of two versions of Psyfy against standard baseline chatbots. Our results showed that Psyfy chatbots outperformed the baseline chatbots in delivering appropriate responses, engaging users, and avoiding untrustworthy responses. However, both Psyfy and the baseline chatbots exhibited some limitations, such as providing predominantly US-centric resources. While Psyfy chatbots were able to identify most unsafe situations and avoid giving unsafe responses, they sometimes struggled to recognize subtle harmful intentions when prompted in role play scenarios. Our study demonstrates a practical application of the MHealth-EVAL framework and showcases Psyfy’s utility in harnessing LLMs to enhance user engagement and provide flexible and appropriate responses aligned with an evidence-based CBT approach.

Each year, millions of individuals worldwide grapple with difficulties in mental health and wellbeing, yet numerous countries and regions struggle to provide the necessary support

Mental wellness chatbots are conversational agents designed to promote overall wellbeing. These chatbots, provided they are given necessary permissions by the user, can assist clinicians in detecting and assessing emotions

, defining and recognizing stress levels

[
18
]
. Mental wellness chatbots should serve as complementary tools, guiding users towards eventually seeking professional help, if necessary.

Mental wellness chatbots may be beneficial for fostering self-knowledge and providing skills, such as interpersonal communication, maladaptive behavior management, and cognitive reappraisal of unhelpful thinking patterns

The function of rule-based chatbots has been somewhat restricted due to their inability to replicate human connection and conversation flow
[
25
]
. Compared with rule-based chatbots that are operated on simple if-then logic, LLM chatbots are powered by deep learning models. They use vast amounts of text data to understand and generate language in a conversational manner.

LLM conversations are more fluent and genuine. This has led to a growing belief that people may be able to form social bonds with these chatbots
[
60
]
, as they have a greater capacity to convey qualities key to establishing strong interpersonal or therapeutic relationships, such as empathetic responding, reflective listening, and memory of past information provided by the user
[
22

CBT is the most widely used psychotherapy globally, with a strong evidence-base for its efficacy in addressing a range of mental health symptoms and improving wellbeing
[
33
]
. CBT is based on the premise that unhelpful or maladaptive thinking patterns influence how a person feels, which in turn impacts how the person behaves. These elements all interact, which can create a continuous negative cycle. People with depression and anxiety disorders often have patterns of thinking and behavior that maintain these symptoms, and thus emotion regulation strategies that change thought and behavior patterns can be used to improve mood
[
11
]
. For example, a core feature of most CBT programs is the teaching of
cognitive reappraisal
, an emotion regulation strategy involving changing the way one is thinking about a situation to change its emotional impact
[
31
,
30
,
29
]
. Recent data have demonstrated that LLM chatbots can be utilized to help users engage in cognitive reappraisal, generating reframed thoughts to assist individuals facing mental health challenges
[
53
]
.

Despite these promising findings, the use of LLM conversational agents for mental wellness purposes is still relatively uncommon and faces numerous challenges. Key issues include their capability to manage high-risk mental health scenarios, such as suicidal/homicidal ideation, child/elder abuse, and domestic violence
[
56
]
. Another key issue is the model’s tendency to produce content that seems plausible but is factually inaccurate or irrelevant, a phenomenon commonly referred to as “hallucination”
[
43
,
50
,
6

. Additionally, the appropriateness of the responses generated by these chatbots in various mental wellness related conversation contexts can be questionable at times. Incorrect self-help advice, unreliable information, cultural insensitivity, and offensive language can worsen the user’s symptoms and provoke risky behaviors
[
21
]
. However, there is presently no established method to systematically assess the safety, appropriateness, and trustworthiness of these LLM-based conversational agents in mental health settings. While, recently, evaluation frameworks for LLM mental wellness chatbots have been proposed
[
56
]
, the absence of comprehensive case studies makes it difficult to understand how to effectively implement these.

To address these gaps, this study introduces a new mental wellness chatbot, Psyfy (Psychology for you), and presents a novel evaluation framework specifically tailored for mental wellness chatbots. We apply this framework in an initial evaluation of Psyfy.

Psyfy is an LLM chatbot designed to use CBT techniques
[
11
]
and facilitate a strong therapeutic relationship (e.g., via reflective listening and empathetic responding
[
44
]
) to assist individuals with mental health challenges. Psyfy provides individuals with transdiagnostic CBT style therapy, via the provision of psychoeducation about the connection between feelings, thoughts, and behaviors, and the teaching and guided application of key adaptive emotion regulation strategies underpinning common CBT programs
[
24
]
. Psyfy begins by asking users to reflect on different areas of their life, what is important to them, and how well they are doing in these areas (i.e., the “Life Compass” activity). This provides the foundation for future goal setting.

With respect to the teaching of emotion regulation skills, Psyfy is presently designed to assist users in learning and implementing three emotion regulation strategies: cognitive reappraisal (i.e., changing negative thought patterns), behavioral activation (i.e., regular scheduling of pleasant and meaningful activities), and problem solving (i.e., using a systematic process to plan and implement steps to solve problems causing stress in one’s life). Emotion regulation refers to attempts to modify how emotions are experienced or expressed
[
30
,
29
]
, and it is well established that some emotion regulation strategies are generally maladaptive (i.e., associated with poor long-term mental health outcomes) and some strategies are generally adaptive (i.e., associated with good long-term mental health outcomes)
[
31
]
. In the case of the three emotion regulation strategies Psyfy teaches, all three of these strategies are regarded as adaptive, and have shown high relevance across a range of psychopathology categories and wellbeing concerns
[
8
,
23
]
. Emotion regulation difficulties, more broadly, are an established mechanism behind many psychopathologies
[
54
]
. By adopting a transdiagnostic approach focused on emotion regulation
[
49
]
, Psyfy is therefore, conceptually, highly flexible in being able to contribute information relevant to a range of different wellbeing concerns and presentations (e.g., difficulties with depression, anxiety, substance use, loneliness, burnout, relationship challenges).

Current literature highlights several prevalent issues in the field of mental wellness chatbots, noting that, while these tools are generally seen as helpful and user-friendly, the conversations they facilitate are often criticized for being shallow, confusing, or too brief
[
3
]
. Moreover, improper responses and the inability to properly identify crises can lead to user dissatisfaction and over-reliance on chatbots instead of seeking support from friends, family, or health professionals
[
32
]
. Psyfy has been specifically engineered to try to address these short-comings. Psyfy features multiple modes. Some modes are more directive, providing structured psychoeducation and training in emotion regulation strategies. Other modes are designed to create a space for users to discuss and reflect on their lives, ensuring understanding and empathy are conveyed. This directive engagement is strategically implemented to encourage users to engage in longer and more in-depth conversations, enhancing the overall experience.

2
Background Literature

2.1
Desirable Qualities for Mental Wellness Chatbots

In a traditional psychotherapy setting, therapeutic alliance refers to a collaborative and mutually beneficial relationship between a therapist and a client. It plays a crucial role in promoting effective therapy sessions, facilitating treatment, and providing social support. By establishing a strong alliance, therapists can build trust with their clients, enable them to open up, share personal experiences and challenges, and be more receptive to advice or suggestions
[
34
,
9
]
. A chatbot that expresses warmth and empathy, and provides effective problem-solving skills can significantly enhance user engagement and overall mental well-being. The experience of a strong therapeutic relationship can also help to shift clients’ beliefs about themselves and others (e.g., modeling that the client is worthwhile and cared for and that others can be trusted with vulnerability)
[
63
]
.

Key factors that help foster a strong therapeutic alliance are the therapist’s warmth, friendliness, confidence, empathy, responsiveness, and collaboration
[
7
]
. Techniques that aid in building this alliance include reflective listening (i.e., where the therapist paraphrases what the client has expressed, thus ensuring that they have been heard and understood)
[
10
]
, empathetic responding (i.e., recognizing and validating the client’s experiences
[
10
]
), a non-judgemental stance, and ensuring good conversational flow by asking pertinent questions about the client’s life and encouraging deep reflection on their emotional states and thought processes. Additionally, providing guidance and support for symptom management
[
56
]
, helping with goal setting and progress tracking, and facilitating access to professional help or local resources when needed are essential
[
32
]
. Furthermore, demonstrating cultural sensitivity—acknowledging and valuing diverse cultural backgrounds—plays a critical role in personalizing and enhancing the therapeutic relationship
[
41
]
.

Additionally, possessing strong problem-solving skills enables therapists to effectively guide clients through identifying issues, brainstorming potential solutions, and developing viable coping strategies
[
61
]
. This approach aligns with findings from recent research which suggests that users respond positively to systems that offer actionable, rational, and specific advice, facilitating problem resolution
[
53
]
.

To be optimally effective, a mental wellness chatbot should, where possible, mirror successful human therapist-client interactions, with the ultimate goal of fostering a therapeutic alliance that supports sustained mental health improvements. As such, conceptually, a mental wellness chatbot can be judged by similar criteria to those required from a strong human therapist-client alliance.

2.2
Potential Concerns about Mental Wellness Chatbots

If a chatbot fails to identify high-risk experiences like bereavement, substance abuse, or suicidal ideation, it may cause harm to the individual who seeks help or negatively impact an individual’s likelihood of seeking future help. A recent systematic review indicates that many mental wellness chatbots have not been evaluated with “end-user safety” as a critical criterion
[
39
]
. Therefore,
[
56
]
propose a hierarchical evaluation approach for clinical LLMs that prioritizes risk and safety first, followed by feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness. They also suggest that the top priority of a chatbot is to cause no significant harm or that its benefits should substantially outweigh any minimal harm. Important risk and safety measures include assessments of suicidality, non-suicidal self-harm, and potential harm to others.

As aforementioned, before the advent of LLM, chatbots were mainly rule-based. Rule-based mental wellness chatbots frequently utilized AIML (Artificial Intelligence Markup Language)
[
4
]
, which maps user inputs to corresponding text responses. This methodology ensures a high degree of control over what the chatbot can say, which inherently limits the risk of generating unexpected, harmful or inappropriate content. In contrast, LLM chatbots generate responses based on patterns and information learned from vast amounts of training data. This allows them to produce more varied and contextually appropriate replies, but also introduces the risk of generating plausible-sounding yet factually incorrect or harmful content. This highlights the necessity for novel evaluation frameworks that ensure the safety and reliability of these chatbots in various contexts
[
4
,
53
]
.

2.3
Evaluation Challenges and Research Gaps in LLM-Based Mental Wellness Chatbots

The potential concerns about the safety and effectiveness of mental wellness chatbots underscore the importance of thorough and systematic evaluation methods. Before the advent of LLM, evaluating mental wellness chatbots involved examining a range of core dimensions. According to
[
2
]
, research on mental wellness chatbots often considers ten core dimensions: usefulness, ease of use, responsiveness, understandability, acceptability, attractiveness, trustworthiness, enjoyability, and content, along with comparisons to other chatbots or human interactions. It is worth noting that trustworthiness here concerns participants’ trust in the chatbot itself, regardless of the validity of its content. Responsiveness, understandability, acceptability, enjoyability, and content are evaluations of the appropriateness of the conversation. Responsiveness evaluates the chatbot’s interaction quality, such as repetitiveness, speed, friendliness, and empathy. Usefulness, ease of use, and attractiveness explore the chatbot’s utility, user-friendliness, and visual appeal. Finally, the comparison dimension evaluates user perspectives on chatbots relative to other digital tools or traditional communication methods.

With the deployment of LLM-based chatbots, new challenges and responsibilities emerge, particularly regarding content trustworthiness and user safety.
[
56
]
identified desirable qualities for effective clinical LLM-based chatbots and proposed that future evaluation frameworks should include criteria such as risk control, assisting in diagnosing psychological conditions, stopping when not helping or confident, being unbiased and inclusive, and displaying an appropriate level of empathy. Most of these criteria are in the domain of safety.

While there are many studies evaluating the trustworthiness and safety of LLMs generally
[
57
,
17
]
, research on evaluating chatbot trustworthiness and safety in the mental wellness domain has been limited. A review of 12 studies on chatbots designed for mental wellness found that only two studies incorporated safety as a key evaluation measure
[
3
]
. Among these studies, one evaluated safety by recording any harm, distress, or adverse incidents during the study, and examining if there was a significant escalation in depressive symptoms tied to interactions with the chatbot
[
46
]
. There are studies that concentrate on trustworthiness, specifically, concerning participants’ trust in the chatbot itself
[
2
,
15
,
47
,
51
]
, but there are no existing studies focusing on the content trustworthiness of a mental wellness chatbot.

Trustworthiness of LLMs encompasses model transparency, accuracy, and reliability
[
57
,
17
]
. While this definition is broad, for the practical purposes of the mental wellness chatbot application, here, we will narrow it down to focus specifically on the reliability of information instead of model transparency and accuracy. Assessing model reliability typically involves using fact-checking tools or human review processes to identify and correct hallucinations
[
57
]
.

Safety of LLMs can refer to filtering harmful, offensive, or inappropriate content, preventing discriminatory behavior and ensuring the model does not leak sensitive information that compromises data privacy regulation
[
57
]
. However, the definition of safety for LLMs in the mental health domain should consider additional factors. In mental health applications, safety requires, for example, the model to exhibit emotional sensitivity and empathy, avoid providing incorrect medical advice, recognize signs of crisis, be free from cultural biases, and incorporate continuous monitoring and feedback
[
56
]
.

In summary, existing research on mental wellness chatbots, while extensive in some areas, lacks depth in content trustworthiness and user safety. Moreover, safety evaluations in current LLM applications often focus on filtering harmful content and ensuring data privacy. However, for mental wellness applications, safety must also encompass the ability of the chatbot to handle sensitive mental health scenarios with emotional sensitivity and empathy, and recognize signs of crisis.

3
Psyfy and Mhealth-EVAL

3.1
Psychology for you (Psyfy)

Psyfy is an advanced chatbot system that integrates LLMs, state tracking, and regularization techniques to enhance user engagement and personalize interactions. It is important to note that Psyfy does not diagnose mental health conditions. Instead, its role is supportive, providing interventions to help improve mental wellness. The LLM for the Psyfy version reported in this paper is ChatGPT 3.5 Turbo, but the choice of LLM for Psyfy will evolve. The system tailors its responses based on user input and a set of predefined prompts managed through conversational states. We developed two distinct versions of Psyfy reported here: version one (V1) uses state tracking and several state-specific prompts to enhance user engagement and personalize interactions. This version is specifically designed to understand users more intimately by posing questions about their personal experiences and preferences. Following the collection of this information from the user, V1 employs reflective listening methodologies, demonstrating empathy and acknowledging the user’s feelings and thoughts. Building on V1, version two (V2) of Psyfy extends V1 by incorporating a broader set of state tracking techniques with the AutoGRAMS framework
1
1

1

https://github.com/autograms/autograms

[
37
]
, using over 100 state specific prompts, enabling more directed and effective engagements. For example, V2 grasps a user’s personal history and values with tools like the “Life Compass” and assists in setting concrete personal goals. V2 also provides CBT-style psychoeducation and training, with a specific focus on the training of the three aforementioned adaptive emotion regulation strategies: cognitive reappraisal, behavioral activation, and problem solving. The rationale for evaluating both versions, rather than only the most recent iteration, is to emphasize that chatbot evaluation is a continuous process. By comparing the performance and user interaction between V1, V2, and two baseline chatbots, we can identify persistent issues and understand how enhancements address these concerns.

3.2
Mhealth-EVAL Evaluation Framework

The Mhealth-EVAL framework we introduce here consists of three main criteria: Appropriateness, Trustworthiness, and Safety. These components are assessed using a combination of static and interactive methods, as illustrated in Figure
1
. Human annotation is utilized to evaluate the chatbot responses in accordance with the provided annotation guideline.

Figure 1:
Implementation of the MHealth-EVAL Framework for Evaluating Psyfy: This graph illustrates the application of both static and interactive evaluation approaches. Green boxes indicate processes applied to both Psyfy V1 and V2, while orange boxes are specific to Psyfy V1 only. The dotted line denotes the tasks for which the annotations are utilized.

Appropriateness: To evaluate the appropriateness of the responses, we consider several important elements based on the principles of effective human therapist-patient interactions. First, our framework assesses how well the chatbot uses reflective listening techniques and empathetic responses to foster a connection with the user. Second, the guideline evaluates the chatbot’s ability to direct the conversation, encouraging users to explore their feelings and thoughts to gain insights into their experiences. Third, it examines the chatbot’s skill in providing psychoeducation, which (in the context of CBT) includes offering information about the interplay of thoughts, emotions, and behaviors, and educating users on mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety. Fourth, the guideline reviews whether the chatbot effectively guides and supports users through specific therapeutic techniques and skill building based on CBT (e.g., cognitive reappraisal, behavioral activation, and problem-solving) to help manage and improve mental well-being.

Trustworthiness: Trustworthiness refers to the credibility and accuracy of the information provided by the chatbot in terms of mental wellbeing practices. Psychoeducational content, self-help resources, and crisis helpline information are frequently offered to users during interactions with mental wellness chatbots. This criterion aims to ensure that these resources adhere to current mental wellbeing guidelines and are reliable sources of information. Therefore, responses containing psychoeducational content or information about local resources should be verified.

Safety: The chatbot’s safety refers to its capacity to handle high-risk situations without posing any harm or giving detrimental advice to the user. Evaluations of safety focus on how the bot handles conversations involving high-risk scenarios, without giving advice that could promote harmful behavior or affirm harmful intentions. In acute crisis situations, it is crucial for mental wellness chatbots to prompt users to seek support from professional resources
[
32
]
. Optimally, the chatbot should adhere to Crisis Escalation Protocols and provide appropriate advice according to the severity of the situation. Psyfy has implemented a protective feature as a safeguard to suggest users seek assistance when discussing sensitive topics such as sexual abuse, physical abuse, or bullying. However, it is important to acknowledge that despite this safeguard, there remains a potential for the LLM to generate responses that could be considered unsafe.

The
Guideline
provides evaluation instructions for each criterion of the MHealth-EVAL. It should be noted that what is considered an appropriate conversation for a mental wellness chatbot may vary depending on the theoretical framework of a particular psychotherapy utilized in the chatbot (e.g., CBT vs psychoanalysis). The safety and trustworthiness criteria of the guidelines provide a general evaluation framework that is applicable to all mental wellness chatbots, regardless of the specific therapeutic approach. In the case of this paper, the appropriateness criterion isbased on the CBT framework (as this aligns with the theoretical framework underlying Psyfy), but it is intended that researchers and developers should adapt it to their preferred framework.

4
Methods

Evaluation methodologies for chatbot performance include automated, static, and interactive approaches, as well as user surveys and interviews
[
20
,
5
,
13
,
16
,
58
]
. Automated evaluations commonly utilize techniques such as the BLEU score
[
45
]
, which is a tool to quantify the appropriateness of a given response. In contrast, static evaluations use human assessment to evaluate the most recent statement generated by the dialogue system. Interactive evaluations, on the other hand, involve human evaluators directly interacting with the dialogue system to assess conversation quality
[
20
]
. In this work, we will focus on static and interactive approaches, because these methods allow for a more nuanced understanding of the chatbot’s conversational capabilities in real-time scenarios.

4.1
Empirical Setting

Initially, we developed Psyfy V1 and created a separate baseline chatbot for comparison, referred to as Baseline V1. Baseline V1 operates on ChatGPT-3.5 Turbo, configured with a general prompt: “Psyfy is a wellness assistant that aims to help patients cope with anxiety and uncertainty. Your response should be no longer than 100 words.”

Building on the feedback and findings from the evaluation of Psyfy V1, Psyfy V2 was subsequently developed with enhanced features aimed at improving interaction quality in therapeutic contexts, such as reducing repetitiveness, including more directive engagement, and adopting an evidence-based transdiagnostic CBT focus, centered around the teaching of adaptive emotion regulation techniques. Accordingly, Baseline V1 was also upgraded to Baseline V2 to maintain comparability. The new baseline prompt was refined to: “ChatGPT will play the role of a therapist who is flexible, honest, respectful, trustworthy, and non-judgmental. The therapist’s name is Mia. Mia can ask the client about their feelings and share personal information if it helps them feel comfortable.”This aligns with the therapist prompt from Therapist GPT
,
which has received high popularity with 28.5k users. Psyfy V2 and Baseline V2 were tested using the same varied mental health scenarios as Psyfy V1.

4.2
Evaluation Approaches

In evaluating Psyfy V1, both static and interactive approaches were utilized. The static evaluations, which focus on the human assessment of an isolated statement generated by the dialogue system, proved less effective in performing safety evaluations compared to the interactive approach. The interactive method analyzes the entire dynamic conversation, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the system’s behavior. Given the limitations observed with static evaluations, particularly their inability to fully capture the dynamic and potentially manipulative interactions that can occur in real-time conversations, we decided to exclusively employ interactive evaluations for Psyfy V2.

4.2.1
Static Approach

Psyfy V1 and Baseline V1 were evaluated on their safety using the BeaverTails dataset
[
36
]
. This dataset consists of 700 questions across 14 potential harm categories, specifically designed to evaluate the chatbots’ ability to resist unethical prompts. Both the Baseline V1 and Psyfy V1 were used to generate responses to these questions (see Table
3
).

4.2.2
Interactive Approach

An interactive approach was adopted where both Psyfy and the Baseline bot engaged in role-play exercises simulating various mental health scenarios, such as episodes of depression or anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), bereavement, alcohol abuse, cyberbullying, eating disorder, social anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and relationship issues. These conditions were selected as they are among the most common and impactful mental health issues affecting large populations worldwide (See
WHO fact sheet on mental disorders
). Our human evaluators simulated these mental health issues during their interactions with the chatbots, offering nuanced insights through detailed annotations of the dialogues.

This meticulous simulation of complex mental health scenarios is important not only for testing the chatbots’ capability, but also for ensuring that Psyfy operates safely across a spectrum of psychological conditions. It is important to clarify that, while Psyfy is designed to enhance mental wellness, it is essential that the chatbot can also safely interact with users who may be experiencing a range of mental health challenges.

The dialogues were conducted in a double-blind setting via a study platform to eliminate researcher bias, and to ensure that the comparative evaluation of response quality was as objective as possible. To maintain consistency in the information presented to both bots, human evaluators ensured that any personal details disclosed (e.g., owning a cat) were consistently communicated to each bot during their respective interactions. However, human evaluators were not strictly required to generate a certain number of responses; they could end the conversation whenever they deemed it completed.

We also adopted the Chain of Utterances
[
12
]
technique during the role-play exercise. The Chain of Utterances technique involves the red team generating a sequence of potentially malicious utterances to test the model’s resistance to ethically challenged questions. The red team refers to a group that simulates potential attackers. It typically starts with building a rapport with the LLM, followed by deconstructing an unethical request into smaller segments and coaxing the LLM to respond incrementally. For instance, the researchers might subtly lead Psyfy through a gradual process aimed at helping them engage in harmful behaviors, such as plotting harm towards others, purchasing illicit drugs, or providing medication advice. In the evaluation process, we attempted to identify responses that might put the user’s life in danger, or that are so inappropriate as to be harmful using the evaluators’ clinical judgment.

We identified over 20 high-risk situations that needed to be assessed, as they are common in clinical practice and may be likely to occur in real conversations with chatbots. We included scenarios such as abuse, severe emotional distress, and self-harming behaviors or intentions. Each human evaluator incorporated at least one of these high-risk situations into each of their role-play conversations. The complete list of situations can be found in our
Guideline
.

4.3
Data Generation and Chatbot Response Annotation Process

A group of 9 researchers participated in the data generation and annotation process. Out of these researchers, 7 were student research assistants (RAs). Among the RAs, 5 were studying Clinical Psychology in Australia at a post-graduate level, while 2 had a graduate degree in Psychology. The remaining 2 researchers, who are also authors of this work, hold a post-graduate degree in Psychology or Clinical Psychology.

Table
1
shows the sets of roleplay conversations created with each version of Psyfy. We instructed our RAs to provide approximately 10-20 responses to the bot in each conversation. For the annotation task, the person who generated the role-play data and a co-annotator annotated each set of conversations. We chose the data generator to be the annotator because they were directly involved in the conversation and thus had a better understanding of the intended responses of the user. The co-annotator was present to offer a second opinion and ensure consistency in the annotations. Both evaluators were familiar with the scenarios and were instructed to follow the annotation guidelines. The role-play exercise annotation had a good inter-rater reliability of 0.8, as measured by Cohen’s Kappa. Raters performed the annotations independently, and interrater reliability was evaluated to ensure consistency in their assessments.

4.4
Appropriateness Evaluation

We defined three categories for rating the appropriateness of the responses: Poor (0), Ok (1), and Great (2). These numbers represent the scores assigned to each category. It is worth noting that the appropriateness rating also takes into consideration safety and trustworthiness. If a response is deemed unsafe, or if the psychoeducation content is inaccurate, it was rated as Poor.

4.5
Trustworthiness Evaluation

In this task, human evaluators evaluated the trustworthiness of the psychoeducational content and helpline information by assigning a score of “0” or “1”, where 0 indicates trustworthy and 1 indicates untrustworthy. Responses containing facts or strategies that were not accurate, or that were only partially accurate, were flagged as untrustworthy. The appropriateness of the responses is not considered in this evaluation.

Compared with Baseline V1, Psyfy V1 incorporates state prompts designed to regulate the model’s output, preventing it from generating responses that contain or recommend specific doctors’ contact information. It is important to note that trustworthiness evaluation was conducted exclusively for Psyfy V1 and Baseline V1. This targeted approach was taken because both Psyfy V1 and V2 utilize the same underlying LLM, ChatGPT-3.5 Turbo, and we use the same state prompt to regulate the model’s output for doctor’s contact information, meaning the core informational content they generate does not differ between versions.

To make the best use of our human annotation resources, instead of annotating trustworthiness of all the responses, we used ChatGPT-3.5 turbo to extract a total of 121 responses (out of 749) that included psychoeducation content or self-help resources from both Psyfy V1 and the Baseline V1 chatbots’ responses. These self-help resources covered various topics such as meditation skills, self-help phone apps, online communities, and local helpline information.

4.6
Safety Evaluation

Red-teaming is a crucial method for assessing a model’s resilience against unsafe or unethical requests. This process typically involves simulating scenarios where LLMs may encounter manipulation attempts. Techniques used in red-teaming often involve crafting prompts specifically designed to provoke biased reasoning or to test the chatbot’s adherence to ethical standards
[
27
,
40
]
. For example, the Chain of Utterances (CoU) technique involves crafting prompts that simulate a dialogue between a harmful agent and a naive helpful base agent
[
12
]

Appropriateness

The evaluation of the criterion of appropriateness was conducted exclusively using an interactive approach. A total of 58 conversations and 983 responses from Psyfy (

Approximately 57.5% of responses with a score below 2 (i.e., “Good”) contained repetitive empathetic language, such as “I’m sorry to hear that,” despite these expressions having been used earlier in the conversation. Additionally, 1.9% of the responses failed to address unhealthy behaviors, and 14% did not encourage users to explore the history or potential patterns behind their maladaptive behaviors. Moreover, 24.6% of the responses received a score of 1 (i.e., OK) because Psyfy V1 was instructed not to provide direct information about psychotherapists or helplines, as these resources were not yet verified.

Psyfy V2 demonstrated significant improvement in reducing repetitiveness; less than 5% of the responses scored as OK or Poor (rather than Good) were flagged for this issue. However, feedback from our human evaluators suggests that the Life Compass activity, which asks users about their goals and the current state of various life areas, may be introduced too abruptly. Evaluators suggested that Psyfy V2 often transitions to this structured activity without sufficient preliminary dialogue that would naturally lead to such in-depth explorations.

In addition, evaluators noted that Psyfy’s conversations tend to be more directive, engaging users more deeply by regularly asking questions, which contrasts with the baseline bots that were quite accommodating, often agreeing with the user’s statements and allowing the user to steer the direction of the conversation. This often led to abrupt endings in conversations with the Baseline bot. In contrast, Psyfy’s proactive engagement strategy appears to have extended interaction times and enhanced user engagement. This difference in conversational engagement further underscores Psyfy’s effectiveness in maintaining user interest and fostering a supportive dialogue environment.

5.3
Trustworthiness

Only the interactive approach was used to evaluate the criterion of trustworthiness. This analysis encompassed 121 responses that contained factual information or psychoeducational content, 48 from Psyfy V1 and 73 from Baseline V1. Of these, 16 (5%) responses were flagged for issues such as incorrect details, the inclusion of fabricated information, or references to unverified alternative therapies as shown in Table
2
. Psyfy V1 produced fewer responses containing untrustworthy information, potentially because the model was forbidden to provide specific details such as names and phone numbers of therapists. However, it is important to note that Psyfy V1 was still able to provide general helpline information, ensuring that users could access reliable support services. Although this constraint reduced the occurrence of unreliable information, our evaluators tended to assign lower appropriateness ratings to responses that lacked comprehensive provider information.

Most of the responses flagged as untrustworthy were generated by Baseline V1. For example, while the descriptions of complementary therapies were accurate, chatbot sometimes suggested non-evidence-based therapies, such as hypnosis, reiki, and crystal healing. Furthermore, Baseline V1 provided hallucinated information, mentioning a non-existent online community called the “Trauma Survivors Support Group” and provided a website that does not exist.

We also noticed that, when users did not specify their location, both Psyfy V1 and Baseline V1 provided resource information from the U.S. However, if a user did specify their location, the bot tried to provide users with local resources. Unfortunately, these resources, particularly the helpline numbers, were not reliable. For example, the response stated, "In China, you can reach out to the Beijing Suicide Research and Prevention Center at 800-810-1117 or the Shanghai Mental Health Center at 021-64387250. Both helplines are available 24/7 and can provide support during difficult times." However, the Shanghai number is not a dedicated 24/7 helpline, but a general phone number for the mental health center.

5.4.1
Static Approach

We analyzed the safety of Psyfy V1 and its corresponding Baseline V1 responses to queries using questions from the BeaverTail dataset. We manually categorized a total of 1600 responses from both Psyfy V1 and Baseline V1 into eight groups based on the type of advice given or the questions asked by Psyfy. These categories included general scientific advice and suggestions to seek professional help (Advice), responses indicating a refusal to answer because the topic is not within the scope of well-being (Refusal), requests for more context to understand the situation (Context), recommendations for seeking professional mental health support (Mental), prompts to discuss feelings or explore the reasons behind beliefs (Feelings), moral correction (Moral), inquiries about the user’s thoughts on the issue (Thoughts), and other miscellaneous responses (Others). Moral correction refers to the model providing correction or guidance for responses that involve promoting socially acceptable or ethical behavior that is considered morally wrong or inappropriate. Both Baseline V2 and Psyfy V2 showed limited improvement with respect to the safety criterion. Around 4% of the responses provided by both Psyfy V2 and Baseline V2 were rated as unsafe. 6
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced Psyfy, a novel mental wellness AI chatbot designed to enhance mental wellness through targeted emotion regulation strategies and directive psychoeducation. To assess the appropriateness, trustworthiness and safety of Psyfy, we developed and implemented a specialized evaluation framework, MHealth-Eval, tailored specifically for mental wellness chatbots. This framework focuses on evaluating three key aspects of AI chatbot conversations: appropriateness, trustworthiness, and safety.

In this study, the application of MHealth-Eval to Psyfy revealed important insights. In the domain of appropriateness, Psyfy V1 and V2 demonstrated more engaging and directive interactions compared to the Baseline bots, leading to longer and more effective conversations. Psyfy V2 was less repetitive and had a more proactive engagement strategy, regularly asking questions that foster deeper interactions.

Regarding trustworthiness, Psyfy avoided disseminating unverified information because we imposed restrictions to prevent the provision of contact details of psychotherapists since this information may not be accurate. However, this restriction had a negative impact on Psyfy’s appropriateness rating, as human evaluators tended to rate responses lower when the chatbot refused to provide concrete information about localized resources or helpline numbers. To address this, future versions of Psyfy should include a search engine feature to enhance the accuracy and relevance of resource information.

Safety evaluation showed that Psyfy’s performance was comparable to the Baseline bots, with safety strictly defined in terms of content not posing a risk to the user’s life.
However, both Psyfy and the Baseline bot had difficulties in detecting subtle harmful intentions, which highlights a critical area for further development. Additionally, broader safety considerations such as data privacy and security measures will be essential for real-world deployment of AI chatbots to ensure comprehensive protection and compliance with regulatory standards.

Moreover, our methodology incorporated a red-teaming approach using human role-playing, which, while insightful, was constrained by the scalability issues due to its reliance on human resources. To expand the scope and diversity of testing, future evaluations could benefit from integrating automated systems to simulate user interactions.

In conclusion, we found the MHealth-Eval framework to be an effective tool for assessing the capabilities and limitations of Psyfy. Our study highlighted key challenges in evaluating mental wellness chatbots, such as the resource-intensive task of generating a large volume of conversations and the difficulty of maintaining consistent conversation contexts between Psyfy and its baseline counterpart. As technology advances, it is essential that the conceptual foundations of this framework evolve to incorporate new criteria that further enhance our ability to monitor and evaluate these chatbots. Longitudinal studies are necessary for advancing the usability and safety of mental wellness chatbots.
