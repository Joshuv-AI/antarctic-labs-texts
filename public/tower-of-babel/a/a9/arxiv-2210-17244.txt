Hyperbolic–parabolic normal form and local classical solutions for cross-diffusion systems with incomplete diffusion
Pierre-Étienne Druet, Katharina Hopf, Ansgar Jüngel

Abstract
. We investigate degenerate cross-diffusion equations with a rank-deficient diffusion matrix that are considered to model populations which move as to avoid spatial crowding and have recently been found to arise in a mean-field limit of interacting stochastic particle systems. To date, their analysis in multiple space dimensions has been confined to the purely convective case with equal mobility coefficients. In this article, we introduce a normal form for an entropic class of such equations which reveals their structure of a symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic system. Due to the state-dependence of the range and kernel of the singular diffusive matrix, our way of rewriting the equations is different from that classically used for symmetric second-order systems with a nullspace invariance property. By means of this change of variables, we solve the Cauchy problem for short times and positive initial data in H s ​ ( 𝕋 d ) H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}) for s > d / 2 + 1 s>d/2+1 .

1.
Introduction

In the present paper, we are interested in the Cauchy problem for a class of cross-diffusion systems with a rank-deficient diffusion matrix and a porous medium-type degeneracy. For
n
species with partial densities
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
and partial velocity fields
{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{n}
, we consider the conservation laws

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{i}+\operatorname{div}(u_{i}\textbf{v}_{i})=0,\quad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{T}^{d},\ i=1,\dots,n.

Here,
\mathbb{T}^{d}
denotes the periodic box and
n
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
are arbitrary integers. From a simple phenomenological viewpoint, the type of diffusion system we are interested in arises when the motion is driven by density gradients according to

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}\nabla u_{j},

where
b_{ij}
are phenomenological coefficients.
For a derivation of such equations from a weakly interacting stochastic many-particle system, we refer to
[
6
]
.

In the special case where
b_{ij}=k\partial_{u_{j}}\widehat{p}
for some
k>0
and a suitable equation of state for the thermodynamic pressure

\widehat{p}=\widehat{p}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
,
we obtain a purely convective motion with Darcy law
{\bf v}_{i}={\bf v}=-k{\nabla}\widehat{p}
.
Problems of this type have been studied in several works concerning the well-posedness of classical and weak solutions
[
8
,
14
]
as well as with regard to their segregation property, see for instance
[
4
,
15
]
, where the model is enhanced by competition-type reactions arising in tissue growth modelling.

A prototypical example motivating the present work is obtained for the choice

(1.3)

\displaystyle b_{ij}=k_{i}a_{j}\quad\text{with}\quad k_{1},\dots,k_{n}>0,\ a_{1},\dots,a_{n}>0,

leading to the equations

(
Rk1
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{i}-\operatorname{div}(k_{i}u_{i}{\nabla}\mathfrak{p}(u))=0\quad\text{with}\quad\mathfrak{p}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}u_{i}.

This is the model considered by Bertsch et al.
[
2
,
3
]
for
d=1
,
n=2
and segregated solutions. It was first proposed in
[
13
]
to describe populations that disperse in order to avoid spatial crowding.
Few further rigorous studies exist for this or similar models when the
k_{i}
are not all equal. The study in
[
21
]
on a two-species model illustrates numerically and through travelling wave analysis that unequal coefficients
k_{1}\not=k_{2}
can lead to instabilities in the presence of a reaction term.

There are several energy functionals
F(u)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(u)\,\mathrm{d}x
available for the simple model (
Rk1
), allowing to exhibit an underlying gradient-flow structure,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{M}(u)\nabla\mu)=0,

where
\mu=\partial_{u}F(u)
is the vector of chemical potentials
and
\mathbb{M}(u)
denotes the mobility tensor. First, we note the so-called Shannon entropy
[
27
]
generated by the density

\displaystyle f_{1}(u):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}(u_{i}\log u_{i}-u_{i})\quad\text{with}\quad\pi_{i}:=\frac{a_{i}}{k_{i}}.

In this case, the
i^{\rm th}
chemical potential is
\mu_{i}=\pi_{i}\log u_{i}
, and (
1.4
) is true for
\mathbb{M}(u)=(ku)\otimes(ku)
with
ku=(k_{1}u_{1},\ldots,k_{n}u_{n})
. Every density function
f
of the form
f(u)=(\varphi\circ\mathfrak{p})(u)
with a strictly convex function
\varphi\in C^{2}(0,\infty)

generates another Lyapunov functional for system (
Rk1
). If we choose, for instance,

(1.5)

\displaystyle f_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}u_{i}\bigg)^{2},

then the chemical potential is given by
\mu_{i}=a_{i}\mathfrak{p}(u)
, yielding (
1.4
) with
\mathbb{M}(u)={\rm diag}(u_{i}/\pi_{i})
.
This shows that the constitutive equations underlying (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), and in particular the rank deficiency of the
diffusion matrix, may be subject to different interpretations: strictly convex free energy and rank-deficient mobility tensor for (
1.4
), or full-rank mobility tensor but rank-deficient Hessian for the free energy (
1.5
).
Interestingly, only the choice
f_{3}(u)=\mathfrak{p}(u)\log\mathfrak{p}(u)-\mathfrak{p}(u)
of the free energy
yields the identity

\mathfrak{p}=-f_{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\partial_{u_{i}}f_{3}
,
which can be interpreted as the Gibbs–Duhem equation
for the thermodynamic pressure
\mathfrak{p}
.

In the general case of (
1.2
), we assume that the matrix

(1.6)

\displaystyle B=(b_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\text{ }

\displaystyle\text{is
symmetric, positive semidefinite}

\displaystyle\text{and has rank }1\leq\mathsf{r}\leq n.

More generally, our results hold for matrices
B
for which there exists a
vector
\widehat{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
such that the product

B\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\pi}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\pi}_{n})

obeys the properties in (
1.6
).
Indeed, this case can be reduced to (
1.6
) by the rescaling

u_{i}\mapsto u_{i}/\widehat{\pi}_{i}
. In particular, the asymmetric
case (
1.3
) can be covered in this way.

Note that in many physical and biological situations, where the dynamics of the species is driven by pressure-like quantities or frictional interactions, it is reasonable to further impose the non-negativity of the coefficients
b_{ij}
.
However, our analysis shall reveal that positivity is a requirement only for

(b_{ij})
as an operator.

System (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) with (
1.6
) admits the free energy functions

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{1}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{i}\log u_{i}-u_{i}),\quad\widetilde{f}_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}b_{ij}u_{i}u_{j}.

In the context of mathematical biology, the functional associated with the quadratic function
\widetilde{f}_{2}
is known as the Rao entropy
[
24
]
.
Rank-deficiency can for example be justified if the driving functional measures some dissimilarity between the species (genetic characters,
\widetilde{f}_{2}(u)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}
). Recently, in the context of electrochemistry, Eisenberg and Lin proposed a similar quadratic correction of the standard entropy to describe repulsive interactions between ions crowded in small channels; see
[
20
, eq. (2.11)]
with
b_{ij}=\epsilon_{ij}(a_{i}+a_{j})^{12}
,
the interaction energies
\epsilon_{ij}\geq 0
and the diameter
a_{i}>0
of the
i^{\rm th}
ion species.
More generally, rank-deficiency for the Hessian of the free energy function is a singular limit, which can be motivated from general representation theorems for the free energy function (see equations (30), (32) in
[
5
]
), when the behaviour of the system is widely dominated by its response under pressure, hence by the equation of state rather than by the statistical entropy of mixing.

Motivation and key aspects

With the choice (
1.3
), the second-order system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) does not enjoy full parabolicity, and even for
u_{i}
strictly positive, the system is not parabolic in the sense of Petrovskii. Thus, the theories by Amann or Solonnikov do not apply in the present setting and we cannot expect the classical parabolic smoothing properties. Therefore, even the local-in-time well-posedness in spaces of high regularity is a non-trivial question, which has hardly been investigated in the literature for rank-deficient cross-diffusions.
The present article aims to provide an answer to this question for system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) under conditions (
1.6
).
Our key idea is to separate the degenerate variables from the parabolic evolution in such a way that the resulting system has a (symmetrisable) hyperbolic–parabolic structure.
After identification of such a proper set of variables, we are able to obtain closed higher-order a priori estimates for short times by means of classical energy methods, which then allow for the construction of local-in-time strong solutions by iteration.
We emphasize that for general second-order quasilinear symmetric systems, the commutator terms arising upon higher-order spatial differentiation in the equation for
\partial^{\alpha}u_{i}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
may not be controlled and standard parabolic estimates generally fail in the rank-deficient case.

For positive densities, the diffusion matrix associated with system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) (resp. (
1.1
), (
1.2
) with (
1.6
)) has rank one (resp. rank
\mathsf{r}
).
Thus, we are looking for a change of variables that transforms the system into a composite problem coupling a Friedrichs-symmetrisable hyperbolic system for
n{-}1
(resp. for
n{-}\mathsf{r}
) components to a scalar parabolic equation (resp. to a parabolic system for
\mathsf{r}
components).
We recall that Friedrichs-symmetrisable first-order systems allow for energy estimates in a similar way as scalar equations and that, as is well known, symmetrisability is a direct consequence of the existence of a strictly convex entropy
[
9
,
12
]
. While the entropic structure of the present models allows us to recast the equations in the form of a (degenerate) second-order symmetric system (see the introductory comments in Section
5
), it is not obvious why symmetrisability should be
inherited by any first-order subsystem that might be obtained after changing coordinates.

The main results of this article can be summarized as follows:

•

We provide transformations leading to a normal form of symmetric
hyperbolic–parabolic type for the rank-one systems (
Rk1
).

•

We derive a transformation that allows us to write
system (
1.1
), (
1.2
) with (
1.6
)
for an arbitrary rank
\mathsf{r}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
in a symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic form.

•

We show that such systems, for any rank
\mathsf{r}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
admit unique local-in-time classical solutions provided the initial densities lie in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for some
s>d/2+1
and are strictly positive.

Comparison to existing literature on hyperbolic–parabolic systems

Normal forms of symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic type have first been introduced in the context of viscous compressible fluid dynamics.
In their seminal work
[
17
]
, Kawashima and Shizuta rely on entropic variables and a nullspace invariance condition of the matrix associated with the second-order spatial differential operator in order to derive an appropriate composite form. In our class of systems, this invariance condition amounts to a state-independence of
the kernel or range of the underlying mobility tensor
\mathbb{M}(u)
, a property which is not fulfilled in the present cross-diffusive problem.
Thus, the results by Kawashima and Shizuta, including their extensions in
[
11
,
25
,
26
]
,
are not directly applicable.

The normal forms that arise here (see equation (
2.10
))
are structurally different from those appearing in the fluid dynamics context.
They also seem to be new with respect to more recent work
on hyperbolic–parabolic problems considered, for instance, in
thermoviscoelasticity
[
7
]
.
A trivial version of our normal form is obtained for (
Rk1
) when setting
k_{i}=1
for all
i
(or equal to any other fixed constant).
Then, if
n=2
, the change of variables
w_{1}=u_{1}/(u_{1}+u_{2})
,
w_{2}=a_{1}u_{1}+a_{2}u_{2}
, as first employed in
[
4
]
, leads to the pure transport equation
\partial_{t}w_{1}=\nabla w_{2}\cdot\nabla w_{1}

whose velocity field is given by the negative gradient of the solution to the
porous-medium equation
\partial_{t}w_{2}=\operatorname{div}(w_{2}\nabla w_{2})
.

From a technical point of view, the difficulties in the Cauchy problem associated with our class of normal forms are not very different from those arising in (viscous) systems of conservations laws. The symmetrisability of the hyperbolic subsystem allows us to adopt an
L^{2}
-approach, which was first applied by Kawashima to symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic systems, requiring initial data in
H^{s}
for
s>d/2+2

[
16
]
.
This regularity condition was improved by Serre
[
25
]
, who only required
s>d/2+1
and obtained strong solutions.
With regard to the classical solutions constructed here, the condition
s>d/2+1
in the
L^{2}
-framework should essentially be optimal, as it just yields the continuous differentiability of the hyperbolic components as well as the spatial Lipschitz continuity of the ‘velocity field’.
We obtain classical solutions by exploiting a modest regularising effect in the quasilinear strongly parabolic subsystem, whose coefficient matrix has limited regularity due to its dependence on the hyperbolic components. It turns out that the regularity is sufficient to deduce classical solvability for small positive times.

Finally, we point out that specific situations may allow for improved results and alternative methods.
Besides the one-dimensional case
d=1
, the binary case
n=2
and, more generally, the case
\mathsf{r}=n-1
are special, since then the hyperbolic part of the system is scalar and can thus be treated by the method of characteristics for quasilinear first-order equations.
Boundary-value problems may also be treated more easily in this case.

.

3.
An explicit normal form for rank-one systems

In this section, we provide the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
Observe that if a diffeomorphism
\Phi
has the required properties in the case
a_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,n
in (
1.3
), then the
transformation
\Phi\circ\Gamma
with
\Gamma_{i}(u)=a_{i}u_{i}
enjoys the properties needed in the general case
a_{i}>0
for all
i
.
We therefore assume that
a_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,n
in (
1.3
).

3.1.
Change of variables

Between the domains
\mathcal{\widehat{D}}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and

\mathcal{D}:=\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
n^{\prime}:=n-1
,
we consider the transformation
\Phi:\mathcal{\widehat{D}}\to\mathcal{D}
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Phi(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\log\big(u_{1}^{1/k_{1}}\big/u_{n}^{1/k_{n}}\big)\\
\vdots\\
\log\big(u_{n^{\prime}}^{1/k_{n^{\prime}}}\big/u_{n}^{1/k_{n}}\big)\\[3.0pt]
\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{for }u\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}.

Its Jacobian

(3.2)

\mathrm{D}\Phi(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{k_{1}u_{1}}&0&\dots&0&-\frac{1}{k_{n}u_{n}}\\
0&\frac{1}{k_{2}u_{2}}&0&\dots&-\frac{1}{k_{n}u_{n}}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&\frac{1}{k_{n^{\prime}}u_{n^{\prime}}}&-\frac{1}{k_{n}u_{n}}\\
1&1&1&\dots&1\end{pmatrix}

has a non-vanishing determinant,

(3.3)

\det\mathrm{D}\Phi(u)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\prod_{i\not=\ell}\frac{1}{k_{i}u_{i}}>0.

Lemma 3.1
.

The map
\Phi:\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathcal{D}
is a
C^{\infty}
-diffeomorphism.

Proof.

By definition,
\Phi\in C^{\infty}
, and by the implicit function theorem
and (
3.3
),
the map
\Phi
is locally invertible with smooth inverse.
To show that
\Phi
is a bijection from
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
to
\mathcal{D}
,
we observe that the equation

\Phi(u)=w
for
u\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
and
w\in\mathcal{D}
is equivalent to

(3.4)

\displaystyle u_{j}=\exp(k_{j}w_{j}){u_{n}}^{k_{j}/k_{n}},\ j=1,\dots,n^{\prime},\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}=w_{n}.

Inserting the first relation into the second one, we find that

(3.5)

\displaystyle g_{w^{\prime}}(u_{n}):=u_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\exp(k_{j}w_{j}){u_{n}}^{k_{j}/k_{n}}=w_{n}.

For fixed
w=(w^{\prime},w_{n})\in\mathcal{D}
, the function
g_{w^{\prime}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is strictly increasing and continuous with

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}g_{w^{\prime}}(s)=0
and
\lim_{s\uparrow+\infty}g_{w^{\prime}}(s)=+\infty
. Hence, there exists a unique solution
u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
of the equation
g_{w^{\prime}}(u_{n})=w_{n}
, which in turn uniquely determines
u_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
, by means of the first condition in (
3.4
).
∎

3.2.
PDE system in new variables

We now formulate our original equation
in the new variables
w=\Phi(u)
. We recall that the original PDE system takes the form

(3.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{i}=\operatorname{div}(k_{i}u_{i}\nabla w_{n}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

and let
\Psi(w)=u
denote the inverse of
\Phi
.
Summing up the
n
equations (
3.6
) leads to

(3.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n}=\operatorname{div}(a(w)\nabla w_{n}),

where
a(w):=\widehat{a}(\Psi(w))
with

\widehat{a}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}u_{i}
. For
u=\Psi(w)
, we have

\widehat{a}(u)\geq(\min_{i}k_{i})\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}=(\min_{i}k_{i})w_{n}
.
Since
\Psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{D};\widehat{\mathcal{D}})
and
\inf_{w\in\mathcal{K}}w_{n}>0
for every
\mathcal{K}\Subset\mathcal{D}
, we find that
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{D};\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Next, let
i=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
. We deduce from (
3.2
)
and (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{u_{\ell}}\Phi_{i}(u)\partial_{t}u_{\ell}=\frac{\partial_{t}u_{i}}{k_{i}u_{i}}-\frac{\partial_{t}u_{n}}{k_{n}u_{n}}=\frac{1}{u_{i}}\operatorname{div}(u_{i}\nabla w_{n})-\frac{1}{u_{n}}\operatorname{div}(u_{n}\nabla w_{n})

\displaystyle=\nabla(\log\Psi_{i}-\log\Psi_{n})\cdot\nabla w_{n}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{w_{\ell}}(\log\Psi_{i}-\log\Psi_{n})\nabla w_{\ell}\cdot\nabla w_{n}.

To determine the term
\partial_{w_{\ell}}(\log\Psi_{i}-\log\Psi_{n})
,
we use relation (
3.5
),

\partial_{w_{n}}u_{n}=\partial_{w_{n}}\bigg(w_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\exp(k_{j}w_{j})u_{n}^{k_{j}/k_{n}}\bigg)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{k_{j}u_{j}}{k_{n}u_{n}}\partial_{w_{n}}u_{n},

and solve this identity for
\partial_{w_{n}}u_{n}
:

(\partial_{w_{n}}\Psi_{n})(w)=\partial_{w_{n}}u_{n}=\bigg(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{k_{j}u_{j}}{k_{n}u_{n}}\bigg)^{-1}=\frac{k_{n}u_{n}}{\widehat{a}(u)}.

In a similar way, it follows for
\ell=1,\ldots,n^{\prime}
that

\partial_{w_{\ell}}u_{n}=-k_{\ell}u_{\ell}-\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{k_{j}u_{j}}{k_{n}u_{n}}\partial_{w_{\ell}}u_{n}

and consequently, after solving for
\partial_{w_{\ell}}u_{n}
,

(\partial_{w_{\ell}}\Psi_{n})(w)=\partial_{w_{\ell}}u_{n}=-k_{\ell}u_{\ell}\bigg(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{k_{j}u_{j}}{k_{n}u_{n}}\bigg)^{-1}=-\frac{k_{n}u_{n}}{\widehat{a}(u)}k_{\ell}u_{\ell}.

Hence,

(\partial_{w_{n}}\log\Psi_{n})(\Phi(u))=\frac{k_{n}}{\widehat{a}(u)},\quad(\partial_{w_{\ell}}\log\Psi_{n})(\Phi(u))=-\frac{k_{n}}{\widehat{a}(u)}k_{\ell}u_{\ell}\text{ for }\ell=1,\dots,n^{\prime}.

Next, we differentiate

w_{i}=\log(u_{i}^{1/k_{i}}/u_{n}^{1/k_{n}})=(1/k_{i})\log\Psi_{i}-(1/k_{n})\log\Psi_{n}
,
i=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
, with respect to
w_{\ell}
,

\ell=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
, which gives
\delta_{i\ell}=(1/k_{i})\partial_{w_{\ell}}\log\Psi_{i}-(1/k_{n})\partial_{w_{\ell}}\log\Psi_{n}
. This shows that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{w_{\ell}}\log\Psi_{i}-\partial_{w_{\ell}}\log\Psi_{n}=k_{i}\delta_{i\ell}+\bigg(\frac{k_{i}}{k_{n}}-1\bigg)\partial_{w_{\ell}}\log\Psi_{n}=k_{i}\delta_{i\ell}+\big(k_{n}-k_{i}\big)\frac{k_{\ell}u_{\ell}}{\widehat{a}(u)},

Similarly, differentiating
w_{i}=(1/k_{i})\log\Psi_{i}-(1/k_{n})\log\Psi_{n}
,

i=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
, with respect to
w_{n}
, we obtain

0=(1/k_{i})\partial_{w_{n}}\log\Psi_{i}-(1/k_{n})\partial_{w_{n}}\log\Psi_{n}

or, equivalently,
\partial_{w_{n}}\log\Psi_{i}=k_{i}/\widehat{a}(u)
. Thus,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{w_{n}}\log\Psi_{i}-\partial_{w_{n}}\log\Psi_{n}=\frac{k_{i}-k_{n}}{\widehat{a}(u)}.

In summary, the components
w^{\prime}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n^{\prime}})
satisfy the system

(3.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\prime}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{n}\mathbb{Y}(w)\partial_{x_{\nu}}w^{\prime}+Y_{n}(w)|\nabla w_{n}|^{2},

where
\mathbb{Y}=(\mathbb{Y}_{i\ell})_{i,\ell=1}^{n^{\prime}}
,
Y_{n}=(Y_{ni})_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}
, and

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}_{i\ell}(w)

\displaystyle:=k_{i}\delta_{i\ell}+\big(k_{n}-k_{i}\big)\frac{k_{\ell}u_{\ell}}{\widehat{a}(u)},\quad

\displaystyle i,\ell=1,\dots,n^{\prime},

(3.12)

\displaystyle Y_{ni}(w)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{Y}_{in}(w):=\frac{k_{i}-k_{n}}{\widehat{a}(u)},\quad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n^{\prime}.

3.3.
Symmetriser for the first-order subsystem

We look for a matrix
\mathbb{A}_{0}(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{sym}}

which is
w
-locally uniformly bounded and positive definite,
such that
\mathbb{A}_{0}(w)\mathbb{Y}(w)
is symmetric for all
w
.
The ansatz of a diagonal symmetrising matrix

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}(w)=\operatorname{diag}(X_{1}(w),\dots,X_{n^{\prime}}(w))

leads to the conditions

X_{i}\mathbb{Y}_{ij}=X_{j}\mathbb{Y}_{ji},\quad i,j=1,\dots,n^{\prime},

for the unknown smooth and positive functions
X_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
.
Since, by hypothesis,
0<k_{1}\leq\ldots\leq k_{n^{\prime}}<k_{n}
, an admissible choice of
X_{i}
is

(3.14)

\displaystyle X_{i}=\frac{k_{i}u_{i}}{k_{n}-k_{i}}.

Thus, (
3.13
), (
3.14
) defines an admissible symmetriser for system (
3.10
).
In view of Lemma
3.1
,
this completes the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

4.
Local classical solutions

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.3
and deduce Theorem
2.4
as a corollary.
Thanks to its symmetry, the hyperbolic part can be treated following a well-established procedure based on
H^{s}
energy estimates at the linear approximate level and a Picard iteration, cf.
[
1
,
22
]
and the references therein. Hence, our main task is to properly take care of the coupling between the hyperbolic and the parabolic problem.

We only present the proof for integer
s
.
Using basic tools from Fourier analysis, the extension to fractional
s>d/2+1
should be straightforward. For the most part, we avoid using the explicit structure obtained in Section
3
. In this way, we only require
a few minor changes to treat the Cauchy problem for the general rank-
\mathsf{r}
system (

B

) once brought into a normal form.

4.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{A}_{0}
be a smooth symmetriser for (
2.1a
). We will construct a strong solution (within the appropriate regularity class) to the symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic system

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}(w)\partial_{t}w^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\nabla w_{n}\cdot\mathbb{A}_{1}(w)\nabla w^{\prime}+V_{n}(w)|\nabla w_{n}|^{2},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}(a(w)\nabla w_{n}),

with
w|_{t=0}=w^{\mathrm{in}}
,
where
\mathbb{A}_{1}(w)=\mathbb{A}_{0}(w)\mathbb{Y}(w)
and
V_{n}(w)=\mathbb{A}_{0}(w)Y_{n}(w)
.

By hypothesis, we can define positive constants

\displaystyle r:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}w^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad R:=\|w^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{H^{s}}.

As an immediate consequence, this gives

\displaystyle\|w^{\mathrm{in}}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq LR\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,n,

where
L<\infty
denotes the constant from the Sobolev embedding
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Defining
a_{1}:=(r/2)\min_{i}k_{i}>0
, we infer the bound
a(v)\geq a_{1}
for all

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{D}_{0}:=\big\{\widetilde{v}\in\mathcal{D}:\widetilde{v}_{n}>r/2\text{ and }|\widetilde{v}_{i}|<2LR,\ i=1,\dots,n\big\}.

We further choose
\lambda_{1}\in(0,1]
and
\Lambda_{1}\geq 1
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}\mathbb{I}_{n^{\prime}}\geq\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)\geq\lambda_{1}\mathbb{I}_{n^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{D}_{0},

and abbreviate

(4.3)

\displaystyle K:=2\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}}}.

Note that
a_{1},\lambda_{1},\Lambda_{1}
and
K
depend on
w^{\mathrm{in}}
through
r
and
R
only.

To avoid regularity issues during the iteration process, we mollify the initial data.
Let
(\eta_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be an approximate identity, that is,
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta_{\ell}\mathrm{d}x=1
,

\|\eta_{\ell}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim 1
and
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{\{|x|>\delta\}}|\eta_{\ell}|\mathrm{d}x=0
for all
0<\delta\ll 1
. Here,
a^{\ell}\lesssim b^{\ell}
means
that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a^{\ell}\leq Cb^{\ell}
for all

\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We choose
(\eta_{\ell})
such that
\eta_{\ell}\geq 0
for all
\ell
and
introduce the mollified initial datum

z^{\ell}:=\eta_{\ell}\ast w^{\mathrm{in}}
for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
After possibly passing to a subsequence of
(\eta_{\ell})
, we may assume that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\|z^{\ell+1}-z^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim 2^{-\ell}R.

The construction by convolution immediately yields
z^{\ell}_{n}\geq r
and
|z^{\ell}_{i}|\leq LR
,
i=1,\dots,n
and thus,

z^{\ell}(x)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Moreover,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\|z^{\ell}\|_{H^{s}}\leq R\quad\text{ for all }\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Before starting with our analysis,
let us recall the following classical inequalities from
calculus
[
18
,
23
]
:
For all
\varphi,\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and any multi-index

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq\sigma
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\alpha}(\psi\varphi)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\varphi\|_{H^{\sigma}}+\|\psi\|_{H^{\sigma}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}},

\displaystyle\|[\partial^{\alpha},\psi]\varphi\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\varphi\|_{H^{\sigma-1}}+\|\nabla\psi\|_{H^{\sigma-1}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}},

where
[A,B]:=AB-BA
denotes the commutator of two linear operators
A
and
B
.
Furthermore, for
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
,
\mathcal{K}\Subset\mathcal{D}
, and

\varphi\in H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathcal{K})
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\|g(\varphi)\|_{H^{\sigma}}\lesssim\|\varphi\|_{H^{\sigma}}+1,

where the constant associated to this inequality depends on

\|g\|_{C^{\sigma}(\mathcal{K})}
and
\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
Finally, let us introduce the Banach space

X^{\sigma}_{t}:=C([0,t];H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

with the norm

(4.8)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{X^{\sigma}_{t}}:=\sup_{\tau\in(0,t)}\|v(\tau)\|_{H^{\sigma}}.

4.2.
Iteration scheme

We initialise
w^{0}:=z^{0}
and
t_{0}=\infty
, and consider the following iteration scheme.
Given

,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, with

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)\partial_{t}w^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\nabla v_{n}\cdot\mathbb{A}_{1}(v)\nabla w^{\prime}+V_{n}(v)|\nabla v_{n}|^{2},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n}

(Control of values)

There exists a time

\displaystyle w^{\ell}(t,x)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}\quad\text{for all }(t,x)\in(0,\widehat{t}_{\ell})\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\mbox{ where }\widehat{t}_{\ell}:=\min\{t_{\ell},T_{1}\}.

.
Let now
\ell\geq 1

for all

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}(w^{\ell})^{\prime}\|_{X_{\tau}^{s-1}}

Remark 4.3

We infer immediately from Lemma

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}(w^{\ell})^{\prime}\|_{X_{T_{*}}^{s-1}}\leq C,\quad\|\partial_{t}w_{n}^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{*};H^{s-1})}\leq C({T_{*}}),

Proof of Lemma

Let

\partial^{\alpha}w_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\partial^{\alpha}w_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+a_{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\partial^{\alpha}\nabla w_{n}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\|[\partial^{\alpha},a(v)]\nabla w_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial^{\alpha}\nabla w_{n}\|_{L^{2}},

and hence,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\partial^{\alpha}w_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{a_{1}}{2}\|\partial^{\alpha}\nabla w_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(a_{1})\|[\partial^{\alpha},a(v)]\nabla w_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Upon taking the sum over
\alpha
,
0\leq|\alpha|\leq s
, integrating in time and using inequalities (
4.6
)–(
4.7
) and the Sobolev embedding
H^{s-1}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
, we arrive at

(4.20)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|w_{n}\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+a_{1}\|\nabla w_{n}\|^{2}_{H^{s}}\leq R^{2}C(R,r^{-1})\|w_{n}\|_{H^{s}}^{2}.

Gronwall’s lemma implies that

(4.21)

\displaystyle\|w_{n}(t)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+a_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla w_{n}\|^{2}_{H^{s}}\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\|w_{n}(0)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\exp(tC(R,r^{-1}))

for all
t\in(0,\widehat{t}_{\ell-1})
.
The rough bound (
4.16
) is a consequence of (
4.21
) and the initial condition

w_{|t=0}=z^{\ell}
, combined with the control (
4.5
) and the fact that the above argument holds for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Re
(ii)
:

In the following, we abbreviate
w_{\alpha}^{\prime}:=\partial^{\alpha}w^{\prime}
. We first left-multiply (
4.9a
)
by the matrix
\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)^{-1}
, differentiate the resulting identity by
\partial^{\alpha}
and subsequently left-multiply by
\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)
. This gives

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)\partial_{t}w^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\nabla v_{n}\cdot\mathbb{A}_{1}(v)\nabla w^{\prime}_{\alpha}+\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)\mathcal{R}_{\alpha},\quad\mbox{where}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}:=\big[\partial^{\alpha},\nabla v_{n}\cdot\mathbb{Y}(v)\big]\nabla w^{\prime}+\partial^{\alpha}\big(Y_{n}(v)|\nabla v_{n}|^{2}\big).

Next, we test this equation with
w_{\alpha}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(w_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)w^{\prime}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(w_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\mathrm{D}\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)\partial_{t}v\big)w_{\alpha}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla v_{n}\cdot\big\{\nabla\big((w_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{1}(v)w_{\alpha}^{\prime}\big)-(w_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\mathrm{D}\mathbb{A}_{1}(v)\nabla v\big)w_{\alpha}^{\prime}\big\}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(w_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}x.

Hence, after a rearrangement and an integration by parts in the second step,

(4.24)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(w_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(v){w}^{\prime}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla v_{n}\cdot\nabla\big((w^{\prime}_{\alpha})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{1}(v)w^{\prime}_{\alpha}\big)\mathrm{d}x

(4.25)

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\big(|\nabla v_{n}||\mathrm{D}\mathbb{A}_{1}(v)\nabla v|+|\mathrm{D}\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)\partial_{t}v|\big)|w^{\prime}_{\alpha}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}||{w}^{\prime}_{\alpha}|\mathrm{d}x

(4.26)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big\||\Delta v_{n}|+|\nabla v|^{2}+|\partial_{t}v|\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w^{\prime}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}}\|w^{\prime}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}},

where the constant associated to the last inequality depends on
R
and
r^{-1}
.

To proceed, we estimate the remainder term
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}
using again
inequalities (
4.6
)–(
4.7
),
the bound
\sup_{t<t_{\ell-1}}\|v\|_{X_{t}^{s}}\leq KR
and the embedding

H^{s-1}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\|\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\Big(\|\nabla(\nabla v_{n}\cdot\mathbb{Y}(v))\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla w^{\prime}\|_{H^{|\alpha|-1}}

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\|\nabla(\nabla v_{n}\cdot\mathbb{Y}(v))\|_{H^{|\alpha|-1}}\|\nabla w^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\partial^{\alpha}\big(Y_{n}(v)|\nabla v_{n}|^{2}\big)\|_{L^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{H^{s}}+1\big)\big(\|\nabla{w}^{\prime}\|_{H^{s-1}}+1\big).

To estimate the time derivative
\partial_{t}v
, we use the equation for
v=w^{\ell-1}
;
i.e. (
4.9
) with
\ell
replaced by
\ell-1
supposing that
\ell\geq 2

(if
\ell=1
, then
\partial_{t}v\equiv 0
and there is nothing to show). This gives

(4.27)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}v\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\partial_{t}v_{n}\big\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\partial_{t}v^{\prime}\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\Delta v_{n}\|_{H^{s-1}}+1\lesssim\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{H^{s}}+1.

In combination, we infer for
\nu(t):=\|\nabla v_{n}(t)\|_{H^{s}}{+}1

and
C_{1}=C_{1}(R,r^{-1})
that

(4.28)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}({w_{\alpha}}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(v){w}^{\prime}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{1}\nu(t)\|{w}^{\prime}(t)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+C_{1}\nu(t),

where we used the elementary estimate
\rho\lesssim\rho^{2}+1
.

Taking into account the equivalence
(cf. (
4.2
) and Lemma
4.1
)

(4.29)

\displaystyle\|w^{\prime}\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\sim\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(w_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(v)w^{\prime}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}x

with associated constants
\lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{1}
,
and Gronwall’s inequality, we deduce a bound of the form

(4.30)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\|w^{\prime}(t)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\leq\big(\Lambda_{1}\|w^{\prime}(0)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\sqrt{t}\beta\big)\exp(\sqrt{t}\beta),\quad\mbox{where}

(4.31)

\displaystyle\beta:=\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg)^{1/2}C_{1}+\sqrt{t}C_{1}.

Recalling (
4.16
), adding inequalities (
4.21
) and (
4.30
),
inserting the initial value
w_{|t=0}=z^{\ell}

and recalling definition (
4.3
) of
K
, we infer the bound

(4.32)

\displaystyle\|w(t)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\leq 2\frac{\Lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}}R^{2}<(KR)^{2}

for all
t\in[0,T_{*}]
, provided that
T_{*}\in(0,\widehat{t}_{\ell-1})

(depending on
R
and

) is small enough.
This implies that
\widehat{t}_{\ell}>T_{*}
, and inductively we
infer
(ii)
.
∎

4.4.
Convergence

We show that the approximate solutions converge to a strong solution of
system (
2.1
) as
\ell\to\infty
.

Lemma 4.4

(Convergence)
.

There exists a solution
w=(w^{\prime},w_{n}):[0,{T_{*}}]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathcal{D}_{0}

to system (
2.1
) in
(0,{T_{*}})\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
satisfying

w_{|t=0}=w^{\mathrm{in}}
and the regularity

(4.33a)

\displaystyle w\in L^{\infty}(0,{T_{*}};H^{s})\cap C_{w}([0,{T_{*}}];H^{s}),\quad w_{n}\in L^{2}(0,{T_{*}};H^{s+1}),

(4.33b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n}\in L^{2}(0,{T_{*}};H^{s-1}),\quad\partial_{t}w^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(0,{T_{*}};H^{s-1})

such that, as
\ell\to\infty
,

(4.34a)

\displaystyle w^{\ell}\to w\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }X_{{T_{*}}}^{\sigma}\text{ for every }\sigma<s,

(4.34b)

\displaystyle w^{\ell}(t)\rightharpoonup w(t)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }H^{s}\text{ uniformly in }t\in[0,{T_{*}}],

(4.34c)

\displaystyle w^{\ell}_{n}\rightharpoonup w_{n}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,{T_{*}};H^{s+1}),

(4.34d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\ell}_{n}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}w_{n}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,{T_{*}};H^{s-1}),

(4.34e)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(w^{\ell})^{\prime}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\partial_{t}w^{\prime}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,{T_{*}};H^{s-1}).

As a consequence,

(4.35a)

\displaystyle w_{n}\in C([0,{T_{*}}];H^{s}),\quad w^{\prime}\in C^{1}([0,{T_{*}}]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}),

(4.35b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n},\,\nabla^{2}w_{n}\in L^{2}(0,{T_{*}};C(\mathbb{T}^{d})).

Proof.

We split the proof into several steps.

Step 1.
We assert that there exists a function

p:\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
of at most polynomial growth
such that for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,

(4.36)

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}^{\ell+1}_{{T_{*}}}\leq 2^{-\ell}p(\ell)+2^{-1}\mathsf{N}^{\ell}_{{T_{*}}},\quad\mbox{where}

(4.37)

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}^{\ell}_{{T_{*}}}:=\sup_{t\in(0,{T_{*}})}\|w^{\ell}(t){-}w^{\ell-1}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla(w^{\ell}_{n}{-}w^{\ell-1}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{*};L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}.

This estimate is the key point of the proof.
To verify the assertion, we subtract the equations for two subsequent iterates,
yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}(w^{\ell})\partial_{t}(w^{\ell+1}-w^{\ell})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\nabla w^{\ell}_{n}\cdot\mathbb{A}_{1}(w^{\ell})\nabla(w^{\ell+1}-w^{\ell})^{\prime}+F_{\ell},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(w_{n}^{\ell+1}-w_{n}^{\ell})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}\big(a(w^{\ell})\nabla(w_{n}^{\ell+1}-w_{n}^{\ell})\big)+G_{\ell},

where

\displaystyle F_{\ell}

\displaystyle=V_{n}(w^{\ell})|\nabla w^{\ell}_{n}|^{2}-V_{n}(w^{\ell-1})|\nabla w^{\ell-1}_{n}|^{2}\big(\mathbb{A}_{0}(w^{\ell})-\mathbb{A}_{0}(w^{\ell-1})\big)\partial_{t}(w^{\ell})^{\prime}

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\big(\nabla w^{\ell}_{n}\cdot\mathbb{A}_{1}(w^{\ell})-\nabla w^{\ell-1}_{n}\cdot\mathbb{A}_{1}(w^{\ell-1})\big)\nabla(w^{\ell})^{\prime},

\displaystyle G_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}\big((a(w^{\ell})-a(w^{\ell-1}))\nabla w_{n}^{\ell}\big).

Energy estimates similar to those in the proof of Lemma
4.2

yield the following stability estimates for the hyperbolic and the parabolic
components, respectively:

(4.38)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{A}_{0}(w^{\ell})^{1/2}(w^{\ell+1}{-}w^{\ell})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(4.39)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\|\partial_{t}w_{n}^{\ell}\|_{H^{s-1}}+\|\Delta w_{n}^{\ell}\|_{H^{s-1}}\big)\|(w^{\ell+1}{-}w^{\ell})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(4.40)

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\big(\|w^{\ell}{-}w^{\ell-1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla(w^{\ell}_{n}{-}w^{\ell-1}_{n})\|_{L^{2}}\big)\|(w^{\ell+1}{-}w^{\ell})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}},

(4.41)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\|w_{n}^{\ell+1}-w_{n}^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{a_{1}}{2}\|\nabla(w_{n}^{\ell+1}-w_{n}^{\ell})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|w^{\ell}-w^{\ell-1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

with associated constants depending on
R
and
r^{-1}
.
Let us now define the quantity

(4.42)

\displaystyle Q_{\ell}(\varphi,\psi)=\|\mathbb{A}_{0}(w^{\ell})^{1/2}(\varphi-\psi)^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\varphi_{n}-\psi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

It satisfies
Q_{\ell}(\varphi,\psi)\sim\|\varphi-\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
for all
\ell
.

We add (
4.38
) and (
4.41
) and use Young’s inequality to
find for any
\delta\in(0,1]
that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle Q_{\ell}(w^{\ell+1},w^{\ell})+a_{1}\|\nabla(w_{n}^{\ell+1}-w_{n}^{\ell})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big(C_{\delta}+C\|\partial_{t}w_{n}^{\ell}\|_{H^{s-1}}+C\|\Delta w_{n}^{\ell}\|_{H^{s-1}}\big)Q_{\ell}(w^{\ell+1},w^{\ell})

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+C\,Q_{\ell-1}(w^{\ell},w^{\ell-1})+\delta\|\nabla(w_{n}^{\ell}-w_{n}^{\ell-1})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Invoking the Gronwall lemma, inserting the bounds (
4.16
)–(
4.17
)
and applying (
4.2
), we conclude that

(4.43)

\displaystyle\mathsf{n}^{\ell+1}(t)\leq C_{T_{*}}\exp(C_{\delta}t)\big(\|w^{\ell+1}(0){-}w^{\ell}(0)\|_{L^{2}}+\delta\mathsf{n}^{\ell}(t)\big),\quad\mbox{where}

(4.44)

\displaystyle\mathsf{n}^{\ell}(t):=\|w^{\ell}(t)-w^{\ell-1}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla(w_{n}^{\ell}-w_{n}^{\ell-1})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg)^{1/2}

for all
t\in[0,\delta]
and any
\delta\in(0,T_{*}]
, where

C_{T_{*}}=C(\sqrt{T_{*}F(T_{*})})
with a function
F
as in (
4.16
).

Let us now fix
\delta=\min\{1/(4C_{T_{*}}),T_{*}\}
.
By construction,
\|w^{\ell+1}(0){-}w^{\ell}(0)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim 2^{-\ell}R

(cf. (
4.4
)). If we choose

{t_{*}}={t_{*}}(\delta,R,r^{-1})\in(0,\delta]
so small that

\exp(C_{\delta}{t_{*}})\leq 2
, we deduce an estimate of the form (
4.36
) with
T_{*}
replaced by
t_{*}
and with
p(\ell)\equiv c=\mathrm{const.}

It follows by recursion that

\mathsf{N}^{\ell+1}_{{t_{*}}}\leq 2^{-\ell}c\ell+2^{-\ell}\mathsf{N}^{1}_{{t_{*}}}
and as a consequence

\|w^{\ell+1}({t_{*}}){-}w^{\ell}({t_{*}})\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-\ell}C\ell
.
Using this decay property in
\ell
at the new initial time
t_{*}
, we can repeat
the above argument on the interval
[t_{*},2t_{*}]
and obtain an estimate of the
form (
4.36
) with
T_{*}
replaced by
2t_{*}
and with

p(\ell)\equiv C\ell
.
Iterating for a total number of
i:=\lfloor T_{*}/t_{*}\rfloor
times,
we infer (
4.36
) with the time
T_{*}
and

p(\ell)\equiv C\ell^{i}
.

Step 2:
Inequality (
4.36
) implies that

\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathsf{N}^{\ell}_{{T_{*}}}<\infty
.
As a consequence, the sequence
(w^{\ell+1}{-}w^{\ell})_{\ell}\subset X^{0}_{{T_{*}}}

is summable, and by completeness, there exists
w\in X^{0}_{{T_{*}}}
such that

w^{\ell}\to w
in
X^{0}_{{T_{*}}}
as
\ell\to\infty
.
The uniform bounds in Lemma
4.2
and Remark
4.3
,
combined with classical compactness and interpolation arguments, further yield the
convergence (
4.34
) as well as the regularity (
4.33
).
We omit the details, since an exposition of such arguments in a similar context
has been provided, e.g., in
[
1
, Chapter 10.1.1]

and
[
22
, p. 39–40]
.

Step 3:
We assert that the limit
w
further has the
regularity (
4.35
) and is a strong solution.
Indeed, the regularity (
4.35a
) is an immediate consequence
of (
4.33b
).
Next, the convergence (
4.34a
) allows us to pass to the limit

\ell\to\infty
in equation (

) (where
v=w^{\ell-1}
and

),
giving in particular

\partial_{t}w^{\prime}\in C([0,{T_{*}}]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
and showing that

is a classical solution to (
4.9a
).
Finally, the convergences (
4.34a
), (
4.34c
) and (
4.34d
)
imply that equation (
4.9b
) is fulfilled in the strong sense, and
the regularity (
4.35b
) follows from the embedding

H^{s-1}\hookrightarrow C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
∎

4.5.
Regularity

To deduce the temporal continuity of
w^{\prime}
with values in
H^{s}
, we need some basic uniqueness properties.

Lemma 4.5

(Uniqueness)
.

Let
T>0
and
s>d/2+1
. Then the following holds:

(i)

For a given initial value
w^{\mathrm{in}}
, there exists at most one
strong solution
w
of system (
4.1
) in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}

satisfying the regularity (
4.33
) (with
{T_{*}}
replaced by
T
),
the initial condition
w|_{t=0}=w^{\mathrm{in}}
and
\min_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}w_{n}>0
.

(ii)

For fixed strictly positive
w_{n}
satisfying the regularity (
4.33b
)
and a given initial condition
(w^{\mathrm{in}})^{\prime}
, the hyperbolic subsystem (
4.9a
)
with the coefficient function
w_{n}
has at most one classical solution
w^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The assertions can be deduced from energy estimates similar to those in the proof
of Lemma
4.4
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

The solution
w
constructed in Lemma
4.4
satisfies

w^{\prime}\in C([0,{T_{*}}];H^{s})
.

Proof.

In the proof we closely follow
[
22
, Theorem 2.1 (b)]
.
Since we already know the weak continuity
w^{\prime}\in C_{w}([0,{T_{*}}];H^{s})
,
it is sufficient to show the
continuity of the norm in the Hilbert space
H^{s}
.
We first show the continuity at
t=0
.
Equip
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}})
with the equivalent norm

(4.45)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{{\widehat{H}^{s}}}:=\bigg(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{\alpha}v^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(w|_{t=0})\partial_{\alpha}v\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{1/2}.

It then suffices to show that

(4.46)

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\|w^{\prime}(t)\|_{{\widehat{H}^{s}}}\leq\|w^{\prime}(0)\|_{{\widehat{H}^{s}}}.

To prove this inequality, we recall estimate (
4.28
), valid for
w=w^{\ell}
and
v=w^{\ell-1}
. Thanks to the uniform bounds in Lemma
4.2
,
the right-hand side of (
4.28
) can be estimated above by an
\ell
-independent function
f\in L^{2}([0,{T_{*}}];[0,\infty))
so that

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{\alpha}(w^{\ell})^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(w^{\ell-1}(t))\partial_{\alpha}(w^{\ell})^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{\alpha}((z^{\ell})^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(z^{\ell-1})\partial_{\alpha}(z^{\ell})^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau

for all
t\in[0,{T_{*}}]
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

It follows from the convergence properties in Section
4.4
and a weak lower semi-continuity argument that, in the limit
\ell\to\infty
,

(4.47)

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{\alpha}w^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(w(t))\partial_{\alpha}w^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}x

(4.48)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{\alpha}w^{\prime}(0)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}_{0}(w(0))\partial_{\alpha}w^{\prime}(0)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau.

Recalling the weak continuity in (
4.33a
) and taking the
\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}
, we
find (
4.46
), where we used the fact that

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\|\mathbb{A}_{0}(w(0))-\mathbb{A}_{0}(w(t))\|_{C(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=0
to recover the

{\widehat{H}^{s}}
-norm on the left-hand side of (
4.46
).

The right-continuity of
w^{\prime}
with values in
H^{s}
at general
\widehat{t}\in[0,{T_{*}})

follows by applying the above result to the time-shifted problem with initial condition
w(\widehat{t})\in H^{s}
and exploiting the fact that

\tau\mapsto w(\widehat{t}+\tau)
is the unique solution emanating from

w(\widehat{t})
.

It remains to show the left-continuity of
w^{\prime}
. To this end, we consider the hyperbolic subsystem with the fixed coefficient function
w_{n}
and apply the above argument to the time-reversed problem.
More precisely, for establishing an analogue of the crucial
H^{s}
energy estimate (
4.47
), one possibility is to use a Picard iteration for the hyperbolic subsystem, while approximating the coefficient function
w_{n}
by the smooth functions
w_{n}^{\ell}
from Lemma
4.4
. The uniqueness property in Lemma
4.5

(ii)
then implies that the limiting function coincides with the time-reversed version of
w^{\prime}
.
∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
2.3
, it remains to show the regularity

(4.49)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{n},\nabla^{2}w_{n}\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,{T_{*}}]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}).

To this end, let
\beta\in(0,\min\{s-d/2-1,1\})
.
The regularity
w^{\prime}\in C([0,{T_{*}}];H^{s})
and the Sobolev embedding imply that
w^{\prime}\in C([0,{T_{*}}];C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Combined with the space-uniform temporal Lipschitz regularity of
w^{\prime}
,
i.e.
\partial_{t}w^{\prime}\in C([0,{T_{*}}]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we infer
from
[
19
, Chapter II, Lemma 3.1]
a temporal Hölder regularity
of the gradient:

(4.50)

\displaystyle\nabla w^{\prime}\in C^{\beta/(1+\beta)}([0,{T_{*}}];C(\mathbb{T}^{d})).

Now, we can use classical regularity results for quasilinear parabolic equations in divergence form, where
w^{\prime}
is viewed as a given function: Thanks to (
4.50
),
\nabla w^{\prime}
satisfies a space-time Hölder condition, which makes Theorem 5.4 in
[
19
, Chapter V]
accessible and gives us an interior space-time Hölder a priori estimate for
\partial_{t}w_{n}
and
\nabla^{2}w_{n}
.
To conclude (
4.49
) from the a priori control, we approximate
w^{\prime}
by smooth functions whose spatial gradient is uniformly bounded in some space-time Hölder norm, and exploit the uniqueness of regular solutions to the parabolic equation in
w_{n}
(with
w^{\prime}
acting as a fixed parameter). As a consequence,

\partial_{t}w_{n}
and
\nabla^{2}w_{n}
satisfy a space-time Hölder condition
away from
t=0
, which entails (
4.49
).

4.6.
Original variables

We now conclude the existence of classical solutions for the degenerate cross-diffusion system (
Rk1
).

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Without loss of generality, after rescaling time, relabelling components and rescaling
u_{i}\mapsto a_{i}u_{i}
, we can assume that
k_{1}\leq\ldots\leq k_{n^{\prime}}\leq k_{n}=1
and
a_{i}=1
for all
i
.
It then suffices to consider the following two cases.

Case 1:
Let
k_{1}\leq\ldots\leq k_{n^{\prime}}<k_{n}
.
Then the assertion is a consequence of Theorems
2.2
and

2.3
.
The time of existence
T
can be bounded below by a positive constant that depends on the datum
u^{\mathrm{in}}
only through
\|w^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{H^{s}}
and
\min_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}w^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}
, where
w^{\mathrm{in}}=\Phi(u^{\mathrm{in}})
with
\Phi
denoting a diffeomorphism as in Theorem
2.2
with the property that

w_{n}=\Phi_{n}(u)=\mathfrak{p}(u)
.

Case 2:
There exists a minimal
m\in\{1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}
such that
k_{i}=1

for
i=m,\dots,n
. In this case, we define
\widetilde{u}_{i}:=u_{i}
for

1\leq i\leq m-1
and
\widetilde{u}_{m}:=u_{m}+\dots+u_{n}
.
The system formulated in terms of
\widetilde{u}
satisfies the hypotheses of
Case 1 with
n
replaced by
m
, which provides a local strong solution
\widetilde{u}
and in particular a velocity field

{\bf\widetilde{v}}=-\nabla\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{u}_{j}
.
Subsequently, we determine the unique solutions to the linear continuity equations
for
u_{m},\dots,u_{n}
with the fixed velocity field
{\bf\widetilde{v}}
,
so that

∎

5.
The general system (

B

) with incomplete diffusion

In this section, we turn to the general PDE system (

B

)
(see page

B

) for symmetric positive semidefinite matrices
B=(b_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with rank
\mathsf{r}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
In particular, we aim to bring equations (

B

) into a normal form that makes them accessible
to the energy methods from Section
4
.

In the following, we use the notation

D(v):=\operatorname{diag}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

for
v=(v_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Moreover, we use the convention
f(v):=(f(v_{1}),\dots,f(v_{n}))^{\mathsf{T}}
for
a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

5.1.
Preliminary consideration: the full-rank case

We first note that for symmetric strongly parabolic PDE systems, local strong solutions to the Cauchy problem in suitable function spaces can be obtained by means of basic energy estimates in the spirit of Section
4
. More generally, initial-boundary value problems may for instance be treated using Schauder-type estimates as in
[
10
]
.

System (

B

) can be symmetrised in several ways.
For instance, following Kawashima and Shizuta
[
17
]
, we may consider a change to entropy variables
\widetilde{v}_{i}=\log u_{i}
for the strictly convex Shannon entropy. In this way, we obtain the symmetric system

\displaystyle D(e^{\widetilde{v}})\partial_{t}\widetilde{v}=\operatorname{div}\big(D(e^{\widetilde{v}})BD(e^{\widetilde{v}})\nabla\widetilde{v}\big),

which is strictly parabolic if and only if
\operatorname{rank}B=n
.
Likewise, we may use the quadratic Rao entropy
H(u)=\frac{1}{2}u^{\mathsf{T}}Bu
, which is strictly convex if
B
possesses full rank. In this case, we find
B^{-1}\partial_{t}v=\operatorname{div}(D(u)\nabla v),

u:=B^{-1}v
.
Of course, the classical inversion of
B
is only possible for
\operatorname{rank}B=n
.
Alternatively, equation (

B

) may be put in symmetric form directly by means of the symmetriser
B
giving

B\partial_{t}u=\operatorname{div}(BD(u)B\nabla u)
, where we used the fact that
B
is constant.

In order to find a symmetrisation suitable to conveniently treat the rank-deficient case, we use again the fact that
B
is constant, but somewhat modify the previous alternative.
To this end, denote by
\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{n}
an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
B
with the corresponding vector of positive eigenvalues

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and define
w_{k}:=\xi^{k}\cdot u

for
k=1,\ldots,n
. With
\mathsf{O}_{ij}=\xi^{i}_{j}
for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
,
we obtain
B\mathsf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathsf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}D(\lambda)
and
u=\mathsf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}w
. Hence, we can write
(

B

), i.e.
\partial_{t}u=\operatorname{div}(D(u)B\nabla u)
, in terms of
the variable
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
as

D(\lambda)\partial_{t}w=D(\lambda)\mathsf{O}\operatorname{div}(D(u)B\mathsf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla w)=\operatorname{div}\big(D(\lambda)\mathsf{O}D(u)\mathsf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}D(\lambda)\nabla w\big).

If
B
possesses rank
n
, these equations define a symmetric strongly parabolic system for
w
as long as
u
remains positive componentwise.
We further develop this approach in the next paragraph to derive a normal form
in the case
\operatorname{rank}B=\mathsf{r}
with
1\leq\mathsf{r}\leq n
.

5.2.
Normal form of symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic type

We suppose that
\operatorname{rank}B=\mathsf{r}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
.
To ease the notation, we partition the set of indices
\{1,\dots,n\}
into

{\mathrm{I}}=\{1,\dots,n-\mathsf{r}\}
and
{\mathrm{II}}=\{n-\mathsf{r}+1,\dots,n\}
.
We choose an orthonormal basis
\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{n}
of eigenvectors of
B
with corresponding eigenvalues

\lambda_{i}=0
for
i\in{\mathrm{I}}
and
\lambda_{i}>0
for
i\in{\mathrm{II}}
, and further introduce the orthogonal matrix
\mathsf{O}_{ij}=\xi^{i}_{j}
for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
and the rectangular blocks

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{ij}:=\xi^{i}_{j}\quad\text{for }i\in{\mathrm{I}},\quad\mathsf{P}_{ij}:=\xi^{i}_{j}\quad\text{for }i\in{\mathrm{II}},\ j=1,\dots,n.

For later reference, we note the elementary matrix identities

(5.2)

\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbb{I}_{n-\mathsf{r}},\quad\mathsf{P}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathsf{r}},\quad\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{Q}+\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{P}=\mathbb{I}_{n},\quad\mathsf{P}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}=0,\quad\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{T}}=0.

Left-multiplying system (

B

) by
(\xi^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}
,
k\in{\mathrm{II}}
,
the functions
w_{n-\mathsf{r}+1},

\dots,w_{n}
defined via

w_{k}:=\xi^{k}\cdot u
satisfy the parabolic system

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\partial_{t}w_{k}=\operatorname{div}\bigg(\sum_{\ell\in{\mathrm{II}}}\alpha_{k\ell}(u)\nabla w_{\ell}\bigg),\quad\text{where}

(5.4)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k\ell}(u)=D(u)(\lambda_{k}\xi^{k})\cdot(\lambda_{\ell}\xi^{\ell})\quad\text{ for all }k,\ell\in{\mathrm{II}}.

Moreover, multiplying (

B

) by
\xi^{k}_{i}/u_{i}
for
k\in{\mathrm{I}}
, summing over
i=1,\dots,n
and using the fact that
\xi^{k}
is in the kernel of
B
yields for
w_{k}:=\xi^{k}\cdot\log u
the first-order equation

(5.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{k}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}b_{ij}\nabla\log u_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{j}\quad\text{ for all }k\in{\mathrm{I}}.

This leads us to propose the change of variables
\Phi(u)=w
defined via

(5.6)

\displaystyle w_{k}:=\begin{cases}\xi^{k}\cdot\log u&\text{ for }k\in{\mathrm{I}},\\
\xi^{k}\cdot u&\text{ for }k\in{\mathrm{II}}.\end{cases}

With
w_{\mathrm{I}}:=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-\mathsf{r}})
and
w_{\mathrm{II}}:=(w_{n-\mathsf{r}+1},\ldots,w_{n})
, we then have
w_{\mathrm{I}}=\mathsf{Q}\log u
and
w_{\mathrm{II}}=\mathsf{P}u
.
We next prove a diffeomorphism property for this change of variables.

Lemma 5.1
.

The map
\Phi
is smoothly invertible between
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}:=\mathbb{R}^{n-\mathsf{r}}\times\mathsf{P}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.

Proof.

It follows from definition (
5.6
) and the identity

\log u_{i}=(\mathsf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{O}\log u)_{i}
that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\log u_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log u\cdot\xi^{k}\xi_{i}^{k}=\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{I}}}w_{k}\xi^{k}_{i}+\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{II}}}\log u\cdot\xi^{k}\xi^{k}_{i}\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n,

(5.8)

\displaystyle w_{\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{\ell}_{i}u_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{\ell}_{i}\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{I}}}w_{k}\xi^{k}_{i}+\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{II}}}\log u\cdot\xi^{k}\xi^{k}_{i}\Big)\quad\text{for }\ell\in{\mathrm{II}}.

We assert that for arbitrary
w\in\mathcal{D}
, the last line defines the
\mathsf{r}
components
\log u\cdot\xi^{j}
,
j\in{\mathrm{II}}
, implicitly as functions
X_{j}(w)
. Indeed, we can write the algebraic system (
5.8
) in the form
F(X;w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})=0
, where

\displaystyle F_{\ell}(X;w):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{\ell}_{i}\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{I}}}w_{k}\xi^{k}_{i}+\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{II}}}X_{k}\xi^{k}_{i}\bigg)-w_{\ell},\quad\ell\in{\mathrm{II}}.

It is readily seen that
F(X;w)=\partial_{X}G(X;w)
for
G
given by

\displaystyle G(X;\,w):=\exp\big(\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}w_{\mathrm{I}}+\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X\big)\cdot{\bf 1}-w_{{\mathrm{II}}}\cdot X,

where we abbreviated
{\bf 1}:=(1,\dots,1)^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Moreover, we compute for
j,\ell\in{\mathrm{II}}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{X_{j}}F_{\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{\ell}_{i}\xi^{j}_{i}\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{I}}}w_{k}\xi^{k}_{i}+\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{II}}}X_{k}\xi^{k}_{i}\bigg)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{\ell}_{i}\xi^{j}_{i}u_{i},

that is
\partial_{X}F=\mathsf{P}D(u)\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}
.
Thus,
\partial_{X}F
is symmetric positive definite, and
X\mapsto G(X;w)
is strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{r}}
. The equations
F(X;w)=0
possess a unique solution
X=X(w)
if and only if
X
is the unique global minimiser of
G(\cdot;w)
. To prove its existence, a sufficient condition is that
G(X;w)\rightarrow+\infty
for
|X|\rightarrow+\infty
. To establish this property, recall that for
w_{{\mathrm{II}}}\in\mathsf{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}
, there exists

\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
such that
w_{{\mathrm{II}}}=\mathsf{P}\zeta
, and then

\displaystyle G(X;w):=\exp\big(\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}w_{\mathrm{I}}+\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X\big)\cdot{\bf 1}-\zeta\cdot\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X.

For
|X|\rightarrow+\infty
, we must also have
|\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X|\rightarrow+\infty
and we distinguish two cases. If
\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}(\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X)_{i}\rightarrow+\infty
, the exponential term dominates and
G(X;w)
tends to infinity.
Otherwise, if
\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}(\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X)_{i}
is bounded above, then
\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}(\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X)_{i}\rightarrow-\infty
as
|X|\to+\infty
. The exponential term is bounded, but since
\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}\zeta_{i}>0
, we see that
-\zeta\cdot\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}X\rightarrow+\infty
, and
G(X;w)
again tends to infinity.
Thus, we may recover
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
from
w\in\mathcal{D}
via

(5.9)

\displaystyle u_{i}=\exp\bigg(\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{I}}}w_{k}\xi^{k}_{i}+\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{II}}}X_{k}(w)\xi^{k}_{i}\Big)=:\Psi_{i}(w),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

This completes the proof.
∎

For later reference, we note the formula

(5.10)

\displaystyle(\partial_{X}F)^{-1}=\mathsf{P}[D(u)]^{-1}\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}-\mathsf{P}[D(u)]^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{Q}D(u)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}D(u)^{-1}\mathsf{P}^{\sf T},

which can be verified using (
5.2
), as well as the identity

(5.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{w_{m}}X(w)=-(\partial_{X}F)^{-1}\mathsf{P}D(u)\xi^{m},\quad m\in{\mathrm{I}},

which follows from
0=\partial_{w_{m}}(F(X;w))=\partial_{X}F\partial_{w_{m}}X+\partial_{w_{m}}F=\partial_{X}F\partial_{w_{m}}X+\mathsf{P}D(u)\xi^{m}
.

We are now in a position to derive an appropriate normal form for system (

B

). We continue to denote by
\Psi
the inverse of the diffeomorphism
\Phi
.

Proposition 5.2

(Normal form)
.

A vector
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
of
positive functions is a classical solution to (

B

) if and only if the transformed variables
w=(w_{\mathrm{I}},w_{\mathrm{II}})
defined via
w_{\mathrm{I}}=\mathsf{Q}\log u
and
w_{\mathrm{II}}=\mathsf{P}u
satisfy, in the classical sense,

(5.12a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)\partial_{t}w_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{I}}_{1}(w,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}})\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{I}}+f^{\mathrm{I}}(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}),

(5.12b)

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{II}}(\lambda)\partial_{t}w_{\mathrm{II}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{A}_{1}^{{\mathrm{II}}}(w)\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}),

where

\mathbb{A}_{1}^{{\mathrm{II}}}(w)=D^{\mathrm{II}}(\lambda)\mathsf{P}D(\Psi(w))\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}D^{{\mathrm{II}}}(\lambda),

D^{\mathrm{II}}(\lambda)=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{n-\mathsf{r}+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and

D(\Psi(w)):={\rm diag}(\Psi_{1}(w),\ldots,\Psi_{n}(w))
.
The maps

\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{(n-\mathsf{r})\times(n-\mathsf{r})}_{\rm spd},\quad\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{I}}_{1}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{r}}\to\mathbb{R}^{(n-\mathsf{r})\times(n-\mathsf{r})}_{\rm sym},\quad f^{\mathrm{I}}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{r}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-\mathsf{r}}

are smooth, and
\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{I}}_{1}
is linear in the second argument.
More specifically,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathsf{Q}D(\Psi(w))^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}\big)^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{I}}_{1}(w,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Q}\Sigma(\Psi(w))D(\Psi(w))^{-1}D[\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}\lambda\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}}]\Sigma(\Psi(w))\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T},

where
D[\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}\lambda\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}}]
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries given by the vector

\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}D^{\mathrm{II}}(\lambda)\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}}
. Moreover,

(5.13)

\displaystyle\Sigma(\Psi(w)):=\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}\big(\mathsf{Q}D(\Psi(w))^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}\big)^{-1}\mathsf{Q},\quad f^{\mathrm{I}}(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}})=\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)g(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}),

and
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-\mathsf{r}})
is defined in (
5.16
).

Proof.

We differentiate (
5.9
) to find that

(5.14)

\displaystyle\partial_{w_{m}}\log u_{i}=\xi^{m}_{i}+\sum_{k\in{\mathrm{II}}}\partial_{w_{m}}X_{k}(w)\xi^{k}_{i},\quad m\in{\mathrm{I}}.

Introducing
\mu_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}u_{j}
, it follows that (
5.5
) is equivalent to

(5.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}\nabla\log u_{i}\cdot\nabla\mu_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}\sum_{m\in{\mathrm{I}}}\partial_{w_{m}}\log u_{i}\nabla w_{m}\cdot\nabla\mu_{i}+g_{k},\ k\in{\mathrm{I}},\qquad

where
\nabla\mu_{i}=\sum_{j\in{\mathrm{II}}}\lambda_{j}\xi^{j}_{i}\nabla w_{j}
does not depend on the gradient of the hyperbolic variables, and

(5.16)

\displaystyle g_{k}=g_{k}(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}\sum_{m\in{\mathrm{II}}}\partial_{w_{m}}\log u_{i}\nabla w_{m}\cdot\nabla\mu_{i}

is a quadratic expression in the second argument.
Thus, for
\nu=1,\ldots,d
, the critical term multiplying
\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{m}
in equations (
5.15
) equals

\displaystyle Z_{km}

\displaystyle=Z_{km}(w,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}\partial_{w_{m}}\log u_{i}\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i}

(5.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}\bigg(\xi^{m}_{i}+\sum_{j\in{\mathrm{II}}}\partial_{w_{m}}X_{j}(w)\xi^{j}_{i}\bigg)\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i},\quad k,m\in{\mathrm{I}},

where the last equality follows from (
5.14
).
Using identity (
5.11
), we have

\displaystyle u_{i}\sum_{j\in{\mathrm{II}}}\partial_{w_{m}}X_{j}(w)\xi^{j}_{i}=e^{i}\cdot\big[D(u)\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}\partial_{w_{m}}X(w)\big]=-e^{i}\cdot\big[D(u)\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}(\partial_{X}F)^{-1}\mathsf{P}D(u)\xi^{m}\big],

where
e^{i}
is the standard
i^{\rm th}
unit vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We combine this result with (
5.2
):

(5.18)

\displaystyle Z_{km}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}u_{i}\bigg(\xi^{m}_{i}+\sum_{j\in{\mathrm{II}}}\partial_{w_{m}}X_{j}(w)\xi^{j}_{i}\bigg)\frac{\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i}}{u_{i}}

(5.19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}e^{i}\cdot\big[\big(D(u)-D(u)\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}(\partial_{X}F)^{-1}\mathsf{P}D(u)\big)\xi^{m}\big]\frac{\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i}}{u_{i}}

(5.20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{k}_{i}e^{i}\cdot(\Sigma(u)\xi^{m})\frac{\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i}}{u_{i}},

where
\Sigma(u):=D(u)-D(u)\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}(\partial_{X}F)^{-1}\mathsf{P}D(u)
.
A computation using (
5.10
) and the third identity in (
5.2
) show that

\displaystyle\Sigma(u)=\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}\big(\mathsf{Q}[D(u)]^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}\big)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}.

The symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-\mathsf{r})\times(n-\mathsf{r})}
, given by

(5.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)=\mathsf{Q}\Sigma(u)\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}=\big(\mathsf{Q}D(u)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}\big)^{-1},\quad u:=\Psi(w),

is our candidate for the symmetriser.
We note that the identity
\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbb{I}_{n-\mathsf{r}}
and the form of
\Sigma(u)
imply that

(5.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\mathsf{Q}D(u)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\mathsf{Q}D(u)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{Q}\Sigma(u).\qquad

For
\ell,m\in{\mathrm{I}}
and
\nu=1,\ldots,d
, we define

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{A}^{\mathrm{I}}_{1}(w,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}})\big)_{\ell m}:=\sum_{j\in{\mathrm{I}}}(\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w))_{\ell j}Z_{jm}(u,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}}).

Using (
5.20
) and (
5.22
), we compute

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{A}_{1}^{\mathrm{I}}(w,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}})\big)_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in{\mathrm{I}}}(\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w))_{\ell j}\mathsf{Q}_{ji}\big(e^{i}\cdot(\Sigma(u)\xi^{m})\big)\frac{\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i}}{u_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in{\mathrm{I}}}\mathsf{Q}_{\ell j}\Sigma(u)_{ji}\big(e^{i}\cdot(\Sigma(u)\xi^{m})\big)\frac{\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i}}{u_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(e^{i}\cdot(\Sigma(u)\xi^{\ell})\big)\big(e^{i}\cdot(\Sigma(u)\xi^{m})\big)\frac{\partial_{x_{\nu}}\mu_{i}}{u_{i}},

which is a symmetric expression in
\ell
and
m
. In matrix notation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{1}^{\mathrm{I}}(w,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}})=\mathsf{Q}\Sigma(u)D(u)^{-1}D[\mathsf{P}^{\sf T}\lambda\partial_{x_{\nu}}w]\Sigma(u)\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T}.

Hence, after left-multiplication by
\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)
,
equations (
5.15
) turn into the system

(5.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)\partial_{t}w_{\mathrm{I}}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\mathbb{A}_{1}^{\mathrm{I}}(w,\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{II}})\partial_{x_{\nu}}w_{\mathrm{I}}+f^{\mathrm{I}}(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}),

whose principle part is symmetric and
where
f^{\mathrm{I}}(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}):=\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)g(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}})
.
Combining (
5.3
) and (
5.23
),
we have obtained a composite symmetric hyperbolic–parabolic normal form as asserted.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

In some cases, we may derive explicit expressions for the
symmetriser. For instance, if
\mathsf{r}=1
and
b_{ij}=k_{i}k_{j}
,
formula (
5.21
), the definition of
\Sigma(u)
and the fact that
\mathsf{P}=\pm(\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}^{2})^{-1/2}k^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}

lead to

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}(w)=\mathsf{Q}\bigg(D(u)-\frac{D(u){k}\otimes D(u){k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}^{2}u_{i}}\bigg)\mathsf{Q}^{\sf T},\quad u:=\Psi(w),

where

.

Remark 5.4
.

In Section
3.2
, we have obtained a different symmetriser,
which takes a simple diagonal form. On the other hand, the corresponding
transformation (

) was not constructed using an orthonormal
system. This shows that other choices for the basis
(\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{n})
might be
practically relevant, at least in the rank-one case. In a similar spirit, the
observations in Appendix

show that more involved nonlinear multipliers might also be considered.

5.3.
Local classical solutions in the general case

We recall from the preceding section that the change of variables
\Phi
maps
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
diffeomorphically onto
\mathcal{D}:=\mathbb{R}^{n-\mathsf{r}}\times\mathsf{P}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}

(Local classical solutions)

Let

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{\mathrm{I}}\in C([0,T];H^{s-1}),\quad w_{\mathrm{I}}\in C^{1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}),

(5.25)

Proof.

By virtue of Theorem
2.5
, we have reduced the question of the local existence of strong solutions to system (

) to that of constructing strong solutions to system (
5.12
).
For the latter, we may essentially follow the proof of Theorem
2.3
,
and we only describe the necessary modifications.
The main point is that for
\mathsf{r}>1

In particular, we need to ensure that, on a short time interval, the approximate solutions stay in an appropriate domain of uniform parabolicity of the parabolic subsystem.
We therefore modify the definition of

, now requiring it to be the maximal time less than or equal to

such that the smooth solution

to the linear approximate problem analogous to (

\displaystyle\|w^{\ell}\|_{X_{t}^{s}}<KR\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{(0,t)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho(w_{\mathrm{II}}^{\ell})>\frac{\bar{\rho}}{4}\quad\text{for all }t<t_{\ell}.

In the next step, we derive an estimate analogous to that in Lemma
4.2

(i)
. Thanks to the relatively simple symmetric form (

(i)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}^{\ell}(\tau)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C(R,\bar{\rho})\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,\min\{t_{\ell-1},1\}),\;\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{+}.

Let us emphasise that we do not yet need the improved control of the values
to conclude (
5.27

At this stage, we are in a position to derive a refined control of the values substituting for Lemma
4.1

w_{\mathrm{I}}
, we proceed as before. For the parabolic components, we rely on the following estimate for all

0\leq t\leq\min\{t_{\ell},1\}

\displaystyle\|w_{\mathrm{II}}^{\ell}(t)-w_{\mathrm{II}}^{\ell}(0)\|_{C(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}w_{\mathrm{II}}^{\ell}(\tau)\|_{H^{s-1}}\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\sqrt{t}C(R,\bar{\rho}),

where in the second step we used the bound (
5.27

by suitable spatial derivatives.
Thus, by choosing

(5.29)

\displaystyle w^{\ell}(t,x)\in\mathcal{D}_{0}:=\bigg\{\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{D}:|\tilde{w}|<2LR\text{ and }\rho(\tilde{w}_{\mathrm{II}})>\frac{\bar{\rho}}{2}\bigg\}

The convergence in a weaker norm and regularity results analogous to those in Lemma

The regularity

It remains to prove the regularity

As in the proof of (

Appendix A
Alternative transformations

A.1.
A general ansatz

Here, we briefly sketch an ansatz towards a characterisation of
the set of possible diffeomorphisms
w=\Phi(u)
,
u\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
,
leading to a normal form of hyperbolic–parabolic type.
For simplicity, we restrict to the rank-one case
\mathsf{r}=1
and abbreviate
n^{\prime}:=n-1
.
As in Section
5
, we introduce the partition

{\mathrm{I}}=\{1,\dots,n^{\prime}\}
and
{\mathrm{II}}=\{n\}
, write

(w_{\mathrm{I}},w_{\mathrm{II}})=(\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(u),\Phi_{\mathrm{II}}(u))
and use the
notational conventions introduced above.
We further set
\Psi=\Phi^{-1}
.

For the hyperbolic components, the essential condition for cancelling the
second-order derivatives is
\mathrm{D}\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(u)D(u)B\equiv 0
.
For problem (
1.3
), i.e. with

B={k}\otimes{a}
for vectors
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}
,

a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}
,
k_{i}
,
a_{i}>0
, this amounts to requiring that

\mathrm{D}\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(u)D(k)u=0\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}
.
Hence, the map
\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}
must be constant along the integral curves

\gamma_{u_{*}}(t)=D(\mathrm{e}^{kt})u_{*}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
u_{*}\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
,
of the vector field
V(u):=D(k)u
.
Any candidate mapping must thus satisfy

(A.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(\gamma_{u_{*}}(t))=c(u_{*})\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R},u_{*}\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}.

Notice that for the transformation in Section
5
, this condition is fulfilled with

\displaystyle{\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}}_{i}(\gamma_{u_{*}}(t))=\sum_{j}\mathsf{Q}_{ij}\log(\gamma_{u_{*},j}(t))=\sum_{j}\mathsf{Q}_{ij}(k_{j}t+\log(u_{*,j}))=\sum_{j}\mathsf{Q}_{ij}\log(u_{*,j}),

where we used the fact that the rows of the matrix
\mathsf{Q}
defined in (
5.1
)
are orthogonal to
{k}
.
For the transformation (
3.1
), property (
A.1
) follows from a similar calculation.
In Section
A.2
, we will briefly discuss a different change of coordinates that is subject to (
A.1
).

With the choice
w_{\mathrm{II}}:=a\cdot u
as the diffusive variable and under condition (
A.1
),
system (
Rk1
) in the new variables
w=\Phi(u)
takes the form

(A.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}\cdot\mathbb{Y}(w)\nabla w_{\mathrm{I}}+f(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}),

(A.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{\mathrm{II}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}\big(\mathfrak{a}(w)\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}\big),

where
\mathfrak{a}(w)=\sum_{i}a_{i}u_{i}k_{i}
and

(A.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(u)D(k)D_{w_{\mathrm{I}}}\Psi(w),

(A.5)

\displaystyle f(w,\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(u)D(k)\mathrm{D}_{w_{\mathrm{II}}}\Psi(w)|\nabla w_{\mathrm{II}}|^{2},

and we recognise the structure of (

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{u_{\mathrm{I}}}\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(u)D^{\mathrm{I}}(k)\mathrm{D}_{w_{\mathrm{I}}}\Psi_{\mathrm{I}}(w)+\mathrm{D}_{u_{\mathrm{II}}}\Phi_{\mathrm{I}}(u)k_{n}\mathrm{D}_{w_{\mathrm{I}}}\Psi_{\mathrm{II}}(w),

A.2.
An example

Consider the system

For

Remark A.1
