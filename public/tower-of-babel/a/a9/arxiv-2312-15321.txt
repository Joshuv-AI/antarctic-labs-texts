Greedy Grammar Induction with Indirect Negative Evidence
Joseph Potashnik

Abstract
This paper offers a fresh look at the pumping lemma constant as an upper bound on the information required for learning Context Free Grammars. An objective function based on indirect negative evidence considers the occurrences, and non-occurrences, of a finite number of strings, encountered after a sufficiently long presentation. This function has optimal substructure in the hypotheses space, giving rise to a greedy search learner in a branch and bound method. A hierarchy of learnable classes is defined in terms of the number of production rules that must be added to interim solutions in order to incrementally fit the input. Efficiency strongly depends on the position of the target grammar in the hierarchy and on the richness of the input.



1
Introduction

In the domain of unsupervised grammar induction, two fundamental problems must be addressed: (i) the choice of the optimisation function, and (ii), the method of hypotheses space traversal. In order to successfully learn grammars from data, one must come up with good solutions for both problems. For example, the limited success of grammar induction models such as the Constituent Context Model
Klein and Manning 2002
or the Dependency Model with Valence
Klein and Manning 2004
does not mean that their optimisations goals are incorrect, but largely stems from the fact they do not offer a good solution for the search space traversal problem. The non-convex landscape of the proposed optimisation functions means that reaching a globally optimal solution in an intractable search space (e.g., with the EM algorithm, see also
Dempster et al. 1977
) is difficult. Meta-heuristics, such as genetic algorithms and evolutionary methods (see
Keller and Lutz 1997
,
Koza 1994
and
Wyard 1994
, among others) are also employed.

In what follows, I propose a fitness function that is monotonically non-increasing in the hypotheses space so the landscape can be exploited by a Branch and Bound method. If a hypothesis grammar is non-optimal, any grammar which contains it as a subset is also non-optimal. In other words, the fitness function shows optimal substructure, which paves the way to a greedy algorithm. The choice of the fitness function then also helps the learner to efficiently traverse the search space in a tractable manner.

The Logical Problem Of Language Acquisition presents difficulties for traditional formal approaches, because there is no mechanism to dispose of a held belief which has no evidence against it, since a negative cannot be proven. The lack of evidence, however substantial, does not allow us to modify our beliefs. This problem is often taken to support the argument from poverty of stimulus.

Bayesian approaches offer us a principled way to modify our beliefs through (iterative) exposure to evidence. According to the support of evidence, our prior belief may strengthen or weaken. Direct evidence contrary to our belief is never received as none exists, yet the nonoccurrence of expected data is informative. This is what has been referred to as Indirect Negative Evidence (see
Chomsky 1981
,
Clark and Lappin 2010
). In what follows, it will be shown that a fitness function that relies on indirect negative evidence is a good choice for both selecting optimal grammars and an efficient search space traversal.

The pumping lemma

The Pumping Lemma

In his seminal paper,

which contains all finite languages and at least one infinite recursive language is not learnable (i.e., not identifiable in the limit). However, Gold’s assumption that a learner should be able to identify a class in the limit given any presentation is too strong. He mentions the possibility that "there is an a priori restriction on the class of texts which can occur" (p. 454). In the words of
Rohde and Plaut 1999

only
if texts are arbitrary or are produced by the powerful class of recursive functions". Learnability can be proven for CFGs for a certain class of texts by using the pumping lemma.

Theorem 2.1
(Learnability of CFGs)

Any target CFG

is identifiable in the limit given a fair basic text generated by it.

Proof

By showing that the occurrence of the basic strings set
B_{G}
constitutes a mind-change bound. That is, no other string can surprise or mislead the learner.

Observe that
B_{G}
contains all production rules of the target grammar

. The pumping lemma tells us that any pumped string of the form
\{s=uv^{n}wx^{m}y,n>1\text{ or }m>1\}
is composed of existing subtrees, so all rules are already accounted for in the data.

Since there are only finitely many parse trees hitherto seen, there are finitely many production rules used in those trees, and consequently finitely many grammars.

Mind change bound entails learnability, because a learner remains with a finite set of candidate grammars, which the learner need not announce. The teacher might present further evidence arbitrarily long afterwards, which can be used to eliminate a finite number of wrong candidates.

In a probabilistic setting, the problem can be viewed as a special case of the second Borel-Cantelli lemma. Assuming I.I.D, any finite subset
B_{G}
that has some fixed nonzero probability occurs almost surely infinitely often. Furthermore, the expected time to encounter all basic strings in
B_{G}
is finite. This problem is reducible to the coupon collector problem: what is the expected time to draw coupons (basic strings) before having drawn each coupon at least once?

These results are a mild relaxation of the findings of

These learnability results are impractical because the enumeration of the finite set of candidate grammars is hopelessly intractable. Each basic string has an exponential number of corresponding trees, and each subset of trees has an exponential number of possible production rules. Lastly, according to the probability distribution of the basic strings,
\mathrm{E}(T)
can be unfathomably high.

In what follows, it is shown that, given a stronger assumption about the rate of the growth of the strings, and using indirect negative evidence, there are computationally feasible learnable classes of grammars.

3
The Fitness Function

A characteristic problem in unsupervised language learning is the difficulty of defining an error term (in the same way it is defined for supervised learning) for strings not in the target language
\mathbb{L}
, since they have zero probability. For example, the promiscuous grammar
Solomonoff 1964
that generates all possible sequences of strings, has zero error. I use indirect negative evidence in lieu of error with the following rationale: treating a hypothesis grammar
G
as a generative process, we have the capacity to enumerate all its strings regardless of actual input. The failure of a string generated by
G
to appear in input
D
after a sufficiently large number of samples, educates us that its true, unknown probability is vanishingly small. The hypothesis grammar would then be a bad hypothesis because it underfits the data.

Let the
Evidence Shape

ES(D|G)=[es_{0},es_{1},es_{2},\dots,es_{p}]

ES(D|G)_{i}\leq GS(G)_{i}
.

The following fitness function

calculates the ratio of observed basic strings out of all possible basic strings, up to the pumping lemma constant

:

f(D|G)=\frac{\sum_{i}ES(D|G)_{i}}{\sum_{i}GS(G)_{i}}

(3)

The range of the fitness function is
0\leq f(D|G)\leq 1
. If
\sum_{i}GS(G)_{i}=0
, the fitness is undefined. A fitness score of 1 is optimal when

maximally fits the data: the evidence and the grammar shapes vectors are identical. A lower score means that there is some basic string generated by the hypothesis grammar that yields an unheard string. That is, indirect negative evidence.

5
The Learning Algorithm

5.1
Lattice Traversal

The learner is a greedy graph traversal algorithm that traverses the hypotheses space through locally optimal solutions and their adjacents, going only through solutions with non-increasing values of
f
. Any other non-optimal solutions and the infinitely many solutions that contain them are discarded (a Branch and Bound method). The implementation offered below is by no means unique. Other implementations might consider changing the representation of the lattice space, the choice of the parser, or the method of graph traversal. Since it is assumed that grammars are nonlexicalized, and Part-Of-Speech (henceforth: POS) categories for lexical tokens are provided separately, the input sentences are mapped into sequences of POS symbols in a preprocessing step. That is, a string generated by a hypothesis
G
is a sequence of POS symbols.

Let us construct the hypotheses space as the infinite powerset lattice of the countable set of production rules. For a (non-lexicalized) CFG
<V,T,P,S>
, terminals are Part of Speech symbols,
T=POS
.
V
are nonterminals except the start symbol
S
. The rules
P
are given in a nearly Chomsky Normal Form: (i)
Core
rules with the form
X\rightarrow YZ
s.t.
X,Y,Z\in V
or the form
S\rightarrow X
s.t.
X\in V
. (ii)
POS Assignment
rules with the form
X\rightarrow x,X\in V,x\in T
.

Each vertex in the lattice represents a set of Core Rules and can be extended with POS Assignment rules; fortunately only those which match the input need be considered. Earley parsers are capable of being run in a "wildcard mode" which allows arbitrary continuations of subtrees
Stolcke 1995
. Here, the wildcard mode is used to find a
satisfaction
of POS Assignment rules that allows the sentence to be parsed. For example, parsing the sentence
John saw Mary
with the set of Core rules
\{S\rightarrow XY,Y\rightarrow ZW\}
in a "wildcard mode", yields the following POS Assignments rules that match the input:
\{X\rightarrow PN,Z\rightarrow V,W\rightarrow PN\}
. (Given separately that
\{PN\rightarrow John|Mary,V\rightarrow saw\}
). This is a flavour of "bottom-up" parsing injected into a top-down parser, and the advantage of being able to discard grammars that do not match the input comes with the cost of keeping track of some information about POS assignments.

The learner performs a BFS over the hypotheses space, For each vertex
v
, we determine whether it contains an optimal
G
, according to the value of the fitness function
f
(see (
3
)), which requires computation of the Evidence Shape Vector
ES(D|G)
and the Grammar Shape Vector
GS(G)
(the way they are computed follows below).

The algorithm receives 3 meta-parameters. Their impact on learnability and complexity is discussed in detail in section
7
.

•

The degree of incrementality,
k
, controls the maximum distance (number of edges) from a vertex after which another string is parsed.

•

Maximum distance,
m
, from the empty set in which a minimal locally optimal solution is found; typically equals the length of the shortest sentence in the input.

•

Fitness threshold,
t
, that controls the value below which a solution is no longer deemed optimal. The unknown fitness of the unknown grammar that generated the input must be above
t
to be discoverable.

The algorithm is given in pseudo-code below; full implementation is provided in the accompanying code. The crux of the algorithm is its greedy nature: advancing only through optimal vertices, so a solution is guaranteed if there exists an optimal path to a global optimum. That is, a path along which we incrementally find optimal grammars for larger and larger parts of the data. Any non-optimal vertex, and the infinitely many vertices that contain it as a subset, are discarded. Stop Criteria can vary; simple choices are to stop once a shortest global optima has been found, or to accumulate weakly equivalent solutions until the distance from the shortest global optima found so far exceeds

5.2
Computation of the evidence shape vector

The parser is given a grammar
G
and input
D
and returns the evidence shape vector: the number of basic strings, per each length up to the pumping lemma constant
p
, as well as the ratio of parsed strings in the input. Basic strings can be separated from pumped strings by examining the number of recursions along a path to a leaf in a parse tree. If a parsed string
s
with length
n
has a derivation in which it is a basic string,
ES[n]
is incremented by 1, regardless of the ambiguity of
s
.

The paper’s parser of choice is an Earley Parser (see
Earley 1970
) that generates the full forest in a single pass over the input. Linking of the vertices (Earley Items) in the forest graph is done during application of the
complete
deduction rule by keeping pointers from the consequent Item to the antecedent Items: (i) the Item whose Earley dot is incremented, (ii) the completed Item:

[A\rightarrow\mu\bullet B\nu,i,j]

[B\rightarrow\zeta\bullet,j,k]

complete

[A\rightarrow\mu B\bullet\nu,i,k]

To avoid exponential increase in Items for highly ambiguous grammars, the parser uses a Shared Packed Parse Forest representation, which has two hallmarks: (i) Subtrees are shared, so common subtrees are represented only once. (ii) Local ambiguity packing: whenever the same nonterminal spans the same input, the corresponding completed Items (with different analyses) are put into one packed node which is called here Earley Span. All Earley Items and Spans are unique (see
Scott 2008
,
Tomita 1985
).

Complexity

Time complexity
: In order to compute the fitness function at each vertex of the lattice, the evidence and grammar shape vectors are computed. For the evidence shape vector, the input
D
is parsed with an Earley parser with complexity
O(n^{3})
where
n

G

D
. Thus, at most
O(mn^{3})
is spent to evaluate the fitness function

per vertex (
m

Time complexity for a BFS is linear with the visited vertices and edges in the hypotheses space. The learner only visits the optimal grammars and their adjacents, up to a maximum distance
k
. The number of adjacents for each vertex is bound from above by the number of production rules, which in itself, given CNF representation, is bound by
|V|^{3}
, where
V
are the nonterminals appearing in the global solutions. The time spent on traversing the graph is
O(|V|^{3k})
per each optimal vertex.

The number of optimal vertices that are discoverable depends on the number of vertices whose fitness values are within the range
[1,t]
(
t
is the fitness threshold). Call
\mathcal{O}(D,t)
the function which returns the optimal vertices set. The efficiency of the learner strongly depends on the growth of
|\mathcal{O}(D,t)|
, discussed in the next section.

In total, time complexity is
O(|V|^{3k}mn^{3}|\mathcal{O}(D,t)|)
, where
V
are nonterminals,
m
number of sentences,
n
maximum sentence length seen so far,
k
is the degree of incrementality,
\mathcal{O}(D,t)
returns the optimal vertices set given input
D
and fitness threshold
t
. Tighter upper bounds are possible.

7.1
Learnable Classes

Define classes of
(m,k)-incremental
grammars
\mathcal{C}_{m,k}
in the following way:

Definition 7.1
(
(m,k)
-incremental classes)

A grammar
G=<V,T,P,S>\in\mathcal{C}_{m,k}
if there is a sequence
s=<G_{1},G_{2},\dots G_{n}>
, where
G_{i}=<V,T,P_{i},S>
and
G_{n}=G
, such that:

•

|P_{1}|=m

•

P_{i}\subset P_{i+1}

•

L(G_{i})\subset L(G_{i+1})

•

|P_{i+1}|-|P_{i}|\leq k

The similarity between
k
and Joshi’s constant growth
Joshi 1985
is striking. A language with constant growth has the property that if its strings are ordered in increasing length, the difference in length (cardinality) between two subsequent strings does not exceed a constant. Here, a
k
-incremental grammar has the property that if subsets of its rules are ordered in the partial order of the languages they yield, the difference between the cardinality of subsequent subsets does not exceed
k
.

It is easy to see that
(m,k)
-incremental class is exactly the set of all grammars in the hypothesis space reachable by the learning algorithm with the meta-parameters
m,k
. For any target grammar
G
, there is some
\mathcal{C}_{m,k}
that contains it. Granted, the learnable classes coincide with the the way the algorithm operates by definition; however this choice has theoretic implications too, as it provides a characteristic relating to the internal structure of the grammar. Grammars in the same class share the same pattern of production rule growth, which in turn affects the string growth we can expect. It is similar to partitioning languages based on the value of their respective (Joshi’s) growth constant. The class of Context free Grammars can be perceived as
\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{m,k}
. Since the learner requires a finite
k
, not all CFGs are learnable.

8
Future Research

A common problem is the estimation of the values of the parameters
k
and
t
(
m
is typically the length of the shortest input sentence). A-priori, nothing guarantees that there is a grammar
h\in\mathcal{C}_{m,k}
that is weakly equivalent to the target grammar. A possible heuristic is to have a variable
\hat{k}
that follows the rate of growth of sentences length in the input. Alternately,
\hat{k}
can be iteratively increased after a certain time threshold has been reached.

Estimating
t
faces a similar difficulty. The estimated threshold
\hat{t}
must not exceed the true unknown fitness value of the target grammar
t
. Ideally
\hat{t}=t
to run the learner as efficiently as possible. The problem is reducible to estimating the number of unseen species (i.e. the number of unobserved basic strings). Estimators such as Good-Toulmin may be used (see
Good and Toulmin 1956
,
Orlitsky et al. 2016
).

Ranking candidates in the optimal solutions set also remains an open problem. The slowest-growing grammar may be considered as the best choice. Alternatively, it is possible to estimate the false negatives ratio by parametric and/or distribution-free metrics. That is, to take into account the probability of yet-unseen strings.

Lastly, it is worthwhile to investigate whether the growth of
\mathcal{O}(D,t)
can be studied from a general perspective. Are there provably polynomial times for certain inputs and certain grammars? At this stage of the enquiry, the learner plots the empirical growth of the optimal points so efficiency is determined on a case-by-case basis.

9
Summary

In this paper, I explore whether indirect negative evidence affects learnability of context free languages. If grammars are generative mathematical and/or mental objects, then we have access to the strings they generate, regardless of input. The fact that expected strings fail to appear in the text, after a sufficient number of samples, informs us that a candidate grammar underfits the data. The fitness function (section
3
) is linear with respect to the input and monotonically nonincreasing with respect to the hypotheses space (a powerset lattice of production rules). It can be viewed as minimising the false positives rates over the basic strings of the grammar (see (
1
)). Crucially, the fitness function makes use of a finite number of strings, because the pumping lemma guarantees that every rule of the target grammar is encountered, so it is possible to predict every pumped string from its basic counterpart.

The reason why the fitness function is doubly useful is due to optimal substructure over the hypotheses space (see section
4
), thus eliminating the problem of non-convex landscapes optimisation. An underfitting grammar and the infinitely many grammars that contain its production rules as a subset can be eliminated from the enumeration of candidate grammars (i.e., a Branch and Bound method).

The learner is a learning algorithm for the class
\mathcal{C}_{m,k}
which contains all grammars whose production rule growth does not exceed a constant
k
, the degree of incrementality (see
7.1
). From a learnability perspective, CFGs can be partitioned into a hierarchy of nested classes ranked by the degree
k
which characterizes how efficiently a learner increasingly fits the data.

Diverse nonlexicalized target grammars were successfully learned from POS-tagged input. These novel positive learnability results demonstrate that it is possible to efficiently learn context free languages with little input, and shed further light on the argument from the poverty of stimulus.
